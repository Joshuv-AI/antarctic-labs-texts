Abnormal serum IL-10 and IL-19 levels in childhood- and adolescent-onset schizophrenia: associations with negative symptoms and language function
Authors: Haidong Yang, Jie Hou, Man Yang, Jing Zhang, Lingshu Luan, Li Xu, Xiaobin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe pathophysiological mechanisms of childhood- and adolescent-onset schizophrenia (CAOS) remain incompletely understood, with cytokine abnormalities potentially playing an important role. This study aimed to investigate the relationships of altered serum interleukin (IL)-10 and IL-19 levels with the clinical symptoms and cognitive function of CAOS patients.MethodsThe cross-sectional study included 97 CAOS patients and 40 healthy controls. Serum IL-10 and IL-19 levels were measured. Clinical symptoms were assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), and cognitive function was evaluated using the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS).ResultsCompared to healthy controls, the CAOS patients had significantly lower serum IL-10 levels (p = 0.005) and higher IL-19 levels (p = 0.046). IL-10 was significantly negatively correlated with PANSS negative symptoms (r=-0.338, p = 0.001). The CAOS patients performed significantly worse than healthy controls across all RBANS cognitive domains (all p < 0.001), with significant correlations observed between the severity of clinical symptoms and cognitive impairment. In the healthy controls, IL-10 was significantly negatively correlated with language (r=-0.394, p = 0.012), whereas no such correlation was observed in the CAOS patients (p > 0.05). Low IL-10 levels were significantly associated with CAOS (B = 0.387, p = 0.002, RR = 1.472, 95% CI: 1.159–1.870), with a population attributable fraction of 19.1%. The association between IL-19 and CAOS did not reach statistical significance (B = 0.217, p = 0.081, RR = 1.243, 95% CI: 0.973–1.586).ConclusionsThe CAOS patients had decreased IL-10 and increased IL-19 serum levels, with IL-10 levels associated with negative symptoms and language. These results suggest that IL-10 may serve as a potential biological marker associated with CAOS susceptibility, while the role of IL-19 requires further investigation.Clinical trialNot applicable.Keywords: Childhood- and adolescent-onset schizophrenia, Interleukin-10, Interleukin-19, Cognitive function, Inflammation

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe mental disorder characterized by significant abnormalities in thinking, perception, and behavior, with a global prevalence of approximately 0.5%–1% [1, 2]. Despite various proposed hypotheses involving neurodevelopment, neurotransmitter imbalance, and immune-related inflammation, the exact pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the onset of schizophrenia remain incompletely understood [3]. Childhood- and adolescent-onset schizophrenia (CAOS) is a distinct subtype characterized by more severe clinical manifestations, poorer prognosis, and more pronounced cognitive impairments as compared to adult-onset cases [4]. In recent years, accumulating evidence indicates that immune-related inflammatory responses may be involved in the pathophysiological processes of schizophrenia, with cytokines, as important modulators of the immune system, potentially playing critical roles [5]. In particular, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 and pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-19 have been implicated in the pathology of various neuropsychiatric disorders [6, 7], but potential roles in CAOS remain insufficiently explored.

IL-10, a crucial anti-inflammatory cytokine mainly produced by T cells, monocytes, and macrophages, inhibits the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines and modulates the intensity and duration of immune responses [8]. In contrast, IL-19, although belonging to the IL-10 family, is a pro-inflammatory cytokine primarily secreted by activated monocytes and macrophages that induces the production of various pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby amplifying inflammatory responses [9]. Previous studies have correlated abnormal serum IL-10 levels with the severity of schizophrenia [10, 11], potentially reflecting an imbalance in immune regulatory functions. Although research on IL-19 in schizophrenia is extremely limited, a previous study reported that the TGAA haplotype of the IL-19 gene is significantly associated with a greater genetic risk of schizophrenia [7]. Additionally, cognitive impairments are prevalent in both adult-onset and adolescent-onset schizophrenia, but are more generally severe in younger patients [12, 13]. Exploring the associations of immune markers with the clinical features of CAOS may contribute to a better understanding of the immunopathological mechanisms of the disease (Fig. 1).

Previous studies have identified altered cytokine levels in schizophrenia patients [5, 14], although studies of CAOS patients remain limited [15]. Cytokines not only participate in disease pathogenesis but have also been associated with specific clinical phenotypes. For instance, IL-10 levels have been correlated with the severity of symptoms in schizophrenia and are predictive of poor outcomes [16]. CAOS patients typically present with more severe clinical symptoms and functional impairments, with a disease course that is often more disruptive and exerts long-term adverse effects on the developing individual [17]. Additionally, CAOS patients frequently exhibit significant cognitive impairments [18]. Given the important roles of IL-10 and IL-19 in immune regulation, exploring alterations to the serum levels of these two cytokines in CAOS and potential relationships with clinical manifestations has important significance.

Based on the above research background, we hypothesized that serum levels of IL-10 and IL-19 would differ between CAOS patients and healthy controls, and that these differences may correlate with the severity of clinical symptoms and cognitive function performance. This study aimed to: (1) compare the serum levels of IL-10 and IL-19 between CAOS patients and healthy controls, (2) analyze the correlations between IL-10 and IL-19 levels and the severity of clinical symptoms of CAOS patients, (3) investigate the relationships of IL-10 and IL-19 levels with the cognitive function of CAOS patients, and (4) evaluate the value of these two cytokines for diagnosis of CAOS. The results of this study provide new evidence to clarify cytokine alterations in CAOS patients and help to explore potential relationships with clinical features.

Subjects and methods

Subjects

The cohort of this cross-sectional investigation consisted of 97 eligible inpatients with schizophrenia recruited from the Department of Psychiatry of the Fourth People’s Hospital of Lianyungang between September 2021 and June 2024. All patients were diagnosed by two senior psychiatrists in accordance with the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV). DSM-IV criteria were used because our institutional diagnostic protocols and clinician training were based on DSM-IV at the time of study initiation, DSM-IV has well-established reliability for childhood and adolescent schizophrenia, and the core diagnostic criteria remain consistent between DSM-IV and DSM-5, ensuring comparability with recent literature.

The inclusion criteria for the patient group were: (1) Han Chinese ethnicity; (2) age between 11 and 18 years; (3) first-episode and antipsychotic-naïve; (4) minimum of 6 years of education; and (5) capable of completing all assessments. Concurrently, 40 healthy adolescents matched for age, sex distribution, educational level, and body mass index (BMI) were recruited from the same geographical region as controls. The inclusion criteria for healthy controls were: (1) absence of psychiatric disorders and psychiatric consultation history; (2) no major physical illnesses; (3) Han Chinese ethnicity; (4) no psychiatric disorders among first-degree relatives; (5) age between 11 and 18 years; and (6) able to cooperate with all assessments. First-episode and antipsychotic-naïve status was verified through comprehensive review of medical records to confirm no prior psychiatric diagnoses or hospitalizations, detailed interviews with patients and their guardians regarding psychiatric and medication history, and review of prescription records when available.

The exclusion criteria for all participants comprised: (1) presence of severe physical or neurological conditions; (2) presence of comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders (including autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and intellectual disability) or other major psychiatric disorders as per DSM-IV criteria; (3) meeting the DSM-IV diagnostic criteria for substance-related disorders, including dependence or abuse (alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, stimulants, opioids, etc.), with particular attention to substance use patterns common among adolescents; (4) history of traumatic brain injury or neurosurgical procedures; (5) infection or any other inflammatory condition within the past four weeks; (6) current use of medications affecting immune function (such as corticosteroids or immunomodulators); and (7) pregnant or breastfeeding female participants.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth People’s Hospital of Lianyungang (approval no. 2021LSYYXLL-P11), and was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the vulnerability of minors with mental illnesses, exceptionally rigorous ethical safeguards were implemented: (1) written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians prior to any study procedures; (2) following assessment of the clinical state and cognitive capacity of all participants, those with adequate comprehension ability were provided with age-appropriate simplified explanations of the study prior to obtaining verbal or written consent; (3) for patients with severely impaired cognitive function or the acute phase of schizophrenia who could not provide valid assent, we strictly adhered to guardian consent and eligibility, as authorized by the Ethics Committee; (4) all participants and their guardians were explicitly informed of the voluntary nature of participation and the unconditional right to withdraw at any stage; (5) stringent data confidentiality measures were implemented to ensure personal information was used exclusively for this study; (6) clinical status was continuously monitored by psychiatric healthcare professionals throughout the study, with treatment needs and welfare prioritized; and (7) blood sample volumes were strictly limited within the safety range for pediatric research. Upon study completion, individualized assessment feedback and appropriate treatment recommendations were provided to the guardians of all participants.

Clinical and cognitive assessment

The clinical symptoms of all schizophrenia patients were evaluated using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) by two trained psychiatrists who had established good inter-rater reliability (Kappa coefficient > 0.80). The PANSS consists of 30 items divided into three subscales [19, 20]: positive symptoms (7 items), negative symptoms (7 items), and general psychopathology (16 items). Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. Assessments were conducted during a period of clinical stabilization after admission (typically 3–7 days post-admission) and prior to the initiation of antipsychotic medication to avoid medication-related effects on evaluation outcomes.

The cognitive functions of all participants were examined using the Chinese version of the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS). This battery evaluates five cognitive domains: immediate memory, visuospatial/constructional, language, attention, and delayed memory, providing standardized scores for each domain and a total scale standard score. To ensure consistency in data collection, all participants were assessed using the standardized RBANS protocol, a method previously applied in studies involving Chinese adolescent populations [21, 22]. All assessments were conducted in quiet, standardized environments by professionally trained psychological testers, with testing sessions lasting approximately 30 min. Quality control of the test results was performed by senior clinical psychologists.

Measurement of serum IL-10 and IL-19 levels

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from all study participants on the morning following admission or recruitment between 7:00 and 9:00 a.m. Participants were instructed to fast for at least 8 h prior to blood collection. Peripheral venous blood (5 mL) was drawn into vacuum tubes coated with the anticoagulant ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid and gently mixed by inverting several times. Samples were immediately centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to separate the serum. The isolated serum was aliquoted into multiple sterile cryogenic vials and stored at -80 °C until batch analysis, while avoiding repeated freeze-thaw cycles. Serum concentrations of IL-10 and IL-19 were determined using Luminex liquid suspension chip technology in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) [23]. Signal acquisition and analysis were performed using the Luminex® 200 Flow Cytometry System (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA), and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated by five-parameter logistic regression modeling. To ensure measurement accuracy, all samples were analyzed in duplicate wells, and the average values were used for comparisons. Inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) were maintained below 15% and intra-assay CVs less than 10%. All testing procedures were performed by experienced laboratory technicians who were blinded to sample grouping information to eliminate potential subjective bias.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 25.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and non-normally distributed data as the median with interquartile range [M (P25, P75)]. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages (n, %). For between-group comparisons, the independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Differences in categorical variables between groups were analyzed using the chi-square test (χ² test). Post-hoc power analyses were conducted using G*Power 3.1 software to evaluate the statistical power of our main findings. The Pearson correlation or Spearman rank correlation coefficient was employed to assess relationships between variables. For the correlation analyses between PANSS and RBANS domains involving multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied to control for Type I error, with the adjusted significance threshold set at α = 0.05/24 = 0.00208. To further explore the association between IL-10 levels and the PANSS negative symptom scores, stepwise linear regression analysis was conducted with PANSS negative symptoms as the dependent variable and IL-10 levels, age, sex, BMI, family history of mental disorders, duration of illness, and age of onset as independent variables.

To evaluate the association between IL-10 and IL-19 with CAOS susceptibility, the study subjects (patients and healthy controls) were combined into one sample, with CAOS status as the dependent variable. IL-10 and IL-19 levels were dichotomized into high and low groups based on median values, with the IL-10 low group coded as 1 and the IL-10 high group as 0, while the IL-19 high group was coded as 1 and the IL-19 low group as 0, serving as independent variables. Modified Poisson regression analysis was performed with age, sex, education, and BMI as control variables. The attributable fraction and population attributable fraction were calculated to assess the impact of IL-10 and IL-19 on the risk of CAOS. All statistical tests were two-sided and a probability (p) value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

As illustrated in Table 1; Fig. 2, serum IL-10 levels were significantly lower in the CAOS group as compared to the healthy control group [2.91 (1.42, 5.07) vs. 4.08 (3.60, 5.07) pg/mL; Z = -2.787; p = 0.005, Cohen’s d = 0.47, power = 0.71], whereas serum IL-19 levels were significantly higher in CAOS patients than the healthy controls [355.35 (278.67, 431.41) vs. 289.47 (210.78, 431.41) pg/mL; Z = -1.991; p = 0.046, Cohen’s d = 0.34, power = 0.44].

Correlation of serum IL-10 and IL-19 with clinical symptoms in CAOS patients

Spearman correlation analysis revealed that serum IL-10 levels of CAOS patients were significantly negatively correlated with PANSS negative subscores (r = -0.338, p = 0.001, Fig. 3) and positively correlated with sex (r = 0.230, p = 0.024). Serum IL-10 levels were not significantly correlated with the PANSS total score, positive symptom score, general psychopathology score, age, BMI, family history of mental disorders, duration of illness, or age of onset (all p > 0.05). Serum IL-19 levels of CAOS patients demonstrated no significant correlations with the PANSS total score, subscale scores, age, BMI, family history of mental disorders, duration of illness, or age of onset (all p > 0.05).

Correlation between serum IL-10 levels and PANSS negative subscores of CAOS patients

Furthermore, stepwise linear regression analysis was conducted with PANSS negative symptoms as the dependent variable and IL-10, age, sex, BMI, family history of mental disorders, duration of illness, and age of onset as independent variables. In the stepwise procedure, only IL-10 was retained in the final model (VIF = 1.000, tolerance = 1.000), while other variables including sex did not meet the criterion for retention (p > 0.05). The results showed that serum IL-10 levels remained significantly correlated with PANSS negative subscores (B = -0.739, beta = -0.348, t = -3.616, p < 0.001, 95% confidence interval [CI]: -1.145 to -0.333, R2 = 0.121).

Correlation of serum IL-10 and IL-19 levels with cognitive functions of CAOS patients and healthy controls

Spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant correlations of serum IL-10 and IL-19 levels with the total RBANS score or any of the subscale scores (immediate memory, visuospatial/constructional, language, attention, and delayed memory) of CAOS patients (all p > 0.05). The relationship between IL-10 and language function in CAOS patients is shown in Fig. 4A. Interestingly, in the healthy control group, serum IL-10 levels showed a significant negative correlation with language subscale scores (r = − 0.394, p = 0.012, Fig. 4B), while there was no significant correlation of serum IL-19 levels with cognitive functions (all p > 0.05).

Correlation analysis between PANSS and RBANS scores of CAOS patients

As shown in Table 2, among the CAOS patients, Pearson correlation analysis showed that the PANSS total score was significantly positively correlated with immediate memory (r = 0.342, p = 0.001), delayed memory (r = 0.453, p < 0.001), and RBANS total score (r = 0.405, p < 0.001). The PANSS positive subscale scores were significantly correlated with immediate memory (r = 0.321, p = 0.001), delayed memory (r = 0.450, p < 0.001), and RBANS total score (r = 0.386, p < 0.001). The PANSS general psychopathology subscale was significantly correlated with delayed memory (r = 0.329, p = 0.001) and RBANS total score (r = 0.324, p = 0.001). After Bonferroni correction, other previously significant correlations did not remain significant (all p > 0.00208), while no significant correlations were observed between the PANSS negative subscale and any RBANS cognitive domains or total score (all p > 0.05).

Independent variables predictive of susceptibility to CAOS

The patient and healthy control groups were combined into one sample, with CAOS status as the dependent variable. IL-10 and IL-19 levels were dichotomized into high and low groups based on median values, with the IL-10 low group coded as 1 and the IL-10 high group as 0, whereas the IL-19 high group was coded as 1 and the IL-19 low group as 0, serving as independent variables. With age, sex, education, and BMI as control variables, modified Poisson regression analysis showed that low IL-10 levels were significantly associated with CAOS (B = 0.387, Wald χ2 = 10.056, p = 0.002, relative risk [RR] = 1.472, 95% CI = 1.159–1.870). The attributable fraction for low IL-10 was 32.1%, with a population attributable fraction of 19.1%. IL-19 did not show a statistically significant association with CAOS (B = 0.217, Wald χ2 = 3.042, p = 0.081, RR = 1.243, 95% CI = 0.973–1.586).

Discussion

The main findings of this study are as follows: (1) as compared to healthy controls, CAOS patients had lower serum IL-10 levels and elevated IL-19 levels; (2) in CAOS patients, serum IL-10 levels were negatively correlated with PANSS negative symptoms; (3) serum IL-10 levels were significantly negatively correlated with language function of the healthy controls, but not in the CAOS group; (4) low IL-10 showed a significant association with CAOS, while IL-19 did not show a significant association; and (5) CAOS patients demonstrated significantly poorer performance across all cognitive domains as compared to healthy controls, with significant correlations observed between PANSS scores and RBANS performance.

Serum IL-10 levels were significantly lower in CAOS patients as compared to healthy controls. This finding differs from some previous studies, as Şimşek et al. [24] found no changes in serum IL-10 levels in 30 adolescent schizophrenia patients aged 10–17 years, Zhang et al. [25] reported no changes in serum IL-10 levels in individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis aged 14–45 years, and Halstead et al. [6] reported elevated IL-10 levels in both acute and chronic schizophrenia patients, whereas Xiu et al. [26] found that serum IL-10 levels were decreased in acute drug-naïve schizophrenia patients. These inconsistencies may reflect differences in immune regulatory functions across different patient populations. A previous study of male rats demonstrated that adolescent social adversity reduced hippocampal anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 protein levels in adulthood, while prenatal exposure to infection prevented this IL-10 reduction [24]. Decreased IL-10 levels may reflect an imbalance between anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory cytokines in CAOS patients, and this dysregulation might be associated with the etiology of schizophrenia.

This study observed elevated serum IL-19 levels in CAOS patients as compared to healthy controls. Although research on IL-19 in schizophrenia remains limited, a previous study reported potential associations of specific haplotypes of the IL-19 gene with schizophrenia. However, the validity of these associations requires further verification across different samples [7]. Nonetheless, IL-19 may be involved in the pathological process of schizophrenia through certain mechanisms, but more research is needed for confirmation. IL-19, as a member of the IL-10 family, exhibits predominantly pro-inflammatory properties in several autoimmune and inflammatory contexts, though its role may be context-dependent. IL-19 is primarily produced by macrophages and can upregulate MMP-9 expression through the ERK/NF-κB signaling pathway [25, 26]. Studies have shown that IL-19 plays a role in the pathological processes of various autoimmune diseases and post-stroke depression [27–29]. The changes to IL-19 levels observed in CAOS patients in this study provide preliminary evidence for potential roles in schizophrenia. Notably, inflammatory dysregulation has been linked to suicidal behavior [30], suggesting that the altered IL-10 and IL-19 levels observed in our CAOS patients may have broader clinical implications beyond cognitive and psychotic symptoms.

In this study, serum IL-10 levels were negatively correlated with PANSS negative symptoms in CAOS patients. The relationship between IL-10 and the severity of clinical symptoms is inconsistent in the current literature. In first-episode drug-naïve schizophrenia patients and medicated schizophrenia patients, elevated serum IL-10 was positively correlated with PANSS negative symptoms [16, 31]. However, previous studies by our group found that serum IL-10 levels were negatively significantly correlated with PANSS negative symptoms in adolescent schizophrenia patients, but not long-term medicated male chronic schizophrenia patients [14, 32]. These discrepancies might be attributed to multiple factors, including patient population heterogeneity (age, sex, illness duration), influence of antipsychotic treatment, disease stage (acute versus chronic), and sample type used for detection (serum versus plasma). Previous research has shown that IL-10 levels differ between adolescent-onset and adult-onset schizophrenia patients [33], which is consistent with the negative correlation between IL-10 and negative symptoms observed in CAOS patients in the present study, though further investigations are needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms.

The results of this study found differences in the relationship between IL-10 and language function between CAOS patients and healthy controls. As an anti-inflammatory cytokine, IL-10 may influence neurocognitive processes by regulating neuroinflammatory responses [34, 35]. A previous study found that in individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis, IL-10 was negatively correlated with verbal learning [36], while in adult schizophrenia patients, serum IL-10 levels were associated with specific cognitive domains, such as social cognition [37], although these relationships may vary across different patient populations. The negative correlation between IL-10 and language function observed in the healthy controls may reflect the regulatory role of IL-10 in language-related neural pathways during normal development, whereas the absence of this relationship in CAOS patients may suggest alterations to immune-neurocognitive interaction patterns in the disease state.

A key finding of our study is that low serum IL-10 levels are significantly associated with CAOS. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, we cannot establish whether low IL-10 precedes or follows the onset of CAOS. However, this finding supports the hypothesis that dysregulated anti-inflammatory responses may be involved in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia [38, 39]. A previous study with the use of mouse models reported that infection during pregnancy increases the risk of psychiatric disorders in adult offspring, as resiquimod-induced pregnant mice had higher IL-10 levels [40]. A gene carried by the cytomegalovirus genome encodes an IL-10 mimic that can bind to the host cognate receptor IL10R, although schizophrenia-related susceptibility genes are reportedly closely associated with the entire life cycle of the involved pathogens [41]. A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that the rs1800896 and rs1800872 polymorphisms of IL-10 confer an increased risk of schizophrenia [42]. IL-19, as a member of the IL-10 family, has recently been found to play a role in various inflammatory and autoimmune diseases [27, 29]. Although this study observed elevated IL-19 levels in CAOS patients, the multivariate analysis did not confirm a significant association after adjusting for confounders. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship and potential causal role of IL-19 in CAOS.

Our study found that CAOS patients performed significantly poorer across all cognitive domains as compared to healthy controls, a finding consistent with previous research supporting cognitive dysfunction as a core feature of schizophrenia [43–45]. We observed that CAOS patients exhibited deficits in immediate memory, visuospatial/constructional abilities, language, attention, and delayed memory, suggesting that cognitive impairment is present early in the disease and broadly affects multiple cognitive domains. Unexpectedly, we observed positive correlations between PANSS scores (except for negative symptoms) and RBANS cognitive domains, suggesting that higher symptom severity was associated with better cognitive performance in this sample. This counterintuitive finding may reflect the complex relationship between symptom severity and cognitive function in first-episode, drug-naive CAOS patients during early clinical stabilization, possibly related to heightened arousal or effort levels in more symptomatic patients during testing. This finding is not entirely consistent with Sağlam et al. [46] and Zhang et al. [13], who reported no significant correlation between cognitive deficits and the severity of psychotic symptoms. The findings of previous reports and the present study suggest that cognitive deficits may be an independent feature of the disease rather than a direct consequence of psychotic symptoms. Adolescence is a critical period for brain development, particularly for maturation of the prefrontal cortex, which is closely related to the development of executive function and higher cognitive abilities [47, 48], as indirectly demonstrated in rat models of maternal immune activation [49].

There were three main limitations to our study. First, the cross-sectional design prevents establishing temporal precedence and causality. We cannot determine whether the observed cytokine alterations precede the onset of CAOS or result from the disease process. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether IL-10 and IL-19 changes are present before symptom onset. Second, while RBANS has been used in various studies, standardized data for Chinese adolescent populations remain limited. Fourth, the positive correlations between PANSS and RBANS scores are counterintuitive and may be influenced by sample-specific factors such as assessment timing during acute stabilization and the restricted range of cognitive scores within the patient group. These findings require replication in independent samples. Finally, the relatively small size of the healthy control group may have affected the statistical power of some analyses. Future research should employ multi-center designs integrating neuroimaging and genetic data, and should also consider controlling for seasonal and circadian effects on cytokine levels, to comprehensively understand the complex relationship between inflammatory mechanisms and CAOS.

In conclusion, we found significantly decreased serum IL-10 levels and elevated IL-19 levels in CAOS patients. Low IL-10 levels showed a significant association with CAOS. IL-10 levels were negatively correlated with PANSS negative symptoms, suggesting that inflammatory regulatory mechanisms may be related to the development of negative symptoms in CAOS. Additionally, the relationship between IL-10 and language function differed between CAOS patients and healthy controls, indicating altered immune-neurocognitive interaction patterns in the disease state. The CAOS patients exhibited significant deficits across all RBANS cognitive domains, highlighting the widespread nature of cognitive impairment in these patients. These findings suggest that alterations to serum IL-10 levels may be associated with immune regulatory imbalance in CAOS patients, while the clinical significance of IL-19 elevation requires further validation.
