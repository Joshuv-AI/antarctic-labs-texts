Public health and community support for prevention of novel viral diseases: a qualitative study with LGBTQIA + people and people with blood-borne viruses
Authors: Kerryn Drysdale, Deborah Lupton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis article presents findings from the ‘Diverse Experiences and Understandings of Immunity in the Pandemic Age’ project. The aim of this research was to identify how Australians from specific communities who are perceived to be more at risk of COVID-19 and mpox experience and understand the relationship between human immunity, health, and illness in the age of existing and emerging infectious diseases and pandemics.MethodsThirty semi-structured interviews were conducted with four key sub-groups: (i) people identifying as LGBTQIA+; (ii) people with lived experience of HIV; (iii) hepatitis B (HBV); and/or (iv) hepatitis C (HCV). These sub-groups were included in the study because they have extensive experience in dealing with infectious disease prevention and management, as well as facing the social stigma and marginalisation that are often part of these experiences. Furthermore, some members of these communities are among those most at risk of contracting COVID-19 and mpox and subsequently experiencing severe or prolonged disease.FindingsThematic analysis captured key findings as related to accessibility and acceptability of public health initiatives; access to health services; public health communication and education; and connection and support. Participants’ responses highlight how successful interactions between health communication strategies, inclusive health service delivery, social supports and public health protections, together with community organisations’ services, education campaigns and other supports, can alleviate the impacts of inequalities related to infectious diseases such as COVID-19 and mpox.ConclusionsThe article discusses participants’ views on how health communication, public health, and community support initiatives have helped or hindered their efforts to keep well since the outbreak of COVID-19 and mpox. It includes recommendations for how these agencies and initiatives can better support communities of people faced with social discrimination and other inequalities in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13924-x.

Main text

Outbreaks of novel and re-emerging infectious diseases are currently causing high burdens of disease globally [1, 2]. The COVID-19 pandemic continues to affect populations around the world, with new sub-variants emerging and causing recurring waves [3]. In addition, another zoonotic disease, mpox (formerly monkeypox) has spread from regions in Africa where it was endemic, affecting new populations in the Global North [4]. The 2022 mpox outbreak mostly affected men who have sex with men. However, the 2024 outbreak involving a more transmissible clade affected other groups, such as other men, women and children [3]. As such, both COVID-19 and the two mpox outbreaks have been declared by the World Health Organization as emergencies of public health concern potentially affecting whole-of-populations [5] – and correspondingly, public health responses have widened in response beyond specific sub-populations.

These new viral diseases have had unequal impacts on different population groups [6, 7]. Existing inequalities exacerbate the effects of emerging infectious disease outbreaks on those groups who are already more vulnerable to infection and reinfection, severe disease, and lack of effective healthcare. Socioeconomic and health inequalities combine with greater exposure to infection and co-infection risks [8]. Health status, social practices and sexuality are also intertwined factors, particularly among groups who may also be currently underserved by healthcare systems and public health initiatives [8, 9]. Identifying these different experiences, impacts, causes, and effects among marginalised and ‘hidden’ or ‘hard to reach’ populations is vital for a more comprehensive account of how public health and community responses to emerging infectious diseases are currently operating, and for identifying what improvements could be made in the future.

This article presents findings from the ‘Diverse Experiences and Understandings of Immunity in the Pandemic Age’ project. The aim of this research was to identify how Australians from specific marginalised communities who are perceived to be more at risk of COVID-19 and mpox experience and understand the relationship between human immunity, health, and illness in the age of existing and emerging infectious diseases and pandemics. It captures insights from four key sub-groups of the Australian population: (i) people identifying as LGBTQIA+; (ii) people with lived experience of HIV; (iii) hepatitis B (HBV); and/or (iv) hepatitis C (HCV). These groups have historically been marginalised in countries such as Australia, sharing forms of entrenched stigmatisation and discrimination which complicates, and often impedes, their access to general healthcare. They are among those most at risk of further infection from other viruses, such the mpox virus and as the novel coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) that causes COVID-19, if they engage in practices involving close sexual and social proximity to others [10, 11].

People living with HIV, HBV and HCV are also aligned with broader LGBTQIA+ communities, including expressions of allyship with people living with blood-borne diseases, and targeted health promotion to communities of people perceived to share similar biomedical and social histories [12]. In Australia, either taken separately or together, these sub-groups are commonly spoken of as ‘communities’ of people. There are distinct but overlapping communities of people living with HBV, HCV and HIV, and these groups are represented as communities by peak organisations. These viral infections are treated as a set of conditions – as blood-borne viruses – with shared infectious characteristics, similar treatment and prevention strategies, and crossovers between priority populations. Moreover, owing to some shared sexual and drug-using practices, support of those people at higher risk of contracting these viruses often fall under the remit of LGBTQIA + organisations as well as specific policy domains within public health [13].

Previous publications from this study have explored all participants’ experiences, logics and understandings of immunity [14], with some articles specifically looking at LGBTIQA+ people (e.g., [15]) and those with blood borne viruses separately (e.g. [16]). The present article combines insights from both cohorts to focus on their shared perceptions of public health responses to COVID-19 and mpox, the role of community organisations as trusted mediators of public health messaging, and the implications for broader mitigation of some of the adverse effects of pandemic responses. It includes discussion of both general public health communication and preventive strategies and ‘counterpublic health’ approaches: that is, the incorporation of medical and public health science into community experience and embodied practice [17, 18]. Members of these sub-groups often benefit from being part of communities that have a long history of successfully engaging members in successful and inclusive risk prevention activities in response to HIV and hepatitis, assisting COVID-19 and mpox risk prevention across these overlapping cohorts [19–21]. They therefore have important insights to offer on how collective action, solidarity, and community support can offer effective ways of informing people about new and re-emerging diseases and providing healthcare services, irrespective of important distinctions between sub-populations.

Background

In Australia, the COVID-19 pandemic and the mpox outbreaks overlapped and required coordinated public health responses, especially for those individuals and social groups considered to be at higher risk of infection and transmission. The first two years of the COVID-19 pandemic (2020-21) were characterised by strong protections and policies for testing and tracing implemented by federal and state governments. These included the early closing of international borders, the introduction of quarantine, testing and tracing protocols, an early national lockdown, and online learning and working. Consequently, COVID-19 cases, hospitalisations, and deaths were very well controlled in Australia compared with most other high-income nations. There were long periods in most parts of Australia where there were no COVID-19 cases during this pandemic phase. From late 2021, however, once targets for mass COVID-19 vaccinations had been achieved, a ‘living with COVID’ approach was introduced by governments, as it was assumed that a highly immunised population would limit further spread of the novel coronavirus. Unfortunately, the Omicron variant entered at the same time, against which the first vaccine doses were less effective. Regular waves of infections from Omicron sub-variants have affected Australia ever since [22–24].

In July 2022, the World Health Organization declared the global monkeypox (renamed mpox in November 2022) outbreak as a public health emergency of international concern, due to evidence of human-to-human transmission and cases in countries that had previously never been affected by the disease [5]. By that time, 45 cases had been identified in Australia. Men who have sex with men were considered as most at risk of infection and encouraged to seek vaccination against the disease [25]. While this first outbreak was well contained after the introduction of surveillance, education, and vaccination measures, mpox has continued to cause occasional outbreaks in Australia. In August 2024, Australia recorded the second highest number of confirmed cases in the world, with several cases in women and involving heterosexual transmission [26].

There is increasing recognition of the distinct health understandings and needs of people who have been positioned as at high risk of COVID-19 infection: including those with chronic health conditions, people who are immunocompromised, and those who engage socially or sexually in close proximity to many others [27]. People living with HIV and/or gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men have been identified as a major risk group for both COVID-19 and mpox [19, 21, 28]. Likewise, people with pre-existing health conditions or who are immunocompromised due to other viral infections, such as HIV, HBV and HCV, are at higher risk of dying from COVID-19 and developing severe disease, Long COVID, and other subsequent health problems from infection [29, 30]. COVID-19 infection and reinfection can cause or worsen existing chronic health conditions, such as liver disease caused by hepatitis infection [31]. In relation to mpox, those who are already immunocompromised are at higher risk of being infected with the mpox virus and progressing to severe disease [32]. People with advanced HIV infection experience worse clinical outcomes and higher mortality if co-infected with the mpox virus [9]. Many of the prevention strategies for COVID-19, such as social distancing and vaccinations, are also relevant for mpox.

To counter these disadvantages, research from the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic and mpox outbreaks focusing on LGBTQIA+ communities found an ethos of community care and support. In Australia, gay and bisexual men were warned by community organisations to reduce casual sexual contacts early in the pandemic to avoid COVID-19. Many responded accordingly. An online survey conducted from April to July 2020 found that as the pandemic continued, some men recommenced sexual activity with casual partners, using a range of strategies they believed would reduce the risk of contracting or transmitting SARS-CoV-2 [19]. Other research found that due to existing norms about regular testing for HIV and sexually transmissible infections, gay and bisexual men were willing to engage in COVID-19 and mpox testing and contact tracing, particularly if they had strong ties to social relationships and networks with other gay men [33].

Several studies have been published identifying attitudes to COVID-19 vaccines among marginalised groups in Australia. One study found that gay and bisexual men expressed high willingness to accept the new COVID-19 vaccines once they became available [11]. In another study, members of groups who face discrimination in Australian healthcare settings (Indigenous Australians, people who inject drugs, people living with HIV, and gay and bisexual men) were interviewed [10]. Concerns about vaccine safety were expressed by members of all groups, but for the most part they were positive about protecting themselves and the community with vaccines. Participants with HIV and gay and bisexual men were more trusting of vaccines. The researchers suggest that this finding may reflect their greater experience with using pharmaceuticals, such as antiviral medications for HIV management and sexual health. A different study with groups who experience discrimination in the Australian healthcare system (Indigenous people, people who inject drugs, people living with HIV and gay and bisexual men) found that all groups considered the vaccines important for individual and community protection [34]. Not all marginalised groups have been receptive to COVID-19 vaccines, however. Research involving interviews with African migrants living in the Australian state of New South Wales found that they were sceptical about the severity of COVID-19 and about the government’s public health response, and expressed hesitancy about accepting the new COVID-19 vaccines [35]. Other mixed methods research involving Australian-born and refugee young mothers from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds showed that both groups held concerns about the safety of the vaccines and their side effects: particularly those women living with poor mental health [36].

The public health response to mpox in Australia was particularly successful due to closely working with established LGBTQIA+ community sexual health organisations and avoiding public health messaging that was stigmatizing or judgmental [37]. One Australian interview study [17] involved 13 cisgender men diagnosed with mpox and three of their close contacts. Findings demonstrated how knowledge about mpox was co-produced by public health authorities, researchers, clinicians, patients, close contacts and affected communities. This was an effective counterpublic approach, in which both expert and lay knowledges contributed to new information as it evolved. These participants were highly motivated to protect community members and loved ones from infection. A number of surveys have also shown high levels of awareness about mpox among men who have sex men and transgender people and that many were willing to be vaccinated or engage in other preventive measures to avoid infection [21, 25, 28].

Methods

A total of 30 in-depth semi-structured interviews were completed by the first author between late 2022 and mid-2023. The method of semi-structured interviews was chosen to gather rich data into how people understand immunity in the context of their biographical experiences and social group membership, especially as they related to the current and overlapping COVID-19 and mpox outbreaks (see interview guide in supplementary materials). This period in time was marked by Australians having experienced several waves of COVID-19 infections since public health orders were relaxed in late 2021, and followed the successful containment of the 2022 mpox outbreak. For this article, we focus on participants’ views on how health communication, public health, and community support initiatives have helped or hindered their efforts to keep well since these COVID-19 and mpox outbreaks. For many participants, their comments related initially to the more sustained public and community health responses to COVID-19, however, they often attributed to the same prevention experiences to mpox. Accordingly, we refer to COVID-19 specific avoidance strategies where specified by participants. Otherwise, where mpox was spoken about in the same context, we refer to novel viral diseases collectively.

We were able to draw on longstanding partnerships with the communities included in our project to ensure that the research was conducted with an ethic of care and inclusion. Ethics approval was provided by UNSW Sydney Human Ethics Review Committee and ACON Research Ethics Review Committee, with letters of support for research provided by peak representative organisations Hepatitis Australia, Hep B Community, The National Association of People with HIV Australia, and LGBTIQ+ Health Australia. We consulted with thse organisations around the design of the research and recruitment strategies, and they also helped with recruiting from their own networks. All participants provided informed consent (written or verbal) prior to the interview and were thanked for their time commitment to the study with an AUD50 e-voucher.

Participants were eligible to participate if they self-identified as belonging to one or more of the target groups, were over 18 years of age and living in Australia. Purposive sampling and screening ensured participant diversity in age, gender, country of origin and geographical location. Recruiting through representative organisations ensured that, for the most part, targeted recruitment of these specific populations could be achieved. Trusted organizations’ support for the study was visible to potential participants to alleviate any concerns they may have had in relation to the historical and ongoing stigma associated with these communities. Participants were recruited to the study by these organisations though both public facing (for example, social media) and closed (for example, email lists) advertisements. This strategy was underscored by the recognition that strong community ties and advocacy have been successful in managing past infectious diseases, and accordingly, representative organizations’ support was key to recruitment of these more ‘hidden’ participants [38]. Offering participants the option of taking part using video conferencing allowed for greater access to participants living in, especially, regional and rural locations, and in Australian states geographically distant from where the research study team resided. This method also supported COVID-19 safe participation for both interviewer and interviewee.

All participants were recruited from the four targeted sub-groups, with efforts made to include roughly equal numbers from these groups for representation and heterogeneity. All but three interviews were conducted via video conferencing software; however, only the audio files of interviews were retained for transcription. The remaining interviews were conducted face to face in local needle and syringe exchanges as this best met the accessibility preferences of some people with lived experience of HCV. The interviews ranged from 28 to 50 min in length.

Table 1 provides the sociodemographic details of the participants. They ranged in age from 20 to 68 years of age. There was inclusive gender distribution among cis and trans men and woman, and non-binary people, and inclusive sexuality distribution among heterosexual, gay/lesbian, bisexual and other identities. The proportion of participants living in regional or rural areas, born in other countries, and speaking languages other than English also make this sample diverse. Two participants identified as Aboriginal.

Analysis

All interviews were conducted by the first author and were then transcribed from audio by a professional working under a confidentiality agreement and were verified and anonymised by the authors. Aligning with what Braun and Clark [39] describe as ‘big Q’ qualitative research, our reflexive thematic analytical approach was post-positivist, recognising the situated subjective and contextual nature of the conduct of interviews and the thematic analysis. Our own positionalities as researchers also influenced the research design, data collection and analysis, as we drew on our prior expertise on and connections with each of the selected sub-groups; that is, our focus on LGBTQIA+ communities and people living with blood-borne viruses. Our method likewise did not follow a standard ‘coding protocol’ or attempts to achieve objectivity. Instead, together both authors developed an iterative approach drawing on repeated reading of the interview transcripts in which we individually conducted thematic analyses and selected illustrative quotations to support these themes. We passed our insights back and forth with each other, building a shared analysis. The findings presented in this article focus on the participants’ responses to our questions about public health, health service and community organisations’ responses to COVID-19, mpox and general health issues since the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, organised under topic thematic headings. When describing participants’ demographic details, we include age, sexuality and gender, current geographical location, country/region of birth and viral status.

Findings

Accessibility and acceptability of public health initiatives

Overall, participants responded favourably to government initiatives and responses to the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly those that were offered in the early stages (2020-21) to prevent the spread of the virus. There was broad support of mask mandates, lockdowns and particularly of vaccine mandates and rollouts, which participants also saw as relevant to subsequent mpox outbreaks:The one positive thing I think the government did and has done a really good job in [was] the vaccinations … everyone was aware of “hey, let’s get this done – it’s our first step to taking steps to being normal again, getting back to a sense of normalcy”. (Participant 2, aged 52, gay man, metropolitan, Northern America, no blood-borne viruses)

The one positive thing I think the government did and has done a really good job in [was] the vaccinations … everyone was aware of “hey, let’s get this done – it’s our first step to taking steps to being normal again, getting back to a sense of normalcy”. (Participant 2, aged 52, gay man, metropolitan, Northern America, no blood-borne viruses)

Specifically, and for most participants, lockdowns were on the whole viewed favourably as necessary to prevent the spread of infection of COVID-19. However, in this later stage of the pandemic, when lockdowns were no longer used and mpox outbreaks began to occur, participants were somewhat critical of the public health reliance on cultivating ‘personal responsibility’ for pandemic control measures, as this was seen as inadequate as a protection for more vulnerable Australians. Instead, a reliance on personal responsibility was perceived to create divisions between those who care about the health of others and want to do the ‘right thing’ compared to those who wanted a swift return to ‘normal’. Participants noted that this division exacerbated existing health inequities. They spoke of new divides between most Australians in good health versus those living with disabilities or chronic health conditions; between various states with differing public health orders, such as Victoria and New South Wales; and between those who resided in ‘fortressed’ states, such as Western Australia, compared with the rest of the nation.

Several people criticised the lack of wrap around supports, such as housing and financial aid, during periods of lockdowns. For example, Participant 7 (aged 20, trans gay person, metropolitan, Australia, no blood-borne viruses) commented that:The big thing is that lockdowns were a really great way to respond to the pandemic. The issue is that people weren’t being given the supports they needed to thrive in lockdown, right? And that’s where we see the issues with the financial aid not being there and people not being able to work.

The big thing is that lockdowns were a really great way to respond to the pandemic. The issue is that people weren’t being given the supports they needed to thrive in lockdown, right? And that’s where we see the issues with the financial aid not being there and people not being able to work.

This participant and several others expressed their view that public health orders and prevention strategies were not maintained for long enough beyond the initial emergency phase of COVID-19 and were largely abandoned at the time of mpox outbreaks. This was especially the case for those who were immunocompromised, living with chronic health conditions, or otherwise especially vulnerable to infection. Specifically in relation to COVID-19, several people felt that government may have capitulated too early, or have been too reactive, in response to negative press around mask mandates. Others stated that mask mandates were lifted too soon or were not uniformly enforced, and therefore Australia missed the opportunity to develop a mask-wearing culture as seen in other international jurisdictions. Participant 4 (aged 34, non-binary pansexual person, regional, Australia, no blood-borne viruses) noted that dropping mask mandates had meant that most people no longer felt obliged to protect themselves or others:Just because everyone doesn’t have to mask now, if people don’t feel at risk or have family who they feel could be at risk, they won’t mask. It’s one of those things where if people don’t feel like they would be personally affected in a huge way, then they are not going to do it.

Just because everyone doesn’t have to mask now, if people don’t feel at risk or have family who they feel could be at risk, they won’t mask. It’s one of those things where if people don’t feel like they would be personally affected in a huge way, then they are not going to do it.

Other people commented on the apparent loss of interest in COVID-19 protections and mitigation of other novel viral diseases in general, including mpox, from federal and state governments. Participant 9 (aged 33, queer woman, metropolitan, Australia, no blood-borne viruses) had these observations to make:I feel like that’s where we really drop the ball. Like nationally, in terms of healthcare, it’s like “we will put a Band Aid on the issue when it arises, but we are not taking preventative measures to prevent chronic health diseases and poor immunity”.

I feel like that’s where we really drop the ball. Like nationally, in terms of healthcare, it’s like “we will put a Band Aid on the issue when it arises, but we are not taking preventative measures to prevent chronic health diseases and poor immunity”.

Several participants commented on their frustration and dismay at not being able to access regular COVID-19 vaccines because they were not deemed eligible by the health system (for example, they were too young or not ‘immunocompromised enough’). Others described staying at home as much as possible to protect themselves in a world in which most other people had dropped precautions, during both the COVID-19 and the mpox outbreaks. Two people were also highly critical of state government decisions to reopen schools without ensuring strong protections, highlighting the increased risk posed to teachers and young people of contracting COVID-19 while at school. Participant 4 commented that as a parent with young children attending school, she was exposed to infection because of a lack of mitigations in that environment:[The school] knew ventilation was a good thing. They should have had like those HEPA machines in every classroom and things like that.

[The school] knew ventilation was a good thing. They should have had like those HEPA machines in every classroom and things like that.

On a similar issue, Participant 13 (aged 33, nonbinary person, metropolitan, Australia, no blood-borne viruses) mentioned that their sister, who was a schoolteacher, had just tested COVID-19 positive at the time of interview. The sister had been told by her supervisor that she had to return to work straight away if she was not feeling too ill, thereby exposing other staff members and pupils to infection: ‘she’s not impressed but she has zero options’.

Access to health services

In relation to other health concerns, participants called for more subsidies and better access to health services. They suggested making HIV antiretroviral drugs free for all people living with HIV and increased rebates for other healthcare in Medicare (the Australian government universal healthcare system). Participants noted that the expansion of mental health plan sessions, pharmacies dispensing medicines longer opening hours for vaccination, and general practice clinics as well as the expansion of number of healthcare providers offering bulk billing, would greatly improve opportunities for healthcare provision for themselves, their family members, and their communities. These issues were recognised by participants as particularly relevant to trans and gender diverse people, for people living with HIV, and for people with prior or current lived experiences of injecting drug use (and associated HCV risk).

People called for better access to healthcare professionals who they knew would be supportive of and sensitive to LGBTQIA+ patients. For instance, Participant 3 (aged 26, queer man, metropolitan, Australia, no blood-borne viruses) commented that:If I am talking about a GP or a medical practice, it’s about having that kind of visibility of acceptance that people can see your website or something and know that you are not going to be condemning or naming them or be unfamiliar with trans issues. Kind of visibly showing support for the community and stuff like that. I know that I have certainly been with friends who are like, “Do you know any good, like, gay doctors around or gay psychologists or like people who are familiar with that?” because they just don’t want to deal with the baggage.

If I am talking about a GP or a medical practice, it’s about having that kind of visibility of acceptance that people can see your website or something and know that you are not going to be condemning or naming them or be unfamiliar with trans issues. Kind of visibly showing support for the community and stuff like that. I know that I have certainly been with friends who are like, “Do you know any good, like, gay doctors around or gay psychologists or like people who are familiar with that?” because they just don’t want to deal with the baggage.

Several people living with blood-borne viral infections commented that their prior experiences of stigma and discrimination in healthcare had led them to distrust the health system. In this situation, they fell on their own resources to self-manage their illness, or sought support from community organisations:Look, I’ve got a fairly reasonable handle on my own body and what goes on medically and I handle medical stuff reasonably well. So, you know, the better I learn, the better I could self-manage. (Participant 12, aged 54, gay man, metropolitan, Australia, HIV and HCV co-infection)

Look, I’ve got a fairly reasonable handle on my own body and what goes on medically and I handle medical stuff reasonably well. So, you know, the better I learn, the better I could self-manage. (Participant 12, aged 54, gay man, metropolitan, Australia, HIV and HCV co-infection)

This was also the case for Participant 24 (aged 58, lesbian, metropolitan, Australia, HCV infection) who was an injecting drug user. She commented that she had developed a strong distrust of the healthcare system due to the discrimination to which she had been treated by health providers:So pretty much for most of my adult life, I have pretty much done my own risk assessments and largely because of being an injecting drug user. Because that always becomes the focus of anything that happens, you know, for any other health professional. They are like “Oh you are a user, so blah, blah, blah”.

Trans and gender diverse people were especially critical of delays in gender affirming care caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. There was a clear demand for more gender affirmation subsidies in healthcare, especially for those who wanted to medically affirm their gender. As Participant 10 (aged 34, non-binary queer/pansexual person, regional, Australia, no blood-borne viruses), argued, the government should ‘make top surgery part of Medicare’ but also ‘just include transpeople more in healthcare and allow them to have access to the things that would improve their overall wellbeing’.

The implementation of telehealth as part of the Medicare system in the early stages of COVID-19 was seen as a highly positive step forward for better access. However, one participant who lived in a rural location pointed to the new levels of COVID-19 infection risk they faced. Despite being able to use telehealth, they still needed trips to the pharmacy to pick up their medications, as delivery services were not available. At the same time, free access to healthcare and other supports were also considered beneficial by many participants to maintaining good their health. These supports included respiratory clinics and vaccinations as well as the expansion of allied health services, such as increased sessions under Medicare-subsidised mental health plans, and improved avenues for accessing healthcare, including services that did not require Medicare cards. The ideal model offered by Participant 19 (aged 61, gay man, regional, Australia, HIV infection) for example, was a ‘one-stop’ facility where he could receive care for several health issues simultaneously in a streamlined way to avoid having to wait to find a GP appointment in busy clinics. This bundling of healthcare needs was seen by participants to minimise exposure to COVID-19 and mpox.

Participants also recommended new or alternative healthcare measures that focused on preventive health. These suggestions included streamlining of HIV medication prescribers and sexual health, targeted healthcare access and support for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, and incentives for people from stigmatised or marginalised communities to take better care of their health, such as people who use illicit or recreational drugs. For some people living with HIV, Australia’s HIV prevention and treatment programs served as a good model for future integrated healthcare and public health responses. Some people commented that the Australian government’s Immunisation Register during the COVID-19 vaccination rollout ensured easy access to vaccination information. However, several people wished that all information on individuals’ vaccinations from birth should be as easily accessible. Often participants did not recall all the vaccinations through their life course other than an assumption that they must have had ‘all the usual childhood vaccinations’. The difficulty of recollection was also exacerbated by different ‘service app’ requirements and availability in different state jurisdictions (with different access to immunisation registers).

Acknowledging the importance of strong personal data privacy and security protections, participants called for better tools to track and record and access health history, as well as automated reminder systems for upcoming vaccinations. This suggests either that this initiative has not been well advertised to the community or is being actively shunned (see, for example [40], for a discussion of the acceptability of Australia’s My Health Record among marginalised or stigmatised communities).

Public health communication and education

While COVID-related public health orders and initiatives were generally perceived favourably, there was some criticism of public health communication from government and general health services. For example, participants noted the need for better accessibility for health-related information to ensure that information reaches its intended audiences. This lack of accessibility is also complicated by what was perceived to be a lack of consistent messaging regarding COVID-19 risk and precautions that could be taken, as one participant noted:This has kind of I think been one of the problems we have had in Australia, with our public health officials and medical experts in the media giving us various different opinions. I think it would be good if we had a consistent message nationally to say, “Here is what we need to do to protect ourselves and protect our community against COVID”. (Participant 23, aged 47, lesbian woman, metropolitan, Australia, no blood-borne viruses)

This has kind of I think been one of the problems we have had in Australia, with our public health officials and medical experts in the media giving us various different opinions. I think it would be good if we had a consistent message nationally to say, “Here is what we need to do to protect ourselves and protect our community against COVID”. (Participant 23, aged 47, lesbian woman, metropolitan, Australia, no blood-borne viruses)

This was especially the case for participants from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, who commented on the ‘muddled’ messaging around COVID-19 vaccination types and eligibility (Participant 14, aged 29 years, bisexual woman, metropolitan, Australia, no blood-borne viruses). While the provision of COVID-19 information was initially detailed and frequent, participants noted that there was an increasingly lack of availability of details such as case numbers over time, leading to the perception of declining public interest in COVID-19 safe precautions. Some participants felt that this was due to a lack of visible respect for scientists.

In response, some participants said that government and public health should ‘hand over’ complex health information and messaging to community organisations, as they are able to ‘properly tailor’ such messages. Targeted, non-stigmatising advice that builds on LGBTQIA+ strengths was seen to be a role for community organisations. Such advice extends beyond the provision of COVID-19- or mpox-related information to sharing details about good experiences with allied healthcare, such as the circulation of information on LGBTQIA+ friendly services. The idea was that health information should be ‘marketed by community’ but ‘funded by government’.

Participants noted that using community or social events to distribute health information was a key strength of community organisations’ communication strategies. Community-led campaigns resonate because ACON (Australia’s largest LGBTQ+ community health organisation) especially, uses ‘real people’ in their campaigns and depends on the role of peers to disseminate information effectively:The thing that I love about ACON campaigns is that the people in the ads are real people. So, I know one of the people in the cervical screening one that’s just gone up, and I knew one of people in the ‘Ending HIV’ one. And you see them and you’re like, “Oh, that’s my friend. I know that person. All these other people are authentic people”. (Participant 16, aged 38, gay man, metropolitan, Australia, no blood-borne viruses)

Free and broad access to annual flu vaccines was also considered beneficial to good management of HBV symptomology and general good health.

Community organisations providing support and information to people beyond the usual groups was also mentioned as something that needed attention. Connection and support

Participants noted that the improved accessibility of COVID-related supports during the emergency phase of the pandemic was also accompanied by greater social acceptability. This was especially the case where such supports were community-oriented and promoted a sense of collective responsibility for self and others’ health. Respiratory clinics, for instance, were seen as helpful beyond the healthcare that they offered; they were also viewed as a version of community support, and there was a demand for more of them. Participant 30 (aged 67, heterosexual woman, metropolitan, Oceania, HCV infection) gave the example of a respiratory clinic next to a major hospital in their city they had heard about as a good model to follow:It helps out with all the COVID – it does help when people go and they’ve got the same illness as somebody else, it does help them. It gives them confidence, that is a problem shared.

It’s a reminder that we are a subgroup, [and] I don’t think that’s good for anyone’s health. (Participant 20, aged 58, gay man, regional, Australia, HIV infection)

For people living with or affected by HCV, participants residing outside the populous capital cities in the eastern seaboard felt that the ‘small town’ syndrome, where everyone knows each other or are tightly networked, inhibited their access to targeted treatment due to lack of anonymity.

It was common for participants to emphasise that there was an integral role for community organisations to play in relation to providing support and connection as well as providing services such as information and vaccinations. In particular, participants highlighted how community organisations promote and maintain a caring ethos towards their community, evident by the numbers of people volunteering in these organisations. This extended to the support provided by community organisations, some of which kept open ‘drop in spaces’ which were perceived to be a great source of support and connection during novel viral disease outbreaks.

The perception of community support was amplified through the consistent social media presence (for example, their Instagram and Facebook accounts) that some community organisations maintained during the early phases of the pandemic. As such, AIDS Councils (which were set up in most Australian states and which over time shifted into community health organisations for all LGBIQA+ people and people living with other blood-borne viruses) were seen as serving a wider community beyond those living with HIV. While a minority of participants pointed to interpersonal dynamics in close-knit community groups, more people held concerns around a lack of current representation in organisations more generally (that is, too many cisgender gay men), alongside recognition that such organisations cannot possibly represent all experiences and identities (for example, a bisexual Aboriginal man is likely to feel a lack of representation in community-run services). Discussion

Our participants’ responses highlight how successful interactions between health communication strategies, inclusive health service delivery, social supports and public health protections, together with community organisations’ services, education campaigns and other supports, can alleviate the impacts of these inequalities. As well as offering insights from the experiences of people with HBV or HCV, our findings provide viewpoints across the subgroups from later on in the pandemic. Our participants were able to reflect on their experiences across the first three years of COVID-19 and over the 2022 mpox outbreak, a period which saw a rapidly changing epidemiological and public health policy environment.

Similar to other Australian-based studies, the participants in our study were strongly supportive of preventive measures against COVID-19 [10, 11, 19, 33, 34] and mpox [17, 21, 25, 28, 37]. Many participants commented favourably about the 2020-21 mitigations against the spread of COVID-19 implemented by governments during this early phase. Most acknowledged the benefits of protections such as mask mandates and mass vaccination programs, and most viewed lockdowns as a necessary mitigation while waiting for vaccines to be offered. While participants could see that the promises offered by governments about the effectiveness of the COVID-19 vaccines in ending the pandemic had not eventuated, they remained supportive of vaccines for the benefits they offered against severe disease. This aligns with previous Australian and international studies that demonstrate that some LGBTQIA+ and blood-borne viruses communities are more open to biomedical interventions to viral prevention and treatment [11]. They appreciated the support offered by community organisations in providing information and healthcare access for people living with or at risk from contagious diseases.

However, our participants also identified numerous disappointments and frustrations and offered opinions on where they saw room for improvement for both general medical and public health services and specific community health organisations. The criticisms voiced by participants may reflect their personal fear of transmission and vulnerability to infection, but also their frustration at the lack of more widespread cultural changes that might prevent future novel viral diseases, such as mpox. Based on their experiences of marginalisation, and for many, lack of access to high quality and inclusive healthcare, participants for the most part recognised that the prevailing emphasis on personal responsibility for good health in medicine and public health systems ignores the impacts of socioeconomic disadvantage and social stigma. They identified new divides in public health protections and healthcare that had emerged since the advent of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Several participants commented on the negative impacts of governments’ and community organisations’ withdrawal of preventive strategies, leaving vulnerable people in particular open to the risks posed by COVID-19 infections. For these people, therefore, good health was conceptualised as a social justice issue. Participants offered strong and helpful suggestions for how public health authorities and community organisations can work together to improve public health communication and services related to infectious diseases management and control as well as broader healthcare needs. Drawing on their extensive experiences of using healthcare both before and after the COVID-19 and mpox outbreaks, participants had numerous suggestions about how governments and community organisations could improve access, privacy, sensitivity, and medical record keeping for themselves and their communities, as well as more effective and inclusive public health messaging and education campaigns. They asserted that the most helpful support came from community organisations that recognise the diversity within their organisation membership and can focus on education and prevention related to the continuing risks posed by COVID-19 and mpox as well as the contagious diseases and other health risks with which they were more familiar.

Limitations

A limitation of this article stems from the impossibility of a sustained engagement with cultural and linguistic diversity within the scope of this article, even though it intersects with gender, sexuality and blood-borne virus status. Migrant and other racial and ethnic communities have different perspectives and experiences of public health communication, and more research to these aspects is needed. Another potential limitation associated with grouping analysis of blood-borne viruses (HIV, HBV, HCV and co-infections) is that while often treated as similar conditions (linked through ‘blood’) this may obscure the ways that prevention and treatment might be designed around the specific cultural meanings and biomedical opportunities available to people living with these viruses.

Conclusion

Building on previous Australian-based research, our findings highlight key ways in which members of the LGBTQIA+ community and people with lived experience of blood-borne viruses have benefited from government or community supports since the outbreak of COVID-19 and mpox, as well as identifying important improvements or gaps. As this study demonstrates, health communication and healthcare services that recognise cultural and health diversity and offer clear, regular and updated advice that is non-judgemental and inclusive are highly important. While some of our participants’ suggestions for improvements to healthcare access were not specific to COVID-19 or mpox outbreaks, they do point to the potential for wider systemic changes; in effect, participants saw the outbreaks as (missed) opportunities to critically rethink how health services could be better delivered. Finding ways for public health agencies, health services and health communication campaign managers to work more closely and productively with trusted community organisations would strengthen prevention and education initiatives for COVID-19 and other infectious disease prevention and treatment. Expanding free or low-cost healthcare for marginalised groups, including better information about and access to vaccines, would improve health and wellbeing in these communities.
