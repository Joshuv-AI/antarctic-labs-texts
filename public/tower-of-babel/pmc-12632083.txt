Readability and information quality of LLM-Generated HIV content: a methodological content evaluation
Authors: Guihua Chen, Chuan Lin, Jae Heon Kim, Fuxi Du, Zhao Luo, Yu Seob Shin, Xianxin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to draft public-facing HIV materials, but the balance between readability and information quality remains unclear. This study provides a methodological, content-level evaluation of unedited LLM outputs.MethodsFifteen high-frequency, HIV-relevant lay queries were drawn from Google Trends (using “AIDS” only as the entry term for salience) and mapped to MeSH. From March 13–20, 2025, each query was posed to ChatGPT-4, DeepSeek-R1, and Grok-3 across three conversational rounds (15 × 3 × 3 = 135 texts). Readability of raw outputs was computed using ARI, FRE, GFI, FKGL, and TSI. Information quality was rated with DISCERN by two non-specialist clinicians for all texts and by two infectious-disease specialists for a randomized subset (45 texts). Inter-rater reliability used two-way random-effects ICC; group differences used one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s post hoc, as appropriate.ResultsARI and GFI showed no between-model differences, whereas FRE, FKGL, and TSI indicated that Grok-3 required higher reading levels than ChatGPT-4 and DeepSeek-R1. In non-specialist ratings, Grok-3 scored higher on DISCERN (56.5 ± 7.7) than ChatGPT-4 (36.3 ± 5.2) and DeepSeek-R1 (36.3 ± 4.9) (both p < 0.0001). Among specialists, Grok-3 (71 [69–73]) exceeded ChatGPT-4 (43.5 [38–49], p < 0.0001) and DeepSeek-R1 (54 [49–59], p = 0.0006); ChatGPT-4 vs. DeepSeek-R1 was not significant (p = 0.2055). ICC among non-specialists was excellent for DeepSeek-R1 (0.95) and Grok-3 (0.98) but moderate for ChatGPT-4 (0.58). Among specialists, ICCs were moderate (≈ 0.59–0.72) with wide confidence intervals.ConclusionsA consistent readability–quality tension emerged: outputs rated higher for information quality tended to be harder to read, whereas more readable texts were less complete. These findings suggest that LLMs may be best suited for upstream use as drafting aids, paired with expert review for accuracy, stigma sensitivity, and audience fit, then edited to target literacy bands and localized, rather than as direct-to-patient systems. This methodological study does not assess reach, behavior change, or clinical outcomes; future work should test comprehension and actionability in target populations and explore low-bandwidth, localized deployments to determine whether content improvements translate into meaningful public-health benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11621-y.

Introduction

Global challenges and strategic shifts in HIV prevention and control

Since the identification of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) in The 1980s, The epidemic has remained a major public-health challenge. In 2023, an estimated 39.6 million people were living with HIV worldwide, with 1.3 million new infections and 630,000 AIDS-related deaths [1]. Despite important gains, the 95–95–95 targets have not been fully achieved, and transmission persists in low- and middle-income countries and among key populations [2–4]. Over time, global strategies have moved from “Zero New Infections” and “90–90–90” to today’s “95–95–95,” reflecting a broader shift toward people-centered governance that integrates biomedical tools with structural and social interventions [5–9].

The HIV epidemic is heterogeneous across regions. Some settings face generalized epidemics, whereas others experience concentrated epidemics among specific key populations. Program priorities therefore differ, for example, testing and linkage to care, pre-exposure prophylaxis, prevention of vertical transmission, or antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation and retention, and communication materials need to be adapted to local needs and delivery channels [5]. In line with contemporary public-health messaging, we center HIV as the primary construct and reserve “AIDS” for AIDS-defining illness or source-specific usage, consistent with person-centered, destigmatizing language that avoids conflating general HIV information with advanced-disease narratives [10]. The sole exception in this study is its use as a Google Trends entry term to surface lay search themes; these items were subsequently mapped to Medical Subject Headings (MeSH)/Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS)-aligned terminology. At the same time, proposals to expand digital health messaging must contend with persistent digital divides, including constraints in internet access, device affordability, digital literacy, language/health-literacy compatibility, and privacy/safety protections, which can limit equity and impact, particularly in resource-constrained settings [11–13]. Taken together, these considerations motivate a content-level evaluation of artificial intelligence (AI)-generated materials before discussing implementation pathways.

AI-Driven innovations in disease prevention

Against this backdrop, advances in artificial intelligence, especially large language models (LLMs), offer new capabilities for HIV communication workflows, from drafting and localization to rapid versioning across channels. Conversational models such as ChatGPT-4, DeepSeek-R1, and Grok-3 can produce coherent public-facing text and may improve the consistency and speed with which educational materials are generated [14–16]. Early reports suggest potential utility for disseminating HIV information, prompting testing, and supporting adherence messaging; however, the current evidence base primarily concerns content characteristics and workflow efficiency, not field-level effects [17]. Moreover, practical use in resource-limited settings would require localization, human-in-the-loop review, and low-bandwidth delivery, for example, community health-worker scripts, Short Message Service (SMS), Unstructured Supplementary Service Data (USSD), Interactive Voice Response (IVR), radio, or printed leaflets, given the divides noted above [11–13]. A focused appraisal of readability and expert-rated information quality on unedited model outputs is therefore a necessary first step, providing methodological evidence to inform upstream drafting and editorial processes without making claims about real-world effectiveness.

Research objectives and study scope

This study conducts a methodological content evaluation of LLM-generated HIV information, comparing readability and expert-rated information quality across models; we do not evaluate communication reach, behavior change, or clinical outcomes. We used zero-shot, keyword-only generation anchored to controlled HIV terminology, and analyzed unedited outputs. Readability was assessed with standard grade-level indices, and information quality was appraised by infectious-disease and non-infectious-disease experts using DISCERN, with inter-rater reliability quantified via a two-way random-effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) (absolute agreement, 95% CI).

Materials and methods

Background data on global HIV trends (UNAIDS Reports)

To provide contextual background, this study referenced epidemiological and treatment data reported by the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV (UNAIDS). The UNAIDS Global AIDS Update (2010–2023) was used to summarize long-term trends in HIV prevalence, new infections, AIDS-related mortality, and ART coverage. Regional distributions for 2023 were also reviewed to highlight disparities across geographic settings.

These data, derived from official national reports compiled by UNAIDS, serve as authoritative references and provide the global context for subsequent analyses of health information needs. Detailed figures and visualizations are presented in the Supplementary Material (Figures S1–S2).

Keyword extraction and AI-Based analysis approach

This is a methodological, content-level evaluation. Primary outcomes were expert-rated information quality and readability indices on unedited model outputs. We did not measure communication reach, access, behavioral change, or health outcomes, and we make no claims of real-world effectiveness; any potential applications are framed as implications requiring separate, context-specific implementation studies with safeguards.

To analyze global search trends related to HIV, we used the entry term “acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS)” on the Google Trends platform (https://trends.google.com/) to capture lay search queries for The period from February 12, 2010, to March 12, 2025 [18]. This choice was made because “AIDS” is widely recognized in the public domain and often used in general information-seeking contexts. For consistency with contemporary destigmatizing language, the manuscript otherwise employs the term “HIV.”

The search initially produced 24 distinct keywords ranked by relevance and normalized search volume. After excluding general terms without a clear HIV-specific orientation (e.g., “deficiency,” “immune,” or “syndrome”), a final set of 15 high-frequency, HIV-relevant queries, such as “AIDS,” “HIV,” “acquired immune system,” and “what is AIDS”, was retained for subsequent analysis. To ensure consistency with biomedical vocabulary and enhance reproducibility, all keywords were mapped against their nearest MeSH terms, with match levels categorized as full, partial, or none (see Supplementary Table S1). This mapping demonstrated a high degree of correspondence between lay search expressions and standardized medical terminology, with most queries showing full or partial alignment with MeSH terms. Google Trends was used solely as background salience rather than as a sampling frame and does not imply that queries originated from the study’s target audiences.

From March 13 to 20, 2025, these queries were systematically input into three state-of-the-art AI chatbot models (ChatGPT-4, DeepSeek-R1, and Grok-3) under identical prompt conditions. Each query was tested across three conversational rounds, following a cyclical mode in which related iterative prompts allowed assessment of logical reasoning and contextual coherence. Each of the 15 selected queries was then submitted sequentially to all three models per cycle, forming a complete input loop without inter-model interference.

To minimize potential bias from session memory or prompt dependencies, all experiments were conducted in independent sessions with no historical context. Specifically, tests were performed on the same device under identical network conditions, using new untrained accounts; after each query was answered, the chat history was cleared. This procedure does not alter model parameters but minimizes contextual leakage and ensures comparability across models. We did not use system/role instructions, few-shot exemplars, embeddings, retrieval augmentation, fine-tuning, one-hot/feature encoding, or prompt frameworks, and we did not impose length or readability targets at generation time.

This study exclusively utilized publicly accessible data and involved no patient or user privacy information; therefore, formal ethical approval was not required.

Readability and quality assessment framework for AI-Generated medical texts

To evaluate the readability and quality of AI-generated medical content, this study employed a set of established readability metrics (https://readabilityformulas.com/readability-scoring-system.php), including the Automated Readability Index (ARI), Flesch Reading Ease (FRE), Gunning Fog Index (GFI), Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), and the Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG Index, TSI). Additionally, the DISCERN instrument was applied to assess the quality of consumer health information, with particular emphasis on reliability and treatment-related content (https://www.ndph.ox.ac.uk/research/research-groups/applied-health-research-unit-ahru/discern) [19, 20]. Readability indices were computed directly on raw, unedited outputs via the cited tool; no manual preprocessing was performed.

The ARI is used to measure the complexity of language and reading difficulty, indicating the level of education required to comprehend the text [21]. For example, an ARI score of 6 corresponds to The reading level of a sixth-grade student, while a score of 16 suggests the text is appropriate for college graduates. Higher ARI scores denote increased textual complexity, suitable for highly educated readers, whereas lower scores reflect simpler language structures, making the content more accessible to the general public.

The ARI is calculated based on the number of characters, words, and sentences in a text, using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ARI=4.71\times\:\left(\frac{characters}{words}\right)+\;0.5\times\:\left(\frac{words}{sentences}\right)-21.43$$\end{document}

The FRE score, developed in 1948 by American linguist Rudolf Flesch, is one of the earliest and most widely applied tools for assessing textual readability [22]. In the fields of medicine and health communication, FRE is commonly used to evaluate the readability of scientific information, public health guidelines, and medication instructions, ensuring that such content is accessible and understandable to the general public.

FRE scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater ease of comprehension, and lower scores reflecting more complex and obscure language. Texts scoring below 30 are typically considered suitable only for professionals or readers with advanced educational backgrounds.

The FRE score is calculated based on sentence length and the number of syllables per word, using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:FRE=206.835-1.015\:\times\:\left(\frac{words}{sentences}\right)-84.6\times\:\left(\frac{syllables}{words}\right)$$\end{document}

The GFI is another widely used tool for assessing text readability. This index defines words with three or more syllables as “complex words” and evaluates reading difficulty based on sentence length and the proportion of complex words within the text [23].

Generally, a GFI score above 12 suggests that the text is relatively difficult to understand and may not be suitable for the general public, being more appropriate for audiences with higher education backgrounds. Compared with other readability metrics, the GFI is more sensitive to technical terms and long sentences, and is therefore often applied to policy documents, academic papers, or highly technical materials.

The GFI is calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:GFI=0.4\times\:\left(\frac{Total\:Words}{Total\:Sentences}+\;\text{Percentage}\:\text{of}\:\text{Complex}\:\text{Words}\right)$$\end{document}

The FKGL index was developed by The U.S. Navy in 1975, building upon the FRE score, as a tool for evaluating the readability of English texts [24]. This metric estimates the minimum U.S. school grade level required to understand a given text by analyzing the average sentence length and word complexity (measured by syllable count).

The FKGL score is presented in terms of U.S. grade levels. For instance, a score of 8.0 indicates that the text is suitable for readers with at least eighth-grade education (approximately 13–14 years old). In contrast to FRE, higher FKGL scores indicate more complex and harder-to-read texts. For public health materials, it is generally recommended that FKGL scores remain below 8 to ensure accessibility for general audiences [25].

This index is widely used across fields such as education, publishing, government communication, and medical and health information dissemination, especially for assessing the readability of public health documents, clinical guidelines, and online medical content.

The formula for calculating FKGL is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:FKGL=0.39\times\:\left(\frac{words}{sentences}\right)+\;11.8\times\:\left(\frac{syllables}{words}\right)-15.59$$\end{document}

The SMOG Index (TSI), developed by G. Harry McLaughlin in 1969, is a widely used readability assessment tool designed to estimate the years of education required to comprehend a given English text [26]. This method calculates the number of words with three or more syllables within a specific text sample to determine its reading difficulty level. A higher score indicates greater textual complexity and reduced readability.

Due to its robustness and high predictive accuracy, the SMOG index is extensively applied in the fields of health communication, public health, and education. In recent years, researchers have increasingly used SMOG to assess the readability of medical websites, patient education materials, and online health information, with the goal of ensuring that content is accessible, especially to populations with limited health literacy [27].

Studies suggest that, compared to traditional readability formulas such as FKGL and FRE, The SMOG index demonstrates greater consistency and practical relevance in evaluating publicly oriented medical texts. It is generally recommended that health communication materials aim for a SMOG score below grades 6–8 to maximize their reach and comprehension effectiveness [28].

The SMOG index is calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:TSI=1.043\times\:Sqrt(hard\:words\times\:\left(\frac{30}{sentences}\right)+\;3.1291)$$\end{document}

The DISCERN scoring system is a structured tool developed by the Department of Public Health and Primary Care at the University of Oxford for evaluating the scientific validity and practical applicability of medical information [29]. This tool comprises 16 items covering three core dimensions: reliability of information, specificity of treatment options, and overall quality. It is widely used to assess the credibility and quality of online health information.

In this study, three independent outputs were generated for each keyword–model pair. Two non-specialist experts, each with over ten years of clinical experience in urology and obstetrics/gynecology respectively, independently evaluated all outputs across the three conversational rounds using the DISCERN framework. The final score for each text was determined as the average of the two evaluators’ ratings, thereby reducing subjective bias and enhancing objectivity. This provided a comprehensive assessment of generalist perspectives on clarity, reliability, and information quality.

In contrast, to ensure feasibility while incorporating domain expertise, two independent infectious disease specialists (external to the author team) evaluated a subset of outputs. For each keyword–model pair, one output was randomly selected from the three available responses using the RAND() function in Excel, yielding 45 representative outputs (15 keywords × 3 models × 1 response). Two infectious disease specialists independently rated these outputs with the DISCERN instrument, focusing on clinical relevance and domain-specific validity.

Interrater reliability was further quantified for both expert groups using ICC (https://www.statstodo.com/IntraclassCorrelation.php), calculated separately for each AI model. This dual-layer design, combining automated readability metrics, full expert assessment by non-specialists, and representative domain-specific evaluation by infectious disease specialists, ensured a balanced and reproducible framework for evaluating AI-generated medical content.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2. To compare readability metrics across AI models, five established indices were calculated: ARI, FRE, GFI, FKGL, and TSI. Given the small sample size per group (n = 3 outputs per model, with each output representing The average score across 15 keyword-based answers), the statistical power of normality tests was limited. Therefore, nonparametric analyses were applied to ensure robustness. Specifically, group differences were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc multiple comparisons with multiplicity-adjusted p-values.

For the scores provided by non-infectious disease experts, normality of scores was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk and D’Agostino–Pearson tests. Homogeneity of variances was further evaluated by Brown–Forsythe and Bartlett’s tests, both indicating no significant variance differences (p > 0.05). Therefore, group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparisons.

For the scores provided by infectious disease experts, normality testing indicated that the Grok-3 scores deviated from normal distribution (Shapiro–Wilk, p < 0.05). Accordingly, a nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc multiple comparisons was conducted to compare the three models. This dual approach, parametric for data meeting assumptions and nonparametric for data with deviations, ensured robustness and validity of the statistical analysis.

Results

Global HIV epidemiological trends and treatment coverage (2010–2023)

According to UNAIDS surveillance reports, The Global HIV epidemic remains a substantial challenge. From 2010 to 2023, the number of people living with HIV continued to rise despite expanded access to antiretroviral therapy, and regional disparities in treatment coverage remain pronounced. Although new infections and AIDS-related deaths have declined during this period, millions of new cases are still reported annually, underscoring the urgent need for more effective communication and prevention strategies (see Supplementary Figures S1–S2).

Progress in epidemic control has thus been offset by persistent structural barriers. Limited health literacy, unequal access to reliable information, and ongoing stigma have emerged as critical bottlenecks, highlighting the importance of improving public health communication.

Against this backdrop, examining how individuals seek HIV-related information, particularly through online search behavior, offers valuable insights into public health communication needs and informational blind spots. Building on this foundation, the next section analyzes global search patterns using semantic network analysis, geospatial mapping, and temporal modeling. Furthermore, with the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, emerging tools such as AI-powered chatbots hold promise for enhancing information accessibility and improving communication quality. This study therefore evaluates the readability and reliability of AI-generated content to inform strategies for technology-enabled HIV prevention.

Geographic distribution of global search interest in the keyword “AIDS”

An analysis of Google Trends data reveals significant geographic disparities in global search interest related to the keyword “AIDS.” As illustrated in Fig. 2 (Fig. 2), countries are color-coded from light to dark blue based on search frequency, with darker shades indicating higher levels of interest in the term “AIDS.”

Fig. 2Geographic Distribution of Global Search Interest in the Keyword “AIDS”

Geographic Distribution of Global Search Interest in the Keyword “AIDS”

The top five countries with The highest search intensity are Jamaica, the Philippines, Nigeria, Myanmar, and Tanzania. Jamaica and the Philippines both scored 100 on the relative search index, indicating the highest concentration of search activity within their respective regions. These high-interest areas are primarily located in the Caribbean, Southeast Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa, regions that are predominantly low- and middle-income countries where HIV continues to pose a major public health concern. In contrast, many countries in Europe, Northern Asia, the Middle East, and parts of Latin America show lower search volumes. Some countries are represented in grey due to insufficient data, indicating minimal search activity.

Several factors contribute to the observed regional differences in search behavior:

High disease burden remains a primary driver. In countries with a high prevalence of HIV, there is a greater urgency for public awareness, prompting individuals to actively seek out information related to prevention and treatment.

Public health education and awareness campaigns have also played a significant role. In nations such as the Philippines and Jamaica, intensified media efforts and sex education initiatives in recent years have likely spurred increased online engagement and curiosity about HIV-related topics.

Internet accessibility is another key determinant. In areas with widespread internet availability, online search has become a dominant method for obtaining health information, contributing to higher keyword search frequency.

It is important to note that Google Trends provides relative rather than absolute search volumes. The reported values reflect the proportion of searches for a specific keyword within each country, normalized against all searches in that region. Therefore, a higher index does not necessarily indicate the largest number of global searches, but rather the prominence of the topic relative to other search queries within that locale.

In summary, global search patterns for the term “AIDS” show notable regional variations that likely reflect differences in local epidemiological burden, public communication strategies, and digital infrastructure. These search behavior insights may serve as potential spatial indicators of public health awareness and could provide a useful basis for designing targeted health communication and intervention strategies.

This pattern underscores the diverse and evolving nature of public health information needs.

Against this backdrop, generative AI chatbots, such as ChatGPT-4, DeepSeek-R1, and Grok-3, are emerging as significant tools for the dissemination of health-related content. The following section of this study will evaluate the performance of these AI models in providing HIV-related information, focusing on the information quality and textual readability of their outputs, to explore their potential and limitations in supporting AIDS prevention and control in the digital era.

Discussion

Interpretation of findings (content level)

Our evaluation addresses content-level performance, expert-rated information quality (DISCERN), and readability (ARI, FRE, FKGL, GFI, TSI), rather than real-world effectiveness, and reveals substantial between-model variability. Effective HIV communication must balance accuracy and completeness with comprehension and actionability across the care cascade (testing, linkage to care, ART initiation/retention, U = U counseling). High-quality but harder-to-read outputs may be suitable for clinician briefings or public materials after simplification; easier-to-read but less complete outputs can serve as drafts requiring expert augmentation (e.g., contraindications, references, referral pathways). Readability reflects processing difficulty, not comprehension per se; understanding also depends on prior knowledge, cultural context, layout/visuals, and delivery channel. Because most formulas were developed for English prose, cross-language generalizability is limited; model differences should therefore be interpreted with the intended audience and dissemination channel in mind.

Pattern-level synthesis of results: Across readability metrics, ARI and GFI showed no between-model differences, whereas FRE, FKGL, and TSI consistently indicated that Grok-3 required higher reading levels than ChatGPT-4 and DeepSeek-R1. In contrast, both clinician panels judged Grok-3 to provide higher information quality on DISCERN, with specialists differentiating models more strongly than non-infectious-disease clinicians. Inter-rater agreement (ICC) varied by panel and model, and several estimates had wide confidence intervals; these comparisons should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Taken together, the results suggest a readability-quality trade-off across models and highlight the importance of audience-specific editing and expert augmentation before dissemination.

Practical use and equity under digital divides

Given persistent digital divides (access, affordability, digital/health literacy, language fit, privacy/safety), LLMs may be best positioned upstream as authoring tools for implementers (ministries, Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs), clinics), not as direct-to-patient systems. After human review for accuracy, cultural appropriateness, and stigma sensitivity, content should be adapted to low-bandwidth or offline channels—Community Health Worker (CHW) counseling cards, SMS/USSD/IVR, radio scripts, clinic kiosks, printed leaflets. For interactive needs, menu-based flows or pre-generated scripts can deliver consistent messages without real-time model access. Relative to traditional workflows, LLMs may provide value by accelerating multilingual drafting, targeting readability bands, maintaining message consistency, and rapidly versioning as guidance evolves, potential benefits to workflow and content quality, but not proof of behavioral or clinical impact. These advantages require further empirical validation.

Limitations

This is a methodological content evaluation; we did not assess reach, access, behavior change, or health outcomes, and we make no claims of real-world effectiveness. We did not conduct a linguistic or stigma audit, so residual language bias may persist. The zero-shot, keyword-based design improves ecological validity but may increase between-model variance relative to templated prompts. Regarding topic sourcing, Google Trends was used only for background salience and mapped to MeSH/UNAIDS terminology with ambiguous or stigmatizing phrases excluded; nevertheless, intent heterogeneity and regional, language, and temporal variation mean selection and salience bias may remain. For model state and “memory,” clearing a chat resets in-session context but does not alter pretrained weights; despite fresh sessions without cross-session memory, outputs inevitably reflect pretraining exposure. Finally, the cross-language validity of readability formulas is limited; findings should guide editing and channel selection, not be over-generalized to comprehension or behavior. These limitations indicate that the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, rather than definitive evidence.

Implications and future work

To move from content quality to real-world impact, subsequent studies will (i) test comprehension and actionability in target populations using quizzes and teach-back, and (ii) conduct field A/B experiments to evaluate uptake (e.g., testing, linkage, and follow-up) alongside implementation studies of localized, privacy-preserving services delivered over low-bandwidth channels, with explicit feasibility and cost assessments.

To mitigate risks related to language and stigma, we will implement a two-track audit: (i) trained human raters using person-centered terminology, with inter-rater reliability reported, and (ii) automated screening for harmful phrasing. Revisions will be made before and after audits to estimate their impact.

Topic-selection bias will be addressed via stratified sampling across region, language, and time, as well as through community co-design of user intents. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted under alternative query sources and time windows.

Finally, we will expand the evaluation to multilingual and multicultural contexts, and to a broader range of query types beyond definitional prompts. By anchoring readability metrics to observed comprehension through teach-back, we aim to explore whether improvements in content quality and readability are associated with meaningful behavioral or clinical benefits under local constraints and safeguards.

Conclusions

This study provides a content-level appraisal of unedited LLM outputs for HIV information and shows a consistent readability–quality tension across models: texts judged higher in information quality by clinicians tended to be harder to read for general audiences, whereas more readable texts tended to be less complete. Agreement between generalist and specialist raters varied, with wide confidence intervals for some estimates, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation.

Practically, these findings suggest that LLMs may be best suited as upstream drafting aids, with expert review for accuracy, stigma sensitivity, and audience fit, rather than as direct-to-patient systems. Outputs should be edited to the target literacy level and localized for the intended audience and channel.

As a methodological evaluation, this work does not assess communication reach, behavior change, or health outcomes; readability metrics also have limited cross-language validity. Future research should test comprehension and actionability in target populations and evaluate real-world deployments to determine whether content gains translate into measurable public-health impact, while acknowledging contextual constraints and current evidentiary limitations.
