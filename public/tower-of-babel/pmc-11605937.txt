Investigation and implementation of case-based learning in the sino-foreign joint program of preventive medicine
Authors: Zhipeng Liu, Guanqiong Na, Lihua Liu, Sicong Tian, Yujuan Shan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe current objective of preventive medicine reform is to guarantee that clinical medical students possess a comprehensive understanding of preventive medicine concepts, knowledge, and skills. Previously, case-based learning (CBL) was mostly employed to reform the clinical medicine curriculum, but there was a lack of reform in preventive medicine for clinical students in Sino-foreign joint training programs. The aim of this study was to investigate the impact of case-based learning in the Sino-foreign joint program of preventive medicine.MethodsA total of 79 clinical medical students from the Sino-foreign joint program at Wenzhou Medical University were selected as participants to engage in 10 case-based learning sessions. The effectiveness of the instruction was assessed by analyzing the final exam scores and questionnaire responses, which encompassed instructor evaluation, self-assessment, and course learning effectiveness evaluation (CLEE).ResultsIn contrast to the traditional approach, which yielded a final score of 69.2, the case-based students achieved an average of 77.62. The results of instructor evaluation showed that students’ academic performance, problem-solving capabilities, research cooperation and communication, and learning attitude all improved significantly following case-based learning (P < 0.05). The self-assessment scores for all five case discussion courses exceeded 75 points, suggesting that students believed their overall competence had increased significantly. The CLEE demonstrated that CBL had a positive impact on students’ learning and was highly advantageous for students.ConclusionsIt is deserving of active promotion in medical education reform that case-based learning can enhance students’ comprehensive quality and classroom participation. Further research is required to ascertain whether it can replace traditional teaching methods.Trial registrationNot clinical trial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06372-4.Keywords: Case-based learning, Preventive medicine, Sino-foreign joint program, Education reform

Introduction

Case-based learning (CBL) is extensively employed in the instruction of medical and health-care fields [1]. On one hand, it can stimulate the subjective initiative of students, while simultaneously harnessing the guiding influence of teachers, facilitating the seamless integration of medical theory and clinical practice [2, 3]. This accelerates the transformation of factual knowledge into practical knowledge and yields the improved teaching outcomes, such as test score, competencies for clinical reasoning and decision-making [4]. The implementation of a comprehensive training system for medical professionals, encompassing both basic and clinical training, along with the development of new teaching materials, has made CBL a crucial teaching method for clinical medical students [5–8]. Preventive Medicine is an applied discipline of medicine and an important part of modern medicine. Based on the new strategy proposed at the 20th National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party, the Chinese Mainland has emphasized the significance of incorporating preventive medicine concepts and expertise into the curriculum of clinical medical students [9].

Nevertheless, due to the prevailing traditional notion of prioritizing treatment and disregarding prevention, clinical medical students tend to overlook preventive medicine courses, resulting in a decrease in the availability of such courses across different educational institutions [10]. The current focus of preventive medicine reform is to ensure that concepts, knowledge, and abilities related to preventive medicine are deeply ingrained in the minds of clinical medical students. Wenzhou Medical University’s Alberta College is the inaugural Sino-foreign cooperative educational institution in China that specializes in clinical medicine. It has received official approval from the Ministry of Education. It introduces innovation to the educational system and the training methods for staff, aiming to develop highly skilled, versatile, and globally-oriented individuals. This also entails setting higher standards for teaching preventative medicine [11, 12].

In order to achieve this objective, we draw on the experience gained from the integration of clinical medicine and basic medicine in the past. We also refer to the “Outline of the Healthy China 2030 Plan for health” for health as a guiding principle [13]. Additionally, we connected the conventional preventive medicine, specifically the “traditional five major health areas (particularly nutrition and food hygiene, environmental health, occupational health)” and “health promotion and management,” to diseases that are clinically relevant. We present case studies from five different perspectives, which include environment, diet, nutrition, lifestyle, and occupational exposure. In addition, a blended teaching approach combining online and offline methods is utilized to merge independent online study of theoretical knowledge with offline case-based learning. This approach aims to enhance the course’s overall quality, foster preventive medicine concepts, knowledge, and skills among clinical medical students, facilitate the integration of clinical and preventive medicine, and enhance the students’ ability to apply their knowledge comprehensively.

Methods

Participants

The study focused on 79 students, consisting of 38 men and 41 women, who were enrolled in the first batch of preventive medicine courses at Alberta College of Wenzhou Medical University. Among of them, 58 students were major in clinical medicine and 21 students in ophthalmology. All students were receiving joint training from both domestic and foreign institutions. Written informed consent was provided by all participants prior to enrollment in the study.

Case designing and writing

The teaching syllabus of Preventive Medicine includes the following chapters as the teaching contents of CBL: “Tobacco control”, “Rational nutrition guidance”, “Prevention and control of infectious diseases”, “Occupation-related diseases and occupational health services”, and “Public health emergencies and their emergency strategies”. By incorporating various areas of expertise, including health education, human nutrition, foodborne diseases, occupational health and occupational medicine, infectious disease epidemiology, etc., and drawing upon knowledge and skills from basic medicine, clinical medicine, humanities, and other fields, five distinct case themes were identified. Using public health hot topics as the central theme and presenting them in a format similar to movie clips, considering the class schedule of Preventive Medicine in Wenzhou Medical University, each case study was divided into two parts: the first and second halves. Case writing teams were assembled, ensuring that each team includes at least one clinician. Once the initial draft was finished, each group would mutually examine and revise one another’s work. The review team then undertook the final evaluation of the case, ultimately resulting in the formation of a comprehensive case.

Organization and implementation of CBL

This course was delivered through a hybrid teaching approach, which combined both online and offline instruction. The learning activities included 280 min of online course video self-study, 4 periods of virtual simulation experiments, 20 periods of traditional lectures, and 20 periods of case teaching. Each case involved 4 periods of study, divided into 2 sessions of 2 periods each. The case-studying model was introduced two weeks prior to the start of class. The teacher provided a comprehensive explanation of the CBL process, including the necessary preparations for students and the methods used to evaluate their performance. Students engaged in communication with the teacher to address any uncertainties or questions they may have had. The team leader and recorder were designated for each case. An online educational course group was established to enhance communication and foster discussion. The specific implementation of the CBL course was as follows:

(1) Before class: A week prior to the start of the class, the teacher provided the students with cases and accompanying teaching materials in the course learning group. Teacher also assigned the students to independently study the online course content. The group leader, a student who was elected, coordinated the other students to engage in initial discussions and prepare materials based on the case topic.

(2) During class: The group leader facilitated CBL discussions and communications, while the recorder documented the classroom content. The students conducted on-site literature searches and discussions on generative issues, and debated controversial issues. The course was facilitated by students, with the teacher providing guidance and oversight. The recorder compiled a concise overview of the topic, while teachers assessed and appraised the course discussion’s contents and the students’ performance.

(3) After class: Students filled in self-assessment forms to assess whether their abilities have improved. Upon completion of a full case study, students also filled out the course learning effectiveness evaluation (CLEE) form to reflect on the effectiveness of their learning in the CBL course. Simultaneously, homework was assigned based on the specific knowledge points and additional challenges related to the case.

Questionnaire collection and data exportation

The questionnaire was generated as a Quick Response (QR) code using the “Questionnaire Star” platform. Following the completion of class, students had the option to utilize the QR code to complete the questionnaire, under the guidance and oversight of the teacher. The “Questionnaire Star” platform automatically gathered data. After eliminating questionnaires with response times that were too short and logic that was confused in relevant questions, a total of 662 valid students’ self-evaluation forms and 408 CLEE forms were obtained in five cases.

Curriculum assessment

Curriculum evaluation included both objective and subjective judgment. Objective evaluation encompassed several methods such as online self-study, self-test scores, virtual simulation assessment, and students’ final exam grades. In this study, we focused on the students’ final exam scores. The final exam score was composed of two parts: objective questions and subjective questions. The objective questions accounted for a total of 60 points, with multiple-choice questions worth 30 points, true/false questions worth 20 points, and definition questions worth 10 points. The subjective questions made up the remaining 40 points, comprising case study questions worth 30 points and discussion questions worth 10 points. The final exam scores were classified as follows: scores ≤ 60 were considered failing, scores between 60 and 74 were considered passing, scores between 75 and 84 were considered good, and scores ≥ 85 were considered outstanding. The subjective evaluation encompassed instructor evaluation, student self-assessment, and CLEE. The instructor evaluation was conducted by the teacher during class and primarily assessed students’ participation, inquiry skills, organizational abilities, classroom communication and discussion, and teamwork. The total score amounted to 10 points, with the breakdown of points allocated as detailed in the Supplementary Note 1. Student self-assessment referred to the mean score derived from the questionnaire completed by students following a lesson. The self-assessment form assessed students’ ability in questioning, problem-solving, collaborative research, communication, and learning disposition (Supplementary Note 2). The CLEE form primarily measures students’ proficiency in self-directed learning, analytical thinking, effective communication, enthusiasm for learning, and application of information (Supplementary Note 3). Students assigned varying scores (0–4 points) based on their self-perception, with higher scores indicating a more favorable overall evaluation of the CBL course. Both the student self-assessment form and the course learning effectiveness evaluation form were scored out of 100 points.

Statistic analysis

SPSS 26.0 software was used for data analysis. The enumeration data are represented as [n (%)], and the χ2 test was utilized. The measurement data are presented as (‾X ± s), and the t-test was employed to compare groups, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

Students’ final exam scores

As shown in Table 1, the final exam scores indicated that the average score for all students was 77.62 points. The objective questions had an average score of 43.70 points, while the subjective questions had an average score of 33.92 points. The scores of women were marginally higher than those of men (P = 0.088). In addition, the number of students who received a ‘good’ grade was the highest, with 15 students achieving an ‘outstanding’ grade, and only three students failing the final exam.

Instructor evaluation

The instructor evaluation form assessed the classroom performance of each student. By evaluating five cases, it was determined that the average score of students in the initial case was 7.846 points. As the CBL courses progress, students’ scores gradually rose, and the average score of students in the final case was 8.541 points (Fig. 1A). It is noteworthy that starting with the fourth case class, there was a deceleration in the rate of improvement of students’ results. Additional examination of the findings for each dimension revealed that, in comparison to the initial case study, the students’ scores in each dimension exhibited substantial improvement with statistical significance (Fig. 1B, P < 0.05). Most students received an evaluation score of above 7.5, with a very low percentage scoring below 6 (Fig. 1C). Compared to the first case, a higher proportion of students received high scores (≥ 7.5 points) in the subsequent cases.

Students’ self-assessment

The student self-assessment allowed students to judge their own progress in developing their abilities through CBL course learning. A higher self-assessment score suggested that students felt they had made greater progress. The first case study resulted in a notable increase in the students’ average self-assessment score, reaching an impressive 83.37 points (Fig. 2A). This indicated that students believed their overall capabilities had significantly improved as a result of the case study. And four case studies had self-assessment scores above 83 points, except for the final case which scored 77.45 points (Fig. 2A, P < 0.05). Subsequent analysis revealed a decrease in the scores of all four dimensions of competence (pose questions, solve problem, cooperation and communication, learning attitude), with the cooperation and communication factor experiencing the most significant decline (Fig. 2B). In addition, the majority of the students achieved scores above 85 points, being the biggest proportion. The second largest group consisted of students who self-rated their scores below 60 points, with relatively few students falling within the 60–74 and 75–84 score ranges (Fig. 2C).

Course learning effectiveness evaluation

The CLEE form was used to assess students’ perceptions, behaviors, or feelings that arose during the CBL course learning process, reflecting the course’s effectiveness. It encompassed various aspects such as professional interest (PI), independent learning (IL), critical thinking (CT), information acquisition (IA), and communication ability (CA). Upon analysis, it was evident that the average score for students’ course learning effectiveness evaluation exceeded 80 points (Fig. 3A), with over half scoring above 85 points (Fig. 3C). It was worth noting that students’ PI significantly increased as the case teaching was implemented (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B). These indicated that students were generally satisfied with the overall evaluation of the CBL course.

Discussion

In this study, the students engaged in online self-study by watching micro-lesson videos during the teaching process. They also analyzed vivid cases, searched for information in books and guides, and consulted relevant literature by academic search engines such as PubMed, WanFang Data, CNKI. Through this process, they identified problems, engaged in group discussions and communication, and ultimately solved these problems. This approach stimulated students’ learning initiative and gradually shifted their focus from simply memorizing knowledge to understanding, analyzing, and applying knowledge. As a result, students achieved stronger academic performance and develop higher levels of post competency.

Under the strong advocacy of the Healthy China initiative, China’s healthcare system is placing growing emphasis on health promotion, prioritizing preventive measures, and highlighting the integration of clinical and preventive approaches [14]. However, the traditional approach to teaching preventative medicine is centered around the teacher, with classroom instruction being the primary method. In this model, students’ engagement is minimal, resulting in low levels of initiative, limited comprehension, and inadequate mastery of knowledge [15]. Hence, it is imperative to implement a curriculum reform in preventive medicine for undergraduate students pursuing clinical medicine. CBL, oriented around students, enables students to constructively analyze and discuss situations, identify difficulties within them, and engage in active thinking and problem-solving [16]. In this study, through the process of categorizing the attributes of preventive medicine knowledge and organizing the structure of case-based knowledge context, the instructors integrated the original scattered, fractured, and mixed knowledge points into a comprehensive network system of interconnected knowledge. In addition, we established specific educational goals and facilitated students’ engagement in case studies and discussions that aligned with the actual teaching process. This approach encouraged independent thinking and judgment among students, ultimately leading to the formulation of conclusions. Consequently, this enhanced the overall quality of teaching and the effectiveness of students’ learning. The reform has also been well-received by students.

The objective evaluation results indicated that the students achieved a final exam score of 77 points, with a remarkably high passing rate of 96.2%. Since the focus of this study was the initial class of Sino-foreign joint training students in Alberta, it was not possible to directly compare the results with earlier results. However, when compared to students who used the traditional teaching method during the same time period, the average final exam score of other medical students (n = 1716) in Preventive Medicine was 69.2 points. The average final exam score of Sino-foreign joint training students was still 7.8 points higher, suggesting that the CBL method had a more positive impact. Furthermore, the mean score for objective questions was 43.7 out of 60, with a scoring rate of 72.8%. This suggested that implementing the CBL method did not diminish students’ understanding of theoretical knowledge. The mean score for the subjective question was 33.92 out of 40, with a scoring percentage of 84.8%. This suggested that the students possessed greater practical application skills.

The subjective evaluation results indicated a progressive enhancement in students’ classroom performance as a result of the implementation of CBL courses, and all students expressed a belief that their personal abilities had improved, including the ability to identify problems, solve problems, collaborate in research and effectively communicate. Furthermore, the students’ overall appraisal of the CBL mode was greater, exceeding 80 points. The majority of students believed they had made substantial progress in their ability to learn independently, communicate well, think critically, develop a strong interest in learning, and utilize information effectively. These indicated that CBL teaching enhanced students’ enthusiasm and proficiency in all aspects, surpassing the effectiveness of traditional teaching methods and elevating the overall quality of instruction.

However, this study had several limitations. First, we did not compare the final scores with those of previous students. Although we compared the results with other medical students who graduated in the same year, the foundational skills and abilities of students in other majors may differ. Second, each group was assigned its own instructor. Despite the implementation of standardized training for all teachers, variations in instructor evaluation may still have exist. Furthermore, our assessment was exclusively focused on students who have received a Sino-foreign joint education at our institution. It was important to note that the outcomes of our evaluation might vary for students from different schools or pursuing different majors.

Conclusion

This course employs case teaching to help medical students acquire fundamental theoretical knowledge in preventive medicine and to firmly establish the principles of “prevention first” and “integration of medical and preventive medicine”. CBL effectively enhances students’ learning enthusiasm, teamwork, interpersonal communication, and knowledge application abilities. Furthermore, it successfully cultivates students’ divergent thinking and lateral thinking skills. CBL has enhanced the capacity to effectively address and manage public health emergencies. It has also improved the post competency of students, enabling them to become proficient medical decision-makers and service managers. Further research is required to ascertain whether it has the potential to supplant conventional instructional approaches.
