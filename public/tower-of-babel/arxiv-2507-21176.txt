Tell Me You’re Biased Without Telling Me You’re Biased! – Toward Revealing Implicit Biases In Medical LLMs
Farzana Islam Adiba, Rahmatollah Beheshti

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) that are used in medical applications are known to show biased and unfair patterns. Prior to adopting these in clinical decision-making applications, it is crucial to identify these bias patterns to enable effective mitigation of their impact. In this study, we present a novel framework combining knowledge graphs (KGs) with auxiliary LLMs to systematically reveal complex bias patterns in medical LLMs. Specifically, the proposed approach integrates adversarial perturbation techniques to identify subtle bias patterns. The approach adopts a customized multi-hop characterization of KGs to enhance the systematic evaluation of arbitrary LLMs. Through a series of comprehensive experiments (on three datasets, six LLMs, and five bias types), we show that our proposed framework has noticeably greater ability and scalability to reveal complex biased patterns of LLMs compared to other baselines.



1
Introduction

Addressing biased and unfair patterns in ever-increasing applications of AI tools has remained a challenging endeavor. In particular, with the rapid growth of large language model (LLM) applications, the concerns regarding the (social) biases they may have in their operations are also growing. In healthcare applications, sustaining the clinical significance that eventually can improve patient outcomes is crucial. LLMs are already implemented in clinical decision-making
(
Hager et al. 2024
;
Thirunavukarasu et al. 2023
;
Piya and Beheshti 2025
)
, and there is a huge excitement about their potential to inform medical practice. Applying LLMs for downstream medical tasks requires reliability and integrity for addressing health-oriented concerns in a contextually correct and clinically relevant manner. Biased behaviors of the LLMs used in medical applications (hereafter, Med LLMs) can negatively impact patient health equity and fairness.

A growing body of work has recently demonstrated the risks of biased outcomes when using LLMs across various medical tasks and domains
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Schmidgall et al. 2024
)
. For example, a study represented intrinsic and extrinsic bias in LLMs considering the association between factors such as Hispanic female or Black male among specific demographic groups
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
;
Adiba et al. 2025
)
.
Many different types of biases in Med LLMs have been considered (e.g., data-centric bias, inference bias, model-parametric bias), and similarly, different sources of biases (e.g., data, model design, or inference procedure) have been studied heavily in the literature
(
Goldfarb-Tarrant et al. 2020
)
.

Prior to mitigating such complex bias patterns, developing rigorous evaluation frameworks is an essential prerequisite. A sizable number of studies related to evaluating bias and fairness patterns in Med LLMs exist
(
Raj et al. 2024
;
Poulain et al. 2024b
;
Poulain et al. 2024a
)
. The vast majority of such studies, however, focus on running empirical analyses aiming to reveal existing bias patterns. These studies have investigated Med LLMs across different clinical domains
(
Yang et al. 2024a
)
, types of LLMs, and sensitive attributes
(
Rawat et al. 2024
)
. There are also a few studies that tried to present general-purpose and foundational methods to find bias patterns in Med LLMs
(
Pan et al. 2024
)
. However, rigorous and systematic frameworks that can investigate the nuances in such bias patterns at a large scale and reveal hidden pitfalls in the application of Med LLMs are missing.

Evaluating LLMs is challenging and an active area of research
(
Zack et al. 2024
;
Bedi et al. 2024
)
. For scenarios where LLM-based applications generate free-text output, with no single right response, evals are more challenging. For example, if an LLM is tasked to generate a ‘discharge summary’ based on a patient’s records, there are a multitude of possible good (and bad) responses. While human judgment is generally acceptable and expected, it becomes infeasible at scale, particularly when iteratively tuning LLMs or applying them in sensitive medical settings. As a result, the emerging standard for automated evaluation involves prompting a strong LLM, referred to as a Judge LLM, to score the outputs of a Target LLM using a structured rubric or scoring criteria
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
. However, such ‘LLMs as judge’
(
Li et al. 2024a
;
Fayyaz et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
approaches often rely on traditional prompt designs and may fail to capture subtle or context-dependent biases, especially those that are implicit (i.e., not explicitly stated) or intersectional (i.e., arising from the combination of multiple attributes such as age, gender, and location). Most existing frameworks either evaluate superficial fairness metrics or focus on isolated attributes. Whereas our study targets deep, multi-attribute bias interactions, offering a more nuanced and clinically relevant evaluation of MedLLMs.

To fill the above gap, we present a comprehensive framework to assess social biases in Med LLMs in this study. Our framework uses several (auxiliary) LLM and knowledge graph (KG) components to generate customized questions that identify biases in Med LLMs. Additionally, it presents a specialized process for requesting answers from the Target LLM to maximize revealing hidden biases. In particular, our framework consists of two main parts. In the first part, a KG is generated using relevant seed questions or text. The KG is then used to extract information from an unstructured dataset containing clinical questions and patient information. To analyze the complex sociodemographic bias patterns, the extracted information is used to derive ‘perturbed questions’ by an ‘Attacker LLM’. In the second part, the perturbed questions are used as prompts to assess the ‘Target LLM’ following in-context few-shot learning, where prompts are designed to respond through three customized stages of KG-based multi-hop reasoning to investigate the implicit relation of multiple bias attributes to generate the answers.

Specifically, the major contributions are as follows:

•

We propose an end-to-end LLM and KG-based reasoning framework to evaluate the complex bias patterns in Med LLMs.

•

We build a set of perturbed questions combining multiple biased scenarios from an input knowledge base that are extracted from the clinical context to generate the implicit relation of the biases and check their validity for the clinical decisions.

•

We demonstrate that perturbing entities, combined with multi-hop reasoning, reveal more hidden biased behaviors compared to various baselines, indicating the potential of our method.

•

We evaluated the robustness and applicability of our bias evaluation framework for implicit bias detection through both ‘judge LLMs’ and human judgment.

2
Related Work

2.1
Adversarial Attacking for Bias Analysis

Approaches based on adversarial attacks for bias evaluation are among the most common ones for evaluating LLMs. Among these types of approaches, a common style involves injecting harmful or biased context or features by attacking with prompting that illustrates the impact of this altered information. Some studies have implemented this type of method to introduce bias in different categories, such as demographics or used medication types
(
Han et al. 2024
;
Poulain et al. 2023
)
. For instance, to generate gender discriminative sentences, an Attacker LLM is utilized by
(
Kumar et al. 2024
)
and
(
Peng et al. 2024
)
to create gender inequity in the sentences intentionally. While adversarial attacks expose model biases, some studies use a different but closely related strategy, creating controlled perturbations rather than direct attacks. For instance, DiversityMedQA
(
Rawat et al. 2024
)
systematically varies features such as ethnicity and gender to generate diverse medical question-answering scenarios. In another study,
(
Fayyaz et al. 2024
)
presents a method to generate medical vignettes (patient scenarios) by utilizing the external knowledge graph (e.g., PubMed) to provide bias-sensitive scenarios for fairness evaluation.

2.2
KG for Bias Evaluation

KGs offer a powerful tool for bias evaluation, providing a structured representation of entities and their associated relationships. Unlike black-box LLM models, KGs can ensure clarity by extracting explicit relationships among the contextual information to efficiently identify bias in different domains, including healthcare. One recent work, SDOH-KG
(
Shang et al. 2024
)
, shows the impact of social determinants of health (SDOH) on health outcomes using the re-weighting techniques of the nodes of the knowledge graph, implementing the MIMIC-SBDH dataset. StereoKG is a framework to identify such stereotypical biases, including those related to religion or nationality
(
Deshpande et al. 2022
)
. These KG methods are largely focused on link-prediction approaches that measure the distance between biased features. However, such procedures often consider the single-attribute biased associations and overlook the effect of multi-attribute biases.

2.3
LLM-augmented KG to Enhance Reasoning

Among growing ways KGs are being integrated with LLMs
(
Pan et al. 2024
)
, such integrations have been explored recently to enhance reasoning in medical question answering (MQA)
(
Deshpande et al. 2022
)
. KGs can offer complementary capabilities related to explicit knowledge base representation to LLMs’ contextual reasoning capabilities. Several recent research works have applied this type of KG and LLM augmentation to enhance domain-specific QA generation. For example, a study on Alzheimer’s disease-related QA implemented LLM for dynamic refinement for KG generation
(
Li et al. 2024b
)
. In another study, the idea of ‘structural knowledge prompting’ was introduced to enhance LLMs’ reasoning in granularity levels to improve the performance in knowledge-intensive tasks in granular stages
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
. A prompt-based approach is also presented to recursively generate bias-related entity relationships using a KG for mapping the implicit stereotypical biases by
(
Salinas et al. 2023
)
.

Some studies have explored the use of KG-based prompt-attacking frameworks. KG-based Prompt Attack (KGPA) framework is such a work that can generate prompts from KG triplets (subject, predicate, object), by introducing adversarial modifications to challenge a target LLM’s understanding
(
Pei et al. 2024
)
.
In another work, BiasKG
(
Luo et al. 2024
)
applied an opposite approach to that of KGPA, where LLM prompts are used to generate biased KG and evaluate the social stereotypical features from the Bias benchmark dataset BBQ. We adopt both of these latter studies in our framework.
Existing work on the LLM augmented KG has been designed on knowledge retrieval
(
Yang et al. 2024b
)
, link prediction
(
Shu et al. 2024
)
, or univariate bias assessment
(
Kumar et al. 2025
)
, which often underestimate the interconnected and complex nature of bias patterns.

3
Method

We propose an LLM-augmented KG framework for evaluating biases in Med LLMs by systematically generating perturbed clinical questions and analyzing their impact on answer generation using multi-hop reasoning. Our framework consists of two main components. The first component (described in §
3.1
) creates perturbed questions from a customized KG. Using retrieved structured information from the KG, an Attacker LLM generates perturbed questions by systematically modifying specific attributes (e.g., age or comorbidities) while keeping other information constant. The second component (described in §
3.2
) generates answers using a customized multi-hop reasoning
(
Saleh et al. 2024
)
. The three-stage multi-hop reasoning process is implemented to aggregate patient information and infer external clinical knowledge while reasoning for MQA.

Figure
1
presents the overview of our proposed framework. The first stage of our framework adopts and extends the design presented in the KG-based Prompt Attack (KGPA) framework, for generating prompts from KG triplets
(
Pei et al. 2024
)
; and the second stage (loosely) follows the BiasKG
(
Luo et al. 2024
)
framework to generate KGs from prompts.

3.1
Perturbed Question Generation from KG

Our framework first constructs a KG by using seed clinical text or QA to retrieve the targeted attributes and their relationship to other clinical concepts from unstructured clinical questions. This step aims to identify the connections between various patient-related factors, such as sociodemographic attributes, medical symptoms, geographical information, and family history, which are vital in clinical decision-making. The KG is represented as triplets in the format of
(h,r,t)
, where
h
,
r
, and
t
refer to the head entity, the relation between the entities, and the tail entity, respectively. The KG aims to form a directed graph consisting of a set of entities (e.g., patients, symptoms, diseases) and corresponding relationships (e.g., lives_in, has_symptoms). For instance, if the input text describes a patient experiencing fatigue and residing in Nairobi, candidate triplets such as:

{{(patient,has\_symptoms,fatigue)}}
or

{{(patient,lives\_in,urban\_area)}}

can be formed from the input.

Extracting KG elements

The relationships between the entities from the context of the input text can be extracted using a natural language processing tool (such as the SpaCy library
(
Honnibal et al. 2020
)
in our implementation). In this process, patient attributes such as symptoms, locations, and travel history are identified. To enhance the efficiency of these processes, the pre-trained SpaCy library is customized with a rule-based approach to retrieve concise clinical entities. We systematically varied the rule-based filtering patterns used for attribute extraction to analyze how different regular expressions affected downstream outputs, including the number of extracted relations and the resulting perturbed questions. The rule-based phrase matching approach can retrieve more important information compared to the extraction method without rule-based matching. More details are provided in Appendix
A
and Table
5
.

The rule-based approach can be characterized as a function
F(T,A)
, where
T
is the context provided as input data, and
A
is a defined set of targeted attributes,

A={{\{a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{n}\}}}
. The function outputs the entity set
\varepsilon={\{e_{1},e_{2},...,e_{n}\}}
, where each
e_{i}\in A
.
We apply filtering rules using regular expressions and phrase-matching techniques to ensure only the relevant entities are selected. If
\varepsilon^{\prime}\subseteq\varepsilon
is the filtered entity set, then
\varepsilon^{\prime}
can be defined as:

\varepsilon^{\prime}={\{e\in\varepsilon\mid R(e)>\theta\}},

(1)

where
R(e)
is the score based on the semantic importance (in this context,
R(e)
extracts entities
e
based on the matched patterns), and
\theta
is the value for filtering the irrelevant context based on the predefined relevance threshold. This set of rules filters out less important information or that which has no meaning.

As an example scenario, consider a pattern for extracting age-related information that targets phrases such as ‘X years old,’ where X is a numerical value typically ranging from infancy to senior age. Similarly, other factors, such as gender and location, can be adjusted by providing various information to create diverse questions.

Sentence Generation from KG

The knowledge base extracted from the KG consists of patient contexts that are used to generate descriptive sentences using a Generator LLM. This LLM aggregates the available information, such as symptoms, demographic information, and other relevant history, in the generation. The generated sentences are structured as a controlled perturbation system by keeping some attributes unchanged. Each generated question Q can be represented as:

Q=f(A).

Perturbed Question Generation

Another LLM, the Attacker LLM, is promoted using a few-shot strategy to generate
n
perturbed questions by systematically modifying the identified attributes. Let
Q
be the original question generated from the KG’s knowledge base by the Generator LLM, including the attribute set
A
. The perturbed function
P
modifies a subset of attributes:

\begin{split}P(Q)=f(A^{\prime}),A^{\prime}={{\{{a_{1}}^{\prime},{a_{2}}^{\prime},...{a^{\prime}_{n}}\}}},\\
a_{i}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}a_{i},\text{if }a_{i}\text{ is not perturbed}\\
\tilde{a}_{i},\text{if }a_{i}\text{ is perturbed}\end{cases}\end{split}

(2)

where
{a_{i}}^{\prime}
s are the attributes that are perturbed (e.g., age and gender), and
\tilde{a}_{i}
presents the structure of the controlled perturbation where the perturbed version of attribute
a_{i}
is modified by partially altering single or more attributes at a time.

This approach (following a process presented in prior work
(
Dwivedi-Yu et al. 2024
)
) is used to generate a set questions
\{Q_{1},Q_{2},...,Q_{n}\}
from the original question
Q
. Figure
6
in Appendix
A
shows an example of the perturbation process using two attributes, age and gender, to create four sets of questions from a single main question.

3.2
Answer Generation with multi-hop Reasoning

The perturbed questions are used in the next stage to generate the answers using multi-hop reasoning.
We propose a three-step multi-hop reasoning process with in-context chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting of the Target LLM. The three steps include a) triplet generation from the perturbed questions, b) expanding the triplets based on Target LLM’s internal knowledge base, and c) generating the final response by reasoning over the generated relationships from the previous step, by limiting the possible recommendations. This three-step process aims to elicit biases that could remain hidden when using common prompting strategies.

Triplet Generation

Similar to the way question generation was initiated (described in the previous stage), the first step of answer generation initially creates structured entities and relationships using the question’s context. This information is extracted following a similar NLP-based KG-triplets representation discussed earlier, transforming the available information into a graph format, as shown in Figure
1
. Example triplets could be
(patient\rightarrow lives\_in\rightarrow location)
and
(patient\rightarrow has\_symptoms\rightarrow symptoms)
, providing the contextual baseline for further reasoning.
The structure for the relation based on patient information can be defined as the directed graph
G_{1}=(E_{1},R_{1})
. This initial step of the reasoning explicitly represents only the information provided in the questions, converting the information into triplets.

Triplet Expansion

The second step uses the initial triples from the previous step and
links the information with the Target LLM’s knowledge source that is not explicitly presented, such as disease ontologies or demographic associations with the given context. This step aims to determine how the Target LLM associates present entities in the context with other related entities, following the LLM’s internal knowledge. The ‘multi-hop’ designation in the process refers to expanding the extracted triplets (showing one-hop relationships) into newer relationships (hence, multi-hops) using Target LLM’s prior knowledge.

From the example provided in Figure
1
, the following three-step reasoning process can be explained. A 66-year-old male patient who presents with fever and skin rash lives in Johannesburg and recently visited Thanda Safari. In the first step of triplet generation, structured triplets from the context are extracted:
(Patient\rightarrow has\_symptom\rightarrow fever)
,
(Patient\rightarrow visited\rightarrow Thanda-Safari)
,
(Patient\rightarrow lives\_in\rightarrow Johannesburg)
, and so on. Triplet expansion is the second stage, in which the model expands upon these using contextual words and clinical knowledge. For example, it infers that ‘Thanda Safari’ is a ‘rural area’ and links this to broader epidemiological contexts, such as ‘environment and vector-borne exposures.’ The information that ‘Thanda Safari is a rural area’ was not given in the context, but based on LLM’s own reasoning, it extended the triplets from the previous steps.
Similarly, if a patient resides in or has recently visited a malaria-endemic region, the location is linked to specific risk categories, which in turn include potential diseases. Such relationships can be structured as
(location\rightarrow linked\_to\rightarrow risks)
and
(risk\_category\rightarrow includes\rightarrow potential\_diseases)
. This process can be shown by applying
G_{1}
to the next second reasoning step, creating a new graph
G_{2}
as:

G_{2}=f_{2}(G_{1})=(E_{2},R_{2})
.

Generating the Response

The third or the final step directs the reasoning to infer the answers by analyzing the supporting information in the previous steps. To illustrate, understanding potential diseases that are linked to the patient information can help map the identified symptoms to their potential causes. With the help of the previous step, this step maps the possible responses by limiting the probable options based on the reasoning from the previous steps.

From the same example in the framework Figure
1
, after the second step (triplet expansion), Target LLM maps these contextual links to possible clinical conditions and narrows down the possible options, such as ‘tick bite fever,’ ‘malaria,’ or ‘schistosomiasis.’ The reasoning here is represented as
(symptoms\rightarrow related\_to\rightarrow potential\_diseases)
, which directed the patient context to diagnostic inference. This way, the entire reasoning phase can be represented as:

G=f_{3}(f_{2}(f_{1}(G_{1})))
.

We can prompt a separate Judge LLM to evaluate the performance of Target LLM based on characterized metrics, where we check for the consistency of the multi-hop reasoning and how the method is actually performing to understand the overall biased response of the model. This part can be implemented using any ‘LLM as a judge’ design, such as generative pre-training score (GPTScore)
(
Fu et al. 2023
)
, which we use in our experiments. Using a Judge LLM enables scalability in calculating the quality of the LLM-generated output, which can be customized with in-context or few-shot prompting techniques.

4
Experiments

We designed our experiments centered around five broad research questions (RQs) that aim to assess the overall performance of the proposed method. We first present the setup used for experiments.

4.1
Model Setup

We implement each of the LLM instances in our pipeline using various LLM types to demonstrate the versatility of our proposed framework across diverse instantiations. In particular, in our experiments, we implement each of the LLMs as described below:

•

Generator LLM: GPT-4o
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)

–

Setup: GPT-4o is used to convert the KG triplets into a patient context based on the available extracted information.

–

Prompt design: The GPT Model was prompted to extract the available fields for each patient based on the patient ID.

•

Attacker LLM: ChatGPT-4o
(
OpenAI 2022
)

–

Setup: The LLM model was prompted to inject a perturbation by altering various combinations of attributes.

–

Prompt design: For each patient entry with available symptoms, we created four variations of clinical questions by adding different combinations of age, gender, and location. The age was randomly sampled from four distinct ranges: 1–10, 20–35, 40–60, and 65–80. Gender was alternated between ‘male’ and ‘female’. The question format used is:

A patient [age]-year-old [gender] having several [symptoms],
lives in [location].

What might be the causes of the symptoms?

•

Target LLM: GPT-4o, GPT-3.5-turbo
(
Ye et al. 2023
)
, Mistral-7B
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
, and LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct
(
Meta 2024
)

–

Setup: The LLMs were asked to perform the multi-hop reasoning with the perturbed questions

–

Prompt design: The instructions provided to perform a three-step multi-hop reasoning to answer clinical questions by extracting KG-triplets in the format
(start_node -> edge -> end_node)
from the contexts. The steps are as follows:

1.

Extract relationships from patients’ context

2.

Connect the contextual information with the risk factors

3.

Infer the possible causes linked to symptoms

4.

Summarize the reasoning outcome in a concise paragraph.

•

Judge LLM: Mistral-7B, LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct
(
Grattafiori et al. 2024
)
, GPT-4o
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
, and GPT-4.1
(
OpenAI 2025
)
.

Judge LLM was used for two evaluations: 1) Perturbed question’s quality evaluation and 2) Bias evaluation.

Perturbed question’s quality evaluation

–

Setup:
The prompts were created to rate perturbed questions based on three criteria: factual consistency, clinical relevance, and coherence.

–

Prompt design: For each evaluation criterion, we have designed the prompts to follow instructions individually to rate them on the logical validity, clinical facts, and finally, the semantic styles and quality of each question.

Bias evaluation

–

Setup:
The prompts were formatted to evaluate the severity of the bias from the QA.

–

Prompt design: The LLM models were instructed to calculate the score of the bias based on the association of factors such as age, gender, and demographics with the diseases, where we considered both the one-to-one association and the combination of the biases (e.g., age+gender or age+gender+location).

A baseline temperature and token size were set to 0.5 and 400, respectively. We used CoT in-context learning with few-shot for the prompts. The detailed prompt design for each LLM is provided in the Appendix
B
.

4.2
Seed Datasets

To feed our pipeline with controlled clinical text, we used three open-source datasets, including EquityMedQA
(
Pfohl et al. 2024
)
, DiversityMedQA
(
Rawat et al. 2024
)
, and Nurse Bias (a set of clinical vignettes by
Zack et al. 2024
). We note that our pipeline works with any type of medical free-form text; however, the above benchmarking datasets could support a more standardized assessment of our method. The datasets are chosen from recent MQA studies, and can serve as direct baselines to our proposed framework and also demonstrate our framework’s ability to serve as a scalable method on top of existing datasets/benchmarks.

The first two datasets lack grounded responses. They are organized by patients’ symptoms, history, and other demographic factors, where these attributes play crucial roles in our framework for evaluating biases from the association of these attributes. Specifically,
EquityMedQA
comprises seven subsets of adversarial questions representing the health equity-related harms generated with LLMs. We utilize the TRINDS (Tropical and Infectious Diseases) subset, which focuses on tropical diseases worldwide. Each question has context about the patient’s disease, location, symptoms, age, and travel history. Similarly,
DiversityMedQA
includes various sets of questions comprising the perturbed questions from MedQA
(
Jin et al. 2021
)
, where the questions are perturbed based on attributes like gender or ethnicity.

The third dataset, the Nurse Bias vignette dataset by
Zack et al. 2024
, contains ground truth. It was originally presented to assess the ability of GPT-4 for diagnosis using clinical vignettes with altered race or gender of outpatient cases. This dataset is a collection of various disease scenarios, including dyspnoea, abdominal pain, chest pain, and pharyngitis.

4.3
Results

The first two RQs (RQ1 and RQ2) aim to assess whether the pipeline does what it is expected to do, across various steps. RQ3 captures the main aspect, studying the effectiveness of our pipeline in revealing biases in Target LLMs. RQ4 runs a human evaluation of the generations. Finally, RQ5 implements an ablation analysis.

4.3.1
RQ1: Are the perturbed questions generated by the ‘Generator LLM’ clinically valid and factual?

To evaluate the quality of the perturbed questions (generated at the end of the first stage of our pipeline), we considered three evaluation criteria, including factual consistency, clinical relevance, and coherence. Here, factual consistency measures whether the perturbed questions contain reliable information that is not supposed to be misleading. The other validity type is clinical relevance, which assesses whether these questions are clinically plausible and free from misleading information. Coherence, on the other hand, evaluates the grammatical structure and semantic fluency. For measuring these criteria on the perturbed questions generated with the LLM, we conducted a structured quality assessment using
GPT-4.1
,
GPT-4o
,
LLaMA-3
, and
Mistral-7B
as judge LLMs. Each perturbed question was compared against its original question from the input datasets and was rated along three listed criteria. The judge LLMs are instructed to rate from 1 (poor quality) to 5 (best quality). Table
1
highlights the validity of the perturbed questions for each dataset on these three criteria.

4.3.2
RQ2: Are the generated answers using the pipeline remaining valid?

To study whether the generated answers by our pipeline, especially following the multi-hop process, remain valid (meaningful and relevant), we compare the generated answers against the ground truth. We only use the third dataset (Nurse Bias, the one with ground truth) for this part.

We compared the generated responses by the Target LLM using our multi-hop reasoning framework, which were based on a total of 84 perturbed questions, with the original grounded diagnoses from the clinical vignettes by
(
Zack et al. 2024
)
. Table
2
presents the BERTScore-based semantic similarity (F1, Precision, Recall) between the LLM-generated outputs and the ground truth across three key demographic configurations: age+gender+location, age+gender, and location. This analysis was performed for multiple Target LLMs, including
GPT-3.5-turbo
,
GPT-4o-mini
,
LLaMA-3.1-8B
, and
Mistral-7B
. Based on the observed values, the values remain close to the ground truth.

Across all models, we observe a trend in which the semantic similarity to the original diagnosis decreases as more attributes are simultaneously perturbed. In other words, outputs generated from questions with fewer demographic changes (e.g., age-gender perturbation) tend to stay more aligned with the original clinical intent, while full intersectional perturbations (e.g., age+gender+location) lead to answers that deviate more significantly. For instance, BERT Score F1 for
GPT-3.5-Turbo
decreases from 0.4829 (age-gender) and 0.4785 (location) to 0.4523 for age-gender-location perturbation.

The method that reveals higher biases is considered superior. In radar plots, this translates to the method with the largest spanning areas.

The radar plots depict the bias scores comparison among different sets of evaluation methods across demographic dimensions. Inside each radar plot, the area covered by the three lines of the same color should be compared to each other. For this evaluation, various Judge LLMs, including
GPT-4o

To conduct this assessment, 20 random QAs were selected. We experimented with three configurations: 1) original, unmodified questions from the dataset, 2) perturbed questions created using an Attacker LLM with answers without multi-hop reasoning, and 3) perturbed questions evaluated with our multi-hop reasoning framework.

This pattern can be observed by noting the bigger covered area by our method, dominating the areas covered by other methods. This demonstrates the greater capability of our method in revealing biased patterns in various Target LLMs (and measured by various Judge LLMs).

RQ4: How do humans evaluate the performance of our method in revealing biases?

To further assess our framework, we conducted a comprehensive survey comparing our multi-hop reasoning-generated answers with baselines (without multi-hop reasoning). To participate in this research study, individuals had to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) familiar with the usage of generative AI ; (2) are currently studying at post-graduate levels. In each survey, five different clinical scenarios are selected (
S1
to
S5
), along with their corresponding diagnoses (DDx), adopted from the Nurse Bias dataset. Based on the above criteria, a total of 15 participants were recruited for our survey.

For each scenario, we included four sets (two pairs) of QAs, where two QAs were generated by our framework, and the other two were from the baselines. Given the ground truth, participants were asked to choose the pair that revealed more biases. Additional details about the experiment (including a link to the survey) are presented in Appendix
B.2.1
.

RQ5: What is the impact of different perturbation types in the pipeline?

In a separate ablation study, we used the Nurse Bias dataset comprising clinical vignettes with ground truth diagnoses. We isolated the effect of individual and combined attribute perturbations on the measured bias. Specifically, we analyzed bias scores resulting from perturbing a single attribute (only location), two attributes (age + gender), or all three attributes simultaneously (age + gender + location). This allowed us to observe whether the degree of perturbation complexity correlated with the strength of bias signals. Our goal was to examine whether increasing the complexity of perturbation (i.e., moving from single to intersectional attributes) correlates with stronger or more diverse bias responses. Figure
5
visualizes the bias scores across five demographic groupings (age, gender, location, and their combinations) under three types of perturbation: age + gender + location, age + gender, and location only, evaluated using two different target LLMs (
GPT-4o
and
LLaMA3.1-8B
) with
GPT-4o
as the judge.

Figure 5
:
Bias evaluation across different perturbation combinations with two different Target-Judge settings.

Across both model configurations, one can observe a consistent pattern: perturbing all three attributes simultaneously (age, gender, and location) results in the highest bias scores across most demographic dimensions. This effect is particularly visible in intersectional groupings (e.g., age + gender + location), suggesting that compound demographic shifts amplify the LLM’s sensitivity to social context and exacerbate disparities in generated outputs. For instance, in both figures, the full intersectional perturbation (brown dashed line) covers most of the areas of the radar plot, indicating stronger bias exposure.

Discussion

In this work, we introduced a new framework comprised of auxiliary (agentic) LLMs and KGs to enhance clinical reasoning for investigating complex bias patterns. Building the perturbed questions leverages the potential scope to trigger biases in different scenarios. Additionally, the implementation of the three-stage multi-hop reasoning process facilitates context-aware answer generation, using structured patient data, clinical knowledge, and inference mechanisms to derive more interpretable conclusions and reveal more nuanced biases. The approach ensures that clinical responses are not directly retrieved from static datasets but are dynamically generated based on structured relationships and reasoning over multiple hops.

At its core, our framework adopts the popular LLM adversarial attacking paradigm for LLM evaluation
(
Li et al. 2024a
;
Fayyaz et al. 2024
)
by perturbing the context for LLMs. Perturbing contexts (a red teaming method) is the most common approach to assess biases by modifying the original information and comparing the modified version with the original to understand the impact of the changes, which also reflects the association of the attributes in various versions of the contexts. This method enables systematic bias identification by observing variations in model-generated responses when key factors such as demographics, socioeconomic status, and prior history of disease are altered
(
Rawat et al. 2024
;
Gupta et al. 2024
)
. Context perturbation in our work is implemented using KG-based structures.

Serving as efficient information extractors, KGs can retrieve desired information alongside the associative relations between the contexts. However, they lack the reasoning capabilities necessary to interpret implicit contextual nuances. On the other hand, LLMs are capable of understanding complex relationships but are prone to hallucinations and inconsistencies due to their black-box nature. Integrating KGs with (auxiliary) LLMs allows leveraging the structured and domain-specific knowledge representation of KGs while enhancing contextual reasoning by LLMs
(
Pan et al. 2024
)
. Such integration can effectively extract unstructured relationships among entities.

Our experiments demonstrate that the proposed framework consistently achieves higher bias detection scores across sociodemographic features such as age, gender, location, and their combinations when compared to baseline models. Particularly, our framework was able to reveal greater biased patterns evaluating both closed and open-source LLMs, further indicating that multi-hop models offer more balanced and widespread bias exposure across QA, in contrast to the models without multi-hop reasoning.

Limitations

While our proposed framework showed promising performance in detecting the implicit intersectionalities of biases, it has several limitations. The inference in the final step is dependent on the initial KG development. It can influence the overall bias evaluation process with different accuracy in relationship extraction, possibly leading to unreliable clinical answers. Our experiments mainly considered age, gender, and demographic location perturbations. Other types of biases arise from patient factors. Integrating bias mitigation strategies alongside the identification of bias patterns represents a significant direction for future research.
