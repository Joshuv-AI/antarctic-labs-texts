Development and validation of a predictive scoring model for post-ESD coagulation syndrome in elderly with colorectal LSTs
Authors: Qian Zhang, Fengcheng Zang, Bin He, Yunfu Feng, Zhibing Wang, Xiaodan Xu, Jian Chen, Luojie Liu, Wenwen Diao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) electrocoagulation syndrome (PEECS) is a known complication in colorectal laterally spreading tumors (LSTs), but its risk factors in elderly patients remain unclear. This study aimed to develop and validate a risk-stratification scoring system for PEECS in elderly ESD patients.MethodsA multicenter retrospective study (2020–2025) enrolled 506 elderly patients with colorectal LSTs undergoing ESD, randomly allocated to training (TC, n = 354) and validation (VC, n = 152) cohorts (7:3). Synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) was used in the TC to identify risk factors and construct a predictive model, which was validated in the VC.ResultsThe incidence of post-ESD PEECS was 8.1% (41 cases). After applying the SMOTE, multivariate analysis identified sex, lesion with fibrosis, and intraoperative bleeding as independent risk factors. The scoring system assigned: 1 point for female sex, 3 points for lesion with fibrosis, and 2 points for intraoperative bleeding. In the VC, the model demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.921, with a specificity of 0.949 and an accuracy of 0.908. Following risk stratification, the low-risk group (0–4 points) showed a PEECS incidence of 10.1% in the TC and 5.1% in the VC, while the high-risk group (5–6 points) exhibited 94.5% in the TC and 53.3% in the VC.ConclusionIn elderly patients with colorectal LSTs undergoing ESD, female sex, fibrotic lesions, and intraoperative bleeding were identified as independent predictors of PEECS. The proposed scoring system demonstrated good discriminatory ability in both cohorts and may be useful for risk stratification in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00384-026-05141-7.

Introduction

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has become a widely accepted minimally invasive technique for treating colorectal laterally spreading tumors (LSTs), particularly in elderly patients who are poor candidates for surgery [1, 2]. While ESD offers advantages such as en bloc resection and accurate histopathological evaluation, it is associated with several complications, including post-ESD electrocoagulation syndrome (PEECS) [3]. PEECS is characterized by localized abdominal pain, fever, and inflammatory signs without evident perforation [4, 5]. The reported incidence of PECS following colorectal ESD varies from 1.2% to 10.8% in different studies, with elderly patients potentially being at higher risk due to age-related physiological changes and comorbidities [6–8].

Several risk factors for PEECS have been identified, including large lesion size (40 mm), prolonged procedure time (60 min), and location in the right colon [3, 9, 10]. However, most existing studies have focused on general populations, and specific risk assessment tools for elderly patients with colorectal LSTs remain lacking. This is particularly concerning as the elderly population represents a growing proportion of patients undergoing colorectal ESD procedures worldwide [11]. Current preventive strategies for PEECS include meticulous technique, careful coagulation of exposed vessels, and novel approaches like line-assisted complete clip closure (LACC) for large mucosal defects [12, 13]. Despite these measures, PEECS continues to occur, highlighting the need for better risk stratification tools.

The development of predictive models for PEECS has gained increasing attention. Several scoring systems have been proposed for various ESD-related complications [14–16], but none specifically address PEECS risk in elderly patients with colorectal LSTs. Given the unique physiological characteristics and higher complication risks in this population, a dedicated predictive model could significantly improve clinical decision-making and postoperative management. This study aims to develop and validate a comprehensive predictive scoring model for PEECS specifically tailored for elderly patients undergoing colorectal ESD for LSTs. By incorporating both established and novel risk factors, we hope to provide clinicians with a practical tool for risk stratification and personalized patient management.

Material and methods

Patients

A retrospective analysis was conducted on clinicopathological data from elderly patients (aged ≥ 60 years) who underwent ESD for colorectal LSTs at five medical institutions (Suzhou BenQ Medical Center, Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University, Kunshan First People's Hospital, Zhangjiagang First People's Hospital, and Nanjing First Hospital) between January 2020 and June 2025. The inclusion criteria were lesions treated with ESD. Exclusion criteria included lesions managed with concurrent surgical intervention for multiplicity, those complicated by intra- or postoperative perforation, and cases with incomplete clinical data. The cohort was randomly divided into a training cohort (TC) and a validation cohort (VC) in a 7:3 ratio. Given the low incidence of post-ESD electrocoagulation syndrome (PEECS), the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was utilized in the TC to balance the class distribution of PEECS outcomes. The study protocol, outlined in Fig. 1, was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Endoscopic procedure and equipments

The treatment for colorectal LSTs in this study was ESD, a technique whose detailed protocol has been described elsewhere [17]. Figure 2 provides a visual representation of the ESD process for a lesion in the transverse colon. Prior to the intervention, all patients were placed under general anesthesia via endotracheal intubation. Patient positioning began in the left lateral decubitus position and was dynamically altered to supine or right lateral decubitus to facilitate the operation. The endoscopic setup involved a single-channel endoscope (GIF-Q260J, Olympus or EG-550, Sonoscape) with a distal cap, powered by an ERBE VIO 200D generator. Surgeons utilized electrosurgical knives such as the Kunpeng knife or the Dual Knife, supported by auxiliary tools like metallic clips and a CO₂ insufflator. To ensure patient safety, standard preoperative blood work was performed, and any anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications were withheld for one week. The procedures were conducted by highly experienced endoscopists, each possessing a record of more than 100 colorectal ESDs. To ensure consistency in the intraoperative assessment of key findings such as submucosal fibrosis, all participating endoscopists adhered to a shared predefined definition prior to the commencement of data collection. Postoperative management and follow-up

Hemostatic clips were used to manage the post-ESD mucosal defect in all patients. Complete closure (defined as clip placement reducing the defect to < 5 mm or approximating the entire margin) was achieved in 78.3% (396/506) of cases, while partial clipping to reduce exposed submucosal area was performed in the remaining 21.7% (110/506) where complete closure was not feasible due to large defect size or unfavorable anatomy. After the tumor is removed, the handling of the specimen depends on the resection type: en bloc lesions are pinned to a foam board, while piecemeal lesions are placed directly into 10% formalin for preservation. All specimens are then prepared for histological assessment via paraffin embedding and sectioning. Patient care after the procedure involves fasting, measures to prevent bleeding, and fluid support. The use of antibiotics is not standard; they are only initiated if a perforation is suspected during the operation or if the patient runs a fever afterwards. Close monitoring for complications like bleeding, perforation, or infection is essential, with treatments applied as necessary. Provided no issues arise, patients can start a liquid diet 24 h after the procedure. A surveillance colonoscopy was scheduled for all patients between 6 months and 1 year post-procedure to evaluate the healing status of the resection site.

Data collection

The variables collected in this investigation covered demographic and clinical characteristics, including sex, body mass index (BMI), major comorbidities, and a history of anticoagulant/antiplatelet medication use. Tumor-related parameters included location, size, depth of invasion, morphology, and histology. Procedural aspects recorded were procedure time, the non-lifting sign, fibrosis degree, intraoperative bleeding, success of en bloc resection, and antibiotic administration. Postoperative outcomes monitored were postoperative bleeding, fasting duration, hospital stay, and PEECS occurrence. All intraoperative findings, including the presence of submucosal fibrosis, were assessed by the operating endoscopist using a shared definition across centers and were recorded prospectively in a standardized manner on the data collection form immediately after the procedure to minimize recall bias and inter-operator variability.

Definitions

PEECS was diagnosed in patients exhibiting concurrent upper abdominal pain and localized tenderness, accompanied by either fever (37.5 C) or systemic inflammatory response (white blood cell count ≥ 10,000 cells/μL or C-reactive protein ≥ 0.5 mg/dL), with no radiographic or endoscopic evidence of perforation [14, 18]. Tumor size and invasion depth were determined primarily from postoperative pathological examination. Complete en bloc resection refers to the removal of the lesion as a single, unfragmented specimen [19]. Laterally spreading tumors were classified into granular (LST-G) and non-granular (LST-NG) types. LST-G lesions present with an aggregated granular or nodular surface resembling a "cobblestone" or "cauliflower" appearance, whereas LST-NG lesions have a smooth, flat surface that may appear subtly depressed, flat, or slightly elevated [20]. Intraoperative findings, including the non-lifting sign and submucosal fibrosis, were recorded based on direct observation during ESD. Submucosal fibrosis was defined based on intraoperative endoscopic findings during submucosal injection and dissection. It was characterized by inadequate or non-lifting of the mucosa after submucosal injection, accompanied by the visualization of web-like, white dense fibrous tissue strands within the submucosal layer. The assessment of fibrosis was performed in real-time by the operating endoscopist using a binary classification (presence or absence), based on previously established endoscopic criteria [21]. Intraoperative bleeding was defined as hemorrhage occurring during ESD that could not be adequately controlled using the endoscopic knife alone and therefore required the use of hemostatic forceps for hemostasis [22]. Minor oozing or bleeding easily controlled by the endoscopic knife with coagulation current was not classified as intraoperative bleeding. Delayed bleeding was identified by clinical signs of hematochezia or melena requiring endoscopic hemostasis within one week post-procedure [23]. Postoperative fever was defined as a maximum body temperature of ≥ 37.5 C occurring within three days after ESD. Finally, total procedure time was calculated from the initiation of submucosal injection to the complete resection of the lesion.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are presented as counts (percentages) and were compared with the Chi-square test. For continuous variables that were not normally distributed, we report median values with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and employed the Mann–Whitney U test for group comparisons. To address class imbalance in the training cohort, the SMOTE was applied to equilibrate the sample sizes between the PEECS and non-PEECS groups. Potential risk factors for PEECS were first screened through univariate analysis. Variables yielding a P-value < 0.05 were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors. A scoring system was then constructed based on the regression weights (β coefficients) from the multivariate model. The predictor with the smallest absolute β coefficient served as the reference and was assigned 1 point. Scores for other variables were calculated by dividing their respective β coefficients by this reference value and rounding to the nearest integer. The model's discriminative ability was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), while its calibration was assessed with a calibration plot. Furthermore, we computed key performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), for a comprehensive evaluation. Throughout the analysis, statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P-value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.0).

Comparison between PEECS group and non-PEECS group

Patients were classified into PEECS and non-PEECS groups based on the development of PEECS following ESD. Within the TC, the PEECS group demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of female patients compared to the non-PEECS group (76.9% vs. 48.2%, P = 0.005). Additionally, the prevalence of larger tumors (≥ 40 mm), the presence of fibrosis, and the incidence of intraoperative bleeding were all markedly higher in the PEECS group (69.2% vs. 36.6%, P = 0.001; 80.8% vs. 24.4%, P < 0.001; 73.1% vs. 25.0%, P < 0.001, respectively), as detailed in Table 3. Comparisons between the two groups in the validation cohort are summarized in Supplementary Table 1, which revealed statistically significant differences in patient sex, the presence of fibrosis, and intraoperative bleeding (P < 0.05 for all). Discussion

This multicenter retrospective study developed and validated a simple, clinically applicable risk-scoring system to predict PEECS specifically in elderly patients (≥ 60 years) with colorectal LSTs. Our analysis of 506 patients identified female sex, the presence of submucosal fibrosis, and intraoperative bleeding as independent predictive factors. Based on these, we constructed a scoring model with an excellent discriminatory capacity, achieving an area under the curve of 0.921 in the external validation cohort. This model enables the stratification of patients into distinct risk categories, which can inform tailored postoperative monitoring and potentially preemptive management strategies for this vulnerable, growing patient population.

Fibrosis has been consistently identified as a significant risk factor for PEECS in this study, aligning with previous research that highlights its role in increasing procedural complexity and thermal injury during ESD. The presence of fibrosis likely exacerbates electrocoagulation-induced tissue damage due to impaired submucosal dissection, leading to deeper thermal penetration and localized inflammation, which are key mechanisms underlying PEECS [24, 25]. This is supported by studies showing that fibrotic lesions prolong procedure time and reduce en bloc resection rates, further contributing to post-ESD complications [24]. However, our findings diverge from some earlier reports in two aspects. First, while prior studies primarily focused on fibrosis as a predictor of technical difficulty (e.g., perforation or incomplete resection) [24], this study specifically establishes its direct association with PEECS in elderly colorectal LST patients, a population underrepresented in earlier literature. Second, unlike some esophageal or gastric PEECS studies that emphasized tumor size or muscle layer exposure as dominant risk factors [26, 27], our model highlights fibrosis as a standalone predictor, suggesting site-specific pathophysiology. Notably, the proposed scoring system quantifies fibrosis’s contribution (3 points), underscoring its clinical weight compared to other variables like intraoperative bleeding. These differences may stem from the unique characteristics of colorectal LSTs in elderly patients, where fibrosis is more prevalent due to chronic mucosal injury or prior interventions. Future studies should explore whether fibrosis severity (e.g., mild vs. severe) further stratifies PEECS risk, as preliminary data suggest graded associations. Overall, our results reinforce fibrosis’s critical role in PEECS pathogenesis while contextualizing its relevance within a geriatric population.

The identification of female sex as an independent risk factor for PEECS in elderly colorectal LST patients aligns with prior studies but also introduces nuanced insights. Previous research on superficial colorectal lesions similarly reported female sex as a significant predictor (OR 3.94, P < 0.001), suggesting a consistent sex-based vulnerability across different colorectal ESD cohorts [3]. This consistency may reflect shared biological mechanisms, such as sex-specific differences in inflammatory responses or mucosal healing, as hinted by studies highlighting sex-dimorphic pathways in other gastrointestinal pathologies [28]. However, our study uniquely focuses on the elderly population, where hormonal changes (e.g., postmenopausal estrogen decline) could further exacerbate these differences, a dimension less explored in earlier PEECS literature [29]. Notably, while prior nomograms for PEECS in colorectal lesions incorporated female sex alongside age > 50 years and muscle layer injury [3], our scoring system specifically emphasizes its predictive weight (1 point) in elderly patients, contrasting with broader age-group analyses. This refinement suggests that sex disparities in PEECS risk may be more pronounced in older adults, possibly due to cumulative exposure to fibrotic changes or altered pain perception, as implied by studies linking female sex to prolonged procedure-related complications [30]. Additionally, our model’s high specificity (0.949) in validation underscores the clinical relevance of sex stratification, whereas earlier works primarily focused on composite risk factors without age-specific validation [3, 27].

Intraoperative bleeding was identified as a significant independent predictor of PEECS in elderly patients with colorectal LSTs, contributing 2 points to the predictive scoring model. This aligns with prior studies suggesting that intraoperative bleeding may exacerbate local tissue injury and inflammatory responses, leading to PEECS [29]. The thermal energy applied during hemostasis could induce deeper tissue damage, triggering a systemic inflammatory reaction characterized by fever, leukocytosis, and localized pain—hallmarks of PEECS [29, 31]. Notably, our findings corroborate earlier reports linking procedural complexity (e.g., prolonged ESD duration or extensive resection) to PEECS risk, as bleeding often necessitates additional coagulation, amplifying thermal injury [4, 14]. However, our study diverges from some previous literature in its specific focus on elderly colorectal LSTs. While prior research on gastric or esophageal ESD highlighted factors like muscle layer exposure or lesion size [4, 26], our model uniquely emphasizes intraoperative bleeding as a modifiable risk factor in this population. This distinction may reflect age-related mucosal fragility or altered healing responses in the elderly. Additionally, unlike studies attributing PEECS primarily to antithrombotic use [32, 33], our analysis identified bleeding itself—rather than its predisposing factors—as a direct contributor, suggesting that intraprocedural hemostatic management may mitigate PEECS risk independently of anticoagulant status. Future studies should explore whether proactive bleeding control strategies (e.g., preemptive coagulation or advanced hemostatic techniques) could reduce PEECS incidence in high-risk cohorts.

This study carries substantial clinical significance by providing the first dedicated predictive scoring model for PEECS specifically in elderly patients with colorectal LSTs. The developed three-factor scoring system—incorporating female sex, fibrotic lesions, and intraoperative bleeding—offers a simple, rapid, and evidence-based tool for risk stratification prior to or immediately following ESD. Its high specificity (0.949) and accuracy (0.908) in the validation cohort enable reliable identification of high-risk individuals, who exhibited a PEECS incidence exceeding 50%. In practice, this model can guide tailored postoperative management. Patients stratified into the high-risk category may benefit from enhanced monitoring and closer postoperative observation, allowing for earlier detection of PEECS should it occur. This risk-stratified approach can guide clinical attention and resource allocation, such as more frequent vital sign checks or earlier diagnostic evaluation in high-risk individuals. Conversely, low-risk patients might be considered for accelerated recovery pathways, including earlier diet advancement or shorter hospital stays, thereby optimizing healthcare resource utilization. Importantly, the management of established PEECS follows standardized clinical protocols regardless of risk category; the primary value of this scoring system lies in risk communication, patient counseling, and informing the intensity of postoperative monitoring rather than altering treatment once PEECS develops.

Despite its strengths, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. Firstly, its retrospective and multicenter design, while providing a robust sample size, inherently carries the risk of selection bias and unmeasured confounding variables. Prospective validation in a dedicated cohort is essential to confirm the generalizability and real-world performance of the scoring system. Secondly, while SMOTE was employed to address class imbalance, this synthetic oversampling technique may not fully replicate the complexities of real-world data distribution, potentially affecting model generalizability. Thirdly, the external validation cohort, although independent, was drawn from similar geographic and clinical settings within China, limiting the model's applicability to other ethnic or healthcare populations without further validation. Fourthly, although all procedures were performed by experienced endoscopists (each with > 100 colorectal ESDs) using standardized definitions and prospective data recording to minimize inter-operator variability, individual endoscopist skill was not directly quantified or included as a covariate in the analysis. Given that technical proficiency in managing submucosal fibrosis and intraoperative bleeding is likely to influence the risk of PEECS, the absence of operator-level adjustment represents a potential source of residual confounding. Future prospective studies should consider incorporating objective metrics of operator experience or technique (e.g., case volume, procedure time-adjusted difficulty, or skill scores) to better isolate the impact of lesion-related factors from operator-dependent effects. Lastly, the study did not investigate dynamic or modifiable perioperative factors, such as specific electrosurgical generator settings, detailed hemostatic techniques, or postoperative pharmacological interventions, which could influence PEECS risk and offer avenues for prevention. Future prospective, multinational studies incorporating objective biomarkers and standardized technical parameters are needed to refine and validate this predictive tool.

In conclusion, this multicenter study developed and validated a simple scoring system for predicting PEECS in elderly patients undergoing ESD for colorectal LSTs. The model, which incorporates female sex, fibrotic lesions, and intraoperative bleeding, demonstrated strong discriminatory ability and good calibration in both the training and validation cohorts. This tool may aid clinicians in identifying individuals at elevated risk for this complication, thereby supporting more informed, risk-stratified postoperative management. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate its clinical utility and to explore whether its application can lead to improved patient outcomes in routine practice.
