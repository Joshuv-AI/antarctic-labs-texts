Randomized Algorithms for Low-Rank Matrix and Tensor Decompositions
Katherine J. Pearce and Per-Gunnar Martinsson

Abstract
This paper surveys randomized algorithms in numerical linear algebra for low-rank decompositions of matrices and tensors. The survey begins with a review of classical matrix algorithms that can be accelerated by randomized dimension reduction, such as the singular value decomposition (SVD) or interpolative (ID) and CUR decompositions. Recent advances in randomized dimensionality reduction are discussed, including new methods of fast matrix sketching and sampling techniques. Randomized dimension reduction maps are incorporated into classical matrix algorithms for fast low-rank matrix approximations. The extension of randomized matrix algorithms to tensors is then explored for several low-rank tensor decompositions in the CP and Tucker formats, including the higher-order SVD, ID, and CUR decomposition.

Introduction

Numerical linear algebra (NLA) is a cornerstone of applied mathematics, scientific computing, and data science.
Throughout its long history, NLA algorithmic development has been driven by two competing imperatives, accuracy and efficiency, with the extant challenge to strike the right balance between them.

Randomization has always played a role in NLA, for instance in sometimes
providing a good starting point for a Krylov iteration.
Another interesting early result was a proposal to randomly precondition
linear systems to avoid the need for pivoting, which increases practical
speed by reducing data movement
162
.
However, randomized methods formed a small niche within the mainstream
literature; one may speculate that concerns about reproducibility played
a role, and perhaps also concerns that randomized methods could not be
executed in a high accuracy mode, since that is a characteristic of
Monte Carlo methods based on the central limit theorem.
(For additional discussion of the role of randomization in NLA, we
refer to
111
;
133
;
140
.)
This survey is concerned with a set of techniques that we refer to
as randomized numerical linear algebra (RNLA) that emerged in the
early 2000s, and have in the years since attracted significant interest.

Why the R in RNLA?
The rise in popularity of randomized algorithms is due in large part to the commensurate progress made in computing technology.
In June 2006, the performance of the world’s top supercomputer BlueGene/L was measured at 2.806 Peta-flops/s (floating point operations per second) on the High Performance Linpack (HPL) benchmark, a program that solves a large dense system of linear equations by LU with partial pivoting.
As of November 2025, that rate has grown to 1.742 Exa-flops/s, achieved by today’s top supercomputer El Capitan.

Modern computing architectures have made it possible to solve increasingly large-scale problems; however, as evidenced by both theory and practice, the performance of classical algorithms is limited by asymptotic complexity and
by the cost of data movement.
As a point of reference, the amount of data collected by the Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration to generate the first image of a black hole in 2019 was over 5 Petabytes, which is almost the full storage capacity of El Capitan.
In other words, classical matrix algorithms with cubic cost in the input size, such as orthogonalization, are untenable given the large-scale problems now considered in scientific investigations.
For the best practical performance, it is necessary to develop algorithms and implementations that can fully leverage computing resources.

Perhaps the greatest benefit of randomization is that it gives us the ability to restructure algorithms to attenuate computational demands on whichever computing resources most hinder performance, such as flops, matrix access, or data movement.
For example, randomization admits algorithms that require just a single pass over the input data
128
;
186
;
187

Randomization can also significantly reduce the number of arithmetic operations required by classical algorithms; e.g., for dense
m\times n
overdetermined least squares problems with
m\gg n

O(mn+n^{3})
operations vs.
O(mn^{2})
with classical approaches
76
;
126
;
155
;
174
.
In short, randomized algorithms produce accurate solutions with high probability while reducing working storage and communication costs, offering improved asymptotic complexity, admitting straightforward parallelization and GPU acceleration, and enabling practical investigations that would be otherwise impossible.
For an expanded discussion, see
140

2.1
Notation

Throughout this manuscript, we work over the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
or complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
(using
\mathbb{F}
to signify either), and we let
[m]
denote the integers
1,2,\ldots,m
.

Vectors are denoted by bold lowercase Roman or Greek letters (e.g.,
\bm{\mathsf{x}},\bm{\mathsf{\omega}}
), whereas matrices are denoted by bold uppercase letters (e.g.,
\bm{\mathsf{X}},\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}
).
We use
\bm{\mathsf{0}}
to denote the zero matrix and
\bm{\mathsf{I}}
for the identity matrix, with their dimensions made explicit by subscripts when needed.
We generally reserve Greek letters for random vectors and matrices.

To refer to coordinates of vectors, we use parentheses with subscripts, i.e.
(\bm{\mathsf{x}})_{i}
refers to the
i
th coordinate of
\bm{\mathsf{x}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptn}
.
We also adopt the notation of Golub and Van Loan
86
to reference submatrices.
Namely, if
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
,
and
I=[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}]\subseteq[m]

and
J=[j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{l}]\subseteq[n]
are (row and column, resp.) index sets,
then
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)
denotes

\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i_{1},j_{1})&\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i_{1},j_{2})&\dots&\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i_{1},j_{l})\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i_{k},j_{1})&\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i_{k},j_{2})&\dots&\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i_{k},j_{l})\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times\ell}.

The abbreviation
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,:)
refers to the submatrix

\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,[n])
, analogously for
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,J)
.

A vector
\bm{\mathsf{x}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptn}
is measured in the Euclidean or
\ell_{2}
-norm
\|\bm{\mathsf{x}}\|_{2}=\left(\sum_{i}|(\bm{\mathsf{x}})_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}
.
A matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
may be equipped with the corresponding operator norm,

\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|_{2}=\sup_{\|\bm{\mathsf{x}}\|=1}\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{x}}\|_{2}
,
or with the Frobenius norm
\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\text{F}}=(\sum_{i,j}|\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i,j)|^{2})^{1/2}
.

The (Hermitian) transpose of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
is denoted by
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{*}
, and the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
is denoted by
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}
.
A matrix
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
is said to be
orthonormal

if its columns are orthonormal, i.e.
\bm{\mathsf{U}}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{U}}=\bm{\mathsf{I}}
.

The trace of a square matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptn\times n}
is the sum of its diagonal elements
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i,i)
.
For matrices
\bm{\mathsf{A}},\bm{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
, we can define the inner product
\langle\bm{\mathsf{A}},\bm{\mathsf{B}}\rangle=\text{trace}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{B}})
, so
\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}=\langle\bm{\mathsf{A}},\bm{\mathsf{A}}\rangle=\textup{trace}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.

The abbreviation
i.i.d.
stands for “independent and identically distributed” to describe random variables.
The probability of a random event is denoted by
\mathbb{P}[\cdot]
, and the expectation of a random variable is given by
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
.
A random vector
\bm{\mathsf{x}}
is isotropic if
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\mathsf{x}}\bm{\mathsf{x}}^{*}]=\bm{\mathsf{I}}
.
If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
, the Rademacher distribution is the uniform distribution on the set
\{\pm 1\}
, or Uniform
\{\pm 1\}
, and if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, a complex Rademacher random variable has the form
(\rho_{1}+i\rho_{2})/\sqrt{2}
, where
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
are
i.i.d
Uniform
\{\pm 1\}
.
The standard normal or Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1 is denoted by
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

2.2
The Singular Value Decomposition

Every matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
admits a
singular value decomposition (SVD)
, given by

\displaystyle\underset{m\times n}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}=\underset{m\times r}{\bm{\mathsf{U}}}\underset{r\times r}{\hskip 5.69054pt\bm{\Sigma}\hskip 5.69054pt}\underset{r\times n}{\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*}},

(1)

where
r=\min(m,n)
,
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{V}}
are orthonormal, and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is diagonal.
The columns
\{\bm{\mathsf{u}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
and
\{\bm{\mathsf{v}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
of
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{V}}
, respectively, are called the left and right singular vectors of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
The diagonal elements
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are the singular values of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, ordered so that
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{r}\geq 0
.
The rank of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
is the number of nonzero singular values.

To obtain a rank-
k
approximation of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, we can truncate its SVD after the first
k
terms, defining
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}\bm{\mathsf{u}}_{i}\bm{\mathsf{v}}_{i}^{*}
.
By the Eckart-Young theorem
74
,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{k}
is the best possible rank-
k
approximation of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, with approximation error given by the singular values:

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{k}\|_{2}=\sigma_{k+1}\ \ \textup{and}\ \ \|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{k}\|_{\text{F}}=\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{r}\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(2)

Frequently, the Golub-Reinsch algorithm
85
is used to compute an SVD, as in the built-in MATLAB function

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{U}},\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\mathsf{V}}\end{bmatrix}=\texttt{svd}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}).

We adopt this notation when referring to the computation of the SVD, as well as
\texttt{svd}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},k)
to represent the function outputting the truncation
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{k}
.

2.3
The QR Decomposition

Every matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
admits a
QR decomposition

\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}
where
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}
is an orthonormal matrix of size
m\times r
, and where
\bm{\mathsf{R}}
is an
r\times n
upper triangular matrix, where
r=\min(m,n)
. In what follows, we are particularly interested in
column pivoted
QR factorizations, which take the form

\displaystyle\underset{m\times n}{\hskip 5.69054pt\bm{\mathsf{A}}\hskip 5.69054pt}\underset{n\times n}{\bm{\mathsf{P}}}=\underset{m\times r}{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}}\underset{r\times n}{\hskip 5.69054pt\bm{\mathsf{R}}\hskip 5.69054pt},

(3)

for some permutation matrix
\bm{\mathsf{P}}
.
The point here is that it is often possible to pick a permutation matrix

\bm{\mathsf{P}}
in such a way that the truncation of (
3
) to the dominant
terms forms a good low rank approximation to
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
Common algorithms such as Golub-Businger with standard column
pivoting perform well in practice, and more advanced pivoting techniques
come with provable guarantees
88
, cf. Sect
2.5
.
These methods are greedy, and can be halted after a fixed number
k
steps.
We use the notation

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{\mathsf{Q}},\bm{\mathsf{R}},\bm{\mathsf{P}}}\end{bmatrix}=\texttt{qr}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})\ \ \textup{and}\ \ \begin{bmatrix}{\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{k},\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k},\bm{\mathsf{P}}}\end{bmatrix}=\texttt{qr}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},k),

(4)

to refer to functions for computing either a full or a partial QR factorization.
It is sometimes convenient to store the permutation using an index vector

I
such that
\bm{\mathsf{P}}=\bm{\mathsf{I}}(\colon,I)
, where
\bm{\mathsf{I}}
is the identity
matrix.
If we need only the factor
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}
, whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the range of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, we use the notation

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{Q}}=\texttt{col}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})\ \ \textup{and}\ \ \bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{k}=\texttt{col}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},k).

(5)

2.4
The LU Decomposition

Every matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
admits an
LU decomposition
given by

\displaystyle\underset{m\times m}{\bm{\mathsf{P}}}\underset{m\times n}{\hskip 5.69054pt\bm{\mathsf{A}}\hskip 5.69054pt}=\underset{m\times r}{\bm{\mathsf{L}}}\underset{r\times n}{\hskip 5.69054pt\bm{\mathsf{U}}\hskip 5.69054pt},

(6)

where
r=\min(m,n)
,
\bm{\mathsf{P}}
is a permutation matrix,
\bm{\mathsf{L}}
is lower triangular and
\bm{\mathsf{R}}
is upper triangular.
We can halt the factorization after computation of the first
k
terms to obtain a “partial” LU factorization
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\approx\bm{\mathsf{L}}_{k}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{k}
.

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{\mathsf{L}},\bm{\mathsf{U}},P}\end{bmatrix}=\texttt{lu}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})\ \ \textup{and}\ \ \begin{bmatrix}{\bm{\mathsf{L}}_{k},\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{k},I}\end{bmatrix}=\texttt{lu}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},k),

(7)

denote functions that yield either the full or partial LU factorization, respectively, where
\bm{\mathsf{P}}
is stored as a permutation vector
P
and
I=P(1:k)
.

2.5
The Interpolative and CUR Decompositions

Every matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
of rank
k
admits
interpolative decompositions
(ID) and a
CUR decomposition
of the form

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\underset{m\times k}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\dagger}}\underset{k\times n}{\bm{\mathsf{R}}},&\hskip 14.22636pt\textit{Row ID}\\
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\underset{m\times k}{\bm{\mathsf{C}}}\underset{k\times n}{\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}},&\hskip 14.22636pt\textit{Column ID}\\
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\underset{m\times k}{\bm{\mathsf{C}}}\underset{k\times k}{\hskip 5.69054pt\bm{\mathsf{U}}\hskip 5.69054pt}\underset{k\times n}{\bm{\mathsf{R}}},&\hskip 14.22636pt\textit{CUR}\end{array}

where
\bm{\mathsf{R}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,:)
for
k
linearly independent rows indexed by
I
;
\bm{\mathsf{C}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,J)
for
k
linearly independent columns indexed by
J
; and
\bm{\mathsf{U}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\dagger}
, though in practice,
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
is often approximated by
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)^{\dagger}
.
The columns of
\bm{\mathsf{C}}
or rows of
\bm{\mathsf{R}}
are known as skeletons.

Typically, constructing the CUR decomposition is more ill-conditioned than the IDs, but a stable construction can be attained through QR factorizations of
\bm{\mathsf{C}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{R}}
.
Namely, let
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{\bm{\mathsf{C}}}=\texttt{col}(\bm{\mathsf{C}})
and
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{\bm{\mathsf{R}}}=\texttt{col}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{*})
.
The CUR factorization is then given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{U}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{\bm{\mathsf{C}}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{\bm{\mathsf{C}}}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{\bm{\mathsf{R}}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{\bm{\mathsf{R}}}^{*}.

(11)

This stabilization tactic also works for the construction of the IDs, if needed.

The accuracy of a rank-
k
ID or CUR approximation depends on the choice of skeletons.
It was shown in
61
that it is possible to construct an ID satisfying

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\text{F}}\leq\sqrt{k+1}\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{k}\|_{\text{F}},

(12)

where the coefficient
\sqrt{k+1}
is optimal and cannot be improved; here,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{k}
is the optimal rank-
k
approximation given by the truncated SVD.
In practice, greedy pivoting algorithms, based on QR, LU, or the SVD, are frequently used to choose skeletons for ID and CUR decompositions.
We discuss several in the following sections.

ID/CUR from QR with Column Pivoting (CPQR)

Assuming
m\geq n
, let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}
represent the resulting matrix after
t
steps of pivoting and updating for
t=0,\ldots,n-2
(with
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(0)}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}
).
At the
(t+1)
-th step, CPQR chooses the column pivot
j_{t+1}
as the column of maximal Euclidean norm in the active submatrix,

\displaystyle j_{t+1}=\arg\max_{t+1\leq j\leq n}\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}([t+1:m],j)\|_{2}.

The selected
k
(column) skeletons are then
[j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}]
.
(Row skeletons can be computed via CPQR on
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{*}
.)
Once a pivot entry is selected, column
j_{t+1}
is swapped with column
t+1
, the normalized reflection vector
\bm{\mathsf{v}}
is formed, and the lower right block of the active submatrix, starting from the
(t+1)
-st main diagonal entry, is updated as
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t+1)}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}-2\bm{\mathsf{v}}\bm{\mathsf{v}}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}.

In practice, CPQR chooses pivots in an order that reveals the rank of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, so that the chosen skeletons form a good basis for its row or column space.
While there are adversarial cases for which CPQR can fail to be rank-revealing (e.g., Kahan-type matrices
109
), these are rarely encountered; cf.
185
.
There are variations of CPQR with better theoretical guarantees, such as rank-revealing QR
36
;
37
or strong rank-revealing QR
89
, but they are more costly to implement.

ID/CUR from LU with Partial Pivoting (LUPP)

For an ID or CUR decomposition using LUPP, row pivots are selected from the active submatrix of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, which is updated after each pivot selection via Schur complements (e.g.,
(
86
, Algorithm 3.2.1)
).
Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}
be the resulting matrix after
t
steps of pivoting and updating for
t=0,1,\ldots,n-2
.
At the (
t+1
)-st step of LUPP, the largest-magnitude column entry of the active submatrix is selected as the next pivot entry:

\displaystyle i_{t+1}=\arg\max_{t+1\leq i\leq m}|\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}(i,t+1)|.

The active submatrix is then updated: defining vectors
\bm{\mathsf{r}}=[t+2:m]
and
\bm{\mathsf{c}}=[t+2:n]
,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t+1)}(\bm{\mathsf{r}},\bm{\mathsf{c}})=\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}(\bm{\mathsf{r}},\bm{\mathsf{c}})-\frac{\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}(\bm{\mathsf{r}},t+1)\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}(t+1,\bm{\mathsf{c}})}{\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}(t+1,t+1)},

(13)

where
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}(t+1,t+1)
has been updated with
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(t)}(i_{t+1},t+1)
following row permutation.
If expressed as the output of the function
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{L}},\bm{\mathsf{U}},P\end{bmatrix}=\texttt{lu}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
,
the row skeletons at the
(t+1)
-th step are given by
I=P(1:t+1)
.

This algorithm leads to an exponential upper bound on the entries of
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
, which is tight for certain matrices (e.g., Kahan-type matrices
109
).
Thus, LUPP can fail to be rank-revealing, but again this is rare in practice
185
.
There are more sophisticated pivoting strategies to lend rank-revealing properties to the LU factorization
35
;
158
, but at significantly greater computational cost.
We will see that randomization can equip LUPP with rank-revealing properties comparable to CPQR even in adversarial cases.

ID/CUR from a Single Truncated SVD

Previously, the tight error bound (
12
) had been achieved by expensive random volume sampling
61
, or computing at least
k
SVDs to choose each pivot from an updated residual subspace.
Recently, in
154
, the optimal error in (
12
) is achieved through a single rank-
k
truncated SVD, with total complexity
O(mnk)
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}
be an arbitrary rank-
k
approximation of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}
.
We want to find columns
\bm{\mathsf{C}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times k}
of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and an interpolation matrix
\bm{\mathsf{W}}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times n}
such that

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\text{F}}\leq\left\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{W}}\right\|_{\text{F}}\leq\sqrt{k+1}\left\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{Z}}\right\|_{\text{F}}.

(14)

For the optimal error, we can take
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
to be the truncated SVD.
Otherwise, if
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
is not a rank-
k
SVD, we compute one singular value decomposition
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\bm{\mathsf{\Sigma}}\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*}.

Define

\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{V}}\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*},

noting that
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}-\bm{\mathsf{Z}}\bm{\mathsf{V}}\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*}=\bm{\mathsf{0}}
, and

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}\|_{\text{F}}=\|(\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{Z}})(\bm{\mathsf{I}}-\bm{\mathsf{V}}\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*})\|_{\text{F}}\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{Z}}\|_{\text{F}}.

Crucially, the construction of
\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}
requires only
O(mnk)
operations.

Letting
\bm{\mathsf{W}}=(\hat{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*})^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*}
, where
\hat{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times k}
is the sub-matrix of
\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times n}
corresponding to the
k
column skeletons in
\bm{\mathsf{C}}
, we have

\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{W}}\|_{\text{F}}=\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{C}}}\bm{\mathsf{W}}\|_{\text{F}},

for
\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{C}}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}-\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{V}}\hat{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*}.

Since
\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{W}}
are not affected by replacing
\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*}
with any unitary transformation
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*}
, we can assume unitary transformations are applied to
\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*}
so that
\hat{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*}
is always upper-triangular.
(In
154
, this is accomplished via Householder reflections.)

Assuming we have selected the first
i-1
columns for
i\geq 1
, the approximation error at the
i
th step is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i)}=\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i-1)}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i-1)}_{1:m,i}(\bar{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*})_{1:(k-i+1),i}^{\dagger}\bar{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*},

(15)

where
\bar{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}^{(k-i+1)\times n}
are the last
k-i+1
rows of
\bm{\mathsf{V}}^{*}
, cf.
(
154
, Equation 5)
.
We choose the next column skeleton as the one that minimizes the error at the
i
th step, which we can do by selecting the column
j\geq i
that minimizes the ratio

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i-1)}_{1:m,j}\|_{2}/\|\bar{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*}_{1:(k-i+1),j}\|_{2}.

(16)

By (
15
) and the upper triangularity of
\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i-1)}_{1:m,\ell}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i-1)}\|^{2}_{\text{F}},

(17)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\|\bar{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*}_{1:(k-i+1),\ell}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bar{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}=k-i+1.

(18)

Then, using the fact that
\sum_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}/\sum_{\ell}\beta_{\ell}\geq\min_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}/\beta_{\ell}
for non-negative
\alpha_{\ell}
and positive
\sum_{\ell}\beta_{\ell}
, we can upper bound (
16
) by the ratio of (
17
) and (
18
), to estimate the error in the
i
th step in terms of the
(i-1)
th step:

\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i)}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}=\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i-1)}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i-1)}_{1:m,i}\bar{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*}_{1:(k-i+1),i}\bar{\bm{\mathsf{V}}}^{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i-1)}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}+\frac{1}{k-i+1}\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i-1)}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}.

Adding up over all previous steps, we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{(i)}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}\leq\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}+\frac{i}{k-i+1}\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}.

(19)

Letting
i=k
in (
19
), we achieve the desired bound in (
12
) in
O(mnk)
operations.

3
Randomized Dimension Reduction: Sketching and Sampling

In this section, we discuss ways that randomization can be integrated into classical algorithms in numerical linear algebra to compute low-rank decompositions more efficiently.
Namely, we will often rely on special types of linear maps that map the row or column space of an input matrix to a smaller-dimensional space in such a way that the original geometry is roughly preserved, called a
dimension reduction map
(DRM).
If the linear map is drawn from a random matrix distribution that achieves geometry-preserving dimension reduction with high probability, it is known as a randomized DRM.

Randomized DRMs are often associated with randomized embeddings, linear maps drawn from random distributions that satisfy the following condition with high probability.
Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
be a matrix of rank
k\leq\min(m,n)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
a distortion parameter.
A linear map
\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}:\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptd}
is an embedding of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
with distortion
\varepsilon
if

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{x}}\|\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{x}}\|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{x}}\|\ \ \forall\ \bm{\mathsf{x}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptn}.

(20)

Usually,
k\leq d\ll m
.
The minimal value of
d
required for
\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}
to satisfy (
20
) depends on the type of embedding
\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}
, the rank
k
of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, and the distortion parameter
\epsilon
.
As a classical example, the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma
108
establishes that
d\geq 8\log k/\varepsilon^{2}
is sufficient for
\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}
with standard Gaussian entries to embed
k
points in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to satisfy (
20
).

More generally, if
\mathcal{S}
is any probability distribution over linear maps
\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptd}
, then
\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}\sim\mathcal{S}
is a randomized embedding if (
20
) holds over
\mathcal{S}
for all
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
with at least constant probability.
If a randomized embedding can be constructed to map an unknown subspace into a lower-dimensional space (i.e. without knowing anything about the input space except its dimension), then it is said to be an oblivious subspace embedding (OSE)
176
.
Many times in practice, randomized DRMs are treated as embeddings, but we will see in Sect.
3.1
that satisfying (
20
) is sufficient but not necessary for the smaller-dimensional space to be a good proxy for the input.

In Sect.
3.1
, we review randomized sketching, a procedure that produces random linear combinations of input matrix coordinates, which we say is “coordinate-mixing.”
We then focus on randomized sampling of matrix coordinates in Sect.
3.2
.
We end with an important application of randomized dimension reduction that will be relevant for our discussion of tensor decompositions: solving overdetermined least squares problems.
For detailed treatments, the reader is referred to
31
;
91
;
141
;
140
;
204
.

3.3
Application: Randomized DRMs for Overdetermined Least Squares

As an illustrative application of randomized DRMs that will also be useful for our tensor algorithms, we consider the overdetermined least squares problem, in which we solve

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{x}}_{*}=\arg\min_{\bm{\mathsf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{x}}-\bm{\mathsf{b}}\|_{2},

(26)

for given
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
\bm{\mathsf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
m\gg n
.
Using CPQR for (
26
) requires
O(mn^{2})
operations, which is undesirable for large
m
.

A natural idea is to apply a randomized DRM to
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{b}}
in (
26
), a strategy known as “sketch-and-solve”
176
.
Namely, we draw a randomized DRM
\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}\in\mathbb{F}^{d\times m}
and solve

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}=\arg\min_{\bm{\mathsf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{x}}-\bm{\mathsf{b}})\|_{2}.

(27)

If
\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}
embeds the
k
-dimensional column space of
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}&\bm{\mathsf{b}}\end{bmatrix}
with distortion
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, then

(1-\varepsilon)\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{x}}_{*}-\bm{\mathsf{b}}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}-\bm{\mathsf{b}}\|_{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{x}}_{*}-\bm{\mathsf{b}}\|_{2}

when
d\approx k/\varepsilon^{2}
.
Solving (
27
) requires just
O(dn^{2})
operations, and often in practice, there are ways to avoid forming
\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{\Gamma}}\bm{\mathsf{b}}
explicitly.

Randomized DRMs are also utilized in other methods of solving (
26
).
Sketched GMRES
(
151
, Algorithm 1.1)
includes a
k
-truncated Arnoldi process to quickly compute an approximate basis of the Krylov subspace.
Sketch-and-precondition methods are also popular
9
;
142
;
144
.
We discuss alternative randomized DRMs for overdetermined least squares problems in computing tensor decompositions in Sect.
6
.

4
Randomized Algorithms for Low-Rank Matrix Decompositions

In this section, we illustrate several ways that randomized DRMs are often used to compute low-rank matrix decompositions.
We introduce the rangefinder problem and show how it can be solved with randomized DRMs in Sect.
4.1
.
We then explain how randomized DRMS can be used to estimate the error in the corresponding low-rank approximation for adaptive rangefinding.
These ideas are foundational for randomized algorithms that have become ubiquitous for fast and reliable low-rank matrix approximation: the randomized SVD (Sect.
4.2
) and randomized ID/CUR (Sect.
4.3
).

4.1
The Randomized Rangefinder

At the heart of many linear algebraic tasks lies the
rangefinder problem
, finding a lower-dimensional space that captures the action of a given matrix.
To do this, for an input matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
and target rank
k\leq\min(m,n)
, we compute an orthonormal matrix
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times k}
whose columns approximately span the column space of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
We then obtain a rank-
k
approximation given by
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, with approximation error

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|=\|(\bm{\mathsf{I}}-\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{*})\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|,

(28)

measured in either the Frobenius or spectral norm in our work.
The Frobenius norm is often preferable for ease of analysis but can be less informative the spectral norm.

The rangefinder problem can be handled effectively with randomized DRMs, as in Algorithm
1
, which produces an orthonormal matrix
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}
via QR factorization of an
m\times\ell
random sketch of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
for desired embedding dimension
\ell
.
The total cost of Algorithm
1
includes the cost of simulating the
n\times\ell
random test matrix,
O(mn\ell)
operations to form the sketch, and
O(mk^{2})
operations for the QR factorization.
Sparsity or availability of fast matrix-vector primitives with
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
can lend additional performance improvements.

Error estimation and certificates of accuracy

The approximation error (
28
) can be estimated reliably and efficiently with randomized sketching, which serves as a “certificate of accuracy” that the basis given by
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}
approximately spans the range of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
Let
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times k}
be an orthonormal matrix whose columns potentially form a basis for the range of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
.
Let
\bm{\mathsf{\Phi}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptn\times s}
be a Gaussian matrix that is independent from any random matrix used to compute
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}
.
In practice,
s
is a small fixed value, such as
s=10
.
We form a sketch, often called the auxiliary sample,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{Z}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{\Phi}},

(29)

From
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
, we obtain an inexpensive Gaussian sketch of the approximation error (
28
):

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathsf{I}}-\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{*})\bm{\mathsf{Z}}=(\bm{\mathsf{I}}-\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{*})\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{\Phi}}.

(30)

We can then estimate the Frobenius norm of (
30
) as

\displaystyle\|(\bm{\mathsf{I}}-\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{*})\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\approx\frac{1}{s}\|(\bm{\mathsf{I}}-\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{*})\bm{\mathsf{Z}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2},

(31)

noting that
\text{trace}(\bm{\mathsf{B}})=\bm{\mathsf{\omega}}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{B}}\bm{\mathsf{\omega}}
in expectation for any matrix
\bm{\mathsf{B}}
and isotropic random vector
\omega
).
Then to reduce the variance, we take the average of
s
random variables in (
31
), using the auxiliary sample
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
.
The error sketch avails us of the powerful theory of Gaussian matrices, regardless of how the column space approximation was obtained.
For spectral norm estimation, see
(
140
, Sects. 5-6)
.

4.3
Randomized ID/CUR

In both the randomized rangefinder and the randomized SVD, orthonormal matrices are computed whose columns form an approximate basis for the row or column space of an input matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
However, for some problems (e.g., if
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
is sparse or non-negative), it may be preferable to have a “natural basis,” consisting of rows or columns of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, as in the ID/CUR of Sect.
2.5
.
In this section, we provide an overview of randomized methods to compute approximate ID/CUR factorizations, including recent work to improve skeleton selection and performance
66
;
67
;
163
;
170
.
We first illustrate how a random sketch can be used as a proxy for the input matrix in ID/CUR approximations.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times n}
be of exact rank
k
for notational convenience.
Let
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hskip 0.56905ptm\times k}
have columns that span the range of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, not necessarily orthogonal.
By definition,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}={\bm{\mathsf{Y}}}{\bm{\mathsf{F}}}
for some matrix
\bm{\mathsf{F}}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times n}
.
Compute a row ID of
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
, e.g., with CPQR via
[\sim,\sim,I]=\texttt{qr}(\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{*},k)
or LUPP via
[\sim,\sim,I]=\texttt{lu}(\bm{\mathsf{Y}},k)
, so that
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}=\bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{Y}}(I,:)
.
We now observe that index set
I
and row interpolation matrix
\bm{\mathsf{X}}
form a row ID of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{Y}}\bm{\mathsf{F}}=\left[\bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{Y}}(I,:)\right]\bm{\mathsf{F}}=\bm{\mathsf{X}}\left[\bm{\mathsf{Y}}(I,:)\bm{\mathsf{F}}\right]=\bm{\mathsf{X}}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,:).

Thus, to compute a row ID of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, it suffices to compute a row ID of a matrix
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
whose columns span the range of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
, and
since we do not need
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
to be orthogonal, we can save on computational cost by using the fact that the range of
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}
is itself an accurate approximation to the range of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
with high probability
(
91
, Theorems 10.5 and 10.6)
.
We observe that the randomized rangefinder error
\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|
using
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}
is equal to
\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{Y}}\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\|
, where
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{\dagger}
is the orthogonal projector onto the column space of
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
(or, equivalently, the column space of
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}
).

\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{W}}^{(t)}\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}<\tau,

where
\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{(t)}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I_{s}^{(t)},:)
.

Fix a block size
b
(e.g.,
b=10
), draw a random matrix
\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times b}
, and form
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{(0)}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{(0)}
.
We use LUPP to compute

\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{(0)}\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{(0)}=\bm{\mathsf{L}}^{(0)}\bm{\mathsf{U}}^{(0)},

so that
the first
b
skeletons
I_{s}^{(0)}
are the first
b
indices of
\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{(0)}[1\ldots m]^{*}
.
To estimate
\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{W}}^{(0)}\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{(0)}\|_{\text{F}}
, we draw an independent random matrix
\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times b}
from the same distribution as
\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{(0)}
and form
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{(1)}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{(1)}
.
By
(
163
, Equation 20)
,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{W}}^{(0)}\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{(0)}\|_{\text{F}}

\displaystyle=\|(\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{(0)}\bm{\mathsf{A}})(b+1:m,:)-\bm{\mathsf{L}}_{2}^{(0)}(\bm{\mathsf{L}}_{1}^{(0)})^{-1}(\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{(0)}\bm{\mathsf{A}})(1:b,:)\|_{\text{F}}

\displaystyle\approx\|\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{(1)}-\bm{\mathsf{W}}^{(0)}\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{(0)}\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{(1)}\|_{\text{F}}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{(0)}\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{(1)}(b+1:m,:)-\bm{\mathsf{L}}_{2}^{(0)}(\bm{\mathsf{L}}_{1}^{(0)})^{-1}(\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{(0)}\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{(1)})(1:b,:)\|_{\text{F}},

where
\bm{\mathsf{L}}^{(0)}_{1}=\bm{\mathsf{L}}^{(0)}(1:b,:)
and
\bm{\mathsf{L}}^{(0)}_{2}=\bm{\mathsf{L}}^{(0)}(b+1:m,:)
.
If the given tolerance is not met, we LU-factorize
[\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{(0)}\ \bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{(1)}]
re-using the previous factorization, and repeat.
In general, the error after
t-1
iterations is given by the Schur complement

\|\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{(t)}\|_{\text{F}}=\|(\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{(t-1)}\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{(t)})(tb+1:m,:)-\bm{\mathsf{L}}^{(t-1)}_{2}(\bm{\mathsf{L}}_{1}^{(t-1)})^{-1}(\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{(t-1)}\bm{\mathsf{Y}}^{(t)})(1:tb,:)\|_{\text{F}}.

A similar idea is presented in
170
for a rank-adaptive CUR factorization, using a single small sketch of the residual matrix
\bm{\mathsf{S}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{C}}\bm{\mathsf{U}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}
.
(Here,
\bm{\mathsf{U}}
is computed as
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)^{\dagger}
vs.
\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}^{\dagger}
as in Sect.

The column residual is initialized as a row sketch,
\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{(C)}_{0}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{1.1b\times n}
, where typically
5\leq b\leq 250
, and the row and column skeletons are empty sets.
While the error criterion is unmet, column skeletons
J
are first computed from
\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{(C)}_{0}
, and the row residual is formed as
\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{(R)}_{1}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,J)-\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{1}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{1}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{1}(:,J)=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,J)
.
Row skeletons
I
are then computed from
\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{(R)}_{1}
, and the column residual is formed as
\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{(C)}_{1}=\bm{\mathsf{G}}\bm{\mathsf{A}}-\bm{\mathsf{G}}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{1}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{1}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{1}
, where now
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{1}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,J)
,
\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{1}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,:)
, and
\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{1}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)^{\dagger}
are no longer empty.
This procedure is repeated, with new skeletons appended to previously selected ones, until the desired approximation accuracy is achieved.

With rank-adaptive random pivoting

There have been several recent advances in coordinate sampling-based methods for ID/CUR decompositions.

In
46
, the randomly pivoted (RP) Cholesky algorithm is introduced for positive semi-definite matrices
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times m}
.
Given a target rank
k
, the algorithm outputs a set of pivot indices
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}
akin to skeletons, and a matrix
\bm{\mathsf{F}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times k}
for approximate Cholesky factorization
\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}=\bm{\mathsf{F}}\bm{\mathsf{F}}^{*}
, which is the factorized form of the column Nystr”om approximation
\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,S)\bm{\mathsf{A}}(S,S)^{{\dagger}}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(S,:)
.
By selecting pivots, or skeletons, with probability proportional to the magnitudes of the diagonal entries of the residual matrix in each iteration, the RP Cholesky algorithm returns the Cholesky-factorized form of the Nyström approximation in
O(km)
matrix entry evaluations and
O(k^{2}m)
additional arithmetic operations, with
O(km)
storage requirements.

We begin by defining
\bm{\mathsf{d}}=\textup{diag}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
and initializing the matrix
\bm{\mathsf{F}}
as
\bm{\mathsf{0}}
.
After sampling each pivot
s_{i}\sim\bm{\mathsf{d}}/\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bm{\mathsf{d}}(j)
, we form
\bm{\mathsf{g}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,s_{i})
and remove any overlap with previously selected columns via

\bm{\mathsf{g}}=\bm{\mathsf{g}}-\bm{\mathsf{F}}(:,1:(i-1))\bm{\mathsf{F}}(s_{i},1:(i-1))^{*}.

The matrix
\bm{\mathsf{F}}
is then updated via
\bm{\mathsf{F}}(:,i)=\bm{\mathsf{g}}/\sqrt{\bm{\mathsf{g}}(s_{i})}
, and the vector
\bm{\mathsf{d}}
of diagonal entries of the residual matrix is updated via
\bm{\mathsf{d}}=\bm{\mathsf{d}}-|\bm{\mathsf{F}}(:,i)|^{2}
.
To achieve an
\varepsilon
-approximation,
d\geq k/\varepsilon+k\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\eta})
columns are required, where
\eta
is the relative trace-norm error, cf.
(
46
, Theorem 2.3)
.
A blocked variant of RP Cholesky is also provided in
(
46
, Algorithm 3)
.
Although RP Cholesky is efficient, simple to implement, and comes with strong theoretical guarantees, it can only be applied to positive semi-definite matrices.
If
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
is not positive semi-definite, then we would need to form the Gram matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{*}
before applying RP Cholesky, which is computationally undesirable.

The recent work of
66
, inspired by RP Cholesky, introduces robust blockwise random pivoting (RBRP) for matrix IDs.
Like RP Cholesky, the RBRP algorithm incorporates both randomness and adaptiveness, in that each iteration of skeleton selection is informed by previous ones.
Blockwise random pivoting replaces greedy pivoting based on squared column norms in CPQR with squared-norm sampling, and the “robustness” of RBRP refers to a filtering step that eliminates redundant points within each block of selected skeleton candidates.
Namely, suppose that we sample
b
row skeleton candidates
S_{t}
for some fixed block size
b
.
We then form

\bm{\mathsf{V}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(S_{t},:)^{*}-\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{(t-1)}((\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{(t-1)})^{*}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(S_{t},:)^{*})\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times b},

where
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{(t-1)}
is an orthonormal basis for the previously selected skeletons.
Given some filtering tolerance
\tau
, we use CPQR to compute the factorization

\bm{\mathsf{V}}(:,P)=\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\bm{\mathsf{R}}\approx\bm{\mathsf{Q}}(:,1:b^{\prime})\bm{\mathsf{R}}(1:b^{\prime},:),

so that

\|\bm{\mathsf{V}}(:,P)-\bm{\mathsf{Q}}(:,1:b^{\prime})\bm{\mathsf{R}}(1:b^{\prime},:)\|^{2}_{\text{F}}=\|\bm{\mathsf{R}}(b^{\prime}+1:b,b^{\prime}+1:b)\|^{2}_{\text{F}}<\tau\|\bm{\mathsf{V}}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}.

This leads to a time complexity of roughly
O(mnk+nk^{2})
for resulting skeleton cardinality
k
, as RBRP is also rank-adaptive, and the optimal associated interpolation matrix
\bm{\mathsf{W}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(S,:)^{\dagger}
can be computed efficiently in
O(mk^{2})
time.

5
Tensor Preliminaries

This section marks the beginning of our exploration of tensors, where we present tensors as natural higher-order generalizations of matrices, from 2-way to
d
-way arrays.
We begin with formal notation and definitions in Sect.
5.1
and discuss important matrix and tensor operations in Sect.
5.2
.
While there are many different ways to represent tensors, we focus on two fundamental tensor decomposition formats: the canonical polyadic (CP) and Tucker.
In Sect.
5.3
, we lay out key concepts and practicalities for each of them, including algorithms to compute them.
We then discuss particular low-rank tensor decompositions that can be represented in each format, such as higher-order extensions of the matrix SVD and ID/CUR.
Throughout this section, we show how the classical matrix methods of Sect.
2
form the foundation of those for tensors.

5.1
Notation and Definitions

A
tensor

\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
is a
d
-way array with entries
\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{d}}
, for all
i_{1}\in[N_{1}]
,
i_{2}\in[N_{2}]
,
\ldots
,
i_{d}\in[N_{d}]
.
A
d
-way tensor is said to be of
order

d
, and each of its
d
ways is referred to as a
mode
; a matrix, for instance, is a tensor of order 2, with mode 1 comprised of its rows and mode 2 comprised of its columns.
A
mode-
j
fiber
of a tensor is a vector obtained by fixing all indices except for the
j
th, e.g., a column of a matrix is a mode-1 fiber, and a row is a mode-2 fiber.
The
mode-
j
slices
of a tensor are the sub-tensors
\bm{\mathcal{X}}(:,\ldots,:,i_{j},:,\ldots,:)
obtained by fixing a single index
i_{j}\in[N_{j}]
, e.g., slices of 3-mode tensors are matrices.

We define
N=\prod_{j=1}^{d}N_{j}=\textup{size}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})
, and
N^{(-j)}=\prod_{k=1,k\neq j}^{d}N_{k}=N/N_{j}
.
Through a rearrangement of entries known as
matricization
, a
d
-mode tensor can be “unfolded” into a matrix.
The
mode-
j
unfolding
of a
d
-mode tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
, denoted
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{j}\times N^{(-j)}}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
, is a matrix whose columns are the mode-
j
fibers of
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
.
We assume the
d
modes are ordered
1,\ldots,d
for simplicity, but for computational reasons, it may be beneficial to re-order the modes for processing.

In our work, the (Frobenius)
norm
of a tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
, denoted by
\|\bm{\mathcal{X}}\|
, is the square root of the sum of squares of all elements, analogous to the Frobenius norm for matrices.
A tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
is said to be
rank-one
if it can be expressed as a vector outer product (denoted by
\circ
) of
d
vectors, i.e.
\bm{\mathcal{X}}=\bm{\mathsf{v}}^{(1)}\circ\bm{\mathsf{v}}^{(2)}\circ\cdots\circ\bm{\mathsf{v}}^{(d)}
.

5.2
Matrix and Tensor Products

We now define operations involving matrices and tensors used throughout our work.

The
Kronecker product
of matrices
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{I\times J}
and
\bm{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathbb{F}^{K\times L}
, denoted
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\otimes\bm{\mathsf{B}}
, is the
(IK)\times(JL)
matrix with entries

(\bm{\mathsf{A}}\otimes\bm{\mathsf{B}})(K(i-1)+k,L(j-1)+\ell)=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i,j)\bm{\mathsf{B}}(k,\ell).

The
Khatri-Rao product
of matrices
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{I\times K}
and
\bm{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathbb{F}^{J\times K}
, denoted
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\odot\bm{\mathsf{B}}
, is the
(IJ)\times K
matrix with entries

(\bm{\mathsf{A}}\odot\bm{\mathsf{B}})(:,k)=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,k)\otimes\bm{\mathsf{B}}(:,k)

for
k=1,\ldots,K.

The
Hadamard product
of matrices
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{I\times J}
and
\bm{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathbb{F}^{I\times J}
, denoted
\bm{\mathsf{A}}*\bm{\mathsf{B}}
, is the
I\times J
matrix of entries

(\bm{\mathsf{A}}*\bm{\mathsf{B}})(i,j)=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(i,j)\bm{\mathsf{B}}(i,j).

The
tensor-times-matrix (TTM) product
is the tensor resulting from the mode-
j
product of a tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{j}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
with a matrix
\bm{\mathsf{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{K\times N_{j}}
, denoted by
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}\times_{j}\bm{\mathsf{U}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{j-1}\times K\times N_{j+1}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
, with entries

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{j-1},k,i_{j+1},\ldots,i_{d})=\sum_{i_{j}=1}^{N_{j}}\bm{\mathcal{X}}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\bm{\mathsf{U}}(k,i_{j}).

We can express this more concisely with mode-
j
matricization:
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}_{(j)}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}
.

The
Multi-TTM product
extends the TTM product to multiple modes.
For
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{j}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
and matrices
\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{j}\in\mathbb{F}^{K_{j}\times N_{j}}
for
j\in[d]
, a Multi-TTM is given by

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}\times_{1}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{1}\times_{2}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{d}.

The order of multiplication does not matter, cf.
(
14
, Proposition 3.19)
.
Equivalently, after mode-
j
matricization,

\bm{\mathsf{Y}}_{(j)}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{j}\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}\left(\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{j+1}\otimes\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{j-1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{1}\right)^{*}.

5.3
Tensor Decompositions

This work considers two fundamental formats of tensor decompositions: the canonical polyadic (CP) decomposition and the Tucker decomposition.
The CP decomposition has been known by many different names since its introduction in 1927 in
101
;
100
, cf.
(
114

This decomposition is often viewed as a higher-order extension of the matrix SVD, as it is formed from rank-one components.
The Tucker decomposition

Like the CP decomposition, the Tucker decomposition has been known by several different names in the literature, cf.

Rank, Uniqueness, and the Choice of Tensor Representation

The
rank
of a tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
is the minimal number of rank-one tensors that are required for equality in (
37

\displaystyle\textup{rank}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})=\min\{R\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ \bm{\mathcal{X}}=\llbracket\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)}\rrbracket,\ \bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{j}\times R}\ \forall j\in[d]\}.

(43)

Mode ordering and TTM products with nonsingular matrices do not affect tensor rank.
However, it is NP-hard to determine
97
.
In practice, the target ranks for low-rank tensor decompositions are chosen heuristically.
For example, we might choose the smallest rank
R
for a CP decomposition that significantly reduces the relative error, in comparison to rank
R-1
, cf.
(
14
, Section 9.4.1)
.
Generally, the optimization procedure in CP-ALS is repeated until the error no longer decreases significantly.

For Tucker decompositions, it is useful to consider the
j
-rank of a tensor, denoted by
\textup{rank}_{j}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})
and defined as the column rank of its mode-
j
unfolding
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}

124
;
53
.
If
R_{j}=\textup{rank}_{j}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})
for
j=1,\ldots,d
, then we say that
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
is a rank-
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})
tensor (distinct from its rank, defined above).
We can compute an exact rank-
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})
Tucker decomposition for any tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
with
R_{j}=\textup{rank}_{j}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})
for
j=1,\ldots,d.

However, if
R_{j}<\textup{rank}_{j}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})
for any
j
, the decomposition is inexact and more difficult to compute.
In practice, we often prescribe a core
j
-rank for
j=1,\ldots,d
that meets some error tolerance or compression ratio
(
14
, Section 4.2)
.

Whether it is better to represent an input tensor in the CP or Tucker format is generally problem-dependent.
Under mild assumptions, the CP decomposition of a tensor is unique, up to scaling and permutation of its rank-one components
18
;
94
;
95
;
123
;
124
;
178
.
The same cannot be said for Tucker decompositions; the mode-
j
factor matrix can be multiplied by a nonsingular matrix and the core tensor updated via mode-
j
TTM with its inverse.
As a result, the factors of a Tucker decomposition are less interpretable in the context of the original data than those of a CP decomposition.

Despite its utility in data compression, the Tucker decomposition still suffers from the curse of dimensionality: letting
k=R_{1}=\cdots=R_{d}
and
n=N_{1}=\cdots=N_{d}
, the cost of storing a Tucker decomposition is
O(k^{d}+dkn)
.
By contrast, the storage cost of a CP decomposition is linear in its rank, but its rank may be very large to achieve the desired accuracy.
In short, the notion of rank for tensor decompositions is more nuanced than for matrices, and the choice of representation and target rank (or
j

For more detailed treatments, we refer the reader to
14
;
53
;
87
;

.

Low-Rank Tensor Factorizations

In the previous sections, we introduced two fundamental tensor representations, the CP and the Tucker decomposition, and we summarized their key properties.
In this section, we survey low-rank tensor factorizations that can be expressed in each format.

Orthogonal factor matrices

Recall from Sect.
5.3
that the (ST-)HOSVD and HOOI methods used to compute Tucker decompositions return orthonormal factor matrices
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j)}
for
j=1,\ldots,d
.
Alternatively, there are algorithms for Tucker decompositions where orthogonality of factor matrices is not enforced, such as algorithms for the tensor ID/CUR factorizations in which factor matrices are computed as sub-matrices of unfoldings.
Other examples beyond the scope of the current work include diagonalization algorithms for Tucker decompositions
183
;
184
or algorithms that compute non-negative or sparse Tucker factorizations
113
;
149
.
These algorithms may be preferable in specific application domains, such as image processing
180
;
210
, but in general, many tensor computations are greatly simplified when the Tucker factor matrices are orthogonal.
Moreover, the (ST-)HOSVD with enforced orthogonality comes with quasi-optimality guarantees.
As such, with the exception of the tensor ID/CUR, we focus on algorithms for Tucker decompositions that compute orthogonal (in fact, orthonormal) factor matrices.
There is also historical precedence for this decision, as the seminal algorithms to compute Tucker decompositions enforced the orthogonality of factor matrices
53
;
54
;
73
;
116
;
195
;
196
;
198
.

Finally, we note that the factor matrices in a CP decomposition are not orthogonal in general.
Some tensors may admit CP decompositions with orthogonal factors
8
;
172
, called ODECO tensors; however, ODECO tensors occur with probability 0
116
;
117
;
119
.
This is in contrast with Tucker factor matrices, which can be transformed straightforwardly into orthogonal matrices, cf.
(
14
, Sections 5.4, 17.1.4)
.
As such, in this work, we focus solely on Tucker decompositions in our discussion of randomized algorithms to compute orthogonal factor matrices in Sect.
6
.

Tensor ID and CUR factorizations

As in the matrix setting (Sect.
2.5
), a tensor ID or CUR factorization comprises actual entries of the input tensor.
The advantages of ID/CUR factorizations for tensors are similar to those for matrices.
The tensor ID/CUR factorization can be interpreted more easily in the context of the original tensorial data than factorizations involving multi-linear transformations of the data.
Moreover, the ID/CUR preserves the structure of the input tensor (e.g., sparsity or non-negativity), and it can be computed efficiently with minimal storage requirements.

Analogous to the different versions of matrix ID/CUR (e.g., row, column, double-sided, CUR), there are several variants in the tensor setting that have been investigated since the ID/CUR was first extended to 3-mode tensors in
73
.
In general, these variants fall into one of two categories:

1.

The core tensor (in the Tucker format) is a sub-tensor of the input tensor, or

2.

The factor matrices (in the CP or Tucker format) are sub-matrices of unfoldings.

The work of
132
that introduced “tensor CUR factorizations” falls into the first category.
In their algorithm, a
\textup{rank-}(N_{1},N_{2},R)
core tensor is constructed by selecting
R
indices from one mode and keeping all indices from the other two.
Core tensor skeleton selection methods for 3-mode tensor CUR were generalized to
d
-mode tensors in
29
, for Tucker decompositions with the same
j
-rank in each mode.
The “structure-preserving ST-HOSVD” of
145
, initially developed for sparse rank-
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})
tensors, may also be interpreted as a tensor CUR factorization of the first category.
The “Chidori” and “Fiber” tensor CUR factorizations with rank-
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})
core tensors were characterized in
27
, inspired by those in
29
.
The recently proposed “CoreID” in
207
is similar to the CUR factorizations of
27
.
In all of these methods, the entries of the associated factor matrices are computed based on the specific form of the factorization and on the skeletons selected for the core tensor, but the factor matrices are not themselves sub-matrices of unfoldings.

The second category above comprises those tensor ID/CUR factorizations in which the entries of the factor matrices are entries of the input tensor.
The early algorithm of
73
to compute a
\textup{rank-}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})
“approximate tensor SVD” forms the factor matrices in a Tucker decomposition as randomly sampled columns of unfoldings; this factorization would later be termed a “higher-order ID” in
175
, where the mode-
j
factor matrix in a Tucker decomposition is computed as column skeletons of the mode-
j
unfolding selected by greedy pivoting.
We detail several recent algorithms that leverage randomization to compute tensor ID/CUR approximations efficiently in Sect.
6
.

6
Randomized Low-Rank Tensor Decompositions

As in the matrix setting, we are interested in algorithms that compute low-rank tensor decompositions accurately and efficiently, with provable performance guarantees, and with demonstrated high
practical speed.
Because many tensor algorithms fundamentally rely on classical matrix algorithms, we can frequently fall back on the ideas introduced in Sect.
4
to leverage randomization for efficient tensor decompositions.
However, given the rapidly increasing number of applications that involve large-scale tensorial data, it is often impractical or infeasible to rely on basic implementations of the randomized rangefinder, e.g., with Gaussian sketching.

In this section, we survey new developments in fast randomized algorithms for low-rank tensor decompositions in the CP and Tucker formats.
Sect.
6.1
explores how CP-ALS can be accelerated with randomization, specifically through fast sampling methods that exploit the structure of the overdetermined least squares problems.
Sect.
6.2
summarizes randomized methods to compute Tucker decompositions with orthogonal factors, which naturally extend the randomized rangefinder to the tensor setting.
These algorithms are contrasted with those for tensor ID/CUR decompositions in Sect.
6.3
, which includes both the CP and Tucker formats.
Our goal is to broadly survey relevant literature and detail recent developments that may stimulate future work.

6.1
Randomized Algorithms for CP Decomposition

Over the last decade, many randomized algorithms have been proposed to compute CP decompositions more efficiently.
We survey several notable algorithms, before summarizing the recent randomized CP-ALS procedure of
127
, which underlies the high-performance algorithm of
19
, as an illustration of how randomization can be leveraged for efficient CP decompositions.

Many randomized algorithms for CP decompositions seek to exploit the structure of the Khatri-Rao product (KRP) in CP-ALS, cf. (
40
), for fast sketching and sampling.
In
203
, the input tensor is sketched without being explicitly formed, then the KRP is computed.
The “randomized ALS” algorithm of
171
incorporates projections onto random tensors to improve the conditioning of the least squares problems in CP-ALS.
A procedure to sample rows of the KRP near-optimally via fast leverage score approximations is presented in
49
.
In
16
, a Kronecker fast Johnson-Lindenstrauss transform (KFJLT, cf.
107
) is applied to the KRP to reduce the coherence for better sampling performance.
The algorithm of
2
builds on
16
with options for adaptive sketch sizes and regularization terms.
Below, we expand on the recent work of
127
on improved leverage score sampling that exploits KRP structure.

Randomized CP-ALS with Fast Leverage Score Sampling

Recall from Sect.
5.3
that within each iteration of CP-ALS (Algorithm
5
), we compute a rank-
R
CP decomposition that approximates our input tensor by solving
d
least squares problems in succession.
More precisely, given input tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
, in each iteration of CP-ALS, we solve a least squares problem in each mode
j=1,\ldots,d
, in which every factor matrix except
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j)}
is fixed, and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j)}
is computed as the solution of

\displaystyle\min_{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{j}\times R}}\left\|\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}\bm{\mathsf{Z}}^{*}-\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}\right\|^{2}_{\text{F}}=\min_{\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{j}\times R}}\left\|\bm{\mathsf{Z}}\widehat{\bm{\mathsf{A}}}^{*}-\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}^{*}\right\|^{2}_{\text{F}},

(44)

where
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)}\odot\cdots\odot\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j+1)}\odot\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j-1)}\odot\cdots\odot\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{F}^{N^{(-j)}\times R}
.

If we solve (
44
) without exploiting any inherent problem structure, the cost of computing the QR decomposition of
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
is
O(R^{2}N^{(-j)})
, and the cost of applying it
N_{j}
times brings the total cost to
O(R^{2}N^{(-j)}+RN)
.

In
127
, the CP-ARLS-LEV algorithm is developed to accelerate each least squares sub-problem (
44
).
Namely,
S\ll N^{(-j)}
rows of
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
are sampled according to an approximate leverage score distribution.
The corresponding
S
rows of
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}^{*}
are then selected for a smaller “sketched” problem, whose solution approximates the solution of (
44
).
Sketching reduces the cost of solving the
j
th least squares problem to
O(R^{2}S+RSN_{j})
.
However, forming
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
or
\bm{\mathsf{X}}
and computing leverage scores would incur a cost of
O(R^{2}N^{(-j)})
.
To avoid this, in CP-ARLS-LEV, the leverage scores are estimated as the product of leverage scores of the respective factor matrices in the KRP, and only the
S
sampled rows of
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
are formed explicitly.

To this end, let
j\in[d]
be fixed.
We first observe that there is a bijection mapping each row
i\in[N^{(-j)}]
of
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
to a
(d-1)
-tuple
(i_{d},\ldots,i_{j+1},i_{j-1},\ldots,i_{1})
, with
i_{k}\in[N_{k}]
,
k\neq j,
which indexes rows of the factor matrices in the KRP:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{Z}}(i,:)=\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)}(i_{d},:)*\cdots*\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j+1)}(i_{j+1},:)*\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j-1)}(i_{j-1},:)*\cdots*\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)}(i_{1},:).

(45)

Using
(
49

, the leverage scores of the rows of
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
are bounded above by the product of the leverage scores of the rows of its constituent factor matrices:

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(\bm{\mathsf{Z}})\leq\hat{\ell_{i}}(\bm{\mathsf{Z}}):=\prod_{k\neq j}\ell_{i_{k}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(k)}\right).

(46)

From (
46
), we define a probability distribution on the rows of
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
:

\displaystyle p_{i}=\hat{\ell_{i}}(\bm{\mathsf{Z}})/R^{d-1}.

(47)

Computing the probabilities in (
47
) explicitly would cost
O(N^{(-j)})
operations.
However,
(
127

establishes that each
i_{k}
in the

\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(k)}

, which cost

to compute.
This gives a multi-index corresponding to the

\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
without computing the Kronecker product of leverage scores, and
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}(i,:)
can be formed in
O(R(d-1))
operations using (
45

There are additional modifications that can be made to CP-ARLS-LEV to further improve performance; see
127

The CP-ARLS-LEV algorithm enjoys nice theoretical guarantees.
Namely, the resulting rank-
R

\varepsilon

(

In particular, CP-ARLS-LEV improves on the sampling complexity
required by the KFJLT-based method of

A distributed-memory implementation of CP-ARLS-LEV is also developed in

The recent work of

This strategy yields a sampling distribution closer to the exact leverage score distribution, but performs similarly to CP-ARLS-LEV in practice, and its implementation details are beyond the scope of the current manuscript.
There has also been success in using stochastic gradient descent on the least squares objective function in (

6.2
Randomized Algorithms for Tucker Decompositions

The Tucker decomposition provides a natural framework for data compression.
As randomized algorithms, particularly the randomized SVD (Algorithm
3
), have become mainstays for fast and reliable matrix compression, it is natural to consider how randomization can be leveraged for fast and reliable tensor compression.

Randomized algorithms for Tucker decompositions have been investigated in many problem settings, generally involving either randomized sampling or sketching.
One of the earliest works on randomized HOSVD and HOOI algorithms is
193
, called the MACH-HOSVD and MACH-HOOI.
In MACH-HOSVD and MACH-HOOI, the input tensor is sparsified according to a coin flip for each nonzero entry; with probability
p
, a nonzero entry is kept and reweighted by
1/p
, and with probability
1-p
, the entry is set to 0.
Leverage score sampling of matrix unfoldings to sparsify the tensor is explored in
165
, and Tucker decompositions of sub-sampled tensors are also investigated in
153
;
200
.
A detailed survey of these methods can be found in
4
.

As the randomized rangefinder gained traction in the numerical linear algebra community, many investigations began to explore randomized sketching for fast Tucker decompositions.
In
208
, the RandTucker algorithm is presented, which relies on the randomized rangefinder to compute an HOSVD from Gaussian sketches of mode unfoldings.
Formal analysis of RandTucker is performed in
79
, which incorporates power iteration into RandTucker for faster spectral decay; it is noted, though, that uniform sampling instead of Gaussian sketching performs similarly and may be preferable for large problems.
Power iteration and randomized sketching are utilized in the related work of
39
;
40
for randomized HOSVD and HOOI, which build on the rank-adaptive algorithm of
43
that computes a basis for the range of each mode unfolding with
\varepsilon
-accuracy.

In
181
, a single-pass algorithm based on the HOSVD is developed that significantly improves on the cost of storing the random sketching matrices, via Khatri-Rao products of random dimension reduction maps (called tensor random projections); details can also be found in
187
.
The Sketch-STHOSVD algorithm of
64
is inspired by
181
but incorporates power iteration, whereas the Tucker-TS and Tucker-TTMTS algorithms of
134
are single-pass variants of HOOI using TensorSketch
157
.
Cost-effective sketching for Tucker decompositions is also investigated in
26
, which replaces the randomized SVD step in computing approximate bases with generalized Nyström.
In the recent work of
96
, the Randomized Tucker with Single-Mode Sketching (RTSMS) algorithm is introduced as a rank-adaptive method that sequentially builds approximate bases of unfoldings by applying small random sketches one mode at a time, similar to the randomized ST-HOSVD of
145
, which we describe next.

Randomized (ST-)HOSVD

The randomized HOSVD is a natural extension of the HOSVD, where we substitute the SVD of each unfolding with the randomized SVD (Algorithm
3
).
The R-HOSVD algorithm of
145
is precisely Algorithm
6
, but with a sketch instead of the unfolding.

If
\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}
is the output of the randomized HOSVD, with Gaussian sketching of each mode-
j
unfolding, the expected error is within
\approx\sqrt{d(k+1)}
of the optimal rank-
(k,\ldots,k)
Tucker approximation error, cf.
(
145
, Theorem 3.1)
.
Letting
n
be the uniform mode size again, the computational cost of R-STHOSVD is
O(\sum_{j=1}^{d}k^{j}n^{d-j+1}+k^{j}n^{d-j})
, where the first term is the cost of computing SVDs of mode unfoldings and the second term is the cost of forming the core tensor.
By contrast, the R-HOSVD algorithm costs
O(dkn^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}k^{j}n^{d-j+1})
operations.
In other words, the cost is reduced roughly by a factor of
n
with Gaussian sketching.
Performance can be improved by processing the largest modes first; while the resulting error does depend on processing order, the worst-case expected error does not, cf.
(
145
, Sect. 3.3)
.

Randomized HOOI

Recall from Section
5.3
that HOOI computes Tucker decompositions iteratively by solving least-squares problems in each mode for the factor matrix, generally more accurate than the HOSVD but more expensive.
Similar to our goal in CP-ALS, for a given input
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
, we are seeking
\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}=\bm{\mathcal{G}}\times_{1}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)}\cdots\times_{d}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)}
that minimizes

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\mathcal{G}},\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)}}\|\bm{\mathcal{X}}-\bm{\mathcal{G}}\times_{1}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)}\cdots\times_{d}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)}\|,

(48)

where
\bm{\mathcal{G}}\in\mathbb{F}^{R_{1}\times\cdots\times R_{d}}
and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{j}\times R_{j}}
.
We can re-write the HOOI procedure in Algorithm
7
as two main steps that are repeated until convergence; cf.
134
:

1.

For
j=1,\ldots,d
, solve
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j)}=\underset{\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{j}\times R_{j}}}{\arg\min}\|\bm{\mathsf{Z}}^{(j)}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(j)}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{*}-\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}^{*}\|^{2}
, where

\bm{\mathsf{Z}}^{(j)}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j+1)}\otimes\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j-1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)},

2.

Update
\bm{\mathcal{G}}=\underset{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{F}^{R_{1}\times R_{d}}}{\arg\min}\|\bm{\mathsf{Z}}\bm{\mathsf{y}}-\bm{\mathsf{x}}\|^{2}
, where
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)}\otimes\cdots\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)}
, and
\bm{\mathsf{y}},\bm{\mathsf{x}}
are (column) vectorizations of
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}
,
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
, respectively.

We observe that each of these two steps involve large overdetermined least squares problems, and similar to the strategy in CP-ARLS-LEV, we will exploit the matrix product structures to solve smaller sketched least squares problems efficiently, now with TensorSketch.

TensorSketch

TensorSketch
157
;
168
;
10
;
63
is a specialized version of CountSketch.
A CountSketch operator can be defined as a linear map
\bm{\mathsf{S}}:\mathbb{R}^{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{J}

given by
\bm{\mathsf{S}}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}\bm{\mathsf{D}}
, where
\bm{\mathsf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times I}
is a matrix with

\bm{\mathsf{P}}(h(i),i)=1
and all other entries zero, for a random map
h:[I]\rightarrow[J]
such that
\mathbb{P}[h(i)=j]=1/J
for all
i\in[I]
,
j\in[J]
.
The matrix
\bm{\mathsf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times I}
is a diagonal matrix of
\pm 1
with equal probability.

The CountSketch operator
\bm{\mathsf{S}}
can be rapidly applied to a matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
in
O(\textup{nnz}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}))
operations, without explicitly forming
\bm{\mathsf{S}}
or
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.
If
\bm{\mathsf{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times I}
and
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}^{I\times L}
, typically
J\ll I
, we form
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}=\bm{\mathsf{S}}\bm{\mathsf{A}}
by hashing each row
i
of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
with an integer
h
sampled uniformly from
[J]
, assigning it a value
s
of
\pm 1
with equal probability, then summing the rows with the same hash value (i.e. adding
s\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{i,:}
to the
h
th row of
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
if
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{i,:}
has hash value
h
).

A TensorSketch operator is a specific type of CountSketch operator, which can be rapidly applied to Kronecker products, e.g.,
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)}\in\mathbb{F}^{n^{d}\times k^{d}}
.
If
\bm{\mathsf{T}}:\mathbb{R}^{n^{d}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
is a TensorSketch operator, then the cost of sketching
\bm{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
is
O(\ell dnk+\ell k^{d})
, suppressing log factors, instead of
O(\ell n^{d}k^{d})
for naive multiplication.
This is accomplished by sketching each factor matrix with independent CountSketch operators, then convolving with Fast Fourier Transforms (FFTs).

Let
h_{j}:[n]\rightarrow[\ell]
for
j=1,\ldots,d
be 3-wise independent hash functions, i.e. for any distinct
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\in[n]
, the hash codes
h_{j}(i_{1})
,
h_{j}(i_{2})
,
h_{j}(i_{3})
are
\textup{Uniform}\{[\ell]\}
.
Let
s_{j}:[n]\rightarrow\{+1,-1\}
be 4-wise independent sign functions (i.e.
\textup{Uniform}\{\pm 1\}
).
We form independent CountSketch matrices
\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{(j)}=\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{(j)}\bm{\mathsf{D}}^{(j)}
for
j=1,\ldots,d
, using the hash functions
h_{j}
to form
\bm{\mathsf{P}}^{(j)}
and
s_{j}(i)=\bm{\mathsf{D}}^{(j)}_{i,i}
for
i\in[n]
.
Applying
\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{(j)}
to each column
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j)}_{:,r_{j}}
, for
r_{j}=1,\ldots,k
, can be represented by an
(\ell-1)
-degree polynomial

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{(j)}_{r_{j}}(\omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{j}(i)\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j)}_{i,r_{j}}\omega^{h_{j}(i)-1}=:\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}c^{(j)}_{l,r_{j}}\omega^{j-1},

(49)

where
\bm{\mathsf{c}}_{r_{j}}^{(j)}
are coefficients
(c^{(j)}_{1,r_{j}},\ldots,c^{(j)}_{\ell,r_{j}})
, grouped in (
49
) by hash value.

The TensorSketch operator
\bm{\mathsf{T}}
is defined as the CountSketch operator comprised of hash function
H
and sign function
S
given by

\displaystyle H:[n]^{d}\rightarrow\ell,\ \ (i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\mapsto\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}(h_{j}(i_{j})-1)\mod\ell\right)+1,

(50)

\displaystyle S:[n]^{d}\rightarrow\{+1,-1\},\ \ (i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\mapsto\prod_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}(i_{j}).

(51)

The
r
-th column of
\bm{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)})=\bm{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
,
r=1,\ldots,k^{d}
, is the polynomial

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{r}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{d}}S(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\bm{\mathsf{Z}}_{i,r}\omega^{H(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})},

(52)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{d}}s_{1}(i_{1})\cdots s_{d}(i_{d})\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)}_{i_{1},r_{1}}\cdots\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(d)}_{i_{d},r_{d}}\omega^{(h_{1}(i_{1})+\cdots+h_{d}(i_{d})-d)\mod\ell}

(53)

\displaystyle=\textup{FFT}^{-1}\left(\textup{FFT}(\bm{\mathsf{c}}^{(1)}_{r_{1}})*\cdots*\textup{FFT}(\bm{\mathsf{c}}_{r_{d}}^{(d)}\right),

(54)

where
*
denotes the Hadamard product,
i
in (
52
) corresponds to the tuple
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in[n]^{d}
, and
r
corresponds to the tuple
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})\in[k]^{d}
.
In other words,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathsf{Z}}=\textup{FFT}^{-1}\left(\left({\bigodot}_{j=1}^{d}\left(\textup{FFT}\left(\bm{\mathsf{S}}^{(j)}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(j)}\right)\right)^{*}\right)^{*}\right),

(55)

where
\odot
is the KRP, and the FFT is applied column-wise.
Instead of the usual
O(\ell n^{d}k^{d})
cost of matrix multiplication, the cost of
\bm{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathsf{Z}}
is
O(\ell dnk+\ell k^{d}\log(\ell))
using (
55
).

Using TensorSketch in HOOI

We now turn our attention back to the randomized HOOI algorithm of
134
, called TUCKER-TS, which uses TensorSketch in each least squares subproblem in the two main steps above.
In Step 1, the factor matrix sub-problems for modes
j=1,\ldots,d
are highly overdetermined, involving an
n^{d-1}\times k^{d-1}
Kronecker product
\bm{\mathsf{Z}}^{(j)}
.
The core tensor sub-problem in Step 2 is also highly overdetermined, involving a
n^{d}\times k^{d}
Kronecker product.
For both steps, we form independent TensorSketches to solve smaller sketched least squares problems, as in Sect.
3.3
.
TUCKER-TS can also be made single-pass; cf.
(
134
, Algorithm S3)
.

The dominant cost of randomized HOOI with TensorSketch (TUCKER-TS) is
O(dnk^{2}+\ell dk^{d}+\ell_{c}k^{d}+k^{2d})
, or roughly
k^{O(d)}
, versus the dominant cost of
O(n^{d})
for classical HOOI (Algorithm
7
), suppressing
\log
factors and letting
\ell=\ell_{1}=\cdots=\ell_{d}
.
See
(
134
, S3.2.6)
for details on the complexity analysis.
We note that the single-pass algorithm presented in
134
uses the same TensorSketch operators on the updated Kronecker products until some convergence criteria is met.
For the theoretical guarantees of
63
to hold, it is technically necessary to form new independent TensorSketch operators in each iteration; however, in practice, it is noted in
134
that re-using TensorSketch operators is more efficient and works just as well.
Their numerical results suggest
\ell=Ck^{d-1}
and
\ell_{c}=Cr^{d}
for constant
C>4
(e.g.,
C=10
) are usually appropriate.

Randomized TSMS

We end this section by highlighting the recent work of
96
on Randomized Tucker decompositions with Single-Mode Sketching (RTSMS).
The RTSMS algorithm sketches the input tensor one mode at a time, i.e. sketching on the left of the mode-
j
unfolding vs. sketching on the right, to avoid the computational bottleneck of multiplying along the larger dimension
N^{(-j)}\equiv n^{d-1}
.
The algorithm is also single-pass and rank-adaptive.

To illustrate, suppose we want to compute a rank-
(R_{1},R_{2},R_{3})
HOSVD approximating
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times N_{3}}
.
(We omit rank-adaptivity and iterative refinement; see
96
for practical implementation details.)
We initialize tensors

\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\textup{old}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}\ \textup{and}\ \bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\textup{new}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}\times_{1}\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{(1)}\iff\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\textup{new}}_{(1)}=\left(\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{(1)}\right)^{*}\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(1)}\in\mathbb{F}^{\tilde{R_{1}}\times(N_{2}N_{3})}

for some sketching dimension
\tilde{R_{1}}>R_{1}
.
There exists a factor matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times\tilde{R_{1}}}
such that
\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\textup{new}}\approx\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\textup{old}}\times_{1}\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(1)}
.
In practice, this is computed as an approximate solution of the corresponding least squares problem, which is being sub-sampled from the right, cf.
(
96
, Section 4.2)
.
We update
\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\textup{old}}=\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\textup{new}}
, and for the next mode, update
\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\textup{new}}
via

\bm{\mathsf{G}}^{\textup{new}}_{(2)}=\left(\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{(2)}\right)^{*}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(2)}^{\textup{old}}\in\mathbb{F}^{\tilde{R_{2}}\times(\tilde{R_{1}}N_{3})}

for some sketching dimension
\tilde{R_{2}}>R_{2}
.
To compute
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{(2)}
, we solve a smaller sketched least squares problem of size
\tilde{R_{2}}\times(\tilde{R_{1}}N_{3})
, and repeat this for the final mode.

The computational complexity of RTSMS is dominated by the first TTM
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\times_{1}\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}_{1}
.
The dominant cost is
O(kn^{d})
to compute the first TTM, but the sketch size is only
k\times n
vs.
k\times n^{d-1}
for R-HOSVD.
The least squares solves are also significantly reduced by sub-sampling and by computing a generalized Nyström decomposition vs. SVD.
The full tensor does not need to be sketched again in later modes, and the core tensor is available immediately upon termination.
Additional details can be found in
96
.

6.3
Randomized Algorithms for Tensor ID/CUR Decompositions

As we observed in Sect.
5.3
, the tensor ID/CUR decomposition has been formulated in many ways in the literature, with no one standard representation.
However, there exists a natural categorization of tensor ID/CUR decompositions, based on whether the factor matrices or core tensors in the computed decompositions are comprised of entries of the input tensor.
We discuss randomized algorithms to compute a Tucker ID/CUR decomposition from each category, as well as recent work on CP tensor ID/CUR.

Randomized Algorithms for Tucker Core ID/CUR

We first consider randomized algorithms for Tucker tensor ID/CUR factorizations of the first type, where the core tensor is a sub-tensor of the input tensor.
In particular, we focus on the tensor ID/CUR decompositions that were recently characterized in
27
, termed the “Chidori” and “Fiber” tensor CUR factorizations.
This work was inspired by numerous investigations of tensor CUR decompositions, including
29
;
73
;
132
.

In
27
, given
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
,
the rank-
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})
tensor CUR factorization is

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}=\bm{\mathcal{G}}\times_{1}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{1}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{1}^{\dagger})\times_{2}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{2}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{2}^{\dagger})\times\cdots\times_{d}(\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{d}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{d}^{\dagger}),

(56)

where the rank-
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})
core tensor is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{G}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}(I_{1},\ldots,I_{d}),

(57)

for index sets
I_{j}\subset[N_{j}]
,
j=1,\ldots,d
.
The mode-
j
factor matrix
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{j}\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{\dagger}
is defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{j}&=\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}(:,J_{j}),\ \ \textup{and}\\
\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{j}&=\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{j}(I_{j},:),\end{split}

(58)

where
J_{j}\subset[N^{(-j)}]
.
If indices
J_{j}
are computed independently from indices
I_{j}
, the decomposition in (
56
) is called a “Fiber” CUR decomposition in
27
.
If
J_{j}=\otimes_{k\neq j}I_{k}
, then (
56
) is called a “Chidori” CUR decomposition.

By
(
27
, Theorem 3.3)
,
(
93
, Corollary 5.2)
, if the indices
I_{j}
and
J_{j}
are each sampled uniformly with
|I_{j}|=O(R_{j}\log N_{j})
and
|J_{j}|=O(R_{j}\log N^{(-j)})
, then the computed Tucker decomposition is a rank-
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})
approximation with high probability, under certain incoherence assumptions on the input tensor; cf.
65
.
With uniform sampling, the computational complexity of computing either CUR decomposition is dominated by the cost of
\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{j}^{\dagger}
, which is
O(k^{d+1}\log^{d}(n))
for Chidori and
O(k^{2}\log^{2}(n))
for Fiber, letting
n=N_{1}=\cdots=N_{d}
and
k=R_{1}=\cdots R_{d}
as before.

The recent work of
207
proposes a similar factorization to (
56
), called the “CoreID.”
The CoreID algorithm is reminiscent of the “structure-preserving” ST-HOSVD developed for sparse input tensors in
145
.
Namely, in the CoreID, as each factor matrix is computed, the input tensor is compressed along that mode before proceeding to the next one, so that each subsequent unfolding is reduced in size.
Through matricization, computing the CoreID is reduced to computing a randomized matrix row ID in each mode, cf. Algorithm
4
.
For
j=1,\ldots,d
, the mode-
j
unfolding of the tensor is sketched by a random matrix
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}_{j}=\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}\bm{\mathsf{\Omega}}_{j}.

Then a row ID of
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}_{j}
is computed by some skeleton selection method.
The selected skeletons
I_{j}
form the core sub-tensor, and the mode-
j
factor matrix for the CoreID is defined as the associated interpolation matrix.
The tensor is then compressed along mode
j
before proceeding to the next.
Sketching is the dominant cost, so many of the numerical experiments in
207
consider structured input tensors for fast sketching, e.g., a sparse input tensor or a CP-factorized input tensor.

Randomized Algorithms for Tucker Factor ID/CUR

We now turn our attention to randomized algorithms for tensor ID/CUR factorizations in the Tucker format in which the factor matrices contain entries of the input tensor.
We detail the “higher-order ID” of
175
, contrasted with the “SatID” of
207
.

For a given
\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}
, the higher-order ID (HOID) of
175
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}=\bm{\mathcal{G}}\times_{1}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{1}\times_{2}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{2}\times\cdots\times_{d}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{d},

(59)

where the rank-
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})
core tensor
\bm{\mathcal{G}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{G}}=\bm{\mathcal{X}}\times_{1}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{1}^{\dagger}\times_{2}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{2}^{\dagger}\times\cdots\times_{d}\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{d}^{\dagger},

(60)

and each factor
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{j}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{j}\times R_{j}}
consists of column skeletons of the mode-
j
unfolding
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}
.
The core tensor in (
60
) is optimal for the factorization in (
59
), cf.
54
.
For the theoretical guarantees of
175
, the strong rank-revealing QR algorithm of
88
is used for skeleton selection on the random sketch of
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}
.
In practice, LUPP in the randomized matrix ID (i.e., Algorithm
4
, with input
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}^{*}
) performs similarly and is less expensive.

For each mode
j
, the cost of sketching is
O(R_{j}N_{j}N^{(-j)})\equiv O(kn^{d})
.
Skeleton selection as in
175
costs
O(R_{j}^{2}N^{(-j)})\equiv O(k^{2}n^{d-1})
.
The total cost is roughly
O(dkn^{d}+dk^{d-1}n^{d-1})
after computing the core tensor.
A sequentially-truncated version of the HOID (ST-HOID), analogous to the ST-HOSVD, is given in
(
175
, Algorithm 4)
.

A very similar decomposition is developed in
207
, termed the “SatID.”
For an
n^{d}
-sized tensor, a QR-based algorithm to compute skeletons for the mode-
j
factor matrix as in
175
requires
n^{d-1}
column norms, which is not optimal for structured tensors and can even have larger storage costs than the input.
By contrast, the SatID algorithm is based on a randomized matrix ID with marginalized norm sampling, which can be viewed as an extension of the original tensor CUR of
73
that uses norm sampling.

We briefly summarize the marginalized norm sampling procedure.
Recall that we can uniquely identify a column index
i\in[N^{(-j)}]
of
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}
with a (
d-1
)-tuple
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{j-1},i_{j+1},\ldots,i_{d})
.
Similar to CP-ARLS-LEV in
127
, the idea is to accelerate sampling of
i\in[N^{(-j)}]
, now based on column norm sampling of
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}
.

Suppose we have already selected some subset of column indices
S
of
\bm{\mathsf{X}}=\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{(j)}
to determine the mode-
j
column skeletons comprising
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{j}
, and we want to select the next column.
Norm sampling (cf. Sect.
3.2
) requires the computation of column scores

\displaystyle d_{i}^{(S)}=\|\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{:,i}-\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{S}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{S}^{*}\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{:,i}\|^{2}=\min_{\bm{\mathsf{v}}}\|\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{:,S}\bm{\mathsf{v}}-\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{:,i}\|^{2},\ \ i\in[N^{(-j)}],

(61)

where
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{S}
is an orthonormal basis for the range of
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{:,S}
.
If
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{j-1},X_{j+1},\ldots,X_{d})
is a random variable representing the next column index, from (
61
),
\mathbb{P}(X=i)\propto d_{i}^{(S)}.

The key observation is that

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=i_{1})\propto\sum_{i_{2},\ldots,i_{d}}d_{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{d}}^{(S)},

so that we can sample
i_{1}
from its marginal distribution, then sample
i_{2}
given
i_{1}
, and continue for all of
i=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{j-1},i_{j+1},\ldots,i_{d})
.
This sum can be computed efficiently via the equation derived in
(
207
, Eq. 18)
:

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=i_{1})\propto\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{X}}}_{:,i_{1}}\|^{2},

where
\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{X}}}=\left(\bm{\mathcal{X}}\times_{j}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{S^{c}}\right)_{(1)}^{*}
with
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}_{S^{c}}
an orthonormal basis for the range of
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{:,[N^{(-j)}]\backslash S}
.
In other words, to sample
i_{1}
, we need only compute the
N_{1}
column norms of
\widetilde{\bm{\mathsf{X}}}
, which can be approximated efficiently using randomized sketching, cf.
(
207
, Algorithm 6)
.
Once
i_{1}
has been sampled, this process is repeated on the sliced tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}(i_{1},:,\ldots,:)
.

For structured inputs, this procedure can be modified for better performance.
The total complexity (including randomized sketching) is
O(\textup{nnz}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})+\ell n^{2}k^{2})
for a rank-
(k,\ldots,k)
SatID of an
n^{d}
-sized tensor with sketch size
\ell
; see
(
207
, Algorithm 7)
.

Randomized Algorithms for CP Factor ID/CUR

We now consider tensor ID/CUR decompositions in the CP format, which necessarily fall into the category of decompositions in which the factor matrices contain entries of the input tensor.
These decompositions can be interpreted as the result of pruning the input tensor, leaving only its most important rank-one components.

The seminal work of
20
introduces a method to obtain rank-
k
CP-tensor IDs from rank-
R
CP-factorized tensors, given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{X}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bm{\mathsf{a}}^{(1)}_{r}\circ\cdots\circ\bm{\mathsf{a}}^{(d)}_{r}\in\mathbb{F}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{d}}.

(62)

To better illustrate the algorithm, we express (
62
) in a vectorized form, where
\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{r}\in\mathbb{F}^{N\times 1}
is a vectorization of the rank-one component
\bm{\mathsf{a}}^{(1)}_{r}\circ\cdots\circ\bm{\mathsf{a}}^{(d)}_{r}
.
Then we can consider the matrix

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{1}\ \vline&\cdots&\vline\ \bm{\mathsf{X}}_{R}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}^{N\times R},

(63)

and within this framework, we are seeking a rank-
k
matrix column ID of
\bm{\mathsf{X}}
, corresponding to a rank-
k
CP decomposition
\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}
denoted by

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{s_{r}},

(64)

for
k
column skeletons of (
63
) indexed by
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}\subset[R]
.
In particular, we want to select these
k
indices without explicitly forming the matrix
\bm{\mathsf{X}}
.

To do this, the randomized CP compression algorithm of
20
uses rank-one random tensors
\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{r}^{(1)}\circ\cdots\circ\bm{\mathsf{r}}_{r}^{(d)}
, with vectorized form
\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{r}
for
r=1,\ldots,\ell
and
\ell=k+p
for some small oversampling parameter
p
.
An
\ell\times R
random sample matrix is formed via

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{Y}}=\begin{bmatrix}\langle\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{1},\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{1}\rangle&\langle\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{1},\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{2}\rangle&\cdots&\langle\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{1},\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{R}\rangle\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\langle\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\ell},\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{1}\rangle&\langle\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\ell},\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{2}\rangle&\cdots&\langle\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{\ell},\bm{\mathsf{X}}_{R}\rangle\end{bmatrix}=:\bm{\mathsf{R}}\bm{\mathsf{X}},

(65)

whose rank-
k
column ID is then computed to obtain skeletons
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}
as in (
64
).
We note that the matrix
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
can be computed efficiently without ever explicitly forming
\bm{\mathsf{R}}
or
\bm{\mathsf{X}}
, since each
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}(l,r)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(j)}_{l},\bm{\mathsf{a}}^{(j)}_{r}\rangle
.
However, we observe that the elements of
\bm{\mathsf{R}}
are not independent, and so theoretical guarantees on the randomized matrix decomposition computed with
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
are not given, though there is evidence that it performs well in practice
20
.

To analyze the cost of this algorithm, let
n=N_{1}=\cdots=N_{d}
.
Forming the
\ell
random tensors costs
O(t_{R}\ell dn)
, where
t_{R}
is the cost of generating a single random number.
The cost of computing each entry of
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
is
O(dn)
, thus the total cost to compute
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
is
O(\ell R\cdot dn)
.
The rank-
k
matrix ID of
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
comes at a cost of
O(k\ell R)
, and forming the rank-
k
tensor ID from the rank-one terms of
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
requires
O(dkn)
additional operations.
In particular, the method of
20
is faster than a rank-
k
ALS procedure by a factor of
k\cdot n_{iter}
, where
n_{iter}
is the number of iterations of ALS.

The recent work of
135
constructs an identical rank-
k
CP approximation to a rank-
R
CP-factorized tensor
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
, but uses the TensorSketch operator instead of rank-one random tensors.
The complexity of the TensorSketch-based algorithm of
135
is dominated by the sketching step, which costs
O(d(nR+R\ell\log\ell))
; the ID of the
\ell\times R
matrix
\bm{\mathsf{Y}}
costs the same as above.
In addition to its theoretical performance guarantees, the algorithm’s practical performance is demonstrated through numerical experiments, in which the TensorSketch-based method exhibits a runtime speed-up of roughly one order of magnitude over other randomized CP-tensor ID algorithms, including the random projection method of
20
.

7
Concluding remarks

The primary purpose of this survey was to review a set of recently developed randomized
algorithms for processing large scale tensor data.
These new methods were in many cases inspired by analogous techniques for processing
matrices that exploit randomized dimension reducing maps to accelerate computations.
While such methods for the matrix case are reaching a certain state of maturity,
the tensor environment is still evolving rapidly.
In our survey, our focus was on techniques that are relatively straight-forward
generalizations of algorithms designed for matrices.

We did not attempt to describe the plethora of algorithms that are designed for
more tensor-specific representations, such as, for instance, tensor trains.
Nor did we cover techniques that are designed for tensors that are so large
they they cannot be formed or stored explicitly.

We observe that much of the success of RNLA stems from the fact that randomized algorithms
often involve less data movement, and therefore execute faster on modern hardware than
many traditional algorithms that were designed to minimize floating point operations.
Such considerations have proven to be even more essential in the tensor case, given the
often enormous size of the data sets under consideration, and the stronger tension between
data being located close by in the tensor on the one hand, and being close in physical
memory on the other.
