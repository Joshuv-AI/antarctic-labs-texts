Scalable dissipative quantum error correction for discrete-variable codes Preprint: APS/123-QED
Ivan Rojkov, Elias Zapusek, Florentin Reiter

Abstract
Dissipative quantum error correction (QEC) autonomously protects quantum information using engineered dissipation and offers a promising alternative to error correction via measurement and feedback. However, scalability remains a challenge, as correcting high-weight errors typically requires increasing dissipation rates and exponentially many correction operators. Here, we present a scalable dissipative QEC protocol for discrete-variable codes, correcting multi-qubit errors via a trickle-down mechanism that sequentially reduces errors weight. Our construction exploits redundancy in the Knill–Laflamme conditions to design correction operators that act on multiple error subspaces simultaneously, thereby reducing the overhead from exponential to polynomial in the number of required operators. We illustrate our approach with repetition codes under biased noise, showing a fourfold improvement in the exponential suppression factor at realistic physical error rates. Our approach connects autonomous QEC for discrete-variable codes with demonstrated dissipative protocols for bosonic codes and opens up new avenues for traditional measurement-feedback QEC and fault-tolerant quantum operations.

Discrete-variable codes, i.e. those relying on a redundant use of two-level systems, represent another class of error correcting codes for which scalable architectures have recently been demonstrated using various technologies such as trapped ions, neutral atoms, superconducting circuits

Shtanko et al. 2023

As a result, until now, the existence of scalable dissipative schemes remains an open question.

In this work, we present a scalable construction of dissipative QEC schemes for discrete-variable systems, designed to correct high-weight errors that go beyond single-qubit noise. Our schemes operate in a
trickle-down
fashion: the system is driven through a sequence of lower-weight error states until it reaches the codespace. This construction is made possible by our observation that the Knill–Laflamme conditions
Knill and Laflamme 1997
, which typically relate the codespace to individual error subspaces, also apply between different error subspaces themselves. If two such subspace pairs satisfy the conditions identically, then the same operator that corrects one pair can also correct the other. This enables the design of correction operators that simultaneously reduce the error weight of multiple subspaces simultaneously, significantly lowering the experimental overhead. Importantly, we show that this overhead reduction can be exponential, with the number of required correction operators scaling only polynomially with the code size. Given that many quantum codes are built from repetition codes, we illustrate our approach using a scalable dissipative correction scheme for repetition codes in biased noise environments

\sim 4
times for a physical error rate of
10^{-2}
, thereby reducing the qubit overhead needed to reach a target logical error rate. Finally, we relate our approach to measurement-feedback QEC methods and to the recent developments in bosonic codes. This advancement offers insights into autonomously error-protected quantum sensors, memories and processing units.

Results

Dissipative QEC

We consider the problem of protecting a logical subspace
\mathsf{C}
of a Hilbert space
\mathsf{H}
from incoherent noise that disturbs the system. We model the dynamics responsible for the errors and their correction using a Lindblad equation

\dot{\rho}=(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{e}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{c}})(\rho),

(1)

where both Liouvillians describe purely incoherent processes

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}(\rho):=\sum_{i}\mathcal{D}[L_{\bullet,i}](\rho)\quad\text{for}\quad\bullet\in\{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{c}\},\end{split}

(2)

with
\mathcal{D}[L](\rho)=L\rho L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left(L^{\dagger}L\rho+\rho L^{\dagger}L\right)
. The set of jump operators
\{L_{\mathrm{e},i}\}
describes the errors caused by undesired couplings of the system to the environment, while the set
\{L_{\mathrm{c},i}\}
represents the correction operators. The goal of the dissipative QEC is to carefully engineer the correction operations in such a way that the logical subspace
\mathsf{C}
is stabilized under the action of the combined Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{e}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{c}}
.

The form of the correction operators is determined by the encoding of the logical subspace
\mathsf{C}
into the physical Hilbert space
\mathsf{H}
. To be more specific, we consider the system to be composed of
n
qubits, i.e.
\mathsf{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
, and assume the code
\sf{C}
to be
k
-dimensional spanned by the logical states
\{\ket{\mu_\n{L}}\}_{\mu=0}^{k-1}
. The code choice is not unique and depends on the error set
\mathbb{E}=\{E_{i}\}
against which we want it to be robust. Note that each
E_{i}
corresponds to the accumulated error operator defined by a product of operators drawn from
\mathbb{L}=\{L_{\mathrm{e},i}\}\!\bigcup\{L_{\mathrm{e},i}^{\dagger}L_{\mathrm{e},i}\}
. To be able to correct these errors, the code
\mathsf{C}
must satisfy the Knill–Laflamme condition
P_{\mathsf{C}}E_{j}^{\dagger}E_{i}P_{\mathsf{C}}=\alpha_{ij}P_{\mathsf{C}}

\forall i,j
with
\alpha_{ij}
being a scalar and
P_{\mathsf{C}}
the projector on the logical subspace
Knill and Laflamme 1997
. This condition ensures that there exist a partitioning of the Hilbert space
\mathsf{H}
into subspaces, each associated with an individual invertible (or approximately invertible
Leung et al. 1997
) error process
\tilde{E}_{i}
. These new error operators are obtained by unitarily transforming the set
\{E_{i}\}
such that
\alpha_{ij}=p_{i}\delta_{ij}
with
p_{i}
quantifying the error detection probability of
\tilde{E}_{i}
. Without loss of generality, we consider
\tilde{E}_{i}\equiv{E}_{i}
. The recovery operation for the code
\mathsf{C}
can then be written as a completely positive trace preserving map

\mathcal{R}(\rho)=\sum_{i}U_{i}^{\dagger}P_{i}\,\rho\,P_{i}U_{i}+P_{\mathsf{E}}\,\rho\,P_{\mathsf{E}}

(3)

where
P_{i}=U_{i}P_{\mathsf{C}}U_{i}^{\dagger}\equiv E_{i}P_{\mathsf{C}}U_{i}^{\dagger}/\sqrt{p_{i}}
and
{P_{\mathsf{E}}=\mathds{1}-\sum_{i}P_{i}}
are the projectors onto the error subspace associated with
E_{i}
and onto the subspace of errors violating the correctability condition, respectively. The unitaries
\{U_{i}\}
are obtained through a polar decomposition of
E_{i}
and, following the Knill–Laflamme condition, are mutually orthogonal
P_{\mathsf{C}}U_{j}^{\dagger}U_{i}P_{\mathsf{C}}=\delta_{ij}P_{\mathsf{C}}

\forall i,j

Leung et al. 1997
. In the measurement-feedback QEC paradigm, a correction protocol consists in coupling the system to an auxiliary one which after being carefully measured projects the state into one of the error subspaces. The measurement information allows then to bring the system’s state back into the codespace through
U_{i}^{\dagger}
. In the dissipative QEC setting, these processes are performed by the incoherent Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{c}}
.

Lookup-table method and its limitations

A naive solution for the correction jump operators is to consider a set
\{L_{\mathrm{c},j}\}
such that

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)=\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathcal{R}(\rho)-\rho)

(4)

with
\mathcal{R}
being the recovery map in Eq. (
3
) and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
the correction rate. This generates a time evolution described by
\rho(t)=e^{-\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}t}\rho(0)+(1-e^{-\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}t})\mathcal{R}(\rho(0))
which ensures that the system converges to the codespace. From the construction of the correction map presented above and assuming that
P_{\mathsf{E}}\equiv 0
, we can see that the jump operators

L_{\mathrm{c},i}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\,U_{i}^{\dagger}\,P_{i}

(5)

realize the desired Liouvillian. If
P_{\mathsf{E}}\neq 0
, additional jump operators are necessary to prevent the system to get stuck in the uncorrected subspace
Lihm et al. 2018
;
Lebreuilly et al. 2021
. However, as noted by
Pastawski et al. 2011
and more recently by
Shtanko et al. 2023
, this solution is suboptimal for several reasons. To illustrate this, let us provide a concrete example.

One of the most studied dissipative QEC codes is the three-qubit bit-flip code
Paz and Zurek 1998
;
Ahn et al. 2002
;
Sarovar and Milburn 2005
;
Ippoliti et al. 2015
;
Reiter et al. 2017
;
Lihm et al. 2018
. In this setting, a single logical qubit is encoded in a system of three qubits using
{\mathsf{C}=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{000},\ket{111}\}}
. It is protected against single qubit bit flips
\{{L_{\mathrm{e},i}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}}\,X_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{3}
, i.e. single-qubit Pauli
X
errors at a rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
, using

L_{\mathrm{c},i}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\,X_{i}\,\frac{1-Z_{i}Z_{j}}{2}\,\frac{1-Z_{i}Z_{l}}{2}

(6)

where
i,j,l\in\{1,2,3\}
. This operator effectively flips the
i
th qubit at a rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}
if and only if its parity disagrees with the one of the two other qubits. In this example,
X_{i}
corresponds to the unitary
U_{i}^{\dagger}
from Eq. (
5
) while the projector
P_{i}
is built using operators
Z_{i}Z_{j}
and
Z_{i}Z_{l}
querying the parity of pairs of qubits. This construction can be straightforwardly generalized to other stabilizer codes
Hsu and Brun 2016
;
Oreshkov 2013
. Every
\llbracket n,k,d\rrbracket
code encoding
k
logical qubits in an
n
-qubit system and robust up to
d
errors lives in a
2^{k}
-dimensional subspace of
\mathsf{H}
that is invariant under the group generated by
n-k
independent operators
S_{i}
. Using these so-called generators, we can construct the set of projectors
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sub}}}
onto
N_{\mathrm{sub}}=2^{n-k}-1
nontrivial and orthogonal error subspaces,

P_{i}=P(\vec{s}_{i}):=\,\prod_{j=1}^{n-k}\,\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+(-1)^{\vec{s}_{i}[j]}\,S_{j}\Big)\,,

(7)

where
{\vec{s}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n-k}}
and
\vec{s}_{i}[j]
the
j
-th element. The set
\{L_{\mathrm{c},i}\}
is then obtained using Eq. (
5
) resulting in
N_{\mathrm{sub}}=\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
jump operators. For non-stabilizer codes, a similar approach can be employed by introducing additional preventive jump operators that map each state in the orthonormal basis spanning the uncorrectable subspace into the correctable subspace
Lihm et al. 2018
;
Lebreuilly et al. 2021
. However, in this case, the number of required jump operators also scales exponentially with the system size.

This highlights a key limitation of the standard dissipative QEC framework. While the general construction in Eq. (
5
) allows the dissipative correction of
\mathsf{C}
, it comes at a significant operational cost: the number of required jump operators scales as
\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
, where
n
is the number of qubits encoding the quantum information. Since each
L_{\mathrm{c},i}
can be implemented using
\mathcal{O}(n)
auxiliary qubits, this results in a tradeoff between space and time complexity. Specifically, one either requires an exponentially large auxiliary system with corrective jumps executed in parallel, or an exponentially long time to implement the correction map
\mathcal{R}
by recycling the auxiliary qubits. This limitation is also observed in measurement-feedback quantum error correction. Indeed, what we constructed here is a lookup-table type decoder which is known to be highly inefficient because of the exponentially growing table with the code size (we will discuss approximate decoders later on). All this naturally raises a critical question: Can dissipative QEC be achieved using only a polynomial number of jump operators, and what are the necessary conditions for this to be feasible?

Before we answer these questions, we must highlight a second, more fundamental limitation of the Liouvillian in Eq. (
4
). Let us consider the quantum trajectories picture of the dynamics
Dalibard et al. 1992
;
Carmichael 2008
which is equivalent to the Lindblad equation
Mølmer et al. 1993
. In this setting, the system is subject to a coherent evolution given by the so-called non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{NH}}=-i/2\sum L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}
that is interleaved with incoherent jumps
L_{j}
. The dynamics is discretized in
\delta t
steps after which a quantum state
\ket{\psi(t)}
is updated to a non-normalized state
\ket{\psi(t+\delta t)}
whose norm is reduced
\delta p=i\,\delta t\expectationvalue{H_\n{NH}-H_\n{NH}^\dagger}{\psi(t)}
. The occurrence of a quantum jump follows a Bernoulli process with a probability
\delta p
where each
L_{j}
occurs with a probability
\delta p_{j}/\delta p
and
\delta p_{j}=\delta t\expectationvalue{L_j^\dagger L_j}{\psi(t)}
. Let us now consider the simple situation of a system subjected to unitary error operators
L_{\mathrm{e},i}
each occurring at a rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
as well as to a dissipative correction with jumps given in Eq. (
5
). Denoting
N_{e}
the number of different errors, the probability of any jump to occur at a time
t
equals

\delta p=\delta t\left(N_{\mathrm{e}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}\right)

(8)

among which the probabilities that this jump corresponds to an error and correction are

p_{\mathrm{e}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{e}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}}{N_{\mathrm{e}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\quad\text{and}\quad p_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}{N_{\mathrm{e}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}+\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\,.

(9)

This illustrates the second limitation of established dissipative QEC models: if the number of noise processes
L_{e,i}
increases polynomially with the system size, the correction rate must scale as
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n))
to maintain a constant ratio
p_{\mathrm{e}}/p_{\mathrm{c}}
. In other words, for a given error rate and correction rate, the time between recovery operations and, consequently, the logical error rate, remain constant and independent of the code
C
. This observation has been noted in Refs.
Pastawski et al. 2011
;
Shtanko et al. 2023
. In the latter work, the authors derived explicit bounds on the logical error rate and concluded that dissipative qubit codes, employing a Liouvillian of the form given in Eq. (
4
), can achieve faster-than-polynomial reduction of the logical error rate when
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}\sim\mathcal{O}(\log(n))
.

Issues with alternative solutions

An alternative solution to this problem is to consider dissipative QEC processes described by a more general Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{c}}
than the construction in Eq. (
4
). Indeed, although the time evolution generated by Eq. (
4
) results in a steady state that lives in the logical subspace, at intermediate times the system’s state remains mainly unchanged. Quantitatively, the trace distance between
\rho(t)
and the steady state
\mathcal{R}(\rho(0))
evolves independently of the code
\mathsf{C}
, i.e.
\norm{\rho(t)-\cl{R}(\rho(0))}_{1}=e^{-\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}t}\norm{\rho(0)-\cl{R}(\rho(0))}_{1}
. In order to have a super-exponential convergence in the distance, a Liouvillian should instead generate a time evolution of the form

\rho(t)=e^{-\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}t}\,\mathcal{K}_{t}(\rho(0))+(1-e^{-\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}t})\mathcal{R}(\rho(0))

(10)

with
\mathcal{K}_{t}
being a time-dependent CPTP map with a nontrivial action on the error subspaces. When
{\norm{\cl{K}_{t}(\rho(0))-\cl{R}(\rho(0))}_{1}<\norm{\cl{K}_{t'}(\rho(0))-\cl{R}(\rho(0))}_{1}}
for
{t<t^{\prime}}
, we can think of it as a partial recovery map
Shtanko et al. 2023
which reduces the error weight without fully recovering the codespace.
Pastawski et al. 2011
demonstrated that such Liouvillians exist by proposing a construction where the
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{c}}
is a sum of recovery operations acting on different stages of a concatenated QEC code whose strength decay as a power law with the level concatenation, i.e.

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)=\sum_{i}\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{(i)}(\mathcal{R}_{i}(\rho)-\rho)

(11)

where
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{O}(\Gamma^{i})
is the correction rate at the concatenation level
i
(
i=1
being the physical level) and
\Gamma<1
– a constant. Although Ref.
Pastawski et al. 2011
showed that partial recovery maps for dissipative QEC can be constructed when one considers specific concatenation of
\llbracket n,3,1\rrbracket
codes, it remains unclear how to design such Liouvillians for general codes, how they perform against the lookup-table solution in Eq. (
4
), and, crucially, how many corrective jump operators must be engineered and what are the necessary resources.

The trickle-down solution

Devising a correction whose dynamics follows Eq. (
10
) is a non-trivial task that requires a careful design of the jump operators. The starting point of our derivation is to note that, although the number of error subspace
N_{\mathrm{sub}}
scales exponentially with the system size, the number of jump operators
L_{\mathrm{e},i}
often increases only polynomialy with
n
. As mentioned earlier, this difference comes from the fact that the error operators
E_{i}
are arbitrary products of the jump operators drawn from
\mathbb{L}
. We can thus partition
\mathbb{E}
into
i
-th order error subsets
\mathbb{E}_{i}
such that
{\mathbb{E}_{0}=\{\mathds{1}\}}
,
{\mathbb{E}_{1}=\mathbb{L}}
,
\mathbb{E}_{2}=\{O_{i}O_{j}\,|\,{O_{k}\in\mathbb{L}},\,\,{i\neq j}\}
, and
\mathbb{E}=\bigcup_{i}\mathbb{E}_{i}
. We can proceed identically with the error subspaces and their projectors, i.e.
{\mathbb{P}_{0}=\{P_{\mathsf{C}}\}}
,
{\mathbb{P}_{1}=\{P_{i}\text{ associated to }E_{i}\in\mathbb{E}_{1}\}}
, etc. The key observation is that similarly to the Knill–Laflamme condition which sets the relationship between the logical and error subspaces we can derive a condition on the relationship between the error subspaces themselves:

Consider a
q
th-order error subspace with a projector
P_{i}^{(q)}\in\mathbb{P}_{q}
associated to the error
E_{i}^{(q)}\in\mathbb{E}_{q}
. This subspace is transformed to a
(q+p)
th-order error subspace under the action of
p
th-order errors. If the chosen code
\mathsf{C}
is capable of correcting errors up to order
{\ell\geq q+p}
, we get the following condition

\begin{split}&P_{i}^{(q)}E_{j}^{(p)\dagger}E_{k}^{(p)}P_{i}^{(q)}\\
&\propto U_{i}^{(q)}\Big[P_{\mathsf{C}}E_{i}^{(q)\dagger}E_{j}^{(p)\dagger}E_{k}^{(p)}E_{i}^{(q)}P_{\mathsf{C}}\Big]U_{i}^{(q)\dagger}\\
&\propto U_{i}^{(q)}\delta_{jk}P_{\mathsf{C}}U_{i}^{(q)\dagger}=\,\delta_{jk}P_{i}^{(q)}\,.\end{split}

(12)

where we use the definition of the projectors
P_{i}
, the recovery unitary
U_{i}^{(q)}
from the polar decomposition of
E_{i}^{(q)}
, and the Knill–Laflamme condition for the
(q+p)
th-order errors. This implies that the codespace
\mathsf{C}
and the subspace
\mathsf{P}_{i}^{(q)}
subjected to
E_{j}^{(p)}
can both be recovered by the same unitary
U_{j}^{(p)\dagger}
obtained from the polar decomposition of this error operator. In other words, a jump operator of the form

L_{\mathrm{c},i}^{(p)}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\,U_{i}^{(p)\dagger}\left(P_{i}^{(p)}+P_{i}^{(q+p)}\right)

(13)

would project error states from
\mathsf{P}_{i}^{(p)}
back into the codespace
\mathsf{C}
while at the same time reducing the order of some error states from
q+p
to
p
. In this manner, we can design a set of jump operators that correct errors in a trickle-down fashion where every jump operator induces a complete recovery together with a reduction of the error weight. It is straightforward to see that the lowest number of jump operators would here be limited by the cardinality of
\mathbb{L}
. This minimal set would then be composed of

L_{\mathrm{c},i}^{(1)}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\,U_{i}^{(1)\dagger}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{j}}P_{i,k}^{(j)}\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,\absolutevalue{\bb{L}}\}

(14)

where
U_{i}^{(1)\dagger}
are the recovery unitaries associated to the error
{E_{i}^{(1)}\!\in{\mathbb{E}_{1}\equiv\mathbb{L}}}
and
{P_{i,k}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{P}_{j}}
are the projectors onto
j
th-order error subspaces
\mathsf{P}_{i}^{(j)}
related to a
(j-1)
th-order subspaces through the error process
E_{i}^{(1)}
, i.e.
{P_{i,k}^{(j)}=U_{i}^{(1)}P_{k}^{(j-1)}U_{i}^{(1)\dagger}}
for some index
k
. This index runs from
1
to
{K_{j}=\absolutevalue{\bb{P}_{j-1}}-1}
where the
-1
comes from the fact that we must exclude from the set
\mathbb{P}_{j-1}
the projector onto the subspace
\mathsf{P}^{(j-1)}_{i}
related to
(j-2)
th-order subspaces through the error process
E_{i}^{(1)}
. Given the number
\absolutevalue{\bb{L}}
of weight-1 error operators, the total number of projectors
P_{i,k}^{(j)}
in each jump operator
i
is a simple combinatorial

N_{\mathrm{proj}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\binom{\absolutevalue{\bb{L}}-1}{j-1}\,.

(15)

We can now verify that our construction in Eq. (
14
) allows for a better ratio of the error and correction probabilities
p_{\mathrm{e}}/p_{\mathrm{c}}
(see Eqs. (
9
)) than using the naive jump operators in Eq. (
5
). In our case, we get

\frac{p_{\mathrm{e}}}{p_{\mathrm{c}}}=\frac{N_{e}\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}}{N_{\mathrm{proj}}\,\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\,.

(16)

We see that the ratio is dependent on the chosen code
\mathsf{C}
which sets the order
\ell
of the largest correctable errors and thus the total number of projectors
N_{\mathrm{proj}}
which scales exponentially with the ratio
\ell/\absolutevalue{\bb{L}}
(see Methods). The choice of the code also determines the number of jump operators that must be engineered. Indeed, considering a system with the only error processes being single qubit jump operators acting uniformly on every qubit, two codes
\mathsf{C}_{1}
and
\mathsf{C}_{2}
both correcting errors up to the order
\ell
but involving different number of physical qubits (e.g., Shor and Steane codes) would require a different number of jump operators.

Repetition code

Let us illustrate these results on a toy example, the repetition code. This code is a simple
\llbracket n,1,n\rrbracket
code encoding a single logical qubit in
n
physical ones. It is protected against single qubit bit-flip errors
X_{i}
at a rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
up to order
\ell=(n-1)/2
. The code subspace
\mathsf{C}
is spanned by the states
\{\ket{0_\n{L}}=\ket{0}^{\otimes n},\ket{1_\n{L}}=\ket{1}^{\otimes n}\}
. The projectors onto the error subspaces can be constructed using Eq. (
7
) and the generators of the code’s stabilizer group,
\{Z_{1}Z_{2},Z_{2}Z_{3},...,Z_{n-1}Z_{n}\}
. Alternatively, we can define these projectors using
P_{\mathsf{C}}
and the error operators. For example, for single qubit bit-flip subspaces the
n
projectors are given by
P_{i}=X_{i}P_{\mathsf{C}}X_{i}\,\in\mathbb{P}_{1}
. The projectors into higher order error subspaces are obtained in a similar way. This construction is more convenient for our need as it allows us to build the desired jump operators using Eq. (
14
), which we can also rewrite as

L_{\mathrm{c},i}^{(1)}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{j}}P_{i,k}^{(j-1)}X_{i}\quad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

(17)

using the fact that the recovery unitaries
U_{i}^{(1)\dagger}
are exactly the bit-flip operators
X_{i}
thanks to their involutory character,
X_{i}^{2}=\mathds{1}
. The system dynamics in Eq. (
1
) is then governed by
n
jump operators competing against
n
error processes. Disregarding their possibly very nonlocal aspect (necessitating all-to-all connectivity), engineering
n
jump operators represents a much more tractable exercise than realizing the lookup-table solution which requires
{2^{n-1}-1}
correction operators.

The two solutions also differ in error correction performances. We study the performance of the jump operators in Eqs. (
5
) and (
17
) by simulating the dynamics of a 11-qubit repetition code initialized in the state
\ket{\psi_\n{L}}\propto\ket{0_\n{L}}+i\ket{1_\n{L}}
and subjected to bit-flip error
{L_{\mathrm{e},i}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}}\,X_{i}}
and correction jumps. After an evolution time
t<3/\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
we decode the system state by majority voting and compute the logical fidelity with the initial state. The results are presented in Fig.
2
. In addition, to simulate the optimal correction operators, we also perform simulations with jump operators correcting errors only up to orders
{m<\ell=5}
(i.e. we build the jump operators in Eq. (
17
) with the first sum going only to
m
). At short timescales all infidelity curves follow an identical trend dictated by the final decoding step of the simulation using majority vote. This decay of the fidelity described by
\mathcal{O}\!\left(t^{\,\ell+1}\right)
comes from the unavoidable probability that multiple errors arise and accumulate resulting in an error of weight at least
\ell+1
and a misdecoding of the system state. Thus, majority vote alone allows a short timescales to slow down the loss of information about the initial logical state by using codes with larger
\ell
. The goal of an error correction protocol is however to (1) reduce the logical error rate at longer timescales, and (2) start this secondary regime at the earliest time possible. A clear example of this is the infidelity evolution using the lookup-table correction jump operators (in black in Fig.
2
). We observe that for
t\gtrsim 0.05/\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
the logical infidelity starts to increase at a much slower rate than for earlier times such that at
3/\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
the infidelity is only
\sim 10^{-4}
as opposed to
0.5
in the uncorrected case. We now compare this to the performance of our trickle-down solution. When considering correction jump operators with projectors up to error weight
{m<4}
, the logical fidelity is lower than for the lookup-table solution. This is expected as the error processes are not fully corrected resulting in always growing uncorrectable error subspace. However, when
m=4
and
m=\ell
, our trickle-down solution outperforms the lookup-table one and, in the optimal case, by more than an order of magnitude. We also notice that for
m=\ell
, the onset of the slow increase of the logical infidelity is shifted to earlier timescales by a factor of
5
which is a signature that correcting errors in a trickle down fashion enhances the probability that a correction jump occurs. In other words, it enhances the ratio
p_{\mathrm{e}}/p_{\mathrm{c}}
.

becomes the logical error rate commonly used in measurement based QEC where it represents the frequency with which the decoder fails. t^{\,\ell+1}
to avoid overfitting the dynamics at short times

\sim 2.2

{n>3}

\Gamma_{\mathrm{e}}

. These values are comparable with our results in Fig.

Trapped-ion implementation

We now present a potential implementation of such a repetition code corrected using the trickle-down solution in a trapped-ion platform. The scheme we propose corresponds to an extension of the one presented in Ref.
Reiter et al. 2017
. Let us consider a system of
n
ions each possessing four addressable internal energy levels,
\{\ket{0},\ket{1},\ket{e},\ket{f}\}
, and three bosonic modes
a
,
b
and
c
corresponding to motional modes of the ion string. We utilize these additional levels and modes to engineer the correction jump operators acting onto the computational subspace spanned by the state
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}^{\otimes n}
. The engineering is performed through reservoir engineering techniques, i.e. the coupling of the system using a Hamiltonian
H
and a tunable nonunital (i.e. entropy removing) dissipation channel such as optical pumping or sympathetic cooling
van Mourik et al. 2024
. In our case illustrated schematically in Fig.
4
,
H
consists of three parts: First, a global laser drive addressing simultaneously
|0\rangle\leftrightarrow|e\rangle
and
|1\rangle\leftrightarrow|f\rangle
with a strength
\Omega
and detuned by
\Delta
from the resonance

H_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta\left(\outerproduct{e}{e}_{i}+\outerproduct{f}{f}_{i}\right)+\frac{\Omega}{2}\left(\outerproduct{e}{0}_{i}+\outerproduct{f}{1}_{i}\right)+\mathrm{H.c.}

(18)

which we can also split into the sum of a rest
H_{0}
and perturbation
V
parts. Next, we require a second global laser beam now coupling
\ket{0}/\ket{f}
and
\ket{1}/\ket{e}
to the motional modes
a
and
b
, respectively, with a strength
G
and a detuning
\delta
,

H_{2}=\delta\left(a^{\dagger}a+b^{\dagger}b\right)+G\sum_{i=1}^{n}a^{\dagger}\outerproduct{1}{e}_{i}+b^{\dagger}\outerproduct{0}{f}_{i}+\mathrm{H.c.}

(19)

Third, we add a single addressing laser coupling an ion at position
i
to an auxiliary motional mode
c_{i}

H_{3,i}=g\,c_{i}^{\dagger}\left(\outerproduct{0}{e}_{i}+\outerproduct{1}{f}_{i}\right)+\mathrm{H.c.}

(20)

We use this mode to remove entropy from the system through jump operators
L_{i}=\sqrt{\kappa}\,c_{i}
with a tunable rate
\kappa
. The error correction mechanism behind the total Hamiltonian
H=H_{1}+H_{2}+H_{3}
is the following: using
H_{2}
in a strong-driving regime creates many-body resonances between computational states and superpositions of states with a single excitation
\ket{e}
or
\ket{f}
. Driving
H_{1}
in a perturbative fashion and carefully setting both detunings
\Delta
and
\delta
, we can address specific transitions between those states. By adiabatically eliminating the local mode
c_{i}
and excited states
\ket{e}
and
\ket{f}
, we can show that the effective dynamics of the system is dissipative with a jump operator (see Supplemental Material)

\begin{split}L_{\mathrm{eff},i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}&\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}(j)}\big(\outerproduct{0}{1}_{i}P_{1}^{(j)}+\outerproduct{1}{0}_{i}P_{0}^{(j)}\big)\\
+&\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}(n-j)}\big(\outerproduct{1}{0}_{i}P_{1}^{(j)}+\outerproduct{0}{1}_{i}P_{0}^{(j)}\big)\,.\end{split}

(21)

where
P_{\psi}^{(j)}
indicate projectors onto all computational state that are
j
bit flips away from the logical state
\ket{\psi_\n{L}}
. The first parenthesis in
L_{\mathrm{eff},i}
displays the
desired
error correction operators, while the second one bares the
undesired
processes increasing the error weight instead of decreasing it. The rate of these two processes follow a Lorentzian function (see Supplemental Material)

\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}}\left[\left(\frac{2G}{\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}\delta}\right)^{\!2}\left(x-\frac{\delta\Delta}{G}\right)^{\!2}+1\,\right]^{-1}

(22)

with
{\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}\propto g^{2}/\kappa}
being the rate of the engineered dissipation. Choosing the median of the Lorentzian
x_{0}=\delta\Delta/G
to be an integer, the process correcting all error states of weight
x_{0}
and erroneous qubit
i
will be on resonance and happening at a rate
\Omega^{2}/\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}
. All the other processes will be off resonant. Importantly, the undesired ones will always stay off resonant as long as
x_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
since they follow
\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}(n-j)
, i.e. the same Lorentzian as for the desired processes, mirrored symmetrically around
\ell
. The normalized rates are represented in Fig.
4
for a system of
n=21
qubits,
x_{0}=5
,
\Delta=4\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}
,
\delta=3.46\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}
and
G=1.66\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}
. The ideal rate function would be a step function with a value of
1
for the desired processes and
0
for the undesired ones. It can potentially be achieved using inhomogeneous and amplitude modulated global drives rendering the parameters ion dependent. We leave this for future studies.

The desired process in Eq. (
21
) looks slightly different than the trickle down solution we proposed in Eqs. (
14
) and (
17
). However, they can be shown to be equivalent. It is straightforward to check that the projector
P_{0}^{(j)}
is related to the projectors
P_{i,k}^{(j)}
in Eq. (
14
) through the relation

\outerproduct{1}{0}_{i}P_{0}^{(j)}=\outerproduct{1}{1}_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{j}}P_{i,k}^{(j)}\,.

(23)

The relation for
P_{1}^{(j)}
reads identically with the roles of
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
interchanged. Given that
X_{i}=\outerproduct{1}{1}_{i}+\outerproduct{0}{0}_{i}
, the sum of these expressions leads to the intended result. In other words, the desired part of the jump operator in Eq. (
21
) realizes to the proposed trickle down solution with the exception that the rates are function of the error weight
j
. To remedy this we can make the global drive
H_{2}
multi-tone. Carefully setting the strength
G_{x}
and detuning
\delta_{x}
of each tone puts several transitions on resonance (according to Eq. (
22
)). The optimal number of tones is
\ell
allowing to address all the desired processes at once. Finally, adding
n
single-ion addressing laser beams with
n
auxiliary motional mode
c_{i}
, i.e. a Hamiltonian
H_{3,i}
in Eq. (
20
) for every ion, engineers a dissipative process with
n
jump operators
\{L_{\mathrm{eff},i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

In summary, our trapped-ion scheme realizes exactly the trickle down error correction of a repetition code using two global laser beams (among which an
\ell
-tone one) and
n
single-ion addressing ones. Such a setup would produce a noise biased quantum code performing as illustrated in Fig.
3
. Moreover, the presence of undesired processes in Eq. (
22
) does not reduce this performance as their rates are sufficiently low. As a matter of fact, the trickle down simulation data presented in Fig.
3
were obtained by simulating the system dynamics under the multi-tone correction operators
\{L_{\mathrm{eff},i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with parameters set to experimentally achievable values (
\delta=10\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}
,
\Omega=3\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}
,
\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}=10~\mathrm{kHz}
). The dynamics under the ideal jump operators given in Eq. (
17
) with rate
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}=\Omega^{2}/\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}
leads to identical results. To reduce the experimental overhead of using
n
single-ion beams, the correction can be performed in a sequential manner by individually addressing subsets of ions one after the other. This would increase the logical error rate by a constant factor but its scaling with the number of qubits would remain unchanged compared to the one presented in Fig.
3
.

Approximate decoders

Before concluding, we must put our trickle down solution within the context of the current most common ways of performing quantum error correction. As noted earlier, a lookup table with exponentially increasing size is unfeasible even in measurement-feedback QEC. Instead, this paradigm relies on approximate decoding algorithms, which use measurement outcomes to estimate the most likely error subspace into which the system has been projected. In the framework of stabilizer codes, these approximate decoders take as input the binary vector
\vec{s}_{i}
(the syndrome) obtained from stabilizer measurements and output a correction unitary
\tilde{U}_{i}^{\dagger}
that ideally approximates the desired
U_{i}^{\dagger}
(recall Eqs. (
3
) and (
7
)). To further simplify the problem, decoders often incorporate a model of the error process in the system, for example, the locality of errors determined by qubit connectivity and single- and two-qubit error probabilities obtained from device calibration. For codes where each independent error produces a syndrome
\vec{s}_{i}
with at most two flagged bits
\vec{s}_{i}[a]=1=\vec{s}_{i}[b]
(as in surface codes), the estimation can be reformulated as a graph problem
Higgott and Gidney 2025
. Here, the
{n-k}
nodes correspond to the entries of
\vec{s}_{i}
and the edges represent the errors (or, equivalently, the required corrections). We show an example of such a decoder graph in Fig.
5
. The decoder’s task is to match each pair of flagged vertices with a path (i.e. an error process) with the lowest possible total weight (with the path weight calculated efficiently using the available information). Various algorithms can address this problem, among which the minimum-weight perfect matching algorithm is the most common
Edmonds 1965a
;
Edmonds 1965b
;
Dennis et al. 2002
;
Fowler et al. 2012
. For further details about decoders, see Refs.
iOlius et al. 2024
;
Higgott and Gidney 2025
. Translating approximate decoders from measurement-feedback to dissipative QEC, one may define the correction jump operators as

L_{\mathrm{c},i}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\,\tilde{U}_{i}^{\dagger}\!(\vec{s}_{i})\,P_{i}

(24)

where
\tilde{U}_{i}^{\dagger}\!(\vec{s}_{i})
is the recovery unitary produced by the approximate decoder for the syndrome
\vec{s}_{i}
and
P_{i}
is the projector onto the
i
th error subspace. These operators are an approximate version of the lookup-table operators in Eq. (
5
) with the advantage of limiting the exponential growth of the number of correction jumps with
n
by excluding the correction of the most unlikely error processes given the error model. However, they still suffer from the requirement
\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}\sim\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n))
(see Eq. (
9
)). To improve this aspect, one must consider the topological properties of certain codes. For example, in surface codes, a closed path in the decoder graph may go undetected because no syndrome bit
\vec{s}_{i}[j]
is flagged; consequently, such a path would not be recognized as an error. Therefore, to correct a pair of flagged vertices, multiple closing-loop correction paths may be constructed. Conversely, a given unitary
\tilde{U}_{i}^{\dagger}\!(\vec{s}_{i})
can recover the logical information from several error subspaces simultaneously, with projectors
\{P_{i,j}\}
(see examples in Fig.
5
). Thus, the action of approximate decoders in measurement-feedback QEC should be modeled in the dissipative framework using the correction jump operators

L_{\mathrm{c},i}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}\,\tilde{U}_{i}^{\dagger}\!(\vec{s}_{i})\,\sum_{j}\,P_{i,j}.

(25)

These operators are similar to those proposed in Eq. (
14
) with the difference that the
P_{i,j}
are not necessarily projectors onto subspaces corresponding to a specific error order. Nevertheless, they lead to improved correction performance, analogous to the trickle down solution. That being said, both sets of jump operators in Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) are challenging to implement in practice, as they require not only knowledge of the syndrome
\vec{s}_{i}
but also the ability to engineer dynamically changing recovery unitaries
\tilde{U}_{i}^{\dagger}\!(\vec{s}_{i})
. Nonetheless, this discussion highlights two key points: (1) approximate decoders overcome the issue of an exponentially increasing number of correction operators as the code size grows; and (2) a code’s topology can induce effects similar to a trickle down correction. These observations also prompt questions regarding measurement-feedback QEC: Is there a sequence of stabilizer measurements whose outcomes can indicate which operation would most effectively reduce the error weight? Can one devise an approximate decoder that functions as a trickle down correction, and would this be more time-efficient? Recent works have begun to address the second question
Jones 2024
;
Ott et al. 2025
;
Beni et al. 2025
. For instance, Ref.
Jones 2024
demonstrated that combining outputs from multiple decoders and selecting the best local correction from each can improve both the error suppression factor
\Lambda
as well as the decoding time. This approach effectively selects the lowest-weight paths across decoder outputs and can be interpreted as minimizing the area of loops in the code (even though not considered as errors). Decoders proposed in Refs.
Ott et al. 2025
;
Beni et al. 2025
follow a different strategy: they construct candidate error sequences by adding faults incrementally, guided by a decision tree, until the resulting syndrome matches the observed one. These algorithms could be adapted to prioritize corrections that reduce the overall error weight, rather than reconstructing the full error, thus bringing them closer in spirit to a trickle down correction strategy.

Bosonic QEC

The trickle-down QEC is analogous to a cooling process, wherein the system is gradually driven towards its lowest energy configuration. In our context, the system is cooled into a degenerate subspace in a coherent way. The correction of bosonic codes is another type of QEC which is often framed as a type of cooling. This becomes evident through the subsystem decomposition approach, which partitions the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space of a quantum harmonic oscillator into a logical qubit subspace and an auxiliary gauge mode
Pantaleoni et al. 2020
. Rewriting the system dynamics in this form allows one to isolate and analyze the effects of both noise and correction on each subsystem.

A canonical example is the dissipative stabilization of the Cat qubit
Mirrahimi et al. 2014
. Consider a quantum harmonic oscillator with annihilation and creation operators
a
and
a^{\dagger}
. A dissipative dynamics governed by the jump operator
L_{\mathrm{cat}}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{c}}}(a^{2}-\alpha^{2})
drives the system into a degenerate ground state manifold spanned by the coherent states
\ket{\pm\alpha}
, which form the logical-Z basis of the Cat qubit
Gilles et al. 1994
;
Mirrahimi et al. 2014
. In the subsystem decomposition of this code (known as the shifted Fock basis) the logical states can be written as
\ket{\pm\alpha}=\ket{0/1}\otimes\ket{0}
, while for sufficiently large
\alpha
, the jump operator becomes
L_{\mathrm{cat}}\propto I\otimes b
, where
I
acts on the logical subspace and
b
lowers the gauge mode by one excitation
Chamberland et al. 2022
. Importantly, in this picture, noise such as dephasing also acts predominantly on the gauge mode, effectively exciting it. That is,
a^{\dagger}a\approx I\otimes(b^{\dagger}b+\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}+\alpha b+\alpha^{*}b^{\dagger})
. Thus, dissipative stabilization of the Cat qubit realizes a trickle-down correction of dephasing noise by gradually removing single excitations from the gauge mode, independent of the gauge mode’s state. In contrast, a lookup-table correction approach would require jump operators that measure the gauge mode’s excitation level and apply corrections accordingly. The number of correction jumps would then scale with the number of gauge mode excitations which one would like to correct.

Other bosonic codes also exhibit trickle-down correction. A notable example is the nonlinear generalization of Cat qubits realized through nonlinear reservoir engineering
Rojkov et al. 2024a
. In their subsystem decomposition, the stabilization operator takes the form
I\otimes f(b^{\dagger}b)b
, where
f
is a nonlinear function of the gauge mode’s excitation number. Similar to our trapped-ion scheme in Eq. (
21
), the function
f
, which is tunable via system parameters, controls the rate of trickle-down at different error weights, and has been shown useful to enhance robustness against noise
Rojkov et al. 2024a
;
Rousseau et al. 2025
.

Our final examples are GKP codes. Designed to protect quantum information against small displacements in position and momentum, GKP codes use two dissipative jump operators—each targeting one quadrature
de Neeve et al. 2022
;
Royer et al. 2020
. These operators induce a trickle-down correction process, as shown in Ref.
Sivak et al. 2023
, via a decomposition of the oscillator Hilbert space into error subspaces of increasing boson number gain or loss. Each jump operator acts to transfer the quantum state down this hierarchy, closer to the code subspace. The authors illustrate this process by showing trajectories of various error states through these subspaces during stabilization. The trickle down aspect of these jump operators can also be shown through their subsystem decomposition (known as the shifted grid or Zak basis) which parametrizes the gauge mode in terms of the modular version of position and momentum operators
Pantaleoni et al. 2020
;
Shaw et al. 2024
. Here, as in the Cat code example, the stabilization gradually displaces the gauge mode state towards its ground state.

In summary, many bosonic QEC schemes developed to date share a trickle-down character. This is largely due to the use of an auxiliary two-level system to mediate correction which intrinsically can only remove one bit of entropy at a time
de Neeve et al. 2025
. Trickling errors down represents a solution that optimizes the number of error states corrected simultaneously through one entropy removal round.
Sivak et al. 2023
qualified it as being an approach that reduces the control overhead in bosonic codes. In our work, we showed that this reduction is in fact exponential and that it is accompanied by an improvement in the error correction performance. Thus, beyond the well-known benefit of reducing the number of physical systems required to encode a logical qubit, the rapid success of bosonic codes may also stem from the intrinsic efficiency of their QEC protocols. Nonetheless, the auxiliary qubit introduces its own challenges: errors in the qubit can propagate to the oscillator and cause logical failures, thereby undermining fault tolerance
Campagne-Ibarcq et al. 2020
;
Royer et al. 2020
;
Sivak et al. 2023
. Solutions to this issue are a topic of ongoing research
Siegele and Campagne-Ibarcq 2023
.

Discussion

In this work, we introduced a novel construction of jump operators for dissipative quantum error correction protocols. Unlike the conventional lookup-table approach, which projects the system into a specific error subspace and applies a dedicated correction unitary, our method implements a trickle-down mechanism that reduces the system’s error weight one level at a time. This construction scales efficiently with code size, reducing the number of required correction operators from exponential to polynomial. Beyond overhead reduction, the trickle-down approach improves correction performance, as demonstrated using the dissipative repetition code: at a physical error rate of
10^{-2}
, it achieves a fourfold increase in the exponential suppression factor
\Lambda
and lowers the logical error rate
p_{L}
by several orders of magnitude. This implies that reaching
p_{L}=10^{-15}
requires only
23
qubits with trickle-down correction, compared to
37
with the lookup-table method. A naive extrapolation to surface codes suggests a qubit overhead reduction of
1-(23/37)^{2}\approx 61\%
.

From a practical perspective, we proposed an implementation of the dissipative repetition code in a trapped-ion platform. Our scheme uses global drives to induce coherent transitions between many-body error states and local beams for entropy removal, extending the scheme from Ref.
Reiter et al. 2017
. Our framework connects autonomous QEC for discrete-variable codes with both measurement-feedback schemes using approximate decoders in topological codes and dissipative bosonic protocols mediated by auxiliary qubits. In this sense, our scalable construction, based on physically motivated principles akin to cooling, offers a unifying perspective with broad applicability across the field.

The main limitation of our method is its reliance on highly nonlocal operators, which poses challenges for hardware platforms constrained by local connectivity, such as superconducting qubit arrays. However, other leading platforms such as trapped ions and Rydberg atom arrays offer more flexibility in this regard (through a natural all-to-all connectivity or rearrangement of atoms). Moreover, current research in quantum error correction has seen a surge of interest in quantum low-density parity-check (qLDPC) codes
Breuckmann and Eberhardt 2021
;
Bravyi et al. 2024
, whose stabilizers are low-rank but often nonlocal. In this context, nonlocality may not be a drawback, and it is worth exploring whether our construction can further improve the logical error rates of qLDPC codes. Another interesting prospect is to learn as to whether measurement-feedback QEC can be implemented in a trickle-down fashion. Would this require measurements that differ from the stabilizer ones? If not, is there a classical decoder capable of outputting correction operations that reduce error weight and suitable for real-time decoding of quantum codes? Addressing these questions is left for future works.

Finally, our work focused on quantum codes functioning as quantum memories. Although these can be applied to quantum sensing and metrology
Reiter et al. 2017
, an intriguing direction for future research is to investigate the impact of trickle-down correction on logical quantum gates for quantum computation.
Lebreuilly et al. 2021
proved the existence of generalized error-transparent Hamiltonians, showing that if a physical operation transforms the codespace and all relevant error subspaces in the same way, then the correct logical gate is realized upon recovery. In other words, if the operation coherently increases the system’s error weight (while retaining it below the uncorrectable level) then dissipative QEC can restore the system such that the overall process implements the intended logical gate. One example of this principle appears in error-corrected gates between GKP codes. These two-qubit gates defined for ideal infinite-energy codewords generate unwanted entanglement when applied to realistic finite-energy GKP states
Gottesman et al. 2001
. However, dissipative trickle-down QEC has shown to correct much of this spurious entanglement, thereby restoring the logical operation to high-fidelity
Rojkov et al. 2024b
. An open question is how such error-transparent operations manifest in other codes and whether they could simplify the experimental requirements for implementing fault-tolerant logical gates. Beyond quantum error correction, our work connects to recent developments in dissipative quantum algorithms, where similar cooling schemes enable scalable protocols
Raghunandan et al. 2020
;
Zapusek et al. 2025
.

Number of projectors in each jump operator

The number of projectors in each trickle down jump operator follows a sum of binomial coefficients shown in Eq. (
15
). This accounts for the fact that a logical state can transition to an erroneous state of order
m

\absolutevalue{\bb{L}}
choose

14

\absolutevalue{\bb{L}-1}
choose
m

{\epsilon=\ell/\absolutevalue{\bb{L}}<1/2}
and using Stirlings formula. We get

\begin{split}N_{\mathrm{proj}}&\geq\frac{2^{\left(\absolutevalue{\bb{L}}-1\right)\,H(\epsilon)}}{\sqrt{8(\ell-1)\left(1-\frac{\ell-1}{\absolutevalue{\bb{L}}-1}\right)}}\\
&\geq\frac{2^{4(\ell-1)\left(1-\frac{\ell-1}{\absolutevalue{\bb{L}}-1}\right)}}{\sqrt{8(\ell-1)\left(1-\frac{\ell-1}{\absolutevalue{\bb{L}}-1}\right)}}\end{split}

(26)

where in the second inequality we are lower bounding the binary entropy function

Effective dynamics of the trapped-ion model

The trapped-ion implementation of the dissipative repetition code relies on the effective operator formalism
Reiter and Sørensen 2012
and resonance engineering
Reiter et al. 2017
techniques. Before using them it is convenient to no longer work in the basis of individual internal states of ions but in a many-body basis involving in addition excitations in modes
a
and
b
introduced

\begin{split}&\ket{i_1,\ldots,i_c}=\\
&\quad\Bigg[\!\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{c}\,\outerproduct{1}{0}_{i_{k}}\right)\!a^{\dagger}\!+\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{c}\,\outerproduct{0}{1}_{i_{k}}\right)\!b^{\dagger}\Bigg]\!\ket{\psi_\n{L}}\!\ket{0,0}\end{split}

(27)

where
\ket{\psi_\n{L}}=\alpha\ket{0_\n{L}}+\beta\ket{1_\n{L}}=\alpha\ket{0}^{\otimes n}+\beta\ket{1}^{\otimes n}
is an arbitrary logical state and
\ket{0,0}
represent the state of oscillators
a
and
b
in the Fock basis. These states represent the erroneous code states of weight
c\leq\ell
with exactly one excitation in either of the two modes. We use
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{c}\}
to indicate the indices of the corrupted physical qubits (we will refer to each individual ion in the state
\ket{0/1}
as physical qubits). The particularity of this definition is that an excitation in mode
a
is associated to the part of the error state decoded as logical zero
\ket{0_\n{L}}
while an excitation in mode
b
– to the part of the error state decoded as
\ket{1_\n{L}}
. We leave the state
\ket{\psi_\n{L}}
arbitrary as the error correction should be agnostic of the logical information. Other important many-body states necessary for the understanding of the scheme are

\ket{\Xi_{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{c}a\outerproduct{e}{1}_{i_{k}}\!+b\outerproduct{f}{0}_{i_{k}}\!\right)\!\ket{i_1,\ldots,i_c}

(28)

which involve ground states of both modes and ions’ internal states
\ket{e}
and
\ket{f}
. Assuming the bosonic mode to remain in the ground and first excited states only, the lowest order term of the Hamiltonian
H_{0}+H_{2}
(where
H_{0}
is the detuning part of
H_{1}
in Eq. (
18
)) in this new basis reads

\begin{split}\tilde{H}=\sum_{c=1}^{\ell}\sum_{i_{1}\neq\ldots\neq i_{c}}^{n}&\Delta\outerproduct{\Xi_{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}{\Xi_{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}\\
+&\delta\outerproduct{i_1,\ldots,i_c}{i_1,\ldots,i_c}\\
+&\sqrt{c}\,G\outerproduct{i_1,\ldots,i_c}{\Xi_{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}+\mathrm{H.c.}\,.\end{split}

(29)

Note that
\tilde{H}
is only a portion of the complete Hamiltonian (indicated by the
tilde
) as it does not capture higher order processes involving two or more ions in the excited manifold. However, these processes will be out of resonance as happening at higher rates, e.g., the second order processes involving states with exactly two
\ket{e/f}
will happen at rates
2\Delta
and
\sqrt{c\choose 2}G
. The most significant undesired process among these is the one that deteriorates an initial erroneous state by introducing one additional error. In order to describe this process, we define the complementary many-body states
\ket{\overbar{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}=X_{\mathrm{L}}\ket{i_1,\ldots,i_c}
with
X_{L}
being the logical Pauli X operator. These states are now associating an excitation in mode
a
to the part of the error state decoded as logical one
\ket{1_\n{L}}
and an excitation in mode
b
– to the part of the error state decoded as
\ket{0_\n{L}}
. The last many-body states that we require are

\begin{split}&\ket{\chi_{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-c}}\\
&\quad\left(\sum_{i_{k}\notin\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{c}\}}a\outerproduct{e}{1}_{i_{k}}+b\outerproduct{f}{0}_{i_{k}}\right)\ket{\overbar{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}\end{split}

(30)

where the sum is taken over all non erroneous qubits of the state
\ket{i_1,\ldots,i_c}
. These states are the undesired counterparts of the states
\ket{\Xi_{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}
defined in Eq. (
28
). The lowest order undesired Hamiltonian associated to
H_{0}+H_{2}
then reads

\begin{split}\tilde{H}_{u}=\sum_{c=1}^{\ell}\sum_{i_{1}\neq\ldots\neq i_{c}}^{n}&\,\Delta\outerproduct{\chi_{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}{\chi_{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}\\
+&\,\delta\outerproduct{\overbar{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}{\overbar{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}\\
+&\,\sqrt{n-c}\,G\outerproduct{\overbar{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}{\chi_{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}\,.\end{split}

(31)

The last term
H_{3}
of the total Hamiltonian
H
represents single addressing lasers that couple each ion at position

c_{j}

We derive the effective Liouvillian that governs the dynamics of the many-body system following the effective operator formalism
Reiter and Sørensen 2012

\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}^{\otimes n}\otimes\ket{0,0}

\ket{e}

\begin{split}H_{\mathrm{NH}}&=H_{0}+H_{2}+H_{3}-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}\\
&\approx H_{0}+H_{2}-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{\mathrm{eng},j}^{\dagger}L_{\mathrm{eng},j}\\
&\approx\tilde{H}+\tilde{H}_{u}-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{\mathrm{eng},j}^{\dagger}L_{\mathrm{eng},j}=:\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{NH}}\end{split}

(33)

where the first approximation uses Eq. (
32

presented in Eqs. (
29

\ket{\Xi_{i, \ldots i_{c}}}

and results in an energy shift of

\Delta^{\prime}(x)=\Delta-\frac{i}{2}\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}-\frac{x\,G^{2}}{\delta}

(35)

To derive the effective jump operators we must express the drive part

of the Hamiltonian

in Eq. (

\begin{split}\tilde{V}_{+}=\tilde{V}_{-}^{\dagger}=\sum_{c=1}^{\ell}\sum_{i_{i}\neq\ldots\neq i_{c}}^{n}\sqrt{c}\,&\frac{\Omega}{2}\outerproduct{\Xi_{i_1,\ldots,i_c}}{i_1,\ldots,i_c}_{0}\\

(36)

where we define

\begin{split}&L_{\mathrm{eff},j}=L_{\mathrm{eng},j}\left[\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{NH}}\right]^{-1}\tilde{V}_{+}\\

+&\sum_{i_{i}\neq\ldots\neq i_{c}\neq j}^{n}\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}(n-c)}\outerproduct{i_1,\ldots,i_c,i_{c+1}=j}{i_1,\ldots,i_c}_{0}\end{split}

These jump operators consist of two processes: a desired one that reduces the weight of error states from
c
to

j
-th ion is erroneous. In contrast, the undesired process flips the state of the
j
-th ion even when it is not erroneous, thereby introducing an additional error. Rewriting the jump operator in terms of projectors into the error subspaces, we get the expression in Eq. (

is obtained using

\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)=\kappa_{\mathrm{eng}}\,\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\big\lvert\Delta^{\prime}(x)\big\rvert^{-2}

(37)
