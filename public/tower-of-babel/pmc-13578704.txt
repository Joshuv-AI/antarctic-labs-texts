Mechanical behavior and multiscale damage evolution of filled fractured sandstone under freeze–thaw cycles
Authors: Rui Li, Jianxi Ren, Kai Su, Mengchen Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the deterioration of rock engineering stability in seasonally frozen regions, this study investigated the mechanical response and damage evolution of sandstone subjected to the coupled effects of freeze-thaw (F-T) cycles and filled fractures. Uniaxial compression tests were performed on intact specimens and specimens containing prefabricated, through-going filled fractures with dip angles of 30°, 45°, and 60° after 0, 20, 40, and 60 F-T cycles. A multi-technique monitoring framework integrating acoustic emission (AE), digital image correlation (DIC), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) was used to characterize energy, deformation, and pore-structure evolution. The results showed that F-T cycles progressively degraded the physical and mechanical properties of sandstone, with strength decreasing approximately linearly over the tested cycle range. Fracture geometry exerted a pronounced influence on damage evolution. Larger fracture dip angles were associated with a stronger shift of the pore-size distribution toward larger pores, consistent with enhanced transmission and concentration of frost-heave stress. NMR T2-spectrum analysis identified a five-stage pore-evolution sequence: “micropore reduction → small-pore increase → medium-pore growth → large-pore expansion → macropore initiation.” These findings provide a quantitative basis for evaluating the stability of rock masses containing filled fractures and for designing reinforcement measures in cold regions.

Introduction

Rapid infrastructure development in cold regions has intensified concerns about the long-term stability of rock engineering exposed to cyclic low-temperature conditions1–4. Freeze-thaw (F-T) action degrades rock integrity and can contribute to instabilities such as rock-slope failure. Natural rock masses commonly contain joints and fissures filled with weak materials. However, how F-T cycles interact with filled fractures to govern damage evolution remains insufficiently understood. A systematic investigation of filled fractured sandstone under F-T cycles is therefore important for understanding failure mechanisms and improving the design of cold-region rock engineering.

Extensive research has examined the deterioration of rock mechanical properties under F-T cycles5,6. In intact rock, uniaxial compressive strength and elastic modulus generally decrease exponentially or linearly with increasing numbers of cycles, whereas Poisson’s ratio and peak strain tend to increase7,8. This deterioration is commonly attributed to stresses generated by the water-ice phase transition, which promote the initiation, propagation, and coalescence of microcracks3. Because natural rock masses contain abundant discontinuities, recent studies have increasingly focused on rocks with pre-existing defects9. F-T cycles can intensify stress concentration at crack tips, turning pre-existing fractures into preferential damage paths and reducing crack-initiation strength10–12. Many previous studies, however, have treated fractures as open and have not considered that natural joints are often filled with weak media such as clay or debris13,14. Such fillings alter the mechanical boundary conditions and can produce more complex crack-growth behavior under F-T cycles15,16. The coupled effects of F-T cycles and filling materials on rock damage evolution therefore remain to be clarified.

Multiscale monitoring techniques have been used to clarify the micro- and meso-scale mechanisms of F-T deterioration17–20. Acoustic emission (AE) monitoring captures microcrack nucleation and coalescence in real time. Previous studies have shown that F-T cycles can cause high-energy AE events to occur earlier during loading and can produce a more pronounced pre-peak decrease in the b-value, indicating progressive destabilization of the internal rock fabric21–23. Digital image correlation (DIC) provides full-field measurements of macroscopic deformation and is particularly useful for tracking strain localization and crack bifurcation. In freeze-thawed rocks, high-strain zones have been reported to form at lower stress levels and to propagate more rapidly24–26. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) can quantify the meso-scale evolution of pore structure during F-T cycles27–30. Nevertheless, these techniques have mainly been applied to intact rocks or rocks with open fractures. The manner in which filled fractures and F-T cycles jointly control the magnitude and spatial orientation of damage remains insufficiently understood.

To reveal the damage mechanism of filled fractured sandstone under F-T cycles, this study investigates the effects of F-T cycles and fracture dip angle on the mechanical and deformation properties of sandstone through uniaxial compression tests. AE and DIC monitoring are used to identify instability precursors, while NMR testing is used to clarify the sequence of pore-structure evolution. The findings provide a theoretical basis for the stability assessment and reinforcement design of fractured rock masses in cold regions.

Experimental methodology

Sample preparation

The specimens were prepared from representative red sandstone obtained from a seasonally frozen region. Cores were machined into standard cylinders 50 mm in diameter and 100 mm in height. Two planar, parallel, through-going fractures were prefabricated by water-jet cutting (Fig. 1). The fracture aperture was 3.0 mm, and dip angles of 30°, 45°, and 60° were investigated. The planar geometry was selected to isolate the effect of dip angle and minimize additional effects associated with roughness, persistence, and connectivity. The 3.0 mm aperture was reproducible by water-jet cutting, permitted complete filling with cement mortar, and remained small relative to the specimen diameter. The selected dip angles represented low, intermediate, and high oblique orientations and enabled systematic comparison of the normal and shear components acting on the filled interfaces under uniaxial compression. Based on previous work13, cement mortar with a water: cement: sand mass ratio of 1.0:1.5:2.0 was used as the filling material. Table 1 summarizes the basic physical and mechanical properties of the sandstone and filling material. Three replicate specimens were tested for each condition, including the intact groups and all fracture-angle groups, to reduce the influence of natural heterogeneity. The F-T procedure followed the Standard for Test Methods of Engineering Rock Mass (GB/T 50266 − 2013). Saturated specimens were frozen at -20 °C for 4 h and then thawed at 20 °C for 4 h, constituting one cycle. The number of F-T cycles, N, was 0, 20, 40, or 60, and the specimens remained saturated throughout the cycling procedure.

Compression test and monitoring

Figure 2 illustrates the experimental system. Uniaxial compression tests were performed using an RMT-201 rock and concrete testing system under displacement control at 0.001 mm/s until failure. AE sensors monitored microcrack initiation and propagation, and AE ring-down counts and signal energy were recorded. A DIC system simultaneously captured full-field surface deformation at 10 frames/s, from which displacement and strain fields were calculated. For the AE and DIC analyses, the replicate whose peak strength was closest to the group mean was selected as the representative specimen. For quantitative DIC analysis, the region of interest excluded the specimen boundaries and the filled-fracture surfaces. A high-strain region was defined by an axial strain exceeding the spatial mean by two standard deviations. Localization onset was taken as the first frame in which a connected high-strain region extended over more than 20% of the specimen height; the corresponding global axial strain was denoted εloc. The normalized localization-onset time was defined as Tloc = tloc/tp and, because the displacement rate was constant, was approximated as εloc/εp, where εp is the axial strain at peak stress. At peak stress, the apparent localization-band width, wloc, was calculated as the mean full width at half maximum of three strain profiles normal to the dominant band.

NMR testing

An NMRC12-010 V NMR analyzer was used to characterize pore-structure evolution before and after F-T cycles. The main magnetic field was 0.30 ± 0.05 T, the probe-coil diameter was 60 mm, and the temperature-control accuracy was ± 0.1 °C. The transverse relaxation-time (T2) spectra were analyzed to quantify changes in pore-size distribution and thereby characterize meso-scale damage induced by F-T cycles.

Experimental results and analysis

Compressive mechanical properties

Influence of F-T cycles

Figure 3 presents the uniaxial compressive stress-strain curves of intact and filled fractured sandstone subjected to different numbers of F-T cycles. ε1 and ε3 denote the axial and radial strains, respectively. Both intact and fractured specimens exhibited five characteristic stages: compaction, elastic deformation, stable crack growth, unstable crack growth, and post-peak deformation. During stable crack growth, radial strain increased rapidly and eventually exceeded axial strain. Little residual load-bearing capacity remained after unstable failure in most specimens. With increasing numbers of F-T cycles, the initial compaction stage became more pronounced because F-T-induced microcracks increased the available pore-closure space.

Fig. 3Stress-strain curves of intact and filled fractured sandstone after different numbers of F-T cycles: (a) intact; (b) θ = 30°; (c) θ = 45°; (d) θ = 60°.

Stress-strain curves of intact and filled fractured sandstone after different numbers of F-T cycles: (a) intact; (b) θ = 30°; (c) θ = 45°; (d) θ = 60°.

Figure 4 illustrates the relationship between mean peak strength and the number of F-T cycles. Peak strength decreased approximately linearly over the tested range. After 20 cycles, the mean peak strengths of the intact specimens and the specimens with fracture dip angles of 30°, 45°, and 60° were 52.76, 45.86, 40.81, and 38.07 MPa, respectively. By 60 cycles, the corresponding strengths had decreased by 22.43%, 21.69%, 29.51%, and 35.17% relative to the 20-cycle values. This progressive deterioration is attributed to repeated water-ice phase transitions, which weaken intergranular cementation and reduce load-bearing capacity.

Peak strength versus number of F-T cycles.

Influence of fracture dip angles

Comparison of the curves in Fig. 3a-d shows that the filled fractured specimens generally had a longer compaction stage than the intact specimens. This behavior is consistent with the lower stiffness and greater porosity of the cement-mortar filling (Table 1), which allowed greater compression during initial loading. Some fractured specimens retained substantial load-bearing capacity after the first stress drop rather than failing immediately. The filling material can provide local support and accommodate deformation along the fracture surfaces, allowing load transfer to continue after local failure. Global instability occurred only after newly formed cracks propagated and coalesced.

Figure 5 shows the variation in peak strength with fracture dip angle. At each number of F-T cycles, mean peak strength decreased monotonically as the dip angle increased from 30° to 60°. The decrease associated with fracture angle became more pronounced after additional F-T cycles, suggesting an interaction between fracture orientation and F-T damage. Changes in fracture orientation alter the balance of normal and shear tractions along the filled interface and can intensify fracture-tip stress concentration, interface slip, and subsequent crack initiation and coalescence under the tested geometry.

Acoustic emission characteristics

Influence of F-T cycles

Figure 6 shows the evolution of AE ring-down counts during uniaxial compression of intact sandstone after different numbers of F-T cycles. AE activity exhibited three stages: a quiet period, a growth period, and a rapid-increase period. During initial loading, closure of pre-existing pores and fissures generated relatively few AE signals, and the cumulative count curve remained nearly flat. With increasing stress, microcracks initiated and propagated stably, increasing the AE event rate and the slope of the cumulative curve. Near point A in Fig. 6, crack growth became unstable and cracks coalesced, producing a sharp increase in ring-down counts and the formation of a macroscopic fracture zone. The final cumulative counts after 0, 20, 40, and 60 F-T cycles were 553,485, 1,206,107, 1,502,045, and 3,174,918, respectively. Point A shifted to an earlier loading time and the growth period shortened with increasing F-T cycles. These changes are consistent with a looser internal structure, greater pore and microcrack connectivity, and a lower stress level required to activate crack growth.

Fig. 6AE ring-down counts of intact sandstone after different numbers of F-T cycles: (a) F-T = 0; (b) F-T = 20; (c) F-T = 40; (d) F-T = 60.

AE ring-down counts of intact sandstone after different numbers of F-T cycles: (a) F-T = 0; (b) F-T = 20; (c) F-T = 40; (d) F-T = 60.

Figure 7 reveals AE amplitude, signal energy, and stress during uniaxial compression of intact sandstone. AE amplitude reflects the intensity of an individual signal, whereas AE energy is an instrument-dependent measure of the signal energy accumulated during a fracture event. AE activity closely followed the loading stages. During pore compaction and crack closure, AE events were sparse and had low amplitudes and energies. As stable damage developed, AE events became more frequent and extended to higher amplitudes and energies, indicating progressive microcrack propagation and interaction. Near peak stress, clusters of high-amplitude and high-energy events coincided with the abrupt stress drop. Increasing the number of F-T cycles shifted both low- and high-energy activity to earlier stages and made the energy release more concentrated. In unfrozen specimens, high-energy events were concentrated immediately before failure, whereas after 60 F-T cycles they occurred frequently during the middle and late loading stages. Thus, F-T damage increased the amount of pre-peak fracture activity and reduced the loading level at which high-energy cracking events emerged.

Fig. 7AE amplitude and energy of intact sandstone after different numbers of F-T cycles: (a) F-T = 0; (b) F-T = 20; (c) F-T = 40; (d) F-T = 60.

AE amplitude and energy of intact sandstone after different numbers of F-T cycles: (a) F-T = 0; (b) F-T = 20; (c) F-T = 40; (d) F-T = 60.

To quantify the energy trend, the relative pre-peak AE energy, Epre, was defined as the cumulative AE energy before peak stress divided by that of a reference specimen (F-T = 0 for the intact series and θ = 30° for the fracture-angle series). Because AE energy depends on the acquisition system and sensor coupling, Epre is interpreted as a relative indicator of fracture-related activity rather than as material fracture energy. For intact sandstone, Epre increased from 1.00 at F-T = 0 to 2.64, 5.14, and 10.18 after 20, 40, and 60 cycles, respectively, while peak strength decreased. This inverse trend suggests that F-T-induced pores and microcracks created more sites for closure, friction, initiation, and propagation during loading, increasing the recorded AE energy despite the lower peak load-bearing capacity.

Influence of fracture dip angles

Figure 8 illustrates the effect of fracture dip angle on AE ring-down counts during loading of specimens subjected to 40 F-T cycles. All three angle groups exhibited a quiet period, a growth period, and a rapid-increase period. Pre-peak AE activity generally became more intense with increasing dip angle: the 60° specimen generated substantially more ring-down-count events during the growth period than the 30° specimen, and its cumulative curve rose more steeply after point A. These observations are consistent with enhanced fracture-tip stress concentration and slip or debonding along the filled interface, which promote microcrack initiation, propagation, and coalescence. Fracture dip angle therefore influenced the timing and rate of damage accumulation and the temporal distribution of instability precursors.

Fig. 8AE ring-down counts of filled fractured sandstone with different fracture dip angles after 40 F-T cycles: (a) θ = 30°; (b) θ = 45°; (c) θ = 60°.

AE ring-down counts of filled fractured sandstone with different fracture dip angles after 40 F-T cycles: (a) θ = 30°; (b) θ = 45°; (c) θ = 60°.

Figure 9 reveals AE amplitude and energy during uniaxial compression of filled fractured sandstone with different fracture dip angles after 40 F-T cycles. The AE signals exhibited a three-stage pattern consistent with the stress evolution and cumulative ring-down counts. Fracture dip angle affected the onset and clustering of high-amplitude and high-energy events. At larger angles, these events appeared earlier during the middle-to-late loading stage and became more densely distributed, consistent with increased fracture-tip stress concentration and interface slip. Epre values for the 30°, 45°, and 60° specimens were 1.00, 0.66, and 0.89, respectively. The non-monotonic variation suggests that fracture orientation altered the crack-growth path and energy-dissipation mechanism rather than simply increasing total AE energy. The relatively large pre-peak energy of the 30° specimen is consistent with tensile-shear crack growth from the fracture tips and subsequent coalescence. The lower Epre of the 45° specimen may reflect more progressive accommodation through interface sliding, debonding, and distributed mixed-mode cracking. For the 60° specimen, the increase in Epre relative to the 45° specimen, together with denser AE activity and the following corresponding DIC pattern, is consistent with enhanced fracture-tip stress concentration, interface slip, crack branching, and network-like coalescence. Accordingly, Epre provides a relative energy-scale comparison, while the stress response and DIC fields provide complementary constraints on the interpretation of the failure mechanism.

Deformation field evolution

Influence of F-T Cycles

Using the procedure described above, the strain-localization indicators were extracted from the representative DIC sequences. The threshold-based definition was applied consistently to all specimens. Figure 10 shows the evolution of the axial-strain field in intact specimens after different numbers of F-T cycles. For each condition, five snapshots were selected at the start of loading, during compaction, at localization onset, at peak stress, and after peak stress. F-T cycles accelerated the development of localized deformation relative to the peak state. In the unfrozen specimen, a distinct high-strain band formed only late in the loading process, whereas in the freeze-thawed specimens localized zones developed earlier and propagated more rapidly. The quantitative indicators support this observation. For 0, 20, 40, and 60 F-T cycles, the peak axial strains were approximately 0.68%, 1.00%, 1.14%, and 1.21%, and the corresponding localization-onset strains were 0.52%, 0.65%, 0.83%, and 0.87%, respectively. Although the absolute localization-onset strain increased as compaction deformation became more pronounced, the normalized onset time decreased from 0.76 in the unfrozen specimen to 0.65–0.73 after F-T cycles. Localization therefore occurred earlier relative to peak stress in the freeze-thawed specimens. The apparent width of the primary localization band also decreased from approximately 7.0 mm at N = 0 to 4.1 mm at N = 60. Thus, F-T damage increased overall deformability while concentrating pre-failure deformation into a narrower localized zone.

Influence of fracture dip angles

Figure 11 displays the axial-strain-field evolution of filled fractured specimens with different dip angles after 40 F-T cycles. For dip angles of 30°, 45°, and 60°, the peak axial strains were approximately 1.02%, 0.93%, and 1.04%, respectively, and the localization-onset strains were 0.71%, 0.65%, and 0.70%. The corresponding normalized onset times were 0.70, 0.70, and 0.67, indicating that the 60° specimen localized earliest relative to its peak state. The apparent widths of the primary bands were approximately 4.8, 4.4, and 3.9 mm, respectively. The 60° specimen, however, also developed several secondary bands, producing a total multi-band localized-zone span of approximately 12–18 mm. Increasing the fracture dip angle therefore did not simply broaden a single band; instead, it promoted multiple narrow competing bands, crack branching, and a more complex network-like failure pattern.

The transverse relaxation-time spectra are shown in Fig. 12. Under identical saturation and acquisition conditions, T2 is positively related to equivalent pore radius, and signal amplitude represents the relative volume of water-filled pores. Rather than assigning each spectral peak to a single pore class, the T2 signals were integrated over the five intervals defined above. The short-T2 component is dominated by micropores and small pores, the intermediate-T2 component mainly represents medium and large pores, and the long-T2 component is associated with macropores and fracture-like voids.

With increasing numbers of F-T cycles, the T2 spectra generally showed higher amplitudes, a rightward shift, and a larger integrated area. These changes indicate progressive transfer of pore volume from short- to long-T2 intervals as pre-existing pores and microcracks expanded and coalesced. F-T damage therefore involved both an increase in water-filled pore volume and the enlargement and interconnection of the pore network. Fracture orientation further modified this evolution. At the same number of cycles, fractured specimens generally exhibited larger spectral areas and amplitudes than intact specimens. As the dip angle increased from 30° to 60°, the rightward spectral shift and long-T2 component became more pronounced. After 60 cycles, the long-T2 signal of the 60° specimen was stronger than that of the 30° specimen. This trend is consistent with larger-angle fractures providing preferential paths for frost-heave stress transmission and promoting pore enlargement, connectivity, and fracture-like void formation.

Figure 13 quantifies the evolution of the five pore-size classes. Before F-T cycles, the pore structure was dominated by micropores, small pores, and medium pores. With increasing numbers of cycles, the micropore proportion decreased, while the small- and medium-pore proportions initially increased. As damage accumulated, large pores expanded and macropores emerged. The evolution can therefore be summarized as micropore reduction → small-pore increase → medium-pore growth → large-pore expansion → macropore initiation. Fracture dip angle significantly modulated this evolution. Even before F-T cycles, fractured specimens contained higher proportions of small and medium pores than intact specimens, indicating that the prefabricated filled fractures acted as preferential regions for pore development. As the dip angle increased from 30° to 60°, the development of medium pores, large pores, and macropores became more pronounced, particularly after repeated cycles. These results are consistent with larger-angle fractures intensifying localized frost-heave effects and facilitating the connection of secondary pores and cracks. Overall, F-T cycles transformed the sandstone from a relatively dense structure dominated by smaller pores into a more connected network containing larger pores and fracture-like voids. The resulting increases in pore size and connectivity provide a meso-scale explanation for the observed loss of strength and deformation stability.

Discussion

The results support a cross-scale damage mechanism in which F-T-induced pore-structure evolution is linked to macroscopic mechanical deterioration. Volume expansion during pore-water freezing weakens matrix cementation and causes irreversible damage to accumulate, while fracture geometry influences the transmission and localization of frost-heave stress. At the meso-scale, the coupled process follows the five-stage sequence of micropore reduction, small-pore increase, medium-pore growth, large-pore expansion, and macropore initiation. Larger fracture dip angles promote the shift toward larger pore classes and cause specimens containing high-angle fractures to reach an accelerated degradation state earlier. The coupled damage also alters the energy-dissipation pathway, as reflected by earlier AE activity and the transition from a dominant localization band to multi-band and network-like deformation.

These findings suggest that cold-region rock engineering should account explicitly for the combined effects of F-T cycles and filled fractures. High-angle filled fractures may warrant priority inspection because the tested 60° specimens exhibited greater pore enlargement and strength loss. Where site-specific stability analyses confirm a need for reinforcement, targeted grouting or anchoring may be considered to interrupt preferential damage paths. In addition, the earlier AE activity and relative advance of strain localization observed after F-T cycles indicate that combined AE and full-field deformation monitoring may support earlier warning of damage acceleration.

This study focused on sandstone containing cement-mortar-filled fractures under uniaxial compression. The effects of filling-material heterogeneity and complex stress states require further investigation. The single mortar mix used here does not represent the range of clay, crushed rock, and other fillings encountered in engineering practice; comparative testing of different filling materials and properties is therefore needed33,34. Future studies could also combine synchronized AE-DIC measurements with mixed-mode stress-intensity factors or J-integral analyses and RA-AF classification to better distinguish tensile, shear, and interface-related contributions to crack propagation and AE-energy evolution under complex stress states.
