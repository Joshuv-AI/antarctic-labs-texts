Geospatial assessment of natural radionuclides in urban soil of Chennai Metropolitan City
Authors: K Manikandan Bharath, Satyanarayan Bramha, S Chandrasekaran, M Krishnaveni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluates the distribution of terrestrial radionuclides and their associated radiological indices in urban topsoil samples from Chennai. Two hundred one (201) urban topsoil samples were collected across the Chennai metropolitan area, and gamma-emitting radionuclides were measured using NaI (TI) gamma-ray spectrometers. The activity concentrations of 238U varied from 12 ± 1.53 to 118 ± 9.63 with an average of 21 (stdev ± 16.14) Bq/kg. dry, 232Th varied from 24 ± 4.23 to 268 ± 17.83 with an average of 96 (stdev ± 45.88) Bq/kg. dry, and 40K varied from 126 ± 11.56 to 918 ± 32.52 with an average of 431 (stdev ± 88.69) Bq/kg. dry. These results indicate that the abundance of terrestrial radionuclides in urban topsoil follows 40K > 232Th > 238U. The study found that the activity concentration of 238U is within the recommended limits, while 232Th and 40K exceed the national limits by 1.58 and 1.11 times, respectively, and the international limits by 3.2 and 1.05 times, respectively. Radiological indices and dose parameters were calculated and described in detail, including Radium Equivalent Activity, Dose Rate, Annual Effective Dose Equivalent, Activity Utilization Index, Internal Hazard Index, External Hazard Index, Alpha Index, and Gamma Index. The activity utilization index (AUI) values ranged from 0.4 to 3.7, with a mean value of 1.4. The radionuclide activity concentration data suggest that geo-genic sources and industrial activities influence the topsoil in Chennai. This reflects the presence of heavy minerals, urban industrialization, and the characteristics of the study area.Keywords: Natural radionuclides, Geospatial mapping, Radiological indices and dose parameters, Gamma spectrometer, Urban topsoil, Chennai Metropolis CitySubject terms: Environmental sciences, Health care

Introduction

Earth’s natural radioactive elements originate in geogenic processes in the crust1,2. Urban soils are formed through natural soil transformation with the participation of technogenic sediment genesis, which leads to disturbance of natural radionuclides migration processes and contributes to the complex structure of nature3. Urban radiological characteristics are particularly informative and can provide valuable insights into transportation mechanisms and the rate of radionuclides4–6. In urban areas, topsoil is primarily derived from the weathering and erosion of inland rocks7,8. These deposited topsoils emit natural radiation and contribute to the background dose of the region. The background radiation varies temporally and spatially due to its origins and types of rocks9–12. Human activities, such as industrial processes, constructions, the use of certain fertilizers, and improper disposal of radioactive materials, can raise radioactivity levels in urban soils, which can adversely affect the urban environment and public health13–16. Urban soil radioactivity must be monitored and regulated to protect inhabitants and habitats.

Terrestrial radionuclides, mainly 238U, 232Th, and 40K, are present everywhere on the Earth’s surface, environment, and also in the human body17–19. Since the soil is the major part of the environment and its derivatives (mostly construction materials) are widely used by humans, both contain natural radiation, which is the cause of human exposure20,21. Accurately assessing natural radionuclides in the environment is crucial for evaluating human exposure22. Investigation on natural radionuclides has received keen attention in the world and has led to extensive studies in many countries12,23–27. Due to the increasing focus on environmental radiological protection. The concentration of the radionuclides 238U, 232Th, and 40K in the soil samples of the urban area, Chennai metropolis, was collected. The gamma ray spectrometer system was widely used for measuring the activity concentrations of soil radionuclides11,12,14,28. Therefore, investigating natural radionuclides in urban soil is essential to public health. This can be used to assess the public’s radiation exposure and estimate radiological health indices. Consequently, monitoring and regulating the levels of terrestrial radioactivity in urban soils is paramount to safeguard the well-being of urban inhabitants and the environment. Evaluating the soil, ensured that the created poetries and artworks had radiological incidence considerably below the natural limit29.

In this study area, many researchers have shown interest in different aspects; assessment of groundwater quality has been carried out30–32. Due to rapid industrialization, the air quality of Chennai city became very poor, and the air quality was evaluated33,34. Geochemical evaluation and heavy metal contamination in urban soil samples35–38. Studies on the prevalence and characteristics of microplastics in dust samples39. The research explores the public radiological adverse effects of perpetual ingestion of quarry-grown plants40. Natural radionuclides indicate the inherent radioactivity within the soil, lithological formations, and various materials positioned on the Earth’s terrestrial surface. Recently, many researchers have carried out levels in Chennai beach/coastal sand and sediment samples12,41–44.

Chennai has rapidly developed into a metropolitan city in the past decades, with industries, metro projects, IT sectors, and institutions. Furthermore, the city’s population has also increased drastically. Therefore, a study was conducted to assess the geospatial distribution of natural radionuclides and the current status of radiological indices and dose parameters, including Radium Equivalent Activity (Raeq), Dose Rate (DR), Annual Effective Dose Equivalent (AEDE), Activity Utilization Index (AUI), Internal Hazard Index (Hin), External Hazard Index (Hex), Alpha Index (Iα), Gamma Index (Iγ), were evaluated based on the activity concentrations, for urban topsoil, Chennai metropolitan city, Tamil Nadu. Hence, radioactive concentrations in research areas must be regulated to safeguard from gamma dose buildup, which may render these areas inappropriate for residents. The radiological survey aims to monitor the urban environment and ensure radiation protection for humans and environment of the Chennai metropolitan city, Tamil Nadu.

Materials and methodology

Study area

Chennai, the capital of Tamil Nadu and a global metropolis, is located in the Northeastern part of the state at 13.04°N 80.17°E on India’s Southeastern coast. The city proper is enormous, 476 km2 (184 sq mi), covering 2,100 acres. Geographically, the city’s four main areas are North, Central, South, and West. Chennai ranks as the fifth most populous city and the fourth largest metropolitan area in India, with a population of 10.43 million33,39. Rapid population growth could lead to various indirect environmental impacts. Chennai has seen rapid urbanization, with more people traveling to the city for better economic and living conditions. The northern part of Chennai has the most factories and industries. The central area of Chennai is the business and commercial hub. South and West Chennai, historically residential areas, are now rapidly transforming into commercial centers with many IT and financial institutions45.

The study area spans 180 km along India’s Coromandel Coast and includes two major ports, Chennai and Ennore. This region’s average annual wind speed is approximately 14.82 Km/h. The coastal area experiences three distinct seasonal patterns: the North-East monsoon from October to January, the Southwest monsoon from June to September, and the summer season from February to May. Seasonal changes have led to noticeable morpho-dynamic activities, prompting the selection of sampling locations based on specific characteristics, particularly near urban and domestic effluent discharge points. Marina Beach lies between Chennai Port and the Adyar River estuary. At the same time, VGP Beach, a popular tourist destination attracting visitors from around the globe, is situated 40 km south of Marina Beach and just 3.3 km from the Muttukadu backwaters, a well-known spot for boating. In addition to trade and transportation, Chennai is a hub for electronics, manufacturing, automotive industries, and medical services. However, the Muttukadu backwaters often receive significant industrial waste, eventually flowing into the nearby marine wetlands.

Geology of the study area

Chennai’s geological comprises three distinct layers, ancient Archaean crystalline rocks like charnockites and gneisses, compacted Gondwana and Tertiary sediments such as shales and sandstones, and surface-level recent Alluvium, especially prominent near rivers and the coastline46. Sandy soil dominates the coastal and riverine zones, enabling swift infiltration of rainwater. In contrast, the city’s interior is largely covered by clay-rich soils, which slow down water flow but retain moisture for longer periods47. Southern localities, including Guindy, Velachery, and Adambakkam, rest on hard-rock formations made up of fractured Archaean charnockites shaped by tectonic forces. This geological diversity plays a crucial role in influencing water movement, soil behavior, and urban development across the region48.

Sampling techniques and processing for measurement

The collection and processing of urban topsoil samples were thoroughly detailed49–51, while Hedge et al. presented techniques for sampling and processing of soil samples52. A systematic grid sampling was implemented to uniformly collect urban topsoil samples (201 locations) from the study area (Fig. 1). A handheld GARMIN GPS (Global Positioning System, Model 12) was used to collect the coordinates of each sampling location. To cover the entire Chennai metro, 2.5 × 2.5 km grid-based sampling was carried out with a sampling area of approximately 1 × 1 m2 for inland soil samples (1 kg) collected at 0–20 cm depth. The soil samples were thoroughly mixed using the cone and quartering method, placed in labelled polyethylene bags, and immediately transported to the laboratory53. The samples were dried using a hot air oven at 100°-110 °C to ensure a steady weight, pulverized, sieved via 0.2 mm sieve to eliminate gravel, pebbles, debris, and other wider-level contaminants, and air-tight packed in 250 ml high-density polyethylene (HDPE) plastic container (approximately 300–400 gms of samples)14. Hermetically and externally, cellophane tape was adopted to seal the container and prevent 222Rn and 220Rn from escaping54,55. To establish a secular equilibrium between 238U and 232Th and their corresponding offspring, the specimen and its sealed container were set aside for roughly four weeks before any laboratory tests were performed11,12,56.

Determination of activity concentrations using gamma-ray spectrometer

Gamma spectrometry with a high absorption efficiency, 3″x3″ NaI (Tl) detector (AMETEK ORTEC, USA) was used to measure radionuclides 238U, 232Th, and 40K of the urban topsoil samples collected from Chennai Metropolitan City49. Gamma spectrum analysis was performed on all samples with a count time of 20,000 s57. Increasing the counting time in gamma spectrometry enhances the gamma peaks’ statistical significance and visibility. Reducing the background count further improves peak prominence58,59. The study explored suitable lead shielding coupled with a multichannel analyser (Maestro, USA) at the Health Physics Section, Health and Industrial Safety Division, Safety, Quality and Resource management Group, Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research (IGCAR), Govt. of India, Kalpakkam, Tamil Nadu. The detector calibration was carried out using gamma energies standard disk sources of 137Cs (661.6 keV), and 60Co (1173 and 1332.5 keV) supplied by Board of Radiation and Isotope Technology. Mumbai. Efficiency calibration was carried out using Uranium Ore (RGU-1), Thorium Ore (RGTh-1), and Potassium (RGK-1). IAEA and AQCS provide information on these reference resources60,61. The photo peaks observed in gamma-ray spectroscopy correspond to 1764 keV (214Bi) for 238U, 2614 keV (208Tl) for 232Th, and 1460 keV for 40K. The precision of activity in 250 ml geometry is estimated within ± 8% accuracy of the certified value. Stick QA/QC procedure followed in measuring NORMs to obtain quality data11,12,62. The minimum detectable activity (MDA) for each radionuclide was calculated using the background radiation spectrum at a 2σ confidence level, and the values for 232Th, 238U, and 40K are 3, 3, and 11 Bq/kg. dry, respectively. The background count was obtained by counting an empty beaker for the same duration as the sample and then subtracting it from the gross count. The average counts of the initial and final channels were multiplied by the total number of channels to obtain the total counts in Compton’s contribution. Then, it was subtracted from the background to correct the total counts under the photo peak63.

The uncertainty in measuring natural radionuclides using a gamma spectrometer is thoroughly evaluated before reporting results. Counting time, the number of counts obtained during spectral analysis, and detector efficiency calibration is given special importance. Additionally, uncertainties from background measurements with various counting periods are assessed. All these factors are considered for the overall uncertainty associated with the measurement of 238U, 232Th, and 40K11,62,64.

Results and discussions

Geospatial distribution of urban topsoil radionuclide activities

The variation of the activity concentrations of natural radionuclides in the Chennai metropolis topsoil samples is depicted in Box-Whisker plots in Fig. 2; Table 1. A box plot of activity concentrations gives information about how the values in the data are spread out. The box and whisker plots are plotted using SPSS (ver. 20). The lower and higher whisker shows minimum and maximum value, the lower and upper line of the box showed first and third quintile of the data whereas the middle line is the median value of the data. From the results it observed that, 238U varied from 12 ± 1.53 to 118 ± 9.63 with an average of 21 (stdev ± 16.14) Bq/kg. dry, 232Th varied from 24 ± 4.23 to 268 ± 17.83 with an average of 96 (stdev ± 45.88) Bq/kg. dry, and 40K varied from 126 ± 11.56 to 918 ± 32.52 with an average of 431 (stdev ± 88.69) Bq/kg. dry. The activity concentration of 40K was much higher than that of the other two naturally occurring radionuclides, which was also reported by Babai68. The geospatial distribution activity concentration of the terrestrial radionuclides (238U, 232Th, 40K) was plotted using the geospatial tools analysis module in Arc GIS (Ver. 10.8.1), which is shown in Fig. 3. The result clearly indicates that the abundance of radionuclides follows 40K>232Th>238U in the urban soil of Chennai. The significant variation of the activity concentrations in the study area may be due to its geological formations69. The Indian average levels of 238U, 232Th, and 40K for the soil are 29, 64, and 400 Bq/kg. dry, respectively, while the world average levels are 32, 45, and 420 Bq/kg. dry, respectively67. The study’s results suggested that only 238U is well within the world and Indian average values. Other radionuclides, such as ²³²Th and ⁴⁰K, exceed the Indian average limits by factors of 1.58 and 1.11, and the global average limits by 3.2 and 1.05 times, respectively.

The high activities of the radionuclides 238U, 232Th, and 40K are present in the northern part of the study area. It is considered an industrial zone and a coastal zone. Additionally, it was determined that the concentration level of 40K inside the samples was higher than that of 238U and 232Th. This was most likely due to greater concentrations of 40K, a micronutrient in the soil whose mobilization and associated transportation have resulted in such 238U levels70. Terrestrial radionuclides found in soil are responsible for external exposures, which add to the total radiation dose absorbed by humans. The current research indicates that 232Th concentrations exceed those of 238U at most sampling sites in the study area. This could be attributed to the variations in heavy mineral content in the environment and the fact that 232Th, an insoluble isotope, tends to accumulate in the solid phases of earth materials compared to 238U71.

The analysis reveals that, 18.75% of the surveyed locations exhibit higher concentrations of 238U than those found in Chennai’s urban soils. 6.25% of the locations show lower concentrations of 232Th. 37.5% of the locations have lower concentrations of 40K compared to Chennai’s topsoil samples.

Ratio between natural radionuclides

Figure 4 shows the geospatial distribution of the eTh/eU ratio, and the value ranges from 0.2 to 5.5, with a mean of 2. The values calculated for all the locations in this study are more significant than Clark’s (Clark’s value = 3.5), indicating enrichment of Thorium ions in the study region, as is commonly observed in sampling sites. Expressing the results as equivalent uranium (eU) and thorium (eTh) concentrations is preferable. Therefore, measurements of ²³⁸U and ²³²Th by gamma spectrometry are labelled with a prefix’ e’, and the Th/U ratio is represented as eTh/eU96,97. The ratio of eTh/eU is mainly reflected in the geological setting of the parent rock. The eTh/eU ratios range from 0 to 2 in anoxic conditions and exceed 7 for strong oxidizing conditions. eTh/eU ratios were less than two (rich uranium), the depositional environment encouraged uranium fixation under likely reducing conditions, and the environment was most likely a coastal environment98. Such eTh/eU ratios are within the range of values obtained in a leached uranium environment (Th/U > 7), indicating that the samples were generated in a highly oxidized terrestrial environment containing sand facies from the uranium-rich environment due to the inland river and soil erosion. Uranium is often found in deficit with respect to thorium in the earth’s crust because the U6+ oxidation state is soluble99,100, whereas the Th4+ oxidation state is quite insoluble101. The natural concentrations of 238U and 232Th in the earth’s crust are 2.9 and 9.6 mg/kg, respectively, leading to a ratio of 0.3 for 238U/232Th, which is the reported global ratio102.

The ratio of Th/U varied from 2 to 49.7 with an average of 18 and Th/K ranged from 3 to 50 with an average of 17. These results suggested that the collected soil samples were maximum enriched in the clay minerals, followed by the mica and feldspar minerals in the study area (Fig. 5). The thorium-to-uranium (eTh/eU) ratio varies with sedimentary processes, ascertaining the sampling sites environmental status and depositional environment (Fig. 5). Radiological indices and dose parameters for urban soil samples

The distribution of natural radionuclides 238U, 232Th, and 40K is not uniform worldwide25. Figure 6 shows the variation of the estimated radiological indices and dose parameters, such as Raeq, DR, AEDE, and AUI, of collected topsoil samples by box-whisker plots, whereas the geospatial distribution is depicted in Fig. 7. The radium equivalent (Raeq) must be determined, which is an index used to compare the activities of materials containing a single quantity of radionuclide 238U, 232Th, and 40K and that takes into consideration the radiological assessments67. Radium equivalent is associated with external and internal gamma exposure from radon and its progeny. Radium equivalent activity (Raeq) varied from 62 to 470 Bq/kg. dry with an average value of 191 Bq/kg. dry, less than the world-recommended safe limit of 370 Bq/kg. dry67,104.

The most significant part of measuring radiation exposure to gamma radiation is determining dose rate (DR), calculated using above Eq. 367,105. The effects of gamma radiation in environmental sources are often represented in terms of DR absorbing a portion of the total gamma radiation dose rate in the air. It is an average energy transferred to matter per unit mass by ionizing radiation106. The estimated DR ranged from 29 to 212 nGy h-1 with an average value of 86 nGy h-1, which is only two points higher than the worldwide average of 84 nGy h-1. The world average values were adopted based on the population-weighted average suggested by UNSCEAR 2. Further, the absorbed air dose rate in the study area is more than the Indian average of 64 nGy h-1. All the degradation products of both radioactive series 238U and 232Th in soil samples are expected to be in radioisotope equilibrium with their precursors, with constant movement with depth107.

The annual effective dose equivalent (AEDE) for indoor and outdoor exposure varies between 0.03 and 0.3 mSv/y and 0.1 to 1.0 mSv/y, respectively (Fig. 6). The average AEDE values are 0.1 mSv/y for indoor exposure and 0.4 mSv/y for outdoor exposure, both of which remain below the globally recommended limit of 1 mSv/y. It indicates the soil samples represent no radioactive risk to the people living in the study region. To determine the annual effective dose equivalent (AEDE), one must consider the conversion factor from an absorbed dose in the air to an effective dose and the indoor and outdoor occupancy factors. As per the UNSCEAR report, a value of 0.7 Sv/Gy was adopted for the conversion factor from the absorbed dose in the air to the effective dose received by human health, and 0.8 and 0.2 for the indoor and outdoor occupancy factor, meaning that 80% and 20% of the time is spent outside on average throughout the world67. As a result, long-term exposure to this soil poses no risk. The proportional contribution of radioisotopes to the annual effective dosage was 40K>232Th>238U. The dosage was thought to be negligible since they contributed relatively low to the overall dose to the environment. The activity utilization index was estimated for the collected soil samples, as shown in Figs. 6 and 7. The AUI values varied from 0.4 to 3.7, with a mean value of 1.4. The computed AUI values of the collected sample from the study area fall within the recommended values (< 2). Hence, collected soil samples do not pose any radiological risk in their usage as construction material.

It is critical to evaluate the various radiological health parameters present in the coastal urban stretches of the study area. Radiological indices and dose parameters such as Hin, Hex, Iα, and Iγ were calculated as per the above Eqs. (6–9). As the soil samples are collected from the urban environment where people live and work, evaluating the radiological health indices will provide valuable information regarding legal limits and their implications for the health of residents in the Chennai metropolis. The variation of the radiological health indices is shown in Fig. 6, and the geospatial variation is depicted in Fig. 7. The internal and external index (Hin, Hex) indicate the external radiation exposure associated with radionuclide-related gamma radiation and help to understand the effects of radionuclide exposure on human health19,108. The internal radiological index quantifies the internal exposure to radon and its daughter products. Radon and its short-lived metabolites and external radiation are harmful to the respiratory organs109. The index value Hin ranges from 0.2 to 1.6 with an average of 0.6 mSv/y, and the Hex index value ranges from 0.2 to 1.2 with an average of 0.5 mSv/y (Fig. 6). The Hex and Hin indexes’ average values are below the threshold UNSCEAR recommended, which was ≤ 167. This study suggested that all the collected sampling sites were under the safe limit.

The gamma representative index (Iγ) is a commonly used screening tool that helps assess the levels of radionuclides in human health following exposure to an annual effective dose of gamma radiation from radioisotopes present in soil samples110. Measuring and monitoring gamma radiation inside the human body is very difficult. The study area’s gamma radiation representative index value ranges from 0.2 to 1.7, with an average of 0.7 (Fig. 6). For structural material, the exemption dosage criterion (annual effective dose) of 0.3 mSv/y corresponds to a gamma index of Iγ ≤ 0.5, whereas the upper dose criterion of 1 mSv/y is fulfilled for Iγ ≤ 1. It is suggested that the gamma index of the collected samples be within the recommended limit. The internal (α-radioactivity) index (Iα) was estimated to be the extra alpha radiation caused by radon inhalation. The alpha index ranges from 0.1 to 0.6, with an average of 0.1 (Fig. 6). The collected samples’ alpha index (Iα) is within the permissible limit.

Limitations of the study

Chennai urban soils exhibit high spatial variability due to diverse sources such as industrial emissions, construction debris, and solid waste from the dumping areas. This uneven soil sample distribution completes the sampling and data interpretation. As the sample variability was very high because of solid waste and pollution, it was difficult to distinguish the anthropogenic contribution to the natural radionuclide activities. NaI (Tl) based gamma ray spectrometer was used to measure the natural radionuclides. Due to extensive sample collection, the counting time was fixed to 20,000 s, which may restrict the ability to measure low activity concentrations. In the measurement processes, achieving secular equilibrium between their daughter products requires long-time storage, which was very challenging. The collection of samples widely varies with physical and chemical properties, which can influence radionuclides’ mobility and gamma signal attenuation. Due to the above, our study showed an extensive variation in the distribution of radionuclides and calculated dose parameters.

Conclusion

The study presents the concentration, spatial distribution, environmental radiological indices, and radiological health risk assessment of radionuclides 238U, 232Th, and 40K in urban topsoil samples collected from the Chennai Metropolitan City, Tamil Nadu, India. Notably, this is the first report of its kind for this region. These results suggest that the abundance of terrestrial radionuclides follows 40K>232Th>238U in the urban topsoil of Chennai. The result of the study indicated that only 238U is well within the recommended values. Other radionuclides, such as 232Th and 40K, are above the national (1.58 and 1.11 times) and world (3.2 and 1.05 times) higher than recommended limits. The high activity is noticed in the northern part of the study area; it is considered an industrial and coastal zone. Raeq is associated with external and internal gamma exposure from radon and its progeny. Radium equivalent activity (Raeq) ranged from 62 to 470 Bq/kg. dry with an average value of 191 Bq/kg. dry, which is less than the world-recommended safe limit (370 Bq/kg. dry) The mean values for indoor and outdoor AEDE are 0.1 and 0.4 mSv/y, respectively, within the world-recommended limits of 1 mSv/y The Th/U ratio ranges from 0.2 to 5.5, with a mean of 2. The thorium values calculated for all locations in this study exceed Clark’s value (3.5), suggesting thorium enrichment in the study region, a common characteristic of sampling sites. To ensure that the radiation hazard from building materials remains insignificant, the Hex and Hin index values must be kept below unity, thereby limiting the annual radiation dose to less than 1.0 mSv. The study findings are crucial for examining the potential health risks linked to the levels of radionuclide activity in Chennai’s soil since they establish a baseline for future variations in environmental radioactivity induced through both naturally occurring radioactive materials and human activity in coastal urban areas. Based on the findings from this study, it has been confirmed that the levels of natural radionuclides are below both the global and Indian average values. Consequently, they do not pose any radiological threat to the public and urban environment.
