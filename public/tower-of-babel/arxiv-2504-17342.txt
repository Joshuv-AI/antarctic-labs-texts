Fréchet Distance in Unweighted Planar Graphs
Ivor van der Hoog, Thijs van der Horst, Eva Rotenberg, Lasse Wulf

Abstract
The Fréchet distance is a distance measure between trajectories in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} or walks in a graph G G . Given constant-time shortest path queries, the Discrete Fréchet distance D_G ⁡ ( P , Q ) \frechet(P,Q) between two walks P P and Q Q can be computed in O ⁡ ( | P | ⋅ | Q | ) O(|P|\cdot|Q|) time using a dynamic program. Driemel, van der Hoog, and Rotenberg [SoCG’22] show that for weighted planar graphs this approach is likely tight, as there can be no strongly-subquadratic algorithm to compute a 1.01 1.01 -approximation of D_G ⁡ ( P , Q ) \frechet(P,Q) unless the Orthogonal Vector Hypothesis (OVH) fails. Such quadratic-time conditional lower bounds are common to many Fréchet distance variants. However, they can be circumvented by assuming that the input comes from some well-behaved class: There exist ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximations, both in weighted graphs and in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , that take near-linear time for c c -packed or κ \kappa -straight walks in the graph. In ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} there also exists a near-linear time algorithm to compute the Fréchet distance whenever all input edges are long compared to the distance. We consider computing the Fréchet distance in unweighted planar graphs. We show that there exist no strongly-subquadratic 1.25 1.25 -approximations of the discrete Fréchet distance between two disjoint simple paths in an unweighted planar graph in strongly subquadratic time, unless OVH fails. This improves the previous lower bound, both in terms of generality and approximation factor. We subsequently show that adding graph structure circumvents this lower bound: If the graph is a regular tiling with unit-weighted edges, then there exists an O ~ ​ ( ( | P | + | Q | ) 1.5 ) \tilde{O}((|P|+|Q|)^{1.5}) -time algorithm to compute D_G ⁡ ( P , Q ) \frechet(P,Q) . Our result has natural implications in the plane, as it allows us to define a new class of well-behaved curves that facilitate ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximations of their discrete Fréchet distance in subquadratic time.

Introduction

The Fréchet distance is a widely used metric for measuring the similarity between trajectories. It is often illustrated through a metaphor: imagine a person walking along one trajectory and their dog following another, connected by a leash. The Fréchet distance is the minimum possible leash length over all synchronized traversals of both trajectories.

This metric has numerous applications, particularly in movement data analysis
[
10
,
19
,
26
,
16
,
5
,
14
,
27
,
36
]
. It is versatile, having been applied to handwriting recognition
[
31
]
, coastlines
[
28
]
, geometric shapes in geographic information systems
[
17
]
, and trajectories of moving objects such as vehicles, animals, and athletes
[
30
,
32
,
6
,
14
]
.
We consider the
discrete
Fréchet distance, where trajectories are modeled as sequences of discrete points.

Alt and Godau
[
2
]
were the first to analyse the Fréchet distance from a computational perspective. They considered trajectories as polygonal curves in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
n
and
m
vertices, and compute the continuous Fréchet distance in
O(mn\log(n+m))
time. Later, Buchin, Buchin, Meulemans and Mulzer
[
12
]
introduced a faster randomized algorithm, achieving a running time of
O(n^{2}\sqrt{\log n}(\log\log n)^{3/2})
on a real-valued pointer machine and
O(n^{2}\log\log n)
on a word RAM.
For the discrete Fréchet distance, Eiter and Mannila
[
21
]
presented an
O(nm)
time algorithm under constant-time distance computations. Agarwal, Avraham, Kaplan, and Sharir
[
1
]
later improved this to
O(nm(\log\log nm)/\log nm)
in the word RAM.

Driemel, van der Hoog, and Rotenberg
[
20
]

initiate the study of Fréchet distance in graphs.
They consider as input a (weighted) graph
G
where a trajectory is a walk in the graph.
The distance between two vertices is the length of their shortest path.
Given a constant-time
distance oracle, the discrete Fréchet distance
\frechet(P,Q)
between any two walks in
G
can then be computed in
O(nm)
time using the algorithm by Eiter and Mannila
[
21
]
.

Conditional Lower Bounds.

Several conditional lower bounds exist for computing the Fréchet distance or its constant-factor approximations. These results rely on complexity assumptions such as the Orthogonal Vector Hypothesis (OVH) and the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH)
[
35
]
.
Bringmann
[
7
]
established that no algorithm can compute the (discrete or continuous) Fréchet distance between two polygonal curves of
n
vertices in
O(n^{2-\delta})
time for any
\delta>0
. The same lower bound holds for small constant-factor approximations. Bringmann’s proof originally involved self-intersecting curves in the plane, but later work by Bringmann and Mulzer
[
9
]
extended the result to intersecting curves in
\mathbb{R}^{1}
. Additionally, Bringmann
[
7
]
proved a lower bound for unbalanced inputs: given two curves with
n
and
m
vertices in the plane, no algorithm can compute the Fréchet distance in
O((nm)^{1-\delta})
time assuming OVH.
Buchin, Ophelders, and Speckmann
[
13
]
further demonstrated that, assuming OVH, no
O((nm)^{1-\delta})
time algorithm can compute better than a 3-approximation of the Fréchet distance for pairwise-disjoint planar curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or intersecting curves in
\mathbb{R}^{1}
.
Driemel, van der Hoog and Rotenberg
[
20
]
give a similar lower bound for paths in a weighted planar graph.
They prove that, unless OVH fails, no strongly-subquadratic algorithm can
1.01
-approximate the discrete Fréchet distance between arbitrary paths in a weighted planar graph — even if the ratio between smallest and highest weight is bounded by a constant.
This lower bound applies only to weighted graphs and does therefore not exclude the existence of a fast algorithm on unweighted planar graphs.
A closer inspection of their argument reveals that their proof cannot simply be adapted to the unweighted case by subdividing long edges often enough.
In this case, new vertices get introduced to the graph and their arguments break down.

Avoiding lower bounds through well-behaved curves.

These lower bounds can be circumvented if the input is well-behaved.
Driemel, Har-peled and Wenk
[
18
]
consider three classes of curves in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which are well-behaved as long as their corresponding behavioural parameter is low.
These are
c
-packed curves
[
18
,
8
]
,
\phi
-low density curves
[
18
]
, and
\kappa
-bounded curves
[
3
,
4
,
18
]
.
They show algorithms to compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation between two well-behaved curves whose running time is near-linear in
n
and
m
.
Gudmundsson, Mirzanezhad, Mohades, and Wenk consider the special case where all edges of the input curves are long with respect to their Fréchet distance
[
24
]
. In this case, the Fréchet distance can be computed in
O((n+m)\log(n+m))
time
[
24
]
.
Driemel, van der Hoog, and Rotenberg
[
20
]
show that if the input are walks in a graph
G
and one of the walks is restricted to a
c
-packed or
\kappa
-straight path, then there exists
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximations that take near-linear time.

Contribution.

Driemel, van der Hoog, and Rotenberg
[
20
]
ruled out a strongly subquadratic algorithm for a
1.01
-approximation between paths in a weighted graph, while the construction by Buchin, Ophelders, and Speckmann
[
13
]
rules out a strongly subquadratic algorithm for a
3
-approximation between walks in an unweighted planar graph.
We consider the discrete Fréchet distance between paths in an unweighted planar graph. We prove (Theorem
5
) that no strongly-subquadratic
1.25
-approximation algorithm exists unless OVH fails, thereby improving both the generality (and also the approximation factor for planar graphs).

Next, we prove that adding graph structure circumvents this lower bound.
If
G
is an unweighted regular tiling of the plane, then there exists an
\tilde{O}((n+m)^{1.5})
-time algorithm to compute the Fréchet distance
\frechet(P,Q)
, between curves of length
n
and
m
.
Our results have natural implications in the plane, for a special class of well-behaved curves (
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves, to be defined). For those, we can compute the Fréchet distance under the
L_{1}
metric (respectively, approximate it for any
L_{c}
metric) in
\tilde{O}(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\varepsilon}(n+m)^{1.5})
time (respectively,
\tilde{O}(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\varepsilon\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(n+m)^{1.5})
time).
For completeness, we explain the continuous analogy to
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves in Appendix
C
.
We compare this new curve class to existing well-behaved curve classes in Appendix
D
.

Preliminaries

Let
G
be an unweighted graph where
V(G)
is its vertex set.
We define
P=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
and
Q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})
as two paths in
G
. A path cannot visit a vertex twice.
For integers
i,i^{\prime}\in[n]
with
i\leq i^{\prime}
we denote by
P[i:i^{\prime}]
the subpath of
P
from
p_{i}
to
p_{i^{\prime}}
.
We define the discrete Fréchet distance
\frechet(P,Q)
between
P
and
Q
as follows
[
20
]
:
A sequence
(a_{i},b_{i})_{i\in[k]}
of pairs of indices is a
monotone walk
if for all
i\in[k-1]
we have
a_{i+1}\in\{a_{i},a_{i}+1\}
and
b_{i+1}\in\{b_{i},b_{i}+1\}
. Let
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
be the set of all monotone walks from
(1,1)
to
(n,m)
.

The cost of
F\in\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
is the maximum of
\dist(p_{i},q_{j})
over
(i,j)\in F
, where
\dist()
denotes the length of the shortest path between vertices.
\frechet(P,Q)
is the minimum cost of a walk in
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
.

\frechet(P,Q)=\min_{F\in\mathbb{F}_{n,m}}\text{cost}(F)=\min_{F\in\mathbb{F}_{n,m}}\max_{(i,j)\in F}\dist(p_{i},q_{j}).

Decision variant.

We focus on the decision variant where the input includes some
\Delta\geq 0
and the goal is to output whether
\frechet(P,Q)\leq\Delta
.
The
\Delta
-free space matrix

M_{\Delta}
is the
n
by
m
matrix where
M_{\Delta}[i,j]
is
0
if
\dist(p_{i},q_{j})\leq\Delta
and
1
otherwise.
We follow geometric convention, where
i
denotes the column of
M_{\Delta}
(column
i
corresponds to some
p_{i}\in P
. Column entries with value
0
correspond to
q_{j}\in Q
with
\dist(p_{i},q_{j})\leq\Delta
).
Eiter and Mannila
[
21
]
define a directed graph over
M_{\Delta}
where there is an edge from
M_{\Delta}[a,b]
to
M_{\Delta}[c,d]
if and only if
c\in\{a,a+1\}
,
d\in\{b,b+1\}
and
M_{\Delta}[a,b]=M_{\Delta}[c,d]=0
.
They prove that
\frechet(P,Q)\leq\Delta
if and only if there exists a directed path from
(1,1)
to
(n,m)
in this graph.

Orthogonal vectors.

Our conditional lower bound reduces from the Orthogonal Vector Hypothesis. The input consists of two ordered sets of
d
-dimensional Boolean vectors
U,W
of sizes
|U|=n
and
|W|=m
. We denote for
u\in U
by

its
i
’th component.
The

is to find out if some vector of

Definition 1

(Williams
[
35
]
)
.

The orthogonal vector hypothesis (OVH) states that for all
\delta>0
, there exists constants
\omega
and
1>\gamma>0
such that the OV problem for vectors of dimension
d=\omega\log n
and
m=n^{\gamma}
cannot be solved in
O((nm)^{1-\delta})

We consider each vector in
U
(or
W

d

and

the concatenation of all strings in

and

A

of some string

for some
1\leq a\leq b\leq k

Regular tilings.

A
regular tiling

T
is an infinite plane graph where each face is a regular polygon and each face has the same degree.
There exist exactly three regular tilings
[

A face

of
T
is an open region bounded by the edges of
T

\overline{F}

The

of

A path in

is a path in its corresponding graph.
We always denote by

T

For any pair of vertices

of

Our algorithm considers what we will call a

of

We show a subquadratic algorithm to decide whether

.
For integers
i,i^{\prime}\in[n]

.

(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves.

Finally, we introduce a new class of well-behaved curves in the plane. Intuitively, an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve, for small
\delta
, is a curve composed of short edges that does not revisit the same region of the plane too frequently.
Formally, fix a universal constant
\gamma\in O(1)
. Let
P
be a curve in the plane, and let
B_{\varepsilon}
denote the set of all balls (in Euclidean metric) of radius
\varepsilon
centred at the vertices of
P
. We say that
P
is an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve if all its edges have length at most
\gamma
, and any point in the plane lies in at most
\delta
balls from
B_{\varepsilon}
.

Note that for any given pair
(P,\varepsilon)
, the value of
\delta
is determined. As
\varepsilon
decreases, so may
\delta
. If
\delta
remains large for small values of
\varepsilon
, then many vertices of
P
are densely clustered— i.e., effectively, the curve frequently revisits the same locations.
We present an exact algorithm for the Discrete Fréchet distance under the
L_{1}
metric with a linear dependency on
\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\varepsilon}
. We assume that the input specifies a desirable value of
\varepsilon
, and that this yields a favourable corresponding
\delta
.
Imposing that
\delta
is constant amounts to requiring that the curve does not revisit any
\varepsilon
-ball more than a constant number of times –
a restriction that somewhat resembles the well-studied
c
-packed curves
[
18
,
20
,
8
]
.
In Appendix
D
we discuss in detail how our new class relates to the prior well-behaved curve classes.

A lower bound for paths in an unweighted planar graph

Let
G
be an unweighted planar graph and
P
and
Q
be paths in
G
.
We show a lower bound, conditioned on OVH, that excludes any strongly subquadratic algorithm to compute a
1.25
-approximation of
\frechet(P,Q)
.
Since we are space-restricted, we present our argument only in Appendix
A
. Here, we assume that the reader is familiar with how these lower bounds are typically constructed
[
7
,
13
,
20
,
9
]
and only mention how our construction differs from prior works.
We start with some OV instance
(U,W)
.
We build an unweighted planar graph
G
and two paths
P
and
Q
in
G
such that
\frechet(P,Q)\leq 4
if and only if the OV instance is a yes-instance and
\frechet(P,Q)\geq 5
otherwise.
One novelty of our approach is a preprocessing step, where we transform
(U,W)
into an equivalent instance with additional properties:

Lemma 1
.

An instance
U^{\prime},W^{\prime}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d^{\prime}}
of OV can be preprocessed in
O(d^{\prime}(n+m))
time, resulting in a new instance
U,W\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
with
d\in O(d^{\prime})
such that:

•

a yes-instance stays a yes-instance and a no-instance stays a no-instance,

•

for all
u\in U
,
u_{1}=u_{d}=0
and for all
w\in W
,
w_{1}=w_{d}=1
,

•

if the instance is a no-instance, then
\forall u\in U
the vector
u
is not only non-orthogonal to every
w\in W
, but even non-orthogonal to all consecutive length-
d
substrings of
\str(W)
.

This preprocessing significantly simplifies our proofs and may be of independent interest.
Previous lower bound constructions
[
7
,
20
,
13
,
9
]
fix some
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
. Given an OV-instance
(U,W)
, they construct curves
(P,Q)
by constructing for each
u\in U
a subcurve
f(u)
and each
w\in W
a subcurve
g(w)
(see Figure
3
).
The construction has a very strong property:
Consider a traversal of
P
and
Q
where the person and the dog remain within distance
\Delta
of one another. If the person is in
f(u)
for some
u\in U
and the dog is in
g(w)
for some
w\in W
then neither the person or the dog can stand still if the other moves.

Requiring the planar graph to be unweighted significantly restricts the freedom we have in engineering the pairwise distance between vertices. We are unable to recreate an equally strong property and instead obtain a much weaker statement: If the person is traversing the subpath
f(u)
for some
u\in U
and the dog is traversing
g(w)
for some
w\in W
and the person moves at least two steps then the dog has to move at least one. We rely upon our preprocessing to then still argue that there exists a traversal of
P
and
Q
such that the leash length is at most
4
if and only if there is an orthogonal pair
(u,w)\in U\times W
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
P
and
Q
be paths in an unweighted planar graph. Let
|P|=n
and
|Q|=m=n^{\gamma}
for some constant
\gamma>0
. Then for all
\delta\in(0,1)
, one cannot approximate
\frechet(P,Q)
by a factor better than
1.25
in
O((nm)^{1-\delta})
time unless OVH fails.

4
Upper bound

We show that subquadratic algorithms
do
exist for unweighted planar graphs that exhibit additional structure. Specifically, our main result is as follows:

Theorem 6
.

Let
T
be a regular unit tiling and
(P,Q)
be paths in
T
with
|P|=n
and
|Q|

=m
.
Given
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
, we can output whether
\f(P,Q)\leq\Delta
in
O((n+m)^{1.5}\log(n+m))
time.

We compute
\f(P,Q)
as follows.
Given
(P,Q)
, we compute the smallest axis-aligned rectangles
R_{P}
and
R_{Q}
that contain
P
and
Q
, respectively, in
O(n+m)
time. Let
d_{0}
be the Euclidean length of the shortest segment
(p,q)
with
p\in R_{P}
and
q\in R_{Q}
, and let
d_{1}
be the Euclidean length of the longest such segment.
Observe that
\f(P,Q)\in[\frac{d_{0}}{2},2d_{1}]
,
that this range contains
O(n+m)
integers, and that
\f(P,Q)
is always integral.
By performing a binary search over
[\frac{d_{0}}{2},2d_{1}]\cap\mathbb{Z}
, applying Theorem
6
at each step, we obtain:

Corollary 7
.

Let
T
be a regular unit tiling, and let

Algorithm overview.

Given
T
and paths
P
and
Q
, we first compute a suitable super-tiling:

Lemma 11
.

Let
T
be a regular unit tiling, and let
P
and
Q
be two paths in
T
of lengths
n
and
m
.
In
O((n+m)^{1.5})
time, we can compute a super-tiling
\mathbb{T}
such that:

•

the edges of
\mathbb{T}
have a length in
\Theta(\sqrt{n+m})
, and

•

the total number of induced subpaths in
S(P,\mathbb{T})\cup S(Q,\mathbb{T})
(see Definition
4
) is
O(\sqrt{n+m})
.

Proof.

Our proof is inspired by that of Chan and Rahmati
[
15
, Lemma 1]
, who prove an analogous statement for grids and curves in higher-dimensional real space (without graphs).
For the proof, we assume without loss of generality that
T
is axis-aligned.

Let
f=\left\lceil\sqrt{n+m}\ \right\rceil
, and let
\mathbb{T}_{0}
be any axis-aligned super-tiling of
T
with an edge length in
\Theta(f)
.
For each
i\in[f-1]
, define
\mathbb{T}_{i}
to be the super-tiling obtained by shifting
\mathbb{T}_{0}
by the vector
(i,i)
if
T
is square, or by
(0,i\sqrt{3})
if
T
is triangular or hexagonal. These shifted super-tilings remain axis-aligned super-tilings of
T
, as all their vertices align with those of
T
.

Since we shift in a direction not aligned with any edge of
T
, and since
i\leq f-1
, each edge or vertex of
T
lies on the boundary of
O(1)
faces across the family
\{\mathbb{T}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{f-1}
. It follows that for any discrete vertex set
V\subseteq V(T)
with
|V|=k
, there exists some
\mathbb{T}_{i}
such that only
O(k/f)
vertices lie on boundaries of faces in
\mathbb{T}_{i}
.
Therefore, there exists a super-tiling
\mathbb{T}\in\{\mathbb{T}_{0},\dots,\mathbb{T}_{f-1}\}
such that the total number of boundary vertices from
P
and
Q
is
O((n+m)/f)
, which implies
O((n+m)/f)=O(\sqrt{n+m})
induced subpaths.
To compute
\mathbb{T}
, we iterate over all vertices
v
of
P
and
Q
and, for each, determine the
O(1)
super-tilings
\mathbb{T}_{i}
with
v\in B(\mathbb{T}_{i})
by iterating over all
\mathbb{T}\in\{\mathbb{T}_{0},\dots,\mathbb{T}_{f-1}\}
. This takes
O((n+m)f)
total time. We then select the super-tiling
\mathbb{T}_{i}
that minimizes

.
∎

Let
\mathbb{T}
be the computed super-tiling of
T
.
We partition
P
and
Q
into induced subpaths, denoted by
S(P,\mathbb{T})
and
S(Q,\mathbb{T})
.
These
O(\sqrt{n+m})
subpaths naturally induce a subdivision of the free-space diagram
M_{\Delta}
into
O(n+m)
rectangular submatrices
M_{\Delta}[i\colon i^{\prime},j:j^{\prime}]
, where
P[i:i^{\prime}]\in S(P,\mathbb{T})
and
Q[j:j^{\prime}]\in S(Q,\mathbb{T})
(see Figure

(a)).

Our algorithm makes use of a wave-front method. We define a wave-front
W
as a monotone walk through the grid
[1,n]\times[1,m]
that separates
(1,1)
from
(n,m)
. At each grid point
(i,j)\in W
, we store a Boolean value indicating whether there exists a monotone walk
F
from
(1,1)
to
(i,j)
such that all points on
F
lie in free space: that is,
M_{\Delta}[x,y]=0
for all
(x,y)\in F
(Figure
8
(b)).

An
update
to the wave-front

selects a pair of subpaths
(P[i:i^{\prime}],Q[j:j^{\prime}])\in S(P,\mathbb{T})\times S(Q,\mathbb{T})
such that the bottom and left facets of the submatrix
M_{\Delta}[i:i^{\prime},j:j^{\prime}]
lie on the current wave-front. The update removes these facets from

and inserts the top and right facets instead.
To perform the update, we consider the faces
F_{P}
and
F_{Q}
of
\mathbb{T}
that
P[i:i^{\prime}]
, respectively
Q[j:j^{\prime}]
, are assigned to, and proceed via a case distinction:

1.

If
F_{P}
and
F_{Q}
are not aligned, then they are well-separated by a vertex

(by Lemma
10
), and we show how to perform a fast update using
v
.

2.

If
F_{P}
and
F_{Q}
are aligned, we perform a brute-force update and use a counting argument to bound the total running time of these updates.

We analyse both cases separately, observing that while some instances may involve only Case 1 or only Case 2 submatrices, we can independently bound the total cost for each case.

Case 1: Updating with
(P[i:i^{\prime}],Q[j:j^{\prime}])
where
F_{P}
and
F_{Q}
are not aligned.

We show an update algorithm that is near-linear in the perimeter of the submatrix

Lemma 12
.

Let
P[i:i^{\prime}]\in S(P,\mathbb{T})

and

Proof.

By Lemma

are well-separated by some vertex

Given

whose

We define these curves as follows.

For a point

Likewise, for a point

Because

The

be this matrix. We compute the top and right facets of

∎

A cell

(
[

Let

and

5
The discrete Fréchet distance under the
L_{1}
metric

We extend our techniques for paths in tilings to curves in the plane.
Fix some universal constant
\gamma>0
and fix some value
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
We consider
\mathbb{R}^{2}

under the
L_{1}
metric, and two polygonal curves
P
and
Q
with
n
and
m
vertices, respectively.
In full generality, we only require that all edges in
P
have length at most
\gamma
and that
Q
is an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve.
Denote their discrete Fréchet distance under the
L_{1}
metric by
D_{F}(P,Q)
. We show that we can compute
D_{F}(P,Q)
in
\tilde{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\varepsilon}(n+m)^{1.5}\right)
time.
We adapt this algorithm in Appendix
B
to make it work for any
L_{c}
metric, by allowing some inaccuracy.

Our approach.

Given some
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
, we first show how to decide whether
D_{F}(P,Q)\leq\Delta
using the wave-front propagation technique from Section
4
, with suitable modifications for curves in the plane.
Let
t>1
be a parameter to be determined later. We define an axis-aligned square tiling
\mathbb{T}
of the plane where each square has edge length
t
.

Definition 16
.

We define the
breakpoints

B(P,\mathbb{T})
of
P
as the set of vertices
p\in V(P)
such that some edge of
P
incident to
p
intersects an edge of
\mathbb{T}
.

Note that
B(P,\mathbb{T})
may contain overlapping but distinct vertices of
P
(since an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve may visit the same point up to
\delta
times).
If we remove all edges from
P
which intersect some edge of
T
, we see that the breakpoints induce a partition of the vertices of
P
into pairwise vertex-disjoint subcurves
P[i:i^{\prime}]
,
each fully contained in the closure
\overline{F}
of a face
F\in\mathbb{T}
.
We refer to these subcurves as the
induced subcurves

S(P,\mathbb{T})
and we assign each induced subcurve to a face in
\mathbb{T}
.
We prove that for a suitable choice of
\mathbb{T}
, the total number of induced subcurves satisfies
|S(P,\mathbb{T})\cup S(Q,\mathbb{T})|=O((n+m)/t)

Choosing a tiling.

We construct a tiling

Appendix A
A lower bound in unweighted planar graphs

In this section we describe the lower bound construction based on the orthogonal vector hypothesis (OVH). The goal is to construct from some given OV instance in linear time a graph
G
and two paths

\frechet(P,Q)\leq 4

\frechet(P,Q)\geq 5

We start by preprocessing the instance, which significantly helps us at reducing the technical complexity of the proof.
For this, if

A

Proof.

For better readability, in this proof we use the notation of binary strings and binary vectors interchangeably, i.e.

We construct a traversal.
First, the one sitting on a 0 stays put, while the other one makes a step.
This is legal, since
\dist(0,x^{\prime})=\dist(x,0^{\prime})=4
.
Now both make a step. This is legal, since
\dist(x,I^{\prime})=\dist(I,x^{\prime})=4
.
The one lagging behind makes a step, so that now both are on
I
and
I^{\prime}
.
Since the vectors are locally orthogonal at the next box, at least one of the person or the dog has a 0 in the next box.
The being with a 0 in the next box makes a step, while the other stays put. This is again legal because
\dist(x,I^{\prime})=\dist(I,x^{\prime})=4
.
Both simultaneously make a step. Since the being with the 0 in the next box went first, they are now on a 0, and we have
\dist(0,x^{\prime})=\dist(x,0^{\prime})=4
. Finally, the being lagging behind makes the last step, so now both beings are inside the next box (which is legal since
\dist(0,0^{\prime}),\dist(0,1^{\prime}),\dist(1,0^{\prime})\leq 4
).
∎

The sufficient condition of Lemma
2
is accompanied by the following necessary condition. Given some orange box
b
, let us say the person is
adjacent to
b
,
if he is positioned either inside
b
, or at a vertex
x
immediately before or after
b
(but not at
A
or
y_{1}
, if

happens to be the first/last box).
Given some blue box
b^{\prime}
, let us say the dog is
adjacent to
b^{\prime}
, if he is inside
b^{\prime}
, or at a vertex
x^{\prime}
immediately before or after
b^{\prime}
.
The following lemma essentially states that if at some point of time the person and dog are both inside a vector gadget,
they are forced to traverse both vector gadgets simultaneously at
“the same speed”
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
b\in\mathcal{B},b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be boxes such that both
\nnext(b),\nnext(b^{\prime})
are defined.
If currently the person is adjacent to
b
and the dog is adjacent to
b^{\prime}
,
and they traverse their paths while maintaining a distance of at most 4,
then after finitely many steps we have that simultaneously the person is adjacent to
\nnext(b)
and the dog is adjacent to
\nnext(b^{\prime})
.
Furthermore, if
\nnext(b),\nnext(b^{\prime})
are not locally orthogonal, then it is impossible for the person and dog to continue on their paths.

Proof.

Since
\dist(x,x^{\prime})=5
, it is not the case that both the person and the dog are currently on
x
or
x^{\prime}
.
Hence at least one of the man and the dog is currently inside a box.
We
claim
that we can assume w.l.o.g. that one of the two is on the vertex 0 in their box
b
or
b^{\prime}

while the other one is on the vertex
x^{\prime}
immediately after box
b
or
b^{\prime}
.
Indeed, in the first case, where both of them are in the box, they cannot move simultaneously, since
\dist(x,x^{\prime})=5
. So only one of them can move, while the other stays in the box.
Since
\dist(1,x^{\prime})=\dist(1^{\prime},x)=5
, the one staying in the box must be on a 0.
In the second case (where one of them is inside the box, and the other one is lagging behind on the
x
before a box) the one inside the box cannot move.
So, they either have to move simultaneously or only the one lagging behind moves on their own.
In both cases we arrive at a situation we analysed before. This proves the claim.

We can hence assume one of them is in box
b
or
b^{\prime}
and the other on is on
x
or
x^{\prime}
after the box
b
or
b^{\prime}
.
Now, again, the one lagging behind cannot move on their own. The one standing on
x
or
x^{\prime}
can also not move on their own, since
\dist(0,I^{\prime})=\dist(0^{\prime},I)=5
and
\dist(1,I^{\prime})=\dist(1^{\prime},I)=5
.
So both have to move simultaneously.
Hence the person is on
x
while the dog is on
I^{\prime}
, or the other way around.
The being in front cannot move on their own, so the one lagging behind has to move, or both have to move simultaneously.
For a reasoning similar to above, after at most two rounds, exactly one of the two is on
I
or
I^{\prime}
, while the other is at
x
or
x^{\prime}
before the box
\nnext(b)
or
(b^{\prime})
.
Now, the only way to make progress is if both move at the same time. Note however that after this step one is in the box
\nnext(b)
or
\nnext(b^{\prime})
while the other one is on the vertex
x
or
x^{\prime}
just before
\nnext(b)
or
\nnext(b^{\prime})
.
This proves the first part of the lemma.
For the second part, observe that in this final situation, the one inside the box
(b)
or
(b^{\prime})
must be on a 0, since
\dist(1,x^{\prime})=\dist(1^{\prime},x)=5
. Hence the boxes
\nnext(b),\nnext(b^{\prime})
are locally orthogonal by definition.
∎

Observe the following properties:
As claimed before, the distance table between vertices in orange and blue gadgets is the same in the larger graph

Lemma 4
.

If

is a yes-instance of OV, then

.

Proof.

If

is orthogonal to

, the person and the dog can employ the following 6-phase strategy:

1.

The dog stays at
\alpha
, while the person goes to the orange gadget

corresponding to

before gadget

2.

Then the dog walks over

to the blue gadget

3.

Lemma 5
.

If
\frechet(P,Q)\leq 4
, then
(U,V)
is a yes-instance of OV.

Proof.

Consider a traversal of
P
and
Q
where at all times, the person and the dog are within distance
4
of one another.
At some point of time, the dog is on
\alpha^{\star}
. This implies the person is on a vertex
A
right before some orange gadget
G_{u}
corresponding to some
u\in U
.
Now the person cannot move on his own, so the dog has to move.
The dog may walk through the blue gadgets for some time on his own, but at some point of time

the person has also to move away from

into
G_{u}
.
More precisely, let
t_{0}
be the smallest time such that at this time the position of the person is the vertex 0 in box
b_{1}(u)
.
Since

is at distance greater than
4
from

and
1
, at time
t_{0}
the dog is not on
z^{\prime}
. Furthermore, due to the properties of
\beta^{\star}
, at time
t_{0}
the dog has also not gone further than
z^{\prime}
.
This means the dog is standing inside some blue vector gadget at time
t_{0}
.
Since
\dist(I^{\prime},0)=5
, the dog is not standing on
I^{\prime}
, and hence is adjacent to some box.
But now due to Lemma
3
the person and the dog have to move
with the same speed
at least until the person has fully traversed all
d
boxes of
G_{u}
.
We note that we need to watch out for the edge case where the person has not fully traversed
G_{u}
yet, but the dog is in the very last box

. In this edge case, Lemma
3
is not applicable because
\nnext(b^{\prime}_{d}(w^{(m)}))
is not defined. But this is no problem. In this case the dog cannot move, since the only vertices within distance 4 of

are

but the person is currently inside

.
But the person cannot get to the next box as well, since the person is standing in a box

with

, and

. So in this case they are stuck, a contradiction to

.
In all other cases that are not the edge case, i.e. the case where the person stands in a box

and the dog stands in a box

, both
\nnext(b_{i}(u))
and

are defined, and so

3
is applicable.
In summary, this implies that

is orthogonal to a consecutive substring of length

of the string
\str(V)
. Due to the preprocessing (Lemma
1
), this actually means that
(U,V)
is a yes-instance.
∎

Appendix B
Approximating the discrete Fréchet distance under any
L_{c}
metric

We extend our techniques for curves in the plane under the
L_{1}
metric to curves in the plane under any
L_{c}
metric, for
c\geq 1
.
Let
\gamma>0
be a universal constant, and fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
We consider
\mathbb{R}^{2}
under the
L_{c}
metric, and two polygonal curves
P
and
Q
with
n
and
m
vertices, respectively.
In full generality, we only require that all edges in
P
have length at most
\gamma
and that
Q
is an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve.
Denote their discrete Fréchet distance under the
L_{c}
metric by
D_{F}(P,Q)
. We show how to compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation of
D_{F}(P,Q)
in
\tilde{O}(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\varepsilon^{1.5}}(n+m)^{1.5})
time.
In particular, we design a
large-scale

(1+\varepsilon)
-
decision algorithm
:

Definition 22
.

For any value
\Delta
and
\varepsilon>0
, a large-scale
(1+\varepsilon)
-decision algorithm outputs
Yes
or
No
under the following conditions:

•

If
D_{F}(P,Q)\leq\Delta
, it must output
Yes
.

•

If
D_{F}(P,Q)>(1+\varepsilon)\Delta
, it must output
No
.

•

Otherwise, its output may be arbitrary.

•

In the special case, when
\Delta\leq 1
it must give the exact answer.

We use the result of
[
18
]
to compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation of
D_{F}(P,Q)
, given a large-scale
(1+\varepsilon)
-decision algorithm:

Lemma 23

(Lemma 3.7 in
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose that there exists a large-scale
(1+\varepsilon)
-decision algorithm that, for any
\Delta
, runs in
T
time.
If
D_{F}(P,Q)
lies in a given interval of width
N
, then this algorithm can compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation of
D_{F}(P,Q)
in
O(T\log\frac{N}{\varepsilon})
time.

Algorithm overview.

Our approach mirrors that of Section
5
.
Given
P
and
Q
, we use Lemma
17
to compute a tiling
\mathbb{T}
where edges have width
t\in\Theta(\frac{\varepsilon^{1.5}\cdot(n+m)}{\sqrt{\delta n}})
.
This partitions
P
and
Q
into at most
O((n+m)/t)
induced subcurves
S(P,\mathbb{T})
and
S(Q,\mathbb{T})
.
The product set
S(P,\mathbb{T})\times S(Q,\mathbb{T})
induces a grid over
M_{\Delta}
with
O((n+m)/t)
rows and columns.

In Section
5
, we distinguished between pairs of faces of
\mathbb{T}
that are aligned and not aligned.
There, we used that any pair of subcurves
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\in S(P,\mathbb{T})\times S(Q,\mathbb{T})
in unaligned faces is well-separated, giving rise to an efficient way of updating the wave-front over their corresponding submatrices.
In this section, we take another approach.
Here, we distinguish between pairs of faces of
\mathbb{T}
based on the distance between them.

Definition 24
.

We say that two faces
F,F^{\prime}
in a tiling
\mathbb{T}
with edge length
t
are
far apart
whenever each point in
F
is at least distance
2^{1+1/c}\cdot\frac{t}{\varepsilon}
(under the
L_{c}
norm) away from any point in
F^{\prime}
.
The faces are
close together
otherwise.

This different distinction becomes useful only now, since we allow approximations.
Critically, for two subcurves
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\in S(P,\mathbb{T})\times S(Q,\mathbb{T})
in faces that are far apart, we may approximate pairwise distances by simply the distance between the two faces, thus treating the pairwise distances as constant.
In the remainder, we let
d(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the distance function under the
L_{c}
metric.

Lemma 25
.

Let
F
and
F^{\prime}
be two faces of
\mathbb{T}
that are far apart.
Let
D
denote the minimum distance (under the
L_{c}
metric) between a point in
F
and a point in
F^{\prime}
.
For any two points
p\in F
and
q\in F^{\prime}
, we have
D\leq d(p,q)\leq(1+\varepsilon)D
.

Proof.

Naturally,
D\leq d(p,q)
.
Given that
F
(resp.
F^{\prime}
) is a square with sides of length
t
, the distance between any two points in
F
(resp.
F^{\prime}
) is at most
2^{1/c}\cdot t
.
Thus
d(p,q)\leq D+2^{1+1/c}\cdot t
.
We have that
D\geq 2^{1+1/c}\cdot\frac{t}{\varepsilon}
, since
F
and
F^{\prime}
are far apart.
Hence

D\leq d(p,q)\leq D+2^{1+1/c}\cdot t\leq(1+\varepsilon)D.\qed

The wave-front algorithm.

We adapt the wave-front propagation technique from Section
4
.
We define the
wave-front

W
as a monotone chain in
M_{\Delta}
that separates
(1,1)
from
(n,m)
.
For each grid point
(x,y)
in
W
, we store the Boolean value:

•

True
if there exists a path
F
from
(1,1)
to
(x,y)
where
\forall(i,j)\in F
,
d(p,q)\leq\Delta
.

•

False
if there exists no path
F
from
(1,1)
to
(x,y)
where
\forall(i,j)\in F
,
d(p,q)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\Delta
.

•

Or an arbitrary Boolean value otherwise.

A wave-front update selects any
(P[i:i^{\prime}],Q[j:j^{\prime}])\in S(P,\mathbb{T})\times S(Q,\mathbb{T})
where the bottom facet of
M_{\Delta}[i:i^{\prime},j:j^{\prime}]
is incident to
W
and it adds its top facet to
W
.
To perform a wave-front update, we distinguish between whether
P[i:i^{\prime}]
and
Q[j:j^{\prime}]
lie in close together or far apart faces.

Let
F_{P}
and
F_{Q}
be faces of
\mathbb{T}
containing
P[i:i^{\prime}]
and
Q[j:j^{\prime}]
, respectively.
We first discuss updating the wave-front when
F_{P}
and
F_{Q}
are far apart.
In this case, we compute the minimum distance
D
between points in the two faces in constant time.
If

, then by Lemma
25
we have that
d(p,q)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\Delta
for all

and

.
We then treat the entire submatrix

as containing only
0

-time procedure.
If

, all entries in

have the value

, as

is the minimum distance between points in
F_{P}
and

.
This again makes updating the wave-front a trivial

Next we discuss updating the wave-front when

and

are close together.
In this case, we upper bound the total number of switching cells

where

.

There are at most

.

Fix a universal constant

.

\gamma

rows and columns.
Thus:

\sum_{\textnormal{submatrices }M^{\prime}\textnormal{ of }M_{\Delta}}\hskip-28.45274pt|\textnormal{facets}(M^{\prime})|=\sum_{(P[i:i^{\prime}],Q[j:j^{\prime}])\in S(P,\mathbb{T})\times S(Q,\mathbb{T})}\hskip-22.76228ptO(|P[i:i^{\prime}]|+|Q[j:j^{\prime}]|)\hskip 28.45274pt\in O((n+m)^{2}/t).

(3)

We iteratively perform wave-front updates with the input
(P[i:i^{\prime}],Q[j:j^{\prime}])
.
Let
F_{P}
and
F_{Q}
denote the corresponding two faces of
\mathbb{T}
.
If
F_{P}
and
F_{Q}
are far apart then we update the wave-front in
O(|P[i:i^{\prime}]|+|Q[j:j^{\prime}]|)
time, as we treat the submatrix corresponding to the subcurves as containing either only
0
-entries or only
1
-entries.
The total time spent in this case equals:

\sum_{(P[i:i^{\prime}],Q[j:j^{\prime}])\textnormal{ where }(F_{P},F_{Q})\textnormal{ are far apart }}\hskip-71.13188ptO(|P[i:i^{\prime}]|+|Q[j:j^{\prime}]|)\hskip 28.45274pt\in O((n+m)^{2}/t)

If
F_{P}
and
F_{Q}
are close together then we apply Lemma
26
instead.
The total time spent is:

\sum_{P[i:i^{\prime}],Q[j:j^{\prime}])\textnormal{ where }(F_{P},F_{Q})\textnormal{ are close together}}\hskip-71.13188ptO(|P[i:i^{\prime}]|+|Q[j:j^{\prime}]|)+\textnormal{switching cells}(M_{\Delta}[i:i^{\prime},j:j^{\prime}]))

The first term is upper bounded by

The second term is upper bounded by

It follows that if we choose

Appendix C
A continuous variant of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves.

For the sake of completeness, we briefly mention a natural continuous generalisation of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves. We observe that, trivially, our algorithms can also approximate the continuous Fréchet distance for these continuous
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves.

Definition 28
.

Fix a universal constant
\gamma\in O(1)
. Let
P
be a curve in the plane.
Denote by
B_{\varepsilon}
a ball of radius
\varepsilon
and by
E_{\varepsilon}
the set of regions obtained by taking for every edge
e
of
P
the Minkowsky-sum
B_{\varepsilon}\oplus e
.
We say that
P
is a continuous
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve if all edges have length at most
\gamma
and all points of the plane intersect at most
\delta
regions in
E_{\varepsilon}
.

We note that, trivially, if we require our curves to be continuous
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves then our approximation algorithms for the discrete Fréchet distance also apply to the continuous Fréchet distance, at a factor
\varepsilon^{-1.5}
overhead.

Specifically, let
P
be a curve whose edges have length at most
\gamma
, and such that
P
has
n
vertices.
Let
Q
be a continuous
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve with
m
vertices.
Let
D(P,Q)
denote their continuous Fréchet distance and let
P^{\prime}
(and
Q^{\prime}
) be the ordered sequence of points obtained by placing points at distance
\frac{\varepsilon}{8}
along
P
and
Q
.

Observation 29
.

The discrete Fréchet distance
D_{F}(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
is a
(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2})
-approximation of the continuous Fréchet distance
D(P,Q)
.

Observation 30
.

The sequence
P^{\prime}
has
O(\frac{n}{\varepsilon})
vertices and the sequence
Q^{\prime}
is an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve with
O(\frac{m}{\varepsilon})
vertices.

Thus, we may apply Theorem
27
to
P^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
to approximate the continuous Fréchet distance
D(P,Q)
:

Theorem 31
.

Fix a universal constant
\gamma\in O(1)
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, let
P
has edge length at most
\gamma
and
Q
be a continuous
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve.
If we denote by
D(P,Q)
the continuous Fréchet distance, under the
L_{c}
metric for any
c\geq 1
, then we can compute a value
F
with
D(P,Q)\in[F-\varepsilon,F+\varepsilon]
in
\tilde{O}(\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\varepsilon^{3}}(n+m)^{1.5})

This theorem offers a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation when

is large enough. Since this section is only meant to illustrate the continuous generalization of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves for the sake completeness, we consider the special case where

is small to be out of scope.

Appendix D
Comparing Well-behaved curve classes

We discuss how the class of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves relates to existing classes of “realistic” curves.
We focus on the following established curve classes:
c
-packed
[
18
,
8
]
,
\phi
-low-density
[
18
]
,
\kappa
-straight
[
18
]
, and
\kappa
-bounded curves
[
3
,
18
]
.
These classes admit faster approximation algorithms for both the discrete and continuous Fréchet distance.

For completeness, we presented a continuous analogue of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves in Appendix
C
.
However, we consider the discrete variant to be more natural, and thus we restrict our comparisons to the discrete setting.

A key distinction between
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves and the existing realistic curve classes is that
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves are defined in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, whereas
c
-packed and
\kappa
-straight curves admit efficient
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithms in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for constant
d
.
For
\phi
-low-density and
\kappa
-bounded curves, approximation algorithms exist but with running time that scales exponentially in
d
.

Another key distinction is that, under the
L_{1}
metric,
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves allow for the exact computation of the discrete Fréchet distance in subquadratic time.

\phi
-low-density curves.

We first consider the class of
\phi
-low-density curves
[
18
,
11
]
.
A curve
P
is said to be
\phi
-low-density if any ball of radius
r
intersects at most
\phi
edges of
P
that are longer than
r
.
This input model resembles our definition of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves: any ball of radius
\varepsilon
intersects at most
\delta
vertices of an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve.
In particular, setting
\phi=\delta
allows both definitions to permit a vertex to coincide with up to
\phi=\delta
other vertices.

There are, however, notable differences. In some respects,
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves are more restrictive:

•

(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves impose a global upper bound on edge lengths, independent of the input complexity.

•

\phi
-low-density curves allow for arbitrarily tight clusters of short edges.

Conversely,
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves are less restrictive in other ways:

•

They only enforce local constraints within balls of radius
\varepsilon
, making the condition inherently more local.

•

The algorithmic dependency on
\phi
in
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithms is poor. The existing running times are only stated for constant
\phi

[
18
]
.

c
-packed curves.

The class of
c
-packed curves is perhaps the most extensively studied
[
18
,
25
,
20
,
8
,
33
]
.
A curve is
c
-packed if its length inside any ball is at most
c
times the ball’s radius (note that overlapping subcurves are counted with multiplicity).
Any
c
-packed curve is also
2c
-low-density
[
18
]
, so the same comparison with
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves applies.

However,
c
-packed curves are even more restrictive. For example, consider a tightly wound spiral of short segments. Such a curve can be
O(1)
-low-density and satisfy the definition of an
(\varepsilon,1)
-curve for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, yet it is
\Theta(n)
-packed.

\kappa
-straight curves.

A curve
P
is
\kappa
-straight if the arc length between any two points on
P
is at most
\kappa
times their Euclidean distance.
Any
\kappa
-straight curve is also
\kappa
-packed.
This class is strictly more restrictive than
c
-packed curves and, notably, excludes self-intersecting curves.
This stands in stark contrast to
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curves, which allow for such intersections.

\kappa
-bounded curves.

Finally, we consider
\kappa
-bounded curves.
A curve
P
is
\kappa
-bounded if, for any two points
P(x)
and
P(x^{\prime})
on the curve, the subcurve between them lies within the union of two balls of radius
\kappa\cdot d(P(x),P(x^{\prime}))
, each centred at
P(x)
and
P(x^{\prime})
respectively.

Although
\kappa
-bounded curves may intersect a given ball arbitrarily many times, they have a significant limitation: they do not permit self-intersections.
This again contrasts sharply with the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-curve model, which permits such behaviour.
