Matrix Concentration for Random Signed Graphs and Community Recovery in the Signed Stochastic Block Model
Sawyer Jack Robertson

Abstract
We consider graphs where edges and their signs are added independently at random from among all pairs of nodes. We establish strong concentration inequalities for adjacency and Laplacian matrices obtained from this family of random graph models. Then, we apply our results to study graphs sampled from the signed stochastic block model. Namely, we take a two-community setting where edges within the communities have positive signs and edges between the communities have negative signs and apply a random sign perturbation with probability 0 < s < 1 / 2 0<s<1/2 . In this setting, our findings include: first, the spectral gap of the corresponding signed Laplacian matrix concentrates near 2 ​ s 2s with high probability; and second, the sign of the first eigenvector of the Laplacian matrix defines a weakly consistent estimator for the balanced community detection problem, or equivalently, the ± 1 \pm 1 synchronization problem. We supplement our theoretical contributions with experimental data obtained from the models under consideration. Introduction

The study of signed graphs, i.e., graphs whose edges carry positive or negative labels, dates back at least sixty-five years to the work of Cartwright and Harary
[
4
]
. In many real-world networks, relationships are not merely present or absent, but can be cooperative or antagonistic. For example, in social networks, edges may represent friendships or enmities; in biological contexts, they may encode synergistic or inhibitive interactions. Therefore it can be said that signed graphs provide a more nuanced modeling tool compared to traditional graphs, allowing researchers to study phenomena such as community structure, conflict, and alignment under one unifying framework. As such, random signed graphs can be used to model and understand various techniques from the world of data science that draw on sign-based structures.

In this paper we consider a general class of random signed graphs constructed as follows. Let
n\geq 2
be a fixed integer and let
[n]=\{1,2,\dotsc,n\}
be a set of nodes. For each pair
e=\{i,j\}\in{[n]\choose 2}
, add
e
to the edge set of the graph with probability
p_{ij}
, and add a negative sign to
e
with probability
s_{ij}
(otherwise, the edge has a positive sign). Here,
0\leq p_{ij},s_{ij}\leq 1
are given probabilities. We refer to graphs sampled in this manner as
inhomogeneous Erdős-Rényi signed graphs
(see
Section

2.2
). We then incorporate the edge and sign information into corresponding adjacency and Laplacian matrices, which we denote
A
and
\mathcal{L}
, respectively. The first half of the paper tackles the following general question: under what conditions on
p_{ij}
and
s_{ij}
can we guarantee that, with high probability,
A
and
\mathcal{L}
will be “close” to their corresponding means? Our main technical contributions consist of two matrix concentration inequalities which, loosely speaking, assert that as long as the probabilities
p_{ij}
are “not too sparse,” and the probabilities
s_{ij}
are bounded away from
1/2
, then we can guarantee concentration near the mean adjacency and Laplacian matrices in operator norm with high probability. We state below the version for adjacency matrices; the version for Laplacian matrices appears as
Theorem

3.6
.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal statement of
Theorem

3.1
)
.

Let

be a random signed adjacency matrix on
n

for some

. Assume that

\displaystyle n\max_{i,j\in[n]}|p_{ij}(1-2s_{ij})|\leq\alpha

for some
\alpha\geq c_{1}\log{n}
and
c_{1}>0
. Then for any
r>0
there exists
C=C(r,c_{0},c_{1})>0
such that for
n
sufficiently large and with probability at least
1-n^{-r}
, it holds

\displaystyle\left\|{A-\overline{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\sqrt{\alpha}.

Note that this result can be considered a generalization of the strong matrix concentration inequality of Lei and Rinaldo in
[
24
]
. As a result of our technical contributions, in the second half of the paper, we are able to facilitate a detailed study of the spectral properties of a two-way signed stochastic block model (SSBM). We describe this model diagrammatically in

The particular case of a stochastic block model defined with respect to an even bipartition, also called a planted community model, has emerged as a testing ground for community detection and node clustering algorithms. In this paper we consider a signed variant of the traditional stochastic block model by incorporating the presence of negative edges within and between communities with various probabilities. Such models have been studied for their applications to graph clustering and signature synchronization problems (see, e.g.,

2.1
Signed graphs, community detection, and synchronization

We consider graphs of the form
G=(V,E)
, where
V=[n]
and
E\subseteq{[n]\choose 2}
; that is,
G
is finite, undirected, and has no loops or multiple edges. We write
i\sim j
whenever
\{i,j\}\in E
and denote by
d_{i}
the
degree
of node
i
, which is the number of edges incident to
i
.

A
signature
is a map
\sigma:E\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
which assigns to each edge a sign which is either positive or negative. We say that
(G,\sigma)
is a
signed graph
. The
signed adjacency matrix
of a graph
G
is the matrix
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with entries

\displaystyle a_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma_{ij}&\text{ if }i\sim j\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},\hskip 14.22636pti,j\in V.

Let
D=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\dotsc,d_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the diagonal matrix of node degrees. The
signed Laplacian matrix
is defined by
L=D-A
. The
normalized signed Laplacian matrix
is given by
\mathcal{L}=D^{-1/2}LD^{-1/2}
where we abuse notation and set
D^{-1/2}_{ii}=0
whenever
d_{i}=0
. The entries of
\mathcal{L}=(l_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
can be expressed in the form

\displaystyle l_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }i=j\\
-\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}&\text{ if }i\sim j\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},\hskip 14.22636pti,j\in[n].

In this article we suppress the dependence of
A,L,\mathcal{L}
on
\sigma
since we do not encounter scenarios in which multiple signatures may play a role. We use
|A|
to refer to the (unsigned) adjacency of the underlying graph, and in general
|\cdot|
to refer to the
entrywise
absolute value of a given matrix. Note that
L
and
\mathcal{L}
are both positive semidefinite and possess a complete set of nonnegative eigenvalues with corresponding eigenvectors. We note that unlike in the case of traditional graph Laplacians, it need not hold that
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})=0
.

When
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is any symmetric matrix, we denote its eigenvalues, ordered in ascending fashion, as follows
\lambda_{1}(X)\leq\lambda_{2}(X)\leq\dotsc\leq\lambda_{n}(X)
. When
X
is any matrix, we denote by
\left\|{X}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
the
\ell_{2}
-operator or spectral norm of
X
and by
\left\|{X}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}
the Frobenius norm of
X
. We denote by
\left\|{x}\right\|_{2}
the
\ell_{2}
norm of a vector
x
.

If
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is any function, we can define its
\ell_{2}
-frustration
by the following expression

\displaystyle\eta_{2}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}|f(i)-\sigma_{ij}f(j)|^{2}}{\sum_{i\in V}|f(i)|^{2}d_{i}}

The frustration of a function can be understood as a measure of how well
f(i)
satisfies the signs of each edge. A signature
\sigma
is said to be
balanced
or
consistent
whenever there exists a function
f:V\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
such that
\sigma_{ij}=f(i)f(j)^{-1}
for each
\{i,j\}\in E
, i.e., such that
\eta_{2}(f)=0
. We define the
\ell_{2}
-frustration index of
\sigma
as the minimum

\displaystyle\eta_{2}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\min_{f:V\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}}\eta_{2}(f).

(1)

The frustration index has been studied in more general settings which include
\mathsf{U}(1)
and
\mathsf{O}(d)
labellings of the edges, which are often called magnetic and connection graphs, respectively (see, e.g.,
[
22
,
32
]
). Note that
\eta_{2}(\sigma)
is related to
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})
by a semidefinite relaxation of
Eq.

1
via the variational characterization of
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})
, namely,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{f^{T}\mathcal{L}f}{f^{T}f}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}|f(i)-\sigma_{ij}f(j)|^{2}}{\sum_{i\in V}|f(i)|^{2}d_{i}}.

In
[
3
]
, the authors show that
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})
and
\eta_{2}(\sigma)
are related by a Cheeger-type inequality of the form
\eta_{2}(\sigma)\leq\sqrt{8\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})}
. Spectral lower bounds can also obtained in the setting where
\eta_{2}(\sigma)
is slightly relaxed to cover
f:V\rightarrow\{\pm 1,0\}
. This bound is obtained through an algorithmic approach to solving the
\{\pm 1,0\}
relaxation by obtaining the first eigenvector
u_{1}
of
\mathcal{L}
and using it to construct a function
f^{\ast}
by modifying
\mathrm{sgn}(u_{1})
whose frustration is provably proximal to the frustration of
\sigma
. Here, if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is any vector,
\mathrm{sgn}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector given by
\mathrm{sgn}(x)_{i}=1
if
x_{i}\geq 0
and
\mathrm{sgn}(x)_{i}=-1
otherwise. We mention in
Remark

4.7
that in the two-community SSBM setting,
\mathrm{sgn}(u_{1})
defines a statistically consistent estimator of
\eta_{2}(\sigma)
in a weak sense.

Signature frustration and the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
are also related to the edge expansion properties of
G
and can thus also be used to study community detection problems on signed networks. As an example of this approach, define for
V_{1}\subseteq V
the
\ell_{1}
-frustration
according to

\displaystyle\eta_{1}(V_{1})=\min_{f:V_{1}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E(V_{1})}|f(i)-\sigma_{ij}f(j)|.

We note that, as was pointed out in
[
22
]
,
\eta_{1}(V)
is equal to the minimum number of edges which need be removed from
E
in order to ensure the resulting graph is balanced. One may then define the
\ell_{1}
-frustration index of
\sigma
according to

\displaystyle\eta_{1}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\min_{{\varnothing\neq V_{1}\subseteq V}}\frac{\eta_{1}(V_{1})+|E(V_{1},V_{1}^{c})|}{\sum_{i\in V_{1}}d_{i}}

The program
\eta_{1}(\sigma)
can be thought of as searching for a subset of
V
which has ideal edge expansion and balance properties; in the community detection literature such problems are often described as finding balanced communities
[
1
,
30
]
. Cheeger-type inequalities related to
\eta_{1}(\sigma)
are also studied extensively in
[
22
]
(with a notable distinction from our case being the authors’ usage of a non-normalized Laplacian matrix). The authors obtain, for example,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{1}(L)\leq\eta_{1}(\sigma)\leq\sqrt{8\Delta\lambda_{1}(L)},

where
\Delta
is the maximum degree of the graph
G
. It is within the context of these interpretations of
\lambda_{1}
that the concentration
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L})\approx 2s
(as described in
Theorem

1.3
) suggests an understanding of
\lambda_{1}
as a probabilistic quantity related to misappropriated edge signatures.

2.2
Random graphs and the SSBM

In this subsection we cover background on random graph models which will be of use to us in the sections to follow. We begin by letting
\mathbf{P}=(p_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric matrix of probabilities, i.e., such that
p_{ij}\in[0,1]
for all
i,j
and
p_{ij}=p_{ji}
. We assume
p_{ii}=0
for all
i
. We can construct a random graph on
[n]
by adding an edge
\{i,j\}
with probability
p_{ij}
, for all such
\{i,j\}\in{[n]\choose 2}
, with the requirement that all pairs are sampled in a mutually independent fashion. A graph which has been constructed according to this model is said to be sampled from the distribution
\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P})
. Note that if
p_{ij}=p
for some
p\in[0,1]
independent of
i,j
then
\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P})
is simply the well-known Erdős-Rényi random graph model. The model
\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P})
is sometimes called the
inhomogeneous Erdős-Rényi
model
[
29
]
. We recall the following concentration inequality of Lei and Rinaldo
[
24
]
for random adjacency matrices drawn from
\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P})
, off of which one of our technical contributions is based, and which will be used directly later on in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
(see
Section

A.2
).

Theorem 2.1

(Lei and Rinaldo,
[
24
,
23
]
)
.

Let
A\sim\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P})
be a random adjacency matrix on
n
nodes. Assume that
\max_{i,j}p_{ij}\leq\alpha/n
for some
\alpha\geq c_{0}\log{n}
and
c_{0}>0
. Then for any
r>0
there exists
C=C(r,c_{0})>0
such that for
n
sufficiently large and with probability at least
1-n^{-r}
, it holds

\displaystyle\left\|{A-\overline{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\sqrt{\alpha}.

In this article we consider an extension of the inhomogeneous Erdős-Rényi model to signed graphs. Let
\mathbf{S}=(s_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be another symmetric matrix of probabilities, again taking
s_{ii}=0
for all
i
. Now having fixed a pair
(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{S})
construct a random signed graph as follows. For each pair
\{i,j\}\in{[n]\choose 2}
, add an edge with probability
p_{ij}
and add a negative sign with probability
s_{ij}
. All pairs and all signs are sampled mutually independently. A signed graph constructed in this manner is to have been sampled from the distribution
\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{S})
, and we will refer to this as the
signed inhomogeneous Erdős-Rényi
model. If this is the case, we write
A\sim\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{S})
.

If
A\sim\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{S})
, then we may write
a_{ij}=P_{ij}(1-2S_{ij})
where
P_{ij}
is a Bernoulli random variable with probability
p_{ij}
and
S_{ij}
is a Bernoulli random variable with probability
s_{ij}
. We write
\overline{A}=(\overline{a}_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to denote the mean signed adjacency matrix for the model
\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{S})
(we suppress the dependence of
\overline{A}
on
\mathbf{P},\mathbf{S}
for brevity; the probability matrices will be clear in context). That is,

\displaystyle\overline{a}_{ij}=p_{ij}(1-2s_{ij}).

(2)

We denote by
\overline{d}_{i}
the expected degree of node
i
, and the matrix
\overline{D}
will denote the diagonal matrix of expected degrees. Concretely,
\overline{d}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{ij}
. The matrix
\overline{L}
is the mean signed Laplacian matrix, and can be expressed
\overline{L}=\overline{D}-\overline{A}
. The matrix
\overline{\mathcal{L}}=\overline{D}^{-1/2}\overline{L}\overline{D}^{-1/2}
is the degree-normalized mean Laplacian matrix.

We now introduce our main model of interest, the SSBM. We start with the general construction. We denote by
J_{s\times t}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
the matrix containing all ones. When
s=t
,
I_{s\times s}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
denotes the identity matrix, and
\widetilde{J}_{s\times s}={J}_{s\times s}-{I}_{s\times s}
is the matrix
J_{s\times s}
with zero diagonal.

Definition 2.2

(Signed stochastic block model)
.

Let
n\geq 2
be fixed, and let
Q_{1},Q_{2},\dotsc,Q_{m}
be a pairwise disjoint and nonempty
m
-partition of the integers
[n]
, where
Q_{i}\subseteq[n]
for each
i
and
m\geq 2
. Let the matrix
P=(p_{st})_{s,t=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
consist of the probabilities that an edge is added between each pair of clusters, i.e.,

\displaystyle p_{st}=\mathbb{P}\left({\text{an edge is added between }i\in Q_{s}\text{ and }j\in Q_{t}}\right)\in[0,1],\hskip 7.11317pt1\leq s,t\leq m.

Similarly, let the matrix
S=(s_{st})_{s,t=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
consist of the probabilities that a negative sign is added between each pair of clusters, i.e.,

\displaystyle s_{st}=\mathbb{P}\left({\text{an negative sign is added between }i\in Q_{s}\text{ and }j\in Q_{t}}\right)\in[0,1],\hskip 7.11317pt1\leq s,t\leq m.

Now extend these in the following way to
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, writing

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}p_{11}\widetilde{J}_{|Q_{1}|\times|Q_{1}|}&p_{12}J_{|Q_{1}|\times|Q_{2}|}&\dotsc&p_{1m}J_{|Q_{1}|\times|Q_{m}|}\\
p_{21}J_{|Q_{2}|\times|Q_{1}|}&p_{22}\widetilde{J}_{|Q_{1}|\times|Q_{1}|}&\dotsc&p_{2m}J_{|Q_{2}|\times|Q_{m}|}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
p_{m1}J_{|Q_{m}|\times|Q_{1}|}&p_{m2}J_{|Q_{m}|\times|Q_{2}|}&\dotsc&p_{mm}\widetilde{J}_{|Q_{m}|\times|Q_{m}|}\end{pmatrix}.

Define
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
in the same manner. Then the corresponding inhomogeneous signed Erdős-Rényi random graph model
\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{S})
is called
the signed stochastic block model (SSBM)
.

We consider, as follows, a specialization of this model to the case consisting of two communities and with signs determined by a single parameter
s
(see
Fig.

1
).

Definition 2.3

(Signed stochastic bisection model)
.

Let

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

be fixed. Let

be fixed parameters and let

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{i}

\displaystyle=p(k-1)+kq=:\overline{d}

(ii)

\displaystyle\overline{A}=\begin{pmatrix}p(1-2s)\widetilde{J}_{k\times k}&q(2s-1)J_{k\times k}\\

\displaystyle(1-2s)(p(k-1)-qk)

\displaystyle-p(1-2s),

\displaystyle 1+(1-2s)\frac{p}{p(k-1)+qk},

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A\sim\mathsf{G}_{n}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{S})
be a random signed adjacency matrix.
Assume

for some

and

. Then for any

there exists
C=C(r,c_{0},c_{1})>0
such that for
n
sufficiently large and with probability at least
1-n^{-r}

\displaystyle\left\|{A-\overline{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C\sqrt{\alpha}.

To bound the distance
\left\|{A-\overline{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, we focus on controlling the term
|x^{T}(A-\overline{A})y|
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\left\|{x}\right\|_{2}=\left\|{y}\right\|_{2}=1
. The first step is to discretize this region as follows. For
t\geq 0
write
B_{t}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\|{x}\right\|_{2}\leq t\}
and
B_{1}=:B
. Then, for
\delta\in(0,1)
, define the following set
\mathfrak{B}
, which serves as a grid approximation of the ball
B
with precision
\delta/\sqrt{n}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}=\left\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{n})\in S:\sqrt{n}x_{i}/\delta\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for each }i\right\}.

We make use of the following lemma, which appears in
[
14
]
and
[
24

. We omit a proof.

Lemma 3.2
.

S_{1-\delta}\subseteq\mathrm{convexhull}(\mathfrak{B})
. Moreover, for each
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, it holds

\displaystyle\left\|{X}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq(1-\delta)^{-2}\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}|x^{T}Xy|.

It can be shown via a volume argument that the cardinality
\#{\mathfrak{B}}
satisfies
\#{\mathfrak{B}}\leq e^{n\log(9/\delta)}
(see, e.g.,
[
14

). Note that, for

\displaystyle x^{T}(A-\overline{A})y

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}x_{i}(a_{ij}-\overline{a}_{ij})y_{j}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}x_{i}w_{ij}y_{j}.

where we let

denote the matrix

. We split the indices

into two complementary sets, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left\{(i,j):|x_{i}y_{j}|\leq\sqrt{\alpha}/n\right\},\hskip 7.11317pt\text{the ``light pairs,'' and}

\displaystyle\overline{\mathfrak{L}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left\{(i,j):|x_{i}y_{j}|>\sqrt{\alpha}/n\right\},\hskip 7.11317pt\text{the ``heavy pairs.''}

Therefore, since we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}|x^{T}Wy|\leq\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}\left|\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathfrak{L}(x,y)}x_{i}w_{ij}y_{j}\right|+\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}\left|\sum_{(i,j)\in\overline{\mathfrak{L}}(x,y)}x_{i}w_{ij}y_{j}\right|

it suffices to separately bound
\mathfrak{L}(x,y)
and
\overline{\mathfrak{L}}(x,y)
. First we handle the light pairs, as follows.

Lemma 3.3
.

Instate the hypotheses of
Theorem

3.1
. For each

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}\left|\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathfrak{L}(x,y)}x_{i}w_{ij}y_{j}\right|\geq t\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)\leq 2\exp\left\{-n\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\frac{2}{c_{0}}+\frac{8t}{3}}-2\log(9/\delta)\right)\right\}.

We include a proof in
Section

A.2
. Having bounded the light pairs, we turn to the complementary set of indices and provide a bound as follows.

Lemma 3.4
.

Instate the hypotheses of
Theorem

3.1
. Then there exists
C>0
depending only on
r,c_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}\left|\sum_{(i,j)\in\overline{\mathfrak{L}}(x,y)}x_{i}y_{j}w_{ij}\right|\geq C\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)\leq n^{-r}.

The proof is given in
Section

A.2
and relies on a lemma (namely,
[

) which appears in the proof of the unsigned concentration inequality

. Finally, we put the ingredients together to prove the main claim.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Letting
\delta\in(0,1)
be fixed, by
Lemma

3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{A-\overline{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\delta)^{-2}\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}|x^{T}(A-\overline{A})y|

(3)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}\left|\sum_{i,j\in\mathfrak{L}(x,y)}x_{i}y_{j}w_{ij}\right|+\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}\left|\sum_{i,j\in\overline{\mathfrak{L}}(x,y)}x_{i}y_{j}w_{ij}\right|

(4)

By

3.4
, there exists
C=C(r,c_{0})>0
such that with probability at least
1-n^{-r}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}\left|\sum_{i,j\in\overline{\mathfrak{L}}(x,y)}x_{i}y_{j}w_{ij}\right|

By

3.3
with

\displaystyle 1-2\exp\left\{-C^{\prime}n+\log{n}\right\}\geq 1-2\exp\left\{-\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}n\right\},

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}\left|\sum_{i,j\in\mathfrak{L}(x,y)}x_{i}y_{j}w_{ij}\right|\leq C\sqrt{\alpha}.

Let

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\sup_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}\left|\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathfrak{L}(x,y)}x_{i}w_{ij}y_{j}\right|\geq t\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y\in\mathfrak{B}}\mathbb{P}\left({\left|\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathfrak{L}(x,y)}x_{i}w_{ij}y_{j}\right|\geq t\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{2n\log(9/\delta)}\mathbb{P}\left({\left|\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathfrak{L}(x,y)}x_{i}w_{ij}y_{j}\right|\geq t\sqrt{\alpha}}\right).

Letting

be fixed, we define
b_{ij}=x_{i}y_{j}\mathbf{1}(|x_{i}y_{j}|\leq\sqrt{\alpha}/n)+x_{j}y_{i}\mathbf{1}(|x_{i}y_{j}|\leq\sqrt{\alpha}/n)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathfrak{L}(x,y)}x_{i}w_{ij}y_{j}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}b_{ij}w_{ij}.

Now since
|b_{ij}|\leq 2\sqrt{\alpha}/n
and
|w_{ij}|\leq 2
, it follows that each term in the sum is mean zero and is bounded above by
4\sqrt{\alpha}/n
almost surely. Set
\nu^{2}:=\sum_{i\leq j}\mathbb{E}\left({b_{ij}^{2}w_{ij}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\left|\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}b_{ij}w_{ij}\right|\geq t\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}d}{\nu^{2}+\frac{t}{3}\frac{4d}{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle=2\exp\left\{-\frac{nt^{2}}{\frac{2n}{{\alpha}}\nu^{2}+\frac{8t}{3}}\right\}.

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq j}b_{ij}^{2}\leq 2\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|x_{i}y_{j}|^{2}\leq 2\left\|{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|{y}\right\|_{2}^{2}=2,

it follows that
\nu^{2}\leq 2\max_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left({w_{ij}^{2}}\right)
, and in turn,

\displaystyle 2\exp\left\{-\frac{nt^{2}}{\frac{2n}{{\alpha}}\nu^{2}+\frac{8t}{3}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{nt^{2}}{\frac{4n}{{\alpha}}\max_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left({w_{ij}^{2}}\right)+\frac{8t}{3}}\right\}

Next, focusing on

are independent. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left({P_{ij}(1-2S_{ij})}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left({P_{ij}^{2}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left({(1-2S_{ij})^{2}}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left({P_{ij}}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left({1-2S_{ij}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{ij}-p_{ij}^{2}(1-2s_{ij})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\overline{a}_{ij}|}{|1-2s_{ij}|}-\overline{a}_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{|\overline{a}_{ij}|}{|1-2s_{ij}|}-\overline{a}_{ij}^{2}\leq\frac{|\overline{a}_{ij}|}{2\rho}-\overline{a}_{ij}^{2}\leq|\overline{a}_{ij}|\left(\frac{1}{2\rho}-|\overline{a}_{ij}|\right)\leq\frac{1}{2\rho}|\overline{a}_{ij}|.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\left|\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}b_{ij}w_{ij}\right|\geq t\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{nt^{2}}{\frac{4n}{{2c_{0}d}}|\overline{a}|_{\max}+\frac{8t}{3}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{nt^{2}}{\frac{2}{{c_{0}}}+\frac{8t}{3}}\right\}

The claim follows.

, upon combining the two estimates, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathcal{L}-\overline{\mathcal{L}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(c_{0},c_{1},r)\frac{\alpha^{1/2}}{d_{\min}}+C_{3}(c_{0},c_{1},r)\frac{\alpha^{5/2}}{\min\left\{d_{\min},\overline{d}_{\min}\right\}^{3}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(c_{0},c_{1},r)\frac{\overline{d}_{\min}^{2}\alpha^{1/2}}{\overline{d}_{\min}^{2}d_{\min}}+C_{3}(c_{0},c_{1},r)\frac{\alpha^{5/2}}{\min\left\{d_{\min},\overline{d}_{\min}\right\}^{3}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}(c_{0},c_{1},r)\frac{\alpha^{5/2}}{\min\left\{d_{\min},\overline{d}_{\min}\right\}^{3}}

with probability at least
1-3n^{-r}
, as claimed.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.3
.

We begin by fixing
i
, which without loss of generality we take to be
i=1
, and observing that

\displaystyle d_{1}=\sum_{j=2}^{k}\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}.

Here,
\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}
denotes the indicator random variable for the event
\{i,j\}\in E
; thus for
2\leq j\leq k
, we have
\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}\sim\mathsf{Bernoulli}(\gamma_{1}k^{-1/2})
, and for
k+1\leq j\leq 2k
, we have
\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}\sim\mathsf{Bernoulli}(\gamma_{2}k^{-1/2})
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left({\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}k^{-1/2}(1-\gamma_{1}k^{-1/2}),\hskip 7.11317pt2\leq j\leq k

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left({\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2}k^{-1/2}(1-\gamma_{2}k^{-1/2}),\hskip 7.11317ptk+1\leq j\leq 2k.

In either case,
\mathrm{Var}\left({\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}}\right)\leq\gamma_{1}k^{-1/2}
. Therefore by Bernstein’s inequality we have that for each
t>0
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({|d_{1}-\overline{d}|\geq t}\right)\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}{2\gamma_{1}k^{1/2}+\frac{1}{3}t}\right\}.

In particular taking
t=\gamma_{1}k^{1/3}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({|d_{1}-\overline{d}|\geq\gamma_{1}k^{1/3}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}^{2}k^{2/3}}{2\gamma_{1}k^{1/2}+\frac{1}{3}\gamma_{1}k^{1/3}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-k^{1/6}\frac{3\gamma_{1}}{14}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-k^{1/6}C(\gamma_{1})}

Thus in turn, if
|d_{1}-\overline{d}|\leq\gamma_{1}k^{1/3}
,

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\overline{d}-\gamma_{1}k^{1/3}

\displaystyle\geq(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})k^{1/2}-\gamma_{1}k^{-1/2}-\gamma_{1}k^{1/3}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}{2}k^{1/2}

for
k
sufficiently large. The claim follows by the union bound.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.10
.

Using the same setup as in the proof of
Lemma

4.3
, we begin by fixing
i
, which without loss of generality we take to be
i=1
, and observing that

\displaystyle d_{1}=\sum_{j=2}^{k}\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}.

Note that for
2\leq j\leq k
, we have
\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}\sim\mathsf{Bernoulli}(\gamma_{1}\log{k}/k)
, and for
k+1\leq j\leq 2k
, we have
\mathbf{1}_{j\sim i}\sim\mathsf{Bernoulli}(\gamma_{2}\log{k}/k)
. Denote
\mathbb{E}\left({d_{1}}\right)=\overline{d}
. By the multiplicative Chernoff bound (see, e.g.,
[
27
, Theorem 4.5]
), we have that for each
0<\delta<1
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({d_{1}\leq(1-\delta)\overline{d}}\right)\leq e^{-\delta^{2}\overline{d}/2}.

We note that

\displaystyle e^{-\delta^{2}\overline{d}/2}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\left((\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\log{k}+\gamma_{1}\log{k}/k\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-\delta^{2}\log{k}\frac{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}{2}\right\}

With
\gamma_{1}>\gamma_{2}>1
, we have
\frac{2}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}<1
,
\delta=\left(\frac{2(1+\theta)}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}\right)^{1/2}
for
\theta>0
small enough, we have

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\delta^{2}\log{k}\frac{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq k^{-(1+\theta)}.

By the union bound, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({d_{\min}\geq\theta\overline{d}}\right)\geq 1-k^{-\theta}

The claim follows.
∎
