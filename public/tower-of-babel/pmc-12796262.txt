Dynamical spin susceptibility and magnetic ordering in a square-octagon lattice in the Hubbard model
Authors: Mona Abdi, Bandar Astinchap, Erfan Norian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this work, we investigate the electronic and magnetic properties of the two-dimensional square-octagon structure (2DSOS) using a tight-binding framework augmented by on-site Coulomb interaction. We examine how varying hopping parameters, magnetic flux, and interaction strength influence the system’s band structure, dynamical transverse spin susceptibility (DTSS), and static spin structure factor (SSSF). Our findings reveal the emergence of a flat band when the third-nearest-neighbor hopping term t3 equals the nearest-neighbor hopping t1, significantly affecting the magnetic response. The frequency dependence of the DTSS demonstrates that both magnetic flux and Coulomb repulsion shift the resonance peaks to higher frequencies and suppress their intensities, indicating a suppression of low-energy spin fluctuations. Moreover, the temperature dependence of the SSSF underlines a transition from paramagnetic to ferromagnetic behavior as the ratio t2/t1 increases. These results highlight the crucial role of electronic correlations and lattice geometry in shaping the spin dynamics of flat-band systems and offer valuable insights for designing future spintronic and correlated electron devices.

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) materials have become a major focus of condensed matter research due to their unconventional electronic, magnetic, and optical properties, as well as their relevance to low-dimensional quantum physics1,2. A broad range of 2D lattice geometries has been investigated in theoretical and computational studies, including honeycomb3,4, triangular5,6, kagome7,8, and various heteropolygonal structures. These systems provide distinct platforms for examining correlation effects, flat-band physics, and geometry-driven electronic behavior. 2D heteropolygonal lattices, composed of more than one type of regular polygon, have attracted interest primarily because their geometry can give rise to nontrivial electronic structures and correlation-driven phases9,10. In these lattices, the inequivalence of bonds arises naturally from the underlying symmetry, leading to direction-dependent hopping amplitudes and modified electronic dispersion. Among these structures, the square–octagon lattice has been studied as a representative example due to its distinct connectivity and its ability to host flat bands and tunable band gaps under suitable parameter conditions11. A good example is the square-octagon lattice, which you can imagine as a square grid where each corner gets a little square added to it, and the gaps between these squares form octagons. Researchers have taken a strong interest in square-octagon lattices, especially when it comes to quantum systems involving multiple particles. The story goes back to 1951 when Utiyama introduced the partition function for this kind of 2D lattice, often called the bathroom-tile lattice12. Since then, it’s been a topic of ongoing study. For instance, Lin and coworkers came up with a formula to describe spontaneous magnetization in what’s known as the 4–8 version of the Ising model13. Later on, Lin and Fang updated those results, and their revised work matched well with exact calculations at low temperatures, all the way up to the twelfth term in the expansion14. Lately, researchers have been looking into how phase transitions and magnetic behavior show up in Ising models with mixed spins, like combinations of (5/2, 3/2)15 and (3/2, 1)16 on square-octagon lattices, using Monte Carlo simulations. What they’ve found is that the system goes from a ferrimagnetic state to a paramagnetic one at a certain critical temperature (Tc). This critical temperature gets higher when the exchange interactions are stronger, but it drops if the crystal field becomes more negative. In earlier work, another approach called the star-triangle mapping transformation was used to figure out the magnetization and phase diagrams for a system with spin-1/2 and spin-S particles on a square-octagon lattice17. They also looked at how single-ion anisotropy affects critical behavior and magnetization, identifying different types of transitions based on Néel’s classification. In their study, Dahbani and colleagues explored how the mixed spin-5/2 and spin-3/2 Ising model behaves on a 2D square-octagon lattice18. They used a mean-field approach based on the Bogoliubov inequality from statistical physics. Their results revealed both second-order and first-order phase transitions, along with the presence of isolated critical points. Wang et al. looked into how various factors like exchange coupling, anisotropy, temperature, and even time-varying magnetic fields affect the magnetic and thermodynamic behavior of the 2D square octagon lattice19. They analyzed things like dynamic magnetization, order parameters, susceptibility, internal energy, and the system’s phase diagram. Norian and co-workers systematically studied the electronic properties and quantum capacitance of the 2D square-octagon lattice under various external conditions using the Hubbard model and Green’s function method20. Their findings reveal that the system’s electronic phase can be tuned by varying the hopping amplitude ratio t2/t1, transitioning between semiconducting (t2 = t1 and t2 = 3t1) and metallic (t2 = 2t1) states. Because of its special properties, this lattice has potential uses in things like carbon monoxide sensors21, ion batteries22, and hydrogen storage systems23, making it an exciting candidate for future materials development. To our knowledge, the dynamical and SSSFs of the 2D square-octagon lattice have not been examined previously. In this work, we use the Hubbard model together with the Green-function formalism to compute these quantities and analyze how they evolve under variations of the hopping parameters, on-site Coulomb repulsion, and external magnetic field. The rest of the paper is organized like this: Sect. 2 introduces the Hubbard model Hamiltonian we’re using for our calculations. Section 3 walks through the analytical and numerical results, highlighting how the spin susceptibility responds to the different parameters. Finally, Sect. 4 wraps things up with a summary of the main findings.

Calculation method

To get started, we use a tight-binding model to understand the dynamical and SSSF of the 2D square octagon lattice. The basic idea is that electrons are mostly localized at atomic sites but can “hop” from one site to another, with that movement described by hopping parameters24. The square-octagon lattice, first proposed by Liu and co-workers, is a 2D carbon allotrope composed of alternating squares and octagons25. Later research even suggested ways it could be synthesized on a large scale26. Even with this intricate design, the structure keeps the symmetry of a square lattice and falls under the C₄ point group. Figure 1 shows a schematic of the square-octagon lattice. Each unit cell contains four atoms, labeled A, B, C, and D. In the present study, we set (t1 = 1) and use it as the fundamental energy unit. This convention is standard in tight-binding studies and allows all other quantities—such as t2, t3, the interaction strength (U), and the excitation frequency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\omega\:$$\end{document})—to be expressed in dimensionless form, avoiding any artificial assignment of physical values to the hopping amplitudes. The hopping processes appearing in Fig. 1 are defined unambiguously as follows. t1 denotes nearest-neighbor hopping between sites inside the same unit cell. t3 corresponds to next-nearest-neighbor hopping along the diagonals of the square plaquette within the unit cell. t2 refers to nearest-neighbor hopping connecting sites that belong to adjacent unit cells.

where Rj denotes the position of site j, k is the wave vector, α labels the sublattice index within the unit cell, and N is the total number of lattice sites.

To account for the interaction of electrons in this system, we employ the Hubbard model. This model builds on the tight-binding approach by including short-range interactions between electrons. The mean-field decoupling of the on-site Hubbard term provides a qualitatively reliable description only in the weak-to-intermediate interaction regime, where (U/t) is not large enough to induce strong dynamical correlations or Mott physics. In this range, mean-field correctly describes the onset of magnetic polarization and band reconstruction while remaining computationally practical. Similar mean-field treatments have been successfully applied to correlated 2D systems and carbon-based lattices27,28. It’s especially useful for understanding materials like Mott insulators, which become insulating because electrons strongly repel each other29–32. Also, we emphasize that the mean-field approach neglects short-range quantum fluctuations and cannot capture strongly correlated phases such as the Mott transition. Our results should therefore be interpreted as qualitative trends rather than quantitative predictions.

It’s a standard trick in physics for finding allowed energy values; it’s how we get from the model to actual energy bands. Since the matrix is 8 × 8, eight energy bands show how energy depends on the wave vector.

Numerical results and discussion

Let’s begin by examining how the hopping parameter t3 influences the electronic behavior of the 2DSOS. Specifically, we look at the case where t3 = 0, meaning there’s no hopping along those particular paths. Figures 2a and 3a show the resulting band structures under two conditions: t2 = t1 (Fig. 2a) and t2 = 2t1(Fig. 3a). In Fig. 2a, the band structure indicates that the system behaves like a metal, electrons can move freely, and there’s no energy gap at the Fermi level. This aligns well with previous theoretical predictions. In Fig. 3a, even when the hopping strength t2 is doubled, the system still shows metallic behavior. In both cases, we don’t see any flat bands; flat bands typically mean localized electronic states, which can be a sign of unusual electronic phases like magnetism or superconductivity. So, when t3 is absent, the system behaves as a conventional metal with dispersive energy bands. When t3 is introduced (t3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\ne\:$$\end{document} 0), it allows electrons to hop along diagonal or longer-range paths that were previously inactive. This additional hopping can significantly alter the band structure’s energy dispersion and symmetry. In the 2DSOS, the appearance of flat bands produces a high density of states at the corresponding energies. Within the mean-field Hubbard framework employed here, this primarily affects the magnetic response by enhancing the spin susceptibility near the flat-band energy. Moreover, adjusting t3 modifies the overall bandwidth and can open or close band gaps between neighboring energy bands. Depending on the values of t3 relative to t1 and t2, the system may transition from a metallic state to a semi-metallic or insulating regime, or vice versa. In Figs. 2b and 3b, we examine the effect of introducing a third-nearest-neighbor hopping term t3 = t1 in the 2DSOS. The band structures are shown for two distinct cases: t2 = t1 and t2 =2t1, respectively. From Fig. 2b, although two flat bands appear when (t3 = t1), the energy spectrum remains gapless. The absence of an energy gap indicates that the system retains its metallic character in this parameter regime. Also, in this case, one of the flat bands is connected to the conduction band. In contrast, Fig. 3 (b) shows the band structure when t2 = 2t1 and t3 =t1. Although two flat bands again appear due to the inclusion of t3, the overall structure remains metallic, meaning the bands continue to cross the Fermi level, and there is no gap preventing electrical conduction. The contrast between Figs. 2(b) and 3(b) highlights how the interplay between hopping parameters t1, t2, and t3 governs the electronic phase of the system. While flat bands are generated in both scenarios (due to enhanced localization from t3), the presence or absence of a band gap depends sensitively on the ratio of t2 to t1 34.

This quantity is directly related to experimental observables obtained from inelastic scattering techniques such as neutron scattering or electron spin resonance. Therefore, studying Imχ+−(q, ω) allows us to understand how electron correlations, hopping parameters, and magnetic flux influence the low-energy spin excitations and the stability of magnetic ordering in the lattice35. Conclusion

In this study, we have systematically explored the electronic and magnetic properties of the 2DSOS using a Hubbard model, with particular focus on the effects of varying hopping amplitudes t2 and t3, applied magnetic flux, and on-site Coulomb repulsion. Our findings highlight several key phenomena relevant to the design of novel quantum materials. The inclusion of the third-nearest-neighbor hopping term t3 introduces flat bands into the system, which are known to enhance correlation effects. Flat bands and band gap openings are evident in the calculated band structures, indicating potential for tunable electronic phases. The DTSS analysis reveals that both magnetic flux and Coulomb interaction have a significant impact on spin excitations. Specifically, the application of magnetic flux shifts the susceptibility peaks to higher frequencies and reduces their intensity, indicating a suppression of low-energy spin fluctuations. Similarly, increasing the on-site Coulomb interaction leads to a reduction in peak intensity and a shift to higher frequencies, reflecting enhanced electronic correlations and modified magnon dynamics. The temperature dependence of the SSSF shows that magnetic ordering is highly sensitive to the hopping parameters and Coulomb strength. For t2 = t1, the system remains in a paramagnetic state across the temperature range, while for t2 = 2t1, the behavior is consistent with a transition to a ferromagnetic phase at low temperatures. Furthermore, the presence of strong Coulomb repulsion suppresses long-range spin correlations, highlighting its role in tuning magnetic responses. Overall, our work provides detailed insights into the interplay between lattice geometry, electronic hopping, and interaction effects in the 2DSOS. These findings may contribute to the development of spintronic devices and quantum simulators based on engineered flat-band systems.
