Hemodialysis equipment malfunction incidence and risk profiling in clinical settings: a retrospective single-center cohort analysis
Authors: Junjie Wang, Tingting Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHemodialysis equipment alarms significantly impact clinical workflow and patient safety. This study aimed to characterize alarm patterns, identify risk factors, and evaluate management effectiveness in hemodialysis facilities.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted at 1 hemodialysis center from January 2020 to December 2023. Equipment alarm data from hemodialysis stations (Nikkiso, Gambro, and B.Braun) were systematically collected and analyzed. Multivariate regression and machine learning approaches identified risk factors and developed predictive model.ResultsAmong 4231 recorded alarm events over 32 months, the monthly alarm rate averaged 132.2 ± 54.4 events. The COVID-19 pandemic period (2020–2021) accounted for 82.2% of alarms, with a significant 78.3% reduction post-pandemic (P = 0.022). B.Braun equipment generated 68.7% of alarms, significantly higher than Gambro (21.2%) and Nikkiso (10.1%) devices (P < 0.001). Engineering analysis revealed B.Braun’s lower pressure thresholds (150 vs. 180 mmHg), higher sensor sensitivity (1 vs. 2–5 mmHg resolution), and conservative air detection algorithms (0.3 vs. 0.5 mL) contributed to increased alarm frequency. Pressure-related alarms predominated (37.4%), while 94.5% occurred during active treatment phases. Vascular access complications contributed to 19.8% of events, with permanent catheters being the primary source (47.3%). Patient-related factors accounted for 83.6% of human factor contributions. Patients with BMI > 30 kg/m2 experienced 28% more alarms (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.12–1.47, P < 0.001), highlighting the need for individualized alarm strategies. The alarm management protocol achieved a 99.3% immediate resolution rate. Predictive modeling (sensitivity 72.3%, specificity 75.8%, PPV 68.9%, NPV 78.6%) enabled targeted interventions, resulting in a 43% alarm reduction, a 56% false alarm decrease, and 51% fewer treatment interruptions (all P < 0.001).ConclusionsEquipment-specific alarm patterns and human factors significantly influence hemodialysis alarm burden. Implementation of predictive analytics and targeted interventions substantially improves alarm management effectiveness and clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Hemodialysis represents a life-sustaining treatment for over 2.6 million patients with end-stage renal disease worldwide, with this number projected to double by 2030 [1]. The safety and efficacy of hemodialysis treatments depend critically on the reliable performance of complex medical equipment, including dialysis machines, water treatment systems, and associated monitoring devices [2]. However, equipment malfunctions and alarm events remain significant challenges in clinical practice, potentially compromising patient safety, treatment adequacy, and healthcare resource utilization [3, 4].

Modern hemodialysis machines incorporate sophisticated alarm systems designed to detect deviations from predetermined safety parameters, including pressure variations, air detection, conductivity changes, and blood flow irregularities [5]. While these alarm systems serve as essential safeguards, the high frequency of alarms in hemodialysis units has emerged as a critical concern. Studies indicate that nurses in hemodialysis units may encounter 150–350 alarms per shift, with up to 85–99% representing clinically insignificant events [6]. This phenomenon, termed "alarm fatigue", can lead to delayed response times, inappropriate alarm silencing, and potentially missed critical events [7].

The clinical engineering perspective on hemodialysis equipment performance has gained increasing attention as healthcare systems strive to optimize patient safety while managing operational efficiency. Previous research has identified multiple factors contributing to alarm generation, including equipment design characteristics, maintenance protocols, user interface complexity, and human factors [8]. Equipment-specific variations in alarm algorithms and sensor sensitivities further complicate standardized approaches to alarm management. Despite these insights, comprehensive analyses examining the interplay between equipment type, temporal patterns, and human factors in real-world clinical settings remain limited.

Recent evidence highlights the multifaceted health challenges faced by hemodialysis patients that may influence alarm patterns and outcomes. Arslan et al. (2023) demonstrated significant associations between cardiometabolic risks and body composition parameters in hemodialysis patients, revealing that individual characteristics such as body mass index, muscle mass, and fluid status substantially affect hemodynamic stability during dialysis treatments [9]. These findings suggest that patient-specific physiological profiles may predispose certain individuals to higher alarm frequencies, particularly pressure-related and vascular access complications. Furthermore, Yaprak et al. (2023) identified multiple factors influencing mortality in hemodialysis patients, including cardiovascular comorbidities, nutritional status, dialysis adequacy, and treatment-related complications [10]. Their comprehensive analysis underscores that effective alarm management, as a component of overall treatment quality, may contribute to improved patient survival by preventing treatment interruptions, reducing hemodynamic instability, and minimizing adverse events. These studies collectively emphasize the importance of developing personalized alarm management strategies that account for individual patient risk profiles, body composition parameters, and comorbidity burden.

The COVID-19 pandemic introduced unprecedented challenges to hemodialysis services globally, necessitating rapid adaptations in clinical protocols, staffing patterns, and infection control measures [11]. These operational changes may have significantly influenced equipment utilization patterns and alarm management strategies, yet their impact on equipment performance metrics remains poorly characterized [12]. Understanding these temporal variations is crucial for developing resilient alarm management systems capable of maintaining effectiveness across diverse operational contexts.

This study aimed to conduct a comprehensive retrospective analysis of hemodialysis equipment alarm events in a single-center clinical setting. The primary objectives were to: (1) characterize the frequency, distribution, and temporal patterns of alarm events across different equipment manufacturers; (2) identify equipment-specific and human factor contributions to alarm generation; (3) evaluate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on alarm patterns and management strategies; and (4) develop predictive models to guide targeted quality improvement interventions. By addressing these objectives, this research seeks to advance clinical engineering approaches to hemodialysis safety and establish evidence-based frameworks for alarm management optimization.

Discussion

Principal findings and implications for clinical engineering practice

Our study represents one of the largest comprehensive analyses of hemodialysis equipment alarm patterns in clinical settings, encompassing 4231 alarm events over 32 months across 1 dialysis center. The overall monthly alarm rate of 132.2 ± 54.4 events reflects the complex nature of modern hemodialysis systems and underscores the critical importance of systematic alarm management in clinical engineering practice. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that hemodialysis units experience frequent alarm events that require immediate attention and pose significant challenges for both healthcare personnel and patients [13].

The predominance of alarms during the active treatment phase (94.5%) is particularly noteworthy and consistent with established literature on dialysis safety monitoring. As noted in clinical engineering guidelines, the treatment phase represents the most critical period for patient safety due to the active blood circulation and ultrafiltration processes [14]. Our findings support the need for enhanced monitoring protocols during this phase, as suggested by recent safety advisories from regulatory agencies highlighting the risks associated with pressure-related alarms and their potential for catastrophic outcomes if not properly managed.

The distribution of alarm types, with pressure-related alarms constituting 37.4% of total events, corroborates findings from previous studies on hemodialysis system failures. The high prevalence of pressure alarms reflects the sensitivity of modern dialysis machines to hemodynamic changes and vascular access complications. This finding is consistent with research by Wang et al., who demonstrated that pressure monitoring systems, while essential for patient safety, can generate numerous false-positive alarms that contribute to alarm fatigue among healthcare providers [15]. The challenge of balancing sensitivity with specificity in alarm systems remains a fundamental concern in clinical engineering, as excessive false alarms can paradoxically reduce patient safety by desensitizing staff to genuine emergencies [16].

Our analysis revealed that patient-related factors contributed to 83.6% of all alarms, with vascular access complications accounting for 19.8% of total events. This finding emphasizes the critical interface between human physiology and machine performance in hemodialysis systems. The predominance of permanent catheter-related alarms (47.3% of vascular access alarms) highlights a specific vulnerability in the dialysis population. As demonstrated in simulation studies, venous needle dislodgement and catheter-related complications can lead to severe adverse events, including life-threatening blood loss, particularly when alarm systems fail to detect subtle pressure changes [17]. These findings underscore the importance of developing more sophisticated alarm algorithms that can differentiate between clinically significant events and nuisance alarms.

The integration of recent evidence from Arslan et al. regarding body composition’s impact on hemodynamic stability and Yaprak et al.’s [10] findings on mortality factors emphasizes the need for personalized alarm management strategies that account for individual patient risk profiles, body composition parameters, and comorbidity burden. Our finding that patients with BMI > 30 kg/m [2] experienced 28% more alarms supports this personalized approach.

COVID-19 pandemic impact on dialysis equipment performance and alarm patterns

The COVID-19 pandemic period (2020–2021) exhibited a significantly higher alarm burden (82.2% of total alarms) compared to the post-pandemic period, with a 78.3% reduction in alarm rates observed in 2023. This dramatic shift likely reflects multiple intersecting factors that fundamentally altered dialysis care delivery during the pandemic. The increased alarm frequency during COVID-19 aligns with reports from multiple international centers documenting operational challenges in maintaining dialysis services during the pandemic [18].

Several mechanisms may explain the elevated alarm rates during the pandemic period. First, staffing challenges were widespread, with many facilities experiencing reduced nurse-to-patient ratios due to illness, quarantine requirements, and redeployment of staff to critical care units. Research has shown that adequate staffing levels are crucial for preventing and rapidly responding to equipment alarms, and the pandemic-induced staffing shortages likely contributed to delayed alarm responses and subsequent cascade effects [19]. Second, supply chain disruptions affected the availability of consumables and replacement parts, potentially leading to extended use of equipment components beyond their optimal performance period.

The pandemic also necessitated significant modifications to standard operating procedures, including enhanced infection control measures that may have inadvertently affected equipment performance. For instance, the increased use of personal protective equipment (PPE) may have impaired staff dexterity and visual assessment capabilities, potentially contributing to higher rates of procedural errors and subsequent alarms [12]. Additionally, patient-related factors during COVID-19, including increased anxiety, reluctance to attend treatments, and altered hemodynamic status in infected patients, likely contributed to the elevated alarm burden [20].

The substantial reduction in alarm rates post-pandemic suggests successful adaptation and implementation of lessons learned during the crisis. This improvement may reflect multiple factors, including enhanced staff training, optimized workflows, and technological upgrades implemented in response to pandemic challenges. The experience gained during this period has likely contributed to more resilient dialysis delivery systems, as evidenced by our finding of a 100% immediate intervention success rate in the post-COVID period compared to 99.2% during the pandemic.

Equipment-specific performance variations and clinical engineering implications

Our analysis revealed significant manufacturer-specific differences in alarm patterns, with B.Braun equipment generating 68.7% of total alarms despite representing a proportional share of the equipment inventory. This finding has important implications for equipment selection, maintenance strategies, and staff training protocols. The monthly alarm rate for B.Braun devices (90.9 ± 45.0) was significantly higher than both Gambro (28.1 ± 11.8) and Nikkiso (13.4 ± 4.2) equipment, suggesting fundamental differences in alarm algorithms, sensor sensitivity, or user interface design.

These manufacturer-specific variations align with previous research indicating that different hemodialysis systems employ varying approaches to alarm management and safety monitoring [21]. The higher alarm rate in B.Braun equipment may reflect more conservative alarm thresholds designed to maximize patient safety, albeit at the cost of increased nuisance alarms. Alternatively, these differences might indicate variations in equipment reliability, maintenance requirements, or compatibility with specific patient populations and treatment protocols.

From a clinical engineering perspective, these findings emphasize the importance of manufacturer-specific training and maintenance protocols. As noted in biomedical equipment management literature, the complexity of modern hemodialysis systems requires specialized knowledge for each equipment type, and standardized approaches may not be optimal across different manufacturers. The observed variations in alarm patterns suggest that clinical engineering departments should develop manufacturer-specific competencies and consider these differences when planning equipment procurement and deployment strategies.

Strategies to address alarm fatigue include: (1) implementing tiered alarm systems with priority classification (critical/high/medium/low); (2) optimizing alarm thresholds based on patient risk stratification; (3) developing smart alarm algorithms that incorporate patient-specific parameters; (4) enhancing staff training on alarm recognition and appropriate response protocols; and (5) establishing multidisciplinary alarm governance committees for continuous quality improvement.

Predictive modeling applications and future directions in dialysis technology

The successful development of our predictive model with an AUC of 0.774 represents a significant advancement in proactive alarm management for hemodialysis systems. This performance level, while not achieving the higher AUCs reported in some machine learning applications for mortality prediction (0.85–0.93), is nonetheless clinically meaningful given the complexity of real-time alarm prediction [22]. The integration of equipment, human, and temporal factors in our model reflects the multifaceted nature of alarm generation in clinical settings.

The implementation of our predictive model resulted in substantial improvements across key performance indicators, including a 43% reduction in total alarm frequency and a 56% reduction in false alarm rates. These improvements are particularly significant given the well-documented problem of alarm fatigue in healthcare settings. By reducing nuisance alarms while maintaining sensitivity for clinically significant events, predictive modeling can enhance both patient safety and staff efficiency [23].

Recent advances in machine learning applications for dialysis care extend beyond alarm management. Studies have demonstrated successful applications in predicting intradialytic hypotension, with deep learning models achieving AUCs exceeding 0.90 for near-term event prediction. Similarly, machine learning algorithms have shown promise in predicting dialysis adequacy, optimizing erythropoietin dosing, and identifying patients at risk for adverse outcomes [24]. These diverse applications suggest a future where artificial intelligence-augmented dialysis systems can provide personalized, adaptive care based on real-time patient and equipment data. The integration of Internet of Things (IoT) technologies with predictive modeling represents a particularly promising direction for future development. Real-time data streaming from dialysis machines, combined with cloud-based analytics, can enable continuous optimization of treatment parameters and early detection of equipment degradation [25]. However, several challenges must be addressed: (1) heterogeneity of dialysis equipment and data systems presents interoperability challenges; (2) regulatory considerations regarding AI in medical devices require careful navigation; and (3) the "black box" nature of some algorithms raises concerns about interpretability and clinical trust.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single-center design may limit generalizability to facilities with different patient populations, staffing models, or equipment configurations. Second, the retrospective nature of this analysis precludes assessment of causality and may introduce selection bias. Third, the 4-month data gap during system maintenance may have affected temporal trend analysis. Fourth, unmeasured confounders such as individual nurse experience, patient comorbidities, and seasonal variations in patient acuity were not captured in our dataset. Fifth, the predictive model requires external validation before implementation in other clinical settings. Finally, alarm resolution outcomes were based on documentation in electronic systems, which may not fully capture all clinical nuances. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable insights for alarm management optimization in hemodialysis settings.

Conclusion

Equipment-specific alarm patterns and human factors significantly influence hemodialysis alarm burden. Implementation of predictive analytics and targeted interventions substantially improves alarm management effectiveness and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single hospital hemodialysis center from January 2020 to December 2023. The study protocol received institutional review board approval with waived informed consent due to the retrospective design using de-identified equipment alarm data. The 32-month observation period encompassed two phases: COVID-19 pandemic period (January 2020–December 2021) and post-pandemic period (May–December 2023), with a 4-month system maintenance gap excluded.

The facility operated 15 hemodialysis stations equipped with three manufacturers’ devices: Nikkiso (DBB-06S and DBB-07), Gambro (AK96), and B.Braun (Dialog +). All equipment maintained compliance with IEC 60601-1-8:2012 standards for medical electrical equipment alarm systems and underwent regular preventive maintenance per manufacturers’ specifications.

Data collection and alarm classification

Alarm event data were automatically captured through the integrated Clinical Engineering Management System (CEMS) and cross-referenced with the Hemodialysis Information System (HIS). Each alarm record included timestamp, equipment identifier, alarm type, duration, resolution status, and contextual parameters.

Alarm selection criteria and threshold determination

Alarms were included if they: (1) occurred during scheduled hemodialysis treatments; (2) required clinical staff intervention or acknowledgment; and (3) were documented in both CEMS and HIS databases with complete data fields.

Alarms were hierarchically classified into five primary categories: technical system alarms, conductivity-related, pressure-related, air detection, and vascular access complications. Secondary classification stratified events by treatment stage (self-check, priming, treatment, reinfusion, disinfection) and shift timing (AM: 07:00–13:00; PM: 13:00–19:00).

Human factor analysis categorized alarm contributors into four groups: nurse-related, physician-related, engineer-related, and patient-related factors. Vascular access complications were sub-classified by access type (blunt needle, sharp needle, temporary catheter, permanent catheter, indwelling needle) and precipitating factors (coughing, position-related, blood flow issues, pressure instability).

Data completeness and quality control

During the 32-month study period, a 4-month gap (January–April 2022) occurred due to system maintenance and upgrade. During this period, no alarm data were collected. Missing data analysis revealed < 5% incomplete records for individual alarm events, which were excluded from analysis using a complete-case analysis approach. Sensitivity analysis confirmed no significant bias introduced by missing data.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized alarm frequencies using mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and median with interquartile range (IQR) for skewed distributions. Normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests and Q-Q plots. Between-group comparisons employed χ2 tests for categorical variables and one-way ANOVA for continuous variables among equipment types. Post hoc pairwise comparisons used Tukey’s HSD test with Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.017). Temporal trend analysis utilized Kruskal–Wallis tests for annual comparisons and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for pandemic versus post-pandemic periods. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent risk factors for alarm occurrence, adjusting for equipment type, shift timing, treatment stage, and human factors. Model assumptions were verified through variance inflation factor assessment (VIF < 5), Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (χ2 = 11.23, P = 0.19), and Box–Tidwell transformation for linearity. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each predictor. Network analysis visualized human factor interactions using correlation-based adjacency matrices. All statistical tests were two-tailed with significance set at P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0).

Predictive model development and quality improvement assessment

A random forest algorithm was developed to predict alarm events, selected based on superior discriminative performance (AUC = 0.774) compared to gradient boosting, support vector machines, and logistic regression. The model incorporated 15 predictor variables across equipment parameters, temporal factors, human elements, and historical alarm patterns. Data were partitioned into training (70%, n = 2962) and test sets (30%, n = 1269) with tenfold cross-validation. Hyperparameters were optimized via grid search. Model performance achieved sensitivity 72.3%, specificity 75.8%, PPV 68.9%, and NPV 78.6%. Quality improvement interventions based on predictions were evaluated using paired t-tests and McNemar’s test (P < 0.05).
