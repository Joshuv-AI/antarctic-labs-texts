Integrating gene expression and morphological traits for drought stress adaptation in maize hybrids
Authors: Muhammad Umar Rasheed, Aiman Malik, Qurban Ali, Aiman Arshad, Muhammad Arshad Javed, Fawad Ali, Ajaz Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maize, the third major crop in Pakistan, faces yield challenges due to climate changes agronomic environment, and genetic factors. There is an urgent need for high-yielding hybrid varieties to adapt to different agro-climactic conditions to meet the day-by-day increasing demand of maize. This study utilized 7 commercially available genotypes to focus on the Line × Tester mating design to analyze the effects of drought stress on maize genotypes by using a Randomized Complete Design Block (RCBD) under normal irrigation conditions and 50% drought conditions to collect data of different agronomic traits such Root Length (RL), Shoot Length (SL), Root-to-Shoot Length Ratio (RSLR), Seedling Biomass (SB), Leaf Length (LL), Leaf Width (LW), and Leaf Area (LA) at the seedling stage, and for the evaluation of the effects of stress, Lipid Peroxidation, Malondialdehyde (MDA), H2O2 Concentration, and Enzymatic Activity analysis were performed. The results show highly significant genotypic differences of all traits with substantial genetic variability. Additive gene action was remarkable in root-to-shoot length ratio (RSLR) and seedling biomass. Dominant gene action under drought was particularly significant in root length and leaf area. Gene expression analysis of antioxidants under control and drought conditions showed great competition among oxidants and antioxidants to minimize the effects of stress. To the best of our knowledge, this is among few studies that present connecting gene expression signatures with seedling morphological responses, this work presents a breeding-relevant framework that makes it possible to select drought-tolerant maize hybrids more precisely.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56731-y.

Introduction

Maize (Zea mays L.) is the third major and important grain crop that is widely cultivated in Pakistan. It is the third most regularly farmed crop in the country, following wheat and rice1. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) statistics database showed that the annual maize yield in Pakistan varied between 6826379 tons in 2018 and 10183109 tons in 2022 (Dahri et al., 2024)2. These fluctuations demonstrate the sensitivity of maize cultivation to numerous climatic, agronomic, and genetic factors of plants. Exports of maize in Pakistan gradually increased from 2018 to 2022 from 6,772 tons to 797,186.02 tons respectively3. FAO_Statss) The production of hybrid maize varieties has significantly boosted Pakistan’s position among the top 20 maize producers. By exploiting heterosis (hybrid vigor), these hybrids demonstrate the higher yield potential, improved stress tolerance, and superior agronomic traits compared to their open-pollinated counterparts4. The growing population at a high rate is another challenge for the world to meet the need for healthy and highly nutritious food. Line × Tester, being an important breeding technique to improve the genotype of current cultivated species, plays an important role in the production of hybrids of maize with superior characteristics5,6. Using the Line × Tester breeding model, each male plants has an equal opportunity to mate with same female plant, allowing the evaluation of both General Combining Ability (GCA) and Specific Combining Ability (SCA) of the parental lines. This study accumulates the genetic factor, effect of all antioxidants during stress condition at seedling stage. This procedure comprises the crossing of a set of genetically diverse inbred lines, which could potentially be utilized as parents with a standard tester line followed by the evaluation of the resulting progeny for desirable agronomic traits at seedling stage. This approach permits the identification of superior parental combinations that can be further used to develop high-yielding hybrid varieties adapted to specific environmental conditions and market demands. Line × Tester is also a method to identify the precise combinations of genes of cultivated inbred lines to evaluate the yield components and specific traits in hybrid progeny (Abrha et al., 2013)5.

This method helps the breeder to study and predict the hybrid performance of all genotypes in all possible parental combinations (Abrha et al., 2013)5. By applying line × tester analysis, breeders can identify hybrid combinations that are performed in specific environments and evaluate their ability to perform under different conditions in different areas of the world by making them adapted. To reduce the yield shortage, make nutritious food, and address the decreasing yield potential of modern maize varieties caused by biotic and abiotic stresses, genetic drift, and seed contamination, there is an urgent need for production of high-yielding varieties that fit different agro-climatic conditions (Gami et al., 2018)7. Previous studies show the line × tester analysis of different crops and maize also but lacks the comparative analysis of drought stress of these specified genotypes which previously have been adopted in multiple areas of Pakistan and line × tester analysis to calculate the GCA, SCA, Dominant Gene Action, Additive Gene Action and genetic parameters. Seedling stage data was utilized for statistical analysis and antioxidant determinations. This study focused on seedling stage because seedlings are more sensitive to abiotic stress and proved studied in this research. Furthermore, this stage is crucial for the evolution of early indications of performance, efficiency and cost-effectiveness, screening for disease resistance and predictive value for later growth stages.

To achieve these qualities, a breeder needs to study and evaluate the genetic diversity within parental lines to improve the traits in offspring and then demonstrate a correlation between the performance of F1 hybrid and the genetic variation through combining ability analysis (Mousa & Aly, 2012)8. The line × tester mating design is a statistical tool to study the parental genotypes and their cross combinations to generate superior maize varieties (Steel et al., 1997)9. This approach is helpful for the calculation of general combining ability (GCA) and specific combining ability (SCA), providing detailed information and the genetic performance of yield and its contributing factors10.

In addition to phenotypic and biochemical evaluations, this study incorporated gene expression analysis to understand the molecular basis of drought tolerance among maize genotypes. Conducting gene expression analysis was essential to uncover the molecular mechanisms that underpin drought tolerance in maize at the seedling stage. By examining the regulation patterns of oxidant and antioxidant genes across different plant organs under stress conditions, this analysis helps identify which specific genes are activated or suppressed during drought11,12. Understanding these transcriptional changes is crucial for linking physiological responses, such as antioxidant activity, with underlying genetic control. This molecular insight complements the phenotypic data from the line × tester analysis, enhancing the accuracy of selecting drought-resilient parental lines. Ultimately, integrating gene expression profiling into breeding strategies provides a powerful approach for developing maize hybrids that are not only high yielding but also genetically equipped to withstand abiotic stresses like drought13. There is a knowledge gap regarding the genetic biochemical basis of drought tolerance because previous research has rarely combined Line × Tester analysis with antioxidant gene expression profiles. Therefore, the objective of this study is to integrate line × tester analysis with gene expression profiling and physiological assays to identify key genetic and biochemical mechanisms underlying drought tolerance in maize hybrids.

Materials and methods

This experimental material consists of seven maize genotypes were collected from the Department of Plant Breeding and Genetics, Faculty of Agricultural Sciences, University of the Punjab Lahore, Pakistan, viz. B-316, EV-340, E-322, F-96, EV-347, B-11, and B-336. These genotypes, having demonstrated adaptability to the environment and proven utility in previous doctoral research, are selected for continued use in this study (http://localhost:80/xmlui/handle/123456789/816). Three genotypes (EV-347, B-11, and B-336) were employed as testers, and four genotypes (B-316, EV-340, E-322, and F-96) were utilized as lines (Table 1).

All these genotypes were elite inbred lines developed at Department of Plant Breeding and Genetics, University of the Punjab and maintained through more than five generations of self-pollination to ensure homozygosity. Each replication consisted of 19 experimental units (7 parents + 12 crosses). For these experiments, each genotype was grown in plastic pots of 25 cm diameter × 30 cm height filled with 8 kg of sandy-loam soil. Three plants per genotype were maintained per pot after thinning, and those pots were spaced 30 cm apart in rows separated by 45 cm to ensure uniform aeration and light penetration.

In RCBD with three replications, each genotype was cultivated in plastic pots measuring 25 by 30 cm, with three plants per pot, using 8 kg of sandy-loam soil. A Line × Tester design was used to cross the genotypes, and the F1 offspring and parents were cultivated in RCBD with three replications under both normal and drought conditions. During the seedling stage (10–29 DAS), a 50% field capacity drought stress was applied, and conditions were tracked to ensure consistency. Drought stress corresponding to 50% field capacity was imposed at the seedling stage (vegetative V3–V4) from the 10th to 29th day after sowing. Soil moisture was gravimetrically controlled as pots at 100% field capacity were weighed daily, and water loss by evapotranspiration was replenished. For drought-stress treatment, pots were maintained at half this weight (≈ 50% FC) by controlled irrigation. Soil moisture was monitored every three days using a portable TDR moisture meter.

Seven 7 traits; Root Length (RL), Shoot Length (SL), Root-to-Shoot Length Ratio (RSLR), Seedling Biomass (SB), Leaf Length (LL), Leaf Width (LW) and Leaf Area (LA) were analyzed of variance to calculate the significant differences between crosses and parents14 Steel et al., 19977). Then calculated the General Combining Ability (GCA) and Specific Combining Ability (SCA).

Oxidants analysis

Antioxidant analyses were conducted at offsprings’ seedling stages of normal and 50% drought conditions after crossing. Those analyses are:

Lipid peroxidation

For the evaluation of lipid peroxidation and malondialdehyde MDA in plants during drought stress, we used a modified form of Thiobarbituric Acid Reactive Substances (TBRAS) method15,16. The 0.25 g leaf samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min to homogenize them in a 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution. A 20% TCA solution containing 0.5% Thiobarbituric acid (TBA) was then heated to 95 °C for 30 min, after which the supernatant was removed. The tubes were cooled in an ice bath for 10 min to stop the reaction and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. After subtracting non-specific absorption at 600 nm, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm. The MDA content was determined and expressed in nmolg− 1 fresh weight of leaves using an extinction value of 155 mM− 1 cm− 1.

H2O2 calculation

A quarter gram (0.25 g) sample of leaves was homogenized in 0.1% trichloroacetic acid and then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for fifteen minutes at four degrees Celsius. A 0.3 ml portion of the supernatant was combined with 1 ml of 1 M potassium iodide solution and 1.7 ml of pH 7.0 potassium phosphate buffer, then incubated for 5 min. The oxidation product was measured at A390, and H₂O₂ concentration was derived from a standard curve established with known H₂O₂ concentrations and expressed in nmol g − 1 fresh weight (Zhou et al., 2006)17.

Enzyme assays

Leaf samples (0.25 g) were homogenized in a pre-chilled mortar and pestle with 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 0.1 mM phenylmethylsulphonyl fluoride (PMSF), 0.5 mM EDTA, 2% PVP, and 5 mM ascorbate. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. Catalase (CAT) activity was evaluated spectrophotometrically (Johansson & Borg, 1988)18 by measuring the preservation of catalase electrophoretic mobility in the presence of dithiothreitol (DTT). Specific CAT activity was reported as µmol min− 1 mg− 1 protein. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was tested according to the method of Dhindsa (Dhindsa et al., 1981)19. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to generate a 50% inhibition of the starting reaction rate in the absence of an enzyme. Specific SOD activity was reported as unit’s min− 1 mg− 1 protein.

The activity of guaiacol peroxidase (GPOD) was assessed in a reaction mixture containing 10 mM H2O2, 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.05% guaiacol, and the enzyme. Specific GPOD activity was expressed as µmol min−1mg−1protein. Ascorbate peroxidase (APOD) activity was measured in a reaction mixture consisting of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1 mM EDTA, 1.0 mM H2O2, 0.25 mM ascorbic acid, and the enzyme. Specific APOD activity was reported as µmol of ascorbate oxidized min−1mg− 1 protein. Glutathione reductase (GR) activity was determined in a reaction mixture containing 3 mM DTNB (5,5-dithio-bis-2-nitrobenzoic acid), 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), 0.1 mM EDTA, 2 mM NADPH, and the enzyme. Specific GR activity was expressed as nmol min−1mg− 1 protein. Protein content was measured using the Bradford method20 with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. Glutathione S-transferase (GST) activity was assayed using 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene as the substrate.

Expression analysis

To assess the effect of drought stress on different organs of maize, the whole-genome peptide sequence of the drought-resistant variety (B73_RefGen_v3) from MaizeGDB (https://maizegdb.org/) database was used. Peptide sequences of these oxidants and antioxidants were retrieved from NCBI database as reference sequence from Arabidopsis Thaliana. These sequences were further used for BLAST-P program in TBTool. To confirm the orthologs, reciprocal BLAST-P analysis was performed against the Arabidopsis thaliana proteome (E-value ≤ 1e-5), and only hits showing reciprocal best matches were retained. Protein domain conservation was further validated using Pfam by Motif finder (https://www.genome.jp/tools/motif/). For the assessment of effects of drought stress on different organs, already published RNA-Seq data with Accession Number GSE132113 from NCBI-Geo (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?%20acc=GSE132113) was used. Drought-responsive gene expressions in maize organs were evaluated using RNA-Seq data from NCBI-GEO. The expression dataset was further analyzed by using TBtools for identified genes. Heatmap feature in TBTools was used to visualize expression, and genes with a log2 fold-change of ± 0.6 were deemed to be significantly regulated15.

Results

The results of Mean Squares (MS) from Analysis Of Variance (ANOVA) are shown in Table 2, 3 and 4, 5 reveal highly significant (P < 0.01) genotypic differences for all seven seedling traits evaluated in the maize line × tester analysis during both normal and under stress conditions: root length (RL), shoot length (SL), root-to-shoot length ratio (RSLR), seedling biomass (SB), leaf length (LL), leaf width (LW), and leaf area (LA). Under normal conditions the B-336 performed better than other genotypes. While under drought EV-347 performed better for most of the studied traits (Table 10, 11). Among crosses B-316 × B-336, B-316 × B-11, B-316 × EV-347 and F-96 × B-11 performed better than other crosses under normal conditions while B-316 × B-336, B-316 × EV-347, B-316 × B-11 and E-322 × B-336 performed better than other crosses under drought conditions (Table 12, 13).

The GCA effects, provided in Tables 6, 7, reveal the performance of various lines and testers which were used for hybrid development. Among testers, T1 (EV-347) likewise tends to give a favorable GCA effect for root length under normal and drought conditions but T2 (B-11) has greatest negative value under both conditions. In contrast, line L1 (B-316) showed greatest value and L4 (F-96) showed the lowest GCA effect for root length under both normal and drought conditions. For shoot length, line L4 (F-96) and tester T3 (B-336) performed the strongest positive GCA effects under normal conditions but under drought conditions, line L4 (F-96) and Tester T2 (B-336) showed strong positive results. In contrast, under normal conditions, L3 (E-322) and T1 (EV-347) have strong negative GCA effect but under drought conditions, L2 (EV-340) and T1 (EV-347) have strong negative results for shoot length. Remarkably, no parental line or tester demonstrated a significant GCA effect for RSLR. For seedling biomass, all parental lines and testers demonstrated low GCA effects. The GCA effects among lines for leaf length, leaf width, and leaf area were typically low under normal growing conditions, except for line L1 (B-316) and L2 (EV-340) and L2 (EV-340) which showed positive GCA effects for traits respectively but L2 (EV-340), L3 (E-322) and L4 (F-96) showed negative GCA effects for traits respectively. Among testers, T3 (B-336), T2 (B-11) and T2 (B-11) showed positive GCA effect for normal conditions and T1 (EV-347), T3 (B-336), T3 (B-336) showed negative GCA effect for normal conditions for above mentioned traits respectively. The GCA effects among lines observed for leaf length, leaf and width, leaf area under drought conditions. Among lines, L3 (E-322), L2 (EV-340) and L4 (F-96) showed the greatest GCA effect but L1 (B-316), L3 (E-322) and L3 (E-322) showed negative GCA effect for traits, respectively. In contrast, among the testers, T3 (B-336), T2 (B-11) and T2 (B-11) showed positive GCA effect and T1 (EV-347) has lowest GCA effect.

The SCA effects, provided in Tables 8 and 9, reveal the performance of various hybrid combinations relative to what would be expected based on the GCA of their parents. Several crossings demonstrated significant positive or negative SCA impacts for certain characteristics. The cross L2 × T2 (EV-340 × B-11) showed the largest positive SCA effect for root length, showing a strong genetic interaction between these parental lines that resulted in improved root growth and L3 × T3 (E-322 × B-336) showed the greatest value of SCA for shoot length under normal agronomic conditions. While under drought conditions, the cross L3 × T3 (E-322 × B-336) showed the greatest SCA value for root length and shoot length. It means that the SCA of cross L3 × T3 (E-322 × B-336) has more stable, greater, and stronger gene action expressing at high level during both conditions for shoot length in normal and root & shoot length under drought conditions. Similarly, other crossings revealed significant SCA effects for other traits, such as L3 × T1 (E-322 × EV-347) for seedling biomass, L4 × T2 (F-96 × B-11) for leaf length, and L2 × T3 (EV-340 × B-336) for leaf area under normal agronomic field conditions. Under 50% drought condition, the crosses L4 × T3 (F-96 × B-336) for seedling biomass, L3 × T1 (E-322 × EV-347) for leaf length, L3 × T1 (E-322 × EV-347) for leaf width and again L3 × T1 (E-322 × EV-347) for leaf area, showed highly significant SCA results. Conversely, other crosses revealed strong negative SCA effects, showing antagonistic interactions that resulted in lowering trait expression. In the cross L2 × T3 (EV-340 × B-336) and L4 × T3 (F-96 × B-336) showed a negative SCA effect for root length and shoot length respectively, while L4 × T1 (F-96 × EV-347) had a negative SCA effect for leaf area under both conditions, normal and drought (Tables 10, 11, 12, 13).

Additive gene action was more prominent for root-to-shoot length ratio (RSLR) and seedling biomass (SB), with values of 0.39 and 0.16, respectively but under drought stress, root length showed results of 1.37 and Shoot length of 0.070. In contrast, results of dominant gene action in root length (1.21), shoot length (0.43), leaf length (0.20), leaf width (0.16), and shocking results in leaf area (13.79) under normal condition were found. Root length (1.67), shoot length (0.564), leaf length (0.33), leaf width (0.15), and shocking results in leaf area (21.03).

The calculated genetic variance values show the level of genetic variety underlying each attribute. Under normal growing conditions, Leaf area (LA) displayed the largest genetic variation (208.88), suggesting a wide range of genetic potential for this characteristic within the examined population. Root length (RL) and shoot length (SL) also demonstrated high genetic variation (1.18 and 3.62, respectively). In contrast, RSLR and SB revealed negligible genetic variation, indicating limited genetic diversity for these characteristics. Under drought stress, plants behave differently, the largest genetic variation (LA-208.88) in normal conditions showed lesser value results 138.48. Root to Shoot Length Ratio (RSLR) and Seedling Biomass (SB) showed negligible value of genetic variation. Narrow-sense heritability (h²) estimates differed widely among the characteristics. RSLR displayed the greatest heritability (0.42). However, most other characteristics exhibited low heritability values (ranging from 0.00 for LA to 0.36 for SB). Under drought conditions, genes for root development were more expressive during stress conditions and showed 0.74 which expressed greater passage of traits from parents to offspring. RSLR (0.42) and LW (0.16) show that the effect is not only due to genes.

The degree of dominance values further revealed the nature of gene activity for each trait. All traits showed overdominance, with values higher than one. The largest degree of dominance was seen for LW (4.82), showing that the heterozygous condition is particularly significant for this characteristic as shown in Table 14, 15. Gene expression

The RNA-Seq dataset from maize reference line B73 was used as a comparative transcriptomic framework rather than a direct genotype-specific expression profile. The goal was to identify conserved drought-responsive gene families (APX, CAT, SOD, GR, GST, LOX) whose regulation patterns provide functional context for the observed biochemical and morphological responses of our hybrid set. This approach aligns with previous comparative transcriptomic studies using B73 as a genomic proxy for functional inference across maize germplasm. The expression analysis of antioxidant and oxidative stress-related genes in maize under drought conditions across ears, kernels, and leaves revealed distinct tissue-specific patterns. RNA-Seq reads were normalized and analyzed in TBtools (v1.120) using the Table to row manipulator to find out hit results and the further proceeded in Microsoft Excel with a significance threshold of log2fold change (≥ 0.6 and FDR < 0.05) for differential expression. For ascorbate peroxidase 1 (APX1), expression in maize ears was regulated by 29 genes, with 12 falling within the threshold of ± 0.6 log2fold change, as shown in Fig. 1. Most genes exhibited slight changes, with GRMZM2G405064 showing the highest upregulation (+ 0.60), while GRMZM2G316256 was slightly downregulated (-0.37). In kernels, expression ranged from noticeable downregulation (GRMZM2G054300: -0.63) to moderate upregulation (GRMZM2G047456: +0.56). In leaves, APX1-regulating genes exhibited broader expression changes, with significant downregulation observed in GRMZM2G103342 (-0.69) and strong upregulation in GRMZM2G014397 (+ 0.56) and GRMZM2G316256 (+ 0.51), indicating a robust adaptive response to drought. Detailed annotation information, gene IDs, log 2-fold-change values, and T-test P-value corrections for all differentially expressed genes are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Discussion

Drought conditions directly affect plant growth stages and developmental stages including seedling establishment, germination, vegetative growth and reproductive phases. In this study, the statistical analysis-Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) revealed highly significant results of these seven traits. Great genetic diversity was observed in the hybrid offspring and parental lines, as evidenced by large genotypic variations across all traits. .

The study of gene action indicated variable contributions of additive and dominant effects across the seedling traits studied. Additive gene action is shown in Tables 14, 15 indicating the cumulative impacts of individual genes under normal and drought conditions. Due to its high narrow-sense heritability, RSLR is a potential target for selection since it appears to be a variable heavily impacted by additive genetic factors. For traits with a high heritability and additive gene action, such as root-to-shoot (RSLR) length ratio and seedling biomass (SB), direct phenotypic selection can be efficient24,25. However, the poor heritability of most other characteristics shows a strong environmental effect, compelling accurate selection procedures that account for genotype-by-environment interactions. In this study, additive gene action was found more prominent for root-to-shoot length ratio (RSLR) and seedling biomass (SB)24. On the other hand, dominant gene action, where one allele masks the effect of another, was more important in determining root length, shoot length, leaf length, leaf width, and had a particularly striking impact on leaf area under normal growth conditions. However, when the same plants were exposed to drought stress, the expression of these traits changed significantly (Ali et al., 2024). In contrast, most other traits had low heritability values, suggesting that environmental or other non-genetic factors have a stronger influence on their expression26. The insights gained into gene action, genetic variance, and heritability pave the way for the integration of diverse approaches, including conventional breeding, marker-assisted selection, and physiological understanding, to develop superior maize hybrids. The results of this study show that drought stress induces a significant increase in oxidative stress in maize, as evidenced by the increased levels of H2O2 and lipid peroxidation. This is coherent with previous research validating that drought conditions disrupt cellular homeostasis in plants, leading to increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)27. ROS can damage cellular components such as lipids, proteins, and DNA, ultimately damaging plant growth and development28.

However, maize plants exhibit a strong antioxidant defense system to counteract drought-induced oxidative stress. The observed increase in the activities of SOD, catalase, guaiacol peroxidase, ascorbate peroxidase, glutathione reductase, and glutathione-S-transferase suggests a coordinated response to scavenge excess ROS and mitigate oxidative damage. Antioxidant enzyme activities were increased to reduce ROS caused by drought29. The upregulation of these antioxidant enzymes under drought stress indicates that maize plants actively adjust their metabolism to cope with adverse conditions. This adaptive response likely contributes to the drought tolerance of maize by minimizing oxidative damage and maintaining cellular function. While our phenotypic and biochemical analyses were performed on specific breeding lines, reference-based expression data from B73 provided complementary insight into the conserved oxidative-stress signaling pathways underlying drought tolerance.

In the end, Integrating the quantitative genetic findings with the conserved molecular framework shows a distinct pattern of stress adaptation. The parental lines B-316 and EV-340 exhibited the highest General Combining Ability (GCA) for root length and biomass under drought stress, appear to leverage the specific antioxidant pathways identified in the reference transcriptomic profile. Specifically, the superior performance of hybrids such as B-316 × EV-347 and B-316 × B-336 aligns with the significant upregulation of the ZmSOD1 (Superoxide Dismutase 1) and ZmCAT3 (Catalase 3) gene families observed in the reference data under similar stress conditions. This suggests that the heterosis observed in these hybrids is physiologically supported by an enhanced capacity for ROS scavenging, a mechanism driven by the APX–SOD–CAT module. Therefore, while direct gene expression varies by genotype, the phenotypic resilience of these elite hybrids is consistent with the effective activation of these conserved oxidative stress-response pathways.

Conclusion

This comprehensive study highlights the complex relationship between maize genetics, environment, and stress responses. Genotypes like E-322, showing high heritability for crucial traits like RSLR and stable performance under the drought stress, offer valuable resources for breeding programs. Furthermore, significant SCA effects reveal the possibility of controlling heterosis for trait enhancement. However, the dominance of environmental influence on some traits requires multi-environment trials for finding stable genotypes. In addition, the gene expression analysis provided molecular validation of physiological responses, confirming the regulatory activity of key oxidant and antioxidant genes under drought stress across different plant organs. A useful framework for choosing genotypes that continue to function in water-limited environments is provided by the combination of physiological and morphological traits with gene expression patterns. The insights into gene action, genetic variance, and heritability emphasize the complexity of trait inheritance and focus the integrated approaches, combining conventional breeding, molecular techniques, and physiological understanding, to develop maize hybrids that are both high-yielding and resilient to environmental challenges, ensuring food security in an era of climate change.
