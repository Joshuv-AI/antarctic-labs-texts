Extraction of Sulfachloropyridazine from Food Samples Using Cellulose: Application of Central Composite Design Approach
Authors: Mbuyelo Shikweni, Nikita Tawanda Tavengwa, Rabelani Mudzielwana, Dakalo Ndou, Tebogo Mokgehle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSulfonamide antibiotics are among the most widely used veterinary drugs in livestock and often end up contaminating milk, which is subsequently consumed by humans. To mitigate this health hazard, cellulose and its functionalized derivatives were investigated for their ability to extract sulfonamides from milk and honey. The approach used a biodegradable sorbent (cellulose modified with oxalic and adipic acids) and a green solvent system (ATPE, which reduces the usage of harmful organic solvents) to improve sorbent‐analyte interactions and extraction efficiency. In comparison to traditional extraction procedures, the suggested method provided a simpler, more cost‐effective, and environmentally friendly method for analysing sulfonamides in complex food matrices. To improve adsorption, cellulose was functionalized with oxalic and adipic acids. The resulting sorbents were then characterised using Fourier‐transform infrared, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller and scanning electron microscope techniques. Batch adsorption experiments using a central composite design approach were conducted using sulfachloropyridazine to evaluate the adsorption efficiency of cellulose and its functionalized derivatives at varying temperature, time and mass of sorbent. The optimal conditions for the adsorption of sulfachloropyridazine using cellulose, adipic acid functionalized cellulose (cell@AA) and oxalic acid functionalized cellulose (cell@OA) yielded 53%, 57% and 52% extraction, respectively. The optimal conditions for the adsorption of sulfachloropyridazine for temperature, time and mass were 75°C, 180 min and 50 mg for cellulose, 60°C, 180 min and 5 mg for cell@OA, and 75°C, 30 min and 5 mg for cell@AA, respectively. Application studies revealed that the extraction percentages of sulfachloropyridazine from milk using cellulose, cell@OA and cell@AA were 57%, 86% and 68%, respectively. Therefore, the functionalization of cellulose with adipic and oxalic acids proved to influence the adsorption of Sulfachloropyridazine when compared to cellulose.

Introduction

Recently, with the rapid growth of the global population and the proliferating demand for conventional meat (beef, pork and poultry), the consumption of veterinary and human antibiotics is continuously increasing worldwide [1, 2]. Following consumption, humans and animals usually cannot completely degrade antibiotics, which causes a certain amount of antibiotics to be excreted through urine or faeces. Residual antibiotics in sewage treatment plants and aquafarms are primary sources of antibiotics in the aquatic environment [3].

As much as these antibiotics have been helpful, these compounds also exhibit toxic effects to the environment as a result of their strong environmental persistence. One class of antibiotics that is notorious for this property is sulfonamides. Sulfonamides possess poor biodegradability and would hence pass through human/animal digestive systems and be excreted via urine and faeces, which subsequently find their way into the aquatic environment. Once sulfonamides are in the water environment, they yield antibiotic‐resistant bacteria carrying antibiotic resistance genes, which are responsible for approximately 700,000 deaths per year [4, 5]. If no thorough action is taken to reduce this carnage, the death toll may reach 10 million per year by 2050 [6].

To remediate this problem, extraction methods have been studied to limit the prevalence of antibiotic residues in the environment and food samples. Extraction methods such as ultrasonic‐assisted extraction [7], pressurised solvent extraction [8], and solid phase extraction [9] have been studied. However, these techniques are time‐consuming and require the use of a lot of toxic organic solvents and lack selectivity. Another promising form of remediation for dealing with antibiotics that researchers are investigating is adsorption.

A greater effort is being focused on applying biodegradable materials for effective preconcentration of sulfonamides. Hence, this work, for the first time, investigated the application of a biodegradable material (cellulose) for the preconcentration of sulfonamides. This further involved comparing adipic acid functionalized cellulose (cell@AA) and oxalic acid functionalized cellulose (cell@OA) with cellulose for the adsorption of sulfachloropyridazine in batch adsorption studies. The motivation for functionalization was to improve the extraction of sulfachloropyridazine, which would not only be dependent on the chemical interactions with cellulose but also on the added organic moieties contained in adipic and oxalic acid. Herein, this work hypothesises that organic acid functionalized cellulose will improve the adsorption of sulfachloropyridazine compared to cellulose alone. Optimisation of the extraction of the bioactive compounds based on a chemometric study design is becoming popular [10, 11]. Through the aid of chemometric computational method design (central composite design [CCD]), the optimal extraction conditions of sulfonamides by cellulose and its functionalized form were studied. These parameters were then applied to milk and honey samples. To overcome the limitations of standard extraction procedures, this study will investigate a simple two‐phase extraction system that uses green solvents, such as water and ethanol. Furthermore, little is known about the use of biodegradable sorbents for the removal of antibiotics from food in an ATPE system. This study will evaluate cellulose and its oxalic and adipic acid functionalized derivatives for the removal of sulfachloropyridazine from honey and milk in an ATPE system. It is hoped that these functionalized sorbents will improve the adsorption of sulfachloropyridazine. CCD will be applied to allow for a systematic optimisation of essential process parameters, where the robustness of the ATPE method will be assessed using parameters such as extraction time, temperature, and sorbent mass, and repeated several times to ascertain reproducibility under different conditions.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Microcrystalline cellulose (catalogue code 9004‐34‐6; molecular formula (C6H10O5) n ; molecular weight 40,000 g/mol; purity ≥ 97%; cellulose content ≥ 97%, moisture < 7%, ash < 0.05% and heavy metals < 10 ppm) was purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Oxalic acid, adipic acid, sodium chloride, and sodium carbonate were obtained from Rochelle Chemicals (Johannesburg, South Africa). Sulfachloropyridazine was sourced from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). High‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)‐grade ethanol, methanol and deionised water were obtained from Van Waters and Rogers (Midrand, South Africa). Deionised water was further purified using a Milli‐Q system (Millipore, Guyancourt, France).

Sample Preparation

To prepare the cellulose composites, 1 g (0.006 mol) of cellulose was combined with either 2 g (0.014 mol) of adipic acid or 1.2 g (0.014 mol) of oxalic acid, maintaining a 1:2 molar ratio of cellulose to acid. The mixture was ground into a fine, homogeneous powder using a mortar and pestle, and 50 mL of distilled water was added. The suspension was transferred to an autoclave and subjected to high‐pressure and high‐temperature treatment to promote chemical reactions. Afterwards, the mixture was filtered to separate the solid residues from the liquid, and the solids were air‐dried at room temperature. The dried material was then characterised using Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR), scanning electron microscope (SEM) and Brunauer–Emmett–Teller analyses to evaluate its structural, chemical, and morphological properties.

Sample Preparation and Batch Adsorption

A standard solution of sulfachloropyridazine (5 mg in 100 mL methanol; 0.05 g L− 1) was prepared and stirred until completely dissolved. Aliquots of 800 µL were added to different masses (5.0, 27.5 and 50.0 mg) of the adsorbents (cell, cell@AA and cell@OA). The mixtures were heated on a regulated hotplate for 30, 60 or 105 min at temperatures of 25, 50 or 75°C to perform controlled adsorption experiments. After heating, the adsorbent was removed by filtration, and the filtrates were collected in vials for analysis. The concentration of sulfachloropyridazine in each filtrate was determined using HPLC with a photodiode array (PDA) detector.

Characterization

Equipment

FTIR spectra were recorded on a PerkinElmer Spectrum 65 FTIR spectrometer with an ATR accessory. The surface morphology of cellulose and its functionalized derivatives was examined using a JEOL JSM‐7600F field emission SEM. Specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution were measured using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 surface area and porosity analyser.

High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography

The chromatographic measurements were conducted using a Shimadzu LC‐2030C 3D Plus HPLC system (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), designed to withstand a maximum pressure of 63 MPa. The system was equipped with a PDA detector to ensure accurate and sensitive detection across a broad wavelength range. The Shim‐pack GIST C18 reversed‐phase column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm particle size) was used for separation and kept at 40°C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in HPLC‐grade water (solvent A) and methanol (solvent B), and the separation was performed using a gradient elution technique. Initially, the concentration of solvent B was kept at 5% from 0 to 2 min. It was then increased linearly to 50% from 2 to 5 min and held at 50% until 6 min. The content of solvent B was increased to 95% between 6 and 8.5 min and remained steady until 9.5 min. Finally, from 9.5 to 10 min, the gradient was reset to the initial condition of 5% B for column re‐equilibration. To detect the target sulfonamides, the flow rate was set to 1.0 mL min−1, the injection volume was 20 µL, and the detection wavelength was 254 nm, which corresponded to their maximum absorbance.

Results and Discussion

Functional Group Analysis

The pure cellulose spectrum (Figure 1a) shows distinct peaks, including a broad O‐H stretching vibration around 3300 cm− 1, which represents the hydroxyl groups responsible for its hydrogen‐bonded crystalline structure. The peaks around 2900 cm− 1 corresponded to C‐H stretching vibrations, whereas the region 1000–1200 cm− 1 comprised C‐O‐C and C‐H bending vibrations, highlighting the unique molecular structure of cellulose. The oxalic acid spectrum (Figure 1b) has a prominent peak about 1700 cm− 1 corresponding to C = O stretching in the carboxylic acid groups. Broad peaks at 1300–1400 cm− 1 were attributed to C‐O‐H bending vibrations, which indicated hydrogen bonding interactions. In Figure 1c of pure adipic acid, a sharp peak at 1700 cm− 1, indicating the C = O stretching vibration of the carboxylic group (‐COOH), was observed, as well as a broad peak at 3000 cm− 1, indicating O‐H stretching associated with the carboxylic acid group. Other peaks near 1200 cm− 1 (C‐O stretching) confirmed the adipic acid's molecular structure. The 60 min autoclave treatment of oxalic acid and cellulose (Figure 1d) produced considerably more significant spectrum alterations. The O‐H stretching peak decreased significantly, indicating increased hydrogen bond breakdown and a more comprehensive chemical interaction between oxalic acid and cellulose. Enhanced peaks in the 1700 cm− 1 region indicated the formation of ester bonds, whereas large shifts in the fingerprint region indicated cellulose backbone hydrolysis and degradation [12]. These alterations imply that extended autoclave treatment resulted in maximum structural modification, making cellulose easier for chemicals to reach and react with. Figure 1e depicts cellulose‐adipic acid derivatives that had been autoclaved for 60 min, respectively. These spectra revealed considerable changes to both the hydroxyl (‐OH) and carbonyl (C = O) functional groups, implying the formation of novel chemical interactions like hydrogen bonding, esterification, or condensation reactions [13]. The hydroxyl peak at 3300 cm− 1 broadened and decreased in intensity, showing esterification between cellulose's hydroxyl groups and adipic acid's carboxyl groups at 1700 cm−1.

Surface Morphological Analysis

The SEM analysis of cellulose before and after 60 min functionalization with oxalic acid and adipic acid, providing information about its morphological characteristics under specific imaging conditions, is depicted in Figure 2. The images were taken at a 15 kV accelerating voltage. Furthermore, the particle size distribution was predominantly within the micrometre range (50–100 µm), supporting its classification as microcrystalline. Figure 2a shows a broader dispersion of cellulose particles, including fragmented and irregularly shaped fragments. The imaging at 450x magnification revealed a diverse particle size distribution with sharp edges and variable diameters, confirming the microcrystalline nature of cellulose. Figure 2b shows cell@OA particles in samples that were autoclaved for 60 min, which appear larger and more aggregated, with sharper and more defined edges. These particles' surfaces were relatively smooth and compact, with few apparent pores, because of extended heat exposure. Heat from autoclaving resulted in smooth, compact surfaces with fewer pores [14]. The particle size in these samples ranged between 80 and 100 µm. Figure 2c presents cell@AA particles with irregular shapes and coarse, fragmented structures. After 60 min of treatment, the particles were more compact, with sharp edges and noticeable fragmentation, indicating efficient functionalization [15]. The particle sizes varied from 80 µm to 100 µm based on the autoclaving period. Longer treatment intervals resulted in more homogeneous and well‐defined structures, indicating a higher level of functionalization [16].

Surface Area Analysis

Cellulose was observed to have the highest surface area (6.21 m2 g−1) and pore volume (0.0137 cm3 g−1), suggesting a porous structure with accessible surface locations (Table 1). Following functionalization, both cell@OA and cell@AA showed a decrease in surface area and pore volume, which can be attributed to cellulose fibre chemical alteration [17]. The introduction of organic acid groups most likely results in partial pore blockage or surface covering by acid molecules, limiting available surface sites. Cell@OA exhibited a considerable decrease in surface area and pore volume (5.55 m2 g−1 and 0.0121 cm3 g−1, respectively (Table 1), probably because of the smaller size and increased reactivity of oxalic acid, resulting in denser cross‐linking or compact surface layers [18]. Cell@AA functionalized with adipic acid exhibits a lesser reduction (5.85 m2 g−1 and 0.0125 cm3 g−1, respectively), possibly because of the longer carbon chain of adipic acid, resulting in less dense packing and greater pore structure [19]. These modifications indicated that functionalization influenced cellulose's surface shape and porosity, which can influence sorption capacity and selectivity by introducing active functional groups while preserving adequate surface area for adsorption [16].

Optimisation of Sulfachloropyridazine Extraction

The Pareto chart for Sulfachloropyridazine extraction showed the p‐values and f‐values of each factor, indicating the relative significance of each factor in the extraction process. The p‐value is a probability value used to estimate the statistical significance of an observed result. The F‐value is an analysis of variance ratio that compares variation between groups to variance within groups [20]. Figure 3 shows the p‐values for each term in the model. In this analysis, none of the factors is statistically significant because their p‐values are all greater than 0.05 (p > 0.05). Although none of the factors was statistically significant (p > 0.05), sorbent mass, temperature, and extraction time were investigated further because their quadratic terms, particularly temperature (B2) and sorbent mass (C2), had the greatest f‐values in the model.

Pareto chart for sulfachloropyridazine extraction on cellulose.

Figure 4 illustrates the response surface methodology (RSM) for sulfachloropyridazine extraction using cellulose, which was evaluated using three parameters: extraction time (A), temperature (B), and cellulose sorbent mass (C). The model equation highlighted each variable's independent and interacting influence on the extraction process. Positive coefficients indicated that increasing the relevant variable improved the extraction, whilst negative coefficients suggested a suppressive effect. The relationship between these parameters was represented by the quadratic model Equation (1): (1) %Sulfachloropyridazineextraction=+31.86+1.60xA+0.80xB−3.50xC+1.75xAB+2.75xAC−0.25xBC+1.41xA2+7.59xB2+7.09xC2 where A represents the extraction time, B the temperature, and C the mass of the cellulose. The linear term (C) also showed that sorbent mass had a favourable impact on the process up to a certain point where the sorbent became saturated and could no longer adsorb more effectively. The relationship between extraction time and temperature (Figure 4a) showed that the extraction efficiency does not always increase with increasing temperature. Initially, increasing the temperature reduced extraction efficiency, but from 55°C, it increased. Furthermore, shorter extraction times at higher temperatures tend to lower efficiency, exacerbating the effects of thermal deterioration. This observation is consistent with what was reported by Darmenbayeva et al. [21] on maintaining cellulose sorbent integrity during adsorption. The relationship between time and cellulose mass (Figure 4b) indicates that higher sorbent masses generally correlated with increased extraction efficiency, emphasising the importance of having enough active sites for sulfachloropyridazine adsorption. However, when time was extended beyond 120–150 min, the marginal gains in extraction efficiency decreased, implying that equilibrium had been reached. This finding is consistent with previous studies that emphasised the importance of sorbent quantity in achieving effective contaminant removal [22]. The response surface plot in Figure 4c indicated that extraction efficiency was highest between 65 and 75°C, when the cellulose mass was approximately 50 mg. At temperatures ranging from 45 to 55°C, adsorption was weak, resulting in limited efficiency. These patterns were consistent with literature highlighting the thermal sensitivity of cellulose‐based sorbents during extraction processes [21].

From the Pareto chart in Figure 5, factors with p < 0.05 were considered significant, but those with p > 0.05 were not. Mass of sorbent (C) was found to have a considerable impact on extraction efficiency, while other variables with p > 0.05 had no impact. The mass of cell@OA (C) had the greatest impact, followed by temperature (B), as illustrated in Figure 5. The quadratic term (C2) revealed a considerable non‐linear effect, underscoring the critical significance of sorbent mass in optimising the process. This suggested that the sorbent mass had a non‐proportional effect on reaction efficiency, most likely due to saturation effects during the adsorption process. Furthermore, the interaction terms AB and BC demonstrated parameter synergy, indicating that temperature and time, as well as temperature and sorbent mass, controlled the extraction process collectively rather than independently.

Chaves et al. [23] found that extending extraction times beyond the optimal range lowered yields in acid‐mediated extraction systems. The presence of oxalic acid improved the analyte's efficiency for the sorbent material, which correlates with the findings by Misiewicz et al. The parameters' correlations were represented using response surface plots, as shown in Figure 6. The time versus temperature plot (Figure 6a) showed that increasing the temperature at shorter extraction times improved the extraction efficiency of sulfachloropyridazine. However, longer extraction times resulted in low extraction efficiency, probably due to compound degradation. Figure 6b revealed that at low extraction time, the extraction efficiency was low. As the extraction time increased from 30 to 180 min and the mass increased from 5 to 50 mg, the extraction % increased. However, the plot had a concave curve, indicating a slower rate of improvement at higher values. The best extraction efficiency was recorded at the maximum extraction time (180 min) and the largest mass of cell@OA (50 mg). Figure 6c demonstrated that the extraction efficiency of sulfachloropyridazine increased with both temperature and cell@OA mass. At higher temperatures, notably around 75°C, molecular diffusion increased due to increased molecular motion, lowering solvent viscosity and improving mass transfer. A higher amount of cell@OA, precisely 50 mg, supplied more adsorption sites, enabling more sulfachloropyridazine molecules to bind and be extracted successfully. Sulfachloropyridazine's solubility also increased at higher temperatures, allowing it to be released into the extraction medium more easily. In contrast, at lower temperatures (<30°C) and a cell@OA mass of 50 mg, extraction efficiency was about 30%, which resulted in fewer available adsorption sites, possibly due to the aggregation of the cell@OA particles.

Application Studies for Batch Adsorption

In the chromatograms (Figure 9), peaks observed at 7.5 min in the milk sample and 8.2 min in the honey sample are likely attributable to sulfachloropyridazine. The slight shifts in retention times across milk and honey matrices can be attributed to several factors, including variations in matrix composition, mobile phase conditions and methodological differences [26]. The composition of complex sample matrices, such as milk and honey, has a considerable impact on chromatographic behaviour during HPLC analysis. Furthermore, the presence of proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates in milk and honey makes sample‐column interactions more complex, since they can bind to the stationary phase and impact sulfonamide retention [27].

Chromatogram of sulfacholoropyridazine extracted from milk using (a) cellulose, (b) cell@OA and (c) cell@AA.

The chromatographic analysis of sulfonamides in milk using cellulose revealed a shift in the retention time of sulfachloropyridazine from 8.5 to 7.5 min, as shown in Figure 9a. The observed shifts could most likely be due to the milk matrix effects. Milk contains a variety of fats, proteins, sugars, minerals, and emulsifiers. These interact with analytes and may influence the partitioning and interactions with the stationary phase of the column, resulting in shifts in the retention times. Sulfachloropyridazine extraction was observed for milk, while no detectable amount was recorded for honey.

The chromatographic analysis (Figure 9b) of milk with cell@OA sorbent indicated that sulfachloropyridazine eluted earlier than expected. The addition of carboxyl (‐COOH) groups via oxalic acid functionalization greatly increased cellulose polarity [28], allowing for improved interactions with sulfachloropyridazine. In the study by Ismail et al. [29], milk proteins, particularly casein, and lipids competed with sulfonamides for accessible binding sites on the sorbent, potentially lowering the quantity of analyte adsorbed. Furthermore, protein‐sulfonamide interactions in the milk solution may have generated complexes, reducing the free sulfonamide concentration available for binding to cellulose. Generally, cell@OA demonstrated higher adsorption effectiveness for sulfachloropyridazine compared to the other sorbents studied.

The chromatogram (Figure 9c) shows the extraction efficiency of cell@AA as a sorbent for extracting sulfachloropyridazine from milk. Adipic acid, when grafted onto cellulose, introduces carboxylic groups capable of dipole‐dipole interactions with the amine and sulfonyl groups of sulfonamides. Furthermore, the matrix impact of milk, which contains proteins, lipids, and sugars, could have contributed to minor differences in analyte retention, as the sulfachloropyridazine may have had different affinities during extraction compared to honey or wastewater solutions.

The chromatogram showed a larger % extraction of sulfachloropyridazine from milk than from honey, implying that the functionalized cellulose sorbent was more effective in the milk matrix. This could be because the adipic acid moieties have improved selectivity for sulfachloropyridazine, possibly by creating stronger hydrogen bonds or engaging in hydrophobic interactions [30].

Table 2 shows the percentage extraction effectiveness of the cell as a sorbent for removing sulfachloropyridazine from honey and milk samples. The studies were carried out under identical conditions: a time of 180 min, a temperature of 25°C, and a sorbent mass of 5 mg. The overall extraction efficiency in milk was 57% for sulfachloropyridazine. In the honey sample, sulfachloropyridazine was not detected. Table 2 also shows the extraction efficiency of sulfachloropyridazine from honey and milk with a cell@OA sorbent under optimum conditions (30 min, 25°C and 50 mg sorbent mass). In milk, sulfachloropyridazine had the highest extraction efficiency (86%). The findings indicated that the cell@OA sorbent was more efficient in extracting sulfachloropyridazine from milk than honey (as compared to raw cellulose). The percentage extraction of sulfachloropyridazine with cell@AA as a sorbent under regulated conditions was also investigated (Table 2). The extractions were performed at 25°C for 30 min with a sorbent mass of 5 mg. In milk, sulfachloropyridazine extraction efficiency significantly improved to 68%. Generally, the results obtained in this study were better than those of prior studies evaluating the extraction of sulfonamides from milk. For instance, Koesukwiwat et al. evaluated a polymer‐based Oasis HLB solid‐phase extraction cartridge for the solid‐phase extraction of sulfonamides from milk. In the same study, poor recoveries of approximately 40% were reported for sulfamethoxypyridazine, an antibiotic similar to sulfachloropyridazine, due to its strong binding affinity to casein, a milk protein [31].

Conclusion

This study successfully established the potential of organic acid‐functionalized cellulose as an efficient and sustainable sorbent for extracting sulfachloropyridazine from honey and milk by batch adsorption. Functionalization with oxalic acid and adipic acid to the cellulose backbone increased its chemical reactivity and affinity for sulfachloropyridazine. The study confirmed the hypothesis that functionalized cellulose(cell@OA) was a better adsorbent for sulfachloropyridazine compared to cellulose. In batch optimisation studies, cell@OA was the most effective sorbent, extracting 57% of sulfachloropyridazine under optimum conditions (180 min, 60°C and 5 mg sorbent). The presence of carboxyl groups promoted hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with the sulfachloropyridazine functional groups, which accounted for improved performance. When applied to real samples, cell@OA revealed high extraction efficiency in milk but no quantifiable extraction in honey, most likely because of its high viscosity and complex matrix that impedes analyte diffusion. Furthermore, this environmentally friendly extraction technique could potentially be utilised on a commercial scale, potentially minimising the number of toxic compounds in food.
