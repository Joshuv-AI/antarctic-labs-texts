Living on the edge: Testing for compact population features at the edges of parameter space
Asad Hussain, Maximiliano Isi, Aaron Zimmerman

Abstract
Many astrophysical population studies involve parameters that exist on a bounded domain, such as the dimensionless spins of black holes or the eccentricities of planetary orbits, both of which are confined to [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . In such scenarios, we often wish to test for distributions clustered near a boundary, e.g., vanishing spin or orbital eccentricity. Conventional approaches—whether based on Monte Carlo, kernel density estimators, or machine-learning techniques—often suffer biases at the boundaries. These biases stem from sparse sampling near the edge, kernel-related smoothing, or artifacts introduced by domain transformations. We introduce a truncated Gaussian mixture model framework that substantially mitigates these issues, enabling accurate inference of narrow, edge-dominated population features. While our method has broad applications to many astronomical domains, we consider gravitational wave catalogs as a concrete example to demonstrate its power. In particular, we maintain agreement with published constraints on the fraction of zero-spin binary black hole systems in the GWTC-3 catalog—–results originally derived at much higher computational cost through dedicated reanalysis of individual events in the catalog. Our method can achieve similarly reliable results with a much lower computational cost. The method is publicly available in the open-source packages gravpop and TruncatedGaussianMixtures .

II
Background: GW inference

The problem of estimating the population of astrophysical parameters near a boundary has surfaced with high prominence in the context of spin measurements in
GW
astronomy.
Here we summarize the nature of the technical problem in this context.

Population inference of
BBHs
detected by the
LVK
begins with the posterior probability distributions inferred from the data for each detection.
These posteriors are typically represented by discrete sets of samples drawn through stochastic algorithms such as
Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

(
N. Metropolis et al. 1953
;
D. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
or nested sampling
(
J. Skilling 2006
)
.
To evaluate the degree to which a proposed astrophysical population model explains the data, one must compute the Bayesian evidence for each event under a prior corresponding to that model
(
E. Thrane & C. Talbot 2019
, see, e.g.,)
.

Rather than recomputing the single-event posteriors for each prior, the evidence is generally estimated by reweighting preexisting posterior samples: each sample is assigned a new weight proportional to the ratio between the proposed prior and the fiducial prior used in the original event-level analysis
(
E. Thrane & C. Talbot 2019
)
.
Additionally, it is standard in
GW
astronomy to account for the Malmquist selection bias using injection campaigns
(see, e.g.,
R. Essick 2023
, for more details)
.
This requires a similar reweighting step
(
W. M. Farr 2019
)
, this time to replace the fiducial distribution used to draw injections with the desired astrophysical one.
Both these calculations suffer from similar difficulties in dealing with sharp population features.

The challenges in assessing population features near domain boundaries begin with problems intrinsic to sharp, localized features in general.
It is well documented that the reweighting approach described above fails for sharp features
(
C. Talbot & E. Thrane 2022
;
C. Talbot & J. Golomb 2023
)
.
The reason for this is straightforward: assuming a broad posterior, it is unlikely that sufficient samples are found in the compact region where the feature lies to inform the estimate accurately
(
C. P. Robert & G. Casella 2004
)
.

There have been many proposals to handle sharp population features within the context of
GW
data analysis.
(see, e.g.,
D. Wysocki et al. 2020
;
B. D. Lackey & L. Wade 2015
;
M. F. Carney et al. 2018
;
V. Delfavero et al. 2022a
;
V. Delfavero et al. 2022b
;
T. A. Callister et al. 2022
;
C. Talbot & E. Thrane 2022
;
T. A. Callister et al. 2024
;
M. Mancarella & D. Gerosa 2025
, to name a few)

T. A. Callister et al. 2022
use one-dimensional Gaussian
kernel density estimates (KDEs)
to represent the posterior in the effective spin parameter
\chi_{\textrm{eff}}
, and use that to interpolate between samples and probe sharp features in this domain.
This is facilitated by properties of the Gaussian distribution, which can be leveraged to perform some of the computation analytically and behaves well even for narrow features.
Similarly
C. Talbot & E. Thrane 2022
and
T. A. Callister et al. 2024
respectively use
Gaussian mixture models (GMMs)
and neural networks to estimate densities from injection campaigns.
Unlike the
KDEs
with analytic integration, these approaches rely on generating samples from the new population prior, and then using the density estimates to reweight these samples.
For sharp population features this can be an efficient estimator of the evidence under the new prior.
In general, the goal of such density estimation techniques is to get an accurate representation of the distribution that makes it computationally efficient to estimate the evidence integral.

However, such density estimation techniques typically fail to capture sharp population features near the edges of a domain.
In fact, most approaches either ignore the existence of a boundary or rely on transforming bounded parameters to unbounded domains.
In both cases, the density estimates introduce inherent biases at the boundary, which can vary in severity.
A Gaussian
KDE
, for example, would have a bias at the boundary which is
\mathcal{O}(1)
in the bandwidth
h
, while having a bias of order
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
in the bulk
(
B.
Silverman 2018
)
.

An ideal density estimation method would fulfill two requirements. Firstly, it should not give biased estimates of the
probability density function (PDF)
at or near the edges. Secondly, the representation of the density should facilitate the evidence computation, making it fast, tractable, and accurate.
In this paper, we find that utilizing
TGMMs
fulfills the above criteria.

III
General Procedure

The main target of our framework is the accurate and fast estimation of integrals of the form

I(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=\int p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{W(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mathrm{d}^{n}\theta=\left\langle\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\Lambda)}{W(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\,,

(1)

given some set of parameter samples
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\sim p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and some corresponding set of weights given by
W_{i}=W(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
.
The target probability distribution is defined by population-level parameters
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.
The notation
\langle f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
represents the expectation of function
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
under the distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

These integrals arise when reweighting posterior samples or injection draws for the selection function.
Below, we show how the integrals appear, describe how to compute them with our method, and compare it to standard procedures used in
GW
astronomy.

III.1
Formal problem statement and notation

We analyze a catalog of
N
observed objects (detection “events” in the
GW
context), indexed by
e=1,\dots,N
.
Each object yields a data set
d_{e}
(e.g., a
GW
strain time series), collectively creating a data catalog
\mathcal{D}=\{d_{e}~\forall~e\in 1\dots N\}
.
Initial parameter estimation applied to each object produces samples drawn from the posterior

p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid d_{e})=\frac{\mathcal{L}(d_{e}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\emptyset)}{\mathcal{Z}(d_{e})}\\
,

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
parameterizes the properties of a single event. This posterior is produced by assuming a fiducial prior
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\emptyset)
called the
sampling prior
, and a model for the data generation process
\mathcal{L}(d_{e}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})
hereby called the
likelihood
; additionally,
\mathcal{Z}(d_{e})
is the
evidence
under the sampling prior, which acts as a normalization factor.

For example, in
GW

BBH
population analyses, one commonly considers the mass of the heavier black hole
m_{1}
, the ratio of the masses in the binary
q
, the cosine of the individual spin tilts
\theta_{i}
, the individual spin magnitudes
\chi_{i}
, and the redshift
z
at which the event took place,
2
2

2

While there are other parameters that are usually inferred, we usually analyze only a subset when modeling the population.

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{BBH}}=[m_{1},q,\cos\theta_{1},\cos\theta_{2},\chi_{1},\chi_{2},z]\,.

(3)

In this case,
\cos\theta_{i}
is bounded within
[-1,1]
and
\chi_{i}
is bounded within
[0,1]
.
The cases where
\chi_{i}\to 0
or
\cos\theta_{i}\to 1
are physically interesting and it is desirable to explore population features which have compact support at these boundaries.

After obtaining posterior samples for each object, we create a model for the population distribution of astrophysical objects
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
, parameterized by some hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.
The goal is to infer the posterior over the parameters of these distributions, given our detected catalog
\mathcal{D}
, written as
p(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mid\mathcal{D})
.
To do this, we set some prior over our hyperparameters,
\pi(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
.
Under suitable assumptions
(
R. Essick & M. Fishbach 2024
)
, the population posterior can then be written as,

p(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mid\mathcal{D})\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\,\xi(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{-N}\prod_{e}^{N}\mathcal{L}(d_{e}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\,,

(4)

with
\mathcal{L}(d_{e}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
and
\xi(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
as defined below.

The
\mathcal{L}(d_{e}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
factors represent the evidence for each event given by data
d_{e}
, under a prior defined by the chosen astrophysical population distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
instead of the sampling prior
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\emptyset)
.
This is typically estimated from the initial set of posterior samples as

\mathcal{L}(d_{e}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=\int p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid d_{e})}{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\emptyset)}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,.

(5)

We typically do not have direct access to
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid d_{e})
but instead have a discrete set of independent samples
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{e,j}\}
drawn from it, where
j
ranges from 1 to
N_{e}
, the number of posterior samples for event
e
.
The integral is then approximated via a Monte Carlo average, as

\mathcal{L}(d_{e}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\approx\frac{1}{N_{e}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{e}}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{e,j}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})}{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{e,j}\mid\emptyset)}\,.

(6)

The
\xi(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
factor is the population-averaged detection efficiency, which accounts for selection effects
(
I. Mandel et al. 2019
)
.
Since the detection process is imperfect, the detectability of different objects varies over parameter space.
The probability of detecting an object with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is denoted
P_{\text{det}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in(0,1)
.
The fraction of events in the population distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
that are expected to be detected is given by

\xi(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=\int p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\,P_{\rm det}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,.

(7)

We typically do not have direct access to
P_{\rm det}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
but must estimate it through injection campaigns.
This entails drawing samples from a fiducial population with broad support,
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\emptyset})
, and generating synthetic data for each sample.
These synthetic data are passed through the same detection pipelines used to construct the catalog
\{d_{e}\}
, with each injection labeled as detected or not detected.
The detection efficiency can then be recast as a Monte Carlo average.
Given
N_{\rm inj}
injected signals drawn from a reference distribution
p_{\rm inj}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, the detection efficiency is approximated by

\xi(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\approx\frac{1}{N_{\rm inj}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm found}}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})}{p_{\rm inj}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}\,,

(8)

where the sum is over
N_{\rm found}
injections that pass the detection criteria.

The fundamental assumption underlying Eqs. (
6
) and (
8
) is that the posterior samples or recovered injections cover the feature of interest sufficiently densely.
This assumption may be violated for narrow features in the population distribution, such as sharp peaks or features near parameter boundaries.

In summary, the main ingredients of a typical hierarchical inference procedure, Eqs. (
6
) and (
8
), take the form of Eq. (
1
).
This is the problem we aim to tackle for narrow population features close to the edge.

III.2
Common techniques for approximating inference integrals

Here we review three common strategies for estimating Eq. (
1
) starting from samples of
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
: a
Monte-Carlo (MC)
estimator, a regular
KDE
, and a
KDE
with reflective boundary conditions.
This is not an exhaustive list, even within the context of gravitational waves, but is meant to be illustrative.

Monte-Carlo:
The most common approach for approximating Eq. (
1
) is to use a
MC
estimator given by

\hat{I}^{MC}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})}{W(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}\,,

(9)

such as Eqs. (
6
) and (
8
).
This is the method of choice in
LVK
population analyses
(
R. Abbott et al. 2023c
)
.
The
MC
estimator can be shown to be asymptotically unbiased: given enough samples it will converge to the correct value of the integral. However, the estimator can have a very large variance for sharp features
(
C. P. Robert & G. Casella 2004
)
.

Kernel Density Estimation:
Density estimation techniques introduce some level of bias in order to tame the excessive variance of the estimator in the regime of sharp population features.
At minimum, this typically includes regularizing the integral by imposing smoothness on the
PDF
.
This class of estimators begins by constructing a smooth approximation to
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
using a kernel function.

A kernel density estimate (KDE) is built by centering a kernel function,
K_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
, at each sample
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
, and summing over all samples:

p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})\,.

(10)

Here,
K_{h}
is typically a normalized, symmetric function (such as a Gaussian) with a parameter
h
called the bandwidth, which controls the width of the kernel and thus the amount of smoothing. The choice of bandwidth is crucial: a small
h
leads to less smoothing and can capture sharp features but may introduce noise, while a large
h
smooths out noise but can wash out narrow features. Bandwidth can be set using rules of thumb or cross-validation techniques
(
A. W. Bowman 1984
;
B.
Silverman 2018
)
.

For example, consider targeting narrow features in the redshift
z
distribution of
BBH
s.
Let us break the parameter space as
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime},z]
where
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
are all the remaining parameters describing the event. Similar to
T. A. Callister et al. 2022
, we can represent
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
through a
KDE
—applying kernel smoothing only in the subspace of interest (the redshift in this example), and using point estimates (delta functions) in the remainder of parameter space.
The density estimate would be,

p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{i})\,\phi(z\mid z_{i},h)\,,

(11)

where
\phi(z\mid z_{i},h)
is a Gaussian kernel centered at
z_{i}
with bandwidth
h
.

Similarly, we may apply a population model such that

p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\prime})\,\phi(z\mid\mu_{z},\sigma_{z})\,,

(12)

setting the population model in the redshift sector to be a Gaussian with some mean
\mu_{z}
and width
\sigma_{z}
, while applying some arbitrary population model to the other parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
.
One could then plug Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
) into Eq. (
1
) and obtain an estimate of the integral as,

\displaystyle\hat{I}^{KDE}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})}{W(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\int\phi(z\mid z_{i},h)\phi(z\mid\mu_{z},\sigma_{z}){\rm d}z\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})}{W(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}F(z_{i},h,\mu_{z},\sigma_{z})\,,

(13)

where
F
is the result of the convolution of the two Gaussians, which has an analytic form that is quick to evaluate.
Since we compute
F
analytically, we can get good estimates of the integral even as the shape of the population model becomes sharp (i.e.,
\sigma_{z}\to 0
).
If the kernel and population model were not both Gaussian,
F
may not have an analytic form, and the benefit of this approach would be reduced.

The bias properties of the integral estimator
\hat{I}^{KDE}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
are only as good as that of the
KDE
estimate itself.
The bias of the density estimate using a Gaussian
KDE
built from
n
samples in
d
dimensions and bandwidth
h
is known to be low in the bulk of parameter space,
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})

(
B.
Silverman 2018
)
; this is especially true for low dimensions because
h\sim n^{-1/d}

Consequently, the
KDE
approach above is a good estimator of the targeted integral when we stay in the bulk.
However, at the boundary, the bias deviates from the
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
in the bulk and becomes
\mathcal{O}(1)
in the case of the standard Gaussian
KDE

(
B.
Silverman 2018
)
. This means that the value at the boundary is always incorrect regardless of the number of samples used.

Reflective
KDE
s:
There are ways to improve the bias of the
KDE
estimators at the boundary. For instance, a common strategy is to reflect the points about the boundary, and have them inform the estimate of the density inside the bounded region
(
E. F.
Schuster 1985
)
.
In the redshift example above, assuming a bounded
z
parameter
z\in[a,b]
, the reflective
KDE
would take the form

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\delta(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{i})\left[\phi(z\mid z_{i}\,,h)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\,\phi(z\mid 2a-z_{i}\,,h)+\phi(z\mid 2b-z_{i}\,,h)\right]\,.

(14)

This improves the bias of the
KDE
to
\mathcal{O}(h)
near the boundary while retaining the smaller
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
bias in the bulk.
The reflective
KDE
produces an expression for the estimator of the integral similar to Eq. (
III.2
),

\displaystyle\hat{I}^{RKDE}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})}{W(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}\big[F(z_{i},h,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{z})

\displaystyle+F(a-z_{i},h,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{z})+F(2b-z_{i},h,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{z})\big]\,.

(15)

However, if the derivative at the boundary is non-zero, as is generically the case, this may lead to critical biases
(
J. S. Marron & D. Ruppert 1994
)
.

III.3
Numerical Comparison of Methods

To explore the different estimators of Eq. (
1
) used in
GW
population analysis we analyze their bias and variance properties for a simple 1D toy population analysis.
This toy model demonstrates how the above
MC
and
KDE
methods break down when the population prior is sharply peaked at a boundary and what a robust estimator must accomplish instead.

We model the population of a single parameter
\chi
that is bounded to the unit interval,
\chi\in[0,1]
, as coming from a truncated normal distribution,

\chi\sim\mathcal{N}_{[0,1]}(\mu,\sigma)\,,

(16)

with mean
\mu
and standard deviation
\sigma
.
We also use this distribution to draw a true value for the parameter
\chi^{{\rm true}}_{e}
for each event, i.e.,
\chi^{{\rm true}}_{e}\sim\mathcal{N}_{[0,1]}(\mu,\sigma)
.

For each event, we assume we have noisy data from which we derive a likelihood that can be approximated as a Gaussian with a width
\sigma_{\text{noise}}
set by the noise level.
For the
e
th event, this results in a likelihood represented by a mean
\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e}
and width
\sigma_{\text{noise}}
, such that

\displaystyle\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\chi^{{\rm true}}_{e},\sigma_{\textrm{noise}})\,.

(17)

The dataset of detections constitute a catalog
\mathcal{D}=\{\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e}\ \forall\ e\in 1,\dots,N\}
and we assume no selection bias.

When assuming a uniform sampling prior
\pi(\chi)=1
, the posterior distribution for the
e
th event then takes the form

p(\chi\mid\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e})=\phi_{[0,1]}(\chi\mid\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e},\sigma_{\textrm{noise}})\,,

(18)

where
\phi_{[a,b]}(x\mid\mu,\sigma)
is the probability density function of the truncated normal distribution
\mathcal{N}_{[a,b]}(\mu,\sigma)
.

We would like to get a posterior distribution over the hyperparameters of the model,
\Lambda=\{\mu,\sigma\}
.
This can be written as

p(\mu,\sigma|\{\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e}\})=\pi(\mu,\sigma)\prod_{e}\mathcal{L}(\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e}|\mu,\sigma)\,,

(19)

where
\pi(\mu,\sigma)
is our prior over the hyper parameters
\mu
and
\sigma
, and the likelihood factors are given by

\mathcal{L}(\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e}|\mu,\sigma)=\int\phi_{[0,1]}(\chi|\mu,\sigma)\,\phi_{[0,1]}(\chi|\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e},\sigma_{\textrm{noise}})\,\mathrm{d}\chi\,.

(20)

This corresponds to Eq. (
1
) with the replacements,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle\to\chi

(21a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}

\displaystyle\to\{\mu,\sigma\}

(21b)

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\to\phi_{[0,1]}(\chi|\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e},\sigma_{\textrm{noise}})

(21c)

\displaystyle W(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\to\pi(\chi)=1

(21d)

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})

\displaystyle\to\phi_{[0,1]}(\chi|\mu,\sigma)

(21e)

\displaystyle I(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{L}(\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e}|\mu,\sigma)\,.

(21f)

For our uniform sampling prior, the
W(\boldsymbol{\theta})
term in Eq. (
1
) is also uniform and can be dropped.

Since we assume that we only have access to draws from
\phi_{[0,1]}(\chi|\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e},\sigma_{\textrm{noise}})
without knowledge of its true functional form, we cannot evaluate Eq. (
20
) exactly and must instead approximate
\mathcal{L}(\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e}|\mu,\sigma)
from these samples.

and look at the evolution of the estimators as
\sigma\to 0
.
We consider a particular event likelihood that follows the shape of a truncated normal with parameters
\phi_{[0,1]}(0.4,0.2)
, which corresponds to a detected value of
\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e}=0.4
and detection noise
\sigma_{\text{noise}}=0.2
.
We subsequently take 1000 draws from this likelihood and utilize the estimator to get one estimate of the integral; for the
MC
estimator, we compute the bias and variance analytically (Appendix
B
).

To begin with, the
MC
estimate transitions to a regime of very large variance at
\sigma\approx 0.025
.
This transition is expected to happen at an increasingly larger value of
\sigma
as the dimensionality of the problem increases
(
C. Talbot & J. Golomb 2023
)
.
Although the
MC
estimates are unbiased (as can be seen by the dotted blue line in the second panel of Fig.
1
), the large variance makes it unusable for practical purposes.
On the other hand, both types of
KDEs
retain a relatively small variance throughout the domain. However, they fail to converge to the correct value of the likelihood estimate at the edge.

Our toy example highlights key limitations of commonly employed integration methods for astrophysical population analysis near parameter boundaries: standard
MC
approaches suffer severe variance limitations, while
KDEs
alleviate variance at the expense of systematic boundary biases—this is the problem we set out to address.
We will next introduce our
TGMM
method, which faithfully captures the true value of the likelihood
\mathcal{L}(\chi^{{\rm obs}}_{e}\mid\mu,\sigma)
with minimal bias, all while having a controlled variance near the edge and computational advantage in this particular test.

IV
Our proposed method: Truncated Gaussian Mixture models

Section
III
showed that, when
the target population is sharply localized near a parameter
boundary, existing estimators incur either large variance or large boundary bias.
Our
TGMM
method addresses this by retaining the analytic convenience of a kernel approach without surrendering boundary fidelity.

In what follows, we denote the set of parameters as
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and let their boundaries be specified by hyper-corner vectors
{\bf a}
and
{\bf b}
, where
{\bf a}
sets the lower limit and
{\bf b}
sets the upper limit for all bounded dimensions.
For parameters that are unbounded, their entries in
{\bf a}
or
{\bf b}
can be taken to infinity to remove one of the boundaries.

The hybrid
KDE
methods described in Section.
III.2
factorize the parameter space into dimensions with an analytically integrable population likelihood and others where we fall back on
MC
estimation. In the same spirit, we separate our parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
into two sectors based on whether the per-event integral of Eq. (
1
) admits analytic or semi-analytic evaluation under the chosen kernel. The Analytic Sector (
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}
) consists of parameters for which this integral can be computed in closed or semi-closed form for the chosen pair of population model
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})
and kernel
\phi
; truncated Gaussians, uniform distributions, and Heaviside functions are examples that yield tractable integrals. The remaining parameters form the Sampled Sector (
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}
), where we estimate the integral via standard Monte Carlo techniques.
While this breakup offers increased flexibility, it does impose the constraint that the population model itself separate into a product of these sectors, i.e., the population model must not correlate
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}
.
Formally, that means the model must factorize as

p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\Lambda)=p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}\mid\Lambda^{a})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}\mid\Lambda^{s})\,,

(22)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}\oplus\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{a}\oplus\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{s}
.
Mixtures of such product distributions could also be allowed, i.e.,

p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\Lambda)=\sum_{k}p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}_{k}\mid\Lambda^{a}_{k})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}_{k}\mid\Lambda^{s}_{k})\,,

(23)

for
k
components.
While it is straightforward to generalize to the mixture case, we describe our method using only one term in the population model as in Eq. (
22
).

Crucially, in the Analytic Sector we would like to utilize analytic estimates of the posterior over
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}
using a
truncated-Gaussian mixture
(TGMM) – setting our choice of kernel to be Truncated Gaussian, while setting our population model to be also a
TGMM
.
This pairing of a truncated Gaussian kernel with truncated Gaussian population components yields closed-form expressions for the per-event integrals like Eq. (
III.2
), enabling accurate boundary behavior and fast evaluation.
In the following, we fix our population model to be a mixture of truncated Gaussians in the Analytic Sector to capture edge behavior of bounded parameters.
Namely,

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{s})\sum_{l}^{L}\eta_{l}\,\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{l},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{l})\,,

(24)

where
L
is the number of components in the population model and
\eta_{l}
are the mixture weights (with
\sum_{l}\eta_{l}=1
).
The formalism is the same for any other choice as long as it is analytically integrable against the
TGMM
kernel.

With this factorized population model,
we can revisit the integral of Eq. (
1
),

\displaystyle I(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=\int

\displaystyle\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{s})}{W^{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s})}\left[\sum_{l}^{L}\eta_{l}\,\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{l},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{l})\right]

(25)

\displaystyle\times\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{W^{a}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a})}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,,

where we have assumed that the weight function
W(\boldsymbol{\theta})
can separate into
W^{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s})W^{a}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a})
.
When this separation cannot be achieved, one can fit the whole dataset with weights
W_{i}=W(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
during the fitting procedure, as opposed to just the analytic portion. We have found that this degrades the fits slightly if the weights span a large dynamic range.

We then fit the weighted probability distribution in the last factor of Eq. (
25
) to a
TGMM
(Appendix
D
).
In doing so, we also impose that the individual Gaussian correlation matrices separate across the analytic and sampled sectors,
i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{a}_{k}\oplus\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}_{k}
.
This gives

\displaystyle\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{W^{a}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a})}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{k}^{K}w_{k}\phi_{[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})

(26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}^{K}w_{k}\phi_{[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}^{a}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{a}_{k})\phi_{[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}^{s}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}_{k})\,.

where
K
is the number of components in the TGMM fit and
w_{k}
are the mixture weights (with
\sum_{k}w_{k}=1
).
While this factorization constraint is a limitation, one can always add enough TGMM components to effectively capture correlations in the likelihoods across sectors.

Above, we use
TGMMs
to fit the target density
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})/W^{a}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a})
rather than
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
itself.
This ensures that the analytic part of the integrand matches the truncated-Gaussian kernel, since the ability to analytically integrate applies to pairs of densities (population and kernel) but not to arbitrary weight functions.
This allows us to further rewrite the integral of Eq. (
25
) as

\displaystyle I(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl}^{K,L}\eta_{l}w_{k}\int\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{s})}{W^{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s})}\phi_{[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}^{s}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}_{k})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}

\displaystyle\times\int\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{l},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{l})\,\phi_{[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}^{a}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{a}_{k})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{a}

(27)

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl}^{K,L}\eta_{l}w_{k}\left\langle\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}|\Lambda^{s})}{W^{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s})}\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{s}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}_{k})}

\displaystyle\times F_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{a}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{a}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{l},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{l})\,,

(28)

where
F_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2})
is the integral of the product of two truncated multivariate gaussians with parameters
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1})
and
(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2})
over the hypercube with bounding corners
{\bf a}
and
{\bf b}
.
While we have assumed that the truncation domain in the population model
[{\bf a},{\bf b}]
is the same as that in our
TGMM
fits, one can relax that constraint, implying that the function
F
in Eq. (
IV
) depends on truncation regions
[{\bf a}_{1},{\bf b}_{1}]
from the
TGMM
fits and
[{\bf a}_{2},{\bf b}_{2}]
from the population model. In general the expression for
F
can be found in Appendix
A
.

Once we have evaluated
F_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{a}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{a}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{l},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{l})
, we could then evaluate the expectation over the sampled parameters using
MC
sampling,

\left\langle\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}|\Lambda^{s})}{W^{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s})}\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{s}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}_{k})}\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{s})}{W^{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n})}\,,

(29)

where we have averaged over
N
samples from the
k
th Gaussian component,
\mathcal{N}_{[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{s}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}_{k})
.

However, we have found better estimates of
I(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
if we do not generate new samples from
\mathcal{N}_{[\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{s}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{s}_{k})
,
but instead use the original event posterior samples for these estimates.
This approach can help address some limitations of the
TGMM
as a density estimator.
For instance, the
TGMM
may struggle to capture non-trivial discontinuities present in the original posterior distribution. By utilizing the original posterior samples, the estimator becomes more robust to such features.

The TGMM fitting procedure provides, for each sample, the probability that it belongs to each mixture component (a “soft assignment”).
We assign each sample to a specific component by randomly choosing according to these probabilities, resulting in
k
groups of data points, each of size
N_{k}
(with
N=\sum_{k}N_{k}
).
With this grouping, we then perform the Monte Carlo sum in Eq. (
29
) using the original samples.

\displaystyle I(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\approx\sum_{kl}

\displaystyle\eta_{l}w_{k}\left[\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}_{i}|\Lambda^{s})}{W^{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{s}_{i})}\right]

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle F_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{a}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{a}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{l},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{l})\,.

This yields the final expression for the integrals required when evaluating the event-level likelihood
\mathcal{L}(d_{e}|\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
and detection efficiency
\xi(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
during population inference.

We next describe the

VI.1
Model details

We replicate the
Extended
model implemented by
H. Tong et al. 2022
, where the mass and redshift sectors are similar to
R. Abbott et al. 2023c
.
The spin magnitude is modeled as a mixture of a delta function spike at
\chi_{1}=0,\chi_{2}=0
and a component where both
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
are
identically and independently distributed (IID)
according to a more extended, truncated distribution.
However, rather than utilizing the Beta distributions of the
Extended

\displaystyle p\left(\chi_{1,2}\mid\mu_{\chi},\sigma_{\chi},\lambda_{0}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\lambda_{0}\right)\phi_{[0,1]}\left(\chi_{1}\mid\mu_{\chi},\sigma_{\chi}\right)\phi_{[0,1]}\left(\chi_{2}\mid\mu_{\chi},\sigma_{\chi}\right)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{0}\delta\left(\chi_{1}\right)\delta\left(\chi_{2}\right)\,

where
\lambda_{0}
is the fraction of
BBHs
with zero spins.

The spin orientation is similar to the
Default
model in
R. Abbott et al. 2023c
, but with
a minimum cosine tilt angle
z_{\min}
.
In particular,

\displaystyle p\left(z_{1,2}\mid\xi_{\textrm{spin}},\sigma_{\textrm{spin}},z_{\min}\right)=

(31)

\displaystyle\xi_{\textrm{spin}}\phi_{[-1,1]}\left(z_{1}\mid\sigma_{\textrm{spin}},z_{\min}\right)\phi_{[-1,1]}\left(z_{2}\mid\sigma_{\textrm{spin}},z_{\min}\right)

\displaystyle+(1-\xi_{\textrm{spin}})\left(\frac{\Theta\left(z_{1}-z_{\min}\right)}{1-z_{\min}}\right)\left(\frac{\Theta\left(z_{2}-z_{\min}\right)}{1-z_{\min}}\right)\,.

An important step in implementing the

Emulating the prior choices of

is straightforward in all sectors except spin magnitude.
There, the prior on the Beta distribution parameters is chosen such that the prior on the true mean
\mathbb{E}(\chi)
and variance of the spin distribution

are flat, together with cuts to ensure the Beta distributions are nonsingular.
To reproduce this
we use JAX to autodifferentiate expressions of

and compute the relevant Jacobian factor
to achieve flat priors on these.
We find that cuts on the Beta parameters are
not needed in the case of truncated normal distributions. Furthermore, as part of their analysis,
H. Tong et al. 2022
ignored selection effects in the spin sectors.
As such, we modify our analysis by artificially inflating the relevant scale parameters in each
TGMM

The result is that each component is essentially a uniform distribution in the spin sectors.

Finally, there is a subtlety in the use of zero-spin parameter estimation samples in this analysis.
In essence using such samples means that for the zero-spin subpopulation, the spin-orientations are not modeled (effectively creating a uniform distribution in
z_{i}=\cos\theta_{i}

In fact, as
\chi\to 0

However, in practice we cannot use Eq. (

H. Tong et al. 2022

Instead, to make a fair comparison with our approach (and others that attempt to extrapolate to the zero-spin population,
T. A. Callister et al. 2022

we must modify our spin-orientation model for the zero-spin subpopulation.
As such, our complete spin model is

\displaystyle p(\chi_{1},\chi_{2},z_{1},z_{2}\mid\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\eta_{0}\right)p(\chi_{1,2}\mid\mu_{\chi},\sigma_{\chi},\lambda_{0}=0)

\displaystyle\times p(z_{1,2}\mid\xi_{\textrm{spin}},\sigma_{\textrm{spin}},z_{\min})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\eta_{0}\delta(\chi)\,.

VII
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced and validated a new approach for inferring populations of astrophysical objects whose parameters are bounded by physical constraints. Such boundary effects frequently arise, for example, in black hole spin magnitudes confined to
[0,1]
or exoplanet eccentricities within the same interval. Traditional estimators often struggle in reconstructing sharp features at the edges — either suffering from high variance in the case of
MC
methods or high biases through edge effects often introduced by density estimation techniques.

We addressed these issues using a truncated Gaussian mixture model (TGMM) framework. By fitting samples to a mixture of truncated Gaussians, our method accurately recovers features near the boundary, while substantially mitigating variance blow-ups. We further showed how to combine TGMMs with standard hierarchical Bayesian population analyses: in the “analytic sector,” where the parameter is bounded and exhibits possible edge features, TGMM integrals can be computed analytically, whereas elsewhere we can fall back on more conventional
MC
based methods.

Through controlled 1D examples, we demonstrated that TGMMs sharply outperform naive Monte Carlo estimators near boundaries, maintaining high accuracy with comparatively fewer samples. Additionally, we incorporated our TGMM method into a realistic population inference analysis for binary black holes from the GWTC-3 catalog – where black hole spins are a bounded domain of interest. We compared results of the analysis using both
MC
techniques and our
TGMM
method, and were able to get agreement between them especially in the spin posterior predictive distributions.

Our technique also probed near-zero spin BBH fractions from the same catalog – a feature where naive
MC
methods may fail – without additional specialized parameter-estimation runs, validating that TGMM-based estimates track closely with results obtained using more computationally expensive methods.

Not requiring additional parameter-estimation runs is a crucial advantage of our method.
Firstly, this can be difficult to scale as the number of events grows in size.
More importantly, in re-doing parameter estimation, the exact subpopulation distribution one would like to to probe needs to be decided apriori. One would like to be able to infer the sub-population’s properties and its fraction simultaneously. Given the accuracy of our estimates for narrow features, we are able to do this and reproduce known results.
This allows us to let the data guide us towards the existence of certain subpopulations as opposed to modelling them explicitly. While TGMMs significantly reduce boundary-induced biases and variance issues, they do require that the population model separates into (i) a tractable part that can be expressed as a sum of truncated Gaussians, and (ii) a “sampled sector” for remaining parameters. This decomposition can still capture realistic physics in many scenarios, but certain highly correlated, multi-parameter distributions may require additional care.

For an end user, the procedure has been streamlined by a suite of packages, and the analysis moves forward as follows. To start, one identifies some subset or a sector of bounded parameters, and defines a population model in that sector of one of the general classes we mention (for example, the Truncated Gaussian), while defining something standard in the rest. Once done, we fit the posterior distributions and selection injections to
TGMMs
(using
TruncatedGaussianMixtures

(
A. Hussain 2024a
)
) and utilize those data products as part of the inference procedure (performed using
gravpop

(
A. Hussain 2024b
)
).
One can then run the inference as normal, and the procedures described in the paper are handled by the package. In this sense
gravpop
is one among many other packages that perform gravitational wave population inference (e.g.
GWPopulation

(
C. Talbot et al. 2025
)
,
GWInferno

(
B. Edelman et al. 2023
;
B. Edelman et al. 2023
)
,
ICAROGW

(
S. Mastrogiovanni et al. 2024
;
S. Mastrogiovanni et al. 2023
)
,
PixelPop
(
J. Heinzel et al. 2025
)
and
GWKokab

(
R. O. Meesum Qazalbash 2024
;
M. Qazalbash et al. 2025
)
among others), but with the additional ability to probe narrow and/or edge-dominant population features.

Here we provide the general analytic expression for the integral of a product of two truncated multivariate Gaussians, with possibly different boundary limits. Consider one truncated multivariate normal distribution with parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}
, bounded within a hypercube defined by the lower corner
{\bf a}_{1}
and upper corner
{\bf b}_{1}
, and another with parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}
and boundaries
{\bf a}_{2}{\bf b}_{2}
. Then the integral of the product of the two pdfs is given by,

\displaystyle F_{[{\bf a}_{1},{\bf b}_{1};{\bf a}_{2},{\bf b}_{2}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2})=

(A1)

\displaystyle\int_{{\bf a}}^{{\bf b}}\phi_{[{\bf a}_{1},{\bf b}_{1}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1})\phi_{[{\bf a}_{2},{\bf b}_{2}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2})d\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{[{\bf a}_{3},{\bf b}_{3}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{3},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{3})}{C_{[{\bf a}_{1},{\bf b}_{1}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1})C_{[{\bf a}_{2},{\bf b}_{2}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2})}\phi(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2},\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2})\,.

Here

\displaystyle C_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\int_{{\bf a}}^{{\bf b}}\phi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})d\boldsymbol{\theta}\,,

(A2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\,

\displaystyle{\bf a}_{3}

\displaystyle=\max({\bf a}_{1},{\bf a}_{2})

\displaystyle{\bf b}_{3}

\displaystyle=\min({\bf b}_{1},{\bf b}_{2})\,,

where
\phi
is a Gaussian distribution (without truncation) and the
\max(\cdot)
and
\min(\cdot)
are taken element wise.

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}[\mathcal{\hat{L}}^{MC}_{N}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left[\langle p(\theta\mid\Lambda)^{2}\rangle_{\theta\sim p(\theta)}-\langle p(\theta\mid\Lambda)\rangle^{2}_{\theta\sim p(\theta)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left[\langle p(\theta\mid\Lambda)^{2}\rangle_{\theta\sim p(\theta)}-\mathcal{L}^{2}\right]\,,

where

is our population model and

(B2)

(B3)

generalizing beyond

\displaystyle\langle p(\theta\mid\Lambda)\rangle_{\theta\sim p(\theta)}

(B4)

\displaystyle=C_{[0,1]}\left(\frac{\mu\sigma_{0}^{2}+\mu_{0}\sigma^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}},\frac{\sigma_{0}\sigma}{\sqrt{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}}}\right)\phi(\mu_{0}\mid\mu,\sqrt{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma^{2}})\,,

\mu

Appendix C
KDE
with
\mathcal{O}(h)
boundary bias

In this section we derive a bounded
KDE
method which is unbiased to
\mathcal{O}(h)
at the boundary.
The
KDE

\hat{p}(x)
of a particular probability distribution is given by

\displaystyle\hat{p}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}K(x|x_{i},h)\,,

(C1)

where
K(x|x_{i},h)
is a (generally non-symmetric) kernel with a bandwidth
h
.
An example would be a Gaussian KDE, in which case
K(x|x_{n},h)=\frac{1}{h}\phi((x-x_{n})/h)
.
However, we are particularly interested in representing distributions on truncated domains
x\in[a,b]
.
In the large
N
limit this estimate becomes

\displaystyle\hat{p}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim p(x)}[K(x|x^{\prime})]

(C2)

We would like that in this limit the estimate becomes the true distribution, up to terms of order
\mathcal{O}(h)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}(x)]=\int K(x|x^{\prime})p(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\,.

(C3)

Assume we can analytically get
p(x^{\prime})
by Taylor expanding around
x
.
This should be possible since
x,x^{\prime}\in[a,b]
and it is a fair assumption that the PDF we want to fit to is analytic inside the truncated domain.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}(x)]

\displaystyle=\int K(x|x^{\prime})\left[p(x)+h\left(\frac{x-x^{\prime}}{h}\right)\cdot\nabla p(x)+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})\right]dx^{\prime}

(C4)

\displaystyle=p(x)\int K(x|x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}+\nabla p(x)\cdot\vec{x}\int K(x|x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}-\nabla p(x)\cdot\int\vec{x}^{\prime}K(x|x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})\,.

This shows that an unbiased estimate at
x
, of order
\mathcal{O}(h)
requires

\displaystyle\int K(x|x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\text{and}

\displaystyle\int\vec{x}^{\prime}K(x|x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\vec{x}+\mathcal{O}(h)\,.

(C5)

We would further like our kernels to be of the form of a truncated normal.
More specifically,

\displaystyle K(x|x^{\prime}h)=\phi_{[a,b]}(x\mid x^{\prime},h)=\frac{\phi(x|x^{\prime},h)}{C(x^{\prime},h)}\,.

(C6)

However this kernel is definitely not unbiased as we get close to the edge, since

\displaystyle\int K(x|x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=\int\frac{\phi(x|x^{\prime}h)}{C(x^{\prime},h)}dx^{\prime}\neq 1\,.

(C7)

However, there is a way to remedy this while retaining truncated Gaussians for our kernels.
If we take the mean parameter of the kernel
\mu
, and instead of placing it at the point
x^{\prime}
it to be some function of the point
x^{\prime}
, we may have enough degrees of freedom to make it an unbiased KDE.
This makes our ansatz for the kernel
K(x|x^{\prime})=\phi_{[a,b]}(x|\mu(x^{\prime})h)
.
The goal is then to find the function
\mu(x^{\prime})
to give us an unbiased estimate of
\hat{p}(x)
.

Using our ansatz, let us assume we can find a function
x^{\prime}(\mu)
such that
dx^{\prime}/d\mu=C(\mu,h)
, which then gives

\displaystyle\int K(x|x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\phi(x|\mu,h)}{C(\mu,h)}\frac{dx^{\prime}}{d\mu}d\mu=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x|\mu,h)d\mu=1\,.

(C8)

This means that in the 1D case that

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(\mu)=\int C(\mu,h)d\mu=\int\Phi\left(\frac{b-\mu}{h}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{a-\mu}{h}\right)d\mu\,.

(C9)

where
\Phi
is the CDF of a gaussian distribution.
This integral of a gaussian CDF is well known and can be computed to give

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(\mu)=b-h\left(\beta\Phi(\beta)+\phi(\beta)\right)+h\left[\alpha\Phi(\alpha)+\phi(\alpha)\right]\,,

(C10)

where
\alpha=(a-\mu)/h
and
\beta=(b-\mu)/h
.
One can invert this equation for any given
x^{\prime}
to find the
\mu
parameter for the truncated Gaussian kernel.
Inversion using root finding is very cheap in this situation and even for
100,000
points, it is not a limiting factor in the speed of the
TGMM
fitting procedure described in Section
III
.
Interpolation is also a good way to cache results and scale this procedure but we did not find that this step needed any further optimization for our use case.

As for the second condition for the kernel to be unbiased, we evaluate the moment

\displaystyle\int x^{\prime}K(x|x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}x^{\prime}\frac{\phi(x|\mu(x^{\prime})h)}{C(\mu(x^{\prime}),h)}dx^{\prime}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{\prime}(\mu)\frac{\phi(x|\mu,h)}{C(\mu,h)}\frac{dx^{\prime}}{d\mu}d\mu=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}h}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{\prime}(\mu)\exp(-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2h^{2}})d\mu

(C11)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(x,h)}\left[x^{\prime}(\mu)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(x,h)}\left[\mu+(x^{\prime}(\mu)-\mu)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(x,h)}\left[\mu\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(x,h)}\left[(x^{\prime}(\mu)-\mu)\right]\,.

Figure 10:
We plot the logarithm of the bias term in Eq. (
C12
) against the logarithm of the bandwidth
h
.
A positive slope of
1
is observed in the plot, which implies that the bias is of order
\mathcal{O}(h)
.

To be able to perform this integral and see the scaling with
h
, we focus on the region near the left edge at
a
let the right most limit
b\to\infty
.
This is because the bias should only occur close to the edges, and if
h
is small the effect of the right edge on the bias at the left edge can be safely ignored.

Hence we have

\displaystyle\int x^{\prime}K(x|x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=x+\mathbb{E}_{\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(x,h)}\left[(x^{\prime}(\mu)-\mu)\right]=x+\mathbb{E}_{\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(x,h)}\left[h\left(\phi(\alpha(\mu))+\alpha(\mu)\Phi(\alpha(\mu))\right)\right]

(C12)

\displaystyle=x+\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left[\phi(\alpha(\mu))+\alpha(\mu)\Phi(\alpha(\mu))\right]\phi\left(\frac{x-\mu}{h}\right)d\mu\,

\displaystyle=x+h\,I\left(\frac{x-a}{h}\right)\,,

where

\displaystyle I(y)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left[\phi(z)+z\Phi(z)\right]\phi(y+z)dz\,.

(C13)

Note that
(x-a)/h
simply counts how many bandwidths away from the boundary we are.
Close to the boundary (
x\to a
) we expect
I([x-a]/h)
to be
\mathcal{O}(1)
and hence

\int x^{\prime}K(x|x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=x+\mathcal{O}(h)

(C14)

as required.
This proves that the KDE is unbiased at order
\mathcal{O}(h)

We can numerically verify this scaling with
h
.
The results are shown on Fig.
10
.
One can clearly see a slope of
\approx 1
, which emperically confirms the
KDE
is indeed biased only at order
\mathcal{O}(h)
.

(D2)

where the distribution is completely characterized by the responsibility matrix

k
th component,

p({\bf z}_{i}=\mathbbm{1}_{k})=\langle z_{ik}\rangle\,,

(D3)

since the expectation of an indicator variable in general is its own probability mass function.

Having knowlege of the latent variables allows us to make hard assignments to each component, and write out our likelihood as
3
3

3

The addition of the
W_{i}

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid{\bf z}_{i}=\mathbbm{1}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Theta})

\displaystyle=\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\,.

(D6)

The likelihood we seek to optimize is the expected value of the log-likelihood over the weighted dataset,

\log\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\mathcal{D})=\sum_{i}^{N}W_{i}\log\left[\sum_{k}^{K}w_{k}\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\right]\,.

(D7)

Of course, we do not yet know
w_{k}
, since it is what we are trying to infer.
To get around this we start with some initial set of TGMM parameters
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(0)}
, compute
\langle z^{(0)}_{ik}\rangle=\mathbb{E}(z_{ik}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(0)})
, and insert them into the expression,

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(0)})=

(D8)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}^{N}W_{i}\log\left[\sum_{k}^{K}\langle z^{(0)}_{ik}\rangle\frac{w_{k}\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})}{\langle z^{(0)}_{ik}\rangle}\right]\,.

The initialziation can be performed using K-means clustering, as is customary with initialization methods for
GMMs

(
S. Lloyd 1982
;
P. Virtanen et al. 2020
)
.

The term inside the logarithm is an expectation over
{\bf z}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(0)}
. We then use Jensen’s inequality to exchange the logarithm and the expectation to get a lower bound on the log-likelihood of our estimate, which we denote as
Q(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathcal{D})
,

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\mathcal{D},\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(0)})\geq Q(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathcal{D})=\sum_{i}^{N}\sum_{k}^{K}W_{i}\langle z^{(0)}_{ik}\rangle\log\left[\frac{w_{k}\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})}{\langle z^{(0)}_{ik}\rangle}\right]\,.

(D9)

To make the inequality in Eq. (
D9
) an equality, we want the term inside the logarithm to be as close to a constant as possible, and so we make the closest guess we can with the initial set of parameters,

\langle z_{ik}^{(0)}\rangle\propto w^{(0)}_{k}\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(0)}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(0)}_{k})\,.

(D10)

Since
\sum_{k}\langle z_{ik}^{(0)}\rangle=1
, we can identify the normalization constant and state the first step in the EM algorithm, called the E-step.

The E-Step:

\langle z_{ik}^{(0)}\rangle
can be computed from the initial parameter guess
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(0)}
as

\langle z_{ik}^{(0)}\rangle=\frac{w^{(0)}_{k}\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(0)}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(0)}_{k})}{\sum_{k}w^{(0)}_{k}\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(0)}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(0)}_{k})}\,,

(D11)

which is a normalized version of Eq. (
D10
).

The M-Step:
We then try and maximize
Q(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(0)})
to get a new estimate for the maximum
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(1)}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(1)}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}Q(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(0)},\mathcal{D})

(D12)

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left[\sum_{i}^{N}\sum_{k}^{K}W_{i}\langle z_{ik}^{(0)}\rangle\log\left[w_{k}\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\right]\right],

subject to the condition that
\sum_{k}w_{k}=1
, and ignoring constant terms independent of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
.

Using this constraint and the separation of the logarithms, we can first find the new estimate of
w_{k}
as

w^{(1)}_{k}=\frac{\sum_{i}W_{i}\langle z_{ik}^{(0)}\rangle}{\sum_{i}W_{i}}\,.

(D13)

With a fixed estimate for
w_{k}
in the M-step, we can write the inference of the remaining parameters (
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
) as
K
separate maximization procedures, and find that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(1)}_{k}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}}\left[\sum_{i}^{N}\eta_{ik}\log\left[\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\right]\right]\,,

(D14)

where we have removed constant terms
4
4

4

This includes
w_{k}
since these are now fixed.
and defined weights for each sample as

\eta_{ik}:=\frac{W_{i}\langle z_{ik}^{(0)}\rangle}{\sum_{i}W_{i}\langle z_{ik}^{(0)}\rangle}\,.

(D15)

Let
\mathcal{D}_{k}
be the weighted dataset
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},\eta_{ik}\}_{i=1\dots N}
.
Then the sum can be written as the expectation

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(1)}_{k}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left[\log\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\right]\right]

(D16)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{c}}{=}\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}}D_{KL}(\mathcal{D}_{k}\ ||\ \mathcal{N}_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}))\,,

(D17)

where
\overset{\mathrm{c}}{=}
denotes equality up to an additive constant. This expectation is simply the KL-divergence between
\mathcal{D}_{k}
and
\mathcal{N}_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
, up to terms independent of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
.
For forms of the target distribution that are in the exponentional family (of which truncated Gaussians are a member), minimizing the KL divergence corresponds to matching the sufficient statistics of the two distributions
(
S. Amari & H. Nagaoka 2000
)
.
Hence we simply chose to find
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
that solve the simultaneous equations involving the sufficient statistics of the truncated Gaussian family—namely the first two moments:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})}[\boldsymbol{\theta}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\eta_{ik}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\,,

(D18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})}[\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\eta_{ik}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}^{T}\,.

(D19)

The algorithm to compute the left hand side of Eq. (
D19
) is outlined in
G. Lee & C. Scott 2012
and implemented in our package
A. Hussain 2024a
.
This matching can then be done by using a fixed point iteration scheme using auto-differentiation techniques to get the required gradients (though some custom gradient rules are required).
This iteration scheme is also implemented in
A. Hussain 2024a
.

D.2
Boundary Unbiasing

We find that when fitting
TGMMs
to samples, getting better convergence at the edge is slow.
Since we we use a K-means initialization, most initialized components lie mostly within the truncation region, and hence the initialization itself is biased to begin with.
To counteract this, we look more closely at Eqs. (
D19
) as part of the M-step in the algorithm.
The right hand side of these equations are the weighted moments of the samples, which can be viewed as weighted moments of a PDF given by
\hat{p}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{i}\eta_{ik}\delta(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
.
To push the algorithm to prioritize fitting edge effects for a certain number of iterations, we propose a variant of the M-step where we replace the right hand side with a
KDE
that is unbiased at the boundary up to
\mathcal{O}(h)
, constructed with non-symmetric truncated Gaussian kernels
k(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})=\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}),{\bf h})
.
This
KDE
is described in Appendix
C
, as is the function
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\cdot)
.
With it, we set our estimate for
\hat{p}_{i}
as

\hat{p}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{ik}\eta_{ik}\phi_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}),{\bf h})\,.

(D21)

Then our modified M-step is as follows.

M-step (
KDE
variant):
For a certain number of iterations we perform a variant of the M-step update using

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})}[\boldsymbol{\theta}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\hat{p}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\boldsymbol{\theta}]=\sum_{i}\eta_{ik}\mathcal{M}_{1i}

(D22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})}[\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\hat{p}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}]=\sum_{i}\eta_{ik}\mathcal{M}_{2i}\,,

where
\mathcal{M}_{1i}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2i}
are the first and second moments of
\mathcal{N}_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}),\ {\bf h})
.
We perform a certain number of EM iterations using this variant of the M-step and subsequently fall back to standard iterations as before.
Our full EM fitting procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Appendix E
Computational Techniques for the evalutation of the multivariate gaussian probability mass

The function
C_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
evaluates the probability mass of some multivariate gaussian
N(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
within a hypercube truncation region defined by
[{\bf a},{\bf b}]
.
This can be computed very quickly analytically for univariate gaussians and with a bit more compute for bivariate Gaussians
(
W.-J. Tsay & P.-H. Ke 2021
;
F. Figueiredo 2023
, see e.g.)
.
With only these two implementations one can compute
C_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
for covariance matrices of higher dimension that separate into blocks of at most
2\times 2
.
We have numerical routines to evaluate
C_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and
F

A1
)
in our package
gravpop

(

)
.

With no structure on
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
there is a psuedo-algorithm described in
A. Genz 1992
that can compute
C_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
for general dimension.
This algorithm is implemented in the
Julia
package
MvNormalCDF.jl

(
MvN 2021
)
, and utilized in our package to fit Truncated Gaussian Mixtures
TruncatedGaussianMixtures.jl

(

.

The algorithm by
A. Genz 1992
for the case of a general covariance structure is not generally amenable to auto-differentiation routines, which we leverage in our population analysis code.
This limits us to covariance structures consisting of
2\times 2
blocks.
However, if future work requires it, one can write custom Jacobians by leveraging the fact that the first and second derivatives of

can be related to the first and second moments of
\mathcal{N}_{[{\bf a},{\bf b}]}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})

The computation of those moments is a crucial part of fitting

As with most estimates of CDFs, for very extreme features and when

To get around this we perform a pre-processing step before population analysis, which maps Gaussian components in the

The transformation

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu-a}{\sigma},\quad\beta=\frac{\mu-b}{\sigma},

(E1)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}
