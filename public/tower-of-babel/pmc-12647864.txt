Endothelial glycocalyx shedding and oxidative stress in neuronal damage after return of spontaneous circulation in cardiac arrest
Authors: Helton de Oliveira Couto, Paula P. Ovídio, Alceu A. Jordão, Carlos Henrique Miranda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Endothelial glycocalyx (eGC) covers the luminal surface of all vessels. Recent evidence has highlighted its pivotal role in maintaining the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) after cardiac arrest (CA) is a main example of global ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury. I/R injury can induce eGC shedding and redox imbalance. This study aims to evaluate the interplay between eGC shedding and oxidative stress (OS) in determing neuronal injury and predicting neurological outcomes following CA. Blood and urine samples were obtained from patients who achieved ROSC after CA. Biomarkers of eGC shedding (syndecan-1 [SDC-1], CD44s, hyaluronan [HYAL], and sulfated glycosaminoglycans [GAGs], of endothelial injury (thrombomodulin [TBML]), of OS (malondialdehyde [MDA], total hydroperoxides [H₂O₂], total antioxidant capacity [CAT], reduced glutathione [GSH], superoxide dismutase [SOD]), and inflammatory mediators (IL-6 and TNF-α) were measured. Neuron-specific enolase (NSE) levels were employed as a surrogate marker of neuronal injury. A score ≥ 4 in the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 28 days was considered as poor neurological outcome. Seventy-one patients were included. The levels of SDC-1, HYAL, CD44s, TBML, TAC, SOD, H₂O₂, IL-6, and TNF-α were statistically correlated with neuronal injury. Higher levels of SDC-1, HYAL, CD44s, TBML, MDA, and SOD were observed in patients with poor neurological outcomes. Multivariable logistic regression identified SDC-1 > 796 ng/mL (p = 0.02), TBML > 446 pg/mL (p = 0.03), and MDA > 179 nmol/g (p = 0.03), as predictors of unfavorable neurological outcomes. eGC shedding and OS are interrelated key pathophysiological mechanisms contributing to neuronal injury following CA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25983-5.

Introduction

Despite advancements in cardiopulmonary resuscitation, favorable neurological outcomes following cardiac arrest (CA) remain limited, ranging from 2.8% to 18.2%1. After return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), patients often develop a systemic inflammatory condition known as post-CA syndrome (PCAS). This syndrome encompasses anoxic brain injury, arrest-related myocardial dysfunction, a systemic ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury, and the persistence of the underlying precipitating pathology2. PCAS represents one of the most robust example of whole-body I/R injury—a key mechanism contributing to secondary neuronal damage3.

The endothelial glycocalyx (eGC) is a carbohydrate-rich layer lining the luminal surface of all blood vessels. It is composed of proteoglycans, such as syndecans and CD44s, anchored to the endothelial cell membrane, and associated glycosaminoglycans, including heparan sulfate (HS) and chondroitin sulfate (CS), the sulfated GAG, as well as hyaluronan, a non-sulfated GAG4,5. Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of the eGC in maintaining blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, particularly in preventing protein and fluid extravasation6–8.

An increasing body of evidence suggests that I/R injury induces eGC shedding and disrupts redox homeostasis, favoring the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) relative to antioxidant defenses9–11. The combination of eGC shedding and oxidative stress (OS) may compromise BBB integrity, contributing to secondary neuronal injury and poorer neurological outcomes in patients after CA.

This study aimed to investigate whether eGC shedding, in combination with OS imbalance, contributes to neuronal injury, and to evaluate whether biomarkers related to these processes could predict neurological outcomes after CA.

Methods

This was a single-center, observational, cross-sectional study. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Hospital das Clínicas, Ribeirão Preto School of Medicine, under protocol number CAAE: 18508719.7.0000.5440. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal representatives. The research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients

Inclusion criteria were patients of either gender, aged 18 years or older; in-hospital or out-of-hospital CA with any initial rhythm; the blood and urine samples must be collected within the first 24 h post-CA; sustained ROSC following cardiopulmonary resuscitation with hemodynamic stability defined as mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥ 65 mmHg without or with one vasopressor, arterial oxygen saturation > 92%, core temperature > 35 °C, and capillary blood glucose > 70 mg/dL for at least one hour, since the refractory shock and hypoxemia could influence biomarker levels.

Exclusion criteria included age over 80 years; advanced liver failure (Child-Pugh class B or C); stage IV or dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease; since severe liver or renal failure could influence biomarker levels; active malignancy; unilateral or bilateral ocular globe resection; brain death at admission; any traumatic cardiac arrest; acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke; pregnant women; and confirmed infection with SARS-CoV-2, given the scientific evidence supporting endothelial envolviment in this viral infection.

Data collection and clinical management

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data, as well as information related to the CA and resuscitation efforts, were extracted from the patient’s medical records. Data were collected at the exact time of patient enrollment in the study. All patients received post-CA care following the American Heart Association guidelines12. In line with current AHA recommendations, targeted temperature management aimed to prevent hyperthermia was implemented13. The target of blood glucose levels were between 70 and 180 mg/dl. Coronary angiography was performed in all patients presenting with ST-segment elevation on electrocardiogram after ROSC.

Imaging test

Non-contrast cranial computed tomography (CT) was performed between 48 and 72 h after ROSC. The scans were acquired using a 161-slice Aquilion Prime CT scanner (Canon Medical Systems) and interpreted by experienced radiologists. CT findings were classified as abnormal in the presence of a diffuse pattern of gray-white matter differentiation loss and sulcal effacement, corresponding to vascular territories in both anterior and posterior circulations, with involvement of the cerebral cortex and deep gray matter structures14.

Optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) ultrasound was conducted by a single trained operator using a LOGIQ V2 ultrasound system (GE Healthcare) with a linear transducer operating at frequencies between 4 and 13 MHz using international recomendations. Three measurements were obtained for each eye (right and left), and we calculated the arithmetic mean of them.

Biomarkers

Blood samples were collected in preservative-free tubes, centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min, and the resulting serum was stored at − 70 °C. Urine samples were collected from the drainage bag of the urinary catheter; one milliliter was transferred to a centrifuge tube, centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was subsequently stored at − 70 °C.

Biomarker levels were quantified using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. eGC shedding biomarkers included syndecan-1 (SDC-1), hyaluronan (HYAL), and soluble CD44 (CD44s) (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA). Urinary sulfated glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) were measured using the colorimetric Dimethylmethylene Blue Assay (DMMB), following established protocols15. GAGs levels were normalized to urinary creatinine concentrations (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA).

Thrombomodulin (TBML) was used as a biomarker of endothelial cell damage. Inflammatory mediators were assessed by tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels. Neuronal injury was quantified using neuron-specific enolase (NSE) (all kits from R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA), a validated biomarker of acute neuronal damage.

Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was determined by the method described by Erel16. Lipid peroxidation was measured via malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances assay, according to Buege and Aust17. Reduced glutathione (GSH) was measured using the Sedlak and Lindsay method18. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using the SOD Activity Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) levels were measured using the total hydroperoxides method described by Galli19. All OS biomarker values were normalized by total protein (g).

Clinical outcomes

Neurological outcomes were evaluated using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 28 days. The mRS was assessed by a blinded evaluator, conducted in person for patients who remained hospitalized until day 28, or by telephone follow-up for those discharged before day 28. A poor neurological outcome was defined as an mRS score ≥ 420.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to evaluate the distribution of quantitative variables. Variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (sd) and analyzed using the Student’s t-test. Quantitative variables exhibiting non-normal distribution were expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as percentages and compared using Fisher’s exact test. Correlations between two quantitative variables were assessed via Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed, and the areas under the curve were analyzed to determine the prognostic accuracy of each biomarker. Youden index was applied to identify the optimal cutoff points for the biomarkers under investigation. Odds ratios (OR) and its respective 95% confidence interval (95%CI) were calculated for each biomarker to identify predictors of poor neurological prognosis. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted, adjusting for the duration of CA (minutes), the presence of a shockable rhythm, and the administration of adrenaline. Two-tailed p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.00 (San Diego, CA, USA) and STATA version 13 (College Station, TX, USA).

Regarding the CA etiology, acute coronary syndrome was more prevalent in the favorable prognosis group 19/25 (76%) vs. 11/46 (24%); p = 0.012, resulting in a greater frequency of coronary angiography in this group 18/25; (72%) vs. 4/46; (9%); p < 0.0001.

Except for GAGs levels, all eGC shedding biomarkers showed a statistically significant correlation with the neuronal injury as assessed by NSE levels: SDC-1 (r = 0.38; p = 0.001), HYAL (r = 0.29; p = 0.013), CD44s (r = 0.47; p < 0.0001), and TBML (r = 0.52; p < 0.0001). Inflammatory mediators also showed a statiscally significant correlation with NSE levels: IL-6 (r = 0.26; p = 0.031) and TNF-α (r = 0.52; p < 0.0001). About the OS biomarkers, the TAC (r = 0.45; p < 0.0001), SOD (r = 0.45; p < 0.0001), and H2O2 (r = 0.24; p = 0.044) showed a statistically signicant correlation with NSE levels. Regarding the ONSD, neither the left nor the right ONSD showed a significant positive correlation with NSE levels. Figure 3.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that eGC shedding and OS biomarkers had a positive correlation with neuronal injury following CA. Similarly, the levels of SDC-1, HYAL, CD44s, TBML, MDA, and SOD were higher among patients with a worse neurological prognosis. SDC-1, TBML, and MDA levels measured within the first 24 h post-CA were independent predictors of unfavorable neurological outcomes.

There is robust scientific evidence that ROS plays a pivotal role in neuronal injury following ROSC after CA21,22. Excessive ROS production induces oxidative modification of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, disruption of cellular homeostasis, and activation of apoptotic and necrotic signaling pathways in neurons21. Concurrently, both experimental and clinical studies have shown that I/R injury drives eGC shedding, increasing circulating levels of its components9,10. This process is mediated by the upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases, heparanase, and hyaluronidase, which cleave core proteoglycans such as syndecan-1 and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), resulting in endothelial barrier dysfunction, increased vascular permeability, and microvascular flow impairment23,24. Our study aimed to elucidate the interplay between eGC shedding and OS and their contribution to neuronal injury following ROSC after CA.

Grundmann et al.25 was the first to report elevated biomarkers of eGC shedding (SDC-1, HYAL, and HS) in 25 CA survivors compared to 12 hemodynamically stable patients with acute coronary syndrome, suggesting that eGC shedding may play a role in the development of PCAS. In that study, SDC-1 increased early, HYAL in an intermediate phase, and HS later. Subsequently, Bro-Jeppesen et al.26 also reported early elevations of SDC-1 (peaking at hospital admission) and TBML (peaking 48 h post-admission) following CA. Bogner-Flatz et al.27 measured SDC-1 and HYAL in 15 patients immediately after ROSC in the prehospital setting and again at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h. Their results showed an SDC-1 peak at 24 h and a HYAL peak at 48 h. Both biomarkers were more elevated in patients who developed multiple organ failure, but only HYAL was associated with 30-day mortality.

In parallel with eGC shedding, some studies have shown increased OS after CA, particularly in patients with worse neurological outcomes11. Recent experimental evidence has revealed that the eGC is a critical blood-brain barrier (BBB) component. Zhu et al.28 demonstrated that eGC degradation led to BBB leakage, cerebral edema, and worsened neurological outcomes after CA in rats. Moreover, eGC exhibits intrinsic antioxidant properties, including extracellular SOD embedded within its structure, which acts as a protective barrier preventing systemically generated ROS from penetrating the brain. Systemic ROS can exacerbate neuronal injury by amplifying the effects of locally produced ROS29.

In our study, we used NSE levels as a biomarker of neuronal injury. NSE levels increase and peak between 48 and 72 h after ROSC, and it is considered the only biomarker currently recommended for assessing neurological prognosis post-CA3,30. NSE was measured within the first 24 h alongside the other biomarkers, not to assess its predictive accuracy for neurological outcomes, but rather to serve as a surrogate marker of early neuronal injury.

The most biomarkers of eCG shedding and OS had a positive correlation with neuron injury assessed through NSE levels. Although other studies have already evaluated these biomarkers for prognostic purposes after CA, to our knowledge, no previous study has unequivocally demonstrated their correlation with the neuronal injury. Among the eGC biomarkers, only GAGs levels did not correlate with neuronal injury, which may be explained by their delayed increase, as reported in previous study25.

Although MDA levels were higher in patients with poor neurological outcomes, they did not correlate directly with NSE. Probably, a single early measurement may not capture the temporal dynamics required to reveal a correlation between these markers.

Conversely, when we classified patients based on cranial CT findings, there were no statistically significant differences in the levels of the same biomarkers. However, it is essential to note that only 48 out of 71 patients (68%) underwent CT imaging, and this modality has known limitations in assessing cerebral edema and neuronal damage. Brain MRI could serve as a more sensitive tool for detecting such alterations3. On the other hand, the ONSD was greater in both eyes among patients with abnormal CT findings, supporting the utility of ultrasound-assessed ONSD in predicting CT abnormalities.

Our study did not include therapeutic hypothermia, which aligns with recent evidence indicating no additional benefit of this intervention in neurological outcomes compared to normothermic or fever-prevention temperature control. In Bro-Jeppesen et al.‘s26 randomized temperature management trial involving 82 patients at 33 °C and 81 patients at 36 °C, SDC-1 and TBML levels were not influenced by target temperature.

Two recent clinical studies addressed the use of eGC shedding biomarkers for prognostic purposes. Li et al.31 included 71 post-CA patients and found that SDC-1 levels on days 0, 1, and 3; HYAL levels on days 1 and 3; and HS levels on day 1 predicted 28-day survival. Only HS on day 1 predicted neurological outcomes at 28 days. A limitation of this study was that only eight patients had favorable neurological outcomes. This study also highlighted the distinct temporal behavior of each biomarker, underscoring the importance of measurement timing in interpreting their pathophysiological significance. He et al.32 evaluated 111 patients post-ROSC and found that elevated HYAL at 24 h was an independent risk factor for poor 30-day neurological outcomes with an OR of 2.48 (95%CI 1.65–3.72) and 30-day mortality with a HR of 2.32 (95%CI 1.77–3.03). SDC-1 predicted 30-day mortality with a HR of 1.70 (95%CI 1.03–2.79), while HS did not predict either outcome. That study had a more balanced sample, with 42 patients showing favorable and 69 showing unfavorable neurological outcomes.

Regarding other biomarkers, Bro-Jeppesen et al.26 reported that elevated TBML was associated with 30-day mortality in univariate analysis with a HR of 1.75 (95% CI 1.0–2.8), p = 0.02, but not in multivariate analysis. Orban et al.11 included 34 patients post-ROSC and observed that those with a favorable prognosis had lower levels of OS markers, particularly MDA.

Our study has some limitations. It is a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. Lack of prehospital care data precluded calculation of no-flow and low-flow times. Biomarker measurements were performed only once within the first 24 h after ROSC. Consequently, we were unable to capture the temporal evolution of these markers, which may have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamic interplay between eGC shedding, OS, and neuronal injury. Unfortunately, NSE levels were measured only within the first 24 h. For this reason, we chose not to include NSE in the multivariable analysis, as doing so could be misleading. Studies that validated NSE as a predictor of neurological outcomes typically measured it within the 48–72-hour window post-CA. Cranial CT was performed in only a subset of patients, likely those with delayed recovery of consciousness, in whom imaging was more likely to reveal abnormalities. ONSD measurements were also conducted only within the first 24 h, which may have been too early to reflect the findings reported in the literature. Nonetheless, this is the first study to simultaneously assess multiple biomarkers representing various pathophysiological mechanisms—including eGC shedding and OS —in the context of PCAS and to correlate these biomarkers with neuronal injury markers such as NSE, cranial CT, ONSD ultrasound, and neurological prognosis.

Conclusion

Our findings underscore the interplay between eGC shedding and oxidative stress in mediating neuronal injury following CA. Moreover, early measured (24 h) biomarkers, as the SDC-1 (a marker of eGC shedding), TBML (a marker of endothelial damage), and MDA (a marker of OS) were independent predictors of unfavorable neurological outcomes. Multicenter studies with larger patient cohorts are warranted to validate these biomarkers’ prognostic utility in supporting clinical decision-making regarding the continuation or withdrawal of life-sustaining therapies. Furthermore, future studies should focus on therapeutic strategies that preserve and restore the eGC while simultaneously neutralizing ROS, as such combined interventions may represent a promising approach to prevent neuronal injury and improve neurological outcomes following CA.
