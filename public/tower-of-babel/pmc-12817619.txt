Age-dependent recovery of white matter integrity after surgical correction in children with infantile esotropia
Authors: Yue Zhang, Yue Wang, Jianlin Guo, Tianyu Fang, Rong Wang, Weijin Liu, Xin Zhao, Qiuyun Fan, Yuanyuan Chen, Yun Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfantile esotropia may interfere with white matter maturation during early childhood, a critical period of brain development. Surgical correction not only restores ocular alignment but may also influence neurodevelopmental trajectories. However, the role of age in modulating white matter recovery after surgery remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the effects of age on white matter rehabilitation following surgical intervention in children with infantile esotropia, with the goal of identifying the optimal therapeutic window to maximize both neurodevelopmental and clinical outcomes.MethodsWe included 29 typically developing children (F/M = 14/15) and 30 children with IE (F/M = 13/17), 17 of whom provided longitudinal data following surgical intervention. All participants underwent MRI scanning and clinical assessments. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) was performed to quantify white matter integrity using fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD). Automated fiber quantification was applied to analyze microstructural properties across 20 major white matter tracts. Cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses were conducted to evaluate developmental trajectories in patients versus controls.ResultsPreoperatively, IE patients exhibited significantly elevated MD across multiple tracts, including the thalamic radiation and forceps minor. Following surgery, MD values decreased significantly in most tracts. FA alterations were less pronounced, with preoperative reductions and postoperative improvement limited to only a few tracts. In controls, age was negatively correlated with MD and FA changes. Longitudinal analysis revealed that surgical intervention was associated with accelerated growth in white matter microstructure compared to typical development, particularly in younger children.ConclusionsSurgical correction of IE facilitates white matter restoration through mechanisms that operate independently of, and synergistically with, typical neurodevelopment. Earlier intervention is associated with faster rates of microstructural recovery, suggesting a higher sensitive period during which surgery can maximize white matter repair and optimize functional outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04578-7.

Introduction

Infantile esotropia is a common congenital ocular disorder characterized by an inward deviation of the eyes, typically manifesting during early childhood [1–3]. Traditionally, studies on infantile esotropia have focused on clinical manifestations such as ocular misalignment and impaired binocular vision [2–5]. However, increasing evidence suggests that abnormal visual experience in early life may have broader implications for brain development, which is not fully understood, particularly in the maturation of white matter microstructure [4, 6–9].

During the critical period before 6 years of age, the brain white matter undergoes rapid developmental processes including myelination, axonal growth, and the reorganization of neural networks [10–12]. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) has emerged as a powerful, noninvasive tool to quantify these microstructural changes of white matter by indices such as mean diffusivity (MD) and fractional anisotropy (FA). These metrics provide insights into the integrity and organization of white matter fiber tracts and have been extensively used to study neurodevelopment in both typical and atypical populations [13–16]. Several studies have shown that visual disorders such as amblyopia and strabismus are associated with changes in white matter microstructure along the visual pathways. For example, patients with strabismus show altered FA values in the visual cortex and related white matter tracts, and surgical intervention may partly reverse these changes [17–19]. Other studies have reported neural plasticity following corrective surgery, suggesting that improved visual input can promote structural reorganization [19, 20]. These findings highlight the potential for surgical repair to affect not only eye alignment but also typical neural development.

In contrast, longitudinal studies of typically developing children have consistently shown that MD decreases and FA increases with age, reflecting the natural maturation of white matter [21–23]. However, it remains unclear whether abnormal visual input due to infantile esotropia disrupts these typical developmental patterns, or to what extent surgical correction can restore them. Recent efforts to distinguish the effects of natural development from those of surgery have provided promising insights [19, 24]. The relationship between disease recovery and neurodevelopment is complex. Surgery may influence not only ocular alignment but also the development of visual processing areas in the brain. Although early intervention may optimize neural plasticity, the connection between the timing of surgery and a child’s overall neurodevelopment is still not well understood [19, 25]. A better understanding of these factors could improve individualized treatment plans, ensuring that both visual and neurodevelopmental milestones are met without compromising the child’s growth and rehabilitation [7, 19, 25]. Therefore, further research is necessary to determine the optimal age for surgery and to understand how recovery from infantile esotropia interacts with the child’s broader developmental pathway.

This study aims to address these questions by using DTI to evaluate white matter microstructural changes in children with infantile esotropia. We compare MD and FA values between an age-matched normal control group and children with infantile esotropia, including longitudinal analysis of pre- and post-operative data. We identify disease-related alterations in white matter integrity and assess their relationship with the severity of strabismus. We also explore age-dependent variability in response to surgery, comparing it with typical age-related changes in healthy controls. By combining cross-sectional and longitudinal approaches, this study seeks to clarify whether and how surgical intervention interacts with ongoing brain development in infantile esotropia. Understanding this relationship is essential for defining the best timing for treatment and for developing personalized strategies that utilize the developing brain’s natural plasticity.

Methods

Participants and data collection

Twenty-nine normal controls(F/M = 14/15), and thirty patients with infantile esotropia(F/M = 13/17), including seventeen participants(F/M = 6/11) with both preoperative and postoperative data were collected in this study. The inclusion criteria for IE were based on inward deviation of both eyes with the same degree, onset age no more than six months and absence of additional neurological disorders, no history of mental illness, no history of head trauma, and no history of drug or alcohol abuse. Other exclusion criteria included the presence of any serious medical conditions, certain image artifacts and pitfalls that would have affected the results of the study. All patients with IE underwent strabismus surgery the next day after baseline MRI scans, among whom seventeen patients had postoperative follow-up visits with an average follow-up period of 11.81 ± 3.04 months. Age- and sex- matched healthy participants were recruited with inclusion criteria as follows: no evidence of ocular diseases, absence of neurological or psychological disorders, and capable of completing MRI examination. This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Child Hospital (approval number: IEC-C-006-A04-V.06). All participants signed informed consent prior to the start of the study, understanding and agreeing to participate in all procedures of the study. This study conducted an analysis of age disparities between the normal control group and the infantile esotropia group, as well as the preoperative and postoperative cohorts. Utilizing rigorous statistical tests, age comparisons between the normal control group and the disease group revealed no significant differences (details in Table 1).

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

All data was acquired at Beijing Children’s Hospital (Capital Medical University) on a General Electric Discovery MR750 3T scanner with an 8-channel head coil. All participants underwent scanning during natural sleep. Earplugs and spongy cushions were used to cut down on the noise and minimize head motion during scanning. A camera facing the scanner bed was applied to monitor the sleeping behaviors of the subjects. T1-weighted, sagittal three-dimension brain volume sequence was acquired with following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 8.2ms, echo time (TE) = 3.2ms, flip angle = 12°, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256mm2, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1 mm, voxel size = 1 mm×1 mm×1 mm, number of slices = 164. Diffusion MRI data were collected using a spin-echo echo planar imaging (SE-EPI) sequence with the following parameters: TR = 6900ms, TE = 60.4ms, flip angle = 90°, FOV = 256 × 256mm2, acquisition matrix = 128 × 128, slice thickness = 2 mm, number of slices = 69, voxel size = 2 mm×2 mm×2 mm, 32 diffusion gradient direction images with the b-value of 1000 s/m2 and a single image with the b-value of 0 s/m2.

All DTI and 3D-T1 data were preprocessed using the FMRIB Software Library (FSL, https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl) [26] and Automated Fiber Quantification toolbox (AFQ, https://github.com/yeatmanlab/AFQ) in MATLAB R2022a. For DTI images, the preprocessing include the following steps: (1) correcting for eddy currents distortions; (2) generating individual brain masks using the brain extraction tool (BET) based on the B0 images of each subject; (3) Framewise displacement (FD) was used to assess the head motion, and datasets with mean FD bigger than 0.3 mm were excluded in the following analysis (Power et al., 2014); (4) calculating the DTI matrix with the DTIFIT from FSL, which provides FA, MD, axial diffusivity (AD), radial diffusivity (RD), eigenvalues (λ1, λ2, and λ3), eigenvectors (V1, V2, and V3), and a raw T2 image without diffusion weighting. For 3D-T1 data, the preprocessing procedure consists of the following steps: (1) applying BET to extract the brain structure; (2) aligning the images to the anterior commissure-posterior commissure (AC-PC) plane for subsequent analysis; (3) using the dtiMakeDt6FromFsl script from the AFQ toolbox to align the T1 image to the b0 image and generate the dt6 diffusion tensor file.

Automating fiber-tract quantification

The Automated Fiber Quantification (AFQ) [27] toolbox was employed to perform white matter fiber tractography, which enables quantitative analysis of specific white matter fiber tracts and provides detailed data along each tract. The process is as follows: (1) Set the parameter structure of AFQ to whole brain tractography. Set one hundred nodes between the ROI pairs for each tract. We employ Gaussian weighting, wherein the contribution of each fiber to the measurement at a node is weighted based on its Gaussian distance from the tract core. Criteria for FA >0.2, turning angle < 30°, and fiber length ranging from 50 to 250 mm were applied. (2) Fiber tract segmentation. We classified the comprehensive brain fiber group into 20 specific fiber tracts. (3) Fiber tract cleaning. Although the generated fiber tracts adhere to the canonical definitions, some aberrant fibers still deviate from the main fascicle core. This step helps identify and eliminate these aberrant fibers. (4) Compute tract profiles. In this step, diffusion metrics (FA, MD) will be computed at each node for the section of the tract that stretches between the two defining ROI locations along the spatial trajectory of each fiber tract. The twenty white matter fiber tracts included in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The independent samples t-test was employed between the normal control and the infantile esotropia groups, and paired t-tests were conducted on the longitudinal data to identify regions of interest (ROIs) exhibiting significant differences attributable to cross-sectional disease effects or to longitudinal recovery. For the analysis of MD, FA, we set a threshold for the number of nodes. Any cases where the number of continuous significant nodes were less than 8 were excluded. Fewer than liminal nodes might result in unstable and unreliable MD, FA values, affecting the accuracy of the analysis. Thus, excluding these data points helped enhance the rigor of the overall analysis. In the analysis of the relationship between age and MD, FA with multivariate linear regression, the effect of gender was first regressed in MD, FA, and then the fitted linear regression model was applied for both healthy subjects and IE patients. Linear age model and quadratic age model were fitted and compared yielding an optimal fitting one. The P-value is corrected by FDR at 0.05 for multiple comparison corrections. To analyze the interaction between infantile esotropia and age on MD and FA, a multivariate linear regression model was employed. The model included strabismus angle, age-related changes and age.

Significant ROIs with MD and FA analysis associated with disease

Groups comparisons showed widely significant increase of MD for IE before the operation in fourteen fiber tracts including thalamic radiation and callosum forceps Minor and cingulum hippocampus, and significant decrease of MD for IE after the operation among most of them (twelve of fourteen fiber tracts, details illustrated in Fig. 1). The region between the two dashed lines shows where significant differences occur. There were also several fiber tracts that only showed postoperative decrease like the bilateral corticospinal. Same comparisons for FA showed quite different results that only five fiber tracts out of eight showed both preoperative alterations and postoperative restores for IE, including bilateral thalamus radiation and left cingulum cingulate (Fig. 2). Most other fiber tracts only showed significant postoperative increase of FA in IE subjects, including cingulum cingulate, callosum forceps Minor and Inferior Longitudinal Fasciculus (Fig. 2).

Age-related effects on MD and FA for healthy subjects

As shown in Figure 3, in the normal control group, MD showed a significant negative correlation with age, whereas FA exhibited a significant positive correlation, consistent with typical developmental trajectories reported in previous studies[21-23]. To enhance the completeness of our analysis, we further evaluated age-related effects in the infantile esotropia group and the post-operative group. Similar to the NC group, the IE group demonstrated widespread age-dependent patterns, with FA increasing and MD decreasing across numerous fiber tracts. In contrast, the post-operative group showed the same direction of age–MD and age–FA associations, but with a reduced number of fibers reaching statistical significance. The corresponding R² values are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Age-dependent variability in longitudinal WM changes

In this investigation, we explored the alterations in both MD and FA values across various age brackets before and after operative intervention. By fitting the cross-sectional data and longitudinal data separately, quadratic curves to the longitudinal dataset and linear curves to the cross-sectional dataset were observed. In detail, concave locus on MD (Fig. 4a) and convex locus on FA (Fig. 4b) were obtained that vividly elucidates the age-dependent variability in MD and FA values for all the fibers with averaging the mean/median R2 = 0.62/0.66 for MD (min./max.=0.38/0.77) and the mean/median R2 = 0.47/0.52 for FA (min./max.=0.00 to 0.69). However, the fitting results on cross-sectional normal dataset showed only linear development changes on both MD and FA for all fibers (Fig. 4c-d), with averaging the mean/median R2 = 0.50/0.51 for MD (min./max.= 0.40/0.70) and the mean/median R2 = 0.25/0.24 for FA (min./max.=0.12/0.49). The longitudinal dataset also showed significant linear decrease with age for MD and increase with age for FA. Specifically, younger patients indicated more substantial deviations in both MD and FA values post-operative, whereas older patients exhibit more stability or smaller deviations from baseline values. Furthermore, the extrema of the quadratic fits across all fibers were calculated, and the results indicated consistent effects for both MD and FA. The turning-point ages were estimated to range from 4.98 to 7.09 years for MD (mean = 5.59) and from 4.82 to 6.43 years for FA (mean = 5.40).

Contrasting effects between age and strabismus angle for WM changes

In our cohort, all patients achieved postoperative orthophoria, and no residual strabismus was detected during follow-up. Positive correlation between the strabismus angle of children with IE and their longitudinal changes of MD was observed, suggesting that greater ocular misalignment is associated with increased MD alterations in brain microstructure (Fig. 5). Conversely, no significant correlation was observed between the strabismus angle and FA changes for children with IE, indicating that, within this context, FA values are not significantly modulated by the severity of strabismus. Furthermore, the study revealed a negative correlation between age-related changes and MD changes, and conversely, age-related changes showed a positive correlation with FA changes.

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into the impact of infantile esotropia (IE) on brain white matter development in children, using advanced diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). Although the control and IE groups were age-matched, a significant age difference existed between the preoperative and postoperative IE cohorts. The increased mean diffusivity (MD) observed preoperatively suggests structural impairment of white matter due to the disease. Conversely, the postoperative reduction in MD indicates a potential recovery or improvement in white matter integrity following surgical intervention. However, we acknowledge a key limitation: although age and sex were included as covariates to minimize developmental confounding, the absence of longitudinal data from a matched healthy control group limits our ability to fully separate maturational effects from surgery-related changes. The observed postoperative reductions in MD occur against a backdrop of rapid typical white matter maturation, as evidenced by the strong negative correlation between age and MD in our cross-sectional control data. Therefore, we cannot attribute the changes solely to the surgical intervention.

Despite this limitation, several lines of evidence suggest our findings reflect a genuine surgical effect superimposed on development. First, and most compellingly, we found a significant positive correlation between the preoperative strabismus angle and the magnitude of MD reduction postoperatively. This ‘dose-response’ relationship—where greater initial pathology predicts greater structural change—is unlikely to be explained by typical development alone and strongly implicates the surgical correction as a driving force. Furthermore, the trajectory of postoperative change was better described by a quadratic model, suggesting a potential acceleration of microstructural refinement beyond the linear trajectory seen in cross-sectional controls.

The structural abnormalities in the white matter of children with IE are likely attributable to disrupted neurodevelopmental processes. IE disrupts binocular vision and normal sensory integration, leading to altered visual input [28, 29]. This aberrant input can affect the development of cortical and subcortical regions responsible for visual processing and motor control [4, 7, 30]. Previous research indicates that visual deprivation in early life can impair normal brain maturation, particularly in critical pathways like the thalamic radiation and corticospinal tract [31]. The increased MD in these regions suggests that abnormal visual input in IE disrupts axonal integrity and myelination. This may reflect a broader developmental delay in neural networks, potentially contributing to the cognitive and motor deficits sometimes observed in these children. The severity of visual misalignment likely exacerbates these structural changes, underscoring the existence of a critical period during which the brain is highly vulnerable to abnormal visual experience.

A key finding of this study is the significant improvement in white matter integrity following surgery, indicating that surgical intervention can mitigate the structural damage caused by IE. The reduction in MD across multiple brain fiber tracts postoperatively points to a restoration of typical white matter architecture, likely due to the correction of ocular misalignment and the reinstatement of binocular vision. These results align with studies of other neurodevelopmental disorders where early intervention helps re-establish normal developmental trajectories [29, 32]. To further interpret the unexpected FA increases observed in certain tracts of the IE group, we additionally examined axial diffusivity (AD) and radial diffusivity (RD) in the regions where FA values were significantly higher in IE than in controls. The supplementary analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1) revealed that in the Left Cingulum Cingulate, Left Superior Longitudinal Fasciculus (SLF), Right Inferior Fronto-Occipital Fasciculus (IFOF), Right Inferior Longitudinal Fasciculus (ILF), and Right SLF, the IE group showed lower RD and higher AD compared with the control group, which jointly contributed to elevated FA values. In contrast, the Right Thalamic Radiation exhibited similar RD but higher AD in the IE group, suggesting that its FA increase was mainly driven by enhanced axial diffusivity. These findings indicate that the FA elevation in the IE group is unlikely to be an artifact, but rather reflects region-specific microstructural reorganization. The pattern of reduced RD and increased AD may correspond to improved axonal coherence or ongoing axonal remodeling, possibly representing adaptive or compensatory plasticity following altered visual experience in early life. Similar FA increases associated with visual disorders have been reported in previous studies [33, 34], supporting the interpretation that changes in diffusion metrics may reflect experience-dependent white matter reorganization rather than simple maturation differences. Furthermore, we found a significant correlation between the strabismus angle (a measure of disease severity) and the extent of white matter alterations. More pronounced ocular misalignment was associated with greater structural changes, highlighting the importance of early correction to protect the neural networks underlying visual and cognitive functions [9, 35, 36]. This finding emphasizes the need for a tailored treatment approach where the timing and strategy of intervention are customized according to individual disease characteristics.

Although the optic radiation (OR) plays an essential role in visual processing and could be affected in strabismus, our results did not reveal significant differences in diffusion metrics (FA and MD) between groups or across pre- and postoperative stages. This finding may indicate that, in infantile esotropia, the structural integrity of the OR is largely preserved, and the observed alterations in other tracts (e.g., SLF, IFOF) may reflect secondary or compensatory changes rather than direct damage to the visual projection pathways. Future studies with higher-resolution diffusion data or functional connectivity analyses could further clarify whether subtle microstructural or functional changes exist within the OR.

Another compelling result is the better model fit between postoperative white matter changes and the typical developmental trajectory compared to the preoperative state. This suggests that postoperative recovery does not merely represent a return to a baseline state but rather an active process that may enhance typical brain maturation. The convergence of postoperative and typical developmental trajectories implies that surgical intervention, particularly when performed early, may stimulate neuroplasticity [37, 38]. This plasticity could facilitate adaptive changes in the brain’s structural connectivity, promoting recovery in previously impaired areas. These findings support the concept that the brain possesses an inherent capacity to reorganize itself in response to functional disruptions [39]. In this context, the combined effects of surgery and natural development may create a window of opportunity for improving white matter integrity. The greater recovery observed in younger children [7, 40] further supports the notion that earlier intervention harnesses the heightened plasticity of the developing brain, suggesting that the age at surgery is a crucial determinant of therapeutic success.

This study underscores the critical importance of early intervention in children with IE. The significant postoperative recovery in white matter integrity demonstrates the potential for neuroplasticity in response to surgical treatment. Timely intervention can correct visual misalignment and mitigate its neurodevelopmental consequences [7, 40]. The correlation between strabismus angle and white matter alterations highlights the need for individualized treatment plans based on disease severity. Future clinical strategies could incorporate early neuroimaging assessments to guide the timing and approach of surgical interventions, potentially optimizing outcomes. Monitoring the trajectory of white matter recovery post-surgery could also serve as a valuable biomarker for assessing treatment efficacy and guiding long-term care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study enhances our understanding of the neurodevelopmental impact of infantile esotropia. It provides insights into the mechanisms underlying white matter changes, the recovery process following surgery, and the critical role of early intervention. Future research is needed to explore the long-term effects of surgical treatment on brain development and to identify other factors influencing recovery, such as genetic predispositions or environmental influences.
