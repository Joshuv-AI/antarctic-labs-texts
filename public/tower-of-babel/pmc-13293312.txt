Theoretical Clinical Utility of Advanced Practice Provider Use of an Artificial Intelligence Radiomics-Based Tool for Pulmonary Nodule Evaluation and Management
Authors: Roger Y Kim, Annika Rings, Lyndsey C Pickup, Omar Ibrahim, Anil Vachani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs integral members of pulmonary nodule (PN) programs, advanced practice providers (APPs) routinely evaluate PN cancer risk and make management recommendations. It is unknown whether an artificial intelligence (AI) tool impacts APP PN assessment and decision-making.Research QuestionWhat is the theoretical effect of APP use of a commercially available AI radiomics-based computer-aided diagnosis tool on PN diagnostic accuracy and management decision-making?Study Design and MethodsIn this retrospective, multireader, multicase study performed from May 2024 to June 2024, 6 APP readers (4 in pulmonology and 2 in thoracic surgery) independently evaluated 300 chest CT scan cases, each with an indeterminate PN 5 to 30 mm in maximal diameter (50% cancer prevalence). Using solely CT imaging data, APPs provided an estimate of cancer risk and management recommendation for each case without and then with AI tool assistance. The effect of the AI tool on readers’ diagnostic performance and management decisions was assessed using descriptive statistics, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and reclassification plots and tables.ResultsWith AI tool assistance, APP readers’ average PN diagnostic accuracy increased by 9 percentage points (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, 0.79 vs 0.88; P < .001). A higher proportion of malignant PNs were classified as high (> 65%) risk (63% vs 46%, P < .001) and recommended for a lung biopsy or surgical resection (72% vs 55%, P < .001) with AI tool assistance. Although benign PNs were more often classified as low (< 5%) risk (39% vs 34%, P < .001) with AI tool assistance, there was no statistically significant difference in the proportion recommended for an invasive procedure (18% vs 17%, P = .30).InterpretationOur results show that APP use of a commercially available AI radiomics-based tool for PN evaluation was associated with increased diagnostic accuracy and invasive diagnostic procedure recommendation for malignant PNs. Future prospective, randomized clinical trials are required to assess its use in routine clinical practice.Key Words: artificial intelligence, clinical decision-making, computer-assisted decision-making, early detection of cancer, pulmonary nodule, risk stratification

Take-Home Points

Study Question: Does the use of a commercially available artificial intelligence (AI) radiomics-based computer-aided diagnosis tool improve advanced practice provider (APP) diagnostic accuracy and medical decision-making when evaluating pulmonary nodules?

Results: In this retrospective, multireader, multicase study, use of the AI tool was associated with a 9 percentage point increase in average APP pulmonary nodule diagnostic accuracy (area under the receiver operating curve, 0.79 vs 0.88; P < .001), and a higher proportion of malignant PNs classified as high (> 65%) risk (63% vs 46%, P < .001) and recommended for a lung biopsy or surgical resection (72% vs 55%, P < .001).

Interpretation: Theoretical use of a commercially available AI radiomics-based tool for pulmonary nodule evaluation was associated with increased APP diagnostic accuracy and appropriate management recommendations.

In the pursuit of diagnostic excellence, pulmonary nodule (PN) programs require multidisciplinary collaboration across medical and surgical disciplines and clinical roles to accurately, efficiently, and safely evaluate and treat patients with PNs.1 Advanced practice providers (APPs) are integral members of many PN programs and work independently or alongside physicians to assess risk and make management decisions for patients with indeterminate PNs.2,3 Accurate cancer risk assessment is crucial because it directly impacts medical decision-making for next steps in diagnostic evaluation.4 Per the American College of Chest Physicians (CHEST) clinical practice guidelines, low-risk PNs (< 5% cancer risk) should be managed with surveillance chest CT imaging, whereas high-risk PNs (> 65% cancer risk) should be considered for lung biopsy or surgical resection.5 However, quantification of PN cancer risk is challenging, and clinical risk prediction models do not outperform routine clinical risk assessment.6

As such, several novel PN biomarkers using data from peripheral blood, nasal swabs, or CT imaging have been developed to assist clinicians in determining PN cancer risk.7,8 Among the most thoroughly examined and externally validated is a commercially available artificial intelligence (AI) radiomics-based tool that analyzes CT images to generate a cancer risk estimate.9,10 Prior studies have demonstrated that this AI tool may improve physicians’ PN cancer risk assessment and medical decision-making, but its use among APPs has not yet been investigated.11, 12, 13 The objective of this study was to determine the impact of this AI tool on diagnostic accuracy and management recommendations when used by APPs practicing within PN programs. Some of the results of this study have been previously reported in the form of an abstact.14

Study Design and Methods

This was a retrospective, multireader, multicase study in which 6 APP readers each independently evaluated 300 chest CT scans with an indeterminate PN first without and then with assistance from the AI tool.15 Because completely deidentified imaging data were used, institutional review board approval and the need for informed consent was not required for this study. Readers completed case evaluation between May 2024 and June 2024, and data analysis was performed from September 2024 to June 2025.

Case Selection

Details on image acquisition and case selection used in this study have previously been described.11 Briefly, cases were randomly selected from a repository of deidentified imaging data including diagnostic and screening chest CT scans retrospectively collected from community and academic sites in the US (Henry Ford Health System and Vanderbilt University) and UK (Royal Berkshire Hospital, Leeds Teaching Hospital, Nottingham University Hospital, and Oxford University Hospitals) with local institutional review board approval and from National Lung Screening Trial data obtained through the National Cancer Institute Cancer Data Access System. No images from training data sets used for development of the AI tool were included in this study. Each of the 300 chest CT scans in the final data set contained a single PN 5 to 30 mm in maximal diameter with a final diagnosis categorized as either malignant or benign based on histologic evaluation when available. Cases without a definitive histologic diagnosis (ie, those managed with surveillance imaging or with a nondiagnostic biopsy) were defined as benign if follow-up imaging showed complete PN resolution or ≥ 2-year diameter stability. Cancer prevalence was 50% among the 300 PN cases (150 malignant and 150 benign).

AI Tool Details

The AI tool assessed in this study was the Lung Cancer Prediction Convolutional Neural Network, a radiomics-based computer-aided diagnosis model that uses solely CT imaging data to estimate cancer risk and is part of a commercially available software package (Virtual Nodule Clinic; Optellum).16,17 Descriptions of AI tool development and external validation have been published previously.9,10 The AI tool generates a Lung Cancer Prediction (LCP) score between 1 and 10 corresponding to deciles of cancer risk, with higher values indicating increased predicted cancer probability (e-Fig 1).11 In a prior validation study with a PN population with 30% cancer prevalence, LCP scores represent the following estimates of cancer probability: 1: 0.2%, 2: 0.4%, 3: 0.8%, 4: 2.0%, 5: 5.6%, 6: 15.0%, 7: 34.0%, 8: 64.0%, 9: 84.0%, and 10: 93.0%.16

Readers and Reading Procedures

The 6 APP readers had current nurse practitioner licenses, actively practiced in US pulmonology or thoracic surgery outpatient clinics within the context of a PN program, and possessed varying levels of experience (e-Table 1). Each reader received 1 hour of training on how to use the AI tool software and interpret LCP scores and were required to practice on 20 sample cases before evaluating images included in the analysis. These 20 practice cases were distinct from the 300 cases included in the analysis. Readers were informed that the cancer prevalence among the 300 cases was 50%, and cases were randomly ordered for each reader. For each case, readers used the AI tool software to scroll through axial chest CT images that had the PN to be reviewed highlighted. Readers were not explicitly told which scans were screening low-dose CT scans and which were diagnostic CT scans, but characteristics such as slice thickness and image quality could be ascertained on personal review of the images. Aside from CT images, readers were not provided any additional clinical information (eg, age, family history, smoking history). Readers were then required to record their estimate of PN cancer risk (0%-100%) and their PN management recommendation (no action, long-term [≥ 6 months] CT follow-up, short-term [6 weeks to 6 months] CT follow-up, immediate imaging follow-up [eg, PET/CT scan], nonsurgical lung biopsy [eg, bronchoscopy], or surgical resection). These responses were then locked when readers clicked on the PN of interest, generating the LCP score. After reviewing the LCP score generated by the AI tool, readers recorded their updated estimates of cancer risk and management recommendation as before. All cases were evaluated independently by each reader, and no time limit was imposed.

Statistical Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to evaluate PN case characteristics, reader cancer risk estimates, and PN management recommendations. Continuous variables were described using averages and SDs, and categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. For each reader, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated using data from all 300 cases before and after AI tool use. Next, these AUCs were averaged across all readers. The primary outcome was change in average reader diagnostic accuracy with and without AI tool assistance, measured by differences in average reader AUC before and after AI tool use. We used the Obuchowski-Rockette method for analyzing multireader, multicase study data and the MRMCaov library to test the null hypothesis that average reader AUC was equivalent with and without AI assistance.18, 19, 20 The Obuchowski-Rockette method includes assumptions for equicovariant error terms between readers and accounts for the fact that the same cases were evaluated by each reader. We calculated 95% CIs using a parametric estimate with the observed variances. Based on effect size and variance estimates from a prior multireader, multicase study using the same 300 cases as the present study,11 we used the R software package RJafroc (R Foundation) to perform power calculations for the Obuchowski-Rockette method.18 With 300 cases, 6 readers, and a 2-tailed alpha level of 0.05, there was 98% power to detect a 7 percentage point difference in average reader AUC with and without AI tool assistance. We additionally performed secondary analyses comparing average reader AUC stratified by individual reader, type of chest CT imaging (diagnostic vs screening), PN diameter (5 to < 10 mm vs ≥ 10 to 30 mm), density (solid vs part-solid), and margins (nonspiculated vs spiculated).

Stratified by PN final diagnosis, we summarized differences in average reader cancer risk estimates with and without AI tool assistance using the paired t test and differences in cancer risk categories at the 5% and 65% thresholds and management recommendations (noninvasive follow-up vs invasive diagnostic procedure) using McNemar test. Reclassification plots were used to display changes in reader cancer risk estimates with AI tool assistance, whereas a reclassification table was used to describe changes in PN management recommendations.

Statistical significance was defined with a 2-sided P < .05, without adjustments for multiple comparisons given the discrete null hypotheses being tested by each statistical test.21,22 Analyses were performed using R software, version 4.1.2 (R Foundation) and Stata/MP, version 18.0 (StataCorp).

Results

Table 1 summarizes characteristics of the 300 PN cases stratified by final diagnosis. Among the 150 malignant PNs, the average maximal diameter ± SD was 14 ± 5.6 mm, and most were solid (132 [82%]) and spiculated (80 [53%]). Of the 150 benign PNs, the average diameter ± SD was 10 ± 5.2 mm, and 122 (81%) were solid and 28 (19%) were spiculated. With AI tool assistance, average APP reader PN diagnostic accuracy increased from an AUC of 0.79 (95% CI, 0.74-0.84) to 0.88 (95% CI, 0.84-0.92), a statistically significant 9 percentage point improvement (P < .001) (Fig 1). In prespecified subgroup analyses, there were statistically significant increases in average diagnostic accuracy for each APP reader (e-Table 2) and by PN diameter, density, margins, and type of chest CT scan (e-Table 3).

Among malignant PN cases, the average LCP score ± SD was 8.5 ± 1.6, and average reader cancer risk estimates ± SD increased from 56% ± 32% to 69% ± 29% with AI tool assistance (P < .001) (e-Table 4, Table 2). Overall, a higher proportion of malignant PN cases were classified as high (> 65%) risk (63% vs 46%, P < .001) with AI assistance. Of the 484 malignant PN cases readers initially estimated to have ≤ 65% cancer risk, 176 (36%) were reclassified to > 65% risk with AI tool assistance, whereas 21 of the 416 malignant PNs (5%) initially assessed as > 65% risk were reclassified to ≤ 65% (Fig 2A). Collectively, a higher proportion of malignant PNs were recommended for lung biopsy or resection (72% vs 55%, P < .001) with AI tool assistance. Readers initially recommended 406 malignant PN cases for noninvasive follow-up (ie, no action, CT surveillance, or PET/CT imaging), but changed their recommendation to an invasive diagnostic procedure (ie, lung biopsy, surgical resection) in 164 cases (40%) with AI tool assistance (Table 4). In contrast, among 494 malignant PNs initially recommended for an invasive diagnostic procedure, only 14 (3%) were instead managed with noninvasive follow-up with AI assistance. In cases where AI tool assistance resulted in a change in management from noninvasive follow-up to an invasive diagnostic procedure, the cancer prevalence was 82% (164 of 201), whereas in cases in which AI assistance resulted in the opposite management change, 33% (14 of 43) were cancer.

Among benign PN cases, the average LCP score ± SD was 4.6 ± 2.5, and average reader cancer risk estimates ± SD decreased from 24% ± 28% to 22% ± 27% with AI tool assistance (P < .001) (e-Table 5, Table 3). Overall, benign PNs were more often classified as low (< 5%) risk (39% vs 34%, P < .001) with AI tool assistance. Of the 591 benign PN cases readers initially estimated to have ≥ 5% risk, 89 (15%) were reclassified to < 5% risk with AI tool assistance, whereas 47 of the 309 benign PNs (15%) initially assessed as < 5% risk were reclassified to ≥ 5% risk (Fig 2B). There was no statistically significant difference in the proportion of benign PN cases recommended for an invasive procedure (18% vs 17%, P = .30). Collectively, readers initially recommended 155 benign PNs for an invasive diagnostic procedure and changed their recommendation to noninvasive follow-up in 29 cases (19%) with AI assistance (Table 4). On the other hand, of the 745 benign PN cases initially managed with noninvasive follow-up, 37 (5%) were instead recommended for invasive diagnostic procedures with AI assistance. Among all 807 PN cases recommended for invasive diagnostic procedures with AI tool assistance, 163 (20%) had a benign final diagnosis.

Discussion

In this retrospective, multireader, multicase study evaluating the impact of an AI radiomics-based computer-aided diagnosis tool on APP PN diagnostic accuracy and management decision-making, we found a 9 percentage point improvement in average diagnostic accuracy, and a higher proportion of malignant PNs were appropriately recommended for lung biopsy or surgical resection. With AI tool assistance, APPs reclassified malignant PNs from ≤ 65% risk to high (> 65%) risk in 36% of cases. This corresponded to a commensurate improvement in management decision-making, with 40% of malignant PN cases initially recommended for less intensive noninvasive follow-up instead being managed with more immediate invasive diagnostic procedures. Overall, these results suggest that use of an AI tool may provide clinically meaningful benefits to APPs for PN cancer risk assessment and management.

To our knowledge, this is the first study assessing PN evaluation and decision-making to focus specifically on APPs, highlighting a major misalignment between the medical literature and real-world clinical practice given the significant role APPs play in many PN programs.1, 2, 3 Notably, the 9 percentage point increase in diagnostic accuracy (AUC, 0.79 vs 0.88) among APPs in pulmonology and thoracic surgery in the current analysis was similar to the 7 percentage point improvement (AUC, 0.82 vs 0.89) previously observed among physician specialists in pulmonology and radiology in a distinct multireader, multicase study.11 Taken together, these results suggest that the AI tool may have broad clinical utility across both clinical roles and subspecialty practice and could play a role within the context of a multidisciplinary PN program.

Importantly, improvements in APP diagnostic accuracy also correlated with clinically relevant changes in PN cancer risk categorization and medical decision-making. In alignment with current American College of Chest Physicians (CHEST) PN clinical practice guidelines,5 we found that higher proportions of cancer risk estimates > 65% corresponded to management recommendations for lung biopsy or surgical resection, which could theoretically reduce time to a cancer diagnosis and expedite treatment initiation. While prior studies evaluating PN biomarkers have demonstrated improvements in PN risk assessment,11,13,23, 24, 25 few have been able to link these findings to meaningful changes in management decisions.12,26 That said, there are currently no published studies prospectively evaluating the impact of a PN biomarker on management decisions; however, there are several studies currently underway.27, 28, 29, 30 In fact, there are currently 2 actively enrolling pragmatic randomized clinical trials (NCT05968898, NCT06638398) investigating the clinical effectiveness of the AI tool assessed in the present study.27,28

Although we found a significant change in APP risk categorization and management decisions with AI tool assistance among malignant PN cases, we did not identify a comparable impact on benign PN management. An equivalent proportion of benign PN cases (15%) were reclassified from ≥ 5% risk to < 5% as were reclassified from < 5% to ≥ 5% risk, corresponding to no statistically significant difference in management recommendations with use of the AI tool. These results are consistent with prior studies assessing this AI tool among physicians.11, 12, 13 However, it is notable that only 24% of all PN cases recommended for an invasive diagnostic procedure at baseline (without AI tool assistance) had a benign final diagnosis. This proportion of benign PNs is considerably lower than the 30% to 40% range that has been historically reported in studies including PN cohorts undergoing invasive diagnostic procedures.13,31, 32, 33 In fact, although not statistically significant, the benign proportion of invasively managed PNs fell from 24% to 20% with AI tool assistance. When considered within the context of the existing literature, these results suggest that the APP readers included in this study may have had a higher cancer risk threshold for recommending invasive diagnostic procedures. In other words, because so few benign PN cases (17% [155 of 900]) were managed invasively even before using the AI tool, this study’s ability to detect a significant change in management decision-making in this group was likely limited.

This study has limitations. First, due to both its retrospective design and its use of solely CT imaging data without key demographic and clinical information, it was only able to evaluate the theoretical clinical utility of using the AI tool for PN evaluation and management, and future prospective studies analyzing its use as part of routine clinical care are required to corroborate these results. As previously mentioned, there are already 2 pragmatic randomized clinical trials currently underway, which will serve to fill this gap in the literature.27,28 Second, because APP readers were informed of both the 50% cancer prevalence among the 300 PN cases and the 30% cancer prevalence in the validation population from which the LCP score was derived before participating in the study, there was a risk of context bias, which could have affected readers’ cancer risk estimates and management decisions.34 Thus, the results of this study may not be generalizable to patient populations with distinct cancer prevalences. That said, as previously discussed, we observed a lower than expected rate of benign PN invasive procedural management despite this potential bias toward inflated cancer risk estimation. Third, there was no delay between reader PN assessments before and after using the AI tool, which could have exaggerated the impact of the AI tool via rereading bias. However, to practicably perform this multireader, multicase study with 300 cases within the prespecified time frame, we decided not to include a built-in washout period. Additionally, we followed all recommendations for performing a high-quality, multireader, multicase study.15 Fourth, because this study focused solely on initial PN presentation, it only evaluated PN evaluation and management at a single time point without incorporating follow-up imaging data; therefore, we were unable to evaluate the longitudinal impact of management recommendations. However, a prior study has suggested that the AI tool may be helpful during serial examinations as well.35 Finally, although our primary analysis comparing average reader diagnostic accuracy via the AUC accounted for correlation between readers and cases within the context of a multireader, multicase study,18 our descriptive analyses exploring reclassification of cancer risk and management recommendations were unable to fully account for correlation across readers among the 300 cases. To mitigate against overinterpretation of these results and ensure transparency of the data we collected, we reported all summary-level data for each individual APP reader. As such, these results should be interpreted within the context of this study’s multireader, multicase design and are not necessarily generalizable to other clinical contexts with different clinicians and PN cases.

Interpretation

This study found that when APPs used a commercially available AI radiomics-based computer-aided diagnosis tool when evaluating PNs on CT imaging, average diagnostic accuracy increased, and malignant PNs were more likely to be classified as high risk and managed with invasive diagnostic procedures. To our knowledge, these findings are the first to describe the potential clinical utility of an AI tool used by APPs to evaluate and manage PNs and provide additional support for clinical implementation of this tool. Future prospective clinical studies will be critical to evaluate the clinical impact of this AI tool in real-world practice.
