Genome-wide analysis and characterization of HSP gene families (HSP20, HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, HSP90) in the yellow fever mosquito (Aedes aegypti) (Diptera: Culicidae)
Authors: Murat Turan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe heat shock protein (HSP) gene families, present across prokaryotes to eukaryotes, play vital roles in growth, development, and heat resistance processes. While HSP proteins have been identified and characterized in various species, this study achieved the first genome-wide identification and characterization of HSP proteins in the Aedes aegypti genome. This study identified and assessed 80 potential HSP genes in Ae. aegypti. The phylogenetic relationships of HSP genes were investigated in Ae. aegypti, Anopheles stephensi, and Drosophila melanogaster. Additionally, the structural features, chromosomal locations, protein characteristics, 3D structure, protein–protein interactions, and microsatellites associated with HSP proteins were examined in Ae. aegypti. The phylogenetic analysis of HSP gene families revealed distinct intra-group relationships for each HSP group. Each family exhibited relatively conserved genetic structures and motif components. In the expression analysis of growth and development, high expression was observed in certain HSP20 and HSP70 genes, while others exhibited low expression. Notably, sex-dependent expression differences were observed, particularly in HSP20 genes. These findings, the relationships, evolution, and modification of HSP gene families are illuminated by these comprehensive findings, and a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying growth, development, and heat resistance in vector organisms is facilitated.

Introduction

Aedes aegypti L. (Diptera: Culicidae), also known as the yellow fever mosquito, is a medically important vector found in more than 129 countries (da Silveira et al. 2019, Ahebwa et al. 2023, Villegas et al. 2023). Aedes aegypti larvae develop in stagnant water and interact with microbial communities in their environment. The gut microbiota of adult individuals largely consists of microorganisms acquired from this environment, and their digestive system also hosts other bacterial communities. Aedes aegypti serves as a vector for many human arboviral pathogens, including dengue, Zika, chikungunya, and yellow fever, affecting millions of people annually and resulting in tens of thousands of deaths (Patterson et al. 2016, Leta et al. 2018). These diseases are generally prevalent in tropical regions, but their distribution is expanding due to climate change and increased global travel (Finetti et al. 2023).

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) estimates that global temperatures will rise by an average of 1.5 °C between 2030 and 2052, with serious implications for insect biodiversity. Specifically, if the temperature increase reaches 2 °C by the year 2100, approximately 18% of the known 19,848 insect species will lose their current habitats (Hoegh-Guldberg et al. 2022, IPCC 2022). Such shifts will strain insect adaptive capacities; while some species may successfully expand their habitats, others will be forced to adapt or migrate (Lancaster et al. 2016). Understanding how different insect species within the same habitat react at the molecular level to temperature changes is critical for assessing broader ecological impacts (Patterson et al. 2016).

Insects are ectothermic organisms with fast adaptation and reproductive capabilities. However, their ability to regulate body temperature is limited at extremely low and high temperatures outside their optimal ranges (Ayres and Schneider 2009, Bodlah et al. 2023). Climate change has the potential to directly affect insect developmental processes, population dynamics, and feeding habits (Bodlah et al. 2023). Specifically, heat stress can disrupt proteins, membrane lipids, and DNA, leading to cellular and tissue damage (Abram et al. 2017, Bodlah et al. 2023). In hot climate conditions, it has been observed that molecular regulators lead to higher energy consumption and insects show varied responses in physiological traits like growth, development, and movement when exposed to heat stress (Sunday et al. 2014, Fan et al. 2017, Bodlah et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2023).

Organisms often synthesize a series of heat shock proteins (HSPs) that act as molecular chaperones when exposed to cold, heat, CO2, heavy metals, and various chemicals (Huang et al. 2009). Conserved across both eukaryotic and prokaryotic organisms, these proteins assist in the correct folding of misfolded proteins and prevent aggregation (Huang et al. 2007, Zhu et al. 2013, Park and Seo 2015, De Leo et al. 2016, Chen et al. 2021). Under heat stress, inducible HSPs like HSP70 rapidly participate in protein translation to repair misfolded proteins. These HSPs continue this repair work even after temperatures return to normal and eventually revert to regular transcription pathways. Beyond stress response, HSP70, HSP60, and small HSPs play roles in various physiological processes like diapause (Gu et al. 2012). The expression of HSP genes can increase survival rates against temperature changes or other environmental stress factors, thus affecting an organism’s adaptability and survival in specific habitats (Alghamdi and Alattal 2023). For example, a study in Drosophila melanogaster L. (Diptera: Culicidae) has indicated that HSPs act as a buffer against developmental disorders and play significant roles in maintaining normal phenotypes under environmental stress (Chapuis et al. 2011).

HSP proteins were first identified in D. melanogaster (Sarkar et al. 2009). These proteins are part of a broad HSP gene family, with specific functions and molecular masses varying within the family. This gene family is divided into different subfamilies, such as HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, HSP90, HSP100, and the smaller ones like HSP20. Additionally, sometimes HSP100 and HSP10 are also included in the HSP superfamily (Huang et al. 2007, 2009, Kaul and Thippeswamy 2011, Gu et al. 2012, Zhu et al. 2013, Chen et al. 2021, Alattal and Alghamdi 2023). HSP40 proteins work independently or in coordination with HSP70 proteins to facilitate ATP hydrolysis and prevent protein aggregation (Suh et al. 1998, Huang et al. 2009).

Understanding the genetic and molecular structure of insects can help us elucidate how they respond to various environmental stress factors and provide enlightening information about the functioning of species that serve as vectors for arboviral pathogens, such as Ae. aegypti. This study aims to comprehensively characterize HSP genes in Ae. aegypti, a key vector for arboviral pathogens. Using various bioinformatic tools, 240 different HSP genes (HSP20, HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90) have been identified in their families in Ae. aegypti, Anopheles stephensi Liston (Diptera: Culicidae), and D. melanogaster. This research includes the gene structures, chromosomal locations, conserved motifs, protein properties, three-dimensional structures, and protein–protein interactions. Additionally, the HSP genes in Ae. aegypti have been phylogenetically compared with those in A. stephensi and D. melanogaster for a holistic understanding. Furthermore, reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) values have been calculated based on data obtained from the NCBI project with the access number PRJNA419241 to measure the impact of HSP genes on development. This research serves as the first extensive catalog of HSP genes in Ae. aegypti and provides valuable data for future cloning, functional analyses, and the control of arboviral diseases.

Materials and Methods

Determination of HSP Genes in Ae. aegypti, A. stephensi, and D. melanogaster

In this study, the accession numbers of HSP20 (PF00011), HSP40 (PF00226), HSP60 (PF00118), HSP70 (PF00012), and HSP90 (PF00183) gene families in the genomes of Ae. aegypti (Aaeg) (Matthews et al. 2018), A. stephensi (Aste), and D. melanogaster (Dmen) (Adams et al. 2000) species were determined using the InterPro database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). In the study conducted using the obtained accession numbers, 240 genes were identified in the InsectBase 2.0 database (http://v2.insect-genome.com/). Protein sequences corresponding to these genes from the respective gene families were obtained. The existence of HSP proteins and the detection of possible additional domains within their sequences were confirmed by utilizing the SMART (Simple Modular Architecture Research Tool) database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) (Letunic et al. 2021).

Sequence Alignment and Phylogenetic Analyses

The protein sequences of the members of HSP gene families in the genomes of Ae. aegypti, A. stephensi, and D. melanogaster species were aligned using the Multiple Sequence Alignment by CLUSTALW tool (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw) (Thompson et al. 1997). The ‘slow/accurate’ option was selected during the sequence alignment process to achieve a more sensitive and accurate alignment. The Blosum weight matrix was chosen as the weight matrix for this alignment. Subsequently, the aligned protein sequences were used to construct a phylogenetic tree using the ITOL (Interactive Tree of Life) tool (https://itol.embl.de/) (Letunic and Bork 2021).

Characteristics of HSP Proteins in Ae. aegypti

The amino acid count, molecular weight (kDa), theoretical isoelectric point (pI), amino acid composition, stability, aliphatic index, and GRAVY (Grand Average of Hydropathy) of HSP proteins in Ae. aegypti were determined using the ‘ProtParam tool’ (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) (Gasteiger et al. 2005).

Structure and Chromosomal Location of HSP Genes

To better understand the evolutionary changes in the gene structures of HSP genes found in Ae. aegypti genomes and their potential effects, the exon and intron distributions were determined using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS) v2.0 database (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/) (Hu et al. 2015). During the usage of the GSDS database, the ‘.gff3’ format files obtained from the InsectBase database for gene sequences and the ‘.nwk’ (Newick) format files obtained from the CLUSTALW tool for phylogenetic tree outputs were utilized. The MapChart software was also utilized to visualize the chromosomal locations of HSP genes in Ae. aegypti species (Voorrips 2002).

Determination of Conserved Motifs

The conserved motifs in the protein sequences encoded by HSP genes were identified using the ‘Multiple EM for Motif Elicitation (MEME)’ tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/index.html) (Bailey et al. 2006). The width range of the identified motifs is between 2 and 50 amino acids, and the maximum number of motifs has been determined as 10. The motif regions were determined to be between 6 and 300 amino acids in length, and the distribution of regions was defined as any number of repetitions. The identified motifs were scanned using the default settings of the InterPro database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/search/sequence/) (Quevillon et al. 2005).

3D Structure and Protein–Protein Interactions of HSP Proteins

The three-dimensional (3D) structures of proteins encoded by HSP genes in Ae. aegypti have been predicted using the bioinformatics tool Phyre2 (Protein Homology/analogY Recognition Engine V 2.0) database (http://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/~phyre2/) (Kelley et al. 2015). After the analysis, results with confidence and coverage values below 75% were eliminated to assess the reliability of the predictions and how well they represented the protein’s actual structure. Moreover, to ascertain the interactions between the proteins encoded by HSP genes, the STRING (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins) database was utilized (https://string-db.org/) (Szklarczyk et al. 2019).

Microsatellites of HSP Proteins in Ae. aegypti

The HSPIR database was used to identify microsatellites associated with HSP genes in Ae. aegypti (http://pdslab.biochem.iisc.ernet.in/hspir/). To determine the HSP genes in Ae. aegypti, a multiple Basic Local Alignment Search Tool comparison was performed between the genes in the HSPIR database and the Ae. aegypti genome, selecting the genes that matched each other (Kumar et al. 2012).

Expression Analysis of Ae. aegypti HSP Genes

The profiles of HSP genes in Ae. aegypti were examined using Illumina RNA-seq data obtained from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database at NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra). The RNA-seq data for Ae. aegypti from 17 different developmental stages (L1 Larvae, L2 Larvae, L3 Larvae Male, L3 Larvae Female, L4 Larvae Male, L4 Larvae Female, Early Pupae Male, Early Pupae Female, Mid Pupae Male, Mid Pupae Female, Late Pupae Male, Late Pupae Female, Male Accessory Gland, Carcass Male, Carcass Female, Testes, and Ovaries) were obtained from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database using the BioProject PRJNA419241 (Matthews et al. 2018). To normalize gene expression values, the RPKM algorithm was employed, which calculates the number of reads per kilobase per million mapped reads for each exon model (Mortazavi et al. 2008). The RPKM values were converted to log2 scale, and a heatmap was generated using the CIMminer (https://discover.nci.nih.gov/cimminer/home.do) algorithm (Luna et al. 2021).

Results

Identification of HSP Genes

The HSP gene families obtained from the InsectBase database were subjected to Blast analysis using SMART, confirming the assignment of each gene to its corresponding HSP family. A total of 240 genes were identified for Ae. aegypti, A. stephensi, and D. melanogaster. Subsequently, abbreviations were created for the species names to enhance clarity in the article, and the genes were numbered according to their chromosomal sequences. Specifically, 80 genes were identified, including 18 HSP20, 32 HSP40, 9 HSP60, 17 HSP70, and 4 HSP90 genes, belonging to the Ae. aegypti species (Supplementary Material 1).

Phylogenetic Analysis of HSP Proteins

Before constructing the phylogenetic tree of HSP families, sequence alignment was performed using CLUSTALW, and the visualization was achieved using the ITOL tool. As a result of the analysis, HSP proteins were grouped within their respective subfamilies except for some members of the HSP40 sub-family and have been divided into 5 main groups, namely HSP20, HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90. It can be observed in the phylogenetic tree that each HSP family forms a distinct cluster due to their close relatedness. Some members of the HSP40 family, known to interact with HSP70, are found to be clustered together with HSP70. In the phylogenetic tree, when the orthologous relationships of HSP genes were examined between Ae. aegypti and A. stephensi, it was determined that HSP40 genes have more orthologous relationships, while D. melanogaster shows fewer orthologous relationships. This situation indicates that from an evolutionary perspective, D. melanogaster evolved earlier than the other 2 species and that speciation occurred more recently between Ae. aegypti and A. stephensi (Fig. 1).

Phylogenetic tree illustrating the relationship of HSP families in Ae. aegypti, A. stephensi, and D. melanogaster.

Characteristics of HSP Proteins

The HSP20 proteins in Ae. aegypti range in length from 178 (AaegHSP20-05) to 225 (AaegHSP20-01) amino acids. The molecular weights of the family members vary between 21 (AaegHSP20-04, AaegHSP20-05, AaegHSP20-06, AaegHSP20-07, AaegHSP20-10) and 25 (AaegHSP20-01) kDa. Among the 18 genes, only 16 possess acidic characteristics, while 2 exhibit alkaline characteristics. It has been found that the AaegHSP20-04, AaegHSP20-06, AaegHSP20-09, and AaegHSP20-10 genes are stable, while the others are unstable. Additionally, all HSP20 genes have been found to exhibit a hydrophilic structure (Supplementary Material 2).

The HSP40 proteins in Ae. aegypti range in length from 118 (AaegHSP40-24) to 2426 (AaegHSP40-09) amino acids. The molecular weights of the family members vary between 13 (AaegHSP40-24) and 276 (AaegHSP40-09) kDa. Among the 32 genes, only 1 possesses neutral characteristics, while 12 exhibit acidic characteristics and 19 exhibit alkaline characteristics. It has been found that AaegHSP40-10, AaegHSP40-11, AaegHSP40-21, AaegHSP40-18, AaegHSP40-19, and AaegHSP40-27 genes are stable, while the others are unstable. Additionally, all HSP40 genes have been found to exhibit a hydrophilic structure (Supplementary Material 2).

The HSP60 proteins in Ae. aegypti range in length from 531 (AaegHSP60-09) to 1733 (AaegHSP60-04) amino acids. The molecular weights of the family members vary between 57 (AaegHSP60-05) and 196 (AaegHSP60-04) kDa. Among the 9 genes, AaegHSP60-05 exhibits characteristics close to alkaline, while the others possess acidic characteristics. It has been found that AaegHSP60-03 and AaegHSP60-04 genes are unstable, while the others are stable. The AaegHSP60-01 gene exhibits hydrophobic characteristics, while the remaining HSP60 genes exhibit a hydrophilic structure (Supplementary Material 2).

The HSP70 proteins in Ae. aegypti range in length from 624 (AaegHSP70-04) to 1111 (AaegHSP70-08) amino acids. The molecular weights of the family members vary between 69 (AaegHSP70-04) and 125 (AaegHSP70-08) kDa. All 17 genes possess acidic characteristics. It has been found that AaegHSP70-08, AaegHSP70-10, and AaegHSP70-11 genes are unstable, while the others are stable. Additionally, all HSP70 genes have been found to exhibit a hydrophilic structure (Supplementary Material 2).

The HSP90 proteins in Ae. aegypti range in length from 703 (AaegHSP90-04) to 795 (AaegHSP90-01) amino acids. The molecular weights of the family members vary between 80 (AaegHSP90-04) and 91 (AaegHSP90-01) kDa. All 4 genes possess acidic characteristics. It has been found that all genes are stable. Additionally, all HSP90 genes have been found to exhibit a hydrophilic structure (Supplementary Material 2).

Structure and Chromosomal Location of HSP Genes

In the Ae. aegypti HSP gene family, it was observed that AaegHSP20-02, AaegHSP20-04, AaegHSP20-06, AaegHSP20-08, AaegHSP20-09, AaegHSP20-10, AaegHSP20-11, AaegHSP20-14, AaegHSP20-15, AaegHSP20-16, AaegHSP20-17, AaegHSP20-18, AaegHSP70-01, AaegHSP70-03, and AaegHSP70-07 genes do not contain introns, while AaegHSP40-09 has the highest number of introns. The gene structure of all HSP classes, such as HSP20, HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 in Ae. aegypti shares a similar structure in each class, consistent with the phylogenetic tree. HSP20 genes were found to have the smallest size in kilobases (kb), while AaegHSP40-09 has the longest size in kb (Fig. 2).

The phylogenetic relationships, length, and position of exons and introns in the HSP genes in Ae. aegypti.

When the genes of the HSP family are displayed on the chromosomes, it has been found that while the HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 genes are distributed across 3 chromosomes, the HSP20 genes are densely clustered in a specific range of loci on the 2nd chromosome (Fig. 3). AaegHSP70-16 and AaegHSP70-17 genes, being in scaffold format, have not been placed on the chromosomes.

Conserved Motifs of HSP Genes

The conserved motifs of HSP proteins of Ae. aegypti were obtained using the MEME Suite. Ten conserved motif patterns were obtained for each HSP family. The length of the identified motifs ranged from 7 to 50 for HSP20, with AaegHSP20-06 (7 motifs) and AaegHSP20-10 (7 motifs) having the highest number of conserved motifs. The second motif was conserved in all genes, and it was observed that the motifs were generally consistent across the other genes except for AaegHSP20-01 (Fig. 4).

The conserved motifs of HSP proteins of Ae. aegypti obtained using the MEME Suite.

It has been observed that HSP40 ranges between 8 and 50, the first motif is conserved in all genes, and the motif with the highest conservation (11 motifs) is found in AaegHSP40-2. It is observed that AaegHSP40-19 and AaegHSP40-21, AaegHSP40-20 and AaegHSP40-27, AaegHSP40-02, AaegHSP40-05, AaegHSP40-06, AaegHSP40-12, AaegHSP40-22, AaegHSP40-25, AaegHSP40-28, AaegHSP40-29, AaegHSP40-32, AaegHSP40-30 and AaegHSP40-31 were found to conserve the same motifs (Fig. 4).

It has been observed that HSP60 ranges between 20 and 50, and the motif with the highest conservation, consisting of 10 motifs, is found in AaegHSP60-01. The second motif is conserved in all genes, and except for AaegHSP60-04 and AaegHSP40-08, the same motifs are conserved in other genes (Fig. 4).

It has been observed that HSP70 ranges between 38 and 50, and genes other than AaegHSP70-08 (5 motifs), AaegHSP70-10 (3 motifs), AaegHSP70-11 (4 motifs), and AaegHSP70-15 (8 motifs) have higher conservation of motifs. The 1st, 5th, and 10th motifs are conserved in all genes. For HSP70, except for AaegHSP70-08, AaegHSP70-10, and AaegHSP70-11, the same motifs are conserved in other genes (Fig. 4).

It has been observed that HSP90 ranges between 15 and 50, and the motifs with the highest conservation, consisting of 15 motifs each, are found in AaegHSP90-02 and AaegHSP90-03. Except for the 9th and 10th motifs, all other motifs are conserved in all genes. AaegHSP90-02 and AaegHSP90-03 exhibit the conservation of the same motifs. (Fig. 4). Upon examining the phylogenetic tree, it was observed that it is consistent with the results from the MEME suite (Fig. 1).

Since some protein sequences obtained from InsectBase did not show compatibility with the sequences in HSPIR, the Blast results were used to select the most suitable matches. In HSP20, there are 17 microsatellites, and the gene AaegHSP20-01 has the highest number of microsatellites. The most repeated sequence, with 5 repetitions of ‘AAAAAAAAAA’ is found in the genes In HSP40, there are a total of 15 microsatellites, and the gene AaegHSP40-26 has the highest number of microsatellites. Additionally, the genes AaegHSP40-05, AaegHSP40-14, AaegHSP40-29, and AaegHSP40-30 have microsatellites with 5 repetitions each. In HSP60, there are a total of 8 microsatellites, and the gene AaegHSP60-07 has the highest number of microsatellites. The most repeated sequence, with 5 repetitions of ‘TTTTTTTTTT’ is found in the gene AaegHSP60-02. The most repeated sequence, with 4 repetitions of ‘AAGAAGAAGAAG’ is found in the genes AaegHSP70-14 and AaegHSP70-16. In HSP90, there are 12 microsatellites, and the gene AaegHSP90-02 has the highest number of microsatellites. The most repeated sequence, with 4 repetitions, is found in the genes AaegHSP90-02, AaegHSP90-03, and AaegHSP90-04. Overall, 284 microsatellites were found in all HSP families (Supplementary Material 3).

Expression Analysis of HSP Genes of Ae. aegypti

When HSP20 genes were examined in different stages, it was observed that they were most highly expressed in L1 larvae and ovaries. In contrast, they were expressed at lower levels in other stages than normal expression levels. Upon gene-specific analysis, it was determined that AaegHSP20-12 was highly expressed in all stages except the male accessory gland and testes. Expression differences between sexes were also detected (Fig. 6a). When HSP40 genes were examined in different stages, it was found that they were most highly expressed in the testes and ovaries. Upon gene-specific analysis, it was determined that AaegHSP40-04 was highly expressed in all stages, while AaegHSP40-09 and AaegHSP40-10 had low expression levels except in testes and ovaries. No significant differences were observed between the sexes (Fig. 6b). When HSP60 genes were examined in different stages, it was observed that there was a decrease in expression levels after the pupal stage (excluding testes and ovaries). In contrast, larvae and pupal stages showed relatively minor changes. A gene-specific analysis determined that AaegHSP60-04 had overall low expression levels. AaegHSP60-08 was found to be moderately low expressed except in the L3 larvae stage. No significant differences were observed between the sexes (Fig. 6c). When HSP70 genes were examined in different stages, it was observed that there were no significant differences between stages. Upon gene-specific analysis, it was found that AaegHSP70-13 was highly expressed in all stages, while AaegHSP70-17 showed moderate to low expression levels without significant variations between stages. No significant differences were observed between the sexes (Fig. 6d). Upon examining HSP90 genes in various stages, it was observed that their expression was generally low during the pupal and carcass stages. In terms of specific genes, AaegHSP90-04 was observed to be moderately expressed at a low level in all stages. No significant differences were observed between the sexes (Fig. 6e).

Discussion

Increasing temperatures and climate change create a stress factor for some organisms by affecting species’ habitats. The rise in temperature due to climate change alters the typical climate structures of many countries, allowing the settlement of species that do not belong there for specific reasons (Li et al. 2023). One of these species is Ae. aegypti, which lives in tropical and subtropical regions. Aedes aegypti is the primary carrier of severe diseases such as yellow fever, Zika, chikungunya, and dengue fever. It causes 50 million infection cases and 300,000 deaths yearly in tropical and subtropical regions (Hussain et al. 2018). Yellow fever spreads due to infected mosquitoes from the Aedes and Haemagogus Williston species biting humans (WHO 2023). In 2013, based on African data sources, the burden of yellow fever disease was estimated by a modeling study to be 84,000–170,000 severe cases and 29,000–60,000 deaths (Garske et al. 2014). Although the carriers of dengue fever are generally mosquitoes of the Aedes genus, the primary carrier is Ae. aegypti. Temperature and climate changes can affect the spread of such vector carriers and, therefore, the spread of these diseases. Consequently, understanding the mechanisms of responding to these stress factors can significantly impact the adaptation of species to climate change and the spread of diseases. In this context, the HSP genes of Ae. aegypti are of great importance. HSPs ensure the correct folding of proteins within the cell and their functioning in response to environmental factors such as heat stress. In this study, the HSP genes of Ae. aegypti were systematically identified and characterized. These genes’ protein characteristics, chromosomal locations, and conserved motifs were examined. In addition, the 3D structures of these genes were determined, and the interactions of the proteins with each other were studied. Also, the phylogenetic relationship of HSP genes of Ae. aegypti with A. stephensi and D. melanogaster were investigated. This is important to understand the evolutionary origin of HSP genes of Ae. aegypti and to determine similarities and differences with other species. Additionally, microsatellites belonging to Ae. aegypti’s HSP genes were investigated. Microsatellites contribute significantly to genetic diversity and evolution, possessing the capacity to influence gene expression. In conclusion, variations in the expression levels of HSP genes throughout different developmental stages of Ae. aegypti were analyzed. This is critical to determine these genes’ role in Ae. aegypti’s development and stress response. This comprehensive study can help us better understand Ae. aegypti’s response to heat stress and determine the species’ adaptation strategies to climate change.

HSP genes are essential in all organisms from prokaryotes to eukaryotes (Zhao et al. 2023). In addition to providing resistance to heat, HSP genes also play a critical role in an organism’s growth and development processes. These genes have a significant place in the literature because they affect the daily functioning of the organism and are also active when a response to heat stress is needed. In the absence of stress conditions, it is established that specific sHSP proteins interact with cytoskeletal components, apoptosis pathways, and autophagy pathways (Carra et al. 2013, Morrow et al. 2015, Liang et al. 2023). HSP genes represent a broad gene family and are divided into subfamilies such as HSP20, HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 based on their molecular weights. These HSP families work in a coordinated manner within the cell. Even though HSP genes have been extensively studied in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes, studies on these genes in insects are relatively scarce in the literature. This situation further emphasizes the importance of studies on HSP genes in insects, especially considering the role of insects as significant vector carriers in public health.

A study by Zhao et al. (2023) on Lentinula edodes (Berk.) Pegler suggested that the abundance of HSP genes is related to genome size. However, this study’s insect analyses reveal that this perspective may only be partially valid. It is observed that the situation may not always hold true in both plants and insects due to their exhibiting greater diversity. This result has been further supported for plants by another study conducted by Zhang et al. (2015). In this study, it was found that Ae. aegypti, located in tropical and subtropical regions and the main vector of many diseases, has a genome of 1294.7 Mb and 80 HSP genes. The distribution of these genes is as follows: 18 HSP20, 32 HSP40, 9 HSP60, 17 HSP70, and 4 HSP90. In contrast, Bemisia tabaci Gennadius with a genome of 615.02 Mb has 5 sHSP, 1 HSP60, 17 HSP70, 3 HSP90; Drosophila ananassae Doleschall with a genome of 30.99 Mb has 2 HSP10, 9 sHSP, 4 HSP60, 1 HSP70, 3 HSP90; Nilaparvata lugens Stål with a genome of 1140.79 Mb has 2 HSP10, 2 sHSP, 4 HSP60, 9 HSP70, 1 HSP90 genes. These results show that the distribution of HSP genes depends not only on genome size but also on the species’ unique evolutionary and ecological features.

In this study, the phylogenetic relationships of HSP genes in Ae. aegypti, A. stephensi, and D. melanogaster species were investigated. Our findings reveal that each HSP group shows intrinsic relatedness, and both among species and in terms of orthologous gene pairs, Ae. aegypti and A. stephensi are closer to each other than to D. melanogaster. Consistent with the phylogenetic tree, Peng et al. (2021) have reported that in terms of evolutionary processes, D. melanogaster diverged from the Contarinia nasturtii Kieffer species approximately 90 MYA, whereas A. stephensi speciated around 40 MYA. Similarly, it is indicated that the Ae. aegypti species evolved approximately 30 MYA. Furthermore, we observed that the HSP40 and HSP70 genes are phylogenetically close to each other. This is consistent with the general notion that HSP40 and HSP70 proteins work together in the cell and are often co-induced, demonstrating cooperative gene families. These findings are supported by a study conducted by Shafqat et al. (2020). In a study on Citrus sinensis Pers., HSP10, HSP20, HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 genes were examined, and it was observed that among these genes, particularly the sHSP genes originated from the same clade as HSP70 and formed a close cluster (Shafqat et al. 2020). These results could contribute to a deeper understanding of the evolutionary relationships of the HSP gene family and the roles of these genes in stress responses.

The length of a gene’s protein products is generally associated with the function and structure of the gene. In the Ae. aegypti species, the protein lengths of HSP genes were examined. The proteins of HSP20, HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 genes are, on average, 195, 503, 677, 701, and 732 amino acids long, respectively. When looking at the structure of HSP proteins, it was found that most are acidic. This is supported by studies on many species, such as Glycine max (L.) Merr. (Zhang et al. 2015), Populus trichocarpa Torr. & A.Gray ex. Hook (Yer et al. 2016), Eucalyptus grandis W. Hill (Çelik Altunoğlu 2016). These results indicate that the size and structure of HSP proteins significantly contribute to the genes’ biological functions and evolutionary history. Generally, larger proteins may have more complex functions, as more space allows proteins to have more numerous and diverse functional regions. However, this general relationship between protein size and functionality is not always definitive, and proteins can often have multiple functions. Therefore, these results necessitate a more detailed biochemical the conserved domains or motifs of proteins are structural units where these proteins usually come together to perform a specific function. These conserved domains and motifs demonstrate that the proteins’ capacity to perform a particular function has been preserved throughout evolutionary processes. The conserved domains found in the proteins of the HSP gene family indicate that these proteins are usually molecular chaperones that play a critical role in protein folding and stress responses. Conserved regions near the C-terminus of genes like HSP70, HSP90, and HSF1 support this. This study also demonstrates noticeable similarities in exon/intron structures, motif distributions, and protein structures among different members of the HSP protein family. These similarities reinforce the prior classification of HSPs. The study by Rehman et al. (2020) supports these findings. These similarities and conserved motifs suggest that members of the HSP gene family may have similar functions and a common evolutionary origin. This supports the theory that these proteins are critical in cellular stress responses and protein folding mechanisms. Therefore, the HSP gene family is a critical research area for understanding fundamental cellular processes like stress response and protein folding.

It is known that members of the HSP superfamily are expressed in various forms in different tissues of insects, indicating that HSP genes play a crucial role in insects’ development and stress responses. The expression analysis of HSP genes was conducted by extracting HSP genes in BioProject number PRJNA419241. Remarkably, some HSP20 and HSP70 genes exhibiting high expression and some showing low expression underscore, as mentioned in the study by Rehman et al. (2020), that HSP genes are components regulating development via a complex transcription network and highlight the role of HSP70 family members in orchestrating the complex transcriptional cascade during the developmental process. Studies indicate gender-based expression differences in HSP20 genes (Lu et al. 2014, Liang et al. 2023). Consistent with the literature, our study observed gender-related expression differences in the HSP20 gene family, but not in other HSP families. Previous research has shown that HSP proteins are associated with heat and cold stress (Liang et al. 2023). Our study’s heat map generated based on RPKM values indicated that almost all stages actively expressed Ae. aegypti HSP genes. These expression levels can fluctuate in some stages, exhibiting upregulation (increase in gene expression) or downregulation (decrease in gene expression). In the study conducted, a heat shock was administered to Ae. aegypti late 3rd instar larvae from the South Andaman population in India. Real-time PCR analysis indicated that the mosquitoes kept at 31 °C, 33 °C, and 34 °C exhibited upregulation in their HSP26, HSP70, and HSP83 genes compared to Ae. aegypti kept at 27°C (Sivan et al. 2017). In another study, Ae. aegypti collected from Monterrey, Mexico, were subjected to a 42°C knock down and Real-Time PCR analysis revealed upregulation in the genes HSP26, HSP70, and HSP83 (Ware-Gilmore et al. 2023). Considering these studies performed with Real-Time PCR, it is observed that the results in our study are consistent with them. HSP genes are essential in stress responses and insects’ growth and development processes. Studies conducted on animals have found that HSP genes directly stimulate the innate immune systems, indicating that HSP genes, irrespective of insects, animals, or plants, possess multiple roles and perform similar functions in response to environmental factors (Zhou et al. 2023). Significant differences exist among organisms regarding expression levels, timing, tissue distributions, and intracellular localizations of sHSPs. This circumstance points to the ability of sHSPs to adapt to external environmental conditions and the specific needs of the cell. This adaptive capacity signifies that HSPs are critical in cellular stress responses and protein folding processes. These findings support that the HSP gene family is an important research field for understanding and developing environmental stress responses.

Conclusions

This study analyzes and characterizes the HSP gene family in Ae. aegypti. Considering that increasing temperatures and climate change could affect the spread of vector carriers and thereby enhance the transmission of infectious diseases, understanding the response mechanisms of HSP genes to thermal stress is of great importance. The research focuses on systematically identifying and characterizing HSP genes in Ae. aegypti, examining their protein properties, structures, phylogenetic relationships, and expression levels. The findings underline the significance of HSP genes in terms of their ability to respond to thermal stress and their relevance to the adaptive strategies of insects. Gaining a deeper comprehension of the functions of HSP genes in the development, stress response, and disease spread of Ae. aegypti can significantly aid in the management of this species and the formulation of effective disease prevention strategies. This study provides valuable information that supports our understanding of the ability of Ae. aegypti and other vector carriers to cope with climate change, and potentially aid in developing more effective intervention strategies to control these diseases.
