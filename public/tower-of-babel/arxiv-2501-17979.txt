Convergence rates for an Adaptive Biasing Potential scheme from a Wasserstein optimization perspective
Tony Lelièvre, Xuyang Lin, Pierre Monmarché

Abstract
Free-energy-based adaptive biasing methods, such as Metadynamics, the Adaptive Biasing Force (ABF) and their variants, are enhanced sampling algorithms widely used in molecular simulations. Although their efficiency has been empirically acknowledged for decades, providing theoretical insights via a quantitative convergence analysis is a difficult problem, in particular for the kinetic Langevin diffusion, which is non-reversible and hypocoercive. We obtain the first exponential convergence result for such a process, in an idealized setting where the dynamics can be associated with a mean-field non-linear flow on the space of probability measures. A key of the analysis is the interpretation of the (idealized) algorithm as the gradient descent of a suitable functional over the space of probability distributions.

1.4
Contributions of this work

In this work, we introduce a PMF-based adaptive biasing algorithm as the Wasserstein gradient flow of a suitable explicit free energy. As explained in Section
1.2
, it is an adaptive biasing potential method in the sense that it is based on the estimation of the PMF, contrary to ABF which estimates the gradient of the PMF (see Section
2.3
for some perspectives on the application of our approach to ABF). It can be seen as one variant of metadynamics
[
43
,
5
]
, which is an adaptive biasing technique extensively used in MD. The first interest of this approach is that the associated free energy turns out to be a very natural quantity to minimize in view of our PMF-based enhanced sampling objective. Moreover, as we will see, this free energy turns out to be convex (along flat interpolations), which gives an insight from an optimization perspective on the efficiency of the algorithm. The second interest is that, thanks to the explicit free energy, we are able to obtain convergence rates for the kinetic process, which is more often used in practice in MD than the overdamped process. We also study the corresponding overdamped process, in which case we get convergence rates explicit enough to quantify an improvement with respect to a naive (unbiased) MCMC sampler.

g:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is an integrable function, both defined on a measurable space
E
, to state that
\rho=(\int_{E}g)^{-1}g
. Depending on the context, this may either state a property of the probability density
\rho
, or define the probability density
\rho
.

Let us now introduce Sobolev spaces on
\mathbb{T}^{m}
. Let us first clarify the use of notation related to multi-indices. We say that
\theta
is a multi-index of dimension
m
, if
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, and we define the length of
\theta
as
|\theta|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\theta_{i}.
Given another multi-index
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},...,\eta_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m},
we define their sum
\theta+\eta
as
(\theta_{1}+\eta_{1},\theta_{2}+\eta_{2},...,\theta_{m}+\eta_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
. Notice that
|\theta+\eta|=|\theta|+|\eta|.
Given a function
g\in C^{|\theta|}(\mathbb{T}^{m}),
we define the derivative

D^{\theta}g=\frac{\partial^{|\theta|}g}{\partial x_{1}^{\theta_{1}}\partial x_{2}^{\theta_{2}}\dots\partial x_{m}^{\theta_{m}}}.

(13)

Given a measurable function
f
defined in
\mathbb{T}^{m}
, we say that
f
admits a weak derivative
D^{\theta}f
, if for any test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m}),

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}\phi D^{\theta}f=(-1)^{|\theta|}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}fD^{\theta}\phi.

(14)

Notice that by Green’s formula, when
g\in C^{|\theta|}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
, the two definitions (
13
) and (
14
) coincide.

For a space
E=\mathbb{T}^{m}
or
E=\Omega
where
\Omega
is a subdomain of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for
p\in[1,\infty]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
W^{k,p}(E)
the Sobolev space of measurable functions
f:E\to\mathbb{R}
such that when
|\theta|\leq k
,
D^{\theta}f\in L^{p}(E)
. The norm on
W^{k,p}(E)
is defined by
\|f\|_{W^{k,p}(E)}=\left(\sum_{|\theta|=0}^{k}\int_{E}|D^{\theta}f|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
for
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\|f\|_{W^{k,\infty}(E)}=\sup_{|\theta|\leq k}\|D^{\theta}f\|_{L^{\infty}(E)}
. In particular, We denote
W^{k,2}(E)
as
H^{k}(E)
. For
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we will use the notation
\|D^{j}f\|_{\infty}=\max_{|\theta|=j}\|D^{\theta}f\|_{\infty}=\max_{|\theta|=j}\sup|D^{\theta}f|
. When
k=0
,
W^{k,p}(E)
reduces to the
L^{p}(E)
space,
p\in[1,\infty]
and we denote the associated norm
\|f\|_{L^{p}(E)}
. When the domain
E
is not made precise, then, implicitly,
E=\mathbb{T}^{m}
in which case, for
f\in W^{k,p}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
(resp.
f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
), we simplify the notation for their norms as

\|f\|_{W^{k,p}}=\|f\|_{W^{k,p}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}
(resp.
\|f\|_{H^{k}}=\|f\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}
).

Finally, we denote by
\mathcal{P}(E)
(resp.
\mathcal{P}_{2}(E)
) the set of probability measures on
E
(resp. with finite second moments).

2
Mathematical framework and results

In all this work, we assume that the collective variable is given by:

\xi:\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbb{T}^{m}\times\mathbb{T}^{d-m}&\to\mathbb{T}^{m}\\
(x_{1},x_{2})&\mapsto x_{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

In particular, the PMF defined by (
11
) is simply:

\forall x_{1}\in\mathbb{T}^{m},\qquad A(x_{1})=-\beta^{-1}\ln\left(\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d-m}}\exp(-\beta U(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\text{d}x_{2}\right).

(15)

Since
U\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, the PMF
A
is also
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
. The objective of this section is to state the main results of this work without any proof, by first presenting the adaptive biasing potential dynamics of interest in an idealized setting in Section
2.1
, and then introducing a regularized version in Section
2.2
together with the associated convergence results. Section
2.3
is finally devoted to a discussion of the difficulties raised by generalizing these convergence results to the Adaptive Biasing Force case.

2.1
Ideal free energy and associated dynamics

Let us introduce the free energy functional: for any probability density
\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}\left(\rho\right)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}U\rho+\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho+\alpha\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}\rho^{1}\ln\rho^{1}\right)\,,

(16)

parametrized by the inverse temperature
\beta
and an additional parameter
\alpha>0
. By convention
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\rho)=+\infty
for probability measures
\rho
that do not admit a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

The free energy is the sum of the energy term
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}U\rho
, the entropy of
\rho
and the entropy of the marginal
\rho^{1}
. When
\alpha=0
, up to an additive constant and a multiplicative factor
\beta^{-1}
, the free energy is exactly the relative entropy with respect to
\rho^{*}
(defined by (
1
)). The additional marginal entropy term is introduced to sample the collective variables at a higher temperature
(\alpha+1)\beta^{-1}
than the rest of the system, as discussed in Section
1.1
, see Equation (
19
) below.

Since the energy term and
\rho\mapsto\rho^{1}
are linear in
\rho
, while the entropy is strictly convex (along flat interpolations), it is straightforward to see that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
is flat-convex, namely, for any
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}
with finite free energy,

\forall t\in[0,1],\qquad\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}\left((1-t)\rho_{0}+t\rho_{1}\right)\leq(1-t)\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\rho_{0})+t\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\rho_{1})\,,

with a strict inequality if
\rho_{0}\neq\rho_{1}
and
t\in(0,1)
.

The minimizer of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
can be characterized as follows.

Proposition 1
.

For all
\alpha>0
,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
admits a unique minimizer
8
8

8

According to the notations introduced in Section
1.6
, we do not indicate explicitly the dependency on
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, neither the normalizing constant. Equation (
17
) means: for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{T}^{m}\times\mathbb{T}^{d-m}
,
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}(x_{1},x_{2})=(\mathcal{Z}^{*}_{\alpha})^{-1}\exp\left(-\beta\left(U(x_{1},x_{2})-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}A(x_{1})\right)\right)
where
\mathcal{Z}^{*}_{\alpha}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}\times\mathbb{T}^{d-m}}\exp\left(-\beta\left(U(x_{1},x_{2})-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}A(x_{1})\right)\right)dx_{1}\,dx_{2}
.

\rho^{*}_{\alpha}\propto\exp\left(-\beta\left(U-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}A\right)\right)\,.

(17)

Moreover, denoting by
D_{\infty}
the optimal log-Sobolev constant of

\bar{\rho}^{*}\propto\exp\bigl(-\beta(U-A)\bigr)
and setting

D_{\alpha}:=D_{\infty}\exp\!\bigl(-\tfrac{2\beta}{\alpha+1}\|A\|_{\infty}\bigr),

(18)

\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
D_{\alpha}
.

The proof of Proposition
1
is given in Section
4.1
. Recall from Section
1.2
that, under suitable conditions (particularily, a good choice of collective variables),
D_{\infty}
is large. The fact that
D_{\alpha}
converges to
D_{\infty}
as
\alpha
goes to infinity motivates the introduction of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
.
More precisely, notice that the marginal of
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
in the collective variables satisfies:

\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{\alpha+1}A\right)\,.

(19)

In other words, compared with
\rho^{*}
,
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
is the probability measure which has, on the one hand, the same conditional densities of
x_{2}
given
x_{1}
, but, on the other hand, a marginal distribution in
x_{1}
for which the temperature has been multiplied by
1+\alpha
. As a consequence, following
[
50
]
, when the conditional distributions of
x_{2}
given
x_{1}
are not multimodal while the marginal law along
x_{1}
is at a sufficiently large temperature, the optimal log-Sobolev constant of
\rho_{\alpha}^{*}
is much larger than the one of
\rho^{*}
, and thus much easier to sample. Indeed, for a non-convex potential, at low temperature, the log-Sobolev constant (namely the parameter
\lambda
in (
6
)) decays exponentially fast to zero with the energy barriers and the system size (besides, let us mention that for temperatures larger than a critical threshold, it can be shown that the log-Sobolev constant becomes independent from the dimension), see
[
21
,
51
,
63
]
and references within.

In order to sample
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
, one can use algorithms to minimize
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
, which can be done following the associated Wasserstein gradient descent
[
1
]
:

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho_{t}\nabla\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}{\delta\rho}(\rho_{t})\right)

where

\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}{\delta\rho}(\rho,x)=U(x)+\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\rho(x)+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\ln\rho^{1}(x_{1})

is the linear functional derivative of the free energy. In other words,

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho_{t}\nabla\left(U+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\ln\rho_{t}^{1}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta\rho_{t}\,.

(20)

This is a non-linear Fokker-Planck equation associated to the McKean-Vlasov diffusion process solution to

\left\{\begin{aligned} \text{d}X_{t}&=-\nabla\left(U+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\ln\rho_{t}^{1}\right)(X_{t})\text{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\text{d}B_{t}\\
\rho_{t}&=\mathcal{L}aw(X_{t})\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(21)

Similarly, one can introduce a kinetic process which can be seen as a Nesterov second-order version
[
54
]
of the gradient descent dynamics (
20
)–(
21
). Denoting by
\nu(x,v)
the probability density in the phase space, with
\nu^{x}
the position marginal (and
\nu^{x,1}
the first
m
-dimensional marginal of
\nu^{x}
), this corresponds to the Vlasov-Fokker-Planck-type equation

\partial_{t}\nu_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\nu_{t}=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\nu_{t}\left(v+\nabla_{x}\left(U+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\ln\nu_{t}^{x,1}\right)\right)\right)+\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta_{v}\nu_{t}\,.

(22)

This is the Kolmogorov equation of a (self-interacting time-inhomogeneous) kinetic Langevin process solving

\left\{\begin{aligned} \text{d}X_{t}&=V_{t}\text{d}t\\
\text{d}V_{t}&=-\nabla_{x}\left(U+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\ln\nu^{x,1}_{t}\right)(X_{t})\text{d}t-V_{t}\text{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\text{d}B_{t}\\
\nu_{t}&=\mathcal{L}aw(X_{t},V_{t})\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(23)

To simulate the dynamics (
21
) or (
23
), one typically introduces an approximation by a system of
N
interacting particles, for which the marginal probability densities
\rho^{1}
or
\nu^{x,1}
are approximated by the convolution of the empirical distribution of the system with a smooth (typically Gaussian) kernel
[
41
]
(and, in addition, the stochastic differential equations are approximated by an appropriate time-discretization). The resulting practical algorithm based on (
23
) can then be seen as a particular version of the well-tempered Metadynamics
[
5
]
, since it is an adaptive biasing potential method, where the potential is eventually modified by using only a fraction of the current estimate of the potential of mean force (see Proposition
1
). However, the algorithm based on (
23
) differs from the practical implementation of the well-tempered Metadynamics since we consider a Markovian mean-field system of interacting particles, while in classical presentation and use of such adaptive algorithms, the particles also interact with their past. Indeed, in well-tempered Metadynamics, the biasing potential is updated using the occupation measure, namely the trajectory in the collective variable space up to the current time. In this context, one needs to add a vanishing adaption mechanism in order for the method to converge, as in any stochastic approximation algorithm: as time goes, the last positions should less and less contribute to the biasing potential. We refer for example to
[
24
,
7
,
6
]
for a mathematical perspective on this non-Markovian setting.

Remark 2
.

In this work, we focus only on the long-time convergence of non-linear dynamics, since it is specific to the particular processes considered here. We will not discuss the error induced by the particle approximation or the time-discretization in a practical implementation. However, when proving our main results (specifically, Theorems
3
and
4
below for the regularized dynamics (
26
) and (
28
)), we check all the conditions of
[
11
,
12
]
where uniform-in-time propagation of chaos is proven. Under the same conditions,
[
74
]
directly gives long-time convergence rates for the particle system and
[
69
,
67
]
additionally address the discretization error (respectively in the overdamped and kinetic case).

The processes (
21
) and (
23
) are natural dynamics to study the efficiency of PMF-based adaptive algorithms in idealized mathematical settings. However, the pointwise non-linearity
\nabla\ln\rho^{1}
or
\nabla\ln\nu^{x,1}
, in the spirit of the Stein variational gradient descent introduced in
[
52
]
, raises some difficulties in the theoretical analysis, starting with the well-posedness of (
20
) and (
22
). It may be possible to work with a suitable notion of solutions, as in the theory of viscosity solutions
[
19
]
, or more specifically in our situation by working with the JKO scheme as in
[
9
]
. However, beyond the qualitative well-posedness of the equation, our analysis requires uniform-in-time bounds on the solutions, see Remarks
5
and
6
in particular. For this reason, as explained in the next section, we will rather work with mollified versions of these processes. Moreover, this is actually more consistent with the algorithms used in practice than the idealized dynamics (
21
) and (
23
), since this mollification corresponds to the Gaussian kernel regularization mentioned above (see also Section
3
for the practical implementation).

2.2
Regularized free energy and processes

As motivated above, we introduce the regularization of the free energy (

\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\rho\right)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}U\rho+\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho+\alpha\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1})\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}U\rho+\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho+\alpha\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho)\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho)\right),

which is parametrized by

and an additional parameter

, with

In particular

is a probability density over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. By convention

for probability measures

.

Theorem 3
.

independent of

to (

\forall t\geq 0,\,\mathcal{H}(\rho_{t}\mid\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq\beta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\rho_{t}\right)-\beta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)\leq\beta\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{0})-\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)\right)e^{-c_{\alpha,\epsilon}t},

5.1

We prove a similar result for the kinetic case (

. Again, by convention,

if

.

Theorem 4
.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,1]

This result is proven in Section

. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first convergence result for a PMF-based enhanced sampling algorithm applied to the kinetic Langevin diffusion. As mentioned in the introduction, the Langevin diffusion is the standard method used in MD.

The convergence rate

\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and
\alpha\rightarrow+\infty
. As a consequence, these results are not sufficiently sharp to compare the efficiencies of these adaptive biasing schemes with the standard naive (overdamped) Langevin dynamics.

This issue stems from a loose bound on the log-Sobolev constant of certain
time-dependent probability distributions appearing in the proof. In the proof of Theorems
3
and
4
, this bound is obtained from the non-explicit fixed-point characterization of

\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, which will be shown in Proposition
8
, together
with uniform bounds on the
Gaussian kernel
K^{m}_{\epsilon}
and a crude dependence on
\alpha
. These bounds deteriorate
as
\epsilon\to 0
and
\alpha\to+\infty
.
Let us explain how we are able to circumvent this difficulty, at least for the overdamped dynamics (
25
).

Thanks to Theorem
3
,
\rho_{t}
converges to
\rho_{\alpha,\epsilon}^{*}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and we thus may expect the convergence rate to be given by the log-Sobolev constant
D_{\alpha,\epsilon}
of
\rho_{\alpha,\epsilon}^{*}
. This is stated in the next theorem.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon},D_{\alpha,\epsilon}
be as in Theorem
2
.
There exists a constant
c>0
independent of
\epsilon,\alpha
, such that when
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{c}{\alpha+1}]
, for any initial condition
\rho_{0}
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with finite entropy, there exists a constant
M>0
which depends on
\epsilon,\alpha,\beta
and
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{0})
, such that
the solution
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to (
25
), satisfies:

\forall t\geq 0,\,\mathcal{H}(\rho_{t}\mid{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq\beta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})-\beta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)\leq Me^{-\frac{2D_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\beta}t}.

(29)

The proof of Theorem
5
is given in Section
5.3
. In contrast with the proof of Theorem
3
, it relies on a technical fixed-point argument (see Theorem
13
below). According to Theorem
2
, for any
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,\frac{c}{\alpha+1}]
, for any
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
,

D_{\alpha,\epsilon}\geq D_{\alpha}\exp(-C_{3}\epsilon_{0})
: for sufficiently small
\epsilon
, the convergence rate in (
29
) can thus be made independent of
\epsilon
.
We will thus refer to Theorem
5
as an exponential convergence result with an
\epsilon
-independent rate in the following, as opposed to the results of Theorems
3
and
4
which provide exponential convergence results with
\epsilon
-dependent rates (in the sense that those rates are
a priori
not bounded away from
0
when
\epsilon\to 0
).

Moreover, according again to Theorem
2
,
D_{\alpha,\epsilon}
can be made arbitrarily close to the optimal log-Sobolev constant of
\rho_{\alpha}^{*}
, for
\epsilon
sufficiently small, which, thanks to
[
50
]
, is bigger (at least at low temperature and when the collective variable is well-chosen) than the one of the Boltzmann-Gibbs measure
\rho^{*}\propto\exp(-\beta U)
, see Section
2.1
.
Theorem
5
thus shows that, in this setting, the dynamics (
25
) converges to equilibrium at a much larger rate than the standard overdamped diffusion (
4
).

The same result could be expected to hold in the kinetic case (
27
), i.e. it may be possible to improve Theorem
4
to get a convergence rate independent of
\epsilon
in the regime
\epsilon\to 0
. However, for the kinetic Langevin diffusion, even in the linear setting, the convergence rate obtained with the modified entropy approach, that we follow here, depends not only on the log-Sobolev constant of the stationary state but also on bounds on the Hessian of some log-densities.
This leads to additional difficulties when trying to establish the analogue of Theorem
5
in the kinetic case, see Remark
6
at the end of Section
5.3
for details.

Remark 3
.

In practice, when the dynamics (
26
) (resp. (
28
)) reach their equilibria, they do not sample the target measure
\rho^{*}
(resp.
\nu^{*}
) but biased measures. However, the ratio of the equilibrium measure over the target measure is proportional to
\omega(x)=\exp(\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho_{t}))
(resp.
\omega(x)=\exp(\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{x}_{t}))
), namely a quantity which depends on the biasing potential which is learnt on the fly: one can thus use weighted averages, as in (
10
) to get unbiased estimators.

2.3
Discussion about the convergence of the Adaptive Biasing Force method

Before presenting the proofs of the convergence results in the following sections, let us make a few comments on applying the mean-field free energy approach to the Adaptive Biasing Force (ABF) method (rather than to an adaptive biasing potential, as the one described above). A key point in the recent works
[
11
,
64
,
60
]
on the mean-field underdamped Langevin diffusion is that the free energy, which can always be defined by (
12
) as discussed in
[
64
]
, is explicit. This is not the case for ABF. Even for the overdamped dynamics, the free energy (in the sense of (
12
)) decays along the non-linear flow (cf.
[
64
, Proposition 2]
), but neither this free energy nor its dissipation along the non-linear flow are explicit. It is actually unclear whether the dynamics can be seen as a Wasserstein gradient flow in the sense of
[
1
]
.
That being said, since ABF relies on the estimation of the gradient of a log density (which is related to what is referred to as score matching in machine learning
[
40
]
), it may be possible to interpret it as a gradient flow of a Fisher Information, but then not with respect to the Wasserstein distance (which would yield a fourth-order equation
[
29
]
) but to another metric, such as the Fisher-Rao one
[
10
]
. To our knowledge, modified entropy methods have not yet been developed for kinetic versions of Fisher-Rao gradient flows. This could be an interesting research direction for the future.

Let us finally comment on the convergence of ABF for the overdamped dynamics. In
[
47
]
, exponential rates of convergence in entropy are established for the overdamped ABF dynamics. The computations are based on the usual relative entropy with respect to the stationary solution (and not on the non-linear free energy given by (
12
)) and they rely on a very specific feature of the dynamics, namely that the marginal distribution in the collective variable solves an autonomous linear Fokker-Planck equation. This gives a convergence of this marginal distribution to its equilibrium, which can be used to control some error terms in the derivation of the full relative entropy, leading to an overall exponential decay (we exemplify in Appendix
C
how such techniques can be used on the example of the non-regularized dynamics (
21
)).
Extending this analysis to the kinetic case is unclear. Indeed, with the standard definition of
\nabla A_{t}
used in practice, it is not true that the marginal density of the collective variables (and possibly their velocities) solves an autonomous equation. We may modify the algorithm to enforce this, but then, with a given fixed biasing force, the process is a non-equilibrium kinetic Langevin process for which the equilibrium is not explicit
[
58
]
. It is then unclear how to apply a modified entropy version of
[
47
]
, since this would require to have a bound on the Hessian of the logarithm of these non-explicit local equilibria.

The convergence of the ABF method for the (kinetic) Langevin dynamics thus remains an open problem.

Numerical Experiments

The objective of this section is to illustrate the efficiency of the Adaptive Biasing Potential method (
26
) on a toy problem. We refer to the review papers
[
71
,
35
,
18
]
for references to realistic molecular systems where similar adaptive biasing techniques are used.

3.1
Numerical scheme

In practice, the McKean-Vlasov process (
26
) is first approximated by a system of
N
interacting particles
(X_{t}^{1},\dots,X_{t}^{N})\in\mathbb{T}^{dN}
evolving according to

\text{d}X_{t}^{i}=-\nabla\left(U+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}K_{\epsilon}^{m}\star\ln\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{\epsilon}^{m}(\cdot-X_{t}^{j})\right)\right)(X_{t}^{i})\text{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\text{d}B_{t}^{i}

(30)

with independent Brownian motions
B^{1},\dots,B^{N}
. Time is then discretized using e.g. an Euler-Maruyama scheme with some step size
h>0
, and space is discretized on a grid
\mathcal{G}^{m}
of
\mathbb{T}^{m}
with step
\delta_{x}
for the convolution terms. Notice that the number of points in the grid
\mathcal{G}^{m}
remains manageable in pratice since
m
is small (typically
m\in\{1,2,3\}
). The resulting practical algorithm is thus the Markov chain
(Y_{k}^{1},\dots,Y_{k}^{N})\in\mathbb{T}^{dN}
with transitions

Y_{k+1}^{i}=Y_{k}^{i}-h\nabla\left(U+W_{k}\right)(Y_{k}^{i})+\sqrt{\frac{2h}{\beta}}G_{k}^{i}

(31)

with
G^{i}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
i.i.d. The biasing potential
W_{k}
and its gradient, which depend only on the first
m
coordinates, are evaluated at
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
as follows:

\displaystyle W_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{x}^{m}\alpha}{\beta}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{G}^{m}}K_{\epsilon}^{m}(x_{1}-y)\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{\epsilon}^{m}(y-Y_{k,1}^{j})\Bigr),

(32)

\displaystyle\nabla W_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\Biggl(\frac{\delta_{x}^{m}\alpha}{\beta}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{G}^{m}}\nabla K_{\epsilon}^{m}(x_{1}-y)\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{\epsilon}^{m}(y-Y_{k,1}^{j})\Bigr),\;0_{d-m}\Biggr),

(33)

where
Y_{k,1}^{i}
denotes the first
m
coordinates of
Y_{k}^{i}
and
0_{d-m}
denotes the zero vector in
\mathbb{T}^{d-m}
.

These approximations for
W_{k}
and
\nabla W_{k}
crucially exploits the smoothness of the Gaussian kernel and the regularizing/convolution structure, which in turn provides additional motivation for introducing convolution-regularized dynamics.

The expectation of an observable
g
with respect to the Gibbs measure
\rho_{*}\propto e^{-\beta U}
is then approximated following the reweighting procedure (
10
). The average is done both over the particles
i\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
and time
k\geqslant 1
. The weight for the samples generated at time
k
is
\omega_{k}(x)=e^{\beta W_{k}(x)}
. After
M
time-steps, the estimator is thus

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_{k}(Y_{k}^{i})\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g\left(Y_{k}^{i}\right)\omega_{k}(Y_{k}^{i})\,.

(34)

As in practical use of MCMC methods, a burn-in time is introduced to reduce the influence of the initial transient behavior: specifically, we sometimes average only over the second half of the trajectory.
More precisely, we introduce a time window
\mathcal{I}\subset\{1,\dots,M\}
, and in practice take

\mathcal{I}=\{\lceil M/2\rceil,\dots,M\}
.
The corresponding windowed reweighted estimator is

\Bigg(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_{k}(Y_{k}^{i})\Bigg)^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g\bigl(Y_{k}^{i}\bigr)\,\omega_{k}(Y_{k}^{i}).

(35)

4
Proofs of the results on the free energies

In this section, we will prove some properties on the minimizers
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
(resp.
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
) of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
), and the fact that when
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+},\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\rightarrow\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
in the sense of
L^{\infty}
norm. To alleviate notation, some proofs will be restricted to the case
\beta=1
, which simply amounts to replace
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
by
\beta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
,
U
by
\beta U
, and
A
by
\beta A
.

4.1
Characterization of the free energy minimizers

In this subsection, we prove the existence and various properties of the minimizers of the free energies
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
, following ideas of
[
39
]
. In particular, we prove the results stated in Proposition
1
.

Proposition 6
.

For
\epsilon\in(0,1]
(resp.
\epsilon=0
), the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
) admits a unique minimizer
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
(resp.
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
), which has a density on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Proof.

We only prove this result for the case
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, the case for
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
follows by the same arguments.
We use standard properties of relative entropy, see
[
23
, Lemma 1.4.3]
. In the following, limit points and semicontinuity are meant in the sense of the weak topology on probability measures.

We recall that the Boltzmann-Gibbs measure
\rho^{*}
is given by (
1
), where
\mathcal{Z}
is the normalizing constant. Denoting by
1_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}
the constant probability density over
\mathbb{T}^{m}
, then for a probability density
\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\beta}(\mathcal{H}(\rho|\rho^{*})-\ln\mathcal{Z})+\alpha\mathcal{H}(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1}|1_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}))\geq-\frac{\ln\mathcal{Z}}{\beta}
. Hence, there exists a sequence of probability densities
(\rho_{n})_{n\geq 0}
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{n})
converges to
\inf_{\rho}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)\geq-\frac{\ln\mathcal{Z}}{\beta}
where the infimum is taken over probability densities on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Since
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{n})
is bounded, thus so is

\mathcal{H}(\rho_{n}|\rho^{*})_{n\geq 0}
, implying that
\rho_{n}
admits a limit point
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
. Since
\mathcal{H}(\cdot|\rho^{*})
is lower semicontinuous,
\mathcal{H}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}|\rho^{*})\leq\sup_{n}\mathcal{H}(\rho_{n}|\rho^{*})<\infty
, thus
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
admits a density with respect to
\rho^{*}
thus also with respect to the Lebesgue measure, which we also denote
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
.
It is easy to verify that
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is also a limit point of
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1}_{n}
. Using again the lower semicontinuity of the relative entropy, we get that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq\inf_{\rho}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)
, so that
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
.

The uniqueness of the minimizer is a consequence of the strict convexity of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, consequence of the strict convexity of
x\mapsto x\ln x
. Indeed, for any two probability densities
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\lambda\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{1})+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{2})\geq\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\lambda\rho_{1}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{2})

with equality if and only if
\lambda\in\{0,1\}
or
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
.

∎

From now on,
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
(resp.
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
) always refers to the unique minimizer of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
) introduced in Proposition
6
. We will need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 7
.

For any probability density
\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\rho)<\infty
,

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\rho).

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\rho)<\infty
,
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho
and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}\rho^{1}\ln\rho^{1}
are finite.
By Lemma
29
,

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho\ln(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho.

Since both
U
and
K^{d}_{\delta}\star U
are bounded by
\|U\|_{\infty}
, Lemma
28
gives
K^{d}_{\delta}\star U\to U
a.e. Moreover,
\rho
being integrable, by dominated convergence theorem,

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}U(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho(K^{d}_{\delta}\star U)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}U\rho.

Noticing that
(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)^{1}=K^{m}_{\delta}\star\rho^{1}
, using again Lemma
29
,

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)^{1}\ln((K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)^{1})=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}(K^{m}_{\delta}\star\rho^{1})\ln(K^{m}_{\delta}\star\rho^{1})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}\rho^{1}\ln\rho^{1}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

To characterize the minimizer
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
for
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, we introduce the map
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
defined as follows. For any probability density
\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)
is the probability density on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
satisfying

\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)\propto\exp(-\beta U-\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho))\,,

(36)

which is well-defined since for any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,
\exp(-\beta U-\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho))
is continuous and positive, hence in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

Proposition 8
.

For
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,

\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is the only probability density on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
which satisfies the relation

\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)=\rho
.
In addition, for
\epsilon=0
, it holds
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}\propto\exp(-\beta(U-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}A))
, and thus
\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha}\propto\exp(-\frac{\beta}{\alpha+1}A).

Proof.

We only prove this result for
\beta=1
. The result for a general
\beta
can then be easily deduced, by changing
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
to
\beta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
,
U
to
\beta U
, and
A
to
\beta A
.

Step 1: Characterization of
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
.
Let us first consider the case
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, and prove that
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})=\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
. The idea is essentially to write the Euler-Lagrange equations associated with the minimization problem defining
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
(see Proposition
6
).
Consider a probability density
\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)<\infty
. Writing
\rho_{\lambda}=\lambda\rho+(1-\lambda)\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon},\lambda\in(0,1]
, then by the convexity of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{\lambda})<\infty
, and
since
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is the minimizer of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{\lambda})\geq\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})
.

Writing
\phi(x)=x\ln x
, let us introduce the scalar valued functions on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
:

\displaystyle g_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\phi(\rho_{\lambda})-\phi(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})+U\rho_{\lambda}-U\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+\alpha\phi(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho_{\lambda})-\alpha\phi(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\right),\lambda\in(0,1]

\displaystyle g_{0}

\displaystyle=(\rho-\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})(1+\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U)+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(\rho-\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})(1+\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})).

Then
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}g_{\lambda}=g_{0}.

By convexity of
\phi
,
\frac{1}{\lambda}(\phi((1-\lambda)x_{0}+\lambda x_{1})-\phi(x_{0}))\leq\phi(x_{1})-\phi(x_{0})
for all
x_{1},x_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\lambda\in(0,1].
Thus
g_{\lambda}\leq g_{1},a.e.
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Moreover, from the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, for
\lambda\in(0,1]
,
g_{\lambda}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, and

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g_{\lambda}=\frac{\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{\lambda})-\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})}{\lambda}\geq 0.

Thus by Fatou’s lemma,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g_{1}-g_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\liminf_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}g_{1}-g_{\lambda}\leq\liminf_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g_{1}-g_{\lambda}\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g_{1}<+\infty.

Notice that
g_{1}-g_{0}\geq 0,a.e.
, so that the first integral is well defined. As a consequence,
g_{1}-g_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and so is
g_{0}
, and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g_{1}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g_{1}-g_{0}\geq 0
. Since
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})
is finite, so that
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U)+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we also get that

\rho(\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U)+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

In particular, if we choose
\rho=1_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
as the probability density on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
that takes the constant value
1
, then we have that
\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U+\alpha\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).
By Lemma
26
,
U
and
\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})
are bounded, thus
\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).
This implies that
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}>0
a.e. and
\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

For general
\rho
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)<\infty,
using that
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g_{0}\geq 0,

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\rho-\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})(1+\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U)+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(\rho-\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})(1+\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\rho-\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\left(\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\right).

By Lemma
26
, when
\delta\in(0,1],x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(K^{d}_{\delta}(x-\cdot))<\infty,
thus we can take
\rho(y)=K^{d}_{\delta}(x-y),y\in\mathbb{T}^{d},\delta\in(0,1]
in the inequality above. By passing to the limit
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
, by Lemma
28
, it holds, a.e.

\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\geq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}))=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}).

Let us define
f=\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})-\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}).
Then
f\geq 0
a.e., thus
f\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\geq 0
a.e. Since
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}=0
, one gets
f\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}=0,
a.e., and thus
f=0
a.e. since
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}>0
a.e. Thus
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}=\exp(-U-\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})+\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}))
a.e. which yields
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}=\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}).

Step 2: Uniqueness of the fixed point of
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
.
Let us now prove that
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is the only fixed point of
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
. Suppose that
\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is a probability density satisfying

\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})=\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}.
Let us define
F(\lambda)=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}((1-\lambda)\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+\lambda\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}),\lambda\in[0,1].
Notice that
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon},\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
both belonging to the image of
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, they are bounded from below and above by positive constants, and this justifies the use of dominated convergence theorem which leads to the right derivative of
F
at
0
, as

\displaystyle F^{\prime}_{+}(0)=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{F(\lambda)-F(0)}{\lambda}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}-\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})(\ln\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}))=0.

The last equality follows from
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})=\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, which implies that
\ln\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+U+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})

is constant.

Then by the convexity of
F
inherited from the convexity of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
we have that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})=F(0)\leq F(1)=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}).
Since
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is the unique minimizer of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, we have that
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}=\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}.
This concludes the proof of the properties stated in the case
\epsilon\in(0,1]
.

Step 3: Results on
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
.
Let us now consider the case
\epsilon=0
. Let us prove that the probability density
\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha}\propto\exp(-U+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}A)
is the minimizer of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}.
For a probability density
\rho
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\rho)<\infty,\delta\in(0,1]
, we define
F(\lambda)=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}((1-\lambda)\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha}+\lambda K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho),\lambda\in[0,1]
. Then, since
\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha}
and
K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho
are bounded from below and above by positive constants, it holds

\displaystyle F^{\prime}_{+}(0)=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{F(\lambda)-F(0)}{\lambda}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho-\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha})(\ln\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha}+U+\alpha\ln\hat{\rho}^{*,1}_{\alpha})=0.

The last equality is again a consequence of the fact that
\ln\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha}+U+\alpha\ln\hat{\rho}^{*,1}_{\alpha}
is a constant, by definition of
\hat{\rho}^{*,1}_{\alpha}
.

By the convexity of
F
,
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha})=F(0)\leq F(1)=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)
. Thanks to Lemma
7
, one thus gets

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha})\leq\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\rho).
This implies that
\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha}
is the minimizer of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}.
Thus, by the uniqueness of the minimizer of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
stated in Proposition
6
,
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}=\hat{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha}\propto\exp(-U+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}A)
, which concludes the proof in the case
\epsilon=0
.
∎

Corollary 9
.

The function
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
satisfies the following:
\inf_{\alpha>0}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{*}_{\alpha}(x)>0
.

Proof.

By Proposition
8
,
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}\propto\exp(-\beta(U-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}A)).
Conclusion follows by noticing that

\inf U-\max\{0,\sup A\}\leq U-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}A\leq\sup U-\min\{0,\inf A\}.

∎

Remark 4
.

Similarly to
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, it is possible to introduce a map
\Gamma_{\alpha}
such that, for any probability density
\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)\propto\exp(-\beta U-\alpha\ln\rho^{1})
. The probability density
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)
is, of course, not well-defined for all
\rho
. A sufficient condition for
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)
to be well defined is for example
\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho>0
. However,
\exp(-\beta U-\alpha\ln\rho^{1})\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
(and consequently

is not well-defined) if we consider for example
\rho

\rho^{1}
is zero over an open interval.

By the explicit formula for
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
obtained in Proposition

, we deduce that
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha})=\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
. Besides, by Lemma
28
, for any regular probability density
\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, we know that
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)\rightarrow\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)
as
\epsilon\to 0
. The characterization of
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
as the fixed point of the map
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
thus passes to the limit
\epsilon\to 0
in order to provide a similar characterization for
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
. In particular,
\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
is the unique fixed point of
\Gamma_{\alpha}
on the set of probability densities
\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that

.

4.2
Convergence of the free energy minimizer
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
when
\epsilon\to 0

As seen in Proposition
8
and Remark
4
, the minimizers of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
are characterized as fixed points of maps
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
and
\Gamma_{\alpha}
. The question of the convergence of
\rho_{\alpha,\epsilon}^{*}
towards
\rho_{\alpha}^{*}
thus requires to quantify how the fixed point of
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
depends on
\epsilon
, which will be done using estimates obtained from the Banach fixed point theorem, see Lemma
12
below, which can be seen as a variant of the implicit function theorem.

Let us start with a technical lemma. Let us recall that the function
A\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
is defined by (
15
), and is such that
\rho^{*,1}=\exp(-\beta A)
.

Lemma 11
.

Suppose that
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
2k>m
.
For
\epsilon\geq 0
and
f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
with
\inf f>0
, let us define

F(\epsilon,f)=\ln f+\beta A+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f),

(37)

where by convention
K^{m}_{0}\star\ln(K^{m}_{0}\star f)=\ln f
. Then
F(\epsilon,f)\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}).

Proof.

Notice that by Lemma
31
,
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
. Furthermore,
\inf K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f\geq\inf f>0
, and by Lemma
20
,
\sup f<\infty
, thus
\sup K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f\leq\sup f<\infty
. Moreover,
\ln x
and
(\ln x)^{(j)}=(-1)^{j-1}\frac{(j-1)!}{x^{j}}
for
j\geq 1
are bounded over
[\inf f,\sup f]\supseteq[\inf K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f,\sup K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f]
. Thus by Proposition
23
,
\ln(f),\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f)\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
. Using again Lemma
31
,
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f)\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}).
Since
A
is smooth, hence in
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
, we get that
F(\epsilon,f)\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}).

∎

In Theorem
13
below,
for sufficiently small
\epsilon\geq 0
, we prove the existence of a zero
f_{\epsilon}
of the map
f\mapsto F(\epsilon,f)
, such that
f_{\epsilon}\rightarrow f_{0}
when
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, in some Sobolev norm. Then, as we will see in the proof of Theorem
2
below, we will express
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
in terms of
f_{\epsilon}
, and thus deduce that
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\rightarrow\rho^{*}_{\alpha}
in a suitable sense, as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
The proof of Theorem
13
relies on a contraction property and Lemma
12
taken from
[
53
, Corollaries 1 and 3 (page 229-230)]
with a slight modification of the notation.

Lemma 12
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space.

1.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r)=\{x\in X,d(x,x_{0})\leq r\}
be the closed ball centered at
x_{0}
with radius
r>0
, and let
\Phi:{\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r)\to X
be a contraction with constant
\|\Phi\|_{Lip}\in[0,1)
such that:

d(x_{0},\Phi(x_{0}))\leq(1-\|\Phi\|_{Lip})r.

Then
\Phi({\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r))\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r)
, and
\Phi
has a unique fixed point in
{\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r)
.

2.

Let
\Phi:X\to X
be a function, let
x_{0}\in X
be a point and let us set
d=d(x_{0},\Phi(x_{0}))
. Assume that
\Phi
is a contraction with constant
\|\Phi\|_{Lip}\in[0,1)
on
{\mathcal{B}}\left(x_{0},\frac{d}{1-\|\Phi\|_{Lip}}\right)
. Then,
\Phi
admits a unique fixed point in
{\mathcal{B}}\left(x_{0},\frac{d}{1-\|\Phi\|_{Lip}}\right)
.

Proof.

Let us provide the proof for completeness. The first result is a simple consequence of the Banach fixed point theorem. Indeed for any
x\in{\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r)
,

\displaystyle d(\Phi(x),x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq d(\Phi(x),\Phi(x_{0}))+d(\Phi(x_{0}),x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi\|_{Lip}d(x,x_{0})+(1-\|\Phi\|_{Lip})r\leq r

and thus
\Phi({\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r))\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r)
and
\Phi:{\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r)\to{\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r)
is a contraction over the complete metric space
{\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r)
, and therefore admits a unique fixed point in
{\mathcal{B}}(x_{0},r)
.

The second result is a direct consequence of the first one with
r=\frac{d}{1-\|\Phi\|_{Lip}}
.
∎

We are now in position to state and prove Theorem
13
.

Theorem 13
.

Suppose that
k\in\mathbb{N^{+}}
and
2k>m
. There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
independent of
\alpha,\epsilon
, such that for all
\epsilon\in[0,\frac{C_{1}}{\alpha+1}]
,
there exists
f_{\epsilon}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
with
\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}f_{\epsilon}>0
such that
F(\epsilon,f_{\epsilon})=0
(where
F
is defined by (
37
)), with additionaly
\|f_{\epsilon}-f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq C_{2}{\epsilon}
. Moreover,
f_{0}=\exp(-\frac{\beta A}{\alpha+1}).

Proof.

In all this proof, the only property we use about
A
is that
A\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m}).
Besides, we can assume without loss of generality that
\beta=1
, the general case being simply obtained by replacing
A
by
\beta A
.

First of all, when
\epsilon=0,

f_{0}=\exp(-\frac{A}{\alpha+1})
already satisfies the requirements that
F(0,f_{0})=0,f_{0}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}),\inf f_{0}>0
. In the rest of the proof we assume that
\epsilon\in(0,1]
(since in the statement of Theorem
13
we can always chose
C_{1}
to be less than
1
, so that the condition
\epsilon\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\alpha+1}
implies
\epsilon\leq 1
). Thus all the estimates on
K_{\epsilon}^{m}
requiring
\epsilon\leq 1
proven in Appendix
B
can be used.

We define, for
f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
such that
\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}f>0
,

K(\epsilon,f)=f-\frac{\exp{(-\frac{A}{\alpha+1})}}{\alpha+1}F(\epsilon,f)=f-\frac{f_{0}}{\alpha+1}F(\epsilon,f).

(38)

By Lemma
11
,
F(\epsilon,f)\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}).
Since
f_{0}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
, then by Lemma
22
,
f_{0}F(\epsilon,f)\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
, and thus
K(\epsilon,f)\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
. Let us denote
H^{k}_{+}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
the subset of functions
f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
such that
\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}f>0
. By Equation (
60
) from Lemma
20
,
H^{k}_{+}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
is an open set of
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
. Thus for fixed
\epsilon
,
K(\epsilon,\cdot)
can be viewed as a map from
H^{k}_{+}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
to
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}).

Notice that if
f_{\epsilon}
exists, then it should be a fixed point in
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
of the map
f\mapsto K(\epsilon,f)
. The map
K(\epsilon,f)
is built such that its Fréchet derivative
D_{f}K(0,f_{0})
at
(0,f_{0})
is zero, and the hope is thus to build a contraction for small enough
\epsilon
.

Let us first give the main ideas of the proof before presenting the details of the arguments. The proof is decomposed into four steps:

1.

The (Fréchet) derivative of
K(\epsilon,\cdot)
is well defined and can be explicitly written. More precisely, there exists a constant
c>0
, such that for all
h_{1}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
with
\|h_{1}-f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq c\inf f_{0}
, and
\inf h_{1}>0
, for all
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, the map
f\mapsto K(\epsilon,f)
is Fréchet differentiable at
f=h_{1}
. Moreover, the Fréchet derivative is given by:
for all
g\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
,

D_{f}K(\epsilon,h_{1})(g)=g-\frac{f_{0}}{\alpha+1}\frac{g}{h_{1}}-\frac{\alpha f_{0}}{\alpha+1}\left(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\frac{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star g}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star h_{1}}\right).

(39)

2.

The linear operator
g\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})\mapsto D_{f}K(\epsilon,f_{0})(g)\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
has an operator norm smaller than
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}\left(1+C\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{1+\alpha}\right)
, for a constant
C
independent of
\alpha
and
\epsilon\in(0,1]
.

3.

For any
h_{1}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
such that

\|h_{1}-f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq c\inf f_{0}
, where
c>0
is the constant introduced in step 1, the linear operator
g\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})\mapsto D_{f}K(\epsilon,h_{1})(g)-D_{f}K(\epsilon,f_{0})(g)\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
has an operator norm smaller than
C\|f_{0}-h_{1}\|_{H^{k}}
, for a constant
C
independent of

\mathcal{H}(\rho\mid{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq\beta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)-\beta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)\leq\mathcal{H}(\rho\mid\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)).

(55)

Let
\rho_{t}
be the probability density of the adaptive biasing potential process (
26
). It holds: for all
t\geq 0
,

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})=-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|\nabla\ln\frac{\rho_{t}}{\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})}\right|^{2}\rho_{t}.

(56)

Proof.

The sandwich inequality (
55
) is a consequence of
[
16
, Lemma 3.4]
. Let us provide the proof of this inequality for the sake of completeness. Let us denote by

G(\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\beta U\rho+\alpha(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho)\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho),

so that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho+G(\rho)\right)
. By standard convexity property, one has, for
\delta\in[0,1]
and two probability densities
\mu
and
\nu

G((1-\delta)\mu+\delta\nu)\leq(1-\delta)G(\mu)+\delta G(\nu)

so that for
\delta\in(0,1]
,

\frac{G(\mu+\delta(\nu-\mu))-G(\mu)}{\delta}\leq G(\nu)-G(\mu).

By sending
\delta
to
0
, and using the boundedness of
U
and
K^{m}_{\epsilon}
from Lemma
26
to apply the dominated convergence theorem, one gets

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\nu-\mu)(\beta U+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\mu))\leq G(\nu)-G(\mu).

By applying this inequality twice with
(\mu,\nu)=(\rho,\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})
and then
(\mu,\nu)=(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon},\rho)
, one gets:

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\rho-\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})(\beta U+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}))\leq G(\rho)-G(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\rho-\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})(\beta U+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho))

which can be rewritten as:

-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\ln(\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}))(\rho-\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq G(\rho)-G(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\ln(\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho))(\rho-\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}).

By adding
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, and using the fact that
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})=\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, one thus gets

\mathcal{H}(\rho\mid{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq\beta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)-\beta\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)\leq\mathcal{H}(\rho\mid\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho))-\mathcal{H}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\mid\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)).

This concludes the proof of (
55
) by using the fact that
\mathcal{H}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\mid\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho))\geq 0
.

The second result (
56
) is obtained by a classical calculation. Let us provide some details, for the sake of completeness. Let us introduce

\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)=\exp(-\beta U-\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho)),

so that
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)=\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)/\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
and

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\left(\frac{\rho}{\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)}\right).

Notice that
\rho_{t}
satisfies the non-linear Fokker-Planck equation (
25
), which can be rewritten as

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\frac{1}{\beta}{\rm div}\left(\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})\nabla\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})}\right)\right).

Thus by noticing that
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{t}\partial_{t}\ln\rho_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{t}=0
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{t}\rho_{t}\ln\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})}\right)-\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{t}\partial_{t}\ln\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}{\rm div}\left(\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})\nabla\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})}\right)\right)\ln\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{t}\partial_{t}(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|\nabla\ln\frac{\rho_{t}}{\gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})}\right|^{2}\rho_{t}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho_{t}\frac{\partial_{t}(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho_{t})}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho_{t}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|\nabla\ln\frac{\rho_{t}}{\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})}\right|^{2}\rho_{t}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Recall that

\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)\propto\exp(-\beta U-\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho)).

Since
\inf K^{m}_{\epsilon}\leq K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho\leq\sup K^{m}_{\epsilon}
(since

and

), by (

\max\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho)-\min\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho)\leq C+\frac{m}{8\epsilon},

for some constant

\rho

,

D\exp(-C\alpha-\frac{m\alpha}{8\epsilon})=\frac{c_{\alpha,\epsilon}\beta}{2}
, where

is defined in the statement of Theorem

.

Writing
f(t)=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})-\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)
, we obtain the result by using (

) and (

) from Proposition

f^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|\nabla\ln\frac{\rho_{t}}{\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})}\right|^{2}\rho_{t}\leq-\frac{c_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\beta}\mathcal{H}(\rho_{t}\mid\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t}))\leq-c_{\alpha,\epsilon}f(t)\,.

5.2
The underdamped case, with an
\epsilon
-dependent convergence rate

In this section, we consider the long time behaviour of the regularized adaptive biasing potential method for the underdamped Langevin dynamics introduced in (
27
).

In a similar way as we defined
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
by (
36
) in the overdamped case,
let us introduce in the kinetic case the map
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
defined as follows (for
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
\alpha>0
): for a given probability density
\nu(x,v)
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu)
is the probability density on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu)\propto\exp\left(-\beta H-\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{x,1})\right)

where, we recall
H(x,v)=U+\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}
.
Equivalently,
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu)=\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu^{x})\otimes\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{-1}I_{d})
.

The next proposition is obtained following the same reasoning as for the proofs of Proposition
6
and Proposition
8
.

Proposition 16
.

For
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, the functional
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}

admits a unique minimizer
\tilde{\rho}^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, which is a probability density on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover
\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}=\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\otimes\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{-1}I_{d})
, so that
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})=\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
.

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of a minimizer
\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
to
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is proven following the same reasoning as in the proof of Proposition
6
.
Let us now prove that
\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}=\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\otimes\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{-1}I_{d})
.

Let us recall (see (
2
)) that
\nu^{*}(x,v)=\nu^{*,x}(x)\nu^{*,v}(v)
where

\nu^{*,x}(x)=\frac{\exp(-\beta U(x))}{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\exp(-\beta U(x))dx},\qquad\nu^{*,v}(v)=\frac{\exp(-\beta\frac{|v|^{2}}{2})}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(-\beta\frac{|v|^{2}}{2})dv}.

Let
\nu
be a probability density on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let us define the probability density on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
:
\tilde{\nu}(x,v)=\nu^{x}(x)\nu^{*,v}(v)
. Simple calculations lead to

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\nu}|\nu^{*})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nu^{x}\nu^{*,v}\ln(\nu^{x}\nu^{*,v})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nu^{x}\nu^{*,v}\ln(\nu^{*,x}\nu^{*,v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nu^{x}\ln\nu^{x}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nu^{*,v}\ln\nu^{*,v}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nu^{x}\ln\nu^{*,x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nu^{*,v}\ln\nu^{*,v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nu^{x}\ln\nu^{x}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nu^{x}\ln\nu^{*,x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\nu^{x}|\nu^{*,x}).

(57)

By the Donsker-Varadhan variational formula of relative entropy (e.g.
[
23
, Lemma 1.4.3]
), denoting by
C_{b}(E)
the set of bounded continuous functions on
E=\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\nu|\nu^{*})=

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in C_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x,v)\nu(x,v)dxdv-\ln\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(g(x,v))\nu^{*}(x,v)dxdv\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in C_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\nu(x,v)dxdv-\ln\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(g(x))\nu^{*}(x,v)dxdv\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in C_{b}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g(x)\nu^{x}(x)dx-\ln\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\exp(g(x))\nu^{*}(x)dx\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\nu^{x}|\nu^{*,x}).

(58)

This inequality is also a direct consequence of the extensivity property of the entropy, see for example
[
50
, Lemma 2.1]
.
By combining (
57
) and (
58
), we thus have proven that

\mathcal{H}(\nu|\nu^{*})\geq\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\nu}|\nu^{*}).

This leads to
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu)\geq\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\tilde{\nu}).

Besides, by direct calculation,
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\tilde{\nu})={\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}({\nu}^{x})-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(-\beta\frac{|v|^{2}}{2})dv
.
Since
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is the minimizer of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu)\geq{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-\beta\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\right)dv.

It is easy to check that the lower bound is obtained by choosing
\nu(x,v)=\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(x)\nu^{*,v}(v)
. This shows that
\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is the (unique) minimizer of
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
.

The fact that
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})=\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
easily follows by using the analytical expression of
\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
, and the fact that
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is a fixed point of
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}
(see Proposition
36

\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\propto\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\nu^{*,v}=\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\nu^{*,v}.

∎

Our objective is now to prove Theorem
4
, namely that
\nu_{t}
solution to (
27

\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
in the sense of relative entropy when

.
Compared to the overdamped dynamics (

[
11

, which relies on a modified free energy.

Notice that
[

is written in the case when the position variable

is in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

, and this is what we use in the following.

Proof of Theorem

.

First of all, by
[
11

, any probability measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies a sandwich inequality similar to the inequality (

\mathcal{H}(\nu\mid\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq\beta\mathcal{\tilde{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\nu\right)-\beta\mathcal{\tilde{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)\leq\mathcal{H}(\nu\mid\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu)).

To prove the exponential convergence, we will use
[
11

Step 1

[
11

By
[

, for

\mathcal{I}\left(\nu_{t_{0}}\mid\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu_{t_{0}})\right)\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\beta^{2}}{t_{0}^{3}}\left(\mathcal{\tilde{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\nu_{0}\right)-\mathcal{\tilde{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)\right),

where
C^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is a constant
9
9

9

The constant
C^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha,\epsilon}
actually depends on
\beta
, which is a fixed constant in all this work. We introduce somewhat artificially the factor
\beta^{2}
in the inequality so that, at the end of Step 1,
C_{\alpha,\epsilon}
is easily expressed in terms of

.
. In particular,
\mathcal{I}\left(\nu_{t_{0}}\mid\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu_{t_{0}})\right)<\infty

Thus,
[

\beta\mathcal{\tilde{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\nu_{t}\right)-\beta\mathcal{\tilde{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)\leq\left(\beta\mathcal{\tilde{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\nu_{t_{0}}\right)-\beta\mathcal{\tilde{F}}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)+C^{\prime}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal{I}\left(\nu_{t_{0}}\mid\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu_{t_{0}})\right)\right)e^{-\kappa_{\alpha,\epsilon}(t-t_{0})},

Step 2

with respect to
\nu^{x}

[

are the following:

1.

The map
\nu^{x}\mapsto F_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu^{x})
is convex, i.e. for any probability densities
\nu^{x}_{1},\nu^{x}_{2}
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}

\lambda F_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu^{x}_{1})+(1-\lambda)F_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu^{x}_{2})\geq F_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\lambda\nu^{x}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\nu^{x}_{2}).

2.

The family of probability densities

3.

The function
\nabla\frac{\delta F_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\delta\nu^{x}}=\beta\nabla U+\alpha\nabla K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{x,1})
(where here and in the following, the derivatives
\nabla

(\nu^{x},x)
, in the following sense: there exists constants
L_{x},L_{\nu}
, such that for any pairs

\left|\nabla\frac{\delta F_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\delta\nu^{x}}(\nu^{x},x)-\nabla\frac{\delta F_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\delta\nu^{x}}(\nu^{\prime x},x^{\prime})\right|\leq L_{x}|x-x^{\prime}|+L_{\nu}W_{1}(\nu^{x},\nu^{\prime x}),

where

is the Wasserstein 1-distance defined as follows: for

,

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f\in C(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\|f\|_{Lip}\leq 1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}fd\mu-fd\nu.

The first condition follows from the convexity of
x\mapsto x\ln x
.

To verify the second condition, by Proposition
10
, it is sufficient to verify that

\nu

K^{m}_{\epsilon}\in[c_{\epsilon},C_{\epsilon}]
for some constants
C_{\epsilon},c_{\epsilon}>0

68

For the third condition, let us first note that
\nabla U(x)
is Lipschitz continuous in

, since
\nabla^{2}U
is continuous in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, thus bounded. Besides, for any
\nu
,

\|\nabla\left(\nabla K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{x,1})\right)\|_{\infty}=\|\nabla^{2}K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{x,1})\|_{\infty}\leq\|\nabla^{2}K^{m}_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}\ln\max\left(C_{\epsilon},\frac{1}{c_{\epsilon}}\right)\,,

which concludes the proof for the Lipschitz continuity in

(notice that the upper bound is independent of
\nu
). For the Lipschitz continuity in
\nu
, we bound

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\frac{\delta F_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\delta\nu^{x}}(\nu^{x},\cdot)-\nabla\frac{\delta F_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\delta\nu^{x}}(\nu^{\prime x},\cdot)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|\nabla K^{m}_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}\|\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{x,1})-\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{\prime x,1})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|\nabla K^{m}_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}\frac{\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{x,1}-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{\prime x,1}\|_{\infty}}{c_{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|\nabla K^{m}_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}\frac{\|\nabla K^{m}_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}W_{1}(\nu^{x,1},\nu^{\prime x,1})}{c_{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|\nabla K^{m}_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}\frac{\|\nabla K^{m}_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}W_{1}(\nu^{x},\nu^{\prime x})}{c_{\epsilon}},

which concludes the proof.

5.3
The overdamped case, with an
\epsilon
-independent convergence rate

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem

. Let us start with a technical lemma.

Lemma 17

Suppose that
\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}

. Then there exist constants
C,c>0
independent of
\alpha

Proof.

First of all, referring to the proof of Corollary
14
, there exists a constant

\alpha,\epsilon

\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\geq\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\geq\frac{\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{*}_{\alpha}}{2}\geq\frac{C_{3}}{2}

Let now
\rho
be a probability density on
\mathbb{T}^{d}

We would like to apply Proposition
10
to
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)
. Since
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho)\propto\exp(-\beta U-\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho))\propto\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\exp(-\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho)+\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}))
, and
\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1})-\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon}))\|_{\infty}\leq\|\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1})-\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\|_{\infty},
we only need to estimate the
L^{\infty}
norm of
\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1})-\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon}).

Using Pinsker’s inequality (see for example
[
2
]
) and the fact that, thanks to (
68
),
\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\|_{\infty}\leq C^{\prime}\epsilon^{-m/2}
for some positive constant
C^{\prime}
independent of
\alpha,\epsilon
,

\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(\rho^{1}-\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\|_{\infty}\leq C^{\prime}\epsilon^{-m/2}\|\rho-\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\|_{TV}\leq C^{\prime}\epsilon^{-m/2}\sqrt{2\mathcal{H}(\rho|\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})},

where
\|\cdot\|_{TV}
is the total variation between probability measure defined as

\|\mu-\nu\|_{TV}=\sup_{\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\int_{E}fd\mu-fd\nu.

As a consequence, when
\rho
is such that
\frac{C_{3}}{2}\geq 2C^{\prime}\epsilon^{-m/2}\sqrt{2\mathcal{H}(\rho|\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})}
, or equivalently
\mathcal{H}(\rho|\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq\epsilon^{m}\frac{C^{2}_{3}}{32C^{\prime 2}}
, we have that

K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1}\geq K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon}-C^{\prime}\epsilon^{-m/2}\sqrt{2\mathcal{H}(\rho|\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})}\geq\inf\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon}-\frac{C_{3}}{4}\geq\frac{1}{2}\inf\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon}.

Hence, using the fact that
\inf\rho_{\alpha,\epsilon}^{*,1}\geq\frac{C_{3}}{2}
,

|\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1})-\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon})|\leq\frac{C^{\prime}\epsilon^{-m/2}\sqrt{2\mathcal{H}(\rho|\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})}}{\frac{1}{2}\inf(\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon})}\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}C^{\prime}}{C_{3}}\epsilon^{-m/2}\sqrt{\mathcal{H}(\rho|\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})}.

As a conclusion, when
\rho
is such that
\mathcal{H}(\rho|\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq\epsilon^{m}\frac{C^{2}_{3}}{32C^{\prime 2}},

\displaystyle\|\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1})-\alpha K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{1})-\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho^{*,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}\alpha C^{\prime}}{C_{3}}\epsilon^{-m/2}\sqrt{\mathcal{H}(\rho|\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})}.

We take
c=\min\{c_{1},\frac{C^{2}_{2}}{32C^{\prime 2}}\}
and
C=\frac{8\sqrt{2}C^{\prime}}{C_{3}}.

Then, we conclude by Proposition
10
.
∎

Notice that when
\epsilon\leq\frac{c}{\alpha+1}
, we have that
\epsilon\leq 1
, and thus all the estimates on
K_{\epsilon}^{m}
requiring
\epsilon\leq 1
proven in Appendix
B
can be used.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

For
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,
we define
f(t)=\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})-\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\left(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\right)
. Denote by
D_{t}
a log-Sobolev inequality constant of
\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})
. From Proposition
15
,

f^{\prime}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})=-\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|\nabla\ln\frac{\rho_{t}}{\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t})}\right|^{2}\rho_{t}\leq-\frac{2}{\beta^{2}}D_{t}\mathcal{H}(\rho_{t}|\Gamma_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho_{t}))\leq-\frac{2}{\beta}D_{t}f(t).

As a consequence, using that we can suppose that
2D_{t}\geq\beta c_{\alpha,\epsilon}
for some
c_{\alpha,\epsilon}>0
as we saw in the proof of Theorem
3
, for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathcal{H}(\rho_{t}\mid\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq\beta f(t)\leq\beta e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{2D_{s}}{\beta}ds}f(0)\leq\beta e^{-tc_{\alpha,\epsilon}}f(0)\,.

Now, let
C,c
be as in Lemma
17
, and let
t_{0}=\frac{1}{c_{\alpha,\epsilon}}\ln\left(\frac{\beta f(0)}{c\epsilon^{m}}\right)
, so that for all
t\geq t_{0}
,
\mathcal{H}(\rho_{t}|\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\leq c\epsilon^{m}
. Applying Lemma
17
, we get that, for all
t\geq t_{0}
, we can suppose

D_{t}\geq D_{\alpha,\epsilon}\exp\left(-C\alpha\epsilon^{-m/2}\sqrt{\mathcal{H}(\rho_{t}|\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})}\right)\geq D_{\alpha,\epsilon}\exp\left(-C\alpha\epsilon^{-m/2}\sqrt{\beta e^{-tc_{\alpha,\epsilon}}f(0)}\right).

Writing
C^{\prime}_{\epsilon}=C\alpha\epsilon^{-m/2}\sqrt{\beta f(0)}
and using that
e^{-C^{\prime}_{\epsilon}x}\geq 1-C^{\prime}_{\epsilon}x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we get that, for all
t\geq t_{0}
,

\int_{t_{0}}^{t}D_{s}ds\geq D_{\alpha,\epsilon}(t-t_{0})-D_{\alpha,\epsilon}C^{\prime}_{\epsilon}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{-\frac{sc_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{2}}ds\geq D_{\alpha,\epsilon}(t-t_{0})-\frac{2D_{\alpha,\epsilon}C^{\prime}_{\epsilon}}{c_{\alpha,\epsilon}}=D_{\alpha,\epsilon}t+M_{1}

for
M_{1}=-D_{\alpha,\epsilon}t_{0}-\frac{2D_{\alpha,\epsilon}C^{\prime}_{\epsilon}}{c_{\alpha,\epsilon}}
.
Thus, for all
t\geq t_{0}
,

f(t)\leq f(0)e^{-\frac{2}{\beta}\int_{0}^{t}D_{s}ds}\leq f(0)e^{-\frac{2}{\beta}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}D_{s}ds}e^{-\frac{2}{\beta}D_{\alpha,\epsilon}t}e^{-\frac{2}{\beta}M_{1}}\leq f(0)e^{-c_{\alpha,\epsilon}t_{0}-\frac{2}{\beta}M_{1}}e^{-\frac{2}{\beta}D_{\alpha,\epsilon}t}.

For
t\leq t_{0}
, we simply bound

f(t)\leq f(0)\leq e^{-\frac{2}{\beta}D_{\alpha,\epsilon}t}e^{\frac{2}{\beta}D_{\alpha,\epsilon}t_{0}}f(0)\,,

which concludes the exponential convergence stated in Theorem
5
.

∎

Remark 6
.

Similarly to Theorem
3
in the overdamped case, the convergence rate in Theorem
4
is a priori not bounded away from
0
as
\epsilon\to 0
. One could expect a result similar to Theorem
5
, namely that in the long time the measure
\nu_{t}
becomes smooth and close to
\nu_{\alpha,\epsilon}^{*}
, so that the exponential rate of convergence can be made independent of
\epsilon
, for sufficiently small
\epsilon
(recall that
\alpha
is fixed). As seen in the proof of Theorem
4
, the convergence rate depends on three quantities, which should be controlled uniformly in
\epsilon
in order to get such a result:

•

The log-Sobolev inequality constant of
{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu^{x}_{t})
. We can use similar arguments as in the proof of Theorem
5
to show that for
t
large enough, it is bounded from below independently of
\epsilon
.

•

The Lipschitz constant
L_{x}
. More precisely, by checking the proof of
[
11
]
, we see that what should be bounded is
L_{x}(t)
where for all
t\geq 0
,

L_{x}(t):=\left\|\nabla^{2}\frac{\delta F_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\delta\nu^{x}}(\cdot,\nu_{t}^{x})\right\|_{\infty}.

In our case, by the fact that
{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})=\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon},\tilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha,\epsilon}(\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon})=\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon},\nu^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\propto\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\exp(-\beta\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}),
we have that (again,
\nabla
refers to derivatives
with respect to position variable
x
)

\nabla\frac{\delta F_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\delta\nu^{x}}=\beta\nabla U+\alpha\nabla K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{x,1})=\nabla\ln\rho^{*}_{\alpha,\epsilon}+\alpha\nabla K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{x,1})-\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\nu^{*,x,1}_{\alpha,\epsilon})).

For fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, the derivative of the second term converges to
0
when
\mathcal{H}(\nu^{x}|\nu^{*,x}_{\alpha,\epsilon})\to 0
by a similar argument as in the proof of Lemma
17
. Thus,
L_{x}(t)
converges to
\|\nabla^{2}\ln\rho^{\star}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\|_{\infty}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. We would then conclude that
L_{x}(t)
is independent of
\epsilon
for large times by applying Theorem
13
to higher order Sobolev space and by a similar argument as in the proof of Theorem
2
to get that
\|\nabla^{2}\ln\rho^{\star}_{\alpha,\epsilon}\|_{\infty}
converges to
\|\nabla^{2}\ln\rho^{\star}_{\alpha}\|_{\infty}

as
\epsilon
vanishes.

•

The Lipschitz constant
L_{\nu}
. Again, by checking the proof of
[
11
]
, we see that what is precisely used is that for all
t\geq 0
and all small
s\geq 0
,

\left\|\nabla\frac{\delta F_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\delta\nu^{x}}(\cdot,\nu_{t}^{x})-\nabla\frac{\delta F_{\alpha,\epsilon}}{\delta\nu^{x}}(\cdot,\nu_{t+s}^{x})\right\|_{\infty}\leq L_{\nu}(t)W_{1}(\nu_{t}^{x},\nu_{t+s}^{x})

where
W_{1}
is the Wasserstein 1-distance.
Here, it is less clear how to exploit that
\nu_{t}\rightarrow\nu_{\alpha,\epsilon}^{*}
for large times. Let us recall that for
\epsilon=0
,
\frac{\delta F_{\alpha,0}}{\delta\nu^{x}}(\cdot,\nu^{x})=\beta U+\alpha\ln\nu^{x,1}
. It is already not trivial to identify a subset of smooth probability measures on
\mathbb{T}^{m}
over which
\mu\mapsto\nabla\ln\mu\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
is Lipschitz,
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
being endowed with the
W_{1}
norm.

This question of the behaviour of these quantities as
\varepsilon
vanishes is related to the question of the well-posedness of the non-regularized dynamics from Section
2.1
.

Standard results on Sobolev spaces

Recall the notation on Sobolev spaces introduced in Section
1.6
.

In all the following, when needed,
\mathbb{T}
is identified to the interval

, and thus
\mathbb{T}^{m}
to

Definition 18
.

Given a function

, we define its extension to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as, for

\tilde{f}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})=f(\pi(x_{1}),\pi(x_{2}),...,\pi(x_{n})),

where

\pi:\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbb{R}&\to I\\
x&\mapsto x\,{\rm mod}\,1\end{aligned}\right.

(59)

where

for the integer

such that

.

Lemma 19
.

Suppose that
B_{1}
is the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N},\,p\in[1,+\infty],\,f\in W^{k,p}(\mathbb{T}^{m}),
let
F
be the restriction of
\tilde{f}
to
B_{1}
,
\tilde{f}
being the extension of
f
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as introduced in Definition
18
. Then we have

2^{-m}\|F\|_{W^{k,p}(B_{1})}\leq\|f\|_{W^{k,p}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}\leq\|F\|_{W^{k,p}(B_{1})}.

Proof.

Notice that
I^{m}=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})^{m}\subset B_{1}
, thus

\|f\|_{W^{k,p}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}=\|F\|_{W^{k,p}(I^{m})}\leq\|F\|_{W^{k,p}(B_{1})}.

Moreover, since
B_{1}\subset(2I)^{m}=[-1,1)^{m}
, it holds

\|F\|_{W^{k,p}(B_{1})}\leq\|\tilde{f}\|_{W^{k,p}([-1,1)^{m}]}=2^{\frac{m}{p}}\|\tilde{f}\|_{W^{k,p}(I^{m})}=2^{\frac{m}{p}}\|f\|_{W^{k,p}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}\leq 2^{m}\|f\|_{W^{k,p}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}.

∎

Lemma 20

(Sobolev embedding theorem in
\mathbb{T}^{m}
)
.

Suppose that
m,k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
2k>m
. Then, there exists a constant
C_{\infty}
, such that for all
f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}),

\|f\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\infty}\|f\|_{H^{k}}.

(60)

Moreover, for
i\in\mathbb{N}\cap[k-\frac{m}{2},k-1],

and
q\in[2,\infty)
such that
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{k}{m}<\frac{1}{q}-\frac{i}{m}
, there exists a constant
C_{i,q}
depending only on

, such that for all

\|D^{\theta}f\|_{q}\leq C_{i,q}\|f\|_{H^{k}}.

(61)

Proof.

By Lemma
19
, we need only to consider the Sobolev space
W^{k,p}(B_{1})
in the unit ball
B_{1}
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
instead of
\mathbb{T}^{m}
. Notice that
B_{1}
is a bounded subdomain of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
C^{1}
boundary.

Equation (
60
) is a direct consequence of
[
25
, Theorem 6, p. 270]
, and the fact that
2k>m
. And to prove (
61
), notice that

\|D^{\theta}f\|_{H^{k-i}}\leq\|f\|_{H^{k}},

We take
p
such that
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{k-i}{m}.
Notice that
p\in[1,2)
, since

\frac{1}{2}<\frac{1}{q}+\frac{k-i}{m}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}=1.

Thus by
[
25
, Theorem 6, p. 270]
, and the fact that

there exists a constant
C^{\prime}_{i,q}
depending only on

(since

), such that

\|D^{\theta}f\|_{q}\leq C^{\prime}_{i,q}\|D^{\theta}f\|_{W^{k-i,p}}.

By Holder’s inequality, there exists a constant
C^{\prime\prime}_{i,q}
only depending on
i,q
(since

depends on

), such that

\|D^{\theta}f\|_{W^{k-i,p}}\leq C^{\prime\prime}_{i,q}\|D^{\theta}f\|_{H^{k-i}}.

The result follows by gathering these inequalities.
∎

Lemma 21
.

Suppose that
j,m,k\in\mathbb{N^{+}}
and
2k>m
. Then there exists a constant
C
, such that if
f_{i}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}),\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}

\sum_{i=1}^{j}|\theta_{i}|\leq k

Proof.

If
\sum_{i=1}^{j}|\theta_{i}|=0

60
) of
Lemma

.

In the following, we assume that
\sum_{i=1}^{j}|\theta_{i}|\geq 1.
Notice that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}\Pi_{i=1}^{j}(D^{\theta_{i}}f_{i})^{2}\leq\Pi_{i=1}^{j}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}(D^{\theta_{i}}f_{i})^{q_{i}}\right)^{\frac{2}{q_{i}}}=\Pi_{i=1}^{j}\|D^{\theta_{i}}f_{i}\|_{q_{i}}^{2}.

To obtain the second inequality, notice that
|\theta_{i}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}|\theta_{i}|\leq k.

If
0\leq|\theta_{i}|<k-\frac{m}{2}
(when
|\theta_{i}|=0,q_{i}=\infty

Thus by (

\|D^{\theta_{i}}f_{i}\|_{q_{i}}\leq\|D^{\theta_{i}}f_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\infty}\|D^{\theta_{i}}f_{i}\|_{H^{k-|\theta_{i}|}}\leq C_{\infty}\|f_{i}\|_{H^{k}}.

If
|\theta_{i}|=k
, then since

, we have that
\sum_{i=1}^{j}|\theta_{i}|=k
, and thus

, and

If
|\theta_{i}|\in\mathbb{N}\cap[k-\frac{m}{2},k-1],
then by the fact that

By convention, we pose
C_{k,2}=1,C_{|\theta_{i}|,q_{i}}=C_{\infty}

The conclusion follows by taking

C=\max\left\{\Pi_{i=1}^{j}C_{|\theta_{i}|,q_{i}}\Big|1\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}|\theta_{i}|\leq k,q_{i}=\frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{j}|\theta_{i}|}{|\theta_{i}|}\right\}

\phi(0)=\phi^{\prime}(0)=0
, one has
\|\phi(g)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}([-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}])}\|g\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}=o(\|g\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})})
.

For the term
j=1
in (
62
), it holds

\lim_{\|g\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}\rightarrow 0}\|\phi^{\prime}(g)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}=\lim_{\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}\rightarrow 0}\|\phi^{\prime}(g)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}=|\phi^{\prime}(0)|=0.

Finally, for the terms
j\geq 2
in (
62
), we notice that

\lim_{\|g\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}\rightarrow 0}\|\phi^{(j)}(g)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}=\lim_{\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}\rightarrow 0}\|\phi^{(j)}(g)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{m})}=|\phi^{(j)}(0)|.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Gaussian kernel on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{m}

This appendix is devoted to the definition and properties the Gaussian kernel on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{m}
, which is extensively used in this work.

B.1
The Gaussian kernel, lower and upper bounds

Definition 25
.

Let
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)
be the density of a standard Gaussian random variable. For any
\epsilon>0
, we define the Gaussian kernel
K^{1}_{\epsilon}
on
\mathbb{T}
by: for all
x\in\mathbb{T}
,

K^{1}_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi\left(\frac{x+n}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right).

(65)

More generally, for
\epsilon>0
and
m\geqslant 1
, we define the Gaussian kernel
K^{m}_{\epsilon}
on
\mathbb{T}^{m}
by: for all
x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{m})\in\mathbb{T}^{m}
:

K^{m}_{\epsilon}(x)=\Pi_{i=1}^{m}K^{1}_{\epsilon}(x_{i}).

(66)

Notice that by definition,
K^{1}_{\epsilon}
is indeed a periodic function (with period
1
). As a periodic function, one can introduce its Fourier coefficients: for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\hat{K^{1}_{\epsilon}}(k)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\exp(-2i\pi kx)K^{1}_{\epsilon}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)\exp(-2i\pi kx)\,dx=\exp(-2\pi^{2}k^{2}\epsilon),

and
K^{1}_{\epsilon}(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{K^{1}_{\epsilon}}(k)\exp(2i\pi kx)
.

The function
\epsilon\mapsto K^{1}_{\epsilon}
can be identified as the heat kernel on
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
with periodic boundary conditions (
\epsilon\geq 0
being the time). Besides, a probabilistic interpretation can be obtained using the wrapping function
\pi
defined in (
59
). Indeed,
K^{1}_{\epsilon}|_{I}
is the density over
I
of
\pi(\sqrt{\epsilon}G)
where
G
is a standard Gaussian random variable. In particular
\int_{I}K^{1}_{\epsilon}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}K^{1}_{\epsilon}=1
.

Let us now provide lower and upper bounds for
K^{m}_{\epsilon}
.

Lemma 26
.

There exists a constant
C>1
such that, for all
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, for all
x\in I
,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)\leq K^{1}_{\epsilon}(x)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right).

(67)

As a consequence, for all
x\in I^{m}
,

\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon}}\right)^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{m}{8\epsilon}\right)\leq K^{m}_{\epsilon}(x)\leq\left(\frac{C}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon}}\right)^{m}.

(68)

Proof.

The lower bound is obvious by simply considering the term
n=0
in the sum defining
K^{1}_{\epsilon}
. Notice that the upper bound is equivalent to: for all
x\in I
,

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left(-\frac{nx}{\epsilon}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}}{2\epsilon}\right)\leq C

which is equivalent to

\sum_{n\geq 1}\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}}{2\epsilon}\right)\cosh\left(\frac{nx}{\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{C-1}{2}.

Since
|x|\leq 1/2
, one has (since
-n^{2}+n\leq-(n-1)^{2}
for all
n\geq 1
)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}}{2\epsilon}\right)\cosh\left(\frac{nx}{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}}{2\epsilon}\right)\cosh\left(\frac{n}{2\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}}{2\epsilon}\right)\left(1+\exp\left(\frac{n}{2\epsilon}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{n\geq 0}\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}}{2\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(1+\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\epsilon}\right)\,dx\right)=2\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\pi}{2}}\right)

which proves the upper bound in (
67
) since
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, and thus yields the result.

Finally, the inequalities (
68
) are consequences of the fact that, for all
x\in I
,
\phi\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)\leq\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)\leq\phi(0)
.
∎

Notice that the previous lemma is useful to provide lower and upper bounds of probability densities convoluted with
K^{m}_{\epsilon}
since, for any probability density
\rho
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, one has (since
\rho\geq 0
and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho=1
):

\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}\leq K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho\leq\sup_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}.

Besides, it is easy to check (since
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\geq 0
and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}=1
) that, for any function
\rho:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\leq K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\rho\leq\sup_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho.

Lemma 27
.

For all
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}
,
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star K^{m}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}=K^{m}_{\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime}}
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
, such that for all
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,
\int_{I^{m}}|x|K^{m}_{\epsilon}(x)dx\leq Cm\sqrt{\epsilon}
and

\int_{I^{m}}|x|^{2}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(x)dx\leq Cm\epsilon
.

Proof.

Let us first consider the case
m=1
.
The first property can be seen as a consequence of a semigroup property, since
K^{1}_{\epsilon}
is the heat kernel on the torus. This can also be checked directly using the wrapping function
\pi
, as follows. Let
G
(resp.
G^{\prime}
) be a Gaussian random variable of variance
\epsilon
(resp.
\epsilon^{\prime}
). Moreover,
\pi(G)
(respectively
\pi(G^{\prime})
) has density
K^{1}_{\epsilon}
(respectively
K^{1}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
) over
\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
. By the probabilistic interpretation of the convolution mentioned above,
\pi(\pi(G)+\pi(G^{\prime}))
then has a density
K^{1}_{\epsilon}\star K^{1}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
. On the other hand,
\pi(\pi(G)+\pi(G^{\prime}))=\pi(G+G^{\prime})
and
G+G^{\prime}
is a Gaussian random variable with variance
\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime}
. Thus
\pi(\pi(G)+\pi(G^{\prime}))
has a density
K^{1}_{\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime}}
over
\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
and this concludes the proof.

For the inequalities, using Lemma
26
, one has (by a change of variable):

\int_{I}|x|K^{1}_{\epsilon}(x)dx\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\int_{I}|x|\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)\,dx\leq C^{\prime}\sqrt{\epsilon}

with
C^{\prime}=C\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\phi(x)\,dx
, and

\int_{I}x^{2}K^{1}_{\epsilon}(x)dx\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\int_{I}x^{2}\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)\,dx\leq C^{\prime\prime}\epsilon,

with
C^{\prime\prime}=C\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{2}\phi(x)\,dx
. This concludes the proof for the case when
m=1
.

The results for
m>1
are easily deduced from the definition of
K^{m}_{\epsilon}
, and the fact that
|x|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}|x_{i}|,|x|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{2}.

∎

B.2
Regularizing using convolution with the Gaussian kernel
K^{m}_{\epsilon}

Let us define the convolution
\star
on the torus as follows: for any two functions
f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
, for all
x\in\mathbb{T}

f\star g(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(x-y)g(y)\,dy.

The probabilistic interpretation of this convolution is the following: if
f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
are such that
f|_{I}
and
g|_{I}
are probability densities over
I
, and if
X\in I
and
Y\in I
are independent random variables with respective densities
f|_{I}
and
g|_{I}
, then
\pi(X+Y)
has density
f\star g
. For the sake of completeness, let us provide a proof of this. First,
X+Y
(seen as a random variable over
\mathbb{R}
) has density: for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
f_{X+Y}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(z-y)1_{z-y\in I}g(y)1_{y\in I}dy
. Notice that
X+Y
is with values in
[-1,1)
. Then
\pi(X+Y)
has density: for all
z\in I
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{X+Y}(z+n)

\displaystyle=f_{X+Y}(z-1)+f_{X+Y}(z)+f_{X+Y}(z+1)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(f(z-1-y)1_{z-1-y\in I}+f(z-y)1_{z-y\in I}+f(z+1-y)1_{z+1-y\in I}\right)g(y)1_{y\in I}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{I}f(z-y)(1_{z-1-y\in I}+1_{z-y\in I}+1_{z+1-y\in I})g(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(z-y)g(y)\,dy

where we used the periodicity of
f
and the fact that for
y\in I
and
z\in I
,

1_{z-1-y\in I}+1_{z-y\in I}+1_{z+1-y\in I}=1
. This shows that
\pi(X+Y)
has density
f\star g
.

Finally, let us recall that if
f(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(k)\exp(2i\pi kx)
and
g(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{g}(k)\exp(2i\pi kx)
, then
f\star g(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{f\star g}(k)\exp(2i\pi kx)
where

\widehat{f\star g}(k)=\hat{f}(k)\hat{g}(k).

(69)

Recall that the centered Gaussian kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with variance
\sigma^{2}{\rm Id}
converges when
\sigma\to 0
to the Dirac measure in some sense (for example, in the sense of distribution). Let us give a lemma to state a similar property for the Gaussian kernel
K^{m}_{\epsilon}
on
\mathbb{T}^{m}
(this lemma will be used in
Lemma
29
below).

Lemma 28
.

Suppose that
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{m}),
then for almost every
x\in\mathbb{T}^{m}
,
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f(x)\rightarrow f(x),
when
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}.

Proof.

This is a standard result, which can be found in
[
68
, p. 112]
for
f
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. For the sake of completeness, let us give an adaptation of the proof on
\mathbb{T}^{m}
.

Let
\tilde{f}
be the extension of
f
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. For
x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{m})\in I^{m}
,

\displaystyle K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f(x)=

\displaystyle\int_{I^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)f(x-y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)^{m}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\phi\left(\frac{y_{i}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)\tilde{f}(x_{1}-y_{1},x_{2}-y_{2},...,x_{m}-y_{m})dy_{1}dy_{2}...dy_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi_{\epsilon}\star\tilde{f}(x),

(70)

where
\Phi_{\epsilon}(y)=(\frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon})^{\frac{m}{2}}\exp(-\frac{|y|^{2}}{2\epsilon})
.

Since
\tilde{f}
is locally integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, by
[
68
, Corollary 1.6, p. 107]
, almost every point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

is a Lebesgue point of
\tilde{f}
, i.e., a point
x
such that

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mathcal{A}(x,r)=0

where

\mathcal{A}(x,r)=\frac{\int_{|y|\leq r}|\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x)|dy}{r^{m}}.

We will prove that for every Lebesgue point
x\in I^{m}
of
\tilde{f}
,
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Phi_{\epsilon}\star\tilde{f}(x)=\tilde{f}(x)=f(x)
, which, thanks to (
70
), yields the result.

Let
x\in I
be a Lebesgue point of
\tilde{f}
so that
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mathcal{A}(x,r)=0
. The function
r\mapsto\mathcal{A}(x,r)
is continuous over
(0,+\infty)
, since
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mapsto|\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x)|
is locally in
L^{1}
. Besides, we claim that the function
r\mapsto\mathcal{A}(x,r)
is bounded, which is a consequence of the fact that

\limsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{A}(x,r)<\infty.

(71)

Indeed, notice that

\frac{\int_{|y|\leq r}|\tilde{f}(x-y)|dy}{r^{m}}\leq\frac{\int_{|y|\leq r+|x|}|\tilde{f}(y)|dy}{r^{m}}.

Let us take
R\in\mathbb{N}
such that
R\leq r+|x|<R+1
, then by the periodicity of
\tilde{f},

\frac{\int_{|y|\leq r+|x|}|\tilde{f}(y)|dy}{r^{m}}\leq\frac{\int_{|y|\leq R+1}|\tilde{f}(y)|dy}{r^{m}}\leq\frac{\int_{[-R-1,R+1]^{m}}|\tilde{f}(y)|dy}{r^{m}}=\frac{4^{m}(R+1)^{m}}{r^{m}}\int_{I^{m}}|\tilde{f}(y)|dy,

and in particular
\frac{4^{m}(R+1)^{m}}{r^{m}}\leq\frac{4^{m}(r+|x|+1)^{m}}{r^{m}}
, which converges to
4^{m}
when
r\rightarrow\infty.

This yields (
71
) since

\limsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{A}(x,r)\leq|\tilde{f}(x)|m(B_{1})+4^{m}\int_{I^{m}}|\tilde{f}(y)|dy

where
m(B_{1})
is the volume of unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Let us now write
the integral

|\Phi_{\epsilon}\star\tilde{f}(x)-\tilde{f}(x)|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x))\Phi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}|\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x)|\Phi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy

over
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as a sum of integrals over annuli as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}|\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x)|\Phi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{|y|\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}}|\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x)|\Phi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{2^{k}\sqrt{\epsilon}<|y|\leq 2^{k+1}\sqrt{\epsilon}}|\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x)|\Phi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy.

We pose
c=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}})^{m}
. The first term is estimated by,

\displaystyle\int_{|y|\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}}|\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x)|\Phi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon}}\right)^{m}\int_{|y|\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}}|\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x)|\,dy=c\mathcal{A}(x,\sqrt{\epsilon}).

And since
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}2^{(k+1)m+k}\exp(-2^{2k-1})=0
, we take
c^{\prime}=c\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}2^{(k+1)m+k}\exp(-2^{2k-1})<\infty,
and thus

\displaystyle\int_{2^{k}\sqrt{\epsilon}<|y|\leq 2^{k+1}\sqrt{\epsilon}}|\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x)|\Phi_{\epsilon}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{(\sqrt{\epsilon})^{m}}\exp(-2^{2k-1})\int_{|y|\leq 2^{k+1}\sqrt{\epsilon}}|\tilde{f}(x-y)-\tilde{f}(x)|\,dy

\displaystyle=c2^{(k+1)m}\exp(-2^{2k-1})\mathcal{A}(x,2^{k+1}\sqrt{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}2^{-k}\mathcal{A}(x,2^{k+1}\sqrt{\epsilon}).

Putting these estimates together, we find that

|\tilde{f}\star\Phi_{\epsilon}(x)-\tilde{f}(x)|\leq c\mathcal{A}(x,\sqrt{\epsilon})+c^{\prime}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}\mathcal{A}(x,2^{k+1}\sqrt{\epsilon}).

Given
\delta>0
, we first choose
N
sufficiently large so that
\sum_{k\geq N}2^{-k}<\delta
. Then, since
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0+}\mathcal{A}(x,r)=0
, by making
\epsilon
sufficiently small, we have

\mathcal{A}(x,2^{k}\sqrt{\epsilon})<\frac{\delta}{N},\quad\text{whenever }k=0,1,\dots,N-1.

Hence, recalling that
\mathcal{A}(x,r)
is bounded, we find

|\tilde{f}\star\Phi_{\epsilon}(x)-\tilde{f}(x)|\leq\max(c,c^{\prime})(\sup_{r}\mathcal{A}(x,r)+1)\delta

for all sufficiently small
\epsilon
, and the theorem is proved, since
\delta
is arbitrary.
∎

A consequence of the previous result is the following lemma.

Lemma 29
.

Suppose that
\rho
is a probability density on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that
\rho\ln\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, then

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)\ln(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the limit above for any sequence
\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
On the one hand, since
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, by Lemma
28
,
K^{d}_{\delta_{n}}\star\rho\rightarrow\rho
a.e.
And since
\phi(x)=x\ln x
is lower bounded, then by Fatou’s lemma,

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(K^{d}_{\delta_{n}}\star\rho)\ln(K^{d}_{\delta_{n}}\star\rho)\geq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho.

On the other hand, by the convexity of
\phi(x)
and Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)\ln(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\phi(K^{d}_{\delta}\star\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\phi\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}K^{d}_{\delta}(x-y)\rho(y)dy\right)dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}K^{d}_{\delta}(x-y)\phi(\rho(y))dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\phi(\rho(y))dy=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho,

where
\rho\ln\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
justifies the Fubini theorem for the interchange of integrations.
Thus it holds

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(K^{d}_{\delta_{n}}\star\rho)\ln(K^{d}_{\delta_{n}}\star\rho)\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\ln\rho.

This concludes the proof.
∎

For regular functions, the convergence of
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star g
to
g
can be quantified in terms of
\epsilon
.

Lemma 30
.

There exists
C>0
such that for any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and any function
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
,
\|g-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star g\|_{\infty}\leq Cm\|D^{1}g\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\epsilon}
.
Likewise, there exists
C>0
such that for any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and any function
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
,
\|g-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star g\|_{\infty}\leq Cm^{2}\|D^{2}g\|_{\infty}\epsilon
.

Proof.

For the first inequality, notice that

\displaystyle|g(x)-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star g(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)(g(x)-g(x-y))dy\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{I^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)(g(x)-g(x-y))dy\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|D^{1}g\|_{\infty}\int_{I^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)|y|dy

which yields the conclusion using Lemma
27
.

Likewise, for the second inequality, we consider the second order Taylor expansion

g(x-y)-g(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{i}}(x)y_{i}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}R_{i,j}(x,y)y_{i}y_{j},

where
R_{i,j}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}(x-ty)\,dt
, and thus
|R_{i,j}(x,y)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|D^{2}g\|_{\infty}.

Thus, using the fact that
K^{m}_{\epsilon}
is even on
I^{m}=(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})^{m}
, it holds: for all
x\in\mathbb{T}^{m}
,

\displaystyle|g(x)-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star g(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)(g(x-y)-g(x))dy\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{I^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{i}}(x)y_{i}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}R_{i,j}(x,y)y_{i}y_{j}\right)dy\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{I^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}R_{i,j}(x,y)y_{i}y_{j}dy\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m}{2}\|D^{2}g\|_{\infty}\int_{I^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)|y|^{2}dy,

which yields the conclusion using again Lemma
27
.
∎

B.3
Estimates involving convolutions with the Gaussian kernel
K^{m}_{\epsilon}

Lemma 31
.

For any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
,

\|f-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f\|_{2}\leq\|f\|_{2}
,
\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f\|_{2}\leq\|f\|_{2}
. Thus, for any
f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T})
,
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f,f-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}),
and

\|f-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f\|_{H^{k}}\leq\|f\|_{H^{k}}
,
\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f\|_{H^{k}}\leq\|f\|_{H^{k}}
.

Proof.

This is a consequence of the Parseval equality, the equality (
69
) and the fact that the Fourier coefficients
\widehat{K^{m}_{\epsilon}}(k)=\exp(-2\pi^{2}\epsilon\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}^{2})
of
K^{m}_{\epsilon}
are in
[0,1]
. Indeed, for example, for
f:\mathbb{T}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
an
L^{2}
function,

\displaystyle\|f-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}|\widehat{(f-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f)}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}|1-\exp(-2\pi^{2}\epsilon\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}^{2})|^{2}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

The other results are obtained in a similar way.
∎

Lemma 32
.

Suppose that
m,k\in\mathbb{N^{+}}
,
2k>m
,
f_{0}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
,
\inf f_{0}>0
. Let
c=\frac{1}{2C_{\infty}}
where
C_{\infty}
has been defined in Lemma
20
.
Then there exists a constant
C>0
, such that for all
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
f_{1}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
such that
\|f_{0}-f_{1}\|_{H^{k}}\leq c\inf f_{0}
, if
f
is one of the functions
f_{0}
,
f_{1}
,
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}
, or
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}
, then
\frac{1}{f}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
and

\left\|\frac{1}{f}\right\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C}{\inf f_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\frac{\|f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}}{\inf f_{0}}\right)^{j}.

As a consequence, if
f_{0}=\exp(-\frac{A}{\alpha+1})
for some function
A\in C^{k+3}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
then there exists a constant
M>0
independent of
\alpha,\epsilon
, such that if
f
is one of the functions
f_{0}
,
f_{1}
,
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}
, or
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}
,

\left\|\frac{1}{f}\right\|_{H^{k}}\leq M.

Proof.

We only prove this result for
f=K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}
. The other cases can be proved in the same way.

First of all, by Lemma
20
, since
\|f_{0}-f_{1}\|_{H^{k}}\leq c\inf f_{0}
with
c=\frac{1}{2C_{\infty}}
,

\inf K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}\geq\inf f_{1}\geq\inf f_{0}-\|f_{0}-f_{1}\|_{\infty}\geq\inf f_{0}-C_{\infty}\|f_{0}-f_{1}\|_{H^{k}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\inf f_{0}>0.

By Lemma
31
,
K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}).
Notice that the function
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{x}\in C^{k}((0,\infty))
and satisfies that, for
j\in\mathbb{N},

\phi^{(j)}(x)=(-1)^{j}\frac{j!}{x^{j+1}}
is bounded by a constant times
(\frac{1}{\inf f_{0}})^{j+1}
over
[\frac{1}{2}\inf f_{0},\infty)\supseteq[\inf K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1},\sup K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}].
By Proposition
23
,
\phi(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1})=\frac{1}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{m}),
and

\left\|\frac{1}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C}{\inf K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\frac{\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}\|_{H^{k}}}{\inf K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}}\right)^{j}.

Then by Lemma
31
and the fact that
\inf K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}\geq\inf f_{1}
, we have that

\frac{C}{\inf K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\frac{\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}\|_{H^{k}}}{\inf K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}}\right)^{j}\leq\frac{C}{\inf f_{1}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\frac{\|f_{1}\|_{H^{k}}}{\inf f_{1}}\right)^{j}.

Finally, we have
\inf f_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}\inf f_{0}
, and
\|f_{1}\|_{H^{k}}\leq\|f_{0}-f_{1}\|_{H^{k}}+\|f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq c\inf f_{0}+\|f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq c\|f_{0}\|_{\infty}+\|f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{3}{2}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}
.
Therefore, upon changing the value of
C
, it holds

\left\|\frac{1}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{1}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C}{\inf f_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\frac{\|f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}}{\inf f_{0}}\right)^{j}.

If in addition
f_{0}=\exp(-\frac{A}{\alpha+1}),
then
\inf f_{0}
is bounded from below by a constant independent of
\alpha
, and
\|f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}
is bounded from above by a constant independent of
\alpha
, as
0\leq\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\leq 1
. This yields the second statement of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 33
.

Suppose
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+},2k>m,

f_{0}=\exp(-\frac{A}{\alpha+1})
, where

. There exists a constant

independent of
(\alpha,\epsilon)
, such that, for

,

\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star D^{\theta}f_{0}-D^{\theta}f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{\alpha+1},

and

\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star D^{\theta}\ln f_{0}-D^{\theta}\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{\alpha+1}.

Proof.

In this proof,
C
denotes a generic constant independent of
(\alpha,\epsilon)
, whose value may vary from one occurrence to another.

By Lemma
30
,

\displaystyle\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln f_{0}-\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{N}^{m},|\eta|=0}^{k}\|D^{\eta}(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln f_{0}-\ln f_{0})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{N}^{m},|\eta|=0}^{k}\|D^{\eta}(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln f_{0}-\ln f_{0})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{N}^{m},|\eta|=0}^{k}\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(D^{\eta}\ln f_{0})-D^{\eta}\ln f_{0}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq Cm^{2}\epsilon\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{N}^{m},|\eta|=2}^{k+2}\|D^{\eta}\ln f_{0}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Cm^{2}\epsilon}{\alpha+1}\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{N}^{m},|\eta|=2}^{k+2}\|D^{\eta}A\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{\alpha+1}.

The other estimates are proven in a similar way by noticing that for

and
\|D^{\eta}f_{0}\|_{\infty}
are bounded by
\frac{C}{\alpha+1},
where

is a constant independent of
\alpha
.
∎

Lemma 34
.

Suppose that
\epsilon\in(0,1],k\in\mathbb{N}^{+},2k>m,

f_{0}=\exp(-\frac{A}{\alpha+1})
, where
A\in C^{k+3}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
. There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
(\alpha,\epsilon)
, such that,

\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0})-\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{\alpha+1}.

Proof.

In the following,
C
denotes a generic constant independent of
(\alpha,\epsilon)
, whose value may vary from one occurrence to another.

By the triangle inequality,

\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0})-\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln f_{0}-\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}+\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0})-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}.

By Lemma
33
,

\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln f_{0}-\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{\alpha+1}.

Besides, by Lemma
31
,

\displaystyle\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0})-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0})-\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0})-\ln f_{0}\|_{\infty}+\sum_{|\theta|=1}\|D^{\theta}\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0})-D^{\theta}\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k-1}}.

We have that, by mean value theorem, Lemma
30
, and the explicit formula of
D^{\eta}f_{0}
, where
\eta\in\mathbb{N}^{m},|\eta|=2
,

\|\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0})-\ln f_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|\frac{1}{f_{0}}\right\|_{\infty}\|K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}-f_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq C\epsilon m^{2}\left\|\frac{1}{f_{0}}\right\|_{\infty}\|D^{2}f_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{\alpha+1}.

And, again by triangle inequality, when
|\theta|=1
,

\displaystyle\|D^{\theta}\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0})-D^{\theta}\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k-1}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star D^{\theta}f_{0}}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}}-\frac{D^{\theta}f_{0}}{f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star D^{\theta}f_{0}-D^{\theta}f_{0}}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k-1}}+\left\|\frac{D^{\theta}f_{0}}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}}-\frac{D^{\theta}f_{0}}{f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k-1}}.

By Lemma
22
, Lemma
32
, and Lemma
33
,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star D^{\theta}f_{0}-D^{\theta}f_{0}}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star D^{\theta}f_{0}-D^{\theta}f_{0}}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\frac{1}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\left\|{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star D^{\theta}f_{0}-D^{\theta}f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{\alpha+1}.

Similarly, by noticing that
\|D^{\theta}f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}
is also bounded from above by a constant independent of
\alpha
,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{D^{\theta}f_{0}}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}}-\frac{D^{\theta}f_{0}}{f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{D^{\theta}f_{0}}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}}-\frac{D^{\theta}f_{0}}{f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\frac{1}{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\left\|\frac{1}{f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k}}\left\|D^{\theta}f_{0}\right\|_{H^{k}}\left\|{K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0}-f_{0}}\right\|_{H^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{\alpha+1}.

Thus
\|\ln(K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star f_{0})-\ln f_{0}\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{\alpha+1}
and this concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 35
.

Suppose that
\epsilon\in(0,1]
. There exists a constant
C>0
, such that for any
h_{1}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{m}),h_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{m})
, it holds:

\|h_{1}K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star h_{2}-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(h_{1}h_{2})\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{\epsilon m}\|D^{1}h_{1}\|_{\infty}\|h_{2}\|_{2}

where, we recall,
\|D^{1}h\|_{\infty}=\max_{|\theta|=1}\|D^{\theta}h\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathbb{T}^{m}
, using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
27
,

\displaystyle(h_{1}(x)K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star h_{2}(x)-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(h_{1}h_{2})(x))^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)h_{2}(x-y)(h_{1}(x)-h_{1}(x-y))dy\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|D^{1}h_{1}\|_{\infty}^{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)|h_{2}(x-y)\|y|dy\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|D^{1}h_{1}\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)|y|^{2}dy\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}(y)h_{2}^{2}(x-y)dy

\displaystyle\leq Cm\epsilon\|D^{1}h_{1}\|_{\infty}^{2}K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(h_{2}^{2})(x).

Thus using the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(h_{2}^{2})(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{m}}h_{2}^{2}(x)dx
, we obtain that

\|h_{1}K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star h_{2}-K^{m}_{\epsilon}\star(h_{1}h_{2})\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{C\epsilon m}\|D^{1}h_{1}\|_{\infty}\|h_{2}\|_{2}.

which yields the result.
∎

Appendix C
About the dynamics (
21
)

In this appendix, we provide some results on the non-regularized dynamics (
21
) using entropy techniques in the spirit of
[
47
]
. As already mentioned in Section
2.3
, we do not know how to generalize such results to the non-regularized kinetic Langevin dynamics (
23
).

C.1
Preliminaries

Let us consider the non-regularized dynamics (
21
) (taking
\beta=1
for simplicity):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle={\rm div}(\nabla(U+\alpha\ln\rho^{1})\rho)+\Delta\rho

\displaystyle={\rm div}\left(\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)\nabla\frac{\rho}{\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)}\right)

\displaystyle={\rm div}\left(\rho\nabla\ln\frac{\rho}{\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)}\right)

where, we recall

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)\propto\exp(-U-\alpha\ln\rho^{1}).

Notice that
\rho^{1}
satisfies the parabolic equation (this can again be seen as a manifestation of the fact that the temperature has been multiplied by a factor
1+\alpha
along the collective variable)

\partial_{t}\rho^{1}=\partial_{1}(A_{t}^{\prime}\rho^{1})+(\alpha+1)\partial_{1,1}\rho^{1}

where

A_{t}^{\prime}(x_{1})=\frac{\int\partial_{x_{1}}U(x_{1},x_{2})\rho(t,x_{1},x_{2})\,dx_{2}}{\rho^{1}(t,x_{1})}.

In particular, one has that for all
t>0

\rho^{1}(t,\cdot)>0
, since it can be written as

\rho^{1}(t,x)=K_{t}\star\rho^{1}(0,\cdot)+\int_{0}^{t}K_{t-s}\star\int\partial_{x_{1}}U(x_{1},x_{2})\rho(s,\cdot,x_{2})\,dx_{2}\,ds

where
K_{t}
denotes the heat kernel.
In the following, we assume for simplicity that this is also true at time
t=0
.

We want to prove the long time convergence of
\rho
towards

\rho_{\infty}\propto\exp\left(-U+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}A\right).

(72)

Recall that
\rho^{1}_{\infty}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{A}{\alpha+1}\right)
. In particular,

\displaystyle A_{\infty}^{\prime}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\partial_{x_{1}}U(x_{1},x_{2})\rho_{\infty}(x_{1},x_{2})\,dx_{2}}{\rho_{\infty}^{1}(x_{1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\partial_{x_{1}}U(x_{1},x_{2})\exp(-U(x_{1},x_{2})\,dx_{2}}{\int\exp(-U(x_{1},x_{2})\,dx_{2}}

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}(x_{1})=-(\alpha+1)(\ln\rho^{1}_{\infty})^{\prime}.

Thus, the equation on
\rho^{1}
can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho^{1}

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}(A_{t}^{\prime}\rho^{1})+(\alpha+1)\partial_{1,1}\rho^{1}

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}((A_{t}^{\prime}-A_{\infty}^{\prime})\rho^{1})+(\alpha+1)\partial_{1}\left(-\ln(\rho^{1}_{\infty})^{\prime}\rho^{1}+\partial_{1}\rho^{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}((A_{t}^{\prime}-A_{\infty}^{\prime})\rho^{1})+(\alpha+1)\partial_{1}\left(\rho^{1}\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho^{1}_{\infty}}\right).

C.2
Long-time behavior

One has on the one hand

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int\rho\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\int{\rm div}\left(\rho\nabla\ln\frac{\rho}{\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)}\right)\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=-\int\rho\nabla\ln\frac{\rho}{\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)}\cdot\nabla\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=-\int\rho\left|\nabla\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}\right|^{2}-\int\rho\nabla\ln\frac{\rho_{\infty}}{\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)}\cdot\nabla\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}.

Besides

\displaystyle\nabla\ln\frac{\rho_{\infty}}{\Gamma_{\alpha}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\nabla\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}A+\alpha\ln\rho^{1}\right)=\alpha\nabla\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho_{\infty}^{1}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int\rho\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=-\int\rho\left|\nabla\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}\right|^{2}-\alpha\int\rho\,\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho_{\infty}^{1}}\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}.

(73)

On the other hand, for the marginal in
x_{1}
, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int\rho^{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho_{\infty}^{1}}

\displaystyle=\int\partial_{1}((A_{t}^{\prime}-A_{\infty}^{\prime})\rho^{1})\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho_{\infty}^{1}}+(\alpha+1)\int\partial_{1}\left(\rho^{1}\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho^{1}_{\infty}}\right)\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho_{\infty}^{1}}

\displaystyle=-\int(A_{t}^{\prime}-A_{\infty}^{\prime})\rho^{1}\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho_{\infty}^{1}}-(\alpha+1)\int\rho^{1}\left|\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho^{1}_{\infty}}\right|^{2}.

Besides, from standard results on the convergence of ABF like dynamics (see
[
47
, Lemmas 4, 5, and 6]
), one has for some constant
c>0

\int(A_{t}^{\prime}-A_{\infty}^{\prime})^{2}\rho^{1}\leq c\frac{\|\partial_{1,2}U\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\delta}\int\rho\left|\partial_{2}\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}\right|^{2}

where
\delta
is a (uniform in
x_{1}
) log-Sobolev constant of the conditional measures (indexed by
x_{1}
):
\rho_{\infty}/\rho_{\infty}^{1}=\exp(-U(x_{1},x_{2}))dx_{2}/\exp(-A(x_{1})
. Let us denote

M=c\frac{\|\partial_{1,2}U\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\delta}.

(74)

The smaller is the coupling and the easier is the sampling of the conditional measures (i.e. the larger
\delta
), the smaller is
M
.
Thus, by Young’s inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int\rho^{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho_{\infty}^{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(\alpha+1)}\int(A_{t}^{\prime}-A_{\infty}^{\prime})^{2}\rho^{1}-\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\int\rho^{1}\left|\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho^{1}_{\infty}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{2(\alpha+1)}\int\rho\left|\partial_{2}\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}\right|^{2}-\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\int\rho^{1}\left|\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho^{1}_{\infty}}\right|^{2}

(75)

By combining a Young’s inequality applied to (
73
), and (
75
), one gets (for
\epsilon,\gamma>0
to be chosen):

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int\rho\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}+\gamma\frac{d}{dt}\int\rho^{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho_{\infty}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq-\int\rho\left|\nabla\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}\right|^{2}+\epsilon\alpha\int\rho_{1}\left|\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho_{\infty}^{1}}\right|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{4\epsilon}\rho\left|\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\gamma M}{2(\alpha+1)}\int\rho\left|\partial_{2}\ln\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\infty}}\right|^{2}-\gamma\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\int\rho^{1}\left|\partial_{1}\ln\frac{\rho^{1}}{\rho^{1}_{\infty}}\right|^{2}.

To get negative signs on the right-hand side, one needs

\epsilon\alpha<\gamma\frac{\alpha+1}{2},\quad\frac{\alpha}{4\epsilon}<1,\quad\frac{\gamma M}{2(\alpha+1)}<1.

We are typically interested in the large
\alpha
regime. It is natural to choose
\epsilon=\frac{\alpha+1}{2}
so that the second constraint disappears, and one needs

\alpha<\gamma,\quad\frac{\gamma M}{2(\alpha+1)}<1

which rewrites

\alpha<\gamma<\frac{2}{M}(1+\alpha).

If
M>2
we thus requires

\alpha<\frac{2}{M-2},

otherwise, there are no constraints on
\alpha
.
Let us choose
\gamma=\frac{1}{M}+\alpha\left(\frac{1}{M}+\frac{1}{2}\right)
(the mean of the upper and lower bounds imposed on
\gamma
). One thus gets an exponential convergence of the entropy to zero with rate

r=2\min\left(D,D_{1}\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)\right)=\min\left(2D,D_{1}(\alpha+1)\frac{1+\alpha-\alpha M/2}{1+\alpha+\alpha M/2}\right)

(76)

where,
D
(resp.
D_{1}
) is a log-Sobolev inequality constant of
\rho_{\infty}
(resp.
\rho_{\infty}^{1}
), and
M
is defined by (
74
).

We thus have obtained the following result:

Lemma 36
.

Let us consider a smooth solution to the non-regularized dynamics (
20
)-(
21
). Let us denote by
D
(resp.
D_{1}
and
\delta
) a log-Sobolev constant for

For example, if
