Understanding Barriers to Home Dialysis Within an Integrated Health Care System: A National Qualitative Study Within the Department of Veterans Affairs
Authors: Yuvaram N.V. Reddy, Caroline S. O’Brien, Robert E. Burke, Ann M. O’Hare, Aneeza Z. Agha, Megan Grabill, Hilena Gebru, Lindsay Pelcher, Jacqueline A. Benson, Amanda D. Peeples, Matthew D. Kearney
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Despite federal initiatives to promote peritoneal dialysis (PD), uptake is limited within the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA).1,2 Understanding how barriers influence PD use within VA can help design implementation strategies tailored to the needs of VA clinicians and Veterans.3 In a recent national survey (the IM-HOME study), we identified 6 major barriers to home dialysis: fear, limited space, inadequate home-based support, insufficient patient education, limited reimbursement for home-based support, and lack of experienced staff.4 To what extent these barriers influence PD uptake within VA is unknown. In this letter, we describe a qualitative study that employs a structured visual aid to understand how VA clinicians, Veterans, and care partners perceive major barriers to PD.

We conducted semistructured interviews with Veterans with experience in PD, their care partners, and their VA clinicians at 9 VA Medical Centers. We purposively sampled participants from sites with lower and higher proportions of PD use (range: 3% to 40%; Item S1). During each interview, we shared a visual aid summarizing the six barriers identified in the IM-HOME study (Fig 1) to validate whether these barriers influence the VA context (as a modified member-checking approach).5 We asked participants to describe how these barriers resonated with their experiences and to identify elements they felt were missing. Interviews were conducted in-person or virtually, audio-recorded, transcribed, and deidentified. We interviewed 82 participants (50 clinicians and 32 Veterans/care partners). Participants generally agreed with the IM-HOME barriers (Table 1). For example, Veterans and care partners were fearful about initiating PD, especially when considering physical and mental comorbid conditions or nuances about their home environment (e.g., pets, stairs, privacy). Clinicians largely echoed these concerns, linking patient fear to perceived safety, infection risk, and Veteran burnout. There were some instances in which Veterans and clinicians had different perspectives on IM-HOME barriers, such as education and home-based support. Several clinicians characterized gaps in patient education, particularly for the “geriatric population” within VA. However, Veterans and care partners generally expressed strong satisfaction with education and training from PD nurses. Clinicians perceived inadequate home-based support as a common reason for not offering PD, particularly when Veterans had functional limitations and/or lived alone. In contrast, most Veterans in the study did not identify lack of home-based support as a significant barrier, although we predominantly interviewed Veterans who had succeeded in starting PD.

Participants also described barriers unique to VA. For example, Veterans and clinicians perceived VA as caring for a population with a higher burden of comorbid conditions and functional impairment, including post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and frailty, which could limit access to PD if unaddressed. Clinicians described the logistical challenges of ensuring Veterans are engaged in mental health services. Clinicians offered examples of patients who were motivated and capable, but lacked the strength or dexterity to manage PD. Additionally, Veterans described difficulty coordinating care across VA and non-VA clinical settings. Clinicians referenced greater difficulty securing institutional support for staffing and space for PD compared with hemodialysis.

Finally, although we interviewed participants from nine sites with varying PD use, we did not identify variations in themes related to the IM-HOME barriers. Instead, we identified facilitators that were common across sites. Veterans greatly valued the collaborative, long-term relationship with their PD clinicians. Those who received most or all of their care within VA expressed appreciation for the integrated nature of the VA system, including having their records and clinicians all within VA.

Our study has limitations. First, the majority of Veterans that we interviewed were receiving or had previously received PD (ie, we identified very few Veterans who were earlier in the decision-making process about whether to pursue PD). This may limit the transferability of our findings, and may explain some of the discrepant findings we identified around Veteran and VA clinician perspectives. Second, given our goal of eliciting the perspectives and experiences related to barriers of PD within VA, we used semistructured interviews rather than a standardized survey. This approach allowed us to gain a richer perspective of barriers to PD, but it also limits our ability to quantify the frequency of particular barriers within this population.

In summary, we used a structured visual aid to validate the idea that the IM-HOME major barriers broadly influence PD use within VA settings, with some caveats. Although the 6 IM-HOME barriers resonated with our study participants, our evaluation showed that implementation strategies to increase VA PD use should also address VA-specific considerations, including frailty, mental health, communication gaps, and care coordination issues. This study illustrates how qualitative inquiry can be used to adapt data from one study population to enhance our understanding of barriers within another population, such as Veterans.
