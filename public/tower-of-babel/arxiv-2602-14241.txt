On the maximum σ \sigma -irregularity of trees with given order and maximum degree Journal: Journal of Combinatorial Optimization
Milan Bašić

Abstract
The σ \sigma -irregularity index of a graph is defined as the sum of squared degree differences over all edges and provides a sensitive measure of structural heterogeneity. In this paper, we study the problem of maximizing σ ⁡ ( T ) \sigma(T) among all trees of fixed order n n and prescribed maximum degree Δ ≥ 4 \Delta\geq 4 . By expressing the problem in terms of edge–degree multiplicities, we derive a linear programming formulation and analyze its dual. This approach yields sharp upper bounds for σ ⁡ ( T ) \sigma(T) and leads to a detailed description of extremal degree–pair distributions. We show that the extremal problem can be completely resolved for the congruence classes n ≡ 1 ( mod Δ ) n\equiv 1\pmod{\Delta} and n ≡ 0 ( mod Δ ) n\equiv 0\pmod{\Delta} . When n ≡ 1 ( mod Δ ) n\equiv 1\pmod{\Delta} , the linear program admits an integral optimal solution, and the bound for σ ⁡ ( T ) \sigma(T) is tight. When n ≡ 0 ( mod Δ ) n\equiv 0\pmod{\Delta} , the linear relaxation is not attainable by any tree; nevertheless, by introducing a penalty function derived from dual slack variables, we determine the exact maximum value of σ ⁡ ( T ) \sigma(T) . In both cases, all extremal trees are characterized explicitly and consist exclusively of vertices of degrees 1 1 , 2 2 , and Δ \Delta , with edges incident to Δ \Delta -vertices playing a dominant role.

Introduction

A graph is called
irregular
if not all of its vertices have the same degree.
Quantifying the extent to which a graph deviates from regularity has long been of
interest in both theoretical and applied graph theory.
One of the earliest quantitative measures of degree heterogeneity is the

degree variance
proposed by Snijders
[
13
]
.
Subsequently, Albertson
[
3
]
introduced the now classical
irregularity index

\mathrm{irr}(G)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}|d_{G}(u)-d_{G}(v)|,

which measures the total imbalance of degrees across adjacent vertices.
This invariant, commonly referred to as the
Albertson irregularity
,
has been widely studied; see, for instance,

[
3
,
1
,
9
]
and the references therein.

Degree–based irregularity measures have proved particularly useful in applications.
In network science, they serve as indicators of structural heterogeneity in
communication and social networks
[
6
]
.
In mathematical chemistry, such indices are employed as molecular descriptors
in quantitative structure–property and structure–activity relationships
(QSPR/QSAR), where they capture variations in local bonding environments

[
7
,
11
]
.
These applications have motivated the introduction of alternative irregularity
indices that are more sensitive to large local degree differences.

Among these variants, a prominent role is played by the

\sigma
-irregularity index
, or simply the
\sigma
-index
, defined
for a graph
G=(V,E)
by

\sigma(G)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}\bigl(d_{G}(u)-d_{G}(v)\bigr)^{2}.

This index may be viewed as a quadratic analogue of Albertson’s irregularity.
By squaring degree differences,
\sigma(G)
places greater weight on edges
joining vertices with highly disparate degrees, making it a particularly
sensitive measure of structural imbalance.
The
\sigma
-index was systematically investigated in

[
2
]
, where extremal graphs maximizing
\sigma(G)
among all graphs of
fixed order were characterized.
Further fundamental properties, including the solution of the inverse

\sigma
-index problem, were obtained in
[
8
]
.
Additional comparisons with other irregularity measures and applications appear
in
[
12
]
, while Arif et al.
[
4
]
studied
\sigma(G)
and
related indices for graph families characterized by two main eigenvalues and
applied the resulting values in QSPR analysis.

A natural and active direction of research concerns extremal problems for

\sigma(G)
under additional structural constraints.
Once the extremal graphs of fixed order are known, it is natural to ask how the
problem behaves when the admissible class of graphs is restricted.
Among the most important such restrictions is the class of trees, which is both
mathematically fundamental and highly relevant in applications.
In chemical graph theory, trees model acyclic molecular structures; in this
context, one often speaks of
chemical trees
when vertex degrees are
bounded by
4
.
The
\sigma
-irregularity of chemical trees was studied in

[
10
]
, where a complete characterization of trees with maximum

\sigma
among all chemical trees of fixed order was obtained.

More recently, Dimitrov
et al.

[
5
]
initiated a systematic
study of
\sigma
-extremal trees under a prescribed maximum degree
\Delta=5
.
They established general structural properties of
\sigma
-maximal trees for
arbitrary
\Delta\geq 3
and carried out a detailed analysis for the case

\Delta=5
.
In that setting, they showed that every
n
-vertex tree maximizing
\sigma(G)

with
\Delta(G)=5
contains only vertices of degrees
1
,
2
, and
5
.
Moreover, almost all edges in such extremal trees join a leaf to a degree-
5

vertex or a degree-
5
vertex to a degree-
2
vertex, while edges between two
degree-
5
vertices or two degree-
2
vertices occur only exceptionally.
These findings support a general heuristic: for fixed maximum degree
\Delta
,
trees with large
\sigma
-irregularity tend to exhibit strongly polarized degree
distributions, with many vertices of degree
\Delta
and many leaves, while
vertices of degree
2
appear in comparable number and serve primarily as
connectors between the two extremes.

In this paper, we study the problem of maximizing the
\sigma
-irregularity index
within the class of trees of fixed order
n
and prescribed maximum degree

\Delta\geq 4
.
The problem appears difficult in full generality, as the extremal structure
depends sensitively on arithmetic relations between
n
and
\Delta
.
To address this, we reformulate the extremal problem as a linear optimization
problem over edge–degree multiplicities and analyze its dual.
This approach yields sharp upper bounds for
\sigma(T)
and allows one to derive
the extremal trees explicitly in terms of their degree distributions and
edge–degree multiplicities.

Our main results show that the extremal problem can be completely resolved for
the congruence classes
n\equiv 1\pmod{\Delta}
and
n\equiv 0\pmod{\Delta}
.
When
n\equiv 1\pmod{\Delta}
, the linear program admits an integral optimal
solution, and the resulting bound for
\sigma(T)
is tight and attained by a
unique extremal degree–pair configuration.
When
n\equiv 0\pmod{\Delta}
, the linear relaxation is not attainable by any tree.
Nevertheless, by introducing an appropriate penalty function derived from the
dual slack variables, we determine the exact maximum value of
\sigma(T)
and
obtain a complete structural characterization of all extremal trees in this
case. In both congruence classes treated here, the extremal trees consist exclusively
of vertices of degrees
1
,
2
, and
\Delta
, with edges of types
(1,\Delta)

and
(2,\Delta)
playing a dominant role.
Preliminary evidence, supported by exhaustive computer search for larger values
of
\Delta
, suggests that for increasing
\Delta
new extremal configurations
may appear that involve additional intermediate degrees, reflecting a gradual
increase of the minimum penalty value as the residue of
n
modulo
\Delta

moves away from
0
and
1
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2 we introduce notation and derive a linear programming formulation
for the maximization of
\sigma(T)
over trees with given order and maximum
degree.
In Section 3 we solve the corresponding linear program and identify the optimal degree and
edge degree–pair distributions, discussing the role of integrality and congruence
conditions on
n
.
In Section 4 we introduce a penalty function and analyze the case

n\equiv 0\pmod{\Delta}
, establishing sharp bounds and uniqueness results.
Finally, in Subsection 4.1 we provide explicit constructions of all extremal trees for
both congruence classes
n\equiv 1\pmod{\Delta}
and
n\equiv 0\pmod{\Delta}
,
thereby complementing the abstract optimization results with concrete
combinatorial realizations.

2
Linear programming formulation of the problem

Let
T
be a tree on
n
vertices with maximum degree
\Delta
.
For
1\leq i\leq\Delta
, denote by
n_{i}
the number of vertices of degree
i
, and for

1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta
, denote by
m_{i,j}
the number of edges joining a vertex
of degree
i
to a vertex of degree
j
.
Thus,

\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}m_{i,j}=|E(T)|=n-1,

and the quantities
m_{i,j}
capture the complete degree–pair structure of
T
.

Definition 2.1
.

For a connected graph
G
with edge set
E(G)
, the
sigma-regularity index

is defined by

\sigma(G)\;=\;\sum_{uv\in E(G)}\bigl(d_{G}(u)-d_{G}(v)\bigr)^{2}.

(1)

The expression for
\sigma(G)
can be restated as follows. For
1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta
,
let
m_{i,j}
denote the number of edges joining a vertex of degree
i
to a vertex
of degree
j
. Then

\sigma(G)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}m_{i,j}\,(i-j)^{2}.

The variables
n_{i}
and
m_{i,j}
are related by the following system of identities, which hold for every tree
T
:

\displaystyle n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{\Delta}

\displaystyle=n,

(2)

\displaystyle n_{1}+2n_{2}+\cdots+\Delta n_{\Delta}

\displaystyle=2n-2,

(3)

\displaystyle m_{12}+m_{13}+\cdots+m_{1\Delta}

\displaystyle=n_{1},

(4)

\displaystyle m_{12}+2m_{22}+m_{23}+\cdots+m_{2\Delta}

\displaystyle=2n_{2},

(5)

\displaystyle m_{13}+m_{23}+2m_{33}+\cdots+m_{3\Delta}

\displaystyle=3n_{3},

(6)

\displaystyle m_{1i}+m_{2i}+\cdots+m_{i-1,i}+2m_{ii}+m_{i,i+1}+\cdots+m_{i\Delta}

\displaystyle=in_{i},

(7)

\displaystyle m_{1\Delta}+m_{2\Delta}+m_{3\Delta}+\cdots+2m_{\Delta\Delta}

\displaystyle=\Delta n_{\Delta}.

(8)

Equations (
4
)–(
8
) express each
n_{i}
in terms of the edge–degree incidence numbers
m_{i,j}
.
Substituting these expressions into (
2
) and (
3
) yields two linear constraints involving only the variables
m_{i,j}
.
A straightforward computation shows that (
3
) becomes

\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}m_{i,j}=n-1,

(9)

reflecting the fact that every tree has exactly
n-1
edges, while
(
2
) transforms into

\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}\Bigl(\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{j}\Bigr)\,m_{i,j}=n.

(10)

These two constraints encapsulate all global restrictions that the tree structure imposes on the family
\{m_{i,j}\}
.

Consequently, maximizing
\sigma(T)
over all trees of order
n
and maximum degree
\Delta
becomes equivalent to maximizing the linear functional

\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}(i-j)^{2}m_{i,j}

over all nonnegative solutions
\{m_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}
satisfying
(
9
) and (
10
).
This is precisely the linear program studied in Theorem
3.2
, whose solution characterizes the extremal degree–pair distributions of trees maximizing
\sigma
.
Thus, maximizing
\sigma(T)
over all trees of order
n
and maximum degree

\Delta
is equivalent to solving the linear program
(
11
)–(
14
).

3
Solution of the linear program

We begin by determining the optimal solution of the linear relaxation,
without imposing integrality conditions on the variables
m_{i,j}
.

Remark 3.1
.

We shall use the following standard facts from linear programming.
If a primal linear program and its dual admit feasible solutions whose
objective values coincide, then both solutions are optimal (strong duality).
Moreover, in an optimal primal–dual pair, a primal variable can be positive
only if the corresponding dual constraint is tight (complementary slackness).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\Delta\geq 4
and
n\geq\Delta+2
be integers. Consider the linear program

\displaystyle\max\

\displaystyle\sigma(T)\;=\;\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}(i-j)^{2}\,m_{i,j},

(11)

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}\Bigl(\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{j}\Bigr)m_{i,j}\;=\;n,

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}m_{i,j}\;=\;n-1,

(13)

\displaystyle m_{i,j}\;\geq\;0\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta.

(14)

Then the problem admits a unique optimal solution in which

m_{1,\Delta}\;=\;\frac{(\Delta-2)n+(\Delta+2)}{\Delta},\qquad m_{2,\Delta}\;=\;\frac{2(n-\Delta-1)}{\Delta},

and

m_{i,j}=0\quad\text{for all }(i,j)\notin\{(1,\Delta),(2,\Delta)\}.

Proof.

Define

w_{i,j}:=\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{j},\qquad\sigma_{i,j}:=(i-j)^{2}.

Then the primal problem (
11
)–(
14
)
can be written in the standard form

\max\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}\sigma_{i,j}m_{i,j}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{i\leq j}w_{i,j}m_{i,j}=n,\;\sum_{i\leq j}m_{i,j}=n-1,\;m_{i,j}\geq 0.

Dual problem.

The dual linear program associated with this primal problem is

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle D(\lambda,\mu)\;=\;n\lambda+(n-1)\mu,

(15)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }

\displaystyle\lambda w_{i,j}+\mu\;\geq\;\sigma_{i,j},\quad 1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta,

where
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
are the dual variables corresponding to
constraints (
12
) and (
13
),
respectively.

For convenience, we define the dual slack function

F(i,j)\;:=\;\lambda w_{i,j}+\mu-\sigma_{i,j}.

(16)

Dual feasibility is equivalent to
F(i,j)\geq 0
for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta
.

Choice of dual variables.

We now determine
\lambda
and
\mu
by requiring that the dual constraints
be tight for the index pairs
(1,\Delta)
and
(2,\Delta)
, that is,

F(1,\Delta)=0,\qquad F(2,\Delta)=0.

(17)

Equivalently,

\lambda w_{1,\Delta}+\mu=\sigma_{1,\Delta}=(\Delta-1)^{2},\qquad\lambda w_{2,\Delta}+\mu=\sigma_{2,\Delta}=(\Delta-2)^{2}.

Since

w_{1,\Delta}=1+\frac{1}{\Delta},\qquad w_{2,\Delta}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\Delta},

subtracting the two equalities gives

(\Delta-1)^{2}-(\Delta-2)^{2}=\lambda\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\Delta}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\Delta}\Bigr)=\frac{\lambda}{2},

hence

\lambda=2\bigl((\Delta-1)^{2}-(\Delta-2)^{2}\bigr)=4\Delta-6.

(18)

Substituting this value into the first equality yields

\mu=(\Delta-1)^{2}-(4\Delta-6)\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\Delta}\Bigr)=\Delta^{2}-6\Delta+3+\frac{6}{\Delta}.

(19)

With this choice of
\lambda
and
\mu
, the slack function
(
16
) takes the explicit form

F(i,j)=(4\Delta-6)\Bigl(\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{j}\Bigr)+\Delta^{2}-6\Delta+3+\frac{6}{\Delta}-(i-j)^{2}.

(20)

Verification of dual feasibility.

Case 1:
i=1
,
1\leq j\leq\Delta
.

From (
20
),

F(1,j)=(4\Delta-6)\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{j}\Bigr)+\Delta^{2}-6\Delta+3+\frac{6}{\Delta}-(1-j)^{2}.

A direct computation yields

\Delta j\,F(1,j)=(\Delta-j)\left(\Delta j^{2}+\Delta^{2}j-2\Delta j+4\Delta-6\right).

(21)

For
1\leq j\leq\Delta-1
we have
\Delta-j>0
, so it suffices to analyze

G_{1}(j)=\Delta j^{2}+(\Delta^{2}-2\Delta)j+4\Delta-6.

This quadratic polynomial is strictly convex, with vertex at

j_{0}=-\frac{\Delta^{2}-2\Delta}{2\Delta}=-\frac{\Delta-2}{2}\leq 0,

and hence is strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
. Therefore,

\min_{1\leq j\leq\Delta-1}G_{1}(j)=G_{1}(1)=\Delta^{2}+3\Delta-6>0\quad(\Delta\geq 4).

It follows from (
21
) that

F(1,j)>0\quad\text{for }1\leq j\leq\Delta-1,\qquad F(1,\Delta)=0.

Case 2:
i=2
,
2\leq j\leq\Delta
.

Similarly,

F(2,j)=(4\Delta-6)\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{j}\Bigr)+\Delta^{2}-6\Delta+3+\frac{6}{\Delta}-(2-j)^{2},

and a direct computation yields

\Delta j\,F(2,j)=(\Delta-j)\left(\Delta j^{2}+\Delta^{2}j-4\Delta j+4\Delta-6\right).

(22)

For
2\leq j\leq\Delta-1
, consider

G_{2}(j)=\Delta j^{2}+(\Delta^{2}-4\Delta)j+4\Delta-6.

This is again strictly convex with vertex

j_{0}=-\frac{\Delta^{2}-4\Delta}{2\Delta}=-\frac{\Delta-4}{2}\leq 0,

so
G_{2}
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
and

\min_{2\leq j\leq\Delta-1}G_{2}(j)=G_{2}(2)=2\Delta^{2}-6>0.

Hence

F(2,j)>0\quad\text{for }2\leq j\leq\Delta-1,\qquad F(2,\Delta)=0.

Case 3:
3\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta
.

Using
1/i\geq 1/\Delta
and
1/j\geq 1/\Delta
, we obtain

F(i,j)\geq(4\Delta-6)\frac{2}{\Delta}+\Delta^{2}-6\Delta+3+\frac{6}{\Delta}-(i-j)^{2}.

Since
0\leq j-i\leq\Delta-3
, we have
(i-j)^{2}\leq(\Delta-3)^{2}
, and therefore

\displaystyle F(i,j)

\displaystyle\geq\Delta^{2}-6\Delta+3+\frac{6}{\Delta}+\frac{8\Delta-12}{\Delta}-(\Delta-3)^{2}

\displaystyle=2-\frac{6}{\Delta}>0\qquad(\Delta\geq 4).

Combining the three cases, we conclude that

F(i,j)\geq 0\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta,

with equality if and only if
(i,j)\in\{(1,\Delta),(2,\Delta)\}
.
Thus
(\lambda,\mu)
is dual feasible.

Construction of a primal feasible solution.

Assume
m_{i,j}=0
for all
(i,j)\notin\{(1,\Delta),(2,\Delta)\}
.
Then constraints (
12
)–(
13
)
reduce to

m_{1,\Delta}+m_{2,\Delta}=n-1,\qquad w_{1,\Delta}m_{1,\Delta}+w_{2,\Delta}m_{2,\Delta}=n.

(23)

Solving this system yields

m_{1,\Delta}=\frac{(\Delta-2)n+(\Delta+2)}{\Delta},\qquad m_{2,\Delta}=\frac{2(n-\Delta-1)}{\Delta}.

Since
n\geq\Delta+2
, both values are positive.

Optimality and uniqueness.

For the primal solution constructed above, we compute

\displaystyle\sigma(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}\sigma_{i,j}m_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1,\Delta}m_{1,\Delta}+\sigma_{2,\Delta}m_{2,\Delta}\qquad(\text{all other }m_{i,j}=0)

\displaystyle=(\lambda w_{1,\Delta}+\mu)m_{1,\Delta}+(\lambda w_{2,\Delta}+\mu)m_{2,\Delta}\qquad(\text{by }F(1,\Delta)=F(2,\Delta)=0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}(\lambda w_{i,j}+\mu)\,m_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{i\leq j}w_{i,j}m_{i,j}+\mu\sum_{i\leq j}m_{i,j}=\lambda n+\mu(n-1)=D(\lambda,\mu),

which coincides with the dual objective value at the feasible dual solution

(\lambda,\mu)
given in (
18
) and (
19
).
By strong duality, both the primal and dual solutions are optimal.

Furthermore, we have shown that

F(i,j)=\lambda w_{i,j}+\mu-\sigma_{i,j}>0\quad\text{for all }(i,j)\notin\{(1,\Delta),(2,\Delta)\},

while
F(1,\Delta)=F(2,\Delta)=0
.
By complementary slackness, this implies that in any optimal primal solution
one must have

m_{i,j}=0\quad\text{for all }(i,j)\notin\{(1,\Delta),(2,\Delta)\}.

Since the reduced system (
23
)
has a unique solution, the optimal primal solution is unique.
This completes the proof.

∎

It is worth noting that the optimal solution described in
Theorem
3.2
need not correspond to an actual tree when the
variables
m_{i,j}
are required to be integers.
The expressions for
m_{1,\Delta}
and
m_{2,\Delta}
are integral if and only if

n\equiv 1\pmod{\Delta}
.
In this case, the linear program yields the exact maximum value of
\sigma(T)
.
For all other congruence classes of
n
modulo
\Delta
, the linear program
provides a strict upper bound.

4
The penalty function and the case
n\equiv 0\pmod{\Delta}

To handle the case in which the linear programming bound is not attainable,
we introduce a penalty function measuring the deviation from dual optimality.

It is now convenient to define a penalty function associated with any tree
T
:

P(T)\;:=\;\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\Delta}F(i,j)\,m_{i,j}.

(24)

Since each
m_{i,j}
is nonnegative and each
F(i,j)
represents the slack in the dual constraint corresponding to
(i,j)
, the penalty
P(T)
measures the total deviation of
T
from dual optimality. In particular, by complementary slackness, every optimal solution of the linear program from Theorem
3.2
satisfies
P(T)=0
, and more generally we have the identity

\sigma(T)=\lambda n+\mu(n{-}1)-P(T),

where
\lambda,\mu
are the dual variables defined in (
18
) and (
19
).
Consequently, maximizing
\sigma(T)
over all trees with fixed
n
and
\Delta

is equivalent to minimizing
P(T)
over the feasible edge degree–pair distributions
of trees
m_{i,j}
.

To estimate
P(T)
from below, we analyze the dual slack
function
F(i,j)
. The next lemmas describe the location of its minimal values
and establish comparison inequalities that will be used to control the total
penalty contributed by various degree–pair configurations.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Delta\geq 4
be fixed, and let
F(i,j)
be defined as in
(
20
).
Then the following holds.

1.

For every
3\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor
, the function

j\mapsto F(i,j)
,
1\leq j\leq\Delta-1
, attains its minimum at
j=\Delta
.

2.

For every
\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor+1\leq i\leq\Delta-1
, the function

j\mapsto F(i,j)
,
1\leq j\leq\Delta-1
, attains its minimum at
j=2
.

Proof.

Fix
\Delta\geq 4
and write
A:=4\Delta-6
. For fixed
i
, the dependence of
F(i,j)

on
j
is given by

g_{i}(j):=\frac{A}{j}-(i-j)^{2},

since

F(i,j)=\left(\frac{A}{i}+\Delta^{2}-6\Delta+3+\frac{6}{\Delta}\right)+g_{i}(j).

Thus, minimizing
F(i,j)
over integers
j
is equivalent to minimizing
g_{i}(j)
.

Case 1:
3\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor
.

For
1\leq j\leq\Delta-1
, we compare
g_{i}(j)
with
g_{i}(\Delta)
.
A direct computation yields

\displaystyle g_{i}(j)-g_{i}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=A\left(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)-\bigl((i-j)^{2}-(i-\Delta)^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=(\Delta-j)\left(\frac{A}{j\Delta}-\bigl(2i-\Delta-j\bigr)\right).

Since
\Delta-j>0
, it suffices to show

\frac{A}{j\Delta}\geq 2i-\Delta-j.

Under the assumption
i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor
we have

2i-\Delta\leq 3
. Hence, for
j\geq 3
,

2i-\Delta-j\leq 3-j\leq 0,

and the desired inequality holds because
A/(j\Delta)>0
.
For
j=1,2
, we use
2i-\Delta\leq 3
to obtain

\frac{A}{\Delta}=4-\frac{6}{\Delta}\geq 2\geq 2i-\Delta-1,

\frac{A}{2\Delta}=2-\frac{3}{\Delta}\geq 1\geq 2i-\Delta-2.

Thus
g_{i}(j)\geq g_{i}(\Delta)
for all
1\leq j\leq\Delta-1
, and the minimum of

F(i,j)
is attained at
j=\Delta
.

Case 2:
\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor+1\leq i\leq\Delta-1
.

We first compare the values at
j=1
and
j=2
:

g_{i}(1)-g_{i}(2)=A\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)-\bigl((i-1)^{2}-(i-2)^{2}\bigr)=\frac{A}{2}-(2i-3)=2(\Delta-i)>0,

so
j=1
cannot be a minimizer.

For
j\geq 3
, we compute

\displaystyle g_{i}(j)-g_{i}(2)

\displaystyle=A\left(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{2}\right)-\bigl((i-j)^{2}-(i-2)^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=(j-2)\left(2i-j-2-\frac{A}{2j}\right).

Since
j-2>0
, it is enough to show that

s(j):=2i-j-2-\frac{A}{2j}\geq 0\qquad(3\leq j\leq\Delta).

Viewed as a real function on
[3,\Delta]
, we have

s^{\prime\prime}(j)=-\frac{A}{j^{3}}<0,

so
s
is concave on
[3,\Delta]
and attains its minimum at an endpoint.
Hence it suffices to verify
s(3)\geq 0
and
s(\Delta)\geq 0
.

Since

i\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor+1\geq\frac{\Delta+3}{2},

we obtain
2i\geq\Delta+3
.
Therefore

s(3)=2i-5-\frac{A}{6}=2i-4-\frac{2\Delta}{3}\geq(\Delta+3)-4-\frac{2\Delta}{3}=\frac{\Delta}{3}-1\geq 0\qquad(\Delta\geq 4).

Moreover,

s(\Delta)=2i-\Delta-2-\frac{A}{2\Delta}=2i-\Delta-4+\frac{3}{\Delta}.

If
\Delta
is even, then

\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor=\frac{\Delta}{2}+1

and hence
i\geq\frac{\Delta}{2}+2
, so
2i\geq\Delta+4
and

s(\Delta)\geq\frac{3}{\Delta}>0
.
If
\Delta
is odd, then

\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor=\frac{\Delta+3}{2}

and hence
i\geq\frac{\Delta+5}{2}
, so
2i\geq\Delta+5
and

s(\Delta)\geq 1+\frac{3}{\Delta}>0
.

Thus
s(j)\geq 0
for all
3\leq j\leq\Delta
, implying

g_{i}(j)\geq g_{i}(2)
for all
j\geq 3
.
Together with
g_{i}(1)>g_{i}(2)
, this shows that the minimum of
F(i,j)

is attained at
j=2
.
∎

We next compare the minimal values identified above with the value

F(\Delta,\Delta)
, which plays a central role in the lower bound for
P(T)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Delta\geq 4
, and let
F(i,j)
be defined as in
(
20
).
For every integer

\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor+1\leq i\leq\Delta-1

the following strict inequality holds:

i\,F(i,2)\;>\;F(\Delta,\Delta).

Proof.

Set
A:=4\Delta-6
and
B:=\Delta^{2}-6\Delta+3+\frac{6}{\Delta}
, so that

F(i,j)=A\left(\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{j}\right)+B-(i-j)^{2}.

For real
x\geq 3
, define

D(x):=x\,F(x,2)-F(\Delta,\Delta).

Using

F(x,2)=A\left(\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{2}\right)+B-(x-2)^{2},\qquad F(\Delta,\Delta)=\frac{2A}{\Delta}+B,

a straightforward simplification yields

D(x)=-x^{3}+4x^{2}+x\Bigl(\Delta^{2}-4\Delta-4+\frac{6}{\Delta}\Bigr)-\Delta^{2}+10\Delta-17+\frac{6}{\Delta}.

(25)

We first observe that
D
is strictly concave on
[3,\Delta-1]
. Indeed,
differentiating (
25
) twice gives

D^{\prime\prime}(x)=-6x+8<0\qquad(x\geq 3).

Consequently, on any subinterval of
[3,\Delta-1]
, the minimum of
D
is attained
at an endpoint.

Let

i_{0}:=\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor+1.

For
\Delta=4,5
the interval
\{i_{0},\dots,\Delta-1\}
is empty, and the claim is
vacuous. Hence we assume
\Delta\geq 6
, so that
i_{0}\leq\Delta-1
.

A direct computation shows that

D(\Delta-1)=\Delta^{2}-\Delta-2=(\Delta-2)(\Delta+1)>0.

(26)

It remains to verify that
D(i_{0})>0
.
Two cases are distinguished according to the parity of
\Delta
.

If
\Delta=2m
is even, then
m\geq 3
and
i_{0}=m+2
. Substitution into
D
gives

D(i_{0})=\frac{3m^{4}-6m^{3}+4m^{2}-14m+9}{m}=3m^{3}-6m^{2}+4m-14+\frac{9}{m}.

The cubic polynomial
p(m):=3m^{3}-6m^{2}+4m-14
is strictly increasing for
m\geq 1
,
and satisfies
p(3)=25>0
. Hence
D(i_{0})>0
for all
m\geq 3
.

If
\Delta=2m+1
is odd, then
\Delta\geq 7
implies
m\geq 3
and
i_{0}=m+3
.
In this case,

D(i_{0})=\frac{6m^{4}+m^{3}-13m^{2}-40m+4}{2m+1}.

Let
q(m):=6m^{4}+m^{3}-13m^{2}-40m+4
. For
m\geq 3
, the derivative
q^{\prime}(m)
is positive,
so
q
is increasing on
[3,\infty)
and

q(m)\geq q(3)=280>0
. Since
2m+1>0
, this yields
D(i_{0})>0
.

Combining the above with the concavity of
D
, we conclude that

D(i)\geq\min\{D(i_{0}),D(\Delta-1)\}>0

for all integers
i_{0}\leq i\leq\Delta-1
. Therefore

iF(i,2)>F(\Delta,\Delta)
throughout the stated range, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\Delta\geq 4
, and let
F(i,j)
be defined as in
(
20
).
Then for every integer

3\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor

we have the strict inequality

(\Delta-1)\,F(i,\Delta)\;>\;F(\Delta,\Delta).

Proof.

Set
A:=4\Delta-6
and
B:=\Delta^{2}-6\Delta+3+\frac{6}{\Delta}
, so that

F(i,j)=A\left(\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{j}\right)+B-(i-j)^{2},\qquad F(\Delta,\Delta)=\frac{2A}{\Delta}+B.

For fixed
\Delta
, consider the real function

\phi(x):=F(x,\Delta)=A\left(\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)+B-(\Delta-x)^{2},\qquad x\geq 3.

A direct differentiation gives

\phi^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{A}{x^{2}}+2(\Delta-x).

On the interval
[3,(\Delta+3)/2]
we have
2(\Delta-x)\geq\Delta-3
and
x^{2}\geq 9
, hence

\phi^{\prime}(x)\geq(\Delta-3)-\frac{A}{9}=(\Delta-3)-\frac{4\Delta-6}{9}=\frac{5\Delta-21}{9}.

The right-hand side is positive for all
\Delta\geq 5
.
For
\Delta=4
the interval
[3,(\Delta+3)/2]
contains only
x=3
.
Consequently,
\phi
is increasing on
[3,(\Delta+3)/2]
(for
\Delta\geq 5
), and thus for every integer

3\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor
we obtain

F(i,\Delta)=\phi(i)\geq\phi(3)=F(3,\Delta).

(27)

Therefore it suffices to prove the strict inequality at
i=3
:

(\Delta-1)F(3,\Delta)>F(\Delta,\Delta).

Indeed, using
F(3,\Delta)=A\left(\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)+B-(\Delta-3)^{2}
and

F(\Delta,\Delta)=\frac{2A}{\Delta}+B
, we compute

\displaystyle(\Delta-1)F(3,\Delta)-F(\Delta,\Delta)

\displaystyle=(\Delta-1)\left(A\Bigl(\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{\Delta}\Bigr)+B-(\Delta-3)^{2}\right)-\left(\frac{2A}{\Delta}+B\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\Delta-3)(\Delta-1)(\Delta+6)}{3\Delta}.

Since
\Delta\geq 4
, this quantity is strictly positive. Hence
(\Delta-1)F(3,\Delta)>F(\Delta,\Delta)
.
Finally, combining this with (
27
) yields

(\Delta-1)F(i,\Delta)\geq(\Delta-1)F(3,\Delta)>F(\Delta,\Delta),

for every integer
3\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor
, as claimed.
∎

The following lemma shows that, in the absence of edges of type
(\Delta,\Delta)
,
a substantial number of edges must occur among vertices of small degrees.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\Delta\geq 4
and let
T
be a tree of order
n
with maximum degree
\Delta
.
Let
n_{i}
and
m_{i,j}
satisfy the identities
(
2
)–(
8
).
Put

t:=\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor,\qquad E_{\leq t}:=\sum_{1\leq p\leq q\leq t}m_{p,q}.

Assume that
\Delta\mid n
,
that
n_{i^{\prime}}=0
for all
i^{\prime}>t
,
and that
m_{\Delta,\Delta}=0
.
Then

E_{\leq t}\;\geq\;\Delta-1.

Proof.

Let

x:=\sum_{k=1}^{\Delta-1}m_{k,\Delta}

be the number of edges incident with a
\Delta
-vertex and a non-
\Delta
-vertex.
From the
\Delta
-handshake identity (
8
) and the assumption
m_{\Delta,\Delta}=0
, we obtain

x=\Delta n_{\Delta},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad x\equiv 0\pmod{\Delta}.

(28)

Since
n_{i^{\prime}}=0
for all
i^{\prime}>t
, every edge not incident with a
\Delta
-vertex has both endpoints in

\{1,2,\dots,t\}
. Using
|E(T)|=n-1
and partitioning edges according to whether they are incident with a

\Delta
-vertex, we obtain

n-1=x+E_{\leq t}.

(29)

Reducing (
29
) modulo
\Delta
and using
\Delta\mid n
yields

x+E_{\leq t}\equiv-1\pmod{\Delta}.

Combining this with (
28
) gives

E_{\leq t}\equiv-1\pmod{\Delta}.

(30)

Since
E_{\leq t}\geq 0
and
E_{\leq t}\equiv-1\pmod{\Delta}
, the smallest possible value of
E_{\leq t}

is
\Delta-1
. Therefore
E_{\leq t}\geq\Delta-1
, as claimed.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\Delta\geq 4
and put

t:=\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor.

Then for all integers
1\leq p\leq q\leq t
we have

F(p,q)\geq F(3,\Delta).

(31)

Proof.

Recall

F(i,j)=A\left(\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{j}\right)+B-(i-j)^{2},\qquad A:=4\Delta-6.

For
1\leq p\leq q\leq t
we use the elementary bounds

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}\geq\frac{2}{t},\qquad(p-q)^{2}\leq(t-1)^{2}.

Hence

\displaystyle F(p,q)-F(3,\Delta)

\displaystyle=A\left(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)-\Bigl((p-q)^{2}-(\Delta-3)^{2}\Bigr)

(32)

\displaystyle\geq A\left(\frac{2}{t}-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)+\Bigl((\Delta-3)^{2}-(t-1)^{2}\Bigr).

Since

t=\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor\leq\frac{\Delta+3}{2},

we have

\frac{2}{t}\geq\frac{4}{\Delta+3}.

Moreover, for
\Delta\geq 8
,

\Delta-3>\frac{\Delta+1}{2}=\frac{\Delta+3}{2}-1\geq t-1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(\Delta-3)^{2}>(t-1)^{2}.

Thus, for
\Delta\geq 8
, the quadratic term

(\Delta-3)^{2}-(t-1)^{2}

is strictly positive and dominates the term

A\left(\frac{4}{\Delta+3}-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{\Delta}\right),

which is bounded below for all
\Delta\geq 4
.
Therefore, the right-hand side of (
32
) is an increasing function, and a direct computation shows that it is positive at
\Delta=8
.
Hence

F(p,q)-F(3,\Delta)\geq 0\qquad(\Delta\geq 8).

For the remaining cases
\Delta=4,5,6,7
, inequality
(
31
) is verified directly by a finite inspection over

1\leq p\leq q\leq t
.
∎

The preceding lemmas allow us to determine the exact minimum of the penalty function when
n\equiv 0\pmod{\Delta}
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\Delta\geq 4
and let
T
be a tree of order
n
with maximum degree
\Delta
,
and let
P(T)
be defined as in (
24
).
If
\Delta\mid n
, then

P(T)\geq F(\Delta,\Delta).

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
T
satisfies

m_{\Delta,\Delta}=1,\qquad m_{1,\Delta}>0,\qquad m_{2,\Delta}>0,\qquad m_{i,j}=0\ \text{for all other }(i,j),

in which case the parameters are uniquely determined by

n_{\Delta}=\frac{n}{\Delta},\qquad n_{2}=\frac{n}{\Delta}-2,\qquad n_{1}=(\Delta-2)\frac{n}{\Delta}+2,

m_{1,\Delta}=n_{1},\qquad m_{2,\Delta}=2n_{2},\qquad m_{\Delta,\Delta}=1.

Proof.

Put
A:=4\Delta-6
and
B:=\Delta^{2}-6\Delta+3+\frac{6}{\Delta}
, so that

F(i,j)=A\left(\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{j}\right)+B-(i-j)^{2},\qquad F(\Delta,\Delta)=\frac{2A}{\Delta}+B.

Assume
\Delta\mid n
and suppose, for contradiction, that

P(T)<F(\Delta,\Delta).

(33)

Elimination of the edge type
(\Delta,\Delta)
.

Under (
33
) we must have

m_{\Delta,\Delta}=0
, since a single
(\Delta,\Delta)
-edge already contributes

F(\Delta,\Delta)
to
P(T)
.

Let

t:=\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta+3}{2}\right\rfloor.

Exclusion of high intermediate degrees.

Suppose
n_{i}\geq 1
for some
i\in\{t+1,\dots,\Delta-1\}
.
By Lemma
4.1
, for such
i
the function
j\mapsto F(i,j)

attains its minimum at
j=2
.
Since a vertex of degree
i
is incident with
i
edges,

P(T)\geq i\,F(i,2).

By Lemma
4.2
, we have

iF(i,2)>F(\Delta,\Delta)
, contradicting
(
33
).
Hence

n_{i}=0\qquad(t+1\leq i\leq\Delta-1).

(34)

Exclusion of degrees
3\leq i\leq t
.

Assume next that
n_{i}\geq 1
for some
3\leq i\leq t
.
By Lemma
4.4
,

E_{\leq t}:=\sum_{1\leq p\leq q\leq t}m_{p,q}\geq\Delta-1.

By Lemma
4.5
, each such edge satisfies

F(p,q)\geq F(3,\Delta)
.
Therefore

P(T)\geq E_{\leq t}\,F(3,\Delta)\geq(\Delta-1)\,F(3,\Delta).

By Lemma
4.3
,

(\Delta-1)\,F(3,\Delta)>F(\Delta,\Delta),

again contradicting (
33
).
Thus

n_{i}=0\qquad(3\leq i\leq t).

(35)

Reduction to degrees
\{1,2,\Delta\}
.

From (
34
) and (
35
), all vertices have degree
in
\{1,2,\Delta\}
.
Since
m_{\Delta,\Delta}=0
, only the edge types

(1,2),\ (1,\Delta),\ (2,2),\ (2,\Delta)

may occur.

A direct computation shows

F(2,2)>F(\Delta,\Delta),\qquad F(1,2)>F(\Delta,\Delta).

Hence
m_{1,2}=m_{2,2}=0
, otherwise
P(T)>F(\Delta,\Delta)
.
Consequently only
(1,\Delta)
and
(2,\Delta)
edges remain, and therefore

P(T)=m_{1,\Delta}F(1,\Delta)+m_{2,\Delta}F(2,\Delta)=0.

By Theorem
3.2
, this occurs only when

n\equiv 1\pmod{\Delta}
, contradicting
\Delta\mid n
.
Thus (
33
) is impossible, and

P(T)\geq F(\Delta,\Delta).

Equality case.

Assume now that
P(T)=F(\Delta,\Delta)
.
Then
m_{\Delta,\Delta}\geq 1
, since otherwise the above argument would again
imply
P(T)=0
.
Because any additional
(\Delta,\Delta)
-edge would contribute at least

F(\Delta,\Delta)
, we must have

m_{\Delta,\Delta}=1.

All strict inequalities used above force that no other edge types occur except

(1,\Delta)
and
(2,\Delta)
.

Under the support

m_{\Delta,\Delta}=1,\qquad m_{1,\Delta}>0,\qquad m_{2,\Delta}>0,\qquad m_{i,j}=0\ \text{otherwise},

the handshake identities reduce to a linear system whose unique solution is

n_{\Delta}=\frac{n}{\Delta},\qquad n_{2}=\frac{n}{\Delta}-2,\qquad n_{1}=(\Delta-2)\frac{n}{\Delta}+2,

m_{1,\Delta}=n_{1},\qquad m_{2,\Delta}=2n_{2},\qquad m_{\Delta,\Delta}=1.

In particular, integrality requires
\Delta\mid n
, and the configuration is unique.
∎

4.1
Explicit constructions of minimizing trees

Each of the preceding theorems provides a complete characterization of the
minimizing configurations in terms of the parameters
m_{i,j}
and
n_{i}
.
To complement these algebraic descriptions, we now give explicit constructive
descriptions of all trees attaining the minimum penalty.

For each congruence class of
n
modulo
\Delta
, we define a natural family of
trees and show that a tree
T
with given order
n
and maximum degree
\Delta

attains the minimum possible value of
P(T)
if and only if
T
belongs to that
family, up to isomorphism.

Let
k\geq 1
.
We denote by
TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
the tree obtained from the path

P_{2k+1}=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{2k+1}
by attaching exactly
\Delta-2
pendant vertices
to each even vertex
v_{2},v_{4},\dots,v_{2k}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
k\geq 1
and set
n=\Delta k+1
.
Then the tree
TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
has order
n
and maximum degree
\Delta\geq 4
,
and satisfies

P\bigl(TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)\bigr)=0.

Moreover, a tree
T
of order
n
and maximum degree
\Delta
satisfies
P(T)=0

if and only if
T\cong TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
.

Proof.

By construction, the tree
TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
is obtained from the path

P_{2k+1}
by attaching exactly
\Delta-2
pendant vertices to each even vertex.
Hence it has

n_{1}=k(\Delta-2)+2,\qquad n_{2}=k-1,\qquad n_{\Delta}=k,

and no vertices of any other degree. Moreover, all edges are of types
(1,\Delta)
or
(2,\Delta)
, so by the
definition of the penalty function and complementary slackness we obtain

P\bigl(TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)\bigr)=0
.

We now prove the converse statement.
Let
T
be a tree of order
n=\Delta k+1
and maximum degree
\Delta
such that

P(T)=0
.
Then, by Theorem
3.2
, the only nonzero edge–degree
multiplicities are

m_{1,\Delta}=k(\Delta-2)+2,\qquad m_{2,\Delta}=2k-2,

and all other
m_{i,j}
vanish.
Consequently,
T
has the same degree sequence as
TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
,
namely

n_{1}=k(\Delta-2)+2,\qquad n_{2}=k-1,\qquad n_{\Delta}=k.

We prove that
T\cong TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
by induction on
k
.

Base case
k=1
.

In this case,
n=\Delta+1
and the degree sequence reduces to

n_{1}=\Delta
,
n_{\Delta}=1
.
Hence
T
is the star
S_{\Delta}
, which coincides with
TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(1)
.

Induction step.

Assume that the statement holds for some
k\geq 1
, and let
T
be a tree of order

n=\Delta(k+1)+1
satisfying
P(T)=0
.
Consider a longest path in
T
and let
u
be one of its endpoints.
Then
u
is a leaf, and its unique neighbor
v
has degree
\Delta
.

Among the
\Delta-1
remaining neighbors of
v
, exactly
\Delta-2
must be
leaves.
Indeed, if at least two of these neighbors had degree at least
2
, then the
path could be extended beyond
u
, contradicting the maximality of the chosen
path.
Therefore,
v
has exactly
\Delta-2
leaf neighbors and exactly one neighbor of
degree
2
.

Remove the vertex
v
together with all its leaf neighbors (there are
\Delta-1

of them, including
u
).
The resulting graph
T^{\prime}
is a tree of order
\Delta k+1
.
A direct inspection shows that this operation reduces the number of edges of
type
(1,\Delta)
by
\Delta-2
and the number of edges of type
(2,\Delta)
by

2
, while all other edge–degree multiplicities remain zero.
Thus
T^{\prime}
has the degree sequence and edge–degree distribution corresponding to

TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
, and hence satisfies
P(T^{\prime})=0
.

By the induction hypothesis,
T^{\prime}\cong TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
.
Reattaching the vertex
v
together with its
\Delta-2
pendant neighbors in the
unique admissible way reconstructs the tree
TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k+1)
.
Therefore
T\cong TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k+1)
.

This completes the induction and the proof.
∎

Let
k\geq 1
and set
n=\Delta k+\Delta
.
We denote by
TT^{0}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
the family of trees obtained from

TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
by subdividing exactly one edge
v_{i}v_{i+1}
with odd

i
, where
3\leq i\leq 2k-1
, and then attaching
\Delta-2
pendant vertices to the
new subdivision vertex.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
k\geq 1
and set
n=\Delta k+\Delta
.
Then a tree
T
of order
n
and maximum degree
\Delta
satisfies

P(T)=F(\Delta,\Delta)

if and only if
T\in TT^{0}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
.

Proof.

Fix
k\geq 1
and put
n=\Delta k+\Delta
.
By Theorem
4.6
, a tree
T
of order
n
and maximum degree

\Delta
satisfies
P(T)=F(\Delta,\Delta)
if and only if

m_{\Delta,\Delta}=1,\qquad m_{1,\Delta}=(\Delta-2)k+\Delta,\qquad m_{2,\Delta}=2k-2,

and all other edge–degree multiplicities vanish.
Equivalently,

n_{\Delta}=k+1,\qquad n_{2}=k-1,\qquad n_{1}=(\Delta-2)k+\Delta.

Let
T\in TT^{0}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
.
By construction,
T
is obtained from
TT^{1}_{\mathrm{opt}}(k)
by subdividing one
edge of type
(2,\Delta)
on the defining path and attaching
\Delta-2
pendant
vertices to the new subdivision vertex.
This operation increases the number of degree–
\Delta
vertices by one, creates
exactly one edge of type
(\Delta,\Delta)
, increases the number of edges of type

(1,\Delta)
by
\Delta-2
, and leaves the number of edges of type
(2,\Delta)

unchanged.
All remaining edges stay of type
(1,\Delta)
or
(2,\Delta)
.
A direct count yields the above parameter values, and hence

P(T)=F(\Delta,\Delta)
.

Conversely, let
T
satisfy
P(T)=F(\Delta,\Delta)
.
Then
T
contains a unique edge of type
(\Delta,\Delta)
.
By Theorem
4.6
,
m_{2,\Delta}>0
and

m_{2,i}=0
, for
i\neq\Delta
, so every
2
-vertex lies on a
\Delta
–
2
–
\Delta
path.
Since
n_{2}=k-1
, these vertices connect all
\Delta
-vertices except for one
additional adjacency, which must be the unique
(\Delta,\Delta)
-edge.
Hence one endpoint
s
of this edge has exactly one
2
-neighbor and therefore
exactly
\Delta-2
pendant neighbors.
Let
s
be an endpoint of this edge that is adjacent to
\Delta-2
leaves.
Remove these
\Delta-2
pendant vertices and suppress

, that is, delete
s
and
replace its two remaining incident edges by a single edge.
The resulting tree
T^{\circ}
has order
\Delta k+1
, maximum degree
\Delta
, and
contains only edges of type
(1,\Delta)
and
(2,\Delta)
.
Thus

, and by Lemma

Reversing the suppression shows that

is obtained from

by subdividing exactly one
(2,\Delta)
-edge on the

\Delta-2
pendant vertices to the new vertex.

.
∎

Together, Lemmas

and

give explicit constructions

and penalty-function analysis.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we investigated the problem of maximizing the

\sigma
-irregularity index among trees of fixed order
n
and prescribed maximum
degree
\Delta\geq 4
.
By reformulating the extremal problem as a linear optimization problem over
edge–degree multiplicities and exploiting its dual, we derived sharp upper

Our results indicate that extending the present analysis to arbitrary values of

As

so does the minimum value of the associated penalty function.
Preliminary evidence, supported by computer-assisted exhaustive searches,

intermediate degrees.
In such cases, new structural patterns emerge that are not captured by the
degree sets
\{1,2,\Delta\}

While the penalty-based optimization framework developed in this paper is
effective for configurations with small penalty values, it remains an open
problem whether all
\sigma
-maximal trees for larger penalties can be
characterized using a purely case-based analysis, even when supplemented by
computer search.
Addressing this question will likely require new ideas that combine optimization
methods with deeper combinatorial insights into the global structure of trees
with highly heterogeneous degree distributions.
