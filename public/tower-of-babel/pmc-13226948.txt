Establishment of an inducible knockout model for the chicken Z-chromosome-linked gene DMRT1
Authors: Guangzheng Liu, Zhi Cao, Yufan He, Xilin Zhu, Malik Ahsan Ali, Hongyan Sun, Qisheng Zuo, Yingjie Niu, Jiuzhou Song, Wei Han, Wanhong Wei, Guohong Chen, Bichun Li, Kai Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sex determination is a crucial process in animal development, regulated by complex genetic networks. In avian species, Doublesex and mab-3 related transcription factor 1 (DMRT1) plays a vital role in gonadal development and sex determination. To unravel the function of DMRT1 in chicken sex determination, establishing an inducible DMRT1 knockout model is essential. In this study, we constructed an inducible DMRT1 knockout system and verified its efficiency and effects on related genes and physiological indicators. To achieve precise genomic ablation, we screened multiple sgRNAs targeting the DMRT1 locus and integrated the optimal sequence into a doxycycline-responsive (Tet-on) CRISPR/Cas9 architecture. For in vitro experiments, vectors were delivered via cell transfection and induced with 20 µg mL–1 doxycycline (DOX), achieving an 80% knockout efficiency. Following the administration of polyethylenimine (PEI)-encapsulated plasmids into chicken embryos, we successfully implemented the inducible system in vivo. Quantitative analysis confirmed a mosaic knockout of DMRT1 with an observed efficiency reaching 45%. Following targeted disruption, we evaluated sex-related gene and protein expression alterations via qRT-PCR and Western blot (WB). Furthermore, ELISA was performed to measure testosterone levels in male embryonic gonads across multiple developmental stages (E4.5 to E18.5). qRT-PCR analysis showed that after induction, female-related genes (CYP19A1, FOXL2, ESR1) were significantly upregulated, and male-related genes (DMRT1, SOX9, AMH) were significantly downregulated. WB results revealed increased protein expression levels of CYP19A1 and FOXL2, and decreased protein expression of SOX9 post-induction. ELISA confirmed that testosterone levels in the gonads of induced male embryos were significantly reduced compared to normal and non-induced males. The study successfully established an inducible DMRT1 knockout system in chickens. This system effectively regulates the expression of sex-related genes and reduces testosterone levels in male embryos, providing theoretical and technical support for breeding novel sex-controlled breeding materials.

Introduction

Sex determination is an important and complex process in biology, influences the sex development and reproductive function of individuals (Hirst et al., 2018). In the production of laying hens and broilers, there is a great difference in the economic benefits produced by chickens of different sexes, and it is often necessary to characterize the sex of the chickens and then cull the unwanted chicks, resulting in a great deal of waste. If it is possible to produce chickens of a specific sex by controlling the sex of the chickens, the waste of resources due to sex identification and elimination can be greatly avoided, and the economic efficiency of the breeding enterprises can be improved. Chicken sex control is an important research area in production, which involves understanding and manipulating the mechanisms of male and female sex determination in chickens, but no switch genes have been identified that can directly control sex.

DMRT1, a key member of the DMRT family, plays a critical role in sex determination and gonadal development in particular (Major and Smith, 2016). The DMRT1 gene is thought to plays an important role, which is located on the chicken Z chromosome and is considered a key sex control gene (Hirst et al., 2018), which is located between 26.45 Mb - 26.50 Mb on the chicken Z chromosome, encodes for an mRNA length of 1,244 bp and consists of six exons. In past studies, it was shown that the expression pattern of the DMRT1 gene is closely related to the sex of chickens and has a significant role in sex determination in chickens. The expression level of DMRT1 gene is higher in male chickens and lower in female chickens (Smith et al., 2009; Major et al., 2019). As early as 1999, Raymond et al. found that DMRT1 is expressed in the germinal crest and Wolf's canal prior to sex differentiation in chickens, and at higher levels in ZZ-type embryos than in ZW embryos (Raymond et al., 1999). Shan et al. found that DMRT1 is more highly expressed in the genital ridge of males than in that of females at E3.5d (Shan et al., 2000). Accordingly, Nanda et al. proposed that DMRT1 is the earliest gene dosage-sensitive factor for sex determination in vertebrates (Nanda et al., 2000). More recently, Ioannidis et al. utilized a CRISPR/Cas9 single-allele targeting approach to mutate DMRT1. They found that chromosomally male (Z + Z-) chickens with only a single functional copy of DMRT1 developed ovaries, providing direct evidence that the avian DMRT1 gene dosage (two copies versus one copy) dictates the fate of testis formation (Ioannidis et al., 2021). As a result, the DMRT1 gene is recognized as one of the major regulators promoting male sex development (Estermann et al., 2021). Although the functional importance of DMRT1 in avian sex determination has been well established, its precise molecular regulatory mechanisms in chickens require further investigation. Notably, because DMRT1 is a dosage-sensitive Z-linked male-determining gene, its inducible knockout in ZZ embryos is expected to disrupt the testicular differentiation pathway and potentially activate the female program, including upregulation of ovary-promoting genes such as CYP19A1, FOXL2, and ESR1. Therefore, in the present study, we examined both male-related genes and testosterone levels, as well as female-related genes, to comprehensively evaluate the consequences of DMRT1 deficiency on gonadal sex differentiation.

In recent years, the rapid development of gene editing technology has provided new means to study the function of the DMRT1 gene in sex determination. In particular, the widespread use of CRISPR/Cas9 technology has enabled researchers to perform precise editing and knockdown of specific genes (Sasaki et al., 2014). Lee et al. used CRISPR/Cas9 to disrupt the DMRT1 start codon, resulting in the early development of male chicken embryos at the stage of gonadal feminization (Lee et al., 2021). Despite its efficiency, conventional CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing often lacks temporal precision, as the initiation of genomic cleavage is governed by the stochastic expression and binding kinetics of the Cas9-gRNA complex. This absence of temporal control can trigger premature gene disruption, potentially leading to confounding pleiotropic effects or developmental arrest in embryonic models, thereby complicating the functional analysis of early-acting factors (Cong et al., 2013; Mali et al., 2013). To address these limitations, inducible regulatory architectures, such as the Tet-on system, have been integrated with CRISPR/Cas9 to enable ligand-dependent activation of gene ablation. By utilizing doxycycline as a chemical switch, this platform provides a sophisticated mechanism for the reversible and time-resolved orchestration of Cas9 expression (Gossen and Bujard, 1992; Platt et al., 2014). Such modular control allows researchers to bypass critical early developmental phases and initiate gene silencing during specific phenotypic windows, offering a level of precision that constitutive systems cannot achieve (Kang et al., 2019). The system allows for more precise control over the timing and location of gene editing. By adding or removing the inducer at the right time, the CRISPR/Cas9 intervention can be selected to be implemented within a specific time window to fulfill the research needs (Zhang et al., 2019; Li et al., 2025). Therefore, in this study, we combined the CRISPR/Cas9 technology with the Tet-on system to construct an inducible DMRT1 knockout model. By overcoming the uncontrollable response time of conventional CRISPR/Cas9 editing, the successful establishment of this inducible system provides a highly controllable and reliable tool to precisely investigate the function of the DMRT1 gene in avian sex determination.

In this investigation, we developed a robust doxycycline-inducible framework tailored for the targeted ablation of the Z-linked DMRT1 gene in chickens. Our findings demonstrate that this system facilitates highly efficient genomic editing, achieving 80% disruption in cultured cells and 45% in vivo. By modulating the onset of DMRT1 deficiency, this model provides a novel vantage point from which to examine the secondary regulatory networks and hormonal shifts that drive avian sex differentiation, ultimately contributing to the strategic advancement of poultry sex-control technologies.

Materials and methods

Animals

Unincubated, fertilized Rugao yellow chicken eggs were procured from the breeding facility of the Poultry Research Institute (Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Yangzhou, China). All animal handling protocols and experimental procedures strictly adhered to the U.S. National Institutes of Health guidelines (NIH Pub. No. 85-23, revised 1996) and received formal approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Yangzhou University (Approval ID: 202203074).

Experimental design

As described above, the primary experimental subjects for this study were the embryonated eggs; adult animals were not directly subjected to any experimental procedures. The overall experimental design was structured into two main phases to systematically establish a controllable gene knockout system. Phase I focused on the constitutive (non-inducible) knockout of the DMRT1 gene. This involved designing multiple sgRNAs, screening for the highest knockout efficiency in vitro, and subsequently validating the cleavage activity of the optimal vector (designated DMRT1-KO) in vivo via in ovo injection. Phase II aimed to develop and validate the targeted Tet-on DOX-inducible CRISPR/Cas9 system. Following the successful construction of the DOX-inducible DMRT1 knockout vector, its precise controllability was first verified in vitro using gradient DOX concentrations. Finally, the inducible system was applied in vivo. For all in vivo experiments, the respective vector systems were encapsulated with PEI and injected into the unhatched eggs prior to incubation (embryonic day 0). Because the genetic sex of the avian embryos could not be determined prior to incubation, a retrospective sex identification strategy was employed post-harvest. Only the tissues from confirmed genetically male (ZZ) embryos were utilized for subsequent phenotypic evaluations, gene and protein expression analyses, and hormone measurements to elucidate the role of DMRT1 in male sex differentiation.

Construction of TRE-Cas9-rtTA-Puro and sgRNA-sy004 vectors

To establish the inducible DMRT1 knockout framework, a dual-vector strategy was employed. The system comprised one plasmid encoding the inducible Cas9 cassette and a second harboring the specific sgRNA. As validated in our previous study focusing on a different target (CYP19A1) (Liu et al., 2025), the TRE-Cas9-rtTA-Puro vector was utilized here with the previously optimized doxycycline (DOX; Solarbio, Beijing, China) induction concentration of 20 µg mL–1. The most efficient guide sequence (sgRNA1) was cloned into the sy004 vector to yield the final DMRT1-sgRNA construct.

Cell transfection

Douglas Foster-1, chicken embryo fibroblasts cell line (DF-1) and Chicken Embryo Fibroblasts (CEF) were adjusted to a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells mL–1 and cultured to 80% confluency. Following the protocol provided with the Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), 1 µg of plasmid and 1.5 µL of transfection reagent were mixed in 25 µL of Opti-MEM™ I Reduced Serum Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The resulting mixture was gently added to the cells, and fluorescence observation as well as sample collection were performed 48 h after transfection.

RNA isolation, reverse transcription, and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from harvested cells (1 × 10⁶) using Trizol reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and purified via chloroform-isopropanol precipitation. RNA integrity and yield were assessed utilizing a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript® III RT SuperMix (+gDNA wiper) Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), which incorporated a genomic DNA elimination step. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was executed utilizing the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) in a 20 µL reaction volume. The thermocycling parameters consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 15 s. Analyzed transcripts included endogenous sex-determining factors (AMH, SOX9, FOXL2, ESR1, CYP19A1, DMRT1) and exogenous vector markers (Cas9, PURO), with β-ACTIN serving as the internal normalization control (Primer sequences in Table 2).

Western blot

Total protein from cells and tissues was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Tiangen, Beijing, China) on ice, and concentrations were subsequently determined. Equivalent amounts of protein (20 µg) were denatured in 6 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), resolved by SDS-PAGE, and electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Non-specific binding was blocked using 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 2 h. Membranes were then probed overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against GFP (Poteintec, Wuhan, China, 66002-1-Ig, 1:200,000), FOXL2 (Poteintec, Wuhan, China, 19672-1-AP, 1:1,000), SOX9 (Poteintec, Wuhan, China, 67439-1-IG, 1:10,000), CYP19A1 (Bio-Rad, Beijing, China, MCA2077, 1:5,000), and Cas9 (Abcam, Shanghai, China, ab189380, 1:5,000). Subsequent to primary antibody binding, the membranes were exposed to the respective secondary antibodies—specifically, Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (CWBIO, Taizhou, China, CW0102S, 1:2000) and Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (CWBIO, Taizhou, China, CW0103S, 1:2000)—for 2 h at room temperature. Following extensive final washes with TBST, the immunoreactive protein bands were captured utilizing an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) approach.

Luciferase-SSA activity assay

To construct the SSA reporter plasmid, the specific sgRNA target sequences were cloned into the luciferase coding region immediately upstream of the terminator within the Luciferase-SSA backbone. For the transfection assays, cells were divided into two cohorts: the experimental group received a co-transfection comprising the reporter, donor, and knockout vectors, whereas the control group was transfected with an empty vector. Forty-eight hours following transfection, the cells were collected, and a luminometer was employed to measure the resultant luciferase activity.

T7E1 enzyme digestion assay

Following transfection, genomic DNA was extracted from the cell cultures (1 × 10⁶ cells/mL) utilizing the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, China). This purified DNA served as the template to amplify a region of interest spanning roughly 500 bp on either side of the targeted cleavage site. The amplification was executed using the PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) alongside three specific primer pairs (DMRT1sg1, DMRT1sg2, and DMRT1sg3; see Table 1 for sequences). The resulting amplicons were then processed with the T7 Endonuclease I Kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). The heteroduplex formation and enzymatic cleavage were carried out using a specialized thermal profile: an initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, a gradual annealing phase (95°C for 2 s, cooling at −0.1°C/2 s down to 75°C, then cooling at −0.1°C/1 s to 16°C), followed by a 2-min hold at 16°C. The digestion was finalized at 37°C for 30 min. Finally, the cleavage fragments were resolved and quantified via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Thymine and adenine (TA) cloning

To precisely identify the mutation profiles, the ∼500 bp PCR amplicons encompassing the cleavage sites were integrated into a T-vector utilizing the 5 min TA/Blunt-Zero Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). These ligated constructs were then introduced into Trelief™ 5α chemically competent cells (Tsingke, Beijing, China) via standard transformation procedures. From the resulting LB agar plates, 15 individual colonies per group were randomly selected and submitted for Sanger sequencing analysis.

Sex identification of embryos

Because the genetic sex of chicken embryos cannot be visually determined prior to incubation, a total of 300 embryonated eggs were randomly selected for the in ovo injections and subsequent incubation. Following the collection of embryonic tissues at specific developmental stages (E4.5 to E18.5), retrospective sex identification was performed. Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from a small piece of tissue (e.g., limb bud or chorioallantoic membrane) using a TIANGEN DNA Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). PCR amplification targeting the sex-specific CHD1 gene was then conducted to distinguish between genetically male (ZZ) and female (ZW) embryos. Only the gonads and associated tissues confirmed to be from genetically male embryos were retained for downstream DMRT1 gene expression, protein, and testosterone level analyses.

PEI-mediated injection

To prepare the injection solutions, the plasmid and PEI were first diluted separately before complexation. For the constitutive (non-inducible) DMRT1-KO vector, the DNA mixture consisted of 12 µg of plasmid diluted in PBS to a volume of 40 µL per egg. The transfection reagent mixture consisted of 18 µg of PEI diluted in PBS to a volume of 60 µL per egg. For the DOX-inducible DMRT1 knockout system, the DNA mixture was modified by incorporating DOX; specifically, 12 µg of plasmid and DOX (final concentration of 20 µg/µL) were mixed with PBS to a volume of 40 µL per egg. The PEI mixture remained identical (18 µg of PEI in 60 µL of PBS per egg). To form the complexes for both systems, the DNA mixture was added dropwise into the corresponding PEI mixture while vortexing thoroughly to ensure uniform mixing. The combined solution (100 µL total volume per egg) was then incubated at 37°C for 15 min. Following incubation, the encapsulated liquid was injected directly into the air cell of the embryonated eggs (in ovo injection at embryonic day 0) using a syringe. The injection sites were then sealed with paraffin. A total of 300 embryonated eggs were immediately transferred to a fully automated incubator (Rcom MARU MAX; Autoelex Co., Ltd., Gimhae, South Korea). The incubation was maintained under standard physiological conditions for chicken embryo development, specifically at a constant temperature of 37.8°C and a relative humidity of 55–60%, with automatic egg turning programmed every 2 h. Embryos were subsequently harvested at the designated developmental stages for analyses.

ELISA detection

To quantify testosterone levels, gonads were harvested from male chicken embryos at specific developmental stages (embryonic days 4.5, 6.5, 8.5, 10.5, 12.5, 14.5, 16.5, and 18.5). Testosterone levels were measured using a commercial Testosterone ELISA Kit (Catalog No. CB10076-Ch; COIBO BIO, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The excised tissues were homogenized in 200 µL of PBS to generate cell suspensions. For the ELISA procedure, 100 µL of the prepared standards, 10 µL of the sample homogenate, and 90 µL of a biotin-conjugated detection antibody were dispensed into the microplate wells and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The plate was subsequently washed five times with wash buffer and blotted dry. Next, 100 µL of the streptavidin-biotin complex (SABC) reagent was added per well, followed by a 20-min incubation at 37°C. After a secondary washing step, 100 µL of the substrate working solution was applied, and the plate was incubated in the dark at 37°C for 15 min. The enzymatic reaction was terminated with 50 µL of stop solution, and the optical absorbance at 450 nm was immediately recorded using a microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates, with representative images presented in the corresponding figures. For statistical analyses, an independent samples Student’s t-test was utilized for comparisons between two groups. For comparisons among three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Prior to statistical analysis, all percentage data were subjected to arcsine transformation. The results are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with significance levels denoted as follows: * P < 0.05; ** P < 0.01; *** P < 0.001; **** P < 0.0001.

Results

Establishment of an in vitro DMRT1 gene knockout system

To construct an in vitro gene-editing model, the genomic sequence of the chicken DMRT1 locus (Gene ID: 769693) was accessed via the NCBI repository. Specific guide RNA sequences were mapped, localizing sgRNA1 to exon 2, while both sgRNA2 and sgRNA3 were designed to target exon 1 (Fig. 1A). Following the synthesis and annealing of the corresponding oligonucleotides, the resulting double-stranded fragments were successfully cloned into the VK001-08 backbone. Sequence verification confirmed the integrity of the generated constructs, which were subsequently designated as DMRT1-sgRNA1, DMRT1-sgRNA2, and DMRT1-sgRNA3 (Fig. 1A). Upon introducing these three distinct targeting vectors into DF-1 cells, robust EGFP fluorescent signals were visualized across all experimental cohorts at 48 h post-transfection (Fig. 1B). Molecular validation via qRT-PCR revealed a marked elevation in the transcript levels of Cas9 and EGFP in the vector-treated cells relative to the mock-transfected controls (Fig. 1C). These transcriptional findings were further corroborated at the translational level; WB assays displayed distinct Cas9 and EGFP protein bands exclusively within the knockout vector groups, with no detectable expression in the control (Fig. 1D). Verification of the DMRT1 gene knockout system

To screen the sgRNA with the highest DMRT1 knockout efficiency, three sgRNA target sites were cloned into the Luciferase-SSA reporter vector to construct DMRT1-SSA1, DMRT1-SSA2, and DMRT1-SSA3 (Fig. 2A). The constructed DMRT1-SSA1, DMRT1-SSA2, DMRT1-SSA3, and the donor vector were co-transfected into DF-1 cells. Results showed that the Luciferase activities of sgRNA1, sgRNA2, and sgRNA3 were extremely significantly different from the control group, indicating all three sgRNAs possessed knockout activity (Fig. 2B). To precisely isolate the most potent candidate, flow cytometry was utilized to enrich for successfully transfected (fluorescent-positive) cells. Subsequent PCR amplification targeted the genomic regions surrounding the cleavage sites (yielding ∼488 bp for sgRNA1 and ∼491 bp for sgRNA2/3). The resulting amplicons were subjected to T7E1 assays. The appearance of three distinct cleavage products in the experimental groups—contrasting with the single intact band in the mock control—substantiated the editing function of all designs (Fig. 2C). Gray value analysis of the bands showed that the knockout activity of DMRT1-sgRNA1 was 74.07%, DMRT1-sgRNA2 was 67.23%, and DMRT1-sgRNA3 was 56.50% (Fig. 2D). To determine the editing efficiency in vitro, the constructed sgRNA expression vectors were transfected into DF-1 cells. Genomic DNA was extracted, and the target DMRT1 region was amplified for TA cloning and Sanger sequencing. The sequencing results of the selected DMRT1-sgRNA1 vector showed that 12 out of 15 randomly selected bacterial clones contained mutations. This indicated an in vitro knockout efficiency of 80% (Fig. 2E). Because it exhibited the highest efficiency among the candidates, this vector was selected for subsequent in vivo experiments and designated as DMRT1-KO. The DMRT1-KO vector was transfected into CEF cells, and fluorescent expression was observed at 48 h post-transfection (Fig. 2F). qRT-PCR results demonstrated that compared with the control group, the expression levels of female-related genes (CYP19A1, FOXL2, and ESR1) were significantly increased, while the expression levels of male-related genes (DMRT1, AMH, and SOX9) were significantly decreased in DMRT1-KO-transfected cells (Fig. 2G). The DMRT1-KO vector was encapsulated with PEI and injected in ovo prior to incubation. At embryonic days 5.5 and 18.5, tissues were collected and subjected to PCR-based sex identification. A total of 24 confirmed genetically male (ZZ) embryos constituted the experimental group for the T7E1 digestion assay. Three bands were detected in 10 out of these 24 male embryos, confirming that the DMRT1-KO vector could effectively cleave the DMRT1 gene in vivo with a knockout efficiency of approximately 42% (Fig. 2H). Sequence analysis via TA cloning of the T7E1-positive embryonic samples definitively verified target site mutagenesis across all evaluated specimens, reinforcing the in vivo editing capability of the DMRT1-KO system (Fig. 2I). Morphological assessment of male gonads at E18.5 indicated that DMRT1 depletion triggered abnormal, asymmetric testicular growth, highlighting severe developmental impairment relative to normal controls (Fig. 2J). qRT-PCR analysis of gonads excised from T7E1-positive, genetically male (ZZ) chicken embryos at embryonic days 5.5 and 18.5 revealed that, compared with the control group, the in ovo injection of the DMRT1-KO vector significantly increased the expression levels of female-related genes (CYP19A1, FOXL2, and ESR1) and significantly decreased the expression levels of male-related genes (DMRT1, SOX9, and AMH) at both time points (Fig. 2K). Concordantly, densitometric quantification of Western blots from E5.5 and E18.5 tissues corroborated these transcriptional changes at the translational tier; protein levels for CYP19A1 and FOXL2 surged, concomitant with a sharp decline in SOX9 expression (Figs. 2L-M). Collectively, these results demonstrated that the DMRT1-KO vector could function in vivo to knockout DMRT1 and regulate the expression of sex-related genes. Chicken embryos from 4.5 days to 18.5 days post-injection were subjected to sex identification. Gonads from male chicken embryos were collected to detect testosterone levels by ELISA. ELISA results showed that testosterone levels were reduced in DMRT1-KO-injected embryos compared with normal male gonads (Fig. 2N). Development of a CRISPR/Cas9-based DOX-inducible DMRT1 gene knockout system in vitro

To engineer a controllable ablation platform for DMRT1, the optimized guide sequence from the constitutive KO vector was transferred into the sgRNA-sy004 backbone. Sequence integrity of the resulting single-guide construct, designated DMRT1-sgRNA, was verified via Sanger sequencing (Fig. 3A). The functional efficacy of this isolated guide was initially evaluated by co-delivering it alongside the Cas9-expressing plasmid (PX458) into DF-1 cultures. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed successful dual-expression: co-transfected cells exhibited overlapping green and red fluorescent signals, in sharp contrast to the single-color emissions (red or green) detected in single-vector transfections (Fig. 3B). Correspondingly, transcriptional analysis confirmed a substantial knockdown of endogenous DMRT1 mRNA strictly within the dual-plasmid cohort, relative to mock controls (Fig. 3C). Genomic cleavage was further authenticated by T7E1 mismatch assays, which yielded the expected triple-band pattern exclusively in the co-transfected group, whereas single-plasmid and untransfected controls maintained an intact single amplicon (Fig. 3D). TA cloning results indicated that the knockout efficiency of DMRT1-sgRNA was 74% (Fig. 3E), confirming its knockout capability. To further select the optimal DOX concentration for inducing the DMRT1 knockout system, TRE-EGFP-rtTA-Puro and DMRT1-sgRNA were co-transfected into DF-1 cells, followed by induction with gradient concentrations of DOX (1, 5, 10, 15, 20 μg mL⁻¹). At 48 h post-induction, T7E1 results showed three bands in all DOX-induced cells (Figs. 3F-G). Peak performance was achieved at 20 μg mL–1 DOX, where the induced mutation rate statistically mirrored the robust efficiency of the non-inducible DMRT1-KO vector (Fig. 3H). TA cloning results showed that 12 out of 15 clones were knocked out when induced with 20 μg mL⁻¹ DOX, with an efficiency of 80%, which was similar to that of DMRT1-KO (80%) (Fig. 3I). These results indicated that 20 μg mL⁻¹ DOX induced the highest knockout efficiency. To demonstrate the function of the inducible DMRT1 knockout system in somatic cells, the system was transfected into CEF cells and induced with DOX for 48 h, resulting in fluorescence expression in all cells (Fig. 3J). qRT-PCR analysis showed that compared with the control group, the expression levels of female-related genes CYP19A1, FOXL2, and ESR1 were significantly increased, while the expression levels of male-related genes DMRT1, SOX9, and AMH were significantly decreased in cells induced with DOX. The expression of sex-related genes without DOX induction showed no significant difference from the blank control group (Fig. 3K). DOX-Induced CRISPR/Cas9 system efficiently knocks out DMRT1 in vivo

The inducible DMRT1 knockout system and DOX were co-encapsulated with PEI and injected into chicken embryos. Tissues collected 48 h post-injection were analyzed for Cas9 activation. Both qRT-PCR and Wb assays verified a robust induction of Cas9 specifically in the DOX-treated cohort, whereas uninduced embryos maintained baseline expression levels comparable to the mock controls, successfully validating the ligand-dependent activation in vivo (Figs. 4A-B). Genomic DNA extracted from E5.5 and E18.5 embryos was subsequently subjected to mismatch cleavage analysis. T7E1 assays revealed characteristic digested fragments in 9 out of 20 DOX-stimulated samples, translating to an in vivo disruption efficiency of 45%; predictably, non-induced counterparts exhibited no such cleavage (Fig. 4C). TA cloning results revealed mutations at the knockout target site in all T7E1-positive embryo samples (Fig. 4D). Morphological examinations of male gonads at E18.5 highlighted a striking phenotypic consequence: DOX-triggered DMRT1 deficiency induced pronounced left-right asymmetry in testicular growth, closely mimicking the natural developmental pattern of avian ovaries. In contrast, paired testes from uninduced embryos retained their typical symmetrical morphology, indicating that the conditional silencing of DMRT1 profoundly disrupts normal male gonadal organogenesis (Fig. 4E). Gonads from T7E1-positive 5.5-day and 18.5-day embryos in the DOX-induced group were subjected to qRT-PCR and WB analysis. qRT-PCR results showed that compared with the control group, DOX induction significantly increased the expression levels of female-related genes (CYP19A1, FOXL2, and ESR1) and significantly decreased the expression levels of male-related genes (DMRT1, SOX9, and AMH) at both 5.5 days and 18.5 days. The expression of sex-related genes in the non-DOX-induced group showed no significant difference from the control group (Fig. 4F). These dramatic transcriptional shifts were directly mirrored at the protein level, where Wb demonstrated a surge in CYP19A1 and FOXL2 alongside a severe reduction in SOX9 expression at both developmental checkpoints (Figs. 4G-H). These results demonstrated that the inducible DMRT1 knockout vector system could knockout DMRT1 in vivo under DOX induction and further regulate the expression of sex-related genes. Chicken embryos from 4.5 days to 18.5 days post-injection were subjected to sex identification. Gonads from male chicken embryos were collected for ELISA detection. The results showed that after transfection with the inducible DMRT1 knockout vector system, the testosterone level in the gonads of embryos induced with DOX was downregulated compared to that in normal male gonads and gonads of embryos without DOX induction (Fig. 4I). Discussion

Although no key gene for sex determination in chickens has been identified to date, in studying male chickens we have found that DMRT1 plays a key role in the development of the chicken testis. Indeed, the DMRT1 gene is highly conserved in vertebrates for testis development and function across all major evolutionary clades. In mice, DMRT1 is required to maintain postnatal support cell phenotypes and regulate gametogenesis (Kim et al., 2007; Matson et al., 2011). Similarly, DMRT1 is essential for testis development in turtles that possess a "genotype" or temperature-dependent sex determination (Ge et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2017). In addition, gonadal development in amphibians and fish is also dependent on the presence of DMRT1 or its paralogous homologous genes (Mawaribuchi et al., 2016; Cui et al., 2017). In chickens, the DMRT1 gene is located on the Z chromosome, and its expression varies significantly between the sexes, and it has an indispensable role in male gonadal differentiation. In Lambeth et al.'s study, they found this by overexpressing the DMRT1 gene in female chickens, which resulted in ectopic DMRT1 expression in the female gonads. This ectopic expression triggered the expression of localized SOX9 and AMH genes and induced the expression of the recently discovered Z-linked male factor hemoglobin (HEMGN), as well as a decrease in the expression of aromatase genes. In observations of the gonads, the androgenized gonads showed rope-like structures, while the cortical development showed clear signs of lagging (Lambeth et al., 2014). In addition, Ioannidis et al. showed that following knockout of the DMRT1 gene in male chickens, the gonads develop into ovary-like tissue, while down-regulation of SOX9 and AMH gene expression was observed, while FOXL2 and aromatase expression increased. Despite the transient nature of the sex reversal, the gonads of male chickens knocked out for DMRT1 continue to develop into ovaries in adulthood, reaffirming that DMRT1 plays a crucial role in male sex development in chickens (Ioannidis et al., 2021).

In this study, three sgRNAs were designed targeting the DMRT1 gene. Through SSA activity assay and T7E1 enzyme digestion analysis, we screened out the sgRNA with the optimal knockout efficiency, which is located in the second exon of the DMRT1 gene. Notably, the DMRT1 knockdown target designed by Ioannidis et al. is situated in the first exon of the gene, with a knockdown efficiency of 40% in primordial germ cells (PGCs) (10 out of 25 clones exhibited gene mutations). In addition, via TA cloning assay, we determined that the knockdown efficiency of this system in DF-1 cells was 80%. When this knockdown system was introduced into chicken embryos by blunt-end injection, the observed knockout efficiency was approximately 45%. This process resulted in a significant decrease in the expression of SOX9 and AMH genes, while the expression of FOXL2 and CYP19A1 genes was significantly increased. This result is in good agreement with the findings of Ioannidis et al. (Ioannidis et al., 2021), which further validates that our constructed DMRT1 knockdown system possesses higher knockdown efficiency and can be better used for DMRT1 knockdown studies. In addition, when analyzing TA clones, we also found that knockdown of the target site may trigger a deletion of approximately 60 bp or result in only a 1-2 bp deletion or base mutation. This suggests that a single sgRNA may lead to an unstable knockdown effect with the risk of off-targeting. Therefore, a combination of multiple sgRNAs, like the one used by Liu et al. in the Arabidopsis IAA2 knockdown study, could improve the stability of the knockdown for a better knockdown effect (Liu et al., 2016).

The CRISPR/Cas9 system is widely used for a variety of functions such as programmable gene editing, transcriptional regulation, epigenetic modulation, and genomic tagging. However, it is crucial to improve the precise control of their activities to achieve higher accuracy and reduce adverse outcomes such as off-target events. In this field of research, drug-inducible CRISPR/Cas9 technology provides a powerful approach. Tet-on induced CRISPR/Cas9 systems are widely used in mammalian research. González et al. optimized the M2rtTA2-TRE system to reduce background activity in human pluripotent stem cells, which was named Icrispr (González et al., 2014). On the other hand, Dow et al. fully leveraged the advantages of the Tet-on system to generate inducible CRISPR transgenic mice via TRE3G (Dow et al., 2015). Similarly, Nguyen et al. engineered the tripartite activator VP64-p65-Rta (VPR) by optimizing transcriptional regulators and fusing it with nuclease-null Cas9, which proved effective in activating endogenous coding and non-coding genes, targeting multiple genes concurrently, and enhancing neuronal differentiation in human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) (Chavez et al., 2015; Nguyen et al., 2016). In addition, sgRNA expression can also be regulated through the TET system, and researchers such as Aubrey et al. used a drug-induced small guide RNA (sgRNA) vector system in conjunction with the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 to achieve effective control of MCL-1 in a human Burkitt's lymphoma cell line (Aubrey et al., 2015). Herold et al. developed a lentiviral single vector system for temporal control of ubiquitous shRNA expression (Herold et al., 2008). Treatment with DOX at very low concentrations resulted in rapid induction of siRNA and ablation of INSR protein expression. Looking forward, future investigations should prioritize mapping the intricate molecular cascades through which DMRT1 orchestrates its downstream effectors, such as the SOX9-AMH axis and the FOXL2-CYP19A1 network. Unraveling these intermediate signaling pathways will clarify how avian sex determination reaches its definitive threshold. Additionally, evaluating the long-term heritability and stability of the DMRT1-knockout phenotype in adult populations will provide critical data on the transgenerational impact of these genomic modifications. From a technological perspective, overcoming the current 45% in vivo efficiency barrier remains a primary objective. Potential strategies include refining delivery protocols through viral vectors or nanoparticle-based carriers and utilizing high-fidelity Cas9 variants to ensure absolute specificity. Ultimately, the successful deployment of this drug-inducible toolkit not only deepens our fundamental comprehension of avian developmental biology but also provides a sophisticated technological foundation for precision breeding and genetic engineering in the poultry industry.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our research definitively reinforces the status of DMRT1 as an indispensable master regulator of gonadal organogenesis and sexual trajectory in Gallus gallus. By strategically coupling a doxycycline-responsive element with a CRISPR/Cas9 architecture, we engineered a highly robust, conditional gene-ablation platform. Comprehensive testing confirmed that chemical induction via DOX reliably triggers targeted genomic disruption, achieving profound editing efficacy both in cultured somatic cell lines and within complex in vivo embryonic environments. Crucially, the deployment of this temporally restricted editing system circumvents the limitations of traditional constitutive knockouts, offering unprecedented, time-resolved insights into the avian sex-determining hierarchy. Moving forward, this advanced genetic toolkit not only enriches our fundamental understanding of vertebrate developmental biology but also supplies a critical technological cornerstone for the future implementation of precision sex-manipulation strategies across the poultry industry.
