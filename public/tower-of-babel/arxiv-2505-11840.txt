Introduction

AdamW, which modifies Adam by decoupling weight decay from gradient-based updates, has emerged as the dominant optimizer for training deep neural networks, particularly for large language models. AdamW represents the pinnacle of adaptive gradient algorithms, having developed through the progression of AdaGrad
(
Duchi et al., 2011
;
McMahan and Streeter, 2010
)
, RMSProp
(
Tieleman and Hinton, 2012
)
, Adam
(
Kingma and Ba, 2015
)
, and finally AdamW
(
Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019
)
itself. Although the literature on the convergence analysis of adaptive gradient algorithms is quite extensive, there has been little research on the convergence properties of AdamW.

Recently,
(
Xie and Li, 2024
)
proved that if the iterates of AdamW converge to some
\mathbf{x}_{\infty}
, then
\mathbf{x}_{\infty}
is a KKT point of the constrained problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x}),\quad s.t.\quad\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda},\end{aligned}

(1)

where
f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\mathbf{x};\zeta)]
,
\zeta
is the sample drawn from the data distribution
\mathcal{P}
, and
\lambda
is the weight-decay parameter. Moreover,
\mathbf{x}
is a KKT point of problem (
1
) iff
(
Xie and Li, 2024
)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left\langle\lambda\mathbf{x},\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle+\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}=0.\end{aligned}

(2)

(
Xie and Li, 2024
)
characterized which solution AdamW converges to, if it indeed converges. The next fundamental question to address is whether and how fast AdamW converges.
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
conducted preliminary exploration on this problem. However, their analysis requires the weight-decay parameter to decrease exponentially, making AdamW reduce to Adam finally. To the best of our knowledge, aside from
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
, we have not found any other literature addressing the convergence issue of AdamW.

In practical deep learning training, we often initialize the network weights to small values and employ modest weight decay, for example,
\lambda=0.01
, which empirically confines the optimization trajectory within the
\ell_{\infty}
norm constraint, as empirically demonstrated in Figure
3
. That is,
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{c}{\lambda}
for some
c<1
, making
\left\langle\lambda\mathbf{x},\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle+\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}
lower bounded by
(1-c)\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}
. This key property enables the use of
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}
as an effective yet significantly simpler convergence metric for AdamW in practical settings. Building on this observation, this paper focuses on the convergence rate of AdamW within the constraint in problem (
1
).

1.1
Contribution

This paper investigates the convergence properties of AdamW. Specifically, we prove the following convergence rate for AdamW

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|_{1}\right]\leq{\cal O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{K^{1/4}}\sqrt[4]{\sigma^{2}L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}+\sqrt{\frac{dL(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K}}\right)\end{aligned}

(3)

under appropriate parameter settings such that
\|\mathbf{x}^{k}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{\lambda}
for all iterates, where
K
is the total iteration number,
d
is the model dimension,
\sigma
is the gradient noise variance,
L
is the Lipschitz smoothness constant, and
f^{*}
is a lower bound of
f(\mathbf{x})
. Recall the classical convergence rate of SGD
(
Bottou et al., 2018
)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|_{2}\right]\leq{\cal O}\left(\frac{\sqrt[4]{\sigma^{2}L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}}{K^{1/4}}\right),\end{aligned}

(4)

which matches the lower bound of non-convex stochastic optimization
(
Arjevani et al., 2023
)
. Comparing (
3
) with (
4
), we see that our convergence rate (
3
) also achieves the same lower bound with respect to
K
,
\sigma
,
L
, and
f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*}
. The only coefficient whose tightness remains unclear is the dimension
d
. Theoretically, we have
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\ll\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{d}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}
for any high-dimensional vector
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}\right]\geq\sqrt{\frac{2d}{\pi}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\right]
when each element of
\nabla f(\mathbf{x})
is drawn from a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Empirically, we have observed
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}=\varTheta(\sqrt{d})\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}
on real-world deep learning tasks, as shown in Figure
2
. Thus, we may regard that our convergence rate (
3
) is analogous to (
4
) of SGD in the ideal case.

As a special case, we also establish the same convergence rate (
3
) for Adam under slightly more relaxed parameter settings than those for AdamW. To the best of our knowledge, this convergence rate only appears for RMSProp first proved in
(
Li et al., 2025b
)
, and similar results for AdaGrad subsequently appeared in
(
Jiang et al., 2025
;
Liu et al., 2025
)
and RMSProp in
(
Xie et al., 2025
)
under different assumptions. Notably, comparable convergence guarantees remain unproven for AdamW and Adam.

Finally, we extend our theory to NAdamW
(
Dozat, 2016
)
and demonstrate that it maintains the same convergence rate. NAdamW is a variant of AdamW that incorporates a double-momentum mechanism and performs slightly better than AdamW verified in a recent benchmark paper
(
Wen et al., 2026
)
.

1.2
Notation and Assumptions

Denote
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma(\mathbf{g}^{1},\mathbf{g}^{2},\cdots,\mathbf{g}^{k})
as the sigma field of the stochastic gradients up to
k
, denote
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{k}}[\cdot]
as the expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]
the conditional expectation with respect to
\mathbf{g}^{k}
conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
. Denote
\|\cdot\|_{1}
,
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
as the
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}
, and
\ell_{\infty}
norms for vectors, respectively. For simplicity, we also use
\|\cdot\|
as the
\ell_{2}
norm. Denote
\odot
for the Hadamard product.

We make the following assumptions throughout this paper:

Assumption 1

Smoothness:
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|\leq L\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|,\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
.

Assumption 2

Unbiased estimator:
\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\mathbf{g}^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]=\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k}),\forall\mathbf{x}^{k}
.

Assumption 3

Bounded noise variance:
\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\|\mathbf{g}^{k}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\leq\sigma^{2},\forall\mathbf{x}^{k}
.

In contrast, our conference paper
(
Li et al., 2025a
)
employed a slightly stronger coordinate-wise bounded noise variance condition
\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{k}-\nabla_{i}f(\mathbf{x}^{k})|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\leq\sigma_{i}^{2}
and used
\sigma_{s}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}^{2}
in the convergence rate, where
\sigma_{s}^{2}
may be larger than
\sigma^{2}
.

2
Convergence Rate of AdamW

This section presents our convergence rate result for AdamW. Algorithm
1
provides the complete AdamW implementation, where setting the weight-decay parameter
\lambda=0
recovers the standard Adam. For analytical simplicity, we omit the bias correction term in our analysis.

Based on Assumptions 1-3, we provide the convergence rate of AdamW in the following theorem. Note that we do not assume the boundedness of the gradient
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})
or stochastic gradient
\mathbf{g}^{k}
.

Theorem 1

Suppose that Assumptions 1-3 hold. Define
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\max\left\{\sigma^{2},\frac{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K\gamma^{2}}\right\}
with any constant
\gamma\in(0,1]
. Let
1-\theta=\sqrt{\frac{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K\hat{\sigma}^{2}}}
,
\theta\leq\beta\leq\sqrt{\theta}
1
1

1

\theta\leq\beta\leq\frac{(1+\theta)^{2}}{4}
.
,
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*}}{4KdL}}
,
\varepsilon=\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{d}
,
\lambda\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{10K^{3/4}}\sqrt[4]{\frac{L^{3}}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}}
, and
\|\mathbf{x}^{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{5}{8}\sqrt{\frac{K(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{dL}}

\|\mathbf{x}^{k}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{\lambda}
for all
k=1,2,\cdots,K
and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|_{1}\right]\leq\frac{10\sqrt{d}}{K^{1/4}}\sqrt[4]{\hat{\sigma}^{2}L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}+66\sqrt{\frac{dL(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K}}.\end{aligned}

In particular, when
\sigma^{2}\leq\frac{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K\gamma^{2}}
, we have

,
\theta\leq\beta\leq\sqrt{\theta}
,
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*}}{4KdL}}

\lambda\leq\frac{1}{10}\sqrt{\frac{dL\gamma}{K(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|_{1}\right]\leq 76\sqrt{\frac{dL(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K\gamma}}.\end{aligned}

\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|

5

1

2

Corollary 1

With the same assumptions and parameter settings of

,
\eta
, and
\varepsilon

2.2
Separating the Convergence Rate by the Noise Variance

In Theorem
1
, we separate the convergence rate by the magnitude of
\sigma

\sigma^{2}\geq\frac{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K\gamma^{2}}

\frac{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K\gamma^{2}}

2.4
Small
\varepsilon
Setting

In practice,
\varepsilon
is typically set to a very small value, for example, approximately
10^{-16}
in PyTorch implementation
3
3

3

In the official PyTorch implementation,
\varepsilon
appeared in a different place in
\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\mathbf{v}^{k}}+\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon=10^{-8}

\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\mathbf{v}^{k}+\varepsilon}}
.
, to prevent division by zero while maintaining the adaptive properties of AdamW and Adam. Larger
\varepsilon

, we set
\varepsilon=\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{d}=\max\left\{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d},\frac{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{dK\gamma^{2}}\right\}

, and

\varepsilon

\varepsilon

2.5
Impractical Settings of
\eta
,
\theta
, and
\beta

In Theorem
1
, we set the learning rate
\eta
very small and the parameters

For instance, prior work

)
demonstrates through constructed examples that Adam with common hyperparameters (
\theta=0.9
,
\beta=\{0.999,0.997,0.995,0.993\}
, and
\eta_{k}=\frac{0.1}{\sqrt{k}}
) fails to converge to stationary points (see
(
Wang et al., 2024b
, Figuire 2)
). On the other hand, empirical evidence from recent studies
(
Tran et al., 2024
, Figure 11)

(
Wen et al., 2026
, Figure 7)
demonstrates that during the training of language models with practical parameter configurations, the gradient norm exhibits little decrease over the training horizon, although the objective function decreases sufficiently.

2.6
No Conflict with
(
Xie and Li, 2024
)

For sufficiently large weight-decay parameter
\lambda
where no critical points exist within the constrained domain of problem (
1
), the KKT conditions (
2
) serve as a natural convergence metric. As
\lambda
diminishes, problem (
1
) asymptotically approaches an unconstrained optimization problem, and AdamW reduces to Adam in the limit. There exists a critical threshold beyond which
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}
also becomes a viable metric for convergence. Consequently, our results do not conflict with
(
Xie and Li, 2024
)
.

3
Extension to NAdamW Incorporating Double-Momentum

In this section, we extend our theory to NAdamW
(
Dozat, 2016
)
. NAdamW is originally inspired by Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method, and after a series of equivalent transformations and approximations, it eventually results in the formulation presented in Algorithm
2
. In contrast to AdamW, NAdamW incorporates a double-momentum mechanism, a strategy that is also employed in LION
(
Chen et al., 2023
)
and Muon
(
Jordan et al., 2024
)
. When
\tau=1
, NAdamW reduces to AdamW.

Theorem 2

Suppose that Assumptions 1-3 hold. Define
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\max\left\{\sigma^{2},\frac{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K\gamma^{2}}\right\}
with any constant
\gamma\in(0,1]
. Let
1-\theta=\sqrt{\frac{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K\hat{\sigma}^{2}}}
,
\theta\leq\tau\leq 1
,
\theta\leq\beta\leq\sqrt{\theta}
,
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*}}{4KdL}}
,
\varepsilon=\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{d}
,
\lambda\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{10K^{3/4}}\sqrt[4]{\frac{L^{3}}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}}
, and
\|\mathbf{x}^{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{5}{8}\sqrt{\frac{K(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{dL}}
. Then for NAdamW, we have
\|\mathbf{x}^{k}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{\lambda}
for all
k=1,2,\cdots,K
and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|_{1}\right]\leq\frac{10\sqrt{d}}{K^{1/4}}\sqrt[4]{\hat{\sigma}^{2}L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}+66\sqrt{\frac{dL(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K}}.\end{aligned}

In particular, when
\sigma^{2}\leq\frac{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K\gamma^{2}}
, we have
1-\theta=\gamma
,
\theta\leq\tau\leq 1
,
\theta\leq\beta\leq\sqrt{\theta}
,
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*}}{4KdL}}
,
\varepsilon=\frac{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{dK\gamma^{2}}
,
\lambda\leq\frac{1}{10}\sqrt{\frac{dL\gamma}{K(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}}
,
\|\mathbf{x}^{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{5}{8}\sqrt{\frac{K(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{dL}}
,
\|\mathbf{x}^{k}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{\lambda}
, and accordingly

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|_{1}\right]\leq 76\sqrt{\frac{dL(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{K\gamma}}.\end{aligned}

In the following corollary, we also establish the same convergence rate for NAdam, that is,
\lambda=0
in Algorithm
2
.

Corollary 2

With the same assumptions and parameter settings of
1-\theta
,
\tau
,
\eta
, and
\varepsilon
as in Theorem
2
, but only requiring
0\leq\beta\leq 1
rather than both
\theta\leq\beta\leq\sqrt{\theta}
and
\|\mathbf{x}^{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{5}{8}\sqrt{\frac{K(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{dL}}
, we have the same convergence rate for NAdam as established in Theorem
2
.

4.1
AdamW: Comparison with
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)

To the best of our knowledge based on a comprehensive literature review,
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
appears to be the only existing paper addressing AdamW’s convergence and convergence rate. We compare with
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
in the following aspects. First, the assumptions in
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
are stronger than ours. Denoting
f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\mathcal{P}}[f(\mathbf{x};\zeta)]
, they assumed
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y};\zeta)-\nabla f(\mathbf{x};\zeta)\|\leq L\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|
(under which the lower bound is
{\cal O}(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{3}})
, rather than
{\cal O}(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{4}})

(
Arjevani et al., 2023
)
) and
\|\mathbf{g}^{k}\|_{\infty}\leq c_{\infty}
, while we only assume
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|\leq L\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|
without the bounded gradient assumption. Second, they set the weight-decay parameter
\lambda_{k}=\lambda(1-\frac{\beta c_{\infty}^{2}}{\varepsilon})^{k}
, which decreases exponentially, making AdamW reduce to standard Adam in the limit. Third, they establish the complexity of
{\cal O}(\max\{\frac{c_{\infty}^{2.5}L\sigma^{2}(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}{\varepsilon^{1.25}\epsilon^{4}},\frac{c_{\infty}^{2}\sigma^{4}}{\varepsilon\epsilon^{4}}\})
to achieve
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla F_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}
, where
F_{k}
is a dynamic
\ell_{2}
-regularized objective. Their complexity depends on
\varepsilon
explicitly, which is usually small in practice, for example,
\varepsilon\approx 10^{-16}
in PyTorch implementation. By contrast, our convergence rate does not depend on
\varepsilon
explicitly.

4.2
Adam: Comparison with
(
Li et al., 2025b
)

(
Li et al., 2025b
)
studied RMSProp and its momentum extension, where RMSProp is a special case of Adam by letting
\theta=0
and
\lambda=0
in Algorithm
1
. The convergence analysis of Adam presents substantially greater challenges than that of RMSProp and we cannot extend the proofs in
(
Li et al., 2025b
)
to Adam. Alternatively, this paper uses a different proof framework to establish for Adam the same convergence rate achieved by
(
Li et al., 2025b
)
. Moreover,
Li et al. (2025b)
employed a slightly stronger coordinate-wise bounded noise variance condition
\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{k}-\nabla_{i}f(\mathbf{x}^{k})|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\leq\sigma_{i}^{2}
while we relax it to the general bounded noise variance. As a trade-off, one limitation of our proof is that it relies on a larger value of parameter
\varepsilon
, although
\varepsilon=\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{d}
is very small in practice. Specifically, under the parameter settings of
\beta=1-\frac{1}{K}
,
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{0}=\lambda\max\{\sigma_{i}^{2},\frac{1}{dK}\}
, and
\lambda\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{KL(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}
in
(
Li et al., 2025b
)
, we have
\frac{1}{e^{2}}\leq\beta^{t}\leq 1
for any

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}_{i}^{k}=\beta^{k}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{0}+(1-\beta)\sum_{t=1}^{k}\beta^{k-t}|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}|^{2}\approx\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{K}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{L(f(\mathbf{x}^{1})-f^{*})}+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{t=1}^{k}|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}|^{2},\end{aligned}

where
\beta^{k}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{0}
plays the role of
\varepsilon
in Algorithm
1
, which is of the order
\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{K}
, or approximately
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{dK}
. By contrast, in this paper, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}_{i}^{k}+\varepsilon=\varepsilon+(1-\beta)\sum_{t=1}^{k}\beta^{k-t}|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}|^{2}=\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{d}+(1-\beta)\sum_{t=1}^{k}\beta^{k-t}|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}|^{2}\approx\sigma_{i}^{2}+(1-\beta)\sum_{t=1}^{k}\beta^{k-t}|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}|^{2}.\end{aligned}

When
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{t})\approx 0
such that
|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}|\approx\sigma_{i}
, we have
(1-\beta)\sum_{t=1}^{k}\beta^{k-t}|\mathbf{g}_{i}^{t}|^{2}\approx\sigma_{i}^{2}
. Thus,
\varepsilon
accounts for nearly half of
(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{k}+\varepsilon)
’s size, while in
(
Li et al., 2025b
)
,
\beta^{k}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{0}

\frac{1}{k}
of
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{k}
’s total size. Other representative studies
(
Hong and Lin, 2023

(
Li et al., 2023

(

Finally, we summarize the key ideas behind the proof in the following two remarks.

Remark 1

Managing Weight Decay
.

To manage the problematic term (b) introduced by weight decay, we first bound
|\lambda\mathbf{x}_{i}^{k}|
in term (b) by
\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{K^{1/4}}
and relax terms (a) and (b) to terms (c) and (d), respectively. Then we can bound the second-order momentum in term (d) by Lemma
4
and absorb term (g) into term (e). By carefully choosing the bound of
|\lambda\mathbf{x}_{i}^{k}|
, we can control the magnitude of term (f). This sequence of operations enables us to manage the problematic term (b) and derive the bound (
15
).

Remark 2

Eliminating the Dependence on
\varepsilon
.

Finally, we eliminate the dependence on
\varepsilon
in term (h) by the tight bound of the second-order momentum in Lemma
4
and the careful configuration of
\varepsilon
. Although smaller value of
\varepsilon
does not affect the convergence, this term cannot be eliminated any more and consequently slows the convergence rate by introducing explicit
\varepsilon
-dependence. On the other hand, while larger
\varepsilon
does not impact the convergence rate, it makes AdamW closer to SGD.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |\widetilde{\mathbf{m}}_{i}^{k}|^{2}=&\left(\tau(1-\theta)\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}\theta^{k-r}\mathbf{g}_{i}^{r}+(1-\tau\theta)\mathbf{g}_{i}^{k}\right)^{2}\\

Experimental Details

As presented in the preceding sections, we conduct several representative deep learning experiments to empirically support our claims, covering classic image classification and natural language processing tasks. For the vision tasks, we independently train ResNet-50
(
He et al., 2016
)
on CIFAR-100
(
Krizhevsky and Hinton, 2009
)
and ImageNet
(
Russakovsky et al., 2015
)
datasets. For the language task, we adopt the GPT-2
(
Radford et al., 2019
)
architecture and pretrain it on the OpenWebText
(
Gokaslan et al., 2019
)
dataset.
To promote maximal transparency and reproducibility, we release all code, configuration files, plotting scripts, and processed result data on GitHub. All experiments can be easily inspected and reproduced through the provided ready-to-run script. Link:

https://github.com/adonis-dym/Convergence-Rate-AdamW

.

Our experiments involve the computation of the full training loss
f(\mathbf{x}^{k})
as well as the full gradient
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})
. However, in the typical stochastic training paradigm, one often updates the parameter
\mathbf{x}^{k}
on the fly immediately after obtaining the stochastic gradient
\mathbf{g}^{k}
from the backward pass. To get an accurate measurement and avoid interfering with the normal training process, we propose to split each epoch into two separate phases:
training phase
and
logging phase
.
In the training phase, we traverse the dataset once with stochastic updates, where the model parameters are updated upon processing each mini-batch. In the logging phase, we conduct a second traversal over the training dataset while keeping the model parameters frozen. Since the loss function is typically defined to be the average over all training samples and the gradient computation is inherently linear, we accumulate the losses and stochastic gradients across mini-batches during this phase. This yields the exact values of the full training loss
f(\mathbf{x}^{k})
and full gradient
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})
at the current iteration.

In the following, we detail each experimental setup:

i) ResNet-50 - CIFAR-100:
CIFAR-100 is a simple benchmark dataset that is widely used for efficient evaluation of deep learning models. It contains a training split of 50000 examples and a test split of 10000 examples, although we do not perform evaluation on the test set in this work. The AdamW optimizer is configured via the official
torch.optim.AdamW
interface, whereas NAdamW employs a commonly used implementation
(
Kasimbeg et al., 2025
;
Wen et al., 2026
)
aligned with Algorithm
2
due to the absence of a unified reference, and is open-sourced for reproducibility. Except for the additional parameter
\tau
introduced by NAdamW, all other settings, including hyperparameters, learning-rate schedule, and random seed, are kept identical across the two optimizers, with
\tau=\theta=0.9
following the common practice. We initialize the learning rate to
3\times 10^{-3}
, train the ResNet-50 model for 100 epochs, and apply a cosine learning-rate decay schedule during the whole training process. Setting the batch size to 128, each epoch consists of
\lfloor 50000/128\rfloor+1=391
steps, where the additional step accounts for the final truncated batch which contains the remaining samples. The total number of steps is
K=391\times 100=39100
. Without loss of generality, we compute the noise vector
\bm{\sigma}^{k}=\mathbf{g}^{k}-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})
using the stochastic gradient
\mathbf{g}^{k}
obtained from the first batch at the logging phase. We leave the weight-decay parameter
\lambda
as its default value 0.01, and complete the training task with a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.

ii) ResNet-50 - ImageNet:

To evaluate the scalability of our conclusions on larger-scale dataset, we conduct experiments on the ImageNet dataset using the same ResNet-50 architecture. ImageNet consists of approximately 1.28 million training images and 50,000 validation images across 1,000 classes, and has an official dataset split. We employ the training script from PyTorch Image Models (
timm
)
(
Wightman, 2019
)
, making only the necessary modifications to suit our experimental setup. We adopt the same optimizer configuration as previously, but compute the noise vector using the last batch at the logging phase, as the
timm
script discards incomplete batches and ensures uniform batch sizes. We follow the standard ImageNet training protocol for ResNet-50, which consists of 90 epochs as commonly adopted in the literature and official implementations
(
He et al., 2016
;
Wightman, 2019
)
. The first 10 epochs are used for learning-rate linear warmup from 0 to
3\times 10^{-3}
, followed by cosine decay over the remaining 80 epochs. We apply standard data augmentation techniques including RandAugment, Mixup (0.1), and CutMix (1.0). With a batch size of 4096, each epoch consists of 312 mini-batches and the total number of steps is
K=28080
. We set
\lambda=0.1
and complete the training task using eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

iii) GPT-2 - OpenWebText:

To assess the generality of our conclusions across different modalities, we further evaluate on a language-modeling task using GPT-2. We pretrain this model on the OpenWebText dataset under the NVIDIA Megatron-LM codebase
(
Shoeybi et al., 2019
)
, which is a widely adopted framework for large-scale language model training. Unlike the previous settings, where computing the full training loss and gradient over the entire dataset is tractable, the OpenWebText dataset is substantially larger, containing approximately 9 billion tokens. Consequently, an entire pass through the dataset to get the full training loss
f(\mathbf{x}^{k})
and gradient
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})
is computationally infeasible. Instead, we approximate these quantities by accumulating their values over 100 consecutive mini-batches at the logging phase. We follow the Megatron-LM official GPT-2 training configuration with minimal modifications to suit our experimental needs. We train a GPT-2 Small model with approximately 125M parameters, setting the learning rate to
3\times 10^{-3}
and weight-decay parameter to 0.05. Following the de facto standard in large-scale language model training, we use
(\theta,\beta)=(0.9,0.95)
instead of the conventional
(0.9,0.999)
setting. We use the fused AdamW implementation from the NVIDIA Apex package, which is the default in Megatron-LM, and employ the same open-sourced version for NAdamW. Training runs for 50,000 iterations, where the learning rate is linearly warmed up for the first 2,000 iterations and then decayed following a cosine schedule. We set the global batch size to 640 and train the model for
K=50000
steps, and complete training using eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Notably, the NAdamW optimizer attains a lower training loss than AdamW after approximately 20k steps, while exhibiting slightly higher loss in the early stage. We attribute this behavior to the well-documented gradient spike phenomenon in LLM training
(
Molybog et al., 2023
;
Takase et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2026
)
, as NAdamW places greater emphasis on the current-step gradient and may therefore stabilize more slowly.

Conclusion

This paper studies the popular AdamW optimizer in large language model training, along with its simplified predecessor Adam and its extension NAdamW. We establish the convergence rate
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|_{1}\right]\leq{\cal O}(\frac{\sqrt{d}C}{K^{1/4}})
for AdamW/Adam/NAdamW measured by the
\ell_{1}
norm. It can be regarded as analogous to the optimal
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|_{2}\right]\leq{\cal O}(\frac{C}{K^{1/4}})
rate of SGD in the ideal case of
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{1}=\varTheta(\sqrt{d})\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}
, which is verified on real-world deep learning tasks.

An important direction for future research would be to investigate the optimal convergence rate using weak
\varepsilon
-dependent analysis (for example,
\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
) for AdamW/ Adam/NAdamW, or to prove that the theoretical setting
\varepsilon=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d}
is necessary for the optimal convergence rate. On the other hand, it is currently unclear whether our upper bound on the weight-decay parameter
\lambda
is tight. Investigating how to prove the optimal convergence rate under a looser upper bound on
\lambda
would be meaningful.
