Effectiveness of the Early Start Denver Model (ESDM) as an early intervention for young children with autism spectrum disorder in clinical settings in Japan: A non‐randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yukie Tateno, Ryunosuke Monden, Kahoru Kumagai, Masaru Tateno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe Early Start Denver Model (ESDM), a core Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Intervention (NDBI), promotes developmental gains in young children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) through socially embedded learning. Because high‐intensity ESDM programs (approximately 20 h/week) are difficult to implement in many Japanese clinical settings, this study evaluated the effectiveness of a low‐intensity ESDM program using standardized assessments.MethodsA non‐randomized controlled design was employed with 47 toddlers aged 18–36 months with a confirmed ASD diagnosis. Thirty participants were allocated to the intervention group, while 17 participants were assigned to the control group and received treatment as usual. Unlike the original ESDM randomized controlled trial implementing approximately 20 h/week, the intervention group (ESDM group, n = 30) received 60‐min ESDM sessions once weekly for 24 weeks, with parents present during sessions. Outcomes were assessed before and after intervention using the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule‐2 (ADOS‐2), Kyoto Scale of Psychological Development 2020 (KSPD‐2020), and Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales‐II (Vineland‐II).ResultsAnalysis of covariance (ancova) controlling for baseline scores revealed a significant group difference in the KSPD‐2020 all domains developmental quotient (DQ) (p = 0.0416). Trend‐level differences were observed in the postural–motor domain (p = 0.0811) and the cognition–adaptation domain (p = 0.0555). No significant between‐group differences were found in the other outcome measures. Adaptive behavior showed modest increases in communication and socialization, although between‐group differences were not statistically significant.ConclusionAlthough the original ESDM recommends intensive delivery exceeding 15 h per week for 2 years, the present findings provide preliminary evidence that a low‐intensity ESDM program—delivered once weekly for 24 sessions—can support meaningful developmental progress, even when changes in core autism symptoms are not detected after statistical adjustment in toddlers with ASD in Japanese clinical settings. These results highlight the feasibility of adapting ESDM principles to low‐intensity service environments and underscore their potential applicability within Japanese cultural and systemic contexts.

INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by difficulties in social interaction and communication, as well as restricted and repetitive behavior. 1 In Japan, two large‐scale epidemiological studies have reported an ASD prevalence of approximately 3%. 2 , 3 This estimate is consistent with findings from a multi‐site study conducted across 11 states in the United States, supporting the notion that the current prevalence of ASD is around 3% globally. 4 In recent years, the number of children visiting psychiatric or child psychiatric clinics in Japan has increased, and a substantial proportion of these children are diagnosed with ASD. 5

Due to the nature of the condition, individuals with ASD are known to be at increased risk of developing various psychiatric symptoms—often referred to as secondary disorders—such as depression and anxiety, particularly during adolescence and beyond. 6 Therefore, early diagnosis and early intervention are considered crucial. Early support for ASD has been reported to significantly influence developmental outcomes. 1 Interventions during the period of heightened neuroplasticity around 2–3 years of age can positively affect multiple developmental domains, such as language, cognition, and social functioning. 7 , 8 , 9 Accordingly, the development and implementation of evidence‐based early interventions have progressed worldwide.

Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Interventions (NDBIs) integrate Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) with developmental and social motivation theories. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 NDBIs utilize children's interests and spontaneity to promote social communication through play and daily routines, combining clear behavioral goals with developmental flexibility. Meta‐analyses and systematic reviews consistently demonstrate positive effects on social communication and general developmental outcomes. 11 , 14

The Early Start Denver Model (ESDM), a representative NDBI, targets children aged 12–48 months with ASD and integrates language, social, and cognitive skill building within natural interactions. 15 Dawson et al. demonstrated significant improvements in intelligence quotient (IQ), language, and social functioning in a randomized controlled trial (RCT), delivering approximately 20 h/week of intervention, which has generally been regarded as high‐intensity ESDM. 16 Accordingly, ESDM programs providing substantially fewer weekly hours have been conceptualized as low‐intensity interventions; however, the specific definitions of low‐intensity ESDM have varied across previous studies, particularly with respect to intervention dosage (see Table S1). 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 In the present study, the term “low‐intensity ESDM” is used to describe a once‐weekly, 60‐min intervention format implemented within routine clinical practice in Japan.

While later studies have reported only partial replications and highlighted important methodological constraints, the broader literature continues to provide compelling evidence for the effectiveness of early developmental–behavioral approaches. Research has demonstrated durable treatment gains over time, 22 robust parent‐mediated generalization across daily contexts, 23 and the feasibility of implementing these interventions in group‐based settings without compromising developmental benefits. 24 Moreover, recent work suggests that meaningful progress can be achieved even when programs are delivered at relatively low levels of intensity, underscoring the adaptability and scalability of these models in real‐world clinical environments. 20 , 25 , 26

Although the use of NDBI practices has been gradually expanding in Japan, few quantitative studies have evaluated the effects of the ESDM using standardized developmental and behavioral assessments. 21 In particular, the effectiveness of low‐intensity, such as once‐weekly sessions commonly adopted in clinical practice, remains largely under‐investigated. Our research group previously conducted a pilot study examining the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of ESDM in Japan. 21 That pilot study served as an initial implementation effort using a small sample and a pre–post design, demonstrating the practicality of introducing ESDM into Japanese clinical services. Building upon those preliminary findings, the present study extends this work by employing a larger sample size, inclusion of a comparison group, standardized outcome measures, and a longer observation period, thereby providing a more rigorous evaluation of low‐intensity ESDM within a non‐randomized controlled framework.

Japan's universal health insurance system restricts outpatient psychiatric psychotherapy to three fixed time categories that can be reimbursed under insurance—more than 5 min, more than 30 min, and more than 60 min only when specific criteria are met. These structural constraints limit the amount of clinician‐delivered intervention that can be provided during routine visits, making the implementation of a standard, high‐intensity ESDM program difficult to carry out in most Japanese medical institutions.

Given this context, it is essential to determine whether short‐term, low‐intensity ESDM, aligned with the conditions under which Japanese clinicians can feasibly operate, can still produce measurable developmental benefits. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a clinically implementable, low‐intensity ESDM program in Japan using standardized psychological assessments that are routinely used in clinical practice and eligible for reimbursement under the national health insurance system.

METHODS

Participants

Study participants were 47 toddlers aged 18–36 months who attended the outpatient clinic of the Tokiwa Hospital Child Development Center between April 2021 and March 2024. The diagnosis of ASD was confirmed by certified child psychiatrists using the diagnostic criteria of the DSM‐5, ensuring consistency and clinical reliability. The intervention group (ESDM group) comprised thirty children whose parents elected to receive ESDM, whereas the control group consisted of seventeen children whose parents declined the intervention and continued with treatment as usual. Additional demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. All participants who completed both pre‐ and post‐intervention assessments were included in the final analyses. Although the intervention framework was planned as a 24‐week program comprising 24 sessions, minor variations in scheduling occurred due to family availability or clinical circumstances. No participants who completed the baseline assessment withdrew from the study prior to the post‐intervention assessment; thus, the analyses were conducted on participants with complete pre–post data.

ESDM group and control group

A non‐randomized controlled design was employed to compare outcomes between an intervention group (ESDM group) and a treatment‐as‐usual group (control group). All participants underwent standardized assessments at two time points—pre‐intervention (Pre) and post‐intervention (Post)—to examine both within‐group and between‐group changes. All assessments were administered within 3 months prior to the intervention and within 1 month following the intervention by examiners who were blinded to group allocation and independent of the treatment procedures.

The schedule of the pre‐intervention assessment, the delivery of the ESDM sessions, and the post‐intervention assessment is summarized in Figure 1.

Study design and timeline. The Early Start Denver Model (ESDM) group began the program within 3 months after the pre‐assessment and completed the post‐assessment within 1 month after completing the 24 sessions. The control group underwent re‐evaluation after the same time interval.

ESDM intervention in this study

The ESDM intervention was delivered in an individualized setting, in which a therapist worked with a young child with ASD while the mother or another family member observed the session in a well‐prepared therapy room. Each ESDM session lasted 60 min and was provided once a week for a total of 24 sessions. The intervention was administered by either two certified ESDM therapists or an experienced clinical psychologist whose fidelity (13 items, 5‐point scale) exceeded 80%. The two certified ESDM therapists were a child psychiatrist (Y.T.) and a licensed psychologist (K.K.). Clinical diagnoses were conducted by board‐certified child psychiatrists (Y.T., M.T.), while developmental assessments (Kyoto Scale of Psychological Development 2020 [KSPD‐2020], Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Second Edition [Vineland‐II]) were administered by multiple clinical psychologists, who were independent of the intervention team and blinded to group allocation.

Although the original ESDM model was developed as a high‐intensity intervention—typically 15 to 25 h per week over a 2‐year period—such intensive formats are rarely feasible in Japanese medical or welfare institutions due to financial and staffing constraints. To align with the realities of community‐based clinical practice, we implemented a low‐intensity version of ESDM while maintaining adherence to the core curriculum standards described in the ESDM manuals. Individual learning objectives were developed using the ESDM Curriculum Checklist 15 and were reviewed regularly by a certified trainer to ensure consistency and quality. Fidelity of implementation by therapists was monitored through onsite supervision and periodic review of recorded sessions.

Sessions began and ended with familiar songs and incorporated Joint Activity Routines (JARs) and Sensory Social Routines (SSRs; e.g., peek‐a‐boo), with alternating table‐based and floor‐based activities. Novel elements were introduced gradually, and routines concluded with natural closure (e.g., clean‐up). Each session also included a brief explanation to the observing parent or family member to support generalization of ESDM strategies in the home environment.

Measures of intervention effectiveness

The effectiveness of the ESDM was evaluated by comparing pre‐ and post‐intervention changes using three standardized psychological assessments that are widely used in routine child psychiatric practice across many clinical settings in Japan. All standardized assessments (Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition [ADOS‐2], KSPD‐2020, and Vineland‐II) were administered by licensed psychologists who were not involved in the delivery of the ESDM intervention and were independent of the study team.

The Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition

The ADOS‐2 is a semi‐structured, standardized assessment designed to evaluate social communication and restricted, repetitive behaviors associated with ASD. 27 , 28 The ADOS‐2 includes five modules (Toddler Module and Modules 1–4), which are selected according to an individual's developmental and language level.

In this study, either the Toddler Module or Module 1 was administered depending on each child's abilities. To enable comparison across modules, Calibrated Severity Scores (CSS) were used as a standardized index of autism symptom severity, as validated in prior research. 29

The Japanese version of the ADOS‐2 was administered by certified examiners. The first author holds research‐level certification, and all other co‐authors are certified clinical‐level administrators. 30

The Kyoto Scale of Psychological Development 2020

The KSPD 31 is a popular developmental test in Japan. The KSPD is a standardized and individualized psychological test that is administered by experienced psychologists to evaluate the developmental level of the subjects in three respective domains: Postural and Motor (P–M; fine and gross motor functions); Cognitive and Adaptive (C–A; non‐verbal reasoning or visuospatial perceptions assessed using materials); and Language and Social (L–S; interpersonal relationships, socialization, and verbal abilities). The developmental quotient (DQ) was calculated by dividing the developmental age (DA) measured by scores in each area by the subject's chronological age. A previous study reported the KSPD‐2001's clinical usefulness for the cognitive assessment of children with pervasive developmental disorders, which is equivalent to the clinical definition of ASD in DSM‐5. 32 Another Japanese group compared domain DQs of the KSPD‐2001 with scores on each of the four regions in the Bayley Scales of Infant Development‐II (BDI‐II), 33 that is, cognitive, language, social, and motor. The correlation between the L–S DQ of the KSPD and the language domain of the BDI‐II at 18 months was high (r = 0.60). 34 Furthermore, unlike other developmental tests that estimate the score by comparing with a norm sample in the same age group, the KSPD calculates the child's developmental level by dividing the DA obtained through the standardized assessment and scoring procedure in the test by chronological age. 34 In general, DQ scores higher than 80 are regarded as typical developmental levels. The KSPD takes approximately 20 to 40 min to complete.

In 2020, the KSPD was revised to include updated normative data and modified content. 35 New items for infants, toddlers, and adults were added, extending the age range and improving its clinical utility.

Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Second Edition

The Vineland‐II is a standardized assessment tool designed to evaluate adaptive behavior in individuals from birth to adulthood. 36 Adaptive behavior refers to the everyday skills people need to function and meet environmental demands, including communication, daily living skills, socialization, and motor skills. The Vineland‐II is widely used in clinical, educational, and research settings to assist in diagnosing developmental and intellectual disabilities, ASDs, and other neurodevelopmental conditions.

The Vineland‐II is typically completed by a caregiver or someone who knows the individual well. It uses a semi‐structured interview or a questionnaire format to gather detailed information on the person's functioning in real‐life contexts. The assessment yields scores in key domains—Communication, Daily Living Skills, Socialization, and Motor Skills—as well as an overall Adaptive Behavior Composite (ABC) score.

This tool is valued for its strong psychometric properties and its ecological validity, as it focuses on real‐world behavior rather than performance in artificial testing situations. Clinicians often use the Vineland‐II to inform diagnostic decisions, treatment planning, and educational programming. It is worth noting that although the most recent edition of the scale in English is the Vineland‐3, 37 the latest version available in Japanese is the Vineland‐II.

Statistical analysis

To account for baseline differences between groups, an analysis of covariance (ancova) was performed. Post‐intervention scores were treated as dependent variables, group (intervention vs. control) as the independent variable, and baseline (pre‐intervention) scores as covariates. Homogeneity of regression slopes was confirmed prior to analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM spss Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Between‐group comparisons at T2 were performed using ancova with baseline scores as covariates. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (η 2). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two‐tailed), and p values < 0.10 were considered to indicate trend‐level significance.

Ethics

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional Ethics Committee (TH‐20210215). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating children after a full explanation of the study's purpose and procedures. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality of personal information was strictly maintained. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics at baseline

Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Forty‐seven toddlers diagnosed with ASD (30 in the ESDM intervention group and 17 in the control group) completed both the pre‐ and post‐intervention assessments. At baseline, there were no significant differences between the groups in age, gender distribution, or developmental and adaptive functioning scores. The mean chronological age was 27.6 ± 4.3 months in the intervention group and 28.9 ± 5.2 months in the control group. The proportion of males was comparable between groups (70.0% vs. 76.5%).

Both groups utilized similar numbers of local developmental support services, and none received concurrent individual occupational or speech therapy during the study period, indicating that the groups were comparable at study entry.

DISCUSSION

This non‐randomized controlled study examined the effectiveness of a low‐intensity ESDM program delivered within a real‐world Japanese clinical setting. Three primary findings emerged.

First, low‐intensity ESDM was associated with significant improvements in developmental outcomes, particularly in overall DQ, with additional trend‐level improvements observed in the postural–motor and cognition–adaptation domains.

Second, autism symptom severity, as measured by the ADOS‐2 total comparison score, did not show statistically significant between‐group differences after adjusting for baseline using ancova, although descriptive improvements were observed within the intervention group.

Third, although group differences were not statistically significant, adaptive behavior measured by the Vineland‐II showed modest yet consistent gains—especially in communication and socialization—suggesting early but emerging generalization of developmental progress.

Taken together, these findings suggest that low‐intensity ESDM may support developmental progress in young children with ASD, particularly in broader developmental domains, even when changes in core autism symptoms are not detected. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the relatively small sample size and the multiple comparisons performed using ancova in this study.

An important point requiring careful interpretation concerns the ADOS‐2 findings. Previous RCTs—including the original high‐intensity ESDM trial by Dawson et al. 16 and a recent multicenter European replication study conducted in France and Belgium 26 —did not report significant between‐group differences in ADOS‐2 severity outcomes, despite longer intervention duration and higher or moderate intervention intensity. The present findings are therefore consistent with prior literature, suggesting that ADOS‐2 may have limited sensitivity to detect short‐term intervention‐related change.

Diagnostic instruments such as the ADOS‐2 are primarily designed for classification rather than for detecting short‐term treatment‐related change. Although the use of ADOS‐2 CSS allows standardized comparison across modules, changes in ADOS‐2 scores do not necessarily translate directly into functional improvements in daily life. Therefore, symptom‐level change and functional adaptation should be interpreted as related but distinct outcome domains.

In real‐world clinical implementation studies such as the present one, complete separation between intervention delivery and outcome assessment can be challenging, even when formal blinding procedures are implemented. As a result, structured observational measures may be influenced by contextual or expectancy‐related factors.

Recent intervention trials have suggested that outcome measures specifically developed to capture short‐term change in social communication—such as the Brief Observation of Social Communication Change (BOSCC)—may be more sensitive than diagnostic instruments. 38 , 39 In the European ESDM trial, although ADOS‐2 outcomes did not differ significantly between groups, modest but statistically significant improvements were observed on BOSCC and communication‐related measures, underscoring the importance of outcome selection. 26

Given the absence of significant ADOS‐2 group differences in the present study, the use of more sensitive change‐detection measures such as BOSCC may be more appropriate and necessary for accurately capturing short‐term intervention‐related change.

Regarding adaptive functioning, the absence of statistically significant between‐group differences on the Vineland‐II should not be interpreted as evidence of no functional impact but rather as an indicator of the complexity of measuring adaptive change over relatively short timeframes.

Adaptive behaviors often require longer periods to consolidate and may be less sensitive to early intervention effects, particularly in low‐intensity formats.

Notably, in the original Dawson et al. trial, 16 significant between‐group differences in Vineland outcomes emerged only after 2 years of high‐intensity intervention (approximately 20 h per week), whereas shorter or lower‐intensity trials—including the recent European study delivering approximately 12 h per week—have reported only modest or domain‐limited Vineland effects.

These findings suggest that Vineland outcomes may be highly dependent on intervention intensity, duration, and follow‐up interval.

In the present study, the intervention intensity (once weekly, 60 min) and the relatively short observation period likely constrained the magnitude of observable adaptive change.

Furthermore, the finding that improvements in ADOS CSS and Vineland communication scores were also observed in the control group indicates that maturation, usual care, or non‐specific treatment effects may have contributed to developmental gains. Accordingly, improvements in adaptive behavior—particularly communication—cannot be attributed solely to the effects of ESDM, and causal interpretation should be approached cautiously.

From an implementation standpoint, this study demonstrates that the ESDM can be feasibly adapted to the constraints of the Japanese clinical context while maintaining fidelity standards. All therapists achieved at least 80% fidelity on standardized fidelity checklists, and goal‐setting procedures were implemented consistently across practitioners. The presence of parents during sessions may have contributed to naturalistic generalization of skills, even in the absence of a formal parent‐training protocol. These features are consistent with previous literature emphasizing that high‐quality therapist–parent collaboration and consistency of routines are key determinants of successful outcomes across diverse cultural and service‐delivery contexts.

The present findings also align with emerging evidence from other Asian studies, including those conducted in Taiwan and China, which have documented developmental and social‐communication benefits under similarly low‐intensity ESDM conditions. 40 , 41 Although the magnitude of effects observed in this study was modest, the effect size for overall developmental outcomes suggests a moderate and clinically meaningful intervention effect, supporting the clinical relevance of low‐intensity ESDM.

It should be noted that the magnitude and pattern of outcomes observed in this study should be interpreted in light of the substantially lower intervention intensity compared with the original ESDM RCT, which delivered approximately 20 h of intervention per week. 16 As summarized in Table S1, prior studies of low‐intensity ESDM differ substantially in intervention intensity, duration, provider involvement, and study design, including pilot studies, non‐RCTs, and RCTs. Compared with these studies, the present study employed a once‐weekly, therapist‐delivered model that reflects routine clinical practice in Japan under health insurance constraints. Although these effects tend to be smaller than those reported in high‐intensity RCTs, 16 they remain clinically meaningful when implemented with fidelity and tailored to local service systems. This suggests that the quality and contextual relevance of engagement may be as important as the nominal intensity of service delivery.

Several limitations warrant careful consideration. The non‐randomized design introduces potential selection bias, as families who elected to receive ESDM may differ in motivation or engagement from those who declined. The relatively small sample size reduces statistical power, and the absence of an active control condition limits direct comparability. Moreover, the single‐site nature of this study may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical or community contexts. Furthermore, the short‐term study design precludes evaluation of long‐term effects or delayed generalization. Although baseline equivalence was achieved on measured variables, unmeasured confounders remain possible. In particular, socioeconomic status (SES)—including parental income and educational background—was not directly assessed, and group allocation was based on parental preference. While no between‐group differences were observed in the use of community‐based services during the study period, unmeasured family‐level factors, such as caregiver motivation or practical constraints (e.g., work‐related inflexibility), may have influenced participation and potentially affected outcomes.

In addition, because multiple outcome measures were analyzed using ancova, there is a potential risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, particularly given the relatively small sample size. Although ancova was applied to adjust for baseline differences between groups, no formal correction for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni adjustment) was performed. Therefore, the findings, particularly those with marginal significance, should be interpreted with caution and considered preliminary. Future studies should consider pre‐specifying primary outcomes and applying appropriate statistical corrections to reduce the risk of false‐positive findings.

Future research should adopt randomized or quasi‐experimental designs to strengthen causal inference and incorporate longitudinal follow‐up to evaluate the durability of outcomes. Integrating structured parent‐coaching components and quantifying home practice may further enhance the generalization of gains into daily life. It will also be important to identify potential moderators—such as baseline developmental level, parental involvement, or therapist fidelity—to clarify which children benefit most under which conditions. Advanced analytic strategies, such as growth‐curve modeling, reliable change indices, or clinically meaningful difference analyses, may also help elucidate the magnitude and practical significance of observed effects.

From clinical and policy perspectives, this study suggests that low‐intensity, resource‐adapted ESDM programs may represent an effective and scalable option within Japanese community‐based healthcare and welfare systems. By prioritizing fidelity, therapist–parent collaboration, and systematic goal setting, such programs may offer a realistic model for expanding access to evidence‐based early intervention in contexts where high‐intensity programs are difficult to implement.

CONCLUSION

In summary, this study provides preliminary evidence that a low‐intensity ESDM program—delivered once weekly for 24 sessions—was associated with measurable improvements in developmental functioning, while autism symptom severity did not show statistically significant between‐group differences after adjusting for baseline using ancova among toddlers with ASD in a Japanese clinical setting. Although adaptive behavior changes on the Vineland‐II did not reach statistical significance, consistent upward trends in communication and socialization suggest that gains may continue to generalize to daily life with ongoing support.

These findings demonstrate that ESDM principles can be feasibly and effectively applied within the cultural and systemic context of Japan, even under limited service intensity. When high fidelity is maintained, individualized goals are carefully set, and parents are actively involved during sessions, meaningful developmental progress can be achieved without the extensive resources required for high‐intensity programs.

Notably, the observed effect size for developmental outcomes suggests a moderate and clinically meaningful intervention effect, supporting the relevance of low‐intensity ESDM in real‐world clinical settings.

This has particular relevance for community and hospital‐based settings where staffing, time, and financial constraints make intensive models difficult to implement.

Future studies should employ randomized or quasi‐experimental designs to verify these results, examine long‐term maintenance of gains, and clarify the mechanisms that support generalization to everyday functioning. In addition, future research should incorporate more sensitive outcome measures of social communication, such as BOSCC, to better capture intervention‐related changes. Incorporating structured parent‐coaching components and quantifying home practice may further enhance adaptive outcomes. Ultimately, developing sustainable, culturally responsive models of early intervention will be essential for expanding access to ESDM and related NDBI approaches for young children with ASD across Japan and other Asian contexts.
