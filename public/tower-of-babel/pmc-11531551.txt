Impact of corn shredlage and crabtree-negative yeast on silage quality and rumen fermentation characteristics
Authors: Chanon Suntara, Anusorn Cherdthong, Sunisa Pongsub, Natdanai Kanakai, Theerachai Haitook, Khundej Suriharn, Rittikeard Prachumchai, Wuttikorn Srakaew, Supanon Tunim, Pin Chanjula, Chaowarit Mapato
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe physical attributes of corn silage are enhanced by shredlage (SHR), while there is a rising interest in boosting its biological performance. This study aimed to assess and compare the impact of both the chopping method and different yeast strains on ensilage quality including the in vitro evaluation of corn silage. Both types of corn, including chopped and shredded, were harvested on the same day from the same field where the same corn hybrid (Suwan 5) was grown. Subsequently, whole-corn plants were fermented with additives. A 2 × 5 Factorial completely randomized design was employed, where factor A represents corn chopped (CON) and corn shredded (SHR), and factor B represents the additives: no additives, molasses + urea (M + U), M + U + Candida tropicalis KKU20, M + U + Pichia kudriavzevii KKU20, and M + U + saccharomyces cerevisiae. The results demonstrated that SHR fermentation with M + U and yeast significantly increased in vitro dry matter degradability (IVDMD) and organic matter degradability (IVOMD). Specifically, at 4 h post-incubation, the addition of Crabtree-negative yeast led to a 5.8% increase in total volatile fatty acids (TVFA) compared to the Crabtree-positive yeast group (P < 0.01). The C2 (acetic acid) + C4 (normal butyric acid and isobutyric acid): C3 (propionic acid) ratio showed a significant decrease without additives, but P. kudriavzevii KKU20 led to the highest ratio and methane production (P < 0.01). Based on this study, it could be concluded that SHR harvesting led to higher digestion efficiency in the rumen. The use of M + U + yeast also demonstrated uncertain effects on rumen fermentation efficiency, and the inclusion of P. kudriazevii KKU20 may potentially reduce rumen fermentation efficiency when used with corn silage.Keywords: Corn shredlage, Crabtree yeast, Nutritive value, Rumen fermentation, In vitro degradationSubject terms: Animal physiology, Microbiology techniques

Introduction

Corn silage is recognized as one of the high-quality roughage feeds for ruminant livestock, boasting a comparatively high biomass yield and energy density in contrast to other forage sources1. However, the development of harvesting methods and fermentation processes to enhance the suitability of corn silage for animal utilization is still ongoing2,3. One of the emerging methods involves increasing the grinding force of the equipment to break up the fibers and corn kernels, thereby enhancing the fermentation and digestion of corn silage in the rumen. This innovative harvesting method is referred to as “shredded corn silage” or “shredlage” (SHR)4. Research on altering the physical characteristics of corn silage using SHR has shown that it is likely to enhance animal utilization and increase productivity5. Moreover, the addition of nutritional value to corn silage through the incorporation of silage additives during the fermentation process is an area of considerable interest for further study. Silage additives utilized in the corn fermentation process, such as molasses and urea, are primarily focused on improving fermentation quality and enhancing the nutritional value of the silage6. Molasses serves as a rich carbon source that promotes microbial proliferation, particularly lactic acid bacteria (LAB7) and yeast, facilitating a more efficient fermentation process. Urea, on the other hand, provides a critical nitrogen source that supports microbial protein synthesis and yeast growth, thus further enriching the silage’s nutritional profile8.

The concept of fermentation, however, can be broadened to focus not only on increasing the number of LABs but also on enhancing the nutritional value of the silage. Various approaches, such as the incorporation of molasses and urea, in addition to yeast, can improve corn silage by enriching it with essential nutrients like protein, energy, and roughage9. Molasses provides a rich carbon source, while urea supplies nitrogen, both of which are crucial for the optimal growth and activity of yeast. Together, these additives enhance the concentration of single-cell protein within the fermented feed, thereby improving its overall nutritional profile.

Moreover, while yeast remains viable within the fermented feed, it can serve as a probiotic, thereby augmenting rumen function10. However, some yeast strains may lack the requisite metabolic machinery to efficiently multiply within the fermenting substrate, thereby limiting their proliferation and impact on feed quality. According to recent investigations conducted by Suntara et al.11,12 it has been revealed that yeast strains intended for fermenting roughage sources can be isolated from the rumen of the cattle. This specific cohort of yeasts exhibits a remarkable enhancement in cellular proliferation by 13% compared to commercially available strains, along with a notable fivefold increase in cellulase enzyme production. Furthermore, it was observed that during the fermentation of rice straw, the ruminal yeast did not exert a discernible impact on the fermentation process. Notably, the pH levels remained within normal parameters post-fermentation, attributed to the symbiotic coexistence of yeast with LAB within the fermented substrate.

The products yielded from the conclusive fermentation process enable animals to efficiently harness the energy, true protein, and probiotics activity. Rumen yeast demonstrates a greater capacity for stimulating bacterial proliferation within the rumen compared to commercial yeast strains13. Accordingly, yeast emerges as a noteworthy supplement additive for our investigation, spurring an inquiry into its effectiveness when integrated into the fermentation processes of corn silage. In our review of prior research, endeavors were undertaken to enhance the quality of corn silage through the incorporation of yeast during the fermentation process. As reported by Duniere et al.14, the inoculation of Saccharomyces yeast strains exhibited neither adverse nor beneficial effects on the quantified microbial populations or the overall quality of the silage. Additionally, it was observed to improve fiber digestibility. However, there is a notable absence of reports on the utilization of yeast in conjunction with SHR, with existing studies primarily limited to physical comparisons between SHR and CON15,16 .

In many countries, studies comparing the SHR method with CON have been conducted regarding the digestibility of nutrients, as evidenced by the work of Jančík et al.16. Their findings indicate that SHR enhances the digestibility of dry matter, organic matter, protein, and neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and demonstrates superior energy efficiency compared to traditional methods. However, in Thailand, the corn shredlage technique remains the sole commercially available option, with no available data as yet. Furthermore, there is a dearth of reports on the utilization of silage additives such as rumen yeast for fermenting corn silage.

Therefore, this research aimed to investigate the impact of chopping and shredding corn, along with the incorporation of rumen-isolated yeast, on alterations in chemical composition post-fermentation. Specifically, the study sought to assess the quality of corn following fermentation, the digestibility of corn silage, and alterations in the ruminal fermentation process.

This interaction yielded a statistically significant variance in NDF fiber content (P < 0.01). Specifically, the CON group, devoid of additives, exhibited a notably higher NDF fiber content compared to the other experimental groups (P < 0.01). However, without additives to SHR, the results are not the same as CON because SHR causes the amount of NDF to decrease.

Corn silage fermentation profile

The alterations in pH, lactic acid, lactic acid bacteria (LAB), ethanol, and yeast count in corn silage are provided in Table 3. The interaction between the chop method and additives exerted a significant influence on both pH levels and lactic acid concentrations (P < 0.01). Specifically, the combination of SHR with S. cerevisiae resulted in elevated pH values following fermentation. Furthermore, lactic acid levels in this combination were notably lower compared to other treatment groups (P < 0.01). Orthogonal contrast analysis revealed that the additives (containing urea) led to higher pH levels after 14 days of fermentation, contrasting with lower lactic acid concentrations. It is worth noting that the increase in pH level is caused by M + U + yeast added in the fermentation process. The inoculation of yeast contributed to elevated pH values ​​as well, particularly notable with the presence of Crabtree-positive yeast strains (S. cerevisiae), which exhibited the most pronounced effect on pH elevation post-fermentation (P < 0.01). Ensuring the appropriate pH level is essential for safeguarding the integrity of ensiled plant material over time. Conversely, the presence of S. cerevisiae, a Crabtree-positive yeast, resulted in a notably diminished quantity of LAB (P < 0.01). The association between yeast and LAB in corn silage exhibited species-specific differences in the current experiment. However, elucidating this relationship proves challenging due to the stark disparities observed in previous investigations.

There was no interaction between the chop method and additives that affected the amount of ethanol produced after fermentation. However, in the main factor of applying M + U and yeast supplementation in ensiling corn silage, there was a significant increase in ethanol content, as opposed to the group without additives. Orthogonal contrast analysis clarified that the additive-supplemented group reached 30.4% ethanol content (P < 0.01). M + U is frequently employed in corn fermentation to enhance its quality. This study also revealed a notable contrast in ethanol production between Crabtree-negative and Crabtree-positive yeast strains after 14 days of fermentation, with the former demonstrating higher ethanol yields (P < 0.01). The amount of yeast population increased when yeast was used in combination with M + U during corn silage in both chop methods (P < 0.01). The data, analyzed through orthogonal contrast, indicated that Crabtree-negative yeast exhibited higher levels compared to Crabtree-positive yeast following the 14-day silage process (P < 0.01), suggesting the resilience of yeast to survive post-fermentation.

In vitro assessment of ruminal degradation in corn silage

The degradability of corn silage processed by different methods with additives was assessed by the in vitro method and is given in Table 4. The mean degradability values at 24 h exhibited variance attributed to the interaction between the chop method and additives. Specifically, higher values of IVDMD and IVOMD were observed in SHR fermentation with M + U and yeast. Conversely, untreated CON displayed the lowest results (P < 0.01). Notably, the digestibility of CON significantly improved with the incorporation of S. cerevisiae (P < 0.01). After 48 h of incubation, both IVDMD and IVOMD exhibited a notable increase of 10.7% and 9.5%, respectively, when SHR was utilized in contrast to CON (P < 0.01).

Ammonia nitrogen and total volatile fatty acid in rumen

The levels of total volatile fatty acids and ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) in the rumen are presented in Table 5. Untreated corn silage displayed notably lower NH3-N levels compared to the other groups, while the addition of M + U or yeast led to increased NH3-N levels during fermentation with both CON and SHR (P < 0.01). Orthogonal contrast analysis revealed that at 4 h post-incubation, Crabtree-positive yeast yielded NH3-N levels 18.9% higher than those observed with Crabtree-negative yeast. However, there wasn’t any noticeable difference between the two types of yeast after 8 h of incubation (P < 0.01). Furthermore, in contrast to Crabtree-negative yeast strains, C. tropicalis KKU20 exhibited significantly elevated NH3-N concentrations compared to P. kudriazevii KKU20 at the 8-hour time point post-incubation (P < 0.01). The influence between the factors of chop method and additives did not affect the mean TVFA values ​​between the groups, but it was found that in the 4th hour after incubation, supplementation with the Crabtree-negative yeast group helped cause corn silage to produce more TVFA than the group Crabtree-positive yeast supplemented at 5.8% (P < 0.01).

Proportion of volatile fatty acid in rumen

The main effects of corn silage on the proportion change of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) in the rumen are presented in Table 6. After 4 h of incubation, no interaction was observed between the chop method and additives affecting the production of C2, C3, Iso-C4, and N-C5. However, it was noted that the utilization of M + U + P. kudriazevii KKU20 led to a significant reduction in the proportion of C3 (P < 0.01). Additionally, the utilization of SHR resulted in a statistically significant reduction in N-C5 (P < 0.01). The orthogonal contrast analysis indicates that Crabtree-negative yeast had a statistically significant effect on the reduction of C2 and Iso-C4 (P < 0.01). Particularly in this group, P. kudriazevii KKU20 exhibited an influence on the reduction of C3 and Iso-C4 (P < 0.01). Significant alterations were observed in branched-chain volatile fatty acids (BCVFA), as illustrated in Fig. 1. The decrease in mean values of N-C4 and Iso-C5 resulted from the interaction between the chop method and additives (P < 0.01). Specifically, it was determined that fermenting CON supplemented with M + U + P. kudriazevii KKU20 led to a reduced proportion of N-C4 and Iso-C5 compared to other groups. Furthermore, employing SHR and fermenting with M + U resulted in the lowest proportion of N-C4 and Iso-C5 (P < 0.01) after 4 h of incubation. After 8 h of incubation, discernible variations in the proportions of C2 and C3 arose, attributable to the interplay between the chop method and additives. Notably, the lowest proportions of C2 and C3 were observed when CON fermented with M + U + P. kudriazevii KKU20. Conversely, the proportion of C3 was highest when CON was fermented without the addition of M + U (P < 0.01, Fig. 2). Moreover, CON without M + U supplementation resulted in the lowest proportions of N-C4 and N-C5 (P < 0.01, Fig. 3).

Discussion

The chemical composition of the corn silage was similar to that in previous studies in Thailand17,18. Corn varieties cultivated in tropical climates, such as Thailand, demonstrate elevated levels of NDF and ADF in comparison to their counterparts from temperate regions, indicative of distinct regional plant attributes14,19. Typically, changes in DM content resulting from additive supplementation are minimal, as bacterial additives generally promote preservation but have no effect on DM20, while enzyme additives may inadvertently lead to losses21. However, the present study diverges from this pattern, employing a supplement solution comprising molasses and urea, whose components serve to stimulate the proliferation of yeast, a departure from the typical use of bacterial inoculants or enzymes. Our study suggests that the elevated DM may likely be attributed to the presence of molasses. Molasses, rich in fermentable carbohydrates, boosts lactic acid bacteria growth, quickly lowering the pH and preserving nutrients, which reduces moisture loss and increases DM content22. In addition, the combination of molasses and urea may stimulate yeast growth, leading to ethanol production that retains DM by reducing volatile losses, while urea enhances microbial protein synthesis, further stabilizing the silage23. In a study conducted by Baytok et al.24, that compared the efficacy of various silage additives in corn silage fermentation, it was observed that the inclusion of molasses led to a notable increase in DM content, approximately 9.7% higher than that of the control group. Furthermore, this experiment implies that molasses predominantly influences the augmentation of CP levels. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that our experiment’s inclusion of urea introduces additional variables, rendering it inconclusive whether the observed effects are solely attributable to molasses.

In this experiment, the observed high DM contents prompted an investigation into the ash content present in molasses, which could potentially account for this effect. Molasses, a viscous syrup utilized as a sweetening agent, is a byproduct of the sugar-making process, characterized by its significant mineral composition, including potassium, calcium, sulfate, chloride, etc. Analysis of ash content was among the numerous analytical procedures conducted on the molasses sample25. Hence, it is probable that the ash content in molasses is elevated, contrasting with the typically low ash content and high nitrogen source and other compounds which are organic matter found in urea26. Therefore, the fact that urea exhibits minimal ash content, suggests its potential lesser influence in this regard. The variability in ash content in molasses typically ranges between 7–15%27, which may explain the increase in DM content observed when additives with a high ash proportion are introduced. Upon examining the main factors (additives) associated with the ash content of the corn silage, it is plausible that all the reasons align consistently.

The CP content was mainly influenced by the main factors including processing methods and additives. Thus, in terms of processing, it was found that CON was characterized by a much lower CP content than the SHR. The CP content was 26.7% for CON and 28.2% for SHR (a difference of 5.6%). Similar to the study conducted Jančík et al.16, which examined the impact of conventional and shredlage harvest processing on the chemical composition and quality of corn silage intended for animal utilization, their experiment revealed notable findings. One plausible explanation for the higher CP content observed in SHR compared to CON could be related to how the seeds are processed during shredding. SHR processing involves a unique cutting mechanism that can potentially break down the corn kernels more effectively than CON methods. This increased exposure of the kernels to the fermentation process may result in greater protein preservation or solubilization, ultimately contributing to higher CP values in the resulting silage. However, the effect of SHR harvesting and processing corn on CP is unclear. There are also previous reports show that the CON and SHR methods have similar results28,29. In addition, the predominant source of nitrogen is obtained from urea, leading to a heightened CP content in corn ensiled with additives. Augmenting CP levels in ruminant feed is a common practice, achieved by incorporating nitrogen sources into feed constituents. Typically, this involves supplementing low-quality roughage sources, such as rice straw30, with cost-effective nitrogen sources, such as agricultural by-products31. This understanding has persisted for over 70 years, rooted in recommendations from the National Research Council (NRC) affirming ruminants’ ability to harness nitrogen and convert it into microbial protein32, which they can effectively utilize. This experiment is designed to elevate the CP content in corn silage, renowned for its high starch and energy content. Augmenting the CP level will further enhance the protein richness of the corn silage.

Limited literature exists regarding the divergence in EE profiles attributed to yeast influence, particularly concerning a comparative analysis between Crabtree-negative and Crabtree-positive yeast strains. Nonetheless, a prior study by Suntara et al.33, which employed C. tropicalis KKU20 in the fermentation of winged bean (Psophocarpus tetragonolobus) tubers (WBT), revealed noteworthy findings. Specifically, the EE content after the fermentation process exhibited a marked increase, approximately 21.4% higher than observed in the group utilizing S. cerevisiae for fermentation. Indeed, the two selected yeast strains, distinguished by their Crabtree-negative and positive characteristics, manifest clear disparities in energy metabolism. Prior experimental evidence substantiates that Crabtree-negative yeasts prefer respiratory metabolism even under conditions of ample glucose availability. This metabolic preference results in heightened flux through the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation pathways34. On the other hand, Crabtree-positive yeasts undergo a distinct metabolic metabolism, where the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDH) enzymes, thereby leading to alcohol fermentation during plentiful oxygenate conditions. This leads them to ferment even in oxygenate and high glucose35. The metabolic pattern expedites energy production via glycolysis which leads to lessened carbon flux through the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation pathways. Accordingly, less carbon is directed to lipid synthesis than in Crabtree-negative yeasts is likely the main reason this occurred in our study.

The observed variance in fiber formation between the CON and SHR treatments presents a challenging aspect to elucidate. This disparity in trends has been documented in the study by Jančík et al.29, where it was noted that the content of CF was marginally higher in the SHR group compared to the CON group (25.4% DM versus 24.5% DM), similar to our findings. One plausible explanation for this phenomenon could be attributed to the increased surface area resulting from chopping and shredding, which in turn may potentiate microbial activity. This leads to the more efficient breakdown of fiber compounds. Conversely, CON may have a different particle size and distribution, impacting microbial access to fiber substrates and subsequent fermentation dynamics. These differences in processing and structural characteristics likely contribute to the observed variations in fiber content between CON and SHR during fermentation.

As observed in numerous prior studies, it has been noted that the alkalinity attributed to the combination of M + U tends to elevate the pH after the fermentation process. Naeini et al.36 demonstrated that the utilization of M + U solution in fermenting maize, sorghum, and sorghum bagasse resulted in a notably greater pH increase compared to the control group. Similarly, Demirel et al.37 a similar trend in corn silage, where the addition of M + U led to a significant rise in pH levels. This phenomenon can be elucidated by the process in which urea decomposes into NH3 through urease activity during the ensiling phase, thus retarding the reduction in pH due to the alkaline nature of NH338. Muck et al.39 note that a pH reading below 4.0 is generally endorsed for facilitating the prolonged storage of ensiled matter. In this experiment, the range of pH values ​​was 4.25–3.88, which is still considered to be at an acceptable level, which means that the fermentation process and the LAB were still functioning well. The utilization of Crabtree-negative yeast in the treatment group did not exert a comparable impact on the abundance of LAB in corn silage as observed in the control group.

The Crabtree-negative yeast strains utilized in our study were consistent with those employed by Suntara et al.11 and Suntara et al.33. Both studies reported a reduction in the LAB population attributed to the presence of Crabtree-positive yeast strains. This phenomenon can be attributed to their shared utilization of the same sugar source for cell entry, thereby engaging in competitive interactions. Indeed, the impact of Crabtree-negative yeast remains somewhat ambiguous. For instance, Nair et al.40 reported that S. cerevisiae exerted no discernible influence on the LAB population. Conversely, Ali et al.15 observed that ensiled corn silage with S. cerevisiae led to an even greater increase in lactic acid levels compared to the control group. Molasses, with its abundance of fermentable sugars exceeding 55%, stands as a readily accessible resource27. Furthermore, urea serves as a nitrogen source crucial for yeast growth and metabolism during fermentation41.

The experiment’s results challenge the common belief that Crabtree-negative yeast strains produce less ethanol. This difference is because these strains mainly use aerobic respiration for sugar breakdown, leading to lower ethanol production compared to Crabtree-positive yeast strains34. On the other hand, Crabtree-positive yeast strains, like S. cerevisiae, swiftly convert sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide through fermentation, even in oxygen-rich conditions a process known as the Crabtree effect42. This aligns with prior research conducted by Suntara et al.43, which demonstrated that S. cerevisiae displayed the highest ethanol production, while P. kudriavzevii-KKU20 and C. tropicalis-KKU20 exhibited relatively lower levels of ethanol production. However, our experiment yields contrasting results. One potential explanation could be that previous experiments, employing larger quantities of sugar such as 25% molasses, might have exerted a more substantial influence on sugar metabolism compared to our experiment, which utilized only 5% molasses.

The increased degradability observed in the SHR in contrast to the CON is in line with findings from a previous study conducted by Jančík et al.29, where the SHR exhibited higher rates of dry matter disappearance after 48 h (+ 3.76%). Similar trends were also noted for the disappearance of organic matter (OM, + 3.33%). This is consistent with Jančík,et al.16, those who found that SHR was able to increase the degradation of DM in corn silage by 7.7% and in OM by 7.5% compared to the CON. The processing method of shredlage results in a more favorable physical structure, potentially increasing surface area and accessibility for rumen microbes to degrade the feed material as suggested by recent studies on forage processing techniques44. The incorporation of M + U (molasses and urea) or yeast further contributes to increased digestibility through different mechanisms. M + U supplementation provides additional fermentable sugars and nitrogen, essential substrates for microbial growth and metabolism in the rumen, which have been documented in several studies on rumen fermentation​45. This supplementation can promote the proliferation of rumen microbes, thereby accelerating the fermentation process and improving overall digestibility. On the other hand, yeast supplementation can enhance digestibility through various mechanisms such as improved fiber degradation, increased production of microbial enzymes, and modulation of rumen pH10. Overall, the use of M + U or yeast in corn silage fermentation contributes to increased digestibility by providing additional substrates and promoting rumen microbial activity, ultimately leading to more efficient nutrient utilization.

NH3-N levels in the rumen serve as indicators of the equilibrium among nitrogen intake, microbial protein synthesis, and microbial degradation within the rumen ecosystem. Typically falling within the range of 0.35-29 mg/dL, NH3-N concentrations exceeding 100 mg/dL may manifest toxic symptoms46. In the experimental group without additives, NH3-N values ranging from 8.55 to 23.41 mg/dL are considered within the normal range required for the metabolic activities of ruminal bacteria. The utilization of M + U enhances NH3-N levels as this additive solution can undergo urea hydrolysis by rumen microbes, consequently yielding NH3 and thereby serving as a pivotal source of NH3-N within the rumen34. This augmentation in NH3-N creates an enriched environment conducive to rumen microbial proliferation and subsequent metabolic activities. This observation is congruent with the findings of Suntara et al.12, those who investigated the impact of different yeast groups. Their study focusing on fermenting rice straw for dairy cow nutrition showed a noteworthy increase in TVFA levels 4 h post-feeding, particularly in the Crabtree-negative yeast group, surpassing the Crabtree-positive yeast group by 6.9%. This reduction can be attributed to the proficient stimulation of bacterial proliferation by Crabtree-negative yeast, thereby leading to heightened TVFA production. Furthermore, in the study conducted by Va et al.47, Crabtree-negative yeasts, specifically C. tropicalis KKU20, were incorporated into the diet of Thai Native × Anglo Nubian crossbred goats. It was observed that the inclusion of yeast notably augmented the TVFA content.

The application of P. kudriazevii KKU20 influenced the VFA profile, with notable alterations observed at various incubation times. The addition of this yeast to the fermentation process resulted in a decrease in the proportion of C3 and BCVFAs. Contrary to previous reports by Suntara et al.12, the use of P. kudriazevii KKU20 increased C3 content when fermented with rice straw as a roughage source in dairy cows. This experiment concluded that Crabtree-negative yeast has a greater potential to increase C3 than Crabtree-positive yeast, while changes in BCVFAs have not been previously reported. It is noteworthy that corn shredlage affects BCVFAs; however, there are very few reports on the influence of shredlage. A reduction in BCVFAs is generally attributed to a decrease in rumen pH. Experiments by Vargas et al.48 revealed that iso-acids or BCVFAs were greater at pH 6.8 than at pH 6.4, consistent with Roman-Garcia et al.49, who noted that a decline in pH led to a decrease in BCVFAs. This decline also affects reduced protein degradation; however, it has no notable impact on microbial protein synthesis. BCVFAs in the rumen primarily arise from the degradation of dietary true protein50. Factors affecting the protein breakdown process may influence the capacity to utilize carbon skeletons, resulting in a decrease. Therefore, in our experiment, the processing of SHR, which breaks down the corn kernel, likely lowers the pH level due to rapid fermentation compared to the CON, thus reducing the production of BCVFAs.

The C2 + C4 to C3 ratio is utilized to indicate the balance of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) in the rumen, which reflects the efficiency of rumen fermentation. According to survey data and meta-analysis, the ideal C2 + C4 to C3 ratio typically ranges from 1.54 to 3.5451. However, some reports suggest that this ratio can be as high as 4.517. In this experiment, the C2 + C4 to C3 ratio ranged from 3.76 to 4.73, which is relatively high. This high ratio can be attributed to both the large production volume of C2 + C4 and the absence of a concentrate diet in the experiment. The concentrat diet is an essential component in C3 production. This study focused on corn silage and additives, which likely explains this high ratio trend. While it seems that the use of additives will result in a higher C2 + C4 to C3 ratio than without supplements, when analyzed by the proportion of VFA, the use of M + U tends to cause a decrease in C3 and an increase in C4 compared to the corn silage group without any additives. This finding is different from a previous experiment using ensiled rice straw, which found that the use of M + U increased the amount of C3 due to the increase in lactic acid content in the fermentation process. The addition of enzymes or LAB into the fermentation process will help increase the amount of C352. In contrast, the current experiment utilized corn silage, which contains high starch in the fermentation process (more than rice straw). Notably, the group without additives had a higher amount of lactic acid compared to the group that received the additive. The addition of the additive in this experiment resulted in a lower amount of lactic acid and a higher pH value. Following secondary fermentation within the silage, lactic acid is metabolized by lactic-utilizing bacteria (LUB) in the rumen53. Furthermore, there is a conversion to C3 through the succinate-propionic acid and acrylate pathway54. Consequently, the level of lactate could influence C3 in the rumen and it may be logical that not using additives in corn silage could reduce this ratio. The C2 + C4:C3 ratio, which encompasses both C2 and C4, involved in H2 production, and C3, which is involved in H2 utilization, showed a slight improvement in the relationship54. This suggests that the C2 + C4:C3 ratio is relatively associated with the amount of CH4 production. In this research, the absence of additives in corn silage altered the rumen VFA composition, increasing C3 and expense of C4 and C2 in corn silage. This change in rumen fermentation patterns towards the H2-consuming pathway (C3) at the cost of the H2-producing pathway (C2 and C4) could indicate an excess of H2 due to reduced CH4 production55. Furthermore, the high level of lactic acid in the group without additives is likely to impact the pH in the rumen and, consequently, the amount of methanogens as well. Valdes et al.56 reported that ruminal methanogens lose the ability to utilize H2 at low pH, leading to free H2 in the gas phase when the pH is less than 5.5. Conversely, starch-fermenting bacteria can compete against methanogens for hydrogen utilization by producing large amounts of propionate. This suggests that rumen fermentation efficiency may be sufficient when utilizing the starch properties of corn in the fermentation process and that the addition of any additives may reduce this efficiency.

Under the conditions of this study, it was concluded that molasses plus urea on silage do not have the same positive effects as SHR in terms of increasing dry matter digestibility when compared to untreated CON. Interestingly, untreated CON surpassed SHR regarding ruminal fermentation efficiency after the fermentation process. The addition of the yeast strain C. tropicalis KKU20 to corn silage significantly reduces the silage’s fiber content in comparison to other additions. Above all, the yeast strain that was isolated from this specific rumen environment had the advantageous characteristic of not causing any disturbance to the lactobacilli population during the silage-making process. There is no appreciable effect was found on changes in the rumen’s fermentation dynamics or on the degradation of dry matter. However, the yeast strain P. kudriazevii KKU20 showed a tendency to increase the ratios of C2 and C4, which in turn resulted in increased synthesis of CH4. These findings suggest that using Crabtree-negative yeasts like C. tropicalis KKU20 and P. kudriazevii KKU20 in silage management can optimize fiber degradation and enhance silage stability, benefiting farmers by improving nutritional quality without needing chemical additives. However, P. kudriazevii KKU20 should be used selectively, especially where methane production is a concern, or where maximizing energy from VFAs is prioritized.

Materials and methods

The study area was accessed with the appropriate permissions and licenses following the regulations. Approval for the use of experimental animals and methodology was obtained from the Animal Ethics Committee of Khon Kaen University, National Research Council of Thailand, under record no. IACUC-KKU-106/65. Throughout the investigation, no anesthesia or animal sacrifice was conducted, ensuring compliance with all relevant guidelines and regulations. Our research adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Whole-corn plat material and silage

Whole-plant corn silage (WPCS) material was obtained from 5 ha of Suwan5 corn silage (SP corn silage company, Khon Buri, Nakhon Ratchasima province, Thailand:14°31′24″N 102°14′54″East), which were grown in October 2022. The Whole-plant corn silage (WPCS) at the one-half milk line stage of maturity was harvested using two self-propelled forage harvesters: SHR was harvested by a Claas Jaguar 860 (Harsewinkel, Germany) self-propelled harvester equipped with a 75-Orbis header, while conventionally processed corn silage (CON) was harvested with an experimental pull-type harvester fitted with on-board rollers. The SHR and CON were harvested in 1 d, and they were stored in separate plastic silo bags. To determine the particle size distribution of the corn silage, the other duplicate fresh feed samples of the corn silage were separated by particle size using a 3-screen Jaylor Particle Separator into 3 particle-size fractions: long (> 19 mm), medium (8–19 mm) and short (< 8 mm).

Yeast obtained

Crabtree-negative ruminal yeasts included Candida tropicalis KKU20 (C. tropicalis KKU20) and Pichia kudriavzevii KKU20 (P. kudriavzevii KKU20) with strain numbers CBS 94T (U45749) and CBS 5147T (MH545928) respectively, were obtained from the experiment of Suntara et al.43. The yeast has been isolated, screened, and identified from the rumen of Thai–Holstein Friesian dairy cattle until the product is in its dry form. These two ruminal yeast strains have been proven to enhance the quality of feedstuffs in previous studies11 before being chosen for use in this trial. Crabtree-positive yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisae (S. cerevisae), was obtained from commercial yeast (Perfect Yeast Co., Ltd., Ubon Ratchathani, Thailand).

Preparation of yeast

To activate and expand yeast powder (1 × 106), it was cultured in a medium consisting of 50 g of molasses (Khon Kaen Dairy Cooperative Co., Ltd., Khon Kaen, Thailand) and 50 g of urea per 1 L of water. The pH of the solution was then regulated with formic acid (L.C. Industrial Co., Ltd, Nakhon Pathom, Thailand) to achieve a final value of 3.5. For adequate respiration and optimal yeast cell growth, the medium solution is pumped directly into an electromagnetic air compressor (HAILEA ACO-318 oxygen pump, Sagar Aquarium, Gujarat, India) and oxygenated for 60 h. Fully grown yeast populations reach 1 × 1012 cells/ml. The medium solution was mixed at a corn silage ratio of 0.5:1 and adjusted to a moisture content of 70% to ensure proper ensilage conditions57. The corn silage (CON and SHR) was combined with yeast and media solution using various methods (details provided in the study design). Once thoroughly mixed with the yeast solution, 300 g of corn silage fermented with the solution and yeast was manually placed into a plastic bag (8 × 12 cm, E-Sann Pass Pack 1999 Co., Ltd., Khon Kaen, Thailand) with five replications for each ensilage. The samples in the packaging were vacuum-sealed (DZ-400 vacuum machine, Nakhoncenterpack Co., Ltd., Nakhon Ratchasima, Thailand) and stored at room temperature for 14 days11.

Study design

A 2 × 5 Factorial Completely Randomized Design (CRD) was employed, where factor A represents the chop method (corn chopped and corn shredded), and factor B represents the additives: no additives, molasses (50 g/distilled water) + urea (50 g/distilled water) (M + U), molasses (50 g/distilled water) + urea (50 g/distilled water) + Candida tropicalis KKU20 (M + U + C), molasses (50 g/distilled water) + urea (50 g/distilled water) + Pichia kudriavzevii KKU20 (M + U + P), and molasses (50 g/distilled water) + urea (50 g/distilled water) + S. cerevisiae (M + U + S). The corn silage treatments were divided into ten groups of treatment that included: CON + no additives (T1), CON + M + U (T2), CON + M + U + C (T3), CON + M + U + P (T4), CON + M + U + S (T5), SHR + no additives (T6), SHR + M + U (T7), SHR + M + U + C (T8), SHR + M + U + P (T9), SHR + M + U + S (T10). Samples were taken alternately from each load of Whole-plant corn silage (WPCS) at the bagger to determine the moisture content. The pre-ensiling moisture content of the WPCS treatments was consistent, averaging 65–70%.

Evaluation of fermentation quality and chemical composition

After the specified 14 days, five plastic bags per treatment were opened. Microbial numbers were assessed by counting individual colonies following the method of Kozaki & Okada54. Ten grams of the corn silage were placed into a plastic bag containing 90 ml of sterilized distilled water and thoroughly mixed. Liquid samples of 1 ml were serially diluted in 0.85% sodium chloride solution at dilutions of 10− 1, 10− 3, and 10− 5. Twenty microliters of various solutions were then spread onto agar plates. LAB was counted on Lactobacilli MRS agar (Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd, Mumbai, India) without an oxygen box at 30 °C for 48 h (Sugiyamagen Ltd. Tokyo, Japan). Yeast and mold presence were determined by cultivating them on potato dextrose agar (Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd, Mumbai, India). The mold count was conducted after a two-day culture period, which aligns with previous studies that demonstrated sufficient mold growth within this timeframe11,58. This duration also helps to avoid potential overgrowth issues that can complicate accurate counting, as noted by Khota et al.59. Yeast was distinguished from molds and other microbes based on colony presence and cell morphology observations. The remaining liquid samples were divided into two parts. The first part underwent analysis for organic acids by centrifugation at 6,000 rpm for 15 min. The liquid above the solid residue was then filtered using a 0.45-micron syringe filter. Subsequently, lactic acid, ethanol, and volatile fatty acid (VFA) analyses were conducted using gas chromatography (Model HP 6890, Hewlett-Packard Co., Ltd. NY, USA). The determination of NH3-N analysis was performed using a spectrophotometer (UV/VIS Spectrometer, PG Instruments Ltd., London, UK) following the methods outlined by Fawcett & Scott55. In the second part, the pH of the liquid samples was measured using a glass electrode pH meter after being stored at 4 °C for 24 h (Hanna HI-8424 Portable pH/ORP Meter, Woonsocket, RI, USA).

Corn silage samples were dried at 60 °C for 48 h in a forced-air drying oven until a constant moisture level was achieved. Subsequently, the samples were ground using a grinder machine through a 1-mm steel screen (Wiley mill, Arthur H. Thomas Co., Philadelphia, PA, USA) before chemical analysis. The corn silage was analyzed for dry matter (DM; ID 967.03), ash (ID 492.05), and ether extract (EE; ID 455.08) according to the AOAC method (1998). CP content was determined by analyzing the nitrogen content multiplied by a conversion factor of 6.25 using a Leco FP-828 P combustion analyzer (Leco FP828 Nitrogen analyzer, LECO Corporation, MI, USA). The content of NDF, acid ADF, and ADL was determined using a detergent analysis method60.

Preparing of in vitro degradability inoculums

Rumen fluid was collected from two rumen-fistulated dairy steers with body weights averaging 385 ± 7.0 kg. The steers were individually housed and were fed rice straw ad libitum, along with a concentrated diet containing 140 g/kg CP, formulated to meet the NRC61 requirements for dairy cattle. The diet included cassava chip (53.91%), palm kernel meal (9.98%), rice bran (9.98%), soybean meal (19.96%), molasses (2.99%), premix (0.25%, vitamins, and minerals; A: 10,000,000 IU; Vitamin E: 70,000 IU; Vitamin D: 1,600,000 IU; Fe: 50 g; Zn: 40 g; Mn: 40 g; Co: 0.1 g; Cu: 10 g; Se: 0.1 g; I: 0.5 g), di-calcium phosphate (0.1%), urea (1.5%), and salt (1.23%). Additionally, the steers were provided with a suitable mineral lick and had permanent access to clean water for at least 14 days. Before the morning feeding, 1500 ml of rumen fluid was obtained from the animals via cannula. The samples were filtered through four layers of cheesecloth and placed in a thermally insulated container (maintained at 39 °C) before being delivered to the laboratory within 15 min. According to Menke and Steingass62, artificial saliva preparations containing distilled water (1095 ml), a micro mineral mixture (0.23 ml; MnCl2.4H2O 10.0 g/100 ml, CaCl2.2H2O 13.2 g/100 ml, CoCl2.6H2O 1.0 g/100 ml, and FeCl3.6H2O 8.0 g/100 ml ), a macro mineral mixture (365 ml; KH2PO4 6.2 g/L, Na2HPO4 5.7 g/L, NaCl 2.22 g/L, and MgSO4.7H2O 0.6 g/L), a resazurin mixture 0.1% (1 ml), a reduction mixture (60 ml; Na2.S.9H2O 580.0 mg/ 60 ml of NaOH), and a buffer mixture (730 ml; NaHCO3 35.0 g/L and NH4HCO3 4.0 g/L). Following that, the rumen fluid (660 ml) was combined with artificial saliva under non-oxygen conditions.

Dietary treatments and design

Weighed 0.5 g of samples in the 50 ml bottles; 40 ml of rumen liquor medium was added to each treatment bottle using an 18 gauge × 1.5-inch needle. All experimental bottles were sealed with butyl rubber stoppers and metal caps before being incubated in a hot-air oven at 39 °C for further measurement. Two experimental bottles were established: Group 1 had the ruminal NH3-N and VFA all examined in the same bottle. The samples were taken at 4 and 8 h of incubation time, from three 3 replicates of the bottle. The last, nutrient degradability was measured in Group 2; samples were obtained at 24 and 48 h after incubation from three bottle replicates.

In vitro degradability and fermentation characteristics

The bottles were divided into two sets. The first set was designated for measuring ruminal parameters at 4 and 8 h post-incubation, including ruminal NH3-N (Kjeldahl methods; AOAC63), and VFA concentrations: total VFA (mol/L) and the major individual VFA (acetic, propionic, n-butyric, iso-butyric, n-valeric, and iso-valeric; mol/100 mol of total VFA) in ruminal fluid, measured by gas chromatography. Methane production is determined by utilizing the VFA values, following the Moss et al.64 equation.

The last set was used to determine IVDMD and IVOMD degradability. The analysis was conducted according to the method described by Menke et al.65.
