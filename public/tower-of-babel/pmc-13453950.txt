Safety of the Dengue Vaccine TAK-003 and Factors Associated with Adverse Events in Travellers from Non-Endemic Areas: A Real-World Prospective Multicentre Pharmacovigilance Cohort Study in Spain
Authors: Daniel Camprubí–Ferrer, Cesc Bertran–Cobo, Lluís Valerio, Meritxell Saloni, Isabel González Nieto, Victoria López López, David Garcia, Elisabet Jurado, Maria Luisa Machado, Daniela Pérez–León, Sarai Cuevas Marín, Carme Subirà, Judit Villar–García, Dolores Herena García, Susana Nuria Ayala Cortés, Maria Sànchez Casado, Aina Gomila–Grange, Claudia Flores, Natalia Morales Llorente, Ester Górriz Hidalgo, Raisa Margarita Morales Martínez, Fátima Vila, Nora Martín, Jose Muñoz, Daniel Camprubí-Ferrer, Daniel Camprubí-Ferrer, Cesc Bertran-Cobo, Meritxell Saloni, David Garcia, Carme Subirà, Fátima Vila, Nora Martín, Claudia Flores, Tessa de Alba, Berta Vicedo, Isabel Vera, Lluís Valerio, Dolores Herena García, Ester Górriz Hidalgo, Laura Soldevila Langa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundTAK-003 is the first dengue vaccine licensed for endemic and non-endemic regions, but real-world safety data in non-endemic travel settings are scarce, particularly for older adults, people with comorbidities, and concomitant vaccinations.MethodsWe conducted a prospective multicentre pharmacovigilance study in eight Spanish travel clinics, enrolling travellers aged ≥4 years who received TAK-003 (January–December 2024). Participants were followed for adverse events (AEs) after each dose. Logistic regression models assessed associations of age, sex, comorbidities, prior flaviviral infection or vaccination history, and concomitant vaccination with risks of any, systemic, or local AEs.FindingsAmong 1028 participants (1851 TAK-003 doses), 998 (97.1%) completed ≥1 follow-up assessments; 587/998 (58.8%, 95% CI 55.7–61.8) reported AEs. After the first dose, 470/998 (47.1%, 44.0–50.2) reported AEs, most often injection site pain and swelling, headache, or malaise, declining to 235/679 (34.6%, 31.1–38.3) after the second dose. No serious AEs, anaphylaxis, or deaths occurred. Female sex was associated with any AEs (adjusted OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.42–2.37) and local AEs (1.89, 1.44–2.48). Prior dengue was associated with any (1.51, 1.07–2.14) and systemic AEs (1.73, 1.22–2.43). Concomitant flaviviral vaccination was associated with higher odds of systemic AEs (1.64, 1.18–2.28). Age ≥60 years and comorbidities were not associated with increased reactogenicity.InterpretationTAK-003 was well tolerated in travellers from non-endemic regions, with no serious AEs reported. Female sex, prior DENV infection, and flaviviral vaccine co-administration were associated with increased reactogenicity; older age, comorbidities, and non-flaviviral vaccine co-administration were not. These findings provide real-world evidence on tolerability of TAK-003 in travellers from non-endemic regions. Larger studies with extended follow-up are needed to better characterise uncommon and longer-term AEs.FundingNo dedicated funding.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science from database inception to December 2025, for studies evaluating the safety, reactogenicity, tolerability, or pharmacovigilance of TAK-003 using the search terms (“TAK-003” OR “Qdenga”) AND “safety”. We included randomised controlled trials, cohort studies, post-marketing studies, and pharmacovigilance reports that described adverse events following TAK-003 in adults, and excluded preprints and non-human studies. We identified one study integrating safety analyses from phase 2 and phase 3 trials, including 788 adult participants. We also identified one post-marketing pharmacovigilance study in travellers from Germany, reporting symptoms after 752 first doses and 387 s doses in participants aged 20 years or older. This study was limited by self-reported data collection, absence of linkage between first and second doses, and potential reporting and selection biases. Overall, real-world evidence from non-endemic travel medicine settings was scarce, particularly for older adults, individuals with comorbidities, and those receiving concomitant vaccines.

Added value of this study

This prospective pharmacovigilance study provides the largest real-world safety assessment of TAK-003 in a non-endemic travel medicine setting, based on active adverse-event surveillance by health-care professionals among 1028 travellers receiving 1851 doses of TAK-003. We confirm the favourable short-term safety profile of TAK-003 and extend the evidence base in several important areas. First, we report no signal of increased short-term reactogenicity in populations underrepresented in clinical trials, including adults older than 60 years and individuals with comorbidities. We identify female sex and prior dengue virus infection as independent and interacting factors associated with adverse event reporting. Finally, we provide a systematic evaluation of concomitant vaccination, showing increased systemic reactogenicity when TAK-003 is co-administered with other flaviviral vaccines, but no major safety concerns when administered with other commonly used travel vaccines.

Implications of all the available evidence

In combination with evidence from phase 3 trials and post-marketing surveillance, these findings support the tolerability of TAK-003 among travellers from non-endemic regions. This study extends the evidence base to real-world travel medicine settings and to populations underrepresented in clinical trials. Sex-related differences in reactogenicity have implications for pre-vaccination counselling, while increased systemic reactogenicity associated with prior dengue virus exposure and concomitant flaviviral vaccination warrants further investigation. The absence of increased risk associated with older age, comorbidities, or co-administration with non-flaviviral vaccines supports the integration of TAK-003 into routine travel medicine practice and may inform future vaccination policy beyond endemic settings.

Introduction

Dengue is the most prevalent arboviral infection worldwide, with transmission reported in more than 100 countries and over 50% of the world's population at risk.1 The global incidence has increased more than eightfold over the past two decades, driven primarily by climate-related expansion of Aedes spp. mosquitoes and the growth in international travel.2,3 In line with global trends, Europe has also experienced a sharp rise in imported dengue cases and autochthonous transmission, making dengue one of the leading causes of fever in international travellers.4, 5, 6 Although most dengue virus (DENV) infections are mild or asymptomatic, severe cases can lead to plasma leakage, severe bleeding, organ impairment, and death.1 Given the lack of any specific antiviral therapies and the persistent challenges in vector control, vaccines have emerged as a promising preventive tool.

In 2022, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) authorised TAK-003, a live-attenuated, tetravalent vaccine against dengue, for individuals aged 4 years and older irrespective of prior DENV exposure, making it the first dengue vaccine approved for non-endemic regions regardless of baseline serostatus.7 Since its approval, the vaccine has been authorized in more than 25 non-endemic countries, with nearly one million doses administered in Europe alone. Despite its expanding use, evidence regarding the vaccine's safety profile among travellers from non-endemic regions remains limited.8 To date, only 788 adults from non-endemic regions have been included in initial clinical trial safety reports,8,9 and a recent pharmacovigilance analysis documented adverse events following 776 first doses and 401 second doses in European travellers, still providing limited evidence on the effects of age, comorbidities, concomitant vaccination, or prior flaviviral exposure on the risk of adverse events (AEs).10 This limited evidence in individuals from non-endemic areas who present lower exposure to DENV, together with the variable efficacy of the vaccine across the four DENV serotypes in seronegative recipients, underscores a critical gap in understanding the risk–benefit balance of vaccination in travellers, and have contributed to divergent national recommendations in Europe.11, 12, 13 Such disparities highlight the urgent need for robust, real-world post-marketing studies to inform, support, and ultimately harmonize dengue vaccination policies across Europe and other non-endemic regions.

To address these gaps, we conducted CATdenga, a real-world prospective study evaluating the safety of TAK-003 in a large cohort of adult travellers in Europe, including substantial numbers of older adults, individuals with comorbidities, and recipients of concomitant vaccines. We also assessed risk factors for AEs to support risk-benefit balanced, evidence-based recommendations on the safe use of dengue vaccination in travellers from non-endemic regions.

Methods

Study design and setting

CATdenga is a prospective, multicentre, post-marketing pharmacovigilance cohort study conducted across eight referral travel clinics in Catalonia, Spain. The study was designed to monitor the short-term safety of the live-attenuated tetravalent dengue vaccine TAK-003 in travellers from non-endemic regions receiving the vaccine as part of their routine pre-travel consultation. Participating sites included Hospital Clínic de Barcelona, ICS Metropolitana Nord, Parc de Salut Mar, Hospital Vall d’Hebron–Drassanes, Hospital Universitari Parc Taulí (Sabadell), Hospital Universitari Arnau de Vilanova (Lleida), Hospital Universitari de Bellvitge, and Complex Sanitari del Garraf i Alt Penedès. The study was supported by the Catalan Public Health Agency.

Participants

Travellers aged 4 years and older with upcoming travel to dengue-endemic countries who attended any participating site between January and December 2024 were eligible if they were prescribed and willing to receive TAK-003 according to national and regional recommendations. In Catalonia, TAK-003 is provided free of charge for: (i) travellers with prior microbiologically confirmed DENV infection travelling to dengue-endemic areas; (ii) travellers at risk of severe dengue with significant travel-exposure to DENV; (iii) female travellers planning pregnancy while residing in or travelling to an endemic area; and (iv) travellers at risk of initiating immunosuppressive therapy who anticipate travel to dengue-endemic regions.13

Individuals outside these categories could receive the dengue vaccine at their own expense when travelling to endemic areas and reporting significant travel exposure to DENV. Prior microbiologically confirmed DENV infection was defined as documentation in medical records of a positive diagnostic test for DENV infection (PCR, NS1 antigen, IgM during acute phase, seroconversion, or IgG for past infection). Significant travel exposure to DENV was defined as travel lasting ≥4 weeks to an endemic area, travel to a region with a declared DENV outbreak, or cumulative exposure resulting from repeated visits to endemic regions.13 In addition to previous DENV infections, risk factors for developing severe dengue included age between 4 and 10 years; diabetes mellitus; obesity; chronic medical conditions (such as cardiovascular diseases, nephropathies, chronic liver and respiratory diseases); and conditions associated with an increased risk of bleeding.13 Exclusion criteria were vaccine contraindications (such as age below 4 years, pregnancy, breastfeeding, or immunosuppression precluding live vaccines) and inability to complete follow-up.

A target sample size of 1000 participants was set based on expected clinic throughput across participating sites during the prespecified recruitment period. With 1000 participants, the probability of observing at least one AE was >99% for AEs with a true frequency of ≥1/100 (common AEs), 86% for 1/500, and 63% for 1/1000 (uncommon AEs).

Study workflow and data collection

At enrolment, trained healthcare workers completed a structured questionnaire capturing demographics, comorbidities, prior arboviral infections, previous flaviviral vaccinations, travel itinerary and duration, and any vaccines co-administered with TAK-003. Concomitant vaccines administered at the same visit as TAK-003 were recorded by trained healthcare professionals. Participant-reported history of prior travel-related vaccinations was routinely verified against a regional registry used by primary care centres and travel clinics.

Participants were contacted by telephone 7 days after each vaccine dose to record AEs occurring within the first 7 days after vaccination. AEs occurring more than 7 days after vaccination were actively solicited at two additional timepoints: (i) three months after the first dose, coinciding with the second-dose visit; and (ii) 14 days after return from travel, to capture events occurring >7 days after the second dose. All follow-up contacts were conducted by travel medicine healthcare professionals using a standardised case report form. Participants were asked to report symptom onset date and whether symptoms occurred before departure, during travel, or after return to support temporal classification. Participants were offered an in-person assessment when clinically indicated.

Post-travel follow-up was conducted via telephone starting 14 days after the participant's return date. Participants were contacted at least three times; those who could not be reached were classified as lost to follow-up for post-travel outcomes. At post-travel follow-up, participants were asked about dengue-like symptoms during travel or after return; those reporting fever and/or rash within the preceding 14 days were invited for clinical evaluation and virological testing by PCR and/or NS1 antigen testing.

Outcomes and definitions

The primary outcome was the occurrence of AEs, described by dose, type, and severity. Local AEs were predefined as injection-site pain, swelling, erythema, and pruritus. Systemic AEs included fever, headache, myalgia/arthralgia, malaise, asthenia, rash, and retro-orbital pain. Allergic AEs included anaphylaxis, urticaria, and angio-oedema. AE severity was classified according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) and graded as mild, moderate, or severe by investigators using a standardised case-report form.

Secondary outcomes included the estimation of odds ratios (OR) for potential factors associated with AEs such as age, sex, comorbidities, previous DENV infection, other prior flaviviral infection or vaccination history, and concomitant vaccination. Prior flaviviral infection history included any self-reported previous non-DENV flaviviral infection, with or without serological confirmation. Prior flaviviral vaccination history included previous vaccination with yellow fever vaccine (YFV), Japanese encephalitis vaccine (JEV), or tick-borne encephalitis vaccine (TBEV). Additional exploratory outcomes included the occurrence of dengue-like symptoms reported during or within 14 days after travel and, when available, the incidence of virologically confirmed DENV infections.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as counts (%) and continuous variables as medians (IQR). The primary safety outcome was participant-level AE reporting (≥1 AE), summarised overall and by prespecified follow-up windows after each dose. Reporting was also summarised by AE category, noting that participants could report multiple AE types. For descriptive safety summaries, N denotes participants with a completed follow-up assessment in the relevant analysis window and n denotes participants reporting ≥1 AE in that category; 95% confidence intervals (CI) for proportions were calculated using Wilson's score method. Unadjusted comparisons of proportions used χ2 tests. Descriptive safety summaries were additionally provided for participants with conditions of clinical relevance.

Associations between prespecified potential predictors (sex, age, comorbidity status, prior dengue and other flaviviral exposures, and concomitant vaccination) and outcomes (any, systemic, or local AEs) were estimated using bivariable and multivariable logistic regressions. For each endpoint, the dependent variable was binary and coded as positive if the participant reported ≥1 event of that AE type and negative otherwise (including participants reporting other AE types). Multivariable models included prespecified covariates; overall co-administration was not additionally included to avoid collinearity with flaviviral co-administration. Sensitivity analyses replaced self-reported dengue history with microbiologically confirmed prior dengue. For predictors consistently associated with AE reporting in primary models, joint effects were explored using logistic regressions.

For analyses of vaccine co-administration recorded per visit, the unit of analysis was the TAK-003 dose because concomitant vaccines could differ between dose 1 and dose 2. In dose-level models, participants could contribute up to two observations.

Results are reported as OR with 95% CI; two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were done in R (version 4.5.0) with RStudio.

Ethics

The protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital Clínic de Barcelona (HCB/2024/0010, 26/01/2024) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, ICH-GCP, and Spanish Royal Decree 957/2020 on observational drug studies. Written informed consent was obtained from adult participants and from legal guardians of minors; minors additionally provided age-appropriate assent. Data were anonymised and stored in a secure LimeSurvey instance hosted by Hospital Clínic de Barcelona.

Results

Participants’ baseline characteristics

Of the 1028 participants enrolled, 823 (80.1%) completed the two-dose vaccination schedule before or after travel, receiving a total of 1851 TAK-003 doses. Overall, 570 (55.4%) were female, 453 (44.1%) were male, and five (0.5%) preferred not to disclose. Median age was 40 years (IQR 29–51); 986 participants (95.9%) were adults and 137 (13.3%) were aged 60 years or older (range 60–84) (Supplementary Figure S1). Previous medical conditions were reported by 415/1028 (40.4%) participants. Most common travel destinations were in the WHO Region of the Americas (57.5%; mainly Argentina, Colombia, and Brazil), the South-East Asia Region (16.3%; particularly Thailand, Indonesia, and Sri Lanka), and the Western Pacific Region (9.1%; mainly Malaysia, the Philippines, and Viet Nam). Adverse events

Overall occurrence and timing

AE reporting was higher following the first dose than the second (470/998, 47.1% vs 235/679, 34.6%). Overall, systemic AEs were reported by 367/998 (36.8%) and local AEs by 428 (42.9%). The most common local reactions were injection site pain (351/998, 35.2%) and swelling (95, 9.5%); most systemic AEs were headache (134, 13.4%), asthenia (135, 13.5%), and malaise (101, 10.1%). Fever or chills occurred in 64 (6.4%) participants and rash in 20 (2.0%). All AEs were classified as mild or moderate; no serious AEs or deaths occurred during the study period (Table 2).

Allergic reactions were rare; no cases of angioedema or anaphylaxis were observed. Urticaria occurred in a single participant (0.1%): a 57-year-old male with no history of allergy, previous flaviviral infection or vaccination, who received the first TAK-003 dose with concomitant typhoid, hepatitis A, and tetanus–diphtheria vaccines. A generalized rash appeared four days after vaccination and resolved with no medication; the second TAK-003 dose was not administered at the participant's request.

After the first dose, 445/978 (45.5%) participants reported AEs within 7 days and 71/828 (8.6%) beyond 7 days after vaccination (Supplementary Table S1). After the second dose, AEs also occurred more commonly within 7 days (219/679; 32.3%) than beyond 7 days after vaccination (16/114; 14.0%). Local AEs predominantly occurred within the first 7 days post-vaccination, while systemic AEs were more frequently observed beyond 7 days after the second dose (Supplementary Table S1). In paired analysis of participants with AE follow-up available after both doses, reporting any AE after the first dose was associated with higher odds of reporting AEs after the second dose (OR 1.63, 95% CI 1.18–2.24; p = 0.0027). AE reporting after the first dose did not differ between participants who subsequently received the second dose (364/810, 44.9%) and those who did not attend the second dose visit (81/168, 48.2%; p = 0.49).

Elderly participants and other age groups

AEs were reported in 36 (26.3%) of the 137 participants aged 60 years or older. AE profiles were similar to the overall cohort, and no serious AEs occurred. Compared with those younger than 20 years, participants aged 20–39 years (OR 2.06, 95% CI 1.09–3.96; p = 0.028) and 40–59 years (OR 2.15, 95% CI 1.12–4.20; p = 0.023) had higher overall risk of AEs, whereas age ≥60 years was not associated with higher odds of AEs. Participants with previous dengue and other prior flaviviral exposures

Prior DENV infection was reported by 198/1028 participants (19.3%), with laboratory confirmation in 162 of them (81.8%) (Table 1). Among participants with previous DENV infection, 131/198 (66.2%) reported AEs, including 81/198 (40.9%) participants with systemic AE and 67/198 (33.8%) with local AEs.

Other previous flaviviral exposures were reported by 413 (40.2%) participants and included prior Zika virus infection (13, 1.3%) and prior flaviviral vaccination (407, 39.6%), mainly YFV (389, 37.8%), with fewer reporting prior JEV (25, 2.4%) or TBEV (14, 1.4%) (Table 1). In those with prior Zika virus infection, 11/13 (84.6%) reported AE (systemic AE occurred in 7/13, 53.8%; and local AE in 3/13, 23.1%). Among participants with a history of prior flaviviral vaccination, AEs occurred in 230/389 (59.1%) with prior YFV (systemic AE in 125/389, 32.1%); 16/25 (64.0%) with prior JEV (systemic AE in 10/25, 40.0%); and 12/14 (85.7%) with prior TBEV (systemic AE in 9/14, 64.3%).

Analyses are event-based (per dose); participants could contribute up to two observations (first and second TAK-003 dose). Single-vaccine analyses included most frequently co-administered vaccines: typhoid, hepatitis A, yellow fever, tetanus–diphtheria, rabies, and poliomyelitis. Number of observations contributing to each single-vaccine estimate are shown as n co-administered doses (any/systemic/local adverse events): typhoid 183 (82/37/52); hepatitis A 58 (20/9/14); yellow fever 100 (45/23/27); tetanus–diphtheria 20 (9/6/3); rabies 36 (17/12/8); poliomyelitis 13 (7/3/5). Dose-level associations should be interpreted as differences in short-term reactogenicity following specific vaccine co-administrations rather than vaccine-specific safety signals.

Supplementary Figure S3 illustrates the joint effect of the most common combinations of two or more concomitant vaccines. Co-administration of YFV, typhoid, and TAK-003 vaccines was associated with higher odds of any AE (OR 2.09, 95% CI 1.21–3.63; p = 0.0083) and systemic AEs (OR 2.92, 95% CI 1.59–5.21; p = 0.0004). The addition of hepatitis A vaccine to the previous combination showed the strongest association with systemic AEs (OR 4.17, 95% CI 2.07–8.22; p < 0.0001). No combination was clearly associated with local AEs.

Risk factor analysis

In bivariable analyses, female sex was associated with higher odds of reporting any AE, systemic AEs, and local AEs. Previous DENV infection was significantly associated with higher odds of reporting any AE and systemic AEs; previous Zika virus infection was associated with higher odds of reporting any AE, although estimates were imprecise because of the small number of cases (n = 13). Concomitant flaviviral vaccination was associated with higher odds of systemic AEs. Factors such as age ≥60 years, comorbidities, overall concomitant vaccination, or prior flaviviral vaccination did not increase the risk of reporting any AE (Table 3).

In multivariable models, female sex increased the overall risk of reporting any AE (adjusted OR [aOR] 1.83, 95% CI 1.42–2.37; p < 0.0001) and local AEs (aOR 1.89, 95% CI 1.44–2.48; p < 0.0001). Self-reported previous DENV infection also increased the overall risk of reporting any AE (aOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.07–2.14; p = 0.0186) and systemic AEs (aOR 1.73, 95% CI 1.22–2.43; p = 0.0017). Concomitant flaviviral vaccination was associated with higher odds of reporting systemic AEs (aOR 1.64, 95% CI 1.18–2.28; p = 0.0029). No other variables showed significant associations in adjusted analyses (Table 3). In sensitivity analyses replacing self-reported with microbiologically confirmed prior DENV infection, associations with systemic and local AE reporting held, while the association with any AE was attenuated in the multivariable model (Supplementary Table S3).

The joint effect of sex and previous DENV infection was then examined, using male participants without prior DENV infection as the reference group (Supplementary Figure S4). Female participants with prior DENV infection had the highest odds of reporting any AE (aOR 3.27, 95% CI 2.03–5.42; p < 0.0001), systemic AEs (aOR 2.28, 95% CI 1.45–3.58; p = 0.0003) and local AEs (aOR 1.83, 95% CI 1.16–2.89; p = 0.008).

Dengue-like symptoms during or after travel

Post-travel follow-up was completed for 835/1028 (81.2%) participants; 193 (18.8%) were not reached after repeated contact attempts and were considered lost to follow-up. A total of 721/835 (86.3%) individuals had received only one TAK-003 dose before departure. Twenty (1.9%) reported dengue-like symptoms during travel or within 14 days of return. None of the five participants tested with direct diagnostic methods (DENV PCR or NS1) had confirmed dengue. One participant was alternatively diagnosed with Influenza virus infection. Among the remaining 14 participants with dengue-like illness, 12 (85.7%) had received only one dose before travelling rather than the full two-dose schedule.

For these 14 participants, we examined the interval from the last TAK-003 dose to dengue-like symptom onset. Ten (71.4%) could recall the date of onset, and all had received a single pre-travel dose. Symptom onset occurred a median of 2 months after vaccination (IQR 1–3 months; range 17–103 days).

Discussion

In this prospective, multicentre pharmacovigilance study of 1851 TAK-003 doses given to 1028 travellers from non-endemic regions (mainly adults), no serious AEs were observed an no anaphylaxis or deaths were reported. Although more than half of participants reported AEs, almost all were mild or moderate and self-limited, with lower AE frequency after the second dose. Female sex, prior DENV infection and co-administration of other flaviviral vaccines were associated with increased reactogenicity. By contrast, age 60 years or older, comorbidities, absence of previous DENV infection, and co-administration of non-flaviviral vaccines were not found to increase the overall AE risk. These findings align with the favourable tolerability profile observed in pre-licensure clinical trials,8 and add real-world evidence on short-term tolerability of TAK-003 in adult travellers from non-endemic regions.

The overall AE pattern was consistent with previous studies, with injection site pain, headache, asthenia, and malaise as the most frequently reported complaints.8,10 Systemic AEs were at least as common as local AEs, a finding that matches with the reactogenicity profile of live attenuated vaccines.14 AE incidence in our study was higher than in Argentinian post-marketing data, likely reflecting clinician-ascertained, active follow-up instead of passive/self-reported surveillance.15 In our study, fever occurred in 6% of participants, a proportion notably lower than the 9% reported in clinical trials in endemic settings and 20% self-reported in post-marketing studies from non-endemic regions.10 Rash occurred in 2%, also below the 11% observed in prior travellers’ reports. The lower incidences observed in our study likely reflect clinician-led AE assessment, which helps avoid overestimation from subjective self-reporting, although delayed-onset symptoms may have been underreported due to the recall bias inherent in retrospective data collection beyond 7 days post-vaccination.

AEs were more common after the first than the second dose, mirroring observations from previous clinical trials and experiences with other live-attenuated vaccines.8,10,15,16 This might reflect immunological attenuation of reactogenicity after priming and, potentially, selection effects if individuals with stronger reactions after dose 1 were less likely to return for dose 2. However, AE reporting after dose 1 was similar among participants who received the second dose and those who did not (44.9% vs 48.2%), suggesting limited evidence of differential drop-out by early reactogenicity in this cohort. Most AEs occurred within seven days of vaccination, but systemic reactions after the second dose tended to occur later. However, in our study, events beyond seven days after the second dose were partly ascertained retrospectively and might therefore be subject to recall bias and misclassification of travel-related illness.

Our study did not identify increased short-term reactogenicity in older adults or participants with comorbidities after TAK-003 vaccination. This finding is particularly relevant given the growing number of elderly travellers and individuals with comorbidities among European travellers, both groups at higher risk of severe dengue.17,18 Biologically, immunosenescence and age-related reductions in inflammatory responses might reduce short-term reactogenicity. Additionally, travel clinic attendees aged ≥60 years may represent a healthier, self-selected subgroup, and reporting behaviour or risk-mitigation counselling could differ by age. While immunosenescence may be potentially beneficial for tolerability, the implications for immunogenicity and long-term protection remain to be determined. TAK-003 clinical trials have primarily enrolled children and adolescents in endemic regions, with limited participation of older adults and exclusion of individuals over 60 years of age so far.8 WHO therefore recommended an upper age limit of 60 years for vaccination pending further evidence.19 Taken together with emerging post-marketing studies in non-endemic settings,10,15 our findings provide real-world evidence on tolerability of TAK-003 in adult and elderly patients. Although the study was not powered to exclude rare serious AEs, and did not assess long-term vaccine safety or immunogenicity, our data help address an important evidence gap by characterising short-term tolerability in older travellers, and participants with comorbidities (40% in our study).

Consistent with previous studies, female sex was associated with a higher risk of overall and local AEs.8,10,15 This observation has been reported with other live-attenuated and inactivated vaccines, and may reflect sex-based biological differences in innate and adaptive immunity, as well as social and psychological factors influencing pain perception and reporting.20, 21, 22, 23 Acknowledging these differences is essential to ensure accurate risk communication and personalised counselling prior to immunisation. Future trials should incorporate sex-disaggregated analyses to better characterise these effects.24

In our study, participants with previous DENV infection showed a higher incidence of systemic AEs. These findings were not consistently observed in trials from endemic regions,8 where previous DENV infections were defined by baseline DENV serostatus in patients with potential exposure to other flaviviruses or several dengue episodes, thereby limiting direct comparability across studies. Our findings suggest that pre-existing flaviviral immunity might modulate TAK-003 reactogenicity. Cross-reactive antibodies from certain prior flaviviral infections or vaccinations may contribute to enhanced responses or cross-protection against subsequent DENV infections,25,26 pointing to the importance of considering prior flavivirus exposure and vaccination history when interpreting immune and safety signals of live-attenuated dengue vaccines.27, 28, 29, 30 By contrast, post-hoc analysis of TAK-003 clinical trials did not identify a clinically relevant impact of prior JEV or YFV on TAK-003 performance.29 Further, immunological studies have suggested that previous Zika virus infection may trigger antibody-dependent enhancement in subsequent DENV infections.31, 32, 33 However, the impact of previous Zika in dengue vaccine studies has not been evaluated.8 In our study, previous Zika virus infection was associated with increased odds of AEs in bivariable analysis, although this relationship was not sustained in multivariable models, and results should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size. Future studies evaluating cross-immunity among different flaviviruses are crucial to clarify potential enhancement or cross-protection with vaccines and/or subsequent natural infections.

A key strength of this study is the evaluation of concomitant vaccination, a common scenario in travel medicine consultations but rarely investigated in dengue vaccine trials. Only two trials evaluated the co-administration of TAK-003 with hepatitis A and YFV.34,35 In our cohort, co-administration of TAK-003 with other vaccines occurred in two thirds of participants (a higher proportion than the 28% reported by Kopke et al.10 and the 3.8% by Castellano et al.15), reflecting our common clinical practice. The most frequent combinations included typhoid, hepatitis A, YFV, tetanus–diphtheria, rabies, and poliomyelitis vaccines. Globally, co-administration of vaccines was not associated with increased reactogenicity. These findings align with recent German and Argentinian post-marketing data and support the practical integration of TAK-003 within routine travel vaccination schedules.10,15 However, vaccine-specific analyses suggested higher AE reporting with typhoid and rabies vaccination. These associations likely reflect expected short-term reactogenicity under co-administration rather than new safety concerns for TAK-003 or co-administered vaccines. This is especially relevant for concomitant live-attenuated vaccines such as YFV, given its established reactogenicity.36

WHO recommended monitoring the co-administration of TAK-003 with flavivirus and other live-attenuated vaccines.19 In our study, co-administration of TAK-003 with other flaviviral vaccines were associated with increased overall and systemic reactogenicity. Particularly, concomitant YFV was associated with higher AE reporting. These findings contrast with a previous trial, in which 299 participants receiving both TAK-003 and YFV reported similar rates of systemic and local AEs compared to those receiving individual vaccines with placebo.34 Data on TBEV and JEV co-administration remain limited: previous reports showed high incidence (68%) of AEs with other flaviviral vaccines such as JEV and one severe case of myocarditis has been reported in a patient receiving concomitant JEV.10,37 In our study, all three TBEV co-recipients and two out of four JEV co-recipients experienced systemic AEs. Additionally, 27 patients were vaccinated with MMR vaccine concurrently with the dengue vaccine, without notable differences in AE frequency or profile.

Nearly one in five participants did not return for the second TAK-003 dose. This warrants attention because it is unknown whether receipt of a single dose could leave individuals with sub-protective antibody levels that might favour infection-enhancing responses upon subsequent DENV infection. Outside a study setting, where follow-up is less proactive, non-completion rates with second doses may be higher. Clear, personalised pre-travel counselling is essential to align vaccination recommendations with traveller preferences, strengthen health literacy, and facilitate completion of the full vaccination schedule.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, serological confirmation of prior DENV infection was not available in all participants, with potential misclassification of asymptomatic past infection, although this is less concerning in travellers from non-endemic regions.38, 39, 40 Further, DENV serotype testing is not routinely performed across centres; the lack of serotype information on previous DENV infections prevented analyses on differential reactogenicity by prior infecting serotype. Second, statistical power was limited for rare AE detection and subgroup analyses, which will be better characterised through large post-marketing surveillance datasets41 and future meta-analyses. Third, although the study was not primarily designed to assess vaccine efficacy, only five out of twenty participants with dengue-like symptoms underwent direct diagnostic testing with PCR and/or NS1, and no virologically confirmed dengue cases were identified. However, interpretation of dengue diagnostics in vaccinated travellers warrants caution, as NS1 antigen detection may be less sensitive after vaccination, and PCR positivity should be interpreted carefully to differentiate between natural infection and vaccine-induced viraemia.42 Fourth, events reported beyond 7 days were partially ascertained retrospectively, introducing the potential for recall bias. Finally, late AEs may have been missed beyond our study windows, especially those potentially associated with waning antibody titres and subsequent breakthrough DENV infections after vaccination. Nonetheless, the prospective, multicentre design, clinician-verified AEs, and the real-world, non-endemic travel setting strengthen internal validity and generalisability of our results.

In conclusion, TAK-003 showed a favourable short-term tolerability profile in travellers from non-endemic regions. Age ≥60 years, comorbidities, absence of previous DENV infection, and concomitant vaccination with non-flaviviral vaccines were not associated with increased AE risk, whereas female sex, prior DENV infection, and flaviviral co-administration were associated with higher reactogenicity. The potential influence of prior flaviviral infection (including dengue) and previous or concomitant administration of other flaviviral vaccines on TAK-003 reactogenicity and immunogenicity require further study. These results provide timely, real-world evidence on the short-term safety of TAK-003 in European travellers and help fill a key post-marketing evidence gap. Continued pharmacovigilance, alongside immunogenicity and effectiveness studies with longer follow-up, are needed to define both long-term safety and public health impact.
