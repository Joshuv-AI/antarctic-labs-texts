The unresolved question of optimal distalization and lateralization in reverse total shoulder arthroplasty
Authors: Hyungsuk Kim, Hyun Seok Song
Published: 2026

Griff et al. [1] analyze whether the degree of distalization (distalization shoulder angle) and lateralization (lateralization shoulder angle) [2] of the humeral implant after reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, as well as the distalization and lateralization index [3] that combines these two measurements, influence the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, a patient-reported outcome measure. A strength of the study by Griff et al. [1] is its analysis of the association between changes in each measurement from before to after surgery and functional outcomes. The authors concluded that the degree of distalization and lateralization did not significantly affect the ASES score, which is consistent with the conclusion reported by Boutsiadis et al. [2] Although not statistically significant, both lateralization index and glenohumeral offset were associated with higher ASES scores on multivariate analysis.

Greater distalization is known to be beneficial in patients with elevation weakness, such as pseudoparesis [4]. The use of lateralized implants has recently increased, and several advantages over the traditional Grammont design have been reported [5]. While some studies have reported better rotational outcomes with lateralized implants, Berthold et al. [6] and Kijewski et al. [7] reported that external rotation was not correlated with the lateralization shoulder angle. It is also important to note that the present study did not analyze each individual measurement in relation to each range of motion (ROM) parameter. The degree of lateralization and distalization differs according to the design concept of each implant, and the resulting humeral position varies depending on the combination of these parameters. In addition, the influence of each measurement may vary depending on the center of rotation.

Some studies have suggested that the selection of a lateralized implant should be tailored to the individual patient's preoperative condition [8]. However, the present study did not demonstrate statistically significant differences, and given that the difference in glenohumeral offset reported by the authors was approximately 1 mm, the issue of measurement error must be considered when applying these findings in clinical practice. Therefore, when interpreting the results of the study by Griff et al. [1], it should be noted that the study did not analyze outcomes according to implant type or the preoperative status of the remaining rotator cuff.

A strength of this study is that it analyzed differences in ASES scores according to the degree of distalization and lateralization resulting from surgical technique and implant size using the same implant. However, further studies are needed to analyze differences in ASES scores according to the degree of distalization and lateralization across various implant designs, as well as differences in outcomes according to patients' preoperative status. Such studies would enable implant design selection tailored to individual patients based on their preoperative condition and the desired postoperative outcomes (pain relief, ROM, strength, etc.).
