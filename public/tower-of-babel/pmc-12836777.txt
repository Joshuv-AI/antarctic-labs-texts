My Heart, My Self: an intensive longitudinal study examining the influence of intersectional discrimination on blood pressure
Authors: Yashika Sharma, JoJo Castellanos, Niurka Suero-Tejeda, Walter Bockting, Ian M. Kronish, Joseph E. Schwartz, Melanie M. Wall, Billy A. Caceres

Background

Discrimination is a significant public health concern in the United States [1–3] that is recognized as a fundamental cause of health disparities [2, 4]. Stigmatized adults in the United States experience significant hypertension disparities that may be influenced by greater exposure to discrimination. Approximately 50% adults in the United States meet criteria for hypertension (defined as an in-office systolic BP [SBP] ≥ 130 mmHg or a diastolic BP [DBP] ≥ 80 mmHg) [5]. Hypertension is the most important modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD; e.g., heart attack, coronary artery disease, stroke); [6, 7] the leading cause of death and disability among adults worldwide [8]. A prospective cohort study of over 155,000 adults in 21 countries found that hypertension alone accounts for 22% of CVD risk [9]. It is well-documented that Black adults as well as some Latinx and Asian subgroups in the United States have a higher prevalence of hypertension than their White counterparts [8, 10]. Further, there is growing evidence that sexual minority (e.g., gay/lesbian, bisexual) adults are at higher risk of hypertension than their heterosexual counterparts [11–14].

Although discrimination is posited to contribute to hypertension disparities, researchers have largely focused on the role of experienced discrimination with limited examination of other types of discrimination [15, 16]. Recent studies indicate that 56–76% of racial, ethnic, and sexual minority adults in the United States report experiences of discrimination in their lifetimes [17–20]. Social attitudes and policies place stigmatized people in the United States at increased risk of anticipated (i.e., expectations of encountering discrimination) and vicarious discrimination (i.e., indirectly experienced such as hearing or witnessing discrimination that occurred to family, friends, or a stranger)—even in the absence of personally experienced discrimination. In 2021, 14–32% of people of color in the United States reported they regularly worried about being threatened or attacked due to their race or ethnicity [21]. Between 71 and 80% of people of color fear being victims of a hate crime [22]. There is growing evidence that anticipated and vicarious discrimination are more common stressors that are more strongly associated with negative health outcomes (e.g., sleep problems, depressive symptoms) than experienced discrimination [23–27]. However, the influence of anticipated and vicarious discrimination on BP has not been comprehensively assessed. Even though these different forms of discrimination are interrelated, prior work has found that anticipated and vicarious discrimination are only moderately correlated with experienced discrimination, suggesting that these are distinct constructs [23, 26, 28].

Weaknesses in prior research on the influence of discrimination on health outcomes have impeded progress in the field. First, researchers have largely focused on experienced discrimination to the exclusion of other forms of discrimination that have been shown to be more persistent stressors in everyday life [15, 16, 29]. Second, most research has focused on discrimination due to a single stigmatized identity (e.g., race, sexual identity) [15, 16, 29]. This current approach ignores intersectional discrimination, which obscures the experiences of individuals who face discrimination due to multiple stigmatized identities (e.g., Black lesbian women) [30]. Therefore, it is theorized that researchers have underestimated the scope and impact of discrimination on CVD risk.

My Heart, My Pride is guided by a combined model adapted from intersectionality and prior work that describes mechanisms by which discrimination influences the health of stigmatized groups (Fig. 1) [15]. Intersectionality is a theoretical framework that is grounded in Black feminist theory [31, 32], which has gained increased attention within health research as an important approach for more comprehensively understanding and addressing health inequities among multiply stigmatized populations (e.g., sexual minority people of color) [33, 34]. Intersectionality explicates how an individual’s social position is shaped by intersecting systems of power and oppression, which produce social and health inequities [31, 32]. We theorize that exposure to intersectional discrimination increases CVD risk above the contribution of established CVD risk factors (e.g., age, obesity) and general life stressors (e.g., financial strain, work stress). Although intersectional anticipated, vicarious, and experienced discrimination are separate constructs, it is recognized that they can mutually influence one another. Exposure to both daily and chronic forms of intersectional discrimination is hypothesized to influence BP through mediated physiological, behavioral, and affective pathways. Further, several factors (e.g., social support, adverse childhood experiences) are posited to moderate the influence of intersectional discrimination on BP.

To our knowledge, only one study has examined the associations of anticipated discrimination on BP [35], and no studies on the link between vicarious discrimination and BP have been published. In a small laboratory reactivity study of 54 Latina college students, Sawyer et al. [35]. found that those who were told that their White interaction partner held ethnic biases had greater concurrent elevations in BP following a mental stress test. However, they only examined the effect of anticipated ethnic discrimination on BP and did not assess experienced discrimination [35]. There is also mixed evidence about the ecological validity (i.e., generalizability to real-world contexts) of laboratory-based studies relative to those conducted in everyday life. Further, researchers have primarily relied on non-validated measures of vicarious discrimination often created for a specific study [27, 36].

Home BP monitoring (HBPM) is a reliable method for assessing out-of-office BP that requires individuals to measure their own BP at home, twice per day across several days using an automated BP device [37, 38]. Home BP is more strongly associated with CVD events and mortality than in-office BP [37, 39, 40]. Because HBPM is conducted in an individual’s real-world environment, it is also superior to in-office BP for assessing daily variations in BP in response to daily stressors [41]. HBPM is better tolerated by patients than other out-of-office BP measures, such as 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring (i.e., automated BP measurement every 20–30 min while awake and asleep). Home BP is strongly correlated with ambulatory BP (r =.74-0.89) [37, 42] and is more feasible for multi-day studies like ours because of reduced participant burden [43].

This study examines the influence of daily and chronic forms of intersectional discrimination on BP in a diverse sample with the goal of providing the groundwork for future interventions. This study is the first to assess physiological, behavioral, and affective mediators that could serve as intermediate targets for interventions to decrease the impact of anticipated and vicarious discrimination on CVD risk. My Heart, My Self addresses limitations of prior work by examining the influence of intersectional anticipated and vicarious discrimination on BP in adults within their real-world contexts. Daily diary studies are ideal for assessing the influence of discrimination on health outcomes in real time. Daily diary studies capture real-time assessments of experiences using active and passive methods that yield data that are less susceptible to recall and self-report bias and more representative of a participant’s everyday life than global retrospective assessments. To address gaps in knowledge, our specific aims and hypotheses are:

Aim 1

To determine the influence of intersectional anticipated and vicarious discrimination on home BP and mediators of these associations. We hypothesize that: 1a) After controlling for experienced discrimination, higher daily and chronic anticipated and vicarious discrimination will be independently associated with higher daily home BP, both within- and between-persons; and 1b) Physiological (primary mediators), behavioral, and affective factors will mediate the associations of daily and chronic anticipated and vicarious discrimination with home BP.

Aim 2

To examine differences in intersectional anticipated and vicarious discrimination and home SBP across stigmatized identities (e.g., ethnoracial, sexual identity) and the intersections between them. We hypothesize that: 2a) Participants who attribute discrimination exposure due to more stigmatized identities will report greater daily and chronic anticipated and vicarious discrimination; and 2b) The influence of daily and chronic anticipated and vicarious discrimination on daily home BP will be greater among those who report more stigmatized identities.

Exploratory aim

To explore moderators (e.g., social support, socioeconomic disadvantage) of the influence of intersectional anticipated and vicarious discrimination on home BP.

Methods and study design

Project overview

My Heart, My Self is a 7-day intensive longitudinal study that includes daily diary assessments, HBPM, actigraphy, and assessments of salivary stress biomarkers to test the independent associations of anticipated and vicarious discrimination with home BP. Our sample will include 400 adults (ages 18–64 years) who are diverse in terms of race, ethnicity, sexual identity, and gender. We chose to focus on these 4 stigmatized identities as they were the most common reasons for discrimination reported in our team’s prior daily diary studies with ethnoracial and sexual minority adults (sexual identity = 72%, gender = 68%, ethnicity = 51%, and race = 42%) [44]. We will also test mediators and moderators of hypothesized associations between anticipated and vicarious discrimination with BP. This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Columbia University Irving Medical Center (CUIMC).

Sample

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We will recruit English-speaking adults (ages 18–64) living in New York City (NYC) who have no prior diagnosis of primary or secondary hypertension or CVD (e.g., heart attack, stroke). The sample will be recruited to ensure substantial proportions of intersecting identities across gender, ethnoracial, and sexual identities. Eligible individuals must also have an in-office BP < 160/<100 mmHg during BP screening at Visit 1. The sample is restricted to ages 18–64 because: (1) hypertension prevalence in adults > age 65 is ~ 75% [5]; (2) targeting adults ages 18–64 will allow us to better characterize the associations of intersectional anticipated and vicarious discrimination with BP by reducing confounding from preclinical CVD (e.g., left ventricular hypertrophy, atherosclerosis) that increases considerably in late life; and (3) targeting this age range will help us identify individuals who may be at greatest risk for future CVD in early and middle adulthood, thus providing important knowledge for interventions to reduce the risk of developing hypertension and CVD prior to late life. Moreover, individuals with cognitive impairment, those taking antihypertensive or other medications that can affect BP, those with conditions that confound salivary biomarker results (e.g., thyroid disease, radiation of salivary glands) [45], and those who are pregnant will be excluded.

Recruitment and enrollment

Participants are recruited using the services of a market research company that has over 2 million adults in NYC in their database. The market sample is compiled from several public and proprietary sources that include memberships and purchase histories. Recruitment flyers that describe the purpose of the study and include contact information for study staff are sent via regular mail. Since the database contains demographic data, we can send targeted mailings to prioritize enrollment of female, ethnoracial, and sexual minority individuals. Given the study goals, we prioritize enrollment of eligible individuals from traditionally stigmatized groups to achieve a sample that is 50% female-identified, 60% racial minority, 50% sexual minority, and 30% Latinx. We will stratify database records by gender, ethnoracial, and sexual identity and then randomly select individuals to contact from each of these strata.

Screening

Potential participants complete screening procedures in one of two ways. First, the recruitment flyer includes a QR code to our online screener that can be completed by potential participants on their own via REDCap. Second, if potential participants prefer, they can contact our research team who administers the screener as part of a 10-minute telephone screen. Regardless of the method used, the screener assesses individuals’ age, biological sex, race, ethnicity, sexual identity, gender identity, medical history, use of antihypertensive medications, and cognitive function [46]. If interested, eligible individuals are invited to schedule Visit 1 in person.

Procedures

Our study protocol is summarized in Fig. 2. Participation in the study includes two in-person visits and a 7-day home assessment.

Study protocol

Visit 1

At Visit 1, participants complete an in-person structured interview, office BP measurement, assessment of height and weight, and training on the study protocol. Research staff escort participants to a private room at the Columbia University School of Nursing. After obtaining written informed consent, research staff assesses BP using established clinical guidelines [38]. After sitting quietly for 5 min, 3 BP readings are taken on the non-dominant arm at least 1 min apart using a validated automatic BP monitor (Omron BP5350) [38]. Those with an average BP ≥ 160/≥100 mmHg are informed that they are ineligible to participate, referred for a follow-up clinical assessment, and are compensated $30 for Visit 1.

Those deemed eligible complete additional components of Visit 1. Before administering the structured interview, we assess participants’ height and weight to calculate body mass index (BMI; kg/m2). Height is measured barefoot using a portable calibrated stadiometer (Seca 213). Weight is assessed using a calibrated digital scale (Tanita 684 W) with participants barefoot and in light clothing.

Home assessment

Starting the morning after Visit 1, participants complete 7 days of: (1) daily structured morning diaries, (2) daily structured evening diaries, (3) self-collection of saliva samples, (4) continuous wrist-worn actigraphy, and (5) HBPM. Home BP was selected for the present study as it is the most appropriate and feasible BP protocol for assessing BP across multiple days.

Participants receive text messages at four time points each day (awakening, 30 min after awakening, before dinner time, and before going to sleep) for the duration of the study prompting them to complete diary assessments. Text messages are sent using Mosio, a third-party application directly embedded within CUIMC’s REDCap system. The awakening text message reminds participants to complete the morning diary, collect the first saliva sample of the day, and measure their BP upon awakening. Text messages include a REDCap link to complete a brief morning diary that asks about daily anticipated discrimination as well as self-reported sleep onset and wake-up times for the previous night. Each morning, participants also measure their BP using an Omron BP5350 upper arm monitor with cuff, a validated FDA-approved device following the American Heart Association’s HBPM clinical guidelines [38]. The device can store up to 80 BP readings, which is sufficient storage for all expected participant BP readings over 7 days (total = 28 readings). Participants take their BP twice (1 min apart) on the non-dominant arm, unless medically contraindicated, while seated [38]. The text messages sent 30 min after the participants’ self-reported awakening and dinner times remind participants to collect the second and third saliva samples of the day.

In the evening, they receive a similar text message reminding them to collect a saliva sample and measure their BP before going to bed (using the same HBPM protocol as morning readings) [38]. The text message includes a REDCap link to the evening diary. The evening diary assesses daily vicarious and experienced discrimination, daily general life stressors, affective factors, and tobacco use. Participants who do not complete their morning or evening diary assessments within 1 h automatically receive a reminder text message.

BP readings are stored on Sphygmo, a third-party application that allows for instantaneous transmission of BP data to the research team. The Sphygmo platform implements Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant data security procedures from data collection to server storage and viewing. After a participant transmits their BP data into the Sphygmo application, data are stored locally on the smartphone using 256-bit advanced encryption standard protocol and then transmitted to the participant’s Sphygmo account.

Visit 2

At Visit 2, participants complete an exit interview and return saliva samples and the BP and actigraphy devices to research staff. Participants are compensated up to $175 for completing all components of the study.

Measures

Structured interview

Data from structured interviews completed at Visit 1 are entered into REDCap by research staff using a tablet or laptop computer. Structured interviews are used to assess data on chronic intersectional discrimination, potential moderators, demographic factors, clinical factors, personality, and chronic general life stressors.

We use a two-stage approach for all discrimination measures following validated methods. First, participants are asked about the frequency of chronic anticipated, vicarious, and experienced discrimination without attribution to a specific identity alone (e.g., race only). Next, for every endorsed item of the chronic anticipated, vicarious, and experienced discrimination measures, they are asked a follow-up question to assess the main identities they attributed the experience to (e.g., race, sexual identity, gender). Participants can endorse more than one reason for discrimination per item. Self-reported attribution data will be used in Aim 2 analyses to operationalize stigmatized identities.

Chronic anticipated discrimination is assessed with the 9-item Intersectional Anticipated Discrimination Index [30]. Participants are asked to rate how strongly they agree that they encountered 9 types of anticipated discrimination (e.g., worry about being harassed or stopped by police or security; expect to be pointed at, called names, or harassed in public) [30]. Responses include: 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Mean scores (range 0–4) will be calculated with higher scores indicating greater chronic anticipated discrimination.

Chronic vicarious discrimination is measured with a validated 7-item scale that has been adapted to assess intersectional forms of vicarious discrimination [24, 25]. Participants are asked to rate the frequency with which they heard about or witnessed 7 types of vicarious discrimination (e.g., happened to friends and family, heard from public figure[s]) on a 5-point scale (0 = never, 5 = every day) [24, 25]. Mean scores (range 0–5) will be calculated with higher scores indicating greater chronic vicarious discrimination.

Chronic experienced discrimination is measured with the original 9-item version of the Everyday Discrimination Scale to assess the frequency in which participants have experienced 9 types of experienced discrimination (e.g., threatened or harassed; called names or insulted) [47]. Participants are asked, “In your day-to-day life, how often do any of the following things happen to you?” Responses range from 1 (never) to 6 (almost every day). Scores will be summed (range 1–54) with higher scores indicating greater chronic experienced discrimination.

Moderators are assessed using validated instruments. Social support is measured with a 12-item measure of multidimensional social support (continuous; range 12–84) [48]. Identity centrality is assessed with the 8-item collective identity sub-scale of the Aspects of Identity Questionnaire IV (continuous; range 8–45) [49]. This sub-scale assesses the subjective importance of the social identities to which one belongs (e.g., race, sexual identity, religion, nationality) to their overall identity [49]. Socioeconomic status is assessed with a comprehensive measure of socioeconomic disadvantage that has been associated with psychological and physiological stress [50–52]. It includes 3 indicators of childhood socioeconomic disadvantage (e.g., parental education) and 5 indicators of adulthood socioeconomic disadvantage (e.g., education, income to poverty ratio) [50]. Scores are summed to create a measure of cumulative socioeconomic disadvantage (continuous, range 0–26); higher scores indicate greater cumulative socioeconomic disadvantage [50]. In addition, we assess 10 types of adverse childhood experiences (e.g., childhood physical abuse, criminal behavior in household). Scores are summed with higher scores indicating greater exposure to more types of adverse childhood experiences (range 0–10).

We use validated measures to assess demographic factors, clinical factors, personality, and chronic general life stressors. Demographic and clinical factors are assessed with items from the National Institutes of Health’s PhenX toolkit [53]. Demographic factors include age, biological sex, race, ethnicity, sexual identity, and gender identity. Clinical factors include health insurance status, BMI, medical history (e.g., HIV, type 2 diabetes), and medication use (e.g., exogenous hormones, oral contraceptives). Personality factors (e.g., neuroticism, extraversion) are assessed with a widely used 10-item measure [54]. The 8-item Chronic Stress Burden Scale [55] is used to assess chronic general life stressors associated with increased CVD risk (e.g., financial concerns, caregiving stress) [56, 57].

Daily discrimination

We use valid and reliable measures, used in prior daily diary studies, to assess daily discrimination. We use a two-stage approach (described above) to assess daily anticipated, vicarious, and experienced discrimination. Daily anticipated discrimination (every morning) is assessed by asking participants 2 questions: (1) “How worried/anxious are you about being treated unfairly today?” and (2) “How likely is it that you will be treated unfairly today?” Responses to the 2 items ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely) will be multiplied to obtain a daily anticipated discrimination score (continuous; range 1–25) [44, 58–60].

To assess daily vicarious and experienced discrimination (every evening), participants are asked the same items as the chronic vicarious and experienced discrimination measures, which have been successfully adapted for daily administration in prior work [61–66]. Daily vicarious discrimination is assessed by asking “Over the course of today, how often did any of the following happen?” to assess whether they heard or witnessed 7 types of vicarious discrimination on that day. Responses include 0 (not at all) to 5 (5 or more times). A score will be determined by averaging responses to these 7 items (continuous; range 0–5) [25, 64]. To assess daily experienced discrimination, we ask participants if they experienced 9 types of experienced discrimination in the past day (yes vs. no). A count of daily experienced discrimination will be created by summing affirmative responses (range 0–9) [61–66].

Home BP

Upon receiving the BP monitor from a participant, BP data are downloaded from Sphygmo and saved as an Excel file labeled with the participant’s ID. We have written SAS code that imports these Excel files from a pre-specified directory and merges them into a single dataset, in which each BP reading is identified by participant ID, date, and time. Data can be aggregated to various levels of analysis (e.g., daily average of the a.m. readings) and easily merged with diary, saliva, actigraphy, and structured interview data.

Mediators

The selection of physiological, behavioral, and affective mediators that are examined daily was informed by the model guiding this study (Fig. 1) [15], prior research on their associations with both discrimination and BP, and the biological plausibility of their impact on BP. Salivary alpha-amylase and cortisol are non-invasive markers of sympathetic arousal and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysfunction, respectively, which are positively associated with experienced discrimination and elevations in BP [67–70]. Using salivary alpha-amylase and cortisol, we will test two physiological mechanisms that potentially link anticipated and vicarious discrimination with elevations in BP. Both salivary alpha-amylase and cortisol have distinct diurnal patterns, requiring assessment using multiple samples per day over several days [71].

Consistent with established recommendations [72, 73], participants collect saliva samples: (1) at awakening, (2) 30 min after awakening, (3) before dinner, and (4) just before bedtime. This saliva collection protocol allows accurate characterization of diurnal salivary alpha-amylase and cortisol output. Saliva collection tubes are pre-labeled with participant ID numbers and the collection day (i.e., Day 1–7). Participants are instructed to avoid brushing or flossing their teeth for at least 2 h before saliva collection and to avoid exercise, smoking, or consuming dairy, alcohol, or caffeine for 30 min prior. They are instructed to: (1) complete a diary entry via text message immediately after each sample is collected to report collection time for each sample, (2) label samples with the date and collection time, (3) send the research team a picture of saliva collection tubes at each timepoint to monitor compliance with collection times, and (4) freeze samples in their home freezer in a provided plastic bag. Saliva is collected via passive drool, which is recommended for salivary stress biomarkers [74, 75].

Participants return saliva samples at Visit 2. Samples are delivered on ice to the CUIMC Biomarkers Core Laboratory, where they are stored in a −20° Celsius freezer until ready for analysis with high-sensitivity enzyme-linked immunoabsorbent assays. We use commercially available salivary alpha-amylase and cortisol testing kits and supplies from Salimetrics (State College, PA) [74, 75].

Emerging evidence suggests that anticipated and vicarious discrimination are independently associated with behavioral (e.g., short sleep duration) and affective (e.g., anxiety and depressive symptoms) factors that influence BP [23, 25, 27, 36, 76]. Behavioral factors assessed in this study include tobacco use, sleep duration, and physical activity. Tobacco use is assessed daily in the structured evening diaries. Participants are asked 3 items that assess the number of cigarettes, cigars, or e-cigarettes used per day [63]. We collect information on both subjective and objective sleep duration (in minutes) obtained from daily morning diary reports and actigraphy, respectively, because subjective report generally overestimates sleep duration compared to actigraphy [77, 78]. To assess subjective sleep duration, participants complete the Consensus Sleep Diary daily in the structured morning diaries [79]. Objective sleep duration and physical activity data are obtained from the GENEActiv wrist-worn actigraphy device. GENEActiv has been shown to be valid and reliable for measuring sleep and physical activity (Activinsights Ltd, Kimbolton, UK) in over 800 peer-reviewed publications [80–82]. GENEActiv is a small, lightweight, waterproof, research-grade device with a storage capacity of 45 days at a sampling frequency of 10 Hz that permits capture of frequent changes in activity. GENEActiv requires no pairing with smartphones or tablets and no programming by participants. It has high validity and sensitivity for detecting sleep compared to polysomnography [83–85]. Self-reported sleep duration data are complemented by objective data obtained from the GeneActiv device. To assess physical activity, we calculate the number of minutes of moderate- and vigorous-intensity activity per day obtained from the GeneActiv device.

Affective factors are assessed daily in the structured evening diaries. Depression, anxiety, and anger are assessed using 3-item versions of sub-scales of the Profile of Mood States, which have been adapted for daily administration [86]. Cronbach’s alphas for the depression, anxiety, and anger sub-scales range from 0.83–0.89 [58, 60, 86–90]. Rumination is assessed with the 5-item brooding sub-scale of the Ruminative Responses Scale. Cronbach’s alphas for this sub-scale range from 0.81–0.92 [91, 92].

Statistical analyses

Data cleaning and preliminary analyses

All available data collected from participants during in-person and the 7-day home assessments will be used regardless of completeness. When necessary, missing assessments will be modeled within the multilevel model framework under appropriate missingness assumptions and/or handled via multiple imputations via the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) package in R [93] or related programs. We will screen and correct for outliers, data entry errors, or other inconsistencies. Then, we will compute descriptive statistics and estimates of the bivariate associations among all key measures.

Pre-processing of salivary stress biomarkers

We will compute the area under the curve (AUC) for salivary alpha-amylase and cortisol for each day. AUC accounts for total output and captures both intensity and sensitivity (i.e., differences between individual saliva samples). In preliminary analyses, we will determine if the day-specific AUCs for salivary alpha-amylase and cortisol are (positively) associated with total objective awake time for that day (from actigraphy). If positively associated, we will use multilevel linear models (MLMs; random intercept, fixed slope) to model the relationship; we will then use the model estimates to generate an adjusted-AUC (i.e., what the AUC would have been if the participant’s total awake time for that day had been 16 h) for each biomarker. If there is no association between day-specific AUCs and total awake time, no adjustment will be made. Finally, we will log-transform the AUCs for each day and also average these across the 7 days.

Multilevel linear models

We will use MLMs to account for the nested nature of the data. To address our hypotheses, we will use MLMs to estimate within- and between-person effects of daily and chronic anticipated and vicarious discrimination on BP. Our primary outcome will be home SBP and our secondary outcome will be home DBP. Covariates that will be included are: daily and chronic experienced discrimination, age, biological sex, race, ethnicity, sexual identity, BMI, chronic general life stressors, and personality. Additional covariates that will be considered in sensitivity analyses will include HIV status and exogenous hormone use.

For Aim 1, we will determine the influence of intersectional anticipated and vicarious discrimination on home SBP and mediators of these associations. The exposure variables are daily anticipated and vicarious discrimination (both continuous) measured with daily diaries across 7 days and chronic anticipated and vicarious discrimination (both continuous) obtained at baseline. To test Hypothesis 1a, MLMs will be estimated to determine the within- and between-person associations of anticipated and vicarious discrimination with daily SBP while controlling for daily and chronic experienced discrimination and other covariates.

To test Hypothesis 1b, we will expand the MLMs fit in 1a to determine whether physiological, behavioral, and affective factors mediate the associations of anticipated and vicarious discrimination with home SBP, adjusted for covariates. We will conduct mediation analyses regardless of finding significant associations in Hypothesis 1a because of the likely greater power to detect mediation than the total effects of anticipated and vicarious discrimination on home SBP [94]. The mediated effects of anticipated and vicarious discrimination on home SBP through daily physiological, behavioral, and affective factors will be derived from the MLMs under the counterfactual framework [95–97]. We will use bootstrapping to obtain confidence intervals and test the significance of the mediated effects of intersectional anticipated and vicarious discrimination on home SBP [98].

For Aim 2, we will examine differences in intersectional anticipated and vicarious discrimination as well as home BP across stigmatized identities and the intersections between them. Several statistical approaches exist to address intersectionality research questions with no consensus as to which is best [99–101]. We will employ the most general, so-called “intersectional approach” and will use categorical groups formed by intersecting stigmatized identities including gender, race, ethnicity, and sexual identity [101]. We will consider them as uniquely different strata created by intersecting the categories. Because this approach leads to many different strata some potentially having small cell sizes, we will employ the Multilevel Analysis of Individual Heterogeneity and Discriminatory Accuracy (MAIHDA) method which considers individuals to be nested within strata that are themselves modeled with random intercepts and a main fixed effect for each identity [101]. For testing Hypothesis 2a where anticipated and vicarious discrimination are the outcomes predicted by intersectional strata, contrasts from the MAIHDA can be formed to test whether strata with more vs. fewer stigmatized identities report higher discrimination. For testing Hypothesis 2b which examines the influence of daily and chronic anticipated and vicarious discrimination on daily home BP for those who report more stigmatized identities, we will expand the MLMs used in Hypothesis 1a to include an interaction with the number of stigmatized identities a person endorses.

Our exploratory analyses will estimate alternative models of moderation to separately explore factors that may moderate the associations of anticipated and vicarious discrimination with SBP. For instance, we will extend the models described above to include interactions (cross-products) between each form of discrimination and the hypothesized moderators (e.g., social support, socioeconomic disadvantage). A significant negative interaction, for instance, will suggest that the influence of daily anticipated discrimination on SBP is weaker (or smaller) for those with greater social support compared to those with less social support.

Sample size calculation

We plan to enroll 400 adults with an expectation of capturing adequate sample sizes across various diverse social identities. For Aim 1, examining the effects of anticipated and vicarious discrimination on BP across the entire sample we will have > 80% power to detect rp=0.15 (small to medium) for a 2-tailed, α = 0.05 test. For Aim 2, we expect varying sizes for each stratum formed by intersecting stigmatized identities. The power to detect effects of stigmatized identities on report of discrimination and their interactive effect on BP varies by sample size of the different strata. With targeted recruitment across stigmatized groups, we can expect ~ 90% of the sample will have more than one stigmatized identity, with half the sample having two or three. Hence we expect to be able to detect differences of Cohen’s d = 0.52 (medium) with 80% power using 2-sided alpha = 0.05 tests between those who report more vs. fewer stigmatized identities.

Discussion

Despite recognition that discrimination is an important social determinant of cardiovascular health, prior research on the impact of discrimination on BP has major limitations. My Heart, My Self addresses significant limitations in prior research on the link between discrimination and health, including: (1) A focus on primarily experienced discrimination to the exclusion of other forms of discrimination; (2) Limited examination of mediators and moderators of the associations between discrimination and BP; and (3) Lack of attention to intersectional discrimination. This study is expected to have a major impact on existing paradigms guiding research on the cardiovascular effects of discrimination. If our hypotheses are supported, our findings may for the first time unveil the insidious effects of indirect forms of discrimination on BP and CVD risk, even in the absence of personally experienced discrimination.

Findings will have important implications for intervention research targeting anticipated and vicarious discrimination as CVD risk factors. Multiple reviews, including our prior work [102–104], have found that behavioral interventions for CVD risk reduction in stigmatized adults have limited long-term effectiveness, but they also have neglected the role of discrimination as a social determinant. Efforts to reduce experienced discrimination are important; however, in the absence of sweeping changes in social attitudes and policies, there is a need for interventions to reduce anticipated and vicarious discrimination among stigmatized groups.

There are several psychosocial interventions (e.g., peer counseling, cognitive-behavioral therapy) to reduce anticipated discrimination among stigmatized adults [105–107]. However, these have focused primarily on mental health and HIV outcomes [105–107]. Although those outcomes co-occur with CVD, we hypothesize that interventions to improve the cardiovascular health of stigmatized adults must integrate components of evidence-based CVD risk and discrimination-related stress reduction interventions to be most effective. If we find that anticipated and vicarious discrimination are associated with BP after controlling for exposure to experienced discrimination, it will change how we think about discrimination and its cardiovascular consequences because stigmatized people continue to live in environments where these forms of discrimination persist—even in the absence of personally experienced discriminatory events. This could, in turn, fundamentally change how we design behavioral interventions to reduce CVD risk among groups that report high exposure to discrimination.

Our intersectional analyses will provide the first evidence of the influence of intersectional anticipated and vicarious discrimination on BP among stigmatized adults. We will identify how exposure to intersectional discrimination influences reports of anticipated and vicarious discrimination and BP. We also expect to identify which stigmatized groups may be at the highest risk of anticipated and vicarious discrimination and increases in BP. Therefore, our findings will inform future interventions tailored to individuals at the highest risk of the negative cardiovascular effects of anticipated and vicarious discrimination.

This is also one of the first studies to simultaneously examine the influence of daily and chronic discrimination on BP. If we find that daily anticipated and vicarious discrimination are more strongly associated with BP than chronic forms of these, this knowledge can guide intervention development. Just-in-time adaptive interventions provide tailored support delivered in real time (e.g., using mobile technologies) to address one’s changing needs and contextual state when they are most needed [108–110]. There are no just-in-time adaptive interventions focused on reducing the impact of discrimination on health. Our study will be the first to link reports of daily anticipated and vicarious discrimination with real-time BP assessment and physiological, behavioral, and affective mechanisms driving these associations. We expect to identify modifiable mechanistic targets (e.g., rumination, sleep duration) for interventions to reduce the adverse effects of anticipated and vicarious discrimination on home BP in adults. Findings could support the development of just-in-time adaptive interventions for delivery in real time to reduce CVD risk in individuals experiencing high daily anticipated or vicarious discrimination.

Conclusion

Discrimination remains a pressing public health concern that has a considerable impact on the everyday lives of stigmatized people in the United States. Hypertension disparities among stigmatized individuals persist and are influenced by greater exposure to discrimination. My Heart, My Self addresses and moves beyond significant limitations of prior research examining the influence of discrimination on BP. Our study design will allow us to identify which forms of chronic and daily discrimination are most strongly associated with BP. In particular, findings could inform just-in-time adaptive interventions focused on reducing anticipated and vicarious discrimination among stigmatized adults. Further, we expect that this study will identify modifiable mechanistic targets for interventions to reduce the adverse effects of anticipated and vicarious discrimination on BP among stigmatized adults. Using an intersectional approach will allow us to identify stigmatized groups that are at greatest risk for the negative cardiovascular effects of discrimination. Findings from our intersectional analysis will inform future interventions tailored to individuals at the highest risk of negative cardiovascular effects.
