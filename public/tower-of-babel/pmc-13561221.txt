Intra-abdominal infections caused by Achromobacter xylosoxidans: a systematic review
Authors: Mrudula Thiriveedi, Hersh Patel, Kalim Parshot, Sujatha Baddam, Siddharth Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Achromobacter xylosoxidans is an emerging cause of intra-abdominal infections, typically after healthcare exposure or in patients with indwelling devices. Intrinsic multidrug resistance and biofilm formation impede empiric therapy and often necessitate source control (catheter/stent exchange or removal). Because guidance is scattered across case reports, we systematically reviewed published episodes to quantify healthcare exposure, antimicrobial selections and susceptibilities, use of source control, and outcomes.Methods:In accordance with PRISMA 2020, we searched PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar from inception to 10 October 2025 for English-language case reports/series, correspondence, and letters. We extracted demographics, presentation, exposures, microbiology, treatment, source control, and outcomes.Results:We included 32 episodes from 29 articles. The mean age was 53.9 years, and 17/32 (53.1%) were female. Healthcare exposure occurred in 30/32 (93.8%) patients, and device- or procedure-related involvement occurred in 22/32 (68.8%) patients. Abdominal pain was reported in 20/32 (62.5%) patients. All cases were culture-confirmed. Source control was performed in 12/32 (37.5%) patients. Clinical improvement occurred in 24/32 (75.0%); relapse occurred in 8/32 (25.0%), with most relapses device-associated; and death occurred in 6/32 (18.8%) cases.Conclusions:A. xylosoxidans intra-abdominal infections are uncommon but largely healthcare-associated and often device-related. Because standard empiric regimens frequently lack activity against A. xylosoxidans, clinicians should suspect it when a nonfermenting Gram-negative rod is reported or when patients fail to improve on initial therapy and promptly initiate susceptibility-guided antibiotics with timely source control to reduce relapse and mortality. Standardized reporting of susceptibilities, device management, and outcomes is needed to refine the regimen and duration choices.

Introduction

Achromobacter xylosoxidans is an aerobic, motile, non-fermenting Gram-negative bacillus belonging to the family Alcaligenaceae (formerly classified as Alcaligenes xylosoxidans) [1]. It is ubiquitously found in water and soil and has been isolated from various clinical specimens, including blood, cerebrospinal fluid, urine, respiratory secretions, stool, peritoneal fluid, and wound exudates, especially from hospitalized or immunocompromised patients. A. xylosoxidans is predominantly recognized as an opportunistic nosocomial pathogen that is associated with indwelling devices or moist environments. Historically, it has been frequently misidentified as Pseudomonas aeruginosa in clinical laboratories because of phenotypic similarities; one distinguishing feature is that A. xylosoxidans possesses peritrichous (multiple) flagella, in contrast to the single polar flagellum of Pseudomonas[1]. Although A. xylosoxidans is best known for causing bacteremia (often catheter-related) and respiratory infections (especially in patients with cystic fibrosis), it can also cause intra-abdominal infections in certain scenarios. Such cases are rare but increasingly reported, especially in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD), abdominal surgeries, or biliary interventions[3]. Patients with intra-abdominal A. xylosoxidans infections often present with signs and symptoms that are indistinguishable from those caused by more common organisms. Typical manifestations include fever, abdominal pain, localized tenderness, and signs of peritoneal inflammation or sepsis, depending on the nature and severity of the infection. Notably, A. xylosoxidans infections can range from acute, fulminant presentations (e.g., septic shock in cholangitis or peritonitis) to indolent, chronic courses (e.g., subacute abscess formation).

These infections are particularly challenging because of intrinsic multidrug resistance and biofilm formation[4]. This systematic review aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of published cases, focusing on characterizing clinical presentations and diagnostic approaches, quantifying healthcare exposure/device association, summarizing antimicrobial selections/susceptibilities, describing treatments and source-control use, and reporting outcomes to guide clinicians in managing rare but serious infections.

Materials and methods

Literature review

This review comprehensively assesses the existing literature on intra-abdominal infections caused by A. xylosoxidans. This analysis was conducted in accordance with the guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)[5] and has been reported in line with the AMSTAR (Assessing the methodological quality of systematic reviews) guidelines. The review synthesized published case reports, case series, correspondence articles, and letters describing intra-abdominal infections caused by A. xylosoxidans in adults. Because the available evidence consisted primarily of descriptive case-level literature and no comparative or interventional analyses were planned, the review was not prospectively registered. This review adheres to the 2025 TITAN Guidelines on the transparent and responsible use of artificial intelligence in scholarly writing and authorship practices[6].

We conducted a systematic search of the PubMed, Google Scholar, and Scopus databases for studies published in English. A comprehensive search strategy included the combined use of Medical Subject Headings terms “A. xylosoxidans,” “intra-abdominal infections,” “peritonitis,” “abscesses,” “cholangitis,” and “infections.” The search concepts combined organisms (Achromobacter/Alcaligenes xylosoxidans) with intra-abdominal syndromes (peritonitis, abscesses, cholangitis, pancreatitis) using Boolean operators (e.g., (Achromobacter OR Alcaligenes) AND xylosoxidans AND (peritonitis OR intra-abdominal OR abscess OR cholangitis)). The search covered from database inception through 10 October 2025; the reference lists of the included articles were manually searched.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the studies were: (1) English-language publications; (2) adults (≥18 years) with intra-abdominal infection attributed to A. xylosoxidans; (3) case reports, case series, correspondence articles, and letters to the editor; and (4) studies with culture-positive A. xylosoxidans from a sterile/target site (e.g., ascitic/peritoneal fluid, bile, abscess/pus). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) insufficient data (e.g., organism not confirmed); (2) pediatric cases; (3) duplicate studies; (4) inaccessible full texts despite efforts to obtain them; and (5) A. xylosoxidans infections without an intra-abdominal source. The reference lists of the included articles were manually screened to ensure comprehensive coverage of the available literature. Data on patient demographics (age and sex), clinical presentation, prior healthcare exposures, antimicrobial resistance patterns, therapeutic interventions, and clinical outcomes were systematically extracted.

Data extraction and statistical analysis

The preliminary title and abstract screening was independently completed by two reviewers. After the initial screening, the full-text review of the screened articles was performed by the same two reviewers. Articles that satisfied all inclusion and exclusion criteria for the full-text review were selected for the final systematic review. A third reviewer performed a full-text review of all the selected articles to eliminate any selection errors.

Quality assessment

The quality of each article was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist[34]. A score of 1 point was assigned to each of the eight separate tools, as per the checklist. Two independent reviewers reviewed each article, and any discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer.

Unit of analysis

When an article reported more than one independent case, each was treated as a separate case. When a single patient experienced a culture-proven relapse after the initial improvement, the relapse contributed to the relapse outcome but was not counted as a new index case.

Statistical approach

Given the descriptive nature of the available literature and the small number of heterogeneous cases, only descriptive statistics were used. Continuous variables were summarized as means and medians, where appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Because all included studies were case reports or small case series, formal meta-analysis and assessment of publication bias were not performed.

Results

Study selection

Database searches identified 319 records from Google Scholar, 102 from PubMed, and 31 from Scopus. After deduplication and screening of titles and abstracts, 36 records were assessed in full, and 29 articles were included in the review. Because two articles reported two and three independent cases, respectively, the final dataset comprised 32 case episodes derived from 29 articles (see PRISMA flow diagram in Fig. 1). Study characteristics and quality assessment

All included items were single-patient case reports, correspondence, or letters with adequate clinical and microbiological details (Table 1). Using the JBI case-report checklist (8 items), Reviewer A’s mean score was 7.41, Reviewer B’s mean was 7.50, and the overall mean (episode-level average of both reviewers) was 7.45 (range: 5–8). Distribution: 14/32 (43.8%) scored 8/8 by both reviewers, 10/32 (31.3%) scored 7/8, 7/32 (21.9%) had mixed scores of 7/8 or 8/7, and 1/32 (3.1%) scored 5/8. Overall, 31/32 (96.9%) episodes averaged ≥7/8, indicating moderate-to-high reporting quality.

Patient demographics, exposures, and presentation

Across 32 episodes, the mean age was 53.9 years (median 52), and 53.1% were females (17/32). Healthcare exposure was present in 30/32 (93.8%) patients, with device/procedural involvement in 22/32 (68.8%) patients. Peritoneal-dialysis peritonitis was noted in 13/32 (40.6%) patients, biliary infections in 5/32 (15.6%) (including Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography [ERCP]/stent-associated in 4/32, 12.5%), post-operative/mesh/foreign-body in 5/32 (15.6%), liver abscess was observed in 3/32 (9.4%), spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)/bacterascites in 3/32 (9.4%), pancreatic collections noted in 2/32 (6.3%), and retroperitoneal abscess in 1/32 (3.1%) patients. Common comorbidities included hypertension in 11/32 (34.4%) patients, end-stage renal disease in 11/32 (34.4%) patients, and type 2 diabetes in 6/32 (18.8%) patients. The most frequent symptom was abdominal pain 20/32 (62.5%). All cases were culture-confirmed (32/32, 100%) from sterile/target sites (blood, peritoneal/ascitic fluid, bile, or pus/abscess).

Microbiology and management

Initial empiric regimens most often included ceftazidime (9/32, 28.1%), carbapenems (6/32, 18.8%), or piperacillin/tazobactam (4/32, 12.5%), and no empiric agents were reported in 4/32 (12.5%). Definitive/active agents, when stated, most commonly included carbapenems (6/32, 18.8%), fluoroquinolones (6/32, 18.8%), piperacillin/tazobactam (5/32, 15.6%), and ceftazidime (5/32, 15.6%). Source control (catheter exchange/removal, drainage, or resection) was performed in 12/32 cases (37.5%).

Outcomes

Clinical improvement/survival occurred in 24/32 (75.0%) episodes. Deaths were reported in 6/32 (18.8%) patients, and the outcome was not clearly stated in 2/32 (6.3%) patients. Relapse of A. xylosoxidans infection was documented in ~25.0% (8/32) of cases, predominantly in device-associated infections (e.g., PD). These outcomes occurred across heterogeneous clinical phenotypes, with PD-associated peritonitis representing the most frequent syndrome, followed by biliary infections, postoperative or mesh-related abscesses, retroperitoneal abscesses, pancreatic collections, and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis/bacterial ascites in cirrhosis.

Discussion

Intra-abdominal infections due to A. xylosoxidans remain uncommon but clinically important, predominantly in patients with recent healthcare exposure or indwelling devices. These infections often present diagnostic challenges owing to their nonspecific clinical manifestations and a low index of suspicion. Moreover, resistance to commonly used empiric antibiotics compounds the complexity of treatment.

In our synthesis of 32 case episodes from 29 articles, patients were middle-aged (mean age of 53.9 years) with a slight female predominance (53.1%). Abdominal pain was the most frequent complaint (62.5%), and diagnoses were laboratory driven; all episodes were culture-confirmed from sterile sites (blood, peritoneal/ascitic fluid, bile, or pus). Outcomes varied by syndrome and host; overall clinical improvement was noted in ~75%, mortality occurred in 6/32 (18.8%) cases, relapse was observed in ~25%, and approximately 37.5% required procedural source control.

PD-associated peritonitis accounted for the largest fraction of episodes and showed a biofilm/device pattern: improvement often followed appropriate antibiotics, but relapse was common unless hardware was addressed[1,3,4,7–16]. Multiple reports have described initial responses with subsequent recurrence necessitating Tenckhoff removal or exchange[1,10,13], and a contemporary refractory case required eravacycline alongside intraperitoneal and intravenous β-lactams before clinical improvement[7]. These observations, together with our ~25% relapse rate concentrated in device-associated disease, support a low threshold for exchanging or removing PD catheters when A. xylosoxidans is identified or when the clinical response to active agents is incomplete.

A. xylosoxidans has also been isolated from pancreatic collections (infected pseudocyst/necrotizing pancreatitis) with good outcomes when carbapenem-based therapy was paired with endoscopic or operative drainage, as indicated[4,17–20]. In contrast, the outcomes were poorer in patients with cirrhosis and transplant recipients. Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) caused by A. xylosoxidans is associated with high mortality in early and modern reports[21,22], and a liver-transplant recipient with biloma and intra-abdominal abscess deteriorated rapidly despite initial regimens until A. xylosoxidans was recognized[23]. These higher-risk hosts are vulnerable to empiric mismatches (e.g., ceftriaxone/cefotaxime alone for SBP), reinforcing the need to escalate early to agents with reliable activity against A. xylosoxidans when nosocomial exposure or lack of response raises concerns.

Biliary infections illustrate the principle that drainage is decisive[24–26]. A. xylosoxidans has also been cultured from bile in a burn-unit patient, in whom environmental sampling implicated contaminated water-contact equipment[25], emphasizing procedural and environmental links. Antibiotics alone frequently underperform when biliary drainage is inadequate, and restoring flow is the turning point.

Postoperative and foreign-body-related infections further highlight the need to search for retained material[27–33]. An example is the diagnosis of a subdiaphragmatic abscess in a patient years after cholecystectomy due to a dropped gallstone, which resolved only after drainage and antibiotics[27]. A. xylosoxidans presented as a retroperitoneal abscess masquerading as femoral pain in a patient, delaying diagnosis until urine/pus cultures directed therapy with drainage[28] and as a mesh-related infection requiring combined surgical and antimicrobial management[29]. In these settings, re-imaging and definitive source control (drainage, debridement, and foreign body removal) frequently mark the point of recovery.

Therapeutically, the pattern across phenotypes is consistent: empiric mismatch is common, with early cephalosporins or fluoroquinolones frequently failing until escalation to piperacillin/tazobactam or a carbapenem; trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole or a fluoroquinolone has been used variably for step-down when supported by susceptibilities, and eravacycline appears as salvage for refractory PD peritonitis[4,7,9,23]. Given the intrinsic multidrug resistance and biofilm-forming capacity of A. xylosoxidans, therapy should be susceptibility-guided, paired with source control, and followed by close monitoring to detect relapse. To improve clinical interpretability, we summarized the reported susceptibility patterns qualitatively in Table 4; however, these data should be interpreted cautiously because susceptibility testing and reporting were not standardized across cases.

Our systematic review has several limitations. First, the evidence base consisted almost entirely of case reports and small case series, making the review inherently vulnerable to publication and reporting bias; unusual, severe, refractory, or procedurally complex cases are more likely to be published than routine or rapidly resolving infections. Because of the descriptive and heterogeneous nature of these reports, formal assessment of publication bias using standard quantitative methods was not feasible. Second, the number of included episodes was small, and clinical phenotypes were heterogeneous, spanning PD-associated peritonitis, biliary infection, postoperative and mesh-related infection, liver abscess, pancreatic infection, retroperitoneal abscess, and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis or bacterascites. This heterogeneity precluded meta-analysis and limited meaningful subgroup comparisons. Third, microbiologic methods and susceptibility reporting were inconsistent across reports, with variation in organism identification techniques, antimicrobial panels tested, interpretive criteria, and completeness of susceptibility data. As a result, our summary of antimicrobial susceptibility patterns is qualitative rather than quantitative. Fourth, details regarding healthcare exposure, device status, timing of procedures, and source-control interventions were variably reported, and some classifications required reasonable inference from the published narrative. Fifth, follow-up duration and outcome definitions were inconsistent, which may have led to under-recognition of relapse. Sixth, given observational, case-level data, confounding is substantial, and causal attribution separating the effects of specific antibiotics from the timing and type of source control is not possible. Finally, the review was limited to English-language publications, and inaccessible full texts were excluded, so additional relevant cases may have been missed.

Recent studies have highlighted the emerging role of artificial intelligence tools, including large language models, in assisting with literature synthesis and medical research analysis. However, their use requires careful validation to ensure scientific accuracy and transparency. Future systematic reviews may incorporate such tools cautiously to enhance the efficiency of literature screening [35].

Clinically, A. xylosoxidans should be considered in healthcare-associated intra-abdominal infections, especially PD peritonitis, post-ERCP cholangitis, and postoperative abscesses when a nonfermenting Gram-negative rod grows or when standard empiric therapy underperforms. Early, comprehensive culturing, proactive source control (catheter exchange/removal, drainage, stent optimization), and timely escalation to agents active against A. xylosoxidans can reduce morbidity and likely mitigate the observed relapse signal. Future multicenter efforts should quantify susceptibility distributions, compare outcomes by regimen and timing of device intervention, and clarify the optimal treatment duration by syndrome and source-control status.

Conclusion

A. xylosoxidans is an uncommon but clinically important cause of intra-abdominal infections that are largely healthcare-associated and often device-related. In our synthesis, 93.8% of the episodes had recent healthcare exposure, 68.8% involved a device or recent procedure, and 37.5% required source control. These findings underscore the importance of early recognition when a nonfermenting Gram-negative rod is isolated or when patients fail to improve on initial therapy, and of promptly administering susceptibility-guided antibiotics paired with timely device intervention or drainage. Future multicenter studies should define optimal initial antimicrobial regimens, indications, and timing for device removal/exchange, and the treatment duration by syndrome and source-control status, while monitoring evolving resistance.
