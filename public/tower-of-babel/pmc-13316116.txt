Geomechanical performance of a novel L-shaped wellbore design for hot dry rock geothermal reservoirs: insights from fully coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical modeling
Authors: Mohammad Aliakbari Khoei, Ali Pak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGSs), developed to extract heat from hot dry rock (HDR) formations, are promising renewable heat resources; however, their long-term performance is governed by coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) processes. This study introduces a novel L-shaped injection-production wellbore configuration designed to enhance reservoir contact while reducing drilling complexity compared to conventional vertical, multilateral, and closed-loop systems. A fully coupled three-dimensional THM numerical model is developed to evaluate its performance, accounting for conductive-convective heat transfer, pore-pressure evolution, and thermally induced deformation. A sensitivity analysis investigates the effects of horizontal wellbore length, injection pressure, injection temperature, rock thermal expansion coefficient, and fluid compressibility. Extending the horizontal section increases the stable production period from 2.6 to 6 years and doubles reservoir lifetime from 6.3 to 12.4 years compared to a quintuplet vertical system, while delivering more than twice the thermal power output. Reducing injection pressure from 10 to 5 MPa suppresses cold-front migration and extends sustainability beyond 20 years. Increasing injection temperature from 323.15 to 343.15 K raises lifetime from 11 to 12.4 years but reduces power by 20%. The results demonstrate that the proposed configuration provides a balanced solution between thermal efficiency, mechanical response, and operational simplicity for HDR development.

Introduction

Geothermal energy has increasingly been recognized as a cornerstone of the global clean-energy transition due to its ability to deliver reliable, continuous, and low-carbon heat and power. Its independence from weather variability and minimal surface footprint distinguish it from other renewables1–4. Among geothermal resources, hot dry rock (HDR) systems represent a particularly attractive option because of their extensive global distribution, high temperatures at depth, and independence from natural hydrothermal circulation5,6. However, HDR reservoirs are mechanically complex, characterized by extremely low permeability, high in situ stresses, strong thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) coupling, and uncertain fracture network configuration. Their successful development depends on a sophisticated understanding of subsurface geomechanics, robust numerical frameworks, and well architecture that can maximize thermal sweep while maintaining operational feasibility7.

Early investigations demonstrated that HDR development is strongly governed by rock mechanics and thermal stress redistribution. Ghassemi9 reviewed key rock-mechanics challenges, emphasizing thermally induced stresses, fracture opening and closure, and the importance of THM coupling in predicting energy extraction success. Laboratory research further revealed severe thermo-mechanical degradation in HDR granites. For example, Zhu et al.10 showed that granite exposed to temperatures exceeding 600 °C and subsequent cooling can lose more than 60% of its compressive strength due to thermal microcracking. These findings highlight the importance of modeling thermal and hydraulic effects on reservoir mechanics, as the stability and permeability of fracture networks are highly temperature-dependent.

Advances in numerical modeling have led to more accurate representation of these complex processes. Foundational work by Pak and Chan11 developed an implicit THM fracture model for hydraulic processes in deformable porous media. Recent experimental studies have also highlighted the significant impact of thermal and cryogenic cycling on fracture evolution and mechanical degradation in granite, emphasizing their relevance for reservoir stimulation and permeability enhancement in enhanced geothermal systems12,13. Additional progress was made by Watanabe et al.5, who conducted uncertainty analysis for THM processes in heterogeneous porous media, showing that spatial variability in permeability and thermal properties can significantly alter reservoir behavior. Gelet et al.14 advanced the field with a dual-porosity, local thermal nonequilibrium THM model for fractured HDR reservoirs, demonstrating the importance of accounting for separate fracture and matrix temperatures. Work on general subsurface energy systems has further informed THM modeling practices. For example, McCartney et al.1 reviewed geotechnical advances in subsurface energy technologies, while Tomac and Sauter4 summarized geomechanical challenges specific to deep geothermal reservoirs, such as fracture stability, induced seismicity, and thermally driven deformation. Mortazavi et al.15 introduced extended finite element formulations capable of simulating cold and hot water injection in naturally fractured rock without remeshing, while Iranmanesh and Pak16 proposed enriched meshfree methods to capture discontinuities in multiphase THM systems. Hashemi et al.17 examined the effects of soil–structure interfaces in energy geo-structures, offering broader insight into THM interactions in engineered subsurface systems, and Cui and Fall18 introduced coupled THMC models in underground backfill applications, contributing a valuable methodological perspective to geothermal THM coupling.

A growing number of studies have also explored the behavior of HDR systems under CO₂ injection, which can be thermally advantageous but geomechanically complex. Li and Laloui19 developed a coupled multiphase THM model for supercritical CO₂ injection, demonstrating the importance of thermally induced deformation on surface uplift predictions. Meanwhile, Mousavi et al.20 examined thermo-hydro-mechanical interactions in unsaturated porous media, contributing to the mechanistic understanding of coupled flow and deformation processes relevant to geothermal reservoirs.

Parallel to modeling advances, substantial research has focused on optimizing well architectures for improved heat extraction. Traditional EGS (Enhanced Geothermal System) designs rely on stimulated vertical doublets or multi-well patterns. Chen and Jiang21 analyzed multi-well HDR layouts and concluded that although additional wells initially increase heat extraction, they adversely intensify localized cooling and accelerate thermal breakthrough. Song et al.22 demonstrated that multi-lateral wells can suffer rapid temperature decline, from 373 K to below 340 K within 15 years, when fractures channel cold fluid directly toward production wells. Zhang et al.23 showed that multi-injection systems in horizontal well patterns can reduce production temperature from 454.5 to ~ 340 K due to intensified cooling, while Wu et al.24 evaluated multiple-well EGSs in the presence of areal flow and demonstrated that temperature fronts become highly asymmetric. Field-scale modeling has provided further insights. Llanos et al.25 developed a numerical model of the Habanero HDR reservoir in Australia, capturing complex flow and temperature distributions that significantly influenced reservoir recoverability.

To enhance heat extraction, researchers have proposed alternative well geometries. Horizontal and directional wells have shown to increase contact with the reservoir26,27. Cui et al.28 examined heat extraction from HDR via recycled heat transmission in horizontal wells and reported significantly higher production temperatures compared to vertical systems of similar depth. Pinnate horizontal configurations, studied by Xie and Wang29, were found to produce 17–20 MW of thermal power under optimized branch spacing, far exceeding that of conventional systems. Additional innovations include horizontally layered EGS configurations30, which exhibit improved lifetime and outlet temperature when the layered structure aligns favorably with fluid flow pathways.

Closed-loop geothermal systems (CLGS) have emerged as an attractive alternative where hydraulic stimulation is undesirable31. White et al.32 investigated deep closed-loop systems and showed that thermal performance is highly sensitive to circulation rate, borehole depth, and fluid type. Liu and Taleghani33 synthesized technological advances in CLGS designs, concluding that although these systems avoid induced seismicity and fluid loss, their performance remains limited by conduction-dominated transfer. As a remedy, complex geometries have been proposed. Hou et al.34 demonstrated that well pattern optimization can enhance production potential in closed loops, while Wang et al.35 evaluated multi-level, multi-branch coaxial borehole heat exchangers and reported significantly expanded thermal footprints. Chappidi et al.36 introduced a hybrid combined coaxial–U-shaped configuration with better heat exchange relative to conventional coaxial systems, and Chen et al.21 compared different well configurations for medium-deep coaxial systems, showing that dual-U and multi-U systems outperform traditional layouts. Jia et al.37 analyzed a novel 3D U-shaped closed-loop system and demonstrated its economic viability for district-scale heating. Cai et al.38 evaluated five-spot horizontal well arrangements in EGS and emphasized the importance of thermal-fluid–solid coupling in maintaining long-term performance. Ghavidel et al.39 investigated multilateral closed-loop systems in HDR and identified key design parameters that control electricity generation efficiency.

Aliakbari Khoei et al.40 demonstrated that strategic plament of injection and production wellbores within low-permeability zones can enhance heat extraction performance, while also highlighting the importance of the effective volume of surronding unfractured rock. Vaezi et al.41 reviewed THM modeling approaches for fractured rock, emphasizing the need to bridge between continuum and discontinuum methods for a reliable assessment of its performance. Zhang et al.42 extended this by quantifying uncertainties in deep-mine geothermal systems under THM coupling. Horne et al.43 provided a broad overview of EGS technologies for clean-firm energy, underscoring the importance of next-generation wellbore geometries. Complementary studies such as Sadeghi et al.44, focusing on borehole thermal storage, and Aljubran and Horne45, examining techno-economics of geothermal deployment, further contextualize the need for cost-effective and operationally feasible wellbore configurations.

Despite these extensive efforts, existing geothermal reservoir architectures still face significant limitations. Vertical or multi-vertical HDR systems provide insufficient lateral sweep. Multilateral and pinnate wells impose substantial drilling and operational complexity. Closed-loop systems require extremely long lateral sections to counteract conduction limitations. Layered and heterogeneous formations challenge existing designs, as thermal asymmetry and mechanical discontinuities reduce efficiency. Fully coupled THM modeling of innovative geometries remains rare, and the geothermal community continues to lack a well architecture that balances efficiency, operational simplicity, and economic feasibility.

Therefore, the present study introduces a novel L-shaped wellbore system that preserves the thermal advantages of directional drilling while substantially reducing drilling complexity and associated costs of coaxial, U-shape, and closed loop systems. First, the influence of the horizontal-to-vertical length ratio on the heat extraction performance of the proposed configuration is systematically assessed. The thermo-hydro-mechanical behavior of the L-shaped system is then compared with that of a conventional Quintuplet vertical arrangement to quantify potential performance improvements and its geotechnical effects on the surrounding environment. Subsequently, the impacts of injection pressure and injection temperature on reservoir thermal output are evaluated. Finally, the sensitivity of production temperature to the thermal expansion coefficient of the rock matrix and the compressibility of the working fluid is examined. Collectively, these analyses establish a comprehensive framework for optimizing the operational and economic viability of L-shaped wellbores in deep HDR geothermal reservoirs.

THM coupling

Fluid flow, heat transfer, and deformation of the solid skeleton constitute the three fundamental processes governing heat extraction in HDR reservoirs. In numerical simulations, these correspond to the seepage, temperature, and stress fields, respectively. The coupling interactions among these fields arise in several ways:(i)The temperature field modifies the seepage field by altering the thermophysical properties of the circulating fluid, while the fluid flow influences the temperature field through thermal exchange between the cooler injected water and the heated rock matrix.(ii)Temperature variations induce thermal stresses that influence the mechanical field; conversely, deformation of the rock mass generates heat and thus affects the temperature field.(iii)Injection-induced pore pressure alters the effective stress, and stress changes subsequently modify pore geometry and permeability within the rock49.

Reservoir characteristics, initial and boundary conditions

A three-dimensional numerical model of the HDR geothermal reservoir was developed in COMSOL Multiphysics, employing a finite-element framework capable of solving the fully coupled thermo–hydro–mechanical (THM) governing equations. The computational domain comprises three principal components: the engineered geothermal reservoir, the surrounding unfractured rock, and the wellbores. The HDR reservoir is represented as a cubic volume of 500 m in each direction and contains an L-shaped injection well paired with an L-shaped production well. In plan view (Fig. 4), the two wellheads are positioned at opposite corners of the cube, aligned along its primary diagonal. Figure 5 illustrates the configuration of the proposed dual L-shaped wellbore system embedded within the three-dimensional HDR geothermal reservoir, along with the vertical wellbore layout used for comparison in this study. In this configuration, both the injection and production wellbores are L-shaped, each consisting of a vertical section that transitions smoothly into a horizontal section. Both L-shaped wellbores penetrate the reservoir vertically but extend laterally in different horizontal directions. Both boreholes have a uniform cross-section of 0.2 m × 0.2 m in the xy-plane. The centerlines of the wells are offset by 50 m in both x- and y-directions from the reservoir boundaries. The thermal performance of HDR systems typically declines over time, with the production temperature decreasing as the reservoir cools gradually. The operational lifetime of an HDR reservoir is therefore limited. Following Jiang et al.21,65, the abandonment temperature is defined as the point at which the produced fluid temperature decreases by 10 K, and the reservoir lifetime corresponds to the time required to reach this threshold.

To ensure numerical robustness and physical fidelity in the coupled THM analysis, substantial attention was devoted to the generation and verification of the computational mesh. Mesh configuration is particularly critical in HDR simulations, where steep thermal gradients, elevated pore-pressure differentials, and pronounced mechanical contrasts arise near the wellbores and at the interface between the geothermal reservoir rock and the surrounding unfractured rock. Therefore, both global mesh quality and localized refinement were optimized based on best practices in THM and geothermal reservoir modeling.

A combination of physics-controlled and manually refined meshing strategies was adopted to achieve an optimal spatial distribution of elements. The maximum element growth rate was limited to 1.4 to ensure smooth transitions between adjacent mesh zones and reduce interpolation errors often observed in tightly coupled THM domains. A curvature factor of 0.4 was applied to refine the mesh along the curved sections of the L-shaped wells and to enhance geometric fidelity where rapid directional changes in heat flow and fluid transport occur. Additionally, a narrow-region resolution parameter of 0.7 was implemented to properly capture confined geometries where sharp gradients may develop. Targeted refinement was imposed around the wellbores, where the highest thermal, hydraulic, and mechanical gradients are expected, thereby improving numerical stability and enabling accurate representation of near-wellbore thermoelastic behavior. The final mesh configuration, including localized refinement near the wellbores and reservoir boundaries, is illustrated in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Mesh configuration of the L-shaped wellbore system.

Mesh configuration of the L-shaped wellbore system.

Region-specific element size limits were introduced to reflect the distinct physical characteristics of each domain. Within the geothermal reservoir rock, a maximum element size of 38.5 m was used, providing sufficient resolution to capture convective–conductive heat transfer and reservoir deformation. In contrast, the surrounding unfractured rock, being impervious and transferring heat only by conduction, was assigned a larger maximum element size of 80 m. This coarser discretization substantially reduces computational cost without compromising accuracy in the conductive temperature field.

Mesh quality was evaluated using standard metrics, including minimum element quality factor, skewness, and aspect ratio. All elements satisfied a minimum quality threshold of 0.25, with most exceeding 0.45, ensuring numerical stability and preventing solver divergence.

A mesh convergence analysis was conducted by progressively increasing the number of tetrahedral elements from 450,000 to 650,000. Refinement from 450,000 to 550,000 resulted in less than a 3% variation in key outputs, including production temperature, pore-pressure distribution, and vertical displacement at the reservoir center. Further refinement to 650,000 elements produced results indistinguishable from the 550,000-element mesh, indicating numerical convergence. Therefore, the mesh with approximately 550,000 elements was selected as the optimal configuration, offering a balance between computational efficiency and accuracy.

Results and discussions

Selecting an appropriate wellbore design is crucial to maximizing heat extraction in HDR geothermal systems. Many advanced concepts that incorporate horizontal drilling, such as multilateral wells, branched horizontals, or closed-loop geothermal systems, often require highly complex geometries that are difficult to construct and financially demanding. In contrast, simpler vertical arrangements like the Quintuplet system or conventional doublets are easier to drill but generally provide limited reservoir contact and lower thermal sweep efficiency.

To bridge the gap between practicality and performance, this study evaluates a novel L-shaped wellbore configuration that combines the drilling simplicity of vertical wells with the enhanced reservoir exposure of horizontal sections. The goal is to determine whether this proposed geometry can deliver improved heat extraction performance without the construction challenges associated with more intricate horizontal well systems.

Proposing the effective length for the horizontal section of the wellbores

The geometry of the wellbore system is a fundamental design variable in HDR operations, as it governs both the circulation pathway and the efficiency of heat extraction. Among the geometric parameters, the length of the horizontal leg plays a critical role in controlling the extent of reservoir contact, hydraulic connectivity between injection and production wells, and the evolution of the thermal sweep. Therefore, this subsection evaluates how variations in the horizontal section influence the performance of the system.

Figure 7a illustrates the sensitivity of the production temperature to variations in the horizontal length of the L-shaped wellbores. All cases follow the expected two-stage thermal behavior of deep HDR systems: an initial period of nearly constant temperature, followed by a gradual decline once the cooling front reaches the production well. Increasing the ratio of horizontal to vertical length (H/V) slightly accelerates the onset of this temperature decline and steepens its slope. This behaviour arises because extending the horizontal leg increases the hydraulic effectiveness of the flow pathway between injection and production wells. A longer horizontal section provides a more direct route for the injected cold water to move through the reservoir, allowing the cooled region to advance more rapidly toward the production well. In addition, a longer horizontal segment intersects a greater extent of the rock mass, enabling the cold fluid to influence a larger area of the reservoir. As a result, the thermal disturbance develops more quickly along the dominant flow corridor, leading to an earlier and more pronounced decline in the production temperature for larger H/V ratios. This earlier temperature drop is also reflected in the reservoir’s useful lifetime, which decreases slightly from 13.2 years at H/V = 0.2 to 12.6 years at H/V = 0.4, and 12.4 years for both H/V = 0.6 and H/V = 0.8. Despite these differences, all curves demonstrate similar behaviour in the early years, confirming that the system maintains a stable thermal performance until the cooling front approaches the production well.Fig. 7Effect of different horizontal-to-vertical length ratios on (a) production temperature and (b) thermal power output of the geothermal system.

Effect of different horizontal-to-vertical length ratios on (a) production temperature and (b) thermal power output of the geothermal system.

Figure 7b shows that the thermal power output is strongly influenced by the relative length of the horizontal wellbore section, with longer horizontal legs consistently producing higher power throughout the extraction period. The highest performance is achieved for H/V = 0.8, where extending the horizontal segment to approximately 400 m allows the production well to intercept a larger volume of high-temperature reservoir. A longer horizontal section not only increases the effective contact area between the circulating fluid and the hot rock but also reduces flow resistance along the production pathway, resulting in higher mass flow rates and, consequently, greater thermal power. This scaling effect is evident in the initial outputs, which rise from 10 MW (H/V = 0.2) to 14.1 MW (H/V = 0.4), 18.9 MW (H/V = 0.6), and 23.2 MW (H/V = 0.8), and it remains significant even at the end of the reservoir’s useful lifetime, where the corresponding power levels are 6 MW, 9 MW, 11.7 MW, and 14 MW, respectively. When comparing the shortest and longest horizontal legs, the increase in thermal output exceeds a factor of two, demonstrating that the geometry of the horizontal section exerts a far more dominant influence on energy delivery than the modest reduction in production temperature noted in Fig. 7a. Even though longer horizontal segments promote a faster approach of the cooling front, their ability to sweep a wider zone of the reservoir and sustain elevated mass flow rates results in substantially greater heat extraction over the reservoir’s operational life, making higher H/V ratios considerably more productive.

As shown in Fig. 7, increasing the length of the horizontal section enhances the overall heat extraction capacity; however, it also leads to a slightly earlier onset of production temperature decline. This behavior can be explained by the coupled interaction between flow pathways, heat exchange efficiency, and thermal breakthrough mechanisms. A longer horizontal section increases the contact area between the circulating fluid and the hot rock, which enhances heat transfer and results in higher initial thermal power. At the same time, this extended contact accelerates heat depletion in the vicinity of the wellbore, leading to faster local cooling of the reservoir.

Moreover, the longer flow path promotes a more efficient hydraulic connection between the injection and production wells, facilitating the propagation of the cold front along the horizontal section. As a result, although the overall reservoir volume contributing to heat extraction is larger, the thermal front reaches the production region slightly earlier. This reflects a trade-off between improved heat extraction efficiency and accelerated thermal breakthrough, which is a key characteristic of the thermo–hydro–mechanical behavior of the system.

Comparing the proposed wellbore system with quintuplet vertical wellbores

Benchmarking the performance of the proposed L-shaped configuration against established geothermal layouts is essential for demonstrating its practical value. Among commonly deployed HDR arrangements, the quintuplet system, consisting of one central injection well and four vertical production wells, is widely considered a standard design due to its geometric simplicity and symmetric drainage pattern. To evaluate whether the proposed L-shaped wells can overcome these limitations, this section compares their performance directly against the conventional quintuplet configuration.

Comparison of thermal performance

Figure 8a compares the long-term production temperature of the proposed dual L-shaped wellbore system with that of a conventional configuration consisting of one vertical injection well and four vertical production wells. The results clearly show that the L-shaped system preserves a stable production temperature for a significantly longer period. While the vertical-well system maintains its initial temperature for only about 2.6 years, the L-shaped wells extend this stable period to approximately 6 years, more than doubling the duration before cooling effects reach the production points. This improvement stems from the physical geometry of the L-shaped system: the horizontal legs intersect a broader portion of the reservoir and draw fluid from hotter zones that remain thermally undisturbed for a longer period compared to the more localized drainage region of vertical wells. As a result, the cooling front develops more slowly around the L-shaped producer, delaying the temperature decline that is unavoidable once cold injected water begins to influence the fluid reaching the production well. Beyond the stable stage, the L-shaped wells also exhibit a more gradual temperature decline, extending the reservoir’s useful lifetime (defined as a 10 K drop in production temperature) from approximately 6.3 years in the vertical configuration to nearly 12.4 years in the L-shaped arrangement. This nearly twofold increase in operational longevity underscores the robustness of the L-shaped architecture, particularly during sustained heat extraction. Such an improvement is particularly notable because traditional horizontal wells, such as multilateral or multibranched systems, do not typically achieve this degree of temperature sustainability. The superior thermal performance of the L-shaped system, therefore, highlights its ability to access deeper, hotter zones of the reservoir while simultaneously minimizing premature thermal breakthrough.Fig. 8Comparison of (a) production temperature and (b) thermal power output between the vertical wellbore system and the proposed L-shaped wellbore system.

Comparison of (a) production temperature and (b) thermal power output between the vertical wellbore system and the proposed L-shaped wellbore system.

Figure 8b compares the thermal power output of the two wellbore configurations and demonstrates a substantial advantage for the proposed L-shaped system. Throughout the entire 20-year period, the L-shaped wells consistently deliver much higher thermal power than the vertical-well system, reaching up to more than twice greater output in the early years, where the initial thermal power output is 10.7 MW for vertical wells and 23.2 MW for L-shaped wells. This enhancement is primarily due to the increased mass flow rate enabled by the extended horizontal segments, which create longer contact zones between the circulating fluid and the hot rock. As the fluid passes along these horizontal legs, it absorbs heat over a larger surface area and from deeper, less-cooled parts of the reservoir, resulting in a significantly higher energy gain per unit volume of fluid. The vertical system, in contrast, draws fluid from a more limited zone near the wellbore, causing the surrounding rock to cool earlier and restricting the achievable flow rate and temperature difference. The comparison confirms that the geometric advantages of the L-shaped wells translate directly into superior power generation, making them a far more effective choice for deep HDR applications where long-term productivity and high heat-recovery ratios are critical.

Comparison of mechanical performances

Evaluating the mechanical response of the reservoir is essential when comparing different geothermal wellbore designs, because long-term heat extraction alters in-situ stresses, induces rock deformation, and leads to surface settlement or heave. While conventional vertical systems typically affect a relatively narrow region around the wellbore, the proposed L-shaped configuration interacts with a much larger rock volume, potentially amplifying geomechanical effects. Understanding how this broader thermal footprint translates into ground displacement is therefore necessary to assess the environmental impact of the geothermal energy extraction operation.

Figure 10a compares the upward displacement that develops above the production well for the L-shaped and the conventional vertical-well configurations. The results show that the L-shaped system induces a substantially larger heave at the ground surface, approximately 3.5 times greater than that produced by the vertical wells. This difference arises from the way each wellbore geometry redistributes temperature and fluid pressure within the reservoir. The production leg of the L-shaped system extends horizontally, intersecting a larger rock volume and cooling a broader region around the well. As the temperature in this zone gradually decreases, the rock undergoes contraction, reducing the confinement on the overlying layers and allowing the ground surface to deform upward. Because the L-shaped producer extracts heat from deeper and more thermally active sections of the reservoir, the cooled zone develops more gradually but extends over a larger area, producing a more pronounced uplift response. After a time equal to the reservoir’s useful lifetime, the vertical-well system generates roughly 11.7 mm of surface heave, whereas the L-shaped system produces about 31.3 mm. Sensitivity analysis

Effect of injection pressure

Injection pressure is one of the most controllable operational parameters in HDR systems, directly governing the circulation rate, thermal sweep efficiency, and the timing of thermal breakthrough. Because the proposed L-shaped configuration enhances hydraulic connectivity through its extended horizontal leg, understanding how different injection pressures influence both short-term heat extraction and long-term thermal sustainability becomes essential. This subsection evaluates how variations in injection pressure modify reservoir behaviour and quantifies the trade-off between immediate power production and long-term reservoir lifetime.

Figure 13a demonstrates that the injection pressure has a profound influence on the thermal sustainability of the L-shaped geothermal system, with lower pressures clearly prolonging the stable temperature stage. When the injection pressure is reduced from 10 to 5 MPa, the production temperature curve shifts upward and remains nearly flat for almost the entire 20-year period. At the lowest pressure (5 MPa), the cooling stage effectively disappears within the simulated time frame, indicating that the cold front requires significantly more time to reach the production well. This behavior is expected because lower injection pressure reduces the mass flow rate through the reservoir, slowing the advance of the cooled fluid and giving the surrounding hot rock more opportunity to conduct heat back toward the circulating fluid. The heat supplied by the surrounding unfractured rocks can partially compensate for the cooling effect under low-pressure conditions, whereas at high injection pressure, this conductive recharge becomes insufficient to offset the rapid advective heat loss. As a result, the thermal front migrates more gradually, and the reservoir maintains its native temperature for a much longer duration. Consequently, the effective thermal lifetime (defined by a 10 K drop) increases dramatically: from 12.4 years at 10 MPa to well beyond 20 years at 5 MPa, so much so that the end of the useful lifetime is no longer detectable within the simulation window. The sharp contrast between these cases highlights a fundamental trade-off in HDR systems: high injection pressures increase circulation efficiency but accelerate thermal breakthrough, whereas low pressures preserve reservoir temperature but at the cost of reduced heat extraction rate.Fig. 13Effect of injection pressure on (a) production temperature and (b) thermal power output of the L-shaped wellbore system.

Effect of injection pressure on (a) production temperature and (b) thermal power output of the L-shaped wellbore system.

Figure 13b reveals the opposite trend for thermal power output, where higher injection pressures result in substantially greater heat extraction throughout the operational period. Increasing the injection pressure from 5 to 10 MPa enhances the mass flow rate entering the reservoir, which directly increases the amount of thermal energy carried to the surface. Because thermal power is proportional to both flow rate and the temperature difference between the produced fluid and the injection fluid, the strong increase in mass flow results in significantly higher power output even though the production temperature declines faster at higher pressures. At the highest injection pressure (10 MPa), the system delivers power levels up to five times greater than those at 5 MPa during the early years, and it remains consistently superior throughout the simulation. This demonstrates that stronger injection not only accelerates circulation but also expands the volume of rock contributing heat to the system, thereby improving the instantaneous energy extraction rate. However, the same mechanism that enhances power also accelerates the movement of the cooling front, shortening the reservoir’s thermal lifetime. Therefore, injection pressure controls a clear performance balance: higher pressures favor short-term productivity and higher power output, while lower pressures maximize long-term thermal sustainability.

Effect of injection temperature

Injection-fluid temperature plays a critical role in determining the thermal gradient that drives heat extraction in HDR systems. Because the L-shaped configuration mobilizes a large heated volume along both vertical and horizontal flow paths, even modest variations in injection temperature can influence the timing of thermal breakthrough and the evolution of power output. This subsection examines how different injection temperatures impact both the sustainability of the thermal response and the resulting energy production. It is worth noting, however, that in practical HDR operations the injected water is typically supplied at near-ambient temperature, and intentional adjustment of its temperature is rarely feasible; thus, this sensitivity analysis is presented primarily to elucidate the fundamental thermal behavior of the reservoir rather than to propose an operational control strategy. Figure 14a illustrates the influence of the injection-fluid temperature on the production temperature and thermal power output of the L-shaped wellbore geothermal system. Discussion and engineering application

The sensitivity analyses presented in this study provide important insights into the coupled thermo–hydro–mechanical (THM) behavior of the reservoir and its implications for practical geothermal system design. A key observation is the existence of a fundamental trade-off between short-term energy production and long-term thermal sustainability. Parameters that enhance fluid circulation, such as higher injection pressure, significantly increase thermal power output but also accelerate cold-front propagation, leading to earlier thermal breakthrough and reduced reservoir lifetime. In contrast, lower injection pressures limit fluid flow intensity, thereby preserving reservoir temperature over extended periods at the expense of reduced power generation.

The effect of injection temperature further highlights this trade-off. Higher injection temperatures delay thermal depletion by reducing the temperature gradient between the injected fluid and the surrounding rock; however, this also leads to lower heat extraction rates. These findings indicate that operational parameters must be carefully selected depending on project objectives, whether prioritizing peak energy production or long-term sustainability.

From a hydraulic and mechanical perspective, the analysis of fluid compressibility and rock thermal expansion demonstrates their significant influence on reservoir performance. Increased fluid compressibility attenuates pressure propagation, resulting in weaker hydraulic connectivity and reduced heat sweep efficiency, which accelerates thermal breakthrough. Similarly, higher thermal expansion of the rock matrix enhances thermally induced deformation and contributes to faster energy depletion. These results emphasize the importance of accurate characterization of reservoir properties in predicting system behavior.

From an engineering perspective, the proposed system offers flexibility in operational design. The ability to adjust parameters such as injection pressure allows operators to balance energy efficiency and reservoir longevity according to economic and technical objectives. Moreover, the simplified well configuration, requiring fewer wells compared to conventional multi-well systems, can reduce drilling complexity while maintaining high thermal performance.

Overall, the combined analysis provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the interactions between operational parameters, reservoir properties, and system performance, offering practical guidance for the design and optimization of HDR geothermal systems using the proposed L-shaped configuration.

Overall, this study demonstrates that the L-shaped wellbore configuration offers a technically robust and economically attractive alternative for HDR development. It should be noted that the economic implications discussed in this study are qualitative and primarily based on the reduced number of required wells. A detailed techno-economic analysis, including drilling costs, operational expenses, and lifecycle assessment, is beyond the scope of the present work and will be the subject of future research.

The present study, while comprehensive, is limited to a single reservoir geometry and assumes a homogeneous and isotropic rock mass, thereby neglecting the effects of geological heterogeneity and directional permeability that commonly characterize natural HDR systems. In addition, large discrete fractures were not incorporated, and hydraulic fracture stimulation and long-term permeability evolution were not considered. Future research will incorporate discrete fracture networks, reservoir heterogeneity, anisotropic properties, and multiphase fluid circulation to better capture the complexity of field-scale geothermal reservoirs.
