Esophageal resonance Raman spectroscopy tracks mixed venous oxygenation in experimental shock and hypoxemia
Authors: Tysen DeWaard, Bonne L Keijer, Franklin Ducatez, Cassidy Ferron, Jason Zhang, Yifeng Peng, Padraic Romfh, John N Kheir
Published: 2026

Introduction

Early recognition of inadequate tissue oxygenation is essential in the management of critically ill patients [1, 2]. Mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2) is an integrative marker of the balance between systemic oxygen delivery (DO2) and oxygen consumption (VO2) [2]. It has established value in the assessment of shock states [3, 4], cardiac output adequacy, and systemic oxygenation beyond conventional vital signs or arterial measurements [5]. However, measuring SvO2 requires pulmonary artery (PA) catheterization or central venous access, which carries risks such as infection, thrombosis, and vascular injury [6], and is generally limited to patients with independent indications for invasive monitoring [5, 7].

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) is widely used to estimate regional tissue oxygenation through differential light absorbance and has also been studied in the context of shock monitoring [8, 9]. It integrates signals from spectrophotometrically active arterial, venous, and intracellular structures, including myoglobin [10], and features deep tissue penetration, yielding a composite regional oxygenation index rather than a discrete measure of venous hemoglobin oxygen saturation [11]. Its accuracy is limited by assumptions of static arterial–venous blood volume ratios [12] and myoglobin contamination (which remains oxygenated in all but the most extremes of tissue hypoxia); this limits detection of critical hypoxia [13]. More recent research approaches compute estimates of SvO2 based on measurements of VO2, cardiac output, and arterial saturation (SaO2) [14], however, these approaches are technically complex and impractical for rapid bedside decision-making [14, 15], highlighting the need for noninvasive, continuous monitoring methods.

In contrast to absorbance-based methods, resonance Raman spectroscopy (RRS) offers molecularly specific detection of hemoglobin vibrational signatures [16]. Raman scattering arises from inelastic photon–molecule interactions, in which energy is transferred to molecular vibrational modes, producing a measurable wavelength shift characteristic of specific molecular bonds. Tuning the wavelength near the Soret absorption band (~ 400–450 nm) selectively amplifies scattering from the heme porphyrin ring [17], yielding a strong hemoglobin signal and allowing distinction from other tissue chromophores, including myoglobin [16, 18–20]. Raman-based assessment of mitochondrial redox state has been proposed as a noninvasive method to evaluate tissue oxygen delivery and mitochondrial function in vivo, with potential applications in monitoring tissue viability, ischemia, and organ preservation [19].

Leveraging the molecular specificity of Raman spectroscopy, we previously described a miniaturized RRS probe fitted into the retropharyngeal space that predicted time to death in a model of lethal hemorrhage [20]. Here, we evaluate a novel designed esophageal RRS probe which measures regional capillary hemoglobin oxygen saturation (SEsO2). We hypothesize that SEsO2 tracks SvO2 across shock states and thereby enables minimally invasive detection of systemic oxygen imbalance.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and animal care

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol 00002467) at Boston Children’s Hospital and performed in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male Sprague Dawley rats (400–500 g) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA, USA) and housed in pairs under controlled environmental conditions (temperature 21–22 °C; humidity 40–50%) with a 12-h light–dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. Animals were acclimated for at least 7 days prior to experimentation. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines (Supplemental 1).

Anesthesia, airway management, and ventilation

On the day of experimentation, anesthesia was induced with inhaled isoflurane (2.5%) in an induction chamber. After loss of righting reflex, animals received intraperitoneal ketamine (45–75 mg/kg) and xylazine (5–10 mg/kg) for sustained sedation and analgesia. Animals were intubated with a 14-gauge, 2-inch catheter under direct visualization and mechanically ventilated (SAR–1000 ventilator, CWE Inc., Ardmore, PA, USA) at tidal volume 8–10 mL/kg, respiratory rate (35–45/min), positive end-expiratory pressure 5 cm H2O, and fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) set to 30–35% at baseline. Anesthesia was titrated with 1–2% isoflurane and (rectal) body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a closed-loop thermoregulated heating pad.

Instrumentation

After induction, one femoral artery was cannulated with a Millar SPR-671 (1.4F) catheter for continuous pressure monitoring, and the contralateral femoral artery was cannulated (24G needle) for arterial blood sampling. Mixed venous blood was collected via thoracotomy and opening of the pericardium followed by direct puncture of the pulmonary artery (26G needle) [20]. Sample volumes were 0.1–0.2 mL, representing minimal blood loss (30 mL estimated circulating volume in a 500-g rat), and thus were not replaced. Bleeding was controlled with a cotton swab, and no hemodynamic changes were observed during sampling. All blood gas measurements were immediately analyzed (ABL90 Flex Plus, Radiometer U.S.A.). Transabdominal NIRS (Somanetics Invos 5100C) was positioned on the abdominal wall over the left upper quadrant and secured with adhesive tape. To prevent signal interference, all rats were shaved prior to sensor placement and the abdominal area was cleaned with medical-grade ethanol wipes. After instrumentation, animals were allowed to stabilize for 20 min prior to baseline data acquisition.

Resonance Raman spectroscopy system

We further developed a previously described RRS system [19, 20] to incorporate optical fibers into a flexible 4 mm diameter linear probe designed for insertion into the esophagus. The probe was advanced into the proximal cervical esophagus under direct visualization (Fig. 1A-B). The system uses 405 nm excitation near the Soret band, enabling resonance enhancement of porphyrin vibrational modes and discrimination between oxy- and deoxyhemoglobin via the ν4 Raman band (Fig. 1C). Results were displayed on a portable bedside system (Fig. 1D) and averaged per 60 s for analysis.

Experimental protocols

Progressive hemorrhagic shock (n=12)

After a 10-min baseline period, controlled phlebotomy was performed via the femoral arterial catheter to reduce mean arterial blood pressure (MABP) to 35 mmHg, designated as timepoint 0. MABP was then sequentially reduced to 30, 25, 20, and 15 mmHg. Each target MABP was maintained for 20 min before sampling by withdrawing small aliquots of blood or administering saline, after which the pulmonary artery (PA) was sampled for SvO2 and lactate analysis. Hemorrhage was continued until MABP reached 15 mmHg or until death, defined as pulse pressure (systolic–diastolic pressure) <5 mmHg. Hemodynamic parameters, transabdominal NIRS and SEsO2 were recorded continuously. For analyses, SEsO2 values were averaged over the 2 min immediately preceding each SvO2 and lactate sample (Fig. 2).

Experimental timelines for progressive hemorrhage and hypoxic ventilation protocols. Model 1: Hemorrhage (n = 12): MABP reduced via phlebotomy in 5 mmHg steps to a terminal level of 15 mmHg; each stage was maintained 20 min before sampling of SvO2 and lactate. Model 2: Hypoxia (n = 9): FiO2 was reduced stepwise from 100 to 5%. Each stage was maintained for 10 min. Shaded areas indicate the 2-min steady-state windows used for data acquisition prior to each sampling stage

Hypoxic ventilation model (n=9)

After baseline stabilization at 100% FiO2, FiO2 was reduced stepwise to 70%, 50%, 35%, 30%, 25%, 20%, 15%, 10%, and 5%. Each FiO2 level was maintained for 10 min prior to arterial and pulmonary artery sampling. Hemodynamic parameters, transabdominal NIRS and SEsO2 were recorded continuously. Like in the hemorrhage model, SEsO2 values were averaged over the 2 min preceding each SvO2 measurement for analysis (Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in Prism (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) and Python (v.3.9). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SEM and two-sided p < 0.05 was considered significant. Per-stage changes in the hemorrhage and hypoxia models were evaluated with one-way repeated-measures ANOVA and Holm-Sidak post-hoc analysis. Separate sensitivity analyses for hemorrhage were conducted by repeating primary analysis for stages with 10 animals (MABP ≥ 25 mmHg) and comparison of baseline (MABP ≈ 60 mmHg) values between completers vs non-completers of the protocol by Welch’s t-test. The primary outcome (SEsO2–SvO2 relationship) was analyzed using linear mixed-effects (LME) models, with SEsO2 as a fixed effect and animal identity as a random intercept; all available observations were included. Model assumptions were assessed visually with residuals-versus-fitted and Q–Q plots (Supplemental 2). An analogous LME model was fit for lactate, with SEsO2 as the fixed effect. For transabdominal NIRS, an LME model was fit with NIRS as the fixed effect and SEsO2 as the outcome, again with animal identity as a random intercept. Pearson correlation coefficients are also reported as descriptive summaries of bivariate associations of other recorded parameters.

A formal agreement analysis (e.g., Bland–Altman) was not performed because SEsO2 and SvO2 sample distinct physiological compartments (regional esophageal capillary versus global mixed-venous blood) with an expected offset, meaning absolute agreement is not expected. Consistency of tracking across animals is visualized by within-animal trajectory plots (Supplemental 3). The diagnostic performance for severe oxygen deficit (SvO2 < 40%, based on established associations with anaerobic metabolism and adverse clinical outcomes [21]) was assessed at the animal level using cluster bootstrap receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with 2,000 resampling iterations. Optimal cut-offs were identified using Youden’s index, and corresponding sensitivity and specificity are reported (Supplemental 5).

Results

Animal preparation and protocol completion

Progressive hemorrhage was initiated in 12 animals and 3 animals survived down to MABP 15 mmHg, 8 to MABP 20 mmHg, and 10 to MABP 25 mmHg, respectively. The baseline SEsO2 and SvO2 did not differ significantly between animals completing the full protocol (n = 3) and those that did not (n = 9). The baseline SEsO2 was 72.4 ± 7.6% vs. 74.4 ± 11.9% (p = 0.75, Welch’s t-test), baseline SvO2 56.6 ± 3.1% vs. 54.3 ± 9.2% (p = 0.54) and baseline lactate 0.8 ± 0.3 vs. 1.2 ± 0.6 mmol/L (p = 0.16) were not significantly different, suggesting that early mortality was related to individual hemodynamic tolerance rather than systematic baseline differences. There was no mortality during instrumentation phase. Hypoxic ventilation was initiated in 9 animals and completed to FiO2 of 10% in 6 animals. Intra-experimental survival is presented per-stage on the x-axis of Figs. 3 and 4. Across both experimental paradigms, baseline SEsO2 values exceeded pulmonary artery SvO2 by approximately 18–22%, consistent with a regional capillary measurement distinct from SvO2.

Progressive hemorrhagic shock

Hemodynamic and oxygenation responses

Controlled phlebotomy resulted in stepwise reductions of MABP from baseline 60 ± 0.0 mmHg to 15.1 ± 0.4 mmHg (p < 0.0001). SvO2 decreased from 53.8 ± 2.1% at baseline to 12.4 ± 1.5% at the final hemorrhage stage 15 mmHg (p < 0.0001). SEsO2 decreased from 72.1 ± 2.8% at baseline to 3.1 ± 0.6% at MABP 15 mmHg (p < 0.0001). SaO2 remained within normal physiological range throughout hemorrhage (baseline 99.4 ± 0.2% vs 99.6 ± 0.1% at final stage, p = 0.042). Although RM-ANOVA yielded a nominally significant p-value (p = 0.042), the absolute change was clinically negligible (< 1%).

Transabdominal NIRS measurements changed mildly with increasing severity of shock (45.0 ± 2.7% at baseline to 29.8 ± 3.4% at MABP 15 mmHg). Serum lactate increased from 1.1 ± 0.2 mmol/L at baseline to 8.4 ± 1.1 mmol/L at MABP 15 mmHg (p < 0.0001), indicating a metabolic response to shock. One-way RM-ANOVA demonstrated overall significance of experimental conditions on all physiological variables except transabdominal NIRS (p < 0.05 for all), with differences from baseline apparent at the initial stage (35–30 mmHg, Holm–Sidak) (Fig. 3A).

Associations of SEsO2 with SvO2, NIRS, and lactate

In the hemorrhage model, SEsO2 was positively associated with SvO2 (Fig. 3B), corresponding to a 0.53 percentage point increase in SvO2 for each 1 percentage point increase in SEsO2 (β = 0.53, 95% CI 0.43–0.62; p < 0.0001) based on 58 observations from 12 animals in a linear mixed-effects model with animal as a random intercept. SEsO2 was also positively associated with transabdominal NIRS (Fig. 3C), corresponding to a 3.06 percentage point increase in SEsO2 for each 1 percentage point increase in NIRS (β = 3.06, 95% CI 2.09–4.02; p < 0.0001) based on 55 observations from 11 animals, although substantial scatter was observed. SEsO2 was inversely associated with lactate (Fig. 3D), corresponding to a 0.15 mmol/L decrease in lactate for each 1 percentage point increase in SEsO2 (β = −0.15, 95% CI −0.18 to −0.12; p < 0.0001), based on 53 observations from 12 animals.

Diagnostic performance

Severe oxygen deficit was defined a priori as SvO2 <40% for ROC analysis. SEsO2 discriminated this state with an AUC of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.95–1.00; 2,000 iterations resampled at the animal level; n = 58 observations). The optimal SEsO2 cutoff identified by Youden’s index was < 43.9%, yielding 89.4% sensitivity, 100% specificity, 100% PPV, and 68.8% NPV. Transabdominal NIRS yielded an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI 0.70 – 0.95, p = 0.0021; n = 56 observations). (Fig. 3E, supplemental 5).

Correlation analysis

SEsO2 demonstrated strong positive correlations with SvO2 (r = 0.81, p < 0.0001) and MABP (r = 0.86, p < 0.0001), alongside a robust inverse association with serum lactate (r = − 0.74, p < 0.0001). There was no apparent correlation between SEsO2 and SaO2 (r = 0.13, p = 0.33) (Fig. 3F).

Hypoxic ventilation model

Oxygenation responses

Stepwise reduction in FiO2 resulted in proportional decreases in arterial and venous oxygen saturation. SvO2 decreased from 49.8 ± 4.2% at baseline to 4.6 ± 0.5% at the lowest FiO2 (p < 0.001). SEsO2 decreased from 82.8 ± 3.0% to 14.6 ± 5.9% across the protocol (p < 0.001). Serum lactate increased progressively from 0.68 ± 0.09 mmol/L to 8.6 ± 1.5 mmol/L (p < 0.001). Both SEsO2 and SvO2 showed significant divergence from baseline at the earliest titration stage, whereas transabdominal NIRS did not reach significance (p = 0.057) (Fig. 4A). Average SaO2 measured invasively in the femoral artery was 90.9 ± 8.2% and dropped down to 47.1 ± 14.2% at the lowest stage of hypoxia reached in each rat (Fig. 4B).

Associations of SEsO2 with SvO2, NIRS, and lactate

In the hypoxia model, SEsO2 was positively associated with SvO2 (Fig. 4C), corresponding to a 0.51 percentage-point increase in SvO2 for each 1-percentage point increase in SEsO2 (β = 0.51, 95% CI 0.43–0.60; p < 0.0001), based on 65 observations from 9 animals. SEsO2 was also positively associated with transabdominal NIRS (Fig. 4D), corresponding to a 3.38 percentage point increase in SEsO2 for each 1-percentage point increase in NIRS (β = 3.38, 95% CI 2.17–4.58; p < 0.0001), based on 32 observations from 4 animals, with substantial scatter present. SEsO2 was inversely associated with lactate (Fig. 4E), corresponding to a 0.11 mmol/L decrease in lactate for each 1-percentage point increase in SEsO2 (β = −0.11, 95% CI −0.13 to −0.09; p < 0.0001), based on 59 observations from 8 animals.

Diagnostic performance

Using the a priori threshold of SvO2 < 40%, SEsO2 identified severe oxygen deficit with an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.82–1.00; n = 65 observations from 9 animals). The optimal SEsO2 cutoff was < 65.0% (Youden’s index), yielding 70.7% sensitivity, 100% specificity, 100% PPV, and 29.2% NPV. Transabdominal NIRS showed limited ability to detect severe hypoxia (AUC 0.56, 95% CI: 0.23–1.00; n = 32, available for n = 4 animals) (Fig. 4F, Supplemental 5).

Correlation analysis

SEsO2 and SvO2 demonstrated strong Pearson correlation (r = 0.82, p < 0.0001). SEsO2 demonstrated positive correlation with MABP (r = 0.82, p < 0.0001) and inverse correlation with serum lactate (r = −0.71, p < 0.0001). SEsO2​ also exhibited a significant positive correlation with arterial saturation (SaO2, r = 0.68, p < 0.0001), accurately reflecting the systemic desaturation inherent to this experimental protocol (Fig. 4G).

Sensitivity analysis for hemorrhage model MABP ≥ 25 mmHg

To evaluate whether the primary findings were driven by the small number of animals surviving to the most severe hemorrhage stages, all analyses were repeated using only stages at which ≥10 animals contributed data (MABP ≥ 25 mmHg; n = 48 observations from 12 animals). SEsO2 remained strongly correlated with SvO2 (r = 0.79, p < 0.0001). The LME slope estimate was β = 0.56 (95% CI 0.45–0.67, p < 0.0001), consistent with the full model estimate (β = 0.53). ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.93–1.00) for detection of SvO2 < 40%, with an optimal SEsO2 cutoff of < 60.7% (sensitivity 97.3%, specificity 90.9%). These results confirm that the observed associations are not driven by the limited number of animals at the most severe stages.

Discussion

We have shown that SEsO2 tracks changes in SvO2 across two physiologically distinct models of systemic oxygen deficit. In both hemorrhagic and hypoxic shock, SEsO2 showed strong association with SvO2 and inverse association with serum lactate, supporting its relevance as a marker of impaired oxygen delivery-consumption balance. In addition, SEsO2 demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for identifying severe oxygen deficit, defined as SvO2 < 40%. Together, these findings suggest that esophageal Raman spectroscopy provides a continuous, minimally invasive measurement of esophageal capillary hemoglobin saturation that reflects physiologically meaningful changes in systemic oxygen balance.

A key implication of our findings is that the primary value of esophageal Raman spectroscopy lies in continuous trend monitoring rather than point estimation of SvO2. At baseline, SEsO2 was approximately 20 percentage points higher than SvO2, consistent with measurement in a regional esophageal capillary bed rather than in true mixed venous blood. This offset was not maintained across all stages. During severe hemorrhage, SEsO2 fell to below SvO2. Its clinical value therefore lies not in absolute agreement with SvO2 (it is not a direct substitute) and more in longitudinal tracking of oxygen imbalance. In many clinical settings, intermittent measurements of central venous saturation are used to guide resuscitation, but these approaches are limited by their episodic nature and dependence on invasive access. In contrast, SEsO2 provides a continuous signal that reflects evolving changes in oxygen delivery and consumption. In both models, SEsO2 declined in parallel with worsening physiologic stress and increasing lactate, suggesting that it captures early deterioration in systemic oxygen balance.

The observed differences in performance between SEsO2 and transabdominal NIRS are likely explained by their underlying measurement principles and the NIRS application used here. NIRS relies on absorbance-based methods that generate a composite signal influenced by arterial, venous, and intracellular chromophores, including myoglobin, and require assumptions regarding vascular compartment contributions [13]. These factors have been shown to limit sensitivity to extreme tissue hypoxia, whether by trend or point measurement, particularly in severe shock states [13, 22]. In contrast, RRS selectively interrogates hemoglobin vibrational structure and directly distinguishes oxyhemoglobin from deoxyhemoglobin. This molecular specificity reduces contamination from non-hemoglobin chromophores and may preserve sensitivity across a wider range of oxygenation states. Consistent with this mechanism, SEsO2 demonstrated strong associations with SvO2, and numerically higher discrimination of severe oxygen deficit compared to transabdominal NIRS in both models, although in the hypoxia model NIRS was available for only four animals. The Youden cut-offs and the corresponding sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were derived and evaluated in the same small datasets. These estimates are exploratory and may be optimistic and require independent validation. Importantly, PPV and NPV also depend on the experimentally generated prevalence of SvO2 < 40%. Lastly, because NIRS was applied transabdominally in this rodent model, the resulting measurements may differ from clinical cerebral NIRS signals, which limits direct generalizability of these findings.

The esophagus represents a practical and physiologically relevant site for monitoring. It is centrally located, readily accessible, and relatively shielded from external optical interference. The esophageal microcirculation is also subject to dynamic redistribution during shock, which may enhance sensitivity to changes in systemic oxygen delivery. These features, combined with the established safety profile of esophageal instrumentation for thermometry and echocardiography, support its potential clinical feasibility [23]. Importantly, the device design may allow integration into existing clinical tools, such as enteric feeding tubes or temperature probes. This may be particularly relevant in patient populations where invasive monitoring is undesirable or impractical, including pediatric patients, individuals with limited vascular access, and patients in whom the risks of pulmonary artery or central venous catheterization outweigh the potential benefits. Future work should prioritize further pre-clinical testing of probe performance during vasoactive hemodynamic support, long-term safety, and efficacy in less-severe models of shock [24]. In parallel, device-focused development should optimize a human-scale, sterilizable probe with practical handling features.

Several limitations warrant discussion. First, the comparative transabdominal NIRS measurements were conducted on the anterior abdominal wall of a rodent. Although there is precedent for this approach [25, 26], it does differ from the more common clinical applications of NIRS on the forehead and/or flank. Second, factors such as (long-term) probe positioning, mucosal integrity, secretions, and motion may influence signal quality. Systemic factors, including anemia, vasoactive medications, and alterations in regional blood flow, may also affect the relationship between SEsO2 and global oxygenation. Third, a formal agreement analysis was not performed, as SEsO2 and SvO2 reflect distinct physiological compartments and measurement techniques; accordingly, analyses prioritized the evaluation of dynamic tracking and temporal correlation rather than absolute interchangeability. Fourth, the effective sample size decreased at the most severe stages of both models because mortality is inherent to lethal shock protocols. While in additional analyses to account for attrition bias similar trends were observed, these sparse data at the most extreme stages may still limit generalizability. Fifth, diagnostic performance was strongest in advanced shock. Whether SEsO2 can effectively detect early imbalance in less severe models requires further investigation.

Conclusions

Esophageal RRS offers minimally invasive, molecularly specific measurement of regional capillary hemoglobin saturation that tracks mixed venous oxygenation in diverse shock states. These preclinical findings support further investigation of esophageal RRS as a potential minimally invasive approach for continuous monitoring of systemic oxygen imbalance in critically ill patients.
