Markov chains on trees: almost lower and upper directed cases
Luis Fredes & Jean Francois Marckert

Abstract
The transition matrix of a Markov chain ( X k , k ≥ 0 ) (X_{k},k\geq 0) on a finite or infinite rooted tree is said to be almost upper-directed if, given X k X_{k} , the node X k + 1 X_{k+1} is either a descendant of X k X_{k} or the parent of X k X_{k} . It is said to be almost lower-directed if given X k X_{k} , X k + 1 X_{k+1} is either an ancestor of X k X_{k} or a child of X k X_{k} . These models include nearest neighbor Markov chains on trees. Under an irreducibility assumption, we show that every almost upper-directed transition matrix on infinite (locally finite) trees has some invariant measures. An invariant measure π \pi is expressed thanks to a determinantal formula. We give general explicit criteria for recurrence and positive recurrence. An efficient algorithm (the leaf addition algorithm) of independent interest allows π \pi to be computed on many trees, without resorting to linear algebra considerations. Flajolet, in a series of papers (starting from [ 12 ] ), provided some relations between continuous fractions, generating functions of weighted Mötzkin paths, and used them in connection with the analysis of birth and death processes. These fruitful representations made it possible to establish many formulae for continuous fractions. Analogous considerations appear here: this type of study can be extended to weighted paths on trees, whose generating functions can also be expressed, this time in terms of multicontinuous fractions.

Introduction

A rooted tree
(t,r)
is a graph, finite or infinite, but always locally finite throughout this paper, connected and having no cycle, in which the root
r
, a node of
t
, is distinguished. In this paper, we are interested in Markov chains on trees. When the tree is finite, the study of such a Markov chain can be done with the standard toolbox of Markov chains on finite spaces. When the tree is infinite, it is generally difficult to find invariant distributions, to decide on recurrence or transience, on positive recurrence.

In the literature, one finds many studies of nearest neighbors random walks on trees, often with transition matrices depending on the degree of the current node, and very few papers discussing general models of Markov chains on trees.

The aim of this paper is to present a large class of transition matrices, much larger than the class of nearest-neighbors random walks, that are accessible to computation and analysis.
These Markov transition matrices will be called either almost upper-directed (
AUD
for short), when only transitions to descendants and the parent are allowed, or almost lower-directed (
ALD
for short), when only transitions to ancestors and to children are allowed.

At the intersection of these two classes are nearest neighbor Markov chains, probably the most studied class of Markov chains on trees. The class

Before entering into the core of the paper, we think that it is useful to say a few words about Markov chains on
\mathbb{N}

\mathbb{N}
can be seen as an infinite tree with no branching points, and many phenomena appearing on

\blacksquare

.

(

(i)

(ii)

(iii)
In the case of random walks, a transition matrix is irreducible iff every edge can be traversed both ways.

Remark 2.3

(Inherent complexity.)
.

When one deals with irreducible Markov chains on an infinite graph, all transitions are important: changing the transitions from a single state can transform a positive recurrent chain into a non-positive recurrent chain. For example, the random walk on
\mathbb{N}
, reflected at zero, with i.i.d. steps

with probability

with probability

is positive recurrent. However, if one replaces only the transition from 0 and takes instead

, the chain is no longer positive recurrent. Since invariant measures must depend algebraically on all transitions, all criteria of recurrence, all formulae providing the invariant measures (of left or right eigenvectors) must depend on all transitions. In this matter, beyond their apparent complexity, formulas such as (
2.1
), which use products of transitions along branches, or determinants of finite matrices, are the simplest formulas that allow each coefficient of the transition matrix to play a role.

2.3
Additional recurrence criteria: Trees with a finite/infinite number of ends

It may seem plausible that a Markov chain on an infinite

However, the restriction/projection of a Markov chain on a subspace is not always a Markov chain, so that some work is needed to give a meaning to this intuition, and to observe, that this “if and only if” is somehow valid when the tree has a finite number of ends; however, it may fail when the number of ends is infinite. 2.3.1

Remark 3.8

It is convenient to see the appearance of the multiplicity of invariant measures as an emergent effect allowed by ends. When one writes the invariance equations solved by the measure on a finite tree, fixing
\rho_{\varnothing}=1
, and tries to compute successively the values of

using

(the weight of the children using that of their parents), it seems that there are a lot of degrees of freedom, since, as in the proof, a linear combination of their sum

, for

Combinatorial representation of

-left eigenvectors on finite trees

In the standard computation of the unique invariant probability measure

\mu M=\mu

as the matrix obtained from

\begin{pmatrix}1&\cdots 1\end{pmatrix}^{t}

\rho\widetilde{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{1}&\cdots&\rho_{n-1}&1\end{pmatrix}

or equivalently

\displaystyle\rho(\widetilde{{\sf Id}}-\widetilde{M})=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&-1\end{pmatrix}

(3.18)

where
\widetilde{{\sf Id}}
is obtained by replacing the last diagonal coefficient of the identity matrix by zero.
The word “morally” is here because (
3.18
) is an affine system admitting a probability distribution as solution, which generates the one-dimensional vector space, solution of the linear system
\mu M=\mu
.
The non-singular system (
3.18

\rho={\sf Cste}\begin{pmatrix}\det((\widetilde{{\sf Id}}-\widetilde{M})_{*i}),1\leq i\leq n\end{pmatrix},

where
(\widetilde{{\sf Id}}-\widetilde{M})_{*i}
is obtained by replacing the
i
th row of
\widetilde{{\sf Id}}-\widetilde{M}
by
\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&-1\end{pmatrix}
. Up to the sign, it amounts to computing the determinant of the matrix obtained by the suppression of the
i
th row and last column of
(\widetilde{{\sf Id}}-\widetilde{M})_{*i}
: then one gets the same results as if, instead, the
i
th row and last column of “the initial matrix”
{{\sf Id}}-{M}
were removed, that is

\rho={\sf Cst}.\det((-1)^{n+i}({{\sf Id}}-{M})^{(i,n)}),

where
(i,n)
is here to denote the suppression of row
i
and column
n
. But in
{{\sf Id}}-{M}
, the sum of the columns is equal to the zero vector: hence, in the determinant computation, the
i
th column can be replaced by the opposite of the sum of the others; by matrix manipulation (adding columns already present to column

. After that the matrix tree theorem, provides an expansion in terms of weighted spanning trees (see e.g. Zeilberger
[
30

When

The above approach cannot be applied directly, because

However, we know that

The last column of

The main message we want to convey here is that

Continuous fraction representations also play an important role in the analysis of birth and death processes. The main technical point, discovered by Flajolet

(and pursued in a series of papers including

Let us be a bit more explicit here. A Mötzkin path of length

with

Given some weights

associated with the steps, the weight of a path

is defined to be simply
W(p)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell(p)}w_{p_{j-1},p_{j}}
, and the total weight of a set

{\cal W}(S)=\sum_{p\in S}W(p).

When

(or any other subset of it), the series

is well defined under some conditions on the weights: Of course, it may be natural to take complex or real numbers (under convergence conditions), but formal variables such as

(where

is a formal variable used to count the number of steps and

) are often preferred since the formal power series of a set of paths containing a finite number of paths of each length is well defined; this is the case, for example, for the set of all Mötzkin paths with a given starting point.

Flajolet
[
12
]
also considers non-commutative formal variables
w_{a,b}
(we will come back to this shortly in order to establish a formula of the greatest degree of generality).

5.2
A family of Markov chains on the rational numbers

Set

For each element

L\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)=\frac{a}{a+b}\quad\text{and }\quad R\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)=\frac{a+b}{b}.

Since
R(a/b)>1
and

a/b

such that

, and then

P\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)=\textbf{1}_{a>b}\frac{a-b}{b}+\textbf{1}_{a<b}\frac{a}{b-a}.

Notice that this definition does not define the ancestor of
1/1

It is possible to define a (time-homogeneous) Markov chain

\bullet

,

\bullet

X_{i+1}=P(a/b)
with probability

\bullet

(and of course, it plays the same role as a transition matrix; it is just presented differently).
Now, we claim the following (simple but fun) result:

.

Every transition family
[(r(x),\ell(x),p(x),s(x)),x\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}]

,

If we take the usual binary tree with nodes are words in the alphabet

the node

\displaystyle q(u)=f(u_{h},f(\cdots,f(u_{1},1/1)\cdots)).

Computation by leaf addition (CLA):

We start by fixing the value at the root
\rho_{\varnothing}=1
and in order to discover the value of the
\mathsf{h}
-invariant measure at a point
u
of the tree, it is sufficient to consider the path
\llbracket\varnothing,u\rrbracket
and use the leaf addition technique as follows: the path
\llbracket\varnothing,u\rrbracket=v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{|u|}
, where
v_{0}=\varnothing
and
v_{|u|}=u
and we consider the family of growing paths (which are trees)
T_{i}=\llbracket\varnothing,v_{i}\rrbracket
.
Then, by (
3.13
),

\displaystyle\rho_{u}={\sum_{v\in\llbracket\varnothing,p(u)\rrbracket}\rho_{v}{\sf U}_{v,T_{u}}}/\,{{\sf U}_{u,p(u)}}.

(6.1)

Notation
For a finite set
E
denote by
{\sf Uniform}(E)
the uniform distribution over
E

In the examples, we will use the following
expansion principle
: assume that a function
g:T\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
g(\varnothing)=1
and for
u\neq\varnothing
, the recurrence,

\displaystyle g(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\llbracket\varnothing,p(u)\rrbracket}g(v)F(v,u)

(6.2)

for some function
F:T^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.
In this case, by expansion

\displaystyle g(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(\varnothing)\sum_{\ell=1}^{|u|}\sum_{(v_{0},\cdots,v_{\ell})}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}F(v_{i-1},v_{i})

(6.3)

where the sequence
(v_{0},\cdots,v_{\ell})
starts at
v_{0}=\varnothing
, ends at
v_{\ell}=u
and is increasing along the line
\llbracket\varnothing,u\rrbracket
in the sense that
|v_{i-1}|<|v_{i}|
.
Denote by
{\sf Seq}(u,\ell)
the set of such sequences.

Example 6.1
.

[Uniform
AUD
in a finite tree]

Let
{\sf U}
be a
AUD
transition matrix on a finite tree
T
characterized as follows: if
(X_{i},i\geq 0)
is a
{\sf U}
-Markov chain, then knowing
X_{i}=u
, then
X_{i+1}
is chosen uniformly in
T_{u}\cup\{p(u)\}
for
u\neq\varnothing
, and uniformly in
T_{\varnothing}=T
if
u=\varnothing
.
We show how to retrieve the value of
\rho_{u}
from the
(CLA)
. First we set
\rho_{\varnothing}=1
and by (
6.1
), for

u\neq\varnothing
write,

\displaystyle\rho_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\llbracket\varnothing,p(u)\rrbracket}\rho_{v}F(v,u)

(6.4)

with

\displaystyle F(v,u):=\frac{|T_{u}|/(|T_{v}|+1_{v\neq\varnothing})}{1/(|T_{u}|+1)}=\frac{|T_{u}|(|T_{u}|+1)}{|T_{v}|+1_{v\neq\varnothing}},

(6.5)

and then, we can use the expansion principle for
g=\rho
with the function
F
.
Some telescopic simplifications arise using Formula (
6.3
), and we get

\displaystyle\rho_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{|T_{u}|+1}{|T_{\varnothing}|}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|u|}\sum_{(v_{0},\cdots,v_{\ell})\in{\sf Seq}(u,\ell)}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}|T_{v_{i}}|

(6.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{|T_{u}|+1}{|T_{\varnothing}|}\left[\prod_{v\in\rrbracket\varnothing,u\llbracket}\left(1+|T_{v}|\right)\right]|T_{u}|

(6.7)

this last identity can be easily checked: the expansion of
\prod_{v\in\rrbracket\varnothing,u\llbracket}\left(1+|T_{v}|\right)
gives the sum on all increasing sequences
s=(u_{m_{1}},\cdots,u_{m_{\ell}})
made of elements of
\rrbracket\varnothing,u\llbracket
, of any size
\ell

.

Example 6.2
.

[The Markov chain: with probability
p
choose a uniform descendant,otherwise the parent]

Let
T
be a finite tree, and
p\in(0,1)
a parameter.
Consider the
AUD
transition matrix
{\sf U}
defined by
{\sf U}_{\varnothing,.}=p{\sf Uniform}(T_{\varnothing})+(1-p)\delta_{\varnothing}
, and for
u\neq\varnothing
,
{\sf U}_{u,p(u)}=1-p
,
{\sf U}_{u,v}=p{\sf Uniform}(T_{u})
.

We compute the invariant measure using the
CLA
: we set
\rho_{\varnothing}=1
and consider
u\in T\backslash\{\varnothing\}
. By equation (
6.1
), we have
\rho_{u}=\sum_{v\in\llbracket\varnothing,p(u)\rrbracket}\rho_{v}F(v,u)
for

F(v,u)=\left(p/(1-p)\right)|T_{u}|/|T_{v}|
. By the expansion principle

\displaystyle\rho_{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{|u|}\sum_{(v_{0},\cdots,v_{\ell})}\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{\ell}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{|T_{v_{i}}|}{|T_{v_{i-1}}|}

(6.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{|T_{u}|}{|T_{\varnothing}|}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|u|}\binom{|u|-1}{\ell-1}\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)^{\ell}=\frac{|T_{u}|}{|T_{\varnothing}|}p(1-p)^{-|u|}

(6.9)

because the cardinality of
\left|{\sf Seq}(u,\ell)\right|
is given by the number of ways to choose
\ell-1
elements in
\rrbracket\varnothing,u\llbracket
.

Example 6.3
.

[Markov chain with a simple “distance to the root projection” in the complete
d
-ary tree]

Let
T
be an infinite complete
d
-ary tree (each node has exactly
d
descendants, for some positive integer
d
, so that the number of descendants of any node at distance
k
is
d^{k}
).

Take an almost upper triangular transition matrix
{\cal U}=({\cal U}_{a,b},a,b\geq 0)
on
\mathbb{N}
(meaning that
{\cal U}_{i,j}>0
implies that
j\geq i-1
), that we suppose irreducible .
Consider the
AUD
transition matrix
{\sf U}
defined by
{\sf U}_{\varnothing,v}={\cal U}_{0,|v|}/d^{|v|}
for all
v\in T
, and for
u\neq\varnothing
and
v\in T
,
{\sf U}_{u,p(u)}={\cal U}_{|u|,|u|-1}
,
{\sf U}_{u,v}=\textbf{1}_{v\in T_{u}}{\cal U}_{|u|,|v|}/d^{|v|-|u|}
.

The following representation can be easily checked from the invariance equations

\displaystyle\rho_{u}

\displaystyle={\rho^{\mathbb{N}}_{|u|}}{d^{-|u|}},

(6.10)

where
\rho^{\mathbb{N}}
denotes the invariant measure of
\mathcal{U}
. The result is intuitive in the sense that
\rho
corresponds to a symmetric partition of
\rho^{\mathbb{N}}_{h}
along the nodes of height
h
.
Notice that
\rho^{\mathbb{N}}
is unique and has an explicit expression since
\mathcal{U}
is an almost upper triangular transition matrix (see
[
14
]
).
However, it is important to notice that this expression corresponds to the
\sf{h}
- invariant measure of the matrix
{\sf U}
but it does not give any hint on how to obtain the set of all left eigenvectors, which in this case has infinite dimension, i.e.
Q(T)=\infty
. The uniqueness of the left eigenvectors for
\mathcal{U}
tells us that for the other left eigenvectors
\nu

{\sf U}
, there exists some level

, such that

is not constant on the set of nodes

at that level. This is consistent with the content of
Section

3.2.1

Example 6.4
.

[Jump to the leaves or to the parent]

Take an infinite
d
-ary tree, i.e. each node has d children or none, with a countable number of ends and a transition matrix given by

{\sf U}_{u,p(u)}=(1-p)\textbf{1}_{u\neq\varnothing}

{\sf U}_{u,v}=\textbf{1}_{v\in\partial T,u\preceq v}d^{|v|-|u|}(p\textbf{1}_{u\neq\varnothing}+\textbf{1}_{u=\varnothing})
; i.e. with probability
1-p

p

u

d^{-1}

u
.

We use the

Example 6.5
.

[Tree with two ends, two non colinear invariant measures, “one end positive recurrent” and “one end transient”. ]

The following example is useful to understand the “projection onto ends”, and maybe to see that the positive recurrence on one end, does not reduce the dimension of the cone of positive invariant measures.

Consider the transition matrix
{\sf M}_{i,i+1}=2/3
,
M_{i,i-1}=1/3
of the simple biased Markov chain on
\mathbb{Z}
. Now, see
\mathbb{Z}
as a tree rooted at 0, with two ends,
\mathbbm{p}^{+}=(0,1,2,\cdots,)
and
\mathbbm{p}^{-}=(0,-1,-2,\cdots)
.
The two corresponding projected transition matrices are given by

\begin{array}[]{cclcclccll}{\sf M}^{\mathbbm{p}^{+}}_{i,i+1}&=&2/3,&{\sf M}^{\mathbbm{p}^{+}}_{i,i-1}&=&(1/3)1_{i>0},&{\sf M}^{\mathbbm{p}^{+}}_{i,i}&=&(1/3)\textbf{1}_{i=0},&\textrm{ for }i\geq 0\\
{\sf M}^{\mathbbm{p}^{-}}_{i,i+1}&=&(2/3)\,1_{i<0},&{\sf M}^{\mathbbm{p}^{+}}_{i,i-1}&=&1/3,&{\sf M}^{\mathbbm{p}^{+}}_{i,i}&=&(2/3)\,\textbf{1}_{i=0},&\textrm{ for }i\leq 0.\end{array}

The standard theory of Markov chains, or the use of criteria for birth and death processes, allows us to see that
{\sf M}^{\mathbbm{p}+}
is transient,
{\sf M}^{\mathbbm{p}^{-}}
is positive recurrent. From
3.7
, we know that the left 1-eigenspace of
{\sf M}
has dimension 2. One of the invariant measures is
\rho=(1,1\in\mathbb{Z})
. To find a second positive invariant measure
\rho^{\prime}=(\rho^{\prime}_{k},k\in\mathbb{Z})
, fix
\rho^{\prime}_{0}=1
, and
\rho^{\prime}_{1}=1+x
, and solve the system
\rho^{\prime}_{j}=(2/3)\rho_{j-1}^{\prime}+(1/3)\rho^{\prime}_{j+1}
, one finds:

\rho^{\prime}_{j}=1-(1-2^{j})x.

For any
x\in(0,1]
, this measure is positive (and linearly independent of
\rho
).

Example 6.6
.

[Infinite number of ends: transient Markov chain with infinite cone of invariant measures where all the chains associated with each end are recurrent]

Here we give an example of a
{\sf M}
-random walk on the complete binary tree
T=\cup_{k\geq 0}\{0,1\}^{k}
where the set of invariant measures is an infinite dimensional cone. This multiplicity implies that
{\sf M}
is transient, while, each chain associated with each end (as defined in
Section

2.3.1
) is recurrent.
In the tree
T
all nodes have degree 3 except the root which has degree 2.

Consider the transition matrix of the simple random walk
{\sf M}
on
T
, defined by
{\sf M}_{u,p(u)}={\sf M}_{p(u),u}=1/3
for all
u\neq\varnothing
, and
{\sf M}_{\varnothing,1}={\sf M}_{\varnothing,0}={\sf M}_{\varnothing,\varnothing}=1/3
.

For each end
\mathbbm{p}
identified with
\mathbb{N}
, the transition matrix
{\sf M}^{\mathbbm{p}}
(see
Section

2.3.1
) is a birth and death process with transition matrix
K
with entries
K_{0,0}=2/3
,
K_{0,1}=1/3
and
K_{i,i-1}=K_{i,i}=K_{i,i+1}=1/3
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
. Hence each
{\sf M}^{\mathbbm{p}}
is recurrent.

Now, we claim that for all
x\in[0,1/2]
, there exists an invariant measure
\rho^{(x)}=\left(\rho_{u}^{(x)},u\in T\right)
, that is solution to
\rho{\sf M}=\rho
, and such that (near the root),

\displaystyle\left(\rho^{(x)}_{\varnothing},\rho^{(x)}_{0},\rho^{(x)}_{1}\right)=\big(1,1+x,1-x\big).

(6.12)

In fact, the
h
-invariant measure of
{\sf U}
is the constant measure
(1,u\in T)
; near the root, it coincides with the case
x=0
, given in (
6.12
).

Once the values (
6.12
) are fixed, in order to construct
(\rho^{(x)}_{u},u\in T)
invariant by
{\sf M}
, it suffices to fix some
\rho^{(x)}_{u}
such that each
\rho^{(x)}_{v}
is the mean of its neighboring values
\rho^{(x)}_{w}
(for
v\neq\varnothing
). A rapid inspection shows that there are many solutions to this problem! It is less easy to find positive solutions.

Let us choose the same weight for siblings
\rho^{(x)}_{u0}=\rho^{(x)}_{u1}
for all
u
at depth at least 2: eventually, all strict descendants
u
of
0
such that
|u|=k
will have weight
\rho_{u}^{(x)}=a_{k}
, all descendants
u
of
1
, such that
|u|=k
will have weight
\rho_{u}^{(x)}=b_{k}
for some sequence
(a_{k})
and
(b_{k})
that we now determine.

We thus have
a_{1}=1+x
and
b_{1}=1-x
. Let us additionally set
a_{0}=b_{0}=1
, which is consistent with
\rho_{\varnothing}^{(x)}=1
.

Control of the sign.

In order that
\rho^{(x)}
satisfies the balance equation at a node
u
at depth
|u|=k\geq 1
, in the subtree
T_{0}
, we need

2a_{k+1}+a_{k-1}=3a_{k}
; this gives
a_{k}=1+\frac{2^{k}-1}{2^{k-1}}x

(they are positive, and even, increasing). While in the right subtree
T_{1}
, by the same reasoning, we need (for
k\geq 1
),
2b_{k+1}+b_{k-1}=3b_{k}
, which gives
b_{k}=1-\frac{2^{k}-1}{2^{k-1}}x

and we see that this is positive for all
x\in[0,1/2]
.

To produce other (linearly independent solutions): it suffices to make a bit more abstract what has been done. For any node
u
, the measure

\rho^{(u)}=\big(\rho^{(u)}_{v},v\in T\big)=\big(\textbf{1}_{v\in T_{u0}}-\textbf{1}_{v\in T_{u1}},v\in T\big)

that is
\rho^{(u)}_{v}=1
(resp. -1) iff
v
a strict left (resp. right) descendant of
u
, and zero otherwise.
Clearly, the measures
(\rho^{(u)},u\in T)
are linearly independent. Moreover, all measures of the type

\mu:=\pi+\sum_{u\in T}\lambda_{u}\rho^{(u)}

for some constants
(\lambda_{u})\in\mathbb{C}^{T}
are well defined and invariant by
{\sf U}
: indeed, the value
\mu_{v}
can be expressed as the finite sum
1+\sum_{u\in\llbracket\varnothing,v\rrbracket}\lambda_{u}.{\sf Sign}(u,v)
with a
{\sf Sign}(u,v)
being
+1
or
-1
depending on whether
v
is in the left or right subtree of
u
and
0
if it does not belong to either of these trees. But, in any case, by taking the
(\lambda_{v},v\in T)
real numbers small enough so that along each end
\mathbbm{p}
,
\sum_{v\in\mathbbm{p}}|\lambda_{v}|<1
, the (row vector) measure
\mu
has all its coordinates positive; this means that the cone of invariant positive measures is infinite dimensional.
