Academic rank and stress among university faculty in a medical university in Yunnan, China
Authors: Ying Chen, Zhen Wan, Siyan Zhou, Huajun Tang, Hongyu Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOccupational stress is a persistent challenge for university faculty, with important implications for well-being and professional performance. This study examined rank-related differences in perceived stress and identified key sources of stress among university faculty.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in March 2024 among university faculty, yielding 269 valid responses. Specific stress sources were assessed using single-item indicators on a 5-point Likert scale, and perceived stress was measured using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) and categorized into low, moderate, and high stress. Multivariable regression analyses were performed to examine associations between stress sources, academic rank, and perceived stress.ResultsProfessors reported the lowest perceived stress levels (mean [SD]: 21.2 [4.6]), whereas lecturers reported the highest levels (24.9 [6.1]), with significant differences across academic ranks (p = 0.001). After adjustment for other stress sources, academic performance-related stress was independently associated with higher academic rank, with increased relative risk ratios among professors (RRR = 2.63, 95% CI: 1.36–5.05), associate professors (RRR = 3.07, 95% CI: 1.95–4.84), and lecturers (RRR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.67–3.89), compared with teaching assistants.Among faculty with high perceived stress, interpersonal relationship stress (coefficient = 0.78, p = 0.014) and academic performance stress (coefficient = 0.66, p = 0.011) were significantly associated with higher PSS scores.ConclusionsPerceived stress levels and dominant stressors varied substantially across academic ranks. Introduction

Occupational stress is a pervasive issue in academia, with significant implications for faculty performance, job satisfaction, and psychological well-being. University faculty commonly face heavy workloads, high expectations in teaching and research, and increasing administrative responsibilities [1]. From an educational psychology perspective, occupational stress among faculty is recognised as a multifaceted phenomenon shaped by both job-related demands and personal factors, with consequences including burnout, reduced productivity, and adverse mental health outcomes [2, 3].

Previous research has identified multiple sources of stress in academic settings. Teaching-related responsibilities, such as curriculum development and student engagement, represent a major source of stress [4]. Research demands, including publication pressure and competition for external funding, further intensify stress levels [5]. Administrative duties, career advancement challenges, and difficulties in achieving a work-life balance further exacerbate stress levels [6]. In addition, job insecurity, excessive workloads, limited institutional support, and emotional labour—particularly among teaching staff—have been identified as important stressors [7, 8].

Although demographic and occupational characteristics such as sex, years of experience, academic department, and rank have been associated with stress levels, evidence regarding how specific stressors differ across academic ranks remains limited and inconsistent. Moreover, few studies have systematically examined whether distinct stressors exert differential effects across academic ranks, particularly in medical universities and within the Chinese higher education context. Most studies treat academic rank as a background variable rather than examining rank-specific stress profiles or the relative importance of distinct stress sources at different career stages. Consequently, it remains unclear whether faculty at early, mid, and senior career stages experience qualitatively different stress patterns. Existing evidence suggests that junior faculty may experience stress related to career establishment and promotion pressures, whereas senior faculty may face stress associated with leadership responsibilities and sustaining research productivity [9, 10].

In the Chinese higher education context, recent reforms emphasizing research output, competitive funding acquisition, and performance-based evaluation have further increased academic pressure, particularly for early- and mid-career faculty [7, 11, 12]. Understanding stress within this institutional and national framework is therefore important for interpreting the findings of the present study.

Against this background, this study aimed to:

examine differences in perceived stress levels across academic ranks among faculty in a Chinese medical university;

examine differences in perceived stress levels across academic ranks among faculty in a Chinese medical university;

identify rank-specific sources of stress and assess their relative contributions to perceived stress; and

identify rank-specific sources of stress and assess their relative contributions to perceived stress; and

evaluate whether key stressors remain independently associated with perceived stress after accounting for other stress domains.

By clarifying rank-related differences in stress levels and stress sources within a specific institutional context, this study contributes to the educational psychology literature on faculty well-being and provides evidence to inform targeted, career-stage–appropriate stress management strategies in higher education.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to explore the sources of stress among university faculty and the factors influencing these factors. A literature review was conducted to develop a questionnaire aimed at identifying the sources of stress and analyzing their contributing factors. Subsequently, three experts in educational psychology and stress research reviewed the questionnaire to ensure its clarity and relevance. Following this, ten university faculty members were randomly selected for a pilot survey to gather feedback and make necessary adjustments to the content. The final version of the questionnaire is provided in the supplementary materials.

The questionnaire began with an introductory statement informing participants that their participation was entirely voluntary and that completion was not mandatory. The statement also outlined the purpose of the study and assured participants that the collected data would be used exclusively for this research. To ensure accuracy and completeness, the online survey form was configured to prevent the submission of incomplete responses.

The questionnaire consisted of three sections. The first section collected demographic information, including age, sex, department, and years of service. The second section employed the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) to assess stress levels over the past month. Each item was scored on a scale from 0 (never) to 4 (very often), with a total score range of 0 to 40. Consistent with previous studies, PSS scores were categorised into low (0–13), moderate (14–26), and high (27–40) perceived stress levels for descriptive and subgroup analyses [13]. The third section assessed specific sources of stress, including partner and family, interpersonal relationship stress, teaching or management responsibilities, academic performance, salary level, career development, and health issues. Each stress source was measured using a single item rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (no stress) to 4 (very high stress). These items were designed to capture distinct and conceptually independent stress domains. For statistical analyses, these item scores were treated as continuous variables and entered directly into the regression models (Supplementary Questionnaire).

Sample size calculation

A statistical power analysis was conducted using G*Power software to determine the minimum sample size required for detecting differences in stress levels among university faculty with varying academic ranks. The analysis was based on the following parameters: an effect size of 0.4, representing a moderate effect according to conventional benchmarks in behavioral research, an alpha level of 0.01, a statistical power of 0.95, and 4 groups. Based on this analysis, the required total sample size was calculated to be 148 participants, ensuring sufficient power (0.95) to detect a statistically significant difference among the groups. (Supplementary Fig. 1)

Data collection

A cross-sectional survey was conducted in March 2024 among university faculty using a snowball sampling approach. Initial participants were invited through institutional contacts and internal communication platforms and were encouraged to share the survey link with eligible colleagues.

Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, and written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. A total of 314 questionnaires were returned. Responses with a completion time of less than two minutes or with the same response option selected consecutively five or more times were excluded to ensure data quality. After data cleaning, 269 questionnaires were retained for analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 85.7%.

To minimise potential bias, several measures were implemented during data collection and analysis. First, although a snowball sampling approach was used, initial participants were recruited through institutional channels to improve sample diversity. Second, data quality control criteria were applied, including the exclusion of responses with extremely short completion times and patterned responses, to reduce information bias. Third, standardised questionnaire administration helped minimise measurement variability. Despite these efforts, residual selection bias and self-report bias cannot be completely excluded and are acknowledged in the limitations.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize faculty characteristics and stress-related variables. Continuous variables were reported as mean (SD) when approximately normally distributed and as median (IQR) when skewed; categorical variables were summarized as frequency (%).

The total PSS score was treated as a continuous outcome. Differences in mean PSS scores between two-group variables (sex) were assessed using an independent-samples t test. Differences across multi-level variables (age range, department, years of work, number of children, education level, marital status, academic rank, and main work responsibilities) were examined using one-way ANOVA. Effect sizes for ANOVA results were estimated using partial eta squared (partial η²).

Prior to inferential analyses, the distributional characteristics of continuous variables involved in the main outcome analyses were examined. When assumptions of approximate normality and homogeneity of variance were not adequately met, non-parametric approaches were considered where appropriate.

Multivariable multinomial logistic regression was used with academic rank as the response variable (reference group: teaching assistant). Predictor variables included the stress-source ratings (partner and family, interpersonal relationship stress, teaching or management, academic performance, salary level, career development, and health issues) measured on the 0–4 Likert scale. Effect estimates were reported as relative risk ratios (RRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Among participants classified as having high stress (PSS 27–40), a linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between stress-source variables and PSS scores. A stepwise variable selection procedure was applied using a predefined p-value threshold (p < 0.05) for entry and retention in the model.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.5). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The median age was highest among professors (50 years, IQR: 45.8–54) and lowest among lecturers (37 years, IQR: 34–44). Median years of work decreased progressively across academic ranks, from 26 years (IQR: 22.2–31.5) among professors to 4 years (IQR: 2–22) among teaching assistants.

Differences were also observed in educational attainment and main work responsibilities across academic ranks. For example, doctoral education was most common among professors (14 [70%]) and least common among teaching assistants (3 [4.3%]), whereas teaching assistants were more frequently engaged in non-teaching roles (33 [47.1%]) compared with other groups.

Stress levels by demographic characteristics

Reliability analysis of the PSS indicated a satisfactory internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81, suggesting good overall reliability (Supplementary Table 1).

Stress levels differed significantly across age groups (analysis of variance, p = 0.015), with a small-to-moderate effect size (partial η² = 0.039) (Table 2). The highest mean stress level was observed among faculty aged 35–44 years (mean [SD]: 24.8 [6.0]), whereas the lowest was found in the 45–54 age group (mean [SD]: 22.2 [4.8]).

Perceived stress levels also varied significantly across departments (analysis of variance, p = 0.024), with a moderate effect size (partial η² = 0.086). Faculty members in the Graduate School reported the highest mean stress level (mean [SD]: 25.8 [7.4]), while those in administrative departments reported the lowest (mean [SD]: 20.4 [4.8]).

Influence of academic rank on stress levels

Perceived stress levels differed significantly across academic ranks (analysis of variance, p = 0.001), with a moderate effect size (partial η² = 0.060) (Table 2). Professors reported the lowest perceived stress levels (mean [SD]: 21.2 [4.6]), whereas lecturers reported the highest levels (mean [SD]: 24.9 [6.1]). Associate professors also exhibited relatively high stress levels (mean [SD]: 24.0 [5.5]), while teaching assistants reported lower levels comparable to professors (mean [SD]: 21.8 [4.9]).

Stress profiles across academic ranks

Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 2 summarise mean scores across seven stress-source domains by academic rank. Across all groups, academic performance was the most prominent stress source, with the highest mean scores observed among associate professors (mean = 3.50, SD = 1.20) and lecturers (mean = 3.35, SD = 1.28), followed by professors (mean = 2.65, SD = 1.09) and teaching assistants (mean = 2.00, SD = 1.13).

A similar pattern was observed for career development and salary-related stress, with higher mean scores among associate professors and lecturers compared with professors and teaching assistants. Stress related to teaching or management responsibilities and health issues showed moderate levels across all academic ranks, whereas partner and family–related stress and interpersonal relationship stress were consistently lower, with limited variation between groups.

To further characterise rank-specific stress profiles, an exploratory multinomial logistic regression analysis was conducted, using teaching assistants as the reference group (Table 3). Academic performance–related stress was consistently associated with higher academic ranks, including professors (RRR = 2.63, 95% CI: 1.36–5.05), associate professors (RRR = 3.07, 95% CI: 1.95–4.84), and lecturers (RRR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.67–3.89). Salary-related stress showed inverse associations with higher academic ranks, particularly among professors and associate professors. Other stress domains did not demonstrate consistent associations across rank groups.

Relationship between high stress and stress factors

A linear regression analysis, followed by stepwise regression, was conducted on 114 faculty members experiencing high stress levels to identify the most significant factors of elevated stress. The stepwise regression analysis refined the model by excluding variables with minimal impact, leaving interpersonal relationship stress (coefficient = 0.78, p = 0.014) and academic performance (coefficient = 0.66, p = 0.011) as significant factors of high stress levels (Table 4). While health issues and salary levels did not reach statistical significance, both demonstrated a positive trend in their association with high stress levels. Results from the full-sample analysis showed broadly similar directions of association (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides insight into rank-related differences in perceived stress among university faculty and highlights how specific stressors contribute differently across academic roles. Overall, academic performance–related stress and interpersonal relationship stress emerged as key contributors to elevated stress levels, underscoring the importance of considering academic rank when interpreting stress patterns and designing institutional interventions. These findings are broadly consistent with patterns observed in other higher education contexts, where early- and mid-career faculty tend to experience higher levels of stress related to career uncertainty and performance pressures. However, the magnitude and specific sources of stress may vary depending on institutional structures and national higher education systems, highlighting the importance of contextual factors.

Variations in stress by academic ranks

Consistent with prior research, perceived stress differed significantly across academic ranks, with professors reporting the lowest stress levels and lecturers reporting the highest. While senior faculty often carry greater administrative and leadership responsibilities, higher academic rank may also confer increased autonomy, institutional support, and job security, which can buffer against stress [7, 14, 15]. In contrast, lecturers—typically at earlier career stages—face greater uncertainty related to promotion, contract stability, and performance evaluation, potentially contributing to higher stress levels [16, 17].

Notably, compared with teaching assistants, professors, associate professors, and lecturers showed stronger associations with academic performance–related stress. This pattern reflects escalating expectations for research output, teaching quality, and career advancement as academic rank increases. These findings suggest that stress is not solely a function of workload intensity, but also of evaluative pressure linked to rank-specific performance criteria.

Key stress factors and rank-specific manifestations

Academic performance stress emerged as a dominant factor associated with high perceived stress. Academic stress, closely related to research expectations, publication requirements, and the need to secure external funding, may be particularly salient for early- and mid-career faculty. Lecturers and associate professors often face strong pressure to establish research productivity and meet promotion criteria within limited time frames, frequently while balancing substantial teaching workloads. In many higher education systems, competitive research funding is a key criterion for career advancement and institutional evaluation, potentially intensifying stress and creating tension between research productivity and teaching responsibilities [18, 19].

Interpersonal relationship stress also played a significant role, although its manifestation may differ by academic rank. For lecturers, interpersonal stress may arise from frequent interactions with students, supervisors, and colleagues, as well as from navigating role ambiguity and power asymmetries early in an academic career [20]. For professors, interpersonal stress may be more closely linked to leadership roles, including mentoring, team management, and conflict resolution. These rank-specific contexts highlight the need for differentiated institutional support strategies rather than uniform stress-reduction approaches [21].

An additional noteworthy finding was the relatively lower perceived stress among teaching assistants compared with lecturers. This contrast may reflect differences in role expectations and career-stage perceptions. Teaching assistants typically face fewer pressures related to research output, grant acquisition, and promotion evaluation, which may shield them from some of the stressors experienced by lecturers [22]. This finding reinforces the importance of career stage in shaping stress experiences within academia.

Implications for policy and practice

These findings suggest that targeted institutional strategies are needed to address rank-specific stressors. Structured mentoring programs for early-career faculty may help alleviate academic performance pressure by providing guidance on research planning, teaching balance, and career progression. In addition, stress management initiatives and efforts to foster supportive workplace relationships—such as team-based collaboration and regular feedback mechanisms—may reduce interpersonal relationship stress and promote faculty well-being.

Limitations and future research

While this study provides a comprehensive analysis of stress factors among university faculty, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inferences regarding the relationships between stress factors and academic rank. Second, stress was assessed using self-reported measures, which may be subject to reporting and recall biases.

Third, the study was conducted at a single medical university, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutional settings, such as non-medical universities or institutions operating under different organizational and cultural contexts. In addition, specific stress sources were assessed using single-item indicators, which may limit measurement precision and preclude formal psychometric evaluation. Future studies could employ validated multi-item scales to further strengthen measurement robustness.

Future research could employ longitudinal designs to explore stress dynamics over time and include objective measures, such as physiological indicators, to complement self-reported data.

Conclusion

This study underscores the multifaceted nature of occupational stress among university faculty and its significant variations across academic ranks and stress factors. By identifying key factors of high stress levels, such as academic and interpersonal relationship stress, these findings offer actionable insights for universities to design effective interventions that promote faculty well-being and productivity. In particular, targeted institutional support, including structured mentoring programs for early-career faculty, may help address rank-specific stressors and foster sustainable academic development.
