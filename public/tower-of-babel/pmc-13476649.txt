Novel mitophagy activator TJ0113 restricts cytosolic mtDNA to suppress ZBP1-driven inflammation in acute lung injury
Authors: Xiaohan Chen, Shunpeng Xing, Xufeng Cen, Jikai Shen, Jiwei Zhu, Xu Lin, Le Xu, Fengqi Wang, Yanan Tian, Xuan Lai, Yuejia Luo, Hongguang Xia, Jian Hu, Luming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute lung injury (ALI) is driven by excessive inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction, but how mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release engages inflammatory signaling remains incompletely understood. Here, we demonstrate that TJ0113, a novel mitophagy activator, confers protection against LPS-induced ALI by promoting mitochondrial quality control and limiting cytosolic mtDNA accumulation. Transcriptomic and ultrastructural analyses showed that TJ0113 restored mitophagy and reduced oxidative stress. Single-cell transcriptomic profiling identified ZBP1 as the most prominently induced cytosolic nucleic acid sensor in injured lungs, revealing inflammatory alveolar macrophages as a major ZBP1-enriched population. Mechanistically, cytosolic mtDNA accumulation triggered ZBP1 activation, leading to necroptotic (MLKL) and pyroptotic (GSDMD) signaling. TJ0113 suppressed ZBP1 activation by enhancing mitophagy and reducing mtDNA release, and inhibition of mitophagy abolished its protective effects. Consistently, ZBP1 knockdown recapitulated the anti-inflammatory effects of TJ0113, as evidenced by reduced downstream inflammatory signaling and decreased cytosolic Z-NA puncta, and pharmacological mitochondrial depletion (EB) similarly attenuated the inflammatory phenotype. Our findings identify the mtDNA–ZBP1 axis as a critical link between mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation in ALI, and position TJ0113 as a promising therapeutic candidate targeting this axis.

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) and its more severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), represent life-threatening clinical conditions characterized by overwhelming pulmonary inflammation, disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier, and subsequent pulmonary edema [1]. Despite advances in supportive care, the mortality rate remains alarmingly high at 30–50%, and no effective pharmacotherapy targeting the underlying pathogenesis is currently available [2,3]. The complexity of ALI/ARDS, involving multiple cell types and interconnected mechanisms such as inflammatory cell infiltration, oxidative stress, and barrier dysfunction, has hindered the development of specific treatments [[4], [5], [6]]. Therefore, elucidating the cellular and molecular mechanisms that drive ALI is urgently needed to identify novel therapeutic targets and develop effective intervention strategies.

Increasing evidence highlights mitochondrial quality control as a central rheostat of the innate immune response [5,7]. In the context of ALI, severe oxidative stress precipitates the structural collapse of mitochondria, leading to the pathological extrusion of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytosol [8]. This cytosolic mtDNA functions as a highly immunogenic damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) [9,10]. While multiple cytosolic nucleic acid sensors are known to exist, the dominant regulatory node that translates mtDNA leakage into explosive inflammatory signaling cascades during acute lung injury remains poorly defined [11]. ALI is characterized by robust activation of necroptotic and pyroptotic pathways, which synergistically amplify inflammation and disrupt the pulmonary barrier [12,13]. Directly targeting these downstream executioners poses pharmacological challenges and risks of immunosuppression. Thus, neutralizing the upstream trigger by restoring mitochondrial fitness to prevent mtDNA leakage represents a more viable therapeutic strategy.

Mitophagy selectively clears damaged mitochondria and serves as a key guardian of cellular homeostasis by eliminating mtROS and cytosolic mtDNA [14]. However, its role in lung disease is a "double-edged sword". While physiological mitophagy is cytoprotective, excessive or sustained activation can precipitate a bioenergetic crisis through the non-selective depletion of healthy mitochondria, thereby exacerbating tissue damage [15,16]. Crucially, during severe LPS-induced ALI, this delicate quality-control mechanism is often aberrantly suppressed. Patoli et al. demonstrated that LPS challenge inhibits PINK1-dependent mitophagy in macrophages, leading to an accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria and robust mtDNA release that drives the inflammatory cascade [17]. Therefore, developing pharmacological agents to precisely restore homeostatic mitophagy—without overstepping into cytotoxicity—represents a vital strategy to short-circuit mtDNA-driven inflammation while preserving cellular viability.

TJ0113 is a highly selective small-molecule mitophagy activator that has recently achieved a major clinical milestone [18,19]. In January 2026, it reported positive top-line results from a Phase II trial (NCT06596005) in early-stage Parkinson's disease, successfully meeting its primary endpoint with statistically significant improvements in motor function. Beyond its robust neuroprotective efficacy, TJ0113 exhibits broad therapeutic potential across a spectrum of inflammatory and ischemic disorders. Indeed, our prior work has demonstrated that it effectively reduces myeloid cell activation in lupus nephritis and limits downstream NF-κB and inflammasome signaling in ALI [20,21]. Despite these well-documented anti-inflammatory benefits, the precise upstream mechanisms by which TJ0113 orchestrates innate immunity remain incompletely understood. Specifically, whether TJ0113 acts at the fundamental source—by restricting mtDNA leakage and silencing upstream cytosolic nucleic acid sensing networks—has yet to be fully elucidated.

Here, we define a mitochondrial-inflammatory signaling axis in ALI and demonstrate that TJ0113 protects against LPS-induced lung injury by restoring mitochondrial fitness. Through unbiased transcriptomic profiling of lung tissues, we identify ZBP1 as the most strongly upregulated cytosolic nucleic acid sensor in response to mtDNA leakage. High-resolution single-cell RNA sequencing further reveals alveolar macrophages (AMs) as the primary cellular hub where this mtDNA-ZBP1 axis operates. Mechanistically, TJ0113 enhances PINK1-dependent mitophagy, clears damaged mitochondria, restricts cytosolic mtDNA accumulation, and subsequently silences ZBP1-driven inflammatory signaling in AMs. Collectively, our findings establish the mtDNA-ZBP1 axis as a critical amplifier of ALI and provide a mechanistic rationale for TJ0113 as an upstream therapeutic intervention.

Results

TJ0113 alleviates LPS-induced acute lung injury

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of TJ0113 in vivo, we employed a murine model of ALI induced by intratracheal LPS instillation (Fig. 1a). Macroscopic examination revealed severe pulmonary congestion, extensive hemorrhage, and consolidation in LPS-challenged mice. These gross pathological changes were visibly mitigated by TJ0113 administration (Fig. 1b). Histological assessment via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining further demonstrated that TJ0113 protected pulmonary architecture, significantly reducing LPS-induced alveolar wall thickening, interstitial edema, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Consistent with the preservation of tissue structure, TJ0113 also protected alveolar-capillary barrier function. LPS challenge caused a substantial increase in total protein concentration in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)—a primary indicator of vascular leakage—which was significantly restricted by TJ0113 treatment (Fig. 1e). Parallel to this barrier protection, the robust local and systemic inflammatory responses triggered by LPS were efficiently blunted. TJ0113 suppressed the hyper-secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6, in both BALF and serum (Fig. 1f and g), and similarly downregulated their mRNA transcription, along with Mcp1, in lung tissues (Fig. 1h).

Ultimately, to determine whether these histological and immunological improvements confer a definitive survival advantage, we monitored mouse lethality under a continuous intervention protocol (Fig. 1i). While LPS administration was highly lethal, TJ0113 treatment significantly delayed mortality and improved the overall survival rate of the ALI mice (Fig. 1j). Collectively, these data establish that TJ0113 provides robust in vivo protection against LPS-induced lung injury and systemic inflammation.

Pharmacological mitophagy enhancement restores mitochondrial quality control in vivo

Initially, to capture global transcriptomic shifts across the entire pulmonary microenvironment, we conducted a condition-level differential expression analysis integrating all captured cells. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that LPS challenge profoundly suppressed gene signatures associated with energy metabolism, oxidation-reduction processes, and essential mitochondrial components. We first verified the in vivo pro-mitophagic action of TJ0113 in the injured lung tissues. Western blot analysis demonstrated that TJ0113 treatment increased the LC3B-II/I ratio and correspondingly decreased the expression of the outer mitochondrial membrane protein TOM20 (Fig. 2b, Fig. S1a). This autophagic signature was accompanied by the accumulation of PINK1 and a reduction in PARKIN levels, indicating the robust activation of PINK1-PARKIN-mediated mitophagy (Fig. 2b, Fig. S1a). This activation was further corroborated by the elevated levels of phosphorylated ubiquitin (p-Ub) following TJ0113 intervention (Fig. S3a). Corroborating these biochemical alterations, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed a significant increase in the abundance of mitophagic vacuoles engulfing damaged mitochondria in the lungs of TJ0113-treated mice (Fig. 2c and d).

Because structurally compromised mitochondria are major generators of pathological reactive oxygen species (ROS) [16,26], we assessed pulmonary oxidative stress. Fluorescence staining indicated that TJ0113 treatment significantly blunted LPS-induced ROS accumulation (Fig. 2e–g) and subsequently reduced oxidative damage to mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), as indicated by decreased 8-OHdG staining (Fig. 2f and g). Furthermore, TJ0113 modulated mitochondrial dynamics; it downregulated the expression of the mitochondrial fission factor DRP1 and slightly increased the fusion protein MFN1, while OPA1 levels remained largely unaltered (Fig. 2b). Finally, high-resolution TEM ultrastructural analysis confirmed that LPS-induced mitochondrial catastrophes—including severe swelling, membrane rupture, and loss of cristae—were substantially prevented by TJ0113, which effectively preserved cristae surface density and overall organelle integrity (Fig. 2h and i). Together, these findings indicate that TJ0113 enforces mitochondrial quality control through efficient mitophagy activation, thereby shielding the lung from mitochondrial oxidative damage and structural collapse.

Restriction of cytosolic mtDNA leakage silences ZBP1-driven inflammatory signaling

Given our preceding observation that TJ0113 physically preserves mitochondrial structural integrity (Fig. 2), we hypothesized that this robust quality control would prevent the pathological extrusion of mitochondrial contents into the cytosol. Since cytosolic mtDNA acts as a potent inflammatory trigger during ALI, we evaluated its subcellular localization. Immunofluorescence co-staining was performed for TOM20 and Z-NA—a conformation-specific marker for immunogenic Z-form nucleic acids predominantly associated with stressed mtDNA [27]. As anticipated, LPS stimulation provoked a massive accumulation of cytosolic Z-NA signals outside the mitochondrial network. To identify the sensor responding to mtDNA leakage, we assessed the expression of nucleic acid sensors in our scRNA-seq dataset. Among these genes, Zbp1 showed the most pronounced upregulation following LPS stimulation (Fig. 3b). Although TJ0113 only partially reversed Zbp1 transcription, ZBP1 requires ligand binding for activation, rather than just protein overexpression. Thus, we reasoned that by clearing cytosolic mtDNA, TJ0113 removes the essential activating ligand, effectively shutting down ZBP1 signaling regardless of residual ZBP1 protein levels [28].

Biochemical evaluation further validated this mechanistic link. Western blot analysis revealed that LPS stimulation upregulated ZBP1 expression and concurrently activated its downstream inflammatory cascades, as evidenced by the phosphorylation of RIPK3 and MLKL, alongside the cleavage of GSDMD (GSDMD-N) and the accumulation of NLRP3 (Fig. 3c–f). Notably, TJ0113 treatment effectively blunted the activation of these signaling pathways. Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed that the elevated ZBP1 expression in LPS-challenged lung tissues was significantly attenuated by TJ0113 administration (Fig. 3d–g). Collectively, these results demonstrate that by restricting cytosolic mtDNA leakage, TJ0113 deprives the ZBP1 sensor of its activating ligand, thereby curtailing the subsequent inflammatory cascade.

To pinpoint the cellular origin of the ZBP1-driven inflammatory cascade, we evaluated Zbp1 expression across all identified clusters. Notably, alveolar macrophages (AMs)—the resident immune sentinels that orchestrate early lung inflammation [29]—exhibited the most pronounced upregulation of Zbp1 following LPS challenge. This robust induction was markedly reversed by TJ0113 treatment (Fig. 4b). While LPS-induced Zbp1 elevation was detectable across multiple ALI-relevant lineages, including neutrophils and epithelial cells, comparative analysis of nucleic acid sensors confirmed that this transcriptomic shift was most prominent in AMs. Crucially, TJ0113 substantially attenuated this sensor upregulation specifically within the AM population (Fig. 4c).

To validate these transcriptomic findings in situ, we evaluated ZBP1 expression alongside the general macrophage marker F4/80 on lung sections. Consistent with the overall transcriptomic trend, LPS challenge markedly increased the abundance of ZBP1+/F4/80+ macrophages, whereas TJ0113 administration effectively reduced this co-localization (Fig. 4d and e). While F4/80 is a well-established pan-macrophage marker widely used to localize the overall macrophage population in mouse tissues, it does not distinguish between resident alveolar macrophages and recruited monocyte-derived subsets [30]. Nevertheless, our scRNA-seq analysis unambiguously identified that the Zbp1 upregulation was overwhelmingly confined to the AM cluster. Thus, these data confirm the robust induction of ZBP1 within the macrophage compartment, which, based on our single-cell profiling, is predominantly driven by the AM population.

Finally, we interrogated the scRNA-seq dataset to confirm whether TJ0113 preserves mitochondrial fitness specifically within this AM subset. Pathway scoring and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that LPS stimulation severely downregulated gene signatures related to mitochondrial respiratory function while concurrently upregulating inflammatory pathways in AMs (Fig. 4f and g). In agreement with our whole-tissue biochemical data, TJ0113 intervention effectively rescued these mitochondrial functional networks and suppressed the inflammatory signatures within AMs. Collectively, these findings identify AMs as the primary cellular hub where TJ0113 restores mitochondrial quality control to restrict ZBP1-mediated inflammation.

In LPS-stimulated MH-S cells, TJ0113 dose-dependently increased the LC3B-II/I ratio and facilitated the clearance of mitochondrial structural proteins TIM23 and TOM20 (Fig. 5b). This reduction was consistently validated in primary alveolar macrophages (Fig. S1h). To confirm dynamic autophagic flux rather than a downstream lysosomal blockade, we evaluated LC3B-II turnover using Bafilomycin A1 (BafA1). Co-treatment with TJ0113 and BafA1 induced an additive accumulation of LC3B-II compared to BafA1 alone, confirming that TJ0113 actively accelerates upstream autophagosome biogenesis (Fig. S1b). Furthermore, fluorescence co-localization analysis demonstrated that TJ0113 markedly augmented the spatial overlap between mitochondria and lysosomes, reflecting the active formation of mitolysosomes (Fig. 5c and d).

We then evaluated whether this mitophagic clearance translates into the functional recovery of mitochondria. Seahorse extracellular flux analysis revealed that LPS stimulation severely impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), leading to significant decreases in basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiratory capacity, and spare respiratory capacity. Treatment with TJ0113 effectively preserved all these critical oxygen consumption parameters (Fig. 5e and f). Maintaining robust OXPHOS relies fundamentally on a stable mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). Consistent with the respirometry data, TMRE staining and flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that TJ0113 robustly reversed the LPS-induced collapse of ΔΨm (Fig. 5g–i, Fig. S1c).

Because depolarized and structurally compromised mitochondria are primary sources of pathological reactive oxygen species (ROS), we evaluated both mitochondria-specific and global intracellular oxidative stress [31,32]. Staining with MitoSOX Red indicated that TJ0113 effectively restricted the LPS-induced overproduction of mitochondrial ROS (Fig. 5j, Fig. S1d). Concordantly, flow cytometric profiling using the DCFH probe showed a massive accumulation of global intracellular ROS following LPS challenge, which was substantially attenuated by TJ0113 treatment at an efficacy comparable to the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MitoTEMPO (Fig. 5k). This MitoTEMPO-equivalent ROS suppression was further confirmed in primary alveolar macrophages under LPS challenge (Fig. S1e). Collectively, these in vitro findings validate that TJ0113 orchestrates a targeted mitophagic response to eliminate damaged mitochondria, thereby rescuing respiratory bioenergetics, stabilizing membrane potential, and restraining oxidative stress in alveolar macrophages.

TJ0113 attenuates ZBP1-driven inflammation by engaging PINK1-associated mitophagy

Since structurally damaged mitochondria act as upstream triggers for cytosolic immune sensors like ZBP1, we investigated whether rescuing mitochondrial fitness translates to inflammatory silencing in vitro. To comprehensively profile this anti-inflammatory action at the cellular level, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on the MH-S macrophages. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis comparing the LPS + TJ0113 and LPS groups revealed that TJ0113 globally downregulated autoimmune and inflammatory cascades, significantly suppressing the TNF, NF-κB, IL-17, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathways, as well as cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions (Fig. 6d). These global transcriptomic shifts perfectly aligned with our targeted protein and mRNA evaluations.

To determine whether this inflammatory silencing depends on restoring mitochondrial quality control, we examined PINK1, the canonical initiator of selective mitophagy. TJ0113 engages this pathway to clear damaged mitochondria [33], a process critical for preventing innate immune hyperactivation in stressed macrophages [17]. Indeed, Western blot analysis revealed that LPS challenge markedly suppressed PINK1 expression in a time- and dose-dependent manner (Fig. S1i)—reflecting severely impaired intrinsic mitochondrial clearance—whereas TJ0113 intervention restored PINK1 levels (Fig. S1j).

To establish a definitive causal link between mitophagy activation and this inflammatory suppression, we employed small interfering RNA targeting PINK1 (siPINK1) to specifically impair autophagic clearance of mitochondria (Fig. S1k). Notably, PINK1 knockdown abrogated TJ0113-induced mitophagy and, consequently, eliminated the ability of TJ0113 to suppress ZBP1 expression and its associated downstream p-MLKL and GSDMD-N (Fig. 6e–g, Fig. S3b). As a result, this genetic disruption of mitophagy compromised the overall anti-inflammatory efficacy of the drug, leading to a marked rebound in the mRNA levels of Tnf, Il1b, Il6, Il18 and Mcp1 despite TJ0113 treatment (Fig. 6g). Consistent with these findings, parallel PINK1-silencing experiments in primary alveolar macrophages yielded similar outcomes, reversing the capacity of TJ0113 to restrict LPS-induced ZBP1 expression and the subsequent transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. S2b and c). Finally, direct genetic silencing of ZBP1 (siZBP1) closely phenocopied the anti-inflammatory efficacy of TJ0113. Notably, co-treatment with TJ0113 and siZBP1 yielded no additive suppression of downstream protein effectors compared to either intervention alone, confirming ZBP1 as the principal downstream mediator of this response (Fig. 6h, Fig. S1l). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TJ0113 alleviates macrophage-mediated inflammation strictly by facilitating PINK1-associated mitophagy to restrain the ZBP1 inflammatory axis.

Having genetically confirmed mtDNA as the indispensable ligand, we next evaluated whether TJ0113 prevents the activation of this axis by restricting cytosolic mtDNA availability. To ensure robust and specific detection of this translocation, we utilized orthogonal platforms: quantitative PCR following subcellular fractionation (purity validated in Fig. S2d) and immunofluorescence targeting cytosolic Z-NA. Crucially, both assays demonstrated that TJ0113 intervention profoundly reduced the LPS-induced extrusion of mtDNA into the cytosol (Fig. 7d, e, Fig. S3c). Validating the absolute specificity of these approaches, EB-treated mtDNA-depleted cells exhibited virtually no cytosolic signal across either assay (Fig. 7e–g). Parallel immunofluorescence analysis in primary alveolar macrophages further confirmed this protective restriction, revealing that TJ0113 similarly attenuated the LPS-induced accumulation of intracellular Z-NA signals (Fig. S2e and f).

Interestingly, genetic silencing of Zbp1 (siZbp1) also notably diminished cytosolic Z-NA accumulation following LPS challenge (Fig. 7f). This observation potentially reflects a feed-forward loop wherein ZBP1 not only stabilizes cytosolic Z-form mtDNA to perpetuate inflammatory signaling [38], but also, via its downstream effectors (e.g., p-MLKL and GSDMD-N), aggravates secondary mitochondrial damage, driving additional mtDNA extrusion and robustly amplifying the inflammatory response [39].

Finally, to confirm that TJ0113-mediated mtDNA clearance is functionally anchored to its mitophagic activity, we evaluated cytosolic ligand levels following PINK1 knockdown. Crucially, the genetic ablation of PINK1 (siPink1) largely abrogated the capacity of TJ0113 to suppress cytosolic mtDNA extrusion and clear Z-NA accumulation (Fig. 7f–h, i). Together, these findings demonstrate that TJ0113 dismantles the mtDNA-ZBP1 inflammatory axis strictly through PINK1-orchestrated mitophagy, thereby neutralizing the proximal inflammatory trigger to alleviate acute lung injury. Discussion

Acute lung injury (ALI) is a devastating respiratory condition characterized by an uncontrolled inflammatory storm, for which precise upstream pharmacological interventions remain severely limited. In this study, we provide the first evidence that TJ0113, a clinically advanced small-molecule mitophagy activator, confers robust protection against LPS-induced ALI by limiting mtDNA release and suppressing ZBP1 activation. Beyond macroscopic protection, our integration of single-cell transcriptomics and targeted genetic ablation delineated a specific, cell-autonomous mechanism. We establish that TJ0113 alleviates pulmonary inflammation by specifically targeting alveolar macrophages (AMs) to engage PINK1-dependent mitophagy; this process accelerates the clearance of damaged mitochondria, fundamentally restricts cytosolic mtDNA leakage, and ultimately dismantles the ZBP1-driven inflammatory cascade.

Conventional anti-inflammatory strategies in ALI, such as broad cytokine antagonists and corticosteroids, typically focus on neutralizing terminal mediators. However, this approach often fails to halt the underlying pathological momentum, as it merely suppresses downstream symptoms without extinguishing the inflammatory source [[40], [41], [42]]. Emerging insights into immunometabolism reveal that structurally compromised mitochondria are not merely passive victims of oxidative stress, but rather the active "epicenter" of innate immune activation [15]. Indeed, dysfunctional mitochondria release mtDNA into the cytosol, where it functions as a critical DAMP to engage multiple nucleic acid sensors—including cGAS-STING and ZBP1—to drive inflammatory signaling cascades [43]. In this context, recent studies linked LPS-induced mitochondrial ROS to ZBP1-dependent necroptosis in alveolar macrophages [[44], [45], [46]]; concurrently, Kondo et al. demonstrated that Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission protects macrophages from mtDNA/ZBP1-driven inflammation during post-infarct cardiac remodeling [47]. Consistent with this emerging paradigm, we now extend this pathogenic mtDNA-ZBP1 axis to ALI. More importantly, we address the critical gap of therapeutic intervention: we demonstrate that pharmacological enhancement of mitophagy—rather than genetic modulation of fission—can effectively eliminate this upstream trigger. By engaging PINK1-dependent mitophagy, TJ0113 intervenes precisely at this metabolic checkpoint [48]. Consistent with previous studies identifying TJ0113 as a specific PINK1 pathway activator [21,33], our autophagic flux validation and the in vivo consumptive degradation of PARKIN definitively confirm that TJ0113 dynamically accelerates upstream mitochondrial clearance, rigorously ruling out a mere downstream lysosomal blockade. This targeted clearance of dysfunctional organelles effectively starves the inflammatory network of its essential upstream DAMPs before the cascade can amplify. Such a mechanism underscores the profound translational potential of utilizing mitochondrial quality control and metabolic reprogramming to disarm immune hyperactivation, offering a decisive therapeutic advantage over traditional terminal immunosuppression [49].

While the involvement of the mtDNA-ZBP1 axis is increasingly recognized, the precise dynamics governing its sustained hyperactivation remain incompletely understood in ALI [50]. By utilizing mtDNA-depleted macrophages, our findings establish cytosolic mtDNA as the indispensable endogenous trigger of the ZBP1 inflammatory cascade. Our temporal analysis revealed that the phosphorylation of downstream effectors precedes the substantial upregulation of ZBP1 protein expression. This asynchrony reflects a canonical two-phase innate immune response: rapid early-stage mitochondrial stress and initial kinase activation are followed by a robust, likely STAT1-dependent, transcriptional induction of ZBP1 [51]. More importantly, our targeted genetic interventions unveil a feed-forward dynamic wherein ZBP1 activation, once engaged, amplifies secondary mitochondrial damage [52]. The silencing of Zbp1 not only attenuated downstream signaling but also resulted in a marked reduction of cytosolic Z-NA accumulation, consistent with the possibility that ZBP1 activation may promote ligand generation or stabilization. This observation is conceptually supported by recent evidence demonstrating that ZBP1 actively stabilizes Z-form mtDNA and nucleates cytosolic complexes to sustain continuous inflammatory signaling [38]. Together, these findings indicate that ZBP1 functions not merely as a passive terminal sensor, but as a potent endogenous amplifier. It sustains the innate immune response by exacerbating secondary mitochondrial damage and perpetuating ligand release—a critical driver of the inflammatory storm in ALI. Crucially, TJ0113 exhibits a comprehensive inhibitory profile by suppressing both the immediate downstream activation and the subsequent marked accumulation of ZBP1. By promoting PINK1-dependent mitophagy, TJ0113 effectively dismantles this vicious cycle at its root, neutralizing the inflammatory amplifier by eliminating the endogenous ligand supply.

It is crucial to acknowledge that canonical nucleic acid sensing pathways, particularly the cGAS-STING axis, also play indispensable roles in amplifying ALI. Recent evidence demonstrates that LPS-induced cytosolic mtDNA leakage potently activates the cGAS-STING cascade to drive the inflammatory storm, and pharmacological inhibition of STING (e.g., using H-151) effectively alleviates ALI [[53], [54], [55]]. While our unbiased transcriptomic profiling identified ZBP1 as a significantly upregulated cytosolic sensor, this transcriptional prominence does not rule out the parallel pathological involvement of cGAS-STING, which is constitutively expressed and governed primarily by post-translational modifications. Because TJ0113 functions as an upstream mitophagic activator—effectively restoring mitochondrial quality control and restricting the cytosolic accumulation of mtDNA at its source—it is highly plausible that TJ0113 confers its robust protection by broadly depriving multiple cytosolic sensing networks of their requisite activating ligand, thereby concurrently dampening both ZBP1- and cGAS-STING-driven inflammatory cascades.

Beyond elucidating the molecular dynamics of the mtDNA-ZBP1 axis, identifying its cellular orchestrators within the pulmonary microenvironment is critical. While bulk transcriptomics obscures cell-specific responses, our single-cell RNA sequencing reveals that ZBP1 upregulation predominantly concentrates in alveolar macrophages (AMs). Under homeostasis, AMs constitute the majority of resident alveolar immune cells, serving as immediate first responders to inhaled endotoxins [56,57]. Although the AM population drastically declines during acute LPS-ALI due to pyroptosis and neutrophil influx, this early depletion underscores their pathogenic significance. As the first to confront the endotoxin, AMs endure an intense metabolic and oxidative burden that drives mitochondrial dynamic imbalance and subsequent cytosolic mtDNA extrusion [12,[58], [59], [60]]. This early metabolic collapse and mtDNA leakage not only underscore their pathogenic significance but also position AMs as the central orchestrators of the ensuing inflammatory cascade. Indeed, AMs have been increasingly recognized as the principal coordinators of both the initiation and resolution of pulmonary inflammation during ALI/ARDS, and their pivotal role in disease pathogenesis has prompted the establishment of dedicated theoretical frameworks aimed at identifying novel therapeutic targets within this population [29,61]. Consequently, this mtDNA-driven ZBP1 hyperactivation triggers the rapid inflammatory death of AMs, acting as the primary catalyst for secondary leukocyte recruitment and barrier disruption. By promoting PINK1-dependent mitophagy specifically within this vulnerable population, TJ0113 provides a precise spatial intervention. This neutralizes the local inflammatory source before systemic amplification, offering a distinct advantage over broad immunosuppression.

The therapeutic potential of dismantling the mtDNA-ZBP1 axis depends on the precision of pharmacological interventions like TJ0113. As a selective mitophagy activator, TJ0113 specifically modulates the PINK1/Parkin-dependent Mcl-1-LC3A axis, offering a targeted approach distinct from broad-spectrum autophagy modulators [21,33]. Given this targeted mechanism, we utilized genetic ablation of PINK1 (siPink1)—the indispensable upstream initiator of mitophagy—rather than pharmacological inhibitors like Mdivi-1 to rigorously establish causal dependence [[62], [63], [64]]. While Mdivi-1 has been documented to attenuate ALI in multiple studies, its protective effects are predominantly attributed to the inhibition of Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission and the subsequent suppression of mitochondrial ROS [65]. Importantly, the inhibition of mitophagy by Mdivi-1 is considered a secondary, downstream consequence of its primary action on Drp1 and mitochondrial dynamics [66,67]. Consequently, utilizing Mdivi-1 makes it difficult to definitively ascertain whether the mitigated inflammation is directly driven by mitophagy regulation or is merely a byproduct of reduced oxidative stress. To circumvent these pharmacological confounders, we relied on targeted genetic ablation. Our specific genetic blockade confirms that the anti-inflammatory efficacy of TJ0113 is functionally coupled to PINK1-associated mitophagy. This canonical pathway acts as a critical repressor of mtDNA release and subsequent innate immune activation in stressed macrophages. Supported by this targeted mechanism, a robust GLP-compliant preclinical safety profile, and ongoing Phase II clinical trials, TJ0113 represents a highly promising translational candidate for acute pulmonary inflammation [18].

Despite these prospects, several limitations warrant consideration. First, while our in vitro knockdown models strongly support the mtDNA-ZBP1 axis, we lack in vivo genetic validation using constitutive or myeloid-specific Zbp1-knockout mice. Definitively establishing this cell-autonomous causality requires future validation utilizing specific conditional knockout models (e.g., Lyz2-Cre mice). Second, as our investigation focused exclusively on the acute hyperinflammatory phase, the long-term impact of TJ0113-enhanced mitophagy on late-stage pulmonary fibrosis and tissue remodeling remains an important avenue for future research. Third, the intricate cross-talk and functional hierarchy between ZBP1 and other constitutively active sensors, particularly the cGAS-STING cascade, remain to be elucidated. Investigating how these distinct mtDNA sensing networks synergize within specific macrophage subpopulations presents a compelling and highly translational avenue for future research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the small-molecule mitophagy activator TJ0113 provides robust protection against LPS-induced acute lung injury (ALI) by restoring mitochondrial quality control. Utilizing single-cell transcriptomics and targeted genetic ablation, we identify alveolar macrophages (AMs) as the primary cellular hub for this intervention and delineate a definitive PINK1-mtDNA-ZBP1 regulatory axis. Specifically, TJ0113 facilitates PINK1-dependent mitophagy to accelerate the clearance of damaged mitochondria. This effectively restricts the cytosolic extrusion of mtDNA, depriving the innate immune sensor ZBP1 of its activating ligand and silencing downstream inflammatory cascades. By intervening at this upstream source of innate immune activation rather than merely neutralizing terminal cytokines, TJ0113 exhibits strong translational potential. Ultimately, these insights establish the mtDNA-ZBP1 axis as a critical driver of ALI and highlight pharmacological mitophagy enhancement as a highly viable therapeutic strategy for severe pulmonary inflammation.

Animals and experimental models

Male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks) were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility. All protocols were approved by the IACUC of ZJCLA (Approval No. ZJCLA-IACUC-20120023).

Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (80 mg kg−1, i.p.). Following tracheal exposure, LPS (5 mg kg−1 in sterile saline) was administered via direct intratracheal (i.t.) instillation. Control mice received i.t. saline. For pharmacological intervention, TJ0113 was formulated in 0.2% DMSO and 30% PEG400 in PBS. The LPS + TJ0113 group received i.p. injections of TJ0113 immediately after modeling (20 mg kg−1, 0 h) and at 7 h post-surgery (10 mg kg−1). Control and LPS groups received equal volumes of the vehicle. At 24 h post-modeling, serum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and lung tissues were collected.

A lethal ALI model was induced using an elevated LPS dose (20 mg kg−1, i.t.), followed by daily TJ0113 administration (10 mg kg−1 d−1, i.p.). Survival and body weight were monitored daily. A 30% loss of initial body weight was designated as a humane endpoint.

The acute split-dose regimen (20 + 10 mg kg−1) was designed to rapidly achieve high target engagement during the explosive early phase of the cytokine storm, whereas the daily maintenance regimen (10 mg kg−1 d−1) was utilized in the survival model to provide steady-state therapeutic coverage while minimizing long-term cumulative toxicity.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) collection

Following euthanasia, the trachea and bronchi were exposed via midline cervical and thoracic dissection. To remove intravascular contaminants, the left atrium was incised, and the pulmonary circulation was perfused with 1.0 mL of sterile saline via right ventricular puncture. Subsequently, a tracheal cannula was inserted and secured with silk sutures. BALF was obtained by slowly instilling 1.0 mL of pre-cooled PBS into the lungs, ensuring uniform pulmonary expansion, followed by gentle aspiration. This procedure was repeated three times, and the total recovered volume was recorded for subsequent analyses.

BALF protein concentration assay

Total protein concentration in the collected BALF was determined using a Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0012). Briefly, the BALF was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to pellet cellular debris. Subsequently, 20 μL of the cell-free supernatant and 200 μL of the BCA working solution were added into a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The absorbance at 562 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad). Final protein concentrations were calculated based on a generated standard curve.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Whole blood was collected via retro-orbital bleeding and kept in an inclined position at room temperature for 30–60 min to allow for complete clot retraction. Serum was subsequently separated by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The concentrations of TNF-α and IL-6 in both the obtained serum and BALF were quantified using specific commercial ELISA kits (Elabscience; E-EL-M3063 and E-EL-M0044) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad), and cytokine concentrations were extrapolated from concurrently generated standard curves.

Histopathological evaluation and lung injury scoring

To evaluate pulmonary morphological alterations, 4 μm-thick FFPE lung sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The sections were subsequently stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) utilizing a commercial staining kit (Servicebio). Following standard dehydration, clearing, and mounting with neutral resin, representative histological images were captured utilizing a bright-field microscope. To quantitatively assess the severity of pulmonary damage, 20 randomly selected fields per slide (400× magnification) were independently evaluated in a blinded manner according to the official American Thoracic Society (ATS) lung injury scoring guidelines.

Single-cell suspension preparation from lung tissue

Under sterile conditions, fresh mouse lung tissues were harvested and mechanically dissociated into approximately 0.5 mm3 fragments. The tissues were subsequently enzymatically digested in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 0.04% BSA and 0.2% collagenase II (Gibco) at 37 °C for 30–60 min with gentle agitation. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (BD Falcon) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Following the depletion of erythrocytes using a red blood cell lysis buffer (Miltenyi), the cell pellet was washed and resuspended in BSA-supplemented medium. Cell concentration and viability were quantitatively assessed using a Luna-FL automated cell counter.

scRNA-seq library construction and sequencing

The final single-cell suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 700–1200 cells uL−1. Library preparation was executed strictly following the manufacturer's protocol using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1 (10x Genomics). The constructed single-cell transcriptomic libraries were subsequently sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) configuration to generate high-throughput data.

scRNA-seq data processing and transcriptomic analysis

Raw FASTQ sequencing files were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using the Cell Ranger pipeline (v9.0.1, 10x Genomics) to generate unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrices. Downstream processing was conducted utilizing the Scanpy package. Stringent quality control criteria were applied to filter out low-quality cells: cells retaining 200–6000 expressed genes, 1000–30000 UMIs, and < 5% mitochondrial or hemoglobin gene proportions were preserved for subsequent analyses. Potential doublets were computationally removed via Doublet Detection 2. Following library size normalization, the top 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were extracted for principal component analysis (PCA). Potential batch effects were corrected using the harmonypy package. Unsupervised graph-based clustering (Leiden algorithm) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were employed for dimensional reduction and two-dimensional visualization. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were evaluated at two distinct resolutions: global condition-level comparisons (to elucidate overall treatment effects, e.g., LPS vs. Control) and specific cluster-level analyses. Both were computed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with statistical significance defined as an adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.58. Cell types were comprehensively annotated by integrating automated CellTypist predictions with canonical marker gene validation. Finally, Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analyses of the DEGs were performed to elucidate the functional transcriptomic shifts underlying the experimental interventions.

Isolation and culture of primary alveolar macrophages

Primary alveolar macrophages (AMs) were isolated via bronchoalveolar lavage using pre-cooled PBS. The collected lavage fluids were pooled on ice and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and subsequently seeded into culture plates. After a 2-h adherence period at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, non-adherent cells were removed by washing with warm PBS. The cultures were replenished with fresh complete medium and incubated overnight to attain a quiescent state prior to subsequent experiments.

Cell culture and treatments

The MH-S mouse alveolar macrophage cell line (ZQ0921) was obtained from Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. To simulate the inflammatory environment of acute lung injury (ALI) in vitro and investigate mitophagy mechanisms, MH-S cells were challenged with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) at indicated concentrations for 24 h. In definitive experiments, cells were subjected to LPS stimulation (100 ng mL−1) for 24 h in the presence or absence of TJ0113 (at indicated concentrations) for 12 or 24 h for protein expression analysis. For the quantification of inflammatory cytokines via qPCR, cells were treated with LPS (100 ng mL−1) for 3 h with or without TJ0113 (10 μM). To specifically evaluate the cytosolic release of mtDNA, cells were exposed to LPS (100 ng mL−1) in the presence or absence of TJ0113 at indicated concentrations for 12 h. Furthermore, to generate mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)-depleted cells, MH-S cells were pre-incubated with 1 μg mL−1 ethidium bromide (EB) for 48 h prior to subsequent investigations.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed utilizing a commercial Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, C0037). Briefly, MH-S macrophages seeded in 96-well plates were treated with varying concentrations of TJ0113 (0–10 μM) for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of the CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1–2 h in the dark. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad). Relative cell viability was calculated as a percentage normalized to the untreated control group.

Bulk RNA-seq and KEGG analysis

MH-S cells were treated with LPS (100 ng mL−1) and TJ0113 (10 μM) for 3 h. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Following quality control via fastp, reads were mapped using HISAT2. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified utilizing DESeq2 (|log2FC| > 1, P < 0.05). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed to identify significantly altered pathways.

Western blotting

To extract total protein from lung tissues, snap-frozen samples were mechanically homogenized on ice utilizing stainless steel beads in an NP-40-based lysis buffer supplemented with appropriate proportions of protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Following centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were carefully collected, and protein concentrations were quantified utilizing a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0012). For in vitro analysis, cultured MH-S cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, lysed in a standard RIPA buffer containing both protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 15 min on ice, and similarly centrifuged to obtain the clarified protein extracts for subsequent BCA quantification. After concentration normalization, equalized protein samples were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, resolved by 10–12% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes utilizing a wet electroblotting system (0.2 A for 1 h on ice). The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat milk in PBST (0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies diluted in PBST containing 5% (w/v) BSA. After extensive washing, the membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, target protein bands were visualized utilizing an ECL reagent (Beijing 4A Biotech, AW001-500) and captured by a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

To evaluate ultrastructural alterations, specifically focusing on mitochondrial morphology and the formation of mitophagosomes within the pulmonary microenvironment, fresh lung tissues were rapidly excised and minced into approximately 1 mm3 blocks. The specimens were immediately immersed in pre-cooled 2.5% glutaraldehyde (in 0.1 M PBS) and fixed overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes with 0.1 M PBS (10 min each), the tissues were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) for 1 h and subsequently washed with double-distilled water. En bloc staining was performed using 2% uranyl acetate for 30 min. The samples were then sequentially dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, and 90% for 15 min each; 100% twice for 20 min) and transitioned into absolute acetone for 20 min. Tissues were infiltrated with a mixture of acetone and embedding resin (1:1 for 2 h, followed by 1:3 overnight) prior to final embedding in pure resin. After heat polymerization, ultrathin sections were generated, specifically stained, and examined using a Philips Tecnai 10 transmission electron microscope (Philips, The Netherlands) operating at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV.

Seahorse metabolic flux analysis

Real-time oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using a Seahorse XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent). Briefly, following a 24 h challenge with LPS (1000 ng mL−1) in the presence or absence of TJ0113 (1 μM), MH-S cells were equilibrated in XF base medium (supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine, pH 7.4) for 1 h at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator. Following baseline OCR acquisition, metabolic modulators were sequentially injected: oligomycin (1.5 μM), FCCP (2.0 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM). Key parameters, including basal, ATP-linked, and maximal respiration, were derived from the OCR kinetic curves and normalized to the uniform cell count seeded per well.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) assessment

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was evaluated utilizing a TMRE Assay Kit (Beyotime, C2001). Briefly, MH-S cells were subjected to LPS challenge (100 ng mL−1) in the presence or absence of TJ0113 (1 μM) for 24 h. To induce complete mitochondrial depolarization as a positive control, a separate group of cells was pre-treated with 10 μM CCCP for 20 min.For confocal imaging, the treated cells were incubated with the TMRE working solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. Following washing with the provided assay buffer, fluorescence images were acquired using an LSM 900 confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was subsequently quantified utilizing ImageJ software. For quantitative flow cytometric analysis, harvested cells were resuspended in the TMRE working solution, incubated under identical conditions, and analyzed using CytoFlex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) via the PE-A channel.

Tissue immunofluorescence

To evaluate protein expression and distribution, multiplex immunofluorescence was performed on 4–5 μm FFPE lung sections. Following deparaffinization, rehydration, and heat-induced antigen retrieval (citrate buffer, pH 6.0), sections were treated with an autofluorescence quencher. After blocking with 3% BSA, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. For primary antibodies from different host species, standard fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were utilized for simultaneous detection. For antibodies derived from the same host species, a sequential Tyramide Signal Amplification (TSA) strategy was implemented. This involved incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and fluorescent tyramide reagents, followed by intermediate antibody stripping utilizing an elution buffer (Servicebio, G1266) to prevent cross-reactivity. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and representative images were acquired utilizing an Eclipse C1 upright fluorescence microscope (Nikon). Quantitative analysis was performed utilizing ImageJ software.

Fluorescence microscopy

To visualize mt-DNA leakage, MH-S cells were cultured in 12-well plates on circular glass coverslips and subjected to the indicated pre-treatments followed by LPS (100 ng mL−1) and TJ0113 (10 μM) for 12 h. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and blocked with blocking buffer (5% FBS, 0.1% Triton X-100, PBS) for 1 h at 25 °C. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies against Z-NA (Absolute, 1:100) and TOM20 overnight at 4 °C. After washing, secondary antibodies were incubated at room temperature for 1 h, followed by DAPI counterstaining.

To dynamically monitor the spatial interplay between mitochondria and lysosomes, live cells were cultured in 3.5 cm glass dishes, treated with LPS 100 ng mL−1 and TJ0113 (10 μM) for 24 h, and co-stained with MitoTracker Deep Red FM and LysoTracker Red DND-99 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 900 AiryScan confocal imaging system and processed with ImageJ software.

Oxidative stress evaluation

To evaluate in situ accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), lung cryosections were incubated with a dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescent probe (Absin, abs810256) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, washed, and visualized utilizing a fluorescence microscope. For in vitro cellular oxidative stress, MH-S cells were subjected to LPS challenge (1000 ng mL−1) in the presence or absence of TJ0113 (10 μM) or the mitochondrial ROS scavenger MitoTEMPO (10 μM) for 24 h. Mitochondrial-specific ROS were assessed using the MitoSOX Red indicator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, M36008) and imaged via a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope (excitation: 510 nm; emission: 580 nm). Concurrently, total intracellular ROS levels were quantified utilizing a DCFH-DA Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0033 M). Harvested cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark, washed to eliminate unbound probes, and immediately analyzed via flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter) using FITC channel. All fluorescence images were processed utilizing ImageJ software.

Furthermore, in situ ROS production in lung tissues was evaluated utilizing a dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescent probe. Briefly, fresh frozen lung sections were incubated with a 10 μM DHE solution at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, washed, and immediately imaged to quantify superoxide anion levels.

Subcellular fractionation and cytosolic mtDNA quantification

To assess the cytosolic release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), harvested MH-S cells were divided into two equal aliquots for total cellular DNA and cytosolic DNA extraction, respectively. For cytosolic fraction isolation, the cell pellet was resuspended in 250 μL of HEPES-KOH buffer (Yeasen, 60165ES60) supplemented with 25 μg mL−1 digitonin. The suspension was incubated at room temperature for 10 min to selectively permeabilize the plasma membrane without disrupting the integrity of mitochondria and nuclei.

The lysate was initially centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min to pellet intact organelles. The supernatant was carefully collected and subjected to a secondary high-speed centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 10 min to stringently eliminate residual heavy membranes and cellular debris. DNA from the highly purified cytosolic supernatant and the paired whole-cell pellet was subsequently extracted utilizing the FastPure Cell/Tissue DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, DC102-01) according to the manufacturer's instructions, ensuring identical final elution volumes for all samples.

Cytosolic mtDNA levels were quantified via qPCR using specific primers targeting mitochondrial CYTB and D-LOOP genes, with all primer sequences comprehensively detailed in Supplementary Table S2. To precisely calibrate for variations in input cell numbers, the cytosolic mtDNA amplification signals were normalized to the nuclear 18S ribosomal DNA (18S rDNA) levels amplified from the paired total cellular DNA extract. Data were expressed as relative fold changes compared to the untreated control group.

siRNA transfection

To specifically silence PINK1 and ZBP1 expression, MH-S cells grown to 60–80% confluence were transiently transfected with target-specific small interfering RNAs (siPINK1 or siZBP1) or a scrambled negative control siRNA (si-NC). Transfections were executed utilizing Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in Opti-MEM reduced-serum medium in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Following transfection, cells were cultured for 24 h prior to total RNA extraction for quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) evaluation, or for 48 h prior to total protein extraction for immunoblotting analysis. All specific siRNA targeting sequences utilized in this study are comprehensively listed in Supplementary Table S3.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA from cultured cells was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, RC112) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, prior to extraction, snap-frozen lung tissue samples were mechanically homogenized in lysis buffer to ensure complete cellular disruption. The purified RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript IV All-in-one RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, R433-01). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a LightCycler 480 II System (Roche) with the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711-02). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. A melt curve analysis was included to verify product specificity. The relative mRNA expression levels of inflammatory cytokines (Tnf, Il6, Mcp1, Il1b, Il18) and regulatory genes (Zbp1, Pink1) were quantified using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, with β-actin as the endogenous reference. All primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed utilizing GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired, two-tailed Student's t-test was employed. For experiments involving multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons was performed. Survival rates were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. For in vitro assays, n = 3 independent biological replicates were performed. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001).
