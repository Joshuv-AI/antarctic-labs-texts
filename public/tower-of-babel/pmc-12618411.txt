Emerging contaminants in rural water: microplastic pollution and its association with agricultural, livestock, and industrial activities in Ecuador
Authors: Angélica Geovanna Zea Cobos, Katerine Ponce Ochoa, Fredi Portilla, Paola Duque
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Microplastic (MP) contamination has emerged as an environmental threat not only in marine systems but increasingly in terrestrial and subsurface environments, where monitoring is often limited. This study investigates the presence, types, and distribution of microplastics in rural drinking water systems in southern Ecuador, with a focus on their association with land-based anthropogenic activities—agriculture, livestock, and local industry. A total of 169 samples were collected from 29 community-managed systems; 61.5% tested positive for MPs. Detected polymers included polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyamide 6, polyethylene, polyvinyl chloride (PVC), and polyester. PET was the most frequently observed, reaching concentrations up to 20 particles per sample, particularly in livestock-dominated catchments. More chemically complex MPs (e.g., PVC, thermoplastics) were predominantly detected in zones with industrial influence. Statistical analysis and spatial visualization (heatmaps) revealed strong correlations between MP distribution and land use type, suggesting that rural MP contamination originates from diffuse and localized terrestrial sources. These findings highlight the potential for MPs to infiltrate groundwater systems via poorly managed soil–water interfaces in rural settings. Introduction

The ubiquity of microplastics in aquatic environments has escalated into a pressing environmental and public health issue, drawing increasing concern from the international scientific community. Microplastics (MPs), commonly defined as synthetic polymer fragments less than 5 mm in diameter, originate from a variety of sources and exhibit high environmental persistence due to their resistance to biodegradation (Li et al., 2018; Pivokonský et al., 2018). Their hydrophobic nature and high surface-area-to-volume ratio allow them to adsorb toxic chemical pollutants such as heavy metals, persistent organic pollutants (POPs), and endocrine-disrupting compounds (Hidalgo-Ruz et al., 2012; Revel et al., 2018), increasing the risk of trophic transfer and human exposure.

While initial research primarily emphasized marine environments, more recent studies have documented alarming levels of microplastics in terrestrial and freshwater systems, including raw and treated drinking water (Sun et al., 2019; World Health Organization [WHO], 2019). Particularly in low- and middle-income countries, rural areas emerge as vulnerable zones where water supply infrastructure is often outdated, insufficient, or lacks mechanisms to detect and mitigate emerging pollutants (Castañeda et al., 2020; Rojas et al., 2020).

Rural drinking water systems in countries like Ecuador are often managed by community water boards that operate with minimal financial or technical support (Mintenig et al., 2017). These systems are embedded within highly heterogeneous landscapes shaped by agricultural intensification, livestock farming, and emerging rural industrial activities such as small-scale manufacturing, textile processing, and food production. Each of these activities introduces unique plastic-derived pollutants into the watershed, including agroplastics, animal feed additives, synthetic fibers, and industrial packaging (Eerkes-Medrano et al., 2015; Maes et al., 2017).

Despite the clear risk, there is a significant gap in scientific knowledge regarding the prevalence, sources, and typology of microplastics in rural water systems, especially in Latin American contexts. Most existing literature focuses on urban centers or coastal ecosystems, thereby overlooking rural populations who may be disproportionately affected due to socio-environmental vulnerabilities (Leslie et al., 2022; Zea Cobos et al., 2024).

This study addresses this critical gap by investigating the presence, concentration, and typology of microplastics in rural drinking water systems across southern Ecuador. By correlating polymer types with land use patterns—agricultural, livestock, and industrial—this research provides novel insights into contamination dynamics specific to rural Andean environments. The findings are intended to inform evidence-based interventions, support regulatory frameworks, and enhance the environmental management capacity of community-operated water systems.

Materials and methods

Study area and sampling design

This study was conducted in Azuay Province, southern Ecuador, and focuses exclusively on the eleven cantons where microplastics were detected: Cuenca, Nabón, Girón, Santa Isabel, Pucará, Chordeleg, Gualaceo, Guachapala, Paute, San Fernando, and Sigsig. Sample collection protocol

At each sampling point, 10 L of water was collected using borosilicate glass bottles that had been pre-cleaned and combusted at 450 °C to eliminate residual organic matter (Mintenig et al., 2017). Field technicians wore nitrile gloves and avoided the use of plastic materials throughout the procedure to minimize the risk of contamination (Mintenig et al., 2017). Water samples were collected at a constant depth of 30 cm below the surface to prevent the inclusion of coarse sediments and floating debris, whereas in shallow water bodies (< 1 m), samples were taken at the maximum available depth, not exceeding 50 cm. The collected samples were transported within 6 h in insulated boxes to the Environmental Laboratory of Universidad Politécnica Salesiana (UPS) in Cuenca, Ecuador, ensuring sample integrity. Procedural blanks, consisting of Milli-Q water, were included at a 1:5 ratio to detect background contamination (Eerkes-Medrano, Thompson, & Aldridge, 2015).

The 10 L sampling volume was selected following international methodological recommendations as an optimal balance between analytical sensitivity and operational feasibility. This volume allows for the detection of microplastic concentrations typically ranging from 0.1 to 1 particle L⁻1 when combined with filtration through 0.45 to 5 µm membranes, while minimizing filter clogging, contamination risk, and excessive handling time (Koelmans et al., 2019). A recent methodological study published in Water confirmed that 10 L per site provides sufficient representativeness in drinking-water systems, recommending the use of cellulose-nitrate (0.45 µm) or PTFE (5 µm) filters with procedural blanks at each station (Zea Cobos, Amón, León, & Caballero, 2024).

During fieldwork, ambient parameters such as air temperature, precipitation, and water turbidity were recorded using a HOBO U23 Pro v2 data logger and a Hanna Instruments HI-98703 turbidimeter. On days with heavy rainfall, when turbidity exceeded 150 NTU, sampling was postponed, avoiding overestimating microplastic concentrations due to storm-driven resuspension processes (Koelmans et al., 2019).

Filtration and pre-treatment

Samples were vacuum filtered through cellulose nitrate membranes (0.45 µm, 47 mm). All filtrations were conducted under laminar flow to avoid airborne particles (Leslie et al., 2022). Filters were refrigerated in sterile Petri dishes.

To remove organic material, filters were immersed in 30% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). For organic matter digestion, a 30 % hydrogen peroxide solution (Merck, Cat. 1.07209.0250; Lot# 578357) was used, following the protocol described by Macias et al. (2019), and incubated at 60 °C for 24 h, ensuring polymer preservation (Maes et al., 2017; Prata, 2018).

Fluorescent staining and microscopy

Figure 1 shows microplastic particles after Nile Red staining as observed in bright-field mode (gray, non-fluorescent) and in epifluorescence mode (green). To prepare the samples, collected filters were uniformly sprayed with a Nile Red solution (1 µg/mL in acetone: Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. N3013, Lot #078K1440, ≥ 98% purity), incubated in the dark for 30 min, and then examined under an epifluorescence microscope (excitation/emission, 485–560 nm). In bright-field mode (Figure 1a), stained particles appear as gray shapes, whereas in fluorescence mode (Figure 1b), the same structures emit a strong green signal. The role of Nile Red is to act as a lipophilic probe that, due to its hydrophobic affinity for polymer surfaces, preferentially binds to microplastics and fluoresces when excited at the appropriate wavelength, thereby greatly enhancing contrast and facilitating detection of plastic particles against the non-fluorescent background (Leslie et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021).

Polymer identification via FTIR spectroscopy

Suspected microplastic particles were extracted and analyzed using FTIR spectroscopy (Bruker Alpha II, ATR mode). Spectral data were compared against certified polymer libraries using OPUS software, and only matches with ≥ 80% confidence were accepted (Zhang et al., 2021).

This protocol follows standardized methods previously validated by Zea Cobos et al. (2024) in a regional context.

Results

Occurrence of microplastics by anthropogenic activity

A total of 169 samples were analyzed, of which 61.5% (n = 104) tested positive for microplastics. The results revealed distinct patterns of contamination depending on the dominant anthropogenic activity in each catchment area. Livestock-only zones registered the highest load of microplastics (33 particles), followed closely by agriculture–livestock mixed areas (31 particles), while industrial and combined zones presented lower but more chemically diverse microplastic profiles.

This distribution suggests a strong association between extensive animal husbandry and the release of polymers commonly used in water troughs, feed bags, mesh fencing, and irrigation systems, consistent with findings by Mintenig et al. and Leslie et al. (Mintenig et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2021; FAO, 2021; Sheriff et al., 2023).

Statistical characterization

Descriptive statistics revealed a heterogeneous distribution of microplastics among the analyzed sampling sites. Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) exhibited significant variability across systems (mean = 3.71; SD = 7.52; max = 20 particles), suggesting the presence of localized point-source pollution hotspots. Chlorinated polyethylene also showed considerable variability (SD = 2.64), likely reflecting industrial discharges or runoff from localized waste management areas.

These findings demonstrate that microplastic contamination in rural water systems is not uniformly distributed, but rather influenced by land-use patterns, hydrological connectivity, and anthropogenic pressures. Such heterogeneity underscores the need for differentiated monitoring and mitigation strategies adapted to the predominant economic activities in each region, particularly in rural Andean basins where agricultural and livestock practices coexist with small-scale industrial operations (Zea Cobos et al., 2024; Maes et al., 2017).

Discussion

The results of this study provide compelling evidence that the presence and distribution of microplastics (MPs) in rural drinking water systems are closely linked to the nature and intensity of surrounding anthropogenic activities. This confirms findings from previous research highlighting the role of land use in influencing environmental microplastic contamination pathways (R Core Team, 2023; Wickham, 2016).

Livestock and agro-livestock zones as critical contamination hotspots

Livestock-exclusive and mixed agricultural–livestock systems presented the highest total microplastic counts, with PET and polyethylene emerging as the most prevalent polymers. These materials are extensively used in rural environments, especially in drinking troughs, mesh fencing, plastic bags for animal feed, fertilizer packaging, and irrigation hoses. The elevated presence of chlorinated polyethylene in these areas, which is resistant to degradation and linked to low-grade materials used in infrastructure, underscores a chronic exposure pathway for rural populations.

These findings are consistent with Mintenig et al. and Prata, who identified agricultural sources—such as mulch films and irrigation tubing—as key contributors to microplastic leaching into water systems (Mintenig et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2019). The presence of MPs in both source water and treated outlets confirms environmental input and potential accumulation within water infrastructure, as previously reported by Sun et al. (Browne et al., 2008).

Diversity and complexity of microplastics in industrial zones

In areas where industrial activity overlapped with agriculture and livestock (e.g., food processing, small-scale manufacturing), the spectrum of microplastics expanded to include PVC, thermoplastics, polyamide-polypropylene blends, and polyester variants. These polymers are commonly associated with industrial packaging, pipes, textiles, and machinery parts (Wang et al., 2021).

Such polymeric complexity is indicative of localized and discrete sources of contamination, as opposed to more diffuse sources in agricultural areas. It also signals the potential for contamination by legacy waste and insufficiently managed solid waste streams. Similar patterns have been observed in peri-urban riverine studies in Asia and Europe, where proximity to manufacturing zones resulted in increased microplastic heterogeneity (Lahens et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022).

Spatial correlation between activity type and polymer presence

The heatmap and principal component analysis (PCA) presented in the “Results” section illustrate clear spatial associations between land use patterns and polymer type occurrence. For instance, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and polyamide (nylon 6) concentrations were markedly higher in agro-livestock zones, while industrial areas displayed greater polymeric diversity, including thermoplastics and PVC. These findings highlight the strong influence of land-use intensity and waste management practices on microplastic composition and distribution (Eerkes-Medrano et al., 2015).

This figure highlights areas with a higher log-density of positive detections, notably in the southern and central cantons, which correspond to livestock and mixed agricultural–livestock zones identified earlier as contamination hotspots. The spatial clustering observed in Fig. 5 confirms that microplastic occurrence is not random but instead reflects anthropogenic-driven deposition and transport mechanisms influenced by both hydrological connectivity and land-use pressures. Moreover, the high standard deviation of PET counts across samples suggests the presence of localized point-source pollution likely related to uncontrolled plastic dumping, degradation of irrigation infrastructure, or runoff from livestock facilities.

This evidence supports the interpretation that rural water systems function not only as passive recipients of downstream pollutants but also as active nodes in the regional plastic cycle, where inefficient waste management, poor infrastructure maintenance, and dispersed human activity contribute cumulatively to contamination (Zhang et al., 2021).

Health and regulatory implications

The chronic presence of MPs in drinking water represents a growing public health concern. Although regulatory bodies such as the World Health Organization (WHO) currently consider the associated risk to be low under current exposure levels, recent research indicates that MPs can induce oxidative stress, cytotoxicity, and endocrine disruption, especially through long-term ingestion (Leslie et al., 2022; Revel et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2019).

This risk is more acute in rural populations that depend on community-managed water systems with limited filtration technologies, minimal oversight, and deteriorating plastic-based infrastructure. Therefore, integrating MP surveillance into national drinking water standards, especially in rural and marginalized areas, is urgent and necessary (World Health Organization [WHO], 2019).

Comparison with regional and global literature

The findings of this study are consistent with emerging global evidence demonstrating that microplastic pollution in rural and agricultural environments is strongly influenced by land-use intensity, waste management practices, and infrastructure degradation. Comparable patterns have been documented in rural China, where Liu et al. (2022) reported high concentrations of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and polyamide in irrigation and drainage systems exposed to agricultural plastics such as mulch films and fertilizer packaging. Similarly, Castañeda et al. (2020) identified small-scale farming and livestock operations as major contributors to plastic-derived pollutants in surface waters of Colombia and Peru, reflecting regional parallels in rural contamination dynamics.

Despite growing international awareness, Latin America remains underrepresented in microplastic freshwater research, particularly in community-managed rural drinking-water systems. The present investigation helps to bridge this gap by providing region-specific data for Andean water systems, integrating field-based sampling, spatial mapping, and polymer identification to characterize contamination across under-monitored territories.

Furthermore, these results emphasize the need for cross-regional scientific collaboration and methodological standardization to enhance comparability among studies. Current disparities in sampling volumes, filtration thresholds, and analytical identification methods limit the development of unified global assessments (Revel et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2021). By following internationally recommended procedures and analytical controls, this study contributes to the establishment of a baseline framework for rural microplastic monitoring in the Andean region and supports the formulation of evidence-based policies that integrate microplastic pollution into water-quality and environmental governance (World Health Organization [WHO], 2019).

Implications for soil–water interfaces and subsurface transport

The observed accumulation of microplastics in rural catchments, particularly within intensive agricultural and livestock zones, raises significant concerns not only for surface water quality but also for potential infiltration into soil matrices and groundwater systems. Residual plastics originating from agrochemical containers, plastic mulch films, and degraded rural infrastructure can enter the soil–water interface through surface runoff, leaching, or direct application, especially under conditions of high rainfall or irrigation intensity.

Figure 6 illustrates the proportion of positive microplastic detections by canton, revealing spatial heterogeneity in contamination intensity across Azuay Province. The highest proportions were observed in Nabón, Gualaceo, and Sigsig, which correspond to areas dominated by mixed agricultural–livestock activities. This distribution supports the hypothesis that microplastic pollution in rural water systems originates predominantly from land-based sources, rather than from external or atmospheric inputs.Fig. 6Proportion of positive microplastic detections by canton, indicating relative contamination levels

Recent studies have demonstrated that small polymer fragments and nanoplastics can migrate through porous soils, reaching shallow aquifers and compromising subsurface water quality (Chu et al., 2019; Ye et al., 2022). This process is particularly relevant in Andean environments, where high hydraulic conductivity, steep slopes, and limited soil sealing facilitate vertical percolation. Although the present study focused on drinking-water systems, the spatial overlap between highly contaminated surface waters and agro-livestock zones strongly indicates a terrestrial origin of pollution, consistent with recent evidence on subsurface plastic transport mechanisms (Colmenarejo Calero et al., 2024).

Overall, these findings highlight the interconnectedness between surface and subsurface contamination pathways, reinforcing the need for integrated watershed management that incorporates both microplastic monitoring and soil–water protection policies. Expanding surveillance networks to include groundwater systems is essential for assessing the long-term mobility and persistence of plastic particles within rural Andean environments.
