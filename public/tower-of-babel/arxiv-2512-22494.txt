1. Introduction

The function of two variables,

\displaystyle f\left(a,b\right)=\frac{\gcd\left(a+b,ab\right)}{\gcd\left(a,b\right)}

is of interest in this paper. At a basic level,
f
packages the two fundamental operations
+
and
\cdot
into a single invariant: it measures the extent to which the additive combination
a+b
shares common divisors with the multiplicative combination
ab
. In other words,
f\left(a,b\right)
investigates the common divisibility forced by the interaction between addition and multiplication.

We claim the function

f:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\quad\text{where}\quad\left(a,b\right)\mapsto\frac{\gcd\left(a+b,ab\right)}{\gcd\left(a,b\right)}

is surjective and that every natural number (in the codomain) can be attained (Propositions
1.1
and
1.2
).

Proposition 1.1
.

f\left(a,b\right)
is an integer for all
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

By Bézout’s lemma, there exist
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x\left(a+b\right)+yab=\operatorname{gcd}\left(a+b,ab\right)
. Note that there exist coprime
k,l
such that
a=k\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)
and
b=l\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)
. Hence,

\displaystyle xk\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)+xl\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)+ykl\left(\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)\right)^{2}=\operatorname{gcd}\left(ab,a+b\right).

So,

\displaystyle f\left(a,b\right)=xk+xl+ykl\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)=xk+xl+byk

which is an integer.
∎

Proposition 1.2
.

f
is a surjection.

Proof.

We effectively wish to show that for any
c\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\left(a,b\right)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
such that
f\left(a,b\right)=c
. Unlike conventional proofs of directly invoking the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, we condition on
c^{2}=a+b\in\mathbb{N}
. Thereafter, we can choose
a=c
and
b=c^{2}-c
so that

\displaystyle f\left(a,b\right)=f\left(c,c^{2}-c\right)=\frac{\operatorname{gcd}\left(c^{2},c^{3}-c^{2}\right)}{\operatorname{gcd}\left(c,c^{2}-c\right)}.

Since
c\geq 1
, then
c^{3}-c^{2}\geq 0
and
c^{2}-c\geq 0
. As such,
f\left(a,b\right)=c
so
f
is surjective.
∎

Theorem 2.1

(limiting density)
.

Define the limiting density
\rho
to be
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n}
. Then,

(1)

\displaystyle\rho=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}\left(p+1\right)}\right)\approx 0.88151.

where the product is taken over all primes
p
. The decimal expansion appears as
A065465
in the online encyclopedia of integer sequences (OEIS).

Proof.

Write
a=da^{\prime}
and
b=db^{\prime}
, where
d=\gcd\left(a,b\right)
and
\gcd\left(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\right)=1
. Then,
f\left(a,b\right)=\gcd\left(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime},d\right)
so
f\left(a,b\right)=1
if and only if there does not exist any prime
p
that divides both
d
and
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}
.

Now, fix a prime
p
. Let
E_{p}
be the event that
p\mid d
and
p\mid\left(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}\right)
. Also, let
\mathbb{P}_{p}
be the limiting probability of
E_{p}
as
n\to\infty
for uniformly random
\left(a,b\right)\in\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}^{2}
. By the Chinese remainder theorem, the events are independent for distinct primes, and we obtain

\rho=\prod_{p}\left(1-\mathbb{P}_{p}\right).

It remains to compute
\mathbb{P}_{p}
. Assume
p\mid d
. Then
p
divides both
a
and
b
, so we may factor out powers of
p
. As such, we write
a=p^{\alpha}u
and
b=p^{\beta}v
, where
\alpha,\beta\geq 1
and
p\nmid u,v
. Without a loss of generality, write
\alpha\leq\beta
. Then,
p^{\alpha}\mid\gcd(a,b)
, and after dividing
a
and
b
by
d
, we have
a^{\prime}=u
and
b^{\prime}=p^{\beta-\alpha}v
up to multiplication by a unit modulo
p
. In particular,
\beta>\alpha
implies
b^{\prime}\equiv 0\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p\right)
and
a^{\prime}\not\equiv 0\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p\right)
, so
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}\not\equiv 0\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p\right)
. Hence, the event
p\mid\left(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}\right)
can occur
only when
\alpha=\beta
.

Now, suppose
\alpha=\beta=k\geq 1
. Then,
a=p^{k}u
and
b=p^{k}v
, where
p\nmid u,v
. Since
d
contains the factor
p^{k}
, we have
a^{\prime}\equiv u\pmod{p}
and
b^{\prime}\equiv v\pmod{p}
, where
u,v\in\left(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times}
. As
n\to\infty
, conditional on the event that
p^{k}\parallel a
and
p^{k}\parallel b
(i.e.
p^{k}\mid a
but
p^{k+1}\nmid a
, and similarly for
b
)

.
, the residues

and

are equidistributed in
\left(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times}
, and independent. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left(p\mid(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime})\text{ given that }p^{k}\parallel a\text{ and }p^{k}\parallel b\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(u+v\equiv 0\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p\right)\right)=\frac{1}{p-1},

since for each non-zero

. Also, to avoid ambiguity, we will only use the

symbol for divisibility instead of also using it for conditional probability.

Next, for a uniformly random integer

, we have

\mathbb{P}(p^{k}\parallel n)=\mathbb{P}(p^{k}\mid n)-\mathbb{P}(p^{k+1}\mid n)

which tends to

\frac{1}{p^{k}}-\frac{1}{p^{k+1}}=\frac{p-1}{p^{k+1}}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Also, the corresponding events for

\mathbb{P}\left(p^{k}\parallel a\text{ and }p^{k}\parallel b\right)\to\left(\frac{p-1}{p^{k+1}}\right)^{2}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(p^{k}\parallel a\text{ and }p^{k}\parallel b\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(p\mid(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime})\text{ given that }p^{k}\parallel a\text{ and }p^{k}\parallel b\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{p-1}{p^{k+1}}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{p-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p-1}{p^{2k+2}}=\frac{1}{p^{2}(p+1)}

Therefore, we obtain the limiting density formula as desired.

\displaystyle\log\left(h\left(D\right)R\left(D\right)\right)\sim\log\sqrt{D}\quad\text{as }D\to\infty\text{ \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{brauer1947}{}{}]}}

While the class number

From an analytic viewpoint, the Euler product in (
1
) should be read as a product of local densities, and Lemma
2.3
realises it as an asymptotic mean value. More generally, a recurring theme in analytic number theory is to understand asymptotics of the form
\sum_{n\leq N}F\left(n\right)
especially when

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\varphi\left(n\right)
denote the Euler totient function and
\sigma\left(n\right)
denote the sum of divisors function. Then,
\varphi\left(n\right)\sigma\left(n\right)\leq n^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
n\geq 1
and write its prime factorisation as

n=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\quad\text{where }p_{i}\text{ are distinct primes and }a_{i}\in\mathbb{N}.

As

\varphi\left(n\right)=n\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{i}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma\left(n\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}+1}-1}{p_{i}-1},

we have

\frac{\sigma\left(n\right)}{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}-p_{i}^{-\alpha_{i}}}{p_{i}-1}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}+1}}\right).

As

\frac{n}{\varphi\left(n\right)}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1},

we see that

\frac{\sigma\left(n\right)}{n}=\frac{n}{\varphi\left(n\right)}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}+1}}\right)\quad\text{so}\quad\frac{\varphi\left(n\right)\sigma\left(n\right)}{n^{2}}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}+1}}\right).

Clearly, this is bounded above by 1, so the result follows.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\varphi\left(n\right)
denote the Euler totient function and
\sigma\left(n\right)
denote the sum of divisors function. Then,

\displaystyle\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}\left(p+1\right)}\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\varphi\left(n\right)\sigma\left(n\right)}{n^{2}}

Proof.

It is a known fact that
\varphi\left(n\right)
and
\sigma\left(n\right)
are multiplicative. Recall that an arithmetic function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is multiplicative if
f\left(1\right)=1
and for any
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)=1
, we have
f\left(ab\right)=f\left(a\right)f\left(b\right)
. Define

\displaystyle f\left(n\right)=\frac{\varphi\left(n\right)\sigma\left(n\right)}{n^{2}}.

One can directly verify that
f\left(1\right)=1
and
f\left(n\right)
is multiplicative. Also, the quotient by
n^{2}
is said to be
local
since it depends on the prime factorisation of
n
. For a multiplicative function, the mean value can be expressed as an Euler product over primes. Wirsing
[
Wirsing1967
,
Elliott1979
]
showed that if
f
is multiplicative and
\left|f\left(n\right)\right|\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n)=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f(p^{k})}{p^{k}}.

See Chapter 6 of
[
Elliott1979
]
for a proof of Wirsing’s result. Note that
\left|f\left(n\right)\right|\leq 1
by Lemma
2.2
. Now, for any prime
p
and exponent
k\geq 0
, we have

(3)

\displaystyle\varphi(p^{k})=p^{k}-p^{k-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma(p^{k})=\frac{p^{k+1}-1}{p-1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle f(p^{k})=\frac{\varphi(p^{k})\sigma(p^{k})}{p^{2k}}=1-\frac{1}{p^{k+1}}\quad\text{so}\quad\frac{f\left(p^{k}\right)}{p^{k}}=\frac{1}{p^{k}}-\frac{1}{p^{2k+1}}.

As such,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f(p^{k})}{p^{k}}=\frac{p}{p-1}-\frac{1}{p^{3}-p}.

Multiplying by
1-\frac{1}{p}
yields the Euler factor so

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\left(\frac{p}{p-1}-\frac{1}{p^{3}-p}\right)=1-\frac{1}{p^{2}\left(p+1\right)}

and the result follows by taking the product over all primes
p
, then applying (
2
).
∎

\varphi\left(n\right)

Usually, we study the general linear group
\operatorname{GL}\left(n,F\right)

\operatorname{Cl}_{G}\left(g\right)=\left\{xgx^{-1}:g\in G\right\}

\mathbf{A}\sim\mathbf{B}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\text{there exists }\mathbf{P}\in\operatorname{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)\text{ such that }\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{PBP}^{-1}.

The conjugacy class
\operatorname{Cl}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)
is defined to be

\operatorname{Cl}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)=\left\{\mathbf{PAP}^{-1}:\mathbf{P}\in\operatorname{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)\right\}

\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
.

For any prime

c_{\mathbf{A}}\left(x\right)=x^{2}-tx+d\quad\text{where }t=\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\text{ and }d=\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\text{ such that }d\neq 0.

Hence, the number of conjugacy classes is equal to the number of possible trace-determinant pairs
\left(t,d\right)\in\left(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\right)^{2}
with

becomes an arithmetic question about pairs
\left(t,d\right)
modulo
n
.

Theorem 2.4
.

The number of conjugacy classes in
\operatorname{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)
is equal to
\varphi\left(n\right)\sigma\left(n\right)
.

Proof.

Let

n=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}.

By the Chinese remainder theorem, as
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\cong\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\mathbb{Z}
, this induces an isomorphism

\operatorname{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)\cong\prod_{i=1}^{r}\operatorname{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\mathbb{Z}\right).

Let
k\left(n\right)
denote the number of conjugacy classes in
\operatorname{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)
. It is a known fact that for a direct product of finite groups, conjugacy classes factor component-wise, so the number of conjugacy classes is multiplicative. That is,

k\left(\operatorname{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}k\left(\operatorname{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\mathbb{Z}\right)\right).

Avni et al. showed that
k\left(\operatorname{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}/p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\mathbb{Z}\right)\right)=p_{i}^{2\alpha_{i}}-p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}

[
Avni2009
]
, and by also considering the formulae for
\varphi
and
\sigma
in (
3
), we have

k\left(\operatorname{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\right)\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(p_{i}^{2\alpha_{i}}-p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\varphi\left(p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\right)\sigma\left(p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\right).

Since
\varphi
and
\sigma
are multiplicative functions, the result follows.

\displaystyle\rho=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left|\left\{\left(a,b\right)\in\left[1,n\right]\times\left[1,n\right]:f_{r}(a,b)=1\right\}\right|=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}.

Theorem 3.1

(Dirichlet hyperbola method)
.

Let

be a multiplicative function. Define

\displaystyle F\left(n\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f\left(k\right).

Let

\displaystyle F\left(n\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{xy=k}g\left(x\right)h\left(y\right).

Then,

\displaystyle F\left(n\right)=\sum_{x=1}^{a}\sum_{y=1}^{n/x}g(x)h(y)+\sum_{y=1}^{b}\sum_{x=1}^{n/y}g(x)h(y)-\sum_{x=1}^{a}\sum_{y=1}^{b}g(x)h(y)

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\varphi\left(n\right)
denote the Euler totient function. For
x>1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\varphi\left(n\right)=\frac{3x^{2}}{\pi^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(x^{3/2}).

Proof.

Note that
\varphi=\mu\ast N
, where
N\left(n\right)=n
denotes the identity function. By applying the Dirichlet hyperbola method with
f=N
and
g=\mu
(Theorem
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\varphi\left(n\right)=\sum_{n\leq y}\mu\left(n\right)F\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)+\sum_{m\leq x/y}N\left(m\right)G\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)-F\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)G\left(y\right),

where

\displaystyle F\left(x\right)=\sum_{n\leq x}N\left(n\right)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}+O\left(x\right)\quad\text{and}\quad G\left(x\right)=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu\left(n\right)=O\left(x\right)

Letting
y=\sqrt{x}
, one can deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\varphi\left(n\right)=\sum_{n\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{\mu\left(n\right)}{n^{2}}\frac{x^{2}}{2}+O(x^{3/2}).

By the Möbius inversion formula, we have

\displaystyle\zeta\left(2\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{2}}=1\quad\text{so}\quad\sum_{n\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{2}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{2}}+O\left(\sum_{n>\sqrt{x}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}+O(x^{-1/2}).

The result follows.
∎

Now, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
S
denote the set of
\left(a,b\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
such that
1\leq a,b\leq N
. Then by Theorem
3.2
, the total number of elements in
S
such that
\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)=1
is given by

\displaystyle 1+2\sum_{b\leq N}\varphi(b)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}N^{2}+O(N^{3/2}),

This shows that the probability that two randomly chosen positive integers are relatively prime is
6/\pi^{2}
.
