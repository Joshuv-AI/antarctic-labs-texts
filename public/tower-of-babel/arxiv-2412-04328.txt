The critical Karp–Sipser core of Erdős–Rényi random graphs
Thomas Budzinski, &, Alice Contat

Abstract
The Karp–Sipser algorithm consists in removing recursively the leaves as well their unique neighbours and all isolated vertices of a given graph. The remaining graph obtained when there is no leaf left is called the Karp–Sipser core. When the underlying graph is the classical sparse Erdős–Rényi random graph G ⁡ [ n , λ / n ] \mathrm{G}[n,\lambda/n] , it is known to exhibit a phase transition at λ = e \lambda=\mathrm{e} . We show that at criticality, the Karp–Sipser core has size of order n 3 / 5 n^{3/5} , which proves a conjecture of Bauer and Golinelli. We provide the asymptotic law of this renormalized size as well as a description of the distribution of the core as a graph. Our approach relies on the differential equation method, and builds up on a previous work on a configuration model with bounded degrees.

Introduction

The Karp–Sipser core.

The Karp–Sipser algorithm on a graph
\mathfrak{g}
consists in removing recursively the isolated vertices and the leaves of
\mathfrak{g}
together with their unique neighbours until all vertices have degree at least
2
. The subgraph that is obtained once there is no leaf and no isolated vertex left is called the
Karp–Sipser core
of
\mathfrak{g}
and we denote it by
|\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathfrak{g})|
.

The initial motivation of Karp and Sipser
[
11
]
to study this algorithm is that it provides a computationally efficient way to find a large independent subset in a graph, although finding the largest such set is NP-hard. Indeed, the leaves and isolated vertices removed during the procedure form an independent set, meaning that no pair of these vertices are neighbours. Moreover, this is an optimal strategy in the sense that there exists an independent set of
\mathfrak{g}
with maximal size which contains all these leaves and isolated vertices. Since then, this procedure has been extensively studied on random graph models, with applications to several different domains such as the rank of the adjacency matrix
[
5
]
or quantum percolation phase transitions
[
3
]
.

The Karp–Sipser core of random graphs.

In their pioneering paper
[
11
]
, Karp and Sipser proved that the KS core of an Erdős–Rényi random graph underlies an interesting phase transition in the sparse regime. More precisely, let
\mathrm{G}[n,p]
be the usual Erdős–Rényi random graph, that is a random graph whose set of vertices is
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and containing each of the
\binom{n}{2}
possible edges with probability
p
independently. Let us fix
\lambda>0
. Then

\frac{|\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathrm{G}[n,\lambda/n])|}{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}C(\lambda),

where
C(\lambda)
is a nonnegative constant and
C(\lambda)=0
if and only if
\lambda\leqslant\mathrm{e}=2,7182...
.

This result was later refined by Aronson, Frieze and Pittel
[
1
]
who proved that in the subcritical regime (i.e. when
\lambda<\mathrm{e}
), the size of the Karp–Sipser core of
\mathrm{G}[n,\lambda/n]
converges in law towards an (explicit) sum of Poisson random variables as
n
goes to infinity. Similar results have been obtained for more general configuration models
[
4
,
10
]
.
In another direction, a central limit theorem for the size of the matching created by the algorithm (using an extended version that keeps running even when there is no leaf left) in the supercritical phase was obtained by Kreacic
[
12
]
. This was recently extended to the subcritical and critical regimes by
\lambda>\mathrm{e}
Glasgow, Kwan, Sah and Sawhney
[
8
]
.

[
6

1

2

3

Ideas of the proof and structure of the paper.

To analyze the Karp–Sipser core of this graph, the global strategy is inspired by
[
6
]
. The first crucial observation is that the Karp–Sipser algorithm is Abelian, in the sense that the Karp–Sipser core does not depend on the order in which the vertices are removed. In particular, we can remove the leaves (and their neighours) one by one. In this setting, the execution of the algorithm can be described by an
\mathbb{N}^{3}
-valued Markov chain
(X_{k},V_{k},M_{k})
already introduced by Karp and Sipser
[
11
]
. The three coordinates of this Markov chain correspond respectively to the number
X_{k}
of vertices of degree
1
, the number
V_{k}
of vertices of degree at least
2
and the total number
M_{k}
of edges after
k
steps of the algorithm. Understanding the size of the Karp–Sipser core is then equivalent to understanding the value of this chain at extinction, i.e. when
X_{k}
hits
0
for the first time.
Rather than the total number of edges, it will be convenient to consider instead the
surplus
of the graph defined by
S_{k}=2M_{k}-X_{k}-2V_{k}
. This is the number of additional half-edges of the graph compared to a graph where vertices would only have degree
1
or
2
. In order to have exactly a Markov chain, it will also be more convenient to consider a multigraph version of the Erdős–Rényi random graph (see Section
2.1
for precise definitions, and Theorem
2
for the analog of Theorem
1
on the multigraph model).

A standard tool to estimate
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Markov chains is the
differential equation method
. In our case, this method gives the convergence of the renormalized process
\left((X_{\lfloor tn\rfloor},V_{\lfloor tn\rfloor},S_{\lfloor tn\rfloor})/n\right)_{t\geqslant 0}
towards a deterministic limit which we call its
fluid limit
. This limit is characterized by a system of differential equations (see Section
3
). However, since the size of the core is
o(n)
, this is not accurate enough close to the extinction time, so we will need to make this fluid limit approximation more quantitative. As in
[
6
]
, the first step is to show that
\varepsilon n
steps before the extinction, the three coordinates
X_{k}
,
V_{k}
and
S_{k}
are typically of order respectively
\varepsilon^{2}n
,
\varepsilon n
and
\varepsilon^{3/2}n
. A difficulty here is that, while the transitions of the Markov chain in
[
6
]
are completely explicit, here they depend on an implicit parameter
z
(defined so that the degrees of non-leaf vertices are well-approximated by Poisson variables with parameter
z
, conditioned to be at least
2
). Together with the fact that vertex degrees are not bounded anymore, this will make the computations more technical.
Our estimates on the transitions of the Markov chain will mostly rely on results from
[
1
]
, which in particular already computed its fluid limit. However, some of the error terms from
[
1
]
are a bit too large for our purpose, so we will need to reprove some estimates in Sections
2.2
and
2.3
. In Section
3
, we will then gather some estimates on the continuous functions appearing in the fluid limit close to their extinction.

After that, as in
[
6
]
, we will control precisely the drift and variance of the Markov chain in the neighbourhood of the trajectory given by the fluid limit approximation (Section
4
). These estimates will allow us to prove that the fluctuations on the number of leaves
\varepsilon n
steps before extinction are typically of order
\varepsilon^{3/4} \sqrt{n}
. As in
[
6
]
, we will argue that the extinction time arises when the fluctuations have the same order of magnitude as the expectation
\approx\varepsilon^{2}n
, that is when
\varepsilon\approx n^{-2/5}
. In particular, the size of the Karp–Sipser core is of order
\varepsilon n\approx n^{3/5}
. Moreover, the vertex degrees in the Karp–Sipser core will be well approximated by Poisson variables with parameter
z\approx\sqrt{\varepsilon}
conditioned to be at least
2
, which is where the various orders of magnitude of Theorem
1
come from. Checking that these approximations remain true even when
\varepsilon\approx n^{-2/5}
requires a careful control of the Markov chain along different scales (Section
5
). This step does not introduce new ideas compared to
[
6
]
.

The last step, which was not needed in
[
6
]
, consists of coming back from the multigraph model of Theorem
2
to the simple graph models
\mathrm{G}[n,\mathrm{e}/n]
and
\mathrm{G}(n,m_{n})
of Theorem
1
. Since
\mathrm{G}(n,m)
is a random multigraph conditioned to be simple, we will need to carefully study the impact of this conditioning on the Karp–Sipser algorithm (Section
6
). We highlight that even though the use of a multigraph model is a classical idea, the most straightforward approaches to deduce Theorem
1
from Theorem
2
fail because the Karp–Sipser core is a very sensitive function of the graph. For this reason, we will actually need a stronger version of Theorem
2
(Theorem
3
), which roughly says that conditioning on the value of the Markov chain
\eta n
steps before extinction for some fixed
\eta>0
does not affect the law of the core.

Comparison with previous works.

Analyzing the Markov chain
(X_{k},V_{k},S_{k})
to study the Karp–Sipser algorithm on Erdős–Rényi random graphs is far from being a new idea. However, in the previous works
[
11
,
1
,
12
]
, it was usually sufficient to estimate this Markov chain up to error terms of order
o(n)
(fluid limit via the differential equation method) or
o(\sqrt{n})
(Gaussian fluctuations via stochastic differential equations). Here, we will need to control
X_{k}
at the order
n^{1/5}
in the end of the process. A key feature that we need to understand here is the “self-correcting” effect of the Markov chain: while the fluctuations on the number
X_{k}
of leaves are of order
\sqrt{n}
during most of the algorithm, they become much smaller when we get close to extinction.

Moreover, many of these works were limited to the subcritical or supercritical regimes, where the limiting drift of the Markov chain behaves pretty nicely around
0
. In the critical regime, the fluid limits becomes more degenerate close to its extinction, which explains that the chain is of order
\left(\varepsilon^{2}n,\varepsilon n,\varepsilon^{3/2}n\right)
before extinction. The results of
[
8
, Sections 4, 5]
can be interpretated as estimates on this Markov chain at time
\varepsilon n
before extinction in the regime where
n\to+\infty
first, and then
\varepsilon\to 0
. On the other hand, the estimates that we obtain in the present work (Proposition
5
) cover the whole range from
\varepsilon
of order
1
to
n^{-2/5}
. In particular, it seems likely to us that the techniques developped here would provide a more precise estimation of the expected matching number in the central limit theorem of
[
8
]
.

A three-dimensional Markov chain

2.1
Definitions

As mentioned above, the execution of the Karp–Sipser algorithm on
\mathrm{G}[n,\mathrm{e}/n]
can be described by a triple with integer values representing the number of leaves, the number of vertices of degree at least
2
and the number of “surplus” half-edges. However, the multigraph version of the model enjoys a nicer Markov property compared to simple graphs. This motivates the following definitions.

Random (multi)graphs with a fixed number of edges.

For
0\leqslant m\leqslant\binom{n}{2}
, we define
\mathrm{G}(n,m)
(resp.
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
) as the uniform graph (resp. multigraph) on
n
vertices with
m
edges. More precisely, we use labels to define this graph to avoid symmetry issues: a
labelled multigraph
with
m
edges on the set of vertices
\{1,\dots,n\}
is defined by its edge-sequence
\left(a_{i},b_{i}\right)_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}
, where
a_{i},b_{i}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
. Note that it is possible that
a_{i}=b_{i}
, in which case the edge
i
is a loop. Then the multigraph
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is obtained by taking a uniform labelled multigraph with
m
edges on the set of vertices
\{1,\dots,n\}
and forgetting its edge-labels, and the graph
\mathrm{G}(n,m)
has the law of
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
conditioned to be simple (i.e. without loops and multiple edges). We will first study the Karp–Sipser core of
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
and then deduce our Theorem
1
. For
d_{1}+\dots+d_{n}
even, a
configuration model
with vertex degrees
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}
is the random multigraph obtained by drawing
d_{i}
half-edges around the vertex
i
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
and pairing those half-edges uniformly at random.

Theorem 2
.

Let
(m_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be a (deterministic) sequence of nonnegative integers such that
m_{n}=\frac{\mathrm{e}}{2}n+O\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
.
For
i\geqslant 2
, let
\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)
be the number of vertices of degree
i
in the Karp–Sipser core of
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
. Then we have the joint convergence in distribution

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n^{-3/5}\cdot\mathbb{D}_{2}(n)\\
n^{-2/5}\cdot\mathbb{D}_{3}(n)\\
n^{-1/5}\cdot\mathbb{D}_{4}(n)\\
\mathbb{D}_{5}(n)\end{array}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{2^{9/5}3^{4/5}}{\mathrm{e}^{3/5}}\vartheta^{-2}\\
\frac{2^{16/5}3^{1/5}}{\mathrm{e}^{2/5}}\vartheta^{-3}\\
\frac{2^{13/5}3^{3/5}}{\mathrm{e}^{1/5}}\vartheta^{-4}\\
\mathrm{Poi}\left(\frac{48}{5}\vartheta^{-5}\right)\end{array}\right),

where
\vartheta=\inf\{t\geqslant 0:W_{t}=t^{-2}\}
for a standard Brownian motion
W
started from
0
. Moreover, with high probability, we have
\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)=0
for all
i\geqslant 6
. Finally, conditionally on
\left(\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)\right)_{i\geqslant 2}
, the Karp–Sipser core is a configuration model.

Fluctuations of the initial condition.

For the rest of the paper (until Section
6
), we fix a deterministic sequence
(m_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
of nonnegative integers such that
m_{n}=\frac{\mathrm{e}}{2}n+O\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
.
Two important quantities in our analysis are the number of leaves and of vertices of degree at least
2
in
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
, that we denote respectively by
X_{0}^{n}
and
V_{0}^{n}
. We also define
S_{0}^{n}=2m_{n}-X_{0}^{n}-2V_{0}^{n}
, which can be seen as the number of “additional half-edges” compared to the case when all vertices of degree at least
2
have degree exactly
2
.
We first estimate the triplet
(X_{0}^{n},V_{0}^{n},S_{0}^{n})
, which will be the initial condition of our Markov chain. Heuristically, the vertex degrees in
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
can be approximated by i.i.d. Poisson variables with parameter
\mathrm{e}
. Thus, at first order, there are
\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}}n
leaves and
(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}}-\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}})n
vertices of degree at least 2. The following result is close to
[
8
, Lemma 4.3]
, except that it is for
m_{n}
fixed instead of random.

Lemma 1
.

The following vector is tight as
n\to+\infty
:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(X_{0}^{n}-\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}}n,\,V_{0}^{n}-\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}}-\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}}\right)n,\,S_{0}^{n}-\left(\mathrm{e}+\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}}+2\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}}-2\right)n\right).

Proof.

First, we note that the degree distribution of the
n
vertices in
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
corresponds exactly to the random allocation problem studied by Janson in
[
9
, Example 3.1]
, where
m=2m_{n}
balls (or half-edges) are thrown uniformly at random in
n
boxes (or vertices). In particular, as an application of
[
9
, Corollary 2.5]
, the normalized fluctuations

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left((X_{0}^{n},V_{0}^{n},S_{0}^{n})-\left(\frac{2m_{n}}{n}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2m_{n}}{n}},1-\left(1+\frac{2m_{n}}{n}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2m_{n}}{n}},\frac{2m_{n}}{n}+2\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2m_{n}}{n}}+\frac{2m_{n}}{n}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2m_{n}}{n}}-2\right)n\right)

converge in distribution towards a Gaussian vector. The covariance matrix of the limit is explicit but we do not need it here. Moreover, since
m_{n}=\frac{\mathrm{e}n}{2}+O\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
, we have

\left(\frac{2m_{n}}{n}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2m_{n}}{n}},1-\left(1+\frac{2m_{n}}{n}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2m_{n}}{n}},\frac{2m_{n}}{n}+2\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2m_{n}}{n}}+\frac{2m_{n}}{n}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2m_{n}}{n}}-2\right)\\
=\left(\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}},1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}}-\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}},\mathrm{e}+\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}}+2\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}}-2\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

which concludes the proof of the Lemma.
∎

Multigraphs with a fixed number of leaves, non-leaf vertices and edges.

An important distribution for us will be uniform multigraphs with given numbers of leaves, of vertices of degree at least
2
and of edges.
For
x,v,s\geqslant 0
such that
x+2v+s
is even, we consider a uniform random variable among all the labelled multigraphs on the vertex set
\{1,\dots,x+v\}
with
\frac{x+2v+s}{2}

1,\dots,x
have degree
1
and the vertices
x+1,\dots,x+v
have degree at least
2
. We denote by
\mathbb{G}(x,v,s)
the random multigraph obtained by forgetting the edge-labels of this labelled multigraph. We call

the

of the multigraph, as it is the additional number of half-edges compared to the case where all vertices have degree either
1
or
2
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
x,v,s\geqslant 0
with
x+2v+s
even. Conditionally on the family of vertex degrees
(\deg(j))_{1\leqslant j\leqslant x+v}
, the multigraph
\mathbb{G}(x,v,s)
is a configuration model.

Proof.

The labelled multigraph
\mathbb{G}(x,v,s)
is uniform among all the labelled multigraphs with
x
leaves, with
v
vertices of degree at least
2
and with surplus
s
, so it is still uniform if we condition on all the vertex degrees.
∎

In particular, the two random multigraphs
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
and
\mathbb{G}(X_{0}^{n},V_{0}^{n},S_{0}^{n})
have the same law.

One step of the Karp–Sipser algorithm.

Equipped with this definition, we now want to apply the Karp–Sipser algorithm on the random multigraphs
\mathbb{G}(x,v,s)
. Given a multigraph
G
which is a possible realization of
\mathbb{G}(x,v,s)
for some
x\geqslant 1
and
v,s\geqslant 0
, we denote by
G^{-}
the multigraph obtained from
G
by the following sequence of operations (see Figure
1
):

1.

remove the leaf with label
1
and its unique neighbour,

2.

remove all the edges incident to that neighbour,

3.

remove the isolated vertices that might appear in the process,

4.

relabel the leaves in increasing order followed by the vertices of degree at least
2
in increasing order.

1

2

Our starting point is the following property, already observed in
[
1

.

Lemma 3
.

If
G
follows the law of
\mathbb{G}(x,v,s)
, then conditionally on the number

of leaves, the number
V^{-}
of non-leaf vertices and the surplus

of

, the graph

follows the law of
\mathbb{G}(X^{-},V^{-},S^{-})

The Markov chain.

As a result, the Karp–Sipser algorithm yields a natural three-dimensional random process defined as follows. Let us start from
G_{0}^{n}:=\mathbb{G}(X_{0}^{n},V_{0}^{n},S_{0}^{n})
, where we recall that
(X_{0}^{n},V_{0}^{n},S_{0}^{n})
are respectively the number of leaves, vertices of degree at least
2
and surplus of
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
.
For all
k\geqslant 0
, if
G_{k}^{n}
has at least one leaf, let
G_{k+1}^{n}=(G_{k}^{n})^{-}
. If
G_{k}^{n}
has no leaf, we just set
G_{k+1}^{n}=G^{n}_{k}
, and we denote by
\theta^{n}
the first time at which this is the case. Then
G_{\theta^{n}}^{n}
is the Karp–Sipser core of
G_{0}^{n}
(up to a relabelling of its vertices).
For all
k\geqslant 0
, we denote by
X_{k}^{n}
(resp.
V_{k}^{n}
,
M_{k}^{n}
) the number of leaves (resp. non-leaf vertices, half-edges) of
G_{k}^{n}
, and by
S_{k}^{n}=2M_{k}^{n}-X_{k}^{n}-2V_{k}^{n}
its surplus. We will also denote by
\left(\mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}\right)_{k\geqslant 0}
the associated filtration, i.e.
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{n}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\left(X_{i}^{n},V_{i}^{n},S_{i}^{n}\right)_{0\leqslant i\leqslant k}
. As observed in
[
11
, Assertion (1), p266]
or
[
1
, Lemma 3]
, this defines a Markov chain.

Lemma 4
.

The process
(X_{k}^{n},V_{k}^{n},S_{k}^{n})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant\theta^{n}}
is a Markov chain. Moreover, for any stopping time
T
, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{T}^{n}
, the graph
G_{T}^{n}
has the law of
\mathbb{G}(X_{T}^{n},V_{T}^{n},S_{T}^{n})
.

The proof of the phase transition by Karp and Sipser
[
11
]
relies on this Markov chain. Using the
differential equation method

[
15
]
for the first time in the context of random graphs, they showed that the renormalized version of this Markov chain is well approximated by a differential equation on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for which they find an explicit solution, see Section
3
below.

To simplify notation, the
n
in the exponent will be implicit for the rest of the paper when there is no ambiguity, even if we will often look at asymptotics as
n\to+\infty
.

2.2
The asymptotic drift of the Markov chain

The next step is to estimate the local drift of the Markov chain
(X,V,S)
(Proposition
1

[
1

[
1
]
, the first step is to understand the distribution of vertex degrees in the random graph
\mathbb{G}(x,v,s)

2

z

v,s>0

z(v,s)>0
the unique solution to the equation

\frac{z(\mathrm{e}^{z}-1)}{\mathrm{e}^{z}-z-1}=2+\frac{s}{v}.

(2)

We also set the convention
z(v,0)=0

v>0
, and we will write
Z_{k}=z(V_{k},S_{k})
. Note that in
[
1
]
, the right-hand side is written in a different form, as it is expressed in terms of
x,v
and the number of edges. Since the number of edges is equal to
\frac{1}{2}(x+2v+s)
, the adaptation is immediate.
It will be important for us to understand the behaviour of
z
when
s
and
v
go to
0
. Although it is not a smooth function of
(v,s)
as
v\to 0
, we can still write it as a smooth function of the ratio
\frac{s}{v}
.

Lemma 5
.

There is a smooth function
\widetilde{z}:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
such that for all
v>0
and
s\geqslant 0
, we have
z(v,s)=\widetilde{z}\left(\frac{s}{v}\right)
. Moreover, we have

z(v,s)=3\frac{s}{v}+O\left(\left(\frac{s}{v}\right)^{2}\right).

(3)

In particular, we will see later that
\varepsilon n
steps before extinction, the quantities
S_{k}
and
V_{k}
are respectively of order
\varepsilon^{3/2}n
and
\varepsilon n
, so
Z_{k}
will be of order
\varepsilon^{1/2}
.

Proof.

We first verify that the derivative of the left-hand side of (
2
) with respect to
z
is positive. Specifically, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\frac{z(\mathrm{e}^{z}-1)}{\mathrm{e}^{z}-z-1}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2z}-2\mathrm{e}^{z}+1-z^{2}\mathrm{e}^{z}}{(\mathrm{e}^{z}-z-1)^{2}}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{z}\left(2\cosh(z)-2-z^{2}\right)}{(\mathrm{e}^{z}-z-1)^{2}}.

(4)

The numerator is positive for
z>0
by expanding
\cosh(z)
into a power series. Note that this proves that the solution to (
2
) is indeed unique. Moreover (
2
) defines
\widetilde{z}\left(\frac{s}{v}\right)
for
\frac{s}{v}\neq 0
and can be extended to a continuous function by setting
\widetilde{z}(0)=0
. Since (
4
) does not vanish, the function
\widetilde{z}
is smooth on
(0,+\infty)
. Therefore, to conclude, all we need to check is that (
4
) does not vanish as
z\to 0
. But in this regime, the numerator of (
4
) is equivalent to
\frac{1}{12}z^{4}
and its denominator to
\frac{z^{4}}{4}
, so
\frac{d}{dz}\frac{z(\mathrm{e}^{z}-1)}{\mathrm{e}^{z}-z-1}>0
on
[0,+\infty)
.

Finally, the second part (
3
) immediately follows from the fact that the left-hand side of (
2
) is
2+\frac{z}{3}+O(z^{2})
as
z\to 0
and is of order
z
as
z\to+\infty
.
∎

Poisson approximation for vertex degrees.

We now show as in

that vertex degrees are well approximated by i.i.d. Poisson variables conditioned to be at least

. As it will appear a lot, we define

Lemma 6
.

Let
C>0
be a constant and assume that

and

1.

For all

(5)

Moreover, if

(7)

For all

and

(9)

4.

Let

(10)

where the

m,C
.

Proof.

1.

This item is exactly the same as

\mathbb{P}\left(\deg_{G_{k}}(j)\geqslant\log n|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)=O\left(\sqrt{V_{k}Z_{k}}\sum_{i>\log n}\frac{Z_{k}^{i}}{i!f(Z_{k})}\right).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\deg_{G_{k}}(j)\geqslant\log n|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)

We now move on to the case

of (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\deg_{G_{k}}(j)=2|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{i\geqslant 3}\mathbb{P}\left(\deg_{G_{k}}(j)=i|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{i=3}^{\log n}\frac{Z_{k}^{i}}{i!f(Z_{k})}+O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}Z_{k}f(Z_{k})}\sum_{i=3}^{\log n}\frac{i^{2}}{i!}Z_{k}^{i}\right)+O\left(n^{-10}\right).

(11)

Since

by the same computation as in the proof of (

O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\right)
. We now have to handle the first error term of (

O\left(\frac{Z_{k}^{2}}{V_{k}f(Z_{k})}\sum_{i=3}^{\log n}\frac{i^{2}}{i!}Z_{k}^{i-3}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\sum_{i\geqslant 3}\frac{i^{2}}{i!}C^{i-3}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\right).

The second error term is

2.

This is proved in
[
1
, Lemma 5]

—

in

\mathbb{P}\left(\deg_{G_{k}}(j_{1})=2\mbox{ and }\deg_{G_{k}}(j_{2})=2|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)\\

The proof of the three-point estimate (

Asymptotic drift.

We now define the functions that will describe the drift of our Markov chain
(X,V,S)
in the fluid limit. In the equations below, we will just write
z
instead of the
z(v,s)
given by (
2
). For
x,v,s>0
, we write

\displaystyle\Phi_{A}(x,v,s)

\displaystyle=-1-\frac{x}{x+2v+s}+\frac{v^{2}z^{4}\mathrm{e}^{z}}{(x+2v+s)^{2}f(z)^{2}}-\frac{xvz^{2}\mathrm{e}^{z}}{(x+2v+s)^{2}f(z)},

(12)

\displaystyle\Phi_{B}(x,v,s)

\displaystyle=-1+\frac{x}{x+2v+s}-\frac{v^{2}z^{4}\mathrm{e}^{z}}{(x+2v+s)^{2}f(z)^{2}},

(13)

\displaystyle\Phi_{C}(x,v,s)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{x}{x+2v+s}-2\frac{vz^{2}\mathrm{e}^{z}}{(x+2v+s)f(z)}+\frac{v^{2}z^{4}\mathrm{e}^{z}}{(x+2v+s)^{2}f(z)^{2}}+\frac{xvz^{2}\mathrm{e}^{z}}{(x+2v+s)^{2}f(z)}.

(14)

It was proved in
[
1
, Lemma 6]
that the fluid limit of the Markov chain
(X,V,S)
satisfies a differential equation given by these three functions. We write
\Delta X_{k}
for
X_{k+1}-X_{k}
, and similarly for the other processes. As we need slightly better error terms than those of
[
1
]
, we will reprove some of those estimates.

Proposition 1
.

Let
C>0
. If
V_{k}Z_{k}\geqslant\log^{3}n
and
Z_{k}\leqslant C
and
X_{k}>0
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta X_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\Phi_{A}\left(X_{k},V_{k},S_{k}\right)+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}}\right),

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta V_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\Phi_{B}\left(X_{k},V_{k},S_{k}\right)+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}Z_{k}}\right),

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta S_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\Phi_{C}\left(X_{k},V_{k},S_{k}\right)+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}Z_{k}}\right),

(17)

where the constants implied by the
O
notation only depend on
C
.

Proof.

To make the following computations more compact, we introduce the number
H_{k}:=2V_{k}+X_{k}+S_{k}=2M_{k}
of half-edges of the graph
G_{k}
. Note that
H_{k}
corresponds to
2m
in the notations of
[
1
, Lemma 6]
, so all the error terms
O\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)
of
[
1
]
can be replaced by
O\left(\frac{1}{H_{k}}\right)
, which is also
O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\right)
.

Equations (
16
) and (
17
) are already proved in
[
1
, Lemma 6]
(with the natural substitution of the number
2m
of half-edges by
x+2v+s
), so there is nothing to do. On the other hand (
15
) is only proved in
[
1
]
with an error term
O\left(\frac{\log^{2}V_{k}}{V_{k}Z_{k}}\right)
, which we need to improve. However, our argument will follow
[
1
]
closely.

Let
k\geqslant 0
be such that
V_{k}
,
X_{k}
and
Z_{k}
satisfy the assumptions of the Proposition. We recall that to pass from
G_{k}
to
G_{k+1}
, we start by removing the leaf
1
of
G_{k}
, and we denote by
y
its unique neighbour in
G_{k}
. All of the vertex degrees in the proof below will be degrees in the graph
G_{k}
. We denote by
D_{k}
the degree of
y
and by
D_{k}^{(1)}
(resp.
D_{k}^{(2)}
,
D_{k}^{(3)}
) the number of neighbours of degree
1
(resp.
2
, at least
3
) of
y
in
G_{k}
, with the leaf
1
counted in
D_{k}^{(1)}
. Let also
\widetilde{D}_{k}
be the total number of loops and multiple edges incident to
y
. It is proved in
[
1
, Equation (8)]
that

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{D}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=O\left(\frac{1}{H_{k}}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\right).

(18)

Moreover, we can write

\Delta X_{k}=-D_{k}^{(1)}-\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}=1}+D_{k}^{(2)}+O\left(\widetilde{D}_{k}\right).

(19)

Indeed, the leaves that are removed are the neighbours of
y
, plus
y
itself if it has degree
1
, whereas the leaves which are created are the neighbours of
y
with degree
2
(unless they are linked to
y
by multiple edges, which is accounted for by the term
O\left(\widetilde{D}_{k}\right)
). We now estimate the expectations of these terms one by one. First, by Lemma
2
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{k}=1|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)=\frac{X_{k}-1}{H_{k}-1}.

(20)

Moreover, let
\mathcal{D}_{k}
be the family of vertex degrees of
G_{k}
. By Lemma
2
again, we can write

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{(1)}|\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathcal{D}_{k}\right]=1+O(\widetilde{D}_{k})+\sum_{j;\deg(j)\geqslant 2}\frac{\deg(j)}{H_{k}-1}\cdot\left(\deg(j)-1\right)\cdot\frac{X_{k}-1}{H_{k}-3},

where the sum over
j
corresponds to the possible values of
y
and the error term accounts for the possibility of loops and multiple edges around
y
. We can now integrate over
\mathcal{D}_{k}
and use (
18
) and Lemma
6
. By decomposing according to the possible values
d
of
\deg(j)
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{(1)}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=1+O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\right)+\frac{X_{k}-1}{(H_{k}-1)(H_{k}-3)}\sum_{j=X_{k}+1}^{X_{k}+V_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\deg(j)\left(\deg(j)-1\right)|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=1+O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\right)+\frac{(X_{k}-1)V_{k}}{(H_{k}-1)(H_{k}-3)}\cdot\Bigg(\frac{2Z_{k}^{2}}{2f(Z_{k})}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{d=3}^{\log n}\frac{d(d-1)Z_{k}^{d}}{d!f(Z_{k})}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}Z_{k}}\right)\right)+O\left(n^{-8}\right)\Bigg),

(21)

where the last term is the contribution of
\deg(j)\geqslant\log n
and comes from (
7
). The second error term is
O\left(\frac{X_{k}Z_{k}^{2}}{H_{k}^{2}f(Z_{k})}\right)
, which is
O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\right)
since
Z_{k}^{2}=O(f(Z_{k}))
and
X_{k},V_{k}\leqslant H_{k}
(we are here slightly more accurate than
[
1
]
). On the other hand, for
3\leqslant d\leqslant\log n
, we have
\frac{Z_{k}^{d}}{f(Z_{k})}=\frac{Z_{k}^{2}}{f(Z_{k})}\cdot Z_{k}\cdot Z_{k}^{d-3}=O\left(C^{d-3}Z_{k}\right)
, where
C
is an upper bound on
Z_{k}
as in the statement of the Proposition. Hence, the corresponding error term is
O\left(\frac{C^{d}\log^{2}n}{(d-2)!V_{k}}\right)
. Therefore, the sum of the error terms over
3\leqslant d\leqslant\log n
is
O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}}\right)
. Finally, the last error term is
O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}}\right)
. Summing the main terms as in
[
1
, Equation (17)]
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{(1)}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=1+\frac{X_{k}V_{k}Z_{k}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{Z_{k}}}{H_{k}^{2}f(Z_{k})}+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}}\right).

(22)

We now estimate the number of neighbours of
y
with degree
2
using similar ideas. We first write

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{(2)}|\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathcal{D}_{k}\right]=O\left(\widetilde{D}_{k}\right)+\sum_{j_{1};\,\deg(j_{1})\geqslant 2}\,\sum_{j_{2};\,\deg(j_{2})=2}\frac{\deg(j_{1})}{H_{k}-1}\times(\deg(j_{1})-1)\times\frac{2}{H_{k}-3},

where
j_{1}
corresponds to possible values of
y
, and
j_{2}
corresponds to possible values of neighbours of
y
other than
1
. Again, we integrate over
\mathcal{D}_{k}
and use (
18
) to bound the error term:

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{(2)}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\right)+\frac{2}{(H_{k}-1)(H_{k}-3)}\sum_{j_{1}=X_{k}+1}^{X_{k}+V_{k}}\,\sum_{j_{2}=X_{k}+1}^{X_{k}+V_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\deg(j_{1})(\deg(j_{1})-1)\mathbbm{1}_{\deg(j_{2})=2}\right].

We then decompose each term according to the value of
\deg(j_{1})
as in (
21
) and apply the estimate (
8
) from Lemma
6
. The error terms are the same as in the computation for
D_{1}^{(k)}
so, using our improved error in (
8
) for
i_{1}=i_{2}=2
, we find

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{(2)}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=\frac{V_{k}^{2}Z_{k}^{4}\mathrm{e}^{Z_{k}}}{H_{k}^{2}f(Z_{k})^{2}}+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}}\right),

(23)

which again is a slight improvement compared to
[
1
, Equation (18)]
. Finally, we obtain (
15
) with the right error term by plugging (
18
), (
20
), (
22
) and (
23
) in (
19
).
∎

2.3
General variance estimates

In order to show that the Markov chain stays close to its fluid limit, we will also need to control its variance. As we will see later in Section
5.2
, it will be particularly important for us to have a precise estimate on
\mathrm{Var}(\Delta X_{k})
in the end of the process. To fix ideas, we mention right now that
\varepsilon n
steps before the end of the process, the ratio
\frac{X_{k}}{V_{k}}
will be of order
\varepsilon
and
Z_{k}
of order
\varepsilon^{1/2}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
C>0
. If
V_{k}Z_{k}\geqslant\log^{3}n
and
Z_{k}\leqslant C
and
X_{k}>0
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\Delta V_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=O(1),

(24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\Delta S_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=O(Z_{k})+O\left(\frac{X_{k}}{V_{k}}\right),

(25)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\Delta X_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=Z_{k}+O\left(Z_{k}^{2}\right)+O\left(\frac{X_{k}}{V_{k}}\right)+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}}\right),

(26)

where the constants implied by the
O
notation only depend on
C
.

Proof.

We keep the notation of the proof of Proposition
1
. We start by proving (
24
). Note that
0\geqslant\Delta V_{k}\geqslant-D_{k}
, so it is enough to prove
\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=O(1)
. For this, we use the same ideas as in the proof of Proposition
1
. We first note that
D_{k}\leqslant n
so by (
7
), the contribution of the event
\{D_{k}>\log n\}
is
O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
. Summing over all possible values of the neighbour
y
of the removed leaf and over all possible values of
D_{k}
between
3
and
\log n
and using Lemma
6
, we find

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}\geqslant 3}|X_{k},V_{k},S_{k}\right]=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)+O\left(\frac{V_{k}}{H_{k}-1}\sum_{d=2}^{\log n}d^{3}\frac{Z_{k}^{d}}{d!f(Z_{k})}\right)=O(1)

using
V_{k}\leqslant H_{k}-1
and
\frac{Z_{k}^{d}}{f(Z_{k})}=O(Z_{k}^{d-2})=O(1)
.

The argument for (
25
) is similar, but we also need to notice that if both
Z_{k}
and
\frac{X_{k}}{V_{k}}
are very small, then the most likely case is that
y
has degree
2
and its second neighbour as well, i.e.
D_{k}=2
and
D_{k}^{(1)}=D_{k}^{(2)}=1
. In this case, we have
\Delta X_{k}=0
but
\Delta V_{k}=-2
and
\Delta H_{k}=-4
, so
\Delta S_{k}=0
. Therefore, we can write the bound

\mathrm{Var}\left(\Delta S_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)=O\left(\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}=1}+\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}\geqslant 3}+\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}=2,D_{k}^{(1)}=2}+\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}=2,D_{k}^{(3)}=1}\right)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\right).

We can then bound each of the four terms one by one. We find that the contributions of the first and third terms are
O\left(\frac{X_{k}}{V_{k}}\right)
, whereas the second and fourth are
O\left(Z_{k}\right)

\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}=2,D_{k}^{(3)}=1}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=\sum_{d\geqslant 3}\sum_{y=X_{k}+1}^{X_{k}+V_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=X_{k}+1\\
w\neq y\end{subarray}}^{X_{k}+V_{k}}4\mathbb{P}\left(\deg(y)=2\mbox{ and }\deg(w)=d\right)\times\frac{2}{H_{k}-1}\times\frac{d}{H_{k}-3}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}=2,D_{k}^{(3)}=1}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=O\left(n^{-8}\right)+O\left(Z_{k}\sum_{d\geqslant 3}\frac{d}{d!}C^{d-3}\right)

where we have used that

, and the assumption

by Lemma

Therefore, by Proposition

\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta X_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]^{2}=O\left(\frac{\log^{4}n}{V_{k}^{2}}\right)+O\left(\frac{X_{k}^{2}}{V_{k}^{2}}\right)+O\left(Z_{k}^{2}\right).

On the other hand, we denote by

the event where

\mathbb{E}\left[(\Delta X_{k})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(A^{1}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(A^{-1}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)+O\left(\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{(A^{0}_{k}\cup A^{1}_{k}\cup A^{-1}_{k})^{c}}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\right).

(27)

\times\frac{3}{H_{k}-1}\frac{2}{H_{k}-3}\frac{2}{H_{k}-5},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A^{1}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{12V_{k}(V_{k}-1)(V_{k}-2)}{(H_{k}-1)(H_{k}-3)(H_{k}-5)}\frac{Z_{k}^{7}}{24f(Z_{k})^{3}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}Z_{k}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}Z_{k}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{V_{k}}\right)+O\left(Z_{k}\right)+O\left(\frac{X_{k}}{H_{k}}\right)+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{V_{k}Z_{k}}\right)\right),

where the second equality uses

Similarly, using Lemma (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}^{-1}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,w=X_{k}+1\\

—

the case

\displaystyle\sum_{d\geqslant 4}\sum_{y,w=X_{k}+1}^{X_{k}+V_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left(\deg(y)=2\mbox{ and }\deg(w)=d|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)\times\frac{2}{H_{k}-1}\frac{d}{H_{k}-3}

—

By a similar computation as in the previous cases, the contribution of the case

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}\geqslant 4}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=O\left(n^{-8}\right)+\sum_{d=4}^{\log n}\sum_{y=X_{k}+1}^{X_{k}+V_{k}}d^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(\deg(y)=d|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)\times\frac{d}{H_{k}-1}

—

Finally, if

and

\displaystyle\sum_{y=X_{k}+1}^{X_{k}+V_{k}}\sum_{w=2}^{X_{k}}\mathbb{P}\left(\deg(y)=3|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)\times\frac{3}{H_{k}-1}\times\frac{2}{H_{k}-3},

.

—

Similarly, the contribution of the case where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[D_{k}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}=3\mbox{ and }D_{k}^{(3)}\geqslant 1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{d\geqslant 3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,w=X_{k}+1\\

3.1
Solution to the differential equations

Following
[
1
]
, let us investigate the solution to the differential equations which appear as the limit in Proposition
1
. We recall from
12
,
13
and
14
the definition of
\Phi_{A}
,
\Phi_{B}
and
\Phi_{C}
. Let
\left(\mathscr{X}(t),\mathscr{V}(t),\mathscr{S}(t)\right)
be the solution of the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathscr{X}^{\prime}&=\Phi_{A}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\right),\\
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}&=\Phi_{B}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\right),\\
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}&=\Phi_{C}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\right),\end{array}\right.

with the initial conditions (coming from Lemma
1
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathscr{X}(0)&=\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}},\\
\mathscr{V}(0)&=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}}-\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}},\\
\mathscr{S}(0)&=\mathrm{e}+\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}}+2\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}}-2.\end{array}\right.

(28)

The solution is given by
[
1
, Lemma 8]
with
c=\mathrm{e}
, where
v_{1}
,
v
and
2m
in
[
1
]
stand respectively for
\mathscr{X}
,
\mathscr{V}
and
\mathscr{X}+2\mathscr{V}+\mathscr{S}
. We write
\mathscr{Z}(t)=z\left(\mathscr{V}(t),\mathscr{S}(t)\right)
, where
z(v,s)
is given by (
2
), and we recall that
f(z)=\mathrm{e}^{z}-z-1
.
For
z\geqslant 0
, let
\beta(z)
be the unique solution in
[\mathrm{e}^{-1},+\infty)
to the equation

\beta(z)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{e}\beta(z)}=\mathrm{e}^{z},

(29)

and note that
\beta(0)=\mathrm{e}^{-1}
. Parametrized by
\mathscr{Z}
, the solutions can be written

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}

\displaystyle=\beta(\mathscr{Z})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathscr{Z}}f(\mathscr{Z}),

(30)

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-1}\mathscr{Z}^{2}-\mathscr{Z}\beta(\mathscr{Z})(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathscr{Z}}),

(31)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}

\displaystyle=\beta(\mathscr{Z})\left(\mathscr{Z}+\mathscr{Z}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathscr{Z}}+2\mathrm{e}^{-\mathscr{Z}}-2\right),

(32)

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=1-\beta(\mathscr{Z})-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{e}}\log^{2}\beta(\mathscr{Z}).

(33)

In
[
1
]
, it is proved in the subcritical and supercritical cases that the Markov chain
(X_{k},V_{k},S_{k})
, once properly rescaled, converges to the process
(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S})
. In the critical case, such a result will follow from the results of Section

.

3.2
The fluid limit near the extinction time

As in
[
6
]
, we will now focus on carefully studying the solutions
\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}
near the extinction time
t^{*}=\inf\{t\geqslant 0|\mathscr{X}(t)=0\}
. We first notice that such a time exists, since plugging
\mathscr{Z}=0
in (
33
) and (
31
) gives
t=1-\frac{3}{2\mathrm{e}}>0
and
\mathscr{X}=0
.

We now argue that we indeed have
t^{*}=1-\frac{3}{2\mathrm{e}}
. For this, let
z^{*}=\mathscr{Z}(t^{*})
. We will show that
z^{*}=0
. Indeed, since
\mathscr{X}(t^{*})=0
, the formula (
31
) gives

\mathrm{e}^{-1}(z^{*})^{2}-z^{*}\beta^{*}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-z^{*}})=0,

where
\beta^{*}=\beta(z^{*})
. Hence, we have either
z^{*}=0
, or
\mathrm{e}^{-1}z^{*}-\beta^{*}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-z^{*}})=0
. In the second case, we replace
z^{*}
by
\mathrm{e}\beta^{*}+\log\beta^{*}
using (
29
). The condition becomes

\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}\log\beta^{*}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}\beta^{*}}=0.

We note that
\beta^{*}=\mathrm{e}^{-1}
(which implies
z^{*}=0
) is a solution, and claim that the left-hand side is increasing in
\beta
, so the solution is unique. Indeed, differentiating the left-hand side with respect to
\beta
, we obtain
\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}\beta}-\mathrm{e}^{1-\mathrm{e}\beta}
. We will prove that this is positive for all
\beta\neq\mathrm{e}^{-1}
, which is equivalent (via the change of variable
y=\mathrm{e}\beta
) to showing
y\mathrm{e}^{1-y}<1
for all
y\neq 1
. This last assertion is immediate by differentiating again with respect to
y
, which proves
\beta^{*}=\mathrm{e}^{-1}
and
z^{*}=0
. Using (
33
), we get
t^{*}=1-\frac{3}{2\mathrm{e}}
.

Moreover, by differentiating (
33
) twice, we find that
t
is a smooth function of
\beta
with

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=0,\quad\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}t}{\mathrm{d}\beta^{2}}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=-2\mathrm{e}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}t}{\mathrm{d}\beta^{3}}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=5\mathrm{e}^{2},

(34)

so
t^{*}-t=\mathrm{e}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right)^{2}-\frac{5}{6}\mathrm{e}^{2}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right)^{3}+O\left((\beta-\beta^{*})^{4}\right)
as
t\to t^{*}
(or equivalently
\beta\to\beta^{*}
). That is, if we write
t=t^{*}-\varepsilon
, we have

\beta=\mathrm{e}^{-1}+\mathrm{e}^{-1/2}\varepsilon^{1/2}+\frac{5}{12}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{3/2}).

(35)

By definition of
\beta
, we have
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=2\mathrm{e}
, so

\mathscr{Z}(t^{*}-\varepsilon)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sim}2\mathrm{e}\left(\beta\left(\mathscr{Z}(t^{*}-\varepsilon)\right)-\beta^{*}\right)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sim}2\mathrm{e}^{1/2}\varepsilon^{1/2}.

(36)

We will need to estimate precisely the processes
\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{Z}
and their derivatives near time
t^{*}
. For this, we note that by replacing
z
by
\mathrm{e}\beta+\log\beta
in (
30
), (
31
), (
32
), these processes are explicit functions of
\beta
. In particular
\mathscr{X}
is a smooth function of
\beta
and we can compute

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{X}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathscr{X}}{\mathrm{d}\beta^{2}}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathscr{X}}{\mathrm{d}\beta^{3}}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=0\mbox{ and }\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{4}\mathscr{X}}{\mathrm{d}\beta^{4}}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=8\mathrm{e}^{3},

(37)

so
\mathscr{X}(t^{*}-\varepsilon)\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{3}}{3}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right)^{4}
, i.e.

\mathscr{X}(t^{*}-\varepsilon)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sim}\frac{\mathrm{e}}{3}\varepsilon^{2}.

(38)

Similarly
\mathscr{V}
is a smooth function of
\beta
with

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{V}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=0\mbox{ and }\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathscr{V}}{\mathrm{d}\beta^{2}}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=4\mathrm{e}\mbox{ and }\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathscr{V}}{\mathrm{d}\beta^{3}}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=-10\mathrm{e}^{2},

(39)

so, using also (
35
):

\mathscr{V}(t^{*}-\varepsilon)=2\mathrm{e}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right)^{2}-\frac{5}{3}\mathrm{e}^{2}(\beta-\beta^{*})^{3}+O\left((\beta-\beta^{*})^{4}\right)=2\varepsilon+O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right).

(40)

We finally do the same computation for
\mathscr{S}
: this is also a smooth function of
\beta
with

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{S}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathscr{S}}{\mathrm{d}\beta^{2}}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=0\mbox{ and }\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathscr{S}}{\mathrm{d}\beta^{3}}\right|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}=8\mathrm{e}^{2},

(41)

so

\mathscr{S}(t^{*}-\varepsilon)=\frac{4}{3}\mathrm{e}^{2}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right)^{3}+O\left((\beta-\beta^{*})^{4}\right)=\frac{4\mathrm{e}^{1/2}}{3}\varepsilon^{3/2}+O(\varepsilon^{2})

(42)

by using (
35
).

Remark
.

As mentioned before, the exponents governing the number of vertices of degrees
1
,
2
and
3
are the same as in the configuration model of
[
6
]
. Moreover, the multiplicative constant
2
appearing in (
40
) is the same as in
[
6
]
and means that in the end, most steps simply consist of removing two vertices of degree
2
. However, we note that the multiplicative constants for
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{S}
are significantly different from their analogs in
[
6
]
. This is due to the impact of vertices of degree
4
. For example, a step where the removed leaf has a neighbour of degree
4
attached to three other vertices of degree
2
will create three new leaves. By (
38
), the (normalized) number of leaves at time

behaves like
\varepsilon^{2}
. On the other hand, by combining the Poisson approximation of Lemma
6
and (
36
), the number of vertices of degree
4
behaves like
\mathscr{V}\times\mathscr{Z}^{2}\approx\varepsilon^{2}
. Hence, the number of vertices of degree
4
is comparable to the number of leaves in the end of the process, which is why their contribution cannot be neglected.

When we will estimate the drift of the Markov chain
\left(X_{k},V_{k},S_{k}\right)
, it will be important to understand the functions
\Phi_{A},\Phi_{B},\Phi_{C}
of (
12
), (
13
), (
14
) in the neighbourhood of the fluid limit
\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\right)
. For this, we will rely on the following estimates on the partial derivatives of
\Phi_{A},\Phi_{B},\Phi_{C}
. For the sake of brevity, we will write
\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}(t)\right)
for
\left(\mathscr{X}(t),\mathscr{V}(t),\mathscr{S}(t)\right)
.

Lemma 7
.

We have the following estimates as
\varepsilon\to 0
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{A}}{\partial x}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\left(t^{*}-\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{-1}+O\left(\varepsilon^{-1/2}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{A}}{\partial v}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\left(t^{*}-\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(1\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{A}}{\partial s}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\left(t^{*}-\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\varepsilon^{-1/2}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{B}}{\partial x}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\left(t^{*}-\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{B}}{\partial v}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\left(t^{*}-\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O(1),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{B}}{\partial s}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\left(t^{*}-\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\varepsilon^{-1/2}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{C}}{\partial x}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\left(t^{*}-\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\varepsilon^{-1/2}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{C}}{\partial v}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\left(t^{*}-\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{1/2}\varepsilon^{-1/2}+O\left(1\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{C}}{\partial s}\left(\mathscr{X},\mathscr{V},\mathscr{S}\left(t^{*}-\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon^{-1}+O\left(\varepsilon^{-1/2}\right).

In particular, the fact that
\frac{\partial\Phi_{A}}{\partial x}
is negative means that
(X_{k})
enjoys a strong “restoring force” towards its fluid limit, which will play an important role later in the paper.

Proof.

The proof is a calculation that we perform using Mathematica (see the attached computation sheet). More precisely, as an example, let us sketch the proof of the estimate on
\frac{\partial\Phi_{A}}{\partial s}
. We recall from (
12
) the formula

\Phi_{A}(x,v,s)=-1-\frac{x}{x+2v+s}+\frac{v^{2}z(v,s)^{4}\mathrm{e}^{z(v,s)}}{(x+2v+s)^{2}f(z(v,s))^{2}}-\frac{xvz(v,s)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{z(v,s)}}{(x+2v+s)^{2}f(z(v,s))},

where we recall that

Lemma 8
.

We denote by

(x,v,s)

where the implied constant only depends on

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi_{A}}{\partial v^{2}}

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{x}{v^{3}}\right)+O\left(\frac{s^{2}}{v^{4}}\right),

(44)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi_{A}}{\partial v\partial s}

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{x}{v^{3}}\right)+O\left(\frac{s}{v^{3}}\right),

(45)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi_{C}}{\partial v^{2}}

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{x}{v^{3}}\right)+O\left(\frac{s}{v^{3}}\right).

(46)

Later, as suggested by (

Proof.

using the assumption

\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi_{A}}{\partial v^{2}}=2\frac{x}{v^{3}}\frac{\partial\overline{\Phi}_{A}}{\partial\overline{x}}+2\frac{s}{v^{3}}\frac{\partial\overline{\Phi}_{A}}{\partial\overline{s}}+\frac{x^{2}}{v^{4}}\frac{\partial^{2}\overline{\Phi}_{A}}{\partial\overline{x}^{2}}+2\frac{xs}{v^{4}}\frac{\partial^{2}\overline{\Phi}_{A}}{\partial\overline{x}\partial\overline{s}}+\frac{s^{2}}{v^{4}}\frac{\partial^{2}\overline{\Phi}_{A}}{\partial\overline{s}^{2}},

.

As

, we have

\displaystyle=O(1),

and

5
Good region and a stochastic differential equation

5.1
Rough behaviour of the fluctuations

The goal of this section is to control the fluctuations of the Markov chain
(X,V,S)
around its fluid limit by using the drift and variance estimates of Section
4
. Since most of the proofs of this section are straightforward adaptations from
[
6
, Section 4.2]
, we focus primarily on emphasizing the differences. We keep the same notation as in Section
4
. In particular, we recall that
\theta^{n}
is the first time where
X
hits
0
and that
\widetilde{\theta}^{n}=\theta^{n}\wedge\left(t^{*}n-n^{3/5-1/100}\right)
, with
t^{*}=1-\frac{3}{2\mathrm{e}}
. We also recall that we are working with the multigraph model
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
with
m_{n}=\frac{\mathrm{e}}{2}n+O(\sqrt{n})
.

We start with the control of the rescaled fluctuations
\widetilde{A}
,
\widetilde{B}
and
\widetilde{C}
in the “bulk” of the interval
[0,t^{*}]
.
For
\eta>0
, we write
k_{0}(\eta)=\lfloor(t^{*}-\eta)n\rfloor
. For
\eta,K>0
, we denote by
\mathcal{R}^{n}_{\eta,K}
the set of triples
(a,b,c)
such that
|a|,|b|,|c|\leqslant K
and

\mathbb{P}\left(\widetilde{A}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)}=a,\widetilde{B}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)}=b,\widetilde{C}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)}=c\right)>0.

We also define the event
\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}^{n}
by

\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}^{n}=\left\{\widetilde{\theta}^{n}>k_{0}(\eta)\ \mbox{ and }\ \left(\widetilde{A}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{B}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{C}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right)\in\mathcal{R}^{n}_{\eta,K}\right\}.

We will make the dependence in
n
implicit when there is no ambiguity.

Lemma 11

(Fluctuations in the bulk)
.

For all
\eta,\delta>0
, there exists
K_{\eta,\delta}>0
such that for
n
large enough, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}^{n}_{\eta,K_{\eta,\delta}}\right)\geqslant 1-\delta.

(74)

Proof.

The proof of this result is similar to that of
[
6
, Lemma 2]
, with the difference that here, the initial fluctuations at time
k=0
are random and of order
\sqrt{n}
instead of just being
0
. More precisely, by Lemma
1
and the choice (
28
) of the initial condition
\left(\mathscr{X}(0),\mathscr{V}(0),\mathscr{S}(0)\right)
, the initial fluctuations
\left(\widetilde{A}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{B}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{C}_{0}^{n}\right)
are tight.

On the other hand, just like in
[
6
]
, we can apply the results of Ethier and Kurtz
[
7
, Theorem 2.3]

conditionally on the initial fluctuations

\left(\widetilde{A}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{B}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{C}_{0}^{n}\right)
. More precisely, if
z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are such that
\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\widetilde{A}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{B}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{C}_{0}^{n}\right)=z_{n}\right)>0
and
z_{n}\to z_{\infty}
, then the law of
\left(\widetilde{A}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{B}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{C}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right)
conditionally on
\left(\widetilde{A}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{B}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{C}_{0}^{n}\right)=z_{n}
converges to the law of a random Gaussian vector
G_{\eta}(z_{\infty})
whose mean and covariances depend on
\eta
and
z_{\infty}
. That is, for any bounded continuous function
f
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\widetilde{A}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{B}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{C}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right)|\left(\widetilde{A}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{B}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{C}_{0}^{n}\right)=z_{n}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\mathbb{E}\left[f(G_{\eta}(z_{\infty}))\right].

(75)

Therefore, let us fix a subsequence. Up to extracting a subsubsequence, we may assume that
\left(\widetilde{A}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{B}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{C}_{0}^{n}\right)
converges in distribution to a random variable
Z_{\infty}
. By the Skorokhod convergence theorem, let us assume that the convergence is almost sure. Then by (
75
), for any bounded continuous
f
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\widetilde{A}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{B}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{C}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right)|\left(\widetilde{A}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{B}_{0}^{n},\widetilde{C}_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{a.s.}\mathbb{E}\left[f(G_{\eta}(Z_{\infty}))\right].

By dominated convergence, this shows that
\left(\widetilde{A}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{B}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{C}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right)
converges in distribution. We have proved that any subsequence admits a subsubsequence along which the triple
\left(\widetilde{A}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{B}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)},\widetilde{C}^{n}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right)
converges in distribution, which shows that it is tight.

Moreover, the same proof applies to show that the variable
\max_{0\leqslant k\leqslant k_{0}(\eta)}|\widetilde{A}^{n}_{k}|
is tight, which implies
X_{k}>0
for
0\leqslant k\leqslant k_{0}(\eta)
with probability
1-o(1)
, so
\widetilde{\theta}^{n}>k_{0}(\eta)
.
∎

We will now see the time
k_{0}(\eta)
as the starting point of our process “near the end”. In order to come back from the multigraph to the simple graph model in Section
6
, it will be convenient to prove a version of Theorem
2
that holds conditionally on
(X,V,S)_{k_{0}(\eta)}
. Moreover, although such a conditional result was not written as such in
[

]
, its proof for the model studied in
[
6
]
would be exactly the same. We recall that

stands for the number of vertices of degree

Theorem 3
.

There is
\eta_{0}>0
such that the following holds. Let

and

, and let

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbf{D}(n))\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbf{D}(\infty))\right]\right|\\

\widetilde{C}
, we consider instead the process
\widetilde{E}
defined by
\widetilde{E}_{k}=\widetilde{C}_{k}-\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}\widetilde{B}_{k}
in view of the form of the drift estimate (
55
). We thus say that
(\widetilde{A}_{k},\widetilde{B}_{k},\widetilde{C}_{k})
is in the
good region
as long as
\max\left(\frac{\widetilde{A}_{k}}{|\log(\varepsilon_{k})|^{3/4}},\widetilde{B}_{k},\widetilde{E}_{k}\right)\leqslant K^{\prime}
. We denote by
L
the first time at which the process exits this good region and evaluate separately the probability to exit this good region via one of the three processes
\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B},\widetilde{E}
. In each case, we will decompose the process into its predictable and martingale parts. For all the proof, we fix
\eta>0
, whose value will be precised later, and work under the event
\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}
.

Control of
\widetilde{B}
.
We start from
k_{0}(\eta)=\lfloor(t^{*}-\eta)n\rfloor
. We can assume
K^{\prime}>K
, so the event
\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}
implies that
L>k_{0}(\eta)
, so we can apply the estimates of Propositions
3
and
4
until the exit time
L
of the good region. We then decompose the fluctuations along scales of the form
k_{j}:=\lfloor(t^{*}-2^{-j}\eta)n\rfloor
. Note that the estimate (
54
) is even better than its equivalent
[
6
, Equation (16)]
. By the same computation as for
[
6
, Proposition 6]
, we obtain that for all
k_{0}(\eta)\leqslant k\leqslant\widetilde{\theta}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}_{k\leqslant L}\left|\sum_{\ell=k_{0}(\eta)}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\widetilde{B}_{\ell}|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\right]\right|\leqslant K^{\prime}\cdot(\mathrm{Cst}\cdot\sqrt{\eta}|\log(\eta)|),

where
\mathrm{Cst}>0
is an absolute constant. In particular, using Doob’s maximal inequality, our estimate (
58
) in the good region and the assumption that
|\widetilde{B}_{k_{0}(\eta)}|\leqslant K<K^{\prime}/2
if
K^{\prime}
was chosen large enough, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(L<\widetilde{\theta}^{n}\mbox{ and we exit the good region by }\widetilde{B}|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right)\leqslant\frac{4}{K^{\prime 2}/4}\mathbb{E}\left[(M_{L}^{B})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right],

(79)

where
M^{B}
is the martingale part of
\widetilde{B}
. Note that this estimate is slightly simpler to obtain than in
[
6
]
since we condition on
\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}
, so we do not need to handle the term
\mathbb{P}\left(|\widetilde{B}_{k_{0}(\eta)}|>K^{\prime}\right)
. By orthogonality of martingale increments in
L^{2}
, Equation (
79
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(L<\widetilde{\theta}^{n}\mbox{ and we exit the good region by }\widetilde{B}|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{16}{K^{\prime 2}}\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{1}_{k\leqslant L}\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\widetilde{B}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k})|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{16K^{\prime\prime}\eta}{K^{\prime 2}},

where
K^{\prime\prime}
is an absolute constant coming from the estimate (
58
). This can be made smaller than
\delta
by taking
K^{\prime}
large enough.

Control of
\widetilde{E}
.
To prove that the process does not exit the good region via
\widetilde{E}
, we use more carefully the “restoring force” effect of its conditional drift. In particular, we introduce
L_{E}^{-}
the last time between
k_{0}(\eta)
and
L
at which
\widetilde{E}
is smaller than
K^{\prime}/2
. For all
L_{E}^{-}\leqslant k\leqslant L
, using the form of the expected drift given by (
54
) and (
55
), we can prove that, if
\eta
was chosen small enough, we have for all
L_{E}^{-}<k<L
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\widetilde{E}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\leqslant-\frac{K^{\prime}}{4\varepsilon_{k}n}<0,

which “pulls back” the fluctuation
\widetilde{E}
towards
0
. Thus, on the event
\{k_{0}(\eta)<L_{E}^{-}<L<\widetilde{\theta}^{n}\mbox{ and }\widetilde{E}_{L}>K^{\prime}\}
, the variation of the martingale part of
\widetilde{E}
must be larger than
K^{\prime}/2
over
[L_{E}^{-},L]
to compensate the predictable part. Using Doob’s maximal inequality and our variance estimates (
58
) and (
59
), we can show that the probability that the martingale part compensates the predictable part over
[L_{E}^{-},L]
is smaller than
\mathrm{Cst}\sqrt{\eta}/K^{\prime 2}
. If
K^{\prime}
was chosen large enough, we thus obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(k_{0}(\eta)<L_{E}^{-}<L<T\mbox{ and }\widetilde{E}_{L}>K^{\prime}|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right)\leqslant\delta.

Combining this with the symmetric case
\widetilde{E}_{L}<-K^{\prime}
, this finishes the control of
\widetilde{E}
.

Control of
\widetilde{A}
.
The control of
\widetilde{A}
is the most subtle since we use the strength of the restoring force of the drift (and not only its sign). The drift estimate (
53
) has the same form as
[
6
, Equation (15)]
in the good region. Moreover, the variance estimate (
57
) only differs by a (multiplicative) constant from
[
6
, Equation (19)]
, which is not important for this rough control. Thus the same proof shows that the probability to exit the good region via
\widetilde{A}
is small and concludes the proof.

We highlight that in all the proof, the reason why we needed to take
\eta
small is that the drift estimate of (
53
) (resp. (
55
)) remains of order
-\frac{\widetilde{A}_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}n}
(resp.
-\frac{\widetilde{E}_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}n}
). Since the constants in Proposition
3
are absolute, the bound
\eta_{0}
on how small
\eta
needs to be depends neither on
\delta
nor on
K
. This will be important later to avoid circular dependencies (in Section
6
, we will choose
\eta
depending on
\delta
).
∎

5.2
Reaching the end via a stochastic differential equation

We recall that the number of leaves after
k
steps is
X_{k}=n\mathscr{X}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)+\varepsilon_{k}^{3/4}\sqrt{n}\widetilde{A}_{k}
. By Proposition
5
and (
38
), we deduce that the process
X_{k}
stays positive as long as

t^{*}n-k\gg n^{3/5}(\log n)^{3/5}.

We will now refine the control on
\widetilde{A}
in order to remove the
\log^{3/5}n
factor. To look more precisely at times of order
n^{3/5}
before the end, we introduce, for all
k\geqslant 0
, the notation

t_{k}:=n^{-3/5}(t^{*}n-k)\quad\mbox{so that}\quad k=t^{*}n-t_{k}n^{3/5}\mbox{ and }\varepsilon_{k}=t_{k}n^{-2/5}.

Proposition 6

(Control of
\widetilde{A}
near the end)
.

There is
\eta_{0}>0
such that for all
0<\eta<\eta_{0}
and
\delta>0
and
K>0
, there exists
K^{\prime}
such that for
n
large enough, under the event
\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\forall k\geqslant k_{0}(\eta)$ such that $t_{k}\geqslant K^{\prime}$, we have $|\widetilde{A}_{k}|<K^{\prime}t_{k}^{1/8}$}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right)\geqslant 1-\delta.

The proof is essentially the same as that of
[
6
, Proposition 7]
, the only differences being the conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}
(which does not change anything to the argument), and the value of the constant in front of the variance (which does not play an important role). For this reason, we do not reproduce it here.
Equipped with this control on the fluctuations, we now have all the tools to study the convergence of the stopping time
\theta^{n}
. We note that
t_{\theta^{n}}\in\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\theta^{n}=t^{*}n-t_{\theta^{n}}n^{3/5}.

We now show the convergence in distribution of
t_{\theta^{n}}
to a certain random variable. We will need a statement in the same form as for Theorem
3
, i.e. a uniform result conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}
.

Proposition 7

(Convergence of the stopping time)
.

There is
\eta_{0}>0
such that the following holds. Let
0<\eta<\eta_{0}
and
K>0
, and let
(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n})\in\mathcal{R}^{n}_{\eta,K}
for all
n
. Then we have the convergence of conditional distributions

t_{\theta^{n}}\Big|\left\{\left(\widetilde{A}^{n},\widetilde{B}^{n},\widetilde{C}^{n}\right)_{k_{0}(\eta)}=(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n})\right\}\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{(d)}6^{4/5}\mathrm{e}^{-3/5}\vartheta^{-2},

(80)

where
\vartheta:=\inf\{t\geqslant 0:W_{t}=-t^{-2}\}
is the hitting time of the curve
t\mapsto-t^{-2}
by a standard Brownian motion
W
started from
0
at time
0
.

Proof.

Let
\eta_{0}
be given by Propositions
5
and
6
, and let us fix
0<\eta<\eta_{0}
,
K>0
and
(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n})\in\mathcal{R}^{n}_{\eta,K}
for all
n
. In all the proof, we work under the conditioning of (
80
). The form of the drift and variance estimates in Equations (
53
) and (
56
) suggests that near the end, the fluctuation process
(\widetilde{A}_{t^{*}n-tn^{3/5}}:-\infty<t<t_{\theta^{n}})
converges weakly (for the
\|\ \|_{\infty}
-norm) toward a process
\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}\right)_{-\infty<t\leqslant\tau}
satisfiying

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}_{t}=-\frac{\mathcal{A}_{t}}{4|t|}\mathrm{d}t+\frac{2\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}{t}\mathrm{d}W_{t},

where
W
is a standard Brownian motion. To make this heuristic precise, the proof is similar to that of
[
6
, Proposition 8]
. In particular, we need to consider the renormalized process

\widetilde{F}_{k}=\frac{\widetilde{A}_{k}}{t_{k}^{1/4}},\quad 0\leqslant k\leqslant\theta^{n}.

We fix
\delta>0
and we take
K^{\prime}>0
such that on an event of probability at least
1-2\delta
, the conclusions of Proposition
5
and
6
hold. We also fix
\xi\in\left(0,K^{\prime-1}\right)
such that
K^{\prime}\xi^{1/8}\leqslant\delta
. To avoid stopping time issues, we extend
\widetilde{F}
after time
\theta^{n}
by a process
\widehat{F}
with increments
\pm\frac{2\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}{t_{k}^{3/2}n^{3/5}}
with probability
1/2
, as in the proof of
[
6
, Proposition 8]
. Then with probability at least
1-2\delta
, for all
k
such that
\xi<t_{k}<\xi^{-1}
, we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle|\widehat{F}_{nt^{*}-\xi^{{-1}}n^{3/5}}|<\delta,\quad(\mbox{by Prop. \ref{prop:controlA} and the assumption }K^{\prime}\xi^{1/8}\leqslant\delta),\\
\\
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta\widehat{F}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]=o(n^{-3/5})\cdot|\widehat{F}_{k}|+o(n^{-3/5}),\\
\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\Delta\widehat{F}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right)=\frac{2\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}{t_{k}^{3/2}n^{3/5}}+o(n^{-3/5}),\\
\displaystyle\|\Delta\widehat{F}_{k}\|_{\infty}=o(1).\end{array}\right.

The proof of these estimates relying on Propositions
3
and
4
is exactly the same as in
[
6
, Proposition 8]
, except for the last one. Indeed, in
[
6
]
the vertex degrees were bounded, so it was immediate that the increments of
X
are
O(1)
, so the increments of
\widehat{F}
are
O\left(n^{-3/5}\right)
. In our setting the vertex degrees are not bounded. However, an immediate consequence of (
7
) applied to
k=k_{0}(\eta)
is that with high conditional probability (under the conditioning of (
80
)), all the vertex degrees at time
k_{0}(\eta)
are smaller than
\log n
. This implies that the increments of
X
after time
k_{0}(\eta)
are at most
\log^{2}n
, so
\|\Delta\widehat{F}_{k}\|_{\infty}=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{3/5}}\right)
on a very high probability event.

Using standard diffusion approximation results (see e.g.
[
13
]
) and the Dubins–Schwarz theorem, and letting
(\delta,\xi)\to(0,0)
, we deduce the following weak convergence over all compact subsets of
(0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{F}_{t^{*}n-tn^{3/5}}\right)_{0<t<\infty}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}2\cdot\mathrm{e}^{1/4}\left(W_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}}\right)_{0<t<\infty},

(81)

still under the conditioning (
80
), and where
W
is a standard Brownian motion with
W_{0}=0
. Then we obtain

t_{\theta^{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\tau:=\sup\{t\geqslant 0|2\cdot\mathrm{e}^{1/4}W_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}}=-\frac{\mathrm{e}}{3}t\},

still under the same conditioning. By scaling, we finally find
\tau=\frac{6^{4/5}}{\mathrm{e}^{3/5}}\vartheta^{-2}
, where
\vartheta
is given by

\vartheta=\inf\{t\geqslant 0|W_{t}=t^{-2}\}.

(82)

∎

5.3
Size and composition of the Karp–Sipser core

We have now all the tools to finish the proof of Theorem
3
. We first recall that by Lemma
4
, conditionally on
\left(V_{\theta^{n}},S_{\theta^{n}}\right)
, the core follows the distribution
\mathbb{G}\left(0,V_{\theta^{n}},S_{\theta^{n}}\right)
. Therefore, we first need to estimate
\left(V_{\theta^{n}},S_{\theta^{n}}\right)
.

For this, let us work under the same conditioning as in (
80
). By Proposition
7
, the renormalized stopping time
t_{\theta^{n}}
converges in distribution towards
6^{4/5}\mathrm{e}^{-3/5}\vartheta^{-2}
. Hence, by definition of the fluctuations
B
, we can write

V_{\theta^{n}}\quad=\underbrace{B_{\theta^{n}}}_{\underset{\mathrm{Prop}.\ref{prop:roughbounds}}{=}O\left(\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{3/4}\right)}+\underbrace{n\mathscr{V}\left(\frac{\theta^{n}}{n}\right)}_{\underset{\eqref{eqn:asymptotic_fluid_vertices}}{\sim}2t_{\theta^{n}}n^{3/5}}\quad=2t_{\theta^{n}}n^{3/5}+o\left(n^{3/5}\right)

in probability. Similarly, at time
\theta_{n}
, the surplus is given by

\displaystyle S_{\theta^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{n\mathscr{S}\left(\frac{\theta^{n}}{n}\right)}_{\underset{\eqref{eqn:asymptotic_fluid_excess}}{\sim}\frac{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}{3}t_{\theta^{n}}^{3/2}n^{2/5}}+\underbrace{C_{\theta^{n}}}_{\underset{\mathrm{Prop}.\ref{prop:roughbounds}}{=}O\left(n^{3/10}\log(n)\right)},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}{3}t_{\theta^{n}}^{3/2}n^{2/5}+o\left(n^{2/5}\right)

in probability. In other words, still under the conditioning of (
80
), we have the convergence in distribution

\displaystyle\left(\frac{V_{\theta^{n}}}{n^{3/5}},\frac{S_{\theta^{n}}}{(V_{\theta^{n}})^{2/3}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}

\displaystyle\left(2\cdot 6^{4/5}\mathrm{e}^{-3/5}\vartheta^{-2},\frac{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}(6^{4/5}\mathrm{e}^{-3/5}\vartheta^{-2})^{3/2}}{3(2\cdot 6^{4/5}\mathrm{e}^{-3/5}\vartheta^{-2})^{2/3}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(2^{9/5}3^{4/5}\mathrm{e}^{-3/5}\vartheta^{-2},2\cdot 3^{-1/3}\vartheta^{-5/3}\right).

(83)

To pass from the two parameters
V_{\theta^{n}},S_{\theta^{n}}
to a full understanding of the vertex degrees, we will use the following lemma.

Lemma 12
.

Let
x>0
and let
(v_{n}),(s_{n})
be sequences of nonnegative integers such that
v_{n}\to+\infty
and
s_{n}=xv_{n}^{2/3}+o(v_{n}^{2/3})
as
n\to+\infty
. We also assume that
\log^{4}n\leqslant v_{n}\leqslant n
and that
s_{n}\leqslant n
.
Then we have the following convergences in distribution:

\displaystyle\frac{\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree $2$ in $\mathbb{G}(0,v_{n},s_{n})$}\}}{v_{n}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}1,

\displaystyle\frac{\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree $3$ in $\mathbb{G}(0,v_{n},s_{n})$}\}}{xv_{n}^{2/3}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}1,

\displaystyle\frac{\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree $4$ in $\mathbb{G}(0,v_{n},s_{n})$}\}}{(3/4)\cdot x^{2}v_{n}^{1/3}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}1,

\displaystyle\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree $5$ in $\mathbb{G}(0,v_{n},s_{n})$}\}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}\mathrm{Poisson}\left(\frac{9x^{3}}{20}\right),

\displaystyle\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree at least $6$ in $\mathbb{G}(0,v_{n},s_{n})$}\}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{(d)}0.

From here, we can easily deduce Theorem
3
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
\eta_{0}
be given by Proposition
7
. As before, we fix
0<\eta<\eta_{0}
,
K>0
and
(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n})\in\mathcal{R}^{n}_{\eta,K}
for all
n
. As it is the only case with two levels of randomness involved, we only write down the proof completely for vertices of degree
5
. We recall that
\mathbb{D}_{5}(n)
stands for the number of vertices of degree
5
in the Karp–Sipser core of
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
. We will also write
\mathbb{E}_{a,b,c}[\cdot]
for conditional expectations on the event
(\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B},\widetilde{C})_{k_{0}(\eta)}=(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n})
.

Let
g,h:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be two bounded continuous functions. By conditioning on
\left(V_{\theta^{n}},S_{\theta^{n}}\right)
and using Lemma
4
, we can write

\mathbb{E}_{a,b,c}\left[g\left(\frac{V_{\theta^{n}}}{n^{3/5}}\right)h\left(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n)\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{a,b,c}\left[g\left(\frac{V_{\theta^{n}}}{n^{3/5}}\right)H\left(V_{\theta^{n}},\frac{S_{\theta^{n}}}{V_{\theta^{n}}^{2/3}}\right)\right],

(84)

where

H\left(v,x\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[h\left(\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree $5$ in $\mathbb{G}(0,v,xv^{2/3})$}\}\right)\right].

By the Skorokhod embedding theorem, we may assume that the convergence in distribution (
83
) is almost sure. Moreover, Lemma
12
implies that when
v_{n}\to+\infty
and
x_{n}\to x
, the quantity
H\left(v_{n},x_{n}\right)
converges to
\mathbb{E}\left[h\left(\mathrm{Poisson}(9x^{3}/20)\right)\right]
. Therefore, we have almost sure convergence of the quantity inside the expectation in the right-hand side of (
84
). By dominated convergence, we finally obtain

\mathbb{E}_{a,b,c}\left[g\left(\frac{V_{\theta^{n}}}{n^{3/5}}\right)h\left(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n)\right)\right]\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\mathbb{E}_{a,b,c}\left[g\left(\frac{2^{9/5}3^{4/5}}{\mathrm{e}^{3/5}\vartheta^{2}}\right)\times\mathbb{E}\left[h\left(\mathrm{Poisson}\left(\frac{9}{20}\left(\frac{2}{3^{1/3}\vartheta^{5/3}}\right)^{3}\right)\right)\Big|\vartheta\right]\right].

In other words, the pair
\left(\frac{V_{\theta^{n}}}{n^{3/5}},\mathbb{D}_{5}(n)\right)
under
\mathbb{P}_{a,b,c}
converges to a pair of random variables where the first coordinate has the law of
2^{9/5}3^{4/5}\mathrm{e}^{-3/5}\vartheta^{-2}
and, conditionally on the first, the second follows a Poisson distribution. This proves the convergence of
\mathbb{D}_{5}(n)
.

Finally, the argument for vertices of other degrees is the same, except that we need to normalize
\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)
by the right power of
V_{\theta^{n}}
before applying
h
. Note that it is immediate that all the convergences in distribution of Theorem
3
are joint, as all convergences except one are actually convergences in probability to a constant.
∎

Finally, we can now prove Lemma
12
, which is a consequence of the Poisson approximation for the vertex degrees provided by Lemma
6
.

Proof of Lemma
12
.

The proof consists of a first and second moment computation which makes use of Lemma
6
. For the sake of brevity, in this proof, we will write
\mathbb{G}(n)
for
\mathbb{G}(0,v_{n},s_{n})
. We also write
z_{n}=z(v_{n},s_{n})
. By Lemma
5
, our assumptions imply
z_{n}=3xv_{n}^{-1/3}+o(v_{n}^{-1/3})
as
n\to+\infty
. In particular, this implies that
v_{n}
,
s_{n}
and
z_{n}
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
6
. We also recall that the
v_{n}
vertices of
\mathbb{G}(n)
are labelled from
1
to
v_{n}
. Using (
5
), for
2\leqslant d\leqslant 5
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree $d$ in $\mathbb{G}(n)$}\}\right]

\displaystyle=v_{n}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{deg}(1)=\ell\right)

\displaystyle=v_{n}\frac{z_{n}^{d}}{d!f(z_{n})}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{v_{n}\cdot v_{n}^{-1/3}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{2(3x)^{d-2}}{d!}\cdot v_{n}^{\frac{5-d}{3}}.

Similarly, using (
5
) and (
7
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree $\geqslant 6$ in $\mathbb{G}(n)$}\}\right]

\displaystyle=O\left(v_{n}\sum_{d=6}^{\log n}\frac{z_{n}^{d}}{d!f(z_{n})}\right)+O\left(n^{-9}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(v_{n}z_{n}^{4}\right)+O\left(n^{-9}\right).

In particular this is
o(1)
, which is sufficient to prove the part of the Lemma on vertices of degree at least
6
.

We now move on to the second moment computation. By Equation (
8
), for all
2\leqslant d\leqslant 4
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree $d$ in $G(n)$}\}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=v_{n}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{deg}(1)=d\right)+v_{n}(v_{n}-1)\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{deg}(1)=\mathrm{deg}(2)=d\right)

\displaystyle=O(v_{n}^{\frac{5-d}{3}})+\left(v_{n}^{2}+O(v_{n})\right)\frac{z_{n}^{2d}}{d!^{2}f(z_{n})^{2}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{v_{n}^{2/3}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{4(3x)^{2d-4}}{d!^{2}}v_{n}^{\frac{10-2d}{3}}.

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\mathbb{E}\left[\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree $\ell$ in $G(n)$}\}\right]^{2},

where we have used the assumption
v_{n}\geqslant\log^{4}n
to get rid of the error term. This is sufficient to prove the convergence of the number of vertices of degree
d
for
d\in\{2,3,4\}
. However, for
d=5
, we cannot expect concentration around the expectation, so we will need to compute all the moments to prove convergence in distribution.

More precisely, we fix
m\geqslant 1
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
(x)_{m}:=x(x-1)\dots(x-m+1)
the falling factorial of
x
. Using Equation (
10
) from Lemma
6
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\#\{\mbox{vertices of degree $5$ in $\mathbb{G}(n)$}\}\right)_{m}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{v_{n}}\sum_{j_{2}\neq j_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{m}\notin\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m-1}\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{deg}(j_{1})=\dots=\mathrm{deg}(j_{m})=5\right)

\displaystyle=(v_{n})_{m}\cdot(1+o(1))\left(\frac{2\cdot 3^{3}x^{3}v_{n}^{-1}}{5!}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\left(\frac{9}{20}x^{3}\right)^{m}

as
n\to+\infty
. This shows that the factorial moments of the number of vertices of degree
5
converge to those of a Poisson variable, which implies convergence in distribution.
∎

6
From the configuration model back to Erdős–Rényi

We now extend our result to simple graphs in order to prove Theorem
1
. We will rely on the fact that the graph
\mathrm{G}(n,m)
has the law of the multigraph
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
, conditioned to be simple. Note that passing from one model to the other is not obvious: since the probability that
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
is simple does not go to one in the regime we are interested in, the conditioning might “twist” the limiting distribution. Another natural approach, which is followed in
[
8
]
, would be to use the fact that
\mathrm{G}(n,m)
and
\mathbb{G}(n,m)
can be coupled so that the edit distance between them (i.e. the number of edges to add or remove to pass from one to the other) is
O(1)
. Unfortunately, this does not adapt to our problem, since contrary to the matching number, the size of the Karp–Sipser core is not a Lipschitz function of the graph for the edit distance.

To pass from one model to the other, it will be useful to know the probability that a configuration model is a simple graph, so we recall the following classical result (see e.g.
[
14
, Corollary 5.3]
). If
\mathbf{d}=(d_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
is a sequence of integers which is
0
eventually, we write
|\mathbf{d}|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\geqslant 1}id_{i}
. If this quantity is an integer, we denote by
\mathrm{CM}(\mathbf{d})
a configuration model with
d_{i}
vertices of degree
i
for all
i
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\mathbf{d}^{(n)}
be degree sequences such that
|\mathbf{d}^{(n)}|\to+\infty
and such that
\max\{i\geqslant 1|d_{i}^{(n)}>0\}=o\left(\sqrt{|\mathbf{d}^{(n)}|}\right)
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\mathrm{CM}(\mathbf{d}^{(n)})$ is simple}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\sum_{i\geqslant 1}i^{2}d_{i}^{(n)}}{|\mathbf{d}^{(n)}|}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)+o(1)

as
n\to+\infty
.

Again, for
\eta>0
, we recall that
k_{0}(\eta)=\lfloor(t^{*}-\eta)n\rfloor
. We will decompose the event that the graph
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
is simple as the intersection of three events:

—

the event
\mathrm{Simp}_{\to k_{0}(\eta)}
that the execution of the Karp–Sipser algorithm does not encounter any multiple edge or loop until step
k_{0}(\eta)
,

—

the event
\mathrm{Simp}_{k_{0}(\eta)\to\theta^{n}}
that the execution of the Karp–Sipser algorithm does not encounter any multiple edge or loop between step
k_{0}(\eta)
and its end
\theta^{n}
,

—

the event that the Karp–Sipser core is simple.

Note that if a graph contains two multiple edges, then these two edges will be discovered at the same step of the Karp–Sipser algorithm, so the decomposition above is not ambiguous. We want to show that the simplicity of
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
is asymptotically independent from the degrees in its Karp–Sipser core. Roughly speaking, the argument will be to handle the three events of this decomposition as follows:

—

the event
\mathrm{Simp}_{\to k_{0}(\eta)}
is almost independent from the Karp–Sipser core because Theorem
3
holds conditionally of
\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}
(this is why Theorem
2
is not sufficient and we needed Theorem
3
),

—

the event
\mathrm{Simp}_{k_{0}(\eta)\to\theta^{n}}
has probability close to
1
,

—

by Lemma
13
, the probability that the Karp–Sipser core is simple conditionally on its vertex degrees is almost deterministic.

To make this sketch precise, we start with the second item.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\delta>0
. Then there is
\eta>0
such that for
n
large enough, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Simp}_{k_{0}(\eta)\to\theta^{n}}\right)\geqslant 1-\delta.

Proof.

We recall that
G_{k}
is the multigraph obtained after
k
steps of the Karp–Sipser algorithm applied to the random multigraph
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
. We will show that if
\eta
is small enough, then with high probability, all the loops and multiple edges in
G_{k_{0}(\eta)}
are either a loop on a vertex of degree
2
or two edges joining the same pair of vertices of degree
2
. In particular, loops and multiple edges do not have leaves in their connected component, so they will never be removed by the Karp–Sipser algorithm.

Let
\eta>0
(we will fix its value later). For the sake of brevity, we will just write
k_{0}
for
k_{0}(\eta)
. We denote by
\mathcal{E}_{\eta}
the event that
|\widetilde{A}_{k_{0}}|,|\widetilde{B}_{k_{0}}|,|\widetilde{C}_{k_{0}}|\leqslant\log n
. We note that by Lemma
11
, the probability of
\mathcal{E}_{\eta}
tends to
1
as
n\to+\infty
. On the event
\mathcal{E}_{\eta}
, Lemma
10
ensures that the assumptions of Lemma
6
are satisfied, so we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\#\{\mbox{loops of $G_{k_{0}}$ attached to a vertex of degree at least $3$}\}|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}}\right]

\displaystyle\underset{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\text{Lem.\penalty\ 6}}}{=}\sum_{j=X_{k_{0}}+1}^{X_{k_{0}}+V_{k_{0}}}\sum_{i\geqslant 3}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{deg}_{G_{k_{0}}}(j)=i\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}}\right)\frac{i(i-1)}{X_{k_{0}}+2V_{k_{0}}+S_{k_{0}}-1}

\displaystyle\underset{\text{Lem.\penalty\ \ref{lem:degrees_are_Poisson}}}{=}\frac{V_{k_{0}}}{X_{k_{0}}+2V_{k_{0}}+S_{k_{0}}-1}\left(\sum_{i=3}^{\log(n)}i(i-1)\times O\left(\frac{Z_{k_{0}}^{i}}{i!f(Z_{k_{0}})}\right)+O\left(n^{-8}\right)\right),

where the implied constants are absolute. By writing
\frac{Z_{k_{0}}^{i}}{f(Z_{k_{0}})}=O\left(Z_{k_{0}}^{i-2}\right)=O\left(Z_{k_{0}}C^{i-3}\right)
where
C
is an upper bound on
Z
, we find that the last display is
O\left(Z_{k}\right)+O\left(n^{-8}\right)
. Finally, if
\mathcal{E}_{\eta}
occurs, then by Lemma
10
we have
Z_{k_{0}}=O\left(\sqrt{\eta}\right)
. Therefore, there is an absolute constant
c
such that for
n
large enough, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$G_{k_{0}}$ has a loop attached to a vertex with degree at least $3$}\right)\leqslant c\sqrt{\eta}.

We now prove the same with multiple edges. On the event
\mathcal{E}_{\eta}
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\#\{\mbox{pairs of multiple edges of $G_{k_{0}}$ adjacent to a vertex of degree at least $3$}\}|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359pt=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2}=X_{k_{0}}+1\\
j_{1}\neq j_{2}\end{subarray}}^{X_{k_{0}}+V_{k_{0}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\geqslant 3\\
i_{2}\geqslant 2\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{deg}_{G_{k_{0}}}(j_{1})=i_{1},\mathrm{deg}_{G_{k_{0}}}(j_{2})=i_{2}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}}\right)\frac{i_{1}(i_{1}-1)\cdot i_{2}(i_{2}-1)}{(X_{k_{0}}+2V_{k_{0}}+S_{k_{0}}-1)(X_{k_{0}}+2V_{k_{0}}+S_{k_{0}}-3)}

\displaystyle\underset{\text{Lem.\penalty\ \ref{lem:degrees_are_Poisson}}}{=}\frac{V_{k_{0}}(V_{k_{0}}-1)}{(X_{k_{0}}+2V_{k_{0}}+S_{k_{0}}-1)(X_{k_{0}}+2V_{k_{0}}+S_{k_{0}}-3)}\left(O\left(n^{-4}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}3\leqslant i_{1}\leqslant\log(n)\\
2\leqslant i_{2}\leqslant\log(n)\end{subarray}}\frac{i_{1}(i_{1}-1)i_{2}(i_{2}-1)}{i_{1}!i_{2}!}O\left(\frac{Z_{k_{0}}^{i_{1}}Z_{k_{0}}^{i_{2}}}{f(Z_{k_{0}})^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359pt=\ O\left(n^{-4}\right)+O(Z_{k}).

Therefore, we have proved that the probability that
G_{k_{0}}
has a loop or multiple edges incident to a vertex of degree at least
3
is bounded by
c\sqrt{\eta}
for some absolute constant
c
. This proves the Lemma by taking
\eta=\left(\frac{\delta}{c}\right)^{2}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We first prove the result for the model
\mathrm{G}(n,m_{n})
with a fixed number
m_{n}
of edges, where
m_{n}=\frac{\mathrm{e}}{2}n+O\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
. Passing from this model to
G\left[n,\frac{\mathrm{e}}{n}\right]
will follow easily by integrating over the possible values of the number of edges of
G\left[n,\frac{\mathrm{e}}{n}\right]
.

Most of the proof will consist in proving the convergence of vertex degrees (
1
). To keep the notation light, we will write down the proof completely only for vertices of degree
5
.

Let
g
be a bounded, continuous function from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
. For
x>0
, we also write

\widehat{g}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(\mathrm{Poi}\left(\frac{48}{5}x^{-5}\right)\right)\right],

where
\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)
denotes a Poisson variable with parameter
\lambda
. We note that
\widehat{g}
is bounded and is a continuous function of
\theta
. We also recall that for
i\geqslant 2
, we denote by
\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)
the number of vertices of degree
i
in the Karp–Sipser core of
\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})
. We want to prove that

\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}}\right]}{\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}\right)}\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{g}(\vartheta)\right],

(85)

where we recall that
\vartheta
is defined by (
82
). We first notice that by the Poisson approximation of Lemma
6
(for
k=0
) and by Lemma
13
, the denominator of (
85
) is bounded away from
0
(it actually goes to
\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{e}}{2}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}}{4}}
but we will not need the exact computation). We now study the numerator. We fix
\delta>0
. Let
\eta>0
small be given by Lemma
14
, and let
K=K_{\eta,\delta}
be the constant provided by Lemma
11
. We recall that
\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}
is the event that
\theta^{n}>k_{0}(\eta)
and
|\widetilde{A}_{k_{0}(\eta)}|,|\widetilde{B}_{k_{0}(\eta)}|,|\widetilde{C}_{k_{0}(\eta)}|\leqslant K
, and that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}\right)\geqslant 1-\delta
for
n
large enough by Lemma
11
. We also write
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\eta}=\mathrm{Simp}_{k_{0}(\eta)\to\theta^{n}}
. Finally, we write

\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}=\left\{\left|\frac{\sum_{i\geqslant 1}i^{2}\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)}{4\sum_{i\geqslant 1}i\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leqslant n^{-1/10}\right\}.

Note that as a consequence of Theorem
2
, we have
\sum_{i\geqslant 2}i\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)=2\mathbb{D}_{2}(n)\left(1+O(n^{-1/5})\right)
and
\sum_{i\geqslant 2}i^{2}\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)=4\mathbb{D}_{2}(n)\left(1+O(n^{-1/5})\right)
in probability, so
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}\right)\to 1
as
n\to+\infty
. Combining these observations, for
n
large enough, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\eta}\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}\right)^{c}\right)\leqslant 3\delta.

Therefore, we can write

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n))\mathbbm{1}_{\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}}\right]\\
=O\left(\delta\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{Simp}_{\to k_{0}(\eta)}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\eta}}g(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n))\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mbox{$\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n}))$ is simple}}\right],

where the constant implied by the
O
notation only depends on the function
g
. Since
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\eta}\right)\geqslant 1-\delta
and everything inside of the expectation is bounded, we can remove the indicator
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\eta}}
and absorb the difference in the
O(\delta)
term. By conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{\theta^{n}}
and on the degrees in the Karp–Sipser core, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n))\mathbbm{1}_{\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}}\right]\\
=O\left(\delta\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{Simp}_{\to k_{0}(\eta)}}g(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n))\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n}))$ is simple}|\mathcal{F}_{\theta^{n}},(\mathbb{D}_{i}(n))_{i\geqslant 2}\right)\right].

By Lemma
13
, under the event
\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
, the conditional probability for
\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n}))
to be simple is
\mathrm{e}^{-3/4}+o(1)
, where
o(1)
is uniform. Again, the error term can be absorbed in the
O(\delta)
term, and we can then remove the indicator
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}}
as we did before for
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{\eta}}
. By conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}
, the last display rewrites

\displaystyle O(\delta)+\mathrm{e}^{-3/4}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{Simp}_{\to k_{0}(\eta)}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}}\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n))|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right]\right].

By the Markov property, on the event
\theta^{n}>k_{0}(\eta)
(which is implied by
\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}
), the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n))|\mathcal{F}_{k_{0}(\eta)}\right]
is a measurable function of
\left(\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B},\widetilde{C}\right)_{k_{0}(\eta)}
. Moreover, Theorem
3
guarantees that on
\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}
, this quantity converges towards
\mathbb{E}\left[g(\widehat{\vartheta})\right]
, uniformly in the values
\left(\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B},\widetilde{C}\right)_{k_{0}(\eta)}
. Therefore, for
n
large enough, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n))\mathbbm{1}_{\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}}\right]=O(\delta)+\mathrm{e}^{-3/4}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{g}(\vartheta)\right]\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Simp}_{\to k_{0}(\eta)}\mbox{ and }\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}\right).

As before, using this time Lemma
11
and the choice of the constant
K
, the event
\mathcal{E}_{\eta,K}
can be absorbed by the
O(\delta)
term. Coming back to (
85
) and using that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}\right)
is bounded away from
0
, we have shown that for any
\delta>0
, there is
\eta>0
such that for
n
large enough, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n))|\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}\right]=O(\delta)+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-3/4}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Simp}_{\to k_{0}(\eta)}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}\right)}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{g}(\vartheta)\right],

(86)

where the constant implied by the
O
notation only depends on
g
. In particular, if
g
is constant and equal to
1
, then so is
\widehat{g}
and the last formula becomes

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Simp}_{\to k_{0}(\eta)}\right)=\left(1+O(\delta)\right)\mathrm{e}^{3/4}\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}\right).

Plugging this back into (
86
), we get

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n))|\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{g}(\vartheta)\right]+O(\delta).

Since we can take
\delta
arbitrarily small, this concludes the proof of convergence of
\mathbb{D}_{5}(n)
for the random simple graph
\mathrm{G}(n,m_{n})
.

To extend the result to
\left(D_{i}(n)\right)_{i\geqslant 2}
instead of just
D_{5}(n)
, the proof is exactly the same. We just need to replace
g(\mathbb{D}_{5}(n))
by

g\left(\frac{\mathbb{D}_{2}(n)}{n^{3/5}},\frac{\mathbb{D}_{3}(n)}{n^{2/5}},\frac{\mathbb{D}_{4}(n)}{n^{1/5}},\mathbb{D}_{5}(n),\sum_{i\geqslant 6}\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)\right),

where
g
is a bounded, continuous function from
\mathbb{R}^{5}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

We now move on to the claim that conditionally on its vertex degrees, the Karp–Sipser core of
\mathrm{G}(n,m_{n})
is a configuration model conditioned to be simple. This will be true without assuming anything about
m_{n}
. For this, let
\mathfrak{g}
be a simple graph with no leaf and no isolated vertex. We need to show that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathrm{G}(n,m_{n}))=\mathfrak{g}\right)
only depends on the vertex degrees of
\mathfrak{g}
. But we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathrm{G}(n,m_{n}))=\mathfrak{g}\right)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple and $\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n}))=\mathfrak{g}$ }\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n})$ is simple}\right)}.

The denominator does not depend on
\mathfrak{g}
, so we only need to consider the numerator. Since
\mathfrak{g}
is simple, it can be rewritten as

\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{$\mathrm{Simp}_{\to k_{0}(\eta)}$ and $\mathrm{Simp}_{k_{0}(\eta)\to\theta^{n}}$ and $\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n}))=\mathfrak{g}$}\right)\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{Simp}_{\to k_{0}(\eta)}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{Simp}_{k_{0}(\eta)\to\theta^{n}}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n}))=\mathfrak{g}|\mathcal{F}_{\theta^{n}}\right)\right].

But by Lemmas
4
and
2
, the core
\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathbb{G}(n,m_{n}))
is a configuration model conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{\theta^{n}}
and on its vertex degrees, so this only depends on the vertex degrees of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Finally, to pass from
\mathrm{G}(n,m_{n})
to the “true” Erdős–Rényi model
\mathrm{G}[n,\frac{\mathrm{e}}{n}]
, let
M_{0}^{n}
be the total number of edges of
\mathrm{G}[n,\frac{\mathrm{e}}{n}]
. By the Skorokhod embedding theorem, we may assume that
\frac{M_{0}^{n}-\mathrm{e}n/2}{\sqrt{n}}
converges a.s. to a Gaussian variable, so in particular
M_{0}^{n}=\frac{\mathrm{e}}{2}n+O\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
. Therefore, if
g:\mathbb{R}^{5}\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded and continuous, we have the almost sure convergence

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{G}[n,\frac{\mathrm{e}}{n}]}\left[g\left(\frac{\mathbb{D}_{2}(n)}{n^{3/5}},\dots,\sum_{i\geqslant 6}\mathbb{D}_{i}(n)\right)\Big|M_{0}^{n}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{a.s.}\mathbb{E}[\widehat{g}(\vartheta)],

and (
1
) for
\mathrm{G}[n,\frac{\mathrm{e}}{n}]
follows by taking the expectation. Finally, for any simple graph
\mathfrak{g}
, we can write the decomposition

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathrm{G}[n,p])=\mathfrak{g}\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{\binom{n}{2}}\mathbb{P}\left(M_{0}^{n}=m\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathrm{G}(n,m))=\mathfrak{g}\right),

which only depends on
\mathfrak{g}
through its vertex degrees. It follows that conditionally on its vertex degrees, the graph
\mathrm{KSCore}(\mathrm{G}[n,p])
is a configuration model conditioned to be simple.
∎
