Cost-effectiveness and budget impact of a fecal immunochemical test–based colorectal cancer screening program in a cancer center
Authors: Carlos Muñoz-Montecinos, Camila Quirland, Felipe Maza, Carlos Barrientos, Cristian Ayala, Catalina González-Browne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) screening reduces cancer-specific mortality and is widely recommended by international agencies. The fecal immunochemical test (FIT) is the preferred strategy for population-based screening. Although FIT-based screening is considered cost-effective internationally, no local evidence is available, and implementing such a program may require substantial resources. This study evaluated the cost-effectiveness and budget impact (BI) of introducing a FIT-based CRC screening program for the insured population of the Fundación Arturo López Pérez (FALP) Cancer Center.MethodsA Markov model was developed to assess the cost-effectiveness of the FIT screening program compared with no screening. Healthy beneficiaries aged 50–69 years were invited to undergo FIT every two years. Expected costs, life-years gained (LYG), and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) were estimated, along with the BI. Deterministic sensitivity analyses were performed, and co-payment scenarios involving out-of-pocket expenses were also explored.ResultsThe FIT-based CRC screening program was cost-effective across all scenarios analyzed, and even in a co-payment scenario it was dominant over no screening. FIT adherence and FIT positivity rate had the greatest influence on cost-effectiveness outcomes. Although the ICER indicated a favorable cost-effectiveness profile, BI estimates for the base-case scenario highlighted the substantial financial burden of implementing the program. Co-payment scenarios markedly reduced BI, making the program more affordable.ConclusionsFIT-based CRC screening is a cost-effective strategy for the FALP-insured population. However, its significant budgetary impact may hinder implementation. Shared funding mechanisms could mitigate these financial barriers and enhance program feasibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14524-z.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common and lethal cancers in Chile and worldwide [1, 2]. It is usually diagnosed at advanced stages, where the 5-year survival rate is only 14%, however, when detected at early stages, survival increases to 90% [3]. Most CRC are adenocarcinomas [4] that typically develop over 10–20 years from adenomatous polyps [5], offering a valuable window of opportunity for detection at precancerous stages. These features make CRC particularly suitable for population-based screening, as demonstrated by numerous programs worldwide [6].

Screening programs based on colonoscopy or the fecal immunochemical test (FIT) have shown a reduction in both CRC incidence and cancer-specific mortality [7]. Moreover, these strategies have shown to be cost-effective when compared to no screening or alternative screening strategies [8]. Although colonoscopy has a higher sensitivity than FIT for detecting precancerous lesions [9], its high cost, limitations in colonoscopy capacity, and psychosocial barriers have led international agencies, such as the World Health Organization and the European Union, to recommend FIT as the preferred test for population-based CRC screening [10, 11].

Several factors beyond cost-effectiveness must be considered when deciding whether to implement a screening program. Budget impact (BI) and resource modelling are critical components when assessing the feasibility of health technologies [12]. Budget impact determines the level of financial investment required to implement the program, whereas resource modelling evaluates whether the provider has sufficient capacity to absorb the additional demand generated by screening. Regardless of the test used for the CRC screening program, organized population-based screening programs are recommended over opportunistic approaches [13], which implies greater resource requirements for implementation [14]. International guidelines for CRC screening emphasize the need for adequate personnel and infrastructure to support recruitment, testing, diagnosis, follow-up, treatment, and overall program management [15, 16].

Despite international recommendations supporting CRC screening using FIT, no organized population-based CRC screening programs currently exist in Chile. Consequently, most of the Chilean population have never undergone CRC screening [17]. The Arturo López Pérez Foundation (FALP, for its name in Spanish) is a specialized oncology center that offers a disease-specific supplementary insurance plan that covers all remaining costs of cancer care after reimbursements from other insurers. Upon a diagnosis of cancer, insured beneficiaries are entitled to receive comprehensive cancer care at FALP with no out-of-pocket payments. FALP is interested in implementing an organized FIT-based CRC screening program for its insured population. Although this strategy has been shown to be cost-effective internationally, evidence regarding its cost-effectiveness in the local context is lacking. Moreover, evaluating economic feasibility requires not only cost-effectiveness analysis but also an assessment of the budgetary impact associated with program implementation. The aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility of implementing a CRC screening program within the FALP-insured population, considering both its potential health benefits and its financial implications.

Methods

Cost-effectiveness assessment

We developed a Markov model to assess the cost-effectiveness of a FIT-based CRC screening program compared with the current scenario of no screening. The analysis was conducted from the perspective of a disease-specific supplementary insurance provider, the Convenio Oncológico (hereafter CO), offered by FALP. This is a closed insurance scheme that becomes active only after a confirmed cancer diagnosis and covers all subsequent cancer-related medical expenses incurred within FALP. As such, it operates as a wraparound coverage on top of the mandatory first-layer health insurance (FONASA or ISAPRE) and second-layer voluntary complementary private insurance, assuming responsibility exclusively for the residual oncology-related costs of affiliated patients.

Health benefits were measured in life-years gained (LYG), and cost-effectiveness results were expressed as incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). A reference willingness-to-pay threshold of one to three times the gross domestic product (GDP) per capita (USD 16,000 to USD 48,000) per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained was applied [18], due to the absence of formally established thresholds for LYG. Health benefits and costs were measured over a time horizon of 20 years, and both were discounted at an annual rate of 3% in accordance with the Methodological guidelines for the economic evaluation of health intervention in Chile [18]. The model was developed in Microsoft Excel (Version 2506).

The model simulated a hypothetical cohort of asymptomatic insured beneficiaries with average CRC risk and aged 50–69, who were invited to undergo FIT screening every two years. People who did not adhere to the test are invited to be tested the following year. Individuals with a positive test were referred for diagnostic colonoscopy. Those with a negative colonoscopy result were invited to resume FIT screening after 10 years, whereas individuals with a positive colonoscopy underwent biopsy and undergo treatment regarding the malignancy of neoplasia. These screening and follow-up strategies represented in the model are consistent with local practice at FALP. The cohort progresses through the model in annual cycles over a 20-year time horizon (Fig. 1).

Model data input

The distribution of CRC stages at diagnosis for the no screening scenario was derived from local institutional data, reflecting a predominance of advanced-stage diagnoses. To simulate the impact of FIT screening, we applied the relative percentage change in stage distribution based on empirical evidence from the Taiwanese screening program, where more than 1,000,000 persons have been tested [19]. CRC survival estimates were obtained from local data and then extrapolated to a 20-years horizon using parametric functions (Log normal for all stages). Survival for stages I and II were modeled as a single combined stage due to the similarity of survival probabilities. External validation of was performed by comparing the model-predicted 10-year stage-specific overall survival estimates with published CRC survival [20] data and web-based prognostic tools [21]. Age-specific all-cause mortality rates were obtained from the National Institute of Statistics [22]. FIT positivity rates and CRC detection rates among individuals undergoing colonoscopy were based on local expert recommendations. Other detection parameters were obtained from published international studies (Table 1).

Cost data

Costs considered for the analysis were direct costs incurred by the insurer, since the analysis was carried out from the perspective of CO. Screening-related costs, which applied exclusively for the screening arm, included the FIT test, medical consultations, colonoscopy, biopsy, polypectomy and costs arising from complications associated with these procedures. Treatment costs for each CRC stage include costs of surgery, radiotherapy or chemotherapy, calculated as the mean total annual treatment costs incurred by our patients during the first five years following diagnosis. These treatment costs are assumed to be identical across study arms and are stratified by disease stage. No treatment costs were considered beyond 5 years after CRC diagnosis. In the base-case scenario, all screening related and CRC treatment costs reflected expenditures incurred after the reimbursement by the mandatory primary health insurance (FONASA or ISAPRE). Budget impact estimation

The hypothetical CRC screening program was designed to target approximately 600,000 beneficiaries aged 50–69 years over a 20-year time horizon. Due to limitations in diagnostic and infrastructure capacity, program implementation was assumed to follow a phased, stepwise approach. Specifically, 10,000 randomly selected beneficiaries would be invited to participate in the first year; 20,000 annually in the second and third years; 30,000 in the fourth and fifth years; 40,000 annually for the following eight years; and finally, 31,500 per year from year 14 through year 20. This gradual scale-up is also intended to distribute the budgetary impact more evenly over time.

Sensitivity and scenario analysis

One-way deterministic sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of results. Key parameters, including FIT adherence, FIT positivity rate and adherence to diagnostic colonoscopy were varied to estimate their effect on the cost-effectiveness and budget impact (Table 1).

Additionally, two alternative financing mechanisms were assessed. In the first scenario, patients covered the FIT tests and FALP paid for subsequent confirmatory tests following a positive FIT result. In the second scenario, our institution paid for the FIT test, while all subsequent diagnostic procedures following a positive test were covered by patients. In both scenarios, FALP financed the complete treatment of CRC.

Results

Model validation

The 10-year survival estimates generated by our model for stages I-II, III, and IV were 80%, 58% and 8%, respectively. A comparison of the annual CRC incidence between the FIT-based screening and no-screening scenarios revealed that, during the initial years, incidence was higher in the screening group; however, after 13 years, this trend reversed, with incidence becoming lower than in the no-screening scenario (Additional file 1).

Cost-effectiveness

In our simulation, 1,000 asymptomatic individuals were invited to undergo biennial FIT screening. The FIT-based CRC screening showed 7.0 LYG per 1000 participants. The incremental cost for the FIT-based program was US$55,828. Overall, FIT screening was cost-effective, with an ICER of US$7,939 per life-year gained, which is well below the commonly accepted cost-effectiveness thresholds (Fig. 2).

Budget impact analysis

The estimated total budget impact (BI) of implementing a FIT-based CRC screening program is presented in Table 4. The total cumulative additional expenditure for the screening program in 5 years and 20 years in the base case is approximately US$4 and US$33 million, respectively. After year 4, when the number of newly invited people reached 30,000, the incremental cost exceeded US$1 million yearly, reaching a peak of US$2,130,227 in year 13. Detailed annual expenditure shows that from year 8 onward, savings in CRC treatment costs begin to emerge due to a stage shift at diagnosis; however, these savings were insufficient to offset the growing costs associated with screening an increasing number of individuals each year (Additional file 2). Consequently, no net cost savings were observed within the time horizon evaluated. Similarly to cost-effectiveness results, increasing adherence to FIT to 80% resulted in a 26% reduction of BI (Additional file 3).

Co-financing scenarios

We explored two financial scenarios to assess their impact on cost-effectiveness and BI. In Scenario 1, patients paid for the FIT test, while all confirmatory tests required in case of a positive FIT were covered by FALP. In Scenario 2, FALP covered the cost of the FIT test, while patients paid out of pocket for subsequent diagnostic procedures after a positive result. For these scenarios, all the parameters remain constant. Under the first scenario, BI was reduced nearly by half, showing the substantial contribution of the FIT test in the total cost of the program. In the second scenario, the FIT-based program became cost-saving, with a negative ICER of -$US1,802, reflecting lower overall costs due to reduced diagnostic expenditures, mainly in colonoscopies, which are the most resource-consuming procedures. BI in this scenario was reduced by 94% (Table 4).

Discussion

FIT is the most recommended strategy for population-based CRC screening, mainly due to its proven ability to decrease CRC-specific mortality by using fewer resources than other strategies such as colonoscopy. Nevertheless, although cost-effective, FIT-based CRC screening implementation could be highly expensive for either public or private organizations. This study aimed to estimate the cost-effectiveness and budgetary impact of implementing a FIT-based CRC screening in the Chilean context.

Our results showed that a FIT-based CRC screening program is cost-effective in both base-case and sensitivity analyses. Despite abundant evidence supporting the cost-effectiveness of FIT programs worldwide, only a few Latin American studies addressed this question [25–27], and, to our knowledge, none have been conducted in Chile. Importantly, the extrapolated stage-specific survival generated by our model and incidence trend after screening implementation were consistent with data reported in real-world FIT-based screening settings [20, 21, 28], supporting the external validity of our projections. This highlights the relevance of generating local evidence to support the implementation of population-based CRC screening programs in our local setting.

Despite being cost-effective, the total resources required to initiate and sustain a population-based CRC screening program represent a major budgetary challenge for FALP. BI results across scenarios revealed that patient’s copayment substantially reduces the payer’s financial burden. While this is valuable information for stakeholders, budget impact alone should not guide decision-making. Cost-sharing strategies may negatively affect patient adherence to follow-up diagnostic procedures, as observed in other international experiences [29, 30]. Additionally, although the co-payment of diagnostic tests after a positive FIT reduced BI much more than the co-payment of FIT and therefore appears financially appealing for the payer, its real-world performance could be undermined by lower colonoscopy uptake after a positive FIT, given the lower adherence [9, 31] and willingness to pay [32] reported for colonoscopy compared with FIT. Since adherence to both the initial FIT and the confirmatory colonoscopy is essential for achieving the expected effectiveness of the screening program, evidence-based interventions that increase participation should be considered. Mailed FIT outreach and SMS reminders significantly improve participation rates [33, 34], while patient navigation after a positive FIT substantially enhances adherence to follow-up colonoscopy [35, 36]. Any strategy aimed at reducing budget impact should carefully balance financial sustainability with the potential risk of reducing participation rates.

In addition to financial constraints, several implementation barriers must be addressed to ensure the success of CRC screening in Chile. A recent study identified lack of knowledge about screening programs and lack of access as key obstacles to screening uptake in the Chilean population [17]. Addressing these barriers through education campaigns, navigation programs will be crucial for maximizing the impact and equity of a future CRC screening program for CO beneficiaries.

Our study has several limitations. First, most parameters used to inform the model were derived from international studies or local expert opinions. Chile currently lacks a national cancer registry and an organized population-based CRC screening program. As a result, several assumptions had to be based on international experiences. A further limitation is the assumption that FALP will be able to fully absorb the additional demand generated by the screening program. Both the cost-effectiveness and budget-impact analyses presume that no delays or capacity constraints will occur. In practice, however, limited resources or service bottlenecks could affect timely access to diagnostic procedures and ultimately influence the program’s performance.

Conclusion

Population-based CRC screening with FIT appears to be cost-effective in our local context. However, cost-effectiveness alone does not guarantee financial feasibility. Our budget impact analysis shows that implementing the program would require substantial financial resources from payers, particularly under the base-case scenario. Co-financing alternatives considerably reduced the budget impact, attenuating the total costs of the program and potentially improving the feasibility of its implementation.
