Genome-wide tandem repeat expansions modify schizophrenia risk in the presence of a 22q11.2 deletion
Authors: Muyang Cheng, Yue Yin, Worrawat Engchuan, Tracy Heung, International 22q11.2 Brain Behavior Consortium (IBBC), Bernice E Morrow, Anne S Bassett, Ryan K C Yuen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchizophrenia develops in one in every four individuals with a pathogenic 22q11.2 deletion, yet the genetic modifiers influencing the manifestation of schizophrenia in this high-risk group remain incompletely understood. Here, we identify rare tandem repeat expansions (TREs) as significant contributors to schizophrenia risk in this population. Genome sequencing of 438 unrelated individuals with 22q11.2 deletions revealed a marked enrichment of rare genic TREs among those with schizophrenia, with effect sizes comparable to common polygenic risk. These TREs are disproportionately located in intronic and splice-adjacent regions relative to other genomic regions, with evidence suggesting that they disrupt gene regulation through mechanisms including altered methylation and splicing. Cell-type-specific analyses indicate that TREs are primarily associated with differentially expressed genes in excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the prefrontal cortex. Affected genes, including DLGAP2 and DMPK, are involved in neurodevelopment and synaptic organization. These findings extend the role of TREs as genetic modifiers, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying schizophrenia in this ultra-high-risk population and into the broader biology of idiopathic schizophrenia.Subject terms: Genetics, Schizophrenia

Introduction

Schizophrenia stands as one of the most disabling human brain disorders in adults, usually requiring lifelong treatment [1]. Among known genetic forms of schizophrenia (mostly copy number variation (CNV)-related), the 22q11.2 deletion confers the highest risk of developing schizophrenia and thus represents a unique genetic model for studying this serious psychotic illness [2–6]. One in every four individuals born with a 22q11.2 deletion develops schizophrenia, 25 times greater than the general population risk [7]. This deletion is present in about 1% of patients with schizophrenia and is one of the most recurrent CNVs, occurring in nearly 1 in 2000 live births, usually as a de novo event, causing 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (22q11.2DS) [7–11]. Although placing individuals at markedly high risk, the majority of individuals with a 22q11.2 deletion do not develop schizophrenia. The clinical features of schizophrenia caused by the 22q11.2 microdeletion appear indistinguishable from other forms of schizophrenia [12, 13], and available data so far indicate that the additional genetic variants contributing to schizophrenia risk are the same as those that contribute to schizophrenia risk in the general population [13, 14], suggesting that individuals with 22q11.2 deletions represent a valuable population for understanding the causes of schizophrenia, and the reduced penetrance and variable expressivity that is characteristic of all schizophrenia-associated genetic variants discovered to date [2, 3, 15–20].

Schizophrenia is well-known for its polygenic nature, where multiple genetic variants contribute to its genetic risk. Previously, we and others have shown that the aggregate effect of common risk alleles and additional rare copy number variants (CNVs) in the genome can affect the penetrance of schizophrenia in individuals with a 22q11.2 deletion [13, 21]. Rare nonsynonymous variants affecting genes involved in cortical development have also been shown to increase risk of schizophrenia in this context [13, 22]. However, these effects are not large enough to allow differentiation of individuals with and without schizophrenia, suggesting that other major modifying factors beyond the high risk imparted by the 22q11.2 deletion remain to be identified. This is a goal of both scientific and clinical interest [3, 4, 8].

In recent studies, we have determined that tandem repeats, which constitute ~8% of the human genome, contribute to schizophrenia risk when they are expanded [23, 24]. These repeats tend to be located within or near genes known to be implicated in schizophrenia, e.g., from genome-wide association studies [23]. Schizophrenia studies that excluded individuals with 22q11.2 deletions showed a differential presence of rare tandem repeat expansions (TREs) in individuals with and without other schizophrenia-associated CNVs of lower penetrance, e.g., 16p11.2 duplication [23]. We therefore proposed to determine whether TREs could be a significant genetic modifier for schizophrenia in the presence of a high-risk 22q11.2 deletion, and to examine potential molecular mechanisms of disease.

Here, we used genome sequencing data of 88 trios (264 individuals) with confirmed de novo 22q11.2 deletion from a deeply-phenotyped Canadian cohort, and 350 other unrelated individuals with 22q11.2 deletion from the same local adult cohort and from an international consortium, to investigate the individual and combined contribution of common variants, rare variants, and TREs to schizophrenia risk. We performed genetic burden analyses using a logistic regression model and highlight the significant role of global rare TREs, a previously unexplored risk factor for schizophrenia in individuals with 22q11.2 deletion. We outline biological functions and mechanisms in which rare TREs may be involved in manifesting schizophrenia in individuals at a priori elevated risk from a 22q11.2 deletion.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All participants of the Canadian cohorts provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Research Ethics Boards at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH) (151/2002, 150-2002, 154-2002, 114-2001) and the University Health Network (UHN) (98-E156), where participants were ascertained through genetic screening or referrals originating from generalists and specialists. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

The International 22q11.2 Brain Behaviour Consortium ascertainment and assessment methods have been previously detailed [4, 13, 21]. Briefly, individuals with a presumed 22q11.2 deletion, who were recruited from 22 international sites in studies approved by local institutional research ethics boards, and for whom existing DNA samples were available, were ascertained for the study.

Sequencing data provided by the Center for Inherited Disease Research were performed on de-identified genomic samples from blood as part of Internal Review Board approved program, #1999-201. The samples involved in the analysis of 22q-SCZ are a subset of the total samples and were recruited and ascertained using the same method as those of the Canadian cohorts described above.

Sample collection and genome sequencing

We used short-read whole genome sequencing data originating from independent de novo 22q11.2 deletion trio Canadian families of European descent. Each trio family contains one proband with a confirmed de novo typical 22q11.2 deletion, i.e., spanning the LCR22A-LCR22B region, and most commonly extending from LCR22A to LCR22D [7]. DNA from the trio cohort extracted from whole blood samples was prepared with a PCR-free library kit and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeqX platforms (2 x 150 bp paired-end reads). Sequence reads were aligned to the CRCh37/hg19 genome.

The singleton study cohorts comprised genome sequencing and available schizophrenia phenotype data of individuals with typical 22q11.2 deletions originating from, respectively, previously published results [13, 21] by the International 22q11.2 Brain Behaviour Consortium [4] (IBBC), and a 22q11.2 deletion congenital heart disease (CHD) study of Professor Bernice E. Morrow with sequencing obtained through the Centre for Inherited Disease Research (CIDR). Sequencing data from the IBBC study using the Illumina HiSeqX platform were subsequently aligned to the CRCh37/hg19 genome; samples from the CHD-CIDR study were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform and aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 genome, with detected variants subsequently lifted over to hg19.

Detection of rare tandem repeat expansions

We used ExpansionHunter Denovo [25] (EHdn) to detect tandem repeats that were larger than 150 bp from short-read sequencing data. EHdn estimates tandem repeat sizes by counting the number of anchored in-repeat reads (IRRs), i.e., read pairs in which one read contains repetitive sequence and the other can be uniquely mapped to the reference genome, normalized by the average read depth of each sample.

To detect samples with repeat sizes that are significantly larger than that of the majority of samples, i.e., tandem repeat expansions (TRE), we applied Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) [26]. The two parameters for DBSCAN were: 1) the maximum allowed distance between the samples of the same cluster (Epsilon) and 2) the minimum number of samples in each cluster (MinPts). These are chosen based on the most optimal yield for repeat size outliers in genic regions in 22q-schizophrenia over the 22q-non-psychotic controls. In the analysis of the trio cohort, we set Epsilon to 2 and MinPts to 9; in the analysis of the singleton cohort, we set the parameters of DBSCAN independently between IBBC and CHD-CIDR samples due to differences in sequencing platform. For the IBBC samples, we set Epsilon to 2.5 and MinPts to 9; for the CHD-CIDR samples, we set Epsilon to 2 and MinPts to 7. The population frequency of TREs was estimated using the Medical Genome Reference Bank and 1000 Genomes Project samples as background, and the cut-off for rare TREs was set at 0.1% for all three cohorts.

Data filtering

In the trio cohort (original n = 99), we filtered out probands with unconfirmed diagnoses (i.e., non-psychotic individuals at age <25 y.o.; n = 9), and those with non-European ancestry (n = 2). In total, 88 samples in the trio cohort were available for analysis, comprising 41 schizophrenia cases (24 male, 17 female; median age as of August 2024: 42 y.o., range: 23–68 y.o.; mean age at onset: 21.0 y.o., SD: 7.4 y.o.) and 47 non-psychotic controls (19 male, 28 female; median age: 38 y.o., range: 27–61 y.o.).

In the singleton 22q11.2 deletion cohorts, we performed data filtering for IBBC (original n = 1355) and CHD-CIDR (original n = 783) samples independently. To account for heterogeneity across the IBBC and CHD-CIDR cohorts, we applied additional quality control steps to obtain a homogeneous set of samples with consistent tandem repeat expansion detection performance. We excluded samples with considerably different (> 1.5 interquartile range from upper or lower quartile) sequencing depth or number of tandem repeats detected from the rest of the samples (IBBC: n = 69; CHD-CIDR: n = 41). We then performed DBSCAN clustering using principal components derived from EHdn tandem repeat calls and removed non-clustered outlier samples (IBBC: n = 84; CHD-CIDR: n = 22). For samples found to be related to each other, we prioritize schizophrenia samples over non-psychotic control samples, and non-psychotic control samples that are older at psychosis ascertainment over younger ones. We excluded duplicate samples (IBBC: n = 1), samples of first-degree relatedness (IBBC: n = 4; CHD-CIDR: n = 1) or second-degree relatedness (IBBC: n = 1), samples of non-European ancestry (IBBC: n = 239; CHD-CIDR: n = 134), and samples with abnormally high relatedness estimated with multiple samples from unrelated families (IBBC: n = 1). Finally, we excluded samples (IBBC: n = 705; CHD-CIDR: n = 486) where the diagnosis was either psychosis that did not fall into the schizophrenia category or where diagnosis remained unconfirmed (i.e., non-psychotic at age <25 years when ascertained). Diagnoses (updated as of February 2025) were available only for the local singleton individuals in the CHD-CIDR; otherwise diagnoses were as deposited in dbGaP (IBBC) [13].

In total, after filtering, there were 350 samples from unrelated individuals with 22q11.2 deletion available for analysis in the singleton cohort, comprising 136 schizophrenia cases (IBBC: n = 99, 46 male, 53 female, median age at assessment: 30 y.o., range: 11-61 y.o., mean age at onset: 22.4 y.o., SD: 7.8 y.o.; CHD-CIDR: n = 37, 19 male, 18 female, median age as of August 2024: 52 y.o., range: 24-69 y.o., mean age at onset: 24.8 y.o., SD: 9.8 y.o.) and 214 non-psychotic controls (IBBC: n = 152, 61 male, 91 female, median age at assessment: 34 y.o., range: 25-67 y.o.; CHD-CIDR: n = 62, 23 male, 39 female, median age at assessment: 37 y.o., range: 25-68 y.o.).

A breakdown of sample composition in the trio and singleton cohort is provided in Supp. Table S1.

Genetic variant filtering

We used ANNOVAR [27] to assign rare TREs to specific functional elements of genes: any rare TRE that maps to the exonic, intronic, UTR5, UTR3, and within 3000 kb upstream or downstream of a protein-coding gene was considered genic; rare TREs that map to non-genic regions were hence considered intergenic. Rare TREs that were mapped to non-coding genes were excluded from the burden analysis. Additionally, we overlapped rare TRE loci to ENCODE [28] Registry of candidate cis-regulatory element (cCREs).

SNVs and indels were annotated using ANNOVAR and further classified into loss-of-function variants and damaging missense variants. Loss-of-function variants include variants that are: 1) frameshift indels, 2) nonsense variants, and 3) located within 2 bp of splice junctions, as reported by ANNOVAR. We used multiple in-silico prediction software programs to estimate the damaging effect of missense SNVs. For a missense SNV to be classified as damaging, it had to exceed score thresholds for at least 4 out of the 7 assessed [29–34] (CADD ( ≥ 15), SIFT ( ≤ 0.05), PolyPhen2 HVAR ( ≥ 0.90), ma (≥1.90) and mt (≥0.5) scores, PhyloPMam (≥2.30) and PhyloPVert (≥4.0)). All variants were considered rare at <0.1% using population frequency estimated in 1000 Genomes and gnomAD.

For additional CNVs (i.e., other than the 22q11.2 deletion), we examined those that: 1) were >10 kb in duplication/deletion size, 2) overlapped the coding sequence of at least one protein-coding gene, 3) autosomal, and 4) heterozygous.

To avoid sex bias, all rare variants were restricted to those located on autosomes.

Polygenic risk score (PRS) calculation

To assess polygenic risk for schizophrenia (PRS-SCZ), we used the training dataset published from the 2014 PGC schizophrenia meta-analysis (Schizophrenia Working Group of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium) [35]. Their reported GWAS list, including 9.4 million SNPs, was used to extract variant calls from the initial VCF files of 22q11.2 deletion WGS samples. Further quality filters on the extracted SNPs were conducted using the PLINK (version 1.9) toolkit [36], including allele frequency less than 0.01 (--maf 0.01), missingness per individual more than 0.01 (--mind 0.01), missingness per marker more than 0.01 (--geno 0.01), and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium individual less than 1 ×10-5 (--hwe 0.00001), and excluding SNPs on the X chromosome, the 22q11.2 deletion region, and the human major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region [13]. The filtered SNPs were input to the PRS calculation tool PRSice [37] (version 1.25) to generate PRS at the individual level. PRS results of the optimal model (program selected based on Nagelkerke’s R2 and p-value) were selected and standardized within the study cohort. No covariates were used in the PRS optimization model.

Burden analysis

We performed logistic regression analysis by regressing the status of schizophrenia diagnosis against the number of each type of rare variant in the probands and the standardized PRS of each proband using a multiple regression model. We included the sex of the proband, top three SNP-derived principal components (PC; to account for ancestry), top three tandem-repeat variation-derived PCs (to account for differences in tandem repeat detection performance across platforms and studies), and whether the probands had a full LCR22A-LCR22D or rarer smaller proximal nested (LCR22A-LCR22B, or LCR22A-LCR22C) 22q11.2 deletion as covariates of the regression model [7]. For burden analysis in the singleton cohort, we additionally included the source cohort (IBBC versus CHD-CIDR) as a covariate to account for platform and ascertainment differences. Empirical p-values are derived from a permutation-based approach using the one-sided Wald test with the hypothesis of a higher burden in individuals with schizophrenia compared to non-psychotic controls. To test the burden of rare TREs affecting different functional elements of protein-coding genes, we assign the impact of each rare TRE to a functional element: upstream (3 kb upstream of the transcriptional start site, TSS), 5’ untranslated regions (UTR), exon, core splice site, intron, 3’ UTR and downstream (3 kb downstream of transcription termination sites). We prioritize the effect of rare TREs that were mapped to more than one functional element based on their impact on the corresponding genes predicted by ANNOVAR [27]. We further assessed the contribution to the fit of logistic regression for each variant type in the genetic modifier model using coefficients of determination (R2) on the liability scale [38] by comparing the difference in coefficients of determination (ΔR2) between the full multivariable regression model and multivariable models that exclude each variant-type burden (Table 1).

Association between modifiers variant types

We performed linear regression analysis to test for the association between each pair of the six genetic variant types involved in the burden analysis. In each linear regression, the number of one genetic variant type is regressed against that of another genetic variant type along the sex of the proband, top three SNP-derived PCs, top three tandem-repeat variation-derived PCs, and whether the probands had a full LCR22A-LCR22D or rarer smaller proximal nested (LCR22A-LCR22B, or LCR22A-LCR22C) 22q11.2 deletion as covariates of the regression model.

Gene interaction network and functional enrichment

We used GeneMANIA [39] (GM) Cytoscape [40] extension to query genes affected by rare TREs in individuals with schizophrenia (n = 73 genes total between trio and singleton cohorts combined: n = 35 genes in the trio cohort, n = 41 genes in the singleton cohort, with 3 recurrent genes shared between the trio and singleton cohorts). We searched for physical interactions and pathways and allowed a maximum of 20 resultant genes; the network weighting model for GM was set to “Gene-Ontology (GO) based weighing, Biological Process base”; all other parameters of GM were set to default. The built-in GeneMANIA functional enrichment considers only annotations in Gene Ontology [41, 42] (GO) with 10 to 300 genes in the organism of interest, GO terms inferred from reviewed computational analysis or electronic annotations were excluded. Q-values of enrichment significance are estimated by FDR-corrected hypergeometric tests using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. GO terms with Q < 0.1 are reported in Supp. Table S2. We then queried genes affected by rare TREs in schizophrenia along with 46 protein-coding genes in the typical LCR-A to LCR-D 22q11.2 deletion region to investigate possible interaction between rare TREs and the 22q11.2 deletion.

Enrichment analysis in gene sets previously implicated in 22q11.2 deletion, schizophrenia, and neurodevelopmental disorders

We hypothesized that genes affected by rare TREs in schizophrenia may be enriched with genes that are implicated by existing studies in the risk of schizophrenia and/or other similar neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders. We curated genes known to be associated with schizophrenia [19, 43], autism spectrum disorder [44, 45], developmental disorders [46, 47], and intellectual disability [48]. Genes reported to be differentially expressed in a 22q11.2 deletion iPSC model [49] were also considered. We further included gene sets important to neuronal and synaptic function from the SynGO annotation [50] and genes that are intolerant to LoF variants [51]. We included two gene sets from Mouse Genome Informatics [52] (MGI): endocrine/exocrine and digestive/hepatic as control gene sets. We used Fisher’s exact test to compare the enrichment of genes affected by rare TREs in a total of 20 gene sets against all other genes in the genome. Adjusted p-values were estimated using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Overlap with tandem repeat quantitative trait loci

We retrieved tandem repeat quantitative trait loci (TR-xQTL) regions linked to molecular features in the brain obtained with multi-omic QTL analysis in different brain regions from ALS and Alzheimer’s Disease brain tissue samples published by Cui et al., 2025 [53]. We then overlapped rare TRE regions detected in this study with the TR-xQTL regions from each brain tissue to define TR-xQTL-associated rare genic TREs. To compare the burden of TR-xQTL-associated TREs, we performed a logistic regression analysis on the entire study cohort, trio and singleton combined (n = 438) by regressing the number of rare TREs overlapping a TR-xQTL in probands with 22q-schizophrenia as compared to the 22q-non-psychotic probands with the sex of the proband, principal components derived from EHdn, the 22q11.2 deletion extent, and the cohort (trio versus singleton) as covariates. We used a one-sided Wald test, hypothesizing a higher burden in individuals with schizophrenia, to estimate the significance of the enrichment.

Gene expression of schizophrenia-associated TRE genes

We retrieved differential gene expression analysis results from a single-cell RNA-seq analysis dataset in schizophrenia first published by Ruzicka et al., 2024 [54]. The dataset contains summary statistics for differentially expressed genes (DEG) in 25 different cell types in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex from primary tissue of schizophrenia patients: astrocytes (Ast), oligodendrocytes (Oli), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPC), microglia (Mic), endothelial cells (Endo), pericytes, 13 subpopulations of excitatory neurons, and 7 subpopulations of inhibitory neurons. We first selected differentially expressed genes at unadjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2(fold-change)| > 0.05 in each cell type. We then merged DEG lists in excitatory neurons and inhibitory neurons, respectively. We performed logistic regression analysis on the entire study cohort, trio and singleton combined (n = 438), by regressing the number of rare TREs affecting schizophrenia DEGs identified in the Ruzieka et al., 2024 study [54] in probands with 22q-schizophrenia as compared to the 22q-non-psychotic probands with the sex of the proband, principal components derived from EHdn, the 22q11.2 deletion extent, and the cohort (trio versus singleton cohort) as covariates.

Experimental validation of tandem repeat expansions

Validation of the SHANK2 TREs detected by EHdn was completed by PCR, gel electrophoresis, and Sanger sequencing. We designed primers flanking the repeat of interest with the following sequences: 5’-TGACAACCATCTCCTCCTTAG-3’ and 5’-GATCGAGTCCCCAGTCTTTC-3’. We performed PCR on the samples using PrimeStar GXL DNA polymerase, at a primer annealing temperature of 60 °C, and an elongation time of 4 min. We then ran the PCR products on a 1.2% agarose gel to confirm the size of the repeats and performed Sanger sequencing to confirm the sequence. DNA samples from the parents of the proband were run under the same condition for comparison; DNA sample from 1000 G sample NA12878 was run under the same conditions except for elongation time (1 min) as a negative control (Supp. Fig. S1).

Validation of the MICAL2 TREs detected by EHdn was completed by PCR, gel electrophoresis, and Sanger sequencing. We designed primers flanking the repeat of interest with the following sequences: 5’-ATGCTGCCCGAATCAACAAG-3’ and 5’-CAGCAGTGGAGAAACGTCAG-3’. We performed PCR on the samples using PrimeStar GXL DNA polymerase, at a primer annealing temperature of 67 °C, and an elongation time of 2 min. We then ran the PCR products on a 1.2% agarose gel to confirm the size of the repeats and performed Sanger sequencing to confirm the sequence. DNA samples from the parents of the proband were run under the same condition for comparison; DNA sample from 1000 G sample NA12878 was run under the same conditions except for elongation time (1 min) as a negative control (Supp. Fig. S2).

The results for all PCR validations performed are summarized in Supp. Table S3.

Results

Genome-wide rare tandem repeat expansions contribute to schizophrenia risk in 22q11.2 deletion

We detected tandem repeats (2-20 bp motif size) larger than 150 bp (sequence read length) from short-read genome sequence data using ExpansionHunter Denovo [25], and defined TREs as those with lengths that were significantly larger than others [23, 55], i.e., outliers (Methods). Sequence data were available for 88 trio probands with a de novo 22q11.2 deletion and their 174 unaffected parents (i.e., without 22q11.2 deletion), and for a singleton cohort comprising 350 other unrelated probands with 22q11.2 deletions (Methods). In the de novo 22q11.2 deletion trio cohort, we identified 159 rare TREs in 72 of 88 probands; in the singleton cohort, we identified 143 rare TREs in 111 individuals. We confirmed the repeat size of select expanded tandem repeats by genotyping using ExpansionHunter (Supp. Table S4) and in PCR experiments (Supp. Table S3) for selected rare genic TREs identified in 22q-SCZ, including those involving SHANK2 (Supp. Fig. S1), MORN1 (Supp. Fig. S2), and DLGAP2 and other genes (Supp. Table S3).

To assess the association of rare TREs (< 0.1% frequency in general population) in probands on risk for schizophrenia, we used a logistic regression model (trio and singleton cohorts; Methods). We compared the total rare TRE burden as well as stratified genic features, i.e., whether the TREs are located in protein-coding genes (genic; Methods) or not (intergenic, excluding non-coding genes); TREs that are located in non-coding genes were not considered as genic in this study.

We found a significantly greater burden in probands with 22q11.2 deletion-schizophrenia (22q-SCZ) for rare genic TREs in both the trio (Fig. 1A; logistic regression, OR = 1.75, p = 0.025) and the singleton (Fig. 1A; OR = 2.14, p = 3.9 × 10-4) cohorts, and for total rare TREs in the singleton cohort (Fig. 1A; logistic regression, odds ratio (OR) = 1.60, p = 2.8 × 10-3). There was no significant difference in burden between individuals with and without schizophrenia for rare intergenic TREs (Fig. 1A). Collectively, 58.5% of individuals with 22q-SCZ in the trio cohort carried at least one rare genic TRE compared to 42.6% of those with no psychotic illness. Similarly, 27.2% of individuals with 22q-SCZ in the singleton cohort carried at least one rare genic TRE compared to 15.9% of individuals without schizophrenia. These results indicate that rare genic TREs may account for an estimated ~2-fold increase in schizophrenia risk in the presence of a 22q11.2 deletion. The complete list of all rare TRE loci detected in probands with 22q-SCZ is provided in Supp. Table S5, including intergenic loci and ENCODE annotations.

We further stratify rare genic TREs based on the functional elements they affect, including non-coding regions of genes that may affect gene expression, such as introns and UTRs (Methods). We found that the rare genic TREs were more frequently located in intronic regions of genes in individuals with 22q-SCZ, compared to the individuals with 22q11.2 deletion and no psychotic illness, in both cohorts (Supp. Table S6; logistic regression; trio: OR = 1.72, p = 0.036; singleton: OR = 2.04, p = 2.3 × 10-3); this is similar to non-22q schizophrenia, where we found rare TREs near intronic splice-sites as major contributor to schizophrenia risk [23, 24]. In the larger singleton cohort, in those with 22q-SCZ there was also significantly greater burden of rare genic TREs that overlap two additional components: exonic regions (logistic regression, OR = 3.86, p = 0.024) and gene splice junctions (logistic regression, OR = 11.25, p = 0.013).

The availability of genomic data from parents in the de novo trio cohort allowed us to compare the estimated size of tandem repeats for the rare TREs identified in 22q-SCZ probands with the respective repeats in their unaffected parents. The results showed that the rare genic TREs in the probands were further expanded from already large tandem repeats in a parent, with the average repeat size of these loci in parents ranking at the 98th percentile or higher of length distribution (Fig. 1B; Wilcoxon’s signed rank test, p = 2.0 × 10-3). While ExpansionHunter Denovo does not provide phasing information of the expanded repeats, we assumed that the parent with larger tandem repeat size represented the origin of the rare TRE in the proband. Among 30 expanded TRs in 22q-SCZ probands that were also detected in the parents, there were 17 TREs of paternal and 13 of maternal origin, showing a slight but not statistically significant paternal bias. There was no significant correlation identified between the parental origin of TREs and the parental origin of the de novo 22q11.2 deletion. To test the assumption for the parent-of-origin of TRE in the proband, we obtained ExpansionHunter [56] called repeat sizes at the expanded loci and compared the genotypes between the probands and the parents. Amongst the 30 TREs, this method allowed us to successfully phase the origin of the smaller unexpanded repeat allele to one of the parents for 9 TREs, confirming that the expanded allele must originate from the other parent, in line with our assumption. For one TRE locus, we found that both parents carried similarly sized large repeat alleles at the locus, thus we could not distinguish likely parent-of-origin. For the remaining 20 TREs, both alleles in the proband were beyond the resolution capability of ExpansionHunter.

To evaluate the contribution of different variant types, we compared the burden of schizophrenia polygenic risk score (SCZ-PRS), genic TREs, loss of function (LoF) single nucleotide variants (SNV), missense SNVs, duplication CNVs, and additional (non-22q11.2) deletion CNVs in individuals with and without schizophrenia using multiple regression analysis. As expected, we identified significantly elevated SCZ-PRS (Fig. 1C; logistic regression; trio, OR = 1.59, p = 0.047; singleton, OR per s.d. = 1.52, p = 5.8 × 10-4) in 22q individuals with, compared to those without, schizophrenia. Importantly, the significantly greater rare genic TRE burden for 22q-SCZ under this model (Fig. 1C; logistic regression; trio, OR = 1.72, p = 0.039; singleton, OR = 1.76, p = 8.4 × 10-3) is comparable in effect size to that of SCZ-PRS. For other genetic variant types, no significant difference in burden was found between individuals with and without schizophrenia, suggesting that these variant types impart a relatively weak collective contribution to schizophrenia risk and/or one of lower effect size than that of rare genic TREs or SCZ-PRS. In addition, we identified a significant association between schizophrenia and male sex in the trio cohort (logistic regression: OR = 1.42, p = 0.018) but not in the singleton cohort. We investigated the correlation between modifier variant burdens within individuals using linear regression (Supp. Fig. S3). In the schizophrenia group of the singleton cohort, we identified that PRS and rare genic TREs are significantly associated (β = 0.14, p = 9.7 × 10-3), and each of these variant classes is significantly associated with rare LoF SNVs (PRS, β = 0.84, p = 0.037; rare genic TREs, β = 1.51, p = 0.015), and with rare damaging missense SNVs (PRS, β = 4.20, p = 1.0 × 10-4; rare genic TREs, β = 4.34, p = 0.011). Within the non-psychotic group in both the singleton and trio cohorts, rare damaging missense SNVs are significantly associated with rare LoF SNVs (Singleton, β = 0.90, p = 1.5 × 10-4; Trio, β = 0.72, p = 0.036), and only for the singleton cohort, rare damaging missense SNVs are also significantly associated with PRS (linear regression, β = 3.06, p = 2.0 × 10-5). There was no significant correlation between any modifier variant classes in the schizophrenia group of the trio cohort.

PRS and rare genic TREs, respectively, account for 6.2% and 6.4% of the liability for schizophrenia in the trio cohort, and for 10.2% and 9.1% of the liability for schizophrenia in the singleton cohort (Table 1). The same model, explaining variance for genetic risk factors while accounting for multiple base covariates, provided estimates of 15.4% and 13.0% for the global contribution to schizophrenia risk of all genetic modifier variant types assessed, respectively, in the trio and singleton cohorts (Table 1).

Past research has identified SCZ-PRS to confer significant genetic liability for schizophrenia in the presence of a 22q11.2 deletion [13]. SCZ-PRS may also be correlated with cognitive measures in individuals with a 22q11.2 deletion [57, 58]. We therefore investigated if the rare genic TRE burden is correlated with measures of cognitive ability. For the trio and local singleton cohorts, we used a clinical metric ranging from levels 1 to 5 (none to severe intellectual disability); for the rest of the singleton cohort, we used full scale IQ (Methods). There was no significant correlation between either measure and the rare genic TRE burden, indicating that the rare genic TRE effect on schizophrenia risk appears to be independent of cognitive measures/metrics (Supp. Fig. S4). The results also showed that the rare genic TRE burden in 22q-SCZ is independent of the individual’s sex, age at onset of schizophrenia, presence of additional CNVs, and genomic extent of the 22q11.2 deletion (Methods) in both the trio and the singleton cohorts (Supp. Fig. S5). Moreover, we tested for difference in rare genic TRE burden between 22q-SCZ individuals who also have a conotruncal CHD condition (including tetralogy of Fallot, persistent truncus arteriosus, interrupted aortic arch type B, right-sided aortic arch, and pulmonary atresia and/or pulmonary stenosis) and those having none of these CHD, and found no significant association, indicating that the rare genic TRE contribution to schizophrenia in 22q11.2 deletion appears independent of CHD presence or absence (Supp. Fig. S5).

Rare tandem repeat expansions have gene regulatory effects in the brain

Given the prevalence of rare TREs in intronic regions in 22q-SCZ, we tested whether the rare TREs are located near exon splice sites, similar to independent reports for non-22q schizophrenia that rare TREs near intronic splice-sites are major contributors to schizophrenia risk [23, 24]. We compared the distribution of distance to the nearest splice site for the rare genic TREs identified in 22q-SCZ individuals to those of all other detected (i.e., non-expanded) TRs in all 438 probands. The results showed that the schizophrenia-associated rare TREs were located significantly closer to splice sites (median: 3458 bp, IQR: 28579 bp) compared to all other non-expanded tandem repeats detected in 22q-SCZ (median: 34188 bp, IQR: 159215 bp; Fig. 2A; Wilcoxon’s sum test, p = 7.9 × 10-10). Also, genes affected by rare TREs in 22q-SCZ are more mutationally constrained compared to all other genes in the genome, measured using the gnomAD loss-of-function observed/expected upper bound fraction (LOEUF) score (Fig. 2B; Wilcoxon’s rank sum test, p = 1.7 × 10-3). As LOEUF scores are gene-length biased [59], we also compared the shet scores, generated by GeneBayes [60], between genes affected by rare TREs in 22q-SCZ and all other genes in the genome; this method showed the same direction of finding but no statistically significant difference (Wilcoxon’s rank sum test, p = 0.13). In addition, we found that genes affected by rare TREs, compared to all other genes in the genome, have significantly longer coding sequences (CDS; Wilcoxon’s rank sum test, p = 7.5× 10-9) and gnomAD expected LoF counts (p = 1.7 × 10-9).

To further characterize the potential molecular mechanism by which TREs may regulate genes in the brain, we assessed whether the identified rare TREs overlapped tandem repeat molecular quantitative trait loci, known as TR-xQTL. These TR-xQTLs were recently identified in human brain tissues from studies of human aging (NYGC-ALS; ROSMAP), including samples from the frontal cortex, motor cortex, cerebellum, spinal cord cervical, and lumbar spinal cord [53]. The five types of TR-xQTL molecular impacts annotated are: expression (eQTL), methylation (mQTL), splicing (sQTL), histone modification (hQTL), and polyadenylation (3aQTL). We merged all TR-xQTL data involving the same molecular mechanism and compared TREs identified in individuals with 22q11.2 deletion with and without schizophrenia using logistic regression analysis. The main impact of rare TREs in 22q-SCZ involved TR-mQTLs in the brain (Fig. 2C; logistic regression, OR = 1.93, p = 0.017, FDR = 0.16).

To illustrate this, we highlight two 22q-SCZ-associated rare TREs that overlap TR-xQTL loci in DLGAP2 and DMPK. DLGAP2 is a known schizophrenia-susceptibility gene that encodes a scaffolding protein in the postsynaptic density important for neuronal signaling. We identified AAAAT intronic TREs in DLGAP2 that overlap a TR-sQTL affecting splicing (Fig. 2D) in the frontal cortex. We also identified a known pathogenic CAG TRE at the 3’ untranslated region of DMPK (Fig. 2E), which overlaps a TR-mQTL affecting methylation and polyadenylation in the frontal cortex. The TRE in DMPK is responsible for myotonic dystrophy type 1, but has also recently been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders including autism/autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [55] and schizophrenia [61]. Our findings suggest that rare TREs may affect the regulation of expression of genes with important neuronal functions by disrupting splicing, methylation, or other mechanisms and thereby contributing to occurrence of schizophrenia in the presence of a high-risk 22q11.2 deletion.

Rare tandem repeat expansions affect genes known to be differentially expressed in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in general schizophrenia versus 22q-non-psychotic controls

To assess whether specific brain regions may be affected by rare TREs, we stratified the TR-xQTL loci by brain region and merged xQTLs of different molecular impacts identified within each brain region. We compared the burden of rare TREs that overlap TR-xQTL from each brain region between 22q11.2 deletion cases with and without schizophrenia using logistic regression analysis. We found that rare TREs (including TREs in intergenic and non-coding regions) in 22q-SCZ were differentially enriched with TR-xQTLs in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DFC) (Fig. 3A; logistic regression, OR = 1.67, p = 0.039, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR = 0.23), consistent with a predominant region of interest in schizophrenia [1]. Many of the genes involved, e.g., DLGAP2 and C9orf72, have known neurological or neurodevelopmental functions (Fig. 3B).

To further assess the potential impact of rare TREs at the cellular level, we investigated whether the genes affected by rare TREs in 22q-SCZ are differentially expressed in specific cell types of the DFC in individuals with general schizophrenia. We retrieved single-cell RNA-seq expression data of 25 cell types in the DFC of schizophrenia patients from PsychENCODE [54]. We selected genes that are differentially expressed (DEG) at 0.05 log2 fold-change and adjusted p-value < 0.05, then merged the DEG list by 6 major cell types: excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells. We compared the burden of rare TREs affecting schizophrenia DEGs in each cell type in individuals with 22q11.2 deletion, with and without schizophrenia, logistic regression analysis. We detected a higher burden of rare TREs (Fig. 3C) that impacts schizophrenia DEGs in excitatory neurons (logistic regression, OR = 1.82, p = 2.3 × 10-3, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR = 0.025) and inhibitory neurons (logistic regression, OR = 1.72, p = 0.014, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR = 0.057) in 22q-SCZ than in those without schizophrenia. Many of the genes involved, particularly those involving excitatory neurons, such as SHANK2, MORN1, and DLGAP2, are known schizophrenia risk genes (Fig. 3D).

Rare TREs affect genes that are involved in nervous system functions and neurodevelopmental disorders

To identify the functional impact of rare TREs, we selected 18 gene sets related to neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric phenotypes, SynGO biological functions, 22q11.2 deletion-DEGs, alongside 2 gene sets used as negative controls (Supp. Table S7; Methods). With these total 20 gene sets, we performed a gene set enrichment analysis using Fisher’s exact tests (Methods). The results showed that the genes affected by rare genic TREs in 22q-SCZ are enriched with FMRP targets [62] (Fisher’s exact test, OR = 4.65, p = 1.9 × 10-5, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR = 3.9 × 10-4), with trends also for a 106-gene set from schizophrenia genome-wide association studies (GWAS; Fig. 4A; Fisher’s exact test, OR = 7.72, p = 8.2 × 10-3, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR = 0.055), genes responsible for synapse organization (Fisher’s exact test, OR = 3.77, p = 7.2 × 10-3, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR = 0.055), and loss-of-function intolerant genes (Fisher’s exact test, OR = 1.94, p = 0.013, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR = 0.064). To further investigate the biological pathways impacted by rare TREs and their relevance in the context of a 22q11.2 deletion, we used GeneMANIA to construct a gene network that assesses the interaction between 73 genes affected by rare TREs in 22q-SCZ and the 46 protein-coding genes in the LCR-A to LCR-D (low copy repeat regions located at the breakpoints of 22q11.2 deletion) 22q11.2 deletion region [13]. The network showed that many of the genes affected by rare TREs interact with genes in the 22q11.2 deletion region. This pathway analysis also showed clusters of genes involved in schizophrenia, in synaptic functions, and in the regulation of nervous system development (Fig. 4B; Supp. Table S8).

We compared the 73 protein-coding genes affected by 22q-SCZ-associated rare genic TREs to the 193 genes identified in schizophrenia with no 22q11.2 deletion from our previous study [23]. We found that 20 genes were recurrently affected in both 22q-SCZ (9 probands from the trio cohort; 11 from the singleton cohort) and general (non-22q11.2 deletion) schizophrenia, significantly more than by chance (Fisher’s exact test, trio: p = 1.5 × 10-11; singleton: p = 1.6 × 10-13; Supp. Table S9). The recurrent genes include CAMK2B, a gene important for calcium signaling [63] in glutamatergic synapses, and DMPK. These results suggest that TREs may affect similar gene functions and/or pathways in both 22q-SCZ and general schizophrenia.

Discussion

Our study assessed the genome-wide contributions of multiple genetic variant types to modifying schizophrenia outcomes in individuals with 22q11.2 deletion using short-read genome sequencing data across two non-overlapping cohorts, providing internally validated evidence of associations between genetic modifier variants and schizophrenia. The results identified rare TREs affecting protein-coding genes and common variant (SCZ-PRS) risks as the two most prominent factors to further elevate the risk of schizophrenia with 22q11.2 deletion. Notably, the rare TRE component identified has an effect size comparable to that of SCZ-PRS. Overall, we estimate from our analyses that rare TREs may collectively account for ~6-9% of the risk of developing schizophrenia in individuals with 22q11.2 deletion.

Having identified rare TREs as significant modifying factors for schizophrenia in individuals without [21, 23, 24] and now with 22q11.2 deletion, newly available data have allowed us in this study to show that TRE effects on specific gene functions, pathways, and brain and cellular regions, can be inferred, something that has been a challenge for common polygenic risk alleles [64]. In terms of molecular mechanisms, we identified that rare TREs in 22q-SCZ are in close proximity to the splice junction of genes, as for TREs in other forms of schizophrenia [21, 23, 24], and also that they overlap with TRE-loci that are known to have quantitative trait effects on gene regulation, particularly those driven by methylation and splicing mechanisms. We showed that genes affected by rare TREs in 22q-SCZ are significantly more intolerant to LoF mutations than the rest of the genome using the LOEUF metric that may not sufficiently assess genes at the lower end of gene length [59, 60]. However, given that the genes affected by rare TREs in 22q-SCZ are significantly longer than genes in the rest of the genome, characteristic of those involved in brain function [65, 66] and development, we believe that the bias in the LOEUF intolerance metric is minimal.

Some genes affected by rare TREs in individuals with 22q-SCZ are involved in important neurodevelopmental functions. These include genes previously found to be implicated in schizophrenia and other neurodevelopmental disorders when affected by rare TREs [23, 55]. Examples include DLGAP2, a brain-expressed gene that encodes a membrane-associated protein involved in synaptic functions [67, 68], and SHANK2, a gene that encodes postsynaptic scaffolding proteins [69, 70]. The rarity of TREs defined in this study means that it is impossible to directly determine the penetrance of any single expanded tandem repeat locus, particularly given the limited size of the cohorts with a rare pathogenic CNV such as 22q11.2 deletion. Despite this limitation, we identified rare TREs that were previously discovered in studies of schizophrenia and other developmental disorders. One such example is the CTG repeat expansion in DMPK, previously detected in schizophrenia [23] and in ASD [55, 71]. We also identified genes affected by rare TREs that interact with genes in the 22q11.2 deletion region, suggesting that rare TREs may be impacting functions that were already affected by reduced gene dosage at this locus, thereby increasing the likelihood of schizophrenia expression.

While previous research on 22q-SCZ reported on significant contributions by SCZ-PRS [13, 57]; other genetic variant types, such as rare SNVs and rare CNVs, were found to have limited genome-wide effects [13, 21]. Our results on TREs represent a significant advance in understanding the genetic modifier effects on schizophrenia expression in the presence of a high-risk 22q11.2 deletion CNV. Crucially, our results allowed direct comparisons between the effects of SCZ-PRS and rare TREs. Although SCZ-PRS is a prominent modifier for schizophrenia risk in the presence of a high-risk 22q11.2 deletion, it has also been reported to be correlated with measures of cognitive ability [57], indicating pleiotropic effects that can be difficult to resolve given the aggregate nature of PRS with risk conferred by thousands of common single nucleotide polymorphism alleles [72]. In contrast, the results of this study show that the genome-wide TRE burden associated with schizophrenia in 22q11.2 deletion appears to be independent of cognitive measures, indicating that the rare TREs identified may have effects that are relatively specific to schizophrenia risk. We also demonstrated that TREs can have a variety of downstream effects, not only on the expressed gene products but also on epigenetic regulation mechanisms such as DNA methylation. We did not identify significant risk conferred by rare SNVs or additional rare CNVs, likely due to the modest sample size, thus being statistically under-powered to detect either variants of relatively small effect size or high-impact rare variants that are inherently scarce in the genome. Previous studies of schizophrenia [73] and ADHD [74] have reported that affected individuals predisposed with risk-CNVs tend to have lower PRS than those that do not. In our study, where all participating individuals have a 22q11.2 microdeletion, we observed significant positive linear associations between several modifier variant types, indicating that the effect of additional genetic risk factors may differ depending on the overall genetic context. As the 22q11.2 deletion is such a high-risk variant for schizophrenia, individuals with this deletion may be more vulnerable to certain modifier risk variants that would otherwise have more subtle effects. Well-powered studies of schizophrenia, using comparable methods to compare those with and without a 22q11.2 deletion, could identify modifier variants that differentially affect individuals with a 22q11.2 deletion with lower effect sizes in the absence of this deletion. We note, however, that the associations between genetic variants can be affected by the varying levels of variants detected, especially across heterogeneous study cohorts and genetic methodologies. While we accounted for these confounding factors as covariates in the regression analyses, we would like to further emphasize the importance of consistent, standardized sequencing methods, data processing, and ascertainment procedures in the field to facilitate large studies that involve samples from multiple sites.

We demonstrated the versatility of genome sequencing, which allowed us to characterize multiple classes of genetic variants. In particular, this technology enabled us to detect TREs in an unbiased manner, a class of variants difficult or impossible to assay using other more common genotyping methods, e.g., microarray or exome sequencing [18, 19]. Genome-wide TREs have been increasingly implicated in complex human diseases, such as ASD [55, 75], cardiomyopathy [76], and schizophrenia [23, 24]. In this study, we showed that contributions of TREs in 22q-SCZ are consistent with those in the general schizophrenia population.

While our study provides novel insight into the role of rare tandem repeats, more investigation is needed to further elucidate the extent of their contribution to schizophrenia risk in the presence of a 22q11.2 deletion, other schizophrenia risk CNVs, and in schizophrenia in general. Our 22q11.2 deletion study cohorts, while being the largest available for schizophrenia, remain modest in sample size, limiting the power for gene- or variant-level analysis. Many samples in the composite study cohorts could not be analyzed as individuals were assessed when too young (i.e., under age 25 years) for determination of non-psychotic status. Given the high impact of recurrent CNVs on psychiatric disorders, and potential benefits to patient care and treatment, and to research, we recommend broad implementation of updated psychiatric assessment for individuals genetically diagnosed with recurrent CNVs even if they were not first assessed for this phenotype. Our current approach of detecting TREs using ExpansionHunter Denovo and short-read genome sequencing data cannot directly measure the actual size of the tandem repeats in the genome, nor distinguish between (i.e., phase) the two alleles. We therefore are unable to report on the pathogenicity of individual repeat expansions, such as the threshold of repeat size for causing disease, or on the parent-of-origin of the expanded allele, which could be important in clinically identifying individuals who are at especially high-risk. The lack of paired gene expression data also limited our ability to directly correlate gene regulation with TREs within study samples/individuals. Moreover, tandem repeats can have complex structures with heterogeneous repeat motifs on top of variable size [77, 78], which may in turn affect how the expanded tandem repeats impact gene functions and disease mechanisms. Our current TRE detection approach does not provide adequate information on the complex structures of the TREs identified, limiting our analysis on this front. Future work will take advantage of the recent advancements in long-read sequencing technologies that are capable of accurately sizing the full repeat locus as well as detecting embedded complex structures. We recommend combining multi-omics approaches to establish a more detailed correlation between tandem repeat variation, gene expression, and epigenetic regulation. We also recognize that only blood samples from the individuals studied were available for DNA extraction and analysis. Direct functional validation of the effects of rare TREs on genes in brain tissue from the 22q participants was not possible. Nonetheless, our study showed that rare TREs have a similar effect size as schizophrenia-polygenic risk score in modifying schizophrenia risk, suggesting these variants as possible predictors of schizophrenia expression in the presence of a 22q11.2 deletion. A major drawback of polygenic risk scores is related to high ancestry-specificity, which limits utility, especially amongst populations with insufficient representation in GWAS [79]. Further investigation is needed to understand the prevalence of schizophrenia-associated TREs across ancestral backgrounds, and to assess the viability of including rare TRE burden when developing tools for predicting the variable phenotypes of the 22q11.2 deletion.

In conclusion, our study establishes TREs as important modifiers of schizophrenia, a key phenotype of 22q11.2 deletions. The results support the potential broader application of genome sequencing in early diagnosis and in development of novel treatment and/or preventive approaches for individuals with rare complex disorders.
