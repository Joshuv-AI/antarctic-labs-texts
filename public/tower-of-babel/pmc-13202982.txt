Spontaneous hemorrhage in the spinal canal leading to Brown–Séquard syndrome: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Wu Li, Lei Lei, Wei Liu, Chao Lv, Xiaosong Wu, Qiting He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage is a rare yet critical neurological emergency, primarily characterized by hematoma formation compressing the spinal cord or nerve roots. This compression may lead to radicular pain, sensorimotor dysfunction, and bladder/bowel dysfunction. Pathogenesis often involves factors, such as coagulopathy, vascular malformations, and neoplasms, though some cases are idiopathic, reflecting the complex etiology of this condition. For example, a 25-year-old patient presented with left-sided limb weakness and right-sided sensory loss without preceding trauma or systemic illness. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed an acute spinal canal hematoma compressing the spinal cord, and neurological function improved significantly postoperatively. This case highlights that idiopathic spinal hematomas may occur and require urgent management [1]. Given their rarity and variable clinical presentations, spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhages are frequently misdiagnosed, particularly when acute neurological deficits mimic stroke symptoms. This can lead to inappropriate treatments such as thrombolysis, potentially worsening the condition or posing life-threatening risks [2]. Hemorrhages are classified into epidural, subarachnoid, and intramedullary types based on location. This report describes a rare case of spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage leading to Brown–Séquard syndrome and discusses the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of this condition through a case report and literature review. By addressing a knowledge gap in the existing literature, this case contributes to improving clinicians' early diagnostic skills, optimizing management strategies, and enhancing patient outcomes.

Literature review

Typical clinical manifestations and diagnosis of Brown–Séquard syndrome

Brown–Séquard syndrome is a characteristic clinical syndrome of unilateral spinal cord injury, primarily presenting as ipsilateral motor impairment and proprioceptive loss, with contralateral pain and temperature sensation deficits. This neurological dysfunction reflects the pathophysiology of unilateral spinal cord injury and is critical for localizing cord lesions. Spontaneous Spinal Canal Hemorrhage leading to Brown–Séquard syndrome is relatively rare, with complex and variable clinical presentations often complicated by additional symptoms (e.g., posterior cord syndrome), which increases diagnostic difficulty. In the reported case, the patient demonstrated left-sided weakness and right-sided analgesia consistent with classic Brown–Séquard syndrome, alongside bilateral proprioceptive loss, indicating hematoma compression of a broad spinal cord segment affecting multiple functional tracts [1]. A literature review revealed that although Brown–Séquard syndrome is uncommon in intradural hemorrhage, specific clinical signs may aid in early identification and prevent misdiagnosis of stroke-like conditions [2]. The diagnosis of Brown–Séquard syndrome secondary to spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage presents significant challenges. During acute presentation, the syndrome may be mistaken for cerebrovascular accidents, particularly in patients with unilateral limb weakness, often leading to misdiagnosis as ischemic stroke and potential deterioration following thrombolytic therapy. When spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage causes Brown–Séquard syndrome, clinical manifestations typically include acute back or neck pain as the primary symptom, accompanied by classic ipsilateral motor and proprioceptive deficits and contralateral loss of pain and temperature sensation. Bladder and bowel dysfunction often occur, and rapid disease progression underscores the importance of vigilant clinical assessment to prevent diagnostic delays [3, 4]. Imaging modalities are critical for diagnosing spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage and Brown–Séquard syndrome, with Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) as the preferred diagnostic tool. MRI provides clear visualization of hematoma location, size, and degree of spinal cord compression. T1- and T2-weighted sequences offer excellent characterization of hematoma signal intensity at different stages: acute hematomas typically appear isointense or slightly hyperintense on T1-weighted images, whereas T2-weighted sequences may demonstrate variable signal intensity depending on hemoglobin oxygenation states. As hematomas evolve, dynamic signal changes aid in determining hemorrhage timing. MRI also detects cord edema, compressive deformity, and abnormal intramedullary signals, assisting in assessing injury severity. Computed tomography (CT) serves as an adjunct modality, primarily used to exclude fractures and bony lesions, offering rapid screening for emergency patients. However, CT has limited soft-tissue resolution compared to MRI. The recent advancements in MRI technology, including high-field MRI and multimodal imaging, have further improved diagnostic sensitivity and specificity. Comprehensive imaging not only localizes hemorrhage but also guides surgical planning and prognostic evaluation, forming the foundation for clinical management [5, 6]. For cases requiring exclusion of vascular malformations, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) is critical to identify vascular abnormalities and guide treatment. In patients with spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage, coagulation function testing is essential for screening bleeding disorders, including routine parameters: prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), fibrinogen (Fib), and platelet count. Coagulation abnormalities may indicate secondary coagulopathy or primary hematological disorders. Recent advances in dynamic coagulation monitoring, such as rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) and coagulation waveform analysis (CWA), have increasingly been adopted in clinical practice. These technologies enable real-time assessment of coagulation status and fibrinolytic activity. When combined with clinical findings and laboratory data, they provide a comprehensive evaluation of bleeding risk and neurological impairment, which is central to developing evidence-based management strategies [7–9].

Etiology and pathogenesis of spontaneous intraspinal hemorrhage

The etiology of spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage is complex and multifactorial, primarily involving vascular lesions, coagulopathy, hypertension, and atherosclerosis. Vascular lesions, such as arteriovenous malformations and ruptured aneurysms, are critical causes, as they predispose to vascular wall rupture and hemorrhage. For example, a pediatric case of paraplegia secondary to spontaneous extradural hematoma associated with intraspinal vascular lesions has been reported in the literature [10]. Coagulopathy, including anticoagulant therapy and thrombocytopenia, represents another significant risk factor. Hypertension and atherosclerosis contribute to vascular wall fragility, increasing the likelihood of rupture, particularly in elderly patients [11]. Rare causes include tumor invasion of extradural vessels, infectious vasculopathy, and idiopathic vascular rupture. Pathophysiologically, spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage leads to hematoma formation, resulting in increased intraspinal pressure and direct spinal cord compression. This compression disrupts nerve conduction, manifesting as neurological dysfunction [3, 11]. Among these, damage to lateral corticospinal tract motor neurons leads to ipsilateral motor deficits, manifesting as hemiparesis or paralysis. Concurrently, spinothalamic tract involvement (crossed pain and temperature pathways) results in contralateral loss of pain and temperature sensation, reflecting sensory deficits [3]. Local ischemia and inflammatory responses within the hematoma exacerbate spinal cord injury, with inflammatory cell and mediator release potentially inducing secondary cord damage [12].

Treatment options for spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage leading to Brown–Sequard syndrome

In patients with Brown–Séquard syndrome secondary to spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage, conservative management is primarily indicated for those with stable clinical status and no significant neurological decline. Candidates for conservative treatment typically present with mild or non-progressive neurological deficits, and imaging demonstrates no significant cord compression or expanding hematoma. Conservative measures include strict bed rest, hemostatic therapy, blood pressure control, and supportive care, aimed at maintaining a stable environment for neuroprotection and preventing rebleeding or disease progression. During treatment, neurological function must be closely monitored, particularly motor strength, sensation, and autonomic function, to detect early clinical deterioration. Emerging evidence suggests that active conservative management combined with symptomatic and supportive care can effectively stabilize hemorrhage, reduce complications, and improve patient outcomes in cases without surgical indications [13]. However, the cornerstone of conservative management is vigilant observation. If neurological decline or hematoma expansion occurs, prompt surgical intervention is warranted. For patients with acute spinal cord compression, surgical decompression and hematoma evacuation remain the treatment of choice. Acute hematomas cause cord compression and ischemia; timely decompression may mitigate injury and facilitate neurological recovery. Surgical timing significantly impacts outcomes: studies suggest early intervention (typically within 24–48 h of symptom onset) improves neurological function and patient outcomes [11, 14]. Surgical approaches usually involve laminectomy with hematoma removal, with spinal stabilization performed if necessary. Intraoperatively, efforts should be made to minimize iatrogenic cord and nerve root injury to reduce postoperative complications. Early diagnosis and timely treatment are critical for optimizing outcomes in Brown–Séquard syndrome secondary to spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage. Animal model studies provide critical experimental evidence for optimizing surgical timing and techniques. Following acute spinal cord injury, hematomas and edema induce marked increases in pathological intraspinal pressure (ISP), exacerbating secondary injury. A rodent model study demonstrated that ISP rises immediately after cervical traumatic spinal cord injury. In this setting, early decompression (e.g., durotomy) has been shown to effectively reduce ISP, hematoma volume, and apoptosis while preserving tissue integrity at two weeks post-injury [15]. A rabbit model of severe cord compression injury further confirmed that duraplasty reduces peak ISP more significantly than laminectomy alone, alleviating cord edema and enhancing spinal cord perfusion [16]. Collectively, these findings emphasize that early surgical decompression to relieve mechanical compression and reduce ISP is critical for salvaging at-risk neural tissue and limiting secondary injury. Prognostic factors include hematoma size and compression severity: larger hematomas with severe cord compression correlate with worse outcomes [11]. Patient baseline characteristics, such as age, comorbidities, and nutritional status, also influence recovery.

Discussion

In this case, the patient's hematoma was located in the epidural space. The patient had a past medical history of hypertension and atherosclerosis. Preoperative cervicocranial computed tomography angiography (CTA) demonstrated atherosclerosis. It is hypothesized that hypertension and atherosclerosis induced vascular wall fragility, leading to rupture and intraspinal hemorrhage. However, spinal digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was not performed preoperatively, leaving the possibility of epidural vascular malformation unaddressed. Surgical intervention was initiated within 24 h of symptom onset. Postoperatively, left limb strength improved from Grade 2 to Grade 4, and pain/temperature sensation deficits resolved partially, supporting the role of early decompression in optimizing neurological outcomes. This aligns with prior literature [11], which emphasizes that “early decompression reduces spinal cord compression duration and improves prognosis”. A high misdiagnosis rate has been reported for spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage presenting as Brown–Séquard syndrome [2]. In this case, the patient was initially suspected of ischemic stroke due to “limb numbness and left-sided weakness”. However, cranial and cervical MRI revealed a cervical spinal canal mass, underscoring the need for clinicians to remain vigilant for spinal canal hemorrhage in patients with acute neurological deficits and expeditiously obtain imaging to avoid misdiagnosis.

Conclusion

Brown–Séquard syndrome secondary to spontaneous spinal canal hemorrhage is a rare yet clinically significant condition, where accurate and timely diagnosis is critical for optimizing patient outcomes. The current research universally recognizes the central role of comprehensive clinical assessment and high-sensitivity MRI in early diagnosis. Conservative management is indicated for patients with mild neurological deficits and stable clinical status, whereas early surgical decompression confers significant functional recovery benefits, particularly in cases of acute neurological decline [11]. However, this case report has limitations: it is a single-case study with limited generalizability. Prospective multicenter trials are needed to validate these findings.
