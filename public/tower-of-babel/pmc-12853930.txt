FOXL2 + cancer-associated fibroblasts enhances epithelial ovarian cancer development via TGFβ/Smad signaling
Authors: Lei Zhao, Jingqi Nie, Jinming Wang, Dongliang Li, Xuewei Hao
Published: 2025

Background

Ovarian cancer is one of the leading causes of gynecological cancer deaths, with an estimated nearly 314, 000 new cases and 207, 000 deaths worldwide [1]. As the dominant pathological type, epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) accounts for nearly 80% of all ovarian malignancies worldwide, and 75% of patients were diagnosed at an advanced stage [2]. At present, treatments include surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and emerging therapies. Despite improvements in treatment, 5-year survival remains around 40%, and primarily patients die of recurrence [3]. Thus, elucidating the mechanism of EOC development is vital for changing management strategies and improving survival outcomes, especially in chemotherapy-resistant patients.

It is well known that dynamic interaction between tumor cells and their surrounding stromal can promote solid tumors’ progression, metastasis, and drug resistance [4, 5]. Prior studies have also shown that the tumor stroma also influences the anti-tumor immune effect [6, 7]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), a major component of tumor stromal cells, engage in bidirectional crosstalk with cancer cells—via paracrine signaling and direct contact—and regulate extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [8]. Consistent with this, CAFs are abundant in metastatic EOC samples [9, 10] and secrete chemokines, proteases, and growth factors to accelerate tumor progression [11]. Thus, targeting CAFs may represent a potential therapeutic strategy against ovarian cancer.

Notably, stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment often exhibit distinct molecular profiles that influence their pro-tumorigenic functions, and emerging evidence points to transcription factors as key regulators of CAF identity and activity. FOXL2, is an early-identified marker of ovarian development and function, playing crucial roles in folliculogenesis and the maintenance of ovarian granulosa cell homeostasis [12]. Existing studies on FOXL2 mainly emphasized the regulation of estrogen synthesis and gene mutations that cause ovarian granulosa cell tumors [13–15]. FOXL2-positive stromal cells are abundant and may be a source of CAFs in human EOC [16]. However, how FOXL2-positive CAFs influence ovarian cancer progression is unknown.

SUMOylation, a post-translational modification (PTM), involves the covalent conjugation of small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMOs) to target proteins, thereby regulating diverse biological processes such as protein subcellular localization, stability, DNA binding, and transcriptional activity [17]. In the context of cancer, dysregulated SUMOylation is a common event, contributing to tumorigenesis by altering the function of key oncoproteins and tumor suppressors [18, 19]. Given that FOXL2 is expressed in EOC-derived CAFs and SUMOylation is a critical regulator of protein function, we hypothesize that SUMOylation of FOXL2 in CAFs enhances their pro-tumorigenic activity by stabilizing FOXL2 protein, promoting the secretion of pro-invasive factors, and thereby activating downstream signaling pathways in cancer cells. This SUMOylation-dependent regulatory axis could serve as a potential therapeutic target, and inhibiting FOXL2 SUMOylation or its downstream pathways may mitigate CAF-mediated EOC progression.

In this study, we aim to address how SUMOylation regulates FOXL2 stability and function in EOC-derived CAFs and whether targeting FOXL2 SUMOylation or its signaling pathways can inhibit EOC progression in preclinical models. Our findings will clarify the role of FOXL2 SUMOylation in CAFs and support the development of stroma-targeted therapies for EOC.

Methods

Patient samples and determination of CAF expression

Ovarian cancer samples were collected from 30 patients who underwent surgical resection at Harbin Medical University. Patient characteristics are illustrated in Supplementary Table 1. This study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was carried out in accordance with The Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki).

Percent CAF expression was performed using double immunofluorescence (IF). FFPE Sect. (10 µM) were deparaffinized and treated with 10 mM citrate buffer (PH 6.0). After washing, slides were treated with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked in 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Solarbio, Shanghai, China). The slides were then incubated overnight with a mixture of two primary antibodies (rabbit α-FOXL2 (F0805) and mouse α-SMA (A5228), Sigma, St Louis. MO. USA, each at 1:500 dilution). After washing, the slides were incubated for 1 h with a mixture of two secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor594-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG and Alexa Fluor488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG, ThermoFisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and Hoechst 33,342 (ThermoFisher Scientific). CAFs expressing FOXL2 was determined using IF image analysis using Image J(version 1.8.0) software. Five regions of interest (ROIs) were selected per image and percent of FOXL2-expressing CAFs were defined as (FOXL2 + CAFs x 100)/(SMA + CAFs).

Isolation of primary CAFs

Primary CAFs were isolated as described previously [20]. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS, 1% pen-strep, 25 mg/L glutamine (Solaibao, Beijing, China). Briefly, tumor tissues were first digested with 0.25% trypsin/EDTA (Gibco) at 37 °C for 30 min. After filtration through a 100 μm cell strainer, the undigested tissue fragments were further digested with the same concentration of trypsin at 37 °C for 45 min. To isolate fibroblasts, the tissues were further digested in 0.1% collagenase IV (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 1 h in the first period, followed by digestion in 0.05% hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 30 min in the second period. The obtained cells were then resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium (Procell, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 20% FBS (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), 1% pen-strep, and 25 mg/L glutamine (Solaibao, Beijing, China) and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. CAFs were further characterized by expression of α-SMA (Catalog# A2547, Sigma). A cell was considered α-SMA positive if its fluorescence intensity exceeded 1.5-fold of the background signal (determined by average intensity of non-immunostained areas) as quantified using ImageJ software. At least 500 cells were counted per sample, and a sample was defined as α-SMA positive when ≥ 70% of the cells met the above intensity criterion (Supplementary Fig. 1A). After 7–14 days, attached and proliferating cells were cryopreserved or passaged for subsequent experiments. Primary cells used in this study were between passages 2 and 5.

Cell culture

Epithelial ovarian cancer cells (CoC1 and A2780) were purchased from American Collection of Tissue Culture (ATCC, USA) and were cultured in F12/DMEM or DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., LTD, Shanghai, China), 1% pen-strep, 25 mg/L glutamine (Solaibao, Beijing, China). For half-life measurement, 100 µg/ml Cycloheximide (CHX, Sigma, C7698), 10µM MG132 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), or 10µM chloroquine (Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., LTD, Shanghai, China) were added at the indicated times. Mycoplasma testing was conducted monthly using the MycoAlert™ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland), and all cells tested negative.

Plasmid transfection and RNA transfection

The FOXL2 cDNA were cloned into the pCMV-HA vector (BioVector, Beijing, China). HA-FOXL2 Lysine (K) to Arginine (R) mutants - K25R, K87R, K114R, K150R, K25/87R (2KR), K25/87/114/150R (4KR) were generated via the QuikChange Lightning Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kits (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Specific primers containing the mutation sites (sequences provided in Supplementary Table 3) were designed and all FOXL2 point mutant plasmids were validated by Sanger sequencing. The results confirmed the accurate introduction of mutation sites, with no template residue or random mutations, indicating their suitability for subsequent experiments. Flag-UBC9, His-SUMO1, His-SUMO2, and His-SUMO3 were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Cells were transfected at 70% confluence using Lipofectamine 3000 Reagents (ThermoFisher Scientific) following manufacturer provided guidelines,1.5µL/well (24-well plate).

Western blotting

Cells were lysed using IP lysis buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific). Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and electrically transferred to PVDF membrane. After blocking, PVDF membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibody. The different primary antibodies used were α-SUMO1 (67559-1-Ig, 1:3000), α-UBC9 (10070-1-AP, 1:2000), α-Vimentin (10366-1-AP, 1:5000), α-N-cadherin (22018-1-AP, 1:5000), α-E-cadherin (20874-1-AP, 1:20000) (Proteintech, Wuhan, China); α-HA (A02041, 1:800), α-His (A02050, 1:800), α-Flag (A02010, 1:800) (Abbkine, Wuhan, China); α-FOXL2 (ab246511, 1:1000), α-Snail (ab216347, 1:1000) (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA); and, α-Smad2/3 (D7G7, 1:1000), α-p-Smad2 (Ser465/467)/Smad3 (Ser423/425) (D27F4, 1:1000) (CST, Boston, MA, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated with anti-Rabbit/mouse IgG-HRP (MF095-01, 1:8000, Mei5 Biotechnology, Beijing, China) for 2 h. Immunoblots were visualized by the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit, and captured by Tanon 5200 (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

In vitro sumoylation assay

The SUMOylation of FOXL2 was analyzed using in vitro SUMOylation assay with Ni2+-NTA agarose beads. HA-FOXL2, His-SUMO1, His-SUMO2, His-SUMO3 and Flag-UBC9 plasmids were transfected into HEK-293T. After 48 h, cells were lysed and added into Ni2+-NTA agarose beads (Qiagen, Germany). After washing, the beads were incubated with elution buffer, and eluates were analyzed by western blotting.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) assay

IP was used to test the association between FOXL2 SUMOylation and ubiquitination effect. HEK-293T cells were transfected with Flag-tagged ubiquitin, HA-FOXL2-WT or HA-FOXL2-MUT. Whole cell lysates were immunoprecipitated overnight with α-HA. FOXL2 ubiquitination was evaluated by immunoblotting with α-ubiquitin (ARG54630, 1:100 Arigo Biolaboratories, Taiwan, China) antibody.

Collection of conditioned medium (CM)

FOXL2-WT, FOXL2-2KR mutant, or control plasmid was transfected into CAFs,. Transfected CAFs (1 × 106 cells) was cultured at least 24 h and supernatant was employed as CM for subsequent experiments.

Colony formation assay

CoC1 and A2780 cells were seeded and grown in DMEM medium and CAFs-CM (1:2) for 2 weeks. Colonies of CoC1 and A2780 cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then dyed with 0.1% crystalline violet for visualization.

Transwell assay

CoC1 and A2780 cells were cultured in DMEM medium and CAFs-CM (1:2). Next, cell suspension (1 × 105/100 µl) was transferred to the upper chamber of invasion or migration Transwell plate (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA). After 48 h, CoC1 and A2780 cells in the lower chambers were stained with 0.1% crystal violet and the numbers of cells in 10 different fields were counted by microscope (Ti-s, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and averaged.

Tube formation assay

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs)(1 × 105 cells) were seeded in a 96-well plate pre-coated with Matrigel and cultivated in endothelial cell growth supplement and CAFs-CM for 6 h [21]. Images were captured using a microscope (Ti-s, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

ELISA assay

The concentration of TGF-β1 in CM derived from CAFs was measured using ELISA kit (Jianglai Bio, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Xenograft model of ovarian cancer

All animal experiments followed care/use guidelines, were approved by Beijing Viewsolid Biotechnology Co., Ltd.’s Ethics Committee, and adhered to ARRIVE guidelines. Twenty-five 6-week-old BALB/c nude mice (Vital River, Beijing) were randomized into 5 groups (n = 5/group): control, CAFs, FOXL2-WT CAFs, FOXL2-2KR CAFs, and FOXL2-WT CAFs + SB431542. For cell inoculation, mice in CAFs, FOXL2-WT CAFs, and FOXL2-2KR CAFs groups received subcutaneous injections of transfected CAFs + A2780 cells (3.2 × 107 : 8 × 106). This ratio was chosen to mimic clinical ovarian cancer stromal-tumor cell proportions (3:1–5:1, volume ratio 4.8:1) and supported by preliminary tests (4:1 group: 100% tumor formation, no necrosis, moderate growth). The control group received 100µL A2780 cells alone. Mice in FOXL2-WT CAFs + SB431542 group got intraperitoneal SB431542 (TGF-β/Smad inhibitor, 10 mg/kg in 20% DMSO/80% corn oil) 3×/week for 4 weeks, starting day 2 post-inoculation. Tumor volume was measured weekly over a 28-day period using a dual approach. The primary method employed a digital caliper (0.01 mm precision) to measure the tumor’s longest diameter (L) and shortest diameter (W); volume was then calculated using the standard ellipsoid formula: V=. This method was validated by a strong correlation with actual tumor weight (R²= 0.93). For auxiliary verification, bioluminescence imaging (BLI) was performed using an A2780-Luc cell line (stably transfected with a luciferase reporter gene), which also showed a high correlation with caliper-measured volume. Double-blind operation was used: independent coding, unaware researchers for measurements (2-person average), code unlocking post-experiment. After 28 days, mice were anesthetized (50 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital), sacrificed (cervical dislocation), and tumors collected, photographed, and weighed.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS 23.0 and represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were. Between group statistical tests were performed using the Student’s t test. Multiple group comparisons were performed using a one-way ANOVA followed by LSD test. P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

FOXL2-positive CAFs in ovarian cancer correlate with poor prognosis

The proportion of FOXL2 + CAFs was higher in ovarian cancer patients (n = 30) with poor prognostic parameters, including high grade (P = 0.0048; Fig. 1A), lymphatic metastasis (P = 0.0008; Fig. 1B), and advanced tumor stage (P = 0.0171; Fig. 1C) (Supplementary Table 1). Importantly, patients with high FOXL2 expression in CAFs had lower overall survival (P = 0.0084; Fig. 1D). Cumulatively, this indicates that FOXL2 + CAFs were significantly associated with poorer progression-free survival and overall survival, suggesting potential prognostic value in ovarian cancer progression.

To test the validity of the predicted SUMOylation sites, we performed in vitro SUMOylation assay with HA-tagged wild-type (WT), or the K25R, K87R, K114R, K150R, and 4KR (where K25, K87, K114 and K150 were all mutated to R). Whereas in vitro SUMOylation efficiency of FOXL2 was increased compared to the control group was observed with WT-FOXL2, both K25R and K87R significantly downregulated SUMOylation (P < 0.001 in each case) (Fig. 2B), indicating that K25 and K87 are the critical lysine residues for FOXL2 SUMOylation. However, whereas mutation of K114 and K150 did not at all impact in vitro SUMOylation, mutation of all 4 predicted sites (K25, K87, K114, and K150) to arginine (R) completely abolished in vitro SUMOylation (Fig. 2B). In fact, the in vitro SUMOylation pattern mimicked the expression of these proteins (Fig. 2B, FOXL2 (anti-HA) lane), indicating that the mutations affected the stability of the FOXL2 proteins as well. Hence, western blotting was employed to detect whether SUMOylation can enhance the stability of FOXL2. The protein level of HA-FOXL2-2KR (K25R and K87R double mutant) were reduced compared to the wild-type group than that of HA-FOXL2-WT in HEK-293T cells (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C), as well as in CAFs (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, compared with the HA-FOXL2-WT group alone, FOXL2 level was increased in HA-FOXL2-WT and His-SUMO1 co-transfected HEK-293T cells, but co-transfection with HA-FOXL2-2KR mutant and His-SUMO1 attenuated the increase (Fig. 2D). MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, inhibited FOXL2 degradation, while chloroquine, a lysosomal inhibitor, did not inhibit FOXL2 degradation (Fig. 2E), indicating involvement of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway in FOXL2 stabilization. When co-transfected with Flag-Ub in HEK-293T cells, we observed more polyubiquitination in HA-FOXL2-MUT compared to HA-FOXL2-WT (Fig. 2F). These findings demonstrated that SUMOylation can protect ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation of FOXL2 and thus enhance its stability.

FOXL2 sumoylation requires SUMO1 and UBC9/UBE2I

We initially evaluated the relationship between FOXL2 and SUMO1-4 using String analysis (https://cn.string-db.org/) (Fig. 3A). The predicted correlation was confirmed by the Ni2+-NTA pull-down assay, which showed that FOXL2 was mainly modified by SUMO1 (Fig. 3B). Apart from that, UBC9 significantly increased FOXL2 modification in presence of SUMO1, suggesting that FOXL2 SUMOylation requires UBC9 (Fig. 3C). Requirement of UBC9 was additionally confirmed in the CAFs. shRNA-mediated knockdown of UBC9 in the CAFs robustly decreased FOXL2 SUMOylation (Fig. 3D), indicating that UBC9 is required for FOXL2 SUMOylation Thus, FOXL2 SUMOylation requires SUMO1 and UBC9/UBE2I.

SUMOylation of FOXL2 in CAFs is associated with pro-tumorigenic functions

We next tested whether SUMOylation of FOXL2 is required for pro-tumorigenic functions in ovarian cancer. We evaluated and compared in vitro colony formation, migration, invasion, and angiogenic capabilities of the ovarian cancer cell lines CoC1 and A2780, ectopically overexpressing either WT or 2KR mutant FOXL2. Whereas colony formation is associated with tumorigenesis per se, ability to migrate, invade, and form neoangiogenic vessels are associated with metastatic progression. Compared to SUMOylation-resistant 2KR-mutant FOXL2, the WT-FOXL2 induced significantly more in vitro colonies (Fig. 4A), migration (Fig. 4B), invasion (Fig. 4C), and angiogenesis (Fig. 4D) (P < 0.05 in each case). We also confirm that SUMOylation inhibitor TAK-981 reverses the cancer-promoting effects of FOXL2 + CAFs (Supplementary Figs. 2 A-B). These results highlight the potential role of FOXL2 SUMOylation in pro-tumorigenic functions in ovarian cancer.

FOXL2-positive CAFs activates TGF-β/Smad signaling in ovarian cancer cells via secretion of TGF-β, which in turn potentially affects ovarian cancer progression

CAFs secrete a variety of growth factors including TGF-β1, which is an extracellular signal that regulates epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) in cancer cells [22]. Ectopic overexpression of FOXL2-WT in CAFs significantly increased the TGF-β1 concentration in conditioned-media compared to control (P < 0.05). However, transfection of the FOXL2-2KR mutant significantly reduced the TGF-β1 concentration in CAFs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). To confirm that the increased TGF-β concentration in driven by FOXL2, we knocked-down expression of FOXL2 using 3 different shRNAs targeting the 3’-UTR of FOXL2 (sh-FOXL2) or a non-targeting control (sh-NC) (Supplementary Table 2). Successful knockdown was confirmed using western blot analysis. sh-FOXL2-3 showed the most robust knockdown among the 3 shRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 3A) and hence used for subsequent experiments.

FOXL2-WT was ectopically overexpressed in CAFs transduced with sh-FOXL2-3. When conditioned media (CM) from sh-NC-transduced CAFs was added to CoC1 or A2780 cells, they showed robust p-Smad2/3 (Supplementary Fig. 3B), a direct readout of TGF-β/Smad signaling activation. The p-Smad2/3 levels significantly decreased in both CoC1 (P < 0.001) and A2780 (P < 0.001) cells when CM from sh-FOXL2-3-transduced CAFs was added (Supplementary Fig. 3B). However, re-expression of in the sh-FOXL2-3 CAFs (sh-FOXL2-3 + FOXL2-WT) resulted in rescue of the TGF-β/Smad signaling activation in both the CoC1 and A2780 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3B; P < 0.01 compared to CM from sh-FOXL2-3-exprssing CAFs). These results provided direct experimental evidence that FOXL2 expression was required for TGF-β/Smad signaling activation in the ovarian cancer cells.

Given that our results indicated that FOXL2 SUMOylation increased its stability, we determined if FOXL2 SUMOylation is critical to its role in TGF-β/Smad signaling activation in the ovarian cancer cells. As expected, CM from FOXL2-WT overexpressing CAFs significantly increased the p-Smad2/3 level in both the CoC1 or A2780 cells, compared to controls (P < 0.001 in each case) (Fig. 5B). However, addition of CM from FOXL2-2KR mutant overexpressing CAFs failed to induce p-Smad2/3 (P < 0.01 in CoC1 and P < 0.001 in A2780 cells) in both the CoC1 (P < 0.01) and A2780 (P < 0.001) cells (Fig. 5B). These results indicated that FOXL2 SUMOylation is critical for activation of the TGF-β/Smad signaling activates pro-tumorigenic TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway in ovarian cancer cells.naling pathway.

To further understand the functions of FOXL2 in ovarian cancer pathogenesis, the effect of CM from FOXL2-WT or 2KR mutant cells on in vitro migration and epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) were evaluated using the small molecule TGF-β/Smad inhibitor (SB431542). Ovarian cancer progression is accompanied by a progressive loss of the epithelial cell marker E-cadherin and gain of the mesenchymal cell markers N-cadherin, Snail, and Vimentin. Similar to Fig. 4B, CM from FOXL2-WT overexpressing CAFs induced in vitro migration in both the CoC1 and A2780 cells, which was attenuated when instead treated with CM from FOXL2-2KR mutant overexpressing CAFs (Fig. 5C). Inhibition of TGF-β/Smad by SB431542 significantly attenuated in vitro migration in both the CoC1 and A2780 cells incubated with CM from FOXL2-WT CAFs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5C). Similar observations were made for the epithelial and mesenchymal cell markers. Inhibition of TGF-β/Smad signaling using SB431542 reversed the decrease of E-cadherin and increase of the mesenchymal cell markers in both CoC1 and A2780 cells treated with CM from FOXL2-WT CAFs (Fig. 5D). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays confirmed that wild-type FOXL2 directly binds to the TGF-β promoter (Fig. 5E). In contrast, the 2KR mutant exhibited reduced binding efficiency to this promoter, which was consistent with the decreased TGF-β secretion observed in the 2KR group. These results indicate that FOXL2 directly regulates TGF-β transcription by binding to its promoter, and that the 2KR mutation impairs this regulatory effect (Fig. 5F). Cumulatively, these data indicate that FOXL2-positive CAFs activates TGF-β/Smad signaling and affects ovarian cancer progression.

FOXL2-expressing CAFs enhances tumorigenesis in vivo via TGF-β/Smad signaling

We next determined if the FOXL2-expressing CAFs can regulate in vivo tumorigenesis and whether that was reliant on TGF-β/Smad signaling. For the control group, BALB/c nude mice were inoculated subcutaneously with A2780 cells alone. For the CAFs group, FOXL2-WT CAFs group, and FOXL2-2KR CAFs group, BALB/c nude mice were inoculated subcutaneously with a mixture of transfected CAFs (WT or 2KR-mutant) and A2780 cells. For the last experimental group (FOXL2-WT CAFs + SB431542), the mice were injected with a mixture of transfected CAFs (WT) and A2780 cells and injected intraperitoneally from the second day onward with SB431542. The tumors in the FOXL2-WT CAFs group were significantly larger than both the Control and CAFs groups (Fig. 6A-D; P < 0.001). The tumors were comparatively smaller in the FOXL2-2KR mutant CAFs group (Fig. 6B-D; P < 0.01). Inhibition of the TGF-β/Smad signaling significantly decreased tumor growth caused by the FOXL2-WT CAFs (Fig. 6B-D; P < 0.001). FOXL2-WT overexpressing CAFs significantly increased the p-Smad2/3 levels, and decreased in FOXL2-2KR mutant CAFs or with SB431542 (Fig. 6E; P < 0.001). These results indicate that SUMOylation-mediated stability of FOXL2 is critical for in vivo TGF-β/Smad signaling and tumorigenesis in ovarian cancer.

Discussion

FOXL2 is critical in the recurrence and prognosis of the ovarian granulosa cell tumors [14, 23]. Our results suggest that FOXL2 + CAFs may be associated with poor prognostic parameters including high grade, lymphatic metastasis, and advanced tumor stage. Our results showed that SUMOylation stabilizes FOXL2 in CAFs which is critical for TGF-β/Smad signaling and pro-tumorigenic functions in EOC.

SUMOylation is a major post-translational modification (PTM) that affects several oncogenic pathways and is protective role in maintaining cell function [17, 24]. This has led to research endeavors to identify clinically relevant SUMO inhibitors, like the TAK-981 (subasumstat) compound [25]. FOXL2 SUMOylation has been previously elucidated in ovarian granulosa cell tumors [19, 26]. Our study shows that FOXL2 was mainly modified by SUMO1 at K25 and K87 residues and requires UBC9, reiterating the involvement of SUMO1 and UBC9 previously shown by Kuo et al. [27]. SUMOylation of FOXL2 at the K25, but not K87, has been previously reported K25 [19]. FOXL2 protein localizes in stromal and glandular cells where it co-localizes with SUMO-1/UBC9 [18, 28]. Our results confirm that in EP300, SUMOylation can protect FOXL2 from degradation by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, thus enhancing its stability [19].

As a member of the FKH/HNF3 family, FOXL2 is involved at different nodes of tumor pathogenesis [29, 30]. FOXL2 somatic mutation (c.402 C >G, p.Cys134Trp) as well as overexpression play critical role in pathogenesis of granulosa cell tumors [14, 15, 23]. In EOC, FOXL2-positive stromal cells provides a source of CAFs [16]. In accordance with this, our study shows that patients with high FOXL2 + CAFs had the lower overall survival, highlighting a pivotal role of FOXL2 + CAFs in EOC progression and prognosis.

CAFs increases the metastatic ability of cancer cells via secretion of chemokines, cytokines, extracellular matrix molecules and growth factors in the tumor microenvironment [31]. TGF-β/Smad, JAK/STAT, NF-κB and MAPK cascades are involved in CAFs-mediated regulation of cancer progression [32]. In ovarian cancer, CAFs potentiates invasive disease [33]. Our results show that the concentration of TGF-β1 in supernatant of CAFs of FOXL-WT group was higher than the control group. Correspondingly, the levels of Smad2/3 and p-Smad2/3 were increased in the CoC1 and A2780 cells after incubation with FOXL2 + CAFs. Other studies have confirmed the oncogenic roles of CAFs via TGF-β/Smad signaling on cancer progression [34, 35].

Our results also show a critical and direct role of FOXL2 SUMOylation in CAFs-mediated tumorigenesis and cancer progression. In vitro colony formation, migration, invasion and angiogenesis of FOXL2-WT group were significantly increased by comparison with the control group, which were attenuated in the FOXL2-2KR mutation group. SUMOylation-mediated increase in FOXL2 stability potentially aids the malignant biological behavior in EOC. FOXL2 mutation at K25 and K87 led to the decreases of p-Smad2/3 in CoC1 and A2780 cells. Moreover, TGF-β/Smad inhibitor (SB431542) attenuated the increase of migration and EMT in CoC1 and A2780 cells incubated with CM from FOXL2-WT CAFs. Collectively, these results confirm that FOXL2 SUMOylation in CAFs activates TGF-β/Smad signaling and potentiates pro-tumorigenic functions in EOC. SUMOylation can protect IGF2BP2 from degradation by ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, thereby enhancing its stability and further promoting glioma progression [36]. So, SUMOylation-mediated stabilization of pro-tumorigenic proteins might be a common mechanism underlying tumorigenesis and disease progression.

Several limitations should be noted to contextualize our findings. First, the sample size for clinical correlation analyses is relatively small (n = 30), although multivariate analysis was performed to control for potential confounding factors, the limited sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle associations between FOXL2 + CAFs and clinical outcomes.Therefore, a multicenter prospective study should be conducted in the next phase. Meanwhile, subgroup analyses ought to be performed to explore associations within specific patient populations, and external validation datasets from public databases should be utilized to further verify the clinical significance of FOXL2-positive cancer-associated fibroblasts (FOXL2 + CAFs)., Second, tumor heterogeneity—with 30% of samples exhibiting mixed histological subtypes—could introduce variability, though multivariate analyses adjusting for subtype retained statistical significance. Third, unmeasured stromal factors, such as immune cell infiltration, might correlate with FOXL2 + CAFs and confound survival associations. Fourth, the median follow-up duration (36 months) may underestimate long-term survival trends.

Beyond clinical data, experimental models have inherent constraints: EOC cell lines (CoC1, A2780) lack the genetic diversity of primary tumors, and immunocompromised xenograft models omit critical host immune-stromal interactions present in human ovarian cancer. To address these, future studies will employ patient-derived organoids and humanized mouse models to better recapitulate the complexity of the tumor microenvironment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identifies a mechanistic model in EOC: FOXL2 SUMOylation at K25/K87 stabilizes FOXL2 in CAFs, promoting TGF-β/Smad signaling activation (Fig. 7). This enhances TGF-β1 secretion by CAFs, driving pro-metastatic phenotypes (migration, invasion, angiogenesis) in EOC cells and accelerating tumor growth in vivo. Clinically, high FOXL2 + CAF density correlates with poor prognosis, suggesting its potential as a stromal biomarker. This work highlights stromal protein SUMOylation as a novel regulator of tumor-stromal crosstalk, distinguishing FOXL2 from other CAF drivers and expanding understanding of PTMs in the tumor microenvironment.
