Establishment and application of a detection method for chinese rice-field eels rhabdovirus (CrERV) using the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a System
Authors: Na Su, Chen Xu, Wenzhi Liu, Mingyang Xue, Zhenyu Huang, Nan Jiang, Yan Meng, Yong Zhou, Yuding Fan, Ya Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Chinese rice-field eel rhabdovirus (CrERV) is an emerging pathogen that causes hemorrhagic disease in Chinese rice-field eels (Monopterus albus), leading to epidemic outbreaks, mass mortality, and considerable economic losses in aquaculture. Thus, the development of rapid and reliable diagnostic tools for on-site detection is urgently needed to address this issue. In this study, we established an RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a-based assay for CrERV detection, which exhibited superior sensitivity, specificity, and stability. The assay achieved a detection limit of 10¹ copies/µL. Specificity testing confirmed the absence of cross-reactivity with five other major aquatic viruses, including Grass carp reovirus (GCRV-II), Spring viraemia of carp virus (SVCV), Largemouth bass virus (LMBV), Cyprinid herpesvirus 2 (CyHV-2), and White spot syndrome virus (WSSV). Reproducibility analysis showed intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation below 10%. Analysis of the 26 clinical samples showed that the RPA‑CRISPR/Cas12a assay achieved a higher positivity rate (23.08%, 6/26) compared to qRT‑PCR (15.38%, 4/26), providing preliminary evidence for its diagnostic potential in detecting CrERV. Collectively, these findings indicate that the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a platform is a highly sensitive, specific, and user-friendly tool for rapid CrERV surveillance in aquaculture settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-026-03185-3.Keywords: Chinese rice-field eels, Monopterus albus, CrERV, CRISPR/Cas12a, RPA amplification

Introduction

The Chinese rice-field eel (Monopterus albus), a member of the family Synbranchidae, is primarily distributed across several Asian countries, with established populations reported in the United States and Australia [1–4]. Renowned for its firm texture, delicate flavor, and medicinal value, this species enjoys high consumer demand and provides substantial economic returns, making it a key freshwater aquaculture species in China [5, 6]. In 2023, the farmed production of Monopterus albus in China reached 355,203 tons, a 6.28% increase compared to 2022 [7]. However, intensive aquaculture practices, characterized by high stocking densities and deteriorating water quality, have compromised the immunity of Monopterus albus to viral pathogens, thereby increasing susceptibility to infection and resulting in significant economic losses [8–11]. Therefore, identification of causative pathogens and development early diagnostic methods are of critical importance.

Rhabdovirus is the principal viral pathogen reported in Chinese rice-field eels [12]. In 2013, a previously unnamed lethal rhabdovirus was first identified in farmed eels, causing hemorrhagic mortality characterized by dermal and visceral lesions [9]. In 2017, our laboratory identified the Chinese rice-field eel rhabdovirus (CrERV), which was taxonomically assigned to the family Rhabdoviridae and genus Perhabdovirus [13]. The complete genome of CrERV was subsequently sequenced, revealing a length of 11,545 bp with the canonical rhabdoviral gene order 3′-N-P-M-G-L-5′, flanked by a 71-bp leader region and a 61-bp trailer region, respectively [12]. This genomic information provides an essential foundation for the development of molecular diagnostics. Traditional viral detection methods include virus isolation [14], serological assays such as ELISA, latex agglutination, and indirect immunofluorescence [14, 15] and nucleic acid-based techniques [16, 17]. Virus isolation is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and requires specialized expertise, making it impractical for use in the field. Although serological assays are rapid and convenient, their sensitivity is insufficient to detect early infections [17]. Molecular approaches, such as PCR, qRT-PCR, and ddPCR, offer high sensitivity and specificity, with qRT-PCR being the laboratory gold standard. However, these methods remain constrained in on-site applications because of their long processing times, reliance on advanced instrumentation, and need for trained personnel [18, 19]. Therefore, there is a pressing demand for the development of rapid, highly sensitive, and accurate point-of-care diagnostic platforms for effective CrERV surveillance in aquaculture.

The CRISPR/Cas system was originally developed as a versatile tool for gene editing, epigenome remodeling and transcriptional regulation [20]. In 2016, it was first adapted for nucleic acid detection, enabling the rapid and specific recognition and cleavage of target sequences [21, 22]. Among the various CRISPR-based platforms, the CRISPR/Cas12a system is a crRNA-guided detection tool that combines high specificity and exceptional sensitivity [23]. Upon binding to and cleaving target nucleic acids, Cas12a collateral cleaves nonspecific single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) cleavage activity. This property allows the cleavage of ssDNA reporter molecules, leading to the separation of fluorophores from quenchers and the generation of measurable fluorescence signals [24]. Recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) is an isothermal amplification technique that operates efficiently at 37–42 °C, can amplify nucleic acids within approximately 20 min, offering considerable time efficiency [25, 26]. By integrating these features, RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a has emerged as a powerful platform for in vitro nucleic acid detection in a one-pot reaction. A landmark study by Jennifer A. Doudna’s group in 2018 demonstrated its utility for the rapid detection of human papillomavirus (HPV) subtypes in clinical samples, establishing one of the most prominent applications of this technology in molecular diagnostics [27]. Since then, the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay has been widely applied across diverse fields, including human medicine, agriculture, animal husbandry, and aquaculture, significantly contributing to pathogen surveillance and disease management. Notable examples include the detection of Potato virus Y (PVY) [28], African swine fever virus (ASFV) [29], Grouper nervous necrosis virus (RGNNV) [30], Infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV) [31], Infectious spleen and kidney necrosis virus (ISKNV), Mandarin fish ranavirus (MRV) [32], and Cyprinid herpesvirus 2 (CyHV-2) [33]. However, to date, the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a system has not been applied for the detection of Chinese rice-field eel rhabdovirus (CrERV).

In this study, we established a novel RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay for CrERV detection that combines high sensitivity, high specificity, rapid turnaround time, and convenient visual readout. Experimental validation demonstrated its effectiveness in clinical samples, demonstrating its potential as a reliable tool for the early diagnosis and surveillance of CrERV.

Materials and methods

Preparation of the standards and template

Pooled liver, kidney, and spleen tissues (20 mg) from diseased Chinese rice-field eels were collected for RNA extraction using a commercial kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA, from which a 1543 bp fragment was amplified with the primers G-PCR-F/R (Table 1). PCR products were resolved on 1% agarose gels, gel-purified (Genenode, Wuhan, China), and sequenced. Purified amplicons were cloned into the pCE3 Blunt Vector using the Ultra-Universal TOPO Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and the ligation products were transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells (Sangon, Shanghai, China). Transformed cells were plated on LB agar supplemented with ampicillin (100 µg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 12–16 h. Random colonies were screened by colony PCR, and sequence-verified positive clones were subjected to plasmid extraction using an Endo-Free Plasmid Mini Kit II (Omega Bio-Tek, USA). Plasmid DNA concentration was determined by UV spectrophotometry, and the copy number was calculated as follows: Copies/µL = [6.02 × 1023 × concentration (ng/µL) × 10− 9]/(base pairs × 660)‌. The recombinant plasmid stock solution was stored at −80 °C for use as a quantitative standard in subsequent experiments.

Design of RPA Primers, crRNA, and ssDNA reporter

The G gene of CrERV was selected as the target sequence based on the complete genome of the reference strain (GenBank accession: MH319839.1) retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. Following primer design guidelines, four pairs of RPA primers were designed using Primer Premier 5.0, and their specificity was rigorously validated using BLAST analysis (Table 1). The crRNA was designed from RPA-amplified fragments as templates. Using the online tool Benchling (https://www.benchling.com/crispr/) to identify protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sites (TTN, N = A/T/C/G), we selected the 20–24 nucleotide sequence downstream of each PAM was selected as the crRNA-specific binding region, with a stem-loop structure added to the 5′ end (Table 1) [34, 35]. The ssDNA reporter was designed as a short thymine/adenine-rich sequence (5′-TTATT-3′), labeled at the 5′ end with 6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM), and at the 3′ end with a black hole quencher-1 (BHQ-1) (Table 1).

Establishment of the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection method

The assay was conducted in two sequential steps: (1) RPA amplification. Reactions were performed using a Basic DNA Isothermal Amplification Kit (Genenode, Wuhan, China). All components were thawed at room temperature for 30 min, vortexed thoroughly, we assembled a 50 µL mixture containing 32.9 µL Buffer A, 2 µL forward primer (10 µM), 2 µL reverse primer (10 µM), 8.6 µL nuclease-free ddH2O, and 2 µL nucleic acid template. Finally, 2.5 µL of Buffer B was added, followed by thorough mixing and brief centrifugation to collect the contents. The tubes were incubated at 42 °C for 15 min in a thermal cycler. (2) CRISPR/Cas12a fluorescence detection. Based on the concentration ranges of the LbCas12a (Cpf1) Nuclease trans-cleavage system (ToloBio, Shanghai, China), a 10 µL detection system was assembled: 1 µL RPA product, 1 µL LbCas12a, 1 µL crRNA, 1 µL HOLMES buffer, 1 µL ssDNA reporter, and 5 µL nuclease-free water. After brief centrifugation and mixing, the reactions were carried out in a Celemetor real-time PCR instrument at 37 °C for 15 min in the FAM channel, with fluorescence signals collected every 30 s over 30 cycles. Both real-time and endpoint fluorescence signals were recorded, and the results were visualized under blue light.

Screening of RPA primers and crRNA

Using the established RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection method, primers and crRNAs were screened using a standard plasmid (1 × 109 copies/µL) as the template. RPA amplification was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by CRISPR/Cas12a detection, including a target-negative control (NC) and a crRNA-blank control (BC). The optimal primer-crRNA combination was determined by comparing the fluorescence amplification curves, threshold times, and visual inspection results.

‌Optimization of RPA amplification temperature and duration

An orthogonal experimental design was employed to evaluate three temperature gradients (37 °C, 39 °C, and 42 °C) and three time gradients (15, 20, and 25 min) for RPA amplification, resulting in nine parameter combinations (37 °C/15, 20, and 25 min; 39 °C/15, 20, and 25 min; and 42 °C/15, 20, and 25 min). Following the reaction, bands were analyzed by grayscale densitometry using ImageJ to determine the optimal RPA amplification conditions.

Optimization of the CRISPR/Cas12a trans-cleavage system

To improve the detection performance, the CRISPR/Cas12a reaction system was optimized with respect to the Cas12a protein, crRNA, and ssDNA reporter concentrations. (1) Optimization of Cas12a/crRNA concentrations: Cas12a protein and crRNA were serially diluted to final concentrations of 250, 200, 150, 100, 50, and 25 nM. Each condition was tested using a fixed ssDNA reporter concentration of 200 nM and recombinant plasmid DNA (1 × 109 copies/µL) as the template. Controls without Cas12a/crRNA were included. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, and endpoint fluorescence was quantified using real-time PCR and visually assessed under blue light. (2) Optimization of ssDNA reporter concentration: The ssDNA reporter was diluted to final concentrations of 200, 150, 100, 50, and 25 nM, with Cas12a/crRNA concentrations fixed at the optimized levels. Reactions were performed as described above, with controls without reporter included. Endpoint fluorescence was quantified and visualized under blue light.

Specificity testing

To assess specificity, nucleic acid templates were extracted from tissue samples infected with various pathogens, including Grass carp reovirus II (GCRV-II), Spring viraemia of carp virus (SVCV), Largemouth bass virus (LMBV), Cyprinid herpesvirus II (CyHV-2), and White spot syndrome virus (WSSV). CrERV-positive samples were used as positive controls, Chinese rice-field eel genomic DNA as a negative control, and nuclease-free water served as a blank control. The RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay was performed under optimized conditions, and its specificity was evaluated through endpoint fluorescence quantification and visual inspection under blue light.

Sensitivity testing

A qRT-PCR assay was developed utilizing the Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (No Rox). Reactions (20 µL) comprised 10 µL of Master Mix, 0.8 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL of template DNA, and 6.4 µL ddH2O. Amplification was conducted on a Celemetor real-time PCR system with the following cycling parameters: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 40 amplification cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Sensitivity was assessed by applying both the qRT-PCR assay and the optimized RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection method to tenfold serial dilutions of standard plasmids ranging from 106 to 100 copies/µL. Each dilution was tested in triplicate, including negative controls. The limit of detection (LOD) for the qRT-PCR assay was determined based on amplification curves, while for RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a, endpoint fluorescence signals and blue light visualization were used. The limits of detection (LOD) of both techniques were compared.

Reproducibility assessment

The reproducibility of the assay was evaluated through three independent reactions utilizing serial dilutions of plasmid templates (106 to 100 copies/µL). Both intra-assay (within-run) and inter-assay (between-run) consistency were analyzed, with negative controls incorporated. Variability was quantified by calculating the coefficients of variation (CVs).

Validation with clinical samples

To establish clinical applicability, both the developed RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay and qRT-PCR was employed to test 26 clinical samples collected from diseased Chinese rice-field eels suspected of CrERV infection in Xiantao City, Hubei Province. Following RNA extraction and reverse transcription, diagnostic methods were performed in parallel. Positivity rates were compared to evaluate diagnostic performance.

Results

Evaluation of RPA primer and crRNA screening

To identify RPA primers with high amplification efficiency and crRNAs with robust cleavage activity, four primer pairs were paired with their respective crRNAs for testing. Among these combinations, both crRNA2-1 and crRNA2-2 exhibited comparable amplification kinetics and fluorescence intensities. Negative control groups (NC) were indistinguishable from the crRNA-blank control (BC) (Fig. 1A-B). Notably, crRNA2-1 exhibited slightly higher end-point fluorescence than crRNA2-2 (Fig. 1B). Conversely, crRNA4-1 produced significant fluorescence signals but also produced elevated background fluorescence in no-template controls. Meanwhile, crRNA1-1 failed to produce detectable signals by either fluorescence detection or blue light transillumination (Fig. 1B-C). Consequently, the F3/R3 primer pair combined with crRNA2-1 was selected as the optimal system, demonstrating rapid amplification (with signals detectable within 1 min) and achieving the highest fluorescence intensity by 15 min (Fig. 1A).

The performance of different Cas12a/crRNA concentrations was systematically evaluated. Specificity assessment of the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection method‌

The specificity of the assay was tested using nucleic acids from five unrelated viruses, in addition to CrERV-positive controls, negative controls, and blank samples. Only CrERV-positive samples generated significantly elevated endpoint fluorescence and visible fluorescence signals under blue light (Fig. 4A, B). Kinetic fluorescence analysis confirmed that only CrERV-positive samples produced typical amplification curves, while all other groups remained at background levels (Fig. 4C). These findings demonstrate the assay has high specificity for CrERV without cross-reactivity.

Sensitivity of the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection assay

Serial dilutions of recombinant plasmids ranging from 106 to 100 copies/µL were analyzed to determine the analytical sensitivity. Strong fluorescence signals were visible at 106 copies/µL, and the signal intensity gradually decreased at lower template concentrations. The limit of detection (LOD) under blue light visualization was 101 copies/µL, with no distinguishable signal observed at 100 copy/µL (Fig. 5A-F). Endpoint fluorescence quantification confirmed highly significant signals at concentrations of 106 to 103 copies/µL (p < 0.0001) and statistically significant increases at 102 to 101 copies/µL compared with controls (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5G). The qRT-PCR method established in this study (see Additional file 2) could reliably detect down to 102 copies/µL, with variability at 101 copies/µL. In summary, the assay achieves a detection limit of 101 copies/µL, thus exhibiting a lower apparent limit of detection than qRT-PCR under the tested conditions (Fig. 5H).

Reproducibility of the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a detection method‌

Six plasmid concentrations (106 to 101 copies/µL) were tested in triplicate across multiple runs. Endpoint fluorescence correlated inversely with plasmid concentration and matched visual assessments under blue light (Fig. 6A, B). The assay demonstrated excellent reproducibility, with intra-assay CVs ranging from 1.92% to 7.67% and inter-assay CVs from 0.77% to 6.00% (Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5). CVs showed no significant association with template concentration, confirming the high reproducibility and reliability of the assay.

Clinical sample validation

To evaluate the clinical performance, 26 samples from suspected CrERV-infected Monopterus albus were tested by both RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a and qRT-PCR. The RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay detected six positive samples (#1, #21, #22, #23, #24, and #25), including two weak positives (#21 and #25) that were missed by qRT-PCR (Fig. 7A–D). In comparison, qRT-PCR identified only four positives (#1, #22, #23, and #24). Correspondingly, the positivity rates were 23.08% and 15.38% for RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a and qRT-PCR, respectively, with all qRT-PCR-positive results confirmed by RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a (Table 6). The result demonstrated the diagnostic efficacy of the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay for CrERV detection in a small set of samples.

Discussion

Rapid and precise identification of viral pathogens in aquaculture environments is essential for maintaining fish health and mitigating biosafety concerns [33]. Chinese rice-field eel rhabdovirus (CrERV) is responsible for hemorrhagic disease in Chinese rice-field eels (Monopterus albus), yet existing diagnostic techniques are limited in terms of speed and practicality. In this study, we developed a rapid, visually interpretable detection assay based on RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a, enabling sensitive and specific detection of CrERV within 30 min. Results can be interpreted through real-time fluorescence measurement or visual observation under blue light, significantly enhancing field applicability [36].

The combination of RPA with CRISPR/Cas12a markedly improves detection sensitivity [37]. Our assay achieves a detection threshold of 101 copies/µL, exhibiting a lower apparent limit of detection than qRT-PCR under the conditions tested. This improvement stems from three mechanisms: (i) high amplification efficiency—RPA polymerase tolerates inhibitors in complex biological samples [38]; (ii) accurate target recognition—the crRNA-guided Cas12a system provides single-nucleotide resolution; and (iii) signal cascade amplification—Cas12a-mediated trans-cleavage generates amplified fluorescence signals, reducing nonspecific interference prevalent in primer- based amplification methods [27]. However, the sensitivity remains below that of droplet digital PCR (0.23 copies/µL) [12], likely due to inherent limitations of RPA efficiency and Cas12a activity at extremely low target concentrations [27]. Notably, sensitivity comparisons were based on plasmid DNA templates. As CrERV is an RNA virus, plasmid DNA does not account for variability introduced during RNA extraction and reverse transcription, meaning that the reported sensitivity may not fully represent real-world performance. Therefore, subsequent studies should use in vitro-transcribed RNA or spiked clinical samples for validation.

Specificity is a key factor in diagnostic accuracy, especially for novel assays targeting aquatic animal pathogens [12]. Our RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a system showed no cross-reactivity with five representative aquatic viruses (WSSV, GCRV-II, SVCV, CyHV-2, and LMBV), including both DNA and RNA types, demonstrating high diagnostic precision. This specificity is attributed to Cas12a’s requirement for at least 17 base pairs of complementarity and its intolerance to tolerate single-base mismatches [39].

Despite these advantages, several challenges remain. First, the current two-step process—nucleic acid amplification followed by CRISPR/Cas12a detection—reduces template or primer degradation but involves manual handling steps that increase the risk of contamination potential [40, 41]. Second, the assay is primarily qualitative capability, lacking the quantitative capacity of qRT-PCR and ddPCR, with subjective fluorescence interpretation may compromise reproducibility [38]. It is a rapid qualitative screening tool suitable for pathogen detection in field or resource-limited settings, but it does not allow precise quantification of viral load. In contrast, qRT-PCR and ddPCR enable accurate viral load quantification and are better suited for applications that require precise quantitative data. The two approaches play complementary roles in diagnostic workflows: RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a is ideal for on-site screening and large-scale preliminary testing, while qRT-PCR and ddPCR are typically reserved for confirmatory diagnosis and viral load analysis [42]. Third, crRNA design is constrained by PAM sequence requirements, and reliance on suboptimal PAMs may limit its broader applicability [43–45]. Despite detecting two additional weak-positive samples, the small sample size limits the generalizability of our findings. Larger studies are therefore needed to validate the diagnostic advantage of the RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay for clinical use. Finally, high reagent costs may hinder routine field application. Addressing these limitations is crucial for advancing CRISPR-based diagnostics.

Future advancements may include developing one-pot reaction systems by physically separating RPA from CRISPR/Cas12a or employing photocleavable linkers [40, 41, 46], thereby eliminating intermediate handling steps. Incorporating bioinformatics tools for crRNA design can minimize off-target effects [38]. Integrating detection with lateral flow or microfluidic devices can facilitate faster, portable testing. Additionally, adopting rapid nucleic acid lysis techniques to replace conventional extraction can reduce the overall assay time [36]. Collectively, these improvements will enhance the practicality and diagnostic accuracy of CRISPR-based detection in aquaculture settings.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed and validated an innovative RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a assay for rapid detection of Chinese rice-field eel rhabdovirus (CrERV), combining high sensitivity, strong specificity and minimal instrumental requirements, and it is suitable for both laboratory and field settings. This platform offers a promising tool for early diagnosis of CrERV, facilitating effective disease control and sustainable aquaculture development.
