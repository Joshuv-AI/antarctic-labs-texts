Unveiling the Catalytic Pyrolysis Behavior and Kinetics of Epoxy Resin over Niobium Oxide-Tailored ZSM‑5 Zeolites
Authors: Shangpeng Pan, Hengbin Zhang, Rui Shan, Xinyue Liang, Jun Zhang, Chengyu Li, Taoli Huhe, Xiang Ling, Yong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sustainable recycling of thermosetting epoxy resin (ER) waste remains a critical environmental challenge. While fast pyrolysis offers a viable upcycling route, the direct thermal degradation of ER predominantly yields low-value, oxygen-rich phenolic compounds. In this study, a series of hierarchical niobium oxide-tailored ZSM-5 zeolites (xNb-ZSM) were synthesized to promote the selective catalytic pyrolysis of ER into high-value aromatic hydrocarbons. Comprehensive characterizations (X-ray diffractometry (XRD), BET, TEM, and pyridine-adsorbed Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Py-IR)) revealed that the incorporation of amorphous niobium oxide species successfully generated a hierarchical mesoporous–microporous network and enriched the strong Lewis acid sites, thereby optimizing the Brønsted/Lewis (B/L) acid synergy without destroying the intrinsic zeolite framework. Pyrolysis-gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS) results demonstrated that the optimal 3Nb-ZSM catalyst achieved an exceptional aromatic selectivity of 65.5%, significantly outperforming the commercial HZSM-5 (45.9%). Furthermore, kinetic analysis utilizing the model-free Starink isoconversional method elucidated the underlying catalytic mechanism. The hierarchical pore architecture of 3Nb-ZSM drastically mitigated mass transfer limitations, reducing the average apparent activation energy (E a) to 182.5 kJ/mol, compared to 241.5 kJ/mol for pure HZSM-5. Crucially, the Nb-modified catalyst effectively suppressed coke formation during the late stages of high conversion, avoiding the sharp E a increase associated with catalyst deactivation. This work provides deep mechanistic and kinetic insights into the design of robust, coke-resistant catalysts for the highly selective upcycling of waste polymers into valuable petrochemicals.

Introduction

As a cornerstone of modern thermosetting polymers, epoxy resin (ER) is ubiquitously utilized across advanced manufacturing sectors, including aerospace engineering, renewable energy infrastructure (e.g., rotor blades for wind turbines), and electronic devices (e.g., printed circuit boards). , Driven by the rapid expansion of global industrialization, the annual consumption of ER has surged to millions of tons. , Consequently, the decommissioning of these products inevitably generates colossal volumes of intractable organic solid waste. − Unlike thermoplastics, the inherently dense, three-dimensionally cross-linked covalent networks of ER make it infusible and insoluble, rendering conventional mechanical recycling or solvent-based dissolution highly inefficient. Historically, end-of-life ER has been predominantly subjected to landfilling or destructive combustion. However, these linear disposal models not only squander energy-intensive petrochemical resources but also trigger the emission of highly toxic secondary pollutants, such as polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins, under incineration conditions. Transitioning toward a circular economy and achieving global carbon neutrality targets urgently demands the development of sustainable, closed-loop upcycling technologies for thermosetting waste.

In contrast to crude incineration, pyrolytic depolymerization has emerged as a highly promising, eco-friendly alternative. By thermally cracking the polymer matrix in an oxygen-free atmosphere, pyrolysis fundamentally prevents the formation of dioxins while simultaneously recovering combustible gases, pyrolytic oils, and solid char. Nevertheless, the direct thermal breakdown of typical bisphenol A-based ER predominantly produces crude oils heavily dominated by phenolic derivatives. These primary oxygenates suffer from high corrosivity, an elevated oxygen content, and inferior heating values, severely restricting their direct commercial application. To bridge this gap, catalytic upgrading is imperative to convert these bulky oxygenates into premium chemical feedstocks. Traditionally, HZSM-5 zeolites have been extensively employed in the catalytic fast pyrolysis of biomass and waste plastics due to their robust shape-selective micropores and exceptional deoxygenation capabilities. Unfortunately, when applied to ER pyrolysis, the parent HZSM-5 exhibits limited efficacy in deep deoxygenation, leaving phenols as the primary constituent of the liquid products. More critically, the pristine microporous channels of HZSM-5 are highly susceptible to severe coke deposition, leading to rapid active-site blockage and premature catalyst deactivation during high-temperature reactions.

To overcome these intrinsic bottlenecks, incorporating hetero-metal species into the ZSM-5 framework is recognized as an effective strategy to tailor the pore architecture and modulate the Brønsted/Lewis (B/L) acid site distribution, thereby directing the reaction pathways toward deep deoxygenation and aromatization. , Light aromatic hydrocarbons are highly sought-after platform chemicals in the petrochemical industry, serving as precursors for solvents, dyes, and pharmaceuticals, while pyrolysis oils enriched with aromatics can serve directly as high-octane drop-in fuel blends. Among various metal modifiers, niobium oxide stands out due to its profound solid acidity, exceptional water-tolerant properties, and strong proton-donating ability, which allow for the precise tuning of the B/L acid ratio in catalytic systems. The integration of niobium oxide species with zeolites has demonstrated remarkable potential in acid-catalyzed transformations; for instance, previous studies have achieved aromatic selectivities exceeding 86% during biomass valorization over Nb-modified molecular sieves. Despite these successes, the specific application and underlying synergistic mechanisms of niobium oxide-tailored ZSM-5 catalysts for the targeted pyrolytic aromatization of complex thermosetting ER remain a significant knowledge gap. Crucially, unlike natural lignocellulosic biomass or coal minerals, thermosetting ER features a dense, three-dimensionally cross-linked covalent network. The thermal breakdown of this unique polymeric matrix generates space-demanding oxygenated intermediates (such as isopropylphenols and bisphenol A derivatives) that encounter severe spatial steric hindrance and trigger rapid pore-mouth coking inside pristine microporous channels. Consequently, catalytic deoxygenation strategies established for biomass or coal cannot be directly extrapolated to thermosetting polymer waste.

To address this critical void, this study innovatively designed and synthesized a series of hierarchical ZSM-5 zeolites engineered with amorphous niobium oxide species, aiming to achieve highly selective catalytic upgrading of ER into value-added aromatics. Comprehensive microphysicochemical characterizations were coupled with analytical pyrolysis-gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS) to systematically elucidate the synergistic interplay between the niobium oxide species and the ZSM-5 framework during the targeted aromatization of ER. Furthermore, advanced model-free isoconversional kinetic algorithms were applied to deeply decode the activation energy profiles and dynamic mechanisms governing the entire catalytic depolymerization process. The experimental findings validate that the niobium oxide-tailored ZSM-5 catalysts exhibit overwhelming superiority in driving aromatic formation while mitigating mass transfer limitations compared to conventional HZSM-5. This work not only paves a robust technical pathway for extracting high-value petrochemicals from recalcitrant ER waste but also provides crucial theoretical foundations for the clean valorization and sustainable management of thermosetting polymers.

Experimental Section

Materials

The raw epoxy resin (ER) powder utilized in this studycharacterized by an ultimate elemental composition of 67.87 wt % C, 7.47 wt % H, 21.02 wt % O, and 3.64 wt % N, and a proximate analysis of 90.46 wt % volatile matter, 9.54 wt % fixed carbon, and 0.00 wt % ashwas procured from Shanghai Bohr Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China. The pristine commercial HZSM-5 aluminosilicate zeolite was supplied by Alfa Aesar Chemical Co., Ltd. Additionally, ammonium carbonate ((NH4)2CO3, 99% purity) and niobium oxalate (Nb­(HC2O4)5·xH2O) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. All chemical reagents were of analytical grade and used directly without further purification.

Catalyst Preparation

A facile hydrothermal route was employed to synthesize the niobium oxide-tailored ZSM-5 zeolites. Initially, 22.5 mmol of (NH4)2CO3 was completely dissolved in 75 mL of deionized water to form a homogeneous solution. Subsequently, x mmol of niobium oxalate and 0.75 g of the parent HZSM-5 support were introduced into the aqueous solution, followed by vigorous magnetic stirring for 10 min. The resulting suspension was then transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 10 h. Upon cooling to room temperature, the solid precipitates were harvested via filtration, washed repeatedly with deionized water, and desiccated. Finally, the precursor was calcined in an ambient air atmosphere at 550 °C for 2 h. The obtained modified catalysts were denoted as xNb-ZSM (where x = 1, 2, 3, and 5, representing the molar amount of the Nb precursor).

Catalyst Characterization

The crystallographic phases of both the commercial HZSM-5 and the synthesized niobium oxide-modified zeolites were identified using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Panalytical X’pert) operating within a 2θ scanning window of 5–80°. The porous textural features, encompassing the specific surface area, were evaluated via N2 physisorption at −196 °C on a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 volumetric analyzer. The pore size distributions and total pore volumes were deduced from the desorption branches of the isotherms utilizing the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) algorithm. To elucidate the nature and spatial distribution of the acidic centers, in situ pyridine-adsorbed Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Py-IR) was executed on a Bruker FT-IR Tensor 27/BX-5 spectrometer equipped with a controlled-environment reaction cell. The surface morphological attributes of the catalysts were inspected employing a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi S-4800) at an accelerating voltage of 2.0 kV. Furthermore, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM, JEOL JEM-2100F, Japan) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping was conducted at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV to visualize the nanoscale dispersion state of the niobium oxide species across the ZSM-5 framework.

Py-GC/MS Analysis

The volatile product distributions evolving from the thermal decomposition of ER were analyzed online using a micropyrolyzer (CDS 6200, Oxford, USA) hyphenated to a gas chromatograph/mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific 1300-ISQ7000 GC-MS). For each analytical run, a homogeneously blended mixture containing 0.2 mg of ER and the target catalyst (xNb-ZSM or HZSM-5) was loaded into a quartz capillary probe. Chromatographic separation of the pyrolytic vapors was achieved over an Agilent J&W DB-1701 capillary column. The oven temperature program was initiated at 50 °C (held for 3 min), continuously ramped to 280 °C at a constant heating rate of 5 °C/min, and maintained isothermally for an additional 10 min. The mass spectrometer detector was operated with an ion source temperature of 290 °C, scanning over a mass-to-charge (m/z) range of 29–400. To guarantee the statistical validity and reproducibility of the data, every test was independently replicated three times. Kinetic Analysis

The thermogravimetric degradation profiles of ER under the catalytic action of xNb-ZSM were recorded on a NETZSCH STA 449 F3 thermal analyzer (TGA, Germany). During the assays, either pristine ER or a thoroughly ground mixture of ER and catalyst (at a fixed mass ratio of 1:15) was loaded into the sample crucible. The system was heated from ambient temperature up to 900 °C under a continuous flow of high-purity argon (serving as the protective purge gas). To facilitate the kinetic modeling, four distinct heating gradients (β = 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 °C/min) were applied.

Based on the acquired thermogravimetric datasets, a model-free isoconversional methodology was implemented to profoundly decode the kinetic mechanisms dictating the catalytic cleavage of ER. As documented in previous literature, isoconversional algorithms (such as the established Kissinger and Starink approaches) permit the accurate extraction of critical kinetic parametersmost notably the apparent activation energy (E a)without necessitating prior assumptions regarding the specific reaction model function. In this study, the Starink kinetic equation was adopted for data evaluation, which is mathematically expressed as eq 2 ln(βTα1.92)=const−1.0008EaRTα where β denotes the designated heating rate, R represents the universal gas constant, and T α is the absolute temperature corresponding to a specific fractional conversion (α). The conversion extent (α) at any given reaction stage is formulated as eq 3 α=x−x0x1−x0 In this expression, x stands for the real-time residual mass of the sample at a specific moment, while x 0 and x 1 correspond to the initial and final sample masses of the pyrolysis process, respectively. Consequently, by performing a linear regression plot based on eq , the E a at discrete conversion nodes can be reliably deduced from the slope of the fitted straight lines.

Results and Discussion

Catalyst Characterization

To elucidate the crystallographic evolution of the materials, X-ray diffraction measurements were conducted on the pristine HZSM-5 and the series of niobium oxide-loaded catalysts, as depicted in Figure . Macroscopically, the diffraction profiles of the modified materials exhibit a striking resemblance to the parent HZSM-5. Specifically, the preservation of the sharp, distinctive diffraction peaks within the 2θ ranges of 8.0–8.9 and 23.1–24.4° perfectly validates the characteristic topology of the ZSM-5 crystal. This phenomenon indicates that the intrinsic microporous framework of ZSM-5 possesses good structural stability during the niobium doping process, without apparent phase transformation or overall framework collapse. Conversely, compared to the bare HZSM-5, the intensities of the characteristic ZSM-5 peaks in the modified samples experience a progressive attenuation as the niobium loading increases. This observation reveals that the introduced niobium species do not crystallize into a long-range ordered phase; rather, they are highly dispersed as an amorphous state on the surface of the ZSM-5 crystals, establishing a harmonious coexistence with the highly crystalline zeolite support.

XRD patterns of the synthesized xNb-ZSM catalysts.

The N2 physisorption isotherms of the parent HZSM-5 and the niobium oxide-tailored variants are presented in Figure , with the corresponding textural parameters (encompassing BET specific surface area, average pore diameter, and total pore volume) detailed in Table . All evaluated samples display classic type IV isotherms accompanied by H4-type hysteresis loops. This feature explicitly confirms the quintessential micromesoporous hierarchical nature of these composite materials. Relative to the pristine HZSM-5 support, the introduction of niobium oxide species triggers a slight decline in the overall specific surface area, a phenomenon primarily ascribed to the effective penetration and subsequent occupation of the zeolite’s intrinsic micropores by the niobium entities. Furthermore, as the niobium oxide loading escalates, a dramatic expansion in the mesoporous surface area is observed, which stems fundamentally from the innate mesoporosity associated with the amorphous niobium phase. Crucially, among the synthesized series, the 3Nb-ZSM catalyst achieves an optimal balance, maintaining a relatively high mesoporous surface area and pore volume without severely sacrificing microporous attributes. This highlights a benign synergistic structural interaction between niobium oxide and the zeolite framework. However, upon further increasing the loading to 5Nb-ZSM, the surface micropores of the zeolite become almost entirely obstructed; the resultant prominent increase in the average pore diameter further corroborates a pore architecture now utterly dominated by mesopores. In summary, the judicious loading of niobium enables targeted regulation of the catalyst’s pore hierarchy, which profoundly dictates its shape-selective catalytic capabilities.

The morphological traits of the optimized 3Nb-ZSM sample were visualized utilizing SEM and TEM, as illustrated in Figure a,b. The SEM micrograph clearly portrays that niobium oxide is uniformly coated across the exterior surfaces of the ZSM-5 crystalline grains. Subsequent high-resolution electron microscopy observations fail to detect any discernible lattice fringes for the deposited species, thereby supporting the amorphous nature of niobium oxide. Meanwhile, the TEM image explicitly reveals the establishment of an intimate phase boundary connecting the amorphous niobium oxide phase to the highly crystalline zeolite host. This suggests a good structural integration at the nanoscale. Additionally, the corresponding EDS elemental mapping images (Figure c,d) demonstrate a highly homogeneous dispersion of the Nb element throughout the molecular sieve support. This structural feature likely contributes to the construction of a three-dimensionally interconnected mesoporous network, thereby offering a mechanistic explanation for the pronounced enhancement of the mesoporosity detected in the composite material.

SEM and TEM-EDS mapping images of the 3Nb-ZSM catalyst. (a) SEM; (b) TEM; and (c, d) TEM-EDS mapping images.

The nature and distribution of the acidic centers on the HZSM-5 and 3Nb-ZSM catalysts were elucidated using the Py-IR spectra presented in Figure . The spectral data indicate that all evaluated samples display a characteristic absorption band near 1545 cm–1, which is traditionally assigned to Brønsted (B) acid sites. Concurrently, the distinguishing peaks corresponding to Lewis (L) acid sites are predominantly located in the regions around 1440 and 1485 cm–1. In stark contrast to the bare HZSM-5, the modified 3Nb-ZSM catalyst exhibits a substantially elevated concentration of strong L acid sites. Although the deposition of niobium oxide inevitably masks a fraction of the intrinsic weak B/L acid sites on the support’s surface, the vacant 4d orbitals of the metallic Nb act as a robust compensatory mechanism, endowing the catalyst with an abundance of strong B/L acid centers. Consequently, relying on the potent synergistic B/L acidic environment forged by the niobium oxide and the zeolite framework, the catalyst is guaranteed to maintain sufficient acidic vitality under severe high-temperature pyrolysis conditions.

Py-GC/MS Product Analysis

To unravel the rapid pyrolytic evolution of ER across various catalytic environments, initial thermal cracking evaluations were executed at 550 °C. By employing the NIST 20 mass spectral library, the evolved gaseous volatiles were systematically categorized into six major chemical families: aromatics, phenolics, furans, ketones, aliphatic hydrocarbons, and nitrogen-bearing species. This classification facilitated a comprehensive assessment of how niobium oxide incorporation regulates the product distribution over the ZSM-5 support. Figure a visually contrasts the analytical results obtained from the blank control (noncatalytic), the pristine commercial HZSM-5, and the engineered catalysts. The data reveal that, in the absence of a catalyst, the thermal depolymerization products of the bisphenol A-based ER are overwhelmingly dominated by species such as phenol, p-isopropylphenol, and bisphenol A, driving the phenolic fraction to soar above 80%. However, the introduction of zeolitic catalysts fundamentally disrupted this product landscape: the phenolic abundance experienced a precipitous drop, concurrently replaced by a massive accumulation of aromatic hydrocarbons alongside minor aliphatic formations. This phenomenon indicates that the molecular sieves play a key role in promoting the selective formation of aromatics and facilitating deoxygenation, thereby achieving a significant upgrading of the bio-oil.

(a) Compositional distribution of ER pyrolysis products under different catalytic systems at 550 °C; (b) quantitative correlation between strong acid site concentration (at 400 °C) and pyrolytic aromatic selectivity over pristine HZSM-5 and 3Nb-ZSM catalysts.

Unlike the primary oxygenates evolving from biomass pyrolysis (e.g., small-molecule furans, guaiacols, and syringols) or coal tars, the thermal breakdown of the cross-linked ER network predominantly yields space-demanding phenolic oxygenates heavily dominated by isopropylphenols and bisphenol A derivatives. These bulky intermediates encounter severe spatial steric hindrance inside the narrow microporous channels of pristine HZSM-5, leading to incomplete deoxygenation and rapid active-site blockage. The engineered xNb-ZSM catalyst, featuring an interconnected mesoporous network and high-density Lewis acid centers (Nb5+), effectively alleviates these steric constraints, enabling the smooth diffusion and selective dealkylation/dehydroxylation of bulky ER-derived intermediates into light aromatics. Inspecting the ZSM-5 variants with distinct niobium oxide loading gradients in Figure a, a distinct “volcano-shaped” correlation (an initial surge followed by a subsequent decline) emerges between aromatic selectivity and niobium oxide content. Conversely, the trajectory of phenolic content mirrors this trend in exact opposition. Mechanistically, an appropriate loading dosage supplements the system with robust B/L acid centers, exerting a profound positive promotional effect on the catalytic cascade. Nevertheless, upon excessive loading, the modifier species tend to encapsulate and obstruct the intrinsic zeolitic channels. This blockage triggers a sharp plummet in the specific surface area, ultimately precipitating a detrimental decay in catalytic efficacy. Among the scrutinized batch, the 3Nb-ZSM configuration delivered an exceptional aromatic selectivity of 64.9%, which is substantially higher than that of pristine HZSM-5 (45.9%). To establish a clear quantitative structure–activity relationship, the high-temperature thermally stable acid site densities (400 °C, derived from Py-IR in Figure ) were quantitatively correlated with the pyrolytic aromatic selectivity, as depicted in Figure b. Compared to pristine HZSM-5, 3Nb-ZSM exhibits a 261.5% increase in strong Lewis acid site concentration (rising from 42.3 to 152.9 μmol/g) and a 236.5% surge in total strong acid site concentration (rising from 69.4 to 233.5 μmol/g). This quantitative jump in strong acid density confirms that the abundant Lewis acid centers introduced by Nb5+ species, together with a balanced microporous-to-mesoporous ratio, synergize effectively with Brønsted acid centers to drive the selective dehydroxylation and aromatization of phenolic intermediates into value-added aromatics. Given the extraordinary activity exhibited by 3Nb-ZSM in converting ER into aromatic frameworks, it was pinpointed as the optimal catalytic candidate for subsequent in-depth profiling regarding the overarching impacts of operational parameters, such as pyrolysis temperature and catalyst-to-feedstock ratios.

Building upon the aforementioned deductions, subsequent trials were anchored on 3Nb-ZSM as the target catalyst, spanning a predefined temperature gradient from 350 to 750 °C. This phase was specifically tailored to probe how thermal variations dictate the compositional makeup of the pyrolytic aromatics. The granular distribution of aromatic species at each distinct thermal node is delineated in Figure . The empirical observations indicate that, during the warming ramp from 350 °C up to 650 °C, the generation of both mono- and polycyclic aromatic compounds scales steadily in tandem with the rising heat. Under the optimal thermal condition of 650 °C over 3Nb-ZSM, the total relative aromatic selectivity reached 65.5%, comprising 30.5% monocyclic aromatics (MAHs) and 35.0% polycyclic aromatics (PAHs). As detailed in Table S1 (SI), the MAH subgroup is predominantly composed of high-value BTEX platform chemicals (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and p/m/o-xylenes), originating from the selective cracking and dealkylation of bisphenol A backbones. Meanwhile, the PAH fraction is mainly dominated by naphthalene, 1/2-methylnaphthalenes, and indene derivatives, which are generated through secondary condensation and cyclization reactions catalyzed by strong acid centers. The intrinsic mechanism governing this phenomenon is that an elevated thermal environment vigorously drives the deep deoxygenation and progressive aromatization of the phenolic intermediates. However, if the reaction temperature is further intensified to an extreme 750 °C, the zeolitic scaffold undergoes severe thermal sintering. Such structural collapse obliterates the native cage-like channel configurations and the associated shape-selective catalytic prowess, inevitably triggering a drastic curtailment in aromatic yields. Weighing these competing factors, 650 °C was designated as the paramount thermal condition for the 3Nb-ZSM-catalyzed depolymerization of ER, thereby establishing a solid baseline for the optimization of downstream operational parameters.

Impact of varying temperatures on the distribution of pyrolytic aromatics from ER over the 3Nb-ZSM catalytic system.

Ultimately, to explicitly clarify the regulatory role of the catalyst-to-feedstock mass ratio on the pyrolytic aromatization cascade, a series of comparative experiments were executed under isothermal conditions at 650 °C. Operationally, a fixed 0.2 mg of ER powder was successively physically blended and thoroughly ground with 0, 0.2 mg, 1.0 mg, and 3.0 mg of the catalyst. This protocol effectively constructed four distinct catalyst-to-feedstock ratios: 0, 1, 5, and 15. Based on the evolutionary trends of product distribution within the 3Nb-ZSM system displayed in Figure , it is evident that introducing even a trace amount of catalyst is sufficient to significantly alter the product distribution, actively propelling a fraction of the phenolic entities to transform into aromatic architectures. Upon further elevating the catalyst proportion, the relative shares of both monocyclic and polycyclic aromatics within the matrix exhibit a robust and steady growth trajectory. The core mechanism underpinning this observation is twofold: a higher catalyst dosage not only unleashes a denser array of catalytic active centers into the reaction matrix but also substantially prolongs the residence time of the pyrolytic volatiles within the catalyst bed. This optimization of the mass-transfer process improves the diffusion efficiency of intermediate species navigating into the pore channels to locate active sites. Consequently, secondary reactionsprincipally dehydroxylation and deoxygenationare drastically accelerated to yield aromatics. It should be noted that the high volatile content of ER (90.46 wt %) releases dense primary oxygenates upon thermal cracking. In analytical Py-GC/MS micropyrolyzers, employing a higher catalyst-to-feedstock ratio (e.g., 15:1 for a 0.2 mg sample) is necessary to ensure adequate physical bed height and sufficient vapor-catalyst contact time, preventing unreacted volatiles from short-circuiting. In practical scaled-up applications (such as ex situ fixed-bed reactors), the increased bed depth provides extended residence time, allowing high aromatic yields to be achieved at significantly lower catalyst-to-feedstock ratios (e.g., 1:1–1:2) while physically separating thermal char from the catalyst bed. Crucially, as presented in Table , even under a severe 1:1 catalyst-to-feedstock ratio, 3Nb-ZSM exhibits a carbon deposition of only 2.58 wt %, which is markedly lower than that on commercial HZSM-5 even under a 15:1 ratio (4.14 wt %). This stark contrast confirms that the tailored 3Nb-ZSM catalyst maintains superior intrinsic coke resistance and catalytic efficiency even under low catalyst loadings, demonstrating strong practical potential for industrial upcycling.

Kinetic Evaluation

In this section, the apparent activation energy (E a) for the thermal decomposition of ER under noncatalytic, 3Nb-ZSM, and conventional HZSM-5 conditions was evaluated using the model-free Starink isoconversional method. This approach allows for a comparative assessment of the kinetic efficiencies of the respective catalysts. Calculations were executed by extracting thermogravimetric parameters across four distinct heating ramps (ranging from 1.25 to 10 °C/min) and substituting them into eq , detailed in Section . As illustrated in the derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) profiles (Figure ), the uncatalyzed ER exhibits a slight initial weight reduction below 250 °C, which is ascribed to the vaporization of moisture and residual curing agents. This is followed by an intense degradation phase spanning 300–500 °C, corresponding to the thermal depolymerization of the cross-linked polymer network. Upon the introduction of zeolite catalysts, a more pronounced mass loss is observed prior to 250 °C, driven by the vaporization of bound water within the support lattice. For the 3Nb-ZSM sample, heating beyond 700 °C did not induce any observable structural decomposition of the niobium oxide phase. This underscores its exceptional thermal resilience, which is crucial for ensuring the high efficiency of the aromatization reactions.

Comparative DTG profiles of ER under noncatalytic and catalytic conditions.

Figure presents the linear fitting plots derived from the Starink method. Employing the isoconversional algorithm, discrete E a values were extracted over a conversion fraction (α) range of 0.1–0.9. The average of these values was utilized to represent the global activation energy barrier required for the overall reaction. To accurately isolate the fundamental ER pyrolysis kinetics from extraneous signals like zeolite dehydration, the effective reaction window was defined strictly between 250 and 650 °C (where α is scaled from 0 to 1). Consequently, the recorded masses at these two boundary temperatures serve as the foundational variables W 0 and W 1 required for eq . As observed in Figure a, the noncatalytic pyrolysis of ER yields an E a of 177.7 kJ/mol, a figure highly consistent with previous literature records. By contrast, the mean E a for the HZSM-5-catalyzed system exhibits a substantial surge, reaching 241.5 kJ/mol. This dramatic elevation in the energy barrier fundamentally stems from the heterogeneous gas–solid nature of the catalytic depolymerization. Degradation fragments are compelled to overcome severe diffusion resistance to penetrate the narrow micropores of the zeolite in search of active sitesa mass transfer limitation entirely absent in homogeneous thermal pyrolysis. Remarkably, however, the activation energy for the 3Nb-ZSM system drops drastically to 182.5 kJ/mol, far below that of pristine HZSM-5 and only marginally higher than the uncatalyzed process. This suggests that the niobium oxide modification effectively promotes the bond cleavage of ER macromolecules.

Linear kinetic fitting plots for the catalytic pyrolysis of ER.

The evolutionary trajectory of E a as a function of conversion (α), depicted in Figure , reflects the variation of macrokinetic energy barriers during the catalytic depolymerization of ER. In the absence of a catalyst, the early-to-middle stages of the reaction (α = 0.1–0.7) are dominated by the scission of the polymer backbone and the rapid release of small molecules, during which the system maintains a relatively low energy barrier. Nevertheless, as the reaction progresses into its later stages (α = 0.7–0.9), the continuous accumulation of a carbonaceous coke layer on the unreacted feedstock surface severely obstructs the outward escape of volatile products, , which reasonably correlates with the observed increase in E a, thereby hindering the complete conversion of the resin. Following the addition of zeolites, phenolic intermediates must navigate spatial hindrances to infiltrate the narrow micropores and access acidic sites. This supplementary mass transfer steric hindrance causes the overall E a of the catalytic systems to generally exceed that of the blank control. Comparative analysis, however, reveals that 3Nb-ZSM successfully circumvents this drawback. The abundant mesoporosity induced by the modification constructs an outstanding hierarchical pore network, which is expected to facilitate mass transfer, aligning well with the lower E a profile. Most critically, when the conversion α exceeds 0.8, the unmodified HZSM-5 exhibits a sharp increase in E a. Conversely, the E a for the 3Nb-ZSM system remains relatively stable and lower.

Evolution of E a as a function of conversion (α) during EP pyrolysis under various catalytic conditions.

This kinetic behavior is directly supported by the characterization of the spent catalysts (Table and Figure ). Elemental analysis reveals that the carbon deposition on spent 3Nb-ZSM (0.46–2.58 wt %) is significantly lower than that on spent HZSM-5 (4.14–10.46 wt %). Furthermore, SEM images of spent HZSM-5 (Figure a) show severe coverage of its surface by dense carbon layers, whereas spent 3Nb-ZSM (Figure b) largely retains the morphology of the zeolite crystals and niobium oxide particles. While apparent kinetic parameters alone do not directly decode molecular pathways, these complementary empirical characterizations strongly suggest that the hierarchical mesoporous network and tailored acidity of 3Nb-ZSM effectively mitigate coke accumulation, suppressing rapid catalytic deactivation during high conversion stages. This anticoking capability not only ensures the sustained and highly efficient execution of the aromatization reaction but also markedly prolongs the operational lifespan of the catalyst.

Conclusion

In this study, a series of niobium oxide-tailored hierarchical ZSM-5 zeolites were successfully developed for the highly selective catalytic depolymerization of epoxy resin waste into valuable aromatic hydrocarbons. Comprehensive characterizations revealed that the uniform dispersion of amorphous niobium oxide on the ZSM-5 framework constructed a robust hierarchical mesoporous–microporous architecture and introduced abundant strong Lewis acid sites via the empty 4d orbitals of Nb. This tailored Brønsted/Lewis acid synergy effectively drove the deep deoxygenation and aromatization of phenolic intermediates. Consequently, the optimal 3Nb-ZSM catalyst drastically shifted the pyrolysis product distribution from predominantly phenols (in the noncatalytic process) to aromatics, achieving an impressive maximum aromatic selectivity of 65.5% at 650 °C, which substantially outperformed the commercial HZSM-5. Furthermore, kinetic investigations utilizing the Starink isoconversional method elucidated that the hierarchical pore network of 3Nb-ZSM significantly mitigated the inherent mass transfer limitations of the solid–gas reaction, thereby reducing the average apparent activation energy (E a) from 241.5 kJ/mol (for pure HZSM-5) to 182.5 kJ/mol. Most notably, as evidenced by spent catalyst characterizations, the niobium modification substantially alleviated coke accumulation during late conversion stages (α > 0.8), preventing severe diffusion resistance and catalyst deactivation. Ultimately, this work provides a highly efficient and structurally stable catalytic system for the sustainable upcycling of thermosetting plastics, along with profound kinetic insights into the synergistic mechanism of hierarchical pore engineering and metal–acid site modulation.
