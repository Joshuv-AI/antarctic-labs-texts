Explainable Reinforcement Learning via Temporal Policy Decomposition
Franco Ruggeri franco.ruggeri@ericsson.com, Alessio Russo arusso2@bu.edu, Rafia Inam rafia.inam@ericsson.com, Karl Henrik Johansson kallej@kth.se

Abstract
We investigate the explainability of Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies from a temporal perspective, focusing on the sequence of future outcomes associated with individual actions. In RL , value functions compress information about rewards collected across multiple trajectories and over an infinite horizon, allowing a compact form of knowledge representation. However, this compression obscures the temporal details inherent in sequential decision-making, presenting a key challenge for interpretability. We present Temporal Policy Decomposition (TPD) , a novel explainability approach that explains individual RL actions in terms of their Expected Future Outcome (EFO) . These explanations decompose generalized value functions into a sequence of EFOs , one for each time step up to a prediction horizon of interest, revealing insights into when specific outcomes are expected to occur. We leverage fixed-horizon temporal difference learning to devise an off-policy method for learning EFOs for both optimal and suboptimal actions, enabling contrastive explanations consisting of EFOs for different state-action pairs. Our experiments demonstrate that TPD generates accurate explanations that (i) clarify the policy’s future strategy and anticipated trajectory for a given action and (ii) improve understanding of the reward composition, facilitating fine-tuning of the reward function to align with human expectations.

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL)
has demonstrated considerable success in a wide range of applications, including complex domains like robotics
(
Kober et al., 2013
;
Polydoros and Nalpantidis, 2017
)
and telecommunications
(
Saxena et al., 2022
;
Vannella et al., 2022
)
. Despite these achievements, a persistent challenge in
RL
is its lack of interpretability. The opacity of
RL
solutions hinders their wider adoption and deployment to real-world applications, especially in safety-critical or highly regulated fields. This challenge has given rise to the field of
Explainable Reinforcement Learning (XRL)
, which aims to provide user-interpretable explanations to make
RL
systems more transparent and interpretable
(
Milani et al., 2023
;
Puiutta and Veith, 2020
)
. A clear explanation of why a model makes certain decisions is essential for gaining trust from human users as well as ensuring robust and reliable deployment.

Research in
XRL
has produced various methods to address the interpretability challenge. One line of research has focused on explaining the function approximators employed in deep
RL
algorithms, where deep neural networks are employed to represent value functions or policies
(
Greydanus et al., 2018
;
Lundberg and Lee, 2017
)
. These methods are generally designed for supervised learning settings, thus neglecting the sequential nature distinctive of
RL
. A promising alternative direction consists of learning additional value functions, often referred to as
Generalized Value Functions (GVFs)
, to explain the dynamics of the
RL
environment under a given
RL
policy
(
Juozapaitis et al., 2019
;
Yau et al., 2020
;
Lin et al., 2021
)
. These
GVFs
are usually designed to decompose across a particular dimension the primary reward-based value function, which is used by the
RL
agent for decision-making.

While these approaches offer valuable insights, they completely overlook the time dimension of
RL
-based decision-making. A core problem in explaining
RL
behavior lies, in fact, in how value functions obscure temporal information. Specifically, value functions summarize the expected rewards over multiple trajectories and an infinite horizon, effectively hiding
when
and
where
rewards are expected to be collected. The temporal ambiguity is especially problematic due to delayed rewards (i.e., rewards that are the result of a sequence of actions). Therefore, effective explanations should clarify the underlying strategy of an
RL
policy: What rewards does the policy intend to collect, and over what time frame? Incorporating this temporal aspect into explanations is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of
RL
behavior.

One attempt to address this gap was made by
van der Waa et al. (2018)
, who proposed analyzing the most likely future trajectory under an
RL
policy and comparing it with a counterfactual aligned with human expectations. However, this approach has two notable limitations:

(i)

it exclusively focuses on the most likely trajectory, potentially neglecting a significant portion of the possible outcomes in stochastic environments, and

(ii)

it assumes a fully known environment model to compute the most likely trajectory with Monte Carlo simulations.

Additionally, while model-based
RL
algorithms aim to learn the environment’s dynamics by modeling the next-state distribution
(
Moerland et al., 2023
)
, they cannot accurately predict state distributions at future time steps due to the challenges of uncertainty propagation and compound errors. As a result, there remains a need for methods that can explain actions in terms of their outcomes in future time steps while also accounting for the inherent uncertainty of
RL
decisions.

In this paper, we present
Temporal Policy Decomposition (TPD)
, a novel
XRL
method that explains
RL
actions in terms of their
Expected Future Outcomes (EFOs)
. Given an initial state-action pair, an outcome function, and an
RL
policy, an
EFO
provides information about the outcome after a specific number of time steps. Our method generates explanations, consisting of
EFOs
for each future time step up to a fixed prediction horizon, that offers time-granular insights into the expected future trajectory. This approach is orthogonal to existing value decomposition techniques
(
Juozapaitis et al., 2019
;
Yau et al., 2020
;
Lin et al., 2021
)
, as the set of
EFOs
for a range of time steps can be seen as a temporal decomposition of the
GVF
associated with the outcome function. In this regard, we explore two important classes of outcomes—rewards and events—that yield meaningful temporal decompositions. To learn
EFOs
, we propose an off-policy learning method based on
Fixed-Horizon Temporal Difference (FHTD)
learning
(
De Asis et al., 2020
)
. The off-policy nature of
TPD
supports the generation of contrastive explanations, often preferred by human users
(
Miller, 2019
)
, by comparing optimal and non-optimal actions. Additionally, we prove the convergence of tabular
FHTD
learning under less restrictive assumptions than those presented in
De Asis et al. (2020)
.

Contributions

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We introduce
TPD
, a novel
XRL
method that explains
RL
actions by decomposing
GVFs
across the temporal dimension. The generated explanations offer time-granular insights into the consequences of each decision.

•

We analyze
TPD
for two relevant classes of outcomes—rewards and events—providing a flexible framework for interpreting
RL
behavior.

•

We prove the convergence of
FHTD
learning in tabular settings under less restrictive assumptions compared to the proof by
De Asis et al. (2020)
.

•

We conduct an empirical evaluation of
TPD
, demonstrating its ability to generate intuitive and reliable explanations.

2
Related Work

The literature of
XRL
presents methods that tackle the interpretability challenge from different perspectives, as outlined by
Milani et al. (2023)
. The most relevant line of research for this paper is the training of auxiliary value functions, sometimes referred to as
GVFs
, to offer a more interpretable view of the primary reward-based value function used for decision-making. Based on this approach,
Juozapaitis et al. (2019)
explored the idea of decomposing the value function into separate components that align with the reward composition, assuming that the reward is formulated as a sum of reward components. The value composition can then be used as an explanation of how each reward component affects decision-making.
Lin et al. (2021)
generalized this method by introducing user-defined outcomes in place of traditional rewards. Specifically, they proposed a two-stage model where

(i)

the first stage learns the
GVFs
as functions of state and action, capturing the user-defined outcomes, and

(ii)

the second stage then uses these
GVFs
to predict the reward-based value function.

This architecture can be trained end-to-end and generate explanations in terms of
GVFs
, which represent expected discounted sums of the user-defined outcomes. Instead,
Yau et al. (2020)
proposed to train, in parallel to the reward-based value function, state-action occupancy maps estimating the expected occurrence of state-action pairs in the future trajectory. Despite providing relevant insights, none of these works considered the temporal dimension, as they all rely on discounted sums spanning an infinite horizon. In this paper, while we use the
GVF
concept and the idea of learning additional
GVFs
to enhance the transparency of the main reward-based value function, we focus on a temporal decomposition of
GVFs
to explain actions in terms of their expected outcomes in future time steps.

Another closely related study is by
van der Waa et al. (2018)
, who devised a method for generating contrastive explanations by comparing the most likely trajectory under the given
RL
policy with the most likely trajectory from a different policy aligned with human expectations. This method produces explanations as a sequence of abstracted states, actions, and rewards. However, it has two main limitations:

(i)

it focuses exclusively on the most likely trajectory, potentially overlooking a significant range of possible outcomes in stochastic environments, and

(ii)

it requires a fully known environment model to identify the most likely trajectory using Monte Carlo simulations.

In contrast, our approach overcomes these limitations by predicting
EFO
based on the full distribution of trajectories and by learning these
EFOs
in a model-free manner.

Model-based
RL
, which involves explicitly learning the environment’s dynamics to guide decision-making
(
Polydoros and Nalpantidis, 2017
)
, is also a related research area. State-of-the-art methods in this field utilize ensembles of probabilistic neural networks to model the next-state distribution
(
Chua et al., 2018
;
Janner et al., 2019
)
. These dynamics models can then generate trajectories through sequential rollouts for planning or for data augmentation. While such trajectories could, in principle, be used to explain actions, there are several limitations. First, a well-known challenge with such models is the compounding error, where small inaccuracies accumulate over a rollout, leading to significant errors in long-horizon trajectories. Second, these models do not estimate the trajectory distribution, as it is not possible to propagate uncertainty analytically over multiple time steps. Thus, an explanation would rely on a limited set of sampled trajectories, which would inevitably capture only a fraction of the full trajectory distribution. A method to manage uncertainty propagation through the dynamics model was introduced by
Deisenroth and Rasmussen (2011)
, using Gaussian processes and Gaussian approximations at each time step. While these approximations have been shown effective for policy optimization, they are problematic when trajectories are used directly for explainability, where trustworthiness and reliability are crucial. In this paper, we take a different approach by designing models that estimate the desired quantities directly at specific future time steps, avoiding relying on rollouts and dynamics models altogether.

3
Background

In this section, we briefly present the technical background and notation on
RL
and
FHTD
learning. These concepts are extensively used in the remainder of the paper.

3.1
Reinforcement Learning

An
RL
problem is mathematically modeled as a
Markov Decision Process (MDP)

(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
)
. An
MDP
is defined by a tuple
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r,\gamma\rangle
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
p:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to[0,1]
are the transition dynamics,
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to[-1,1]
is the reward function, which we assume bounded
1
1

1

We assume intervals
[-1,1]
to simplify the analysis, which can be extended to generic bounded intervals. The same applies to outcome functions.
, and
\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor. Specifically, at time
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, an agent observes the environment state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
and selects an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Then, the
MDP
transitions to a new state
s_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})
and gives the agent a reward
r_{t}=r(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})
. An
MDP
is called finite if both its state and action spaces are discrete and continuous if either the state or action space is continuous. A policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
defines the probability
\pi(a|s)
of taking action
a\in\mathcal{A}
in state
s\in\mathcal{S}
. The state-action value function under a policy
\pi
, denoted
Q^{\pi}
, represents the expected return collected by the policy
\pi
conditioned to an initial state-action pair:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\middle|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a,\pi\right]

(1)

where the condition on
\pi
indicates
a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t})
for
t>0
.
Instead, the state value function under a policy
\pi
, denoted
V^{\pi}
, is conditioned only on the initial state:
V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)}[Q^{\pi}(s,a)]
. A generalization of the value function concept is the
GVF
, which replaces rewards with generic outcomes
(
Sutton et al., 2011
;
Lin et al., 2021
)
. Specifically, given a measurable and bounded outcome function
o:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to[-1,1]
, with
o_{t}=o(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})
, the state-action
GVF
is:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}_{o}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}o_{t}\middle|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a,\pi\right]

(2)

where
a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t})
for
t>0
. The two fundamental
RL
problems are
prediction
and
control
. Prediction consists of learning the value function under a given policy
\pi
,
V^{\pi}
or
Q^{\pi}
, whereas control involves learning an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
that maximizes the value in each state. An
RL
algorithm learns to perform prediction or control based on experiences obtained through interactions between the agent and the environment without prior knowledge of the transition probabilities.

3.2
Fixed-Horizon Temporal Difference Learning

The
fixed-horizon
state-action value function under a policy
\pi
, denoted
Q_{h}^{\pi}
, is defined as the expected return collected over a fixed horizon:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}_{h}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{h-1}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\middle|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a,\pi\right]

(3)

where
a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t})
for
t=1,\dots,h-1
. A state-of-the-art
RL
prediction algorithm that learns this function is
FHTD
learning
(
De Asis et al., 2020
)
. Consider a transition at time
t
from state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
to state
s_{t+1}\in\mathcal{S}
after taking action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
, with reward
r_{t}=r(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})
. In a finite
MDP
, let
\hat{Q}^{(t)}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
be the estimate of
Q^{\pi}_{h}
at time
t
. The
FHTD
update is:

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{h}

\displaystyle=r_{t}+\gamma\hat{Q}_{h-1}(s_{t+1},a_{t+1}),\;a_{t+1}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t+1})

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{(t+1)}_{h}(s_{t},a_{t})

\displaystyle\leftarrow\hat{Q}^{(t)}_{h}(s_{t},a_{t})+\alpha_{t,h}(s_{t},a_{t})[\hat{G}_{h}-\hat{Q}^{(t)}_{h}(s_{t},a_{t})]

(5)

for
h=0,\dots,H-1
, where
H\in\mathbb{N}
is the prediction horizon and
\alpha_{t,h}
a learning rate schedule.

4
Temporal Policy Decomposition

In this section, we first define the concept of
EFO
and clarify its utility for explainability purposes. Then, we devise an
XRL
method to explain actions selected by a given black-box policy, such as one learned with a
RL
control algorithm, in terms of
EFOs
.

4.1
Expected Future Outcomes

Since
RL
involves sequential decision-making, each
RL
action should be explained in terms of the sequence of expected outcomes happening after the action is taken. This type of explanation allows us to quantify and interpret the potential effects of actions across different time steps, thus enabling more explainable and interpretable decision-making. To formalize this idea, we introduce the following definition:

Definition 1

(Expected Future Outcome)
.

Given a policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
, a measurable and bounded function
o:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to[-1,1]
, an initial state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and a starting action
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the
EFO
after
h
time steps under the policy
\pi
is:

\displaystyle O_{h}^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\left[o(s_{h},a_{h},s_{h+1})\middle|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a,\pi\right]

(6)

where
a_{i}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,h-1
.

A state-action
GVF
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}_{o}(s,a)=\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{h}O^{\pi}_{h}(s,a)

(7)

Intuitively, the
EFOs
allow for a temporal decomposition of
GVFs
under the policy. In the case of a state-action value function
Q^{\pi}
, this approach effectively restores temporal information about
when
rewards are expected to be collected that is aggregated and lost in
RL
algorithms. For this reason, we call our method Temporal Policy Decomposition (TPD).

Figure 1
:
Conceptual overview of the method. Given the sequential interaction between agent and environment, human observers need to interpret each selected action in terms of its expected impact on the future trajectory. Thus,
TPD
explains the action by generating the
EFOs
for a long-enough prediction horizon.

We propose to generate explanations that include
EFOs
up to a finite prediction horizon, as dealing with an infinite number of
EFOs
is infeasible in practice. Combining the concepts of
GVF

(
Sutton et al., 2011
;
Lin et al., 2021
)
and fixed-horizon value function
(
De Asis et al., 2020
)
, we have the following definition:

Definition 2

(Fixed-Horizon Generalized Value Function)
.

Given a policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
, a prediction horizon
H\in\mathbb{N}
, and a measurable and bounded function
o:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to[-1,1]
, the
Fixed-Horizon Generalized Value Function (FHGVF)
under the policy
\pi
is:

\displaystyle Q_{o,H}^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}o_{h}\middle|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a,\pi\right]

(8)

where
o_{h}=o(s_{h},a_{h},o_{h+1})
and
a_{h}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{h})
for
h=1,\dots,H-1
.

The temporal decomposition with a finite prediction horizon
H\in\mathbb{N}
can thus be expressed as:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}_{o}(s,a)=Q^{\pi}_{o,H}(s,a)+c=\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}O_{h}^{\pi}(s,a)+c

(9)

where
|c|\leq\frac{\gamma^{H}}{1-\gamma}
represents the value part neglected by the explanation. With a sufficiently large prediction horizon, this approach can reveal key insights into the expected future trajectory under the policy
\pi
, starting in state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and taking action
a\in\mathcal{A}
. A conceptual overview of the method is provided in
Fig.

1
.

The concept of
EFO
provides a structured approach to capturing relevant information at a desired level of abstraction, avoiding the complexity of low-level features typically used in deep
RL
. By defining outcome functions that represent quantities of interest, users can focus on the essential, interpretable aspects of the
MDP
and gain insights into the Markov chain induced by steering the
MDP
with the policy
\pi
. Specifically, given a set of interesting outcome functions
[o_{k}]_{k=1}^{K}
, for a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the explanation consists of the sequence of
EFOs

[O^{\pi}_{h,k}(s,a)]_{h=0,k=1}^{H-1,K}
.

Furthermore,
EFOs
can also provide
contrastive
explanations by comparing the
EFOs
of different state-action pairs. For example, given the selected action
a_{1}\in\mathcal{A}
(fact) and a different action
a_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
(foil), the comparison of the respective
EFOs

[O^{\pi}_{h}(s,a_{i})]_{h=0}^{H}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
(e.g., through the difference) answers the contrastive question, “Why did you choose action
a_{1}
over action
a_{2}
?”. Such contrastive explanations are often more intuitive and valuable for human users
(
Miller, 2019
)
. Notably, the ability to generate contrastive explanations is the key motivation for defining
EFOs
as functions of both state and action rather than only state.

4.2
Relevant Classes of Expected Future Outcomes

In the following subsection, we discuss two particularly interesting types of outcomes, namely rewards and events.

4.2.1
Expected Future Rewards

Rewards are natural choices for outcomes, as
RL
control algorithms are designed to maximize expected discounted cumulative rewards. Thus, a simple and effective outcome function is the reward function itself,
r(s,a,s^{\prime})
, which allows for a temporal decomposition of the state-action value function
Q^{\pi}
. This decomposition offers insights into when rewards are collected under a given policy
\pi
.

Inspired by
Juozapaitis et al. (2019)
, when rewards are composed of multiple components, each representing a distinct aspect, we can gain an even deeper understanding. Let
r(s,a,s^{\prime})=g(\mathbf{r}(s,a,s^{\prime}))
be the reward function, with
g:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}
linear and
\mathbf{r}(s,a,s^{\prime})=[r_{k}(s,a,s^{\prime})]_{k=1}^{K}
a vector of reward components. Let
R^{\pi}_{h,k}(s,a)
be the
EFO
corresponding to
r_{k}
, for
k=1,\dots,K
. The state-action value function in
Eq.

1
can then be written as:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma^{h}g(\mathbf{r}(s_{h},a_{h},s_{h+1}))\middle|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a,\pi\right]

\displaystyle=g\left(\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{h}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{r}(s_{h},a_{h},s_{h+1})\middle|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a,\pi\right]\right)

\displaystyle=g\left(\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{h}[R^{\pi}_{h,k}(s,a)]_{k=1}^{K}\right)

(10)

where we have used the linearity of
g
to swap it with expectation and sum. This formulation extends the reward decomposition method proposed by
Juozapaitis et al. (2019)
. In addition to decomposing the value into distinct value components mirroring the reward structure, this approach also introduces a temporal aspect, allowing for a more granular understanding of how reward components are accumulated over time under a given policy.

4.2.2
Probabilities of Future Events

A particularly interesting type of outcome is an event. Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
be the set of all possible transitions
(s,a,s^{\prime})
and let
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be the set of transitions where the event occurs. We can then define the event as an outcome function
e
using an indicator function:

\displaystyle e(s,a,s^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}}(s,a,s^{\prime})

(11)

and the corresponding
EFO

E^{\pi}_{h}
becomes:

\displaystyle E^{\pi}_{h}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[(s_{h},a_{h},s_{h+1})\in\mathcal{E}\middle|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a,\pi\right]

(12)

Intuitively, when the outcome is an event
e(s,a,s^{\prime})
, the corresponding
EFO

E^{\pi}_{h}(s,a)
is the probability that in
h
time steps the event will occur when starting from state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and taking action
a\in\mathcal{A}
. We argue that events and probabilities of future events are particularly interpretable for human users.

Events can also be used to decompose the state-action value function
Q^{\pi}
not only over the time dimension but also over the state-action space, allowing a variant of the reward decomposition discussed in the previous section. Let
x=(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}
be a transition. Consider a complete set of events
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{K}\}
(i.e., exhaustive and mutually exclusive), with corresponding subsets
\{\mathcal{E}_{k}:\mathcal{E}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{X},k=1,\dots,K\}
, and such that each event is associated to a deterministic reward:

\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{E}_{k}=\mathcal{X}

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}\cap\mathcal{E}_{j}=\emptyset,\;\forall i\neq j,1\leq i,j\leq K

(14)

\displaystyle r(x_{i})=r(x_{j}),\;\forall x_{i},x_{j}\in\mathcal{E}_{k},\;k=1,\dots,K

(15)

Let
R^{\pi}_{h}
and
E^{\pi}_{h,k}
be the
EFOs
corresponding to the reward
r
and the events
e_{k}
, for
k=1,\dots,K
, respectively. The following relationship holds:

\displaystyle R^{\pi}_{h}(s,a)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}E_{h,k}^{\pi}(s,a)

(16)

and the state-action value function in
Eq.

1
can be re-written as:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{h}R^{\pi}_{h}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{h}E^{\pi}_{h,k}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}\mu_{k}^{\pi}(s)

(17)

where
\mu_{k}^{\pi}(s)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}E_{t,k}^{\pi}(s,a)
is the unnormalized discounted occupancy over the event
\mathcal{E}_{k}
under
\pi
. An advantage of this value decomposition using a complete set of events is that, once the probability of future events for the events are learned, it is possible to reconstruct also the expected future rewards as well as their composition across the state-action space.

Note that, by defining reward components as
r_{k}(s,a,s^{\prime})=r(s,a,s^{\prime})\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{k}}(s,a,s^{\prime})
and the reward as
r(s,a,s^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}(s,a,s^{\prime})
, we find that the reward decomposition across the state-action space is a special case of the previous section. Nevertheless, since we suggest that probabilities of future events are particularly easy to interpret for human users, learning
EFOs
from events and reconstructing expected future rewards from them is valuable.

4.3
Learning Expected Future Outcomes

Generating contrastive explanations requires learning to predict
EFOs

[O^{\pi}_{h}(s,a)]_{h=0}^{H-1}
for all actions
a\in\mathcal{A}
, not just for the actions selected by the target policy
\pi
, represented as
[O^{\pi}_{h}(s,a)]_{h=0}^{H-1}
. This requirement raises the need for an off-policy algorithm capable of utilizing data collected under an explorative behavioral policy different from the target policy that needs to be explained.

We propose a method that meets the off-policy requirement. For a given outcome
o
, a policy
\pi
, and a prediction horizon
H
, we can write the following recursive relationship:

\displaystyle Q_{o,h}^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle=Q_{o,h-1}^{\pi}(s,a)+\gamma^{h}O^{\pi}_{h}(s,a)

(18)

with the initialization
Q_{o,-1}^{\pi}(s,a)=0
for all state-action pairs. This formulation allows the problem of learning the
EFOs

[O_{h}^{\pi}(s,a)]_{h=0}^{H-1}
to be reframed as the task of learning
FHGVFs

[Q_{o,h}(s,a)]_{h=0}^{H-1}
. Once the
FHGVFs
are learned, we can solve the following set of linear equations to find the
EFOs
:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
1&\gamma&0&\cdots&0\\
1&\gamma&\gamma^{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\gamma&\gamma^{2}&\cdots&\gamma^{H-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}O_{0}(s,a)\\
O_{1}(s,a)\\
O_{2}(s,a)\\
\vdots\\
O_{H-1}(s,a)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{o,0}^{\pi}(s,a)\\
Q_{o,1}^{\pi}(s,a)\\
Q_{o,2}^{\pi}(s,a)\\
\vdots\\
Q_{o,H-1}^{\pi}(s,a)\end{bmatrix}

(19)

where
\gamma\in(0,1]
is the discount factor used while learning
Q^{\pi}_{o,h}
. We refer to solving this set of linear equations as
FHGVF
decomposition.

For learning
FHGVFs
, we utilize
FHTD
learning, devised by
De Asis et al. (2020)
and summarized in
Section

3
, under the assumption that the
Q
-value is linearly realizable. Although
FHTD
methods were proposed as a solution to the deadly triad problem
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
)
, we leverage them for explainability purposes as a step of the
TPD
method. Additionally, the convergence result from
(
De Asis et al., 2020
)
depends on an assumption on the discount factor
\gamma
and the features of the true
Q
-value (see
De Asis et al. (2020, Eq. 20)
) that may be hard to verify in practice. In the following theorem, we overcome this limitation and provide a simple version of
FHTD
learning for tabular
MDPs
that does not require any assumption on
\gamma
, nor requires multiple time-scales for learning the different
Q
-values
Q_{1}^{\pi},Q_{2}^{\pi},\dots,Q_{H}^{\pi}
.

Theorem 3
.

Assume that

(i)

the behavioral policies
(\beta_{t})_{t},\beta_{t}:{\cal S}\to\Delta({\cal A})

(s,a)
is visited infinitely often and

(ii)

for every

the sequences

are positive, non-increasing in

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n,h}(s,a)=\infty
,
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n,h}(s,a)^{2}<\infty
.

Then, under these assumptions and using the update in
Eq.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t+1)}(s,a)=Q_{h}^{\pi}(s,a)
almost surely for every

5
Experiments and Results

In this section, we describe the simulation-based experiments conducted to evaluate our method. We begin with an overview of the experimental setup, detailing the
RL
environment and the training of both the policy and the explainers. After that, we present qualitative examples of explanations and provide a quantitative assessment of prediction errors compared to ground truth values.

5.1
Experimental Setup

We adapted the Taxi environment from Gymnasium
(
Towers et al., 2024
)
by incorporating fuel consumption and traffic dynamics. Visual examples of the modified environment states are shown in
Figs.

3(a)
,
3(b)
and
4(a)
. The environment is formulated as the following
MDP
:

•

State Space
: Each state is represented as an integer that encodes the taxi’s position (row and column), its fuel level, and whether the passenger is in the taxi or not. To limit the state space dimension, the destination, gas station, and initial passenger location are fixed to the same corner in all episodes.

•

Action Space
: The taxi can perform the following actions: move in four directions (south, north, east, west), pick up the passenger, drop off the passenger, and refuel at the gas station.

•

Reward Function
: The taxi earns a reward of
+20
for successfully dropping off the passenger at the destination and
+10
for picking up the passenger. A penalty of
-100
is incurred for running out of fuel or performing invalid actions, while a small penalty of
-1
is applied for each movement action.

•

Transition Dynamics
: Each movement action decreases the fuel level by 1 while refueling increases it by 2. The fuel level ranges from
0
to
20
. Movement actions have a
0.1
probability of failing, which leaves the taxi’s position unchanged but still decreases the fuel level, simulating the taxi stuck in traffic. Invalid actions, such as refueling when the taxi is not at the gas station, leave the state unchanged. The episode ends with either success (when the passenger is dropped off at the destination) or failure (when the taxi runs out of fuel).

The addition of fuel consumption and traffic dynamics introduces stochasticity to the environment, making the interpretation of the taxi’s behavior under a policy more challenging. Even with an optimal policy, uncertainty due to traffic conditions makes it difficult for a human observer to anticipate the policy’s behavior. This unpredictability is further amplified when the policy is suboptimal, underscoring the need for explanations.

In this environment, we trained a near-optimal policy using Q-learning with action masking
(
Watkins and Dayan, 1992
;
Sutton and Barto, 2018
)
. Based on this policy, we then trained an explainer for each of the following events:
dropoff
,
pickup
,
refuel
,
failure
,
traffic
, and
move
. These explainers are designed to predict the probabilities of future events, as described in
Section

4.2.2
. The probability of the
terminated
event is derived by exclusion without requiring an additional explainer. Since this set of events is exhaustive, and each event corresponds to a deterministic reward, the assumptions in
Eqs.

13
to
15
are satisfied. Consequently, the expected future reward composition can be reconstructed from the predicted event probabilities, as outlined in
Section

4.2.2
. Detailed information on the training procedure can be found in
Appendix

B
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced Temporal Policy Decomposition (TPD), an
XRL
method designed to clarify
RL
decision-making by explaining actions in terms of their Expected Future Outcomes (EFOs) across time steps. We showed how
GVFs
, including the value functions used for decision-making in
RL
control algorithms, can be decomposed along the temporal dimension to provide insights into when specific outcomes are expected to occur. To achieve this, we leveraged Fixed-Horizon Temporal Difference learning to devise an off-policy approach that efficiently exploits the sequential nature of
MDPs
. Our results demonstrated that
TPD
delivers both accurate and interpretable explanations that clarify the policy strategy for a prediction horizon of interest, building trust among human users. Additionally, we discussed how contrastive explanations in terms of
EFOs
can also serve as a tool to guide fine-tuning of the reward function for better alignment with human expectations.

There are several promising directions for future research. First, experimenting with this method in continuous problems, such as radio network optimization, could validate its performance in more realistic and complex settings. Second, integrating uncertainty estimates into predictions—using techniques like conformal prediction to compute confidence intervals—could be beneficial to inform users about the reliability of
EFO
predictions. Lastly, investigating approaches to predict the full distribution of future outcomes, rather than focusing solely on expected values, could be particularly beneficial in problems where rewards cannot be easily decomposed into discrete events.

In this appendix, we prove
Theorem

3
presented in
Section

4.3
.

Assume that

(i)

the behavioral policies
(\beta_{t})_{t},\beta_{t}:{\cal S}\to\Delta({\cal A})
, with
a_{t}\sim\beta_{t}(\cdot|s_{t})
, ensure that every state-action pair
(s,a)
is visited infinitely often and

(ii)

for every
h
the sequences
\alpha_{t,h}(s,a)
are positive, non-increasing in
t
, satisfying
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n,h}(s,a)=\infty
,
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n,h}(s,a)^{2}<\infty
.

Then, under these assumptions and using the update in
Eq.

5
, we have that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t+1)}(s,a)=Q_{h}^{\pi}(s,a)
almost surely for every
(s,a)
.

Proof.

Denote by
\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(s,a)
the fixed-horizon
Q
-value for horizon
h
at time step
t
in a given state-action pair
(s,a)
. We can rewrite the update rule in
Eq.

5
as:

\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t+1)}(s,a)=\begin{cases}\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(s,a)+\alpha_{t,h}(s,a)\left(r_{t}+\gamma\hat{Q}_{h-1}^{(t)}(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})-\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(s,a)\right),&\text{if }(s,a)=(s_{t},a_{t})\\
\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(s,a),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
a_{t+1}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t+1}),r_{t}\sim q(s_{t},a_{t})
, with
r(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{r\sim q(s,a)}[r]
and
Q_{0}^{t}(s,a)=0
for every
t
and
(s,a)
.

We exploit the structure of the update scheme, that is
Q_{h-1}^{\pi}
does not depend on
Q_{h}^{\pi},Q_{h+1}^{\pi},\dots
to prove the claim by induction.

Proof of convergence for
h=1
.

We first prove that the iteration converges for
h=1
, which is given by

\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t+1)}(s,a)=\begin{cases}\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(s,a)+\alpha_{t,h}\left(r_{t}-\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(s,a)\right),&\text{if }(s,a)=(s_{t},a_{t})\\
\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}(s,a),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The proof is rather standard. Let
\{t_{n}\}
be the sequence of time steps at which the state-action pair
(s_{t},a_{t})=(s,a)
occurs. We define
n
as the index for these occurrences. The update rule at these times becomes:

\hat{Q}_{1}^{(n+1)}(s,a)=\hat{Q}_{1}^{(n)}(s,a)+\alpha_{n,1}(s,a)\left(r_{t_{n}}-\hat{Q}_{1}^{(n)}(s,a)\right),

where
\alpha_{n,1}(s,a)=\alpha_{t_{n},1}(s,a)
. Now, define the error term
M_{n+1}=r_{t_{n}}-r(s,a)
and note that
\mathbb{E}[M_{n+1}]=0
. Hence, the update equation can now be written in the form of a standard stochastic approximation:

\hat{Q}_{1}^{(n+1)}(s,a)=\hat{Q}_{1}^{(n)}(s,a)+\alpha_{n,1}(s,a)\left(g(\hat{Q}_{1}^{(n)}(s,a))+M_{n+1}\right),

where
g(x)=r(s,a)-x
. Clearly
g
is Lipschitz, of constant
1
. Since every state-action pair
(s,a)
is visited infinitely often, the rewards are bounded, and the sequence
\{\alpha_{n,1}(s,a)\}
is positive, non-increasing, and satisfy
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n,1}(s,a)=\infty,\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}[\alpha_{n,1}(s,a)]^{2}<\infty
we have that the sequence
\hat{Q}_{1}^{(n+1)}
converges almost surely
\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{Q}_{1}^{(n)}(s,a)=r(s,a)
by standard stochastic approximation techniques
(
Borkar, 2023
)
.

h

h-1

h

Q

\{t_{n}\}
the sequence of time steps at which the state-action pair
(s_{t},a_{t})=(s,a)
occurs. We define
n

\hat{Q}_{h}^{(n+1)}(s,a)=\hat{Q}_{h}^{(n)}(s,a)+\alpha_{n,h}(s,a)\left(r_{t_{n}}+\hat{Q}_{h-1}^{(n)}(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})-\hat{Q}_{h}^{(n)}(s,a)\right),

where
\alpha_{n,h}(s,a)=\alpha_{t_{n},h}(s,a)

\displaystyle M_{n+1}

\displaystyle=r_{t_{n}}+Q_{h-1}^{\pi}(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})-r(s,a)-\mathbb{E}[Q_{h-1}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|s=s_{t},a=a_{t},\pi],

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=r(s,a)+\mathbb{E}[Q_{h-1}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|s,a,\pi]-x,

\displaystyle E_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\hat{Q}_{h-1}^{(n)}(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})-Q_{h-1}^{\pi}(s_{t+1},a_{t+1}).

Hence, the update rule in

becomes:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{h}^{(n+1)}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\hat{Q}_{h}^{(n)}(s,a)+\alpha_{n,h}(s,a)\left(g\left(\hat{Q}_{h}^{(n)}(s,a)\right)+M_{n+1}+E_{n+1}\right).

Now, observe that: (1)
M_{n+1}
is a martingale difference sequence; (2)
\hat{Q}_{h}^{(t)}
remains bounded; (3)
g(x)
is Lipschitz continuous; (4)
E_{n+1}=o(1)
(bounded), from the induction hypothesis (hence
E_{n+1}\to 0
almost surely). Then, since every state-action pair
(s,a)
is visited infinitely often, and the sequence
\{\alpha_{n,h}(s,a)\}
is positive, non-increasing, and satisfy
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n,h}(s,a)=\infty,\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}[\alpha_{n,h}(s,a)]^{2}<\infty
we have that the sequence
\hat{Q}_{h}^{(n)}
converges almost surely
\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{h}^{n}(s,a)=r(s,a)+\mathbb{E}[Q_{h-1}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|s,a,\pi]
by standard stochastic approximation techniques
(
Kushner and Clark, 1978
;
Borkar, 2023
)
. Regarding the condition
E_{n+1}=o(1)
