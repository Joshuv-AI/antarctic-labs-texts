First report of seropositivity to pathogenic Leptospira in backyard hens: potential poultry health issue and zoonotic risk
Authors: Michelle Carvajal, Lucía Azócar-Aedo, Natasha Barrios, María Gallardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leptospirosis, a bacterial zoonotic disease of worldwide distribution, has not been extensively studied in birds, although leptospiral infection has been described in these animals. In this study, 62 backyard hens were sampled in three suburban areas near the city of Puerto Montt, southern Chile. A panel of eight serogroups and serovars of pathogenic Leptospira were tested using the Microscopic Agglutination Test to determine the seropositivity rate, serogroups, and serovars causing serological reactions, and to quantify antibody titers. A seropositivity rate of 51.61% (32/62) (95% Confidence Interval= 39.17%–64.05%) was estimated, with reactivity detected most frequently for the Sejroe (serovar Hardjo) (34.4%; n = 11), followed by Autumnalis (serovar Autumnalis) (21.9%; n = 7), Pomona (serovar Pomona) (9.4%; n = 3), and Canicola (serovar Canicola) (3.1%; n = 1) serogroups. Of the animals that tested positive, 53.1% reacted to a single serogroup, while 25.0% reacted to four different serogroups; 12.5% reacted to three serogroups, and 9.4% reacted to two serogroups. Coagglutinations were present in 31.3% (n = 10) of the seropositive samples, with Hardjo/Pomona/Tarassovi/Autumnalis (40.0%; n = 4) being the most frequent combination. Antibody titers ranged from 1:100 to 1:3200, with 1:100 being the highest proportion. The high seropositivity rate, presence of anti-Leptospira antibodies, and cross-reactivity among serogroups/serovars in the sampled hens indicate exposure to the bacteria and reveal an unknown potential poultry health issue and a zoonotic risk concern that require awareness given the widespread practice of poultry farming worldwide.

Introduction

In developing countries, backyard poultry production is generally based on extensive systems that require minimal investment. Although it is not usually the main source of income or the principal agricultural activity for poultry farmers, it plays an important role in supporting household income and providing food domestic consumption (Henning et al., 2006; Liao et al., 2009). Moreover, poultry farming has become increasingly popular in industrialized countries and in addition to raising these birds for egg and meat production, owners often keep them as pets (Pohjola et al., 2016).

Backyard poultry keeping has been associated with the potential risk of zoonotic disease transmission to domestic mammals (Whitehead and Roberts, 2014). However, the epidemiology of these diseases remains poorly characterized, largely because of the limited number of available studies (Pohjola et al., 2016). Avian diseases emerge and re-emerge, and some have the potential to spill over to humans and other animal species, posing a risk for public health and generating economic burden due to animal mortality, as well the need for pharmacological treatments, and environmental management measures (Agunos et al., 2016). Human health problems resulting from contact with birds involve bacterial, viral, fungal, and allergenic agents (Bosert et al., 2013). Among the most important zoonotic diseases transmissible from birds are salmonellosis, campylobacteriosis, and avian influenza (Behravesh, 2018).

Although several zoonotic diseases are currently subject to control measures, knowledge of their epidemiological characteristics, including frequency and distribution, remains limited (Rahman et al., 2020). Leptospirosis, one of the most important zoonoses worldwide, is caused by spirochete bacteria of the genus Leptospira (Adler and De la Peña Moctezuma, 2010; Costa et al., 2015; Levett, 2001) and has been recognized as a neglected disease (WHO-ILS, 2003; Lim, 2011). Until some time ago, Leptospira species were classified according their pathogenic potential into three groups: i) pathogenic species, ii) saprophytic species, and iii) species with intermediate pathogenicity (Mohamed et al., 2011; Bulach and Adler, 2018; Samrot et al., 2021), however, they are currently divided into two clades called P and S, which are further subdivided into four subclades: P1 (subdivided into P11, or high-virulence strains, and P12, or low-virulence strains), P2, S1, and S2. The P11 group includes L. interrogans, L. kirschneri, L. noguchii, L. santarosai, L. mayottensis, L. borgpetersenii, L. alexanderi, and L. weilii, which are associated with human leptospirosis. P12 has been largely isolated from the environment, excluding L. alstonii (amphibians) and L. tipperaryensis (shrews). P2 comprises the group formerly called "intermediate," while S1 and S2 include saprophytic species (Guglielmini et al., 2019; Vincent et al., 2019; Nieves et al., 2024).

In leptospirosis, maintenance hosts are those animals that act as reservoirs, becoming infected while showing mild or no clinical signs. In these hosts, the bacteria colonize the renal tubules and are subsequently eliminated in the urine (Guerra, 2009). Different animal species are recognized as maintenance hosts for specific serovars, e.g.: cattle are maintenance hosts for serovar Hardjo, pigs for serovar Pomona, horses for serovar Bratislava, and dogs for serovar Canicola (Greene et al., 2008; Aliberti et al., 2022). A recent systematic review reported that Leptospira serovars detected in the largest number of different animal species included Grippotyphosa, Icterohaemorrhagiae, Pomona, Australis, and Ballum, with more frequency of descriptions in animals such as Bos taurus, Rattus rattus, Mus musculus, Sus scrofa, Canis lupus, and Rattus norvegicus (Hagedoorn et al., 2024).

Conversely, incidental hosts typically develop acute disease and may exhibit clinical signs associated with hepatic, renal, or multisystemic involvement (Guerra, 2009). In livestock species such cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, and horses, leptospirosis can cause abortions, infertility, or reproductive problems, leading to important animal and economic losses (Lima et al., 2017). It is also possible for an animal to act as a maintenance host for one Leptospira serovar while being an incidental host for others (Levett, 2004).

Information on the frequency and characteristics of leptospirosis in birds is limited, and their participation in the epidemiology of the disease is still poorly understood (Chalquest, 1957). Faine (1994) described evidence of avian infection in eggs after exposure to Leptospira. In addition, Kenzy (1954) (cited by Chalquest, 1957) demonstrated the presence of agglutination-lysis antibodies for the Pomona serovar in chickens exposed to bovine urine containing approximately 8,000 leptospires per inoculum. In Chile, diagnosis of Magellanic penguins sampled on Magdalena Island using the Microscopic Agglutination Test (MAT) revealed no anti-Leptospira antibodies (Acosta et al., 2019). However, a recent epidemiological study conducted at a wildlife rehabilitation center in southern Chile, reported a 19.51% seroprevalence of anti-pathogenic Leptospira antibodies in wild birds, with serological reactivity in choroy parrots, ibises, and Magellanic penguins (Balcázar et al., 2024). Similarly, in Iran, leptospiral DNA was detected in the fecal and tissue samples of ornamental birds (Enferadi et al., 2024). Together, these findings raise questions about the potential role of birds in the transmission of leptospirosis and whether seropositivity or active infection occurs in backyard poultry. Accordingly, the objectives of the present study were: i) to detect the presence of anti-pathogenic Leptospira antibodies in backyard hens in suburban areas near Puerto Montt, southern Chile, using the MAT and calculate the seropositivity rate; ii) to determine the most frequent serogroups/serovars in seropositive backyard hens; and iii) to quantify antibody titers in positive animals.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is an observational, cross-sectional, descriptive, and quantitative epidemiological study (Hernández et al., 2010).

Study area

Backyard hens (Gallus gallus domesticus) were sampled in southern Chile, in areas near Puerto Montt, at the urban-rural interface of the Llanquihue province in the Los Lagos Region. Sampling sites included Piedra Azul (41° 30′ 24″ S, 72° 47′ 41″ W), Nueva Braunau (41° 19′ 29″ S, 73° 04′ 59″ and Lenca (41° 30′ 00″ S, 72° 30′ 00″ W). These locations were selected because of their proximity to the city of Puerto Montt, easy accessibility, and the willingness of local poultry producers to participate in the study.

Sampling

Sample size was calculated using WinEpi (De Blas et al., 2006). Based on a population of 390,138 backyard poultry in the Los Lagos Region (INE, 2021), an expected prevalence of anti-Leptospira antibodies of 50.0%, a confidence level of 90.0%, and a precision of 11.0%, the estimated sample size was 56 animals. Ultimately, 62 hens were included in the study. The sampling method was non-probabilistic (Dohoo et al., 2003) with a convenience sampling, including individuals easily accessible to the researchers (Dohoo, 2003; Stevenson, 2010). Poultry producers voluntarily agreed to participate in the study due to their interest in detecting anti-Leptospira antibodies in their animals and signed informed consent forms.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Clinically healthy juvenile and adult crossbred Creole hens were sampled. Animals were considered suitable for the study if their physiological and clinical condition was compatible with restraint and blood sample collection. Dehydrated animals and those in which 1% of their live weight was <1 g were also excluded (Balcázar et al., 2024). The clinical condition and suitability of the animals for sampling were determined by a veterinary clinical practitioner. All sampled hens were clinically healthy.

Blood and serum sample collection

Approximately 0.5–1 ml of blood was collected from the brachial vein of each animal. Samples were placed in tubes without anticoagulant, maintained at 4–6 °C for 8–24 h, and subsequently centrifuged to separate the serum. The recovered serum was stored at −20 °C until analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study was reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Ethics Committee of Universidad San Sebastián, Chile (project number: 28-2025; resolution date: November 5, 2025).

MAT

MAT was used to diagnose seropositivity, determine Leptospira serogroups and serovars, and quantify antibody levels, and follows the protocol established by Faine (1994) and WHO-ILS (2003). The following panel of eight serogroups belonging to three pathogenic species of the genus Leptospira was used:- Leptospira interrogans, serogroups: Pomona (serovar Pomona, strain Pomona), Canicola (serovar Canicola, strain Hond Utrech IV), Icterohaemhorragiae (serovar Icterohaemorrhagiae, strain Icterohaemorrhagiae), Autumnalis (serovar Autumnalis, strain Akiyami A), Australis (serovar Bratislava, strain Jez Bratislava).- Leptospira borgpetersenii: Sejroe (serovar Hardjo, strain Ar) and Tarassovi (serovar Tarassovi, strain Perepeletsin).- Leptospira kirschneri, serogroup Grippotyphosa (serovar Grippotyphosa, strain Sag).

Titers of 1:100, 1:200, 1:400, 1:800, 1:1600, and 1:3200 were considered, with titers of 1:100 and above were categorized as seropositivity to pathogenic Leptospira (WHO-ILS, 2003). Samples reacting to more than one serogroup with identical antibody titer levels were classified as coagglutinations (Silva and Riedemann, 2008).

Seropositivity rate for pathogenic Leptospira, frequency of serogroups/serovars, and antibody titers

The seropositivity rate for pathogenic Leptospira was calculated using the formula described by Beaglehole et al. (2003) and Stevenson (2010), and 95% Confidence Intervals (95% CI) were also estimated (Noordhuizen et al., 1997).

The frequency of presentation for different serogroups/serovars and coagglutinations in the seropositive animals was described based on proportions, showing the relative frequency (Loebel et al., 2025).

Considering the antibody titers of the positive hens, these were grouped according to the serogroup/serovar associated with the serological reaction, indicating their absolute frequency (Uribe et al., 2025).

Statistical analyses

Differences between the frequencies of serogroups/serovars, coagglutinations, and seropositivity to different numbers of serogroups/serovars were assessed using the Chi-square test with Yates’ correction (P < 0.05) (Hazra and Gogtay, 2016) with Epi Info version 6.04.

RStudio version 4.4.2 (R Core Tam, 2025) with the ggplot2 package (Wickham, 2016) was employed to graph the results.

Results

Seropositivity rate for pathogenic Leptospira

Of the 62 hens sampled, 32 tested positives for anti-pathogenic Leptospira antibodies, yielding a seropositivity rate of 51.61% (95% CI= 39.17%–64.05%).

Most frequent serogroups and serovars

Among the eight serogroups and serovars evaluated by MAT, seropositivity was detected in the following four, presented in descending order of frequency: Sejroe serogroup (serovar Hardjo) (34.4%; n = 11), Autumnalis serogroup (serovar Autumnalis) (21.9%; n = 7), Pomona serogroup (serovar Pomona) (9.4%; n = 3), and Canicola serogroup (serovar Canicola) (3.1%; n = 3). Discussion

An epidemiological study conducted at a wildlife rehabilitation center in southern Chile reported a seroprevalence of anti-Leptospira antibodies of 19.51% in wild birds, with seropositivity to the Tarassovi and Grippotyphosa serogroups detected in choroy parrots, ibises, and Magellanic penguins with antibody titers ranging from 1:100 to 1:1600 (Balcázar et al., 2024). These findings contrast with those of the present study, in which a substantially higher seropositivity rate was observed in backyard hens (51.61%; 95% CI= 39.17–64.05). Epidemiological studies on leptospirosis in Chile have been conducted in a wide range of animal species, including domestic dogs and cats, ruminants, horses, pigs, and wildlife. Although different diagnostic approaches have been used, serological testing by MAT remains the most commonly applied method, with prevalence and frequencies estimates varying according to host and geographic area (Azócar-Aedo, 2023). In the Los Lagos Region, recent studies have reported prevalence rates of 5.8% in domestic cats (Carvajal, 2024), 36.5% in domestic dogs (Azócar-Aedo et al., 2025), 68.6% in horses (Moreno et al., 2024), and frequencies of 85.0% in cattle, 66.7% in sheep, and 25.0% in goats (Uribe et al., 2025). These findings suggest that seropositivity and the presence of anti-pathogenic Leptospira antibodies are common in the area, underscoring the need for ongoing scientific research, including literature reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and epidemiological studies with different designs to maintain updated the knowledge of the disease status (Azócar-Aedo, 2023). Although leptospirosis is a notifiable disease in humans in Chile (Minsal, 2020), it is not currently listed as notifiable in animals. However, this regulation status may change as new evidence becomes available (Azócar-Aedo, 2023). The existing epidemiological information on leptospirosis in humans is limited to the semi-annual or annual reports of the Ministry of Health, which document cases of the disease across different regions of the country (Azócar-Aedo, 2023). Reported incidence remains below 1 case per 100,000 inhabitants, and leptospirosis is primarily classified as an occupational disease affecting agricultural workers, including veterinarians, slaughterhouse personnel and, butchers, as well miners, and laboratory personnel (Minsal, 2021).

In Chile, backyard poultry production systems typically involve flocks of up to 100 birds managed under low-input, low-output conditions. Under these systems, birds primarily forage for food, with only occasional supplementation of food scraps and grains (Hamilton-West et al., 2012). According to Conan et al. (2012), poultry raised in extensive production systems are in close contact with humans, domestic animals, and other birds or wild animals, consequently deficient or nonexistent disease control strategies and inadequate production and health management practices could result in high morbidity levels and the potential for the presentation of infectious diseases (Conan et al., 2012). In a study conducted in central Chile, only 20.0% of backyard poultry producers reported receiving veterinary visits, suggesting that husbandry and maintenance conditions may play an important role in disease transmission and spread (Hamilton-West et al., 2012).

Pathogenic Leptospira has the ability to colonize the proximal renal tubules of maintenance hosts, and to a lesser extent, incidental hosts, leading to bacterial excretion in the urine (Stone et al., 2022). Transmission occurs mainly through two exposure routes: i) direct contact with an infected animal or ii) indirect contact with soil and water contaminated with urine (Haake and Levett, 2015). In addition, Leptospira can survive for weeks to months in moist environments, such as water, soil and may form biofilms, which supports their survival outside the host (Ko et al., 2009). The high seropositivity rate observed in the birds sampled in this study indicates exposure to pathogenic Leptospira, likely from environmental or animal sources, and raises concerns about the susceptibility of these animals to the disease. In this context, Torten et al. (1965) reported that young birds may become infected, while adults produce agglutinins, noting that anti-Leptospira antibodies usually appear in significant concentrations in the blood but disappear rapidly. Bernkopf (1948) showed that domestic chickens were susceptible to experimental infection with Leptospira spp., but this infection was asymptomatic. In contrast, Chalquest (1957) investigated the potential fecal transmission of Leptospira from birds seropositive for serovar Pomona by intraperitoneal inoculating two-day-old with diluted fecal samples and demonstrated no evidence of infection. However, a recent study in Iran detected leptospiral DNA in fecal and tissue samples in 16.52% of ornamental birds (Enferadi et al., 2024). These findings highlight the need for further epidemiological studies for antibody detection in birds, together with the use of complementary molecular diagnostic methods, such as Polymerase Chain Reaction for the potential detection of the bacteria (WHO-ILS, 2003; OMSA, 2021).

The MAT uses live Leptospira antigens with a panel of serogroups prevalent in the geographic area to determine the infecting serogroup and antibody titers. It is the most widely used diagnostic method worldwide and is considered the serological “gold standard” due to its specificity in detecting the serogroup involved in seropositivity (WHO-ILS, 2003). In the hens sampled in this study, seropositivity was detected against for four different serogroups- most frequently the Sejroe serogroup (serovar Hardjo), followed by Autumnalis (serovar Autumnalis), Pomona (serogroup Pomona), and Canicola (serovar Canicola). Exposure to mammalian hosts could indicate the possible origin of the serological reactivity, specifically cattle (serovar Hardjo), mice (serovar Autumnalis), cattle, pigs, wildlife (serovar Pomona), and domestic dogs (serovar Canicola) (Greene et al., 2008). However, in leptospirosis, a single individual can harbor different serovars, serving as a maintenance host for one serovar, while acting as an incidental host for others, either individually or concurrently. This may increase the probability of bacteria exposure, leading to antibody development or a successful infection (Levett, 2004). Nevertheless, the species considered the most important sources of infection are small mammals, particularly wild and peridomestic rodents (rats and mice), insectivorous mammals, and domestic animals (WHO-ILS, 2003). In the context of climate change, urbanization, and increasing human-animal interaction, the threat of leptospirosis and other zoonotic diseases will continue to emerge (Bilung et al., 2026).

The antibody titers detected in the sampled hens ranged from 1:100 to 1:3200, with a predominance of titers of 1:100. This contrasts with the results reported by Balcázar et al. (2024) in wild birds in southern Chile, where the most frequent titers were 1:200 and 1:400. In leptospirosis, titers from 1:100 to 1:200 are considered indicative of past exposure to the agent (CDC, 1997; Musso and La Scola, 2013), while titers equal to or greater than 1:400 indicate probable active or recent infections (Faine, 1994; Musso and La Scola, 2013). However, as mentioned earlier, some authors have noted antibodies with high titers in adult birds, which then decline rapidly (Torten et al., 1965). Further research is required to determine the precise connotation of anti-Leptospira antibody levels in birds. Ideally, studies should utilize paired serum samples, as a four-fold rise in titer between acute and convalescent specimens can confirm a diagnosis of leptospirosis; however, obtaining paired samples at appropriate time points under field conditions is often very difficult (Goris et al., 2012).

Seropositivity for more than one serogroup and coagglutinations were frequently observed in the seropositive birds. Antibodies in serum may cross-react with different serovars in the MAT as a paradoxical reaction, particularly in specimens collected during acute infections. This cross-reactivity may be related to the detection of both IgM and IgG antibodies by MAT, as well as to the presence of shared antigens among leptospires; as the immune response matures, the antibody cross-reactivity will lessen, and the test will become relatively serogroup-specific (Guerra, 2009). Serology offers several advantages in terms of the duration of positivity and the potential to learn from antibody titer kinetics; MAT captures information on past infections as well as acute and convalescent cases (Mummah et al., 2024).

The results of this study reveal an unrecognized potential health issue for birds and a potential zoonotic risk in suburban areas of southern Chile that warrants attention. Veterinary and public health professionals should maintain a high index of suspicion for leptospirosis (Sykes et al., 2022), not only in poultry but also in other avian species. According to Varela et al. (2022), certain practices among backyard poultry producer practices- including allowing birds to roam freely inside the house, cleaning poultry supplies indoors, wearing the same shoes or clothing when handling the birds or their coops, and inadequate or absent handwashing after handling birds or potentially contaminated supplies- increase the risk of disease transmission. The health of backyard poultry may be improved through basic measures involving appropriate husbandry, biosecurity, hygiene and preventive medicine, particularly through the implementation of flock-focused health plans (Whitehead and Roberts, 2014). In the case of leptospirosis, is described that early detection, risk assessment, provider awareness, and supportive care are relevant measures (Limothai et al., 2026).

Further studies are needed to determine the prevalence and distribution of Leptospira infection, with particular emphasis on strengthening epidemiological surveillance, as differences have been observed in the presentation of positivity at the urban-rural interface (Bradley and Lockaby, 2023). A better understanding of the epidemiological characteristics of the disease in specific regions will help researchers to elucidate the factors associated with the persistence and transmission of the bacteria and research on these trends along the urban-rural gradient is likely to reveal opportunities for more precise and effective management and prevention strategies (Freudenberg et al., 2011). In this regard, the One Health approach provides a relevant framework by integrating human, animal, and environmental health systems to address zoonoses and is increasingly recognized as essential for diseases prevention, as well as for environmental management and the improvement of public health (Dunga et al., 2025). Priority actions may include expanding genomic surveillance to capture the full diversity of Leptospira spp., improving the accessibility of diagnostic tools and deciphering virulence mechanisms with molecular methods (Bilung et al., 2026).

The possible limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, and we encourage the use of direct diagnostic tests for the detection of the agent in future studies, however, this constitute the first report of anti-Leptospira antibodies in backyard hens. We conclude that the high seropositivity rate, the presence of anti-Leptospira antibodies, and cross-reactivity between serogroups/serovars in the sampled hens indicate exposure to the bacteria and highlight a potential concern for poultry health, public health and a zoonotic risk, also opening a relevant line of research in avian medicine. These findings warrant increased awareness, given that poultry farming is a common practice worldwide, and human contact with these animals is frequent. Further epidemiological studies are needed, ideally incorporating molecular diagnostic methods to complement serological testing, as well as to clarify the clinical consequences of antibodies detection in birds and the potential role of these animals in the epidemiology of leptospirosis.
