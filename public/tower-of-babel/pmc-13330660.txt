The Effect of Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists on Pain Management in Chronic Pancreatitis: A Real-World Cohort Study
Authors: Arkadeep Dhali, Rick Maity, Fayaz Khan, Abdul Rafae Faisal, Jyotirmoy Biswas, Adnan Bhat, Sushobhon Ghosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsChronic pancreatitis (CP) is a debilitating inflammatory condition characterized by irreversible glandular destruction and intractable pain. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties in preclinical models, yet their impact on clinical outcomes in CP remains unexplored.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, adult patients (≥18 years) with CP were identified from the TriNetX US Collaborative Network. Patients were stratified into the following two cohorts: those prescribed GLP-1 RAs and a control group without such prescriptions. 1:1 propensity score matching was performed to balance cohorts for age, sex, race, and key comorbidities. Primary outcomes were assessed over a 3-year observation period. Hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis.ResultsAfter matching, 10,625 patients were included in each cohort (mean age 59.6 ± 13.1 years; 53.7% female). GLP-1 RA users had a 41% lower hazard of initiating chronic opioids (HR 0.59, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.52–0.67; P < .001) and a 42% lower hazard of developing opioid use disorder (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.48–0.69; P < .001). GLP-1 RA therapy was also associated with a marked reduction in the risk of requiring celiac plexus block (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.34–0.78; P = .001) and endoscopic pancreatic interventions (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.41–0.56; P < .001). Furthermore, the risk of undergoing major pancreatic surgery was significantly lower in the GLP-1 RA group (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.33–0.59; P < .001).ConclusionGLP-1 RA use was associated with a substantial reduction in new opioid prescriptions and invasive pancreatic interventions in CP. These findings suggest that GLP-1 RAs may be helpful in symptom control and multidisciplinary management of selected cases.

Introduction

Chronic pancreatitis (CP) is a progressive inflammatory disorder characterized by irreversible morphological destruction, fibrosis, and debilitating abdominal pain.1 The management of pain in CP remains a significant clinical challenge. Current clinical guidelines, including those from the American College of Gastroenterology and United European Gastroenterology, often necessitate a step-up approach to opioids when nonnarcotic analgesics fail.2,3 Consequently, opioid dependence is highly prevalent, with up to 50% of patients requiring chronic opioid therapy, leading to tolerance, opioid-induced hyperalgesia, and profound reductions in quality of life.4,5 When pharmacological management proves inadequate, patients frequently undergo invasive interventions, such as celiac plexus blocks, endoscopic decompression, or major resection, which carry significant morbidity and variable long-term efficacy.6 Thus, there is an urgent unmet need for disease-modifying therapies that can simultaneously address pain, inflammation, and the fibrotic drive of the disease.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have revolutionized the management of type 2 diabetes and obesity. Beyond their metabolic effects, emerging basic science and clinical data suggest that GLP-1 RAs possess potent anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties.7 Preclinical studies indicate that GLP-1 receptors are expressed on microglia in the spinal dorsal horn and in the mesolimbic reward system, where their activation may modulate nociception and substance craving.8,9 Furthermore, GLP-1 signaling has been implicated in the regulation of pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs), the key drivers of pancreatic fibrosis.10 Despite these promising mechanistic links, the clinical impact of GLP-1 RAs on the trajectory of CP remains largely unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the association between GLP-1 RA use and the risk of chronic opioid use, opioid use disorder (OUD), and invasive pancreatic interventions in a large, real-world cohort of patients with CP.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective cohort study utilized the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, with analysis performed on December 2, 2025. The study population comprised adults (aged ≥18 years) with CP, identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes for alcohol-induced (K86.0) or other CP (K86.1). This study adhered to the STROBE guidelines.11

Cohorts and Index Event

Patients were stratified into two cohorts based on GLP-1 RA exposure. The GLP-1 RA cohort (Cohort 1) included patients prescribed dulaglutide (RxNorm: 1551291), semaglutide (RxNorm: 1991302), or tirzepatide (RxNorm: 2601723). The control cohort (Cohort 2) included patients without GLP-1 RA prescriptions; those with prior records of GLP-1 analogs (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical code: A10BJ) or tirzepatide were excluded. The index event was the first occurrence meeting the cohort criteria. Index events >20 years prior to analysis were excluded, although no patients were removed based on this criterion.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were assessed 1 to 1095 days (3 years) postindex. Incident events were defined using specific codes, excluding patients with prior outcome history as follows:•Chronic opioid prescriptions: New prescriptions for morphine, oxycodone, methadone, buprenorphine, oxymorphone, fentanyl, tapentadol, hydromorphone, or hydrocodone.•Opioid use disorder: ICD-10-CM F11.1 (abuse), F11.2 (dependence), or F11.9 (unspecified use).•Celiac plexus block/neurolysis: Current Procedural Terminology (CPT): 64530 or 64680.•Endoscopic pancreatic interventions: Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography with stent placement/exchange, balloon dilation, sphincterotomy, or stone removal (CPT: 43274, 43276, 43277, 43273, 43270).•Major pancreatic surgery: Distal pancreatectomy, Puestow procedure, or Whipple procedure (CPT: 48140, 48145, 48548, 48150, 48152, 48153, 48154).

Statistical Analysis

To account for baseline differences, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed, balancing age, sex, race, and comorbidities (hypertension, chronic kidney disease, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, lipid disorders, tobacco use, alcohol-related disorders, type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity, sleep apnea). Outcomes were analyzed using risk, risk difference, and risk ratio. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with the log-rank test estimated outcome probability, calculating hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Results

Chronic Opioid Prescriptions

Among 8888 patients in the GLP-1 RA cohort and 8710 in the control cohort at risk for chronic opioid initiation, 349 (3.9%) and 638 (7.3%) received chronic opioid prescriptions, respectively, during the 3-year observation period. The adjusted risk difference was −3.4% (95% CI: −4.1% to −2.7%; P < .001), with a risk ratio of 0.536 (95% CI: 0.472–0.609). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated a significant protective effect of GLP-1 RA exposure (log-rank test: χ2 = 64.699, P < .001). The probability of remaining free from chronic opioid prescriptions at 3 years was 93.70% in the GLP-1 RA cohort vs 90.44% in the control cohort (HR: 0.588; 95% CI: 0.516 to 0.671; P < .001), representing a 41.2% reduction in hazard (Figure 2). Opioid Use Disorder

New diagnoses of OUD developed in 176 (1.8%) of 9707 at-risk patients in the GLP-1 RA cohort compared to 357 (3.6%) of 9954 at-risk controls (risk difference: −1.8%; 95% CI: −2.2% to −1.3%; P < .001; risk ratio: 0.506; 95% CI: 0.423–0.604). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significant differences in freedom from OUD between cohorts (log-rank test: χ2 = 36.248, P < .001). The 3-year probability of remaining disorder-free was 97.08% for GLP-1 RA users vs 94.82% for controls (HR: 0.577; 95% CI: 0.482–0.692; P < .001), indicating a 42.3% reduction in hazard (Figure 3). The proportionality of hazards assumption was satisfied (P = .397). Nine hundred eighteen patients in the GLP-1 RA cohort and 671 in the control cohort had prior OUD and were excluded.Figure 3Kaplan-Meier curve showing the probability of remaining free of opioid use disorder at 3 years, excluding patients with outcome prior to the time window.

Celiac Plexus Block and Neurolysis

Among 10,571 GLP-1 RA patients and 10,607 control patients at risk, 32 (0.3%) and 68 (0.6%) underwent celiac plexus block or neurolysis, respectively (risk difference: −0.3%; 95% CI: −0.5% to −0.2%; P < .001; risk ratio: 0.472; 95% CI: 0.310–0.718). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significantly superior outcomes in GLP-1 RA users (log-rank test: χ2 = 10.175; P = .001). The probability of avoiding celiac plexus intervention at 3 years was 99.56% for GLP-1 RA patients vs 99.18% for controls (HR: 0.511; 95% CI: 0.335–0.778; P = .001), representing a 48.9% reduction in hazard (Figure 4). The proportionality assumption was satisfied (P = .428). Fifty-four patients in the GLP-1 RA cohort and 18 in the control cohort had prior procedures and were excluded.Figure 4Kaplan-Meier curve showing the probability of avoiding celiac plexus intervention at 3 years, excluding patients with outcome prior to the time window.

Endoscopic Interventions for Pancreatic Disease

Endoscopic therapeutic interventions (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography with stent placement, stent exchange, balloon dilation, or sphincterotomy) were required in 229 (2.3%) of 10,040 at-risk GLP-1 RA patients vs 507 (5.0%) of 10,057 controls (risk difference: −2.8%; 95% CI: −3.3% to −2.2%; P < .001; risk ratio: 0.452; 95% CI: 0.388–0.528). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed marked differences in freedom from endoscopic intervention (log-rank test: χ2 = 91.490; P < .001), the highest statistical significance among all primary outcomes. The 3-year probability of avoiding endoscopic intervention was 96.92% for GLP-1 RA patients vs 93.98% for controls (HR: 0.475; 95% CI: 0.406–0.555; P < .001), representing a 52.5% reduction in hazard (Figure 5). The proportionality assumption was borderline (P = .053). Major Pancreatic Surgery

Major surgical interventions (distal pancreatectomy, Puestow procedure, or Whipple procedure) were performed in 67 (0.6%) of 10,411 at-risk GLP-1 RA patients compared to 156 (1.5%) of 10,364 controls (risk difference: −0.9%; 95% CI: −1.1% to −0.6%; P < .001; risk ratio: 0.428; 95% CI: 0.322–0.569). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a significant protective effect of GLP-1 RA therapy (log-rank test: χ2 = 32.873; P < .001). The probability of avoiding major surgery at 3 years was 99.17% for GLP-1 RA patients vs 98.24% for controls (HR: 0.443; 95% CI: 0.332–0.589; P < .001), indicating a 55.7% reduction in hazard (Figure 6). The proportionality assumption was satisfied (P = .062). Discussion

This retrospective cohort study provides large-scale real-world evidence suggesting that GLP-1 RAs are associated with a modest reduction in clinical outcomes and treatment requirements for patients with CP. In a propensity-matched analysis of 21,250 patients, GLP-1 RA therapy was associated with a 41.2% reduction in the hazard of initiating chronic opioids and a 42.3% reduction in the hazard of developing OUD. Furthermore, the therapy was linked to a significantly lower requirement for invasive management, including reductions in celiac plexus blocks (−0.3% risk difference), endoscopic interventions (−2.8% risk difference), and major pancreatic surgery (−0.9% risk difference). These findings align with a growing body of literature suggesting that GLP-1 RAs exert pleiotropic effects on pain processing, reward pathways, and tissue inflammation.

The reduction in chronic opioid use observed in our cohort supports the hypothesis that GLP-1 RAs modulate central pain processing. While peripheral inflammation is a key driver of CP pain, central sensitization and microglial activation in the spinal cord play a critical role in maintaining the chronic pain state.12 Ma et al13 demonstrated in a neuropathic pain model that GLP-1 receptors are expressed on spinal microglia and that their activation by exenatide inhibits the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor alpha, interleukin-1 beta) while stimulating the release of the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10 and β-endorphin. This ‘microglial switch’ from a pro-inflammatory M1 to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype attenuates synaptic plasticity associated with chronic pain. Additionally, Go et al14 recently identified that GLP-1 and its metabolites can directly inhibit transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 channels in sensory neurons, providing a peripheral mechanism for reducing visceral nociception distinct from their metabolic effects. By dampening both peripheral nociceptive input and central sensitization, GLP-1 RAs may reduce the ‘pain drive’ that necessitates opioid escalation in CP patients.

The significant reduction in incident OUD (−1.8% absolute risk difference) and the decreased need for opioids may also reflect the action of GLP-1 RAs on the brain's reward system. The mesolimbic dopamine system, particularly the ventral tegmental area and nucleus accumbens, governs substance craving. Merkel et al15 demonstrated that endogenous GLP-1 circuits synapse directly onto GABAergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area; activation of these circuits inhibits dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens, thereby blunting the reinforcing effects of substances. This mechanism has been validated clinically in recent trials. A large Swedish cohort study by identifying over 80,000 individuals with alcohol use disorder found that semaglutide use was associated with a marked reduction in alcohol-related hospitalizations.16 Similarly, a randomized clinical trial by Hendershot et al17 confirmed that semaglutide significantly reduced alcohol consumption and craving in patients with alcohol use disorder. Given that alcohol is the primary etiological factor for CP, the dual benefit of reducing alcohol craving and modulating opioid reward offers a compelling explanation for the improved outcomes in our study.

Beyond symptom control, the reduction in structural interventions (endoscopy and surgery) suggests a potential disease-modifying effect on pancreatic fibrosis. PSCs are the effector cells of fibrosis in CP. Kim et al18 reported that the GLP-1 agonist exendin-4 suppresses the proliferation of PSCs and inhibits the production of reactive oxygen species and collagen in response to high glucose and angiotensin II. By inhibiting PSC activation, GLP-1 RAs may retard the progression of perilobular fibrosis that leads to ductal strictures and hypertension. However, the data on this are complex; earlier work by Gier et al19 and Yang et al20 in rodent models suggested that GLP-1 activation might induce proliferation of pancreatic duct glands or PSCs. Our clinical data, showing a 55% reduction in the hazard of major surgery and a 52% reduction in endoscopic interventions, argue against a deleterious proliferative effect in the human CP population and support a net anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic benefit.

The safety of GLP-1 RAs in patients with a history of pancreatitis has been a subject of historical debate. Early case reports raised concerns that these agents might induce pancreatitis. However, recent rigorous meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have largely alleviated these fears. A 2020 meta-analysis by Cao et al21 and a 2022 analysis by Zhang et al22 found no significant association between GLP-1 RA use and the incidence of pancreatitis or pancreatic cancer in patients with type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, a 2025 meta-analysis stratified by background medication use confirmed that while a slight statistical signal exists, it is likely confounded by other risk factors.23 Kodali et al24 specifically examined patients with a prior history of pancreatitis, a group often excluded from RCTs and found that the rate of recurrent pancreatitis in GLP-1 users was comparable to the background rate, suggesting they are safe to use in this population. Our study adds to this evidence base, showing that in a high-risk CP cohort, the need for interventions related to disease progression was actually reduced, not increased.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. First, its retrospective, observational design using the TriNetX network means that all variables were derived from routinely collected electronic health records and ICD-10 coding. As a result, misclassification of diagnoses and underascertainment of subjective symptoms such as pain or substance craving is possible, and we may not fully capture all relevant episodes of disease activity. Similarly, procedural and medication data reflect recorded orders rather than actual use at the bedside. Second, although we used PSM on multiple demographic and clinical covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded. In particular, we were neither able to robustly characterize CP severity, etiology, or underlying genetic mutations (eg, PRSS1, CFTR) nor could we fully account for lifestyle factors, socioeconomic status, or center-level practice patterns. Because GLP-1 RAs are typically prescribed to selected patients with diabetes and/or obesity, some degree of confounding by indication and ‘healthier user’ selection is possible despite matching. These factors may have influenced both the likelihood of receiving GLP-1 RAs and the intensity of downstream pain and procedural management. Third, our exposure and outcome measures have inherent constraints. We could not reliably assess medication adherence, treatment duration, or exact GLP-1 RA dosing, which may differ between diabetes and obesity indications. Moreover, medication exposure in TriNetX cannot be linked to a specific prescribing indication, limiting our ability to interpret the reason GLP-1 RA therapy was started in this cohort. Our definition of opioid outcomes is based on incident prescriptions rather than direct assessment of long-term, patient-reported chronic opioid use, and procedure rates (eg, endoscopic or surgical interventions) may vary across institutions according to available expertise and local practice philosophy. Finally, while we speculate that reduced pancreatic fibrosis and disease progression could underlie the lower rates of surgery observed in GLP-1 RA users, we lacked histologic data or detailed imaging parameters to directly confirm structural tissue changes. Prospective studies with richer phenotyping and mechanistic end points will be essential to validate and extend these hypothesis-generating findings.

Conclusion

This cohort study provides real-world evidence that GLP-1 RAs were associated with lower rates of new opioid prescriptions and invasive pancreatic interventions compared with matched controls. GLP-1 RA use was associated with a 41% reduction in chronic opioid initiation, 42% reduction in OUD, and 49% to 56% reductions in invasive interventions. These findings suggest that GLP-1 RAs may have a role in the multidisciplinary management of selected patients with CP and concomitant diabetes or obesity. Prospective RCTs are needed to validate these observations and optimize clinical application.
