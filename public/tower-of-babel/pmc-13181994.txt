Acute effects of French contrast and post-activation performance enhancement on throwing, jump performance, and training load in handball players
Authors: Nebahat Eler, Serdar Eler, Aydın Şentürk, Celal Gençoğlu, Tevfik Cem Akalın, Selim Karaağaçlı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the acute effects of the Band-French Contrast Method (Band-FCM) and Band-Post-Activation Performance Enhancement (Band-PAPE) protocols on throwing velocity, countermovement jump (CMJ) height, and internal training load (RPE) in elite youth male handball players. A total of 23 athletes from the U18 national team voluntarily participated in the study. The experimental design included four sessions: an initial familiarization and band-1RM testing day, followed by three intervention sessions arranged in a Latin square design. The Band-PAPE protocol consisted of two sets of shoulder press actions performed at 90% band-1RM, incorporating 3-second isometric holds followed immediately by explosive concentric movements, followed by 4 min of passive recovery before performance testing. The Band-FCM protocol comprised three sets of upper-body isometric, dynamic, and plyometric exercises applied in a sequential structure. Throwing velocity (measured via radar), CMJ height (assessed using Optojump), and RPE (CR-10 scale) were recorded before and 2–4 min after the intervention. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Results showed that the Band-PAPE protocol significantly increased throwing velocity (+ 5.6 km·h⁻¹) without elevating RPE. Both protocols induced moderate improvements in CMJ height; however, only Band-FCM significantly increased RPE. In conclusion, Band-PAPE may be suitable for pre-competition and in-game use (e.g., pre-match warm-up, halftime, and preparing substitute players) due to its performance benefits with minimal increase in internal load, whereas Band-FCM appears more appropriate as a training-oriented strategy for longer-term power development rather than acute performance enhancement.Keywords: Throwing velocity, Countermovement jump, Internal load, FCM, PAPE, Youth handball, Band resistance

Introduction

Handball is characterized as a high-intensity intermittent team sport requiring players to execute repeated explosive actions including throwing, jumping, and rapid directional changes throughout match play [1]. The sport’s competitive demands necessitate exceptional neuromuscular capabilities, with throwing velocity and vertical jump performance serving as fundamental determinants of on-court success [2–4]. Specifically, enhanced throwing velocity reduces goalkeeper reaction time and increases scoring probability, while superior vertical jump ability facilitates critical game actions including defensive rebounds, shot blocking, and elevated shooting positions [3, 4]. Given the direct relationship between these explosive performance parameters and competitive outcomes, optimizing neuromuscular function represents a primary objective for handball practitioners and researchers alike.

Contemporary sports science literature has identified numerous strategies for acute neuromuscular performance enhancement, with Post-Activation Performance Enhancement (PAPE) emerging as a particularly promising warm-up intervention [5]. PAPE describes the acute improvement in voluntary muscle performance following a conditioning activity (CA), distinguished from traditional Post-Activation Potentiation (PAP) by its extended duration and reliance on voluntary rather than electrically-stimulated contractions [6]. The underlying mechanisms driving PAPE responses include elevated muscle temperature, enhanced intracellular calcium availability, increased motor unit recruitment, and optimized neuromuscular coordination [5]. These physiological adaptations collectively facilitate transient improvements in explosive strength, power output, and movement velocity [4, 7]. The effectiveness of PAPE interventions is critically dependent on the balance between performance potentiation and residual fatigue, with optimal outcomes achieved when potentiation effects exceed fatigue-induced decrements [8]. This balance is modulated by multiple factors, including conditioning activity intensity and volume, recovery duration, training status, and individual physiological characteristics [9, 10]. Notably, substantial inter-individual variability in PAPE responses has been documented even among elite athletes, emphasizing the importance of individualized protocol design and implementation [11].

Various conditioning modalities have been employed to elicit PAPE responses, with Contrast Training (CT) representing one of the most widely adopted approaches [10]. CT protocols systematically pair high-load resistance exercises with biomechanically similar explosive movements, designed to stimulate the neuromuscular system and optimize stretch-shortening cycle function, thereby enhancing rate of force development [4]. The French Contrast Method (FCM) represents a sophisticated evolution of traditional CT, incorporating four sequential phases: (1) a heavy compound lift, (2) a plyometric movement, (3) a moderate-load compound exercise, and (4) a secondary plyometric task [12]. This structured approach enables comprehensive training across the entire force-velocity spectrum, theoretically maximizing neuromuscular adaptations and performance enhancement potential.

Concurrently, Variable Resistance Training (VRT) utilizing elastic bands has gained recognition as a practical and effective means of inducing PAPE responses [13, 14]. Elastic resistance provides accommodating resistance throughout the range of motion, characterized by increasing load during the concentric phase and enhanced eccentric loading potential. This resistance profile facilitates greater motor unit recruitment, particularly of Type II muscle fibers, and may optimize the force-velocity relationship for explosive movement patterns [5]. Advanced implementations utilizing Functional Electromechanical Dynamometers (FEMD) allow precise control of resistance characteristics, enabling optimal loading parameters to maximize PAPE effects [3].

Despite the theoretical rationale and emerging evidence supporting PAPE interventions, research specifically examining band-assisted protocols in handball remains limited and inconclusive. Existing studies have produced mixed findings regarding the effectiveness of various conditioning activities on handball-specific performance measures. For instance, investigations utilizing heavy-load bench press or eccentric overload with conical pulleys have failed to demonstrate significant improvements in throwing velocity among amateur handball players [7, 15]. Furthermore, while FCM protocols have been investigated in related explosive sports such as 3 × 3 basketball [12], no comprehensive research has examined the acute effects of band-modified FCM and PAPE protocols on key performance indicators in handball populations. This represents a significant knowledge gap, particularly considering the practical advantages of elastic band training including cost-effectiveness, portability, and ease of implementation in various training environments.

The application of PAPE protocols in youth populations presents unique considerations that have received limited research attention. Adolescent athletes (U18) exhibit distinct physiological characteristics including developing neuromuscular systems, varying training experience levels, and potentially different fatigue-recovery profiles compared to adult populations. These factors may significantly influence PAPE response patterns and optimal protocol design, necessitating age-specific investigation and validation.

The present study addresses these critical research gaps by investigating the acute effects of band-modified FCM (Band-FCM) and band-based PAPE (Band-PAPE) protocols on throwing velocity, countermovement jump performance, and perceived exertion in youth handball players. This study aims to provide empirical support for cost-effective, age-specific (U18), field-ready warm-up strategies in youth handball. We hypothesize that both Band-FCM and Band-PAPE protocols will elicit acute improvements in throwing velocity and countermovement jump performance compared to control conditions in youth handball players. Furthermore, we anticipate that these protocols will demonstrate distinct effects on internal load dynamics, with potential differences in RPE responses reflecting varying physiological demands and recovery requirements. Finally, we expect individual response variability will be evident, supporting the need for personalized protocol implementation in youth handball training environments.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-three male athletes (age: 17.4 ± 0.5 years; training experience: 6.8 ± 1.9 years) from the Turkish U18 national handball team voluntarily participated in this study. Participation criteria were: (a) ≥ 3 years of organized handball experience, (b) no musculoskeletal injury in the previous six months, and (c) medical approval from the team doctor. Exclusion criteria included any acute illness, use of performance-enhancing substances, or failure to complete all test protocols. All 23 athletes were enrolled; however, outcome-specific missing data occurred in two athletes (one missing CMJ and RPE in one session, and one missing throwing velocity and RPE in one session). Accordingly, the sample size was n = 23 for descriptive analyses, n = 22 for CMJ and throwing-velocity analyses, and n = 21 for RPE analyses. (Fig. 1).

Participant flow diagram (CONSORT-style)

All procedures were explained in detail to participants and their legal guardians. Written informed consent was obtained from both athletes and guardians prior to participation. The study protocol was approved by the Gazi University Ethics Committee (Approval Code: 2025 − 1342) and was conducted in full accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration regarding human research.

Experimental design

This study employed a randomized, repeated-measures crossover design conducted over four sessions separated by ≥ 48 h to minimize carry-over effects. The experimental timeline is presented in Fig. 2. Session order was counterbalanced using a Latin square design to control for potential order effects.

Study Design

Day 1 (baseline measurements and familiarization)

Anthropometric measurements (height, body mass) were recorded, and individual one-repetition maximum band resistance (band-1RM) was determined using progressive loading with Theraband CLX until volitional failure. The corresponding resistance band color (gray, 32 mm width) was standardized across all participants. Familiarization trials were conducted for all testing equipment including the radar velocity measurement system, CMJ platform, and Borg CR10 rating of perceived exertion (RPE) scale. During Day 1, participants also completed a brief familiarization specifically for maximal isometric shoulder-press efforts, including 2–3 submaximal practice trials (approximately 50–70% perceived effort) to standardize body position, breathing, and maintenance of the target joint angles for the 3-s hold. Day 2 (Control condition): Following a standardized dynamic handball warm-up (8 min), participants completed a 2-minute passive rest period before performance testing. This session served as the baseline control condition without any resistance band intervention.

Day 3 (band-FCM protocol)

After the identical warm-up, athletes performed the Band Functional Complex Movement (Band-FCM) protocol consisting of 4 sets of complex band exercises (total duration: 6–8 min), followed by 4 min of passive recovery before performance testing.

Day 4 (band-PAPE protocol)

Following warm-up, participants completed the Band Post-Activation Potentiation Enhancement (Band-PAPE) protocol consisting of 2 sets of high-intensity band exercises (total duration: 4 min), followed by 4 min of passive recovery before testing. A standardized 4-minute passive recovery period was implemented following the conditioning activities (Band-FCM and Band-PAPE) before performance testing, based on previous research suggesting an optimal potentiation–fatigue balance within this timeframe.

Resistance band protocols

“Individual band-1RM was determined using a standardized elastic band system (Theraband CLX, grey, 32 mm) during an isometric shoulder press task. Testing was performed with a consistent setup across participants and sessions: the band was anchored under both feet, grip position was standardized using the CLX loop handles, and participants maintained an upright posture with a stable stance (feet shoulder-width) and neutral trunk alignment. The target position for the isometric effort was standardized (shoulder press position with fixed joint angles) and verified by the investigator before each attempt; participants were instructed to reach the target position and maintain it without compensatory movement for 3 s.

Participants first completed a standardized warm-up consisting of 2–3 submaximal isometric practice trials at low-to-moderate perceived intensity. Subsequently, single maximal attempts were performed with progressive increases in resistance. Resistance was increased by shortening the effective band length in a standardized stepwise manner (loop-to-loop adjustment), while maintaining identical stance and grip position across attempts. Rest intervals of 90 s passive recovery were provided between maximal attempts to limit fatigue accumulation and to maintain maximal effort quality. A maximum of five maximal attempts was allowed to minimize fatigue.

An attempt was classified as successful only if the athlete maintained the target position for the full 3 s without visible joint displacement or compensatory strategies. Attempts were terminated and recorded as unsuccessful if any of the following occurred: (a) inability to sustain the isometric hold for the full 3 s, (b) marked trunk extension, trunk lean, or loss of neutral posture, (c) loss of prescribed arm alignment or failure to maintain the target joint angles, or (d) stepping, re-positioning, or instability that altered the standardized setup. The highest resistance successfully held for 3 s was recorded as band-1RM and used to prescribe subsequent protocol intensity (e.g., 90% band-1RM). Specifically, the target band intensities used in the experimental protocols (e.g., 50% and 90%) were derived as relative percentages of each athlete’s individually determined isometric band-1RM. Absolute band tension (force output in Newtons) at specific elongations was not directly measured; therefore, loading was standardized within the study using an identical band model and a fixed setup combined with individualized band-1RM–based relative intensities. Although test–retest reliability of band-1RM was not formally assessed in this study, repeatability was enhanced through standardized equipment, positioning, investigator cues, fixed rest intervals, and predefined termination criteria.

Data collection procedures

Data collection was conducted across four consecutive days in the same indoor facility between 07:30 and 10:30 h, under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 1 °C; 45 ± 5% RH). During each testing session, players were evaluated in groups of no more than five. A standardized testing flow, managed by a stopwatch, was initiated by a lead investigator, while two assistant researchers recorded all data. On Day 1, anthropometric measurements (stature and body mass) were obtained with participants barefoot and wearing light clothing, using a fixed stadiometer (Seca 213; ±0.1 cm) and a calibrated digital scale (Tanita BC-418; ±0.1 kg). Chronological age was verified via the Turkish Handball Federation’s licensing database. All protocols utilized a single standardized resistance band (Theraband CLX, grey, 32 mm width). Performance assessments were conducted in the same sequence across all sessions. First, throwing velocity was measured: athletes performed three consecutive overarm throws from a distance of 6 m from the goalkeeper. Ball release speed was recorded using a Doppler radar device (SpeedTrac X™, accuracy ± 0.3 km·h⁻¹) positioned 2 m behind the target at a height of 2 m. The highest velocity value obtained from the three attempts was used for analysis. Next, the countermovement jump (CMJ) test was performed with arms freely swinging. Each player completed two maximal vertical jumps on an OptoJump contact platform, and the mean of the jump heights—calculated from flight time—was recorded. Following each performance task, rating of perceived exertion (RPE) was assessed. Athletes were shown the laminated Turkish version of the Borg CR10 scale and verbally reported their exertion level, which was documented alongside the session code in an Excel data sheet. All equipment was calibrated each morning prior to testing according to manufacturer specifications.

Participants had a mean age of 16.5 ± 0.6 years, a height of 184.9 ± 5.8 cm, and a body mass of 83.4 ± 13.9 kg. Baseline throwing velocity was 76.4 ± 7.7 km·h⁻¹, increasing to 79.2 ± 7.6 km·h⁻¹ following the Band-FCM protocol and to 82.0 ± 5.8 km·h⁻¹ after the Band-PAPE protocol. Mean countermovement jump (CMJ) heights across conditions were 35.1 cm (baseline), 35.5 cm (FCM), and 35.7 cm (Band-PAPE), indicating only minimal changes in vertical jump performance. Perceived exertion (RPE) scores were 4.39 ± 1.08 at baseline, 5.91 ± 1.23 following FCM, and 4.74 ± 0.86 after Band-PAPE. The highest RPE value was observed during the FCM session, indicating a greater internal load associated with this protocol (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SigmaPlot version 11.0 (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and median (interquartile range) for ordinal data. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed variables (throwing velocity and CMJ height), within-protocol pre-post comparisons were conducted using paired-samples t-tests. A repeated-measures ANOVA with a 2 (Time: pre vs. post) × 3 (Protocol: Control, Band-FCM, Band-PAPE) design was used to analyze the interaction effects. When significant main effects or interactions were detected (p < 0.05), Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc tests were applied for pairwise comparisons. For RPE data, which violated normality assumptions, within-protocol changes were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Between-protocol differences were assessed using Friedman tests, followed by Dunn-Bonferroni pairwise comparisons when significant main effects were detected. For parametric data, effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for paired comparisons (trivial: d < 0.20; small: d = 0.20–0.49; medium: d = 0.50–0.79; large: d ≥ 0.80) and partial eta squared (η²p) for ANOVA effects (small: η²p = 0.01–0.05; medium: η²p = 0.06–0.13; large: η²p ≥ 0.14). For non-parametric analyses, effect sizes were reported as r for Wilcoxon tests and Kendall’s W for Friedman tests. The alpha level was set at p < 0.05 for all statistical decisions, with 95% confidence intervals reported where appropriate.

The protocol factor had a large effect on throwing velocity (F = 10.72, η²p = 0.33) and perceived exertion (RPE) (F = 14.73, η²p = 0.41), and a moderate effect on countermovement jump (CMJ) height (F = 4.11, η²p = 0.16). All effects were statistically significant (p ≤ 0.023) (Table 2). These findings indicate that Band-PAPE effectively enhanced throwing performance while maintaining lower RPE levels, whereas Band-FCM induced the highest internal load among the protocols.

Discussion

The present investigation examined the acute effects of two elastic resistance protocols—Band-FCM and Band-PAPE—on performance outcomes and internal load responses in elite youth handball players. The primary findings demonstrate that Band-PAPE significantly enhanced throwing velocity (+ 7%, + 5.6 km·h⁻¹; p < 0.001; d = 1.05), while Band-FCM produced a smaller, non-significant improvement (+ 3%, + 2.8 km·h⁻¹; p = 0.057). Although both protocols elicited statistically significant increases in countermovement jump (CMJ) height (~ 0.5 cm), these changes lacked practical significance. Notably, the protocols demonstrated divergent effects on perceived exertion, with Band-FCM elevating RPE by approximately 1.5 points while Band-PAPE produced no significant change in internal load. These findings provide important insights into the differential effectiveness and practical applications of band-based conditioning protocols in youth handball. Importantly, our individual-response analysis showed substantial variability in throwing-velocity changes, with 43.5% and 56.5% of athletes classified as responders to Band-FCM and Band-PAPE, respectively (≥ 5% threshold). Baseline throwing velocity was inversely associated with relative improvements, suggesting that athletes with lower baseline performance may derive larger percentage benefits; however, these associations should be interpreted as exploratory. Collectively, these findings reinforce the need for individualized implementation and monitoring when applying band-based potentiation strategies in youth handball.

Importantly, the biomechanical specificity of the isometric shoulder press may help explain the superior transfer of Band-PAPE to throwing velocity. The overhead isometric shoulder-press position requires scapulothoracic stabilization and glenohumeral force production at joint angles that are highly relevant to the overhead throw, thereby priming the neuromuscular system under task-specific kinetic-chain constraints. Moreover, the lower overall volume and simpler structure of Band-PAPE likely minimized residual fatigue while preserving a high-specificity stimulus, resulting in a more favorable potentiation–fatigue balance for acute throwing performance. In contrast, the higher-volume and more complex Band-FCM sequence may have increased peripheral and coordinative fatigue demands, attenuating net potentiation despite similar initial activation.

The substantial improvement in throwing velocity following Band-PAPE aligns with established principles of post-activation performance enhancement and supports previous research in handball populations. The observed 7% increase with a large effect size (d = 1.05) represents a practically meaningful enhancement that could translate to a competitive advantage. This finding corroborates the work of Cifuentes-Zapata et al. [3] who reported significant throwingvelocity improvements in elite female handball players following variable-resistance PAPE protocols, emphasizing that two conditioning sets were sufficient to elicit performance benefits.

The effectiveness of our Band-PAPE protocol can be attributed to several key factors. First, the biomechanical specificity of isometric shoulder pressing closely matches the movement patterns and muscle activation sequences involved in handball throwing. In particular, the overhead isometric shoulder-press position requires scapulothoracic stabilization and glenohumeral force production at joint angles that are highly relevant to the late cocking and acceleration phases of the overhead throw. This task likely primes the neuromuscular system within the same kinetic-chain constraints required for high-velocity throwing, thereby enhancing the transfer of potentiation to the sport-specific performance outcome. From an applied perspective, this supports the principle that conditioning activities with closer joint-angle and movement-pattern similarity yield greater acute performance benefits for skill-dominant tasks such as handball throwing. This sport-specific approach contrasts with previous investigations that failed to demonstrate throwing velocity improvements using bench press exercises [7, 15], which lack the overhead movement pattern characteristic of handball shooting. The biomechanical congruence between the conditioning activity and performance task appears critical for optimizing PAPE responses, supporting the principle of movement-specific potentiation. Second, the 4-minute recovery period employed in our protocol appears to optimize the potentiation-fatigue balance. This timing aligns with findings by Finlay et al. [15] who identified 4 min as the optimal recovery duration for enhancing throwing velocity, with longer intervals diminishing the effect. Conversely, Ishak et al. [1] [found that 12-minute rest periods were superior for jump and sprint performance in handball players, suggesting that optimal recovery duration may be task-specific. Our results support the notion that upper-body explosive tasks may require shorter recovery periods compared to lower-body activities, possibly due to differences in metabolic demands and neuromuscular fatigue characteristics. The superior effectiveness of Band-PAPE over Band-FCM for throwing velocity enhancement may be explained by the potentiation-fatigue paradigm. While both protocols likely induced similar initial potentiation responses, the higher volume and complexity of Band-FCM (four sets vs. two sets) may have generated greater residual fatigue, thereby attenuating the net performance benefit. This interpretation is supported by the concurrent elevation in RPE following Band-FCM, indicating higher physiological stress and potential fatigue accumulation. Moreover, the lower overall volume and simpler structure of Band-PAPE may have minimized residual fatigue while preserving a high specificity stimulus, resulting in a more favorable potentiation–fatigue balance for acute throwing performance. In contrast, the higher-volume and more complex Band-FCM sequence may have increased peripheral and coordinative fatigue demands, which could attenuate net potentiation despite similar initial activation.In contrast to throwing velocity, neither protocol produced practically meaningful improvements in CMJ performance, despite achieving statistical significance. The minimal increases (~ 0.5 cm) observed fall well below the typical error of measurement for jump testing and are unlikely to translate to performance benefits in match situations. Using a conservative smallest worthwhile change (SWC) defined as 0.2 × baseline SD (≈ 1.1 cm in our sample), the observed CMJ increases (~ 0.5 cm) fall below this threshold and are therefore unlikely to be practically meaningful for handball performance. This finding aligns with several recent investigations that have reported limited effectiveness of upper-body conditioning activities for enhancing lower-body explosive performance. Tseng et al. [16] similarly found that accentuated eccentric loading protocols maintained isometric peak force and rate of force development but failed to improve CMJ or spike jump height in volleyball players. Likewise, Terbalyan et al. [17] reported no advantage of unilateral isometric PAPE over standard aerobic warm-up for CMJ performance. These findings collectively emphasize the importance of anatomical and biomechanical specificity in PAPE protocol design.

However, contrasting evidence exists in the literature. Villalon-Gasch et al. [18] reported meaningful CMJ improvements (1.3 cm; ES = 0.7) following 90% 1RM squats in elite volleyball players, with effects sustained into competitive performance. Similarly, a comprehensive systematic review by Ulloa-Sánchez et al. [10] across 53 studies found moderate improvements in CMJ (ES = 0.54) following PAPE interventions. The discrepancy between these findings and our results likely reflects the importance of anatomical specificity—lower-body conditioning activities for lower-body performance tasks.

The minimal CMJ improvements observed in our study suggest that upper-body conditioning primarily induces generalized central nervous system activation without providing sufficient mechanical stimulus to the lower-limb musculature. This interpretation is consistent with the specificity principle of PAPE, which emphasizes that conditioning activities should target the same muscle groups and movement patterns as the subsequent performance task [8]. Future investigations might explore lower-body specific conditioning activities (e.g., band-assisted squats or jumps) to optimize jump performance enhancement in handball players.

The most pronounced difference between protocols emerged in perceived exertion responses, with Band-FCM significantly elevating RPE while Band-PAPE produced no change in internal load. This finding has important implications for protocol selection and practical implementation. The elevated RPE following Band-FCM likely reflects the higher training volume (four sets vs. two sets) and greater metabolic demands associated with the complex movement sequences employed in the French Contrast Method.

From a mechanistic perspective, PAPE effectiveness depends on achieving an optimal balance between potentiation and fatigue [6]. When conditioning activities are excessively intense or voluminous, the resultant fatigue can override potentiation effects, leading to decreased rather than enhanced performance. Our findings suggest that Band-FCM may have exceeded this optimal threshold, generating sufficient fatigue to attenuate potential performance benefits while simultaneously increasing perceived exertion.

The ability of Band-PAPE to enhance throwing velocity without elevating RPE represents a particularly valuable characteristic for practical implementation. This suggests that the protocol provides efficient neuromuscular stimulation with minimal physiological stress, making it suitable for use in competitive settings where managing athlete fatigue is paramount. The absence of increased perceived exertion also indicates that Band-PAPE is unlikely to compromise psychological readiness or confidence, which are important considerations for pre-competition interventions. The differential effects of Band-FCM and Band-PAPE on performance and internal load have important implications for their practical application in handball training and competition settings. Band-PAPE emerges as an ideal pre-competition or in-competition intervention due to its effectiveness in enhancing throwing velocity without increasing fatigue. The protocol’s simplicity (two sets at 90% band-1RM incorporating a 3-second isometric hold followed by explosive concentric actions, with 90 s rest between sets) and minimal equipment requirements make it highly feasible for implementation in various settings, including halftime breaks, pre-match warm-ups, and preparation of substitute players.

The practical significance of a 7% improvement in throwing velocity should not be underestimated. In elite handball, where matches are often decided by narrow margins, even small performance enhancements can influence competitive outcomes. Faster ball velocities reduce goalkeeper reaction time and increase scoring probability, potentially providing teams with a meaningful competitive advantage. The ability to implement this intervention courtside with minimal disruption to normal preparation routines further enhances its practical value.

Conversely, Band-FCM appears less suitable for acute performance enhancement in competitive settings due to its higher internal load and lack of significant performance benefits. However, this should not be interpreted as evidence of ineffectiveness for all applications. The French Contrast Method has demonstrated efficacy for chronic power development across various sports [19, 20], suggesting that Band-FCM may be more appropriately reserved for training sessions focused on long-term adaptation rather than acute performance enhancement.

The distinct characteristics of each protocol suggest complementary rather than competing applications. Band-PAPE may be optimal for acute performance enhancement in competitive settings, while Band-FCM could serve as a valuable training tool for developing explosive power over time. This dual approach would allow practitioners to maximize both immediate performance benefits and long-term athletic development.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, the study was conducted exclusively with elite male youth handball players, which may limit generalizability to other populations. As PAPE responsiveness may vary with training status, age, and sex (8), future studies should replicate these protocols in female youth players and in non-elite cohorts across different competitive levels and examine potential moderation by sex and playing level. Second, we assessed only acute responses within a single session; therefore, the chronic effects of repeated implementation and long-term performance adaptations remain unknown. Third, the recovery period was standardized at 4 min and individual optimization was not explored; thus, individualized or condition-specific recovery windows (e.g., 2–8 min) should be tested in upper-body handball tasks, given the substantial inter-individual variability in PAPE responses. An important consideration is that PAPE responses in U18 athletes may be influenced by maturational heterogeneity, even within the same chronological age group. Youth athletes may also exhibit different fatigue and recovery kinetics than adults, which could shift the potentiation–fatigue balance and contribute to the inter-individual variability observed in our results. Fourth, our conditioning activities were limited to the upper body; future work should examine lower-body and whole-body or sport-specific band-based protocols (e.g., band-assisted throwing motions) to better target jump-related outcomes. Finally, we did not evaluate how these interventions interact with different phases of training periodization or competitive schedules, and we did not mechanically characterize the elastic band force–elongation relationship (e.g., force in Newtons), which may limit cross-study comparability of absolute loading despite individualized band-1RM standardization.

Conclusion

The present investigation demonstrates that Band-PAPE represents an effective, practical intervention for acutely enhancing throwing velocity in elite youth male handball players. The protocol’s ability to produce meaningful performance improvements without increasing perceived exertion makes it particularly valuable for competitive applications. The simplicity and minimal equipment requirements further enhance its practical appeal for handball practitioners. Based on these findings, we recommend implementing Band-PAPE in the following contexts: (1) pre-match warm-up routines, (2) halftime interventions, (3) preparation of substitute players, and (4) specific skill training sessions focused on throwing performance. The protocol should consist of two sets of band-resisted shoulder press performed at 90% of band-1RM, incorporating a 3-second maximal isometric hold followed immediately by explosive concentric actions, with 90 s of rest between sets, followed by 4 min of passive recovery before performance tasks. Conversely, Band-FCM appears more suitable for training contexts focused on long-term power development rather than acute performance enhancement.
