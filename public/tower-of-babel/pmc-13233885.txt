Extensively and Pan-Drug Resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa Strains from Burn Patients in Mexico City are Genetically Related to Isolates Recovered a Decade Earlier in Local Hospitals
Authors: Rogelio Rojas-Rios, Sara E. Quiroz-Morales, Monica Dominguez-Alvarez, Esaú López-Jácome, Abigail González-Valdez, Luis Servín-González, Abigail Pérez-Valdespino, Gloria Soberón-Chávez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pseudomonas aeruginosa causes life-threatening infections among patients with burn-wounds and other debilitating conditions, especially if they are resistant to carbapenem antibiotics. This study aimed to perform a genomic characterization of two multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa strains (P1025 and P2279) isolated from burn patients in 2016 and 2021 in Mexico City, with a focus on the identification and characterization of their class 1 integrons. Their genomes were sequenced using combined Oxford Nanopore and Illumina technologies, and they were assigned to sequence type (ST) 309 using the PubMLST database. Their phylogenetic analysis with respect to two ST309 carbapenem resistant P. aeruginosa strains previously isolated in this city in 2006 showed that they are closely related. In addition, the genome of P1025 and P2279 revealed similar elements related to antibiotic resistance and both strains carry a chromosomal class 1 integron encoding resistance to β−lactams and aminoglycosides. The locus of the class 1 integron insertion in strain P2279 is identical to that observed in the two ST309 strains previously isolated in Mexico City in 2006, while the insertion of the P1025 class 1 integron represents an independent event. These findings show that carbapenem-resistant ST309 P. aeruginosa strains have been present in different Mexico City hospitals from 2006 to at least 2021 and might represent an important health hazard.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00284-026-04979-y.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is an environmental bacterium and an opportunistic pathogen that represents an important public-health risk due to its production of virulence factors and its high intrinsic and acquired antibiotic resistance [1–3]. Carbapenems-resistant strains represent a major health concern and are classified as a high-priority research topic by the World Health Organization [4]. Among the genes involved in antibiotic resistance, those carried by the so-called integrons (INT), which are genetic elements with an expression platform and different cassettes of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), play a key role in the high prevalence of multidrug-resistant strains, due to their horizontal transfer and the large number of ARGs they carry [5].

In this work, two P. aeruginosa strains isolated from burn patients in a tertiary hospital in Mexico City [6] were analyzed to determine their antibiotic resistance profiles. These strains were previously classified as part of the PA14 phylogroup (Group 2) [6]. Strain P1025 was isolated in 2016 and strain P2279 was isolated in 2021. The genome sequence of both strains showed that they belong to the high-risk clone ST309, as other strains previously reported in different Mexico City hospitals [7, 8], and that they carried class 1 integrons (C-1-INT), which were further characterized. Our findings suggest that carbapenem-resistant ST309 P. aeruginosa strains carrying these mobile genetic elements might be an important source of infections in Mexico City hospitals.

Materials and Methods

Antimicrobial Resistance Determination

Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles were first determined with Vitek 2 and then confirmed by broth microdilution, according to the 35th edition (2025) of the CLSI M100 guidelines and the cutoff values were compared with those of the CLSI [9]. Antimicrobials tested at 0.062–64 µg/mL were amikacin (AMK; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), gentamicin (GM; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), aztreonam (ATM; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), ceftazidime (CAZ; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), cefepime (FEP; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), ciprofloxacin (CIP; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), levofloxacin (LVX; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), doripenem (DOR; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), imipenem (IMP; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), meropenem (MEM; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), and colistin (COL; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA). Piperacillin/tazobactam (TZP; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) was tested at 0.25/4–128/4 µg/mL. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 and P. aeruginosa 27,853 were used as controls according to CLSI recommendations [9]. The resistance profiles of strains were defined according to Magiorakos et al. [10], multidrug-resistant (MDR) was defined as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories, XDR (extensively-resistant) was defined as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in all but two or fewer antimicrobial categories (i.e. bacterial isolates remain susceptible to only one or two categories) and pan drug-resistant (PDR) was defined as non-susceptibility to all agents in all antimicrobial categories.

Genome Sequencing and Antimicrobial Resistance Genes (ARGs) Identification

The genome sequences of P1025 and P2279 strains were determined by the Instituto de Biotecnología at the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México (UNAM) using combined Nanopore and Illumina sequencing technologies. A summary of the genome assemblies and annotation characteristics are listed in Table S1. The Whole Genome Shotgun project of strains studied were deposited in the DDBJ/ENA/GenBank under the following accession numbers: P1025: SAMN48669260 and P2279: SAMN48669307. Sequence type was determined using the PubMLST (Public database for molecular typing and microbial genome diversity, https://pubmlst.org/) [11]. Integrons and attC sites were searched with Integronfinder v2.0.2 [12]. ARGs were identified using AMRfinder v4.0.3 with the database version 2024-12-18.1 [13]; all genes showed 100% coverage and > 90% identity.

Construction of the Phylogenetic Tree

To construct the phylogenetic tree of strains studied here, we used the following genomes: PA124 (GCF_002192475.1), PA127 (GCF_002205355.1), PA14 (GCA_000014625.1), ID4365 (GCA_000647615.1), PAO1 (GCA_000006765.1), BWH053 (GCF_000629525.1), and PA7 (GCA_000017205.1) which was used as the outgroup. Genomes were annotated using Prokka v1.14.6 [14]. The core genome alignment was generated with Panaroo v1.1.2 in strict mode with a core genome threshold of 0.98 [15]. Model selection was conducted using ModelFinder in IQ-TreE v2.3.6 [16] identifying GTR + F+I+R4 as the best fitting nucleotide substitution model based on Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Branch support was assessed with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The resulting tree was midpoint-rooted and visualized using iTOL v6 [17].

Expression of the Integrase in P. aeruginosa P1025 and P2279

The expression of the integrases intI1 genes in strains P1025 and P2279 was determined by RT-qPCR using standard methods at mid-exponential growth-phase (9 h) as a control, at the same time point but after inducing the SOS response by addition of 0.2 µg/mL of mitomycin-C, and at the stationary phase of growth (24 h). Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using the SV RNA isolation system (Promega, Madison WI, USA). The purity and concentration of RNA were evaluated by spectrophotometry using the NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, Lituania) and qPCR was performed with GoTaq master mix (Promega, USA). Expression values were normalized using rpsL and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method [18]. Assays were performed in triplicate in three independent experiments for each condition, and statistical comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test in R v4.5.1. The oligonucleotides used for these experiments were previously reported [19, 20].

Results and Discussion

The Highly Resistant P. aeruginosa Isolates P1025 and P2279 Belong to ST309

P. aeruginosa strains P1025 and P2279 were previously classified as Group 2 strains, which are characterized by the production of the highly cytotoxic ExoU protein [6]. Strain P1025 was isolated from an endotracheal aspirate of a burn patient in 2016 and strain P2279 was isolated from a burn-wound biopsy in 2021. Their resistance-profile to 12 antimicrobials showed that strain P1025 is pan-drug-resistant (PDR), and strain P2279 is extensively-drug-resistant (XDR) (Fig. 1A).

To further characterize these highly resistant strains their genome sequences were determined (Table S1). We found that both belong to ST309, even though they were isolated almost five years apart, suggesting the persistence of this high-risk clone among the collection of strains isolated from burn patients [6]. In addition, an outbreak caused by carbapenem-resistant ST309 P. aeruginosa isolates was reported in a pediatric tertiary hospital in Mexico City from 2007 to 2013 [21]. Furthermore, carbapenem-resistant strains belonging to this high-risk clone have also been reported from two independent hospitals in the metropolitan area during this period [22], as well as from other tertiary hospitals between 2021 and 2023 [23], and 2018 to 2020 [24].

P1025 and P2279 Strains are Genetically Related to Strains Previously Isolated in Mexico City

The genome sequences of two ST309 isolates from adults with ventilator-associated pneumonia at a tertiary referral hospital in Mexico City in 2006 have been reported (strains PA124 and PA127) [7], thus it seems possible that carbapenem-resistant ST309 isolates might be prevalent in Mexico, since outbreaks have been reported in this country [8, 21–24] and other Latin American countries [25–28]. Furthermore, ST309 strains isolated in Mexico [7, 23, 24], Uruguay [27] and the United States [29], have been shown to carry a C-1-INT that encode carbapenemases.

To determine the relatedness of strains P1025, P2279, PA124 and PA127, and to assess whether a ST309 clone has been circulating in Mexico City since 2006 [7, 8, 20–24], we constructed a whole-genome phylogenetic tree including P. aeruginosa type strains and other strains belonging to the PA14 Group 2. This analysis shows that these strains are closely related, supporting the possibility of the prevalence of a single clone across different hospitals from 2006 to 2021 (Fig. 2).

Strains P1025 and P2279 Carry Chromosomal C-1-INT that are Related to the INT of ST309 PA124 and PA127 Isolates from an Independent Mexico City Hospital

The analysis of ARGs showed that both P1025 and P2279 have a high prevalence of multiple genes encoding β-lactamases conferring resistance to different β-lactam antibiotics including the oxacillin hydrolyzing β-lactamase (blaOXA-2) (Figs. 1B and 3). In addition, strain P1025 encodes a carbapenemase (blaGES20) arranged in tandem with an extended spectrum β-lactamase (blaGES19) (Fig. 1B). This extended-spectrum β-lactamase was reported to form part of a plasmid-encoded C-1-INT [30].

A C-1-INT was detected in strain P1025 carrying the cassette arrangement acc(6)-33-blaGES−19-blaGES20- acc(6’)-lb-blaOXA−2-aadA1 (Fig. 3). A notable feature of this arrangement is the presence of tandem bla cassettes. C-1-INT with this genetic arrangement are also present in P. aeruginosa PA124 and PA127 strains isolated in 2006 [7] (Fig. 3). This cassette arrangement has also been reported in other ST309 P. aeruginosa isolates, including two strains isolated in 2019 from patients with sepsis treated in different hospitals in a large urban area of the United States, that were highly resistant to carbapenems [29]. Our isolation in 2016 of a ST309 strain (P1025) recovered from a burn patient with this C-1-INT with tandem β-lactamases suggests that it constitutes a widespread threat for hospitalized patients in Mexico City.

Strain P2279 also harbors a C-1-INT that, although lacking the GES-β-lactamases, is inserted at the same genetic locus as the integrons of strains PA124 and PA127 between the fabF and glmS genes (Fig. 3). This integron in strain P2279 encodes one extended-spectrum β-lactamase (blaOXA−2) and other 3 ARGs (Figs. 1B and 3).

The site of insertion of the P1025 integron is also linked to glmS although in the opposite direction as those in PA124, PA127 and P2279, showing that it constitutes an independent insertion event into the chromosome. The insertion of the C-1-INT in the glmS locus suggests that the transposase associated with them is a member of the Tn7 transposases that have a specificity of insertion in this locus [31]. Comparing the sequence between P1025 and P2279 linked to glmS gene, a Tn7 transposon was identified only in P1025. In contrast, a conserved IS6 (IS6 100) composite transposon carrying the C-1-INT is present in all four studied strains (P1025, P2279, PA214 and PA127) (Fig. S1). Notably, the structure of the insertion sequences associated with the C-1-INT of strains PA124 and PA127 is identical to that observed in strain P2279, supporting the possibility that these strains belong to a clone that is prevalent in Mexico City hospitals.

The Class 1 Integrase Present in Strain P1025 is Induced by the SOS Response, but that of P2279 is Not

The recruitment of ARGs cassettes in integrons depends on the induction of the integrase encoded by the intI1 gene. Therefore, we were interested in determining whether they respond to the SOS-response and stationary phase conditions as was previously reported [32]. To determine whether there was a difference in the expression of the integrases of the two studied strains we determined the expression of the intI1 genes in strains P1025 and P2279 by RT-qPCR (Fig. 4). The results obtained show that expression of the integrase gene present in strain P1025 was induced in the presence of mitomycin-C, but not during the stationary phase, while expression of the integrase gene of strain P2279 was higher, but not significantly induced by mitomycin treatment and unresponsive in stationary phase. The high level of induction of the P1025 intI1gene under stress conditions might be related to the high complexity of the variable region of the C-1-INT present in this strain.

The intI1 gene expression is dependent on the SOS-response, due to the presence of a LexA binding site in its promoter region [32], which is conserved in the P1025 and P2279 class 1 integrons. Thus, it is intriguing that the P2279 intI1 gene expression is not significantly induced in the presence of mitomycin-C even though the LexA and RecA amino acid sequences encoded in this strain are identical to those of strain P1025. The reason for the lack of significant induction of the intI1 gene by the SOS response remains to be determined.

Conclusions

The genome sequences of a PDR P. aeruginosa strain isolated in 2016 (P1025) and an XDR strain isolated in 2021 (P2279) from burn patients at a tertiary hospital in Mexico City [6] revealed that both strains belong to the ST309 and harbor a C-1-INT. Furthermore, these strains are closely related to previously characterized strains isolated in 2006 in a different Mexico City hospital from adults with ventilator-related pneumonia [7], suggesting that they may represent a single clone (Fig. 2) with two independent events of C-1-INT insertions. These findings raise great concerns since they might be related to the high prevalence during the last 20 years of this carbapenem-resistant ST309 high-risk clone in different Mexico City hospitals and urge for the sequencing and characterization of a larger number of carbapenems resistant P. aeruginosa strains to fully understand the magnitude of the problem and to establish institutional measures to contend with it.

These results and the high incidence of carbapenems resistant ST309 clones that have been isolated in different hospitals in Mexico City during 2006 to 2023 [7, 8, 21–24] are in accordance with the recent publication by the National Mexican Academy of Medicine [33] that states that ST309 is a public health threat in Mexico.
