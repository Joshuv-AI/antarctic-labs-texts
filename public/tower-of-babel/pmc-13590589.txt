A machine-learning-based reconstruction of surface mass balance over the Greenland Ice Sheet from 1950 to 2020
Authors: Yulun Zhang, Yetang Wang, Xavier Fettweis, Shugui Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An accurate estimation of surface mass balance (SMB) is imperative for reliable quantification of the Greenland Ice Sheet (GrIS) mass changes and associated global sea level rise. Here, we present two new reconstructions of gridded annual SMB at the resolution of 0.1° × 0.1° across the GrIS from 1950 to 2020 by means of a CNN–Transformer model. This machine learning model is trained on the mostly recent compiled in-situ SMB observations, integrating with ERA5 Land reanalysis product, and outputs of the polar regional climate model MAR, respectively. When trained on the full dataset, the best-performing model predictions highly and significantly correlate with observations, with correlation coefficients of 0.96 and 0.93 for ERA5-Land-based and MAR-based reconstructions, respectively. Spatial cross-validation over unseen regions shows that reconstruction errors reduce by approximately 50% relative to the original datasets (ERA5-Land and MAR), with both reconstructions yielding statistically comparable RMSE (~53 mm w.e. yr−1) against in-situ observations. Independent validation against IceBridge airborne radar products further confirms their robustness, with 35% and 50% reduction in relative absolute error relative to ERA5-Land and MAR, respectively. The spatially and temporally complete annual SMB datasets can be used for the input of ice-sheet models and surface hydrological studies.

Background & Summary

The Greenland ice sheet (GrIS) is the second largest freshwater reservoir on Earth, and holds ice enough to cause global sea level rise of about 7.3 m1. Against rapid warming, approximately fourfold as fast as global mean in the Arctic from 1979 onwards the GrIS has undergone mass loss at an accelerated rate via dynamical calving icebergs, submarine melting and surface meltwater runoff2–5, making it one of tipping elements in the climate system and a dominant contributor of current global sea level rise6–9. Since the mid-1990s, the ice sheet mass loss has shifted from being primarily driven by dynamic ice discharge to being more significantly influenced by variations of surface mass balance (SMB) defined by mass input through net snow accumulation minus the runoff of meltwater10. As a key determinant of the GrIS mass variations, SMB is of vital importance for constraining ice sheet model projections of GrIS change and contribution to global sea level rise11.

The pioneering in-situ GrIS SMB measurements date to the late 19th century12. After the Second World War, SMB observations by means of stakes and ice cores/snow pits greatly increased, accompanied by large amounts of scientific expeditions or glaciological activities organized over the GrIS13–16. With the introduction of ice-penetrating radar during the mid-1960s, continuous measurements over vast regions can be achieved by ground-based radar surveys and airborne campaigns, which enable mapping the accumulation component of SMB at the ice sheet regions beyond the reach of in-situ measurements. With extensive spatial coverage across the ice sheet, radar retrievals can advance insights into accumulation dynamics and regional variations. For instance, Koenig et al.17 provided ice-sheet scale SMB for the 2009–2012 period, derived from NASA’s Operation Ice Bridge (OIB) airborne snow radar. Building on this work, Montgomery et al.18 extended the time series of these OIB airborne radar-determined SMB to 2017. Until now, there are more than 4000 in-situ measurements by ice core, snow pits and stakes, and over 1.7 million ground and airborne radar SMB values across the GrIS19. Despite these extensive SMB observations, large gaps in their spatial coverage still exist, and their temporal coverage is usually discontinuous over the duration of just a few years or less. Thus, accurate quantification of long-term SMB changes across the GrIS by indirect interpolation of measurements remains an enormous challenge due to large spatial and temporal variations of SMB.

Given the limitations of direct measurements, global and regional atmospheric models have become indispensable tools for estimating spatially and temporally continuous SMB fields. Global reanalyses like ERA5, assimilating observations of atmospheric variables such as wind speed, air temperature and surface pressure, offer an alternative for the estimation of spatiotemporally continuous SMB across the GrIS by means of atmospheric variables (e.g., precipitation, temperature, runoff). They are also used as the boundary conditions to force regional climate models (RCMs) to simulate Greenland SMB (e.g., Alexander et al.20, van der Schot et al.21). With the improvement of cryospheric process schemes, polar RCMs such as MAR (Modèle Atmosphérique Régional) and RACMO (Regional Atmospheric Climate Model) have become an essential tool for investigating GrIS SMB changes. They demonstrate robust skill in replicating observed temporal variability and spatial patterns22–26. As a result, the RCM simulations have been applied for the calculation of GrIS mass balance10,27. However, substantial uncertainties persist in these models19,28, particularly pronounced in high mass-loss regions. For example, MAR v3.5.2 systematically overestimates SMB across Southeast Greenland17. RACMO2 (the Polar version 2 of RACMO) exhibits considerable snowfall biases over the coastal regions of Greenland when validated against radar data18. To address these limitations, recent studies emphasize critical needs for refining precipitation parameterization in existing models, adopting non-hydrostatic frameworks (e.g., WRF: Weather Research and Forecasting model, HARMONIE reanalyses)18,29 and implementing bias correction for RCMs.

Many attempts have been made to correct for the biases in simulated SMB from RCMs. Franco et al.30 statistically downscaled the GrIS SMB from the original 25 km MAR output to 15 km resolution by interpolating each component using local daily vertical gradients (except for precipitation) calculated by elevation differences between the digital elevation model (DEM) in MAR and the 5 km DEM from Bamber et al.31. Noël et al.32 and Tedesco et al.33 further incorporated the altitude dependence of variables such as surface temperature and runoff to perform spatial downscaling of RACMO and MAR simulations, thereby substantially improving the representation of individual SMB components. Subsequent studies have progressively refined this framework by introducing newly available observational data and enhancing model simulations25,27,34. Most recently, an empirical orthogonal function (EOF) was used to calibrate SMB outputs of two RCMs (HIRHAM5: HIRLAM–ECHAM Regional Climate Model version 5) and RACMO 2.4p1: the Polar version 2.4p1 of RACMO) and a reanalysis product (CARRA) based on observations from the SUMup surface mass balance dataset35. Despite considerable progress made in mapping ice-sheet scale SMB, most of methods depend on the assumption of linear statistical relationships or specific data distributions, which constrain their ability to handle the complex, nonlinear SMB processes. Recent advances in machine learning permits the further enhancement of GrIS SMB estimates by extracting complex patterns and non-linear relationships within heterogeneous datasets36,37. In particular, machine learning algorithms can exploit synergies between physically based and data-driven models to better represent key surface mass balance processes, such as snowfall accumulation, melt, runoff, and refreezing, thereby reducing uncertainties and improving prediction reliability38.

In this study, we generate a new reconstruction of gridded annual SMB spanning from 1950 to 2020, using a machine learning framework based on a CNN–Transformer architecture, trained on an extensive compilation of 37532 annual SMB measurements across the GrIS. The model inputs also include 24 parameters from the ERA5-Land reanalysis and MAR outputs (Table S1), which represent both long-term climatological conditions and short-term climate memory of precipitation and SMB. This approach effectively captures spatiotemporal SMB variability by combining the strengths of convolutional neural networks for spatial feature extraction with transformers for modeling temporal dependencies. To comprehensively evaluate the model’s accuracy and generalization ability, we conduct both spatial 5-fold cross-validation and independent validation using observational data not included in the training process. To present potential application of the reconstructed datasets, we compare spatial patterns and trends in SMB reconstructed by the CNN-Transformer models and original datasets (ERA5-Land and MAR) across the different ice sheet sectors.

Methods

In situ SMB observations

SMB observations are derived from the Surface Mass Balance and Snow on Sea Ice Working Group (SUMup) dataset compiled by Vandecrux, et al.19, which is an extensible, community-based, standardized dataset (accessed from: https://doi.org/10.18739/A2M61BR5M). The SUMup dataset compiles measurements from a wide range of field campaigns and research projects, including ice cores and/or boreholes, stake measurements, snow pits, radar isochrones16,19. Unlike previous compilations focused mainly on ice sheet SMB, SUMup encompasses all types of measurements extending into ablation areas, thus providing the most comprehensive observational basis for SMB studies on the GrIS19. In order to obtain an appropriate dataset for reconstructing the GrIS SMB, we firstly excluded records outside the study period, as well as measurements shorter than three years. Secondly, for datasets with monthly or daily resolution, the annual SMB was calculated by accumulating the data. On the other hand, the snow radar measurements were overly dense and unevenly distributed across the study area, which could result in excessive weighting during data analysis and model training. To eliminate this influence, snow radar data located within the same grid cell were averaged, ensuring a more balanced spatial representation and preventing localized data clusters from dominating the analysis. Finally, we clipped data outside the range defined by the GIMP ice sheet delineation39. To independently evaluate model performance, we additionally employed 18,704 records derived from NASA’s Operation IceBridge (OIB) snow radar (accessed from: https://doi.org/10.5194/tc-11-773-2017-supplement). These records were calculated from radar data collected during 25 OIB flights conducted between 2013 and 2014, covering over 17,700 km across the dry-snow and upper percolation zones40.The snow accumulation was calculated from the ice core depth–age scales, modeled depth–density profiles, and each pair of adjacent IRHs for every radar trace along the flight lines40. Given that ablation is negligible in the dry-snow zone, only accumulation measurements from this region were treated as equivalent to SMB for our validation (Fig. 1). Here, the dry-snow zone is defined as regions where surface melt is absent or fully refrozen without runoff. Importantly, these data were not included in model training, but used exclusively for validation.

ERA5 reanalysis product

ERA5 is the fifth generation of atmospheric reanalysis data produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) at a spatial resolution of 0.25° × 0.25°. It integrates a large number of historical observations through advanced data assimilation systems and modelling techniques to generate globally consistent estimates41. Compared to ERA5, ERA5-Land is a reanalysis dataset produced by replaying the land component of the ERA5 climate reanalysis, and provides a consistent representation of land surface variables over several decades at enhanced spatial resolution (0.1° × 0.1°). In this study, we used ERA5-Land during 1950–2020 as an input parameter of machine learning model, including near-surface variables such as 2 m air temperature and precipitation. These variables were further processed into derived predictors (e.g., decadal statistics and lagged terms), as described in the “Input data pre-processing” section. For 10-year moving average prior to 1949, the original ERA5 (1940–1950) is also used (available at: https://doi.org/10.24381/cds.68d2bb30), and bilinearly interpolated to 0.1° grid specification of ERA5-Land (available at: https://doi.org/10.24381/cds.f17050d7). Since ERA5 dataset does not provide runoff variables, melting and runoff were estimated using a positive degree-day (PDD) runoff/retention model following Hanna, et al.23. Monthly ERA5 near-surface air temperature and precipitation were used as inputs to the PDD routine to calculate annual SMB. We assumed a temperature variability (σ σ) of 4.2 °C to account for the daily cycle and random weather fluctuations and used a degree-day factor for snowmelt = 2.7 mm °C–1d–1, and degree-day factor for ice melt = 8.0 mm °C–1d–1. Runoff occurs only after the snowpack becomes saturated, which typically happens when cumulative melt reaches approximately 60% of the annual precipitation. Outputs of regional climate models

Developed by the University of Liège, Belgium, MAR is a hydrostatic atmospheric model specifically optimized for high-latitude climate simulations28. Its modular architecture includes an atmospheric dynamic core43,44 coupled with the Soil-Ice-Snow Energy Balance Vegetation Model45–47 and ecRad radiation scheme48. Precipitation physics are simulated through prognostic equations governing cloud droplets, ice crystals, raindrops, snow particles and specific humidity44,46. Additional details are described by Fettweis et al.26,28. Here, we use MAR version 3.14 forced by ERA5 at 6-hourly intervals (available at: http://ftp.climato.be/fettweis/MARv3.14/Greenland/). The simulations are performed on a 10-km resolution grid in the EPSG:3413 polar stereographic projection from 1940 to 2020.

Given the good performance of RACMO2.3p2 (the Polar version 2.3p2 of RACMO) in simulating the related cryospheric processes, we also use its outputs as comparison. RACMO2.3p2 incorporates an advanced snow module which explicitly simulates key cryospheric processes including meltwater percolation, refreezing dynamics, runoff generation, dry snow densification, and drifting snow transport49–51. The detailed description of the model follows Noël et al.52. Its simulations employ a Polar Stereographic North projection (EPSG:3413) at the 1 km × 1 km resolution. To maintain temporal consistency in atmospheric boundary conditions, the model is forced by ERA40 (1958–1978), ERA-Interim (1979–1989), and ERA5 (1990–2020). It should be noted that the RACMO outputs are statistically downscaled to 1 km resolution after the RCM simulations are completed, rather than being dynamically simulated at such high resolution. Since the predictor construction requires a preceding 10-year climatological window, a reconstruction based on RACMO would only be available from 1968 onwards.To ensure consistency in the temporal coverage among reconstructed datasets, the RACMO outputs are utilized for comparison purposes only, rather than for reconstruction.

An overview of reconstruction processing

Point observations of SMB provide only a partial description of ice-sheet surface mass balance because they are discontinuous in both space and time. To characterize the evolution of SMB over the entire ice sheet and over the last decades, one can train a machine-learning framework that links SMB to an input dataset which is itself continuous in space and time and assumed to drive changes in SMB. Once the relationship between input and SMB is learned by the algorithm, the algorithm can be driven by the entire input dataset to reconstruct the SMB, even at places where no observations are available. Figure 2 outlines the end-to-end workflow of SMB reconstruction across the GrIS from 1950 to 2020. The workflow begins with preprocessing and quality control of observational SUMup SMB records, which are used as the target variable for CNN-Transformer training and validation. ERA5-Land and MAR-derived climatic variables are used as predictor sources. Following preprocessing, the climatic variables are transformed into spatiotemporal predictor features (detailed descriptions are provided in the “Input data pre-processing” section). The processed datasets then undergo systematic partitioning into training and validation subsets for CNN-Transformer model development and hyperparameter optimization. The optimized model is subsequently used to reconstruct annual SMB variability across the GrIS. Finally, uncertainty estimation and model evaluation are performed using spatial cross-validation and independent observational datasets. Selection of the machine learning models

Before reconstructing SMB across the GrIS, we estimate the performance of multiple machine-learning algorithms, including neural network models (ANN and LSTM), tree-based algorithms (XGBoost) and the hybrid models (CNN-Transformer and CNN-LSTM). Each model was trained using the same set of predictors and observational SMB records and was employed as a statistical emulator to reconstruct the spatiotemporal variability of SMB. To ensure a robust evaluation of generalization capability, model performance was assessed using a 5-fold cross-validation framework with RMSE as the primary metric. The CNN-Transformer exhibits superior performance with the RMSE value of 54 mm w.e. yr−1, over the other models (RMSE of ANN: 120.4 mm w.e. yr−1; RMSE of LSTM: 105.0 mm w.e. yr−1; RMSE of XGBoost: 93.2 mm w.e. yr−1; RMSE of CNN-LSTM: 70.4 mm w.e. yr−1). Therefore, CNN-Transformer is selected as the core reconstruction framework. During model tuning, we prioritize generalization capacity over peak performance metrics. By deliberately constraining architectural complexity and adjusting input parameter sensitivity, the model reduces overfitting risks, enhances extrapolation potential beyond training data distributions and establishes robustness against spatial-temporal discontinuities in validation data. It is notable that no universal optimal configuration exists for model architecture, input parameters, or training protocols in this approach. The calibrated CNN-Transformer achieves optimal balance between precision and generalizability for Arctic SMB reconstruction.

CNN–Transformer Model

Feature extraction serves as a critical step in machine learning applications to generate meaningful information that enables predictive models to make accurate forecasts53. As a widely used algorithm, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) automatically extract features through performing convolutional operations, which are applicable to both image-type and time-series data. The CNN architecture consists of an input layer, convolutional layers, pooling layers and fully connected layers. The input layer receives multidimensional data, followed by stacked convolutional layers that apply learnable filters to extract local features from input data. Each convolution operation is accompanied by a nonlinear activation function to model complex relationships. ReLU is selected as the activation function in this study. Pooling layers perform dimensionality reduction and extract the most significant local features, thereby enhancing computational efficiency and feature invariance. The fully connected layers integrate the CNN with the Transformer via a softmax function, and convey CNN-extracted multi-scale local features (e.g., short-term trends and periodic fluctuations) to the Transformer.

The Transformer encompasses input embedding, positional encoding, multi-head self-attention, feed-forward networks (FFN), along with layer normalization and residual connections. The input embedding layer projects raw data into a latent feature space, which is subsequently transformed into queries, keys and values. Subsequently, the data is processed by a multi-head self-attention layer, which dynamically computes a weighted sum of input features to capture the dependencies between different locations in the input data. Each attention head learns different feature interaction patterns, extracting global features while avoiding inductive biases of sequential and local interactions. The output of the self-attention layer undergoes layer normalization and residual connections before being fed into the FFN. The FFN, composed of two fully connected layers with ReLU activation functions, further increases the nonlinearity and the capacity of the models. Layer normalization54 and residual connections55 are applied to stabilize the training process.

CNN-Transformer is a fusion of convolutional CNN and Transformer feature representations. Fusion strategies include weighted summation, nonlinear transformation after concatenation, or gating mechanisms. The gating units dynamically weight CNN-derived local features and Transformer-processed global dependencies, with the fused output processed by fully connected layers for final prediction. For the GrIS annual SMB prediction, the architecture uniquely represents short-term local features via CNN’s spatial filtering, long-range climate dependencies through Transformer’s attention mechanism, and nonlinear interactions between temporal scales and spatial gradients via adaptive gating during fusion. This synergistic design significantly enhances representation of complex spatiotemporal variations of SMB, particularly critical for this data-sparse region where observational networks exhibit spatial fragmentation and temporal gaps.

For hyperparameter tuning, the dataset is randomly partitioned into validation and training sets, with 80% of the data used for training and the remaining 20% (7506 observations) allocated for validation. For each parameter configuration, hyperparameter optimization is performed using Optuna, which employs Bayesian optimization based on the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator algorithm. Each configuration is estimated across four dimensions including training convergence speed and computational efficiency (training dynamics), mean difference (MD) and root mean square difference (RMSE) of trained model on training and validation data (performance metrics), parameter efficiency and memory requirements (resource utilization), and validation performance stability (generalization capacity). Furthermore, early stopping is also implemented during optimization of hyperparameter tuning. Note that the early stopping will tend to reduce the apparent performance of the CNN–Transformer, but this step enhances model robustness, mitigates overfitting risks and improve extrapolation capability. Input data pre-processing

In this study, the pre-processed in-situ SMB measurements (detailed in the “In situ SMB observations” section) serve as the target variables for model training. The predictor variables are constructed from annual-scale spatiotemporal climatic information derived from ERA5-Land and MAR datasets. We use historical SMB conditions as the main predictor, supplemented by precipitation as an additional input variable due to its dominant positive contribution to the GrIS SMB2,56. Near-surface air temperature (T2m) is incorporated as a key predictor because it governs Clausius-Clapeyron constrained moisture-holding capacity of the atmosphere, and influences precipitation regime changes and snowpack evolution57,58. This variable selection aligns with established SMB reconstruction frameworks where temperature serves as a critical proxy25.

In order to simultaneously resolve geographic and temporal gradients, long-term climatic baselines and oscillations and lagged snowpack responses to recent climate forcing, our predictor selection strategy is composed of spatiotemporal anchors, including latitude, longitude, and year to provide fundamental spatial positioning and temporal context, decadal climate context, including the 10-year averages (smb_10y_avg, t2m_10y_avg, precip_10y_avg) and amplitudes (here defined as the maximum minus the minimum within each 10-year window; smb_amp, t2m_amp, precip_amp) of 2m air temperature, SMB and precipitation (snowfall plus rainfall) to define the baseline conditions and quantify decadal variability at a given time and location, and short-term climate memory, including the preceding 5-year averages of SMB, precipitation and air temperature. This approach captures near-term hysteresis effects, specifically the delayed response of key ice sheet surface processes (e.g., surface albedo, thermal structure, density profiles, melt, and refreezing), which modulate the sensitivity of current mass balance to instantaneous forcing.

ERA5-Land exhibits superior strong consistency with temperature observations across the Arctic and outperforms precipitation representation compared to other reanalysis datasets59–61. MAR shows particularly high skill in simulating SMB components and near surface climate28,59 (Delhasse et al., 2020). Thus, temperature and precipitation and evaporation variables are extracted from ERA5-Land and MAR to generate model inputs. To enhance model robustness against spatial sampling biases from the heterogeneous spatial distribution of observational stations, all input variables are standardized by subtracting the mean of each variable from each data point and dividing by the temporal (on the time) standard deviation before entering the machine learning model.

Uncertainty estimation

In order to estimate the performance of the CNN-Transformer model, we firstly compare the trained model using all available observations and ERA5-Land or MAR, termed as “Best model”, against these measurements. While this baseline analysis establishes model capability, it may overestimate performance by lacking generalization testing for unseen regions or conditions. Therefore, we quantify uncertainty of the reconstruction by means of spatial cross-validation, which rigorously tests the model’s extrapolation capacity while mitigating spatial autocorrelation biases. For this purpose, a 5-fold spatial cross-validation strategy was adopted, in which the dataset was divided into five mutually exclusive subsets. Each subset contains approximately 7506 randomly selected observations (20% of the total data). In each iteration, one subset serves as unseen validation data, while the remaining four subsets were used to train the CNN-Transformer model (hereafter called CV model). The trained CV model then predicts the withheld subset, and the predict uncertainty is assessed against observations. Furthermore, 18,704 OIB records, which were completely excluded from the training process, were used for independent validation.

The corresponding results are quantified by three metrics including mean bias (MB), root-mean-square error (RMSE) and correlation coefficient (R). To avoid cancellation effects between positive and negative deviations, we also compute the relative bias (Rbias) as the absolute error normalized by the observed value. Data Records

The reconstruction datasets based on the Best model have been deposited in National Tibetan Plateau/Third Pole Environment Data Center63 for open access. This dataset contains annual surface mass balance (SMB) reconstructions over the GrIS for the period 1950–2020, at a spatial resolution of 0.1° × 0.1°. The data are provided in NetCDF format, which can be readily read using common programming languages such as R and Python. Technical Validation

Performance of the CNN-Transformer

Against in-situ SMB observations (Fig. 3), the best CNN-Transformer models trained on ERA5-Land and MAR datasets yield RMSE values of 39.2 mm w.e. yr−1 and 36.2 mm w.e. yr−1, respectively, with high correlation coefficients of >0.9 (p < 0.01). Under spatial cross-validation on unseen locations, RMSEs increase to 53.9 mm w.e. yr−1 and 51.3 mm w.e. yr−1 respectively, while the correlation coefficients remain above 0.85. While the initial evaluation appears overly optimistic, the cross-validated models trained on only using a subset of the datasets still outperforms the two original gridded products with RMSE values of 116.2 mm w.e. yr−1 (ERA5-Land), 108.8 mm w.e. yr−1 (MAR), respectively. This provides realistic bounds on the model’s true predictive capability. The mean annual SMB represented in ERA5-Land and MAR is 142 ± 68 mm w.e. yr−1 and 114 ± 65 mm w.e. yr−1, respectively. The absolute error of each reconstructed dataset decreases by nearly 50% relative to its original dataset, highlighting the substantial improvement achieved by the reconstructions. Moreover, we additionally accounted for observational uncertainty in the evaluation. After incorporating observational errors, the overall reconstruction errors increased by approximately 2–3 mm w.e. yr−1. The difference in accuracy between the two reconstructions is minor or even negligible, with the difference of <5 mm w.e. yr−1. This suggests that the machine-learning approach can enhance coherence across multi-source reconstructions by mitigating inconsistencies inherent in the original gridded products without respecting their data quality differences.Fig. 3Evaluation of the GrIS SMB (mm w.e. yr−1) predicted by Best model and CV model trained on ERA5-Land (a) and MAR (b) against in-situ observations during 1950–2020. Spatial difference in uncertainty of annual SMB reconstructions

In terms of spatial reconstruction uncertainties, the two reconstructions exhibit the best performance across the dry snow zone (|MB| < 20 mm w.e. yr−1) (Fig. 4a,d). Uncertainty in SMB increases toward the edges of the dry snow zone, peaking along the ice sheet margins in northwest and southeast Greenland, where two simulations overestimate actual SMB at most sites (|MB| > 80 mm w.e. yr−1). Compared to original datasets, the CV model significantly reduces errors particularly in northwest Greenland. In the southeast, while the CV model also shows a notable error reduction, some sites in the marginal non-dry snow zone still exhibited absolute errors reaching >80 mm w.e. yr−1. Our uncertainty estimation is conservative since the final CNN-Transformer model is trained on all available observations. The Best model demonstrates a marked enhancement in performance compared to the CV model, especially in the southeastern region where most sites errors are reduced to below 60 mm w.e. yr−1. Overall, both the CV and Best models effectively reduce original data errors and exhibit similar spatial error patterns, with the largest biases concentrated in the southeastern region. The relative absolute biases of the original datasets are consistently high across all regions, reaching 25–31% (Table 2). Owing to the substantially higher SMB in the non-dry snow zone, the absolute errors there are particularly large, with MAE of >189 mm w.e. yr−1 and RMSE of >276 mm w.e. yr−1. The errors of the CV model significantly reduce, with relative deviations decreasing by a factor of 2–3. In the non-dry snow zone, MAE and RMSE decrease to 89 and 131 mm w.e. yr−1, respectively, while in the dry-snow zone, MAE and RMSE fall below 23 and 47 mm w.e. yr−1, respectively. The Best model achieves further improvements across all regions and datasets, with relative bias reducing to below 8%. Its performance is particularly notable in the non-dry snow zone, where errors decrease by more than 120 mm w.e. yr−1 compared to the original datasets. Therefore, the application of machine learning not only narrows the difference between ERA5-Land and MAR in non-dry snow areas, but also improves reconstruction accuracy by two to three times.

Spatial and temporal variability in reconstructed SMB

To demonstrate the potential applications of the reconstructed dataset, we compare spatial patterns and trends of reconstructed SMB by the Best model (hereafter referred to as RECON_ERA5-Land for the ERA5-Land-based reconstruction and RECON_MAR for the MAR-based reconstruction) across different ice sheet regions with the original datasets (ERA5-Land and MAR). As shown in Fig. 6 and Figure S1, the spatial patterns of both multi-year averaged and individual annual SMB are broadly consistent among the datasets, characterized by negative values along the coastal margins, maximum values over southeastern Greenland, and lower positive values in the ice sheet interior. This consistency demonstrates that the reconstruction framework robustly preserves the inherent spatial structure of the SMB field, regardless of interannual climatic variability or snow conditions. However, ERA5-Land and MAR simulate higher SMB than the reconstructed datasets along the margin of the southeastern percolation zone. This likely reflects the overestimation of the original products for this region (Fig. 5), which the machine learning model corrects downward by means of in situ observations. In addition, north of 70°N within the dry-snow zone, MAR shows a much larger area with annual SMB below 100 mm w.e. yr−1 than ERA5-Land (Fig. 6b,d and Figure S1). Comparison with RACMO outputs

To further benchmark the reconstructions, we compared them with outputs of RACMO, a state-of-the-art regional climate model available from 1958 onwards. In terms of the spatial distribution of SMB, all datasets over the common period (1958–2020) display consistent spatial patterns with the 1950–2020 period on both multi-year mean and annual scales (Fig. 6, S1, S2). However, RACMO reproduces SMB spatial patterns more realistically than the original ERA5-Land and MAR products in some inland regions, but still exhibits systematic regional biases and weaker long-term trend signals (Figure S3 and Table S2). The reconstruction datasets generally show closer agreement with observations in terms of spatial bias, inter-annual variability, and long-term trends (Figure S4, S5). (Detailed comparisons with RACMO, including spatial bias distributions, regional trend analyses, and station-scale evaluations, are provided in the Supporting Information Text S1). These evaluations demonstrate that while RACMO represents an improvement over ERA5-Land and MAR in the dry snow area owing to its higher resolution and more cryosphere physical parameterizations such as drifting snow scheme, it still exhibits systematic regional biases and weaker long-term trend signals. Compared with the original ERA5-Land and MAR datasets, the reconstructed products substantially reduce these biases and provide more consistent agreement with both observed SMB magnitude and inter-annual variability, underscoring their added value for extending reliable SMB estimates back to 1950.

Usage Notes

We present two gridded datasets of annual SMB over the Greenland Ice Sheet (GrIS) covering the period 1950–2020. These datasets are reconstructed using a CNN–Transformer hybrid deep learning model based on the most comprehensive compilation of in situ SMB observations available over the GrIS to date, combined with predictors derived from ERA5-Land and regional climate model MAR. This approach enables the reconstruction of spatially continuous and temporally complete SMB fields across data-scarce regions. A spatial cross-validation on unseen areas shows that the reconstruction reduces model errors by approximately 50% compared to the original predictors. Independent validation using IceBridge long-term averaged SMB demonstrates a reduction in RMSE of 36–44 mm w.e. yr−1 in the original predictors to ~23.5 mm w.e. yr−1 in the reconstructions.

To be clear, the CNN-Transformer model has the advantage in closely fitting training data, however, the machine learning model still encounters some limitations. Firstly, the CNN-Transformer model fundamentally relies on the distribution and representativeness of the training data. Although we adjust the training strategy to enhance generalization and reduce the weight of densely clustered radar observations to improve their spatial representativeness, the model performance is still constrained by the availability of observations. Sensitivity experiments, in which 10% of the full dataset was reserved for independent validation and the remaining data were sub-sampled into different training fractions (10–95%), further support this conclusion. Reconstruction accuracy (e.g., RMSE) consistently improves as the training set size increases, without reaching a clear saturation plateau (Figure S6). This continuous improvement indicates that the model is still far from being over-fitted and its potential is primarily limited by the scarcity of current ground-truth measurements. In particular, there remain specific temperature and snowfall combination areas where observational data are lacking, compelling the model to extrapolate. Therefore, more observations need to be performed on the regions with insufficient observations (such as coastal margins and northeastern regions) to further improve the reconstructions. Secondly, errors in the input predictors (e.g., ERA5-Land precipitation biases) are possibly propagated into the reconstructions61. Besides, the current input parameters are somewhat simplified, which may introduce a certain uncertainty through oversimplification of complex physical processes. However, some widespread processes, such as shortwave radiation penetration into the snow/firn66, orographic enhancement and hydrometeor advection67, cloud-phase partitioning, and near-surface ventilation/wind-pumping68, etc., are more likely to be explained by machine learning model because the observational data affected by these processes are included in the dataset. Thirdly, the relatively low resolution of the model (~0.1°) also prevents it from resolving local-scale processes, such as melt-freeze cycles, and orographic precipitation61. Consequently, while the reconstruction effectively captures large-scale variability and reduces systematic biases in regional climate models, it also smooths out some fine-scale extreme phenomena, particularly in the southeastern coastal regions. Finally, SMB in non-dry snow regions is impacted by complex melt-refreeze and seepage processes that are highly sensitive to short-term temperature variations and surface characteristics. Small biases in surface temperature or shortwave radiation can lead to disproportionately large errors in melt and runoff. The parameterization of these processes in models varies and lack direct observational constraints, resulting in larger discrepancies of the original model outputs in this area. In comparison to RCMs, which can explicitly simulate atmospheric and surface processes but are sensitive to parameterization schemes and boundary conditions26,66, machine-learning-based reconstruction offers complementary advantages. They can more consistently recover long-term accumulated signals and achieve better consistency with independent observational data, thereby serving as an important tool to correct the original model fields.
