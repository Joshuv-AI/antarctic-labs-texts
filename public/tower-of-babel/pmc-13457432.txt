Crowe severity and femoral stem geometry are associated with lower-limb alignment after total hip arthroplasty for unilateral developmental dysplasia of the hip–related secondary osteoarthritis: valgus alignment trajectories
Authors: Muhammed Yusuf Afacan, Mahmut Gorkem Gurcinar, Cumhur Deniz Davulcu, Mehmet Can Unlu, Gokhan Kaynak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTotal hip arthroplasty (THA) for secondary osteoarthritis related to developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) restores hip mechanics. We aimed to quantify pre-to-postoperative changes in ipsilateral knee and global lower-limb alignment after unilateral THA to determine whether alignment changes differ by Crowe classification and femoral stem geometry.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 74 patients who underwent unilateral primary THA for DDH-related secondary osteoarthritis, with a mean follow-up of 45.4 months. The predefined primary radiographic outcome set comprised preoperative-to-postoperative changes and final follow-up values in coronal lower-limb alignment parameters, including mechanical and anatomic lateral distal femoral angles (mLDFA and aLDFA), mechanical medial proximal tibial angle (mMPTA), joint line convergence angle (JLCA), global offset (GO), hip–knee–ankle angle (HKA), knee mechanical axis deviation ratio (KMADR), and femoral mechanical–anatomic axis angle (FMMA). The contralateral reference limb was used for side-to-side radiographic comparison. Patients were stratified by Crowe classification (I–II vs. III–IV), and femoral stem geometry analyses were performed according to conical, wedge, and rectangular stem designs. Clinical outcomes included hip/knee visual analog scale, Harris Hip Score, Knee Society Score, Oxford Knee Score, KOOS, and WOMAC.ResultsAnalysis of the predefined radiographic outcome set demonstrated divergent alignment profiles according to Crowe severity. In Crowe I–II, several preoperative-to-postoperative changes were nominally observed, but these did not remain statistically significant after FDR adjustment; postoperative side-to-side differences largely converged toward the contralateral reference limb. In Crowe III–IV, a more robust valgus-oriented postoperative profile was observed, with FDR-significant changes in mLDFA, aLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA, GO, HKA, and FMMA, and persistent postoperative asymmetry versus the contralateral reference limb for most parameters except GO. Between Crowe groups, postoperative HKA was significantly higher and FMMA significantly lower in Crowe III–IV after multiplicity adjustment, whereas mMPTA and KMADR showed nominal between-group differences. In exploratory stem-geometry analyses, postoperative between-group differences in mLDFA and FMMA remained significant after FDR adjustment, whereas aLDFA and HKA showed nominal differences only. Clinical outcomes improved substantially in both Crowe strata, and clinical scores were comparable across femoral stem geometry groups.ConclusionsIn unilateral DDH-related osteoarthritis treated with THA, postoperative lower-limb alignment changes were associated with Crowe severity and showed only partial normalization relative to the contralateral reference limb. High-grade DDH demonstrated a more valgus-oriented postoperative radiographic profile, whereas low-grade DDH showed smaller changes with partial convergence toward the contralateral side. Stem geometry was associated with different postoperative radiographic profiles in exploratory analyses; however, because stem selection was not randomized and likely reflected underlying femoral morphology and reconstruction requirements, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Radiographic differences were not accompanied by significant differences in mid-term clinical outcomes.Level of evidenceA level 3 retrospective cohort study.

Introduction

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) is a major cause of secondary hip osteoarthritis, and total hip arthroplasty (THA) is the definitive treatment in advanced disease. In dysplastic hips, THA is performed not only to relieve pain but also to restore hip biomechanics (hip center, femoral offset, and limb length), with the expectation of improving global lower-extremity alignment and function [1]. Because DDH-related deformity affects the entire kinetic chain, hip pathology and its reconstruction may have measurable downstream effects on ipsilateral knee alignment and loading [2–4]. Prior work has shown that DDH is frequently associated with valgus-oriented limb alignment and that structural changes at the hip can be accompanied by alterations in knee morphology and patellofemoral parameters [5–7].

Despite this recognized hip–knee interdependence, the effect of THA for DDH on ipsilateral knee and overall limb alignment remains inconsistently reported. Published data vary by dysplasia severity (including high-grade cases requiring femoral osteotomy), reconstruction strategy, and the radiographic metrics used to quantify coronal and rotational alignment; consequently, valgus alignment may persist, normalize, or even worsen after surgery [2, 3, 8–10]. Additionally, reconstruction-related factors such as hip center position and implant-related restoration of femoral mechanics may influence distal joint biomechanics and potentially knee symptoms or degenerative risk [11, 12]. A focused evaluation in unilateral DDH treated with unilateral THA, using standardized full-length standing radiographs and severity stratification, is therefore needed to clarify the direction and magnitude of postoperative changes at the knee and across the limb. Recent long-term evidence has also shown that Crowe IV DDH may demonstrate persistent changes in lower-limb axial alignment, gait biomechanics, and plantar force after THA [13].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate a predefined coronal radiographic alignment profile, including mLDFA, aLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA, GO, HKA, KMADR, and FMMA, before and after unilateral THA for DDH-related secondary osteoarthritis. We further sought to determine whether this radiographic alignment profile differed according to Crowe severity and femoral stem geometry, and whether radiographic changes were associated with clinical outcomes.

Methods

This retrospective, single-center, observational analytic cohort study included patients who underwent elective primary THA for secondary osteoarthritis due to DDH at our institution between 2013 and 2023, with a minimum 1-year follow-up. Following ethics approval, eligible patients were recalled for updated radiographic and clinical evaluation, and complete preoperative radiographic, clinical, and functional data were retrieved from institutional records. The study was approved by the Institutional Clinical Research Ethics Committee with the approval date and number of 20.03.2024–949,023. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (or legal representatives). The investigation adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, with no external funding and no conflicts of interest.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) native-level language proficiency and capacity to provide consent, (3) diagnosis of unilateral DDH-related secondary hip osteoarthritis, (4) treatment with unilateral primary THA, (5) anatomic hip center reconstruction, (6) postoperative follow-up ≥ 1 year, (7) complete preoperative radiographic and clinical records, and (8) ability to return for final clinical examination and updated radiographs. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior hip surgery for etiologies other than DDH, (2) revision THA, (3) follow-up < 1 year, (4) incomplete radiographic or clinical data, (5) non-anatomic hip center reconstruction, and (6) concomitant conditions likely to affect alignment or gait (e.g., pre-existing lower-extremity deformity, residual neurologic disease, or metabolic bone disease). DDH severity was graded using the Crowe classification, and patients were stratified a priori as low-grade (Crowe I–II) versus high-grade (Crowe III–IV).

All procedures were performed by the same dedicated arthroplasty team according to a standardized institutional protocol, with operative decision-making supervised by senior arthroplasty surgeons. With the patient in the lateral decubitus position, THA was performed through a posterior approach. Anatomic hip center reconstruction was defined as placement of the acetabular component within the true acetabulum, with avoidance of intentional high hip center reconstruction. The true acetabular floor and teardrop region were used as radiographic and intraoperative references. Offset restoration was assessed intraoperatively using trial reduction, soft-tissue tension, stability, leg length, and comparison with preoperative templating. After femoral head resection, the true acetabulum was prepared using sequential reaming. The acetabular component was implanted and secured with 2 to 3 screws when required; a highly cross-linked polyethylene liner was then inserted. Femoral preparation was performed using standard canal entry and sequential broaching, followed by trial reduction to confirm stability, range of motion, and leg length before implantation of the definitive components. The cases were reconstructed using a cementless femoral stem and a ceramic femoral head. When necessary to achieve concentric reduction without excessive lengthening, a subtrochanteric transverse femoral shortening osteotomy was performed. In osteotomy cases, autograft bone was applied at the osteotomy site and stabilized using cerclage cables to enhance rotational stability. Femoral stems were categorized by cross-sectional geometry as wedge, rectangular, or conical. No modular femoral stems were used in this cohort. Femoral stem geometry was selected intraoperatively according to proximal femoral morphology, canal geometry, metaphyseal-diaphyseal mismatch, version-control requirements, and the need to obtain stable cementless fixation. Conical stems were preferentially considered in patients with narrow or distorted canals, higher version-control requirements, or more complex proximal femoral anatomy, whereas wedge and rectangular stems were used when adequate metaphyseal or metaphyseal-diaphyseal fit and rotational stability could be achieved with these designs. Therefore, femoral stem geometry was regarded as a reconstruction-related variable reflecting underlying anatomy and surgical requirements rather than as a randomized exposure.

Patients began mobilization on postoperative day 1 with a walker and were allowed full weight bearing as tolerated. In patients who underwent femoral shortening osteotomy, the operated limb was kept non–weight bearing for 6 weeks. Deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis was initiated on the night of surgery using oral acetylsalicylic acid according to institutional protocol. Patients were followed clinically and radiographically at routine outpatient visits, and the final study assessment was performed at ≥ 1 year after surgery.

Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, operated side, time from surgery to final assessment, comorbidities, and postoperative complications. Pain and function were assessed using validated native versions of the following instruments: hip and knee visual analog scale (VAS), Harris Hip Score (HHS), Knee Society Score (KSS), Oxford Knee Score (OKS), KOOS, and WOMAC. Preoperative scores were obtained from medical records and preoperative examination notes, supplemented by patient interview when required. Postoperative scores were collected during the final in-person evaluation.

Radiographic assessment included anteroposterior pelvis radiographs and standardized standing long-leg (hip–knee–ankle)/leg-length radiographs obtained preoperatively and at final follow-up. To ensure accurate scaling, a calibration object of known dimensions was used. All measurements were performed digitally using PACS tools on calibrated images. Radiographic parameters were measured preoperatively and postoperatively on the operated limb, including hip–knee–ankle angle (HKA), knee mechanical axis deviation ratio (KMADR), mechanical lateral distal femoral angle (mLDFA), anatomic lateral distal femoral angle (aLDFA), mechanical medial proximal tibial angle (mMPTA), joint line convergence angle (JLCA), femoral mechanical–anatomic axis angle (FMMA), and global offset (GO) (Fig. 1). Pelvic obliquity and leg-length discrepancy were additionally recorded as ancillary reconstruction- and posture-related radiographic variables, but they were not included in the predefined primary radiographic outcome set to preserve the focus on coronal lower-limb alignment parameters. For HKA, positive values indicated valgus alignment and negative values indicated varus alignment. For JLCA, positive values indicated varus-type joint-line convergence, whereas negative values indicated valgus-type joint-line convergence. KMADR was calculated as the relative position of the mechanical axis across the tibial plateau, with negative values indicating medial deviation and positive values indicating lateral deviation from the reference midpoint (Fig. 1). The contralateral non-operated limb was used as an internal radiographic reference for side-to-side comparison rather than as an external healthy control. Contralateral reference-limb measurements were obtained using the same radiographic protocol and measurement definitions. All radiographic measurements were performed independently by two observers using PACS. Interobserver agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC point estimates indicated excellent interobserver reliability for all radiographic parameters, with values of 0.96 (95% CI 0.92–0.98) for HKA, 0.93 (95% CI 0.87–0.96) for KMADR, 0.93 (95% CI 0.88–0.96) for mLDFA, 0.95 (95% CI 0.90–0.97) for aLDFA, 0.94 (95% CI 0.88–0.97) for mMPTA, 0.96 (95% CI 0.93–0.98) for JLCA, 0.95 (95% CI 0.90–0.97) for FMMA, and 0.94 (95% CI 0.90–0.96) for GO. The overall interobserver ICC was 0.95 (95% CI 0.88–0.97), indicating excellent overall measurement reproducibility.

The predefined primary radiographic outcome set included mLDFA, aLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA, GO, HKA, KMADR, and FMMA, because no single coronal-plane measurement fully captures the combined femoral, tibial, joint-line, hip-offset, and global mechanical-axis changes that may occur after THA for DDH. These parameters were analyzed as a radiographic alignment profile rather than as isolated competing endpoints. Preoperative-to-postoperative changes, final follow-up values, and side-to-side differences relative to the contralateral reference limb were assessed for each parameter. Clinical outcome scores and femoral stem geometry–based subgroup analyses were considered secondary/exploratory analyses.

Statistical analysis

A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). With α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and an assumed medium effect size of 0.50, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 34 patients. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate, and categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Distributional assumptions were evaluated using descriptive normality assessment, including skewness and kurtosis, and formal normality testing where appropriate. Within-patient preoperative-to-postoperative comparisons were performed using the paired-samples t-test for normally distributed variables or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons, including Crowe I–II versus Crowe III–IV, and one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons involving three or more femoral stem geometry groups. When an overall difference was detected among three or more groups, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with adjustment for multiple testing, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Associations between continuous variables were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients according to data distribution. Because the primary radiographic outcome was defined as a predefined alignment-parameter set rather than a single isolated measurement, multiplicity was addressed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Adjusted q values were calculated within each prespecified radiographic comparison family, including within-group preoperative-to-postoperative comparisons, side-to-side comparisons with the contralateral reference limb, and between-group comparisons according to Crowe classification or femoral stem geometry. Unadjusted p values are reported together with adjusted q values where applicable. Results with q < 0.05 were considered statistically significant after multiplicity adjustment. Findings with unadjusted p < 0.05 but q ≥ 0.05 were interpreted as nominal associations rather than definitive statistically significant findings. Exploratory multivariable linear regression models were constructed to evaluate whether Crowe classification and femoral stem geometry were associated with selected postoperative alignment parameters, including postoperative HKA, mMPTA, FMMA, and KMADR, after adjustment for age, sex, and the corresponding baseline radiographic value. In these models, Crowe I–II, rectangular stem geometry, and female sex were used as reference categories. The two patients with non-modular stem designs outside the predefined wedge, rectangular, or conical geometry categories were excluded from stem-geometry regression models. Results are reported as unstandardized regression coefficients (B), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p values. Because femoral stem geometry was not randomly assigned and was selected according to underlying femoral morphology, canal geometry, reconstruction requirements, and surgeon judgment, stem-related findings were interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance after multiplicity adjustment was defined as q < 0.05 for radiographic outcome analyses and p < 0.05 for non-radiographic analyses.

Radiographic measurements according to Crowe classification

Radiographic alignment parameters stratified by Crowe classification are summarized in Table 2. In Crowe I–II patients, several preoperative-to-postoperative changes were nominally observed in mLDFA, aLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA, GO, HKA, and KMADR; however, these changes did not remain statistically significant after FDR adjustment. Preoperative side-to-side differences versus the contralateral reference limb remained significant after FDR adjustment for mLDFA, aLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA, GO, and HKA, whereas postoperative side-to-side differences no longer remained significant after multiplicity adjustment. This pattern suggested partial postoperative convergence toward the contralateral reference limb in low-grade DDH. In Crowe III–IV patients, the valgus-oriented postoperative alignment profile was more robust. Preoperative-to-postoperative changes in mLDFA, aLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA, GO, HKA, and FMMA remained significant after FDR adjustment, whereas KMADR showed a nominal change. Postoperative side-to-side differences versus the contralateral reference limb persisted after FDR adjustment for mLDFA, aLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA, HKA, KMADR, and FMMA, whereas GO was not significantly different from the contralateral reference limb at final follow-up. Between Crowe groups, postoperative HKA and FMMA remained significantly different after FDR adjustment. Postoperative mMPTA and KMADR showed nominal between-group differences but did not remain statistically significant after multiplicity adjustment. No significant between-group differences were observed in contralateral reference-limb measurements after FDR adjustment (Table 2). Ancillary analysis of reconstruction- and posture-related variables showed no significant preoperative-to-postoperative change in pelvic obliquity or leg-length discrepancy within the unilateral THA cohort. Pelvic obliquity was 2.69 ± 2.04° preoperatively and 2.64 ± 2.12° postoperatively (p = 0.791), whereas leg-length discrepancy was 1.39 ± 1.42 cm preoperatively and 1.56 ± 1.33 cm postoperatively (p = 0.191). These variables were therefore considered potential contextual factors rather than primary radiographic endpoints and were not tabulated separately to avoid overloading the main results tables.

Radiographic measurements stratified by femoral stem geometry

Exploratory radiographic analyses according to femoral stem geometry are summarized in Table 3. Preoperatively, radiographic parameters were comparable across femoral stem geometry groups after FDR adjustment. At final follow-up, exploratory between-group differences in mLDFA and FMMA remained significant after FDR adjustment, whereas aLDFA and HKA showed nominal between-group differences only. In within-stem analyses, the conical stem group demonstrated FDR-significant preoperative-to-postoperative changes in mLDFA, aLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA, GO, HKA, and FMMA. In the wedge stem group, the decrease in GO was nominal after FDR adjustment. In the rectangular stem group, aLDFA and GO remained significant after FDR adjustment, whereas JLCA showed a nominal change. Because stem geometry was not randomly assigned, these findings were interpreted as exploratory associations rather than causal effects (Table 3).

Exploratory adjusted multivariable analyses

Exploratory multivariable linear regression models are summarized in Table 4. After adjustment for age, sex, femoral stem geometry, and the corresponding baseline radiographic value, Crowe III–IV was not independently associated with postoperative HKA, postoperative mMPTA, postoperative FMMA, or postoperative KMADR. Wedge stems were not independently associated with any postoperative alignment parameter. These findings support interpreting the stem-geometry results as exploratory associations that may reflect underlying femoral morphology and reconstruction requirements rather than causal effects.

Associations between radiographic alignment and clinical outcomes

Exploratory correlation analyses between postoperative radiographic alignment parameters and final clinical outcomes demonstrated only isolated weak associations and no consistent clinically meaningful correlation pattern. Postoperative mLDFA showed a weak positive correlation with postoperative Knee Society Score (r = 0.243, p = 0.037), and postoperative mMPTA showed a weak negative correlation with postoperative knee VAS (r = −0.275, p = 0.018). Postoperative HKA showed a weak negative correlation with postoperative Harris Hip Score (r = −0.253, p = 0.029). However, postoperative KMADR and FMMA were not significantly correlated with hip VAS, knee VAS, Harris Hip Score, Knee Society Score, Oxford Knee Score, KOOS, or WOMAC. Overall, these exploratory findings suggest that the observed postoperative radiographic alignment differences were not accompanied by a consistent deterioration in patient-reported or clinician-reported mid-term hip and knee outcomes.

Discussion

In this unilateral DDH cohort undergoing THA, we observed a severity-dependent postoperative shift toward valgus-oriented alignment that remained incompletely normalized relative to the contralateral reference limb. In Crowe III–IV patients, the pattern of decreased mLDFA/aLDFA, increased mMPTA, a more negative JLCA, increased HKA, and decreased FMMA indicated a valgus-oriented postoperative radiographic profile and persistent postoperative asymmetry. By contrast, Crowe I–II patients demonstrated smaller changes with partial convergence toward the contralateral reference limb. Exploratory stem-geometry analyses showed different postoperative radiographic profiles; however, these associations were attenuated after adjustment and should be interpreted as reflecting underlying femoral morphology and reconstruction requirements rather than a direct effect of stem design. Several prior studies have emphasized that THA for high-grade DDH is technically demanding and that residual coronal malalignment at the knee can persist after surgery [14]. Clinical series have reported ongoing or symptomatic genu valgum after THA in dysplastic hips, particularly in severe cases [8, 10, 15]. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating a severity-dependent valgus trajectory on standing long-leg radiographs: while lower-grade dysplasia (Crowe I–II) tended to show partial convergence toward the contralateral reference limb, high-grade dysplasia (Crowe III–IV) showed a radiographic pattern consistent with increased valgus orientation and persistent asymmetry across multiple indices. Importantly, this valgus-oriented radiographic evolution did not translate into inferior mid-term clinical scores in our cohort, as hip and knee pain and functional outcomes improved substantially after THA. The valgus-oriented pattern in high-grade DDH is biologically plausible given the well-described structural and developmental changes in the femur and tibia associated with dysplasia. Comparative and morphologic studies have shown that DDH is frequently associated with ipsilateral valgus alignment and characteristic knee morphology, with severity-dependent changes in distal femoral geometry and proximal tibial orientation [5–7, 16–18]. In this context, coronal alignment descriptors such as LDFA and MPTA represent robust indicators of bony contribution to valgus alignment: lower distal femoral angles and higher proximal tibial angles align with a valgus phenotype [19, 20]. Kandemir et al. highlighted that neglected DDH can be associated with reduced LDFA and increased MPTA, supporting a structural basis for valgus alignment even in the absence of predominant ligamentous or chondral pathology [7]. Similarly, other investigations have reinforced that valgus deformity in DDH can arise not solely from hip mechanics but also from distal femoral morphology and tibial orientation [5–7, 10, 17]. In addition, limb lengthening–related iliotibial band tension and postoperative postural adaptations secondary to hip reconstruction have been proposed as contributors to valgus-oriented knee alignment after THA [7, 8, 10, 21]. Our results are consistent with these observations and suggest that the pre-existing bony and soft-tissue envelope in Crowe III–IV may predispose patients to a postoperative valgus-oriented alignment pattern even after restoration of the anatomic hip center. Our findings also relate to the heterogeneous literature on how THA affects ipsilateral knee alignment in severe dysplasia. Kılıçarslan et al. reported painful valgus deformity and early postoperative knee symptoms after THA in Crowe III–IV DDH, linking limb lengthening and periarticular tension to postoperative complaints [8]. In contrast, Kocabıyık et al. found coronal alignment changes after THA with transverse femoral shortening osteotomy in Crowe IV hips and suggested that reconstructing hip anatomy and modifying offset can contribute to distal alignment changes [2]. Other retrospective series have similarly demonstrated significant postoperative changes in HKA in Crowe IV dysplasia, with the direction and magnitude varying by cohort and reconstruction method [12]. Sun et al. evaluated factors associated with postoperative valgus alignment in Crowe IV and emphasized the importance of distal femoral alignment metrics, while reporting that limb lengthening and offset restoration did not independently predict valgus in their multivariable model [10]. More recently, Chen et al. evaluated Crowe IV DDH patients with a mean 10-year follow-up and reported that THA partially corrected abnormal lower-limb axial alignment, gait biomechanics, and plantar force distribution, while residual abnormalities persisted in some alignment and functional parameters [13]. Their findings are consistent with our observation that THA improves clinical outcomes but does not necessarily achieve complete radiographic normalization, particularly in high-grade dysplasia. In our study, the consistency of a valgus-oriented pattern in Crowe III–IV—captured by simultaneous shifts in distal femoral angles, proximal tibial angles, joint line convergence, and global axis measures— supports the concept that preoperative dysplastic morphology and severity may be important contributors to postoperative coronal phenotype, even when anatomic hip center reconstruction is achieved. A notable contribution of the present work is the exploratory evaluation of femoral stem geometry within a coronal alignment framework. While the relationship between reconstruction parameters, including offset and hip center position, and distal alignment has been discussed previously, the stem-design dimension has been less frequently addressed in a coronal alignment framework [5, 7, 10, 22]. In the unadjusted and FDR-adjusted subgroup analyses, stem geometry was associated with different postoperative radiographic profiles, most notably for mLDFA and FMMA; however, these findings were attenuated in the adjusted models, in which conical stem geometry remained associated only with lower postoperative FMMA. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that stem geometry directly determines postoperative valgus alignment; rather, stem choice may represent a surrogate for underlying proximal femoral morphology, version, canal configuration, and reconstruction complexity. Importantly, ancillary analyses did not show significant preoperative-to-postoperative changes in pelvic obliquity or leg-length discrepancy within the unilateral cohort, suggesting that the observed coronal alignment patterns were not simply explained by gross changes in these two radiographic variables. Nevertheless, pelvic posture, residual hip adduction, soft-tissue tension, and limb-length restoration remain biologically plausible contributors to postoperative alignment and should be evaluated more comprehensively in future studies. This interpretation is supported by recent EOS-based morphologic data from Gharanizadeh et al., who showed that Crowe IV hips have a shorter greater trochanter height, a more distally located lesser trochanter, and a narrower canal at the upper border of the lesser trochanter compared with normal hips [23]. These anatomic differences may affect neck-cut level, canal preparation, and the feasibility of wedge-stem fixation; therefore, our observed stem-geometry associations are more appropriately interpreted as reflecting femoral morphology and reconstruction demands rather than a direct valgus-producing effect of the stem design. Prior work has cautioned that excessive offset strategies may carry trade-offs, including implant-related risks in certain constructs [24]. Additionally, studies emphasizing reconstruction strategy, including hip center position, suggest that surgical reconstruction variables may be associated with distal biomechanics and knee function [11, 25, 26]. Future prospective work integrating detailed reconstruction metrics, including true acetabular offset, femoral offset, global offset restoration, implant design features, 3-dimensional femoral morphology, version, and gait analysis, would help clarify whether implant geometry has any independent biomechanical role beyond its association with case complexity and reconstruction demands. Finally, the apparent dissociation between radiographic valgus-oriented patterns and mid-term clinical outcomes merits emphasis. Although postoperative alignment in Crowe III–IV remained more valgus-oriented relative to the contralateral reference limb, pain and functional scores improved substantially and were largely comparable across Crowe strata, with the exception of a higher postoperative Harris Hip Score in the Crowe III–IV group. This finding aligns with prior work showing that THA reliably improves pain and function in DDH even when biomechanical parameters do not fully normalize [27]. However, the absence of inferior mid-term clinical scores should not be interpreted as proof that persistent valgus-oriented alignment is clinically benign. Nevertheless, whether persistent valgus-oriented mechanics predispose to longer-term knee symptoms or osteoarthritic progression remains unknown and should be evaluated in longer follow-up studies [1]. This study has several strengths, including a unilateral DDH cohort treated with a standardized surgical approach, evaluation with calibrated standing full-length radiographs, and use of the contralateral non-operated reference limb as an internal radiographic reference for side-to-side comparison. Stratification by Crowe severity and femoral stem geometry, along with assessment of both radiographic and validated clinical outcomes, provides a comprehensive view of alignment trajectories after THA. To our knowledge, this is among the first reports to stratify alignment outcomes by femoral stem geometry and to provide detailed clinical outcomes in unilateral DDH THA. Limitations include the retrospective, single-center design, modest sample size—particularly in subgroup and stem-geometry analyses—and heterogeneous follow-up duration. Although standing full-length radiographs allowed standardized coronal-plane assessment, the study did not incorporate 3-dimensional imaging, femoral version, stem version, tibiofemoral rotation, or dynamic gait biomechanics. Pelvic obliquity and leg-length discrepancy were recorded as ancillary radiographic variables and did not show significant preoperative-to-postoperative changes within the unilateral cohort; however, they were not incorporated into the primary adjusted models to avoid overfitting and because they were not part of the predefined primary radiographic outcome set. Residual hip adduction contracture, preoperative or postoperative flexion deformities of the hip, knee, or ankle, sagittal pelvic tilt, soft-tissue tension, and dynamic pelvic compensation were not comprehensively quantified and may have influenced apparent coronal alignment. Similarly, leg-length restoration, version correction, offset reconstruction, and the use of subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy (STSO) could not be fully disentangled from Crowe severity, femoral morphology, and stem selection. Because STSO was closely linked to high-grade dysplasia and reconstruction complexity, it was not included as an additional covariate in the adjusted models to avoid collinearity and overfitting in this modest-sized cohort. The contralateral limb was used as an internal radiographic reference rather than as a verified external healthy control; therefore, subtle contralateral dysplasia, early degenerative change, or subclinical knee osteoarthritis cannot be completely excluded. In addition, clinically meaningful thresholds for small changes in mLDFA, mMPTA, JLCA, KMADR, FMMA, and GO after THA for DDH are not well established. Finally, the follow-up reflects mid-term outcomes; longer-term surveillance is needed to determine whether persistent valgus-oriented radiographic patterns translate into clinically meaningful knee symptoms or degenerative progression.

Conclusion

Postoperative lower-limb alignment changes after unilateral THA for DDH-related secondary osteoarthritis were severity-dependent and only partially normalized relative to the contralateral reference limb. High-grade DDH (Crowe III–IV) demonstrated a postoperative radiographic profile consistent with greater valgus orientation and persistent side-to-side asymmetry, whereas low-grade DDH (Crowe I–II) showed smaller changes with partial convergence toward the contralateral reference limb. Femoral stem geometry was associated with different postoperative radiographic profiles in exploratory analyses; however, adjusted models suggested that most stem-related associations were attenuated after accounting for baseline alignment and patient factors. Therefore, stem-geometry findings should be interpreted as associative and potentially reflective of underlying femoral morphology and reconstruction complexity rather than causal. Radiographic differences were not accompanied by inferior mid-term clinical outcomes, as pain and functional scores improved substantially and were largely comparable across Crowe strata and stem geometries. These findings support alignment-aware preoperative planning in DDH, particularly in high-grade deformity, while emphasizing the need for longer-term studies incorporating 3-dimensional morphology, version, offset restoration, and gait analysis.
