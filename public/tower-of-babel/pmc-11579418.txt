FPGA-based low-light image enhancement using Retinex algorithm and coarse-grained reconfigurable architecture
Authors: S Munaf, A Bharathi, A N Jayanthi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvancements in digital imaging and video processing are often challenged by low-light environments, leading to degraded visual quality. This affects critical sectors such as medical imaging, aerospace, and underwater exploration, where uneven lighting can compromise safety and clarity. To enhance image quality in low-light conditions using a computationally efficient system. This paper introduces an FPGA-based system utilizing the Retinex algorithm for low-light image enhancement, implemented on a Coarse-Grained Reconfigurable Architecture (CGRA). The system is designed using Verilog HDL on a Xilinx FPGA, prioritizing hardware optimization to achieve high-quality outputs with minimal latency. The system achieves a processing rate of 60 frames per second (fps) for images with a resolution of 720 × 576. Quantitative evaluations show a Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) improvement to 43.18 dB, a Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) of 0.92, and a Mean Squared Error (MSE) reduction, demonstrating significant enhancements in image quality. The design also achieves a low power consumption of 0.186 W and efficient resource utilization, with only 2.2% of Slice LUTs and Slice Registers used. The FPGA-based system demonstrates significant improvements in image quality with high computational efficiency, proving beneficial for critical applications in various sectors.Keywords: CGRA, FPGA, Low-light enhancement, Retinex, Image processing, Gaussian filterSubject terms: Health care, Engineering

Introduction

The Retinex theory1 serves as a basis for understanding and improving image quality under varying lighting conditions. It separates an image into the product of illumination and reflectance components. This helps in enhancing images where lighting is uneven or insufficient. This theory is especially relevant in the context of advanced digital camera technologies and integrated chip systems, which have pushed the boundaries of research in image processing.

The advent of High Dynamic Range Images (HDRI)2 marks a significant advancement in this field. HDRI allows for a more dynamic presentation of visual information, overcoming the limitations of traditional digital cameras. These typically exhibit a dynamic range of 212 dB per channel, which often gets reduced to about 28 dB due to system constraints. In contrast, the human eye can process a much broader dynamic range in real-time, estimated between 120 to 180 dB.

To bridge the gap between human visual perception and digital imaging, various adaptive methods have been developed3. These methods enhance the clarity, color, and visibility of images under diverse illumination levels. For instance, algorithms like the Retinex are used to adjust the brightness and contrast of images to mimic the human eye’s ability to perceive details in both brightly lit and shadowed areas.

Non-uniform illumination remains a challenge in image processing, particularly in conditions like backlighting4 or presence of bright sources in dark settings. This often results in loss of detail due to overexposure or underexposure in parts of the image. The Retinex technique addresses these issues by simplifying the enhancement process through effective use of algorithms that manipulate pixel data on both a global scale (considering the overall image structure)5 and a local scale (focusing on pixel-level details), thus maintaining image integrity and detail.

The implementation of Retinex-based models and algorithms has revolutionized the field of image synthesis, allowing for more accurate simulations of light distribution and realistic rendering of scenes. This not only improves the practical visual appearance of images but also ensures that they closely resemble the modeled scenes, irrespective of the lighting conditions.

Related work

The need to enhance image brightness and contrast is a crucial aspect of both human and machine vision, driven by the need to optimize visual data in varying light conditions. Various techniques, such as gamma correction, histogram equalization, and tone mapping, have been proposed and developed over the years to address these challenges effectively.

Wang and Zhang6 presents a global attention related Retinex network for enhancing low-light images. The technique incorporates a novel global attention module into the Retinex framework to capture channel and spatial attention, significantly improving feature extraction from images captured in poor lighting. Evaluation metrics are used, with GARN showing improvements over existing methods, for instance, achieving a PSNR of 21.48, SSIM of 0.88, NIQE of 3.87, and Entropy of 7.51 on one of the datasets. The main advantage of this method is its ability to effectively recover details in dark regions and reduce colour distortions, which are common issues in other low-light enhancement techniques. However, the complexity introduced by the global attention mechanism might impact the computational efficiency, represents a challenge for real-time applications.

With the use of a noise map, the Retinex model for low-light image enhancement proposed by Li et al.7 is able to better handle intense noise in dark images. This method effectively solves problems by using an optimization function that incorporates new regularization terms for the illumination and reflectance components and by using an alternating direction minimization algorithm based on augmented Lagrange multipliers, which does not use logarithmic transformation. This technology has the potential to improve low-light photos by reducing intense noise while maintaining fine-grained structures. It could also be used to enhance underwater or remote sensing images.

A new approach to low-light picture enhancement, CA&R Net, was introduced by Wang et al.8. It combines the attention mechanism with the Retinex model to improve images by addressing issues like underexposure, colour distortion, and noise. The methodology depends on developing an attention map that guides the enhancement process adaptively, focusing more on underexposed areas without over-amplifying properly exposed regions. It also jointly predicts the illumination layers of images, which helps in achieving better results. The main merit of this approach is its advanced handling of underexposure and noise, significantly improving image quality. The complexity might impact the model’s computational efficiency, which could be a potential drawback for real-time applications.

A Retinex-based network for low-light picture augmentation that incorporates a Decom-Net, Denoise-Net, and Relight-Net is introduced by Hai et al.9 called R2RNet. The aim of these subnets is to improve contrast and brightness in images, decrease noise, and decompose images into illumination and reflectance maps, respectively. In order to maintain details while reducing noise, the network makes use of both spatial and frequency information. Notable for its improved generalizability to current situations, it was trained using a newly-created dataset of real-world low/normal-light image pairs. The paper evaluates the network using PSNR and SSIM metrics, where R2RNet shows superior performance. A key advantage of this approach is its robustness in enhancing visual quality and facilitating high-level visual tasks like face detection.

A Variational Retinex Model with Structure-Awareness Regularization is presented by Zhang et al.10 as a means of improving low-light images. By adding a structure-awareness regularization term to the classic total variational Retinex model, this model-based method improves the interplay between global spatial smoothness and feature recovery. Unlike learning-based methods, this model does not rely on extensive training data or architecture engineering, making it robust and generalizable across various conditions without the need for super-parameter tuning. For instance, the PSNR and SSIM values show significant improvements, indicating enhanced image quality and detail preservation. The primary advantage of this approach is its flexibility and effectiveness, allowing deployment as a pre-processing subroutine for higher-level computer vision applications.

Duong et al.11 discusses DMT-Net, an advanced approach for enhancing low-light images using a deep multiple networks architecture based on the Retinex model. This method operates within the YCbCr color space, deploying four distinct sub-networks. Each designed to mitigate specific enhancement tasks such as noise reduction, illumination boost, and color distortion correction. The evaluation metrics used to assess the performance include PSNR, SSIM, and no-reference metrics like NIQE, with notable improvements over other existing methods. The proposed approach has demonstrated efficacy in producing visually pleasing enhancements while preserving natural color and detail, highlighting its potential superiority in handling low-light conditions effectively.

In their paper on Retinex-Based Low-Light Image Enhancement, Park et al.12 present a Dual Autoencoder Network that uses layered and convolutional autoencoders to improve low-light images. This network addresses both brightness enhancement and noise reduction simultaneously. This method uses a convolutional autoencoder to deal with reflectance noise reduction and a stacked autoencoder to estimate a spatially smooth illumination component. Performance evaluation involves comparative assessments with existing methods using metrics such as PSNR and SSIM. The proposed model shows improvements in enhancing image visibility without introducing noise, making it beneficial for applications like surveillance and remote sensing.

Huang et al.13 presents a low-light image enhancement network called Retinex with attention network, which utilizes both the Retinex model and an attention mechanism. This method allows for specific improvement by first estimating the input image’s illumination mask. The network uses an attention mechanism to enhance feature extraction, improving the prediction of illumination maps. The enhanced images are then obtained by modifying color distortions and suppressing noise through convolutional layers. The effectiveness of the method is demonstrated using different metrics, showing significant improvements over existing methods in handling uneven lighting and maintaining color fidelity. The main advantages include better handling of localized light variations and enhanced detail preservation. However, the complexity of the model and potential computational demands might limit its real-time application capabilities.

In order to improve low-light images, Zhang and Wang14 present the Multi-Scale Attention Retinex Network (MARN). This network uses a multi-scale attention module in conjunction with a novel illuminance-attention map to forecast a comprehensive inverse illumination map, which in turn greatly improves picture quality in a variety of lighting scenarios. This method enhances image visibility and detail by combining local and global elements. MARN is evaluated using multiple metrics including PSNR, SSIM, and colorfulness index, achieving superior performance compared to existing methods. The primary merits of MARN include its ability to handle images with complex lighting, preserve natural colours, and maintain image details. However, its potential demerits may involve computational complexity due to the multi-scale processing and the advanced architecture that might affect real-time processing capabilities. Additionally, the method’s performance is highly reliant on the effective training of its attention mechanisms, which can be challenging and resource intensive.

In order to improve low-light images in a way that is more natural and consistent with human vision, Chen et al.15 present an attention-based Retinex network that uses deep learning. This method trains and decomposes illumination and reflectance maps using paired low-light and normal-light images. An attention mechanism is then applied during the convolution process to refine the illumination adjustment, enhancing image brightness, reducing noise, and improving color reproduction. In order to measure the efficacy of the network, measurable standards like PSNR and SSIM are used. To give only two examples, it outperforms various methodologies with a PSNR of 22.5792 and an SSIM of 0.9063. The main benefits of this method include less noise, improved brightness and color accuracy, and improved handling of low-light images. But, the method’s generalizability to varied real-world situations may be affected if paired training data is not available, which can limit its application in such situations.

Lv et al.16 introduces an innovative attention-guided enhancement approach that effectively addresses the challenges of low-light image enhancement through a multi-branch CNN. This technique utilizes two types of attention maps—one for underexposed areas and another for noise detection—to guide the enhancement process, ensuring more precise brightness adjustments and effective denoising. A novel large-scale synthetic low-light dataset is useful for training and testing the approach because it gives a variety of low-light situations. Outperforming the state-of-the-art on several datasets in terms of both quantitative measurements and visual quality is one of the significant achievements. An outstanding PSNR of 25.24 and SSIM of 0.94 were shown by the method. The suggested network is advanced, and it uses synthetic data for training, which would make it unsuitable for use in the actual world without such controlled settings. Additionally, the computational demands of handling multiple attention-guided processes concurrently could pose challenges for real-time applications.

Retinex Decomposition and Multi-scale Adjustment (RDMA) is a low-light picture improvement approach introduced by Li et al.17. Using a framework that allows for multi-scale adjustments to an image’s reflectance and lighting, this method combines a feature interaction module with a feature fusion module. By enhancing color integrity, detail visibility, and noise reduction, this technique greatly improves the decomposition and enhancement quality. RDMA outperforms state-of-the-art approaches in common measures like PSNR and SSIM, with remarkable outcomes (e.g., PSNR of 23.92 and SSIM of 0.847). The pros lie in its robust handling of various image degradation issues and superior performance on qualitative and quantitative grounds. However, its complex multi-scale processing might hinder its computational efficiency, potentially limiting real-time application use.

The three-stage deep learning-based architecture for low-light image improvement proposed by Lv et al.18 uses bilateral learning and Retinex decomposition to improve fusion refinement, attention-guided illumination adjustment, and reflectance and illumination decomposition. The approach utilizes bilateral grids for decomposition, integrating attention mechanisms to preserve edge details while adjusting illumination. The network further refines results through adversarial learning to enhance naturalness and authenticity. Performance is evaluated using PSNR, SSIM, and a color difference metric (ΔE*), with the method achieving robust scores, such as PSNR of 20.56 and SSIM of 0.86 on the SICE* dataset. This indicates superior handling of color fidelity and detail retention compared to existing methods. The multi-stage process, while effective in improving image quality under varied lighting conditions, might be computationally intensive, potentially limiting its real-time application.

To increase image quality in low-light conditions, Wang et al.19 introduce an optimization approach for low-light image enhancement that combines frame accumulation with multi-scale Retinex processing. The technique focuses on increasing the structural similarity index (SSIM) and peak to signal noise ratio of images, achieving a PSNR increase to 51.2041 dB from the original 35.9408 dB and an SSIM enhancement to 0.75. The method’s strength lies in its ability to preserve more image details and edges, enhance grayscale resolution, and reduce color distortion and aperture artifacts. However, the complexity of the process involving frame accumulation and multi-scale adjustments could potentially limit its application in real-time processing scenarios, where faster and less computationally intensive solutions might be preferable.

A novel low-light image enhancement method is proposed in20 via Retinex-inline cycle-consistent generative adversarial network named Cycle-Retinex, whose training is totally dependent on unpaired datasets. Specifically, Retinex theory with CycleGAN is combined, by which low-light image enhancement task is decoupled into two sub-tasks, i.e. illumination map enhancement and reflectance map restoration. Retinex theory helps CycleGAN simplify low-light image enhancement problem and CycleGAN provides synthetic paired images to guide the training of Retinex decomposition network. a self-augmented method is introduced to address the color distortion and noise problem, thus making the network learn to enhance low-light images adaptively. Extensive experiments show that this method can achieve promising results.

A MER-Retinex (Multiscale Expansion Reconstruction Retinex) algorithm is proposed in21 that integrates attention mechanism and multi-scale expansion pyramid reconstruction. It includes two parts: decomposition and enhancement module. In the decomposition module, two U-shaped networks are used to decompose the image into reflectance and illumination, then, use multi-layer convolution to expand the field of perception and improve the ability to decompose the image to obtain reflectance and illumination. In the enhancement module, a U-shaped network is used to fuse multi-scale expansion pyramids with a multi-attention mechanism to enrich image information, increase image brightness, and fuse the processed global information with local information to enhance the recovered image details. In the enhanced reconstruction section super-resolution techniques are used to enhance and denoise image feature details. Experimental analysis of the MER-Retinex algorithm was carried out on the LOL dataset. The PSNR of the algorithm in this paper was 25.26 and the NIQE was 3.43. The algorithm in this paper can effectively solve the problems of blurred images and poor visual effects, and has improved in both subjective perception and objective evaluation indexes.

A robust Retinex-based model is proposed in22 with reflectance map re-weighting that can improve the brightness level of the low-light image and re-balance the brightness concurrently. An alternating scheme is proposed to solve this model, in which the illumination map, reflectance map, and weighting map are updated iteratively. By utilizing the regularization terms, the noise is well-suppressed during the process. An initialization scheme for the weighting map is also proposed to make model adaptable to a wide range of light conditions. A variational model is introduced with an explicitly constructed re-weighting prior and the associated weighing map concept for the reflectance map. It can estimate the reflectance map, suppress noise, and re-balance the brightness simultaneously. A series of experimental results on a variety of popular datasets demonstrate the efficacy of method and its superiority in enhancing real low-light images when compared to other state-of-the-art methods.

A new method Maximum Decomposition Diffusion Enhancement (MDDE) for low light image enhancement is proposed in23. This method combines the advantages of Retinex theory and diffusion models, making the model physically interpretable and improving the contrast of low light images, making the images clearer. This method is divided into two modules: Maximum Value Decomposition Module and Diffusion Enhancement Module. The Maximum Value Decomposition Module is mainly based on Retinex theory and decomposes the image into illumination and reflection maps based on the maximum value of the three channels of the image. The Diffusion Enhancement Module is divided into two modules: Enhanced Illumination Diffusion Module and Final Enhanced Diffusion Module. This module mainly implements the image processing of low light images based on the diffusion probability model. The experiment shows that this method produces clearer images compared to other methods.

The Retinex method primarily involves estimation and normalization phases to refine image quality. An enhanced multi-scale global Retinex algorithm proposes using varied Gaussian filters to systematically calculate and fuse background information from each frame, leading to a more accurate and detailed enhancement.

Research gap

In spite of advancements in image processing techniques, many of the current solutions are either computationally intensive or limited in their effectiveness when applied to real-time systems. Traditional software-based implementations of low-light image enhancement algorithms, such as those utilizing the Retinex theory, are often too slow for real-time applications due to the high computational cost of operations like Gaussian filtering and image decomposition. Additionally, many methods rely on general-purpose processors or GPUs, which, while powerful, lack the energy efficiency and flexibility needed for embedded or portable applications. FPGA-based implementations have been explored as a potential solution due to their ability to perform parallel processing and their reconfigurability, but many existing FPGA-based systems still suffer from high power consumption or are unable to handle the complex operations required for effective image enhancement. Moreover, while Coarse-Grained Reconfigurable Architectures (CGRA) have been proposed for various tasks, their specific benefits in low-light image enhancement have not been fully realized or explored.

Proposed approach

This paper aims to address these gaps by introducing an FPGA-based low-light image enhancement system that combines the Retinex algorithm with a CGRA architecture. The system is designed to balance computational complexity, power consumption, and image quality, offering a real-time solution for environments with poor illumination. The key innovations of this work include the use of a low-complexity Gaussian smoothing filter and a memory-efficient line buffer architecture, which together enable high-performance processing with minimal resource usage.

Proposed work

Visual data is captured in real-time through transmission cables or digital sensors, which might introduce unwanted signal noise, potentially corrupting the image data pixels. To counteract this noise, a filtering process is implemented. The results from these filters are typically displayed in matrix format, utilizing both the spatial and frequency domains, with the spatial domain often being preferred for its simplicity and effectiveness. The Retinex model, which describes human color perception through the interaction of light sources and object reflectance, utilizes these principles. It enhances dimly lit images by estimating the correct illumination or reflectance from degraded images using a variety of filtering techniques. One widely used method in this approach is Gaussian filtering, which traditionally involves integer operations such as multiplications and additions to produce a pixel output that represents a weighted average of surrounding pixel values. This process, however, demands significant memory and hardware resources due to its complexity.

In this newly proposed approach, the Retinex Algorithm is applied to enhance the darker regions of an image and is implemented on a cost-effective FPGA. This allows the process to operate in real-time while preserving the quality of the image. Nonetheless, the substantial resource consumption associated with this method does lead to some performance degradation in FPGA implementations.

Utilizing a 3 × 3 Gaussian smoothing filter within the CGRA, this approach transforms the video frame of size 720 × 576 into hexadecimal RGB data via MATLAB. This data is stored within the main memory, formatted in a matrix to commence the Gaussian smoothing operation.

Using integer multiplications and additions, the Gaussian mask operation produces an output pixel as a weighted sum of the adjacent pixels. Because this integer multiplication takes a long time to compute, it uses more RAM logic and slows down the system’s performance. The operations of the present Gaussian filter take up more time and resources. Since the amount of noise removed is so little, achieving the desired outcome necessitates the use of greater hardware resources. Overcoming the hardware and computation time requirement is a significant challenge in real-time image processing applications.

The fundamental principle behind the suggested method is to break down an arithmetic operation into four stages of additions and accumulations (West, North, South and East). The name of each step defines the direction in which each desired pixel will accumulate, as well as the position of each pixel. In this technique, the primary goal is to build an alternative arithmetic Pre-processing method that is less complicated. Overall, the multiplication and addition operations that comprise the Gaussian filtering convolution process results in a real-time processing that is difficult using conventional algorithms. The Pre-processing techniques discussed here are entirely addition-based directional bit serial shifting algorithms, which are intended to simplify the complexity of processing and the hardware requirements. The architecture outlined in Fig. 3 introduces a sophisticated hardware design that incorporates an external memory, a global memory, an array of processing elements (PE), and shared memory configurations. Each segment of the external memory is allocated to distinct RGB blocks designed specifically to manage the manipulation of red, green, and blue pixel data. Integration of these functional blocks is achieved through a comprehensive address and data bus system. All computational results are stored in the global memory. RGB channel data from the input image is processed and stored in the external main memory in blocks designated for each color channel, with each channel being represented in an 8-bit format. The Retinex algorithm has been implemented using the Verilog HDL programming language to facilitate both reading from and writing to a storage system. This setup allows for testing by reading data from a storage device, generating stimulus signals within a Verilog test module, and then sending the results back to the storage device. Specifically, this research utilized the Verilog HDL to simulate an effective naturalness restoration process of the Retinex algorithm on the Xilinx FPGA Artix-7 XC7A200TFBG676-2, with the entire RTL architecture displayed in Fig. 4.

The process starts by converting an image file in JPEG format into a hexadecimal file via MATLAB, which is then read by a file reader and uploaded into the external memory as either memory data or binary data. Due to the CGRA-based design, data manipulations for reading and writing employ a 19-bit address and an 8-bit data context format. This facilitates handling RGB data for the red, green, and blue image channels, which are stored across three designated memory blocks in the external memory system. Following the data reading, a Gaussian smoothing process is initiated, using data fetched from the main memory. This involves a shift-based directed addition and accumulation strategy. Upon completion of the filtering process, the accumulated Gaussian output RGB data is stored in three separate blocks within the global memory, each holding 414,720 × 8 data points, accessed via the RGB block’s write address (18:0). Further in the process, a multiplexer unit segregates the luminance data for the naturalness restoration, taking the maximum value of the Rc data from the main memory as Ln (x, y). The G (x, y) values are then retrieved from the global memory to update the weight map by multiplying two 8-bit values, resulting in a 16-bit value, of which only the most significant 8 bits are stored in shared memory. Following weight mapping, the Calc-FM process begins, enhancing the edges and blurring flat regions using a specific formula accessed from shared memory. Finally, the contrast enhancement stage employs an RGB threshold comparison to enhance the image quality. Required channel data for this process is sourced from the shared and global memories, with the outcomes stored back into the external memory. The comprehensive CGRA-based Retinex architecture and its connections are outlined in the RTL schematic, and the concluding results are transformed into text files by a file reader and subsequently analyzed using MATLAB.

Limitations and future work

While the results are promising, the system does have some limitations that need to be addressed. The reported power consumption does not include switching power, or the power consumed by IO operations and external memory access, which are critical factors in practical applications. Additionally, the system’s performance has been validated on relatively small image sizes (720 × 576 pixels), and scalability to higher resolutions or more complex scenarios, such as dynamic video streams, needs further investigation. Future work will focus on addressing these limitations by conducting a more comprehensive power analysis and exploring optimizations for larger-scale implementations.

In summary, the results demonstrate that the proposed system provides an effective, efficient, and scalable solution for low-light image enhancement in real-time environments. The combination of high image quality, real-time processing, and low power consumption positions this design as a viable option for a variety of critical applications, though further work is necessary to fully explore its potential in more demanding scenarios.

Conclusion

This research highlights the successful implementation of a low-light image enhancement system using an FPGA-based architecture with Coarse-Grained Reconfigurable Architecture (CGRA). The primary contributions include the development of a low-complexity Gaussian smoothing filter, which optimizes resource usage and reduces computational overhead while maintaining high image quality. The system achieves significant improvements in image quality, demonstrated by high PSNR and SSIM values, while operating efficiently in real-time with minimal power consumption. The use of Hardware Description Language (HDL) was critical in realizing the design’s real-time performance and hardware optimization. The experimental results validate the system’s effectiveness, showing that it can be a viable solution for applications requiring efficient low-light image enhancement. Future work will involve a more detailed experimental validation of the complexity reduction and comparisons with other FPGA-based implementations to further substantiate the system’s advantages. Additionally, the potential for further optimization and adaptation of the architecture for various image processing applications will be explored.
