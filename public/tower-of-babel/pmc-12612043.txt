The expression signature, prognostic significance and immune cell infiltration of the OAS gene family in gastric cancer
Authors: Minglin Zhang, Jie Xie, Shuoyi Yao, Ting Cai, Lingzhi Yuan, Xiaoming Liu, Fen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric cancer (GC) is a common malignant tumour and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Although the OAS gene family has been implicated in various tumour-related biological processes, its specific role in GC remains unclear. This study integrated bioinformatics analysis of public datasets with experimental validation using clinical GC specimens to investigate the function of the OAS gene family in GC. We found that OAS family members were significantly upregulated in GC and associated with poor patient prognosis. Furthermore, OAS expression levels correlated with genomic mutation profiles and showed positive associations with neutrophil and dendritic cell infiltration in the tumour microenvironment, but negative correlation with B cell infiltration. In vitro functional experiments demonstrated that knockdown of OAS3 or OASL inhibited GC cell proliferation and migration, while overexpression of OASL enhanced these malignant behaviors. RNA-seq analysis revealed upregulation of SFRP4 and JUN and downregulation of ACTA2. KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in necroptosis, MAPK signaling, PI3K-Akt signaling, cAMP signaling, viral carcinogenesis, cancer pathways, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and IL-17 signaling pathways. Additionally, OASL may regulate DNA damage response and metabolic reprogramming. Drug prediction and molecular docking identified chlorendic acid, idarubicin, PHA-848,125, and tosedostat as potential activators of the OAS family due to their strong binding affinity. Conversely, GW842166, NSC 23,766, and metolazone showed high binding affinity for OASL and may inhibit its expression. In summary, The OAS gene family, associated with poor prognosis in gastric cancer, promotes tumour progression and represents a promising therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23277-4.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Immune cell infiltration, OAS gene family, OASL, PrognosisSubject terms: Cancer, Genetics, Biomarkers

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a common cancer and a leading cause of cancer-related death, with increasing morbidity and mortality in recent years. In 2020, it ranked fifth in terms of morbidity and fourth in terms of mortality1. At present, combined treatment with surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy and immunotherapy has improved the survival rate of patients with GC, but many patients experience recurrence and metastasis, leading to poor prognosis. GC can be molecularly classified into four subtypes: Epstein–Barr virus-positive tumours, microsatellite unstable tumours, genomically stable tumours, and chromosomally unstable tumours. These molecular subtypes provide the basis for stratified and targeted therapy2.

2’,5’-Oligoadenylate synthetase (OAS) is an interferon (IFN)-induced enzyme that converts ATP to 2’,5’-linked adenosine oligomers upon activation by double-stranded RNA. The OAS gene family is involved in the regulation of viral infection by activating RNase L, which degrades cellular and viral RNA, and the genes encoding the OAS protein are clustered on human chromosome 12 (region 12q24.2)3,4. OAS gene family proteins include OAS1, OAS2, OAS3 and OAS-like proteins (OASL)5, which have multiple alternative splice isoforms and important physiological functions3,6–8. The OAS gene family is associated with the development of many diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)9, stomach adenocarcinoma10, necroptosis-mediated antiviral immunity11, autoinflammatory disorders and immunodeficiencies12, Alzheimer’s disease13, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia14 and breast cancer15. However, the role of the OAS gene family in GC is unclear and needs further study.

In this study, the TCGA, GEO, GEPIA, TIMER, STRING, starBase, and Kaplan-Meier plotter databases were used in combination with R language analysis packages to analyse the relationships between OAS gene family expression and prognosis, immune infiltration and biological function in patients with GC. Furthermore, we validated the bioinformatics results in human GC cells and clinical samples of GC. These results indicate the important role of the OAS gene family in GC and its potential biological mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of GC datasets

We obtained mRNA expression, mutation, and clinical data from the TCGA database. There were 413 samples in the mRNA file (379 GC samples and 34 nontumour samples). We downloaded mRNA expression matrices and corresponding clinical data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO)16. The clinical data included age, sex, survival time, and disease diagnosis. We transformed the data matrix, removed incomplete entries, and filtered out overlapping genes. The following datasets were used: GSE84437 (433 GC samples), GSE159721 (123 adenocarcinoma gastroesophageal junction samples and 123 nontumour samples), GSE66229 (100 GC samples and 300 nontumour samples), GSE13195 (25 GC samples and 27 nontumour samples), GSE26899 (12 GC samples and 93 nontumour samples), and GSE65801 (32 GC samples and 32 nontumour samples). A list of the online databases used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Functional enrichment analysis

R software and the packages “clusterProfiler”, “org.Hs.eg.db”, “enrichplot” and “ggplot2” were used to perform Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)17,18 and Gene Ontology (GO)19 analyses to identify the pathways and functions of genes enriched in the OAS gene family.

Tumour immunological features of OAS gene family members

Tumour Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER)20 is an online tool for tumour immune cell evaluation. According to TIMER, the correlations of five types of immune cells (B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages and neutrophils) with OAS gene family members were analysed, and p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. CIBERSORT was employed to deconvolve the expression matrix of human immune cell subtypes and visualize the distribution and expression differences across 22 immune cell subtypes21. Using GC datasets from GEO (GSE13195, GSE26899, and GSE65801), we merged and normalized the three datasets. By applying CIBERSORT and R-based analytical approaches, immune cell infiltration profiles were derived. Integrated with the merged gene expression data, we further investigated the correlations between OAS1, OAS2, OAS3, OASL and various immune cell subtypes.

Kaplan‒Meier of OAS gene family types in GC

With the Kaplan‒Meier (KM) plotter online database22, we determined the prognostic value of OAS gene family expression in GC. The Affymetrix (Affy) IDs of the OAS gene family members are as follows: OAS1 (Affy ID: 202869_at), OAS2 (Affy ID:206553_at), OAS3 (Affy ID:218400_at), and OASL (Affy ID:210797_s_at).

Interactions of OAS gene family members

STRING23 is an online database for analysing protein interactions. In this study, the OAS gene family was comprehensively analysed by STRING.

Clinical samples

This study strictly adhered to the inclusion/exclusion criteria to collect archived formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples (adjacent non-tumorous tissues, n = 15; GC tissues, n = 20) from the Department of Pathology, The Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, covering the period from February 2023 to December 2024. Concurrently, fresh tissue specimens (GC tissues, n = 6; paired adjacent non-tumorous tissues, n = 6) were collected postoperatively from GC patients in the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery at our hospital, with patient pathological outcomes tracked. All procedures complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of The Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (No. Fast|25108). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians prior to specimen collection. Fresh surgical resection specimens were collected within 30 min post-surgery, rinsed with pre-chilled (4 °C) saline, and subsequently used for tissue RNA and protein extraction. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Age between 18 and 70 years, regardless of gender; (2) Patients undergoing gastroscopy with gastric mucosal biopsy or surgical resection for GC; (3) No history of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy prior to surgery. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Receipt of preoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy; (2) Presence of distant tumour metastasis; (3) Unclear diagnosis; (4) Incomplete clinical data.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin sections were subjected to deparaffinization, dehydration, antigen repair with sodium citrate, and antigen blocking with goat serum. Primary antibodies were added, and the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies from the same source were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by 3,3’-diamino-benzidine (DAB) staining and counterstaining with haematoxylin. Observation was conducted by microscopy. Finally, statistical analysis was performed. The staining intensity was scored as 0 (no staining), 1 (weak staining), 2 (moderate staining), or 3 (strong staining). The degree of cell staining (0% = 0, 1 to 24% = 1, 25 to 49% = 2, 50 to 74% = 3, and 75 to 100% = 4) was multiplied by the staining intensity score to calculate the immunoreactive intensity score, from 0 (lowest score) to 12 (highest score). An immunohistochemical score ≥ 6 indicated high expression, and an immunohistochemical score < 6 indicated low expression. IHC scores have been described in previous studies24. The list of primary antibodies is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Cell culture

Normal gastric mucosal GES-1 cells and GC cells (SGC7901, AGS, MKN45 and HGC27) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, USA). Cells were cultured in 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR)

The expression of OAS mRNA in GC cell lines (SGC7901, AGS, MKN45 and HGC27) and in the normal gastric mucosa cell line GES-1 was detected by RT‒qPCR. To culture cells, TRIzol was used to extract total RNA from cells, which was reverse transcribed into cDNA, and cDNA amplification was performed according to the instructions. OAS gene family primers were designed by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The expression data were normalized to that of the reference gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and mRNA levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The sequences of the RT‒qPCR primers are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Western blotting (WB)

Total protein was extracted from GC cell lines (SGC7901, AGS, MKN45 and HGC27) and normal gastric mucosal cells (GES-1). The protein concentration was determined by the BCA method. Thirty micrograms of protein was added to each well and separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE, after which the proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Billerica, USA). The membrane was sealed with 5% skim milk powder for 2 h (23 °C). The PVDF membrane was incubated with primary antibody overnight (4 °C), TBST was used to wash the membrane, and the PVDF membrane was incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h (23 °C). ECL reagent was added for development. Finally, the statistical analysis was repeated three times for each protein. WB was performed as described previously24,25. The list of primary antibodies is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Construction of overexpressing and silenced cells

The OASL overexpression plasmid, OASL small interfering RNA (siOASL), and OAS3 small interfering RNA (siOAS3) were commissioned by HanBio Technology (Shanghai, China). HGC27, AGS and MKN45 cells were transfected using transfection reagents. The transfection efficiency was verified by RT‒qPCR and WB. The target sequences of the lentiviral siRNAs used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2000/well. The 96-well plates were removed at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h and 96 h, and 10 µl of CCK8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. OD450 values were measured to assess the number of viable cells.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4–5 × 102/well and cultured in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 14 days, but MKN45 cells were cultured for 18 days. The 6-well plates were removed, washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with crystal violet for 20 min, photographed and analysed statistically.

Transwell migration assay

The cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin, resuspended in serum-free medium, and seeded into 24-well plates with culture chambers at 2 × 105 cells/well. The plates were subsequently washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with crystal violet for 20 min, sealed with neutral resin, photographed under a microscope, and analysed statistically (n = 3).

Cellular immunofluorescence

The cell slides were placed in 6-well plates and inoculated with the intervention cells, and the cells were 95% confluent. The 6-well plates were removed, washed twice with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. The membranes were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 15 min. The cells were blocked with PBST supplemented with 1% BSA for 30 min. Fluorescent primary antibodies were added overnight at 4 °C. Fluorescent secondary antibodies were added, and the samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were stained with DAPI for 5 min, sealed with a fluorescence quenching agent, and observed by fluorescence microscopy, and the results were recorded.

RNA-seq

We constructed OASL-overexpressing cell lines, extracted total RNA, and used an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) to perform deep sequencing on the library, annotate the genome, and identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs); adjusted p value < 0.05, absolute |log2(foldchange)| > 1. This part was completed previously. In this study, RNA-seq was used to identify enriched downstream target genes and pathways.

Compound prediction and molecular docking for the OAS gene family

Enrichr-KG is a widely used web-based gene set enrichment analysis search engine that contains hundreds of thousands of annotated gene sets26. By selecting the entries “LINCS L1000 Chem Pert Consensus Sigs” and “Proteomics Drug Atlas 2023” connections between the OAS gene family and compounds were established. The 3D structures of the compounds were filtered using the PubChem database27, while the protein structures of the OAS gene family were obtained from the RCSB PDB database28. Molecular docking between the OAS gene family and the compounds was performed using the CB-Dock platform, which employs a deep learning-based scoring function29. A lower (more negative) Vina score indicates stronger binding, with values ≤ −5.0 kcal/mol generally suggesting potential activity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software; two-tailed Student’s t test and one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance computed by the Wilcoxon test is indicated by the number of stars. The data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). All analyses were performed at least in triplicate. Statistically significant differences are indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, and ns: not significantly different.

Results

Expression and prognosis of the OAS gene family in GC tissues

We detected the expression of the OAS gene family in various tumours through TIMER and found that the expression of the OAS gene family was upregulated in GC tissues (Fig. 1A–D). Moreover, we detected mRNA levels in GC tissues through the TCGA, GEO and Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis Platform (GEPIA) databases. The results revealed that the mRNA levels of OAS1, OAS2 and OAS3 were upregulated in GC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2A), suggesting that the transcription levels of the OAS gene family may be molecular markers for GC. Through the prognostic network map, we found that the OAS gene family was positively associated with prognosis (Fig. 2B). In the STRING online database, coexpression, gene neighbourhood and protein homology among the OAS gene family were detected (Fig. 2C). The results of the prognostic value of the OAS gene family in the KM plotter suggest that high expression of OAS1, OAS2, OAS3 and OASL is associated with poor prognosis, including poor OS, first progression (FP) and post progression survival (PPS) (Fig. 2D–G).

To verify the bioinformatics results shown in Figs. 1 and 2A, we detected the protein levels of OAS1, OAS2, OAS3 and OASL in human GC tissues and adjacent tissues by IHC. Their expression was significantly upregulated in GC (Fig. 2H). RT‒qPCR and WB revealed that OAS1, OAS2, OAS3 and OASL were upregulated in GC (Fig. 2I–J). Further validation in the GEPIA database revealed that the OAS gene family was upregulated in GC (Fig. 2K). Therefore, the OAS gene family plays an important role in GC, but the exact mechanism remains unclear and needs to be further studied.

Correlations between OAS gene family members and tumour immune infiltrating cells

Next, the potential correlations between OAS gene family members and tumour immune cells (B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils and dendritic cells) were estimated with TIMER. Interestingly, OAS1 expression was positively correlated with the infiltration level of neutrophils (cor = 0.218) and negatively correlated with the infiltration level of B cells, CD4+ T cells and macrophages (cor = −0.244, cor= −0.158, and cor = −0.145, respectively) (Fig. 3A). OAS2 expression was positively correlated with the infiltration level of CD8+ T cells, neutrophils and dendritic cells (cor = 0.296, cor = 0.48, and cor = 0.365, respectively) and negatively correlated with the infiltration level of B cells (cor = −0.174) (Fig. 3B). The association between OAS3 expression and the infiltration level of neutrophils and dendritic cells was positive (cor = 0.297 and cor = 0.167, respectively), but the association with the infiltration level of B cells was negative (cor = −0.205) (Fig. 3C). OASL was positively associated with the infiltration level of CD8+ T cells, neutrophils and dendritic cells (cor = 0.218, cor = 0.369, and cor = 0.217, respectively) and negatively associated with the infiltration level of B cells (cor = −0.149) (Fig. 3D). We further integrated CIBERSORT to validate the relationships between the OAS gene family and immune cells. The analysis revealed that OAS1 was positively correlated with macrophages M1 and NK cells activated (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). OAS2 showed positive correlations with macrophages M1, CD4+ memory activated T cells, and dendritic cells activated, but was negatively correlated with monocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1B). OAS3 was positively associated with macrophages M1, CD4+ memory activated T cells, dendritic cells activated, and NK cells activated, while exhibiting negative correlations with CD4+ memory resting T cells and monocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). OASL demonstrated a positive correlation with macrophages M1 and plasma cells, and was negatively correlated with macrophages M2 and mast cells resting (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

Integrated analysis using both TIMER and CIBERSORT revealed that members of the OAS gene family (OAS1, OAS2, OAS3, and OASL) show significant correlations with the infiltration levels of various immune cells. Notably, the positive correlation with M1 macrophages was consistently validated across both computational platforms and analytical methods, suggesting that this gene family may play an important role in the tumour microenvironment by regulating M1 macrophage-mediated immune responses.

Immune infiltration and enrichment analysis of OAS gene family types in GC

The expression levels of the OAS gene family samples were divided into type A and type B, and survival analysis was conducted for each type (Fig. 4A). The results revealed that both type A and type B were associated with poor prognosis, and the survival time of type A patients was short (Fig. 4B). A gene heatmap of the OAS gene family was constructed, and the expression of OAS gene family members was upregulated in type B (Fig. 4C). Enrichment analysis of KEGG and GO Biological Process (GOBP) signalling pathways for types A and B revealed that KEGG and GO-related signalling pathways were obviously enriched in type B (Fig. 4D–E). Further immune infiltration analysis of types A and B revealed that all immune cells were more obvious in type B (Fig. 4F). Principal component analysis (PCA) and a Venn diagram were constructed for types A and B (Fig. 4G). Further enrichment analysis of these DEGs by GO and KEGG revealed that most of the genes were enriched in response to and defence against viruses (Fig. 4H–I).

Mutations of OAS gene family members in GC in the TCGA and TIMER cohorts

Our analysis revealed that the mutation incidence of the OAS gene family in GC (Fig. 5A) was 1% (OAS1), 3% (OAS2), 2% (OAS3) and 1% (OASL). The locations of copy number variation (CNV) alterations in GC on 23 pairs of human chromosomes are shown in Fig. 5C. The frequency of CNV variations in the OAS gene family is shown, with OAS1, OAS2 and OAS3 CNV copy numbers increasing more frequently than missing copy numbers and OASL CNV copy numbers missing more frequently than increasing copy numbers (Fig. 5B). CNVs in OAS1, OAS2, OAS3 and OASL were significantly associated with decreased numbers of six types of immune cells (B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils and dendritic cells) (Fig. 5D–G). Therefore, changes in gene copy number can significantly regulate immune cell infiltration. OAS1, OAS2, OAS3 and OASL wild-type and mutant types were not significantly associated with 4 immune cell types (B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells and neutrophils) (Fig. 5H–K).

Establishing a risk prediction model for the OAS gene family

First, we combined the information of the TCGA platoon and GSE84437 cohorts, identified the coexpressed differential genes, and divided the samples into A, B and C types. Survival analysis revealed that the prognostic associations of type A samples was better than that of type B samples, and the prognostic associations of type B samples was poor (Fig. 6A). A heatmap revealed that the OAS gene family was upregulated in type A samples (Fig. 6B). The expression of the OAS gene family was downregulated in type B and type C samples (Fig. 6C). Finally, crossover genes were screened by LASSO regression (Fig. 6D). Sankey charts were constructed (Fig. 6E), and this network revealed the regulatory relationships among these subtypes. Risk scores were downregulated in type B and type C samples (Fig. 6F). A predictive risk model was constructed according to the differential gene set, and the patients were divided into a high-risk group and a low-risk group according to the risk score of the patient cohort in R, which was verified in the retraining cohort, validation cohort and total TCGA cohort, respectively. The calibration plots demonstrated a remarkable consistency between the predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 6G). The expression of the OAS gene family was downregulated in the high-risk group (Fig. 6H). High-risk patients in the TRAIN and TEST groups in had poorer outcomes (Fig. 6I). In terms of the RS distribution, patient health status and OAS gene family expression profile, statistically significant differences were detected between the high-risk (HR) group and the low-risk (LR) group (Fig. 6L–N). In addition, the ROC curves of 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS demonstrated that our model has a high predictive capacity (Fig. 6J). To predict OS in patients with GC, we created a nomogram based on patient age, sex, TNM stage and risk score; for example, a male patient in the high-risk group (total score: 292 points) had a survival probability of 0.806 at 1 year, 0.539 at 3 years, and 0.435 at 5 years, as marked in Fig. 6K.

OAS3 and OASL modulate cell proliferation and migration

To characterize OAS gene expression in GC cells, we performed RT‒qPCR and WB analyses on four human GC cell lines (SGC7901, AGS, MKN45, and HGC27) and the normal gastric mucosa cell line GES-1. RT‒qPCR revealed significantly higher mRNA levels of the OAS gene family in all the GC cell lines than in GES-1 cells (Fig. 8A). Consistent with these findings, WB analysis demonstrated markedly increased protein expression of OAS1, OAS2, OAS3, and OASL in the GC cell lines compared with GES-1 cells (Fig. 8B).

Given this elevated OAS gene expression and to investigate the specific biological functions of OAS3 and OASL, we analysed their protein levels across the GC cell lines. Notably, WB results (Fig. 8B) indicated that OAS3 and OASL expression was highest in AGS and MKN45 cells and lowest in HGC27 cells. On the basis of these differential expression profiles, we selected AGS, MKN45, and HGC27 cells for further functional and mechanistic studies. To modulate OAS3 and OASL expression, we transfected these cell lines with specific siRNAs and overexpression plasmids, generating the following models: HGC27_OASL cells for OASL overexpression, AGS_siOASL and MKN45_siOASL cells for OASL knockdown, and AGS_siOAS3 and MKN45_siOAS3 cells for OAS3 knockdown. Subsequent WB analysis confirmed the efficacy of these manipulations, showing successful upregulation of OASL in HGC27_OASL cells, effective downregulation of OASL in AGS_siOASL and MKN45_siOASL cells, and specific downregulation of OAS3 in AGS_siOAS3 and MKN45_siOAS3 cells (Fig. 8C–E).

Immunofluorescence analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C) demonstrated that overexpression of OASL increased its expression and revealed its nuclear localization, with increased accumulation in the nucleus. Conversely, knockdown of OAS3 or OASL inhibited their expression. Cell viability assays (CCK-8 and colony formation) revealed that knockdown of OASL or OAS3 inhibited the viability of AGS and MKN45 cells (Fig. 8F–I). Conversely, OASL overexpression promoted the viability of HGC27 cells (Fig. 8J–K). Furthermore, the upregulation of OASL promoted the migration of GC cells, whereas the downregulation of OASL or OAS3 inhibited GC cell migration (Fig. 8L–N). We examined the expression of ZO-1, Vimentin, and Fibronectin under conditions of OASL overexpression or knockdown. We found that overexpression of OASL led to downregulation of ZO-1 and upregulation of Vimentin and Fibronectin. Conversely, knockdown of OASL resulted in upregulation of ZO-1 and downregulation of Vimentin and Fibronectin (Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). Therefore, OAS3 and OASL promote the proliferation and migration of GC cells, contributing to GC progression. The specific molecular mechanisms underlying these effects require further investigation.

Mechanistic insights into OASL regulation: Through RNA-seq analysis of HGC27 cells overexpressing OASL (HGC27_OASL), we identified OASL-mediated transcriptional reprogramming characterized by upregulation of SFRP4 and JUN and downregulation of ACTA2 (Supplementary Fig. 4A–B). KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in the following pathways: pathways related to necroptosis, the MAPK signalling pathway, the PI3K-Akt signalling pathway, the cAMP signalling pathway, pathways related to viral carcinogenesis, pathways related to cancer, pathways related to neutrophil extracellular trap formation and the IL-17 signalling pathway (Supplementary Fig. 4 C). Furthermore, we conducted a systematic analysis of DEGs enriched in key signalling pathways, including the MAPK signalling pathway, the PI3K-Akt-mTOR signalling pathway, the FoxO signalling pathway and necroptosis (Supplementary Fig. 4D–G). In OASL overexpression experiments, we observed significant upregulation of indicators related to cellular response to DNA damage stimulus, oxidative stress, DNA repair, and necroptosis (Supplementary Fig. 5A–D), suggesting that OASL may play an important role in DNA damage and repair processes. Concurrently, metabolic pathways including glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism were also upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Based on these findings, our future research will focus on investigating whether OASL promotes GC progression through involvement in DNA damage repair and metabolic reprogramming.

Compound prediction and molecular docking for the OAS gene family

Using the Enrichr-KG database, five small-molecule compounds targeting the OAS gene family were screened, including SA-88,642, idarubicin, PHA-848,125, chlorendic-acid, and tosedostat (Supplementary Fig. 6 A). Molecular docking results demonstrated strong binding affinities between these compounds and each member of the OAS family. Specifically, the vina scores for OAS1 binding with chlorendic-acid, idarubicin, PHA-848,125 and tosedostat were − 5.8 kcal/mol, −6.7 kcal/mol, −7.0 kcal/mol, and − 6.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 9A-D), respectively; for OAS2, the corresponding vina scores were − 6.6 kcal/mol, −8.9 kcal/mol, −8.8 kcal/mol, and − 7.1 kcal/mol (Fig. 9E-H); for OAS3, the scores were − 6.9 kcal/mol, −9.7 kcal/mol, −9.0 kcal/mol, and − 7.1 kcal/mol (Fig. 9I-L); and for OASL, the binding energies were − 6.6 kcal/mol, −9.2 kcal/mol, −10.6 kcal/mol, and − 8.6 kcal/mol (Fig. 9M-P). Notably, all these compounds upregulate the expression of the OAS gene family, indicating their role in promoting the transcriptional activity of these genes. Further analysis revealed that GW842166, NSC 23,766, and metolazone inhibit OASL expression (Supplementary Fig. 6 A). Molecular docking results indicated that these drugs bind to OASL with a binding affinity of −8.4 kcal/mol each, suggesting their potential to suppress OASL expression (Supplementary Fig. 7A–C).

Discussion

GC is a common disease of the digestive system, and its morbidity and mortality are increasing globally, threatening human health. In this study, we found that OAS1, OAS2, OAS3 and OASL are upregulated in GC and are associated with prognosis. Therefore, this study provides a theoretical basis and a scientific basis for the use of OAS gene family members as new prognostic biomarkers for GC.

Previously, members of the OAS gene family have been linked to a variety of diseases, including infections, immune system disorders and tumours. OAS1 and OAS2 are involved in cell proliferation and migration in breast cancer30,31. OAS2 and DUXAP10 are involved in gefitinib resistance in non-small cell lung cancer32. Silencing OASL inhibits the progression of gastric adenocarcinoma by modulating the mTORC1 signalling pathway10. OASL is involved in the progression of lung cancer33. Moreover, the prognostic value of OAS gene family members in various cancers, including breast cancer15, lung carcinogenesis34, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia14, stomach adenocarcinoma10, bladder cancer35 and pancreatic cancer36, has been evaluated. At present, there is no correlation between OAS gene family members and the prognosis and immune invasion of GC, and we analysed the prognosis and immune infiltration of OAS gene family members using a public database.

We found through a pancancer analysis that the expression of OAS gene family members was upregulated in most tumours and significantly upregulated in GC. Moreover, the same conclusion was obtained in the TCGA and GEO databases: the expression of the OAS gene family was upregulated in GC, which was related to patient outcomes. We detected the protein expression of OAS1, OAS2, OAS3 and OASL in human GC tissues and normal tissues by IHC, RT‒qPCR, WB and IF, and their expression was significantly upregulated in GC. We also determined the homology and interactions among OAS gene family members through the use of the STRING online database. However, the incidence of adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG), a malignant tumour occurring at the gastroesophageal junction (near the cardia), has increased in recent years37,38. Studies have revealed that AEG patients benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy, with improved OS and reduced postoperative recurrence risk39,40. In this study, analysis of AEG datasets from the GEO database revealed significant upregulation of OAS1, OAS2, and OAS3 and downregulation of OASL in AEG tissues (Supplementary Fig. 8 A). RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play critical roles in multiple aspects of RNA metabolism, including splicing, editing, transport, stability maintenance, degradation, intracellular localization and translational regulation41. Using starBase42, we predicted 23 RBPs interacting with OASL pre-mRNAs (Supplementary Table 4). Further exploration of OASL upstream regulators revealed that most identified RBPs were significantly upregulated in AEG (Supplementary Fig. 8B-C). Further analysis of the expression of RBPs in GC (GSE26899 and GSE66229) revealed that EIF3B, IGF2BP3, METTL1, RNPS1 and YTHDF1 were significantly upregulated in GC, whereas RBM20 and SOX2 were significantly downregulated in GC (Supplementary Fig. 8D–E). RBPs may regulate the involvement of OASL in the occurrence and development of GC.

There are many gene-related predictive models that analyse the genotype and the correlation between the sample type and the tumour as a tool for predicting tumours43–46. We constructed genotyping models for the OAS gene family and reported that genotyping was correlated with survival and prognosis. The OAS gene family was significantly upregulated in genotype B, and immune cells showed obvious immune infiltration in genotype B. At the same time, KEGG and GO enrichment analyses revealed that the OAS gene family was correlated with the viral response, interferon response and infection response. These findings provide a strong basis for future signalling pathway research. We constructed LASSO regression models to analyse OAS gene family characteristics, age, sex and stage, and the survival rate of GC patients was predicted by the nomogram. The 1-year survival rate was 0.806, the 3-year survival rate was 0.539, and the 5-year survival rate was 0.435. ROC analysis and a calibration chart were used to verify the prediction accuracy of the model, and the results revealed that the model had strong prediction ability. A comparison of the predictive performance of the ROC curve model and the TNM staging system (nomogram model) for survival outcomes revealed that the nomogram model demonstrated better ability to predict the 1-year survival rate, whereas the ROC curve model showed superior ability to predict the 3-year and 5-year survival rates.

Gene copy number changes affect GC prognosis and are associated with patient recurrence and survival47. Changes in chromosome copy number can cause genomic instability, and cancer cells use aneuploidy-induced genomic instability to produce gene copy number changes that affect chemical resistance48. In recent years, studies have shown that gene copy number instability and tumour mutation burden affect the GC prognosis49. We found that the copy frequencies of OAS1, OAS2, OAS3 and OASL were as follows: OAS2 (3%), OAS3 (2%), OASL (1%), and OAS1 (1%). CNV alterations in the OAS family members showed significant associations with the infiltration levels of certain immune cell types. Therefore, the OAS gene family may be a marker for identifying invasive immune cells associated with poor prognosis.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have significantly changed the treatment landscape for patients with advanced GC. ICIs have shown long-lasting responses and extended survival in patients with GC50. Resistance to ICI therapy is partly related to immunosuppression of the TME. An immunosuppressive TME causes increases in Tregs, myeloid suppressor cells (MDSCs), and tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs). These processes reduce the activity of immune effector cells and the production of immunosuppressive cytokines and chemokines51. By analysing the unique classes and subclasses of the tumour immune microenvironment (TIME), the ability to predict immunotherapy reactivity can be improved, thereby revealing new therapeutic targets. Therefore, gene expression is associated with survival and ICI response in patients with GC. Our analysis revealed a correlation between immune cells and the risk score, which was positively correlated with B cells, mast cells, resting memory CD4+ T cells and Tregs, and it was negatively correlated with NK cells, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, neutrophils, CD4+ T cells, follicular T cells, eosinophils and activated memory CD4+ T cells.

This study integrated two computational biology approaches TIMER and CIBERSORT to systematically reveal broad associations between the OAS gene family and patterns of tumour immune infiltration. Although specific correlated immune cell types varied among individual OAS members, the family as a whole generally showed positive correlations with pro-inflammatory immune cells (such as M1 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, and NK cells) and negative correlations with certain anti-inflammatory or resting cell types (such as M2 macrophages and resting memory CD4+ T cells). This highly consistent pattern strongly implies that the OAS gene family, collectively, may help shape an inflammatory tumour microenvironment by activating and recruiting anti- tumour immune cells (particularly M1 macrophages) thereby potentially influencing tumour immune surveillance and responses to immunotherapy. Future functional studies should focus on the specific molecular mechanisms through which OAS genes regulate M1 macrophage polarization. In the future, we will study the mechanism of OAS gene family involvement in the response to ICIs to provide a basis for immunotherapy in GC.

Through bioinformatics analysis, we found that the OAS gene family was upregulated in GC and correlated with prognosis. The OAS gene family is closely related to immune cell infiltration, the TME and tumour burden mutations. To further verify the role of the OAS gene family in the carcinogenesis and progression of GC, we verified the expression of the OAS gene family (OAS1/2/3 and OASL) in GC cells and tissues and found that the OAS gene family was upregulated in GC cells and tissues. On the basis of the homology and similar function of the OAS gene family, we chose OAS3 and OASL to conduct functional experiments and explore the underlying molecular mechanism.

In GC cells, OASL overexpression promoted cell proliferation and migration; in contrast, OASL silencing inhibited cell proliferation and migration. Silencing OAS3 inhibited cell proliferation and migration. We analysed the top 20 DEGs after OASL expression by RNA-seq. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in the following pathways: pathways related to necroptosis, the MAPK signalling pathway, the PI3K-Akt signalling pathway, the cAMP signalling pathway, pathways related to viral carcinogenesis, pathways related to cancer, pathways related to neutrophil extracellular trap formation and the IL-17 signalling pathway. These results suggest that OAS3 and OASL play a role in promoting proliferation and migration during GC progression. Our previous study found that H. pylori infection induces the expression of the OAS gene family. We also discovered that TRAF1 promotes GC progression via the EGFR/STAT/OAS signalling axis25. OASL promotes proliferation and migration and inhibits apoptosis in GC by regulating mTORC1 signalling10, which is consistent with our results.

This study further integrated the Enrichr-KG knowledge graph screening and molecular docking simulations to successfully identify lead compounds capable of bidirectionally regulating the OAS gene family: Idarubicin and PHA-848,125 demonstrated strong binding affinity and significantly upregulated OAS expression, suggesting their potential as antiviral agonists; while inhibitors such as GW842166 could specifically suppress OASL activity, offering a new direction for the treatment of autoimmune diseases. In the future, functional and mechanistic studies will be conducted, but the current results have already laid an important foundation for developing precision immunomodulatory drugs targeting the OAS pathway.

However, this study has certain limitations. We validated only the biological effects of OASL and OAS3, performed RNA-seq analysis solely on OASL-overexpressing cells, and did not explore the effects of alterations in OAS1 and OAS2 expression on disease progression. Additionally, further nude mouse xenograft experiments are needed to verify the effects of the OAS gene family on tumorigenesis, which will provide critical evidence for the development of therapeutic targets within this gene family. Finally, future investigations should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which the OAS gene family drives GC development and their specific associations with relevant signalling pathways.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the OAS gene family is highly expressed in a variety of tumours and is associated with survival outcomes. In addition, we investigated the relationships between the OAS gene family and immune-infiltrating cells, the TME and tumour burden mutations. In vitro, OASL promoted proliferation and migration, whereas silencing OAS3 and OASL inhibited proliferation and migration. OASL upregulation may promote gastric carcinogenesis by regulating necroptosis and modulating the MAPK, PI3K-Akt, cAMP and IL-17 signalling pathways. Additionally, OASL may regulate DNA damage response and metabolic reprogramming. Drug prediction and molecular docking identified chlorendic acid, idarubicin, PHA-848,125, and tosedostat as potential activators of the OAS family due to their strong binding affinity. Conversely, GW842166, NSC 23,766, and metolazone showed high binding affinity for OASL and may inhibit its expression. The OAS gene family members may become molecular markers and therapeutic targets for GC.
