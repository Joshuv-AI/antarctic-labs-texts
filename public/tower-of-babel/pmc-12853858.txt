Rumen microbiota–host interactions regulate intramuscular fat deposition in cattle via the alpha-linolenic acid–fatty acid β-oxidation/L-carnitine–MPO axis
Authors: Wen-Tao Zhang, Miao-Li Wang, Chen-Yang Liu, Chuang Li, Xiao-Ping He, Hong-Bao Wang, Lin-Sen Zan, Gong Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBeef, known for its high protein, low fat, and rich amino acid content, is a premium source of nutrition compared to other meats. As living standards improve, higher demands are being placed on beef quality, which is closely related to intramuscular fat (IMF) content. Jiaxian Red Cattle, a local Chinese breed, is renowned for its high meat quality, characterized by reddish muscle color and snow-white fat. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying differences in intramuscular fat deposition and meat quality in this breed remain unclear. This study aims to explore the potential molecular mechanisms of intramuscular fat deposition in beef cattle using multi-omics approaches.ResultsNon-targeted metabolomic analysis of rumen fluid identified α-linolenic acid as a key metabolite promoting fat accumulation. Four genera—Cnuella, Gaetbulibacter, Moheibacter, and Lacibacter—were significantly positively correlated with α-linolenic acid (r > 0.83, p < 0.05), highlighting their roles in shaping a metabolic environment conducive to intramuscular fat deposition. Pathway analysis revealed that in the high intramuscular fat group (group H-IMF), the fatty acid β-oxidation pathway was significantly inhibited, reducing fatty acid oxidation and promoting fat deposition. Additionally, metabolomic data from longissimus dorsi muscle tissue showed significantly lower levels of N,N,N-trimethyllysine, L-carnitine, and betaine in group H-IMF (p < 0.05). These metabolites were negatively correlated with Cnuella, Gaetbulibacter, Moheibacter, and Lacibacter, indicating a complex interplay between these microbiota and the metabolic network regulating intramuscular fat deposition. These metabolites also interacted with proteins like ACSS1, ACSF2, and MPO to modulate fat formation. In vitro experiments confirmed these findings: overexpression of the MPO gene significantly enhanced intramuscular fat accumulation (p < 0.05), while L-carnitine and betaine suppressed MPO expression (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThis study reveals that Moheibacter, Cnuella, Gaetbulibacter, and Lacibacter play central regulatory roles in intramuscular fat deposition in beef cattle via the α-linolenic acid–fatty acid β-oxidation/L-carnitine–MPO axis. These findings highlight the critical role of microbial communities in regulating fat deposition through complex host–microbe interactions, providing insights for future strategies aimed at enhancing beef quality. Background

Beef is widely regarded as a premium meat due to its high protein, low fat, low cholesterol, and rich amino acid content, all of which contribute to growth, development, and immune function [1–3]. Its nutritional richness makes it a staple in many diets, promoting a healthy lifestyle [4]. As living standards rise, consumers increasingly demand higher-quality beef, with intramuscular fat (IMF) content being a key indicator of meat quality. IMF content is closely related to marbling grade, which is a critical factor in evaluating beef carcasses and determining their market value, particularly in the high-end beef market [5, 6]. Jiaxian Red Cattle, a local breed in China, is renowned for its high meat quality, featuring a reddish color, fine marbling, tenderness, juiciness, and rich flavor [7]. Despite the breed’s distinctive meat quality, trait-related research in Jiaxian Red Cattle remains limited [8–10], and the mechanisms underlying fat deposition and meat quality variation are still not well characterized.

With the rapid development of high-throughput technologies, multi-omics sequencing has become an essential tool for investigating the complex genetic and environmental factors underlying traits such as meat quality. The role of microbiota in fat deposition has garnered increasing attention. Species like Barnesiella intestinihominis and Faecalibaculum rodentium have been linked to fasting-induced fat accumulation via the inactivation of intestinal glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) [11]; microorganisms such as Microbacterium and Sphingomonas influence growth performance in chickens through lipid metabolism [12], and Akkermansia sp. DSM 33459 significantly improves body weight, fat mass, and insulin sensitivity in obese mice [13]. The interactions between the microbiota and host genes have also been confirmed by relevant studies. Alterations in the microbiota can improve the metabolism and egg production parameters of breeding hens. Additionally, changes in the bacterial populations and the presence of polyphenols in the cecum seem to enhance intestinal homeostasis, resulting in a better antioxidant status and a reduction in the expression of pro-inflammatory factors [14]. Wen et al. found that fat deposition in yellow broilers is jointly regulated by the host and cecal microbiota, including Methanobrevibacter and Mucispirillum schaedleri [15]. The colon, adipose tissue, and muscle transcriptomes in Prevotella copri-gavaged mice indicated that colonization of Prevotella copri activated the host’s chronic inflammatory response via the TLR4 and mTOR signaling pathways, significantly upregulating gene expression associated with lipogenesis and fat accumulation while downregulating genes related to lipolysis, lipid transport, and muscle growth [16]. The interaction between the microbiota Prevotella copri and host genes collectively led to fat deposition. Related studies have also been conducted in cattle. Fecal samples and longissimus dorsi muscle tissues were collected from two animal groups for metagenomic and transcriptomic analysis, revealing a positive correlation between Eubacterium rectale and genes involved in muscle and fat metabolism [17]. This study reveals that the gut microbiome interacts with host muscle genes to jointly influence intramuscular fat content. Although there have been numerous studies on how the synergistic interaction between the host and microbiota can alter host traits, research on the specific mechanisms through which the host and microbiota influence traits is scarce, especially in the context of intramuscular fat deposition, where research is relatively lacking.

In this study, we aim to elucidate how microbiota and host genes interact to regulate intramuscular fat deposition. To do this, we performed a comprehensive multi-omics analysis on longissimus dorsi muscle tissue and rumen fluid samples from Jiaxian Red Cattle. Our approach integrated metagenomics, untargeted metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics to identify key microbial taxa, critical metabolites, and other pivotal factors. These factors were found to interact and regulate fat formation via the β-oxidation pathway of fatty acids. We further validated and expanded these findings using in vitro cell models (Fig. 1). Materials and methods

Sample collection

The Jiaxian Red Cattle were raised in well-maintained farms under conditions that adhered to relevant industry standards, including the “Feeding Standard of Beef Cattle” (NY/T 815-2004) and the “Laboratory animal—Guideline for ethical review of animal welfare” (GB/T 35892-2018). All procedures were conducted in accordance with Chinese animal research laws and approved by the Experimental Animal Management Committee (EAMC) of Northwest A&F University (protocol no. NWAFUCAST2018-167). Furthermore, the use and care of animals strictly followed the guidelines set by the EAMC. The cattle were allowed ad libitum access to feed, and the detailed dietary composition is provided in Table S1. The genetic background of Jiaxian Red Cattle has been presented in previous studies [18]. The Jiaxian Red Cattle were raised for a total of 30± 3 months, which included a fattening period of 15 months. After this period, nineteen animals were slaughtered simultaneously (Table S2). Longissimus dorsi muscle samples were collected between the 12th and 13th ribs. The samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C until analysis. These samples were used for the determination of intramuscular fat and crude protein contents, as well as for transcriptomic, proteomic, and non-targeted metabolomic sequencing. Additionally, rumen fluid samples were collected in duplicate and stored at −80 °C for metagenomic and non-targeted metabolomic analyses. All sample collection procedures were conducted under sterile conditions and were approved by the Experimental Animal Management Committee of Northwest A&F University (protocol no. NWAFUCAST2018-167).

Measurement of intramuscular fat and protein content and sample grouping

Intramuscular fat content was determined using the Soxhlet extraction method in accordance with the China National Standard GB 5009.6-2016 [19]. In short, the sample was dried and ground into small particles/powder and transferred into a filter paper cylinder. Then the sample was extracted with anhydrous ether, evaporated to remove the solvent, and dried. The specific process was carried out by Norminkoda Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) [20, 21].

Protein content was measured using the Kjeldahl method adhered to the protocol specified in the China National Standard GB 5009.5-2016 [22]. In this method, protein is decomposed under catalytic heating, releasing ammonia that reacts with sulfuric acid to form ammonium sulfate. The sample is then alkalized and distilled, freeing the ammonia, which is absorbed by boric acid. The ammonia is subsequently titrated with a standard solution of sulfuric or hydrochloric acid. The nitrogen content is calculated based on the acid consumption and converted into protein content using a conversion factor of 6.25. Norminkoda Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) was responsible for the completion of the specific process [23].

Based on the intramuscular fat content (measured on a dry sample basis), the 19 cattle were ranked, and the top 6 animals were designated as the high intramuscular fat group (group H-IMF, n = 6), while the lowest 6 animals were designated as the low intramuscular fat group (group L-IMF, n = 6). These two groups were subjected to untargeted metabolomic analysis. Each group was further divided into three pooled samples: H1, H2, and H3 for group H-IMF and L1, L2, and L3 for group L-IMF. Each pooled sample consisted of a random mix of two individuals from the respective group, ensuring the representation of intragroup variability while minimizing individual bias [24, 25]. All omics analyses employed the same pooling strategy, in which each pooled sample was treated as an independent biological replicate (n = 3) for transcriptomic, proteomic, and metagenomic analyses. When integrating the metabolomic data with other omics layers, the average value of the corresponding metabolomic samples was used for downstream analysis [26].

Detection of untargeted metabolites in longissimus dorsi muscle and rumen fluid

Metabolite extraction methods were as described [27]. The extracted samples were separated using an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system equipped with a HILIC column. Primary and secondary mass spectra were acquired using an AB Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer. The raw data were converted to MzML format using ProteoWizard (v 3.0.6428) and processed using the XCMS (online 3.7.1) program for peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction. The resulting data were first assessed for completeness. Metabolites with more than 50% missing values within a group were excluded from subsequent analysis. Missing values were imputed using the KNN (k-nearest neighbors) method. Extreme values were removed, and the data were normalized to the total peak area.

Ruminal fluid metagenomic sequencing

Genomic DNA from rumen fluid was extracted using the HiPure Stool DNA Kit (D3141, Magen). The quality of the extracted DNA was assessed using Agarose Gel Electrophoresis and Agilent 5400 Fragment Analyzer system (Agilent Technologies). After passing quality control, DNA was fragmented by sonication to a size of 350 bp to prepare the DNA library using NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) according to the preparation protocol. All samples were paired-end sequences with a 150-bp read length on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. The raw sequencing reads were filtered and aligned to the Bos taurus genome (Ensembl_release109) using Bowtie2 (v 2.2.5). The reads considered from the host were removed. MEGAHIT (v1.1.2) was used to assemble clean reads with k-mers of 27, 37, 47, 57, 67, 77, 87, 97, 107, 117, and 127, generating contigs. Genes were predicted from contigs longer than 500 bp using MetaGeneMark (v3.38). A nonredundant gene catalog with 95% sequence identity and 90% coverage was constructed using CD-HIT (v 4.6). The longest sequence within each cluster was selected as the representative, referred to as a Unigene. Bowtie (v2.2.5) realigned reads to the Unigenes and counted mapped reads. Genes with fewer than two supporting reads were excluded, and the remaining Unigenes formed the final nonredundant gene set. Gene abundance was calculated based on Bowtie (v2.2.5) alignment results, and PathoScope (v2.0.7) reassigned reads to the most likely corresponding genes. The abundance of each Unigene was determined using the corresponding formula [28].

Transcriptomic and proteomic sequencing of the longissimus dorsi muscle

Total RNA was extracted from the longissimus dorsi muscle using the TRIzol method, and RNA-seq was performed by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were quality-checked using fastp (v0.18.0) to filter out low-quality data, resulting in clean reads. The filtering steps included removing adapter-containing reads, reads with > 10% N bases, reads composed entirely of A bases, and low-quality reads (with > 50% bases having a quality score of Q ≤ 20). Clean reads were aligned to the Bos taurus ribosomal database (Ensembl_release109) using Bowtie (v2.4.4), with no mismatches allowed. Ribosomal-aligned reads were removed, and the remaining unmapped reads were used for transcriptome analysis. Paired-end sequencing data were aligned to the Bos taurus genome (Ensembl_release109) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Transcripts were reconstructed using StringTie (v2.1.0) based on HISAT2 (v2.1.0) alignment, and gene expression levels were calculated using RSEM (v1.2.19) and quantified as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads).

The protein extraction methods were as described in the reference [29]. Protein samples were processed by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China) using an Orbitrap Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) coupled with an EASY-nLC 1200 system. DIA raw data were analyzed using Spectronaut 18 (Biognosys AG, Switzerland) with default settings, and retention time prediction was set to dynamic iRT. Data extraction was determined by Spectronaut 18 based on extensive mass calibration, with the extraction window dynamically adjusted depending on iRT calibration and gradient stability. The Q-value (FDR) cutoff was set to 1% for both precursor and protein levels. Decoy generation was set to mutated, applying random AA position swaps (min = 2, max = length/2). The normalization strategy was local normalization, and peptides passing the 1% Q-value cutoff were used to calculate major group quantities using the MaxLFQ method.

Real-time quantitative PCR, triglycerides, and Oil Red O staining

Intramuscular preadipocytes isolation and plasmid construction

Intramuscular preadipocytes of the longissimus dorsi muscle were isolated from calves of Qinchuan cattle at National Beef Cattle Improvement Center Breeding Farm using 0.2% type II collagenase (1148090, Sigma), following the protocol described in our laboratory’s previous studies [30–32]. The pcDNA3.1 MPO plasmid and gene primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Table S3). The process was as follows: The CDS region of MPO (NM_001113298.2) with a length of 2159 bp was inserted into the pcDNA3.1 + vector, using the restriction enzyme sites NheI (EG15552S, Yugong Biotech) and HindIII (ER0501, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The recombinant plasmid (OE-MPO) and the empty pcDNA3.1 + vector (NC) were separately transfected into TOP10 bacterial strains, followed by endotoxin-free plasmid extraction for each (DP118, TIANGEN).

Differentiation and transfection of bovine intramuscular preadipocytes

Bovine intramuscular preadipocytes were cultured in 6-well plates in a 5% CO₂ incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C until they reached 80% confluence. The cells were cultured in complete medium consisting of 90% DMEM/F-12 (SH30023.01, HyClone), 10% fetal bovine serum (ST30-3302p, PAN), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (SV30010, Hyclone). The Lipofectamine™ 3000 Kit (L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to transfect the cells with two plasmids: the 2.5 μg negative control plasmid pcDNA3.1 and the 2.5 μg pcDNA3.1 MPO plasmid. After 48 h of transfection, differentiation was induced using induction solution I (Insulin: 20 μM, IBMX: 1.5 μM, Dexamethasone: 0.1 μM, Rosiglitazone: 0.078 μM). After 2 days, the induction solution was replaced with induction solution II (Insulin: 20 μM) to maintain differentiation. Induction solution II was replaced every 2 days thereafter.

MPO efficiency assessment

When the cell density reached 80%, the cells were treated with three different conditions: transfection with the MPO plasmid and the addition of L-carnitine (3 mM, HY-B0399, MCE) and betaine (2 mM, HY-B0710, MCE). After the 48 h treatment period, the efficiency of MPO gene expression is assessed by real-time quantitative PCR for each condition.

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the intramuscular preadipocytes on day 6 of differentiation using the SevenFast Total RNA Extraction Kit for Cells (SM130, Seven). RNA quality and concentration were determined by absorbance using a microplate reader (TECAN). Reverse transcription and real-time quantitative PCR were performed according to the instructions provided with the Evo M-MLV RT Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR II (AG11728, AG) and PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix Kit (AQ601-02, TransGen Biotech). After obtaining the Cq values, the relative expression levels of the genes are calculated using β-ACTIN and GAPDH as reference genes with the 2^−ΔΔCt method. The primer information is provided in Table S3.

Triglyceride assay

On day 6 of induced differentiation, intramuscular adipocytes were digested with 0.25% trypsin (25200056, Gibco), and the digestion was stopped by adding an equal volume of complete medium. The cells were transferred to a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Triglyceride levels were determined according to the triglyceride assay kit (E1013-105, Applygen), and protein quantification was performed using the protein quantification assay kit (P1511-1, Applygen). Finally, the relative triglyceride content was obtained based on the standard calibration curve.

Oil Red O staining

On day 6 of differentiation, intramuscular adipocytes were washed three times with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were then washed three times with PBS and incubated with Oil Red O working solution (G1260, Solarbio) for 30 min, protected from light. The cells were rinsed with PBS three times, and images were captured using the EVOS® FL auto imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical analysis

Functional enrichment analysis of differential metabolites in rumen fluid and longissimus dorsi muscle

Metabolites with significant differences between the two groups were selected based on t-test p-values and fold change (FC, H-IMF/L-IMF). The significance thresholds were set as follows: t-test p < 0.05 and |log₂ FC|≥ 0.26.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted based on metabolite expression levels using the R package gmodels.

After identifying the differential metabolites, their corresponding compound IDs (C_id) were mapped to KEGG pathways for further enrichment analysis [33]. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the ClusterProfiler and Enrichplot R packages, with hypergeometric tests comparing the differential metabolites to the background metabolome to identify significantly enriched pathways. After multiple testing correction, pathways with Q-value ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. KEGG pathway enrichment results were visualized as bubble plots using ggplot2 and Enrichplot.

Additionally, the MSEA mode of the MetaboAnalystR package was used for quantitative enrichment analysis (QEA), analyzing metabolites across all samples and generating corresponding p-values and enriched metabolites. All analyses were performed in R (v3.6.3), with pathway annotation based on the latest available KEGG database.

Functional annotation of rumen fluid macrogenomes

Unigene sequences were aligned against the nr database, and taxonomic annotations were assigned using the lowest common ancestor (LCA) algorithm in MEGAN (v6.19.9). Species abundance was determined based on gene abundance. DIAMOND (v0.9.24) was used to annotate the Unigenes by aligning them with multiple databases, including Nr, KEGG, eggNOG, CAZy, PHI, VFDB, CARD, MGE, and BacMet.

PCA was performed based on phylum abundance using the R package gmodels. A Venn diagram was generated using the VennDiagram package in R (v3.6.3) to visualize shared and unique species between groups. Differential species were identified by considering presence or absence only, without accounting for abundance. A permutation test was applied across the entire sample set to calculate p-values, followed by multiple testing correction to compute the Q-value. Boxplots were created using ggplot2 in R (v3.6.3) for visualization.

The top 20 species, ranked by average abundance across all samples, were selected using the Gini index as the criterion. Random Forest analysis was performed using the randomForest package.

The top 20 pathways, ranked by average abundance in the KEGG pathway analysis, were selected using the Gini index as the criterion. Random Forest analysis was performed using the randomForest package.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes and proteins in the longissimus dorsi muscle

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package, with read counts as input data. The package normalizes the read counts and calculates p-values based on statistical models. The significance thresholds were set as follows: p-value < 0.05 and |log₂ FC |≥ 0.58. The threshold for differential proteins was set as follows: t-test p-value < 0.05 and |log₂ FC|≥ 0.58.

A Venn diagram was generated using the VennDiagram package in R (v3.6.3) to visualize shared and unique genes (proteins) between groups. PCA was performed based on gene expression and protein abundance using the gmodels package in R.

After identifying differentially expressed genes, their corresponding gene IDs (C_id) were retrieved from the KEGG database [33]. The identified genes were mapped to KEGG pathways for enrichment analysis using the ClusterProfiler and Enrichplot R packages. A hypergeometric test was applied to compare the differentially expressed genes with the background genome to identify significantly enriched pathways. After multiple test correction, pathways with a Q-value ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly enriched, with the Q-value representing the FDR-corrected p-value. KEGG pathway enrichment results were visualized as enrichment circle plots using the circlize and Enrichplot packages.

Based on the gene expression data of all genes, the Signal2Noise algorithm was applied to rank the genes. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and the MSigDB database were used to identify differentially enriched pathways between the two groups. Genes ranked in positions 15–500 were used as input for the GSEA analysis [34]. An enrichment score (ES) for specific gene sets within pathways or GO terms was calculated based on gene set ranking. Permutation tests were then performed to calculate the significance p-value, and the normalized enrichment score (NES) was corrected for multiple testing to obtain the FDR value. Finally, significantly enriched pathways and gene sets were visualized using enrichment plots generated by the clusterProfiler package.

The expression abundance of adipogenesis-related proteins LPL, PLN1, PLN4, and COL6A3 was visualized using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 based on counts values.

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation coefficients between species and metabolites were calculated using the cor.test function in R, with p-values derived from Fisher-Ztransformation. Scatter plots and Heatmaps of key genera and metabolites were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2.

Pearson correlation coefficients between protein expression levels and metabolite abundances were calculated using the cor.test function in R (v3.6.3), with p-values derived from Fisher-Ztransformation. The top 250 differentially expressed proteins and metabolites with the highest correlation coefficients were selected for visualization. A heatmap was generated using the pheatmap package in R to display these correlations. Additionally, proteins significantly correlated with L-carnitine and betaine (p < 0.05) were identified. A dynamic network Venn diagram was constructed using the OmicShare bioinformatics platform (Gene Denovo) to visualize the relationships between significantly correlated differential proteins and metabolites.

The bar chart in the pathway association analysis was generated based on the top 20 enriched pathways, determined by the number of significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs), differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), and their correlations. DEGs and DEPs in the transcriptomic and proteomic data were counted separately, and Venn diagrams were created using the VennDiagram package in R (v3.6.3). A nine-quadrant scatter plot was constructed based on the fold change (FC) values of significant DEGs and DEPs. The pathway enrichment significance plot was generated using the Q-values of enriched pathways associated with significant DEGs and DEPs.

XY scatter plots were generated in Excel to visualize the correlations based on intramuscular fat content, alpha-linolenic acid abundance, N,N,N-trimethyllysine abundance, L-carnitine abundance, betaine abundance, MPO abundance, and the relative abundances of Moheibacter, Cnuella, Gaetbulibacter, and Lacibacter.

Bacterial genera with a p-value < 0.05 and those significantly associated with alpha-linolenic acid were selected. For longissimus dorsi muscle proteins, those with a p-value < 0.05 and |log₂ FC|≥ 0.26 were included. Similarly, for metabolites detected in the cationic mode of longissimus dorsi muscle and rumen fluid, the same selection criteria (p-value < 0.05, |log₂ FC|≥ 0.26) were applied. After MSEA analysis, metabolites enriched in KEGG pathways unrelated to lipid synthesis were excluded. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for microbial abundance, metabolite levels, and gene expression (protein level), retaining only relationships with |r|> 0.85 and p < 0.05. Network visualization was performed using Cytoscape (v3.7.1).

The Gini index was used as the feature selection method, and Random Forest analysis is conducted using the randomForest package to analyze the nine key factors.

Significance was expressed as follows: 0.01 < p < 0.05: marked with “*”, 0.001 < p < 0.01: marked with “**”, and p ≤ 0.0001: marked with “****”.

Results

Intramuscular fat and protein content in the longissimus muscle of Jiaxian Red Cattle

The average intramuscular fat content in group H-IMF was 12.44 g/100 g, while in group L-IMF it was 8.21 g/100 g (Table S4) (Fig. 2 A & B). The average protein content in group H-IMF was 17.89 g/100 g, compared to 18.65 g/100 g in group L-IMF (Fig. 2C). Additionally, the meat color of the longissimus dorsi was evaluated. Rumen fluid metabolites are enriched in multiple fatty acid metabolic pathways

A total of 15,827 metabolites were detected in the rumen fluid under mixed mode, with 6948 metabolites identified in negative ion mode and 8879 in positive ion mode (Tables S6-8). In negative ion mode, the group H-IMF exhibits clear clustering, whereas the group L-IMF shows a more dispersed distribution. The separation between the two groups is not distinct, with partial overlap observed (Fig. S1). Ten differential metabolites were identified between groups H-IMF and L-IMF, with 15 up-regulated and 11 down-regulated. KEGG analysis revealed that these differential metabolites were enriched in pathways, ranked by Q-value, such as biotin metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, arachidonic acid metabolism, and tyrosine metabolism. MSEA analysis further indicated that the top 20 pathways were enriched in mitochondrial β-oxidation of short-chain saturated fatty acids, β-oxidation of very long-chain fatty acids, mitochondrial β-oxidation of long-chain saturated fatty acids, arachidonic acid metabolism, galactose metabolism, mitochondrial β-oxidation of medium-chain saturated fatty acids, thiamine metabolism, valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation, lactose degradation, lysine degradation, and selenoamino acid metabolism (Fig. S2). These results suggest significant involvement of rumen fluid metabolites in the β-oxidation pathway.

In positive ion mode, both the H-IMF and L-IMF groups showed some clustering, with moderate separation observed between the two groups, although there was partial overlap (Fig. S1). Sixteen differential metabolites were identified between groups H-IMF and L-IMF, with 17 upregulated and 15 downregulated (Fig. 3A). MSEA analysis of all identified metabolites revealed enrichment in pathways such as alpha-linolenic acid and linoleic acid metabolism, glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, glycine and serine metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, lactose synthesis, tryptophan metabolism, cysteine metabolism, alanine metabolism, and mitochondrial β-oxidation of medium-chain saturated fatty acids (Fig. 3B). In positive ion mode, both KEGG and MSEA analyses indicated that α-linolenic acid might serve as a key metabolite. As a polyunsaturated fatty acid, α-linolenic acid positively regulates fat deposition. Further analysis of KEGG pathways enriched with α-linolenic acid revealed that six pathways were enriched, accounting for two-thirds of all enriched pathways, highlighting the significant role of this metabolite (Fig. 3D). Notably, the α-linolenic acid metabolism and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis pathways were significantly enriched, suggesting that α-linolenic acid in rumen fluid may promote fat synthesis and deposition in the longissimus dorsi muscle of the H group through these pathways.

Ruminal microorganisms are highly involved in pathways related to organismal development

To further investigate the differences in rumen microbiota composition between beef cattle with varying levels of intramuscular fat deposition, the metagenomic sequencing was performed on the rumen fluid of the beef cattle. The microbiome data from rumen fluid demonstrated the group H-IMF exhibits clear clustering, whereas the group L-IMF shows more dispersed distribution. The separation between the two groups is not distinct, with partial overlap observed (Fig. S1). The group H-IMF and group L-IMF shared 102 phyla, with 13 phyla unique to the group L-IMF and 14 phyla unique to the group H-IMF. Discussion

In multi-omics studies, analyzing individual samples enables exploration of individual-level variability and supports robust statistical inference [24]. However, this approach demands larger sample sizes, higher sequencing costs, and sufficient material from each animal—challenges that intensify with multiple omics layers [25]. To address these limitations, this study adopted a within-group pooling strategy. Pooling reduces biological noise, ensures adequate material for analysis, and emphasizes group-level biological trends. However, it masks inter-individual variation and precludes individual-level statistical comparisons. Therefore, the results represent average group profiles. Future studies could enhance resolution and statistical power by incorporating individual-level validation.

Alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) is an essential fatty acid composed of 18 carbon atoms and 3 cis double bonds, which cannot be synthesized by the body and must be obtained through dietary sources [35]. In this study, ALA, as a key metabolite, not only promoted intramuscular fat deposition in beef cattle but also alleviated oxidative stress caused by the MPO gene during its role in fat deposition and host defense mechanisms. Supplementation with ALA has been shown to effectively reduce serum triglyceride levels and significantly improve cardiovascular risk factors, making it a high-quality polyunsaturated fatty acid [36]. Additionally, literature has reported that ALA can inhibit RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis via the FFAR4/βarr2 signaling pathway and prevent inflammation, which is consistent with the findings of this study [37].

In our results, the genera Cnuella, Gaetbulibacter, Moheibacter, and Lacibacter were significantly positively correlated with ALA, suggesting that these genera play a positive role in the production, transport, or functional activity of ALA [38–43]. These genera have previously been identified in marine, freshwater, and sediment environments, with studies primarily focusing on their roles in carbon and nitrogen cycling [44–48]. Notably, Moheibacter possesses exogenous biodegradation and metabolic capabilities [40], including promoting lignocellulose degradation [41]. Additionally, Moheibacter can colonize the digestive tract of Protaetia brevitarsis larvae and assist in the decomposition of organic matter. It is speculated to have a strong capacity for breaking down and utilizing Auricularia auricula spent mushroom substrate (AASMS) and is likely one of the key functional genera responsible for enhancing the palatability and digestibility of AASMS [49]. Moheibacter was identified as a significantly different microorganism in the gut microbiota of golden pompano on the 5th day of high-fat diet feeding. This suggests that it may be associated with gut microbiota structural changes under high-fat dietary conditions and could play a crucial role in lipid metabolism, gut health, or other physiological processes [50]. Combined with the multi-omics data from this study, it is speculated that this genus may play a critical role in the synthesis of ALA. Additionally, all of these genera were negatively correlated with key metabolites in the longissimus dorsi muscle, including N,N,N-trimethyllysine, L-carnitine, and betaine, suggesting that these genera are highly involved in and contribute to the formation of intramuscular fat in the longissimus dorsi muscle of beef cattle.

Within organisms, the primary function of L-carnitine is to facilitate the oxidation of fatty acids for energy production. Fat metabolism involves transport across the mitochondrial membrane, where fatty acids are oxidized in the mitochondria to release energy for the body. However, this process requires L-carnitine as a carrier to promote the entry of fatty acids into the mitochondria for oxidation and breakdown. Thus, L-carnitine is thought to help increase fatty acid utilization and may help reduce fat accumulation [51–54]. Betaine has been reported to be involved in lipid metabolism, promoting the oxidative metabolism of fats and contributing to the maintenance of lipid homeostasis [55–58], which is consistent with the findings of this study. These results suggest that L-carnitine and betaine play roles in inhibiting intramuscular fat deposition in the longissimus dorsi muscle of beef cattle.

N,N,N-Trimethyllysine is significantly positively correlated with ACSS1 and ACSF2. Each of these factors independently promotes fatty acid oxidation, and their strong correlation suggests a potential synergistic interaction between them. Investigating the mechanisms underlying this interaction holds significant value. Humans can obtain levocarnitine through the endogenous synthesis of trimethyllysine, which is subsequently utilized in metabolic pathways, including mitochondrial β-oxidation [59]. ACSS1 is a mitochondrial matrix enzyme that is strongly expressed in the heart, skeletal muscle, and brown adipose tissue. This isoform performs the same enzymatic reaction as ACSS2 in the mitochondria, primarily generating acetyl-CoA through fatty acid oxidation, which is further oxidized to CO₂ [60]. The protein encoded by ACSF2 is partially characterized as mitochondrial acyl-CoA synthetase, which catalyzes the formation of fatty acyl-CoA from ATP, CoA, and medium-chain fatty acids. This reaction may serve two important functions in alleviating hepatic steatosis: firstly, the bioactivation of medium-chain fatty acids for β-oxidation, and secondly, the production of hexanoyl-CoA and octanoyl-CoA, which inhibit the transcription of lipogenesis genes, such as ACACA and FASN [61]. N,N,N-Trimethyllysine, ACSS1, and ACSF2 each contribute specifically to mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation. However, direct studies on their functional interactions are lacking. Nonetheless, their coordinated interaction likely enhances fatty acid oxidation. Further investigation into these mechanisms will be a promising area for future research.

The MPO gene plays a critical role in innate immune responses by converting hydrogen peroxide and chloride ions into hypochlorous acid (HOCl), thereby generating reactive oxygen species (ROS). Additionally, myeloperoxidase (MPO) is an inflammatory enzyme associated with obesity, hypertension, and heart failure, influencing cellular metabolism and tissue function, including lipid metabolism and adipogenesis in certain contexts. Studies have shown that women with breast cancer exhibit higher MPO levels during exercise, along with increased body and visceral fat, suggesting a potential correlation between MPO and adipogenesis [62]. Additionally, researchers found that MPO levels were significantly elevated in mice fed a high-fat, high-sugar diet [63]. This increase was reversible, returning to normal with a regular diet, suggesting a potential link between diet-induced obesity and increased MPO enzyme activity. In this study, the MPO gene significantly promoted intramuscular fat deposition at both the omics and cellular levels, whereas L-carnitine and betaine significantly inhibited MPO gene expression. Numerous studies have demonstrated that treatment with L-carnitine and betaine significantly suppresses MPO gene expression [64–71]. Conversely, MPO gene overexpression increases total cholesterol, free cholesterol, cholesterol esters, phospholipids, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels in mice [72]. Inhibition of the MPO gene has also been shown to mitigate obesity and liver damage in mice [73]. These findings align with the results of this study and further support its conclusions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Moheibacter, Cnuella, Gaetbulibacter, and Lacibacte regulated the production of alpha-linolenic acid, which is subsequently transported to the longissimus dorsi muscle to participate in intramuscular fat deposition. The inhibition of the fatty acid β-oxidation pathway was coordinated with the lipogenic activity of MPO and other key genes, collectively promoting the accumulation of high intramuscular fat content in beef cattle. Additionally, in vitro cellular assays not only confirmed the coordination between fatty acid β-oxidation and MPO gene expression but also validated the generality and robustness of this regulatory pathway. These findings provide a compelling rationale for improving beef quality through microbiota regulation and offer valuable insights for future studies investigating the role of microbiota-host interactions in intramuscular fat formation.
