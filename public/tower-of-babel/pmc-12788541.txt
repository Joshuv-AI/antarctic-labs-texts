Retrospective Study on Various Surgical Approaches for Gastric Ulcer Perforation. A Comparative Study
Authors: Saurabh Chauhan, Anil Kushwanshi, Sangeeta Rajput, Babita Chauhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Gastric ulcer perforation remains a life-threatening emergency requiring timely surgical intervention. Multiple operative techniques exist, including primary closure, modified Graham’s patch repair, gastrojejunostomy, and use of falciform ligament in cases of omental deficiency.Materials and Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted from January 2020 to December 2024 involving 120 patients diagnosed with gastric ulcer perforation. The study included patients treated at both SRVS Medical College and a local private hospital. Based on intraoperative findings and surgeon preference, patients underwent one of the following surgical techniques: Group A (n = 40)—primary closure; Group B (n = 35)—modified Graham’s patch repair; Group C (n = 25)—gastrojejunostomy for giant perforations; Group D (n = 20)—falciform ligament patch in cases with deficient greater omentum.Results:Group A showed shorter operative time (mean 55 ± 10 min) and hospital stay (mean 6 ± 2 days), but had a 15% rate of minor leakage. Group B had a leakage rate of 8.5%, with slightly longer operative time (mean 70 ± 12 min). Group C, used in giant perforations (>2 cm), had the longest operative time (mean 95 ± 15 min) but the lowest leak rate (4%), albeit with a prolonged hospital stay (mean 10 ± 3 days). Group D showed moderate outcomes with a 10% complication rate and hospital stay of 8 ± 2 days. Mortality was highest in Group A (2 cases), likely due to delayed presentation and peritonitis.Conclusion:Modified Graham’s patch repair and gastrojejunostomy offer better outcomes in terms of leakage prevention and postoperative recovery compared to primary closure. The falciform ligament serves as a viable alternative in cases of omental deficiency.

INTRODUCTION

Gastric ulcer perforation is a common surgical emergency associated with high morbidity and mortality if not treated promptly and effectively. It represents a severe complication of peptic ulcer disease and can lead to generalized peritonitis, septic shock, and death in the absence of timely intervention.[1] The management of perforated gastric ulcers has evolved significantly over the years, ranging from simple closure techniques to complex procedures such as gastrojejunostomy and the use of autologous tissue patches in special scenarios.[2]

Primary closure of the perforation remains the most frequently performed procedure, especially in small perforations found early. However, in cases of larger perforations or friable tissue margins, a reinforced repair using the omentum, as described in the modified Graham’s patch technique, is often preferred due to its improved sealing ability and lower leakage rate.[3] Giant perforations, typically defined as greater than 2 cm in diameter, pose a greater technical challenge and are more prone to postoperative complications; hence, more extensive surgical options, such as gastrojejunostomy, may be warranted.[4]

In rare instances where the greater omentum is necrotic, absent, or insufficient due to previous surgeries or severe inflammation, alternative autologous tissues, like the falciform ligament, can be utilized to reinforce the repair site. The falciform ligament, being anatomically accessible and richly vascularized, has demonstrated satisfactory outcomes in such circumstances.[5]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective comparative study was conducted at SRVS Medical College and a private hospital in Shivpuri, covering a period from January 2020 to December 2024. A total of 120 patients who underwent emergency surgery for gastric ulcer perforation were included in the analysis.

Inclusion criteria

Patients aged 18 years and above who were intraoperatively diagnosed with gastric ulcer perforation and managed surgically with one of the following procedures: primary closure, modified Graham’s patch repair, gastrojejunostomy (for perforations >2 cm), or falciform ligament patching (in cases with absent or necrotic omentum).

Exclusion criteria

Patients with duodenal perforations, malignancy-associated perforations, prior upper GI surgery, or incomplete clinical records were excluded.

Patient grouping

Patients were divided into four groups based on the surgical technique employed:

Group A (n = 40): Primary closure of the perforation using interrupted absorbable sutures.

Data collection

Patient records were reviewed for demographics, clinical presentation, perforation size and location, operative findings, surgical technique used, duration of surgery, postoperative complications (e.g.,leakage, sepsis, wound infection), hospital stay, and mortality. Data were collected and tabulated using Microsoft Excel.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.

RESULTS

A total of 120 patients with gastric ulcer perforation were included in the study. The patients were categorized into four groups based on the surgical approach used: Group A (primary closure, n = 40), Group B (modified Graham’s patch, n = 35), Group C (gastrojejunostomy, n = 25), and Group D (falciform ligament patch, n = 20). The mean age of the study population was 47.6 ± 12.4 years, with a male predominance (M/F ratio = 3.5:1).

DISCUSSION

This retrospective comparative study evaluated four different surgical methods—primary closure, modified Graham’s patch, gastrojejunostomy, and falciform ligament patching—based on intraoperative findings and patient conditions. The results support previous literature indicating that primary closure, while expedient, carries a higher risk of postoperative leakage and complications when used in larger or friable perforations.[1]

Modified Graham’s patch repair remains a preferred technique for moderate-sized perforations due to its simplicity, reinforcement strength, and adaptability. Our study showed a lower leakage rate (8.5%) and moderate operative time in this group, consistent with previous findings demonstrating its efficacy and safety profile in peptic perforation repair.[23] This technique allows for effective sealing by using a vascularized omental flap, which enhances healing and provides mechanical support.

Gastrojejunostomy, though technically more complex and time-consuming, yielded the lowest leakage rate (4%) and no recorded mortality in our study. This aligns with other studies suggesting that gastrojejunostomy offers better long-term outcomes in cases of giant perforations (>2 cm) or when primary repair is not feasible.[45] However, the prolonged operative time and extended hospital stay associated with this approach must be weighed against its benefits, particularly in hemodynamically unstable patients.

In situations where the greater omentum is necrotic, absent, or surgically inaccessible, alternative tissue sources must be considered. The falciform ligament, due to its anatomical proximity and vascularity, has been used as an effective patch material. In our study, this method showed satisfactory outcomes with a 10% leakage rate and acceptable recovery periods, similar to outcomes reported by other surgical series.[67]

CONCLUSION

Modified Graham’s patch and gastrojejunostomy offer superior outcomes in managing gastric ulcer perforations, especially in moderate to large defects. The falciform ligament is a reliable alternative when omental tissue is unavailable. Surgical approach should be tailored based on perforation size, tissue condition, and patient stability to optimize recovery and reduce complications.
