Assessing the Business Process Modeling Competences of Large Language Models Journal: AI-Enhanced Business Process Management
Chantale Lauer, Peter Pfeiffer, Alexander Rombach, Nijat Mehdiyev

Abstract
The creation of Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN) models is a complex and time-consuming task requiring both domain knowledge and proficiency in modeling conventions. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have significantly expanded the possibilities for generating BPMN models directly from natural language, building upon earlier text-to-process methods with enhanced capabilities in handling complex descriptions. However, there is a lack of systematic evaluations of LLM-generated process models. Current efforts either use LLM-as-a-judge approaches or do not consider established dimensions of model quality. To this end, we introduce BEF4LLM, a novel LLM evaluation framework comprising four perspectives: syntactic quality, pragmatic quality, semantic quality, and validity. Using BEF4LLM, we conduct a comprehensive analysis of open-source LLMs and benchmark their performance against human modeling experts. Results indicate that LLMs excel in syntactic and pragmatic quality, while humans outperform in semantic aspects; however, the differences in scores are relatively modest, highlighting LLMs’ competitive potential despite challenges in validity and semantic quality. The insights highlight current strengths and limitations of using LLMs for BPMN modeling and guide future model development and fine-tuning. Addressing these areas is essential for advancing the practical deployment of LLMs in business process modeling.

Although these tools exist, they have not been applied in a systematic manner to LLM-based BPMN modeling yet. This work aims to fill this gap by making the following main contributions:

1.

We propose the BEF4LLM framework, building upon the SIQ framework

Preliminaries

In this section, we want to present and define the basic knowledge to understand this paper, which includes business process modeling with BPMNs, process model quality, and LLMs.

2.1
Business Process Modeling Notation (BPMN)

Foundations and Modeling Elements

Business process modeling refers to the systematic abstraction and representation of organizational activities as structured process models. Such process models capture those aspects of business processes that are pertinent to analysis, communication, or automation objectives, deliberately omitting extraneous detail. Among available modeling approaches, the BPMN standard is extensively adopted in the information systems (IS) discipline due to its expressive power and rigorous semantics
[
38
]
. BPMN specifies four principal categories of modeling constructs
[
14
,
17
]
: (i)
flow objects
(events, activities, gateways), (ii)
connecting objects
(sequence flows, message flows, associations), (iii)
swimlanes
(pools and lanes), and (iv)
artifacts
(data objects, groups, annotations). Data objects, although sometimes discussed separately, are formally classified as artifacts.

Structural Formalization and Notation

Figure
1
presents a BPMN model featuring two processes — one for a customer and another for an organizational entity (“Company xy”). Each process is encapsulated in a pool
\mathcal{PO}
, which may be subdivided into lanes
\mathcal{L}
to represent internal subunits such as “logistics” or “accounting”. Collectively, pools and lanes are termed
swimlanes
.

Figure 1:
Example of a BPMN Model.

Formally, a BPMN process (set
\mathcal{P}
) is modeled as a directed graph composed of flow objects (
\mathcal{FO}
) and flow connections (
\mathcal{F}
). Flow objects include:
events
(
\mathcal{E}
), partitioned into start (
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{S}}
), intermediate (
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{I}}
), and end (
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{E}}
) events — with message events denoted as
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{S}_{\!M}}
,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{I}_{\!M}}
, and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{E}_{\!M}}
;

activities
(
\mathcal{A}
), comprising atomic tasks (
\mathcal{T}
) and subprocesses; and
gateways
(
\mathcal{G}
), classified as parallel (
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{AND}}
), exclusive (
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{XOR}}
), inclusive (
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{OR}}
), or event-based (
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{EVENT}}
), and further as split (
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{S}}
) or join (
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{J}}
) gateways. Corresponding split and join gateways are formally related via a mapping function
Matchgate:\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{S}}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{J}}
. Exception handling is modeled by the partial function
Excp:\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{I}}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{A}
, associating
interrupting
intermediate events with the activities they abort. For any flow object
x\in\mathcal{FO}
,
\mathit{label}(x)
retrieves its textual label (or returns
\varnothing
if unlabeled). Diagrammatically, tasks (rectangles), events (circles), and gateways (diamonds) are visually distinguished, with tasks typically labeled (e.g., “send order”).

Process Connectivity and Control Flow

Flow connections are described by the set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq(\mathcal{FO}\cup\mathcal{PO})\times(\mathcal{FO}\cup\mathcal{PO})
, partitioned into
sequence flows
(
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{S}}
, solid arrows) specifying intra-process execution order, and
message flows
(
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{M}}
, dashed arrows) representing inter-pool communication.
Message flows depict communication between pools or between a flow object and a pool (e.g., a customer sending an order to a company).
For any
x\in\mathcal{FO}
, the set of incoming sequence flows is
in(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{FO}\mid(y,x)\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{S}}\}
, and the set of outgoing sequence flows is
out(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{FO}\mid(x,y)\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{S}}\}
. A
path
is defined as a non-empty sequence of flow objects connected by sequence flows, from a start to an end event.

2.2
Process Model Quality

With process model quality, we refer to the extent to which a process model meets certain standards and criteria. Thereby, frameworks define the scope and terms of process model quality, while metrics provide concrete measurements for assessing the quality of process models. Those metrics enable the measurement of certain aspects of the process model complexity, like process model complexity: size, density, partitionability, connector interplay, cyclicity, and concurrency, edges style, crossing edges, edge angles, symmetry in blocks, as well as the consistency flow
[
48
,
38
]
. These evaluate the process specifications encoded in the process model as well as the process model layout and representation. Those metrics now allow us to analyze and judge the process model and its quality aspects after modeling.

Several frameworks defining process model quality, like SEQUAL
[
31
]
or SIQ
[
42
]
, exist. We build upon the SIQ framework
[
42
]
that defines process model quality through the three dimensions: syntactic, pragmatic, and semantic quality. The abbreviation SIQ also hints at its characteristics, as "it is
S
imple enough to be practically applicable, yet
I
ntegrates the most relevant insights from the BPM field, while it deals with
Q
uality"
[
42
, p.171]
. Thus, the intention behind building the SIQ framework was to establish a simple process quality definition that can easily be used in practice without neglecting important aspects of BPM field.

Figure 2:
SIQ Framework
[
42
]
.

The SIQ framework, as shown in
Figure 2
is based on three basic types of process model quality displayed in the center: syntactic, pragmatic, and semantic quality. The "wall" surrounding the heart of the framework concerns the establishment of the validity of the solution. For each of the three approaches, validation, verification, and certification, different approaches can be used to inspect the degree of validity. Lastly, there is a wall of ensuring, which introduces concepts, namely "truth-by-design", "understandable-by-design", and "Correct-by-design", that should prevent any threats to the correctness of the process model.

2.3
Large Language Models

We refer to a Large Language Model (LLM) as a language model, pretrained on large amounts of text, that exhibits strong knowledge about natural language and the world around us
[
24
]
. It processes and generates natural language, making it a generative machine learning model. LLMs are neural networks with billions of trainable parameters, comprising stacked layers of transformer blocks with the attention mechanism
[
52
]
. Such models can be prompted, i.e., they generate answers based on the instruction in the prompt and the input received. This makes them particularly useful for solving a variety of tasks.

provides an overview of open-source LLM families that will be used in the benchmark presented in

The context length defines the maximum number of tokens an LLM can process at once, limiting how much information it can take into account when generating the output.
Another important parameter for LLMs is the temperature, which controls the uncertainty or randomness in the generation process

Training LLMs is a multi-step procedure. First, the model is pretrained like other language models in a self-supervised manner by predicting the next token in a sentence on corpora of billions of tokens
[
24
]
. Post-training further trains the model to follow instructions, i.e., prompts, and to ensure that the model’s responses align with human needs. Therefore, during instruction finetuning, the model is given instruction-response pairs where the model is trained to generate the response. Although the model generates the answer by predicting token-by-token as during pretraining, this training step is supervised since the responses are curated with human supervision
[
24
]
. Thereby, the model solves a meta-objective by learning to follow instructions. Finally, to ensure that the model responses align with human values and needs (and to prevent harmful or toxic responses), the LLM is finetuned to generate responses from human feedback, often involving reinforcement learning techniques. Note that some LLMs are not instruction-tuned, usually resulting in lower performance in following instructions in prompts.

In order to reduce the computational and memory costs of LLM, a technique called quantization is often applied
[
12
]
. The idea is to reduce the representation of weights and activations from high-precision data types like 32-bit floating point to lower-precision ones like 8-bit integer, which can be processed faster by processors. Quantization comes at the cost of a loss in precision and a drop in performance of the LLM. The lower the target data type, the greater the precision loss. One of the most common quantization cases is going from 32-bit float to 8-bit integer, which typically offers a good balance between computational costs and precision
[
12
]
.

3.1
Process Model Quality Frameworks and Assessment

This subsection summarizes related work on both process quality frameworks and process model quality assessment. The frameworks provide an overview of what constitutes process model quality, but do not specify how to evaluate it. To address this gap, process quality assessment methods were developed, enabling the measurement of the process model quality based on the aggregation of different process model metrics.

The first step in establishing the term process model quality was guidelines for process modeling, like Guidelines of Modeling (GoM)
[
1
]
or the 7 process modeling guidelines (G7)
[
36
]
. They are meant to be used by non-experts while creating a process model to ensure that the resulting process model has a decent quality. However, those guidelines only provide tools to ensure a high quality of the process model, but do not define the term process quality.

Next to the SIQ framework, SEQUAL
[
31
]
is also a common framework to define the process model quality. It does not only focuses on a process model to be knowledge, but also as a contribution to knowledge when it is interpreted by a human or another intelligent agent. As
Figure 3
shows, there are six quality dimensions, which can be derived from the relation of the different requirements needed for interactive process models. In
[
32
]
, the SEQUAL model is especially adapted to business process modeling, whereby aspects specific to business processes are included.

Figure 3:
SEQUAL framework
[
31
]
.

Since process modeling has so far been conducted by human experts, most work centers around quality assessments with a human perspective. The assessments aggregate existing metrics that measure specific characteristics of a process model. Often, the metrics are mapped to process quality dimensions or perspectives of the process, to allow a better interpretation of the scores.

The work
[
27
]
aims at measuring the pragmatic quality of a process model, based on different pragmatic quality metrics. To do so, they use the metrics to measure the degree of fulfillment of the seven modeling guidelines G7. However, a weak point of such works is that they only focus on one dimension, while a detailed analysis demands evaluating a process model from multiple quality perspectives.

In
[
34
]
, a tool is presented that evaluates the quality of business process models based on different metrics and process perspectives. In a first step, the quality metric framework for BPMN is presented, which is based on the four process perspectives: functional, behavioral, organizational, and informational. For each dimension, multiple metrics are collected, which are later used to measure the quality. All in all, the paper used 9 separate metrics, which mostly focus on pragmatic quality aspects. After that, they present the implemented tool, allowing for the assessment of the metric values for a given BPMN. For each process perspective, each metric is mapped to at least one of the four quality dimensions, namely maintainability, comprehension, reuse, and redesign. Further, for each metric, the user can provide thresholds, which are seen as optimal values for the concerned metric and a priority value. Based on this information, an assessment for each quality dimension in the chosen perspective is computed. As the thresholds are not fixed, the evaluations done by different individuals are not comparable, as they most likely use different thresholds.
The BPMIMA framework
[
44
]
is a framework used to support the improvement of business process models. The framework has three stages. It starts with
measuring
the process model quality based on different empirically proven metrics. Based on this, the evaluation of the measurements starts. As the pure numerical results for pragmatic quality metrics are only informative for comparative purposes, they employ thresholds for the
evaluation
to indicate at which value the process quality starts to decline. Contrary to
[
34
]
, empirically proven thresholds were used, not user-chosen, allowing an objective interpretation of the metric scores. Finally, based on the evaluation results,
redesign
actions are indicated, aiming at increasing the process model quality.

The drawback of the approaches shown in
[
34
]
and
[
44
]
is that only a part of the process quality is measured, as certain aspects are disregarded. Both are neglecting the semantic quality aspects. While
[
34
]
omitted syntactic quality aspects entirely,
[
44
]
included them partially through the correctness dimension. However, this dimension only measures the likelihood of an error occurring, based on different pragmatic quality measures. But it is not examined whether the process model actually contains errors.

Ullrich
[
49
]
, on the other hand, presents an e-assessment platform that enables an automated assessment of different graphical modeling tasks by including all three quality dimensions.
The tasks can be based on different modeling languages like BPMN, EPC, Entity Relationship (ER) models, UML, and Petri nets. The platform is to be used in the context of learning and improving the competences needed for process modeling, e.g., for students. The assessment, as presented in
[
49
]
, mainly focused on the usage of Petri nets. Four checkers were implemented, which assess the solution of, e.g., a student, for tasks related to graphical process modeling. For each quality dimension of SIQ, a separate checker was implemented, i.e., a syntactic checker, a pragmatic checker, and a semantic checker, checking different aspects of the quality dimension through several metrics. Moreover, they implemented a reachability checker, focusing on the specific characteristics that a Petri net as a workflow net should possess. Based on the results of the checker, a score for each checker is calculated and displayed, but also the issues in the process model are marked and explained in the form of a text.

3.2
LLMs in BPM

LLMs have quickly found application in BPM tasks, such as process modeling or automating certain tasks. The applicability and advantages of its use are demonstrated both in theory and in practice through various publications.

According to Norouzifar et al.
[
39
]
, LLMs can effectively bridge the gap between process knowledge expressed in natural language and the discovery of robust process models. They propose to construct a process model based on declarative rules, which are translated by the LLM based on the textual input. This approach aims to guide the construction of the process model, ensuring that it aligns with both domain knowledge and actual process executions.
Grohs et al.
[
21
]
find that LLMs with prompting perform comparably to or better than existing specialized solutions on tasks such as mining imperative or declarative process models from textual descriptions.
An implemented tool is presented by Kourani et al.
[
30
]
, utilizing commercial LLMs like OpenAI’s LLMs
[
18
]
to generate a process model based on a textual description. Moreover, they implemented the opportunity for iterative refinement of the generated BPMNs through user feedback, allowing users to suggest changes and receive adapted models in response.
Also, first benchmarks, like
[
4
]
, confirm that LLMs can automate the generation and refinement of BPMN models from textual descriptions. Ziche et al.
[
56
]
present a real-world case study where an LLM-based chatbot was successfully deployed at the Hilti Group to support human process modelers in creating process flow diagrams, demonstrating real-world applicability of LLM-driven process model generation.

LLMs have a broader range of application opportunities in the BPM field, beyond process modeling, extending to various other BPM tasks, which are showcased through multiple experiments, studies, and benchmarks. The study
[
21
]
finds that, apart from mining process models, LLMs are also applicable to assess tasks for robotic process automation with a better or similar performance to existing solutions. LLMs also have a strong performance in object-centric process mining
[
5
]
. In
[
23
]
, OpenAI LLM was used to transform complex process mining data into intuitive formats, significantly improving the efficiency and precision of process analysis. The practical relevance and application of LLMs in BPM tasks are showcased in
[
41
]
. The authors detail four use cases spanning different domains where LLMs, combined with explainable AI and process intelligence, are used for process modeling, prediction, decision support, and automation.

The advantage of using LLMs for BPM is presented in studies like
[
9
]
, which demonstrate that conversational AI through LLMs can significantly enhance the interpretability and usability of AI-augmented BPM Systems. Another advantage of using LLMs in the field of BPM is that people with a lower level of expertise can handle BPM tasks. Large Process Models (LPMs)
[
25
]
, which combine the generative capabilities of LLMs with the analytical precision of traditional BPM tools, are envisioned to lower the expertise required for BPM tasks, improve process observability by integrating structured and unstructured data, and support more continuous, automated process improvement. The authors conclude that while LLM-augmented BPM is already feasible for modeling and analysis, fully automated, context-aware BPM driven by LPMs remains a long-term goal requiring further research.

3.3
LLM Benchmarks

The rapid advancement of LLMs has led to significant progress across many different tasks and domains. To systematically evaluate these models’ capabilities, a plethora of benchmarks have been proposed — each designed to target different linguistic, reasoning, and domain-specific challenges. One prominent example is LiveBench
[
53
]
, which evaluates LLMs in 21 diverse tasks across 7 domains, like math, coding, or reasoning. The LLMs’ performance is evaluated based on their responses to an extensive set of over 1000 questions, which are continually updated to reflect new information and challenges. The LLM-generated answers are evaluated against an objective ground truth, which serves as the basis for computing the LLM’s score. The format of the answers and the scoring methodology are tailored to the specific domain being assessed.

However, the diversity and specialization of LLM benchmarks have also revealed gaps in coverage for certain domains, such as BPM, where the unique requirements and complexities demand tailored evaluation metrics and datasets. Recognizing this, there has been a growing effort within the research community to establish dedicated benchmarks for BPM-related tasks.

3.3.1
LLM Benchmarks for BPM Tasks

Currently, significant efforts are being made to establish comprehensive benchmarks for various BPM tasks, evaluating on single or multiple tasks. Further, the evaluation of LLMs in BPM tasks can be realized with different approaches as shown by Betri et al.
[
3
]
. The first approach is automated, utilizing either an LLM-as-a-judge or a metric-based method. Alternatively, evaluations can also be performed by human judges or through self-evaluation by the evaluated LLM. In the following, corresponding benchmarks with different evaluation strategies in the context of BPM will be presented.

In
[
19
]
, a benchmark is presented testing the ability of an LLM to generate sound answers to questions about a business process model. For instance, on the relationship and order between tasks in the process, or if the execution of one task causes the execution of another. These questions can be answered with "yes" or "no", allowing the calculation of a score for the correctly answered questions.

Further, multiple benchmarks have been established, which not only focus on one task in BPM but also benchmark multiple tasks together. The benchmark by
[
8
]
tests 4 different BPM tasks: activity recommendation, identification of RPA candidates, process question answering, and mining of declarative process models. The benchmark compares open-source and commercial LLMs to point out task-related performance differences.
WONDERBREAD
[
55
]
is the first benchmark for evaluating multimodal foundation models (including LLMs) on BPM tasks beyond automation. It covers different tasks like workflow documentation generation, knowledge transfer and process improvement, or the validation of workflow completion.

3.3.2
LLM Benchmarks for Process Modeling

In this part, we focus on benchmarks that evaluate the ability of LLMs to generate imperative or declarative process models.
The chatbot, called PRODIGY, deployed at the Hilti Group
[
56
]
was evaluated based on human judgment. To do so, the users of PRODIGY had to return feedback via a 90-minute semi-structured interview. Based on the feedback, the quality of PRODIGY was assessed. The evaluation is limited to 9 participants.

Most previously mentioned benchmarks applied an automatic evaluation procedure.
The PM-LLM-Benchmark by
[
4
]
used an LLM-as-a-judge approach. The LLM-generated process models, like Petri nets or BPMNs, are evaluated through an LLM that ranks the process models on a scale from 1 to 10. As no ground truth is provided for the ranking, the scoring of the LLM is rather subject to the preferences of the LLM. Further, there is a risk that the LLM fails to recognize issues in the process model, especially when the same LLM is used for evaluation as for generation. The benchmark compares the LLMs on fewer than a dozen samples.
Other benchmarks implemented a metric-based approach, so the score of the LLMs is based on different metrics that calculate the quality of the LLM-generated BPMN. In
[
8
]
, the generation of declarative process models by LLMs is evaluated based on the F1-score, with the focus on the modeling language DECLARE. To do so, they provided a ground truth model for each textual description. As declarative process models consist of constraints of different types, the true positives, false positives, and false negatives per constraint type are assessed to calculate the precision and recall for each type of constraint, as well as across all constraint types. Based on those metrics, the F1 score, being the harmonic mean of precision and recall, is again calculated for each type of constraint, as well as across all constraint types. The F1 score is used as the final score for each LLM to be compared to other LLMs.

There is already a benchmark solely focusing on the generation of process models. In
[
29
]
, the ability of LLMs to generate process models in the modeling language POWL is evaluated. The evaluation of the outputs is done by simulating event logs based on the generated process models and comparing these event logs with the help of conformance checking to a ground event log simulated through a ground truth process model. Additionally, they took the time efficiency of the generation into consideration for the evaluation. The dataset for the evaluation consisted of 20 business process models.

Contrary to the LLM-as-a-judge approach as presented in
[
4
]
, the metric-based approach allows a more detailed analysis of the abilities of LLM. This requires that a large number of metrics be used for evaluation. However, the previously mentioned metric-based approaches only employ a small number of metrics, which does not allow for a detailed analysis. Further, to provide a detailed picture, metrics for all quality dimensions, so syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic quality, must be included in the analysis. This is a limitation of the approaches presented by Busch et al.
[
8
]
and Kourani et al.
[
29
]
, which do not cover all quality dimensions and miss, e.g., the syntactic quality.

4
BEF4LLM - BPMN Evaluation Framework for LLMs

In this section, the BEF4LLM framework for evaluating the abilities of LLMs to generate BPMNs is presented. First, the motivation and goal of the framework are shown. Following this, the elements of the framework, along with the calculation of scores, are explained in greater detail.

First experiments with LLM showed promising results in BPMN modeling
[
30
,
21
]
with differences between LLM families
[
4
]
. However, previous work lacks a detailed assessment of the generated BPMN models based on established process model quality frameworks and metrics, such as size, density, and sequentiality. For this, the BEF4LLM framework has been developed that allows for a detailed evaluation of LLM-generated BPMN models, highlighting the strengths and weaknesses of different LLMs. Similar to
[
49
]
, we extend the SIQ framework, focusing on BPMNs by mapping multiple process model metrics to each quality dimension. We preferred SIQ over SEQUAL as the latter includes social factors that are beyond the scope of a quality assessment of BPMNs. To ensure a detailed picture, it is important that each metric measures different aspects of the quality dimension. Other frameworks proposed in
[
34
]
and
[
44
]
neglect, e.g., syntactical errors, omitting important quality aspects.

In contrast, the BEF4LLM framework, displayed in
Figure 4
, measures the quality of BPMN models in four dimensions - syntactic quality, pragmatic quality, semantic quality, as well as validity of the BPMN-XML file. The latter is required since LLMs are generative models, leading to the possibility that generated BPMNs do not comply with file formats given in BPMN 2.0 regulations
[
22
]
. In contrast, the validity of human-generated process model files is typically ensured through the tool they use for process modeling. For instance, a graphical modeling editor always generates a valid BPMN-XML file. While a validity check is therefore not required for human-generated BPMNS, it is necessary for LLM-generated ones. Validity is an important aspect since only a valid BPMN-XML file can be displayed and used by subsequent software. It is a single measure that evaluates whether the generated XML file is a valid BPMN 2.0 file using the XSD scheme.

Figure 4:
BEF4LLM - BPMN Evaluation Framework for LLMs.

The syntactic dimension focuses on syntactic rules that should be obeyed when modeling a BPMN model. Thereby, universal as well as modeling language-specific rules are included. Pragmatic quality measures the understandability of the process model for humans through different measures like size, density, connector interplay, concurrency, or partitionability. There are, for example, big process models that are precise in terms of semantic quality and obey all modeling rules. But due to the high number of tasks, gateways, and connections, it is nearly impossible for a human to interpret it. However, a process model is only useful when a human can read and interpret it, so modelers strive for a high pragmatic quality. The semantic quality aspects are assessed based on the similarity between the generated BPMN models and the ground truth BPMNs, evaluated across three categories: natural language, graph structure, and behavior.

In total, the BEF4LLM framework comprises 39 individual metrics: One for validity, 16 for syntactic quality, 15 for pragmatic quality, and seven for semantic quality. Each metric takes the generated BPMN as input (as well as the ground truth BPMN model for the semantic quality) and returns a value between 0 (worst) and 1 (best).

Each generated BPMN is assessed in two steps. First, the validity of the BPMN is checked. Only valid BPMNs are further evaluated for their syntactic, pragmatic, and semantic quality, since the quality can only be checked if the BPMN model is free of invalid BPMN-XML elements. Per quality dimension, the metrics are aggregated to a single score, i.e.,
Q_{\text{syn}}
for the syntactic quality,
Q_{\text{prag}}
for the pragmatic quality, and
Q_{\text{sem}}
for the semantic quality. Therefore, normalization is necessary, ensuring that all metrics are measured on the same scale, ranging from 0 to 1. As each dimension has different types of metrics, each dimension has another normalization strategy, explained in the corresponding section. Further, we decided to introduce a total process quality score
Q_{\text{qual}}
and a total score
Q_{\text{total}}
. While the total process quality score
Q_{\text{qual}}
only aggregates the three scores of the process quality dimensions, i.e., the syntactic, pragmatic, and semantic quality, the total score
Q_{\text{total}}
additionally includes the validity. The total score provides us with a general overview of the abilities of LLMs and enables easy comparison using a single metric. We introduced the total quality score as well, as it gives the opportunity to compare the capabilities to humans, for which the validity score is not relevant. In addition, this value makes it possible to estimate the quality of valid BPMNs or to compare them easily.

The framework allows for a standardized procedure to evaluate LLM capabilities in BPMN modeling based on established metrics. Thereby, a systematic analysis per dimension and between LLMs can be made. However, note that the layout of the BPMN is not part of the assessment and therefore not checked. The layout can be added algorithmically to a BPMN-XML, omitting the requirement to generate it with an LLM.

5
Experiment settings

In the following, we explain how we conducted the benchmark, evaluating the ability of several open-source LLMs to generate BPMNs based on textual descriptions. With the help of the benchmark, we want to answer the following questions:

1.

Which LLMs are the best to use for process modeling based on a textual description?

2.

How far does the size of the LLM influence the quality of the generated process models?

3.

What is the difference in the quality of a process model generated by an LLM in contrast to a process model modeled by a human?

To answer those questions, the BEF4LLM framework and the different quality scores are used. The individual scores give a detailed picture of the modeling abilities of different LLMS, while the
Q_{\text{qual}}
score allows for comparing the quality of human-generated BPMNs to LLM-generated ones. Next, the concrete procedure, including prompting, LLM choices, and the implementation details, is shown. This setting is largely similar to the one presented in
[
33
]
.

5.5
Quality Score Calculation & Implementation Details

For each sample, the
Q_{\text{val}}
score is computed. The quality scores
Q_{\text{syn}}
,
Q_{\text{prag}}
, and
Q_{\text{sem}}
can only be computed if the resulting BPMN is valid. Similarly, if the LLM did not return a result after six minutes, the attempt was treated as invalid and no quality scores were calculated for this sample. This issue happened randomly and without any pattern or relation to a certain sample. Therefore, we attribute this to the LLM generating text infinitely, which is a technical issue.
Additionally, if the resulting BPMN consists of less than two nodes (all ground truth BPMN models have more than 2 nodes), no quality scores can be calculated, and the run is also counted as invalid. Therefore, the
Q_{\text{val}}
can only be
1
if and only if a valid BPMN-XML with at least two flow objects has been returned after 6 minutes. Otherwise, the score is
0
.

Since we intentionally created variation in the generated BPMNs by setting the temperature to
0.1
, five runs per sample are conducted and averaged. In total, for each LLM, 5 runs for each of the 105 samples are performed and averaged. In the results section, we will report the average values, indicated by
\overline{Q}
, for all quality scores introduced before.

5.6
Statistical Evaluation

To determine if the observed performance variations between LLMs were statistically significant, we adopted a robust, non-parametric evaluation framework. This approach was chosen because our quality scores are ordinal (rank-based) and our dataset has missing values whenever an LLM fails to produce a valid BPMN-XML, making standard parametric tests unsuitable.

Our analysis began with a global test using the Skillings-Mack test
[
10
]
, selected for its unique ability to handle missing data where other methods like the Friedman test would fail. This test ranks the valid LLM outputs for each prompt to determine if any significant performance variations exist across the entire group, yielding a test statistic
T
which is compared against a
\chi^{2}
distribution. Once a global effect was confirmed, we described each LLM’s performance by calculating the mean score (
\bar{y}
) of its valid outputs.

Finally, to isolate specific performance gaps, we conducted head-to-head comparisons using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test
[
20
]
, a powerful method chosen because our data consists of paired samples (i.e., two LLMs evaluated on the same textual description) and does not assume a normal distribution. To ensure the integrity of these multiple comparisons, we applied a strict Bonferroni correction, a conservative method selected to prevent false positives rigorously. This technique adjusts each raw p-value
p
from the
m
tests to
p_{\text{adj}}=\min(mp,1)
, and a difference was only deemed significant if this adjusted value fell below 0.05.

7
Discussion

In the following, we discuss the BEF4LLM framework, the results of the experiments, as well as limitations of our work.

7.1
BEF4LLM Framework

The BEF4LLM framework, presented in this work, is the first LLM-specific framework for assessing and comparing their performance in BPMN modeling, focusing on qualitative aspects of the generated process models. It gives a detailed view of the syntactical, pragmatic, and semantic quality, as well as the validity of the BPMNs. It’s grounding the established process model quality metrics and frameworks allows for a detailed and systematic evaluation of LLMs using 39 different metrics. Validity, which has been added as a fourth dimension to BEF4LLM due to the generative nature of LLMs, adds an important aspect to LLM-driven BPMN modeling: The generated BPMNs are only useful if they conform to the BPMN standard, which is often not the case when generating BPMN models with LLMs. Even if an LLM scores high in the three quality dimensions, it is not useful when most BPMN models are invalid and thus not parsable. In combination with the three quality dimensions, they allow for a detailed and extensive analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of LLMs in BPMN modeling.

The BEF4LLM framework is adaptable in terms of the metrics it consists of, as well as the modeling languages covered. New metrics can be added or existing ones removed based on their relevance. Further, most metrics can also be computed on other modeling languages like Petri nets or EPCs, which allows adapting the framework to those languages. Currently, all metrics and dimensions in the BEF4LLM framework are weighted equally. If there is evidence that certain metrics or dimensions are more important than others, the weights can be adjusted accordingly.

7.2
Strengths and Weaknesses of LLMs in BPMN modeling

The experimental results reveal several strengths and weaknesses of LLMs in BPMN modeling. In the following, we will elaborate on them.
The high pragmatic scores disclose a strength of LLMs in generating BPMN models that are easy to understand by humans, visible by the high
Q_{\text{prag}}
consistently above 0.8. Similarly, the syntactic scores
Q_{\text{syn}}
of more than 0.75 for all LLMs hint at strong performance in following formal BPMN 2.0 syntax rules. However, deficits remain in the semantic quality of the generated BPMNs.

Further inspection of specific quality metrics revealed common limitations. In syntactic quality, many LLMs frequently violated the requirement of matching join gateways for each split gateway, suggesting a systemic issue.
Pragmatic quality metrics, particularly sequentiality and separability, were often lower (around 0.5), indicating that LLMs often generated processes with multiple parallel paths or loops and limited block structuring. This makes the BPMN appear unorganized.
Within semantic quality, labeling accuracy (natural language subgroup) consistently scored lower than structural semantic quality metrics. This indicates that the LLMs have issues in generating fitting labels.

A major weakness of LLMs is their ability to generate valid BPMN-XML files, even if not including the layout information, and allowing for one refinement try. Only Llama 3.1 70b reached a validity correctness of above 90%, allowing it to be used in practice. There is substantial variability among LLMs, with
Q_{\text{prag}}
values ranging from 0.3067 for Falcon 3 10b to 0.9733 for Llama 3.1 70b.
Nevertheless, no specific textual description consistently led to invalid outputs across all LLMs, suggesting validity issues are primarily LLM-specific rather than input-dependent. However, generating a valid BPMN-XML file can be considered a challenge in its own, given the length of a typical BPMN-XML file and its strict requirements.

Additionally, operational challenges such as frequent generation time-outs, particularly in smaller LLMs, were identified. Further, the limited context length, especially for smaller LLMs, can quickly become a major issue in conversational BPMN-generating settings. In the current setting, the input given to the LLMs is already close to the limits of some LLMs like Phi 4 with 14k context length. If the context length is exceeded, important information for the LLM is lost. Further, we noticed that some LLMs start to generate unrelated content when the context length is exceeded. Therefore, more compact BPMN representations are required
[
7
]
.

Different LLMs show irregular variation in the generated BPMN. While we intentionally aimed for some variety in the output of the LLMs to obtain different BPMN models, the variation was quite stark. This holds for the generated BPMNs within the same run, but also the generated BPMNs from the same LLM across the five runs. Some LLMs generated very different BPMN models with highly variable quality scores even for the same input across the five runs. Llama 3.1 70b was the LLMs performing most deterministically.

Overall,
Llama 3.1 70b
performed best across the LLMs and significantly better than any other LLM if the total score is considered. However, smaller LLMs like
Llama 3.1 8b
offer comparable BPMN quality at significantly lower computation time, potentially compensating for the worse validity scores. In summary, the descriptive analysis highlights promising capabilities in syntactic and pragmatic dimensions while underscoring significant limitations in semantic accuracy, output consistency, and validity. These findings provide clear insights into areas requiring future LLM development and refinement.

7.3
Comparison of BPMN-Modeling between LLMs and Human Experts

Interestingly, the scores of the LLMs and human experts are largely comparable and fall within a similar range. They even show a similar tendency, i.e., the scores of both LLMs and experts tend to be low in the same areas. However, a significant difference can be found by comparing the scores for the individual BPMNs. The quality scores of LLM-generated BPMNs often exhibit a high variance, while the quality scores of human-created BPMNs show a relatively low variance. This suggests that human experts, in contrast to an LLM, tend to create process models with a relatively consistent level of quality. However, it is worth noting that even for human experts, certain metrics such as partitionability exhibit a high degree of variability in scores, suggesting that for some quality aspects, consistency can be challenging to achieve even among skilled modelers.

Overall, the results indicate that LLM can compete with the abilities of humans regarding the process model capabilities measured within the BEF4LLM framework, with similar strengths and weaknesses. This result can also be interpreted as certain LLMs reaching the human "baseline" of modeling competencies. This can be explained by the fact that LLMs are also trained on human-generated BPMN models.

7.4
Limitations

Several limitations apply to the design of the framework and the conducted experiments.
For now, the framework includes a considerable number of curated metrics. However, more metrics exist than can enhance the significance of the framework and extend it towards other aspects not covered for now, e.g., cyclicity metrics.
The layout is not considered yet, but has an impact on the pragmatic quality since the positioning of elements has an influence on the readability of the BPMN
[
49
]
. However, we did not find established metrics with defined thresholds that could effectively assess the impact of the layout on the comprehensibility of BPMN models. This type of metric is therefore not consistent with the other metrics used in the pragmatic quality dimension, hindering its integration in the BEF4LLM framework. Further, not generating the layout with an LLM reduces the amount of tokens required significantly, since the layout makes up almost 70% of a BPMN-XML
[
7
]
. It can, by contrast, easily be added algorithmically to a BPMN-XML. Therefore, we argue that the layout is not a relevant aspect for LLM-driven BPMN modeling.

Although the pragmatic scores are normalized by empirically validated thresholds, they are still affected by the overall size of the resulting BPMN model. If a complex process is described, resulting in a large BPMN model, this leads to a worse pragmatic score compared to a simple BPMN model. This tendency is also validated by the statistical tests with smaller LLMs, generating smaller BPMN models, reaching better pragmatic scores compared to larger LLMs generating larger BPMNs. Nevertheless, none of the 105 samples used in the experiments demands creating a disproportionately large BPMN model.

Further, the quality scores can only be calculated if the BPMN-XML is valid, which affects the interpretation of the results. Although the statistical tests using the Skillings-Mack test can deal with missing data and the results are therefore sound, the computed quality scores are still subject to being computed on potentially different subsets of BPMNs. We opted for having the validity as a separate dimension instead of rating invalid BPMNs with 0 in all quality dimensions to give a detailed picture of the ability of LLMs to generate valid BPMNs. This ability is indispensable for the practical applicability of LLMs. However, alternatives exist in existing research that propose process model formats that LLMs might understand more easily, e.g., in the form of a JSON
[
30
]
.
Finally, the BEF4LLM framework does not yet include LLM-specific metrics like the run-time of LLMs or memory requirements, which should be added in the future, since the waiting time is also an important factor for the applicability of such a solution.

The experiments are also affected by several limitations. In general, the comparatively low number of available text-BPMN pairs of only 105 samples limits the generalizability of the results. Similarly, the comparison with human experts is based on only nine different textual descriptions and 45 samples in total, limiting its significance. Therefore, one should interpret the results with these limitations in mind. The temperature, potentially affecting the quality of the generated BPMN, was also fixed to
0.1
. Although initial experiments with other values did not result in better scores (neither lower temperature nor higher), the parameter has an effect on the quality of the generated BPMNs. Further research is required to determine the effect of this parameter. Due to the high token count of BPMN-XML files, the input given to the LLMs might be exceeded for some LLMs, resulting in unpredictable results. Especially when the refinement loop is triggered, where the input to the LLM also contains to currently faulty BPMN-XML, this quickly happens. Also, a comparison to commercial LLMs like those from OpenAI or Google is missing.
Finally, a human interpretation of the generated BPMNs by the LLMs is missing. For now, the BPMNs are automatically inspected. However, since they are intended to be used by humans, a human judgment of the result is essential and might change the overall interpretation of the results.

8
Conclusion

This paper presented the BEF4LLM framework, accompanied by an extensive benchmark of recent and prominent open-source LLMs, to assess and compare their abilities in generating BPMN models from textual descriptions. Building upon the well-established SIQ framework and extending it to capture validity aspects unique to generative LLMs, our approach provides the first systematic and in-depth evaluation of LLMs’ capabilities for BPMN modeling.

Our findings reveal insightful distinctions regarding the strengths and weaknesses of open-source LLMs, particularly in comparison to a selected group of human modeling experts. Notably, LLM performance on key aspects, as measured by our framework, is often comparable to that of human modelers. Larger LLMs do not always yield better results; in fact, increased parameter counts sometimes correlate with reduced pragmatic quality in generated BPMNs. Substantial differences exist between LLM families, with the larger models from the Llama 3 family achieving the highest overall scores. Nonetheless, generating valid BPMN-XML files remains a significant hurdle for most LLMs, further amplified by context window limitations.

Our results also demonstrate that advances in benchmark scores for LLMs do not automatically translate to improved performance in the text-to-BPMN task. Instead, instruction-tuned variants consistently outperform their non-instruction-tuned counterparts, suggesting these should be favored in practice. However, the often-undisclosed nature of instruction-tuned LLMs limits their broader adoption.

From an application standpoint, several LLMs show strong potential for practical use in text-to-BPMN tasks and conversational process modeling. Even without perfect results, these models can address the “blank-page” problem in process modeling, enabling more users to generate high-quality BPMN models with greater ease. In this way, AI-driven process design can substantially reduce barriers to entry for process modeling.

The results also aid future research. There is a great necessity for more compact and comprehensible BPMN representations to reduce token usage during generation—thereby making generation more efficient and increasing validity rates. Further, addressing LLMs’ deficiencies in semantic quality through targeted fine-tuning for BPMN tasks holds promise. The BEF4LLM framework can facilitate the development of such approaches by providing reliable feedback through its metrics. Finally, training LLMs with multi-turn conversational data could further enable robust deployment in chat-based process modeling scenarios.
