Occurrence of Cold Agglutinin Disease among Patients with Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia: A Hungarian Single-Center Experience
Authors: Boglárka Brugós, Márta Kerecsenyi, Zsófia Simon, Árpád Illés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCold agglutinin disease is a rare autoimmune hemolytic anemia, a low-grade lymphoproliferative disease. Our objective was to determine the occurrence of cold agglutinin disease among patients with autoimmune hemolytic anemia and to map its specific characteristics, with a particular focus on thromboembolic complications.MethodsIn a Hungarian hematology center, we retrospectively studied clinical data, laboratory parameters, and therapy of 84 patients with autoimmune hemolytic anemia.ResultsA total of 17% of patients had cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia, with 8 patients fulfilling the criteria for cold agglutinin disease and 7 patients having cold agglutinin syndrome. Most patients were older than 70 years, and the disease was more common in women (49 women, 35 men). Here, 71% of patients with CAD were administered rituximab, while some received additional chemotherapy. The 10-year survival rate was significantly worse in cold-type hemolytic anemia than in warm-type autoimmune hemolytic anemia (66.7% vs. 82.4%). Congenital and acquired causes of thrombophilia were examined in patients with severe hemolysis (hemoglobin <60 g/L, LDH >1.5× the upper limit of normal) and in cases with thromboembolic complications.ConclusionThis is the first case series from a country in Central Eastern Europe with a four-season climate, demonstrating the occurrence of cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Overall survival was influenced by age, type of hemolytic anemia, and total bilirubin level during presentation. Inherited and acquired thrombophilia risk factors and antiphospholipid antibodies were detected in more patients with cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia than those with warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

Introduction

Cold agglutinin disease (CAD) is a rare autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) classified as a lymphoproliferative disorder. Based on the thermal amplitude and isotypes of the autoantibodies, warm (wAIHA) and cold types of AIHA (cAIHA) can be distinguished. Antibodies in cAIHA usually cause complement-mediated hemolysis, leading to mild to life-threatening symptoms [1]. cAIHA accounts for 20–30% of all AIHA [2]. Cold agglutinins (CAs) are cold autoantibodies responsible for hemolysis and clinical symptoms [1]. These antibodies bind to the surface of target antigens on red blood cells at 0–4°C in vitro [2]. CA could also generally occur in circulation. However, these antibodies are polyclonal and lack clinical relevance, with titers below 64 [3–5]. Thermal amplitude is the highest temperature at which the CA reacts with the antigen. If the thermal amplitude widens, the CA will bind antigens at lower temperatures (28–30°C), leading to agglutination in the arteries of extremities, causing complement activation, and severe hemolysis [4].

CAs are antibodies against the surface antigens “I” and “i” on red blood cells, and rarely against “Pr” and “IH”; these pathogen-specific antibodies are primarily monoclonal IgM, with IgG accounting for only 5% and IgA rarely [4]. While the titer of CA can be extremely high, the titer is not related to the severity of hemolysis. By definition, based on the latest WHO 2022 classification, CAD is a clonal, lymphoproliferative disorder detected in the bone marrow with no other clinical or radiological findings [4, 5]. Bone marrow examination is necessary; flow cytometry usually shows B-cells expressing IgM kappa light chain. The immunophenotype of the cells is CD19+, CD20+, CD22+, CD79b+, CD23−, and CD5 could be either positive or negative in half of the cases [6]. Cytogenetic examination and next-generation sequencing (NGS) could detect mutations in IGHV4-34, IGKV3-20, IGKV3-15, KMT2D, IGLL5, CARD11, and CXCR4 [6, 7]. As a differential diagnostic challenge, it is essential to state that the MYD88 L265D mutation is negative in CAD, and nodal lymphoproliferation is not detectable [6].

Cold agglutinin syndrome (CAS) is a heterogeneous group. CA-associated AIHA in different other conditions, such as infections (mainly Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, SARS-CoV-2, etc.), lymphoid malignancies (non-Hodgkin lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, Waldenström macroglobulinemia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, etc.), and systemic autoimmune diseases (systemic lupus erythematosus [SLE], rheumatoid arthritis [RA], etc.), can occur [8, 9].

Our objectives were to determine the occurrence of CAD among patients diagnosed with and followed for AIHA and to characterize its specific features. We also aimed to determine the factors affecting survival and the incidence of thromboembolic complications.

Methods and Patients

We conducted a retrospective study between 2010 and 2024 among patients with hemolytic anemia treated at Department of Internal Medicine, Division of Hematology and Rare Diseases, Faculty of Medicine, University of Debrecen (UD). The patients’ records were reviewed using UD’s electronic registries (MedSolution and UDMed). We evaluated demographic data, clinical presentations, laboratory findings, treatment modalities, disease recurrence, and patient outcomes. Patients with laboratory signs of hemolysis (anemia, reticulocytosis, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, increased lactate dehydrogenase [LDH] enzyme, and low haptoglobin) were included in this study. Patients with membrane defects and hemoglobinopathies were excluded. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was performed in all cases [10]. DATs were done at the Hungarian National Blood Transfusion Service using polyvalent antibodies. When professionally justified, a monovalent test was also used to detect IgG and C3d on red blood cells. Determining the thermal amplitude of antibodies helps differentiate types of AIHA. CA titer is necessary for the diagnosis; a CA titer of more than 64 at 4°C is considered significant, but a titer of less than 1:64 does not rule out the diagnosis of cAIHA. We followed the recommendations of the First International Consensus Meeting when processing the retrospective data. Bone marrow examination was performed if indicated in cAIHA. Flow cytometry, cytogenetics, and, after becoming available, lymphoid NGS were performed in some cases. Secondary causes were also investigated. Inherited (factor II and V mutation, protein S, protein C, and antithrombin III activities) and acquired factors of thrombophilia, such as antiphospholipid (APL) antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anti-cardiolipin, and anti-beta2-glycoprotein antibodies [aB2GPI]), were also investigated.

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations. The distributions were found to be elevated by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. We used the χ2 and Fisher’s exact test to determine differences between cohorts for categorical variables. We used the Mann-Whitney U test to address discrepancies in nonparametric continuous variables. We also generated Kaplan-Meier survival curves for our patients. We used a log-rank test to assess differences in survival rates. HR was determined using univariate and multivariate Cox regression tests. All p values are two-tailed with a significance of 0.05 to detect statistical significance.

Results

Patient’s Demographics

Over 14 years (2010–2024), we diagnosed and treated 84 patients with hemolytic anemia in the Department of Internal Medicine, Division of Hematology and Rare Diseases. The mean age of the patients was 60.45 ± 17.64 years. Significantly more patients were female (n = 50) (p = 0.021). Among these, we diagnosed 15 patients with cAIHA and 69 with wAIHA. Clinical Characteristics of wAIHA Patients

Eighty-four patients with AIHA were diagnosed and included in this study. Sixty-nine patients had warm-type autoantibodies. Thirty-one patients had primary wAIHA; in these patients, the usual causes of secondary AIHA were excluded. The causes of secondary wAIHA are shown in Figure 1.

Causes of secondary wAIHA. ITP, immune thrombocytopenia; MDS, myelodysplastic syndrome; EBV, Epstein-Barr virus; CMV, cytomegalovirus; C, Chlamydia pneumoniae; M, Mycoplasma pneumoniae; SARS-CoV-2, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2.

The majority of the patients had different types of lymphoproliferative diseases (chronic lymphocytic leukemia, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, or marginal zone lymphoma) (40%, n = 27), systemic autoimmune disease (RA, SLE, Sjögren syndrome) was present in 16% (n = 11) of patients, while myelodysplastic syndrome or myelofibrosis was present in 10% (n = 7) of all cases. Infections induced hemolysis in 13% of patients, most commonly due to SARS-CoV-2, Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, Chlamydia pneumoniae, M. pneumoniae, and Parvovirus B19.

No significant differences were detected in laboratory data between patients with primary and secondary wAIHA or between genders. When comparing patients with wAIHA and cAIHA, the only significant difference was observed in total bilirubin level (Table 1). Twenty-two AIHA patients had experienced three or more relapses, but we found no difference between the two groups in the number of relapses (p = 0.4). All wAIHA patients received corticosteroid treatment as first-line treatment lasting 2–3 months, 1–3 mg/kg depending on the severity of presentation; 40% (n = 28) of wAIHA patients were administered 375 mg/m2 rituximab weekly for four cycles, 10 patients were taking azathioprine, and 3 patients received either cyclosporine or intravenous immunoglobulin. Transfusions were used only in those cases where clinical symptoms warranted. In steroid-refractory cases, second-line treatment was used; 47 patients received rituximab at a dose of 375 mg/m2 weekly for 4 cycles, yielding a 61% overall response rate. In patients with lymphomas and CLL, anti-CD20 therapy was used in first-line treatment according to guidelines. After it became available in Hungary, we administered obinutuzumab to 4 patients. The use of rituximab was significantly higher in wAIHA patients (p = 0.017). Secondary wAIHA patients with different lymphoproliferative diseases were administered chemotherapy, based on the guidelines (bendamustine 90 mg/m2 on days 1 and 2, or cyclophosphamide 500–1,000 mg/m2) (Table 2).

Other immunosuppressive treatments, such as azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclosporine, and methotrexate, were used in patients diagnosed with systemic autoimmune diseases. Although splenectomy is only the third-line treatment in wAIHA, 6 patients underwent splenectomy after vaccination against encapsulated bacteria (in 3 patients, it was performed because of therapy-refractory anemia, while in three cases because of the co-occurrence of immune thrombocytopenia). Seven patients received intravenous immunoglobulin. Plasma exchange (PEX) was performed in life-threatening cases as a last resort (n = 2). In 24% of cases, we used second-line treatment, and in 9 cases, third-line treatment (Table 2).

Thromboembolic side effects are common in severe hemolysis. We diagnosed deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism in 5 patients; they all received anticoagulant treatment. We performed tests for acquired and inherited thrombophilia in patients with thromboembolism (TE) and severe hemolysis (hemoglobin [Hb] level <60 g/L, LDH enzyme >1.5× the upper limit of normal) (n = 10). Malignancies were excluded in all these cases. The thrombophilia screening showed a prothrombin heterozygous mutation and lower protein C level (68%) in 2 patients. Acquired risk factors, such as APL antibodies, have also been investigated. Anticardiolipin IgM positivity was observed in 1 case. It was repeated after 12 weeks, yielding the same result, but it did not meet the criteria for APL syndrome. We used prophylactic anticoagulation in patients with severe hemolysis and in those diagnosed with positive APL antibodies.

Characterization of Patients with cAIHA

Patients with cAIHA have a unique clinical appearance. Three groups of patients with mild, moderate, and severe life-threatening symptoms could be distinguished. Fifteen patients in our center were diagnosed with cAIHA and were followed; their clinical data are presented in Table 3. In this group, significantly more patients were older than 70 years (p = 0.03). The mean age of patients was 68.87 ± 14.15 years, and the male:female ratio was 1:3.6. The average disease duration was 79.89 ± 65.46 months.

Eight of 15 patients fulfilled the criteria for CAD. All patients showed DAT positivity; the monospecific DAT was positive for C3d. Bone marrow aspiration was performed in all cases, and in most patients, it showed a low-grade monoclonal B-cell clone. Secondary causes such as infections, other malignancies, and autoimmune diseases were excluded. Cytogenetics was done in all cases, and no chromosomal abnormalities were detected. Lymphoid NGS was performed in three cases, with no genetic mutations detected. According to the disease mechanism, most patients have low complement levels. Three patients had significantly increased CA levels (>1,000 L/titer, 1,075.9 ± 1,742 L/titer). They all had a 10-year history of cold-related circulatory symptoms, including Raynaud’s phenomenon, which immunologists had previously followed. All patients were complaining of chronic fatigue, and on presentation, the Hb level was 77.57 ± 15.97 g/L, LDH: 409.29 ± 110.91 U/L. One male patient with CAD has erectile dysfunction, livedo reticularis, and severe circulatory symptoms (nose, fingers).

Before establishing the diagnosis, corticosteroid (1–2 mg/kg) was used in cAIHA, but after the diagnosis of CAD was evident, rituximab was started (375 mg/m2 weekly in four cycles). Three patients received bendamustine treatment (90 mg/m2 for 2 days) in 4 cycles every 28 days.

Thromboembolic complications and thrombophilia testing were also performed in patients with cAIHA. APL antibodies were detected in a higher proportion in CAD/CAS patients than in wAIHA patients (n = 2). The patients had mildly elevated D-dimer levels (0.81 mg FEU/L). One patient had a factor V (FV) Leiden heterozygous mutation, and reduced protein S levels were detected in 3 patients (53%, 37%, 58%, respectively).

Thromboprophylaxis was used in all patients who had increased levels of aB2GPI and anticardiolipin antibodies (low molecular weight heparin [LMWH]) FV Leiden mutation, or reduced PS levels. In patients with elevated APL antibodies, repeated tests were done after 12 weeks; none of them fulfilled the criteria of APL syndrome. In contrast, a therapeutic dose of LMWH or a new oral anticoagulant was used in patients with manifest thrombosis or pulmonary embolism (n = 2). Coagulation activation markers were elevated in patients with wAIHA, but the difference was not significant compared to cAIHA (thrombin-antithrombin complex 9.92 ± 8.67 vs. 5.02 ± 1.65 μg/L, p = 0.24; fibrin monomer 34.52 ± 77.13 vs. 11.8 ± 16.7 μg/mL, p = 0.53; prothrombin fragment 1+2, 204.04 ± 41.16 vs. 187.69 ± 100.78 pmol/L, p = 0.74).

Case Report of a Patient with CAD

To highlight the difficulties in the treatment of CAD, we present a case of a 48-year-old female patient who was diagnosed with cAIHA (retrospectively met the criteria for CAD) in 2011 (flow cytometry: 8% CD20+, CD5+ monoclonal B-cells) and received four cycles of rituximab. In 2022, she had a severe relapse (flow cytometry 6.4% monoclonal B-cells). She received six cycles of B-cell-depleting therapy again (rituximab-venetoclax), and the flow cytometry became negative (monoclonal B-cells: 0.02%). She continued to have transfusion dependency and severe fatigue. We planned to give sutimlimab, a complement inhibitor. This treatment is on-label in Hungary but not reimbursed; thus, compassionate use is required. Unfortunately, the patient’s symptoms had deteriorated; she was placed in the intensive care unit, receiving pulse corticosteroid treatment, and even PEX was done, but she finally died before we could administer the complement-inhibiting therapy. The remaining 7 patients are all in complete remission.

Patients with CAS

Secondary causes must be excluded during the investigation of cAIHA. Patients with cAIHA accompanying other underlying diseases are known to have CAS. In the past decade, 7 patients were found to have CAS. The average age of the patients was 71.71 ± 16.55 years (four females and three males). Three patients had myelodysplastic neoplasm and were treated beyond corticosteroids with a hypomethylating drug (azacitidine) and thalidomide. One patient with severe hemolysis also received rituximab treatment. A 99-year-old woman was diagnosed with marginal zone lymphoma and had three severe relapses; she received a combination of rituximab and bendamustine at a reduced dose. In 2 cases, we made the diagnosis of RA and SLE; both received corticosteroids and rituximab. The patient diagnosed with RA had hemolytic relapses three times, and the abdominal ultrasound showed splenomegaly. Finally, while splenectomy is not recommended in cold hemolytic anemia, but 1 patient underwent a splenectomy after proper vaccination because of steroid dependency and splenomegaly. He is now in remission. The average Hb level of the patients was 67.14 ± 9.77 g/L, which was lower than that of patients with CAD (p = 0.08). LDH level on presentation was 474.57 ± 200.32 U/L vs. 409 ± 110.91 U/L in patients with CAD (p = 0.23). Three patients in this group died because of different infectious complications of the underlying lymphoproliferative malignancies, while the other 4 patients are in complete remission.

Survival of AIHA Patients

Fifteen patients out of 84 died (17.8%). The average survival time was 324.06 ± 23.26 months (95% CI: 278.46–369.66). The 5-year survival rate was 88.9%, while the 10-year survival rate was 82.4%. The overall survival of cAIHA patients was significantly worse than that of wAIHA using the log-rank test (wAIHA 342.91 ± 24.06 months and cAIHA 118.28 ± 20.92 months, p = 0.035) (95% CI: 295.83 ± 389.98 vs. 77.27 ± 159.29) (Fig. 2). The 5- and 10-year survival rates of cAIHA patients were 66.7%.

Overall survival of patients with cAIHA and wAIHA.

The survival of patients with primary AIHA was significantly better than the mortality of patients with secondary AIHA (p = 0.02); the worst prognosis is seen in patients with lymphoproliferative malignancies (Fig. 3). Using the Cox regression test to determine the risk factors for overall survival, we have found that older age (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.99–1.06, p = 0.07), the cAIHA (HR 2.67, 95% CI 0.84–8.45, p = 0.09), and total bilirubin level on presentation (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–1, p = 0.13) have a mild, not significant, effect on survival.

Discussion

CAD is a rare AIHA. According to the latest WHO classification (2022) and the International Consensus Classification, it is classified as a distinct clonal B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder. Still, it was already mentioned by Berentsen et al. [9], representing a spectrum of clonal lymphoproliferative bone marrow disorders. The disease’s background is a low-grade lymphoproliferative clone that secretes autoantibodies against red blood cell surface antigens, causing complement-mediated hemolysis [8, 11]. The exact incidence of the disease is unknown; it is more common in countries with cold climates. Based on a previous Norwegian study, the incidence of CAD is 1/1 million, and the prevalence is 16/1 million; there is a mild female predominance, and the average age at diagnosis is 76 years [9]. There are no data about the incidence of the disease in other European countries. In our single-center study, 17% of the AIHA patients had CAD/CAS, which is comparable to previous published data (15–25%) [12, 13]. The mean age (68 years) of cAIHA patients was comparable to that in other studies (67 years) [14]; patients with CAD/CAS were significantly older than those with wAIHA. In our study, female predominance was observed. We experienced a modest increase in wAIHA cases in the COVID-19 era, but not in CAD cases. SARS-CoV-2 infection induced wAIHA in 6 patients, which is a known complication of infection; based on previous reports, it is known that DAT positivity was significantly increased among patients being hospitalized because of COVID-19 infection and this infection also induced both wAIHA and CAD as published in a recent systematic review [14, 15].

The most common clinical presentation of CAD is complement-mediated hemolysis and cold-induced agglutination, which cause acrocyanosis, Raynaud phenomenon, and livedo reticularis [16]. All our patients had a history of more than 10 years of symptoms mentioned above. Anemia is usually not severe; in a Norwegian review of 86 CAD patients, the mean Hb level was 87 g/L, and the lowest level was 45 g/L [9]. Our patients’ average Hb level was comparable to that reported in previous studies (80.69 g/L). Chronic fatigue also affects patients’ daily lives, occurring in 90% of patients and due to anemia, chronic complement activation, and elevated proinflammatory cytokine levels [16].

In most cases of CAD, avoiding cold and protecting extremities from cold is essential. Usually, anemia is mild, and hemolysis is compensated; thus, treatment is recommended only in severe hemolysis (Hb <60 g/L, LDH >1.5× the upper limit of normal U/L). Transfusion is not contraindicated; the blood products must be warmed to 37°C. In our practice, we try to avoid using transfusions in AIHA patients. Corticosteroids are not effective; the response rate is less than 20% in cases [17]. We used corticosteroids only at the beginning, before establishing the diagnosis of CAD/CAS. Splenectomy is usually not recommended because red blood cell sequestration occurs in the liver. Still, we were forced to use it in one therapy-refractory, steroid-dependent CAS patient [18]. PEX should be performed in therapy-refractory cases of severe hemolysis (Hb <60 g/L, LDH >1.5x the upper limit of normal U/L), as we used it in 1 patient with hyperviscosity symptoms due to Waldenström macroglobulinemia. Although PEX therapy is not a usual treatment in wAIHA, in some life-threatening cases, it can be an option [19].

Based on the first international consensus meeting in CAD, B-cell-depleting and complement-inhibiting therapy are recommended [8]. The first line of treatment is anti-CD20 therapy, rituximab (375 mg/m2 weekly in four cycles) [20]. The combination of rituximab and bendamustine is recommended as a second-line treatment for older patients [21]. There is no evidence-based recommendation for third-line treatment. The other treatment possibility is complement inhibition. Sutimlimab was approved by the FDA in 2022 as a selective C1s fragment inhibitor. The effectiveness of the drug was supported by CARDINAL and CADENZA studies. Complement inhibition also improves fatigue in these patients [22–25].

The 10-year survival of our patients with wAIHA was higher (82%) than that of patients with CAD/CAS (66.7%), but the difference was not significant. Here, 33% of the patients died during the follow-up, which was comparable to previous observations due to the Norwegian multicentric study (31%) [26]. The background of death was due to underlying lymphoproliferative conditions (mainly CLL), side effects of chemotherapy (primarily infections), and thromboembolic complications (pulmonary embolism). The increased incidence of TE in patients with AIHA is well known; previously, it was reported to be within 11% to 27% [27]. Thrombotic events were also more common in a Danish cohort of CAD patients (7.2% vs. 1.9%) [28].

In 71% of cases, we detected a low-grade lymphoproliferative clone in the bone marrow; cytogenetics was negative in all cases. We also performed NGS in three cases and excluded any genetic mutation behind the disease. In 1 patient, we detected a FV Leiden heterozygous mutation with a reduced protein S level. APL antibodies were detected in a higher proportion in CAD/CAS patients. We used thromboprophylaxis for all patients with severe hemolysis (Hb <60 g/L) and positive APL antibody levels, based on the recommendation of the 2020 International Consensus Meeting on AIHA [8]. Previous studies also suggest that antithrombotic prophylaxis with LMWH is recommended in severe hemolysis, moreover in the presence of a risk factor for TE as acquired thrombophilia [8, 29, 30].

Although in a multinational study of 232 CAD patients, more than 30% had compensated hemolysis or mild anemia, our patients had moderate or severe hemolysis, requiring treatment [26, 31]. All CAD patients and 71% of CAS patients received rituximab; half were administered chemotherapy, primarily bendamustine. One patient with refractory disease received bortezomib following a previous successful response [32]. Treatment of refractory patients needs to be personalized; targeted therapies could also be used in ongoing clinical trials. Unfortunately, we could not use complement-mediated therapy for our patients because the drug needs specific permission in Hungary. Because of milder circulatory symptoms, we used B-cell-depleting therapy, but complement inhibition is the preferred treatment in acute severe cases.

Conclusions

This is the first case series from a country in the Central East of Europe with a four-season climate, showing the occurrence of cAIHA. We recognize our study’s limitations. This was a retrospective single-center review of a rare disease with a limited sample size, which was insufficient for a serious statistical analysis. Multicenter data with long-term follow-up can improve our results.

We recommend detecting APL antibodies in CAD patients at increased risk of TE and investigating all cases of severe AIHA. Prophylactic anticoagulation is essential in all cases of severe hemolysis when the test is positive.
