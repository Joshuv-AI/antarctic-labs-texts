From Attraction to Repulsion: Emergent Interactions in Harmonically Coupled Active Binary System
Urna Basu

Abstract
We investigate the emergent interactions between two active Brownian particles coupled by an attractive harmonic potential and in contact with a thermal reservoir. By analyzing the stationary distribution of their separation, we demonstrate that the effective interaction can be either attractive or repulsive, depending on the interplay between activity, coupling strength, and temperature. Notably, we find that an effective short-range repulsion emerges in the strong and moderate-coupling regimes, when the temperature is below some threshold value, which we characterize analytically. In the strong-coupling regime, the repulsion emerges due to the difference in the self-propulsion speeds of the particles. We also compute the short-time position distribution of the centroid of the coupled particles, which shows strongly non-Gaussian fluctuations at low temperatures.

In this work, we present an extensive study of a binary system of active Brownian particles coupled via a harmonic potential and in contact with a thermal reservoir. 2
Model and results

We consider two harmonically coupled overdamped active Brownian particles moving on a
2d
plane, in contact with a thermal bath of temperature
T
. Let
{\bm{r}}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
denote the position of the
i
-th particle with
i=1,2
, and
k
denote the coupling strength. Moreover, let
v_{i}
denote the self-propulsion speed of the
i
-th particle along its internal orientation
\hat{\bm{n}}_{i}=(\cos{\theta_{i}},\sin{\theta_{i}})
. The internal orientations of both particles undergo rotational diffusion with diffusion coefficients
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, respectively. The Langevin equations governing the time-evolution of the positions of the particles are given by,

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{r}}_{1}=-k(\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2})+v_{1}\hat{\bm{n}}_{1}+\sqrt{2T}\,{\bm{\xi}}_{1}(t),

(1)

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{r}}_{2}=-k(\bm{r}_{2}-\bm{r}_{1})+v_{2}\hat{\bm{n}}_{2}+\sqrt{2T}\,{\bm{\xi}}_{2}(t),

(2)

where
\{{\bm{\xi}}_{i}=(\xi_{i}^{x},\xi_{i}^{y});i=1,2\}
denote independent white noises with zero mean and correlations given by

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{i}^{\alpha}(t)\xi_{j}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta{(t-t^{\prime})}.

(3)

The internal orientations
\{\theta_{i}\}
evolve according to,

\displaystyle\dot{\theta_{i}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2D_{i}}\,\eta_{i}(t).

(4)

Here,
\{\eta_{i}(t)\}
denote another set of independent white noises with zero mean and correlation
\langle\eta_{i}(t)\eta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. We assume that both the particles are at the origin at
t=0
with random initial orientations, i.e.,
x_{i}(0)=y_{i}(0)=0
and
(\theta_{1}(0),\theta_{2}(0))
are chosen independently from the uniform distribution in
[0,2\pi]
. Therefore, the components of the active noises
\hat{\bm{n}}_{i}
have zero mean and the following autocorrelation
[
35
,
36
]
,

\displaystyle\langle\cos\theta_{i}(t)\cos\theta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sin\theta_{i}(t)\sin\theta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\frac{1}{2}e^{-D_{i}|t-t^{\prime}|}.

(5)

To understand the behavior of this two-particle system, it is convenient to rewrite Eqs.(
1
)-(
2
) in terms of the relative coordinate
{\bm{r}}={\bm{r}}_{1}-{\bm{r}}_{2}
and the centroid position
{\bm{R}}=({\bm{r}}_{1}+{\bm{r}}_{2})/2
. From Eqs. (
1
)-(
2
) it is straightforward to see that the relative coordinate
{\bm{r}}
follows a Langevin equation,

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{r}}(t)

\displaystyle=-2k{\bm{r}}(t)+v_{1}\hat{\bm{n}}_{1}(t)-v_{2}\hat{\bm{n}}_{2}(t)+\sqrt{4T}{\bm{\xi}}(t),~~\text{with}~~{\bm{\xi}}(t)=\frac{{\bm{\xi}}_{1}(t)-{\bm{\xi}}_{2}(t)}{\sqrt{2}}.

(6)

On the other hand, the centroid position evolves according to,

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{R}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[v_{1}\hat{\bm{n}}_{1}(t)+v_{2}\hat{\bm{n}}_{2}(t)\right]+\sqrt{T}{\bm{\zeta}}(t),~~\text{with}~~{\bm{\zeta}}(t)=\frac{{\bm{\xi}}_{1}(t)+{\bm{\xi}}_{2}(t)}{\sqrt{2}}.

(7)

Clearly,
{\bm{\xi}}
and
{\bm{\zeta}}
are two independent white noises with zero mean and correlators
\langle\xi^{\alpha}(t)\xi^{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
,
\langle\zeta^{\alpha}(t)\zeta^{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
and
\langle\xi^{\alpha}(t)\zeta^{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=0
.

Equation (
6
) implies that the motion of the relative coordinate resembles that of a particle in a harmonic potential of strength
2k
in contact with a thermal reservoir at temperature
2T
, driven by the active noise
v_{1}\hat{\bm{n}}_{1}-v_{2}\hat{\bm{n}}_{2}
. This motion is characterized by three different time-scales—the relaxation time-scale
(2k)^{-1}
associated with the harmonic trap and the active time-scales
D_{1}^{-1}
and
D_{2}^{-1}
associated with the rotational diffusion of the internal orientations of the two particles. The relative coordinate
\bm{r}
is expected to reach a stationary state at times much larger than all three time-scales.

On the other hand, Eq. (
7
) implies that the centroid undergoes an unbounded motion driven by a combination of active noises and thermal noise. In the absence of the thermal bath, the motion of the centroid is bounded in the annular region
C_{\mathrm{min}}\leq R\equiv|{\bm{R}}(t)|\leq C_{\mathrm{max}}
with,

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{min}}=\frac{|v_{1}-v_{2}|t}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{(v_{1}+v_{2})t}{2}.

(8)

Figure 1:
Stationary distribution
P\left((x,y)\right)
of the relative coordinate
\bm{r}=(x,y)
, obtained from numerical simulations, in the strong-coupling regime (a), moderate-coupling regime (b), and weak-coupling regime (c). The projection on the
x-y
plane is shown by the green contour plot. For (a), the parameters are
D_{1}=D_{2}=0.01,k=4,T=0.05,v_{1}=2
and

. The self-propulsion velocities are fixed at
v_{1}=4,v_{2}=1
for the moderate and weak-coupling regimes. The remaining parameters for (b) are
D_{1}=0.001,D_{2}=10,k=0.2,T=1
, and for (c)
D_{1}=10,D_{2}=20,k=0.02,T=0.1
.

In this work, we present a comprehensive study of the stationary distribution of the relative coordinate
P({\bm{r}})
and show that it has strikingly different features depending on the relative strengths of the different time-scales. In particular, we analytically compute the stationary distribution
P({\bm{r}})
in the three limiting scenarios —

•

Strong-coupling regime:
k\gg(D_{1},D_{2})
,

•

Moderate-coupling regime:
D_{1}\ll k\ll D_{2}
, and

•

Weak-coupling regime:
(D_{1},D_{2})\gg k
.

Figure
1
shows plots of the typical stationary distribution
P\left({\bm{r}}=(x,y)\right)
in these three different regimes.

The strong-coupling regime is characterized by the emergence of an unusual effective short-range repulsion between the particles for
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
. This was first shown in Ref
[
34
]
where the stationary distribution of the relative coordinate was computed in the absence of thermal noise. Here we analytically compute the marginal distributions of the radial distance
r
and its
x
-component for
T>0
and show that the repulsion survives for finite temperatures. To quantify this emergent repulsion, we define an effective potential which remains repulsive below a threshold temperature. In the moderate-coupling regime, the time evolution of the relative coordinate is equivalent to the motion of a single ABP in a harmonic trap with an effective temperature. The effective repulsion is also present in this regime, that vanishes with increasing
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

The emergent repulsion disappears in the weak-coupling regime, where both the particles behave like passive ones and the typical distribution of the components of the separation vector
\bm{r}
becomes Gaussian. Finally, we also investigate the motion of the centroid. At short-times, i.e., for
t\ll((2k)^{-1},D_{1}^{-1},D_{2}^{-1})
it shows strongly non-Gaussian fluctuations, which we characterize analytically.

3
Stationary distribution of the separation

In this section, we focus on the stationary fluctuations of the separation variable
{\bm{r}}(t)
. The probability that the particles are separated by a distance
{\bm{r}}
at time
t
can be formally expressed as
P({\bm{r}},t)=\int d\theta_{1}d\theta_{2}{\cal P}({\bm{r}},\theta_{1},\theta_{2},t)
where the joint distribution
{\cal P}({\bm{r}},\theta_{1},\theta_{2},t)
satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\cal P}({\bm{r}},\theta_{1},\theta_{2},t)=\nabla\cdot\left[(2k{\bm{r}}-v_{1}\hat{\bm{n}}_{1}+v_{2}\hat{\bm{n}}_{2}){\cal P}\right]+4T\nabla^{2}{\cal P}+D_{1}\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal P}}{\partial\theta_{1}^{2}}+D_{2}\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal P}}{\partial\theta_{2}^{2}}.

(9)

Equation (
9
) is hard to solve even in the steady state. Instead, we focus on the Langevin Eq. (
6
) and adopt a trajectory-based approach to explore the statistical fluctuations of the separation
{\bm{r}}
. To this end, we write the formal solution of Eq. (
6
),

\displaystyle{\bm{r}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}ds\,e^{-2k(t-s)}\left[v_{1}\hat{\bm{n}}_{1}(s)-v_{2}\hat{\bm{n}}_{2}(s)+\sqrt{4T}{\bm{\xi}}(s)\right],

(10)

To get a preliminary idea about the fluctuation of
r(t)
, we first calculate the mean and variance of the separation
{\bm{r}}(t)
.
Since the initial
{\theta_{i}}
is chose from the uniform distribution in
[0,2\pi]
, we have
\langle\cos\theta_{i}(t)\rangle=0=\langle\sin\theta_{i}(t)\rangle
for all
t
. Moreover, we have assumed both the particles start from the origin,
{\bm{r}}(0)=0
and hence, both
\langle x(t)\rangle
and
\langle y(t)\rangle
vanish at all times.

The second moment of the
x(t)
and
y(t)
can also be computed from Eq. (
10
) as,

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{t}ds^{\prime}e^{-2k(2t-s-s^{\prime})}\Big[v_{1}^{2}\langle\cos\theta_{1}(s)\cos{\theta_{1}(s^{\prime})}\rangle+v_{2}^{2}\langle\cos\theta_{2}(s)\cos{\theta_{2}(s^{\prime})}\rangle

(11)

\displaystyle\qquad+4T\langle\xi_{x}(s)\xi_{x}(s^{\prime})\rangle\Big],

(12)

\displaystyle\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{t}ds^{\prime}e^{-2k(2t-s-s^{\prime})}\Big[v_{1}^{2}\langle\sin\theta_{1}(s)\sin{\theta_{1}(s^{\prime})}\rangle+v_{2}^{2}\langle\sin\theta_{2}(s)\sin{\theta_{2}(s^{\prime})}\rangle

(13)

\displaystyle\qquad+4T\langle\xi_{y}(s)\xi_{y}(s^{\prime})\rangle\Big].

(14)

Using the active noise correlations in Eq. (
5
) and evaluating the integrals, we have,

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle=\langle y^{2}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=v_{1}^{2}\mathcal{G}(k,D_{1},t)+v_{2}^{2}\mathcal{G}(k,D_{2},t)+\frac{T}{k}\big(1-e^{-4kt}\big).

(15)

where
\mathcal{G}(k,D,t)
is given by,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(k,D,t)=\frac{1-e^{-4kt}}{4k(D+2k)}+\frac{e^{-2kt}(e^{-Dt}-e^{-2kt})}{D^{2}-4k^{2}}.

(16)

Figure 2:
Plot of
\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle
, the second moment of the
x
-component of the radial separation
{\bm{r}}
, as a function of time for different values of the temperature
T
. The symbols indicate the data obtained from the numerical simulations, whereas the black solid lines correspond to the theoretical prediction Eq. (
15
). The dashed lines indicate the stationary values (

). Here we have taken
k=1
,
D_{1}=1
,
D_{2}=4
,
v_{1}=7
, and
v_{2}=3
.

At short-times, i.e., for
t\ll(D_{1}^{-1},D_{2}^{-1},k^{-1})
, we have, from Eq. (
15
),

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq 4Tt-\Big(8kT-\frac{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}}{2}\Big)t^{2}+\mathcal{O}[t^{3}].

(17)

Clearly, in the absence of a thermal bath,
\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle
grows ballistically at short-times. However, for
T>0
, the short-time behaviour becomes diffusive. At large times, i.e.,
t\gg(D_{1}^{-1},D_{2}^{-1},k^{-1})
,
\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle
reaches a stationary value,

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}\rangle=\frac{T}{k}+\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{4k(D_{1}+2k)}+\frac{v_{2}^{2}}{4k(D_{2}+2k)}.

(18)

Figure
2
illustrates the behaviour of
\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle
for different values of
T
.

In the following, we investigate the stationary state fluctuations of the relative coordinate
{\bm{r}}
in the three regimes separately.

3.1
Strong-coupling regime

The strong-coupling regime emerges when the coupling strength
k
is much larger than the rotational diffusion constants of the particles
D_{1},D_{2}
. Consequently, the typical relaxation time of
{\bm{r}}
in the trap
k^{-1}
is much smaller than the time
D_{i}^{-1}
taken by the orientations
\hat{\bm{n}}_{i}
of the particles to change appreciably. As shown below, this allows one to obtain the stationary distribution exactly.

The radial and
x
-marginal distributions in the strong-coupling regime in the absence of temperature have recently been computed analytically, and it has been shown that heterogeneity in self-propulsion speed leads to the emergence of a short-range repulsion among the two particles
[
34
]
. More specifically, it was found that the distance
r=|{\bm{r}}|
between the two particles must be bound in the regime
R_{\mathrm{min}}\leq r\leq R_{\mathrm{max}}
, with,

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{min}}=\frac{|v_{1}-v_{2}|}{2k}\text{~and~}R_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{v_{1}+v_{2}}{2k}.

(19)

The radial distribution, in this case, follows a scaling form,

\displaystyle P(r)=\frac{1}{R_{\text{max}}}{\cal R}\left(\frac{r}{R_{\text{max}}}\right),

(20)

with the scaling function,

\displaystyle{\cal R}(u)=\frac{u}{\pi\sqrt{(1-u^{2})(u^{2}-R_{\text{min}}^{2}/R_{\text{max}}^{2})}}~~\text{for}~~R_{\text{min}}/R_{\text{max}}\leq u\leq 1.

(21)

In the following, we investigate the stationary distribution in the presence of thermal noise.

As mentioned before, the strong-coupling regime is characterized by a time-scale separation between the relaxation of
{\bm{r}}
in the potential and the evolution of orientations. Moreover, Eq (
4
) implies that the orientations
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
eventually relax to independent uniform distributions in
[0,2\pi]
. Hence, to the leading order in
D_{i}^{-1}
, the stationary distribution of the separation vector must follow,

\displaystyle P({\bm{r}})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta_{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta_{2}~{\cal P}({\bm{r}}|\theta_{1},\theta_{2}),

(22)

where
{\cal P}({\bm{r}}|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
denotes the stationary distribution of the separation
{\bm{r}}
for fixed
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
. The conditional distribution
{\cal P}({\bm{r}}|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
can be computed from the Langevin equation Eq. (
6
) in a straightforward manner by taking the limit
t\gg k^{-1}
. In this limit, Eq. (
6
) can be recast as,

\displaystyle{\bm{r}}=\frac{1}{2k}\left(v_{1}\hat{\bm{n}}_{1}-v_{2}\hat{\bm{n}}_{2}\right)+{\bm{w}}.

(23)

where
{\bm{w}}=(w_{x},w_{y})
is a Gaussian noise with independent components
w_{x}
and
w_{y}
, distributed according to,

\displaystyle G(w_{\alpha})=\sqrt{\frac{k}{2\pi T}}\exp{\left(-\frac{k\,w_{\alpha}^{2}}{2T}\right)},~~\mathrm{with}~~\alpha=x,y.

(24)

Using Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
), we can now obtain,

\displaystyle{\cal P}({\bm{r}}|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\int dw_{x}\int dw_{y}~\delta(\bm{r-q-w})G(w_{x})G(w_{y}),

(25)

where, for notational convenience, we have introduced
{\bm{q}}=\frac{1}{2k}\left(v_{1}\hat{\bm{n}}_{1}-v_{2}\hat{\bm{n}}_{2}\right)
. Obviously,

\displaystyle q_{x}=\frac{1}{2k}(v_{1}\cos\theta_{1}-v_{2}\cos\theta_{2}),~\quad\text{and},\quad q_{y}=\frac{1}{2k}(v_{1}\sin\theta_{1}-v_{2}\sin\theta_{2}).

(26)

The stationary distribution of the separation in the strong-coupling regime can be computed using Eqs. (
23
)-(
25
) along with Eq. (
22
). In the following, we compute the radial and
x
-component marginal distributions explicitly.

3.1.1
Radial distribution:

The radial distribution
P(r)
denotes the probability that the radial distance between the two particles is
r
in the stationary state and is normalized as
\int drP(r)=1
. To obtain this distribution, it is convenient to first compute the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}(r^{2})
of
r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
. The radial distribution is then obtained as
P(r)=2r\mathbb{P}(r^{2})
. To compute
\mathbb{P}(r^{2})
, we start from Eq. (
25
) and formally write,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(z=r^{2}|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dw_{x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dw_{y}G(w_{x})G(w_{y})\delta\left[z-(q^{2}+w^{2}+2q_{x}w_{x}+2q_{y}w_{y})\right],

(27)

where,
q^{2}=[v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}-2v_{1}v_{2}\cos(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})]/(4k^{2})
and
w^{2}=w_{x}^{2}+w_{y}^{2}
. To proceed further, we take the Laplace transform of the above equation with respect to
z
and evaluate the Gaussian integrals over
w_{x}
and
w_{y}
, which leads to,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(s|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,e^{-sz}{\mathbb{P}}(z|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{k+2sT}\exp{\left(-\frac{s(v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2})}{4k(k+2sT)}\right)}\exp{\left(\frac{sv_{1}v_{2}\cos{(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})}}{2k(k+2sT)}\right)}.

(29)

.

Integrating over
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
[see Eq (
22
)], we get the Laplace transform of
{\mathbb{P}}(z)
as,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(s)=\frac{k}{k+2sT}\exp{\left[-\frac{s(v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2})}{4k(k+2sT)}\right]}I_{0}\left(\frac{v_{1}v_{2}s}{2k(k+2sT)}\right),

(30)

where
I_{0}(u)
denotes the zeroth-order modified Bessel function of the first kind. It is hard to invert the Laplace transform and get a closed-form expression for
{\mathbb{P}}(r^{2})
. Instead, we expand the exponential and
I_{0}(u)
and compute the inverse Laplace transform term by term, which leads to,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{(v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2})^{n}(v_{1}v_{2})^{2m}4k^{2}}{n!(m!)^{2}(8kT)^{2m+n+1}}{}_{1}F_{1}\left(2m+n+1,1,-\frac{kz}{2T}\right),

(31)

where
{}_{1}F_{1}(a;b;z)
is the confluent hypergeometric function of the first kind or Kummer’s function
[
37
]
. The sum over
m
can be performed exactly and yields the radial distribution,

\displaystyle P(r)

\displaystyle=2r{\mathbb{P}}(r^{2})

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{kr}{T}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-(v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}))^{\ell}}{\ell!\,(8kT)^{\ell}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left[\frac{1-\ell}{2},-\frac{\ell}{2},1,\frac{4v_{1}^{2}v_{2}^{2}}{(v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2})^{2}}\right]{}_{1}F_{1}\left[\ell+1,1,-\frac{kr^{2}}{2T}\right].

(33)

Here
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
in Eq. (
33
) is Gauss’s hypergeometric function
[
37
]
. Although the sum over
\ell
cannot be evaluated analytically, the radial distribution can be obtained to arbitrary accuracy by evaluating the sum numerically. Figure
3
(a) compares this theoretical prediction with the radial distribution
P(r)
obtained from numerical simulations for different values of
T
. It is evident that
P(r)
is a single-peaked distribution that becomes broader as the temperature increases.

To understand the emergent interaction between the two particles quantitatively, it is useful to define an ‘effective potential’
[
29
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
. This is usually done by recasting the radial distribution in the form,

\displaystyle P(r)=A\,r\exp{\left[-\frac{V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)}{T}\right]},

(34)

where
A
is the normalization constant. Using the above definition, the effective potential can be extracted from Eq. (
33
), or equivalently, the
P(r)
measured from numerical simulations, as

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)-V_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)=-T\log{\left(\frac{P(r)}{r}\right)}+T\left[\log{\left(\frac{P(r)}{r}\right)}\Bigg|_{r=0}\right].

(35)

Figure
3
(b) shows a plot of the effective potential for different values of
T
. It is evident from this plot that for low temperatures, the effective potential
V_{\text{eff}}(r)-V_{\text{eff}}(0)
has a minimum at some
r^{*}>0
, indicating that the typical distance between the two particles is finite. This, in turn, implies that, at low temperatures, there is an emergent repulsive interaction between the two particles. The repulsion disappears, evidenced by
r^{*}
approaching zero, as the temperature is increased beyond a threshold value
T^{*}
.

To understand how the threshold temperature depends on
(v_{1},v_{2})

, we look at the behaviour of the effective potential near the origin

. It is clear from Eq. (

is a function of
r^{2}
, and hence, we must have, near
r=0
,

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)-V_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)=Br^{2}+\mathcal{O}[r^{4}],

(36)

where
B
depends on
v_{1},v_{2},k
and
T
. Clearly, if
B<0

then the effective potential is repulsive near
r=0
and
B>0
indicates an effective attraction. The coefficient
B
can be explicitly computed from the analytical expression of
P(r)
. To this end, it is convenient to start from Eq. (
31
), and expand the right hand side around
r=0
to get,

\displaystyle\frac{P(r)}{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{(v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2})^{n}(v_{1}v_{2})^{2m}8k^{2}}{n!(m!)^{2}(8kT)^{2m+n+1}}\left(1-\frac{kr^{2}(2m+n+1)}{2T}+\mathcal{O}[r^{4}]\right),

(37)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{k}{T}e^{-\frac{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}}{8kT}}I_{0}\left(\frac{v_{1}v_{2}}{4kT}\right)\left[1-\frac{r^{2}}{16T^{2}}\left(8kT-v_{1}^{2}-v_{2}^{2}+2v_{1}v_{2}\frac{I_{1}\left(\frac{v_{1}v_{2}}{4kT}\right)}{I_{0}\left(\frac{v_{1}v_{2}}{4kT}\right)}\right)+\mathcal{O}[r^{4}]\right].

(38)

Computing the effective potential from the above equation, we get,

\displaystyle B=8kT-v^{2}_{1}-v^{2}_{2}+2v_{1}v_{2}\frac{I_{1}\left(\frac{v_{1}v_{2}}{4kT}\right)}{I_{0}\left(\frac{v_{1}v_{2}}{4kT}\right)}.

(40)

The threshold temperature satisfies
B(T^{*})=0
. Although it is hard to solve this equation analytically, it can be solved numerically to obtain the threshold temperature
T^{*}
. Figure
4
(a) and (b) show the variation of
T^{*}
with
k
and
v_{1}
, respectively. Clearly, the threshold temperature increases, i.e., the repulsion survives for larger thermal noise when
v_{1}-v_{2}
is larger.

3.1.2
Marginal
x
-distribution:

In this section, we compute the marginal distribution
P(x)
of the
x
-component of the separation vector in the strong-coupling regime. In the absence of the thermal noise,
P(x)
follows a scaling form
[
34
]
,

\displaystyle P(x)=\frac{1}{R_{\text{max}}}{\cal F}\left(\frac{x}{R_{\text{max}}}\right),

(41)

with the scaling function

\displaystyle{\cal F}(u)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{2i}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{R^{2}_{\text{min}}/R^{2}_{\text{max}}-u^{2}}}\Bigg[K\bigg(\frac{1-u^{2}}{R^{2}_{\text{min}}/R^{2}_{\text{max}}-u^{2}}\bigg)\cr\displaystyle\,-\sqrt{\frac{R^{2}_{\text{min}}/R^{2}_{\text{max}}-u^{2}}{1-u^{2}}}K\bigg(\frac{R^{2}_{\text{min}}/R^{2}_{\text{max}}-u^{2}}{1-u^{2}}\bigg)\Bigg]~\text{for}~~|u|<R_{\text{min}}/R_{\text{max}},~~\cr\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi^{2}\sqrt{u^{2}-R^{2}_{\text{min}}/R^{2}_{\text{max}}}}K\Bigg[\frac{1-u^{2}}{R^{2}_{\text{min}}/R^{2}_{\text{max}}-u^{2}}\Bigg]~\text{for}~~R_{\text{min}}/R_{\text{max}}<|u|<1,\end{cases}

(42)

where
K(u)
denotes the complete Elliptic integral of the first kind
[
37
]
. The emergent repulsion is apparent from the double-peaked nature of the distribution. For non-zero temperature, also such a double-peaked nature of
P(x)
is expected for
T<T^{*}
.

To compute the marginal distribution
P(x)
, we follow a similar approach as used in the previous section. From Eqs. (
10
) and (
25
), we can write the probability density function of
x
, for fixed
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
, as,

\displaystyle P(x|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dw_{x}\,G(w_{x})\delta\left[x-\left(\frac{v_{1}}{2k}\cos{\theta_{1}}-\frac{v_{2}}{2k}\cos{\theta_{2}}+w_{x}\right)\right].

(43)

To proceed further, it is convenient to compute the Fourier transform of
P(x|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
with respect to
x
. This leads to,

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(\lambda|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,e^{i\lambda x}P(x|\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{e^{-\frac{T\lambda^{2}}{2k}}}{2\pi}\cos{\left(\frac{\lambda}{2k}[v_{1}\cos{\theta_{1}-v_{2}\cos{\theta_{2}}]}{}\right)}.

(44)

Integrating over
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, we have the characteristic function of the
x
-marginal distribution,

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(\lambda)\equiv\langle e^{i\lambda x}\rangle=J_{0}\left(\frac{v_{1}\lambda}{2k}\right)J_{0}\left(\frac{v_{2}\lambda}{2k}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{T\lambda^{2}}{2k}\right),

(45)

where
J_{\nu}(z)
denotes the
\nu
-th order Bessel function of the first kind
[
37
]
. Inverse Fourier transform of
\tilde{P}(\lambda)
gives the marginal distribution
P(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\lambda\cos{(\lambda x)}\tilde{P}(\lambda)/(2\pi)
. To compute the integral over
\lambda
, we use the following identity of the Bessel function,

\displaystyle J_{0}(a\,\lambda)J_{0}(b\,\lambda)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}a^{2\ell}}{(\ell!)^{2}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-\ell,-\ell;1;\frac{b^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)^{2\ell}.

(46)

Substituting the above equation in Eq. (
45
) and integrating term by term, we get
P(x)
as an infinite series sum,

\displaystyle P(x)=\sqrt{\frac{k}{2\pi^{2}T}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(l+\frac{1}{2})}{(l!)^{2}}\left(-\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{8kT}\right)^{l}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-l,-l;1;\frac{v_{2}^{2}}{v_{1}^{2}}\right){}_{1}F_{1}\left(l+\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{2};-\frac{kx^{2}}{2T}\right),

(47)

were
\Gamma(z)
denotes the Gamma function. The marginal distribution
P(x)
can be obtained up to arbitrary accuracy by numerically evaluating the series term by term. This is illustrated in Fig.
5
(a), which compares the
P(x)
computed from Eq. (
47
) along with the same obtained from numerical simulations. For temperatures below the threshold value
T^{*}
,

P(x)
is bimodal with peaks at
x=\pm r^{*}
, signalling the presence of repulsion. At high temperatures
T>T^{*}
, the repulsion disappears, leading to a single-peaked distribution.

The special case

For the special case

\displaystyle P(r)

3.2
Moderate-coupling regime

The moderate-coupling regime emerges when the rotational diffusion coefficient of one of the particles, say
D_{2}
, is much larger than both
D_{1}
and
k
. As shown in Fig.
1
(b), the qualitative behaviour of

in this regime is similar to the strong-coupling regime, with the particles most likely to be at a finite distance from each other. In this section, we compute the separation distribution in this regime analytically.

It is well known that for large
D_{2}
and
v_{2}
, the effective noise correlations for the second particle in Eq. (
5
) reduces to,

\displaystyle\langle\cos\theta_{2}(t)\cos\theta_{2}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sin\theta_{2}(t)\sin\theta_{2}(t^{\prime})\rangle\simeq 2T^{\prime}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\quad\mathrm{with,}\quad T^{\prime}=\frac{v_{2}^{2}}{2D_{2}},

(52)

and the typical motion of the ABP with high rotational diffusion constant resembles a Brownian motion with effective temperature
T

[
2
,
36
]
. Thus, in this regime, the time-evolution of the separation vector [see Eq. (

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{r}}(t)=-2k{\bm{r}}(t)+v_{1}{\hat{\bm{n}}}_{1}(t)+\sqrt{2T_{\mathrm{eff}}}\,{\bm{\xi}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(t),\text{~and~}\dot{\theta_{1}}(t)=\sqrt{2D_{1}}\,\eta_{1}(t),

(53)

where
\{{\bm{\xi}}_{\mathrm{eff}}=(\xi_{\mathrm{eff}}^{x},\xi_{\mathrm{eff}}^{y})\}
denote independent white noises with zero mean and correlations given by

\displaystyle\langle\xi_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\alpha}(t)\xi_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta{(t-t^{\prime})}.

(54)

and the effective temperature
T_{\mathrm{eff}}=2T+T^{\prime}
.

It should be noted that Eq. (
53

2k
and in contact with a thermal reservoir at temperature

. The corresponding stationary distribution of the radial coordinate

has been computed by Malakar et al. in Ref.
[

-marginal distribution of the separation vector in this regime.

Radial distribution:

The moderate-coupling is characterized by
D_{1}\ll k\ll D_{2}
. To the leading order, the radial distribution in this regime can be obtained by taking the
D_{1}\to 0
limit in the radial distribution computed in Ref.
[

]
[see Eq. (C19) therein]. For our case, this translates to,

\displaystyle P(r)=\frac{2kr}{T_{\text{eff}}}\exp{\left[-\frac{v_{1}^{2}+4k^{2}r^{2}}{4kT_{\text{eff}}}\right]}I_{0}\left(\frac{v_{1}r}{T_{\text{eff}}}\right),

denotes the zero-th order modified Bessel function of the first kind
[
37
]
. Figure

gets broader as the temperature is increased.

As mentioned already, the effective dynamics of the relative coordinate in the moderate-coupling regime is similar to a single active particle in an external harmonic potential, and the effective repulsion emerges from the fact that active particles tend to stay away from the minimum of the potential

3.3
Weak-coupling regime

The weak-coupling regime emerges when both the rotational diffusion constants
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are much larger than the coupling strength
k
, i.e., when the activity time-scales of both the ABPs are much smaller than the relaxation time-scale in the trap. In this case, for large
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, the typical motions of both the ABPs are similar to passive Brownian particles with effective diffusion constant
v_{1}^{2}/(2D_{1})
and
v_{2}^{2}/(2D_{2})
, respectively [see Eq. (
52
)]. Consequently, the Langevin equation Eq. (
6
) in the weak-coupling regime can be rewritten as,

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{r}}(t)=-2k{\bm{r}}(t)+\sqrt{2\tilde{T}}~{\bm{\eta}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(t),~\text{with}~~\tilde{T}=2T+\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{2D_{1}}+\frac{v_{2}^{2}}{2D_{2}},

(61)

where
\{{\bm{\eta}}_{\mathrm{eff}}=(\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}^{x},\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}^{y})\}
denote independent white noises with zero mean and correlations
\langle\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\alpha}(t)\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta{(t-t^{\prime})}.
The above equation describes the two-dimensional motion of a passive particle in a harmonic trap of strength
2k
at temperature
\tilde{T}
. Consequently,
P(\bm{r})
is nothing but a two-dimensional Gaussian distribution. Hence, it is straightforward to show that the stationary radial and
x
-marginal distributions must have the scaling form,

\displaystyle P(r)=\sqrt{\frac{k}{\tilde{T}}}\,H_{1}\left(r\sqrt{\frac{k}{\tilde{T}}}\right),\quad\text{and},P(x)=\sqrt{\frac{k}{\tilde{T}}}\,H_{2}\left(r\sqrt{\frac{k}{\tilde{T}}}\right),

(62)

where the scaling functions are given by,

\displaystyle H_{1}(z)=2ze^{-z^{2}},\quad\text{and},\quad H_{2}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-z^{2}}.

(63)

Figure
9
(a) and (b) show scaled plots of
P(r)
and
P(x)
, respectively, for different temperatures.

4
Motion of the centroid

To comprehensively understand the behaviour of the binary system, we also characterize the motion of the centroid
{\bm{R}}(t)
, which evolves according to the Langevin equation (
7
). Clearly, the centroid motion is unaffected by the coupling strength of the coupling. At late-times, i.e., times larger than the active time-scales, the centroid is expected to show a diffusive motion with typically Gaussian position fluctuation. On the other hand, signatures of activity are expected at short-times, which we characterize in this section.

4.1
Position moments

First, we compute the mean and variance of the position of the centroid. The formal solution of Eq. (
7
) reads,

\displaystyle R_{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}ds\left[\frac{v_{1}}{2}\cos{\theta_{1}(s)}+\frac{v_{2}}{2}\cos{\theta_{2}(s)}+\sqrt{T}\zeta^{x}(s)\right],

(65)

\displaystyle R_{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}ds\left[\frac{v_{1}}{2}\sin{\theta_{1}(s)}+\frac{v_{2}}{2}\sin{\theta_{2}(s)}+\sqrt{T}\zeta^{y}(s)\right],

(66)

where
R_{x}
and
R_{y}
denote the
x
and
y
components of the centroid position
{\bm{R}}
. Clearly,
\langle R_{x}(t)\rangle=\langle R_{y}(t)\rangle=0
at all times. The second moments of
R_{x}(t)
and
R_{y}(t)
can be computed using Eq. (
5
) and are given by,

\displaystyle\langle R_{x}^{2}(t)\rangle=\langle R_{y}^{2}(t)\rangle=Tt+\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{4D_{1}^{2}}\left(D_{1}t+e^{-D_{1}t}-1\right)+\frac{v_{2}^{2}}{4D_{2}^{2}}\left(D_{2}t+e^{-D_{2}t}-1\right).

(67)

As expected, at late times
t\gg(D_{1}^{-1},D_{2}^{-1})
,

\displaystyle\langle R^{2}(t)\rangle\equiv\langle R_{x}^{2}(t)\rangle+\langle R_{y}^{2}(t)\rangle\simeq\left[2T+\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{2D_{1}}+\frac{v_{2}^{2}}{2D_{2}}\right]t

(68)

which indicates the diffusive nature of the centroid motion. On the other hand, at short-times
t\ll(D_{1}^{-1},D_{2}^{-1})
,

\displaystyle\langle R_{x}^{2}(t)\rangle\simeq Tt+\left(\frac{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}}{8}\right)t^{2}+\mathcal{O}[t^{3}].

(69)

The presence of the activity adds a ballistic component to the short-time dynamics of the centroid, similar to single active particles
[
2
,
5
,
48
,
49
]
. Fig.
10
(a) compares the theoretical prediction Eq. (
67
) with the second moment of the centroid obtained from numerical simulations for different bath temperatures
T
, which shows an excellent agreement.

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2D_{i}}\,\eta_{i}(t).

(70)

At short-times, i.e., for

\displaystyle R_{x}(t)={\cal A}t-{\cal B}_{1}\int_{0}^{t}ds~\phi_{1}(s)-{\cal B}_{2}\int_{0}^{t}ds~\phi_{2}(s)+\sqrt{T}\,\int_{0}^{t}ds~\zeta^{x}(s)

Each of the three terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (

\displaystyle{\cal P}(R_{x},t|\psi_{1},\psi_{2})=\frac{\exp{\left[-\frac{(R_{x}-\mathcal{A}t)^{2}}{2tT+\frac{4}{3}t^{3}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{2}\,D_{1}+\mathcal{B}_{2}^{2}D_{2})}\right]}}{\sqrt{\pi\left(2tT+\frac{4}{3}t^{3}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{2}D_{1}+\mathcal{B}_{2}^{2}D_{2})\right)}}.

\displaystyle P(R_{x},t)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\psi_{2}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\psi_{1}}{2\pi}\frac{\exp{\left[-\frac{(R_{x}-\mathcal{A}t)^{2}}{2tT+\frac{4}{3}t^{3}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{2}\,D_{1}+\mathcal{B}_{2}^{2}D_{2})}\right]}}{\sqrt{\pi\left(2tT+\frac{4}{3}t^{3}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{2}D_{1}+\mathcal{B}_{2}^{2}D_{2})\right)}}.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we study the behaviour of two harmonically coupled active Brownian particles in the presence of thermal noise. In particular, we characterize the nonequilibrium stationary state of the separation of the two particles and the short-time dynamics of the centroid. We show that the stationary separation statistics shows very different behaviour depending on the relative strengths of the two active time-scales and the relaxation time-scale corresponding to the coupling, which we characterize analytically.

At low temperatures, both the strong-coupling and moderate-coupling regimes are characterized by the emergence of an effective short-range repulsion between the two particles. The effective repulsion disappears as the temperature is increased beyond a threshold value
T^{*}
, which we obtain analytically. We further characterize the emergent repulsion by computing the effective potential
V_{\text{eff}}(r)
which shows a minimum at a finite
r^{*}>0
. However, the physical origin of the repulsion in these two regimes are different. In the strong-coupling regime, the repulsion emerges solely due to the difference in the self-propulsion speeds of the two particles. In fact, when the propulsion speeds of the two particles are same, they feel an effective attraction. On the other hand, in the moderate-coupling regime, the effective repulsion is similar to the high probability of a single active particle being away from the minimum of an external potential.

With the rapid progress in robotics, colloidal physics, and surface chemistry, it is possible to investigate active matter systems in controlled ways
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,
63
]
. Therefore, we expect that the emergence of the short-range repulsion between harmonically coupled active particles can be observed in an experimental setup. Furthermore, recent studies suggest that diversity in propulsion speed and attractive interaction oppose cluster formation in active systems
[

,

,

,

,

,

]
. It is worth exploring whether the minimal theoretical framework considered here can account for these observations.
