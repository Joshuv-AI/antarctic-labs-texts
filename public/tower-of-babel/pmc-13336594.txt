Commentary on “Association between urinary heavy metal burden and overactive bladder in adults: evidence from NHANES 2005–2018”
Authors: Zeming Qiu, Xueyan Xia, Xiancheng Li
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Wang et al on urinary heavy metal burden and overactive bladder (OAB) in United States adults using seven NHANES cycles[1]. The authors are to be commended for leveraging a large national dataset and for applying both single metal and mixture models to an important question in lower urinary tract symptoms. We would like to offer several methodological comments to refine interpretation of their findings.

First, the OAB definition warrants scrutiny. The outcome was derived from only two symptom questions – urgency urinary incontinence and nocturia – summed into a modified OAB symptom score. This omits important aspects of validated overactive bladder symptom scales, which were developed as multi-item symptom severity scores to quantify several dimensions of OAB rather than to function as binary diagnostic gold standards[2]. In addition, the two items employed here use different recall periods (urgency urinary incontinence over the past 12 months vs nocturia over the past 30 days). Collapsing heterogeneous recall windows into a single score, without prior validation, may introduce misclassification and limit comparability with OAB symptom score-based literature. Clarifying this limitation and, where feasible, presenting sensitivity analyses using individual symptoms or more conventional OAB definitions would strengthen the outcome assessment.

Second, the handling of the NHANES complex survey design is insufficiently detailed. Although data from seven cycles were pooled, the methods do not clearly state whether sampling weights, strata and primary sampling units were incorporated into the logistic regressions and mixture models, as is standard practice in contemporary NHANES analyses[3]. If complex survey design variables were not used, estimates of OAB prevalence and heavy-metal associations may not be nationally representative and standard errors could be underestimated, yielding overly narrow confidence intervals and optimistic P-values. Explicitly describing the use of NHANES weights and design variables, or acknowledging the absence of survey adjustment as a key limitation, would help readers judge the generalisability of the results.

Third, the exposure assessment and adjustment for kidney function merit further discussion. Urinary metal concentrations were creatinine-corrected and log-transformed, which is common practice, but urinary creatinine strongly depends on age, sex, body composition, comorbidities and renal function – factors also related to OAB. Several metals studied (e.g. cadmium, lead) are nephrotoxic and can both impair glomerular filtration and have their excretion altered by chronic kidney disease. In this context, ratio-based correction may introduce bias in populations with heterogeneous kidney function, and the role of estimated glomerular filtration rate as a covariate is complex, potentially acting as a mediator rather than a pure confounder. Using creatinine-corrected concentrations while adjusting for kidney function may represent over-adjustment or collider bias. A concise causal rationale (for example, via a directed acyclic graph), together with sensitivity analyses using alternative dilution corrections (such as including creatinine as a separate covariate, using specific gravity where available, or stratification by kidney function), would increase confidence in the reported associations[4].

Finally, the interpretation of mixture analyses and the issue of multiplicity could be more cautious. Single-metal models suggest positive associations of several metals (such as cadmium and lead) with OAB and inverse associations for others (such as barium and thallium), whereas weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression and quantile g-computation (qgcomp), as implemented in the present study, appear to yield partly divergent impressions of the overall mixture effect. Without a fuller exploration of collinearity, direction constraints and the stability of estimated weights – issues known to influence WQS and related mixture methods[5,6] – there is a risk of over-interpreting these complex outputs as evidence of uniformly harmful or uniformly protective mixtures. Moreover, a large number of tests were conducted across ten metals, multiple models, non-linear evaluations and subgroup analyses, yet formal multiple-comparison control or even a qualitative discussion of chance findings is absent. Modern approaches such as control of the false discovery rate have been recommended as pragmatic alternatives to Bonferroni-type adjustments in health studies with many correlated hypotheses[7]. Emphasising the hypothesis-generating nature of these results, and considering false discovery rate control or additional sensitivity checks, would provide a more conservative and balanced message.

In summary, this study makes a valuable contribution to the emerging literature on environmental exposures and OAB, and we again commend the authors for addressing an important clinical topic with rich national data. Clarifying the outcome definition, fully accounting for the survey design and more deeply interrogating exposure and mixture modelling choices would, in our view, further enhance the robustness and translational value of their conclusions.
