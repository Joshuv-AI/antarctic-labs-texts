A tensor-train reduced basis solver for parameterized partial differential equations on Cartesian grids
Nicholas Mueller †, Yiran Zhao ¶, Santiago Badia †, Tiangang Cui ¶

Abstract
. In this manuscript, we introduce the tensor-train reduced basis method, a novel projection-based reduced-order model designed for the efficient solution of parameterized partial differential equations. While reduced-order models are widely used for their computational efficiency compared to full-order models, they often involve significant offline computational costs. Our proposed approach mitigates this limitation by leveraging the tensor train format to efficiently represent high-dimensional finite element quantities. This method offers several advantages, including a reduced number of operations for constructing the reduced subspaces, a cost-effective hyper-reduction strategy for assembling the PDE residual and Jacobian, and a lower dimensionality of the projection subspaces for a given accuracy. We provide a posteriori error estimates to validate the accuracy of the method and evaluate its computational performance on benchmark problems, including the Poisson equation, heat equation, and transient linear elasticity in two- and three-dimensional domains. Although the current framework is restricted to problems defined on Cartesian grids, we anticipate that it can be extended to arbitrary shapes by integrating the tensor-train reduced basis method with unfitted finite element techniques.



1.
Introduction

Projection-based
rom
are advanced numerical techniques designed to approximate parametric
hf
(
hf
) models, which typically involve finely resolved spatio-temporal discretizations of
pde
. These methods aim to capture the
hf
parameter-to-solution manifold within a carefully chosen vector subspace. The process generally consists of a computationally intensive offline phase, during which the subspace is constructed and the (Petrov-)Galerkin projection of the
hf
equations onto this subspace is performed. This phase often includes the hyper-reduction of nonaffinely parameterized
hf
quantities, such as residuals and Jacobians. Once the offline phase is complete, an efficient online phase follows, enabling the rapid computation of accurate solutions for new parameter selections.

Among the most widely recognized projection-based
rom
is the
rb
(
rb
) method
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. This approach constructs a reduced-dimensional subspace by extracting it from a dataset of
hf
solutions, commonly referred to as snapshots. The reduced subspace is then used to minimize the
fom
(
fom
) residual under a suitable norm. However, the standard
rb
algorithm struggles to efficiently address time-dependent problems, prompting the development of novel space-time
rom
that simultaneously reduce both the spatial and temporal complexity of the
fom
. Among these, the
strb
(
strb
) method, first introduced in
[
5
]
for solving a linear 2D Boltzmann transport equation, stands out as a prominent example. This method constructs a projection space by taking the Kronecker product of spatial and temporal subspaces, which are derived from the
hf
snapshots.

The
strb
approach has been extended in various works, such as
[
6
]
, which addresses a more complex 3D Stokes equation, and
[
7
]
, where multiple hyper-reduction strategies are proposed within the same space-time framework. In
strb
, the
hf
snapshots are reshaped into either a spatial or temporal matrix. The column space of the spatial matrix captures the spatial evolution of the
hf
variable for fixed time and parameter values and is used to compute the spatial reduced subspace. Similarly, the columns of the temporal matrix represent the temporal evolution of the
hf
variable for fixed spatial coordinates and parameter values and are used to compute the temporal reduced subspace. These subspaces are typically constructed using direct methods such as
tpod
(
tpod
), although greedy algorithms
[
1
,
8
,
9
]
may also be employed. This procedure can be interpreted as a Tucker decomposition
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
of the snapshots, where the data is viewed as a tensor with axes corresponding to the spatial, temporal, and parametric dimensions.

In this work, we introduce a novel
rb
method, termed
ttrb
(
ttrb
), which leverages the recently developed
tt
(
tt
) decomposition technique for tensors. Our approach identifies a joint spatio-temporal subspace by applying a
tt
decomposition
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
to the snapshots, represented as a tensor, akin to the process used in Tucker decompositions. The
tt
representation of a tensor is typically computed using one of two strategies. The first, known as
ttsvd
(
ttsvd
)
[
13
,
14
]
, involves the successive application of
tpod

ttcross
(
ttcross
)
[
15
,
13
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
, constructs the
tt
decomposition greedily through a pseudo-skeleton approximation
[
19
,
20
]
. While
ttsvd
generally provides higher accuracy, it is computationally more expensive compared to
ttcross
.

Recent works have explored the use of
tt
decompositions within the
rb
framework
[
21
,
22
]
, exploiting the Cartesian structure of parameter spaces to achieve low-rank tensor approximations. While these approaches show promise, we argue that addressing the spatial complexity of solutions offers even greater potential for computational speedup. This is because the spatial number of
dof
is often significantly larger than the temporal and parametric sizes. The core efficiency of our
ttrb
method lies in the so-called “split-axes” representation of snapshots, which decomposes the spatial evolution of the snapshots along each Cartesian direction. This representation enables more efficient computation of operations involving spatial quantities. For problems defined on Cartesian geometries, the “split-axes” representation is straightforward to derive. For more general geometries, it could potentially be obtained using unfitted element discretizations
[
23
]
, though we leave this avenue for future investigation. While this strategy could, in principle, be applied within a
strb
framework, doing so would significantly increase the dimensionality of the reduced subspaces, thereby degrading the online performance of the algorithm.

ttrb
decompositions offer significantly higher accuracy than
strb
for a given reduced subspace dimension. This advantage enables the construction of reduced-order models on subspaces with very small dimensions while maintaining high accuracy. Throughout this work, we substantiate this claim through both theoretical analysis and numerical validation. While our primary focus is on addressing time-dependent problems, we present
ttrb
as a robust alternative to
strb
. Nonetheless, it is important to highlight that
ttrb
is a versatile
rom
framework, capable of efficiently approximating parameterized
pde
, whether or not they involve time dependence.

The goal of this work is to develop a projection-based
rom
tailored for the efficient approximation of parametric, potentially transient
pde
, which satisfy the reducibility criteria outlined in
[
24
,
1
]
. Our proposed
rom
exclusively exploits
tt
decompositions of
hf
quantities and performs all necessary operations directly within this decomposition framework. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

(1)

We propose a novel
ttsvd
algorithm for efficiently constructing a projection subspace characterized by a non-standard orthogonality condition, such as one induced by a given inner product norm. Unlike the standard
ttsvd
, which produces a basis orthogonal in the Euclidean norm, our approach accommodates alternative orthogonality conditions. We provide a detailed comparison between the proposed algorithm and the basis construction via
tpod
, highlighting differences in computational cost and accuracy.

(2)

We introduce
ttmdeim
(
ttmdeim
), a hyper-reduction strategy within the
tt
framework that simplifies the TT-cross-DEIM procedure presented in
[
25
]
. Unlike the standard
mdeim
(
mdeim
), this method empirically interpolates tensors directly in their

to achieve an affine decomposition of the residual and Jacobian for the problems under consideration, demonstrating that it achieves accuracy comparable to
mdeim
.

(3)

We derive a posteriori error estimates for the combination of the method indicated above, referred to as the
ttrb
method. These estimates reveal that the accuracy of the procedure is closely tied to a user-defined tolerance, which governs both the precision of the
tt
subspace and the
ttmdeim

1.1.
Notation

The notation used in this paper is inspired by
[
7
]
. We work with multidimensional arrays (tensors) that represent quantities dependent on space, time, and parameters. The subscripts
s
,
t
, and
\mu
denote the spatial, temporal, and parametric axes, respectively. For Cartesian geometries, the spatial axis can be further decomposed into
d
Cartesian directions, with subscripts
1,\dots,d
referring to each direction. A superscript
\mu
is used to indicate quantities that depend on an unspecified parameter value. The parameters considered in this work are
p
-dimensional vectors sampled from a given parameter space, with a generic parameter denoted as
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

In the two-dimensional case, we have the parameter-dependent vectors

\bm{U}^{\mu}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}},\qquad\bm{U}^{\mu}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}},\qquad\bm{U}^{\mu}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}}

belonging to the first coordinate space
\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}_{1}
, the second coordinate space
\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}_{2}
and the temporal space
\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}_{t}
, respectively. Their tensor product

\bm{U}^{\mu}_{1,2,t}=\bm{U}^{\mu}_{1}\otimes\bm{U}^{\mu}_{2}\otimes\bm{U}^{\mu}_{t}

is an element in the parameter-dependent tensor product space

\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}_{1,2,t}=\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}_{1}\otimes\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}_{2}\otimes\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}_{t}.

Throughout this work, we frequently perform re-indexing operations on tensors, which rearrange their subscripts without altering their entries. To simplify notation, we use the same variable names to represent these tensors, even when their subscripts are rearranged. For instance, consider a tensor

\bm{U}_{1,2,t,\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}\times N_{t}\times N_{\mu}}.

We indicate with

\bm{U}_{2,1,\mu,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}\times N_{1}\times N_{\mu}\times N_{t}}

the result of a
permutation of axes
, and with

\bm{U}_{12,t\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{12}\times N_{t}N_{\mu}},\quad N_{12}=N_{1}N_{2},

the result of a
merging of axes
. For convenience, we define
N_{s}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}N_{i}
, where the subscript
s
denotes the merging all spatial axes.

The goal of our research is to leverage low-rank approximations of solution manifolds to develop an efficient
rom
solver for
pde
. This involves identifying a subspace of
\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{\mu}_{1,2,t}
that effectively represents its elements. Throughout this work, we use the
\widehat{\cdot}

\widehat{\bm{U}}^{\mu}_{1,2,t}\approx\bm{U}^{\mu}_{1,2,t}
. The low-rank approximation
\widehat{\bm{U}}^{\mu}_{1,2,t}
belongs to a reduced subspace spanned by a specific reduced basis, which we represent in a
tt
format. The
tt
format expresses (an approximation of) the entries of a multidimensional tensor as a sequence of three-dimensional
tt
cores. For instance, consider
\bm{U}_{1,2,t,\mu}

(1)

\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{0},1,\widehat{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N_{1}\times r_{1}},\quad\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{1},2,\widehat{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times N_{2}\times r_{2}},\quad\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{2},t,\widehat{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{2}\times N_{t}\times r_{t}},\quad\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{t},\mu,\widehat{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}\times N_{\mu}\times 1},

with reduced ranks (or reduced dimensions)

This representation is particularly advantageous for high-dimensional tensors, as the storage requirements for the
tt
decomposition scale linearly with the number of dimensions and quadratically with the ranks. Low-rank algorithms are designed to compute the
tt

[
1
]

r_{i}\ll N_{i}

Since (
2

In order to simplify the notation of (

\mathbb{R}^{N_{a}\times N_{b}\times N_{d}\times N_{e}}\ni\bm{T}_{a,b,d,e}\doteq\bm{R}_{a,b,c}\bm{S}_{c,d,e},\qquad\bm{T}_{a,b,d,e}\left[\alpha_{a},\alpha_{b},\alpha_{d},\alpha_{e}\right]=\sum\limits_{\alpha_{c}}\bm{R}_{a,b,c}\left[\alpha_{a},\alpha_{b},\alpha_{c}\right]\bm{S}_{c,d,e}\left[\alpha_{c},\alpha_{d},\alpha_{e}\right].

By applying (

2.
Reduced basis method in space time

We begin this section by introducing the

2.1.
Full order model

We consider a
d
-cube

\Omega=\Omega_{1}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{d}\quad\text{where}\quad\Omega_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}\ \forall\ i=1,\ldots,d,

with boundary
\partial\Omega
. For transient problems, we also introduce a temporal domain
[0,T]\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{0\}
and a parameter space
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. For a given parameter
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}
, we consider a generic parameterized
pde
defined over the space-time domain
\Omega\times[0,T]
, which takes the form:

(11)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial u^{\mu}}{\partial t}+\mathcal{A}^{\mu}(u^{\mu})&=f^{\mu}\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T],\\
u^{\mu}&=u_{0}^{\mu}\quad\text{in }\Omega\times\{0\},\end{split}

with appropriate boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
. Here,
u^{\mu}:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
represents the unknown state variable,
f^{\mu}:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is the forcing term, and
u_{0}^{\mu}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
specifies the initial condition. The operator
\mathcal{A}^{\mu}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a linear differential operator, whose explicit form is left unspecified. We also define the Dirichlet and Neumann boundaries,
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}
, respectively, such that
\{\Gamma_{D},\Gamma_{N}\}
partitions
\partial\Omega
. For simplicity, we assume that
\Gamma_{D}
consists of entire legs of the
d
-cube, meaning that each element of
\Gamma_{D}
corresponds to a complete
(d-1)
-dimensional facet of
\partial\Omega
. This assumption is made solely to facilitate the analysis of the
fom
with strongly imposed Dirichlet conditions and does not entail a loss of generality. If this condition is not satisfied, the Dirichlet data can instead be imposed weakly, for instance, using a Nitsche penalty method
[
26

]

We now introduce a conforming, quasi-uniform quadrilateral partition of

, denoted by

, along with a uniform temporal partition of

, denoted by

is characterized by a parameter

representing its size, while the temporal mesh is defined such that

is the time-step size. Notably,

can be expressed as the tensor product of one-dimensional partitions defined on

, a property that will be extensively utilized throughout this work. For the spatial discretization of (
11

(12)

\mathcal{V}=H^{1}(\Omega);\qquad\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\Gamma_{D}}=\left\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\ \ :\ v=0\ \text{on}\ \Gamma_{D}\right\},

and their finite-dimensional counterparts

\mathcal{V}_{h}=\{v_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega)\ :\ v_{h}|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(K)\ \forall\ K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},\ p\geq 1\}\subset\mathcal{V},\qquad\mathcal{V}_{h}^{0}=\mathcal{V}_{h}\cap\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\Gamma_{D}}.

Here,

represents the space of polynomials of degree at most

. For temporal discretization, we adopt the

be
) scheme for clarity of exposition, although a Crank-Nicolson method is employed in our numerical experiments. In this work, Dirichlet boundary conditions are enforced strongly. By formulating the lifted weak form of (

(13)

\displaystyle\left(\delta^{-1}\bm{M}_{s,s}+\bm{A}_{s,s}^{\mu}(t_{n})\right)\left(\bm{U}^{\mu}_{s}\right)_{n}=\bm{L}_{s}^{\mu}(t_{n})+\delta^{-1}\bm{M}_{s,s}\left(\bm{U}^{\mu}_{s}\right)_{n-1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}(N_{t}),

Writing (

(14)

\displaystyle\bm{K}^{\mu}_{st,st}\bm{U}^{\mu}_{st}=\bm{L}^{\mu}_{st}.

In this formulation,
\bm{K}^{\mu}_{st,st}
is a block bi-diagonal matrix with
N_{t}
diagonal blocks of the form
\delta^{-1}\bm{M}_{s,s}+\bm{A}_{s,s}^{\mu}(t_{i})
and lower diagonal blocks given by
\delta^{-1}\bm{M}_{s,s}
. The space-time vectors
\bm{U}^{\mu}_{st}
and
\bm{L}^{\mu}_{st}
are constructed by vertically concatenating their spatial components across all time steps. Additionally, we define the discrete Laplacian matrix
\bm{X}_{s,s}
, which is symmetric, positive definite, and represents the
H^{1}_{0}
inner product on
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{0}
. On a Cartesian mesh, this matrix exhibits a structured sparsity pattern.

(15)

\begin{split}\bm{X}_{s,s}&=\bm{X}_{1,1}\otimes\bm{M}_{2,2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{M}_{d,d}+\bm{M}_{1,1}\otimes\bm{X}_{2,2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{M}_{d,d}+\ldots+\bm{M}_{1,1}\otimes\bm{M}_{2,2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{X}_{d,d}\\
&=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d}\overset{d}{\underset{i=1}{\otimes}}\bm{Y}_{i,i}^{k},\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{Y}_{i,i}^{k}=\bm{X}_{i,i}\quad\text{if}\quad i=k,\quad\bm{Y}_{i,i}^{k}=\bm{M}_{i,i}\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{split}

Here,
\bm{X}_{i,i}
and
\bm{M}_{i,i}
denote the discrete Laplacian and mass matrices associated with the
i
th one-dimensional
fe

\mathcal{V}_{h}
and
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{0}
are constructed with a tensor product structure. Consequently,
\bm{X}_{s,s}
can be interpreted as a
d

\bm{X}_{st,st}
as a block-diagonal matrix with
N_{t}
blocks, each given by
\delta\bm{X}_{s,s}
. The factor
\delta
accounts for the
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{V})
inner product of the
fe
basis functions.

2.2.
Space-time reduced-basis method

The

(1)

A computationally intensive

, during which the spatio-temporal basis is constructed, and the (Petrov-) Galerkin projection of the

(2)

A computationally efficient

We define two distinct sets of parameters: the offline parameter set,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{off}}

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{on}}

(16)

-orthogonal, is then constructed as the Kronecker product of these two bases. This space-time basis defines the projection subspace used in the

The two
RSVD
functions are used to compute the
r_{s}
-dimensional spatial subspace and the
r_{t}
-dimensional temporal subspace, respectively. The
rsvd
(
rsvd
), originally introduced in
[
29
]
, is designed to reduce the computational cost of the traditional
svd
(
svd
). Assuming
N_{s}>N_{t}N_{\mu}
, the computational complexity of a full

is given by:

(17)

\texttt{RSVD}(\bm{U}_{s,t\mu})\sim\mathcal{O}(N_{s}N_{t}N_{\mu}\log{(N_{t}N_{\mu})}).

The ranks
r_{s}
and
r_{t}
are determined using the relative energy criterion, as described in
[
1
]
. For further details on the practical implementation of
RSVD
, we refer the reader to
[
30
]
. Additionally, we note that the quantity

(18)

\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{\widehat{s},t\mu}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{s},\widehat{s}}\bm{V}^{T}_{\widehat{s},t\mu},

represents the residual of the spatial compression. Specifically, the matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{s},\widehat{s}}
contains the singular values, while
\bm{V}^{T}_{\widehat{s},t\mu}
holds the right singular vectors of
\bm{U}_{s,t\mu}
. Similarly,
\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{\widehat{t},\widehat{s}\mu}
represents the residual of the temporal compression. These residuals play a crucial role, particularly when employing
ttsvd
, as will be discussed in the next section. The cost estimates in Alg.
1
are derived based on (
17
) and the following observations:

•

The Cholesky factorization of an
N_{s}\times N_{s}
sparse matrix has a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{s}b^{2}\right)

[
31
]
, where
b
denotes the semi-bandwidth of the matrix. Further details regarding
b
are provided in Sect.
3
.

•

The Cholesky factor
\bm{H}_{s,s}
is sparse, with approximately
N_{z}
nonzero entries. Consequently, the cost of the spatial rescaling
\bm{H}_{s,s}\bm{U}_{s,t\mu}
is
\mathcal{O}\left(N_{z}N_{t}N_{\mu}\right)
. On a Cartesian mesh, the number of nonzero entries is given by

N_{z}=\mathcal{O}\left((pd)^{d}N_{s}\right),

where
p
is the polynomial order of the
fe
space. Thus, the complexity can also be expressed as
\mathcal{O}\left((pd)^{d}N_{s}N_{t}N_{\mu}\right)
.

•

Since
\bm{H}_{s,s}
is sparse, the inverse rescaling
\bm{H}_{s,s}^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}_{s,\widehat{s}}
generally requires fewer operations than the worst-case scenario of
\mathcal{O}(r_{s}N_{s}^{2})
, which would occur if
\bm{H}_{s,s}
were a full triangular matrix. However, due to the lack of a general expression for the bandwidth of
\bm{H}_{s,s}
, we conservatively assume the worst-case cost. This assumption does not affect the overall computational cost analysis, as this step is computationally negligible.

•

The spatial contraction step corresponds to the
sthosvd
(
sthosvd
) approach
[
32
]
, which reduces the cost of temporal compression. The computational cost of
sthosvd
is dominated by the spatial compression step, which involves two matrix-matrix multiplications. In comparison, the subsequent
rsvd
step is negligible. Alternatively, the temporal basis could be computed directly from
\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{\widehat{s},t\mu}
, bypassing the spatial contraction step. This approach essentially corresponds to the Tucker decomposition of
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{s,t,\mu}
. However, both strategies yield similar results and do not impact the overall computational cost of
tpod
, so this remains a minor implementation detail.

As outlined earlier, the
strb
method employs a space-time basis defined as

\mathbb{R}^{N_{st}\times r_{st}}\ni\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{st}}=\bm{\Phi}_{s,\widehat{s}}\otimes\bm{\Phi}_{t,\widehat{t}},

where
r_{st}=r_{s}r_{t}
denotes the dimension of the reduced subspace, indexed by the subscript
\widehat{st}
. In
tpod
, this dimension is the product of the spatial and temporal subspace dimensions. The accuracy of the space-time basis is given by:

(19)

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N_{\mu}}\|\left(\bm{U}_{st,\mu}-\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{st}}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{st},st}\bm{X}_{st,st}\bm{U}_{st,\mu}\right)\left[:,j\right]\|^{2}_{\bm{X}_{st,st}}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\left(\|\bm{U}_{s,t\mu}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widehat{\bm{U}}_{t,\widehat{s}\mu}\|_{F}^{2}\right).

The term
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{t,\widehat{s}\mu}
in the estimate corresponds to the spatial contraction computed during the
sthosvd
procedure. The result in (
19
) is derived by combining the findings from
[
7
]
, which address the case of an
\ell^{2}
-orthogonal basis, with the results from
[
1
,
2
]
, where the relationship between an
\ell^{2}
-orthogonal basis and an
\bm{X}_{st,st}
-orthogonal basis is established.

We now describe the online phase of the
strb
method, where the reduced version of (
14
) is assembled and solved. Specifically, we consider the reduced equations obtained by projecting (
14
) onto the subspace spanned by
\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{st}}
using a Galerkin projection. For a comprehensive review of the more general Petrov-Galerkin projections in the context of
rb
methods, we refer the reader to
[
4
]
. Using algebraic notation, the
strb
problem is formulated as:

(20)

\text{find}\quad\widehat{\bm{U}}_{\widehat{st}}^{\mu}\quad\text{such that}\quad\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{st},st}\left(\bm{L}^{\mu}_{st}-\bm{K}^{\mu}_{st,st}\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{st}}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{\widehat{st}}^{\mu}\right)=\bm{0}_{\widehat{st}}\Longleftrightarrow\widehat{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{st},\widehat{st}}^{\mu}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{\widehat{st}}^{\mu}=\widehat{\bm{L}}_{\widehat{st}}^{\mu},

where

\widehat{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{st},\widehat{st}}^{\mu}=\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{st},st}\bm{K}^{\mu}_{st,st}\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{st}},\qquad\widehat{\bm{L}}_{\widehat{st}}^{\mu}=\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{st},st}\bm{L}^{\mu}_{st}

represent the Galerkin projections of the space-time Jacobian and residual, respectively. Since computing these quantities involves operations that scale with the full-order dimensions, employing a hyper-reduction strategy to approximate the Jacobians and residuals is essential for efficiency.

Remark 2
.

Although this work focuses exclusively on linear problems, we use the terms Jacobian and residual to refer to the
lhs
(
lhs
) and
rhs
of the problem, respectively, for the sake of generality. Notably, the described hyper-reduction techniques are equally applicable to both linear and nonlinear problems without requiring any modifications.

To achieve hyper-reduction, methods like
mdeim

[
2
,
7
]
aim to construct the following affine expansions:

(21)

\bm{K}^{\mu}_{st,st}\approx\sum_{i=1}^{r_{st}^{\bm{K}}}\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{st,\widehat{st}^{\bm{K}},st}[:,i,:]\widehat{\bm{K}}^{\mu}_{\widehat{st}^{\bm{K}}}[i];\qquad\bm{L}^{\mu}_{st}\approx\sum_{i=1}^{r_{st}^{\bm{L}}}\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{L}}_{st,\widehat{st}^{\bm{L}}}[:,i]\widehat{\bm{L}}^{\mu}_{\widehat{st}^{\bm{L}}}[i].

Here,

\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{st,\widehat{st}^{\bm{K}},st}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{st}\times r_{st}^{\bm{K}}\times N_{st}},\qquad\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{L}}_{st,\widehat{st}^{\bm{K}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{st}\times r_{st}^{\bm{L}}}

represent two bases that span reduced-dimensional subspaces, used to approximate the manifold of parameterized Jacobians and residuals. The goal is to solve the approximated
rom
by substituting the affine expansions (
21
) into (
20
):

(22)

\text{find}\quad\widehat{\bm{U}}_{\widehat{st}}^{\mu}\quad\text{such that}\quad\widebar{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{st},\widehat{st}}^{\mu}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{\widehat{st}}^{\mu}=\widebar{\bm{L}}_{\widehat{st}}^{\mu},

where

\widehat{\bm{K}}^{\mu}_{\widehat{st},\widehat{st}}\approx\widebar{\bm{K}}^{\mu}_{st,st}=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{st}^{\bm{K}}}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{st},st}\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{st,\widehat{st}^{\bm{K}},st}\left[:,i,:\right]\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{st}}\widehat{\bm{K}}^{\mu}_{\widehat{st}^{\bm{K}}}\left[i\right];\qquad\widehat{\bm{L}}^{\mu}_{\widehat{st}}\approx\widebar{\bm{L}}^{\mu}_{st}=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{st}^{\bm{L}}}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{st},st}\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{L}}_{st,\widehat{st}^{\bm{L}}}\left[:,i\right]\widehat{\bm{L}}^{\mu}_{\widehat{st}^{\bm{L}}}\left[i\right].

Since the bases are
\bm{\mu}
-independent, most of the Galerkin projection computations can be performed offline. During the online phase, it suffices to compute the reduced coefficients
\widehat{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{st}^{\bm{K}}}
and
\widehat{\bm{L}}_{\widehat{st}^{\bm{L}}}
, followed by their respective multiplications with the projected bases. These operations depend only on the reduced dimensions
r_{st}
,
r_{st}^{\bm{K}}
, and
r_{st}^{\bm{L}}
, making them independent of the
fom
dimensions. This ensures the computational efficiency typically associated with
rom
. In this work, we employ
mdeim
as the hyper-reduction strategy, with all relevant details provided in Subsection
3.4
.

3.
A novel TT-RB solver

In this section, we discuss the
ttrb

20

(23)

\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{t}}=\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{0},1,\widehat{1}}\cdots\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d},t,\widehat{t}}.

We recall that the dimension of the projection subspace is now represented by the axis
\widehat{t}

\widehat{st}

6

strb

tt

strb

The content of the section is organized as follows:

•

We introduce the standard (Euclidean)

[
14
]
. We also discuss the accuracy of the procedure, both in the Euclidean case and when imposing an

•

We present a modified

-orthogonal
tt

\bm{X}_{s,s}

•

We extend the previous algorithm to the more general case of a rank-

•

We define the standard

•

We elaborate a method for projecting the

•

Lastly, we present an accuracy measure for the resulting

The
ttsvd
computes the
tt

rsvd
on the (truncated) remainder of the previous iteration. Similarly to

, we use the hyper-parameter
\varepsilon
to control the error of the algorithm. In particular, the following accuracy statement holds.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose the unfolding matrices
\bm{T}_{\widehat{i-1}i,i+1:\mu}

\bm{T}_{1,2:\mu}=\bm{U}_{1,2:\mu},\quad\bm{T}_{\widehat{i-1}i,i+1:\mu}=\bm{R}_{\widehat{i-1}i,i+1:\mu}\quad\forall\ i=2,\ldots,d+1

admit a low-rank approximation with relative errors
\varepsilon_{i}
for given ranks
r_{i}
:

(24)

\displaystyle\bm{T}_{\widehat{i-1}i,i+1:\mu}=\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{i-1}i,\widehat{i}}\bm{R}_{\widehat{i},i+1:\mu}+\bm{E}_{\widehat{i-1}i,i+1:\mu}\ ,\quad\|\bm{E}_{\widehat{i-1}i,i+1:\mu}\|^{2}_{F}=\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\|\bm{T}_{\widehat{i-1}i,i+1:\mu}\|^{2}_{F},\quad\forall\ i=1,\ldots,d+1.

Then, the projection operator
\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{t}}
in (
23
) satisfies:

(25)

\|\bm{U}_{st,\mu}-\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{t}}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{t},st}\bm{U}_{st,\mu}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d+1}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\|\bm{T}_{\widehat{i-1}i,i+1:\mu}\|^{2}_{F}\leq\varepsilon^{2}(d+1)\|\bm{U}_{st,\mu}\|^{2}_{F},

where
\varepsilon=\sup_{i}\varepsilon_{i}
.

Proof.

By virtue of (
24
), we may express
\bm{U}_{st,\mu}
as:

\bm{U}_{1,\ldots,\mu}=\bm{\Phi}_{1,\widehat{1}}\bm{R}_{\widehat{1},2,\ldots,\mu}+\bm{E}_{1,\ldots,\mu}=\bm{\Phi}_{1,\widehat{1}}\left(\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{1},2,\widehat{2}}\left(\cdots\left(\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d},t,\widehat{t}}\bm{R}_{\widehat{t},\mu}+\bm{E}_{\widehat{d},t,\mu}\right)\cdots\right)+\bm{E}_{\widehat{1},2,\ldots,\mu}\right)+\bm{E}_{1,\ldots,\mu}.

On the other hand, the approximated snapshots

\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{t}}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{t},st}\bm{U}_{st,\mu}=\widehat{\bm{U}}_{st,\mu}

can be written according to

\widehat{\bm{U}}_{1,\ldots,\mu}=\bm{\Phi}_{1,\widehat{1}}\widehat{\bm{R}}_{\widehat{1},2,\ldots,\mu},

where
\widehat{\bm{R}}_{\widehat{1},2,\ldots,\mu}
is the
ttsvd
approximation of
\bm{R}_{\widehat{1},2,\ldots,\mu}
, with ranks
(r_{2},\ldots,r_{t})
. By performing all the
ttsvd

\widehat{\bm{U}}_{1,\ldots,\mu}=\bm{\Phi}_{1,\widehat{1}}\widehat{\bm{R}}_{\widehat{1},2,\ldots,\mu}=\bm{\Phi}_{1,\widehat{1}}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{1},2,\widehat{2}}\cdots\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d},t,\widehat{t}}\bm{R}_{\widehat{t},\mu}.

Consequently, we can write

\bm{U}_{1,2:\mu}-\widehat{\bm{U}}_{1,2:\mu}=\bm{\Phi}_{1,\widehat{1}}\bm{R}_{\widehat{1},2:\mu}+\bm{E}_{1,2:\mu}-\bm{\Phi}_{1,\widehat{1}}\widehat{\bm{R}}_{\widehat{1},2:\mu}.

By virtue of (
24
) and of the orthogonality of every matrix
\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{i-1}i,\widehat{i}}
, we have:

\begin{split}\|\bm{U}_{1,2:\mu}-\widehat{\bm{U}}_{1,2:\mu}\|_{F}^{2}&\leq\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\|\bm{T}_{1,2:\mu}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{R}_{\widehat{1},2:\mu}-\widehat{\bm{R}}_{\widehat{1},2:\mu}\|_{F}^{2}=\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\|\bm{T}_{1,2:\mu}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{1},2,\widehat{2}}\bm{R}_{\widehat{2},3:\mu}+\bm{E}_{\widehat{1},2,3:\mu}-\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{1},2,\widehat{2}}\widehat{\bm{R}}_{\widehat{2},3:\mu}\|_{F}^{2}\\

Since
\bm{T}_{\widehat{i-1}i,i+1:\mu}=\bm{R}_{\widehat{i-1},i:\mu}
is the output of an

\|\bm{T}_{\widehat{i-1}i,i+1:\mu}\|^{2}_{F}\leq\|\bm{T}_{\widehat{i-2}\,i-1,i:\mu}\|^{2}_{F}\leq\cdots\leq\|\bm{T}_{1,2:\mu}\|^{2}_{F}=\|\bm{U}_{1,2:\mu}\|^{2}_{F}.

Then the result follows.

3.2.
Basis construction: the case of a rank-1 norm matrix

When
\bm{X}_{s,s}
is a rank-1 matrix, the Cholesky decomposition of a Kronecker product matrix applies:

(31)

\texttt{Cholesky}\left(\bm{X}_{1,1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{X}_{d,d}\right)=\bm{H}_{1,1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{H}_{d,d}.

Now, let us recall the diadic representation
[
14
]
of
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{s,t\mu}
. Let us consider the first unfolding

\widetilde{\bm{T}}_{1,\ldots,\mu}=\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{1,\ldots,\mu}.

By applying a
RSVD
with
\varepsilon=0
, we can write

\widetilde{\bm{T}}_{1,2:\mu}=\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}_{1,\sigma_{1}}\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{\sigma_{1},2:\mu},

where the subscript
\sigma_{1}
refers to
N_{\sigma_{1}}=\min\{N_{1},N_{2:\mu}\}

\widetilde{\bm{T}}_{1,2:\mu}

\widetilde{\bm{T}}_{\sigma_{1}2,3:\mu}=\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{\sigma_{1}2,3:\mu},

we can write

\widetilde{\bm{T}}_{\sigma_{1}2,3:\mu}=\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}_{\sigma_{1}2,\sigma_{2}}\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{\sigma_{2},3:\mu},

where
\sigma_{2}
refers to
N_{\sigma_{2}}=\min\{r_{1}N_{2},N_{3:\mu}\}
. This procedure continues iteratively, until we get

(32)

\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{s,t\mu}=\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}_{1,\sigma_{1}}\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}_{\sigma_{1},2,\sigma_{2}}\cdots\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}_{\sigma_{d-1},d,\sigma_{d}}\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{\sigma_{d},t\mu}.

Now let us consider a different diadic decomposition for
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{s,t\mu}
. Notice that

\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{s,t\mu}=\left(\bm{H}_{1,1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{H}_{d,d}\right)\bm{U}_{s,t\mu}=\bm{H}_{d,d}\odot_{d}\left(\cdots\ \left(\bm{H}_{1,1}\odot_{1}\bm{U}_{1,\ldots,\mu}\right)\right).

We introduce the rescaled unfolding

\widebar{\bm{T}}_{1,2:\mu}=\bm{H}_{1,1}\bm{U}_{1,2:\mu},

which admits a diadic representation

\widebar{\bm{T}}_{1,2:\mu}=\widebar{\bm{\Phi}}_{1,\sigma_{1}}\widebar{\bm{R}}_{\sigma_{1},2:\mu}.

Then, we rescale the unfolding
\widebar{\bm{R}}_{\sigma_{1},2:\mu}
by
\bm{H}_{2,2}
, i.e.

\widebar{\bm{T}}_{\sigma_{1},2,3:\mu}=\bm{H}_{2,2}\odot_{2}\widebar{\bm{R}}_{\sigma_{1},2,3:\mu},

which admits its own diadic format, and so on. By iteration, we can derive a similar expression to (
32

(33)

\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{s,t\mu}=\widebar{\bm{\Phi}}_{1,\sigma_{1}}\widebar{\bm{\Phi}}_{\sigma_{1},2,\sigma_{2}}\cdots\widebar{\bm{\Phi}}_{\sigma_{d-1},d,\sigma_{d}}\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{\sigma_{d},t\mu}.

The representations (

\bm{X}_{s,s}
does not affect the temporal evolution of the snapshots. On the other hand, the spatial cores in (

By virtue of the hierarchical property (

•

•

Under the “perfect cube” assumption, we can express the semi-bandwidth in Alg.

b=\mathcal{O}(M^{d-1}).

This result is intuitive: since

On the other hand, under the same assumptions, we can bound the complexity of Alg.

\text{cost}(\text{{$X^{1}$-TT-SVD}})\leq(d+1)\cdot\text{cost}(\text{$1$st iteration of {$X^{1}$-TT-SVD}}).

This result is a consequence of the decreasing cost of the

itself decreases. Based on this observation, and recalling that

(35)

(d+1)\cdot\mathcal{O}\left(M+M^{d+2}+M^{d+2}\log{M}+rM^{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left((d+1)M^{d+2}\log{M}\right)\ \forall\ d\geq 1.

Comparing the estimates (

(36)

\text{cost}(\text{{$X^{1}$-TT-SVD}})<\text{cost}(\text{{TPOD}})\ \forall\ d>2.

The reason

Now, let us consider the more involved case where

(1)

For simplicity, we consider the case

(39)

\begin{split}\widecheck{\bm{\Phi}}_{\widehat{2},12}\bm{X}_{12,12}\widecheck{\bm{\Phi}}_{12,\widehat{2}}&=\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},k}\left(\widecheck{\bm{\Phi}}_{\widehat{0},1,\widehat{1}}\left[1,:,\alpha_{1}\right]^{T}\bm{Y}_{1,1}^{k}\widecheck{\bm{\Phi}}_{\widehat{0},1,\widehat{1}}\left[1,:,\beta_{1}\right]\right)\left(\widecheck{\bm{\Phi}}_{\widehat{1},2,\widehat{2}}\left[\alpha_{1},:,\alpha_{2}\right]^{T}\bm{Y}_{2,2}^{k}\widecheck{\bm{\Phi}}_{\widehat{1},2,\widehat{2}}\left[\beta_{1},:,\beta_{2}\right]\right)\\

3.5.
Approximation of the Jacobians

To implement the
ttmdeim
approximation for space-time Jacobians, we first require a “split axes” format for representing these quantities. We assume for simplicity that the sparsity pattern of the Jacobians does not vary for different values of
\bm{\mu}
. Let us momentarily consider a steady-state Jacobian
\bm{K}_{s,s}^{\mu}
. Since we operate within a Cartesian framework, we can define an index mapping

(53)

\mathcal{I}_{z}:\mathbb{N}(N_{z_{1}})\times\cdots\times\mathbb{N}(N_{z_{d}})\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}(N_{z})

which associates a global index corresponding to a nonzero entry of
\bm{K}_{s,s}^{\mu}
with a tuple of indices corresponding to nonzero entries of the 1-d Jacobians

\bm{K}_{1,1}^{\mu},\ldots,\bm{K}_{d,d}^{\mu}.

Note that, in general, there is no direct relationship between the entries of
\bm{K}_{s,s}^{\mu}
and those of the 1-d Jacobians, in the sense that usually

\bm{K}_{1,1}^{\mu}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{K}_{d,d}^{\mu}\neq\bm{K}_{s,s}^{\mu}.

Nonetheless, it is still possible to infer information about the sparsity of
\bm{K}_{s,s}^{\mu}
from the sparsity patterns of
\bm{K}_{1,1}^{\mu},\ldots,\bm{K}_{d,d}^{\mu}
.
The mapping (
53
) allows us to identify the following “split-axes” formulation for the steady-state Jacobian:

\bm{K}_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}}^{\mu}[i_{z_{1}},\ldots,i_{z_{d}}]=\bm{K}_{z}^{\mu}[i_{z}],\qquad\mathcal{I}_{z}(i_{z_{1}},\ldots,i_{z_{d}})=i_{z}.

Given our assumption of fixed sparsity across the parameters, we can use the mapping (
53
) to interchangeably consider the snapshots tensors
\bm{K}_{s,s,\mu}
,
\bm{K}_{z,\mu}
and
\bm{K}_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{d},\mu}
. Similarly, in unsteady applications we have the congruence by isometry relationships

\bm{K}_{st,st,\mu}\cong\bm{K}_{z,t,\mu}\cong\bm{K}_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{d},t,\mu}.

Therefore, we may run Alg.
2
on
\bm{K}_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{d},t,\mu}
to compute the
tt
decomposition

\{\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{0},z_{1},\widehat{1}},\ \ldots,\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{d-1},z_{d},\widehat{d}}\ ,\ \bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{d},t,\widehat{t}}\},

and consequently perform a
ttmdeim
approximation of the Jacobians:

(54)

\bm{K}_{zt}^{\mu}\approx\widehat{\bm{K}}_{zt}^{\mu}=\left(\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{0},z_{1},\widehat{1}}^{\bm{K}}\cdots\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d},t,\widehat{t}}^{\bm{K}}\right)\widehat{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t}}^{\mu},

where

(55)

\widehat{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t}}^{\mu}=\left(\bm{P}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t},zt}(\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{0},z_{1},\widehat{1}}^{\bm{K}}\cdots\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d},t,\widehat{t}}^{\bm{K}})\right)^{-1}\bm{P}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t},zt}\bm{K}_{zt}^{\mu}=\left(\bm{P}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t},zt}\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{zt,\widehat{t}}\right)^{-1}\bm{P}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t},zt}\bm{K}_{zt}^{\mu}.

We lastly remark that, by exploiting the sparsity of
\bm{K}^{\mu}_{i,i}
, the spatial cores can equivalently be represented as 4-d sparse arrays:

(56)

\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{i-1},i,i,\widehat{i}}\cong\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{i-1},z_{i},\widehat{i}}\ .

subspace. As in previous subsections, we commence by recalling the procedure in a standard
strb
setting. Moreover, we only focus on the projection of the Jacobian, as it is more intricate than that of the residual. Recalling the definition of
\mathcal{K}
from (
47
), the Galerkin projection of the Jacobian in a standard
strb
framework requires computing

(57)

\begin{split}\widebar{\bm{K}}^{\mu}_{\widehat{st},\widehat{st}}&=\left(\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{s},s}\otimes\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{t},t}\right)\left(\sum\limits_{i_{s}=1}^{r_{s}^{\bm{K}}}\sum\limits_{i_{t}=1}^{r_{t}^{\bm{K}}}\left(\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{s,\widehat{s}^{\bm{K}},s}\left[:,i_{s},:\right]\otimes\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{t,\widehat{t}^{\bm{K}}}\left[:,i_{t}\right]\right)\widehat{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{st}^{\bm{K}}}^{\mu}\left[\mathcal{K}\left(i_{s},i_{t}\right)\right]\right)\left(\bm{\Phi}_{s,\widehat{s}}\otimes\bm{\Phi}_{t,\widehat{t}}\right)\\
&=\sum\limits_{i_{s}=1}^{r_{s}^{\bm{K}}}\sum\limits_{i_{t}=1}^{r_{t}^{\bm{K}}}\bigg(\left(\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{s},s}\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{s,\widehat{s}^{\bm{K}},s}\left[:,i_{s},:\right]\bm{\Phi}_{s,\widehat{s}}\right)\otimes\widehat{\bm{\Phi}}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t},\widehat{t}^{\bm{K}},\widehat{t}}\left[:,i_{t},:\right]\bigg)\widehat{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{st}^{\bm{K}}}^{\mu}\left[\mathcal{K}\left(i_{s},i_{t}\right)\right],\end{split}

where we have introduced

\widehat{\bm{\Phi}}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t},\widehat{t}^{\bm{K}},\widehat{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times n_{t}^{\bm{K}}\times n_{t}},\quad\widehat{\bm{\Phi}}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t},\widehat{t}^{\bm{K}},\widehat{t}}\left[\alpha,i_{t},\beta\right]=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N_{t}}\bm{\Phi}_{t,\widehat{t}}\left[n,\alpha\right]\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{t,\widehat{t}^{\bm{K}}}\left[n,i_{t}\right]\bm{\Phi}_{t,\widehat{t}}\left[n,\beta\right].

As shown above, the Jacobian reduction comprises a Kronecker product between a spatial and a temporal factor, the result of which is then multiplied by the
\bm{\mu}
-dependent coefficient. Since
N_{z}\gg N_{t}
in practical applications, the cost of (
57
) scales as
\mathcal{O}\left(r_{s}^{\bm{K}}r_{s}^{2}N_{z}\right)
, i.e. the complexity of computing the spatial factor.

Let us now consider the Jacobian projection in a
tt
framework. Exploiting the mixed-product property of the Kronecker product, and skipping some computations that are conceptually straightforward but of tedious notation, we have

(58)

\begin{split}\widebar{\bm{K}}^{\mu}_{\widehat{t},\widehat{t}}=&\sum_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\delta_{1}}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{0},1,\widehat{1}}\left[1,:,\alpha_{1}\right]^{T}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{0}^{\bm{K}},1,1,\widehat{1}^{\bm{K}}}^{\bm{K}}\left[1,:,:,\beta_{1}\right]\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{0},1,\widehat{1}}\left[1,:,\delta_{1}\right]\cdot\\
&\sum_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2},\delta_{2}}\ldots\sum_{\alpha_{d},\beta_{d},\delta_{d}}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d-1},d,\widehat{d}}\left[\alpha_{d-1},:,\alpha_{d}\right]^{T}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d-1}^{\bm{K}},d,d,\widehat{d}^{\bm{K}}}^{\bm{K}}\left[\beta_{d-1},:,:,\beta_{d}\right]\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d-1},d,\widehat{d}}\left[\delta_{d-1},:,\delta_{d}\right]\cdot\\
&\ \ \ \sum_{\beta_{t}}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N_{t}}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d},t,\widehat{t}}\left[\alpha_{d},n,:\right]^{T}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d}^{\bm{K}},t,\widehat{t}^{\bm{K}}}^{\bm{K}}\left[\beta_{d},n,\beta_{t}\right]\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{d},t,\widehat{t}}\left[\delta_{d},n,:\right]\widehat{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t}^{\bm{K}}}^{\mu}\left[\beta_{t}\right]\\
=&\ \ \sum\limits_{\beta_{t}}\widehat{\bm{\Phi}}_{\widehat{t},\widehat{t}^{\bm{K}},\widehat{t}}^{\bm{K}}[:,\beta_{t},:]\widehat{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{t}^{\bm{K}}}^{\mu}[\beta_{t}].\end{split}

Despite the presence of numerous indices, (
58
) simply expresses, in terms of several 3d and 4-d
tt
cores, the same spatial and temporal operations already discussed in (
57
) for
strb
. Before deriving the cost of (
58
), we recall that the number of nonzeros in a sparse
fe
matrix in a 1-d problem is
\mathcal{O}\left(M\right)
. Consequently, the cost of computing each spatial compression

\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{i-1},i,\widehat{i}}\left[\alpha_{i-1},:,\alpha_{i}\right]^{T}\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{i-1}^{\bm{K}},i,i,\widehat{i}^{\bm{K}}}^{\bm{K}}\left[\beta_{i-1},:,:,\beta_{i}\right]\bm{\Phi}_{\widehat{i-1},i,\widehat{i}}\left[\delta_{i-1},:,\delta_{i}\right]

scales as
\mathcal{O}\left(M\right)
. Summing over the indices, and introducing a “bounding rank” for the Jacobians

r_{i}^{\bm{K}}<r^{\bm{K}}\ \forall\ i

we can show that the cost scales as
\mathcal{O}\left(dM(r^{2}r^{\bm{K}})^{2}\right)
. Depending on the expression of
r
and
r^{\bm{K}}
, the cost of this step can be considerable, though in our experience it is comparable to the Galerkin projection in an
strb
framework.

Remark 6
.

When solving a vector-valued problem, the hyper-reduction for the residuals/Jacobians involves the
tt
decompositions

(59)

\displaystyle\left\{\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{L}}_{\widehat{0},1,\widehat{1}}\ ,\ldots,\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{L}}_{\widehat{d-1},d,\widehat{d}}\ ,\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{L}}_{\widehat{d},c,\widehat{c}}\ ,\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{L}}_{\widehat{c},t,\widehat{t}}\right\}\ ;\qquad\left\{\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{0},1,1,\widehat{1}}\ ,\ldots,\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{d-1},d,d,\widehat{d}}\ ,\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{d},c,c,\widehat{c}}\ ,\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{K}}_{\widehat{c},t,\widehat{t}}\right\}\ .

Recall that the subscript
c
denotes the components axis, as explained in (
10
). In this case, the Galerkin projection increases by one step, due to the presence of the additional component core.

3.7.
A posteriori error estimate

In this subsection we present the accuracy estimate for the
ttrb
method. The result follows directly from an adaptation of the analysis in
[
7
]
, originally developed for the standard
strb
approach. Before stating the result, we introduce a few key quantities:

•

The coercivity constant, in the
\bm{X}_{st,st}
-norm, of the full-order Jacobian:

(60)

\displaystyle C=\inf\limits_{\bm{V}_{st}\neq\bm{0}_{st}}\frac{\|\bm{K}^{\mu}_{st,st}\bm{V}_{st}\|_{\bm{X}_{st,st}^{-1}}}{\left\lVert\bm{V}_{st}\right\rVert_{\bm{X}_{st,st}}}=\left\lVert\bm{X}_{st,st}^{-1/2}\bm{K}^{\mu}_{st,st}\bm{X}_{st,st}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}.

•

The
ttmdeim
error due to interpolation for the Jacobian and the residual, respectively denoted as
\chi^{\bm{K}}
and
\chi^{\bm{L}}
(see (
50
)).

As in
[
37
,
7
]
, the total error introduced by the
ttrb
method can be decomposed into two main contributions. The first is associated with the
ttmdeim
hyper-reduction, while the second stems from the Galerkin projection onto the subspace spanned by
\bm{\Phi}_{st,\widehat{t}}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let us consider the well-posed problem defined in (
14
), and its reduced approximation (
22
), obtained by combining the
ttsvd
and
ttmdeim
procedures. Let
\bm{U}^{\mu}_{st}
denote the solution of the full-order model (
fom
), and let
\widehat{\bm{U}}^{\mu}_{st}
denote its reduced approximation. The following estimate holds:

(61)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{U}^{\mu}_{st}-\widehat{\bm{U}}_{st}^{\mu}\right\rVert_{\bm{X}_{st,st}}\leq

\displaystyle\ C^{-1}\left(\chi^{\bm{L}}\left\lVert\bm{L}_{s,t\mu}\right\rVert_{F}\left\lVert\bm{X}_{st,st}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}+\chi^{\bm{K}}\left\lVert\bm{K}_{s,t\mu}\right\rVert_{F}\left\lVert\bm{X}_{st,st}^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{U}}_{st}^{\mu}\right\rVert_{\bm{X}_{st,st}}\right)\sqrt{d+1}\varepsilon

(62)

\displaystyle+C^{-1}\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{L}}^{\mu}_{st}-\widehat{\bm{K}}^{\mu}_{st,st}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{st}^{\mu}\right\rVert_{\bm{X}_{st,st}^{-1}}.

Proof.

See the proof in
[
7
]
, Theorem 1. The only difference lies in the presence of the
\sqrt{d+1}
factor, which arises from applying a
ttsvd
on the residuals and Jacobians.
∎

We refer to
[
7
]
for additional details. Here, we simply remark that the bound in (
61
) may appear conservative, since not all terms explicitly decay with
\varepsilon
. Nevertheless, in practice, the residual-like quantity

\left\lVert\widehat{\bm{L}}^{\mu}_{st}-\widehat{\bm{K}}^{\mu}_{st,st}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{st}^{\mu}\right\rVert_{\bm{X}_{st,st}^{-1}}

is strongly correlated with
\varepsilon
. Specifically, when the problem is reducible according to the definition in
[
1
]
, if the reduced subspace is adequately constructed (i.e. via a sufficiently rich and representative parameter sampling during snapshot generation), this residual exhibits the same behavior as the estimate in (
28
).

•

Online performance

and

hf

61
) for various tolerances

4.3.
Linear Elasticity Problem

In this subsection, we solve a transient version of the linear elasticity equation:

(71)

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial\underline{u}^{\mu}}{\partial t}-\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\underline{\underline{\sigma}}^{\mu}(\underline{u}^{\mu}))=\underline{0}&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T],\\
\underline{u}^{\mu}=\underline{g}^{\mu}&\text{on}\ \Gamma_{D}\times(0,T],\\
\underline{\underline{\sigma}}^{\mu}(\underline{u}^{\mu})\cdot\underline{n}=\underline{h}^{\mu}&\text{on}\ \Gamma_{N}\times(0,T],\\
\underline{u}^{\mu}=\underline{u}_{0}^{\mu}&\text{in}\ \Omega\times\{0\}.\end{cases}

The displacement field
\underline{u}
is vector-valued, requiring the inclusion of a component axis as illustrated in (
10
). The stress tensor
\underline{\underline{\sigma}}^{\mu}
is expressed as

(72)

\underline{\underline{\sigma}}^{\mu}(\underline{u}^{\mu})=2p^{\mu}\underline{\epsilon}(\underline{u}^{\mu})+\lambda^{\mu}\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\underline{u}^{\mu})\underline{I},

where
\underline{\epsilon}
is the symmetric gradient operator, and
\lambda^{\mu}
,
p^{\mu}
are the Lamé coefficients one can express as functions of the Young modulus
E^{\mu}
and the Poisson coefficient
\nu^{\mu}
as

(73)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\mu}=\frac{E^{\mu}\nu^{\mu}}{(1+\nu^{\mu})(1-2\nu^{\mu})};\qquad p^{\mu}=\frac{E^{\mu}}{2(1+\nu^{\mu})}.

We consider a 3-
d
domain

\Omega=[0,1]\times[0,1/8]^{2}

equipped with a Dirichlet boundary defined as in (
67
), on which we impose a homogeneous condition, and three Neumann boundaries

(74)

\begin{split}\Gamma_{N_{1}}&=\{\underline{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{T}\in\partial\Omega:x_{1}=1\};\\
\Gamma_{N_{2}}&=\{\underline{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{T}\in\partial\Omega:x_{2}=0,x_{1}\in(0,1)\};\\
\Gamma_{N_{3}}&=\{\underline{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{T}\in\partial\Omega:x_{3}=0,x_{1}\in(0,1)\}.\end{split}

We consider the parametric data

(75)

\begin{split}E^{\mu}(t)&=\mu_{1}e^{\sin{(2\pi t/T)}},\quad\nu^{\mu}(t)=\mu_{2}e^{\sin{(2\pi t/T)}},\quad\underline{g}^{\mu}(\underline{x},t)=\underline{0},\quad u_{0}^{\mu}(\underline{x})=\underline{0},\\
\underline{h}^{\mu}(\underline{x},t)&=\begin{cases}\mu_{3}(1+t)\underline{n}_{1}\qquad\ \ \underline{x}\in\Gamma_{N_{1}},\ \underline{n}_{1}=(1,0,0)^{T};\\
\mu_{4}e^{\sin{(2\pi t/T)}}\underline{n}_{2}\quad\underline{x}\in\Gamma_{N_{2}},\ \underline{n}_{2}=(0,1,0)^{T};\\
\mu_{5}e^{\cos{(2\pi t/T)}}\underline{n}_{3}\quad\underline{x}\in\Gamma_{N_{3}},\ \underline{n}_{3}=(0,0,1)^{T}.\end{cases}\quad\end{split}

The parametric domain is

(76)

\mathcal{D}=\left[10^{10},9\cdot 10^{10}\right]\times\left[0.25,0.42\right]\times\left[-4\cdot 10^{5},4\cdot 10^{5}\right]^{3}.

5.
Conclusions and future work

In this work, we introduce
ttrb
, a projection-based
rom
that leverages the
tt
decomposition of
hf
snapshots as an alternative to conventional
tpod
-based
rb
methods. Through various numerical experiments, we demonstrate that
ttrb
achieves significant offline speedup compared to its
strb
counterpart. This efficiency stems from the “split-axes” representation, which organizes the
dof
associated with
fe
functions into tensors. This structure facilitates the use of tensor rank-reduction techniques, such as the
tt
decomposition, which we show to be computationally more efficient than traditional
tpod
-based approaches. The key innovation of the
ttrb
algorithm lies in its reliance on operations whose complexity scales with the dimension of a single full-order axis, rather than the overall size of the
fom
. Furthermore, the proposed method achieves superior compression in terms of reduction factor compared to traditional algorithms, making it particularly advantageous for approximating
pde
in very high dimensions. From an accuracy perspective, we demonstrate that
ttrb
provides approximation capabilities comparable to those of
strb
, as evidenced by our numerical results.

A promising direction for future work is extending our method to parameterized problems defined on non-Cartesian grids. To this end, we plan to explore unfitted element discretizations that enable the use of the “split-axes” representation of the snapshots. We anticipate no significant theoretical challenges, as the
ttrb
algorithm is largely independent of the discretization or geometry. Additionally, we aim to tackle more complex and practically relevant problems, such as saddle point problems and nonlinear applications.
