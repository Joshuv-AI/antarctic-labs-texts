Second Harmonic Generation by Multilayer Graphenes and its Dependence on Stacking Order and Encapsulation Environment
Patrick Johansen Sarsfield, Takaaki V. Joya, Takuto Kawakami, Mikito Koshino, Vladimir Fal’ko

Abstract
In this work, we analyze the effects of external influences on the second harmonic generation (SHG) signal produced by multilayer graphenes. Specifically, we vary the encapsulation conditions and doping of graphenes, seeking to identify SHG “fingerprints” that are unsensitive to external perturbations. We identify characteristic features in the infrared regime that may be used as a stacking-order fingerprint for the mixed stacking tetralayer and the trilayer polytypes caused by a strong double resonance effect. Further to this, we find that the tetralayers systematically produce an SHG response that is significantly larger as compared to the trilayers. We then studied the SHG signal by the tetralayer polytypes in the terahertz regime and demonstrated the signal is highly sensitive the environmental conditions. Furthermore, we also find that accidental doping of graphene will strongly suppress the SHG signal in this regime. The above findings emphasize the need for a spectral approach to the identification of stacking order by SHG. Thus, measurements at one frequency are insufficient to confidently identify the stacking order due to the environmental sensitivities, since SHG can be readily induced with a comparable magnitude in the centrosymmetric polytypes through external symmetry-breaking.

II
Theoretical treatment of second harmonic generation

Second harmonic generation (SHG) is a second order optical response where a frequency doubling occurs; the frequency of the output current is double the frequency of the input field
\omega
.
Summing over repeated indices, the generic SHG photocurrent can be expressed as

j_{\mu}(2\omega)=\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\,E_{\alpha}(\omega)E_{\beta}(\omega),

(1)

where
j_{\mu}
is the photocurrent in the
\mu
direction,
E_{\alpha}
,
E_{\beta}
are the electric fields of the incoming beams respectively polarized in the
\alpha
,
\beta
directions, and
\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}
is the SHG conductivity tensor.
For the multilayer graphenes,
\mu,\alpha,\beta=x,y
, where
x
and
y
are aligned with the zig-zag and armchair edges, respectively, as shown in Fig.
1
(a).
It is well known that SHG is only finite in crystals with broken inversion symmetry
Kleinman 1962
;
Sipe and Shkrebtii 2000
and it is often used as a probe to confirm the symmetry-breaking of experimental samples.
This can also be checked by inspecting the behavior of both sides of Eq. (
1
) under inversion symmetry; the left-hand side is inversion-odd while the right-hand side is inversion-even, thus, the signal is only finite in noncentrosymmetric crystals.
In a 2D system, not only do we need broken inversion symmetry, but, we also need the in-plane twofold rotational symmetry
C_{2z}
to be absent.
We further note that charge neutral systems with electron-hole symmetry also suppress the SHG response.
This is because the contribution from the electron-like transitions and the hole-like transitions cancel each other
Garcia-Ruiz et al. 2023
.

The conductivity tensor
\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}
can be calculated using the high-order matrix elements obtained through a perturbative approach
Sipe and Shkrebtii 2000
;
Parker et al. 2019
which are in turn used to generalize the linear response theory to NLOR.
In the present paper, we adopt the velocity gauge where the external field is introduced to the Hamiltonian via the minimal-coupling approach.
This method is favorable when the velocity operators are known; here, they can be analytically obtained from the Dirac-type Hamiltonians.
Noting that the Hamiltonian is linear in
{\bf\it k}
and setting
\alpha=\beta
, the SHG conductivity is given as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\alpha}(\omega)=\frac{2e^{3}}{\omega^{2}}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{a,b,c}\frac{v^{\mu}_{ac}v^{\alpha}_{cb}v^{\alpha}_{ba}}{\epsilon_{ba}-\epsilon_{cb}}\;\times

\displaystyle\left(\frac{f_{ab}}{\hbar\omega-\epsilon_{ba}+i\eta}+\frac{f_{bc}}{\hbar\omega-\epsilon_{cb}+i\eta}-\frac{2f_{ac}}{2\hbar\omega-\epsilon_{ca}+2i\eta}\right),

(2)

from the Feynman diagram shown in Fig.
1
(b)
Parker et al. 2019
.
Here,
e
is the elemental charge,
\epsilon_{ba}=\epsilon_{b}-\epsilon_{a}
, where
\epsilon_{a}
is the eigenenergy of the Bloch state
\ket{a}
,
f_{ab}=f(\epsilon_{a})-f(\epsilon_{b})
, where
f(\epsilon)
is the Fermi-Dirac occupation function,
v^{\alpha}=\hbar^{-1}\partial_{\alpha}H
is the velocity operator, where
\partial_{\alpha}
is used as a shorthand for
\partial/\partial k_{\alpha}
.
The broadening parameter
\eta=
1 meV is used, and, in this paper, we focus on the real component of the SHG tensor.
The imaginary parts of
\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\alpha}
are related with its real counterparts through the Kramers-Kronig relation.

Upon inspecting Eq. (
2
), we can break down the signal into two factors: the one-photon and the two-photon absorptions.
The one-photon and two-photon absorption respectively refers to the resonance at
\hbar\omega
and
2\hbar\omega
; the former corresponds to the first two terms while the latter corresponds to the final term inside the brackets.
We note that the two factors contribute with opposite signs to the frequency-doubled SHG signal.
Importantly, when we have three equidistant energy levels, we hit a double resonance, where both the one-photon and two-photon terms are resonant.
To clarify this, we consider three energy levels
\epsilon_{a}<\epsilon_{b}<\epsilon_{c}
, where
\epsilon_{b}-\epsilon_{a}=\epsilon_{c}-\epsilon_{b}=\Delta\epsilon
.
When the Fermi level is set, for example, between
\epsilon_{a}
and
\epsilon_{b}
, we can see that this system is resonant via the one-photon absorption from
\epsilon_{a}
to
\epsilon_{b}
at
\hbar\omega=\Delta\epsilon
and the two-photon absorption from
\epsilon_{a}
to
\epsilon_{c}
at
2\hbar\omega=2\Delta\epsilon
.
The same situation is obtained when the Fermi level is set between
\epsilon_{b}
and
\epsilon_{c}
.
The double resonance is mathematically incorporated through the overall energy denominator
1/\epsilon_{ba}-\epsilon_{cb}
which enhances the signal as we approach the condition, and thus, is an inherently three-band effect.

.

Double resonances can dominate the spectral SHG signal as we will show in the next section.
This type of SHG feature does however pose some computational challenges.
Since the strength of the signal is not derived from a large density of states but rather a precise energy matching condition in the denominators of Eq. (
2
), the effect relies often on transitions in a very small region of momentum space.
As such, an exceptionally fine grid must be chosen in-order to properly resolve the double resonance peaks.
To get around this issue and not deal with prohibitively lengthy computational times, we implement an adaptive grid for every chosen
\omega
.
In other words, we define the relevant regions in momentum space that can contribute to the SHG conductivity by analyzing the energy separation between bands using a corse grid.
We then proceed by applying a fine grid, only in these predetermined regions of momentum space.

Finally, we discuss the independent components of the conductivity tensor in multilayer graphenes.
In the current systems, we have an in-plane threefold rotational symmetry
C_{3z}
and a mirror symmetry across the
y
-
z
plane
M_{x}
, as shown in Fig.
1
(a).
In order to find the independent and finite components of the
\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}
tensor under these symmetries, we can perform a simple symmetry analysis.
We begin by considering some general transformation
T
from coordinates
{\bf\it r}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
to a new set of coordinates
{\bf\it r}=(x,y)
given by
{\bf\it r}^{\prime}=T\,{\bf\it r}
. Under
T
, we can see that Eq. (
1
) transforms as

\displaystyle j_{\mu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}\;E_{\alpha^{\prime}}E_{\beta^{\prime}}

\displaystyle T_{\mu^{\prime}\mu}j_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}\;T_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}E_{\alpha}\;T_{\beta^{\prime}\beta}E_{\beta}

\displaystyle j_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\left(T^{-1}_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}T_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}T_{\beta^{\prime}\beta}\right)E_{\alpha}E_{\beta},

(3)

giving us the following transformation law

\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}=T^{-1}_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}T_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}T_{\beta^{\prime}\beta}.

(4)

For
C_{3z}
, we take
T=R(2\pi/3)
, the in-plane
120^{\circ}
rotation matrix. Then, setting
\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}=\sigma^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}
restricts
\sigma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}
to two independent components

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sigma^{x}_{xx}&=-\sigma^{y}_{xy}=-\sigma^{y}_{yx}=-\sigma^{x}_{yy},\\
\sigma^{y}_{yy}&=-\sigma^{x}_{xy}=-\sigma^{x}_{yx}=-\sigma^{y}_{xx}.\end{split}

(5)

By further applying
M_{x}
, where
T=\rm{diag}(-1,1)
, we find that
\sigma^{x}_{xx}=0
, thus, the only non-vanishing component of the SHG conductivity tensor is
\sigma^{y}_{yy}
.

In this section we will analyze the SHG signal produced by the inversion symmetry broken tetralayer
Wirth et al. 2022
(mixed stacking ABCB) in the infrared frequency regime and the terahertz frequency regime. We will make comparisons to the inversion symmetric tetralayers too by externally inducing symmetry breaking in the system. Throughout this section, we seek to identify signals in the second order conductivity that are not easily perturbed by the sample’s environment, but rather act as a “fingerprint” of the stacking order and layer number irrespective of encapsulation or substrate effects. To perform this analysis, we apply on-layer proximity-induced potentials to the topmost (
\Delta_{t}
), and or, bottommost (
\Delta_{b}
) graphene layer in the stack to represent the presence of an unknown substrate or encapsulating layer such as Silicon Nitride or hBN. It is estimated that

We begin by considering the mixed stacking tetralayer graphene in the infrared regime. There is only one tetralayer which intrinsically breaks inversion symmetry: the mixed stacking polytype. In the left hand column in Fig.
3
we show the second order conductivity for the mixed tetralayer (ABCB), for a number of different proximity induced potentials arranged with both symmetric and asymmetric placement indicated in the table. These spectra are entirely dominated by strong double resonant features. These features take the shape of “winged” Lorentzians which are formed when two Lorentzian curves with opposite signs are summed. This line shape is characteristic of double resonances as described in the previous section. This line shape may be seen more clearly in the zoomed in narrow energy range spectra at the bottom of Fig.
3
.

We highlight in blue, two regions where the position of peaks has little to no sensitivity to the environmental perturbations. These peaks are derived from both transitions between larger momentum states away from the band edges as well as band edge transitions, indicated by arrows on the band structure and correspondingly labeled above the spectra in the figure. These double resonant features are prone to vary significantly in magnitude and line shape since small perturbations to the bands can cause subtle de-tuning of the three bands involved; for example, when
\epsilon_{ba}
and
\epsilon_{cb}
in Eq. (
2
) are similar but not perfectly equal. Crucially however, the position of the feature in energy is largely unaffected, thus offering a reliable fingerprint for identification of the mixed tetralayer. Again this is highlighted in the exemplary zoomed in narrow energy range spectra at the bottom of Fig.
3
.

Now, we turn our attention to the two trilayer graphene polytypes, Bernal (ABA) and Rhombohedral (ABC), for comparison. In Fig.
3
, we briefly take a moment to highlight the need for electron-hole symmetry breaking to produce an SHG signal. We show an exemplary case for the ABA trilayer using a simplified model, applying only
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}

For the other ABA spectra in the middle column of panels in Fig.
3
, we restore all the coupling terms in our model and proceed with the analysis of SHG for differing encapsulation proximity potentials. Highlighted regions in blue indicate clusters of features whose position in energy does not vary significantly with the environmental perturbations. The left hand blue box includes two shoulder-like peaks which grow in magnitude for larger proximity potentials. The origin of these shoulders can be understood by inspection of the band structure, also shown in Fig.
3
. The position at which these shoulders occur is at
\omega=\epsilon_{-3,+1}/2
and
\omega=\epsilon_{-1,+3}/2
, setting
\hbar=1
. This is the signal from the two-photon resonance given by the final term in Eq. (
2
), which allows for a signal at a frequency equal to half the size of the gap. The line shape of these shoulders are a direct result of the joint density of states between the bands involved in these transitions.

The second highlighted blue box includes a set of features with a double resonance line shape, as described above. These originate from the
\omega=\epsilon_{-3,+1}
,
\omega=\epsilon_{-1,+3}
and
\omega=\epsilon_{-3,+3}/2
transitions as labeled in the figure. Again, although these features vary in size and shape as we change the external environment due to the subtle de-tuning of bands, their position in energy remains approximately the same, offering a reliable spectral indicator of the stacking.

In the right hand column of panels we show the SHG response for ABC graphene. In contrast to ABA stacking, ABC does not intrinsically break inversion symmetry and therefore will not produce an SHG signal on its own, which is shown explicitly in the
\Delta_{b}=0
,
\Delta_{t}=0
case which preserves the inversion symmetry. We can, however, induce an SHG signal by breaking the inversion symmetry externally, inducing a potential of differing size on the top and bottom layers. This would represent a case where the sample is not encapsulated symmetrically but rather is placed on a substrate exposed to vacuum. Much like in the previous cases, we identify two regions highlighted in blue where the peak position of features do not vary with the external perturbation. The nature of these features are much like in the ABA case with shoulder like features as well as a cluster of double resonant transitions which can be understood by analyzing the corresponding band structure and considering the labeled band transitions indicated by the arrows.

Overall, we find that the SHG signal produced by the ABA stack with intrinsic inversion symmetry breaking and the ABC stack with externally induced symmetry breaking are of comparable size. For this reason, one must be careful when analyzing SHG of samples whose symmetry is broken by the environment, as equally strong signals may be observed in both cases depending on the probing photon frequency. This highlights the need to take a spectral approach to SHG measurements and not simply relying on a single frequency.

We do however notice that the signal produced by the mixed tetralayer is approximately an order of magnitude greater in size as compared to the trilayers. This result is consistent, although some what accidental. This is because, for the ABCB stack, many relevant transitions occur between high momentum states where there is better energy-matching between states for the double resonances and therefore less de-tuned transitions. In other words the
\epsilon_{ba}-\epsilon_{cb}
denominator in Eq. (
2
) is generally smaller and thus the conductivity is enhanced.

Finally, for completeness, in the bottom left corner of Fig.
3
, we show characteristic SHG spectra for the intrinsically inversion-symmetric tetralayers subjected to external symmetry-breaking on-layer potentials. The ABCA case displays a strong double resonant response at approximately 400 meV, similar to the ABCB spectra. This is however the only stong SHG response from the ABCA stack in the infrared regime. The ABAB stack generally displays very little SHG double resonance in the infrared reigime. In general we see that the mixed tetralayer (compared to the other multilayer polytypes) is most prone to double resonance due to a greater number of band energy difference matching cases described above.

SHG by tetralayer graphene polytypes in the low-energy terahertz frequency range

Here, we show some exemplary

Now we consider the low energy terahertz range of photon frequency for the tetralayers. In Fig.
4
, we observe a mixture of shoulder-like and double resonant like features for the ABCB mixed stacking tetralayer (results in the left hand column). These are all derived from transitions in the low-energy part of the band structure (example case shown). Since the low energy band edges are highly modified by the proximity induced potentials, it is unsurprising that the observed result will be dramatically influenced by the environment of the graphene. We do however, make an observation here that is consistent across all the studied polytypes in this work. That is, doping will always suppress the low energy SHG features. We show an example of this for the
\Delta_{b}=18
meV,
\Delta_{t}=75
meV case where the black line is calculated at change neutrality, the orange line is for
5\times 10^{11}
cm
-2

2\times 10^{12}
cm
-2
doping (marked on the corresponding band structure), at which point the SHG is almost totally suppressed. This is simply due to that fact that with sufficient electron or hole doping, all the relevant low energy transitions are totally blocked. The same is however not true for the higher energy transition discussed in Fig.
3
, since many of the double resonant features we observe originate from transitions between states at larger momentum away from the band edges, which would require significantly larger doping to suppress.

Finally, we consider the remaining inversion-symmetric tetralayer polytypes. Isolated, these would not produce an SHG signal. For this reason, again, we apply external symmetry breaking proximity induced potentials so that we can force an SHG response. We notice that, unsurprisingly, the ABCA stack features no double resonant transitions. This is because there are only two low-energy bands present, removing all possibility of double resonance. In contrast the ABAB stack shows many sporadic sharp features owing to the four low energy bands and the highly variable energy separations between them. Overall, we can conclude that if the multilayer graphene is in an asymmetric environment, such as, placed on a substrate, with even a small amount of externally induced inversion-symmetry breaking, one cannot reliable use the mere presence of an SHG signal to conclude that their sample is the ABCB mixed stack. This is because, as we show in Fig.
4
, equally strong signals may be present in the ABAB and the ABCA stacks depending on the chosen incident probing photon energy.

IV
Discussion and Conclusions

We performed a systematic analysis of the second harmonic generation (SHG) produced by tetralayer and trilayer graphene polytypes implementing a perturbative approach to the nonlinear optical response.
We identified SHG conductivity peaks in the infrared regime that are robust against external changes in the graphenes environment, such as a substrate perturbation or encapsulation of the sample, arising from the double resonance effect.

We further demonstrated that the tetralayers generate an SHG response that is approximately an order of magnitude stronger compared to the trilayers, which can be understood from the energy-matching condition that is better fulfilled in the former than the latter.
In future work, we will explore this idea further by tuning the frequency offset
\delta\omega
of two input frequencies
\omega\pm\delta\omega
to maximize the energy-matching condition in de-tuned bands with a view to further enhance the magnitude of SHG produced by multilayer graphenes.
Correspondingly, we will also analyze two-photon generation, the reverse process of SHG, where the input photon with frequency
2\omega
is decomposed into two photons with frequencies
\omega\pm\delta\omega
to probe the relative energy spacing of bands in graphitic samples.

Finally, we studied the SHG response in the low-energy terahertz regime for all tetralayer poltypes.
We showed that the response is highly sensitive to the encapsulation environment since the low-energy band structure is greatly modified.
Furthermore, accidental doping of graphene can strongly suppress the terahertz SHG signal since the relevant transitions in this energy range can be easily blocked by moderate doping.
We emphasize that a spectral approach is crucial in the identification of graphene stacking orders as signals of comparable size could be generated in centrosymmetric polytypes with externally broken symmetry by asymmetric encapsulation.
