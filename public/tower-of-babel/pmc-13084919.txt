Occupational exposure to toxic particles and risk of pulmonary sarcoidosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Nada Kotti, Amel Kchaou, Walid Feki, Asma Daoud, Feriel Dhouib, Nehla Rmadi, Wiem Feki, Mouna Snoussi, Rim Kammoun, Faten Omrane, Slim Charfi, Nadia Moussa, Rym Sahnoun, Mounira Hajjaji, Kaouthar Jmal Hammami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe onset of sarcoidosis is likely the result of a complex genetic-environment-immunological interaction. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to identify occupational toxic particles associated with an increased risk of developing pulmonary sarcoidosis.MethodsPublications in English, published from January 2000 to January 2025, were searched in PubMed, Scopus and JSTOR. The risk of bias was assessed for all included studies. Sensitivity analyses stratified by study quality were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings and potential bias. To assess publication bias, a funnel plot was used when more than six studies were included in the analysis.ResultsFive categories of toxic particles were identified to be associated with increased risk of the onset of sarcoidosis: chemicals, inorganic dusts, metals, mixed dusts and fumes and organic dusts. The quantitative analysis includes data from 13 studies. The results suggested that occupational silica, pesticides, mould/mildew and World Trade Center ((WTC) dust exposures were associated with increased odds of pulmonary sarcoidosis. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the association for WTC dust and mould, whereas the risk associated with silica appeared attenuated in high-quality studies. However, gold exposure was identified as a protective factor.ConclusionPulmonary sarcoidosis is associated with occupational silica, pesticides, WTC dust and mould. Future research should prioritise gene-environment interactions and granuloma mineralogy to refine preventive strategies and disease management.Keywords: Occupational Lung Disease, Sarcoidosis, Systemic disease and lungs

Introduction

Sarcoidosis is still regarded as a disease of unknown aetiology.1 2 Over the past 20 years, this paradigm has gradually shifted, with more rapid progress during the last decade. It is a multisystem chronic inflammatory disease that affects multiple organs in the body, including lymph nodes, eyes, skin and, most commonly, lungs.3 The American Thoracic Society has established three criteria for diagnosing sarcoidosis: (1) characteristic clinical and radiological presentation; (2) evidence of non-caseating granulomas in one or more tissue samples; and (3) exclusion of alternative causes of granulomatous disease.1 Worldwide prevalence varies significantly across different regions, reflecting diverse epidemiological factors. It was 2–80 per 100 000 people, and incidence is 1.0–35.3 per 100 000 people per year.4

As the lungs, skin and eyes directly contact the environment, external factors crucially influence sarcoidosis phenotype.5 While specific exposure links remain debated,6 the disease likely results from gene-environment interactions7 driving a TH1-predominant immunopathology with potential TH2/TH17 shifts.8 Under normal circumstances, antigen clearance leads to self-regulation and resolution of the immune response.9 While genetic-environmental interaction is crucial in understanding sarcoidosis, some researchers argue that the role of specific pathogens or environmental exposures may be overstated, suggesting that further research is needed to clarify these relationships.8 10 Online supplemental Figure S1 illustrates the wide range of factors that have been proposed to influence the aetiology of sarcoidosis.

Recent epidemiological studies have provided evidence suggesting causal associations between occupational exposure to specific agents and sarcoidosis.2 Pathogenesis has been more clearly defined, including the role of gene-exposure interactions. Certain occupations such as firefighters11 and military service12 have been associated with an increased risk of sarcoidosis. Occupational exposure to toxic particles like silica,13 metal dust14 and pesticides,15 among others, has been associated with an increased risk of developing granulomatous lung disease. Blanc et al reported that the proportion of sarcoidosis attributable to occupational exposure ranged from 0% to 54%, with an overall attributable fraction of 30% (95% CI 17% to 45%).16

Evidence linking occupational exposure to sarcoidosis remains inconsistent. While Huntley et al recently identified risks regarding silica, pesticides and mould,7 this study extends those findings through updated literature and more granular exposure classification. We aimed to identify specific toxic particles associated with pulmonary sarcoidosis risk through a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Methods

This study was designed and reported in accordance with the guidelines of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)17 and has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews PROSPERO databases under the identification number: CRD420251030872.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search in English was performed from January 2000 to January 2025 was conducted in these databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus and JSTOR. Additional studies were also identified using the references of the reviewed publications. We included studies published from January 2000 onwards to improve methodological consistency, reflecting more standardised sarcoidosis diagnostic criteria and advances in occupational exposure assessment. Older studies often used less precise exposure classification or outdated diagnostic definitions, which could increase heterogeneity and reduce comparability.

The search strategy was built using Medical Subject Headings terms and keywords related to pulmonary sarcoidosis and occupational exposure.

The following bibliographic query was used: (“Sarcoidosis” OR “Sarcoid-like” OR “Pulmonary sarcoidosis”) AND (“Occupational exposure” OR “Workplace exposure” OR “Occupational hazard” OR “Work related exposure” OR “Occupational disease”). Keywords relevant to the study topic were searched in the “All Fields” category in each database to ensure comprehensive retrieval.

The retrieval procedure was conducted by two independent researchers.

Eligibility criteria

Only cohort and case–control studies were included to ensure methodological robustness and appropriate assessment of exposure-outcome relationships. Cross-sectional studies and case series were excluded due to their limitations in establishing temporality. Online supplemental Table S1 summarises the inclusion and exclusion criteria according to the PICOS framework (ie, populations/participants, interventions and comparators, outcomes of interest and study designs/types).

Exclusion criteria included experimental studies, case reports, reviews, cross-sectional studies and ecological studies, studies using non-standardised diagnostic methods or having a high risk of assessment inaccuracies, and studies with inadequate data for meta-analysis, such as insufficient information on relative risk and CIs.

Data collection

Data were extracted independently by two authors (AK and NK) with any discrepancies resolved through consultation and discussion with authors FD and WF.

Studies were categorised based on the reported occupational exposure to toxic particles and study design. If a study included multiple exposures, all were considered. Demographic information, including gender, age and ethnicity, as well as the diagnostic criteria for sarcoidosis, was collected. The OR for specific occupational toxic exposures was extracted or calculated using available data for case–control and cohort studies.

Preference for fully adjusted risk estimates: The most thoroughly adjusted risk estimates in each article were preferred over less adjusted or crude estimates.

Quality analysis

The quality of case–control and cohort studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Studies with a score of more than seven were classified as high-quality research, those with a score of between four and six were classified as medium-quality research, and studies with a score of three or less were classified as low-quality research (online supplemental Tables S2 and S3). To assess the robustness of our findings and evaluate the influence of study quality on the pooled risk estimates, we performed sensitivity analyses stratifying the studies by quality level (high-quality vs medium-quality).

Statistical analysis

The compiled data classifies all studies according to reported occupational exposures. A meta-analysis was performed when two or more studies reported an OR for a specific occupational exposure. Data included the frequency of pulmonary sarcoidosis cases linked to occupational exposure to toxic particles, as well as the prevalence of occupational exposures in pulmonary sarcoidosis cases. In addition, studies using mineralogical analysis techniques on sarcoidosis are also described.

The meta-analysis was carried out using RevMan V.5.4.1 software. A 95% CI and relative risk were chosen as the statistics to assess effect. The significance level was set at 0.05. Inter-study heterogeneity was estimated using the I² test. When I² ≤50%, this was interpreted as a sign of homogeneity, and a fixed-effects model was used for analysis. When I² ≥50%, heterogeneity was considered significant, and a random effects model was used.

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was performed using the ‘leave-one-out’ method, whereby each study was individually excluded in order to assess the impact of each study on the results. This procedure was used to test the robustness of the results by comparing the effects before and after the elimination of a study.

To assess publication bias, a funnel plot was used when more than six studies were included in the analysis.

Results

A total of 21 studies meeting the research criteria were selected. Among them, 13 studies were included in the quantitative analysis (online supplemental Figure S2).

Qualitative studies

The compendium of occupational exposure to toxic particles associated with pulmonary sarcoidosis and the summary of each individual study included in the compendium are presented in online supplemental tables S4 and S5.

Five categories of occupational toxic particles were identified to be associated with pulmonary sarcoidosis: chemical agents (5 studies), inorganic dusts (10 studies), metals (10 studies), mixed dust/fumes (6 studies) and organic dusts (5 studies).

Research most often focused on the following toxic particles: silica (8 studies), aluminium (5 studies), chromium (5 studies), cobalt (3 studies), gold (3 studies), nickel (3 studies), pesticides (4 studies), World Trade Center (WTC) dusts (3 studies) and vegetable dust (3 studies).

Silica exposure showed a modest primary association with pulmonary sarcoidosis (OR 1.12; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.23; I2=44%; Figure 1 and online supplemental Figure S1-S4). However, sensitivity analysis revealed significant subgroup heterogeneity (p=0.007): risk was elevated in medium-quality studies (OR 1.26; 95% CI 1.13 to 1.42) but attenuated and non-significant in high-quality studies (OR 1.05; 95% CI 0.99 to 1.13) (online supplemental Figure S3).

Similarly, occupational exposure to pesticides (OR 1.44; 95% CI 1.21 to 1.72, I2=30 %) (figure 2). The sensitivity analysis revealed that the overall significant association was largely driven by the high-quality study (Newman et al15). While the pooled estimate for medium-quality studies did not reach statistical significance (OR 1.21; 95% CI 0.91 to 1.60), the high-quality evidence showed a strong and significant association (OR 1.62; 95% CI 1.29 to 2.03), contributing 57.4% of the weight to the overall analysis (online supplemental Figure S5).

Furthermore, occupational exposure to mould or mildew was significantly associated with the development of pulmonary sarcoidosis (OR 1.59; 95% CI 1.33 to 1.90), showing perfect consistency across studies (I²=0%) (figure 3 and online supplemental Figure S6). Importantly, risk estimates for mould exposure were highly consistent across study quality levels (high-quality OR 1.62 vs medium-quality OR 1.54), reinforcing the validity of this finding. Conversely, results for vegetable and animal dusts exhibited significant heterogeneity (I2 >90%), largely attributable to differences between older case–control studies and recent cohort data.

Forest plot of studies examining the relation between occupational organic dust exposures and diagnosis of pulmonary sarcoidosis (random effect).

The risk of developing pulmonary sarcoidosis was not significantly associated with occupational exposure to aluminium (OR 1.23; 95% CI 0.90 to 1.67, I2=37 %), chromium (OR 1.16; 95% CI 0.67 to 1.99, I2=33 %) or nickel (OR 1.06; 95% CI 0.61 to 1.82, I2=9%). Furthermore, occupational exposure to gold was associated with a significant reduction in the risk of pulmonary sarcoidosis (OR 0.36; 95% CI 0.15 to 0.90, I2=21 %) (online supplemental Figure S7 and S8). Sensitivity analysis for metal exposures confirmed the stability of the results across quality levels. Lower-quality studies did not skew the overall neutral effect (pooled OR 1.07), as their CIs consistently overlapped the null. Notably, the significant protective association observed for ‘Gold’ exposure was derived from high-quality evidence (Newman et al15), reinforcing the reliability of this specific subgroup finding.

WTC dust exposure was strongly associated with sarcoidosis (OR 2.75; 95% CI 2.02 to 3.75; I2=0%; online supplemental Figure S9). Sensitivity analysis showed this effect was driven entirely by high-quality studies (OR 2.75) compared with medium-quality data (OR 0.96), with a significant subgroup difference (p=0.03) and zero heterogeneity among high-quality cohorts (online supplemental Figure S10).

Evaluation of the mineralogical composition

Four case–control studies presented mineralogical analyses of tissue biopsies from patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis (online supplemental Table S6).

Using TEM (transmission electron microscopy)/EDX (energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy) analysis of BAL (bronchoalveolar lavage), Catinon et al18 found significantly higher steel particle loads (p=0.029) and building activity exposure (p=0.018) in patients with sarcoidosis compared with controls, despite similar total inorganic burdens.

Corradi et al19 analysed trace elements in exhaled breath condensate using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry in patients with sarcoidosis, non-specific interstitial pneumonia and idiopathic interstitial pneumonia. While distinguishing specific pathologies proved difficult, a multi-element pattern (Si, Ni, Zn, SE, Cu, Co) successfully discriminated patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) from healthy controls (sensitivity 96.4%, specificity 90.9%).

Verma20 analysed lung tissue using atomic absorption spectrophotometry. Despite higher beryllium levels in a CBD (chronic beryllium disease) case, significant overlap with sarcoidosis and controls prevented reliable differentiation-based solely on tissue analysis.

Using neutron activation analysis on lung tissue, Denisova et al21 found significantly elevated calcium, iron, chromium and cobalt levels in patients with sarcoidosis versus controls. Strong elemental correlations (eg, Fe-Cr) supported an environmental heavy metal aetiology.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis identified occupational exposures to silica, mould/mildew, pesticides and WTC dust as being associated with an increased risk of developing pulmonary sarcoidosis. In contrast, occupational exposure to gold was associated with a reduced risk of pulmonary sarcoidosis.

Evidence remains inconclusive regarding the potential causal relationship between exposure to vegetable dust, animal dust and certain metals, including aluminium, chromium and nickel.

Occupational toxic particles significantly contribute to sarcoidosis onset and progression. Inhalation triggers an exaggerated immune response, where alveolar macrophage phagocytosis drives pro-inflammatory cytokine release, lymphocyte recruitment and subsequent granuloma formation.22

Pesticides

Pesticides, known to trigger autoimmun e pathologies sharing pathways with sarcoidosis,23,25 were confirmed as a significant risk factor in our analysis. Crucially, our results substantiate these epidemiological observations. Regarding pesticide exposure, our sensitivity analysis reinforces the validity of the association. Unlike silica, where higher quality studies showed attenuated risks, the link between pesticides and sarcoidosis was most pronounced in the highest quality study included (Newman et al15). This suggests that the association is not an artefact of bias in lower-quality studies but rather a robust finding supported by rigorous methodology.

Biologically, pesticides impair alveolar epithelium, driving inflammation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, a hallmark of sarcoidosis.26 Furthermore, interactions with susceptibility genes like BTNL2 likely modulate this risk.27

Silica

There is growing evidence linking occupational exposure to respirable crystalline silica (RCS) with an increased incidence of sarcoidosis, placing workers in mining, construction and iron-steel industries at higher risk.28 Biologically, silica is a plausible trigger as its inhalation can induce an immune response leading to the formation of non-caseating granulomas—the hallmark of sarcoidosis—in genetically predisposed individuals.29,32 Consistent with previous reviews,2 33 34 our primary analysis identifies RCS as a compelling occupational risk factor. However, our sensitivity analysis adds a crucial nuance regarding the magnitude of this risk: stratification by quality suggests that medium-quality studies may overestimate the association due to potential recall bias. High-quality cohorts (eg, Iversen et al,35 Jonsson et al36) which employ rigorous exposure assessment demonstrate a persistent but more modest association.

Metals

In our review, equipoise persists for aluminium, nickel and general metal dust exposure with sarcoidosis. Metals possess properties that stimulate antigen-specific cellular immune responses, which have potential to induce similar clinical and pathological conditions to sarcoidosis.37 Importantly, our sensitivity analysis indicates that this lack of association is robust and not due to the dilution of effects by lower-quality studies. The consistency of findings across varying quality scores suggests that current evidence does not support a major role for broad metal exposure in sarcoidosis aetiology, except for specific isolated findings (eg, gold) that warrant further high-quality investigation.

Exposure to metals can lead to immunological sensitisation, resulting in the activation of CD4+T cells and the release of inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-12, interferon-γ and tumour necrosis factor-α, which promote granuloma formation.32 38

Aluminium, copper, titanium and zirconium have all been associated with formation of non-caseating granuloma in the lungs.39 Iron, copper, cadmium, chromium and nickel possess the ability to produce reactive radicals, most notably ROS.40

Immunoreactivity to metals such as aluminium, beryllium and zirconium was significantly higher in patients with sarcoidosis compared with controls, suggesting these antigens may be involved in the pathogenesis of a distinct subgroup of patients with sarcoidosis.41

The lymphocyte proliferation test, used in diagnosing CBD, is suggested for identifying patients with sarcoidosis with inorganic antigenic triggers.29

Metals like tungsten and cobalt have been found in lung tissues of patients with ILD, indicating their role in forming fibrotic lesions through macrophage phagocytosis and interaction with cytotoxic T lymphocytes.42

Mineralogical detection of relevant elements within granulomas supports a causal role for inhaled particles.18 21 Conversely, we observed an unexpected inverse association with gold exposure. While biologically plausible given gold’s immunomodulatory history,43 this finding relies on limited data (n=2) and may reflect bias. It should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Organic dusts

Organic dusts, such as those from vegetable and animal sources, contain a variety of microbial and non-microbial antigens that can trigger an immune response in genetically susceptible individuals.44 Among these exposures, the specific link between mould/mildew exposure and sarcoidosis appears particularly robust. Unlike other exposures where study quality influenced the magnitude of risk, the association with mould remained stable and significant regardless of the study’s quality score or design. This consistency across different cohorts and methodologies (Kucera et al; Newman et al) strongly supports an aetiological role for fungal antigens in sarcoidosis.

Agricultural organic dust exposure correlates with specific phenotypes45 and a 1.5-fold increased risk.15 Similarly, mould and ‘musty’ environments are significant triggers. Mechanistically, microbial cell walls likely induce granulomatous hypersensitivity in susceptible individuals,46 47 a hypothesis reinforced by sarcoidosis clusters in water-damaged buildings.48

The strongest occupational association is in agricultural workers (farmworkers, raising birds, cotton ginning, exposure to organic/vegetable dust or insecticides) and workers in industries related to organic dust exposure, rubber factories, gardening materials or pesticide-using industries.1549,51 The long list of potential toxins in agricultural work includes chemicals and aerosolised particulates such as grains, bedding materials, silicates, animal proteins, insect proteins, fungi, bacteria, mycotoxins, endotoxins and, especially, pesticides.

Mixed dusts and fumes

Studies suggest that exposure to mixed dusts and fumes, such as those encountered by military personnel and firefighters, may increase the risk of developing sarcoidosis.47 The presence of inorganic particles, such as those from building activities, further supports the hypothesis that airborne inorganic dust may contribute to sarcoidosis.18

New York firefighters exposed to the complex WTC dust cloud (cement, silica, asbestos, metals and combustion by-products) showed significantly elevated sarcoidosis incidence.52 Our quality-stratified analysis provides statistical confirmation of this observation. The strong risk observed (OR 2.75) is derived exclusively from high-quality studies involving large cohorts of firefighters and rescue workers (Izbicki et al; Weber et al). The perfect consistency (I2=0%) between these high-quality studies reinforces the conclusion that intense exposure to mixed dusts at the WTC site is a definitive trigger for sarcoidosis. In contrast, the lack of association in the lower-quality study is likely attributable to limited statistical power or exposure misclassification.

Beyond the exposure itself, a significant link has been observed between the development of sarcoidosis after exposure to the WTC dust and certain variants of the HLA-DQB1 gene. Although the WTC event highlighted the influence of environmental factors in triggering sarcoidosis, the precise biological mechanisms and the exact causative exposures remain unclear.53

Mineralogical studies

Mineralogical studies provide a biological link between occupational exposures and sarcoidosis. Inorganic particles and metals—including silica, aluminium, titanium, nickel, iron, zinc and steel-related particles—have been identified in granulomas or respiratory samples of sarcoidosis patients, reflecting exposures typical of high-risk occupations such as construction, mining, metalworking and firefighting.

Catinon et al reported significantly higher levels of steel particles in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from patients with sarcoidosis, together with increased construction-related occupational exposure, supporting our meta-analytical findings linking building-related dusts and silica exposure to sarcoidosis risk.18

Similarly, the detection of silica and metals such as nickel, chromium, cobalt and iron in lung tissues aligns with epidemiological evidence of increased sarcoidosis risk among workers exposed to respirable crystalline silica and metal dusts. The presence of these particles within granulomas supports the hypothesis that persistent inhaled inorganic antigens may trigger granulomatous inflammation in genetically susceptible individuals.18 54

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of the recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Huntley et al, which reported increased risks of sarcoidosis associated with occupational exposure to silica, pesticides and mould/mildew.40 While our results are largely concordant with these key associations, important methodological differences distinguish the two studies and justify the added value of the present analysis.

In contrast to Huntley et al, who primarily analysed broader occupational exposure categories, our study employed a more detailed exposure-specific approach and conducted separate meta-analyses for individual agents, including metals and mixed dusts such as WTC dust. This allowed us to highlight areas of equipoise for certain metals, as well as to identify a potentially inverse association with gold exposure, a finding not previously reported.

Furthermore, our review incorporated additional studies published after the search period of Huntley et al and explicitly integrated mineralogical and mechanistic evidence to strengthen biological plausibility. Taken together, these methodological differences support the complementary nature of our findings and reinforce the robustness of the associations observed across independent analyses.

Strengths, limitations and implications of this review

This meta-analysis represents one of the earliest comprehensive efforts to systematically examine the link between occupational exposure to toxic agents and the development of sarcoidosis. Conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, the review followed a rigorous and transparent methodology for study selection, screening and data reporting. Moreover, the inclusion of a wide spectrum of occupational exposures including chemicals, inorganic dusts, metals and organic particles provides a broad and integrative perspective on potential environmental contributors to the disease. The evidence compiled in this review is intended to guide and support future research into environmental factors involved in sarcoidosis, given the ongoing clinical uncertainty surrounding its aetiology.

Our meta-analysis has several limitations. First, most studies relied on indirect exposure assessments (eg, job-exposure matrices (JEMs), retrospective reviews). The resulting heterogeneity in exposure definitions and potential misclassification may bias estimates. Consequently, design-specific subgroup analyses were feasible only for silica.

Second, the diversity of exposure types and the lack of granular data on dose, duration and latency prevented detailed dose-response analyses. Findings may also be influenced by the healthy worker effect, diagnostic variability and the inherent limitations of observational designs in establishing causality. Furthermore, due to the small number of studies (<10 per subgroup), funnel plots offered limited power to rule out publication bias.

Third, excluding databases like Embase and the geographical concentration of studies may limit generalisability and introduce selection bias regarding specific cohorts.

Despite these limitations, we highlight the crucial role of occupational history. Since specific exposures are linked to severe phenotypes and increased mortality,55 identifying these environmental triggers is essential for tailoring clinical management strategies.

The meta-analysis highlights the need for improved exposure assessment and longitudinal studies, offering valuable guidance for future research.

Although several pooled ORs observed in this meta-analysis were modest (eg, an OR of approximately 1.12 for silica exposure), this should not be interpreted as indicating limited public health relevance. At the individual level, such effect sizes may have limited implications for clinical risk prediction. However, from a population perspective, even small increases in relative risk can translate into a meaningful number of attributable cases when the exposure is highly prevalent.

Occupational exposures such as silica and metal fumes remain widespread globally. As a result, the population-attributable risk associated with these exposures may be substantial despite modest individual-level effect estimates. This distinction between individual-level clinical relevance and population-level impact is central to occupational and environmental epidemiology, where prevention and exposure reduction are key objectives. Consequently, small effect sizes, when applied to large exposed populations, may still support preventive strategies and regulatory measures aimed at reducing the overall burden of respiratory disease.

Conclusion

The onset of pulmonary sarcoidosis was associated with occupational silica, pesticides, mould or mildew and WTC dust exposure. Equipoise persists regarding the potential causal relationship between exposure to vegetable dust, animal dust and certain metals, including aluminium, chromium and nickel. However, gold exposure was identified as a protective factor.

The onset is far more likely the result of a complex genetic-environment-immunological interaction. Future studies should examine the potentially complex relationship between genetic factors and airborne occupational exposures and the mineralogical composition of sarcoidosis granulomas. Understanding these environmental triggers is crucial for developing preventive strategies and improving disease management.
