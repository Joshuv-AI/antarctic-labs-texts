Single-nucleus analysis reveals human-specific oligodendrocyte polarization and conserved neuronal responses after severe traumatic brain injury
Authors: Jiatong Ji, Honglu Chao, Chengzhi Chen, Xinyue Wang, Wenqian Shi, Jun Liao, Hai Qian, Hao Shi, Jingming Hu, Yangfan Ye, Yiming Tu, Xiao Xu, Zheng Li, Zhen Yue, Wei Yan, Xunning Hong, Huibo Wang, Huimei Chen, Enrico Petretto, Jing Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe traumatic brain injury (sTBI) is a leading cause of edema and neurological dysfunction. However, the molecular mechanisms driving injury progression remain poorly understood. Here, we present a single-nucleus transcriptomic atlas of human and male mouse cortex across multiple sTBI stages, providing a systematic landscape of dynamic, cell-type-specific responses to injury and edema. Cross-species analyses reveal both conserved and human-specific programs, including a polarized oligodendrocyte state absent in murine sTBI. This state shows increased ABCA1 and adhesion-related genes and is associated with edema progression. In vitro experiments support an ABCA1-linked role in lipid handling that may support oligodendrocyte-lineage cell function, reflected by the maintenance of myelin-associated marker expression. Analyses combining ligand–receptor interactions, co-expression, and perturbation experiments link microglial CALM2–HMGB1 signaling to oligodendrocyte ABCA1-associated lipid regulation during injury progression. These human-specific programs indicate that rodent models may not fully recapitulate human sTBI mechanisms, underscoring the need for caution when extrapolating cross species findings.

Introduction

Severe traumatic brain injury (sTBI), caused by external mechanical forces to the head, is a leading cause of death and long-term disability among adults all over the world1. Damage to the frontal lobe (FL) and temporal lobe (TL) often results in coma and impaired consciousness, severely impacting cognitive, emotional, and motor functions2. Moreover, survivors of sTBI face a significantly increased risk of developing major depressive disorder (MDD), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and other psychiatric or neurodegenerative conditions later in life3,4.

In sTBI, multiple cell types contribute to tissue repair and functional remodeling in a phase-dependent manner3. Activated microglia amplify post-injury inflammation through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, contributing to blood–brain barrier disruption and secondary neuronal injury5,6. In response, neurons engage antioxidant and calcium-regulatory mechanisms to mitigate oxidative stress7,8. Oligodendrocytes and astrocytes further support tissue repair and cellular protection through lipid metabolism, including cholesterol synthesis and transport9,10. In addition, cerebral edema is one of the most severe secondary complications of sTBI, with widespread effects on intracranial pressure, cerebral perfusion, and neurological recovery11,12. Persistent edema in the chronic phase causes white matter degeneration, synaptic dysfunction, and neurotransmitter imbalances in the brain13,14, thereby contributing to cognitive impairments as well as an increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases4,15–17. Therefore, understanding the dynamic molecular pathology of sTBI- and edema-associated signatures is critical for developing targeted interventions to improve clinical outcomes and prevent the progression of long-term neurodegenerative disorders.

Pathophysiological changes in sTBI evolve over time, reflecting a dynamic and heterogeneous process. While sharp temporal boundaries are difficult to define in practice, a commonly used phased model—hyperacute (< 8 h), acute (< 24 h), subacute (1 day to 3 weeks), and chronic (> 3 weeks)—provides a useful conceptual framework for investigating the temporal molecular events18. Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) have rapidly advanced TBI research by resolving injury responses into cell-type-specific programs across time. Early murine studies focused on mild or concussive TBI, demonstrating that subtle injury elicits distinct transcriptional alterations across neuronal and glial compartments19. Subsequent work expanded toward target discovery, including candidate therapeutic signals such as humanin, highlighting astrocytes as central coordinators of multicellular responses20. Single-cell transcriptomic studies on sTBI, which is characterized by extensive tissue damage and secondary injury, often emphasize longer post-injury windows or link to secondary clinical processes. For example, emerging snRNA-seq studies of human acute sTBI have begun to capture clinically relevant sTBI programs, revealing regulatory features that are not readily inferred from mouse models, such as oligodendroglial activation of endogenous retroviral elements21. More recent analyses emphasize intercellular communication and metabolic rewiring during subacute injury evolution, alongside the development of large-scale murine atlases spanning multiple models, regions, sexes, and timepoints to address injury heterogeneity22. Despite these advances, snRNA-seq studies of sTBI have rarely been aligned with dynamic edema evolution, which is a major secondary complication at the acute to subacute stage11. Moreover, cross-species comparisons are often performed without temporal coordination, limiting translational interpretation.

To address these gaps, we performed snRNA-seq on frontal or temporal cortical tissue from 22 human individuals, including 18 sTBI patients spanning 8 to 94 h post-injury with varying degrees of edema, and 4 donors as controls, yielding a total of 146,413 high-quality nuclei for downstream analysis. The systematic and comprehensive cellular atlas revealed dynamic, stage-dependent transcriptional cascades across sTBI stages and their relevance to brain edema. We further conducted snRNA-seq on a murine sTBI model using cortical tissue from 12 mice collected at comparable post-injury stages, generating 141,593 nuclei for analysis. Cross-species comparisons enabled us to profile both conserved dynamics and human-specific cellular responses23.

In detail, by analyzing compositional shifts and their association with edema progression across neuronal and non-neuronal cell types, we identified vulnerable populations following sTBI. Specifically, our high-resolution atlas uncovered a human-specific oligodendrocyte state in sTBI that is strongly correlated with edema progression, characterized by cholesterol regulation (ABCA1), cell adhesion (CDH19), and extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesion (ITGB8). We validated the protective role of ABCA1 following sTBI through treatment experiments on the MO3.13 cell line using the ABCA1 agonist and inhibitor. In addition, we identified distinct state transitions within microglial subpopulations and specific communication with oligodendrocytes, shedding light on their roles in oligodendrocyte repair. Furthermore, excitatory neurons in cortical layer 5/6 showed significant sensitivity to sTBI and sTBI-induced edema. The dysfunction of ion balance (ATPase signaling), cAMP/PKA signaling, and synaptic transmission in cortical layer 5/6 potentially links sTBI to the development of psychiatric disorders.

Results

snRNA-seq reveals transcriptionally distinct cell populations and compositional changes in human sTBI

To map the cellular landscape of sTBI, we performed snRNA-seq on human frontal and temporal lobes from 18 patients spanning 8 to 94 h post-injury (median = 38 h, Fig. 1A and Supplementary Data 1). All patients represented clinically severe TBI, with Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores ranging from 3 to 8, Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) Head scores of 4–5 prior to surgery, and poor functional outcomes defined by Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended (GOSE) scores of 1–4. All patients exhibited varying degrees of cerebral edema, which peaked at approximately 48 h post-injury (Fig. 1B, right panel of Fig. 1C), as quantified using a computed tomography (CT)-based edema ratio (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). After quality control (Supplementary Fig. 2), we obtained 146,413 high-quality nuclei (Supplementary Fig. 3A–E) and identified 25 transcriptionally distinct clusters using UMAP and marker genes (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Data 2). These were annotated into 10 major brain cell types, including oligodendrocytes (Oligs), astrocytes (Astros), endothelial cells (Endos), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), microglia (Micro), VLMCs, excitatory (Ex) and inhibitory (Inh) neurons, and NK cells, based on canonical marker genes (Fig. 1D, E and Supplementary Fig. 3F). Further sub-clustering revealed molecularly distinct subtypes in excitatory and inhibitory neurons, and oligodendrocytes. Notably, genes such as ABCA1 and SELENOP were enriched in specific oligodendrocyte states (Fig. 1E). Cell identities were conserved across individuals and corresponded well with the reference Allen Brain Atlas24 (Supplementary Fig. 3G, H), establishing a high-resolution cell atlas of the human sTBI cortex.

Since different phases of sTBI are potentially associated with activation of specific cell type compartments, we performed differential cell composition analysis across sTBI groups in humans. To this end, transcriptome similarity analysis clustered 22 donors into 4 sTBI sub-groups: control, < 24 h, 24–49 h and 72–94 h after sTBI (Supplementary Fig. 4A). We visualized the spatial distribution of cells across sTBI groups by generating 2D density plots, which represent the concentration of projected cells in a two-dimensional space (Fig. 1F), and analyzed how the cell composition changed across sTBI groups. The proportion of Olig3 increased after 24 h post-sTBI, and Micro increased after 72 h (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. 4B). In contrast, Ex_L5-IT and Ex_L5/6-IT_Car3 increased within the first 24 h, suggesting their acute sensitivity to early injury (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. 4B).

In addition, by investigating the effects of sTBI-induced edema as well as other factors on cell composition alterations, we found the proportion of Olig3 and Micro showed a positive correlation with edema ratio (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). Temporal analysis showed that Olig3 abundance peaked at approximately 72 h post-injury, after the edema peak, whereas microglia expanded after 3 days post-injury, following edema progression (Fig. 1C, H). The proportion of Ex in layer 5/6 (Ex_L5/6), particularly in Ex_L5/6-IT_1, Ex_L5/6-NP, Ex_L5-IT, and Ex_L5/6-IT_Car3, declined during the early edema establishment phase (< 48 h) and stabilized at the maintenance phase (> 48 h), reflecting their vulnerability to edema-associated stress (Fig. 1C, H).

By comparing each sTBI stage to controls across all human excitatory neuron subtypes, we found a high number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) within the first 24 h post-injury (Supplementary Fig. 4E). The substantial transcriptional similarity among Ex_L5/6 subtypes during the early phase indicated the activation of a shared molecular cascade at this cortical layer (Supplementary Fig. 4F and Supplementary Data 3). Taken together, the rapid and coordinated transcriptional activation, high transcriptional similarity, and edema-sensitive depletion of several Ex_L5/6 subtypes highlighted a shared vulnerability of this cortical layer to acute injury (< 24 h) and edema. Many sTBI stage-dependent genes were differentially expressed in a cell-type-specific manner. Microglia-specific genes, upregulated after 72 h post-sTBI, were involved in immune activation (SPP1, SAT1), cytokines (CTSB, CD84), and clearance of cellular debris (MS4A7, STAB1)25 (Supplementary Fig. 4G). Markers of Olig3 included cholesterol transporter (ABCA1), remyelination marker (BCAS1), and type II cadherin protein for axonal interaction (CDH19)26,27, which were upregulated after 24 h post-sTBI and were positively correlated with edema (Supplementary Fig. 4G, I). Focusing on the Ex_L5/6 specific genes, RORB (transcription factor for layer 5 neuronal identity), GABRG1 (GABA_A receptors) were specifically enriched in Ex_L5-IT neurons28 (Supplementary Fig. 4G, H), while LSAMP (limbic system-associated membrane protein)29 and SYT1 (Synaptotagmin 1)30 were general markers across Ex_L5/6 subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 4H). These genes were upregulated within 24 h post-sTBI but showed a progressive downregulation as edema worsened (Supplementary Fig. 4H, I).

These results highlighted a stage-specific reorganization of Olig3, Micro and Ex_L5/6 following sTBI, showing temporal concordance with edema dynamics (Fig. 1C, H), with Olig3 oligodendrocytes increasing and Ex_L5/6 excitatory neurons rapidly declining at the edema development stage (< 48 h). Early injury triggered a coordinated transcriptional response in Ex_L5/6 neurons linked to synaptic remodeling, while Olig3 and microglia showed lipid metabolism and immune activation signatures.

Cross-species comparison reveals conserved cellular programs and divergent edema-associated cellular dynamics after sTBI

Understanding cross-species conservation and defining species-specific features can potentially improve the translation of findings from animal models to humans31. To this end, we established a murine sTBI model ranging from the acute stage to later post-sTBI responses that correspond to human sTBI phases (6 h, 2 days, and 4 days post-sTBI and control, Supplementary Fig. 5A). Histological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed a focal cortical contusion centered at the impact site, with tissue disruption largely confined to the ipsilateral neocortex (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Longitudinal T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated progressive edema formation surrounding the lesion, peaking around 2 days post-injury and partially resolving by 3 days (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D). Consistently, quantitative analysis showed substantial lesion volumes from 6 h to 4 days post-injury, confirming the induction of a severe cortical injury in the murine model (Supplementary Fig. 5D). These pathological and imaging-based assessments establish a temporal framework for subsequent cross-species transcriptomic analyses.

After filtering low-quality cells and batch correction using the same data processing pipeline applied to the human dataset (Supplementary Figs. 5E, F, 6A–E), we derived a total of 141,593 cells and identified 6,233 cell-type-specific genes (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B and Supplementary Data 2). We recapitulated all major cell types identified in human sTBI in the mouse sTBI model. We compared gene expression signatures of mouse cell populations with reference cell-type profiles from the mouse Allen Brain Atlas, showing concordant transcriptional identity between our clusters and previously reported populations24 (Supplementary Fig. 6F, G).

Comparing the cell type composition induced by sTBI across two species, mouse cluster Ex_L4/5-IT_1 displayed the same trend as Ex_L5-IT in humans, which increased within 24 h post-sTBI (Fig. 1F, G and Supplementary Fig. 7C–E). Notably, the increase in the proportion of Olig3 after 24 h post-sTBI was specific to humans. Both Micro and Micro1 populations increased after 72 h in both species, contributing to later phases (Fig. 1F, G and Supplementary Fig. 7C–E). To account for baseline differences in cell-type composition between species (Supplementary Fig. 8A), we examined their relationship to anatomical sampling and found that cell-type compositions in both species were consistent with reference datasets32,33, indicating that these differences primarily reflect sampling depth rather than technical bias (Supplementary Fig. 8B, C). Despite differences in baseline composition, many of the microglia-specific genes, such as SPP1, SAT1, CD84, STAB1, SLC11A1, and SLCO2B1, which were sTBI-dependent in humans, exhibited similar microglia-specific expression changes in the mouse sTBI model. We also observed that RORB showed increased expression in mouse Ex_L4/5/6 after 2 days post-sTBI, consistent with upregulation in human excitatory neurons (Supplementary Fig. 7F).

We next compared pathological and cellular responses between human and mouse sTBI and focused on edema dynamics, injury-associated changes in cellular composition, and conserved transcriptional programs. In both species, tissue edema increased during the early post-injury period and reached a peak before 2 days post-injury (Supplementary Fig. 9A, B). Following this peak, edema declined in mice, whereas in humans, edema plateaued without clear resolution within the same time window (Supplementary Fig. 9A, B). Volume of the primary cortical contusion was positively associated with edema extent in both species, consistent with the expected pathological relationship between primary cortical contusion and secondary injury responses (Supplementary Fig. 9C).

We also compared injury-associated changes in cellular composition and transcriptional programs across species. In mice, microglial proportions increased by 2 days post-injury, coinciding with the onset of edema resolution (Supplementary Fig. 9D). In contrast, microglial proportions in human sTBI samples remained relatively stable across the corresponding time window (Supplementary Fig. 9D). Despite these differences in cellular dynamics, multiple injury-responsive transcriptional programs were conserved across species, including microglial pathways related to immune activation, cytokine signaling, and phagocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 9E, F). However, these programs exhibited activation in mice after 2 days post-injury in parallel with edema resolution, whereas their expression remained comparatively low in humans. In excitatory neurons, programs involved in excitotoxicity showed coordinated downregulation during the acute post-injury phase in both species. However, pathways related to recovery, such as synaptic function, displayed divergent trajectories between species as edema progressed to distinct temporal courses after 2 days post-sTBI (Supplementary Fig. 9G, H).

In summary, cross-species analyses revealed conserved injury-associated programs in microglia, excitatory neurons, and oligodendrocytes following sTBI, alongside divergent cellular dynamics. While microglial activation and excitatory neuron transcriptional responses were broadly shared, expansion of Olig3 oligodendrocytes emerged as a human-specific feature, suggestive of distinct lipid metabolic adaptations. Alignment based on physiological features such as cerebral edema highlights that comparable post-injury time points may reflect different biological stages across species.

ABCA1 defines a human-specific oligodendrocyte state associated with sTBI progression

To better understand the specific transcriptional programs of oligodendrocytes and their roles in injury progression or repair, we first examined the heterogeneity of human oligodendrocytes (hOligs) in detail. Clustering analysis revealed five distinct hOlig subtypes, each defined by unique marker genes (Fig. 2A, B, Supplementary Fig. 10A and Supplementary Data 4). Notably, we found a significant expansion of hOlig-ABCA1 after 24 h post-sTBI, which was derived from Olig3 in Fig. 1D (Fig. 2C, D and Supplementary Fig. 10B). RNA velocity analysis suggests that hOlig-ABCA1 is a dominant intermediate or transitional state emerging from hOlig-FOS, which remains prominent with stabilized transcriptional trajectories (Fig. 2E). hOlig-FOS was enriched in cellular responses to stress and inflammation, which increased within the first 49 h following sTBI (Fig. 2C, D and Supplementary Fig. 10A). Therefore, RNA velocity analysis revealed a dynamic trajectory of hOligs following sTBI: from early stress/inflammatory states (hOlig-FOS) toward a metabolically reprogrammed state marked by ABCA1 upregulation at 24–49 h. Consistent with the temporal progression of edema, both ABCA1 expression and abundance of hOlig-ABCA1 increased during the edema establishment phase (<48 h) and positively correlated with edema ratio, followed by a peak and subsequent decline of hOlig-ABCA1 by 72–94 h as edema stabilized (Figs. 2F, 1C and Supplementary Fig. 10C). By 72–94 h, these transitions stabilized, indicating the resolution or consolidation of the adaptive response. In addition to the activation of cholesterol metabolism (ABCA1) and cell adhesion molecules at 24–72 h post-injury, cytokine-related responses were activated (group 1 in Supplementary Fig. 10D and Supplementary Data 5), which were involved in promoting neuronal survival, modulating immune response, enhancing cell adhesion, and driving tissue remodeling. In the later stages (group 5 and 2), genes related to cell proliferation (MBP, MOBP) and apoptosis regulation (e.g., VMP1, VGF) were upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 10D). We hypothesize that hOligs undergo a phased response to injury: from early inflammation and metabolic stress to mid-stage cellular adaptation and remodeling (e.g., hOlig-ABCA1), and finally toward proliferative or homeostatic functions.

We next examined the gene co-expression programs associated with the hOlig-ABCA1 state during sTBI. We identified a co-expression module (M3) significantly associated with this cluster (Supplementary Fig. 10E). The pathways enriched in M3 include cell adhesion, cholesterol metabolism, cell migration, and cell apoptosis regulation, characterized by hub genes, such as HGF, CDH19, ITGB8, CD44, SRGAP1, and ABCA1 (Fig. 2H). Among these, the key hub gene ABCA1 encodes a cholesterol transporter that maintains membrane lipid homeostasis via cholesterol efflux34. Adhesion- and migration-related genes were also prominent: CDH19 is involved in calcium-dependent cell–cell adhesion; ITGB8 mediates interactions with ECM and activates TGF-β signaling; CD44 functions as a receptor for ECM components and regulates cell motility35; and SRGAP1 regulates actin cytoskeletal dynamics, supporting directional migration36. To investigate these transcriptomic findings at the protein level, we performed immunofluorescence staining in human brains, revealing an increased number of ABCA1+ cells in OLIG2+ hOlig cells 24–49 h post-sTBI (Fig. 2I, J), as well as other proteins such as ITGB8 and CDH19 (Supplementary Fig. 10F–I). These ABCA1 co-expressed genes in hOlig also showed positive associations with edema ratio and exhibited coordinated upregulation during the edema establishment phase (< 48 h), indicating a transcriptional program associated with edema progression (Supplementary Fig. 11A and Fig. 1C).

Using the murine sTBI model, we re-clustered mouse oligodendrocytes (mOligs) into five subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 11B, C). Cross-species correlation revealed that hOlig-ABCA1 showed minimal similarity to any mOlig subtype (r < 0.5; Supplementary Fig. 11D), indicating its human specificity. This was further supported by higher expression of hOlig-ABCA1 markers (ABCA1, HGF, CDH19) compared to mouse (Supplementary Fig. 11E) and by the lack of ABCA1 upregulation in mOligs after sTBI, in contrast to the increase observed in humans (Supplementary Fig. 11F, G). Further supporting this species specificity, hOlig-ABCA1-associated genes (ABCA1, CDH19, ITGB8, HGF, NRCAM, and SRGAP1) showed significantly higher expression in our hOligs at 2 days post-sTBI than in mouse oligodendrocytes at 24 h or 7 days (derived from Jha et al. dataset37, Supplementary Fig. 11H). mOligs upregulated genes involved in cell adhesion regulation following injury (group 3; Supplementary Fig. 11I and Supplementary Data 6), suggesting alternative repair strategies that do not involve ABCA1-mediated lipid remodeling.

In summary, sTBI induces a dynamic and staged oligodendrocyte response in humans, characterized by the emergence of a transient, ABCA1-enriched state associated with lipid remodeling, adhesion, and edema progression, a program that is largely absent in mouse models.

ABCA1-associated lipid homeostasis characterizes an injury-responsive state in oligodendrocyte-lineage cells

The emergence of a transient, ABCA1-enriched oligodendrocyte state during the early post-injury period suggested a functional link between ABCA1-mediated lipid homeostasis and oligodendrocyte responses under injury-associated stress. We then employed an in vitro oligodendrocyte differentiation and stress paradigm using MO3.13 cells. Following phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) treatment, MO3.13 cells exhibited oligodendrocyte-like morphology and increased expression of myelin-associated markers, including MAG, MBP and CNPase, accompanied by reduced expression of the astrocytic marker GFAP (Supplementary Fig. 12A–C). Cholesterol efflux was used as a functional readout of ABCA1 activity. Inflammatory stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 μg/mL, 24 h) reduced extracellular cholesterol release, whereas cells treated with ABCA1 agonist CS-6253 (1 μM, 24 h) restored efflux (Supplementary Fig. 12D). Consistently, activation of ABCA1 by CS-6253 significantly preserved MBP and CNP expression under oxidative stress induced by H₂O₂ (100 μM, 24 h), while pharmacological inhibition of ABCA1 with probucol further exacerbated marker loss (Fig. 2K, L).

We further extended these findings in a more physiological system in human primary oligodendrocytes. Based on the enhanced cholesterol efflux using CS-6253, we assessed intracellular lipid accumulation using BODIPY staining and observed a marked reduction in lipid droplet burden upon ABCA1 activation (Supplementary Fig. 12E, F). In parallel, immunofluorescence analysis revealed stabilization of the oligodendrocyte maturation–associated marker MAG following ABCA1 activation, whereas LPS (1 μg/mL, 24 h) alone suppressed MAG expression (Supplementary Fig. 12G, H). Together, these results indicate that ABCA1-associated lipid homeostasis corresponds to an injury-adaptive state in oligodendrocyte-lineage cells under inflammatory stress, reflected by maintenance of myelin-associated protein expression.

Given that ABCA1-associated transcriptional programs in sTBI were enriched for lipid metabolic and junctional pathways, we asked whether similar molecular signatures are engaged in other demyelinating conditions. We analyzed published proteomic data from relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS)38 — a chronic neuroinflammatory disorder marked by extensive myelin loss — using samples from 3 RRMS patients and 3 matched controls (two replicates each). The analysis revealed that ABCA1, CD44, and CLIC4 were upregulated (log₂FC > 0.25) compared to controls, supporting their involvement in response to demyelinating injury (Supplementary Fig. 12I). Proteins co-expressed with ABCA1 (Spearman correlation > 0.6) were enriched in fatty acid metabolism (e.g., ACSS3, CROT, ACOT2) and cell junction (e.g., CDH11, FLNA, SORBS1) (Fig. 2M, right). These proteins, upregulated in RRMS (Fig. 2M, left), were also highly expressed in the hOlig-ABCA1 cluster at the single-cell level. Cell junction proteins correlated with ABCA1 in RRMS, which are implicated in myelin stability, overlapped with pathways identified in the hOlig-ABCA1 cluster of sTBI, where ABCA1 was also co-expressed with junction- and adhesion-related genes such as CDH19, CD44, and ITGB8 (Fig. 2H).

Together, these findings indicated that ABCA1-mediated lipid homeostasis functionally supports oligodendrocyte-lineage function under injury-associated stress, as demonstrated by preserved cholesterol efflux and maintenance of myelin-associated protein expression in vitro and in primary human oligodendrocytes. The concordant upregulation of ABCA1 and associated lipid metabolic and junctional proteins in RRMS further suggests that this program may represent a conserved oligodendrocyte response to demyelinating injury across acute and chronic neuroinflammatory conditions.

Species-specific interaction linking microglial CALM2 and HMGB1 with oligodendrocyte ABCA1 after sTBI

Given the dynamic changes in inflammatory and synaptic programs across sTBI stages, we examined the ligand–receptor communication landscape across sTBI stages (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 13C). hMicro ligands showed temporal changes after sTBI, most notably regulating astrocytes and oligodendrocytes, where we observed an interaction between microglial CALM2 and hOlig-ABCA1 via the CALM2-ABCA1 pair, suggesting a potential link between microglial signaling and oligodendrocyte lipid metabolism (Fig. 3F). Consistent with this, we observed a significant negative correlation between CALM2 and ABCA1 across sTBI progression (P = 0.039, Spearman r = − 0.51), indicating an opposing regulation relationship (Fig. 3G, H). We also identified co-expression genes with CALM2 enriched in pathways related to negative regulation of cytokine signaling and IL-10 activity, suggesting a role for CALM2 in modulating anti-inflammatory responses during sTBI progression41 (Supplementary Fig. 13D). Notably, HMGB1, a gene strongly correlated with CALM2 (Spearman r = 0.81, P = 4.9 × 10−6; Supplementary Fig. 13E) was predicted to interact with ABCA1 by NicheNet42 (Fig. 3I). Moreover, HMGB1 expression in hMicro showed a significant negative correlation with ABCA1 within the first 49 h post-injury, suggesting potential regulation between HMGB1 and ABCA1 (Fig. 3J, K). Previous studies have shown that, as a damage-associated molecular pattern, HMGB1 suppresses cholesterol efflux by inhibiting the LXR–ABCA1 pathway43, supporting the functional link between HMGB1 and ABCA1 and potentially allowing for ABCA1 upregulation in human oligodendrocytes after sTBI.

Targeted in vitro perturbation experiments were used to test this regulatory relationship between CALM2, HMGB1, and ABCA1. In HMC3 cells under LPS stimulation (1 μg/mL), extracellular HMGB1 levels measured by ELISA were significantly reduced following CALM2 knockdown compared with CALM2 overexpression conditions (Fig. 3L). HMGB1 (100 ng/mL) significantly reduced TO901317-induced (1 μM) ABCA1 mRNA expression in differentiated MO3.13 cells, and this effect was reversed by an HMGB1-neutralizing antibody (1 μg/mL), indicating an HMGB1-dependent suppression of ABCA1 induction (Fig. 3M). Notably, HMGB1 had minimal effects on basal ABCA1 expression in the absence of TO901317, suggesting that HMGB1 primarily constrains ABCA1 induction rather than altering baseline levels (Supplementary Fig. 14L). Consistently, the HMGB1-mediated decrease in ABCA1 expression was accompanied by reduced extracellular cholesterol release, supporting a functional impact on cholesterol efflux (Fig. 3N). Analysis of an independent mouse TBI dataset37 revealed a conserved negative correlation between CALM2 and ABCA1 in microglia (Spearman r = -0.89, P = 0.012, Supplementary Fig. 14A–D). Moreover, CALM2 and HMGB1 were in the same WGCNA module within microglia, and genes co-expressed with CALM2 in our sTBI dataset significantly overlapped with those in the Jha et al. dataset37 (p = 1.1 × 10−16; Supplementary Fig. 14E–H). HMGB1 and ABCA1 also exhibited a significant negative Spearman correlation in microglia in the Jha et al. dataset (Supplementary Fig. 14I), supporting the reproducibility of the CALM2-HMGB1-ABCA1 co-expression pattern across independent datasets. However, different from hMicro, HMGB1 and CALM2 were upregulated after 2 days post-sTBI in mice, coinciding with microglia expansion and edema resolution (Supplementary Figs. 14J, K, 9A, B, D).

In summary, integrated single-cell, intercellular communication inference, and in vitro perturbation experiments identify shared microglia–oligodendrocyte association involving CALM2, HMGB1, and ABCA1-linked lipid handling (Fig. 3O). While this association was engaged across species, its temporal coordination differed between humans and mice, pointing to species-specific control of lipid homeostasis during sTBI-induced edema.

Conserved transcriptional modules in deep-layer excitatory neurons indicate transcriptional patterns linked to sTBI‑associated edema and psychiatric genetic risk

Differentially expressed genes in Ex_L5/6 neurons within the first 24 h post-sTBI substantially overlapped with later-stage responses across major human excitatory neuron subtypes, indicating that early transcriptional reprogramming is initiated during this critical window (Supplementary Fig. 15A). Considering the broad functional suppression observed in Ex_L5/6 neurons during edema progression, we examined whether sTBI- and edema-associated genes in these neurons overlap with genetic risk for psychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders44,45. Using MAGMA-derived gene-level statistics, these genes were tested for enrichment of genome-wide association signals across autism spectrum disorder (ASD)46, major depressive disorder (MDD)47, bipolar disorder (BP)48, schizophrenia (SCZ)49, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD)50. Modules M6 and M11 showed enrichment for schizophrenia GWAS association signals using GSEA (Fig. 4H). Examining the MAGMA gene-level statistics within M6 and M11 revealed that genes involved in axon guidance (DCC, FOXP1, CHN1) and calcium signaling (ATP2B2, PRKACB) showed relatively higher association, indicating the contribution to the module-level convergence with SCZ (Fig. 4I). In addition, analysis of independent scRNA-seq datasets from AD, ASD, MDD, BP, and SCZ patients’ brains showed that genes derived from these modules were preferentially enriched among differentially expressed genes in Ex_L5/6 neurons of SCZ individuals (Supplementary Fig. 16A, B).

We next examined whether sTBI-induced intercellular signaling from microglia and oligodendrocytes targets Ex_L5/6 transcriptional programs. Ligand-receptor analysis revealed that sTBI-perturbed ligands showed high regulatory potential to receptors in M6, M9, and M11, including APP, ADCY1, BMPR2, and EPHA4, with APP and MET interacting with hOlig-ABCA1 co-expressed ligands HGF, CD44, and CD47 (Figs. 4J, 2H). These receptors were enriched for calcium signaling (ADCY1), axon guidance (EPHA4, BMPR2), and synaptic signaling (APP), functionally overlapping with genes showing relatively higher association with SCZ GWAS (Fig. 4I, J). STRING analysis confirmed these strong functional associations between ADCY1 and PRKACB, and between APP and synaptic genes including ELAVL4, SYT11, and RTN3 (Fig. 4I and Supplementary Fig. 16C). Importantly, ligand expression exhibited sTBI stage-dependent dynamics across oligodendrocytes and microglia, while receptor expression was not only associated with sTBI stages but also correlated with edema progression (Fig. 4K, L and Supplementary Fig. 15L).

In summary, analyses of deep-layer excitatory neurons identify early transcriptional programs engaged within 24 h of sTBI that are associated with edema progression, enriched for SCZ GWAS signals, and targeted by injury-responsive ligand–receptor signaling from microglia and oligodendrocytes. These findings link sTBI-induced intercellular communication with excitatory neuron vulnerability and neuropsychiatric disease risk.

Robustness and extended analyses

To ensure the robustness of the main findings, we performed sensitivity analyses across QC thresholds, sequencing batch, and sex composition. Cell clustering, cell type proportions, and sTBI-associated genes remained highly consistent, with preserved edema associations in the human dataset (Supplementary Figs. 17, 18, 19A–C). Key injury-associated programs, including oligodendrocyte and microglia subclusters, their trajectories, modules of Ex_L5/6, and the expression relationships among ABCA1, CALM2, and HMGB1, were maintained across conditions (Supplementary Figs. 19D, E, 20). Batch1-restricted and male-only analyses yielded concordant results in cell composition and sTBI signatures, with core genes (ABCA1, CALM2, and HMGB1) maintaining stable expression patterns across conditions (Supplementary Figs. 21, 22). These analyses demonstrated the robustness of the main findings.

As part of the atlas, astrocyte and OPC responses showed stage-dependent dynamics from early stress and immune activation to later extracellular matrix and neuronal interaction–related programs (Supplementary Figs. 23A, 24A). These responses were partially conserved across species but also exhibited distinct temporal patterns between humans and mice, paralleling species-specific edema dynamics (Supplementary Figs. 9A, B, 23B–G, 24B–G).

Discussion

We generated a single-cell transcriptomic atlas of human and mouse cortex across stages of sTBI, uncovering dynamic, cell-type-specific responses to injury and edema. While microglia, oligodendrocytes, and deep-layer excitatory neurons (L5/6) exhibited marked shifts in composition and transcriptional profiles, other populations, such as astrocytes and OPCs, remained relatively stable (Fig. 5). This high-resolution atlas revealed both conserved and, notably, human-specific cellular programs, including a distinct oligodendrocyte state marked by ABCA1 that was absent in mouse, highlighting species-specific mechanisms of injury adaptation and repair. One of the key findings in our study was identifying a human-sTBI-specific polarization state of oligodendrocytes after 24 h in response to sTBI and sTBI-induced edema, characterized by high ABCA1 expression alongside co-expressed adhesion molecules such as CDH19, ITGB8, CD44, and SRGAP1. This transcriptional program was enriched in contexts of sTBI-induced edema and linked to genes involved in lipid metabolism, cell adhesion, and migration. In vitro experiments using differentiated MO3.13 cells supported a functional role for ABCA1 in protecting cell integrity under oxidative stress. ABCA1 is a cholesterol transporter involved in lipid efflux and membrane remodeling51, and has been shown to modulate antioxidant and inflammatory pathways40,52, including NRF2, which increases detoxifying enzymes GPX4 and SOD53, and NF-κB signaling, which reduces secondary damage triggered by oxidative stress54. The calcium-dependent adhesion molecule CDH19 regulates cell migration through the β-catenin signaling27, while ITGB8 mediates cell–cell and cell–ECM adhesion via TGF-β signaling35. Upregulation of CDH19 and ITGB8 facilitates stable oligodendrocyte–axon interactions, contributing to the structural support required for effective remyelination. CD44 interacts with hyaluronic acid in the ECM, while SRGAP1 modulates cytoskeletal remodeling through Rho GTPases36,55—both proteins may influence oligodendrocyte migration and position. The co-expression of these adhesion-related genes with ABCA1 suggests that it may coordinate lipid homeostasis with structural remodeling, potentially facilitating oligodendrocyte positioning and stabilization within the injured cortex. These findings suggest a coordinated oligodendrocyte response in human sTBI that integrates metabolic and adhesive mechanisms to support myelin integrity. Proteomic data from iPSC-derived oligodendrocytes in RRMS offered a comparative framework to explore ABCA1-associated mechanisms in sTBI. Several ABCA1-correlated proteins in RRMS, particularly those linked to lipid metabolism and cell matrix, were also enriched in the hOlig-ABCA1 population after sTBI. This partial overlap suggests that ABCA1 may engage conserved cellular programs in response to demyelination. While our in vitro assays demonstrate that ABCA1 activation enhances cholesterol efflux and preserves myelin-associated proteins in oligodendrocyte-lineage cells under inflammatory stress, direct assessment of remyelination in vivo will require future studies using physiologically relevant systems such as iPSC-derived oligodendrocytes and/or animal models with myelin repair readouts.

Our analyses suggested a framework linking CALM2 and HMGB1 from microglial to oligodendrocyte ABCA1 expression during sTBI. In human microglia, reduced CALM2 and HMGB1 expression during the 24–94 h post-injury window coincides with increased ABCA1 expression in oligodendrocytes, consistent with coordinated cross-cell-type transcriptional changes. This hypothesis is consistent with previous reports showing that CALM2, as a central calcium sensor, regulates diverse inflammatory responses through calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase (CaMK) signaling56,57, while HMGB1, as a damage-associated molecular pattern, is calcium-dependent58. Prior studies showed that HMGB1 negatively regulates ABCA1 via inhibition of the LXR pathway43. Importantly, our in vitro perturbation assays provide functional support for this relationship: CALM2 modulation altered extracellular HMGB1 levels in HMC3 cells, and HMGB1 attenuated inducible ABCA1 activation and cholesterol efflux in oligodendrocyte-lineage cells under inflammatory stress. The observation that HMGB1 minimally affected basal ABCA1 expression but impaired its inducible upregulation suggests a context-dependent brake on lipid-remodeling programs rather than constitutive repression. These relationships highlighted calcium- and HMGB1- associated signaling as candidate pathways for future mechanistic studies of microglia–oligodendrocyte interactions in vivo, and opened a potential avenue for clinical intervention.

Leveraging our single-cell atlas of human sTBI, we examined transcriptional vulnerabilities across neuronal subtypes and identified deep-layer excitatory neurons as key responders to injury. We identified Ex_L5/6 excitatory neurons as particularly vulnerable to sTBI and edema. These deep-layer neurons, critical for cortical integration, are prone to excitotoxicity and inflammation after injury59,60. Within 24 h post-sTBI, protective pathways including ATPase activity61, cAMP/PKA signaling62, and GABAergic transmission63 were activated, likely reflecting an acute attempt to restore ionic balance and suppress excitotoxicity. However, these responses were disrupted as edema progressed. Notably, we report that sTBI- and edema-induced transcriptional changes in these neurons converge with schizophrenia GWAS signals. This supports a two-hit model in which inherited risk is compounded by acute brain injury. This suggests that Ex_L5/6 neurons may represent a potential cellular target linking TBI to schizophrenia risk. While sTBI module gene expression changes are also observed in other neuron subtypes in SCZ, consistent with prior reports of multi-layer involvement64, the GWAS enrichment and transcriptional alterations remain concentrated in Ex_L5/6 neurons. This suggested Ex_L5/6 as the primary site of sTBI-related schizophrenia risk, with broader neuronal dysregulation occurring during disease progression. Our findings suggest that preserving key neuroprotective pathways within the first 24 h post-sTBI may reduce long-term psychiatric sequelae, particularly in genetically susceptible individuals.

Single-cell studies of TBI have largely focused on concussive or mild TBI, showing transient immune and glial stress responses at early post-injury windows19,20. Consistent with these studies, we observe early inflammatory activation across cell types. However, our data indicate that sTBI within the acute to subacute stage can be distinguished not by the presence of edema, but by its progression, which acts as a dominant secondary injury process at the acute to subacute stage that reshapes molecular timing. In Ex_L5/6, conserved transcriptional modules enriched for schizophrenia GWAS signals are downregulated during edema establishment and remain inhibited throughout edema stabilization in human sTBI, whereas in the mouse CCI model, these modules became activated after edema resolution. Glial programs show a parallel pattern: in mice, edema resolution coincides with recovery of CALM2 and HMGB1 expression and microglia activation, whereas in human sTBI, persistent edema is associated with sustained suppression of CALM2 and HMGB1 alongside ABCA1 upregulation. Together, these findings indicated that sTBI involves stage-specific cellular programs that are temporally shifted in association with progressive secondary injury, such as edema, rather than representing a simple amplification of mTBI-associated responses.

Accordingly, our cross-species comparisons emphasize that some similar molecular and cellular responses are engaged in both species but are deployed at different times in parallel with divergent secondary injury trajectories such as edema. This observation supports the use of cerebral edema as a physiological anchor for cross-species alignment, while also highlighting species-specific kinetics of injury responses. Within this framework, the ABCA1-expressing oligodendrocyte state and HMGB1-ABCA1 interaction might represent a human-specific adaptation to prolonged secondary injury rather than a conserved molecular response.

Our atlas and findings may provide valuable insights into the field. First, by generating a cross-species single-cell atlas of sTBI, we highlighted the importance of dissecting injury heterogeneity at cellular resolution. This approach led to the discovery of a human-specific oligodendrocyte polarization state (ABCA1⁺-Olig), which was absent in murine sTBI, suggesting potential therapeutic targets, such as ABCA1, ITGB8, and CDH19, for modulating edema and promoting repair. Second, we revealed a microglia-to-oligodendrocyte signaling pathway involving CALM2, HMGB1, and ABCA1, providing mechanistic insight into how microglial modulation of oligodendrocyte function may influence myelin repair in human brain injury. Third, our comparative analysis across human and mouse brains uncovered species-specific injury responses and identified biomarkers that may be overlooked in preclinical models, such as a longer phase of acute inflammation and edema in hMicro compared with mMicro, which might allow the upregulation of ABCA1 in hOligs, thus enhancing the translational relevance of sTBI research19,21.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, human control samples were obtained from cortical tissue resected during epilepsy or tumor surgery rather than from neurologically healthy individuals. Although these samples were collected at sites distant from the primary pathology, they may still reflect secondary or systemic effects, a common constraint in human sTBI studies. To mitigate this limitation, our analyses focus on relative, time-dependent changes following sTBI and on conserved response patterns, rather than on absolute baseline differences between conditions. Second, precise alignment across species is challenging. Human samples were derived from frontal and temporal cortices, whereas mouse samples were collected from the parietal neocortex. Given fundamental differences in cortical organization and regional specialization across species, cross-species comparisons were performed at the level of shared cell types and transcriptional programs rather than strict regional homology. Residual regional heterogeneity may therefore contribute to quantitative variation in observed responses. Third, cerebral edema was used as a primary pathophysiological anchor to align injury progression across species. While edema represents a clinically relevant feature of sTBI at the acute to subacute stage, it captures only one dimension of injury severity, and other physiological and long-term functional outcomes were not systematically assessed. Future studies integrating broader cortical sampling, spatially resolved transcriptomics, and multimodal clinical phenotyping will be important for refining cross-species alignment and extending these findings.

In summary, our cross-species single-cell atlas of sTBI serves as a resource for the neuroscience community, enabling detailed exploration of cell-type-specific responses across time and species. By capturing human-specific cellular states and molecular programs, this atlas provides a platform for generating new, clinically relevant hypotheses that are not accessible through traditional single-species or single-timepoint approaches. It lays the groundwork for more precise, human-informed strategies to understand and treat TBI.

Methods

Ethics

All human brain cortex for snRNA-seq were obtained from the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Sample collection was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (approval number: 2022-SRFA-329). Written informed consent was obtained from patients when possible, or otherwise from their legally authorized representative (next of kin) according to local ethical and legal regulations. All mouse experiments in our study were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (approval number: IACUC-2206006).

Human sTBI participants, sTBI tissue collection

We included 22 human samples (17 male, 5 female) with a mean age of 52.8 ± 13.4 years (range 30–73, Supplementary Data 1). All sTBI patients were defined primarily by a GCS score of 3–8 at admission65. All patients also had CT-confirmed traumatic intracranial lesions and underwent contusion and hematoma evacuation with or without decompressive craniectomy, which enabled procurement of contusion-associated brain tissue for snRNA-seq. To further document patient severity using standardized clinical metrics, we recorded AIS (Head) scores (range 4–5) and GOSE outcomes (range 1–4) for all patients. Patients with the following criteria were excluded: (1) hemodynamic instability; (2) intracranial hypertension that may lead to further brain injury; (3) coagulopathy; (4) multiorgan dysfunction, such as liver or kidney failure, which may increase the risk of complications. Control tissue was obtained from patients undergoing sugery for non-traumatic neurological conditions, such as tumor. Tissue obtained from regions distant from the lesion was defined as control tissue because excess brain tissue from the surgical site can be collected at the discretion of the surgeon during the normal course of the procedure.

During hematoma and contusion evacuation, contusion-associated brain tissue was resected and separated from blood clots on the surgical bench using fine neurosurgical micro-instruments (e.g., spring scissors and dissectors). As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1B, the contusion-associated brain tissue (green) included a hemorrhagic core (red) and adjacent injured parenchyma. Importantly, the tissue used for snRNA-seq corresponded to parenchymal fragments carefully dissected from the hemorrhagic core, rather than the blood clot itself. Tissue isolated from the hematoma was rinsed with sterile saline, gently blotted to remove excess fluid, and subdivided into 3–4 small pieces (typically derived from a ~ 1–2 cm diameter tissue block, depending on the amount of resected tissue). All collected tissue fragments were rapidly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 10 min after resection.

Edema ratio measurement in human sTBI

The brain edema ratio was evaluated by three-dimensional reconstruction, referring to the volumetric assessment of brain structures based on the CT images. This process allows for a detailed evaluation of the total injury volume, hematoma volume, and edema volume, facilitating a comprehensive analysis of brain edema and contusion extent. A neuroradiologist independently measured the total contusion volume, hematoma volume, and edema volume using the 3D slicer software (v.5.6.1; NIH US) for each patient upon admission. The same process was independently performed by two additional researchers (from another group), and the final assessment for each sample was obtained by averaging the three measurements. Total contusion and hematoma volume on CT was measured using a semiautomatic volumetric algorithm in our study. The edema ratio was calculated using the following formula: (Total contusion volume - hematoma volume) / Total contusion volume.

Mouse sTBI models and edema measurement

Male 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice obtained from the Animal Core Facility of Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing, China) were maintained under a light 12 h/dark 12 h cycle at constant temperature 20–25 °C and humidity ranging from 40%–70%.

Mouse sTBI model in vivo was established using controlled cortical impact (CCI)66. Briefly, after being anesthetized with 4% isoflurane, mice underwent a midline longitudinal scalp incision to expose the skull. A 4 mm diameter craniotomy was then performed on the right parietal bone, and the flap was subsequently removed. Brain injury was induced by impacting the right cortex with a 3 mm diameter impactor, at a velocity of 6.0 ± 0.2 m/s, with a depth of 1.4 mm and a residence time of 50 ms. The scalp was then closed with standard suture material. For the sham group, the surgery was only to expose the dura. After the procedure, mice were returned to their cages until they regained consciousness and normal motor function and were allowed unrestricted access to water and food during this recovery period. After being anesthetized with 4% isoflurane, the injured cortex was collected from all sTBI-induced mice at three specific time points: 6 h, 2 days, and 4 days post-injury. To remove irrelevant tissue for our study, we removed the pia mater, and the tissue was washed with pre-cooled RNase-free saline to remove blood stains and debris. Finally, all mice were euthanized according to the institutional guidelines.

Brain water content was determined using the wet–dry weight method. Ipsilateral and contralateral hemispheres were weighed immediately after dissection to obtain wet weight, then dried in an oven at 85 °C for 48–72 h. Dry weight was measured using the same digital balance, and tissue water content was calculated as the fractional water content per gram of dry weight. Mouse MRI was performed using a 3-T scanner. Total lesion volume was quantified on T2-weighted images by manually delineating the lesion area on each coronal slice. Single-nucleus RNA-seq library construction for human and mouse

All tissues from both human and mouse brain cortex were stored in liquid nitrogen before the isolation of intact nuclei. The frozen tissue was homogenized in NLB buffer, which contained 250 mM sucrose, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 3 mM MgAc2, 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich), 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.2 U/μL RNase Inhibitor (Sigma Aldrich). Various concentrations of sucrose were used to purify nuclei. The concentration of nuclei was adjusted to approximately 1000 nuclei/μL for snRNA-seq. The snRNA-seq libraries were generated using the 10X Genomics Chromium Controller Instrument and Chromium Single Cell 3’ V3.1 Reagent Kits (10X Genomics). Briefly, nuclei were concentrated to approximately 1000 nuclei/µL and loaded into each channel to generate single-nucleus Gel Bead-In-Emulsions (GEMs). After the RT step, GEMs were broken, and barcoded cDNA was purified and amplified. The amplified barcoded cDNA was fragmented, A-tailed, ligated with adapters and index PCR amplified. The final libraries were quantified using the Qubit High Sensitivity DNA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the size distribution of the libraries was determined using a High Sensitivity DNA chip on a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent). All libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) on a 150 bp paired-end run.

Preprocessing of snRNA-seq data and quality control

Raw reads were processed using the CellRanger software (v. 6.0.3). The cellranger count command was used to generate the gene-per-cell expression matrices for each sample by aligning the human reads to the Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 38 (GRCh38) genome assemblies, while mouse reads were aligned to Genome Reference Consortium Mouse Build 38 (GRCm38) genome assemblies. A gene expression matrix was generated for each sample, corresponding to 22 human samples and 12 mouse samples, respectively. For both human and mouse snRNA-seq data, each dataset was processed separately using the R package Seurat (v4.0.2)67. The expression matrices of all human samples were merged into a global Seurat object using the MergeSeurat function, and the expression matrices of all mouse samples were merged separately using the same function. The merged datasets contained 175,163 human nuclei and 162,907 mouse nuclei, respectively. Nuclei were retained for downstream analyses if they expressed at least 500 detected genes (nGenes) and passed a data-driven lower Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs) threshold determined using barcode-rank (knee/inflection) curves (Supplementary Figs. 2C, 5F). Based on the distribution of mitochondrial read percentages (percent.MT) in our data (Supplementary Figs. 17A, 18A), a threshold of 10% was applied. To assess the robustness of the main results, we performed sensitivity analyses using an alternative, data-driven mitochondrial threshold. Specifically, we identified the dominant density peak corresponding to high-quality nuclei and selected the first local minimum on the right side of this peak as the cutoff, which separates the main population from a high-mitochondrial tail. To determine the maximum values of nGenes or UMIs, we individually ranked the cells of each sample in descending order based on nGenes and generated the curve of cell number against nGenes. Subsequently, the inflection point at which the curve became continuous was determined as a cutoff, and nuclei with nGenes values exceeding this threshold were removed. The same procedure was applied to UMIs. DoubletFinder was used to remove doublets68.

The data were normalized for library size by a scale factor of 10,000 and log-transformed using the NormalizeData function in Seurat. Highly variable genes (HVGs) were determined using the function FindVariableFeatures from Seurat, selecting the top 2000 variable genes. Mitochondrial genes were excluded from the HVG set to reduce the influence of technical variation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to obtain a small number of principal components as input to the UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) algorithm. The number of principal components was then chosen at the elbow of the plot, where a substantial drop was observed in the proportion of variance explained.

Single-nucleus RNA-seq data analysis

We performed the following data processing steps for human and mouse snRNA-seq data separately. We ran the canonical correlation analysis (CCA) to align samples in a low-dimensional space for clustering and UMAP visualization67. The functions used for this step were FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData from Seurat. We applied the ScaleData function to scale the expression data by regressing percent.MT, followed by RunPCA for dimensionality reduction. We then used FindNeighbors to identify similar cells based on the PCA results, followed by RunUMAP to visualize the data in two dimensions. Finally, we executed FindClusters to identify distinct clusters of cells with similar expression profiles. To determine the optimal cluster resolution, we first clustered the data on a range of resolutions (from 0.1 to 1, with steps of 0.1). Then, we plotted the number of clusters obtained versus the resolution using the clustree package and chose a resolution (0.5 for mouse data, 0.5 for human data) for which the number of clusters remained constant across multiple resolutions.

Cell type annotation

To annotate cell types for each cluster or subcluster in the mouse and human dataset, we identified marker genes using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test by comparing each cluster with others using the threshold: average log2FC > 0.25 and Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted P-value < 0.05. Next, we checked the expression of known marker genes in our human snRNA-seq dataset68 (Olig: MBP, PLP1, CERCAM; Astro: AQP4, GFAP; Ex: ENC1, SLC17A7, NRN1; Inh: GAD1, GAD2; Micro: STAB1, SPP1, SLC11A1; OPC: VCAN, PDGFRA; Endo: RGS5, CLDN5; VLMC: COL1A2, COL1A1). We used clusterProfiler69 package (v4.6.2) to perform functional enrichment based on Gene Ontology (GO), and significantly enriched biological processes were identified using a BH-adjusted P-value < 0.05.

To verify the cell type classification in our sTBI snRNA-seq, we acquired both human and mouse SMART-seq data from frontal and temporal lobe regions. The gene expression matrices (49,275 human cells, 94,078 mouse cells) were obtained from https://portal.brain-map.org/atlases-and-data/rnaseq, which classified a highly diverse set of Ex and Inh subtypes24. We first computed the average gene expression per cell type for both the sTBI snRNA-seq and Allen datasets using the AverageExpression function. To focus on highly variable genes, we calculated the standard deviation for each gene across all cell types within each dataset and selected the top 500 most variable genes from each. The intersection of these two gene sets was defined as the common gene set. We then extracted the expression matrices of genes in common across cell types in both datasets and calculated pairwise Spearman correlations between the average expression profiles.

Transcriptional and cell proportion alterations between each sTBI group and control

We visualized the spatial distribution of cells across time points following sTBI by generating 2D density plots. Specifically, we used the stat_density_2d function to compute the concentration of projected cells in a two-dimensional UMAP space. This method allowed us to depict the density of cells across the UMAP_1 and UMAP_2 axes. For each cell type in humans or mice, proportion comparisons between any two sTBI groups were evaluated using a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test70. Statistical significance was defined as P-values < 0.05.

Differential expression analysis between each sTBI group (i.e., timepoint) and control (i.e., patients in < 24 h group vs control) for both human and mouse samples were performed using the MAST method as implemented in Seurat, with statistical significance assessed using a two-sided likelihood ratio test. Age, brain region (FL/TL), log10-transformed UMI counts and percent.MT were considered as covariates. Genes with absolute log2FC greater than 0.5 and BH-adjusted P-value less than 0.05 were considered significant and retained for further analysis. Functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed using the ReactomePA (v1.34.0) and clusterProfiler packages to identify pathways from the Reactome database and GO Biological Process.

Assessing the contribution of clinical variables to cell proportions or edema ratio

We assessed the independent associations between clinical variables and cell-type proportions using multivariable linear regression models. Cell-type proportion was used as the dependent variable, with edema ratio, age, brain region, injury type, decompressive craniectomy (DC), and contusion volume included as covariates. Separately, to model edema dynamics over time, we fitted generalized additive models (GAMs), in which epidural hematoma (EDH), traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage (tSAH), subdural hematoma (SDH), DC status, contusion volume and time were included.

Similarity of cell type expression across species

First, we generated two matrices depicting the average expression of each gene within individual clusters for both human and mouse species. We then identified 1000 genes for each cluster based on their highest standard deviation of expression levels across cell types, indicating those with the most variation in expression across clusters. We then transferred human SYMBOL ID to mouse SYMBOL ID using the BioMart tool (v2.46.3) from the Ensembl database71, focusing specifically on one-to-one orthologs. Spearman correlations between mouse and human cell types were calculated in the space of shared one-to-one orthologous genes of clusters.

Identification of sTBI stage-associated genes in mouse or human clusters

We obtained sTBI stage-associated genes (TSAGs) using the Monocle package (v2.18.0)72. A newCellDataSet was constructed, and stage-associated genes were identified using the differentialGeneTest function based on a generalized linear model and two-sided likelihood ratio test (~ stage), with BH adjustment for multiple comparisons (q-value < 0.01). Pseudotime values were assigned to each cell, and TSAGs were the overlap between genes significantly correlated with sTBI stages and differentially expressed compared to the control at each sTBI group. TSAGs were used for dimensionality reduction via the DDRTree method (max_components = 2, norm_method = log), followed by cell ordering with the orderCells function. The procedures in both species were the same.

Due to the limited number of female donors in our study (restricted to the sTBI 24–48 h group) and considering the effect of sex on TSAGs, we performed a sensitivity analysis using male samples only. TSAGs identified in the male-only dataset were then compared with those from the full cohort using a one-sided hypergeometric test to assess their overlap.

Construction of cellular trajectories using RNA velocity

Cell RNA velocity analysis for human oligodendrocytes was performed using the Velocyto program73, which used the relative proportion of unspliced and spliced mRNA abundance as an indicator of the cell state. We first generated a loom file based on the fastq file for each sample, which was the required format as Velocyto input. Annotation of spliced and unspliced reads was then performed using the velocyto.py command-line tools. Counts were filtered and normalized using the filter_and_normalize function as recommended in standard pipeline. RNA velocity was estimated by a stochastic model that predicted individual cell transitions by estimating their direction and speed74. Finally, the velocity field was projected onto the existing UMAP space from Seurat (v4.0.5). velocity_embedding_stream was used to visualize cell directions.

Projecting co-expression modules in human oligodendrocytes or excitatory neurons

High-dimensional Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (hdWGCNA: v0.2.24), an extension of WGCNA for single-cell data, was used to identify co-expression modules75. First, we executed SetupForWGCNA to set up the object using genes expressed in a minimum of 5% of cells, followed by the construction of metacells, aggregating cells with similar biological characteristics based on cell type classification using the k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm. Pairwise gene–gene correlations were computed across metacells and transformed into a weighted adjacency matrix using a soft-thresholding approach. Subsequently, a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM) co-expression network was established, and modules within each cell subpopulation were identified using the soft_power=5 parameter at the elbow of the topology model fit line76. Gene expression summaries within a module were computed using the ModuleEigengenes function, providing the activity of each module across all included cells. Finally, we evaluated the module eigengene-based connectivity (kME), quantifying the correlation between individual gene expression profiles and the module eigengene. To assess the conservation of human co-expression modules to the mouse cell type, Zsummary.pres (Z-summary of module preservation) with 500 permutations was used to represent the summary statistic of module preservation across multiple species76 (cutoff of preservation = 5). Representative hdWGCNA-derived co-expression subnetworks were visualized in Cytoscape (v 3.9.0), with node size scaled by kME.

Gene-level alignment of trajectories between human and mouse

We applied Genes2Genes77, a Bayesian information-theoretic dynamic programming framework for aligning single-cell trajectories between human and mouse. First, pseudotime values calculated from Monocle were normalized to range 0 to 1, cells were then grouped into 15 spaced pseudotime bins. We next designated the human trajectory as the reference and the mouse trajectory as the query. For gene set alignment (e.g., module genes), this framework aggregated the alignment results by averaging the gene-level alignment scores across all genes in the set, thereby summarizing the overall alignment path.

Cell-cell interaction analysis

We performed cell-cell interaction analysis using a curated ligand-receptor dataset78. For each ligand and receptor, average normalized expression levels were calculated across cell types. Ligand-receptor pairs with average expression greater than 1 (normalized counts) were retained. Ligand-receptor pairs with corresponding ligands or receptors significantly associated with sTBI stage across interacting cell types, as determined by Monocle analysis (q-value < 0.01), were included.

We also performed ligand-receptor prediction using NicheNet42. For excitatory neurons (< 24 h post-injury), genes expressed in at least 5% of cells were defined as the background expressed gene set. Candidate receptors were defined as the intersection between hub genes in M6, M9, M11 identified by hdWGCNA (kME > 0.4), and genes annotated as receptors in the ligand–receptor network. Potential ligands were selected based on hub genes identified in hOlig_ABCA1 and sTBI stage-associated genes in human microglia. Ligand activities were predicted using the predict_ligand_activities function. For each prioritized ligand, weighted ligand–target links to receptor genes were extracted to visualize putative oligodendrocyte/microglia-to-excitatory neuron signaling relationships.

Analysis of proteomics data

Mass spectrometry-based proteomics data of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived oligodendrocytes from relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) patients and controls were obtained from the ProteomeXchange Consortium (ID: PXD016043)38, comprising three independent donors per group, each profiled with two technical replicates. Protein group-level LFQ intensity data were filtered to retain proteins detected in at least two biological replicates in either condition (non-zero or non-NA values). For each biological sample, technical replicates were then averaged. The resulting protein intensity matrix was log₂-transformed, after which missing values were replaced by a Perseus-style missing-not-at-random (MNAR) imputation approach (downshift 1.8, width 0.3)38. The imputed matrix was then used for differential expression analysis between RRMS and control using the limma package (v3.46.0).

To identify proteins potentially involved in ABCA1-related processes, we calculated Spearman correlation coefficients between ABCA1 and all other proteins across biological samples. Proteins with Spearman correlation coefficients r > 0.6 and log₂FC > 0.25 (RRMS versus control) were selected for downstream functional enrichment analysis using Gene Ontology, Reactome, and WikiPathways databases.

GWAS enrichment analysis of gene co-expression modules

To evaluate whether genes within each module were enriched for stronger GWAS association signals, we performed a rank-based enrichment test. GWAS summary statistics were obtained from the following datasets: ASD46, MDD47, BP48, SCZ49, and AD50. GWAS summary statistics for each disorder were first processed using the MAGMA (v1.10)64 --bfile pipeline with the European reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3. SNPs were mapped to genes using a 35 kb upstream and 10 kb downstream window around each gene.

For each excitatory-neuron module identified by hdWGCNA, module genes were used to evaluate psychiatric disorder enrichment. For each disorder, all genes detected in ≥ 5% of excitatory neurons with gene-level statistics derived from MAGMA were ranked by GWAS association strength (-log10P). Module enrichment was assessed using permutation-based Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the fgsea package (nperm = 1000). Multiple testing correction was performed across the tested modules using the BH procedure79.

Analysis of external snRNA-seq

The ASD and AD datasets were obtained from the UCSC Cell Browser45,80. The MDD datasets were downloaded from the GEO database (GSE213982)44,81. The SCZ, BP dataset was obtained from PsychENCODE64. Neuronal subtypes were identified after CCA-based batch correction. Differential expression analysis between disease and control within each neuronal subtype of the disorder dataset was performed by a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Seurat. Genes with a |log2FC | > 0.5 and BH-adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. We performed a one-sided hypergeometric test within each Ex-subtype to evaluate the significance of the overlap between sTBI module genes and disorder-associated DEGs (disorder vs control). P-values across different disorders, Ex subtypes, and sTBI modules were corrected for multiple testing using the BH method. The background gene set consisted of the union of DEGs from all neuronal subtypes in the psychiatric disorder datasets and all Ex-subtype DEGs identified from the sTBI dataset (sTBI vs control). External mouse TBI data (GSE269748) were processed with quality control filtering and CCA-based batch correction, followed by cell type annotation, consistent with the processing of our snRNA-seq. We included only the CCI-treated samples. Microglia were selected, and hdWGCNA was performed to identify gene modules. Genes (kME > 0.4) were overlapped with CALM2 target genes predicted by GENIE3 in human microglia from our sTBI dataset82.

Cell culture and differentiation

MO3.13 cells (Procell, China; CL-0772) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin and 10 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were passaged at approximately 90% confluency using 0.05% trypsin and reseeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates. After 24 h of attachment, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 1% FBS and phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, MCE, HY-18739) at a final concentration of 100 ng/mL. Cells were differentiated for 7 days.

The human microglial cell line HMC3 (Procell, China; CL-0620) was seeded onto 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105. Cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Primary human oligodendrocytes were obtained from Aoruicell (Shanghai, ORCH401) and cultured in the proprietary primary oligodendrocyte growth medium provided by the manufacturer (ORCCMH121). Cells were seeded onto 6-well plates with glass coverslips pre-coated by poly-L-lysine (PLL, 0.1 mg/mL) at a density of 2 × 105, and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was refreshed every 3 days.

In vitro perturbation assays

Differentiated MO3.13 cells were treated with ABCA1 agonist T0901317 (MCE, HY-10626, 1 μM), T0901317 + recombinant HMGB1 protein (MCE, HY-P70570, 100 ng/mL), or T0901317 + HMGB1-neutralizing antibody (Proteintech, 10829-1-AP, 1 μg/mL) for 24 h. After treatment, the medium was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min to remove detached cells and debris.

HMC3 cells were transfected at approximately 70% confluence using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The CALM2 overexpression plasmid pCMV3-SV40-EGFP(2 A) Puro-CMV-c3×Flag-CALM2 and the corresponding control vector were purchased from Corues (Nanjing, China). For CALM2 knockdown, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) constructs cloned into the pRNA3-SV40-EGFP(2 A) Puro-U6 vector were obtained from Corues, including three targeting sequences CALM2-i1, CALM2-i2, and CALM2-i3 (Supplementary Data 9). The medium was replaced with fresh complete medium 6 h after transfection, and cells were harvested for downstream analyses 48 h post-transfection.

After primary human oligodendrocytes reached approximately 60% confluence, they were treated with LPS (Beyotime, S1732, 1 µg/mL) alone, or in combination with the ABCA1 agonist CS-6253 (MCE, HY-P6306, 1 µM) for 24 h.

Lipid and cholesterol measurements

Extracellular total cholesterol measurement

Extracellular total cholesterol was measured using the Amplex Red Cholesterol and Cholesteryl Ester Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0211S) following the manufacturer’s protocol (with cholesterol esterase for total cholesterol). Briefly, clarified conditioned medium was incubated with the reaction mixture, and fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader. Cholesterol concentrations were calculated from a standard curve (0–500 μM) generated in parallel and were reported as µM.

BODIPY staining

Cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 15 min. Following fixation, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with BODIPY 493/503 (Beyotime, C2053S) diluted 1:1000 for 20 min at room temperature in the dark to label neutral lipid droplets. After staining, cells were washed with PBS and mounted using a DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium (SouthernBiotech, 0100-20). Fluorescence images were acquired using a 63× objective under identical settings across all experimental groups. For each biological replicate (three biological replicates in total), 9 randomly selected fields of view were imaged. Lipid droplet accumulation was quantified using ImageJ by measuring fluorescence intensity with consistent thresholding parameters applied to all images.

ELISA assay

After LPS stimulation (Beyotime, S1732, 1 μg/mL) for 24 h in transfected HMC3 cells, HMGB1 levels in equal volumes of culture supernatants were quantified using an ELISA kit (Elabscience, E-EL-H1554) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA of differentiated MO3.13 cells was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (Beyotime, R0027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including column-based purification with centrifugation. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by NanoDrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of RNA (500 ng) were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, R433-01). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711-02) on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH. The primer sequences are in Supplementary Data 9.

Immunofluorescence staining and statistical analysis

sTBI tissue

All clinical samples were promptly fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h. Sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 15 min, washed three times with PBS, and blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibody combinations: (1) anti-ABCA1 (HUABIO, R1510-42, 1:100) and anti-OLIG2 (Invitrogen, MA5-15810, 1:100); (2) anti-CDH19 (Invitrogen, PA5-50923, 1:200) and anti-OLIG2; (3) anti-ITGB8 (Bioss, bs-16615R, 1:200) and anti-OLIG2. After washing three times with PBS, sections were incubated with Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (Abcam, ab150117, 1:500) and Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 647) (Abcam, ab150083, 1:500) at room temperature for 1 h. Sections were washed three times with PBS and mounted with an antifade medium containing DAPI. Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Leica) under 20× and analyzed using ImageJ software.

OLIG2⁺ nuclei were identified by thresholding and size filtering (minimum area: 20 μm²) to exclude debris and fragmented nuclei. The OLIG2-defined nuclear regions of interest (ROIs) were then expanded by 3 μm to generate perinuclear ROIs capturing cytoplasmic and membrane-associated ABCA1 signal. For each ROI, ABCA1 fluorescence was quantified as background-corrected integrated density (IntDen) and averaged per field for statistical analysis.

Cultured cells

Cells were washed three times with PBS (5 min each) and fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min. Then, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocked in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature.

For the CNP–MBP–DAPI group, cells were first incubated with anti-CNP antibody (Abcam, Ab227218, 1:100) overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, cells were incubated with Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor®488) (Jackson, 111-545-144, 1:500) at 37 °C for 1 h. Following this, anti-MBP antibody (Abcam, ab209328, 1:100) was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 594) (STARTER, S0B4007, 1:500) the next day. For the MAG-DAPI group, cells were first incubated with anti-MAG antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 9043, 1:100) overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, cells were incubated with Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 647) (Abcam, ab150083, 1:500) the next day. After antibody staining, all samples were stained with DAPI (5–10 min) and washed twice with PBS to remove excess dye. Mean fluorescence intensity for each field was quantified using ImageJ software.

Western blot

Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated with primary antibodies against GAPDH (HUABIO, ET1601-4, 1:20000), CNPase (Abcam, ab227218, 1:1000), GFAP (Abcam, ab7260, 1:1000), MBP (Cell Signaling Technology, 78896, 1:1000), and MAG (Cell Signaling Technology, 9043, 1:1000). HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, ab6721, 1:2000) was used, and signals were detected using ECL chemiluminescence reagents (Thermo Scientific). For sequential probing, membranes were stripped using Restore™ Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Thermo Scientific) and re-probed as indicated.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Fresh tissue samples from sTBI patients and controls were cut into small pieces (< 1 mm³) and fixed in TEM fixative at 4 °C. After washing three times with 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4), samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO₄) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by three additional PBS washes. Dehydration was performed using a graded ethanol series (30%–100%), followed by two changes of 100% acetone. Samples were then infiltrated and embedded in pure 812 resin, cured overnight at 37 °C, and polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h. Semi-thin sections (1.5 μm) were stained with toluidine blue for light microscopic localization. Ultrathin sections were cut and stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 8 min in the dark, followed by 2.6% lead citrate for 8 min in a CO₂-free environment. After triple rinsing with ultrapure water, sections were air-dried overnight at room temperature and imaged using a HITACHI HT7800/HT7700 transmission electron microscope.

Statistics & reproducibility

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Human sample sizes were determined based on tissue availability and clinical inclusion criteria, whereas mouse and in vitro experiments were performed using sample sizes consistent with standard practice in the field. The sample sizes were considered sufficient to support the conclusions. No data were excluded except for nuclei failing predefined quality control criteria in snRNA-seq analyses. Samples were not randomly allocated, as the study is observational in humans and based on predefined time points in mouse experiments. Where appropriate, relevant clinical covariates (including age, brain region, injury subtype, and surgical intervention) were included in statistical models. All data analyses, including sample clustering analyses were data-driven to minimize bias. Blinding was not performed. Edema volume was independently quantified by three clinicians based on CT imaging, who were blinded to the sTBI group and clinical metadata. As edema is macroscopically visible on CT imaging, complete blinding to injury severity was not possible. Reproducibility was supported by consistent findings across independent biological samples, including multiple human donors and species (humans and mice). Key observations were further supported by validation in independent public datasets and by concordant results across in vitro systems (MO3.13 cells and primary human oligodendrocytes).
