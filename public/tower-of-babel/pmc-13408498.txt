Plastinated sectional anatomy applied in CT and MRI studies of the body of the crab-eating fox (Cerdocyon thous)
Authors: Mayla Magalhães de Oliveira Alcobaça, Athelson Stefanon Bittencourt, Yuri Favalessa Monteiro, Khallil Taverna Chaim, Aureo Banhos, Flavia María Pía Montenegro Donoso, Marilú Cristofoli, Antônio Chaves de Assis Neto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The crab-eating fox (Cerdocyon thous) is a wild canid with a wide geographic distribution and a high propensity for roadkill in Brazil. Accurate diagnosis and appropriate clinical-surgical management require in-depth anatomical knowledge of the species, which is still limited for C. thous. This study aimed to correlate the sectional anatomy of C. thous through plastination, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) following the anatomical principle of metamerism to build the vertebrate body. Four animals underwent CT and MRI scans, and the images were stored in DICOM format. Then, the specimens were subjected to silicone plastination at a cold temperature using the forced impregnation technique in four stages: fixation, dehydration, impregnation, and curing. Additionally, three-dimensional rendering and multiplanar virtual dissections were performed to identify and describe the anatomical and syntopic correlations. Furthermore, the transverse slices of plastinated specimens were used to describe the anatomical details of the head, neck, thorax, abdomen, and pelvis. This study showed that correlating the plastinated sections with CT and MRI images may enhance anatomical knowledge and the reading and interpretation of imaging examinations in wild species that lack comprehensive anatomical or clinical-surgical information.

Introduction

The study of the anatomy of wild animals, far from being merely academic, holds deeper implications as it is a crucial aspect of species preservation and protection1,2. This knowledge is pivotal for accurate disease diagnosis, treatment standardization, comparative anatomy, and the selection of the most effective clinical-surgical approaches3–6. Thus, in addition to expanding scientific knowledge, it would positively impact the lives of these creatures and enable successful professional practices.

The crab-eating fox, Cerdocyon thous, belongs to the family Canidae and order Carnivora. It is a wild canid widely distributed throughout South America, including most Brazilian biomes, where it inhabits a variety of environments, demonstrating high ecological adaptability and a broad geographical range7–10. With over ten living canid species, South America holds the richest regional diversity of the family globally11,12. Within this context, the crab-eating fox stands out as one of the most adaptable and widespread species across the continent and faces constant threats because of human-animal interactions such as increasing rates of roadkill and illegal hunting2,13. It may also mediate zoonoses in humans, which necessitates an in-depth knowledge of this wild species. Acquiring anatomical knowledge would assist in accurately interpreting imaging examinations and their association with clinical and surgical practices. Additionally, it may benefit the animals that are victims of accidents.

Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are essential imaging diagnostic tools that enable the study of various areas of the body in sectional images, evaluation of images without overlaps, and observation of anatomical relationships with depth and precision14,15. Therefore, improved training of professionals in the field is necessary to interpret the results of these examinations and arrive at precise diagnosis16,17.

Plastination is a valuable educational tool for identifying anatomical components. It accurately preserves the topographic relationships of sections, which enables the production of durable, dry, and non-toxic specimens without pungent odor18–22.

Beyond rich and comprehensive anatomical documentation, this study was designed to integrate sectional plastinated anatomy with CT and MRI datasets to enable precise anatomical-imaging identification across sequential transverse levels. The plastination protocol was selected to preserve structural fidelity and minimize tissue distortion, thereby supporting reliable anatomical-imaging correlation and contributing to the understanding of cross-sectional anatomy in wild canids.

The primary scientific contribution of this study is the establishment of a sectional anatomical-imaging reference model for C. thous, integrating plastinated metameric sections with CT and MRI. Secondarily, the study demonstrates the applicability of plastination as a durable tool for systematic anatomical-imaging correlation and quantifies tissue shrinkage to assess the anatomical reliability of plastinated material. This framework provides a reference for anatomical and imaging studies in C. thous, with potential applicability to other canids. Furthermore, the methodological approach described here is reproducible and may be extended to other species, supporting broader applications in comparative anatomy and anatomical imaging.

We hypothesized that transverse plastinated sections prepared using cold-temperature silicone impregnation would preserve anatomical fidelity with limited tissue distortion, enabling consistent anatomical-imaging correlation with CT and MRI across sequential metameric levels of the entire body.

Therefore, this study aimed to (i) integrate plastinated cross-sectional anatomy with CT and MRI datasets of the head, neck, thorax, abdomen, and pelvis of Cerdocyon thous; (ii) perform systematic cross-modality identification of anatomical structures following the metameric organization of the vertebrate body; and (iii) quantify plastination-related changes (volume, weight, and thickness) to support the reliability of sectional plastinated material as a durable anatomical-imaging reference for wild canids.

Materials and methods

Animals

This project was approved by the Ethics Committee on the Use of Animals (CEUA) of the School of Veterinary Medicine and Animal Science (FMVZ) of the University of São Paulo (USP) [processes 4143100220], Federal University of Espírito Santo [processes 31/2019], and Biodiversity Authorization and Information System (SISBIO) [processes 31762-5 and 78227-3]. All experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines and regulations.

Three adult females and one male that were run over were used in this study. The specimens were frozen and stored immediately after death for up to 1 year. They were thawed slowly at 7–10 °C for 48 h before performing the imaging and plastination procedures.

Acquisition of imaging exams

Both male and female specimens were subjected to CT and MRI examinations. The animals were placed on the examination table in the prone position and in the most symmetrical manner possible. The images were acquired in the craniocaudal direction from the tip of the snout to the tip of the tail. The images obtained from the examinations were saved in DICOM format for later analysis.

CT data were acquired using two scanners. Whole-body CT examinations were performed on a Philips Brilliance 64 system using helical acquisition at 120 kVp, with a tube current of 330 mA, exposure time of 940 ms, and CTDIvol of 51 mGy. Images were reconstructed with a 512 × 512 matrix and in-plane pixel spacing of 0.488 × 0.488 mm, with a slice thickness of 1.0 mm and slice increment of 0.5 mm. The reconstruction diameter was 250 mm. Two reconstruction series were generated: a bone series reconstructed using convolution kernel D and displayed with a window center of 600 HU and window width of 4095 HU, and a soft-tissue series reconstructed using convolution kernel B and displayed with a window center of 30 HU and window width of 400 HU. CT images were reconstructed using rescale settings of intercept − 1024 and slope 1.

The selection of reconstruction kernels and window settings was based on the of optimizing tissue-specific attenuation characterization. The high-frequency convolution kernel D was selected to enhance cortical bone edge definition and spatial resolution, facilitating precise evaluation of osseous structures. In contrast, the soft-tissue kernel B was applied to improve contrast resolution in muscular and visceral tissues by reducing image noise and enhancing subtle attenuation differences. Window center and width values were adjusted to maximize differentiation between bone, soft tissues, and air-containing structures. These reconstruction strategies enabled accurate distinction of anatomy based on their intrinsic attenuation properties expressed in Hounsfield units.

In addition, lung parenchyma CT examinations were acquired using a Toshiba Aquilion 64 system with helical CT at 120 kVp, tube current of 40 mA, and exposure time of 500 ms. The acquisition protocol included slice thickness of 2.0 mm and reconstruction diameter of 181.64 mm, with convolution kernel FC52. Images were reconstructed with a 512 × 512 matrix and in-plane pixel spacing of 0.354 × 0.354 mm. Parenchymal display settings were adjusted to a window center of − 550 HU and window width of 1600 HU. The FC52 kernel and lung window settings were selected to enhance visualization of pulmonary parenchymal architecture by accentuating low-attenuation air spaces and fine structural details, thereby improving differentiation between bronchi, vessels, and surrounding tissues.

MRI datasets were acquired on both 7 T and Philips Achieva 1.5 T systems. High-field MRI was performed on a Siemens investigational 7 T system operating at 6.98 T. T2-weighted turbo spin-echo sequences were selected to optimize soft tissue contrast and enhance anatomical delineation for correlation, as T2 weighting increased sensitivity to differences in water content and tissue microstructure. These sequences were acquired with the following parameters: TR 5000 ms, TE 41 ms, slice thickness 3.0 mm, slice spacing 3.0 mm, acquisition matrix 640 × 384, and in-plane pixel spacing 0.40 × 0.40 mm; and TR 6500 ms, TE 39 ms, slice thickness 2.0 mm, slice spacing 2.0 mm, acquisition matrix 320 × 320, and in-plane pixel spacing 0.40 × 0.40 mm. The selected TR and TE values allowed appropriate T2 contrast while preserving signal-to-noise ratio and spatial resolution for detailed anatomical evaluation.

Conventional MRI was performed on a Philips Achieva 1.5 T system. For the T2-weighted series, acquisition parameters included TR 3499.9998 ms, TE 120 ms, slice thickness 5.0 mm, slice spacing 5.4 mm, acquisition matrix 480 × 480, and in-plane pixel spacing 0.2917 × 0.2917 mm. For the T1-weighted series, parameters included TR 1581.6368 ms, TE 15 ms, slice thickness 7.0 mm, slice spacing 7.7 mm, acquisition matrix 560 × 560, and in-plane pixel spacing 0.8929 × 0.8929 mm.

The combined use of T1- and T2-weighted sequences was intentionally adopted to characterize differences in tissue signal intensity based on proton density and relaxation properties, enabling identification of variations in tissue weighting across bone marrow, musculature, connective tissues, and pulmonary structures. T1-weighted imaging enhanced structural delineation and anatomical boundary definition, whereas T2-weighted imaging emphasized differences in water content and tissue composition. This multimodal approach allowed precise anatomical differentiation by exploiting intrinsic signal contrast variations, facilitating accurate recognition of specific anatomical structures and their morphological particularities through grayscale signal differences. Slice thickness, matrix size, and in-plane resolution were selected to balance spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio while ensuring consistent correspondence between imaging planes and sections.

All acquisition and reconstruction parameters were extracted directly from DICOM metadata to ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility.

Plastination

The specimens were sectioned and subjected to silicone plastination by forced impregnation at cold temperature (− 25 °C) using a modified method described by von Hagens, Tiedemann, and Kriz18. Throughout all stages of the plastination process, environmental and technical parameters such as temperature, pressure, solvent concentration, and solution volumes were carefully monitored and controlled to ensure methodological consistency and preservation of anatomical integrity.

Fixation

After thawing the specimens for 48 h at a 2–6 °C, the animals were positioned with four limbs extended and tail elongated in a longitudinal direction to the body and subjected to fixation in 10% formalin. Fixation was performed at controlled room temperature (22 °C). Formalin was injected with a syringe and needle, followed by immersion for 4 weeks. Formalin was made to infiltrate directly into the major muscle groups and thoracic and abdominal cavities to ensure uniform and rapid fixation.

The specimens were immersed in large volumes of formalin solution to prevent depletion of fixative and to ensure complete tissue penetration throughout the fixation period. The volume of formalin was monitored weekly to ensure that the specimens remained fully submerged and that no reduction in solution volume compromised tissue preservation.

After the fixation period, the freezing step was performed by cooling the specimens in a refrigerator for 5 days at 5 °C, followed by transfer to a freezer at − 25 °C for 1 week.

Then, the specimens were embedded in polyurethane (PU) to facilitate and standardize sectioning adapting the method described by Monteiro et al.22 for use in animals. Components "A" and "B" of polyurethane were mixed in equal proportions (1:1) and poured into a custom-made tight box containing the frozen specimen such that the entire animal was immersed in the compound-hardened PU. The specimen embedded in the PU block was frozen at − 25 °C for another 7 days.

On successful completion of the blocking and freezing stages, a band saw (Skymsen, type SSI no 1974) was used to section the animals into 13-mm thick transverse slices that were systematically labeled to preserve the sequence and anatomical position of the slices for easy identification later. Next, the sections were thawed in a 10% formalin solution for 12 h and subjected to bleaching.

Dehydration

The slices were immersed in 10 volumes (3%) of a hydrogen peroxide bleaching solution for 24 h at 20–22 °C. Then, the sections were placed in running water for 12 h to remove the peroxide. Dehydration was achieved through 4 weekly immersion baths in pure acetone (100%) at low temperature (− 25 °C). Temperature was strictly controlled at -25 °C to minimize tissue shrinkage and distortion during Dehydration. After each immersion bath, acetone concentration was monitored using an acetonometer. Dehydration was considered complete only when acetone concentration exceeded 99% (v/v), ensuring effective water replacement prior to silicone impregnation.

Impregnation

The dehydrated slices were subjected to forced impregnation in a vacuum chamber at cold temperature (− 18 °C). The slices were immersed in a mixture comprising the low viscosity silicone (Poliplast 1 - P1; Polisil®, Brazil) polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and catalyst dibutyl tin dilaurate (DBTL) in a 100:1 ratio (m/m) for 24 h. The progression and evolution of vacuum were continuously monitored using both mercury and digital manometers to ensure controlled pressure reduction23,24. The progression and evolution of vacuum were measured using a mercury and digital manometer. This step was considered completed in the absence of bubbles on the silicone surface and when maximum vacuum was achieved by the pump. Forced impregnation step lasted 28 days and reaches a minimum pressure (maximum vacuum) of 6 mmHg.

Curing

The impregnated sections were subjected to natural drainage at room temperature (20–25 °C) for 4 weeks. Room temperature was monitored to maintain stability during drainage and prevent premature polymerization. Then, the sections were placed in a curing chamber for volatilizing in the crosslinking compound tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), which promotes crosslinking between the silicone molecules and makes them solid. Curing was performed under controlled environmental conditions to ensure uniform crosslinking. The curing of the pieces was completed after 2 days of volatilization.

Measurements and statistical analysis

The difference in volume is the best measure for quantifying shrinkage of samples during plastination25. The shrinkage of the plastinated parts was evaluated by measuring the volume in milliliters (mL) in graduated vats, weight in grams (g) on a calibrated scale, and thickness in centimeters (cm) using an analog caliper at the four ends of the sections (the mean the four measurements was used). The measured time was indicated as t1 before dehydration and t2 after curing. The values were expressed as arithmetic means, and (difference) t1 − t2 was calculated at 95% confidence interval.

The normality of data distribution was verified for all analyzed variables and anatomical regions using the Shapiro–Wilk test prior to parametric analysis.

To assess tissue changes, a one-tailed paired Student’s t-test was used to compare different samples (Ha: t1 > t2; hence, t1 − t2 is > 0 for volume, weight, and thickness; H0: t1 ≤ t2; hence, t1 − t2 is ≤ 0). The use of a one-tailed test was justified by a priori directional hypothesis, as the dehydration and curing stages of plastination are widely reported to induce tissue shrinkage rather than expansion. Accordingly, we tested whether the difference (t1 − t2) was significantly greater than zero.

Statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05. P-values were interpreted as the probability of the null hypothesis (H0: t1 ≤ t2) being true. Additionally, we calculated the percentage retraction or reduction for each sample using the formula ((t1 − t2)/t1) × 100, summarized by the mean reduction and standard deviation.

Photodocumentation and image processing

The caudal face of each sliced metamer was photographed using a Canon EOS Rebel T3i professional digital camera with an EF-S 18-135 mm lens. Subsequently, these images were correlated with the CT and MRI images obtained in DICOM format and identified using the RadiAnt DICOM Viewer26 and 3D Slicer27 software. The images were used for multiplanar three-dimensional reconstruction using different filters and for segmenting structures. Finally, virtual dissections and the identification of the anatomical structures of each related image were performed according to the Veterinary Anatomical Nomina28. The images were all presented in caudal view, which moved the right side of the images to the viewer’s right, to facilitate assimilation and identification.

Ethical Approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee on the Use of Animals (CEUA) of the School of Veterinary Medicine and Animal Science (FMVZ) at the University of São Paulo (USP) [process no. 4143100220], the Federal University of Espírito Santo [process no. 31/2019], and the Biodiversity Authorization and Information System (SISBIO) [processes no. 31762-5 and 78227-3]. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines and ethical standards. No live animals were used, and no animals were euthanized for this research. The study was conducted exclusively on cadavers of animals that had died due to traffic accidents.

Results

Analysis of plastinated sections

After performing plastination, the specimen slices were obtained corresponding to the head, neck, thorax, abdomen, and pelvis. These slices were suitable for manipulation because they exhibited rigid characteristics, and the anatomical structures that were fixed in each section did not loosen or fall apart.

The sections exhibited a precise and uniform color without evidence of tissue degradation or post-mortem marks. Furthermore, they did not emit any pungent or unpleasant odors and showed olfactory characteristics similar to those of post-catalytic silicone (plastic).

The slices helped elucidate the anatomy of the species by enabling easy and complete identification and differentiation of the structures. They maintained visual characteristics similar to those of fresh specimens, and showed color variation according to the characteristics of the muscle, adipose, and bone tissues (Fig. 1). Anatomical description

The nasal septum was identified at the start of the nostril; it vertically separated the structures that formed the left and right nasal cavities and extended to the most caudal region of the choanal cavity. Nasal meatuses are cavities between the nasal turbinates but not between the nasal cartilages. The nasal cartilages were visualized through all techniques, and the spaces between them formed the nasal orifices (Fig. 7, 8, and 9).

Images obtained from plastination, CT, and MRI techniques showed the internal lamina of the incisor bone surrounding the dental parts of the first and second upper incisor teeth on both sides (right and left). The incisor bone represents the floor of the nasal cavity and the roof of the oral cavity rostrally. The nasal bone follows the incisor and also forms the roof of the nasal cavity (Fig. 7 and 8).

The maxillary bone comprises the floor of the nasal cavity and roof of the oral cavity. In the latter, it is visualized in the dorsolateral region and extends caudally to the beginning of the palatine bone (Fig. 7 and 8).

The two parts of the mandible meet to form the mandibular symphysis, which is evident in all three techniques used. Additionally, the tongue was easily identified among the mandibular bodies in the oral cavity (Fig. 7 and 8).

The upper premolars were identified to be attached to the dental alveoli of the maxilla, and the lower premolars were attached to the dental alveoli of the mandible (Fig. 7 and 8).

The frontal bone and start of the medial and lateral frontal sinuses were identified in the lateral and dorsal regions of the ethmoturbinate bones (Fig. 9). Lateral to the temporal bone, the ocular bulb, lens, and the start of the medial pterygoid muscle were identified. Next, the nasopharyngeal meatus, which was represented by a flat circular structure (Fig. 9), was identified at the midline.

The frontal and dorsal lobes of the cerebral hemisphere and the well-defined frontal sinus were visualized. The passage of the ophthalmic plexus was identified between the choana and frontal lobe. A pair of circular structures was located on each side of the metamere views in the dorsolateral region of the nasopharyngeal meatus, and the medial pterygoid muscle were located adjacent and dorsal to it. The zygomatic arch was visible at the lateral ends; medial to it, the temporal and masseter muscles were observed more ventrally. The mandibular lymph nodes were identified lateral to the mandibular bones with a circular contour; these features were easy to identify both on MRI and in plastinated specimens. However, they were not as visible on the CT images (Fig. 9).

The differentiation of the tongue muscles and surrounding areas were well elucidated and easily identifiable in the MRI images. The division of the styloglossus, hyoglossus, and genioglossus muscles was identified by their subtle separation and differences in the attenuation of the structures, which, despite possessing homogeneity in the attenuation of each structure, differed between them. However, this identification was less clear on CT and plastination images (Fig. 9 and 10).

In the brain, structures such as the frontal lobe and longitudinal fissure were visible in the plastinated specimens and MRI images. Other structures were identified on the CT images, including the alar foramen, orbital fissure, branch of the mandibular notch, zygomatic arch, and skull bone. Bones corresponding to the skull, such as the parietal bone and temporal bone, and musculature of this region were identified in the plastinated pieces. The MRI image of this region showed the division of the muscle belly, which aided in the identification of each muscle in the section. Furthermore, brain structures such as the corpus callosum and diencephalon were evident. Thus, plastination and MRI made the structures visible and easily identifiable (Fig. 11).

The tympanic bulla was identified in the specimen and CT image, where its bony contour was visible, and its structure was delimited just below the midbrain. The external auditory canal was visible primarily in the plastinated piece, and the digastric muscle was located ventral to it. The temporal muscle was identified along the dorsolateral region of the head; its muscles primarily permeate the lateral region, lateral dorsum of the skull bones, and the brain. The parotid gland was visible just below the external canal of the ear and appeared to be extensive and occupying a large portion of this region. Just lower to it, the mandibular gland was visible in both the plastinated image and MRI with a circular and well-defined shape; however, its shape made it impossible to be identified on the CT image (Fig. 12 and 13).

The seventh cervical vertebra was identified as the last cervical vertebra (Fig. 14). This section highlights the transverse canal and ventral muscles of the neck, including the omotransverse, sternocephalic, and brachiocephalic muscles and their cleidocephalic and cervical portions. The esophagus was identified as a circular structure immediately to the left side of the trachea in both the plastinated section and CT and MRI images (Fig. 14).

The scapula and its bony delimitations were easily identified and visualized as were the spine of the scapula, scapular fossa, and adjacent supporting muscles such as the infraspinatus muscle, supraspinatus muscle, thoracic part of the rhomboid muscle, ventral serratus muscle of the thorax, and subscapularis muscle (Fig. 15).

The left lung lobe was identified as the first lung lobe on craniocaudal evaluation of the plastinated sections; it appeared juxtaposed in the medial region of the thoracic cavity (Fig. 15) and was followed by the caudal and cranial portions of the same lobe. The right cranial and middle lobes were on the right side of the metamere and right caudal lobe (Fig. 16 and 17). The trachea (Fig. 12, 13, 14, and 15) and its division into the right and left main bronchi (Fig. 16) were visualized after correlating the plastinated, CT, and MRI images.

Cardiac structures, such as the right and left atria, vena cava, and aorta, were distinctly visualized in the metamer figures (Fig. 17 and 18). The right ventricle followed the vena cava on the right and ventral sides; on the left side, the aorta was observed ventral to the left ventricle and transversely sectioned (Fig. 17 and 18).

After delimiting the myocardium, the pericardium was visualized as a thin film juxtaposed with the heart. In correlation with the CT and MR images, the structures were repeated, and the liquid was deposited inside the cardiac chambers owing to its hyperattenuation and delimitation of its adjacent structures (Fig. 17).

The esophagus passed through all sections from the cervical region to the thorax (Fig. 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, and 16) and entered the stomach (Fig. 18). The stomach comprised a large part of the left antimere. Initially, the gastric fundus was visualized with the gastric contents present. In the liver, part of the hepatic lobes (left lateral and medial) was still visualized in the plastinated metameres, as shown in Fig. 18. Hepatic lobation continued to be visible in the following sections: the right medial and lateral hepatic lobes and quadrate lobe (Fig. 19).

The spleen was located in the left antimere and passed through the abdominal cavity sections. It was always lateralized and close to the left abdominal wall (Fig. 18 and 19).

The kidneys were visualized in metameric sections using these techniques. The right kidney, a part of the pelvis, and a small portion of the cranial pole of the left kidney were visible in the same section (Fig. 20). The renal pelvis of the left kidney is described in the next section, and the conformation between them defines them as being similar to a bean grain (Fig. 21).

The anatomical components of the intestinal tract, such as the duodenum (descending and ascending) and colon, were identified in the abdominal cavity. Their structural characteristics and circular and tubular conformations were distinct and visible in correlation with the CT and MRI images (Fig. 20, 21, and 22). The jejunal loops and mesentery were surrounded by adipose tissue, which facilitated their differentiation due to attenuation on CT and MRI images (Fig. 21 and 22).

The final portion of the descending colon terminated in the rectum and was identified in the sections shown in Fig. 23 (Fig. 22 and 23). In the anal region, the adrenal gland was positioned laterally on the left and right sides and juxtaposed with the sternal anal sphincter muscle. The urethra could be identified in the sections from both male and female specimens (Fig. 23).

The penis was visible in the ventral region of the caudal abdomen, whereas the scrotum, testicles, and adjacent structures were visible in the scrotal region (Fig. 23 and 24). The bladder neck was visualized in the female specimen, and the urethra was dorsal. The last section (Fig. 24a, b, c) revealed the vulva, which was visualized through all three techniques, although the plastinated figure showed hair coverage. The anus was identified in both the female and male specimens using all three techniques (Fig. 24).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated, for the first time, that several anatomical structures of the crab-eating fox (C. thous), a wild canid species, can be identified using CT and MRI imaging examinations of the head, neck, thorax, abdomen, and pelvis when correlated with plastinated metameric slices. This integrative anatomical-imaging approach establishes a structured reference framework that supports both scientific investigation and educational applications.

The crab-eating fox is frequently affected by roadkill incidents in Brazil29–33, which contributes to biodiversity loss and creates a demand for improved veterinary training in trauma management and diagnostic imaging. In this context, accurate interpretation of imaging examinations depends on detailed knowledge of sectional anatomy. However, anatomical references for wild canids remain limited, reinforcing the relevance of the present anatomical-imaging correlation.

Advanced imaging methods such as CT and MRI enable detailed morphological assessment and multiplanar visualization. However, knowledge of sectional anatomy and its correlation these images are fundamental to successful clinical outcomes. Therefore, such anatomical correlations, as presented here, are essential for studies on this species or other closely related canids. According to Ford et al.34, a key advantage of CT and MRI is the possibility of multiplanar visualization. Thus, the animal may be viewed three-dimensionally at any angle and under any degree of rotation, and the handler may opt for the preferred position depending on the target anatomical structures being inspected.

Roadkill specimens typically exhibit trauma to organs and bones. Depending on the extent of the injury, these may be incompatible with microscopic or macroscopic studies. However, studying preserved animals with normal anatomy improves the anatomical knowledge of wild species and reduces the use of animals in research35 thereby aligning with ethical principles by minimizing the need for live animal use.

Our CT and MRI provided detailed three-dimensional visualization. Thus, these technologies significantly contribute to comparative anatomy studies. Vieira et al.36 mention that this type of technology complements the use of cadavers in anatomical medical studies and primarily benefits professionals in the fields of human and animal health. Possible restrictions may be imposed on the use of cadavers in research, especially with regard to potentially contagious corpses, such as during the SARS-COV-2 pandemic; however, this technology may negate this issue because virtual reconstruction enables the virtual manipulation of cadavers for inspection, and studies may be conducted without the possible risks of propagation of pathogenic biological agents37. This aspect of virtual autopsy not only facilitates virtual dissection and manipulation of structures but also ensures biosafety of researchers.

The results presented in the current study can be used for training personnel in virtual autopsy of unconventional animal species that live in the wild. According to Poulsen et al.38 and Kanchan et al.39 certain favorable aspects of virtual autopsy, such as the absence of cadaveric destruction, non-invasiveness of methods for accessing bodily structures, repeatability of actions, reevaluation, and reinterpretation, cannot be achieved in a traditional autopsy. Furthermore, identification of skeletal changes and accumulation of gases or liquids, such as pneumothorax and hemothorax40–42, and detection of gas emboli21 may be possible through virtual autopsy. These advantages may result in better evaluation of biological materials and greater assertiveness in diagnoses and reports than that achieved with traditional autopsy.

Therefore, vitopsy is a remarkably versatile tool that is applicable to various bodies, tissues, and regions of different species. Its utility extends beyond anatomical examination of the crab-eating fox (C. thous) (presented in this study), to other species of wild animals. Examples include the Boa constrictor (Boa constrictor) diagnosed with inclusion body disease43, the Indo-Pacific humpback dolphin (Sousa chinensis) with trauma incurred from collision with vessels44, and even humans infected with biological agents such as SARS COV-237 or requiring evaluation of cerebral edema45. This wide applicability reinforces the relevance of the proposed anatomical-imaging framework in both veterinary and comparative contexts.

The analysis of plastinated pieces of crab-eating fox facilitates an anatomical understanding of the specimen. Furthermore, it aids in the anatomical interpretation of the results of other diagnostic imaging techniques such as CT and MRI by enabling a clear and easy syntopic and topographic correlation of each region for a significantly enhanced understanding of the anatomy. In the study conducted by Haque et al.46 on the assimilation and understanding of anatomy by medical students using plastinated specimens, most students preferred to study with plastinated pieces because it facilitated a three-dimensional perception of anatomy.

Baker et al.47 used traditionally dissected anatomical and plastinated pieces to teach dentistry students the functional and clinical applications of each structure. When plastination replaced dissected specimens in conjunction with several other study modalities, students obtained a high percentage of 91.4% on national exams, which reduced failure rates in anatomical sciences. The experiment demonstrates the advantage of applying plastinated pieces together with other anatomical study modalities to the teaching field. Additionally, the present study demonstrates that the integration of CT, MRI, and plastination may further enhance learning outcomes by combining physical and virtual modalities, promoting a more comprehensive understanding of anatomical structures.

Nevertheless, the major disadvantage of implementing the plastination technique is its high costs; however, this is outweighed by the advantages, including its positive contribution to the study of anatomy. Fontoura et al.48 compared different anatomical techniques and highlighted the significant favorable characteristics of plastination. These include low toxicity, no maintenance requirements, high resistance and durability, lack of odor, and preservation of original characteristics. In the present study, the plastinated pieces prepared from the crab-eating fox provide rich detail and are highly durable for studying the specimens.

Although this study focused on C. thous, the preservation performance observed during silicone plastination suggests broader applicability. The minimal residual dehydration, controlled reduction in weight and volume, and maintenance of anatomical relationships indicate high structural fidelity,precise anatomical-imaging correlation with computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging. Such characteristics support the potential use of plastination as a long-term anatomical preservation strategy for wild species, including those that are rare or difficult to obtain. In contexts where specimen acquisition is limited, sectional plastinated material may serve as a durable, long-lasting anatomical archive, preserving morphological characteristics and enabling continued anatomical-imaging integration. The quantitative parameters evaluated in this study reinforce the feasibility of plastination not only as a tool for anatomical documentation, but also as a durable anatomical-imaging reference framework.

Thus, the primary contribution of this study is the establishment of an anatomical-imaging reference model for C. thous, integrating plastinated metameric sections with CT and MRI. Secondarily, plastination proved to be a viable and durable tool for systematic anatomical-imaging correlation, and the measured tissue shrinkage of 11.29% in volume and 6.17% in thickness provides evidence of the anatomical reliability of the plastinated material without compromising essential topographic relationships. This framework serves as a reference for anatomical and imaging studies in C. thous, with potential applicability to other canids. In addition, the methodological approach described here may be replicated in other species, supporting broader applications in comparative anatomy and anatomical imaging.

However, in the present study, the sections showed a reduction in volume, weight, and thickness, and each analyzed region showed different maximum and minimum percentage shrinkage. These variations may be attributed to the different structural characteristics of each region, such as the type of musculature, presence of adipose tissue, and the different organs and bones present in each region. These findings corroborate those described by Henry23 and Sora et al.49. They reported varying degrees of retraction in different biological tissues for different polymers such as silicone, epoxy, and polyester.

Importantly, although statistically significant reductions in volume, weight, and thickness were observed, the shrinkage did not compromise anatomical interpretation or the correlation with CT and MRI images. The preservation of structural fidelity, as well as essential topographic and syntopic relationships across metameric sections, indicates that the observed shrinkage has limited practical impact on anatomical analysis. Therefore, plastinated specimens can be considered reliable for anatomical evaluation and for correlation with imaging modalities, as their rigid characteristics and absence of tissue degradation allow precise correspondence with digital datasets.

To date, no studies have reported the standardization of the maximum permitted degree of retraction in plastination. This may be attributed to the significant variability in retraction in different tissues and the increasing and diverse applications of plastination. Nevertheless, some factors intrinsic to the technique increase or reduce shrinkage. For example, dehydration at room temperature results in increased shrinkage, whereas dehydration at cold temperatures results in minimal shrinkage25. Similarly, varying silicone viscosities affect shrinkage percentage22.

Studies that have correlated anatomical plastinated sections with CT or MRI results are rare. Some recent studies correlated the results of three techniques; example: description of the head of the Rhinoceros Iguana (Cyclura cornuta cornuta)50 and section of the blue shark (Triakis scyllium)51. Another study used only one of the imaging techniques (MRI) to analyze the head of the Atlantic puffin (Fratercula arctica)52. Such studies provide unique and rich information on metameria. However, none of the existing studies have used plastination, which makes the present study on crab-eating fox unique and unprecedented. Moreover, it has provided specimen pieces that are rich in detail and suitable for handling during the study.

The association of CT and MRI technologies with plastination provides an essential advantage for students and professionals in diagnostic imaging and anatomists through the significant enhancement of learning curve, reading, and interpretation of CT and MRI images. This concurs with the findings of the study by Jinga et al.53, in which students demonstrated significant improvement in understanding anatomy and showed increased confidence in interpreting CT reports after the segmentation of spine structures generated by CT imaging. Moreover, studies by Squelch54, Erolin55, and Ye et al.56 demonstrated significant improvements in the identification and understanding of anatomical structures when using three-dimensional virtual parts generated by CT and MRI.

Other studies have established that physical models increase stimulation and interest among students and significantly improve their understanding of anatomy57. The use of virtual tools such as CT and MRI, which provide three-dimensional reconstruction and segmentation, are unique methods that refine anatomical knowledge and enhance satisfaction and engagement among students58,59. The association of the three techniques in the present study combines the benefits of using virtual and physical materials, as previously mentioned. It facilitates the use of different forms of learning, namely, virtual and physical, to provide a better understanding of the structure and fixation of the study content.

A study on the anatomical analysis and identification of sections from a dog showed that some structures are best observed using certain techniques. For example, bones were best observed using CT bone windowing and liquids using T2-weighted MRI. This may be attributed to the physical characteristics of each exam (such as X-radiation used in tomography and magnetic fields and electromagnetic waves used in resonance) and the processing, windowing, and weighting characteristics.

Ayesa et al.60 highlighted another important issue that needs to be addressed, which is the gap between professionals and students in basic medical imaging training. Many participants did not demonstrate a critical role in interpreting exams, which necessitates better medical imaging education at all levels of training. The sections generated by the correlation of plastinated, CT, and MRI images (as in the case of the crab-eating fox (C. thous)) are an effective teaching and study tool for training students, professionals, and specialists. Thus, this study proposes a new pedagogy for teaching anatomy.

Compared to domestic dogs (Canis familiaris), C. thous presents significant similarities and important differences with clinical implications. The axial skeleton (7 cervical vertebrae, 13 thoracic, 7 lumbar; 2–3 sacral) resembles that of dogs, facilitating the interpretation of trauma imaging exams. However, C. thous presents spinous processes positioned more cranially in the cervicolumbar regions, corroborating what was described by Barisson et al.61.

Additionally, in the present study, we observed that the orbits of C. thous appear relatively shallower than those described in domestic dogs, potentially increasing susceptibility to cranial trauma. According to Lantyer-Araujo et al.62, computed tomography reveals comparable extraocular muscles and nictitating gland morphology between wild and domestic canids. However, differences in retrobulbar fat contours may influence both imaging interpretation and surgical approaches.

A limitation of the present study is the relatively small sample size, which restricts a comprehensive assessment of intraspecific anatomical variability, including possible sex- and age-related differences. Nevertheless, the metameric correlations consistently observed across specimens indicate a stable anatomical pattern, supporting the reliable identification of anatomical structures. For a wild species such as C. thous, this limitation is offset by the relevance of the study and its contribution to addressing significant gaps in the current literature. Comparable investigations based on a single domestic specimen, such as the study Czeibert et al.63 on canine brain anatomy, have established important anatomical references despite similarly limited sample sizes.

Despite the valuable anatomical and imaging correlations presented, this study has limitations that must be considered. The sample size was limited to four individuals, reflecting the constraints inherent to studies involving wild species and the use of roadkill-derived specimens. This limitation restricts the assessment of intraspecific variability, including potential influences of sex, body size, and individual anatomical variation. Similar constraints have been reported in studies involving wild animals, largely due to ethical and logistical challenges64.

The use of roadkill specimens introduces the possibility of post-traumatic alterations. To mitigate this, anatomical analyses were restricted to structurally preserved regions, minimizing the impact of tissue distortion or early post-mortem changes. Nevertheless, such alterations cannot be entirely excluded, as the degree of tissue preservation may vary depending on factors such as post-mortem interval and trauma severity65.

Furthermore, this study was not designed to evaluate morphometric variation or to perform comparisons between sexes. Although both sexes were included, the sample distribution was not sufficient to support comparative analyses. Therefore, no inferences regarding sex-related or size-related anatomical variability were made.

Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as a descriptive anatomical-imaging reference model rather than a representation of population-level variability. Future studies including larger sample sizes and more balanced sex distributions will be important to validate and expand these observations. Notably, anatomical-imaging reference models based on limited samples remain valuable for advancing structural understanding, particularly in species for which anatomical data are scarce63.

Conclusion

This study establishes a sectional anatomical-imaging reference model for C. thous by integrating plastinated metameric sections with computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging. This approach enhances anatomical interpretation and supports its application in veterinary education and diagnostic imaging. The plastinated specimens demonstrated structural preservation and anatomical consistency, and although measurable reductions in volume, weight, and thickness were observed, these did not compromise anatomical interpretation or imaging correlation. However, the findings should be interpreted as a descriptive anatomical-imaging reference derived from a limited sample. Nevertheless, the proposed framework provides a reproducible foundation for future studies and may be extended to other species, supporting advances in comparative anatomy and imaging-based investigations.
