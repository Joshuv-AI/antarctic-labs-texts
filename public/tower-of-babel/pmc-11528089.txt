LINC00342 regulates the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway via the miR-149-5p/FGF11 axis and affects the progression of oral cancer
Authors: Yimiao Chen, Yanchun Wang, Wenbo Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA large number of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have been implicated in the progression of oral cancer (OC). This study aimed to investigate the role of a novel lncRNA, LINC00342, in OC and elucidate its molecular mechanism.MethodsDifferential expression of lncRNA/miRNA/mRNA was analyzed using the Gene Expression Omnibus database and validated with RT-qPCR. Additionally, the expression levels of these molecules in OC cells and their effects on cell viability and cell cycle were assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 and flow cytometry. RNA bindings was analyzed by dual luciferase, and Western blot was used to detect the activation of relevant pathways.ResultsThis study showed that, in contrast to miR-149-5p, the expression of LINC00342 and fibroblast growth factor 11 (FGF11) were upregulated in OC cells (LINC00342: 10.00 ± 1.06 (FaDu) and 3.55 ± 0.25 (CAL-27) vs 1.00 ± 0.07 (HOECs), P < 0.05; FGF11: 7.31 ± 0.33 (FaDu) and 3.43 ± 0.08 (CAL-27) vs 1.00 ± 0.10 (HOECs), P < 0.05). Dual-luciferase assays confirmed that LINC00342 bind to miR-149-5p in a direct targeting manner. Furthermore, inhibition of LINC00342 expression resulted in decreased proliferation rate (FaDu: 136.22 ± 22.10% vs 59.36 ± 8.98% (control), P < 0.05; CAL-27: 131.40 ± 11.58% vs 49.83 ± 11.19 (control), P < 0.05) and migration ability of OC cells, cell cycle arrest in G1 phase, and inhibition of PI3K-AKT signaling. Inhibition of miR-149-5p or overexpression of FGF11 reversed the effects of si-LINC00342.ConclusionsLINC00342 promotes PI3K-AKT signaling by activating FGF11 through adsorption of miR-149-5p, thereby regulating the progression of OC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01457-4.Keywords: Oral cancer, LINC00342, MiR-149-5p, FGF11, PI3K/AKT

Introduction

Oral cancer (OC) is one of the most common cancers [1, 2], with squamous cell carcinoma accounting for more than 90% of cases [3]. Lack of awareness and late presentation lead to high mortality rates among patients in underdeveloped and developing countries [4–6]. Early detection and timely treatment are crucial because OC is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage, and once distant migration of cancer cells occurs [7], the prognosis is extremely poor [8]. Therefore, OC tumor markers have important clinical value for elucidating the mechanism of initiation and metastasis and for developing new therapeutic methods.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are RNA transcripts larger than 200 nucleotides that do not encode proteins. Specific lncRNAs, such as CYTOR and XIST, have been shown to regulate proliferation and distal migration of cancer cells in oral carcinogenesis, thereby profoundly influencing the progression of OC [9, 10]. Furthermore, lncRNAs can interact with miRNAs as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), affecting various disease processes, including diabetic nephropathy [11] and rectal cancer [12]. In addition, an increasing number of studies have demonstrated that lncRNA can act as molecular sponges to adsorb miRNAs, thus influencing OC development [13]. LINC00342 has been extensively studied and shown to promote malignancy by participating in ceRNA networks in many cancers, including non-small cell lung cancer [14], head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [15], gastric cancer [16], and colorectal cancer [17]. LINC00342 promotes non-small cell lung cancer progression by functioning as a ceRNA that competitively binds to miR-203a-3p, thereby relieving the suppression of miR-203a-3p's target genes, which ultimately enhances cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion [14]. For another example, in HNSCC, LINC00342 expression positively correlates with cervical lymph node metastasis and pathological grade, and its knockdown inhibits cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and promotes apoptosis, indicating its oncogenic role. This study also demonstrated LINC00342's role in HNSCC malignant progression through a ceRNA network involving 10 downregulated microRNAs and 647 upregulated mRNAs [15]. Similarly, in gastric cancer (GC), LINC00342 regulates cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by targeting miR-596, as confirmed by a dual-luciferase reporter analysis demonstrating the specific binding between LINC00342 and miR-596 [16]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), LINC00342 is highly expressed and promotes tumor progression by sponging miR-19a-3p to regulate NPEPL1 expression, with its downregulation inhibiting cell proliferation and metastasis both in vitro and in vivo [17]. However, it remains unclear whether LINC00342 functions through similar ceRNA mechanisms in oral cancer.

Previous studies have confirmed that miRNAs play significant regulatory roles in the progression of various cancers, including OC [18, 19]. Notably, knockdown of miR-149-5p expression has been shown to promote the progression of oral squamous cell carcinoma (a subtype of OC), leading to increased proliferation, migration and invasion, while inhibiting apoptosis in these cells [20]. miRNAs typically target the 3' untranslated region (UTR) of mRNA, causing mRNA degradation or translation inhibition [21], thereby affecting changes in cellular functions such as cell proliferation, differentiation and migration [22–24]. Fibroblast growth factor (FGF) 11, a member of the FGF family, is known for its role in cancer cell growth, differentiation, and oncogenesis [25, 26]. It acts as an oncogene in several cancers, including thyroid and non-small cell lung cancer [27, 28]. Zhang et al. hypothesized that FGF11 plays a regulatory role in OC [29], but there is currently no evidence of a direct association between FGF11 and miR-149-5p, and its mechanism in OC remains unclear.

The PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, a classical pathway involved in oral cancer progression, was selected as the investigation focus of our ceRNA signaling axis. This choice was motivated by several factors, despite the lack of direct evidence linking LINC00342 to PI3K-AKT regulation. Previous studies have demonstrated the involvement of miR-149-5p in the PI3K-AKT pathway, as well as connections between FGF family genes and this signaling cascade. In the context of osteosarcoma, miR-149-5p acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting the Tweak/FN14/PI3K/AKT pathway, with Xu et al. reporting its downregulation in osteosarcoma tissues. The restoration of miR-149-5p expression inhibits tumor growth by targeting TNFRSF12A (Fn14), a component of the Tweak/Fn14 axis, thereby reducing PI3K/AKT pathway activation, suppressing cell proliferation, and promoting apoptosis [30]. Furthermore, various FGF family genes have been implicated in activating the PI3K-AKT pathway [31]. For instance, FGF1 has been shown to activate not only the MEK/ERK pathway but also the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, resulting in increased resistance to the microtubule polymerization inhibitor taltobulin in MCF-7 breast cancer cells. This dual activation leads to enhanced cell survival, reduced apoptosis, and increased cell migration [32]. Additionally, FGF6 has been implicated in bladder cancer progression through its effects on the PI3K/AKT pathway. Upon binding to its receptor, FGF6 promotes PI3K activation and subsequent AKT phosphorylation, ultimately upregulating glycolysis-related proteins and angiogenic factors. This mechanism enhances aerobic glycolysis in bladder cancer cells and promotes tumor angiogenesis [33]. Given these established connections between various FGF family members and the PI3K-AKT pathway, it is plausible that FGF11 might also interact with this signaling cascade, potentially influencing cancer progression through similar mechanisms. However, the specific role of FGF11 in modulating the PI3K-AKT pathway, particularly in the context of oral cancer, remains to be elucidated and warrants further investigation.

In this study, the role of the LINC00342 in the progression of OC and its molecular mechanism of action is explored. Specifically, the investigation focuses on the mechanism by which the LINC00342/miR-149-5p/FGF11 axis regulates the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, contributing to cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. The hypothesis posits that LINC00342 acts as a ceRNA that sponges miR-149-5p, thereby modulating FGF11 expression and consequently influencing the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. This axis has not been previously studied in OC, and its elucidation could provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying OC progression. By elucidating this regulatory mechanism, this study aims to identify potential novel therapeutic targets for OC treatment.

Materials and methods

Analysis of raw microarray data

We analyzed the sample data downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database. The GSE25099 dataset was divided into two groups: 10 normal oral tissues (GSM616588-GSM616597) and 10 OC patient tissues (GSM616647-GSM616656), which were analyzed to generate lncRNA/mRNA expression profiles. The GSE98463 dataset was divided into two groups: 4 normal oral tissues (GSM2596879-GSM2596882) and 4 OC patient tissues (GSM2596874-GSM2596877), which were analyzed to generate miRNA expression profiles. The criterion for differential expression was P < 0.05, lncRNAs/mRNAs/miRNAs were selected by the criterion of |fold change|≥ 2, and hierarchical clustering heatmap or volcano plot was constructed. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the functional annotation tool of DAVID Bioinformatics Resources 6.8 (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/summary.jsp). Binding between lncRNAs and miRNAs was predicted using Starbase (https://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/). miRNA-mRNA binding was predicted using Targetscan 7.2 (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_72/).

Cytoscape software (version 3.10.2, Cytoscape Consortium, CA, USA) was employed to conduct comprehensive network analyses of lncRNA-miRNA and miRNA-mRNA interactions. Pearson correlation analysis was performed using Cytoscape to explore potential interactions within the GSE25099 and GSE98463 datasets. The analysis was designed to identify significant correlations between non-coding RNAs and their potential target molecules, with a threshold set at a correlation coefficient (|r|) greater than 0.4 and a P < 0.05 to ensure statistical significance.

Cell lines, culture and transfection

Human OC cell lines (CAL-27 and FaDu) and human embryonic kidney 293 cells (HEK 293) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; VA, USA), and human oral epithelial cells (HOECs) were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). All cells were maintained in complete medium containing the following components: Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (HyClone, UT, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone).

Transient silencing of LINC00342

RNA was transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 and used in subsequent experiments 48 h later. FGF11 overexpression (ov-FGF11), LINC00342 small interfering (si)RNA, and miR-149-5p mimic/inhibitor and their scrambled negative controls (ov-NC, si-NC, mimic NC, and inhibitor NC) were purchased from Sangon Biotech Co, Ltd (Shanghai, China). si-LINC00342-1, si-LINC00342-2, si-LINC00342-3, and scrambled siRNA (si-NC) were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. The siRNA sequences were as follows: si-LINC00342-1, 5′-TGGCTTGTTTACTCCAAATGATC-3′; si-LINC00342-2, 5′-CTCCACAAACCAGAAGACTTATT-3′; si-LINC00342-3, 5′-TACAGAAGATGCTAACTAGAATA-3′; si-NC, 5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACG-3′.

Nucleocytoplasmic separation

Cytoplasmic and nuclear RNA purification kit (Norgen Biotek; Ontario, Canada) was isolated from cells according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 400 μL cell isolation buffer was added to the cell pellet. After 10 min of incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 500×g for 1 min to separate the cytoplasm and nucleus. The nuclear and cytoplasmic samples were then thoroughly mixed with Lysis Buffer at 21 ℃. RNA from the cytoplasm and nucleus was added separately to the pre-warmed eluate and then collected for real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis.

RNA pull-down assay

Biotinylated NC, LINC00342 and LINC00342 mut were transfected into FaDu and CAL-27 cells. Cell lysates were incubated (30 min; 25 ℃) with Dynabeads M-280 streptavidin (Invitrogen, MA, USA) and shaken slowly according to the manufacturer's instructions. RT-qPCR was performed for quantitative analysis.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from FaDu and CAL-27 cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Real-time mRNA quantification for LINC00342, miR-149-5p, FGF11, U6, and 18sRNA was performed using SYBR Green qPCR SuperMix (Invitrogen) on a 7500 RT-qPCR system (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primers for LINC00342, FGF11, ITGB3, PDGFC, miR-149-5p, 18sRNA (as an internal normalization control for LINC00342, FGF11, ITGB3, PDGFC), and U6 (as an internal normalization control for miRNAs) were as follows: LINC00342 forward, 5'-TCCACAGACACTACCCAAAGC-3'; and reverse, 5'-GCAGTTCACTCTGCTGCTTC-3'; FGF11 forward, 5'-TGTCTCTCTCTCCAGAGCCT-3'; and reverse, 5'-CTGTGAAATGCGGCGAACTG-3'. ITGB3 forward, 5'-AGATTGGAGACACGGTGAGC-3'; and reverse, 5'-CGGCATACCCCACACTCAAA-3'. PDGFC forward, 5'-TGGCGGTGGTGAAAGAGACT-3'; and reverse, 5'-GCTGAGGATCTTGTACTCCGTTC-3'. miR-30a-5p forward, 5'-ACACTCCAGCTGGGTGTAAACATCCTCGAC-3'; and reverse, 5'-CTCAACTGGTGTCGTGGA-3'. miR-139-5p forward, 5'-ACACTCCAGCTGGGTCTACAGTGCACGTGTC-3'; and reverse, 5'-CTCAACTGGTGTCGTGGA-3'. miR-149-5p forward, 5'-ACACTCCAGCTGGGTCTGGCTCCGTGTCTTC-3'; and reverse, 5'-CTCAACTGGTGTCGTGGA-3'. 18sRNA forward, 5'-CCTGGATACCGCAGCTAGGA-3'; and reverse, 5'-GCGGCGCAATACGAATGCCCC-3'. U6 forward, 5'-CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACA-3'; and reverse, 5′-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT-3'. Relative expression levels of LINC00342, FGF11, and miR-149-5p were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [34, 35]. All RT-qPCR experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell proliferation rate

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) reagent (Solarbio; 10 μL) was added to 96-well plates (containing FaDu and CAL-27 3 × 103 cells) at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with an enzyme-labeled instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) after 60 min of incubation in the dark at 21 °C.

Cell cycle assay

FaDu and CAL-27 cell suspensions were centrifuged at 1000×g for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and immobilized in 70% ethyl alcohol at 4 °C overnight. The samples were washed with PBS and resuspended in 500 μL PBS containing 50 μg/mL PI and 100 μg/mL RNase A (with 0.2% Triton X-100). After 30 min of incubation (4 °C), flow cytometry (FCM) was used to analyze cell cycle using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, CA, USA).

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Before determining the invasion ability of FaDu and CAL-27 cells, a 24-well transwell insert (BD Biosciences) was coated with Matrigel® (BD Biosciences) for 6 h at 37 °C. Serum-free medium was then added to the upper chamber and complete medium to the lower chamber. After 24 h of incubation, invasive cells were fixed with anhydrous ethanol for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio Beijing, China; 25 °C) for 25 min. The cells were then fixed, stained, and counted. The cells were then examined under a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at ×200 magnification. Matrigel incubation of the insert is not required for the migration assay.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The amplified 3'-UTR fragments of LINC00342 and FGF11 containing miR-149-5p binding sites were cloned into the psiCHECK-2 dual-luciferase reporter vector (Promega, WI, USA). HEK 293 cells were transfected with 0.5 μg reporter construct and 50 nM miR-149-5p mimic per well using Lipofectamine 3000. After 4 h of transfection, the transfection medium was replaced with complete culture medium. After 48 h of culture, cells were lysed with passive lysis buffer (Promega), and luciferase activity was measured at 490 nm using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter assay system (Promega). The ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase activity was used to normalize firefly luciferase values.

Western blotting

FaDu and CAL-27 cells were lysed using Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay lysis buffer (Solarbio) and estimated using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Solarbio). Denatured proteins were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Solarbio), and protein bands were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (Solarbio), and incubated overnight (4 °C) with FGF11 (1:500; ab89713), PI3K (1:1000, ab191606), AKT (1:500, ab8805), phospho (p)-PI3K (1:500, ab182651), p-AKT (1:500, ab38449) primary antibodies from abcam (Cambridge, UK). They were then incubated with goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:20,000, ab205718; Abcam) for 2 h at 25 °C. Anti-GAPDH antibody (1:10000, ab181602; Abcam) was used as a loading control. Proteins were visualized using Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore, MA, USA). Chemiluminescence signals were captured with X-ray film (Kodak, NY, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.3.0). All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Bonferroni post hoc test to determine specific group differences. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

LINC00342 is upregulated in OC cell lines

Based on the results of lncRNA microarray data analysis, we identified 20 differentially expressed lncRNAs, including 15 upregulated lncRNAs and 5 downregulated lncRNAs (Fig. 1A). Among them, LINC00342 as a known oncogene [36, 37], the molecular mechanism in OC has not been reported. Furthermore, LINC00342 expression was significantly upregulated in 2 OC cell lines (FaDu and CAL-27) based on RT-qPCR assays (Fig. 1B). After transfection of the three siRNAs against LINC00342 into FaDu and CAL-27 cells, it was found that siRNA-LINC00342-2 had the best inhibitory effect. Therefore, in the subsequent verification experiments, we used siRNA-LINC00342-2 to investigate the effect of LINC00342 mechanism (Fig. 1C). By nucleocytoplasmic separation and RT-qPCR analysis, we observed that LINC00342 was mainly distributed in the cytoplasm of FaDu and CAL-27 cells (Fig. 1D), so the effect of LINC00342 on OC may be realized by the ceRNA mechanism. The results of CCK8 and transwell and FCM showed that low expression of LINC00342 reduced the proliferation and migration and invasion abilities of FaDu and CAL-27 cells (Fig. 1E–H), and arrested the cell cycle in G1 phase (Fig. 1I). These results indicate that LINC00342 is a key gene that influences the biological behavior of OC cells.

LINC00342 directly targets miR-149-5p

According to the miRNA microarray analysis, 209 differentially expressed hsa-miRNAs were identified, of which 121 were downregulated (Fig. 2A). Combined with the starbase database and co-analysis of 121 downregulated miRNAs (Fig. 2B), we observed that 3 miRNAs were overlapped, namely miR-30a-5p, miR-139-5p, and miR-149-5p. Network analysis of lncRNA-miRNA interactions conducted using Cytoscape software indicated that miR-149-5p is a promising potential binding miRNA for LINC00342 among the miRNAs analyzed (Fig. 2C). Subsequent RT-qPCR analysis revealed that miR-30a-5p, miR-139-5p, and miR-149-5p were all low expressed in OC cells, and miR-149-5p had the most obvious expression in FaDu and CAL-27 cells of low expression (Fig. 2D), further confirm miR-149-5p as a potential target of LINC00342. After validating the efficacy of miR-149-5p mimic/inhibitor in FaDu and CAL-27 cells by RT-qPCR (Fig. 2E), it was observed that knockdown of LINC00342 expression could increase the expression of miR-149-5p (Fig. 2F), but no significant effect of miR-149-5p expression change on LINC00342 expression (Fig. 2G). Bioinformatics analysis revealed potential binding sites between LINC00342 and miR-149-5p, and subsequent dual-luciferase experiments confirmed that LINC00342 directly targets miR-149-5p (Fig. 2H). In addition, RNA pulldown results showed a significantly higher enrichment of miR-149-5p in bio-LINC00342 than in bio-NC and bio-LINC00342 mut groups in FaDu and CAL-27 cells (Fig. 2I). These results confirm that miR-149-5p directly targets LINC00342.

FGF11 is a direct target of miR-149-5p

According to the results of mRNA microarray data analysis, 1496 upregulated mRNAs were identified (Fig. 3A). In addition, we found through KEGG that PI3K-AKT, which is regulated by 72 mRNAs, may be a key signaling pathway affecting OC progression (Fig. 3B). By combining the analysis of these 72 mRNAs with the TargetScan database, we identified 6 overlapping mRNAs: IL6, PDGFRB, ITGB3, FGF11, PDGFC, and CDK6 (Fig. 3C). Notably, the roles of ITGB3, FGF11, and PDGFC in OC have not been previously reported. Subsequent RT-qPCR validation showed that ITGB3, FGF11, and PDGFC were upregulated in FaDu and CAL-27 cells (Fig. 3D). Bioinformatic analysis revealed potential binding sites between these three genes and miR-149-5p (Fig. 3E). A dual-luciferase reporter assay confirmed that only FGF11 is a direct target of miR-149-5p (Fig. 3F). Therefore, we selected the FGF11 gene as a potential target for miR-149-5p. Network analysis of miRNA-mRNA interactions performed using Cytoscape software revealed that FGF11 is a promising potential binding target of miR-149-5p among the mRNAs analyzed (Fig. 3G). We then observed that FGF11 expression was inhibited by knockdown of LINC00342 expression (Fig. 3H) and negatively regulated by miR-149-5p (Fig. 3I), so we constructed an FGF11 overexpression vector and validated its gene (Fig. 3J) and protein (Fig. 3K) levels in FaDu and CAL-27 cells. These results demonstrate that miR-149-5p directly targets FGF11.

LINC00342/miR-149-5p/FGF11 axis affects OC development through PI3K-AKT pathway

Since KEGG analysis revealed that PI3K-AKT may be an important signaling pathway affecting OC development, we investigated the relationship between LINC00342/miR-149-5p/FGF11 and PI3K-AKT signaling. According to the western blot results, we found that LINC00342 knockdown significantly reduced PI3K and AKT phosphorylation and FGF11 protein levels in FaDu and CAL-27 cells, with no difference in total PI3K and AKT protein levels (Fig. 4).

Effect of the interaction between LINC00342, miR-149-5p and FGF11 on cell function

After co-transfection of miR-149-5p inhibitor, the si-LINC00342-mediated reduction of FGF11 protein level, PI3K and AKT phosphorylation levels was reversed (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the PI3K and AKT phosphorylation inhibited by miR-149-5p was reversed by FGF11 overexpression in the presence of si-LINC00342 (Fig. 5A). The results of CCK8, transwell and FCM showed that miR-149-5p decreased the proliferation rate (Fig. 5B, C) and migration and invasion ability (Fig. 5D) of FaDu and CAL-27 cells; and arrested the cell cycle in G1 phase (Fig. 5E) in the presence of si-LINC00342. The effect of miR-149-5p was significantly reversed under the influence of excess FGF11. In conclusion, the LINC00342 sponge adsorbs miR-149-5p and regulates the transcription and translation of FGF11, thereby affecting downstream PI3K-AKT signaling and interfering with OC development.

Discussion

Metastasis of cancer cells is a crucial factor contributing to the poor prognosis of OC. Previous reports have indicated that lncRNAs are actively involved in OC development [38, 39]. For example, Lu et al. found that HOTAIR could inhibit the metastasis of OC stem cells through regulation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition [40]. Similarly, Fang et al. demonstrated that lncRNA DNM3OS promoted the viability and migration of OC cells [41]. The aim of this study was to identify a novel lncRNA as a potential therapeutic target for OC. A comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms of lncRNAs may help develop new and promising therapeutic strategies for OC treatment. In our study, we identified the oncogenic roles of LINC00342 and FGF11 in OC for the first time.

LINC00342 plays a supportive regulatory role in various cancers. For example, Tang et al. found that LINC00342 was strongly associated with poor prognosis in human non-small cell lung cancer patients [37], while Su et al. demonstrated that LINC00342 could induce metastasis of lung adenocarcinoma cells [42]. In line with these findings, our study revealed that LINC00342 was significantly upregulated in OC cells, highlighting its potential role in OC tumorigenesis and the need for further investigation into its exact function and mechanisms in this cancer type. Subsequently, we observed that knockdown of LINC00342 expression in FaDu and CAL-27 cells significantly reduced proliferation, migration and invasion, with cell cycle arrest in the G1 phase. This is the first confirmation of LINC00342’s role in OC. The observation of its cytoplasmic localization further supports the possibility that it acts through a ceRNA mechanism. It is well established that lncRNAs act as ceRNAs by interacting with miRNAs through microRNA response elements, thereby participating in the regulation of target gene expression [43, 44]. In the present study, we confirmed by RT-qPCR, dual luciferase reporter assay, and RNA pulldown that miR-149-5p, identified in the GSE98463 dataset and shown to be a major therapeutic target for OC [45], is a direct target of LINC00342. Its expression is negatively regulated by LINC00342 in FaDu and CAL-27 cells.

Interestingly, our results showed that while LINC00342 knockdown increased miR-149-5p expression, changes in miR-149-5p levels did not significantly affect LINC00342 expression (Fig. 2G). This unidirectional regulation is consistent with the known behavior of lincRNAs, where changes in downstream target expression do not affect the upstream lincRNA [46]. This unidirectional regulatory relationship provides strong evidence for the ceRNA mechanism of LINC00342. It suggests that LINC00342 acts upstream of miR-149-5p, functioning as a molecular sponge to sequester miR-149-5p and reduce its availability for target gene repression. This finding is consistent with the classic ceRNA hypothesis, where lncRNAs serve as decoys or sponges for miRNAs, but not vice versa. The lack of reciprocal regulation further emphasizes LINC00342's role as a key initiator in this regulatory axis, highlighting its potential as a primary therapeutic target in oral cancer. Moreover, this unidirectional regulation underscores the importance of targeting LINC00342 rather than miR-149-5p for potential therapeutic interventions, as modulating LINC00342 would likely have more comprehensive effects on the entire signaling axis.

MiR-149-5p has been recognized as a tumor suppressor in several cancers, including ovarian cancer and OC [20, 47], where it targets various oncogenes to inhibit tumor growth and metastasis. Our results further support this role in OC, demonstrating that miR-149-5p negatively regulates FGF11, a novel target identified in this study. This finding enhances our understanding of miR-149-5p function and underscores its potential as a therapeutic target in multiple cancer types.

Another important finding of this study is the identification of FGF11 as a direct target of miR-149-5p. Many members of the FGF family are known oncogenes, and our study suggests that FGF11 is no exception. FGF11, an oncogene, is upregulated in several cancers such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma [48] and lung adenocarcinoma [49]. Although no study has reported its mechanism of action in OC, we observed high expression of FGF11 in OC cells, particularly in FaDu and CAL-27 cells, indicating its potential importance in OC pathogenesis. Our data suggest a possible suppressive role of miR-149-5p on FGF11, creating a complex regulatory network involving LINC00342, miR-149-5p and FGF11. Our study unravels the involvement of the PI3K/AKT pathway in this molecular interplay. Known for its critical role in cell survival, growth and proliferation, dysregulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway is frequently implicated in various cancers, including OC [50, 51]. We observed that LINC00342 was synergistically upregulated with FGF11, a target gene of miR-149-5p, in OC cells. The miR-149-5p inhibitor reversed si-LINC00342-mediated reduction of FGF11 protein levels and phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT. In the presence of si-LINC00342, miR-149-5p decreased the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT, and reduced the proliferation, migration and invasion abilities of OC cells. Concurrently, OC cells exhibited damage, reduced DNA synthesis and replication, leading to cell cycle arrest in the G1 phase. Excessive FGF11 promoted cancer cell development, increased PI3K and AKT phosphorylation levels, and reversed the efficacy of si-LINC00342/miR-149-5p combination on OC cells. These results suggest that the LINC00342-miR-149-5p-FGF11 axis controls the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, thereby regulating the progression of OC. Previous studies have demonstrated the oncogenic role of LINC00342 in other cancers, such as gastric cancer and colorectal cancer [16, 17]. Our research confirms its function as an oncogenic factor in OC as well. Comparing our results with these studies provides deeper insights into the conserved nature of LINC00342 function across different cancer types. Based on these findings, developing small molecule inhibitors or RNA-based therapeutics targeting LINC00342 could represent promising therapeutic strategies.

Our study's findings regarding the LINC00342/miR-149-5p/FGF11 axis in OC both align with and extend current understanding of lncRNA-mediated regulatory mechanisms in cancer progression. Previous studies have identified several lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA axes in OC, such as the LINC01929/miR-137-3p/F0XC1 axis [52] and CASC18/miR-20a-3p/TGFB2 axis [53]. Similar to our findings, these studies demonstrated the oncogenic roles of lncRNAs in OC progression. However, our study is the first to elucidate the specific role of LINC00342 in OC and its interaction with miR-149-5p and FGF11. This novel axis provides a unique perspective on OC pathogenesis, particularly in the context of the PI3K-AKT pathway. Interestingly, while our study identifies FGF11 as a key player in OC progression, previous research has primarily focused on other FGF family members in OC, such as FGF2 [54] and FGF8 [55]. Our finding that FGF11 is regulated by the LINC00342/miR-149-5p axis and contributes to OC progression through the PI3K-AKT pathway represents a significant advancement in understanding the diverse roles of FGF family members in cancer. Furthermore, our results align with studies in other cancer types where LINC00342 has been shown to act as a ceRNA. For instance, in heptatocellular carcinoma, LINC00342 sponges miR-203a-3p [56], while in gastric cancer, it targets miR-545-5p [16]. Our identification of miR-149-5p as a target of LINC00342 in OC adds to this growing body of evidence supporting LINC00342's role as a multifaceted regulator in various cancers. The involvement of the PI3K-AKT pathway in our proposed mechanism is consistent with its well-established role in OC progression [57]. However, our study uniquely links this pathway to the LINC00342/miR-149-5p/FGF11 axis, providing a new perspective on how lncRNAs can modulate this critical signaling cascade in OC. In summary, while our findings share commonalities with existing literature on lncRNA-mediated regulation in cancer, they also present several novel aspects specific to OC, including the identification of the LINC00342/miR-149-5p/FGF11 axis and its connection to the PI3K-AKT pathway.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this study relies primarily on in vitro experiments using OC cell lines. While these models are valuable for understanding cellular processes, they cannot fully replicate the complex tumor microenvironment present in vivo. Therefore, future studies should include in vivo experiments, such as xenograft models, to validate our findings in a more physiologically relevant context. Additionally, the collection and analysis of clinical samples from OC patients would provide crucial validation of the LINC00342/miR-149-5p/FGF11 axis in human tumors. Second, while we focused on the LINC00342/miR-149-5p/FGF11 axis, LINC00342 may interact with other miRNAs or signaling pathways not explored in this study, which could contribute to OC progression. A more comprehensive analysis, such as RNA-sequencing or proteomics, could provide a broader understanding of LINC00342's regulatory network in OC. Third, although we employed multiple siRNA sequences and validated their specificity using RT-qPCR and other assays, off-target effects cannot be entirely ruled out. Future studies could benefit from using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing to create LINC00342 knockout cell lines, providing a more definitive assessment of its function. Fourth, our study focused on the role of LINC00342 in cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. However, other important aspects of cancer biology, such as angiogenesis, immune evasion, and therapy resistance, were not investigated. Finally, while our findings suggest potential therapeutic implications, we have not explored aspects of translating these findings into potential therapeutic strategies. The development of targeted therapies based on lncRNAs remains challenging due to their complex secondary structures and potential off-target effects. Future studies should investigate the feasibility of targeting LINC00342 or its downstream effectors as a therapeutic approach for OC. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into the role of LINC00342 in OC and lays the groundwork for future investigations that could ultimately lead to novel therapeutic strategies for OC patients.

The findings of this study have several potential clinical implications that could translate into novel therapeutic strategies for oral cancer. The identification of LINC00342 as a key regulator in the LINC00342/miR-149-5p/FGF11 axis presents a promising target for future interventions. One potential approach could be the development of antisense oligonucleotides or small interfering RNAs specifically targeting LINC00342, which could disrupt this oncogenic pathway. Additionally, the elucidation of this regulatory axis provides opportunities for combination therapies. For instance, inhibitors of the PI3K-AKT pathway, some of which are already in clinical trials for various cancers, could potentially be combined with LINC00342 targeting strategies for a more potent anti-tumor effect. Furthermore, the identification of FGF11 as a downstream effector in this pathway suggests that FGF11 inhibitors, if developed, could serve as another therapeutic avenue. The miR-149-5p mimics could also be explored as a potential therapeutic to counteract the effects of LINC00342 upregulation. Importantly, the components of this axis could serve as biomarkers for disease progression or treatment response in OC patients. While significant work remains to translate these findings into clinical applications, including the need for in vivo studies and clinical trials, this study lays a foundation for future research aimed at developing targeted therapies for oral cancer based on the LINC00342/miR-149-5p/FGF11 axis.

Conclusion

Our study sheds new light on the molecular interactions involving LINC00342, miR-149-5p and FGF11 in OC development. It highlights the function of LINC00342 as a sponge for miR-149-5p, leading to the modulation of FGF11 transcription and translation, and ultimately influencing the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 6). Our findings provide a solid foundation for future research to explore this intricate mechanism and its potential as a novel therapeutic target in OC.
