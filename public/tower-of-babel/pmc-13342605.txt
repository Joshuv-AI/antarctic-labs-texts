Microstructural evolution and mechanical performance correlation of WC-reinforced iron-based coatings fabricated by laser cladding
Authors: Yu Wang, Xin Li, Peiying Bian, Di Wang, Fangxia Ye, Nan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In laser cladding, iron-based alloy coatings are prone to issues including microstructural inhomogeneity, elemental segregation, and weak interfacial bonding under high energy density and complex thermal cycling. In this study, WC particles were incorporated as a reinforcing phase into an iron-based matrix to enhance coating densification and achieve synergistic multi-property optimization. Composite coatings composed of a nickel-enriched iron-based matrix and WC were deposited onto H13 tool steel substrates using coaxial powder-feeding fiber laser cladding. The dissolution characteristics of WC particles, elemental diffusion behavior, and phase transformation were systematically characterized via SEM, EDS, XRD, and EBSD. The overall performance was assessed through microhardness testing, tribological evaluation under friction-wear conditions, and electrochemical polarization measurements.Results demonstrate that under the laser processing parameters used in this study, the coating formed a smooth and dense metallurgically bonded interface. In the high-temperature molten pool, partial dissolution of WC particles facilitated reactions with the Fe matrix, resulting in the formation of dispersed strengthening phases (Fe₃W₃C and Fe₇C₃) and substantial grain refinement. The cladding layer demonstrated outstanding performance, with an average hardness of 892 HV0.2—approximately 80% higher than that of the substrate—a wear rate reduced to 38% of the substrate’s value, and a corrosion current density lowered by roughly 69%, indicating excellent wear and corrosion resistance. The partial dissolution of WC particles and the associated elemental diffusion behavior provide favorable conditions for the formation of carbide phases, while simultaneously promoting grain refinement and strengthening effects to a certain extent. The synergistic interaction between the refined grain structure and the dispersed carbide phases collectively contributes to a pronounced multi-mechanism strengthening effect. These results offer experimentally verifiable evidence and theoretical backing for microstructural control and performance enhancement in iron-based composite coatings.

Introduction

As an advanced manufacturing technique that combines material preparation with surface reinforcement, laser cladding technology shows considerable promise for applications in mold repair, energy equipment, and high-end manufacturing1. By utilizing a high-energy laser beam to rapidly melt and solidify alloy powder together with the substrate surface, this method enables the formation of metallurgically bonded coatings featuring dense microstructures and strong adhesion, thereby substantially improving properties such as wear resistance, corrosion resistance, and high-temperature performance2. Nevertheless, under conditions of high energy density and complex thermal cycling, the microstructural evolution within the cladding layer becomes extremely intricate. Large temperature gradients and molten pool convection often lead to elemental segregation, grain coarsening, and microstructural non-uniformity3, which in turn compromise the mechanical integrity and stability of the coatings. Traditional optimization of process parameters largely depends on empirical approaches, making it difficult to achieve precise alignment between coating microstructure and macroscopic performance. Thus, achieving controllable microstructural growth and synergistic property optimization under complex thermophysical coupling conditions has become a key scientific challenge in current laser cladding research.

Owing to its ultra-high hardness, excellent chemical stability, and favorable thermal conductivity, tungsten carbide (WC) has become one of the most commonly employed and effective reinforcement phases in laser cladding4. Research indicates that the addition of WC can effectively suppress the formation of coarse grains while promoting a fine-grained microstructure, thereby improving coating microhardness and thermal stability5. In terms of performance, WC-reinforced iron-based coatings demonstrate superior characteristics—such as wear rate, friction coefficient, and corrosion current density—relative to unreinforced systems, exhibiting notable multi-property synergy6,7. Moreover, multilayer cladding or laser remelting as a post-treatment has been shown to further enhance interfacial bonding by reducing common metallurgical defects like pores and cracks, thus extending coating service life8,9. Although considerable progress has been achieved in microstructure optimization and performance enhancement, existing studies predominantly focus on macroscopic property comparisons or isolated microstructural features. Systematic experimental investigations and unified understanding regarding the dissolution behavior of WC particles during cladding, their elemental diffusion characteristics, and the coupled relationships among carbide formation, grain evolution, and strain accumulation remain insufficient. In particular, under high-energy–density transient cladding conditions, the spatial-scale characteristics of elemental migration and their influence on the formation of gradient microstructures have not been fully elucidated. To address these issues, this study takes laser-clad iron-based composite coatings as the research object and combines microstructural characterization with performance evaluation to investigate the dissolution and diffusion behavior of WC particles under high-temperature cladding conditions, as well as their effects on carbide formation and microstructural evolution. Furthermore, the relationships between these processes and the resulting multi-property variations are discussed. The findings provide a systematic experimental analysis of WC particle behavior and interfacial reaction phenomena under high-energy–density cladding environments, and to a certain extent, elucidate the intrinsic correlations between microstructural evolution and performance response.

Literature review

In the fields of high-performance surface strengthening, wear-resistant protection, and remanufacturing engineering, laser cladding technology occupies a critical position. Its notable strengths in energy density control, composite material deposition, and microstructural tunability have established it as a significant research direction in advanced manufacturing10,11. Current research has made dual advancements: on one hand, enabling more precise regulation of cladding layer formation quality and defect suppression; on the other, deepening insight into microstructural evolution mechanisms and the principles governing synergistic multi-property optimization. These achievements provide novel technical approaches and theoretical foundations for the fabrication of functionalized metal surfaces and the reinforcement of composite materials, holding substantial promise for engineering applications and growth.

The forming quality of laser cladding is governed by multiple process parameters, including laser power, scanning speed, powder feed rate, and spot size. In recent years, researchers have systematically investigated the effects of these parameters on geometric morphology, dilution rate, and defect control using mathematical modeling, optimization algorithms, and experimental validation. Cao et al. performed a sensitivity analysis and found that laser power and scanning speed predominantly influence geometric characteristics, with width being most sensitive to laser power and height to scanning speed12. Zhang et al. conducted a parameter sensitivity study on Mo₂FeB₂ ternary boride coatings, quantitatively assessing the impact of process parameters on cladding layer thickness, width, and dilution rate13. Notably, Liang et al. successfully fabricated dense cladding layers with low dilution rates and defect-free microstructures (free of pores and cracks) in novel AlCrFeNiW0.2 high-entropy alloy coatings by optimizing laser power and scanning speed combinations14. He et al. developed a hybrid GA-ACO/RFR predictive model combined with a multi-objective GNSGA-II optimization algorithm for parameter optimization in 15-5PH alloy cladding, achieving significant improvements in coating quality15. Mechanistic research by Zhou et al. on single-track formation behavior during high-speed laser cladding on copper substrates revealed that powder melting behavior and laser energy absorption characteristics after penetrating the powder stream critically influence interfacial structure formation16.

The rapid heating and cooling inherent to laser cladding facilitate the formation of complex non-equilibrium microstructures within the cladding layer, where microstructural evolution directly determines mechanical properties and associated strengthening mechanisms. Current research emphasizes microstructural engineering, substructure formation, and their relationship with strengthening mechanisms across various alloy systems. Jia et al. investigated sulfur-containing Fe60 alloy cladding, revealing that sulfur significantly alters Marangoni convection patterns in the molten pool, thereby modifying the nucleation and growth behavior of microstructures17. Zhou et al. applied GWO-BPNN optimization to improve geometric quality in 15-5PH alloy cladding while characterizing its microstructure—comprising martensite, δ-ferrite, and oxides—which corresponded to markedly enhanced wear and corrosion resistance18. Du et al. systematically examined the formation mechanisms of pores and cracks in martensitic stainless steel cladding under varying process parameters, identifying residual stress as a primary driver of structural defects19. Innovative process developments include a laser cleaning–cladding hybrid technique proposed by Fan et al., which significantly improved metallurgical bonding interfaces and microstructural density, thereby strengthening mechanical performance20. Rafiei and Ghasemi fabricated 17-4PH cladding layers with exceptional microhardness (minimum dilution rate: 9%) and microstructural uniformity, highlighting the critical role of microstructural refinement in performance enhancement21.

To address the demands of complex service environments, current research emphasizes multi-property synergistic optimization and composite-enhanced materials, particularly through high-entropy alloys (HEAs), ceramic particles, and nanomaterials. Cui et al. pioneered a neural network-based approach for recognizing cladding profile and dilution rate, enabling intelligent quality evaluation and providing supporting tools for multi-objective optimization22. Du et al. employed the grey-fuzzy Taguchi method to simultaneously enhance surface morphology, wear resistance, and corrosion performance in 15-5PH cladding, validating the effectiveness of multi-response optimization23. On the theoretical front, Prakash et al. provided a comprehensive review of HEA cladding, demonstrating superior resistance to corrosion, wear, and oxidation, and highlighting significant potential for component remanufacturing24. Rafiei and Ghasemi conducted thermodynamic simulations confirming that WC reinforcement substantially affects hardness and thermal stress in WC/Inconel625 composites25. Chen et al. established relationships between process parameters and microproperties for AlCoCrFeNi HEA cladding using NSGA-II optimization combined with thermal analysis simulations, achieving coordinated optimization of hardness, dilution rate, and geometry26.

Existing studies have revealed the coupling relationships among laser energy input, scanning speed, and molten pool dynamics, thereby providing an important theoretical foundation for optimizing energy density and controlling forming stability in iron-based systems. Meanwhile, it is generally accepted that WC particles undergo varying degrees of dissolution during high-temperature cladding and react with the Fe-based melt to form carbide phases such as Fe3W3C and Fe7C3, which play a crucial role in determining the microstructure and properties of the coatings. However, most of these studies primarily focus on phase identification and macroscopic performance comparisons, while the spatial-scale characteristics of elemental diffusion during WC dissolution, the evolution of compositional gradients in the interfacial region, and their relationships with grain refinement and strain accumulation remain insufficiently explored. Based on this, the present study builds upon existing research and employs multi-scale characterization techniques, including EDS, XRD, and EBSD, to systematically analyze the elemental migration behavior and spatial distribution characteristics following WC dissolution. Furthermore, from the perspectives of elemental diffusion, carbide formation, and microstructural evolution, the relationships between these processes and the resulting variations in mechanical properties are discussed.

Research design

Raw materials and sample preparation

H13 hot-work die steel was selected as the substrate material in this study. Its chemical composition, containing alloying elements such as Cr, Mo, and V, provides excellent high-temperature strength and thermal stability. Tungsten carbide (WC) particles with an average particle size of 2–5 μm were employed as the reinforcing phase. The composite powder matrix consisted of a Ni-enriched iron-based alloy system (primarily Fe, Ni, Cr, B, and Si), in which Ni improves wettability and interfacial bonding, while B and Si contribute to microstructure refinement and a reduction in melting temperature.

During the preparation of composite powders, iron-based alloy powder and WC particles were first blended at mass fractions of 20 wt%, 30 wt%, and 40 wt%, respectively, and mixed in a planetary ball mill at 200 rpm for 1 h to ensure uniform distribution. Considering that the typical addition range of WC in Fe-based cladding coatings is 20–40 wt%, the microstructural characteristics and mechanical properties vary significantly under different contents. At relatively low WC content, the insufficient amount of reinforcement phase limits the formation of an effective dispersion-strengthened structure. In contrast, excessive WC content tends to induce particle agglomeration, coating embrittlement, and increased crack sensitivity, while also reducing molten pool fluidity and coating densification27,28. Therefore, these three representative compositions were selected for comparative analysis. After mixing, the powders were vacuum-dried at 120 °C for 2 h to remove adsorbed moisture. The H13 substrate was mechanically polished, ultrasonically cleaned in anhydrous ethanol, and preheated at 450 °C for 1 h to reduce thermal stress during laser cladding.

Prior to cladding, a Ni-based transition layer (~ 0.3 mm in thickness) was pre-deposited on the substrate surface to alleviate thermal expansion mismatch and improve interfacial metallurgical bonding. The introduction of a Ni-based transition layer on H13 steel can regulate microstructural evolution in the interfacial region and enhance bonding stability and service performance to a certain extent29. Subsequently, a Fe–WC composite reinforcement layer with a thickness of approximately 0.8–1.0 mm was deposited on the transition layer. The cladding process was conducted using a fiber laser with coaxial powder feeding, with a rated power of 2 kW and a spot diameter of 2.0 mm. High-purity argon (99.99%) was employed as the shielding gas, with a flow rate controlled at 10–12 L/min.

After cladding, the samples were sectioned by wire cutting into specimens with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 5 mm for subsequent microstructural characterization and performance evaluation. The sample surfaces were sequentially ground using 240, 600, 1200, and 2000 grit sandpapers, followed by polishing to a mirror finish using W1.0 diamond polishing paste. After polishing, the samples were cleaned with anhydrous ethanol and dried immediately to prevent surface oxidation. Subsequently, the samples were etched for approximately 10 s using a hydrochloric acid–nitric acid solution with a volume ratio of 3:1 to reveal the microstructural features.

Microstructural characterization was carried out using a Leica DM4 M optical microscope and a Thermo Fisher Apreo 2C scanning electron microscope (accelerating voltage: 15 kV), combined with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) for elemental distribution analysis. Phase identification was performed using a PANalytical Empyrean X-ray diffractometer (Cu Kα, λ = 1.5418 Å), with test conditions set to a tube voltage of 40 kV, tube current of 40 mA, scanning range of 20°–90°, and a step size of 0.05°.

Laser cladding process parameter setting

During the laser cladding process, processing parameters play a crucial role in governing molten pool stability, the dissolution behavior of WC particles, and the microstructural evolution of the coating. Parameters such as laser power, scanning speed, powder feeding rate, and overlap ratio collectively determine the thermal input conditions and forming quality of the cladding layer, thereby significantly influencing its microstructure and mechanical properties. Previous studies have demonstrated that an appropriate matching between laser power and scanning speed can ensure sufficient powder melting while maintaining molten pool stability, thus enabling the formation of coatings with uniform microstructure and sound metallurgical bonding30,31. Therefore, the laser cladding parameters employed in this study were determined based on preliminary experimental results and the parameter ranges reported in the literature for Fe-based composite coatings, in order to ensure stable molten pool formation and achieve reliable cladding quality.

Laser power, scanning speed, and spot diameter jointly determine the cladding energy density, which is a critical factor governing the dilution rate and microstructural morphology of the coating. In laser cladding processes, the laser energy density is commonly employed to characterize the thermal input intensity per unit area. This parameter comprehensively reflects the coupled effects of laser power, scanning speed, and spot size on the heat input to the molten pool, and directly influences the temperature field distribution, the dissolution behavior of WC particles, as well as the solidification characteristics of the coating microstructure. By adjusting the combination of laser power and scanning speed, the thermal input level during the cladding process can be effectively regulated, thereby enabling control over molten pool stability and microstructural evolution behavior.

The laser power was set in the range of 0.9–1.2 kW, while the scanning speed was controlled at 500–800 mm/min, with the spot diameter maintained at 2.0 mm. Based on these parameter combinations, the calculated laser energy density during the experiments ranged from approximately 33–72 J/mm2. By adjusting the matching relationship between laser power and scanning speed within this energy density window, stable cladding conditions could be achieved, providing a basis for analyzing the dissolution behavior of WC particles and the microstructural evolution of the cladding layer under different thermal input conditions. An appropriate combination of power and scanning speed can ensure the effective retention of WC particles while suppressing excessive substrate melting, thereby forming a metallurgically bonded zone with a smooth interface.

Powder delivery was implemented using a coaxial feeding system, with a feeding rate controlled at 8–12 g/min. High-purity argon (99.99%) was employed as both the carrier gas and shielding gas, with a flow rate maintained at 10–12 L/min. This configuration ensures stable powder transport within the gas stream while effectively reducing molten pool oxidation and porosity formation. The cladding path followed a staggered parallel scanning strategy, with an overlap ratio of adjacent tracks controlled at 35–40%. Previous studies have indicated that an appropriate overlap ratio contributes to improved surface flatness and reduced interlayer defects, thereby enhancing microstructural uniformity and wear resistance of the coating32. Through the above parameter design, cladding layers with uniform microstructure and sound metallurgical bonding can be obtained, providing stable experimental conditions for subsequent analysis of WC particle dissolution behavior and microstructural evolution of the coatings.

Performance test method

Microhardness test

Microhardness measurements were conducted using an HVS-1000 microhardness tester, with a load of 0.2 kgf (1.96 N) and a dwell time of 15 s. Testing points were arranged along the normal direction of the cladding layer cross-section, starting at 0.05 mm from the surface and spaced at intervals of 0.1 mm until reaching the substrate region. Three measurements were taken at each point, and the average value was calculated, with indentation spacing maintained at more than three times the diagonal length to prevent mutual interference. Based on the measured hardness gradient profile, the transition in performance across the coating, transition zone, and substrate was analyzed. The microstructural mechanisms underlying microhardness variations were interpreted in relation to WC distribution characteristics and the effect of grain refinement.

Wear resistance test

Wear resistance was evaluated using an MMU-10G pin-on-disk tribometer. The friction pair consisted of a GCr15 steel pin (5 mm in diameter, hardness of approximately 62 HRC, and surface roughness Ra < 0.2 μm). Tests were conducted under dry sliding conditions at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). The applied load was 15 N, rotational speed 200 r/min, friction radius 5 mm, and test duration 8 min. Mass loss before and after testing was measured using an electronic balance with an accuracy of 0.1 mg, and the wear rate was subsequently calculated.2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W = \frac{\Delta m}{{\rho L}}$$\end{document}where Δm is the mass loss (g), ρ is the material density (g/cm), and l is the wear path length (m).

Corrosion resistance test

The corrosion resistance was characterized by means of electrochemical potentiodynamic polarization. The test was carried out in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution (analytical reagent grade, prepared with deionized water) at 25 ± 1 °C. The sample was used as the working electrode, exposing an area of 1 cm2, with the remaining portion covered by epoxy resin. The electrode setup comprised a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference and a platinum sheet as the counter electrode. Prior to measurement, the electrolyte was deoxygenated by bubbling high-purity nitrogen for 10 min. Testing was performed on a CHI660E electrochemical workstation at a scan rate of 1 mV/s over a potential range of − 1.0 to + 0.5 V (relative to the open-circuit potential). The corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (Icorr) were determined using the Tafel extrapolation method, and the corrosion rate was then calculated according to the following equation:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v = 3.27 \times 10^{ - 3} \times \frac{{I_{{{\mathrm{corr}}}} \times EW}}{\rho }$$\end{document}where EW is equivalent weight (g/equiv) and ρ is material density (g/cm3).

Data processing and error analysis

Each experiment was repeated a minimum of three times under the same conditions, and the resulting data were averaged to obtain the final values. OriginPro 2023 and Excel were used for data processing, with outliers identified and eliminated based on the 3σ rule. Microhardness, friction coefficient, and corrosion current density are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, maintaining an error range within 5%. To guarantee consistency among various test results, all specimens underwent preparation, polishing, and testing in a uniform environment.

Research results and analysis

Microstructure and phase composition analysis

The overall forming quality of laser-clad Fe-based alloy coatings with different WC contents (20 wt%, 30 wt%, and 40 wt%) is relatively high. The cross-sectional morphology of all samples exhibits a typical three-layer structural feature, consisting of the surface cladding zone, transition zone, and substrate zone (Fig. 1). The coating surfaces are generally flat and dense, and the laser scanning tracks are clearly distinguishable, indicating a relatively uniform energy distribution and stable overlap during molten pool solidification. Under different WC contents, the coating thickness remains relatively uniform, and the interfaces exhibit good continuity without observable metallurgical defects such as pores, inclusions, or lack of fusion, demonstrating that the selected process parameters can effectively ensure high forming quality.Fig. 1Cross-sectional macroscopic morphology of Fe-based alloy coatings with different WC contents (20 wt%, 30 wt%, and 40 wt%).

Cross-sectional macroscopic morphology of Fe-based alloy coatings with different WC contents (20 wt%, 30 wt%, and 40 wt%).

From the perspective of interfacial morphology, a transition zone with moderate width is formed between the cladding layer and the substrate in all samples. Variations in WC content lead to certain differences in interfacial characteristics. Specifically, the interface in the 20 wt% sample appears relatively smooth, the 30 wt% sample exhibits superior continuity and uniformity, while slight local fluctuations are observed in the 40 wt% condition, although the overall metallurgical bonding remains intact. These variations in interfacial morphology reflect the regulatory effect of WC content on molten pool flow behavior and heat input distribution. In addition, WC particles are generally uniformly distributed within the cladding layer, with no evident large-scale agglomeration or sedimentation zones, indicating stable powder transport and molten pool dynamics. Overall, within the investigated range of 20–40 wt%, the 30 wt% sample demonstrates relatively superior performance in terms of microstructural uniformity and interfacial smoothness, providing a reliable morphological basis for subsequent analyses of microstructural evolution and mechanical properties.

To further elucidate the influence of WC content on the microstructure of laser-clad Fe-based composite coatings, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations were conducted on both the internal regions of the cladding layer and the interfacial zones of representative samples, as shown in Fig. 2. As illustrated in Fig. 2a–c, the coatings under different WC contents are predominantly composed of fine dendritic and cellular structures, exhibiting a relatively dense solidification morphology; however, the distribution characteristics of the reinforcing phase show distinct variations.Fig. 2SEM microstructures of Fe-based composite coatings with different WC contents: (a) internal microstructure of the 20 wt% WC coating; (b) internal microstructure of the 30 wt% WC coating; (c) internal microstructure of the 40 wt% WC coating; (d) interfacial morphology between the 30 wt% WC coating and the substrate.

SEM microstructures of Fe-based composite coatings with different WC contents: (a) internal microstructure of the 20 wt% WC coating; (b) internal microstructure of the 30 wt% WC coating; (c) internal microstructure of the 40 wt% WC coating; (d) interfacial morphology between the 30 wt% WC coating and the substrate.

At 20 wt% WC (Fig. 2a), the number of WC particles is relatively low, and they are mainly dispersed within the matrix with relatively large interparticle spacing, resulting in a comparatively uniform microstructure. When the WC content increases to 30 wt% (Fig. 2c), the reinforcing phase becomes more uniformly distributed with an appropriate particle density. WC particles are dispersed throughout the matrix and exert a pronounced grain refinement effect on the surrounding matrix, leading to improved microstructural uniformity and densification. With a further increase to 40 wt% (Fig. 2b), the number of WC particles increases significantly, and local enrichment becomes evident, resulting in reduced interparticle spacing and the appearance of adjacent or even contacting particles in certain regions. This phenomenon indicates that at higher WC contents, the distribution of the reinforcing phase gradually transitions from a dispersed state to localized aggregation, which can be regarded as the initial manifestation of particle agglomeration.

Such locally enriched structures may enhance regional hardness; however, they can also induce non-uniform stress distribution and stress concentration, thereby increasing crack sensitivity. Moreover, with further increases in WC content, the spatial connectivity between reinforcing particles is enhanced, potentially forming a quasi-continuous rigid skeleton in localized regions, approaching a percolation threshold. While this structure contributes to improved load-bearing capacity, it may adversely affect the overall toughness of the material.

Most WC particles retain a relatively intact morphology during the high-temperature cladding process, with only partial edge dissolution occurring and forming continuous transition interfaces with the surrounding Fe-based matrix. Local magnified observations reveal that the particle edges exhibit a certain degree of rounding, with dissolution regions approximately 1–3 μm in width, indicating limited dissolution of WC under molten pool conditions. Compared with the original WC particle size range (2–5 μm), a reduction in effective particle size is observed after cladding, further confirming the occurrence of partial dissolution during processing. The grains surrounding the particles are significantly refined, suggesting that WC particles act as heterogeneous nucleation sites during solidification, thereby effectively promoting grain refinement. Although a small number of micropores or minor defects can be observed in localized regions, no interconnected defects are formed, indicating that the selected processing parameters can effectively suppress defect propagation. Figure 2d presents the interfacial microstructure between the 30 wt% WC coating and the substrate. A smooth and continuous metallurgical bonding interface is clearly observed, with a transition zone of approximately 10–20 μm in width and a dense structure. Grains on both sides of the interface exhibit directional growth along the heat flow direction, showing typical epitaxial growth characteristics. This indicates sufficient elemental diffusion and strong interfacial bonding during the cladding process.

To further elucidate the elemental diffusion behavior and phase composition characteristics within the cladding layer, energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were conducted in the interfacial region, as shown in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively. As observed in Fig. 3, under both 30 wt% and 20 wt% WC conditions, Fe exhibits a strong signal in the substrate region and gradually transitions toward the cladding layer near the interface, indicating the upward diffusion of Fe during the cladding process. In contrast, the distributions of W and C elements show a more pronounced dependence on WC content. At 30 wt% WC, W and C exhibit relatively continuous enrichment within the cladding layer, forming an enrichment zone near the interface with a width of approximately 40–70 μm and good continuity along the interfacial direction. This suggests that W and C released from partially dissolved WC particles undergo more extensive diffusion and redistribution within the molten pool. At 20 wt% WC, however, the signal intensity of W and C is significantly reduced, with the enrichment zone narrowing to approximately 20–40 μm and the distribution transitioning from a relatively continuous band to a more discrete, localized pattern. This indicates that a lower WC content limits both the diffusion range and distribution continuity of W and C due to the reduced amount of reinforcing phase.

In addition, under both WC contents, W and C exhibit a certain degree of enrichment in the interfacial region, which is more pronounced in the 30 wt% sample. This observation indicates that WC particles undergo more evident localized dissolution under high-temperature cladding conditions and react with the Fe-based molten pool, thereby promoting elemental migration and the formation of carbide precursors. Meanwhile, considering the presence of a pre-deposited Ni-based transition layer and the high-temperature diffusion behavior during cladding, it can be inferred that Ni participates in remelting and interdiffusion processes within the interfacial region, forming a compositional gradient zone between the substrate and the cladding layer. This transition layer reduces compositional discontinuities and thermal expansion mismatch at the interface.

Combined with the interfacial elemental gradient distribution and the dense, continuous interface morphology shown in Fig. 2d, this transition structure likely contributes to alleviating local stress concentration and enhancing interfacial bonding stability. In contrast, conventional Fe-based cladding systems without a transition layer typically exhibit more pronounced compositional discontinuities and thermal stress concentrations at the interface, which can lead to microcracks or lack-of-fusion defects. Overall, the combined analysis of interfacial morphology and elemental distribution further demonstrates that the Ni-based transition layer plays a significant role in improving metallurgical bonding quality and maintaining interfacial structural integrity.

To further characterize the compositional variation in the interfacial region, EDS line scanning analysis was performed on representative areas, as shown in Fig. 4. The Fe content remains relatively high in the substrate region and gradually decreases toward the cladding layer along the thickness direction. In contrast, W and C exhibit an opposite trend, showing progressive enrichment within the cladding layer. This indicates that WC particles undergo dissolution during the cladding process, releasing W and C elements that subsequently participate in diffusion and redistribution within the molten pool. Meanwhile, Ni exhibits a certain degree of enrichment near the interface, suggesting that the pre-deposited Ni-based transition layer undergoes remelting and participates in interdiffusion during cladding, thereby forming a continuous compositional transition region between the substrate and the cladding layer. Overall, the major elements display continuous variation across the interface, forming a compositional gradient zone with a width of approximately 10–20 μm between the substrate and the cladding layer. This observation is consistent with the EDS mapping results, further confirming the presence of pronounced elemental diffusion and gradient transition characteristics in the interfacial region.

The XRD results shown in Fig. 5 further validate the above interpretations. Compared with the H13 substrate, in addition to the dominant α-Fe peak, characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to WC, Fe7C3 and Fe3C are detected in the cladding layer, indicating that WC particles undergo partial dissolution during the cladding process and subsequently react with elements in the Fe-based molten pool to form multiple carbide phases. It should be noted that Fe7C3 and Fe3C exhibit partial overlap in certain diffraction peak positions. Through local magnification of the 40–50° range and analysis of characteristic peak positions, the diffraction peak near 43.2° can be attributed to Fe7C3, while the peak around 44.7° corresponds to Fe₃C, enabling effective distinction between the two phases. These results indicate that the carbides formed during cladding are predominantly reaction products within the Fe–C system.Fig. 5XRD patterns of Fe-based composite coatings with different WC contents.

XRD patterns of Fe-based composite coatings with different WC contents.

Furthermore, comparison of XRD patterns under different WC contents shows that the primary phase compositions remain essentially consistent across all samples, with variations mainly reflected in the relative intensities of diffraction peaks. This suggests that changes in WC content primarily influence the relative fraction of carbide phases rather than introducing new phase types. Combining the EDS and XRD results, it can be concluded that the evolution behavior of WC particles during cladding is governed by the synergistic effects of thermodynamic and kinetic factors. Under high-energy laser irradiation, the molten pool temperature rises significantly, rendering WC relatively unstable and leading to partial dissolution with the release of W and C elements. Meanwhile, the relatively high carbon activity in the Fe-based melt provides favorable conditions for carbide formation, promoting the preferential growth of Fe₇C₃ and Fe₃C during solidification.

From a kinetic perspective, elemental migration is jointly driven by concentration gradients and temperature gradients, and its diffusion behavior can be estimated based on classical diffusion relationships:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x \approx \sqrt {Dt}$$\end{document}where x represents the diffusion distance, D is the diffusion coefficient, and t denotes the high-temperature residence time. During the laser cladding process, the molten pool exists under a transient high-temperature state (approximately 10−3–10−2 s), while the diffusion coefficients of W and C in liquid Fe are typically on the order of 10⁻⁸–10⁻⁹ m2/s. Based on these parameters, the calculated diffusion distance ranges from approximately 10 to 80 μm, which is in good agreement with the width of the W and C enrichment regions observed in the EDS results (approximately 20–70 μm). This consistency indicates that the W and C elements released from the dissolution of WC particles can undergo effective diffusion within the molten pool and subsequently participate in carbide formation during rapid solidification. Therefore, the diffusion process not only determines the spatial distribution range of carbide phases but also provides a fundamental basis for the formation of compositional gradients in the interfacial region. Furthermore, this process is closely associated with subsequent grain refinement and local strain accumulation, thereby exerting a combined influence on the microstructural evolution and overall performance of the coating. In addition, under rapid solidification conditions, the combined effects of temperature gradients and molten pool convection further promote elemental redistribution, leading to more pronounced enrichment regions near the interface.

In the transition layer, grains show pronounced preferred orientation, growing along the molten pool boundary, reflecting a columnar grain structure formed under the combined influence of thermal gradient and solidification rate. The substrate region displays large grains with random orientation distribution, retaining the original annealing characteristics. Analysis of mechanical properties

To further clarify the correspondence between the microstructural characteristics of the coating and its macroscopic mechanical performance, the relationships among grain structure, carbide distribution, dislocation characteristics, and property variations were analyzed in conjunction with the above microstructural characterization results. Figure 9 illustrates the microhardness distribution characteristics of the laser-clad WC-reinforced Fe-based alloy coating along the thickness direction. The cladding layer exhibits the highest hardness, averaging approximately 892 ± 25 HV0.2, indicating a dense microstructure with significant grain refinement. The transition layer shows a slight decrease in hardness, averaging about 715 ± 20 HV0.2, reflecting the structural transformation from fine equiaxed grains to oriented columnar grains. The substrate region displays the lowest hardness, with an average value of approximately 498 ± 18 HV0.2, maintaining the softening characteristics of the original annealed structure. Compared with Fe-based cladding coatings without hard reinforcement phases, whose hardness is primarily constrained by the matrix microstructure and thus unable to form effective dispersion-strengthened structures, the WC-reinforced Fe-based coating in this study exhibits a higher hardness level in the cladding layer region. Moreover, a more pronounced gradient variation along the depth direction is observed compared with NiCrBSi/WC coatings, indicating a superior overall strengthening effect. The significant enhancement in hardness of the cladding layer is mainly attributed to the multiple strengthening mechanisms associated with WC particles. On the one hand, WC acts as heterogeneous nucleation sites during solidification, effectively refining the Fe-based matrix grains. On the other hand, the dispersed carbides formed at high temperatures, such as Fe₃W₃C and Fe₇C₃, enhance the load-bearing capacity and impede dislocation motion. In addition, KAM analysis reveals higher intragranular orientation gradients and strain accumulation in regions surrounding WC particles, indicating a markedly increased dislocation storage capacity. This further enhances strain hardening capability and contributes significantly to the improvement in hardness. The intermediate hardness observed in the transition layer reflects the combined effects of higher thermal input, partial dissolution of carbides, and enhanced grain orientation, whereas the relatively lower hardness of the substrate is associated with its coarse-grained structure and the absence of strengthening phases. Overall, the variation in hardness along the thickness direction corresponds closely to the spatial distributions of grain size, carbide dispersion, and dislocation density, indicating that the microstructural gradient plays a critical role in determining the mechanical performance.Fig. 9Microhardness distribution along the thickness direction of the coating–substrate system.

Discussion

This study prepared WC-reinforced Fe-based alloy coatings via laser cladding and systematically analyzed their strengthening behavior from the perspectives of microstructural evolution and performance variation. During the cladding process, WC particles undergo partial dissolution and react with the Fe-based melt to form carbide phases such as Fe₃W₃C and Fe₇C₃, which contribute to the strengthening of the coating in terms of both microstructure and mechanical properties. Based on EDS, XRD, and EBSD analyses, the diffusion behavior of elements released from WC dissolution and their spatial distribution characteristics were further examined, allowing the strengthening pathway to be interpreted in terms of element diffusion, carbide formation, and microstructural evolution. The results indicate that W and C elements form enrichment zones near the interface, showing consistent trends with grain refinement and local strain accumulation. Compared with Fe-based coatings without hard reinforcement, which are typically dominated by coarse dendritic or columnar grains and exhibit limited strengthening mechanisms, the introduction of WC promotes carbide formation and dispersion through dissolution and diffusion processes, while also acting as heterogeneous nucleation sites during solidification, thereby influencing the overall microstructure. At the microscale, the synergistic effects of grain refinement strengthening, dislocation strengthening, and carbide strengthening collectively enhance the hardness, wear resistance, and corrosion resistance of the coating. Overall, key microstructural parameters—such as grain size, carbide distribution, and dislocation density—show consistent correlations with hardness, wear resistance, and corrosion performance, with aligned trends between structural features and property responses.

With increasing WC content, the spatial distribution of the reinforcement phase in the cladding layer gradually evolves. At moderate WC content, particles are uniformly dispersed, facilitating the formation of a stable multiphase strengthening structure. However, at higher WC content, local enrichment of particles is observed, consistent with the microstructural characterization, indicating a transition from uniform dispersion to localized aggregation. While such local enrichment can significantly enhance regional hardness, it may also induce stress concentration and increase susceptibility to crack initiation, reflecting a trade-off between strengthening efficiency and structural stability. Furthermore, as the interparticle spacing decreases, the connectivity between reinforcement phases increases, and a quasi-continuous rigid skeleton may form locally, approaching a percolation threshold. Under these conditions, the load transfer mechanism shifts from matrix-dominated to reinforcement-dominated, which benefits wear resistance but may compromise overall toughness. Based on the comparative analysis of different WC contents, within the investigated range of 20–40 wt%, the coating with 30 wt% WC achieves an optimal balance between strengthening effectiveness and structural stability.

Compared with existing studies, this work provides a more refined analysis of the correlation between WC particle dissolution behavior and element diffusion processes. Through the combined use of EDS and XRD, the dissolution of WC during laser cladding and the subsequent migration of W and C elements are systematically interpreted at the experimental level, thereby offering additional insight into the underlying mechanisms and complementing current understanding in this area. Wei et al. investigated the influence of WC on the microstructure and wear resistance of Fe-based coatings fabricated by laser cladding, noting that the strengthening effect of WC is primarily attributable to the dispersion and precipitation of carbides. However, the mechanisms governing WC dissolution and elemental migration at elevated temperatures were not systematically elucidated35. Through integrated EDS and XRD analyses, the present study reveals the dynamic process of ″partial WC dissolution—elemental diffusion—carbide formation,″ addressing the lack of qualitative mechanistic insight in previous work. Meanwhile, Liverani et al. prepared multilayer WC–Co coatings via direct energy deposition and reported that laser remelting can mitigate interface defects and improve bonding strength36. However, its research mainly focuses on macro defect control, and this study further proves that more uniform WC distribution and smoother interfacial diffusion layer can be obtained by adjusting the powder feeding rate and scanning overlap rate, thus making a breakthrough in the synergistic improvement of microstructure stability and mechanical properties. Li et al. found that the laser power has a significant effect on the retention rate and phase structure transformation of WC particles when studying the laser directional energy deposition behavior of WC-12Co composite coatings, but its analysis mainly focused on the relationship between energy density and macro-properties, and lacked systematic explanation at the organizational level37. In contrast, by combining EBSD, KAM distribution and microhardness analysis, this study reveals the mechanism of WC promoting grain refinement and strain accumulation from the micro level, and realizes the internal relationship between microstructure evolution and performance response.

This study systematically analyzes the role of WC particles during the laser cladding process by integrating the effects of rapid solidification and carbide phase formation, and summarizes their strengthening pathways from the perspective of microstructural evolution. The results provide supplementary experimental evidence for understanding the interactions between the reinforcement phase and the matrix in composite systems. In addition, by correlating KAM-based orientation differences with microstructural features, the relationship between grain refinement strengthening and dislocation accumulation is discussed, thereby further deepening the understanding of structure–property correlations to a certain extent.

Despite the relatively comprehensive results obtained in this study, certain constraints should be acknowledged. Firstly, the investigated process parameters do not fully cover the microstructural control mechanisms associated with different energy densities, potentially limiting the broader applicability of the findings. Secondly, performance characterization was mainly carried out under ambient temperature and static loading conditions, without considering service performance in high-temperature or cyclic-load scenarios. Lastly, although the role of WC particle size and distribution in microstructural evolution and property synergy has been qualitatively identified, quantitative analysis via statistical or modeling methods has yet to be performed, which constrains a more rigorous verification of the strengthening mechanisms.

Future research should involve systematic experimentation over a wide range of energy densities and scanning speeds, combined with multiphysics numerical simulation to develop a quantitative coupling model linking energy input to microstructural evolution. Such an approach would reveal the dynamic behavior of grain refinement, phase transformation, and stress distribution under different thermal input conditions. Furthermore, investigations into service performance under combined high-temperature, cyclic load, and corrosion conditions are warranted, utilizing in situ microscopic techniques and fatigue life prediction models to understand failure mechanisms in complex service environments, thereby enhancing the service reliability assessment system for these coatings. In addition, the introduction of machine learning or statistical regression models is suggested to quantitatively establish relationships between WC particle size, distribution density, and microstructural parameters, facilitating predictable microstructure optimization and intelligent parameter design. This would offer a theoretical basis and methodological support for the accurate preparation of high-performance iron-based composite coatings.
