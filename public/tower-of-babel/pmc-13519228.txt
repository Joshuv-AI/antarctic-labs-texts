Participant Experience, Acceptability and Feasibility of Virtual and Digitally Supported Visits in a Phase IIa COPD Trial
Authors: Renate Kat, Wytske van der Veen, Yoran H. Gerritsma, Marika T. Leving, William Dott, Olga Elizarova, Rod Hughes, Caroline Jonstrand, Cecilia Kristensson, Andrzej Nowojewski, Alex Wheal, Janwillem W. H. Kocks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding participant experience is crucial for improving participant satisfaction, retention, data quality, and clinical trial efficiency; particularly when incorporating digital patient solutions and virtual elements. CRESCENDO, an international phase-II chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) trial, incorporated daily at-home digital measurements, remote coached spirometry, and a virtual visit to evaluate feasibility and participant acceptance.This study explored participant experience in CRESCENDO through semi-structured interviews (n=21) and a Study Participant Feedback Questionnaire (SPFQ, n=81), conducted across Bulgaria, Canada, Poland, Spain, UK, and USA. Interview data were analysed thematically.Participants were generally satisfied and found digital and virtual elements user-friendly, although technical issues caused frustration. When functioning properly, participants considered remote and in-person spirometry coaching comparable. Nonetheless, participants preferred in-person visits due to positive interactions with trial staff, which increased satisfaction and feeling of appreciation. High concordance between interview and SPFQ data supported the validity of qualitative interviews for assessing participant experience.The study highlights the potential of digital elements and offers practical recommendations, like providing options for in-person and virtual visits, to enhance satisfaction and engagement.This qualitative sub-study was conducted within the CRESCENDO trial (https://clinicaltrials.gov reference: NCT05492877).

Introduction

The complexity of clinical trial designs may hinder participant recruitment, compliance, and retention. 1 In the context of clinical trials, patient experience can be assumed to be the first-hand knowledge a person has on receiving health-care services. 2 Therefore, to increase efficiency and improve participant experience, it is crucial to obtain feedback from participants and identify aspects of the trial design that may require improvement. 3 Although opportunities to enhance patient engagement in clinical trials have increased, more work is needed. 3

Participant feedback becomes especially valuable when incorporating novel procedures, such as digital patient solutions aimed at reducing overall participant burden.3,4 Digital solutions, such as smartphones, clinical devices or wearables, have become increasingly available over the past years. 5 When tested and implemented well, such solutions enable remote trial participation and real-time data-collection on outcomes that matter to participants, increasing medical service accessibility (e.g., by reducing in-person visits). This could reduce trial burden for participants and increase the amount and quality of clinical trial data. 4

The CRESCENDO trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of a novel myeloperoxidase (MPO) inhibitor in patients with COPD, a disease that impacts daily life and can make everyday tasks challenging. For this trial, participants performed home-based measurements twice-daily for 24 weeks. 6 Multiple digital patient solutions were implemented, which were hypothesized to improve participant experience and reduce participant burden. The primary and secondary endpoints were collected using the UNIFY application(Unify (Unified Trial Solution) is a clinical trial solution developed by AstraZeneca with modular features such as eCOA, telehealth, home supply and others. The solution includes a patient mobile application and a platform for healthcare professionals.) on a phone and a hand-held spirometer (connected to the phone via Bluetooth). The trial also incorporated virtual trial elements, namely a virtual trial visit and virtually coached spirometry, both performed through a video-connection (e.g., Zoom).

Besides general experiences and satisfaction of participants, CRESCENDO enabled the assessment of the feasibility and acceptability of these innovative digital patient solutions and virtual elements, including virtually coached spirometry, 6 from a participant’s perspective. Remote spirometry shows promise particularly for clinical trials as it correlates well with in-clinic measurements and reduces logistical burden for participants thereby contributing to increased engagement.7-10 However, to meet the requirements of the European Medicines Agency, such as the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society standards for spirometry, 11 remote coaching remains crucial to ensure accuracy and consistency of the collected data. Studies on AI-based quality control tools support the need for coaching to maintain high-quality remote spirometry. 12 It is therefore necessary to inspect whether remote spirometry is equivalent to in-person spirometry assessments.

Participant feedback is essential to determine practical feasibility and acceptance of remote spirometry, 13 ensuring that the advancing technology is implemented in ways that optimise trial experience, ultimately enhancing recruitment, compliance and retention. 14 For instance, in the case of remote spirometry, previous studies have shown that its implementation is feasible when offering care to patients with COPD.15-18 However, findings about the trial-related experiences of patients with COPD with remote spirometry are very scarce.18,19 Thus, this study aimed to gather more in-depth information about participants’ experiences, with a specific focus on the feasibility and acceptance of the virtual elements introduced in the trial. Through participant interviews, we aimed to gain insights into how these digital technologies are perceived and to identify any barriers or concerns that might hinder the use of these technologies.

Methods

Study Design

This qualitative sub-study was conducted within the CRESCENDO trial (https://clinicaltrials.gov reference: NCT05492877). CRESCENDO is a Phase-IIa randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel arm, multi-centre trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of AZD4831, a myeloperoxidase inhibitor, in moderate to severe COPD. Throughout this manuscript, we will refer to CRESCENDO as “trial”, and to our study as “study”.

The trial consisted of 8 visits (Vs) (Figure 1). V1 and V2 were screening visits. At V3, participants were randomized into the 24-week intervention period during which participants performed the home measurements twice-daily. During the intervention period, participants completed V4-7. Remote coached spirometry was performed during V3-5 & V7. At V3 and V5, participants first underwent face-to-face spirometry on-site, followed by virtual spirometry via video link. At V4 and V7, virtual supervised, coached spirometry were performed away from site prior to attendance at the visit. V6 was fully virtual. This visit was performed through the UNIFY platform videoconference option. The trial was completed by a follow-up visit (V8).

This study was added after commencement of the CRESCENDO trial and performed in a subset of the participating countries, namely: Bulgaria (BGR), Canada (CND), Poland (POL), Spain (ESP), United Kingdom (GBR), and the United States of America (USA). These countries were selected to include geographical and cultural diversity within the study sample. Participants were only interviewed once and interview timing was semi-randomized to take place after V3 (n=5), V5 (n=9) or V7 (n=7) to gather contextualized feedback. The study enrolled participants while the trial was ongoing, so those recruited after earlier visits (e.g. V3) were only eligible for interviews at later timepoints. The median number of days between visit and interview was 8 days (range: 0-20).

From all participants who consented, we applied purposive sampling at the trial-site level, aiming to maximize the number of trial sites from which participants were included in the study.

The study was approved by medical ethical committees in the participating countries. In Bulgaria, Canada, the United Kingdom and the United States, participants received an e-gift voucher their participation for a reasonable amount corresponding to their invested time, in the local currency. In Spain, participants received a financial compensation via bank-transfer. Polish participants could not be reimbursed due to local regulations. The study is reported in accordance with Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) criteria 20 (for COREQ checklist see Appendix I).

Participants

This study was implemented as part of the fourth version of the clinical study protocol of the CRESCENDO trial. All randomized participants of CRESCENDO in the participating countries were eligible for participation in this study. Trial participants were between 40 and 80 years of age, had moderate to severe COPD with risk of exacerbations, and no current or past diagnosis of asthma. A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found at https://clinicaltrials.gov. Participants were invited for participation in the study by the trial staff at their research site.

Interviews were conducted in the participants native language by local moderators. Moderators from Bulgaria (male, Prof. MD), Spain (female, PhD MD) and Poland (male, Prof. MD) were clinical doctors and researchers. Moderators for Canada/United States (female, BCom) and United Kingdom (male, MSc) were qualitative researchers working as consultants. All received training on qualitative versus clinical interview techniques, the CRESCENDO trial, study objectives and the interview guide. Feedback on interview (technique) was provided using a standardized form and elucidated in a video-call with the study team. Feedback started after the first interview and was repeated after until all items were resolved. Moderators had no prior relationship with the participants. At the start of the interview moderators clarified their role and that participant responses would not be shared with site staff. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. No field notes were taken during the interviews and transcripts were not returned to participants or moderators for commenting/correction. Interviews were translated to English and transcripts were anonymized before analysis.

Study Participant Feedback Questionnaire (SPFQ)

To gather participant feedback on their understanding of clinical trial procedures and experiences, the Study Participant Feedback Questionnaire (SPFQ) was used. The SPFQ was developed by TransCelerate BioPharma. 21 It was validated for diverse geographies and indications, 22 and is divided into three parts (Appendix V) which participants were asked to complete after different trial stages: Part A (within 1 month of enrolment), Part B (during trial), and Part C (post-trial). It assesses satisfaction with procedures, understanding of information, daily life impact, ability to ask questions, quality of responses, and post-trial support. The questionnaire contains 17 questions of which 12 (A1-A4, B1-B6, and C3-4) use a 5-point Likert scale (“strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”), four (B7, B8, C1 and C2) use a binary yes/no scale and one (C5) uses a reverse 5-point Likert scale (“much less than expected” to “much more than expected”). In this study, the SPFQ was implemented in December 2023 in six countries (BGR, CAN, ESP, GBR, POL, USA). SPFQ has recently been used as a measure of participant experience in an oncology study, where they assessed the SPFQ design and implementation in depth. 23

Data Analysis

Data were analysed continuously using thematic analysis. The initial coding tree, based on the interview guide, was iteratively updated in response to the data, following a grounded theory approach which was selected for its suitability to the novelty of the study. Classic Glaserian grounded theory was implemented in our study, since the Glaserian approach emphasizes the attempt to discover unbiased knowledge about the world. 24 For the thematic analysis, coding was conducted by two researchers (R.K., PhD and W.v.d.V., MA) and clinical oversight of data analysis was provided by J.W.H.K. (Prof. MD.). The GPRI research institute participated in the CRESCENDO trial as a research site, giving R.K., W.v.d.V., Y.H.G. and J.W.H.K. in-depth insight into the study procedures, but GPRI did not recruit for this sub-study to prevent bias. The first seven interviews were independently double-coded by both researchers in the Dedoose (v9.2.6, SocioCultural Research Consultants, Los Angeles, CA). Reconciliation meetings to refine the coding tree took place after each interview. After the seventh interview, the calculated inter-rater reliability as a percentage of agreement of applied codes on pre-defined excerpts was sufficient (80.2%). Therefore, coding continued as single coding, where R.K. coded eight interviews and W.v.d.V. coded six interviews. Reconciliation meetings took place every five interviews to discuss additions to the coding tree (identified themes and codes in Figure 2). All interviews were recoded based on the updated coding tree.Figure 2.Coding tree. The coding tree presents an overview of the identified (sub-) themes in the study. Saturation was defined as an absence of new codes or perspectives within a code for three consecutive interviews. Representative participant quotes were selected to best reflect the full participant sample and the various countries and identified by coded participant number and country.

To analyse the participants’ views on the trial staff, descriptive terms in the sub-theme “interactions with trial staff” were extracted from all coded excerpts without limit per excerpt or participant. A word cloud was created using the wordcloud2 package in R. 25

Descriptive statistics on the participant population and the word cloud analysis were performed in R (v4.4.1, RStudio v2024.04.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Participants did not provide feedback on the findings that are shared in this article.

Overall Experience

Participants in the CRESCENDO trial reported overall satisfaction with their trial participation:“I am generally satisfied. Very nice people, very kind and I really regret that it has already ended”. (P08, POL)

I said, no, you know, nothing for an hour and a half. We worked with them, and they could not get it. They were on the helpline. And so finally, she came in and said, all right, you go home. Discussion

Incorporating patients as a partner in their care enhances study design, promoting more relevant and innovative outcomes. 3 This study explored experiences of participants enrolled into a multi-country phase IIa clinical trial in COPD, CRESCENDO, focusing on the feasibility and acceptability of digital patient solutions and virtual trial procedures, including virtually coached spirometry. The CRESCENDO study was innovative with its digital solutions and included patients as part of the Clinical Project Team to develop the study. For these two reasons, the CRESCENDO study was selected for the current patient experience sub-study.

Overall, participants were very satisfied with their trial participation in CRESCENDO. The acceptability and feasibility of the digital patient solutions used for the daily collection of data at home were generally high as they were considered easy to use, though technical issues caused frustration.

Although the SPFQ contained no questions specific to digital solutions, >95% of SPFQ respondents found the time required for data collection (questions B7) and the impact of the trial on daily activities (questions B8) acceptable. Many participants reported difficulties with setting up the video-connections required for remote spirometry and virtual visits. When functioning, virtually coached spirometry was well received. This mirrors findings from a cystic fibrosis study, where the method was well accepted but hampered by technical issues with video camera and device connectivity. 26 Minimizing technical issues is therefore critical for implementation of digital patient solutions and virtual trial elements. Previous research 27 has offered recommendations for this. For instance, to have video connections that run as smoothly as possible, environmental distractions must be avoided (e.g., by silencing phone notifications and/or having the appointment in a quiet room). Regarding device connectivity, by combining thorough Quality Assurance (QA) (i.e.,process of systematically testing an app for defects) with User Acceptance Testing (UAT) (i.e.,real users verifying that the app works correctly in real-world scenarios), the likelihood of technical glitches appearing during the clinical trial can be significantly reduced. Also, connectivity issues must be troubleshot 5-10 minutes before the start of the time scheduled for the visit and, if they still occurred, the video can be turned off and/or the participants can be offered a phone call instead. 27

Most participants preferred in-person visits, mainly because they enjoyed the interactions with trial staff and for many this was valued more than reduced travel burden. The SPFQ showed >95% responders found their trial visits well-organized (question B2) and scheduled conveniently (questions B3) supporting diminished differences between in-person and virtual visits. This supports our finding that personal interactions remain important and appreciated during a clinical trial. However, making sure that patients have autonomy to choose between in-person and online visits is paramount. There is a nuanced balance between preferring in-person visits and accepting virtual visits under certain conditions. For example, a 2023 survey of over 12,000 participants found that 58% considered “having some study visits be virtual” a reason to stay enrolled in a study. 28 Conversely, a large survey study in the USA found lower acceptance of virtual clinical care visits when patients had a prior relationship with the clinician, regardless of age. 29

Importantly, not all participants experienced virtual trial elements at the time they were interviewed, as these occurred later in the trial. Therefore, the virtual trial elements were only discussed with a subset of the participants. Further studies exploring participants’ experience of virtual visits specifically in clinical trials would help inform future trials.

Participant perspectives concerning virtually coached spirometry, the virtual visit and the preference for provided versus bring your own device (BYOD) varied. Participant characteristics (e.g., digital literacy, geography, employment status) may affect the personal preferences. The mean age in this study was 67 years, and earlier studies comparing provided devices and BYOD found a similar split in preference in a COPD population (mean age 59), 30 while a younger population with chronic health conditions (mean age 49) preferred BYOD. 31 Offering options to suit participant preferences can enhance acceptability of trial elements and thereby enhance participant recruitment, compliance and retention. Future studies into participant experiences may elucidate general preferences in different (patient) populations.

This study highlighted the central role that trial staff play in shaping participants’ experiences. Participants were almost exclusively positive about the trial staff, both in the interviews and the SPFQ (questions A4, B4-6). Staff influenced motivation, satisfaction with communication preference for in-person visits and perceived appreciation for participation. Additionally, when experiencing technical issues, the hindrance to acceptability of the digital patient solutions, participants turned to the site staff for assistance instead of the available helpdesk. Supporting trial staff in their central role will enhance participants’ trial experience.

The multinational scope of this study provided some insights into cultural differences, though this was not an objective of the current study and the sample size does not allow for strong conclusions. Two themes were identified. First, time-windows (especially PM) for daily data collection were considered too narrow in Spain and North America, but not in Eastern Europe. Differences may reflect cultural rhythms of activities outside the home suggesting a benefit of regionally adapted time-windows. Second, most participants from the USA indicated that financial compensation motivated them to join the trial, similar to previous findings in the USA,32,33 while all participants who said they would have participated without compensation were from the UK.

There is little research assessing cultural differences in participants’ clinical trial experience, but for example another study found that as compared to ‘white’ participants, British South-Asian participants preferred female trial staff among other differences. 34 Accommodating such cultural differences may contribute to optimising diverse and inclusive participation in clinical trials. 35

Interviews were consistent with SPFQ responses, strengthening the validity of the interview results and the utility of the SPFQ to measure patient experience. Due to late introduction of the SPFQ in only a subset of countries, we lack a full global picture, early-stage data and Part A response rates.

The lower Part C response rate may be explained by the change to the schedule of activities once the target number of events was reached for the study. Participants might have been reluctant to complete another SPFQ feedback so quickly. A lower completion rate potentially bias the results and so in the future it may be important to implement the SPFQ as a mandatory feedback mechanism. 23

When interpreting the results of this study, it is important to keep selection and recall bias in mind. Although participation in the study was offered to all participants of included countries, more motivated participants may have been more likely to join this sub-study. Those who withdrew from CRESCENDO were not included, potentially excluding more negative perspectives. Nevertheless, the study presents a heterogenous population with a range of perspectives on the trial experience.

To minimize recall bias, participants were interviewed at various time-points. No differences were found in participant responses between time-points, though some participants struggled to recall trial elements and procedures. The interview guide was updated twice to improve clarity, which did improve recognition, but recall limitations likely remained. Furthermore, the study design did not allow linking SPFQ responses and interviews at the individual level, preventing direct comparison. Still, findings from both methods were consistent, highlighting their complimentary value. Interviews can help clarify trends captured with the SPFQ and support targeted trial improvements.

Regarding the thematic analysis, we acknowledge that saturation was not achieved for certain themes (due to the limitation in continuing participants recruitment). However, we do expect that the most relevant topics in the themes were uncovered.

In conclusion, this study offers insight into experiences of patients with COPD as participants in an international clinical trial. It highlights the promise of digital and virtual trial elements, opportunities for improvement (e.g., minimizing technical issues and maximizing flexibility), and the importance of study site staff. Practical recommendations for designing a clinical trial, as identified in this study, include ensuring site staff is well-trained in use of digital tools and support, testing digital solutions in advance, allowing choice between virtual or in-person elements, and offering preferred devices/operating systems (see Box 1).
