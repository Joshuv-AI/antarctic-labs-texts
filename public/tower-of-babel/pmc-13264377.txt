AI policy instrument and urban carbon emission intensity: Spatial analysis of Chinese cities
Authors: Hui Yu, Ling Tang, Ziyu Qin, Zhuoming He, Yan Huang, Jianhui Ruan, Shouyang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryArtificial intelligence (AI) can enhance efficiency and reduce emissions, yet also intensifies energy demand through computation and infrastructure. This paradox raises a key question: whether AI policy instrument can reduce urban carbon emission intensity (CI)? Using the quasi-natural experiment of “National New Generation Artificial Intelligence Innovation Development Pilot Zone” (AIPZ), this paper investigates the policy’s local and spillover impacts on urban CI. Results show that AIPZ reduces CI in pilot cities and neighboring regions. Mechanism analysis reveals that the improvements in labor productivity and green technology innovation serve as main mitigation pathways across local and neighboring areas, while rising computing power demand increases emissions. Energy efficiency gains, though effective in reducing local CI, generate no significant spatial spillovers. Together, the mitigation effects dominate, yielding a net carbon-mitigating outcome especially in eastern, resource-based, and high digital-infrastructure cities. These findings offer insights for fostering AI development via coordinated regional strategies.Subject areas: Applied sciences, Artificial intelligence, Environmental science

Introduction

Climate change has become a serious global challenge, with increasing frequency of extreme weather events and widespread impacts on ecosystems and human society.1 China, the biggest carbon emitter globally, has proactively engaged in climate change, incorporating the goal of reducing carbon emission intensity into its overall plan for economic and social growth,2 and implementing a range of initiatives aimed at enhancing energy conservation and emission reduction, committed to achieving coordinated progress between economic development and green transformation.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has risen to prominence, permeating diverse sectors and propelling the evolution of contemporary low-carbon development.3 AI is the science and technology employing computer technology to imitate human intelligent behaviors, enabling machines to perceive, learn, reason, and make decisions to complete complex tasks.4 The application of AI offering capabilities for advanced data analysis, predictive modeling, and resource optimization across sectors, including energy, transportation, and urban management.5,6 To further institutionalize and scale AI adoption, China launched “National New Generation Artificial Intelligence Innovation Development Pilot Zone” (AIPZ) policy in August 2019. Unlike individual AI applications, AIPZ represents a policy-driven, city-level institutional experiment capable of shaping economic structure, technology adoption, and energy use patterns. Policy implementation can drive the adoption of AI, which may improve efficiency and reduce emissions,7 yet may also increase energy consumption through computational demands and data center construction.8 This paradox raises a fundamental question: whether AI policy instrument can effectively lower urban carbon emission intensity (CI)?

Despite growing interest, empirical research on the role of AIPZ in promoting urban low-carbonization remains limited. Existing studies have predominantly centered on the mitigation impacts of specific AI applications, often relying on AI indicators such as industrial robot use9 or the number of AI patents.10 While these metrics offer valuable insights, they capture only fragmented aspects of AI deployment.11 Emerging studies have begun to resort to AIPZ, assessing its effects on urban environmental performance, such as carbon emissions and environmental pollution,8,12 yet no study systematically investigates the potential negative environmental impacts brought about by AI, as well as the spillover effects in non-pilot areas, a critical consideration given the typical “pilot-diffusion-promotion” policy sequence.13 These research gaps, therefore, limit understanding of how AI policies function to reduce urban carbon emission intensity.

This paper uses spatial econometric methods to study the effectiveness of AIPZ, aiming to address the following issues: (1) does the establishment of AIPZ reduce urban CI? (2) Considering spatial correlation, does the AIPZ have spillover effects on CI in surrounding areas? (3) What are the possible mechanisms that could mediate the above effects? (4) Do differences exist in the effectiveness of policies among cities with heterogeneous characteristics?

The primary contributions of this paper are as follows. First, leveraging the quasi-experimental setting of the AIPZ policy, this paper quantifies both its direct and spatial spillover effects on urban CI, bridging a key gap in prior studies. Second, by integrating production, innovation, energy, and computing power channels, we identify the mechanisms through which AI policy instrument translates into positive or negative environmental benefits, advancing theoretical understanding of technology-driven policy effects. Third, this paper uncovers the heterogeneous policy impacts across urban contexts (city location, resource endowment, and digital infrastructure), offering solid evidence for promoting AI strategies in a location-specific manner.

The structure of the rest of this paper is arranged as follows. The policy background section introduces the relevant policy context. The theorized analysis section develops the theoretical arguments and derives the research hypotheses. The results section presents the empirical findings. The discussion section draws conclusions and presents policy recommendations.

Policy background

In July 2017, the State Council of China issued the New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan, officially positioning AI as a national strategic technology and a key industrial direction.14 To accelerate the deep integration of AI with economic and social development, the Ministry of Science and Technology launched the AIPZ program in August 2019, clearly defining the policy objectives, implementation process, and institutional design of the pilot zones.15 In this sense, the AIPZ initiative represents a policy instrument designed to translate strategic technological vision into structured governance practice. From the perspective of policy objectives, the pilot zones are designed to serve as experimental platforms for institutional innovation. By 2023, around 20 pilot zones were planned nationwide. These zones are expected to explore effective policy instruments that facilitate the integration of AI and the real economy. Through localized experimentation, the pilot zones aim to generate replicable and scalable development models, ultimately forming AI innovation “highlands” with significant demonstration and spillover effects. Notably, the core objective of the policy is not the direct creation of AI technologies per se, but the optimization of the innovation ecosystem in which such technologies can be developed, deployed, and commercialized. In terms of implementation process, the AIPZ program adopts a whole-process management model characterized by a progressive mechanism of “local application—expert evaluation—construction initiation—operational management—demonstration and promotion.” Local governments first submit applications based on their scientific resources, industrial foundations, and application scenarios. After expert assessment and approval, pilot zone construction is formally initiated. Subsequent stages emphasize standardized operational management and performance evaluation, culminating in the dissemination of successful experiences to other regions. This staged governance approach reflects a combination of central coordination and local experimentation, ensuring policy coherence while facilitating the formation of scalable governance models. Regarding institutional design, pilot zone construction emphasizes local authorities and policy pioneering. Authorities are encouraged to experiment with data governance, achievement transformation, talent development, and fiscal incentives, complemented by improved infrastructure construction, organizational safeguards, and evaluation mechanisms. Through these institutional arrangements, the pilot zones function as structured environments for testing governance innovations alongside technological applications. Overall, the construction of pilot zones provides an important platform for the deployment and industrialization of AI technologies, opening new pathways for urban low-carbon and high-quality development.

Theorized analysis

The AIPZ, as an innovative policy tool, aims to facilitate the close combination of AI technology and economic and social progress. It intends to accelerate the construction of a new society driven by intelligence and has obvious green attributes.8 According to the externality theory, the construction of AIPZ can mitigate the negative externalities arising from production and consumption activities by promoting the development of environmental regulatory technologies.16 From the production side, AI technology is applied throughout all aspects of production activities. Advanced algorithms and optimization models can effectively maintain the continuity and stability of production, while significantly lessening energy consumption and corresponding emissions.17,18 From the consumer side, AI also significantly contributes to the green transformation of the consumer market and consumption patterns.19 For example, by leveraging intelligent technologies, AI can help promote green consumption, optimize consumer behavior and decision-making, and reduce unnecessary carbon emissions.20 However, the AIPZ may also have negative impacts on reducing urban CI. First, there is a “high-carbon paradox” in AI infrastructure, as the construction of supporting facilities such as data centers and computing power networks increases energy consumption.8 Second, pilot cities may prioritize ensuring the expansion of the scale of the AI industry (such as intelligent equipment manufacturing), squeezing the low-carbon investment funds of traditional high-carbon industries like steel and chemical industries, and hindering progress toward low-carbon development targets.21

Thus, the following hypotheses are established.

Hypothesis 1a. AIPZ reduces CI in pilot cities.

Hypothesis 1b. AIPZ increases CI in pilot cities.

The evolution and deployment of AI can generate reciprocal benefits and regional spillovers via cross-regional factor flow.22 At the same time, carbon emissions also have spatial mobility and may change due to policy factors.23 Therefore, it is valuable to investigate the spatial spillover effects of AIPZ. From the positive perspective, AIPZ may promote the demonstration effect among regions. When pilot cities achieve positive results in policy implementation, other regions will follow the example of the pilot cities in AI technology R&D, AI scenario application, and management measures, thereby reducing CI of surrounding non-pilot areas.24 Meanwhile, AIPZ may enhance the inter-regional linkage effect. Adjacent cities can achieve resource complementation and optimal utilization through sharing resources and regional cooperation with the pilot cities, thereby reducing urban CI.25 From the negative perspective, the AIPZ may have a “siphon effect” on the surrounding areas. The AI industry is a typical capital- and technology-intensive industry. Through the signal effect and substantive subsidies, the AIPZ has created a “policy highland” in the pilot cities, which not only attracts AI enterprises but, more importantly, a large amount of capital and high-end talents that are in synergy with them.26 The resources of non-pilot cities are crowded out, leading to their low-carbon development falling into a situation of “hollowing out of elements.”

Thus, the following hypotheses are established.

Hypothesis 2a. AIPZ reduces CI in non-pilot cities.

Hypothesis 2b. AIPZ increases CI in non-pilot cities.

As a policy platform that concentrates the application, experimentation, and diffusion of AI, AIPZ reshapes the conditions under which cities pursue low-carbon development. Theoretically, AI—as a general-purpose technology—operates through two fundamental and potentially competing channels. On the one hand, AI reduces urban CI via the efficiency-enhancing channel: AI can reshape factor allocation, drive technological innovation, and optimize resource utilization, thereby lowering urban CI; on the other hand, AI increases urban CI via the energy-intensive channel: the deployment of AI systems relies heavily on data centers, algorithmic computing, and high-density digital infrastructure, which substantially increases electricity consumption and may generate a counteracting pressure on low-carbon development. Based on the above analysis, we examine the mechanisms of AIPZ from four perspectives: labor productivity, green technology innovation, energy efficiency, and computing power demand.

First, the AIPZ significantly improves labor productivity by accelerating the deployment of AI technology to automate and intelligently handle a large number of repetitive and regular work tasks.27,28 Within the framework of the neo-classical production function, this process can be understood as the optimization of the combination of capital and labor inputs through technological progress. AI, as an embedded technology, improves the marginal output efficiency of factors, enabling the same input of factors to achieve a larger output scale, that is, achieving an outward shift of the production possibility curve.29 This change can lead to more efficient and precise production processes, enabling more products or services to be produced in the same amount of time, while reducing resource consumption and carbon emissions.

Second, the AIPZ promotes the construction of AI research and development bases and open innovation platforms, providing a favorable policy environment and resource support for green technology innovation in the pilot areas. In line with endogenous growth theory, innovation input is regarded as the central driving force behind technological advancement and economic expansion.16 The setup of the AIPZ has prompted all parties to increase their R&D investment in green technology innovation, giving rise to a range of low-carbon innovation achievements.7 Furthermore, the theory of innovation diffusion posits that innovative technologies can be disseminated across social systems through social networks and policy context.30 Enterprises, universities, and research institutions form a close innovation community, and this geographical agglomeration effect accelerates the development and promotion of eco-friendly technologies,31 effectively reducing CI in local and surrounding cities.

Third, the AIPZ significantly improves energy efficiency by extensively integrating AI into the energy domain. The theory of circular economy emphasizes the establishment of a “resources-products-renewable resources” closed-loop flow model, driving the transformation of production methods through technological progress, and reducing waste at the source and enhancing system sustainability.32 AI provides strong support for the circular economy. It could improve the full life cycle of energy production, distribution, use and recycling through data analysis, predictive modeling, and intelligent control systems, thereby realizing intelligent management of energy resources.33,34,35 For example, intelligent energy management systems embedding AI technology can not only track energy consumption in real time, accurately predict demand fluctuations, and dynamically adjust energy distribution, but also conduct intelligent classification and recycling planning for waste energy materials generated by equipment such as solar panels and wind turbines.36,37 By implementing this refined management model covering the entire chain, it guides cities toward greener and more low-carbon pathways.

Thus, hypothesis 3 is established.

Hypothesis 3. Improvements in labor productivity, green technology innovation, and energy efficiency are key mechanisms through which AIPZ reduces CI in pilot and non-pilot cities.

Finally, the AIPZ may increase urban CI through the expansion of computing power demand. The deployment of AI, especially its machine learning, deep learning, and large model training stages, is highly “compute-intensive” and “energy-intensive.”38 The operation of computing power itself brings huge and continuously growing energy consumption, posing a challenge to urban low-carbon development.39 This process conforms to the theory of the “Jevons Paradox.”40 AI technology could promote emission reduction by enhancing production efficiency, promoting green technology innovation, and optimizing the energy system (technical efficiency effect), but the expansion of computing power demand it triggers (scale effect) may completely offset or even exceed its efficiency gains, ultimately leading to an increase in total carbon emissions.

Thus, hypothesis 4 is established.

Hypothesis 4. Growing computing power demand is the key mechanism through which AIPZ increases urban CI in pilot and non-pilot cities.

Results

Variable selection

The explained variable

Drawing on previous research,41 this paper employs the logarithm of carbon emission intensity, defined as the ratio of total carbon emissions to GDP. It reflects carbon emissions associated with a unit of economic output and functions as a vital measure for evaluating urban carbon efficiency. Logarithmic transformation alleviates the effects of heteroskedasticity and outliers, facilitating the economic interpretation of the estimated coefficients.42,43,44 Figure 1 shows the distribution of carbon emission intensity in 2012 and 2022.

Key explanatory variable

The key explanatory variable is defined as the interaction term of the policy dummy and the time dummy. Specifically, for cities that have launched the AI pilot, the variable is assigned as 1 both in the year of policy landing and thereafter, while for cities that have not launched the AI pilot, the variable remains at 0 throughout the study period. Mechanism variables

Drawing on the previous work, this paper identifies labor productivity (labor), green technology innovation (gti), energy efficiency (ee), and computing power demand (com) as key mechanisms. Specifically, labor productivity (labor) is gauged by the ratio of regional real GDP to the number of employed individuals.45 Green technology innovation (gti) is assessed as the logarithm of green invention patent plus one.46 Energy efficiency (ee) is calculated by using the ratio of GDP to total energy consumption (expressed in standard coal units).47 The characteristic of enterprises with computing power resources is that they own Internet data centers (IDCs).48 Therefore, in this paper, the logarithm of the cumulative number of local enterprises holding IDC licenses is used as a proxy variable for computing power demand (com).48

Control variables

We have selected the following control variables with the aim of mitigating the influence of other potential factors. Specifically, it includes: economic level (lnpgdp), measured by the logarithm of per capita GDP; population density (lnpd), calculated as the logarithm of the population per unit area; financial development (fd), determined by the year-end deposit and loan balances of financial institutions to GDP; level of consumption (consume), determined by the total retail sales of consumer goods divided by GDP; science and education expenditures (se), calculated as the share of local budget expenditure spent on science, technology, and education; government draw (gd), determined by the ratio of local general budget expenditure to general budget revenues; industrial structure (is), reflected by the ratio of the secondary industry’s added value to GDP; foreign direct investment (fdi), refers to the ratio of actually utilized FDI in GDP.

Data

This paper compiles a panel dataset encompassing 284 cities in China from 2012 to 2022. Data from Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau are excluded due to their unavailability. The raw data of carbon emissions is sourced from the ODIAC fossil fuel emission dataset, which is converted into monthly emissions of prefecture-level cities through 1 × 1 km grid data conversion and summarized to obtain the total annual carbon emissions. IDC licenses data are sourced from the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology of China, which is linked to the computing power demand for cities. Additional raw data comes from various platforms, including the China City Statistical Yearbook, the Wind Database, and official bulletins of prefecture-level cities. Parallel trend test

The parallel trend examination is conducted to determine if the trends of the two datasets were similar before the implementation of the intervention, serving as a crucial prerequisite for SDID (spatial difference-in-differences) modeling.49,50 If the parallel trend hypothesis holds, it suggests that the difference between the two groups was stable before the intervention, thus allowing for the attribution of post-intervention differences to policy or treatment effects.

Figure 4 illustrates that no obvious differences were observed between the two city groups during the period from the 5th year to the 2nd year prior to the implementation of AIPZ. Consequently, the parallel trend hypothesis is validated.

Robustness test

Placebo test

Next, this paper randomly allocates cities to the experimental group and generates fictitious policy implementation times. Figure 5 shows that after 500 iterations of estimation, the coefficients of the randomly generated policy shock variables are approximately normally distributed around zero, which contrasts with the findings obtained using the actual AI dummy variable (i.e., −0.138 in column (2) of Table 5). These findings validate that our conclusions are not spurious.

Endogeneity test

The selection of AI pilot zones is affected by various factors, including scientific and educational resources, industrial foundation, and location. Given the non-random nature of the sample selection, the tests of AIPZ validity may be subject to bias. Therefore, the propensity score matching (PSM) approach is introduced to avoid the potential endogenous bias. Specifically, we estimate propensity scores for each city using a logit regression, and then individuals are matched through the 1:5 nearest neighbor matching method. The results after matching, as shown in Table 8, indicate that the indirect effect of policy is significantly negative at the 5% level, while the direct effect and total effect are significantly negative at the 1% level. This further validates the crucial role of AIPZ in advancing local and neighboring low-carbon development.

We have also adopted an instrumental variable (IV) approach to address the endogenous bias caused by reverse causality and omitted variables. Specifically, following prior studies,54,55 we employed the city terrain ruggedness index as the IV. The validity of this IV rests on two considerations. First, terrain ruggedness reflects the complexity of local topography, which directly influences the construction and deployment costs of AI infrastructure and is closely related to the location choice of AI innovation and development zones, thereby satisfying the relevance condition. Second, as a natural geographic endowment, terrain ruggedness is plausibly exogenous to urban CI and does not directly affect it except through its impact on the endogenous explanatory variable, thus fulfilling the exogeneity condition. Table 12 shows the outcomes of the intermediate channel test of AIPZ on urban carbon emission intensity. All the coefficients of W∗M are significantly positive, indicating that they have a significant endogenous interaction effect among cities. The findings indicate that AIPZ significantly improves labor productivity (labor), promotes green technology innovation (gti), energy efficiency (ee) in pilot cities. These improvements serve as primary mitigation pathways. Specifically, enhancing labor productivity signifies a rise in the economic value created by a unit of labor force, which will encourage governments and enterprises to proactively adopt more efficient and eco-friendly production methods.58 Green technology innovation provides technical support for urban low-carbon development, effectively promoting the upgrading of industrial structure and the broad application of mitigation technologies.59 Improvements in energy efficiency, in turn, reduce the energy required per unit of output, directly lowering the carbon intensity of economic activity.60

Heterogeneity analysis

City location

According to Table 13, the coefficient on W∗lnci remains statistically significant across the sub-samples. This result implies that the inter-city dependence in carbon emission intensity is a stable feature rather than a pattern driven by a specific group of cities. To test the heterogeneous role of AIPZ on CI in cities with different regions, the sample is categorized into the eastern and the mid-western groups. Columns (1) and (2) display the results. In eastern cities, the direct and indirect effects of policy are significantly negative at least at the 5% level, suggesting that AIPZ has lowered local carbon emission intensity and simultaneously exerted a significant spatial spillover effect on the carbon emission intensity of non-pilot cities. In contrast, in mid-western cities, only the carbon emission intensity in local areas is significantly suppressed. This might be because the eastern cities have a more advanced economy and form highly mature and complex urban agglomerations.63 These cities have close industrial connections and numerous industrial clusters. When the AIPZ generates an emission reduction effect, it is more likely to spread to surrounding cities. Compared with the eastern cities, the mid-western cities are relatively weak in terms of industrial association and absorption capacity, which makes it difficult for them to produce extensive spillover effects like the eastern cities.

Resource endowment

Considering the distinct resource attributes of cities, the sample cities have been classified into resource-based and non-resource-based cities. Columns (3) and (4) of Table 13 show that AIPZ has a significant carbon emission intensity suppression effect in both city types, with a more distinct impact on resource-based cities. Generally, resource extraction and processing are the main industrial activities in these areas, with a relatively homogeneous industrial structure and a large share of heavy industries with high energy consumption and emissions.64 AIPZ is capable of effectively dismantling the traditional high-carbon production model and fully unleashing its larger emission reduction potential.

Digital infrastructure

The completeness of a city’s digital infrastructure is a key variable determining the effectiveness of AI policy implementation.65 Based on existing research, we divide the sample into “high” and “low” categories according to the frequency of new digital infrastructure in government work reports.66 Columns (5) and (6) of Table 13 reveal that cities with better digital infrastructure are more effective in enhancing the low-carbon effect of the AIPZ and exhibit a significant spatial spillover effect. The underlying logic lies in the fact that high-density digital interconnection not only amplifies the penetration depth of AI in various scenarios across cities but also aligns with sustainable development goal, ultimately magnifying the green benefits of the policy.7

Discussion

Taking the AI frontier policy as a starting point, this paper carries out an empirical analysis of AIPZ’s effect on urban CI with the panel data of 284 Chinese cities spanning 2012 to 2022, providing empirical evidence for the achievement of urban green and sustainable development.

This paper concludes as follows. (1) The AIPZ significantly not only lowers CI in the pilot cities but also demonstrates a geographic spillover effect. These results still hold true after multiple checks. This finding extends the existing literature, which has largely focused on the localized impacts of AIPZ,11,12,24 by demonstrating that AI-related policy interventions can produce environmental benefits beyond directly treated regions. Such spillover effects operate through interregional production networks, knowledge diffusion, and technology transfer. (2) The underlying mechanisms reveal that while the implementation of AIPZ will increase computational power requirements and generate additional carbon emission, this effect is outweighed by the mitigation channels. Specifically, AIPZ significantly enhances labor productivity, promotes green technology innovation, and improves energy efficiency in pilot cities, thereby reducing urban CI. Moreover, labor productivity and green technology innovation exhibit significant spatial spillovers to neighboring cities, whereas energy efficiency improvements remain mainly local and have not yet produced significant spillover effects. This finding deepens the theoretical understanding of the mechanisms through which AIPZ operates and contributes to the ongoing debate over the potential adverse environmental impacts of AI development.8,39,40 (3) The AIPZ shows heterogeneity in its impact on urban CI, with the most pronounced benefits observed in eastern, resource-based cities and those with a stronger digital infrastructure. This result aligns with prior research suggesting that digital- or AI-driven policy interventions generate differentiated environmental outcomes across regions.24,65 Specifically, the effectiveness of such policies is amplified or constrained by economic development, resource endowments, and digital readiness.

This paper makes policy recommendations in four areas based on the above findings. (1) The government should speed up the promotion and implementation of AI pilot policies by extending inclusion to cities with foundational capabilities yet not currently designated as pilot zones. Meanwhile, this effort should be accompanied by targeted financial incentives67 and technical support.68 (2) It is suggested that the government enhance regional synergy and cooperation to fully capitalize on the spatial spillover effects of AI. For example, a regional AI collaborative innovation center should be established in major city clusters to break down administrative barriers between cities and facilitate the free flow and sharing of factors within the region.69 (3) The government should promote renewable energy use in data centers,70 improve computing efficiency through advanced hardware and cooling technologies, and develop a green computing framework to reduce the environmental footprint of AI deployment.71 (4) Meanwhile, the multiple mechanisms, mainly including the improvement in labor productivity and green technology innovation, of AI should be fully leveraged to drive urban low-carbon development. This involves integrating AI into traditional industries while investing in workforce upskilling72,73 and establishing dedicated funds to support AI-driven green innovation.74

Limitations of the study

This paper comprehensively analyses the role of AIPZ on urban CI, but there is still room for future research. (1) Policy effects often need to undergo a certain time cycle to become apparent, and the relatively brief implementation duration of AIPZ makes it significantly more difficult to conduct a comprehensive assessment of its long-term impact. Therefore, future research should aim to expand the dataset and explore the impact of AIPZ over a longer time span. (2) This paper has not yet explored in depth the synergies between AIPZ and other related policies. Future research could focus on the interactions among multiple policies to assess the integrated influence of various types of policies on urban CI more comprehensively.
