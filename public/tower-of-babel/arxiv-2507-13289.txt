The Directed Spanning Forest: coalescence versus dimension
Tom Garcia-Sanchez

Abstract
For p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] p\in[1,\infty] , the ℓ p \ell^{p} directed spanning forest (DSF) of dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 is an oriented random geometric graph whose vertex set is given by a homogeneous Poisson point process 𝒩 \mathcal{N} on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} and whose edges consist of all pairs ( x , y ) ∈ 𝒩 2 (x,y)\in\mathcal{N}^{2} such that y y is the closest point to x x in 𝒩 \mathcal{N} for the ℓ p \ell^{p} distance among points with a strictly larger e d e_{d} coordinate. First introduced by Baccelli and Bordenave in 2007 in the case p = d = 2 p=d=2 , this graph has a natural forest structure: it is a collection of unrooted directed trees . In this work, we study the number of disjoint trees in the ℓ p \ell^{p} DSF for arbitrary dimensions d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 and various values of p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] p\in[1,\infty] . We prove that for p ∈ { 1 , 2 , ∞ } p\in\{1,2,\infty\} , the graph is almost surely a tree when d = 3 d=3 , and consists of infinitely many disjoint trees when d ≥ 4 d\geq 4 . Additionally, we show that for all p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] p\in[1,\infty] , the DSF in dimension 2 2 is almost surely a tree and, under appropriate diffusive scaling, converges weakly to the Brownian web, generalizing the approach of Coupier, Saha, Sarkar, and Tran (2021) for p = 2 p=2 , and thus also covering recent results of Pal and Saha (2025) for p = ∞ p=\infty . Although these results were expected from a heuristic point of view, and the main strategies and tools were largely understood, extending them beyond the planar setting ( d = 2 d=2 ) and to the singular case p = ∞ p=\infty presented a significant challenge. Notably, in the absence of planarity , which plays a crucial role in existing arguments, delicate and innovative techniques were required to manage the complex geometric dependencies of the model. We develop substantially new ideas to handle arbitrary dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 and various values of p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] p\in[1,\infty] within a unified framework. In particular, we introduce a novel stochastic domination argument that allows us to compare the fully dependent model with a simplified version in which the geometric correlations are suppressed. This relation had long been sought, but until now had never been established.



Keywords
: Stochastic geometry, Random graph, Random walk, Lyapunov function, Poisson point process, Renewal time, Brownian web.

1
Introduction

In this paper, we study the
\ell^{p}

Directed Spanning Forest
(DSF) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for arbitrary fixed dimension
d\geq 2
and exponent
p\in[1,\infty]
. The model is constructed as follows. We consider a
homogeneous Poisson point process

\mathcal{N}
of unit intensity in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
\Psi(x)
as the point of
\mathcal{N}
that realizes

\|\Psi(x)-x\|=\min\{\|y-x\|:y\in\mathcal{N},\penalty\ y\cdot e_{d}>x\cdot e_{d}\},

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the
\ell^{p}
norm,
e_{d}
denotes the
d
-th canonical basis vector, and the dot represents the standard scalar product. It is a.s. well defined as there are almost surely no two distinct points of
\mathcal{N}
at the same distance from
x
. In words,
\Psi(x)
is the closest point to
x
in
\mathcal{N}
having strictly larger
e_{d}
coordinate, for the
\ell^{p}
distance. The
\ell^{p}
DSF of dimension
d
is then defined as the
random geometric graph
whose vertex set is
\mathcal{N}
and set of directed edges is given by

\{(x,\Psi(x)):x\in\mathcal{N}\}.

By construction, all vertices have
out-degree
one and for all edge
(u,v)
we have
(v-u)\cdot e_{d}>0
, preventing the presence of
cycles
in the graph. This confers on the DSF a
forest
structure, justifying the nomenclature. In particular, the graph consists of a collection of
unrooted directed trees
. A natural question is then:
how many such trees are there?
The main goal of this work is to answer this question. To this end, it is convenient to consider
trajectories
of the DSF, i.e. infinite sequences
(v_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, with
v_{0}\in\mathcal{N}
and
v_{n+1}=\Psi(v_{n})
for all
n\geq 0
. The DSF consists of a single tree if and only if all trajectories
coalesce
, i.e. eventually coincide. Similarly, there are at least
k\geq 1
disjoint trees in the DSF if and only if we can find
k
trajectories that do not coalesce. A realization of the DSF in a finite rectangle with some trajectories is presented in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Simulation of a realization of the DSF in a finite box with
p=d=2
. We represent the edges of the DSF in gray and some trajectories in red. Note that
e_{d}
is pointing to the top in this picture, so all the edges are directed upward.

We now give an overview of the origin of the model, existing results, and related works. The DSF was introduced in 2007 by Baccelli and Bordenave
[
BB07
]
in the
planar Euclidean
case
p=d=2
, as an auxiliary model in their analysis of the
Radial Spanning Tree
(RST), another geometric random graph originally introduced to model telecommunication networks. However, the DSF quickly proved to be of independent interest, due to both its natural geometric construction and the challenging conjectures it raises, some of which were already mentioned in the original paper.

In the original setting
p=d=2
, the first conjecture posed was that the DSF is almost surely a tree. It was confirmed in 2013 by Coupier and Tran
[
CT13
]
using an efficient percolation technique that relies on the planar structure, namely the so-called Burton and Keane argument (see
[
BK89
]
). In the same setting, they also showed that there is almost surely no
bi-infinite
backward path, i.e. infinite sequence
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
with
v_{0}\in\mathcal{N}
and
\Psi(v_{n})=v_{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

In 2021, Coupier, Saha, Sarkar, and Tran
[
CSST21
]
proved a strong conjecture of Baccelli and Bordenave concerning the planar Euclidean DSF: under appropriate
diffusive scaling
, it
converges weakly
to the
Brownian web
. Notably, their proof involves sharp estimates for the coalescence time of two trajectories. Intuitively, the Brownian web can be thought of as a collection of coalescing
1
-dimensional
Brownian motions
starting at every point of space and time
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. We refer to
[
SSS17
]
for an overview of the Brownian web.

More recently, Pal and Saha
[
PS25
]
extended the coalescing time estimates of
[
CSST21
]
to the
\ell^{\infty}
DSF in dimension
2
, and proved in particular that it is almost surely a tree.

An important related work is the study of a discrete analogue of the
\ell^{1}
DSF on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
from Roy, Saha, and Sarkar
[
RSS16
]
, which shows that the resulting graph is almost surely a tree when
d\leq 3
and consists of infinitely many trees when
d\geq 4
.

It is also worth mentioning that the DSF has been studied in a hyperbolic setting by Flammant
[
Fla24
]
, who showed that the corresponding graph is almost surely a tree, whatever the dimension.

To summarize, the behavior of the DSF is now well understood in the original setting
p=d=2
, as well as to some extent for
p=\infty
in dimension 2 and in certain discrete or hyperbolic variants. However,
nothing has been done beyond the planar setting
.

The main result of this paper is the following theorem. It addresses, for the first time, the fundamental question of how many disjoint trees the DSF contains beyond the planar setting, across different values of
p
.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
d=2
and
p\in[1,\infty]
or
d=3
and
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
, the DSF is almost surely a tree. Furthermore, if
d\geq 4
and
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
, the DSF consists almost surely of an infinite collection of disjoint trees.

This result was expected: it mirrors what was established in the discrete setting by
[
RSS16
]
, and aligns with a natural heuristic that will guide our analysis throughout the paper. The trajectories of the DSF roughly behave like centered random walks in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, where the
e_{d}
direction plays the role of time. Moreover, two distant trajectories evolve approximately independently, so their difference behaves like a centered random walk in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
when far from the origin. Since coalescence amounts to asking whether this difference walk eventually hits
0
, the question essentially reduces to the recurrence or transience of such walks. Since, under suitable assumptions, centered random walks are recurrent in dimensions one and two but transient in higher dimensions (see, e.g.,
[
Dur19
, Chapter 4]
), one may conjecture that DSF trajectories coalesce if and only
d-1\leq 2
, that is, if and only if
d\leq 3
.

We expect the dimensional dichotomy in our result to hold for all
p\in[1,\infty]
. However, as will be explained later, the technical restriction to
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
when
d\neq 2
arises from limitations in a crucial stochastic domination argument developed in our analysis, which compares the fully dependent model to a simplified one where geometric correlations are suppressed. Beyond its role in our proof, this argument constitutes a significant contribution, as such a domination had long been sought but was never established until now.

As a secondary result, we also establish the following theorem concerning the dimension
d=2
. This extends the main result of
[
CSST21
]
, which treated the case
p=2
. In fact, the case
p<\infty
can be obtained through relatively straightforward adaptations of
[
CSST21
]
, and the case
p=\infty
was already nearly settled in
[
PS25
]
. Still, our framework provides a unified and simplified treatment of all values of
p
. We defer the formal definition of convergence under appropriate diffusive scaling toward the Brownian web to a dedicated section.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
p\in[1,\infty]
, the
\ell^{p}
DSF of dimension
2
converges weakly, under appropriate diffusive scaling, to the Brownian web.

Let us emphasize that studying the DSF presents significant challenges due to the complex geometrical dependencies that arise in its construction. For any
h\in\mathbb{R}
we define the
open half-space
of points whose
e_{d}
coordinates is strictly greater than
h
with

\mathbb{H}^{+}(h)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x\cdot e_{d}>h\}.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we denote by
B(x,r)
the
\ell^{p}
open ball
of radius
r
centered at
x
, and we define the
open half-ball

B^{+}(x,r)\coloneqq B(x,r)\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(x\cdot e_{d}).

In the DSF, for any
x\in\mathcal{N}
, the presence of the edge
(x,\Psi(x))
imposes that
B^{+}(x,\|\Psi(x)-x\|)
contains no points of
\mathcal{N}
. Since this half-ball typically overlaps
\mathbb{H}^{+}(\Psi(x)\cdot e_{d})
, the edge
(\Psi(x),\Psi^{2}(x))
is strongly constrained by the edge
(x,\Psi(x))
, where
\Psi^{2}\coloneqq\Psi\circ\Psi
. This heavy dependence phenomenon prevents us from directly exhibiting any Markov property while exploring iteratively a single trajectory in the DSF. Similarly, different trajectories in the DSF are significantly correlated, as the presence of one trajectory imposes strong geometric constraints for
\mathcal{N}
. An illustration of these complex dependencies is provided in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
Visualization of the geometrical constraints imposed during the construction of the DSF for
p=d=2
. In grey the half-ball that must be empty of points.

To tackle the complex correlations of the model, Coupier, Saha, Sarkar, and Tran
[
CSST21
]
proposed a method to decompose the trajectories of the DSF into quasi-independent blocks of random size. This method is at the core of their proof that the planar Euclidean DSF is almost surely a tree, and converges, under appropriate diffusive scaling, to the Brownian web. In fact, their arguments extend with minimal modification to the case
d=2
and
p\in[1,\infty)
. The delicate adaptation to the singular case
p=\infty
was recently carried out in
[
PS25
]
. However, substantial mathematical barriers prevent a straightforward extension to higher dimensions, where planarity is lost, existing difficulties are exacerbated, and entirely new challenges arise.

Structure of the paper and strategies of the proofs

In the first part of this work, we build on the ideas of
[
CSST21
]
, introducing a unified framework that extends the analysis beyond the planar Euclidean setting. This involves new techniques and arguments to address the challenges posed by higher dimensions and general values of
p
, including the singular case
p=\infty
. Notably, we establish a novel stochastic domination result of independent interest. In the second part, we combine this framework with random walk techniques to prove Theorem
1.1
, and show how Theorem
1.2
follows from the results of
[
CSST21
]
within our setting.

The main notation is introduced in section
2
, where we define the
joint exploration process
of trajectories in the DSF. Following the approach of
[
CSST21
]
, we use
history sets
to record the geometrical constraints imposed during the exploration and to provide a useful characterization of the process.

In Section
3
, we focus on controlling the size of the history sets, demonstrating that their size will return infinitely often below a specific threshold. To achieve this, we develop a novel approach, which includes proving a new stochastic domination argument. This is where the condition
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
of Theorem
1.1
will arise.

In Section
4
, we use the control over the size of the history set to show that certain
renewal events
occur at random times, as in
[
CSST21
]
. This allows us to properly formalize the heuristic that trajectories of the DSF behave like random walks. A key ingredient of this section will be to control the fluctuations of the trajectories between two consecutive
renewal times
. While inspired by the ideas in
[
CSST21
]
, the proofs we develop are substantially different and rely on new techniques tailored to our more general setting.

In Section
5
, we focus on establishing the coalescence part of Theorem
1.1
, i.e., that when either
d=2
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, or
d=3
and
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
, the DSF is almost surely a tree. This will be achieved by combining the renewal decomposition with the use of
Lyapunov functions
.

In Section
6
, we address the high-dimensional regime of Theorem
1.1
, showing that when
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
and
d\geq 4
, the DSF consists almost surely of infinitely many disjoint trees. The proof relies on the renewal decomposition along with a general result by Kesten
[
Kes78
]
on the rate of escape to infinity of random walks.

Finally, in Section
7
, we prove Theorem
1.2
by defining and establishing the convergence toward the Brownian web. This step follows directly from the results in
[
CSST21
]
, which we apply without modification.

2
The exploration process

In this section, we define the central process that will allow us to explore trajectories in the DSF iteratively. Recall that
d\geq 2
and
p\in[1,\infty]
are arbitrary fixed. From now on, we fix
k\geq 1
as the number of trajectories we will consider. We also fix deterministic
starting points

(\mathbf{u}_{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}
for our trajectories having the same
e_{d}
coordinate. The
exploration process

((g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i}))_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]})_{n\geq 0}

is constructed as follows. We set
g_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\coloneqq\mathbf{u}_{i}
for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
. Now let
n\geq 0
and assume that the process is constructed until step
n
. We define

m_{n}\coloneqq\min\{g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\cdot e_{d}\penalty\ :\penalty\ i\in[\![1,k]\!]\},

and we fix
x_{n}\in\{g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\penalty\ :\penalty\ i\in[\![1,k]\!]\}
such that
x_{n}\cdot e_{d}=m_{n}
, which will represent the moving vertex of the step. In case of multiple possibilities, we choose one deterministically. Then, for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
we set

g_{n+1}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\Psi(x_{n})&\text{if $g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})=x_{n}$}\\
g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In words, at step
n
, we explore one step forward along the trajectory whose current vertex
x_{n}
has the lowest
e_{d}
coordinate. As explained in the introduction, this exploration process is highly non-Markovian, as the next step depends in a complex way on the entire previously explored region. Each edge
(x,\Psi(x))
revealed during the exploration imposes that the corresponding half-ball
B^{+}(x,\|\Psi(x)-x\|)
must be empty of Poisson points, and such constraints can influence arbitrarily distant edges.

To record the geometrical constraints imposed by the observation of the first steps of the exploration process, we define the
history sets

(H_{n})_{n\geq 0}
as follows. For each
n\geq 0
, we set

H_{n}\coloneqq\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{n})\cap\bigcup_{i=0}^{n-1}B^{+}(x_{i},\|\Psi(x_{i})-x_{i}\|)

(2.1)

In words, for all
n\geq 0
,
H_{n}
corresponds to the intersection of the half-space
\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{n})
with the union of all the half-balls that must be empty of Poisson points due to the edges revealed during the
n
first steps of exploration. By construction, for all
n\geq 0
,

\mathcal{N}\cap H_{n}=\emptyset,

and

\mathcal{N}\cap\partial H_{n}=\{g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i}):i\in[\![1,k]\!]\}\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{n}),

where
\partial H_{n}
denotes the boundary of
H_{n}
. An illustration of the construction of the exploration process together with history sets is provided in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Illustration of the construction of the exploration process with history sets.

We now define a natural filtration associated with the exploration process. For all
n\geq 0
, let

\mathcal{F}_{n}\coloneqq\sigma\big(\mathcal{N}\cap\mathbb{H}^{-}(m_{n}),\penalty\ \mathcal{N}\cap\overline{H}_{n}\big),

where
\overline{H}_{n}
denotes the closure of
H_{n}
and
\mathbb{H}^{-}(h)\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathbb{H}^{+}(h)
for any
h\in\mathbb{R}
. By construction, the
exploration process with history

((g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i}))_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]},H_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. It is worth noting that, for technical reasons to be addressed later, this filtration contains more information than what is strictly used by the exploration process.

We can now state the key proposition of this section, which justifies the importance of the history sets. It formalizes the intuitive idea that after
n\geq 0
steps of exploration, no information about the Process point
\mathcal{N}
has been revealed outside the region
\mathbb{H}^{-}(m_{n})\cup\overline{H}_{n}
. implying that the process behaves as if
\mathcal{N}
were independently
resampled
outside the region
\mathbb{H}^{-}(m_{n})\cup H_{n}
(the closure can be omitted as
\partial H_{n}
has a.s. measure
0
). This crucial property was previously used implicitly in
[
BB07
]
, and explicitly in
[
CSST21
]
. We omit its proof, which is straightforward and technical.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
denote an independent copy of
\mathcal{N}
. Then for any
n\geq 0
, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the random variable

\mathcal{N}\setminus\big(\mathbb{H}^{-}(m_{n})\cup\overline{H}_{n}\big)

is distributed as
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\setminus(\mathbb{H}^{-}(m_{n})\cup H_{n})
.

As a consequence of Proposition
2.1
, the exploration process with history
((g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i}))_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]},H_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Markov chain. Its distribution can be characterized as follows. Fix
n\geq 0
, and let
y_{n+1}
denote the closest point to
x_{n}
in
\mathcal{N}\setminus\overline{H}_{n}
having strictly larger
e_{d}
-coordinate. Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
,
y_{n+1}
is distributed as the closest point to
x_{n}
in
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\setminus H_{n}
having strictly large
e_{d}
-coordinate. Then,
\Psi(x_{n})
is obtained as the closest point to
x_{n}
in
\{y_{n+1}\}\cup\{g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i}):i\in[\![1,k]\!]\}\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{n})
.

This convenient characterization of the conditional law of the process—with resampling outside the explored region—will play a central role in the analysis of the exploration with history.

3
Good steps

As the history sets encode the geometrical constraints imposed during the exploration process, it is crucial to control their size to understand how far the interactions can extend into the future. To this end, for all
n\geq 0
, we define

M_{n}\coloneqq\max(\{m_{n}\}\cup\{x\cdot e_{d}\penalty\ :\penalty\ x\in H_{n}\}),

and we set

L_{n}\coloneqq M_{n}-m_{n}.

By construction,
L_{n}
represents the height of the history set
H_{n}
with respect to the
e_{d}
-coordinate while
M_{n}
and
m_{n}
represent the
e_{d}
level of its top and bottom, respectively. It is worth noticing that both processes
(m_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are non-decreasing, as during exploration, both the top and bottom of the history sets move upward at each step. This section aims to prove the following theorem, which provides control over
(L_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. It gives in particular that there exists a threshold
\kappa>0
such that
L_{n}\leq\kappa
for infinitely many
n\geq 0
. In this work, we use
\alpha
and
\beta
to denote constants whose values may vary from one statement to another.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume
d=2
or
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
. For all
\kappa>0
and
R>0
, we define a sequence
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}=(\tau_{n}(\kappa,R))_{n\geq 0}
of
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
stopping times by induction with
\tau_{0}\coloneqq 0
and

\tau_{n+1}\coloneqq\inf\{j>\tau_{n}:L_{j}\leq\kappa,\penalty\ m_{j}-m_{\tau_{n}}\geq\kappa+R\}

for each
n\geq 0
. Then there exist
\kappa=\kappa(k,d,p)>0
,
\alpha=\alpha(k,d,p)>0
and
\beta=\beta(k,d,p)>0
such that for all
R>0
, the variables
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are almost surely finite, and all
n,\ell\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n}>\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}})\leq\alpha e^{R-\beta\ell}.

The steps associated to the indices
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are then called
good steps
.

Note that the condition
d=2
or
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
serves as a concise shorthand we will frequently use to refer to the two case
d=2
with
p\in[1,\infty]
, and
d\geq 3
with
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
.

The strategy of the proof of Theorem
3.1
is inspired by
[
CSST21
]
, but we develop an original approach that overcomes the key mathematical obstacles that previously prevented generalization both to the singular
\ell^{\infty}
norm and, more importantly, beyond the planar setting. Our framework handles both challenges in a unified way. A central contribution is a novel stochastic domination that allows us to compare any arbitrary step of the exploration process with the idealized case where the history set is empty, that is, where no constraints from the past are present. We begin by establishing an upper bound on the growth of
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in Subsection
3.1
. In Subsection
3.2
, we introduce the novel stochastic domination result, which will allow us to derive a lower bound on the growth of
(m_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in Subsection
3.3
. Finally, in Subsection
3.4
, we combine these ingredients to prove Theorem
3.1
.

3.1
Upper control for the increase of
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}

In this subsection, we focus on establishing an upper bound on the growth of the sequence
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. We begin by proving the following proposition, which forms the core of the argument. In the second part, we use this result to derive some exponential moment bounds.

Proposition 3.2
.

For arbitrary dimension
d\geq 2
and parameter
p\in[1,\infty]
, there exist a function
f=f_{d,p}:(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}\to[0,1]
such that

(a)

For each
n\geq 0
and
\ell\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\mathbb{P}\big(M_{n+1}-M_{n}\geq\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\big)\leq f(\ell,L_{n}).

(b)

There exist
c=c(d,p)>0
such that for all
(\ell,L)\in(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
,

f(\ell,L)\leq\exp(-c\ell^{d}).

(c)

For each
\ell\in(0,\infty)
,
L\mapsto f(\ell,L)
is non-increasing and
\lim_{L\to\infty}f(\ell,L)=0
.

In words, Proposition
3.2
states that for any
n\geq 0
, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the increment
M_{n+1}-M_{n}
has a distribution with a rapidly decaying tail. Moreover, each tail probability is arbitrarily small when
L_{n}
is sufficiently large. The key idea to prove Proposition
3.2
comes from the following lemma, which provides an upper bound on the conditional probability of interest in terms of the measure of some region.

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
n\geq 0
and
\ell\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\big(M_{n+1}-M_{n}\geq\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\big)\leq\exp{(-|B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)\setminus H_{n}|)},

where we recall that
x_{n}
is the vertex that will move at the next step, and
|\cdot|
denotes the Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

First, observe that by construction of the exploration process,

M_{n+1}=\min\{M_{n},\penalty\ m_{n}+\|\psi(x_{n})-x_{n}\|\}.

Therefore, if
M_{n+1}-M_{n}\geq\ell
, it must be that
\|\Psi(x_{n})-x_{n}\|\geq M_{n}+\ell-m_{n}=L_{n}+\ell
, i.e.

\mathcal{N}\cap B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)=\emptyset,

and in particular,
\mathcal{N}\cap B^{+}(x_{n},\penalty\ L_{n}+\ell)\setminus\overline{H}_{n}=\emptyset
. This shows that

\mathbb{P}\big(M_{n+1}-M_{n}\geq\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\big)\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}\cap B^{+}(x_{n},\penalty\ L_{n}+\ell)\setminus\overline{H}_{n}=\emptyset\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}).

Finally, since

\mathcal{N}\cap B^{+}(x_{n},\penalty\ L_{n}+\ell)\setminus\overline{H}_{n}\subset\mathcal{N}\setminus\big(\mathbb{H}^{-}(m_{n})\cup\overline{H}_{n}\big),

applying Proposition
2.1
, we have that, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the number of points of
\mathcal{N}
in
B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)\setminus\overline{H}_{n}
is distributed as a Poisson random variable of parameter
|B^{+}(x_{n},\penalty\ L_{n}+\ell)\setminus H_{n}|
, implying the result.
∎

Now it remains to derive a suitable lower bound on the measure of the set
B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)\setminus H_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
and
\ell\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
to prove Proposition
3.2
. This reduces the problem to a deterministic geometric one, where we must leverage the inherent properties of the exploration process. We proceed by treating the cases
p=\infty
and
p<\infty
separately. We begin with the case
p=\infty
, which is addressed by the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
p=\infty
, then for all
n\geq 0
and
\ell\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

|B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)\setminus H_{n}|\geq\ell(L_{n}+\ell)^{d-1}.

Proof.

We start by setting

R_{n}(\ell)\coloneqq B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(M_{n}).

By construction, since
H_{n}\subset\mathbb{H}^{-}(M_{n})
, we have

R_{n}(\ell)\subset B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)\setminus H_{n}.

We refer to Figure
4
for a graphic illustration. Finally, observing that we can write

R_{n}(\ell)=x_{n}+(-L_{n}+\ell,L_{n}+\ell)^{d-1}\times(L_{n},L_{n}+\ell),

we deduce
|R_{n}(\ell)|=\ell(L_{n}+\ell)^{d-1}
, implying the result.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

It could be possible to adapt the argument used in the proof of Lemma
3.4
to derive a suitable similar result for all
p>1
, using that the top of the
\ell^{p}
balls are locally flat. However, this argument breaks down for
p=1
due to the singularity of the
\ell^{1}
ball.

We now turn to the case
p<\infty
. The following lemma is the key to our argument. Informally, it says that any ball contained in
\mathbb{H}^{-}(1)
, whose center lies in
\mathbb{H}^{-}(0)
and which avoids the origin, must be disjoint from a ball centered at
e_{d}
with universal radius. This geometric property, which relies on the rounded shape of the
\ell^{p}
balls when
p<\infty
, will allow us, after a suitable translation and scaling, to construct a nontrivial region that systematically avoids the history set. This argument breaks down for
p=\infty
, due to the flatness of the
\ell^{\infty}
ball.

Lemma 3.6
.

Assume
p<\infty
. Let
c\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(0)
and
r>0
such that
0\notin B(c,r)
and
B(c,r)\subset\mathbb{H}^{-}(1)
, then

B(e_{d},\alpha_{p})\cap B(c,r)=\emptyset,\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha_{p}\coloneqq 2^{\frac{1}{p}}-1\in(0,1).

Proof.

First, note that since
c\in\mathbb{H}^{-}(0)
, we can write
c=c_{0}-\beta e_{d}
with
c_{0}\cdot e_{d}=0
and
\beta\geq 0
. Since
B(c,r)\subset\mathbb{H}^{-}(1)
and
c_{0}+e_{d}\in\mathbb{\partial}\mathbb{H}^{-}(1)
, we must have

r\leq\|e_{d}+c_{0}-c\|=\|e_{d}+\beta e_{d}\|=\beta+1

(3.1)

Moreover, since
0\notin B(c,r)
, we must have
r\leq\|c\|
and thus

\begin{split}\|e_{d}-c\|^{p}-r^{p}&\geq\|e_{d}-c\|^{p}-\|c\|^{p}\\
&=\|c_{0}\|^{p}+(\beta+1)^{p}-\|c_{0}\|^{p}-\beta^{p}\\
&=(\beta+1)^{p}-\beta^{p}.\end{split}

(3.2)

Now, we can write

\begin{split}\|e_{d}-c\|-r&=(r^{p}+\|e_{d}-c\|^{p}-r^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}-(r^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}\\
&\geq\big((\beta+1)^{p}+\|e_{d}-c\|^{p}-r^{p}\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}-(\beta+1)\\
&\geq\big(2(\beta+1)^{p}-\beta^{p}\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}-(\beta^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}-1\\
&\geq\big(2(\beta+1)^{p}\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}-(2\beta^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}-1\\
&=2^{\frac{1}{p}}-1\\
&=\alpha_{p},\end{split}

(3.3)

where the third line is obtained from (
3.2
) and the first and last inequality comes from concavity of
z\mapsto z^{1/p}
with (
3.1
)
\beta^{p}\geq 0
as for all
0\leq a\leq b
and
c\geq 0
we have
b^{1/p}-a^{1/p}\geq(b+c)^{1/p}-(a+c)^{1/p}
. Finally let
x\in B(e_{d},\alpha_{p})
. From triangle inequality and (
3.3
), we can write

\|x-c\|\geq\|e_{d}-c\|-\|x-e_{d}\|\geq r+\alpha_{p}-\alpha_{p}=r,

implying
x\notin B(c,r)
. The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.7

The radius

We can now derive a suitable lower bound on the measure of

for all

and

Lemma 3.8
.

For all
p<\infty
, there exists

and

Proof.

First, observing that since
H_{n}
consists of the intersection of
\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{n})
with an union of balls avoiding
x_{n}
, included in
\mathbb{H}^{-}(M_{n})
with centers in
\mathbb{H}^{-}(m_{n})
, performing a translation by
-x_{n}
and a scaling by
{L_{n}}^{-1}
, we can apply Lemma
3.6
to get that

H_{n}\cap B\left(x_{n}+L_{n}e_{d},\alpha_{p}L_{n}\right)=\emptyset.

(3.4)

Then, setting
c_{n}\coloneqq x_{n}+(1-\frac{\alpha_{p}}{2})L_{n}e_{d}
, we have that

B\left(c_{n},\frac{\alpha_{p}}{2}L_{n}\right)\subset B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)\setminus H_{n}.

(3.5)

Indeed, if
z\in B(c_{n},\frac{\alpha_{p}}{2}L_{n})

\|z-x_{n}\|\leq\|z-c_{n}\|+\|c_{n}-x_{n}\|<\frac{\alpha_{p}}{2}L_{n}+(1-\frac{\alpha_{p}}{2})L_{n}=L_{n}
implying

\|z-(x_{n}+L_{n}e_{d})\|\leq\|z-c_{n}\|+\|c_{n}-(x_{n}+L_{n}e_{d})\|\leq\frac{\alpha_{p}}{2}L_{n}+\frac{\alpha_{p}}{2}L_{n}=\alpha_{p}L_{n}

from (

(3.6)

Additionally, we have that

B^{+}(x_{n}+L_{n}e_{d},\ell)\subset B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)\setminus H_{n}.

Indeed
B^{+}(x_{n}+L_{n}e_{d},\ell)\subset\mathbb{H}^{+}(M_{n})
whereas

, and
B^{+}(x_{n}+L_{n}e_{d},\ell)\subset B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)
using triangle inequality. Taking the Lebesgue measure, we get that

|B^{+}(x_{n},L_{n}+\ell)\setminus H_{n}|\geq\ell^{d}|B^{+}(0,1)|.

Together with (

), it gives the result.
∎

Putting everything together, we prove Proposition

.

To conclude this subsection, we derive two lemmas from Proposition

that establish exponential moment bounds, which will be useful in later arguments.

Lemma 3.9
.

For every
n\geq 0
and

we denote

Y(n,i)\coloneqq\big(M_{i+1}-M_{i}-(L_{i}-L_{n})_{-}\big)_{+}.

Fix

. There exist a non-increasing function
G_{t}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\lim_{L\to\infty}G_{t}(L)=1
such that for all

and

, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tY(n,i)}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{i}\right]\leq G_{t}(L_{n}).

Proof.

We set

G_{t}:L\mapsto 1+\int_{0}^{\infty}te^{t\ell}f(\ell,L)\mathrm{d}\ell,

where

is given by Proposition
3.2
. Using point (b) of Proposition
3.2
, we get
G_{t}(0)<\infty
. The integrand in the definition of
G_{t}(L)
decreases to
0
as
L\rightarrow\infty
for all
\ell>0
from point (c), therefore by dominated convergence,
G_{t}(L)
decreases to
1
as
L\rightarrow\infty
. Now, observe that applying Fubini, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tY(n,i)}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{i}\right]&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}te^{t\ell}\mathbb{P}[Y(n,i)\geq\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{i}]\mathrm{d}\ell\\
&=1+\int_{0}^{\infty}te^{t\ell}\mathbb{P}[Y(n,i)\geq\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{i}]\mathrm{d}\ell,\end{split}

(3.7)

where the last equality comes from the fact that
Y(n,i)\geq 0
almost surely and
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}te^{t\ell}\mathrm{d}\ell=1
. Now, using Proposition
3.2
again, for all

we write

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}[Y(n,i)\geq\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{i}]&=\mathbb{P}(M_{i+1}-M_{i}\geq\ell+[L_{i}-L_{n}]_{-}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{i})\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{i+1}-M_{i}\geq\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{i})\\
&\leq f(\ell,L_{n}).\end{split}

Injecting in (

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tY(n,i)}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{j}\right]\leq 1+\int_{0}^{\infty}te^{t\ell}f(\ell,L_{n})\mathrm{d}\ell=G_{t}(L_{n}).

Lemma 3.10
.

For all
n\geq 0
and
j>n
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t(L_{j}-L_{n})_{+}}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t(L_{j-1}-L_{n}+M_{j}-M_{j-1})_{+}}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\leq\big(G_{t}(L_{n})\big)^{j-n}.

Proof.

The first inequality comes from the inequality of the corresponding variables since we have
L_{j}-L_{j-1}\leq M_{j}-M_{j-1}
. To prove the second inequality, we proceed by induction on
j
. If
j=n+1
, we have

L_{j-1}-L_{n}+M_{j}-M_{j-1}=M_{n+1}-M_{n}=Y(n,n),

hence the initialization follows from Lemma
3.9
. Now, suppose the result holds for some
j>n
. We can write

\begin{split}(L_{j}-L_{n}+M_{j+1}-M_{j})_{+}&=(L_{j}-L_{n})_{+}+(M_{j+1}-M_{j}-[L_{j}-L_{n}]_{-})_{+}\\
&=(L_{j}-L_{n})_{+}+Y(n,j),\end{split}

where the first equality comes from the fact that
(a+b)_{+}=b_{+}+(a-b_{-})_{+}
for all
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
. We deduce then that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t(L_{j}-L_{n}+M_{j+1}-M_{j})_{+}}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right]&=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t(L_{j}-L_{n})_{+}}e^{tY(n,j)}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t(L_{j}-L_{n})_{+}}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right]G_{t}(L_{n})\\
&\leq\big(G_{t}(L_{n})\big)^{j-n+1},\end{split}

where the second inequality comes from Proposition
3.9
since
e^{t(L_{j}-L_{n})_{+}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{j}
measurable, and the last inequality comes from the induction hypothesis. The heredity is thus verified, and the proof is complete.
∎

3.2
A useful stochastic domination

In this subsection, we establish a novel and useful stochastic domination result, which will later be used to obtain a lower bound on the growth of the sequence
(m_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. The idea is as follows. For all
n\geq 0
, recall that
y_{n+1}
denotes the closest point to the moving vertex
x_{n}
in
\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{n})\cap\mathcal{N}\setminus\overline{H}_{n}
. The key difference between
\Psi(x_{n})
and
y_{n+1}
is that
y_{n+1}
ignores the other trajectories, whose current positions may lie on
\partial H_{n}
. To obtain a lower bound the increments of
(m_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, we aim to bound from below, for each
n\geq 0
, the quantity

(y_{n+1}-x_{n})\cdot e_{d}.

To study this quantity, for any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite measure, we introduce the random variable
X^{A}
, which is the closest point to the origin in
\mathbb{H}^{+}(0)\cap\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\setminus A
, for the
\ell^{p}
distance and where
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
denotes an independent copy of
\mathcal{N}
. By construction, using a spatial translation along with Proposition
2.1
, we have that for all
n\geq 0
, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the displacement
y_{n+1}-x_{n}
is distributed as
X^{H_{n}-x_{n}}
. In particular, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
,
(y_{n+1}-x_{n})\cdot e_{d}
is distributed as

X^{H_{n}-x_{n}}\cdot e_{d}.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{H}_{0}
the collection of all subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that can be written as the intersection of
\mathbb{H}^{+}(0)
with a finite union of balls that avoid the origin and whose centers lie in
\mathbb{H}^{-}(0)
. In words,
\mathcal{H}_{0}
represents the collection of all
re-centered valid history sets
. By construction, we have
H_{n}-x_{n}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
almost surely. It is therefore sufficient to study the random variable
X^{H}\cdot e_{d}
for any fixed
H\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
. In the remainder of this subsection, we fix such a deterministic set
H\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
. The main theorem of this section asserts that, for all
n\geq 0
, conditionally on the first
n
steps of the exploration process,
(y_{n+1}-x_{n})\cdot e_{d}
is stochastically larger than it would be if the history set
H_{n}
were empty.

Theorem 3.11
.

If
d=2
or
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
, we have the following stochastic domination:

X^{H}\cdot e_{d}\succeq_{\mathrm{sto}}X^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d},

that is,
\mathbb{P}(X^{H}\cdot e_{d}\geq h)\geq\mathbb{P}(X^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}\geq h)
for for all
h\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In particular, for all
n\geq 0
, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, we have
(y_{n+1}-x_{n})\cdot e_{d}\succeq_{\mathrm{sto}}X^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}
, meaning that

\mathbb{P}\big((y_{n+1}-x_{n})\cdot e_{d}\geq h\penalty\ \big|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\big)\geq\mathbb{P}(X^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}\geq h)

for each
h\geq 0
.

To our knowledge, such stochastic domination was never stated before. It is worth noting that we will not provide an explicit coupling that realizes the stochastic domination. Indeed, the most natural coupling one might consider, namely, using the same Poisson point process, fails to achieve it. We therefore adopt a computational strategy, comparing the distributions directly. We begin by establishing an intermediate stochastic domination in Section
3.2.1
, which forms the core of the proof of Theorem
3.11
, presented in Section
3.2.2
.

The fact that Theorem
3.11
holds for any
p\in[1,\infty]
when
d=2
naturally raises the question of whether the result remains valid beyond these specific assumptions. As we will see in the sequel, however, one of the intermediate results crucial to our proof fails to hold in general once these conditions are removed. This failure is far from obvious and suggests that the general case is genuinely delicate. Whether the theorem itself extends remains an open and subtle question. There is, at this stage, no clear reason why it should or should not.

3.2.1
An intermediate stochastic domination

In this subsection, we establish an intermediate stochastic domination that will be crucial in the proof of Theorem
3.11
. For any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
|B^{+}(0,1)\setminus A|>0
, we denote by
U^{A}
a uniform point of
B^{+}(0,1)\setminus A
. The aim is to show the following proposition. Recall that
H\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
is a fixed re-centered valid history set.

Proposition 3.12
.

We have the stochastic domination
U^{H}\cdot e_{d}\succeq_{\mathrm{sto}}U^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}
, that is for all
h\in[0,1)

\mathbb{P}(U^{H}\cdot e_{d}\geq h)\geq\mathbb{P}(U^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}\geq h).

(3.8)

Let us start with some notations. For all
h\in[0,1)
, we define the section at level
h
by

S_{h}\coloneqq\{x\in B(0,1):x\cdot e_{d}=h\}.

Let
\mathbf{p}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d-1},\penalty\ (x_{k})_{1\leq k\leq d}\mapsto(x_{k})_{1\leq k\leq d-1}
be the application that forgets the
e_{d}
coordinate. For all
h\in[0,1)
, we define the proportion of
S_{h}
that is not covered by
H
by

\alpha_{h}\coloneqq\frac{|\mathbf{p}(S_{h}\setminus H)|}{|\mathbf{p}(S_{h})|}.

Note that here
|\cdot|
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. The main idea is the following. The random variable
U^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}
has density

g_{\emptyset}:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\penalty\ h\mapsto\frac{1}{|B^{+}(0,1)|}|\mathbf{p}(S_{h})|,

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1)
, whereas
U^{H}\cdot e_{d}
has density

g_{H}:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\penalty\ h\mapsto\frac{1}{|B^{+}(0,1)\setminus H|}\alpha_{h}|\mathbf{p}(S_{h})|.

Therefore, denoting the normalizing constant
Z\coloneqq|B^{+}(0,1)\setminus H|/|B^{+}(0,1)|
, for all
h\in[0,1)
, the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the law of
U^{H}\cdot e_{d}
with respect to the law of
U^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}
satisfies

\forall h\in[0,1),\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{U^{H}\cdot e_{d}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{U^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}}}(h)=\frac{\alpha_{h}}{Z}.

(3.9)

Intuitively, we can say that if
h\mapsto\alpha_{h}
is non-decreasing, then the random variable
U^{H}\cdot e_{d}
tends to place more mass on larger values than
U_{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}
, suggesting the stochastic domination
U^{H}\cdot e_{d}\succeq_{\mathrm{sto}}U^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}
. The following lemma confirms that this intuition is indeed correct.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
with
\nu
absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
and such that the Radon-Nikodym derivative

D:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\penalty\ h\mapsto\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}(h)

is non-decreasing. Then
\nu\succeq_{\mathrm{sto}}\mu
in the sense that for all
h\in\mathbb{R}
,

\nu([h,\infty])\geq\mu([h,\infty]).

Proof.

Let
h\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
D
is non-decreasing, we have

\nu([h,\infty])=\int_{[h,\infty]}D(t)\mathrm{d}\mu(t)\geq D(h)\int_{[h,\infty]}\mathrm{d}\mu(t)=D(h)\mu([h,\infty]).

(3.10)

Similarly, we have

\nu(\mathbb{R}\setminus[h,\infty])\leq D(h)\mu(\mathbb{R}\setminus[h,\infty]).

(3.11)

If
D(h)\geq 1
, the sought inequality follows from (
3.10
), otherwise (
3.11
) gives it. The proof is complete.
∎

We now dedicate the remainder of this section to proving that the proportion function
h\mapsto\alpha_{h}
is indeed non-decreasing. To do this, we compare different sections by mapping them onto a common reference, namely
S_{0}
, through a combination of translation and scaling. For that purpose, for every
h\in[0,1)
, we define the radius of the section
S_{h}
by

\rho(h)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\quad p=\infty\\
(1-h^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We then define the map

\Phi:B(0,1)\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(0)\mapsto S_{0},\penalty\ x\mapsto\frac{x-(x\cdot e_{d})e_{d}}{\rho(x\cdot e_{d})}.

For each
h\in[0,1)
, the restriction of
\Phi
to
S_{h}
is a bijection onto
S_{0}
, and its inverse is given by

{\Phi_{|S_{h}}}^{-1}:S_{0}\ni x_{0}\mapsto\rho(h)x_{0}+he_{d}.

An illustration of the function
\Phi
is presented in Figure
5
. The following lemma uses the map
\Phi
to compare the sections via set inclusion. The proof is technical and relies on convexity, triangle inequalities, and orthogonal decomposition. The condition that
d=2
or
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
emerges naturally in the proof. Roughly speaking, this arises from the following specific singularities: when
p=\infty
, all sections have the same radius; when
p=2
, the Pythagorean Theorem applies; when
p=1
, the function

3.3
Lower control for the growth of
(m_{n})_{n\geq 0}

From now on, we will work with the assumption

or

.

In this subsection, we use the stochastic domination of Theorem
3.11
to provide an exponential moment lower bound on the growth of the bottom of the history sets,
(m_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. Recall that as a consequence of Proposition
2.1
, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the displacement
y_{n+1}-x_{n}
is distributed as
X^{H_{n}-x_{n}}
. Furthermore,
\Psi(x_{n})
is the closest point to
x_{n}
in

\{y_{n+1}\}\cup\{g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i}):i\in[\![1,k]\!]\}\setminus\{x_{n}\}.

Hence, when

, due to the presence of

\Psi(x_{n})
is not necessarily equal to
y_{n+1}
. This complicates the analysis conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
: for instance, if the other trajectories are very close to
x_{n}
, coalescence will very likely occur and the increment
m_{n+1}-m_{n}
can be very small. To overcome this issue, we consider the progress over
k
consecutive steps of the exploration process, rather than just one. The following lemma ensures that, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the increment
m_{n+k}-m_{n}
stochastically dominates a fixed law.

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
(X^{\emptyset}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}
denote i.i.d. copies of
X^{\emptyset}
. We set

\Gamma_{k}\coloneqq\min_{1\leq i\leq k}(X_{i}^{\emptyset}\cdot e_{d}).

Then, for all
n\geq 0
, the law of
m_{n+k}-m_{n}
conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
stochastically dominates the law of
\Gamma_{k}
, that is, for all

\mathbb{P}\left(m_{n+k}-m_{n}\geq h\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}(\Gamma_{k}\geq h).

Proof.

Let
n\geq 0
. To begin, let us show that we have almost surely

m_{n+k}\geq\min_{n\leq i<n+k}(y_{i+1}\cdot e_{d}).

(3.17)

The intuition is that making
k
steps, coalescence can occur on at most
k-1
of them, hence at least one step will connect to some
y_{j+1}
with
n\leq j<n+k
. However, for a fixed
j\geq 0
, we don’t necessarily have
m_{j+1}=\Psi(x_{j})\cdot e_{d}
, which makes the analysis more delicate. To begin, observe that for all
j\geq 0
, we have

\Psi(x_{j})\in\{y_{j+1}\}\cup\{g_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}\setminus\{x_{j}\}.

Hence, since only
x_{j}
moves during the step, we deduce that

\{g_{j+1}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}\subset\{y_{j+1}\}\cup\{g_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}\setminus\{x_{j}\},

with
x_{j}\in\{g_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
. By induction, it follows that

\{g_{n+k}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}\subset\{y_{\ell+1}\}_{n\leq\ell<n+k}\cup\{g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}\setminus\{x_{\ell}\}_{n\leq\ell<n+k},

(3.18)

with
\{x_{\ell}\}_{n\leq l<n+k}\subset\{y_{\ell+1}\}_{n\leq l<n+k}\cup\{g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
. We proceed by treating two cases. If we have

\{x_{\ell}\}_{n\leq\ell<n+k}\cap\{y_{\ell+1}\}_{n\leq\ell<n+k}\neq\emptyset,

then there exist
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in[\![n,n+k-1]\!]
such that

and thus (

) is verified as

In the other case, we have
\{x_{\ell}\}_{n\leq\ell<n+k}\subset\{g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
. Hence, since the
(x_{\ell})_{n\leq\ell<n+k}
are disjoints, by checking cardinals we get
\{x_{\ell}\}_{n\leq\ell<n+k}=\{g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
. From (
3.18
) we get then

\{g_{n+k}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq k}\subset\{y_{\ell+1}\}_{n\leq\ell<n+k},

and (
3.17
) follows. We have proved (
3.17
). Now, observe that for each

so that together with (

Therefore, for any

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(m_{n+k}-m_{n}\geq h\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)&\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{n\leq i<n+k}\big((y_{i+1}-x_{i})\cdot e_{d}\big)\geq h\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right)\\

where for the second inequality, we used that for all

stochastically dominates the law of

Lemma 3.20
.

\mathbb{P}\left(\nu_{n+1}-\nu_{n}>\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}}\right)\leq\alpha e^{-\beta\ell}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction.
\nu_{0}=0
is almost surely finite. Now let
n\geq 0
. Assume that
\nu_{n}
is almost surely finite. Denote
N_{0}\coloneqq\nu_{n}
and for all

,

N_{i+1}\coloneqq N_{i}+K_{N_{i}}.

Since

for all

, then for all

we have
N_{\ell}-\nu_{n}\leq k\ell
, hence

\mathbb{P}\left(\nu_{n+1}-\nu_{n}>k\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(N_{\ell}<\nu_{n+1}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}}\right).

(3.19)

Now observe that for each
\ell\geq 1

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(N_{\ell}<\nu_{n+1}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}})&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{L_{N_{\ell}}>\kappa}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\mathds{1}_{L_{N_{i}}>\kappa}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}}\right]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{L_{N_{\ell}}-\kappa}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\mathds{1}_{L_{N_{i}}>\kappa}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{L_{N_{1}}-\kappa}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\mathds{1}_{L_{N_{i}}>\kappa}e^{L_{N_{i}+K_{N_{i}}}-L_{N_{i}}}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}}\right]\end{split}

(3.20)

where the second inequality comes from
\mathds{1}_{a>b}\leq e^{a-b}
for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and the last equality comes from a telescopic product. Now, in the last expectation of the display, we have a product of
\ell
terms, where the first
i
terms are
\mathcal{F}_{N_{i}}
-measurable. Therefore, using Proposition
3.21
, we get

\mathbb{P}(N_{\ell}<\nu_{n+1}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}})\leq\mathbb{E}[e^{L_{N_{1}}-\kappa}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}}]q^{\ell-1}\leq\mathbb{E}[e^{L_{\nu_{n}+K_{\nu_{n}}}-L_{\nu_{n}}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}}]q^{\ell-1}\leq G_{1}(0)^{k}q^{\ell-1},

where the second inequality comes from
L_{\nu_{n}}\leq\kappa
and the last one from Lemma
3.10
since
K_{\nu_{n}}\leq k
is
\mathcal{F}_{\nu_{n}}
-measurable. Injecting in (

) we get the result.
∎

To transfer the properties we just established for the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\geq 0}
to the sequence
(\tau_{n}(\kappa,R))_{n\geq 0}
, we define, for each
n\geq 0
, the random variable

T_{n}\coloneqq\inf\{j>0:m_{n+j}-m_{n}\geq\kappa+R\}.

In words,
T_{n}
is the time we have to wait from index
n
to reach a progress of at least
\kappa+R
. The following lemma ensures that the
(T_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are distributed with exponentially decaying tails.

Lemma 3.23
.

There exist
\alpha=\alpha(k,d,p)>0
and
\beta=\beta(k,d,p)>0
such that for all
n,\ell\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(T_{n}>\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n})\leq\alpha e^{R-\beta\ell}.

Proof.

Let
n,\ell\geq 0
. Using Markov’s inequality, we write

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(T_{n}>k\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n})=\mathbb{E}[m_{n+k\ell}-m_{n}<\kappa+R\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}]&\leq\mathbb{E}[e^{\kappa+R-(m_{n+k\ell}-m_{n})}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}]\\
&=e^{\kappa+R}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}e^{-(m_{n+ik+k}-m_{n+ik})}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\\
&\leq e^{\kappa+R}\mathbb{E}[e^{-\Gamma_{k}}]^{\ell},\end{split}

where the last inequality comes from Lemma
3.19
. Since
\mathbb{E}[e^{-\Gamma_{k}}]<1
as
\Gamma_{k}>0
almost surely, we deduce the sought exponential decay. The proof is complete.
∎

We now prove the main result of this section by bringing together all the preceding ingredients.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We proceed by induction to show that
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}\coloneqq(\tau_{n}^{\kappa,R})_{n\geq 0}
indeed satisfy the sought properties. We have
\tau_{0}=0
that is almost surely finite. Now let
n\geq 0
and assume that
\tau_{n}
is almost surely finite. First, observe that by definition,
\tau_{n}=\nu_{A}
for some
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}
-measurable

and
\tau_{n+1}=\nu_{B}
where

B=\inf\{j\geq A\penalty\ :\penalty\ \nu_{j}\geq\nu_{A}+T_{\tau_{n}}\}.

Since

is increasing, we have
\nu_{j}-\nu_{A}\geq j-A
hence
\nu_{A+T_{\tau_{n}}}-\nu_{A}\geq T_{\tau_{n}}
and thus we must have

1\leq B-A\leq T_{\tau_{n}}

(3.21)

By definition of

\nu_{B-1}<\tau_{n}+T_{\tau_{n}}
, hence

\nu_{B}-\nu_{B-1}\geq\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n}-T_{\tau_{n}}.

(3.22)

Now let
\ell\geq 0
. Observe that from (

), and using an union bound, we can write

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n}>\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}\right)&\leq\mathbb{P}(\nu_{B}-\nu_{B-1}>\ell-T_{\tau_{n}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}})\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}\left(T_{\tau_{n}}>\frac{\ell}{2}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(T_{\tau_{n}}\leq\frac{\ell}{2},\penalty\ \nu_{B}-\nu_{B-1}>\frac{\ell}{2}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}\right)\end{split}

(3.23)

Next, using (

\mathbb{P}\left(T_{\tau_{n}}\leq\frac{\ell}{2},\penalty\ \nu_{B}-\nu_{B-1}\geq\frac{\ell}{2}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{b=0}^{\frac{\ell}{2}-1}\left\{\nu_{A+b+1}-\nu_{A+b}>\frac{\ell}{2}\right\}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}\right)\leq\frac{\ell}{2}\alpha e^{-\beta\frac{\ell}{2}},

where the last inequality comes from a union bound and Lemma

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n}>\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(T_{\tau_{n}}>\frac{\ell}{2}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}\right)+\frac{\ell}{2}\alpha e^{-\beta\frac{\ell}{2}}\leq\alpha^{\prime}e^{R-\beta^{\prime}\frac{\ell}{2}}+\frac{\ell}{2}\alpha e^{-\beta\frac{\ell}{2}},

where

∎

4
Renewal decomposition

In this section, we adapt the technique of
[
CSST21
]
to decompose the exploration process into blocks of random size, thereby making rigorous the intuition that the trajectories behave like random walks in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, that do not interact when they are sufficiently far from each other. To this end, in Subsection
4.1
, we first introduce certain
joint renewal events
for the exploration process, along with associated random times, and show that these times are almost surely finite and that their spacing has an exponentially decaying tail. In Subsection
4.2
, we then control the fluctuations of the trajectories between consecutive
renewal times
. Finally, in Subsection
4.3
, we use the renewal decomposition to describe the behavior of the trajectories via an auxiliary process, namely, the
independent process
.

4.1
Joint renewal events

Before describing intuitively the idea, let us start with some notations. For all
n\geq 0
and
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
, we define

g_{n}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\coloneqq g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})+(m_{n}+\kappa-g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\cdot e_{d})e_{d}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{n}^{\downarrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\coloneqq g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})+(m_{n}-g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\cdot e_{d})e_{d}.

In words,
g_{n}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i})
corresponds to the projection of
g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})
on the
e_{d}
-level
m_{n}+\kappa
while
g_{n}^{\downarrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i})
corresponds to the projection of
g_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i})
on the level
m_{n}
. Let
\kappa>0
be given by Theorem
3.1
and
R>0
be arbitrarily fixed. For any
j\geq 0
, we say that the good step
j
, occurring at time
\tau_{j}
, is a
joint renewal step
, if the event

A_{j}\coloneqq\{\forall i\in[\![1,k]\!],\penalty\ \#\mathcal{N}\cap B^{+}(g_{\tau_{j}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),R)=\#\mathcal{N}\cap B(g_{\tau_{j}}^{\downarrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),\kappa+R)\setminus\overline{H}_{\tau_{j}}=1\}

occurs. In words, we say that we have joint renewal of the trajectories at time
\tau_{j}
if for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
we have only one Poisson point in
B(g_{\tau_{j}}^{\downarrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),\kappa+R)\setminus\overline{H}_{\tau_{j}}
which is actually in
B^{+}(g_{\tau_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),R)
. An illustration of the joint renewal event is provided in Figure
6
.

Figure 6:
Illustration of the occurrence of the renewal event
A_{j}
, for
2
different setups with
k=3
. The light gray and black regions contain no point of
\mathcal{N}
while each translucent colorful region (red, blue, and green) contains exactly one point of
\mathcal{N}
.

We define
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 0}
by induction with
\gamma_{0}\coloneqq\inf\{i\geq 0:A_{i}\penalty\ \text{occurs}\}
and for all
n\geq 0
,

\gamma_{n+1}\coloneqq\inf\{i>\gamma_{n}:A_{i}\penalty\ \text{occurs}\}.

In words, for each
n\geq 0
, the renewal step
n
occurs at the good step index
\gamma_{n}
, that is at step

\beta_{n}\coloneqq\tau_{\gamma_{n}}.

We now give some intuition behind the construction of these renewal steps in the case
k=1
, where only a single trajectory is being explored. Fix
n\geq 0
and suppose that
\beta_{n}<\infty
. By construction of the renewal event, we have

\Psi(x_{\beta_{n}})=\Psi\big(g_{\beta_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})\big)=\Psi\big(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{1})\big).

In other words, at time
\beta_{n}
, we can replace the current point
x_{\beta_{n}}
by
g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{1})
without altering the future evolution of the process. This replacement point lies at level
m_{\beta_{n}}+\kappa
, which is above the current history set. As a result, we can effectively forget the past in some sense. More precisely, conditional on the information revealed up to time
\beta_{n}
, the point process

\big(\mathcal{N}-g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{1})\big)\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(0)

is distributed as
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(0)
, where
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
denotes an independent copy of
\mathcal{N}
, conditioned on the event

\#\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\cap B^{+}(0,R)=\#\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(0)\cap B(-\kappa e_{d},\kappa+R)=1.

Intuitively, this means that the future of the process depends only on the current position and thus behaves like a Markov process. Even more, recalling that
\mathbf{p}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
denotes the projection parallel to
e_{d}
, if the renewal times
(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are almost surely finite, the sequence

\big(\mathbf{p}(g_{\beta_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{1}))\big)_{n\geq 0}

forms a random walk with i.i.d. symmetric increments in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. This formalizes the heuristic that individual trajectories behave as random walks in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. For the cases
k\geq 2
, where we simultaneously explore multiple trajectories, the situation becomes more delicate. Specifically, it is no longer true that for
n\geq 0
and
\beta_{n}<\infty
, we have
\Psi(g_{\beta_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{i}))=\Psi(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}))
for every
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
. In fact, as illustrated in Figure
6
, coalescence can occur after time
\beta_{n}
below the level
m_{\beta_{n}}+\kappa
, when the points
(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}))_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
are not separated by a distance of at least
\kappa+R
. Due to this possibility of early coalescence, we do not have a Markovian structure a priori. However, we will see that when the trajectories are sufficiently far apart from one another, they evolve approximately as independent random walks. Note that good steps play a crucial role in the construction of the renewal times. The remainder of the section is devoted to making the above ideas into a rigorous argument.

As a first step, in the remainder of this subsection, we aim to show that the random times
(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are almost surely finite, and that their spacing is distributed with an exponentially decaying tail. It is worth noting that
(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are not
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
stopping times and that we will need to define enhanced sigma fields. For all
i\geq 0
we define

\mathcal{S}_{i}\coloneqq\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}},\penalty\ A_{i}).

The next lemma ensures that the sequence
(\mathcal{S}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is indeed a filtration.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
i\geq 0
, we have
\mathcal{S}_{i}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i+1}}
. In particular,
(\mathcal{S}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is a filtration.

Proof.

The event
A_{i}
is
\sigma(\mathcal{N}\cap\mathbb{H}^{-}(m_{\tau_{i}}+\kappa+R))
measurable. Since by construction
m_{\tau_{i+1}}-m_{\tau_{i}}\geq\kappa+R
, then
A_{i}
is
\sigma(\mathcal{N}\cap\mathbb{H}^{-}(m_{\tau_{i+1}}))\subset\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i+1}}
measurable. To show that we have a filtration, we just write that
\mathcal{S}_{i}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i+1}}\subset\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i+1}},A_{i+1})=\mathcal{S}_{i+1}
.
∎

We start by showing that, at each good step
j\geq 0
, the renewal event
A_{j}
occurs with probability uniformly bounded away from zero. To establish this, we rely on the following proposition. More than merely nontrivial, its proof requires a substantial and delicate geometric analysis. This argument is entirely deterministic and independent of the probabilistic aspects of the paper, which is why we defer it to the appendix (section
B
). A brief treatment of the special case

was given in
[
CSST21
]
, but in the general setting, the proof becomes significantly more intricate, which is why we provided a detailed presentation.

Proposition 4.2
.

There exists two constants
a_{0}=a_{0}(d,p,k)>0
and
R_{0}=R_{0}(d,p,k)>0
such that for all configuration
(c_{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}\in\mathbb{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
with
c_{i}\cdot e_{d}=0
for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
, there exist a partition
\Pi
of
[\![1,k]\!]
such that for all
\pi\in\Pi
,

|P_{\pi}^{R_{0}}|\geq a_{0}\quad\text{where}\quad P_{\pi}^{R_{0}}\coloneqq\bigcap_{i\in\pi}B^{+}(c_{i},R_{0})\setminus\bigcup_{j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi}B(c_{j}-\kappa e_{d},\kappa+R_{0}).

We now set
R\coloneqq R_{0}
, as specified in the preceding proposition. The value of
R
was left arbitrary until now precisely to allow this choice.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exist
p_{0}=p_{0}(k,d,p)>0
such that for all
n\geq 0
, we have

\mathbb{P}(A_{n}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}})\geq p_{0}.

In particular, for all
m>n
, we have
\mathbb{P}(A_{m}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{S}_{n})\geq p_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
n\geq 0
. For each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
, we set

c_{i}\coloneqq g_{\tau_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})-(g_{\tau_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\cdot e_{d})e_{d}.

Applying Proposition
4.2
, there exist
\Pi
a partition of
[\![1,k]\!]
such that for all
\pi\in\Pi
,
|P_{\pi}^{R_{0}}|\geq a_{0}
. By translation, this implies that for all
\pi\in\Pi

|\Lambda_{\pi}|\geq a_{0}\quad\text{where}\quad\Lambda_{\pi}\coloneqq\bigcap_{i\in\pi}B^{+}\big(g_{\tau_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),R\big)\setminus\bigcup_{j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi}B\big(g_{\tau_{n}}^{\downarrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),\kappa+R\big).

Let us denote

By construction, we have that

Now, observe that thanks to Proposition
2.1
, conditionally on

\mathcal{N}\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{\tau_{n}})\setminus\overline{H}_{\tau_{n}}

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(A_{n}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}})&\geq\mathbb{P}(\#\mathcal{N}\cap E\quad\text{and}\quad\forall\pi\in\Pi,\penalty\ \#\mathcal{N}\cap\Lambda_{\pi}=1\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}})\\

\mathbb{P}(A_{n}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}})\geq p_{0}\coloneqq e^{-k|B^{+}(0,\kappa+R)|}\left(\inf_{a_{0}\leq t\leq|B^{+}(0,R)|}te^{-t}\right)^{k}.

Finally, if

We now use the previous result to show that the random variables

Lemma 4.4
.

The random variables
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are
(\mathcal{S}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
stopping times, and for all

and

\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_{n}>\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{S}_{\gamma_{n}})\leq(1-p_{0})^{\ell}.

First, observe that if

\{\gamma_{n}=j\}=A_{j}\cap\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\mathds{1}_{A_{i}}=n-1\right\},

is a
(\mathcal{S}_{i})_{i\geq 0}

\mathbb{P}(A_{\gamma_{n}+j+1}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{S}_{\gamma_{n}+j})\geq p_{0}.

Finally, recalling that

\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_{n}>\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{S}_{\gamma_{n}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(1-\mathds{1}_{A_{\gamma_{n}+i}})\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{S}_{\gamma_{n}}\right]\leq(1-p_{0})^{\ell}.

We now define a new filtration that will make the sequence

for all

Lemma 4.5

\mathbb{E}[X\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}]=\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{n}=i}\frac{\mathbb{E}[X\mathds{1}_{A_{i}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}}]}{\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}})}\leq\frac{1}{p_{0}}\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{n}=i}\mathbb{E}[X\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}}]

Since

is an almost surely finite

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[X\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[X\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{S}_{\gamma_{n}}]&=\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{n}=i}\mathds{1}_{A_{i}}\mathbb{E}[X\penalty\ |\penalty\ \sigma(\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}},\penalty\ A_{i})]\\

Then, since for each

We can now prove that the random variables

are almost surely finite and have spacing distributed with an exponentially decaying tail.

Proposition 4.6
.

The random variables
(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
adapted, almost surely finite, and there exist
\alpha=\alpha(k,d,p)>0
and

\mathbb{P}(\beta_{n+1}-\beta_{n}>\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})\leq\alpha e^{-\beta\ell}.

Proof.

First, observe that for all

\{\beta_{n}=j\}=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\{\gamma_{n}=i,\penalty\ \tau_{i}=j\},

where, by definition, each event in the union is
\mathcal{S}_{\gamma_{n}}
measurable. Hence
(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
adapted. Now for all

and

, observe that we can write

\mathbb{P}(\beta_{n+1}-\beta_{n}>A\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})\leq\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_{n}>\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})+\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\gamma_{n}+m}-\tau_{\gamma_{n}}>A\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}).

(4.1)

Using Lemma

, we have

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\gamma_{n}+\ell}-\tau_{\gamma_{n}}>A\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})\leq\frac{1}{p_{0}}\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{n}=i}\\
P(\tau_{i+\ell}-\tau_{i}>A\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}}).

(4.2)

From Theorem

, there exist two constant

such that, for all

\mathbb{E}[e^{t(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}]\leq C<\infty.

Therefore, for all
i\geq 0
, using Markov’s inequality we get

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{i+\ell}-\tau_{i}>Am\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}})\leq e^{-tA\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}e^{t(\tau_{i+j+1}-\tau_{i+j})}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}}\right]\leq e^{-tA\ell}C^{\ell}.

Now injecting in (
4.2
) and then in (
4.1
), using Lemma
4.4
we get that

\mathbb{P}(\beta_{n+1}-\beta_{n}>A\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})\leq(1-p_{0})^{\ell}+\frac{1}{p_{0}}e^{-tA\ell}C^{\ell}.

The result follows then by fixing
A>0
big enough so that
Ce^{-tA}<1
.
∎

4.2
Block size

In this subsection, we introduce a crucial quantity that will allow us to control the displacement of the trajectories between consecutive renewal times. For all integers
b>a\geq 0
we define

W(a,b)\coloneqq\sum_{i=a}^{b-1}\|x_{i}-\Psi(x_{i})\|.

By construction, for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
, from triangle inequality,

\|g_{b}(\mathbf{u}_{i})-g_{a}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\|\leq W(a,b).

The goal is to show the following proposition.

Proposition 4.7
.

For all
n\geq 0
, define

W_{n+1}\coloneqq W(\beta_{n},\beta_{n+1}).

Then there exist
\alpha=\alpha(k,d,p)>0
and
\beta=\beta(k,d,p)>0
such that for all
n\geq 0
and
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}(W_{n+1}\geq t\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})\leq\alpha e^{-\beta\sqrt{t}}.

The random variables
(W_{n+1})_{n\geq 0}
are called
block sizes
, and they will later be used to control the range of interaction as well as the displacement of each trajectory between consecutive renewal steps. For each
n\geq 0
and
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
we have by construction that

\|g_{\beta_{n+1}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})-g_{\beta_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\|\leq W_{n+1},

and furthermore,

\bigcup_{j=\beta_{n}}^{\beta_{n+1}-1}B^{+}\big(g_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),\|g_{j+1}(\mathbf{u}_{i})-g_{j}(\mathbf{u}_{i})\|\big)\subset B^{+}\big(g_{\beta_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),W_{n+1}\big).

In words, between step
\beta_{n}
and
\beta_{n+1}
, each trajectory explores a region whose size is controlled by
W_{n+1}
. We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

For all
b>a\geq 0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{W(a,b)}{b-a}\right)\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{a}\right]\leq e^{L_{a}}G_{2}(0)^{b-a}.

Proof.

First, recall that for all
i\in[\![a,b-1]\!]
we have
(\|x_{i}-\Psi(x_{i})\|-L_{i})_{+}=M_{i+1}-M_{i}
, giving

\|x_{i}-\Psi(x_{i})\|\leq L_{i}+M_{i+1}-M_{i}.

Summing for
i
between
a
and
b-1
we get

W(a,b)\leq M_{b}-M_{a}+\sum_{i=a}^{b-1}L_{i}.

(4.3)

Now observe that

\begin{split}\sum_{i=a}^{b-1}L_{i}=\sum_{i=a}^{b-1}\left(L_{a}+\sum_{j=a}^{i-1}(L_{j+1}-L_{j})\right)&=(b-a)L_{a}+\sum_{i=a}^{b-1}(b-1-i)(L_{i+1}-L_{i})\\
&\leq(b-a)L_{a}+(b-a)(M_{b}-M_{a}),\end{split}

(4.4)

where in the last line we used that for all
i\in[\![a,b-1]\!]
,

(b-1-i)(L_{i+1}-L_{i})\leq(b-a)(M_{j+1}-M_{j}).

Putting together (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), we get that

\frac{W(a,b)}{b-a}\leq\frac{M_{b}-M_{a}}{b-a}+L_{a}+M_{b}-M_{a}\leq L_{a}+2(M_{b}-M_{a}).

Hence, taking the exponential and the expectation,

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{W(a,b)}{b-a}\right)\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{a}\right]\leq e^{L_{a}}\mathbb{E}[e^{2(M_{b}-M_{a})}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{a}]\leq e^{L_{a}}G_{2}(0)^{b-a},

where for the last inequality we used Proposition
3.9
to get that for all
i\in[\![a,b-1]\!]
,

\mathbb{E}[e^{2(M_{i+1}-M_{i})}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{i}]\leq G_{2}(L_{i})\leq G_{2}(0).

The proof is complete.
∎

We can now prove Proposition
4.7
.

Proof of Proposition
4.7
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
A>0
. First, observe that we can write

\mathbb{P}(W_{n+1}\geq A\ell^{2}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})\leq\mathbb{P}(\beta_{n+1}-\beta_{n}>\ell\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})+\mathbb{P}(W(\beta_{n},\beta_{n}+\ell)\geq A\ell^{2}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}).

(4.5)

Using Lemma
4.5
, we can then write

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(W(\beta_{n},\beta_{n}+\ell)\geq A\ell^{2}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})&\leq\frac{1}{p_{0}}\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{n}=i}\mathbb{P}(W(\tau_{i},\tau_{i}+\ell)>A\ell^{2}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}})\\
&\leq\frac{e^{-A\ell}}{p_{0}}\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{n}=i}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{W(\tau_{i},\tau_{i}+\ell)}{\ell}\right)\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}}\right]\\
&\leq\frac{e^{-A\ell}G_{2}(0)^{\ell}}{p_{0}}\sum_{i\geq 0}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{n}=i}e^{L_{\tau_{i}}}\\
&\leq\frac{e^{\kappa}}{p_{0}}e^{-A\ell}G_{2}(0)^{\ell},\end{split}

where the second inequality follows from Markov’s inequality, the third one from Lemma
4.8
, and the last inequality uses that
L_{\tau_{i}}\leq\kappa
for all
i\geq 0
by definition of
\tau_{i}
. Now injecting in (
4.5
) and using Proposition
4.6
, we get

\mathbb{P}(W_{n+1}\geq A\ell^{2}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})\leq\alpha e^{-\beta\ell}+\frac{e^{\kappa}}{p_{0}}e^{-A\ell}G_{2}(0)^{\ell}.

Finally the result follows by choosing
A>0
big enough so that
e^{-A}G_{2}(0)<1
.
∎

4.3
The independent process

In this subsection, we introduce a new process, referred to as the
independent process
, which intuitively captures the behavior of trajectories when they evolve independently of one another. The rough idea behind this construction is inspired by
[
RSS16
]
. Its definition closely parallels that of the original exploration process, with the key difference being that the
k
trajectories evolve in
independent environments
rather than a shared one. This process, together with renewal times and block sizes, will allow us to rigorously formalize the heuristic that trajectories behave approximately independently when they are sufficiently far apart.

Here is the construction of the independent process. Let
(\mathcal{N}_{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
be i.i.d. random variables distributed as
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
conditioned on the event

\#\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\cap B^{+}(0,R)=\#\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\cap B(-\kappa e_{d},\kappa+R)\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(0)=1,

which is a centered version of the renewal event for a single trajectory. These random variables represent the independent environments in which each trajectory will evolve. We define the independent process
((g^{\prime}_{n}(i))_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]})_{n\geq 0}
by induction as follows. For each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
, we set
g_{0}^{\prime}(i)\coloneqq 0
. Note that we have re-centered everything for simplicity: since the trajectories will not interact, their absolute positions play no role in the analysis, and placing them all at the origin serves to streamline the construction. Let
n\geq 0
and assume that
(g_{n}^{\prime}(i))_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
is constructed. We set

m^{\prime}_{n}\coloneqq\min_{1\leq i\leq k}\big(g^{\prime}_{n}(i)\cdot e_{d}\big),

and we fix
i_{n}\in[\![1,k]\!]
such that
g^{\prime}_{n}(i_{n})\cdot e_{d}=m^{\prime}_{n}
. In case of multiple possibilities, we chose one deterministically. Then, for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
and
n\geq 0
we define

g^{\prime}_{n+1}(i)=\begin{cases}\Psi_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}\big(g_{n}^{\prime}(i)\big)&\text{if $i=i_{n}$}\\
g^{\prime}_{n}(i)&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\Psi_{\mathcal{N}_{i}^{\prime}}(g_{n}^{\prime}(i))
denotes the closest point to
g^{\prime}_{n}(i)
in
\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{n}^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{N}_{i}
with respect to the
\ell^{p}
distance. In words, analogously to the original exploration process, at each step we advance the trajectory whose current position has the lowest
e_{d}
-coordinate. Now that the independent exploration process is constructed, we define some quantities of interest. For all
n\geq 0
and each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
we define an
individual history set

H_{n}^{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{H}^{+}(m^{\prime}_{n})\cap\bigcup_{j=0}^{n-1}B^{+}\big(g_{n}^{\prime}(i),\|g_{n+1}^{\prime}(i)-g_{n}^{\prime}(i)\|\big),

as well as an
individual history size

L_{n}^{i}\coloneqq\max(\{0\}\cup\{x\cdot e_{d}-m^{\prime}_{n}:x\in H_{n}^{i}\}).

For each
n\geq 0
and
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
we set

g_{n}^{+}(i)=g_{n}(i)+(m_{n}+\kappa-g_{n}(i)\cdot e_{d})e_{d}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{n}^{-}(i)=g_{n}(i)+(m_{n}-g_{n}(i)\cdot e_{d})e_{d},

and we define

\begin{split}\zeta\coloneqq\inf\{n\geq 0:\quad&m^{\prime}_{n}\geq R,\penalty\ \forall i\in[\![1,k]\!],\penalty\ L_{n}^{i}\leq\kappa,\\
&\text{and}\quad\forall i\in[\![1,k]\!],\penalty\ \#\mathcal{N}_{i}\cap B^{+}(g_{n}^{-}(i),\kappa+R)\setminus\overline{H}_{n}^{i}=\#B^{+}(g_{n}^{+}(i),R)=1\}.\end{split}

This random index is the analog of the first renewal time in the exploration process. Finally for all
1\leq i<j\leq k
, we define

\Delta^{i,j}\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{\zeta<\infty}\mathbf{p}\big(g^{\prime}_{\zeta}(i)-g^{\prime}_{\zeta}(j)\big),

In words,
\Delta^{i,j}
represent the drift from time
0
to
\zeta
between trajectory
i
and
j
. We also set

W\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[\![1,k]\!]\\
0\leq n<\zeta\end{subarray}}\|g^{\prime}_{n+1}(i)-g^{\prime}_{n}(i)\|,

so that by construction, for all
1\leq i<j\leq k
,

\|\Delta^{i,j}\|\leq W.

Before relating the independent process to the exploration process, we establish some properties on the distribution of
(\Delta^{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq k}
in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

The
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
-valued random variables
(\Delta^{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq k}
are identically distributed, and their common distribution is sign-symmetric and exchangeable. More precisely, for any permutation
\sigma
of
[\![1,d-1]\!]
and sign function
\varepsilon:[\![1,d-1]\!]\to\{-1,1\}
, the random variable

\big((\varepsilon(s)\Delta^{1,2}\cdot e_{\sigma(s)})_{1\leq s<d},W\big)

is distributed as
(\Delta^{1,2},W)
.

Proof.

To begin, observe that the only element in the construction that could prevent the variables to be identically distributed is the dissymmetry introduced when choosing
i_{n}
for
n\geq 0
when there is a tie, i.e. more than one index
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
such that
g^{\prime}_{n}(i)\cdot e_{d}=m^{\prime}_{n}
. Then, observe that almost surely, ties occur only in the
k
first steps. In fact, for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
,
g_{0}(i)\cdot e_{d}=0
, thus
\zeta\geq k
and

g_{k}^{\prime}(i)=\Psi_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}(0)

for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
. The variables
(g_{k}(i)\cdot e_{d})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
are thus i.i.d. and almost surely distinct. By induction, we then get that, almost surely, for each
n\geq k
, the random variables
(g_{k}^{\prime}(i)\cdot e_{d})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
are distinct. Hence, we deduce that the random variables
((g^{\prime}_{n}(i))_{n\geq k})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
are identically distributed and the first statement follows. It remains to check that
\Delta^{1,2}
is exchangeable and sign-symmetric. Let
\sigma
be a permutation of
[\![1,d-1]\!]
and
\varepsilon:[\![1,d-1]\!]\to\{-1,1\}
be a sign function. Let us define

S=S_{\sigma,\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\penalty\ x\mapsto(x\cdot e_{d})e_{d}+\sum_{s=1}^{d-1}\varepsilon(s)(x\cdot e_{\sigma(s)})e_{s}.

Since
S
preserves the Lebesgue measure, we have that
S(\mathcal{N}^{\prime})
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
. Hence, we deduce that

\big(S(\mathcal{N}_{i})\big)_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}.

is distributed as
(\mathcal{N}_{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
. Finally, we conclude the proof by observing that we obtain almost surely

\big((\varepsilon(s)\Delta^{1,2}\cdot e_{\sigma(s)})_{1\leq s<d},W\big)

by constructing the independent process using the transformed environments
(S(\mathcal{N}_{i}))_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
.
∎

Using the independent process as well as renewal times and block sizes, we conclude the section by showing the following theorem.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
n\geq 0
. For all
1\leq i<j\leq k
, we define

Z_{n}^{i,j}\coloneqq\mathbf{p}\big(g_{\beta_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{i})-g_{\beta_{n}}(\mathbf{u}_{j})\big),\quad\text{and we set}\quad r_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\min_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\|Z_{n}^{i,j}\|.

Then, conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, when
r_{n}\geq\kappa+R
,

\big(W_{n+1}\wedge r_{n},\penalty\ \big((Z^{i,j}_{n+1}-Z^{i,j}_{n})\mathds{1}_{W_{n+1}<r_{n}}\big)_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\big)\quad\text{is distributed as}\quad\big(W\wedge r_{n},\penalty\ (\Delta^{i,j}\mathds{1}_{W<r_{n}})_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\big).

Furthermore, there exists
\alpha=\alpha(k,d,p)>0
and
\beta=\beta(k,d,p)>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(W\geq t)\leq\alpha e^{-\beta\sqrt{t}}.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{N}_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
be i.i.d. copies of
\mathcal{N}
. From now on, we work conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Assume
r_{n}\geq\kappa+R
. For each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
we set

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}^{i}\coloneqq(\mathcal{N}_{i}^{\prime}\setminus A)\cup\big((\mathcal{N}-g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}))\cap A\big)\quad\text{where}\quad A\coloneqq(-r_{n},r_{n}]^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}.

We refer to Figure
7
for an illustration. Let us check that the random variables
(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}^{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
are independent and identically distributed as
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
conditioned on the event

\#\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\cap B^{+}(0,R)=\#\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\cap B(-\kappa e_{d},\kappa+R)\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(0)=1.

Thanks to Lemma
4.5
and Proposition
2.1
,

\mathcal{N}\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{n}+\kappa)

is distributed as
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{n}+\kappa)
conditioned on

\{\forall i\in[\![1,k]\!],\penalty\ \#\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\cap B^{+}(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),R)=\#\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\cap B(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\downarrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),\kappa+R)\cap\mathbb{H}^{+}(m_{n}+\kappa)=1\}.

Since
r_{n}\geq\kappa+R
and the sets
(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i})+A)_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
are disjoints by construction, this condition transfers to the constructed variables
(\mathcal{N}_{c}^{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
, yielding the claimed distribution. Now, let
((\Delta^{i,j}_{\mathrm{c}})_{1\leq i<j\leq k},W_{\mathrm{c}})
be defined by constructing the independent process using the environments
(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}^{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
. By construction, we have

W_{n+1}\wedge r_{n}=W_{\mathrm{c}}\wedge r_{n},

and if
W_{n+1}<r_{n}
then no trajectory will explore the resampled regions, hence for all
1\leq i<j\leq k
,

Z_{n+1}^{i,j}-Z_{n}^{i,j}=\Delta^{i,j}_{\mathrm{c}},

implying the first part of the theorem. To get the last statement, let
t>0
,
M\geq\kappa+R
and consider
k=2
,
n=0
,
\mathbf{u}_{1}=-Me_{1}
and
\mathbf{u}_{2}=Me_{1}
. The event
A_{0}
, on which
\beta_{0}=0
and thus
r_{0}=M\geq\kappa+R
, occurs with positive probability thanks to Lemma
4.3
. On that event, we then have

\mathbb{P}(W\wedge M>t)=\mathbb{P}(W_{0}\wedge M>t\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{0})\leq\mathbb{P}(W_{0}>t\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{0})\leq\alpha e^{-\beta\sqrt{t}}.

Finally, since
M
was arbitrary, we get the sought result. The proof is complete.
∎

5
Coalescence in low dimensions

In this section, we establish the coalescence statement of our main result, stated precisely in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that
d=2
with
p\in[1,\infty]
or
d=3
with
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
. Then the DSF is almost surely a tree.

In the remaining of this section, we assume that
d=2
with
p\in[1,\infty]
or
d=3
with
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
.

To prove the theorem, we analyze the case
k=2
of the exploration process, starting from two fixed deterministic points
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
\mathbf{u}_{1}\cdot e_{d}=\mathbf{u}_{2}\cdot e_{d}
. Our goal is to show that the two trajectories coalesce, which amounts to proving that

(Z_{n})_{n\geq 0}\coloneqq(Z_{n}^{1,2})_{n\geq 0}

reaches eventually
0
, almost surely. We start by presenting a sketch of the proof. The behavior of the
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
-adapted process
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 0}
far from the origin can be approximated by a process with i.i.d. increments. More precisely, thanks to Theorem
4.10
, for all
n\geq 0
, conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, if
\|Z_{n}\|\geq 2(\kappa+R)
,

(Z_{n+1}-Z_{n})\mathds{1}_{2W_{n+1}<\|Z_{n}\|}\quad\text{is distributed as}\quad\Delta\mathds{1}_{2W<\|Z_{n}\|},\quad\text{where}\quad\Delta\coloneqq\Delta^{1,2}.

The distribution of the
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
-valued random variable
\Delta
is sign-symmetric and exchangeable by Lemma
4.9
and
\|\Delta\|
admits moments of all orders from Theorem
4.10
as
\|\Delta\|\leq W
.

Then, the overall approach is similar to the strategy developed in
[
RSS16
, Section 4]
. We want to use the increment approximation far from
0
in order to find a function
V:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying
V(z)\to\infty
as
z\to\infty
, and a constant
M>0
such that, for all
n\geq 0
,

\mathds{1}_{\|Z_{n}\|>M}\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n+1})-V(Z_{n})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}]\leq 0.

Such a function
V
is called a
Lyapunov function
(see e.g.
[
MT12
]
). We will see that its existence ensures the process does not escape to infinity. Then, since for any fixed
K>0
, whenever
\|Z_{n}\|_{p}\leq K
for some
n\geq 0
, there is a probability uniformly bounded away from
0
of coalescing in one step, we will deduce that coalescence occurs almost surely. In the case of simple random walks on
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, functions of the form
z\mapsto\log^{\alpha}(1+\|z\|_{2})
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
can be used as Lyapounov function. In our case, for computational convenience, we choose instead

V:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\penalty\ z\mapsto\log\log(e+\|z\|_{2}^{2}).

(5.1)

We emphasize that we use the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
in the definition of
V
, even when
p\neq 2
, as it simplifies later computations. In order to avoid ambiguity, we will often denote the
\ell^{p}
norm by
\|\cdot\|_{p}
rather than
\|\cdot\|
in what follows. Note that for each
n\geq 0
,
V(Z_{n})
is integrable as
V(z)=o_{z\to\infty}(\|z\|_{p})
and

\|Z_{n}\|_{p}\leq Z_{0}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}W_{i}.

To prepare for the Taylor expansion used in the analysis of the increments of the process
(V(Z_{n}))_{n\geq 0}
, we begin by computing some expectations involving the asymptotic increment variable
\Delta
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. Define

X(y)\coloneqq\|y+\Delta\|_{2}^{2}-\|y\|_{2}^{2}.

Then we have

•

E[X(y)]=(d-1)\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{1})^{2}]
,

•

\mathbb{E}[X(y)^{2}]\geq 4\|y\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{1})^{2}]
,

•

and
\mathbb{E}[X(y)^{3}]=\mathcal{O}_{\|y\|_{2}\to\infty}(\|y\|_{2}^{2})
.

Proof.

First, observe that we can write

X(y)=(y+\Delta)\cdot(y+\Delta)-y\cdot y=\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}+2y\cdot\Delta.

(5.2)

Taking the expectation, since
\Delta
is symmetric and exchangeable, we get

\mathbb{E}[X(y)]=\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}]=\sum_{s=1}^{d-1}\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{s})^{2}]=(d-1)\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{1})^{2}].

Now, squaring (
5.2
) and using symmetry, we get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[X(y)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta\|_{2}^{4}]+4\mathbb{E}[(y\cdot\Delta)^{2}]\geq 4\mathbb{E}[(y\cdot\Delta)^{2}]=4\|y\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{1})^{2}],\end{split}

where for the last inequality we used that
(\Delta\cdot e_{s_{1}})(\Delta\cdot e_{s_{2}})
is symmetric for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in[\![1,d-1]\!]
with
s_{1}\neq s_{2}
from Lemma
4.9
. Similarly, from symmetry of
\Delta
and using (
5.2
), we can write

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[X(y)^{3}]&=\mathbb{E}[(\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}+2y\cdot\Delta)^{3}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta\|_{2}^{6}]+3\mathbb{E}[(2y\cdot\Delta)^{2}\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[\|\Delta\|_{2}^{6}]+12\|y\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{1})^{2}\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}],\end{split}

(5.3)

where for the last inequality we used that
(\Delta\cdot e_{s_{1}})(\Delta\cdot e_{s_{2}})\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}
is symmetric for all
s_{1},s_{2}\in[\![1,d-1]\!]
with
s_{1}\neq s_{2}
. The proof is complete.
∎

We now define the function

\delta:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R},\penalty\ y\mapsto\mathbb{E}[V(y+\Delta)-V(y)],

where
V
is defined in (
5.1
). This function will be central to approximate
\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n+1})-V(Z_{n})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}]
when
\|Z_{n}\|_{p}
is large. The following lemma gives an asymptotic estimate of
\delta
using a Taylor expansion. It gives in particular that
\delta(y)
is negative when
\|y\|_{2}
is big enough. Note that, as in the case of simple walks in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, this is where the fact that
d\leq 3
, or equivalently
d-1\leq 2
, plays a role.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume
d\leq 3
. Then we have that

\delta(y)\leq-\frac{2\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{1})^{2}]+o_{y\to\infty}(1)}{\|y\|_{2}^{2}(\log\|y\|_{2}^{2})^{2}}

Proof.

Let
f:(1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\penalty\ z\mapsto\log\log z
. From direct computations, for all
z>1
we have

f^{(1)}(z)=\frac{1}{z\log z},\quad f^{(2)}(z)=-\frac{\log z+1}{(z\log z)^{2}},\quad f^{(3)}(z)=\frac{2+o_{z\to\infty}(1)}{z^{3}\log z}\quad\text{and}\quad f^{(4)}(z)\leq 0.

(5.4)

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. We set

z\coloneqq e+\|y\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
f^{(4)}
is non-positive, using a Taylor expansion at order
3
we get that

\begin{split}V(y+\Delta)-V(y)&=f[z+X(y)]-f(z)\\
&\leq X(y)f^{(1)}(z)+\frac{X(y)^{2}}{2}f^{(2)}(z)+\frac{X(y)^{3}}{6}f^{(3)}(z).\end{split}

(5.5)

Now taking the expectation on both sides and using Lemma
5.2
as well as
f^{(2)}\leq 0
, we get

\begin{split}\delta(y)&=\mathbb{E}[V(y+\Delta)-V(y)]\\
&\leq(d-1)\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{1})^{2}]f^{(1)}(z)+2\|y\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{1})^{2}]f^{(2)}(z)+f^{(3)}(z)\mathcal{O}_{z\to\infty}(z).\end{split}

Now since
\|y\|_{2}^{2}=z-e
we can rewrite the inequality as

\delta(y)\leq 2\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{1})^{2}]\left(\frac{d-1}{2}f^{(1)}(z)+zf^{(2)}(z)-ef^{(2)}(z)\right)+f^{(3)}(z)\mathcal{O}_{z\to\infty}(z).

(5.6)

Now, observe that since
d-1\leq 2
and
f^{(1)}\geq 0
, then

\frac{d-1}{2}f^{(1)}(z)+zf^{(2)}(z)\leq f^{(1)}(z)+zf^{(2)}(z)=-\frac{1}{z\log^{2}z}.

Moreover,

f^{(2)}(z)=o_{z\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{z\log^{2}z}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad zf^{(3)}(z)=o_{z\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{z\log^{2}z}\right),

Hence injecting in (
5.6
) we get

\delta(y)\leq-\frac{2\mathbb{E}[(\Delta\cdot e_{1})^{2}]+o_{z\to\infty}(1)}{z\log^{2}z},

and the sought result follows by definition of
z
. The proof is complete.
∎

Using the previous lemma, we now prove that
V
is indeed a Lyapunov function.

Proposition 5.4
.

There exist a constant
M>0
such that for all
n\geq 0

\mathds{1}_{\|Z_{n}\|_{p}>M}\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n+1})-V(Z_{n})\penalty\ |\penalty\ Z_{n}]\leq 0.

Proof.

Let
n\geq 0
and assume
\|Z_{n}\|_{p}\geq 2(\kappa+R)
. Using Theorem
4.10
, we have that

\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n+1})\mathds{1}_{2W_{n+1}<\|Z_{n}\|_{p}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}]=h(Z_{n}),

where

h:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R},\penalty\ y\mapsto\mathbb{E}[V(y+\Delta)\mathds{1}_{2W<r_{n}}]\leq\mathbb{E}[V(y+\Delta)],

as
V\geq 0
. Therefore, we deduce that

\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n+1})\mathds{1}_{2W_{n+1}<\|Z_{n}\|_{p}}-V(Z_{n})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}]\leq\delta(Z_{n}).

(5.7)

By equivalence of norms on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, there exist
C>0
such that
\|\cdot\|_{2}\leq C\|\cdot\|_{p}
. By construction, we have
\|Z_{n+1}-Z_{n}\|_{p}\leq W_{n+1}
, hence using triangle inequality we get

\|Z_{n+1}\|_{2}\leq C\|Z_{n+1}\|_{p}\leq C(\|Z_{n}\|_{p}+W_{n+1}).

This imply that on the event
\{2W_{n+1}\geq\|Z_{n}\|_{p}\}
,
\|Z_{n+1}\|_{2}\leq 3CW_{n+1}
hence using the definition of
V
, we get that

V(Z_{n+1})\mathds{1}_{2W_{n+1}\geq\|Z_{n}\|_{p}}\leq g(W_{n+1})\mathds{1}_{2W_{n+1}\geq\|Z_{n}\|_{p}}.

where

g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\penalty\ z\mapsto\log\log(e+9C^{2}z^{2}).

Using Proposition
4.7
, since
g(z)=o_{z\to\infty}(\log z)
, there exists
C^{\prime}<\infty
such that for all
m\geq 0
,
\mathbb{E}[g(W_{m})^{2}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{m}]\leq C^{\prime}
. Then, using a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[g(W_{n+1})\mathds{1}_{2W_{n+1}\geq\|Z_{n}\|_{p}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}]^{2}&\leq\mathbb{P}[2W_{n+1}\geq\|Z_{n}\|_{p}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}]\mathbb{E}[g(W_{n+1})^{2}\mathds{1}_{2W_{n+1}\geq\|Z_{n}\|_{p}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}]\\
&\leq\alpha C^{\prime}e^{-\beta\sqrt{\|Z_{n}\|_{p}/2}}=\tilde{\delta}(Z_{n})^{2},\end{split}

(5.8)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are given by Proposition
4.7
and

\tilde{\delta}:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R},\penalty\ y\mapsto\sqrt{\alpha C^{\prime}}e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\sqrt{\|Z_{n}\|_{p}/2}}.

Combining (
5.8
) with (
5.7
) we get

\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n+1})-V(Z_{n})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}]\leq\delta(Z_{n})+\tilde{\delta}(Z_{n}).

Finally, since
\tilde{\delta}(y)=o_{y\to\infty}(\delta(y))
and
\delta(y)\to 0^{-}
as
y\to\infty
, we deduce that we can find
M\geq 2(\kappa+R)
such that for all
n\geq 0
,

\big(\delta(Z_{n})+\tilde{\delta}(Z_{n})\big)\mathds{1}_{\|Z_{n}\|_{p}>M}\leq 0.

The proof is complete.

∎

We now prove from the existence of the Lyapunov function that
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 0}
can not escape to infinity.

Lemma 5.5
.

Almost surely,
Z_{n}\not\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

For all
n_{0}\geq 0
, we define

\zeta_{n_{0}}\coloneqq\inf\{n\geq n_{0}:\|Z_{n}\|\leq M\},

Where
M
is given by Proposition
5.4
. Now observe that if
Z_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, there exist
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,
\|Z_{n}\|>M
. In that case
\zeta_{n_{0}}=\infty
and since
V(z)\to\infty
as
z\to\infty
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}V(Z_{n\wedge\zeta_{n_{0}}})=\liminf_{n\to\infty}V(Z_{n})=\infty.

To show that
Z_{n}\not\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, it is therefore enough to check that for all
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\liminf_{n\to\infty}V(Z_{n\wedge\zeta_{n_{0}}})<\infty\quad\text{almost surely}.

(5.9)

To conclude the proof, let us show that it is indeed the case. Fix
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
n\geq n_{0}
. The event
\zeta_{n_{0}}>n
is
\mathcal{G}_{n}
measurable, hence we can write

\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{(n+1)\wedge\zeta_{n_{0}}})-V(Z_{n\wedge\zeta_{n_{0}}})]=\mathbb{E}(\mathds{1}_{\zeta_{n_{0}}>n}\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n+1})-V(Z_{n})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n}])\leq 0

where the last inequality follows from Proposition
5.4
since
\|Z_{n}\|_{p}>M
on the event

by definition. We deduce then by induction that for all

\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n\wedge\zeta_{n_{0}}})]\leq\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n_{0}})].

Finally using Fatou’s Lemma, since

, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\liminf_{n\to\infty}V(Z_{n\wedge\zeta_{n_{0}}})\right]\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n\wedge\zeta_{n_{0}}})]\leq\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{n_{0}})]<\infty.

This proves (
5.9
). The proof is complete.
∎

We now show that for all
K>0
, coalescence in one step occurs with probability bounded away from zero at any step
n\geq 0
, provided

.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let

. There exist

such that for all for all

,

\mathds{1}_{\|Z_{n}\|\leq K}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n+1}\neq 0\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})\leq q_{K}.

Proof.

Let
n\geq 0
. We set

U_{n}=\bigcup_{i\in\{1,2\}}B^{+}\big(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{i}),R\big),

and

Thanks to Lemma
4.5
and Proposition

\mathbb{P}(\#\mathcal{N}\cap V_{n}=0\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})=e^{-|V_{n}|}\geq e^{-2|B^{+}(0,K+R)|}.

Now observe that if
Z_{n}\leq K
, then

B^{+}\big(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{2}),R\big)\subset B^{+}\big(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{1}),K+R\big)\quad\text{and}\quad B^{+}\big(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{1}),R\big)\subset B^{+}\big(g_{\beta_{n}}^{\uparrow}(\mathbf{u}_{2}),K+R\big).

Therefore, if
Z_{n}\leq K
and
\#\mathcal{N}\cap V_{n}=0
and if
x
and
y
respectively denotes the only points of
\mathcal{N}
in

and

\Psi(x)=y\quad\text{or}\quad\Psi(y)=x\quad\text{or}\quad x=y.

This ensures that when

, either

. It follows that,

\begin{split}\mathds{1}_{\|Z_{n}\|\leq K}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n+1}\neq 0\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})&\leq\mathds{1}_{\|Z_{n}\|\leq K}\mathbb{P}(\#\mathcal{N}\cap V_{n}\neq 0\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathcal{G}_{n})\\
&\leq(1-e^{-2|B^{+}(0,K+R)|})\eqqcolon q_{K}<1.\end{split}

The proof is complete.
∎

We now derive that almost surely, the trajectories eventually coalesce.

Proposition 5.7
.

Almost surely, the process
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 0}
eventually hits
0
, meaning that the trajectories from
\mathbf{u}_{1}
and
\mathbf{u}_{2}
coalesce.

Proof.

Since
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|Z_{n}\|

, we have

\mathbb{P}(\forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ Z_{n}\neq 0)=\lim_{K\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|Z_{n}\|<K,\penalty\ \forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ Z_{n}\neq 0\right).

\mathbb{P}\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|Z_{n}\|<K,\penalty\ \forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ Z_{n}\neq 0\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(\theta_{\ell}<\infty,\penalty\ \forall i\in[\![0,\ell]\!],\penalty\ Z_{\theta_{i}+1}\neq 0)\leq{q_{K}}^{\ell},

∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of the section.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

From Proposition
5.7
, we have that almost surely, for all

∎

6
Non-coalescence in high dimensions

In this section, we prove the non-coalescence part of our main result, stated precisely in the following theorem.

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
and
d\geq 4
. Then the DSF consists almost surely of infinitely many disjoint trees.

For the remainder of this section, we assume that
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
and
d\geq 4
. The strategy is the following. Fix an arbitrary
k\geq 0
. Our goal is to find deterministic starting points
(\mathbf{u}_{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot e_{d}=0
for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
, such that the probability that the corresponding explored trajectories never coalesce is positive. That is

\mathbb{P}(\forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ r_{n}>0)>0.

To this end, consider a sequence
((\Delta^{i,j}_{n})_{1\leq i<j\leq k},D_{n+1})_{n\geq 0}
of i.i.d. copies of
((\Delta^{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq k},W)
, independent of the Poisson point process
\mathcal{N}
. For each
n\geq 0
and pair
1\leq i<j\leq k
, define

S_{n}^{i,j}\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{n}\Delta_{l}^{i,j}\quad\text{and we set}\quad\tilde{r}_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\max_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\|Z_{0}^{i,j}+S_{n}^{i,j}\|.

The processes
((Z_{n}^{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq k})_{n\geq 0}
and
((Z_{0}^{i,j}+S_{n}^{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq k})_{n\geq 0}
are linked via Theorem
4.10
. The following lemma leverages this connection to prove a key intermediate result used in the next step of the argument.

Lemma 6.2
.

For all
n\geq 0
, let us define

•

G_{n}\coloneqq\{\forall k\leq n-1,\penalty\ r_{n}>W_{n+1}\}\cap\{\forall k\leq n,\penalty\ r_{n}\geq\kappa+R\}
,

•

\tilde{G}_{n}\coloneqq\{\forall k\leq n-1,\penalty\ \tilde{r}_{n}>D_{n+1}\}\cap\{\forall k\leq n,\penalty\ \tilde{r}_{n}\geq\kappa+R\}
.

Then,

(\mathds{1}_{G_{n}}\penalty\ ,(Z_{n}^{i,j}\mathds{1}_{G_{n}})_{1\leq i<j\leq k})\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathds{1}_{\tilde{G}_{n}},\penalty\ ((Z_{0}^{i,j}+S_{n}^{i,j})\mathds{1}_{\tilde{G}_{n}})_{1\leq i<j\leq k})

\mathbb{P}(G_{n})=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{G}_{n})
.

Proof.

Obtained by induction over
n\geq 0
. The initialization is clear since
r_{0}=\tilde{r}_{0}
and the heredity is ensured by Theorem
4.10
.
∎

Using Lemma
6.2
, we can write that

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ r_{n}>0)&\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n\geq 0}G_{n}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(G_{n})\\
&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{G}_{n})=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\tilde{G}_{n}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}(\forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ \tilde{r}_{n}\geq D_{n+1}+\kappa+R).\end{split}

(6.1)

Then, observing that on the renewal event
A_{0}
, we have
\beta_{0}=\tau_{0}=0
and thus
Z_{0}^{i,j}=\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})
for all
1\leq i<j\leq k
, we derive from (
6.1
) that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}(\forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ r_{n}>0)\\
&\quad\geq\mathbb{P}(A_{0},\penalty\ \forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ \tilde{r}_{n}\geq D_{n+1}+\kappa+R)\\
&\quad=\mathbb{P}(A_{0},\penalty\ \forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ \forall 1\leq i<j\leq k,\penalty\ \|\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})+S_{n}^{i,j}\|\geq 2(D_{n+1}+\kappa+R)).\end{split}

It therefore suffices to verify that we can choose
(\mathbf{u}_{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
so that

\mathbb{P}(A_{0},\penalty\ \forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ \forall 1\leq i<j\leq k,\penalty\ \|\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j})+S_{n}^{i,j}\|_{p}\geq 2(D_{n+1}+\kappa+R))>0.

(6.2)

The advantage of this reformulation is that we are now working with a system built on i.i.d. random variables. Before proceeding with the proof of Theorem
6.1
, we establish the following lemma, which is based on a general result of Kesten
[
Kes78
]
concerning the rate of escape to infinity for transient random walks in dimension larger than three. Note that this is where we use that

.

Lemma 6.3
.

For all
A>0
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists
(s_{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})^{k}
such that

\mathbb{P}(\forall n\geq 0,\penalty\ \forall 1\leq i<j\leq k,\penalty\ \|s_{i}-s_{j}+S_{n}^{i,j}\|\geq A+n^{1/3})\geq 1-\epsilon.

Proof.

For each

,
(S_{n}^{i,j})_{n\geq 0}
is a random walk with i.i.d. increments distributed as
\Delta
, whose support spans
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
as it is exchangeable and non-trivial. Let

is increasing and the function
t\mapsto f(t)t^{-1/2}
decreases to
0
as

f(t)^{d-3}t^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\sim t^{\frac{d-3}{3}-\frac{d-1}{2}}=t^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d}{6}}=o\left(\frac{1}{t^{1+1/6}}\right),

implying that

\int_{[1,\infty]}f(t)^{(d-1)-2}t^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

These are precisely the conditions required to apply the main theorem of
[
Kes78
]
, which implies that, almost surely, for each
1\leq i<j\leq k

\frac{\|S_{n}^{i,j}\|}{f(n)}\to\infty.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(\forall n\geq n_{0}\penalty\ \forall 1\leq i<j\leq k,\penalty\ \|S_{n}^{i,j}\|\geq f(n))\to 1\quad\text{as}\quad n_{0}\to\infty.

Now, fixing

big enough and using that the process
((S_{n}^{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq k})_{n\geq 0}
is a random walk with i.i.d. increments in
(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})^{k(k+1)/2}
, we can write

1-\epsilon\leq\mathbb{P}(\forall n\geq n_{0}\penalty\ \forall 1\leq i<j\leq k\penalty\ \|S_{n}^{i,j}\|\geq f(n))=\mathbb{E}[P((S_{n_{0}}^{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq k})],

(6.3)

where

P:(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})^{k(k+1)/2}\to[0,1],\penalty\ (s_{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\mapsto\mathbb{P}(\forall n\geq 0\penalty\ \forall i\in[\![1,k]\!]\penalty\ \|s_{i,j}+S_{n}^{i,j}\|\geq f(n_{0}+n)).

Since for each
1\leq i<j<\ell\leq k
we have
S_{n_{0}}^{i,j}+S_{n_{0}}^{j,\ell}=S_{n_{0}}^{i,\ell}
, then (
6.3
) implies that there exists
(s_{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d-1})^{k(k+1)/2}
such that

1-\epsilon\leq P((s_{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq k})

with
s_{i,j}+s_{j,\ell}=s_{i,\ell}
for all
1\leq i<j<\ell\leq k
. Then, setting
s_{1}\coloneqq 0
and
s_{i}\coloneqq s_{1,i}
for each
1<i\leq k
, we have
s_{i,j}=s_{i}-s_{j}
for all

inductively, we can show the following clustering property.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\delta>0
. There exists
\epsilon>0
such that for any
(c_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
c_{i}\cdot e_{d}=0
for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
, we can construct a partition
\Pi
of
[\![1,k]\!]
such that for all
\pi\in\Pi

•

r_{\pi}\leq\delta
,

•

and for all
j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi
we have
\|c_{j}-c_{\pi}\|>r_{\pi}+\epsilon
.

Proof.

Let us construct by induction some sequences
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
(\Pi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(\xi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
such that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a sequence of subset of
[\![1,k]\!]
increasing to
[\![1,k]\!]
and for all
n\geq 0
,
\xi_{n}\leq\delta/C_{k}
and
\Pi_{n}
is a partition of
X_{n}
satisfying for all
\pi\in\Pi_{n}

(A)

r_{\pi}\leq\delta
,

(B)

for all
j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi
we have
\|c_{j}-c_{\pi}\|_{p}>r_{\pi}+\xi_{n}
.

We start by setting
\Pi_{0}\coloneqq\emptyset
and
\xi_{0}\coloneqq\delta/C_{k}
. Since
\Pi_{0}
is empty, the two points are verified, and the initialization is verified.

Now let
n\geq 0
and assume we have constructed
X_{n}
,
\Pi_{n}
and
\xi_{n}
. If
X_{n}=[\![1,k]\!]
, we set
\Pi_{n+1}=\Pi_{n}
,
\xi_{n+1}=\xi_{n}
and the heredity holds. Otherwise, we need to enlarge
X_{n}
. To do so we fix
i_{n}\coloneqq\min X_{n}\setminus[\![1,k]\!]
and we set

\xi_{n+1}\coloneqq\frac{\xi_{n}}{2C_{k}}.

We have
\xi_{n+1}\leq\xi_{n}\leq\delta/C_{k}
. Applying Lemma
B.2
to get a subset
\pi_{n}\subset[\![1,k]\!]
such that
i_{n}\in\pi_{n}
,
r_{\pi}\leq C_{k}\xi_{n+1}\leq\delta
and for all
j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi_{n}
,
\|c_{j}-c_{\pi_{n}}\|>r_{\pi}+\xi_{n+1}
. We set
\Pi_{n+1}\coloneqq\Pi_{n}\cup\{\pi_{n}\}
and
X_{n+1}\coloneqq X_{n}\cup\pi_{n}
. Since
\xi_{n+1}\leq\xi_{n}
, then (A) and (B) are verified.

Now it remains to check that
\Pi_{n+1}
is a partition, i.e. that
\pi_{n}\cap X_{n}=\emptyset
. Let
j\in X_{n}
. By definition, there exists
\pi\in\Pi_{n}
such that
j\in\pi
. Since
i_{n}\notin\pi
, we can write

\begin{split}r_{\pi^{\prime}}+\xi_{n}&<\|c_{i_{n}}-c_{\pi^{\prime}}\|\\
&\leq\|c_{i_{n}}-c_{\pi_{n}}\|+\|c_{\pi_{n}}-c_{j}\|+\|c_{j}-c_{\pi}\|\\
&\leq r_{\pi_{n}}+\|c_{\pi}-c_{j}\|+r_{\pi},\end{split}

which implies that

\|c_{\pi_{n}}-c_{j}\|-r_{\pi_{n}}>\xi_{n}-2r_{\pi_{n}}\geq\xi_{n}-2C_{k}\xi_{n+1}=0.

By definition of
r_{\pi_{n}}
, this implies
j\notin\pi_{n}
. We then have
\pi_{n}\cap X_{n}=\emptyset
, and thus
\Pi_{n+1}
is indeed a partition; the construction is complete.

Now since
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
increases to
[\![1,k]\!]
, we always have
X_{k}=[\![1,k]\!]
. Therefore, setting
\Pi=\Pi_{k}
, from (A) and (B), the proposition holds with

\epsilon\coloneqq\xi_{k}=\frac{\delta}{C_{k}(2C_{k})^{k}}>0.

∎

We now prove the sought proposition.

Proof of Proposition
B.1
in the case
p<\infty
.

Fix
\delta<\frac{1}{2}
. Let
\epsilon>0
and
\Pi
be given by Lemma
B.3
. Up to choosing a smaller,
\epsilon
, we can assume
\epsilon<\delta
. Fix
\pi\in\Pi
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
x\cdot e_{d}=0
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
. Define
\alpha(\pi,x,h)\coloneqq c_{\pi}+x+he_{d}
and observe that for
i\in\pi
, since
\|c_{i}-c_{\pi}\|\leq r_{\pi}
we have

\begin{split}\|\alpha(\pi,x,h)-c_{i}\|^{p}&=\|c_{\pi}-c_{i}+x+he_{d}\|^{p}\\
&=|h|^{p}+\|c_{\pi}-c_{i}+x\|^{p}\\
&=|h|^{p}+(\|c_{\pi}-c_{i}\|+\|x\|)^{p}\\
&\leq|h|^{p}+(r_{\pi}+\|x\|)^{p}.\end{split}

We deduce then that

|h|^{p}+(r_{\pi}+\|x\|)^{p}<1\implies\alpha(\pi,x,h)\in\bigcap_{i\in\pi}B(c_{i},1).

(B.3)

Similarly, if
j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi
, since
\|c_{j}-c_{\pi}\|>r_{\pi}+\epsilon
, we have

\begin{split}\|\alpha(\pi,x,h)-c_{i}\|^{p}&=|h|^{p}+\|c_{\pi}-c_{i}+x\|^{p}\\
&\geq|h|^{p}+(\|c_{\pi}-c_{i}\|-\|x\|)_{+}^{p}\\
&>|h|^{p}+(r_{\pi}+\epsilon-\|x\|)_{+}^{p},\end{split}

and thus we deduce that

h^{p}+(r_{\pi}+\epsilon-\|x\|)_{+}^{p}\geq 1\implies\alpha(\pi,x,h)\notin\bigcup_{j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi}B(c_{j},1).

(B.4)

Now set

h_{\pi}\coloneqq\left(1-\left(r_{\pi}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)^{p}\right)^{1/p}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{\pi}\coloneqq\alpha(\pi,0,h_{\pi}).

Note that
h_{\pi}
is well defined as
r_{\pi}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\leq\delta+\epsilon\leq 1
. Let us check that we can choose
\eta>0
sufficiently small independently on the configuration so that
B(\alpha_{\pi},\eta)\subset P_{\pi}
using (
B.3
) and (
B.4
). Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. There exists
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
x\cdot e_{d}=0
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
such that
z=\alpha(\pi,x,h)
. Denote
\ell\coloneqq\|z-\alpha_{h}\|
. When
\ell\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, we have

\begin{split}|h|^{p}+(r_{\pi}+\|x\|)^{p}&=1+|{h_{\pi}}|^{p}-|h|^{p}+(r_{\pi}+\|x\|)^{p}-(r_{\pi}+\epsilon/2)^{p}\\
&\leq 1+|{h_{\pi}}|^{p}-|h|^{p}+\|x\|^{p}-(\epsilon/2)^{p}\\
&\leq 1+p\ell(1+\ell)^{p-1}+\ell^{p}-(\epsilon/2)^{p}\\
&\rightarrow 1-(\epsilon/2)^{p}<1\quad\text{as}\quad\ell\rightarrow 0,\end{split}

(B.5)

where the first inequality comes from the convexity of
y\mapsto y^{p}
, uses that
\|x\|\leq\ell\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, and the second from an application of the mean value Theorem using
|h_{\pi}|\leq 1
. Note that convergence only depends on
\epsilon
, which is fixed and not on the configuration of centers. Similarly, we have
have

\begin{split}|h|^{p}+(r_{\pi}+\epsilon-\|x\|)_{+}^{p}&=1+|{h_{\pi}}|^{p}-|h|^{p}+(r_{\pi}+\epsilon-\|x\|)_{+}^{p}-(r_{\pi}+\epsilon/2)^{p}\\
&\geq 1-\ell^{p}+(r_{\pi}+\epsilon-\|x\|)_{+}^{p}-(r_{\pi}+\epsilon/2)_{+}^{p}\\
&\geq 1-\ell^{p}+(\epsilon-\ell)_{+}^{p}-(\epsilon/2)^{p}\\
&\rightarrow 1+\epsilon^{p}-(\epsilon/2)^{p}>1\quad\text{as}\quad\ell\rightarrow 0,\end{split}

(B.6)

where the first and second inequalities come from the convexity of
y\mapsto y^{p}_{+}
. Here, the last convergence only depends on
\epsilon
. From (
B.5
) and (
B.6
) we deduce that we can choose
\eta=\eta(\epsilon)>0
small enough so that
B(\alpha_{\pi},\eta)\subset P_{\pi}
. Since
\eta
only depends on
\epsilon
, the proof is complete.
∎

B.2
The case
p=\infty

Throughout this subsection, we assume
p=\infty
. The goal is to show Proposition
B.1
. We start with the following lemma, which addresses the case
d=1
.

Lemma B.4
.

Assume
d=1
. Let
(c_{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. There exists a partition
\Pi
of
[\![1,k]\!]
such that for all
\pi\in\Pi
,

|P_{\pi}|\geq\frac{2}{k!}.

In particular, since all
(P_{\pi})_{\pi\in\Pi}
are segments, there exist real points
(\alpha_{\pi})_{\pi\in\Pi}
such that for all
\pi\in\Pi
, we have

B\left(\alpha_{\pi},\frac{1}{k!}\right)\subset P_{\pi}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that the
(c_{i})_{i\in[\![1,k]\!]}
are ordered in a non-decreasing manner. First observe that if
I\subset[\![1,k]\!]
, then
I\neq[\![\min I,\max I]\!]
, then
P_{I}=\emptyset
. Indeed, if
i\in[\![\min I,\max I]\!]
, from the ordering if
x\in B(c_{\min I},1)\cap B(c_{\max I},1)
then
x<c_{\min I}+1\leq c_{i}+1
and
x>c_{\max I}-1\geq c_{i}+1
and thus
x\in B(c_{i},1)
, giving us
P_{I}\subset B(c_{\min I},1)\cap B(c_{\max I},1)\subset B(c_{i},1)
and finally
i\in I
if
P_{I}\neq\emptyset
.

We now construct by induction a sequence
(k_{n})_{1\leq n\leq k}\in{\mathbb{N}^{*}}^{k}
increasing to
k
and a sequence
(\Pi_{n})_{1\leq n\leq k}
such that for all
n\in[\![1,k]\!]
,
\Pi_{n}
is a partition of
[\![1,k_{n}]\!]
and

\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}|P_{\pi}|\geq 2\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k-i}.

(B.7)

For the initialization, since
B(c_{1},1)
has size
2
and is composed of at most
k
non-empty pieces, that are the
P_{[\![1,a]\!]}
for
a\in[\![1,k]\!]
, at least one of them has size bigger than
\frac{2}{k}
. We set then
k_{1}
to be the smallest index in
[\![1,k]\!]
such that

|P_{[\![1,k_{1}]\!]}|\geq\frac{2}{k}.

By construction, the initialization is then verified, letting
\Pi_{1}=\{[\![1,k_{1}]\!]\}
.

Now let
n\in[\![0,k-1]\!]
. If
k_{n}=k
, we set
\Pi_{n+1}\coloneqq\Pi_{n}
,
k_{n+1}=k_{n}
and the heredity is clearly verified. If not, then
n\leq k_{n}<k
and we want to select a range of indices containing
k_{n}+1
to make the heredity work. To do that, let
\pi_{n}
be the element of
\Pi_{n}
containing
k_{n}
and write

\begin{split}\sum_{i=k_{n}+1}^{k}|P_{[\![i_{0}+1,i]\!]}|&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq b\leq k\\
k_{n}+1\in[\![a,b]\!]\end{subarray}}|P_{[\![a,b]\!]}|-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a\leq b\leq k\\
\{k_{n},k_{n}+1\}\subset[\![a,b]\!]\end{subarray}}|P_{[\![a,b]\!]}|\\
&=|B(c_{k_{n}+1},1)|-|B(c_{k_{n}},1)\cap B(c_{k_{n}+1},1)|\\
&=|B(c_{k_{n}},1)|-|B(c_{k_{n}},1)\cap B(c_{k_{n}+1},1)|\\
&=|B(c_{k_{n}+1},1)|-|B(c_{k_{n}},1)\cap B(c_{k_{n}+1},1)|\\
&=|B(c_{k_{n}},1)\setminus B(c_{k_{n}+1},1)|\\
&\geq|P_{\pi_{n}}|\\
&\geq\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}|P_{\pi}|\\
&\geq 2\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k-i}.\end{split}

(B.8)

No observe that the sum in the LHS of (
B.8
) has
k-k_{n}\leq k-n
terms, so one of them is bigger than
\frac{1}{k-n}
of the total sum. Using the inequality, we can thus choose
k_{n+1}
to be the minimal index in
[\![k_{n}+1,k]\!]
such that

|P_{[\![k_{n}+1,k_{n+1}]\!]}|\geq\frac{2}{k-n}\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k-i}=2\prod_{i=0}^{n}\frac{1}{k-i}.

Then letting
\Pi_{n+1}\coloneqq\Pi_{n}\cup\{[\![k_{n}+1,k_{n+1}]\!]\}
, the heredity follows.

To conclude, we set
\Pi=\Pi_{k}
. By construction
\Pi
is a partition of
[\![1,k]\!]
and

\min_{\pi\in\Pi}|P_{\pi}|\geq 2\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{k-i}=\frac{2}{k!}.

∎

Remark B.5
.

In practice, it seems that the optimal constant in Lemma
B.4
is
\frac{2}{k}
but is not reached by the algorithm we propose in the proof.

We now prove the main proposition in the case
p=\infty
.

Proof of Proposition
B.1
in the case
p=\infty
.

The idea is to decompose into the different dimensions. For each
s\in[\![1,d-1]\!]
, we apply Lemma
B.4
to get a partition
\Pi_{s}
of
[\![1,k]\!]
and real numbers
(\alpha_{s,\pi})_{\pi\in\Pi_{s}}
such that for all
\pi\in\Pi_{s}
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

|x-\alpha_{s,\pi}|<\frac{1}{k!}\implies\begin{cases}\forall i\in\pi,\penalty\ |x\cdot e_{s}-c_{i}\cdot e_{s}|<1,\penalty\ \text{and}\\
\forall j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi,\penalty\ |x\cdot e_{s}-c_{j}\cdot e_{s}|\geq 1.\end{cases}

(B.9)

We set

\Pi\coloneqq\left\{\bigcap_{s=1}^{d-1}\pi_{s},\penalty\ (\pi_{s})_{1\leq s\leq d}\in\prod_{s=1}^{d-1}\Pi_{s}\right\}.

Let us check that for each element
\pi
of this partition we can find a center
\alpha_{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
B\left(\alpha_{\pi},\frac{1}{2k!}\right)\subset P_{\pi}
. Let
\pi\in\Pi
. By definition there exists
(\pi_{s})_{1\leq s\leq d}\in\prod_{s=1}^{d-1}\Pi_{s}
such that
\pi=\bigcap_{s=1}^{d-1}\pi_{s}
. Set

\alpha_{\pi}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}e_{d}+\sum_{s=1}^{d-1}\alpha_{s,\pi_{s}}e_{s}.

Now let
x\in B\left(\alpha_{\pi},\frac{1}{2k!}\right)
and
i\in\pi
. By construction, for each
s\in[\![1,d-1]\!]
we have
i\in\pi_{s}
and
|x\cdot e_{s}-\alpha_{\pi}\cdot e_{s}|<\frac{1}{k!}
and thus from (
B.9
),
|c_{i}\cdot e_{s}-x\cdot e_{s}|<1
. We deduce than
\|x-c_{i}\|<1
, that is
x\in B(c_{i},1)
.

Now let
j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi
. By definition, there exists
s\in[\![1,d-1]\!]
such that
j\notin\pi_{s}
. Since
|x\cdot e_{s}-\alpha_{\pi}\cdot e_{s}|<\frac{1}{k!}
we have then from (
B.9
) that
|x\cdot e_{s}-c_{i}j\cdot e_{s}|\geq 1
. This implies
\|x-c_{j}\|\geq 1
, i.e.
x\notin B(c_{j},1)
.

Finally, we have by construction that
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2k!}\geq 0
, hence
x\in\mathbb{H}^{+}(0)
. We just proved that
x\in P_{\pi}
, the proof is complete.
∎

B.3
Conclusion

In this subsection, we use Proposition
B.1
to derive Proposition
4.2
. We start with the following lemma, which will be useful.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
z,c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
, then

B(z,r)\setminus B(c,1)=\emptyset\implies B\left(z,\frac{r}{2}\right)\setminus B\left(c,1+\frac{r}{2}\right)=\emptyset.

Proof.

Let
c\neq x\in B(z,\frac{r}{2})
. Define

x^{\prime}\coloneqq x-\frac{r}{2}\frac{x-c}{\|x-c\|}.

Observe that by the triangle inequality,

\|x^{\prime}-z\|\leq\|x-z\|+\frac{r}{2}<r,

hence
x^{\prime}\in B(z,r)
and thus

1\leq\|x^{\prime}-c\|=\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)\|x-c\|=\|x-c\|-\frac{r}{2}\|x-c\|\leq\|x-c\|-\frac{r}{2}.

Where the last inequality comes from
x\notin B(c,1)
. We deduce then
\|x-c\|\geq 1+\frac{r}{2}
, that is
x\notin B(c,1+\frac{r}{2})
, the proof is complete.
∎

Finally, we prove the proposition.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

From Proposition
B.1
, there exists
\eta>0
such that for every configuration, fixing

R_{0}\coloneqq\frac{4\kappa}{\eta},

we can find a partition
\Pi
of
[\![1,k]\!]
and
(\alpha_{\pi})_{\pi\in\Pi}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}
such that for each
\pi\in\Pi
,

B(\alpha_{\pi},\eta)\subset\bigcap_{i\in\pi}B^{+}\left(\frac{c_{i}}{R_{0}},1\right)\setminus\bigcup_{j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi}B\left(\frac{c_{j}}{R_{0}},1\right).

From Lemma
B.6
since for all
\pi\in\Pi

j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi
we have
B(\alpha_{\pi},\eta)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(\frac{c_{j}}{R_{0}},1)
, then we have
B(\alpha_{\pi},\frac{\eta}{2})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(\frac{c_{j}}{R_{0}},1+\frac{\eta}{2})
. We then deduce that for all
\pi\in\Pi
,

B\left(\alpha_{\pi},\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\subset\bigcap_{i\in\pi}B^{+}\left(\frac{c_{i}}{R_{0}},1\right)\setminus\bigcup_{j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi}B\left(\frac{c_{j}}{R_{0}},1+\frac{\eta}{2}\right).

Now scaling everything by
R_{0}
we deduce for all
\pi\in\Pi
,

B\left(R_{0}\alpha_{\pi},\frac{R_{0}\eta}{2}\right)\subset\bigcap_{i\in\pi}B^{+}\left(c_{i},R_{0}\right)\setminus\bigcup_{j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi}B\left(c_{j},R_{0}+\frac{R_{0}\eta}{2}\right).

(B.10)

Finally observe that from triangular inequality for all
j\in[\![1,k]\!]
,

B(c_{j}-\kappa e_{d},\kappa+R_{0})\subset B(c_{j},R_{0}+2\kappa)=B\left(c_{j},R_{0}+\frac{R_{0}\eta}{2}\right),

where the last equality comes from the definition of
R_{0}
. Finally injecting in (
B.10
) we get

B\left(R_{0}\alpha_{\pi},\frac{R_{0}\eta}{2}\right)\subset\bigcap_{i\in\pi}B^{+}(c_{i},R_{0})\setminus\bigcup_{j\in[\![1,k]\!]\setminus\pi}B(c_{j}-\kappa e_{d},\kappa+R_{0}).

This shows that for all
\pi\in\Pi
, we have

|P^{\kappa,R_{0}}_{\pi}|\geq\left|B\left(R_{0}\alpha_{\pi},\frac{R_{0}\eta}{2}\right)\right|\eqqcolon a_{0}>0.

The proof is complete.
∎
