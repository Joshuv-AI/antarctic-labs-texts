Back to the Future: The Role of Past and Future Context Predictability in Incremental Language Production
Shiva Upadhye and Richard Futrell Department of Language Science University of California, Irvine

Abstract
Contextual predictability shapes both the form and choice of words in online language production. The effects of the predictability of a word given its previous context are generally well-understood in both production and comprehension, but studies of naturalistic production have also revealed a poorly-understood backward predictability effect of a word given its future context, which may be related to future planning. Here, in two studies of naturalistic speech corpora, we investigate backward predictability effects using improved measures and more powerful language models, introducing a new principled and conceptually motivated information-theoretic predictability measure that integrates predictability from both the future and the past context. Our first study revisits classic predictability effects on word duration. Our second study investigates substitution errors within a generative framework that independently models the effects of lexical, contextual, and communicative factors on word choice, while predicting the actual words that surface as speech errors. We find that our proposed conceptually-motivated alternative to backward predictability yields qualitatively similar effects across both studies. Through a fine-grained analysis of substitution errors, we further show that different kinds of errors are suggestive of how speakers prioritize form, meaning, and context-based information during lexical planning. Together, these findings illuminate the functional roles of past and future context in how speakers encode and choose words, offering a bridge between contextual predictability effects and the mechanisms of sentence planning. Keywords : Language Production; Information-Theoretic Linguistics; Probabilistic Reduction; Word Choice; Language Models;

A number of conceptual interpretations of backward predictability effects have been proposed. In the articulatory-motor domain,
104
propose a link between the backward predictability effect and anticipatory motor planning: when the current and following word are highly informative about each other, speakers speed-through the articulation of the current word to initiate motor planning of the upcoming word, resulting in reduced durations. This account is
prospective
in that it attributes (non-)reduction to the availability of upcoming material. In contrast,
7
put forth a
retrospective
explanation of this effect: predictability—whether unigram, forward or backward—facilitates retrieval of the current word, leading to reduced buffering and smoother coordination between planning and articulation. Another interpretation of this quantity comes from
65
, who treat the predictability of the preceding sequence conditioned on the present word as form of
reactivation
. Once reactivated (through repetition), the preceding sequence serves as a cue that facilitates retrieval of the upcoming word.

One barrier to finding a clear interpretation of the backward predictability effect is that we do not yet have the full empirical picture of the effects of predictability on word duration and choice. A maxjor limitation of previous work is that the commonly-used measure of backward predictability assumes implicitly that the future context is produced
independently
of the previously produced or past context. That is, although the measure captures the predictive effects of future material on the present word, it disregards the specific context in which the speaker produces that future material. Crucially, this assumption is inconsistent with theoretical proposals in sentence production. First, the notions of
past
and
future
may not be intrinsic to how speakers plan their utterance but rather to how they produce it. That is, they arise due to serialization, where an order-agnostic representation of the utterance is transformed into a linear sequence according to the typological constraints imposed by the language
(

. This means that both the past and future context may, in turn, reflect the influence of more abstract representations of the speaker’s message
(
12
;
40
)
.

Moreover, cross-linguistic evidence suggests that lexical planning can proceed

However, since backward predictability measures only the predictability of the current word given the future context and disregards the past context altogether, it precludes the possibility of any such direct associations between words in the past and future contexts. Beyond this conceptual limitation, the interpretation of backward predictability in regression models that also include forward predictability is complicated by the fact that the two probabilistic variables are highly collinear
(
7
)
, which obfuscates the distinct effects of past and future context.

Here, we give a more complete characterization of the predictive effects of past and future context on speaker choices in naturalistic production. Conceptually, we argue that backward predictability reflects one particular operationalization of future context predictability, one that assumes independent effects of the past and future contexts. We propose an alternate formulation of future context effects based on the
Conditional Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI)

(
33
)
of the current word and future after extracting information from the past context—a measure that avoids the independence assumption implicit in backward predictability. Methodologically, we address concerns about collinearity and language modeling to improve the estimation of contextual predictability measures.

Empirically, this work is grounded in two case studies that illuminate the nuanced effects of contextual predictability on lexical planning. Our first study revisits
probabilistic reduction
as a testbed for evaluating the improved contextual measures, including our proposed conditional PMI-based formulation of future context predictability. Our second study probes the effects of context on word choice by independently modeling how lexical availability, communicative alignment, and past- and future-context predictability shape the content of lexical substitutions in naturalistic productions.

Our findings have implications for accounts of probabilistic reduction and incremental planning in sentence production. First, we find that our principled conditional PMI-based measure qualitatively replicates the effects of backward predictability in predicting word durations and word choice. Our first study, which focuses on probabilistic reduction, does not reproduce the asymmetric effects of context on function and content words observed by
7
. Instead, we find that lexical and contextual predictability from either direction predict reduction for both content and function words, although function word durations exhibit reduced sensitivity to predictability. Finally, our model of substitution errors broadens the empirical scope of research on trade-offs in word choice, which has so far been limited to single-word production. This analysis reveals distinct functional pressures imposed by past and future context, which correspond to goal-invariant (availability-based) and goal-directed mechanisms in sentence planning.

The goal of the present study is to estimate the distinct contributions of contextual predictability from the past and future contexts on articulatory duration and word choice in naturalistic production. For the speaker, when future plans are entirely unavailable, the current word may be predicted solely from its past context (Figure
3

p(w_{t}\mid C_{<t})

(1)

where

is the previously produced or past context. Backward predictability provides an analog of this effect in the reversed direction:

p(w_{t}\mid C_{>t})

(2)

where
C_{>t}=w_{t+1}\cdots w_{t}
is an upcoming sequence of words. This measure can be seen as quantifying the predictability of the current word under a future sequence, for a speaker who has more or less fully planned the future context as it will be produced.

As in some previous work (e.g.,
104
), backward associations between the current word and the future word or sequence can also be modeled using (unconditional) Pointwise Mutual Information or PMI:

\textrm{PMI}(w_{t};C_{>t})=\log p(w_{t}\mid C_{>t})-\log p(w_{t})

(3)

where
\textrm{PMI}(w_{t};C_{>t})
measures the strength of association between the word
w_{t}
and the future
C_{>t}
after accounting for how frequently they occur independently. Thus, a positive value of
\texttt{PMI}(w_{t};C_{>t})
indicates that the word and the future sequence are more likely co-occur than would be predicted by chance.

However, as predictors of planning difficulty, backward predictability or
\textrm{PMI}(w_{t};C_{>t})
are limited in one crucial aspect: they treat the speaker’s current state—i.e. the context produced so far—as irrelevant to the speaker’s future plans. Formally, these measures model the conditional dependence of the current word (
w_{t}
) on the future context (
C_{>t}
), but disregard the past (
C_{<t}
). For the speaker, both the observed past and planned future are shaped by a conceptual representation of their intended message (

However, backward predictability neglects this shared dependence on

and treats future context as a completely independent source of predictability from past context (Figure
3
b). Therefore, without explicitly accounting for the joint effects of
both
the observed past context and the planned future context, predictability from the future may over- or underestimate how informative that future is about the current word, given the speaker’s knowledge of what they have produced so far (Figure
3

2.1
Quantifying Future Context Predictability

In this work, we relax the assumption that past and future contexts have independent effects. With this goal in mind, we propose a principled alternative to backward predictability, based on the
conditional PMI
of the current word
w_{t}
and the future context
C_{>t}
given the past context
C_{<t}
:

\displaystyle\textrm{conditional PMI}(w_{t};\ C_{>t}\mid C_{<t})

\displaystyle=\log\frac{p(w_{t}\mid C_{>t},C_{<t})}{p(w_{t}\mid C_{<t})}

(4)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\log p(w_{t}\mid C_{>t},C_{<t})}_{\textrm{Bidirectional word probability}}-\underbrace{\log p(w_{t}\mid C_{<t})}_{\textrm{Forward word probability}}

(5)

The bidirectional probability term in Eq.
5
quantifies how both past and future context jointly predict the current word, thereby preserving associations between non-adjacent words within the utterance. In related work,
133
showed that bidirectional context shared higher mutual information with word duration than past context alone; however, their study did not isolate the informativity between future context and word duration (among other prosodic features) above and beyond the past context. In Eq.
5
, the difference between the log-transformed bidirectional and forward probability quantifies how informative the current word and the future sequence are about each other once the past context has already constrained the speaker’s expectations. In comparison, backward predictability provides an estimate of this effect that is
context-independent
across all possible past sequences that the speaker could have produced
3
3

3

Put differently, backward predictability can be viewed as analogous to the bidirectional probability in Eq.
5
but with the past context marginalized out i.e.,
\sum\limits_{c}p(w\mid C_{>t},C_{<t}=c)
.

A positive value of conditional PMI suggests that the current word and planned future sequence are informative about each other
beyond
what can be predicted by the past context. For instance, consider the following utterances:

Since PMI is symmetric, it does not commit to whether the future sequence influences the choice of the current word or vice versa. A positive value of conditional PMI is, therefore, compatible with (i) a
retrospective
interpretation, i.e. the planned future sequence is highly informative about word choice at the current time-step, or (ii) a
prospective
interpretation, i.e. the choice of the current word facilitates production of upcoming material.

This latter interpretation is broadly aligned with the concept of
value-to-go

(
121
;
123
)
in action planning, optimal control, and reinforcement learning. Value-to-go is the total expected value of taking an action, consisting of the immediate utility of the action itself and the expected value of future actions after it. In theories of optimal planning, an agent chooses actions to maximize this value-to-go function. As applied to language production, value-to-go includes predictability as a factor—more predictable actions or words are higher value because they incur less cognitive cost, and can be produced in a more routinized way
(
51
;
53
;
81
;

)
. Therefore, an optimal planning agent will tend to produce a word
w_{t}
that increases the predictability of a high-value
future
sequence
C_{>t}
, as measured by the surprisal

-\log p(C_{>t}\mid w_{t},C_{<t}).

(6)

Instead of comparing conditional PMI to backward predictability directly, we evaluate it against an alternative that is decorrelated from forward predictability. As noted above, backward predictability may confound the predictive effect of the future context with the joint predictability from the past and future. To enable a principled comparison between backward predictability and conditional PMI vis-à-vis other variables such as frequency and forward predictability, we propose the following decorrelated alternative to backward predictability
4
4

4

A commonly used alternative method for de-correlating predictors is
residualization
—a process that involves regressing the variable of interest on a correlated variable and using the residuals or prediction errors in lieu of the target variable. While residualization can effectively de-correlate predictors by removing covariance between these variables, this process may alter the interpretability of the construct represented by the target variable
(
14
;
134
)
. In other words, a residualized variant of backward predictability may address the problem of collinearity, but may render this effect less cognitively interpretable. See also

:

\textrm{Relative Backward Predictability}=\underbrace{\log p(w_{t}\mid C_{>t})}_{\textrm{Backward Conditional Probability}}-\underbrace{\log p(w_{t}\mid C_{<t})}_{\textrm{Forward Conditional Probability}}

(7)

Both relative backward predictability and conditional PMI extract out the predictive effect of past context, but differ in that conditional PMI does not assume that past and future are independent sources of predictability. From an information-theoretic perspective, relative backward predictability can be viewed as a log-likelihood ratio that represents how much more informative the upcoming sequence is about the current word
compared
to the past context. A positive value of relative backward predictability reflects that the future context outperforms the past in terms of predicting the current word while a negative value suggests the opposite.

It should be noted that relative backward predictability can be viewed as an
asymmetric
formulation of future context predictability since it assumes that it is the future context that influences the current word. By contrast, conditional PMI is
symmetric
: it measures the association between the current word and future sequence given past context, but is agnostic about the direction of influence between the current word and the future context. For a full comparison, we can also consider the unconditional PMI in Eq.
3
, which is an alternative symmetric formulation of future context predictability that, like relative backward predictability, assumes independent effects of past and future. We discuss effects of unconditional PMI relative to other formulations of future context predictability in Appendix
E.1
.

3
Study 1: Revisiting Predictability Effects on Word Durations

Since much of our understanding of the backward predictability effect comes from research on probabilistic reduction, this study revisits models of word duration in spontaneous speech using probabilistic variables such as unigram and forward predictability alongside our proposed measures of future context predictability. The aims of this study are two-fold. First, we seek to conduct a controlled comparison of relative backward predictability and conditional PMI to examine how the assumption of (in)dependence between the past and future context affects the magnitude of the future context predictability effect. The secondary aim of this study is to re-examine the effects of contextual predictability and lexical class on articulatory reduction, given that previous work has revealed inconsistent effects of forward and backward predictability on the duration of function versus content words (
7

cf.

106
).

3.3
Discussion

The key findings of this study can be summarized as follows. First, we find that both relative backward predictability and conditional PMI show qualitatively similar behavior: both measures exhibit an inverse relationship with word duration in line with the previously observed backward predictability effect. However, the two measures of future context predictability diverge quantitatively. Our analysis revealed that relative backward predictability provided a better fit to word durations than conditional PMI. This was corroborated by a further observation that in the model with both measures, relative backward predictability emerges as the strongest contextual predictor of word duration, followed by forward predictability and conditional PMI. Despite this, a model of word durations with both relative backward predictability and conditional PMI showed a significant improvement in goodness of fit, which suggests that conditional PMI and relative backward predictability are not redundant predictors. That is, conditional PMI contributes complementary information beyond what is accounted for by relative backward predictability. This result indicates that word duration is influenced to some extent by the predictability of the word given both the past and the future
jointly
, beyond what could be explained by predictability from the past and future separately. We revisit the discussion about these two measures in Sections
4.4
and
5
.

A second key finding of this study concerns the function-content asymmetry in probabilistic reduction. Notably,
7
observed that the effects of forward predictability on articulatory duration were limited to high-frequency function words whereas backward predictability emerged as the strongest contextual predictor of content word durations. Our results did not replicate these differential effects of past and future context predictability on the reduction of function versus content word duration. Instead, we find that predictability from either direction was negatively correlated with reduction in both function and content words. However, the effect of lexical class was borne out in the sensitivity to predictability, with content words showing stronger reduction regardless of the source of this predictability i.e., unigram, past, or future. Among the probabilistic variables, unigram predictability and predictability from the future (i.e., relative backward predictability and conditional PMI) showed stronger effects on content word durations than predictability from the past, with conditional PMI specifically demonstrating the least sensitivity to function words. We discuss the theoretical implications of these results in Section
5
.

Our analysis of contextual predictability effects on word durations differs from
7
along two key dimensions: the language model and the corresponding size of the context window. In their analysis, context windows were restricted to the preceding or following word, and forward and backward predictability were estimated using count-based bigram models. Furthermore,
7
reported no significant effects of larger context windows on duration. In comparison, our study utilized the representation-based GPT-2 LM capable of attending to or extracting information from context windows up to 1024 tokens. Other recent studies employing modern LMs such as LSTM or transformers have similarly observed significant effects of forward predictability or surprisal on content word durations
(
106
;
20
)
. In line with this recent work and our current findings, we conclude that the absence of an effect of past context on content word durations and of future context on function word durations, as reported by
7
, likely reflects limitations of the language modeling performance of
n
-gram models.

4
Study 2: Modeling Substitution Errors in Naturalistic Productions

The above study presents a controlled comparison between two alternative operationalizations of future context predictability, both of which exhibit qualitatively similar behavior on probabilistic reduction. While we did not observe asymmetric effects of past and future context predictability on the duration of function versus content words, word durations were generally more sensitive to predictability from the future than from the past. This observation is consistent with broader findings that backward predictability emerges as the strongest contextual predictor of other planning difficulties such as filled pauses and repetitions
(
118
;
22
;
23
;
65
)
.

However, modeling reduction or disfluency may not be the most suitable paradigm for investigating the distinct influences of past and future context on lexical planning. These phenomena primarily index the ease of cognitive processing during production, with (un)predictability from either direction correlating with an increase in planning difficulty. Therefore, they offer a limited window into the sentence planning mechanisms by which context influences speaker choices during lexical planning. For example, consider the following utterances from the Switchboard corpus:

7.

becoming

chronic illness and and things like that

your sick leave. And take your sick leave

) illustrate the distinct pressures imposed by past and future context on word choice. Whereas the preceding context may steer the speaker into selecting
becoming
in (

accumulate

Prior work has shown that lexical availability and semantic alignment can similarly exert competing influences on word choice, leading to mis-selections or
good-enough choices

(
107
;
38
;
79
;
58
)
. Because speakers operate under information processing constraints, lexical selection may reflect a trade-off between automatic or
goal-invariant
strands of processing that prioritize
economy of effort
and controlled or
goal-directed
processing that aims to satisfy the speaker’s communicative intent
(
9
;
39
;
66
;
47
)
. Through independent manipulation of availability and alignment,
79
demonstrated evidence of this trade-off in a gamified word production paradigm: speakers often preferred highly frequent but semantically imprecise alternatives over infrequent yet semantically precise choices to describe a production target.

Beyond word production, previous work has shown that highly incremental (i.e., left-to-right) planning is heavily influenced by conceptual and lexical accessibility
(
11
;
92
;
62
;
56
;
25
;
69
;

. This preference for efficiency is reflected in the
principle of immediate mention

(
37

, that is, speakers choose to prioritize the overt production of highly available lexical representations to routinely free-up working memory resources
(
37
;
35
;
120
;
18

. Under this accessibility-driven strategy, the previously produced context may steer the speaker toward highly predictable lexical choices, potentially at the cost of alignment with desired semantics or upcoming context. In contrast, advance planning of the upcoming sequence may impose greater cognitive demands on the speaker since lexical representations need to be planned in advance and sustained in working memory for much longer
(
127
;
82
;
95
)
.

Therefore, one possible interpretation that we seek to explore in this study is that past and future context exert competing influences of word choice, reflecting aspects of goal-invariant and goal-directed aspects of processing, respectively. Previously, the study of trade-offs in word choice has been restricted to single-word production since it enables precise manipulation of availability and alignment, but neglects the role of context. In this study, we focus on modeling production choice in naturalistic substitution contexts such as (
7.
)-(

(

)
and the effects of contextual predictability on the
occurrence
of substitution disfluencies
(
22
)
, our proposed paradigm aims to predict the
content

7.

9.

Finally, we apply a syntactic category constraint, which restricts our set of utterances to those where the part of speech of the reparandum matched that of the self-repair. The selected utterances were then processed into utterance frames consisting of the context preceding the error (
C_{<t}
) and the context following the error (
C_{>t}

N

4.1.2
Model

We predict substitution choices within a framework that models the effects of lexical availability, contextual predictability, and communicative alignment on the choice of what word to say next. Specifically, we aim to predict which word the speaker actually produced at the substitution position
t
in the utterance context, framing this task as a logistic regression model that predicts the positive class for the observed error and the negative class for all other alternatives. In addition to unigram, past, and future context predictability, we also consider the
communicative reward
of choosing a given word, defined as its proximity to the self-repair or intended target
w^{*}_{t}
along both semantic and phonological dimensions. Therefore, variables in this model are functions of a possible word
w_{t}
, the entire utterance context
C
, and the intended target
w^{*}_{t}
.

Operationalizing communicative reward

Although the observed self-repair provides an approximation of the speaker’s communicative intent, several lexical representations may be compatible with the intended semantics. Moreover, speakers may trade-off semantic alignment in favor of lexical availability because of interactions between semantic and phonological processing in the production system, particularly when multiple competing forms fall within the ballpark of the target semantics
(
26
;
21
;
126
;
38
;
79
)
. Beyond this uncertainty at the level of lemma representations,
Tip-of-the-Tongue (ToT)
phenomena highlight that speakers may experience difficulty in accessing the phonological form of the word even when they have access to the precise semantic and syntactic properties of the lexical representation
(
15
;
77
)
. Realistically, speakers may have uncertainty about both the target semantics and phonological form during online production. To simulate this uncertainty, we generate “noisy” semantic and phonetic representations of the self-repair
\hat{w}_{t}
, which we treat as the intended production target. We then define communicative success (or reward) of a word
w_{i}
as the semantic and phonetic distance between this word and the noisy representation of the production target
\hat{w}_{t}
.

Semantic representations were estimated by mapping words to real-valued word embedding vectors. We derive these vectors by fitting
fasttext
embeddings
(
13
)
to the CANDOR corpus, which yield 100-dimensional semantic vector representations. To generate a noisy semantic representation of the target, we inject Gaussian noise into the target word vector as follows:

\hat{\textbf{w}}_{t}=\textbf{w}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(8)

where
\textbf{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{100}
is the vectorized representation of the target and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.1)\in\mathbb{R}^{100}
is the noise vector. We then operationalize the semantic distance as the cosine distance between the noisy target vector
\hat{\textbf{w}}_{t}
and a given word
\textbf{w}_{i}
.

We generate a “noisy” phonetic representation of the target by uniformly sampling phonemes from the IPA form of the target word, and for each selected phoneme, uniformly sampling a phonetic feature to modify. For each chosen feature, we then randomly sample an alternative categorical value to add uncertainty to the target phonetic representation (see Appendix
F
for an algorithmic implementation of this process). Finally, phonetic distance was operationalized as the feature-wise distance between the phonetic representation of a given word
w_{i}
and the noisy phonetic representation of the target
\hat{w}_{t}
. We use the
panphon
package in Python
(
98
)
to generate categorical phonetic feature representations of words and to compute the feature-based edit distance between the phonetic representation of a given word and the noisy target. See Figure
7
for an example of how these processes generate noisy semantic and phonetic representations of the production target.

Produced

Log Unigram Predictability + Log Forward predictability + (Noisy) Semantic Distance + (Noisy) Phonetic Distance

where
w_{i}
is a word in the vocabulary and
Produced

In the modeling presented above, we collapse the distinction between different categories of lexical substitution errors. (

;

(

;
21
)
while purely phonological errors arise at the level of phonological or articulatory planning
(

;

;

)
.

4.4
Discussion

The above analysis aims to explicate the distinct contributions of various lexical, contextual, and similarity-based factors that affect word choice in naturalistic substitution contexts. Across all categories of substitution errors, we observe a strong frequency effect consistent with prior work
(
28
;
118
;
75
)
: higher frequency words were more likely to surface as errors than less frequent targets, which speakers subsequently produced as repairs. In the case of phonologically-related substitutions, frequency or unigram predictability emerged as the sole factor in determining word choice whereas for morphosyntactic substitutions, there was an additional effect of predictability from the past. These findings are consistent with attested
availability-based
explanations of production choices
(
11
;
37
)
: substitution errors reflect instances where speakers initially mis-select contextually inaccurate yet more easily accessible phonological competitors or morphological forms before overriding these choices with the repair that is closer to the target form.

In contrast, mixed and semantic errors exhibit evidence of a more subtle trade-off between production effort and communicative accuracy
(
38
;
79
;
58
)
. Similar to form-based errors, mixed errors were characterized by facilitative effects of frequency and past predictability and constraining effects of semantic and phonetic distances. In other words, mixed errors were not only more available to the speaker than the alternatives, but also closer to the target along both semantic and phonetic dimensions, unlike purely form-based errors. These effects were qualitatively replicated in the case of semantic errors along with an additional inverse effect of predictability from the future: words that were more predictable or informative about the speaker’s future plans were less likely to surface as errors compared to the target and other alternatives. Therefore, beyond availability-based and alignment-based pressures on word choice, semantic substitutions may reflect insufficient or suboptimal planning of future context.

Our analysis also revealed qualitatively similar effects of relative backward predictability and conditional PMI across all error categories, although the two measures did not emerge as significant predictors of word choice for phonologically-related, mixed, and morphosyntactic errors. In the case of semantic substitutions, both measures of future context predictability emerged as stronger contextual predictors of word choice than forward predictability. Unlike Study 1, we find that the substitution model with conditional PMI outperforms the model with relative backward predictability. Furthermore, including both predictors in the model did not yield improvements in the model’s explanatory power, which suggests that the contribution of relative backward predictability was redundant once conditional PMI was accounted for in the model. A potential explanation for this difference in the explanatory power of the two predictors is that lexical planning is more likely to recruit the richer contextual representation captured by conditional PMI, which captures how informative the future context is about the present word under the constraints of the observed past context. Consequently, adding relative backward predictability—which ignores the specific past context in which both the future context and the current word are planned—may not contribute additional explanatory value for lexical planning once the more contextualized conditional PMI is taken into account.

General Discussion

Online production is highly, but not strictly, incremental, as the order in which constituents are retrieved or planned may not reflect the order in which they are produced
(
85
;
12
;
41
;
113
;
82
;
95
)
. Consequently, speaker choices are shaped not only by predictability from previously produced or past context, but also by planned future context. Prior work on predictive effects of future context have operationalized this effect as backward predictability—the conditional probability of the current word given some future context. However, much of our current understanding of backward predictability effects comes from corpus studies that involving weaker
N
-gram and LSTM models for estimating this quantity
(
8
;
7
;
23
;
65
;
106
)
. Intriguingly, many of these studies have noted stronger effects of backward predictability on symptoms of production difficulty, such as elongated pronunciation and disfluencies. However, the interpretation of backward predictability both as a probabilistic variable and a cognitive construct has been complicated by a few factors. First, backward predictability is often highly correlated with forward predictability, which makes it difficult to disentengle their distinct contributions on speaker choices. More importantly, backward predictability captures the predictive effects of future context on the present word, but ignores the context in which both of them occur. Therefore, it can be viewed as a coarser aggregate or de-contextualized estimate of the effect that future plans may have on the speaker’s current choices.

This work presents an examination of contextual predictability effects, both from the past and future, on two aspects of speaker choice: articulatory encoding and word choice. In addition to clarifying the distinct contributions of past and future context on how speakers choose and encode words, our work addresses the aforementioned limitations with current ways of estimating the predictive effects of future context. First, we present two variants of backward predictability that address issues of high correlation with forward predictability: relative backward predictability, defined as the contribution of backward predictability relative to forward predictability, and conditional PMI, which implicitly controls for the information provided by the past context. Furthermore, conditional PMI offers a measure of future context predictability that takes into consideration the (past) context in which speaker’s future and the current choices are planned.

5.1
Effects of Predictability on Wordform Encoding

Our first study focused on the effects of predictability on articulatory encoding in naturalistic speech. Previous work has established a robust inverse relationship between predictability and variation in wordform encoding: predictability from either direction was associated with a reduction in phonetic, acoustic, and articulatory detail. Predictability from the future has been observed to have a stronger effect on duration than predictability from the past, although previous work has shown that this effect is modulated by lexical category (
7

cf.

106
). In our first study, we find that both proposed variants of backward predictability—relative backward predictability and conditional PMI—not only demonstrate this inverse relationship but also emerge as stronger predictors of word duration than forward predictability, validating the previous result. Furthermore, the effect of conditional PMI on word durations (even in a model that also includes relative backward predictability) demonstrates an effect of predictability from the past and future context jointly, which has not been demonstrated before to our knowledge.

Previously,
7
reported asymmetric effects of frequency and contextual predictability on articulatory reduction: whereas frequency and backward predictability emerged as significant predictors of content word duration, forward predictability only affected the duration of high-frequency function words. One possible explanation for the lack of a forward predictability effect on content word durations put forth by Bell and colleagues was that preceding words may be less informative about word choice than following words. Crucially, Bell and colleagues reported no effects of contexts larger than the preceding or following word.

However, a possible explanation for the stronger influence of future context predictability on word durations is that this effect may capture two sources of difficulty: the predictability of word given some representation of the future sequence, but also the availability of the future representation itself.

5.2
The Role of Context in Lexical (Mis-)Selection

The core claim of speaker-oriented accounts of probabilistic reduction is that predictability facilitates lexical access, which, in turn, leads to reduced effort in the phonetic and articulatory encoding of the word. Yet the effects of contextual predictability on the
content
of speaker choices remain underexplored in naturalistic speech, owing to the difficulty of inferring the intended lexical meaning that the speakers aim to convey through their choice of lexical representation. In our second study, we examined the effects of form-based, lexico-semantic, and contextual factors on word choice in substitution contexts, where the speaker’s self-repair serves as an approximation of their production target.

A large body of psycholinguistic research has linked the different categories of speech errors to different loci in the production system. Indeed, lexical production is generally assumed to be a multistage process comprising two broad levels of representation: the lemma, which encodes lexico-semantic and syntactic features of a word and its corresponding phonological form (
49
;
77
;
110
;
26

cf
.
101
). A further point of contention even within staged proposals of lexical selection is whether frequency or lexical-level availability effects operate solely at the level of the phonological form
(
71
)
or are shared across both form and lemma representations
(
28
;
48
)
.

We explicate our findings within a view of lexical selection that is agnostic to assumptions about the nature of lexical representation and stages (or lack thereof) in processing. Instead, we view the lexical (mis-)selections in substitution errors as resulting from competition between form-, meaning-, and context-based information. While self-repairs may reflect a more optimal prioritization of these information sources, substitution errors reveal suboptimal weighting of these cues under intrinsic constraints on information processing
(
9
;
39
;
47
)
.

The different types of errors provide a diagnostic of the specific cue-weighting failures that occurred during lexical production. Across the four types examined in our study, lexical frequency emerged as a consistent predictor of substitution choice, with purely form-based or phonologically-related errors, in particular, exhibiting no influence of meaning- or context-based information. In other words, mis-selecting a purely phonological competitor may reflect a lapse in attending both to information beyond the current word and to the speaker’s intended lexical meaning, therefore reflecting a strong availability-based production preference.

In comparison to phonologically-related errors, which emerge from disproportionately prioritizing form-based similarity over other cues, morphosyntactic errors reflect correct selection of the underlying lemma but incorrect selection of its morphosyntactic form. For these errors, we observe not only a frequency effect, but also an effect of forward predictability. This suggests that relative to phonologically-related errors, morphosyntactic errors integrate non-local contextual information, albeit only from past context. Similar to lexical frequency, predictability from the past may prime certain lexical choices, even if they are incompatible with the speaker’s communicative goal or future plans. Therefore, both frequency and predictability from the past may be indicative of processing that skews toward goal-invariance since it relies on more automatic or experience-based processing. Whereas phonological errors may reflect inattention to both meaning and context-based cues, morphosyntactic errors appear to integrate at goal-invariant contextual information.

The balance between goal-invariant or goal-driven processing is captured by the idea of
Good-Enough (GN)
choices in production
(
38
;
58
)
. Previously,
79
demonstrated a trade-off between message alignment and lexical accessibility in word choice: given a speed-accuracy trade-off and multiple possible alternatives to describe a production target, speakers produced the less precise but more frequently experienced alternatives over the more precise yet less accessible choice. Our study builds on this work in two important ways. Whereas
79
contrasted the effects of lexical frequency and semantic alignment in a controlled single-word production paradigm, our study extends the scope to word choice in naturalistic utterances, which enables us to examine the role of context in word choice errors. Second, we adopt a comprehender-oriented notion of communicative reward, which encompasses both semantic alignment and proximity to the phonological form of the target. The latter stems from the assumption that comprehenders can infer the intended form through rational inference
(
54
;
111
)
, particularly in the case of errors that share characteristics of the form with the target (e.g., phonologically-related and morphosyntactic errors). In other words, errors that are more likely to be correctly inferred by the comprehender may be less costly, and therefore, more communicatively robust compared to those that the interlocutor may find difficult to correct.

In our analysis, mixed errors exhibited a good-enough production preference: words that emerged as intrusions were frequent and predictable from the past context while also falling within the semantic and phonological ballpark of the target. However, the lack of evidence for an effect of future context predictability leaves open the possibility that these choices may be unconstrained by the speaker’s future plans. In contrast, semantic substitutions not only exhibited this trade-off between lexical accessibility and communicative reward, but also a strong inhibitory effect of future context predictability. That is, words that were informative about the speaker’s future plans were less likely to be produced
in error
. Thus, we propose that semantic substitutions arise, not from a failure to incorporate any information from future context, but from insufficient or sub-optimal integration of this information. While speakers may plan some upcoming material in advance
(
82
;
95
;
99
)
, actively maintaining pre-planned representations imposes a further burden on verbal working memory
(
37
;
127
;
120
;
18
)
. Consequently, speakers may generate a sparse or under-determined plan of the future sequence, which may not sufficiently inhibit the production of the otherwise ‘good-enough’ substitution word.

While our account does not commit to the assumption of discrete representational levels in production, our findings remain compatible with multi-stage theories that map different categories of word choice errors to distinct processing stages in the time-course of lexical production. Although multi-stage proposals differ on several key details, a few assumptions have received substantial empirical support. First, lexico-semantic errors occur due to incorrect lemma selection—a process that notably recruits goal-directed cognitive control
(
39
;
109
;
124
)
. Furthermore, morphosyntactic errors have been mapped to the subsequent
positional
processing, wherein the selected lemma undergoes morphological encoding
(
49
;
12
;
40
)
. Mixed errors further reflect the interaction between form-availability and lexical semantics which may bias the speaker to mis-select phonological competitors of the target lemma
(
26
;
21
;
107
;
38
)
. Finally, purely form-based errors have been attributed to latter stages of production processing, which are assumed to be highly automated and outside of conscious cognitive control
(
39
;
16
;
75
)
.

Our study replicates this hierarchy within a functional, computational-level
(
91
)
view of word-choice errors, which frames mis-selections in terms of the information processing within the production system. More broadly, this framing aligns with a
resource-rational
view of choice behavior, according to which
agents operate under intrinsic cognitive capacity limitations that effectively constrain the information processing that may be used to select the utility-maximizing choice

(

)
. A
rational
response to these constraints is that agents are incentivized to arbitrate between easy-first, heuristic, or automatic, and effortful, goal-driven, or controlled modes of processing, which can lead to choices that reflect a trade-off between utility and information processing costs
(

5.3
Relationship between Contextual Predictability and Theories of Sentence Planning

Finally, we discuss the link between contextual predictability and theories of planning in sentence production. It is widely assumed that sentence production proceeds through conceptual, functional, positional, and articulatory-motor stages of processing, with ‘planning’ unfolding at each of these levels of representation
(
12
;
40
;
68
)
. Under this framework, planning at the conceptual stage involves selecting the relevant semantic, relational, and communicative features of the pre-verbal message; at the functional level, it encompasses both lexical selection and hierarchical structure-building; at the positional level, planning involves involves morphological encoding and serialization; and at the articulatory-motor level, it involves generating and executing the phonetic plan of the speaker’s utterance. These planning processes are assumed to proceed sequentially yet incrementally. As discussed in the preceding section, incrementality in language production emerges as a response to both cognitive constraints and conversational pressures that limit the extent to which speakers can plan their utterance prior to the onset of production (
19
;
76
;
85
;
41
;
37
;
35
;
130
;
86

inter alia
).

However, an outstanding issue in theories of sentence production concerns not just the scope, but also the order in which constituents are planned. A
strictly linear
view of sentence planning maintains that the order of planning roughly mirrors the order in which words appear in the surface form of the utterance
(
76
;
85
;
24
;
62
;
56
;
69
;
18
)
. This strategy exemplifies an
economy of effort
principle in planning: by prioritizing the retrieval and encoding of words that are accessible
8
8

8

As operationalized by various correlates of accessibility such as givenness, imageability, animacy, and subjecthood

(
10
;
92
;
62
;
56
)
, and minimizing the lag between retrieval and production to the extent permissible by word order constraints, speakers can leverage the processing benefits offered by accessibility while reducing demands on working memory
(
37
;
120
;
18
)
.

In comparison, a
hierarchical
view of sentence planning underscores the role of structural relations in determining the order in which words are retrieved, with verbs occupying a privileged status in planning due to their central role in specifying clause structure
(
12
;
36
;
113
;
95
)
. Under a strong version of this view, words that are linked in a dependency may be retrieved concurrently, regardless of their respective positions in the surface form of the utterance. However, cross-linguistic empirical evidence favors a weaker version of this hypothesis: speakers appear to flexibly arbitrate between highly sequential and hierarchical or look-ahead planning strategies depending on linguistic and meta-linguistic factors such as argument structure, head directionality, word order constraints, and working memory constraints
(
113
;
82
;
129
;
96
;
97
;
95
;
99
;
78
)
.

Although the present study does not account for how speakers arbitrate between strictly linear and hierarchical planning or generate predictions about the order in which words are planned, these two strategies provide a rationale for the observed effects of past and future context predictability on both word duration and word choice. Strictly linear planning can be viewed as analogous to incremental prediction, wherein the production of previous words facilitates access of contextually predictable continuations, reflecting an accessibility-driven strategy. Hierarchical planning, on the other hand, provides a motivation for the early availability of future material. Since both the scope and content of a speaker’s future plans appear to be sensitive to cognitive load, this planning strategy can be viewed as reflecting more effortful or goal-oriented planning. From a resource-rational perspective, this interleaving of planning strategies is consistent with our findings that past and future context predictability appear to reflect goal-invariant and goal-directed pressures on lexical choice, respectively.

Moreover, this flexibility in planning also provides further motivation for our proposed measure of future context predictability based on conditional PMI
(w_{t},C_{>t}\mid C_{<t})
. Although backward predictability quantifies the facilitative effect of future context on the present word, this predictive effect is not situated in the context of what the speaker has already produced. Furthermore, by ignoring any direct information-processing dependencies between words in the past and future contexts, the measure assumes that the future context depends only on the current word. An implication of this assumption that is of relevance to theories of sentence planning is that it assumes that words in the future are planned strictly linearly. By preserving dependencies between past and future contexts and providing a contextualized measure of informativity between the speaker’s future plans and the current word, conditional PMI can serve as a more interpretable probabilistic variable for modeling the effects of non-linear or advance planning.

Moreoever, conditional PMI is a
symmetric
quantity that measures how informative the current word and the future context are about each other in the context of past sequence. In earlier discussion, we focused on a backward-looking interpretation, where conditional PMI captures the constraining effect of an already planned future sequence on present choice. However, this quantity is also compatible with a forward-looking or prospective interpretation of planning such as
forward simulation
, which aligns with how planning is widely conceptualized in action planning, optimal control, and reinforcement learning. Forward simulation, broadly construed, involves generating possible future trajectories of actions according to the agent’s policy, conditioned on both the present state (which reflects the outcomes of past actions) and an action sampled at the current time step
(
121
)
. Action selection at the current time-step, therefore, is guided by the
future value
of a candidate action i.e., the expected return aggregated over the simulated future trajectories
(
123
)
. In other words, agents choose the current action with the objective of facilitating a future trajectory that maximizes expected utility or communicative value. In the context of sentence planning, this prospective interpretation of conditional PMI can be viewed as speakers choosing words that are informative and facilitate production of any upcoming material. Since the present study assumes the entire future sequence as a single deterministic chunk, our findings are compatible with both interpretations. We leave it to future work to examine how uncertainty in the representation of the future sequence may affect how speakers choose and encode words.

6
Conclusion

This work examines the role of past and future context on wordform encoding and word choice in naturalistic language production. The effect of future context or backward predictability, in particular, has long remained understudied compared to the effects of past or forward predictability. Beyond the methodological and statistical confounds linked to language modeling and multi-collinearity, the link between backward predictability and mechanisms of sentence planning is obscured by the assumption that
future context is statistically independent of the past context. In this work, we introduce a principled alternative to backward predictability based on the conditional pointwise mutual between the current word and the future sequence conditioned on the previously produced context. Our empirical contributions are two-fold. First, we revisit findings in probabilistic reduction, both as a validation of our proposed alternative and to re-evaluate earlier claims in light of improved measures of contextual predictability. Broadly, results from our first study reveal that predictability from the future exerts a stronger influence on word duration regardless of lexical class, which suggests uncertainty about the future context may further incentivize speakers to slow production. In our second study, we present a model of substitution errors in context, which explicates the unique contributions of availability-based preferences, communicative utility, and context on word choice. Beyond replicating trade-offs between lexical availability and communicative utility that have previously been observed in single-word production, our findings reveal nuanced effects of past and future context influence lexical choice, as reflected in systematic differences across categories of substitution errors. Overall, our study helps bridge psycholinguistic theories of sentence production with probabilistic and resource-rational approaches to modeling the mechanisms that underlie language production.

E.1
Extended comparisons between alternative formulations of Backward Predictability

In our main analysis, we focused on two formulations of future context predictability: relative backward predictability and conditional PMI. Our rationale for using relative backward predictability is that it (i) maintains the asymmetry of backward predictability, (ii) is effectively decorrelated from forward predictability, and (iii) yet yields a model with equivalent explanatory power. As noted in Section
2.1
, unconditional PMI (Eq.
3
) also assumes independent effects of past and future context similar to relative backward predictability. Where these two measures differ, however, is that unconditional PMI is a symmetric measure that quantifies the strength of association between the current word and the future. Below, we replicate the analysis conducted in Section
3
with standard backward predictability and unconditional PMI to sketch a complete comparison between conditional PMI, standard backward predictability, unconditional PMI, and relative backward predictability.

To determine the cause of this sign flip, we examined the pairwise correlations between all probabilistic predictors (Figure
12
). First, we find that backward predictability is highly correlated with both unigram predictability (
r=0.74
) and forward predictability (
r=0.98
). In the backward predictability model, the observed positive effect of forward predictability likely reflects an artifact of the high pairwise correlation between the two predictors. Although the model with unconditional PMI addresses the issue of collinearity between backward and unigram predictability, it does not address the issue of co-variance between forward and backward predictability, which may lead to instability in coefficient estimation.

As discussed in Section
3.2
, the model with the “decorrelated” variant of backward predictability i.e., relative backward predictability not only showed an expected inverse effect of future context predictability on duration (
\beta=-22.44,\ SE=0.313,\ p<0.001
), but also produced an interpretable inverse relationship between duration and forward predictability (
\beta=-5.38,\ SE=0.0819,\ p<0.001
). Since this measure is effectively uncorrelated with unigram predictability (
r=-0.07
) and exhibits reduced correlation with forward predictability (
r=-0.20
), we argue that relative backward predictability offers a stable alternative to both unmodified backward predictability and unconditional PMI since it mitigates issues of multi-collinearity without affecting explanatory power.

E.2
Model Comparisons with Bayesian Information Criterion

In Sections
3.2
and
4.2
, we presented model comparisons to evaluate improvements in models’ explanatory power when relative backward predictability and conditional PMI were added to the baseline model individually and when they were added together. Since our mixed-effects regression models had a nested structure, and because the goal of our analysis was to identify whether relative backward predictability and conditional PMI contribute redundant or unique sources of variance across both models, we used log-likelihood as the criterion for goodness of fit. To assess whether these findings hold when a more stringent measure of explanatory power is adopted, we also replicate the analysis using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which additionally penalizes the model for the number of predictors. That is, we may expect BIC to diverge from log-likelihood when comparing (i) the baseline model and models with relative backward predictability or conditional PMI added and (ii) comparing the models with either relative backward predictability and conditional PMI to one with both predictors.

BIC-based comparisons for both studies are summarized in Figure
13
. In study 1, the duration model with relative backward predictability had lower BIC than the model with conditional PMI, indicating a better fit to the data (
\Delta
BIC = -3890). Adding conditional PMI to the model with relative backward predictability further reduced the BIC compared to the model with only backward predictability; since this difference in BIC
>10
(
\Delta
BIC=-69), we interpret it as strong evidence that the model with both predictors was a better fit to word durations, even after correcting for model complexity. In study 2, the substitution model with conditional PMI yielded a lower BIC than the model with relative backward predictability (
\Delta
BIC = -13.451). However, when compared to a model with only conditional PMI, the model with both conditional PMI and relative backward predictability had a higher BIC, suggesting that the additional parameter did not improve model performance (
\Delta
BIC = 14.28). These results confirm that the findings about the distinct contributions of relative backward predictability and conditional PMI remain robust after adopting a more conservative, complexity-penalizing criterion for model comparison.
