Indigenous application of self-management: A concept analysis
Authors: Heather Burnside, Marie-Claude Tremblay, Monica Parry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo report an analysis of the concept of self-management in the context of type 2 diabetes, examining how it has been defined, understood, and applied within Indigenous contexts and grounded in Indigenous ways of knowing.DesignConcept analysis.Data sourcesKeywords and Medical Subject Headings for self-management, type 2 diabetes, and Indigenous Peoples were used to search six databases for articles published in English from January 2002-May 2026.MethodThe six steps of Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis guided the search, with specific attention to how the concept of self-management was defined, applied, and understood in Indigenous contexts. The analysis focused on studies involving Indigenous Peoples living in Canada, the United States, Australia, and New Zealand and examined attributes, antecedents, and consequences of the concept of self-management as applied in Indigenous contexts.ResultsAfter full text screening, a total of 15 articles were included in the final concept analysis. Antecedents of the self-management of type 2 diabetes from an Indigenous perspective included self-determination, cultural continuity, impact of colonization, problem solving skills, and respect for traditions. Attributes were classified into seven categories: physical function, cognitive ability, spirituality, cultural connection, access to care, knowledge regarding diabetes, and social support. However, Indigenous Peoples have long engaged in practices of balance, wellness, and collective responsibility for health that align with and expand beyond this concept. Situating type 2 diabetes self-management within Indigenous contexts underscores the importance of Indigenous perspectives of health and well-being while acknowledging the ongoing harmful effects of colonial violence on these practices.ImpactSelf-management from an Indigenous perspective is complex, with strong socio-historical underpinnings. This concept analysis informs a broad wholistic approach to education when discussing the self-management of type 2 diabetes with Indigenous Peoples. This approach must focus on physical function, cognitive ability, spirituality, cultural connection, access to care, education, and social support. Integrating Indigenous perspectives into self-management approaches can support more culturally safe, relational, and contextually relevant care for individuals living with type 2 diabetes.No Patient or Public Contribution.Keywords: Concept analysis, Nursing, Indigenous peoples, Type 2 diabetes, Colonization, Patient education

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (Pulver et al., 2010), type 2 diabetes, the most common form of diabetes, affects an estimated 350 million people globally (United Nations, 2014). Indigenous Peoples experience disproportionately high rates of type 2 diabetes; they are diagnosed at younger ages, experience greater disease severity, and face decreased life expectancy due to complications (Crowshoe et al., 2018). Current best-practice guidelines emphasize self-management practices to control type 2 diabetes and reduce complications (Registered Nurses’ Association of Ontario, 2010; Sherifali et al., 2018). Sherifali et al. (2018) define self-management as “a systematic intervention that involves active participation by the individual in self-monitoring of health parameters and/or decision-making with the application of knowledge and skills” (p. 36), acknowledging that patient–provider collaboration and problem-solving skills are crucial for sustained self-care. Effective self-management practices have been shown to improve glycemic control, self-efficacy, and self-care behaviours, and to reduce type 2 diabetes related distress (Captieux et al., 2018).

Self-management originates from a biomedical and Westernized perspective of health and well-being, emphasizing individual responsibility for managing chronic illness (Institute of Health Economics; Craig and Rodger, 2018; Sherifali et al., 2018). While several biomedical definitions of self-management exist, limited literature explores how this Western concept is received, interpreted, and applied in Indigenous contexts. This gap is significant because self-management, as framed biomedically, does not fully align with Indigenous worldviews of health and wellness, which are multidimensional, relational, and grounded in community and land-based values. Exploring how the concept of self-management has been applied and interpreted in Indigenous contexts is therefore critical.

Colonialism and its enduring impacts have profoundly affected Indigenous health and well-being. The introduction of communicable and non-communicable diseases, coupled with the intergenerational effects of assimilation policies such as residential schools, the Sixties Scoop, Indian Day Schools, and Indian Hospitals, have caused lasting trauma (National Collaborating Centre for Aboriginal Health, 2013). The prohibition of traditional healing practices through the Indian Act (1876) further disrupted Indigenous systems of health and knowledge (Horrill et al., 2018). Today, the persistence of Western biomedical dominance continues to act as a subtle form of colonization, often marginalizing Indigenous knowledge systems and perpetuating inequities in health care. In this paper, colonial violence is understood as the ongoing structural, social, and institutional harms resulting from colonization, including policies, practices, and systems that have disrupted Indigenous cultures, lands, and ways of life and continue to impact the health and well-being of these populations (Czyzewski, 2011; National Collaborating Centre for Aboriginal Health, 2013)

The World Health Organization (2010) acknowledges that Canada, the United States, Australia, and New Zealand share a British colonial history that has resulted in lasting impacts on Indigenous Peoples’ health, including the high prevalence of type 2 diabetes. Across these four countries, health and social inequities continue to exist between Indigenous and non-Indigenous populations (Pulver et al., 2010). Canada identifies First Nations, Inuit, and Métis as Indigenous Peoples; The United States is home to Native American tribes from various nations, such as the Lakota and the Navajo; Australia recognizes Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples as its first inhabitants; and New Zealand identifies Māori as Indigenous. While culturally distinct, these populations share comparable colonial histories and face similar disparities in TYPE 2 DIABETES prevalence, management, complications, and mortality (Dyck et al., 2010; Si et al., 2010).

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s Calls to Action (2015) emphasize the need to uphold Indigenous rights to health, safety, and well-being, to provide culturally safe health care, and to recognize Indigenous healing practices. These Calls to Action provide a guiding framework for promoting Indigenous perspectives of health grounded in equity, respect, and Indigenous ways of knowing and being.

This concept analysis, led and conducted by an Indigenous researcher and nurse (HB), examines how the Western concept of self-management has been applied and interpreted in Indigenous contexts across Canada, the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. Using a decolonizing lens, the analysis seeks to identify how self-management has been operationalized, where it aligns or conflicts with Indigenous understandings of wellness, and how this knowledge can inform culturally safe and contextually meaningful approaches to type 2 diabetes care for Indigenous Peoples. In doing so, this analysis not only examines how self-management has been applied within Indigenous contexts but also considers how Indigenous perspectives extend and challenge the conceptual boundaries of self-management as defined in Western literature.

Methods

Aim

The overall aim of this concept analysis is to explore the antecedents, attributes and consequences of self-management from an Indigenous perspective, based on literature that explored Indigenous perspectives on type 2 diabetes self-management. The results of this analysis will help health care providers understand self-management in Indigenous populations and assist them in supporting these practices in Indigenous populations, thereby aiding in reducing potential complications and promoting better health and wellbeing.

Design

A concept analysis is a method of analyzing the meaning of a concept or idea, typically by breaking it down into its component parts and examining the relationships between them using scientific literature published on the topic (Walker and Avant, 1995; Tofthagen and Fagerstrøm, 2010). It is used in various fields such as linguistics, psychology, and philosophy. A concept analysis is often used to better understand a concept, to develop a model of a concept, or to compare different concepts or ideas (Tofthagen and Fagerstrøm, 2010; Walker and Avant, 1995).

Rodgers’ evolutionary approach (Tofthagen and Fagerstrøm, 2010) was used to guide this concept analysis. This model emphasizes the role of time (e.g., effects of colonization) and context (Indigenous People) in understanding the concept under investigation (self-management). Rodgers' Concept Analysis has previously been used to analyze concepts related to Indigenous health. For example, Smith (2021) utilized Rodgers’ concept analysis to define the meaning of cultural responsiveness as it applies to the work of health care practitioners who engage with Indigenous patients. Rodgers’ outlines six steps in defining a concept: 1) specifying the concept and alternate terminologies, 2) determining and selecting the scope for data collection, 3) collecting data related to the concept’s attributes, antecedents and consequences which might include sociocultural, interdisciplinary, and temporal variables, 4) analyzing the data based on the attributes of the concept, 5) providing examples that fit the concept, if needed, and 6) determining hypotheses and applications for further evolution of the concept (Rodgers and Knafl, 2000). Consistent with Step 1 of Rodgers’ evolutionary approach, the concept of interest was defined as self-management of type 2 diabetes among Indigenous Peoples. During preliminary engagement with the literature, surrogate and related terms (e.g., self-care, diabetes management, chronic disease management) were identified and used to inform the development of the search strategy and inclusion criteria. Step 2 consisted of determining the scope and selecting relevant literature through a structured database search. Step 3 involved data extraction related to attributes, antecedents, and consequences of self-management as well as initial coding of relevant concepts described in the included studies, with attention to socio-cultural and historical context. Step 4 included comparison and synthesis of extracted data to refine and categorize these attributes, antecedents, and consequences. During data analysis, extracted data from each study were reviewed line-by-line and coded according to their relevance to antecedents, attributes, and consequences of self-management. Similar concepts and patterns across studies were identified through constant comparison, allowing for grouping of related data into preliminary categories. These categories were then iteratively refined by comparing across studies to ensure consistency and conceptual clarity. Where overlap or ambiguity occurred, categories were discussed and adjusted to best reflect the meaning and context presented in the literature. This process resulted in the final categorization of antecedents, attributes, and consequences presented in the results. Step 5 was addressed using illustrative examples from the literature to demonstrate how the concept is utilized in Indigenous contexts. Step 6 involved identifying implications for further development of the concept, which are presented in the discussion in relation to future research, nursing practice and policy. Aligned with Rodgers’ approach, this analysis examines how the concept of self-management is applied and understood within Indigenous contexts, rather than seeking to fully redefine the concept.

Attributes can be defined as characteristics or features of the concept. Antecedents are the conditions or factors that precede or lead up to the development of a concept. These can include historical events, theories, observations, or other related concepts that are influential in the development of the concept in question. In Rodgers’ evolutionary concept analysis, consequences are defined as the immediate or eventual outcomes of a behavior, action, or event. Consequences can be divided into two categories: positive and negative. Positive consequences are those that are beneficial for the individual or society, and negative consequences are those that are detrimental to the individual or society (Tofthagen and Fagerstrøm, 2010). Rodgers’ method was selected because it is well suited to capturing the dynamic, context-dependent nature of concepts, making it particularly appropriate for exploring self-management as it is defined, interpreted, and applied within Indigenous contexts.

Data sources

Ovid Medline, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Cochrane, and the Native Health Database were searched using the terms “self-management,” “Indigenous,” and “diabetes.” The Native Health Database was included to improve access to Indigenous-focused literature relevant to the topic. A larger set of keywords for “Indigenous” was also applied (e.g., “Cree,” “Anishinaabe,” “Mohawk”) to ensure representation of Indigenous Peoples in Canada, the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. Keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms were combined using Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”).

Inclusion criteria for articles were: (1) full-text, peer-reviewed articles published in English; (2) published between January 2002 and May 2026; (3) explicitly reported and discussed the concept of self-management of type 2 diabetes; and (4) examined Indigenous Peoples’ understandings, experiences, or applications of self-management within Canada, the United States, Australia, or New Zealand. Articles that only referenced Indigenous Peoples without exploring self-management from their perspectives were excluded. All included articles were read in detail, and study information (author, year, title, study design) was extracted alongside data on definitions, attributes, antecedents, and consequences of self-management.

Results

A total of 7,354 articles were retrieved and after duplicates were removed, 2,354 articles were identified for title and abstract screening. The research team used Bramer et al.'s (2016) method for managing duplicate scholarly literature in EndNote (Bramer et al., 2016). After de-duplication in EndNote, all abstracts and full-text articles were uploaded into Covidence where the first author (H.B.) reviewed the titles and abstracts for relevance and removed irrelevant studies. After title and abstract screening, 203 full text citations were assessed for full-text screening (HB). After full-text screening, a total of 15 articles were included for the concept analysis of self-management of type 2 diabetes from an Indigenous perspective (Fig. 1). The identified antecedents, attributes, and consequences were derived through a synthesis of patterns and themes across the included studies, reflecting how self-management has been described and interpreted within the existing literature. Consistent with Rodgers’ evolutionary approach, the identification and categorization of antecedents, attributes, and consequences involved interpretive synthesis by the researchers, particularly in areas where the literature was limited or conceptually fragmented.

Definition

The attributes, antecedents, and consequences extracted as constructs of self-management of type 2 diabetes from an Indigenous perspective are presented according to Rodgers’ phases of an evolutionary concept analysis (Table 1). Based on these results, self-management of type 2 diabetes among Indigenous people can be defined as the following: the dynamic process in which Indigenous Peoples living with TYPE 2 DIABETES utilize individual and collective forms of self-determination to manage their diabetes and incorporate strategies into daily life, strengthened by wholistic cultural continuity and traditional worldviews of health, wellbeing, and wellness. This definition reflects not only the application of self-management within Indigenous contexts, but also a reframing of the concept to incorporate relational, cultural, and structural dimensions that are not fully captured in dominant biomedical understandings.

Antecedents of self-management

Antecedents are events or factors that are essential for self-management but precede its occurrence (Walker and Avant, 1995). These antecedents reflect the contextual and temporal conditions that shape the development and expression of self-management within Indigenous contexts. From the data extracted from the studies, the antecedents of self-management of type 2 diabetes from an Indigenous perspective were classified into five categories: Self-Determination, Cultural Continuity, Impact of Colonization, Problem Solving Skills and Respect for Traditions (Table 2).

Self-determination emerged as a central antecedent of self-management in many of the included articles, reflecting Indigenous Peoples’ rights, strengths, and capacities to manage their health and well-being in culturally meaningful ways. Barton (2008) and Bruyere and Garro (2000) described how individual self-determination enables Indigenous Peoples to take charge of treatment decisions, actively participate in their care, and cultivate empowerment and resilience. At the health care provider level, Handley et al. (2010) and Henderson (2010) emphasized the importance of recognizing Indigenous Peoples’ rights and competencies in directing their own health, noting that culturally safe care is often lacking in mainstream practice. For example, Handley et al. (2010) observed that some clinicians continued to rely on standardized type 2 diabetes education materials that failed to reflect Indigenous cultural values or community priorities, thereby limiting opportunities for Indigenous Peoples to exercise autonomy in their care. Similarly, Henderson (2010) reported that institutional structures and provider attitudes sometimes constrained Indigenous participation in care planning, reinforcing biomedical dominance and undermining culturally grounded approaches to type 2 diabetes management. At the system level, Bruyere and Garro (2000) highlighted that partnerships prioritizing Indigenous control and culturally grounded care contribute to improved type 2 diabetes management outcomes. These findings underscore that self-determination at individual, provider, and system levels is an essential antecedent of self-management for Indigenous Peoples, highlighting the contextual influences shaping how the concept is understood and enacted over time. This includes health system structures that support Indigenous leadership and decision-making in care delivery (Fournie et al., 2023).

Cultural safety was identified across several included articles as a critical antecedent of self-management, emphasizing the importance of care that is free from judgement, bias, and discrimination. For instance, Bird et al. (2008) and Handley et al. (2010) described how culturally safe health care enabled individuals to integrate Indigenous values, teachings, and traditions into their type 2 diabetes self-management practices. Lautenschlager and Smith (2006) highlighted how patient-provider relationships built on respect and understanding fostered trust, which directly influenced individuals’ ability to engage in self-management. At the health system level, Roubideaux et al. (2000) and Tiedt (2010) discussed how historical and ongoing experiences of racism and exclusion in health care created barriers to effective self-management, reinforcing the need for systemic policy changes to ensure culturally safe services and underscoring the context-dependent nature of self-management in Indigenous health (Bird et al., 2008; Roubideaux et al., 2000).

Access to care was consistently described as a prerequisite for self-management. Studies emphasized that without accessible, appropriate, and culturally safe health care services, Indigenous Peoples faced barriers that limited their ability to effectively self-manage type 2 diabetes (Aronson, 2016; Bird et al., 2008; Felix et al., 2018; Handley et al., 2010; Henderson, 2010). Access to care includes not only geographic availability of services but also the quality of those services, such as whether they are culturally safe, free from discrimination, and supportive of Indigenous knowledge and practices. These findings also emphasize culturally safe, relational, and community-embedded models of care as critical to supporting self-management. Indigenous-led services, continuity of care, and organizational capacity were identified as key factors influencing individuals’ ability to engage meaningfully in type 2 diabetes self-management (Fournie et al., 2023). In this sense, access to care represents a necessary condition that must be in place before Indigenous Peoples can meaningfully engage in self-management.

The impact of colonization and ongoing structural barriers was identified in many included articles as a central antecedent influencing type 2 diabetes self-management among Indigenous Peoples. For instance, Aronson (2016) and Webster et al. (2017) described how colonial violence continued to shape access to health care through uneven geographical distribution of services and limited availability of culturally safe care. Henderson (2010) highlighted that discrimination and racism from health care providers remained common experiences, leading some Indigenous Peoples to avoid or delay care, which directly undermined their ability to develop and sustain effective self-management practices. Collectively, these studies illustrate how colonization not only created historical trauma but also continued to operate through systemic barriers that directly affected Indigenous Peoples’ capacity to self-manage type 2 diabetes, reflecting the temporal and structural nature of the concept’s evolution within Indigenous contexts. These ongoing structural barriers are also reflected in challenges related to health system design and service delivery, which can limit access to culturally appropriate and continuous care for Indigenous Peoples (Swampy et al., 2026).

Respect for traditions and cultural practices was identified in several included articles as an important antecedent of effective type 2 diabetes self-management. In particular, respect from health care providers and health systems for Indigenous cultural practices supported self-management in meaningful ways. For example, Johnson et al. (2021) described how incorporating traditional foods, ceremonies, and healing practices into care provided spiritual and emotional support that enhanced self-management and strengthened connections to cultural identity. Similarly, Webster et al. (2017) highlighted that when health care providers acknowledged and supported traditional Indigenous healing methods, individuals were better able to integrate self-management strategies into their daily lives. In contrast, Henderson (2010) noted that when health systems privileged biomedical models and dismissed Indigenous traditions, individuals experienced barriers to practicing self-management that aligned with their values. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that when health care providers and health systems honor cultural traditions, they provide both practical and spiritual tools for type 2 diabetes self-management, while lack of respect for these practices created significant obstacles.

Attributes of self-management

The attributes of self-management of type 2 diabetes among Indigenous Peoples were classified into seven categories: physical function, cognitive ability, spirituality, cultural connection, access to care, knowledge regarding type 2 diabetes, and social support (Table 3). While physical and cognitive capacities may influence the extent to which individuals can engage in self-management, they are better understood as attributes of the concept, as they are enacted within the process itself rather than preceding it. These attributes were consistently linked to Indigenous Peoples’ ability to self-manage type 2 diabetes across the included studies.

Cognitive ability included both the capacity to understand information related to type 2 diabetes care and the ability to apply this knowledge in everyday life. Bird et al. (2008) and Felix et al. (2018) noted that comprehension of type 2 diabetes education was essential, while Aronson (2016) emphasized the importance of translating knowledge into practice. Spirituality was identified as an integral attribute of self-management in the context of Indigenous health, with several studies highlighting the role of Indigenous spiritual practices in supporting health and wellness. For example, Johnson et al. (2021) and Webster et al. (2017) reported that incorporating Indigenous spiritual practices into care provided strength, resilience, and guidance in managing type 2 diabetes. Similarly, Bird et al. (2008) described empowerment as a spiritual dimension of self-management, emphasizing that spiritual connection was a key component of how Indigenous Peoples engaged in and sustained self-management practices. Cultural connection, which was enacted through the use of traditional medicines by involving traditional healers in care. Henderson (2010) and Johnson et al. (2021) discussed how traditional medicines were viewed as complementary to biomedical treatments, while Bird et al. (2008) highlighted the importance of integrating traditional healers into TYPE 2 DIABETES care. Knowledge regarding type 2 diabetes was an important attribute of self-management, particularly when it reflected information and understanding gained in a culturally safe context. Study findings indicated that when health care professionals supported Indigenous Peoples to develop knowledge in ways that respected cultural perspectives, individuals were better able to understand and manage their type 2 diabetes (Bird et al., 2008). Finally, social support was recognized as essential, with Barton (2008) noting the importance of networks that included family, friends, Elders, and traditional healers in supporting ongoing TYPE 2 DIABETES self-management. Self-management was thus understood as a relational and context-dependent practice shaped by identity, gender roles, and community relationships, rather than solely an individual behavioural task (Burnside et al., 2023). These relationships were shaped by broader community roles and identities, which influenced how individuals understood and engaged in self-management practices (Burnside et al., 2023).

Consequences of self-management

The consequences of self-management of type 2 diabetes among Indigenous Peoples were classified into two categories: wholistic well-being and cultural resiliency (Table 4). Wholistic well-being was a vision of health that considered physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual dimensions. The term “wholism” was written with a “w” to reflect the interconnectedness of all things; a fundamental concept aligned with Indigenous ways of knowing and being. In the context of self-management, wholistic well-being represented the outcome or experience of effectively managing type 2 diabetes in ways that were culturally meaningful. Individuals who successfully engaged in self-management practices experienced a sense of balance and harmony across all aspects of their health, reflecting not only control over their type 2 diabetes, but also strengthened spiritual, emotional, and social connections. This approach emphasized caring for the whole person rather than focusing solely on biomedical targets. This was supported by care environments that enabled continuity, culturally grounded approaches to health, and attention to broader social and system-level factors that influenced care (Fournie et al., 2023; Swampy et al., 2026). Across the included studies, wholistic well-being was reflected in several outcomes related to both physical and overall health. These included improvements in self-management practices, better blood glucose control, and a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes related complications (Barton, 2008; Bird et al., 2008; Handley et al., 2010; Johnson et al., 2021; Lautenschlager and Smith, 2006; Webster et al., 2017). In addition, individuals described improvements in quality of life, reflecting a sense of balance across physical, emotional, and social dimensions of health (Bruyere and Garro, 2000; Roubideaux et al., 2000; Webster et al., 2017). Together, these findings highlighted that successful self-management was associated not only with clinical outcomes but also with broader experiences of wellness. Cultural resiliency included a sense of security, connection to culture, and restoration of self-reliance through traditional healing methods. It was the ability to adapt and overcome adversity while maintaining one’s cultural identity, drawing on cultural resources, traditional knowledge, and values to cope with challenges and strengthen communities. In the context of self-management of type 2 diabetes, cultural resiliency enabled Indigenous Peoples to engage in care practices that were meaningful and culturally grounded. For example, individuals incorporated traditional foods, ceremonies, or healing practices into daily routines, helping them maintain motivation, problem-solve challenges, and sustain long-term management of their condition. Cultural resiliency was therefore closely linked to cultural safety, cultural connection, and the effectiveness of type 2 diabetes self-management strategies. Cultural resiliency was further reflected in specific outcomes related to the integration of culture into care. Individuals described a greater sense of security and connection when cultural practices were incorporated into self-management, as well as increased empowerment and confidence in managing their health (Lautenschlager and Smith, 2006; Tiedt, 2010; Johnson et al., 2021). The ability to exercise self-determination in incorporating traditional practices into care was also identified as an important outcome, reinforcing the role of culture in supporting sustained engagement in self-management (Roubideaux et al., 2000). These findings highlighted that culturally grounded approaches to self-management supported not only engagement in care but also the development of cultural identity, confidence, and resilience, which were instrumental to well-being.

Discussion

The concept of “self-management” of type 2 diabetes among Indigenous Peoples has been poorly explored in the literature, often citing Euro-centric and biomedical definitions of self-management (Institute Of Health Economics; Craig, Rodger, 2018; Sherifali et al., 2018). However, the results of this analysis demonstrate that self-management of type 2 diabetes among Indigenous Peoples is a dynamic process that needs to account for the detrimental effects of colonization, including the devastating impacts of forced assimilation practices, by encompassing specific attributes and antecedents (Horrill et al., 2018; Mosby and Galloway, 2017).

Among the five categories of antecedents identified, those at the health care provider and health system levels were particularly influential in determining whether Indigenous Peoples could engage in effective type 2 diabetes self-management. Respect for patients’ rights to self-determination and cultural safety, recognition of the impact of colonization, support for problem-solving skills, access to care, and respect for Indigenous customs and traditions were consistently described as prerequisites for developing a culturally relevant understanding of self-management in Indigenous contexts. These findings echo broader national and international calls for systemic change. For example, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s Calls to Action (2015) highlight the need for culturally safe health care, the recognition of Indigenous healing practices, and the elimination of health inequities between Indigenous and non-Indigenous populations. The antecedents identified in this analysis align closely with these Calls to Action, underscoring how systemic reforms are essential for Indigenous Peoples to meaningfully engage in self-management. Collectively, these findings highlight the interconnected nature of self-management within Indigenous contexts. Antecedents such as self-determination, cultural safety, access to care, and the ongoing impacts of colonization shape the conditions under which individuals are able to engage in self-management. These conditions influence the development of key attributes, including cultural connection, spirituality, knowledge, and social support, which are enacted in relational and community contexts. In turn, these attributes contribute to outcomes such as wholistic well-being and cultural resiliency, demonstrating that self-management is not a linear process but one that is shaped by dynamic interactions across individual, community, and system levels. These findings also challenge dominant biomedical understandings of self-management that emphasize individual responsibility and behaviour change. Instead, self-management within Indigenous contexts is shaped by relationships, cultural identity, and the broader social and health system environments in which individuals are situated. This includes the influence of community connections, access to culturally safe and Indigenous-led services, and structural factors that enable or constrain engagement in care. As such, self-management cannot be understood as an isolated individual practice, but rather as a relational and context-dependent process that is enacted across individuals, communities, and health systems.

The findings of this analysis also point to important tensions within the concept of self-management when considered from an Indigenous perspective. In particular, dominant biomedical models emphasize individual responsibility and behaviour change, whereas Indigenous perspectives of health are often grounded in relationality, community, and collective responsibility (Crowshoe et al., 2018; Horrill et al., 2018; Burnside et al., 2023). This creates a tension between individualistic approaches to managing type 2 diabetes and the ways in which self-management is enacted within family, community, and cultural contexts. As a result, behaviours traditionally associated with self-management may take on different meanings, being shaped not only by individual choice but also by social roles, cultural obligations, and community relationships (Czyzewski, 2011). Recognizing these tensions is essential for developing approaches to self-management that are both culturally relevant and responsive to Indigenous ways of knowing and being.

This analysis suggests that self-management within Indigenous contexts is inherently relational, grounded in culture, and shaped by both community and health system influences. The emphasis on Indigenous-led services, continuity of care, and broader structural supports challenges individualistic understandings of self-management by situating it within social and systemic contexts. These insights add depth to the identified antecedents, attributes, and consequences and further strengthen the conceptualization of self-management within Indigenous contexts. While this analysis demonstrates how self-management is enacted within Indigenous contexts, it also highlights important limitations of existing conceptualizations, suggesting the need to move beyond application toward a more substantive reconceptualization of self-management that reflects Indigenous worldviews of health, relationality, and collective responsibility.

At the individual level, self-determination is critical in promoting the self-management of type 2 diabetes. Indigenous Peoples must have control over their health decisions and have access to culturally appropriate health information to make informed choices about their type 2 diabetes management. Self-determination provides the tools and resources to manage type 2 diabetes in ways that are culturally meaningful and appropriate. In many included articles, self-determination was also linked to reducing the stigma and shame associated with type 2 diabetes. For instance, Barton (2008) and Handley et al. (2010) noted that when individuals could draw on traditional teachings and exercise autonomy in their care, they were less likely to internalize blame or feel shame about their condition. Henderson (2010) similarly observed that culturally safe care grounded in self-determination helped to shift responsibility away from the individual alone and toward a recognition of broader social and historical factors, reducing stigma while reinforcing traditional health practices and teachings. These findings support Crowshoe et al. (2018), who argue that harmonizing biomedical and Indigenous approaches to health is both a professional and moral responsibility.

There has historically been a lack of understanding of the concept of self-management from an Indigenous perspective, and the term is complex and multifactorial in its meaning. Understanding the historical evolution of type 2 diabetes and the impact of ongoing colonial violence in Indigenous communities is crucial to appreciating the barriers Indigenous Peoples face in appropriately and effectively managing type 2 diabetes. This resonates with earlier reports from the National Collaborating Centre for Aboriginal Health (2013), which identified colonization and systemic inequities as fundamental determinants of Indigenous health disparities. The present analysis builds on this work by offering conceptual clarity on how self-management is shaped by Indigenous values such as wholism, cultural continuity, and community support, while also revealing how systemic inequities obstruct these practices.

The results of this analysis will assist health care providers in supporting self-management practices in Indigenous populations, thereby aiding in reducing potential complications and promoting better health and well-being. By explicitly situating self-management within the broader context of reconciliation, cultural safety, and Indigenous rights, this work both supports and extends earlier literature. It suggests that without structural reforms in health care policy and practice, including the adoption of Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s Calls to Action, self-management will remain constrained by colonial legacies.

These findings also have important implications for nursing practice. Nurses can operationalize this understanding of self-management by incorporating culturally safe and relational approaches into assessment, education, and care planning (Sherifali et al., 2018; Crowshoe et al., 2018). This includes assessing not only clinical indicators of type 2 diabetes, but also cultural connection, access to care, and social supports that influence individuals’ ability to engage in self-management. In practice, this may involve collaborating with patients who are Indigenous, their families and communities, to co-develop care plans that integrate traditional practices alongside biomedical approaches, supporting access to Indigenous-led services where available, and fostering ongoing, trust-based relationships that facilitate continuity of care. In addition, nurses can support self-determination by engaging patients in shared decision-making and tailoring education to reflect cultural values, lived experiences, and community contexts.

The findings suggest the concept of self-management requires further development to more fully reflect Indigenous perspectives of health and well-being. While existing frameworks emphasize individual behaviours, this analysis highlights the need for a reconceptualization of self-management to incorporate relational, cultural, and structural dimensions.

Despite the strengths of this concept analysis, there are some limitations to be acknowledged. Despite a comprehensive search strategy, a relatively small number of publications were found, which may not have been large enough to fully discover or acknowledge potential knowledge related to this concept. In addition, the limited number of included studies may reflect broader structural issues related to the visibility of Indigenous knowledges within academic literature. Disciplinary boundaries and Western publication norms often privilege written, peer-reviewed sources, which may not fully capture knowledge shared through oral traditions, community-based practices, or Indigenous-led research that is not indexed in major databases. As a result, the scope of literature identified in this analysis may not fully represent the breadth of Indigenous perspectives on self-management, highlighting the need for approaches that more meaningfully engage with diverse forms of knowledge. Moreover, the nature of reviewing academic literature through the use of database searches on concepts related to Indigenous health is not necessarily in alignment with how Indigenous knowledges have traditionally been disseminated, which often occurs through oral teachings, ceremony, and community practices. While the Native Health Database was included to capture Indigenous-focused literature, it is possible that relevant studies indexed in other Indigenous-specific or community-based sources were not identified. This represents a limitation of the search strategy. However, the findings from this study can serve as a foundation for dialogue with Elders, Knowledge Keepers, and Indigenous Peoples living with type 2 diabetes. These conversations could guide the development of culturally relevant practice guidelines and inform policies that better integrate Indigenous healing practices into Western health care settings. For example, some centres in Canada have already introduced traditional healers as members of the health care team, incorporated sweat lodges as part of wellness programming, or provided space for ceremonial and land-based practices alongside biomedical care (Crowshoe et al., 2018; Horrill et al., 2018). Embedding such approaches within health services not only responds to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s Calls to Action (2015) but also reflects a practical application of the antecedents, attributes, and consequences of self-management identified in this study.

Conclusion

In this concept analysis, five antecedents, seven attributes, and two consequences of the concept of self-management of type 2 diabetes among Indigenous Peoples were identified, and the process of understanding and utilizing the self-management term was described. The attributes of self-management can serve as guidelines for nursing practice, helping health care providers recognize and support the skills, knowledge, and cultural resources that Indigenous Peoples use to manage type 2 diabetes. Similarly, the consequences of self-management can serve as outcome indicators, allowing nurses and health care teams to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions in promoting wholistic well-being, empowerment, and culturally meaningful type 2 diabetes care. This analysis provides a foundation for understanding self-management of type 2 diabetes from an Indigenous perspective, highlighting the importance of incorporating all elements of the concept, including antecedents, attributes, and consequences into practice to enhance patient-centered and culturally safe care. These findings reinforce the need to rethink self-management in ways that are responsive to Indigenous cultures, systems, and ways of knowing.

Recommendations for future practice include integrating the results of this analysis into culturally appropriate best practice guidelines for nurses and health care providers working with Indigenous Peoples living with type 2 diabetes. However, before such recommendations are advanced, it is critical to collaborate with Indigenous community partners to corroborate the findings, ensure that they align with community priorities, and co-identify next steps. This collaborative approach would guide the adaptation of practice and the modification of guidelines to better support the self-management of type 2 diabetes in ways that are meaningful and culturally resonant. Additionally, the results of this analysis can aid in the development of culturally appropriate and responsive type 2 diabetes education materials for nurses and other health care providers.
