ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations and the World Health Organization (WHO) recently convened an expert consultation to address the increasing but inconsistent use of Precautionary Allergen (“may contain”) Labelling (PAL).ObjectivesWe conducted a Delphi study to explore the perspectives of clinicians, patient representatives, and other interest-holders on PAL practice.MethodsWe followed previous guidance on Delphi study development and reporting. A narrative review exploring the FAO/WHO Expert Consultation informed the Delphi process. The working group generated 35 items based on the review of FAO/WHO consultation, categorized into 7 domains: (i) Importance of the issue, (ii) Informing use of PAL, (iii) Action level cut-offs, (iv) Communication, (v) Liability considerations, (vi) Free From statements, and (vii) Areas for further research. Items underwent 3 iterative Delphi rounds involving a panel of 35 experts who rated their agreement narratively and on a 5-point Likert scale. Consensus was defined as ≥70% consistency in agreement or disagreement (excluding neutral responses); stability was defined as <10% variation in average scores across rounds. A public consultation was held to collect different viewpoints arising from the consensus activity.ResultsThe Delphi was concluded after 3 rounds with consensus achieved for 33/35 items. Response rate was 100% across all 3 rounds. The panel strongly agreed that PAL should only be applied when an unintended allergen may be present above a cut-off (action level) and that it should always be based on a formal risk assessment, preferably quantitative, and mandated through legislation. Furthermore, action levels should be based on “reference doses” (amount of total allergenic protein), rather than on a concentration (eg, 10 ppm), and more specifically on population-adjusted eliciting doses ED05 (amount of allergen expected to elicit an objective reaction in no more than 5% of the allergic population). To achieve a balance between protection and the risk of PAL overuse, ED05 was deemed preferable to ED01. There was 70% agreement that consumers should be informed of residual risk (ie, unintended allergen presence below the reference dose), but no consensus as to how this might be done. Finally, PAL should not be used for an allergen declared to be absent with a “free from” statement, nor should a PAL appear for an allergen listed as an ingredient.ConclusionsThis Delphi explored a comprehensive set of statements across multiple aspects of PAL development, application, and communication. On the one hand, it provides systematically derived considerations, aligned with the FAO/WHO consultation recommendations, to support future harmonization of practices. On the other, it highlights several areas of uncertainty to prioritize for future research.Keywords: Food allergy, Food labelling, Precautionary allergen labelling, FAO/WHO, Codex, Risk management, Risk communication, Prepacked, Anaphylaxis

Introduction

Food allergy is a major global health concern, affecting millions of individuals and impacting their quality of life.1, 2, 3, 4 To protect allergic consumers from the risk of unintended allergen presence (UAP), food manufacturers often apply Precautionary Allergen Labelling (PAL; “may contain”) to communicate the potential presence of unintended allergens.5 However, in most countries, PAL is not regulated and its application is voluntary, leading to an inconsistent use. To further complicate matters, food businesses often adopt defensive precautionary over-labelling strategies, declaring the presence of food allergens even when the likelihood of UAP is minimal, or even circumventing labelling legislation by intentionally including a cross-contamination allergen as an ingredient.6 Consistently, food analytical surveys have shown that the majority of products with PAL did not present detectable levels of UAP, whereas, in other cases, they have detected food products with UAP bearing no PAL.7 As a result of this heterogeneity and perceived PAL overuse, consumers frequently disregard it, hence diluting its effectiveness as a risk communication tool.8, 9, 10 Henceforth, the need to establish an international framework for the use of PAL is clear and was highlighted by the World Allergy Organization (WAO) over 10 years ago.

To harmonize food standards across the world and support international trade, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations and the World Health Organization (WHO), primarily through the Codex Alimentarius Commission, have developed a collection of internationally recognized standards, codes of practice, and recommendations relating to food production, food labelling, and food safety. In 2019, the Codex Committee on Food Labelling requested the FAO and WHO to convene an expert consultation to provide scientific advice with respect to which food allergens should be considered global “priority” allergens, and establish threshold levels for these priority allergens to inform the use of PAL.11, 12, 13, 14, 15

In the first paper from the WAO ACT-UP! Group, we reviewed the recommendations from the FAO/WHO Expert Consultation, and the potential barriers to their adoption.16 These include concerns around the acceptability of an ED05 (Eliciting dose) threshold (the amount of allergen needed to trigger a reaction in 5% of the allergic population) to inform the need for PAL,17 the protection of more-sensitive individuals who may react to lower doses of allergen, the standardization of risk assessment procedures,18,19 and how clinicians can best support food-allergic individuals in interpreting PAL.20,21 Further considerations include how “free-from” S, “vegan”, and “gluten-free” claims intersect with PAL, particularly because many people with food allergies use these labels to guide their food choices.22, 23, 24, 25

The recommendations of the FAO/WHO Expert Consultation are currently being considered by multiple interest-holders through extensive Codex Committee on Food Labelling consultation. However, given the limited scope for input from healthcare professionals, the complexity and heterogeneity of perspectives surrounding the issues, we undertook a Delphi approach amongst healthcare professionals, patient advocacy groups, and scientists involved in food safety, risk assessment, and regulation. The aim was to explore and achieve an international expert consensus on.

The use of thresholds and risk assessment processes to inform PAL application;

ii.The standardization of PAL terminology and risk communication;

iii.The implementation of PAL within national and international regulatory systems, focusing on consumer protection and feasibility considerations.

To ensure consistent terminology for the food-amount input used in PAL calculations, we followed the WHO/FAO conceptual approach,26 which states that action levels should be based on the amount of food consumed in a single eating occasion. As WHO/FAO guidance does not provide a standardized term for this “single-occasion amount”, we made a distinction between 2 related concepts. Specifically, we used the term “serving size”, adopting the FDA nomenclature,27 to refer to the standardized amount of food “customarily consumed in one eating occasion” commonly used in nutrition labelling. However, we acknowledged that so-defined “serving sizes” may not always reflect actual intake in real-world settings.28,29 Therefore, we used the term “portion size” as a generic descriptor of the amount of food consumed in a single eating occasion.

Methods

This Delphi study was conducted as part of the WAO ACT-UP! initiative. Consistent with best practice for Delphi studies,30, 31, 32, 33, 34 all study procedures, including criteria for dropping statements and stopping rounds, were defined a priori.

Panel selection

We employed purposive sampling, focusing on healthcare professionals to maximize representativeness across expertise, geographic distribution, and gender. Eligible participants who met the following inclusion criteria were invited to participate by email: (i) age 18 years or older; (ii) fluency in English; and (iii) active involvement in research or policymaking in allergy, food risk assessment, or food regulation for at least 5 years.

Participants were recruited from 5 sources: WAO membership (n = 23), academic experts known to WAO (n = 3), food allergy patient associations’ representatives (n = 3), regulators (n = 2), and authors of relevant publications on food allergy and regulation (n = 4). Based on an anticipated 20% dropout rate, derived from previous Delphi studies,35,36 we aimed to recruit a minimum panel of 29 experts.

Development of Delphi statements

The preliminary Delphi statements were developed through a structured process by the steering committee, which included 3 members of the expert panel (AB, AF, PJT). Draft statements were based on the key themes and concepts outlined in the FAO/WHO Expert Consultation and the narrative review published by the WAO ACT-UP initiative on the use of PAL.16 These initial statements were then iteratively refined with input from the broader panel, in a series of meetings and email exchanges. The resulting 35 statements were organized across 7 thematic domains: Importance of the issue (4 statements), Improving the process informing PAL application (8 statements), What cut-off should be used as an action level? (11 statements), How to communicate PAL (6 statements), Liability considerations for Food Business Operators (1 statement), How to account for “Free-from” labels (4 statements), and Future research areas (1 statement). The statements list is in Suppl. Appendix 1.

Delphi process

The electronic Delphi (e-Delphi) process was conducted anonymously via Google Forms (Suppl. Appendices 2-4), with a planned sequence of 3 to 4 rounds depending on whether predefined closing criteria were met by Round 3.

Throughout the rounds, participants rated their agreement with the 35 statements using a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 3 = neutral; 5 = strongly agree). Participants were also asked to provide optional narrative feedback for each statement. Demographic information (age, sex, country of residence) and primary area of expertise were collected in the first round.

Starting from the conclusion of round 1, participants received individualized forms containing: i) the revised versions of the statements, if previous-round feedback highlighted the need for an edit; ii) a summary of the previous group's response distribution (percentage of responses ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’); iii) a reminder of each participant's own previous responses to facilitate reflective judgment. At the end of each round, panelists were provided with a summary of the round's quantitative results, thematic analyses, and, if applicable, suggested changes to existing statements (Suppl. Appendices 5-7). At this stage, the experts could provide additional feedback, suggest additional edits, or propose new Delphi statements.

Data analysis

Participants’ responses were analyzed quantitatively (central tendencies and percentages) to identify consensus and stability. Consensus was defined a priori as ≥70% agreement/disagreement, calculated by combining the proportions of ‘agree’ and ‘strongly agree’ responses, or ‘disagree’ and ‘strongly disagree’ responses, excluding neutral responses from the denominator. We considered “stability” as a variation of mean values between rounds of group responses, inferior to or equal to 10%.37

Qualitative comments for each statement were reviewed independently by members of the steering committee at the end of every voting round and grouped into thematic categories using an iterative, inductive approach. Similar responses were aggregated and synthesized into narrative summaries. Disagreements were resolved through consensus discussions among steering committee members. The synthesized summaries were used to provide additional information to the Delphi items, identify additional statements, or refine existing statements. The results of each qualitative assessment were presented to the panel of experts at the end of each voting round.

Closing and dropping criteria

A subset of 16 Delphi statements (Suppl. Appendix 1) was identified by the steering committee before the start of the Delphi. These items were then used as “core-statements” to evaluate whether closing criteria were met. To avoid conformity bias and strategic responses aimed at stopping the process prematurely, the panel was kept unaware of which statements qualified as “core statements”. The distribution of core statements varied across domains: Improving PAL application and Cut-off for Action Level each included 6 core statements, PAL communication included 3, and Liability included 1. Each statement was accompanied by explanatory materials to ensure a shared understanding.

The Delphi process was designed to be halted upon meeting either of 2 predefined conditions: i) achieving consensus and stability on the set of 16 “core statements” or ii) after completion of 4 rounds.

To streamline the process and minimize survey fatigue, we applied specific criteria for dropping statements across rounds. Specifically, statements were removed if all the following requirements were met: i) no change in their consensus or stability status was observed across the previous 2 rounds; ii) if an agreement/disagreement rate of 90% or higher was achieved at the end of the previous round; iii) if the narrative feedback indicated that no modifications were necessary. Dropped statements were excluded from further rounds of quantitative voting, but panel members could still provide narrative comments.

Public consultation

Finally, a public consultation was conducted to collect additional perspectives on the results of the consensus activity. The manuscript was made publicly available in its original form (Suppl. Appendix 8) on the WAO website. A Request for Information (RFI) was published, inviting the scientific community, healthcare professionals, individual FBOs, patient associations, and members of the public to provide comments and suggestions, with a focus on the feasibility, acceptability, and implementation capacity of the proposed consensus statements. The consultation was open for 45 days (June 1st to July 15th, 2025). A notification of the consultation was disseminated to the WAO Centers of Excellence involved in food allergy and to representatives of the 95 WAO constituent national societies.

All submissions were reviewed by the steering committee, with comments being assessed qualitatively using an iterative thematic approach and categorized according to the 7 Delphi domains. Proposed revisions to the manuscript were discussed with the expert panel and implemented when comments introduced (i) novel concepts not previously addressed or (ii) disagreements with the consensus statements.

Results

We invited 36 experts to participate in the Delphi process; 1 expert declined due to employer restrictions, resulting in a final panel of 35 participants. All experts completed every round of the Delphi.

The panel included a broad range of professional roles, with the majority (60%) being clinicians. In summary, all statements related to Importance of the Issue, reached consensus already in the first round, with minimal neutrality and disagreement, and were dropped before round 3. For Improving the process informing PAL application, consensus was also reached for all statements in round 1, although the agreement levels were slightly more variable compared to the first domain (ranging from 77% to 97% at round 1). Six of the 8 statements were dropped after round 2, with the remaining 2 progressing to round 3 following clarifying edits suggested by the panel.

The domain What cut-off should be used as an action level exhibited the widest spread in judgements. Nine of 11 statements reached consensus in round 1, and ten by round 3. The main areas of debate concerned the use of fixed concentration thresholds for action levels (10 ppm and 5 ppm), and did not reach consensus in round 1. Consensus against 5 ppm threshold was reached by round 2, whereas the 10 ppm threshold remained contentious across rounds, though with judgements leaning toward disagreement. By contrast, statements supporting the use of a reference dose based on ED05 achieved consensus in round 1.

Regarding the choice of wording used to communicate PAL, May contain ‘X’ consistently emerged as the preferred option, increasing from 52% preference in round 1–71% by round 3. May contain traces of ‘X’ and Not suitable for people with an allergy to ‘X’ were judged respectively as the second-best (57% votes as second-best by round 3) and third-best (40% votes as third-best by round 3) options. There was less consensus on the need to communicate residual low risk due to UAP (there is UAP, but below the action level needed for PAL). Specifically, the statement that residual risk should be communicated achieved consensus in rounds 1 and 3, but not in round 2. The proposal to do this through a two-tier PAL approach consistently failed to achieve consensus across rounds.

With respect to ”Free-from” labels, mixed results were observed. Only 1 statement reached consensus already in round 1 and could be dropped after round 2, while the others achieved consensus only after round 2 or with relatively low agreement rates (neutrality up to 17% and disagreement up to 26%).

The statements related to Liability considerations for FBOs and Future research areas consistently reached a strong consensus across rounds.

Thematic analysis and public consultation

The analysis of participants' narrative feedback identified several overarching themes across the domains (Table 3). Key points included widespread concerns about the current confusion on PAL and its defensive overuse, underscoring the need for standardized practice and clearer communication. Participants broadly agreed on the necessity of mandatory, evidence-based risk assessment processes, though flexibility for small and medium enterprises was emphasized. A strong preference emerged for reference-dose-based action levels, with considerable disagreement towards fixed concentration thresholds (eg, 5 ppm or 10 ppm). The experts supported the need for clear and concise communication wording, with the label ”May contain ‘X’ being the preferred option. Still, the idea of introducing symbols to signal the conduction of a risk assessment, or multi-tier systems was highly debated. Then, the panelists supported the mitigation of liability for companies adhering to regulations and acknowledged complexities in “free-from” labelling practices, particularly for vegan and gluten-free products. Finally, the panel consistently highlighted the need for ongoing research, particularly to refine population-based thresholds that account for diverse patient characteristics and contexts.

A total of 25 submissions were received during the public consultation, including contributions from individual healthcare professionals and researchers (n = 10), representatives of professional societies and patients’ advocacy groups (n = 9), and members of FBOs (n = 6). We included a structured list and summary of all comments received during the public consultation, including interest-holder categorization and corresponding manuscript changes, in Suppl. Appendix 9.

Discussion

This Delphi study explored the insight of an international panel of experts using a comprehensive set of statements covering multiple aspects of PAL practice, aiming to provide a structured conceptual priority framework to support future harmonization. The process reached a stable consensus on 33 of the 35 proposed statements, which focused on (i) the circumstances and risk thresholds under which PAL should be applied, (ii) PAL terminology and communication practices, and (iii) key principles for implementing PAL within regulatory systems, highlighting consumer protection and procedural feasibility. The consensus suggested a strong international need for PAL reform and alignment, and underscored several priority areas for action. Narrative feedback collected across the Delphi rounds and through public consultation highlighted important conceptual and practical challenges that future research and policy development should address.

An emerging priority is establishing PAL practices around a structured, evidence-based process. Participants strongly endorsed adopting mandatory and ideally quantitative risk assessment based on population-based reference doses such as ED05 to guide the application of PAL. There was a clear preference for ED05 over ED01 or ‘zero tolerance’ approaches, reflecting the need to balance scientific rigor and transparency with implementation flexibility and consideration of current analytical capabilities.38 The importance of a flexible approach was highlighted during the public consultation, with many food business operators having proactively adopted a layered, risk-based approach to PAL, promoted by FoodDrinkEurope.39

In parallel with the call for structured risk assessment, participants emphasized the urgency of clarifying and simplifying PAL communication to rebuild consumer trust. Specifically, the endorsement of “May contain ‘X’” as the preferred standard wording reflects a preference for simplicity, immediacy, and broad understandability – something consistent with patient surveys.9

A related issue is whether there is a need to communicate potential risk to more sensitive allergic consumers who might react to levels of UAP below the reference dose and thus would not require PAL labeling. While most reactions to this exposure would be subjective (by definition, objective reactions would be expected in fewer than 5% of allergic individuals), consumers would still seek reassurance and require guidance to interpret the residual risk. Still, this is not an insignificant group, with up to 30% of allergic individuals possibly experiencing subjective symptoms from exposures below ED05, and up to 5% having an objective reaction, although most of these reactions are expected to be limited to mild symptoms and unlikely to progress to more severe reactions.40 Despite 70% agreement (after 3 Delphi rounds) on the need to communicate residual risk (where UAP is below the RfD), there was no consensus on how to achieve this, thus reinforcing the conclusion of the FAO/WHO Expert consultation, that “further work is needed as to how to ensure these individuals can receive appropriate information to make informed safe food choices”. A suggestion of a tiered approach to PAL was not supported due to concerns over complexity. An alternative approach, also considering the potential cost burden of additional pack labelling, was to use barcoding to access additional online information on the risk assessment and level of residual risk. Suggestions from the panel included to strengthen complementary strategies, such as (i) targeted education to raise awareness among both allergic consumers (and those buying food for them) and healthcare professionals regarding the application and interpretation of PAL (Credit to Allergy & Anaphylaxis Australia (A&AA); see feedback, Suppl. Appendix 9), and (ii) promoting the active engagement of healthcare providers. Specifically, the panel emphasized the latter's role in identifying and providing appropriate advice to allergic consumers who might react to sub-ED05 levels, for example, through low-level, single-dose challenges. This would also provide important reassurance to the vast majority of food-allergic individuals that they are protected.41,42

Both the Delphi process and subsequent public consultation revealed underlying concerns over the current legal landscape of PAL application. Defensive overuse of PAL by manufacturers (driven by brand protection and fears of litigation) was identified as a key factor undermining trustworthiness of PAL. Participants strongly supported the idea that liability protection for compliant FBOs could foster collaboration toward an evidence-based process and transparent risk communication. However, such protection would need to be conditional on demonstrating adherence to rigorous assessment and documentation standards. In this context, the VITAL standards43 program in Australia/New Zealand has been developed to support businesses in implementing structured risk assessment approaches, demonstrating compliance and therefore mitigating legal liability.

With respect to “free-from” claims, the Delphi participants broadly agreed that such labels should ideally imply the complete absence of the stated allergen, including UAP, and therefore should not be accompanied by PAL. At the same time, panellists noted that zero-tolerance may not be achievable in practice. Nevertheless, the coexistence of “free-from” claims and PAL was widely considered confusing. As a result, the Delphi panel and the public consultation concluded that food businesses producing “free-from” products should implement appropriate measures to minimize UAP and thus avoid the need for PAL. Similar confusion can occur with respect to “lifestyle” food claims such as “suitable for vegans”. These labels are often used by people with food allergies to animal-derived allergens to make food choices, yet evidence shows that vegan claims cannot be reliably used for this purpose,22 and often vegan claims occur with PAL. Both panellists and respondents to the public consultation commented on the need to clearly distinguish between allergen-specific “free-from” claims (ideally intended to support risk management for allergic consumers) from “lifestyle” food labels (eg, “vegan”). One possible approach, supported by panellists, is that foods with vegan claims should not have PAL to the implicated allergens (ie, any UAP must be lower than the applicable RfD). Also, additional effort is needed to educate food-allergic individuals, food businesses, and the public that vegan products may not automatically be “safe” for those allergic to animal-derived proteins. A further issue raised during the public consultation is the increasing availability of food products containing animal-protein analogs produced through precision fermentation and thus considered as “animal-free”/vegan (credit to Myrthe van den Dunge, DSM-Firmenich; Suppl. Appendix 9). These proteins are structurally similar to their animal-derived counterparts, and can elicit reactions in susceptible individuals.44,45

A further anomaly was noted with respect to gluten-free labelling. It is the only “free-from” claim that has an international, although inconsistently applied, definition: in Europe, North America and in Codex, “gluten-free” is defined as ≤20 ppm gluten, while in Australia and New Zealand, it is defined as no detectable gluten. Individuals with IgE-mediated wheat allergy often use “gluten-free” as a proxy for wheat avoidance. However, more sensitive individuals may still react to products meeting the ≤20 ppm threshold, particularly when consumed in larger portions, even if exposures are typically below ED05 (Fig. 2). This reflects the fact that gluten-free labelling is designed to protect individuals with coeliac disease, with an emphasis on chronic exposure.46 Given the higher prevalence of coeliac disease compared with IgE-mediated wheat allergy, the panel observed that a pragmatic approach would be to educate wheat-allergic individuals to help them interpret gluten-free labels correctly. While there was consensus that, from an allergy perspective, gluten-free claims should be more restrictive (ie, no gluten present), adopting a more stringent definition could further restrict food choices for individuals with coeliac disease, as highlighted during the public consultation (Suppl. Appendix 9), although evidence supporting this concern remains limited. Similarly, gluten-free products often carry PAL for gluten-containing grains. This can occur when large portion sizes (>200 g) result in a total exposure exceeding the RfD, even when compliance with the ≤20 ppm criterion is met. Therefore, in line with the recent FAO/WHO Expert Consultation on PAL for gluten-containing grains,46 the panel agreed that such scenarios should be minimized through appropriate risk assessment and management, and that wheat-allergic individuals should be informed of the risk, particularly when consuming larger portions of gluten-free foods.

- Illustrative proportion of individuals with wheat-allergy who could react to gluten-free foods, assuming the product contains 20 ppm gluten. Gluten exposure was estimated by multiplying the food size by 20 mg gluten/kg food. For example, a typical 250 g gluten-free pizza base might comply with the 20 ppm threshold but contain 5 mg of gluten protein, an amount that could elicit an objective reaction in approximately 2–7% of wheat-allergic individuals.47 Because wheat-allergy reference doses are expressed as total wheat protein, and gluten represents only part of total wheat protein, this gluten-based calculation may underestimate total wheat-protein exposure. Thus, gluten-free labelling may not always be protective for the most sensitive wheat-allergic people

Even for the Delphi items with strong consensus, the discussion highlighted ambiguities and future challenges. These included using evidence from cross-reactivity patterns to determine an RfD where challenge data are lacking for a particular allergen; addressing cumulative exposures across multiple foods; and estimating variations in portion sizes, both within and across countries. Selecting an appropriate portion-size estimate is a practical challenge for risk-based PAL, as larger portion sizes increase potential UAP exposure. The FAO/WHO expert consultation recommended using the p50 (median portion size) based on consumption data, although this may vary across countries, and estimates may not exist for some populations. Therefore, establishing internationally harmonized food consumption datasets, based on standardized methodology, is important (credit to Giulia Innocenzi, Ferrero, Italy; Suppl. Appendix 9). While the panel acknowledged that PAL application should be informed by ‘‘real-world’’ estimates of food consumption, where such data are absent the serving sizes for nutritional labelling could be considered so long as these are realistic.

Another implementation challenge concerns the monitoring of regulatory compliance, as current strategies may not reliably detect particulate contamination, which can lead to heterogeneous allergen distribution and localized “hot spots.” This highlights an important limitation of current sampling strategies for identifying (and monitoring) true exposure risk, underscoring the need for improved approaches to capture real-world contamination patterns.

Finally, as highlighted by both Delphi panellists and the public consultation, PAL communication is inherently context- and language-sensitive. Therefore, multilingual and cross-cultural validation will be required to ensure consistent interpretation. In parallel, culturally and linguistically adapted educational initiatives for both healthcare providers and allergic consumers will be essential (credit to Panagiotis Chaslaridis, EFA; Suppl. Appendix 9).

Strengths and limitations

This work has several strengths. First, we engaged a broad international panel of allergy clinicians, supported by methodologists, food scientists, risk analysts, regulators, and patient representatives, capturing perspectives across multiple disciplines involved in managing food allergen risks. The public consultation further strengthened the study, yielding relevant practical considerations to help contextualize the results of this consensus. Second, the final panel size exceeded the minimum recommended threshold for Delphi studies while adhering to pre-specified eligibility criteria, allowing for comprehensive and diverse conclusions without compromising the integrity of the process. In addition, the Delphi was preceded by an evidence synthesis effort, which, although non-systematic, enabled all panel members to share a good common understanding of the topic, hence maximizing the probability of providing informed input.

From a methodological perspective, the use of an e-Delphi approach with asynchronous consultation was well suited to integrating diverse perspectives across geographic and professional settings. Also, the adherence to established Delphi methodological principles helped minimize bias related to group dynamics and ensured that individual viewpoints were adequately represented. Finally, the use of 2 complementary input streams (Delphi panel and public consultation) partially mitigated potential response bias associated with recruitment through the WAO professional networks, thereby enhancing the overall credibility and generalizability of the findings.

Yet, there are several limitations to acknowledge. Certain regions, particularly low- and middle-income countries, were underrepresented in both the Delphi panel and the public consultation, which may limit the global generalizability of our considerations. In addition, the panel was predominantly composed of clinicians, potentially underrepresenting the perspectives of other interest-holder groups. This imbalance also limited our ability to explore differences in opinions across professional categories, which should be considered as a priority for next research efforts. Furthermore, panel recruitment partly relied on professional networks and involved inviting participants as contributors to the work, which, while on the one hand resulted in a strong panel engagement with a complete response rate across rounds, on the other it may have introduced a degree of commitment bias.

Conclusion

The Delphi process, conducted with an international expert panel, explored perspectives and reached consensus on statements addressing key aspects of PAL practice. It was recognized that PAL is a multifaceted process that extends beyond the simple application of allergen warnings on food packaging and involves considerations of risk assessment methodology, communication strategies, regulatory frameworks, and consumer protection. Alt-text: Box 1

Several areas of strong agreement emerged that could inform future development of a harmonized PAL framework. In particular, participants endorsed the current recommendations from the recent FAO/WHO Codex Expert Consultation regarding reference dose-based action levels to guide PAL use, emphasizing the importance of evidence-based, population-adjusted thresholds rather than arbitrary fixed concentrations. There was also a clear consensus on the preference for simple, standardized PAL wording, with “May contain ‘X’ identified as the preferred format. The need for a mandatory, structured risk assessment to inform PAL use, preferably quantitative, was strongly supported. In addition, the panel emphasized the importance of establishing a “living” framework, with periodic revisions and validation of the evidence base underlying food consumption data, action level thresholds, and resulting PAL estimates. Experts also supported the idea that liability protection should be formalised for compliant FBOs, together with a clear delineation of interest-holder responsibilities. At the same time, several challenges were identified that must be addressed to improve the effective use of PAL. A recurring theme was the need for structured, third-party action to ensure legally-mandated implementation, standardized technical guidelines, and training support for both industry, consumers and healthcare providers. Particular attention should then be given to implementation in low- and middle-income countries, where infrastructure, regulatory, and educational constraints may require adapted and phased approaches.

Overall, this Delphi provides a structured, consensus-based foundation for advancing a harmonized, risk-based approach to PAL. The next step will be to translate these concepts into actionable priorities for research and policy development through a sustained collaboration between consumers, healthcare professionals, FBOs, regulators, and scientific organizations. The WAO aims to facilitate this process by supporting international alignment and encouraging implementation strategies that can be adapted across diverse settings.
