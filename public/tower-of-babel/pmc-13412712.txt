Improving Communication Around Discharge Readiness at a Satellite Children’s Hospital: A Single-site Improvement Initiative
Authors: Brittany B. Hubbell, Stephanie Allen, Yamini Kuchipudi, Emily Schmidlin, Deanna Verdin, Madison Archer, Jennifer Albrecht, Eric Mailloux, Christine M. White, Angela M. Statile, Lisa E. Herrmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Discharge efficiency enhances hospital throughput, enabling timely, appropriate care while improving patient/caregiver experience and provider satisfaction. Prior efforts at our institution focused on implementing a discharge workflow based on the early identification of medical discharge goals. However, our satellite hospital team lacked consistent nurse-driven discharge activation when patients met these goals, which could delay the identification of discharge readiness. We aimed to increase the percentage of patients for whom nurses identified discharge readiness and activated the discharge process from 28% to 60% within 6 months.Methods:A multidisciplinary team applied quality improvement methods to identify key drivers and implement targeted interventions. Interventions included process education, structured communication during rounds, targeted feedback and email reminders, scheduled rounding times, and use of conditional discharge orders. We tracked the primary measure, the percentage of patients with nurse-driven discharge activation, using a statistical process control chart. Secondary measures included the percentage of patients with nurse-documented discharge readiness, the percentage of patients discharged within 2 hours of meeting medical discharge goals, and hospital length of stay. We monitored 7-day readmission rates as a balancing measure.Results:The mean percentage of patients with nurse-driven discharge activation increased to 67% and remained above this level for more than 8 months. The percentage of patients with nurse-documented discharge readiness improved from 71% to 86%; the mean percentage of patients discharged within 2 hours of nurse-identified readiness increased from 85% to 89%. Length of stay and 7-day readmission rates were unchanged.Conclusions:Through multidisciplinary efforts to improve process transparency and collaborative communication frameworks, we enhanced nurse-driven discharge activation at a satellite children’s hospital.

INTRODUCTION

The hospital discharge process should be a safe, efficient transition.1,2 Discharge efficiency enhances hospital throughput, enabling timely care while improving patient/caregiver experience and provider satisfaction.2–5 Prior work at our institution established a discharge process based on standardized discharge criteria, termed “Medically Ready for Discharge” (MRD), that focused on early identification of medically appropriate discharge goals and aimed to discharge patients within 2 hours of meeting those goals.6–8 The admitting provider initiates the process by defining medical discharge goals upon admission via an electronic health record (EHR) order; providers modify goals as needed throughout hospitalization. The nurse indicates that the patient has met final goals via an EHR timestamp, notifies the provider, who then places the discharge order, and the patient is discharged (Fig. 1A). This patient-focused process bases discharge readiness on clinical improvement rather than on discharging by a set time of day and empowers nurses to activate the discharge process.9 Our institution implemented the process for patients admitted to the pediatric hospital medicine (HM) service,8 then adapted it for patients with medical complexity7 and specialty patients6; it has since been spread across our institution, including to our satellite hospital.

We implemented the MRD process in our satellite hospital medical-surgical unit, where efficient discharge is critical given short lengths of stay (LOS) and high turnover. However, as the hospital expanded in capacity and volume, we observed a drift in adherence. Specifically, nurses no longer consistently communicated when patients met discharge goals to activate the discharge process, as evidenced by discharge-readiness timestamps entered after the provider had already placed the discharge order. We presumed these late entries were procedural rather than reflective of real-time discharge activation, highlighting missed opportunities to communicate discharge readiness earlier. In response, we applied improvement science to strengthen team communication and empower nurses to activate discharge workflows, ensuring timely, efficient patient transitions for HM patients at the satellite children’s hospital. We aimed to increase the percentage of patients for whom nurses identified discharge readiness and activated the discharge process from 28% to 60% within 6 months.

METHODS

Context

This improvement study took place at the community satellite campus of a freestanding, quaternary-care children’s hospital. This freestanding pediatric inpatient and outpatient facility is located 20 miles from the main campus. Pediatric nurses staff the 22-bed medical-surgical unit and admit patients to multiple services, including HM, adolescent medicine, gastroenterology, and general surgery. The HM service, in which patient care is provided by HM attendings, fellows, advanced practice providers, pediatric and family medicine residents, and medical students, admits approximately 1,800 patients each year. The hospital system uses the Epic EHR (Epic Systems, Verona, WI).

Interventions

In October 2020, we formed an improvement team and announced the project. A bedside nurse and HM attending coled the team, which included nurses, the nursing clinical director, advanced practice providers, pediatric residents, quality improvement (QI) experts, and other HM physicians. We mapped ideal processes for nurses and providers based on the original MRD process.8 Then, we conducted a failure mode and effects analysis10 for nurse-driven discharge activation failures. These steps informed our key drivers: (1) understanding the MRD workflow, (2) awareness of the benefits of capturing discharge delays after patients met medical goals, (3) nursing self-efficacy in the discharge process, and (4) effective bidirectional communication (Fig. 2).

Discharge Process Education

We identified a shared understanding of the discharge workflow among all care team members as a key driver of success. Prior work leveraging EHR order set functionality6–8 ensured providers consistently defined medical discharge goals at admission. Inconsistent execution of the remaining process stemmed from a lack of clarity about the logistics and rationale of the discharge process. To address this gap, we developed targeted educational campaigns for nurses (February 2021) and medical providers (March 2021). The education featured EHR screenshots to demonstrate an ideal discharge workflow based on medical discharge goals (eg, supplemental oxygen discontinued for 6 h). Nonphysiological tasks (eg, filling prescriptions, arranging transportation) should begin before the patient meets medical discharge goals. Nurse education also highlighted nurses’ key role in identifying and communicating discharge readiness to activate the discharge process, providing a foundation for nurse self-efficacy, one’s belief in one’s own capability to perform a task successfully.11 The presentations also defined the benefits of capturing discharge delays after patients met medical goals, framing these delays as learning opportunities rather than failures.

Nurse education was completed by 52% of nurses, tracked via pre- and postassessment surveys as part of a concurrent initiative. We distributed slides via email, posting in workrooms, nurse and provider virtual meetings, and in clinical file-sharing repositories. We incorporated formal education into the new-team-member orientation for nurses and providers.

Structured Nurse Communication During Rounds

The team identified the key driver of effective bidirectional communication between providers and nurses and sought to standardize nurse input during rounds. In March 2021, we instructed bedside nurses to report patients’ medical discharge goals via EHR order during family-centered rounds (FCR) to promote a shared understanding of these goals. Nurses used a paper rounding sheet, informed by nurse and physician focus groups, to standardize reporting. In addition to the identified discharge goals, the sheet included 2 prompts: (1) questions or concerns from the nurse or patient/family and (2) orders needing clarification. The overnight nurse provided this information to the daytime nurse during handoff. Physicians invited nurses to present this structured communication in patient rooms at the start of FCR.12 We adopted the structured nurse communication during rounds.

Regular Reminders and Targeted Feedback

To maintain clinician engagement, we frequently updated providers on the improvement work beginning in April 2021. Attending physicians beginning clinical service received an email with reminders to review discharge goals, an example script to invite nurses’ participation during FCR, and tips for navigating the EHR discharge workflow. The team shared project data in monthly provider meetings and biweekly nursing emails.

We conducted random audits to verify providers’ use of objective physiological discharge goals in the EHR and whether nurses were timestamping their achievement upon meeting those goals. The nurse and physician coleaders each conducted real-time audits to provide feedback during a team member’s shift, with an estimated 3–4 team members receiving feedback several times per week. Nurses and providers who had not followed the intended process received feedback to review best practices. Provider feedback included instruction to correct nonspecific or inaccurate discharge criteria (eg, if the admitting diagnosis had changed). Nurses received feedback to seek clarification from the provider if discharge criteria were unclear and to record the timestamp when their patients met discharge goals. This intervention continued through August 2021, after which we shifted our efforts.

Scheduled Rounds

Ad hoc nursing feedback revealed that unpredictable nurse availability for rounds led to inconsistent communication. Although providers were encouraged to send secure text messages at the start of each patient’s rounds, these messages were unreliable and often provided insufficient notice, leading to poor nurse attendance. Nurse presence during rounds was critical for effective team communication and shared understanding of patient status and discharge progress. In August 2021, the team piloted a daily rounding schedule that listed the sequence and estimated time for each patient. The senior resident documented the schedule on paper and shared it with the nursing staff via secure text photograph. This advanced notice allowed nurses to plan their tasks accordingly to join rounds. We instructed providers to notify each bedside nurse 1 patient before their scheduled rounding time to plan for arrival. We adopted the rounding schedule intervention to facilitate nurse communication during rounds.

Encouraging Use of Conditional Discharge Orders

In June 2021, the improvement team began manually reviewing cases when the nurse discharge-readiness timestamp occurred after the discharge order. In some cases, providers placed a prospective “conditional” discharge order before the patient had met discharge goals, specifying criteria for the nurse to discharge the patient (eg, after the first dose of oral antibiotics). This order empowered nurses to initiate discharge once patients met the specified goals, without requiring further correspondence with the medical team13 (Fig. 1B), but it resulted in a timestamp that was out of sequence relative to the discharge order. Although not a new process, conditional discharges had been used inconsistently before this work. In September 2021, our improvement team identified a positive association between increasing census and the use of conditional discharge orders. We encouraged teams to use this process regularly to ensure clear discharge communication and to expedite throughput during periods of high census. In line with the MRD workflow, we also encouraged teams to use the patient’s MRD goals as discharge conditions. We adopted the intervention of promoting conditional discharge orders.

Study of the Interventions

We used plan-do-study-act cycles to iteratively design and evaluate our interventions.10 We extracted data from the EHR for all discharged HM patients, including the nurse-documented discharge-readiness timestamp, the provider discharge order date/time, and the hospital discharge date/time. A separate EHR report detailed the discharge orders and comments related to discharge conditions. We retrospectively collected baseline discharge data for a period of 6 months (July–December 2020), encompassing 553 discharges. Following the initial intervention, we prospectively collected data for 13 months—8 months of active improvement work and 5 months of sustainability monitoring—during which we captured 1,555 additional discharges.

Measures

The primary measure was the percentage of HM patients for whom the nurse identified discharge readiness and activated the discharge process. We defined process success as either a nurse timestamp placed within 5 minutes of a provider discharge order or a conditional discharge order specifying medical conditions for discharge. Process failures included missing nurse timestamps or timestamps placed more than 5 minutes after the provider discharge order, without eligible discharge conditions. We reviewed the content of discharge orders and excluded those with nonphysiological criteria (eg, “when transportation is available”). Process measures included the rate of nurse-time-stamped documentation (irrespective of provider discharge-order timing) and the frequency of conditional discharge orders over time.

Our secondary measures included the percentage of HM patients with a documented nurse timestamp who were discharged from the hospital within 2 hours of meeting discharge goals and admission LOS. To ensure that our work expediting discharge did not negatively affect readmissions, we monitored 7-day readmissions as a balancing metric.

Analysis

We used statistical process control (SPC) p-charts to monitor the discharge process over time and followed median LOS on a run chart.13–15 As we tracked the effect of interventions on our measures, we used established rules for identifying special causes to detect centerline shifts, specifically 8 points on one side of the centerline, which would occur by chance less than 0.4% of the time, and the aggregate point rule, which applies a 3-sigma criterion to a group of data points on one side of the centerline.13–16 We also monitored readmission rate, nurse timestamp documentation rate, and conditional discharge orders on SPC p-charts.

Ethical Considerations

This QI initiative aimed at improving the local quality of care. It does not constitute human subject research and did not require review by our institutional review board per institutional protocols.

RESULTS

The mean percentage of patients with successful nurse-driven discharge activation was initially 28% when excluding delayed timestamps but increased to 40% after an ad hoc analysis that applied an expanded operational definition, including conditional discharge orders, to the baseline study period. This measure increased from 40% to 67% between October 2020 and May 2021 and was sustained for 9 months (Fig. 3). We observed special-cause variation, resulting in the first centerline shift after the project was announced, likely due to increased attention to the MRD process. After reinforcing awareness through education and reminders, we again observed special-cause variation, resulting in another centerline shift above goal. We saw sustained improvement with scheduling rounds, which facilitated structured nurse communication and encouraged the use of conditional discharge orders. We sustained improvement for 8 months, including 4 months after the interventions.

In accordance with previously established work,8 the secondary measure of patients with a nurse-documented timestamp who were discharged within 2 hours of meeting medical discharge goals was initially 85% and increased to 89% during the intervention period (Fig. 4). Median LOS (0.96 d) and 7-day readmission rate (2.5%) remained unchanged after project implementation. The baseline rate of nurse timestamp documentation (71%) increased to 86% after the project announcement. It sustained throughout the study period (Fig. 5). The proportion of conditional discharge orders relative to conventional discharge orders increased from a mean of 21% before the intervention to 33% after (Fig. 6).

DISCUSSION

Using the Model for Improvement,10 we designed, implemented, and studied interventions to improve nurse-driven discharge activation for HM patients admitted to our satellite hospital from 40% to 67%. We saw improved nurse documentation of discharge readiness, from 71% to 86%, and increased discharge efficiency, from 85% to 89%, with 89% of patients discharged within 2 hours of documented discharge readiness. Although there was no change in the already low median LOS, this work demonstrated the effect of team-based interventions on process adherence, with the potential to improve discharge efficiency and enhance communication. Importantly, our interventions did not lead to an increase in readmissions.

Our study focused on empowering bedside nurses to assess discharge readiness and independently initiate discharge workflows. Prior local improvement work that implemented the MRD process, followed by its expansion to complex patient populations, established the elements necessary for a successful process: a standardized, multidisciplinary discharge approach, shared mental models of patients’ clinical progress, and frontline provider engagement.6–8 Several studies have since demonstrated the impact of standardized discharge criteria for general inpatient pediatric wards17 and, in specialized pediatric populations,18,19 have improved discharge processes. Other documented strategies include an EHR tool to provide visibility of progress toward discharge readiness, with a similar goal of enhancing team communication and task prioritization,20 and application of a nurse-initiated conditional discharge process, which led to increased discharges before noon, though it did not significantly change LOS.21

Our focus on strategies to enhance team communication and improve nurse activation of the discharge workflow contributes to this literature. Nurses play an important role in family communication, which can impact the transition home. Family perceptions of discharge readiness correlate with decreased likelihood of readmission.22 Additionally, our study’s setting in a satellite children’s hospital expands existing literature on discharge efficiency for pediatric patients at academic tertiary care institutions.6–8,17–19,21,23 Our interventions to engage frontline nurses and promote self-efficacy were instrumental in achieving and sustaining improvements. Efficient discharge processes and effective team communication are paramount, as our satellite hospital helps relieve system strain by addressing overflow needs and providing care for less medically complex, higher turnover patients.24

Discharge process education provided the foundation for nurses and providers to effectively fulfill their roles, resulting in a centerline shift in our primary measure. Additionally, multidisciplinary interventions enhanced bidirectional nurse-physician communication regarding discharge goals, including efforts to optimize nurses’ participation in FCR.12,25 Aligning with the original MRD process design, in which nurses drive discharge-readiness identification, we engaged frontline nurses in developing the structured nurse communication during rounds. However, nurse feedback revealed inconsistent nurse presence on FCR to share this communication. By scheduling rounds, we prioritized nurse presence and contribution to the care plan, leading to sustained improvement.

Providers were initially apprehensive about disruptions to FCR and delays caused by waiting for nurses to be available. To mitigate these concerns, we encouraged providers to notify nurses early via secure text, inviting them to join FCR. Nurses reported initial hesitation to speak during FCR; feedback indicated that being “invited to contribute” fostered greater psychological safety than simply being “notified to attend.” We shared this insight with providers to promote a collaborative environment. Enhancing nurses’ sense of being valued contributors may have improved their self-efficacy in the discharge process.

This project also demonstrated the value of using real-time data to learn from failures and identify solutions, such as conditional discharge orders, that could benefit the system when broadly adopted. As occurs during improvement work, a natural shift in clinician processes identified an opportunity to further promote strong team communication and discharge efficiency. Conditional discharges rely on clear team communication and emphasize nurses’ active roles in identifying discharge readiness. With encouraging use of conditional discharge orders, we saw sustained improvement above our initial goal.

This study has limitations. Generalizability may be limited given the small unit size and short LOS; however, our focus on nurse self-efficacy and team communication has relevance in other contexts, particularly where process adherence is essential to achieving desired outcomes. Though a low-reliability intervention, education provided the necessary context for team members and a basis for future interventions. We used elements of our primary measure of nurse-driven discharge activation—the timely nurse timestamp or the conditional discharge order—as proxy measures of communication and understanding of discharge goals. Additionally, we did not directly measure the frequency of nurse presence during rounds or adherence to structured communication; instead, we assessed these through nurses’ subjective feedback. The prior practice of nurses placing the EHR timestamp after the provider’s discharge order may have led to a falsely high measure of discharges within 2 hours of meeting discharge goals. However, the improvement in the measure pre- to postintervention argues against this. Although maintaining consistency with FCR was a priority, our study lacked direct patient and family perspectives, which are important to consider when assessing interventions that affect team communication.

CONCLUSIONS

Using QI methods, we enhanced nurse-driven discharge activation at our satellite children’s hospital. Despite a well-established system for setting discharge goals, opportunities remained to strengthen the process by ensuring that all stakeholders understood their roles and felt empowered. Multidisciplinary collaboration was essential to drive improvement through increasing transparency, securing stakeholder buy-in, promoting nurse self-efficacy, and improving team communication. Future directions include evaluating patient and family perspectives, ensuring equitable implementation, assessing the process in populations with longer LOS and at other community sites, and exploring financial impact.
