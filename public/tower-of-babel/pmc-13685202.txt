Comparative evaluation of the nutritional composition, antioxidant capacity and inhibitory activities against α-amylase and α-glucosidase of three Nigerian leafy vegetables (Amaranthus hybridus L., Celosia argentea L. and Solanum macrocarpon L.)
Authors: Oluwasegun Joseph Anjorin, Kayode Olayele Karigidi, Mary Olaoluwa Agunloye, Charles O. Olaiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndigenous leafy vegetables are important components of diet and are recognized for their nutritional value and bioactive phytochemicals. Their antioxidant properties and ability to inhibit carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes may contribute to the regulation of postprandial glucose levels and reduction of oxidative stress. This study evaluated the nutritional composition, antioxidant capacity, and inhibitory activities against α-amylase and α-glucosidase of the leaves of Amaranthus hybridus L. (AH), Celosia argentea L. (CA), and Solanum macrocarpon L. (SM), three commonly consumed Nigerian leafy vegetables.MethodProximate composition and mineral contents were determined in the dried leaves using standard analytical procedures. Methanolic leaf extracts were evaluated for total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, total antioxidant capacity, reducing power, Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity, metal-chelating activity, and inhibitory activities against α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes.ResultsAH, CA, and SM exhibited appreciable nutritional value and mineral composition, with significant differences (p < 0.05) observed for most parameters evaluated. AH contained the highest crude protein (22.08 g/100 g), crude fibre (18.02 g/100 g), ash (9.25 g/100 g), estimated metabolizable energy (275.54 kcal/100 g dry weight), and higher concentrations of most mineral elements. CA exhibited the highest total flavonoid content (5.84 mg QE/g extract), while total phenolic content ranged from 11.27 to 12.07 mg GAE/g extract with no significant differences among the vegetables. All extracts demonstrated concentration-dependent DPPH radical scavenging, metal-chelating, α-amylase inhibitory, and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities. Among the vegetables, AH exhibited the strongest overall antioxidant activity, with the lowest DPPH IC₅₀ (250.15 ± 8.45 µg/mL) and metal-chelating IC₅₀ (952.28 ± 10.56 µg/mL). Enzyme inhibitory activities also increased in a concentration-dependent manner, suggesting potential modulation of carbohydrate-digesting enzymes.ConclusionThe studied vegetables possess valuable nutritional properties and exhibit antioxidant and carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzyme inhibitory activities under in vitro conditions. These findings suggest that Amaranthus hybridus, Celosia argentea, and Solanum macrocarpon may serve as important dietary sources of nutrients and bioactive compounds with potential relevance to oxidative stress management and postprandial glycemic control. Further studies involving chromatographic characterization of individual compounds and in vivo investigations are required to confirm their biological significance.

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic-endocrine disorder of high prevalence, characterized by persistent hyperglycemia [1]. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) results from impaired insulin secretion, insulin resistance, or both, leading to disturbances in carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism [1–3]. The global burden of diabetes continues to increase, with recent estimates indicating that more than half a billion people are affected worldwide [4, 5]. Sustained hyperglycemia is associated with excessive generation of reactive oxygen species and oxidative stress, which contributes significantly to the development of diabetic complications, including cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, neuropathy, and retinopathy [6, 7]. One of the major contributors to postprandial hyperglycemia is the rapid digestion and absorption of dietary carbohydrates mediated by α-amylase and α-glucosidase, two key carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes [8–10]. Inhibition of these enzymes delays carbohydrate digestion and glucose absorption, thereby reducing postprandial blood glucose excursions [8–10]. Consequently, α-amylase and α-glucosidase have become important therapeutic targets for the dietary management of postprandial hyperglycemia associated with T2DM [11].

Vegetables constitute an essential component of a healthy diet owing to their high nutritional value and abundance of biologically active phytochemicals. They are important sources of dietary fibre, vitamins, minerals, phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and other phytochemicals that contribute to health promotion and disease prevention [12–14]. Epidemiological studies have associated regular vegetable consumption with a reduced risk of several chronic diseases, including diabetes mellitus, coronary heart diseases, and certain forms of cancer [15, 16]. In many sub-Saharan African countries, staple foods are predominantly cereals and root tubers, which are often limited in micronutrients. Therefore, incorporation of leafy vegetables into daily diets may improve dietary quality, enhance micronutrient intake, and help address hidden hunger and nutrient deficiencies [17]. In addition, numerous studies have reported antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antihypertensive, anticancer, and carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzyme inhibitory activities in various leafy vegetables, highlighting their potential in food security, disease prevention, and management of diseases [18–22].

Amaranthus hybridus L. (African spinach; locally known as Efo tete in southwestern Nigeria), Celosia argentea L. (Soko yokoto), and Solanum macrocarpon L. (African eggplant leaf; Efo gbagba) are among the most widely cultivated and consumed indigenous leafy vegetables in Nigeria and other tropical regions of Africa [1, 23–26]. These vegetables are valued for their affordability, year-round availability, adaptability to local agroecological conditions, and culinary versatility, particularly in the preparation of soups and vegetable-based dishes [1, 17, 20].

Amaranthus hybridus L. belongs to the family Amaranthaceae and is regarded as one of the most consumed leafy vegetables in Africa [23]. In traditional medicine, A. hybridus has been used to manage various ailments, including diabetes mellitus [27].

Similarly, Celosia argentea L., another member of the Amaranthaceae family, is an economically important leafy vegetable cultivated throughout tropical Africa and Asia [28]. Ethnomedicinally, it has been used in the management of conditions such as diarrhoea, jaundice, and mouth sores [29]. Experimental studies have also demonstrated the blood glucose-lowering potential of C. argentea leaf extracts in animal models, suggesting possible benefits in glycemic regulation [30].

Solanum macrocarpon L., a member of the family Solanaceae, is widely cultivated and consumed as both a leafy and fruit vegetable in tropical regions [1, 31]. Its leaves are recognized as valuable sources of dietary fibre, zinc, protein, fat and calcium [1, 32]. Previous studies have reported antioxidant properties and beneficial effects of S. macrocarpon extracts, which have been largely attributed to their phytochemical constituents [1, 33]. Such properties suggest that the vegetable may help reduce oxidative stress and support metabolic health.

Despite the widespread consumption of leafy vegetables in tropical regions, detailed comparative information on the extent to which commonly consumed Nigerian leafy vegetables contribute to the modulation of key biochemical pathways associated with postprandial glycemic regulation remains limited. There is insufficient integrated evidence linking their proximate composition, mineral content, antioxidant potential, and inhibitory activities against carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes under the same experimental conditions. Most existing studies have evaluated these vegetables in isolation or focused on either nutritional composition or antioxidant properties, without simultaneously assessing their functional potential in relation to α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition.

Furthermore, variations in analytical approaches, extraction methods, and assay conditions across previous reports make direct comparison of their biological activities difficult. This limits a clear understanding of their relative functional efficacy and potential contribution as dietary components in glycemic control and oxidative stress management. Therefore, there is a need for a standardized comparative evaluation of these underutilized yet widely consumed leafy vegetables to better understand their nutritional quality and potential functional properties. Such information is essential for supporting evidence-based dietary recommendations, improving awareness of their functional value, and promoting their inclusion in local diets as part of sustainable nutrition strategies in tropical populations.

Accordingly, the present study was designed to comparatively evaluate the nutritional composition, antioxidant capacity, and inhibitory activities against α-amylase and α-glucosidase of Amaranthus hybridus L., Celosia argentea L., and Solanum macrocarpon L. under identical experimental conditions. The study aims to provide a clearer scientific basis for their potential role as functional foods that may contribute to dietary antioxidant intake and support postprandial glycemic regulation.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and authentication

Fresh leaves of Amaranthus hybridus L. (AH), Celosia argentea L. (CA), and Solanum macrocarpon L. (SM) were purchased from Bodija Market, Ibadan, Oyo State, Nigeria. The vegetables were obtained directly from local farmers during the harvesting season and transported immediately to the laboratory for analysis. Botanical identification and authentication were carried out at the Department of Botany, University of Ibadan, Nigeria by Donatus Eskimashi. Voucher specimens were deposited in the University of Ibadan Herbarium (UIH) under the accession numbers SM: UIH-22,543, AH: UIH-22,545, and CA: UIH-22,544.

The vegetables were carefully inspected, and damaged, diseased, and shriveled leaves were discarded. The selected leaves were thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove adhering soil particles and other contaminants. The cleaned samples were air-dried under laboratory conditions (25 ± 2 °C) for three weeks. The dried samples were subsequently milled into fine powder using an electric blender (Qasa QBL-8008 pro2+) and stored in airtight containers until analysis.

All chemicals, reagents, and solvents used in this study were of analytical grade and were obtained from standard commercial suppliers.

Preparation of extracts for antioxidant and enzyme inhibitory assays

Powdered leaf samples (50 g) were separately extracted with 500 mL of methanol (1:10 w/v). Methanol was selected as the extraction solvent because of its high efficiency in extracting phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and other bioactive constituents commonly associated with antioxidant and enzyme inhibitory activities, and it has been widely used in phytochemical investigations of leafy vegetables. The extraction mixtures were maintained at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) and shaken intermittently for 72 h to facilitate solvent penetration and extraction of bioactive compounds. The extraction vessels were protected from direct light during the extraction period to minimize oxidative degradation of phytochemicals.

Following extraction, the mixtures were filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the filtrates were concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph Laborata 4000) at 40 °C. The concentrated extracts were transferred into airtight containers and stored at 4 °C until further analyses.

Determination of proximate composition

Proximate composition was determined according to standard procedures of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 2018) [34]. Moisture content, crude fat, crude protein (N × 6.25), crude fibre and ash contents were determined according to AOAC Official Methods 925.09, 922.06, 960.52, 991.43, and 923.39, respectively. All results were expressed on a dry weight basis (g/100 g dry weight).

The metabolizable energy value of each vegetable was estimated using Atwater conversion factors as follows: Energy (kcal/100 g) = (4 × protein) + (9 × fat) + (4 × carbohydrate).

Determination of mineral composition

Mineral composition was determined using dried leaf powder samples. The samples were subjected to wet digestion using a mixture of concentrated nitric acid and hydrochloric acid. The digested solutions were analysed using an Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (AAS) (Buck 200VGP, Buck Scientific, UK). Calibration curves were prepared using ISO-certified mineral standard solutions obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Inc, and mineral concentrations were calculated from the respective standard curves. Results were expressed as g/100 g dry weight for macro- minerals and ppm (mg/kg dry weight) for trace minerals.

In vitro antioxidant assays

All absorbance measurements were obtained using a UV-Visible spectrophotometer (Spectrum 752 S).

Determination of total phenolic content

Total phenolic content (TPC) was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method described by Singleton and Rossi [35] and modified by Kim et al. [36]. Briefly, 1 mL of sample extract (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 1 mL of 10% Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After 5 min, 5 mL of 7% sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) solution and 5 mL distilled water were added. The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 90 min, and absorbance was measured at 750 nm.

TPC was calculated from a gallic acid standard calibration curve (y = 1.137x + 0.0644, R2 = 0.9816) and expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of extract (mg GAE/g extract).

Determination of total flavonoid content

Total flavonoid content (TFC) was determined according to the method of Zhishen et al. [37]. as modified by Park et al. [38]. Briefly, 0.3 mL of sample extract (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 3.4 mL of 30% methanol, 0.15 mL of 0.5 M sodium nitrite (NaNO2), and 0.15 mL of 0.3 M aluminium chloride. After incubation for 5 min, 1 mL of 1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was added, and absorbance was measured at 506 nm.

TFC was calculated from a quercetin standard calibration curve (y = 0.691x + 0.01654, R2 = 0.9708) and expressed as milligrams of quercetin equivalents per gram of extract (mg QE/g extract).

Determination of total antioxidant capacity

Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was determined using the phosphomolybdenum method described by Prieto et al. [39]. Briefly, 0.5 mL of sample extract was mixed with 5 mL of reagent solution containing 0.6 M sulphuric acid, 28 mM sodium phosphate, and 4 mM ammonium molybdate. The tubes were capped and incubated in a boiling water bath (95 °C) for 90 min. After cooling to room temperature, absorbance was measured at 695 nm.

TAC was calculated from an ascorbic acid standard calibration curve (y = 2.3155x + 0.3773, R2 = 0.9611) and expressed as milligrams of ascorbic acid equivalents per gram of extract (mg AAE/g extract).

Determination of reducing power

Reducing power was determined according to the method of Oyaizu [40]. Briefly, 1 mL of sample extract was mixed with 1 mL phosphate buffer (0.2 M, pH 6.6) and 1 mL of 1% potassium ferricyanide solution. The mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 20 min, followed by the addition of 1 mL of 10% trichloroacetic acid to terminate the reaction.

An aliquot (1 mL) of the upper layer was mixed with 1 mL distilled water and 1 mL of 0.1% ferric chloride solution. Absorbance was measured at 700 nm. Results were calculated using an ascorbic acid calibration curve (y = 1.5572x + 0.6215, R2 = 0.9777) and expressed as mg AAE/g extract.

Determination of diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) scavenging activity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity of the extracts was determined using the method of Blois [41] as modified by Gyamfi et al. [42]. Different concentrations of the extracts (200–1000 µg/mL) were prepared. One millilitre of each concentration was mixed with 4 mL of DPPH solution prepared in methanol. The mixtures were incubated in the dark for 30 min and absorbance was measured at 520 nm.

Determination of metal-chelating activity

Metal-chelating activity was determined according to the method of Dinis et al. [43]. Briefly, 0.5 mL of 0.2 mM ferrous chloride solution was mixed with 0.2 mL of 5 mM ferrozine solution. Thereafter, 0.1 mL of extract (200–1000 µg/mL) was added and the mixture incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Absorbance was measured at 562 nm.

The IC₅₀ values were subsequently determined.

α-Amylase inhibitory activity

α-Amylase inhibitory activity was determined according to Worthington [44] with slight modifications. Briefly, 500 µL of sample extract (200–1000 µg/mL) was mixed with 500 µL of α-amylase solution (0.5 mg/mL) prepared in 0.02 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.9 containing 0.006 M sodium chloride). The mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. Thereafter, 500 µL of 1% soluble starch solution prepared in the same buffer was added and incubated for another 10 min.

The reaction was terminated by adding 1 mL of dinitrosalicylic acid reagent, followed by heating in a boiling water bath for 5 min. After cooling to room temperature, absorbance was measured at 540 nm. Acarbose was used as a positive control.

α-Glucosidase inhibitory activity

α-Glucosidase inhibitory activity was determined using the method of Apostolidis et al. [45]. Briefly, 500 µL of sample extract (200–1000 µg/mL) was mixed with 1000 µL of α-glucosidase solution (1.0 U/L) prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.9) and pre-incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 500 µL of 5 mM p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside solution prepared in the same buffer was added.

The reaction mixtures were incubated at 25 °C for 5 min and absorbance measured at 405 nm. Acarbose was used as a positive control.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of triplicate measurements. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Differences among means were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc test. Values were considered significantly different at p < 0.05. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 5.0.

Significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed among the vegetables for all proximate parameters evaluated. AH contained significantly higher crude protein (22.08 ± 0.11 g/100 g), ash (9.25 ± 0.03 g/100 g), and crude fibre (18.02 ± 0.02 g/100 g) contents than CA and SM. The crude fat content was highest in SM (3.87 g/100 g) and differed significantly (p < 0.05) from those of AH and CA.

Mineral composition of the leafy vegetables

AH contained significantly higher concentrations of calcium, magnesium, potassium, manganese, iron, copper and zinc than CA and SM. DPPH radical scavenging and metal-chelating activities

The DPPH radical scavenging and metal-chelating activities of the methanolic leaf extracts are presented in Fig. 1a and b, respectively. All extracts exhibited concentration-dependent increases in DPPH radical scavenging and metal-chelating activities across the tested concentration range (200–1000 µg/mL). For DPPH radical scavenging activity (Fig. 1a), AH consistently exhibited significantly (p < 0.05) higher percentage inhibition than CA and SM at all concentrations tested. At 1000 µg/mL, AH achieved approximately 70% inhibition, whereas CA and SM exhibited approximately 53% and 54% inhibition, respectively.

A similar concentration-dependent trend was observed for metal-chelating activity (Fig. 1b). Although CA and SM showed relatively higher inhibition than AH at some of the lower concentrations tested, AH exhibited the greatest chelating activity at the highest concentration (1000 µg/mL), reaching approximately 67% inhibition.

The IC₅₀ values derived from the concentration-response curves are presented in Table 4. Discussion

Leafy vegetables are important components of human diets because they provide essential nutrients and bioactive compounds associated with health promotion and disease prevention. In the present study, the nutritional composition, antioxidant capacity, and inhibitory activities against α-amylase and α-glucosidase of Amaranthus hybridus L., Celosia argentea L., and Solanum macrocarpon L. were comparatively evaluated to determine their potential contribution as nutrient-rich vegetables and sources of bioactive compounds associated with antioxidant activity and postprandial glycemic regulation.

The proximate composition analysis demonstrated that Amaranthus hybridus L., Celosia argentea L., and Solanum macrocarpon L. contain appreciable amounts of protein, dietary fibre, carbohydrates, and minerals, supporting their nutritional importance as edible leafy vegetables. Similar nutritional profiles have been reported for A. hybridus by Abiona et al. and Bando et al. for C. argentea by Arawande et al. and for S. macrocarpon by Eletta et al. indicating that these vegetables are valuable dietary sources of essential nutrients [25, 46–48]. Among the vegetables evaluated, A. hybridus exhibited significantly higher protein and fibre contents than C. argentea and S. macrocarpon. The protein contents obtained in the present study (19.32–22.08% of leafy vegetable dry weight) suggest that these vegetables may contribute meaningfully to dietary protein intake, particularly in populations where access to animal-derived protein sources is limited. Dietary proteins play indispensable roles in enzyme synthesis, tissue repair, growth, immune function, and maintenance of normal cellular processes, as reported by Corsetti et al. [49]. In addition, green leafy vegetables contain substantial amounts of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco), the most abundant leaf protein, which is highly digestible and serves as a valuable source of amino acids and bioactive peptide precursors, thereby enhancing the nutritional quality of leafy vegetables [50–52].

The relatively high fibre contents observed (15.84–18.02%) further enhance the nutritional value of these vegetables. Dietary fibre has been associated with improved gastrointestinal health, modulation of glucose absorption, enhanced satiety, and reduced risk of several metabolic disorders as reported by Yuan et al., Wu et al., Warrilow et al. [53–55]. The protein contents recorded in this study were higher than those reported for A. hybridus by Oyelola et al. (16.38%), Abiona et al. (15.72–17.70%), and Bando et al. (4.25 ± 0.10%) [24, 46, 47], but lower than the value reported by Ntuli (33.16%) for selected African leafy vegetables [56]. Similarly, the crude fibre contents obtained in the present study (15.84–18.02%) were higher than the 7.9% reported by Oyelola et al. [24]. and comparable to the range reported by Abiona et al. (16.89–19.53%) [46], although lower than the 41.21% reported by Wakili et al. [57]. for another leafy vegetable species. Comparisons with other African leafy vegetables reported by Ntuli, including Asystasia delagoensis (20.18%), Rhoicissus tomentosa (31.18%), and Ipomoea wightii (24.87%), further demonstrate the nutritional relevance of the vegetables investigated in the present study [56]. Variations in proximate composition among studies may be attributed to differences in plant genotype, environmental conditions, soil characteristics, agronomic practices, maturity stage at harvest, and post-harvest processing methods, all of which are known to influence nutrient accumulation in leafy vegetables as reported by Oluwole et al., Nsiah et al. and Agunloye et al. [58–60].

In addition to their protein and fibre contents, the vegetables contained appreciable amounts of carbohydrates and lipids, which contributed to their estimated metabolizable energy values. The energy values ranged from 268.25 to 275.54 kcal/100 g dry weight, with A. hybridus exhibiting the highest value, closely followed by S. macrocarpon. The relatively higher energy values observed in these vegetables may be attributed to their comparatively higher protein and lipid contents. Although leafy vegetables are generally consumed primarily for their micronutrients and phytochemicals rather than as major energy sources, their contribution to dietary energy intake may be beneficial, particularly in resource-limited settings where dietary diversification is constrained. The energy values obtained in this study are comparable to those reported for C. argentea by Arawande et al. (297.38 ± 0.89 kcal/100 g) and for A. hybridus by Oyelola et al. (289.92 kcal/100 g) [24, 25], further supporting the nutritional significance of these vegetables as components of balanced diets.

The mineral composition analysis demonstrated that Amaranthus hybridus L. (AH), Celosia argentea L. (CA), and Solanum macrocarpon L. (SM) are valuable sources of essential mineral elements, with AH exhibiting significantly higher concentrations of most of the minerals evaluated, including calcium, magnesium, potassium, iron, zinc, copper, and manganese. These minerals are indispensable for the maintenance of normal physiological processes and overall health. As reported by Liguori et al., Souza et al., Fatima et al. and Ciosek et al. calcium and magnesium play crucial roles in skeletal development and maintenance, neuromuscular transmission, muscle contraction, and the activation of numerous metabolic enzymes [61–64]. Potassium is equally important for maintaining fluid and electrolyte balance, nerve impulse transmission, and cardiovascular function, as highlighted by Penton et al. and Udensi and Tchounwou [65, 66]. Iron serves as an essential component of haemoglobin and several enzymes involved in oxygen transport and cellular energy metabolism, as described by Gupta [67]. Similarly, zinc functions as a structural and catalytic cofactor for numerous enzymes involved in antioxidant defence mechanisms, immune regulation, protein synthesis, and cellular metabolism, as demonstrated by Marreiro et al., Lee, and Nawaz and Sivakumar [68–70]. The appreciable concentrations of these minerals suggest that regular consumption of the studied vegetables may contribute substantially to micronutrient intake and help alleviate mineral deficiencies, particularly in populations where access to diverse nutrient-rich foods is limited.

The methanolic leaf extracts of Amaranthus hybridus, Celosia argentea, and Solanum macrocarpon contained appreciable amounts of total phenolics and total flavonoids, indicating the presence of polyphenolic constituents that may contribute to their observed antioxidant and enzyme inhibitory activities. Phenolic compounds and flavonoids represent major classes of plant secondary metabolites that are widely recognized for their health-promoting properties. As reported by Yu et al., Olaiya et al., Vuolo et al. and Zeb, these compounds contribute significantly to the antioxidant potential of medicinal and edible plants through their ability to neutralize free radicals, chelate pro-oxidant metal ions, and interrupt oxidative chain reactions [71–74]. Consequently, diets rich in polyphenolic compounds have been associated with reduced risks of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, cancer, and age-related disorders [72–74]. Furthermore, Dias et al. and D’Amelia et al. demonstrated that flavonoids play important roles in controlling the accumulation of reactive oxygen species by scavenging free radicals and modulating cellular antioxidant defence systems. Beyond their antioxidant functions, flavonoids have also been reported to possess anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties, thereby contributing to overall health maintenance [75, 76].

Although total phenolic contents did not differ significantly among the methanolic leaf extracts, Celosia argentea exhibited a significantly higher flavonoid content than Amaranthus hybridus and Solanum macrocarpon. The relatively higher flavonoid concent observed in C. argentea may partly contribute to its biological activities, given the established antioxidant and enzyme inhibitory properties associated with flavonoid compounds. The appreciable total phenolic and total flavonoid content obtained in this study are consistent with previous reports by IswaryaVelu et al. and Osei-Owusu et al. who documented the occurrence of phenolic compounds and flavonoids in Amaranthus species, Celosia argentea, and Solanum macrocarpon [77, 78]. These polyphenolic constituents have been widely associated with the antioxidant properties and health-promoting potential of leafy vegetables.

The antioxidant assays demonstrated that the methanolic leaf extracts of the three leafy vegetables possessed appreciable antioxidant activity, although differences were observed among the species. Amaranthus hybridus exhibited the highest total antioxidant capacity and the lowest IC₅₀ values for DPPH radical scavenging and metal-chelating activities, indicating stronger antioxidant effectiveness under the experimental conditions employed. The concentration-dependent increases observed in DPPH scavenging and metal-chelating activities further suggest the presence of bioactive compounds capable of mitigating oxidative processes.

The antioxidant effects observed in this study may be partly attributed to the phenolic and flavonoid constituents quantified in the methanolic leaf extracts. Previous studies by Chen et al., Vuolo et al. and Zeb reported that polyphenolic compounds contribute substantially to the antioxidant properties of plant-derived foods through mechanisms such as free radical scavenging, metal chelation, and interruption of oxidative chain reactions [73, 74, 79]. Similarly, Dias et al. and D’Amelia et al. highlighted the role of flavonoids in regulating oxidative stress through their antioxidant activities and their ability to neutralize reactive oxygen species [75, 76]. The relatively stronger antioxidant performance of A. hybridus, despite its lower flavonoid content compared with C. argentea, suggests that antioxidant activity may be influenced not only by the concentration of phenolic compounds but also by their structural characteristics, degree of polymerization, and possible synergistic interactions among phytochemical constituents, as reported by Chen et al. and Hajimehdipoor et al. [79, 80]. Comparable antioxidant activities have been reported for leafy vegetables in previous studies. For instance, Sahu et al. reported strong dose-dependent DPPH radical scavenging activities in methanolic extracts of edible leafy vegetables, with percentage inhibition increasing markedly with concentration [81]. Similarly, Obeng et al. documented antioxidant activities in Amaranthus species, Solanum macrocarpon, and other tropical leafy vegetables, further supporting their potential as natural dietary antioxidants [20]. Although the antioxidant assays employed were conducted in vitro, the findings indicate that regular consumption of these vegetables may contribute to dietary antioxidant intake and may help mitigate oxidative stress-associated disorders.

The inhibition of α-amylase and α-glucosidase observed in this study indicates that the methanolic leaf extracts of the three vegetables contain bioactive constituents capable of modulating carbohydrate digestion. These enzymes play key roles in the hydrolysis of dietary starch and oligosaccharides into absorbable glucose, and their inhibition is a recognised strategy for the management of postprandial hyperglycaemia, as reported by Lin et al. and Li et al. [82, 83]. Consequently, the inhibitory activities observed suggest a potential reduction in the rate of glucose release and absorption in the gastrointestinal tract. All extracts exhibited concentration-dependent inhibition against both enzymes, indicating progressive enzyme suppression with increasing extract concentration.

Similar concentration-dependent inhibitory activities have been reported in previous studies on plant-derived extracts. Kavya et al. demonstrated that ethyl acetate extracts of Chlorophytum comosum inhibited both α-amylase and α-glucosidase in a dose-dependent manner, with IC₅₀ values ranging from 547.99 to 205.39 µg/mL for α-amylase and 198.18 to 179.34 µg/mL for α-glucosidase, respectively [84]. Likewise, Ademiluyi et al. reported that aqueous extracts of red and white varieties of Hibiscus sabdariffa calyces exhibited inhibitory effects against α-amylase and α-glucosidase, with the inhibitory activities corresponding with their phenolic contents and antioxidant properties [85]. Similarly, Maradesha et al. reported strong inhibitory effects of phenolic-rich fractions against carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes, with caffeic acid and syringic acid showing low IC₅₀ values against α-amylase and α-glucosidase, respectively [86].

The inhibitory activities observed in the present study may be partly attributed to the phenolic and flavonoid constituents quantified in the methanolic leaf extracts. These compounds are known to interact with carbohydrate-hydrolysing enzymes through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions at or near the active site, thereby reducing catalytic efficiency. The relatively stronger performance of Amaranthus hybridus may reflect variations in phytochemical composition and overall antioxidant capacity compared with the other vegetables. Overall, these findings suggest that the observed enzyme inhibitory effects are largely associated with the polyphenolic constituents present in the extracts. Furthermore, the inhibitory activities were interpreted relative to acarbose, a standard α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibitor used as a positive control in this study. Acarbose demonstrated markedly stronger inhibition at lower concentrations compared with the plant extracts, thereby confirming the validity of the assay and providing a pharmacological benchmark for comparison.

However, the enzyme inhibition results should be interpreted cautiously because the present investigation was limited to in vitro assays and did not include isolation or characterization of specific bioactive compounds. Therefore, although the findings demonstrate inhibitory activities against α-amylase and α-glucosidase, they do not establish therapeutic efficacy against diabetes mellitus. Rather, the results suggest that these vegetables may serve as dietary sources of bioactive compounds that could contribute to postprandial glycemic regulation when consumed as part of a balanced diet.

Study limitations

The antioxidant and enzyme inhibitory activities evaluated in this study were based on in vitro assays, which may not fully reflect the complexity of physiological conditions in vivo. In addition, the phytochemical analyses were limited to total phenolic and total flavonoid contents; therefore, the specific compounds responsible for the observed biological activities were not identified or quantified. Advanced analytical techniques such as HPLC-DAD, LC-MS/MS, or GC-MS could provide more detailed characterization of the bioactive constituents. Furthermore, factors influencing the bioavailability, metabolism, and physiological interactions of the bioactive compounds following consumption were not investigated. Future studies involving detailed phytochemical profiling, assessment of bioaccessibility and bioavailability, and validation using appropriate in vivo models are recommended.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that Amaranthus hybridus L., Celosia argentea L., and Solanum macrocarpon L. are valuable sources of nutrients and minerals, with A. hybridus generally exhibiting higher concentrations of most proximate components and mineral elements evaluated. The leaf extracts also contained appreciable amounts of total phenolics and flavonoids and demonstrated measurable antioxidant activities, including DPPH radical scavenging, metal-chelating, and reducing capacities. Furthermore, the extracts exhibited concentration-dependent inhibitory effects against α-amylase and α-glucosidase, suggesting the presence of bioactive constituents with potential roles in modulating carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzyme activity.

The findings highlight the nutritional importance of these commonly consumed Nigerian leafy vegetables and suggest that they may serve as valuable dietary sources of essential nutrients and antioxidant compounds. Their inclusion in diets may contribute to improved nutritional quality and increased intake of plant-derived bioactive compounds, particularly in populations where these vegetables form an important component of daily meals.

However, the antioxidant and enzyme inhibitory activities reported in this study were determined using in vitro assays and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of therapeutic effects. Further investigations involving chromatographic identification of individual bioactive compounds, assessment of bioavailability, and validation using appropriate in vivo models are required to establish their physiological relevance. Additionally, promoting the utilization and cultivation of these indigenous vegetables may contribute to dietary diversification, nutritional security, and sustainable food systems.
