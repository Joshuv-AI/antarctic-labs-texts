Intensive arable farming calls for resilient wild bee restoration amid severe abundance fluctuations
Authors: Imre Sándor Piross, Áron Domonkos Bihaly, Viktor Szigeti, Ildikó Arany, Imre Demeter, Fruzsina Kőhalmi, Eszter Lellei‐Kovács, András Máté, Virág Eszter Németh, Raoul Pellaton, Miklós Sárospataki, Zoltán Soltész, László Somay, Dávid Lucas Stephenson, Krisztina Szabó, Eszter Tanács, Dóra Teplánszki, Flóra Vajna, Bernadett Zsinka, Anikó Kovács‐Hostyánszki, András Báldi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntensive arable farming threatens wild bees by reducing floral and nesting resources, increasing agrochemical exposure, and amplifying climatic stress. Ecological restoration using native wildflower mixtures is a promising intervention to recover pollinator diversity and the ecosystem service of pollination. However, restoration outcomes remain context dependent, and it is still poorly understood how restoration can be sustainably integrated into agricultural landscapes over time. We used a replicated, landscape‐scale field experiment in East‐Central Europe (Hungary) to test how spatial configuration, distance from restored plots, and landscape context jointly shape wild bee communities in intensively managed farmland. Twenty‐four landscape plots were established across homogeneous and heterogeneous landscapes and assigned to two spatial configurations, compact wildflower fields and dispersed wildflower strips, with unplanted controls. We sampled the landscape plots within a 500‐m radius using pan traps arranged in a grid over 4 years (2020–2023). Wild bee communities exhibited strong interannual fluctuations in abundance, species richness, and composition, with year emerging as the dominant driver and explaining more than one‐third of variation in community composition. Bee abundance peaked in 2021–2022 but abruptly dropped in 2023, which may suggest heightened sensitivity of pollinator communities to the severe drought that occurred in 2022. Despite this strong temporal variability, wildflower fields in homogeneous landscapes supported significantly higher wild bee abundance than either homogeneous controls or fields in heterogeneous landscapes, suggesting that restoration benefits may be greater where floral deficits are most severe. Together, these findings indicate that pollinator restoration success depends on both spatial context and temporal variability. We suggest that restoration planning should prioritize long‐term viability and resilience through sustained monitoring, stakeholder codesign, the use of diverse and locally adapted plant communities, and restoration schemes adapted to climatic variability. Restoration in homogeneous agricultural landscapes may provide benefits for pollinator communities and pollination services, while heterogeneous landscapes and semi‐natural habitats remain essential for conserving less‐degraded communities and rare species. Our results show that interannual variability can overshadow spatial configuration and landscape context, underscoring that even well‐designed restorations must accommodate climatic and local variability to remain effective over time.

INTRODUCTION

Pollinator decline is a growing ecological crisis caused by anthropogenic disturbances that produce problems on multiple fronts (Potts et al., 2016; Sánchez‐Bayo & Wyckhuys, 2019) and poses dire ecological, economic, and societal consequences (Feuerbacher et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2015). Agricultural intensification drives local declines through habitat loss, agrochemical exposure, and pathogen spillover (Cole et al., 2020; Goulson et al., 2015; Koh et al., 2016), and these impacts are further exacerbated by invasive alien species (Kovács‐Hostyánszki et al., 2022) and climate change (Kőrösi et al., 2018). Recent large‐scale assessments have shown that crop‐dominated fields are consistently the poorest in wild bee species and individuals compared to semi‐natural habitats or restored grasslands (Batáry et al., 2020; Vajna et al., 2024). Moreover, agricultural intensification and land‐use change have been found to reduce pollinator alpha diversity, even when regional diversity is retained (Tsang et al., 2025). These landscape‐scale pressures disrupt both foraging and nesting opportunities, undermining the resilience of wild bee populations across agricultural regions. Habitat destruction and degradation are the most critical drivers of pollinator loss, yet they can be mitigated through targeted habitat restoration (Jaureguiberry et al., 2022; Nieto et al., 2014).

Restoring habitats is one of the most effective ways to support pollinator populations and counteract their ongoing decline (Dicks et al., 2021; Kovács‐Hostyánszki et al., 2017). By enhancing floral diversity, nesting substrate availability, and spatial connectivity, restoration can buffer pollinators against the fragmentation and homogenization typical of agricultural landscapes. Evidence increasingly shows that even small‐scale interventions, such as sown wildflower strips or patches, can bolster both abundance and richness of wild bees (Batáry et al., 2020; Scheper et al., 2015), particularly in structurally simplified landscapes (Marja et al., 2019; Vajna et al., 2024). In addition to improving local biodiversity, restoration efforts contribute to ecosystem service provision, including pollination stability, which is vital for both wild plants and crop production (Aizen et al., 2019; Garibaldi et al., 2013; Steffan‐Dewenter & Westphal, 2008). This recognition has now been embedded in high‐level policy frameworks, for example, the European Union's Nature Restoration Law, which establishes a legally binding target to reverse pollinator decline by 2030 and promotes harmonized monitoring and evidence‐based restoration across member states (Dicks et al., 2021; European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024). These policies emphasize that habitat quality, landscape context, and land management all critically influence wild pollinator dynamics (González‐Varo et al., 2013; Kennedy et al., 2013) and require monitoring schemes capable of detecting ecological change with statistical reliability (Klaus et al., 2024; Marini et al., 2025).

Here, we present findings from a large‐scale, long‐term pollinator restoration experiment carried out in intensively managed arable landscapes (Báldi et al., 2022). The experiment was designed to inform restoration strategies under international biodiversity targets and regional policy frameworks, such as the EU Nature Restoration Law and national agri‐environmental policies. Our study addresses three key dilemmas for implementing habitat improvements in active arable landscapes: (1) where interventions should be prioritized, (2) how they should be spatially configured, and (3) how far their effects extend into the surrounding landscape. Given the substantial societal investment in pollinator conservation, it is crucial that restoration actions are not only ecologically effective but also cost‐efficient and policy‐relevant. We argue that successful interventions must demonstrably promote population growth where pollinators are most depleted and integrate well into local land‐use and ecological contexts (Báldi et al., 2022).

Successful restoration depends on both how interventions are designed and where they are implemented, in order to support positive population trajectories and long‐term ecological resilience (Krimmer et al., 2019; Williams et al., 2015). The long‐standing SLOSS dilemma (Single Large Or Several Small) is especially relevant for wildflower restoration (Fahrig et al., 2022; Szangolies et al., 2022). Larger patches may provide microhabitat buffering and favorable edge‐to‐interior ratios, whereas dispersed strips can enhance landscape connectivity and support a broader range of pollinator species (Bihaly et al., 2024; Rösch et al., 2015; Vajna et al., 2024). Ultimately, restoration must be both ecologically grounded and adapted to the specific characteristics of the surrounding land‐use matrix (Kovács‐Hostyánszki et al., 2017).

Landscape composition can play an important role in shaping pollinator communities and restoration efficiency through habitat distribution and resource availability (Bottero et al., 2023; Kennedy et al., 2013). The same intervention may yield greater results in homogeneous areas simply because it addresses a more severe lack of resources (Bihaly et al., 2024; Marja et al., 2025). In uniform, intensively farmed landscapes, the scarcity of semi‐natural habitats reduces floral resources, nesting sites, and safe refuges, while also limiting spillover from adjacent habitats that could otherwise sustain pollinator populations (Kremen et al., 2002; Öckinger & Smith, 2007; Ricketts et al., 2008). This highlights a fundamental consideration for restoration: whether to target degraded semi‐natural areas that already support pollinators or to intervene in highly homogeneous landscapes where ecological deficits are greatest (Batáry et al., 2020; Marja et al., 2025). Restoring semi‐natural areas can provide strong local benefits, but adding wildflower resources to homogeneous fields may offer broader landscape‐scale gains by creating stepping stones, improving connectivity, and reducing isolation (Geppert et al., 2020; Krimmer et al., 2019; Van Drunen et al., 2022). This discussion connects our applied restoration experiment to the long‐standing land‐sharing versus land‐sparing debate, which contrasts integrating biodiversity conservation into farmland with concentrating it in separate habitats (Phalan et al., 2011). These context‐dependent outcomes exacerbate the need for robust monitoring that considers not only the interventions themselves but also their context, in order to separate the effects of restoration from those of the environment.

Selecting an appropriate seed mixture is a critical step in pollinator restoration. Native, locally sourced species support regional pollinator communities and are better adapted to local environmental conditions (Kramer et al., 2019; Seitz et al., 2020). Non‐native or commercially imported mixtures, by contrast, may offer limited ecological value, particularly when dominated by a narrow subset of flowering species (Hadrava et al., 2022). Providing flower resources throughout the season is important for pollinator foraging continuity and population stability (Buhk et al., 2018; Van Drunen et al., 2022). Beyond species composition, spatial configuration represents another decisive design choice.

Effective pollinator sampling is a complex task that must account for different spatial and temporal scales. It remains poorly understood how far the ecological effects of restored plots extend into the surrounding landscape. To evaluate how restored patches integrate into the wider matrix, sampling must be scaled beyond the immediate treatment sites. Interpreting such patterns requires caution, as short‐term fluctuations in activity need to be distinguished from true differences in population size, habitat quality, and long‐term trends. Observed patterns may reflect momentary foraging behavior, weather variability, or local resource pulses rather than true population trends (Vellend et al., 2014). Stochastic processes add further uncertainty, particularly in fragmented landscapes where small, localized populations are more vulnerable to fluctuations (Sydenham et al., 2017). Ultimately, reliable assessments require not only ecological sensitivity but also spatial breadth and methodological consistency across environmental gradients.

In this study, we use a large‐scale, multiyear restoration experiment to test how locally designed interventions with native wildflower seed mixtures influence wild bee communities in intensively managed farmland, supported by landscape‐scale sampling covering the surrounding landscape within a 500‐m radius. We address three hypotheses: (1) the spatial distribution of restored plots shapes pollinator responses; (2) the effectiveness of interventions depends on the surrounding landscape matrix, producing different outcomes in homogeneous versus heterogeneous environments; and (3) restored plots have a measurable area of effect that extends into the surrounding landscape. This replicated, landscape‐scale field experiment provides rare empirical evidence on how restoration design and landscape context shape pollinator communities and their spatial dynamics, yielding insights relevant for both ecological theory and applied conservation practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study area and design

We conducted this study as part of a long‐term ecological experiment initiated in 2020 to test the effects of restored species‐rich grassland patches sown with a native wildflower seed mixture on biodiversity and ecosystem services in East‐Central European arable landscapes. Our restoration project aims to demonstrate an effective grassland restoration protocol for farm management and policy guidance by studying the effects of ecological intensification on ecosystem services and monitoring long‐term successional processes in restored wildflower parcels. The experiment stands out for its large‐scale design, its use of locally adapted, yet species‐rich seed mixtures, and its incorporation of multiyear monitoring to capture long‐term dynamics. See Báldi et al. (2022) for the detailed description of the project.

The study area lies in Central Hungary, within the Pannonian Basin of East‐Central Europe, a predominantly flat agricultural region between the Alps and the Carpathian Mountains. The study site spans from 46.7088 °N, 19.0572 °E to 46.9239 °N, 19.1523 °E. The surrounding landscape is a sparsely populated, intensively managed mosaic of mainly sunflower, maize, winter barley, winter wheat, oilseed rape, alfalfa, and sugarbeet fields, punctuated by remnant semi‐natural grasslands, hedgerows, fragmented forest strips, and occasional wetland patches. Before intensive farming, this region consisted of sandy ridges and low‐lying plains dominated by open xeric grasslands, willow‐poplar gallery woodlands along seasonal streams, and scattered marshes in depressions (Mezősi, 2017; Sándor & Sümegi, 2009). Beginning in the early 19th century, these landscapes were progressively drained, deforested, and converted to cereal fields and pasture. Intensive farming began after World War II, introducing mechanized deep plowing, heavy fertilizer, and pesticide use. These changes lowered groundwater levels, depleted soil structure, and fragmented native habitats, leading to stark declines in specialist grassland plants, ground‐nesting bees, and wetland‐dependent species (Stoate et al., 2009). Continuous agricultural intensification has left only small, isolated fragments of the original mosaic (Timár et al., 2024). This region is among Hungary's most drought‐prone and climate‐vulnerable areas, posing serious challenges for both farmers and conservationists alike (Kovács et al., 2017).

We designated 24 circular landscape plots (500‐m radius) as study sites, stratifying them by landscape context: Half were placed in homogeneous agricultural matrices (< 10% semi‐natural habitat), and half in heterogeneous landscapes (40%–60% semi‐natural habitat). We then assigned each plot to one of three spatial configuration treatments: a single, compact 0.5‐ha wildflower planting (“field”), three spatially separate strips spaced 100–150 m apart with a total area of 0.5 ha (“strips”) in the center of the landscape plots, or an unplanted control. This 2 × 3 factorial design, with four replicates of each spatial configuration–landscape context combination, provides a total of 24 surveyed landscape plots (Báldi et al., 2022). For brevity, we refer to combinations of landscape context and spatial configuration using compound terms (“homogeneous field,” “heterogeneous field,” “homogeneous control,” etc.). These terms denote the landscape plots located in homogeneous or heterogeneous agricultural surroundings and assigned to the field, strips, or control configurations, respectively. We sowed all wildflower plantings in January 2020 using a locally sourced, species‐rich mixture of 32 native annual, biennial, and perennial species selected to flower sequentially from early spring through autumn (see Appendix S1, Section S8 and Báldi et al. (2022) for full species list and flowering schedules). We deliberately omitted grasses to prevent their rapid dominance and promote long‐term persistence of insect‐pollinated flowers (Meissen et al., 2020). After mowing the entire plots twice in the first year and once in the second year, we implemented a biennial rotational mowing regime with biomass removal from 2022 onward. This approach preserves floral resources by keeping invasive alien plants and competitive grasses in check, while also maintaining overwintering habitat. All wildflower plots and nearly all of the surrounding arable fields are managed by the same contractor under consistent practices to minimize variability in cultivation, although adjacent crop rotations differ between years.

Sampling

We used a large‐scale pan‐trap survey to assess the abundance and species richness of wild bees in the wider area of the wildflower plantings. We chose pan traps because they suited the large spatial scale of our agricultural study and the flower‐scarce conditions of most sampling sites. Pan traps are particularly effective when floral resources are limited (Templ et al., 2019) and are recommended by the FAO for monitoring pollinators in agricultural habitats (LeBuhn et al., 2016). They also often yield higher species accumulation per individual than transect walks (O'Connor et al., 2019) and capture a broader range of solitary bees, although they tend to under‐sample bumble bees (Hutchinson et al., 2022). Unlike transect surveys, pan‐trap catches do not depend on observer expertise (Lezzeri et al., 2024), which was essential given the number of field assistants needed for our landscape‐wide design. The 48‐h sampling window also reduced sensitivity to short‐term weather variation. A known drawback is that capture rates depend on bee density and the traps' immediate surroundings (O'Connor et al., 2019), which we accounted for by recording detailed trap‐level habitat data and controlling for these effects in the statistical analyses (see below).

We conducted the sampling over four consecutive years (2020–2023), each year during the second half of July (20–23 July 2020, 19–22 July 2021, 18–21 July 2022, and 17–20 July 2023). At this time, winter cereals are largely harvested, while maize and sunflower crops are flowering, resulting in strong contrasts among harvested, flowering, and semi‐natural habitats. Sampling was conducted consistently during this period to ensure comparability among years.

We deployed 24 pan traps within each 500‐m‐radius landscape plot on a 150 × 150 m grid (Figure 1b–g), which resulted in four discrete distance classes from the plot center (d 1 = 106 m, N = 4; d 2 = 237 m, N = 8; d 3 = 318 m, N = 4; d 4 = 382 m, N = 8; Appendix S1: Figure S1). We assigned each trap a unique identifier matching its grid coordinates so that catch data could be modeled precisely against distance from the treatment center. At each grid point, we mounted a 500‐cm3 yellow plastic bowl (14 cm diameter; painted with Sparvar pigment) atop a 1.4‐ m wooden pole (3 × 3 cm cross‐section). We filled each trap with water and a drop of detergent to break surface tension. We collected samples from the traps after 48 h.

During each pan‐trap deployment, our field teams recorded detailed contextual data on standardized datasheets, noting the landscape plot code, trap ID, surveyor, and date. We classified the immediate habitat type at each trap and documented recent management (mown, grazed, harvested, tilled, or left untreated). We measured live vegetation height (cm) at knee height (40 cm) and estimated the percentage cover of live plants, litter, and bare soil in a 2 × 2 m quadrat around the trap. Within that same quadrat, observers counted the number of flowering morphospecies and recorded the abundance of total flower units on a logarithmic scale. Because many of these descriptors covaried strongly, we aggregated the categorical habitat and management classes into a single “trap environment” factor: harvested grain, maize, grassland, reed bed, harvested rapeseed, other cultivated plant, roadside, tree alley, alfalfa, not harvested grain, fallow or set‐aside field or our sown plots, and canal bank (listed by decreasing frequency; Appendix S1: Table S1). To avoid multicollinearity in our analyses, we then retained the trap‐environment variable alongside vegetation height (cm), live vegetation cover (%), and flower abundance category (three‐level factor: 0, 1–10, ≥11) to characterize each trap's surroundings in our wild bee abundance and richness models. The field effort amounted to 240 person‐days, calculated as 4 years with four sampling days each, involving teams of 15 people per sampling round.

Statistical methods

We analyzed the data with three complementary approaches: We modeled wild bee abundance and species richness (number of observed species in the sample) using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs), and we analyzed community composition using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), and nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). The detailed methodologies and results are also presented in Appendix S1, Sections S3 and S6.

We modeled wild bee abundance and species richness observed at the trap level using GLMMs with negative binomial distributions and a log‐link (Zuur et al., 2009). Fixed effects included year (factor; 2020–2023), landscape context (factor; homogeneous vs. heterogeneous), spatial configuration (factor; control, field, strips), distance from the plot center (factor; four levels: 106, 237, 318, 382 m), trap environment (factor; 13 levels: harvested grain, maize, grassland, sunflower, reed bed, harvested rapeseed, other cultivated plant, roadside, tree alley, alfalfa, not harvested grain, fallow or set‐aside field, canal bank), vegetation height (numeric; cm), live vegetation cover (numeric; %), and flower abundance (factor; three levels: 0, 1–10, ≥11). We tested interaction terms among key factors (year × landscape context, year × configuration, year × distance, landscape context × configuration, landscape context × distance, configuration × distance, and configuration × flower abundance). We included nested random intercept terms using unique landscape plot and trap level identifiers to account for the variation structure. We also incorporated a spatial autocorrelation component by scaling and centering trap coordinates and including them as an exponential spatial term that varied by year. Preliminary analyses indicated excess zeros in the data; therefore, we incorporated a zero‐inflation component with year as a predictor to account for interannual differences in zero inflation while avoiding unnecessary model complexity. This single‐term zero‐inflation structure was sufficient to adequately handle the excess zeros and achieve good overall model fit. We based model selection on Akaike information criterion (AIC), removing terms until further reduction increased AIC by >2 (Faraway, 2016). We evaluated model term significance (ANOVA) with Wald χ2 tests. To evaluate the predictions, we calculated marginal means and calculated custom contrast comparisons, all adjusted with Holm corrections (Holm, 1979). Finally, we calculated marginal and conditional R 2 values following Nakagawa et al. (2017) to measure the explanatory power of the models. See Appendix S1, Section S3 for the detailed description.

For the community structure analysis, we pooled the data at the landscape plot level to obtain meaningful community data and to avoid having “empty communities” (communities, i.e., traps with no observed specimens) that cannot be handled by community analysis approaches. To test for differences in community structure, we conducted PERMANOVA using Bray–Curtis dissimilarities derived from species abundances (Anderson, 2001). Main effects included year, landscape context, and spatial configuration; additional models tested their interactions. Permutations (n = 1000) were constrained within landscape plots. We visualized compositional differences with NMDS (Kruskal, 1964) with two dimensions and evaluated stress values to ensure ordination reliability.

We prepared all analyses and statistical figures in R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024). See Appendix S1, Section S7 for the detailed list of all used packages. We used colorblind‐friendly colors by applying the Turbo colormap (Mikhailov, 2019), implemented in the viridisLite 0.4.2 package (Garnier et al., 2023). Because abundance and species richness were heavily right‐skewed, with occasional extreme values, we omitted individual observations from the main figures to avoid obscuring differences in central tendencies. For transparency, we also provide companion versions that include the observed data in Appendix S1. Figure 1 was prepared in QGIS 3.40.10 using OpenStreetMap data (downloaded 2025‐08‐01; © OpenStreetMap contributors, CC‐BY‐SA). The data and the complete statistical analysis code are publicly available via Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21219233; Piross et al., 2026).

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics of species abundance

Across the four sampling years, we expected to collect 2304 pan‐trap samples, and only four were lost (three in 2020 and one in 2023), resulting in 2300 samples with usable data. These traps yielded 19,911 wild bee specimens representing 167 species. We collected 2239 individuals in 2020, 9814 in 2021, 6940 in 2022, and only 918 in 2023, revealing strong interannual variation in wild bee abundance despite near‐identical sample sizes across years. Patterns of interannual variation in relative abundance for the 50 most prevalent species are illustrated in Figure 2, while analogous plots for all recorded species are provided in Appendix S1, Section S2, Figures S2–S5.

Wild bee abundance model

The final GLMM explained a large proportion of the variance in wild bee abundance (marginal R 2 = 80.6%, conditional R 2 = 83.4%). We found four significant predictors of abundance: year (p < 0.0001), distance from the plot center (p = 0.0316), the interaction between landscape context and spatial configuration (p = 0.0274), and trap environment (p < 0.0001). For full results, including detailed marginal means, contrasts, and graphical summaries, see Appendix S1, Section S4.

Year strongly affected wild bee abundance, measured as the number of wild bees caught per trap (p < 0.0001). Wild bee abundance was lowest in 2020 and 2023, with mean values of 2.74 (95% CI: 1.97–3.83) and 1.04 (95% CI: 0.73–1.48), respectively, while it was notably higher in 2021 (10.23, 95% CI: 7.33–14.27) and 2022 (9.99, 95% CI: 7.18–13.89). We tested pairwise comparisons between consecutive years (Appendix S1: Tables S5, S6 and Figures S7 and S8) and found significant increases in abundance from 2020 to 2021 (p < 0.0001), where abundance more than tripled (ratio = 3.73, 95% CI: 2.15–6.46). In contrast, we observed a significant tenfold decline (p < 0.0001) from 2022 to 2023 (ratio = 0.1, 95% CI: 0.06–0.18). See Figure 3a,d and Appendix S1, Section S4 for details.

Wild bee species‐richness model

The final GLMM explained a substantial proportion of the variance in wild bee species richness (marginal R 2 = 58.4%, conditional R 2 = 59.5%). We found three significant predictors of species richness: year (p < 0.0001), distance from the plot center (p = 0.0124), and trap environment (p < 0.0001). Live vegetation cover showed a marginal effect (p = 0.055), while flower abundance, spatial configuration, landscape context, and vegetation height were not significant. Richness was highest at canal banks (mean = 3.94; 95% CI: 2.89–5.37), maize fields (3.84; 95% CI: 3.33–4.44), and reed beds (3.35; 95% CI: 2.84–3.95), whereas sunflower fields had the lowest richness (1.32; 95% CI: 1.12–1.57). See Figure 4 and Appendix S1, Section S5 for details. Similarly to the abundance model, the zero‐inflation component of the species‐richness model indicated relatively low zero‐inflation probabilities in 2020–2022, whereas substantially higher zero inflation was estimated for 2023, indicating a markedly higher frequency of structural zeros during the final sampling year (see Appendix S1, Section S5, Table S13).

Community composition

The main‐effects PERMANOVA model (Appendix S1: Table S20) showed that year, spatial configuration, and landscape context each significantly influenced wild bee community composition (all p < 0.0001). Year accounted for the largest proportion of variance (R 2 = 36.7%). Although spatial configuration (R 2 = 1.5%) and landscape context (R 2 = 1.4%) were statistically significant, their low R 2 values indicate a relatively small influence on community composition.

The full PERMANOVA model (Appendix S1: Table S21), which included interaction terms, identified a significant interaction between spatial configuration and landscape context (R 2 = 2.1%, p < 0.0001), suggesting that the effect of spatial configuration on community composition is context‐dependent. However, the small R2 value for this interaction indicates that its overall impact is limited. The interactions between year and spatial configuration (p = 0.9398) and year and landscape context (p = 0.1242) were not significant. See Figure 6 and Appendix S1, Section S6 for details.

DISCUSSION

Our results show that wild bee populations exhibited high interannual variability, possibly driven both by intrinsic population dynamics and by climatic extremes associated with climate change. These findings indicate that interventions must be long‐term and sufficiently resilient to allow positive population trajectories to emerge despite pronounced year effects. Long‐term monitoring is therefore essential to distinguish genuine population trends from natural year‐to‐year variability and to reliably evaluate restoration success. Within this strong temporal variability, we nevertheless found some evidence that restoration outcomes depended on landscape context. In particular, wildflower fields established in homogeneous agricultural landscapes supported higher wild bee abundance than homogeneous controls and heterogeneous fields. These findings suggest that restoration in resource‐limited agricultural settings can still provide measurable benefits for wild bee communities and pollination services, even under substantial temporal variability.

Our analysis of a four‐year, large‐scale dataset yielded highly robust results. The final wild bee abundance model explained 83% of the variance (conditional R 2), and the species‐richness model accounted for 60%. These findings indicate that our models capture the primary drivers of wild bee abundance in the studied system and, to a lesser extent, the observed species richness across the study area. Three variables emerged as the most important explanatory factors in the GLMM analyses: year, the distance from the landscape plot center, and the immediate trap environment. The importance of interannual fluctuations is further underscored by PERMANOVA results, which attribute 36% of the variance (R 2) to year. In this analysis, we also observed an effect of spatial configuration and landscape context, as well as their interaction. While these terms accounted for smaller variance in community composition compared with year, the interaction appears to be the most informative factor for guiding best practices in establishing wildflower plantings.

Wild bee communities exhibited pronounced interannual fluctuations in abundance, species richness, and composition. Year effects in ecology are often overlooked, yet can strongly influence communities (Vaughn & Young, 2010; Werner et al., 2020). In our study, year emerged as the strongest predictor across all models after the local trap environment, explaining more than a third of the variation in community structure. The sharp contrast between the population peaks of 2021–2022 and the collapse in 2023 may reflect heightened sensitivity to annual weather anomalies. Weather changes and climatic extremes could be a major driver behind these patterns (Kammerer et al., 2021), acting through cascading effects on floral resources, nesting conditions, and phenological matching between plants and pollinators (Kudo & Cooper, 2019; Scaven & Rafferty, 2013). Between 2019 and 2023, the region experienced high climatic variability with recurrent droughts, and 2022 was recognized as one of the driest years in recent decades (Kis et al., 2025). Such conditions can affect bees not only directly (Arroyo‐Correa, 2025) and through reduced flowering, but also by limiting nesting opportunities as drought impairs soil quality (You et al., 2025). Importantly, these impacts may manifest with delays: Poor conditions in 1 year can reduce reproduction, with the consequences appearing most strongly in the following season. This is consistent with the exceptionally dry year of 2022 being followed by the marked population collapse observed in 2023.

Such volatility emphasizes that short‐term increases or declines may reflect transient climatic or environmental fluctuations rather than underlying ecological recovery. Irregular boom–bust dynamics are likely in short‐lived insect populations when climatic extremes interact with internal population processes. In addition, wildflower plantings themselves may follow characteristic temporal phases, often beginning with a steep rise in pollinator responses before reaching a plateau and eventually declining after several years (Lybbert et al., 2022). Such intrinsic rise–plateau–fall trajectories further complicate interpretation, as restoration signals may be difficult to separate from these natural inherent temporal dynamics. This complicates the interpretation of restoration outcomes, particularly over short assessment windows. Sustained, multiyear monitoring that integrates climatic covariates is therefore essential to disentangle stochastic fluctuations from genuine restoration trajectories and to adapt conservation efforts to shifting climatic baselines, aligning with the monitoring priorities of the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (Affinito et al., 2025). Despite this pronounced temporal variability, our results also indicate that restoration outcomes may depend on landscape context and the spatial configuration of interventions, providing potentially useful insights for optimizing the establishment of wildflower plantings under different landscape contexts.

Wildflower fields in homogeneous landscapes yielded higher wild bee abundance than both homogeneous controls and heterogeneous wildflower fields, highlighting the context dependence of restoration success (Batáry et al., 2010). This interaction between landscape context and spatial configuration suggests that restoration effectiveness may depend on baseline habitat conditions. In structurally simplified agricultural matrices, landscape plots with wildflower fields supported higher wild bee abundance than control sites, whereas the differences were less pronounced in heterogeneous landscapes. These findings are consistent with Bihaly et al. (2024), who reported from the same experimental system that restoration effects within planted wildflower plots were stronger where background floral resources were limited. Similarly, other studies have shown that landscape‐scale floral deficits can increase the relative importance of small‐scale restoration interventions, particularly in intensive farming systems (Batáry et al., 2010; Holzschuh et al., 2007; Kremen & M'Gonigle, 2015). Together, these results suggest that pollinator restoration in resource‐limited agricultural landscapes can provide measurable benefits for wild bee communities and pollination services. However, these outcomes may still vary substantially among years because of climatic variability, extreme weather events, and broader landscape‐scale disturbances (Senapathi et al., 2021; Zabin et al., 2022).

Distance from the landscape plot center had a measurable effect on wild bees, indicating that restoration actions generated a local area of influence around the flower plots. However, the interaction between distance and spatial configuration was not significant, suggesting that our data and analyses lacked the statistical power to detect subtle differences in how bee abundance declined with distance across alternative spatial implementations of wildflower plantings. Because only the main distance effect remained in the model, the same pattern was predicted even for control areas without wildflower plots. We interpret this as a result of the current stage of the study rather than an ecological reality. Continued monitoring and the inclusion of complementary sampling methods should help better quantify the spatial extent of restoration effects. Alternatively, the use of pan traps—an inherently attractive, luring method (Lezzeri et al., 2024; O'Connor et al., 2019)—arranged in a grid may have contributed to higher captures near the center of the array.

The immediate trap environment was the strongest predictor for both abundance and species richness, although it was included primarily as a covariate to control for local variation. The catch probability of pan traps depends not only on the occurrence of bees but also on habitat characteristics (O'Connor et al., 2019). In flower‐poor habitats, traps may appear more attractive (Hutchinson et al., 2022; Prendergast et al., 2020), while in open habitats with higher visibility, bees may detect the traps more easily. Consequently, we observed striking contrasts among crop types, with up to eightfold more wild bees captured in maize fields than in sunflower fields, and higher catches on harvested grain fields than on fallows. Because sampling took place in late July, after the main flowering peak, differences in floral resources among habitats were likely reduced, suggesting that these contrasts reflect broader habitat or landscape effects rather than immediate flower availability alone. These counter‐intuitive patterns indicate that habitat types themselves cannot be directly compared using pan‐trap data (Krahner et al., 2024; Templ et al., 2019). However, with careful environmental recording and appropriate statistical controls, we can account for these habitat effects and isolate the influence of our main restoration variables more accurately.

Our results did not provide sufficient evidence to fully resolve the SLOSS dilemma in the context of landscape‐scale effects of wildflower restoration. Although the models indicated slightly higher bee abundance in field‐sized plantings compared to smaller strips, these differences were modest. This may imply that managers can have some flexibility in implementing wildflower habitats since both configurations appear to be comparable. Similarly, the role of landscape context proved difficult to isolate. While we detected an interaction between configuration and landscape context, indicating that the two factors jointly influence bee responses, the main effect of context alone remained inconclusive. Semi‐natural habitats surrounding the study plots—such as grasslands, tree lines, and fallows—can enhance pollinator populations by providing additional foraging and nesting resources, connectivity, suitable microclimate, and refuge during disturbances (Clough et al., 2014; Kovács‐Hostyánszki et al., 2017; Vajna et al., 2024). Continued monitoring of our experimental system should allow us to separate these effects more clearly in future analyses.

Our large‐scale field experiment with wildflower plantings allows us to offer several practical recommendations for pollinator restoration in farmlands. Most importantly, our results emphasize that restoration efforts should be designed with long‐term viability and resilience in mind. The pronounced interannual fluctuations observed in wild bee abundance, species richness, and community composition indicate that short‐term monitoring may provide a misleading picture of restoration success. Sustained, multiyear monitoring is therefore essential to distinguish stochastic fluctuations from genuine ecological recovery and to evaluate long‐term population trajectories (Bihaly et al., 2024; Grass et al., 2016). Accordingly, agri‐environmental policies should support long‐term restoration schemes rather than only annual or short‐duration interventions, while maintaining the long‐term engagement of farmers, local stakeholders, and authorities, potentially through bottom‐up, stakeholder‐driven approaches such as Living Labs (Klein et al., 2025). Maintaining high crop and plant diversity with locally adapted species may also help buffer pollinator populations against climatic variability and support more resilient pollinator communities and ecosystem services (Schmidt et al., 2020). Our results further suggest that restoration interventions in resource‐limited, homogeneous agricultural landscapes can provide measurable benefits for wild bee communities and pollination services (Batáry et al., 2020). However, when the goal is the conservation of less‐degraded pollinator communities or rare species, conserving and enhancing heterogeneous landscapes and existing semi‐natural habitats remains essential, as such species often depend on larger, long‐established, and well‐connected habitats (Kleijn et al., 2015). Larger, continuous patches should generally be favored to maximize habitat continuity (Kovács‐Hostyánszki et al., 2017), although our results also suggest some flexibility in configuration where local conditions favor dispersed or smaller plantings.

Our study emphasizes that wild bee restoration outcomes are inherently context‐dependent and temporally dynamic, limiting straightforward generalization across systems. Interannual variation outweighed both spatial and treatment effects, indicating that year‐to‐year dynamics exerted a stronger influence on bee communities than differences in plot configuration or surrounding environmental conditions. Shifts in indicator taxa further point to fluid community assembly and potential sensitivity to climatic extremes, whose frequency is expected to increase under climate change (Forzieri et al., 2016; Spinoni et al., 2018). Together, these processes help explain why universal restoration recipes remain elusive. Pollinator restoration is a priority at both global and European policy levels, including commitments under the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (Affinito et al., 2025) and the EU Nature Restoration Regulation (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024), which explicitly aims to halt pollinator declines. However, while such policy frameworks often favor simple and standardized implementation, effective restoration strategies must recognize and accommodate strong ecological and temporal variability. By demonstrating how context and time shape restoration outcomes, our findings support adaptive, evidence‐based approaches and underscore the need for long‐term monitoring to guide pollinator recovery under accelerating global change.
