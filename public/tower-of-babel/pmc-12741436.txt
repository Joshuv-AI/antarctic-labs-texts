Fresh frozen plasma therapy in type I plasminogen deficiency: a case of ligneous conjunctivitis with hydrocephalus
Authors: Ibrahim AboGhayyada, Mohammad Zeidan, Taha Z Makhlouf, Amir AbuGhiyatha, Mosab Ghnimat, Mohammad Najajreh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Plasminogen deficiency is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by impaired fibrinolysis and the formation of fibrin-rich pseudomembranes. Ligneous conjunctivitis is the most common manifestation, whereas central nervous system involvement, such as hydrocephalus, is rare. Early recognition is essential to prevent irreversible complications. Case Presentation: We report a 3-year-old girl with progressive macrocephaly, antenatal hydrocephalus, and persistent pseudomembranous conjunctivitis. Imaging showed fused lateral ventricles and agenesis of the corpus callosum, consistent with obstructive hydrocephalus requiring multiple ventriculoperitoneal shunt revisions. Genetic testing confirmed type I plasminogen deficiency with a homozygous PLG mutation. Recombinant plasminogen was unavailable, and she was managed with fresh frozen plasma (FFP) infusions plus ophthalmic and anticonvulsant therapy. Outcome: Regular FFP stabilized her ocular and neurological condition, though recurrent shunt complications necessitated repeated hospitalizations. Conclusion: This case illustrates the diagnostic challenges of type I plasminogen deficiency and highlights FFP as a practical therapeutic option in resource-limited settings.Keywords: plasminogen deficiency, ligneous conjunctivitis, hydrocephalus, fresh frozen plasma, Pediatrics, case report

Introduction

Plasminogen is the inactive precursor of plasmin, an enzyme that plays a key role in breaking down fibrin both intravascular and extravascular. As a proteolytic enzyme, it breaks down fibrin and other extracellular matrix proteins, triggers metalloproteases, and aids in angiogenesis and wound healing. The liver produces PLG, and activating and inhibitory chemicals control its homeostasis [1]. Plasminogen deficiency (PLGD) is a very rare condition, with an estimated prevalence of 1.6 per million people [2]. The most common clinical feature is ligneous conjunctivitis [3]. Diagnosis is made clinically and can be confirmed with genetic testing [4]. Recent expert guidance underlines the potential for FFP-based approaches (such as eye drops, or systemic infusions) to stabilize clinical progression and reduce complications when specific plasminogen products are not yet accessible [5]. We report a case of a 3-year-old girl diagnosed with plasminogen deficiency, presenting with ligneous conjunctivitis and obstructive hydrocephalus.

Case presentation

A 3-year-old female patient presented with a complex medical history, which is progressive head enlargement, and persistent bilateral eye redness and tearing since birth. She was delivered by elective cesarean section following antenatal diagnosis of hydrocephalus. Birth weight was 2970 g, length 49 cm, and head circumference (HC) 37 cm. Antenatal ultrasound at 6 weeks’ gestation showed bilateral ventricular dilation. There were no perinatal complications. Her 34-year-old mother (gravida 11, para 5) had six first-trimester miscarriages but no chronic medical conditions, gestational diabetes, or hypertension. The patient is the child of consanguineous parents from the same extended family. A distant paternal relative was reportedly diagnosed with PLGD. Her three brothers and one sister have no similar health issues.

At two weeks of age, progressive head enlargement was observed. CT showed bilateral ventricular dilation, and MRI revealed a large monoventricle with absent septum pellucidum, indicating semilobar holoprosencephaly (Fig. 1). By three months, head circumference reached 44 cm with a bulging fontanelle but no focal deficits. Repeat CT showed fused lateral ventricles and corpus callosum agenesis, consistent with obstructive hydrocephalus (Fig. 2). A non-contrast CT was performed due to the patient’s infancy, to avoid unnecessary contrast-related risks. Despite the absence of contrast, the imaging clearly demonstrated marked ventricular dilatation and agenesis of the corpus callosum, which was sufficient for diagnosis and management. Ongoing neurodevelopmental monitoring and early imaging are crucial.

Axial brain MRI images: (A) T1-weighted sequence and (B) T2-weighted sequence showing markedly dilated lateral ventricles with thinning of the cortical mantle, consistent with obstructive hydrocephalus.

Non-contrast axial CT image (contrast was avoided due to patient age and sufficient diagnostic clarity) showing massive bilateral ventricular enlargement, fused lateral ventricles, and absent corpus callosum—findings consistent with obstructive hydrocephalus.

Ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunting was initially delayed due to recurrent respiratory tract infections, but it was eventually performed at 7 months of age. Postoperatively, the patient experienced multiple episodes of shunt obstruction and required frequent revisions.

In parallel with her neurological symptoms, the child had persistent bilateral eye redness and tearing since birth. At 3 months, she was diagnosed with presumed pseudomembranous adenoviral conjunctivitis and was treated with lubricants and topical corticosteroids without significant improvement. At 4 months, a re-evaluation by an ophthalmologist raised the suspicion of ligneous conjunctivitis, and specific topical therapy was initiated (Fig. 3).

Photograph of the patient showing bilateral thick pseudomembranous lesions and severe inflammation of the eyelids and conjunctiva, consistent with ligneous conjunctivitis.

At the age of 2.5 years, the patient was hospitalized due to sepsis secondary to a (VP) shunt infection. She presented with high fever and prolonged seizures. Imaging done and revealed worsening hydrocephalus, and lumbar puncture showed elevated protein levels and pleocytosis. A diagnosis of meningitis was made, and appropriate antimicrobial therapy was initiated. She was also started on anticonvulsants for seizure control. After this episode, the patient began experiencing recurrent seizures occurring once every few months. An electroencephalogram (EEG) was done, and showed a symmetrical background with no epileptic activity. Since then, she has been maintained on a combination of topiramate, levetiracetam, and phenobarbital for seizure management.

Given the combination of refractory ocular disease and recurrent shunt obstruction, the patient was referred for genetic evaluation. At 5 months of age, molecular testing confirmed the diagnosis of Type I plasminogen deficiency (PLGD), identifying a homozygous pathogenic mutation in the PLG gene: c.1752-1753insG (p.Cys585ValfsX15) in exon 14. Although treatment with recombinant human plasminogen (Ryplazim) was recommended, the patient’s family was unable to obtain it due to limited availability and financial constraints. As an alternative, the patient was managed with fresh frozen plasma (FFP) infusions.

At present, the child is conscious and alert on clinical examination, with no pallor or jaundice. Her vital signs are stable. She weighs 11.2 kg, height is 90 cm, and head circumference 57 cm. The anterior fontanelle remains mildly bulging, while the posterior fontanelle is closed. Bilateral pseudomembranous lesions are observed on the conjunctiva. Cardiovascular, respiratory, and abdominal examinations are unremarkable.

She is currently maintained on antiepileptic therapy with topiramate, levetiracetam, and phenobarbital. In addition, she continues to receive fresh frozen plasma (FFP) infusions three times per week as a source of plasminogen. Ophthalmic treatment includes moxifloxacin eye drops, topical heparin, and hydroxyethyl cellulose lubricant. The patient requires frequent hospital admissions due to recurrent upper respiratory tract infections or malfunction/obstruction of the VP shunt. She is under regular follow-up with both neurology and ophthalmology specialists (Fig. 4). She is up-to-date on immunizations and has no known allergies.

Discussion

Plasminogen Deficiency is an ultra-rare multisystem disorder resulting from a mutation in the plasminogen (PLG) gene. It is an autosomal recessive disease. The condition is classified into two forms: congenital type I plasminogen deficiency and congenital type II plasminogen deficiency [6].

The underlying pathophysiology of this disease involves the accumulation of fibrin on mucosal surfaces—including the eyes, middle ear, mouth, respiratory tract, female genital tract, and pharynx—as well as at injury and inflammation sites. This results from impaired fibrin clearance and leads to a variety of clinical symptoms. Ligneous conjunctivitis is the most common and well-known manifestation [3].

The disease is categorized into two types: quantitative and qualitative plasminogen defects. Type I plasminogen deficiency (hypoplasminogenemia) is a quantitative disorder marked by significantly reduced plasminogen antigen and activity levels. Type II (dysplasminogenemia) is a qualitative defect with normal antigen levels but reduced activity due to abnormal plasminogen structure. Both result from mutations in the PLG gene, but only type I is consistently linked to ligneous disease and mucosal pseudomembranous lesions. Type II is usually clinically silent [6].

The disease can affect multiple systems with variable presentation. Ligneous conjunctivitis is the most common and best-characterized symptom of type I. Though once believed to affect only children, it can also appear in adults [7]. The oral cavity is the second most commonly affected site, with ligneous lesions either limited to the mouth or co-occurring with conjunctival disease [6].

Congenital occlusive hydrocephalus has been reported in several children with plasminogen deficiency and ligneous conjunctivitis. In one case series, 7 out of 16 children with ligneous conjunctivitis were found to have hydrocephalus attributed to the sameunderlying etiology [8].

Dysfunctional fibrinolysis leads to fibrin accumulation within cerebrospinal fluid pathways, resulting in obstruction and congenital hydrocephalus. In these instances, fresh-frozen plasma (FFP) has been utilized to provide external plasminogen, resulting in a partial recovery of fibrinolytic activity and temporary clinical enhancement, which includes alleviation of shunt blockage [9].

Diagnosis of plasminogen deficiency involves clinical assessment, laboratory testing, and molecular analysis. Clinically, patients present with fibrin-rich pseudomembranes on mucous membranes [4]. Laboratory diagnosis includes measurement of plasminogen activity (PLG:A) and antigen levels [6].

There are several therapeutic options for managing type I plasminogen deficiency, particularly for ligneous lesions. One promising treatment is plasminogen concentrate, especially in the form of topical eye drops, which have shown good efficacy in treating ligneous conjunctivitis [6]. Fresh-frozen plasma (FFP)—administered systemically or locally—has also been used successfully to supply plasminogen directly to affected tissues [6].

In resource-limited countries where plasminogen concentrate is unavailable, fresh frozen plasma (FFP) can be used as an alternative therapy for congenital plasminogen deficiency. FFP can be administered intravenously to temporarily increase plasminogen levels and treat manifestations such as ligneous conjunctivitis and systemic fibrin-rich lesions [10].

FFP therapy has been shown not only to reverse existing ligneous lesions but also to play a prophylactic role by preventing progression to other organs. Its relative availability, affordability, and established safety profile make it a valuable frontline or adjunctive option in selected cases [11].

Topical corticosteroids, heparin, and plasminogen, sometimes combined with immunosuppressants like azathioprine, have shown mixed results. Systemic therapies and hormonal treatments may help in select cases. Surgical excision is an option, but lesions often recur without medical therapy. Hormonal influence has been noted in a few female patients [6].

Identifying type I plasminogen deficiency early is essential to avoid irreversible issues caused by the formation of fibrin-rich membranes in crucial organs. Prompt initiation of fresh-frozen plasma (FFP) treatment delivers external plasminogen, which helps restore fibrinolytic function. Benefits reported include a decrease in ligneous conjunctival lesions, prevention of airway obstruction, stabilization of hydrocephalus by diminishing fibrin accumulation in cerebrospinal fluid pathways, and extended shunt operation. Consistent FFP treatments have also been linked to better respiratory health and overall clinical stability, highlighting its importance when plasminogen concentrates are not accessible [9].

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare presentation of Type I plasminogen deficiency in a 3-year-old girl with ligneous conjunctivitis and obstructive hydrocephalus. Early diagnosis through genetic testing enabled targeted management with fresh frozen plasma. Despite limited resources, supportive treatment has stabilized her condition. This case emphasizes the need for early recognition and multidisciplinary care to manage complications and improve outcomes in this ultra-rare genetic disorder.
