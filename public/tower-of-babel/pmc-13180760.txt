Clinical cutoff value of lumbar bending range for predicting residual pelvic obliquity after total hip arthroplasty in dysplastic hip osteoarthritis
Authors: Hiroyuki Yokoi, Yusuke Osawa, Yuto Ozawa, Hiroto Funahashi, Yasuhiko Takegami, Shiro Imagama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionResidual pelvic obliquity (PO) after total hip arthroplasty (THA) for dysplastic hip osteoarthritis (DHOA) may adversely affect coronal balance and postoperative outcomes. Although lumbar bending range (LBR) is considered critical for PO improvement, the clinical cutoff value for predicting residual PO remains unclear. This study aimed to determine the cutoff value of LBR associated with residual PO after THA.Materials and methodsAmong 382 patients who underwent primary THA for unilateral DHOA between July 2019 and June 2024, 98 patients with preoperative downward PO of ≥ 2° on the affected side were included. Patients were classified into a residual group (postoperative PO ≥ 2° at 1 year) and an improvement group (postoperative PO < 2°). Demographic data and radiographic parameters of the hip, lower limbs, and spine were compared. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with residual PO, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to determine the optimal LBR cutoff value.ResultsResidual PO was observed in 28 patients (29%). Compared with the improvement group, the residual group demonstrated significantly greater Crowe index values, preoperative radiographic leg length discrepancy, and preoperative PO. Affected-side LBR was significantly smaller in the residual group (4.3° ± 3.0° vs. 7.7° ± 4.0°, P < 0.001), whereas the unaffected-side LBR exhibited no significant difference. Multivariate analysis identified affected-side LBR as the only independent factor associated with residual PO (odds ratio, 0.698; 95% confidence interval, 0.580–0.840; P < 0.001). ROC analysis demonstrated an optimal affected-side LBR cutoff value of 4.5° (area under the curve, 0.783).ConclusionsReduced affected-side LBR was strongly associated with residual PO following THA in patients with DHOA presenting with downward PO. An affected-side LBR cutoff value of 4.5° is helpful in predicting residual PO and assist in coronal compensatory capacity before THA.Keywords: Pelvic obliquity, Total hip arthroplasty, Dysplastic hip osteoarthritis, Lumbar bending range

Introduction

Dysplastic hip osteoarthritis (DHOA) is frequently accompanied by coronal pelvic obliquity (PO) and leg length discrepancy (LLD) due to abnormal hip morphology. Coronal plane alignment abnormalities can cause gait disturbances, low back pain, knee pain, and other adjacent joint disorders, thereby impairing posture and daily function [1, 2]. Additionally, several patients with DHOA exhibit coronal malalignment extending from the pelvis to the spine, and PO is often present preoperatively [3]. PO can be classified into two types: downward PO on the affected side (D-PO) and upward PO on the affected side (U-PO). We previously reported that U-PO is relatively uncommon, is often associated with severe subluxation or hip adduction contracture, and tends to persist postoperatively. Conversely, D-PO is considered primarily a compensatory change secondary to leg shortening and is therefore more likely to improve after total hip arthroplasty (THA), making it common in daily clinical practice [4]. Although THA is expected to restore hip function and improve coronal alignment, including PO; however, PO persists postoperatively in a subset of patients and has been reported to adversely affect postoperative outcomes and patient satisfaction [5–7].

Coronal alignment maintenance depends not only on optimizing hip reconstruction, including restoration of the hip center, offset, and leg length restoration, but also on pelvic leveling through lateral lumbar bending. Yu et al. reported that trunk balance can be maintained through spinopelvic compensation even in the presence of coronal LLD imbalance [8]. Moreover, Betsch et al. demonstrated that increasing LLD aggravates PO and spinal scoliosis, indicating that lumbar flexibility plays an essential role in maintaining coronal alignment [9]. Recently, reduced lumbar bending range (LBR) has been suggested to be associated with residual PO after THA [10], and we have also reported its importance in our previous study [11]. Nevertheless, no previous studies have identified a specific clinical threshold for LBR associated with residual PO. Regarding coronal kinematics, in patients with D-PO, postoperative pelvic leveling may depend more strongly on lumbar lateral bending toward the affected side. Thus, affected-side LBR is essential to compensate for pelvic tilt that may persist after leg length restoration by THA, and patients with low affected-side LBR are more likely to have residual PO after THA. Accordingly, we hypothesized that decreased affected-side LBR is associated with residual PO 1 year after THA.

This study focused on patients with D-PO and DHOA and addressed the following research questions: (1) Which factors, including LBR, are associated with residual PO 1 year after THA? (2) Does a decreased LBR toward the unaffected or affected side have a stronger influence on residual PO 1 year after THA? (3) Is there a clinically useful cutoff value of LBR for predicting residual PO 1 year after THA? By addressing these questions, this study provides a quantitative cutoff value of LBR for predicting residual PO after THA, offering clinically useful information for preoperative evaluation of coronal compensatory capacity.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective observational study was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital. Between July 2019 and June 2024, 382 patients who underwent primary unilateral THA for DHOA at our institution were initially identified. Patients with a history of lumbar spinal fusion, end-stage osteoarthritis of the unaffected hip, missing radiographic data, or postoperative complications, including infection or dislocation, were excluded. Furthermore, 39 patients who demonstrated U-PO of ≥ 2° on preoperative standing full-spine radiographs were excluded. U-PO in DHOA is relatively uncommon and is frequently associated with severe subluxation; moreover, it is considered to reflect a biomechanical condition different from D-PO. Therefore, these patients were excluded from analysis. Ultimately, 98 patients (98 hips) who demonstrated D-PO of ≥ 2° on preoperative standing full-spine radiographs were included in the final study population.

Based on previous reports, a PO ≥ 2° was considered clinically significant [12]. Patients whose PO improved to < 2° at 1 year postoperatively were classified as the improvement group, whereas those with a PO of ≥ 2° at 1 year were assigned to the residual group. The background characteristics and radiographic parameters were compared between the two groups.

Preoperative three-dimensional templating was performed using ZedHip software (Lexi Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) to restore leg length and offset to match the unaffected side as closely as possible. All procedures were performed using a standard posterior approach. The selection of the acetabular cup and femoral stem, as well as the use of cement, was left to the discretion of the operating surgeon. The distribution of cup and stem fixation (cemented or cementless), femoral offset type, femoral head size, and neck length is summarized in Table 1. Leg length restoration was planned to achieve anatomical equalization with the contralateral limb based on preoperative templating. Soft-tissue releases were not routinely performed. Intraoperative assessment of leg length and offset was performed using anatomical landmarks, including the greater trochanter, lesser trochanter, and knee position, with comparison to the contralateral limb.

Postoperatively, all the patients followed a standardized rehabilitation protocol. Gait training and range-of-motion exercises were initiated according to this protocol, and postoperative management was unified across patients.

Evaluation of the hip and lower limb radiographic parameters

Radiographic analysis was performed using standardized anteroposterior pelvic radiographs and full-length standing lower extremity radiographs obtained preoperatively and at 1 year postoperatively. The evaluated hip and lower-limb parameters were the Crowe index, acetabular offset, femoral offset, radiographic LLD (RLLD), and hip adduction angle (Fig. 1). The Crowe index quantifies the femoral head subluxation degree relative to the acetabulum [13]. Acetabular offset was defined as the horizontal distance from the femoral head center to a vertical reference line passing through the pubic symphysis. Femoral offset was measured as the perpendicular distance from the femoral head center to the femoral anatomical axis [14]. RLLD was calculated as the difference in vertical height from the inter-teardrop line to the lesser trochanter tip between the affected and unaffected sides, with a positive value indicating a shorter affected limb [15]. Hip adduction angle was measured as the angle between the femoral shaft axis and the vertical line drawn from the teardrop inferior border on the affected side, with positive values indicating adduction [3]. Parameter changes were calculated by subtracting preoperative values from postoperative values.

Evaluation of the spinal radiographic parameters

Spinal and pelvic alignments were assessed using standing full-spine anteroposterior radiographs obtained preoperatively and 1 year postoperatively. The evaluated parameters included PO, lumbar scoliosis angle (LSA), C7 coronal vertical axis (C7CVA), and LBR. PO was measured according to the method described by Osebold et al. as the angle between the horizontal plane and the line connecting the superior margins of the bilateral iliac crests [16]. To standardize deviation direction, PO was defined as positive when the affected side was lower. C7CVA and LSA were measured following the method described by Nakashima et al. [17]. C7CVA was defined as the horizontal distance from the sacral center to the C7 vertebral body, with positive values indicating deviation toward the unaffected side. LSA was defined as the angle between a vertical line drawn from the L1 superior endplate and a line connecting the superior margins of the iliac crests, with positive values indicating convexity toward the affected side. LBR was calculated as the difference in the angle formed by a line connecting the L1 superior endplate and the superior margins of the bilateral iliac crests between the neutral standing position and the maximal lateral bending position. LBR was evaluated separately by bending direction: affected-side LBR was defined as the difference between the neutral standing position and maximal lateral bending toward the affected side, whereas unaffected-side LBR was defined as the difference between the neutral standing position and maximal lateral bending toward the unaffected side. Positive values indicated increased inclination toward the lateral bending direction (Fig. 2). As with the hip and lower-limb parameters, changes in spinal radiographic parameters were calculated by subtracting preoperative values from postoperative values.

Clinical evaluation

Patient demographic data, including age, sex, height, body weight, body mass index (BMI), affected side, condition of the unaffected hip, and duration of hip pain, were extracted from medical records. Hip function was evaluated using the Harris Hip Score (HHS) and range of motion (ROM) preoperatively and at one year postoperatively. ROM was measured with a goniometer by a senior surgeon. Perceived leg length discrepancy (P-LLD) was quantitatively assessed according to the method described by Takemoto et al. [2]. P-LLD was evaluated by recording both the presence of perceived discrepancy (positive or negative) and the magnitude of the discrepancy. the absolute value of P-LLD was used for analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using EZR version 1.52 (Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan), a graphical user interface for R designed for medical statistics [18]. Continuous and categorical variables were compared between groups using the Student’s t-tests and Fisher’s exact tests, respectively. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with residual PO. Variables that demonstrated significant differences in the univariate analysis, together with age, sex, and BMI, were entered into the multivariate model. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

To assess measurement reliability, two orthopaedic surgeons independently evaluated 20 randomly selected images. The sample size of 20 was chosen according to common practice in radiological reliability studies. Reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement. Interobserver ICCs were 0.823 (95% CI 0.625–0.930) for preoperative PO, 0.882 (95% CI 0.737–0.954) for postoperative PO, 0.903 (95% CI 0.867–0.978) for affected-side LBR, 0.850 (95% CI 0.832–0.972) for unaffected-side LBR, 0.945 (95% CI 0.864–0.977) for LSA, 0.900 (95% CI 0.755–0.957) for preoperative RLLD, and 0.818 (95% CI 0.702–0.947) for postoperative RLLD. The ICCs, ranging from 0.818 to 0.945, indicated good to excellent reliability [19].

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine the optimal LBR cutoff value for predicting residual PO. The cutoff value was defined using the maximum Youden index.

For radiographic parameters, the residual group exhibited greater preoperative Crowe index, RLLD, and PO compared to the improvement group (26.2 ± 18.0 vs. 15.8 ± 15.1, P = 0.008; 16.9 ± 10.4 mm vs. 10.3 ± 6.9 mm, P < 0.001; and 5.3 ± 2.4° vs. 4.0 ± 1.5°, P = 0.002, respectively). ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the optimal cutoff value of affected-side LBR for predicting residual PO was 4.5°, with sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC) of 0.886, 0.679, and 0.783, respectively (Fig. 3). The standard error of measurement (SEM) for LBR was 1.25°, calculated using an SD of 4.03° and an ICC of 0.90.

When patients were stratified by this cutoff value, preoperative and postoperative PO in patients with an affected-side LBR of ≥ 4.5° were 4.2 ± 1.6° and 0.4 ± 1.5°, respectively; the corresponding values in patients with an affected-side LBR of < 4.5° were 4.8 ± 2.3° and 2.8 ± 2.2°. Discussion

Here, we investigated the factors associated with residual PO at 1 year after THA in patients with D-PO and DHOA. The clinical relevance of the LBR merits consideration. Residual PO of ≥ 2° was present in 29% of patients at 1 year postoperatively. Compared with the improvement group, the residual group demonstrated higher Crowe index values, preoperative RLLD, and preoperative PO angles. Among these variables, the most pronounced between-group difference was observed for affected-side LBR, which was lower in the residual group than in the improvement group. Multivariate analysis identified affected-side LBR as the only independent factor associated with residual PO. Consistently, ROC analysis indicated that an affected-side LBR of 4.5° represented the optimal cutoff value for predicting residual PO.

PO is generally considered a compensatory change secondary to leg shortening [8, 20], and several studies have reported that greater LLD is associated with increased pelvic tilt [5, 6, 21]. Here, univariate analysis demonstrated that the residual group exhibited significantly greater Crowe index values, preoperative RLLD, and preoperative PO compared with the improvement group, suggesting that hip morphology and LLD contributed to PO development. Nevertheless, multivariate analysis identified the affected-side LBR as the only independent factor associated with residual PO, indicating that the dynamic coronal compensatory capacity of the spine is more critical in postoperative pelvic leveling than static factors such as hip morphology or LLD alone. Leg length restoration is a fundamental goal of THA. Here, leg length was reconstructed to match the unaffected side as closely as possible. Nevertheless, PO persisted in approximately one-third of patients (29%), a clinically non-negligible proportion. Therefore, correcting the LLD alone may be insufficient to restore coronal alignment in some patients and underscores the importance of considering spinal compensatory mechanisms when evaluating postoperative pelvic balance.

Spinal flexibility in the coronal plane has been recognized as an important factor influencing PO. Banno et al. reported a strong correlation between spinal scoliosis and PO [22]. Additionally, Blizzard et al. demonstrated that limited lumbar spine flexibility associated with spinal deformity may lead to PO [23]. Abe et al. investigated patients undergoing THA with leg lengthening. They reported that postoperative PO improved in approximately 90% of the patients with flexible spines [24]. Conversely, those with limited spinal flexibility were at risk of developing a new imbalance or pain after surgery. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether lumbar flexibility toward the unaffected or affected side is associated with residual PO after THA. Our finding suggests that postoperative pelvic leveling depends on both global spinal flexibility and directional flexibility toward the affected side, which are required to compensate for residual pelvic tilt after leg length restoration. Thus, lateral bending toward the affected side appears to be indispensable for achieving coronal balance after THA in patients with D-PO.

To date, no quantitative criterion has been established to define the lumbar flexibility required for PO improvement, and most prior evaluations have emphasized sagittal-plane flexibility rather than coronal-plane compensation. Koga et al. defined patients with an affected-side LBR of < 5° as having a rigid lumbar spine and reported that these patients showed less PO improvement after THA [10]. However, this threshold was not quantitatively validated. In contrast, our analysis identified an optimal cutoff value of 4.5° for affected-side LBR in predicting residual PO using ROC analysis. By focusing on patients with D-PO and evaluating LBR separately according to bending direction, the present study provides a statistically derived and clinically useful indicator of coronal compensatory capacity (Figs. 5 and 6). Moreover, LBR can be assessed readily using simple standing radiographs, without specialized equipment. Therefore, considering LBR, in addition to LLD, is important for THA preoperative planning and may also inform postoperative rehabilitation strategies and patient counseling.

This study had some limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size; therefore, caution is necessary when generalizing the results. Although uniform surgical technique and postoperative management may be advantageous for reducing variability, larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm the reproducibility and external validity of the measurements, including LBR, which may be affected by patient effort or pain during examination. Second, the study did not assess the effect of reduced lumbar flexibility on patient-reported quality of life. Decreased lumbar flexibility may influence not only residual PO but also patient satisfaction and postoperative low back pain, and these outcomes should be examined in future studies. Third, hip abductor muscle strength and functional status were not assessed in this study, although these factors may also contribute to coronal pelvic alignment and residual PO after THA. Fourth, follow-up was limited to 1 year postoperatively, and the long-term relationship between coronal balance and clinical outcomes remains unclear. In addition, the analysis focused exclusively on patients with D-PO; patients with U-PO were not evaluated. Because D-PO may involve distinct pathophysiologies and compensatory mechanisms, further comparative studies are warranted. Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. The affected and unaffected-side LBR were evaluated separately, and a specific cutoff value for predicting residual PO was identified. Clarifying residual PO mechanisms from the perspective of lateral lumbar bending provides clinically meaningful insights for patients with DHOA undergoing THA.

Conclusion

We investigated the factors associated with residual PO after THA in patients with DHOA and demonstrated that reduced affected-side LBR was an independent risk factor for residual PO. The affected-side LBR cutoff value of 4.5°, identified by ROC analysis, may serve as an indicator for predicting patients in whom postoperative pelvic leveling is unlikely to be achieved, and reflects insufficient coronal compensatory capacity in cases with D-PO. Assessing lumbar lateral bending range, in addition to LLD, may be critical when evaluating coronal balance before THA; particular caution is warranted regarding postoperative residual PO in patients with reduced affected-side LBR.
