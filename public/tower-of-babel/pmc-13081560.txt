Future perspectives in prehospital emergency medicine
Authors: Åke Erling L Andresen, Ulf-Erik Kongsgaard, Thomas Kristiansen, Hans Morten Lossius
Published: 2026

Changing context and resource strain

Prehospital emergency medicine (PHEM) encompasses the structured assessment, initial stabilization, definitive prehospital treatment, and safe transportation of patients with acute illness or injury [1]. These services are delivered by emergency medical systems operating across multiple tiers of clinical competence.

Over the past decades, the distance between patients in acute need of emergency interventions and the locations of specialized care, has grown. Modern health care systems in the Western societies increasingly rely on PHEM to bridge this gap. Limited intrahospital resources dictate that PHEM continue to adapt its gatekeeper function, triaging patients to the appropriate level of care. PHEM faces converging pressures from demographic change, technological innovation with artificial intelligence (AI), and professional development.

An ageing population with a higher burden of chronic disease and multimorbidity will place significant strain on both PHEM and the general healthcare systems [2]. Demographic shifts and workforce shortages, particular in rural areas, will limit available health care resources. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted pre- and inhospital capacity problems, resulting in calls for more robust contingency planning [3].

Constraints in both prehospital and in-hospital resources necessitate innovative strategies to optimize resource utilization and to mitigate disparities in access to healthcare. Mobile health units and community-based emergency response programs have been advocated [4]. Public education campaigns aimed at improving recognition of medical emergencies and promoting the provision of first aid should target laypersons, as well as firefighters and police officers, who frequently serve as first responders. The strain on the healt care system demands a higher rate of non-conveyance of patients and alternative care pathways [5]. The future also represents opportunities, and we aim to explore key factors regarding the development of PHEM.

Technological advancements in the field

Developments in technology represent some of the key drivers of change in PHEM. The adoption of telemedicine enables paramedics to consult with specialists in real time, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and decision-making in the field. Portable diagnostic tools, such as point-of-care ultrasound, are increasingly accessible, allowing PHEM personnel to perform rapid imaging and identify life-threatening conditions [6, 7].

These advancements are expanding the scope of prehospital interventions. Advanced therapies, including prehospital blood transfusions, resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta, the potential use of CT scanners in helicopters, and extracorporeal life support, are emerging innovations that may significantly reduce time to treatment in time-sensitive emergencies [8]. Early administration of new medications by paramedics in the prehospital setting has the potential to reduce morbidity across a wide range of patient groups.

Unmanned aerial vehicles is offering rapid scene overview capabilities and enabling the delivery of automated external defibrillators [9]. This technology is expected to expand in the future, potentially including the transport of PHEM personnel and retrieval of patients [10]. Additionally, mobile phones, and wearable devices with health applications facilitate video streaming and continuous data transmission from the emergency site to emergency medical communication centres, enhancing situational awareness and early clinical assessment.

Artificial intelligence and data use

AI and machine learning algorithms are poised to revolutionize how technology is used in PHEM. At system level, predictive models can support dispatchers in prioritizing calls, allocating resources, and anticipating surges, while integrated digital platforms facilitate real-time information sharing across agencies [11]. Algorithems based on large datasets, can estimate risk factors and severity and refine clinical treatment and triage decisions in time critical incidents. However, the benefits of AI, depends on data quality, appropriate training, and clinical knowledge. PHEM providers must understand the basics of strengths and limitations of AI techcnology, and future studies should compare the new methods to the conventional standard of care. It is thus likely that AI technology increase, rather than reduce, the need of PHEM provider competence in the future.

Training, standards, and research

As the complexity of PHEM increases, so do the demands on knowledge and quality. Simulation-based training and structured team exercises provide immersive, hands-on experience in managing complex scenarios, improving both technical- and non-technical skills. Providers must continuously integrate emerging evidence to manage time-critical conditions effectively. To standardize and optimize patient care, clinical guidelines must be high quality and evidence based, thereby reducing unwarranted variation [12]. However, the dynamic and unpredictable nature of PHEM makes it essential to recognize when deviation from guidelines is appropriate and when strict adherence is required.

Because PHEM involves complex interventions delivered in heterogenous environments, conventional randomized trials are difficult [13]. Robust methodological frameworks for the evaluation of complex interventions, together with enhanced digital data capture systems, will be crucial to strengthening the evidence base [14]. The UK Medical Research Council has published a framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions which should be increasingly used in PHEM research [15].

Preparedness, ethics, and the road ahead

Future PHEM must be resilient to broad range of threats, including climate-related disasters, natural catastrophes, and complex security challenges, including hybrid-warfare. Effective emergency preparedness necessitates coordinated, cross-sectoral planning, resilient infrastructure, and adaptive operational models capable of sustaining essential functions during disruptions to critical systems. Concurrently, the expansion of technical capabilities and the intensified use of data raise complex ethical considerations, including the protection of patient autonomy, the validity of informed consent in emergency contexts, and data protection issues.

Ensuring equitable access to services will be important, while supporting the mental health and long-term sustainability of the PHEM workforce.Expanding the scope of practice for paramedics and emergency medical technicians with advanced training and new roles can enable them to manage a broader range of conditions in the field. PHEM in the communities focusing on preventive care and chronic disease management allows providers to address non-emergency health issues and reduce the burden on emergency departments, possibly also with a growing focus on non-conveyance of patients.

Conclusion

The future of PHEM is both promising and complex, as several factors will reshape its role. Further knowledge about cost-effectiveness for different patient groups and conditions should be prioritized. Leveraging community first responder programs and integrating laypeople into the emergency response system can enhance overall efficiency, while better surveillance and patient monitoring may allow for more community based management of non-critical conditions. Ensuring equitable access to advanced technologies and care models, addressing ethical concerns related to patient data, and navigating regulatory hurdles will be critical. Decision makers must address systemic disparities, ethical dilemmas, and resource limitations to ensure these innovations benefit all members of society and that PHEM withstands challenges from limited resources and growing needs. By embracing innovation, fostering collaboration, and addressing systemic challenges, PHEM can continue to save lives and enhance public health in the years to come.
