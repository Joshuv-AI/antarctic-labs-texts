The prevalence of depressive symptoms in surrogate mothers following child relinquishment: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Pegah Rashidian, Ehsan Amini-Salehi, Mohammad Haddadi, Seyyed Amirhossein Salehi, Sepide Javankiani, Shiva Abedinzadeh, Shaghayegh Karami, Mohammadamin Parsaei, Marjan Ghaemi, Sedigheh Hantoushzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Surrogacy is a common assisted reproductive practice, yet its psychological impact on surrogate mothers remains a topic of concern. Understanding the prevalence of depressive symptoms among surrogate mothers is crucial for ensuring adequate mental health support. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the prevalence of depressive symptoms among surrogate mothers.Methods:A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase for studies published between 1 January 1990 and 31 December 2025. Reference lists of eligible articles were also screened. Studies examining rates of depressive symptoms in surrogate mothers within 1 year postpartum were included in the meta-analysis. A random-effects model was employed to estimate the pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms.Results:Eleven studies encompassing 637 surrogate mothers met the inclusion criteria, with six studies qualifying for meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms was 3% (95% CI: 0–16%). Subgroup analyses revealed a higher prevalence in India (27.3%) compared to studies from the UK and USA (0%). Additionally, studies with a low risk of bias reported a higher prevalence (9%) than those with a moderate risk (0%). No significant publication bias was detected.Conclusions:The overall prevalence of depressive symptoms among surrogate mothers following child relinquishment appears low; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory in light of the limited underlying evidence. Further well-designed studies are needed to clarify contextual factors influencing mental health outcomes in surrogacy.

Introduction

A significant number of women experience infertility, defined as the inability to conceive after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse[1], due to biological or medical factors, leading them to seek assisted reproductive technologies (ART) as a means to conceive[2–4]. Surrogacy, a form of third-party reproduction, serves as a viable option for individuals and couples facing infertility, same-sex couples, and those with medical conditions preventing pregnancy. This practice involves a woman, the surrogate, carrying and delivering a child for the intended parent or parents[5]. Additional motivations include advanced maternal age, recurrent pregnancy loss, single parenthood by choice, and personal or career-related considerations[6]. While surrogacy can be altruistic or commercial, both arrangements require the surrogate to relinquish the child to the intended parent or parents after birth[5]. Most research indicates that surrogates are primarily driven by a desire to assist childless couples, with few references to financial benefits[7]. Surrogacy has led to numerous ethical and legal controversies, and it is considered illegal in certain countries, including Germany and Sweden[8,9]. Maternal complications that may jeopardize the health of the surrogate mother include uterine rupture, preeclampsia, and gestational diabetes[7,10]. Pregnancy represents a period of potential vulnerability for numerous women, primarily due to the mental health challenges associated with psychological and social changes occurring during gestation. A recent study found that mental health issues affect around 20% of pregnant women during both the prenatal and postpartum phases[12]. Additionally, the surrogate’s experience differs from a typical pregnancy because the involvement of the intended parent or parents in treatment and decision-making, the pregnancy’s purpose, and the surrogate’s relationship with the intended parent or parents all affect surrogacy satisfaction[10,13]. Some studies suggest that surrogacy is predominantly a positive experience for many surrogate mothers. However, given that surrogate mothers may also encounter challenging experiences, surrogacy can be considered a high-risk emotional process, potentially leading to anxiety and depression[9].

Postpartum depression (PPD) in surrogate mothers may arise from the unique emotional process of child relinquishment, compounded by hormonal changes and limited attachment[14]. Research on the psychological dimensions of surrogacy, particularly concerning depressive symptoms, is limited, and existing studies yield varying results. Current prevalence estimates of depressive symptoms range from 0% to 36% across studies, often assessed using self-report tools such as the Edinburgh Depression Scale or Beck Depression Inventory[11,13]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis aimed at evaluating the prevalence of postpartum depressive symptoms among surrogate women following child relinquishment, drawing on available studies, which may provide a clearer understanding of the psychological impact of surrogacy on birth mothers.

Materials and methods

The review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines[15], ensuring methodological rigor and transparency throughout the research process. In addition, this systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the AMSTAR (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews) guidelines[16]. This work was also reported in line with the TITAN (Transparency in the Reporting of Artificial Intelligence) guidelines[17]. No artificial intelligence tools were used in the research or manuscript development.

Research question

This systematic review aimed to evaluate the prevalence of depressive symptoms in surrogate women following the relinquishment of the child. The primary objective of the meta-analysis was to assess the prevalence of depressive symptoms among surrogate women, specifically focusing on studies with a follow-up period not exceeding 1 year.

Search strategy

The search for this systematic review covered studies published from 1 January 1990 to 31 December 2025. A comprehensive search strategy was implemented across four key electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase. To ensure a thorough examination, the reference lists of included studies were also screened for additional relevant articles. The search was performed without language restrictions to ensure broad inclusivity. Keywords were carefully selected to capture two main themes: surrogates and depression. Detailed information on the search terms, filters, and database-specific strategies is provided in Supplemental Digital Content Material 1, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B373.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were deemed eligible for inclusion if they investigated depressive symptoms in surrogate women, provided sufficient data to calculate the prevalence of depressive symptoms, or compared rates of depressive symptoms between surrogate and non-surrogate women. Depressive symptoms were operationalized as elevated scores on validated self-report screening tools (e.g., the Edinburgh Depression Scale, Beck Depression Inventory) indicating probable depression, and searches included terms such as dysthymia to capture persistent depressive symptoms. Prevalence was calculated based on the proportion of surrogates meeting tool-specific cut-off scores for probable depression. Additionally, studies that reported the mean scores from depression assessment tools were included. For the systematic review, studies assessing depressive symptoms at any time post-relinquishment were included. For the meta-analysis, inclusion was limited to studies with follow-up not exceeding 1 year postpartum to focus on immediate postpartum effects.

Studies were excluded if they did not address the primary outcome, lacked essential data, involved animal subjects, or were classified as review articles, case reports, case series, brief communications, meeting abstracts, book chapters, letters, editorials, commentaries, correspondence, or study protocols.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of identified records for relevance. Inter-rater agreement for title/abstract screening was 92%. The full texts of potentially eligible studies were then assessed independently by the two reviewers based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted if consensus could not be reached.

Data collection

Data extraction was carried out independently by two reviewers. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted to ensure accuracy. The extracted data from the included studies were systematically organized into three main categories: (1) general information (first author, publication year, country of origin, and study design), (2) surrogate participants’ characteristics (number of participants, number of depressed and non-depressed women, and age), and (3) depression-related data (assessment tools used, mean depression scores, and comparisons with non-surrogate women).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of each included study was assessed by two independent reviewers using a 10-item checklist developed by Hoy et al[18]. This checklist is designed to evaluate the risk of bias (ROB) in studies examining prevalence data. It includes four questions related to external validity, such as sample representativeness and random selection, and six questions focusing on internal validity, including the clarity of case definitions and consistency in data collection methods. For each question, studies were rated as “Yes” (indicating low ROB), “No” (indicating high ROB), or “Unclear” when there was insufficient information to make a definitive judgment. Based on these ratings, studies were classified into one of three categories: (1) Low ROB, (2) Moderate ROB, and (3) High ROB. Disagreements between reviewers regarding the ROB were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted for a final decision. This approach ensured a thorough and consistent assessment of study quality.

Meta-analysis

Extracted data were first organized in Microsoft Excel and then imported into Stata/MP 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) for analysis. The meta-analysis aimed to determine the prevalence of depressive symptoms among surrogate mothers after child relinquishment. The effect size was reported as a proportion (percentage) with a corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Q test and the I2 statistic. An I2 value greater than 50% was interpreted as substantial heterogeneity. The extent of heterogeneity was visually represented with a forest plot. Additionally, a Galbraith plot was generated to illustrate the positioning of individual studies within the 95% CI and to identify any significant deviations.

A random-effects model employing the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method was used to estimate pooled prevalence rates. Sensitivity analysis was performed using a leave-one-out approach, systematically excluding each study to evaluate its influence on the overall findings.

To explore sources of heterogeneity, two subgroup analyses were conducted: one based on the overall ROB in the studies and another based on the country of origin of each study.

Publication bias was examined using both qualitative and quantitative methods. A contour-enhanced funnel plot was created for visual assessment of asymmetry. Additionally, Begg’s nonparametric rank correlation test[19] and Egger’s regression test[20] were applied to statistically determine the presence of small-study effects. A P-value below 0.05 in these tests suggested potential publication bias. Moreover, Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill method was implemented to adjust the pooled estimates accordingly[21].

Moderator and subgroup analyses

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity in the estimated prevalence of depressive symptoms among surrogate mothers, predefined subgroup analyses and a meta-regression were conducted. The selection of moderators was guided by theoretical relevance, prior literature, and data availability across the included studies, while also considering the limited number of studies eligible for meta-analysis.

Subgroup analyses were performed according to risk of bias (ROB) and country of origin. ROB was examined as a moderator because methodological quality is known to influence prevalence estimates in observational studies, with lower-quality studies potentially underestimating or overestimating outcomes. Country of origin was selected a priori due to well-documented differences in surrogacy practices, legal frameworks, socioeconomic conditions, and access to healthcare across regions, all of which may substantially affect maternal mental health outcomes.

In addition, a meta-regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between age and the prevalence of depressive symptoms. Maternal age is a recognized factor in perinatal mental health and was among the few continuous variables consistently reported across the included studies, making it suitable for quantitative exploration.

Other potential moderators, such as type of surrogacy, depression assessment tool, and follow-up duration, were considered but not examined quantitatively. These variables were either inconsistently reported, showed insufficient variability, or were available in too few studies to allow reliable analysis.

Results

Search results

The systematic search initially yielded a total of 1132 records. Following the removal of 405 duplicate entries, 727 unique records remained. Subsequent screening of titles and abstracts resulted in the selection of 32 potentially relevant studies for full-text assessment. After a thorough evaluation based on the predefined inclusion criteria, nine articles were deemed eligible for inclusion in the systematic review[22–30]. Additionally, a manual review of the reference lists from the included studies identified two relevant records[31,32]. Study characteristics

A total of eleven studies were included in this systematic review, encompassing a combined sample of 637 surrogate mothers. The sample size of the included studies ranged from 15 to 295 participants. The predominant study design was the cohort design, with publication dates spanning from 1999 to 2023. Among these, six studies provided sufficient data for quantitative synthesis and were incorporated into the meta-analysis[22–24,28,31,32]. Eight studies explicitly reported the depression assessment tools utilized, with a total of six different questionnaires employed across the studies[24–31]. The timing of depression assessment varied significantly, ranging from 4–6 months postpartum to up to 12 years after pregnancy. Notably, one study investigated the impact of the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic on the mental health and depression levels of surrogate mothers. While its findings were reviewed, this study was not included in the meta-analysis due to its unique focus[29]. Quality assessment

A comprehensive evaluation of study quality revealed that nine of the included studies had a moderate ROB[22,23,25–27,29–32], while the remaining two were classified as having a low ROB[24,28]. A detailed summary of the quality assessment results is provided in Supplemental Digital Content Materials 2, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B374. The primary contributors to an increased ROB were concerns regarding external validity, specifically the representativeness of the target population, the adequacy of the sampling frame, and the methodology used for case selection. Conversely, internal validity was found to be more robust. In all eight studies, key internal validity criteria – including the data collection approach (whether conducted directly or via proxy), case definition, and data collection methodology – were consistently rated as “yes,” demonstrating methodological rigor in these aspects.

Review of the findings

A review of eleven studies provides valuable insights into the prevalence of depressive symptoms among surrogate mothers following the relinquishment of their child.

Parkinson et al (1999) identified five cases of mild, transient postpartum “maternal blues” among 95 in-vitro fertilization (IVF) surrogates in the USA, with no instances of PPD[22]. Similarly, Söderström-Anttila found low rates of depressive symptoms among surrogates, with only two out of 17 requiring psychotherapy or antidepressants[23]. Van de Akker (2003) found no evidence of depression in a sample of 15 surrogate mothers[31]. In a subsequent study in 2005, Van de Akker examined 23 surrogates and found no indication of true postnatal depression within 6 months of relinquishment, despite previous self-reported postnatal rates of depressive symptoms of 18 and 21% among surrogates, respectively[32]. A 2007 study by Van de Akker further assessed postnatal depression at multiple time points (6 days, 6 weeks, and 6 months) in 21 surrogate mothers and 11 intended mothers. Depression scores revealed no significant differences between the two groups[25]. Notably, the 2005 and 2007 studies by Van de Akker appear to have involved overlapping populations, though they employed distinct methods – one assessing the number of depression cases among 23 surrogates and the other comparing depression scores between 21 surrogates and 11 intended mothers[25,32]. Jadva et al (2003) examined the mental health of 33 surrogates 1-year post-birth, finding an average Edinburgh Depression Scale score of 4.88 ± 3.1. No significant difference in depression scores was observed between full and partial surrogacy. Nine percent of surrogates reported psychological issues before and after birth, with 6% seeking help from a general practitioner[24]. In a 2015 follow-up, 18 surrogates from the original cohort were revisited. One surrogate scored within the mild depression range, while the others exhibited no signs of depression. Notably, three surrogates had been prescribed stimulants or sedatives since the previous study, and two were on medication at the time of the interview, although one had previously discontinued antidepressant use after her own child’s birth[26]. In 2014, Imrie et al[30] published a study involving 31 surrogates, categorized as 14 gestational, 11 genetic, and 6 both gestational and genetic. Of these, 20 participants had previously taken part in a 2003 study by Jadva et al[24]. The surrogates completed the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) questionnaire 5–12 years after their delivery. Among the participants, five surrogates were pregnant with further surrogacy arrangements at the time of data collection. The study found no significant difference in psychological well-being between genetic and gestational surrogates. Furthermore, none of the participants scored within the clinical depression range, with 28 showing minimal symptoms, 2 indicating mild symptoms, and 1 exhibiting moderate symptoms[30]. Lorenceau et al (2015) compared 44 surrogate mothers (28 gestational and 16 traditional) with 32 normative mothers and found lower levels of anxiety and depression in both surrogate groups relative to the normative sample[27].

In contrast, in 2018, Lamba et al reported higher rates of depressive symptoms among 50 Indian surrogates, with 36% experiencing severe depression during pregnancy, compared to 13.8% of 69 expectant mothers. Postpartum, 27.3% of surrogates still exhibited severe depression, compared to 13.3% of expectant mothers. The study reported response rates of 90% (45 surrogates) and 71% (49 expectant mothers) participating in the follow-up interviews. Predictors of PPD included low social support, secrecy about the surrogacy arrangement, and criticism from others[28]. A further study explored the mental health impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on surrogates. Yee et al found that 65.1% of surrogates reported at least one mental health issue, such as panic attacks, anxiety, and PPD, during the COVID-19 pandemic. Although the findings may not be generalizable due to the specific circumstances of the study, they offer important insights into the mental health challenges faced by surrogates during the pandemic[29].

Meta-analysis

Prevalence of depressive symptoms among surrogate women

A prevalence meta-analysis was conducted using data from six studies[22–24,28,31,32] that assessed the rate of depression among surrogate women up to 1 year after delivery. The overall pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms was estimated at 3% (95% CI: 0–16%), with substantial heterogeneity observed across studies (I2 = 83.25%). The prediction interval ranged from 0.001 to 0.505. The Galbraith plot analysis identified two studies, Lamba (2018)[28] and Parkinson (1999)[22], as outliers because they fell outside the 95% confidence interval. This finding suggests that these studies may be key contributors to the heterogeneity observed in the meta-analysis. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the pooled prevalence estimates ranged from 0% to 5%, indicating no single study had a dominant influence. The lowest prevalence [0% (95% CI: 0.00–0.03), P-value = 0.690] was observed when Lamba (2018)[28] was omitted, while the highest [5% (95% CI: 0.00–0.17), P-value = 0.122] occurred with the exclusion of Parkinson (1999)[22]. Despite wide confidence intervals, the overall findings remained robust.

To further explore sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted. First, studies were stratified by ROB. The pooled prevalence in studies with a low ROB was 9% (95% CI: 0–49%; I2 = 93.93%), whereas studies with a moderate ROB reported a pooled prevalence of 0% (95% CI: 0–6%; I2 = 53.72%). Second, a subgroup analysis based on the country of origin revealed notable differences: studies from the UK and USA reported a prevalence of 0%, while those from Finland and India reported prevalence values of 12% and 27%, respectively. Results should be interpreted cautiously, given the small subgroup sizes.

Publication bias was assessed using both Egger’s and Begg’s tests, which yielded P-values of 0.88 and 0.45, respectively, indicating no significant publication bias. Meta-regression

Lastly, a meta-regression was performed to assess the effect of age on the prevalence of depressive symptoms. There was a non-significant trend suggesting that depressive symptoms decreased with increasing age (P = 0.833), which was accompanied by high heterogeneity (I2 = 93.77%) (Fig. 2). Results should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample sizes.

Discussion

The systematic review and meta-analysis examined the prevalence of depressive symptoms among surrogate mothers after relinquishing the child, based on eleven studies involving 637 participants. Six studies provided data for meta-analysis, revealing an overall pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms of 3% (95% CI: 0–16%), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 83.25%). Two studies, Lamba (2018) and Parkinson (1999), were identified as outliers, contributing to this heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis showed prevalence estimates ranging from 0% to 5%, indicating no single study dominated the results. Subgroup analyses revealed differences based on risk of bias (ROB) and country of origin. Studies with a low ROB reported a higher prevalence (9%), while those with a moderate ROB reported 0%. Geographically, studies from the UK and USA reported 0% prevalence, whereas Finland and India reported 12% and 27%, respectively. No significant publication bias was detected. The review highlighted generally low rates of depressive symptoms among surrogates, with most studies reporting minimal or no clinical depression. However, Lamba (2018) found higher rates of depressive symptoms in Indian surrogates. Additionally, a study on the COVID-19 pandemic noted increased mental health issues among surrogates, though it was not included in the meta-analysis. High heterogeneity may stem from differences in surrogacy types, legal frameworks, and socio-economic contexts[33], making pooled estimates conceptual approximations rather than direct comparisons.

Although this review represents the most comprehensive synthesis currently available on depressive symptoms among surrogate mothers, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Only six studies were eligible for quantitative synthesis, and several included studies had relatively small sample sizes, which may limit the precision and generalizability of the pooled estimates. Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies, likely reflecting differences in study populations, socio-cultural contexts, surrogacy regulations, timing of assessments, and the instruments used to evaluate depressive symptoms. Consequently, the pooled prevalence estimate and subgroup findings should be considered exploratory rather than definitive and interpreted within the context of the limited available evidence.

Surrogacy, while offering a pathway to parenthood for many individuals and couples, has been associated with potential psychological challenges, including depression. The emotional complexity of surrogacy can affect all parties involved – intended parents, surrogates, and even the child. For surrogates, the process of carrying a child for someone else can lead to feelings of loss, grief, or identity confusion after birth, which may contribute to depressive symptoms[25]. Intended parents, particularly those who have faced infertility or pregnancy loss, may experience heightened anxiety or depression during the surrogacy process due to the uncertainty and lack of control over the pregnancy[34]. Additionally, societal stigma or lack of understanding about surrogacy can exacerbate feelings of isolation or stress. Research suggests that psychological support and counseling for both surrogates and intended parents can mitigate these risks, promoting better mental health outcomes[5]. While surrogacy itself is not a direct cause of depression, the unique emotional and social dynamics involved may increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms, underscoring the importance of comprehensive mental health care throughout the surrogacy journey.

The relatively higher rates of depression among surrogate mothers in developing countries (India, in this case) compared to their counterparts in developed countries, as demonstrated in this study, may be explained by several socio-economic, cultural, and healthcare factors. In less developed countries, several surrogate mothers come from low-income backgrounds and use surrogacy as an important source of income. This dependency on income, along with a lack of prospective employment, creates significant stress and anxiety about the adequacy of their work as judged by potential employers, which can lead to depression[8]. Surrogacy in these regions is often shrouded in social stigma and secrecy. In narrow cultural contexts, surrogate mothers may be stigmatized, ostracized, or pressured to hide their surrogacy arrangements, producing a state of alienation, suffering, and distress[35]. The study by Lamba et al (2018)[28] identified low social support, concealment, and stigmatization by society as major predictors of PPD for Indian surrogates. In poorer countries, this is compounded by limited access to appropriate healthcare or mental health services. Prenatal or postnatal care may be lacking for surrogate mothers, leading to mental health conditions like depression going undiagnosed or untreated[36]. Cultural norms and legal frameworks also factor in; in certain parts of the world, surrogacy is poorly regulated or openly exploitative, worsening mental health struggles. By contrast, in wealthier nations such as the UK and USA, there is a higher availability of social support systems as well as less stigma, which correspond to overall lower rates of depressive symptoms seen in surrogates from these areas[37]. We have noted a similar pattern regarding kidney donors, where those from economically poor countries like India, with socio-economic pressures, lack of post-operative care, and social stigma, end up suffering from poor mental health outcomes[38]. Studies have indicated that kidney donation in low-income settings is associated with higher rates of depression and anxiety compared to high-income countries, where donors are provided with robust medical and psychological care[39]. Addressing these contextual factors – for example, by improving social support, reducing stigma, enhancing access to health care, and instituting protective legal frameworks – is essential to mitigate the risk to the mental health of surrogate mothers in poorer countries[40–44].

The overall pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms was 3% among surrogate mothers according to the meta-analysis, which is significantly lower than the population-level prevalence of PPD, which varies from 10% to 15% globally[45]. This finding aligns with the results of Lorenceau et al, which indicated that the mean score of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) reflected lower levels of anxiety and depression in surrogate groups compared with the normative sample[27]. The overall finding shows that while surrogate mothers in decent conditions have lower depression compared with the regular postpartum population, mothers in resource-constrained settings can have similar or even higher depression levels compared with the general postpartum population; hence, there is a need to address socio-economic and cultural factors in understanding and intervening for mental health.

Since most surrogate pregnancies result from IVF, comparing PPD between IVF groups and the general population can be helpful. The prevalence of depressive symptoms among women who undergo IVF and subsequently give birth appears to be comparable to, or in some cases slightly lower than, the rates of PPD observed in the general population. Studies on post-IVF depression suggest that women who conceive through IVF may experience PPD rates ranging from 10% to 20%, which overlap with the general PPD prevalence of 10–15% but can be higher in certain contexts[46]. This similarity is notable, as IVF pregnancies are often associated with significant emotional, physical, and financial stress, which could theoretically increase the risk of depression. However, some research indicates that IVF mothers may have lower PPD rates due to the extensive psychological support and preparation they receive throughout the IVF process, as well as the high levels of motivation and resilience often seen in this group[47,48].

This relatively low pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms may also be explained by several underlying protective mechanisms. One important factor is psychological adaptation and cognitive reframing among surrogate mothers, who often enter surrogacy with a clear understanding and acceptance of child relinquishment as a pre-agreed outcome, which may reduce the risk of unresolved grief. In addition, structured clinical follow-up, counseling during IVF-based surrogacy cycles, and selection processes that often include psychological screening may further enhance emotional preparedness[10,49].

This study has important clinical implications for the mental health care of surrogate mothers, especially in resource-limited settings, along with relevant training for health care providers working in this area. A low prevalence of depressive symptoms (3%) among surrogates suggests that in high-income countries, carefully regulated surrogacy programs, combined with adequate social and psychological support, are likely to minimize potential mental health harms. But higher rates of depressive symptoms in poorer countries, such as India (27.3%), indicate the need for focused action in these countries. And in under-resourced settings, clinicians should emphasize the need to screen surrogate mothers for depression and ensure access to mental health services, including counseling and support groups. Tackling socio-economic stressors, reducing stigma, and enhancing social support networks are equally critical to lowering depression risk. The study also highlights the need for individualized care for surrogates that accounts for cultural context, legal protections, and access to medical care. The integration of mental health support in surrogacy programs can address the psychological and emotional challenges faced by surrogate mothers[36].

Importantly, several protective factors may mitigate the risk of depressive symptoms among surrogate mothers. These include adequate psychological preparation before and during the surrogacy process, strong social and familial support systems, access to ongoing counseling services, and voluntary, well-informed participation in surrogacy arrangements free from coercion or undue financial pressure. Such factors may enhance emotional resilience and improve overall mental health outcomes throughout the surrogacy journey.

Strengths of this study include the following: First, it uses a systematic review and meta-analysis approach, thus offering a broader synthesis that considers studies across various contexts. Second, although five studies were not included in the meta-analysis, the findings of all eleven studies were thoroughly reviewed. Third, subgroup analyses based on ROB and country of origin are conducted, providing further depth to the findings and helping identify sources of heterogeneity. Finally, the study follows the PRISMA guidelines, providing methodological rigor and transparency.

However, this study has several limitations. First, only six studies were eligible for meta-analysis, and the sample sizes of individual studies were generally small, limiting the representativeness and precision of the pooled estimates. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed among studies, which may reflect differences in participant characteristics, surrogacy arrangements, legal frameworks, healthcare systems, and in the timing of depression assessment across studies. Although all studies assessed depressive symptoms within the first postpartum year, follow-up periods were not uniform, which may have influenced reported prevalence estimates. Earlier assessments may capture acute psychological distress, whereas later assessments may reflect adaptation over time, thereby contributing to variability in reported outcomes. Third, depressive symptoms were assessed using different screening instruments and diagnostic thresholds, which may have affected comparability across studies. Finally, subgroup analyses were based on a limited number of studies and participants, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. These limitations highlight the need for careful interpretation of the findings and underscore the limited evidence currently available on this topic.

Future research should focus on large, prospective, multicenter studies using standardized and validated measures of depressive symptoms to improve comparability across studies. Additional research from diverse geographic and socio-economic settings is needed to better understand the influence of cultural, legal, and healthcare factors on surrogate mothers’ mental health. Furthermore, longitudinal studies with extended follow-up beyond the first postpartum year are essential to evaluate the long-term psychological outcomes of surrogacy, including the persistence, resolution, or delayed onset of depressive symptoms following child relinquishment. Such studies would also help identify risk and protective factors associated with sustained psychological well-being over time.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis provides the most comprehensive synthesis to date of the available evidence on depressive symptoms among surrogate mothers following child relinquishment. Overall, the pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms was low; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of studies available for quantitative synthesis, the relatively small sample sizes of the included studies, and the substantial between-study heterogeneity. While marked variation was observed across geographical settings, with higher prevalence estimates reported in resource-limited contexts, these findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted considering the limited underlying evidence. Nevertheless, the available data suggest that contextual factors, including socio-economic conditions, social support, stigma, legal frameworks, and access to healthcare services, may play an important role in shaping the psychological well-being of surrogate mothers. From a clinical perspective, these findings support the integration of psychological assessment, counseling, and ongoing mental health support into surrogacy care pathways, particularly in settings where social and healthcare resources may be limited.
