Beg to Differ: Understanding Reasoning-Answer Misalignment Across Languages
Anaelia Ovalle , Candace Ross , Sebastian Ruder , Adina Williams , Karen Ullrich , Mark Ibrahim , Levent Sagun

Abstract
Large language models demonstrate strong reasoning capabilities through chain-of-thought prompting, but whether this reasoning quality transfers across languages remains underexplored. We introduce a human-validated framework to evaluate whether model-generated reasoning traces logically support their conclusions across languages. Analyzing 65k reasoning traces from GlobalMMLU questions across 6 languages and 6 frontier models, we uncover a critical blind spot: while models achieve high task accuracy, their reasoning can fail to support their conclusions. Reasoning traces in non-Latin scripts show at least twice as much misalignment between their reasoning and conclusions than those in Latin scripts. We develop an error taxonomy through human annotation to characterize these failures, finding they stem primarily from evidential errors (unsupported claims, ambiguous facts) followed by illogical reasoning steps. Our findings demonstrate that current multilingual evaluation practices provide an incomplete picture of model reasoning capabilities and highlight the need for reasoning-aware evaluation frameworks.

Introduction

Reasoning models have demonstrated remarkable reasoning capabilities through the use of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, achieving strong performance across several tasks
(
Arcuschin et al. 2025
;
Lai and Nissim 2024
;
Barua et al. 2025
)
.
However, these reasoning capabilities exhibit significant disparities across languages, with models performing best for high resource languages
(
Shi et al. 2022
;
Qin et al. 2023
;
Lai and Nissim 2024
)
.
While multilingual evaluation typically focuses on answer accuracy, we argue this provides an incomplete picture of model capabilities.
A model might produce correct answers through flawed reasoning (
Figure 1
), or conversely, demonstrate sound logical steps while arriving at incorrect conclusions.
Understanding this distinction is crucial for assessing whether models truly transfer reasoning skills across languages or simply memorize.

To address this, we propose a framework that evaluates whether model-generated reasoning traces logically support their conclusions across languages, measuring crosslingual
reasoning–answer alignment
.
We hypothesize that models exhibit different reasoning processes depending on language resource level and script type, with differences in how they access and use factual knowledge to support their conclusions.
We isolate reasoning traces from their context: evaluators assess whether reasoning steps alone provide sufficient justification to reach the model’s conclusion, without seeing the model’s final answer.
Through this human annotation process, we develop an error taxonomy that captures common reasoning failures, which then informs our automated evaluation approach that we validate against the human judgments.
We apply this framework to analyze reasoning traces across five languages with different resource levels and scripts using the Global MMLU dataset.
We address three key research questions: (RQ1) How does reasoning-answer misalignment vary between high/low resource (or Latin vs. non-Latin script languages)? (RQ2) Can automatic evaluation reliably assess reasoning trace quality compared to human judgment? (RQ3) What are the primary failure modes in multilingual reasoning traces?

Our evaluation framework reveals a interesting finding:
While answer accuracy predictably dips from high to low-resource languages (80% -> 70%), reasoning-answer alignment disproportionately drops over two-fold.
In other words, models on average, produced LRL reasoning traces that logically support those conclusions—getting the right answer for the wrong reasons.
We found that lower-resourced languages were able can reach correct conclusions through reasoning that does not logically support those conclusions, getting the right answer for the wrong reasons.
We validate that automated evaluation correlates strongly with human judgment (RQ2), enabling systematic analysis of reasoning failures.
Critically, this misalignment occurs both when models answer correctly and incorrectly, and varies significantly between Latin and non-Latin script languages (RQ1), indicating systematic issues with reasoning processes rather than simple knowledge gaps.

Our key contributions are: (1) evidence of systematic reasoning-answer misalignment in multilingual settings, with alignment dropping twice as much for non-latin scripts than latin-scripts; (2) human-validated evaluation framework for evaluating reasoning coherence at scale; (3) analysis showing misalignment occurs independently of answer correctness; and (4) characterization of how reasoning failures are distributed across language scripts and subjects.

These findings reveal that answer accuracy provides an incomplete picture of multilingual model capabilities. The reasoning-answer misalignment we identify suggests that models may be learning different problem-solving strategies across languages rather than a learning a uniform and coherent reasoning process.
Our results indicate that reasoning quality assessment requires separate evaluation from answer correctness in multilingual settings.

Benchmarking multilingual reasoning has largely centered on mathematical domains, with datasets such as MGSM

Yet, these works largely equate multilingual reasoning evaluation with performance on reasoning-intensive tasks, judged by whether models reach correct answers, rather than by whether their reasoning processes actually support stated conclusions.

Consistency and faithfulness in LLM reasoning

Recent work has highlighted persistent issues with the faithfulness of LLM reasoning, particularly for English inputs.
Studies show that chain-of-thought traces often diverge from genuine problem-solving processes
(
Turpin et al. 2023
)
, with models producing post-hoc rationalizations rather than faithful reasoning explanations
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)

(

and in real-world scenarios
(
Arcuschin et al. 2025
)
.
To address consistency,
Wan et al. 2024

Liu et al. 2024
analyze step-level self-contradictions on ambiguous questions.
In contrast, our focus lies on reasoning trace inference: whether model-generated traces logically support their conclusions in non-ambiguous settings, and crucially, how this differs between non-English languages and language resource level.
Moreover, research that enables automatic prediction of reasoning process failure modes remains understudied.

3
Methodology

We introduce a framework for systematically evaluating reasoning-answer alignment across languages without requiring gold standard reasoning traces. Our approach centers on a key insight: by isolating reasoning content from conclusions, we can assess whether a model’s reasoning actually supports its stated answer, which is crucial for developing trustworthy multilingual models.

3.1
Evaluation Framework

Given a model that generates reasoning trace
T
in language
\ell
concluding with answer
a_{\text{model}}
, we first back-translate non-English responses to English using a top-performing translator for our target languages.

1

We do this because LLMs have been shown to perform better when inputs are provided in English
Etxaniz et al. 2024
.
We then create a truncated trace
T^{\prime}
by removing the final answer, allowing us to evaluate what conclusion the reasoning itself supports. Human annotators or automated evaluators assess these truncated traces, selecting from the original multiple-choice options or marking cases as “inconclusive” when the reasoning provides insufficient evidence.

We evaluate each response along two dimensions:
(1)
Correctness
: Whether the model’s final answer matches the gold label, and (2)
Consistency
: Whether the reasoning trace logically supports the model’s stated conclusion. These dimensions can be broken down into the cases detailed in
Table 1

Our primary metric is the
Trace Inconsistency Rate
(TIR), the proportion of cases where reasoning fails to support the model’s conclusion, regardless of answer correctness.
Additionally, our human evaluation protocol assesses reasoning quality through supplementary measures including logical coherence and information sufficiency.

3.3
Evaluation Protocols

Human Evaluation

We design a two-phase human annotation study to systematically evaluate model reasoning quality. In Phase 1, we first conduct a pilot study where 2 expert annotators examine approximately 100 reasoning traces from binary tasks. Taking an inductive approach, we carefully observe patterns in the generated traces, conduct thematic analysis
Braun and Clarke 2006
to identify recurring issues, and iteratively refine our error taxonomy based on these empirical observations.

Table 2
reflects a sample of a constructed taxonomy.

For Phase 2, we scale up our evaluation to GlobalMMLU using stratified sampling across multiple dimensions—language, model, and cultural sensitivity—to ensure broad coverage. We collect 72 annotations total, with 2 annotators per shard to enable reliability analysis. Our annotation protocol asks human evaluators to examine both the original question and the model’s reasoning trace
T^{\prime}
, then complete four key tasks: (1) determine the final answer (selecting from original multiple-choice options or marking “inconclusive”), (2) assess the logical coherence of the reasoning, (3) evaluate whether the trace contains sufficient information to answer the question, and (4) flag any concerning or problematic aspects of the model’s reasoning process.

Automated Evaluation

We replicate the human evaluation protocol using
Llama-4-Scout-Instruct
as an automated evaluator. The model receives the same truncated traces and applies identical selection criteria.
We employ the evaluator over 66,615 global mmlu examples, keeping only instances where the model returned a valid letter prediction, A-D.

5
Results

As shown in
Table 4
, both accuracy and trace inconsistency rates reveal considerable cross-lingual variation, but capture distinct aspects of reasoning quality. Accuracy ranges substantially across languages, where English peaks at 87-89% while Hindi and Korean drop (70-81%), and seem consistent with language hierarchies reflective of current literature on language resourcing: English
>
Spanish
>
Ukrainian/Arabic
>
Hindi/Korean
Costa-Jussà et al. 2022
.
However, TIR patterns diverge from accuracy trends, exposing reasoning breakdowns that final answer correctness can obscure.
For instance, while Qwen3-32B maintains reasonable accuracy in Korean (78.4%), its TIR jumps to 9.81%, which is over 4
\times
higher than English (2.16%).
Similarly, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct exhibits 13.3% Korean TIR despite 77% accuracy, meaning 1-in-8 responses contain reasoning that contradicts its final answer.
Interestingly though, Qwen3-32B-thinking consistently outperforms its non-thinking version across languages, achieving 0.91% English TIR versus 2.16% for the base model, reflecting clear gains of extended reasoning.

Latin script languages (LS) outperform non-Latin script languages (NLS) by 4-6 percentage points in both accuracy and TIR, twice the gap observed between high and low-resource languages!
This pattern seems to hold across models, indicating systematic script-based biases in reasoning quality are stronger than resource availability effects in this study.

5.2
Analysis of Automated Human-Derived Error Taxonomy

Our automated evaluation framework, employing the human-derived error taxonomy described in §
3.3
, reveals systematic differences in error patterns across language resource levels (Figure
2
).
Unsupported claims and ambiguous facts constitute the majority of detected errors across all evaluated models (23% and 24%respectively), while illogical leaps account for 18% of flagged instances.
Qwen3-32B-thinking demonstrates the largest resource gap, with 27% of responses flagged for unsupported claims in LRL settings compared to 18% in HRL settings.

The prevalence of
evidential errors
(unsupported claims, ambiguous facts) over logical contradictions seems to indicate that models struggle more with grounding their reasoning in verifiable information than with maintaining internal logical consistency.

Expanding into long-CoT paradigms with thinking models, while they show better consistency overall (as seen in
section 5
), the substantial jump in unsupported claims for low-resource languages may hint at potential trade-offs in extended reasoning traces, where while beneficial for complex problems, may drift from well-supported claims when operating with limited linguistic knowledge. Moving forward, we encourage further evaluation on thinking models in this setting.

6
Conclusion

In this work we introduced an automatic evaluation framwork to assess reasoning quality through misalignment between trace and answer in a multilingual setting.
We find that models, even when seemingly similar in accuracy, reflect a varying degree of reasoning trace fidelity.
While lower-resourced languages are expected to perform worse than higher-resourced languages, accuracy on its own underestimates reasoning trace quality, with languages of non-latin script reflecting twice as many reasoning contradictions than their latin counterparts.
These findings also motivate a closer look at thinking models, namely, to what extent they can maintain factual grounding in more resource-constrained language settings.
Moving forward, our evaluation framework can serve to help diagnose reasoning trace errors in order to ultimately improve multilingual reasoning capabilities.

Limitations

While our evaluation framework can be employed across other datasets that requiring reasoning, we only test over global mmlu and small sample of boolean comparative questions
Arcuschin et al. 2025
.
We conduct human annotations over a limited number of samples, which can be scaled up in a future iteration.
Furthermore, we only used one judge to assess reasoning traces, where moving forward we can move to using majority voting over 3 judges for robust outputs.
Evaluation can also take place over a wider set of languages that are even lower-resourced than those initially selected, though our initial set serves for script diversity.
Translators can also be updated to models like gpt-4o and double-checked by additional native speakers across selected languages.

Taxonomy

Error Type

Description

Example Pattern

Illogical Leap / Unjustified Conclusion

A reasoning step or conclusion is not supported by the stated evidence and may rely on implicit or unstated assumptions. This includes flawed deductive reasoning where the inference from premises to conclusion is logically invalid or incorrect. In other words, the reasoning "jumps" to a conclusion without sufficient logical basis.

Drawing a conclusion that option A is correct without evidence, or making a logical inference that does not follow from the given premises.

Logical Contradiction / Reasoning Inversion

Reasoning steps or claims that conflict with each other, either within the same step or across different steps, resulting in internal inconsistency. This means the trace contains contradictory statements that cannot all be true simultaneously.

The trace first claims option B is correct, then later claims option C is correct without reconciling the contradiction.

Multiple Answers

The reasoning trace concluded with more than one answer option without clear preference.

The trace concludes with both options A and D.

Conflicting Facts

The trace contains facts that contradict each other within the same step or across different steps.

One step claims a fact that is directly negated in another step.

Unsupported Claims

Claims are made without explicit explanation or data in the reasoning trace. The trace may rely on "common knowledge" or "standard reasoning" without justification.

Claiming "The product is the best choice because it has superior features," without any objective data or concrete evidence provided in the reasoning trace.

Ambiguous Facts

Claims are underspecified, partial, or lack sufficient detail to support the conclusion clearly.

Using vague terms like "usually" or "often" without any objective data or concrete evidence provided in the reasoning trace.

Linguistic/Translation Errors

The reasoning trace has issues with coherence, grammar, fluency, or clarity that impede understanding.

Sentences that are fragmented, poorly translated, or confusing.

Irrelevant/Excessive Content

The trace contains information that is unrelated to the question or unnecessarily verbose beyond what is needed for reasoning.

Including unrelated background information that does not affect the answer.

Other

A reasoning trace error not already reflected in the taxonomy.

Any pattern not described above.

Appendix C
Global MMLU

C.1
Pipeline details

All models generate 4096 tokens to respond to each question in global mmlu.
We follow each model’s recommended sampling parameters. If none is available, we proceed with nucleus sampling with top-p=0.95.
To account for differences in tokenization across languages, we allot a larger set of tokens for the translator during backtranslation from the original language to english, 8192. Backtranslation is done with temperature set to 0 and top-p set to 1.
Finally, the judge will generates 8192 tokens, and we keep a large window size of 32768 to account for the detailed ai evaluator prompt and the reasoning trace to evaluate. To encourage deterministic output was also set temperature set to 0 and top-p set to 1.

C.2
Translation Quality Check

We run a small annotation to evaluate the command r plus quality of backtranslation. We employ a native spanish speaker to annotate 30 spanish examples backtranslated to english, and find that only 2/30 examples were marked for errors in translation.

Reasoning Trace Error Example 2: Ambiguous Facts and Multiple Answers

(Model is llama 3.3 70b instruct, original language was korean, and sample id was global-facts/test/22)

Question

In 1997, the World Bank found that the fraction of the world’s population living in low and lower-middle income countries—that is, countries with per capita incomes of $1230 or less—was approximately

Reasoning Trace

To calculate the percentage of the world’s population living in low-income and lower-middle-income countries, i.e., countries with a per capita income of $1230 or less, according to the World Bank’s 1997 report, we need to consider both the population of each country and its per capita income. However, as this detailed data is not provided, we have to rely on general inferences.

Typically, low-income and lower-middle-income countries account for a significant portion of the world’s population. Many developing nations fall into this category, and a substantial number of the most populous countries are in this group. For instance, countries like India, China, Indonesia, and Pakistan are in this category, and their populations make up a significant portion of the world’s population.

Therefore, it is estimated that the population share of these countries is considerable and may be over 40%. But to determine the exact percentage, more detailed data and analysis are required.

In conclusion, while it is expected that the percentage of the population living in low-income and lower-middle-income countries is significant, more information is needed to ascertain the exact percentage. If we have to rely on general inferences, we could choose a value close to the median, such as 40% or 55%, but more data is necessary.
