Epidemiological characteristics of influenza outbreaks in schools in Jiangsu Province, China, 2020–2023 post-COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Jia-Le Peng, Ke Xu, Ye Tong, Shi-Zhi Wang, Hao-Di Huang, Chang-Jun Bao, Qi-Gang Dai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to analyze the epidemic characteristics and influencing factors of school influenza outbreaks in Jiangsu Province, China from 2020 to 2023,following the COVID-19 pandemic, to inform prevention and control strategies.MethodsData on influenza-like illness(ILI) outbreaks from the Chinese Influenza Surveillance Information System and national-level influenza surveillance sentinel hospitals were analyzed. The temporal distribution, school type, virus strains, and outbreak scales were examined using descriptive statistics.ResultsFrom 2020 to 2023, 1142 influenza outbreaks occurred in schools, with primary schools(ages 6 to 12) accounting for 71.80%. Most large outbreaks were caused by A(H1N1) and A(H3N2), responsible for 8.99% of total outbreaks. Outbreaks were predominantly reported in the pre-peak periods of B(Victoria) and A(H1N1) circulation, accounting for 86.31% and 92.32% of their respective total outbreaks. No concurrent influenza and COVID-19 outbreaks were observed during the study period.ConclusionPrimary and secondary schools are high-risk settings for influenza outbreaks. A(H3N2) shows higher adaptability and is more likely to co-circulate with other subtypes/lineages, especially A(H1N1), leading to larger outbreaks. B(Victoria)-caused outbreaks are more frequent but smaller in scale. School influenza outbreaks are more likely to occur during the early stages of seasonal peaks, particularly for B(Victoria) and A(H1N1). This suggests that influenza outbreaks in schools may play a crucial role in seeding and accelerating the spread of the virus within the broader community.Keywords: Influenza-like cases, Outbreak, Epidemic characteristics, Schools

Background

Influenza is an acute respiratory infectious disease caused by influenza viruses, and it is generally highly contagious among the population [1]. Infections with influenza often lead to numerous secondary infections and complications. Common complications include influenza-associated encephalitis/encephalopathy, acute necrotizing encephalitis, transverse myelitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, and other autoimmune diseases [2]. Additionally, influenza infections in children can result in increased absenteeism, medical visits, frequent use of antipyretics, antibiotics, and injections, leading to economic losses and a burden on the healthcare system [3]. Schools, due to the close interaction and congregation of individuals, are prone to outbreaks. After the global COVID-19 pandemic in late 2019, the healthcare and education departments in Jiangsu Province, China, intensified monitoring for fever and upper respiratory symptoms in crowded public places, including schools [4]. Because schools have well-established absenteeism monitoring systems for illness, it is easier to detect outbreaks. This study analyzed the epidemic characteristics of school influenza outbreaks in Jiangsu Province from 2020 to 2023, aiming to understand the patterns of influenza spread and provide a scientific basis for influenza prevention and control efforts.

Methods

Data sources

The data for this study were derived from reports of influenza-like cases outbreaks in the Chinese Influenza Surveillance Information System and monitoring data from 29 national-level influenza surveillance sentinel hospitals. The microbiological results were obtained from RT-PCR testing of throat swab specimens from Influenza-like illness (ILI) cases associated with outbreak events, conducted by influenza monitoring network laboratories with influenza testing qualifications. The study period was set from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2023, following the COVID-19 pandemic.

Case and outbreak definition

ILI was defined as the presence of fever (body temperature ≥ 38℃) accompanied by either cough or sore throat. ILI outbreaks refer to a sudden increase in the number of ILI cases within a short period in the same area or organization. An outbreak is typically considered to occur when 10 or more ILI cases are reported within one week in a school, childcare facility, or other collective workplace. The county-level Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) responsible for the area must report relevant information through the Chinese Influenza Surveillance Information System. An outbreak is deemed to have ended when no new cases occur within one maximum incubation period, which is typically one week for influenza [5].

Specimen collection and laboratory testing

Upper respiratory specimens, including nasal and throat swabs, were collected from symptomatic cases within three days of symptom onset. At least 10 specimens were obtained per outbreak for pathogen identification. These specimens were transported to influenza monitoring network laboratories within 48 h for testing. Initial Real-time RT-PCR tests were conducted to detect influenza and SARS-CoV-2. If both results were negative, multiplex PCR was performed to detect a broader range of pathogens, including but not limited to adenovirus, respiratory syncytial virus, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae, covering over a dozen respiratory pathogens.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted on the temporal distribution, school type distribution, virus strain distribution, and outbreak size of influenza outbreaks reported in Jiangsu Province from 2020 to 2023. Comparisons of rates were conducted using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact probability test, as appropriate. To account for multiple comparisons and reduce the likelihood of Type I errors, the Bonferroni correction was applied, the significance level (α) was adjusted by dividing the original α (0.05) by the number of group comparisons conducted. Data were organized and analyzed using R 4.3.0, and data visualization was performed with Excel 2019.

Results

Overview of the epidemic situation

From 2020 to 2023, a total of 1233 outbreaks of influenza-like cases through the Chinese Influenza Surveillance Information System were reported in Jiangsu Province. Among these, 1153 (93.51%) were caused by influenza viruses, the remaining 80 (6.49%) outbreaks were caused by pathogens other than influenza viruses, including 7 outbreaks of rhinovirus, 6 outbreaks of human metapneumovirus, 11 outbreaks of Mycoplasma pneumoniae, 3outbreaks of respiratory syncytial virus, 2 outbreaks of enteric viruses, 1 outbreak of novel coronavirus, 1 outbreak of Streptococcus pneumoniae, and 49 outbreaks where no pathogen was detected after multiple pathogen screening. Out of the 1153 influenza-related outbreaks, 1142 occurred in schools, accounting for 99.05%, and these 1142 influenza outbreaks were included in this analysis.

Temporal distribution

Among the 1142 outbreaks that occurred in schools, 58 were reported in 2020 (5.08%), 106 in 2021 (9.28%), 135 in 2022 (11.82%), and 843 in 2023 (73.82%). From 2020 to 2022, the majority of outbreak reports were concentrated in January and October to December, with a total of 242 reports, accounting for 80.94% of the reports during that period. In contrast, in 2023, the majority of outbreak reports were concentrated in February and March, with a total of 843 reports, accounting for 61.09% of the reports for the year. School type distribution

From 2020 to 2023, the reported school influenza outbreaks in Jiangsu Province were primarily observed in primary schools, serving students aged 6–12 years, with a cumulative total of 820 reports (71.80%). Secondary/vocational schools followed with 208 reports (18.21%), and childcare institutions had 106 reports (9.28%). The number of reports from universities was relatively low, with a cumulative total of 8 reports (0.70%).

There were statistically significant differences in the composition ratios of reported cases among different types of schools in different years (P < 0.001). Specifically, the proportion of outbreaks in secondary schools/vocational schools in 2020 and 2023 was significantly higher than that in 2021–2022 (P < 0.05). For details, refer to Table 1.

Relationship between ILI specimen testing results at sentinel hospitals and outbreaks

In Jiangsu Province, specimens collected by 29 national-level influenza surveillance sentinel hospitals were sent to network laboratories for influenza virus nucleic acid testing. The results showed that in 2020, the nucleic acid positivity rate of ILI specimens reached its highest point in the 3rd week (54.37%) before rapidly declining due to the impact of COVID-19 control measures. The predominant circulating strains were A(H3N2) and B(Victoria), and for the subsequent 18 months, influenza remained in a state of very low prevalence until the onset of seasonal outbreaks in the 28th week of 2021.

From 2021 to 2023, there were three complete seasonal influenza peaks: 1.From the 28th week of 2021 to the 23rd week of 2022, lasting 48 weeks, the predominant strain was B(Victoria), referred to as the B-V peak, with a peak nucleic acid positivity rate of 28.09%. 2.From the 24th week of 2022 to the 4th week of 2023, lasting 33 weeks, the predominant strain was A(H3N2), referred to as the H3 peak, with a peak nucleic acid positivity rate of 41.54%. 3.From the 5th week of 2022 to the 22nd week of 2023, lasting 18 weeks, the predominant strain was A(H1N1), referred to as the H1 peak, with a peak nucleic acid positivity rate of 61.35%. The Relationship between the COVID-19 pandemic and influenza epidemics

Prior to December 2022, 29 sentinel hospitals across Jiangsu Province each randomly selected 20 ILI cases per week to collect throat swabs solely for influenza virus nucleic acid testing. However, in December 2022, Jiangsu Province experienced its first wave of high-intensity SARS-CoV-2 transmission. Concurrently, Jiangsu Province initiated the simultaneous PCR testing of SARS-CoV-2 and influenza in throat swab samples collected from ILI cases at sentinel hospitals’ outpatient, emergency, and fever clinics. The weekly nucleic acid positivity rates for both pathogens from December 2022 to 2023 are depicted in Fig. 3. During this period, Jiangsu Province reported 853 influenza outbreak events; however, there were no reported outbreaks resulting from the co-circulation of COVID-19 and influenza.

Discussion

This study analyzed the epidemiological characteristics of school influenza outbreaks in Jiangsu Province from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2023, covering three complete influenza seasons after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Following the onset of global COVID-19 outbreak in December 2019, emergency response measures not only curbed the spread of COVID-19 but also significantly disrupted influenza transmission due to the viruses’ similar modes of spread. Consequently, influenza activity sharply declined in January 2020 and remained at very low levels for over a year [6–8], after which it gradually resumed its seasonal activity. However, at the end of 2022, when influenza was expected to peak during the winter and spring seasons, the issuance of China’s “Ten Measures for COVID-19 Prevention and Control” interrupted seasonal influenza outbreaks. In early 2023, as the COVID-19 pandemic waned and population mobility resumed [9], influenza activity rebounded [10].

In 2023, the intensity of influenza activity and the number of reported influenza outbreaks were higher than in 2020–2022, largely due to “immunity debt“ [11]. This concept refers to reduced population immunity caused by the absence of A(H1N1) virus circulation and low influenza vaccination rates during the pandemic. In China, vaccination rates dropped from 3.16% in the 2020–2021 season to 2.47% in the 2021–2022 season [12], further increasing susceptibility to influenza outbreaks in 2023.

From the number of reported influenza outbreaks each year, it can be observed that in 2020, there were the fewest reports. Apart from 58 reports in January, there were no reports of outbreaks in the other months in 2020. Monitoring results from other provinces in China also indicated that there were almost no reports of influenza outbreaks in 2020 [13, 14]. Additionally, unlike in 2020–2022 when most outbreak reports were concentrated between October and January of the following year, the influenza outbreak in schools in 2023 was somewhat delayed, with the peak of reports occurring during February-March in 2023.This situation was influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic. In 2020, most schools were closed or under strict management, leading to fewer gatherings and movements of students. Additionally, various preventive measures for respiratory infectious diseases were implemented, which had a positive impact on the prevention and control of influenza [15]. From December 2022 to January 2023, during the first wave of the high-intensity COVID-19 pandemic in China, most people stayed at home, adopted staggered work schedules, or stopped working altogether. The majority of primary and secondary schools, as well as childcare institutions, suspended classes, reducing population gatherings and the risk of virus transmission. This had a certain degree of suppression on the prevalence of respiratory viruses, including influenza [16]. Moreover, some cell and animal studies have found that prior infection of the host with one virus may enhance (positive viral interaction) or suppress (negative viral interaction) subsequent infections [17], with negative viral interactions being more common. This phenomenon, known as viral interference, is a form of competitive antagonism between viruses. This argument is often used to explain the staggered prevalence of multiple respiratory viruses within the same population [17, 18]. For instance, it has been reported that rhinovirus delayed the spread of the 2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic in France [19]. Therefore, we believe that during the period of intense COVID-19 prevalence in Jiangsu, both the intensity of influenza prevalence and outbreak events were low, possibly due to viral interference between SARS-CoV-2 and influenza viruses. On one hand, from the perspective of viral competition, this interference may involve competition for cellular resources (such as receptors, replication sites, and host factors related to replication), as well as the timing and order of infections, all of which can impact the infection process in individuals [17, 20]. On the other hand, from the perspective of population immunity, infection with one respiratory virus may induce cross-immune responses, meaning the memory cells and antibodies produced could also exert some inhibitory effects on other types of viruses [21].This might reduce the likelihood of co-infections within the population. Therefore, as of now, there have been no reports of large-scale school outbreaks caused by the simultaneous prevalence of COVID-19 and influenza in Jiangsu.

In Jiangsu Province from 2020 to 2023, among the school-based influenza outbreaks, primary schools had the highest number of reported cases, accounting for 70.1% of the total. Secondary/Vocational schools followed as the second-highest category. A study indicated that, due to factors such as inadequate home isolation periods and less effective isolation measures compared to childcare facilities, the basic reproduction number (R0) for influenza cluster outbreaks in primary and secondary schools was higher than that in childcare facilities [22–24]. This finding aligns with the results of our research. Apart from the lower effectiveness of isolation measures, primary and secondary schools have more densely populated classrooms with larger class sizes. Classrooms are semi-enclosed spaces, and during the fall and winter, when temperatures drop, classroom doors and windows are often kept closed. This leads to poor ventilation and limited air circulation, making it easier for the influenza virus to spread among individuals. Consequently, this environment can result in the occurrence of outbreak events [25].In addition to crowded, enclosed spaces being a major factor driving school outbreaks, multiple studies have shown that influenza incidence is age-related during flu season, with younger individuals generally having higher infection rates. Moreover, those who have previously contracted influenza or been vaccinated have lower infection rates compared to those who have not [26–30].This suggests that the lower proportion of outbreaks among older students in our study could be due to better ventilation practices and higher immunity levels in this group, as they are more likely to have been previously infected or vaccinated against influenza.

Every year, influenza virus subtypes alternate in circulation, and this may be influenced by factors such as population immunity levels and environmental conditions [31, 32]. Dominant influenza virus subtypes continually shift, showing a certain pattern of alternation. In previous studies, A(H1N1) influenza and B influenza displayed annual cycles, while A(H3N2) influenza showed a semi-annual cycle and caused more infections in China [33].However, in our retrospective observation, we found that from 2020 to 2022, the predominant influenza virus subtypes were mainly B (Victoria) lineage and A (H3N2). The peak of A(H3N2) only began in the summer of 2022, but outbreaks caused by A(H3N2) were also few, and A(H1N1) did not appear during this period. In 2023, A(H1N1) became dominant, co-circulating with A(H3N2), while B(Yamagata) has not been detected since 2020. The global influenza surveillance network has also not identified this subtype, suggesting it may have disappeared. The influenza virus subtypes responsible for outbreak events were consistent with the dominant subtypes during the same period. When multiple influenza subtypes co-circulated, outbreak events were mainly caused by the dominant subtype at that time. For instance, during the H1 peak, A (H3N2) was relatively less prevalent, and A (H1N1) caused 378 outbreak events during that period, accounting for 70.79% of the outbreaks. Furthermore, our study observed that influenza outbreak events emerged concurrently with rising influenza nucleic acid positivity rates in ILI specimens from sentinel surveillance hospitals. These outbreaks, however, were predominantly reported during the early and middle stages of the epidemic peak, with a noticeable decline in outbreak frequency by the later stages. This pattern may be attributed to the close interactions and high population density within schools, where rapid transmission occurs, leading to a surge in infections in the early epidemic phase. By the middle to late stages, the student population likely develops a relatively higher level of immunity, reducing the occurrence of outbreaks. From a temporal perspective, the outbreak and infection timeline in schools precedes that of the general population, indirectly indicates that the occurrence of influenza in schools plays a crucial role in driving the onset and development of influenza within the broader community. Supporting this, studies have shown that children, particularly those aged 10–19 years, often lead influenza epidemic waves by peaking earlier in infection rates than other age groups, implying a pivotal role for younger school-aged children in driving local transmission dynamics [34]. Additionally, Household caregivers, especially those caring for symptomatic children, are at a significantly higher risk of acquiring influenza compared to healthcare workers [35]. These findings reinforce the notion that school-based influenza outbreaks, where transmission occurs rapidly due to high population density and frequent close interactions, can seed and accelerate influenza spread in the wider population. Additionally, this finding further suggests that implementing influenza vaccination for student populations early in the epidemic phase could provide protective effects for the entire population. This is consistent with results from some model-based and community trial studies conducted abroad [34, 36].

Regarding the scale of outbreak events in schools under different dominant influenza virus subtypes, each subtype has its own characteristics. B-V Peak: Outbreak events caused by the B-V peak had a higher number but relatively smaller scale compared to those caused by influenza A viruses. H3 Peak: When the H3 peak occurred during the summer months, and although the positivity rate of specimens from sentinel hospitals was high in July and August, the number of outbreak events was relatively low. This could be attributed to Chinese schools being in summer vacation during these months. H1 Peak: Under the dominance of A (H1N1) during the H1 peak, accompanied by the circulation of A (H3N2), the number of influenza outbreak events was the highest. During this period, there were also 6 reports of influenza outbreak events in universities, which were not observed under the B-V and H3 peaks, and the number of large-scale outbreaks with over 100 cases was the highest. Additionally, the study observed that the A (H3N2) subtype had a higher tendency to co-circulate with other subtypes/lineages. When the A (H3N2) subtype co-circulated with B (Victoria) lineage or A (H1N1), it was more likely to trigger clustered outbreak events with mixed infections, and the likelihood of large-scale outbreaks was higher. The differing outbreak scales of various influenza subtypes in human populations are largely due to variations in their environmental adaptability and evolutionary mechanisms. For example, the influenza A virus exhibits strong adaptability and co-circulation capabilities, partly attributed to its rapid evolution. Studies have shown that the evolutionary dynamics of A(H3N2), including nucleotide insertions, deletions, and a higher frequency of reassortment both between and within subtypes, are significantly greater than those of influenza B viruses [37]. This frequent evolution enables A(H3N2) to quickly adapt to environmental changes [38]and overcome host immune barriers, leading to sustained transmission. Specifically, A(H3N2) achieves rapid evolution by adding N-linked glycans to hemagglutinin (HA) and modifying the receptor binding and neuraminidase (NA) functions [39]. These adaptations not only enhance the virus’s co-circulation ability during severe seasons but also allow it to dominate within human populations, resulting in larger-scale epidemics compared to other seasonal influenza viruses.

This study has limitations as it is retrospective in nature, relying on the investigation of symptomatic cases in past influenza-like illness outbreaks. Consequently, we are unable to ascertain the proportion of asymptomatic cases or evaluate their role in transmission dynamics within these outbreaks. Asymptomatic infections are known to vary by influenza subtype and population characteristics [35, 40]. For instance, some studies suggest that B-type influenza viruses might have a higher asymptomatic infection rate than A-type, potentially influencing the frequency and scale of outbreaks. If asymptomatic carriers were significant in the transmission process, the total number of cases in the outbreak could be underestimated, leading to biases in the conclusions about the relative impact of different subtypes. To better understand the full transmission dynamics, future studies should include asymptomatic cases through more comprehensive sampling methods, such as routine screening or serological studies [41], to evaluate both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals. Additionally, due to the absence of school population size data, we cannot analyze the association between school population size and outbreak occurrence. Lastly, our data were derived from passive reporting, and variations in the sensitivity of outbreak detection and reporting practices across different schools and regions may exist, introducing potential biases that cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

Primary and secondary schools are high-risk settings for influenza outbreaks. A(H3N2) subtype is prone to co-circulate with other subtypes/lineages, possibly due to its strong environmental adaptability, when the A(H3N2) subtype co-circulates with B(Victoria) or A(H1N1), it is more likely to trigger clustered outbreaks with the potential for large-scale epidemics; B(Victoria)-caused outbreaks are more frequent but smaller in scale. School influenza outbreaks are more likely to occur during the early stages of seasonal peaks, this suggests that influenza outbreaks in schools may play a crucial role in seeding and accelerating the spread of the virus within the broader community. Early detection and intervention in school settings are crucial for reducing influenza outbreaks in the post-COVID-19 era. Furthermore, implementing vaccination for students prior to the influenza season could reduce infection rates in school-age children and help mitigate broader community transmission.
