Fenchel-Willmore inequality for submanifolds in manifolds with non-negative k k -Ricci curvature
Meng Ji, Kwok-Kun Kwong

Abstract
. We establish a sharp Fenchel-Willmore inequality for closed submanifolds of arbitrary dimension and codimension immersed in a complete Riemannian manifold with non-negative intermediate Ricci curvature and Euclidean volume growth. In the hypersurface case, this reduces to non-negative Ricci curvature. We also characterize the equality case. This generalizes the recent work of Agostiniani, Fogagnolo, and Mazzieri [ 1 ] , as well as classical results by Chen, Fenchel, Willmore, and others.

1.
Introduction

The celebrated Willmore inequality
[
19
]
states that for a bounded domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with smooth boundary, we have

\int_{\partial\Omega}\sigma^{2}\geq 4\pi,

where
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr }\mathrm{II}
is the normalized mean curvature of
\partial\Omega
and
\mathrm{II}
denotes the second fundamental form.
The inequality is sharp and the equality holds if and only if
\Omega
is a ball.

Another fundamental geometric inequality, Fenchel inequality
[
11
]
, asserts that for any closed, embedded curve
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the integral of the length of its curvature vector
\kappa
satisfies

\int_{\Sigma}|\kappa|\geq 2\pi,

with equality only for planar convex curves.

These inequalities have motivated extensive research. Notably, in a relatively recent influential work, Agostiniani, Fogagnolo, and Mazzieri
[
1
]
extended the classical Willmore inequality to the boundary of a smooth, bounded open set
\Omega
in a complete
n+1
dimensional manifold
M
(
n\geq 2
) with nonnegative Ricci curvature and Euclidean volume growth. They established the following inequality:

\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|\sigma\right|^{n}\geq\theta\left|\mathbb{S}^{n}\right|,

where
\sigma=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{II}
is the normalized mean curvature vector and
\theta
is the asymptotic volume ratio defined by (which is well-defined and independent of
p
by the Bishop-Gromov volume comparison theorem)

\theta=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|B(p,r)|}{|\mathbb{B}^{n+1}|r^{n+1}}.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
M\setminus\Omega
is isometric to
\left(\left[r_{0},\infty\right)\times\partial\Omega,dr^{2}+\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{2}g_{\partial\Omega}\right)
with
r_{0}=\left(\frac{|\partial\Omega|}{\theta\left|\mathbb{S}^{n}\right|}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}
.
In particular,
\partial\Omega
is a connected umbilical hypersurface with constant mean curvature.
Here,
\mathbb{B}^{n+1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
denotes the standard unit ball, and
\mathbb{S}^{n}=\partial\mathbb{B}^{n+1}
is the unit round sphere.

The result in
[
1
]
, derived via a level-set method based on potential theory, has drawn significant interest due to its geometric implications. Wang
[
18
]
subsequently provided a more concise argument based on classical comparison techniques. Both works are confined to closed hypersurfaces that form the boundary of open bounded subsets. Generalizing the level-set approach to an immersed
\Sigma
or to higher codimension appears to be challenging.

In a different direction, Chen
[
10
]
established a Fenchel-Borsuk-Willmore type inequality for higher-codimensional submanifolds immersed in the Euclidean space. Specifically, he showed that if
\Sigma
is an
n
-dimensional closed submanifold immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
and
\sigma
is its normalized mean curvature vector, then

(1.1)

\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|^{n}\geq\left|\mathbb{S}^{n}\right|.

Chen’s proof of the Fenchel-Willmore inequality (
1.1
) relies on the Gauss map into the unit sphere, a construction specific to the Euclidean space that does not seem to be easily generalizable to more general ambient manifolds.

In light of the results discussed above, it is natural to ask whether a Fenchel-Willmore type inequality holds for submanifolds of arbitrary codimension immersed in a manifold with some kind of non-negative curvature. However, when the codimension is greater than one, the nonnegative Ricci curvature condition is insufficient to guarantee such an inequality. For example, the Eguchi-Hanson metric on
T\mathbb{S}^{2}
is Ricci flat with Euclidean volume growth and has a totally geodesic submanifold
\mathbb{S}^{2}

[
2
, p. 270]
, thus violating the inequality of the form
\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|^{2}\geq\theta|\mathbb{S}^{2}|
. Therefore, it is reasonable to consider extensions where the ambient manifold has nonnegative sectional curvature.

In this regard, we are able to prove the following sharp Fenchel-Willmore inequality for closed submanifolds

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
(M,g)
be a complete non-compact Riemannian manifold of dimension
n+m
with nonnegative sectional curvature and asymptotic volume ratio
\theta>0
. Suppose
\Sigma
is a closed
n
-dimensional submanifold immersed in
M
. Then

(1.2)

\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|^{n}\geq\theta|\mathbb{S}^{n}|.

If
m=1
, the curvature assumption may be relaxed to require only non-negative Ricci curvature.

Indeed, the same result remains valid under the weaker assumption of non-negative
k
-Ricci curvature for some
k
depending on
n
and
m
. To avoid disrupting the flow of exposition with technical details regarding this curvature condition, we defer the definition of the
k
-Ricci curvature and the related discussion to the end of the section.

To state the equality case of Theorem
1.1
, we need to introduce some notations. Let
\Sigma^{+}:=\{x\in\Sigma:\sigma(x)\neq 0\}
.
On
\Sigma^{+}
, we decompose
T_{x}^{\perp}\Sigma
into the orthogonal direct sum
\widetilde{T}_{x}^{\perp}\Sigma\oplus\operatorname{span}(\sigma)
, where
\widetilde{T}_{x}^{\perp}\Sigma
is the orthogonal complement of
\sigma(x)
in
T_{x}^{\perp}\Sigma
. In this case,
z\in T_{x}^{\perp}\Sigma
can be written as
z=y+t\frac{\sigma}{|\sigma|}
, and we identify it with the pair
(y,t)
. We use the convention that for the unit sphere in the Euclidean space,
\sigma
is the inward pointing unit normal.

We now characterize the equality case in Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume either the curvature condition stated in Theorem
1.1
, or the weaker condition given in Theorem
1.3
.

(a)

Suppose
n=1
and the equality in (
1.2
) holds.
Then
\Sigma
is connected and the normal exponential map when restricted to

\{(x,z)\in T^{\perp}\Sigma^{+}:\langle z,\sigma(x)\rangle\leq 0\}=\{(x,y,t)\in\widetilde{T}^{\perp}\Sigma^{+}\times\mathbb{R}:t\leq 0\}

is a diffeomorphism onto its image. Moreover, the pullback of the ambient metric under the exponential map on this set is given by

(1.3)

g(x,y,t)=dt^{2}+dy^{2}+(1-t|\sigma(x)|)^{2}g_{\Sigma}.

(b)

Suppose
n\geq 2
. Then the equality in (
1.2
) holds if and only if all the conclusions in (a) are satisfied and moreover,
\Sigma
is an embedded umbilical submanifold and
\sigma
is parallel and nonzero everywhere.

Furthermore, with a change of variable
r=r_{0}-t
with
r_{0}=\left(\frac{|\Sigma|}{\theta\left|\mathbb{S}^{n}\right|}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}
,
the pullback metric in (
1.3
) can be written as

g(x,y,r)=dr^{2}+dy^{2}+\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{2}g_{\Sigma},

defined on
\widetilde{T}^{\perp}\Sigma\times[r_{0},\infty)
.

(c)

Let
n\geq 1
and suppose the equality in (
1.2
) holds. For each
r>0
, let
\Sigma_{r}^{+}:=\{(x,z)\in T^{\perp}\Sigma^{+}:\langle z,\sigma(x)\rangle\leq 0,|z|<r\}

and
N_{r}(\Sigma):=\{p\in M:d(p,\Sigma)<r\}
be the tubular neighborhood of
\Sigma
. Then

\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{|\exp(\Sigma_{r}^{+})|}{|N_{r}(\Sigma)|}=1.

i.e. the image under the normal exponential map of normal vectors lying in the half-space in the direction of
-\sigma
fills the neighborhood of
\Sigma
with asymptotic volume density one.

We remark that if a sharp constant is not required, a Fenchel-Willmore type inequality for a closed submanifold can already be derived by setting
f=1
in
[
5
, Theorem 1.4]
, followed by an application of Holder’s inequality, at least in the case where
M
has non-negative sectional curvature.

In addition to the classical and more recent results on Fenchel-Willmore-type inequalities, this work is also inspired by the approach introduced by Brendle in his proof of Sobolev-type inequalities on manifolds with non-negative sectional curvature
[
5
]
. His approach is in turn partly motivated by techniques underlying the Alexandrov-Bakelman-Pucci maximum principle
[
8
]
. It also has some similarities with the optimal transport proof of the isoperimetric inequality
[
6
]
. In
[
5
, Theorem 1.4]
, Brendle constructs a “transport map” from a subset of the normal bundle of
\Sigma
into the ambient manifold
M
by using the solution to a linear elliptic equation on
\Sigma
with a Neumann boundary condition (if
\partial\Sigma\neq\emptyset
). He then proves the surjectivity of this map onto suitable subsets of
M
, estimates its Jacobian by analyzing the elliptic equation together with an associated matrix Riccati equation along geodesics coming from
\Sigma
, and completes the argument via the change-of-variables formula for integrals.

We follow a similar strategy: constructing a “transport map”, establishing its surjectivity onto suitable subsets, and applying Jacobian estimates in a change-of-variables formula to control the corresponding integrals. However, our approach differs in several respects. First, we avoid solving an auxiliary elliptic equation; our transport map is defined directly using the normal exponential map. Second, instead of the Alexandrov-Bakelman-Pucci principle and matrix Riccati methods, we prove a Heintze-Karcher type Jacobian comparison (which is already known) and a monotonicity formula for the Jacobian determinant (Lemma
2.4
) by working with the scalar Riccati inequality, which lets us assume weaker curvature conditions. Third, in the higher-codimension setting, we carry out a more precise analysis of the fiber integrals arising from the volume estimate, as discussed below.

Brendle’s Sobolev inequality (
[
5
, Theorem 1.4]
) is sharp in the Euclidean setting for codimensions up to two. In higher codimensions, sharpness fails because one of the fiber‐integral estimates relies on the algebraic inequality
b^{\frac{m}{2}}-a^{\frac{m}{2}}\leq\frac{m}{2}(b-a)
for
0\leq a<b\leq 1
and
m\geq 2
, which is no longer optimal when
m>2
.

We overcome this difficulty by carrying out a more precise evaluation of the relevant fiber integrals, rather than bounding them, using a representation formula (
2.7
) related to the so-called Euler integral of the first kind, expressed in terms of the gamma function
\Gamma
. Integrals of the form
\int_{0}^{1}t^{p}(1-t^{2})^{q}\,dt
appear repeatedly in our analysis, both in the estimation of fiber integrals in the proof of the Fenchel-Willmore inequality and in the proof of rigidity. Specifically, we compute the fiber integrals (up to error terms that vanish in the limiting process) using this representation formula. The use of the gamma function in this context allows us to obtain rather precise expressions, thereby preserving the sharp constant for all codimensions
m
and
\theta>0
.

Moreover, the equality case in Brendle’s result is achieved only when
\Sigma
is a flat disk (with boundary) of codimension up to two in Euclidean space, whereas in our setting, equality holds only if
\Sigma
is closed, umbilical, and with constant
|\sigma|>0
(if
n>1
), with
\theta
possibly less than
1
. Consequently, our analysis must proceed along a different path to address this difference. For example, our argument requires separate treatment on the set where the mean curvature vector vanishes and where it is non-zero, and our equality case analysis will accordingly focus on the region where
\sigma\neq 0
.

We now return to the earlier remark concerning a weaker curvature assumption. It turns out that the curvature condition stated in Theorem
1.1
can be substantially relaxed, especially when
n
and
m
are large or when
m=1
, to a condition involving the so-called
k
-Ricci curvature.

We begin by recalling the definition of
k
-Ricci curvature (also referred to as intermediate Ricci curvature) on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
. Let
v\in T_{p}M
be a unit tangent vector, and let
W\subset T_{p}M
be a
k
-dimensional subspace orthogonal to
v
. The
k
-Ricci curvature in the direction of
v
with respect to
W
is defined as

\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}_{k}(v,W)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\bar{R}(v,e_{i})e_{i},v\rangle,

where
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is any orthonormal basis of
W
and
\bar{R}
is the Riemannian curvature tensor. This notion interpolates between sectional curvature and Ricci curvature: the case
k=1
recovers sectional curvature, while
k=\dim M-1
gives the Ricci curvature in the direction of
v
.

We say
(M,g)
has non-negative
k
-Ricci curvature, denoted
\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}_{k}\geq 0
, if
\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}_{k}(v,W)\geq 0
at every point
p\in M
, for every unit vector
v\in T_{p}M
, and every
k
-plane
W\subset T_{p}M
orthogonal to
v
. Clearly, the cases
k=1
and
k=\dim M-1
recover non-negative sectional curvature and non-negative Ricci curvature, respectively. It is straightforward to verify that
\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}_{k}\geq 0
implies
\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}_{l}\geq 0
for all
l>k
.

Lower bounds on the intermediate
k
-Ricci curvature have been used to bridge the gap between results based on sectional curvature and those based on Ricci curvature in the earlier works of Wu
[
20
]
, Shen
[
16
]
, and Shen-Wei
[
17
]
. Since then, many results related to volume growth, diameter bounds, Betti numbers, geometric flows, optimal transport, and other geometric and topological applications have been obtained under a lower bound on this curvature. See, for instance,
[
7
,
12
,
9
,
14
]
. For more details, a comprehensive list of articles on intermediate
k
-Ricci curvature can be found on the webpage
[
15
]
.

We are able to weaken the curvature assumption in Theorem
1.1
as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

The conclusion of Theorem
1.1
still holds under the weaker assumption that the
k
-Ricci curvature is non-negative, where

(1.4)

k=\begin{cases}\min(n,m-1),&\text{if }m>1,\\
n,&\text{if }m=1.\end{cases}

Note that in both cases, the Ricci curvature of
M
is non-negative and
\theta
is still well-defined.

As an immediate consequence, we have

Corollary 1.4
.

There is no
n
-dimensional closed minimal submanifold immersed in a complete Riemannian manifold
(M^{n+m},g)
with
\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}_{k}\geq 0
and Euclidean volume growth, where
k
is given by (
1.4
).

The weakening of the curvature assumption to a
k
-Ricci condition is made possible by a more refined analysis of Jacobian determinant via the Riccati inequality, which strengthens the classical Jacobian comparison theorem of Heintze and Karcher
[
13
]
. While a comparison result involving
k
-Ricci curvature is known (see
[
9
]
), we are not aware of any reference establishing the monotonicity of the Jacobian determinant under this weaker assumption (Lemma
2.4
). As this monotonicity plays a crucial role in the rigidity argument, we provide the full details here.

Moreover, the non-negative
k
-Ricci curvature condition also proves sufficient for characterizing umbilicity, and for establishing that the mean curvature vector is parallel when
n>1
by invoking the Codazzi equation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the proof of the Fenchel-Willmore inequality, Theorem
1.3
, under the non-negative
k
-Ricci curvature assumption. In Section
3

\Sigma
and
M
that attain the equality, and then proceed to analyze the equality case and prove Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Proof of the Fenchel-Willmore inequality

In this paper, we consider a complete, noncompact Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
of dimension
n+m
, together with a closed immersed submanifold
\Sigma\subset M
of dimension
n
. The Levi-Civita connection of
(M,g)
is denoted by
\bar{D}
, and its Riemann curvature tensor by
\bar{R}
, with the convention
\bar{R}(X,Y)Z=\bar{D}_{X}\bar{D}_{Y}Z-\bar{D}_{Y}\bar{D}_{X}Z-\bar{D}_{[X,Y]}Z
. The second fundamental form of
\Sigma
, denoted by
\mathrm{II}
, is a symmetric bilinear form on the tangent bundle of
\Sigma
that takes values in the normal bundle
T^{\perp}\Sigma
. At a point
x\in\Sigma
, for tangent vector fields
X,Y
and a normal vector field
V
, the second fundamental form satisfies
\langle\mathrm{II}(X,Y),V\rangle=\langle\bar{D}_{X}Y,V\rangle
. The normalized mean curvature vector of
\Sigma
is given by
\sigma=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}\mathrm{II}
.

From now on, we only assume the weaker curvature assumption stated in Theorem
1.3
.

We define
\Phi_{r}(x,z):=\exp_{x}\left(rz\right)
for
(x,z)\in T^{\perp}\Sigma
and
r>0
, and

\displaystyle A_{r}:=\{(x,z)\in T^{\perp}\Sigma:|z|<1\text{ and }d\left(q,\exp_{x}\left(rz\right)\right)\geq r|z|\text{ for all }q\in\Sigma\}.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
0<\alpha<1
and
r>0
,

\{p\in M:\alpha r<d(x,p)<r\text{ for all }x\in\Sigma\}

is contained in
\left\{\Phi_{r}(x,z):(x,z)\in A_{r}\text{ and }|z|>\alpha\right\}
.

Proof.

Fix a real number
0<\alpha<1
and a point
p\in M
such that
\alpha r<d(x,p)<r
for all
x\in\Sigma
.
Suppose the distance function
d(\cdot,p)
attains its minimum at a point
\bar{x}\in\Sigma
.
Let
\bar{\gamma}:[0,r]\rightarrow M
be a minimizing geodesic such that
\bar{\gamma}(0)=\bar{x}
and
\bar{\gamma}(r)=p
.
Then
\bar{z}:=\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)\in T_{\bar{x}}^{\perp}\Sigma
by the first variation formula. Clearly,
r|\bar{z}|=r\left|\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)\right|=d(\bar{x},p)\in(\alpha r,r)
, and

\Phi_{r}(\bar{x},\bar{z})=\exp_{\bar{x}}\left(r\bar{z}\right)=\exp_{\bar{\gamma}(0)}\left(r\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)\right)=\bar{\gamma}(r)=p
.
For any
x\in\Sigma
,
d(x,p)\geq d(\bar{x},p)=r|z|
, i.e.
(\bar{x},\bar{z})\in A_{r}
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

For every
(\bar{x},\bar{z})\in A_{r}
, we have
1-r\langle\sigma(\bar{x}),\bar{z}\rangle\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{\gamma}(s):=\exp_{\bar{x}}\left(s\bar{z}\right)
for
s\in[0,r]
.
Let
\gamma:[0,r]\rightarrow M
be an arbitrary smooth path satisfying
\gamma(0)\in\Sigma
and
\gamma(r)=\bar{\gamma}(r)
. Since
(\bar{x},\bar{z})\in A_{r}
, we have

\displaystyle r\int_{0}^{r}\left|\gamma^{\prime}(s)\right|^{2}ds\geq d(\gamma(0),\gamma(r))^{2}=d\left(\gamma(0),\exp_{\bar{x}}\left(r\bar{z}\right)\right)^{2}\geq r^{2}|\bar{z}|^{2}=r\int_{0}^{r}\left|\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)\right|^{2}ds.

In other words,
\bar{\gamma}
minimizes the energy functional
\int_{0}^{r}\left|\gamma^{\prime}(s)\right|^{2}ds
among all smooth paths
\gamma:[0,r]\rightarrow M
satisfying
\gamma(0)\in\Sigma
and
\gamma(r)=\bar{\gamma}(r)
. Let
Z
be a smooth vector field along
\bar{\gamma}
such that
Z(0)\in T_{\bar{x}}\Sigma
and
Z(r)=0
.
By the second variation formula of energy, we obtain

\displaystyle-\left\langle\mathrm{II}(Z(0),Z(0)),\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)\right\rangle+\int_{0}^{r}\left(\left|\bar{D}_{s}Z(s)\right|^{2}-\left\langle\bar{R}\left(\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s),Z(s)\right)Z(s),\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)\right\rangle\right)ds\geq 0.

Take an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
T_{\bar{x}}\Sigma
and let
E_{i}
be the parallel transport of
e_{i}
along
\bar{\gamma}
. Applying the above inequality to the vector fields
Z_{i}(s):=(r-s)E_{i}(s)
and summing from
i=1
to
n
gives

nr-r^{2}\langle n\sigma(\bar{x}),\bar{z}\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{r}(r-s)^{2}\left\langle\bar{R}\left(\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s),E_{i}(s)\right)E_{i}(s),\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)\right\rangle ds\geq 0.

In view of the assumption
\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}_{k}\geq 0
, with
k
as defined in (
1.4
), which implies
\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}_{n}\geq 0
, the desired result follows.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
q_{1},q_{2}:(0,a]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be smooth functions
such that

q_{1}^{\prime}(s)+q_{1}(s)^{2}\leq q_{2}^{\prime}(s)+q_{2}(s)^{2}

and both
q_{i}^{\prime}(s)+q_{i}(s)^{2}
extend smoothly to
[0,a]
.
If
\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}(q_{2}(s)-q_{1}(s))=0
,
then
q_{1}(s)\leq q_{2}(s)
.
If
q_{1}(a)=q_{2}(a)
, then
q_{1}=q_{2}
on
(0,a]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume that
(x,z)\in A_{r}
. Then the function

s\mapsto\frac{|\det D\Phi_{s}(x,z)|}{s^{m}(1-s\langle\sigma(x),z\rangle)^{n}}

is monotone decreasing and

\displaystyle|\det D\Phi_{s}(x,z)|\leq{s^{m}(1-s\langle\sigma,z\rangle)^{n}}.

for
s\in(0,r)
.

Proof.

The estimate for the Jacobian determinant actually follows from
[
13
, Corollary 3.3.1]
(or
[
13
, Corollary 3.3.2]
in the case when
m=1
). See also
[
9
]
. But since we also need the monotonicity formula, we will carry out the complete proof here.

Take any
r>0
and
(\bar{x},\bar{z})\in A_{r}
.
Define the geodesic

\bar{\gamma}(s)=\exp_{\bar{x}}(s\bar{z}),\qquad s\in[0,r].

Choose an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
of
T_{\bar{x}}\Sigma
such that the matrix
\left(-\langle\mathrm{II}(e_{i},e_{j}),\bar{z}\rangle\right)=\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
is diagonal.
Select an orthonormal frame
\{e_{n+1},\dots,e_{n+m}\}
of
T^{\perp}\Sigma
near
\bar{x}
with
\langle\bar{D}_{e_{i}}e_{\alpha},e_{\beta}\rangle=0
at
\bar{x}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
n+1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq n+m
, such that
e_{n+m}
is parallel to
\bar{z}
and
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{n+m}\}
is positively oriented.
Let
E_{j}(s)
be the parallel transport of
e_{j}
along
\bar{\gamma}
for
j=1,\cdots,n+m
.

For each
1\leq i\leq n
, we define the Jacobi field
X_{i}(s)
along
\bar{\gamma}
by

X_{i}(0)=e_{i}\text{ and }X_{i}^{\prime}(0)=\lambda_{i}e_{i}.

For each
n+1\leq\alpha\leq n+m
, we denote by
X_{\alpha}(s)
the Jacobi field along
\bar{\gamma}
satisfying

X_{\alpha}(0)=0\text{ and }\bar{D}_{s}X_{\alpha}(0)=e_{\alpha}.

Define the matrix
P(s)
by setting
P_{ij}(s)=\langle E_{j}(s),X_{i}(s)\rangle
and
S_{ij}(s)=\langle\bar{R}(E_{i}(s),\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s))\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s),E_{j}(s)\rangle
. Then
P
satisfies the Jacobi equation
P^{\prime\prime}(s)=-S(s)P(s)
.

From the definition of
A_{r}
, there is no focal point of
\Sigma
along
\bar{\gamma}(s)
for
0<s<r
. In particular,
\{X_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n+m}
is linearly independent and
P(s)
is invertible. Define
Q(s)=P^{\prime}(s)P^{-1}(s)
, then
Q(s)
is symmetric and satisfies the Riccati equation

Q^{\prime}(s)+Q(s)^{2}=-S(s).

We remark that
Q(s)
can be interpreted as the matrix representation of
|\bar{z}|B(s)
with respect to
\{E_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n+m}
, where
B(s)=\bar{D}^{2}\rho
is the shape operator of
\Sigma_{s}
at
\bar{\gamma}(s)
and
\Sigma_{s}
is the hypersurface whose distance
\rho
from
\Sigma
is
|\bar{z}|s
.

Define the normalized partial traces

q_{n}(s)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{ii}(s),\quad q_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\alpha=n+1}^{n+m}Q_{\alpha\alpha}(s).

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, they satisfy the scalar Riccati inequality

q_{n}^{\prime}(s)+q_{n}(s)^{2}\leq q_{n}^{\prime}(s)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Q(s)^{2}\right)_{ii}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\bar{R}\left(E_{i}(s),\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)\right)\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s),E_{i}(s)\right\rangle\leq 0

and

q_{m}^{\prime}(s)+q_{m}(s)^{2}\leq q_{m}^{\prime}(s)+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\alpha=n+1}^{n+m}(Q(s)^{2})_{ii}=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\alpha=n+1}^{n+m}\langle\bar{R}(E_{\alpha}(s),\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s))\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s),E_{\alpha}(s)\rangle\leq 0.

More precisely, the first inequality comes from

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Q(s)^{2}\right)_{ii}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{ii}(s)^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}Q_{ij}(s)^{2}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{ii}(s)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{ii}(s)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{n}(s)^{2}.

Notice also that the curvature assumption
\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}_{k}\geq 0
with
k
given by (
1.4
) implies both

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\bar{R}\left(E_{i}(s),\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)\right)\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s),E_{i}(s)\right\rangle\geq 0

and

\sum_{\alpha=n+1}^{n+m}\left\langle\bar{R}\left(E_{\alpha}(s),\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)\right)\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s),E_{\alpha}(s)\right\rangle=\sum_{\alpha=n+1}^{n+m-1}\left\langle\bar{R}\left(E_{\alpha}(s),\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)\right)\bar{\gamma}^{\prime}(s),E_{\alpha}(s)\right\rangle\geq 0.

As shown in
[
9
, Equation 2.4]
, by computing the Taylor expansion of
\bar{D}^{2}\rho
, where
\rho
is the distance from
\Sigma
, it holds that

(2.2)

\displaystyle Q(s)=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda+O(s)&O(s)\\
O(s)&\frac{1}{s}I_{m}+O(s)\end{bmatrix}

as
s\to 0^{+}
.

From this, we have

\displaystyle q_{n}(s)=-\langle\sigma,\bar{z}\rangle+O(s)\quad\text{ and }q_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{s}+O(s)

as
s\to 0^{+}
.

Let
\bar{q}_{n}(s)=-\frac{\langle\sigma,\bar{z}\rangle}{1-s\langle\sigma,\bar{z}\rangle}
and
\bar{q}_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{s}
, then they both satisfy the scalar Riccati equation
f^{\prime}(s)+f(s)^{2}=0
. In particular,

\displaystyle q_{n}^{\prime}(s)+q_{n}(s)^{2}\leq\bar{q}_{n}^{\prime}(s)+\bar{q}_{n}(s)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{m}^{\prime}(s)+q_{m}(s)^{2}\leq\bar{q}_{m}^{\prime}(s)+\bar{q}_{m}(s)^{2}.

By Lemma
2.3
, we conclude that

\displaystyle q_{n}(s)\leq\bar{q}_{n}(s)\quad\text{and}\quad q_{m}(s)\leq\bar{q}_{m}(s).

We then have

\frac{d}{ds}\log(\det P(s))=\operatorname{tr}Q(s)=nq_{n}(s)+mq_{m}(s)\leq n\bar{q}_{n}(s)+m\bar{q}_{m}(s)=\frac{m}{s}-\frac{n\langle\sigma,\bar{z}\rangle}{1-s\langle\sigma,\bar{z}\rangle}.

From the observation that
\det D\Phi_{s}=\det P(s)
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{|\det D\Phi_{s}|}{s^{m}(1-s\langle\sigma,\bar{z}\rangle)^{n}}

is non-increasing, and so by the initial condition

P(s)=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}+O(s)&O(s)\\
O(s)&sI_{m}+O(s^{2})\end{bmatrix},

we can then integrate this inequality to get

\displaystyle|\det D\Phi_{s}|\leq{s^{m}(1-s\langle\sigma,\bar{z}\rangle)^{n}}.

∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
r>0
. Define
\Sigma^{+}=\{x\in\Sigma:\sigma(x)\neq 0\}
and
\Sigma^{0}=\{x\in\Sigma:\sigma(x)=0\}
.

By Lemma
2.1
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle|\{p\in M:\alpha r<d(x,p)<r\text{ for all }x\in\Sigma\}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\int_{Z_{\alpha}}\left|\det D\Phi_{r}(x,z)\right|1_{A_{r}}(x,z)dzd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{0}}\int_{Z_{\alpha}}\left|\det D\Phi_{r}(x,z)\right|1_{A_{r}}(x,z)dzd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}+\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{Z_{\alpha}}\left|\det D\Phi_{r}(x,z)\right|1_{A_{r}}(x,z)dzd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I^{0}(\alpha,r)+I^{+}(\alpha,r),

where
Z_{\alpha}=\{z\in T_{x}^{\perp}\Sigma:\alpha<|z|<1\}
.

Note that

\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r^{n+m}}|\{p\in M:\alpha r<d(x,p)<r\text{ for all }x\in\Sigma\}|=\theta\left|\mathbb{B}^{n+m}\right|\left(1-\alpha^{n+m}\right)

and hence

(2.4)

\begin{split}\lim_{\alpha\to 1^{-}}\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{r^{n+m}}|\{p\in M:\alpha r<d(x,p)<r\text{ for all }x\in\Sigma\}|=(n+m)\theta\left|\mathbb{B}^{n+m}\right|.\end{split}

Let us assume
m\geq 3
at the moment.

We first consider the integral
I^{+}(\alpha,r)
.
As explained in the Introduction (Section
1
), on
\Sigma^{+}
we decompose
T_{x}^{\perp}\Sigma=\widetilde{T}_{x}^{\perp}\Sigma\oplus\operatorname{span}(\sigma)
, so any normal vector can be written as
y+t\frac{\sigma}{|\sigma|}
with
y\in\widetilde{T}_{x}^{\perp}\Sigma
, and we identify it with
(y,t)
.
Lemma
2.2
implies that

t
satisfies
-1<t\leq\frac{1}{r|\sigma(x)|}
for
(x,y,t)\in A_{r}
, so Lemmas
2.1
and
2.4
give

(2.5)

\displaystyle I^{+}(\alpha,r)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{-1}^{\frac{1}{r|\sigma(x)|}}\int_{Y_{\alpha,x,t}}\left|\det D\Phi_{r}(x,y,t)\right|1_{A_{r}}(x,y,t)dydtd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{-1}^{\frac{1}{r|\sigma|}}\int_{Y_{\alpha,x,t}}r^{m}(1-r|\sigma(x)|t)^{n}dydtd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma},

where
Y_{\alpha,x,t}:=\left\{y\in\widetilde{T}_{x}^{\perp}\Sigma:\alpha^{2}<|y|^{2}+t^{2}<1\right\}
.

For
|t|<1
, we have the limit

\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\frac{\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}-\left(\alpha^{2}-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}}{1-\alpha}=(m-1)\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-3}{2}}.

Therefore, for
(x,y,t)\in A_{r}
, as
\alpha\to 1^{-}
and
r\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\left|Y_{\alpha,x,t}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{B}^{m-1}\right|\left(\left(1-t^{2}\right)_{+}^{\frac{m-1}{2}}-\left(\alpha^{2}-t^{2}\right)_{+}^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=(m-1)\left|\mathbb{B}^{m-1}\right|\left(\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-3}{2}}(1-\alpha)+O\left((1-\alpha)^{2}\right)\right).

Here
O\bigl((1-\alpha)^{p}\bigr)
is a quantity whose absolute value is bounded by
C(1-\alpha)^{p}
for some constant
C>0
independent of
\alpha
, when
\alpha
is sufficiently close to
1
.

Hence for
r
large enough and
\alpha
close enough to
1
, the fiber integral in (
2.5
) satisfies

(2.6)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{\frac{1}{r|\sigma|}}\int_{Y_{\alpha,x,t}}r^{m}(1-r|\sigma|t)^{n}dydt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m-1)\left|\mathbb{B}^{m-1}\right|r^{m}\int_{-1}^{\frac{1}{r|\sigma|}}\left(\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-3}{2}}(1-\alpha)+O\left((1-\alpha)^{2}\right)\right)(1-r|\sigma|t)^{n}dt.

Now, recall the identity
[
3
, Eqn. (13.45), (13.49)]

(2.7)

\int_{0}^{1}s^{p-1}(1-s)^{q-1}ds=\frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)},

where
p,q>0
and
\Gamma(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}e^{-t}dt
is the gamma function.

For
r
large enough, we consider

(2.8)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{\frac{1}{r|\sigma|}}\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-3}{2}}(1-r|\sigma|t)^{n}dt=

\displaystyle r^{n}|\sigma|^{n}\int_{-1}^{0}(-t)^{n}\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-3}{2}}dt+O\left(r^{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{m-1}{2}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{n+m}{2}\right)}r^{n}|\sigma|^{n}+O\left(r^{n-1}\right).

(Here

is a quantity whose absolute value is bounded by
Cr^{p}
for some constant
C>0
independent of
r
, when
r
is large enough.)

On the other hand,

(2.9)

\int_{-1}^{\frac{1}{r|\sigma|}}(1-r|\sigma|t)^{n}dt=\frac{(1+r|\sigma|)^{n+1}}{(n+1)r|\sigma|}=O(r^{n}).

For sufficiently large

and
\alpha
close to
1
, (
2.5
), (
2.6
), (
2.8
) and (
2.9
), together with the identity
\left|\mathbb{B}^{d}\right|=\frac{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)}
and the relation

, yield

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{m+n}}I^{+}(\alpha,r)\leq

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)(m-1)\left|\mathbb{B}^{m-1}\right|\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{m-1}{2}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{n+m}{2}\right)}\int_{\Sigma^{+}}|\sigma|^{n}+O((1-\alpha)^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\pi^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+m}{2}\right)}\int_{\Sigma^{+}}|\sigma|^{n}+O((1-\alpha)^{2}).

Therefore

(2.10)

\limsup_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\limsup_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{r^{n+m}}I^{+}(\alpha,r)\leq\pi^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+m}{2}\right)}\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|^{n}.

We now turn to

. In this case, by Lemma

we have

\displaystyle I^{0}(\alpha,r)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{0}}\int_{Z_{\alpha}}\left|\det D\Phi_{r}(x,z)\right|1_{A_{r}}(x,z)dzd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{0}}\int_{Z_{\alpha}}r^{m}dzd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathbb{B}^{m}|(1-\alpha^{m})r^{m}|\Sigma^{0}|,

and so

\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{r^{n+m}}I^{0}(\alpha,r)=0.

Combining this with (

), (
2.4
) and (

), we obtain

\theta(n+m)\left|\mathbb{B}^{n+m}\right|\leq\pi^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+m}{2}\right)}\int_{\Sigma^{+}}|\sigma|^{n}.

This inequality is of the form

for some positive constant

.

Using

From this, we arrive at

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|^{n}\geq\theta|\mathbb{S}^{n}|.

Let us now consider the case where

or
2
.

In the case where

, note that the condition in Theorem

is that the sectional curvature of

The remaining case is when

. Note that it is no longer true that the condition

) and (

\displaystyle I^{+}(\alpha,r)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{T_{\alpha,r,x}}\left|\det D\Phi_{r}(x,t)\right|1_{A_{r}}(x,t)\,dt\,d\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{T_{\alpha,r,x}}r(1-r|\sigma(x)|t)^{n}\,dt\,d\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}(x),

where

For fixed

\displaystyle r\int_{-1}^{-\alpha}(1-r|\sigma(x)|t)^{n}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{n+1}|\sigma(x)|^{n}\int_{\alpha}^{1}t^{n}dt+O\left(r^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r^{n+1}|\sigma(x)|^{n}}{n+1}\left(1-\alpha^{n+1}\right)+O\left(r^{n}\right).

Dividing both sides by

∎

3.
Equality case

In this section, we analyze the equality case in Theorem
1.3
. First of all, it is easy to see that the inequality is sharp. For example, take
\Sigma
to be a closed
n
-dimensional manifold with positive curvature and define the conical metric

g=dr^{2}+\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{2}g_{\Sigma}

on
[r_{0},\infty)\times\Sigma
, where
r_{0}=\left(\frac{|\Sigma|}{\theta|\mathbb{S}^{n}|}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}
.
By gluing with a suitable compact set along the boundary, the metric can be extended to a complete
n+1
dimensional manifold
M
with non-negative curvature. One then finds that the asymptotic volume ratio is
\theta
, and
\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|^{n}=\theta|\mathbb{S}^{n}|
. Taking the product of
M
with
\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
then yields a codimension-
m
submanifold with the same integral
\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|^{n}=\theta|\mathbb{S}^{n}|
.

In the case where
n=1
, we cannot even expect
\Sigma
to have constant
|\sigma|
, since any closed curve in
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
that lies on a two-dimensional plane and is convex in that plane satisfies
\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|=2\pi
when regarded as a closed curve in
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
. Indeed, this condition precisely characterizes the equality case of the classical Fenchel inequality.

To analyze the equality case in Theorem
1.3
, from now on we are going to assume that the equality
\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|^{n}=\theta|\mathbb{S}^{n}|
holds.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
x\in\Sigma^{+}
,

such that

Define

Suppose the conclusion is not true, then there exist

,

,

\displaystyle d(q_{0},\exp_{x_{0}}(rz_{0})\leq

\displaystyle d(q_{0},\exp_{x_{0}}(r_{0}z_{0})+(r-r_{0})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle r_{0}+(r-r_{0})=r,\quad\text{i.e.}(x_{0},z_{0})\notin\mathcal{A}_{r}.

It then follows that there is a neighborhood

of

in

such that for

\displaystyle|\{p\in M:\alpha r<d(x,p)<r\text{ for all }x\in\Sigma\}|

) by

Lemma 3.2

\displaystyle|\{p\in M:\alpha r<d(x,p)<r\text{ for all }x\in\Sigma\}|

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{-1}^{\frac{1}{r|\sigma|}}\int_{Y_{\alpha}}\big(1-\varepsilon 1_{V}(x,y,t)\big)r^{m}\left(1-r|\sigma|t\right)^{n}dydtd\mathrm{vol}_{\Sigma}+I^{0}(\alpha,r)

\displaystyle:J(\alpha,r)-\varepsilon I(\alpha,r)+\mathrm{remainder}

for all

\alpha

.

We now claim that
\displaystyle\liminf_{\alpha\to 1^{-}}\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{r^{n+m}}I(\alpha,r)
is positive. This is straightforward but slightly tedious.

As

Hence, for

Consequently, for

for some

, where

.

Because the integrand of the integral on the RHS in (

\displaystyle\int_{T_{x_{0},r}\cap V}\bigl(1-r|\sigma(x_{0})|t\bigr)^{n}dt\geq

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}-\delta_{2}}^{t_{0}}\bigl(1-r|\sigma(x_{0})|t\bigr)^{n}dt

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{-\delta_{2}}^{0}\bigl(1-r|\sigma(x_{0})|t\bigr)^{n}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r|\sigma(x_{0})|(n+1)}\left[\bigl(1+r|\sigma(x_{0})|\delta_{2}\bigr)^{n+1}-1\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{2}^{n+1}r^{n}|\sigma(x_{0})|^{n}}{n+1},

where

is chosen so that

contains the interval

.
By continuity,

\int_{T_{x,r}\cap V}\bigl(1-r|\sigma|t\bigr)^{n}dt\geq\frac{\delta_{2}^{\,n+1}r^{n}}{2(n+1)}|\sigma(x_{0})|^{n}

for

.

Combining this with (

), we find that

\liminf_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\liminf_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{r^{n+m}}I(\alpha,r)>0.

Putting this into (

) and proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we conclude that

\theta|\mathbb{S}^{n}|<\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|^{n},

which is a contradiction.

If

is that the sectional curvature of

is non-negative. By taking the product of

, we can view

as a codimension

still has nonnegative sectional curvature, while both

and
\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma|^{n}
remain invariant. The preceding argument can still be applied, which yields a contradiction.

When

, the previous argument requires a slight modification. Observe that
z_{0}=-\frac{\sigma(x_{0})}{|\sigma(x_{0})|}
, and hence, under our identification,

. We choose a sufficiently small neighborhood

. Here

and

We replace (

) with

(3.5)

\displaystyle|\{p\in M:\alpha r<d(x,p)<r\text{ for all }x\in\Sigma\}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{T_{\alpha,r,x}}\left|\det D\Phi_{r}(x,t)\right|1_{A_{r}}(x,t)dtd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{T_{\alpha,r,x}}\left(1-\varepsilon 1_{V}(x,t)\right)r(1-r|\sigma|t)^{n}dtd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}+I^{0}(\alpha,r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{T_{\alpha,r,x}}r(1-r|\sigma|t)^{n}dtd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}-\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{T_{\alpha,r,x}\cap V_{x}}r(1-r|\sigma|t)^{n}dtd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}+I^{0}(\alpha,r).

\displaystyle\int_{T_{\alpha,r,x_{0}}\cap V_{x_{0}}}r\left(1-r\left|\sigma\left(x_{0}\right)\right|t\right)^{n}dt\geq

\displaystyle r\int_{-1}^{-\alpha}\left(1-r\left|\sigma\left(x_{0}\right)\right|t\right)^{n}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(1+r\left|\sigma\left(x_{0}\right)\right|\right)^{n+1}-\left(1+\alpha r\left|\sigma\left(x_{0}\right)\right|\right)^{n+1}}{(n+1)\left|\sigma\left(x_{0}\right)\right|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle r\left(1+r\left|\sigma\left(x_{0}\right)\right|\right)^{n}}(1-\alpha)+O(r^{n+1}(1-\alpha)^{2})

if

is close enough to

and

By continuity, it is then easy to see that

\liminf_{\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}}\liminf_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{T_{\alpha,r,x}\cap V_{x}}r(1-r|\sigma|t)^{n}dtd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}>0.

In view of (
3.5
), the same reasoning as in the previous argument applies, which leads to a contradiction.
∎

, the equality case of Theorem

.

Proof.

(c)
We now show that the image of the normal exponential map applied to normal vectors in the half-space opposite the mean curvature vector fills the tubular neighbourhood of

with asymptotic volume density one. A precise formulation is as follows.

Let
\Sigma_{r}^{+}
denote the subset of the normal bundle of
\Sigma^{+}
given by

\Sigma_{r}^{+}:=\{(x,z)\in T^{\perp}\Sigma^{+}:\langle\sigma(x),z\rangle\leq 0,\;|z|<r\}.

We compute the volume of the image of
\Sigma_{r}^{+}
under the normal exponential map. By (

\displaystyle|\exp(\Sigma^{+}_{r})|=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{r}^{+}}\exp^{*}\mathrm{vol}_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{-1}^{0}\int_{Y_{x,t}}\left|\det D\Phi_{r}(x,y,t)\right|dydtd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{-1}^{0}\int_{Y_{x,t}}r^{m}(1-r|\sigma(x)|t)^{n}dydtd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{B}^{m-1}\right|r^{m}\int_{\Sigma^{+}}\int_{-1}^{0}\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}(1-r|\sigma(x)|t)^{n}dtd\operatorname{vol}_{\Sigma}(x).

\displaystyle=r^{n}|\sigma(x)|^{n}\int_{0}^{1}u^{n}\left(1-u^{2}\right)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}du+O\left(r^{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{n+m+2}{2}\right)}|\sigma(x)|^{n}r^{n}+O\left(r^{n-1}\right)

.

\displaystyle|\exp(\Sigma^{+}_{r})|=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{n+m+2}{2}\right)}|\mathbb{B}^{m-1}|r^{n+m}\int_{\Sigma^{+}}|\sigma(x)|^{n}+O(r^{n+m-1})

\displaystyle\theta\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\right)}{2\Gamma\left(\frac{n+m+2}{2}\right)}|\mathbb{B}^{m-1}||\mathbb{S}^{n}|r^{n+m}+O(r^{n+m-1}).
