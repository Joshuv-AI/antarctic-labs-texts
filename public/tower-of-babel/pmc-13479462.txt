Examining the alignment between an intervention-based paramedic model of care and 9-1-1 utilization
Authors: Walter Tavares, Ryan P. Strum, Lindsay Henderson, Phil Moran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare services must align with public needs and adapt to changing healthcare landscapes to be effective. Originating from emergency medicine and public safety, the paramedic model has traditionally focused on resuscitation, emergent needs and patient transportation. However, increased volume of non-emergent cases and a broadening use of paramedics suggest a potential misalignment with public utilization patterns. This study evaluates the alignment of paramedicine’s model of care with the public’s health requirements.MethodsA retrospective observational cohort study analyzed electronic patient care records completed by paramedics following patient interactions from January 1, 2017, to June 30, 2023, assessing the use of an existing model of care during paramedic responses across urban, suburban, and rural communities in Ontario, Canada. Each patient interaction was assessed for the presence or absence of interventions included in the provincial Advanced Life Support Patient Care Standards, used as a model of care framework for this region. Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to explore alignment.ResultsA total of 1,244,537 patient-initiated 9-1-1 calls from six paramedic services were examined. Only 20% (n = 249,312) of incidents involved the existing model of care, with a distinct ordinal reduction pattern of interventions as patient acuity levels decreased. No significant explanatory differences or predictors were observed in age, sex, dispatch priority assigned prior to arrival, or location of the event. Receiving medical care was associated only with conditions of cardiac arrest, cardiac ischemia and gastrointestinal symptoms. This sample of events suggests patients access care using the 9-1-1 system mostly for non-emergency events that do not align with the current model of care.ConclusionsFor patients who access healthcare using the 9-1-1 system, this study identifies a limited correspondence between the current intervention-based paramedic model of care and patterns of 9-1-1 utilization. These findings suggest that the existing model of care is applied in a minority of encounters. Prevailing paradigms guiding core paramedic systems may need to be reconsidered or augmented.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14890-8.

Background

Ideally, healthcare services are designed and continuously adapted to meet the evolving needs of the public and the changing healthcare landscape. Frenk discusses health system stewardship, highlighting the need to ensure that the healthcare system’s design and operation are closely aligned with public needs and health goals [1]. This alignment principle is critical because the effectiveness of healthcare provision hinges on its responsiveness to the demographic, epidemiological, policy, regulatory and technological changes within a society. Services that are closely attuned to the public’s health requirements are more likely to achieve better health outcomes, enhance patient satisfaction, and ensure the equitable distribution of healthcare resources [2]. Alignment with public need facilitates the detection of needs, efficient use of healthcare planning, design, delivery and funding, as resources are directed towards services where they are most needed and can have the greatest impact. To achieve such alignment, healthcare systems can engage in regular and thorough evaluations that encompass current health issues, the healthcare landscape and their own structure and role within those contexts. In this study, we apply this evaluative lens of health systems and alignment to paramedicine – a large health human resource – which we suspect has become poorly aligned with the types of encounters occurring within the 9-1-1 system.

Paramedicine represents a large, geographically dispersed, mobile and community-based health human resource whose scope of practice and role in the health care system has been evolving [3]. Rooted in patient assessment and management of critically ill and injured patients, with a parallel scope of practice deeply embedded in emergency medicine, rapid transportation, public safety and society’s need for emergency medical services, the traditional scope of practice and models of care within paramedicine have been influenced by and aligned with these fundamental life-sustaining principles. However, while paramedicine is designed generally to serve emergent or resuscitative needs (e.g., respiratory distress, life-threatening traumatic injuries, cardiac arrest), there is an increasing trend of 9-1-1 initiated calls for lower acuity and non-emergent complaints or events (e.g., mild pain, social isolation, falls) [4–6]. Though some international commonalities exist, decisions regarding scopes of practice and services provided are often adapted to regional contexts. Newer ways of thinking, for example, the ethos of interprofessional primary care or for specific patient populations (e.g., mental health) or approaches (e.g., palliative care), are finding their way into core paramedicine systems, based largely on the success of targeted local innovative clinical programs [7–9].

Despite ongoing efforts to better support public utilization patterns, core paramedicine systems may not have sufficiently kept pace in guiding paradigms (i.e., ways of thinking) and service models, leaving paramedicine potentially poorly aligned and underutilized. Recent research identified that call volumes in paramedic systems have been increasing at a rate of 38.3% per decade, outpacing population growth fourfold (9.4%) [10]. Notably, paramedics were utilized more for non-emergent health conditions (60%) than time-sensitive emergencies [10]. This expansion in paramedic call volume and likely type of event may reflect changes in healthcare demand, accessibility issues with primary care, evolving public expectations, increased demand for immediate care, the perceived expansion of paramedicine roles and services, and other health or health system-related factors (e.g., healthcare policies, social, financial, cultural factors). Others have reported that a majority of patients who access healthcare using paramedic services and who are transported to an emergency department (ED) are discharged without admission [11, 12]. For these patients, the alignment of services provided by paramedics only ranged from 5 to 10% [11]. This is leading researchers to explore ways of optimizing alignment [13–15]. This shifting healthcare landscape and the changing behaviour of the public suggest misalignments between how paramedicine systems are designed and the distribution of encounters occurring through the 9-1-1 system.

Understanding this alignment issue in paramedicine has at least two broad implications. First, healthcare services are guided by paradigms that ultimately shape their structure, models of care, educational strategies, policies and more. If alignment could be better understood, this might serve as the point at which guiding paradigms are solidified, replaced or augmented with others that may be more satisfying. This represents the “healthcare stewardship” described above [1]. Second, new models of care are continuously being explored and proposed in healthcare systems. For instance, moves to deinstitutionalize healthcare, reduce the reliance on hospitals for patient-centred community options, improve access to and availability of primary care, the introduction of family health teams, and more integrated home and community-based care have been proposed as solutions [16–18]. As paramedicine continues to evolve and be explored as an additional means toward supporting solutions to healthcare system strain, identifying (a) where paramedicine may be able to contribute further and (b) what, if any, previous changes in services have promoted alignment, is likely to support future enhancements or shifts in paramedicine and how it integrates with other parts of the healthcare system.

Therefore, our objective in this study was to explore the degree to which current intervention-based paramedic services align with patterns of 9-1-1 utilization. Because the public accesses healthcare as they see fit, we examined how well paramedic services’ scope of practice aligns with those self-identified events. We examined the utilization of existing models of care as a measure of paramedicine system alignment and efficiency by asking:

What proportion of the population who access care using 9-1-1 were serviced by the existing model of care provided by paramedics in Ontario? Methods

Design

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study of existing paramedic electronic patient care records (ePCR) to examine the utilization of existing models of care as a measure of system alignment and efficiency to public care use [19, 20]. We adhered to the Report of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely-Collected Health Data (RECORD) statement for reporting our results [21].

Setting

This study analyzed 9-1-1-initiated calls for paramedics in a specific region of Ontario, Canada. In Ontario, paramedic services are regulated by the Ministry of Health (MOH) and the Ambulance Act of Ontario to provide emergency medical services. The scope of practice is relatively consistent across the province and is defined in part by the Advanced Life Support Patient Care Standards (ALS-PCS) issued by the MOH [22]. Regional “Base Hospitals” provide medical oversight and maintain electronic patient care records (ePCRs) of all patient interactions and help shape these standards. The base hospital in this region provides medical oversight, quality assurance and record keeping for approximately 1,600 paramedics servicing over 220,000 emergency calls per year to over 2.2 million residents. The region includes a mix of urban, suburban and rural communities across 17,116 sq/km. It includes eight regional and community hospitals and includes a range of primary health and social care services supported by 4 regional Ontario Health Care Teams.

Data Collection

Our study utilized patient-level data measured and recorded on ePCRs by paramedics at the time of their patient encounters following 9-1-1 activation. ePCRs include identifying personal demographics and health information (e.g., age, primary medical problems, health history), clinical procedural data using a standardized list of coded clinical activities, and room for notes (e.g., referral to community services). These patient records are protected by local privacy regulations but are made available for research with appropriate research ethics and privacy approvals.

Our inclusion criteria were any completed ePCRs involving a patient contact during our study period, regardless of location, event, priority, intervention or disposition following a 9-1-1 call. Exclusions were instances where paramedics reported “no patient found/contact”, “out of service/administration”, or “standby”. While some of the included regions had community paramedicine (mobile integrated health) programs during the study period, our data did not include any of those activities if they occurred outside of the 9-1-1 system. We were unable to explore any sources of bias in the data.

Data were extracted by a single base hospital servicing six paramedic services. These data were obtained from the base hospital’s central data repository, a 9-1-1 incidence database compiling all call features and ePCRs completed by paramedics after each 9-1-1 call response. We collected data between January 1, 2017 and June 30, 2023.

Selection of patient, clinical and logistical characteristics was based on relevance to the study objectives, clinical judgement and data availability. We extracted non-identifying clinical data (e.g., reason for 9-1-1 call, priority, clinical profile, final primary problem, patient acuity level using Canadian Triage Acuity Scale (CTAS), final primary problem, disposition), as well as ALS-PCS interventions provided. All data, including CTAS were recorded by paramedics. Administrative data (e.g., location of event, paramedic type, time on task) were also collected. We ensured all data in the aggregate (within case) remained non-identifying. See supplemental file 1 for a list of variables and their descriptions.

Defining existing model of care

Alignment was defined by the performance of at least one medical delegated act represented in the model of care by paramedics in the community. We defined the “model of care” as any intervention listed in the MOH ALS-PCS Ver. 5.1 [22]. No interventions had been removed since July 1, 2017, except for the Electronic Control Device. Other changes include revisions and updates to existing medical directives as well as some additions to the core (e.g., analgesia in Sept 2019, three new medications including Oxytocin, Dexamethasone, and Ondesatron in 2023) and auxiliary medical directives (e.g., COVID-19 April 2020). A summary of changes is included in supplemental file 2. While the “Basic Life Support Patient Care Standard” (BLS-PCS) provides guidelines for common patient encounters (e.g., assessment approaches, 12-lead electrocardiogram findings indicative of acute myocardial infarction, triage reporting details) [23], the ALS-PCS outlines interventions, particularly those including delegated controlled medical acts. The ALS-PCS also includes a comprehensive list of guidelines (e.g., consent, refusal of treatment, responsibility for care) and 18 and 29 core Medical Directives for “Primary Care Paramedics” (PCP) and “Advanced Care Paramedics” (ACP), respectively [22]. There are an additional 15 “Auxiliary Medicine Directives” for PCPs and ACPs. We used both core and auxiliary directives for both PCPs and ACPs to best represent the prescribed and intervention-based model of care. No other practice-based intervention framework exists for paramedicine in this context.

Our intention was not to reduce paramedicine to clinical interventions. We recognize that narrowing paramedic practice to Advanced Life Support Patient Care Standards (ALS-PCS) interventions is inherently incomplete. Several other activities—including basic life support care, assessment, reassurance, navigation, and referral—may also represent meaningful services provided to patients. Accordingly, the presence of an ALS-PCS intervention in this study should not be interpreted as indicating that patient needs were met, nor should the absence of an intervention be interpreted as indicating that needs were unmet. Rather, ALS-PCS interventions are used here as an indicator of how the current paramedic model of care is structured and prioritized within the 9-1-1 system, allowing us to examine the relationship between patterns of service utilization and an intervention-focused model of care.

Analysis

We reported descriptive statistics of patient, paramedic and logistical characteristics using measures of central tendency (frequencies, proportions), with statistical differences assessed using chi-squared tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables. Statistical significance was defined at a threshold of p < 0.05 for all comparisons. The overall prevalence of patient contacts involving ALS-PCS care was reported as the top ten most frequently recorded primary problems. The top ten most frequently performed ALS-PCS interventions were also reported for the cohort. We used multivariable binary logistic regression to study independent associations between receiving an ALS-PCS intervention and patient characteristics. Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and statistical significance was determined using p-values (p < 0.05) to identify significant associations. Characteristics were collapsed into ordinal or nominal categories to facilitate model stability when data were non-continuous or truncated (i.e., < 2% per cell of cohort). Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) [24]. Data were managed and analyzed in R statistical software (v.3.6). Because we were exploring the use of any intervention during our study period, not the proportion of use, the most recent version of the ALS-PCS at the conclusion of this study (Ver. 5.1) provided the most robust way of ensuring any relevant intervention could be detected. No data linkages were included in this study.

Research ethics review and approval were provided by the University of Toronto (#45456) and Lakeridge Health (2023-014). Given the study design, the need for patients to consent to participate was waived by these two institutional review boards.

Results

We received 1,342,488 patient records. After exclusions, our cohort contained 1,244,537 9-1-1-initated calls where paramedics attended the scene and made patient contact. Overall, 249,312 (20%) incidents received at least one intervention listed in the ALS-PCS, and 995,225 (80%) did not. Table 1 shows descriptive characteristics of these two groups. There did not appear to be any explanatory differences or predictors in age, sex, dispatch priority assigned prior to arrival, location of event, or paramedic type. We observed that the majority of the highest acuity patients (CTAS 1) received ALS-PCS interventions (n = 14,018; 66.1% vs. n = 7,188; 33.9%). However, these patients only account for 1.7% (n = 21,206) of all instances, representing 5.6% and 0.7% of the intervention and no intervention designations, respectively. From CTAS 2 to 5, despite being the majority of patient events (n = 1,009,897; 79.2%) a minority of patients received any interventions, showing a distinct ordinal reduction as acuity decreased (CTAS 2 = 44.6%, CTAS 3 = 18.0%%; CTAS 4 = 6.3%; CTAS 5 = 1.6%). A similar pattern was observed for transport priority, with higher acuity events being associated with more interventions but accounting for a significantly lower proportion of patients encountered. Collectively, these results suggest an emergency-focused intervention model of care, but a population who access care for non-emergency reasons and who are left underserved by the existing model of care.

Table 4 presents unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models for receiving ALS-PCS care by paramedics. Patients with primary problems of medical cardiac arrest (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05), cardiac ischemia (OR 3.21, 95% CI 3.06–3.37) and nausea, vomiting or diarrhea (OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.76–1.90) were independently associated with receiving ALS care. Additionally, patients aged 40 to 49 (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.07–1.14), having an emergent acuity (OR 3.68, 95% CI 3.63–3.73), and picked up from home (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.09–1.13), were also significantly associated with receiving ALS care in our model. Independent associations were also observed when ACPs managed patients and paramedics conducted assessments and interventions within the BLS-PCS framework (oxygen administration, electrocardiogram, blood glucometry, stroke assessment, and identification of traumatic injuries). The model’s AUC was 0.84, indicating the adjusted model is an excellent classifier of characteristics associated with receiving care listed in the ALS-PCS. The absence of associations between administered ALS-PCS care and characteristics of patients that are not evident in emergencies underscores the need for re-evaluating the scope of practice to ensure better alignment with patient needs in the community.

Discussion

This study critically examined the alignment of paramedicine with the public’s health needs when accessing care using a 9-1-1 system, probing whether current paramedic services meet the evolving demand profile of the community they serve. We explored the scope and utilization of paramedic services in the context of a retrospective observational cohort study, encompassing a substantial and comprehensive dataset derived from paramedic records. We limited this exploration to the ALS-PCS scope of practice as an indicator of what the system prioritizes in their model of care within the 9-1-1 system. Results suggest (a) that majority of patient interactions are not classified by paramedics as emergent, and (b) that the majority of patient interactions do not receive interventions included in the model of care for this region. The most prevalent problems received care minimally, and care predominantly resided in three domains: IV administration, pain relief (e.g., Tylenol), and symptom relief (e.g. Gravol). These lower acuity changes seem to be explaining some of the increase in alignment that was observed over time and reflect an appreciation for the broadening needs of patients beyond emergency intervention. Finally, associations between patient characteristics and the receipt of interventions were mostly absent. Despite a slight increase over time, these findings suggest that the intervention-focused paramedic model of care is infrequently used in contemporary 9-1-1 encounters, especially for lower-acuity events, raising questions about how well the design of this model corresponds with patterns of service utilization. This is especially pronounced in non-emergent situations, calling for reform to optimize the utilization of paramedic resources in response to the diverse and evolving healthcare needs of the population.

The implications of these results suggest that paramedicine services may not optimally align with the types of encounters occurring through the 9-1-1 system and, leaving this health human resource underutilized and misaligned, particularly in non-emergent situations. The rapid evolution of the healthcare landscape necessitates continuous adaptation by healthcare professionals and institutions [25]. Shifts in patient demographics, increased wait times, difficulty accessing primary care and changes in healthcare policy and best practices (e.g., triple to the quintuple aim, redefining of “value-based care models”) are examples of a continually changing healthcare landscape and the importance of continuous learning and adaptation [26–28]. In response, in Ontario, Canada (i.e., the context of this study) and elsewhere there is a growing emphasis on the deinstitutionalization of healthcare for more community-based services, the integration of new and existing health services, and on patient-centered, team based or interprofessional models [17, 18, 29]. Staying informed of and adapting to these changes is crucial for optimizing the effectiveness and efficiency of healthcare systems.

Core paramedicine systems have been slow to adapt and this study provides further evidence supporting the necessity for shifts in the design, organization, and enactment of these systems in Canada, and what guides those changes. For instance, earlier research has proposed several potential shifts to models of care within core paramedicine systems to better support community-based services [11]. This included increased diagnostic capabilities, targeting healthcare services for the most common encounters, and building in consultations and interventions for lower acuity events [11]. In 2021, after exploring paramedicine’s role as a solution to a changing healthcare landscape, researchers highlighted the importance of rethinking paramedicine as a health and social service, including promoting more integrated services and better alignment with patient needs in their community contexts [30]. These principles and other related research exploring healthcare integration, specifically in paramedicine [31] represent a transformative shift in defining the role of paramedicine. When effectively implemented, they offer an opportunity to enhance service alignment. Clinical programs involving paramedics outside of core 9-1-1 systems, broadly referred to as community paramedicine [32–34], demonstrate the potential, opportunities, and benefits that can be realized within core paramedicine systems if successfully transferred into new scopes of practice, practice patterns, and regulation, and education.

The modest increase in alignment over time suggests that certain system changes may have enabled a greater proportion of patients to receive care that better corresponds to their needs, consistent with principles of health system stewardship. However, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that care is comprehensive or that patient needs are fully addressed, particularly given the range of alternative interventions available within paramedicine. The concept of alignment itself warrants further conceptual refinement. There is no objective threshold by which alignment can be definitively established or refuted; rather, it is inherently subjective, context-dependent, and shaped by the paradigms that inform evaluative judgements.

This distinction is reflected in the principle underlying the prudent layperson standard, which recognizes that individuals may reasonably seek emergency care based on symptoms or perceived risk, regardless of whether a clinical intervention ultimately proves necessary [6, 35]). Other frameworks provide related insights into the complex nature of healthcare access [36]. In this context, the absence of an intervention does not imply that seeking care was inappropriate or that needs were not addressed. Patients may seek evaluation, reassurance, access to the broader healthcare system or for needs not reflected in the model of care we examined. Accordingly, our analysis focuses on the relationship between patterns of 9-1-1 utilization and the structure of the current intervention-based paramedic model of care, rather than assessing the comprehensive and multidimensional needs of individual patient encounters. Accordingly, our characterization of “alignment” is intended not as a definitive conclusion about whether needs were met, but as a way of examining how this current intervention-based model of care corresponds to patterns of 9-1-1 utilization, and as a means of prompting critical reflection and further inquiry.

The prevailing paradigm in paramedicine, predominantly aligned with emergency medicine, transportation and public safety, may not fully capture or align with the complexities of today’s healthcare needs of the public this system encounters. Current paramedic practices emphasize rapid response, accessibility, and immediate care for acute medical incidents. However, this study’s findings and those highlighted above indicate potential missed opportunities for paramedic engagement with the community, particularly in non-emergency interactions, which now represent the majority of paramedic interactions. There is a traditional emphasis on paramedicine primarily for emergency intervention; it is evident that evolving healthcare challenges demand broader thinking and scope. Considering national efforts to optimize healthcare worker roles and address systemic issues like resource allocation [37], the adoption of more versatile, community-oriented, and interprofessional primary care approaches within 9-1-1 paramedicine system could be an effective response to such changes. While research in this area continues to be needed [5, 9], this shift could enhance the alignment of paramedic services with public needs, demonstrating the necessity for reevaluation and possible augmentation of foundational paramedicine paradigms.

In Ontario, Canada, specifically, there has been notable progress in paramedicine services to meet public healthcare needs better. Although updates to the model of care primarily target critical yet infrequent conditions (e.g., suspected adrenal crisis, refinement of neonatal resuscitation techniques), the Ministry of Health has endorsed innovative care models that signal a transformative phase in paramedicine [16]. Particularly noteworthy are ’treat and refer’ strategies, which enhance the continuity of patient care, and ‘treat and discharge’ pathways, representing a shift from traditional transportation-centric approaches to more holistic and patient-oriented models that do not incorporate hospital care. Moreover, the implementation of ‘alternative destination’ pathways signifies a significant evolution, proposing to optimize patient care by facilitating the most suitable treatment settings beyond the ED. The integration of these new approaches and acknowledging the need to be more patient-centred (e.g., integrating better care coordination and planning) is responding to calls for reform [38, 39], and contributing to the incremental realignment of paramedic services with several recently proposed principles guiding the future of paramedicine [40] and the community’s health care needs. In Table 5, we expand on these concepts, outlining proposed implications and recommendations for the community to consider. These recommendations should be evaluated in the context of potential policy adjustments, practice enhancements, evidence-based innovations, and educational reforms, with particular attention to the healthcare providers who will implement these changes.

Experience with alternative pathways internationally illustrates both the potential and challenges associated with such system redesign. For example, the Emergency Triage, Treat, and Transport (ET3) model in the United States sought to allow paramedics to transport patients to alternative care settings or provide treatment in place supported by telehealth [41, 42]. Although the program was ultimately discontinued, analyses suggest that its implementation was constrained by regulatory fragmentation, reimbursement limitations, and integration challenges across healthcare systems. These experiences reinforce that reform pathways require careful alignment of guiding paradigms, governance, system, provider and public readiness, and associated healthcare infrastructure.

Limitations

This study must be considered in the context of its limitations. First, retrospective observational cohort study designs have some inherent limitations [43]. In this context, we were dependent on the reporting and coding of events in the clinical environment by paramedics who, by the nature of their work, may have variable reporting of some variables (e.g., CTAS). Still, ongoing quality assurance practices by the base hospital contribute to the trustworthiness of this data. Second, patient care encompasses other activities not sufficiently represented in the ALS-PCS. For example, BLS-PCS (as described above) and other activities (e.g., coaching, reassurance, referrals) are not captured. The inclusion of these activities would likely increase the degree of alignment. However, for this study, we chose the ALS-PCS practice document because it provides a reasonable reflection of the scope of practice and services available to the community and, more importantly, reflects what system leaders prioritize. For example, education, certification, and continuing medical and quality assurance practices also leverage the ALS-PCS for guidance. Still, we must be careful to avoid being overly critical, given the other services paramedics provide. Third, while our data represents a large geographical region made up of a mix of community types, populations, health care policies, health services and access to care, it may not be representative of all areas in Ontario, Canada or of international contexts. Finally, we focused on core paramedicine systems (i.e., the 9-1-1 system) that the public navigates access to independently and for which most funding is allocated. As such, this data is not inclusive of all paramedic activities regionally, provincially, nationally or internationally.

Conclusions

For patients who access healthcare using the 9-1-1 system, this study reveals that the intervention-based paramedic model of care is used in a minority of 9-1-1 encounters, prompting further consideration of how paramedicine systems are designed relative to contemporary patterns of care demand. While alignment remains a context-dependent and conceptually underdeveloped construct, likely multidimensional, the findings of this study prompt reflection and further investigation rather than serve as a definitive judgment about whether community needs are being met. This study underscores the necessity for core paramedicine systems to evolve to reflect changes in public demand and healthcare delivery models, and provides a baseline for evaluating future enhancements. Prevailing emergency medicine, transportation and public safety paradigms in paramedicine may need to be reconsidered or augmented. It is imperative for researchers and healthcare leaders to continue to reconsider the design and function of paramedic services, aligning them more closely with contemporary healthcare objectives to enhance their contribution to the health and well-being of the communities they serve.
