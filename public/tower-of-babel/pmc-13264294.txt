Climatic drivers and niche dynamics: Modeling the current and future habitats of Prionailurusbengalensis in China
Authors: Fan Yong, Yanjing Zhang, Weibo Guo, Yanfei Liu, Xu Zhou, Genya Lei, Ruifeng Lin, Fangzhou Ma, Peng Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe leopard cat (Prionailurus bengalensis) is an ecologically significant class II-protected species in China. Using an ensemble species distribution model, niche dynamics analysis, and environmental similarity assessments, we evaluated climate risks to this species. Currently, suitable habitat covers 16.17% of China, primarily in the hilly southeast and Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau, with temperature seasonality and slope as key drivers. Future projections indicate stable core areas, northward expansion, local contraction, and a northward shift of the distribution centroid. Niche dynamics show high similarity (0.89–0.91) between future and current niches, suggesting limited range expansion. Nevertheless, over 80% of the current habitat is projected to experience high climatic novelty, introducing conservation uncertainty. To address these challenges, we propose a climate-adaptive framework focused on enhancing habitat connectivity, mitigating fragmentation, and implementing preventive measures in human-wildlife conflict zones, thereby supporting core habitat identification, corridor planning, and adaptive strategy formulation.Subject areas: Environmental science, Global change, Environmental management, Ecology, Zoology

Introduction

The global populations and geographic ranges of large carnivores have experienced substantial declines during the past two centuries,1,2,3 weakening their ecological function of regulating mesopredators and herbivores through top-down effects. This has triggered a “mesopredator releaseˮ phenomenon, whereby in the absence of apex predators, mesopredators may ascend to become the new dominant predators. This phenomenon could potentially initiate trophic cascades,4,5 as mesopredators exhibit a broad ecological niche and frequently serve as scavengers.6 Moreover, many omnivorous species (e.g., foxes and bears) act as seed dispersers,7 thereby filling multiple functional roles within ecosystems.8 The leopard cat (Prionailurus bengalensis), a widely distributed mesocarnivore felid across Asia, exemplifies such an ecological role. Its range extends from Afghanistan eastward to the Russian Far East and spans the archipelagos of Southeast Asia.9 However, this extensive distribution masks severe localized threats. Since the 20th century, habitat loss and degradation, hunting, and the fur trade have reduced leopard cat populations in China,10,11 and their current numbers starkly contrast with their historically widespread presence.12 This decline underpins its status as a National Secondary Protected Animal and has made it a source of growing conservation concern.13,14 Climate change has introduced a new selective pressure15,16,17 that, in addition to the typical felid life-history traits (e.g., low reproductive rates and population densities), may increase its vulnerability. However, the leopard cat’s opportunistic generalist ecology could be to its benefit.18,19 Specifically, its potential to utilize expanding human-modified landscapes (e.g., degraded forests and farmland20) suggests that its range may stabilize or even expand.14 Yet, quantitative assessments of its niche conservatism and distribution dynamics under climate change across China remain scarce, a gap this study aims to fill.

Species distribution patterns are governed by an interplay of biotic and abiotic factors.21 Of these factors, human-induced land-use changes, habitat fragmentation, and climate change are key drivers.22,23 Agricultural and infrastructure expansion cause direct habitat loss and impede species dispersal and gene flow.24,25 For example, the leopard cat strongly avoids human-dominated landscapes such as those fragmented by agricultural expansion, a characteristic that limits its available habitat.26 There are a variety of environmental factors that shape its distribution: It prefers warm, humid climates with stable precipitation,13,27 relies on forests and shrublands for shelter and prey resources, and benefits from gentle terrain that is conducive to hunting.13,28 Elevation serves as an indirect influence on its distribution by modulating the intensity of human disturbance and interspecific competition.29 Leopard cats do exhibit a degree of adaptability to anthropogenic pressures,30 but habitat fragmentation and human-wildlife conflict resulting from deforestation and settlement expansion remain major threats that reduce habitat suitability.31 In order to systematically address these environmental variables when identifying potentially suitable habitats for leopard cats and attempting to understand their future dynamics, distribution modeling must be used.

Species distribution models (SDMs) serve as essential tools to quantify species-environment relationships, predict spatial distributions, and inform conservation decisions.31 These models identify suitable habitats and evaluate species’ potential responses to environmental stressors such as climate change and habitat degradation.32 However, single-model approaches may be highly uncertain. To generate robust forecasts for conservation planning, we employed ensemble SDM techniques that integrate multiple algorithms to mitigate model-specific biases and improve predictive reliability.33,34,35 This approach provides critical technical support to address complex challenges in global biodiversity conservation.31 Furthermore, the mechanisms behind shifts in the leopard cat’s projected distribution remain poorly understood. To investigate this, we first applied a conceptual framework of the ecological niche, which defines the set of conditions for a species’ persistence36 and its dynamics in response to environmental change.32 The quantification of niche dynamics, typically through SDMs that evaluate differentiation and overlap,37 is key to revealing the mechanisms behind range shifts and assessing adaptive capacity.38,39 To identify the primary climatic factors that drive distributional changes, tools such as the multivariate environmental similarity surface (MESS) and the most dissimilar variable (MoD) analysis are required.40 These methods move beyond suitability mapping to critically assess the ecological realism and vulnerability of the projected habitats.41 Despite the widespread application of ensemble SDMs, niche dynamics analyses, and climatic novelty assessments in biodiversity research, these three analytical dimensions have yet to be integrated into a unified framework for assessing climate risks to the leopard cat (P. bengalensis) at a national scale across China. To address this gap, we here synthesize these approaches to comprehensively evaluate climate-driven risks facing this species.

The leopard cat represents a species of high ecological and conservation priority across China. To this end, we assembled multi-source occurrence records and curated a suite of environmental predictors spanning climate, topography, vegetation, and anthropogenic pressures. Our analytical framework comprised three sequential stages (Figure 1): (1) ensemble SDM modeling to project current and future habitat suitability and range shifts via the Biomod2 platform; (2) quantification of niche dynamics between climate periods using overlap metrics and hypothesis-driven tests; and (3) delineation of regions exhibiting high climatic novelty and identification of key distribution drivers through MESS and MoD analyses. Synthesizing these approaches, we characterized range shifts, centroid trajectories, and niche evolution for the leopard cat under climate change. This integrated framework, therefore, underpins core habitat delineation, informs protected area expansion, and guides climate-adaptive management strategies for this mesopredator.

Evaluation of model accuracy

All six candidate models (RF, GBM, MaxNet, MARS, XGBoost, and CTA) met the performance threshold (TSS >0.70) (Figure 2; Table S1) and were therefore selected for ensemble construction. Their mean TSS values ranged from 0.718 to 0.985, with corresponding ROC values all exceeding 0.92. The resulting ensemble models (EMmean, EMmedian, EMca, and EMwmean) all demonstrated high performance (TSS: 0.77–0.79; ROC: 0.956–0.957) (Table S2). Among them, EMca achieved the optimal balance of high TSS (0.79) and ROC (0.96), and was therefore selected for all subsequent visualization and analysis.

In the EMca model used for final analysis, temperature seasonality (bio4, contribution: 0.108) and slope (0.093) were the most important drivers, followed by mean diurnal temperature range (bio2, 0.061) and precipitation in the warmest quarter (bio18, 0.042) (Figure 3; Table S3). Although the specific rankings varied among different ensemble methods (e.g., population density showed higher importance in the EMcv model), the core pattern was consistent: Climatic variables (bio4, bio2) and topography (slope) were consistently the dominant factors. Current and potential future geographic distribution

The suitable habitat area for P. bengalensis in China under the current climate conditions encompasses approximately 25.35% of the country’s total land area (Table 1). Within these areas, the highly suitable habitats (16.17%) are concentrated in Southern China (Figure 4). Their core contiguous area is bounded to the north by the Qinling Mountains-Huai River line that stretches from Yunnan to the southeastern coast (Figure 5). It also encompasses regions that include Taiwan and portions of South, Central, and Southwest China. Additionally, scattered patches are present in subtropical zones such as southern Zhejiang, western Anhui, central Sichuan, and southern Shaanxi. Moderately suitable habitats (2.64%) primarily form a transitional zone that surrounds the highly suitable areas. Low-suitability habitats (6.54%) are sporadically distributed in the northern and northwestern regions, including Heilongjiang, Henan, Qinghai, and Xinjiang.

Future climate projections indicated a net increase in the total suitable habitat for the leopard cat. However, there existed distinct patterns between the SSP126 and SSP585 pathways (Figure 6; Table 2). The total area increased more sharply under SSP585, peaking in the 2070s (+6.8% vs. current), then declined. Under SSP126, the growth was more gradual and sustained, reaching a maximum in the 2090s (+3.8%). By the 2090s, high-suitability habitats showed a net increase under SSP126 (+6.7%) but a net decrease under SSP585 (−4.0%). Conversely, low-suitability habitats expanded markedly under SSP585 (proportion +1.1 percentage points), while decreasing under SSP126. Moderately suitable habitats remained stable (approximately 2.6%–3.1%). The projected expansions, particularly under novel future climates, were evaluated for their robustness to algorithmic uncertainty. Spatial uncertainty, quantified using the coefficient of variation (EMcv) and 90% confidence interval width (EMci), remained at moderate levels across all scenarios, with no widespread areas of extreme disagreement (see Figures S1–S7 for spatial patterns). Crucially, the core finding of a net habitat gain was robust, as uncertainty-weighted ensemble strategies (EMcv/EMci) yielded more conservative yet consistent estimates in potential expansion zones. This supports the overall reliability of the projected distributional changes.

Habitat expansion exceeded contraction under all scenarios, except for the SSP126 scenario in the 2050s. This result indicated a net gain in suitable area. Expansion zones occurred primarily along the periphery of the stable core, such as the border between East and Central China. Examination of current land cover indicates that these expansion zones are situated within agriculturally dominated and peri-urban landscapes, where habitat fragmentation and anthropogenic barriers (e.g., cropland matrix and transportation networks) are prevalent. This spatial context suggests that functional connectivity between expansion zones and the existing stable core may be limited, potentially constraining natural dispersal processes despite climatic suitability. Expansion was more pronounced under the high-emission scenario (SSP585). It peaked in the 2070s (26.6 km2) and extended northward into the northern North China Plain, reflecting a clear northward shift. Expansion under SSP585 consistently surpassed that under SSP126 during the same periods. This underscored a stronger climate-driven expansion effect under higher emissions. By the 2090s, expansion had further reached portions of southern Northeast China, suggesting a progressive northward trend over time. However, these northerly expansion areas are separated from the current core range by extensive agricultural belts and urbanized corridors, raising questions about their accessibility under natural dispersal scenarios.

In contrast, contraction zones were small in area and showed a fluctuating decline. This result implied a low climate-driven compression risk for the existing habitat. Contraction occurred only sporadically in localized patches along core-area margins (e.g., portions of East and Central China) and did not form large, contiguous areas of loss. These areas were spatially dispersed and consistently low in proportional coverage, indicating that climate-induced habitat reduction was confined to localized marginal zones.

The centroids migration of suitable areas of P. bengalensis

The current centroid of suitable habitats for P. bengalensis is located in northern Guizhou, close to the border with Chongqing (107.764816°E, 28.839697°N) (Figure 8). Under future climate scenarios, the centroid experiences comparatively smaller shifts between the border area of northern Guizhou and southern Chongqing. Overall, the centroid shows a consistent northward shift across all three future periods (2050s, 2070s, and 2090s). Under the SSP126 scenario, the centroid shifted to the northwest (107.6283°E, 28.9762°N) during the 2050s, then eastward (107.9013°E, 28.9762°N) during the 2070s, and remained at this eastern position through the 2090s. Under the SSP585 scenario, the centroid moved to the northeast (107.9013°E, 29.1127°N) in the 2050s, then to the northwest (107.7648°E, 29.2492°N) in the 2070s, and finally eastward (107.9013°E, 29.2492°N) in the 2090s. Overall, the centroid exhibits a stable northward shift in latitude under both scenarios, while its longitudinal movement oscillates without a consistent directional trend.

Ecological niche analysis

The leopard cat ecological niche under future climate conditions was characterized using high overlap with the current niche, exceptional stability, and minimal expansion (Table 4; Figure 9). The key findings were as follows. (1) High niche overlap: The niche similarity between the current and future distributions was measured using Schoener’s D. They remained high across all scenarios (range: 0.888–0.909). However, overlap by the 2090s was slightly lower under the high-emissions pathway (SSP585) than under the low-emissions scenario (SSP126). This result suggested a minor niche shift under stronger climate forcing. (2) Stability-dominated niche dynamics: The stable component of the niche was overwhelmingly dominant (mean = 99.51%), while expansion and unfilling were negligible (means = 0.0043 and 0.00013, respectively). The mean stability was higher under SSP126 (99.73%) than under SSP585 (99.27%). Over time, the stability increased slightly under SSP126 but declined gradually under SSP585. This included a small but consistent increase in expansion. (3) Consistent niche space structure: A principal component analysis (PCA) indicated that the cumulative variance explained by the first two axes, which define the niche space, remained stable at approximately 55.02% across all periods and scenarios. This reflected a consistent structure of the environmental space represented by the core variables used in the modeling.

Discussion

Model prediction reliability

Individual models exhibit variations in prediction processes and parameterization algorithms, which may introduce uncertainties in their projection outcomes.42 To ensure the robustness and accuracy of predictions, we integrated optimal individual models into the ensemble frameworks to mitigate typical errors associated with single-model approaches. Furthermore, we developed predictive models for the leopard cat's potential distribution using six ensemble integration methods. Among these methods, EMca (TSS >0.792) was selected for the final predictions due to its outstanding performance. The model accurately identified the potentially suitable habitats and demonstrated a spatial pattern closely aligned with both known occurrence records and the updated International Union for the Conservation of Nature distribution range by Ghimirey et al.9These results indicated that the model outputs were accurate and reliable.

Multilevel environmental drivers of the leopard cat distribution

The key environmental drivers of the leopard cat distribution, according to the ensemble modeling analysis, were temperature seasonality (bio4), slope, mean diurnal range (bio2), and precipitation of the warmest quarter (bio18). These factors shape the distribution by meeting the species’ core ecological needs (Table S3). The leopard cat’s preference for warm, humid conditions13,27 was reflected by the importance of hydrothermal variables (bio18, bio4) that support forest-shrub habitats and provide shelter and sustain core prey populations such as pikas and rodents.31,43,44,45 The selection of gentle, north-facing slopes and low-elevation terrain13,43 facilitates movement,46 and may increase access to prey in areas adjacent to human activity.47,48 The species’ use of human-modified habitats (e.g., farmland and settlements)13,49,50 is primarily driven by tracking rodent prey,47,51 with documented population success in landscapes such as oil palm plantations.52 Habitat selection showed functional differentiation. Open areas (e.g., plantations with moderate canopy cover) were favored for foraging due to better visibility and mobility,48 while denser forests and thickets provide essential refuge and potential breeding sites13,43,48

The quantified ranking of the factor contributions was as follows: hydrothermal > topography > anthropogenic > habitat type. This result agrees with an ecological logic that spans climate adaptation, terrain use, and prey tracking. The resulting behavioral plasticity presents dual implications for conservation. While it may enhance resilience by allowing populations to persist in human-modified landscapes, it also increases the frequency of encounters that would elevate potential human-wildlife conflict. Future behavioral and habitat-use responses under novel climates require validation through long-term monitoring. Consequently, conservation planning should prioritize the maintenance of the core prey base and the protection of forest refuges. In addition, it should incorporate proactive measures to manage human-wildlife interfaces and mitigate conflict risks.

Response of the leopard cat spatial distribution pattern to climate change

The current suitable habitat of the leopard cat is predominantly distributed across the hilly regions of southeastern China and the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau. This distribution pattern is closely associated with the warm, humid climate and extensive forest cover in these areas, which provide essential water sources, shelter, and abundant prey. Notably, the leopard cat’s range largely aligns with the Heihe-Tengchong Line, a key demographic divide in China. The region east of this line consists of the Guangdong, Guangxi, Yunnan, Sichuan, Fujian, and Zhejiang Provinces, and is characterized by a high human population density, making it a hotspot of conflict between humans and leopard cats.53 In this context, “hotspot” refers to an area with significant spatial clustering of wildlife-caused damage that typically coincides with human settlements or protected area boundaries.54 Nevertheless, these human-dominated landscapes also offer potential opportunities for leopard cat persistence. The species exhibits considerable adaptability to anthropogenic environments. For example, in portions of Sichuan where traditional cultural practices prevail, leopard cats do not exhibit a “landscape of fear.”30 Human activities may even facilitate leopard cat survival through the “human shield” effect—a reduction of pressure from large predators—as well as by providing refugia and supplementary food resources.55,56 These findings suggest that community-based conservation strategies should facilitate the coexistence of humans and leopard cats in conflict-prone regions through targeted interventions.

Our analysis revealed a spatial pattern of a “stable core, significant northward expansion, and localized contraction” in the leopard cat’s response to future climate change. These were accompanied by a northward centroid shift. This northward expansion into newly suitable areas agreed with projections for other generalist felids (e.g., jaguars, leopards, and tigers).57 The trend likely originates from the species’ broad ecological tolerance and demonstrated capacity to exploit resources in human-modified landscapes. These are traits that are shared with other adaptable mammals such as the large Indian civet, masked palm civet, Chinese serow, and wild boar.31 As a habitat-generalist with considerable ecological plasticity,58 the leopard cat may benefit from future shrub-dominated habitats promoted by dual climate and anthropogenic pressures.20 These habitats could provide niches consistent with its known adaptability.13 However, as noted in the Results, these climatically suitable expansion zones are situated within fragmented, human-dominated landscapes, raising questions about their functional connectivity with current core habitats.

However, despite this adaptive potential, landscape resistance created by agricultural and urban lands that fragment expansion zones from core habitats may still impede dispersal. Therefore, our findings indicated that conservation strategies should leverage these spatial patterns and focus on the following targeted, community-integrated interventions: (1) designing short-distance ecological corridors along key low-resistance pathways to connect core habitats with expansion areas. This approach has been successfully applied in leopard cat conservation, exemplified by the 1.09-km corridor planned in Pyeongchang, South Korea.59 Similar strategies have also been implemented for Asian elephants in Jiangcheng County, Southwestern China, where 92 ecological corridors (including three key corridors) were identified to enhance habitat connectivity and facilitate population dispersal.60 (2) constructing wildlife-crossing structures at identified high-resistance points (e.g., road corridors) to mitigate habitat fragmentation, an approach supported by research61; and (3) actively conserving key habitats such as shrub-dominated summer slopes,13 while implementing preemptive conflict mitigation in potential “conflict hotspots” where model-predicted expansion zones overlap with human activity areas.62,63,64 For practical implementation, recent work in the Giant Panda National Park, China, has established a multi-species and multi-scale conservation framework, including the design of 39 vegetated overpasses, 34 underpasses, and 27 canopy bridges for arboreal species, providing a theoretical basis and practical strategies for integrated ecological and transportation planning in national parks.65

The leopard cat exhibits niche conservatism coupled with geographic range flexibility

The niche dynamics and habitat responses of the leopard cat under future climate scenarios presented a complex and multidimensional pattern. Some analytical outcomes may have initially appeared divergent, but the integration of multiple methodological approaches helped to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and potential risks. Within our analytical framework, the niche dynamics analyses revealed highly conserved niche characteristics, with high overlap between the current and future niches (Schoener’s D = 0.888–0.909) and limited niche expansion. However, these estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they may be contingent on variable selection and the ordination technique employed.66 In contrast, spatially explicit habitat suitability models projected a net geographic expansion of suitable areas that occurred primarily at the stable suitable zone margins. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by the non-linear mapping from niche space to geographic space. The relative stability of the core climatic drivers likely would maintain the continuity of core habitats (stable area: 228.98–235.89 × 104 km2). In addition, climate-driven reorganization of environmental variables may create secondary, sub-optimal habitat patches beyond the current niche margins that remain accessible (expansion area: 11.31–26.62 × 104 km2). For example, fragmented shrub-forest landscapes that emerge under combined climatic and anthropogenic pressures20 could serve as alternative habitats.67 It should be noted that these novel habitats may differ from contiguous core forests in terms of landscape connectivity and resource availability68; hence, their long-term ecological carrying capacity and conservation value require further evaluation.

A multidimensional climate risk assessment integrates niche stability with climatic novelty

High niche stability and areas of climatic novelty (identified by negative MESS values) are not mutually exclusive; rather, they represent two distinct yet complementary dimensions in climate change risk assessments. Niche stability reflects the relative conservation of a species’ niche across multidimensional environmental gradients. Analyses such as Schoener’s D in the PCA environmental space quantify niche overlap between current and future (or invaded) environmental spaces.69 If the relative structure of a species’ niche remains consistent across environmental gradients, meaning its position within the overall environmental background shifts only slightly, high niche overlap can persist despite changes in absolute climatic values.70 Thus, the high stability observed for the leopard cat suggests a broad fundamental niche that may accommodate future shifts in climatic relationships. Nevertheless, as noted above, the methodological dependence of this result warrants its interpretation alongside complementary lines of evidence.66

Climatic novelty, assessed via the MESS, measures how much future climate conditions deviate from the historical ranges experienced by a species. MESS analysis identifies regions where model predictions rely on extrapolation by comparing the projected climate variables to the training data range (typically from the current distributions).71 Negative MESS values indicate climatic conditions outside the historical range, i.e., novel climates.72 Our analysis showed that much of the leopard cat’s current range will face novel climate combinations. Crucially, niche conservatism in the relative environmental space does not guarantee physiological or ecological plasticity to cope with absolute climatic novelty.

In summary, high niche stability may indicate potential adaptability, but it does not preclude risks from absolute climatic extremes. For example, Alpinia officinarum (galangal) suffered significant habitat loss under SSP585 due to widespread climatic novelty despite stable niche characteristics.73 This underscores that high stability metrics—which, as noted above, may carry methodological caveats—should be interpreted alongside novelty analyses, as they may be influenced by methodological smoothing and sensitivity to model parameters and data quality.74

Uncertainty analysis

SDM projections are inherently conditional and subject to uncertainties that originate from multiple stages of model construction and scenario projections.75 We systematically evaluated and managed these uncertainties using ensemble modeling, bias correction, and spatial validation. First, spatial sampling bias in occurrence records may distort modeled ecological niches.76 To reduce overfitting to well-sampled areas, we used a bias surface derived from the human footprint index (HF) to guide the background point selection. The model performance was assessed using spatial block cross-validation,77 which minimizes the influence of spatial autocorrelation and provides a more unbiased estimate of generalizability.

Second, differences in the algorithm structure contribute substantially to predictive uncertainty.71 We used the Biomod2 platform33 to implement six ensemble strategies. Among them, the coefficient-of-variation-weighted (EMcv) and confidence-interval-weighted (EMci) strategies directly quantified the algorithmic uncertainty by weighting each pixel based on the statistical dispersion of the individual algorithm outputs.34 We found that all ensemble strategies closely agreed within the species’ current core range. In contrast, in future expansion zones, the uncertainty-weighted strategies (EMcv/EMci) yielded more conservative suitability estimates (Figures S1–S7). This result demonstrates our approach’s capacity to identify and down-weight high-uncertainty areas, thereby strengthening the overall reliability of projections.

Thirdly, climate-scenario choice is a major source of uncertainty in future projections. The multi-scenario analysis revealed a consistent net increase in habitat area across emission pathways, but the magnitude and spatial pattern of expansion varied markedly. For instance, expansion was greater under the high-emission SSP585 scenario than under SSP126, with a more pronounced northward shift. This result highlighted the sensitivity of long-term projections to mitigation policies.78

Finally, the setting of thresholds also constitutes a source of uncertainty. The final suitability threshold of 0.20 was determined using the bm_FindOptimStat function in biomod2, informed by methodological literature41 and the Natural Breaks method. Although our primary conclusions were robust to threshold choice (assessed via TSS maximization),73 the selection itself constitutes a recognized source of uncertainty, as alternative rules can significantly alter estimated distribution extents.79 Therefore, the resulting binary maps should be interpreted as visualizations of a continuous probabilistic trend under a specific threshold rule, rather than as definitive spatial outcomes.

Limitations and future research directions

Our modeling framework has two primary limitations. First, it simplifies key ecological processes by omitting interspecific interactions (e.g., prey availability) and assuming unlimited dispersal.80,81 This could potentially lead to over-optimistic estimates of the colonization potential for new suitable habitats beyond the current range (e.g., predicted expansion frontiers). Second, the scale and resolution of the environmental variables have constraints. The absence of higher-resolution proxies for human activity (e.g., night-time lights) may affect the precision of the distribution boundaries in fragmented landscapes, while the omission of local-scale factors (e.g., prey resources) could limit mechanistic insight into fine-scale habitat selection. Third, while our model includes both climate and LULC variables as predictors within the Biomod2 ensemble, it does not specifically capture potential interactive effects between these two drivers. In reality, land use and climate change may operate synergistically or antagonistically—for example, climate-driven suitability gains could be offset by simultaneous land conversion, or vice versa. Our framework may therefore underestimate uncertainty in regions where such synergies or antagonisms are pronounced.

To improve the ecological realism of SDMs, future studies should focus on the following: 1) Multi-source data and process-explicit models should be integrated. For example, frameworks should be developed to represent biotic factors, and the coupling of habitat connectivity models should occur to separate “climatically suitableˮ from “demographically accessibleˮ habitats. 2) Multi-scale validation and mechanistic research that includes ground-truthing in areas such as predicted expansion frontiers should be implemented. This will quantify the contribution of local ecological drivers, helping to calibrate models across scales and reveal underlying ecological mechanisms. 3)Future research should explicitly incorporate interaction terms between climate and land use variables, or adopt scenario-based sensitivity analyses, to better disentangle the coupled effects of these two major drivers and provide more robust habitat forecasts.

Method details

Overall workflow

This study employed an integrated species distribution modeling (SDM) and niche dynamics analysis framework to predict the potential geographical distribution of the leopard cat under current and future climate scenarios. The overall workflow (Figure 1) comprises four core stages: (1) Data Preparation and Processing: collection and filtering of species occurrence records and environmental variables; (2) Ensemble Species Distribution Modeling: execution and integration of multiple algorithms based on the selected variables to generate habitat suitability maps, followed by classification of suitability levels and analysis of spatiotemporal changes in the distribution area; (3) Habitat Classification and Niche Dynamics Analysis: quantification of niche stability, expansion, and unfilling using PCA-based ordination in environmental space; (4) Assessment of Future Climate Anomalies (MESS/MoD Analysis): evaluation of areas with novel future climates and identification of dominant environmental drivers using Multivariate Environmental Similarity Surface (MESS) and Most Dissimilar Variable (MoD) analyses. Each of these stages is described in detail in the following sections.

Species occurrence data collection and filtering

Occurrence records of the leopard cat (P. bengalensis) in China were obtained from the following sources: the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF; http://www.gbif.org/, 115 records), iNaturalist (https://www.inaturalist.org/, 141 records), China BON (https://geobon.org/bons/national-regional-bon/national-bon/china-bon/, 50 records), infrared camera surveys (136 records), and relevant literature (20 records). To improve model reliability, the following three-step filtering protocol was applied to the species occurrence data: (1) duplicate record removals; (2) exclusion of records from regions without established stable populations (e.g., marine areas); and (3) spatial validation in ArcGIS 10.8 that involved visual coordinate verification and the manual removal of spatially anomalous points. Furthermore, to match the resolution of environmental variables, the data were filtered using Ecological Niche Modeling tools to ensure only one record was retained per 2.5 arc-minute grid cells.82 This procedure also effectively reduced the spatial autocorrelation effects. Without this step, spatial autocorrelation would have resulted in clustered occurrence points, thereby introducing bias into the results.83 Finally, based on the filtered occurrence data (Figure 5), we predicted the potential geographical distribution of leopard cats in China. This dataset established a robust foundation for subsequent predictive modeling to ensure a reliable analysis of the relationship between the species distribution and environmental variables. The map of China is sourced from the Standard Map Service System of the Ministry of Natural Resources of the People’s Republic of China (http://bzdt.ch.mnr.gov.cn/), with the map review number GS(2024)0650.

Environmental variable selection and processing

According to Zanin et al.,57 habitat selection of the leopard cat is jointly influenced by climatic factors (preference for warm and humid environments) and habitat features (such as proximity to forests, shrublands, and gentle slopes). This species exhibits high sensitivity to climate change,13 which partly explains its significant distributional overlap with human settlements.43 Additionally, gentle terrains are preferred due to the facilitation of hunting activities.13 Based on these considerations, a total of 26 environmental variables were selected and categorized into four groups: (1) 19 bioclimatic variables; (2) 3 topographic variables (elevation, slope, and aspect); (3) 3 anthropogenic disturbance variables (population density, human influence index, human footprint); and (4) 4 habitat characteristic variables (land use and cover change, normalized difference vegetation index, road and settlements). The detailed data sources are as follows:

The bioclimatic variables were obtained from the WorldClim database (v2.1, http://www.worldclim.org). These included data for the current climate (1970–2000) and three future periods (2041–2060 and 2081–2100) at spatial resolutions of 2.5 arc minutes. Future climate data were based on three shared socioeconomic pathways (SSP126 and SSP585) under the CMIP6 framework. To reduce uncertain-ties associated with individual climate models,84 an ensemble average was calculated from the three general circulation models (ACCESS-CM2, BCC-CSM2-MR, and CCC-ESM2). Topographic data that included the digital elevation model (DEM) were sourced from the Geospatial Data Cloud platform (https://www.gscloud.cn). Slopes and aspects were derived from the DEM. The population density (PD) and human influence index (HII) were obtained from the Socioeconomic Data and Applications Center (http://sedac.ciesin.columbia.edu). Land use and cover change (LUCC) and the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) were acquired from the Resource and Environmental Science and Data Center of the Institute of Geographic Sciences and Natural Resources Research, the Chinese Academy of Sciences (https://www.resdc.cn/). Human Footprint (HF) was obtained from the annual terrestrial human footprint dataset.85 Land use and land cover (LULC) data for historical and future periods (including the 2050s, 2070s, and 2090s) were obtained from a global future LULC projection dataset.86 These data incorporate climate and socioeconomic drivers under shared socioeconomic pathways. The distances to roads and to settlements were derived using a ArcGIS analysis based on fundamental geographic datasets of the road network and settlement locations.They were then processed to align with the spatial resolution and extent of our study.

To mitigate the risk of model overfitting caused by multicollinearity among bio-climatic variables,17,87 a Spearman correlation analysis combined with a variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess the inter-variable correlations and significance.88 Variables with an absolute correlation coefficient <0.8 and VIF <5 were retained.89 The VIF evaluation criteria were defined as follows: 0 ≤ VIF ≤10 indicated no multicollinearity, 10 ≤ VIF ≤100 indicated moderate multicollinearity, and a VIF >100 indicated severe multicollinearity.90 Through this process, 14 environmental variables were ultimately selected for subsequent modeling (Table 2) to ensure the robustness and explanatory power of the ecological niche models.

Construction and evaluation of ensemble species distribution models

We used the Biomod2 platform to integrate the following 12 modeling algorithms: Generalized Linear Model (GLM), Generalized Additive Model (GAM), Generalized Boosting Model (GBM), Classification Tree Analysis (CTA), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Flexible Discriminant Analysis (FDA), Surface Range Envelope (SRE), Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest (RF), Maximum Entropy (MAXENT), and MAXNET (Maximum Entropy Model with LASSO regularization).41 These algorithms were selected as they cover a broad spectrum of methodological approaches (statistical, machine-learning, and semi-parametric) and are widely used and validated in the species distribution modeling literature.This step ensured robust ensemble projections and comparability with prior studies.33,42

To correct for spatial sampling bias inherent in occurrence records, we employed a bias-weighted random sampling strategy to generate pseudo-absence points.91 A sampling bias surface was first derived from the Human Footprint Index (HFI), serving as a proxy for spatial variation in sampling effort. Background points were then drawn with selection probabilities inversely proportional to this bias surface, thereby down-weighting over-sampled regions and ensuring that pseudo-absences reflect the available environmental background rather than sampling intensity. Following established guidelines for ensemble models combining regression and machine-learning algorithms,91 we generated 1000 pseudo-absence points—approximately 3.1 times our 325 filtered presence points—for each of two independent replicates. This ratio falls within the recommended 2– to 5-fold range for ensemble approaches and closely aligns with the biomod2 team’s empirical guideline of three times the presence count.

To rigorously evaluate the model’s spatial transferability, a spatial block cross-validation scheme was implemented using the blockCV R package.92,93 The spatial autocorrelation range of the environmental variables was first estimated using the blockCV::cv_spatial_autocor function. Based on the calculated range, the study area was partitioned into a grid with cell sizes exceeding this autocorrelation distance. This design resulted in a 10-row × 10-column grid across China (approximately 450 km × 450 km per block). This design ensures that each grid cell is sufficiently large to minimize spatial dependency within the data.94 The 5-fold configuration follows established recommendations in the spatial cross-validation literature, balancing robust performance estimation with computational efficiency.95 The grid blocks were then randomly and evenly assigned to 5 spatial folds through 50 iterations, creating spatially independent training and testing sets. This approach mitigates model performance overestimation due to spatial autocorrelation—a well-documented inflation effect in predictive modeling96—thereby providing a more unbiased assessment of predictive accuracy in unsampled areas.92 As noted by,95 even optimal block size selection cannot completely eliminate performance estimation bias; however, blocks exceeding the spatial autocorrelation range—as implemented here—maximize the approximation of true generalization error.

Modeling was conducted using the biomod2 platform. To strictly adhere to the spatial validation design, the spatial fold indices generated above were supplied as the DataSplitTable argument within the BIOMOD_Modeling function. This entire process was independently repeated five times, and model performance was evaluated on the spatial test sets using the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and the True Skill Statistic (TSS).97 TSS evaluates classification models, with values closer to 1 indicating a higher predictive accuracy.98 The AUC ranges from 0 to 1, and it is a standard performance measure, where 0.9–1.0 denotes an excellent predictive ability.99

TSS was selected for the model evaluation and weighting due to its insensitivity to class imbalances and clear ecological interpretability.98 A threshold (TSS >0.70) was applied to integrate only the top-performing models into the ensemble. This was based on a consensus for ‘good’ to ‘excellent' performance.98 Variable contributions were assessed using the Biomod2 package. Projections were synthesized using six ensemble strategies: simple average (EMmean), median (EMmedian), consensus threshold average (EMca), TSS-weighted mean (EMwmean), and variation-weighted strategies (EMcv, EMci). The potential distribution of the leopard cat under climate change was forecasted using these ensembles. The final ensemble model for presentation (EMca) was selected based on its optimal comprehensive performance that was characterized by the highest TSS score coupled with a high AUC value on the independent validation set. The spatial uncertainty associated with the ensemble projections was quantified using two metrics provided by the Biomod2 platform: the coefficient of variation among individual model outputs (EMcv, representing relative inter-algorithm dispersion) and the width of the 90% confidence interval (EMci, representing the absolute range of predicted suitability values).

Ensemble projections are, however, subject to three principal uncertainty sources: algorithm uncertainty, climate model uncertainty (variability across GCMs), and scenario uncertainty (divergence between SSPs).100 Accumulating evidence indicates that algorithm choice frequently constitutes the dominant source of uncertainty in ensemble projections, often exceeding the variability introduced by climate models or emission scenarios.101,102 Although a formal partitioning of these uncertainty components lies beyond the scope of this study, we acknowledge that our reported metrics predominantly capture algorithm-induced variability, and that additional uncertainties arising from climate models and scenarios warrant consideration when interpreting future projections.

Threshold definition and habitat classification

The optimal presence or absence threshold was set at 0.20 as determined using the bm_FindOptimStat function in Biomod2 with reference to the methodological literature41 and the natural breaks method.To evaluate the sensitivity of our results to threshold selection, we employed thresholds that maximize the True Skill Statistic (TSS), following established methods for converting continuous habitat suitability into binary predictions.79 Our main conclusions remained unchanged across the tested thresholds. Continuous suitability scores (0−1000) were classified into low (200−400), medium (400−600), and high (>600) suitability tiers based on natural breaks in the data distribution that were calibrated to round values for clarity.41,103 The spatiotemporal habitat dynamics that included stable, expansion, and contraction areas, and the centroid position for each period were calculated using the SDM Toolbox in ArcGIS.104 The centroid displacement between periods was computed to map shifts and visualize trajectory changes.

Niche dynamics analysis

In this study, we adopted the statistical framework proposed by Broennimann et al.69 to quantify the niche dynamics of the leopard cat under climate change using PCA ordination. The species distribution data were first projected onto a two-dimensional environmental space defined by the minimum and maximum values of the first two PCA axes. This space was then divided into a 100 × 100 grid. Within the R package ecospat, we applied the ecospat.grid.clim.dyn() function, which utilizes a Gaussian (normal) kernel for the density estimation. The bandwidth for kernel smoothing was automatically selected using the package’s default reference method. This process estimates the smoothed occurrence density of the species in the grid cells, thereby mitigating the effect of sampling bias.

Niche overlap between the current and future projected distributions was quantified using Schoener’s D index, which was computed with the ecospat.niche.overlap() function in ecospat. This index ranges from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (complete overlap).105 Based on the gridded environmental variables and smoothed species occurrence density, three niche dynamic indices were further calculated: (1) the niche stability that represents the environmental conditions of both current and future periods; (2) niche expansion that refers to environments available during both periods but are only occupied in the future; and (3) niche unfilling that indicates environments that are currently occupied and still available in the future but not utilized. Niche expansion serves as a key indicator of actual niche shifts.32

Niche equivalency and similarity tests were performed following the method proposed by Warren et al.106 The niche equivalency test evaluates whether the niches of a population under current and future climate conditions are statistically equivalent. This was achieved by performing 1000 random reallocations of the species occurrence records between the two periods. Rejection of the null hypothesis indicated a significant niche difference. The niche similarity test assessed whether the niches of the two entities being compared were more similar (or different) than expected by chance. Rejection of the null hypothesis suggested that the niche similarity exceeded random expectation. A significance level of 0.05 was adopted for all tests. All calculations of the niche overlap, dynamic indices, and statistical tests were performed in R version 4.3.1, primarily using the ade4 and ecospat packages.70,107

Assessment of future climate anomalies

Environmental variables from the current potential distribution area of the leopard cat was used as a reference layer. A MESS analysis was employed to evaluate the extent of future climatic anomalies compared to the current period under future climate scenarios. The MoD method was used to identify key environmental drivers responsible for changes in the potential geographical distribution. A positive MESS value indicates the degree of climatic deviation, with smaller values reflecting greater variation and a value of 100 indicating no variation. A negative MESS value signifies that at least one environmental variable at the location exceeds the reference range, indicating a climatic anomaly.71 The variable with the lowest similarity in the MESS results was extracted as the MoD and visualized spatially using ArcGIS 10.8.

The MESS calculation was performed by comparing environmental variable values at target points with the reference layer range following the methodology of Elith et al. and Clarke.71,108 Similarity scores were computed based on the percentile (fi) of each variable value within the reference distribution to represent a continuous gradient from the lower to the upper limit of environmental suitability. A post-processing MESS and associated MoD analysis was conducted to assess the reliability of projections under novel climates. This was performed using the density.tools.Novel utility in MaxEnt that was applied to the same set of environmental variables used for the model calibration. This step did not modify the default parameters or internal structure of the previously trained models.109 This incorporation of MESS and MoD analysis results into the distribution prediction process allowed for more precise identification and assessment of the impact of climatic anomalies on the reliability of the projected geographical distribution of the leopard cat.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and model evaluation

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.0 with the primary packages of Biomod2 version 4.3.1. The spatial analyses was conducted using ArcGIS 10.8.1. The study utilized 325 spatially filtered occurrence records across P. bengalensis, with each modeling run incorporating 1000 pseudo-absence points. Sample sizes were determined by the available occurrence data following the standard practice for species distribution modeling.

The statistical tests and procedures included the following: (1) variable selection using Spearman correlation and VIF analysis; (2) niche overlap quantification using Schoener’s D index; and (3) niche equivalency and similarity tests (based on 1000 randomization iterations) to statistically compare niches. The model performance was assessed based on AUC and TSS and reported as mean values across model runs. The final ensemble model was constructed as the weighted mean of top-performing models (TSS >0.7). To identify the key environmental drivers of distributional shifts under climate change, a MESS analysis was performed that used the current potential distribution of the leopard cat as the reference. Positive MESS values indicate climatic deviation (smaller values = greater change), while negative values signal a climatic anomaly where at least one variable exceeds the reference range. Detailed results of these analyses, including the MESS values, MoD, niche overlap indices (D), p-values from hypothesis tests, and model evaluation metrics, are provided in the results section and corresponding figure legends. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05, all tests were two-tailed, and the exact p-values are reported throughout the manuscript to facilitate accurate interpretations.
