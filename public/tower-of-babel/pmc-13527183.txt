WTAP‐mediated m6A RNA methylation promotes diabetic foot ulcer healing under negative pressure wound therapy
Authors: Yutong Li, Wenhua Chen, Jianhao Lu, Xiaoyue Lin, Ying Tang, Jiaxu Li, Mingwei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo investigate the role of RNA N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) methyltransferase Wilms tumor 1‐associated protein (WTAP) in negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) for diabetic foot ulcers (DFU).Materials and MethodsWe enrolled 46 DFU patients and 16 non‐diabetic chronic ulcer patients treated with NPWT. Total m6A levels and the expression of methyltransferases (METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP) in wound margin tissues were measured before and after NPWT. High glucose‐induced HaCaT and HDF cell models, alongside an in vitro negative pressure system, were used to evaluate the effect of negative pressure on WTAP expression and the impact of WTAP alteration on cellular functions.ResultsBefore NPWT, METTL3, METTL14, WTAP and m6A levels were significantly lower in DFU patients than in the non‐diabetic chronic ulcer patients. After one week of NPWT, these parameters increased significantly in both groups, and a positive correlation was observed between WTAP and m6A levels. Notably, the increase in WTAP expression exhibited a significant positive correlation with the 4‐week healing outcome in DFU patients. In vitro, negative pressure significantly reversed the high glucose‐induced WTAP downregulation in both HaCaT and HDF cells, while WTAP knockdown profoundly impaired their proliferation and migration.ConclusionsNPWT is associated with improved DFU healing and the restoration of WTAP‐m6A levels, and its effect may be related to negative pressure promoting WTAP expression to enhance wound cell proliferation and migration.Keywords: diabetic foot ulcers, m6A methylation, Wilms tumor 1‐associated protein

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) has become one of the most critical chronic diseases affecting global public health. The global number of adults with diabetes reached 589 million in 2024 and is expected to rise to 853 million by 2050 1 . As a serious complication of DM, diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is characterized by chronic non‐healing wounds of the lower extremities 2 . It brings a heavy burden to patients, with a lifetime incidence ranging from 19% to 34% 3 . Furthermore, DFU shows high recurrence rates, occurring in approximately 40% of cases within one year 3 . Clinical outcomes are often poor, and the mortality rate exceeds 70% within five years after amputation 3 . The pathogenesis of DFU is closely associated with diabetic peripheral neuropathy and peripheral arterial disease 4 , 5 . Normally, wound repair requires orderly and coordinated stages. However, diabetic wounds fail to progress through these phases in a timely manner. Persistent hyperglycemia promotes chronic inflammation, impairs angiogenesis, delays re‐epithelialization, and disrupts extracellular matrix remodeling. These factors ultimately trap the wound in a chronic state and lead to prolonged non‐healing 6 . Currently, the clinical management of DFU includes glycemic control, vascular reconstruction, debridement, negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT), and infection control 7 . In addition, emerging therapies like biological agents and molecular‐targeted drugs have expanded treatment options 8 , 9 , 10 . Nevertheless, despite continuous advances in these therapeutic strategies, accelerating ulcer healing and preventing high recurrence rates remain major clinical challenges.

NPWT is a widely used treatment for acute and chronic wounds, delivering localized negative pressure through specialized devices. By draining exudate, reducing periwound edema, improving local blood flow, and clearing bacteria, NPWT creates a favorable environment for wound repair 11 . Clinically, it significantly shortens the healing time of DFU, reduces ulcer size, and decreases amputation rates 12 , 13 . At the cellular and molecular levels, NPWT stimulates the proliferation and migration of keratinocytes and fibroblasts. Furthermore, it promotes growth factor expression, suppresses excessive pro‐inflammatory mediators, and modulates matrix metalloproteinase activity 14 . Together, these effects enhance angiogenesis, reduce inflammation, and accelerate tissue remodeling in diabetic wounds 15 . Although multiple clinical guidelines recommend NPWT as a key adjunctive therapy for DFU, research regarding its deep molecular mechanisms during the healing process remains in its early stages.

N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) is a prevalent RNA epigenetic modification. This modification is primarily catalyzed by a methyltransferase (“writer”) complex consisting of METTL3, METTL14, and Wilms tumor 1‐associated protein (WTAP) 16 . Although WTAP lacks catalytic activity, it is indispensable for recruiting the METTL3/14 complex to target specific RNA sequences 17 . m6A modification has been implicated in various pathophysiological processes, such as cancers 18 , atherosclerosis 19 , diabetic microangiopathy 20 , and skin morphogenesis 21 , 22 . A growing body of research suggests that m6A methylation and its associated proteins participate in the pathophysiological process of diabetic wound healing by altering RNA metabolism 23 , 24 , 25 . Specifically, both total m6A levels and WTAP expression are significantly decreased in the wound margin skin tissues of diabetic mice 26 . WTAP has been shown to regulate keratinocyte proliferation, a critical step in wound re‐epithelialization 27 .

Currently, it remains unclear how NPWT alters WTAP expression and whether WTAP influences diabetic wound healing via m6A methylation. This study aims to investigate WTAP expression and m6A levels in the wound margin tissues of DFU and non‐diabetic patients before and after NPWT. Additionally, in vitro experiments utilizing a negative pressure system were conducted to evaluate how negative pressure regulates WTAP expression to influence the cellular functions of keratinocytes and fibroblasts. Ultimately, this study aimed to preliminarily explore the potential association of the WTAP‐m6A pathway with NPWT‐accelerated DFU healing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The overall study design and patient selection process are illustrated in Figure 1.

Flow diagram of the clinical study design and patient selection. This diagram illustrates the detailed specific inclusion, exclusion criteria, group allocation, sample collection, molecular analyses, and follow‐up assessments. A total of 46 patients with DFU and 16 patients with SUC were finally included in the study, all of whom received NPWT for 1 week. Wound margin skin tissues were collected pre‐ and post‐NPWT to evaluate the expression of WTAP, METTL3, METTL14, and total m6A levels.

Study subjects

Between December 2023 and October 2024, this study enrolled 46 DFU patients (DFU group) and 16 non‐diabetic patients with chronic lower‐extremity ulcers [skin ulcer control (SUC) group] treated with NPWT at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. The DFU patients were recruited from the Department of Endocrinology and Metabolism, while the SUC patients were from the Burn Department. Based on the timing of NPWT, patients in each group were further categorized into the pre‐treatment (pre‐DFU and pre‐SUC) and post‐treatment (post‐DFU and post‐SUC) subgroups.

To further validate NPWT‐mediated regulation of WTAP expression, we also retrospectively included 8 DFU patients who received conventional therapy (primarily consisting of wound debridement; CT group) alone between January 2017 and December 2019.

Treatment and clinical sample collection

All patients received standard comprehensive care based on their individual medical history and examination results. The DFU group additionally received standardized glycemic control. Following thorough surgical debridement (removal of necrotic tissue, hemostasis, and irrigation), a customized polyurethane foam dressing was applied and sealed with a semi‐permeable film. Continuous NPWT was then administered using the V.A.C.® negative pressure‐assisted healing therapy system (KCI, USA) at −125 mmHg for one week.

Full‐thickness skin specimens (within 1 cm of the wound edge) were harvested at two time points: immediately before NPWT application and upon its removal one week later. The collected specimens were subsequently used for RNA expression analysis, protein expression analysis, or immunohistochemical staining (IHC). Due to the limited amount of tissue obtained from each wound‐edge specimen, different subsets of specimens were used for downstream analyses. Specimens for Western blot and immunohistochemical staining were randomly selected to minimize potential selection bias, whereas RNA‐based analyses were performed using all specimens with available tissue and qualified RNA samples after quality control assessment. The detailed number of specimens included in each downstream assay is presented in Figure 1. Patients were followed up at 4 weeks to evaluate ulcer healing, defined as complete re‐epithelialization and spontaneous closure. Any decisions regarding amputation were strictly managed by a multidisciplinary diabetic foot team.

General clinical data and laboratory assessments

At admission, baseline clinical data including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), ulcer duration, and smoking/alcohol history were recorded. For the DFU group, diabetes duration and Wagner ulcer grade were additionally assessed. Following an overnight fast, venous blood samples were collected to evaluate routine laboratory parameters, including glycemic and lipid profiles, complete blood count, inflammatory markers, liver and renal function indicators, electrolytes, and coagulation profiles. Additionally, the ankle‐brachial index (ABI) of the affected limb was measured using a Doppler flow detector (Bestman Instrument, Shenzhen, China). The magnitude of change for each parameter after one week of treatment was calculated as the Δvalue (post‐NPWT value minus pre‐NPWT value).

Cell culture

Human immortalized keratinocytes (HaCaT) and human dermal fibroblast (HDF) cells (Pricella, Wuhan, China) were cultured in MEM and DMEM, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Wisent, Nanjing, China) at 37°C in a humidified 5%CO2. Cells were cultured with the medium refreshed every two days and passaged upon reaching 90% confluence. To establish the high‐glucose injury model, HaCaT and HDF cells were divided into the normal glucose (NG) group (5.5 mmol/L D‐glucose) and the high glucose (HG) group, the latter of which was treated with 35 mmol/L D‐glucose for 4 days. The in vitro negative pressure model was established using a customized negative pressure incubator previously independently developed by our research team 28 . A manometer was used to adjust the atmospheric pressure within the incubator, maintaining it at 0 mmHg or −125 mmHg for 24 h. WTAP mRNA and protein expression in HaCaT and HDF cells was detected after the 24 h incubation under normal or negative pressure. All subsequent cell experiments were performed with three biological replicates per group.

Cell transfection

For gene silencing, human WTAP siRNAs and their negative control (NC) were purchased from Geneadv (Suzhou, China) and transfected into HaCaT and HDF cells using Hieff Trans siRNA In Vitro Transfection Reagent (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The specific sequences are listed in Table S1. At 24 h post‐transfection, cells were collected to verify the silencing or overexpression efficiency before being subjected to subsequent functional analyses.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HaCaT and HDF cells using TRIzol reagent (Biosharp, Beijing, China) and reverse‐transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (Yugong Biotech, Lianyungang, China). Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was subsequently performed using a qRT‐PCR kit (Yugong Biotech) according to the manufacturer's instructions. All reactions were analyzed in duplicate, and relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^‐ΔΔCt method, with normalization to an internal control. The specific primer sequences are detailed in Table S2.

Western blot (WB)

Total protein was extracted from frozen wound margin tissues and cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Biosharp, Beijing, China). Protein samples were resolved by 10% SDS‐PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non‐fat dry milk for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Glpbio, Montclair, USA), and band intensities were quantified via densitometry using ImageJ software. The specific antibodies used in this study are detailed in Table S3.

RNA total m6A quantification

Total RNA was extracted from frozen wound margin tissues using TRIzol reagent (Biosharp). Global m6A RNA methylation levels were subsequently quantified using the EpiQuik m6A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit (Epigentek, New York, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. A standard curve was generated via linear regression to determine the slope (OD/ng). For each sample, the absolute mass of m6A (ng) was calculated using the formula: (Sample OD − Negative Control OD) / slope. Finally, the relative percentage of m6A in the total RNA (m6A%) was determined as follows: [total m6A mass (ng) / total RNA input (ng)] × 100%.

IHC staining

Fresh wound margin tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, paraffin‐embedded, and sectioned at a thickness of 4–5 μm. Following routine deparaffinization and rehydration, heat‐induced antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA buffer (pH 8.0; Zsbio, Beijing, China). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% H2O2 (Zsbio). The sections were then incubated with a primary anti‐WTAP antibody (1:100; Affinity, Shanghai, China) for 60 min at 37°C, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (Zsbio), and cell nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. For quantitative analysis, the average optical density (AOD) of WTAP‐positive areas was evaluated across three randomly selected fields per section using ImageJ software.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8; Biosharp, Beijing, China). Briefly, HaCaT and HDF cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well, with ten replicate wells per group. Following 24 h of incubation, the cells were transfected as described above and cultured for an additional 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with 100 μL of basal medium containing 10% (v/v) CCK‐8 reagent, followed by further incubation for 1 h at 37°C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. After subtracting the background optical density (OD) of the blank wells, relative cell viability was calculated as the ratio of the absorbance of the experimental groups to that of the respective control group.

Wound healing assay

HaCaT and HDF cells were seeded into 6‐well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/ml, with each group having three biological replicates. After 24 h of incubation, cells were transfected as previously described and further cultured for another 24 h. When the cell density reached over 90% confluence, a linear wound was created by vertically scratching the cell monolayer with a sterile 200 μL pipette tip guided by a ruler. After washing thrice with PBS to remove debris, cells were incubated in basal medium. Images were captured at 0 h and 24 h using an inverted microscope and analyzed via ImageJ software. The –wound healing rate was calculated as follows: (Area0h–Area24h) / Area0h × 100%.

Transwell assay

HaCaT and HDF cells were transfected and cultured for 24 h as described above. HaCaT and HDF cells were seeded into the upper chamber of 24‐well Transwell inserts (8.0 μm pore size) at a density of 5 × 105 cells/ml (100 μL/well) in serum‐free medium, with each group performed in triplicate. The lower chamber was filled with 600 μL of MEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS to serve as a chemoattractant. After 48 h of incubation, the non‐migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. The cells that had migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Images were captured using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and three random fields were selected for cell counting. The migratory capacity was expressed as the average number of migrated cells per field.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., NY, USA), and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using independent or paired Student's t‐tests. Non‐normally distributed data were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test (for independent samples) or the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test (for paired samples). For multi‐group comparisons, one‐way anova (followed by the LSD post‐hoc test), Welch's test, or the Kruskal‐Wallis test was applied depending on data distribution and variance homogeneity (assessed via Levene's test). Categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐square or Fisher's exact test. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson, Spearman, or point‐biserial methods, as appropriate. A two‐sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All procedures involving human participants in this study were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. This study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (Ethics batch number CDEC000004982), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Reduced m6A methylation and methyltransferase expression in DFU tissues, with WTAP positively predicting m6A levels

Prior to NPWT, total RNA m6A levels (m6A%) in wound margin tissues were quantified via colorimetric assay. The pre‐DFU group exhibited significantly lower m6A% compared to the pre‐SUC group (P = 0.003) (Figure 2a). Consistently, both qRT‐PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that the mRNA and protein expression levels of the core m6A methyltransferases (WTAP, METTL3, and METTL14) were significantly downregulated in the pre‐DFU group relative to the pre‐SUC group (all P < 0.05) (Figure 2b; Figure 2c).

To identify the key methyltransferase driving this differential methylation, we evaluated the correlations between m6A% and the mRNA expression of these enzymes (Figure 2d; Table S4). Pearson analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between m6A% and WTAP expression in both the pre‐DFU (r = 0.484, P = 0.008) and pre‐SUC (r = 0.877, P < 0.001) groups. In contrast, neither METTL3 nor METTL14 showed significant correlations with m6A% in either group (P > 0.05). Furthermore, regression models confirmed that WTAP was a significant positive predictor of m6A methylation in both the DFU (R 2 = 0.234, β = 0.022, P = 0.008) and SUC (R 2 = 0.770, β = 0.012, P < 0.001) groups (Table S5).

Increased m6A methylation and methyltransferase expression following NPWT, with ΔWTAP positively predicting Δm6A%

Following NPWT, m6A% and methyltransferase expression in wound margin tissues were re‐evaluated. The dynamic changes (Δ) for these parameters were subsequently calculated by subtracting the pre‐treatment baseline values from the post‐treatment values. Compared to their respective baseline levels, m6A% was significantly elevated in both the post‐DFU and post‐SUC groups (both P < 0.05) (Figure 3a). Consistently, qRT‐PCR and Western blot analyses revealed that the mRNA and protein expression of WTAP, METTL3, and METTL14 were significantly upregulated in both groups post‐treatment (all P < 0.05) (Figure 3b, Figure 3c). This upregulation was further verified by IHC, which showed significantly increased AOD for WTAP protein in the post‐NPWT groups (both P < 0.05) (Figure 3d). While the magnitude of these changes (Δm6A%, ΔWTAP, ΔMETTL3, and ΔMETTL14) tended to be numerically higher in the SUC group, the differences between the DFU and SUC groups did not reach statistical significance (all P > 0.05). To confirm whether this WTAP upregulation was specifically induced by NPWT rather than natural healing, a retrospective control of 8 DFU patients receiving conventional debridement alone was analyzed (Figure S1). No significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the groups (all P > 0.05) (Table S8). IHC analysis revealed that CT groups showed no significant changes in AOD for WTAP protein after 1 week (P = 0.379) (Figure S1c), further corroborating the role of NPWT in upregulating WTAP expression.

Furthermore, we evaluated the correlations between the dynamic changes in m6A% and methyltransferase expression (Figure 3e; Table S6). Pearson analysis revealed that Δm6A% was positively associated with ΔWTAP in both the DFU (r = 0.592, P = 0.001) and SUC (r = 0.674, P = 0.023) groups. In contrast, neither ΔMETTL3 nor ΔMETTL14 exhibited significant correlations with Δm6A% (all P > 0.05). Regression models further confirmed that ΔWTAP was a significant positive predictor of Δm6A levels in both the DFU (R 2 = 0.351, β = 0.014, P = 0.001) and SUC (R 2 = 0.454, β = 0.009, P = 0.023) groups (Table S7).

Correlations of baseline WTAP expression with METTL3/14 and clinical indicators

As summarized in Table 2, baseline WTAP mRNA expression was significantly and positively correlated with METTL3 levels in both the pre‐DFU (r = 0.499, P = 0.006) and pre‐SUC (r = 0.691, P = 0.019) groups. Additionally, WTAP expression in the pre‐DFU group exhibited a positive association with diabetes duration (r = 0.457, P = 0.013). Conversely, no significant correlations were observed between WTAP and METTL14 or any other clinical parameters in either group (all P > 0.05).

Negative pressure reverses the high glucose‐induced suppression of WTAP expression

Given our clinical findings that WTAP is downregulated in DFU periwound tissues and restored by NPWT, we hypothesized that a high‐glucose environment downregulates WTAP expression, whereas negative pressure reverses it. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated HaCaT and HDF cells in vitro. Whether cultured under negative pressure, the high‐glucose (HG) environment significantly inhibited WTAP expression in HaCaT and HDF cells at both the mRNA and protein levels (all P < 0.05) (Figure 4b; Figure 4c). Under both normal glucose (NG) and HG conditions, the −125 mmHg negative pressure treatment significantly increased WTAP expression in both cell lines at the mRNA and protein levels compared to the 0 mmHg control (all P < 0.05) (Figure 4b; Figure 4c). These results demonstrate that negative pressure effectively reverses the HG‐induced downregulation of WTAP.

WTAP knockdown exacerbates high glucose‐induced impairment of keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation and migration

Based on our clinical observation that a higher magnitude of WTAP upregulation correlates with improved ulcer healing, we hypothesized that WTAP modulates the essential biological functions of wound cells. Following the verification of siRNA knockdown efficiency (Figure 4a), the most effective sequence (S1) was selected. Both qRT‐PCR and Western blot analyses confirmed successful WTAP downregulation in the siWTAP groups compared to their respective negative controls (siNC) in both HaCaT and HDF lines under both NG and HG conditions (all P < 0.05) (Figure 4b; Figure 4c). Subsequent functional analyses performed under normal atmospheric pressure (0 mmHg) demonstrated that the HG environment impaired cellular functions in both HaCaT and HDF lines: the HG + siNC group exhibited significantly lower cell viability (Figure 4d), migration (Figure 4e), and wound closure rates (Figure 4f) than the NG + siNC group (all P < 0.05). Crucially, WTAP knockdown further aggravated these dysfunctions. Compared to their respective siNC controls, WTAP depletion in both HaCaT and HDF lines under both NG and HG environments led to markedly impaired cell viability (Figure 4d), reduced cell migration (Figure 4e), and decreased wound healing rates (Figure 4f) (all P < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

DFU is a severe and prevalent complication of diabetes, characterized by high morbidity, recurrence, and amputation rates. It significantly diminishes patients' quality of life, adversely affects prognosis, and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden 29 . The etiology of DFUs is highly complex, driven by metabolic dysregulation, peripheral neuropathy, and vascular disease 29 , 30 . Although the clinical management of DFUs has advanced, the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying delayed wound healing remain poorly understood. Elucidating these mechanisms is imperative for identifying novel therapeutic targets and improving patient prognosis.

Clinically, while our DFU and non‐diabetic SUC cohorts were well‐matched in baseline demographics and ulcer chronicity, DFU patients exhibited distinct systemic derangements. Specifically, DFU patients presented with significantly impaired renal function (elevated creatinine, reduced eGFR), compromised nutritional status (lower BMI, RBC, Hb, ALB, TC), and a heightened state of systemic inflammation and hypercoagulability. These findings align with previous studies identifying diabetic nephropathy and DFUs as bidirectional risk factors 31 , 32 , and highlighting the high prevalence of malnutrition (up to 38.36%) and severe concomitant infections in this population 5 , 33 . Consequently, the successful management of DFUs requires not only localized surgical interventions like debridement and NPWT but also comprehensive, individualized systemic care.

Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of epigenetics, particularly m6A RNA methylation, in diabetic wound healing. The m6A methyltransferase complex (MTC) is primarily composed of the catalytic core METTL3, the structural stabilizer METTL14, and WTAP, which regulates the complex's nuclear localization and function 34 . Our study revealed that, prior to NPWT, global m6A levels and the expression of METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP were significantly suppressed in DFU periwound tissues compared to SUC controls. Crucially, following NPWT, both cohorts exhibited a marked restoration of global m6A levels and the expression of these MTC proteins. This implies that the reactivation of the m6A methylation machinery is actively involved in the NPWT‐mediated wound healing process. Consistent with our results, a genome‐wide screening of the m6A profile during skin wound healing in diabetic mice revealed that METTL3, METTL14, and WTAP are significantly downregulated, accompanied by a decrease in global m6A levels compared to non‐diabetic controls 26 . Clinically, reduced METTL3 expression in diabetic wounds has also been correlated with a poor patient prognosis 24 . Mechanistically, hyperglycemia‐induced METTL3 downregulation decreases the mRNA stability of CDP‐diacylglycerol synthase 2 (Cds2) in an m6A‐dependent manner. This impairs mitochondrial function and the angiogenic capacity of vascular endothelial cells, ultimately hindering diabetic wound healing 24 . Furthermore, in a DFU mouse model, adipose‐derived stem cells have been shown to accelerate diabetic wound closure by modulating vascular endothelial growth factor‐C (VEGF‐C)‐mediated lymphangiogenesis via the m6A methylation pathway 25 . Aligning perfectly with our clinical data, a recent study also reported a global reduction in mRNA m6A methylation in human DFUs 35 . Their in vivo experiments demonstrated that enhancing the m6A modification of the pro‐inflammatory factor MMP9 suppresses its mRNA stability, thereby accelerating wound closure, granulation tissue formation, collagen deposition, and the regeneration of skin appendages 35 . Taken together, these studies strongly support our findings, underscoring that m6A methylation regulates multiple pathological stages of diabetic wound healing through the dynamic modulation of RNA metabolism.

A key novelty of our study is identifying WTAP as the primary regulatory “writer” driving these epigenetic changes. While initially identified as an RNA splicing factor, WTAP is essential for diverse biological processes, including cell cycle progression and differentiation 36 , 37 , 38 . Our correlation and regression analyses revealed that both baseline m6A levels and their dynamic changes during NPWT (Δm6A) were strongly and positively predicted by WTAP expression (ΔWTAP), rather than by METTL3 or METTL14. Although METTL3 expression correlated with WTAP—suggesting a synergistic relationship within the MTC—WTAP appears to be the dominant regulatory bottleneck in this context. Clinically, the magnitude of WTAP upregulation was significantly and positively correlated with the 4‐week healing outcome, supporting a potential association between WTAP upregulation and early DFU healing.

To validate these clinical observations, we focused on keratinocytes and fibroblasts, which are primarily responsible for re‐epithelialization and granulation tissue formation 39 , 40 . In both HaCaT and HDF cells, high‐glucose conditions suppressed WTAP expression and impaired cellular proliferation and migration. Importantly, in vitro negative pressure treatment effectively reversed this WTAP downregulation. Furthermore, targeted knockdown of WTAP profoundly exacerbated these cellular dysfunctions, regardless of glucose concentration. These in vitro results strongly corroborate our clinical findings. Supporting our hypothesis, previous studies on the pathogenesis of psoriasis have revealed that WTAP is highly expressed in the epidermis; moreover, in vitro assays confirmed that downregulating WTAP inhibits keratinocyte proliferation, whereas its overexpression exerts the opposite effect 27 . Another study highlighted that WTAP expression is significantly elevated in condyloma acuminatum tissues, which are predominantly characterized by highly hyperplastic keratinocytes, compared to normal skin 41 . Similarly, regarding fibroblasts, a recent study on keloids demonstrated that WTAP is significantly upregulated in dermal fibroblasts, contributing to their hyperactive state and excessive fibroproliferation 42 .

Currently, NPWT is a cornerstone therapy for complex ulcers, known to accelerate granulation and re‐epithelialization by mechanically stimulating cellular pathways 43 , 44 , 45 . Integrating our clinical and in vitro data, we propose a novel epigenetic mechanism for this well‐established therapy: NPWT‐accelerated DFU healing is accompanied by upregulated WTAP expression and restored m6A methylation levels, which may help rescue the proliferative and migratory capacities of wound cells impaired by the diabetic environment. While this study is the first to propose the WTAP‐m6A axis as a novel mechanism in NPWT‐treated DFUs, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, a major limitation of this study is that the conventional debridement control group was based on a small retrospective case series (Table S8; Figure S1). Although these data provide preliminary evidence consistent with an NPWT‐associated increase in WTAP rather than a change observed during natural healing, their retrospective design and small sample size risk selection bias. Additionally, our evaluations were restricted to semi‐quantitative IHC analysis without absolute quantification. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes incorporating a concurrent CT arm and quantitative methods are necessary to further validate these findings. Second, the single‐center design and the relatively small sample size, particularly in the correlation and regression analyses, introduce a risk of selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. Future large‐scale, multi‐center cohorts are needed to robustly validate these results. Finally, while our in vitro data confirmed WTAP's essential role in keratinocyte function, the specific downstream RNA targets regulated by this methylation remain unidentified. Future studies employing in vivo animal models and multi‐omics sequencing are warranted to fully map this regulatory network.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that WTAP expression and global m6A methylation are significantly downregulated and strongly positively correlated in DFU tissues. Clinical intervention with NPWT effectively restores both parameters, with the magnitude of WTAP upregulation serving as a robust positive predictor for m6A elevation and exhibiting a significant positive correlation with early ulcer healing. In vitro assays corroborated these clinical observations, confirming that negative pressure reverses the high glucose‐induced suppression of WTAP expression, and that a high‐glucose environment impairs keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation and migration by downregulating WTAP. Taken together, our findings reveal that NPWT‐accelerated DFU healing is strongly associated with the restoration of WTAP‐mediated m6A RNA methylation, which in turn relates to the critical proliferative and migratory functions of wound cells.
