Exploring nurses’ experiences and perceptions of mentorship programs in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia: an interpretive phenomenological analysis
Authors: Waleed M Alshehri, Abdulaziz M Alodhailah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMentorship programs have emerged as critical interventions to support and retain nursing staff within complex healthcare environments. Although mentorship benefits are well documented globally, there remains limited qualitative evidence on how nurses in Saudi Arabia’s multicultural healthcare context experience mentorship relationships.AimThis study aimed to explore nurses’ experiences and perceptions of mentorship programs in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The objectives were to identify perceived benefits and challenges of mentorship, examine cultural influences on mentorship relationships, and develop evidence-based recommendations to enhance mentorship programs.MethodsA qualitative interpretive phenomenological approach was used. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 17 registered nurses recruited through purposive maximum-variation sampling. Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive thematic analysis, implemented iteratively within a Heideggerian interpretive framework.ResultsFour major themes emerged: [1] Professional growth and development [2], Emotional support and psychological well-being [3], Cultural navigation and adaptation, and [4] Organizational integration and belonging. A unique conceptual insight, “supported vulnerability,” was identified, illustrating how mentorship fosters both emotional openness and professional confidence in challenging clinical environments.ConclusionsMentorship programs have significant influence on nurses’ professional identity, emotional resilience, and cultural integration. Recommendations include establishing structured, culturally responsive mentorship programs with protected time and mentor preparation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04250-y.Keywords: Mentorship, Nursing, Qualitative research, Phenomenology, Professional development, Saudi arabia, Healthcare workforce, Cultural competence

Background

Mentorship is recognized globally as a key strategy for supporting nursing workforce development in response to challenges such as turnover, burnout, and transition stress [1–3] .While these challenges are global, they are particularly pronounced in Saudi Arabia, where a rapidly expanding healthcare sector relies heavily on a multicultural nursing workforce [4–6].

Saudi Arabia’s healthcare workforce comprises approximately 60% expatriate nurses, creating unique cultural, communication, and professional integration dynamics [7–9]. Although mentorship initiatives exist, little is known about how nurses, Saudi and expatriate, experience mentorship within these multicultural settings, and how cultural dynamics shape these relationships [9].

Existing regional studies have largely adopted quantitative or descriptive designs [9–11]. To date, no interpretive phenomenological study has examined mentorship as a lived experience within Saudi Arabia, nor explored how cultural, emotional, and organizational factors intersect in mentorship processes [12–16].

This study draws on Kram’s mentorship theory [17], social learning theory [18], and Benner’s novice-to-expert model [19, 20]. These theories inform the study’s exploration of developmental, relational, and experiential aspects of mentorship. Additionally, Heideggerian phenomenology guides interpretation by emphasizing nurses’ being-in-practice within the cultural and organizational world of Saudi healthcare [12, 13].

Mentorship has been associated with increased job satisfaction, improved clinical competence, and enhanced organizational commitment [9, 21]. However, quantitative outcomes do not capture the deeper experiential meanings of mentorship, particularly the emotional, cultural, and identity-forming dimensions that nurses navigate [22, 23].

Therefore, this study addresses a critical gap by using interpretive phenomenology to explore nurses’ lived experiences of mentorship in Riyadh. It examines not only the professional benefits and challenges but also how mentorship supports cultural navigation, emotional safety, and organizational belonging within a multicultural workforce.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed an interpretive phenomenological approach grounded in Heideggerian philosophy to explore nurses’ lived experiences within mentorship programs [12]. This approach was selected because it emphasizes interpretive meaning-making, allowing researchers to examine how nurses understand and navigate mentorship within their cultural, emotional, and organizational contexts [13].

The study was conducted with nurses from multiple healthcare institutions in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Participants were drawn from four healthcare institutions: two governmental hospitals, one private hospital, and one specialized medical center. Riyadh was selected due to its large concentration of healthcare facilities and culturally diverse nursing workforce.

Participants and sampling

Participants were recruited using purposive maximum-variation sampling to capture diverse backgrounds, roles, and mentorship experiences. Eligibility required participants to be registered nurses who had participated in a mentorship program for at least six months.

The final sample consisted of 17 participants, including both mentors and mentees, representing a broad range of nationalities, clinical specialties, and years of professional experience [20]. Of these 17 participants, 10 were from governmental hospitals, 4 from the private hospital, and 3 from the specialized medical center. Data saturation was reached at 15 interviews, with two additional interviews conducted for confirmation.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted via secure online platforms (Zoom Professional and Microsoft Teams) to accommodate geographical and scheduling constraints. The interview guide was developed through a literature review, expert consultation, and pilot testing with two nurses (excluded from the final analysis). The final study-specific guide (Supplementary File 1) was used for all 17 interviews conducted in August and September 2025. Interviews explored participants’ experiences with mentorship, including entry into mentorship, mentor–mentee matching, relationship dynamics, learning and development, cultural influences, emotional and psychological dimensions, and organizational factors affecting program effectiveness. Participants also discussed outcomes for professional and personal growth and offered recommendations for improvement. Interviews lasted approximately 40 min (30–60 min), were audio-recorded with consent, and supplemented by detailed field notes to support reflexivity.

Data analysis

Data analysis employed Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis, selected for its theoretical flexibility and systematic approach to pattern identification [24]. The six-phase process was implemented as follows: (1) Familiarization, transcripts were read repeatedly to immerse researchers in the data; (2) Initial coding, line-by-line inductive coding in NVivo 14 captured both semantic and latent meanings; (3) Searching for themes, related codes were clustered into preliminary themes using visual mapping; (4) Reviewing themes, themes were checked for coherence and consistency against the full dataset; (5) Defining and naming themes, themes were refined, clearly defined, and labeled; (6) Producing the final narrative, the final analysis connected participants’ experiences with relevant theoretical frameworks and existing literature [25].

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was ensured using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria. Credibility was strengthened through methodological triangulation, member checking, and peer debriefing. Reflexivity was maintained through researcher journals, documentation of assumptions, and regular reflexive debriefing sessions. The research team also examined their positionality as educators and clinicians and engaged in bracketing to reduce interpretive bias. Transferability was supported by providing thick descriptions of the study setting and participants. Dependability and confirmability were ensured through systematic documentation and detailed audit trails.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was granted by King Saud University Institutional Review Board (KSU-HE-25-956). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and anonymity were assured throughout the research process. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration.

Theme 1: Professional growth and development through guided practice

This theme captures mentorship’s fundamental role in facilitating professional advancement through structured learning experiences, skill development, and career guidance. Participants consistently described mentorship as a catalyst for professional transformation that accelerated their development beyond what formal education or independent learning could achieve.

Participants emphasized how mentorship provided opportunities for hands-on learning that bridged the theory-practice gap. Mentees described observing their mentors’ clinical decision-making processes, which revealed the tacit knowledge underlying expert practice. One mentee with three years of experience shared: “My mentor didn’t just show me procedures; she explained her thinking process. When assessing a deteriorating patient, she would verbalize her observations, prioritization, and decision-making. This metacognitive approach transformed how I approach patient care. I learned to think like an experienced nurse, not just act like one.”

Mentors also reported professional growth through the mentoring process, particularly in developing their teaching and leadership capabilities. A mentor with 15 years’ experience reflected: “Mentoring forced me to articulate my implicit knowledge. When my mentee asked ‘why do you do it that way?‘, I had to examine my practice critically. This reflection enhanced my own clinical reasoning and made me a more thoughtful practitioner.”

Participants described how mentorship facilitated the integration of diverse knowledge sources - theoretical, empirical, and experiential - into coherent professional understanding. This integration was particularly valuable for international nurses adapting to Saudi healthcare protocols. One mentee explained: “The Saudi healthcare system integrates Western medicine with Islamic considerations in unique ways. My mentor helped me understand not just the policies but the reasoning behind them. For example, she explained how we modify cardiac rehabilitation protocols during Ramadan, considering both medical evidence and religious practices.”

Mentorship significantly influenced participants’ career planning and professional advancement. Mentors provided crucial guidance on navigating organizational structures, identifying development opportunities, and building professional networks. A mentee noted: “My mentor opened my eyes to possibilities I hadn’t considered. She encouraged me to pursue certification in critical care nursing and introduced me to the Saudi Critical Care Nursing Society. These connections accelerated my career progression from staff nurse to charge nurse in just two years.”

Theme 2: Emotional support and psychological well-being

This theme illuminates the profound psychological dimensions of mentorship, revealing how these relationships provide essential emotional support that enables nurses to navigate the intense demands of healthcare practice.

Participants described mentorship as providing safe spaces for emotional expression and processing of challenging clinical experiences. This emotional support was particularly crucial for managing moral distress, compassion fatigue, and burnout. A mentee in oncology shared: “After losing a young patient I had cared for months, I was devastated. My mentor didn’t just offer platitudes; she shared her own experiences with loss and taught me healthy coping strategies. She normalized my grief while helping me maintain professional boundaries. This support prevented me from leaving oncology nursing.”

The emotional support extended beyond crisis moments to encompass ongoing stress management and work-life balance. A mentor reflected: “In Saudi culture, there’s sometimes reluctance to discuss emotional struggles, seeing it as weakness. Creating a safe space where my mentee could express vulnerabilities without judgment was transformative for both of us. She learned emotional regulation strategies, and I developed greater cultural sensitivity in providing support.”

Participants consistently reported increased professional confidence through mentorship. This confidence emerged from supportive feedback, skill validation, and encouragement to take on challenging situations. A newly graduated nurse described: “I was terrified of making mistakes that could harm patients. My mentor’s approach was brilliant - she stayed close enough to prevent errors but far enough to let me develop independence. Her phrase ‘trust your training and instincts’ became my mantra. Within months, my confidence soared.”

Mentorship relationships provided psychological safety that enabled risk-taking and learning from mistakes without fear of judgment or punitive consequences. A mentee explained: “In my previous hospital, admitting uncertainty was seen as incompetence. My mentor here created an environment where questions were welcomed and mistakes became learning opportunities. This psychological safety accelerated my learning exponentially.”

Theme 3: Cultural navigation and adaptation

This theme reveals the crucial role of mentorship in facilitating cultural adaptation and navigation of the complex multicultural dynamics within Saudi healthcare institutions.

International nurses consistently emphasized how mentorship was essential for understanding the Saudi healthcare system’s unique characteristics, including organizational structures, documentation requirements, and regulatory frameworks. One nurse reflected: “The Saudi healthcare system was overwhelming initially - different hierarchy, documentation systems, and quality standards. My mentor served as a cultural translator, explaining not just what to do but why things operated differently here. She helped me understand the Ministry of Health protocols, CBAHI standards, and hospital-specific policies.”

Participants described learning to provide culturally sensitive care that respects Islamic values and practices, with mentorship providing nuanced understanding beyond surface-level cultural training. A mentee shared: “Textbook cultural training didn’t prepare me for the complexities of providing care during Ramadan, managing gender preferences in care delivery, or understanding family dynamics in medical decision-making. My Saudi mentor provided insights that transformed my practice. I learned when flexibility was appropriate and when religious requirements were absolute.”

The cultural learning was bidirectional, with Saudi mentors learning about international perspectives and practices. A mentor noted: “My Filipino mentee brought different approaches to patient education that were more visual and family-inclusive. I integrated these methods into my practice, enriching my cultural competence even in my own country.”

Participants described how mentorship facilitated development of culturally appropriate communication strategies, including understanding indirect communication styles, hierarchical communication patterns, and language nuances. One nurse explained: “Direct communication, which I valued, was sometimes perceived as rude here. My mentor taught me the art of indirect communication - how to be assertive while maintaining respect, how to disagree diplomatically, and how to read between the lines in conversations.”

Theme 4: Organizational integration and professional belonging

This theme captures how mentorship facilitates integration into organizational cultures and development of professional identity within the Saudi healthcare context.

Mentorship served as a gateway to broader professional networks, with mentors facilitating introductions and connections that extended beyond the immediate workplace. A mentee described: “My mentor introduced me to key people across departments - not just socially, but strategically connecting me with professionals who could support my growth. Through her, I joined the hospital’s research committee and quality improvement team. These connections transformed me from an outsider to an integrated team member.”

Participants described how mentorship facilitated understanding and internalization of organizational values, norms, and expectations beyond formal orientation programs. A mentor explained: “Every hospital has its unique culture - unwritten rules about how things really work. I helped my mentee understand our hospital’s emphasis on interdisciplinary collaboration, the importance of connections in getting things done, and how to navigate politics while maintaining professionalism.”

Mentorship profoundly influenced participants’ professional identity development, helping them integrate their cultural identity with their professional role. A nurse reflected: “I struggled with my identity - was I an Egyptian nurse in Saudi Arabia or a nurse who happened to be Egyptian? My mentor helped me integrate these identities, showing me how my Egyptian background enriched my nursing practice while fully embracing the Saudi healthcare context. I developed a hybrid professional identity that honors both cultures.”

Challenges and barriers

While participants predominantly reported positive experiences, they also identified significant challenges that limited mentorship effectiveness.

The most frequently cited challenge was insufficient protected time for mentorship activities (82.4% of participants). A mentor in the emergency department explained: “We’re supposed to meet weekly, but with 12-hour shifts and chronic understaffing, meaningful mentorship becomes impossible. We end up having hurried conversations during break times instead of the reflective discussions mentorship requires. The administration supports mentorship in principle but doesn’t provide the practical resources needed.”

Several participants (41.2%) experienced relationship difficulties due to personality conflicts, communication style differences, or misaligned expectations. A mentee shared: “My assigned mentor was technically excellent but emotionally unavailable. When I tried discussing work-stress, she dismissed it as ‘part of nursing.’ I needed emotional support, not just clinical guidance. The program lacked mechanisms for addressing mismatches, so I endured six months of ineffective mentorship.”

Despite mentorship’s role in cultural navigation, cultural differences sometimes created barriers within mentorship relationships themselves (35.3% of participants). A mentee noted: “My Western mentor had different expectations about professional boundaries. She encouraged informality that made me uncomfortable given my cultural background. While she meant to be friendly, I interpreted it as lack of professionalism. These cultural misunderstandings created tension until we openly discussed our different perspectives.”

Many participants (64.7%) reported that their mentorship programs lacked clear structure, objectives, and evaluation mechanisms. A mentor criticized: “The program was poorly defined - no clear objectives, no training for mentors, no evaluation criteria. We were essentially told to ‘mentor’ without guidance on what that meant. This ambiguity led to inconsistent experiences and missed opportunities for systematic professional development.”

Discussion

This Heideggerian interpretive phenomenological inquiry into nurses’ mentorship experiences in Riyadh healthcare institutions reveals mentorship as a transformative phenomenon that profoundly shapes professional development, psychological well-being, cultural adaptation, and organizational integration. The four emergent themes illuminate how mentorship functions as both a practical and existential resource within the unique Saudi Arabian healthcare context [26].

Professional development as embodied learning

The theme of Professional Growth and Development reflects the Heideggerian concept of being-in-the-world, where learning emerges through embodied engagement within authentic practice contexts. Participants’ experiences of observing mentors’ clinical reasoning and gradually assuming greater responsibility exemplify legitimate peripheral participation in communities of practice [26]. The mentors’ articulation of tacit knowledge through “thinking aloud” demonstrates how experienced practitioners make visible the fore-structure of understanding, the taken-for-granted knowledge that frames competent action. This situated learning enables nurses to integrate theoretical, empirical, and experiential knowledge in ways that formal curricula alone cannot achieve, bridging the persistent theory-practice gap that characterizes nursing education [20, 25].

Emotional dimensions and the concept of supported vulnerability

The Emotional Support and Psychological Well-being theme reveals mentorship’s role as both a relational and existential resource. The emergent concept of “supported vulnerability” demonstrates how mentorship creates spaces where nurses can authentically confront their anxieties, uncertainties, and limitations while building professional competence. This psychological safety aligns with the Job Demands-Resources model, wherein mentorship serves as a crucial resource that buffers against burnout, compassion fatigue, and moral distress. By enabling reflection and emotional processing, mentorship fosters authentic professional engagement and supports critical outcomes such as error reporting and quality improvement initiatives.

Cultural navigation and synergistic learning

The Cultural Navigation and Adaptation theme illustrates how mentorship facilitates nurses’ understanding of the complex cultural dynamics within Saudi healthcare. From a Heideggerian perspective, this represents the negotiation of world hood, the contextual background that gives meaning to professional practice. Participants’ accounts of bidirectional cultural learning reflect cultural synergy, where interaction between diverse perspectives generates innovative practices that exceed the capabilities of any single cultural approach. This finding extends existing models of cultural competence by demonstrating how mentorship serves as a medium for creating hybrid professional practices that honor both local and international nursing traditions [27].

Organizational integration and identity formation

The Organizational Integration and Professional Belonging theme aligns with organizational socialization theory while incorporating insights from Heideggerian authenticity. Mentorship supports not merely the acquisition of organizational knowledge and behaviors, but the development of what participants described as hybrid professional identities. This identity formation represents the authentic reconciliation of personal, cultural, and professional selves within the demands of the organizational context. Mentorship serves as both a social and existential scaffold, enabling nurses to inhabit their professional roles with meaning and agency while navigating complex cultural and organizational expectations [22].

Challenges as constraints on authentic being-in-practice

The identified challenges, time constraints, mentor-mentee mismatches, cultural barriers, and lack of structure, represent what Heidegger might describe as constraints on authentic being-in-the-world [23]. These barriers limit mentorship’s potential to function as a transformative relationship, reducing it to superficial interactions that fail to address the deep learning and identity formation that effective mentorship enables. The prevalence of these challenges suggests that many mentorship programs operate at a surface level, missing opportunities for the profound professional and personal development that participants described in effective mentoring relationships [8].

Theoretical and practical implications

These findings contribute to mentorship theory by demonstrating how effective mentoring relationships function across multiple dimensions simultaneously, professional, emotional, cultural, and organizational. The concept of “supported vulnerability” offers a new lens for understanding how mentorship creates conditions for both emotional safety and professional growth. The bidirectional nature of cultural learning challenges traditional mentor-mentee hierarchies and suggests that effective mentorship in multicultural contexts requires mutual cultural humility and openness to learning [28].

For practice, these findings suggest that effective mentorship programs require more than pairing experienced and novice nurses. They need structured approaches that explicitly address emotional support, cultural navigation, and organizational integration while providing adequate time and resources for relationship development. The challenges identified underscore the need for mentor preparation, ongoing support, and program evaluation mechanisms that extend beyond simple satisfaction measures [29].

Study limitations and future directions

This study’s focus on Riyadh may limit transferability to other contexts, though insights regarding multicultural healthcare settings may have broader relevance. A second limitation is the potential for self-selection bias, as participants with more positive mentorship experiences may have been more likely to volunteer. Additionally, the study design captured participants’ experiences at a single point in time, which may not reflect how mentorship relationships evolve. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to examine changes in mentorship relationships over time and investigate links between mentorship processes and patient care outcomes.

Study recommendations

Based on the study findings, the following recommendations are proposed: structured mentorship programs with clear objectives, cultural sensitivity training for mentors, allocation of protected time for mentorship activities, and periodic evaluation of programs to ensure effectiveness and continuous improvement. These measures aim to enhance mentorship quality, support professional development, and potentially improve patient care outcomes.

Conclusion

This study highlights mentorship in nursing as a multidimensional and transformative process that influences nurses’ professional growth, emotional well-being, cultural adaptation, and organizational belonging. The concept of “supported vulnerability” emerged as a key insight, showing how mentorship fosters psychological safety, enabling nurses to be open and confident in high-stress settings.

The research also reveals mutual cultural learning between mentors and mentees, challenging traditional one-way mentorship models and emphasizing the importance of cultural humility in diverse healthcare contexts. However, barriers such as limited time, mismatched relationships, and lack of structure point to the need for systematic, evidence-based mentorship programs.

Overall, the study positions mentorship as a critical foundation for workforce development, retention, and quality care, offering guidance for creating programs that embrace cultural diversity while promoting professional excellence and personal growth.
