Wavelet-based estimation of long-memory parameter in stochastic volatility models using a robust log-periodogram
Manganaw N’Daam, Tchilabalo Abozou Kpanzou, Edoh Katchekpele

Abstract




Abstract:

In this paper, we propose a novel method for estimating the long-memory parameter in time series. By combining the multi-resolution framework of wavelets with the robustness of the Least Absolute Deviations (LAD) criterion, we introduce a periodogram providing a robust alternative to classical methods in the presence of non-Gaussian noise. Incorporating this periodogram into a log-periodogram regression, we develop a new estimator. Simulation studies demonstrate that our estimator outperforms the Geweke and Porter-Hudak (GPH) and Wavelet-Based Log-Periodogram (WBLP) estimators, particularly in terms of mean squared error, across various sample sizes and parameter configurations.

Key Words and Phrases
: Long-memory parameter estimation, Wavelet-based methods, Log-periodogram regression, Simulation.

2010 Mathematics Subject Classifications:
Primary 62M10; Secondary 62J05.

1. Introduction

The estimation of the long-memory parameter is a fundamental problem in time
series analysis, particularly for long-memory processes where the autocorrelation function decays hyperbolically. These processes are prevalent in various
fields, including econometrics, finance, climatology, and social sciences, where
accurate characterization of long-range dependence is crucial for effective modeling and forecasting.

Since the seminal work of
[
8
]
, numerous methods have been proposed for estimating the long-memory parameter, denoted
\displaystyle d
. One of the most widely used approaches is the semi-parametric log-periodogram regression method introduced by
[
7
]
. Despite its popularity, this method suffers from significant limitations, particularly its sensitivity to non-Gaussian noise, which can induce substantial negative bias in the estimation, as noted by
[
10
]
. Addressing these shortcomings has been a key focus of recent research efforts.

Wavelet-based methods have emerged as a promising alternative, offering superior robustness by leveraging both time and frequency localization properties.

[
10
]
introduced an estimator based on the wavelet periodogram, demonstrating improved performance under non-Gaussian conditions. More recently,
[
19
]
proposed a wavelet-based approach for estimating
\displaystyle d
in potentially non-linear and non-Gaussian time series, further highlighting the advantages of wavelet techniques. Similarly,
[
5
]
developed an estimator based on fractional spline wavelets, reinforcing the effectiveness of wavelet-based estimation in capturing long-memory behavior.

In this paper, we propose a novel estimation method for the long-memory parameter in long-memory stochastic volatility (LMSV) models. Our approach builds upon the wavelet log-periodogram framework, introducing a periodogram, which relies on the least absolute deviations (LAD) method applied to wavelet coefficients. This modification enhances robustness against noise and improves estimation accuracy, particularly in financial time series characterized by heavy-tailed distributions and volatility clustering.

2. Wavelet Analysis

In this section, we introduce the discrete wavelet transform. This method relies on the construction of orthonormal bases obtained by dyadic dilation and translation of a pair of specific functions,
\displaystyle\phi
and
\displaystyle\psi
, called the father wavelet and mother wavelet, respectively, such that:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x)\,dx=1,\quad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x)\,dx=0.

In time series analysis, the father wavelet
\displaystyle\phi(\cdot)
is used to represent smooth and low-frequency components of the series, while the mother wavelet
\displaystyle\psi(\cdot)
captures details and high-frequency components.

The wavelet family
\displaystyle\{\psi_{j,q},\,(j,q)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\}
and scaling functions
\displaystyle\{\phi_{j,q},\,(j,q)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\}
are constructed by performing dilation and translation operations on the functions
\displaystyle\phi
and
\displaystyle\psi
, defined as:

\psi_{j,q}(t)=2^{\frac{j}{2}}\psi(2^{j}t-q),\quad\phi_{j,q}(t)=2^{\frac{j}{2}}\phi(2^{j}t-q),

where
\displaystyle j=1,\dots,J
represents the scale, and
\displaystyle q=1,\dots,2^{j}
corresponds to the translation.
The parameter
\displaystyle j
dilates the wavelet functions to adjust their support and capture characteristics of low or high frequencies, while
\displaystyle q
performs temporal translation. The maximum number of scales
\displaystyle J
depends on the number of observations
\displaystyle n
, with the constraint
\displaystyle n\geq 2^{J}
. These families are used to compute wavelet and scaling coefficients, respectively.

An important property of wavelets is localization: the coefficient associated with
\displaystyle\psi_{j,q}(t)
provides local information about the function around the approximate position
\displaystyle q2^{-j}
and frequency
\displaystyle 2^{j}
.

Any function belonging to
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
can be developed over a wavelet basis as follows:

X(t)=\sum_{q}a_{J_{0},q}\phi_{J_{0},q}(t)+\sum_{j>J_{0}}\sum_{q}b_{j,q}\psi_{j,q}(t),

where
\displaystyle\phi_{J_{0},q}
is a scaling function associated with coarse coefficients
\displaystyle a_{J_{0},q}
, and
\displaystyle\psi_{j,q}
is associated with fine coefficients
\displaystyle b_{j,q}
, all given by the relations:

a_{J_{0},q}=\int X(t)\phi_{J_{0},q}(t)\,dt,\quad b_{j,q}=\int X(t)\psi_{j,q}(t)\,dt.

See
[
2
]
for
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
functions.

These coefficients measure the respective contributions of the scaling functions and wavelets to reconstruct
\displaystyle X(t)
. This decomposition allows
\displaystyle X(t)
to be represented as orthogonal components at different resolutions, constituting a multi-resolution analysis (MRA). For more details, see
[
15
]
.

We now explicitly present the properties of wavelet functions (see
[
10
]
).

Assumption 2.1

(a)

\displaystyle\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x)\,dx=0,\quad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(x)|\,dx<\infty,\quad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(1+x^{2})|\psi(x)|\,dx<\infty.

(b)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\psi}(\lambda)|=|\lambda|^{\nu}b(\lambda)
, with
\displaystyle\dfrac{b(t\lambda)}{b(\lambda)}=1
for all
\displaystyle t
, as
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
, where
\displaystyle\nu
is a positive integer,
\displaystyle 0<b(0)<\infty
, and
\displaystyle\hat{\psi}(\lambda)
is the Fourier transform of
\displaystyle\psi
, defined as:

\hat{\psi}(\lambda)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x)e^{-i\lambda x}\,dx.

Assumption
2.1
(a) describes the properties of the wavelet function
\displaystyle\psi\left(\cdot\right)
. Assumption
2.1
(b), on the other hand, models the spectral behavior of the Fourier transform around
\displaystyle\lambda=0
. The integer parameter
\displaystyle\nu
corresponds to the number of vanishing moments of
\displaystyle\psi\left(\cdot\right)
, meaning:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{r}\psi(x)\,dx=0,\quad\text{for }r=0,1,\dots,\nu-1.

The vanishing of the first
\displaystyle\nu
moments is equivalent to the vanishing of the first
\displaystyle\nu
spectral derivatives at zero, that is:

\frac{d^{r}}{d\lambda^{r}}\hat{\psi}(\lambda)\Big|_{\lambda=0}=0,\quad\text{for }r=0,1,\dots,\nu-1.

This hypothesis is satisfied if
\displaystyle\psi(\cdot)
has a compact support and belongs to the class
\displaystyle C^{\nu}(\mathbb{R})
, meaning that all derivatives up to order
\displaystyle\nu
exist and the
\displaystyle\nu
-th derivative
\displaystyle f^{(\nu)}
is continuous on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
.

In practical applications, time series are generally discrete rather than continuous. Consequently, instead of using continuous wavelets, discrete sequences called wavelet filters are employed (see
[
4
]
). The length of these sequences corresponds to the width of the wavelet filter. This filtering-based approach makes wavelet analysis particularly suitable for time series.

In the discrete wavelet transform (DWT), wavelet coefficients are obtained through the multi-resolution analysis (MRA) scheme, which is implemented using a two-channel filter bank representing the wavelet transform, see
[
15
]
.

[
4
]
introduced an important class of wavelet filters called compactly supported Daubechies wavelet filters, which have the smallest support for a given number of vanishing moments. Two types of filters are distinguished: the extremal phase filters
\displaystyle D(L)
and the least asymmetric filters
\displaystyle LA(L)
.

An improved version of the DWT is the maximal overlap discrete wavelet transform (MODWT). As explained by
[
16
]
, the MODWT algorithm follows the same filtering steps as the standard DWT but without down-sampling, i.e., without halving the resolution. Thus, the number of scaling and wavelet coefficients at each level of the transform remains equal to the total number of observations in the sample. For more details, see
[
16
]
.

After examining the fundamental principles of wavelet analysis, we now turn our attention to estimators of the long-memory parameter.

3. Long-Memory Parameter Estimators

In this section, we present two estimators for evaluating the long-memory parameter. Each method relies on specific assumptions and tools, offering advantages and limitations depending on the application context. The goal is to compare these approaches and analyze their relevance for different types of processes, considering their robustness and accuracy.

3.1. GPH Estimator

In this subsection, we present the estimation method developed by
[
7
]
, which is based on a least squares regression in the spectral domain, leveraging the spectral density behavior
\displaystyle f(\cdot)
of a long-memory process at the origin:

Assumption 3.1

f(\lambda)=|\lambda|^{-2d}f_{u}(\lambda),\quad\text{as }\lambda\rightarrow 0,

(3.1)

where
\displaystyle d
is the long-memory parameter and
\displaystyle f_{u}(\lambda)
is a function assumed to be finite, bounded away from zero and continuous on the interval
\displaystyle\left[-\pi,\pi\right]
.

Consider the problem of estimating the parameter
\displaystyle d
in the general integrated long-memory process
\displaystyle\left\{X_{t}\right\}
(see
[
8
]
). Assume that:

(1-B)^{d}X_{t}=u_{t},

where
\displaystyle B
is the backward shift operator, and
\displaystyle\left\{u_{t}\right\}
is a stationary linear process with a spectral density function
\displaystyle f_{u}(\lambda)
that is finite, bounded away from zero, and continuous on the interval
\displaystyle\left[-\pi,\pi\right]
.

The spectral density
\displaystyle f(\cdot)
of the integrated long-memory process
\displaystyle\left\{X_{t}\right\}
is given by:

f(\lambda)=\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\right]^{-d}f_{u}(\lambda).

(3.2)

Exploiting these spectral properties,
[
7
]
propose a log-periodogram regression method for semi-parametrically estimating
\displaystyle d
based on the first harmonic frequencies. While widely adopted for its efficiency and simplicity, this approach can be sensitive to certain violations of assumptions, such as the presence of outliers, heavy-tailed distributed errors (see
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
), or non-Gaussian noise.

Applying the logarithm to (
3.2
), we obtain:

\log f\left(\lambda\right)=\log f_{u}\left(0\right)-d\log\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\right]+\log\left[\frac{f_{u}\left(\lambda\right)}{f_{u}\left(0\right)}\right].

(3.3)

Let
\displaystyle I_{n}\left(\lambda_{k}\right)
denote the ordinary periodogram of the integrated long-memory process
\displaystyle\left\{X_{t}\right\}
evaluated at the Fourier frequencies
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\frac{2\pi k}{n}
, with
\displaystyle k=1,2,\cdots,m
, where
\displaystyle n
is the sample size and
\displaystyle m
is the number of Fourier frequencies considered. Adding
\displaystyle\log I_{n}\left(\lambda_{k}\right)
to both sides of (
3.3
), we obtain:

\log I_{n}\left(\lambda_{k}\right)=\log f_{u}\left(0\right)-d\log\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\right)\right]+\log\left[\frac{I_{n}\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}{f\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}\right]+\log\left[\frac{f_{u}\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}{f_{u}\left(0\right)}\right],

(3.4)

where the ordinary periodogram at frequency
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}
is defined as:

I_{n}\left(\lambda_{k}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi n}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}e^{it\lambda_{k}}\right|^{2}\quad\text{with}\quad k=1,2,\cdots,m.

The log-periodogram estimator relies on the following hypothesis:

Assumption 3.2

(a)

For low frequencies, the term
\displaystyle\log\left[\frac{f_{u}\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}{f_{u}\left(0\right)}\right]
is negligible.

(b)

The random variables
\displaystyle\log\left[\frac{I_{n}\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}{f\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}\right]
,
\displaystyle k=1,2,\cdots,m
, are asymptotically
\displaystyle i.i.d.

Under Assumption
3.2
,
[
7
]
rewrite equation (
3.4
) as:

Z_{k}=c_{0}+dR_{k}+\epsilon_{k},

where
\displaystyle Z_{k}=\log I_{n}\left(\lambda_{k}\right)
,
\displaystyle c_{0}=\log f_{u}\left(0\right)+C
,
\displaystyle R_{k}=-\log\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\right)\right]
,
\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}=\log\left[\frac{I_{n}\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}{f\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}\right]-C
, and
\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}\sim i.i.d(-C,\frac{\pi^{2}}{6})
with
\displaystyle C=-0.5772
being the Euler constant. The ordinary least squares estimator is then given by:

\hat{d}_{GPH}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(R_{k}-\overline{R}_{m}\right)Z_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(R_{k}-\overline{R}_{m}\right)^{2}},

where
\displaystyle\overline{R}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}R_{k}.

Assumption 3.3

\displaystyle m=m(n)\rightarrow\infty
,
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
, and
\displaystyle\frac{m(n)}{n}\xrightarrow[n\rightarrow\infty]{}0
.

Under Assumption
3.3
,
[
7
]
prove the asymptotic normality of the estimator
\displaystyle\hat{d}_{GPH}
when
\displaystyle|d|<\frac{1}{2}
:

m^{\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{d}_{GPH}-d)\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(R_{k}-\overline{R}_{m}\right)^{2}\right]^{-1}\right)\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

where
\displaystyle\xrightarrow{D}
denotes the convergence in distribution (see
[
14
]
for details).

Although the classical log-periodogram estimator is widely used to estimate the long-memory parameter, it can be significantly affected by the presence of noise or complex data structures. To overcome these limitations, wavelet-based approaches provide a powerful alternative by leveraging the time-frequency localization properties of wavelets.

3.2. Wavelet-Based Log-Periodogram Estimator

[
10
]
proposed a significant improvement to the semi-parametric log-periodogram regression method for estimating the memory parameter in long-memory stochastic volatility (LMSV) models. Although the log-periodogram estimator of
[
7
]
is widely used, it has important limitations. In particular, it violates the Gaussianity or martingale assumptions, resulting in a pronounced negative bias due to the presence of a non-Gaussian noise spectrum.
[
10
]
demonstrated that applying the wavelet transform to the squared process effectively reduces the noise spectrum around zero frequency, yielding a quasi-Gaussian spectral representation at this frequency. Based on this, he proposed a regression estimator in the wavelet domain and established its asymptotic mean squared error and consistency in accordance with the asymptotic theory of long-memory processes developed by
[
9
]
.

Consider a long-memory stochastic volatility model for discrete time series
\displaystyle\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}\}
, defined by:

X_{t}=\sigma\exp\left(\frac{Z_{t}}{2}\right)e_{t},\quad t=1,\cdots,n,

(3.5)

where
\displaystyle\{Z_{t}\}
is a latent Gaussian long-memory process with a memory parameter
\displaystyle d\in(0,0.5)
, independent of the process
\displaystyle\{e_{t}\}
, which is an i.i.d. noise process with zero mean. The spectral density of the process
\displaystyle\{Z_{t}\}
at zero frequency follows the form (
3.1
).

The log-squared process is used as a measure of volatility and is expressed as:

Y_{t}=\log(X_{t}^{2})=\eta+Z_{t}+U_{t},

where
\displaystyle\eta=\log\sigma^{2}+\mathbb{E}(\log e_{t}^{2})
and
\displaystyle U_{t}=\log e_{t}^{2}-\mathbb{E}(\log e_{t}^{2})
. Here,
\displaystyle\{U_{t}\}
is an i.i.d. process with zero mean and variance
\displaystyle\sigma_{U}^{2}
. The autocovariances
\displaystyle R(h)
of
\displaystyle\{Y_{t}\}
are identical to those of
\displaystyle\{Z_{t}\}
for
\displaystyle h\neq 0
.

The spectral density of the process
\displaystyle\{Y_{t}\}
is thus the sum of the spectral density of the Gaussian long-memory process
\displaystyle\{Z_{t}\}
and that of the non-Gaussian noise
\displaystyle\{U_{t}\}
, giving the following relationship:

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{Y}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=f_{Z}(\lambda)+f_{U}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|\lambda|^{-2d}f_{u}(\lambda)+\frac{\sigma_{U}^{2}}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{Y}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|\lambda|^{-2d}\left(f_{u}(\lambda)+\frac{\sigma_{U}^{2}}{2\pi}|\lambda|^{2d}\right),\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\to 0,

where
\displaystyle\sigma_{U}^{2}
is the variance of the non-Gaussian noise.

Thus, this approach accounts for the long-memory effects while integrating the impact of non-Gaussian noise, as emphasized by
[
10
]
.

Due to the presence of the non-Gaussian noise spectrum,
[
10
]
shows that the log-periodogram estimator suffers from a significant negative bias that behaves at the order of
\displaystyle|\lambda|^{2d}
. To address this, he proposes using the wavelet transform of the squared process
\displaystyle\{Y_{t}\}
to obtain an approximately Gaussian spectral representation by effectively eliminating the noise spectrum around zero frequency.

The discrete wavelet transform of the process
\displaystyle\left\{Y_{1},...,Y_{n}\right\}
is defined as follows:

w_{jq}=2^{\frac{j}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}Y_{t}\psi\left(2^{j}t-q\right),

(3.6)

where
\displaystyle t
is appropriately re-indexed to ensure full coverage of the wavelet support. For example, if the support of
\displaystyle\psi
is
\displaystyle[0,1]
, then
\displaystyle t=i/n
, for
\displaystyle i=1,2,\dots,n
. The integers
\displaystyle j
and
\displaystyle q
are the scale and translation parameters, respectively, where
\displaystyle j=0,1,\dots,J
and
\displaystyle q=0,1,\dots,2^{j}-1
. The finest scale is set at
\displaystyle J
, where
\displaystyle n=2^{J}
.

Let Haar wavelet satisfies Assumption 1 with
\displaystyle\nu=1
and be defined as:

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }0\leq x\leq 0.5,\\
-1&\text{if }0.5<x\leq 1,\end{cases}

and its Fourier transform is:

|\widehat{\psi}(\lambda)|=\dfrac{|\lambda|}{4}\left[\frac{\sin^{2}(\lambda/4)}{(\lambda/4)^{2}}\right].

By applying the discrete wavelet transform to the process
\displaystyle\left\{Y_{1},...,Y_{n}\right\}
with the Haar wavelet, it can be expressed as:

w_{jq}=\alpha_{jq}+\beta_{jq},

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\alpha_{jq}=2^{\frac{j}{2}}\sum_{t=1/n}Z_{t}\psi(2^{j}t-q)\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{jq}=2^{\frac{j}{2}}\sum_{t=1/n}U_{t}\psi(2^{j}t-q).

[
10
]
shows that the spectral density of
\displaystyle\beta_{jq}
becomes zero at frequency zero. Let
\displaystyle R_{\beta}(m)=\mathbb{E}[\beta_{jq}\beta_{jq+m}]
, the autocovariance of the transformed series
\displaystyle\beta_{jq}
at scale
\displaystyle j
, and
\displaystyle f_{\beta}^{(j)}
the spectral density at scale
\displaystyle j
. The wavelet transform
\displaystyle\beta_{jq}
is a linear combination of the i.i.d. noise process
\displaystyle U_{t}
.

When applying the Haar wavelet, it is simply the difference between local sums of
\displaystyle U_{t}
over the intervals
\displaystyle t\in[2^{-j}q,2^{-j}(q+0.5)]
and
\displaystyle t\in(2^{-j}(q+0.5),2^{-j}(q+1)]
. Furthermore, the sequence
\displaystyle\beta_{jq}
forms a
\displaystyle 1
-dependent process, effectively behaving like a moving average (MA) process of order
\displaystyle p
, with
\displaystyle p
determined by
\displaystyle j
. As
\displaystyle j
increases, the width of the interval decreases. Ultimately, when
\displaystyle j
reaches the largest scale
\displaystyle J
, the process
\displaystyle\beta_{jq}
approximates an
\displaystyle MA(1)
process, expressed as:

\beta_{jq}=2^{J/2}\left(U_{2^{-J}q}-U_{2^{-J}(q+1)}\right).

Define the autocovariance of
\displaystyle\beta_{jq}
, denoted as
\displaystyle R_{\beta}(m)=\mathbb{E}[\beta_{jq}\beta_{jq+m}]
. When
\displaystyle j=J
, we obtain:

f_{\beta}^{(J)}(0)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}R_{\beta}(m)=0,

(3.7)

where

R_{\beta}(m)=\begin{cases}2\sigma^{2}_{U},&\text{if }m=0,\\
-\sigma^{2}_{U},&\text{if }m=\pm 1,\\
0,&\text{if }|m|>1.\end{cases}

Now, consider the spectral density of
\displaystyle\alpha_{jq}
at scale
\displaystyle j
, denoted as
\displaystyle f_{\alpha}^{(j)}(\lambda)
. Under Assumption 2, it is obtained directly as follows. The autocovariances of the wavelet coefficients at scale
\displaystyle j
are written as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{jq}\alpha_{j\tau}]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=2^{j}\sum_{t}\sum_{s}\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}Z_{s}]\psi(2^{j}t-q)\psi(2^{j}s-\tau)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=2^{j}\sum_{t}\sum_{s}\left(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f_{Z}(\lambda)e^{i(t-s)\lambda}d\lambda\right)\psi(2^{j}t-q)\psi(2^{j}s-\tau).

This leads to:

\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{jq}\alpha_{j\tau}]=2^{-j}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f_{Z}(\lambda)|\hat{\psi}(2^{-j}\lambda)|^{2}e^{i2^{-j}(q-\tau)\lambda}d\lambda.

Thus, for
\displaystyle j=J
, the spectral density of
\displaystyle\alpha_{jq}
is given by:

f_{\alpha}^{(J)}(\lambda)=2^{-J}f_{Z}(\lambda)|\hat{\psi}(2^{-J}\lambda)|^{2},\quad\lambda\in[-\pi,\pi].

(3.8)

Combining Equations (
3.7
) and (
3.8
),
[
10
]
derives the approximate Gaussian spectral representation of the discrete wavelet transform around frequency zero:

f_{w}(\lambda)=C_{J}|\lambda|^{-2(d-\nu)}f_{u}(\lambda)h(\lambda)\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\to 0,\quad\text{for}\quad d\in(0,0.5),

(3.9)

where
\displaystyle C_{J}=2^{-J(1+2\nu)}
term and
\displaystyle h(\lambda)=b^{2}(\lambda)
.

This spectral representation (
3.9
) provides a foundation for the semi-parametric estimation of
\displaystyle d
. The spectral density
\displaystyle f_{w}(\lambda)
behaves as
\displaystyle|\lambda|^{-2(d-v)}
around frequency zero, thus
\displaystyle f_{w}(\lambda)\sim|\lambda|^{-2(d-1)}
when the Haar wavelet is used. The functions
\displaystyle f_{u}(\lambda)
and
\displaystyle h(\lambda)
originate from the short-term dependence in
\displaystyle\left\{Z_{t}\right\}
and the discrete wavelet transform, respectively. Note that these two functions are even, continuous over
\displaystyle[-\pi,\pi]
, and non-zero at
\displaystyle\lambda=0
.

In his work,
[
10
]
extends the wavelet-based log-periodogram estimation method, drawing inspiration from
[
9
]
to analyze its asymptotic properties. The approach constructs a periodogram for the wavelet transform at scale
\displaystyle j
, defined as:

I_{k}=I_{k}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{2\pi n}\sum_{q=0}^{2^{J}-1}\left|w_{jq}\exp(i\lambda_{k}q)\right|^{2},\quad k=1,2,\dots,m,

where
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\frac{2\pi k}{n}
represents the associated frequencies.

Under the spectral representation
\displaystyle f_{w}(\lambda)
given in (
3.9
) and by letting
\displaystyle s(\lambda)=f_{u}(\lambda)h(\lambda)
,
[
10
]
reformulates the log-periodogram regression as follows:

\log I_{k}=\alpha+\gamma X_{k}+\log\left[\frac{s(\lambda_{k})}{s(0)}\right]+\varepsilon_{k},\quad k=1,2,\dots,m,

(3.10)

where:

\alpha=\log C_{J}+\log s(0),\quad\gamma=d-1,\quad X_{k}=-2\log(\lambda_{k}),\quad\varepsilon_{k}=\log\left[\frac{I_{k}}{f_{w}(\lambda_{k})}\right].

The wavelet-based log-periodogram estimator, denoted as
\displaystyle\hat{d}_{WBLP}
, is obtained by applying a logarithmic transformation to equation (
3.10
). Specifically, it involves performing a regression of the transformed log-periodogram,
\displaystyle\log I_{k}
, on the regressors
\displaystyle-2\log(\lambda_{k})
for
\displaystyle k=1,2,\dots,m
, and then adding one to the estimate:

\hat{d}_{WBLP}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right)\log I_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right)^{2}}+1,

where
\displaystyle\overline{X}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}.

In this regression, the term
\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{s(\lambda_{k})}{s(0)}\right)=\log\left(\frac{f_{u}(\lambda_{k})}{f_{u}(0)}\right)+\log\left(\frac{h(\lambda_{k})}{h(0)}\right)
plays a critical role in the asymptotic bias. Through a Taylor expansion around
\displaystyle\lambda=0
, this term is approximately expressed as:

\log\left(\frac{s(\lambda_{k})}{s(0)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{s^{\prime\prime}(0)}{s(0)}\lambda_{k}^{2}+O(\lambda_{k}^{4}).

Under Assumptions 1, 2, and 4,
[
10
]
demonstrates that:

\displaystyle(a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{d}_{WBLP}-d]=-\frac{2\pi^{2}}{9}\frac{s^{\prime\prime}(0)}{s(0)}\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}(1+o(1))+O\left(\frac{m^{4}}{n^{4}}\right)+O\left(\frac{\log^{3}m}{m}\right),

\displaystyle(b)

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{d}_{WBLP})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{24m}+o\left(\frac{1}{m}\right).

These results show that the wavelet-based log-periodogram estimator
\displaystyle\hat{d}_{WBLP}
is consistent for
\displaystyle d\in(0,0.5)
, and that its variance retains a form similar to that observed in the stationary Gaussian case (for more details, see
[
9
]
).

The mean squared error (
MSE
) for the estimation of the parameter
\displaystyle\hat{d}_{WBLP}
is given by:

\text{MSE}(\hat{d}_{WBLP})=\left(\frac{2\pi^{2}}{9}\frac{s^{\prime\prime}(0)}{s(0)}\right)^{2}\frac{m^{4}}{n^{4}}(1+o(1))+O\left(\frac{m^{3}\log^{3}m}{n^{4}}\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{24m}(1+o(1)).

By exploiting this expression, the optimal rate for
\displaystyle m
is determined as:

m^{*}=\left[0.4634\cdot\left(\frac{s(0)}{s^{\prime\prime}(0)}\right)^{2/5}n^{4/5}\right],

where
\displaystyle[x]
denotes the integer part of
\displaystyle x
.

Although the terms
\displaystyle s(0)
and
\displaystyle s^{\prime\prime}(0)
are unknown, this relation implies that:

\text{MSE}(\hat{d}_{WBLP})=O(n^{-4/5}),

which corresponds to the same convergence rate as that of the
\displaystyle\hat{d}_{GPH}
estimator in the stationary case.

[
10
]
also establishes that, under the assumption
\displaystyle m=O(n^{4/5})
, the estimator
\displaystyle\hat{d}_{WBLP}
follows an asymptotic normal distribution:

\sqrt{m}(\hat{d}_{WBLP}-d)\xrightarrow{D}\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\pi^{2}}{24})\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

These results, based on the works of
[
9
]
and
[
1
]
, demonstrate the relevance of this approach in the context of semi-parametric estimation for long-memory time series.

After examining the method proposed by
[
10
]
, which uses the wavelet transform to refine the estimation of long-memory parameters, we now turn to an alternative approach aimed at improving this estimation.

4. Log Regression via A New Periodogram

In this section, we propose a new periodogram along with an innovative method for estimating the long-memory parameter in the wavelet domain. This method aims to improve the estimation of this parameter in long-memory stochastic volatility (LMSV) models. We will denote our periodogram by NKK periodogram and our estimator by NKK estimator.

4.1. NKK Periodogram

Consider a time series
\displaystyle\{Y_{1},...,Y_{n}\}
, where
\displaystyle n
denotes the sample size. By applying the discrete wavelet transform to
\displaystyle\left\{Y_{t}\right\}
, we obtain the wavelet coefficients given by formula (
3.6
). The periodogram for the wavelet transform at scale
\displaystyle j
, introduced by
[
10
]
, is defined as:

I_{k}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{2\pi n}\sum_{q=0}^{2^{J}-1}\left|w_{jq}\exp(i\lambda_{k}q)\right|^{2},\quad k=1,2,\dots,m,

where
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\frac{2\pi k}{n}
. The integers
\displaystyle j
and
\displaystyle q
represent the scale and translation parameters, respectively, where
\displaystyle j=0,1,\dots,J
and
\displaystyle q=0,1,\dots,2^{j}-1
. The finest (maximum) scale is fixed at
\displaystyle J
, where
\displaystyle n=2^{J}
.

This periodogram
\displaystyle I_{k}^{(j)}
at scale
\displaystyle j
can also be expressed in a linear regression framework as follows:

I_{k}^{(j)}=\frac{n}{8\pi}\|\tilde{\beta}^{(j)}(\lambda_{k})\|^{2},

where
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean (
\displaystyle\ell_{2}
) norm of vectors, and
\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}^{(j)}(\lambda_{k})
represents the solution to the classical least squares problem:

\tilde{\beta}^{(j)}(\lambda_{k})=\arg\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\sum_{q=0}^{2^{J}-1}\left|w_{jq}-h_{q}^{\top}(\lambda_{k})\beta\right|^{2},

with
\displaystyle h_{q}(\lambda_{k})=[\cos(\lambda_{k}q),\sin(\lambda_{k}q)]^{\top}
as the harmonic regressor vector.

To improve the robustness compared to the periodogram proposed by
[
10
]
, we suggest replacing the least squares criterion with the least absolute deviations (LAD) criterion, as done by
[
11
]
with the ordinary periodogram.
Thus, the new regression coefficient is expressed as:

\hat{\beta}_{LAD}^{(j)}(\lambda_{k})=\arg\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\sum_{q=0}^{2^{J}-1}\left|w_{jq}-h_{q}^{\top}(\lambda_{k})\beta\right|.

Definition 4.1

Let
\displaystyle\left\{Y_{1},...,Y_{n}\right\}
be a time series of size
\displaystyle n
, and let
\displaystyle\{w_{jq}\}
be the set of coefficients obtained after applying the discrete wavelet transform to this time series, where
\displaystyle j=0,1,\dots,J
denotes the scales and
\displaystyle q=0,1,\dots,2^{j}-1
the translation parameters. The NKK periodogram at scale
\displaystyle j
is defined as:

N_{k}=N_{k}^{(j)}=\frac{n}{8\pi}\|\hat{\beta}_{LAD}^{(j)}(\lambda_{k})\|^{2},

where
\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{LAD}^{(j)}(\lambda_{k})
represents the solution to the LAD minimization problem applied to the wavelet coefficients
\displaystyle\{w_{jq}\}
at scale
\displaystyle j
.

This periodogram is expected to inherit the robustness benefits associated with the LAD regression, as discussed by
[
3
]
.

With the NKK periodogram defined, we now proceed to introduce the NKK estimator derived from it.

4.2. NKK Estimator

In this subsection, we propose a method for estimating the long-memory parameter by combining the log periodogram regression and the NKK periodogram. This approach aims to leverage the robustness of the LAD criterion while exploiting the multi-resolution properties of wavelets.

We adopt the same procedure described in
[
10
]
, but replace the wavelet-based periodogram
\displaystyle I_{k}^{(j)}
with the NKK periodogram
\displaystyle N_{k}^{(j)}
at scale
\displaystyle j
. By substituting this modification into Equation (
3.10
), we obtain:

\log N_{k}=\alpha+\gamma X_{k}+\log\left[\frac{s\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}{s\left(0\right)}\right]+\varepsilon^{N}_{k},\quad k=1,2,\dots,m,

where:

\alpha=\log C_{J}+\log s(0),\quad\gamma=d-1,\quad X_{k}=-2\log(\lambda_{k}),\quad\varepsilon^{N}_{k}=\log\left[\frac{N_{k}}{f_{w}(\lambda_{k})}\right].

We establish the necessary assumption for defining our estimator.

Assumption 4.1

(a)

For low frequencies, the term
\displaystyle\log\left[\frac{s\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}{s\left(0\right)}\right]
is negligible.

(b)

The random variables
\displaystyle\log\left[\frac{N_{k}}{f_{w}(\lambda_{k})}\right]
,
\displaystyle k=1,2,\dots,m
, are asymptotically
\displaystyle i.i.d.

We now give on estimator in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1

Assuming that Assumptions
2.1
,
3.1
, and
4.1
hold, the NKK estimator
\displaystyle\hat{d}_{NKK}
is given by:

\hat{d}_{NKK}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right)\log N_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right)^{2}}+1,

where
\displaystyle\overline{X}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}
.

Proof 4.1

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
3.1
, the approximate Gaussian spectral representation of the discrete wavelet transform around the zero frequency is given by Equation (
3.9
):

f_{w}(\lambda)=C_{J}|\lambda|^{-2(d-\nu)}f_{u}(\lambda)h(\lambda)\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\to 0,\quad\text{for}\quad d\in(0,0.5),

where
\displaystyle C_{J}=2^{-J(1+2\nu)}
and
\displaystyle h(\lambda)=b^{2}(\lambda)
.

Using the Haar wavelet (
\displaystyle\nu=1
) in the wavelet transform, and letting
\displaystyle s(\lambda)=f_{u}(\lambda)h(\lambda)
, we apply the logarithm to Equation (
3.9
) to obtain:

\log f_{w}\left(\lambda\right)=\log C_{J}+\log s\left(0\right)-2(d-1)\log\left(\lambda\right)+\log\left[\frac{s\left(\lambda\right)}{s\left(0\right)}\right].

(4.1)

Adding
\displaystyle\log N_{k}
, evaluated at the Fourier frequencies
\displaystyle\lambda_{k}
for
\displaystyle k=1,2,\dots,m
, to both sides of Equation (
4.1
), we obtain:

\log N_{k}=\log C_{J}+\log s\left(0\right)-2(d-1)\log\left(\lambda_{k}\right)+\log\left[\frac{s\left(\lambda_{k}\right)}{s\left(0\right)}\right]+\log\left[\frac{N_{k}}{f_{w}(\lambda_{k})}\right].

(4.2)

Under Assumption
4.1
, we can rewrite (
4.2
) as a linear regression equation:

\log N_{k}=\alpha+\gamma X_{k}+\varepsilon^{N}_{k},\quad k=1,2,\dots,m,

where:

\alpha=\log C_{J}+\log s(0),\quad\gamma=d-1,\quad Y_{k}=\log N_{k},\quad X_{k}=-2\log(\lambda_{k}),\quad\varepsilon^{N}_{k}=\log\left[\frac{N_{k}}{f_{w}(\lambda_{k})}\right].

Solving this regression using least squares yields the following estimator for
\displaystyle\gamma
:

\hat{\gamma}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right)(Y_{k}-\overline{Y}_{m})}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right)^{2}},

where
\displaystyle\overline{X}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}
and
\displaystyle\overline{Y}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}Y_{k}
.

Notice that in this expression,
\displaystyle\overline{Y}_{m}
represents the mean of
\displaystyle\log N_{k}
. A key property of centered deviations is that the sum of deviations from the mean is always zero:

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right)=0.

Using this property, we can expand the numerator of
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}
as follows:

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right)(Y_{k}-\overline{Y}_{m})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}Y_{k}(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m})-\overline{Y}_{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right).

Since
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right)=0
, the second term in the expansion becomes zero. Thus, the numerator simplifies to:

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}\right)(Y_{k}-\overline{Y}_{m})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}Y_{k}(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m}).

Substituting this result back into the expression for
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}
, we obtain:

\hat{\gamma}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m})\log N_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}(X_{k}-\overline{X}_{m})^{2}}.

Therefore, the final estimator for the memory parameter is given by:

\hat{d}_{NKK}=\hat{\gamma}+1.

Having defined the NKK periodogram and its corresponding estimator, we now proceed to an empirical evaluation of its performance through numerical simulations, comparing it to existing methods.

6. Concluding remarks

In this paper, we explored new perspectives for estimating the long-memory parameter
\displaystyle d
by introducing the NKK periodogram, a robust extension of the wavelet-based log-periodogram. This method is based on least absolute deviations applied to wavelet coefficients, offering improved resistance to non-Gaussian noise spectra and enhancing estimation accuracy.

The simulation results highlighted the superiority of the NKK estimator over existing approaches, particularly the GPH estimator of
[
7
]
and the WBLP estimator proposed by
[
10
]
. The NKK estimator stands out for its robustness and precision over a wide range of frequencies, especially in contexts where noise presence can undermine the performance of classical methods.

The integration of wavelets, thanks to their multi-resolution properties and their frequency and time localization, opens new perspectives for the analysis of complex time series, allowing for better modeling of long memory while mitigating the impact of perturbations and complex dynamics.
