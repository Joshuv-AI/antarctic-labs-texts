Liposomal bupivacaine versus ropivacaine in four-in-one nerve block for total knee arthroplasty: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Meirong Wang, Jing Li, Xiao Wu, Jiaming Dai, Wenlue Wu, Chuiliang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective postoperative pain control following total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is essential to enhance for early functional recovery. Liposomal bupivacaine (LB) is a sustained-release formulation of bupivacaine is used to prolong analgesia after regional nerve block. This study evaluated the analgesic efficacy of a single injection of LB for a four-in-one nerve block after TKA.MethodsBetween September 2023 and July 2024, 120 patients aged 65–80 years undergoing unilateral TKA under spinal anesthesia were randomized into two groups: LB (n = 60) and RP (ropivacaine, n = 60). This was a randomized, single-blinded trial. In the post-anesthesia care unit, a four-in-one nerve block was performed under ultrasound guidance using either LB or RP. The primary endpoint was the visual analog scale pain score at rest (VAS-R) at 72 h postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included the visual analog scale pain score during movement (VAS-M) over 72 h, opioid consumption on Post Operative Day 1–3 (POD1-3), muscle strength (Medical Research Council [MRC] scale) over 72 h, range of motion (ROM), and length of hospital stay (LOS).ResultsOver time, VAS-R and VAS-M from 24 to 72 h postoperatively were significantly lower in the LB group compared with the RP group (P < 0.001), effect size analysis revealed moderate effects (r = 0.53–0.88). But the effects in VAS-R and VAS-M within 12 h postoperatively and the VAS-R AUC over 72 h revealed small effects (r = 0.20–0.45). No opioid consumption was recorded in either group on POD 1, whereas opioid consumption was lower in the LB group on POD2 and POD3 (P < 0.001). Knee ROM and Medical Research Council (MRC) grades were higher in the LB group during the first 72 h postoperatively (P < 0.001). LOS did not differ between groups (P = 0.980). Prolonged numbness occurred in 5 patients (8.3%) in the LB group and in none of the RP group, although the between-group difference was not statistically significant.ConclusionIn patients undergoing four-in-one nerve block after TKA, a single injection of LB provided statistically significant but modest improvement in analgesia, particularly between 24 and 72 h postoperatively. LB was also associated with lower opioid consumption on POD2-3 and better early functional recovery. However, the clinical significance of these findings remains uncertain.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2500102073. Reg Date 08/05/2025. Introduction

Acute postoperative pain after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a common and clinical problem. Persistent severe pain can impair the knee function, delay recovery, prolong hospital stay, and increase the risk of immobility-related complications such as deep venous thrombosis (DVT) [1, 2]. Multimodal analgesia, including oral analgesics, regional nerve block, local infiltration and patient-controlled analgesia, is widely used after TKA [3]. Among these strategies, peripheral nerve block is regarded as an important component because it can reduce opioid use and facilitate postoperative recovery [4, 5]. In our preliminary study, we found that four-in-one nerve block could preserve quadriceps strength while providing adequate analgesia after TKA without injuring surrounding neurovascular structures [6].

Continuous peripheral nerve block can prolong postoperative analgesia by providing sustained local anesthetic delivery [7]. However, it is associated with multiple complications, including catheter dislodgement, obstruction, fluid leakage, and infection, and increases cost or resources [8–10]. Single-injection techniques are simpler, but their duration depends on the anesthetic selected. Ropivacaine (RP) is widely used for regional nerve block, yet its analgesic duration of approximately 7–15 h may be insufficient for postoperative pain control after TKA [11–13].

Liposome bupivacaine (LB) is a prolonged-release multivesicular liposomal formulation of bupivacaine indicated for single-dose administration to provide postsurgical analgesia and reduce opioid use [14]. Its prolonged-release profile is achieved through the DepoFoam® drug delivery technology, in which aqueous chambers containing bupivacaine are encapsulated within multivesicular phospholipid bilayers [15–17]. This formulation has been reported to extend analgesic effects to 72–96 h after TKA [16, 18]. Following a prospective randomized controlled trial, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved LB for peripheral nerve block in the interscalene brachial plexus in 2018 [19].

However, several studies have reported no clear clinical advantage of LB in peripheral nerve block or local infiltration settings [20–24], the results are inconsistent and require further validation in the context of TKA. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the analgesic efficacy and early functional recovery of a single-injection four-in-one nerve block using LB versus ropivacaine in patients undergoing TKA, specifically regarding reducing pain intensity (VAS scores) and opioid consumption within the first 72 h after surgery.

Patients and methods

Participants

Patients aged 65–80 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status II–III and body mass index (BMI) of 18–27 kg/m² who underwent TKA were assessed for eligibility. The exclusion criteria included neurological or neuromuscular diseases, allergic to local anesthetics or opioids, severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, liver and kidney dysfunction, contraindications to regional blockade (abnormal coagulation function, local skin infection, neurological deficit in the target area), chronic analgesic therapy, psychological disorders, communication disorders, and mental illness. Patients who refused to participate were also excluded. A total of 120 eligible patients provided written informed consent and were randomly assigned to Group LB or Group RP.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was achieved through the random numbers generated by IBM SPSS Statistics, version 24.0. The random numbered, opaque, sealed envelope was opened by the nurse who prepared the study syringes. The trial was single blind. Because the study drugs differed in appearance, the anesthesiologist performing the nerve block was aware of treatment allocation. Patients, postoperative outcome assessors, and data analysts were strictly blinded to group allocation.

Interventions

All patients received spinal anesthesia intraoperatively with 0.75% ropivacaine 0.3 mg/kg at L3-4. A standard multimodal pain management protocol was followed for all patients. It included an intraoperative cocktail of ketorolac 30 mg, adrenaline 1 mg, and tranexamic 1.0 g. Patients also received intravenous flurbiprofen 50 mg at the end of surgery and 1 mg/kg every 12 h during the first 72 postoperative hours. A four-in-one nerve block was performed postoperatively in the post-anesthesia care unit under ultrasound guidance at the mid-thigh level. The block targeted the saphenous nerve (SaN), sciatic nerve (SN), nerve to vastus medialis (NVM), and medial femoral cutaneous nerve (MFCN) through one approach using an 18-gauge needle. The SN was identified under the deep surface of the adductor magnus muscle with a low-frequency convex array probe (5–2 MHz). The needle was then withdrawn to the medial femoral plane, and the remaining nerves were sequentially blocked with a high-frequency convex array probe (15–8 MHz): the NVM, the SaN within the adductor canal, and the MFCN. When anatomical structures were unclear, nerve stimulation was used to confirm nerve identification (Fig. 1). In Group LB, LB 266 mg (20 mL, No: 230215BL, Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd) was diluted with 20 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride to a total volume of 40 mL, and 10 mL was injected per nerve. In Group RP, 13 mL of 0.75% ropivacaine (97.5 mg) was diluted with 27 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride to a total volume of 40 mL, and 10 mL was injected per nerve. Procedures were performed by one anesthesiologist who had at least 100 successful four-in-one nerve block before study participation. This anesthesiologist did not participate in postoperative assessment.

Postoperative pain intensity was assessed with an 11-point visual analog scale (VAS), where 0 indicated no pain, 1–3 mild pain, 4–6 moderate pain, and 7–10 breakthrough pain. Oral oxycodone 5 mg was given every 6 h for moderate pain (VAS 4–6), and 10 mg was given as needed for breakthrough pain (VAS rated 7 to 10), with a maximum daily dose of 60 mg in 24 h. After the first rescue dose, additional analgesics were permitted according to the institutional pain-management protocol. The physical therapeutic exercise was performed by one physician for all the included patients after surgery on Post Operative Day (POD) 1 until discharge.

Primary and Secondary Study Outcomes

Demographic factors including age, sex, BMI, and clinical history, baseline pain scores and ROM were collected during preoperative assessment visit. Data were collected the day before surgery and during the first 72 h after the single-shot nerve block. The primary outcome of this study was VAS pain score at rest (VAS-R) at 72 h postoperatively. This endpoint was chosen to reflect the prolonged-release profile of LB and to determine whether any analgesic benefit persisted after the expected duration of single-shot RP. Secondary outcomes included VAS-R at 2, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48 h; VAS pain score at mobilization (VAS-M) at 2, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72 h post-surgery; opioid consumption of oxycodone (each containing oxycodone 5 mg and acetaminophen 325 mg) at 24-h intervals on POD1, POD2, and POD3. The muscle strength during active knee extension and flexion against gravity was assessed during the first 72 h postoperatively with the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale (0: no muscle contraction, 1: flicker or trace of muscle contraction and inability to move joints, 2: have muscle contraction and active movement, with gravity eliminated, 3: active movement, against gravity, but not against resistance, 4: active movement, against gravity and resistance, 5: normal power) [25]. An MRC grade ≥ 3 was considered functionally acceptable because it indicates the ability to perform active movement against gravity [26]. ROM was measured every 24 h from the POD1 with Continuous Passive Motion System (CPM), which has been demonstrated to be reliable for postoperative rehabilitation exercise [25]. Patients’ and surgeons’ satisfaction were measured with 5 grades (0: extremely dissatisfied, 1: dissatisfied, 2: basically satisfied, 3: very satisfied, 4: extremely satisfied). Adverse events, including nausea, vomiting, postoperative falls and neurological complications, along with length of hospital stay (LOS), were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics, version 24.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared with the unpaired t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables and ordinal variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and were compared with the Mann-Whitney test. Categorical variables are presented as number (%) and were compared with the chi-square test. Repeated outcome variables were compared between groups with generalized estimating equations (GEE). Bonferroni correction was applied for repeated group comparisons as appropriate. The area under the curve (AUC) for VAS-R, VAS-M, and muscle strength over 72 h was calculated using the trapezoidal rule. Opioid consumption was standardized to morphine milligram equivalents (MME) (oral oxycodone 1 mg = 1.5 mg oral MME). Effect sizes were calculated according to data type. For normally distributed continuous variables, Cohen’s d was used, with d = 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, moderate, and large effects, respectively. For non-normally distributed continuous variables and ordinal variables, r = |Z|/√N was used, with r = 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 representing small, moderate, and large effects, respectively. For binary variables, the phi coefficient (φ) was used, with the same interpretation as r. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Sample size was calculated using PASS 11.0 based on pilot data (n = 10 per group), which showed a mean difference of 0.70 points in VAS-R at 72 h postoperatively (LB: 2.10 ± 1.37 vs. RP: 2.80 ± 1.03; pooled SD = 1.21), which was the primary outcome. Using a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, the required sample size was 47 patients per group. After accounting for a 20% dropout rate, the final sample size was set at 60 patients per group (120 patients in total).

Results

Patients’ data at baseline

A total of 126 patients were assessed for eligibility. Six patients were excluded: three patients declined participation, two had liver and kidney dysfunction, and one had taken opioids after inclusion. Consequently, 120 eligible patients signed the informed consent and were randomized. One patient in the RP group developed deep vein thrombosis (DVT) postoperatively and was excluded from the analgesic efficacy analysis due to the potential confounding effect of DVT-related pain. Therefore, 60 patients in the LB group and 59 patients in the RP group were included in the final analysis based on a per-protocol principle (Fig. 2). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally balanced between groups, with small standardized mean differences (SMD) < 0.3 (Table 1).

Analgesia

For the primary endpoint, VAS-R at 72 h postoperatively was lower in the LB group compared to the RP group [M(IQR): 2.0 (1, 2) vs. 3.0 (2, 4); median difference (MD) = -1.3, 95%CI: -2.0 to -0.6, adjusted P < 0.001]. GEE analysis revealed significant group-by-time interactions for both VAS-R (Wald chi-square = 240.0, df = 7, P < 0.001) and VAS-M (Wald chi-square = 389.5, df = 7, P < 0.001), indicating that pain trajectories differed over 72 h. Following Bonferroni correction, the LB group demonstrated significantly lower VAS-R scores than the RP group at 2, 8, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h postoperatively (adjusted P < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed at 12 h (adjusted P = 0.232). The effect size for VAS-R and VAS-M were small from 2 to 12 h after surgery (r = 0.20–0.45). Over time, VAS-R and VAS-M from 24 to 72 h postoperatively were significantly lower in the LB group compared with the RP group (P < 0.001), effect size analysis revealed large or moderate effects (r = 0.53–0.88) (Table 2).

ROM and muscle strength

The LB group demonstrated greater knee ROM on POD1, POD2, and POD3 compared to the RP group (adjusted P < 0.001). GEE analysis revealed a significant group-by-time interaction for ROM (Wald χ² = 44.126, df = 3, P < 0.001). Complications and LOS

Four patients (6.8%) in the RP group experienced nausea on POD2 or POD3. Five patients (8.3%) in the LB group were observed prolonged numbness in the SN territory (especially in the lower leg or dorsum of the foot). These symptoms persisted beyond 72 h, but resolved completely within 1 week, and with no significant impact on rehabilitation. Telephone follow-ups were conducted at 2 weeks, 4 weeks, and 3 months after discharge revealed no persistent sensory abnormality. However, the difference between two groups has no statistical significance (P = 0.057). LOS did not differ between groups (P = 0.980). Patient and surgeon satisfaction scores were higher in the LB group (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

Analgesic efficacy of liposomal bupivacaine

This study showed that LB was associated with lower VAS-R and VAS-M from 24 to 72 h postoperatively and lower VAS-M throughout the 72-h observation period. Oral opioid consumption was also lower in the LB group on POD2 and POD3. These findings should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. Several pain outcomes reached statistical significance, but the effect sizes for VAS-R and VAS-M were small from 2 to 12 h, and no opioid benefit was observed on POD1. Taken together, the data support a modest analgesic benefit rather than a broad or clearly superior analgesic effect across the entire early postoperative period. Both groups had satisfactory pain control on POD1, likely due to the multimodal regimen. The clinical value of LB appears to manifest primarily between 24 and 72 h postoperatively. This prolonged effect is consistent with LB’s pharmacokinetic profile, which is characterized by a half-life of 34 h and sustained-release properties lasting up to 96 h, whereas RP has a half-life of only 4 h [27], making it insufficient to cover the intense pain period following TKA. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that pain scores are highest within the first 48 h after TKA surgery, which is similar to the time point at which the most pronounced differences in pain scores were observed in the present study [28]. The literature on LB after TKA remains inconsistent. Favorable findings have been reported with other block strategies, including parasacral ischial plane block, infiltration between the popliteal artery and the posterior knee capsule (IPACK), and adductor canal block (ACB) [29, 30]. However, some studies have reported no clinical relevance [31, 32]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in block type, comparator anesthetic, background multimodal analgesia, and the time window selected for outcome assessment. A phase III trial with no confounding multimodal analgesic interventions demonstrated that LB administered via adductor canal block provided superior analgesia and reduced opioid consumption compared with pure bupivacaine [33]. A meta-analysis showed that the LB technique applied to ACB could provide short-term pain relief on the POD 0–1, which was different from the results of this study [34]. Relatively, the prolonged effects of LB in the present study may be related to the combined blockade of SN, NVM, and MFCN. This broader distribution may have influenced both the duration and the pattern of analgesia. Further investigation is nevertheless required.

Although the concept of minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is often used to contextualize pain outcomes, MCID thresholds are primarily defined at the individual patient level and reflect changes relative to a patient’s own baseline. These thresholds are not universally transferable to between-group comparisons, particularly in acute postoperative pain. Accordingly, applying MCID values to interpret the group differences observed in this study would not be appropriate. We therefore considered clinical relevance in conjunction with effect sizes, opioid use and sensory outcomes.

Volume considerations

LB is available as 266 mg/20 mL vials (1.33% [13.3 mg/mL]), which is the maximum US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved dose [35]. In our study, 266 mg LB was diluted to a total volume of 40 mL (0.67% [6.65 mg/mL]). The RP group received 97.5 mg of ropivacaine diluted to 40 mL (0.24% [2.44 mg/mL]). These concentrations were based on prior studies and local clinical experience [31, 36, 37]. Available evidence suggests that volume expansion does not affect the analgesic effect and pharmacokinetic profile of the same dose, higher concentrations of LB were not associated with lower pain intensity [17, 18, 38], which may be explained by the ceiling effect that may occur when all nociception in the surrounding tissues are exposed to the minimum threshold of bupivacaine required to produce analgesia [38, 39]. As the saline diluent is absorbed, it leaves the liposomes in the tissue as micro-depots that continue to release bupivacaine over time [40]. However, concerning about concentration for the minimum threshold level, additional studies are needed regarding the safety and efficacy of this prolonged-release formulation when administered into the different surgical sites with diverse techniques and patient populations.

Impact on functional recovery

The LB group exhibited significantly better knee ROM and muscle strength during the first three post operative days compared to the RP group. The overall better preservation of muscle strength in the LB group may be attributed to its sustained-release technology. LB maintains a lower effective analgesic concentration over time, which may preferentially block sensory fibers, with minimal motor involvement [41–43]. The sustained analgesia provided by LB enabled patients to participate in rehabilitation exercises earlier and more actively, thereby facilitating the recovery of ROM and muscle strength. Conversely, RP does not exhibit such an effect. The minimum threshold may depend on a number of factors, including whether the mass of drug around local tissue nociceptors reaches the threshold needed to inhibit depolarization [44]. Future studies should investigate the optimal concentration threshold of liposomal bupivacaine that preserves motor function while providing prolonged analgesia.

Complications

Although LB preserved better lower extremity muscle strength within 72 h, which provides with a better rehabilitation for the patients, but we found that 8.3% of LB patients experienced numbness in lower leg or dorsal foot which was innervated by the sensory branch of the SN and lasted for 72 h or more. This phenomenon may be connected with the prolonged effect of LB on a sensory block of SN. Although all patients had no sensory abnormalities before discharge or during follow-up, the combination of prolonged numbness and preserved muscle strength could theoretically increase the risk of falls. A careful balance must be struck between the benefits of extended analgesia and the inconveniences and potential risks of prolonged sensory blockade. Patients should be counseled preoperatively about this risk. Future studies should systematically assess sensory function and fall risk using validated tools. Although motor-sparing alternatives such as IPACK or popliteal plexus block are available for TKA [45], we selected the mid-thigh four-in-one technique (including the SN). This decision was based on the following considerations: the IPACK approach may increase the risk of surgical site infection, surgical dressings limited the popliteal approach, and the SN block provided robust posterior knee analgesia while preserving quadriceps strength. Although this deviates from contemporary ERAS principles, no falls occurred in our study. Currently, prospective clinical studies have applied LB to IPACK and ACB blocks, but no significant clinical benefits have been observed [31]. The incidences of nausea and numbness were higher in the experimental group than in the LB group, the difference was not statistically significant and the effect size was a small effect, which might be related to the small sample size. No subject in our study experienced local anesthetic systemic toxicity, such as bradycardia, hypotension, arrhythmia, or seizure.

Limitations

There were several limitations in our study. First, due to the different physical characteristics of LB and RP, the anesthesiologist performing the block could not be blinded, introducing potential performance bias. However, detection bias was minimized through strict blinding of all outcome assessors to group allocation. Second, one patient in the RP group was excluded due to DVT, resulting in a per-protocol rather than intention-to-treat analysis. Third, based on the pharmacokinetic properties of LB, follow-up data were collected within 72 h postoperatively in our study. Nevertheless, subsequent observations showed that the effect of LB lasted for more than 72 h, further studies are needed to confirm this finding. Fourth, the appropriate doses and concentrations of LB and RP for nerve block in the two groups were based on clinical experience and previous studies [6, 35–37], rather than on pharmacological dose equivalence. This is mainly because of lacking available studies on dose equivalence between LB and RP to inform drug selection. Fifth, motor assessment was incomplete, specifically lacking objective assessment of sciatic-innervated ankle movements (dorsiflexion/plantarflexion), thereby limiting the comprehensive evaluation of sciatic nerve motor function. Future research should explore the application of LB in more modern, motor-sparing block techniques. Sixth, no significant difference in LOS, as discharge decisions are influenced by multiple non-medical factors beyond pain control and function. Future studies should address these limitations through double blinding, comprehensive motor assessment, and extended follow-up. Seventh, although some differences were statistically significant, the magnitude of benefit for several pain outcomes was modest, and the overall clinical significance remains uncertain. Furthermore, the trial was retrospectively registered, which may increase the risk of reporting bias. We acknowledge that prospective registration would have been preferable to ensure full transparency. Finally, we did not perform a cost-effectiveness analysis. While LB is substantially more expensive than ropivacaine, the LB group also required less rescue opioid medication on POD2 and POD3. Therefore, the net cost-effectiveness of LB remains uncertain and requires dedicated cost-effectiveness analysis in future studies.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing TKA with a four-in-one nerve block, a single injection of LB provided modest improvements in pain control between 24 and 72 h postoperatively, with lower opioid consumption on POD2-3 and better early functional recovery. However, the clinical significance of these findings remains uncertain.
