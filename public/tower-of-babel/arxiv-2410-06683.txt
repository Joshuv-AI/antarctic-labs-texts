Barter Exchange with Bounded Trading Cycles
Yuval Emek, Matan-El Shpiro

Abstract
Consider a barter exchange problem over a finite set of agents, where each agent owns an item and is also associated with a (privately known) wish list of items belonging to the other agents. An outcome of the problem is a (re)allocation of the items to the agents such that each agent either keeps her own item or receives an item from her (reported) wish list, subject to the constraint that the length of the trading cycles induced by the allocation is up-bounded by a prespecified length bound k k . The utility of an agent from an allocation is 1 1 if she receives an item from her (true) wish list and 0 0 if she keeps her own item (the agent incurs a large dis-utility if she receives an item that is neither hers nor belongs to her wish list). In this paper, we investigate the aforementioned barter exchange problem from the perspective of mechanism design without money, aiming for truthful (and individually rational) mechanisms whose objective is to maximize the social welfare . As the construction of a social welfare maximizing allocation is computationally intractable for length bounds k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , this paper focuses on (computationally efficient) truthful mechanisms that approximate the (combinatorially) optimal social welfare. We also study a more general version of the barter exchange problem, where the utility of an agent from participating in a trading cycle of length 2 ≤ ℓ ≤ k 2\leq\ell\leq k is λ ⁡ ( ℓ ) \lambda(\ell) , where λ \lambda is a general (monotonically non-increasing) length function . Our results include upper and lower bounds on the guaranteed approximation ratio, expressed in terms of the length bound k k and the length function λ \lambda . On the technical side, our main contribution is an algorithmic tool that can be viewed as a truthful version of the local search paradigm. As it turns out, this tool can be applied to more general (bounded size) coalition formation problems.

Barter markets, where players exchange goods without involving any payments,

Modern forms of barter markets include the extensively studied kidney exchange
market

,

[

(cf.
[

).

In the current paper, we study an abstract game theoretic model for barter
exchange that was introduced by Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver

[
RSÜ05
]
and studied further by Hatfield

[

]
and in a follow up paper of Roth et al.
[

]
, as well as serving as the cornerstone for various
other game theoretic models studied subsequently (refer to

12

This model is defined over a set of agents, each holding an item that she
wishes to exchange for one item from a certain subset of items held by the
other agents.
1
1

1

Roth et al. use kidney exchange terminology in their model description.
We prefer the more “neutral” agent-item terminology since we do not regard
kidney exchange as this paper’s main application.

The agents report their item wish lists to a mechanism that determines the
outcome in the form of a collection of trading cycles, effectively
reallocating the items among the agents.

A fundamental characteristic of the model of
[
RSÜ05
]

is the

\{0,1\}
-preferences
assumption, stating that the agents are indifferent about which item they
receive as long as this item belongs to their wish list.
Specifically, an agent that ends up with an item from her wish list enjoys a
unit utility, whereas her utility is zero if she retains her original item
(Roth et al. assume that the agent cannot receive a non-wished item).
Among other aspects of this barter exchange model, Roth et al.
[
RSÜ05
]
investigate the design of truthful (strategy proof)
mechanisms with the objective of maximizing the social welfare.

Motivated by real-life difficulties in implementing long trading cycles, the
authors of

[
RSÜ05
,
Hat05
,
RSÜ07
]

advocate for mechanisms that generate trading cycles of bounded length.
The extreme case of trading cycles that consist of (exactly) two agents
reduces the mechanism designer’s task to that of constructing a maximum
matching in an undirected graph
[
RSÜ05
]
, a classic
optimization problem that admits computationally efficient algorithms.
As demonstrated in
[
RSÜ07
]
though, there is a significant
social welfare gain in allowing slightly longer trading cycles, thus raising
the challenge of designing truthful mechanisms for the
k
-barter
exchange
problem, namely, the problem of constructing a collection of trading
cycles that maximizes the number of participating agents (and thus also the
social welfare) under the constraint that each trading cycle is of length at
most
k
, for small constants

k\geq 3
.

Example
.

For a concrete application, consider a home swap platform where users
register their home for specific vacation dates and report on their wish list
over other homes registered for the same dates.
The platform then determines an exchange that speciﬁes an assignment of the
users to vacation homes.
2
2

2

Although a more general abstraction of the home swap application may include
ordinal utilities or heterogeneous cardinal utilities, we feel that the

\{0,1\}
-preferences
assumption is reasonable also for this application, where users will probably
refrain from including a home in their wish list unless it perfectly fits
their needs.

A downside of this application is that a single user that defects and stays
in her own home leads to a chain of cancellations affecting all participants
of the corresponding trading cycle.
Since users are likely to be highly upset by such forced cancellations,
especially if they happen at the last minute, it makes sense for the
platform to bound the maximum length
k
of the trading cycles, thus hedging
the reputation loss resulting from unsatisﬁed users.

Of particular interest is the

k=3

case that comes with the desirable feature that each user is acquainted with
all other users in her trading cycle (they are assigned to her home or she is
assigned to theirs).
In this case, a last minute cancellation is attributed to a person the user is
actually aware of (rather than some obscure defector up the cancellation
chain), hopefully preventing resentment towards the platform.

Unfortunately, as proved by Abraham, Blum, and Sandholm

[
ABS07
]
, solving the aforementioned
k
-barter exchange
problem optimally is computationally intractable for any

k\geq 3
,
irrespective of truthfulness considerations.
Consequently, the goal of this paper is to design efficient truthful
mechanisms for the
k
-barter exchange problem,

k\geq 3
,
that approximate the (combinatorially) optimal solution.
Our main technical contribution is a novel framework for the design of
truthful versions of
local search
algorithms for a related graph
theoretic problem.
Employing this framework, we develop the first efficient truthful
k
-barter
exchange mechanism with a non-trivial approximation ratio for every constant

k\geq 3
.

While the

\{0,1\}
-preferences
assumption of
[
RSÜ05
]
states that an agent has no priorities
over the items in her wish list, it is often natural to assume that she
prefers to receive a wished item through shorter trading cycles over longer
ones (up to the length bound
k
).
Indeed, the longer is the trading cycle, the higher is the risk for the
exchange to break down due to defecting participants (cf.
[
DPS19
]
).
Moreover, a trading cycle of length
2
has the unique
“accountability advantage” (in the aforementioned home swap application,
this means that you stay in the home of the person who stays in your home)
which is lost with longer trading cycles, so it is reasonable to assume that
agents would prioritize trading cycles of length
2
even if longer trading
cycles are allowed.

Motivated by these insights, we wish to extend the scope of our research to a
more general barter exchange setting where each agent is associated with a
non-increasing
length function

\lambda:\{2,3,\dots,k\}\rightarrow(0,1]

so that her utility from receiving a wished item
via a trading cycle of length

2\leq\ell\leq k

is
\lambda(\ell)
.
As a first step in this direction, we assume that the same length function

\lambda
is shared by all agents, introducing the

(k,\lambda)
-barter
exchange
problem.
It turns out that the combinatorial aspect of this problem remains intractable
for any length function
\lambda
, thus we still aim for approximate
solutions.
Relying on the aforementioned local search framework, we develop efficient
truthful

(k,\lambda)
-barter
exchange mechanisms with a non-trivial approximation ratio for any choice of
function
\lambda
.
On the negative side, we establish approximability lower bounds, showing that
the dependency of our mechanisms’ approximation ratio on
\lambda
is
optimal.

1.1
Model and Problem

Consider a collection of

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

agents indexed by the integers in
[n]
.
A
wish list
of agent

i\in[n]

is a subset

\mathit{W}_{i}\subseteq[n]-\{i\}

of the other agents.
Let

\mathcal{W}_{n}=2^{[n]-\{1\}}\times\cdots\times 2^{[n]-\{n\}}

denote the set of all wish list vectors, indexed by the agents in

[n]
.

An
exchange
over
[n]
is a bijection

\pi:[n]\rightarrow[n]
;
we say that an agent

i\in[n]

partakes
in the exchange
\pi
if

\pi(i)\neq i
,
denoting the set of agents that partake in
\pi
by

\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
.
Observe that from a graph theoretic perspective, the exchange
\pi
induces a
collection
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
of disjoint directed cycles on
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
,
referred to as the
trading cycles
of
\pi
, so that an arc

(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\times\mathcal{P}_{\pi}

is included in a trading cycle if and only if

\pi(i)=j
.
3
3

3

Unless stated otherwise, the graph theoretic term cycle refers in this paper
to a simple cycle, i.e., a cycle in which each vertex appears at most once.

Given a wish list vector

\mathit{W}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}
,
we say that the exchange
\pi
, as well as the trading cycles in

\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
,

respect

\mathit{W}
if

\pi(i)\in\mathit{W}_{i}

for each

i\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
.

Given a trading cycle
c
, let

\alpha(c)\subseteq[n]

denote the set of agents that form
c
.
The
length
of
c
is defined as

\operatorname{len}(c)=|\alpha(c)|
.
The
length
of an exchange
\pi
, denoted by
\operatorname{len}(\pi)
,
is defined to be the maximum length of any of its trading cycles, i.e.,

\operatorname{len}(\pi)=\max\{\operatorname{len}(c)\,:\,c\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}\}
.
For an integer

k\geq 2
,
let
\Pi_{n}^{k}
denote the set of all exchanges over
[n]
whose length at
most
k
.
Given a wish list vector

\mathit{W}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}
,
let

\Pi_{\mathit{W}}^{k}\subseteq\Pi_{n}^{k}

denote the set of all exchanges over
[n]
that respect
\mathit{W}
whose length
is at most
k
.
Likewise, let

\mathcal{C}_{\mathit{W}}^{k}=\bigcup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathit{W}}^{k}}\mathcal{C}_{\pi}

denote the set of all trading cycles over
[n]
that respect
\mathit{W}
whose
length is at most
k
.

Given an integral
length bound

k\geq 2
,
an instance of the
k
-barter exchange (
k
-BE)
problem over

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

agents is characterized by a wish list

\mathit{W}^{*}_{i}\subseteq[n]-\{i\}

for each agent

i\in[n]
,
regarded as the agent’s private information.
The outcome space of the
k
-BE problem is the collection

\Pi_{n}^{k}

of exchanges over
[n]
of length at most
k
.
The utility of an agent

i\in[n]

from an exchange

\pi\in\Pi_{n}^{k}

is determined by the utility function

\mathit{u}:[n]\times\Pi_{n}^{k}\rightarrow\{-\infty\}\cup[0,1]

defined so that

\textstyle\mathit{u}(i,\pi)\,=\,\begin{cases}0,&i\notin\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\\
1,&i\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\text{ and }\pi(i)\in\mathit{W}^{*}_{i}\\
-\infty,&i\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\text{ and }\pi(i)\notin\mathit{W}^{*}_{i}\end{cases}

(1)

(cf.
[
RSÜ05
,
AHM15
]
).
This allows us to define the
social welfare
of
\pi
, denoted by

\mathrm{SW}(\pi)
, as the sum of all agent utilities, observing that if

\pi\in\Pi_{\mathit{W}^{*}}^{k}
,
then

\mathrm{SW}(\pi)=|\mathcal{P}_{\pi}|
.

A (deterministic)
mechanism
for the
k
-BE problem is a function
f

that for any

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
maps a given wish list vector

\mathit{W}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}

to an exchange

f(\mathit{W})\in\Pi_{n}^{k}
,
regarding
\mathit{W}_{i}
as the wish list reported to the mechanism by agent

i\in[n]
.
Following
[
RSÜ05
]
(see also
[
AHM15
]
), we
make the simplifying
subset wish list assumption
stating that an
agent’s reported wish list is always a subset of her true wish list, i.e.,

\mathit{W}_{i}\subseteq\mathit{W}^{*}_{i}

for every

i\in[n]
.
4
4

4

In Appendix
A
, we present a simple method that
lifts this assumption using an arbitrarily small amount of randomness.

The mechanism
f
is said to be
truthful
(a.k.a.
strategy
proof
) if for every
wish list vector

\mathit{W}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}

(that satisfies

\mathit{W}_{j}\subseteq\mathit{W}^{*}_{j}

for all

j\in[n]
)
and for every agent

i\in[n]
,
it holds that

\textstyle\mathit{u}(i,f(\mathit{W}))\,\leq\,\mathit{u}\left(i,f\left(\mathit{W}_{-i},\mathit{W}^{*}_{i}\right)\right)\,;

in other words, it is a dominant strategy for agent
i
to report her (full)
true wish list
\mathit{W}^{*}_{i}
to the mechanism.
5
5

5

Given a wish list vector

\mathit{W}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}

and an agent

i\in[n]
,
we adhere to the convention that
\mathit{W}_{-i}
denotes the vector
obtained from
\mathit{W}
by omitting its
i
th coordinate, that is,

\mathit{W}_{-i}=(\mathit{W}_{1},\dots,\mathit{W}_{i-1},\mathit{W}_{i+1},\dots,\mathit{W}_{n})
.
This notation is naturally extended to agent subsets

S\subseteq[n]
,
using
W_{-S}
to denote the vector obtained from
\mathit{W}
by omitting its

i
th coordinate for every

i\in S
.

To ensure that mechanism
f
is
individually rational
, we further
require that
f(\mathit{W})
respects
\mathit{W}

(i.e.,

f(\mathit{W})\in\Pi_{\mathit{W}}^{k}
),
which means that the utility of the agents from
f
is never negative.
Consequently, we can express the social welfare of the mechanism’s outcome as

\textstyle\mathrm{SW}(f(\mathit{W}))\,=\,\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathit{u}(i,f(\mathit{W}))\,=\,\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{f(\mathit{W})}}\operatorname{len}(c)\,=\,|\mathcal{P}_{f(\mathit{W})}|\,.

(2)

We aim for truthful (and individually rational) mechanisms that maximize the
social welfare of the exchanges they produce.
To this end, we say that a truthful
k
-BE mechanism
f
admits

approximation ratio

r\geq 1

if for every

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

and wish list vector

\mathit{W}^{*}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}
,
it is guaranteed that

\textstyle\mathrm{SW}(f(\mathit{W}^{*}))\,\geq\,\frac{1}{r}\cdot\max\left\{\mathrm{SW}(\pi)\,:\,\pi\in\Pi_{\mathit{W}^{*}}^{k}\right\}\,;

that is, the benchmark of the approximation is the optimal exchange of length
at most
k
that respects
\mathit{W}^{*}
.
We typically regard the length bound
k
as a small constant (recall our
particular interest in

k=3
),
whereas the number
n
of agents grows asymptotically.
Our ultimate goal is to design truthful mechanisms whose approximation ratio
is expressed as a function of
k
(i.e., constant approximation) and whose
running time is polynomial in
n
(i.e., they are
computationally
efficient
).

General Length Functions.

As discussed earlier, we also consider a generalization of the
k
-BE problem,
referred to as

(k,\lambda)
-BE
,
where

\lambda:\{2,3,\dots,k\}\rightarrow(0,1]

is a non-increasing
length function
.
This generalization is obtained by redefining the utility function from
(
1
) as

\textstyle\mathit{u}(i,\pi)\,=\,\begin{cases}0,&i\notin\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\\
\lambda(\operatorname{len}(c)),&i\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\,,\pi(i)\in\mathit{W}^{*}_{i}\,,\text{ and $c$ is the trading cycle in $\mathcal{C}_{\pi}$ s.t.\ $i\in\alpha(c)$}\\
-\infty,&i\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}\text{ and }\pi(i)\notin\mathit{W}^{*}_{i}\end{cases}\,.

(3)

Consequently, if

\pi\in\Pi_{\mathit{W}^{*}}^{k}
,
then the social welfare
\mathrm{SW}(\pi)
of
\pi
becomes

\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}}\operatorname{len}(c)\cdot\lambda(\operatorname{len}(c))

and the social welfare expression in
(
2
) is now replaced by

\textstyle\mathrm{SW}(f(\mathit{W}))\,=\,\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathit{u}(i,f(\mathit{W}))\,=\,\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{f(\mathit{W})}}\operatorname{len}(c)\cdot\lambda(\operatorname{len}(c))\,.

(4)

Similarly to the length bound
k
, the images

\lambda(2),\lambda(3),\dots,\lambda(k)

of the length function are also regarded as constants (part of the problem’s
specification).

To see that the

(k,\lambda)
-BE
problem indeed generalizes the
k
-BE problem, simply take the
uniform

length function
\lambda^{u}
defined by setting

\lambda^{u}(\ell)=1

for every

2\leq\ell\leq k
.
In this regard, a length function
\lambda
is
non-uniform
if

\lambda(\ell)>\lambda(\ell^{\prime})

for some

2\leq\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k
.
6
6

6

Note that scaling the images of
\lambda
by a constant factor has no effect
on the approximation ratio.

Unless stated otherwise, we subsequently address the

(k,\lambda)
-BE
problem in its general form that includes both uniform and non-uniform length
functions
\lambda
.
Although the
k
-BE problem is a special case of this more general problem, it
is undoubtedly the most important special case and therefore, receives its own
focus.

1.2
Our Contribution

As observed by Roth et al.
[
RSÜ05
]
, the

2
-BE problem admits an efficient optimal truthful mechanism based on
constructing a maximum size matching (breaking ties consistently) in the
undirected graph

G=([n],E_{\leftrightarrow})

obtained from the reported wish list vector

\mathit{W}\in\mathcal{W}_{n}

by setting

E_{\leftrightarrow}=\{\{i,j\}\mid i\in\mathit{W}_{j}\text{ and }j\in\mathit{W}_{i}\}
.
7
7

7

Notice that when

k=2
,
the length functions
\lambda
have no role as all trading cycles are of the
same length.

Consequently, our attention in the remainder of this paper is devoted to
length bounds

k\geq 3
.

Arguably the simplest efficient

(k,\lambda)
-BE
mechanism is the one we denote by
\mathtt{Greedy}
.
This naive mechanism iterates over the possible trading cycles
c
in
increasing order of length

\operatorname{len}(c)=2,3,\dots,k
,
greedily adding trading cycle
c
to
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
if
c
does not intersect
with any trading cycle already included in
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

\mathtt{Greedy}
is truthful and approximates the

(k,\lambda)
-BE
problem within ratio
k
for every length bound

k\geq 3

and (uniform or non-uniform) length function
\lambda
.

Using the approximation bound of Thm.
1.1
as the
starting point of our journey, this work is guided by the
following two research questions:
Does there exist an efficient truthful mechanism that approximates the

(k,\lambda)
-BE
problem within ratio

r<k
?
If so, how does the approximation ratio
r
depend on the length function

\lambda
?
To answer these questions, we first investigate the uniform length function

\lambda=\lambda^{u}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

The

k
-BE
problem admits an efficient truthful mechanism with approximation ratio

k-1+\epsilon

for every length bound

and constant

.

Forming Small Coalitions.

The

(k,\lambda)
-BE
problem is related to a family of problems whose outcome is a partition of the
agent set
[n]
into clusters, a.k.a.

An algorithm

for the

-maxCGIS

for a discussion of related game

The importance of the INPA property of maxCGIS algorithms, specifically in the
context of the uniform length function, is demonstrated by the following
observation.

Observation 2.4
.

For every length bound

k\geq 3
,
any INPA algorithm for the

-maxCGIS problem is truthful.

Proof.

Let
\mathtt{Alg}
be an INPA algorithm for the

k
-maxCGIS
problem.
Fix some

and consider a
k
-cycle graph

and an agent

i\in[n]
.
If

i\in\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}(G))
,
then

\mathit{u}(i,\mathtt{Alg}(G))=1

and agent

cannot increase her utility by omitting any nodes from

.
So, assume that

and consider a node subset

.
Since
\mathtt{Alg}
is INPA, it follows that

,

.
Therefore,

does not improve her utility by omitting

from

\alpha^{-1}(i)
.
∎

Local Search Algorithms

In this section, we introduce the class of local search

(k,\lambda)
-maxCGIS
algorithms that is pivotal to our work.
We then establish a sufficient condition for the truthfulness of
algorithms in this class in the context of the
k
-maxCGIS problem (i.e., the
special case of

\lambda=\lambda^{u}
).

Definition

(improvement rule, loyal, INPA, efficient)
.

An
improvement rule

r
for the

(k,\lambda)
-maxCGIS
problem is a function that maps a pair

(G,I)
,
consisting of a
k
-cycle graph

G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}
,

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
and an IS
I
in
G
,
either to an IS
I^{\prime}
in
G
satisfying

\mathit{w}(I^{\prime})>\mathit{w}(I)

or to
\bot
.
The improvement rule
r
is said to be
loyal
if

r(G,I)=I^{\prime}\neq\bot

implies that

\alpha(I)\subseteq\alpha(I^{\prime})
.
The improvement rule
r
is said to be
independent of non-partaking
agents (INPA)
if
(1)

r(G,I)=I^{\prime}\neq\bot

implies that

r(G[V-S],I)=I^{\prime}

for every agent

i\in[n]-(\alpha(I)\cup\alpha(I^{\prime}))

and node subset

S\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(i)
;
and
(2)

r(G,I)=\bot

implies that

r(G[V-S],I)=\bot

for every agent

i\in[n]-\alpha(I)

and node subset

S\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(i)
.
Finally, the improvement rule
r
is said to be
efficient
if it admits
a computationally efficient implementation.

In words, the loyal property means that any agent that partakes in the
original IS
I
must also partake in the new IS
I^{\prime}
.
The INPA property means that agents that do not partake in
I
nor in
I^{\prime}

have no impact on the improvement rule’s outcome.
We can now define a local search algorithm over a list of improvement rules.

Definition

(local search algorithm)
.

A
local search
algorithm
\mathtt{Alg}
for the

(k,\lambda)
-maxCGIS
problem is characterized by an (ordered) list

R=(r_{1},\dots,r_{|R|})

of improvement rules.
Given a
k
-cycle graph

G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}
,

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
the (deterministic) algorithm returns the IS

I=\mathtt{Alg}(G)

constructed via the following iterative process:

(1)
set

t\leftarrow 0

and

I^{t}\leftarrow\emptyset
;

(2)
if

r_{j}(G,I^{t})=\bot

for all

1\leq j\leq|R|
,
then return the set

I\leftarrow I^{t}

and halt;

(3)
let
j^{t}
be the smallest

1\leq j\leq|R|

such that

r_{j}(G,I^{t})\neq\bot
;

(4)
set

I^{t+1}\leftarrow r_{j^{t}}(G,I^{t})
,
set

t\leftarrow t+1
,
and go to step (2).

Let
\mathtt{Alg}^{t}(G)
denote the IS
I^{t}
associated with iteration
t
of this
process.

It turns out that improvement rules which are loyal and INPA lead to an INPA
(local search) algorithm.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider a local search algorithm
\mathtt{Alg}
for the

(k,\lambda)
-maxCGIS
problem characterized by a list

R=(r_{1},\dots,r_{|R|})

of improvement rules.
If all improvement rules in
R
are loyal and INPA, then
\mathtt{Alg}
is INPA.
Moreover, if
|R|
is fixed and all improvement rules in
R
are efficient,
then
\mathtt{Alg}
is efficient.

Proof.

Suppose that all improvement rules in
R
are loyal and INPA and assume
towards contradiction that
\mathtt{Alg}
is not INPA which means that there exist

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
a
k
-cycle graph

G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}
,
an agent

i\in[n]-\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}(G))
,
and a node subset

S\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(i)

such that

\mathtt{Alg}(G[V-S])\neq\mathtt{Alg}(G)
.
Let
t
be the smallest integer such that

\mathtt{Alg}^{t}(G[V-S])\neq\mathtt{Alg}^{t}(G)

(
t
is well defined since

\mathtt{Alg}(G[V-S])\neq\mathtt{Alg}(G)
).
Notice that

\mathtt{Alg}^{0}(G[V-S])=\emptyset=\mathtt{Alg}^{0}(G)
;
let
r_{j}
,

1\leq j\leq|R|
,
be the improvement rule whose application to

(G,\mathtt{Alg}^{t-1}(G))

\mathtt{Alg}^{t}(G)
during the application of
\mathtt{Alg}
to
G
.

Recalling that all improvement rules in
R
are loyal, we know that

\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}^{s}(G))\subseteq\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}^{s+1}(G))

for all

.
Since

i\notin\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}(G))
,
it follows that

i\notin\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}^{s}(G))

for all
s
.
In particular, we deduce that

i\notin\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}^{t-1}(G))=\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}^{t-1}(G[V-S]))
.
By the definition of a local search algorithm, we know that

r_{h}(G,\mathtt{Alg}^{t-1}(G))=\bot

for every

1\leq h<j
.
Since the improvement rules

,

1\leq h<j
,
are INPA, it follows that

\textstyle r_{h}(G[V-S],\mathtt{Alg}^{t-1}(G[V-S]))\,=\,r_{h}(G[V-S],\mathtt{Alg}^{t-1}(G))\,=\,\bot

for every

1\leq h<j
.
As

is also INPA and recalling that

\textstyle r_{j}(G[V-S],\mathtt{Alg}^{t-1}(G[V-S]))\,=\,r_{j}(G[V-S],\mathtt{Alg}^{t-1}(G))\,=\,r_{j}(G,\mathtt{Alg}^{t-1}(G))\,=\,\mathtt{Alg}^{t}(G)\,.

But this means that

\mathtt{Alg}^{t}(G[V-S])=\mathtt{Alg}^{t}(G)
,
deriving a contradiction.
Therefore,
\mathtt{Alg}
is INPA.

Now, suppose that
|R|
is fixed and all improvement rules in
R
are
efficient.
To deduce that
\mathtt{Alg}
is efficient, we argue that the application
of
\mathtt{Alg}
to any

-cycle graph

for all

.
Since the weight of any IS

in
G
can be expressed as

\mathit{w}(I)=x\cdot c
,
where
x
is a positive integer and

is a constant in

(0,1]
,
it follows that

\mathit{w}(\mathtt{Alg}^{t+1}(G))-\mathit{w}(\mathtt{Alg}^{t}(G))\geq\Omega(1)

for all

.
The argument is completed since

and since Obs.
2.3
implies that

\mathit{w}(\mathtt{Alg}(G))\leq nk
.
∎

Note that if we omit the requirement that all improvement rules in

are

\mathtt{Alg}

\mathtt{Alg}

\mathtt{Alg}

2.4

.

Corollary 3.2
.

Consider a local search algorithm
\mathtt{Alg}
for the

k
-maxCGIS
problem characterized by a fixed length list

If all improvement rules in
R
are loyal, INPA, and efficient, then
\mathtt{Alg}
is
truthful and efficient.

then reducing
\alpha^{-1}(i)
to

\alpha^{-1}(i)-S

for some node subset

S\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(i)

only decrease the collection of candidates from which

r_{E}(G,I)

is selected.
Therefore,
(1)
if

r_{E}(G,I)=I^{\prime}\neq\bot

and

i\notin\alpha(I^{\prime})
,
then

r_{E}(G[V-S],I)=I^{\prime}
;
and
(2)
if

r_{E}(G,I)=\bot
,
then

r_{E}(G[V-S],I)=\bot
.
Finally, to see that
r_{E}
is efficient, notice that it can be
implemented simply by inspecting all nodes in

V-I
.
The assertion follows from the
\operatorname{poly}(n)
size of
G
as
promised in Obs.
2.3
.
∎

All-For-
q
Improvement Rule.

Given a
k
-cycle graph

G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}

and an IS
I
in
G
, the
all-for-
q
improvement rule
r_{q}

for a constant

q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

returns the lexicographically first IS

I^{\prime}=r_{q}(G,I)

that satisfies
(1)

\alpha(I^{\prime})\supset\alpha(I)
;
and
(2)

I^{\prime}=(I\cup X)-(\mathit{N}_{G}(X)\cap I)
,
where

X\subseteq\mathit{N}_{G}(I)

is an IS in
G
and

|\mathit{N}_{G}(X)\cap I|\leq q
.
If no such IS
I^{\prime}
exists, then
r_{q}
returns

r_{q}(G,I)=\bot
.
We refer to any IS
I^{\prime}
that satisfies conditions (1) and (2) as a

candidate
of
r_{q}
.
Refer to Figures
2(d)
–
2(f)
for an
illustration of
r_{q}
.

Observation 4.2
.

The all-for-
q
improvement rule is loyal, INPA, and efficient for any
constant

q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

Proof.

The fact that
r_{q}
is loyal follows from requirement (1).
To see that
r_{q}
is INPA, observe that if

i\notin\alpha(I)
,
then reducing
\alpha^{-1}(i)
to

\alpha^{-1}(i)-S

for some node subset

S\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(i)

can only decrease the collection of candidates from which

r_{q}(G,I)

is selected.
Therefore,
(1)
if

r_{q}(G,I)=I^{\prime}\neq\bot

and

i\notin\alpha(I^{\prime})
,
then

r_{q}(G[V-S],I)=I^{\prime}
;
and
(2)
if

r_{q}(G,I)=\bot
,
then

r_{q}(G[V-S],I)=\bot
.
Finally, to see that
r_{q}
is efficient, recall that
Obs.
2.3
guarantees that the

k
-cycle graph
G
is

(k+1)
-claw
free.
Therefore, if

X\subseteq\mathit{N}_{G}(I)

is an IS in
G
and

|\mathit{N}_{G}(X)\cap I|\leq q
,
then

|X|\leq qk
.
This means that one can implement the all-for-
q
improvement rule by
inspecting all subsets of

V-I

of size at most

qk

which is a constant by the assumption that
k
and
q
are constants.
The assertion follows from the
\operatorname{poly}(n)
size of
G
as
promised in Obs.
2.3
.
∎

The
\mathtt{LS}_{q}
Algorithm.

Given a constant parameter

q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
our main algorithm for the
k
-maxCGIS problem, denoted by

\mathtt{LS}_{q}
, is defined to be the local search algorithm
characterized by the improvement rule list

(r_{E},r_{q})
.
By combining Obs.
4.1
and

4.2
with
Corollary
3.2
,
we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.3
.

\mathtt{LS}_{q}
is truthful and efficient.

4.1
Bounding the Approximation Ratio

Our goal in this section is to analyze the approximation ratio of

\mathtt{LS}_{q}
and establish
Thm.
4.4
that, combined with
Obs.
2.2
and
Corollary
4.3
, yields
Thm.
1.2
by choosing

q\geq 1/\epsilon
.
In Sec.
8
, we show that the
upper bound established here on the approximation ratio of

\mathtt{LS}_{q}
is nearly tight.

Theorem 4.4
.

\mathtt{LS}_{q}
approximates the
k
-maxCGIS problem within ratio

k-1+1/q
.

Consider a
k
-cycle graph

G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}

input to
\mathtt{LS}_{q}
.
Let

I=\mathtt{LS}_{q}(G)

be the IS output by
\mathtt{LS}_{q}
and let
O
be an optimal
(maximum weight) IS in
G
.
To establish Thm.
4.4
, it
suffices to show that if

H=(V_{H},E_{H})

is a connected component of

G[I\cup O]

with

I_{H}=V_{H}\cap I

and

O_{H}=V_{H}\cap O
,
then

\textstyle\mathit{w}(O_{H})\,\leq\,\left(k-1+\frac{1}{q}\right)\mathit{w}(I_{H})\,;

(5)

indeed, summing this inequality over all connected components
H
of

G[I\cup O]

yields the desired bound

\mathit{w}(O)\leq\left(k-1+\frac{1}{q}\right)\mathit{w}(I)
.

Consider a connected component

H=(V_{H},E_{H})

of

G[I\cup O]

with

I_{H}=V_{H}\cap I

and

O_{H}=V_{H}\cap O
.
If

V_{H}=\{v\}

is a singleton set, then the inclusion of the expansion improvement rule

r_{E}
in
\mathtt{LS}_{q}
ensures that

I_{H}=\{v\}
,
hence inequality (
5
) clearly holds.
Assume hereafter that

|V_{H}|>1

and notice that in this case,
H
must be a bipartite graph with
I_{H}
on
one side and
O_{H}
on the other.

The construction of
H
implies that

(O\cup I_{H})-O_{H}

is an independent set in
G
, thus the optimality of
O
ensures that

\mathit{w}(I_{H})\leq\mathit{w}(O_{H})
.
If

\mathit{w}(I_{H})=\mathit{w}(O_{H})
,
then inequality (
5
) holds trivially, so
assume hereafter that

\mathit{w}(I_{H})<\mathit{w}(O_{H})
.
By Obs.
2.3
, we know that

\operatorname{deg}_{H}(v)\leq|\alpha(v)|=\mathit{w}(v)

for every

v\in I_{H}
.
We shall establish inequality (
5
) by
proving Lem.
4.6
and

4.7
for which we need the following combinatorial
proposition.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let

P=(A\uplus B,E)

be a connected bipartite graph and let

w:A\cup B\rightarrow\{2,3,\dots,k\}

be a weight function that satisfies

w(a)\geq\operatorname{deg}_{P}(a)

for each node

a\in A
.
Then,

w(B)\leq w(A)\left(k-1+\frac{1}{|A|}\right)
.
Moreover, if there exists a node

a\in A

with

w(a)>\operatorname{deg}_{P}(a)
,
then

w(B)\leq w(A)(k-1)
.

Proof.

Assume that the bipartite graph

P=(A\uplus B,E)

is cycle free;
this assumption is without loss of generality since the removal of an edge
from a cycle does not break the graph’s connectivity nor does it violate the
condition

\operatorname{deg}_{P}(a)\leq w(a)

for any node

a\in A
.
This means that
P
is a tree, hence

|E|\,=\,|A|+|B|-1\,.

On the other hand, as

\operatorname{deg}_{P}(a)\leq w(a)

for all nodes

a\in A
, we deduce that

|E|\,=\,\sum_{a\in A}\operatorname{deg}_{P}(a)\,\leq\,\sum_{a\in A}w(a)\,=\,w(A)\,.

Recalling that the images of
w
are up-bounded by
k
, we know that

w(A)\leq k\cdot|A|

and

w(B)\leq k\cdot|B|
.
Put together, we conclude that

\frac{w(A)}{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{|A|}\right)+\frac{w(B)}{k}\,\leq\,|A|\left(1-\frac{1}{|A|}\right)+|B|\,=\,|E|\,\leq\,w(A)\,,

thus

w(B)\,\leq\,w(A)\left(k-1+\frac{1}{|A|}\right)\,.

Now, assume that there exists a node

a\in A

such that

\operatorname{deg}_{P}(a)<w(a)
.
Since
\operatorname{deg}_{P}(a)
and
w(a)
are integers, it follows that

\operatorname{deg}_{P}(a)\leq w(a)-1
,
hence

|E|\,=\,\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}\operatorname{deg}_{P}(a^{\prime})\,\leq\,\left(\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}w(a^{\prime})\right)-1\,=\,w(A)-1\,.

Therefore,

\frac{w(A)}{k}+\frac{w(B)}{k}-1\leq|A|+|B|-1=|E|\leq w(A)-1\,,

which implies that

w(B)\leq(k-1)\cdot w(A)\,,

thus completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

If

\operatorname{deg}_{H}(v)=\mathit{w}(v)

for every

v\in I_{H}
,
then

\mathit{w}(O_{H})<\left(k-1+\frac{1}{q}\right)\mathit{w}(I_{H})
.

Proof.

If

|I_{H}|>q
,
then by plugging

A=I_{H}

and

B=O_{H}

into Prop.
4.5
,
we conclude that

\mathit{w}(O_{H})\leq\left(k-1+\frac{1}{|I_{H}|}\right)\mathit{w}(I_{H})<\left(k-1+\frac{1}{q}\right)\mathit{w}(I_{H})
,
thus proving the assertion.
So, assume towards contradiction that

|I_{H}|\leq q
,
and recall that

I=\mathtt{LS}_{q}(G)

is the IS returned by
\mathtt{LS}_{q}
on
G
.
By the construction of
H
, we know that

I^{\prime}=(I\cup O_{H})-I_{H}

is an IS in
G
.
We shall reach a contradiction by showing that
I^{\prime}
is a candidate of

r_{q}
when applied to
G
and
I
, which means that the application
of
\mathtt{LS}_{q}
to
G
should not have returned
I
.

To this end, consider a node

v\in I_{H}
.
The assumption that

\operatorname{deg}_{H}(v)=\mathit{w}(v)

implies that

|\mathit{N}_{H}(v)|=|\alpha(v)|
.
As
\mathit{N}_{H}(v)
is an IS
(recall that

\mathit{N}_{H}(v)\subseteq O_{H}
),
we know that

\alpha(u)\cap\alpha(u^{\prime})=\emptyset

for every

u,u^{\prime}\in\mathit{N}_{H}(v)
.
This means that for every agent

i\in\alpha(v)
,
there exists a unique node

u=u(i)\in\mathit{N}_{H}(v)

such that

i\in\alpha(u)
.
In particular, we conclude that

\alpha(v)\subseteq\alpha(\mathit{N}_{H}(v))\subseteq\alpha(O_{H})
,
hence

\alpha(I_{H})\subseteq\alpha(O_{H})
.
Therefore,

\alpha(I)\subseteq\alpha(I^{\prime})

and the assumption that

\mathit{w}(I_{H})<\mathit{w}(O_{H})

guarantees that

\alpha(I)\subset\alpha(I^{\prime})
.
Since

|I_{H}|\leq q

and since

O_{H}\subseteq\mathit{N}_{G}(I)
,
it follows that
I^{\prime}
is indeed a candidate of
r_{q}
, realized by taking

X=O_{H}
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

If there exists a node

v\in I_{H}

such that

\operatorname{deg}_{H}(v)<\mathit{w}(v)
,
then

\mathit{w}(O_{H})\leq(k-1)\mathit{w}(I_{H})
.

Proof.

Follows directly from Prop.
4.5
by
plugging

A=I_{H}

and

B=O_{H}
.
∎

5
Concatenation of Truthful Algorithms

In this section, we introduce the notion of algorithm concatenation that
facilitates the construction of our main algorithm for non-uniform length
functions, presented in Sec.
7
.
We also introduce a sufficient condition for the truthfulness of concatenated
algorithms;
the usefulness of this condition is demonstrated already in
Sec.
6
, where it allows us to establish the truthfulness
of
\mathtt{Greedy}
.
All algorithms addressed in this section are

(k,\lambda)
-maxCGIS
algorithms for a length bound

k\geq 2

and a (uniform or non-uniform) length function
\lambda
.

Definition

(algorithm concatenation)
.

The
concatenation
of algorithms
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}
and
\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
, denoted by

\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
,
is the algorithm that given an input
k
-cycle graph

G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}
,
outputs the IS

\textstyle\left(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2}\right)(G)\,=\,\mathtt{Alg}_{1}(G)\cup\mathtt{Alg}_{2}\left(G\left[V-\left(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}(G)\cup\mathit{N}_{G}\left(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}(G)\right)\right)\right]\right)\,.

Intuitively, the concatenated algorithm

\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2}

works as follows:
first, run
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}
on
G
to get the IS
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}(G)
;
then, expand
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}(G)
with the IS constructed by running
\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
on
the graph obtained from
G
by removing the nodes in
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}(G)
and their
neighbors.
The following definition provides a sufficient condition for the agents to
favor partaking in the output of
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}
over that of
\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
.

Definition

(the
\succeq
relation)
.

Algorithms
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}
and
\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
satisfy the relation

\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\succeq\mathtt{Alg}_{2}

if the maximum length of a node included in any IS output by
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}
is
up-bounded by the minimum length of a node included in any IS output by

\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
, namely,

\max_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}\,\max_{G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}}\,\max_{v\in\mathtt{Alg}_{1}(G)}\,|\alpha(v)|\,\leq\,\min_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}\,\min_{G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}}\,\min_{v\in\mathtt{Alg}_{2}(G)}\,|\alpha(v)|\,.

Based on the definitions of algorithm concatenation and the
\succeq

relation, we can now state the following important lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}
be a truthful INPA algorithm and let
\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
be a truthful
algorithm such that

\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\succeq\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
.
Then, the concatenated algorithm

\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2}

is truthful.

Proof.

Consider a
k
-cycle graph

G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}

and an agent

i\in[n]

and fix a node subset

S\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(i)
.
We establish the assertion by proving that

\textstyle\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G[V-S]))\,\leq\,\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G))\,.

To avoid cumbersome expressions, we denote

G^{\prime}=G[V-S]

and

\textstyle I_{1}=\mathtt{Alg}_{1}(G)\,,\quad I_{2}=\mathtt{Alg}_{2}(G)\,,\quad I^{\prime}_{1}=\mathtt{Alg}_{1}(G^{\prime})\,,\quad\text{and}\quad I^{\prime}_{2}=\mathtt{Alg}_{2}(G^{\prime})\,.

The proof is divided into two cases depending on whether agent
i
partakes in

I_{1}
.

Suppose first that

i\in\alpha(I_{1})

which means, by the definition of algorithm concatenation, that

\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G))=\mathit{u}(i,I_{1})
.
If

i\in\alpha(I^{\prime}_{1})
,
then
i
’s utility from

(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G^{\prime})

is equal to her utility from

I^{\prime}_{1}
.
Since
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}
is truthful, it follows that

\mathit{u}(i,I^{\prime}_{1})\leq\mathit{u}(i,I_{1})
,
hence

\textstyle\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G^{\prime}))\,=\,\mathit{u}(i,I^{\prime}_{1})\,\leq\,\mathit{u}(i,I_{1})\,=\,\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G))\,.

If

i\notin\alpha(I^{\prime}_{1})
,
then
i
’s utility from

(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G^{\prime})

is equal to her utility from

\mathtt{Alg}_{2}(G^{\prime}[(V-S)-(I^{\prime}_{1}\cup\mathit{N}_{G^{\prime}}(I^{\prime}_{1}))])
.
Recalling that

\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\succeq\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
,
we conclude that the utility of
i
from
I_{1}
is at least as high as her
utility from any IS output by
\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
, thus

\displaystyle\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G^{\prime}))\,=\,

\displaystyle\mathit{u}(i,\mathtt{Alg}_{2}(G^{\prime}[(V-S)-(I^{\prime}_{1}\cup\mathit{N}_{G^{\prime}}(I^{\prime}_{1}))]))

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\mathit{u}(i,I_{1})\,=\,\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G))\,.

Therefore, the assertion holds when

i\in\alpha(I_{1})
.

So, suppose that

i\notin\alpha(I_{1})

which means, by the definition of algorithm concatenation, that

\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G))=\mathit{u}(i,\mathtt{Alg}_{2}(G[V-(I_{1}\cup\mathit{N}_{G}(I_{1}))]))
.
By the truthfulness of
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}
, we deduce that

i\notin\alpha(I^{\prime}_{1})
,
thus

\textstyle\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G^{\prime}))\,=\,\mathit{u}(i,\mathtt{Alg}_{2}(G^{\prime}[(V-S)-(I^{\prime}_{1}\cup\mathit{N}_{G^{\prime}}(I^{\prime}_{1}))]))\,.

Since
\mathtt{Alg}_{1}
is INPA, it follows that

I^{\prime}_{1}=I_{1}
,
therefore

\textstyle G^{\prime}[(V-S)-(I^{\prime}_{1}\cup\mathit{N}_{G^{\prime}}(I^{\prime}_{1}))]\,=\,G^{\prime}[(V-S)-(I_{1}\cup\mathit{N}_{G^{\prime}}(I_{1}))]\,=\,G[(V-(I_{1}\cup\mathit{N}_{G}(I_{1})))-S]\,.

The truthfulness of
\mathtt{Alg}_{2}
ensures that

\textstyle\mathit{u}(i,\mathtt{Alg}_{2}(G[(V-(I_{1}\cup\mathit{N}_{G}(I_{1})))-S]))\,\leq\,\mathit{u}(i,\mathtt{Alg}_{2}(G[V-(I_{1}\cup\mathit{N}_{G}(I_{1}))]))\,,

hence

\textstyle\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G^{\prime}))\,\leq\,\mathit{u}(i,\mathtt{Alg}_{2}(G[V-(I_{1}\cup\mathit{N}_{G}(I_{1}))]))\,=\,\mathit{u}(i,(\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdot\mathtt{Alg}_{2})(G))\,,

where the second transition holds by the definition of algorithm
concatenation.
This means that the assertion holds also when

i\notin\alpha(I_{1})

which completes the proof.
∎

A repeated application of Lem.
5.1
leads to
the following corollary.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let

\mathtt{Alg}_{1},\dots,\mathtt{Alg}_{r-1}

be truthful INPA algorithms, let
\mathtt{Alg}_{r}
be a truthful algorithm, and
assume that

\mathtt{Alg}_{j}\succeq\mathtt{Alg}_{j+1}

for every

1\leq j\leq r-1
.
Then, the concatenated algorithm

\mathtt{Alg}_{1}\cdots\mathtt{Alg}_{r}

is truthful.

6
The Greedy Approach

Before moving to the more involved

(k,\lambda)
-maxCGIS
algorithm of Sec.
7
, let us analyze what
is perhaps the simplest approach for designing efficient truthful

(k,\lambda)
-maxCGIS
algorithms.
Given a
k
-cycle graph

G=(V,E)
,
the greedy approach iterates over all nodes in
V
in a predefined order and
adds a node

v\in V

to the constructed IS
I
, setting

I\leftarrow I\cup\{v\}
,
if
v
is not adjacent to any node

v^{\prime}\in I

(i.e.,
I
remains an IS in
G
).
It is not difficult to show that a greedy algorithm that prioritizes the nodes

v\in V

according to their weight

\mathit{w}(v)=|\alpha(v)|\cdot\lambda(|\alpha(v)|)

admits an approximation ratio
k
.
However, this algorithm is not necessarily truthful as the objective of the
individual agent

i\in[n]

is to minimize the length
|\alpha(v)|
of (the unique) node

v\in\alpha^{-1}(i)\cap I

(recall that the length function
\lambda
is monotonically non-increasing)
which is not necessarily aligned with maximizing

|\alpha(v)|\cdot\lambda(|\alpha(v)|)
.

To overcome this difficulty, we propose a greedy algorithm, denoted by

\mathtt{Greedy}
, that prioritizes the nodes

v\in V

according to their length
|\alpha(v)|
, thus adding the nodes
v
in
non-decreasing order of their contribution to the utility of the individual
agents included in
\alpha(v)
.
Formally, for

j=2,\dots,k
,
let
r_{E}^{j}
be the improvement rule derived from the expansion
improvement rule
r_{E}
, introduced in Sec.
4
,
by augmenting it with the requirement that the nodes
v
included in any
candidate IS
I
are of length

|\alpha(v)|=j
;
let
\mathtt{Greedy}^{j}
be the local search algorithm characterized by the lone
improvement rule
r_{E}^{j}
.
The greedy algorithm is taken to be the concatenation

\mathtt{Greedy}=\mathtt{Greedy}^{2}\cdots\mathtt{Greedy}^{k}
.

Observation 6.1
.

Algorithm
\mathtt{Greedy}^{j}
is truthful and INPA for each

2\leq j\leq k
.
Moreover,

\mathtt{Greedy}^{j}\succeq\mathtt{Greedy}^{j+1}

for each

2\leq j\leq k-1
.

Proof.

The fact that
\mathtt{Greedy}^{j}
is truthful and INPA follows from the same line
of arguments as the one used in Sec.
4

regarding the expansion improvement rule
r_{E}
.
The fact that

\mathtt{Greedy}^{j}\succeq\mathtt{Greedy}^{j+1}

holds as
\mathtt{Greedy}^{j}
outputs ISs with nodes of length
j
and

\mathtt{Greedy}^{j+1}

outputs ISs with nodes of length

j+1
.
∎

The truthfulness of
\mathtt{Greedy}
follows immediately from
Corollary
5.2
.

Corollary 6.2
.

\mathtt{Greedy}
is truthful.

In the remainder of this section, we show that
\mathtt{Greedy}
approximates the

(k,\lambda)
-maxCGIS
problem within ratio
k
, thus establishing
Thm.
1.1
.
This relies on the following observation that serves us in
Sec.
7
as well.

Observation 6.3
.

Consider a
k
-cycle graph

G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}

and let

I=\mathtt{Greedy}(G)
.
For every IS
J
in
G
, there exists a function

\mu:J\rightarrow I

such that
(1)

|\alpha(u)|\geq|\alpha(\mu(u))|

for every node

u\in J
;
and
(2)

|\mu^{-1}(v)|\leq|\alpha(v)|

for every node

v\in I
,
where

\mu^{-1}(v)=\{u\in J:\mu(u)=v\}
.

Proof.

We construct the function
\mu
by setting
\mu(u)
as follows for each node

u\in J
:
If

u\in I
,
then set

\mu(u)=u
.
Otherwise
(
u\notin I
),
the design of
\mathtt{Greedy}
ensures that there exists a node

v\in\mathit{N}_{G}(u)\cap I

whose length low-bounds that of
u
, i.e.,

|\alpha(v)|\leq|\alpha(u)|
,
so we set

\mu(u)=v
.
Condition (1) follows directly by the construction of
\mu
.
To see that condition (2) also holds, we employ
Obs.
2.3
to deduce that since

Upper Bound for Non-Uniform Length Functions

Consider the

(k,\lambda)
-maxCGIS
problem for a length bound

In the current section, we develop an efficient truthful algorithm for this

The
\mathtt{NU}_{q}

Let

be the largest

(notice that
\ell^{*}
is well defined as

Let

Analogously to the definition of the improvement rule

(see
Sec.
6
), let

(resp.,

r_{q}^{>\ell^{*}}

be the improvement rule derived from the expansion improvement rule

(resp., all-for-

), introduced in

that the nodes
v
included in any candidate IS

|\alpha(v)|>\ell^{*}
.
Let

\mathtt{LS}_{q}^{>\ell^{*}}

be the local search algorithm characterized
by the improvement rule list

(r_{E}^{>\ell^{*}},r_{q}^{>\ell^{*}})
.
Given a constant parameter

,
our algorithm for the non-uniform case, denoted by

defined to be the concatenation of

\mathtt{Greedy}^{\leq\ell^{*}}

and

.

\mathtt{NU}_{q}

Proof.

Notice first that the proofs of Obs.
4.1
and

4.2
can be applied, verbatim, to

and

r_{q}^{>\ell^{*}}

respectively, concluding that these improvement rules are loyal, INPA, and

By Lem.

\mathtt{LS}_{q}^{>\ell^{*}}

The efficiency of
\mathtt{NU}_{q}

Since algorithm

\mathtt{LS}_{q}^{>\ell^{*}}

is oblivious to the length function
\lambda
.

2.4
, whose scope is
restricted (originally) to the uniform length function, to deduce that the

\mathtt{LS}_{q}^{>\ell^{*}}

Notice that

\mathtt{Greedy}^{\leq\ell^{*}}\succeq\mathtt{LS}_{q}^{>\ell^{*}}

H_{\ell}[V_{\ell}]
and the node set

\bigcup_{j\in L_{\ell}}O_{\ell}^{j}

is an IS in
H_{\ell}[V_{\ell}]
, it follows that

\lambda(\ell)\cdot\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|O_{\ell}^{j}|=\mathit{w}(\bigcup_{j\in L_{\ell}}O_{\ell}^{j})\leq\mathit{w}(I_{\ell})=\lambda(\ell)\cdot\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|I_{\ell}^{j}|\,,

which implies that

\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|O_{\ell}^{j}|\leq\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|I_{\ell}^{j}|\,.

(10)

Recall our beloved Obs.
2.3
,
stating that a node of length
j
in
I_{\ell}
has at most
j

neighbors in
O_{\ell}
.
Using the fact that
I_{\ell}
is a maximal IS in

G[I_{\ell}\cup N_{\ell}]

and that

I_{\ell},O_{\ell}

are disjoint,
we deduce that

|O_{\ell}|=|\mathit{N}_{G}(I_{\ell})\cap O_{\ell}|\,\leq\,\sum_{v\in I_{\ell}}|\mathit{N}_{G}(v)\cap O_{\ell}|\,=\,\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}\sum_{v\in I_{\ell}^{j}}|\mathit{N}_{G}(v)\cap O_{\ell}|\,\leq\,\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|I_{\ell}^{j}|\,,

hence

|O_{\ell}^{>\ell}|\leq\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|I_{\ell}^{j}|-\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}|O_{\ell}^{j}|\,.

(11)

The rest of the proof is divided into two cases depending on whether the following inequality holds

\ell\lambda(\ell)\leq\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}\,.

(12)

We shall prove a different upper bound to
\mathit{w}(O_{\ell})
in each case and we will get the desired approximation ratio by taking the maximum of those bounds.

Assume first that (
12
) holds.
Applying a few straightforward algebraic transitions, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathit{w}(O_{\ell})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equation:iterative-opt-2}}}{{\leq}}\lambda(\ell)\cdot\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}|O_{\ell}^{j}|\cdot j+\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}\cdot|O_{\ell}^{>\ell}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equation:iterative-opt-*}}}{{\leq}}\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\ell}\cdot\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|O_{\ell}^{j}|+|O_{\ell}^{>\ell}|\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equation:iterative-opt-4}}}{{\leq}}\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\ell}\cdot\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|O_{\ell}^{j}|+\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|I_{\ell}^{j}|-\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}|O_{\ell}^{j}|\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\forall j\in L_{\ell}:\,j\leq\ell}}{{\leq}}\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}\cdot\left(\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|I_{\ell}^{j}|\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equation:iterative-opt-1}}}{{=}}\mathit{w}(I_{\ell})\cdot\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\lambda(\ell)}\,.

On the other hand, if inequality (
12
) is not satisfied,
we can take advantage of our aforementioned equations and use algebraic manipulations to deduce that

\displaystyle\mathit{w}(O_{\ell})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equation:iterative-opt-2}}}{{\leq}}\lambda(\ell)\cdot\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}|O_{\ell}^{j}|\cdot j+\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}\cdot|O_{\ell}^{>\ell}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equation:iterative-opt-4}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|O_{\ell}^{j}|\cdot\lambda(\ell)+\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}\cdot\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|I_{\ell}^{j}|-\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}\cdot\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}|O_{\ell}^{j}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equation:iterative-opt-1}}}{{=}}\mathit{w}(I_{\ell})\cdot\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\lambda(\ell)}+\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|O_{\ell}^{j}|\left(\lambda(\ell)-\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{j}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\forall j\in L_{\ell}:\,j\leq\ell}}{{\leq}}\mathit{w}(I_{\ell})\cdot\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\lambda(\ell)}+\left(\lambda(\ell)-\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\ell}\right)\cdot\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|O_{\ell}^{j}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equation:iterative-opt-3},\neg\eqref{equation:iterative-opt-*}}}{{\leq}}\mathit{w}(I_{\ell})\cdot\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\lambda(\ell)}+\left(\lambda(\ell)-\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\ell}\right)\cdot\sum_{j\in L_{\ell}}j\cdot|I_{\ell}^{j}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equation:iterative-opt-1}}}{{=}}\mathit{w}(I_{\ell})\cdot\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\lambda(\ell)}+\left(\lambda(\ell)-\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\ell}\right)\cdot\frac{\mathit{w}(I_{\ell})}{\lambda(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\mathit{w}(I_{\ell})\cdot\left(\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\lambda(\ell)}+1-\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\ell\lambda(\ell)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathit{w}(I_{\ell})\cdot\left(1+\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\cdot\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\lambda(\ell)}\right)\,.

Finally, by taking the maximum of the bounds on
\mathit{w}(O_{\ell})
from both cases we conclude that

\mathit{w}(O_{\ell})\leq\mathit{w}(I_{\ell})\cdot\max\left\{\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\lambda(\ell)},1+\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\cdot\frac{\max_{\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k}\{\ell^{\prime}\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\}}{\lambda(\ell)}\right\}\,,

thus completing the proof of
Lem.
9.4
.
∎

Additional Related Work

Barter Exchange from a Game Theoretic View Point

The game theoretic study of barter markets dates back to the seminal
work of Shapley and Scarf
[
SS74
]
that considers a model
where each agent is associated with a preference list over all other agents.
Shapley and Scarf introduced the classic top trading cycle (TTC) mechanism for
this setting whose truthfulness was established by Roth

[
Rot82
]
.

The trend of looking at the kidney exchange market through a rigorous game
theoretic lens was initiated by Roth et al.
[
RSÜ04
]
.
In contrast to the model considered in the current paper, the one studied in

[
RSÜ04
]
includes (strict) preferences over the agent wish lists
as well as non-cyclic trades.
Another major difference is that in
[
RSÜ04
]
, Roth et al. allow
for trading cycles (and chains) of unbounded length.

As discussed in Sec.
1
, the model considered in
the current paper was introduced by Roth et al. in
[
RSÜ05
]
.
In the language of our BE problem, each agent in
[
RSÜ05
]

corresponds to a pair consisting of a patient that needs a kidney transplant
and an incompatible donor that is willing to donate a healthy kidney.
The inclusion of agent
i
in the wish list of agent
j
corresponds in this
regard to a compatibility between the healthy donor associated with agent
i

and the patient associated with agent
j
.
Among other results, Roth et al. reduce (implicitly) the task of designing a
truthful Pareto efficient mechanism for the case of trading cycles of length

k=2

to that of constructing a maximum size matching in an undirected graph.
They also prove that in the scope of deterministic mechanisms, Pareto
efficiency is equivalent to maximizing the social welfare, which leads to the

(k,\lambda_{k}^{u})
-BE
problem defined in the current paper.
Hatfield
[
Hat05
]
extends some of the arguments of

[
RSÜ05
]
to trading cycles of length at most

k\geq 3

(among other constraints), which enables the development of an optimal, alas
computationally inefficient, truthful mechanism for the

(k,\lambda_{k}^{u})
-BE
problem.

In
[
RSÜ07
]
, Roth et al. analyze the social welfare gain
obtained from increasing the trading cycles length bound
k
assuming that the
donation compatibility is determined by the
4
possible blood types.
They prove that the increase from

k=2

to

k=3

leads to a dramatic social welfare gain and that this gain is substantially
larger than the one obtained by further increasing the trading cycle length
bound from

k=3

to

k=4
.
Moreover, any further increase in
k
does not improve the optimal social
welfare.

We emphasize that despite the extensive treatment that the

(k,\lambda_{k}^{u})
-BE
problem received in the last two decades, the current paper is the first to
develop efficient truthful mechanisms for this problem with a non-trivial
approximation ratio.
To the best of our knowledge, the extension to non-uniform length functions

\lambda\neq\lambda_{k}^{u}

has not been studied so far.

Regarding the

(k,\lambda_{k}^{u})
-maxCGIS
problem, the notion of INPA algorithms, which is pivotal to the truthfulness
proofs in the current paper, is closely related to the notion of
“consistent” mechanisms, introduced in
[
Hat05
]
.
Consistency states that if an exchange is chosen from some set of feasible
exchanges, then it is also chosen from any subset of that set.
From the viewpoint of the

(k,\lambda_{k}^{u})
-maxCGIS
problem, INPA is a weak version of consistency as it merely suppresses
manipulations of non-partaking agents.
Other related concepts that limit the effect of individual manipulations on
the group outcome (alas in the context of binary outcomes) include the notions
of “bitonic” mechanisms
[
MN08
]
and “XBONE” and
“non-bossy” mechanisms
[
AKLM21
]
.

Abbassi et al.
[
AHM15
]
introduced a generalization of our

(k,\lambda_{k}^{u})
-BE
problem, where each agent holds a set of items that she wishes to exchange for
certain items held by the other agents.
As in our BE problem, the exchanges are carried out via trading cycles,
however, an agent may now participate in multiple trading cycles and her
utility is the number of wished items she obtains.
The main positive results of Abbassi et al. are
(1)
an efficient optimal truthful mechanism for the problem version with trading
cycles of unbounded length;
and
(2)
an efficient truthful mechanism that approximates the optimal solution within
ratio
8
for pairwise exchanges, i.e., for trading cycles of length

k=2
.
The version with trading cycles of length at most

k\geq 3
,
that is closer to the focus of the current paper, is treated in

[
AHM15
]
only on the negative side, establishing
approximability lower bounds both for the combinatorial optimization problem
and for the task of designing truthful mechanisms.

In a related problem, studied by Ashlagi et al.
[
AFKP15
,
AR14
]
,
the strategic entities are hospitals, each responsible for a subset of
patient-donor pairs.
The objective of a hospital is to maximize the number of patients under its
responsibility that receive a donation through pairwise exchanges
(i.e.,

k=2
)
and to this end, the hospital may choose to match some of their patient-donor
pairs internally, rather than revealing them to the mechanism so that they
become available for inter-hospital matches.
Another variant of the barter exchange setting studied recently by Dickerson et
al.
[
DPS19
]
is the one where each arc in the agent digraph
is associated with a failure probability.

Also related to the

(k,\lambda_{k}^{u})
-maxCGIS
problem are the
hedonic coalition formation
games in which a mechanism
partitions the players into disjoint sets (coalitions) and each player’s
utility is fully determined by the other members of her
coalition
[
Woe13
]
.
Specifically, our maxCGIS problem resembles the case of
anonymous
preferences
, where the players are indifferent about coalitions of the same
size
[
Woe13
]
.
The (informally defined)

(\infty,\lambda_{\infty}^{u})
-maxCGIS
problem falls under the family of
group activity selection problems
(GASPs)

[
DEK+12
]
.
The goal in GASP is to assign agents to activities in a “good way”, where
agents have preferences over

\langle\text{activity},\text{group size}\rangle

pairs.
Specifically, the

(\infty,\lambda_{\infty}^{u})
-maxCGIS
problem corresponds to the special case in which agents are indifferent
between “accepted” coalitions” and there is a single
\infty
-copyable
activity.

The main difference between the maxCGIS problem and the ones mentioned in the
previous paragraph is our hard bound on the coalition size (or, using the
maxCGIS terminology, the number of agents included in the label of each node).
A setting that includes such a hard bound was recently investigated in

[
DDDS22
]
in the context of
simplified GASPs
, where
agents only express their preferences over the set of activities with
restrictions on the number of participants in each activity.
Notice that the simplified GASP setting differs from our maxCGIS problem as
the agents in the latter problem admit (cardinal) preferences over the
coalitions (based on their size), rather than the activities.

Barter Exchange from a Combinatorial Optimization View Point

The family of BE problems turns out to admit an interesting combinatorial
structure even when one focuses merely on the construction of a social welfare
maximizing exchange, putting truthfulness considerations aside.
While the

k=2

case reduces to maximum size matching, and hence admits an efficient
algorithm, Abraham et al.
[
ABS07
]
proved that the

(k,\lambda)
-BE
problem is NP-hard for every length function
\lambda
already for

k=3
.
Biro et al.
[
BMR09
]
enhanced this hardness result and proved
that the problem is also APX-hard.
An explicit approximation lower bound of

698/697

was established by Luo et al.
[
LTWZ16
]
.
We note that the original constructions in

[
ABS07
,
BMR09
,
LTWZ16
]

are designed for the case of uniform length functions

\lambda=\lambda_{k}^{u}
,
however the hard instances they use can be adjusted so that all trading cycles
in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathit{W}}^{k}
are of length (exactly)
k
, which means that
the problem becomes oblivious to the length function
\lambda
.

The instances of the

(k,\lambda)
-BE
problem where all trading cycles in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathit{W}}^{k}
are of length
k
is
a special case of the classic
k
-set packing (
k
-SP) problem.
As proved by Hazan et al.
[
HSS03
,
HSS06
]
,
this problem is hard to approximate within ratio better then

\Omega(\frac{k}{\ln k})
.
It is not clear if this lower bound can be extended also to our

(k,\lambda)
-BE
problem.

On the positive side, it is well known that the greedy approach leads to a

k
-approximation of
k
-SP.
A folklore result (see, e.g.,
[
Hal98
]
) states that
the local search algorithm relying on the
2
-for-
1
policy provides a

\frac{k+1}{2}
-approximation.
As proved in
[
HS89
]
, the approximation ratio improves to

\frac{k}{2}+\varepsilon

if the
2
-for-
1
policy is replaced by the
t
-local search policy.
More recently, Sviridenko and Ward
[
SW13
]
and Cygan

[
Cyg13
]
used more sophisticated local search algorithms to
further improve the approximation ratio upper bound to

\frac{k+2}{3}

and

\frac{k+1}{3}+\varepsilon
,
respectively.

As discussed in Sec.
2
(see Obs.
2.2
and

2.3
), the combinatorial optimization
aspect of the

(k,\lambda)
-BE
problem reduces to the problem of constructing a maximum weight IS in

(k+1)
-claw
free graphs (this is true also for the
k
-SP problem).
This problem has received considerable attention and several approximation
algorithms were developed for it.
Specifically, Arkin and Hassin
[
AH98
]
analyze the
t
-local
search policy in

(k+1)
-claw
free graphs and prove that it is guaranteed to approximate the maximum weight
IS within a

k-1+\varepsilon

ratio.
Biro et al.
[
BMR09
]
, who investigated the maximum weight IS
problem in

(k+1)
-claw
free graphs specifically in the context of the

(k,\lambda_{k}^{u})
-BE
problem, obtain the same approximation guarantee with another local search
algorithm which is simpler to analyze.
The approximation ratio upper bound was improved to

\frac{2(k+1)}{3}+\varepsilon

by Chandra and Halldórsson

[
CH01
]

and to

\frac{k+1}{2}+\varepsilon

by Berman
[
Ber00
]
;
these two works also use local search algorithm alas with slightly more
advanced policies.

It is important to point out that while there is a resemblance between the
improvement rules used by our local search algorithm
\mathtt{LS}
and some
of the existing local search policies, our improvement rules must take into
account the strings associated with each node, a feature that becomes
effective only when one aims for a truthful algorithm.
Consequently, the approximation ratio analysis of
\mathtt{LS}
(see
Thm.
4.4
) follows a
different path in comparison to the analyses used in the existing literature.
Moreover, the algorithms developed in

[
AH98
,
Ber00
,
CH01
,
BMR09
]

belong to the family of standard local search algorithms, hence, by
Thm.
1.6
, there is no hope in relying on
them to obtain a truthful algorithm with approximation ratio better than

k-1
.

Appendix A
Lifting the Subset Wish List Assumption

Recall that in Sec.
1.1
, we make the subset wish list
assumption stating that the wish list
\mathit{W}_{i}
reported by an agent

i\in[n]

must be a subset of her true wish list
\mathit{W}^{*}_{i}
.
In this section, we present a simple method for lifting this assumption by
developing a reduction that transforms any “reasonable” (in a sense revealed
soon) truthful

(k,\lambda)
-BE
mechanism
f
working under the subset wish list assumption into a randomized
truthful

(k,\lambda)
-BE
mechanism
f^{\prime}
that does not rely on the subset wish list assumption, while
increasing the guaranteed approximation ratio by no more than a

(1+\zeta)
-factor
for an arbitrarily small parameter

\zeta>0
.
This transformation is designed so that
f^{\prime}
is “almost deterministic” in
the sense that the amount of entropy in the image of
f^{\prime}
is arbitrarily close
to
0
.
Moreover, if
f
is computationally efficient, than so is
f^{\prime}
.

The Assumption’s Critical Role

Before presenting the aforementioned reduction, let us explain the critical
role that the subset wish list assumption plays in the mechanisms developed in
the current paper.
Recall that our

(k,\lambda)
-BE
mechanisms are developed as

(k,\lambda)
-maxCGIS
algorithms
\mathtt{Alg}
and that a key feature of these algorithms is that they obey
the INPA property.
This property ensures that given a
k
-cycle graph

G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{CG}_{n}^{k}

and an agent

i\in[n]
,
if

i\notin\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}(G))
,
then

\mathtt{Alg}(G[V-S])=\mathtt{Alg}(G)

for any node subset

S\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(i)
.
In other words, a non-partaking agent
i
cannot affect the outcome of the
algorithm by removing some of its nodes.

The INPA property on the other hand, does not give us any guarantees regarding
agents

i\in[n]

that do partake in
\mathtt{Alg}(G)
.
In particular, if

i\in\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}(G))
,
then we cannot rule out that

i\notin\mathtt{Alg}(G[V-S])

for some node subsets

S\subseteq\alpha^{-1}(i)

even if

S\cap\mathtt{Alg}(G)=\emptyset
;
in fact, simple examples demonstrate that this phenomenon exists in our main
algorithm
\mathtt{LS}
and its derivatives.
Looking at it from the converse direction, agent
i
, that does not partake in
the outcome

\mathtt{Alg}(G[V-S])
,
may manipulate the algorithm, adding the node subset
S
to the graph

G[V-S]
,
so that it does partake in

\mathtt{Alg}(G)

with

i\in\alpha(\mathtt{Alg}(G)-S)
,
thus gaining from the manipulation.

This is exactly where the subset wish list assumption comes into play:
It “guards” our maxCGIS algorithms from such manipulations,
allowing us to concentrate on the removal of node subsets when trying to
establish truthfulness.

The Reduction

Consider a truthful mechanism
f
for the

(k,\lambda)

Matching Lower and Upper Bounds

Since
\rho(\lambda)
appears in both the lower bound of
Thm.
1.3
and the upper bound of
Thm.
1.4
, in the remainder of this
section, we wish to reveal the
properties of the length function
\lambda
that ensure that the two bounds
match or, equivalently, the properties that yield

\rho(\lambda)\,>\,k-1\,.

First, let us understand when each of the components
\rho_{1}
and

\rho_{2}
is dominant.
As the intuition from Figure
6
and the
corresponding lower bound proof in Section
10.2

suggest, the trade-off between
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
is
the trade-off between the social welfare obtained from the rightmost vertical
trading cycle in Figure
6
and that
obtained from the right horizontal trading cycle in
Figure
6
;
that is,

\rho_{1}(\lambda)\geq\rho_{2}(\lambda)\iff\ell^{\prime}\cdot\lambda(\ell^{\prime})\geq\ell\cdot\lambda(\ell)

for the choice of

2\leq\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k
,

\lambda(\ell)>\lambda(\ell^{\prime})
,
for which

\rho(\lambda)=\max\left\{\frac{\ell^{\prime}\cdot\lambda(\ell^{\prime})}{\lambda(\ell)},\,\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\cdot\frac{\ell^{\prime}\cdot\lambda(\ell^{\prime})}{\lambda(\ell)}+1\right\}\,.

Hence, if

\rho_{1}(\lambda)<\rho_{2}(\lambda)
,
then

\displaystyle\rho(\lambda)\,=\,\rho_{2}(\lambda)\,=\,

\displaystyle\max_{2\leq\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k\,:\,\lambda(\ell)>\lambda(\ell^{\prime})}\left\{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\cdot\frac{\ell^{\prime}\cdot\lambda(\ell^{\prime})}{\lambda(\ell)}+1\right\}

\displaystyle<\,

\displaystyle\max_{2\leq\ell<\ell^{\prime}\leq k\,:\,\lambda(\ell)>\lambda(\ell^{\prime})}\left\{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\cdot\frac{\ell\cdot\lambda(\ell)}{\lambda(\ell)}+1\right\}

Moreover, it is necessary that

Lemma B.2
.

The expression
\rho(\lambda)
satisfies

\rho(\lambda)>k-1

\frac{\lambda(k)}{\lambda(\ell^{*})}\,>\,\frac{k-1}{k}\,=\,1-\frac{1}{k}\,,

recalling that
\ell^{*}
is the largest

2\leq\ell\leq k-1

that satisfies

\lambda(\ell)>\lambda(k)
.

In other words, the ratio of the smallest possible agent utility, i.e., the
one obtained from a trading cycle of length
k

utility (obtained from a trading cycle of length

) must be
strictly larger than

1-\frac{1}{k}
.
So, for the condition

\frac{\lambda(k)}{\lambda(\ell^{*})}>1-\frac{1}{k}

presented in Lem.

to

\lambda
must “tail (sufficiently) flatly”.

Any “near-uniform” length function
\lambda
in particular tails flatly and
thus, satisfies the condition of
Lem.
B.2
.
An example for a length function

that tails sufficiently flatly
although it is far from being “near-uniform” is the exponential length

\lambda(\ell)=C\cdot(1-\epsilon)^{\ell}

where

0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{k}

and

We note that although this exponential length function is convex, in general,
convex functions (let alone concave functions) do not necessarily satisfy the
condition of Lem.

they may fail to tail sufficiently flatly.
