The Free Energy of the Elastic Manifold
Gérard Ben Arous, Pax Kivimae

Abstract
This is the first of a series of three papers about the Elastic Manifold model. This classical model proposes a rich picture due to the competition between the inherent disorder and the smoothing effect of elasticity. In this paper, we prove a Parisi formula, i.e. we compute the asymptotic quenched free energy and show it is given by the solution to a certain variational problem. This work comes after a long and distinguished line of work in the Physics literature, going back to the 1980’s (including the foundational work by Daniel Fisher [ 29 ] , Marc Mézard and Giorgio Parisi [ 50 , 51 ] , and more recently by Yan Fyodorov and Pierre Le Doussal [ 34 , 35 ] . Even though the mathematical study of Spin Glasses has seen deep progress in the recent years, after the celebrated work by Michel Talagrand [ 68 , 67 ] , the Elastic Manifold model has been studied from a mathematical perspective, only recently and at zero temperature. The annealed topological complexity has been computed, by the first author with Paul Bourgade and Benjamin McKenna [ 16 , 15 ] . Here we begin the study of this model at positive temperature by computing the quenched free energy. We obtain our Parisi formula by first applying Laplace’s method to reduce the question to a related new family of spherical Spin Glass models with an elastic interaction. The upper bound is then obtained through an interpolation argument initially developed by Francisco Guerra [ 42 ] for the study of Spin Glasses. The lower bound follows by adapting the cavity method along the lines explored by Wei-Kuo Chen [ 23 ] and the multi-species synchronization method of Dmitry Panchenko [ 55 ] . In our next papers [ 19 , 20 ] we will analyze the consequences of this Parisi formula.

Introduction

The study of disordered elastic systems is an area of long-standing importance, whose breadth contains a large number of well-studied models tied together by a rich set of new phenomena. The theory concerns the behavior of random manifolds, under an elastic self-interaction and subjected to a disordered potential. The behavior of the resulting manifold is thus shaped by a competition between an elastic self-interaction, which tends to smoothen and flatten it, and the disordered potential, which promotes rougher configurations. Typical examples involve random polymers, as well as the
d
-dimensional domain walls in the
(d+1)
-dimensional random field Ising model. For more background on these models, further examples, and an overview of their physical phenomenology, we suggest the review
[
41
]
.

Our focus will be on the elastic manifold, a prototypical model in this class. The study of this model was initiated by Fischer
[
29
]
, and extended in subsequent works
[
10
,
9
]
, to be used as an effective model for the behavior of elastic surfaces.

Let us first describe the continuous model. The phase space of this model is the collection of functions
\bm{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
\Omega=[1,L]^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the Hamiltonian is given by:

\mathcal{H}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(\mu\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}-t(\bm{u}(x),\Delta\bm{u}(x))\right)dx^{d}+\int_{\Omega}V(x,\bm{u}(x))dx^{d},

(1.1)

where
V(x,\bm{u}(x))
is the disorder potential, which is taken to be a centered Gaussian process on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
with covariance chosen to be white in
x
and isotropic in
u
, i.e. given for
(x,u),(x^{\prime},u^{\prime})\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
by

\mathbb{E}[V(x,u)V(x^{\prime},u^{\prime})]=\delta(x-x^{\prime})B(\|u-u^{\prime}\|^{2}),

(1.2)

where
\delta(x-x^{\prime})
denotes the Dirac
\delta
-function and
B
is some fixed correlation function.
The model is parametrized by two important real parameters: the mass
\mu
and the elastic interaction strength
t
.

A deep understanding of this model came from the work by Mézard and Parisi
[
50
]
. They studied the behavior of this model in the high-dimensional (i.e., large
N
) limit and were able to compute the quenched free energy by employing the (beautiful but non rigorous) replica symmetry breaking (RSB) scheme classical in the study of spin glasses. Differentiating the free energy, they were then able to obtain results for a number of statistics of the model. This line of works includes many other important contributions
[
28
,
9
,
10
]
.

In this paper, we compute the limiting quenched free energy of this model in a discrete variant, as proposed by the recent works by Fyodorov and Le Doussal
[
34
,
35
]
. This limiting free energy is given in terms of a new variational formula. While the new variational formula given here is complex, it is also very general, applying to more general discrete surface models than the elastic manifold.

We leave the analysis of this functional to our companion paper
[
19
]
, where we show that we can considerably reduce the complexity of the variational formula proven here. Obtaining the results for from this simplification in the continuum limit, and in particular confirming physical predictions for certain scaling exponents, is left for our third companion paper
[
20
]
. Combined, we can confirm much of the celebrated picture obtained in the original work of
[
50
]
.

Finally, we obtain similar results for a related family of spherical models. This generalization is in fact required to show our results for the elastic manifold, but it is also an interesting variant per se, due to the non-trivial effect an external field term may have on the Gibbs measure in this case, the so-called pinning/depinning transition.

1.1
Results on the Limiting Free Energy

Here we give our main results, the first of which is a formula for the large-
N
asymptotics of the quenched free energy of the discrete Elastic manifold model (Theorem
1.3
). As mentioned above, on our way to proving this result, we need to introduce a new spherical variant of the Elastic manifold model. Our computation of the asymptotic free energy for this model (Theorem

Thus now we introduce both models, and state our results. We begin with the discrete elastic manifold model as studied by Fyodorov and Le Doussal
[

]
. Fix integers

, which will be referred to as the length and internal dimension, respectively, and positive numbers

[[1,L]]^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
understood in the periodic sense. Next, we let
V_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a centered Gaussian random field with isotropic covariance given for

by

\mathbb{E}[V_{N}(u)V_{N}(v)]=NB\left(\|u-v\|^{2}_{N}\right).

(1.3)

where here

where
(x,y)_{N}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}y_{i}

An old result of Schoenberg

gives a full description of the possible functions

B(x)=c_{0}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\lambda^{2}x)\nu(d\lambda),

(1.4)

where

The model’s Hamiltonian is then a function on point configurations
\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}
. Such configurations
\bm{u}
may be thought of as functions on the discrete graph
\Omega
, and for
x\in\Omega
, we will use the notation
\bm{u}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
to denote the point such that
\bm{u}(x)_{i}=\bm{u}_{(i,x)}
. However, when
\bm{f}
is a function taking values in
A^{\Omega}
, for some space

, we will use the notation

. Finally, when

we will use the notation

to denote the constant function
\bm{c}(x)=c
.

With these notations set, we define our Hamiltonian for
\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}
by

\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}(\mu I-t\Delta)_{xy}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}V_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x))+\sqrt{N}h\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{u}_{1}(x),

(1.5)

where here
(V_{N,x})_{x\in\Omega}
is a family of i.i.d copies of
V_{N}
, and
\Delta
denotes the graph Laplacian of
\Omega
(where we take the sign convention where
\Delta
is negative semi-definite, following
[
50
,
34
]
). Then for any inverse temperature
\beta>0
, we define the associated partition function

Z_{N,\beta}=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}}e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u})}d\bm{u},

(1.6)

where
d\bm{u}
is the standard Lebesgue measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}
. Given this, we define the (normalized) free energy of the model as

f_{N,\beta}:=|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log Z_{N,\beta}

(1.7)

Results for the Euclidean Model

Our main result is the convergence of
f_{N,\beta}
a.s. to a deterministic value given by a Parisi-type variational problem, which we now define, beginning with some notation. If one has
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}

will refer to the diagonal matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}

\text{di}(\bm{u})_{xy}=\delta_{xy}\bm{u}(x)
for
x,y\in\Omega
. When
D\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
, we will use the shorthand
D+\bm{u}:=D+\text{di}(\bm{u})
. When
A
and
B
are matrices, we will use the notation

to denote that is positive definite.

Now, we will need some functions associated to the discrete Laplacian
\Delta

C.1

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\bm{K}^{-t\Delta}:(0,\infty)^{\Omega}\to(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
be the function defined by the relation

[\left(\bm{K}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{u})-t\Delta\right)^{-1}]_{xx}=\bm{u}(x)\text{ for }x\in\Omega.

(1.8)

Equivalently,
\bm{K}^{-t\Delta}
is the functional inverse of the function
\bm{u}\mapsto([\left(\bm{u}-t\Delta\right)^{-1}]_{xx})_{x\in\Omega}
. We also define for
\bm{u}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, the function

\Lambda^{-t\Delta}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega}K_{x}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{u})\bm{u}(x)-\log\det(\bm{K}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{u})-t\Delta)\right),

(1.9)

This is the Legendre transform of the strictly concave function
\bm{u}\mapsto\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\log\det(\bm{u}-t\Delta)
,so that in particular
|\Omega|\nabla\Lambda^{-t\Delta}(\bm{u})=\bm{K}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{u})
.

Next, we specify the domain for the functional of our variational problem. For
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
we let
\mathcal{P}(S)
denote the set of probability measures on
S
.

Definition 1.2
.

Fix

and let

\zeta\in\mathcal{P}([0,q_{t}])
and
\bm{\Phi}:[0,q_{t}]\to\prod_{x\in\Omega}[0,\bm{q}(x)]
is a coordinate-wise non-decreasing function, which jointly satisfy the following conditions: For each

we have

\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{q}(x)^{-1}\Phi_{x}(s)=q_{t}^{-1}s,

(1.10)

and there there is some

is such that
\zeta([0,q_{*}))=0
and
\Phi_{x}(q_{*})<\bm{q}(x)
for each
x\in\Omega
.

We are now ready to proceed to defining our functional. Given
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})
, we define the continuous (coordinate-wise) non-increasing function
\bm{\delta}:[0,q_{t}]\to[0,\infty)^{\Omega}
by

Theorem 1.3
.

We have a.s. that

\lim_{N\to\infty}f_{N,\beta}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}f_{N,\beta}=\sup_{\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}}\left(\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\beta,\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\right).

Remark 1.4
.

We note that for

we have

\delta_{x}(s)=\Phi_{x}(q_{t})-\Phi_{x}(s)=\bm{q}(x)-\Phi_{x}(s).

(1.14)

Thus for

\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\int_{q_{*}}^{q_{*}^{\prime}}\beta K^{-t\Delta}_{x}(\beta\bm{\delta}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq=\Lambda^{-t\Delta}(\beta\bm{\delta}(q_{*}))-\Lambda^{-t\Delta}(\beta\bm{\delta}(q_{*}^{\prime})).

(1.15)

In particular,

1.1.2
Results for the Spherical Model

Before discussing the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we will introduce a similar result for a more general family of spherical models. This result is not only a major component in the proof of Theorem
1.3
, but also of independent interest due to the spherical models exhibiting a more non-trivial dependence on the external field.

The model here is similar to the above model, except that instead of taking an isotropic field on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
in the presence of a quadratic potential, we instead consider isotropic fields restricted to the sphere. Such isotropic fields are more well known, as they form the family of spherical spin glass models, which has seen extensive study.

In particular, we fix, for each
N\geq 1
, a choice of centered Gaussian random field
H_{N}:S_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
with isotropic covariance given for
\sigma,\tau\in S_{N}
by

\mathbb{E}[H_{N}(\sigma)H_{N}(\tau)]=N\xi((\sigma,\tau)_{N}),

(1.16)

where
\xi:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is some fixed function. As before, it is also a result of Schoenberg
[
60
]
that for such a family to exist for all
N\geq 1
, it is necessary and sufficient that
\xi
is an analytic function on
[-1,1]
with positive coefficients. That is, there are coefficients
\beta_{p}\geq 0
such that

\xi(x)=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\beta_{p}^{2}x^{p},

(1.17)

and such that
\xi(1)<\infty
. We will assume as before that for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, that
\xi(1+\epsilon)<\infty
, so that
\xi
extends to an analytic function on
(-1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)
.

Let us now take some finite set
\Omega
, and fix a positive semi-definite matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
. For each
x\in\Omega
, fix a mixing function
\xi_{x}
in (
1.17
), and let
H_{N,x}
be its associated Hamiltonian (chosen independently for each
x\in\Omega
). Finally, we choose an external field vector
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
. We define the spherical version of our Hamiltonian by taking, for
\bm{u}\in S_{N}^{\Omega}

\mathcal{H}_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D_{xy}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}H_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x))+\sqrt{N}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{h}(x)\bm{u}_{1}(x).

(1.18)

Associated with this, we may define the partition function

Z_{N,\beta,\mathrm{Sph}}=\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}}e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}(\bm{u})}\omega(d\bm{u}),

(1.19)

where here
\omega(d\bm{u})
the surface measure on
S_{N}^{\Omega}
induced by the inclusion
S_{N}^{\Omega}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}
. We similarly define the models normalized free energy as

f_{N,\beta,\mathrm{Sph}}=|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log Z_{N,\beta,\mathrm{Sph}}.

(1.20)

To give the Parisi-functional in this case, we will need a generalization of Definition
1.1
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
K^{D}:\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:D+\bm{u}>0\}\to(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
be the function defined by

[\left(\bm{u}+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})\right)^{-1}]_{xx}=\bm{u}(x)\text{ for }x\in\Omega.

(1.21)

We also define for
\bm{u}\in\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:D+\bm{u}>0\}
, the function

\Lambda^{D}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega}K_{x}^{D}(\bm{u})\bm{u}(x)-\log\det(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u}))\right).

(1.22)

If we denote
\mathscr{Y}:=\mathscr{Y}(\bm{1})
, then for
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}
and any
q_{*}
such that
\zeta([0,q_{*}])=1
and
\Phi_{x}(q_{*})<1
for all
x\in\Omega
, we may define the functional

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{\beta}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=&\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\log\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)+\Lambda^{D}(\beta\bm{\delta}(q_{*}))+\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bigg(\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\beta K_{x}^{D}(\beta\bm{\delta}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq\\
&+\int_{0}^{1}\beta^{2}\zeta([0,u])\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\Phi_{x}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq+\sum_{y\in\Omega}\beta^{2}[D+\bm{K}^{D}(\beta\bm{\delta}(0))^{-1}]_{xy}\bm{h}(x)\bm{h}(y)\bigg)\bigg).\end{split}

(1.23)

As in Remark
1.4
, it may be checked that this functional is independent of the choice of
q_{*}
. Our main result for this class is then the following computation for the quenched free energy.

Theorem 1.6
.

We have a.s. that

\lim_{N\to\infty}f_{N,\beta,\mathrm{Sph}}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}f_{N,\beta,\mathrm{Sph}}=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}}\mathcal{B}_{\beta}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

1.2
Outline of the Proof

We now outline the major steps in our proofs, as well as the general structure of this paper. In Section
2
, we give the proof of with Theorem
1.3
, assuming Theorem
1.6
. To do this, we first reduce to the case of
\beta=1
and
h=0
by reparametrization. Then, we view the partition function
Z_{N,\beta}
as an integral over the contributions of products of spheres of fixed radii using the coarea formula. The Euclidean model, restricted to a product of spheres, may then be related to an inhomogeneous spherical model by rescaling the radius. This model may then be treated by Theorem
1.6
. In particular, we may understand the expected exponential order of this contribution by employing Theorem
1.6
. This task is completed in Corollary
2.4
, which essentially shows that the contribution is precisely

\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\beta,\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(1.25)

With this result, Theorem
1.3
heuristically follows by Laplace’s method.
However, as each of these contributions are random, replacing them with their expected exponential order is non-trivial. Instead, we show a continuity estimate which shows that the surface-area contribution over a given product of spheres is not significantly changed by slightly thickening each sphere. This allows us to work with the sum of contributions over products of thickened spheres instead. With some simple decay estimates to reduce this to a finite sum, we may then control their fluctuations by standard Gaussian concentration inequalities. Taking the thickness to zero then gives Theorem
1.3
.

We now move on to discuss the proof of Theorem
1.6
. The first step, is to show we may express the asymptotic free energy in terms of a different functional (see Theorem

3.1
). This new functional is somewhat artificial, and even in the one-site case does not coincide with the functional obtained by Crisanti and Sommers
[
25
]
. Such auxiliary functionals however have appeared both in the rigorous computations of the free energy in the one-site case by Talagrand
[
67
]
and in the related multi-species spherical model by
[
12
,
11
]
, though if it has a physical meaning is still unclear. Proving that these expressions are equivalent (i.e that Theorem
3.1
implies Theorem
1.6
) is the focus of Section 3.

Now we are left with Theorem
3.1
. On a broad scale, the mechanical basis for our proof begins with a combination of the multi-species synchronization method introduced in the computation of free energy in the convex multi-species spin glass model by Panchenko
[
55
]
, supplemented by a number of apparatuses used in the computation of the free energy in the unipartite spherical spin glass models
[
67
,
65
,
52
,
23
]
.

Specifically, we are able to obtain an upper bound by employing a Guerra-style interpolation argument, and our lower bound employs the extended cavity method, as introduced by Chen
[
23
]
, as well as the Aizenman-Sims-Star scheme
[
3
,
4
]
. Both of these require us to first add a slight perturbation term to the Hamiltonian, small enough to not affect the free energy, but strong enough to force a number of strong properties for the Gibbs measure. For both the upper and lower bound, this allows us to replace the free energy with a difference defined in terms of certain “synchronized” Ruelle probability cascades. This reduces the problem to instead computing asymptotics for a certain functional over such functionals, which is where effects of the confining potential
\mu I-t\Delta
come into play, as computing such functionals on a non-product measure adds a number of technicalities.

The bulk of this analysis is contained in Sections
4
and
5
, which also recall the majority of the technological results and constructions we use from this theory. Roughly, the first section, Section
4
, involves the study of Ruelle probability cascades and the interpretation of the Parisi functional in terms of them, as well as some further approximation and rigidity results. The second, Section
5
, focuses more on the multi-species Ghirlanda-Guerra identities, their relation to “synchronized” Ruelle probability cascades, and the perturbation of the Hamiltonian.

To the reader not versed in these methods, we suggest the essentially self-contained and incredibly well-written monograph of Panchenko
[
53
]
, which we will also use to set the majority of our notation. We will also crucially use the synchronization method of
[
55
]
. We will also reference often the work
[
12
]
, which is quite thoroughly written. Unfortunately, their multi-site model is essentially orthogonal to ours, so we can only use some technical lemmas.

With these tools developed, we finally complete the proof of Theorem
1.6
, which is the subject of Section
6.2
. As discussed above, this is heavily reliant on the tools in Sections
4
and
5
. However, with these tools, the upper bound follows immediately from a Guerra-style interpolation and an application of Talagrand’s positivity principle
[
65
]
. The lower bound is more involved, as we need to use the extended cavity method of Chen
[
23
]
, combined with the multi-species synchronization approach of Panchenko
[
55
]
.

Finally, we provide three appendices. Appendix A provides some basic results about Gaussian processes used throughout the text. Appendix B provides some properties of the functionals
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. Appendix C provides some important technical results about the functions
\bm{K}^{D}
and

1.3
Related Works

We first review the results for the one-site case (i.e. when
|\Omega|=1
), where much more is known. Beginning with the spherical model (
1.18
), an expression for the quenched free energy, which henceforth we will simply refer to as the free energy for simplicity, was derived on a physical basis by Crisanti and Sommers
[
25
]
, employing the replica-symmetry breaking scheme used by Parisi
[
56
]
to treat the Ising spin glass model. A rigorous derivation of these formulas in the even case (i.e. when
\xi
is an even function) was obtained by Talagrand
[
67
,
68
]
, cleverly employing a version of the interpolation method used by Guerra to obtain the upper bound in the Ising case
[
42
]
. The general Ising case was later obtained by Panchenko
[
54
]
, with the general spherical case soon after being obtained by Chen
[
23
]
.

Moving onto the elastic model, an asymptotic formula for the free energy was essentially conjectured by Mézard and Parisi
[
50
,
51
]
, and was further studied in the one-site case by Engel
[
28
]
. The one-site computation was then made rigorous in the note of Klimovsky
[
47
]
, by employing the computation of the spherical case.

We also mention that a number of computations for the free energy of different multi-site models have recently been obtained. Many of these rely on the foundational work of Panchenko
[
55
]
, who computed the free energy of the multi-species Ising spin glass model in the convex case. Following this, Bates and Sohn
[
12
,
11
]
recently computed the free energy for the multi-species spherical spin glass model, again in the convex case. Finally, we mention the work of Ko
[
48
]
, who computed the free energy in the spherical case with contained overlaps.

Moving back to the case of the elastic manifold, recent work of Xu and Zeng
[
69
]
established a formula for the ground-state energy in the replica symmetric regime. These coincide with the general formula for the ground-state energy we obtain in our companion works
[
19
,
20
]
. However, unlike the previously mentioned works, the method of
[
69
]
does not depend on a Guerra-style interpolation.

Instead, their result is based on the (annealed) complexity, which is roughly the logarithm of the expected number of critical points, possibly subject to some conditions. For the elastic manifold, the complexity was first computed by Fyodorov and Le Doussal
[
34
]
. This builds on early computations of the complexity in the one-site case by Fyodorov
[
30
]
, which was followed by Fyodorov and Williams who computed the complexity of only local minima
[
38
]
. The rigor of the results coming from works have mostly been confirmed in the work of Bourgade, McKenna, and the first author
[
16
]
, using a general framework they developed to treat complexity computations in
[
15
]
.

Typically, the complexity is positive, so that one expects an exponentially large number of critical points. However, typically when the system is subject to a suitably strong external field or confining potential, the complexity may vanish, and more so the expected number of critical points or local minima may tend to one. This possibility was first noticed by Cugliandolo and Dean
[
26
]
in a simple case of the one-site spherical model, and has been coined ”topological trivialization” by Fyodorov and Le Doussal
[
33
]
who studied the behavior of in more detail.

If one restricts the count of critical points to those whose energy is near a given value, then the computation of the ground-state energy amounts to refining the computation of the complexity to vanishing order. This approach was pioneered by Fyodorov in the case of pure one-site spherical models
[
31
]
, and indeed is the method used by
[
69
]
to obtain their formula. This approach has since been generalized to mixed one-site spherical models by Belius, Černý, Nakajima and Schmidt
[
14
]
, and finally to the case of multi-species spherical models by Huang and Selke
[
43
]
.

Returning more to related work on complexity, the work
[
38
]
actually studies a more general model than (
1.5
) where the confining term
\frac{\mu}{2}\|x\|^{2}
is related by a more general term
NU\left(\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}_{N}\right)
where
U
is an increasing and convex function. Another common generalization is to instead weaken the condition of isotropy of
V_{N}
to assume that
V_{N}
only possesses isotropic increments. These, and similar models, were studied extensively by Fyodorov et. al. in
[
37
,
32
,
36
]
, many of whose results were later confirmed and extended by Auffinger and Zeng
[
8
,
7
]
.

Much more is known about the complexity of the spherical case. In particular, the complexity in this case was computed in works by Auffinger, Černý, and one of the authors
[
6
,
5
]
. These results match those obtained earlier in physics literature
[
1
,
22
]
, for which they correspond to the zero-temperature case of a more general computation for the complexity of TAP states.

It is at this point that one may ask if computations of the ground state energy can be obtained outside of the topological trivialization regime. For this, it is important to introduce the distinction between the annealed complexity, and the quenched complexity, which is given by the typical exponential order of the number of critical points rather than its average. In general, it is known that these two quantities cannot coincide in general. For example, they are known to differ at sufficiently low energy for
2
RSB mixed spherical spin glass models
[
24
]
.

It is a remarkable result then due to Subag
[
61
]
that in the case of the pure
p
-spin glass model, annealed complexity of local minima concentrates around its average in any level-set above the ground-state energy. This was later extended by Subag and Zeitouni
[
64
]
to general critical points in the pure model, under mild assumptions on
p
, and further to low-lying local minima for a certain class of
1
RSB mixed spin glass models by Subag, Zeitouni, and the first author in
[
21
]
. Not only does this lead to a computation of the ground-state energy, but such results have led to a more complete understanding of the geometry of the low-temperature Gibbs measure, as shown in follow-up works by Subag and Zeitouni
[
62
,
63
]
. In addition, these have played an important role in studying the associated Langevin dynamics, as in the work of Gheissari and Jagannath
[
39
]
. In addition, the work of Jagannath and the first author
[
18
]
discusses further ramifications of this study to concepts such as shattering and metastability. Finally, we mention a new novel approach to the model’s Langevin dynamics by Gheissari and Jagannath and the first author
[
17
]
.

However, outside of these special cases, such concentration results are scarce, with the only full extension being to the non-gradient generalization of the spherical pure
p
-spin model by the second
[
45
]
, some partial results on the spherical bipartite model
[
46
]
, both by the second author, and a general result for the total local minima of the mixed spin glass model by Belius and Schmidt
[

, though with no control on their energy values.

1.4
Notation

Here we will set some notation and conventions, beginning with the thermodynamic notation. With a choice of topological space
\Sigma
, a measurable function
H:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
, and positive Borel measure
m
, we will define the partition function associated to
(H,m)
as

Z(H,m)=\int_{\Sigma}\exp(H(x))m(dx),

(1.26)

when the integral on the right is finite. This definition encompasses all the objects referred to as partition functions above. For example, the partition function
Z_{N}
coincides with
Z(-\mathcal{H}_{N},d\bm{u})
. When
Z(H,m)
is finite, we define the Gibbs measure associated to
(H,m)
as the probability measure on
\Sigma
,
G_{(H,m)}
, given by

G_{(H,m)}(dx)=\frac{\exp(H(x))m(dx)}{Z(H,m)}.

(1.27)

For a measurable function
f:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
, we will often use the notation

\langle f(x)\rangle_{H,m}=G_{(H,m)}(f):=\int_{\Sigma}f(x)G_{(H,m)}(dx).

(1.28)

Associated as well, for any
n\geq 1
, is the (replicated) Gibbs measure on
G_{(H,m)}^{\otimes n}
on
\Sigma^{n}
. When one instead has a function
f:\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
we will also use the mildly abusive notation

\langle f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\rangle_{H,m}=G_{(H,m)}^{\otimes n}(f)=\int_{\Sigma^{n}}f(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n}G_{(H,m)}(dx^{i}),

(1.29)

and in the special case of
n=2
we will often write

\langle f(x,x^{\prime})\rangle_{H,m}=G_{(H,m)}^{\otimes 2}(f)=\int_{\Sigma^{2}}f(x,x^{\prime})G_{(H,m)}(dx)G_{(H,m)}(dx^{\prime}).

(1.30)

In addition, we will often use the notation
\langle*\rangle_{(H,m)}
in place of
G_{(H,m)}
.

Next, we define some subsets that will be useful throughout the work. First, for
0\leq q_{1}<q_{2}\leq\infty
we define the annulus

S_{N}(q_{1},q_{2})=\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:q_{1}\leq\|u\|^{2}_{N}\leq q_{2}\right\},

(1.31)

and for
q>\epsilon>0
we define the thickened sphere

S_{N}^{\epsilon}(q)=S_{N}(q,q+\epsilon).

(1.32)

Next, for
q>0
, let
S_{N}(q)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
denote the sphere of radius
\sqrt{Nq}
, and for
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
let
S_{N}(\bm{q})=\prod_{x\in\Omega}S_{N}(\bm{q}(x))\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}
.
We define
S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})
and
S_{N}(\bm{q}_{0},\bm{q}_{1})
similarly.

From the Spherical to the Euclidean Model

In this section we will prove Theorem
1.3
, our result for the Euclidean model, assuming our result for the spherical model, Theorem
1.6
. The argument follows a few basic steps, beginning with two elementary reductions.

We first reduce to the case of
\beta=1
by absorbing the factor into the other model parameters. If we temporarily write the Parisi-function in terms of the auxiliary parameters
(h,\mu,t,B)
as
\mathcal{P}_{\beta,q}(*;h,\mu,t,B)
, and similarly denote the partition function as
Z_{N,\beta}(h,\mu,t,B)
. Then it is easily checked that

Z_{N,\beta}(h,\mu,t,B)=Z_{N,1}(\beta h,\beta\mu,\beta t,\beta^{2}B),\;\mathcal{P}_{\beta,\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi};h,\mu,t,B)=\mathcal{P}_{1,\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi};\beta h,\beta\mu,\beta t,\beta^{2}B).

(2.1)

In particular, to establish Theorem
1.3
, it suffices to treat the case of
\beta=1
. A similar argument shows that it suffices to treat the case of
\beta=1
to show Theorem
1.6
. Thus we will assume that
\beta=1
henceforth unless otherwise stated. We will also use the notations
Z_{N}:=Z_{N,1}
,
f_{N}:=f_{N,1}
, etc. for simplicity.

The next step is to reduce to the case of
h=0
and is also a fairly simple manipulation, involving taking a fixed translation of target Euclidean space in the model. This does not affect the distribution of the random component of the Hamiltonian, as it is isotropic. However, it changes the linear term through the deterministic quadratic piece, which may be used to absorb the external field term. In particular, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let us use the notation
\mathcal{H}_{N,h}
to indicate the dependence of the function
\mathcal{H}_{N}
given by (
1.5
) on the choice
h
. Then we have the following distributional equality of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{N}

\mathcal{H}_{N,h}(\bm{u})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{H}_{N,0}\left(\bm{u}+\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1}\right)-N|\Omega|\frac{h^{2}}{2\mu},

(2.2)

where
\bm{e}_{1}\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}
is the vector such that
[\bm{e}_{1}(y)]_{j}=\delta_{j1}
for
y\in\Omega
and
1\leq j\leq N
.

In particular, if we use the notation
Z_{N}(h)
to indicate the dependence of the partition function on
h
, we have the following distributional equality

|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log Z_{N}(h)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log Z_{N}(0)+\frac{h^{2}}{2\mu}.

(2.3)

Proof.

We observe that the constant vector
\bm{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
is an eigenvector of
\mu I-t\Delta
with eigenvalue
\mu
, so that

\sum_{y\in\Omega}(\mu I-t\Delta)_{xy}=\sum_{y\in\Omega}(\mu I-t\Delta)_{xy}\bm{1}(y)=\mu\bm{1}(x)=\mu.

(2.4)

In particular, it is easily verified that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}(\mu I-t\Delta)_{xy}\left(\bm{u}(x)+\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1},\bm{u}(y)+\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1}\right)=\\
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}(\mu I-t\Delta)_{xy}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))+\frac{1}{2}N|\Omega|\frac{h^{2}}{\mu}+\sqrt{N}h\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{u}_{1}(x).\end{split}

(2.5)

We recognize the latter two terms as the deterministic part of
\mathcal{H}_{N,h}(\bm{u})
so if we let

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}(\mu I-t\Delta)_{xy}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}V_{N,x}\left(\bm{u}(x)-\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1}\right),

(2.6)

then we see that

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{N}\left(\bm{u}+\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1}\right)=N|\Omega|\frac{h^{2}}{2\mu}+\mathcal{H}_{N,h}(\bm{u}).

(2.7)

Now, as each
V_{N,x}
is isotropic, we see that as a function on
\bm{u}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{N}(\bm{u})
coincides in law with
\mathcal{H}_{N,0}(\bm{u})
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

It is crucial to note that this manipulation is very specific to the Euclidean model in the case of a quadratic potential. Indeed, if one works either in the case of the spherical model, or if one were to take a non-quadratic confining potential, such a simplification need not hold. In particular, in this case one expects the external field to meaningfully affect the statics of the model, as is well known in the one-site case.

With this Lemma, we see it suffices to show Theorem
1.3
in the case of
h=0
and
\beta=1
. Next we analyze the contribution to the partition function
Z_{N}
coming from a product of spheres of different radii. Rescaling, we may use Theorem
1.6
to compute this, and then we may relate the result to
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}
, where the

\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\frac{h^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(-\mu\bm{q}(x)+\log(\bm{q}(x))\right)+\mathcal{B}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta^{\bm{q}},\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{q}}).

(2.8)

Proof.

\begin{split}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\log(\bm{q}(x))+|\Omega|\Lambda^{\bm{q}}(\bm{q}^{-1}\bm{u})=&\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\log(\bm{q}(x))+K_{x}^{\bm{q}}(\bm{q}^{-1}\bm{u})\bm{q}(x)^{-1}\bm{u}(x)\right)\\

(2.10)

Next, if we denote by

(2.11)

and more simply that

\int_{0}^{1}\zeta^{\bm{q}}([0,u])\bm{q}(x)B_{x,\bm{q}(x)}^{\prime}(\Phi_{x}^{\bm{q}}(q))(\Phi_{x}^{\bm{q}})^{\prime}(q)dq=-2\int_{0}^{q_{t}}\zeta([0,u])B^{\prime}(2(\bm{q}(x)-\Phi_{x}(u)))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du.

(2.12)

Altogether this gives the final result by matching terms.
∎

Then we define the restriction of the partition function to

by

Z_{N}(\bm{q})=\int_{S_{N}(\bm{q})}\exp(-\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u}))\omega(d\bm{u}),

Corollary 2.4
.

Assume that
h=0
. For any choice of

\lim_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}(\bm{q})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(2.14)

Proof.

We will freely use the notation of Lemma
2.3
in this proof. Observe that by a change of variables,

Z_{N}(\bm{q})=\left(\prod_{x\in\Omega}\bm{q}(x)^{(N-1)/2}\right)\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}}\exp(-\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{q}\bm{u}))\omega(d\bm{u}).

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{q}\bm{u})=&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}(\mu I-t\Delta)_{x,y}\sqrt{\bm{q}(x)\bm{q}(y)}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}V_{N,x}(\sqrt{\bm{q}(x)}\bm{u}(x))\\

where

(2.28)

. As
\Delta
is negative semi-definite, we see that

Z_{N}^{0}(A_{x,N}(I))\leq\int_{A_{x,N}(I)}\exp\left(-\frac{\mu}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}\right)d\bm{u}.

(2.31)

The right-hand side is easily simplified to

\left(\frac{2\pi}{\mu}\right)^{N(|\Omega|-1)/2}\mathrm{vol}(S^{N-1}(\sqrt{N}))\int_{I}r^{N-1}\exp\left(-N\frac{\mu r^{2}}{2}\right)dr.

(2.32)

By Stirling’s formula we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log(\mathrm{vol}(S^{N-1}(\sqrt{N})))=\log(\sqrt{2\pi e}).

(2.33)

Finally, by Laplace’s method we have that for open
I\subseteq(0,\infty)
we have that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log\left(\int_{I}r^{N-1}\exp\left(-N\frac{\mu r^{2}}{2}\right)dr\right)=\sup_{r\in I}\left(\log(r)-\frac{\mu r^{2}}{2}\right).

(2.34)

In particular, the desired bounds follow from the fact that
\log(r)-\frac{\mu r^{2}}{2}\to-\infty
when either
r\to 0
or
r\to\infty
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

This proof also shows a related statement, needed elsewhere. Namely, there is some small
c>0
, such that for any large enough
r>0

N^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}Z_{N}(A^{N}_{max}(r))\leq-cr^{2}.

(2.35)

With these prerequisites completed, we are ready to begin the proof of Theorem
1.3
. This will consist of using Lemma
2.6
to reduce our consideration to a compact subset of possible
\bm{q}
, then choosing an
\epsilon>0
, we may cover this with a finite number of the
Z_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})
. These may be understood by using Lemma
2.5
to relate them to
Z_{N}(\bm{q})
, where we may use Corollary
2.8

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By Lemma
2.1
, it suffices to assume that
h=0
. Now fix
q\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
. Then combining Corollary
2.8
and Lemma
2.5
with Jensen’s inequality we see that

\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\liminf_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}.

(2.36)

In particular, this establishes the lower bound in expectation

\sup_{\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}}\left(\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\right)\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}.

(2.37)

Before proceeding to the upper bound we prove a basic result about the concentration of
|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log Z_{N}
. We note that by Jensen’s inequality

\log\mathbb{E}Z_{N}=\log\left(\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}(\mu I-t\Delta)_{xy}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))\right)d\bm{u}\right)+N|\Omega|B(0).

(2.38)

As
\Delta
is negative semi-definite, this easily shows that
\log\mathbb{E}Z_{N}
is uniformly bounded above in
N
. Thus by Corollary
A.3
we have for any
\alpha>0
that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left||\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log Z_{N}-|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}\right|>\alpha\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{N|\Omega|\alpha^{2}}{4B(0)}\right).

(2.39)

Now we proceed to show the upper bound. Define

F^{N}_{m}:=\{\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}:\|\bm{u}\|_{N}\in[m,m^{-1}]^{\Omega}\}=\left(A^{N}_{min}(m)\cup A^{N}_{max}(m^{-1})\right)^{c}.

(2.40)

By Lemma
2.6
, for sufficiently small
m>0
we have that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}=\limsup_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}(F^{N}_{m}).

(2.41)

Now fix
\epsilon>0
. By Lemma
2.5
we may choose small enough
\delta>0
such that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{\bm{q}\in[\sqrt{m}/2,2\sqrt{m^{-1}}]^{\Omega}}\bigg(|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}|\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{\delta}(\bm{q})-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}(\bm{q})|\bigg)\leq\epsilon.

(2.42)

Now as
[m,m^{-1}]^{\Omega}
is compact we may find
\bm{q}_{1},\cdots,\bm{q}_{n-1},\bm{q}_{n}\in[\sqrt{m}/2,2\sqrt{m^{-1}}]^{\Omega}
such that

[m,m^{-1}]^{\Omega}\subseteq\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n}\prod_{x\in\Omega}[\sqrt{\bm{q}_{i}(x)},\sqrt{\bm{q}_{i}(x)+\delta}],

(2.43)

so that in particular,
F^{N}_{\delta}\subseteq\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n}S_{N}^{\delta}(\bm{q}_{i})
. Let us denote the events

\mathcal{E}_{N}^{i}=\{N^{-1}\log Z_{N}^{\delta}(\bm{q}_{i})\geq N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{\delta}(\bm{q}_{i})+\epsilon\},\;\;\;\mathcal{E}_{N}=\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n}\left(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{i}\right)^{c}.

(2.44)

We see that

\begin{split}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[I_{\mathcal{E}_{N}}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log Z_{N}(F^{N}_{m})]\leq\epsilon+\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\limsup_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{\delta}(\bm{q}_{i})\leq\\
2\epsilon+\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}(\bm{q}_{i})\leq 2\epsilon+\sup_{\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}}\left(\inf_{\zeta,\bm{\Phi}\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\right).\end{split}

(2.45)

We note that by Proposition
A.2
, we have for each
i
, that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{N}^{i})\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{N\epsilon^{2}}{4|\Omega|B(0)}\right).

(2.46)

Thus by the Borel-Cantelli Lemma we see that a.s.

\limsup_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log Z_{N}(F^{N}_{m})\leq 2\epsilon+\sup_{\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}}\left(\inf_{\zeta,\bm{\Phi}\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\right).

(2.47)

Using (
2.39
), (
2.41
) and the observation that
|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}\leq|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}Z_{N}
is uniformly bounded in
N
, we see that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}(F^{N}_{m})\leq 2\epsilon+\sup_{\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}}\left(\inf_{\zeta,\bm{\Phi}\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\right).

(2.48)

Thus we have established the desired convergence in expectation. The statement about a.s. convergence follows immediately from (
2.39
) and another application of the Borel-Cantelli Lemma.
∎

We complete this section by also noting two corollaries of this proof that will be useful in our companion works
[

,
20
]
. The first is that this proof actually establishes a more general formula for the free energy of the partition function over sets of restricted radii in the case where
h=0
. In particular, our proof immediately gives the following result.

Corollary 2.8
.

Assume that
h=0
. Then for any open subset
U\subseteq[0,\infty)^{\Omega}
such that
U\cap(0,\infty)^{\Omega}\neq\varnothing
, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}(\{\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}:\|\bm{u}\|^{2}_{N}\in U\})=\sup_{\bm{q}\in U}\left(\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\right).

(2.49)

This corollary combined with Lemma
2.6
also gives the following result.

Corollary 2.9
.

There is sufficiently small

such that

\sup_{\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}}\left(\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\right)=\sup_{\bm{q}\in[m,m^{-1}]^{\Omega}}\left(\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\right).

(2.50)

3
A First Step to a Crisanti-Sommers Formula

In this section, we introduce a new formula for the asymptotic free energy of the spherical variant of the elastic manifold, given by Theorem
3.1
. The proof of this new formula will be the goal of the subsequent sections of this work.

The focus of this section is to prove that this alternate Parisi formula implies our Theorem
1.6
, which generalizes to the elastic manifold context the well-know Crisanti-Sommers formula
[
25
]
about the one-site model (i.e spherical spin glasses). As we saw in Section
2
, this implies our main result about the asymptotic free energy for the Euclidean model of the elastic manifold (i.e. Theorem
1.3
).

In order to introduce this variant, we now need to introduce an alternative version of the Parisi functional, which will be denoted as
\mathcal{A}
. To begin, for a choice of
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}
, we define the function
\bm{d}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)^{\Omega}
by

\bm{d}(u)=\int_{u}^{1}\zeta([0,s])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(s)ds.

(3.1)

We then choose
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
such that
D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0)>0
. With these choices, we define the functional

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi},\bm{b})=\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\bigg[&|\Omega|\log(2\pi)+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{1}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(s))^{-1}]_{xx}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds\\
&+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\bm{b}(x)-\int_{0}^{1}\zeta([0,s])\Phi_{x}(s)\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds\right)\\
&+\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0))^{-1}]_{xy}\left(\bm{h}(x)\bm{h}(y)+\delta_{xy}\xi_{x}^{\prime}(0)\right)\bigg],\end{split}

(3.2)

We then minimize over
\bm{b}
to define the functional

\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:\;D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0)>0}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi},\bm{b}).

(3.3)

While this functional is defined on all of
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}
, for technical reasons, we restrict this functional a subset of finite measures,

\mathscr{Y}_{fin}=\{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}:\text{supp}(\zeta)\text{ is a finite set}\}.

(3.4)

We now give our new computation for the asymptotic free energy in terms of
\mathcal{A}
.

Theorem 3.1

(A New Parisi Formula)
.

\lim_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(3.5)

We mention that using the functional
\mathcal{A}
is important in our Parisi formula, as
\mathcal{A}
is what appears more naturally in our analysis of the free energy. However
\mathcal{B}
is both more simple to analyze and also the functional which appears by applying the replica-trick. As discussed above, this matches a similar issue in the original proof of the even one-site case by Talagrand
[
67
]
. One may also see the work
[
11
]
which establishes a similar result in the case of multi-species spin glasses.

Remark 3.2
.

We believe that the restriction to finite measures in Theorem
3.1
should not affect the infimum of
\mathcal{A}
, or more specifically, that

\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(3.6)

However, as this result is not needed here, we have not pursued this issue for brevity. We do however, show the analogous result for the functional
\mathcal{B}
in Appendix
B
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
is the focus of all subsequent sections in this paper. The focus of the remainder of this section is to prove Theorem
1.6
using Theorem
3.1
. The key to this is the following result, which shows that these functionals have the same infimum over
\mathscr{Y}_{fin}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

We have that

\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(3.7)

Given this result, we may now easily obtain Theorem
1.6
from Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

By Theorem
3.1
and Propositions
3.3
and
B.1
we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}}\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(3.8)

The a.s. statement then follows from this, Proposition
A.2
and the Borel-Cantelli lemma.
∎

To prepare for the proof of Proposition
3.3
, we must study
\mathscr{Y}_{fin}
. To begin, let us choose
r\geq 1
, as well as a sequence of real numbers

0=m_{0}<m_{1}<\cdots<m_{r-1}<m_{r}=1,

(3.9)

and a sequence
(\bm{s}^{k}(x))_{k=1}^{r}
, for each
x\in\Omega
, such that

0=\bm{s}^{0}(x)\leq\bm{s}^{1}(x)\leq\cdots\leq\bm{s}^{r}(x)<\bm{s}^{r+1}(x)=1.

(3.10)

We will use the notations
\vec{m}=(m_{i})_{0\leq i\leq r-1}
and
\vec{\bm{s}}=(\bm{s}^{i})_{1\leq i\leq r}
, and similarly for other such sequences. In the next remark, we summarize how we may associate a pair
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}
to the pair
(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
.

Remark 3.4
.

Let us take sequences
\vec{m}
and
\vec{\bm{s}}
as in (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) of length parameter
r
. By defining

s^{k}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{s}^{k}(x)

(3.11)

we obtain a sequence
\vec{s}:=(s^{k})_{k=1}^{r}
of the form

0=s^{0}\leq s^{1}\leq\dots\leq s^{r}<s^{r+1}=1.

(3.12)

We may associate with
(\vec{m},\vec{s})
a discrete probability measure on
[0,1]
,
\zeta
, by

\zeta=\sum_{0\leq k\leq r}(m_{k}-m_{k-1})\delta_{s^{k}},

(3.13)

where
\delta_{s}
denotes the Dirac
\delta
-function supported at
s
. We also may associate a function
\bm{\Phi}:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{\Omega}
by letting
\bm{\Phi}(s^{k})=\bm{s}^{k}
, then extending the function to all of
[0,1]
by linear interpolation. It is easily checked that this satisfies (
1.10
). Moreover, we see that
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}
, and more specifically that

(\Phi_{x})_{*}(\zeta)=\sum_{0\leq k\leq r}(m_{k}-m_{k-1})\delta_{\bm{s}^{k}}.

(3.14)

We note two things about the process given in Remark
3.4
. The first is that when one has some
\bm{s}^{k}=\bm{s}^{k+1}
for some
k
, the measure no longer depends on
m_{k}
, so that the process is not injective. However, restricted to the set of choices where all
\bm{s}^{k}
are distinct, the process is indeed injective. In particular, this construction overparameterized its image, though only mildly.

The second and more important fact is that this process does not surject onto
\mathscr{Y}_{fin}
. Indeed, to obtain a surjection, we would have to include all possible
\bm{\Phi}
which take the values
\bm{\Phi}(s^{k})=\bm{s}^{k}
, instead of simply taking them to be a linear interpolation. However, in Appendix
B
, it is checked that if two choices of
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}),(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}^{\prime})\in\mathscr{Y}
are such that
\bm{\Phi}(s)=\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}(s)
for
s\in\text{supp}(\zeta)
, then one has that
\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}^{\prime})
(
Proposition
B.5
) and
\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}^{\prime})
(Proposition
B.4
). So, as far as these functionals concerned, we can restrict to this class of finite measures.

In particular, let us define
\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}):=\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
, where
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}
is the pair associated to
(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
by Remark
3.4
. We define
\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
identically. Then the above discussion demonstrates the following:

\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\text{ and }\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(3.15)

Our next step will be to evaluate
\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
and
\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
. To evaluate
\mathcal{B}
we define a sequence for
x\in\Omega
and
1\leq l\leq r
by

\delta^{l}_{x}=\sum_{l\leq k\leq l}m_{k}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x)-\bm{s}^{k}(x)),\;\;\delta^{r+1}_{x}=0.

(3.16)

Routine computation then gives that

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\bigg[&|\Omega|\log(2\pi)+|\Omega|\Lambda^{D}(\bm{\delta}^{r})-\sum_{1\leq k<r}\frac{|\Omega|}{m_{k}}\bigg(\Lambda^{D}(\bm{\delta}^{k+1})-\Lambda^{D}(\bm{\delta}^{k})\bigg)\\
&+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(-K_{x}^{D}(\bm{\delta}^{1})\bm{s}^{1}(x)+\sum_{1\leq k<r}m_{k}(\xi_{x}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x))-\xi_{x}(\bm{s}^{k}(x)))\right)\\
&+\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{\delta}^{1}))^{-1}]_{xy}\bm{h}(x)\bm{h}(y)\bigg].\end{split}

(3.17)

Next we give an expression for
\mathcal{A}
. Given such choices we may inductively define for
x\in\Omega
and
1\leq l\leq r

\bm{d}^{l}(x)=\sum_{l\leq k\leq r}m_{k}(\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x))-\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{k}(x))),\;\;\bm{d}^{r+1}(x)=0.

(3.18)

Finally let us take a choice of some
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
such that
D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1}>0
. Associated with these choices, we may compute that

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})=\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\bigg[&|\Omega|\log(2\pi)+\sum_{1\leq k\leq r}\frac{1}{m_{k}}\log\frac{\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k+1})}{\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k})}-\log\det(D+\bm{b})\\
&+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\bm{b}(x)-\sum_{1\leq k<r}m_{k}(\theta_{x}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x))-\theta_{x}(\bm{s}^{k}(x)))\right)\\
&+\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xy}\left(\bm{h}(x)\bm{h}(y)+\delta_{xy}\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{1}(x))\right)\bigg],\end{split}

(3.19)

where here
\theta_{x}(r)=\xi_{x}^{\prime}(r)-\xi_{x}(r)+\xi_{x}(0)
. Then we finally have that

\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\inf_{\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:\;D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1}>0}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b}).

(3.20)

The main technical result we use to establish Proposition
3.3
is the following.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let us assume that for each
x\in\Omega
, we have that
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(0),\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0)\neq 0
. Then for each
r\geq 1
and fixed choice of
\vec{m}
, we have that

\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}}}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}}}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}),

(3.21)

where here
\vec{\bm{s}}
is taken over all choices of (
3.10
).

An important difference between Propositions
3.3
and
3.5
, beyond the parametrization, is that we do not need to minimize over
\vec{m}
, let alone the choice of
r\geq 1
. In particular, Proposition
3.5
gives a much stronger result on the minimization than Proposition
3.3
, under only a slight restriction on
\bm{\xi}
. For example, keeping
r\geq 1
fixed, but taking the infimum over
\vec{m}
, we see that Proposition
3.5
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let us assume that for each
x\in\Omega
, we have that
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(0),\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0)\neq 0
. For
r\geq 1
, let us define

\mathscr{Y}_{r}:=\{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}:\text{supp}(\bm{\Phi}_{*}(\zeta))\text{ consists of at-most }r\text{ points}\}.

(3.22)

Then we have that

\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{r}}\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{r}}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(3.23)

In particular, using some final continuity results from the appendices, we may obtain Proposition
3.3
from Proposition
3.5
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Minimization of Proposition
3.5
over
\vec{m}
, combined with (
3.15
), demonstrates that if we have that
\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0),\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
for each
x\in\Omega
then

\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(3.24)

What is left is to show this is true if we remove the condition on
\bm{\xi}
. However, by Proposition
B.6
the right hand side is continuous in
\bm{\xi}
. Moreover, by applying Corollary
A.5
and Theorem
3.1
we see that the same holds for
\mathcal{A}
. In particular, the general statement follows from (
3.24
) and a density argument in
\bm{\xi}
.
∎

The remainder of this section is devoted to proving Proposition
3.5
. The proof follows the standard framework of variational calculus. We compute the derivatives of each functional in
\vec{\bm{s}}
(and for
\mathcal{A}
, also in
\bm{b}
). Assuming these derivatives vanish for hold some
(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})
, then we show that
\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})=\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
. After this, we verify that each functional indeed has a global minimizer for which all derivatives vanish. This fact is non-trivial, as the functionals’ shared domain is neither compact nor open, and in fact turns out to be quite difficult.

For simplicity, we fix
D
and use the abusive notation
K:=K^{D}
and
\Lambda:=\Lambda^{D}
for this section alone. We begin with our result which compares the functionals, roughly assuming a set of minimization equations.

Lemma 3.7
.

Assume that
(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})
satisfies, for all
x\in\Omega
and
1\leq l<r
,

m_{l}\left(\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{l+1}(x))-\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{l}(x))\right)=K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{l+1})-K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{l}),

(3.25)

[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{r})^{-1}]_{xx}=\bm{\delta}^{r}(x)=1-\bm{s}^{r}(x).

(3.26)

Then
\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})=\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
.

Proof.

We may write (
3.25
) and (
3.26
) as

\bm{d}^{l+1}(x)-\bm{d}^{l}(x)=K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{l+1})-K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{l})\text{ and }\bm{b}(x)-\bm{d}^{r}(x)=K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k}).

(3.27)

From this and (
3.26
), we inductively conclude, for each
1\leq l\leq r
and
x\in\Omega
that

\bm{b}(x)-\bm{d}^{l}(x)=K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{l})\text{ and }[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{l})^{-1}]_{xx}=\bm{\delta}^{l}(x).

(3.28)

Employing this and canceling the term involving only
\log\det(D+\bm{b})
we see that

\begin{split}\sum_{1\leq k\leq r}\frac{1}{m_{k}}\log\frac{\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k+1})}{\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k})}-\log\det(D+\bm{b})=\\
-\log\det(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}^{r}))+\sum_{1\leq k<r}\frac{1}{m_{k}}\bigg(\log\det(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}^{k+1}))-\log\det(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}^{k}))\bigg).\end{split}

(3.29)

Using (
3.25
) and noting that
\bm{\delta}^{l}(x)-\bm{\delta}^{l+1}(x)=m_{l}(\bm{s}^{l+1}(x)-\bm{s}^{l}(x))
, we see that

\begin{split}\sum_{1\leq k<r}m_{k}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x)\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x))-\bm{s}^{k}(x)\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{k}(x)))=\\
\sum_{1\leq k<r}(\bm{\delta}^{k}(x)-\bm{\delta}^{k+1}(x))\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x))+\bm{s}^{k}(x)m_{k}(\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x))-\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{k}(x)))=\\
\sum_{1\leq k<r}(\bm{\delta}^{k}(x)-\bm{\delta}^{k+1}(x))\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x))+\sum_{1\leq k<r}\bm{s}^{k}(x)\left(-K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k})+K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k+1})\right).\end{split}

(3.30)

We may write the first summation on the right as

\begin{split}&\bm{\delta}^{1}(x)\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{1}(x))-\bm{\delta}^{r}(x)\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{r}(x))+\sum_{1\leq k<r}\bm{\delta}^{k}(x)\left(\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x))-\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{k}(x))\right)=\\
&\bm{\delta}^{1}(x)\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{1}(x))-\bm{\delta}^{r}(x)\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{r}(x))-\sum_{1\leq k<r}\frac{\bm{\delta}^{k}(x)}{m_{k}}\left(K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k+1})-K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k})\right).\end{split}

(3.31)

Similarly, we may write the second summation on the right as

\begin{split}&\bm{s}^{r}(x)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{r})-\bm{s}^{1}(x)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{1})+\sum_{1\leq k<r}K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k+1})(-\bm{s}^{k+1}(x)+\bm{s}^{k}(x))=\\
&\bm{s}^{r}(x)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{r})-\bm{s}^{1}(x)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{1})-\sum_{1\leq k<r}\frac{K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k+1})}{m_{k}}(\bm{\delta}^{k+1}(x)-\bm{\delta}^{k}(x)).\end{split}

(3.32)

In particular we may write the quantity in (
3.30
) as

\begin{split}\bm{\delta}^{1}(x)\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{1}(x))-\bm{\delta}^{r}(x)\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{r}(x))+\bm{s}^{r}(x)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{r})-\bm{s}^{1}(x)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{1})\\
+\sum_{1\leq k<r}\frac{1}{m_{k}}\left(\bm{\delta}^{k}(x)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k})-\bm{\delta}^{k+1}(x)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k+1})\right).\end{split}

(3.33)

Combining this with (
3.29
) and recalling the definition of
\Lambda
(
1.22
) we see that,

\begin{split}|\Omega|\Lambda(\bm{\delta}^{r})&-|\Omega|\sum_{1\leq k<r}\frac{1}{m_{k}}\bigg(\Lambda(\bm{\delta}^{k+1})-\Lambda(\bm{\delta}^{k})\bigg)=\\
&-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bigg(\sum_{1\leq k<r}m_{k}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x)\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{k+1}(x))-\bm{s}^{k}(x)\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{k}(x)))\bigg)\\
&+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bigg(K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{r})+\bm{\delta}^{1}(x)\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{1}(x))-\bm{\delta}^{r}(x)\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{r}(x))-\bm{s}^{1}(x)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{1})\bigg)\\
&+\sum_{1\leq k\leq r}\frac{1}{m_{k}}\log\frac{\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k+1})}{\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k})}-\log\det(D+\bm{b}).\end{split}

(3.34)

In particular, canceling a common summation and recalling that
[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xx}=\bm{\delta}^{1}(x)
, we see that

2|\Omega|\left(\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})-\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})\right)=\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bigg(\bm{b}(x)+\bm{\delta}^{r}(x)\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{r}(x))-K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{r})\bigg).

(3.35)

Using the inverse of (
3.26
) we see that

\bm{b}(x)-(1-\bm{s}^{r}(x))\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{r}(x))=K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{r}),

(3.36)

which shows all the terms on the right-hand of (
3.35
) vanishes, completing the proof.
∎

While we described the equations in the above lemma as minimization equations, we now further elaborate on them. The first set of equations essentially emerge by setting all derivatives of either the map
\vec{\bm{s}}\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\vec{s},\vec{m})
or
\vec{\bm{s}}\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\vec{s},\vec{m})
to zero. The second set of equations follow by simultaneously setting the derivatives of the map
\bm{b}\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\vec{s},\vec{m},\bm{b})
to zero.

However, even if we assume each functional has a minimizer, it is not clear that these derivatives vanish, and it is still difficult to obtain the equations of Lemma
3.7
directly from the derivatives. Our next lemma establishes these claims for
\mathcal{B}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Fix
r\geq 1
and
\vec{m}
. Then if
\vec{\bm{s}}
is a minimizer of
\vec{\bm{s}}\mapsto\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
, for each
x\in\Omega
and
1\leq l<r
, (
3.25
) holds.

Proof of Lemma
3.8
.

We first rewrite

\begin{split}2\mathcal{B}&(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\log(2\pi)+\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(-K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{1})\bm{s}^{1}(x)+\xi_{x}(1)+\sum_{1\leq k\leq r}(m_{k}-m_{k-1})\xi_{x}(\bm{s}^{k}(x))\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}^{1}))^{-1}]_{xy}\bm{h}(x)\bm{h}(y)-\frac{1}{m_{1}}\Lambda(\bm{\delta}^{1})+\sum_{2\leq k\leq r}\left(\frac{1}{m_{k-1}}-\frac{1}{m_{k}}\right)\Lambda(\bm{\delta}^{k}).\end{split}

(3.37)

We compute that

|\Omega|\frac{d\Lambda(\bm{\delta}^{k})}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}=K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k}(x))\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{k}(x)}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}.

(3.38)

We also note that

\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{l}(x)}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}=0\text{ if }l>n,\;\;\;\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{l}(x)}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}=-\left(m_{n-1}-m_{n}\right)\text{ if }l<n,\;\;\;\frac{d\bm{\delta}^{n}(x)}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}=-m_{n}.

(3.39)

Using these formulas as well as the simple manipulation

(m_{n}-m_{n-1})^{-1}m_{n}\left(\frac{1}{m_{n-1}}-\frac{1}{m_{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{m_{n-1}},

(3.40)

we see that if we denote the derivative in the
x
-th coordinate by
\nabla_{x}
, than for
r\geq n>1

\begin{split}2|\Omega|(m_{n}&-m_{n-1})^{-1}\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\sum_{y\in\Omega}\nabla_{x}K_{y}(\bm{\delta}^{1})\bm{s}^{1}(y)-\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{n}(x))\\
&+\sum_{w,y,z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}^{1}))^{-1}]_{wy}[(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}^{1}))^{-1}]_{wz}\nabla_{x}K_{w}(\bm{\delta}^{1})\bm{h}(y)\bm{h}(z)\\
&-\frac{1}{m_{1}}K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{1})-\sum_{2\leq k<n}\left(\frac{1}{m_{k-1}}-\frac{1}{m_{k}}\right)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k})+\frac{1}{m_{n-1}}K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{n}).\end{split}

(3.41)

Next, we obtain that

\begin{split}2|\Omega|m^{-1}_{1}\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{1}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},&\vec{m})=-\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{1}(x))+\sum_{y\in\Omega}\nabla_{x}K_{y}(\bm{\delta}^{1})\bm{s}^{1}(y)\\
&+\sum_{w,y,z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}^{1}))^{-1}]_{wy}[(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}^{1}))^{-1}]_{wz}\nabla_{x}K_{w}(\bm{\delta}^{1})\bm{h}(y)\bm{h}(z).\end{split}

(3.42)

Let us assume for the moment that both (
3.41
) and (
3.42
) vanish. Then equating these expressions and canceling like terms shows that for any
1\leq n\leq r
and
x\in\Omega

\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{s}^{n}(x))=\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{1}(x))-\frac{1}{m_{1}}K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{1})-\sum_{2\leq k<n}\left(\frac{1}{m_{k-1}}-\frac{1}{m_{k}}\right)K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{k})+\frac{1}{m_{n-1}}K_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{n}).

(3.43)

Subtraction of these relations from each other yields (
3.25
).

Next we show that if
\vec{\bm{s}}
is a minimizer of
\vec{\bm{s}}\mapsto\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
, then both expressions in (
3.41
) and (
3.42
) vanish, or equivalently that all derivatives in
\bm{s}^{k}(x)
vanish. If a minimizer satisfies
0<\bm{s}^{1}(x)<\dots<\bm{s}^{r-1}(x)<\bm{s}^{r}(x)<1
for each
x\in\Omega
, this is obvious, with the problems occurring when there are equalities, so that we are on the boundary of functionals domain. On the other hand, if say
\bm{s}^{n-1}(x)=\bm{s}^{n}(x)
for some
n>1
, we see that
\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}^{n})=\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}^{n-1})
so one can check that

(m_{n-1}-m_{n})^{-1}\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=(m_{n-2}-m_{n-1})^{-1}\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{n-1}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}).

(3.44)

Applying this inductively to a maximal chain of equalities
\bm{s}^{k}(x)=\bm{s}^{k+1}(x)=\dots=\bm{s}^{l}(x)
, we see that the derivative in
\bm{s}^{k}(x)
coincides with the derivative in
\bm{s}^{l}(x)
up to a positive constant. In particular, this shows derivatives of all must vanish unless either
k=0
or
l=r+1
. However if
l=r+1
the minimizer must have that
\bm{s}^{r+1}(x)=1
and so is not in the domain, so we may assume that
k=0
and
l<r+1
. Then (
3.44
) shows in particular that
\bm{s}^{1}(x)=0
and that
\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{1}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})\geq 0
, and finally that to conclude all derivatives vanish it suffices to show that
\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{1}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=0
.

Now note that the vector
(\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{1}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}))_{x\in\Omega}
has all non-negative entries. On the other hand, note that the first and last terms on the right-hand side of (
3.42
) are strictly negative, so that
moving these terms to the left-hand side, we conclude the second term is non-negative. In particular that
(\sum_{y\in\Omega}\nabla_{x}K_{y}(\bm{\delta}^{1})\bm{s}^{1}(y))_{x\in\Omega}
has non-negative entries. However the matrix
\nabla_{x}K_{y}(\bm{\delta}^{1})
is negative-definite (see Proposition
C.1
), so we conclude that all entries of
(\sum_{y\in\Omega}\nabla_{x}K_{y}(\bm{\delta}^{1})\bm{s}^{1}(y))_{x\in\Omega}
are infact zero. Again using (
3.42
) we conclude that the entries of
(\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{1}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}))_{x\in\Omega}
are non-positive, so combined with the non-negativity shown above, we have completed the proof that
\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{1}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=0
for all
x\in\Omega
.
∎

Next, we obtain a result for minimizers of
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Fix
r\geq 1
and
\vec{m}
, and assume that
\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0),\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(0)\neq 0
for each
x\in\Omega
. Then if
(\vec{\bm{s}},\bm{b})
is a minimizer of the
(\vec{\bm{s}},\bm{b})\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})
one has for each
x\in\Omega
and
1\leq l\leq r
, that (
3.25
) and (
3.26
) hold.

Proof.

We first rewrite

\begin{split}2|\Omega|\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})=&|\Omega|\log(2\pi)-\frac{1}{m_{1}}\log\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})\\
&+\sum_{2\leq k\leq r}\left(\frac{1}{m_{k-1}}-\frac{1}{m_{k}}\right)\log\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k})\\
&+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\bm{b}(x)+\theta_{x}(1)+\sum_{1\leq k\leq r}(m_{k}-m_{k-1})\theta_{x}(\bm{s}^{k}(x))\right)\\
&+\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xy}\left(\bm{h}(x)\bm{h}(y)+\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{1}(x))\right).\end{split}

(3.45)

We note that

\frac{d\bm{d}^{l}(x)}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}=0\text{ if }l>n,\;\;\;\frac{d\bm{d}^{l}(x)}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}=\left(m_{n-1}-m_{n}\right)\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(\bm{s}^{n}(x))\text{ if }l<n,\;\;\;\frac{d\bm{d}^{n}(x)}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}=-m_{n}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(\bm{s}^{n}(x)).

(3.46)

Then using that
\theta_{x}^{\prime}(s)=s\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(s)
and (
3.40
) we see that for
1<n\leq r

\begin{split}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(\bm{s}^{n}(x))^{-1}&2|\Omega|(m_{n}-m_{n-1})^{-1}\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{n}(x)}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})=\\
&-\frac{1}{m_{1}}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xx}+\sum_{2\leq k<n}\left(\frac{1}{m_{k-1}}-\frac{1}{m_{k}}\right)[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k})^{-1}]_{xx}\\
&+\frac{1}{m_{n-1}}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{n})^{-1}]_{xx}+\bm{s}^{n}(x)\\
&-\sum_{y,z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{yx}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xz}(\xi_{y}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{1}(y))\delta_{yz}+\bm{h}(y)\bm{h}(z)).\end{split}

(3.47)

The
n=1
case gives instead that

\begin{split}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(\bm{s}^{1}(x))^{-1}2|\Omega|m_{1}^{-1}\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{1}(x)}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})=-\sum_{y,z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{yx}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xz}\\
\times(\xi_{y}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{1}(y))\delta_{yz}+\bm{h}(y)\bm{h}(z))+\bm{s}^{1}(x).\end{split}

(3.48)

Let us assume for the moment that we have shown for each
1\leq n\leq r
and
x\in\Omega

\begin{split}0=&-\frac{1}{m_{1}}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xx}+\sum_{2\leq k<n}\left(\frac{1}{m_{k-1}}-\frac{1}{m_{k}}\right)[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k})^{-1}]_{xx}\\
&+\frac{1}{m_{n-1}}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{n})^{-1}]_{xx}+\bm{s}^{n}(x)\\
&-\sum_{y,z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{yx}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xz}(\xi_{y}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{1}(y))\delta_{yz}+\bm{h}(y)\bm{h}(z)).\end{split}

(3.49)

Subtracting these equations from each other we obtain that for
r>n\geq 1

\bm{s}^{n+1}(x)-\bm{s}^{n}(x)=\frac{1}{m_{n}}\bigg([(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{n})^{-1}]_{xx}-[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{n+1})^{-1}]_{xx}\bigg).

(3.50)

Minimization over
\bm{b}(x)
yields (
3.64
), and subtracting and (
3.49
) with
n=r
yields (
3.26
). Combined with (
3.50
) we obtain that for any
1\leq k\leq r

[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k})^{-1}]_{xx}=\bm{\delta}^{k}(x).

(3.51)

Applying the inverse function
\bm{K}
to this relation, and then taking differences, yields (
3.25
).

What is left is to verify that (
3.49
) holds. By an argument similar to that in Lemma
3.8
, it suffices to assume that
\bm{s}^{1}(x)=0
for some
x\in\Omega
and show that
\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{1}(x)}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})=0
. However, if
\bm{s}^{1}(x)=0
, then we have that

\begin{split}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(0)^{-1}2|\Omega|m_{1}^{-1}\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{1}(x)}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})&=\\
-\sum_{y,z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{yx}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xz}(\xi_{y}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{1}(y))\delta_{yz}+\bm{h}(y)\bm{h}(z))&\leq\\
-[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xx}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xx}\xi_{x}^{\prime}(0)&<0.\end{split}

(3.52)

This contradicts the minimization assumption.
∎

Now finally we give a lemma which establishes the existence of minimizers.

Lemma 3.10
.

Fix
\vec{m}
as above, and assume that
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(0)\neq 0
and
\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0)\neq 0
for each
x\in\Omega

(\vec{\bm{s}},\bm{b})\to\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})
and
\vec{\bm{s}}\to\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})
possess a minimizer.

Before proceeding to the proof of Lemma
3.10
we complete the proof of Proposition
3.5
.

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

Lemmas
3.7
,
3.9
, and
3.10
imply that

\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}}}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})\leq\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}},\bm{b}}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})=\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}}}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}).

(3.53)

For the reverse inequality, note that Lemmas
3.8
and
3.10
implies that there is
\vec{\bm{s}}^{*}
such
\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}}}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}}^{*},\vec{m})
and such that (
3.25
) holds. However, observe that once
(\vec{\bm{s}}^{*},\vec{m})
is fixed, one may apply
\bm{K}
to find a unique
\bm{b}^{*}
such that
(\vec{\bm{s}}^{*},\vec{m},\bm{b}^{*})

\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}}}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}}^{*},\vec{m})=\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}}^{*},\vec{m},\bm{b}^{*})\geq\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}}}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}).

(3.54)

∎

All that is left is to prove Lemma
3.10
. We begin with a technical result we will use to show the existence of minimizers for
\mathcal{B}
.

Lemma 3.11
.

For a choice of
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}
, and
x\in\Omega
, we define the function

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{x}(q):=&\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{q}\nabla_{y}K_{x}(\bm{\delta}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right)+\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(q))\\
&+\sum_{y,z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}(0)))^{-1}]_{yx}[(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}(0)))^{-1}]_{zx}\bm{h}(y)\bm{h}(z).\end{split}

(3.55)

Then there exists some small
c>0
, such that if we denote
q_{M}=\sup(\text{supp}(\zeta))
and assume that
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
is such that
\mathcal{G}_{x}(q_{M})\geq 0
for each
x\in\Omega
, then we have that

\text{supp}(\bm{\Phi}_{*}(\zeta))\in[0,1-c]^{\Omega}.

(3.56)

Proof.

For the moment, let us choose some
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
in
\mathscr{Y}
. We observe the trivial identity

\delta_{x}(q)=\int_{q}^{1}\zeta([0,u])\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\leq\int_{q}^{1}\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du=1-\Phi_{x}(q)\leq 1.

(3.57)

By applying Theorem
C.2
, there is
\epsilon:=\epsilon(D)>0
such that

\begin{split}\sum_{y\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{q_{M}}\nabla_{y}K_{x}(\bm{\delta}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq\leq-\epsilon\int_{0}^{q_{M}}\delta_{x}(q)^{-2}\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq+\epsilon^{-1}\sum_{y\in\Omega\setminus\{x\}}\int_{0}^{q_{M}}d\Phi_{y}(q)\leq\\
-\epsilon\int_{0}^{q_{M}}(1-\Phi_{x}(q))^{-2}\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq+\epsilon^{-1}(|\Omega|-1)=\epsilon\left(1-\frac{1}{1-\Phi_{x}(q_{M})}\right)+\epsilon^{-1}(|\Omega|-1).\end{split}

(3.58)

We also note that

\begin{split}\sum_{y,z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}(0)))^{-1}]_{yx}[(D+\bm{K}(\bm{\delta}(0)))^{-1}]_{zx}\bm{h}(y)\bm{h}(z)\leq\\
\left(s_{1}((D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{\delta}(0)))^{-1})\right)^{2}\|\bm{h}\|^{2}\leq\left(|\Omega|\mathrm{tr}((D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{\delta}(0)))^{-1})\right)^{2}\|\bm{h}\|^{2}=\\
\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega}\delta_{x}(0)\right)^{2}\|\bm{h}\|^{2}\leq|\Omega|^{2}\|\bm{h}\|^{2}.\end{split}

(3.59)

In particular,

\mathcal{G}_{x}(q_{M})\leq\epsilon\left(1-\frac{1}{1-\Phi_{x}(q_{M})}\right)+\xi^{\prime}_{x}(1)+\epsilon^{-1}(|\Omega|-1)+|\Omega|^{2}\|\bm{h}\|^{2}.

(3.60)

We note that the final term on the right-hand side grows to
-\infty
as
\Phi_{x}(q_{M})\to 1
, so it is clear there is some fixed
c_{x}:=c_{x}(D,\xi_{x}^{\prime}(1),\|\bm{h}\|)>0
so that if
\mathcal{G}_{x}(q_{M})\geq 0
we must have that
\Phi_{x}(q_{M})<1-c_{x}
. Taking
c=\min_{x\in\Omega}c_{x}
completes the proof.
∎

With this, we can now prove the existence of minimizers for
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.10
for
\mathcal{B}
.

Let us denote, for
0<\epsilon<1
, the space of sequences
\vec{\bm{s}}
such that

0=\bm{s}^{0}(x)\leq\bm{s}^{1}(x)\leq\cdots\leq\bm{s}^{r}(x)\leq 1-\epsilon,

(3.61)

as
\mathscr{S}_{\epsilon}
. It is clear that
\mathscr{S}_{\epsilon}
is compact, so that
\vec{\bm{s}}\mapsto\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
possesses a minimizer over
\mathscr{S}_{\epsilon}
. As all possible
\vec{\bm{s}}
lie in some
\mathscr{S}_{\epsilon}
, we see that if choose some small
c>0
, and show that for any
0<\epsilon<c
, the minimizer of
\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
over
\mathscr{S}_{\epsilon}
actually lies in
\mathscr{S}_{c}
, then

\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}}\in\mathscr{S}_{c}}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}}}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}),

(3.62)

so in particular, the minimizer of
\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
over
\mathscr{S}_{c}
is actually a global minimizer. We will prove this claim for
c>0
satisfying Lemma
3.11
. By this Lemma, we need only show that any minimizer over
\mathscr{S}_{\epsilon}
, say
(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
, satisfies
\mathcal{G}_{x}(s^{r})\geq 0
for all
x\in\Omega
if
0<\epsilon<c
.

However, by routine computation and (
3.41
) we see that

-\mathcal{G}_{x}(s^{r})=2|\Omega|(1-m_{r-1})^{-1}\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{r}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}).

(3.63)

However, employing the work around (
3.44
), we see that minimization over
\mathscr{S}_{\epsilon}
implies that
\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{r}(x)}\mathcal{B}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})\leq 0
. Thus the previous relation immediately implies that
\mathcal{G}_{x}(s^{r})\geq 0
, as desired.
∎

Now we focus on the minimizers of
\mathcal{A}
. To demonstrate the existence of minimizers of
\mathcal{A}
, it is convenient here, and crucial in subsequent sections, to mildly extend the domain of the functional. The key is that while the functional
\mathcal{B}
requires us to avoid pairs
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
such that
(\Phi_{x})_{*}(\zeta)
is supported at
1
in order to be well-defined, the functional
\mathcal{A}
needs no such restriction.

In particular, let us define the following extension of
\mathscr{Y}
. Let
\zeta
be a probability measure on
[0,1]
, and let
\bm{\Phi}:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{\Omega}
be a non-decreasing function such that (
1.10
) holds with
\bm{q}=\bm{1}
. We denote the set of such pairs as
\mathscr{Y}^{0}
. We observe that
\mathscr{Y}\subseteq\mathscr{Y}^{0}
, with the difference being that
\mathscr{Y}^{0}
places no restraints on the support. The expression for
\mathcal{A}
given in (
3.2
) immediately extends
\mathcal{A}
to the set
\mathscr{Y}^{0}
. We define
\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{fin}
as the subset of
\mathscr{Y}^{0}
such that
\bm{\Phi}_{*}(\zeta)
has finite support.

In generality, we are now ready to study the minimization of
\mathcal{A}
in terms of
\bm{b}
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let us fix a choice of
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}^{0}
, and assume that
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(1)\neq 0
for each
x\in\Omega
. Then the function
\bm{b}\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi},\bm{b})
is strictly convex and possesses a unique minimizer on its domain if either
\min_{x}d_{x}(0)>0
or
\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0)\neq 0
for all
x\in\Omega
.

Moreover, the minimizing
\bm{b}
given as the unique solution to the critical equation

\begin{split}&-\frac{1}{m_{1}}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xx}+\sum_{2\leq k\leq r}\left(\frac{1}{m_{k-1}}-\frac{1}{m_{k}}\right)[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k})^{-1}]_{xx}\\
&-\sum_{y,z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{yx}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xz}(\xi_{y}^{\prime}(\bm{s}^{1}(y))\delta_{yz}+\bm{h}(y)\bm{h}(z))+1=0.\end{split}

(3.64)

Proof.

To begin we analyze the terms in the continuum expression (
3.2
) for
\mathcal{A}
. We note all terms on the right-hand side are clearly convex, while the second term in the first line is clearly strictly convex as
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(r)>0
for all
r>0
by the assumption that
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(1)\neq 0
. This establishes strict convexity, and so to establish the existence of a unique minimizer, we only need to show that we may restrict the infimum to a compact subset of the domain

\{\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0)>0\}.

(3.65)

For this, note that it is clear that the first and third lines on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) are non-negative. Moreover, as
\zeta([0,s])\leq 1
and
\Phi_{x}(s)\leq 1
, we have that

\int_{0}^{1}\zeta([0,s])\Phi_{x}(s)\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds\leq\int_{0}^{1}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds=\xi_{x}^{\prime}(1)-\xi^{\prime}(0)\leq\xi_{x}^{\prime}(1).

(3.66)

In particular, we obtain the lower bound

\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi},\bm{b})\geq\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{b}(x)-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\xi_{x}^{\prime}(1)\right).

(3.67)

Noting that we must have that
\bm{b}(x)>-s_{1}(D)
, this bound makes it clear that for some large
C>0
we may restrict the infimum to the subset

\{\bm{b}\in[-s_{1}(D),C]^{\Omega}:D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0)>0\}.

(3.68)

Next, we note that

\begin{split}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{1}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(s))^{-1}]_{xx}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds&\geq\\
\sum_{x\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{1}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(s))^{-1}]_{xx}\zeta([0,s])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds&=\\
-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{1}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(s))^{-1}]_{xx}\bm{d}^{\prime}(s)ds&=\\
\log\det(D+\bm{b})-\log\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0)).&\end{split}

(3.69)

Using this and previous observations, we see that

\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi},\bm{b})\geq\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\left(\log\det(D+\bm{b})-\log\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0))-|\Omega|s_{1}(D)-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\xi_{x}^{\prime}(1)\right).

(3.70)

If we then assume that
\epsilon:=\min_{x\in\Omega}d_{x}(0)>0
, then it is clear that
\log\det(D+\bm{b})\geq|\Omega|\log\epsilon
. In particular, up to a constant
C>0
, we have that

\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi},\bm{b})\geq-C-\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\log\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0)).

(3.71)

This clearly shows that for some small
c>0
, we may further restrict the infimum to the domain

\{\bm{b}\in[-s_{1}(D),C]^{\Omega}:D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0)\geq cI\},

(3.72)

which completes the proof in this case. If we instead assume that
\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0)>0
for all
x\in\Omega
, then we instead use the third term as a lower bound to obtain that

\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi},\bm{b})\geq-C+\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0))^{-1}]_{xx}\xi_{x}^{\prime}(0).

(3.73)

This term again allows us to restrict the domain of our infimum to

\{\bm{b}\in[-s_{1}(D),C]^{\Omega}:D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}(0)\geq cI\},

(3.74)

showing a unique minimizer exists in either case. Lastly, the final claim then follows by a routine computation of
2|\Omega|\frac{d}{d\bm{b}(x)}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the second half of Lemma
3.10
and complete the section.

Proof of Lemma
3.10
for
\mathcal{A}
.

Observe that if we weaken the restriction on
\vec{\bm{s}}
to simply

0=\bm{s}^{0}(x)\leq\bm{s}^{1}(x)\leq\cdots\leq\bm{s}^{r}(x)\leq\bm{s}^{r+1}(x)=1.

(3.75)

then the process of Remark
3.4
still associates to a pair
(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
, a measure
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}^{0}
. Note that with this extensions, the set of possible
\vec{\bm{s}}
is now compact. Thus using Lemma
3.12
, and its bounds, it easy to show that the map
\vec{\bm{s}}\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
is continous and thus have a minimizer, and using Lemma
3.12
again we may conclude the existence of a minimizing pair
(\vec{\bm{s}},\bm{b})
. This only differs from what we want to show in that
\vec{\bm{s}}
may satisfy the weakened requirement (
3.75
), so to complete the proof we only need to show that our minimizer satisfies
\bm{s}^{r}(x)<1
for all
x\in\Omega
.

Now note the computations of the derivatives from Lemma
3.9
still hold. In particular, combining (
3.64
) with (
3.41
) in the case of
n=r
, we conclude that

2|\Omega|(1-m_{r-1})^{-1}\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{r}(x)}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})=\sum_{x\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{r})^{-1}]_{xx}+\bm{s}^{r}(x)-1.

(3.76)

If
\bm{s}^{r}(x)=1
, the minimization condition clearly implies that

\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{r}(x)}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})\leq 0.

(3.77)

However, in this case (
3.76
) implies that

2|\Omega|(1-m_{r-1})^{-1}\frac{d}{d\bm{s}^{r}(x)}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})=\sum_{x\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{r})^{-1}]_{xx}>0,

(3.78)

which gives a contradiction.
∎

4
A Useful Approximation of the Parisi Functional

In this section, we will develop some results needed to establish Theorem
3.1
. The most important result is Proposition
4.6
, which essentially shows that the functional
\mathcal{A}
can be expressed as a limit of certain functionals
\mathcal{A}_{M}
as
M\to\infty
. This is important in our proof, as for example, the upper bound we use to obtain Theorem
3.1
is of the form

\mathbb{E}f_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}\leq\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})}\mathcal{A}_{N}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})+o(1),

(4.1)

and so only becomes tight as
N\to\infty
.

However, the functionals
\mathcal{A}_{M}
are complicated, and require us to recall a number of important objects and constructions from the study of unipartite spin glass models, and in particular the family of Ruelle probability cascades (RPCs). These objects were originally introduced by Ruelle
[
58
]
, and will play a key role in our derivation. As such, this section also serves to set notation and recall some basic properties. We will try to follow the notation of the book by Panchenko
[
53
]
closely, and reference this book for our required results on these objects when possible.

Thus we have broken up this section into three parts. First, we introduce a modification of the class of finite measures used in the previous section (see Remarks
4.1
and
4.2
), which is an important technicality. Next, we recall the RPCs, their associated random measure, and some basic properties. Finally, we introduce some auxiliary functions, and evaluate them on the random measures coming from an RPC to define the functional
\mathcal{A}_{M}
. We finish the subsection by introducing and proving Proposition
4.6
, the main result of this section, which essentially shows that
\lim_{M\to\infty}\inf\mathcal{A}_{M}=\inf\mathcal{A}
.

4.1
A Reparameterization of the Set of Finite Measures

Here we address an important, though tedious, change to the way we use sequences to describe finite pairs
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
. The need for a second discretization procedure is common, and matches the difference between the procedure of
[
67
]
and
[
53
]
.

In particular, recall that we considered pairs
(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
which obeyed the inequalities (
3.10
) and (
3.9
). Via Remark
3.4
, we may associate with this a pair
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}
. This parametrization follows the one used in the original work on a one-site case by Talagrand
[
67
]
.

However, when working with RPCs, it is convenient to restrict ourselves to measures whose support contain both
0
and
1
(see Section 3.4, and especially Remark 4.1, of
[
53
]
for a discussion of this).

However, there are a number of reasons we could not just use this modification in the previous section. The first issue is that while functional
\mathcal{A}
may be defined for such measures (see the discussion immediately prior to Lemma
3.12
), the functional
\mathcal{B}
cannot. The second issue is that as we never minimized over the weights of the measure, only the locations of points in the support, in the previous section. So forcing
0
(let alone
1
) to be included in the support of the measure would break our argument, and require us to minimize over the weights.

With this explained, we now give our parametrization. To start, take
r\geq 1
, as well as a choice of

0=t_{-1}<t_{0}<\cdots<t_{r}=1.

(4.2)

For each
x\in\Omega
, we additionally choose a sequence
(\bm{q}^{k}(x))_{k=1}^{r}
such that

0=\bm{q}^{0}(x)\leq\bm{q}^{1}(x)\leq\cdots\leq\bm{q}^{r}(x)=1.

(4.3)

We use the notation
(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})
as before. We will also be careful to only use the notation similar to
(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})
henceforth, to limit confusion with pairs
(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})
which instead follow (
3.9
) and (
3.10
)

We now describe how to construct a pair
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{fin}
from these sequences. For convenience, we will split this into two remarks. First, note that we associate a sequence
\vec{q}
from
\vec{\bm{q}}
by taking

q^{k}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{q}^{k}(x).

(4.4)

This sequence satisfies

0=q^{0}\leq q^{1}\leq\cdots\leq q^{r}=1,

(4.5)

Given the
(\vec{q},\vec{t})
pair, we may associate a probability measure on
[0,1]
.

Remark 4.1
.

Let us take sequences
\vec{t}
and
\vec{q}
as in (
4.2
) and (
4.5
) of length parameter
r
. We may associate to this a discrete probability measure on
\zeta
on
[0,1]
given by

\zeta=\sum_{0\leq k\leq r}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})\delta_{q^{k}},

(4.6)

where
\delta_{q}
denotes the Dirac
\delta
-function supported at
q
. We note that conversely, any probability measure on
[0,1]
, whose support contains
\{0,1\}
, and consists of
(r+1)
-points or less, may be obtained from such a pair
(\vec{t},\vec{q})
.

Moreover, if a probability measure is such that its support contains
\{0,1\}
and it has at most
(r+1)
-points in its support, then there is a such pair
(\vec{t},\vec{q})
which generates it. If it has exactly
(r+1)
-points in its support, this representation is unique.

Now that we have associated generated a measure
\zeta
given
(\vec{t},\vec{q})
, we will generate the pair
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
from the additional information in
\vec{\bm{q}}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Let us take sequences
\vec{t}
and
\vec{\bm{q}}
as in (
4.2
) and (
4.3
). To the data
(\vec{t},\vec{q})
we may take the measure
\zeta
associated by Remark
4.1
. We define functions
\bm{\Phi}:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{\Omega}
by fixing the values
\bm{\Phi}(q^{k})=\bm{q}^{k}
, and otherwise by linear interpolation. For each
x\in\Omega
, we observe that the push-forward measures
(\Phi_{x})_{*}(\zeta)
are now also measures on
[0,1]
, which are of the form of Remark
4.1
, except associated to the pair of sequences
(\vec{t},\vec{\bm{q}(x)})
. Moreover, we have that

(\bm{\Phi})_{*}(\zeta)=\sum_{0\leq k\leq r}(t_{k}-t_{k-1})\delta_{\bm{q}^{k}}.

(4.7)

Finally, we observe that
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{0}
.

Now with our mechanism to generate pairs
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{fin}
from pairs
(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})
, our next task will be to give the computation of
\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})
.

We define for
x\in\Omega
and
1\leq l\leq r

\bm{d}^{l}(x)=\sum_{l\leq k<r}t_{k}(\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{q}^{k+1}(x))-\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{q}^{k}(x))),\;\;\bm{d}^{r}(x)=0.

(4.8)

Next, we take a choice of some
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
, such that
D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1}>0
. Associated with these choices we have that

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t},\bm{b})=\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\bigg(&|\Omega|\log(2\pi)+\sum_{0\leq k<r}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log\frac{\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k+1})}{\det(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{k})}-\log\det(D+\bm{b})\\
&+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\bm{b}(x)-\sum_{0\leq k<r}t_{k}(\theta_{x}(\bm{q}^{k+1}(x))-\theta_{x}(\bm{q}^{k}(x)))\right)\\
&+\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1})^{-1}]_{xy}(\bm{h}(x)\bm{h}(y)+\delta_{xy}\xi_{x}^{\prime}(0))\bigg),\end{split}

(4.9)

and then further define

\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})=\inf_{\bm{b}:D+\bm{b}-\bm{d}^{1}>0}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t},\bm{b}).

(4.10)

For comparison, the evaluation of
\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m},\bm{b})
is given in (
3.19
). Now while the measures arising from both conventions lead to disjoint compose disjoint subsets of
\mathscr{Y}^{0}_{fin}
, they are both dense (at least up to the equivalence given in Proposition
B.5
). As such, one may easily obtain the following result.

Lemma 4.3
.

\inf_{\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t}}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})=\inf_{\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m}}\mathcal{A}(\vec{\bm{s}},\vec{m})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}^{0}}\mathcal{A}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(4.11)

4.2
Properties of Ruelle Probability Cascades

We will now recall some basic information about RPCs and their associated measures. For a precise construction of these objects and derivation of the stated results, see Chapter 2 of
[
53
]
, whose notation we try to closely follow. To begin, take
r\geq 1
, and fix a choice of sequence
\vec{t}
as in (
4.2
). Given this, there is an associated random probability measure on
\mathbb{N}^{r}
, whose weights we will denote
(v_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}
, known as the associated RPC.

Now fix an infinite dimensional separable real Hilbert space
H
. Given an additional sequence
there is a further random measure on
H
, which is a.s. supported on some set of non-random collection vectors
(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}
. These vectors have a key property.
For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, we will denote by
\alpha\wedge\beta
the index of the least common ancestor:

\alpha\wedge\beta=\inf\left(\{r\}\cup\{i:\alpha_{i}\neq\beta_{i}\}\right).

(4.12)

Then the vectors are such that
(h_{\alpha},h_{\beta})_{H}=q^{\alpha\wedge\beta}
.
G
is then given by the random probability measure,
G
, supported on the points
h_{\alpha}\in H
, with weights
G(\{h_{\alpha}\})=v_{\alpha}
.

We will now begin recalling the properties of
G
we will require, beginning first with how to recover some of the data
(\vec{t},\vec{q})
from it. Let us denote the probability measure associated with our fixed
(\vec{t},\vec{q})
via Remark
4.1
as
\zeta
. The first property we need (see (2.82) of
[
53
]
) is that the law of
(v,v^{\prime})_{H}
, where
(v,v^{\prime})\in H^{\times 2}
is sampled from
\mathbb{E}G^{\otimes 2}
, is given by
\zeta
. That is for any continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{E}\langle f((v,v^{\prime})_{H})\rangle=\int f(q)\zeta(dq),

(4.13)

where
\langle*\rangle
denotes the average of
(v,v^{\prime})
under
G^{\otimes 2}
.

The next property we will need is the existence of some centered Gaussian processes defined on
\{h_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}
. Let
\phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be any non-decreasing function. Then there is a centered Gaussian process,
g_{\phi}
, defined on the points
\{h_{\alpha}\}
, such that for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
,

\mathbb{E}[g_{\phi}(h_{\alpha})g_{\phi}(h_{\beta})]=\phi(q^{\alpha\wedge\beta}).

(4.14)

Remark 4.4
.

We observe that if we denote by
\langle f(v)\rangle
the average over
v
sampled from
G
, then defining
f(\alpha):=f(h_{\alpha})
, we have that

\langle f(v)\rangle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}v_{\alpha}f(\alpha).

(4.15)

We at times employ the latter method of writing this average when we believe one is more clear.

We now state the final property we need for
G
. Let us fix some random variable
\omega
taking values in some Borel space
T
(for example
\mathbb{R}^{n}
), as well as some measurable function
F:T^{r}\to\mathbb{R}
. Let us furthermore fix a family of i.i.d copies of
\omega
,
(\omega_{\beta})_{\beta\in\mathscr{A}}
. We will use the abuse of notation by letting, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
,

F(h_{\alpha})=F(\alpha):=F((\omega_{\beta})_{\beta\in p(\alpha)}).

(4.16)

We will need to evaluate expressions of the form

\mathbb{E}\log\langle\exp F(v)\rangle=\mathbb{E}\log\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}v_{\alpha}\exp(F(\alpha))\right).

(4.17)

The final result we need is a recursive formula to compute this. Define
F_{r}=F
, and for
0\leq k<r
, we define
F_{k}:T^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
by the iterative relation

F_{k}(z_{0},\dots,z_{k})=\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log\mathbb{E}\exp t_{k}F_{k+1}(z_{0},\dots,z_{k},\omega).

(4.18)

It then follows from Theorem 2.9 of
[
53
]
that

\mathbb{E}\log\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}v_{\alpha}\exp F(\alpha)\right)=F_{0}.

(4.19)

Finally, we will need to recall an important invariance-property which RPC’s satisfy, which follows as a special case of Theorem 2.11 of
[
53
]
. Namely, with
F
as above and any
f:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}f(q^{\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}})v_{\alpha}v_{\alpha^{\prime}}\exp(F(\alpha)+F(\alpha^{\prime}))}{\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}v_{\alpha}\exp(F(\alpha))\right)^{2}}\right]=\int f(q)\zeta(dq).

(4.20)

Note that when
F=0
this coincides with (
4.13
) above.

4.3
An Approximate Parisi Functional

We now define a family of functionals defined on a certain class of random probability measures. We will primarily evaluate these functionals on the Gibbs measure associated to our Hamiltonians (or more precisely, their perturbed variants) as well as on a family of RPCs which we will show approximate our Gibbs measure. For now however, it will be advantageous to adopt a general framework.

Let
\mu
be a random probability measure on
H
. We will assume that a.s. the support of

With this we see that the family

is continuous on its domain. In particular, we obtain Proposition
4.6
from this and Lemma
4.7
by a standard argument.

5
The Ghirlanda-Guerra Identities, Synchronization, and Talagrand’s Positivity Principle

In further preparation for the proof of Theorem
1.6
, in this section we will introduce a family of perturbations of our Hamiltonian. These perturbations are of insufficient strength to change the limiting value of any of the free energies we consider, but allow us to enforce a number of properties to asymptotically hold for the associated Gibbs measure. Depending on the property, these will asymptotically hold after averaging over the family or with non-zero probability.

The property of key importance for the upper bound of Theorem
3.1
is Talagrand’s positivity principle. Roughly, this principle states that if we takes two samples from the Gibbs measure
\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime}\in(S_{N})^{\Omega}
, then for each
x\in\Omega
, we should have that
(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N}\geq-\epsilon
with high probability when
N
is large. A formal statement is given in Theorem
5.1
below. This property is of key importance as it makes the error terms appearing in an application of Guerra’s interpolation scheme positive, which is otherwise only clear for models in which
\xi_{x}
is convex for each
x\in\Omega
.

The lower bound however relies on a much stronger property. Roughly, this states that for specific choices of perturbation, the Gibbs measure may be approximated by a sequence of RPCs with “synchronized” spins, at-least on the level of overlaps. Such a representation was originally discovered by Panchenko in the case of multi-species Ising spin glass models
[
55
]
, and have since appeared in a number of cases. These both generalize the case of
|\Omega|=1
, where it is shown that such perturbations force the Gibbs measure to be approximated by a sequence of RPCs, again on the level of overlaps. This property was again originally demonstrated by Panchenko
[
52
]
.

We will begin by recalling the family of perturbation Hamiltonians. Then we will give Talagrand’s positivity principle. After this, we will recall the multi-species Ghirlanda-Guerra identities, and a formal version of the identification theorem associated with them, which we alluded to in the previous paragraph. Finally, we will show, for a quite general class of Hamiltonians, that there are choices of our perturbation term which lead to approximate satisfaction of the multi-species Ghirlanda-Guerra identities.

For this section, we will fix a family of (random) continuous functions
H_{N}:S_{N}^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
. We now define the perturbation term, which based on the corresponding multi-species perturbation from
[
55
]
(see as well Chapter 3 or
[
53
]
and Appendix A of
[
11
]
). To begin, define for
\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime}\in(S_{N})^{\Omega}
, let us define the overlap vector
\bm{R}_{N}(\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})\in[-1,1]^{\Omega}
by

\bm{R}_{N}(\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})(x)=(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N}.

(5.1)

For any
\bm{w}\in[0,1]^{\Omega}
, we may also define the following inner-product:

\bm{R}_{N}^{\bm{w}}(\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{w}(x)\bm{R}_{N}(\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})(x)=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{w}(x)(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N}.

(5.2)

Let
\mathcal{W}=\{\bm{w}_{1},\bm{w}_{2},\dots\}
be a countable dense subset of
[0,1]^{\Omega}
, all of which we assume are non-zero. We will assume in addition that
\mathcal{W}
contains each standard unit vector in
[0,1]^{\Omega}
. To each such choice, we may associate (see (24) of
[

]
) a centered Gaussian random function,
h_{N,p,q}
, on
S_{N}^{\Omega}
with covariance function given by

\mathbb{E}[h_{N,p,q}(\bm{u})h_{N,p,q}(\bm{u}^{\prime})]=\bm{R}_{N}^{\bm{w}_{q}}(\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})^{p}.

(5.3)

We independently take such a choice for each
(p,q)
. Now fix in addition double-indexed sequence
\mathfrak{X}=(x_{p,q})_{p,q\geq 1}\in[1,2]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}
and form the perturbation Hamiltonian

h_{N}(\bm{u})=\sum_{p,q\geq 1}2^{-(p+q)}x_{p,q}h_{N,p,q}(\bm{u}).

(5.4)

This is a centered Gaussian process with covariance

\sum_{p,q\geq 1}x_{p,q}^{2}4^{-(p+q)}\bm{R}_{N}^{\bm{w}_{q}}(\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})^{p}.

(5.5)

Note that in particular that as
|\bm{R}_{N}^{\bm{w}}(\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\bm{w}(x)|\leq 1
, the variance of
h_{N}(\bm{u})
is bounded by
\sup_{p,q}x_{p,q}^{2}\leq 4
.
We now introduce the perturbed Hamiltonian

H^{\mathfrak{X}}_{N}(\bm{u})=H_{N}(\bm{u})+s_{N}h_{N}(\bm{u}),

(5.6)

where
s_{N}=N^{\gamma}
for any
1/4<\gamma<1/2
.

Let us now fix any sequence of (deterministic) finite positive measures on
S_{N}^{\Omega}
,
m_{N}
, with continuous bounded density with respect to the uniform measure
\omega
. We denote the partition function over
S_{N}^{\Omega}
with respect to
(H_{N},m_{N})
as
Z_{N}
, and the partition function with respect to
(H_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}},m_{N})
as

, we note that by Jensen’s inequality,

N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}\leq N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}\leq N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}+2s_{N}^{2}N^{-1},

(5.7)

so that in particular, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{x\in[1,2]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}}|N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}-N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}|=0.

(5.8)

It will also at times be convenient to take an average over
\mathfrak{X}=(x_{p,q})_{p,q\geq 1}\in[1,2]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}
, where each
(x_{p,q})
is thought of as an independent uniform r.v. on
[1,2]
. We will denote this average as
\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}
, taken independently of all other randomness, and reserve the notation
\mathbb{E}
to refer to the conditional expectation. The same application of Jensen’s inequality shows that

\lim_{N\to\infty}|N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}-N^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}|=0.

(5.9)

We will use the notation
\langle*\rangle_{\mathfrak{X}}
to denote the Gibbs measure with respect to
(H_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}},m_{N})
. We will need the following multi-species version of the positivity principle, which follows immediately from the deterministic Lemma 2.3 of
[
12
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let us assume that
H_{N}:S_{N}^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
is deterministic and satisfies

\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}}\exp(|H_{N}(\bm{u})|)m_{N}(d\bm{u})<\infty.

(5.10)

Fix any choice of
s_{N}
such that
s_{N}\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, and
x\in\Omega
, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{H_{N}(\bm{u})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\langle I((\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N}\geq-\epsilon)\rangle_{\mathfrak{X}}=1,

(5.11)

where here the supremum is taken over all such functions
H_{N}
which satisfy (

).

5.1
The Multi-Species Ghirlanda-Guerra Identities and Their Consequences

In this subsection we will recall the multi-species Ghirlanda-Guerra identities and some of their important consequences. The non-multi-species version of these identities were first discovered in the titular work by Ghirlanda and Guerra
[
40
]
. This was followed by extensive work by Talagrand
[
66
]
and Panchenko
[
53
,
52
,
54
]
who found that these identities both contain a given random array to be quite close to the overlaps of a Ruelle probability cascade, and asymptotically hold (possibly after a slight perturbation) for the overlap obtained by taking multiple samples from the Gibbs measure, at least in the case of the one-site model. The multi-species version of these identities were found in the case of multi-site spin glasses by Pancheko
[
55
]
, who further found they imply a surprising synchronization property which significantly constrains the possible values over the overlap array even more.

Definition 5.2

(Multi-Species Ghirlanda-Guerra Identities)
.

Let us consider a random array
\bm{R}\in([-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}})^{\Omega}
. For any
\ell\geq 1
, we may consider the truncation of this array
\bm{R}^{\ell}\in([-1,1]^{\ell\times\ell})^{\Omega}
by taking only the first
\ell
-coordinates.

Then for any continuous functions
f:([-1,1]^{\ell\times\ell})^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi:[-1,1]^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the multi-species Guerra-Ghirlanda difference by

\Delta(\ell,f,\varphi):=\bigg|\ell\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{R}^{\ell})\varphi(\bm{R}_{1,\ell+1})]-\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{R}^{\ell})]\mathbb{E}[\varphi([\bm{R}_{1,2}])]-\sum_{k=2}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{R}^{\ell})\varphi(\bm{R}_{1,k})]\bigg|.

(5.12)

If
\Delta(\ell,f,\varphi)=0
for all possible of choices of
(\ell,f,\varphi)
, we will say that
\bm{R}
satisfies the multi-species Guerra-Ghirlanda identities.

The first major consequence of these identities is the following synchronization result due to Panchenko.

Theorem 5.3

(Theorem 4,
[
55
]
)
.

If a random infinite array
\bm{R}\in([-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}})^{\Omega}
satisfies the multi-species Guerra-Ghirlanda identities then there exists, for each
x\in\Omega
, a non-decreasing continuous function
\Phi_{x}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, such that a.s. for each
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\geq 1
, we have that
\bm{R}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}(x)=\Phi_{x}(R_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}})
, where here

R_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{R}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}(x).

(5.13)

This reduces the study of the overlap to the study of array
R=[R_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}]_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\geq 1}\in[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}
. To do this we now recall the regular Ghirlanda-Guerra identities, which state that for continuous functions
\varphi:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
and
f:[-1,1]^{\ell\times\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\ell\mathbb{E}[f(R^{\ell})\varphi(R)_{1,\ell+1}]=\mathbb{E}[f(R^{\ell})]\mathbb{E}[\varphi(R_{1,2})]+\sum_{k=2}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}[f(R^{\ell})\varphi(R_{1,k})],

(5.14)

where
R^{\ell}
is the truncation of
R
as before. It is clear if
\bm{R}
satisfies the multi-species Guerra-Ghirlanda identities, then
R
satisfies the regular Ghirlanda-Guerra identities. These identities are still quite powerful, and further determine the law of the array
R
off the diagonal in terms of the law of a single off-diagonal element.

To recall this result, we will first need to recall the following representation theorem. We will say an array is exchangeable if for any finitely supported permutation
\sigma:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, we have that

R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}R_{\sigma}:=[[R]_{\sigma(i),\sigma(j)}]_{i,j\geq 1}.

(5.15)

Theorem 5.4

(Dovbysh–Sudakov
[
27
]
)
.

Let
R\in[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}
be a symmetric, exchangeable, and positive semi-definite array, and fix a separable infinite Hilbert space
H
. Then there is a random measure,
\mu
on
H
, such that if
(v^{1},v^{2},\dots)\in H^{\mathbb{N}}
are an sequence of i.i.d samples from
\mu
, we have that

[R_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}]_{\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}\geq 1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}[(v^{\ell},v^{\ell^{\prime}})_{H}]_{\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}\geq 1},

(5.16)

where
(,)_{H}
denotes the inner product on the Hilbert space
H
.

A remarkable fact is that RPCs not only satisfy the Ghirlanda-Guerra identities, but they are essentially dense in that class, which is the content of the following result.

Theorem 5.5

(Theorems 2.13 and 2.17 and Remark 2.1 of
[
53
]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a random measure on
H
, a.s. supported on the unit ball, and whose overlap array,
R
, satisfies (
5.14
) for each choice of
(\ell,f,\varphi)
. Then the law of
R
is uniquely determined by that law of
R_{12}
.

Moreover, let us denote the law of
R_{12}
as
\zeta
. Then
\zeta
is supported on
[0,1]
, and
[R]_{\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}\geq 1}
coincides in law with the limiting law of the (off-diagonal) overlap array generated by the RPCs corresponding to any sequence of discrete probability measures
\zeta_{n}
such that
\zeta_{n}\Rightarrow\zeta
in measure.

5.2
Effect of the Perturbation

Now that we have introduced the Ghirlanda-Guerra identities, and highlighted their power, we will show how they can be guaranteed in the limit through the use of the perturbation term given in (
5.6
). To explain this we will now make the assumption that our choice of Hamiltonian satisfies

\mathbb{E}\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}}\exp(|H_{N}(\bm{u})|)m_{N}(d\bm{u})<\infty.

(5.17)

We consider an infinite sequence of vectors sampled from the Gibbs measure
\langle*\rangle_{\mathfrak{X}}
, which we denote
(\bm{u}^{1},\bm{u}^{2},\dots)
. Associated to these we form the infinite array
\bm{R}_{N}
given by

[\bm{R}_{N}]_{i,j}=\bm{R}_{N}(\bm{u}^{i},\bm{u}^{j})\in[-1,1]^{\Omega}.

(5.18)

Then any choice of
\ell\geq 1
, continuous functions
f:([-1,1]^{\ell\times\ell})^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\bm{w}\in[0,1]^{\Omega}
, and
p\geq 1
, we define

\begin{split}\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\ell,f,\bm{w},p):=\bigg|&\ell\mathbb{E}\langle f(\bm{R}_{N}^{\ell})(\bm{R}_{N}^{\bm{w}_{q}}(\bm{u}^{1},\bm{u}^{\ell+1}))^{p}\rangle_{\mathfrak{X}}-\mathbb{E}\langle f(\bm{R}_{N}^{\ell})\rangle_{\mathfrak{X}}\mathbb{E}\langle(\bm{R}_{N}^{\bm{w}}(\bm{u}^{1},\bm{u}^{2}))^{p}\rangle_{\mathfrak{X}}\\
&-\sum_{k=2}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\langle f(\bm{R}_{N}^{\ell})(\bm{R}_{N}^{\bm{w}}(\bm{u}^{1},\bm{u}^{2}))^{p}\rangle_{\mathfrak{X}}\bigg|.\end{split}

(5.19)

Note that this coincides with a choice of Ghirlanda-Guerra difference defined in (
5.12
). Namely, if we understand the random measure in question in Definition
5.2
as the Gibbs measure of
H_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}
, and define the function
\varphi_{\bm{w},p}(\bm{R})=(\bm{R}^{\bm{w}})^{p}
, we have that
\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\ell,f,\bm{w},p)=\Delta(\ell,f,\varphi_{\bm{w},p})
.

We now show that
\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\ell,f,\bm{w},p)
is reasonably small after averaging with respect to
x_{p,q}\in[1,2]
. In particular, we have the following special case of the quite general Theorem A.3 of
[
11
]
, which is a direct adaptation of Theorem 3.2 of
[
53
]
. Note that as our
\Delta
differs from theirs by a factor of
\ell
, this factor is missing from the corresponding expression (A.11) in
[
11
]
.

Theorem 5.6

(Theorem A.3,
[
11
]
)
.

Let us fix
p,q\geq 1
. Let us denote make dependance of
Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}
on
x_{p,q}
explicit by writing
Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(x_{p,q})
, as well as writing
\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\ell,f,\bm{w}_{q},p,x_{p,q})
. We then fix choices of
x_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}\in[0,3]
for
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\neq(p,q)
. We then define the quantity

v_{N,p,q}=\sup\{\mathbb{E}|\log Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(u)-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(u)|:u\in[0,3]\},

(5.20)

and denote by
\rho=4^{-(p+q)}(\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{w}_{q}(x))^{p}
the variance of
h_{N,p,q}
. Then for
N
such that
2^{-1}s_{N}^{-2}v_{N,p,q}/\rho<1
, we have that

\int_{1}^{2}\Delta_{N}(\ell,f,\bm{w}_{q},p,x)dx\leq 24\|f\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\rho}s_{N}^{-1}(1+\sqrt{v_{N,p,q}}).

(5.21)

To apply this, let us now finally set our final condition on
H_{N}
. We assume that it is a centered Gaussian random function with variance bounded by
N\sigma
for some fixed
\sigma
. In this case, we may apply Theorem
A.1
to verify (
5.17
). Similarly, we recall that
\mathbb{E}[h_{p,q}(\bm{u})^{2}]\leq 1
so with notation Theorem
5.6
, we may apply Proposition
A.2
to conclude that

\mathbb{P}(|\log Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(u)-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(u)|\geq\alpha)\leq 2\exp(-\alpha^{2}/4(N\sigma+9s_{N}^{2})),

(5.22)

so that

\mathbb{E}|\log Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(u)-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(u)|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}2\exp(-\alpha^{2}/4(N+9s_{N}^{2}))d\alpha=\sqrt{\pi 8(N\sigma+9s_{N}^{2})}.

(5.23)

In particular, as
s_{N}=N^{\gamma}
, with
\gamma<1/2
, we for sufficiently large
N
that
v_{N,p,q}\leq\sqrt{\pi 9N\sigma}\leq 6\sqrt{N\sigma}
. It is important to note that while
v_{N,p,q}
is random quantity depandant on the choice of
\mathfrak{X}\setminus\{x_{p,q}\}
, is is always bounded by
6\sqrt{N\sigma}
. In particular, as
\gamma>1/4
, we see that
2^{-1}s_{N}^{-2}v_{N,p,q}/\rho\to 0
. From all these observations, and noting that
\rho\leq 1
, we see that Theorem
5.6
may be applied to obtain the following.

Lemma 5.7
.

Fix
p,q\geq 1
. Then with notation as in Theorem
5.6
, and
N
sufficiently large,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\ell,f,\bm{w}_{q},p)\leq 24\|f\|_{\infty}\sqrt{\rho}N^{-\gamma}(1+N^{1/4}\sqrt{6\sigma}).

(5.24)

In particular

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\ell,f,\bm{w}_{q},p)=0.

(5.25)

Putting these together we will be able to obtain the following result used crucially in the lower bound.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
H_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}
be as above and the associated overlap array as
\bm{R}_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}\in([-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}})^{\Omega}
. Then we may make, for each
N\geq 1
, a choice
\mathfrak{X}^{N}
, such that for any subsequence
N_{n}
, such that
\bm{R}_{N_{n}}^{\mathfrak{X}^{N_{n}}}
converges in law to a some random array
\bm{R}\in([-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}})^{\Omega}
, one has that
\bm{R}
satisfies the multi-species Ghirlanda-Guerra identities.

Proof.

We will begin by using Lemma
5.7
to show how we may guarantee that the asymptotic Gibbs measure satisfies the multi-species Ghirlanda-Guerra identities for some value of
\mathfrak{X}
. For each
\ell\geq 1
, let us choose a countable dense subset of continuous functions
f_{\ell,k}:[-1,1]^{\ell\times\ell}\to[-1,1]
. We form the sum

\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}=\sum_{p,q,\ell,k\geq 1}\frac{\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\ell,f_{\ell,k},\bm{w}_{q},p,x)}{2\ell 2^{\ell+k+p+q}}.

(5.26)

We observe that as
\|f_{\ell,k}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, we have that
\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\ell,f_{\ell,k},\bm{w}_{q},p,x)\leq 2\ell
, so that
0\leq\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}\leq 1
. In particular, by Tonelli’s theorem and the dominated convergence theorem we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}=\sum_{p,q,\ell,k\geq 1}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\ell,f_{\ell,k},\bm{w}_{q},p)}{2\ell 2^{\ell+k+p+q}}=0.

(5.27)

We note that by Chebyshev’s inequality

\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}[I(\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(x)\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(x))]\geq 1/2.

(5.28)

Thus for each
N\geq 1
, we may fix a choice of
\mathfrak{X}^{N}
such that
\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}^{N}}\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}
. In particular, for such a choice, we have that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}^{N}}=0
.

Now we first note that the expression
\Delta(\ell,f,\varphi)
depends continuously on the law of the array
\bm{R}
. So if we denote by
\Delta_{\bm{R}}(\ell,f,\varphi)
the Ghirlanda-Guerra difference with respect to
\bm{R}
, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}|\Delta^{\mathfrak{X}^{N_{n}}}_{N}(\ell,f,\varphi)-\Delta_{\bm{R}}(\ell,f,\varphi)|=0.

(5.29)

In addition, the expressions
\Delta(\ell,f,\varphi)
from Definition
5.2
are continuous in the choice of
(f,\varphi)
(with respect to the
L^{\infty}
-norm). In particular, we note that a random array
\bm{R}
satisfies the multi-species Ghirlanda-Guerra identities if and only if

\sum_{p,q,\ell,k\geq 1}\frac{\Delta(\ell,f_{\ell,k},\bm{w}_{q},p)}{2\ell 2^{\ell+k+p+q}}=0.

(5.30)

Thus we see that
\bm{R}^{M}
satisfies the multi-species Ghirlanda-Guerra identities.
As we have that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\Delta_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}^{N}}=0
, we conclude that
\bm{R}
must satisfy the multi-species Ghirlanda-Guerra identities.
∎

(6.1)

6.1
Proof of the Upper Bound

By Proposition

Lemma 6.1
.

We have that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}f_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}-\inf_{r,\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t}}\mathcal{A}_{N}(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})\right)\leq 0.

(6.2)

Proof.

By (

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left(|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\mathbb{E}\log Z^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}_{N}-\inf_{r,\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t}}\mathcal{A}_{N}(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})\right)\leq 0.

(6.3)

Now fix a choice of
(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})
and let
G_{\zeta}
denote the RPC corresponding to the measure
\zeta
associated to
(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})
by Remarks
4.1
and
4.2
with weights given by
(v_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}
. Moreover, let
Z_{x}(\alpha)
and
Y(\alpha)
be the centered Gaussian fields on
\mathbb{N}^{r}
by (
4.21
) and (
4.22
) (for existence (
4.14
)).

We want to apply Proposition
A.4
, with the choices

V^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u},\alpha)=H_{N}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u})+\sqrt{N}Y(\alpha),\;W^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u},\alpha)=\sum_{x\in\Omega}(\vec{Z}_{x}(\alpha),\bm{u}(x))+s_{N}h_{N}(\bm{u}).

(6.4)

For this, define for
0\leq t\leq 1
and
(\bm{u},\alpha)\in S_{N}^{\Omega}\times\mathbb{N}^{r}

H_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u})=\sqrt{1-t}V^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u},\alpha)+\sqrt{t}W^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u},\alpha),

(6.5)

and denote the Gibbs measure associated to
(H_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}},\;\omega_{N,D,\bm{h}}\otimes G_{\zeta})
as
\langle*\rangle_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}}
. Finally denote

\delta(\bm{u},\alpha,\bm{u}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}V^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u},\alpha)V^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}W^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u},\alpha)W^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}).

(6.6)

Note that the contribution coming from
h_{N}(\bm{u})
(the only term dependent on the choice
\mathfrak{X}
) cancel, so that
\delta
is independent of
\mathfrak{X}
as the notation suggests. Then Proposition
A.4
states that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{\xi})+\mathbb{E}\log\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}v_{\alpha}e^{\sqrt{N}Y(\alpha)}-\mathbb{E}\log\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{r}}v_{\alpha}\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}}\exp\bigg(V^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u},\alpha)\bigg)\omega_{N,D,\bm{h}}(d\bm{u})\right)=\\
&\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\left\langle\delta(\bm{u},\alpha,\bm{u},\alpha)\right\rangle_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}}-\mathbb{E}\left\langle\delta(\bm{u},\alpha,\bm{u}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\right\rangle_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}}\bigg)dt.\end{split}

(6.7)

By applying Jensen’s inequality to
h_{N}
, we see in particular that

\begin{split}&|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}-\mathcal{A}_{N}(\vec{\bm{q}},\vec{t})\leq\\
&2|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}s_{N}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\left\langle\delta(\bm{u},\alpha,\bm{u},\alpha)\right\rangle_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}}-\mathbb{E}\left\langle\delta(\bm{u},\alpha,\bm{u}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\right\rangle_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}}\bigg)dt\end{split}

(6.8)

As the first term on the right hand side goes to zero, we study the second term. We define for
x\in\Omega
,

\rho_{x}(u,v)=\left(v-u\right)\xi^{\prime}_{x}(v)+\xi_{x}(u)-\xi_{x}(v).

(6.9)

We then record

\begin{split}N^{-1}\delta(\bm{u},\alpha,\bm{u}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})=&\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\bm{q}^{\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}}(x)-(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N}\right)\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{q}^{\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}}(x))\\
&+\xi_{x}((\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N})-\xi_{x}(\bm{q}^{\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}})+\xi_{x}(0)\\
=&\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\rho_{x}(\bm{q}^{\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}}(x),(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N})+\xi_{x}(0)\right).\end{split}

(6.10)

Thus we see that the first term on the right-hand side of (
6.7
) coincides with
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\xi_{x}(0)
, which cancels a similar term in the second term. In particular, we see that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\left\langle\delta(\bm{u},\alpha,\bm{u},\alpha)\right\rangle_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}}-\mathbb{E}\left\langle\delta(\bm{u},\alpha,\bm{u}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\right\rangle_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}}\bigg)dt=\\
-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\mathbb{E}\left\langle\rho_{x}((\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N},\bm{q}^{\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}}(x))\right\rangle_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}}dt.\end{split}

(6.11)

By Taylor’s theorem, and convexity of
\xi_{x}
on
[0,1]
, we see that for
u,v\in[0,1]
,
\rho_{x}(u,v)\geq 0
. In particular, we may conclude that there is
C
(independent of
\epsilon
) such that
u,v\in[-\epsilon,1]
and
x\in\Omega
, we have that
\rho_{x}(u,v)\geq-C\epsilon
. Potentially enlarging
C
we may also guarantee that for
u,v\in[-1,1]
,
\rho_{x}(u,v)\geq-C
. With these facts noted, we see that (
6.11
) is less than

\frac{C}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\bigg(\epsilon+\mathbb{E}_{\mathfrak{X}}\langle I((\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N}\leq-\epsilon)\rangle_{t}^{\mathfrak{X}}\bigg)dt

(6.12)

Applying Theorem
5.1
and taking
\epsilon\to 0
yields the desired result.
∎

6.2
Proof of the Lower Bound Part I: The Aizenman-Sims-Star Scheme

To being with the lower bound, note that for any sequence of positive numbers
z_{N}>0
and
M\geq 1
, we have the following the cavity inequality (see
[
23
]
)

\liminf_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\log z_{N}\geq M^{-1}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\left(\log z_{N+M}-\log z_{N}\right).

(6.13)

Using this and the above results we see that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}\geq\limsup_{M\to\infty}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\sup_{\mathfrak{X}\in[1,2]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}}M^{-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N+M}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}\right).

(6.14)

Next, we will need to give a cavity decomposition for
H_{N+M}
and
H_{N}
, which will follow from the
|\Omega|=1
case. Specifically, let us define for each
x\in\Omega
, the following centered Gaussian functions on
S_{N}
, specified giving their covariance functions: For
u,u^{\prime}\in S_{N}

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[Z_{N,x}(u)Z_{N,x}(u^{\prime})]&=\xi_{x}^{\prime}\left((u,u^{\prime})_{N}\right),\\
\mathbb{E}[Y_{N,x}(u)Y_{N,x}(u^{\prime})]&=\theta_{x}((u,u^{\prime})_{N}),\\
\mathbb{E}[H_{N,M,x}(u)H_{N,M,x}(u^{\prime})]&=(N+M)\left(\xi_{x}\left((u,u^{\prime})_{N+M}\right)-\xi_{x}(0)\right).\end{split}

(6.15)

We define the core Hamiltonian as

\mathcal{H}_{N,M}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D_{x,y}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}H_{N,M,x}(\bm{u}(x))+\sqrt{N}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{h}(x)\bm{u}_{1}(x),

(6.16)

and let
\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u})=\mathcal{H}_{N,M}(\bm{u})+s_{N}h_{N}(\bm{u})
.
We first define a function
\mathcal{Y}_{N}
on
S_{N}^{\Omega}
by

\mathcal{Y}_{N}(\bm{u})=\sum_{x\in\Omega}Y_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x)),

(6.17)

and then define an associated partition function on
S_{N}^{\Omega}
by

\hat{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}=\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}}\exp(-\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u})-\sqrt{M}\mathcal{Y}_{N}(\bm{u}))\omega(d\bm{u}).

(6.18)

Next, let us further consider a sequence of
N
i.i.d copies of
Z_{N,x}
which we denote
\vec{Z}_{N,x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We then define a function on
(\bm{u},\bm{v})\in S_{N+M}^{\Omega}
(with
\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}
and
\bm{v}\in(\mathbb{R}^{M})^{\Omega}
) by

\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D_{x,y}(\bm{v}(x),\bm{v}(y))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}(\vec{Z}_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x)),\bm{v}(x))+\sqrt{M}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{h}(x)\bm{v}_{1}(x).

(6.19)

Then for any
\delta>0
, we define the set

S_{N,M}(\delta)=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\cap S_{N+M}:\|v\|_{M}^{2}\leq 1+\delta\}=S_{N+M}\cap\mathbb{R}^{M}\times S_{M}(0,1+\delta),

(6.20)

and the partition function

\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta}=\int_{S_{N,M}(\delta)^{\Omega}}\exp(-\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\bm{v}))\omega(d\bm{u}d\bm{v}).

(6.21)

Our first result shows that for our lower bound, it will essentially suffice to deal with these partition functions instead.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any
\delta>0
we have that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N+M}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}-\mathbb{E}\log\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}&\geq-\left(\frac{M^{2}\delta}{N}+\frac{M}{N}\right)\sum_{x\in\Omega}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(1),\\
\bigg|\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}-\mathbb{E}\log\hat{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}\bigg|&\leq\frac{M^{2}}{N}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(1).\end{split}

(6.22)

Proof.

We note that by applying a suitable rotation, we may replace the external field term in
\mathcal{H}_{N+M}^{\mathfrak{X}}
as

\sqrt{N+M}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{h}(x)\bm{u}_{1}(x)\mapsto\sqrt{N}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{h}(x)\bm{u}_{1}(x)+\sqrt{M}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{h}(x)\bm{v}_{1}(x),

(6.23)

without affecting the value of
\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N+M}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}
. After this replacement is done, we denote by
Z_{N,M}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X},\delta}
the restriction of the partition function
Z_{N+M}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}
to the subset
S_{N,M}(\delta)^{\Omega}\subset S_{N+M}^{\Omega}
. As
Z_{N+M}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}\geq Z_{N,M}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X},\delta}
, we see to establish the first claim, it suffices to show that

|\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,M}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X},\delta}-\mathbb{E}\log\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta}|\leq\left(\frac{M^{2}\delta}{N}+\frac{M}{N}\right)\sum_{x\in\Omega}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(1).

(6.24)

For this, note that the deterministic components of both Hamiltonians coincide. For the centered Gaussian components, we apply Corollary
A.6
to see that

\begin{split}|\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N+M}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X},\delta}-\mathbb{E}\log\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}|\leq&\sum_{x\in\Omega}\sup_{(u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in S_{N,M}(\delta)}\bigg|(N+M)\xi_{x}((u,u^{\prime})_{N+M}+(v,v^{\prime})_{N+M})\\
&-(N+M)\xi_{x}((u,u^{\prime})_{N+M})-(v,v^{\prime})\xi_{x}^{\prime}((u,u^{\prime})_{N})\bigg|.\end{split}

(6.25)

It is easily checked using Taylor’s theorem that for
(u,v),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in S_{N+M}

\begin{split}|(N+M)\xi_{x}((u,u^{\prime})_{N+M}+(v,v^{\prime})_{N+M})-(N+M)\xi_{x}((u,u^{\prime})_{N+M})&\\
-(v,v^{\prime})\xi_{x}^{\prime}((u,u^{\prime})_{N+M})|&\leq\frac{(v,v^{\prime})^{2}}{2(N+M)}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(1).\end{split}

(6.26)

Similarly one may check that

|\xi_{x}^{\prime}((u,u^{\prime})_{N+M})-\xi_{x}^{\prime}((u,u^{\prime})_{N})|\leq\frac{M}{N}|(u,u^{\prime})_{N+M}|\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(1)\leq\frac{M}{N}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(1).

(6.27)

Combining these observations with (
6.25
) yields (
6.24
), establishing the first claim. For the second claim, we first note that one may routinely show that for
u,u^{\prime}\in S_{N}
,

|N\xi_{x}((u,u^{\prime})_{N})-(N+M)\xi_{x}\left((u,u^{\prime})_{N+M}\right)-M\theta_{x}((u,u^{\prime})_{N})-M\xi_{x}(0)|\leq\frac{M^{2}}{N}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(1).

(6.28)

Noting that making the changes
\xi_{x}(r)\mapsto\xi_{x}(r)-\xi_{x}(0)
does not affect the value of
\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}^{Sph,\mathfrak{X}}
, we see that (
6.28
) and Corollary
A.6
demonstrates the second claim.
∎

For our next step, we want to relate the measure spaces
(S_{N+M}^{\Omega},\omega_{N+M})
and
(S_{N}^{\Omega}\times(S_{M})^{\Omega},c_{N,M}^{|\Omega|}\omega_{N}^{\otimes\Omega}\otimes\gamma_{M}^{\otimes\Omega})
where
\gamma_{M}
denotes the standard Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
c_{N,M}
denotes the constant

c_{N,M}=\frac{\mathrm{vol}(S_{N+M})}{\mathrm{vol}(S_{N})}.

(6.29)

This will be adapted from
[
23
]
in the
|\Omega|=1
case, and will allow us to treat
S_{N+M}
as a product measure when
N
is large. For this, we recall from
[
23
]
the set

A_{M,N}=\prod_{j=1}^{M}[-\sqrt{(M+N+1-j)},\sqrt{(M+N+1-j)}],

(6.30)

Next, we define functions of
v\in A_{M,N}
by

\begin{split}a_{\ell}(v)&=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\sqrt{1+\frac{1-v^{2}_{j}}{M+N-j}},\;\;2\leq\ell\leq M+1,\;\;\ a_{1}(v)=1.\\
\phi_{M}(v)&=(v_{1}a_{1}(v),\dots,v_{M}a_{M}(v)),\;\;\psi_{M}(v)=a_{M+1}(v).\end{split}

(6.31)

Finally, we define a measure on
\gamma_{N,M}
on
A_{M,N}
by

\begin{split}\gamma_{N,M}(dv)&=b_{M,N}\prod_{j=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{v_{j}^{2}}{M+N+1-j}\right)^{\frac{M+N-j-2}{2}}dv,\\
b_{M,N}&=\prod_{j=1}^{M}\frac{\Gamma((N+j)/2)}{\Gamma((N+j-1)/2)\sqrt{\pi(N+j)}}.\end{split}

(6.32)

With this notation set, and writing elements of
w\in\mathbb{R}^{N+M}
as
w=(u,v)
with
u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we recall the following result.

Lemma 6.3

(Lemma 3,
[
23
]
)
.

For a non-negative function
f
on
S_{N+M}
we have that

\int_{S_{N+M}}f(w)\omega(dw)=c_{N,M}\int_{A_{M,N}}\left(\int_{S_{N}}f(\psi_{M}(v)u,\phi_{M}(v))\omega(du)\right)\gamma_{M,N}(dv).

(6.33)

A standard argument then shows the following.

Lemma 6.4
.

For a non-negative function
f
on
S_{N+M}^{\Omega}
we have that

\int_{S_{N+M}^{\Omega}}f(w)\omega(d\bm{w})=c_{N,M}^{|\Omega|}\int_{A_{M,N}^{\Omega}}\left(\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}}f(\bm{\psi}_{M}(\bm{v})\bm{u},\bm{\phi}_{M}(\bm{v}))\omega(d\bm{u})\right)\gamma_{M,N}(d\bm{v}),

(6.34)

where here
\bm{\psi}_{M}(\bm{v})(x)=\psi_{M}(\bm{v}(x))
, and similarly for
\bm{\phi}_{M}(\bm{v})
,
(\bm{\psi}_{M}(\bm{v})\bm{u})(x)=\psi_{M}(\bm{v}(x))\bm{u}(x)
and finally

\gamma_{M,N}(d\bm{v})=\prod_{x\in\Omega}\gamma_{M,N}(\bm{v}(x))

(6.35)

Now we set
A_{N,M}(\delta):=\phi_{M}^{-1}\left(S_{M}(0,1+\delta)\right)\cap A_{N,M}(\delta)
. Then by Lemma
6.4
we have

\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta}=c_{N,M}^{|\Omega|}\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}\times A_{N,M}(\delta)^{\Omega}}\exp(-\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{\psi}_{M}(\bm{v})\bm{u}))-\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{\psi}(\bm{v})\bm{u},\bm{\phi}(\bm{v}))\omega(d\bm{u})\gamma_{M,N}(d\bm{v}).

(6.36)

In the next lemma, we compare this to the following partition function

\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta,I}=c_{N,M}^{|\Omega|}\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}\times(S_{M}^{\delta})^{\Omega}}\exp(-\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\bm{v}))\omega(d\bm{u})\gamma_{M,N}(d\bm{v}).

(6.37)

Lemma 6.5
.

Let us choose
M\geq 10\delta>0
. Then we have that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}M^{-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\log\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},2\delta}-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta}\right)\geq-4\delta\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\sum_{y\in\Omega}|D_{xy}|+|\bm{h}(x)|+\xi^{\prime}_{x}(1)\right).

(6.38)

Proof.

To begin we will need an estimate. By construction, for any
v\in A_{M,N}

\|\phi_{M}(v)\|^{2}+N\psi_{M}(v)^{2}=N+M.

(6.39)

Note moreover that
1\leq a_{\ell}(v)\leq a_{M+1}(v)
, so that
\|v\|^{2}\leq\|\phi_{M}(v)\|^{2}\leq\psi_{M}(v)^{2}\|v\|^{2}
. Combined with (
6.39
), this implies that if
v\in S^{\delta}_{M}\cap A_{M,N}

0\leq N(\psi_{M}(v)^{2}-1)\leq\psi_{M}(v)^{2}\|v\|^{2}-M\leq\psi_{M}(v)^{2}M(\delta+1)-M.

(6.40)

Thus if we assume that
M/\delta,N/M>10
, one derives routinely that

0\leq N\left(\psi_{M}(v)^{2}-1\right)\leq 2\delta M.

(6.41)

From this we conclude that for
v,v^{\prime}\in S_{N}^{\delta}
, we have that

\begin{split}N|(\phi_{M}(v),\phi_{M}(v^{\prime}))-(v,v^{\prime})|&\leq 4\delta N^{-1}M^{2},\;\;\;N|\psi_{M}(v^{\prime})\psi_{M}(v)-1|\leq 2\delta N^{-1}M,\\
N|\psi_{M}(v)-1|&\leq 2\delta N^{-1}M.\end{split}

(6.42)

Now we will fix
M,\delta>0
, assume that
N\geq 10M
. We now restrict this integration in
\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},2\delta}
. For this note by the above estimate, for sufficiently large
N
we have that
\phi_{M}(S_{M}^{\delta})\subseteq S_{M}(0,1+2\delta)
. Moreover, as
100M\delta\leq N
, we have that
S_{M}^{\delta}\subseteq A_{N,M}
. Thus if we define

\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta,res}=c_{N,M}^{|\Omega|}\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}\times S_{M}(\delta)^{\Omega}}\exp(-\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{\psi}_{M}(\bm{v})\bm{u}))-\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{\psi}(\bm{v})\bm{u},\bm{\phi}(\bm{v}))\omega(d\bm{u})\gamma_{M,N}(d\bm{v}),

(6.43)

it suffices for us to show that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}M^{-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\log\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta,res}-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta}\right)\geq-4\delta\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\sum_{y\in\Omega}|D_{xy}|+|\bm{h}(x)|+\xi^{\prime}_{x}(1)\right)

(6.44)

Returning to (
6.44
), we note that to bound the difference, we must we must deal with the effect of both the deterministic and random components of

\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{\psi}_{M}(\bm{v})\bm{u})+\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{\psi}_{M}(\bm{v})\bm{u},\bm{\phi}_{M}(\bm{v}))\text{ and }\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u})+\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\bm{v}).

(6.45)

To begin we will deal with the deterministic pieces, for which we just bound the differences. For this, note that for
N
sufficiently large and
(\bm{u},\bm{v}),(\bm{u}^{\prime},\bm{v}^{\prime})\in(S_{N}\times S_{M}^{\delta})^{\Omega}

\begin{split}\bigg|\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D_{x,y}(\psi_{M}(\bm{v}(x))\bm{u}(x),\psi_{M}(\bm{v^{\prime}}(x))\bm{u^{\prime}}(x))-\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D_{x,y}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u^{\prime}}(x))\bigg|\leq\\
\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}|D_{x,y}||(\psi_{M}(\bm{v}(x))\psi_{M}(\bm{v^{\prime}}(x))-1)(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u^{\prime}}(x))|\leq 2\delta M\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}|D_{x,y}|.\end{split}

(6.46)

A similar argument shows that

\bigg|\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D_{x,y}(\phi_{M}(\bm{v}(x)),\phi_{M}(\bm{v^{\prime}}(x)))-\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D_{x,y}(\bm{v}(x),\bm{v^{\prime}}(x))\bigg|\leq 4N^{-1}M^{2}\delta\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}|D_{x,y}|.

(6.47)

Finally, to contend with the external field, we note that

\left|\sqrt{N}\bm{h}(x)\bm{\psi}_{M}(\bm{v}(x))\bm{u}_{1}(x)-\sqrt{N}\bm{h}(x)\bm{u}_{1}(x)\right|\leq|\bm{h}(x)||\leq N|\bm{\psi}_{M}(\bm{v}(x))-1|\leq 2\delta M|\bm{h}(x)|.

(6.48)

This completes the analysis of the deterministic terms. The random components will be dealt with by employing Corollary
A.6
. To do so, we note that the covariance of
\mathcal{H}_{N,M}(\bm{u})+\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\bm{v})
at points
(\bm{u},\bm{v})
and
(\bm{u}^{\prime},\bm{v}^{\prime})
is given by

\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bigg((N+M)\xi_{x}((\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N+M})+\xi^{\prime}_{x}((\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N})(\bm{v}(x),\bm{v}^{\prime}(x))\bigg).

(6.49)

In particular, the difference between the variance functions of the Hamiltonians in (
6.45
), excluding the perturbation terms, may be bounded above by

\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(3\delta M\xi_{x}^{\prime}(1)+3\delta N^{-1}M(1+\delta)+4M^{2}N^{-1}\xi_{x}^{\prime\prime}(1)\delta(1+\delta)\right).

(6.50)

Finally for the perturbation term, we recall that the covariance of
h_{N}
is given by (
5.5
). We note that for any
\bm{w}\in[0,1]^{\Omega}
, and
(\bm{u},\bm{v})\in S_{N}^{\Omega}\times(S_{N}^{\delta})^{\Omega}

|\bm{R}^{\bm{w}}_{N+M}(\bm{\psi}_{M}(\bm{v})\bm{u},\bm{\phi}_{M}(\bm{v}))-\bm{R}^{\bm{w}}_{N+M}(\bm{u},\bm{v})|\leq\left(\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{w}(x)\right)(4M^{2}N^{-1}(1+\delta)+2MN^{-1}\delta).

(6.51)

Using this, it is easily shown that the perturbation term is negligible as well.
∎

We next make the replacement
\gamma_{M,N}\mapsto\gamma_{M}
and introduce

\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta,II}=c_{N,M}^{|\Omega|}\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}\times(S_{M}^{\delta})^{\Omega}}\exp(-\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\bm{v}))\omega(d\bm{u})\gamma_{M}(d\bm{v}).

(6.52)

We then have the following result.

Lemma 6.6
.

For any
M\geq 1
and
\delta>0
we have that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\log\frac{\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta,II}}{\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta,I}}\geq 0.

(6.53)

Proof.

The only change between these integrals is the replacement
\gamma_{M,N}\mapsto\gamma_{M}
. Both of these measures have a density, and are products of the measures in the
|\Omega|=1
case. As Lemma 5 of
[
23
]
shows this ratio is bounded below by a constant going to
1
, from which this result immediately follows.
∎

Now finally, we will relate
\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta,II}
to our final product partition function

\tilde{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}=\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}\times S_{M}^{\Omega}}\exp(-\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\bm{v})\omega(d\bm{u})\omega(d\bm{v}).

(6.54)

Lemma 6.7
.

For fixed
M\geq 1
and
\delta>0
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\log\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta,II}-\mathbb{E}\log\tilde{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}\geq-\frac{M\delta}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}|D_{x,y}|+|\Omega|\log\frac{c_{N,M}\gamma_{M}(S^{\delta}_{M})}{\mathrm{vol}(S_{M})},

(6.55)

Proof.

Consider the mapping
\hat{\bm{v}}(x)=\|\bm{v}(x)\|^{-1}\bm{v}(x)
defined on
(S_{M}^{\delta})^{\Omega}
. We will try to replacement the factor of
\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\bm{v})
with
\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\hat{\bm{v}})
in
\bar{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta,II}
. For the first term, note that for
\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime}\in(S_{M}^{\delta})^{\Omega}
, we have that

\bigg|\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D_{x,y}(\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime})-\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D_{x,y}(\hat{\bm{v}},\hat{\bm{v}}^{\prime})\bigg|\leq M\delta\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}|D_{x,y}|.

(6.56)

The remaining terms in
\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\bm{v})
are linear in
\bm{v}
. To deal with them we recall that for any vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, the function

\lambda\mapsto\int_{S_{M}}\exp(\lambda(v,u))\omega(du)=\int_{S_{M}}\cosh(\lambda(v,u))\omega(du),

(6.57)

is increasing in
\lambda>0
(see Lemma 1 of
[
23
]
). In particular, we see that
\log(Z_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X},\delta})
is bounded below by

-\frac{M\delta}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}|D_{x,y}|+\log\left(c_{N,M}^{|\Omega|}\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}\times(S_{M}^{\delta})^{\Omega}}\exp(-\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\hat{\bm{v}}))\omega(d\bm{u})\gamma_{M}(d\bm{v})\right).

(6.58)

By the co-area formula the final term may be written as

|\Omega|\log\frac{c_{N,M}\gamma_{M}(S^{\delta}_{M})}{\mathrm{vol}(S_{M})}+\log(\tilde{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}).

(6.59)

Taking expectations then completes the proof.
∎

By Stirling’s approximation we see that

\lim_{M\to\infty}M^{-1}\log\gamma_{M}(S_{M}^{\delta})=0,\;\;\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}M^{-1}\log c_{N,M}/\mathrm{vol}(S_{M})=0.

(6.60)

In addition, all of the above bounds are independent of
(x_{p,q})_{p,q\geq 1}\in[1,2]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}
, and go to zero if the limits
N\to\infty
,
M\to\infty
, and then
\delta\to 0
are taken. Thus in total, we have shown the following result.

Lemma 6.8
.

We have that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,Sph}\geq\limsup_{M\to\infty}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\sup_{x\in[1,2]^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}}|\Omega|^{-1}M^{-1}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\log\tilde{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}-\mathbb{E}\log\hat{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}\bigg).

(6.61)

6.3
Proof of the Lower Bound Part II: Synchronization and Perturbation

The next step is to understand the quantity

|\Omega|^{-1}M^{-1}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\log\tilde{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}-\mathbb{E}\log\hat{Z}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}\bigg).

(6.62)

Let us denote the Gibbs measure on
S_{N}^{\Omega}
with respect to
(\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}},\omega_{N})
as
\langle*\rangle_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}
. Subtracting a factor of the partition function of
(\mathcal{H}_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}},\omega_{N})
from both terms shows that (
6.62
) is equal to

|\Omega|^{-1}M^{-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\log\left\langle\int_{S_{M}^{\Omega}}\exp(\mathcal{Z}_{N,M}(\bm{u},\bm{v}))\omega(d\bm{v})\right\rangle_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}-\mathbb{E}\log\left\langle\exp(\sqrt{M}\mathcal{Y}_{N}(\bm{u}))\right\rangle_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}\right).

(6.63)

Thus the analysis of the right-hand side of Lemma
6.8
comes down to understanding the Gibbs measure
\langle*\rangle_{N,M}^{\mathfrak{X}}
.

This is where the importance of the perturbation term comes in, as we may apply Proposition
5.8
to make, a sequences of choices of

which force the results of Section
5
to hold. We summarize these results in the following corollary.

Let us denote by

the infinite overlap array associated to an infinite sequence of i.i.d samples from the Gibbs measure

We are now ready to establish the lower bound, and complete the proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem

.

As noted above, the upper bound follows from Proposition

To establish the lower bound, let us take the set-up of Corollary

Now choose

Appendix A
Concentration and Interpolation Results for Gaussian Functions

This appendix consists of some results about Gaussian random functions we need throughout the text. The purpose is to recall and derive these in the generality required above, as well as to provide a convenient reference for the main text. To begin, we recall the Borell-Tsirelson-Ibragimov-Sudakov (commonly abbreviated to Borell-TIS) inequality (see Theorem 2.1.1 of
[
2
]
)

Theorem A.1
.

[Borell-TIS]
Let
h
be some a.s. bounded, centered continuous Gaussian function on some topological space
M
, such that
\sup_{s\in M}\mathbb{E}[h(s)^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
. Then
\sup_{s\in M}h(s)
possesses a finite first moment, and for any
c>0

\mathbb{P}\left(|\sup_{s\in M}h(s)-\mathbb{E}\sup_{s\in M}h(s)|>c\right)\leq 2\exp(-c^{2}/4\sigma^{2}).

(A.1)

Identical statements hold for
\sup_{s\in M}|h(s)|
.

Our second claim follows simply from the concentration of Lipshitz statistics for Gaussians, and is a generalization of Theorem 1.2 of
[
53
]
, which treats the case where
M
is a finite set. It may also be thought of as a positive temperature version of Theorem
A.1
.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
h
be some continuous centered Gaussian function on some compact metric space
T
, with finite positive Borel measure
m
, and such that
\sup_{s\in T}\mathbb{E}[h(s)^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
. Let us define the partition function
Z=\int_{T}\exp(h(s))m(ds)
. Then
\log Z
possesses a finite first moment and for any
c>0

\mathbb{P}\left(|\log Z-\mathbb{E}\log Z|>c\right)\leq 2\exp(-c^{2}/4\sigma^{2}).

(A.2)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
m
is a probability measure.
Employing the Karhunen–Loève expansion (see
[
44
]
), we see that there exists a sequence of random variables
(Z_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, which are i.i.d. centered Gaussians, as well as a deterministic sequence
\varphi_{k}\in L^{2}(T,m)
, such that we may write a.s.
h(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}Z_{k}\varphi_{k}(s)
and where
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varphi_{k}(s)^{2}=\mathbb{E}[h(s)^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\begin{split}&\log\int_{T}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v_{k}\varphi_{k}(s)\right)m(ds)\\
&\leq\log\int_{T}\exp\left(\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v_{k}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right)m(ds)\leq\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v_{k}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.\end{split}

(A.3)

In particular, we see that
(v_{k})_{k\geq 1}\mapsto\log\int_{T}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v_{k}\varphi_{k}(s)\right)m(ds)
is
\ell^{2}
-Lipshitz with constant less than
\sigma
.

We recall that a standard Gaussian random variable satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality with constant
1
, by the Bakry-Emery criterion
[
49
]
. In particular, so does the sequence
(Z_{k})_{k\geq 1}
. Thus by Herbst’s argument
[
49
]
we have for any
\alpha>0
that

\log\mathbb{E}Z^{\alpha}\leq\alpha\mathbb{E}\log Z+\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}/2.

(A.4)

Thus the claim now follows from Markov’s inequality.
∎

Next we will need a slightly variant of this result which removes the compactness assumption.

Corollary A.3
.

Let
(h,T,m)
be as in Proposition
A.2
except that the assumption that
T
is compact is weakened to the assumption
T
is
\sigma
-compact. Then if we have that
\mathbb{E}\log Z\leq\log\mathbb{E}Z<\infty
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(|\log Z-\mathbb{E}\log Z|>c\right)\leq 2\exp(-c^{2}/4\sigma^{2}).

(A.5)

Moreover, let us fix an increasing sequence of compact
T_{n}\subseteq T
such that
\bigcup_{n\geq 1}^{\infty}T_{n}=T
, and denote
Z_{n}=\int_{T_{n}}\exp(h(s))m(ds)
. Then we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{n}=\mathbb{E}\log Z.

(A.6)

Proof.

By Jensen’s inequality we have that
\mathbb{E}\log Z\leq\log\mathbb{E}Z<\infty
. We next show that
\mathbb{E}|\log Z|<\infty
. As
\mathbb{E}\log Z<\infty
, we see that it suffices to show that
\mathbb{E}\log ZI(Z\geq 1)<\infty
. For this, we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{P}(Z\geq 1)>0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\log ZI(Z\geq 1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\log Z|Z\geq 1]\mathbb{P}(Z\geq 1)\leq\log\left(\mathbb{E}[Z|Z\geq 1]\right)\mathbb{P}(Z\geq 1)

(A.7)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[ZI(Z\geq 1)]}{\mathbb{P}(Z\geq 1)}\right)\mathbb{P}(Z\geq 1)\leq\log\mathbb{E}Z-\mathbb{P}(Z\geq 1)\log\mathbb{P}(Z\geq 1).

(A.8)

As
-x\log(x)
is bounded for
x\in(0,1)
, we see
\mathbb{E}\log ZI(Z\geq 1)<\infty
so
\mathbb{E}|\log Z|<\infty
.

As
\mathbb{E}|\log Z|<\infty
we see that
\log Z
is a.s. finite, and by the monotone convergence theorem that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\log Z_{n}=\log Z
. By the dominated convergence theorem, we thus obtain (
A.6
). From this we have a.s. that

\lim_{n\to\infty}|\log Z_{n}-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{n}|=|\log Z-\mathbb{E}\log Z|.

(A.9)

Thus we see that a.s.

\liminf_{n\to\infty}I(|\log Z_{n}-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{n}|>\alpha)\geq I(|\log Z-\mathbb{E}\log Z|\geq\alpha).

(A.10)

As
\mathbb{P}(|\log Z-\mathbb{E}\log Z|=\alpha)=0
, we see by Fatou’s lemma that

\mathbb{P}(\log Z-\mathbb{E}\log Z|\geq\alpha)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(|\log Z_{n}-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{n}|>\alpha)\leq 2\exp(-c^{2}/4\sigma^{2}).

(A.11)

∎

Next we will need the following consequence of Gaussian integration by parts. The only difficulty is up to the verification of convergence conditions to allow the interchange of integration and differentiation.

Proposition A.4
.

Let
V
and
W
two independent centered Gaussian processes, defined on some topological space
A
with finite positive Borel measure
m
, with covariance functions given by
C_{V}
and
C_{W}
, respectively. Let us assume that both
C_{V}
and
C_{W}
are bounded. Define the interpolating function

V_{t}(x)=\sqrt{(1-t)}V(x)+\sqrt{t}W(x).

(A.12)

Then we have that

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbb{E}\log\int_{A}e^{V_{t}(x)}m(dx)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\left\langle C_{W}(x,x)-C_{V}(x,x)\right\rangle_{t}-\mathbb{E}\left\langle C_{W}(x,x^{\prime})-C_{V}(x,x^{\prime})\right\rangle_{t}\bigg),

(A.13)

where here
\langle*\rangle_{t}
denotes the Gibbs measure with respect to
(V_{t},m)
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a constant bounding both the variance of
V
and
W
. We may assume that
m
is a probability measure. Then by Jensen’s inequality we have that

0\leq\mathbb{E}\log\int_{A}e^{V_{t}(x)}m(dx)\leq\log\mathbb{E}\int_{A}e^{V_{t}(x)}m(dx)\leq C/2

(A.14)

so that
\log\int_{A}e^{V_{t}(x)}m(dx)
has a first moment and is a.s. finite. Next, note the inequality

\left|\frac{d}{dt}e^{V_{t}(x)}\right|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}\cosh(V(x))\cosh(W(x)).

(A.15)

Thus a similar application of Jensen’s inequality shows that
|\frac{d}{dt}e^{V_{t}(x)}|
is a.s. uniformly integrable with respect to
(A,m)
, so by the Leibniz rule we a.s. have that

\frac{d}{dt}\log\int_{A}e^{V_{t}(x)}m(dx)=\frac{\int_{A}\frac{d}{dt}V_{t}(x)e^{V_{t}(x)}m(dx)}{\int_{A}e^{V_{t}(x)}m(dx)}=\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}V_{t}(x)\right\rangle_{t}.

(A.16)

An easy computation using functional Gaussian integration by parts yields that

\mathbb{E}\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}V_{t}(x)\right\rangle_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\left\langle C_{W}(x,x)-C_{V}(x,x)\right\rangle_{t}-\mathbb{E}\left\langle C_{W}(x,x^{\prime})-C_{V}(x,x^{\prime})\right\rangle_{t}\bigg).

(A.17)

The final verification requires us to show that

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\log\int_{A}e^{V_{t}(x)}m(dx)=\mathbb{E}\frac{d}{dt}\log\int_{A}e^{V_{t}(x)}m(dx)=\mathbb{E}\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}V_{t}(x)\right\rangle_{t}.

(A.18)

Again we will apply the Leibniz rule, for which we verify that
|\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}V_{t}(x)\right\rangle_{t}|
has a uniform
1
st moment with respect to
t
. For this, we note that

\left|\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}V_{t}(x)\right\rangle_{t}\right|\leq\sqrt{t}\langle|W(x)|\rangle_{t}+\sqrt{1-t}\langle|V(x)|\rangle_{t}.

(A.19)

We show that a uniform bound on
\langle|W(x)|\rangle_{t}
, the other being identical. For this, we note that employing again functional Gaussian integration by parts we have that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\langle|W(x)|\rangle_{t}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\langle W(x)^{2}\rangle_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(&\mathbb{E}\langle C_{W}(x,x)\rangle_{t}+\sqrt{t}\mathbb{E}\langle C_{W}(x,x)W(x)\rangle_{t}\\
&-\sqrt{t}\mathbb{E}\langle C_{W}(x,y)W(x)\rangle_{t}\bigg)\leq 2C(1+\mathbb{E}\langle|W(x)|\rangle_{t}).\end{split}

(A.20)

This implies uniform finiteness of
\mathbb{E}\langle|W(x)|\rangle_{t}
by the quadratic formula.
∎

As an elementary consequence of Proposition
A.4
we may obtain continuity of both the free energy of the spherical models in the choice of mixing function.

Corollary A.5
.

For any choice of valid mixing functions
\bm{\xi}^{0}
and
\bm{\xi}^{1}
, and let us denote the coefficients with respect to
\bm{\xi}^{i}
as
\bm{\beta}_{p}^{i}(x)
. We have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}|\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}(\bm{\xi}^{0})-\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}(\bm{\xi}^{1})|\leq\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega,p\geq 0}\left|\bm{\beta}^{0}_{p}(x)^{2}-\bm{\beta}^{1}_{p}(x)^{2}\right|.

(A.21)

Finally, another incredibly useful corollary of this result is the following.

Corollary A.6
.

Let
V
,
W
, and
(A,m)
be as in Proposition
A.4
. Then we have that

\bigg|\mathbb{E}\log\int_{A}e^{V(x)}m(dx)-\mathbb{E}\log\int_{A}e^{W(x)}m(dx)\bigg|\leq\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in A}|C_{V}(x,x^{\prime})-C_{W}(x,x^{\prime})|.

(A.22)

A more complicated consequence is the following result, which follows from a mixture of using Corollary
A.6
to deal with the Gaussian component, Taylor’s theorem for the deterministic part, and then the co-area formula. For the context, let us fix some
\epsilon>0
, some finite index set
\Omega
, and some
\bm{q}\in(2\epsilon,\infty)^{\Omega}
. We now give a simple result which allows us to compare the effect of an
\epsilon
-thickening on the partition function.

Proposition A.7
.

Let
(S,\mu)
denote some finite Borel measure space, and furthermore, let us fix a choice of
N\geq 1
, deterministic differentiable function
g:S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})\to\mathbb{R}
and a family
(V_{x})_{x\in\Omega}
of a centered continuous Gaussian functions
V_{x}:S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}(x))\times S\to\mathbb{R}
such that for
(u,\alpha),(u^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\in S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}(x))\times S
we have that

\mathbb{E}V_{x}(u,\alpha)V_{x}(u^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})=Nf_{x,\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}(\|u\|^{2}_{N},\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{N},(u,u^{\prime})_{N}),

(A.23)

where
f_{x,\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}
are some fixed functions. Let us define the partition functions:

Z=\int_{S_{N}(\bm{q})\times S}e^{\sum_{x\in\Omega}V_{x}(\bm{u}(x),\alpha)+g(\bm{u})}\omega(d\bm{u})\mu(d\alpha),\;\;Z^{\epsilon}=\int_{S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})\times S}e^{\sum_{x\in\Omega}V_{x}(\bm{u}(x),\alpha)+g(\bm{u})}d\bm{u}\mu(d\alpha).

(A.24)

Then we have that

\begin{split}N^{-1}|\mathbb{E}\log Z-\mathbb{E}\log Z^{\epsilon}|\leq&\sqrt{3}\epsilon\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\sup_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in S}\|\|\nabla f_{x,\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\|\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)+N^{-1/2}\|\|\nabla g\|\|_{L^{\infty}}\sqrt{|\Omega|\epsilon}\\
&+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2\bm{q}(x)}+\frac{1}{2N}|\log(N\epsilon/\bm{q}(x))|+\frac{1}{2N}|\log(N^{-1}\bm{q}(x))|\right).\end{split}

(A.25)

Proof.

We begin by introducing the normalization function
S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})\to S_{N}(\bm{q})
given for
x\in\Omega
by

\bm{u}_{\bm{q}}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{\bm{q}(x)}}{\|\bm{u}(x)\|_{N}}\bm{u}(x),

(A.26)

and for convenience we define
V(\bm{u},\alpha)=\sum_{x\in\Omega}V_{x}(\bm{u}(x),\alpha)
. With this notation, we bound our quantity as the sum of three differences,

N^{-1}|\mathbb{E}\log Z-\mathbb{E}\log Z^{\epsilon}|\leq I+II+III,

(A.27)

which are given by

\begin{split}I&=N^{-1}\left|\mathbb{E}\log\int_{S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})\times S}e^{V(\bm{u},\alpha)+g(\bm{u})}d\bm{u}\mu(d\alpha)-\mathbb{E}\log\int_{S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})\times S}e^{V(\bm{u}_{\bm{q}},\alpha)+g(\bm{u})}d\bm{u}\mu(d\alpha)\right|,\\
II&=N^{-1}\left|\mathbb{E}\log\int_{S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})\times S}e^{V(\bm{u}_{\bm{q}},\alpha)+g(\bm{u})}d\bm{u}\mu(d\alpha)-\mathbb{E}\log\int_{S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})\times S}e^{V(\bm{u}_{\bm{q}},\alpha)+g(\bm{u}_{\bm{q}})}d\bm{u}\mu(d\alpha)\right|,\\
III&=N^{-1}\left|\mathbb{E}\log\int_{S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})\times S}e^{V(\bm{u}_{\bm{q}},\alpha)+g(\bm{u}_{\bm{q}})}d\bm{u}\mu(d\alpha)-\mathbb{E}\log\int_{S_{N}(\bm{q})\times S}e^{V(\bm{u}_{\bm{q}},\alpha)+g(\bm{u}_{\bm{q}})}\omega(d\bm{u})\mu(d\alpha)\right|.\end{split}

(A.28)

We begin by treating
I
. By Corollary
A.6
, we have that

\begin{split}I\leq\sup_{(\bm{u},\alpha),(\bm{u}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\in S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})\times S}\bigg|\sum_{x\in\Omega}&\bigg(f_{x,\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}_{N},\|\bm{u}^{\prime}(x)\|^{2}_{N},(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N}\right)\\
&-f_{x,\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\bm{q}(x),\bm{q}(x),(\bm{u}_{\bm{q}}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}_{\bm{q}}(x))_{N}\right)\bigg)\bigg|.\end{split}

(A.29)

This may further be bounded by

\sup_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in S}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\sup_{r,r^{\prime}\in\left[\sqrt{\bm{q}(x)},\sqrt{\bm{q}(x)+\epsilon}\right],\rho\in[-1,1]}\bigg|f_{x,\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\left(r^{2},(r^{\prime})^{2},rr^{\prime}\rho\right)-f_{x,\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\left(\bm{q}(x),\bm{q}(x),\bm{q}(x)\rho\right)\bigg|\right).

(A.30)

We note that

\left\|\left(r^{2}-\bm{q}(x),(r^{\prime})^{2}-\bm{q}(x),rr^{\prime}\rho-\bm{q}(x)\rho\right)\right\|\leq\sqrt{3}\epsilon.

(A.31)

So applying Taylor’s theorem, we see that

I\leq\sqrt{3}\epsilon\sum_{x\in\Omega}\sup_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in S}\|\|\nabla f_{x,\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\|\|_{L^{\infty}}.

(A.32)

For
II
, we use the bound

II\leq N^{-1}\sup_{\bm{u}\in S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})}|g(\bm{u})-g(\bm{u}_{\bm{q}})|.

(A.33)

Combining this with Taylor’s theorem we conclude that

II\leq N^{-1/2}\|\|\nabla g\|\|_{L^{\infty}}\sup_{\bm{u}\in S_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q})}\sqrt{\sum_{x\in\Omega}\|\bm{u}(x)-\bm{q}(x)\|^{2}_{N}}\leq N^{-1/2}\|\|\nabla g\|\|_{L^{\infty}}\sqrt{|\Omega|\epsilon}.

(A.34)

For
III
, by expanding the first integral using the co-area formula with respect to
\bm{u}\mapsto\bm{u}_{\bm{q}}
we have that

III=N^{-1}\left|\log\left(\prod_{x\in\Omega}\sqrt{N\bm{q}(x)}\int_{1}^{\sqrt{1+\epsilon/\bm{q}(x)}}r^{N-1}dr\right)\right|.

(A.35)

We note that

\sqrt{N\bm{q}(x)}\int_{1}^{\sqrt{1+\epsilon/\bm{q}(x)}}r^{N-1}dr=\sqrt{N^{-1}\bm{q}(x)}\left((1+\epsilon/\bm{q}(x))^{N/2}-1\right).

(A.36)

Using that for
x>0
, we have that

Nx\leq\left((1+x)^{N}-1\right)\leq\exp(Nx),

(A.37)

we see that

\frac{1}{2}\log(N\epsilon/\bm{q}(x))\leq\log\left(\sqrt{N^{-1}\bm{q}(x)}\left((1+\epsilon/\bm{q}(x))^{N/2}-1\right)\right)\leq\frac{N\epsilon}{2\bm{q}(x)}+\frac{1}{2}\log(N^{-1}\bm{q}(x))

(A.38)

and so we obtain the bound

III\leq\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2\bm{q}(x)}+\frac{1}{2N}|\log(N\epsilon/\bm{q}(x))|+\frac{1}{2N}|\log(N^{-1}\bm{q}(x))|\right).

(A.39)

Combining all of these bounds completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Analytic Properties of the Functionals
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}

In this appendix we will prove some results concerning the functionals
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{A}
introduced in (
1.23
) (with
\beta=1
) and (
3.3
) above. We first begin with a result that shows that the infimum of
\mathcal{B}
over
\mathcal{Y}
and its subset
\mathcal{Y}_{fin}
coincide.

Proposition B.1
.

We have that

\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}}\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(B.1)

The main tool we use to prove this is the following explicit bound.

Lemma B.2
.

Let us fix some small
c,c^{\prime}>0
. Then there is some large
C:=C(c^{\prime},c)>0
such that the following holds: Let
\zeta_{0}
and
\zeta_{1}
be probability measures on
[0,1]
such that
\zeta_{i}([0,1-c])=1
, and choose some monotone increasing
\bm{\Phi}:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{\Omega}
such that
\Phi_{x}(1-c)\geq c^{\prime}
and
(\zeta^{i},\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}
. Then we have that

|\mathcal{B}(\zeta_{0},\bm{\Phi})-\mathcal{B}(\zeta_{1},\bm{\Phi})|\leq C\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\left|\zeta_{0}([0,s])-\zeta_{1}([0,s])\right|.

(B.2)

Proof.

For convenience, let us define
\epsilon:=\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\left|\zeta_{0}([0,s])-\zeta_{1}([0,s])\right|
. To treat one term of
\mathcal{B}
, we see that

\begin{split}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\zeta_{0}([0,s])\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds-\int_{0}^{1}\zeta_{1}([0,s])\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds\right|\leq&\\
\int_{0}^{1}\left|\zeta_{0}([0,s])\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)-\zeta_{1}([0,s])\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)\right|ds\leq&\\
\epsilon\int_{0}^{1}\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(s))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(s)ds=\epsilon(\xi^{\prime}_{x}(1)-\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\bm{\Phi}_{x}(0)))\leq&\epsilon\xi_{x}(1).\end{split}

(B.3)

Further, let us write
\bm{\delta}^{i}
for the function
\bm{\delta}
corresponding to the choice
(\zeta_{i},\bm{\Phi})
. Then as in (
B.3
) we see that

|\delta_{x}^{0}(u)-\delta_{x}^{1}(u)|\leq\epsilon.

(B.4)

We also that we may take
q_{*}=1-c^{\prime}
for both endpoints, and we have that
\delta_{x}^{i}(1-c)=1-\Phi_{x}(1-c)\geq c^{\prime}
. Thus we have that
\bm{\delta}:[0,1]\to[c^{\prime},1]^{\Omega}
, and as
\bm{K}^{D}
is uniformly differentiable on
[c^{\prime},1]^{\Omega}
, for some large
C_{0}:=C_{0}(c^{\prime})>0
we have that

\sup_{s\in[0,1],x\in\Omega}|K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{0}(s))-K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{1}(s))|\leq C_{0}\epsilon.

(B.5)

Similarly we may find some
C_{1}>0
such that

\|(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{\delta}^{0}(0)))^{-1}-(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{\delta}^{1}(0)))^{-1}\|_{F}\leq C_{1}\epsilon.

(B.6)

Together, these show that

|\mathcal{B}(\zeta_{0},\bm{\Phi})-\mathcal{B}(\zeta_{1},\bm{\Phi})|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left(\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\xi_{x}(1)+C_{0}+\frac{C_{1}}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}|\bm{h}(x)\bm{h}(y)|\right),

(B.7)

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
B.1
.

Let us take some
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}
. Note that this pair satisfies the conditions of Lemma
B.2
for some
c,c^{\prime}>0
. Moreover, note that the right-hand side of (
B.2
) is proportional to the Wasserstein metric, and so it is well known that we may find a sequence (for example by taking suitably small quantiles approximation of
\zeta
) of probability measures
\zeta_{n}
, such that
\zeta_{n}([0,1-c])=\zeta([0,1-c])=0
, and such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\sup_{s\in[0,1]}|\zeta([0,s])-\zeta_{n}([0,s])|=0,

(B.8)

So that in particular, we have that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})-\mathcal{B}(\zeta_{n},\bm{\Phi})|=0.

(B.9)

Thus we have that
\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\geq\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{fin}}\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
, which suffices to complete the proof.
∎

The next result we need shows that we have that
\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{Phi})=\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}^{\prime})
when
\bm{\Phi}(s)=\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}(s)
for
s\in\text{supp}(\zeta)
. For this we need a technical lemma.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\phi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be a non-decreasing and absolutely continuous function,
\mu
a finite measure on
[0,1]
, and
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
integrable. Then we have that

\int_{0}^{1}\mu([0,x])f(\phi(x))\phi^{\prime}(x)dx=\int_{\phi(0)}^{\phi(1)}\phi_{*}(\mu)([0,x])f(x)dx.

(B.10)

Proof.

By definition
\phi_{*}(\mu)([0,x])=\mu(\phi^{-1}([0,x]))
, and as this is monotone, and thus integrable, we see by employing a change of variables with respect to function
\phi
, we have that

\int_{\phi(0)}^{\phi(1)}\mu(\phi^{-1}([0,x]))f(x)dx=\int_{0}^{1}\mu(\phi(\phi^{-1}([0,x])))f(\phi(x))\phi^{\prime}(x)dx.

(B.11)

To compare this to the desired integral, we note that if
\phi
is strictly increasing in a neighborhood of
x
, we have that

\phi(\phi^{-1}([0,x]))=\{y\in[0,1]:\phi(y)\leq\phi(x)\}=[0,x].

(B.12)

In particular, if
\phi^{\prime}(x)
exists and is positive, this holds. However, as
\phi^{\prime}
must be either positive or zero, and exists a.e, we see that a.e. we have that

\mu(\phi(\phi^{-1}([0,x])))\phi^{\prime}(x)=\mu([0,x])\phi^{\prime}(x).

(B.13)

Using this to replace the integrand of (
B.11
) completes the proof.
∎

We can now give the second lemma needed to prove Proposition
B.1
.

Proposition B.4
.

Let us fix
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}),(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}^{\prime})\in\mathscr{Y}
. Let us assume that for any
s\in\text{supp}(\zeta)
, we have that
\bm{\Phi}(s)=\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}(s)
. Then

\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\mathcal{B}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}).

(B.14)

Proof.

To begin we note that our assumptions imply that
(\bm{\Phi}_{*})(\zeta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}_{*})(\zeta)
. In particular by applying Lemma
B.3
we see that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\zeta([0,q])\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(q))\Phi_{x}(dq)&=\int_{0}^{1}(\Phi_{x})_{*}(\zeta)([0,q])\xi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq,\\
\delta_{x}(s)&=\int_{\Phi_{x}(s)}^{1}(\Phi_{x})_{*}(\zeta)([0,s])ds.\end{split}

(B.15)

In particular, if we write
\bm{\delta}
and
\bm{\delta}^{\prime}
for the functions corresponding to
\bm{\Phi}
and
\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}
, then
\bm{\delta}=\bm{\delta}^{\prime}
. With these observations, the only term that is not clearly invariant under
\bm{\Phi}\mapsto\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}
is

\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\bm{K}_{x}^{D}(\bm{\delta}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds.

(B.16)

It is clear that the integrands coincide on
\text{supp}(\zeta)
, so let us consider the integral over some open connected component of
\text{supp}(\zeta)^{c}\cap(0,q_{*})
, say
(a,b)
. We note that
\bm{\Phi}(s)=\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}(s)
for
s\in\{a,b\}
. Let us write
\zeta_{0}=\zeta([0,a])=\zeta([0,b))>0
for simplicity. Then for
s\in(a,b)

\delta_{x}(s)=\delta_{x}(b)+\zeta_{0}(\Phi_{x}(b)-\Phi_{x}(s)),

(B.17)

so that recalling
|\Omega|\nabla\Lambda^{D}=\bm{K}^{D}

\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\int_{a}^{b}\bm{K}_{x}^{D}(\bm{\delta}(s))\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds=-\frac{1}{\zeta_{0}}\left(\Lambda^{D}(\bm{\delta}(b))-\Lambda^{D}(\bm{\delta}(a))\right).

(B.18)

As
\bm{\delta}=\bm{\delta}^{\prime}

Appendix C
Properties of Functions
\mathbb{K}^{D}
and
\Lambda^{D}

Let us fix a positive semi-definite matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
, where
\Omega
is some finite set, and recall the functions
\bm{K}^{D}
and
\Lambda^{D}
defined by (
1.21
) and (
1.22
). The purpose of this appendix will be to prove the analytic results we need about these functions above.

To begin, let use the notation
\odot
to denote the Hadamard product (i.e.
[A\odot B]_{ij}=A_{ij}B_{ij}
). By the Schur Product Theorem, this defines an operation that takes two positive definite matrices to another positive definite matrix. Moreover, for
n
-by-
n
positive semi-definite
A
and
B
, we have that

s_{1}(A\odot B)\leq s_{1}(A)s_{1}(B),\;\;\;\;s_{|\Omega|}(A\odot B)\geq s_{|\Omega|}(A)s_{|\Omega|}(B).

(C.1)

We will additionally employ the shorthand
A^{\odot 2}=A\odot A
. The following proposition comprises the facts about
\Lambda^{D}
and
\bm{K}^{D}
we will require.

Proposition C.1
.

\Lambda^{D}
is a strictly concave function on
(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, occurring as the Legendre transform of the function strictly concave function
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\log\det(D+\bm{u})
. The function
\bm{K}^{D}
is a well-defined homeomorphism of
(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
onto
\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:D+\bm{u}>0\}
. Moreover,
|\Omega|\nabla\Lambda^{D}(\bm{u})=\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})
and

\nabla\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})=-\bigg(\big((D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u}))^{-1}\big)^{\odot 2}\bigg)^{-1}.

(C.2)

Finally, both
\|\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})\|
and
-\Lambda^{D}(\bm{u})
grow to
\infty
as
\bm{u}\to\partial(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
.

Proof.

In the course of this proof we denote
\mathcal{X}=\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:D+\bm{u}>0\}
. We first compute that for
x,y\in\Omega
, we have that

\nabla_{x}\log\det(D+\bm{u})=[(D+\bm{u})^{-1}]_{xx},\;\;\;\nabla_{xy}^{2}\log\det(D+\bm{u})=-[(D+\bm{u})^{-1}]_{xy}^{2}.

(C.3)

In particular
\nabla^{2}\log\det(D+\bm{u})=-[(D+\bm{u})^{-1}]^{\odot 2}
, so for
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{X}
, we see that
\log\det(D+\bm{u})
is strictly concave. This and our computation of
\nabla\log\det(D+\bm{u})
makes it clear that
\Lambda^{D}(\bm{u})
coincides with the Legendre transform of
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\log\det(D+\bm{u})
when
\bm{K}^{D}
is well-defined.

We next show that
\log\det(D+\bm{u})
is essentially smooth in the sense of
[
57
]
: if
\bm{u}_{n}
is a sequence in
\mathcal{X}
, such that
\bm{u}_{n}
converges to some
\bm{u}\in\partial\mathcal{X}
, we have that
\|\nabla\log\det(D+\bm{u}_{n})\|\to\infty
. As all entries of
\nabla\log\det(D+\bm{u}_{n})
are positive, we may equivalently show that

\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\nabla_{x}\log\det(D+\bm{u}_{n})=\mathrm{tr}((D+\bm{u}_{n})^{-1})\to\infty.

(C.4)

For this note that
\partial\mathcal{X}
consists of non-invertible positive semi-definite matrices, so in particular,
\det(D+\bm{u}_{n})\to\det(D+\bm{u})=0
, so that
s_{|\Omega|}(D+\bm{u}_{n})\to 0
. In particular, as
\mathrm{tr}((D+\bm{u}_{n})^{-1})\geq s_{|\Omega|}(D+\bm{u}_{n})^{-1}
, we conclude that
\mathrm{tr}((D+\bm{u}_{n})^{-1})\to\infty
as desired. Note this also shows that
\log\det(D+\bm{u}_{n})\to-\infty
. In particular, this shows that for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the level set
\{\bm{v}:\log\det(D+\bm{v})\geq\alpha\}
is closed, so that
\log\det(D+\bm{u})
is a closed concave function.

Finally, as
\log\det(D+\bm{v})
is strictly concave, we see that
\log\det(D+\bm{v})
is a concave function of Legendre-type in the sense of
[
57
]
. In particular, we may apply Theorem 26.5 of
[
57
]
which shows a few things. First it shows that the Legendre and Fenchel transforms of
\log\det(D+\bm{u})
coincide. Next, it shows that
\bm{K}^{D}
is a well-defined homeomorphism from the domain of the Fenchel conjugate of
\log\det(D+\bm{u})
, i.e.

\mathcal{Y}:=\bigg\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}:\inf_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{v}(x)\bm{u}(x)-\log\det(D+\bm{v})\right)>-\infty\bigg\},

(C.5)

onto
\mathcal{X}
. On this domain, the Legendre transform of
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\log\det(D+\bm{v})
, which coincides with
\Lambda^{D}
, is itself an essentially smooth and closed strictly concave function. Finally, we have that
|\Omega|\nabla\Lambda^{D}(\bm{u})=\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})
and
|\Omega|\nabla^{2}\Lambda^{D}(\bm{u})=\nabla\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})=(\nabla^{2}\log(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})))^{-1}
. In particular, if we verify that
\mathcal{Y}=(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, all of the remaining claims immediately follow from these statements.

For this note that as
[(D+\bm{u})^{-1}]_{xx}>0
for
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{X}
and
x\in\Omega
, we have that
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
. On the other hand, for
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have that

\log\det(D+\bm{v})\leq\sum_{x\in\Omega}\log(s_{1}(D)+\bm{v}(x)),

(C.6)

so that

\inf_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{v}(x)\bm{u}(x)-\log(D+\bm{v})\geq\inf_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{x\in\Omega}(\bm{v}(x)\bm{u}(x)-\log(s_{1}(D)+\bm{v}(x)))>-\infty.

(C.7)

as the function
x\mapsto ax-\log(b+x)
is bounded below for any
a,b>0
. This shows that
(0,\infty)^{\Omega}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
, so
(0,\infty)^{\Omega}=\mathcal{Y}
, completing the proof.
∎

We will also need the following more technical result, which shows that the Jacobian of
\bm{K}^{D}
is dominated by its entries along the diagonal when one approaches a point on the boundary of its domain.

Theorem C.2
.

Fix
K>0
. Then there is small
\epsilon>0
such that for any
\bm{u}\in(0,K)^{\Omega}
and
x\in\Omega
, we have that
\nabla_{x}\bm{K}^{D}_{x}(\bm{u})\leq-\epsilon\bm{u}(x)^{-2}
. Moreover, for
y\in\Omega\setminus\{x\}
we have that
|\nabla_{x}\bm{K}^{D}_{y}(\bm{u})|\leq\epsilon^{-1}
.

Before proving Theorem
C.2
, we will first prove two technical results required for its proof. The first studies the somewhat obscure matrix transform which occurs in the Jacobian of
\bm{K}^{D}
.

Lemma C.3
.

Fix a symmetric matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
, such that
M_{xx}=0
for
x\in\Omega
. Additionally, fix small
\epsilon>0
. Then for
\bm{u}\in[\epsilon,\infty)^{\Omega}
, let
M(\bm{u})\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
be the matrix defined by
M(\bm{u})_{xy}=\bm{u}(x)^{-1/2}\bm{u}(y)^{-1/2}M_{xy}
. Consider the function
F:[\epsilon,\infty)^{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
given by

F_{M}(\bm{u})=\bigg(\big((I+M(\bm{u}))^{-1}\big)^{\odot 2}\bigg)^{-1}.

(C.8)

Then there is large
C>0
, such that for any
\bm{u}\in[\epsilon,\infty)^{\Omega}
, with
I+M(\bm{u})\geq\epsilon I
, we have for any
x,y\in\Omega
, with
x\neq y
that

|[F_{M}(\bm{u})]_{xy}|\leq C\bm{u}(x)^{-1}\bm{u}(y)^{-1}\text{ and }[F_{M}(\bm{u})]_{xx}\geq C^{-1}.

(C.9)

Proof.

In the course of this proof,
C>0
will be a large constant, allowed to change line by line, but always independent of
\bm{u}
. For convenience, we will write
F_{M}(\bm{u})=F(\bm{u})
and
n=|\Omega|
.

To begin we note that

[F(\bm{u})]_{xx}\geq s_{n}(F(\bm{u}))=(s_{1}((I+M(\bm{u}))^{-1})^{\odot 2})^{-1}\geq s_{n}(I+M(\bm{u}))^{2}\geq\epsilon^{2},

(C.10)

which demonstrates the second claim of (
C.9
). We now focus our attention on the first claim of (
C.9
). To begin, let us notate the terms of the square
\{x,y\}
-by-
\Omega\setminus\{x,y\}
block decomposition of
M
as

M=\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{t}\\
B&D\end{bmatrix}.

(C.11)

We will use the notations
A(\bm{u})
,
B(\bm{u})
, and
D(\bm{u})
to denote the corresponding submatrices of
M(\bm{u})
. We will now compute
(I+M(\bm{u}))^{-1}
in block form using the Schur complement formula, but for this, we will need to establish some notation. Namely let:

Z(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{u}(x)^{-1}&(\bm{u}(x)\bm{u}(y))^{-1/2}\\
(\bm{u}(x)\bm{u}(y))^{-1/2}&\bm{u}(y)^{-1}\\
\end{bmatrix},\;L(\bm{u})=A-B^{t}(I+D(\bm{u}))^{-1}B.

(C.12)

R(\bm{u})=(I+Z(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))\odot L(\bm{u}))^{-1},\;\;\;S(\bm{u})=-(I+D(\bm{u}))^{-1}B(\bm{u})R(\bm{u}),

(C.13)

T(\bm{u})=(I+D(\bm{u}))^{-1}+(I+D(\bm{u}))^{-1}B(\bm{u})R(\bm{u})B(\bm{u})^{t}(I+D(\bm{u}))^{-1}.

(C.14)

Then we have the following square
\{x,y\}
-by-
\Omega\setminus\{x,y\}
block decomposition:

(I+M(\bm{u}))^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}R(\bm{u})&S(\bm{u})^{t}\\
S(\bm{u})&T(\bm{u})\end{bmatrix}.

(C.15)

We now define the following
\{x,y\}
-by-
\{x,y\}
matrix:

Q(\bm{u})=R(\bm{u})^{\odot 2}-(S(\bm{u})^{\odot 2})^{t}(T(\bm{u})^{\odot 2})^{-1}(S(\bm{u})^{\odot 2}).

(C.16)

By employing Schur’s complement formula again, we see that the
\{x,y\}
-by-
\{x,y\}
block of
F(\bm{u})
is given by
Q(\bm{u})^{-1}
. From this we see that

[F(\bm{u})]_{xy}=\det(Q(\bm{u}))^{-1}[Q(\bm{u})]_{xy},

(C.17)

which shows that the first claim of (
C.9
) follows from the following two claims:

|[Q(\bm{u})]_{xy}|\leq C\bm{u}(x)^{-1}\bm{u}(y)^{-1},\;\;\;\det(Q(\bm{u}))\geq C^{-1}.

(C.18)

We first show the second claim, which is equivalent to showing that
\det(Q(\bm{u})^{-1})\leq C
. We observe the crude bound:
\det(Q(\bm{u})^{-1})\leq[F(\bm{u})]_{xx}[F(\bm{u})]_{yy}\leq s_{1}(F(\bm{u}))^{2}
. We compute that

s_{1}(F(\bm{u}))=s_{n}\bigg(\big((I+M(\bm{u}))^{-1}\big)^{\odot 2}\bigg)^{-1}\leq s_{n}\big((I+M(\bm{u}))^{-1}\big)^{-2}=s_{1}(I+M(\bm{u}))^{2}.

(C.19)

Combined with the trivial bound
s_{1}(I+M(\bm{u}))\leq n\mathrm{tr}(I+M(\bm{u}))=n
, establishes the second claim of (
C.18
). For the first claim, we observing the form (
C.16
), than it suffices to show the following claims

\begin{split}[R(\bm{u})]_{xy}&\leq C\bm{u}(x)^{-1/2}\bm{u}(y)^{-1/2},\\
[(S(\bm{u})^{\odot 2})^{t}(T(\bm{u})^{\odot 2})^{-1}(S(\bm{u})^{\odot 2})]_{xy}&\leq C\bm{u}(x)^{-1}\bm{u}(y)^{-1}.\end{split}

(C.20)

We first focus on the first claim. As
R(\bm{u})
is a submatrix of
(I+M(\bm{u}))^{-1}
, a similar argument shows that
\det(R(\bm{u}))\geq C^{-1}
. Moreover, we may explicitly write
R(\bm{u})^{-1}
as

R(\bm{u})^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1+\bm{u}(y)^{-1}L_{yy}(\bm{u})&\bm{u}(x)^{-1/2}\bm{u}(y)^{-1/2}L_{xy}(\bm{u})\\
\bm{u}(x)^{-1/2}\bm{u}(y)^{-1/2}L_{xy}(\bm{u})&1+\bm{u}(x)^{-1}L_{xx}(\bm{u})\end{bmatrix}.

(C.21)

It is clear that the entries of
L(\bm{u})
are uniformly bounded, which together with the bound
\det(R(\bm{u}))\geq C^{-1}
shows the first claim of (
C.20
).

To show the second claim, we will first study
(T(\bm{u})^{\odot 2})^{-1}
. For this, note that the second term in the definition of
T(\bm{u})
is positive definite, so that

s_{1}((T(\bm{u})^{\odot 2})^{-1})\leq s_{n}(T(\bm{u}))^{-2}\leq s_{n}((I+D(\bm{u}))^{-1})^{-2}=s_{1}(I+D(\bm{u}))^{2},

(C.22)

which we showed before was bounded. In particular, this shows that the entries of
(T(\bm{u})^{\odot 2})^{-1}
are uniformly bounded. We now note that if we define

W(\bm{u})=-(I+D(\bm{u}))^{-1}B(\bm{u}),

(C.23)

it is easily checked that
\|W(\bm{u})e_{x}\|\leq C\bm{u}(x)^{-1/2}
and
\|W(\bm{u})e_{y}\|\leq C\bm{u}(y)^{-1/2}
. On the other hand, we have that

S(\bm{u})e_{1}=[R(\bm{u})]_{xx}W(\bm{u})e_{x}+[R(\bm{u})]_{xy}W(\bm{u})e_{y}.

(C.24)

So using that
|[R(\bm{u})]_{xy}|\leq C\bm{u}(x)^{-1/2}\bm{u}(y)^{-1/2}
, we see that
\|S(\bm{u})e_{x}\|\leq C\bm{u}(x)^{-1/2}
. Similarly we see that
\|S(\bm{u})e_{y}\|\leq C\bm{u}(y)^{-1/2}
. Combining this with the boundedness of
(T(\bm{u})^{\odot 2})^{-1}
, we conclude the second claim of (
C.20
).
∎

We also need the following bound, which will allow us to control the size of diagonal terms of a positive definite matrix
A
, purely in terms of the behavior of the diagonal entries of
A^{-1}
and the off-diagonal entries of
A
.

Lemma C.4
.

Fix positive definite
A\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
. Then

\sum_{x\in\Omega}A_{xx}[A^{-1}]_{xx}\leq|\Omega|+\left(\sum_{x,y\in\Omega,x\neq y}A_{xy}^{2}\right)\left(|\Omega|\mathrm{tr}(A^{-1})\right)^{2}.

(C.25)

Proof.

It is sufficient to show that for all fixed
z\in\Omega

A_{zz}[A^{-1}]_{zz}\leq 1+\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega\setminus\{z\}}A_{xz}^{2}\right)\left(|\Omega|\mathrm{tr}(A^{-1})\right)^{2}.

(C.26)

Let us denote the terms in the block decomposition of
A
with square
\{z\}
and
\Omega\setminus\{z\}

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{zz}&A_{z}^{t}\\
A_{z}&A^{z}\end{bmatrix}.

(C.27)

By the Shur’s complement formula we have that
[A^{-1}]_{zz}=(A_{zz}-A_{z}^{t}(A^{z})^{-1}A_{z})^{-1}
, so using the formula
x(x-y)^{-1}=1+y(x-y)^{-1}
, we may write

A_{zz}[A^{-1}]_{zz}=1+A_{z}^{t}(A^{z})^{-1}A_{z}(A_{zz}-A_{z}^{t}(A^{z})^{-1}A_{z})^{-1}.

(C.28)

Using that
s_{1}((A^{z})^{-1})\leq s_{1}(A^{-1})\leq|\Omega|\mathrm{tr}(A^{-1})
, we see that

A_{z}^{t}(A^{z})^{-1}A_{z}\leq|\Omega|\mathrm{tr}(A^{-1})\sum_{x\in\Omega\setminus\{z\}}A_{xz}^{2}.

(C.29)

Combined with the bound
(A_{zz}-A_{z}^{t}(A^{z})^{-1}A_{z})^{-1}=[A^{-1}]_{zz}\leq|\Omega|\mathrm{tr}(A^{-1})
, we see that (
C.28
) yields the desired result.
∎

Proof of Theorem
C.2
.

For
\bm{u}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, we define a symmetric matrix
G_{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
, given for
z,w\in\Omega
by

[G_{\bm{u}}]_{zw}=(D_{zz}+K_{z}(\bm{u}))^{-1/2}(D_{ww}+K_{w}(\bm{u}))^{-1/2}D_{zw}(1-\delta_{zw}).

(C.30)

To explain the importance of this matrix, let us further define
G\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
by
[G]_{zw}=D_{zw}(1-\delta_{zw})
, and
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
by
\bm{d}(z)=D_{zz}
. Then in the notation of Lemma
C.3
, we have that
G_{\bm{u}}=F_{G}(\bm{d}+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u}))
. We also note that by Proposition
C.1
, and again in the notation of Lemma
C.3
,

\nabla_{z}K_{w}(\bm{u})=-(D_{zz}+K_{z}(\bm{u}))(D_{ww}+K_{w}(\bm{u}))F_{G}(\bm{d}+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})).

(C.31)

We seek to apply Lemma
C.3
to (
C.31
), so we begin by verifying its conditions. The first statement we will show is that for any
\bm{u}\in(0,K)^{\Omega}
, we that
\bm{d}+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})\in(K^{-1},\infty)^{\Omega}
.

For this take
z\in\Omega
, and let us notate the terms in the block decomposition of
D
with square
\{z\}
-by-
\Omega\setminus\{z\}
blocks as

D=\begin{bmatrix}D_{zz}&D_{z}^{t}\\
D_{z}&D^{z}\end{bmatrix}.

(C.32)

Similarly, let
\bm{K}^{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\setminus\{z\}}
denote the vector obtained from
\bm{K}
by omitting the entry
K_{z}
. We note by Schur’s complement formula that

([(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u}))^{-1}]_{zz})^{-1}=D_{zz}+K_{z}(\bm{u})-D_{z}^{t}(D^{z}+\bm{K}^{z}(\bm{u}))^{-1}D_{z},

(C.33)

This implies, in particular, that

\bm{u}(z)^{-1}=([(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u}))^{-1}]_{zz})^{-1}\leq D_{zz}+K_{z}(\bm{u}).

(C.34)

This implies the desired claim that
\bm{d}+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})\in(K^{-1},\infty)^{\Omega}
if
\bm{u}\in(0,K)^{\Omega}
. Next we will show that there is
\epsilon>0
such that
I+G_{\bm{u}}\geq\epsilon I
. We first note that as
I+G_{\bm{u}}
is congruent to
D+\bm{K}^{D}(u)
, it is positive definite, leaving us to provide a lower bound for
s_{|\Omega|}(I+G_{\bm{u}})
. We note that

s_{|\Omega|}(I+G_{\bm{u}})^{-1}=s_{1}((I+G_{\bm{u}})^{-1})\leq|\Omega|\mathrm{tr}((I+G_{\bm{u}})^{-1})\leq\sum_{z\in\Omega}\bm{u}(z)[D_{zz}+K_{z}(\bm{u})].

(C.35)

Recalling that
\bm{u}(z)=[(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u}))^{-1}]_{zz}
, we see by Lemma
C.4
, that the right-hand side is further less than

|\Omega|^{2}\mathrm{tr}((D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u}))^{-1})^{2}\sum_{z,w\in\Omega,z\neq w}D_{zw}^{2}\leq|\Omega|^{2}K^{2}\sum_{z,w\in\Omega,z\neq w}D_{zw}^{2}.

(C.36)

In particular, this shows that

s_{|\Omega|}(I+G_{\bm{u}})\geq\left(|\Omega|^{2}K^{2}\sum_{z,w\in\Omega,z\neq w}D_{zw}^{2}\right)^{-1}.

(C.37)

Thus we may now apply Lemma
C.3
to conclude there is large
C>0
such that for
\bm{u}\in(0,K)^{\Omega}
, we have that

|[F_{G}(\bm{d}+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u}))]_{xy}|\leq C(D_{xx}+K^{D}_{x}(\bm{u}))^{-1}(D_{yy}+K^{D}_{y}(\bm{u}))^{-1},\;\;\;\;[F_{G}(\bm{d}+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u}))]_{xx}\geq C^{-1}.

(C.38)

These statements, combined with (
C.31
) and (
C.34
), yield the desired claims.
∎
