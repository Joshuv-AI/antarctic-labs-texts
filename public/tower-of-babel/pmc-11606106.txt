Bayesian geo-additive model to analyze spatial pattern and determinants of maternal mortality in Ethiopia
Authors: Yitagesu Eshetu, Tigist Getachew
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsMaternal mortality is defined as the death of a woman from any cause associated to or made worse by her pregnancy, either during her pregnancy or within 42 days of the pregnancy's termination, regardless of the length of the pregnancy or its location. The objective of this study is to determine the factors influencing maternal mortality as well as to examine the regional distribution of maternal deaths in Ethiopia.MethodThis study was conducted in Ethiopia and the data was basically secondary which is obtained from 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health survey (EDHS). The Bayesian Geo-additive regression model is used to identify the major risk factors and spatial effects (spatial pattern) on maternal death in Ethiopia.ResultPregnancy-related problems or childbirth were the cause of death for 1.43% of the 10,009 women in the research, whose ages ranged from 15 to 49. In contrast to the semi-parametric and generalized linear models, the Bayesian Geo-additive regression model is based on the DIC and better fits the data. According to the Bayesian Geo-additive regression model's results, maternal death is significantly affected by the place of delivery, the number of prenatal care visits, marital status, wealth index, mother's age and the number of birth orders. The Afar, Somali, Benishangul Gumuz, and Gambela regions have higher rates of maternal death, according to evidence of geographic variation in a model.ConclusionThe findings of the study revealed that maternal mortality is influenced by numerous social, demographic, and geographic variables. Geographic variations exist in the patterns of maternal mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20812-2.Keywords: Bayesian Geo-additive regression model, Maternal death, Spatial pattern

Introduction

Maternal death refers to the death of a woman during pregnancy or within 42 days after abortion, irrespective of the duration and place of the pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management but not from accidental or unintentional causes (World Health Organisation [27].

An estimated 585, 000 women worldwide die suddenly every year as a result of pregnancy and childbirth problems. [26]. Hemorrhage, infection, unsafe abortion, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and obstructed labor are the five major problems that account for more than 70% of all maternal deaths. Most maternal deaths (61%) happen during the following pregnancy phase, and over half of them happen the day after delivery [22].

Almost 99% of maternal deaths were reported from the developing regions that showed the largest discrepancy between developed and developing countries [25]. Among the developing countries, sub-Saharan Africa alone accounts for approximately 66% followed by Southern Asia, 22% of maternal deaths. Ethiopia is also categorized under the countries with high maternal mortality [25].

Using a variety of statistical models, different studies have examined into the major risk factors for maternal mortality in Ethiopia. For instance, [20] utilized multivariable logistic regression to identify these risk factors, while another study by [24] employed multilevel logistic regression. However, non-spatial statistical approaches and parametric estimate techniques were the primary methods of previous studies of maternal death risk variables. It's possible that these methods miss the nonlinear linkages and geographic diversity connected to maternal mortality. The authors of the current study fill in these gaps by taking into consideration the nonlinear effects of metric variables and spatial factors when assessing the spatial patterns and drivers of maternal death. We conducted this by employing a Geo-additive regression model within a Bayesian framework.

Methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Ethiopia. Ethiopia is strategically located in the northeastern part of Africa popularly known as “the Horn of Africa". It shares a boundary with the North and South Sudan on the west, Somalia and Djibouti on the East, Eritrea on the North and northwest, and Kenya on the South. Ethiopia is officially known as the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia, is a landlocked country located in the Horn of Africa. It is the second-most populous nation in Africa, with over 105,350,020 populations [5] and the tenth-largest by area, occupying 1,100,000k2m.

Source of data

The data used for this study was the 2016 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (2016 EDHS). The data was implemented by the Central Statistical Agency (CSA) at the request of the Ministry of Health (MoH) and Data collection took place from January 18, 2016, to June 27, 2016, with national representative of 18,008 households selected based on a nationally representative sample that provides estimates at the national and regional levels and for urban and rural areas. The Woman’s Questionnaire from 15,683 was used to collect information from all women age 15–49 from the selected households. Since the data of EDHS has many missed cases across some variables, after clearing those missing values, a total of sample of 10,009 women between the ages of 15–49 years in Ethiopia was included in this study.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this study was maternal death at a reproductive age and it is a dichotomous (binary) variable, which is coded as zero if death has not occurred with related pregnancy and 1 if death has occurred due to pregnancy.

Statistical analysis

Spatial data analysis

The main concern of spatial statistics is to account for observation correlational effects arising from the geographic configuration of data [11]. The geographical configurations of maternal death prevalence were assessed to investigate the presence of spatial autocorrelation in the distribution of the data. The essence of spatial analysis is that “space matters”, i.e., what happens in one region is related to what happens in neighboring regions. This has been made more precise in what [23] refers to as the First Law of Geography. One way to approach this is via the notion of spatial autocorrelation [1]. Therefore, the spatial distribution of childhood maternal mortality in Ethiopia was determined by the geographic relationships among them.

Global measures of spatial autocorrelation

Examining the overall spatial autocorrelation of the data (against Ho: no spatial autocorrelation) as well as the degree and direction (positive or negative) of any spatial autocorrelation are the main goals of global spatial autocorrelation tests [2]. The two most widely used global measures of spatial autocorrelation are Geary's c [9] and Moran's I [17].

Spatial weights matrix

To assess the nature and degree of spatial autocorrelation, it is necessary to represent the spatial arrangement of observations to get a sense of how close or distant they are apart from each other. To express the degree of proximity between observations in space we may attribute a value of one if the observations are nearby (neighbors) and zero otherwise. There are different other options for defining these weights, they may be based on neighborhood which has common boundary [15]. In these cases, pairs of observations might be defined as neighborhoods as a measure of the degree of proximity. In this research, we were adopting the binary adjacency weights such that ωij = 1 if sites i and j are neighbor’s and zero otherwise.

Tests of spatial autocorrelation

The two most commonly used measures for spatial autocorrelation are Moran’s I and Geary’s C statistics. These tests indicate the degree of spatial association as reflected in the data set as a whole [2]. Test for spatial autocorrelation is designed to quantify the extent of clustering and to allow for statistical inference. The null hypothesis (under the normality and independence assumptions) is given by: H0: No spatial autocorrelation (H0: ρ = 0). Under the alternative hypothesis (H1: ρ ≠ 0) of spatial autocorrelation (spatial dependence), the interest focuses on instances where large values are systematically surrounded by other large values, or where small values are surrounded by small values.

Advantages of bayesian methods

From the complex nature of hierarchical models to the knowledge required to simulate from the posterior distribution, Bayesian methods can appear daunting. However, Bayesian methods also offer many advantages over classical inference. This is especially true for spatial models. Spatial data are by nature autocorrelated. To account for autocorrelation, it is necessary to use some sort of hierarchical structure. Bayesian models are inherently hierarchical and thus accounting for autocorrelation within the Bayesian framework is straightforward. This includes the use of the CAR models as MRF prior distributions for Bayesian inference. The complicated structure of hierarchical models often results in the posterior being intractable, but MCMC sampling techniques overcome this problem [4].

Advantages of bayesian Geo-Additive model

There are many potential advantages of this approach over classical approaches like regression models with fixed or random provinces effects; or standard 2-level multilevel modeling with unstructured spatial effects [10]. In the classical models, it is assumed that the random components at the contextual level (a region in our case) are mutually independent. In practice, these approaches specify correlated random effects [13], which are contrary to that assumption.

Demographic and Health Survey data are based on a random sample of the region which, in turn, introduces a structured component. Such a component allows us to borrow strength from neighbors to cope with the posterior uncertainty of the region effect and obtain estimates for areas that may have inadequate sample sizes or are not represented in the sample. In an attempt to highlight the advantages of our approach in a spatial context and examine the potential bias incurred when ignoring the dependence between aggregated spatial areas, we fitted three models with and without the structured and random components in this study. Controlling for important risk factors such as geographical location (spatial autocorrelation) arising from environment impact on health gives statistically robust estimates of prevalence.

Bayesian Geo-additive regression model for binary spatial data

A common way to build regression models extending the classical linear model for Gaussian responses to more general situations such as binary responses are generalized linear models. While being flexible in terms of the supported response distributions, generalized linear models obey rather strong assumptions considering the linearity of the influence of covariates and the independence of the observations.

A model with predictors that contain spatial effect and non-linear effect of metrical covariate called Geo-additive regression model was propose [14]. Geo-additive model is one class of structural additive regression (STAR) model which based on the framework of Bayesian generalized linear models [12].

Model building

In this study the following two models model 1 which represent fixed effect of all covariate and model 2 which represent fixed effect of categorical covariate plus non-linear effect of continuous covariate without including spatial effect were fitted together with model3 (geo-additive regression model) for response variable (Survival status of mother) conditionally on (region), with a logit link function given with predictors increasing in complexity in order to show the appropriateness of geo-additive model over the rest of the models.

Selection of prior

In a Bayesian approach parameters are considered random variables and have to be supplemented with appropriate prior assumptions. Diffuse priors are the suitable alternative for fixed effects parameters if there is no any prior knowledge [6] (Echavarria L (2004).

Posterior inference

The type of inferential concept used for estimation of regression parameters in this study was full Bayesian inference via Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). Bayesian inference is based on posterior distributions and is carried out using MCMC simulation techniques so that samples are drawn from full conditionals of single parameters or block parameters given the rest.

Model comparison

In this study in order to select the best model among several fitted model in this study, Deviance information criterion (DIC) was used. In a Bayesian frame work the Deviance Information Criterion is a widely used statistic for comparing models. The deviance information criterion.

(DIC) is a hierarchical modeling generalization of the AIC (Akaike information criterion) and BIC (Bayesian information criterion).

Model diagnostic

Model diagnosis was performed based on MCMC post-estimation diagnosis, to examine the convergence of MCMC. Among several ways of test of convergence, the most popular and straight forward convergence assessment methods have been used for this study. The following two methods were more likely considered for this study.

Trace plots

Iteration numbers on -axis and parameter value on -axis are commonly used to assess convergence. If the plot looks like a horizontal band, with no long upward or downward trends, then we have evidence that the chain has converged. The posterior distribution is obtained by sampling toward the end of this longer iteration sequence when the posterior distribution is stationary, as determined by an examination of trace plots of the iteration history of selected model quantities.

Missing data treatment

Missing data is defined as the data value that is not stored for a variable in the observation of interest. Missing values are a common issue in a lot of practical data situations in DHS. Commonly, there are three types of missing data according to the mechanisms of missingness. These are missing completely at random (MCAR), missing at random (MAR) and missing not at random (MNAR) [16]. In these cases, we used the best possible method of handling the missing data to prevent the problem by well planning in the study and collected the data carefully. In this study, we used multiple imputations missing data handling mechanisms.

The metrical covariates of mother age and number of birth orders were found to significantly affect mother survival status; this is because their corresponding credible interval has not included zero. The fixed effect results of this model show that place of delivery, number of antenatal care visits, wealth index of women, and mother's marital status were found to have a significant effect on maternal death. As their corresponding credible interval contained zero, maternal death was not significantly affected by exposure to mass media, use of contraceptives, mother's educational level, or place of residence, in contrast to this supply of drinkable water.

The health facility's delivery categories had a coefficient value of OR = exp (−0.4447) = 0.64 with 95% CI (0.42, 0.94). mothers who gave birth at a medical facility had a 36% reduced chance of dying than moms who gave birth at home, according to this interpretation. In relation to the number of prenatal care visits, the coefficient value for categories 1–3 was OR = exp (−2.3707) = 0.093 with 95% CI (0.034,0.281). This indicates that moms who attended 1–3 prenatal care visits had a 9.3% reduced chance of dying than mothers who did not. Likewise, individuals who attended four or more prenatal care visits had a 24% lower chance of dying than those who did not (OR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.098, 0.614).

According to the wealth index's coefficients for the rich category (OR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.102, 0.614), rich women had a 25% lower chance of dying during pregnancy than poor women. Another finding of this study showed that marital status of mother has a significant contribution to maternal death. The odds of maternal death for divorced/widowed women were 2.86 times (OR = 2.86, 95%CI: 1.26, 7.93) and separated/no longer living together women was 5.52 times (OR = 5.52, 95%CI: 1.57, 18.82) more likely than the odds of not married women respectively.

Both the structural and un-structural spatial effects were found to have a significant impact on maternal death in this study; therefore, the corresponding 95% credible intervals for the structured and un-structured spatial effects, which do not include zero, are (0.023, 3.7446) and (0.01, 1.5022), respectively. The structured geographically correlated effect (0.9207) considerably exceeds the unstructured effect (0.2886) based on the posterior mean estimate, suggesting that our data have a larger spatial dependence.

Discussion

Applying exploratory spatial data analysis techniques such as Moran I and Geary'c, the existence of geographical dependency was examined. Given that there was a spatial correlation between maternal deaths in Ethiopia's neighboring regions, this result provides sufficient evidence. A previous study by [21] in the western part of Uganda provided support for this claim.

The mother's delivery location was found to have an impact on her risk of pregnancy-related death; mothers who gave birth at home had a higher risk than mothers who delivered their babies in a hospital. However, it indicates that the likelihood of a mother dying at a hospital was (OR = 0.64), which is a significant decrease from the probability of a mother dying at home. This finding aligns with the previous study conducted by [18].

The results of our study, which were consistent with [3] previous study, showed that mothers with higher wealth indexes had a lower risk of maternal death than mothers with lower wealth indexes, or low incomes.

Also, compared to mothers who had not had any prenatal care visits, we found that pregnancy-related deaths were less common among mothers who had visited 1–3 and 4 + times. Another way to look at this would be to say that the odds of pregnancy-related death for mothers who had 1–3 prenatal visits were 0.093 times lower than the odds for mothers who had 4 or more prenatal visits, and the odds for mothers who had 4 or more prenatal visits were 0.24 times lower than the odds for mothers who had not had any prenatal visits. Also, a previous investigation by [20] almost supported this finding.

Another important risk factor for pregnancy-related mortality was the mother's marital status. This is why we found that women who were no longer living together, separated, or divorced had a significant association with pregnancy-related deaths. This means that these women had a higher risk of dying from pregnancy than did single women. Previous research [7] also confirmed this finding.

The mother's age has a continuous negative impact on pregnancy-related deaths when the mother is younger than 20 and between the ages of 30–39. This means that the mother's age interval (20–29) and the age range (30–39) are associated with a higher risk of maternal death, or pregnancy-related deaths. [19] found that this outcome shared the same premise as his previous study.

There is a small correlation between a mother's birth order and pregnancy-related deaths; specifically, mothers with birth orders greater than five have been associated to a higher risk of maternal death than mothers with birth orders lower. This outcome was also in line with previous studies [8].

Limitations of the study

The Ethiopian Demographic Health Survey (EDHS) of 2016 provided the data used in this study, although it was incomplete because some women were not eligible for certain covariates and the factors that may be related to maternal mortality were not fully covered by the EDHS. Another disadvantage of this study is that the EDHS data is not sampled by grouping the region/country by the lowest administrative units, making it unable to create risk maps at the lowest administrative units.

Conclusions

Using data from the 2016 EDHS, the study's main goal was to identify regional patterns and the impact of socioeconomic, bio-demographic, and health determinants on maternal death. This can be achieved by applying three different fully Bayesian techniques, namely the Bayesian Generalized linear model, the Bayesian semi parametric regression model, and the Bayesian Geo-additive regression model, which are all based on Markov chain Monte Carlo techniques and improve complexity from simple to more sophisticated models.

The idea that maternal mortality is a fundamental public health concern in the nation with regional variation is supported by our data. The findings of this research revealed that maternal mortality is influenced by intricate social, demographic, and geographic factors. The hot spot regions were found after examining the spatial pattern of maternal deaths in Ethiopia. This pattern indicates that the regions of Benishangul Gumuz, Gambela, Somalia, and Afar had the highest risk of maternal deaths. In order to control the major factors that lead to maternal deaths, the government and other relevant bodies should raise awareness about the need of giving birth in a medical facility rather than at home, the use of prenatal care follow-up, and what can be done to improve the mother's economic standing.
