Color stability of polyetheretherketone and graphene-reinforced polymethyl methacrylate after in-office bleaching
Authors: Ali Can Bulut, Gokce Soganci Unsal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis in vitro study evaluated the color stability of polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and graphene-reinforced polymethyl methacrylate (G-CAM) following direct exposure to a high-concentration in-office bleaching gel.MethodsThirty square-shaped specimens (10 × 10 × 3 mm) were prepared: 15 PEEK (BioHPP, Bredent, Germany) in shade A2 and 15 G-CAM (Graphenano Dental, Spain) in shade A2, with baseline color coordinates recorded using a spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade Advance 4.0, Vita Zahnfabrik, Germany). All specimens were surface-ground, ultrasonically cleaned, and stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h prior to measurements. A 40% hydrogen peroxide bleaching gel (Opalescence Boost, Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA) was applied for 20 min and repeated after a one-week interval, with color measurements obtained after each session. Color differences were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula (ΔE₀₀). The observed CIELAB coordinates (L*, a*, b*) were analyzed using a 2 × 3 mixed-design ANOVA (material × bleaching stage), whereas the derived ΔE₀₀ contrasts were evaluated secondarily, with Mann–Whitney U tests used as supplementary between-material analyses (α = 0.05).ResultsA significant material × time interaction was found for all CIELAB coordinates (L*, a*, b*; all p < .001), indicating that the two materials followed distinct temporal patterns of color change. After the first bleaching session, G-CAM exhibited a markedly higher color change (ΔE₀₀ = 7.97 ± 3.12) compared with PEEK (ΔE₀₀ = 0.55 ± 0.23). Between the first and second sessions, G-CAM continued to show a higher incremental color change (ΔE₀₀ = 7.02 ± 2.61) than PEEK (ΔE₀₀ = 3.66 ± 0.26). When total color change from baseline to the second bleaching session was evaluated, G-CAM demonstrated a lower ΔE₀₀ value (1.81 ± 1.04) compared with PEEK (3.86 ± 0.34).ConclusionPEEK and G-CAM exhibited markedly different color-stability behaviors under high-concentration in-office bleaching. Under the tested direct-exposure conditions, the mean baseline-referenced ΔE₀₀ of G-CAM exceeded the 1.8 acceptability threshold after the first session and was approximately at the threshold after the second session (1.81 ± 1.04), whereas that of PEEK remained below the threshold after the first session and exceeded it after repeated exposure. Under these in vitro conditions, the two materials behaved differently, but the results require clinical confirmation and should not be interpreted as direct treatment guidance. These findings are specific to the direct-exposure protocol used and should not be extrapolated to lower-concentration home-bleaching systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-09488-9.Keywords: Bleaching, Color stability, Graphene-reinforced PMMA, Hydrogen peroxide, PEEK

Introduction

Polymers manufactured through advances in computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technology are being increasingly utilized alongside ceramics, owing to advantages such as reduced polymerization shrinkage, low porosity, and high dimensional accuracy [1]. These materials, which are fabricated using subtractive milling techniques, exhibit superior mechanical and physical properties — including flexural strength, wear resistance, and reduced porosity — compared with conventionally processed resin-based materials [2–4].

Color stability is a critical property, as it directly influences esthetics and patient satisfaction. The inherent susceptibility of polymer-based restorations — such as provisional crowns and denture base materials — to discoloration over time has increased interest in comparing the color behavior of different materials under aging processes and simulated clinical conditions [2–6]. In this context, high-performance polymers — such as polyetheretherketone (PEEK) — and next-generation nanocomposites, including graphene-reinforced polymethyl methacrylate (G-CAM), have emerged as promising CAD/CAM restorative materials [1, 3–5].

PEEK is a metal-free, polyaromatic, semicrystalline thermoplastic. Owing to its high fatigue resistance, bone-like elastic modulus, low plaque affinity, and excellent chemical stability, PEEK has been increasingly utilized in dental applications such as implant abutments, removable partial denture frameworks, and fixed dental prostheses, including bridges and crowns [7–13]. The opaque, grayish-beige color of PEEK constitutes an esthetic limitation; therefore, it is commonly veneered with composite resin or ceramic materials to achieve a natural tooth appearance. When veneering is not performed, the intrinsic color characteristics of the PEEK surface become clinically relevant [3]. PEEK exhibits considerably lower water absorption and solubility compared with composite resins and PMMA-based materials [3, 6]. Previous studies have reported that color alteration in PEEK is markedly lower than that observed in conventional acrylic resins; specifically, after ultraviolet (UV) exposure and thermal cycling, PEEK demonstrated smaller color changes than PMMA under identical conditions [7, 8]. In another study, PEEK showed the lowest degree of color change among the tested denture base materials (ΔE ≈ 2–3), whereas PMMA resins exhibited ΔE values ranging from 5 to 8 [2]. These findings suggest that the hydrophobic and chemically inert surface characteristics of PEEK confer increased resistance to staining and moisture absorption. Nevertheless, other studies have reported perceptible color changes and reduced translucency in PEEK materials under artificial aging conditions, although these alterations remained lower than those observed in conventional resin-based materials [3].

In recent years, nanotechnology has enabled the enhancement of polymeric dental materials through the incorporation of innovative filler systems. Graphene is a carbon-based nanomaterial with a single-atom thickness and has attracted considerable interest due to its exceptional mechanical strength, large surface area, and antimicrobial potential [12, 13]. Graphene and its oxide derivatives have been incorporated into PMMA-based systems to improve mechanical durability, wear resistance, and color stability [13].

Accordingly, graphene-reinforced PMMA blocks or discs (G-CAM) have been introduced as CAD/CAM-processable restorative materials. Manufacturers report that the incorporation of the graphene phase imparts superior mechanical, physicochemical, and biological properties to PMMA [12, 14]. It has been suggested that the integration of graphene into a pre-polymerized PMMA matrix results in a stronger and more durable polymeric structure in G-CAM materials [15–17]. G-CAM is a CAD/CAM block composed of a graphene-reinforced PMMA matrix that can be used directly as a definitive restorative material [18, 19]. G-CAM discs are manufactured in various VITA shades and provide esthetics comparable to natural tooth structure due to their high translucency [20]. However, clinical data regarding the color stability of this novel material remain limited. It has been suggested that the incorporation of graphene may alter the water absorption behavior and optical properties of the resin matrix, thereby influencing color stability. An in vitro study demonstrated that G-CAM exhibited less color change than conventional provisional materials after prolonged exposure to staining solutions [13].

Nevertheless, the existing literature has primarily focused on staining and artificial aging tests [21], and the behavior of G-CAM under bleaching conditions has not yet been sufficiently investigated. Tooth bleaching is a widely performed procedure to meet increasing esthetic demands. In-office bleaching using high-concentration hydrogen peroxide gels can produce pronounced whitening effects within a short time [22]. While the effects of bleaching agents on dental hard tissues are well documented, their impact on restorative materials continues to be an area of active investigation.

The literature reports that bleaching procedures generally result in limited color changes in composite resins, typically below the threshold of clinical perceptibility [22–25]. However, data regarding post-bleaching color behavior remain scarce for recently introduced CAD/CAM-fabricated esthetic materials. Moreover, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, no studies have yet evaluated the effects of bleaching agents on high-performance polymeric materials such as G-CAM and PEEK. This represents a clinically relevant knowledge gap, as patients with such restorations may undergo bleaching procedures without evidence-based guidance on the optical response of these materials. Color changes in restorative materials are objectively assessed using the parameters of the CIE L*a*b* color space. In contemporary literature, the CIEDE2000 color difference formula (ΔE₀₀) is the most widely accepted system for evaluating clinically perceptible and acceptable color differences [20–26].

According to established threshold values for ΔE₀₀, color differences below 0.8 are considered imperceptible, whereas values exceeding 1.8 are regarded as clinically unacceptable [19–23]. These thresholds serve as reference values for the clinical evaluation of color stability following aging or bleaching procedures. It should be noted that ΔE threshold values may vary depending on lighting conditions and the intrinsic color of the tested samples [23–26].

Bleaching agents may interact not only with dental hard tissues but also with existing restorative materials, potentially altering their color and surface properties [27]. Previous in vitro studies on CAD/CAM restorative materials have also applied high-concentration in-office hydrogen peroxide protocols to evaluate direct material response after bleaching exposure [28–30]. Accordingly, the present study was designed as a standardized direct-exposure model representing possible restorative-surface contact with an in-office bleaching gel, rather than as a simulation of tray-based home bleaching.

Within this standardized direct-exposure model, the present in vitro study aimed to comparatively evaluate the color change of PEEK and G-CAM after repeated application of a 40% hydrogen peroxide gel. The null hypotheses were that there would be no material × bleaching-stage interaction and no effect of bleaching stage on the observed CIELAB coordinates (L*, a*, b*) of the two materials. As a secondary, clinically oriented objective, the derived ΔE₀₀ values were interpreted relative to the 50:50% perceptibility (ΔE₀₀ = 0.8) and acceptability (ΔE₀₀ = 1.8) thresholds.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Square-shaped specimens (10 × 10 × 3 mm) were prepared from PEEK (BioHPP, Bredent, Germany) and G-CAM (Graphenano Dental, Spain) CAD/CAM blanks. A total of 30 specimens were fabricated, with 15 specimens allocated to each material group. Both materials were used in shade A2; baseline L*, a*, b* coordinates were recorded using a spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade Advance 4.0, Vita Zahnfabrik, Germany). The specimens were sectioned under water cooling using a low-speed precision cutting device (Metkon Micracut 201, Bursa, Turkey).

Following fabrication, the flat surfaces of all specimens were standardized by sequential grinding under water using 600-, 800-, and 1200-grit silicon carbide abrasive papers, simulating clinically relevant surface finishing. Subsequently, all specimens were ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 10 min using an ultrasonic cleaner (CD 4820, Istanbul, Turkey). Prior to color measurements, the specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h to ensure complete hydration.

Sample size calculation

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on a repeated-measures ANOVA design (within-between interaction: 2 groups × 3 time points), a significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of 0.90, and an assumed effect size of f = 0.40—adopted from a previous study evaluating color change of bleached CAD/CAM materials using repeated-measures ANOVA and ΔE₀₀ measurements [29]—the minimum required sample size was calculated as 14 specimens per group. To compensate for potential data loss, the sample size was set at 15 specimens per group (30 in total). The same 2 × 3 (material × bleaching-stage) factorial structure adopted for this calculation was retained in the final primary analysis, which modeled the observed CIELAB coordinates; ΔE₀₀, on which the benchmark effect size was based, was retained as a derived secondary outcome. Because the reference study did not report coordinate-specific interaction effect sizes, the ΔE₀₀-based effect size was used as a pragmatic approximation for the 2 × 3 longitudinal design.

Color measurement

Baseline L*, a*, and b* color coordinates of each specimen were measured using a spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade Advance 4.0, Vita Zahnfabrik, Germany) under standardized conditions (controlled ambient light, room temperature 23 ± 1 °C). Measurements were performed according to the CIE L*a*b* color space on a white background, as this condition has been shown to provide more reproducible readings for opaque and semi-translucent materials and is consistent with previously reported measurement protocols [31]. The probe tip was positioned perpendicular (90°) to the specimen surface. For each specimen, three measurements were obtained from the central region, and the mean values were recorded.

Baseline color measurements were designated as L₀, a₀, and b₀. The CIE L*a*b* color space was defined relative to the CIE standard illuminant D65 and a 10° standard observer, where L* represents lightness (0–100), a* denotes the red–green axis, and b* indicates the yellow–blue axis. Prior to each measurement session, the spectrophotometer was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and each specimen was measured three times, with the mean value used for statistical analysis [31].

Bleaching procedure

The specimens were subjected to a standardized in-office bleaching protocol designed to assess the intrinsic material response to a high-concentration peroxide gel under controlled in vitro conditions. A 40% hydrogen peroxide gel (Opalescence Boost, Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA) was applied to the upper surface of each specimen as an approximately 1-mm-thick layer and left for 20 min in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, without light activation. The gel was applied once per session without refreshing during the application period. At the end of the application period, the specimens were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water and gently cleaned with a soft brush. Between bleaching sessions, specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C to maintain hydration. For secondary color measurements, the specimens were dried and measured again (L₁, a₁, b₁). One week later, the same bleaching protocol was reapplied identically. After gel removal, the specimens were rinsed, gently brushed, and subjected to a third color measurement (L₂, a₂, b₂). All measurements were performed by the same operator under identical conditions.

Color difference calculations

To quantitatively assess the color changes induced by the bleaching procedures, the CIEDE2000 (ΔE₀₀) formula was applied using the standard parametric factors (kL = kC = kH = 1) under illuminant D65 and a 10° standard observer [32]. Three color difference values were calculated for each specimen:

ΔE₀₀ (Baseline–1st): color difference between baseline and post-first bleaching measurements.

ΔE₀₀ (Baseline–1st): color difference between baseline and post-first bleaching measurements.

ΔE₀₀ (1st–2nd): incremental color difference between first and second bleaching sessions.

ΔE₀₀ (1st–2nd): incremental color difference between first and second bleaching sessions.

ΔE₀₀ (Baseline–2nd): total color difference from baseline to post-second bleaching measurements.

ΔE₀₀ (Baseline–2nd): total color difference from baseline to post-second bleaching measurements.

ΔE₀₀ values were calculated using an established online calculator (https://www.colormine.org/delta-e-calculator/Cie2000.) [33], with standard parametric weighting factors applied consistently across all calculations. For clinical interpretation, the 50:50% perceptibility threshold (PT₀₀ = 0.8) and the 50:50% acceptability threshold (AT₀₀ = 1.8) were adopted as reference values [19].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with the significance level set at α = 0.05. The primary inferential analysis modeled the observed CIELAB color coordinates (L*, a*, b*) across the three measurement points (baseline, after the first bleaching session, and after the second bleaching session). For each coordinate, a 2 × 3 mixed-design analysis of variance was fitted, with material (PEEK vs. G-CAM) as the between-subjects factor and bleaching stage (time) as the within-subjects factor, together with their interaction. Normality was assessed with Shapiro–Wilk tests and Q–Q plots of the model residuals and within-subject difference scores, and homogeneity of between-material variance with the median-based Levene (Brown–Forsythe) test. Sphericity was evaluated with Mauchly’s test; the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied to the within-subjects and interaction terms of L*, for which sphericity was violated, whereas uncorrected degrees of freedom were retained for a* and b*. Partial eta squared (ηp²) was reported as the effect-size estimate and, because three coordinates were analyzed, the Holm–Bonferroni procedure was applied to the family of three material × time interaction p-values (one per coordinate) to control the family-wise error rate. A significant material × time interaction was decomposed into simple main effects: the effect of time within each material (pairwise comparisons among the three stages with Bonferroni adjustment and Hedges’ gₐv) and the effect of material at each stage (Welch’s t test). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

A mixed-design ANOVA on the observed coordinates showed a significant material × time interaction for every coordinate (L*: F(1.45, 40.62) = 59.3; a*: F(2, 56) = 222.6; b*: F(2, 56) = 142.5; all p < .001; ηp² = 0.68, 0.89, and 0.84, respectively), together with significant main effects of time (all p < .001) (Table 4). This interaction confirms that the magnitude and direction of color change differed between materials across bleaching stages. For PEEK, the overall color difference after the first session remained below the perceptibility threshold; L* did not change significantly (adjusted p = 1.000), although statistically significant shifts occurred in a* (adjusted p = .021) and b* (adjusted p < .001). The principal coordinate changes occurred after the second session, with decreases in L* and b* and a shift toward more negative a* values (all adjusted p < .001; signed baseline-to-second Hedges’ gₐv = 6.76, 11.25, and 8.38 for L*, a*, and b*, respectively). G-CAM changed markedly after the first session—its lightness increasing from 11.71 ± 2.30 to 23.33 ± 3.83 (signed Hedges’ gₐv = −3.47)—and then reverted toward baseline after the second session (L* = 13.68 ± 1.89). The cumulative baseline-to-second change remained significant for L* (adjusted p = .024; signed Hedges’ gₐv = −0.88) but was non-significant for a* and b* (signed Hedges’ gₐv = −0.37 and − 0.41, respectively). The large main effect of material on the absolute coordinates (all p < .001) reflected differences in the measured baseline colors rather than bleaching-induced change and was not the outcome of interest. Although both materials were manufacturer-designated as shade A2, their baseline L*, a*, and b* coordinates differed significantly (all p < .001); therefore, PEEK and G-CAM were not optically equivalent at baseline.

As a derived clinical outcome, the ΔE₀₀ color differences mirrored these coordinate findings. After the first bleaching session, G-CAM exhibited a markedly higher color change (7.97 ± 3.12; 95% CI: 6.24–9.70) compared with PEEK (0.55 ± 0.23; 95% CI: 0.42–0.68), and this difference was statistically significant (p < .001) (Table 6). Between the first and second bleaching sessions, G-CAM continued to show a higher incremental color change (7.02 ± 2.61; 95% CI: 5.57–8.46) than PEEK (3.66 ± 0.26; 95% CI: 3.51–3.80) (p < .001). When total color change from baseline to the second bleaching session was evaluated, G-CAM demonstrated a lower ΔE₀₀ value (1.81 ± 1.04; 95% CI: 1.23–2.39) compared with PEEK (3.86 ± 0.34; 95% CI: 3.67–4.05), and this difference remained statistically significant (p < .001).

Discussion

In the present study, the color stability of PEEK and G-CAM was evaluated following exposure to an in-office bleaching agent containing 40% hydrogen peroxide. The findings demonstrated that the two materials exhibited distinctly different optical responses to the bleaching procedure. The present findings should be interpreted within the context of direct exposure to a high-concentration in-office bleaching gel, representing a controlled challenge condition rather than the most common clinical exposure pathway. Both null hypotheses were rejected because significant effects of bleaching stage and significant material × bleaching-stage interactions were identified for the observed CIELAB coordinates (L*, a*, b*).

PEEK is a high-performance polymer widely used in prosthetic dentistry as an esthetic alternative to metal substructures [1–5]. Owing to its bioinert nature, PEEK exhibits high biocompatibility and low plaque affinity [2–6]. However, its opaque, grayish-white appearance compared with natural tooth structure limits its use as a monolithic esthetic restorative material; therefore, veneering with composite resin is generally recommended [9–11].

In contrast, G-CAM is a CAD/CAM restorative material composed of a graphene-reinforced PMMA matrix, designed to provide tooth-colored appearance and improved esthetic integration. According to manufacturer data, graphene-enhanced PMMA restorations exhibit low water absorption and a closed-pore surface structure, which may reduce plaque accumulation and minimize discoloration [13, 23]. Previous studies have suggested that graphene incorporation may contribute to improved color stability by reducing water absorption in PMMA-based systems [3, 12, 13].

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no previous study has directly compared the color changes of PEEK and G-CAM following in-office bleaching. Previous studies have demonstrated that PEEK color may change over time following thermal aging and exposure to staining agents [20]. Nevertheless, the general trend reported in in vitro studies indicates that color changes in PEEK remain within clinically acceptable limits after water immersion, thermal cycling, and staining solution exposure [3]. In a study evaluating graphene-reinforced PMMA under coffee exposure and thermal cycling, G-CAM exhibited less color change than conventional PMMA, with changes reported as clinically imperceptible [12]. However, another study reported that graphene incorporation did not provide a statistically significant improvement in color stability under long-term staining exposure [31]. These findings suggest that the protective effect of graphene may be condition-dependent and may not extend equally to strong oxidizing agents such as high-concentration hydrogen peroxide.

In the present study, PEEK exhibited a color change below the 0.8 perceptibility threshold after the first bleaching session (ΔE₀₀ Baseline–1st = 0.55 ± 0.23), consistent with the material’s reported chemical inertness, low water absorption, and high thermal stability [1, 3]. However, the total color change from baseline to the second bleaching session increased to 3.86 ± 0.34, exceeding the clinical acceptability threshold (ΔE₀₀ = 1.8). Because the present design did not include surface, topographic, or chemical analyses or a non-bleached time-control group, the process underlying this increase cannot be identified. One hypothesis consistent with the observed behavior—but not tested here—is that repeated exposure to a high-concentration peroxide gel could progressively affect the PEEK surface and thereby its light-reflection properties; this remains speculative and would require complementary surface and spectroscopic evaluation to confirm.

G-CAM exhibited a pronounced color change after the first bleaching session (ΔE₀₀ Baseline–1st = 7.97 ± 3.12), corresponding to a mean ΔE₀₀ above the 1.8 acceptability threshold. A hypothesis consistent with this response—though not directly tested, as no surface or chemical analyses were performed—is that the PMMA matrix may be susceptible to interaction with high-concentration hydrogen peroxide; any effect on surface topography or light scattering remains to be demonstrated. Although graphene incorporation has been reported to reduce water absorption and improve color stability against staining solutions [12, 13], it may not confer equivalent protection against strong oxidizing bleaching agents. The interaction between hydrogen peroxide and the graphene-PMMA interface warrants further investigation.

When total color change from baseline to the second bleaching session was evaluated, G-CAM demonstrated a lower ΔE₀₀ value (1.81 ± 1.04) than its baseline-to-first-session change. The individual coordinates clarify this apparently counterintuitive pattern: the lightness (L*) of G-CAM increased markedly after the first session and then reverted toward its baseline value after the second (Table 1), so that the cumulative baseline-to-second coordinate difference was relatively small even though the group-level color difference remained substantial during each interval. The incremental color change between sessions was 7.02 ± 2.61, indicating continued optical alteration despite the lower baseline-to-second difference. At the specimen level, 7 of 15 G-CAM specimens exceeded the acceptability threshold for the baseline-to-second contrast. Thus, the group mean was approximately at the acceptability threshold, with substantial interspecimen variability, rather than indicating uniform unacceptable change in all specimens.

When the comparative results of PEEK and G-CAM are considered, PEEK is known to be advantageous in terms of water absorption and chemical resistance, exhibiting lower water sorption and solubility than composite resins and PMMA-based materials [3, 6]. This characteristic suggests that PEEK may demonstrate greater long-term resistance to staining agents. Indeed, several studies have reported that PEEK-based prosthetic frameworks show favorable discoloration behavior compared with conventional resin materials in simulated oral conditions [1, 6].

Under the in vitro conditions tested—direct exposure of specimens to a high-concentration in-office gel—the two materials showed clearly different color-change behavior. Because this model does not reproduce clinical exposure pathways (for example, tray delivery, saliva, pellicle, brushing, dietary pigments, or restoration geometry) and did not include a non-bleached control, these observations should be regarded as hypotheses to be tested clinically rather than as direct treatment guidance. They may nonetheless indicate directions for future clinical evaluation: whether the pronounced initial change seen in G-CAM and the cumulative change seen in PEEK after repeated exposure occur to a clinically relevant degree in vivo, and how baseline shade differences between a restoration and adjacent teeth influence the outcome. Any practice-oriented recommendation, including the number or spacing of bleaching sessions, would require confirmation under clinically representative bleaching regimens and aging conditions.

The limitations of the present study include the use of a single bleaching agent and concentration, the absence of a non-bleached time-control group — which limits the ability to distinguish bleaching-induced changes from those related to repeated handling or measurement — the absence of direct comparison with natural tooth structure, the use of square-shaped specimens that may not fully represent clinical restoration geometry, and the lack of complementary surface or microstructural analyses that would allow mechanistic interpretation of the observed color changes. Furthermore, only a direct-exposure in-office bleaching protocol was evaluated; therefore, the present findings cannot be generalized to tray-based home bleaching systems, which involve different peroxide concentrations, contact times, and exposure patterns. Finally, in vitro conditions — including static distilled water storage and controlled temperature and lighting — do not fully replicate the complex dynamics of the oral environment.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of the present study, the following conclusions were drawn:

In PEEK specimens, the first bleaching session produced a color change below the 0.8 perceptibility threshold (ΔE₀₀ = 0.55 ± 0.23); however, repeated bleaching resulted in a mean total color difference from baseline exceeding the 1.8 acceptability threshold (ΔE₀₀ = 3.86 ± 0.34). These results apply to the in vitro condition of repeated direct exposure to a high-concentration in-office gel.

In G-CAM specimens, a pronounced color change exceeding the 1.8 acceptability threshold was observed after the first bleaching session (ΔE₀₀ = 7.97 ± 3.12) under direct exposure to a high-concentration in-office gel. The mean total baseline-to-second-session difference was lower and approximately at the acceptability threshold (ΔE₀₀ = 1.81 ± 1.04), with 7 of 15 specimens exceeding 1.8. Because the tested model does not reproduce clinical bleaching conditions, these findings should be confirmed in vivo before they inform treatment decisions for patients with G-CAM restorations.

Under the tested in vitro conditions, PEEK and G-CAM showed different, material-specific color-change behaviors in response to direct exposure to a high-concentration in-office bleaching agent. Further studies using more clinically representative bleaching regimens, aging conditions, and surface analyses are needed before practice-oriented recommendations can be made.
