Turbulent dynamos in a collapsing cloud
Muhammed Irshad P, Pallavi Bhat, Kandaswamy Subramanian, Anvar Shukurov

Abstract
The amplification of magnetic fields is crucial for understanding the observed magnetization of stars and galaxies. Turbulent dynamo is the primary mechanism responsible for that but the understanding of its action in a collapsing environment is still rudimentary and relies on limited numerical experiments. We develop an analytical framework and perform numerical simulations to investigate the behavior of small-scale and large-scale dynamos in a collapsing turbulent cloud. This approach is also applicable to expanding environments and facilitates the application of standard dynamo theory to evolving systems. Using a supercomoving formulation of the magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equations, we demonstrate that dynamo action in a collapsing background leads to a super-exponential growth of magnetic fields in time, significantly faster than the exponential growth seen in stationary turbulence. The enhancement is mainly due to the increasing eddy turnover rate during the collapse, which boosts the instantaneous growth rate of the dynamo. We also show that the scaling of final saturated magnetic field strength with density robustly exceeds the expectation from considerations of pure flux-freezing. Apart from establishing a formal framework for studying magnetic field evolution in collapsing (or expanding) turbulent plasmas, these findings suggest that during star and galaxy formation magnetic fields can become dynamically relevant much earlier than previously thought.

Collapse of a homogeneous cloud.—

Consider a turbulent cloud undergoing homologous collapse. The position of the fluid element and its density are given by,

\mathbf{r}=a(t)\mathbf{r}_{\text{c}},\quad\rho(t)=\rho_{\text{c}}/a^{3}(t),\quad 0<a(t)\leq 1,

(1)

where
a(t)
is the scale factor determining the size,
\mathbf{r}_{\text{c}}
and
\rho_{\text{c}}
are the comoving position and density, respectively.

Even in the comoving coordinates, the magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) equations are still intricate because of the contribution from the background collapse to the total velocity of fluid element given by

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}+H\mathbf{r}
. Here
\mathbf{v}
is the peculiar velocity and
H\mathbf{r}
is the velocity of the collapsing background with the Hubble parameter

H=\mathrm{d}\ln a/\mathrm{d}t
.
Following
[
45
,
46
]
, we introduce the so-called
supercomoving variables denoted here with tilde,

\begin{split}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}&=\mathbf{r}/a,\quad\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}=\mathrm{d}t/{a^{2}},\quad\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{t})=a^{3}\rho(t),\\
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(\tilde{t})&=a\mathbf{v}(t),\quad\tilde{p}(\tilde{t})=a^{5}p(t),\quad\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(\tilde{t})=a^{2}\mathbf{B}(t),\end{split}

(2)

where
p
is the pressure and
\mathbf{B}
is the magnetic field. This results in a simpler structure of the governing equations.
Note that we use the supercomoving time
\tilde{t}
instead of
t
in the analysis and
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
accounts for the evolution of peculiar velocity
as
1/a
in a homologous collapse
[
47
]
. Similarly,
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
is defined so that the magnetic field amplification via flux freezing, in proportion to
1/a^{2}
, due to the overall collapse is factored out. In physical variables, this represents an adiabatically collapsing sphere, evident from pressure and density scaling.

For an incompressible comoving flow, the MHD equations in supercomoving coordinates
follow as

\displaystyle\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=0,

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathbf{v}}}{\partial\tilde{t}}+(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\tilde{\nabla})\tilde{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{p}}{\tilde{\rho}}+\tilde{a}\frac{(\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{\mathbf{B}})\times\tilde{\mathbf{B}}}{4\pi\tilde{\rho}}+\nu\tilde{\nabla}^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{v}},

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathbf{B}}}{\partial\tilde{t}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\nabla}\times(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{B}})+\eta\tilde{\nabla}^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{B}},\quad\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{B}}=0.

(5)

The gravitational term
is balanced by the acceleration of the collapse and, thus, does not appear in the momentum equation.
This choice of incompressible supercomoving flow is consistent with simulations showing that solenoidal turbulence dominates in collapsing primordial cores, being enhanced by the spin-up of turbulent vortices
[
30
]
.
These equations have the same form as the MHD equations in a stationary background except for the factor
\tilde{a}(\tilde{t})\equiv a(t)
in the Lorentz force.
The major advantage of these variables is that the induction equation retains its form. This helps to extend the
standard kinematic dynamo theory to a homologous collapsing background.

The form of
a(t)
is governed by the Friedmann equation

{\ddot{a}}/{a}=-4\pi G\rho(t)/3,

where the dot denotes the time derivative, which
can be integrated with the free-fall initial conditions
a(0)=1
,
\dot{a}(0)=0
:

\arctan\left(\sqrt{(1-a)/a}\right)+\sqrt{a(1-a)}={s}t,

(6)

where
{s}=\sqrt{8\pi G\tilde{\rho}/3}
. In the limit
a\to 0
,
t
tends to the free-fall time,
t_{\rm ff}={\pi}/{(2{s})}=\left[{3\pi}/{(32G\tilde{\rho})}\right]^{1/2},
which is inversely proportional to the initial density of the plasma.
Taking the differential of Eq. (
6
), we have
\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}=\mathrm{d}t/a^{2}=-{s}^{-1}a^{-3/2}(1-a)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}a
, which gives the relation,

\tilde{t}=\frac{2}{{s}}\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{a}}\equiv f(t),

(7)

where the constant of integration follows from the requirement that
\tilde{t}=0
at
a=1
and
t=0
.
Inverting Eq. (
7
) gives the scale factor in terms of
\tilde{t}
,

\tilde{a}(\tilde{t})=\frac{1}{1+{{s}^{2}\tilde{t}^{2}}/{4}}.

(8)

Note that
a\to 0,\>t\to t_{\rm ff}
in the limit
\tilde{t}\to\infty
. Thus, we cannot track the collapse up to the singularity in terms of the supercomoving time. A fit
for the scale factor (accurate within 2%) given by
a(t)=\left(1-{t^{2}}/{t_{\rm ff}^{2}}\right)^{2/3}
, shows that initially the collapse is slow but it accelerates to reach a singularity at
t=t_{\rm ff}

[
48
]
.
The gas pressure stops the collapse before a singular state is reached as the cloud becomes sufficiently inhomogeneous and spherical symmetry breaks
[
49
]
.
Therefore, in the simulations discussed below, the collapse is only extended to a certain earlier time
t_{*}
, after which magnetic field continues evolving against a stationary background.

Turbulent dynamos in a collapsing background.—
In a stationary environment, the coefficients of the induction equation are independent of time, so dynamo action results in an exponentially fast amplification of an initial (weak) magnetic field at the expense of the kinetic energy of the plasma
[
50
,
51
]
. The exponential growth continues until the Lorentz force becomes comparable to the forces driving the plasma flow; after that, the exponential growth saturates. Since the induction equation, Eq. (
5
) in the supercomoving variables has the same form as in a stationary background, the dynamo action causes an exponential amplification of
\tilde{B}
in terms of the time variable
\tilde{t}
,

\tilde{B}\propto\exp{\left(\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{t}\right)}.

In a dynamo-passive system (when the flow does not support dynamo action),
\tilde{\gamma}<0
because of the magnetic diffusion, but the dynamo action results in
\tilde{\gamma}>0
. Suitable expressions for the growth rate
\tilde{\gamma}
of the turbulent dynamos (SSD and LSD) can be found in
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
.
Using Eqs (
2
) and (
7
)
we obtain
the corresponding magnetic field strength in the rest frame,

B\propto\frac{1}{a^{2}(t)}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\tilde{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}}{a^{2}(t^{\prime})}\right)=\frac{1}{a^{2}(t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\tilde{\gamma}f(t)},

(9)

where the factor
1/a^{2}
is due to the
overall collapse and

\tilde{\gamma}=\text{const}
for both SSD and LSD.
Instead of a constant growth rate in a stationary environment, the dynamo action in a collapsing background produces a growth rate increasing with time because of the factor
f(t)
in the exponent.
We refer to this regime as a

super-exponential
growth of the magnetic field.
This is a robust regime
which persists even
if the supercomoving turbulence in a collapsing cloud is decaying
[
52
]
.

By the end of the kinematic regime, the Lorentz force affects the plasma flow and the magnetic field strength reaches a stationary saturation level. The magnetic energy density becomes comparable to the turbulent energy density,

\displaystyle\tilde{a}{\tilde{B}^{2}}/{8\pi}\simeq\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{\rho}\tilde{v}^{2}

(10)

in terms of the rms values of
\tilde{B}
and
\tilde{\rho}\tilde{v}^{2}
,
where the factor
\tilde{a}
is due to the coefficient of the Lorentz force in Eq. (
4
).
In terms of the physical saturated magnetic field strength, assuming
\tilde{\rho}\tilde{v}^{2}=\text{const}
, this implies (see
[
52
]
for the decaying case)

\displaystyle B\propto a^{-5/2}\propto\rho^{5/6}.

(11)

This magnetic field strength significantly exceeds that due to pure flux freezing alone which leads to

B\propto\rho^{2/3}
, and/or that from considering dynamo in a stationary background.

Kinematic dynamos.—
To verify and refine the analytical arguments, we solve
Eqs (
3
)–(
5
) and (
8
) numerically, with the induction equation written in terms of the vector potential.

A random flow
at an energy-range scale
\tilde{l}_{0}

is driven by a random force added on the right-hand side of the momentum equation, Eq. (
4
).
We use the forcing function
[
55
]
from the
Pencil Code

[
56
]
,
rewritten for the
supercomoving coordinates
(see
[
52
]
).
The magnitude of the force driving the random flow is independent of the scale while the forcing wavenumbers
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}
are random in direction and have
magnitudes from the uniform distribution in the ranges
1\leq\tilde{k}\leq 3
(the average
\tilde{k}_{0}=2\pi/\tilde{l}_{0}=2
)
for the SSD simulations and
3\leq\tilde{k}\leq 5
(
\tilde{k}_{0}=4
) for the LSD.
We note that the amplitude of the forcing increases and
the forcing scale
decreases with time in the physical variables, which also allows for the additional driving by the gravitational contraction

[
26
]
.

The driving force is mirror-symmetric in the SSD simulations and helical when the LSD is considered. The resulting LSD is of the
\alpha^{2}
type because of the lack of differential rotation
[
57
]
. In differentially rotating clouds, the LSD can be significantly more efficient
[
6
]
. A random flow can be helical, and thus drive an LSD, in a rotating system. We neglect any deviation from spherical symmetry which may arise from the rotation.

We non-dimensionalize the equations with the forcing scale
\tilde{l}_{0}
, the rms speed
\tilde{v}_{0}
(even in simulations without forcing), and density
\rho_{0}=\tilde{\rho}_{0}
, at
t=\tilde{t}=0
. The unit time is
\tilde{t}_{0}=\tilde{l}_{0}/\tilde{v}_{0}
and the unit magnetic field is
\tilde{B}_{0}=(4\pi\tilde{\rho}_{0}\tilde{v}_{0}^{2})^{1/2}
. The kinetic and magnetic Reynolds numbers,
\text{Re}=\tilde{l}_{0}\tilde{v}_{0}/\nu

and
R_{\text{m}}=\tilde{l}_{0}\tilde{v}_{0}/\eta
are defined in the supercomoving variables and remain constant.
For illustration,
\tilde{t}_{0}\simeq 10^{8}\,{\rm yr}
and
\tilde{B}_{0}\simeq 4\times 10^{-7}\,{\rm G}
for
\tilde{l}_{0}=100\,{\rm pc}
,
\tilde{v}_{0}=1\,\text{km}\,{\rm s}^{-1}
, and
\tilde{\rho}_{0}=10^{-24}\,{\rm g}\,{\rm cm}^{-3},
parameters often used in the simulations of primordial star formation
[
38
]
.

We used the publicly available code
Dedalus

[
58
]
, a
pseudospectral
solver for partial differential equations.
The simulations were
carried out in a periodic cubic box

(2\pi)^{3}
in size,
with a resolution of
128^{3}
.
The initial vector potential is a random Gaussian noise with
a
strength
10^{-5}
.

The kinetic
and magnetic Reynolds numbers
used are

\text{Re}=R_{\text{m}}=415
for the SSD simulations,
while
\text{Re}=180
and
R_{\text{m}}=18
for the LSD. The smaller
R_{\text{m}}

in the latter case
support the LSD but not the SSD (which requires
R_{\text{m}}\gtrsim 100

[
59
]
),
so that we can study the two dynamo mechanisms separately.

To reveal the effect of a collapsing background on dynamo action, we use three simulation runs for each of the SSD and LSD: (i)
dynamo action
in a stationary environment (referred to as the ‘standard’ dynamo)
leading to an exponential magnetic field amplification, (ii) background collapse with
t_{\rm ff}=50
without any random flow, thus no dynamo, and the magnetic field is only amplified due to magnetic flux freezing weakened by the magnetic diffusion, (iii) collapse with
t_{\rm ff}=50
and driven random flow, so the field grows due to both dynamo action and the overall collapse of the cloud. The free-fall time chosen is shorter than the time at which the kinematic regime ends for the standard dynamo,
t_{\text{nl}}
(which depends on the seed magnetic field strength and the dynamo amplification time scale).

Nonlinear dynamos.—

To investigate the nonlinear dynamos numerically, we stop the collapse at a certain time
t_{*}
well before the singular state is reached. After that, the scale factor remains constant at
a_{*}
. If
t_{*}
occurs in the kinematic dynamo stage, the magnetic field continues growing exponentially (without acceleration) until the saturation. We also considered cases where the dynamo is already in the nonlinear stage at
t_{*}
.
Thus,
Eq. (
8
)
is modified as

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(\tilde{t})=\begin{cases}(1+{{s}^{2}\tilde{t}^{2}}/{4})^{-1}&\text{if}\quad\tilde{t}\leq\tilde{t}_{*},\\
(1+{{s}^{2}\tilde{t}_{*}^{2}}/{4})^{-1}&\text{if}\quad\tilde{t}>\tilde{t}_{*}.\end{cases}

(12)

The resulting discontinuity in the time derivative of
\tilde{a}
leads to a discontinuity in the time variation of the Lorentz force. Therefore, the transition to a constant
\tilde{a}
was smoothed about
\tilde{t}=\tilde{t}_{*}
using a moving average.
To convert the supercomoving time
\tilde{t}
to the real time
t
, we numerically integrate

d\tilde{t}=dt/a^{2}
given in
Eq. (
2
)
using Eq. (
12
). We use
a_{*}
in the range 0.1–1 because smaller values require very long simulation runs.

The rate at which magnetic field is amplified in the kinematic dynamo stage depends on both the free-fall time
t_{\rm ff}
(thus on the initial cloud density) and the time when the collapse stops, i.e., on
a_{*}
(if the dynamo is still kinematic at
t_{*}
), whereas the steady-state magnetic field strength depends only on
a_{*}
. We consider three cases where
t_{\rm ff}
differently compares with
t_{\text{nl}}
, the time at which standard dynamo becomes nonlinear:

(i)
t_{\text{nl}}>t_{\rm ff}=50
, (ii)
t_{\text{nl}}\sim t_{\rm ff}=250~(\text{SSD}),\ 200~(\text{LSD})
, (iii)
t_{\text{nl}}<t_{\rm ff}=400
.
In all cases, we stop the collapse at two different
times,
t_{*}/t_{\text{ff}}=0.8
and
0.95
which correspond to different simulations with
a_{*}\simeq 0.5
and
0.2
, respectively.

Conclusions and discussion.—

Dynamo action in a stationary environment exponentially amplify a seed magnetic field. We have shown that in a collapsing cloud the dynamo leads to a faster, super-exponential growth of the magnetic field. This effect is stronger than just a superposition of compression and exponential growth: the variable
a^{2}B
, where the overall impact of compression is factored out, also grows super-exponentially because the instantaneous growth rate of the magnetic field increases in the course of the collapse.
The analysis in this work has benefited from using a framework of supercomoving variables that allowed us to recover the MHD equations in the collapsing background in nearly their original form.

In general, local vortical motions in turbulent
flows are
amplified during the
collapse (because of the specific angular momentum conservation), leading to a shorter eddy turnover time and larger dynamo growth rate as the collapse progresses.
The results obtained here
in the context of homologous collapse
can be adapted to any mode of the collapse (e.g., the hierarchical collapse
[
49
,
60
]
). In particular, the conclusion that dynamo action leads to a super-exponential amplification of the magnetic field applies to any collapsing system.

As shown by us in
[
52
]
, super-exponential magnetic field amplification is possible even when the turbulence is not continuously driven but rather decays. Motivated by our work and using
the formalism developed here,
[
61
]
have addressed this possibility, and confirmed it via simulations.

Apart from enhancing the growth rate, collapse also increases the steady-state
strength of the magnetic field.
For a spherically-symmetric collapse, a frozen-in magnetic field increases with the gas density
\rho
as
B\propto\rho^{2/3}
, whereas the dynamo action leads to
B\propto\rho^{5/6}
in the continuously driven case. A steeper scaling with exponent between
2/3
and
5/6
is predicted even if the supercomoving turbulence is decaying
[
52
]
.
Galaxies form from the intergalactic medium with a density enhancement by a factor of
10^{5}
–
10^{6}
.
Without any dynamo action, a frozen-in magnetic field would evolve from an initial value
B_{i}
to

(\rho/\rho_{0})^{2/3}B_{i}\simeq 10^{4}B_{i}
.
However, with a dynamo operating,
the final field strength,
in equipartition with the turbulent flow, is

10^{5}(4\pi\rho_{0}\tilde{v}_{0}^{2})^{1/2}
, where
(\rho/\rho_{0})^{5/6}\simeq 10^{5}
. This is significantly stronger than what is achieved by flux-freezing alone or by a standard dynamo without collapse.
The contributions of the dynamo action and compression with flux-freezing (in addition to the dynamo amplification) can easily be separated in the framework of super-comoving coordinates.
We discussed this for random (SSD) and large-scale (LSD) magnetic fields;
however, the analysis applies to any fast dynamo.

The super-exponential growth of both random and mean magnetic fields in a collapsing turbulent cloud could
significantly alter estimates of the age at which young galaxies develop observable magnetic fields.
Current dynamo models
which do not include this effect explain
large-scale magnetic fields at the redshift
z\simeq 3

[
62
,
63
]
, whereas random magnetic fields can become significant even earlier.
Ordered galactic magnetic fields have been reported at redshifts up to
z=2.6

[
64
]
and even
z=5.6

[
11
]
. The polarized dust emission detected
can emerge from both anisotropic random magnetic fields (which naturally occur in spiral arms
due to differential rotation
[
6
]
) and mean magnetic fields.
Whatever is the nature of the galactic magnetic fields at high redshifts, its super-exponential amplification may be a crucial aspect of the theory.
To illustrate the new opportunities, we note that, in
the case
t_{\rm ff}\sim 0.1t_{\text{nl}}
,
where
t_{\text{nl}}

marks field saturation at presumably an observable strength,
the super-exponential growth over
15
orders of magnitude
reduces
t_{\text{nl}}
of the standard dynamo by a factor of
10

[
52
]
,
significantly affecting our understanding of galactic magnetic fields at high redshifts
[
65
]
.
Cosmological MHD simulations of galaxy formation suggest turbulent dynamo action
[
66
,
67
]
, but evidence for super-exponential growth—potentially a key dynamo signature in a collapsing environment—has yet to be presented.

Our results also apply
to primordial star formation.
The efficient dynamo action resulting from the strong coupling between primordial gas and magnetic fields
[
68
,
69
,
70
]
, combined with the enormous compression factor from the molecular cloud (density
n\sim 10^{3}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
) to the protostellar core
(
n\sim 10^{24}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
),
leads to significantly amplified magnetic fields in protostars
[
26
]
.
However, it is also possible that the dynamo is inefficient in these environments due to a low Prandtl number or weak turbulence, leading to a very large
t_{\text{nl}}
. Even then the
collapse can significantly enhance the dynamo, with the extent of the speed-up depending on how large the ratio
t_{\text{nl}}/t_{\rm ff}
is. A more detailed calculation of both the speed-up timescales and saturated magnetic field strengths in early stars and galaxies will be presented in a future work.

Our analysis focuses on homogeneous, incompressible supercomoving turbulence in a collapsing cloud, both decaying and maintained by an explicit forcing, using a formalism which can be extended to compressible flows. The form of the forcing used reproduces the amplification of random flows in the gravity-driven turbulence
[
30
]
although the effects of the density inhomogeneities remain to be explored.

I
Dynamo equations and their solutions in supercomoving variables

The interaction between a collisional plasma flow and magnetic field is described by the equations of magnetohydrodynamics (MHD),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\mathbf{u}\right)=0,

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{D}\mathbf{u}}{\text{D}t}=-\nabla\Phi-\frac{\nabla p}{\rho}+\frac{\left(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}\right)\times\mathbf{B}}{4\pi\rho}+\nu\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}=\nabla\times\left(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B}\right)+\eta\nabla^{2}\mathbf{B},\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0,

(15)

where
\text{D}/\text{D}t=\partial/\partial t+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla),

\mathbf{u}
,
p
, and
\rho
are the gas velocity, pressure and density, respectively,
\Phi
is the gravitational potential,
\mathbf{B}
is the magnetic field,
\nu
and
\eta
are the kinematic viscosity and magnetic diffusivity, respectively.
The equations are closed by relating the pressure and density with the help of an equation of state.

As shown in the main text, the induction equation does not change its algebraic form when written in supercomoving variables. In this section, we present its solutions in supercomoving variables.

I.1
The small-scale dynamo (SSD)

Kazantsev’s solution of the induction equation for the correlation function of the magnetic field in a random flow remains one of the most often used models of the SSD in a stationary background
[
50
,
6
]
. The model assumes incompressible, statistically isotropic and
homogeneous, mirror-symmetric,
\updelta
-correlated in time Gaussian random velocity field. Consider such an incompressible supercomoving velocity field
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{t})
and its supercomoving correlation tensor
\tilde{T}_{ij}(\tilde{r})
,

\left<\tilde{v}_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{t})\;\tilde{v}_{j}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}},\tilde{s})\right>=\tilde{T}_{ij}(\tilde{r})\,\updelta(\tilde{t}-\tilde{s})\,,

(16)

where
\tilde{r}=|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}|
and we note that the velocity field is
\updelta
-correlated in both original and supercomoving variables. Since the continuity equation preserves its form when written in the supercomoving variables (Sect. 4.2 of Ref.
[
45
]
) and the gas density is assumed to be independent of position,
\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{v}}=0
implies that the flow is incompressible in the physical variables as well,
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=0
.
The velocity correlation tensor can be represented in terms of the longitudinal and transverse parts,
\tilde{T}_{\text{L}}
and
\tilde{T}_{\text{N}}
, respectively, as
\tilde{T}_{ij}=(\updelta_{ij}-\tilde{r}_{i}\tilde{r}_{j}/\tilde{r}^{2})\tilde{T}_{\text{N}}+\tilde{r}_{i}\tilde{r}_{j}\tilde{T}_{\text{L}}/\tilde{r}^{2}
, where
\tilde{T}_{\text{L}}
and
\tilde{T}_{\text{N}}
are related to each other because of the flow incompressibility, similarly to the relation (
20
) for the magnetic field correlators.

To derive the correlation tensor in terms of the peculiar velocity, we use

\tilde{\mathbf{v}}=a\mathbf{v}
and
\tilde{t}=f(t)
in Eq. (
16
):

\left<a(t)v_{i}(t)\;a(s)v_{j}(s)\right>=\tilde{T}_{ij}(\tilde{r})\updelta\left(f(t)-f(s)\right).

(17)

Since

\updelta[g(t)]=\frac{\updelta\left(t-t_{0}\right)}{|g^{\prime}(t_{0})|},

where
t_{0}
is the root of
g(t)
(i.e.,
t=s
in our case), and

\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}=\mathrm{d}t/a^{2}
, we obtain

\updelta\left[f(t)-f(s)\right]=\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(t)|_{t=s}}\updelta(t-s)=a(s)^{2}\updelta(t-s),

implying that

\left<v_{i}(t)v_{j}(s)\right>=\tilde{T}_{ij}(\tilde{r})\updelta\left(t-s\right).

(18)

Hence the peculiar velocity correlation tensor is
\updelta
-correlated in real time.

The magnetic field correlation tensor
\tilde{M}_{ij}(\tilde{r},\tilde{t})
cannot be assumed to be
\updelta
-correlated in time
[
6
]
, and it has the form

\left<\tilde{B}_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{t})\;\tilde{B}_{j}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}},\tilde{t})\right>=\tilde{M}_{ij}(\tilde{r},\tilde{t}).

(19)

Since
\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{B}}=0
, the magnetic field correlation tensor
\tilde{M}_{ij}
can be written in terms of its longitudinal and transverse
correlators

\tilde{M}_{\text{L}}
and
\tilde{M}_{\text{N}}
,
\tilde{M}_{ij}=(\updelta_{ij}-\tilde{r}_{i}\tilde{r}_{j}/\tilde{r}^{2})\tilde{M}_{\text{N}}+\tilde{r}_{i}\tilde{r}_{j}\tilde{M}_{\text{L}}/\tilde{r}^{2}
, which are related as

\tilde{M}_{\text{N}}=\frac{1}{2\tilde{r}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{r}}\left(\tilde{r}^{2}\tilde{M}_{\text{L}}\right).

(20)

Since the supercomoving equations are similar to MHD equations in a stationary background,
the
derivation of the equation for the longitudinal magnetic correlator in Ref.
[
50
]
remains applicable, and we obtain

\frac{\partial\tilde{M}_{\text{L}}}{\partial\tilde{t}}=\frac{2}{\tilde{r}^{4}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{r}}\left(\tilde{r}^{4}\tilde{\eta}_{\text{T}}\frac{\partial\tilde{M}_{\text{L}}}{\partial\tilde{r}}\right)+\tilde{G}\tilde{M}_{\text{L}},

(21)

where

\tilde{G}=-4\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}}\left(\frac{\tilde{T}_{\text{N}}}{\tilde{r}}\right)+\frac{1}{\tilde{r}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}}\left(\tilde{r}\tilde{T}_{\text{L}}\right)\right]

is responsible for the magnetic field amplification and
\tilde{\eta}_{\text{T}}=\eta+\tilde{T}_{\text{L}}(0)-\tilde{T}_{\text{L}}(\tilde{r})
represents the dissipation due to the electric resistivity and turbulent diffusion. This equation can be reduced to a Schrödinger-type equation and solved using
the WKBJ approximation. For a Kolmogorov turbulent spectra, the magnetic energy amplifies in the initial kinematic stage as

[
51
,
5
,
6
]

\tilde{\text{B}}_{\text{rms}}\propto\exp{\left(\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{t}\right)},\quad\tilde{\gamma}\simeq{\frac{\tilde{v}_{\nu}}{\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}}=}\frac{\tilde{v}_{0}}{\tilde{l}_{0}}{\tilde{\text{Re}}^{1/2}},

(22)

where
\tilde{B}_{\text{rms}}
is the rms magnetic field strength,
\ell_{\nu}
,
v_{\nu}
are the viscous eddy scale, velocity, and
{{\tilde{\text{Re}}}}={\tilde{v}_{0}\tilde{l}_{0}/\nu}
with
\tilde{v}_{0}
and
\tilde{l}_{0}
are the integral speed and scale of the flow. Since
\tilde{v}_{0}
and
\tilde{l}_{0}
remain constant during the collapse,
\tilde{\gamma}=\text{const}.

The initial kinematic stage continues until the magnetic energy reaches equipartition with the kinetic energy of the viscous eddies. After that the growth rate is governed by the next larger eddy and it continues until magnetic energy reaches equipartition with the integral scale over a time scale of the largest eddy turnover time.

While the
\delta
-correlation assumption simplifies our analysis, it is not essential. In standard dynamos, finite correlation times modify growth rates to some degree but preserve exponential growth
[
53
,
54
]
; we thus expect the key finding of super-exponential growth to remain robust.

I.2
The large-scale dynamo (LSD)

The averaged supercomoving induction equation for the mean magnetic field
\langle\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\rangle
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\langle\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\rangle}{\partial\tilde{t}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\nabla}\times\left(\langle\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\rangle\times\langle\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\rangle+\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}-\eta\tilde{\nabla}\times\langle\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\rangle\right),

(23)

where
\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}=\langle\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{b}}\rangle
and angular brackets denote a suitable averaging (e.g., ensemble or volume averaging or a filtering – see Sect. 7.2 of Ref.
[
6
]
).
It can be shown that
\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}\simeq\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}-\tilde{\eta}_{\text{t}}\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{\mathbf{B}}

[
6
]
,
where
\tilde{\alpha}
and
\tilde{\eta}_{t}
are the turbulent transport coefficients

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}\approx-\frac{1}{3}\tilde{\tau}_{0}\left<\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\tilde{\bm{\omega}}\right>,\quad\tilde{\eta}_{t}\approx\frac{1}{3}\tilde{\tau}_{0}\left<\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{2}\right>,

(24)

where
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}=\tilde{\nabla}\times\tilde{\mathbf{v}}
and
\tau_{0}
is the correlation time of the random flow. The derivation assumes a small
\widetilde{\tau}_{0}
and

R_{\text{m}}\gg 1
, such that the resistive and nonlinear terms in the fluctuations are negligible in comparison to the time derivative term.
Assuming for simplicity that
\langle\mathbf{V}\rangle
represents a solid-body rotation, the averaged induction equation written in the rotating frame follows as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\langle\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\rangle}{\partial\tilde{t}}=\tilde{\nabla}\times\left[\tilde{\alpha}\langle\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\rangle\;{-}\;\tilde{\eta}_{\text{T}}\tilde{\nabla}\times\langle\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\rangle\right],

(25)

with
\tilde{\eta}_{\text{T}}
introduced in Eq. (
21
).
This type of the turbulent dynamo is known as the
\alpha^{2}
-dynamo.
The dependence of the growth rate of the magnetic field on the parameters can be illustrated using the simplest solution of Eq. (
25
) in infinite space with
\tilde{\alpha}
and
\tilde{\eta}_{\text{T}}
independent of time and position (Section 7.5 of Ref.
[
6
]
and Ref.
[
57
]
),

\tilde{\gamma}=\tilde{k}\tilde{\alpha}-\tilde{\eta}_{\text{T}}\tilde{k}^{2},

where
\tilde{k}
is the wavenumber of the mean magnetic field. A similar expression is valid for a spherically symmetric distribution of
\tilde{\alpha}

[
57
]
. The magnetic field of the scale
2\pi/\tilde{k}_{\text{m}}=4\pi\tilde{\eta}_{\text{T}}/\tilde{\alpha}
grows most rapidly as
\exp(\tilde{\gamma}_{\text{m}}\tilde{t})
with
\tilde{\gamma}_{\text{m}}=\tilde{\gamma}(\tilde{k}_{\text{m}})=\tilde{\alpha}^{2}/(4\tilde{\eta}_{\text{T}})
.

Since
\tilde{t}=f(t)
, the magnetic field grows super-exponentially in the physical time. It can be argued that the magnetic field growth rate in physical variables
\gamma_{\text{m}}=\alpha^{2}/(4\eta_{\text{T}})
increases as
a
decreases but the growth rate in the supercomoving variables
\tilde{\gamma}_{\text{m}}
remains constant. The supercomoving scale and rms speed of the random flow remain constant during the collapse,
\tilde{l}_{0}=l_{0}/a=\text{const}
and
\tilde{v}_{0}=av_{0}=\text{const}
, whereas the correlation time varies as
\tau_{0}\simeq l_{0}/v_{0}\propto a^{2}
. This is true at the kinematic stage of the dynamo action since the Lorentz force is negligible and the remaining terms in the supercomoving momentum equation do not include
a
explicitly. As a result, the mean helicity density of the random flow
\langle\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\omega}\rangle
increases during the collapse as
\langle\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\omega}\rangle\propto a^{-3}
, while the turbulent magnetic diffusivity remain constant,
\eta_{\text{t}}=\text{const}
. This implies that the growth rate of the physical mean magnetic field varies as
\gamma_{\text{m}}\propto a^{-2}
(we note that
\eta\ll\eta_{\text{t}}
under normal conditions, so
\eta_{\text{T}}\approx\eta_{\text{t}}
). Since
\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}=\mathrm{d}t/a^{2}
, this implies
\tilde{\gamma}_{\text{m}}=\text{const}
.

II
Duration of the kinematic stage

During the kinematic regime of the SSD in a homologous collapsing background,
the physical magnetic field evolves as

B=\frac{B_{0}}{a^{2}}\exp{\left[\tilde{\gamma}f(t)\right]},

(26)

where
B_{0}
is the initial field, with the scale factor approximated with

a(t)=\left(1-\frac{t^{2}}{t_{\rm ff}^{2}}\right)^{2/3},

(27)

and

f(t)=\frac{4t_{\rm ff}}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{a}},

(28)

with
t_{\rm ff}
the free-fall time and
\tilde{\gamma}
the growth rate.
The time at which kinematic regime ends for the
dynamo in a stationary background (the ‘standard dynamo’) is related to
\tilde{\gamma}
and
B_{0}
as

t_{\text{nl}}^{\rm std}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\gamma}}\ln{\left(\frac{B_{\text{nl}}}{B_{0}}\right)},

(29)

where
B_{\text{nl}}
is the magnetic field strength at which the Lorentz force becomes sufficiently strong to affect the flow and the magnetic field growth slows down. Substituting Eqs (
29
) and (
28
) in Eq. (
26
), we get

\ln{\left(\frac{a^{2}B_{\text{nl}}}{B_{0}}\right)}=\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{t_{\rm ff}}{t_{\rm nl}^{\rm std}}\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{a}}\;\ln{\left(\frac{B_{\text{nl}}}{B_{0}}\right)}.

(30)

To derive the scale factor,
a_{\text{nl}}
, at which kinematic regime ends for the dynamo in a collapsing background,
we compare the left- and right-hand sides of
Eq. (
30
)
to obtain the corresponding time
t_{\text{nl}}
using Eq. (
27
).

For example, consider the amplification of a seed magnetic field by 15 orders of magnitude,
B_{\text{nl}}/B_{0}=10^{15}

in a cloud with
t_{\rm ff}=0.1t_{\rm nl}^{\rm std}
. From Eq. (
30
), we obtain

a_{\text{nl}}=0.025
, corresponding to
t_{\text{nl}}=0.09t_{\text{nl}}^{\rm std},
using Eq. (
27
).
Thus, it takes a ten times shorter time for a dynamo in the collapsing cloud to reach its nonlinear stage than in a stationary background.

III
Dynamo action of decaying turbulence

Super-exponential amplification of magnetic field can occur even if turbulence in a collapsing cloud is not continuously driven but rather decays. Thus, we
consider the case of an initially turbulent plasma, with no forcing and a weak seed magnetic fields undergoing gravitational collapse.
Now we have decaying turbulence, unlike the case of a driven turbulence explored in the main text.

The supercomoving (SC) MHD equations in the kinematic stage (when the Lorentz force is negligible) are same as the standard MHD equations. Since the initial magnetic field is weak, the turbulence decays as in a purely hydrodynamic case. In a Kolmogorov turbulence, the rate of decay of supercomoving kinetic energy is given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{v}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}}=-{\eta_{\rm d}}\;\frac{\tilde{v}^{3}}{\tilde{l}},

(31)

where
\eta_{\rm d}
is the dissipation factor
[
34
]
.

From the conservation of either the angular momentum (Loitsyansky invariant) or the linear momentum (Saffman invariant), we have the following constraint on the evolution of the outer turbulent scale:

\tilde{v}_{0}^{\alpha}\tilde{l}_{0}=\text{const}={\tilde{v}_{0i}^{\alpha}\tilde{l}_{0i}=\psi_{0},}

(32)

where
\alpha=2/5
if the Loitsyansky invariant applies and
\alpha=2/3
in the case of the Saffman invariant
[
72
]
,

and the subscript
i
denotes the initial values.
We assume a peaked velocity spectrum as is standard in decaying turbulence literature. Thus we can combine Eq. (
32
) with Eq. (
31
) to obtain
\tilde{v}_{0}\propto\tilde{t}^{-{1}/{\left(1+\alpha\right)}}
.
Substituting this in Eq. (
32
) gives the evolution of the length scale as
\tilde{l}_{0}\propto\tilde{t}^{{\alpha}/{\left(1+\alpha\right)}}.

Magnetic field amplification is possible if the system is super-critical with respect to the dynamo action, that is magnetic Reynolds number is larger than the critical Reynolds number,
R_{\text{m}}>R_{\text{m}}^{\text{cr}}
. The amplification continues as long as the eddies available in the spectrum remain
super-critical.
During this period, the magnetic field strength grows as

\displaystyle a^{2}B=B_{0}\exp{\left(\int\tilde{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}\right)},

(33)

where
B_{0}
is the seed field strength,
a
is the scale factor and
\tilde{\gamma}
is the turnover rate (in the SC frame) of the fastest eddy available. As discussed in the main text, an exponential growth in the SC coordinates (which is similar to the standard kinematic dynamo) implies a super-exponential amplification in the physical frame.

In the decaying turbulence, the growth rate
\tilde{\gamma}_{0}
due to the largest super-critical eddy of a scale
\tilde{l}_{0}
with
R_{\text{m}}(\tilde{l}_{0})>R_{\text{m}}^{\text{cr}}
is given by its turnover rate,

\tilde{\gamma}_{0}=\frac{\tilde{v}_{0}}{\tilde{l}_{0}}\propto\frac{1}{\tilde{t}},

(34)

and using the Kolmogorov scaling
\tilde{v}(\tilde{l})\propto\tilde{l}^{1/3}
we obtain the growth rate due to eddy of scale
\tilde{l}

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(\tilde{l})=\frac{\tilde{v}}{\tilde{l}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{l}}\frac{\tilde{v}_{0}\tilde{l}^{1/3}}{\tilde{l}_{0}^{1/3}}=\frac{\tilde{v}_{0}}{\tilde{l}_{0}}\left(\frac{\tilde{l}_{0}}{\tilde{l}}\right)^{2/3}.

(35)

Focusing on the stretching of field lines due to the eddy of the size
\tilde{l}
, we fix
\tilde{l}
to obtain the growth rate as

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(\tilde{l})=\frac{\tilde{v}_{0}}{\tilde{l}_{0}^{1/3}}\frac{1}{\tilde{l}^{2/3}}=\tilde{\gamma}_{C}(\tilde{l})\,\tilde{t}^{-(3+\alpha)/[3(1+\alpha)]}.

(36)

where
\tilde{\gamma}_{C}(\tilde{l})
is a constant of proportionality.

For the case of the Loitsyansky invariant, we obtain
\alpha=2/5
, hence

\tilde{\gamma}(\tilde{l})=\tilde{\gamma}_{C}(\tilde{l})\tilde{t}^{-{17}/{21}}.

(37)

Equation (
33
) then shows that the magnetic field strength increases as

\displaystyle a^{2}B

\displaystyle=B_{0}\exp{\left(\tilde{\gamma}_{C}(\tilde{l})\int\tilde{t}^{-17/21}\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}\right)},

\displaystyle=B_{0}\exp{\left(\frac{21}{4}\tilde{\gamma}_{C}\tilde{t}^{4/21}\right)}.

(38)

Similarly, for the Saffman-type decay we obtain
\tilde{\gamma}(\tilde{l})=\tilde{\gamma}_{C}(\tilde{l})\tilde{t}^{-11/15}
and

\displaystyle{a^{2}B=B_{0}\exp{\left(\frac{15}{4}\tilde{\gamma}_{C}\tilde{t}^{4/15}\right)}.}

(39)

The field strength in a freely decaying turbulence increases slower than in a steady supercomoving turbulence discussed in the main text, where the power of
\tilde{t}
in the exponent is unity. From the definition of
\tilde{t}
in Eq. (3) and Eq. (9) of the main text, it is clear that
\tilde{t}
is a steeply increasing function of
t
. We conclude that a super-exponential magnetic field growth discussed in the main text occurs, albeit with a different rate of growth, even in this least favorable scenario, the case of decaying turbulence.

We emphasize that the magnetic field can only grow in a super-critical decaying turbulence. Once the turbulence becomes subcritical, the magnetic field will decay. But as far as astrophysical systems are considered,
R_{\text{m}}\gg R_{\text{m}}^{\text{cr}}
, and it can take a long time (say,
t_{R}
) to reduce
R_{\text{m}}
to
R_{\text{m}}^{\text{cr}}
, thus providing sufficient time for the magnetic field amplification. When
t_{R}\gtrsim t_{\rm ff}
, we get a significant additional super-exponential growth due to the collapse. We also note that maintaining
R_{\text{m}}>R_{\text{m}}^{\text{cr}}
for a sufficient time in numerical simulations and thus detecting the scalings of Eqs (
38
) and/or (
39
) can be difficult because of computational constraints on the numerical resolution.

At the end of the kinematic stage, the back-reaction from the Lorentz force saturates the magnetic field growth. The dependence of the magnetic field strength on the gas density in the saturated dynamo can be derived as follows.

The dependence of the flow speed on the scale factor is obtained by integrating Eqs (
31
) and (
32
),

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{v}_{0i}}^{\tilde{v}_{0}}\tilde{v}^{-(2+\alpha)}\mathrm{d}\tilde{v}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta_{\rm d}}{2\psi_{0}}\int_{0}^{\tilde{t}}\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}^{\prime},

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{0}^{-(1+\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\tilde{v}_{0i}^{-(1+\alpha)}+\frac{\eta_{\rm d}(1+\alpha)}{2\psi_{0}}\tilde{t},

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=\tilde{v}_{0i}\left[1+\frac{\eta_{\rm d}(1+\alpha)}{2\psi_{0}\tilde{v}_{0i}^{-(1+\alpha)}}\tilde{t}\right]^{-1/(1+\alpha)}.

(40)

From
\psi_{0}=\tilde{v}_{0i}^{\alpha}\tilde{l}_{0i}
, we obtain
\psi_{0}\tilde{v}_{0i}^{-(1+\alpha)}=\tilde{l}_{0i}/\tilde{v}_{0i}=\tau_{L}
, the turnover time of the outer eddy. Using

\tilde{t}=\frac{2}{s}\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{a}},\quad s=\frac{\pi}{2t_{\rm ff}},

Eq. (
40
) can be written in terms of the scale factor as

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=\tilde{v}_{0i}\left[1+(1+\alpha)\chi\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{a}}\right]^{-1/(1+\alpha)},

(41)

where
\chi=\eta_{\rm d}/s\tau_{L}\sim t_{\rm ff}/t_{\text{nl}}.

The saturation stage of the dynamo is characterized by the equipartition between magnetic and kinetic energies (Eq. (10) of the main text),

\displaystyle a(t)\frac{\tilde{B}^{2}}{8\pi}\simeq\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\rho}\tilde{v}_{0}^{2}.

(42)

Assuming that Eq. (
41
) applies during the nonlinear dynamo stage,
we obtain for
a\ll 1
, the scaling

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{0}\propto a^{1/[2(1+\alpha)]}\propto\rho^{-1/[6(1+\alpha)]},

(43)

whereas the peculiar velocity field scales as

\displaystyle v_{0}\propto a^{-(1+2\alpha)/2(1+\alpha)}\propto\rho^{(1+2\alpha)/6(1+\alpha)}.

(44)

Finally, from Eq. (
42
), we obtain the scaling of the magnetic field strength as

\displaystyle B\propto\sqrt{\rho v_{0}^{2}}\propto a^{-(4+5\alpha)/2(1+\alpha)}\propto\rho^{(4+5\alpha)/[6(1+\alpha)]}.

(45)

In the limit of
\alpha\to 0
, this expression reproduces the flux-freezing exponent of 2/3, and the exponent 5/6 of the forced case is retrieved in the limit
\alpha\to\infty
which corresponds
to a constant
\tilde{v}
. The exponent is greater than 2/3 for any
\alpha>0
, irrespectively of the invariant that governs the decay. After the collapse, the magnetic field can be sustained by other processes such as convection in the case of stars, magnetic or fluid instabilities for accretion-disk flows and stellar feedback in the case of galaxies.

IV
The forcing function

We use the
Pencil Code
forcing function
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}
written in supercomoving coordinates to generate a random flow in the simulations
[
56
,
55
]
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{f}}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\left\{N\mathbf{f}_{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}(\tilde{t})}\;\exp{\left[\mathrm{i}\tilde{\mathbf{k}}(\tilde{t})\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+\mathrm{i}\phi(\tilde{t})\right]}\right\},

(46)

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=f_{0}\tilde{v}_{0}\left(\frac{\tilde{k}\tilde{v}_{0}}{\Delta\tilde{t}}\right)^{1/2},\quad\mathbf{f}_{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}}=\mathbf{R}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle R_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\updelta_{ij}-\mathrm{i}\sigma\epsilon_{ijk}\hat{\tilde{k}}_{k}}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}},\quad\mathbf{f}_{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}}^{(0)}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathbf{e}}{\sqrt{\tilde{k}^{2}-(\tilde{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathbf{e})^{2}}},

where
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}
is the supercomoving wave-vector. The normalization factor
N
decreases with the time step
\Delta\tilde{t}
to ensure
that the forcing is approximately
\updelta
-correlated in the supercomoving time.
The time step is calculated using the CFL condition.
Although the forcing function is
\delta
-correlated in
\tilde{t}
, the resulting supercomoving velocity is not. However, this forcing is sufficient to generate randomness in the flow, essential for exciting turbulent dynamos.
The remaining factors in
N
