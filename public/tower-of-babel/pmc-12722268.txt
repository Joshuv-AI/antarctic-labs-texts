Vicinal disulfide bond modulates A1-GPIbα interaction by altering the force-induced conformational changes of A2 domain
Authors: Weixuan Chen, Decheng Hou, Yi Liu, X Frank Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVon Willebrand Factor (VWF) plays a crucial role in hemostasis and thrombosis, with its A1 and A2 domains being keys to its function. This study investigates the impact of the vicinal disulfide bond in the A2 domain on the interaction between the A1 domain and platelet glycoprotein Ibα (GPIbα). Using single-molecule optical tweezers, we evaluated the mechanical unfolding and refolding behaviors of wild-type A2 (WT A2) and an A2 mutant without the vicinal disulfide bond (CC-AA A2). Our results reveal that CC-AA A2 exhibits heterogeneous unfolding patterns and altered refolding characteristics compared to WT A2. Furthermore, microscale thermophoresis and AlphaFold3 predictions demonstrate enhanced binding affinity of CC-AA A2 to the A1 domain and decreased binding affinity of CC-AA A1A2 to the ligand binding domain (LBD) of GPIbα. These findings advance our understanding of the regulatory role of the vicinal disulfide bond in VWF function and its potential implications in hemostasis and thrombosis.Subject terms: Single-molecule biophysics, Blood proteins

Introduction

Von Willebrand factor (VWF) is a multimeric plasma glycoprotein that plays a crucial role in hemostasis and thrombosis with two functions: mediating platelet adhesion to injured vessel walls and protecting blood coagulation factor VIII from proteolysis1. Synthesized in vascular endothelial cells and megakaryocytes, each VWF subunit is composed of repeated motifs or domains in the sequence of D1-D2-D’-D3-A1-A2-A3-D4-C1-C2-C3-C4-C5-C6-CK. After translocation into the endoplasmic reticulum, VWF subunits dimerize through disulfide bonds between CK domains and are assembled into multimers varying from 500 to 20,000 kDa by the formation of disulfide bonds between D3 domains, followed by the cleavage of propeptide D1-D2 in Weibel-Palade bodies 2,3.

Among the 13 domains of a mature VWF monomer, the A1-A2-A3 tri-domain is the key to VWF’s function in hemostasis. Under high shear induced by vascular injury, the binding site for platelet glycoprotein (GP) Ibα on the A1 domain is exposed and allows VWF to capture platelets from the blood flow. Simultaneously, the A3 domain binds to exposed subendothelial collagen, enabling platelet adhesion to the injury site4. Located between the A1 and A3 domains, the A2 domain acts as a shear blot to regulate the activity of platelet adhesion. When activated by shear force, the A2 domain unfolds and leads to the cleavage of VWF by ADAMTS13, giving rise to a smaller VWF multimer, which is less active in platelet adhesion5. Mutations in the A1 domain can engender enhanced or reduced interaction between the A1 domain and GPIbα, resulting in bleeding disorders, termed type 2B or type 2M von Willebrand diseases6–8. Previous studies indicated that the shear-induced binding between the A1 domain and GPIbα is regulated via autoinhibitory mechanisms. D’D3 domain9, the terminal flanking region of the A1 domain (named as the autoinhibitory module)10–12, and A2 domain13–17 have been implied to mask the A1 binding site and limit its binding affinity to GPIbα.

The vicinal disulfide bond of the A2 domain, which links the adjacent Cys1669-Cys1670 residues and forms an 8-membered ring, functions as a critical part of the hydrophobic core of A2 domain and provides resistance to unfolding5. Removal of this vicinal disulfide bond in the A2 domain and full-length VWF increased their susceptibility to ADMATS13 cleavage18. Besides, prior research revealed the involvement of the A2 domain vicinal disulfide bond in the modulation of VWF-platelet interaction. The reduced A2 domain without the vicinal disulfide bond exhibited higher binding affinity to the A1 domain and competitively inhibited the binding between GPIb and the A1 domain. Double Cys mutant VWF, with both Cys1669 and Cys1670 replaced by Ala, showed reduced platelet adhesion under fluid shear conditions16. Nevertheless, whether and how the vicinal disulfide bond affects the force-induced conformational change of the A2 domain and thereby regulates the binding of the A1 domain to GPIbα remains elusive.

In this study, we strived to reveal the effects of vicinal disulfide bond on mechanical unfolding and refolding of VWF A2 domain using single-molecule optical tweezer measurements of A2 mutant with both Cys1669 and Cys1670 replaced by Ala (CC-AA A2). In addition, how the vicinal disulfide bond modifies the interaction between the A2 domain and A1 domain, as well as between the A1A2 domains and LBD of GPIbα, was investigated by microscale thermophoresis (MST) and interaction prediction via AlphaFold 3. Together, the results of this study demonstrate that the removal of the vicinal disulfide bond alters the force-induced conformational change of the A2 domain and altered the binding affinity of the A2 domain to the A1 domain and the A1A2 domains to GPIbα, suggesting that the inhibition of VWF-platelet interaction caused by the removal of vicinal disulfide bond is due to the enhanced masking of the platelet binding site by A2 domain.

Results

Heterogeneous mechanical unfolding behavior of the A2 domain without the vicinal disulfide bond

The crystal structure of the VWF A2 domain shown in Fig. 1A highlights the structural features of the domain: the Cys1669-Cys1670 vicinal disulfide bond that provides rigidity to the domain and the ADAMTS13 cleavage site (Y1605-M1606). Recombinant VWF A2 domain and CC-AA A2 (A2 mutant with Cys1669 and Cys1670 replaced by Ala to remove vicinal disulfide bond) were expressed in HEK293 cells and purified (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1). To investigate the effects of vicinal disulfide bond on the mechanical unfolding of the A2 domain, wildtype A2 (WT A2) or CC-AA A2 with a N-terminal SpyTag and a C-terminal biotin was coupled to a biotinylated SpyCatcher-DNA handle and held between streptavidin beads fixed to a micropipette and a laser trap (Fig. 1C). A2 fragments (WT A2 and CC-AA A2) underwent cycles of force increasing and decreasing, with a 10-s relaxing time at a low force between each cycle to enable refolding. The unfolding of WT A2 and CC-AA A2 was defined by abrupt extension and force-dropping events (marked by arrows in Fig. 1D, with both approach and retract traces shown in Supplementary Fig. 2). Compared to the representative force-extension trace of WT A2 (Fig. 1D(a)), which showed a single unfolding event (with an extension of around 40 nm) in each approach-retraction cycle, CC-AA A2 displayed various unfolding populations: single unfolding event with long extension (~40 nm, Fig. 1D(b)) or short extension (~20 nm, Fig. 1D(c)) per cycle (termed as single-population), and multiple unfolding events with one long extension (~30 nm) and one short extension (~10 nm) per cycle (termed as multiple-population) (Fig. 1D(d–e)).

Extension and force histograms of WT A2 and CC-AA A2 at varying pulling speeds (5–500 nm/s) were fitted with Gaussian distribution (Figs. 2–3). The pulling traces of CC-AA A2 were analyzed separately based on different types of unfolding population (single or multiple) (Fig. 2B–D, Fig. 3B, C). WT A2 exhibited single-population unfolding behavior, with the most probable extension distance ranging from 41.6 ± 0.2 nm to 46 ± 0.6 nm (Fig. 2A) and the most probable unfolding force ranging from 10.7 ± 0.6 nm to 16.1 ± 0.4 nm (Fig. 3A) with varied pulling speed, which is comparable to the previous study19. Distinctively, two unfolding events were observed in most extension histograms of CC-AA A2 for both single-population and multiple-population (Fig. 2B, C). There were two unfolding events detected in the extension histograms of single CC-AA A2 unfolding at 50 or 100 nm/s, with one peak at 19.7 ± 0.3 nm or 25 ± 0.7 nm, and another peak at 33 ± 0.5 nm or 40 ± 0.8 nm. Only one peak at around 35 nm was detected in other extension histograms of single-population of CC-AA A2, which may be because the two peaks were too close to each other and could not be distinguished (Fig. 2B). For multiple-population of CC-AA A2, two peaks were observed in most extension histograms, with one peak changing from 11.1 ± 0.3 nm to 16.9 ± 1.0 nm, and another peak changing from 25.6 ± 0.2 nm to 35.1 ± 1.5 nm (Fig. 2C). In addition, extension histograms of the sum-ups of unfolding events in each pulling trace of multiple-population presented one unfolding event with the extension peaks ranging from 31 ± 0.8 nm to 54.7 ± 0.4 nm (Fig. 2D), which is similar to the ones of WT A2. These findings suggest there are three populations of unfolding when pulling CC-AA A2: single-population with a similar extension to WT A2 (around 41–48 nm), single-population with a short extension (around 11–17 nm), and multiple-population with a short extension (around 11–17 nm) and a long extension (around 25–35 nm), whose add-up is similar to the unfolding extension of WT A2. These populations of unfolding account for ~47%, 31%, and 22% of all pulling traces (194, 128, and 91 out of 413 traces), respectively.

Two unfolding events were also observed in most force histograms of CC-AA A2 (Fig. 3B, C). All force histograms of CC-AA A2’s single-population displayed two peaks, which changed from 11.7 ± 0.9 pN to 25.7 ± 0.5 pN and from 28.4 ± 2.2 pN to 48.8 ± 4 pN, respectively (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 3B). Meanwhile, most force histograms of multiple-population of CC-AA A2 showed two peaks, one of which ranged from 17.8 ± 1.4 pN to 25.7 ± 1.1 pN and another ranged from 31.2 ± 0.8 pN to 54.8 ± 1.6 pN (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. 3C). These observations imply that CC-AA A2 exhibits three types of unfolding populations: single-population with an unfolding force indistinguishable from WT A2 (around 10–20 pN), single-population with an unfolding force of around 30–55 pN, and multiple-population with one event unfolding at around 16–24 pN and another event unfolding at 30–55 pN. The observations from histograms of CC-AA A2 suggest that the mechanical unfolding behavior of CC-AA A2 is diverse: it can unfold completely at once, the same as WT A2, but it also can unfold in two parts, one of which has a long extension of 25–35 nm and the other of which has a short extension of 11–17 nm. Overall, these results demonstrate that the removal of the vicinal disulfide bond alters the force-induced conformational change of the A2 domain, resulting in heterogeneous mechanical unfolding behavior of the A2 domain, which may be due to the reduction of the A2 domain’s rigidity.

Mutagenesis of the vicinal disulfide bond alters the refolding of the A2 domain

Given the impact of the vicinal disulfide bond on the mechanical unfolding of the A2 domain, whether the mutagenesis of vicinal disulfide bond modifies the refolding of the A2 domain was evaluated by using single-molecule optical tweezers (Fig. 1C). An A2 fragment held between two beads was relaxed at different low forces (~1, 2, or 3 pN) for certain time durations (6, 7, 10, or 15 s). Due to the predominantly unapparent refolding of A2 fragments (Supplementary fig. 2), the refolding was determined by the observation of subsequent unfolding following a relaxation period at a low force, and a lack of subsequent unfolding was identified as an absence of refolding. While WT A2 usually showed either complete unfolding (Fig. 1D(a)) or an absence of unfolding after a relaxation period, CC-AA A2 showed various populations of subsequent unfolding (Fig. 1D(b–g)). Subsequent unfolding (single-population or multiple-population) with a total extension length similar to the one of WT A2 (Fig. 1D(b, d–g)) were defined to indicate full refolding, and the subsequent unfolding of single-population with a much shorter extension (Fig. 1D(c)) referred to partial refolding.

The force-dependent fractions of refolded A2 fragments were fitted to an τ~exp(f2) model20,21(Fig. 4A–D), which has been employed to delineate the refolding of proteins19. The fitting yielded the refolding rates in the absence of force kf0 and compliances κ of unfolded A2 fragment shown in Fig. 4E. Although the fitting of all refolding events (the sum of full refolding and partial refolding) of CC-AA A2 (Fig. 4B) yielded a higher refolding rate than the one of WT A2 (Fig. 4A), the full refolding of CC-AA A2 (Fig. 4C) exhibited a lower refolding rate, and the partial refolding of CC-AA A2 (Fig. 4D) showed a similar refolding rate. The difference between the compliances of WT A2 and CC-AA A2 also indicates that compared to WT A2, CC-AA A2 is less flexible to fully refold and more flexible to partially refold. In general, these results reveal the alteration of the refolding behavior of the A2 domain via the mutagenesis of the vicinal disulfide bond, which leads to the increasing potential of the A2 domain for partial refolding.

Enhanced binding affinity of CC-AA A2 to A1 domain and its effect on A1-A2 interaction

To estimate the influence of the vicinal disulfide bond on the interaction between the A2 domain and the A1 domain, microscale thermophoresis (MST) was utilized to detect the binding affinity between A2 fragments and the A1 domain (Fig. 5A, C). The binding affinity of CC-AA A2 to A1 domain was 10.0 ± 2.6 nM, implying that the interaction between CC-AA A2 and A1 has a five-fold higher affinity compared to the 62.8 ± 9.1 nM binding affinity of the interaction between WT A2 and A1 (Fig. 5C).

Binding affinities (Kd) of A1-A2 fragment interaction (C) and GPIbα-A1A2 fragment interaction (D) were acquired by fitting the data into the Kd model of MO Affinity Analysis software. Error in the tables is the standard deviation of the fitted binding affinity.

To further investigate how these enhancements in binding affinity affect A1-A2 interaction, AlphaFold3 was utilized to predict the binding between A2 fragments and the A1 domain, considering its efficiency and accuracy in complex prediction. Though single or limited mutations usually will be smoothed out due to the AlphaFold algorithm22, our mutations of C1669A and C1670A included a significant structural change (the removal of a disulfide bond) which is likely to shift the AlphaFold3 predicting results, offering different predictions between WT A2 and CC-AA A2 to demonstrate the impact of these mutations on residues of interest in the A2 domain.

The prediction results of A1 binding to WT A2/CC-AA A2 are shown in Fig. 6, with Confidence scores shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. The overall arrangements of the A1 domain and WT A2/CC-AA A2 are very similar but differences existed around residues of interest and interaction interface in selected prediction models, which were chosen based on the prediction ranking from AlphaFold3 and reasonable structural characteristics in predicted WT A2 models. In the interaction between A1 and WT A2, the representative interacting residue pairs of A1 and of WT A2 are: F1366 (A1)—W1644 (WT A2), E1359 (A1)—G1621 (WT A2), and R1392 (A1)—E1598/D1622 (WT A2). Among these interacting residue pairs, most are hydrogen bonds forming polar contacts, and a salt bridge could be formed between R1392 (A1) and E1598/D1622 (WT A2). A π-π interaction was also predicted between F1366 (A1)—W1644 (WT A2). Other potential interacting pairs could be formed on K1362 (A1) - L1619 (WT A2), R1399 (A1) - N1646 (WT A2), and F1366 (A1) - R1618 (WT A2) (Fig. 6A).

Compared to A1 binding to WT A2, the bound complex of A1 and CC-AA A2 has more interacting residue pairs because of the mutations on C1669A and C1670A. These mutations eliminated the disulfide bond at the end of a neighbored α-helix, which could result in a potential elongation of this α-helix and guide the free C-terminal end pointing toward the interaction interface. According to the binding prediction results of A1 and CC-AA A2 (Fig. 6B), the representative interacting residue pairs are: E1359 (A1)—G1621 (CC-AA A2), R1392 (A1)—D1622/D1596/E1598 (CC-AA A2), and R1395 (A1)—G1672 (CC-AA A2). These representative residue pairs are mostly similar to A1 binding to WT A2. However, CC-AA A2 uniquely has a residue G1672 near the C-terminal free end participating in the interaction between A1 and CC-AA A2, which is very likely a consequence of C1669A and C1670A mutations. Another residue near the free end of CC-AA A2, G1674, also showed the potential to interact with R1399 (A1), a possible interacting residue being predicted frequently. Interactions of F1366 (A1)—W1644 (CC-AA A2), F1366(A1)—R1618 (CC-AA A2), R1399 (A1)—N1646 (CC-AA A2) also appeared among some predictions between the wildtype group and the mutant group. Therefore, combining with our previous findings, C1669A and C1670A mutations potentially increase the interacting residue pairs between A1 and A2 domains, enhancing their binding activities.

Decreased binding affinity of CC-AA A1A2 to GPIbα and its effect on A1A2- GPIbα interaction

To validate the impact of the vicinal disulfide bond on the interaction between the A1A2 domains and GPIbα, MST experiments were performed to check the binding affinity between A1A2 fragments and the LBD of GPIbα (Fig. 5B, D). The binding affinity of CC-AA A1A2 to GPIbα LBD was 121.6 ± 7.4 nM, showing that the interaction between CC-AA A1A2 and GPIbα has a six-fold lower affinity compared to the 16.8 ± 5.1 nM binding affinity of the interaction between WT A1A2 and GPIbα (Fig. 5D).

In addition, to study the possible impact of the CC-AA mutation on A1A2 interacting with GPIbα LBD, we have predicted the bound complex between VWF A1A2 domains with C1669A and C1670A and GPIbα LBD via AlphaFold3. It is interesting to find that the CC-AA A2 could possibly compete with the A1 domain on the flexible loop area of GPIbα LBD, though the A1 domain is still the main docking component in the A1A2-GPIbα LBD complex (Fig. 7). A key docking area between A1 and GPIbα LBD is their bond formation between their beta sheets and representative residue pairs are: A1327 (A1) - K237 (GPIbα) and S1325 (A1) - M239 (GPIbα). And there are some other interacting residues between A1 and GPIbα LBD beta-sheet curved bay, such as R1334 (A1)—D18/H37 (GPIbα), K1371 (A1)—T103 (GPIbα), F1366 (A1)—K152 (GPIbα), Q1367 (A1)—K152/T176 (GPIbα), K1362 (A1)—F199/P198/N226/E225/Y228 (GPIbα), and E1359 (A1) - Y228 (GPIbα). Compared to previous individual interaction between A1 and A2, A1 primarily binds to GPIbα LBD and there is no significant interaction between A1 and A2 domains and C1669A and C1670A are not highly involved as shown in previous prediction. However, the possible interacting interface on A2 shown in our previous prediction is facing toward the A1 domain and the flexible loop area of GPIbα LBD is located between these two domains. In this situation, this flexible loop area of GPIbα LBD also interacts with the A2 domain and some representative residues are: D235 (GPIbα)—R1618 (A2), V234 (GPIbα)—R1641 (A2), and G233 (GPIbα)—R1641 (A2). The interaction between GPIbα LBD and A2 may potentially distort the flexible loop area and weaken its interaction with the A1 domain.

Discussion

This study focuses on the role of the vicinal disulfide bond in the VWF A2 domain and how it mediates the interaction between the VWF A1 domain and platelet GPIbα. While previous studies have highlighted the importance of this disulfide bond in the ADAMTS13 cleavage of VWF and VWF-platelet interaction, the specific mechanisms of its effect on the mechanical properties of VWF and WVF’s interaction with GPIbα remain unclear. Using optical tweezer experiments, MST, and AlphaFold 3 prediction, we provide a molecular-level evaluation of how the vicinal disulfide bond affects the force-induced conformational changes of the A2 domain and its interactions with the A1 domain.

Observed from our results of unfolding experiments, CC-AA A2, the A2 mutant without the vicinal disulfide bond, displayed heterogeneous unfolding behavior with multiple unfolding events in one pulling trace, in contrast to the single unfolding event per pulling trace detected in WT A2. The refolding characteristics of CC-AA A2 were also altered, indicating an increased tendency for partial refolding compared to WT A2, potentially leading to non-native conformation and resulting in subsequent differential unfoldings. While the heterogeneous unfolding pattern could suggest reduced structural rigidity due to the absence of the vicinal disulfide bond, an alternative interpretation is that the higher forces observed in some CC-AA A2 populations indicate the formation of more stable intermediate states rather than simply reduced rigidity. In this view, the vicinal disulfide bond in WT A2 promotes a single, cooperative unfolding pathway, while its absence in CC-AA A2 allows for stepwise unfolding involving multiple mechanical pathways through intermediate states. This interpretation is consistent with our refolding data showing increased partial refolding in CC-AA A2, as these intermediate conformations could serve as kinetic traps that lead to the observed heterogeneous behavior. Thus, the vicinal disulfide bond may function not only to maintain overall structural integrity but also to constrain the A2 domain to a single dominant unfolding pathway, thereby ensuring more predictable mechanical behavior under physiological stress conditions. This mechanism could explain the increased susceptibility to ADAMTS13 proteolysis reported previously18, as the multiple unfolding pathways in CC-AA A2 may expose the cleavage site in different conformations. In addition, CC-AA A2 showed enhanced binding affinity to the A1 domain in the MST measurements, while CC-AA A1A2 presented reduced binding affinity to the LBD of GPIbα, implying the modulation of the vicinal disulfide bond in these interactions, which potentially serves as a regulatory mechanism for A1 autoinhibition, further supporting the previous study of Butera et al.16. The following AlphaFold 3 predictions provide a potential explanation of CC-AA A2’s enhanced binding affinity to A1 and CC-AA A1A2’s reduced binding affinity to the LBD of GPIbα: the removal of the vicinal disulfide bond increases the interacting residue pairs between A1 and A2 domains and thus inhibits the interaction between A1A2 and GPIbα.

The heterogeneous unfolding patterns observed in CC-AA A2 have significant implications for VWF function under physiological shear stress conditions in blood flow. Under high shear stress, such as that encountered in stenotic arteries or artificial devices (such as left ventricular assist devices), the altered mechanical behavior of CC-AA A2, representing the fraction of A2 with reduced disulfide, could lead to increased exposure of ADAMTS13 binding sites and enhanced susceptibility to proteolysis. This heterogeneous behavior may create a population of partially unfolded VWF molecules with varying degrees of accessibility to ADAMTS13 cleavage, potentially leading to dysregulated hemostasis. Furthermore, the tendency for partial refolding in CC-AA A2 might create non-native conformations that could serve as nucleation sites for pathological VWF aggregation or result in altered cleavage patterns by ADAMTS13, which could have significant clinical implications for thrombotic disorders.

Our findings also suggest that targeting the vicinal disulfide bond could represent an innovative therapeutic strategy for VWF-related disorders, though this must be considered with appropriate caution given the complexity of targeting this system. Stabilizing the disulfide bond through small molecules or redox modulators could be considered to enhance VWF-platelet interactions in bleeding disorders such as von Willebrand disease. Conversely, in thrombotic conditions, controlled administration of reducing agents that transiently break the vicinal disulfide bond could inhibit platelet adhesion by promoting the inhibitory interaction between A1 and A2 domains, thereby reducing the risk of pathological thrombus formation. However, the selectivity of such interventions presents a major challenge, as systemic redox modulators could have widespread off-target effects on other disulfide-containing proteins, and the complex in vivo redox environment makes such targeted interventions particularly challenging.

In addition, it has been reported that heart failure patients who have received extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) have more oxidized circulating VWF, while the patients who have not received ECMO have more reduced circulating VWF, which is the VWF without the vicinal disulfide bond in A2 domain. This indicates the involvement of A2 redox forms in the circulation, which has implications for related hemostatic disorders16, suggesting the potential utility of measuring the A2 disulfide bond’s redox state as a biomarker for thrombotic risk assessment in patients undergoing ECMO or those with sepsis, where dysregulated hemostasis is common. The ratio of oxidized to reduced VWF in circulation could provide valuable insights into a patient’s hemostatic status and guide therapeutic decisions. This biomarker approach could be particularly valuable in critical care settings, where rapid assessment of thrombotic risk is essential for patient management. Our study, which evaluated the effect of the vicinal disulfide bond in the mechanical properties of the A2 domain, aids in exploring the potential biomarker role of the A2 redox state in the circulation in a biomechanical aspect and may help to estimate the effect of ECMO for heart failure patients. While the reversible nature of this disulfide bond makes it an attractive theoretical target for pharmacological intervention that could be titrated based on clinical needs, the development of specific, bioavailable agents that can selectively target this bond without affecting other cellular processes remains a major difficulty.

While our results provide a detailed explanation for those previously observed phenomena, the use of isolated domains instead of full-length VWF in our experiments may not fully manifest the complicated inter- and intra-molecular interactions present in native proteins. Full-length VWF exists as large multimers with complex interdomain interactions, and the presence of other domains, particularly the D’D3 domains that are known to shield the A1 domain9, could significantly influence the observed effects. Multimerization itself introduces additional levels of complexity, as mechanical forces are distributed across multiple VWF monomers, potentially altering the force-dependent behaviors we observed in isolated domains. Furthermore, the physiological context includes interactions with other plasma proteins, cell surfaces, and the complex rheological environment of blood flow, all of which could modulate the role of the vicinal disulfide bond. Meanwhile, as a computational approach, the predictions of AlphaFold 3 may not perfectly reflect in vivo conditions. Future studies of the mutagenesis of predicted interaction residues (such as G1672 and G1674 in CC-AA A2) with subsequent measurements of binding affinities would provide experimental validation and strengthen current conclusions. Since our experiments were mainly performed in static conditions, further study is required to investigate the role of the vicinal disulfide bond in A2 proteolysis and A1 autoinhibition by A2 under flow conditions. Further structural studies can also be conducted to reveal the precise conformational changes induced by the CC-AA mutations. Besides, molecular dynamics simulations may be beneficial to complement our experimental findings and provide additional perception into the force-dependent behavior of VWF.

In conclusion, our study sheds light on the effect of the vicinal disulfide bond on the function of the VWF A2 domain. We demonstrate that the vicinal disulfide bond is critical for maintaining the mechanical stability of the A2 domain as well as regulating its interactions with the A1 domain, which is assumed to modulate the A1- GPIbα interaction. The removal of the vicinal disulfide bond leads to altered unfolding and refolding behaviors, as well as enhanced binding affinities, indicating its involvement in the complex regulatory mechanism of VWF function. These findings contribute to our understanding of the vicinal disulfide bond’s modulation in VWF function and may inform the development of innovative therapeutic strategies for VWF-related disorders.

Methods

Recombinant protein expression and purification

Recombinant human VWF A1 domain (residues Q1238 to T1468, with prepro-VWF numbering), A2 domain (residues G1484 to I1677, with prepro-VWF numbering), A2 mutant (CC-AA A2), A1A2 domains (residues Q1238 to I1677, with prepro-VWF numbering), and A1A2 mutant (CC-AA A1A2) constructs were cloned into a pHLsec backbone with N-terminal SpyTag and C-terminal Avi-His tag. Recombinant A2 domain fragments and A1A2 domains fragments were expressed in human embryonic kidney 293 cells (ATCC CRL-1573), purified with Ni-NTA affinity chromatography followed by size-exclusion chromatography5,19, and stored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4, at −80 °C. Ligand-binding domain (LBD, residues H1 to R290) of human GPIbα was produced using stably expressing clones generated in Expi293F-BirA cells, purified using a GE Healthcare Ni Sepharose excel column followed by size exclusion chromatography on a GE Healthcare HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg column11 and stored in −80 °C. The purity of proteins was analyzed by gel electrophoresis using a 4–16% SDS-PAGE gel, followed by Coomassie stain.

Sample preparation for optical tweezer experiments

The biotinylated 802 bp dsDNA handle was generated by PCR, activated, and coupled to SpyCatcher via disulfide bonds as previously described19. E. coli-produced SpyCatcher was a gift from Dr Howarth at the University of Oxford, London, England23. Biotinylation of A2 fragments and A1A2 fragments was performed using the BirA500 biotin-labeling kit (Avidity, Aurora, CO), which adds biotin to the N-terminal Avi-tag of A2 fragments and A1A2 fragments24. Excess-free biotin was removed using Zeba™ Spin Desalting Columns, 7K MWCO (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Biotinylated A2 fragments and A1A2 fragments were conjugated to streptavidin-coated polystyrene beads (2 μm diameter; Spherotech, Lake Forest, IL) via 15-min incubation at room temperature. The mixture was diluted with TBS buffer (10 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) and used for the experiments. SpyCatcher-DNA handle was incubated with beads and diluted with the same method.

Single-molecule optical tweezer assay

All single-molecule measurements were conducted using an optical tweezer instrument (“MiniTweezers”; Tweezers LAB). TBS buffer mentioned in the sample preparation was used as the experimental buffer. In the optical tweezer experiments, a bead conjugated with SpyCatcher-DNA handle was fixed by the micropipette, while another bead conjugated with A2 fragments was optically trapped. By moving the trapped bead close to the other bead, the SpyTag and SpyCatcher would bind each other and form a tether during the pulling and retracting process, enabling the measurement of the unfolding and refolding behavior of the A2 fragments. Unfolding experiments were conducted at five pulling speeds (50, 100, 200, 400, 500 nm/s). The force-loading rate data were fitted to the Bell-Evans model7,20 for the characterization of unfolding kinetics. Although the refolding of A2 fragments is predominantly unapparent (Supplementary fig. 2), their refolding could be identified by subsequent unfolding following a certain relaxation period (6–15 s) at low forces (1–3.6 pN), while a lack of unfolding referred to an absence of refolding. For refolding experiments, the pipette position was held constant while the laser trap was positioned to reach a low relaxing force of ~1, 2, or 3 pN. The fluctuation ranges for each set relaxing force were recorded and are presented in Fig. 4 as mean ± standard deviation. All optical tweezer experiments were performed at room temperature and the force-distance curves were recorded. All shown force-distance curves were raw data without smoothing to preserve the authenticity of the mechanical events.

For unfolding experiments, force and extension histograms at varying pulling speeds were fitted with Gaussian distribution. The fitting values were presented as the fitted peak values ± standard error of the fit. Microscale thermophoresis assay

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) measurements were conducted as previously described25. In general, the recombinant A1 domain or GPIbα LBD was labeled with RED-NHS (2nd Generation) dye using the Monolith Protein Labeling Kit (NanoTemper Technologies, München, Germany). A2 fragments or A1A2 fragments were 2-fold diluted in a 15-step serial dilution in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.01% Pluronic® F-127 and mixed with labeled A1 domain or GPIbα LBD with a final concentration of 40 nM. The mixed samples were loaded into 16 premium capillaries (NanoTemper Technologies) of equal value and placed in the reaction chamber in order of A2 or A1A2 fragment concentration. MST measurements were performed on a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies) at 60% excitation power and 40% MST power at 21 °C. At least four independent replicates were analyzed to estimate the binding affinity (Kd) using the MO Affinity Analysis software (NanoTemper Technologies, version 2.3). Error bars represent standard deviation calculated from the replicates. Fitted values were presented with standard deviation.

Bound complex prediction using AlphaFold3 and analysis

Predicted bound complex models (A2 fragments and A1 domain or A1A2 fragments and GPIbα LBD) were generated using AlphaFold3. AlphaFold3 is a state-of-the-art predicting tool based on the deep-learning framework, focusing on biomolecule interaction prediction, and has much higher accuracy and efficiency compared to its previous version, AlphaFold2-Multimer26. The sequence information of the A1 domain, A2 fragments, A1A2 fragments, and GPIbα LBD were retrieved from the protein database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and were properly trimmed to match the actual sequences used in protein expression. The sequence info of the A1 domain (Q1238-T1468), WT A2 (G1484-I1677), and CC-AA A2 (G1484-G1674 with C1669A and C1670A mutations) or the GPIbα LBD was submitted to AlphaFold Server, a web-service platform powered by the latest AlphaFold3 model. The molecule type was set to “protein”, no post-translational modification was set, and the seed number was set to auto. Once the AlphaFold3 finished the prediction, files were downloaded to local and analyzed via PyMOL3. Predicted models were first renumbered if needed by using renumber, an open-source Python script developed by Thomas Holder (2012). Models were then processed by using InterfaceResidues, an open-source Python script developed by Jason Vertrees (2009), to obtain a group of residues located in the interacting interface between two proteins. Potential polar contacts, salt bridges, and π – -π/π-cation interactions were found and marked by PyMOL3. Proper labeling, color change, and image output were proceeded with PyMOL3.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation or the fitted peak values ± standard error, as described in each figure legend. The number of unfolding or refolding events used in fitting is also indicated in the relevant figure legends. For MST experiments, the measurement was repeated at least 4 times, with each repetition considered a replicate. Relevant fitting parameters for single-molecule optical tweezer experiments are described above.
