Association Between Serum Atherogenic Index and Cardiovascular Disease in Early Adulthood
Authors: Mianwang He, Nana Yin, Chi Wang, Zekun Feng, Shouling Wu, Hao Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated as log(triglycerides/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol), is a novel marker of atherogenic risk, but its association with cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality in early adulthood remains unclear.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate whether AIP independently predicts the risk of CVD (including stroke) and all-cause mortality in young adults.MethodsWe analyzed 64,416 participants aged 18-44 years without prior CVD from the Kailuan Study (2006-2016). AIP was calculated at baseline, and participants were categorized into quartiles. The primary outcomes were incident CVD, stroke, and all-cause mortality, ascertained through December 2023. Multivariable Cox models adjusted for traditional risk factors, lipids, renal function, and medication use were used to estimate HRs.ResultsOver a mean follow-up of 14.65 years (Q1-Q3: 10.60-16.90 years), 1,853 CVD events, 1,571 stroke events, and 1,332 cases of all-cause mortality occurred. After full adjustment, compared with the lowest AIP quartile (Q1), the HRs for CVD in Q2-Q4 were 1.16 (95% CI: 0.99-1.36), 1.29 (95% CI: 1.11-1.50), and 1.36 (95% CI: 1.17-1.59), respectively. Corresponding HRs for stroke were 1.14 (95% CI: 0.97-1.35), 1.21 (95% CI: 1.03-1.43), and 1.26 (95% CI: 1.07-1.49). No significant association was found between AIP and all-cause mortality after multivariable adjustment. Sensitivity and competing risk analyses supported the robustness of these findings.ConclusionsHigher AIP is independently associated with increased risks of CVD and stroke in young adults. AIP may serve as a useful biomarker for early cardiovascular risk stratification in this population.

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD), primarily comprising stroke and ischemic heart disease, remain the leading cause of global mortality, accounting for approximately one-third of all deaths worldwide.1 Although significant progress has been made in assessing traditional risk factors, residual risk has driven the exploration of novel biomarkers.2, 3, 4, 5 The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated as the logarithmic ratio of triglycerides (TG) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, is considered a promising indicator of atherogenic dyslipidemia and cardiovascular risk,6 and is correlated with diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and metabolic syndrome.7, 8, 9 Moreover, AIP reflects pathophysiological disturbances in lipid metabolism,10,11 thereby offering insights beyond conventional lipid parameters. Although previous studies on the association between AIP and CVD were mainly based on populations at high risk, such as individuals with diabetes12, 13, 14 and nondiabetic hypertensive elderly,15 evidence in the general population remains limited,16,17 and is particularly scarce in the population of early adulthood. Furthermore, given the increasing recognition that early exposure to cardiovascular risk factors significantly affects later pathogenesis of CVD,18, 19, 20, 21 the identification of young adults at risk is crucial. Although clinical manifestations of CVD typically emerge after age 45 years,21 the atherosclerotic process begins decades earlier. In fact, lipid monitoring before age 40 years identifies a majority of individuals with a high likelihood of lifetime elevated lipid levels who consequently have a high long-term risk for CVD.22 Therefore, we investigated whether an elevated AIP before age 45 years independently predicts future CVD, stroke, and all-cause mortality.

Methods

Study participants

The Kailuan Study is an ongoing prospective cohort study based in Tangshan, China. Conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kailuan General Hospital. The cohort consists of employees and retirees of the Kailuan Group, a coal mining company located in Tangshan. Biennial health examinations including all cohort participants were conducted since 2006. All study participants provided informed consent. Detailed descriptions of the study design and procedures have been published previously.23,24

For this analysis, we initially included 66,012 adults aged 18 to 44 years who underwent their baseline health examinations at the Kailuan General Hospital between 2006 and 2016. After excluding 216 participants with pre-existing cardiovascular diseases and 1,380 with missing AIP data, a total of 64,416 participants were eligible for the current analysis. Measurements of AIP and other covariates

Baseline data were collected using standardized questionnaires, anthropometric measurements, and laboratory blood tests. All participants provided fasting blood samples after an overnight fast during each physical examination. Blood samples were analyzed on the same day using an auto-analyzer (Hitachi 747; Hitachi). The biochemical parameters assessed included fasting blood glucose (FBG), TG, total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), serum creatinine, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (Hs-CRP). The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) was calculated as log10[TG/HDL-C]. Body mass index (BMI) was computed as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Smoking status was categorized as ever-smokers (those with a history of smoking or current smokers) and never-smokers (those with no history of smoking and not current smokers). Drinking status was similarly categorized as ever-drinkers (those with a history of alcohol consumption or current drinkers) and never-drinkers (those with no history of alcohol consumption and not current drinkers). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation.25 Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, use of antihypertensive drugs, or self-reported history of physician-diagnosed hypertension. Diabetes was defined as FBG ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), or treatment with antidiabetic drugs, or self-reported physician-diagnosed type 2 diabetes.

Definition of outcomes

Follow-up time was calculated from the date of the baseline health examination at Kailuan General Hospital (2006-2016) until the date of the first occurrence of a primary outcome event (incident CVD, stroke, or death), or until the end of the follow-up period (December 31, 2023), whichever came first. Participants who were lost to follow-up or did not experience any event by the end of the study were censored at the date of their last known contact or at the administrative end of follow-up, respectively. No additional truncation of follow-up time was applied beyond this predefined endpoint. CVD cases included myocardial infarction (MI), ischemic stroke (IS), and hemorrhagic stroke (HS). These events were identified using the following International Classification of Diseases-Tenth Revision codes: I21 for MI, I63 for IS, and I60-I61 for HS. Data on CVD diagnoses were obtained from the Municipal Social Insurance Institution and the Hospital Discharge Register and were updated annually throughout the follow-up period. An expert panel collected and reviewed annual discharge records from 11 local hospitals to identify patients with suspected CVD. Myocardial infarction was diagnosed according to the World Health Organization’s Multinational Monitoring of Trends and Determinants in Cardiovascular diseases (MONICA) criteria,26 based on clinical symptoms, electrocardiographic findings, and dynamic changes in myocardial enzyme levels. Stroke was diagnosed in accordance with the World Health Organization criteria,27 involving neurological signs, clinical symptoms, and neuroimaging results—such as computed tomography or cardiac magnetic resonance. All-cause mortality data were collected from provincial vital statistics offices and reviewed by physicians.

Statistical analysis

Participants were stratified into quartiles (Q1-Q4) based on their AIP values. Continuous variables were compared using analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate for their distribution, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. The proportion of missing data was <10% for all variables, and the data were not missing completely at random; therefore, multiple imputation was used to handle missing values. Incidence rates of CVD, stroke, and all-cause mortality were calculated per 1,000 person-years across AIP quartiles. We used Cox regression28 to calculate HRs with 95% CIs for CVD, stroke, and all-cause mortality among participants in higher AIP quartiles (Q2-Q4), using the lowest quartile (Q1) as the reference group. In the analysis, Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, the year of enrollment, body mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, systolic blood pressure, LDL-C, Hs-CRP, FBG, and eGFR; and Model 3, based on Model 2, further adjusted for the use of oral antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, and lipid-lowering drugs. Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to compare cumulative incidence of CVD, stroke, and all-cause mortality across AIP quartiles. Additionally, we calculated HRs for specific CVD subtypes, including MI, IS, and HS. In addition, the proportional hazards assumption of the Cox models was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. For covariates that violated this assumption, time-dependent interaction terms between the covariates and follow-up time were introduced in sensitivity analyses (Supplemental Table 1).

To test the robustness of our findings, we further conducted 5 sensitivity analyses. First, to assess the potential impact of missing data on the study results, sensitivity analyses were performed using both the original data set (with missing values) and the data set after imputing missing data. Second, a comparison of included vs excluded participants was performed. Third, we excluded outcome events occurring within the first 2 years of the follow-up period to minimize potential reverse causation. Fourth, to minimize the influence of treatment on our results, we excluded users of antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, or lipid-lowering drugs. Fifth, in light of the competitive risk of death, a competing risks model was utilized. Specifically, we used the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model to account for competing risks when estimating the association between the exposure and the study outcome. Finally, to assess whether the association between the exposure and outcomes varied across different subgroups (eg, sex, BMI, or other stratification variables), we introduced multiplicative interaction terms into the Cox proportional hazards models. These terms were constructed as the product of the exposure variable and each subgroup indicator. A P value for interaction was calculated using the Wald test, with a significance threshold of P<0.05 indicating potential effect modification.

To assess the relationship between AIP and the risk of CVD, we evaluated predictive performance using the C-statistic, integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and net reclassification index (NRI) to predict the risk of CVD, with the conventional China-PAR model29 as the reference. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell's c-statistic (with 95% CIs). Incremental value was evaluated using IDI and continuous NRI, with 95% CIs estimated via bootstrap (1,000 replications) and P values from hypothesis tests. Decision curve analysis was used to evaluate the clinical utility of the AIP by quantifying net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities for predicting CVD. Predictive performance within the 10-year horizon of the China-PAR model was further examined by excluding CVD events occurring beyond 10 years of follow-up.

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and R software version 3.6.0 (R Core Team). All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P <0.05 was considered statistically significant. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kailuan General Hospital (approval number: 2006-5). We obtained written informed consent from all participants.

Results

Compared with the included participants, the excluded participants were significantly older (57.81 ± 8.95 years vs 34.38 ± 7.14 years; P < 0.0001) and had a higher proportion of men (80.17% [49,793 of 64,416] vs 77.30% [82,588 of 100,716]; P < 0.0001). Additionally, the excluded participants exhibited more adverse cardiometabolic profiles, including higher FBG, TG, TC, and Hs-CRP levels, as well as greater BMI, waist circumference, and blood pressure. They also had a higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes. Furthermore, the excluded participants had lower renal function (eGFR: 83.23 ± 17.64 mL/min/1.73 m2 vs 100.77 ± 20.96 mL/min/1.73 m2; P < 0.0001) (Supplemental Table 2).

The missing rates were 0% for age and sex, 2.44% for BMI, 2.87% for eGFR, 7.29% for heart rate, 1.69% for systolic blood pressure, 1.67% for diastolic blood pressure, 0.56% for FBG, 0% for TG and HDL-C, 0.07% for TC, 0.33% for LDL-C, 7.66% for C-reactive protein, 8.37% for hip circumference, and 8.02% for waist circumference.

Incidence of CVD, stroke, and all-cause mortality by AIP quartile in early adulthood

As shown in the Kaplan-Meier curves in the Central Illustration, the cumulative incidence of CVD and stroke increased progressively across ascending AIP quartiles. Relationship between AIP and the risks for CVD

Compared with the first quartile (Q1), the age- and sex-adjusted HRs for CVD were significantly elevated in Q2 (HR: 1.29; 95% CI: 1.10-1.50), Q3 (HR: 1.53; 95% CI: 1.32-1.78), and Q4 (HR: 1.81; 95% CI: 1.56-2.08). A similar increasing trend was observed for stroke: Q2 (HR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.06-1.47), Q3 (HR: 1.42; 95% CI: 1.21-1.67), Q4 (HR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.42-1.94). For all-cause mortality, significantly increased HRs were observed in Q3 and Q4 relative to Q1: Q2 (HR: 1.03; 95% CI: 0.87-1.23), Q3 (HR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.02-1.42), Q4 (HR: 1.34; 95% CI: 1.14-1.57). Following multivariate adjustment for age, sex, the year of enrollment, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, systolic blood pressure, LDL-C, Hs-CRP, FBG, eGFR, use of lipid-lowering drugs, use of hypoglycemic drugs, and use of antihypertensive drugs, a significant and graded increase in risk persisted for both CVD (including stroke) across higher AIP quartiles. The fully adjusted HRs for CVD were as follows: Q2 (HR: 1.16; 95% CI: 0.99-1.36); Q3 (HR: 1.29; 95% CI: 1.11-1.50), Q4 (HR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.17-1.59). Similarly, the adjusted HRs for stroke were Q2 (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 0.97-1.35), Q3 (HR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.03-1.43), Q4 (HR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.07-1.49) (Table 2). In contrast, no significant association was observed between AIP quartiles and all-cause mortality after full adjustment.

Sensitivity analysis yielded results consistent with the primary analysis, whether using the original data set (with missing values) (Supplemental Table 3) or the imputed data set (Table 2). Consistent findings were also observed after excluding cardiovascular events occurring within 2 years, excluding the use of oral antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and glucose-lowering medications, and applying a competing risk model for mortality (Supplemental Table 4).

Stratified analyses indicated that BMI and hypertension significantly modified the association between AIP and all-cause mortality (P for interaction = 0.01 and 0.04, respectively). In contrast, no significant interaction impact was observed for the other stratification variables (all P for interaction >0.05) (Supplemental Tables 5 to 7).

Incremental predictive value of AIP

We incorporated LDL-C and AIP separately into the conventional China-PAR prediction model. Adding AIP yielded marginal improvements in model discrimination for CVD with the C-index increasing from 0.7921 (original model) and 0.7922 (LDL-C–enhanced model) to 0.7939 (AIP-enhanced model)—representing absolute increases of 0.0018 and 0.0017, respectively. The IDI showed similarly modest gains of 0.000505 and 0.000239. However, the category-free NRI was negative (−10.88%) compared with the original model, although it showed a positive improvement of 11.21% relative to the LDL-C–enhanced model (Supplemental Table 8). To evaluate the predictive performance specifically within the intended 10-year prediction horizon of the China-PAR model, analyses were repeated after excluding CVD events occurring beyond 10 years. Under this restriction, the baseline China-PAR model showed a C-index of 0.7894 (95% CI: 0.7801-0.7987). Adding LDL-C yielded a C-index of 0.7895 (95% CI: 0.7802-0.7988), whereas the addition of AIP improved the C-index to 0.7910 (95% CI: 0.7817-0.8003). Consistent with the primary analysis, the addition of both LDL-C and AIP significantly improved model reclassification and discrimination. Specifically, LDL-C yielded a category-free NRI of −0.2946 (P < 0.0001) and an IDI of 0.000115 (P < 0.0001), while AIP showed a category-free NRI of −0.1874 (P = 0.0008) and an IDI of 0.000281 (P = 0.0002) (Supplemental Table 9). Discussion

This large prospective cohort study establishes a significant and independent association between elevated AIP and an increased risk of incident CVD and stroke in adults aged 18 to 44 years. To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify AIP as an early metabolic predictor of cardiovascular diseases in young adults, demonstrating a graded dose-response relationship.

The robustness of our findings was supported by consistent results across multiple sensitivity analyses, including the use of original vs imputed data sets; exclusion of early cardiovascular events; adjustment for antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and glucose-lowering medications; and application of a competing risk model for mortality. Stratified analyses showed that BMI and hypertension significantly modified the association between AIP and all-cause mortality, highlighting the importance of considering these clinical characteristics in risk stratification. Although excluded participants had older age, were more often men, and had worse cardiometabolic profiles, which may have introduced selection bias, the consistent findings across sensitivity analyses support the validity of our conclusions.

However, despite the robust association, the incremental predictive value of AIP beyond established risk models was modest. When evaluated for its incremental predictive value, adding AIP to the China-PAR model resulted in a slight improvement in the C-index (from 79.21% to 79.39%) and IDI, although the category-free NRI was negative. The DCA results further support its potential value in clinical decision-making. These findings indicate that while AIP is associated with ASCVD risk at a population level, its ability to improve individual risk stratification beyond established models remains uncertain. Further studies in diverse populations are needed to clarify the potential role of AIP in clinical risk prediction.

Nevertheless, the limitations in incremental predictive value do not diminish the potential importance of AIP as an early risk marker in young adults. Our findings extend previous research on the association between AIP and cardiovascular risk, previously established in diabetic patients,14 nondiabetic hypertensive older adults,15 healthy adults,30 and the general population,16,17 to young adults. Adjusting for conventional risk factors, lipids, and renal function, the robust and graded increase of CVD (including stroke) risk observed across AIP quartiles in this demographic persisted, highlighting the value of AIP in detecting atherogenic dyslipidemia at an early stage. The underlying pathological mechanism of this association is atherosclerosis, because most CVD events usually begin to progress asymptotically years before the clinical onset.31,32 Therefore, AIP measured in young adulthood may reflect the cumulative burden and progression of subclinical atherosclerosis, supporting it as a reliable marker of atherosclerotic development and a practical tool for early CVD risk stratification. This association was particularly pronounced in the highest AIP quartile, which exhibited a substantially elevated risk of CVD. A similar trend was observed for stroke. Furthermore, subtype analyses revealed consistent associations between elevated AIP and ischemic events (MI and IS), but not with HS, further supporting a pathophysiology rooted in atherosclerotic progression. This association is biologically plausible, further supported by the established correlation between AIP and atherogenic small dense LDL particles.33 Functionally, AIP reflects lipoprotein particle size and the balance between proatherogenic and antiatherogenic lipoproteins, making it a clinically useful surrogate marker for small dense LDL34 and a potent predictor of major adverse cardiovascular events.12,15 Moreover, the association of AIP with carotid intima-media thickness,35 arterial stiffness,36 and coronary artery atherosclerosis37 further strengthens its pathophysiological relevance and confirms its role as a reliable predictor of atherosclerosis progression. The absence of a significant association between AIP and all-cause mortality may reflect the young cohort and limited follow-up; however, the modifying effects of BMI and hypertension suggest that the prognostic value of AIP varies by adiposity and blood pressure status.

Our study possesses notable strengths. We demonstrated a significant association between AIP in early adulthood and subsequent risks of CVD and stroke by adjusting multiple confounding variables and conducting subgroup analyses. Therefore, early-life AIP assessment may help identify individuals with elevated cardiovascular risk before the clinical onset of disease.

Study limitations

First, the generalizability of our findings may be limited, because the study cohort consisted exclusively of Chinese adults, and the results may not apply to other ethnic populations. Second, although we observed significant associations between AIP and cardiovascular outcomes, the number of incident events was relatively modest given that our study population consisted of young adults without pre-existing CVD. This may have resulted in insufficient statistical power for some subgroup analyses or for detecting smaller effect sizes. Third, AIP was calculated using lipid parameters measured only at baseline. As such, we were unable to account for potential changes in AIP over time or examine how longitudinal trajectories of AIP may influence future cardiovascular risk. Fourth, despite the observed statistical association between AIP and CVD risk, its incremental predictive value beyond the established China-PAR model was modest. In particular, the negative NRI suggests that adding AIP did not meaningfully improve individual risk classification. Therefore, the clinical utility of AIP for guiding preventive interventions remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

An elevated AIP increased the risk of CVD including stroke, in young adults. AIP may be a biomarker for identifying young adults at a high risk of CVD. Further prospective studies are needed to investigate the impact of AIP on cardiovascular events.
