Alpha-thalassaemia: from regional burden to global priority
Authors: Ali Amid, Duantida Songdej, Adisak Tantiworawit, Pimlak Charoenkwan
Published: 2026

The 40th Congress of the International Society of Haematology (ISH 2026), held jointly with the Thai Society of Haematology in Bangkok on May 17–19, 2026, provided a rare international haematology forum in which α-thalassaemia was prominently highlighted with focused discussions on the epidemiology, clinical burden, and future management of this condition.

α-thalassaemia is among the most common inherited blood disorders worldwide, with an estimated 20% of the global population carrying an α+-thalassaemia allele resulting from deletion of a single α-globin gene.1 Its distribution reflects evolutionary selection in malaria-endemic regions, where carrier states confer partial protection against severe malaria. As a result, α-thalassaemia is highly prevalent across Africa, the Mediterranean, West Asia, the Indian subcontinent, and Southeast Asia.2 Although migration has broadened its global relevance, the clinical burden remains disproportionately concentrated in Southeast Asia, driven by the high prevalence of α0-thalassaemia deletions (involving both α-globin genes in cis), with carrier rates exceeding 5% in some areas of Thailand, as well as regionally prevalent non-deletional α-globin mutations (e.g. haemoglobin Constant Spring).3 Together, these variants contribute to a high burden of clinically significant α-thalassaemia, including haemoglobin H (HbH) disease and haemoglobin Bart's hydrops fetalis (α-thalassaemia major). Despite the substantial global and regional burden, α-thalassaemia continues to receive less attention than β-thalassaemia, reflecting persistent gaps in awareness, research prioritisation, and resource allocation.4

This under-recognised disorder includes a wide spectrum of disease manifestations, from mild haematological changes to foetal death. Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis syndrome, the most severe phenotype of α-thalassaemia, remains the leading cause of non-immune hydrops fetalis in Southeast Asia.5 Earlier, it was considered universally fatal in utero; however, advances in intrauterine transfusion have enabled survival into infancy and adulthood. There is a disproportionately high regional distribution, with an estimated 500 affected pregnancies occurring annually in Thailand, with even higher numbers in southern China.6 Homozygosity for haemoglobin Constant Spring can result in severe foetal anaemia and hydrops fetalis, but the postnatal disease course can be generally mild following intrauterine transfusion. Another form, HbH disease, is considerably more prevalent and is often considered relatively mild; however, growing evidence suggests significant cumulative morbidity across the lifespan.7

Epidemiological data on α-thalassaemia remain limited in Southeast Asia. The disorder encompasses α+ and α0 deletions as well as non-deletional variants that interact to produce a broad phenotypic spectrum. In Southeast Asia, the frequent co-inheritance of haemoglobin E and β-thalassaemia further complicates diagnosis and clinical management.8 In addition, some carriers may have normal or only subtly abnormal haematological indices, contributing to missed diagnoses and inaccurate estimates of disease burden. Without robust epidemiological data and rational screening strategies, equitable allocation of healthcare resources remains constrained.

Screening represents one of the most effective approaches to reducing disease burden, and early identification of carriers of α0-thalassaemia is essential to preventing severe disease, including Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis. Although some countries of the region have established prenatal and neonatal screening programmes, many at-risk populations remain underserved due to inconsistent implementation. Misdiagnosis as iron deficiency anaemia is common because of overlapping laboratory findings and limited access to molecular diagnostics, particularly in rural and resource-limited settings. Effective screening also requires access to genetic counselling to support interpretation of test results and informed reproductive decision-making, yet these services remain limited across much of Southeast Asia because of workforce and infrastructure constraints.9 Integrating α-thalassaemia screening into maternal and child health programmes, particularly neonatal screening through detection of Hb Bart’s, offers a pragmatic and potentially cost-effective strategy, but requires sustained policy commitment and investment in laboratory infrastructure and workforce capacity.

Important gaps also remain in clinical management and research. Much of the evidence base is derived from retrospective or cross-sectional studies, with limited representation of diverse genotypes and ageing populations. The natural history of HbH disease remains incompletely defined, particularly in older adults, while distinctions between deletional and non-deletional forms are often inadequately captured in clinical studies. At the same time, patients with α-thalassaemia remain underrepresented in clinical trials of emerging disease-modifying therapies. Curative approaches, including haematopoietic stem cell transplantation and gene-based therapies, also remain insufficiently studied in this population, raising concerns regarding equitable access to innovation.10,11

Addressing these challenges will require sustained advocacy, investment, and stronger alignment between regional expertise and global health priorities (Table 1). α-thalassaemia is not simply a regional disorder, but a global condition whose clinical and scientific centre of gravity lies in Southeast Asia. The imperative now is to ensure that regional expertise is matched by equitable global collaboration, sustained investment, and a shared commitment to translating knowledge into improved outcomes.
