Burgers equation from non-thermal stationary states in nearly-integrable gases
Paweł Lisiak, Maciej Łebek, Miłosz Panfil

Abstract
When a gas of particles interacts with much a larger reservoir the dynamics of density on large scales is typically governed by diffusion. We study this paradigmatic problem for weakly coupled integrable systems and show that this picture is altered when transport is investigated on top of long-lived non-thermal states. Remarkably, for states non-invariant under parity we find Burgers equation arising in the hydrodynamic limit. We explicitly compute the diffusion constant and nonlinear Euler-scale coupling of the Burgers equation using a variant of the Chapman-Enskog theory. We find excellent agreement between our theory and numerical simulations of a simplified model of stochastic two-body collisions, which we call velocity swap models. Our conclusions are based only on Galilean invariance, existence of a small system-bath coupling parameter and a small momentum exchange between the system and the bath.

In this work we continue following that program and consider a canonical problem of diffusion of Galilean particles.
The mechanism for diffusion is provided by a long-range coupling to a much larger bath, also assumed to be described by a one-dimensional integrable model. As the particles of the smaller system, due to interactions, exchange momentum and energy with the bath, the only conserved quantity is their total number. On a phenomenological level, when the bath state is assumed to be thermal, the continuity equation for the density
\varrho(x,t)
, combined with the gradient expansion of the current, lead to the diffusion equation

\partial_{t}\varrho=\partial_{x}\left(\mathcal{D}(\varrho)\partial_{x}\varrho\right),

(1)

with the density dependent diffusion coefficient
\mathcal{D}(\varrho)
. The standard form of the diffusion equation is obtained by linearising the theory with the diffusion coefficient attaining a constant value. In such case the diffusion coefficient can be also computed through the Green-Kubo formula from the velocity autocorrelation function.

The diffusion equation can be also obtained from microscopic considerations based on the kinetic theory and Boltzmann collision operator
\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}},\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(b)}]
for system-bath interactions. Here
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(x,\lambda,t)
is one-body density function (
\lambda
labels momentum) and
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(b)}
denotes thermal state of the bath.
The density is
\varrho(x,t)=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(x,\lambda,t)
and under the assumption
\varrho\ll\varrho^{(b)}
the dynamics of the bath is effectively frozen out.
The diffusion coefficient can be computed with the Chapman-Enskog method, discussed later in the text. It is given by an integral equation formulated in terms of the collision operator
\mathcal{I}
.

Our results generalize in various way the diffusion equation (
1
). Firstly, the resulting diffusion coefficient incorporates in non-perturbative way the integrable interactions which extends the applicability of the standard kinetic theory methods to strongly correlated systems. Secondly, isolated integrable systems do not equilibrate to thermal states due to presence of an extensive number of conservation laws. While breaking the integrability through system-bath interactions ultimately thermalizes the system, if it is sufficiently weak it creates a time span in which the system might be in a prethermal quasi-stationary state
Łebek et al. 2024
;
Biagetti et al. 2024a
;
Tang et al. 2018c

\partial_{t}\varrho+u(\varrho)\partial_{x}\varrho=\partial_{x}\left(\mathcal{D}(\varrho)\partial_{x}\varrho\right),

(2)

where
u(\varrho)

1

We exemplify our approach by considering three different scenarios. First, we study a system of two coupled 1d Bose gases which mimics the experimental settings of cold atomic gases consisting of arrays of 1d systems with possibly tunable couplings
Tang et al. 2018b
. It is known that the collision integral for such systems supports athermal stationary states
Łebek et al. 2024
. This provides a concrete physical realisation for testing the ideas presented in this work. Next, we abstract from a concrete realization by considering a generic collision integral in the relaxation time approximation (RTA) with respect to athermal state and with particle number being the only conserved quantity. Within the RTA the Chapman-Enskog equation for the diffusion constant can be solved. This allows us to draw general conclusions for diffusion processes in such circumstances and show how Burgers equations arise for athermal and parity-breaking background states.

Finally, we consider a simple stochastic model of classical or quantum particles colliding with bath particles at constant rate. This serves as a minimal model in which all the phenomena seen in a more sophisticated situations are present but without technical complications. This allows us to pinpoint the sufficient conditions required to see the Burgers’ equations. At the same time this model can be simulated efficiently and we test the effective theory given by (
3

The paper is organized in the following way. In Section
II
we provide the necessary ingredients of the kinetic theory of nearly integrable system, specifically discuss in the details the GHD-Boltzmann equation and the generalized Gibbs ensemble formulation of the quasi-particle distribution function. In Section
III

IV
we use the Chapman-Enskog method to derive from the GHD-Boltzmann equation the Burgers equation. In Section
V

We compute the diffusion coefficient in the latter approximation and link it with thermodynamic properties of the gas also in athermal states. We also show how Burgers equation arises for states without parity invariance. In Section
VI
we demonstrate that our predictions match the microscopic simulations of the molecular dynamics. We relegated some more technical aspects of our work to the appendices, to which we refer when needed.

II
Preliminaries

We consider a system described by a Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{(b)}+\alpha\hat{H}_{\mathrm{pert}},

(4)

where
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
and
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{(b)}
are some integrable Hamiltonians modelling system and bath, respectively and

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{pert}}
is some small integrability-breaking perturbation
Hamiltonian (modelling system-bath interaction). The system is assumed to be Galilean-invariant.
We consider only the case when
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{pert}}
does not depend on position and time explicitly.
The dynamics of nearly-integrable models, after the initial decoherence phase, is effectively described by a kinetic theory
Tang et al. 2018c
;
Biagetti et al. 2024a
;
Durnin et al. 2021b
;
Panfil et al. 2023
. This kinetic theory is formulated in terms of the space-time dependent distribution function of quasi-particles
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(x,\lambda,t)
with quasi-momentum (rapidity)
\lambda
. To understand the basics of quasiparticle description we review first the thermodynamic Bethe ansatz (TBA) and GHD of integrable models.

II.1
Basics of TBA and GHD

The integrable systems are characterized by infinite number of local conserved charges
\hat{Q}_{k}
fulfilling
[H_{\rm int},\hat{Q}_{k}]=0
.
Their eigenvalues for a state described by quasiparticle distribution
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}
are

Q_{k}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{k}(\lambda)\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(\lambda).

(5)

For Galilean invariant models of particles with unit mass, as is assumed here, it is convenient to choose
h_{k}(\lambda)=\lambda^{k}/k!
which implies that
\hat{Q}_{k}
are ultra-local charges. Specifically, for total momentum
h_{1}(\lambda)=\lambda
and for total energy
h_{2}(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}/2
. The quasiparticle picture is based upon the assumption of relaxation to Generalized Gibbs Ensemble (GGE)
Yang and Yang 1969
;
Mossel and Caux 2012
with the density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{\rm GGE}\propto\exp(-\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\hat{Q}_{j})
, where
\beta_{j}
are Lagrange multipliers.

We introduce now more ingredients of the TBA in simlar way as presented in
Doyon 2020
. We start with free energy density
\mathsf{f}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int{\rm d}\lambda F[\epsilon(\lambda)]
, where
\epsilon(\lambda)
is pseudoenergy and function
F
depends on particles’ statistics (for fermionic particles
F[\epsilon(\lambda)]=-\log(1+e^{-\epsilon(\lambda)})
). The pseudoenergy is determined from the following equation

\epsilon(\lambda)=\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)+\int{\rm d}\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})F[\epsilon(\lambda^{\prime})],

(6)

with bare pseudoenergy given by
\epsilon_{0}(\lambda)=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}h_{j}(\lambda)
. The set
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=0,1,\ldots}
specifies the state. From pseudoenergy we can compute statistical factor
f(\lambda)=({\rm d}^{2}F/{\rm d}\epsilon^{2})/({\rm d}F/{\rm d}\epsilon)
and occupation function
n={\rm d}F/{\rm d}\epsilon
defined also as
n=\rho_{\mathrm{p}}/\rho_{\rm t}
, where
\rho_{\rm t}
is the total density of states. It fulfills the following integral equation

\rho_{\rm t}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}+\int{\rm d}\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(\lambda^{\prime}).

(7)

Next, we introduce dressing operations. The small dressing is defined through integral equation

g^{\rm dr}=g+\mathcal{T}n\cdot g^{\rm dr}.

(8)

The big Dressing is the result of the action of the operator
\mathbb{F}
,
g^{\rm Dr}=\mathbb{F}^{t}g
with

\mathbb{F}(\lambda,\mu)=\delta(\lambda-\mu)+\partial_{\lambda}\left(n(\lambda)F(\lambda|\mu)\right).

(9)

Above we introduced backflow function
F(\lambda|\mu)

Korepin et al. 1993
. The two dressings are related

\left(g^{\rm Dr}\right)^{\prime}=(g^{\prime})^{\rm dr}.

(10)

For future reference we introduce Dressed momentum
k(\lambda)=(h_{1})^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)
and energy
\omega(\lambda)=(h_{2})^{\rm Dr}(\lambda)
.

We move on and discuss ingredients of GHD
Doyon 2020
. The effective velocity
v
of quasiparticles obeys the integral equation

v(\lambda)=h_{1}(\lambda)+2\pi\int{\rm d}\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(\lambda^{\prime})\left(v(\lambda^{\prime})-v(\lambda)\right).

(11)

Alternatively, the effective velocity can be written as
v(\lambda)=(h_{1})^{\rm dr}(\lambda)/(h_{0})^{\rm dr}(\lambda)
. For later use it will be convenient to introduce kernels of hydrodynamic matrices: flux Jacobian

\mathcal{A}=(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1}v(1-n\mathcal{T})^{-1},

(12)

along with charge and current susceptibility matrices

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\left(1-n\mathcal{T}\right)^{-1}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}f\left(1-\mathcal{T}n\right)^{-1},

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=\left(1-n\mathcal{T}\right)^{-1}v\,\rho_{\mathrm{p}}f\left(1-\mathcal{T}n\right)^{-1}.

(14)

The last ingredient is the diffusion operator, which we denote by
\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}=\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}(\lambda,\mu;x,t)
. It is state-dependent and expressible through objects already introduced. For specific form of
\mathfrak{D}
and discussion of its properties we refer to Appendix
C.1
.

II.2
GHD-Boltzmann equation

The dynamics of integrable systems in the presence of integrability-breaking effects is given by GHD-Boltzmann equation

\partial_{t}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}+\partial_{x}(v_{\rho}\,\rho_{\mathrm{p}})=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}\partial_{x}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}\right)+\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}].

(15)

where
\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}]
is the collision operator responsible for interaction between the observed system
\hat{H}_{\rm int}
and bath
\hat{H}^{(b)}_{\rm int}
. We assume that the bath system is much larger and in consequence, does not evolve in time. Equation (
15
) describes the system only and thus does not conserve momentum and energy. However, it does conserve particle number which is reflected in collision integral

\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{0}(\lambda)\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}]=0.

(16)

We also assume that
\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}]
has a family of stationary states
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\rm st}
parametrised by their density
\varrho
. We will give explicit examples of collision integrals with such properties in the following section.

III
Athermal stationary states

The aim of this section is to provide a construction of collision integrals that support athermal stationary states and to expose relevant physics behind them. We can focus here on the homogeneous systems. The generalization to inhomogeneous systems follows then from general arguments of separation of scales.

Athermal states relevant to the present context were first found in the setup of coupled Lieb-Liniger gases
Łebek et al. 2024
. In this work, thanks to the recent development of gBBGKY
Biagetti et al. 2024a
, we present arguments which hold for arbitrary weakly coupled integrable systems. The gBBGKY assumes that the system and bath interact through long-range density-density interaction given by

V(x)=\frac{V_{0}}{\xi}\varphi\left(\frac{x}{\xi}\right),

(17)

where
\xi
is the characteristic range of the potential,
V_{0}
measures it’s strength and function
\varphi
gives the interaction profile. We will assume here that the bath does not evolve, which can be realized for instance by a large density imbalance between the two systems. The aforementioned kinetic framework predicts that the dynamics of the system is given by Boltzmann-like kinetic equation.

\partial_{t}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}=\mathcal{I}_{2p}+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{-1}),

(18)

where collision integral
\mathcal{I}_{2p}
describes two-particle scattering processes, which will be discussed in the following. There are important corrections, written above as
\mathcal{O}(\xi^{-1})
, which involve collision operators responsible for three and more particle collisions. These processes are relevant on timescales longer than
\mathcal{I}_{2p}
and eventually thermalize the system. In what follows, we neglect them focusing on timescales relevant for
\mathcal{I}_{2p}
. Let us also note that the same picture emerges from Fermi’s Golden Rule treatment of integrability breaking as developed in
Durnin et al. 2021b
;
Panfil et al. 2023
. There, the collsion operators involve terms with more and more particle collision processes, with the two-particle scatterings dominating in the system-bath setup.

To understand the simplest relevant physical scenario and minimal ingredients for athermal states let us abstract from the specific form of
\mathcal{I}_{2p}
arising from a microscopic theory. We start broadly and consider general Master equation for two-particle scatterings between classical system and classical bath

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}](\lambda)]

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}\lambda^{\prime}{\rm d}\lambda_{b}{\rm d}\lambda_{b}^{\prime}\mathcal{W}(\lambda,\lambda_{b};\lambda^{\prime},\lambda_{b}^{\prime})\delta(k)\delta(\omega)\times

(19)

\displaystyle\left(\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(\lambda)\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(b)}(\lambda^{\prime})-\rho(\lambda^{\prime})\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(b)}(\lambda)\right),

where conservation laws are imposed by Dirac deltas with
k=k(\lambda)+k_{b}(\lambda_{b})-k(\lambda^{\prime})-k_{b}(\lambda^{\prime}_{b})
and
\omega=\omega(\lambda)+\omega_{b}(\lambda_{b})-\omega(\lambda^{\prime})-\omega_{b}(\lambda^{\prime}_{b})
, where
k,k_{b}
are momenta of system and bath particles, the same goes for energies
\omega,\omega_{b}
. The quantity
\mathcal{W}(\lambda,\lambda_{b};\lambda^{\prime},\lambda_{b}^{\prime})
is naturally related to a rate of scattering
\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime}_{b}\to\lambda+\lambda_{b}
.

Importantly, we assume that dispersion relations in both systems are not necessarily the same (meaning
\omega_{b}\neq\omega
and
k_{b}\neq k
), which in the context of classical particles can be realized for instance by different masses, or different length of the rods (in the case of hard rod gas). Non-equal dispersions generically prohibit the existence of athermal state and thermal states are the only fixed points of (
19
).

The situation of equal dispersion happens when both gases consist of identical free classical particles
k(\lambda)=\lambda
and
\omega(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}/2
, the momentum-energy conservation laws imply that scattering amounts to permutation of velocities. We readily find the stationary state which is

\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\rm st}(\lambda)=\frac{\varrho}{\varrho^{(b)}}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(b)}(\lambda).

(20)

Obviously, if
\rho^{(b)}
is athermal then the state above is athermal as well. This is the simplest example of dynamics leading to non-thermal stationary state.

Remarkably, this changes in the
grazing limit
of the equation (
19
), which amounts to restricting possible processes to only these, where the momentum exchange is small. In that limit, the collision integral acquires the Landau form

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\rm gr}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}](\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\lambda}\int{\rm d}\lambda_{b}\tilde{W}(\lambda,\lambda_{b})\delta(v(\lambda)-v_{b}(\lambda_{b}))\times

(21)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(\lambda)\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(b)}(\lambda_{b})\left[\epsilon^{\prime}(\lambda)-\frac{k^{\prime}(\lambda)}{k^{\prime}_{b}(\lambda_{b})}(\epsilon^{(b)})^{\prime}(\lambda_{b})\right],

with
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}(\lambda,\lambda_{b})
directly related to collision rate
\mathcal{W}
.
The stationary state fulfils two sets of equations

v(\lambda)=v_{b}(\lambda_{b}),\quad\epsilon^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{k^{\prime}(\lambda)}{k^{\prime}_{b}(\lambda_{b})}\epsilon^{\prime}_{b}(\lambda_{b}).

(22)

This stationary state appears even though two-body scattering
does not
lead to simple momentum exchange. Let us stress that the solution to the equation above is athermal in the case when bath is not in a thermal state. In
Łebek et al. 2024
such athermal states were found in the setup, where both the system and bath are described by the Lieb-Liniger models.

In the generic interacting integrable models, the dispersion relation is a dynamic quantity, it depends on the state of the system which in turn reflects e.g. the interactions and the density. Therefore, two such systems that are weakly coupled naturally provide a physical realisation in which two body scatterings in (1+1)d lead to momentum redistribution. In this case we expect the stationary state described by
(

In what follows we present different treatments of weakly coupled integrable systems. We start with the most concrete case of coupled Lieb-Linger models, which can be studied using Fermi’s Golden Rule. This analysis is later generalized to arbitrary weakly coupled integrable models using the gBBGKY hierarchy. It also has the advantage of efficient numerical simulation and exact expressions for diffusion constants.

III.1
Fermi’s Golden Rule

We start with the Fermi’s Golden rule treatment of nearly integrable quantum models.
Within this approximation, the collision integral involves matrix elements of the perturbing operator. In our case this origins from the integrability breaking term in the Hamiltonian which we assume couples to the local particles’ density operator
\hat{q}_{0}(x)
. However, we can be slightly more general and assume that it couples to density
\hat{q}_{j}(x)
of any of the conserved charges
\hat{Q}_{j}=\int{\rm d}x\,\hat{q}_{j}(x)
.

The collision integral takes then the following form

\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\rm p}]=\mathbb{F}\mathcal{I}_{0}[\rho_{\rm p}],

(23)

where the back-flow operator
\mathbb{F}
describes the response of the whole state on the scattering process described by the bare collision integral
Q_{0}[\rho_{\rm p}]
. The latter is given by the usual form from the Master equation,

\mathcal{I}_{0}[\rho_{\rm p}](\lambda)=\int{\rm d}\mu\left[w(\lambda,\mu)\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(\mu)\rho_{\mathrm{h}}(\lambda)-(\lambda\leftrightarrow\mu)\right],

(24)

with the transition rate
w(\lambda,\mu)
. The transition rate is computed from the Fermi’s golden rule and involves matrix elements of the perturbing operator
\hat{q}_{j}(x)
. Typically, the transition rate obeys a detailed balance relation,

w(\lambda,\mu)=e^{-\beta(\omega(\lambda)-\omega(\mu))}w(\mu,\lambda),

(25)

and in such circumstances, the stationary state of (
24
) is thermal with inverse temperature
\beta
. Lack of the detailed balance relation open ways for athermal stationary states.

To illustrate a situation in which athermal stationary states exist we consider a two coupled 1d systems, see Fig.
1
with total Hamiltonian

H=H_{1}+H_{2}+V_{\rm pert}.

(26)

We assume that
H_{i}
are integrable, for example are given by the Lieb-Liniger model, and
V_{12}
breaks the integrability. In this formulation it is natural to treat system and bath on equal footing which is reflected in a slightly different notation than in eq. (
4
). Experimentally relevant choice of
V_{12}
is the long-range interaction potential between two tubes that couples to local densities

V_{\rm pert}=\int{\rm d}x_{1}{\rm d}x_{2}\,V(x_{1}-x_{2})\hat{q}_{i}(x_{1})\hat{q}_{j}(x_{2}).

(27)

In writing the perturbation term we allow for coupling between densities of different charges. The usual density-density interaction corresponds to
i=j=0
.

In the homogeneous system, the quasi-particles density in the two tubes evolves according to the set of equations

\partial_{t}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(1)}=\mathcal{I}_{12}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(1)},\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(2)}],\quad\partial_{t}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(2)}=\mathcal{I}_{21}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(2)},\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(1)}],

(28)

with the corresponding transition rates

\displaystyle w_{12}(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle=\hat{V}^{2}(k)|F_{i}^{(1)}(\lambda,\mu))|^{2}S_{j}^{(2)}(-k,-\omega),

(29)

\displaystyle w_{21}(\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle=\hat{V}^{2}(k)|F_{j}^{(2)}(\lambda,\mu))|^{2}S_{i}^{(1)}(-k,-\omega).

(30)

Here
F_{i}^{(1)}(\lambda,\mu)
is the one particle-hole form factor of the operator
\hat{q}_{i}
evaluated in the first tube. The rapidity of the particle and the hole are
\lambda
and
\mu
respectively. With
k\equiv k(\lambda)-k(\mu)
and
\omega\equiv\omega(\lambda)-\omega(\mu)
we denote momentum and energy of the particle-hole pair.
\hat{V}(k)
is the Fourier transform of the interaction potential from eq. (
27
). Finally,
S_{j}^{(2)}(k,\omega)
is the dynamic structure factor in the second tube, namely Fourier transform of the connected two point function
\langle\hat{q}_{j}^{(2)}(x,t)\hat{q}_{j}^{(2)}(0)\rangle^{c}
of density
\hat{q}_{j}
. In principle, there are also contributions to the transition rates that involve form-factors with larger number of particle-hole pairs. As shown in
Łebek et al. 2024
their contribution is subleading in the momentum transferred between the tubes and is important at larger time scales. Importantly, the higher particle-hole processes are responsible for ultimate thermalization of the system
Panfil et al. 2023
.

We consider now an imbalance limit in which density of particles in the second tube is much larger than in the first one. In the Boltzmann picture, the evolution proceeds as a succession of

Following

IV
Hydrodynamic equation

The effective hydrodynamic equation is obtained using the Chapman–Enskog method known from the kinetic theory
Résibois and De Leener 1977
. Before going into details, let us start with summarizing the most important results of this section.

The equation for density which we find on large scales in the most general case takes the form (
2
).
Expressions for the parameter
u
, related to the local center-of-mass velocity of the system, and the diffusion coefficient
\mathcal{D}(\varrho)
are

\displaystyle\begin{split}u(\varrho)=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{1}\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial\varrho},\quad\mathcal{D}(\varrho)=-\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{1}a(\lambda,\varrho).\end{split}

(40)

Here
a(\lambda,\varrho)
is a solution to the Chapman-Enskog equation detailed below. The parameter
u(\varrho)
vanishes for symmetric stationary states
\rho^{\rm st}
, leading to diffusion equation (
1
). For asymmetric state
\rho^{\rm st}
, which cannot be brought to symmetric states by Galilean boost, there are two cases. For classical statistics of system particles,
u(\varrho)=\text{const}
and it can be removed through boost. For quantum statistics,
u(\varrho)
is density-dependent, leading to Burgers equation.

We move now to the Chapman-Enskog method. In papers
Łebek and Panfil 2025
;
Łebek and Panfil 2025
similar computation is performed for nearly-integrable system with total number of particles, momentum and energy conserved.
At the foundations of the Chapman–Enskog method lies the separation of scales between the microscopic collision integral relaxation time
\tau_{\mathcal{I}}
and macroscopic relaxation due to
\partial_{x}\left(v_{\rho_{\mathrm{p}}}\,\rho_{\mathrm{p}}\right)
term in the GHD equation.
The separation of scales gives us a suitable small parameter in which various quantities can be expanded.
To accommodate the GHD diffusion in the (perturbative) Chapman–Enskog method we must additionally
assume separation between characteristic spatial scale of the GHD diffusion and the characteristic
spatial scale of
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}

Łebek and Panfil 2025
.
More precisely, we demand

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta_{\mathcal{I}}:=(\tau_{\mathcal{I}}\langle v\rangle)/\ell_{\mathrm{h}}\ll 1,\quad\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}:=\ell_{\mathrm{mic}}/\ell_{\mathrm{h}}\ll 1,\end{split}

(41)

where
\ell_{\rm h}
is macroscopic length characterizing spatial variations of
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}
and

\langle v\rangle
is the characteristic velocity of the state
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}
.
By
\ell_{\mathrm{mic}}
we denoted characteristic spatial scale of the GHD diffusion process.
The introduced small parameters are called Chapman–Enskog parameters.
In classical kinetic theory
\delta_{\mathcal{I}}
is known as Knudsen number.
For more discussion on the second Chapman–Enskog parameter we refer to
Łebek and Panfil 2025
. Another important aspect of the Chapman–Enskog method is the assumption that time enters

\rho_{\mathrm{p}}
only implicitly through the time-dependence of conserved charge, i.e.,

\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(\lambda,x,t)=\rho_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\lambda,x,\,\varrho\left(x,t\right)\right).

(42)

Solution to GHD-Boltzmann equation satisfying this condition is called a normal solution.

IV.1
Evaluation of Chapman–Enskog procedure

We start by integrating the GHD-Boltzmann equation over
{\rm d}\lambda
. As a result, we find the continuity equation for density

\partial_{t}\varrho+\partial_{x}j_{\rho}=0,

(43)

with the following particle current

j_{\rho}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,v_{\rho}\,\rho_{\mathrm{p}}=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{1}\,\rho_{\mathrm{p}},

(44)

where in the second equality we used that, for a Galilean invariant system,

satisfies integral equation (
11
), supplemented with a fact that

is symmetric

. In obtaining (

To see how Chapman–Enskog parameters (
41

the GHD-Boltzmann equation (
15

;
Łebek and Panfil 2025

Résibois and De Leener 1977

In the new variables equation (

\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{t}}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}+\partial_{\bar{x}}\left(\overline{v}_{\rho}\,\rho_{\mathrm{p}}\right)=\frac{\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}}{2}\partial_{\bar{x}}\left(\overline{\mathfrak{D}}_{\rho}\partial_{\bar{x}}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}\right)+\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathcal{I}}}\,\bar{\mathcal{I}}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}],

where
\overline{v}_{\rho}=v_{\rho}/\langle v\rangle
,
\overline{\mathfrak{D}}_{\rho}=\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}/(\ell_{\mathrm{mic}}\,\langle v\rangle)
and
\bar{\mathcal{I}}=\tau_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{I}
are respectively velocity, diffusion operator and collision integral in the natural units and
\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}=l_{\rm mic}/l_{h}
and
\delta_{Q}=\tau_{\mathcal{I}}\langle v\rangle/l_{h}
are two small parameters.
In what follows we drop
\bar{\cdot}
symbols from the notation and keep
\delta
parameters. After truncating to a given order, we will restore the original units.
Since we assumed
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}
to have the normal form (
42

43

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}}{\partial\varrho}&G+\partial_{x}\left(v_{\rho}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}\right)=\frac{\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}\partial_{x}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}\right)+\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathcal{I}}}\,\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}].\end{split}

(46)

In the equation above we introduced function

\partial_{t}\varrho=G(x,\varrho).

(47)

Solution of the Chapman-Enskog procedure will determine

in terms of
\varrho

Next we proceed with expansion in small Chapman–Enskog parameters (
41

According to the usual scheme, we keep the full dependence on
\delta_{\mathcal{I}}
and
\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}
in the conserved quantity
\varrho

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(i,j)}\,\delta_{\mathcal{I}}^{i}\,\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}^{j},\qquad G=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}G^{(i,j)}\delta_{\mathcal{I}}^{i}\,\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}^{j},\\
\end{split}

(48)

with
G^{(i,j)}=-\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{1}\,\partial_{x}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(i,j)}
.
For notational clarity we introduce symbol
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{n})
to mean all
\mathcal{O}(\delta_{\mathcal{I}}^{i}\,\delta_{\mathfrak{D}}^{j})
contributions such that
i+j=n
,

.
This notation becomes handy later.

In order to solve (
46
) perturbatively in Chapman–Enskog parameters we also have to expand
\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}]
,

and

Proceeding in analogy to (

Since they are all functionals of

and
\mathfrak{D}^{(i,j)}

,
since
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(0,0)}
is the only factor of order unity in the expansion of

.

Keeping full dependence on Chapman–Enskog parameters in

Since the only quantity of order unity in expansion of

These are the constitutive relations that uniquely determine

IV.2
Euler scale

At the leading order
\mathcal{O}\left(\delta_{\mathcal{I}}^{-1}\right)
, the GHD-Boltzmann equation (
15
) simplifies to

\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(0,0)}]=0.

(50)

This condition fixes a state in each fluid cell to be stationary.
From now on, we will denote the stationary state
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(0,0)}
as
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{st}}
.
In the present context, we assume that the stationary states are parametrized by their density. Therefore, equation (
50
) has a family of solutions
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{st}}(\lambda,\varrho)
where
\varrho=\int{\rm d}\lambda\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{st}}
.

To find the Euler scale equations we recall that the density
\varrho
varies in space and the quasi-particle distribution is given by
\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{st}}(\lambda,\varrho(x,t))
. We can evaluate now the leading contribution to
G
thus closing the continuity equation (
47
). We have

G^{(0,0)}=-\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{1}\,\partial_{x}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\rm st}=-u\partial_{x}\varrho,

(51)

where we used the chain rule and introduced

u=\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{1}\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial\varrho}.

(52)

Changing variables to the original, dimensionful, ones we arrive at
the Euler-scale evolution equation

\partial_{t}\varrho+u(\varrho)\partial_{x}{\varrho}=0.

(53)

Let us now gain some intuition on the velocity parameter
u
.
First of all it can be shown that under the Galilean boost with velocity
w
, it transforms indeed as velocity:
u\rightarrow u+w
. This renders the Euler-scale equation Galilean invariant as it should be.

Furthermore, for symmetric states (this includes thermal states)
u=0
. This follows from the observations that the derivative can be taken in front of the integral and that the first moment of the thermal distribution is
0
. A Galilean boost makes the first moment finite but density independent. Therefore, for standard collision integral with only thermal stationary term there is no dynamics at the Euler scale. A result known from the standard kinetic theory. However, for non-thermal stationary states
u
can be non-zero. This requires the stationary state to be asymmetric in
\lambda
and this asymmetry cannot be removed by a boost.

IV.3
Diffusive scale

Let us now turn to finding the first-order contribution to (
46
). There will be two contributions at order
\delta^{0}
from the collision integral

\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathcal{I}}}\mathcal{I}[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}]=\Gamma^{\mathrm{st}}\cdot\left[\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(1,0)}+\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{D}}}{\delta_{\mathcal{I}}}\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(0,1)}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta),

(54)

where we introduced linear operator

\Gamma^{\mathrm{st}}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{I}}{\delta\rho_{\mathrm{p}}}\Big|_{\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{st}}}.

(55)

At the same time the GHD diffusion does not contribute at this order. This gives then two equations

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathrm{st}}\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(1,0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\rho^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial\varrho}G^{(0,0)}+\frac{\partial\left(v^{\mathrm{st}}\rho^{\mathrm{st}}\right)}{\partial x},

(56)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathrm{st}}\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(0,1)}

\displaystyle=0.

(57)

The second equation implies that
\rho^{(0,1)}
is in the kernel of
\Gamma^{\mathrm{st}}
. This implies that
\rho^{(0,1)}=0
. We are left with the equation for
\rho^{(1,0)}
. We substitute for
G^{(0,0)}
and use the chain rule in the last term to obtain

\Gamma^{\mathrm{st}}\cdot\rho^{(1,0)}=\left(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{st}}-u\right)\cdot\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial\varrho}\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial x},

(58)

where we used that the hydrodynamic matrix
\mathcal{A}\equiv\mathcal{B}\mathcal{C}^{-1}
obeys
\delta(v\rho_{\mathrm{p}})=\mathcal{A}\cdot\delta\rho_{p}

Doyon and Spohn 2017
.
The integral equation (
58
) is simplified by the following ansatz

\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{(1,0)}(\lambda,\varrho(x))=a(\lambda,\varrho(x))\partial_{x}\varrho.

(59)

The resulting equation, free of explicit space-time dependence, is then

\Gamma^{\mathrm{st}}\cdot a=\left(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{st}}-u\right)\cdot\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\mathrm{st}}}{\partial\varrho}.

(60)

The constitutive relation (
49
) additionally implies

\int{\rm d}\lambda\,a(\lambda,\varrho)=0.

(61)

The resulting contribution to the hydrodynamic equation is
G^{(1,0)}=\partial_{x}\left(\mathcal{D}(\varrho)\partial_{x}\varrho\right)

where we defined the diffusion coefficient

\mathcal{D}(\varrho)=-\int{\rm d}\lambda h_{1}(\lambda)a(\lambda,\varrho).

(62)

By combining the Euler scale equation with the contribution from the diffusive scale, we finally obtain the Burgers (
2
).
The diffusion coefficient
\mathcal{D}(\varrho)
is invariant under the Galilean transformations, see Appendix
C.3
. Note that there is no contribution from the diffusion present in the GHD as
G^{(0,1)}=0
. This concludes the derivation of the hydrodynamic equation for density.

VII
Summary and discussion

In this work, we have considered a canonical problem of diffusion in which a minority species undergoes an irreversible motion due to coupling to the majority species whose dynamics is not monitored and which, as is customary to assume, is in a spatially homogenous state. Due to the conservation of particle number of the minority species, its density obeys a continuity equation. In the standard situation of classical particles this continuity equation leads to a (possibly non-linear) diffusion equation. In this work we identified the conditions under which the density follows instead the Burgers’ equation.

The two sufficient conditions are: a) athermal and asymmetric state of the majority species (bath) and b) quantum statistics of minority species (system).

We have demonstrated that these two conditions are fulfilled for effective dynamics of nearly integrable coupled gases. In such scenario, the integrability naturally makes athermal states possible. We have provided a kinetic explanation for appearance of such athermal states through limitations of two-body scattering in 1D. In our approach, we assume a large density imbalance between the two gases allowing for a separation of timescales between the slow dynamics in the dense tube (bath) and fast dynamics in the dilute tube (system). Starting from the GHD-Boltzmann description, we have derived an effective hydrodynamic equation for the density of the system particles (minority species). We have shown that the resulting equations become a standard diffusion equation when the bath is in a thermal state. Instead, for athermal state of a bath we obtain Burgers’ equation. Our method provides also expressions for transport coefficients: convective coefficient and diffusion constant.

To unravel the essential ingredients leading to the Burgers’ equation we have considered also a simple stochastic model of two-particle scatterings between system and the bath. We observed an excellent agreement between numerical simulations and hydrodynamics derived from kinetic equation describing such random scattering events.

Lastly, let us discuss the nature of the hydrodynamic density-density correlation function. Under the presence of ballistic mode with a nonlinear current the correlation function is expected to exhibit a breakdown of diffusive behavior at the largest scales
Spohn 2014
. Close to the integrability, we generically expect large diffusion constants, which push away the anomalous broadening to very large space-time scales. In this sense, we expect a scenario similar to the one analyzed in the context of Navier-Stokes equations
Łebek and Panfil 2025
. We also note that one has to be careful when looking at asymptotically large scales. There are three-particle collision processes, which provide a mechanism for thermalization at late times, assuming that the density of the bath is finite. This leads ultimately to a breakdown of Burgers equation.

Appendix B
Molecular dynamics simulation algorithm

To account for spatial locality of the collision integral (
93
) we begin by dividing the system into sufficiently small cells of equal length and consider each cell as a separate system, while allowing the particles to flow between different cells.
In the molecular dynamics simulation for the velocity-swap models we only scatter system particles with heat bath particles.
For the classical case, we have no restriction on the momenta of the scattered particles, so number of such collisions in each cell per unit time (rate) is

R=\frac{\gamma\cdot N\cdot N_{\rm bath}}{L/({\rm Number\;of\;cells})},

(118)

where
N
and
N_{\rm bath}
are respectively number of system particles and number of bath particles in the cell and
L
is total length of the system.
In the fermionic case we need to account for the Pauli exclusion principle, which prohibits scattering if we already have a particle with the same (outgoing) momentum.
Consequently, scattering rate needs to be modified for fermions:

R=\frac{\gamma\cdot N\cdot|\{v_{b,i}\notin V_{\rm system}:i\in\{1,...,N_{\rm bath}\}\}|}{L/({\rm Number\;of\;cells})},

(119)

where by
V_{\rm system}
we understand set of velocities of system particles in the cell and
v_{b,i}
denotes velocity of
i
-th bath particle in the cell.
For fermions we need to be careful, as dividing the system into cells we ran into a situation where the ballistic propagation of a particle can cause the exclusion principle to be violated.
For the simulation presented in this paper, we resolve this problem by artificially scattering particles violating the exclusion principle with the particles from the bath. For the parameters used for the simulation presented in Fig.
5
, the described situation is rare and we observed that no matter how this issue is resolved, density profiles do not change significantly.

In the simulations shown in Fig.
5
we performed the following Monte Carlo algorithm with a fixed time step, which we denote
\Delta t
:

1.

Segregate all system particles into cells of equal size.

2.

For each cell:

(a)

Compute rate
R
.

(b)

If we find exclusion principle to be violated (due to particle streaming), we scatter duplicates with the particles from the bath, so that there are no two particles with the same velocity in each cell.

(c)

Draw a random number from 0 to 1. If
R\cdot\Delta t
is greater then the drawn number, go to next cell.

(d)

Otherwise, randomly choose system particle and bath particle in the cell and set the momentum of the system particle to the momentum of the bath particle.

3.

Ballistically propagate system particles by a time
\Delta t
and go to the step 1.

C.1
Diffusion operator and its markovianity

We recall here the result of showing the GHD diffusion operator to be Markovian

(120)

(121)

(122)

with

C.2
Existence and uniqueness of the diffusion coefficient

The diffusion coefficient is expressed by the solution to the integral equation (
60
). We show here that the solution to this equation exists and is unique. This follows from the standard result in the theory of linear integral equations which we now recall following
Résibois and De Leener 1977
.

The equation in question is a linear inhomogeneous integral equation of the form

L\cdot a=b.

(126)

Let us assume that
L
is hermitian. The solution to such equation exists if
b
is orthogonal to all solutions
\phi_{j}
of the homogeneous equation

L\cdot\phi_{j}=0.

(127)

In the present context, there is only one solution,
\phi={\rm const}
, to the homogeneous equation
\Gamma^{\rm st}\cdot\phi=0
. It corresponds to the single conserved quantity, that is the particle number. Therefore, we need to check that

\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathcal{A}^{\rm st}\cdot\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\rm st}}{\partial\varrho}=u,

(128)

where we used that

\mathbf{1}\cdot\frac{\partial\rho_{\mathrm{p}}^{\rm st}}{\partial\varrho}=\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial\varrho}=1.

(129)

From the definitions of the hydrodynamic operator
\mathcal{A}
and the effective velocity we have

\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathcal{A}^{\rm st}=(h_{1}^{\prime})^{\rm dr}v_{\rho}\cdot(1-n\mathcal{T})=h_{2}^{\prime}=h_{1}.

(130)

In the second equality we used that
v_{\rho}=(h_{2}^{\prime})^{\rm dr}/(h_{1}^{\prime})^{\rm dr}
and that
(1-n\mathcal{T})
removes the dressing from the function that it acts upon. Recalling the definition (
52
) of
u
we confirm the sought after relation. This proves that if the operator
\Gamma^{\rm st}
is hermitian then there is always a solution to the equation specifying the diffusion coefficient. The operators
\Gamma^{\rm st}
obtained from the Fermi’s golden rule or from RTA are hermitian which concludes the existence part of the proof.

The constitutive relation (
61
) guarantees the uniqueness of the solution by removing from the space of solutions cases that differ by the kernel of
\Gamma^{\rm st}
.

C.3
Galilean transformations

The Galilean transformations describe the effect of the boost, by a constant velocity
u
, on the system. We denote with
bar
quantities referring to the system moving with velocity
u
with respect to the stationary one.

The quasi-particle distribution functions of the two systems obey
\bar{\rho}_{\rm p}(\lambda)=\rho_{\mathrm{p}}(\lambda-u)
.
The parameter
u
transforms as velocity under the boost,

\bar{u}=u+u,

(131)

as can be directly verified from its definition (
52
) and the transformation rule for the quasi-particles distributions. In a similar fashion we find transformation rules for the effective velocity and the flux Jacobian transform

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\rho}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=v_{\rho}(\lambda-u)+u,

(132)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\lambda-u,\lambda^{\prime}-u)+u.

This implies that the solutions Chapman-Enskog equation (
60
) in the moving and in the stationary system are related by
\bar{a}(\lambda)=a(\lambda-u)
. The diffusion coefficient in the moving system is then

\bar{\mathcal{D}}=-\int{\rm d}\lambda\,h_{1}(\lambda)\bar{a}(\lambda)=\mathcal{D}-\int{\rm d}\lambda\,a(\lambda).

(133)

The remaining integral is zero due to the constitutive relation (
61
). This shows that the diffusion coefficient is invariant under the Galilean transformations.

Additional numerical results and verifications

In this section we describe numerical computations of the diffusion
coefficient for the two density-imbalanced coupled Lieb–Liniger tubes.
First, note that the only non-trivial step needed to compute diffusion coefficient is
solving the integral equation (
60

The equation is an example of a Fredholm integral equation of the first kind.
A simple way to solve it is by choosing a basis and restricting it to
a finite dimension.
Using (
136

60

\tilde{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{st}}\cdot\alpha=\left(\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{st}}-u\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{st}}\right)\cdot\partial_{\varrho}\epsilon_{0},

(141)

where
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{St}}\cdot\alpha=a
.
Continuing, we write
\alpha

The basis is constructed by performing Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization on the ultra-local charge

Łebek and Panfil 2025

We will denote elements of the new basis as

Note, that from the construction procedure follows
\mathfrak{h}_{0}\sim h_{0}

Expanding

where Einstein’s summation convention is assumed.
Matrix elements are defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{st}}_{ij}&=\langle\mathfrak{h}_{i}|\tilde{\Gamma^{\mathrm{st}}}\mathfrak{h}_{j}\rangle,\\

Inverting the matrix

Because of the particle
