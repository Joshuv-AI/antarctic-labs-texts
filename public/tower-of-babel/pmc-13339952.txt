Long-term eculizumab treatment failure in C3 glomerulonephritis: two case reports
Authors: Dalia Zubidat, Maria Jose Soler, Anila Cara, Sanjeev Sethi, Gerald B Appel, Dominick Santoriello, Fernanda G Dos Santos, Fernando C Fervenza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTC3 glomerulonephritis (C3GN) is a rare kidney disease driven by dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway. We report two challenging cases of patients with C3GN in whom various immunosuppressant therapies have failed to achieve complete remission, prompting treatment with eculizumab, an anti-C5 humanized monoclonal antibody that inhibits the terminal complement pathway. Although transient improvement in kidney function was observed, both patients eventually experienced worsening renal parameters, with increasing proteinuria and hematuria, culminating in progression to end-stage kidney disease. Repeated kidney biopsies following eculizumab therapy demonstrated persistent, granular mesangial and capillary wall C3 (3+) staining on immunofluorescence studies, indicating ongoing complement activation upstream of the C5 blockade and the perpetuation of C3 deposits in the glomeruli. These cases underscore the limited efficacy of terminal complement inhibition in halting disease progression in C3GN.

INTRODUCTION

C3 glomerulopathy (C3GN) is a rare renal disorder characterized by dysregulation of the alternative pathway (AP) of the complement system. It affects both pediatric and adult populations and is associated with a poor renal prognosis, with approximately 50% of patients progressing to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) within 10 years. In addition, C3GN has a high risk for relapse after kidney transplantation [1–4].

Several mechanisms have been described in C3GN pathogenesis that results in hyperactivation of the AP, including genetic variants or acquired factors (e.g., C3 nephritic factor or factor H autoantibodies) that modulate the C3 convertase activity, leading to uncontrolled deposition of C3 and its cleavage products, which in turn causes inflammation and subsequent glomerular injury [5]. Clinically, C3GN exhibits a broad spectrum of presentation, ranging from asymptomatic hematuria and proteinuria with preserved kidney function to nephrotic syndrome or rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis [6]. The most frequent histopathologic finding observed in C3GN on light microscopy is membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN), with predominant bright C3 deposition along the glomerular capillary walls in the absence or presence of scant concomitant immunoglobulin deposits on immunofluorescence (IF) [7]. Other patterns of injury have also been reported, including mesangial, endocapillary, exudative, necrotizing, crescentic, sclerosing or mixed patterns [8].

Retrospective studies suggested that mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) in combination with corticosteroids might be a favorable treatment in C3GN in terms of kidney survival and remissions rates when compared with conservative therapy [9]. A few case reports have suggested eculizumab efficacy in C3GN [10–12]. An open-label study demonstrated beneficial responses to eculizumab in a specific group of patients with characteristics such as a rapidly progressive disease and extracapillar proliferation on kidney biopsy [13].

Eculizumab studies in C3GN are scarce, with results showing treatment response heterogeneity [10–15]. Furthermore, long-term eculizumab response in C3GN has not been addressed. Here, we describe two cases of patients diagnosed with C3GN who initially responded to eculizumab therapy but ultimately progressed to ESKD.

CASE DESCRIPTION

PATIENT 1

In October 2012, a 20-year-old female with history of intravenous (IV) drug abuse and anxiety presented with migratory polyarthralgia, petechial rash and lower extremity edema following streptococcal pharyngitis. Anti-streptolysin O titer was elevated with a normal serum creatinine (sCr) level of 0.5 mg/dL and urinalysis with dysmorphic red blood cells (see Table 1). She was diagnosed with post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, along with reactive arthritis, for which conservative management was advised. Two months later, she was admitted to the hospital with acute right flank pain and gross hematuria. Her clinical tests revealed acute kidney injury with rising sCr (1.7 mg/dL), and nephrotic-range proteinuria (4.9 g/24 h). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein were elevated. A renal ultrasound showed bilateral mild echogenic parenchyma without evidence for kidney stones or renal vein thrombosis. Autoimmune and infectious causes were ruled out. Complement levels were normal. Testing for cryoglobulins, hemolysis and ADAMTS13 were negative. A kidney biopsy was performed and showed focal endocapillary proliferative, crescentic and necrotizing glomerulonephritis on light microscopy (see Fig. 2). On IF study there was 3+ granular staining for C3, trace IgG, trace segmental semi-linear mesangial positivity for immunoglobulin A (IgA) and lambda, and negative staining for IgM, C1q, fibrinogen and kappa. On electron microscopy, abundant mesangial and small segmental intramembranous and subepithelial electron dense deposits were seen, compatible with the diagnosis of C3GN (Fig. 2). The patient received pulse IV steroid therapy (1 g/day × 2 days), followed by prednisone taper and MMF. During hospitalization, the patient developed alveolar hemorrhage. Repeated work-up showed low C3 (40 mg/dL) and C4 (6 mg/dL) with suppressed AP functional activity. MMF was discontinued. Testing for acquired factors were negative. Genetic testing showed three risk alleles associated with dense deposit disease C3 glomerulopathy. She received additional three pulses of methylprednisolone (1 g each) and five sessions of plasma exchange, followed by prednisone 60 mg a day with taper over the following 6 months. Her kidney function improved, reaching a nadir sCr of 0.9 mg/dL. At follow-up visits, the patient was initiated on 50 mg azathioprine daily; however, the treatment was not tolerated, and MMF was resumed 3 months after discharge.

Over the following 2 years, the patient was maintained on MMF at a dose of 1 g twice daily in combination with lisinopril (5 mg daily). However, despite this treatment, kidney function gradually deteriorated with sCr increasing to 5.1 mg/dL, accompanied by active urine sediment and nephrotic-range proteinuria (4.5 g/24 h). In February 2015, treatment with eculizumab was initiated at a dose of 900 mg weekly for 4 weeks, followed by a maintenance dose of 1200 mg every 2 weeks. One month after eculizumab initiation, both renal function (sCr 2.3 mg/dL) and proteinuria (urine protein 148 mg/dL) showed improvement. However, after 8 months of eculizumab treatment her kidney function started to deteriorate, and in September 2015 sCr reached 4 mg/dL with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 14.3 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Fig. 1A). A repeat kidney biopsy showed severe chronicity and persistent C3 (3+) diffuse granular mesangial and capillary wall staining (Fig. 2). In view of the progressive nature of the disease, patient was referred for kidney transplant but because of the history of IV drug abuse she was not eligible and died of sepsis while on dialysis.

PATIENT 2

Patient 2 was a 32-year-old female with a history of childhood-onset proteinuria and Graves’ disease. The patient underwent kidney biopsy in 2005 (age 24 years). Light microscopy showed an MPGN pattern of injury with duplicate basal membranes, mesangial and subendothelial deposits. On IF microscopy there was bright C3 3+, negative IgA and IgG, and IgM 1+ (see Fig. 3). Electron microscopy showed subendothelial and mesangial electron dense deposits, and a diagnosis of MPGN Type I with C3 dominant deposits was made. She was treated conservatively with anti-hypertensive medications and diuretics. A brief course of corticosteroids (60 mg/day) was started around 2007 with no significant clinical improvement.

In August 2012, the patient was evaluated at Presbyterian Hospital–Columbia University due to progressive kidney dysfunction. Her sCr had risen from a baseline of 0.9 mg/dL to 1.8 mg/dL. Laboratory tests revealed proteinuria of 17.0 g/24 h, and persistently low serum C3 levels with normal C4 levels. Serologic evaluation for connective tissue diseases, including lupus, scleroderma, mixed connective tissue disease, Sjögren syndrome, and rheumatoid arthritis, was negative. Given the worsening of her kidney function and proteinuria, a repeat kidney biopsy was performed that confirmed the diagnosis of C3GN (Fig. 3). Genetic testing revealed risk alleles in CFH and C3, and both C3 nephritic factor and factor H autoantibodies were detected [2]. Treatment was started with eculizumab (1200 mg every 2 weeks) resulting in stabilization of renal function (sCr 1.5 mg/dL), improvement of proteinuria to 710 mg/24 h, with resolution of hematuria (Table 1). In August 2013, after 1 year of treatment, eculizumab discontinuation was attempted for 2 months which resulted in worsening proteinuria and decline in kidney function. Eculizumab was therefore resumed. Over the subsequent 2 years, her renal function progressively deteriorated, with sCr rising to 2.6 mg/dL, serum albumin dropping to 3.0 g/dL, and proteinuria increasing to 5.3 g/24 h. A repeat biopsy in September 2014 showed persistent C3GN with diffuse granular mesangial and capillary staining (Fig. 3). She was started on MMF 500 mg twice daily (June 2016), but her condition continued to decline, with persistent proteinuria of 5.4 g/24 h and sCr increasing to to 3.8 mg/dL together with persistently low serum C3 levels (25 mg/dL) (Fig. 1B). Ultimately, she was referred to for evaluation for preemptive kidney transplantation and received a living donor kidney transplantation from her sibling in 2017. Following living donor kidney transplantation in 2017, the patient remained on eculizumab therapy. A protocol allograft biopsy performed 1 month post-transplant demonstrated mild histologic evidence of recurrent C3GN, characterized by mesangial C3 deposition. However, there was no clinical evidence of recurrence, as kidney allograft function remained stable without proteinuria or hematuria. Given the subclinical nature of the recurrence and stable graft function, complement inhibition therapy was continued. The patient was subsequently offered transition to alternative complement-targeted therapies, including iptacopan or pegcetacoplan, but declined both options and elected to remain on eculizumab.

DISCUSSION

We report two cases of patients with C3GN treated with eculizumab that showed an initial positive clinical response that was not, however, sustained over the long-term, and their renal disease progressed to ESKD. These cases highlight the limited effectiveness of downstream complement pathway inhibition in C3GN, and point to the C3 convertase breakdown products (i.e. C3a and C3b) as a major players in the pathogenesis of C3GN [16].

Triggering events for C3GN may include infection, monoclonal gammopathy and positive autoimmune findings [17]. For instance, in Patient 1, the initial presentation following streptococcal pharyngitis, with elevated ASO titers, hypocomplementemia, hematuria and a nephritic syndrome, was initially interpreted as classic post-infectious glomerulonephritis (PIGN). However, the subsequent clinical course was not typical, as the patient showed persistent active urinary sediment, ongoing proteinuria and hematuria, and progressive deterioration of renal function. Sethi et al. described this entity as “atypical” PIGN, characterized by persistent proteinuria and hematuria, progressive kidney function decline and an underlying genetic predisposition affecting the AP that is unmasked by infection [18]. These cases are now recognized as atypical presentations of C3GN. In the current case, the renal biopsy demonstrating dominant C3 deposition further supported this reclassification, aligning the diagnosis of C3GN. Monoclonal gammopathy have also been described as a potential culprit in hematological malignancies for dysregulating complement proteins, particularly in elderly patients [19, 20].

In Patient 2, the presence of both C3 nephritic factor and factor H autoantibodies is highly relevant, as it indicates simultaneous dysregulation of complement activation. C3NeF stabilizes the alternative pathway C3 convertase (C3bBb), leading to persistent C3 activation and consumption, while factor H autoantibodies impair the principal negative regulator of the pathway, further amplifying uncontrolled complement activation. This dual mechanism results in an upstream and sustained activation of the AP, which may not be adequately controlled by terminal pathway inhibition alone.

Until recently, there was limited information regarding treatment for patients with progressive disease needing immunosuppression. Data from observational studies have shown that a combined regimen of MMF and glucocorticoids induces remission in C3GN patients when compared with other immunosuppressive regimens [9]. In addition, patients who were treated with this regimen had lower rates of progression to kidney failure. Of note, complete remission was more frequently observed in autoantibody-mediated C3GN, whereas patients with pathogenic variants in complement genes achieved only partial remission [9, 21, 22].

A retrospective study from the French registry database which including 26 pediatric (n = 13) and adult (n = 13) patients with C3GN treated with eculizumab demonstrated positive response predominantly in those with crescentic rapidly progressive C3GN, while responses where limited in non-rapidly progressive cases [14]. Similarly, an open-label study by Bomback et al. reported heterogeneous responses in six C3GN patients receiving eculizumab, suggesting potential benefit in patients with particular characteristics such as a rapidly progressive disease and extracapillary proliferation on kidney biopsy [13]. The study was limited by small sample size and only 12 months of follow-up; histopathological evaluation at 12 months showed decreased proliferative activity, whereas IF staining for C3 persisted post-eculizumab treatment [23], but long-term follow-up is missing.

In our two patients, the initial positive response to eculizumab may have reflected the anti-inflammatory effect of C5a-bockade preventing mediated inflammatory cell recruitment. Nevertheless, repeated kidney biopsy revealed persistent strong C3 deposition (3+) irrespectively of eculizumab therapy, highlighting the discordance between terminal pathway inhibition and ongoing complement dysregulation at the level of C3 and its breakdown products that continue to deposit in the glomeruli perpetuating glomerular injury (Table 2). Although eculizumab may stabilize kidney function and reduce proteinuria in selected patients, particularly in the presence of C5b-9 deposition, its impact on C3 deposition remains uncertain, and treatment responses are heterogeneous [24]. The initial beneficial effect of eculuzimab seen in our two patients may be explained by the prevention of the powerful anaphylatoxin C5a generation, and the prevention of the subsequent leukocyte infiltration as the additional anti-inflammatory effects [25]. However, the limited long-term response to eculizumab in our patients may be in part explained by persistent upstream alternative pathway dysregulation driven by genetic risk variants and acquired autoantibodies, particularly C3 nephritic factor and factor H autoantibodies, which promote continuous C3 convertase activation and persistent deposition of C3 fragments (iC3b, C3d, C3g) in the glomeruli which is not prevented by C5 blockade (e.g., eculizumab), resulting in ongoing glomerular injury independently of terminal complement blockade. It is plausible that monitoring with biomarkers such as soluble C5b-9, and ex vivo C5b-9 deposition could potentially help identify incomplete terminal pathway blockade [26]. However, we would like to point out that we confirmed multiple times that terminal complement pathway was blocked by checking levels of functional C5, which were always undetectable [27] (data not shown). Ravulizumab is a modified form of eculizumab with an extended half-life that may also have potential use in managing C3G [24]. Consistent with our now reported cases, a randomized clinical trial evaluating avacopan, a C5a receptor antagonist, showed no benefit over placebo in C3GN [28], further supporting the limited impact of targeting the terminal complement pathway alone.

The treatment landscape has recently changed after two seminal clinical trials that evaluate the efficacy of upstream anti-complement therapies (Table 3). Iptacopan, an oral factor B inhibitor, was first evaluated in C3 glomerulopathy in early phase II studies (APPEAR-C3G), demonstrating proof-of-concept efficacy through alternative pathway inhibition, with subsequent expansion of investigation into immune-complex MPGN in the ongoing phase 3 clinical study evaluating the efficacy and safety of the oral drug iptacopan in patients with idiopathic immune complex-mediated membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (APPARENT) trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05755386) [29]. In the early phase II APPEAR-C3G, which enrolled 27 C3GN patients (16 native cohort and 11 recurrent kidney transplant cohort), iptacopan induced a rapid reduction of proteinuria over 12 weeks in the native cohort, along with a decreased in glomerular C3 deposition scores in transplant recipients [30, 31]. These findings were subsequently confirmed in the phase III randomized clinical trial, where treatment with iptacopan on top of standard of care resulted in 35% reduction in proteinuria at 6 months compared with placebo, with sustained effects at 12 months [32]. Additionally, the Phase 3 Trial of Pegcetacoplan for Patients With Native or Post-Transplant Recurrent C3G or Primary IC-MPGN (VALIANT) demonstrated that pegcetacoplan, an inhibitor of C3 and its activation fragment C3b, resulted in a 68% relative reduction in proteinuria versus placebo, eGFR stabilization and improvement in circulating complements biomarkers. Moreover, repeat kidney biopsy showed a reduction in glomerular C3 staining in 74% of treated patients versus 12% in the placebo arm [33, 34]. Despite these promising results, long-term data on kidney function outcomes and durability of response remain limited.

In summary, these two cases illustrate that targeting downstream in the complement cascade with eculizumab or avacopan have limited benefit in patients with C3GN, underscoring that terminal complement inhibition alone is inadequate to halt disease progression. The recent approval of iptacopan and pegcetacoplan by the Food and Drug Adminstration which target upstream in the complement cascade bring new promising therapeutic options for patients with C3GN.
