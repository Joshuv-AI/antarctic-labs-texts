Desmoid tumor mimicking cancer recurrence in a patient with colon cancer and Gardner syndrome: A case report
Authors: Jing Zhang, Yan Fu, Peng Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gardner syndrome, a subtype of familial adenomatous polyposis, features colorectal polyposis, osteomas and soft-tissue tumors with elevated colorectal carcinoma risk, and abdominal masses in these patients are easily misdiagnosed as malignant recurrence, especially with concomitant bone lesions. We report a 33-year-old female familial adenomatous polyposis patient with a history of surgically treated stage IIIA colorectal adenocarcinoma, who presented with a 3-month abdominal mass. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography indicated a large cystic-solid mass with lymphadenopathy and peritoneal thickening, suggesting recurrence, while magnetic resonance imaging suggested a desmoid tumor and identified bilateral femoral head avascular necrosis. Surgical resection and pathogenic adenomatous polyposis coli gene mutation detection confirmed an abdominal desmoid tumor. This case highlights that Gardner syndrome can induce benign space-occupying desmoid tumors, and for familial adenomatous polyposis patients with bone lesions, combining advanced imaging, histopathology and genetic testing is critical to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions for suspected cancer recurrence.

Introduction

Gardner syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder and a phenotypic variant of familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP). It is classically defined by the triad of colorectal polyposis, osteomas, and desmoid‑type fibromatoses. 1 This syndrome results from a germline mutation in the adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) gene on chromosome 5q21, leading to constitutive activation of the Wnt signaling pathway.2,3 Patients with Gardner syndrome invariably develop hundreds to thousands of colorectal adenomas, conferring a near‑100% lifetime risk of colorectal carcinoma if prophylactic colectomy is not performed. 4

Beyond the colorectum, management of extracolonic manifestations poses significant clinical challenges. Desmoid tumors, although histologically benign, are locally aggressive, non‑metastasizing neoplasms that may cause severe morbidity through mass effect on abdominal organs, blood vessels, or nerves. 5 A particularly complex diagnostic scenario arises in patients with a history of treated colorectal cancer who subsequently develop an abdominal mass. In such cases, radiologic findings can be ambiguous, and the lesion is frequently presumed to represent metastatic disease recurrence. This misdiagnosis can potentially lead to inappropriate systemic therapy and patient distress. Therefore, a high index of suspicion and a systematic diagnostic approach are essential. We present a case that vividly illustrates this dilemma and highlights the critical role of multimodal assessment in achieving an accurate diagnosis.

Case report

In November 2024, a 33‑year‑old woman was admitted to our department with a 3‑month history of a palpable and gradually enlarging abdominal mass, first noticed in August 2024. She also reported persistent abdominal bloating and dull pain localized to the periumbilical region. Her medical history was notable for FAP. In August 2014, she underwent colonoscopic polypectomy for numerous tubular adenomas. In May 2021, she was diagnosed with pT2N1M0 stage ⅢA colorectal adenocarcinoma and underwent laparoscopic total colorectal resection with end ileostomy. Her mother also had a confirmed history of FAP and colorectal surgery. The patient had recovered well from her cancer surgery without receiving adjuvant therapy.

Physical examination revealed a firm, tender, fixed mass in the periumbilical region. Routine blood tests were normal. Serum tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), CA19‑9, CA125, and CA72‑4, were all within normal limits. Contrast‑enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a large (14.7 × 5.4 × 11 cm) irregular cystic‑solid mass in the mid‑lower abdomen. The mass showed mild heterogeneous enhancement, had ill‑defined borders with the adjacent small bowel wall, and was associated with multiple enlarged abdominal and retroperitoneal lymph nodes as well as peritoneal thickening (Figure 1(a) and (b)). These findings were highly suspicious for recurrent or metastatic carcinoma.

An ultrasound‑guided percutaneous biopsy of the mass was performed. Histopathology showed hyperplastic collagenous fibrous tissue lacking epithelial elements, precluding a definitive diagnosis. Given the high clinical suspicion for malignancy, further characterization was pursued. Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed that the mass originated from the small bowel mesentery. Its signal characteristics and enhancement pattern were suggestive of a desmoid‑type fibromatosis (Figure 1(c) and (d)). Concurrently, MRI incidentally identified bilateral femoral head avascular necrosis (AVN; Figure 1(e) and (f)). The combination of a mesenteric mass, bony lesions (femoral head necrosis), and a compelling personal and family history of FAP strongly suggested Gardner syndrome.

After a thorough discussion of the surgical risks, benefits, and alternative options, the patient provided written informed consent for the procedure. To obtain a definitive diagnosis and achieve therapeutic relief of symptoms, the patient underwent exploratory laparotomy in December 2024. Intraoperative findings included two large, firm masses infiltrating the small bowel mesentery (Figure 2(a)). Complete surgical resection was achieved. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen revealed a hypocellular proliferation of spindle‑shaped cells within a collagenous stroma, consistent with a low‑grade spindle cell neoplasm. Hematoxylin and eosin staining confirmed the spindle cell morphology (Figure 2(b)). Immunohistochemistry showed tumor cells positive for β‑catenin (with nuclear staining in a subset of cells; Figure 2(c)) and a low Ki‑67 proliferation index (~3%; Figure 2(d)). The cells were negative for Stat6, CD117, CD34, Desmin, DOG1, S‑100, and SMA. Immunohistochemical staining also demonstrated the presence of APC protein in the tumor tissue (Figure 2(e)). Subsequent genetic testing of a peripheral blood sample identified a heterozygous pathogenic mutation in the APC gene (c.1370C>G, p.Ser457*), with a variant allele frequency of 50% (Table 1).

The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful. The definitive diagnosis of Gardner syndrome with an associated intra-abdominal desmoid tumor was established based on integration of pathological findings, imaging features, family history, and molecular evidence. Given the complete surgical resection (R0 status) and low proliferative index of the tumor, no adjuvant therapy was initiated in accordance with current management guidelines. The patient was placed on a structured surveillance protocol comprising clinical assessment and serum CEA measurement every 3–6 months for the first 2 years, along with annual contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scans for at least 5 years to monitor for local recurrence or new lesions.

Discussion

Distinguishing between malignant recurrence and benign lesions related to underlying genetic syndromes remains a major challenge in the long-term follow-up of patients with colorectal cancer and predisposing conditions such as FAP. Desmoid tumors, although histologically non-malignant, are well recognized as a locally aggressive manifestation of FAP and Gardner syndrome, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 10% to 20% among affected individuals. 6 Their tendency to arise in the mesentery or abdominal wall and to grow insidiously can closely mimic peritoneal carcinomatosis or recurrent colorectal carcinoma on conventional imaging studies.

In the present case, the initial contrast-enhanced CT scan demonstrated a large complex mass in the mid-lower abdomen, accompanied by regional lymphadenopathy and peritoneal thickening. These features generated a strong suspicion of peritoneal carcinomatosis from recurrent colon cancer. Such findings underscore a common diagnostic pitfall: in patients with a history of FAP and previous colorectal malignancy, any newly detected intra-abdominal mass should prompt consideration of desmoid disease alongside recurrent carcinoma. The presence of concurrent osseous abnormalities, such as the bilateral femoral head AVN seen in our patient, should heighten clinical vigilance for Gardner syndrome, given that osteomas and other bone changes form part of its classical phenotypic triad. 7 However, it is important to note that AVN is not a recognized manifestation of Gardner syndrome. After excluding common causes such as steroid use or alcohol intake, this finding was considered incidental in our patient. Nevertheless, the presence of any bone abnormality, even an incidental one, in a patient with FAP and an abdominal mass should raise clinical suspicion for Gardner syndrome and prompt further evaluation.

MRI played a pivotal role in refining the differential diagnosis. Typical MR features of desmoid tumors include low to intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted sequences, variable signal on T2-weighted images depending on cellularity, and a pattern of progressive delayed enhancement. 8 In our patient, MRI showed a mesenteric origin of the mass with signal characteristics consistent with desmoid-type fibromatosis. Nevertheless, imaging alone cannot provide a definitive diagnosis. Histopathological examination revealed a hypocellular spindle cell proliferation within a dense collagenous matrix, accompanied by immunohistochemical evidence of nuclear β‑catenin accumulation in a subset of tumor cells. Nuclear β‑catenin positivity is found in ~70% of sporadic desmoid tumors and is strongly associated with both sporadic and APC-related forms.9,10

Definitive confirmation was achieved through identification of a heterozygous pathogenic germline mutation in the APC gene (c.1370C>G, p.Ser457*), establishing the diagnosis of Gardner syndrome. Genetic testing is particularly valuable in cases where histomorphology and imaging are inconclusive, as it not only confirms the syndromic nature of the tumor but also has implications for family screening and surveillance strategies.

The management of intra-abdominal desmoid tumors in FAP patients requires a personalized, multidisciplinary approach. Asymptomatic or slowly progressing lesions may be managed conservatively with active surveillance, given the potential for spontaneous regression. 11 For symptomatic or functionally threatening tumors, options include surgical resection aiming for R0 clearance, medical therapies such as NSAIDs, anti-estrogens, or tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and occasionally radiotherapy.12–14 Although the tumor exhibited a low Ki‑67 index (3%), surgical resection was pursued because the patient was symptomatic (abdominal bloating and dull pain) with a progressively enlarging mass over 3 months. The retroperitoneal location adjacent to the mesentery and bowel carried a substantial risk of future obstruction, perforation, or vascular compromise. Given that preoperative imaging confirmed feasibility of R0 resection, and for this young patient with a localized, resectable desmoid tumor, R0 resection offers durable local control with a single intervention and the lowest recurrence rate. 15

This case illustrates the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating abdominal masses in FAP patients with prior colorectal cancer. Incorporating detailed personal and family history, advanced cross-sectional imaging, targeted immunohistochemistry, and germline genetic testing into the diagnostic algorithm can prevent misclassification of desmoid tumors as recurrent malignancy. Avoiding unwarranted systemic anticancer therapy not only spares patients from potential toxicity but also allows timely initiation of appropriate syndrome-specific management.

Conclusion

Patients with FAP and a prior history of colorectal cancer require careful evaluation when a new abdominal mass is identified, since such a finding should not be presumptively attributed to metastatic disease. Desmoid tumors associated with Gardner syndrome constitute an important benign mimic that can closely resemble recurrent malignancy. A rigorous diagnostic approach is therefore essential, incorporating detailed personal and family history, dedicated MRI, image-guided biopsy with a panel of immunohistochemical stains including β‑catenin, and confirmatory APC genetic testing. This comprehensive strategy helps prevent misdiagnosis, avoids unnecessary systemic anticancer therapy, and enables appropriate, patient‑centered management tailored to the underlying syndrome.
