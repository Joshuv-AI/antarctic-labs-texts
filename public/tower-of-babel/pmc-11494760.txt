Exosomal miR-1a-3p derived from glucocorticoid-stimulated M1 macrophages promotes the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs in glucocorticoid-associated osteonecrosis of the femoral head by targeting Cebpz
Authors: Ping Duan, Yong-Le Yu, Yan-Nan Cheng, Meng-Han Nie, Qing Yang, Liang-Hui Xia, Yan-Xiao Ji, Zhen-Yu Pan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBy interacting with bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and regulating their function through exosomes, bone macrophages play crucial roles in various bone-related diseases. Research has highlighted a notable increase in the number of M1 macrophages in glucocorticoid-associated osteonecrosis of the femoral head (GA-ONFH). Nevertheless, the intricate crosstalk between M1 macrophages and BMSCs in the glucocorticoid-stimulated environment has not been fully elucidated, and the underlying regulatory mechanisms involved in the occurrence of GA-ONFH remain unclear.MethodsWe employed in vivo mouse models and clinical samples from GA-ONFH patients to investigate the interactions between M1 macrophages and BMSCs. Immunofluorescence staining was used to assess the colocalization of M1 macrophages and BMSCs. Flow cytometry and transcriptomic analysis were performed to evaluate the impact of exosomes derived from normal (n-M1) and glucocorticoid-stimulated M1 macrophages (GC-M1) on BMSC differentiation. Additionally, miR-1a-3p expression was altered in vitro and in vivo to assess its role in regulating adipogenic differentiation.ResultsIn vivo, the colocalization of M1 macrophages and BMSCs was observed, and an increase in M1 macrophage numbers and a decrease in bone repair capabilities were further confirmed in both GA-ONFH patients and mouse models. Both n-M1 and GC-M1 were identified as contributors to the inhibition of osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs to a certain extent via exosome secretion. More importantly, exosomes derived from GC-M1 macrophages exhibited a heightened capacity to regulate the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, which was mediated by miR-1a-3p. In vivo and in vitro, miR-1a-3p promoted the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs by targeting Cebpz and played an important role in the onset and progression of GA-ONFH.ConclusionWe demonstrated that exosomes derived from GC-M1 macrophages disrupt the balance between osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation in BMSCs, contributing to the pathogenesis of GA-ONFH. Inhibiting miR-1a-3p expression, both in vitro and in vivo, significantly mitigates the preferential adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, thus slowing the progression of GA-ONFH. These findings provide new insights into the regulatory mechanisms underlying GA-ONFH and highlight potential therapeutic targets for intervention.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02923-5.

Introduction

Glucocorticoid-associated osteonecrosis of the femoral head (GA-ONFH) is a metabolic disease that is characterized by progressive necrosis of the femoral head after long-term/high-dose administration of glucocorticoids (GCs) [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of GA-ONFH is complex and diverse, and its final pathological results are caused by multiple factors [3]. Notably, when signs of necrosis of the femoral head are observed in clinical practice, preventing its progression via commonly used treatment methods is difficult. Most patients experience femoral head collapse within 2–3 years, and there are currently no effective drugs available to prevent femoral head collapse [4]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of GA-ONFH and identifying effective therapeutic targets are highly clinically and socially important.

With continuous investigations of the mechanism underlying GA-ONFH, increasing evidence has shown that an imbalance in the osteogenic/adipogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) is an important mechanism that ultimately leads to the occurrence and development of GA-ONFH [5–8]. BMSC transplantation has shown some efficacy in treating GA-ONFH in animal models and clinical trials [9–11]. BMSCs are common progenitor cells of osteoblasts and bone marrow adipocytes, and the balance between their osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation is temporally and spatially regulated via precise mechanisms. The differentiation of BMSCs is closely related to their bone microenvironment [12, 13]. Clinical evidence reveals a large degree of inflammatory macrophage infiltration in the femoral head of GA-ONFH patients [14, 15], and immune activation disorders are significantly associated with the occurrence of GA-ONFH [16]. In various animal models, early inflammatory conditions/environments can significantly improve the success of GA-ONFH modelling [17–19]. These findings suggest that the inflammatory microenvironment within the femoral head may also be an important cause of GA-ONFH. Our previous findings revealed that M1 macrophages play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of GA-ONFH, highlighting their indispensable role in disease progression. Depletion of M1 macrophages at early time points delays the progression of GA-ONFH [20]. However, thus far, there have been no reports on the crosstalk that occurs between M1 macrophages and BMSC differentiation during the pathogenesis of GA-ONFH.

Exosomes are extracellular vesicles with diameters of approximately 50–100 nm that are secreted by almost all types of cells. Exosomes release numerous regulatory factors into the extracellular space; perform functions related to intercellular signal transduction; and affect the proliferation, differentiation, and functional gene activity of surrounding recipient cells [21]. According to the literature, various factors, such as high glucose levels, ageing, and damage, can cause changes in the extracellular vesicles that are secreted by macrophages, which in turn participate in the occurrence of diseases [22–24]. Extracellular vesicles contain signalling factors, such as proteins, DNA, mRNAs, and miRNAs; among these factors, miRNAs are highly conserved tissue-specific noncoding RNAs that maintain cellular homeostasis by negatively regulating gene expression [25]. Research has shown that the osteogenic/adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs is regulated by external exosomal miRNAs and is involved in pathological processes related to bone repair and many bone-related diseases [26–28].

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the regulatory effect of glucocorticoid-stimulated M1 macrophages on the differentiation of BMSCs into osteoblasts/adipocytes in a GA-ONFH model. Through miRNA sequencing analysis of exosomes derived from M1 macrophages, we identified a key miRNA, miR-1a-3p, in the exosomes. We clarified that miR-1a-3p promotes the preferential differentiation of BMSCs into adipocytes by negatively regulating the expression of Cebpz. This molecular mechanism provides a deeper understanding of the interactive mechanisms between M1 macrophages and BMSCs during the occurrence of GA-ONFH.

Results

M1 macrophages and BMSCs are involved in the occurrence of GA-ONFH

To study the role of M1 macrophages and BMSCs in GA-ONFH, we collected femoral head specimens from patients who underwent hip replacement due to femoral neck fracture and GA-ONFH and performed a comparative analysis. GA-ONFH patients exhibit a significant area of osteonecrosis under the femoral head cartilage (Fig. 1A). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed a substantial decrease in the expression of the osteogenesis-related markers type I collagen (COL1) and osteocalcin (OCN) in the GA-ONFH group (Supplementary Data. 1A, B). The Masson staining results also revealed extensive necrosis of the femoral head trabeculae in GA-ONFH, accompanied by significant loss of bone structure and substantial fibrous tissue proliferation (Fig. 1B). Moreover, immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed the upregulation of the M1 macrophage marker CD86 in the bone marrow flushing solution of GA-ONFH patients (Supplementary Data. 1C, D), which was consistent with the results of IHC staining of femoral head tissues (Fig. 1B, C). Furthermore, we established a GA-ONFH mouse model with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) combined with GC injection, which exhibited significant bone loss and structural damage in the femoral head, as observed via micro-CT scans (Fig. 1D). This damage was characterized by a decrease in bone volume and trabecular quantity and an increase in trabecular separation compared with those of the normal group (Supplementary Data. 1E); these results confirmed the successful establishment of the model. In the model group, the osteogenesis-related indicators COL1 and Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) were significantly downregulated (Fig. 1E, F). Additionally, IF staining of mouse femoral head tissues also revealed significant upregulation of CD86 in the model group (Fig. 1G, H). These results indicate that both GA-ONFH patient samples and model mice exhibit bone structure destruction and that the number of M1 macrophages in the femoral head is significantly increased. Previous studies have suggested an imbalance in the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in GA-ONFH [29]. In addition, we performed multiple IF staining experiments on mouse femoral head samples, and the results showed spatial colocalization of BMSCs (colabelled with CD29 and CD44) and M1 macrophages (Fig. 1I). Collectively, these results suggest that both M1 macrophages and BMSCs are involved in the occurrence of GA-ONFH.

M1 macrophages inhibit the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in vitro

RAW264.7 cells were induced to polarize into normal M1 macrophages (n-M1), and 1 µM dexamethasone was added to stimulate these M1 macrophages (GC-M1). Flow cytometry data indicated that both n-M1 and GC-M1 macrophages expressed the surface markers CD86 and CD11b (Fig. 2A), suggesting that GC stimulation might not alter the polarization of M1 macrophages. Subsequently, we cocultured n-M1 or GC-M1 macrophages separately with BMSCs (Fig. 2B). Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT–qPCR) and western blotting results showed that, compared with BMSCs that were cultured alone (control group), BMSCs that were cocultured with n-M1 or GC-M1 macrophages exhibited varying degrees of decreased expression of the osteogenesis-related genes bone morphogenetic protein 2 (Bmp2), Col1a1, and Runx2 (Fig. 2C, D). After 21 days of osteogenic differentiation induction, we assessed the osteogenic differentiation potential of all three groups of BMSCs using alizarin red staining, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and ALP activity assay. The results indicated a significant decrease in the osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs in both the n-M1 and GC-M1 groups (Fig. 2E-G).

M1 macrophage-derived exosomes impair the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs

As macrophages can influence the osteogenesis of BMSCs through paracrine mechanisms, such as exosomes [27], we isolated exosomes from both n-M1 and GC-M1 macrophages (abbreviated as n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos, respectively) to determine the effect on the function of BMSCs (as shown schematically in Supplementary Data. 2A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed that both sets of exosomes exhibited characteristic cup-shaped or discoid morphologies with sizes ranging from 50 to 100 nm (Supplementary Data. 2B, C). Western blot analysis confirmed the positive expression of exosomal markers (CD9, CD63, and TSG101) in n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos, while the negative marker Calnexin was not expressed (Supplementary Data. 2D). Furthermore, exosome internalization experiments showed that both n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos were effectively taken up by BMSCs (Fig. 3A).

Subsequently, we treated BMSCs with PBS, GC-M1-exos, or n-M1-exos and examined several osteogenesis-related genes [Bmp2, Col1a1, bone gamma-carboxyglutamic acid-containing proteins (Bglap), and Runx2]. Only Col1a1 expression was downregulated in both the n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos treatment groups compared to the control group (Supplementary Data. 2E); these results were consistent with the trend in COL1a1 expression observed through Western blotting (Fig. 3B). In addition, the alizarin red staining, ALP staining and ALP activity assay results suggested that the osteogenic differentiation and mineralization capabilities of the BMSCs that internalized the n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos were significantly reduced (Fig. 3C-E). To further elucidate the functional changes in BMSCs after different treatments, we analysed the transcriptome via RNA sequencing. Volcano plots showed varying degrees of changes in gene expression in the BMSCs treated with n-M1-exos or GC-M1-exos compared to those in the control group (Fig. 3F). A total of 480 genes were altered in both the n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos treated BMSCs. Gene Ontology (GO) and heatmap analyses revealed similar changes in bone mineralization-related gene expression in both the n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos treated BMSCs (Fig. 3F). Taken together, these results indicated that both n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos could downregulate the expression of certain bone mineralization-related genes in BMSCs, thereby inhibiting their osteogenic differentiation capacity to some extent.

GC-M1-exos significantly promote the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs

We further analyzed the transcriptome data to investigate the effects of GC-treated M1 macrophages compared to normal M1 macrophages on the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Only 51 genes were differentially expressed between the n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos. GO analysis revealed that the fat cell differentiation signalling pathway was the most enriched pathway (Fig. 4A). A heatmap showed that genes related to fat cell differentiation were upregulated specifically in the GC-M1-exos treatment group compared to those in the control and n-M1-exos groups (Fig. 4A), suggesting that GC-M1-exos play a pivotal role in promoting the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs. RT‒qPCR and Western blotting confirmed that compared with normal conditions, both n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos upregulated the expression of CCAAT/enhancer binding protein α (C/EBPα) and fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4) in BMSCs without changing the expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) (Fig. 4B, C). After 3 days of adipogenic differentiation, RT‒qPCR and Western blotting revealed that compared with that in the control and n-M1-exos groups, the expression of C/EBPα, FABP4, and PPARγ in the GC-M1-exos group was significantly upregulated (Fig. 4D, E). After the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs was induced in these three groups for 14 days, oil red O staining demonstrated that GC-M1-exos treatment significantly promoted adipogenic differentiation and lipid formation in BMSCs (Fig. 4F, G). Clinical and animal samples revealed significant lipid accumulation and adipogenesis in the femoral heads of patients and animals with GA-ONFH (Fig. 4H and Supplementary Data. 1F).

miR-1a-3p expression is strongly correlated with GA-ONFH development

To further explore the molecular mechanism by which GC-M1-exos influence the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, we conducted miRNA sequencing analysis of the n-M1 and GC-M1 exosomes. As shown in Fig. 5A, the heatmap showed 9 differentially expressed miRNAs between these two groups, and these 9 miRNAs were highly enriched in the fat cell differentiation signalling pathway according to the GO analysis (Fig. 5B). By intersecting the target genes of miRNAs with genes related to the regulation of fat cell differentiation in the GO database, we found that mmu-miR-1a-3p affected the largest number of genes that regulate fat cell differentiation (Fig. 5C, D). Therefore, we verified the expression of miR-1a-3p in exosomes, bone marrow blood samples from patients, and femoral head tissues in mice. RT‒qPCR analysis revealed significant upregulation of miR-1a-3p in GC-M1-exos (Fig. 5E). Consistently, the expression of hsa-miR-1-3p was significantly increased in bone marrow blood samples from GA-ONFH patients (Fig. 5F). Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) assays of the femoral head showed that miR-1a-3p was significantly increased in the model group (Fig. 5G, H).

miR-1a-3p promotes the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs in vitro

To investigate the function of miR-1a-3p in the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, we constructed miR-1a-3p-overexpressing (OE-miR-1a-3p) or miR-1a-3p knockdown (sh-miR-1a-3p) BMSCs using lentivirus (Fig. 6A). miR-1a-3p overexpression significantly increased the mRNA and protein levels of C/EBPα, FABP4, and PPARγ, while miR-1a-3p knockdown inhibited their expression (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Data. 3A). Oil red O staining also demonstrated that miR-1a-3p plays a positive role in adipogenic differentiation and lipid droplet formation in BMSCs, and inhibition of miR-1a-3p expression significantly reduced lipid droplets (Fig. 6C, D). To further elucidate the positive regulatory role of GC-M1 macrophages in the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs via the secretion of exosomal miR-1a-3p, we established a model in which miR-1a-3p was knocked down in GC-M1 macrophages, as shown in Fig. 6E. Western blotting and RT‒qPCR analysis revealed that the adipogenesis-related genes C/EBPα, FABP4, and PPARγ were significantly downregulated in the sh-miR-exos group (Fig. 6F and Supplementary Data. 3B). Oil red O staining also indicated a significant reduction in the adipogenic differentiation capacity of the BMSCs in the sh-miR-exos group (Fig. 6G, H).

miR-1a-3p regulates the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs by targeting Cebpz

To further elucidate the mechanism by which miR-1a-3p regulates the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, four bioinformatics databases (DIANA, TargetScan, miRDB, and miRmap) were used to collectively predict and identify the targets of miR-1a-3p (Supplementary Data. 4A). After comparison and screening, CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein zeta (C/EBPZ) was selected for further investigation based on its critical role in regulating fat formation [30], and the binding site of miR-1a-3p in the 3’ UTR of C/EBPZ is shown in Supplementary Data. 4B. RT-qPCR data showed that miR-1a-3p negatively regulates Cebpz expression (Supplementary Data. 4C). Moreover, mRNA stability assays further revealed that miR-1a-3p influences the mRNA stability of Cebpz (Supplementary Data. 4D). In addition, these findings were consistent with the trends observed in the Western blotting analysis (Fig. 7A), supporting the regulatory role of miR-1a-3p on C/EBPZ at both the mRNA and protein levels. Furthermore, a dual-luciferase assay also revealed that miR-1a-3p overexpression decreased the luciferase activity of Cebpz but had no effect when the Cebpz binding site was mutated (Fig. 7B, C).

Next, we investigated whether miR-1a-3p-mediated regulation of the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs is dependent on C/EBPZ. First, plasmids that effectively knocked down Cebpz were screened by RT‒qPCR and Western blotting, and si-C/EBPZ-1 was selected for subsequent experiments (Supplementary Data. 5A, B). RT‒qPCR and Western blotting results demonstrated that C/EBPZ-1 deficiency significantly upregulated the expression of C/EBPα and PPARγ (Fig. 7D and Supplementary Data. 5C, D). Consistently, oil red O staining indicated that si-C/EBPZ-1 significantly promoted the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs (Fig. 7E, F). Notably, reversal experiments showed that sh-miR-1a-3p significantly increased the expression level of C/EBPZ, but this effect was reversed by si-C/EBPZ-1 (Fig. 7G, H). We actively detected the expression of Cebpz at both the mRNA and protein levels in the previous experimental samples. As expected, we found that Cebpz was significantly downregulated in the GC-M1-exos and GA-ONFH groups but upregulated in the sh-miR-exos group (Fig. 7I, J and Supplementary Data. 5E-H). Finally, oil red O staining revealed that sh-miR-1a-3p significantly inhibited the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, and this inhibitory effect was mitigated by the addition of si-C/EBPZ-1 (Fig. 7K, L), suggesting that miR-1a-3p regulates the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs by targeting and suppressing Cebpz.

miR-1a-3p promotes the occurrence and progression of GA-ONFH in vivo

To evaluate the function of miR-1a-3p in GA-ONFH in vivo, we established four animal models, as shown in Fig. 8A. Compared with that in the normal group, the fluorescence of miR-1a-3p in the model group, model + OE-miR group, and model + sh-miR group was elevated to varying degrees, with the highest miR-1a-3p fluorescence in the model + OE-miR group and the lowest miR-1a-3p fluorescence in the model + sh-miR group (Fig. 8B, C). Furthermore, we examined the expression levels of adipogenic differentiation-related genes across different groups. Both IHC staining and Western blotting results showed that, in vivo, the adipogenic differentiation-related genes were highly expressed in the model group, whereas C/EBPZ exhibited low expression. Overexpression of miR-1a-3p significantly suppressed C/EBPZ expression, thereby promoting the high expression of adipogenic differentiation-related genes. Conversely, inhibition of miR-1a-3p partially reversed these changes (Fig. 8D-F). Macroscopic analysis, including 3D reconstruction data, H&E staining and Safranin O/Fast Green staining of the femoral head, consistently showed that in vivo overexpression of miR-1a-3p significantly exacerbated bone loss and destruction, promoting the progression of GA-ONFH. In contrast, miR-1a-3p knockout partially alleviated these damages (Fig. 8G-I). These results suggest that modulating miR-1a-3p expression in vivo can aggravate or mitigate GA-ONFH by affecting the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs in the femoral head. To further investigate whether miR-1a-3p influences femoral head necrosis by partially affecting the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, we performed transcriptome sequencing on BMSCs with overexpressed or knocked-down miR-1a-3p (Supplementary Data. 6A, F). Bioinformatics analysis revealed that OE-miR-1a-3p significantly promoted the expression of adipogenic differentiation-related genes while downregulating several genes related to bone mineralization (Supplementary Data. 6B-E). Conversely, knockdown of miR-1a-3p inhibited the expression of adipogenic-related genes and upregulated the expression of bone mineralization-related genes to some extent (Supplementary Data. 6G-J). Finally, we examined the expression of bone mineralization-related genes in the femoral head through IHC staining and Western blotting (Supplementary Data. 7A-C). Combining these results with previous findings, we found that miR-1a-3p may also downregulate osteogenic-related gene expression, thereby exacerbating femoral head necrosis, while knockdown of miR-1a-3p can reverse this process.

Discussion

Epidemiological studies of non-traumatic necrosis of the femoral head have reported 100,000 to 200,000 new cases in China each year, of which GA-ONFH accounts for the highest proportion [31]. GA-ONFH has become a common and refractory disease in clinic, which seriously affects the quality of life of patients [32]. GCs, which are potent immunoregulators that are commonly used in clinical practice to control severe infections and autoimmune disorders, are highly likely to induce osteonecrosis of the femoral head. Our previous research revealed that GCs can alter the epigenetic state of adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, enriching the understanding of epigenetic mechanisms that are involved in the development of GA-ONFH [29]. Recent studies have shown that the polarization of bone macrophages is involved in bone immunity related to GA-ONFH [33], especially since an imbalance in M1/M2 macrophages promotes the progression of nontraumatic osteonecrosis of the femoral head [34]. Further research indicated that treatment that targets M1 macrophages can significantly inhibit the progression of GA-ONFH, highlighting the crucial role of these cells in the early stages of this disease [20, 33, 35]. Bone macrophages, which are located adjacent to BMSCs, play unique roles in regulating bone formation and maintaining skeletal homeostasis [36]. However, to date, the regulatory mechanism of communication between M1 macrophages and BMSCs in the context of GC treatment has not been reported. Understanding these mechanisms will contribute to the precise prevention and treatment of GA-ONFH in the future.

Building on previous research, our study also confirmed a significant increase in M1 macrophages in femoral head samples and in the bone marrow blood samples from GA-ONFH patients. In vivo, we found reduced expression of osteogenic differentiation genes in the femoral head microenvironment, along with spatial colocalization of M1 macrophages and BMSCs. in vitro, we used a GC-stimulated M1 macrophage model to better simulate the in vivo GA-ONFH microenvironment. Furthermore, we established coculture systems with M1 macrophages and BMSCs and performed experiments involving the addition of exosomes derived from M1 macrophages to BMSC cultures. The results revealed that both n-M1 and GC-M1 macrophages significantly inhibited the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Several recent studies have shown that M1 macrophages exert adverse effects on BMSCs in different disease models [27, 37–39]. In contrast, some studies have suggested that inflammatory M1 macrophages present in the early stage of fracture repair contribute to BMSC-mediated bone repair [40, 41]. In our study, transcriptome sequencing data revealed the mechanisms by which n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos inhibited the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, namely, via the widespread suppression of genes that are associated with bone mineralization.

Another significant pathological process that accompanies the development of GA-ONFH is the accumulation of intramedullary fat [1, 2, 14, 42, 43]. Furthermore, we analyzed the effects of n-M1-exos and GC-M1-exos on the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Surprisingly, we found that GC-M1-exos strongly enhanced the adipogenic differentiation and lipid droplet formation of BMSCs, particularly during the induction of adipogenic differentiation. Subsequently, we investigated the mechanisms by which GC-M1-exos significantly promote adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs, and we identified the key regulatory molecule miR-1a-3p through miRNA sequencing. We observed that miR-1a-3p was significantly upregulated in GC-M1-exos, in mouse GA-ONFH femoral head samples, and in the bone marrow blood samples from GA-ONFH patients. Previous studies focused more on the effect of noncoding RNAs on osteonecrosis and the impaired osteogenic potential of BMSCs [44–46]. One study revealed that in GA-ONFH, the circular RNA CDR1 can preferentially promote the differentiation of BMSCs into adipocytes [47]. In our study, we mainly analyzed and screened miRNAs that enhance the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs. in vitro, miR-1a-3p positively regulated the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Further results suggested that GC-M1 macrophages may enhance the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs by secreting exosomes that deliver miR-1a-3p to BMSCs.

Previous studies have shown that miRNAs can recognize homologous sequences, thereby inhibiting mRNA translation or promoting mRNA degradation, which regulates the expression of target genes at the transcriptional or post-transcriptional level [48, 49]. Through bioinformatics analysis combined with a literature review, we identified a negative regulator of adipogenesis, namely, C/EBPZ, as a potential target gene of miR-1a-3p. This conclusion was supported by the in vitro RT‒qPCR, Western blotting, and dual-luciferase reporter assay results. Similar to the findings of Chen et al. [30], knocking down C/EBPZ in BMSCs upregulated the expression of C/EBPα and PPARγ, promoting adipogenic differentiation. Our results confirmed that miR-1a-3p regulated the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs by targeting Cebpz.

In animal experiments, we observed that the overexpression of miR-1a-3p exacerbated femoral head necrosis in mice, whereas the knockdown of miR-1a-3p alleviated GA-ONFH. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of the potential mechanisms involved: (1) Anatomically and pathologically, the femoral head in GA-ONFH shows significant fat accumulation, which impairs blood circulation. Modulating the expression of miR-1a-3p to reduce intramedullary fat generation can significantly slow bone necrosis. Similarly, research by Wei Zou et al. indicated that ablation of intramedullary adipocytes in adult mice can induce substantial bone formation [50]; (2) Numerous studies have demonstrated that BMSCs possess multilineage differentiation potential and that inhibiting their adipogenic differentiation has a markedly beneficial effect on enhancing bone repair [51–54]. Our findings indicate that overexpression or knockdown of miR-1a-3p significantly enhances or diminishes BMSC adipogenic differentiation and impacts the expression of osteogenic differentiation-related genes to some extent; (3) Transcriptomic sequencing of BMSCs with miR-1a-3p overexpression or knockdown in vitro revealed that overexpression of miR-1a-3p significantly suppressed the expression of osteogenic-related genes, whereas its knockdown enhanced the expression of osteogenic differentiation genes (Supplementary Data 6). On the basis of the characteristics of miRNAs, we speculate that miR-1a-3p may directly regulate the expression of specific osteogenic-related genes, and further studies will be conducted to explore this possibility in the future.

This study has several limitations. The signaling communication between BMSCs and M1 macrophages is bidirectional. While this study focused on BMSCs as recipient cells and examined the regulatory effects of M1 macrophages on BMSCs, it did not extensively investigate the potential impact of BMSCs on M1 macrophages. Additionally, we did not conduct in-depth research on changes in M2 macrophages during the development of GA-ONFH or the possible associated mechanisms. The mechanisms by which M2 macrophages regulate BMSCs under different disease/external stress conditions are complex and varied. In the future, we plan to further fill this research gap.

Overall, our research elucidated the unique regulatory role of M1 macrophages in the osteogenic/adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs in the context of GC treatment as well as the underlying mechanism. Additionally, this study revealed that exosomal miR-1a-3p promotes the adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs to participate in the occurrence and development of GA-ONFH via the targeted inhibition of C/EBPZ expression. These findings enrich the understanding of GA-ONFH pathogenesis and provide new insights and potential targets for the precise prevention and treatment of this disease.

Materials and methods

Animal model of GA-ONFH

Female C57BL/6 mice (approximately 7 weeks old, weighing 15–20 g) were purchased from SIPEIFU Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All the animals were uniformly managed by professional animal caretakers at the Institute of Model Animals, Wuhan University, under standard conditions. After one week of acclimatization to the housing conditions, the mouse model of GA-ONFH was established following the protocol that was reported in the literature [19]. The mice in the model group first received two intraperitoneal injections of LPS (Sigma‒Aldrich), each at a dose of 20 µg/kg, with a 24-hour interval between injections. Subsequently, the mice were given intramuscular injections of methylprednisolone sodium succinate (40 mg/kg, MCE) at 24-hour intervals four times a week for four weeks. The mice in the control group were treated with saline solution, and the dose, frequency, and duration were consistent with those of the model group. During the entire breeding period, the height of all the feeding troughs was raised to force the mice to stand on their hind legs while eating and drinking. Additionally, in the model + OE-miR group, an intra-articular injection of OE-miR-1a-3p lentivirus was administered prior to model establishment. In the model + sh-miR group, an intra-articular injection of sh-miR-1a-3p lentivirus was similarly administered before model establishment. Injections were given to both groups once a week for a total of two injections.

Tissue/blood sample collection

Clinical samples: After screening, a total of 5 patients who were diagnosed with GA-ONFH (male = 2, female = 3) and 5 patients who were diagnosed with traumatic femoral head necrosis (male = 2, female = 3) were included in the study. Patients with stages III and IV femoral head necrosis (Ficat and Arlet classification) who were admitted to the Department of Orthopedics Trauma and Microsurgery, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, between June 2022 and June 2023 were investigated with noninvasive imaging techniques (simple X-ray, MRI, CT), after which they underwent hip replacement surgery. All the procedures were conducted after informed consent was obtained from the patients. Femoral head tissues and bone marrow blood samples were collected from patients during hip replacement surgery. Information on patient demographics is provided in the Supplementary Data. 8.

Mouse samples: All the mice were euthanized with an overdose of pentobarbital (100 mg/kg) in the fifth week after their initial treatment. Femoral head samples were obtained through surgical procedures.

Micro-CT scanning and analysis

Samples were scanned using a micro-CT system with a resolution of 4 μm for the direct observation and assessment of osteonecrosis in mouse femoral heads. 3D models of the femoral heads were reconstructed to analyze trabecular bone parameters, including BV/TV, Tb.Sp, Tb.Th, and bone mineral density.

Pathological histological staining

Femoral head samples were progressively fixed in formaldehyde, decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned consecutively. H&E staining was performed on sections from each group. For Safranin O/Fast Green staining, sections were treated with Safranin O for 5 min and Fast Green for 3 min, followed by dehydration, clearing, and mounting. Masson’s trichrome staining was used to stain nuclei with hematoxylin, cytoplasm and muscle with Biebrich scarlet, and collagen with aniline blue. For IHC staining, deparaffinized sections underwent antigen retrieval, followed by incubation with primary antibodies (COL1, RUNX2, OCN, CD86, BMP2, PPARγ, and C/EBPZ), and then with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Sections were developed with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were captured using a Leica Aperio VERSA 8, with at least three representative fields per slide. Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software was used for analysis, and IHC results were quantified via integrated optical density.

IF staining

The femoral head tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval using sodium citrate. The sections were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. In this study, CD86 (91882, CST) was used to label M1 macrophages, while CD29 (ab30394, Abcam) and CD44 (5640, CST) were used together to label and localize BMSCs. DAPI staining was used to stain the cell nuclei.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 cells and mouse BMSCs were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (Guangzhou) Inc., and all the cells met quality verification standards. RAW264.7 cells were cultured in a specialized medium that was designed for RAW 264.7 cells (CM-0190, Procell). In accordance with the protocol described in the literature [55], M1 macrophages were generated by stimulation with 100 ng/mL LPS + 2.5 ng/mL IFN-γ. BMSCs were cultured in complete medium specifically formulated for mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (MUXMX-90011, Cyagen). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in an environment of saturated humidity and 5% CO2. Once the cells reached 60–80% confluence, they were used for further experimentation.

Flow cytometry

RAW 264.7 cells and M1 macrophages were analyzed using flow cytometry. M1 macrophages from the two different treatment groups were costained with an anti-CD86 antibody (91882, CST) and an anti-CD11b antibody (93169, CST). The results were then analyzed and processed using FlowJo software.

Coculture model

To better study the impact of M1 macrophages (n-M1 and GC-M1) on the functionality of BMSCs, a common in vitro coculture model was used. Briefly, the first method involved seeding M1 macrophages and BMSCs in the upper and lower chambers, respectively, of a permeable cell culture chamber (Corning). The second method involved the addition of exosomes derived from M1 macrophages to BMSC culture medium, with a final exosome concentration of 40 µg/ml. After 12 h of coculture, the next steps of the experimental procedure were carried out.

Induction of osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation

BMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at an appropriate density. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, cell differentiation was induced by culture in a specialized osteogenic induction medium (MUXMX-90021, Cyagen) to promote osteogenic differentiation or in adipogenic induction medium (MUXMX-90031, Cyagen) to promote adipogenic differentiation. After 21 days of induction, alizarin red staining and ALP staining were performed to detect calcium deposits and bone mineralization. The absorbance value of Alizarin Red staining at 570 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by quantitative analysis. ALP activity was quantitatively measured using a commercial kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (P0321M, Beyotime). The optical density at 450 nm was recorded using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lipid vacuoles were detected by oil red O staining after 14 days of induction and the absorbance value of oil red O staining at 405 nm was measured for quantitative analysis.

Extraction and identification of exosomes

Cell supernatants were collected from M1 macrophages in each group, and exosomes were extracted using high-speed centrifugation [56]. TEM analysis: Exosome solutions were directly applied to copper grids, and excess liquid was removed with filter paper. Then, the samples were allowed to dry naturally at room temperature. Subsequently, 3% ammonium molybdate negative staining solution was added to the grids and incubated for 3 min. Then, excess stain was removed with filter paper. The grids were then placed in a transmission electron microscope for observation, and electron microscopy images were captured. NTA was performed using a dynamic light scattering instrument to determine the size of the exosomes. The expression of exosome-specific membrane proteins CD9, CD63, and TSG101, as well as the negative marker Calnexin, was then measured using Western blotting.

Exosome internalization experiment

Briefly, following the manufacturer’s instructions, PKH26 dye solution (Sigma‒Aldrich) was added to exosomes, thoroughly mixed, and then incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. The exosomes were then centrifuged at 120,000 × g for 90 min to remove unbound dye. The exosomes were coincubated with cells for 12 h. The supernatants were discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. The cells were subsequently washed three times with PBS and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min. The cells were then washed three times with PBS in the dark. Phalloidin staining was performed by diluting the solution to 2 drops/ml in PBS and incubating it at 37 °C for 30 min. The phalloidin solution was then discarded, and the cells were washed 3–5 times with PBS in the dark. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were subsequently acquired via a laser confocal microscope.

RT‒qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells lysed with TRIzol reagent (Sigma), after which miRNAs and mRNAs were separately extracted using a miRNA extraction kit (ER601-01-V2, TransGen) and a mRNA extraction kit (ER111, TransGen), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For miRNA, cDNA was synthesized using a miRNA cDNA synthesis kit (CW2141, CWBIO), and quantitative analysis was conducted with the corresponding miRNA qPCR detection kit (CW2142, CWBIO). For mRNA, cDNA was synthesized using a Revertaid RT reverse transcription Kit (Thermo Scientific, K2981), and real-time RT–qPCR was performed using a SYBR Color qPCR mixture (Vazyme) and a Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR System. The sequences of primers that were used in this study are listed in the Supplementary Data. 9, 10. The relative expression levels of mRNA or miRNA were normalized to those of β-actin or U6, respectively, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was used for evaluation.

Western blotting

To extract proteins from exosomes, cells, and femoral head tissues, lysis was performed in RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (5892970001, Roche) for 30 min. The lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the supernatants. The protein concentrations of the supernatants were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher). Samples were prepared for SDS‒PAGE by adding an appropriate volume of SDS‒PAGE protein buffer and boiling for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein were loaded and separated via SDS‒PAGE, after which the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in TBST for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against β-actin (Abcam, ab227387), COL1a1 (CST, 91144 S), C/EBPα (CST, 2295 S), PPARγ (Abcam, ab41928), FABP4 (Proteintech, 12802-1-AP), C/EBPZ (ABclonal, A16478), BMP2 (ABclonal, A0231), RUNX2 (CST, 12556), CD9 (ABclonal, A19027), CD63 (ABclonal, A19023), and TSG101 (ABclonal, A2216). After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. β-Actin was used as an internal control. Visualization of the protein bands was achieved via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent.

miR-1a-3p lentivirus and Cebpz plasmid

To evaluate the effects of miR-1a-3p overexpression and knockdown in BMSCs and M1 macrophages, we constructed miR-1a-3p overexpression and knockdown lentiviruses; the sequence information is provided in the Supplementary Data. 11. These lentiviruses were prepared by GenePharma Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China), and the optimal multiplicity of infection was determined to be 40 after experimentation. Four knockdown plasmids targeting the Cebpz gene were designed, and the sequence information is available in the Supplementary Data. 12. These plasmids were prepared by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Transfection of BMSCs was performed according to the standard protocol provided with the Lipofectamine 2000 Reagent (Thermo Fisher). Ultimately, sh-C/EBPZ-1 was verified to have the most effective knockdown effect.

mRNA stability assay

To assess mRNA stability, BMSCs were transduced with lentivirus for miR-1a-3p overexpression or knockdown. After 72 h of transduction, actinomycin D (A9415, Sigma-Aldrich) was added at a final concentration of 5 µg/mL to inhibit transcription. The cells were collected at different time points (0, 2, 4, and 8 h) after actinomycin D treatment. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and the expression levels of Cebpz mRNA were quantified at each time point via RT-qPCR. The mRNA decay rate of Cebpz was calculated relative to the 0-hour time point.

FISH analysis of miR-1a-3p

Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (prepared in DEPC water) for more than 12 hours. After fixation, the tissues were subjected to gradient alcohol dehydration, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were deparaffinized and hydrated, followed by antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using microwave heating. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2. Prehybridization was performed at 37°C for 1 hour, followed by hybridization with 2 µM mmu-miR-1a-3p probe at 42°C overnight. Posthybridization washing was performed with saline sodium citrate buffers of decreasing concentrations. The nuclei were stained with DAPI, and the slides were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium for imaging and analysis with a fluorescence microscope (CY3 channel). The probe sequence was 5’-TGGAATGTAAAGAAGTATGTAT-3’.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the samples using TRIzol reagent, after which the quality and concentration of the RNA were quantified. Library preparation was performed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit. Sequencing was conducted with an Illumina platform with a paired-end 150 bp configuration. The raw data were processed for quality control, and the reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2, and functional enrichment analysis was performed for the significantly differentially expressed genes.

Exosomal miRNA sequencing

Exosomes were isolated from cell culture supernatants using ultracentrifugation, and miRNA was extracted from the exosomes using a miRNA extraction kit. The RNA integrity and concentration were assessed. Small RNA libraries were constructed using the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit and quantified. High-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with single-end 50-bp reads. Quality control of the raw data was conducted, followed by trimming and filtering. The miRNA sequences were aligned to the reference genome using the Bowtie algorithm. Quantification of miRNA expression was performed with miRDeep2, and differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package, incorporating normalization and statistical testing to identify significantly regulated miRNAs.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

293T cells were seeded in a 12-well plate at the appropriate density per well and cultured for 24 hours. The dual-luciferase reporter vectors GP-transfect-Mate (Cebpz-miR-1a-3p-wt and Cebpz-miR-1a-3p-mut) were cotransfected into 293T cells with either the miR-1a-3p mimic or mimic-NC. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours posttransfection, luciferase activity was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega). The assay included measuring firefly luciferase activity followed by Renilla luciferase activity in the same sample. The results were normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity to determine the transfection efficiency. This normalization allows accurate assessment of the interaction between miR-1a-3p and the Cebpz 3’ UTR.

Quantitative and statistical analysis

All the data are presented as the mean ± SD for each group. Analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 22.0) and GraphPad Prism 10.0. All the experiments were independently replicated at least three times. Differences between groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Holm-Sidak multiple comparison test, two-tailed Student t test and two-way ANOVA followed by Holm-Sidak multiple comparison test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.
