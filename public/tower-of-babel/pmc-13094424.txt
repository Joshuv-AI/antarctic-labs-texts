Global real-world data and evidence landscape

A growing body of work now recognises real-world evidence (RWE) as a necessary complement to randomised clinical trials for answering questions that trials cannot fully resolve, including treatment effectiveness in routine practice, long-term safety, and outcomes in under-represented patient groups.1 Unlike tightly controlled trials, studies using real-world data (RWD) capture the heterogeneity of clinical decision making stemming from variation by treating physician, local diagnostic practices, comorbidity profiles, and local resource constraints, and therefore help to contextualise trial findings, identify subgroups with differential advantage or disadvantage, reveal gaps between guideline recommendations and day-to-day practice, and inform pragmatic therapeutic choices.2,3 Regulatory and health-technology bodies have increasingly formalised this role, as major frameworks now describe when and how RWD and RWE can raise study quality and reproducibility so that observational findings can more reliably support regulatory decisions, guideline development, and reimbursement assessments, provided study design and data quality meet rigorous standards.4, 5, 6, 7 At the same time, the field has emphasised the need for methodological rigour, transparent protocols, prespecified analytic plans, and appropriate causal-inference methods to address confounding, selection bias, and incomplete follow-up, while also exploring federated and big-data approaches to aggregate insights across institutions without centralising sensitive records.8,9

Real-world challenges in africa

In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly across Africa, routine oncology care is dominated by conventional cytotoxic regimens, most commonly platinum-based chemotherapy across all cancer types, with radiotherapy used for locally advanced disease, for brain metastases, and for urgent palliative indications such as spinal cord compression or superior vena cava syndrome.10, 11, 12 Surgery is rarely pursued for either practical, feasibility, or out of pocket reasons. Late-stage presentation, constrained drug formularies, and limited access to targeted agents and supportive care amplify reliance on archaic conventional modalities and shape day-to-day clinical practice. At the same time, high-quality cancer registries and harmonised diagnostic services are often incomplete and fragmented, if not absent, so RWD on survival outcomes, staging, treatment patterns, pathology and molecular diagnostics, response rates, and toxicity profiles are poorly documented at national and regional levels. This undermines, directly or indirectly, the ability to adapt international trial results to local practice, to develop context-sensitive guidelines, and to prioritise scarce resources for the greatest population benefit.13, 14, 15 Pathology and laboratory shortfalls, from limited histopathology capacity and scarce molecular testing to inconsistent quality assurance, further restrict accurate staging and biomarker-driven treatment selection and prevent the creation of robust observational datasets.13,16, 17, 18 Screening programmes are largely absent except for breast, cervical, and colorectal cancers.19, 20, 21, 22 Adverse market dynamics, from low commercial incentives for manufacturers and high prices to fragile supply chains, frequently prevent adherence to international recommendations and reduce the feasibility of implementing trial-proven regimens locally. As a result, African oncology research remains persistently under-represented in the scientific literature, as publication bias against small-scale or context-specific studies perpetuates invisibility and limits the policy impact of locally generated RWD. The combined effect has multifold consequences, as patients are less likely to be identified for or enrolled in clinical trials; local investigators face obstacles to designing and conducting context-relevant studies; and policymakers lack the empirical basis to negotiate procurement, reimbursement, or guideline adaptation.

Real-world challenges in morocco

At the Moroccan level, another point raised from our experience in both public and private academic institutions, with the lack of cost-effectiveness studies and its comparison between the two settings, is cancer management that differs markedly between public and private settings. Cancer management is heavily influenced by patients’ ability to afford and continue planned treatment, institutional caseload, and the range and timeliness of services available, particularly rapid access to treating physicians and the capacity to initiate and sustain therapy without delay. This produces measurable variation in access to therapies, dose intensity, supportive care, response rates, quality of life, and survival outcomes. In private centres, for instance, patients who can afford treatment costs and uninterrupted supportive care are more likely to complete recommended regimens, whereas in public hospitals, treatment interruptions, dose reductions, or substitution with older, less costly regimens are common.

Reporting these observations as real-world, retrospective, or prospective studies depends on several practical and methodological elements. Firstly, human resources that include departmental leaders, faculty, treating physicians, residents, and PhD candidates are the actors who generate evidence and shape local practice. While formal qualifications matter, research productivity often hinges more on curiosity, motivation, and perseverance, qualities that can offset limited resources when supported by mentorship and protected time. Secondly, clinical workload and patient-management burdens shape what is feasible, particularly in busy services with high patient turnover that require multidisciplinary teams and routine, self-directed learning to sustain research activities. Thirdly, single-centre series that are not representative of the institution’s case mix risk biased inference; where representativeness is limited, multicentre collaboration is encouraged so that studies include sufficient numbers of treated patients and reflect the heterogeneity of care. Fourthly, collaboration and governance barriers frequently undermine multicentre work, as variable engagement from national centres, delays or restrictive local ethics committee processes, low contributor motivation, and the absence of funding or remuneration for data collection all limit participation. Fifthly, data collection and quality are often the rate-limiting steps, since much information still requires manual extraction from handwritten medical records, producing missing fields, transcription errors, inconsistent granularity, and time-consuming workflows, problems that are magnified when studies are unfunded. Finally, heterogeneity in extracted variables across centres, especially between public and private institutions, complicates pooling and analysis. For instance, public records may lack copies of imaging, pathology reports, RECIST v1.1 response assessments, nonhaematological adverse event documentation. They may lack unclassified adverse event (emerged or related) or cause-of-death information, patient histories may be incomplete, and the definitions and grading of variables may vary with local training and practice.

These considerations raise several practical questions for any multicentre study in Morocco, particularly if we suppose a collaborative study was designed and has taken place following consent of the participating department. In the absence of prior multicentre experience, there is no agreed framework for consent, de-identification, data use agreements, or the application of national law, prerequisites for ethical data sharing and institutional review. Harmonising by adopting a common data dictionary and a standard data model is essential to reduce heterogeneity at extraction and enable pooling. Ensuring data reliability requires training and remuneration for data abstractors, standardised case-report forms, double-entry or adjudication procedures, and pilot testing to quantify missingness and abstraction error. Long-term solutions include investments in data governance, interoperable digital systems, and phased electronic medical records and registry implementation. Short-term pragmatic measures, standardised consent and data use templates, a minimum dataset, secure electronic capture platforms for pilot registries, and national guidance on de-identification and data-sharing must also be considered, as they can substantially lower barriers to multicentre real-world research.

From barriers to solutions

Several international associations and journals, depending on the targeted field, have launched international collaborative studies and initiatives to encourage submissions from LMICs with the laudable goal of elevating research capacity and visibility. Diving back into the literature, many submissions from LMIC settings are hampered by small sample sizes, incomplete or poorly described methods, inadequate statistical analysis, and substandard reporting and interpretation, problems that reflect structural and capacity gaps rather than lack of scientific intent. In such a case, and depending on the journals, editors either reject the paper or respond with extended peer review and editorial support, yet repeated revision cycles cannot substitute for robust study design, reliable data collection, or appropriate analytic expertise, particularly in the absence of an expert mentor.

Many would argue that the common explanations for low contribution rates are frequently invoked due to financial constraints, limited access to diagnostics and treatment, and weak screening and referral systems, but they only tell part of the story. However, from our position, many social, intellectual, and psychological items, in addition to the above-mentioned practical and methodological elements, contribute directly or indirectly to weak evidence or data generation. Such disparities must be documented and accounted for when reporting real-world outcomes; otherwise, analyses risk bias, and a substantial proportion of claims will lose their value.

To reduce risks, improve instruments, and build local ownership before scaling, while avoiding false accusations and hypothetical hypotheses, we plan to investigate the root causes of limited research output by launching ONMORE-Africa (ONcology MOrocco Real Evidence – Africa), a mixed-methods pilot designed to diagnose the root causes of limited oncology research output and barriers to generating RWD and RWE. The pilot study will prioritise inclusion of all public and private academic Moroccan hospitals and up to six nonacademic institutions, as the bulk of oncology research activity, infrastructure and research ethics committee (REC) capacity is located in academic centres; nonacademic hospitals are often less engaged in research and may lack local REC infrastructure. Department heads of the medical and surgical oncology, radiotherapy, and anatomopathology teams, residents, and PhD candidates will be engaging within each site. Data collection comprises, firstly, a structured, standardised survey-based multiple-choice questionnaire, with structured fields and targeted free-text ‘case summary’ prompts capturing infrastructure, workforce, diagnostics, data systems, workflow, incentives, ethical/regulatory bottlenecks, governance and routine metrics, research motivations, and/or solutions in point form. These vary depending on the discipline, participant profile, and specialisation. Secondly, data collection comprises a chart-based audit of 30-50 recent oncology cases per department to quantify data completeness, abstraction time, inter-rater reliability, and core outcome availability (patients characteristics, diagnosis, pathology, molecular biology, consumables, procedures and techniques, stage, treatment, response, toxicity, supportive care, survival). Analysis will use descriptive quantitative statistics for survey and audit data and thematic analysis for free-text case summaries, with explicit triangulation across data streams. To ensure data quality we will pilot test instruments, provide standardised training for abstractors, implement 25% double abstraction with predefined discrepancy resolution procedures, and apply validation checks guided by a data dictionary. Informed consent will be obtained before questionnaire participation. Retrospective chart audits will proceed under institutional authorisation or an REC-approved waiver justified by minimal risk and impracticability of individual consent, in accordance with Moroccan Law No. 28-13 and applicable data-protection requirements. Governance steps include centralised protocol approval, local REC submissions, and template data use agreements/material transfer agreement drafts; all data will be de-identified locally and analysed centrally. The pilot will run over 6 months, in which the approvals and instrument finalisation will require 6-8 weeks, fieldwork 8-10 weeks, and analysis and co-design workshops 4-6 weeks. If ethics or institutional approvals exceed 6 weeks, fieldwork may be extended by 4 weeks as a contingency. Predefined feasibility metrics include a site recruitment rate of >50%, a median abstraction time of ≤20 min/chart, and ≥80% completeness for the minimum dataset variables. Findings from the pilot will be used to co-design interventions for the local government and international associations to include training modules, mentorship, registry pilots, and the linking of evidence to policy to increase impact and buy-in. Findings will also be used to streamline ethics pathways and to refine instruments and workflows, before expanding the study across the African continent. The ultimate aims are to strengthen local research capacity, improve the quality and representativeness of real-world oncology evidence, and produce actionable recommendations for institutions, funders, and journals.
