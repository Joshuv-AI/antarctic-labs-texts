Non-Asymptotic Error Bounds for Causally Conditioned Directed Information Rates of Gaussian Sequences This work was supported in part by NSF ECCS 2412435 and NIH 1R01NS147767-01.
Yuping Zheng, Andrew Lamperski

Abstract
Directed information and its causally conditioned variations are often used to measure causal influences between random processes. In practice, these quantities must be measured from data. Non-asymptotic error bounds for these estimates are known for sequences over finite alphabets, but less is known for real-valued data. This paper examines the case in which the data are sequences of Gaussian vectors. We provide an explicit formula for causally conditioned directed information rate based on optimal prediction and define an estimator based on this formula. We show that our estimator gives an error of order O ( N − 1 / 2 log ( N ) ) O\left(N^{-1/2}\log(N)\right) with high probability, where N N is the total sample size.

INTRODUCTION

Directed information (DI) measures the direction of the information flow in coupled dynamical systems.
Over the years, directed information has been widely used for diverse areas including networked control systems
(
Tanaka et al. 2017a
;
Tanaka et al. 2017b
)
, econometrics
(
Permuter et al. 2011
;
Etesami et al. 2017
)
, neuroscience
(
Quinn et al. 2011a
;
Tsur et al. 2023
)
, genetics
(
Rao et al. 2007b
;
Rao et al. 2007a
)
, and social sciences
(
Ver Steeg and Galstyan 2012
)
.

The idea of directed information originated from the work of
(
Marko 1973
)
in the context of bidirectional communication theory. Based on these ideas,
(
Massey et al. 1990
)
introduced the definition of directed information for a communication channel with feedback.
Causally conditioned directed information was introduced in
(
Kramer 1998
)
, and can be viewed as generalization of directed information.

Another information measure closely related to DI, and commonly studied in physics, is transfer entropy
(
Geweke 1982
;
Schreiber 2000
;
Barnett et al. 2009
;
Hempel and Loos 2024
)
.

The review
(
Amblard and Michel 2012
)
points out that tools from directed information theory can be naturally used to measure Granger causality
(
Granger 1969
)
. In particular, it is shown in
(
Amblard and Michel 2011
)
that DI theory can be used to study Granger causality graphs of time series.

There have been extensive studies on using directed information to determine the structure of networked stochastic dynamic systems
(
Quinn et al. 2011b
;
Quinn et al. 2015
;
Quinn et al. 2017
;
Young et al. 2021
;
Subramanian et al. 2021
;
Etesami and Kiyavash 2014
;
Etesami et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2018
;
Divernois et al. 2024
;
Etesami et al. 2023
;
Hempel and Loos 2024
)
.

For sequences over finite alphabets, non-asymptotic error bounds for directed information and causally conditioned variants were given in
Jiao et al. 2013
;
Quinn et al. 2015
. However, no non-asymptotic bounds are known for data from infinite sets.

This paper examines the special case of data from infinite sets in which the data are sequences of Gaussian vectors. The main contribution is an estimation scheme for causally conditioned directed information rate which achieves an error of order
O(N^{-1/2}\log(N))
, where
N
is the length of the time series data. To derive the estimator, we provide an analytic formula for the causally conditioned directed information rate based on optimal prediction theory.
Several closely related formulas appear in prior works
(
Etesami and Kiyavash 2014
;
Divernois et al. 2024
;
Barnett et al. 2009
;
Chicharro 2011
;
Hempel and Loos 2024
)
and match intermediate steps of the derivation of Theorem
1
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present preliminaries to define the problem. Section
3
gives the main results. The analytic formula for causally conditioned directed information rate is proved in Section
4
and the error bound for the estimator is proved in Section
5
. Conclusions are given in Section
6
.

2.2
Causally Conditioned Direceted Information Rates

If
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}
, and
\mathbf{z}
are random variables with joint density
p_{XYZ}
with respect to Lebesgue measure, then the mutual information of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, conditioned on
\mathbf{z}
is given by:

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{p_{Y|XZ}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})}{p_{Y|Z}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z})}\right]

(1)

Here
p_{Y|Z}
is the conditional density of
\mathbf{y}
given
\mathbf{z}
while
p_{Y|XZ}
is the conditional density of
\mathbf{y}
given
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
.

For stochastic processes
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{z}
, causally conditioned directed information is defined as in
(
Quinn et al. 2015
)
by:

I(\mathbf{x}[0:k]\to\mathbf{y}[0:k]\|\mathbf{z}[0:k])=\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}I(\mathbf{x}[0:i];\mathbf{y}[i]|\mathbf{y}[0:i],\mathbf{z}[0:i]).

(2)

When
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}
, and
\mathbf{z}
are vector-valued, our Python-style indexing convention gives that

\mathbf{x}[0:i]=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}[0]^{\top}&\cdots&\mathbf{x}[i-1]^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

We do not include the term with
i=0
in the sum, since, in this case,
\mathbf{x}[0:0]
,
\mathbf{y}[0:0]
, and
\mathbf{z}[0:0]
correspond to empty lists of vectors.

The definition of causally conditioned directed information from (
2
) corresponds to Equation (5) from
(
Quinn et al. 2015
)
, and quantifies how much information the strict past values
\mathbf{x}[0],\ldots,\mathbf{x}[i-1]
contains about the present value of
\mathbf{y}[i]
, conditioned on the strict past values
\mathbf{y}[0],\ldots,\mathbf{y}[i-1]
and
\mathbf{z}[0],\ldots,\mathbf{z}[i-1]
. The original definition from
(
Kramer 1998
)
deals with non-strict conditioning, and in that work, the terms in the sum actually correspond to
I(\mathbf{x}[0:i+1];\mathbf{y}[i]|\mathbf{y}[0:i],\mathbf{z}[0:i+1])
in our notation.

The causally conditioned directed information rate is

I^{\infty}(\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y}\|\mathbf{z})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}I(\mathbf{x}[0:k]\to\mathbf{y}[0:k]\|\mathbf{z}[0:k]).

2.3
Problem

Let
\mathbf{w}[k]=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}[k]^{\top}&\mathbf{y}[k]^{\top}&\mathbf{z}[k]^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
be a stationary stochastic process such that each
\mathbf{w}[k]
is a zero mean vector with real entries. The autocovariance sequence and power spectral density (PSD) are

R[k]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}[i+k]\mathbf{w}[i]^{\top}]\quad\Phi(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}z^{-k}R[k].

We make the following assumptions on
\mathbf{w}[k]
:

Assumption 1

There exist positive
c_{\min}
and
c_{\max}
, such that

c_{\min}I\preceq\Phi(e^{j\omega})\preceq c_{\max}I
for all
\omega\in[0,2\pi]
.

Assumption 2

\Phi(z)
is rational.

Assumption 3

\mathbf{w}[k]
is Gaussian.

Under these assumptions, the problem studied in this paper is to compute estimates of the causally conditioned directed information rate,
I^{\infty}(\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y}\|\mathbf{z})
, from a finite collection of data,
\mathbf{w}[0:N]
, and quantify the error.

3
Information Rate Formula and Estimate

3.1
Optimal Prediction

Recall that
\mathbf{w}[k]=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}[k]^{\top}&\mathbf{y}[k]^{\top}&\mathbf{z}[k]^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
. Set
\mathbf{v}[k]=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}[k]^{\top}&\mathbf{z}[k]^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
. Then
\mathbf{v}
satisfies Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
, with the same constants
c_{\min}
and
c_{\max}
for Assumption
1
.

Assumptions
1
and
2
imply that there is a strictly causal transfer matrices,
H
and
J
, such that the minimum mean-squared error predictors for
\mathbf{w}[k]
and
\mathbf{v}[k]
are given by:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{w}}[k]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}H_{i}\mathbf{w}[k-i]

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{v}}[k]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}\mathbf{v}[k-i].

The predictions,
\hat{\mathbf{w}}[k]
and prediction errors
\mathbf{w}[k]-\hat{\mathbf{w}}[k]
are zero-mean by the assumption that
\mathbf{w}[k]
are zero mean.
Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, the prediction errors
\mathbf{w}[k]-\hat{\mathbf{w}}[k]
are orthogonal of the linear span of the past data. Specifically, if
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is vector which is a linear function of
\mathbf{w}[k-1],\mathbf{w}[k-2],\ldots
, then
\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{w}[k]-\hat{\mathbf{w}}[k])\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\top}]=0
. See
Wiener and Masani 1957
.

Similarly,
\hat{\mathbf{v}}[k]
gives the minimum mean-squared error prediction of
\mathbf{v}[k]
, given
\mathbf{v}[k-1],\mathbf{v}[k-2],\ldots
, and the prediction errors are zero mean and orthogonal to linear functions of the past measurements.

Under Assumption
2
,
H
and
J
can be computed from the Kalman filter, if
\Phi
were known. See
Kailath et al. 2000
.

Denote the prediction error covariance matrices by:

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{xx}&\Sigma_{xy}&\Sigma_{xz}\\
\Sigma_{yx}&\Sigma_{yy}&\Sigma_{yz}\\
\ \Sigma_{zx}&\Sigma_{zy}&\Sigma_{zz}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{w}[k]-\hat{\mathbf{w}}[k])(\mathbf{w}[k]-\hat{\mathbf{w}}[k])^{\top}\right]

(3a)

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{yy}&\Gamma_{yz}\\
\Gamma_{zy}&\Gamma_{zz}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{v}[k]-\hat{\mathbf{v}}[k])(\mathbf{v}[k]-\hat{\mathbf{v}}[k])^{\top}\right]

(3b)

3.2
Causally Conditioned Directed Information Rates from Optimal Predictors

The following theorem gives an exact formula for the causally conditioned directed information rate in terms of the prediction error covariances described above. It is proved in Section
4
.

Theorem 1

Let
\mathbf{w}[k]=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}[k]^{\top}&\mathbf{y}[k]^{\top}&\mathbf{z}[k]^{\top}\end{bmatrix}
be a stationary, zero-mean stochastic process satisfying Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
. Let
\Sigma
and
\Gamma
be the matrices from (
3
). The causally conditioned directed information rate is given by:

\displaystyle I^{\infty}(\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y}\|\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\Gamma_{yy})}{\det(\Sigma_{yy})}.

3.3
Estimating Causally Conditioned DI Rate

Here we present our estimation procedure and corresponding error bound. The estimation procedure is based on the Theorem
1
and the following preliminary result:

Lemma 1

For
i\geq 1
let
A_{i}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}[0:i]\mathbf{w}[0:i]^{\top}]
and
B_{i}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}[i]\mathbf{w}[0:i]^{\top}]
. Let
\Sigma
be the error covariance matrix from (
3
). Let
H
be the optimal predictor for
\mathbf{w}
. Let
\rho\in(0,1)
be such that every pole,
s
, of
H
, satisfies
|s|<\rho
and let
b\geq\sup_{|z|\geq\rho}\|H(z)\|_{2}
.

If Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, then for all
i\geq 1
the following bound holds:

\|\Sigma-\left(R[0]-B_{i}A_{i}^{-1}B_{i}^{\top}\right)\|_{2}\leq\frac{b^{2}\rho^{2(i+1)}}{(1-\rho)^{2}}c_{\max}

An analogous bound based on the properties of the predictor,
J
also holds. To cover both cases, let
\rho\in(0,1)
be such that
|s|\leq\rho
if
s
is a pole of either
H
or
J
, and let
b=\max\{\sup_{|z|\geq\rho}\|H(z)\|_{2},\sup_{|z|\geq\rho}\|J(z)\|_{2}\}
.

For
p\geq 1
, note that

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}[0:p+1]\mathbf{w}[0:p+1]^{\top}]=\begin{bmatrix}A_{p}&B_{p}^{\top}\\
B_{p}&R[0]\end{bmatrix}.

So, Lemma
1
indicates that to estimate
\Sigma
, it suffices to estimate
\underline{R}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}[0:p+1]\mathbf{w}[0:p+1]^{\top}]
for sufficiently large
p
. Similarly, to estimate
\Gamma
, it suffices to estimate
\underline{Q}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}[0:p+1]\mathbf{v}[0:p+1]^{\top}]
.

Let
\mathbf{w}[0:N]
be the available data set. (Recall that
\mathbf{v}[i]
is a subvector of
\mathbf{w}[i]
.) Fix
1\leq p<N
and set
M=N-p
. Estimates of
\underline{R}
and
\underline{Q}
are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{R}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathbf{w}[k:k+p+1]\mathbf{w}[k:k+p+1]^{\top}

(4a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathbf{v}[k:k+p+1]\mathbf{v}[k:k+p+1]^{\top}.

(4b)

Partition the estimates as:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{R}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}&\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}&\tilde{\mathbf{R}}[0]\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{C}}&\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}&\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}[0]\end{bmatrix}.

Then our estimates for the error covariances,
\Sigma
and
\Gamma
are given, respectively by:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{R}}[0]-\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{xx}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{xy}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{xz}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yx}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yz}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{zx}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{zy}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{zz}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}[0]-\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\tilde{\mathbf{C}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{yy}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{yz}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{zy}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{zz}\end{bmatrix}.

In our analysis, we will show that the required inverses exist with high probability, for sufficiently large
M
.

Our estimate for causally conditioned directed information rate is then given by:

\tilde{\mathbf{I}}=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{yy})}{\det(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy})}.

Our main result is the following non-asymptotic bound. It is proved in Section
5
.

Theorem 2

Let
N
,
M
,
p
,
I(\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y}\|\mathbf{z})
, and
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
be defined as above. Say that
\mathbf{y}[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{Y}}
. For
\nu\in(0,1)
, set

\displaystyle\epsilon=

\displaystyle 2c_{\max}\max\left\{(p+1)8\left(\frac{\log(2\nu^{-1})}{M}+\frac{n_{W}(1+p)}{M}\log(13)\right)\!,\right.

\displaystyle\left.\sqrt{(2p+1)8\left(\frac{\log(2\nu^{-1})}{M}\!+\!\frac{n_{W}(1+p)}{M}\log(13)\right)}\right\}.

If
\epsilon<c_{\min}
, then with probability at least
1-\nu
, the inverses required to compute
\tilde{\mathbf{I}}
exist, and the following error bound holds:

|I(\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y}\|\mathbf{z})-\tilde{\mathbf{I}}|\leq\frac{n_{Y}}{c_{\min}-\epsilon}\cdot\\
\left(c_{\max}\frac{b^{2}\rho^{2(p+1)}}{(1-\rho)^{2}}+\epsilon\left(1+\frac{(c_{\max}+\epsilon)^{2}}{(c_{\min}-\epsilon)^{2}}\right)\right).

In particular, if
a_{1}\log(N)\leq p\leq a_{2}\log(N)
for some
a_{2}\geq a_{1}\geq\frac{1}{4\log(\rho^{-1})}
, then with probability at least
1-\nu

|I(\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y}\|\mathbf{z})-\tilde{\mathbf{I}}|=O\left(\log(N)\sqrt{\frac{\log(\nu^{-1})}{N}}\right).

Remark 1

Recall that
\rho\in(0,1)
is a bound on the magnitudes of the poles of
H
and
J
. When estimating the causally conditioned directed information rates from data, such a bound will typically be unknown, and so choosing
p=\Theta(\log(N))
could result in
\rho^{2p}
shrinking more slowly than
N^{-1/2}
. This problem could be resolved by choosing a polylogarithmic
p
, e.g.,
p=(\log(N))^{2}
, which does not rely on knowing
\rho
, but results in the slightly worse bound of
O\left((\log(N))^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\nu^{-1})}{N}}\right)
.

Remark 2

Our estimators for
\Sigma
and
\Gamma
are precisely the empirical covariances of the residual errors from order
p
vector autoregressive models (fit by least-squares) for
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{v}
, respectively. So, the estimates can be computed from autoregessive fits, rather than directly from empirical covariances of
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{v}
.

Proof of Theorem

First we present some preliminary facts, including a proof of Lemma

. Then Theorem

4.1
Preliminary results

Lemma 2

If Assumption
1
holds, then for all integers with
i<k
,

c_{\min}I\preceq\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}[i:k]\mathbf{w}[i:k]^{\top}]\preceq c_{\max}I.

Proof:

The following proof is modified from Lemma 3 in
(
Lamperski 2023
)
.

Let
\underline{R}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}[i:k]\mathbf{w}[i:k]^{\top}]
. Assume that
\mathbf{w}[\ell]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{W}}
so that
\underline{R}
is an
(k-i)n_{W}\times(k-i)n_{W}
matrix. For any vector,
q\in\mathbb{R}^{(k-i)n_{W}}
, there is a signal
u[\ell]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{W}}
, such that
q=u[0:k-i]
,
u[\ell]=0
for
\ell<0
, and
u[\ell]=0
for
\ell\geq k-i
. Let
\hat{u}
be the Fourier transform of
u
. Then using Assumption
1
, Plancharel’s theorem, and direct computations gives:

\displaystyle c_{\min}q^{\top}q

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{\min}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\hat{u}(e^{j\omega})^{\star}\hat{u}(e^{j\omega})d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\hat{u}(e^{j\omega})^{\star}\Phi(e^{j\omega})\hat{u}(e^{j\omega})d\omega

\displaystyle=q^{\top}\underline{R}q

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{\max}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\hat{u}(e^{j\omega})^{\star}\hat{u}(e^{j\omega})d\omega

\displaystyle=c_{\max}q^{\top}q.

∎

The next result is a variation of Lemma 2 of
Lee and Lamperski 2020
, which is, in turn, a generalization of Lemma 1 of
Goldenshluger et al. 2001
from scalar-valued transfer functions to transfer matrices.

Lemma 3

Let
\Phi
satisfy Assumptions
1
and
2
. Let
H
be the corresponding optimal predictor. Let
\rho\in(0,1)
be such that every pole,
s
, of
H
satisfies
|s|<\rho
. Then
\sup_{|z|\geq\rho}\|H(z)\|_{2}<\infty
. If
H_{i,\mathrm{tail}}(z)=\sum_{k=i+1}^{\infty}H_{k}z^{-k}
for
i\geq 1
and
b\geq\sup_{|z|\geq\rho}\|H(z)\|_{2}
, then

\|H_{i,\mathrm{tail}}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\frac{b\rho^{i+1}}{1-\rho}.

The following elementary fact will be used in a few places, including Lemma
5
, which follows afterwards.

Lemma 4

Let
\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{\top}\\
B&C\end{bmatrix}
be a block matrix such that
A
is positive definite. For any
\Theta
such that
\Theta B^{\top}
and
C
have the same dimensions, the following bound holds:

\displaystyle C-BA^{-1}B^{\top}

\displaystyle\preceq C-\Theta B^{\top}-B\Theta^{\top}+\Theta A\Theta^{\top}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\Theta&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{\top}\\
B&C\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\Theta^{\top}\\
I\end{bmatrix}

Proof:

The equality is a direct calculation. The inequality follows from completing the square:

C-\Theta B^{\top}-B\Theta^{\top}+\Theta A\Theta^{\top}=\\
C-BA^{-1}B^{\top}+(\Theta-BA^{-1})A(\Theta-BA^{-1})^{\top}.

∎

Lemma 5

For
i\geq 1
let
A_{i}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}[0:i]\mathbf{w}[0:i]^{\top}]
,
B_{i}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}[i]\mathbf{w}[0:i]^{\top}]
, and let
H_{i,\mathrm{tail}}
be as in Lemma
3
. Let
\Sigma
be the error covariance matrix from (
3
). If Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, then for all
i\geq 1
the following bounds hold:

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle\preceq R[0]-B_{i}A_{i}^{-1}B_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle\preceq\Sigma+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}H_{i,\mathrm{tail}}(e^{i\theta})\Phi(e^{j\theta})(H_{i,\mathrm{tail}}(e^{i\theta}))^{\star}d\theta

Proof:

First, we prove a more general bound that implies the first inequality. Recall that the prediction errors,
\mathbf{w}[i]-\hat{\mathbf{w}}[i]
are orthogonal to the linear span of
\mathbf{w}[i-1],\mathbf{w}[i-2],\ldots
. For any
\Theta
such that
\mathbf{w}[i]
and
\Theta\mathbf{w}[0:i]
, have the same dimensions, we have:

\displaystyle R[0]-\Theta B_{i}^{\top}-B_{i}\Theta^{\top}+\Theta A_{i}\Theta^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\left(\mathbf{w}[i]-\Theta\mathbf{w}[0:i]\right)\left(\mathbf{w}[i]-\Theta\mathbf{w}[0:i]\right)^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}[i]-\hat{\mathbf{w}}[i]+\hat{\mathbf{w}}[i]-\Theta\mathbf{w}[0:i]\right)\cdot\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left(\mathbf{w}[i]-\hat{\mathbf{w}}[i]+\hat{\mathbf{w}}[i]-\Theta\mathbf{w}[0:i]\right)^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\Sigma+\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\mathbf{w}}[i]-\Theta\mathbf{w}[0:i])(\hat{\mathbf{w}}[i]-\Theta\mathbf{w}[0:i])^{\top}\right]

(5)

\displaystyle\succeq\Sigma,

where the third equality follows from the orthogonality property.

Choosing
\Theta=B_{i}A_{i}^{-1}
implies that
\Sigma\preceq R[0]-B_{i}A_{i}^{-1}B_{i}^{\top}
for all
i\geq 1
.

Finally, we prove the second inequality in the lemma.

For general
\Theta
as above, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{w}[i]-\Theta\mathbf{w}[0:i])(\mathbf{w}[i]-\Theta\mathbf{w}[0:i])^{\top}]

\displaystyle=R[0]-\Theta B_{i}^{\top}-B_{i}\Theta^{\top}+\Theta A_{i}\Theta^{\top}

(6)

\displaystyle\hskip-40.0pt=\Sigma+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}H_{i,\mathrm{tail}}(e^{i\theta})\Phi(e^{j\theta})(H_{i,\mathrm{tail}}(e^{i\theta}))^{\star}d\theta.

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{x}[0:i];\mathbf{y}[i]|\mathbf{y}[0:i],\mathbf{z}[0:i])

A_{i}

.

5.1
Probabilistic Error Bound

Lemma 6

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}
be defined from (
4
) and set
\underline{R}=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{R}}]
. Say that
\mathbf{w}[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{W}}
.
If Assumption
1
holds and
M>0
, then, for all
\nu\in(0,1)
, the following bound holds with probability at least
1-\nu
:

\displaystyle\|\underline{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle 2c_{\max}\max\left\{\!\sqrt{(2p+1)8\left(\frac{\log(2\nu^{-1})}{M}\!+\!\frac{n_{W}(1+p)}{M}\log(13)\right)},\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt\left.(p+1)8\left(\frac{\log(2\nu^{-1})}{M}+\frac{n_{W}(1+p)}{M}\log(13)\right)\right\}.

Proof:

Since
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R}
is symmetric, the variational characterization of the matrix
2
-norm implies that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{\|v\|_{2}\leq 1,s\in\{-1,1\}}sv^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})v.

We will use a covering argument to reduce the problem of bounding the norm to bounding a finite number of terms of the form on the right.

Let
\mathbb{B}
denote the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{W}+n_{W}p}
.
Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{B}
be a
\delta
-cover of
\mathbb{B}
, i.e.,
\forall\|u\|_{2}\leq 1
,
\exists v\in\mathcal{C}
s.t.
\|v\|_{2}\leq 1
and
\|u-v\|_{2}\leq\delta
. As discussed in Example 5.8 of
Wainwright 2019
, the cardinality of
\mathcal{C}
is bounded by

|\mathcal{C}|\leq\left(1+\frac{2}{\delta}\right)^{n_{W}+n_{W}p}.

For any vector,
u
, with
\|u\|_{2}\leq 1
, there is a vector
v\in\mathcal{C}
such that

\|u-v\|_{2}\leq\delta
. Then, for
s\in\{-1,1\}
we have

\displaystyle su^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})u

\displaystyle=s(v+u-v)^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})(v+u-v)

\displaystyle=sv^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})v+s(u-v)^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})(u-v)

\displaystyle+s(u-v)^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})v+sv^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-R)(u-v)

\displaystyle\leq sv^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})v+\|\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R}\|_{2}(2\delta+\delta^{2})

Maximizing both gives:

\displaystyle\sup_{(s,v)\subset\{-1,1\}\times\mathbb{B}}sv^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})v

\displaystyle\leq\max_{(s,v)\subset\{-1,1\}\times\mathcal{C}}sv^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})v

\displaystyle+\|\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R}\|_{2}(2\delta+\delta^{2}).

Therefore, if
2\delta+\delta^{2}<1
, i.e.
\delta<\sqrt{2}-1
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-3\delta}\max_{(s,v)\subset\{-1,1\}\times\mathcal{C}}sv^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})v.

In order to obtain the bound with high probability of the left side, it suffices to bound
sv^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R})v
for fixed
s
and
v
and then use a union bound.

We will utilize an argument based on the Hanson-Wright inequality.

Recall that
N=M+p
. For
k=0,\ldots,M-1
, let
F_{k}
be the matrix such that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathbf{w}[k:k+p+1]=F_{k}\mathbf{w}[0:N]
.
Note that
F_{k}
will have the form
F_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\begin{bmatrix}0&I&0\end{bmatrix}
for zero matrices of appropriate size.

Let
\mathbf{U}=\underline{R}^{-1/2}\mathbf{w}[0:N]
. Then
\mathbf{U}
is a Gaussian vector with mean
0
and covariance
I
. Then, we can express:

\tilde{\mathbf{R}}=\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}F_{i}\underline{R}^{1/2}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\underline{R}^{1/2}F_{i}^{\top}.

If
A=s\underline{R}^{1/2}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}F_{k}^{\top}vv^{\top}F_{k}\right)\underline{R}^{1/2}
, then
sv^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}v=\mathbf{U}^{\top}A\mathbf{U}
. Recall that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{R}}]=\underline{R}
.

The Hanson-Wright inequality for Gaussians implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(sv^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}v-sv^{\top}\underline{R}v>\epsilon\right)

(7)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\|A\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{\epsilon}{\|A\|_{2}}\right\}\right).

See
(
Vershynin 2018
)
a general discussion of the Hanson-Wright inequality and
(
Lamperski 2023
)
for a derivation of the explicit
1/8
factor for Gaussians.

So, we need to bound the matrix norms.

Note that

\displaystyle\|A\|_{F}\leq\|\underline{R}\|_{2}\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}F_{k}^{\top}vv^{\top}F_{k}\right\|_{F}\!.

Lemma
2
implies that
\|\underline{R}\|_{2}\leq c_{\max}
.

Then we calculate

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}F_{k}^{\top}vv^{\top}F_{k}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathrm{Tr}\left(F_{i}^{\top}vv^{\top}F_{i}F_{k}^{\top}vv^{\top}F_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\left(v^{\top}F_{i}F_{k}^{\top}v\right)^{2}.

To bound the right, note that
F_{i}F_{k}^{\top}
can only be non-zero when the index sets
\{i,\ldots,i+p\}
and
\{k,\ldots,k+p\}
have a non-empty intersection. In turn, a non-empty intersection can only occur when
|i-k|\leq p
. For each
i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}
, there are at most
2p+1
values of
k\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}
such that
F_{i}F_{k}^{\top}\neq 0
. For any such pair,

\left(v^{\top}F_{i}F_{k}^{\top}v\right)^{2}\leq\|F_{i}^{\top}v\|_{2}^{2}\|F_{k}^{\top}v\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\|v\|_{2}^{4}}{M^{2}}.

Using that
\|v\|_{2}\leq 1
, it follows that

\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}F_{k}^{\top}vv^{\top}F_{k}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{2p+1}{M}\implies\|A\|_{F}^{2}\leq c_{\max}^{2}\frac{2p+1}{M}.

To bound
\|A\|_{2}
, note that

\|A\|_{2}\leq\|\underline{R}\|_{2}\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}F_{k}vv^{\top}F_{k}^{\top}\right\|_{2}.

Then, since
\|v\|_{2}\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}F_{k}^{\top}vv^{\top}F_{k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{\|u\|_{2}\leq 1}u^{\top}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}F_{k}^{\top}vv^{\top}F_{k}\right)u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\max_{\|u\|_{2}\leq 1}\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}(u[i:i+p+1]^{\top}v)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M}\max_{\|u\|_{2}\leq 1}\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\|u[i:i+p+1]\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p+1}{M}.

The final inequality follows because each term,
\|u[i]\|_{2}^{2}
, appears in the sum at most
p+1
times.

Plugging the bounds on
\|A\|_{2}
and
\|A\|_{F}
into (
7
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(sv^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}v-sv^{\top}\underline{R}v>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c_{\max}^{2}\frac{2p+1}{M}},\frac{\epsilon}{c_{\max}\frac{p+1}{M}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt\leq\exp\left(-M\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c_{\max}^{2}(2p+1)8},\frac{\epsilon}{c_{\max}(p+1)8}\right\}\right).

Set
\kappa(\epsilon)=\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c_{\max}^{2}(2p+1)8},\frac{\epsilon}{c_{\max}(p+1)8}\right\}
. Note that
\kappa
is invertible mapping from
[0,\infty)
to
[0,\infty)
, with

\kappa^{-1}(y)=c_{\max}\max\left\{\sqrt{(2p+1)8y},(p+1)8y\right\}.

Then, we can obtain the following union bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\underline{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\|_{2}>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(s,v)\in\{-1,1\}\times\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{P}\left(sv^{\top}(\underline{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{R}})v>(1-3\delta)\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(1+\frac{2}{\delta}\right)^{n_{W}+n_{W}p}\exp(-M\kappa\left((1-3\delta)\epsilon)\right)

Set the right side to
\nu
, for
\nu\in(0,1)
. Then, we have the following:

\displaystyle\log(\nu)\!=\!\log 2\!+\!(n_{W}+n_{W}p)\log\left(1+\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\!-\!M\kappa\left((1-3\delta)\epsilon\right).

For simplicity set
\delta=\frac{1}{6}
above, to give

\displaystyle\kappa\left(\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\right)=\frac{\log(2\nu^{-1})}{M}+\frac{n_{W}+n_{W}p}{M}\log(13).

The result follows by solving for
\epsilon
.

∎

5.2
Elementary Matrix Calculus

Lemma 7

Let
A
and
B
be positive definite
n\times n
matrices.

\left|\log\det(A)-\log\det(B)\right|\leq\\
n\max\{\|A^{-1}\|_{2},\|B^{-1}\|_{2}\}\|A-B\|_{2}.

Proof:

Let
f(t)=\log\det(A+t(B-A))
, so a standard matrix calculation, e.g. Section 0.8.10 of
Horn and Johnson 2012
, gives that

\frac{df(t)}{dt}=\mathrm{Tr}\left((A+t(B-A))^{-1}(B-A)\right).

The mean value theorem implies that there is some
t\in[0,1]
such that

\log\det(B)-\log\det(A)=\frac{df(t)}{dt}.

The von Neuman trace inequality implies that

\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left((A+t(B-A))^{-1}(B-A)\right)\right|\leq\\
n\|(A+t(B-A))^{-1}\|_{2}\|A-B\|_{2}.

The function
g(A)=\|A^{-1}\|_{2}
is convex over the set of positive definite matrices. Indeed,
(A,\alpha)
is in its epigraph if and only if

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha I&I\\
I&A\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

Convexity, and the fact that
t\in[0,1]
implies that

\displaystyle\|(A+t(B-A))^{-1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)\|A^{-1}\|_{2}+t\|B^{-1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\|A^{-1}\|_{2},\|B^{-1}\|_{2}\}.

Combining the bounds gives the result.

∎

Lemma 8

If

\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{\top}\\
B&C\end{bmatrix}\succeq cI

for some
c>0

then the Schur complement satisfies

\left\|\left(C-BA^{-1}B^{\top}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{2}\leq c^{-1}.

Proof:

The following direct calculation suffices:

\displaystyle C-BA^{-1}B^{\top}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-BA^{-1}&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{\top}\\
B&C\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-A^{-1}B^{\top}\\
I\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\succeq c\begin{bmatrix}-BA^{-1}&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-A^{-1}B^{\top}\\
I\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\succeq cI.

∎

Lemma 9

If the following bounds hold

\displaystyle c_{1}I\preceq\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{\top}\\
B&C\end{bmatrix}\preceq c_{2}I,\quad c_{1}I\preceq\begin{bmatrix}\hat{A}&\hat{B}^{\top}\\
\hat{B}&\hat{C}\end{bmatrix}\preceq c_{2}I

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{\top}\\
B&C\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\hat{A}&\hat{B}^{\top}\\
\hat{B}&\hat{C}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq\delta

for some
c_{1}>0
,
c_{2}>0
, and
\delta>0
, then

\left\|\left(C-BA^{-1}B^{\top}\right)-\left(\hat{C}-\hat{B}\hat{A}^{-1}\hat{B}^{\top}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\delta\left(1+\frac{c_{2}^{2}}{c_{1}^{2}}\right).

Applying Lemma
4

\Theta=\hat{B}\hat{A}^{-1}

\displaystyle\left(C-BA^{-1}B^{\top}\right)-\left(\hat{C}-\hat{B}\hat{A}^{-1}\hat{B}^{\top}\right)

5.3
Proof of Theorem
2

First, we give a sufficient condition for the required inverses to exist.
For compact notation, let
\epsilon
denote the upper bound on
\|\tilde{\mathbf{R}}-\underline{R}\|_{2}
from Lemma
6
.

Then, with probability at least
1-\nu
:

(c_{\min}-\epsilon)I\preceq\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\preceq(c_{\max}+\epsilon)I.

Note that
\epsilon=O\left(\frac{p\log(\nu^{-1})}{M}\right)
. So
\epsilon<c_{\min}
for sufficiently large
M/p
, and in this case
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}
is positive definite with probability at least
1-\nu
.

To bound
|I^{\infty}(\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y}\|\mathbf{z})-\tilde{\mathbf{I}}|
, it suffices to bound:

\displaystyle\left|\log\det(\Gamma_{yy})-\log\det(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{yy})\right|\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle\left|\log\det(\Sigma_{yy})-\log\det(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy})\right|.

We focus on bounding
\left|\log\det(\Sigma_{yy})-\log\det(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy})\right|
, since the other term will satisfy the same bound.

If
\mathbf{y}[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{Y}}
, then Lemma
7
implies that

\left|\log\det(\Sigma_{yy})-\log\det(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy})\right|\\
\leq n_{Y}\max\{\|\Sigma_{yy}^{-1}\|_{2},\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}^{-1}\|_{2}\}\|\Sigma_{yy}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}\|_{2}.

Lemma
8
implies
\|\Sigma_{yy}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq c_{\min}^{-1}
. Similarly, using the lower bound on
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}
and Lemma
8
implies that
\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{c_{\min}-\epsilon}
.

Let
S_{p}=R[0]-B_{p}A_{p}^{-1}B_{p}^{\top}
. The triangle inequality, followed by Lemma
1
implies that:

\displaystyle\|\Sigma_{yy}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Sigma_{yy}-S_{p}\|_{2}+\|S_{p}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{\max}\frac{b^{2}\rho^{2(p+1)}}{(1-\rho)^{2}}+\|S_{p}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}\|_{2}.

Then Lemma
9
implies that

\|S_{p}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\left(1+\frac{(c_{\max}+\epsilon)^{2}}{(c_{\min}-\epsilon)^{2}}\right).

Putting the bounds together gives:

\left|\log\det(\Sigma_{yy})-\log\det(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{yy})\right|\leq\frac{n_{Y}}{c_{\min}-\epsilon}\cdot\\
\left(c_{\max}\frac{b^{2}\rho^{2(p+1)}}{(1-\rho)^{2}}+\epsilon\left(1+\frac{(c_{\max}+\epsilon)^{2}}{(c_{\min}-\epsilon)^{2}}\right)\right).

Now,
\left|\log\det(\Gamma_{yy})-\log\det(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{yy})\right|
satisfies the same bound, and so the factor of
1/2
cancels out, and the main bound has been proved.

In the case that
p\geq\frac{\log(N)}{4\log(\rho^{-1})}
, we have that
\rho^{2p}\leq N^{-1/2}
. Furthermore, if
p\leq a_{2}\log(N)
, we have that
M=N-p=\Omega(N)
. Plugging the bounds on
M
and
p
into the expression for
\epsilon
shows that
\epsilon=O\left(\log(N)\sqrt{\frac{\log(\nu^{-1})}{N}}\right)
. Plugging the bound on
\epsilon
into the bound above gives the result.

∎

6
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presents a non-asymptotic error bound for the causally conditioned DI rate for stationary Gaussian sequences. The algorithm was enabled by a simple formula for the causally conditioned DI rate in terms of prediction error covariance matrices.
Future work will focus on non-Gaussian and possibly non-stationary sequences. Such extensions will be applicable for the analysis of more complex dynamical systems, with nonlinear and time-varying behaviors.
