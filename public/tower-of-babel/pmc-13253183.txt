Effects of clove extract and hydrolyzed whey peptides supplementation on health, performance and blood parameters of suckling dairy calves
Authors: E Mohammadi, MA Norouzian, A Assadi-Alamouti, M Bagheri Varzaneh, M Ranjbaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of clove extract and hydrolyzed whey protein supplementation, alone or in combination, on growth performance, physiological responses, hematological and biochemical parameters, antioxidant status, and health indicators of suckling dairy calves during the first 14 days of life. A total of 100 newborn dairy calves were randomly assigned to one of four treatments in a completely randomized design (n = 25 calves per treatment): (1) control (CON), milk without supplementation; (2) clove extract (CL), milk supplemented with 10 mL clove extract per day; (3) hydrolyzed whey protein (W), milk supplemented with 10 mL hydrolyzed whey protein per day; and (4) mixture (MIX), milk supplemented with 10 mL clove extract plus 10 mL hydrolyzed whey protein per day. Daily dry matter intake, body weight at 15 days of age, average daily gain, and feed conversion ratio were not affected by treatment. Rectal temperature did not differ among treatments. Hematological parameters showed no treatment effects on white blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, platelet count, or differential leukocyte counts. However, red blood cell count differed among treatments, with calves in the control group exhibiting a higher RBC concentration compared with those receiving clove extract, hydrolyzed whey protein, or their combination (P = 0.048). Blood biochemical parameters, including malondialdehyde, total antioxidant capacity, cholesterol, blood urea nitrogen, liver enzymes, minerals, albumin, total protein, and ferritin concentrations, were not influenced by treatment. The incidence and duration of diarrhea and fecal scores were similar among treatments; however, the day of diarrhea onset differed significantly among treatments (P < 0.01), with the latest onset observed in calves receiving clove extract–containing treatments. In conclusion, supplementation of milk with clove extract and/or hydrolyzed whey protein during the early neonatal period had minimal effects on growth performance, physiological status, oxidative balance, and most health-related parameters of dairy calves, with the exception of alterations in red blood cell count and the timing of diarrhea onset.Keywords: Dairy calves, Clove extract, Hydrolyzed whey protein, Diarrhea, Hematological parameters, Early-life nutrition

Introduction

The neonatal period is a critical phase in the life of dairy calves, during which profound physiological, immunological, and microbial transitions occur. The first two weeks after birth are particularly decisive, as calves shift from a sterile intrauterine environment to one characterized by rapid gastrointestinal colonization, dietary change, and immune system activation. During this window, the gastrointestinal tract, especially the small intestine, remains structurally and functionally immature, rendering neonatal calves highly susceptible to enteric disorders, metabolic instability, and systemic inflammation (Steele et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2016).

Neonatal diarrhea remains one of the most prevalent health challenges in preweaned dairy calves worldwide and is a major contributor to morbidity, mortality, and impaired growth performance (Cho & Yoon, 2014; NAHMS, 2018). Recent studies have further confirmed that neonatal calf diarrhea remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in dairy production systems and continues to impose substantial economic losses despite advances in management and preventive strategies (Coelho et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2023). Epidemiological studies consistently indicate that the highest incidence of diarrhea occurs within the first 7 to 14 days of life, coinciding with incomplete intestinal barrier maturation and limited immune competence (Windeyer et al., 2014; Fischer et al., 2021). Beyond its immediate clinical consequences, early-life enteric disease has been associated with reduced feed efficiency, delayed rumen development, increased antimicrobial use, and lower milk yield later in life (Heinrichs & Heinrichs, 2011; Soberon et al., 2012).

Historically, in-feed or in-milk antimicrobials were widely used to control neonatal enteric disease. However, increasing concerns over antimicrobial resistance and the implementation of stricter regulations, including the Veterinary Feed Directive, have intensified interest in non-antibiotic nutritional interventions (FDA, 2015; Manyi-Loh et al., 2018). Recent evidence highlights that reliance on antimicrobial therapies in calves may exacerbate gut dysbiosis and contribute to antimicrobial resistance, reinforcing the need for sustainable nutritional alternatives (Wilson et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2023). Among these alternatives, phytogenic feed additives and functional dairy-derived peptides have gained attention for their capacity to modulate gut health through antimicrobial, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory pathways (Steiner & Shah, 2015).

Clove (Syzygium aromaticum) is a medicinal plant rich in phenolic compounds, particularly eugenol, which exhibits potent antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties. In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that clove extract inhibits the growth of common enteric pathogens, scavenges reactive oxygen species, and suppresses inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK (Pereira et al., 2007; Sánchez-Quesada et al., 2013; Hu et al., 2018). Recent animal studies suggest that phytogenic additives containing clove or eugenol can stabilize intestinal microbiota, improve antioxidant status, and reduce markers of systemic inflammation, particularly under conditions of enteric challenge (Yang et al., 2015; Singh et al., 2020). Despite these promising findings, limited data are available regarding the application of clove extract in neonatal dairy calves under commercial field conditions.

In parallel, hydrolyzed whey peptides represent a class of bioactive dairy ingredients with increasing relevance in neonatal nutrition. Whey protein hydrolysates contain low-molecular-weight peptides that are rapidly digestible and possess biological activities beyond their nutritional value. These peptides have been shown to enhance intestinal epithelial cell proliferation, stimulate tight junction protein expression, and modulate immune responses through regulation of cytokine production and gut-associated lymphoid tissue activity (Korhonen & Pihlanto, 2006; Wang et al., 2024). Recent experimental evidence also indicates that nutritional interventions targeting gut microbiota and intestinal barrier function can significantly influence disease progression and recovery in diarrheic calves, further supporting the use of functional bioactive compounds in early-life nutrition (Zakia et al., 2025). In young animals, dietary whey peptides have been associated with improved nitrogen utilization, enhanced gut morphology, and improved resistance to enteric stress (Zhao et al., 2022).

The combination of phytogenic extracts and bioactive whey peptides may provide complementary and potentially synergistic benefits to the neonatal calf. While clove extract may primarily exert antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects, hydrolyzed whey peptides may support mucosal repair, immune maturation, and nutrient absorption. Such combined strategies are particularly relevant during the first 14 days of life, when calves experience peak vulnerability to intestinal dysfunction but also exhibit high responsiveness to nutritional modulation. Therefore, the objective of the present field study was to investigate the effects of dietary supplementation with clove extract and hydrolyzed whey peptides during the first 14 days of life on health performance and blood parameters of suckling dairy calves. It was hypothesized that early-life supplementation with these bioactive compounds would improve intestinal health, attenuate inflammatory responses, and promote favorable metabolic and immune profiles during the neonatal period.

Materials and methods

Experimental site, study design, and ethical considerations

This field study was conducted at Safary Dairy Farm (Chaltasian Agricultural and Livestock Company), located in Tehran Province, Iran, during the summer of 2025. Prior to initiation of the experiment, full coordination was carried out with farm management and all relevant units, including the maternity pen, colostrum management unit, calf barn, and on-farm laboratory, to ensure strict adherence to uniform experimental protocols. All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with accepted principles of animal welfare and standard management practices for neonatal dairy calves.

Housing preparation and hygiene management

To provide uniform sanitary and management conditions and minimize the risk of neonatal infections, all calves were housed individually in disinfected pens immediately after birth. Pens were thoroughly cleaned and disinfected before calf placement, and fresh bedding was provided. Calves were transferred from the maternity pen to the colostrum unit with minimal handling stress. High-quality colostrum (Brix value > 26%) was administered as soon as possible after birth, and routine preventive health monitoring was consistently applied throughout the experimental period according to established neonatal calf management guidelines (Field, 2005).

After colostrum feeding, calves were moved to individual pens in the calf barn and maintained under identical environmental and management conditions, including temperature, humidity, ventilation, hygiene, and access to feed and water throughout the study.

Experimental period, animals, and feeding management

The overall experimental period extended from July 21 to August 31, 2025 (41 days), as calves were enrolled sequentially according to calving dates. However, the experimental treatments and all associated measurements for each calf were conducted exclusively during the first 14 days of life. A total of 100 purebred Holstein female calves with birth weights ranging from 35 to 42 kg were enrolled. Calves born as twins, calves from crossbred dams, and calves experiencing dystocia were excluded from the study.

Colostrum feeding was standardized across all calves, with each calf receiving a total volume equivalent to 12% of body weight within the first 12 h of life, ensuring consistent passive transfer of immunity among treatment groups. On day 2, each calf received one feeding of pasteurized milk (a mixture of 50% whole milk and 50% milk replacer) at 2.7 L with 14% dry matter. Calves were subsequently transferred to the calf barn when conditions permitted. In the calf barn, calves were fed pasteurized milk twice daily (3 L per feeding; 6 L/d total). To eliminate confounding effects, routine farm supplements including probiotics and AD₃E vitamins were omitted throughout the experimental period. From the first day in the calf barn, drinking water and calf starter were offered ad libitum. For precise intake measurement, 200 g of starter was offered daily to each calf, and refusals were collected and weighed the following morning.

To minimize environmental variation, calves were housed in individual pens with straw bedding under standard farm conditions, with natural ventilation. Dehorning was postponed until day 15 to avoid additional stress during the experimental period.

Preparation and characterization of experimental supplements

Clove extract preparation and antimicrobial evaluation

Clove extract dosage was determined based on preliminary trials and previous literature, indicating that 10–20 mg of clove extract dry matter per kg of body weight is effective for antimicrobial activity and diarrhea prevention in calves (Dorman and Deans, 2000; Nazzaro et al., 2013). Clove buds were obtained from Rayeh Salamat Shamin Co. and extracted using 70% ethanol. Briefly, 500 g of ground clove buds were mixed with 2 L of 70% ethanol and incubated for 72 h at 25 °C on a shaker. The extract was filtered and concentrated using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph Hei-VAP G1) at 50–55 °C.

To compare antimicrobial activity of different extract forms, both liquid and lyophilized clove extracts were evaluated for minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus using a 96-well microplate broth dilution method. Dry matter content of the liquid extract was determined using a hot-air oven method (85 °C for 1.5 h) and estimated at 13% (130 mg/mL). Based on antimicrobial efficacy, uniformity, and feasibility, the liquid extract was selected, and a daily dose of 10 mL per calf was administered during the experimental period.

Hydrolyzed whey protein preparation and degree of hydrolysis

Whey protein concentrate (80% CP) was sourced from Pakban Co. Enzymatic hydrolysis was performed using trypsin to generate bioactive peptides (Salami et al., 2010). Whey protein was hydrated in 20 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8.7) at a concentration of 300 mg per 10 mL and stored overnight at 4 °C. Hydrolysis was conducted by adding 1, 2, or 3 mg of trypsin per 10 mL of solution and incubating at 37 °C for 5 h in a shaking incubator. Enzyme activity was terminated by heating samples in a water bath at 85–90 °C for 15–20 min, followed by rapid cooling.

Samples were centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. Degree of hydrolysis (DH) was determined using the o-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) spectrophotometric method at 340 nm (Nielsen et al., 2001). The highest DH was observed with 2 mg trypsin; therefore, this enzyme concentration was selected for preparation of the hydrolyzed whey protein used in the field trial. The final daily supplementation dose was 10 mL per calf, equivalent to approximately 240–300 mg hydrolyzed whey protein.

The supplements were prepared under controlled conditions and stored in a cool, dry environment until use.

Experimental treatments and supplementation protocol

Calves were randomly allocated to treatments using a completely randomized design, based on birth weight, using random number table (n = 25 calves per treatment): (1) Control (CON), milk without supplementation; (2) Clove extract (CL), milk supplemented with 10 mL clove extract per day; (3) Hydrolyzed whey protein (W), milk supplemented with 10 mL hydrolyzed whey protein per day; and 4) Mixture (MIX), milk supplemented with 10 mL clove extract plus 10 mL hydrolyzed whey protein per day. Supplements were administered with colostrum on day 1 and with milk from day 2 through day 14.

Health parameters and clinical monitoring

Health indicators included rectal temperature, fecal score, and incidence, duration, and severity of diarrhea. Rectal temperature was measured on days 1, 7, and 14 using a calibrated digital thermometer after the morning feeding. Fecal consistency was evaluated daily using a standardized 4-point scoring system (0 = normal, firm feces; 1 = soft feces; 2 = loose feces; 3 = watery diarrhea) according to the method described by McGuirk (2008). Calves with diarrhea were identified based on fecal score, reduced appetite, and general depression, and were treated according to farm protocols, including oral electrolyte therapy, fluid therapy, and, when necessary, antibiotic treatment under veterinary supervision. Dates of onset and recovery were recorded to calculate disease duration.

Growth performance and feed efficiency

Starter intake was calculated by subtracting daily refusals from the amount offered. Body weight was recorded at birth and on day 15 using a calibrated digital scale. Total weight gain and average daily gain (ADG) were calculated for each calf. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as total dry matter intake (from colostrum, milk, and starter) divided by total body weight gain over the 14-day period.

Blood sampling and hematological analyses

Blood samples were collected randomly from 10 calves per treatment. Hematological parameters were measured on days 1, 7, and 15. Blood was collected from the jugular vein into EDTA-containing tubes for complete blood count (CBC) analysis and into plain tubes for serum separation. CBC parameters, including WBC, RBC, hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV, platelet count, and leukocyte differential counts (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils), were analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer (Exigo Cell Counter), complemented by manual differential counts using Giemsa-stained blood smears.

Serum biochemical and oxidative stress analyses

Serum samples were stored at −20 °C until analysis. Biochemical parameters measured included total protein, albumin, cholesterol, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), calcium, phosphorus, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), ferritin, malondialdehyde (MDA), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC). Commercial diagnostic kits (Pars Azmoon and Randox) were used following manufacturer protocols. MDA concentration was determined using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.4.2). The assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variances, and independence of residuals were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk, Levene’s, and Durbin–Watson tests, respectively. In addition, residuals were visually inspected using boxplots and Q–Q plots.

Repeated-measure variables (rectal temperature and hematological parameters) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, with calf included as a random effect and treatment, time, and their interaction as fixed effects. An appropriate covariance structure was selected based on model fit criteria.

Growth performance and blood biochemical parameters were analyzed using ANOVA or ANCOVA models, with birth weight included as a covariate where appropriate.

When significant main effects were detected, comparisons among treatment means were performed using Duncan’s multiple range test. Results are presented as least squares means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was declared at P ≤ 0.05.

Discussion

The present field study evaluated the effects of early-life supplementation with clove extract, hydrolyzed whey peptides, and their combination on growth performance, health status, hematological indices, and blood biochemical parameters of suckling dairy calves during the first 14 days of life. The neonatal period is characterized by rapid metabolic adaptation, immature intestinal and immune function, and high susceptibility to enteric disturbances; therefore, even subtle nutritional interventions may exert measurable physiological effects during this critical period.

Growth performance and feed intake

In the present study, no significant differences were observed among treatments for starter intake, body weight at day 15, average daily gain, or feed conversion ratio. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that solid feed intake and growth responses are limited during the first two weeks of life due to the preruminant physiological state of neonatal calves (Steele et al., 2016; Heinrichs & Heinrichs, 2011). During this period, nutrient utilization is dominated by abomasal digestion and intestinal absorption of milk-derived nutrients, while ruminal fermentation and microbial protein synthesis remain negligible. Nevertheless, calves receiving the combined treatment tended to exhibit a 23% higher average daily gain compared with the control group, accompanied by an approximately 15% improvement in feed conversion ratio (Table 2). These responses suggest that bioactive milk-derived peptides, particularly when combined with clove extract, may be associated with enhanced metabolic efficiency and improved nutrient utilization at the intestinal level. Whey-derived peptides are rapidly absorbed and provide readily available amino acids that can support protein synthesis, intestinal epithelial turnover, and enzymatic activity (Korhonen & Pihlanto, 2006). Moreover, certain bioactive peptides have been shown to stimulate insulin and insulin-like growth factor-1 signaling, thereby enhancing nutrient partitioning toward lean tissue accretion (Zhao et al., 2022).

Diarrhea incidence and intestinal health

Although the overall incidence of diarrhea was high and not affected by treatment, supplementation with clove extract was associated with a significant delay in the onset of diarrhea compared with all other experimental groups. This finding suggests a potential early-life modulatory effect of clove-derived bioactive compounds on intestinal adaptation during the neonatal period. Such a delay in the appearance of clinical signs may be indicative of improved initial stabilization of the gastrointestinal environment and a more gradual progression toward enteric imbalance rather than an immediate protective effect against diarrhea occurrence. Clove extract is rich in eugenol and other phenolic compounds that have demonstrated antimicrobial activity against a range of enteric bacteria relevant to neonatal calf health, including E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus (Pereira et al., 2007; Hu et al., 2018). At a mechanistic level, eugenol has been reported to disrupt bacterial cell membranes, interfere with quorum sensing pathways, and reduce microbial adhesion to intestinal epithelial surfaces, which may collectively limit early bacterial proliferation (Nazzaro et al., 2013). In addition, clove-derived phenolic compounds have been shown to modulate inflammatory signaling pathways, including suppression of NF-κB activation and downstream pro-inflammatory cytokine production, potentially contributing to improved epithelial integrity and resilience of the intestinal barrier (Sánchez-Quesada et al., 2013). Nevertheless, in the present study, these mechanisms remain speculative, and the observed effect is more appropriately interpreted as a functional outcome requiring confirmation through targeted microbiological and immunological assessments in future research.

Although treatment effects on diarrhea duration were not statistically significant, the approximately 20% numerical reduction (≈1 day; Fig. 2) observed in calves receiving the combined supplementation is biologically and functionally relevant during the neonatal period. A shorter diarrheic episode at this stage may alleviate fluid loss, support intestinal recovery, and mitigate early growth impairment. The reduced duration of diarrhea in the combined treatment likely reflects complementary actions of clove extract and hydrolyzed whey peptides. Bioactive whey peptides have been shown to enhance tight junction integrity and promote enterocyte proliferation, thereby accelerating epithelial repair and restoring barrier function following enteric challenge (Wang et al., 2024).

Hematological responses and immune adaptation

The lack of treatment effects on total white blood cell counts and leukocyte differentials suggests that none of the supplements induced systemic immune activation or immunosuppression. The significant effects of time on leukocyte populations reflect normal postnatal immune maturation, characterized by gradual stabilization of neutrophil and lymphocyte proportions during the first weeks of life (Roland et al., 2014).

The observed treatment effect on red blood cell (RBC) count, in the absence of concomitant changes in hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, or mean corpuscular volume, suggests that the finding is unlikely to represent a primary effect on erythropoiesis. Given the rapid physiological and hematological adaptations that occur during the neonatal period, inherent biological variability may also have contributed to the observed differences. Alternatively, subtle and indirect effects of dietary supplementation on fluid homeostasis or gastrointestinal function may plausibly account for the findings; however, these mechanisms remain speculative and warrant confirmation through targeted physiological and metabolic investigations in future studies.

Blood biochemical parameters and metabolic stability

The absence of treatment effects on serum biochemical parameters, including liver enzymes (AST and ALT), blood urea nitrogen, cholesterol, and mineral concentrations, indicates that supplementation with clove extract and hydrolyzed whey peptides was metabolically safe during the neonatal period. Maintenance of normal AST and ALT activities suggests that hepatic metabolic pathways were not adversely affected by the experimental supplements.

Markers of oxidative status, including malondialdehyde and total antioxidant capacity, were also unaffected by treatment. Although clove extract possesses strong antioxidant properties in vitro, its effects on systemic oxidative markers may be limited under non-challenging physiological conditions. Previous studies have shown that antioxidant responses to phytogenic additives are more pronounced under conditions of oxidative stress or pathogen challenge (Yang et al., 2015). The lack of difference observed in the present study may therefore reflect the relatively short experimental duration or effective baseline antioxidant defenses in neonatal calves.

Serum total protein and albumin concentrations were not influenced by treatment, whereas birth values significantly affected total protein concentrations at day 15. This finding underscores the dominant role of colostral immunoglobulin absorption in determining circulating protein levels during early life and highlights the importance of colostrum management in neonatal calf studies (Soberon et al., 2012).

Conclusion

Overall, early-life supplementation with clove extract and hydrolyzed whey peptides exerted limited effects on growth performance and systemic metabolic parameters during the first 14 days of life. However, the observed delay in the onset of diarrhea suggests a potential modulatory role of these bioactive compounds in supporting early gastrointestinal adaptation. While these effects were not reflected in measurable changes in growth or blood biochemical indices, they may indicate subtle functional improvements in gut physiology with possible implications for longer-term calf health and performance.

The interpretation of these findings should be considered with due consideration of several limitations. The relatively short experimental period (14 days), characteristic of the neonatal phase, may have limited the ability to capture treatment-related differences in growth performance and metabolic responses, particularly given the low intake of starter feed during this stage. In addition, the study was conducted under typical field conditions, which enhances its practical relevance but may also introduce biological variability that can influence the detectability of treatment effects. Accordingly, further studies with extended feeding periods and more comprehensive physiological assessments would help to better characterize the role of clove extract and hydrolyzed whey peptides in supporting calf health, particularly during early-life development.
