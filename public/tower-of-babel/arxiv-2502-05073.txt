Noise Sensitivity and Learning Lower Bounds for Hierarchical Functions
Rupert Li, Elchanan Mossel

Abstract
. Recent works explore deep learning’s success by examining functions or data with hierarchical structure. To study the learning complexity of functions with hierarchical structure, we study the noise stability of functions with tree hierarchical structure on independent inputs. We show that if each function in the hierarchy is ε \varepsilon -far from linear, the noise stability is exponentially small in the depth of the hierarchy. Our results have immediate applications for learning. In the Boolean setting using the results of Dachman-Soled, Feldman, Tan, Wan and Wimmer (2014) our results provide Statistical Query super-polynomial lower bounds for learning classes that are based on hierarchical functions. Similarly, using the results of Diakonikolas, Kane, Pittas and Zarifis (2021) our results provide super-polynomial lower bounds for SQ learning under the Gaussian measure. Using the results of Abbe, Bengio, Cornacchiam, Kleinberg, Lotfi, Raghu and Zhang (2022) our results imply sample complexity lower bounds for learning hierarchical functions with gradient descent on fully connected neural networks.



1.
Introduction

Our goal is to bridge two lines of research in learning theory. The first line proposed that the success of neural networks can be explained in terms
of hierarchical structures in the data.
The second line of work provides learning lower bounds for noise sensitive functions, or classes of such functions.

Multiple works have argued that the success of deep learning is intimately related to hierarchical properties in data generative processes.
This hypothesis has been independently proposed by multiple researchers, including:

•

Bruna and Mallat’s wavelet scattering networks
[
5
]
,

•

Mhaskar, Liao, and Poggio’s compositional function models
[
15
]
,

•

Patel, Nguyen, and Baraniuk’s hierarchical rendering models
[
20
]
.

Mossel
[
18
]
provided a rigorous attempt to establish computational hardness, though subsequent work
[
14
,
13
]
only demonstrated low-degree hardness.
In terms of compositions of functions,
Telgarsky
[
22
]
presents a notable result demonstrating an iteratively composed simple function that can be precisely expressed as a deep narrow network, yet requires super-polynomial computational complexity for small network depth.

The second research line establishes learning lower bounds in terms of the Fourier expansion of functions or classes of functions.
This includes in particular SQ lower bounds in terms of the noise-sensitivity in both the Boolean case
[
7
]
and the Gaussian case
[
8
]
as well as sample complexity lower bounds for learning such functions using fully connected neural networks and
stochastic gradient descent
[
1
]
.

In our main result we show that hierarchical functions, i.e., functions obtained by decompositions of non-linear functions on disjoint independent inputs, are noise sensitive.
This implies SQ lower bounds for learning such functions in both the Boolean and the Gaussian case as well as sample lower bounds for learning such functions using neural networks.

Our work builds upon a long line of work in discrete Fourier analysis.
The fact that recursively composing the majority function on
3
bits leads to a noise sensitive function is folklore in the area, see e.g.,
[
3
,
4
]
.
Mossel and O’Donnell
[
16
]
further study decompositions of monotone functions and construct such decompositions that are essentially “most” noise sensitive among monotone functions.
We also note that recursive constructions, i.e., Sipser functions, provide the best results in circuit lower bounds for circuits with AND, OR and NOT gates
[
11
]
.

The main contribution of the current paper is by showing that noise sensitivity of composition of non-linear functions on non-overlapping inputs is generic—the functions to be composed do not have to be identical, to be chosen carefully, or to be balanced or have any other property other than being somewhat uncorrelated with linear functions of their inputs.
Our results hold for all product measures, both discrete and continuous.

1.1.
Our Results

1.1.1.
Multilinear functions

To begin with we consider the case where all functions involved are multilinear.
This includes in particular the case where all functions have binary inputs and outputs which is the classical case where analysis of Boolean functions and noise sensitivity are discussed.
In this case we obtain the following result concerning a single multilinear function.
Precise definitions are provided in
Section

2
, though we comment that
d(f,\operatorname{Lin})
quantifies the non-linearity of
f
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Suppose
\left\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):i\in[n]\right\}
are mutually independent, where for some
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, we have
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})\leq\rho
for all
i
.
Suppose
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
have the same first and second moments, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]<\infty
, for all
i\in[n]
.
If
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a multilinear function satisfying
d(f,\operatorname{Lin})\geq\varepsilon>0
, then

(1.1)

\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))\leq(1-\varepsilon)\rho+\varepsilon\rho^{2}.

Remark 1.2
.

Such a
Y
can always be generated by setting
Y_{i}=X_{i}
with probability
\rho
and otherwise letting
Y_{i}
be independent and identically distributed as
X_{i}
, independently for all
i
; note that this is not in general the unique construction.
Moreover, this construction yields
X
and
Y
that have the same marginal distribution, which does not need to be true in general for
Lemma

1.1
to hold.

Applying
Lemma

1.1
inductively yields the following result demonstrating exponential decay in noise stability for a hierarchical multilinear function, precisely defined in
Section

2.3
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose
\left\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):i\in[n]\right\}
are mutually independent, where for some
0\leq\rho<1
, we have
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})\leq\rho
for all
i
.
Suppose
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
have the same first and second moments, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]<\infty
, for all
i\in[n]
.
If
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a hierarchical multilinear function of non-linearity
\varepsilon>0
and depth
d
, then for any
\delta<\varepsilon
,

\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))\leq\left(1-\delta\right)^{d-C_{\varepsilon,\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)}

for some constant
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}>0
depending only on
\varepsilon
and
\delta
.

As discussed in
Remark

2.2
, this bound is essentially tight, as it is impossible to have exponential rate smaller than
1-\varepsilon
.

Remark

2.3
discusses how this can be improved to doubly exponential decay in depth in the case where each component function has
0
correlation with linear functions, such as
t
-resilient functions from cryptography, which are further discussed in
Remark

2.4
, and the parity bit function as further discussed in
Remark

2.5
.

1.1.2.
General functions

The results above leave something to be desired, as neural network activation functions, notably ReLU, are typically not multilinear in their inputs.
However, some care is needed in this general case as the following examples show.

Example 1.4
.

Consider a hierarchical function with a binary tree structure, where at even levels we have component functions
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\cos(\pi x_{1})
and at odd levels
g(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{\arccos(x_{1})}{\pi}
, where each of the component variables takes values in
[0,1]
.
For example, such a function of depth 2 would be given by
g(f(x_{1},x_{2}),f(x_{3},x_{4}))
.
We note that
f
and
g
are clearly non-linear, but any such function of even depth will output
x_{1}
, which is as noise stable as possible, and there is no decay in correlation as depth increases.

Example 1.5
.

The phenomenon above does not require the functions to have continuous inputs.
In fact, it suffices for the inputs and outputs of the functions to take on a small number of values, e.g., four.
For example, we can consider the input distributions to be uniform over
\left\{-1,1\right\}^{3^{d}}
, i.e.,
3^{d}
i.i.d. variables uniformly distributed on
\{-1,1\}
.
In our correlated setup for noise sensitivity, we consider two such random vectors
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
and
Y=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
coupled such that
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are mutually independent, and
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})=\rho
for all
i
.

In our construction, our functions can output at most four values,
\pm 10\pm 1
, and our overall hierarchical function has a ternary tree structure.
For a number
x=10b_{1}+b_{2}
for
b_{1},b_{2}\in\{-1,1\}
, let us write
B_{1}(x)=b_{1}
and
B_{2}(x)=b_{2}
.
We can then take the functions at the lowest layer (depth
d
) to be

f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{1}+10\operatorname{maj}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),

where
\operatorname{maj}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=2\cdot 1_{\left\{x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}>0\right\}}-1
is the majority function, i.e., takes value 1 if a majority of inputs take value 1, and takes value
-1
if a majority of inputs takes value
-1
.
We let the remaining functions be

g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=B_{2}(x_{1})+10\operatorname{maj}(B_{1}(x_{1}),B_{1}(x_{2}),B_{1}(x_{3})),

It is easy to see that all functions involved are far from linear,
but the hierarchical function on
3^{d}
inputs computes the first input plus
10
times the recursive majority of all
3^{d}
inputs, and is therefore very noise stable as it has constant correlation with
x_{1}
.

To account for potential non-linear relationships, we strengthen our notions of correlation in both our assumptions and our conclusions.
The precise definitions are provided in
Section

3
, but loosely speaking,
\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(X),L^{2}(Y))
denotes the maximum correlation between any two functions where one is a function of
X
and the other is a function of
Y
.
The analog of
Lemma

1.1
in this more general setting is the following lemma.

Lemma 1.6
.

Suppose
\left\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):i\in[n]\right\}
are mutually independent, where for some
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, we have
\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(X_{i}),L^{2}(Y_{i}))\leq\rho
for all
i
.
If
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[f(X)^{2}],\mathbb{E}[f(Y)^{2}]<\infty
and

(1.2)

\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),L^{2}(X_{1})+\cdots+L^{2}(X_{n})),\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(Y)),L^{2}(Y_{1})+\cdots+L^{2}(Y_{n}))\leq\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}

for some
\varepsilon>0
, then

\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),L^{2}(f(Y)))\leq(1-\varepsilon)\rho+\varepsilon\rho^{2}.

In
Section

3.3
, we justify why (
1.2
) is the natural analog of the condition
d(f,\operatorname{Lin})\geq\varepsilon
in
Lemma

1.1
.

Remark 1.7
.

It is straightforward to check that the construction in
Remark

1.2
still satisfies the stronger condition
\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(X_{i}),L^{2}(Y_{i}))=\rho
: letting
Z_{i}
be an independent copy of
X_{i}
such that
Y_{i}=X_{i}
with probability
\rho
and
Y_{i}=Z_{i}
otherwise, we have, for
f\in L^{2}(X_{i})
and
g\in L^{2}(Y_{i})
normalized so that
\mathbb{E}[f]=\mathbb{E}[g]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[f^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[g^{2}]=1
, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\operatorname{Corr}(f(X_{i}),g(Y_{i}))=\rho\mathbb{E}[f(X_{i})g(X_{i})]+(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}[f(X_{i})g(Z_{i})]\leq\rho\cdot 1+(1-\rho)\cdot 0=\rho,

where equality is achieved when
f=g
as functions
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Using
Lemma

1.6
, we obtain the following general version of
Theorem

1.3
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Suppose
\left\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):i\in[n]\right\}
are mutually independent, where for some
0\leq\rho<1
, we have
\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(X_{i}),L^{2}(Y_{i}))\leq\rho
for all
i
.
If
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a hierarchical function of depth
d
and non-separability
\varepsilon>0
in both
L^{2}(X)
and
L^{2}(Y)
, then for any
\delta<\varepsilon
,

\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),L^{2}(f(Y)))\leq(1-\delta)^{d-C_{\varepsilon,\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)}

for some constant
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}>0
depending only on
\varepsilon
and
\delta
.

1.2.
Applications

Theorem

1.3
immediately implies a low-degree bound on a hierarchical multilinear function—in particular we get that the correlation
M
of such a function
f
with a degree-
D
polynomial is bounded by

(1.3)

M\leq\rho^{-D/2}(1-\delta)^{d/2-C_{\varepsilon,\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)},

for any
\rho\in(0,1)
; see
Lemma

2.6
.
Taking
\rho=1-e^{-cd}
for some small
c\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2C_{\varepsilon,\delta}}\right)
, we get a degree bound that is exponential in the circuit depth, i.e., in order to get
M=\Omega(1)
correlation, one requires
D=e^{\Omega(d)}
.

In the Boolean setting, which is contained in our framework of multilinear functions, using the results of Dachman-Soled, Feldman, Tan, Wan and Wimmer
[
7
]
, the bound (
1.3
) implies super-polynomial lower bounds for Statistical Query (SQ) agnostic learning of concept classes based on hierarchical multilinear functions.
We refer readers to
[
7
]
for background on agnostic learning in the SQ model.

Theorem 1.9
.

For any constant
\varepsilon>0
, for any hierarchical multilinear function
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n^{1/3}}\to[-1,1]
of depth
d=\Theta(\log n)
and non-linearity
\varepsilon
, let

be the concept class of functions

Analogously,

1.8

).
The correlation

of such a function

with a function of Efron–Stein degree at most

see

3.4
.
As before, this implies a degree bound exponential in depth.

When our inputs are Gaussian, using the results of Diakonikolas, Kane, Pittas and Zarifis
[
8
]

1.8
implies super-exponential lower bounds for agnostic learning in the SQ model for concept classes based on hierarchical functions.

Theorem 1.10
.

Fix an arbitrary constant
\varepsilon>0
.
Let positive integers

and

m\leq n^{\alpha}

Our final application is for learning such functions using stochastic gradient descent on fully connected deep neural networks.
The results of

Let

2.
Noise stability of compositions of multilinear functions

2.1.
Preliminary setup

For any probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
, function
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
, and subset
V\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
, define the (squared) distance between
f
and
V
by

d(f,V)=\inf_{g\in V}\frac{\mathbb{E}[(f-g)^{2}]}{\operatorname{Var}(f)}.

If
f
is almost surely constant, i.e.,
\operatorname{Var}(f)=0
, we define
d(f,V)=0
.
Similarly, throughout this paper we use the convention
\operatorname{Corr}(X,Y)=0
if either
X
or
Y
are almost surely constant.
We now specialize to the case where
\mathbb{P}
is a product measure on product space
\Omega
over laws of real-valued random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
.
In the product measure, these random variables are mutually independent, and we additionally assume the random variables have finite second moment, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]<\infty
for all
i\in[n]
.
Beyond this, we make no further assumptions on the distributions of the random variables, e.g., some may be discrete, others continuous, or neither.
We will consider the setting where
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
is multilinear, i.e.,
f
is of the form

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}a_{S}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}

for some coefficients
a_{S}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note that any function of the above form is in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
.
In the Boolean case, i.e., when
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are each random variables supported on at most two points, note that all
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
are multilinear in
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
.

Let
\operatorname{Lin}
denote the subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
spanned by linear functions, i.e.,

\operatorname{Lin}=\operatorname{span}(\{1,x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}).

For each
i\in[n]
, define the normalized random variable
\widetilde{X}_{i}=\frac{X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_{i})}}
, which has mean 0 and variance 1, and similarly denote
\widetilde{x}_{i}=\frac{x_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_{i})}}
for all
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then for any multilinear
f
, we let
\widehat{f}(S)\in\mathbb{R}
for
S\subseteq[n]
denote the unique coefficients satisfying

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\prod_{i\in S}\widetilde{x}_{i}.

For each
S\subseteq[n]
, we let
\chi_{S}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the function given by

\chi_{S}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\prod_{i\in S}\widetilde{x}_{i}.

By independence of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, we have
\left\{\chi_{S}:S\subseteq[n]\right\}
is a set of orthonormal multilinear polynomials: for distinct
S,S^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}\chi_{S^{\prime}}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}^{2}]=1
.
Also note that
\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}]=1_{\left\{S=\emptyset\right\}}
.
Hence, we have

\mathbb{E}[f^{2}]=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Var}(f)=\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}.

From this, it is easy to see that

d(f,\operatorname{Lin})=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert\geq 2}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}}.

For any
S\subseteq[n]
, we let
X_{S}
denote the random vector consisting of the
X_{i}
for
i\in S
, and similarly define
Y_{S}
, as well as
x_{S}
and
y_{S}
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For example,
\chi_{S}(x)
depends only on
x_{S}
.

2.2.
Main lemma

Lemma

1.1
shows that if we have some noisy observation
Y_{i}
of each
X_{i}
, namely
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})\leq\rho
for all
i\in[n]
for some constant
\rho<1
, and
f
is far from linear, then
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))<\rho
, with this correlation displaying exponential decay with respect to application of
f
.
We now prove this lemma.

Proof of
Lemma

1.1
.

Note that because
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are mutually independent, and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are also mutually independent, we have
\operatorname{Var}(f(X))=\operatorname{Var}(f(Y))
as

\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}(X)\chi_{S^{\prime}}(X)]=\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}(Y)\chi_{S^{\prime}}(Y)]=1_{\left\{S=S^{\prime}\right\}}.

We similarly also have
\mathbb{E}[f(X)]=\mathbb{E}[f(Y)]=\widehat{f}(0)
as
\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}(X)]=\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}(Y)]=1_{\left\{S=\emptyset\right\}}
.
Thus, without loss of generality, we can rescale and shift
f
so that
\operatorname{Var}(f)=1
and
\mathbb{E}[f]=0
.
Then we expand

\displaystyle\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f(X)f(Y)]=\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\sum_{S^{\prime}\neq\emptyset}\widehat{f}(S)\widehat{f}(S^{\prime})\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}(X)\chi_{S^{\prime}}(Y)].

Note that if
S\neq S^{\prime}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}(X)\chi_{S^{\prime}}(Y)]=0
.
Then either
S\not\subseteq S^{\prime}
or
S^{\prime}\not\subseteq S
.
Say
S\not\subseteq S^{\prime}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}(X)\chi_{S^{\prime}}(Y)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}(X)\chi_{S^{\prime}}(Y)|(X_{S^{\prime}},Y_{S^{\prime}})]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}(X)|(X_{S^{\prime}},Y_{S^{\prime}})]\chi_{S^{\prime}}(Y)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[0\cdot\chi_{S^{\prime}}(Y)]

\displaystyle=0.

The case
S^{\prime}\not\subseteq S
follows symmetrically.
If
S=S^{\prime}
, then by independence of the
(X_{i},Y_{i})
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\chi_{S}(X)\chi_{S}(Y)]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in S}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{i}\widetilde{Y}_{i}]=\prod_{i\in S}\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})\leq\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}.

Thus,

\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))\leq\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}.

Recalling that

\operatorname{Var}(f)=\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}=1

while

d(f,\operatorname{Lin})=\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert\geq 2}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\geq\varepsilon,

we obtain the bound

\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))\leq(1-\varepsilon)\rho+\varepsilon\rho^{2},

as desired.
∎

Note that this bound is sharp when the conditions in the statement of the lemma are tight, i.e.,
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})=\rho
for all
i
, and
d(f,\operatorname{Lin})=\varepsilon
, and
f
is of degree 2.

Remark 2.1
.

In the Boolean setting, i.e., when the
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
are all Boolean random variables, when
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})=\rho
for all
i
, then the quantity
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))
that we study in
Lemma

1.1
coincides with the well-studied noise stability
\operatorname{Stab}_{\rho}(f)
.
We refer readers to
[
19
]
for a comprehensive overview of the analysis of Boolean functions.
As we wish to state results that apply for general settings beyond just the Boolean setting, we will not use the specific definition of noise stability
\operatorname{Stab}_{\rho}(f)
, though the spirit remains the same.

2.3.
Hierarchical multilinear functions

We now consider functions consisting of a composition of multilinear functions in a hierarchical structure.
Specifically, for any positive integers
n
and
d
, we define a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
to be a
hierarchical multilinear function
of non-linearity
\varepsilon>0
and depth
d
recursively as follows.
A hierarchical multilinear function of non-linearity
\varepsilon>0
and depth 1 is simply a function satisfying the conditions of
Lemma

1.1
.
Note that
d(f,\operatorname{Lin})
does not depend on the underlying random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
.
A hierarchical multilinear function of non-linearity
\varepsilon>0
and depth
d\geq 2
is a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form

f(x)=g(h_{1}(x_{\mathcal{P}_{1}}),\dots,h_{m}(x_{\mathcal{P}_{m}}))

for some partition
\mathcal{P}
of
[n]
with
m\geq 2
parts and hierarchical multilinear functions
g,h_{1},\dots,h_{m}
of non-linearity
\varepsilon
, where
g
has depth 1 and each
h_{i}
has depth at least
d-1
.
While this definition allows for the
h_{i}
to have depth greater than or equal to
d
, note that any depth-
d
hierarchical multilinear function of non-linearity
\varepsilon>0
must be a function of at least
2^{d}
variables, so our recursive definition does terminate and is well-defined.

As previously mentioned, all functions on Boolean random variables are multilinear, so any function on Boolean random variables of this hierarchical form whose component functions all have Boolean outputs, i.e., take on two values, except for potentially the top component function, are hierarchical multilinear functions.
Thus, our definition of a hierarchical multilinear function includes all hierarchical Boolean functions.

We can now prove
Theorem

1.3
, which shows that
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))
decays exponentially in depth
d
, for hierarchical multilinear functions of positive non-linearity.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Let
g(x)=(1-\varepsilon)x+\varepsilon x^{2}
.
For positive integer
m\geq 1
, let
g^{m}
denote the composition of
g
a total of
m
times.
Because
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
have the same first and second moments, and because the
(X_{i},Y_{i})
are mutually independent across
i
, any multilinear function
h
will have
\mathbb{E}[h(X)]=\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]
and
\mathbb{E}[h(X)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[h(Y)^{2}]
, and thus we can repeatedly apply
Lemma

1.1
for all component functions of our hierarchical multilinear function
f
, as the first and second moment conditions of
Lemma

1.1
inductively hold.
By repeatedly applying
Lemma

1.1
throughout the hierarchical structure of
f
, and noting that
g
is an increasing function on
[0,1]
with
0\leq g(x)<x
for all
0\leq x<1
, we have
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))\leq g^{d}(\rho)
, so it suffices to show
g^{d}(\rho)\leq\left(1-\delta\right)^{d-C_{\varepsilon,\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)}
for all
d\geq 1
and
0\leq\rho<1
for some constant
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
.
Note that
\frac{g(x)}{x}=1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon x
, so there exists some
\alpha=\alpha_{\varepsilon,\delta}\in(0,1)
such that for all
x\leq\alpha
, we have
g(x)\leq(1-\delta)x
.
Thus it suffices to show there exists some nonnegative integer
N\leq C_{\varepsilon,\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)
such that
g^{N}(\rho)\leq\alpha
.
If
\rho\leq\alpha
, we simply take
N=0
.
Otherwise, note that for
\alpha\leq x<1
, we have

\frac{1-g(x)}{1-x}=1+\varepsilon-\varepsilon(1-x)\geq 1+\varepsilon\alpha>1,

so we may take
N\geq\log_{1+\varepsilon\alpha}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\rho}\right).

∎

Remark 2.2
.

We remark that this bound is essentially tight.
Suppose all component functions defining our hierarchical multilinear function
f
have non-linearity exactly
\varepsilon>0
, i.e., each component function
g
satisfies
d(g,\operatorname{Lin})=\varepsilon
, and
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})=\rho
for all
i
.
With these tight conditions, following the proof of
Lemma

1.1
, it is straightforward to see that we could have proven
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))\geq(1-\varepsilon)\rho
instead of (
1.1
).
Inductively applying this bound instead of
Lemma

1.1
, we see that our hierarchical multilinear function of depth
d
has
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{d}\rho
.
Hence, it is impossible to have exponential rate smaller than
1-\varepsilon
, and we have provided a bound with exponential rate
\alpha
for all
\alpha>1-\varepsilon
.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that in the case
\varepsilon=1
, i.e.,
f
is a hierarchical multilinear function where each component function is completely uncorrelated with any linear function,
Lemma

1.1
actually implies
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))\leq\rho^{2^{d}}
.
In this case, we have doubly exponential decay in depth, with decay rate depending on
\rho
, stronger than the singly exponential decay provided by
Theorem

1.3
, where the decay rate depends on
\varepsilon
.

Remark 2.4
.

In the literature of cryptography, a Boolean function is
t
-resilient
if it is balanced and remains unpredictable even if an adversary knows up to
t
of the
n
input bits.
Concretely, this means
\widehat{f}(S)=0
for all
\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq t
.
We refer interested readers to
[
6
, Chapter 8]
for further background on Boolean functions in cryptography.
Our results do not care about the balanced condition, i.e.,
\widehat{f}(\emptyset)=0
, but aside from this, our case
\varepsilon=1
in the Boolean setting corresponds to 1-resilient functions.
It is straightforward to see, by adapting the proof of
Lemma

1.1
, that for any positive integer
t
, if we have a hierarchical function whose components are all
t
-resilient Boolean functions, then we have
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))\leq\rho^{(t+1)^{d}}
.

Remark 2.5
.

One example of such a component function is the parity function in the Boolean case, i.e.,
\operatorname{Parity}(x)=x_{1}\cdot x_{2}\cdots x_{n}
, where
X_{i}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\left\{\pm 1\right\})
for all
i
.
This function takes value 1 if an even number of bits are on, i.e., have value
-1
, and value
-1
if an odd number of bits are on.
In the coding theory literature, this function is more commonly written as

\operatorname{Parity}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=x_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{n},

where
\oplus
denotes the XOR (exclusive OR), and each
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is a bit.
However, to fit this into our multilinear polynomial framework, we convert the additive group
\{0,1\}
modulo 2, i.e.,
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, into the multiplicative group
\{1,-1\}
, which is a standard transformation in the literature of analysis of Boolean functions.
Assuming
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})=\rho
for all
i
, it is well-known, and can be seen by adapting the proof of
Lemma

1.1
, that
\operatorname{Corr}(\operatorname{Parity}(X),\operatorname{Parity}(Y))=\rho^{n}
, i.e., exponential in
n
.
Note that one can view the parity function as a hierarchical function of smaller component parity functions, and by expressing the
n
-bit parity function as a hierarchical function of depth
\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor
with components being 2-bit parity functions, we have, via the
\varepsilon=1
version of
Theorem

1.3
discussed in
Remark

2.3
, that

\operatorname{Corr}(\operatorname{Parity}(X),\operatorname{Parity}(Y))\leq\rho^{2^{\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor}}<\rho^{n/2},

so our result still demonstrates exponential decay in
n
, albeit with a different rate than the true rate.

For example, note that
Theorem

1.3
shows that the noise stability of the Boolean function on
3^{d}
bits consisting of recursively composing the majority function on 3 bits, which is not linear, decays exponentially in
d
.
Thus, the recursive majority function is noise sensitive for logarithmic depth.
Recall that the fact that the recursive majority function is noise sensitive is folklore; see, for example,
[
3
,
4
]
.

Theorem

1.3
implies low-degree bounds on hierarchical multilinear functions.
To make this concrete, the following lemma shows how bounds on noise stability, that is,
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))
, imply bounds on the maximum correlation between
f
and any multilinear function of degree at most
D
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose
\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):i\in[n]\}
are mutually independent, where for some
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, we have
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})=\rho
for all
i
.
Suppose
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
have the same first and second moments, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]<\infty
, for all
i\in[n]
.
Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a multilinear function, and let
M
denote the maximum correlation between
f
and any multilinear function of degree at most
D
, i.e.,

M=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ multilinear}\\
\operatorname{deg}(g)\leq D\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),g(X)).

Then

M\leq\rho^{-D/2}\sqrt{\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))}.

Proof.

We wish to bound

M=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ multilinear}\\
\operatorname{deg}(g)\leq D\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),g(X)).

For any such
g
, decompose
f
and
g
as

f=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S},\quad g=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{g}(S)\chi_{S},

and without loss of generality rescale
g
so that
\operatorname{Var}(g)=1
, i.e.,

\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\widehat{g}(S)^{2}=1.

Following the proof of
Lemma

1.1
, we know

\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Var}(f)}\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}.

Similarly, i.e., via orthogonality of
\left\{\chi_{S}:S\subseteq[n]\right\}
, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),g(X))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(f)}}\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\widehat{f}(S)\widehat{g}(S)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(f)}}\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\widehat{f}(S)\widehat{g}(S)\leq\frac{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(f)}},

with equality if and only if

g=C+\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S}}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}}}

for some constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
.
Thus,

M=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(f)}}\sqrt{\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}}.

Then we relate
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))
with
M
via

\displaystyle\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Var}(f)}\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\operatorname{Var}(f)}\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\operatorname{Var}(f)}\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\rho^{D}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{D}M^{2},

which rearranges to our desired inequality.
∎

Using
Theorem

1.3
, for any hierarchical multilinear function
f
of non-linearity
\varepsilon>0
and depth
d
, and for any
\delta<\varepsilon
and
0\leq\rho<1
, applying
Lemma

2.6
yields (
1.3
).
Note that this is because, as previously discussed in
Remark

1.2
, we can always construct a
\rho
-correlated
Y
satisfying the conditions of
Theorem

1.3
.
In particular, for fixed
D
, our bound (
1.3
) decays exponentially in depth
d
.

This in turn lets us prove
Theorem

1.9
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.9
.

From the proof of
Lemma

2.6
, we have for any
D\in[n]
,

M=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(f)}}\sqrt{\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}}\geq\sqrt{\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}}

as
f(x)\in[-1,1]
for all
x
.
Then by (
1.3
), we have, fixing
\rho\in(0,1)
to be a constant,

\sqrt{\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}}\leq C_{\rho,\varepsilon,\delta}\rho^{-D/2}(1-\delta)^{d/2}=e^{\Theta(D)-\Theta(\log n)},

for some constant
C_{\rho,\varepsilon,\delta}>0
.
Pick constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
sufficiently small with
c_{2}\geq c_{1}
so that, letting
D=\frac{c_{1}\log n}{\log\log n}
and
x=n^{c_{2}}
, we have

xe^{D}C_{\rho,\varepsilon,\delta}\rho^{-D/2}(1-\delta)^{d/2}=o(1).

Let this expression be denoted by
\tau
.

[
7
, Theorem 3.2]
implies there exists constant
K>0
such that
f
is
O\left(\tau+\exp\left(-Kx^{2/D}\right)n^{2D+2}\right)
-approximately
D
-resilient.
Note that, re-picking
c_{1}
as necessary, this asymptotic expression is
o(1)
, because

-Kx^{2/D}+(2D+2)\log n=-K(\log n)^{\frac{2c_{2}}{c_{1}}}+\Theta\left(\frac{\log^{2}(n)}{\log\log(n)}\right)=-\omega(1).

Applying
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
then completes the proof.
∎

3.
Non-separable functions

3.1.
Correlated probability spaces

If we hope to generalize
Theorem

1.3
beyond multilinear functions, we will need to strengthen our initial assumption, i.e., that
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})\leq\rho
.
Because
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})
only captures linear relationships between
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
, if our function is not multilinear, it has the potential to have higher correlation
\operatorname{Corr}(f(X),f(Y))
than that of its components, i.e.,
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})
, by utilizing these nonlinear relationships.
Recall
Examples

1.4
and
1.5
, which illustrate how nonlinear relationships in a single variable can lead to noise stability that does not decay with depth.
To this end, we broaden our concept of correlation via the following definition of correlation between probability spaces, sometimes referred to as the “Hirschfeld–Gebelein–Rényi maximal correlation” as it was first introduced by Hirschfeld
[
12
]
and Gebelein
[
10
]
and then studied by Rényi
[
21
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
defined on
\Omega=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Omega_{i}
, we say that
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{k}
are
correlated spaces
.
Given two linear subspaces
A
and
B
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
, we define the
correlation
between
A
and
B
by

\rho(A,B;\mathbb{P})=\rho(A,B)=\sup\left\{\operatorname{Corr}(f,g):f\in A,g\in B\right\}

For
i,j\in[k]
, we define the
correlation

\rho(\Omega_{i},\Omega_{j};\mathbb{P})
by

\rho(\Omega_{i},\Omega_{j};\mathbb{P})=\rho(\Omega_{i},\Omega_{j})=\rho(L^{2}(\Omega_{i},\mathbb{P}),L^{2}(\Omega_{j},\mathbb{P});\mathbb{P}).

As
\rho
also denotes a parameter in this paper, we will also use the notation
\operatorname{Corr}(\cdot)
in place of
\rho(\cdot)
for clarity.

For random vector
X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
L^{2}(X)
denote the linear subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
that is
\sigma(X)
-measurable.
Each
f\in L^{2}(X)
can be identified with a function
\mathfrak{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{f}(X)^{2}]<\infty
, and we will frequently make no distinction between these two functions.
Equipped with this new definition, we will consider the condition that
\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(X_{i}),L^{2}(Y_{i}))\leq\rho
, in place of the condition that
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})\leq\rho
.

We recall some theory from Mossel
[
17
, §2.2]
concerning noise correlation of functions.
For two random variables
X,Y:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, if
f\in L^{2}(X)
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[f(X)]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[f(X)^{2}]=1
, then the maximizer of
\left\lvert\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)]\right\rvert
among
g\in L^{2}(Y)
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[g(Y)^{2}]=1
is given by

g=\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(X)|Y]}{\left\lVert\mathbb{E}[f(X)|Y]\right\rVert_{2}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(X)|Y]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[f(X)|Y]^{2}]}},

and

\left\lvert\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)]\right\rvert=\left\lVert\mathbb{E}[f(X)|Y]\right\rVert_{2}

The function
\mathbb{E}[f(X)|Y]\in L^{2}(Y)
is the
Markov operator
from
L^{2}(X)
to
L^{2}(Y)
, and as such we will denote this
g
by
Tf\in L^{2}(Y)
.
We have
\mathbb{E}[Tf]=0
, so

(3.1)

\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(X),L^{2}(Y))=\sup\left\{\mathbb{E}[f(X)Tf(X)]:f\in L^{2}(X),\mathbb{E}[f]=0,\mathbb{E}[f^{2}]=1\right\}.

3.2.
Efron–Stein decomposition

Returning to our setup in
Section

2
, where
\mathbb{P}
is a product measure on product space
\Omega
over the laws of correlated pairs of random variables
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
, we now introduce the Efron–Stein decomposition as our analog to the
\chi_{S}
that decomposed our multilinear polynomials.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(\Omega_{1},\mu_{1}),\dots,(\Omega_{n},\mu_{n})
be laws of correlated pairs of real-valued random variables
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
, and let
(\Omega,\mu)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\Omega_{i},\mu_{i})
.
The
Efron–Stein decomposition
of
f\in L^{2}(X)
is given by

f(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}f_{S}(x),

where each function
f_{S}
depends only on
x_{S}
and for all
S\not\subseteq S^{\prime}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[f_{S}|X_{S^{\prime}}]=0
almost surely.
We can similarly define the Efron–Stein decomposition for any
g\in L^{2}(Y)
.

It is well-known that the Efron–Stein decomposition exists and is unique
[
9
]
.
A standard result (see, for example,
[
17
, Proposition 2.11]
, which only proves the result in the discrete case but naturally holds in general) states that the Efron–Stein decomposition “commutes” with the Markov operator, i.e.,
(Tf)_{S}=T(f_{S})\in L^{2}(Y)
, where
T:L^{2}(X)\to L^{2}(Y)
is the Markov operator, which is given by
T=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}
for Markov operators
T_{i}:L^{2}(X_{i})\to L^{2}(Y_{i})
.
It thus is unambiguous to write
Tf_{S}
.
Similarly generalizing the proof of
[
17
, Proposition 2.12]
, if
\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(X_{i}),L^{2}(Y_{i}))\leq\rho_{i}
for some
\rho_{i}
for each
i
, then for all
S\subseteq[n]
,

(3.2)

\left\lVert Tf_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left(\prod_{i\in S}\rho_{i}\right)\left\lVert f_{S}\right\rVert_{2}.

3.3.
Hierarchical non-separable functions

Recall the goal of this section is to generalize
Theorem

1.3
beyond multilinear functions.
Just as we had to broaden our assumption that
\operatorname{Corr}(X_{i},Y_{i})\leq\rho
to
\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(X_{i}),L^{2}(Y_{i}))\leq\rho
, we must also broaden our assumption that
f
is non-linear to account for non-linear relationships in a single variable.
To this end, we replace the concept of non-linearity with non-separability, where recall an (additively) separable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function of the form
f(x)=f_{1}(x_{1})+\cdots+f_{n}(x_{n})
for
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Note that in the context of multilinear polynomials, separability and linearity are equivalent, so this definition of a separable function is consistent with our setup in
Section

2
.
We can then define non-separability by the condition that

\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),L^{2}(X_{1})+\cdots+L^{2}(X_{n}))\leq 1-\varepsilon.

For any
g\in L^{2}(f(X))
and
h_{i}\in L^{2}(X_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
, where we normalize
\mathbb{E}[g]=0=\mathbb{E}[h_{i}]
for all
i
and
\mathbb{E}[g^{2}]=1=\mathbb{E}[(h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n})^{2}]
, we have by the defining properties of the Efron–Stein decomposition, where we view
G=g\circ f\in L^{2}(X)
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Corr}(g,h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[g(f(X))(h_{1}(X_{1})+\cdots+h_{n}(X_{n}))]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\mathbb{E}[G_{S}(h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n})_{S}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[G_{\{i\}}h_{i}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert G_{\{i\}}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert h_{i}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert G_{\{i\}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert h_{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert G_{\{i\}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}.

Note that by picking
h_{i}
appropriately, one can make both applications of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality tight, and thus

(3.3)

\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),L^{2}(X_{1})+\cdots+L^{2}(X_{n}))=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in L^{2}(f(X))\\
\mathbb{E}[g]=0,\mathbb{E}[g^{2}]=1\end{subarray}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert G_{\{i\}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}.

Because of the square root on the right hand side, we will reparametrize
\varepsilon
so that our non-separability condition is

\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),L^{2}(X_{1})+\cdots+L^{2}(X_{n}))\leq\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}.

By naturally extending our definition of
d(f,V)
to
d(U,V)
for
U,V\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
by

d(U,V)=\inf_{f\in U,g\in V}\frac{\mathbb{E}[(f-g)^{2}]}{\operatorname{Var}(f)},

note that

\displaystyle d(L^{2}(f(X)),L^{2}(X_{1})+\cdots+L^{2}(X_{n}))

\displaystyle=\inf_{g,h_{1},\dots,h_{n}}\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{S}\left\lVert G_{S}-(h_{1})_{S}-\cdots-(h_{n})_{S})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in L^{2}(f(X))\\
\mathbb{E}[g]=0,\mathbb{E}[g^{2}]=1\end{subarray}}\displaystyle\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert\geq 2}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

where we have used the fact that
f\mapsto f_{S}
is a linear operator, which follows from linearity of expectation.
Assuming
\mathbb{E}[g]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[g^{2}]=1
, we have
G_{\emptyset}\equiv 0
and
\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=1
, so we see that the extremizers in (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) are the same
g
, and letting
V=L^{2}(X_{1})+\cdots+L^{2}(X_{n})
,

(3.5)

\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),V)\leq\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\iff d(L^{2}(f(X)),V)\geq\varepsilon.

While the latter condition is more consistent with our condition in
Section

2
, we will use the former condition to consistently use the language of correlated probability spaces.
Of course, this choice is purely stylistic, as these two conditions are equivalent.
We say a function
f\in L^{2}(X)
satisfying this condition is
\varepsilon
-non-separable
.
Likewise, we can define a
hierarchical function

f\in L^{2}(X)
of non-separability
\varepsilon>0
and depth
d
analogously to how we defined hierarchical multilinear functions.

We are now ready to prove
Lemma

1.6
, our generalization of
Lemma

1.1
to non-separable functions.

Proof of
Lemma

1.6
.

Let
Z=f(X)
and
W=f(Y)
.
Pick arbitrary
g,h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{E}[g(Z)]=\mathbb{E}[h(W)]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[g(Z)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[h(W)^{2}]=1
.
It suffices to show

\mathbb{E}[g(Z)h(W)]\leq(1-\varepsilon)\rho+\varepsilon\rho^{2}.

Let

G=g\circ f\in L^{2}(X)\quad\text{and}\quad H=h\circ f\in L^{2}(Y).

Note that for distinct
S,S^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
, we have
\mathbb{E}[G_{S}(X)H_{S^{\prime}}(Y)]=0
.
This is because we either have
S\not\subseteq S^{\prime}
or
S^{\prime}\not\subseteq S
, and in the former case, by the defining properties of the Efron–Stein decomposition and recalling the
(X_{i},Y_{i})
are mutually independent across
i
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G_{S}(X)H_{S^{\prime}}(Y)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[G_{S}(X)H_{S^{\prime}}(Y)|(X_{S^{\prime}},Y_{S^{\prime}})]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[G_{S}(X)|(X_{S^{\prime}},Y_{S^{\prime}})]H_{S^{\prime}}(Y)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[0\cdot H_{S^{\prime}}(Y)]

\displaystyle=0.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(Z)h(W)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\mathbb{E}[G_{S}(X)H_{S}(Y)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{G_{S}(X)}{\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}}\frac{H_{S}(Y)}{\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{G_{S}(X)}{\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}}T\left(\frac{G_{S}}{\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert TG_{S}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert},

where the first inequality uses the property that the Markov operator maximizes covariance as discussed in
Section

3.1
, and the second inequality follows from (
3.2
).
Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and then our non-separability condition, we bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(Z)h(W)]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}}\sqrt{\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\varepsilon)\rho+\varepsilon\rho^{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem

1.8
follows from the same proof as that of
Theorem

1.3
using
Lemma

1.6
in place of
Lemma

1.1
.

Remark 3.3
.

Again, we note that in the case
\varepsilon=1
, i.e.,
f
is a hierarchical function where each component function has zero correlation with any function of a single one of its component variables,
Lemma

1.6
implies
\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),L^{2}(f(Y)))\leq\rho^{2^{d}}
.
So, analogously to
Remark

2.3
, in this case we have doubly exponential decay in depth with decay rate depending on
\rho
, stronger than the singly exponential decay provided by
Theorem

1.8
with decay rate depending on
\varepsilon
.

Next, we state and prove the following general version of
Lemma

2.6
, which we use to justify (
1.4
).

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):i\in[n]\}
are mutually independent, where for some
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, we have
\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(X_{i}),L^{2}(Y_{i}))=\rho
for all
i
.
Suppose
X
and
Y
have the same marginal distributions.
If
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[f(X)^{2}]<\infty
, then let
M
denote the maximum correlation between
L^{2}(f)
and any function of Efron–Stein degree at most
D
, i.e.,

M=\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),\mathcal{G}),

where

\mathcal{G}=\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}L^{2}(X_{S}).

Let

\gamma=\sup_{G,H}\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert},

where the supremum is taken over all
g,h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{E}[g(f(X))]=\mathbb{E}[h(f(Y))]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[g(f(X))^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[h(f(Y))^{2}]=1
, and
G=g\circ f\in L^{2}(X)
and
H=h\circ f\in L^{2}(Y)
.
Then

M\leq\rho^{-D/2}\sqrt{\gamma}.

Proof.

Let
Z=f(X)
and
W=f(Y)
.
Using a slightly generalized but essentially similar argument as that which we used to show (
3.3
), we have

M=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in L^{2}(f(X))\\
\mathbb{E}[g]=0,\mathbb{E}[g^{2}]=1\end{subarray}}\sqrt{\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}},

where
G=g\circ f\in L^{2}(X)
.
It then suffices to show that for any such
g
,

\sqrt{\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\leq\rho^{-D/2}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in L^{2}(f(Y))\\
\mathbb{E}[h]=0,\mathbb{E}[h^{2}]=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\right)^{1/2},

where
H=h\circ f\in L^{2}(Y)
.
Letting
g
and
h
be the same when viewed as functions
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, because
X
and
Y
have the same marginal distributions, we note that
H_{S}=G_{S}
for all
S
, which achieves the equality case of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that implies

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in L^{2}(f(Y))\\
\mathbb{E}[h]=0,\mathbb{E}[h^{2}]=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}=\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}.

Hence, it suffices to show

\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq D}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho^{D}\leq\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert},

which is clearly true as
0\leq\rho\leq 1
.
∎

To justify (
1.4
), note that for any
0\leq\rho<1
, by
Remark

1.7
, we can construct
Y
satisfying the conditions of
Lemma

3.4
.
Recall that
Theorem

1.8
was proven by inductively applying
Lemma

1.6
.
Using the fact that the proof of
Lemma

1.6
implies the stronger technical statement

\sup_{G,H}\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\leq(1-\varepsilon)\rho+\varepsilon\rho^{2},

the proof of
Theorem

1.8
implies that for a hierarchical function
f\in L^{2}(X)
of non-separability
\varepsilon>0
and depth
d
, and for any
\delta<\varepsilon
and
0\leq\rho<1
(where because our constructed
Y
has the same marginal distribution as

, we have
f
is also such a hierarchical function in

\sup_{G,H}\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\left\lVert G_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert H_{S}\right\rVert_{2}\rho^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\leq(1-\delta)^{d-C_{\varepsilon,\delta}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right)}.

).

Lastly, we use
Theorem

1.8
to prove
Theorem

1.10
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.10
.

By
[
8
, Theorem 1.7]
, it suffices to show that for any polynomial
p:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree less than
D=n^{c}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\left\lvert f(X)-p(X)\right\rvert]\geq\frac{1}{8}-o(1).

To show this we will use
[
8
, Theorem 1.5]
.
By
Theorem

1.8
,

\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),L^{2}(f(Y)))\leq(1-\delta)^{d-\Theta(\log\frac{1}{1-\rho})}.

Fixing
\delta<\varepsilon
and setting
1-\rho=\frac{1}{D^{3}}
, this bound is
e^{-\Theta(\log n)+\Theta(\log D)}
, and choosing
c
sufficiently small ensures this is
o(1)
.
For any such polynomial
p
, by
[
8
, Theorem 1.5]
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\left\lvert f(X)-p(X)\right\rvert]\geq\frac{\mathbb{P}(f(X)\neq f(Y))}{4}-O(D\sqrt{1-\rho})=\frac{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[f(X)f(Y)]}{4}-o(1),

where
Y
is a
\rho
-correlated spherical Gaussian.
By (
3.1
),
\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(X_{i}),L^{2}(Y_{i}))=\rho
for all
i
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left\lvert f(X)-p(X)\right\rvert]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[f(X)f(Y)]}{8}-o(1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(f(X))\operatorname{Corr}(L^{2}(f(X)),L^{2}(f(Y)))}{8}-o(1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}-\frac{(1-\delta)^{d-\Theta(\log\frac{1}{1-\rho})}}{8}-o(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}-o(1),

which completes the proof.
∎
