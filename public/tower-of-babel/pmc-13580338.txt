Self‐Induced Stretch Syncope: An Underrecognized Condition
Authors: Samia M Khan, Vandana J Vedanarayanan
Published: 2026

We describe a child with position‐independent self‐induced stretch syncope preceded by an atypical prodrome of diminished hearing and a sense of heaviness of the head. A sibling has postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS). This condition is rarely reported and likely underrecognized.

Patient Description

This previously healthy 12‐year‐old boy presented to the dysautonomia clinic for evaluation of a 4‐month history of syncope precipitated by stretching. These events consisted of an initial sensation of his back “feeling funny” that induced stretching. When performed in any position, this resulted in what he described as “tunnel vision” and syncope lasting up to 30 s, with occasional brief, subtle jerking of his extremities. He described a brief prodrome of diminished hearing and a "heavy" feeling in his head. Following the episode, he experienced confusion but quickly returned to baseline. Temperature, large meals, and poor hydration did not affect frequency, and there were no other associated symptoms. His past history includes anxiety and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder, treated with sertraline and dexmethylphenidate. His family history was contributory only for a sister with POTS.

His neurological examination was normal. When transitioning from lying to sitting to standing, his heart rate increased by 25 beats per minute, below the criteria for a POTS diagnosis.

An earlier inpatient electroencephalogram had a normal background rhythm without epileptiform activity, including during an episode of dizziness and left arm shaking. Other normal diagnostic studies included a comprehensive metabolic panel, an electrocardiogram, magnetic resonance imaging of the brain without contrast, and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) of the head and neck.

Discussion

Self‐induced stretch syncope is a rare but benign disorder characterized by stereotypic stretching of the upper back, neck, and shoulder muscles, typically with neck hyperextension, leading to a brief loss of consciousness [1]. This patient described daily symptomatic episodes after stretching, prompted by a “funny feeling” in his back. In addition to neck hyperextension and arching of the back, he experienced symptoms while straightening his arms, pushing against an object, or lowering his head below his arms in a standing position. This disorder has been described in individuals who have a compulsion to stretch and induce syncope, while other reports do not describe a specific urge to stretch [2]. This may reflect a sensory tic with compulsive behavioral response that leads to the episode of stretch syncope. Stretch syncope has been described in fewer than 30 patients in the literature [3]. However, it is probably encountered more frequently in practice than these few reports would suggest. This disorder was originally thought to occur based on anatomic disruption of blood flow within vertebral arteries but is now considered a multifactorial neurological disorder that occurs with exaggerated vasodepressor response after stretch, with blunted tachycardic compensation [4]. This aligns with our patient's normal MRA without evidence of flow‐limiting stenosis and normal baseline active stand testing.

Our patient's prodrome of diminished hearing and a feeling of heaviness is atypical of stretch syncope; most individuals report lightheadedness or dizziness [3]. This may reflect the auditory reverberations often observed in the initial evolution of a syncopal attack and may be more clearly described by this patient than others. Furthermore, this is the first documented patient whose sibling has autonomic dysfunction, suggesting a possible familial predisposition or genetic basis for this disorder. Awareness of the disorder is important to avoid misdiagnosis, prevent unnecessary testing, and encourage further investigation into its exact mechanism.
