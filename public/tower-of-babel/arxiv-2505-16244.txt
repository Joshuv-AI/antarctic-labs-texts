Generalized Power Priors for Improved Bayesian Inference with Historical Data
Masanari Kimura and Howard Bondell

Abstract
The power prior is a class of informative priors designed to incorporate historical data alongside current data in a Bayesian framework. It includes a power parameter that controls the influence of historical data, providing flexibility and adaptability. A key property of the power prior is that the resulting posterior minimizes a linear combination of KL divergences between two pseudo-posterior distributions: one ignoring historical data and the other fully incorporating it. We extend this framework by identifying the posterior distribution as the minimizer of a linear combination of Amari’s α \alpha -divergence, a generalization of KL divergence. We show that this generalization can lead to improved performance by allowing for the data to adapt to appropriate choices of the α \alpha parameter. Theoretical properties of this generalized power posterior are established, including behavior as a generalized geodesic on the Riemannian manifold of probability distributions, offering novel insights into its geometric interpretation.



Keywords: Power prior, Bayesian analysis, divergence

Introduction

Scientific investigations in areas such as early-phase
clinical trials, rare-disease studies and high-dimensional omics often suffer from data that are sparse, noisy or otherwise unrepresentative of the target population.
Concurrently, investigators typically possess an expanding archive of historical trials, registries and real-world evidence that can stabilize estimation and sharpen decision making.
The methodological challenge is therefore not whether to borrow information, but how much to borrow and how to calibrate that borrowing so that efficiency gains are not paid for with excessive sensitivity to model–data conflict.
A widely adopted solution is the power prior of
Ibrahim & Chen (2000)
, which raises the historical likelihood to a scalar weight
\xi\in[0,1]
before combining it with a baseline prior.
Its conceptual clarity and conjugate structure have spurred numerous extensions including joint and normalised forms
(
Chen et al., 2000
;
Duan et al., 2006
)
, commensurate and location-adaptive variants
(
Hobbs et al., 2011
)
, effective sample size rules
(
Ollier et al., 2020
)
, and scale-transformed approaches for discordant endpoints
(
Alt et al., 2023
)
.
The theoretical underpinning is elegant:

Ibrahim et al. (2003)
showed that the power-prior
posterior minimizes a convex combination of two
Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergences, one anchored at the
no-borrowing and the other at the full-borrowing posterior.

Despite this progress, existing variants inherit the KL foundation of the original power prior.
While KL optimality is attractive for well-specified models, its heavy tail and asymmetric penalty make the resulting posterior sensitive to outliers, prior–data conflict and model misspecification issues that are accentuated when historical studies differ systematically from contemporaneous trials.
This paper closes that gap by lifting the KL criterion to Amari’s
\alpha
-divergence
(
Amari, 2009
)
which is a one-parameter family that interpolates smoothly between the forward and reverse KL limits and encompasses a broad spectrum of robust divergences.
The resulting posterior is shown to be the unique minimizer of a weighted sum of
\alpha
-divergences to the no- and full-borrowing pseudo–posteriors, and retaining the optimization strategy of the classical power prior while injecting a tunable robustness parameter
\alpha
.
Moreover, the solution lies on the

\alpha
-geodesic connecting the two pseudo-posteriors on the statistical manifold, exposing an information-geometric structure not apparent in previous formulations.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows.

•

Divergence-optimal characterization.

We generalize existing power prior and prove that the resulting posterior is a minimizer of a weighted sum of
\alpha
–divergences.

•

Theoretical guarantees.

We establish (i) global prior–data robustness bounds under
Huber-type contamination, (ii) shape theorems showing how
\alpha

controls uni- versus multi-modality, and (iii) consistency and
higher-order asymptotics whose leading variance term coincides with that
of standard weighted likelihood but whose
O(n^{-2})
correction depends
on
(\alpha,\xi)
.

•

Information-geometric insight.

We show that the new posterior traces an
\alpha
-geodesic between two distributions on the statistical manifold.

•

Worked examples and case study.

We provide fully-worked Bayesian survival analysis of two ECOG melanoma trials that demonstrates practical gains in hazard-ratio estimation and predictive concordance.

2
Backgroud

In Bayesian analysis, it is often the case that one can access historical data
D_{0}
in addition to the current data
D
.
The power prior, which is useful in such cases, is a class of informative prior distributions first developed by
Ibrahim & Chen (2000)
.

Definition 1

(Power prior
(
Ibrahim & Chen, 2000
)
)
.

Let the data for the current study be denoted by
D
, and denote the corresponding likelihood function by
L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)
, where
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
is a vector of parameters in the parameter space
\Theta
.
Suppose we have historical data
D_{0}
from a similar previous study, and let
L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})
be the corresponding likelihood function.
Then, the power prior is defined as
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0},\xi)\propto L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})^{\xi}\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})
, where
0\leq\xi\leq 1
is a scalar parameter and
\pi_{0}
is the initial prior for
\bm{\theta}
before the historical data
D_{0}
is observed.

Using the power prior in Definition
1
, the corresponding posterior distribution of
\bm{\theta}
is given by
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid D,D_{0},\xi)\propto L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})^{\xi}\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})
.
One can see that
\xi
weights the historical data relative to the likelihood of the current study, and thus
\xi
controls the influence of the historical data.
Considering two extreme cases, we can see that

•

\xi=0
: ignore the historical data
D_{0}
as
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid D,D_{0},\xi=0)\propto L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})
, and

•

\xi=1
: give equal importance to
D_{0}
and
D
, relative to their sample sizes, as
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid D,D_{0},\xi=1)\propto L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})
.

Since the power prior is a likelihood function raised to a power, it shares all of the properties of likelihood functions, and therefore has several advantages over other priors.
For example, it can be shown that for many classes of models,

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0},\xi)\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{0},\xi^{-1}H^{-1}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\right),

where
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
is the mode of the power prior and

\displaystyle H^{-1}(\bm{\theta})=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}\ln\left(\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0},\xi)\right).

The power prior has been applied in many fields as the useful informative prior, including survival analysis for clinical trials
(
Chen et al., 1999
;
Chen et al., 2002
;
Ibrahim et al., 2012
)
, non-inferiority trials for anti-infective products
(
Gamalo et al., 2014
)
, anxiety and depression scale in psychology
(
Matteucci & Veldkamp, 2014
)
, benchmark dose estimation in toxicology
(
Shao, 2011
)
, randomized therapeutic trials
(
Rietbergen et al., 2011
)
, pediatric quality of care
(
Neelon & O’Malley, 2010
)
and evaluating water quality in environmental science
(
Duan et al., 2006
)
.

Moreover, many variants of the power prior have been proposed due to its simplicity of formulation.

Chen et al. (2000)
developed the hierarchical prior specification by taking
\xi\in[0,1]
random, and proposed a variant
\pi^{\mathrm{JPP}}
called the joint power prior (JPP).
For example, one can use a beta distribution for
\pi_{0}(\xi)
.
Another modification of the power prior when
\xi
is random, which was introduced by
Duan et al. (2006)
, is called the normalized power prior
\pi^{\mathrm{NPP}}
.
The main difference between
\pi^{\mathrm{JPP}}
and
\pi^{\mathrm{NPP}}
is that
\pi^{\mathrm{JPP}}
specifies a joint prior distribution directly for
(\bm{\theta},\xi)
while
\pi^{\mathrm{NPP}}
first specifies a conditional prior distribution for
\bm{\theta}
given
\xi
and then specifies a marginal distribution for
\xi
.
A problem with the JPP
\pi^{\mathrm{JPP}}
is that they do not directly parameterize the commensurability of the historical data and new data.
To solve this problem,
Hobbs et al. (2011)
proposed the location commensurate power prior (LCPP).
Similar ideas include the partial discounting power prior
(
Chen et al., 2001
)
and partial borrowing power prior
(
Ibrahim et al., 2012
)
.
The adaptive power prior (APP)
(
Ollier et al., 2020
)
is based on a criterion constructed using the effective sample size (ESS) and the Hellinger distance.
In this criterion, the ESS is used for checking the maximum desired amount of information, and the Hellinger distance is used for tuning the final balance.
Several variants of power prior are developed for settings where historical and current data involve different data types, such as binary and continuous data.
One of these variants is the scale transformed power prior (straPP)
(
Alt et al., 2023
)
.
The derivation for the straPP is based on the assumption that the standardized parameter values are approximately equal for the historical and current data models and it makes sense to consider such a prior when historical and current data have different outcomes, but nonetheless outcomes that measure related characteristics.
In addition,
Alt et al. (2023)
developed the normalized version of the straPP.
Moreover, it is useful to develop a generalization of the straPP that provides a degree of robustness when the assumption of equal standardized parameter values does not hold.
Towards this goal,
Alt et al. (2023)
developed a generalized scale transformed power prior (Gen-straPP).

One of the most important properties of the power prior is its optimality under the KL divergence.
This property is given by
Ibrahim et al. (2003)
.

Theorem 1

(Optimality of power prior under KL divergence
(
Ibrahim et al., 2003
)
)
.

Let
\bm{\theta}\mapsto p_{0}(\bm{\theta})
and
\bm{\theta}\mapsto p_{1}(\bm{\theta})
be pseudo posteriors defined as follows.

\displaystyle p_{0}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid D,D_{0},\xi=0)=L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta}),

\displaystyle p_{1}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid D,D_{0},\xi=1)=L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta}).

Here, the posterior
\bm{\theta}\mapsto g(\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid D,D_{0},\xi)
is a minimizer of the following criterion.

\displaystyle(1-\xi)D_{\mathrm{KL}}[g\|p_{0}]+\xi D_{\mathrm{KL}}[g\|p_{1}],

where
(p,q)\mapsto D_{\mathrm{KL}}[p\|q]
is the KL divergence defined as

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}[p\|q]\coloneqq\int_{\Theta}p(\bm{\theta})\ln\frac{p(\bm{\theta})}{q(\bm{\theta})}d\bm{\theta}.

Another proof using Lagrangians, different from the one given by
Ibrahim et al. (2003)
is provided in Section B of the Supplementary Material, for the sake of unifying the discussion that follows.
While the above theorem guarantees optimality under power prior to KL divergence, it is known to have problems such as a lack of robustness to outliers.
In the following, the optimal form of the posterior distribution under generalized divergence is identified and its properties are investigated.

3
Generalization

Consider the following generalized divergence.

Definition 2

(Amari’s
\alpha
-divergence
(
Amari, 2009
)
)
.

For two probability densities
\bm{\theta}\mapsto p(\bm{\theta})
and
\bm{\theta}\mapsto q(\bm{\theta})
on the same space
\Theta
, Amari’s
\alpha
-divergence
(p,q)\mapsto D_{\alpha}[p\|q]
is given as follows.

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}[p\|q]\coloneqq\frac{4}{1-\alpha^{2}}\left(1-\int p(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}q(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}d\bm{\theta}\right),

where
\alpha\neq 1
.

Here, it is known that Amari’s
\alpha
-divergence corresponds to KL divergence and its dual, respectively, at
\alpha\to\pm 1
.

For the generalization of Theorem
1
, consider the following minimization problem.

\displaystyle\min_{g(\bm{\theta})}\left\{(1-\xi)D_{\alpha}[g\|p_{0}]+\xi D_{\alpha}[g\|p_{1}]\right\},

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}=1,\ g(\bm{\theta})\geq 0.

Let
S_{i}(g)=\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}p_{i}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}
for
i\in\{0,1\}
.
Then,

\displaystyle(1-\xi)D_{\alpha}[g\|p_{0}]+\xi D_{\alpha}[g\|p_{1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1-\alpha^{2}}\left\{(1-\xi)\left(1-S_{0}(g)\right)+\xi\left(1-S_{1}(g)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1-\alpha^{2}}\left\{1-\left((1-\xi)S_{0}(g)+\xi S_{1}(g)\right)\right\}.

Minimizing the above is equivalent to maximizing the following.

\displaystyle(1-\xi)S_{0}(g)+\xi S_{1}(g)

\displaystyle=(1-\xi)\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}p_{0}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}d\bm{\theta}+\xi\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}p_{1}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}d\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\left\{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+\xi p_{1}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right\}d\bm{\theta}.

Let
A(\bm{\theta})=(1-\xi)p_{0}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+\xi p_{1}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}
.
Now, the objective is

\displaystyle g^{*}=\argmax_{g\colon\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}=1}\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}A(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}.

Introduce a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
for the constraint
\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}=1
, and define the Lagrangian functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(g,\lambda)\coloneqq\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}A(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}-\lambda\left\{\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}-1\right\}.

Then, consider the Gateaux derivative of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
g(\bm{\theta})
and require it to vanish at the optimum as
\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}(g,\lambda)}{\delta g(\bm{\theta})}=0
.
Since

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta g(\bm{\theta})}\left(g(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}A(\bm{\theta})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}g(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}-1}A(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1-\alpha}{2}g(\bm{\theta})^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}A(\bm{\theta}),

the stationarity condition gives

\displaystyle\frac{1-\alpha}{2}g(\bm{\theta})^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}A(\bm{\theta})-\lambda=0,\quad\Rightarrow\quad g(\bm{\theta})=\left\{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\lambda}A(\bm{\theta})\right\}^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}.

Here, for enforcing normalization,
1=\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}=\int_{\Theta}CA(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}
, where
C=((1-\alpha)/2\lambda)^{2/(1+\alpha)}
, and
C=1/\int_{\Theta}A(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}d\bm{\theta}
.
Therefore, the unique minimizer is

\displaystyle g^{*}(\bm{\theta}\mid D)=\frac{A(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}}{\int_{\Theta}A(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}d\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}=\frac{\left\{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+\xi p_{1}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right\}^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}}{\int_{\Theta}\left\{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+\xi p_{1}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right\}^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}d\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}.

(1)

Denote
g^{*}(\theta)
in Eq. (
1
) as the generalized power posterior.

Definition 3

(Generalized power posterior)
.

The generalized power posterior is defined as Eq. (
1
).

Proposition 1

(Optimality of generalized power posterior under
\alpha
-divergence)
.

The generalized power posterior is the minimizer of the linear combination of Amari’s
\alpha
-divergence.

Next, to identify the prior, consider the decomposition

\displaystyle g^{*}(\bm{\theta}\mid D)\propto\pi_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})\times L(\bm{\theta}\mid D).

Then,
\pi_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})=g^{*}(\bm{\theta})/L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)
, and

\displaystyle g^{*}(\bm{\theta}\mid D)

\displaystyle\propto\left\{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+\xi p_{1}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right\}^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\left\{(1-\xi)\left(L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})\right)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+\xi\left(L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})\right)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right\}^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\left\{L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\left((1-\xi)+\xi L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right)\right\}^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}

\displaystyle=L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)\times\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})\times\left\{(1-\xi)+\xi L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right\}^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}},

\displaystyle\pi_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\propto\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})\left\{(1-\xi)+\xi L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right\}^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}.

(2)

Since
\pi_{\alpha}(\theta)
does not depend on the current data
D
, it is a valid prior distribution.
Therefore, the following definition can be obtained.

Definition 4

(Generalized power prior)
.

The generalized power prior corresponding to the generalized power posterior is defined as Eq. (
2
).

Example 1

(Univeriate Gaussian)
.

Let
D=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
,
X_{i}\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
and
D_{0}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
,
Y_{j}\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
be the current and historical data with known
\sigma^{2}
.
Also
\pi_{0}(\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\tau_{0}^{2})
be the baseline prior.
Then, for
\alpha\neq\pm 1
and
\xi\in[0,1]
, the generalized power posterior
\theta\mapsto g(\theta)
is given as

\displaystyle g(\theta)\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\right)\theta+\left(\frac{S_{X}}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\right)\theta\right\}\left\{1+C\exp\left(\frac{zS_{Y}}{\sigma^{2}}\theta-\frac{zn_{0}}{2\sigma^{2}}\theta^{2}\right)\right\}^{1/z}.

where
z=(1+\alpha)/2
,
S_{X}=\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}
,
S_{Y}=\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}
, and

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{1}}{Q_{0}}\exp\left[-\frac{z}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}^{2}\right],

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=(1-\xi)\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{nz}{2}}}\frac{1}{(2\pi\tau_{0}^{2})^{\frac{z}{2}}},

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=\xi\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{(n+n_{0})z}{2}}}\frac{1}{(2\pi\tau_{0}^{2})^{\frac{z}{2}}}.

The normalization constant is determined by integrating with respect to
\theta
.

Example 2

(Beta–Bernoulli)
.

Let
D=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
with
X_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and
D_{0}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n_{0}}\}
with
Y_{j}\in\{0,1\}
be the current and historical data from Bernoulli distributions with parameter
\theta\in(0,1)
.
Also, suppose that the baseline prior is
\pi_{0}(\theta)=\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
for
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}>0
.
Then, the corresponding generalized power posterior is given as

\displaystyle g(\theta)\propto\left\{F(\theta)\right\}^{\frac{1}{z}},

where
z=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
, and

\displaystyle F(\theta)

\displaystyle=\theta^{z(S_{X}+\alpha_{0}-1)}(1-\theta)^{z(n-S_{X}+\beta_{0}-1)}\times\left\{(1-\xi)+\xi\theta^{zS_{Y}}(1-\theta)^{z(n_{0}-S_{Y})}\right\},

with
S_{X}=\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}
and
S_{Y}=\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}
.

\alpha=1
or
\xi=0,1
.

Example 3

(Dirichlet–Multinomial)
.

Let
D=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
with
\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}=n
and
D_{0}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n_{0}}\}
with
\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}=n_{0}
from the multinomial distribution having the parameter
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{k})
.
Also, suppose that the baseline prior is
\pi_{0}(\theta)=\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha_{0,1},\alpha_{0,2},\dots,\alpha_{0,k})
.
Then, the corresponding generalized power posterior is given as

\displaystyle g(\bm{\theta})\propto\left\{(1-\xi)\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta_{i}^{z(X_{i}+\alpha_{0,i}-1)}+\xi\prod^{k}_{j=1}\theta_{i}^{z(X_{i}+Y_{j}+\alpha_{0,j}-1)}\right\}^{\frac{1}{z}},

where
z=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
.
This does not yield a simple Dirichlet form, unless
\alpha=1
, that is a non-Dirichlet shape on the simplex.

(Non-Conjugacy)
.

Even if
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
arise from a conjugate family, the resulting generalized power posterior is typically not in the same conjugate family unless
\alpha=1
or
\xi\in\{0,1\}
.

Global Prior Sensitivity and Robustness against Outliers

One of the important aspects of posterior distributions in Bayesian analysis is its prior sensitivity

;

)

(

)
.

Definition 5

(Huber’s location shift contamination model
(
Huber, 1992
)
)
.

For the data
D=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}^{n}_{i=1}

\displaystyle X_{i}\sim P_{H}\coloneqq(1-\epsilon_{H})\mathcal{N}(\theta_{0},\sigma^{2})+\epsilon_{H}\mathcal{N}(\theta_{H},\sigma^{2}),

where
\theta_{0}
is the true parameter,
\theta_{H}
(
\theta_{0}\neq\theta_{H}
) is the outlier parameter,
\sigma^{2}>0
is the known variance and

Theorem 2

(Robustness of generalized power posterior)
.

Suppose that the current data
D\sim P_{H}
and the historical data
D_{0}\sim P
, where
P_{H}=(1-\epsilon_{H})\mathcal{N}(\theta_{0},\sigma^{2})+\epsilon_{H}\mathcal{N}(\theta_{H},\sigma^{2})
and
P=\mathcal{N}(\theta_{0},\sigma^{2})
are the contaminated and clean distributions.
Let
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
be reference densities as

\displaystyle p_{0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=L(\theta)\pi_{0}(\theta),\quad p_{1}(\theta)=L(\theta)L_{0}(\theta)\pi_{0}(\theta),

\displaystyle p_{0}^{F}(\theta)

\displaystyle=L^{F}(\theta)\pi_{0}(\theta),\quad p_{1}^{F}(\theta)=L^{F}(\theta)L_{0}(\theta)\pi_{0}(\theta).

where

\displaystyle L(\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod^{n}_{i=1}(1-\epsilon_{H})f_{N}(X_{i};\theta,\sigma^{2})+\epsilon_{H}f_{N}(X_{i};\theta_{H},\sigma^{2}),

\displaystyle L_{0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod^{n_{0}}_{j=1}f_{N}(Y_{j};\theta,\sigma^{2}),

\displaystyle L^{F}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod^{n}_{i=1}f_{N}(X_{i};\theta,\sigma^{2}),

\pi_{0}
is the base prior and
f_{N}(\cdot;\theta,\sigma^{2})
denotes the density of
\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
.
Also, write posterior densities as

\displaystyle g^{*}(\theta;P_{H})

\displaystyle=\frac{\left[(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+\xi p_{1}(\theta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right]^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}}{\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\left[(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta^{\prime})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+\xi p_{1}(\theta^{\prime})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right]^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}d\theta^{\prime}},

\displaystyle g^{*}(\theta;P)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left[(1-\xi)p^{F}_{0}(\theta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+\xi p^{F}_{1}(\theta)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right]^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}}{\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\left[(1-\xi)p^{F}_{0}(\theta^{\prime})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+\xi p^{F}_{1}(\theta^{\prime})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right]^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}d\theta^{\prime}}.

The total variation distance between these two posteriors satisfies

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Bigl(g^{*}(\cdot;P_{H}),\,g^{*}(\cdot;P)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\Bigl|g^{*}(\theta;P_{H})-g^{*}(\theta;P)\Bigr|d\theta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{R_{\max}}{R_{\min}}\Bigr)^{1/z}-1\Bigr],

where

\displaystyle R(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\xi)\,\bigl[p_{0}(\theta)\bigr]^{z}+\xi\,\bigl[p_{1}(\theta)\bigr]^{z}}{(1-\xi)\,\bigl[p_{0}^{F}(\theta)\bigr]^{z}+\xi\,\bigl[p_{1}^{F}(\theta)\bigr]^{z}},

\displaystyle R_{\max}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta}\;R(\theta),\quad R_{\min}=\inf_{\theta}\;R(\theta).

In particular, the bound goes to 0 when
\epsilon_{H}\to 0
or
\Delta_{H}\to 0
, and it exhibits the limiting behavior in
\xi\to 0
or
1
where the dependence on
\alpha
disappears at the endpoints, and in
\epsilon_{H}\to 0
or
\Delta_{H}\to 0
where contamination vanishes.

Corollary 1
.

The total variation distance between two posteriors in Theorem
2
satisfies

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\Bigl(g^{*}(\cdot;P_{H}),\,g^{*}(\cdot;P)\Bigr)\leq\frac{1}{2}\max\left\{R_{\max}^{1/z}-1,\ 1-R_{\min}^{1/z}\right\}.

Corollary 2
.

Under the contamination setting in Theorem
2
and its reverse setting, let

\displaystyle B(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\Biggl[\Bigl(\frac{R_{\max}(\alpha)}{R_{\min}(\alpha)}\Bigr)^{1/z}-1\Biggr],\quad z=\frac{1+\alpha}{2},

where
R(\theta)
(and hence
R_{\max}(\alpha),\,R_{\min}(\alpha)
) is defined in terms of the likelihoods and the mixing parameter
\xi
.
Then the following holds:

(i)

When the current data is contaminated and the historical data is clean, the total–variation bound is an increasing function of
\alpha
.

(ii)

When the current data is clean and the historical data is contaminated, the corresponding total–variation bound is a decreasing function of
\alpha
.

Assumption 1

(Unimodality of base distributions)
.

Each
\theta\mapsto p_{i}(\theta)
is assumed strictly unimodal, with a unique global mode
m_{i}
.
That is, there is exactly one
m_{i}
such that
p^{\prime}_{i}(m_{i})=0
,
p^{\prime\prime}_{i}(m_{i})<0
and
p_{i}(\theta)
is increasing on
(-\infty,m_{i})
and decreasing on
(m_{i},\infty)
.

Assumption 2

(Mode locations)
.

Assume that
m_{0}<m_{1}
.

Assumption 3

(Swapping dominance)
.

Assume that there is at least one point
\theta^{*}\in(m_{0},m_{1})
with
p_{0}(\theta^{*})=p_{1}(\theta^{*})
and
p_{0}(\theta)>p_{1}(\theta)
for
\theta\in(m_{0},\theta^{*})
,
p_{1}(\theta)>p_{0}(\theta)
for
\theta\in(\theta^{*},m_{1})
.
Equivalently, assume that the ratio
p_{1}(\theta)/p_{0}(\theta)
transitions from below
1
to above
1
within
(m_{0},m_{1})
.

For the simplicity of the discussion, this subsection assumes a one-dimensional case, but the results obtained can be generalized to multidimensional cases with the same logic.

Lemma 1
.

For
z\neq 0
and two densities
\theta\mapsto p_{0}(\theta),\theta\mapsto p_{1}(\theta)
, let

\displaystyle L_{z}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{z}\ln\left\{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta)^{z}+\xi p_{1}(\theta)^{z}\right\}.

Then, its derivative is given as

\displaystyle L^{\prime}_{z}(\theta)=\frac{p^{\prime}_{0}(\theta)}{p_{0}(\theta)}+\frac{\xi R(\theta)^{z-1}R^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1-\xi)+\xi R(\theta)^{z}},

where
R(\theta)=p_{1}(\theta)/p_{0}(\theta)
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\theta\mapsto p_{0}(\theta)
and
\theta\mapsto p_{1}(\theta)
be two strictly unimodal, continuous density functions on
\mathbb{R}
with distinct global modes
m_{0}<m_{1}
.
Suppose there is a nonempty interval
(m_{0},m_{1})
on which
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
swap dominance.
For
\alpha\neq-1
and
\xi\in(0,1)
,

i)

for large
\alpha
, generalized power posterior
g
has at least two local maxima, one near
m_{0}
and another near
m_{1}
. In particular,
g
is multimodal.

ii)

for small
\alpha
, generalized power posterior
g
has exactly one global maximum. Hence
g
is unimodal.

5
shows the numerical demonstration of the role of
\alpha
on the shape of the generalized power posterior.
In this illustrative example,
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
are two Gaussian distributions centered at
1
and
-1
, and
\xi=0.5
.
As stated in Theorem
3
, it can be observed that the parameter
\alpha
changes the shape of the posterior distribution.

5.3
Consistency and Higher-order Asymptotic Analysis

Finally, we consider the following consistency and asymptotic variance of the generalized power posterior.

Theorem 4

(Consistency of generalized power posterior)
.

Let the current data
D=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
and
D_{0}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n_{0}}\}
be i.i.d. from a parametric model
P_{\theta}
with true parameter
\theta_{0}
.
Assume that

•

\theta_{0}\in\mathrm{int}(\Theta)
,

•

the likelihoods
L(\theta\mid D)
and
L(\theta\mid D_{0})
are identifiable, strictly positive and continuous in
\theta
, and

•

the baseline prior
\pi_{0}(\theta)
is strictly positive and continuous in a neighborhood of
\theta_{0}
.

Then, the generalized power posterior is consistent for
\theta_{0}
.
That is, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\pi\left(\left\{\theta\in\Theta\ \colon\ \|\theta-\theta_{0}\|>\epsilon\right\}\mid D,D_{0},\xi,\alpha\right)\overset{P_{\theta_{0}}}{\underset{n,n_{0}\to\infty}{\to}}0.

Theorem 5

(Asymptotic variance of generalized power posterior)
.

Suppose suitable regularity conditions hold so that a Laplace‐type expansion is valid around
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\left[I(\theta_{0})+\frac{\xi n_{0}}{n}I_{0}(\theta_{0})\right]^{-1}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Gamma_{\alpha,\xi}+o(1/n^{2}),

where
I(\theta_{0})
and
I(\theta_{0})
are the Fisher‐information‐type matrices based on
D
and
D_{0}
, and
\Gamma_{\alpha,\xi}
is the matrix which depends on
\alpha
and
\xi
.

From the above statement, we can see that the leading term does not depend on
\alpha
, while it appears in the next higher-order term.

5.4
Information Geometry of Generalized Power Posterior

Information geometry is a powerful tool to understand statistical procedure in terms of differential geometry
(
Amari & Nagaoka, 2000
;
Amari, 2016
;
Ay et al., 2017
;
Nielsen, 2020
)
.
The core idea of information geometry is to capture statistical concepts as geometric objects on a Riemannian manifold consisting of a set of probability distributions.
This framework, which combines geometry and statistics, has attracted much attention in recent years for its usefulness, especially in understanding statistical and machine learning procedures and deriving new algorithms
(
Amari, 1995
;
Amari, 1998
;
Ay et al., 2015
;
Hino et al., 2024
;
Kimura, 2021
;
Kimura & Hino, 2021
;
Kimura & Hino, 2022
;
Kimura & Hino, 2024
;
Kimura & Bondell, 2024a
;
Martens, 2020
;
Müller & Zeinhofer, 2023
;
Murata et al., 2004
)
.

Assume that the mapping
\bm{\theta}\mapsto p(X;\bm{\theta})
is one-to-one.
That is, the probability density function is identifiable by its parameters.
Under this assumption, the parameter space
\Theta
can be regarded as a coordinate system of the manifold consisting of a set of probability distributions, and one can call this
\bm{\theta}
-coordinate system.
Furthermore, it is known that the Fisher information matrix
\bm{G}=(g_{ij})
is a metric of this manifold.
Let
\mathcal{M}
denote this manifold and call it a statistical manifold.
Here,
\mathcal{M}
is a Riemannian manifold, and there is no guarantee that the Euclidean distance serves as a proper distance function.
Indeed, a class of divergence functions plays this role.

On Riemannian manifolds
(
Lee, 2018
;
Lang, 1995
)
, geometric objects and procedures such as curves and transport depend on the metric equipped by the manifold.
Specifically, consider the straight line, called a geodesic on the
\bm{\theta}
-coordinate system, connecting two distributions
p(X;\bm{\theta}_{1})
and
p(X;\bm{\theta}_{2})
.

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}(t)=(1-t)\bm{\theta}_{1}+t\bm{\theta}_{2},

(3)

where
t\in[0,1]
.
Assume that
\mathcal{M}
is the manifold of exponential family distributions.

\displaystyle p(X;\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}\theta^{i}h_{i}(X)+k(X)-\psi(\bm{\theta})\right\},

where
h_{i}(X)
are functions of
X
which are linearly independent,
k(X)
is a function of
X
,
\psi
corresponds to the normalization factor.
Then, the probability distributions on the geodesic are

\displaystyle p(X;t)=\{p(X;\bm{\theta}(t))\}=\exp\left\{t(\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1})\cdot X+\bm{\theta_{1}}X-\psi(t)\right\}

Hence, a geodesic itself is a one-dimensional exponential family, where
t
is the natural parameter.
By taking the logarithm,

\displaystyle\ln p(X;t)=(1-t)\ln p(X;\bm{\theta}_{1})+t\ln p(X;\bm{\theta}_{2})-\psi(t).

Therefore, a geodesic in Eq. (
3
) consists of a linear interpolation of the two probability distributions in the logarithmic scale, and it is called an
e
-geodesic, where
e
stands for exponential.
Using this notation, the
\bm{\theta}
-coordinate system is called
e
-coordinate system.
Note that a submanifold which is defined by linear constraints in
\bm{\theta}
is said to be
e
-flat.

One of the most interesting concepts in Riemannian manifolds is coordinate transformation.
As with the geodesics above, local geometric objects are dependent on the coordinate system.
That is, considering that the discussion of statistical procedures is carried forward on Riemannian manifolds, the interpretation may change depending on the choice of coordinate system.
In particular, statistical manifolds are known to have a dual coordinate system corresponding to a certain coordinate system.
Let
\bm{\eta}
be the dual coordinate system of
\bm{\theta}
.
The dual geodesic connecting two distributions specified
\bm{\eta}_{1}
and
\bm{\eta}_{2}
is given as

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}(t)=(1-t)\bm{\eta}_{1}+t\bm{\eta}_{2}.

It is known that this geodesic can be written as

\displaystyle p(X;t)=(1-t)p_{1}(X;\bm{\eta}_{1})+tp_{2}(X;\bm{\eta}),

which is a mixture of two distributions.
This geodesic is called an
m
-geodesic, where
m
stands for the mixture.

Consider more general coordinate systems.
Specifically, in the literature of information geometry, the
\alpha
-coordinate system is known as one of the most important classes of coordinate systems.
The
\alpha
-coordinate system given as the coordinate transformation as
\bm{m}^{(\alpha)}=p(X;\bm{\theta})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}
.
Then, the probability distributions on the
\alpha
-geodesic is given as

\displaystyle p(X;t)=C(t)\left\{(1-t)p(X;\bm{\theta}_{1})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}+tp(X;\bm{\theta}_{2})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\right\},

where
C(t)
is the normalization factor.
In addition, setting
\alpha^{\prime}=-\alpha
yields the dual coordinate system and dual geodesic.
That is, the dual
\alpha
-geodesic is given as

\displaystyle p(X;t)=C(t)\left\{(1-t)p(X;\bm{\theta}_{1})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}+tp(X;\bm{\theta}_{2})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\right\}.

This shows that the generalized power posterior can be identified with the
\alpha
-geodesics on the statistical manifold.
One can see that
\alpha\to\pm 1
yields
e
- and
m
-geodesics, respectively.

Remark 2

(Information geometry of generalized power posterior)
.

The optimal posterior under Amari’s
\alpha
-divergence, which is the generalization of the posterior corresponding to the power prior, can be identified with
\alpha
-geodesics on the statistical manifold
\mathcal{M}
.

On the
\alpha
-coordinate systems, it is known that many geometrical procedures such as
\alpha
-Pythagorean theorem and
\alpha
-projection theorem can be justified.

6
Application

We consider the Bayesian survival analysis for Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) phase III clinical trials, labelled E1684 and E1690.
Let
D=\{t_{i},\delta_{i},g_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
be the current trial data (E1690), where
t_{i}
is the observed time-to-event for subject,
\delta_{i}
is the event indicator (1 if event occurred, 0 if censored), and
g_{i}
indicates the treatment group such that
0 = observation
and
1 = interferon
.
The trial of E1684 is a two-arm clinical trial comparing high dose interferon with an observation arm, and
n_{0}=286
patients were enrolled in the study.
Let
D_{i}=\{t^{\text{hist}}_{j},\delta^{\text{hist}}_{j}\}^{n_{0}}_{j=1}
be the historical data (E1684) assumed here for the observation arm as
g=0
.
The findings from this study indicated that interferon alpha-2b significantly improves relapse-free survival and overall survival, prompting the US Food and Drug Administration to approve this regimen as an adjuvant treatment for patients with high-risk melanoma.
In this context, relapse-free survival refers to the duration from randomization to either tumor progression or death, whichever occurs first, while survival is measured as the time from randomization to death.
The findings of the E1684 trial were published by
Kirkwood et al. (1996)
.
This regimen is commonly utilized as adjuvant therapy for patients with high-risk melanoma and serves as the benchmark for assessing alternative treatments, including vaccines, in ongoing US Cooperative Group trials.
The ECOG trial E1690 was a phase III clinical study with three treatment groups: high-dose interferon, low-dose interferon, and an observation group, and this study had
n=427
patients on the high dose interferon arm and observation arm.
In our analysis, we use only the data from these two arms of the E1690 trial for compatibility with E1684 trial.

For subject
i
in the current trial, we suppose a cure–rate model where the overall survival function is a mixture of a cured fraction and an uncured Weibull survival component.
Let
\pi_{i}
be the cure probability defined as

\displaystyle\pi_{i}=\sigma\left(\gamma_{0}^{\text{current}}+\gamma_{1}^{\text{current}}\cdot g_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left[-(\gamma_{0}^{\text{current}}+\gamma_{1}^{\text{current}}\cdot g_{i})\right]},

where
\gamma_{0}^{\text{current}}
and
\gamma_{1}^{\text{current}}
are regression parameters.
For the uncured subjects with probability
1-\pi_{i}
, the survival time follows a Weibull distribution with shape
\alpha^{\text{current}}
and scale
\beta^{\text{current}}
.
Its probability density function is

\displaystyle f(t_{i}\mid\alpha^{\text{current}},\beta^{\text{current}})=\frac{\alpha^{\text{current}}}{\beta^{\text{current}}}\left(\frac{t_{i}}{\beta^{\text{current}}}\right)^{\alpha^{\text{current}}-1}\exp\left[-\left(\frac{t_{i}}{\beta^{\text{current}}}\right)^{\alpha^{\text{current}}}\right],

and the corresponding survival function is

\displaystyle S(t_{i}\mid\alpha^{\text{current}},\beta^{\text{current}})=\exp\left[-\left(\frac{t_{i}}{\beta^{\text{current}}}\right)^{\alpha^{\text{current}}}\right].

The likelihood contribution is then defined as a mixture:

\displaystyle L_{i}=\left[(1-\pi_{i})f(t_{i}\mid\alpha^{\text{current}},\beta^{\text{current}})\right]^{\delta_{i}}\left[\pi_{i}+(1-\pi_{i})S(t_{i}\mid\alpha^{\text{current}},\beta^{\text{current}})\right]^{1-\delta_{i}}.

For historical subjects (controls only), we use a separate set of parameters.
Let the historical cure probability be defined by

\displaystyle\pi^{\text{hist}}=\sigma\left(\gamma_{0}^{\text{hist}}\right)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left[-\gamma_{0}^{\text{hist}}\right]},

and let the uncured survival be modeled with (potentially different) Weibull parameters
\alpha^{\text{hist}}
and
\beta^{\text{hist}}
.
Then the density and survival functions are defined as

\displaystyle f^{\text{hist}}(t\mid\alpha^{\text{hist}},\beta^{\text{hist}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{\text{hist}}}{\beta^{\text{hist}}}\left(\frac{t}{\beta^{\text{hist}}}\right)^{\alpha^{\text{hist}}-1}\exp\left[-\left(\frac{t}{\beta^{\text{hist}}}\right)^{\alpha^{\text{hist}}}\right],

\displaystyle S^{\text{hist}}(t\mid\alpha^{\text{hist}},\beta^{\text{hist}})

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\left(\frac{t}{\beta^{\text{hist}}}\right)^{\alpha^{\text{hist}}}\right].

Thus, for historical subject
j
, let the likelihood be
L^{\text{hist}}_{j}
and

\displaystyle\ell^{\text{hist}}=\sum_{j}\ln L_{j}^{\text{hist}}.

The full posterior for the model combining current and historical data is then proportional to

\displaystyle\pi(\Theta\mid D,D_{0})\propto\left[\prod_{i}L_{i}\right]\times\left\{(1-\xi)+\xi\exp\left(z\cdot\ell^{\text{hist}}\right)\right\}^{1/z}\times\pi_{0}(\Theta),

where
\Theta
collects all parameters:

\displaystyle\Theta=\{\gamma_{0}^{\text{current}},\gamma_{1}^{\text{current}},\alpha^{\text{current}},\beta^{\text{current}},\gamma_{0}^{\text{hist}},\alpha^{\text{hist}},\beta^{\text{hist}},\xi,\alpha\}.

For a subject with treatment
g
(0 for control, 1 for treatment), the instantaneous hazard at time
t
is computed as

\displaystyle h(t\mid g)=\frac{(1-\pi(g))f(t\mid\alpha^{\text{current}},\beta^{\text{current}})}{\pi(g)+(1-\pi(g))S(t\mid\alpha^{\text{current}},\beta^{\text{current}})}.

The hazard ratio comparing treatment to control at
t_{0}
is

\displaystyle\text{HR}(t_{0})=\frac{h(t_{0}\mid g=1)}{h(t_{0}\mid g=0)}.

We set

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}^{\text{current}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,10\right),\quad\gamma_{1}^{\text{current}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,10\right),

\displaystyle\alpha^{\text{current}}\sim\text{Gamma}(2,1),\quad\beta^{\text{current}}\sim\text{Gamma}(2,1),

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}^{\text{hist}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,10\right),

\displaystyle\alpha^{\text{hist}}\sim\text{Gamma}(2,1),\quad\beta^{\text{hist}}\sim\text{Gamma}(2,1),

\displaystyle\alpha\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\alpha},\sigma_{\alpha}),\quad\xi\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha_{\xi},\beta_{\xi}),

and construct
D
of sample size
n=100
by random sampling from the E1690 trial, and
t_{0}
to be median follow-up time.
We use Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling with 50,000 iterations and 10,000 burn-in steps to estimate posterior distributions of the parameters, with 95% acceptance rate.
Figure
9
is an example of MCMC trace plot of last 5,000 sample with specific parameters.
The convergence of the Gibbs
sampler was checked using several diagnostic procedures as recommended by
Cowles & Carlin (1996)
including
\hat{R}
and ESS.
The computing time required to fit the model is approximately 20 min on an Apple M1 Max processor with 64 GB of RAM.

For the model assessment, we consider the concordance index (C-index) which is a measure of the predictive accuracy of a survival model.
It quantifies how well the risk scores of the model agree with the actual ordering of the observed survival times.
Let
r_{i}
be the risk score predicted by the model defined as

\displaystyle r_{i}=\pi_{i}+(1-\pi_{i})\times\exp\left[-\left(\frac{t_{i}}{\beta^{\text{current}}}\right)^{\alpha^{\text{current}}}\right].

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of all comparable pairs.
For each comparable pair
(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}
, define an indicator function as

\displaystyle C_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $r_{i}>r_{j}$ (correct ordering)},\\
0.5&\text{if $r_{i}=r_{j}$ (tie)},\\
0&\text{if $r_{i}<r_{j}$ (incorrect ordering)}.\end{cases}

The C-index is the proportion of all comparable pairs that are concordant,

\displaystyle\text{C-index}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}}C_{ij}.

\text{C-index}=1
means the perfect prediction, that is, all comparable pairs are correctly ordered,
\text{C-index}=0.5
means no better than random chance, and
\text{C-index}<0.5
means predictions are in reverse order relative to the observed outcomes.

Here, the KM estimator is a non-parametric method used to estimate the survival function from observed survival data, especially in the presence of censored observations.
Given a dataset of survival times

, where
t_{(j)}
represents a distinct observed failure time among the
n

Let

be the number of failures at time

be the number of subjects at risk just prior to time
t_{(j)}
.
Then, the KM estimator of the survival function

is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{S}(t)=\prod_{j\colon t_{(j)}\leq t}\left(1-\frac{d_{j}}{n_{j}}\right),\quad t\geq 0.

(

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\hat{S}(t)\right)=\hat{S}(t)^{2}\sum_{j\colon t_{(j)}\leq t}\frac{d_{j}}{n_{j}(n_{j}-d_{j})}.

This variance estimate allows constructing confidence intervals around the KM estimate.
In this figure, we show a common 95% confidence interval:

\displaystyle\hat{S}(t)=\pm z_{0.975}\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\hat{S}(t)\right)},

where
z_{0.975}\approx 1.96
is the 97.5th percentile of the standard normal distribution.

Conclusion

This study generalizes the posterior distribution corresponding to power prior, which is known to be optimal under KL divergence, under Amari’s alpha-divergence, and investigates its various properties.
The derived posterior distribution was found to depend on the generalization parameters in terms of its shape and sensitivity to the prior distribution.
Moreover, through the lens of information geometry, one can see that this posterior can be identified with the
\alpha
-geodesics on the Riemannian manifold.

Such variants of the Bayesian posterior distribution may lead to improved performance when integrated into appropriate algorithms
(
Erixon et al., 2003
;
Hill, 1968
;
Huang et al., 2023
;
Kimura & Bondell, 2024b
;
Lewis & Raftery, 1997
)
.
For example, one might take advantage of the fact that the properties of the posterior distribution derived in this study are controlled by a generalization parameter and optimize this parameter directly.

There have been several recent derivations of algorithms based on geodesics and projection theorems under the framework of information geometry
(
Akaho, 2004
;
Carter et al., 2011
;
Sharp et al., 2022
)
.
Combining these findings with the findings of this study is expected to lead to the derivation of novel algorithms.

That is,

Then, we impose this constraint using a Lagrange multiplier

Define the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(g,\lambda)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(1-\xi)D_{\mathrm{KL}}[g\|p_{0}]+\xi D_{\mathrm{KL}}[g\|p_{1}]+\lambda\left(\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\int g(\bm{\theta})\ln g(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}-(1-\xi)\int g(\bm{\theta})\ln p_{0}(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\xi\int g(\bm{\theta})\ln p_{1}(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}+\lambda\left(\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}-1\right).

To find the minimum, take the functional derivative of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
g(\bm{\theta})
and set it to zero.
The variations of each term are as follows.

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta g(\bm{\theta})}\left\{\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})\ln g(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}\right\}

\displaystyle=\ln g(\bm{\theta})+1,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta g(\bm{\theta})}\left\{-(1-\xi)\int g(\bm{\theta})\ln p_{0}(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}\right\}

\displaystyle=-(1-\xi)\ln p_{0}(\bm{\theta}),

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta g(\bm{\theta})}\left\{-\xi\int g(\bm{\theta})\ln p_{1}(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\xi\ln p_{1}(\bm{\theta}),

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta g(\bm{\theta})}\left\{\lambda\left(\int_{\Theta}g(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}-1\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\lambda.

Putting these together and set this equal to zero.

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}(g,\lambda)}{\delta g(\bm{\theta})}

\displaystyle=\ln g(\bm{\theta})+1-(1-\xi)\ln p_{0}(\bm{\theta})-\xi\ln p_{1}(\bm{\theta})+\lambda

\displaystyle=(1-\xi)\ln p_{0}(\bm{\theta})+\xi\ln p_{1}(\bm{\theta})-1-\lambda

\displaystyle g(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{(1-\xi)\ln p_{0}(\bm{\theta})+\xi\ln p_{1}(\bm{\theta})-1-\lambda\right\}

Notice that

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}Cp_{0}(\bm{\theta})^{1-\xi}p_{1}(\bm{\theta})^{\xi}d\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle C\int_{\Theta}p_{0}(\bm{\theta})^{1-\xi}p_{1}(\bm{\theta})^{\xi}d\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\int_{\Theta}p_{0}(\bm{\theta})^{1-\xi}p_{1}(\bm{\theta})^{\xi}d\bm{\theta}}.

In addition,

\displaystyle=L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})^{\xi}\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta}).

\displaystyle g(\bm{\theta})=\frac{L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})^{\xi}\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})}{\int_{\Theta}L(\bm{\theta^{\prime}}\mid D)L(\bm{\theta^{\prime}}\mid D_{0})^{\xi}\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta^{\prime}})d\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}.

Proof for Corollary
2
.

By the definition of total variation distance,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Theta}\bigl|\,p(\theta)-q(\theta)\bigr|\,d\theta.

We can rewrite each integrand
|p(\theta)-q(\theta)|
by factoring out
q(\theta)
, since
q(\theta)\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\left|\,p(\theta)-q(\theta)\right|=q(\theta)\,\left|\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\right|.

Hence

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Theta}q(\theta)\,\left|\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\right|d\theta.

We next observe that for any measurable function
g(\theta)
taking real values,

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}q(\theta)\,\bigl|\,g(\theta)\bigr|\;d\theta\leq\Bigl(\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\bigl|g(\theta)\bigr|\Bigr)\times\int_{\Theta}q(\theta)\,d\theta.

But
\int_{\Theta}q(\theta)\,d\theta=1
because
q
is a probability density. Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}q(\theta)\,\bigl|\,g(\theta)\bigr|\;d\theta\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\bigl|\,g(\theta)\bigr|.

In our application,
g(\theta)=\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1
, so

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}q(\theta)\,\left|\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\right|\,d\theta\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\left|\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\right|.

Consequently

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}\int q(\theta)\,\left|\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\right|\;d\theta\leq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\left|\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\right|.

Recall the definitions in the statement of the theorem:

\displaystyle m=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)},\quad M=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}.

Thus for every
\theta
,

\displaystyle m\;\leq\;\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}\;\leq\;M,

and we want to bound

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta}\,\Bigl|\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\Bigr|.

Observe that for any real
x
,

\displaystyle\bigl|\,x-1\bigr|=\begin{cases}x-1,&\text{if }x\geq 1,\\
1-x,&\text{if }x<1.\end{cases}

Hence as
x
ranges over
[\,m,\;M\,]
, the maximum deviation
|x-1|
is

\displaystyle\max\!\bigl\{\;M-1,\;1-m\,\bigr\}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta}\,\left|\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\right|

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\,\sup_{\theta}\,\left[\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\right],\sup_{\theta}\,\left[1-\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\!\bigl\{\,M-1,\;1-m\bigr\}.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta}\;\Bigl(\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\Bigr)=M-1,\quad\sup_{\theta}\;\Bigl(1-\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}\Bigr)=1-m,

so the overall supremum norm
\sup_{\theta}|\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1|
is
\max\{\,M-1,\;1-m\}
.
Putting them together,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)\leq\frac{1}{2}\,\sup_{\theta}\,\Bigl|\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}-1\Bigr|=\frac{1}{2}\,\max\!\bigl\{\,M-1,\;1-m\bigr\}.

That is precisely the statement of the theorem.

z=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
is also large.
Then,
R(\theta)^{z}
has a threshold behavior.
If
R(\theta)<1
, then
R(\theta)^{z}\approx 0
, and if
R(\theta)>1
then
R(\theta)^{z}\approx\infty
.
Hence,
\xi R(\theta)^{z}
is nearly
0
in the region where
p_{1}<p_{0}
, and nearly infinite where
p_{1}>p_{0}
.
Consequently, for near
m_{0}
, we have
p_{0}(m_{0})\gg p_{1}(m_{0})
and
R(m_{0})\ll 1
,
\xi R(m_{0})^{z}\approx 0
as
z\to\infty
.
Therefore, in a neighborhood of
m_{0}
, the bracket
(1-\xi)+\xi R(\theta)^{z}\approx(1-\xi)\neq 0
.
So, let
F(\theta)\coloneqq\{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta)^{z}+\xi p_{1}(\theta)^{z}\}^{1/z}
and

\displaystyle F(\theta)\approx\left\{p(\theta^{z})(1-\xi)\right\}^{1/z}.

The factor
(1-\xi)^{1/z}
is constant with respect to
\theta
.
Hence, near
m_{0}
,
F(\theta)
behaves essentially like
p_{0}(\theta)
and we get a local maximum near
m_{0}
.
Also, near
m_{1}
, we have
p_{1}(m_{1})\gg p_{0}(m_{1})
, so
R(m_{1})\gg 1
, and
R(m_{1})^{z}\to\infty
.
Then,
\xi R(\theta)^{z}
dominates and

\displaystyle F(\theta)

\displaystyle\approx\left(p_{0}(\theta)^{z}\xi R(\theta)^{z}\right)^{1/z}

\displaystyle=\left\{p_{1}(\theta)^{z}\left(\frac{p_{0}(\theta)}{p_{1}(\theta)}\right)^{z}\xi R(\theta)^{z}\right\}^{1/z}

\displaystyle=\left\{p_{1}(\theta)^{z}\xi\left(\frac{1}{R(\theta)}\right)^{z}\right\}^{1/z}

\displaystyle=\xi^{1/z}p_{1}(\theta)\left\{\frac{R(\theta)^{z}}{R(\theta)^{z}}\right\}^{1/z}

\displaystyle=\xi^{1/z}p_{1}(\theta).

This again is just
p_{1}(\theta)
up to a multiplicative constant.
So there is a local maximum near
m_{1}
.

Therefore, for large
\alpha
, there are two distinct neighborhoods, one near
m_{0}
and one near
m_{1}
, in which
F(\theta)
approximates
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
respectively.
Since each
p_{i}
has a strict mode, we conclude
F(\theta)
has at least two local maxima.
In particular, the normalized version
g(\theta)
is multimodal.

When
\alpha
is small, specifically when
\alpha<1
, then
z=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}<1
.
Now the map
t\mapsto t^{z}
is concave on
(0,\infty)
.
As a result, the function
(p_{0}(\theta)^{z}+p_{1}(\theta)^{z})
is more merged than max-like.
Concretely, by Lemma 4.3,

\displaystyle L^{\prime}_{z}(\theta)=\frac{p^{\prime}_{0}(\theta)}{p_{0}(\theta)}+\frac{\xi R(\theta)^{z-1}R^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1-\xi)+\xi R(\theta)^{z}},

and because
z<1
, the exponent
(z-1)
is negative.
So if
p_{1}(\theta)>p_{0}(\theta)
, the ratio
(p_{1}(\theta)/p_{0}(\theta))^{z-1}
is less dramatic.
This ensures only one sign change in
L^{\prime}_{z}(\theta)
.
This yields exavtly one local maximum overall.
Hence, for smaller
\alpha
, we see unimodality, only one local maximum emerges.
∎

Proof for Theorem
4
.

Recall the definitions

\displaystyle p_{0}(\theta)=L(\theta\mid D)\pi_{0}(\theta),\quad p_{1}(\theta)=L(\theta\mid D)L(\theta\mid D_{0})\pi_{0}(\theta).

Also, let
z=(1+\alpha)/2
and

\displaystyle R(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta)^{z}+\xi p_{1}(\theta)^{z},

\displaystyle\pi(\theta\mid D,D_{0},\xi,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{R(\theta)^{1/z}}{\int_{\Theta}R(\theta^{\prime})^{1/z}d\theta^{\prime}}.

Let
C_{0}\coloneqq R(\theta_{0})
, which is strictly positive.
Then,

\displaystyle\frac{R(\theta)}{R(\theta_{0})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta)^{z}+\xi p_{1}(\theta)^{z}}{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta_{0})+\xi p_{1}(\theta_{0})^{z}}=\frac{1}{C_{0}}\left\{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta)^{z}+\xi p_{1}(\theta)^{z}\right\},

\displaystyle\ln\frac{R(\theta)}{R(\theta_{0})}

\displaystyle=-\ln C_{0}+\ln\left\{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta)^{z}+\xi p_{1}(\theta)^{z}\right\}\eqqcolon\Delta_{n}(\theta).

Recall that

\displaystyle p_{0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=L(\theta\mid D)\pi_{0}(\theta)=\left[\prod^{n}_{i=1}f(X_{i};\theta)\right]\times\pi_{0}(\theta),

\displaystyle p_{1}(\theta)

\displaystyle=L(\theta\mid D)L(\theta\mid D_{0})\pi_{0}(\theta)=\left[\prod^{n}_{i=1}f(X_{i};\theta)\right]\times\left[\prod^{n_{0}}_{j=1}f(Y_{j};\theta)\right]\times\pi_{0}(\theta).

Hence,

\displaystyle p_{0}(\theta)^{z}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{z\ln(L(\theta\mid D)\pi_{0}(\theta))\right\}=\exp\left\{z\left[\ln L(\theta\mid D)+\ln\pi_{0}(\theta)\right]\right\},

\displaystyle p_{1}(\theta)^{z}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{z\ln(L(\theta\mid D)L(\theta\mid D_{0})\pi_{0}(\theta))\right\}.

Let

\displaystyle A_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\ln\left(\frac{L(\theta\mid D)\pi_{0}(\theta)}{L(\theta_{0}\mid D)\pi_{0}(\theta_{0})}\right)=\ln\frac{\pi_{0}(\theta)}{\pi_{0}(\theta_{0})}+\sum^{n}_{i=1}\ln\frac{f(X_{i};\theta)}{f(X_{i};\theta_{0})},

\displaystyle B_{n_{0}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\ln\left(\frac{L(\theta\mid D_{0})}{L(\theta_{0}\mid D_{0})}\right)=\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}\ln\frac{f(Y_{j};\theta)}{f(Y_{j};\theta_{0})}.

Then,

\displaystyle p_{0}(\theta)^{z}

\displaystyle=p_{0}(\theta_{0})^{z}\exp\left\{zA_{n}(\theta)\right\},

\displaystyle p_{1}(\theta)^{z}

\displaystyle=p_{1}(\theta_{0})^{z}\exp\left\{zA_{n}(\theta)+zB_{n_{0}}(\theta)\right\},

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\ln C_{0}+\ln\left\{a_{0}e^{zA_{n}(\theta)}+b_{0}e^{z\left[A_{n}(\theta)+B_{n_{0}}(\theta)\right]}\right\},

where
a_{0}=(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta)^{z}
and
b_{0}=\xi p_{1}(\theta)^{z}
, and hence
C_{0}=a_{0}+b_{0}
.
For
A_{n}(\theta)
, by the Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\ln\frac{f(X_{i};\theta)}{f(X_{i};\theta_{0})}\overset{a.s.}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\theta_{0}\|\theta]<0,

whenever
\theta\neq\theta_{0}
.
The additional term
\ln(\pi_{0}(\theta)/\pi_{0}(\theta_{0}))
is constant in
n
and hence negligible at order
n
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle A_{n}(\theta)=O_{\theta_{0}}(n),\quad\frac{1}{n}A_{n}(\theta)\overset{a.s.}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\theta_{0}\|\theta].

For
B_{n_{0}}(\theta)
, if
n_{0}\to\infty
similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}\ln\frac{f(Y_{j};\theta)}{f(Y_{j};\theta_{0})}\overset{a.s.}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\theta_{0}\|\theta]<0.

Thus, for every
\theta\neq\theta_{0}
, with probability going to
1
as
n\to\infty
and
n_{0}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle zA_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle\approx znD_{\mathrm{KL}}[\theta_{0}\|\theta]\to-\infty,

\displaystyle zB_{n_{0}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\approx zn_{0}D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\theta_{0}\|\theta]\to-\infty.

Hence the exponential terms
\exp\{zA_{n}(\theta)\}
and
\exp\{z[A_{n}(\theta)+B_{n_{0}}(\theta)]\}
both goes to
0
as
n,n_{0}\to\infty
, whenever
\theta\neq\theta_{0}
.

Given that
a_{0},b_{0}>0
are constants and the exponential terms vanish exponentially fast for each fixed
\theta\neq\theta_{0}
, it follows that

\displaystyle(1-\xi)p_{o}(\theta)^{z}+\xi p_{1}(\theta)^{z}=a_{0}e^{zA_{n}(\theta)}+b_{0}e^{z[A_{n}(\theta)+B_{n_{0}}(\theta)]}\overset{a.s.}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}}0,

for each fixed
\theta\neq\theta_{0}
.
Fix
\epsilon>0
.
Consider the posterior mass of the set
\{\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|>\epsilon\}
,

\displaystyle\pi\left(\{\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|>\epsilon\}\mid D,D_{0},\xi,\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|>\epsilon}R(\theta)^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}}{\int_{\Theta}R(\theta)^{\prime\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}d\theta^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{R(\theta_{0})^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}\int_{\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|>\epsilon}\left(\frac{R(\theta)}{R(\theta_{0})}\right)^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}}{R(\theta_{0})^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}\int_{\Theta}\left(\frac{R(\theta^{\prime})}{R(\theta_{0})}\right)^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|>\epsilon}\left(\frac{R(\theta)}{R(\theta_{0})}\right)^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}}{\int_{\Theta}\left(\frac{R(\theta^{\prime})}{R(\theta_{0})}\right)^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|>\epsilon}\left(\frac{R(\theta)}{R(\theta_{0})}\right)^{\frac{2}{1+\alpha}}\overset{P_{\theta_{0}}}{\underset{n,n_{0}\to\infty}{\to}}0.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Example 4

(Univeriate Gaussian)
.

Let
D=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
,
X_{i}\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
and
D_{0}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\}
,
Y_{j}\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
be the current and historical data.
Also
\pi_{0}(\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\tau_{0}^{2})
be the baseline prior.
Then, for
\alpha\neq\pm 1
and
\xi\in[0,1]
, the generalized power posterior
\theta\mapsto g(\theta)
is given as

\displaystyle g(\theta)\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\right)\theta+\left(\frac{S_{X}}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\right)\theta\right\}\left\{1+C\exp\left(\frac{zS_{Y}}{\sigma^{2}}\theta-\frac{zn_{0}}{2\sigma^{2}}\theta^{2}\right)\right\}^{1/z}.

where
S_{X}=\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}
,
S_{Y}=\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}
, and

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{1}}{Q_{0}}\exp\left[-\frac{z}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}^{2}\right],

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=(1-\xi)\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{nz}{2}}}\frac{1}{(2\pi\tau_{0}^{2})^{\frac{z}{2}}},

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=\xi\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{(n+n_{0})z}{2}}}\frac{1}{(2\pi\tau_{0}^{2})^{\frac{z}{2}}}.

The normalization constant is determined by integrating with respect to
\theta
.

Proof.

First, we have

\displaystyle L(\theta\mid D)

\displaystyle=\prod^{n}_{i=1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle L(\theta\mid D_{0})

\displaystyle=\prod^{n_{0}}_{j=1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(Y_{j}-\theta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau_{0}^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta-\mu_{0})}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}\right).

Then,

\displaystyle p_{0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=L(\theta\mid D)\pi_{0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left\{\prod^{n}_{i=1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(X_{i}-\theta)}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right\}\times\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta-\mu_{0})^{2}}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}\right\}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau_{0}^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(\theta-\mu_{0})^{2}}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}\right\},

\displaystyle p_{0}(\theta)^{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{nz}{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{z}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}\right\}\times\frac{1}{(2\pi\tau_{0}^{2})^{\frac{z}{2}}}\exp\left\{-z\frac{(\theta-\mu_{0})^{2}}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}\right\},

\displaystyle p_{1}(\theta)

\displaystyle=L(\theta\mid D)L(\theta\mid D_{0})\pi_{0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\left\{\prod^{n}_{i=1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right\}\times\left\{\prod^{n_{0}}_{j=1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(Y_{j}-\theta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta-Y_{j})^{2}}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{n+n_{0}}{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}(Y_{j}-\theta)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta-Y_{j})^{2}}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle p_{1}(\theta)^{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{(n+n_{0})z}{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{z}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}-\frac{z}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}(Y_{j}-\theta)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\frac{1}{(2\pi\tau_{0}^{2})^{\frac{z}{2}}}\exp\left(-z\frac{(\theta-Y_{j})^{2}}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}\right).

Hence, we can write

\displaystyle p_{0}(\theta)^{z}

\displaystyle=\tilde{Q}_{0}\exp\left(-zA_{0}(\theta)\right),

\displaystyle p_{1}(\theta)^{z}

\displaystyle=\tilde{Q}_{1}\exp\left(-zA_{1}(\theta)\right),

where

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{nz}{2}}(2\pi\tau_{0}^{2})^{\frac{z}{2}}},

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{(n+n_{0})z}{2}}(2\pi\tau_{0}^{2})^{\frac{z}{2}}},

\displaystyle A_{0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}+\frac{(\theta-\mu_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}},

\displaystyle A_{1}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}(Y_{j}-\theta)+\frac{(\theta-\mu_{0})^{2}}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}.

Then,

\displaystyle(1-\xi)p_{0}(\theta)^{z}+\xi p_{1}(\theta)^{z}

\displaystyle=(1-\xi)\tilde{Q}_{0}\exp(-zA_{0}(\theta))+\xi\tilde{Q}_{1}\exp(-zA_{1}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\exp(-zA_{0}(\theta))\left\{(1-\xi)\tilde{Q}_{0}+\xi\tilde{Q}_{1}\exp(-z(A_{1}(\theta)-A_{0}(\theta)))\right\},

\displaystyle g(\theta)

\displaystyle\propto\exp(-A_{0}\theta)\left\{(1-\xi)\tilde{Q}_{0}+\xi\tilde{Q}_{1}\exp(-z(A_{1}(\theta)-A_{0}(\theta)))\right\}^{\frac{1}{z}}.

Since

\displaystyle A_{1}(\theta)-A_{0}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}(Y_{j}-\theta)^{2},

we have

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-z(A_{1}(\theta)-A_{0}(\theta))\right\}=\exp\left\{-z\cdot\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}(Y_{j}-\theta)^{2}\right\}.

Define

\displaystyle S_{X}

\displaystyle=\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i},

\displaystyle S_{Y}

\displaystyle=\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}.

Then, we expand each squared term as

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i=1}(X_{i}-\theta)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}^{2}-2\theta S_{X}+n\theta^{2},

\displaystyle(\theta-\mu_{0})^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}-2\mu_{0}\theta+\mu_{0}^{2},

\displaystyle\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}(Y_{j}-\theta)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}^{2}-2\theta S_{Y}+n_{0}\theta^{2}.

Since factors that do not involve
\theta
can be absorbed into the proportionality constant, we drop all terms independed of
\theta
.
Thus, the parts depending on
\theta
become

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\frac{\theta S_{X}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{n\theta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{0}\theta}{\tau_{0}^{2}}-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}\right\}=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\right)\theta^{2}+\left(\frac{S_{X}}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\right)\theta\right\},

and

\displaystyle Q_{0}+Q_{1}\exp\left[-\frac{z}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}^{2}-2\theta S_{Y}+n_{0}\theta^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad=Q_{0}+Q_{1}\exp\left[-\frac{z}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}\right]\exp\left\{\frac{z\theta S_{Y}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{zn_{0}\theta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\propto\left\{1+C\exp\left(\frac{z\theta S_{Y}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{zn_{0}\theta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right\}^{1/z},

where

\displaystyle C=\frac{Q_{1}}{Q_{0}}\exp\left[-\frac{z}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}^{2}\right].

Putting these together, we obtain

\displaystyle g(\theta)\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\right)\theta+\left(\frac{S_{X}}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{0}}{\tau_{0}^{2}}\right)\theta\right\}\left\{1+C\exp\left(\frac{zS_{Y}}{\sigma^{2}}\theta-\frac{zn_{0}}{2\sigma^{2}}\theta^{2}\right)\right\}^{1/z}.

∎

Example 5

(Beta–Bernoulli)
.

Let
D=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
with
X_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and
D_{0}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n_{0}}\}
with
Y_{j}\in\{0,1\}
be the current and historical data from Bernoulli distributions with parameter
\theta\in(0,1)
.
Also, suppose that the baseline prior is
\pi_{0}(\theta)=\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
for
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}>0
.
Then, the corresponding generalized power posterior is given as

\displaystyle g(\theta)\propto\left\{F(\theta)\right\}^{\frac{1}{z}},

where
z=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}

\displaystyle F(\theta)

\displaystyle=(1-\xi)\left\{\theta^{\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}+\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}+\beta_{0}-1}\right\}^{z}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\xi\left\{\theta^{\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}+\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}+\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\theta)^{n+n_{0}-\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}-\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}+\beta_{0}-1}\right\}^{z}

This is generally not Beta anymore unless
\alpha=1
or
\xi=0,1
.

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle L(\theta\mid D)

\displaystyle=\theta^{\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}},

\displaystyle L(\theta\mid D_{0})

\displaystyle=\theta^{\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}}(1-\theta)^{n_{0}\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}},

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}),

simple calculation yields

\displaystyle p_{0}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\theta^{\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}}\theta^{\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta_{0}-1}

\displaystyle=\theta^{\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}+\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}+\beta_{0}-1},

\displaystyle p_{1}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\theta^{\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}}\theta^{\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}}(1-\theta)^{n_{0}-\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}}\theta^{\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta_{0}-1}

\displaystyle=\theta^{\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}+\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}+\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\theta)^{n+n_{0}-\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}-\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}+\beta_{0}-1}.

Let

\displaystyle S_{X}=\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i},\quad S_{Y}=\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}.

Then,

\displaystyle F(\theta)

\displaystyle=(1-\xi)\left\{\theta^{S_{X}+\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\theta)^{n-S_{X}+\beta_{0}-1}\right\}^{z}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\xi\left\{\theta^{S_{X}+S_{Y}+\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\theta)^{n+n_{0}-S_{X}-S_{Y}+\beta_{0}-1}\right\}^{z}

Notice that the
\theta
-dependent part in the first term is

\displaystyle\theta^{S_{X}+\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\theta)^{n-S_{X}+\beta_{0}-1}.

Since this is common in both terms, we factor it out as

\displaystyle F(\theta)

\displaystyle=\theta^{z(S_{X}+\alpha_{0}-1)}(1-\theta)^{z(n-S_{X}+\beta_{0}-1)}\times\left\{(1-\xi)+\xi\theta^{zS_{Y}}(1-\theta)^{z(n_{0}-S_{Y})}\right\}.

Then,

\displaystyle g(\theta)

\displaystyle\propto\left\{F(\theta)\right\}^{1/z}

\displaystyle=\theta^{S_{X}+\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\theta)^{n-S_{X}+\beta_{0}-1}\times\left\{(1-\xi)+\xi\theta^{zS_{Y}}(1-\theta)^{z(n_{0}-S_{Y})}\right\}^{1/z}.

∎

Example 6

(Dirichlet–Multinomial)
.

Let
D=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
with
\sum^{n}_{i=1}X_{i}=n
and
D_{0}=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n_{0}}\}
with
\sum^{n_{0}}_{j=1}Y_{j}=n_{0}
from the multinomial distribution having the parameter
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{k})
.
Also, suppose that the baseline prior is
\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})=\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha_{0,1},\alpha_{0,2},\dots,\alpha_{0,k})
.
Then, the corresponding generalized power posterior is given as

\displaystyle g(\bm{\theta})\propto\left\{(1-\xi)\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta_{i}^{z(X_{i}+\alpha_{0,i}-1)}+\xi\prod^{k}_{j=1}\theta_{i}^{z(X_{i}+Y_{j}+\alpha_{0,j}-1)}\right\}^{\frac{1}{z}},

where
z=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
.
This does not yield a simple Dirichlet form, unless
\alpha=1
, that is a non-Dirichlet shape on the simplex.

Proof.

The multinomial likelihood for
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{k})
and the Dirichlet prior are given as

\displaystyle L(\bm{\theta}\mid D)

\displaystyle=n!\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta_{i}^{X_{i}},

\displaystyle L(\bm{\theta}\mid D_{0})

\displaystyle=n_{0}!\prod^{k}_{j=1}\theta_{j}^{Y_{j}},

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\propto\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta^{\alpha_{0,i}-1}.

Then,

\displaystyle p_{0}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\propto\left(\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta_{i}^{X_{i}}\right)\left(\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta^{\alpha_{0,i}-1}\right)=\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta_{i}^{X_{i}+\alpha_{0,i}-1},

\displaystyle p_{1}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\propto\left(\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta_{i}^{X_{i}}\right)\left(\prod^{k}_{j=1}\theta_{j}^{Y_{j}}\right)\left(\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta^{\alpha_{0,i}-1}\right)=\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta_{i}^{X_{i}+Y_{i}+\alpha_{0,i}-1}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle g(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\propto\left\{(1-\xi)p_{0}(\bm{\theta})+\xi p_{1}(\bm{\theta})\right\}^{\frac{1}{z}}

\displaystyle=\left\{(1-\xi)\left(\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta_{i}^{X_{i}+\alpha_{0,i}-1}\right)^{z}+\xi\left(\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta_{i}^{X_{i}+Y_{i}+\alpha_{0,i}-1}\right)^{z}\right\}^{\frac{1}{z}}

\displaystyle=\left\{(1-\xi)\prod^{k}_{i=1}\theta_{i}^{z(X_{i}+\alpha_{0,i}-1)}+\xi\prod^{k}_{j=1}\theta_{i}^{z(X_{i}+Y_{j}+\alpha_{0,j}-1)}\right\}^{\frac{1}{z}}.

∎
