Structural and mechanistic insights into the divergence of pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase
Authors: Nivetha Shanmuganathan, Sasireka Manikandan, Jerimon Johnson, Sabaridaran Balasubramani, Angayarkanni Jayaraman, Sruthi Paleri, Theertha Ranjith, Thasleema Parveen A
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractPterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase are key members of the amidohydrolase superfamily and play essential roles in pteridine metabolism across a wide range of biological systems. Pterin deaminase catalyzes the deamination of pterins, contributing to the regulation of purine and pyrimidine metabolism, whereas sepiapterin deaminase is specifically involved in the degradation of sepiapterin, a critical intermediate in the tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) biosynthetic pathway. These enzymes are distributed throughout prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms and display diverse substrate specificity, metal dependency, and enzymatic stability. Pterin deaminase has been identified in bacterial and fungal species, as well as in mammalian liver tissues, where it plays a role in folate metabolism and cellular signalling. In contrast, sepiapterin deaminase is predominantly found in vertebrate tissues with a high BH4 demand, particularly in the neurological and vascular systems. Structural analyses reveal that both enzymes share the conserved metal-binding characteristics of the amidohydrolase superfamily, with pterin deaminase being a zinc-dependent metalloenzyme. Despite sharing the (β/α)₈-barrel fold and metal-binding catalytic core, subtle variations in active-site residues, such as the substitution of histidine with cysteine, determines their catalytic behavior, metal preference, and redox sensitivity. Comparative kinetic and structural analyses revealed that pterin deaminase displayed broader catalytic flexibility and higher metal tolerance, whereas sepiapterin deaminase maintained strict substrate selectivity and redox regulation. These adaptive differences reflect an evolutionary transition from general pterin degradation to specialized cofactor maintenance and pigment biosynthesis. Beyond their biochemical distinctiveness, both enzymes exhibit emerging biomedical and biotechnological significance. Pterin deaminase in folate catabolism and potential antitumor applications and sepiapterin deaminase in neurotransmitter regulation and pigment-associated disorders. Introduction

Amidohydrolases a metal-dependent enzyme superfamily, catalyzes the hydrolysis of diverse amide and ester-containing substrates at carbon and phosphorus centers. The superfamily was first delineated by Holm and Sander (1997) based on three-dimensional structural similarities among urease, phosphotriesterase, and adenosine deaminase. A defining feature of these enzymes is their contorted (β/α)₈-barrel structural fold with a divalent metal cation in the active site that facilitates water activation for nucleophilic attack (Seibert and Raushel 2005). Approximately 25 of 44 amidohydrolase superfamily function as deaminases, highlighting their central role in nucleic acid, cofactor, and secondary metabolite metabolism (Pospisilova and Frebort 2007).

Within this superfamily, Pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase are two different but mechanistically related enzymes that function on pterin-based substrates. These enzymes exert a major influence on the larger pteridine–folate–tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) metabolic framework. In the interconnected BH₄ biosynthetic and salvage network formed by deaminases like GTP cyclohydrolase I, 6-Pyruvoyl-tetrahydropterin synthase (PTPS), Sepiapterin reductase (SR), Dihydropteridine reductase (DHPR), and xanthine dehydrogenase, sepiapterin deaminase mediates the interconversion of sepiapterin, dihydrobiopterin, and biopterin in conjunction with SPR, DHPR, carbonyl reductase, and dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) (Pospisilova and Frebort 2007). Sepiapterin deaminase uses these pathways to connect the production and recycling of pterin cofactors necessary for redox and neurotransmitter metabolism to the catalytic chemistry of the amidohydrolase family.

Pteridines are a diverse class of heterocyclic compounds ubiquitous across living systems, functioning as cofactors, metabolic intermediates, and in certain organisms, pigments (Whitaker and Rabinowitz 1969; Ziegler et al. 2000). Derivatives of the pteridine scaffold, including folates, tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄), and pterin-related cofactors involved in flavin biosynthesis, are indispensable for enzymatic redox reactions and the synthesis of key biomolecules (Bacher and Rappold 1980; Rappold and Bacher 1974). Among these, BH₄ serves as an essential cofactor for aromatic amino acid hydroxylases, thereby supporting phenylalanine metabolism and the biosynthesis of neurotransmitters such as dopamine and serotonin (Eichwald et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2020). Consequently, disturbances in pteridine metabolism have been implicated in neurological dysfunction, pigmentation abnormalities, and a range of inherited and acquired metabolic disorders (Delbreil et al. 2024; Meng et al. 2009).

Recent biochemical and structural investigations have revitalized interest in both pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase for their dual metabolic and therapeutic potential. Pterin deaminase from Variovorax sp. F1 was shown to mediate folic acid degradation via deaminative pathways, elucidating its role in vitamin B₉ turnover (You et al. 2022). Similarly, a structurally uncharacterized amidohydrolase from Agrobacterium radiobacter (Arad3529) was modeled to deaminate a spectrum of pterin derivatives, including sepiapterin, suggesting broader substrate specificity and previously unrecognized catabolic pathways (Hermann et al., 2013). Moreover, microbial pterin deaminase from A. tumefaciens LBA4404 exhibited antioxidant and anticancer properties, underscoring the therapeutic implications of these enzymes (Dhanapal et al. 2023).Genetic studies also identified multiple allelic variants in BH₄-related enzymes linked to metabolic and cardiovascular disorders.

Together, pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase play important but different roles in the pteridine metabolic network. Pterin deaminase mainly targets oxidized pterins to help keep pteridine turnover steady in both bacteria and eukaryotes (Jayaraman et al. 2016; Dhanapal et al. 2023). In contrast, Sepiapterin deaminase operates within the BH₄ salvage pathway (Tsusue and Akino 1965). This review explores pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase within the pteridine–folate–tetrahydrobiopterin metabolic framework. It first outlines their placement within the amidohydrolase superfamily and then compares their biochemical properties, substrate specificities, and roles in pterin turnover and BH₄ salvage pathways. Structural insights, physiological relevance, evolutionary divergence, and emerging biomedical significance are subsequently discussed, highlighting key gaps in current knowledge.

Structural chemistry of pterin and sepiapterin

Pterin is a heterocyclic compound composed of a pyrazine ring fused to a pyrimidine moiety, forming the characteristic 2-amino-4-hydroxypteridine skeleton (Rembold and Hayaishi, 1959; Dhanapal et al. 2023). The conjugated ring system of the compound contributes to its fluorescent properties and its ability to undergo redox and substitution reactions, making it a key structural core for a variety of biologically active derivatives. Pterin derivatives occur in oxidized, reduced, and substituted forms, depending on cellular redox status and enzyme-mediated transformations (You et al. 2022) (Fig. 1).

In contrast, Sepiapterin was identified as a reduced derivative of pterin. It has the molecular structure 2-amino-4-hydroxy-6-(1,2-dihydroxy propyl) pteridine (Tsusue and Akino 1965; Matsuura et al., 1978).The substitution of a dihydroxy propyl side chain at the C-6 position distinguished sepiapterin from the parent pterin molecule. This structural modification enhanced its polarity and facilitates its subsequent enzymatic conversion into dihydrobiopterin (BH₂) and finally into tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) through reductive processes. These transformations connected sepiapterin to the BH₄ salvage pathway, as reported in earlier biochemical studies (Tsusue 1967, 1971) (Fig. 1).

Pterin is the foundational chemical motif from which sepiapterin is derived. Sepiapterin is distinguished from the basic pterin structure by a lactoyl side chain (2—hydroxy-1-oxo-propyl group) at the C6 position of dihydropteridine ring.

In summary, the structural difference between pterin and sepiapterin showed how small changes in the pteridine ring system can change biological function. While pterin with no substitution acted as the basic core for various pteridine derivatives, the specific substitution at the C-6 position in sepiapterin turned it into a metabolically active intermediate connected to the BH₄ salvage pathway. Substitution in C6 position directly influences the interaction pattern of pterin with enzymes. The specific chemical nature and stereochemistry determine the affinity and efficacy of binding to specific enzymes. This structural difference highlighted the chemical basis of their unique physiological roles.

Enzyme activity of pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase

Pterin deaminase is an amidohydrolase enzyme assigned to EC number of 3.5.4.11 which catalyzes the hydrolytic deamination of the amino group at the C-2 position of pterin derivatives, converting them into their corresponding hydroxylated or oxo forms (Rembold and Hayaishi, 1959; Wurster et al. 1981). This reaction represents a key step in the catabolic arm of pteridine metabolism, contributing to the degradation and recycling of pterin compounds. The enzyme primarily acts on 6-hydroxypterin and its derivatives, producing 6,7-dihydroxylumazine as an end product (Ziegler et al. 2000; You et al. 2022) (Fig. 2).

Pterin deaminases belong to the amidohydrolase superfamily and adopt a distorted (β/α)₈-barrel fold with a monomolecular metal ion (Zn2⁺ or Fe2⁺) at the active site catalytic center. This metal ion binds and activates a water molecule, enabling it to perform nucleophilic attack on the pterin substrate (Seibert and Raushel 2005). Divalent‑metal ion coordination is critical for pterin deaminases because the metal polarizes the active‑site water, stabilizes the transition state, and enables nucleophilic attack that releases ammonia from the pterin substrate (Hall et al. 2010). Pterin deaminase activity has been reported in bacterial species such as Alcaligenes faecalis where it participates in pterin catabolism. The resulting lumazine derivatives, including 6,7-dihydroxylumazine, are fluorescent compounds that may contribute to photoprotection and serve as intermediates in riboflavin and other secondary metabolic pathways. (You et al. 2022; Dhanapal et al. 2023).

Sepiapterin deaminase is a member of the amidohydrolase-linked oxidoreductase family, sharing catalytic residues with pterin deaminase but exhibiting higher substrate specificity due to the presence of a dihydroxy propyl side chain in sepiapterin (Fig. 3). Its activity has been demonstrated in insect eye tissues and vertebrate neural systems, where it participates in the recycling of BH₄ a critical cofactor for aromatic amino acid hydroxylases involved in neurotransmitter biosynthesis (Matsuura et al., 1978).This reaction has historically been classified as a hydrolytic deamination of sepiapterin EC number 3.5.4.24, although the molecular and structural characterization of sepiapterin deaminase remains limited compared with that of pterin deaminase. Sepiapterin deaminase catalyzes the hydrolytic deamination of sepiapterin at the C-2 position, resulting in the formation of a 2-oxo-sepiapterin derivative with the release of ammonia (Fig. 3). In contrast to bacterial pterin deaminase, sepiapterin deaminase exhibits a narrower substrate range and operates under reductive cellular environments, linking its function directly to redox-dependent metabolic regulation. The enzyme contributes to maintaining neurotransmitter balance and nitric oxide synthesis, as BH₄ availability is directly regulated through this pathway (Tsusue 1971; Eichwald et al. 2023).

Sepiapterin deaminase catalyzes the hydrolytic deamination of sepiapterin at the C-2 position, resulting in the formation of a 2-oxo-sepiapterin derivative with the release of ammonia

Sepiapterin deaminase functions within the tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) salvage pathway, catalyzing the deamination of sepiapterin into biopterin or dihydrobiopterin intermediates (Tsusue and Akino 1965; Tsusue 1971). Unlike pterin deaminase, which primarily acts on oxidized pterins, sepiapterin deaminase operates on reduced or substituted pterins, specifically targeting 2-amino-4-hydroxy-6-(1,2-dihydroxypropyl) pteridine (Matsuura et al., 1978). Its activity is closely associated with a network of redox enzymes including sepiapterin reductase (SR), dihydropteridine reductase (DHPR), and dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), which collectively mediate the interconversion between sepiapterin, dihydrobiopterin (BH₂), and tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) (Tsusue 1971; Hall et al. 2010). Sepiapterin reductase (SR) using NAPDH catalyzes the final reduction steps to generate BH4. Both pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase belong to the amidohydrolase superfamily, sharing a conserved metal-dependent catalytic framework yet diverging in substrate preference and physiological roles. Pterin deaminase primarily acts on oxidized pterins, participating in catabolic and pigment-forming pathways of prokaryotes, whereas sepiapterin deaminase targets reduced pterins such as sepiapterin within the tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) salvage cycle of eukaryotes. The broader catalytic flexibility of pterin deaminase contrasted with the higher substrate specificity and redox-linked regulation of sepiapterin deaminase, reflects their evolutionary adaptations to distinct metabolic contexts.

Occurrence of pterin and sepiapterin deaminases in prokayotes and eukaryotes sysytems

In prokaryotes

Pterin and sepiapterin deaminases show different patterns of evolution and physiological presence among prokaryotic organisms. Pterin deaminase is commonly found in various bacterial genera, including Alcaligenes, Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Micrococcus, while sepiapterin deaminase is less frequently encountered, indicating its specific function in redox adaptation and protection against oxidative stress and neuronal regulation (Levenberg and Hayaishi 1959).

The first prokaryotic pterin deaminase activity was reported in Alcaligenes metalcaligenes (Rembold and Hayaishi 1959), followed by A. faecalis (McNutt 1963), which produced xanthopterin deaminase acting on isoxanthopterin. Recent findings confirmed that A. faecalis deaminated and oxidized 6-hydroxypterin (6-HP) and 7-hydroxypterin (7-HP) to form 6,7-dihydroxylumazine (Yuki Doi et al. 2025), demonstrating the metabolic flexibility of pterin deaminase enzymes. Additional reports identified pterin deaminase in Pseudomonas fluorescens, Bacillus megaterium (Takikawa et al., 1979), B. subtilis (Tsusue et al., 1978) and Agrobacterium tumefaciens, where the enzyme supported pterin turnover and lumazine pigment production (Whitakker and Rabinowitz 1969; Fan et al. 2013a, b). Sabarathinam et al. (2011) identified pterin deaminase in Micrococcus luteus and Whitakker and Rabinowitz (1969) identified pterin deaminase in Clostridium acidi-urici. Subsequently, Pseudomonas sp. F01 and F08 (Rappold and Bacher 1974) and Pseudomonas fluorescens UK1 (Soini and Backman 1975; Pan and Wurster 1978) have also been shown to produce this enzyme.

In contrast, sepiapterin deaminase activity is less commonly observed in bacteria. Although most prokaryotes lacked the complete tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) biosynthetic and salvage pathways, sepiapterin deaminase like enzymes are detected in selective Gram-positive species (Bacillus subtilis, Staphylococcus aureus) and Gram-negative bacteria (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli), as well as in archaea such as Methanocaldococcus jannaschii (Choi et al. 2013; Jayaraman et al. 2016). These enzymes hydrolytically deaminate sepiapterin into sepialumazine, contributing to redox regulation under oxidative stress. Unlike pterin deaminase, which performs a general metabolic role in eukaryotes, sepiapterin deaminase like enzymes in prokaryotes appear stress-inducible, with expression modulated by oxidative response factors such as SoxRS and fumarate-nitrate reductase (FNR), an adaptive role in redox regulation and biofilm formation (Hitchcock 2014).

Bioinformatic surveys of bacterial genomes indicate that pterin deaminase–like genes are considerably more prevalent than sepiapterin deaminase–like sequences, reflecting the broader metabolic distribution of pterin deaminase in prokaryotes. This distributional asymmetry implies that sepiapterin deaminase likely evolved from a pterin deaminase like progenitor through selective adaptation to redox-regulated environments.

Thus, pterin deaminase represents a conserved catabolic enzyme in diverse bacterial systems facilitating pterin degradation and pigment formation, whereas sepiapterin deaminase emerges sporadically as an adaptive enzyme supporting oxidative stress tolerance and redox homeostasis in specific prokaryotic taxa.

In eukaryotes

In eukaryotic systems, both pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase are associated with highly specialized physiological roles, reflecting the greater biochemical complexity of pteridine metabolism.

Pterin deaminase activity was first reported in Apis mellifera (honey bee) (Rembold and Buschmann 1963) and later detected in the liver tissues of rats (Rembold and Simmersbach 1969; Sabarathinam et al. 2011). The enzyme was also reported in silkworms (Bombyx mori) (Tsusue M et.al 1977) and in slime molds such as Polysphondylium violaceum (Wurster et al. 1976) Dictyostelium discoideum (Wurster et al. 1981) and Dictyostelium lacteum (Van Haastert and Konijn, 1982), indicating its evolutionary conservation across metazoans and protists. In butterflies such as Pieris rapae and Colias erate, pterin deaminase mediated deamination of pterins contributes to the formation of yellow and white wing pigments, which play roles in visual signaling and mate attraction (Yuki Doi et al., 2025; Ziegler et al. 2000). Additionally, a U.S. patent (No.3,930,955) reported that fungal genera such as Aspergillus, Mucor, Rhizopus, and Penicillium also produced this enzyme (Kusakabe et al. 1976). In mammals, the enzyme was associated with folate and pterin catabolism, suggesting a conserved role in one-carbon and redox metabolism (Ziegler et al. 1983). And the enzyme appears more prevalent due to its role in maintaining redox stability and conserving tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) pools under relatively reducing cellular conditions.

Conversely, sepiapterin deaminase activity was localized more specifically in eukaryotic tissues directly involved in the tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) cycle. In insects, sepiapterin accumulates in the crystalline cone and pigment granules of compound eyes, where sepiapterin deaminase catalyzed its deamination to dihydrobiopterin (BH₂) and subsequently to BH₄ (Tsusue 1971; Matsuura et al. 1978). In mammals, sepiapterin and sepiapterin deaminase like activity were detected in the brain, liver, and adrenal glands, where they supported BH₄-dependent biosynthesis of monoamine neurotransmitters and nitric oxide (Chauvin and Kristensen 2003; Sawada et al. 2002). Although direct biochemical evidence for sepiapterin deaminase in higher eukaryotes remains limited, its putative role in maintaining BH₄ homeostasis underscores its potential metabolic and physiological importance (Ziegler et al. 2000). In insects, sepiapterin deaminase predominates in pigment-forming tissues where fluctuating oxidative environments favour the diversion of sepiapterin into lumazine derivatives rather than BH₄ regeneration (Ferré, 2024; Ziegler 2003). The biosynthetic pathway of BH₄ in mammals primarily depended on three key enzymes—GTP cyclohydrolase I, 6-pyruvoyl tetrahydropterin synthase, and sepiapterin reductase. However, the presence of sepiapterin deaminase activity within BH₄ biosynthesis had not been conclusively demonstrated to date. Studies suggest that certain lower eukaryotes, including protists and fungi, possess deaminase-like enzymes that participate in alternative pteridine metabolic routes. Additionally, Ziegler et al., (2000) emphasize the potential involvement of pterin deaminase in sepiapterin metabolism in zebrafish (Danio rerio).

Collectively, pterin deaminase exhibits a wide distribution and performs diverse functions in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic systems. In contrast, sepiapterin deaminase displays a more restricted yet crucial role associated with redox regulation, pigmentation, and neurotransmitter metabolism. This difference in distribution reflects the evolutionary divergence between the two enzymes, where pterin deaminase likely evolved from a general pterin-degrading enzyme to a specialized catalyst involved in cofactor regeneration for sepiapterin deaminase.

Structural comparison pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase

Both pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase are classified as members of the amidohydrolase superfamily (AHS), which comprises metal-dependent hydrolases that catalyze a wide range of deamination and amide hydrolysis reactions (Seibert and Raushel 2005).

Members of the amidohydrolase superfamily typically adopt a conserved (β/α)₈ TIM-barrel fold with a metal-binding active site, reflecting their shared structural framework and suggesting an evolutionary relationship among these functionally diverse enzymes (Holm and Sander 1997; Liu and Huo 2014). However, the substrate-binding residues and metal coordination geometries vary considerably between pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase reflecting their divergence in substrate specificity and physiological function.

(3D) 3 dimensional fold

Recent homology modeling and X-ray crystallographic analyses have provided insights into the three-dimensional organization of pterin deaminase. (Fan et al. 2013a, b) demonstrated that bacterial pterin deaminase from Variovorax sp. adopts a canonical (β/α)₈ TIM-barrel topology characteristic of amidohydrolase superfamily enzymes, comparable to cytosine deaminase (Ko et al. 2003) and isoxanthopterin deaminase (Hall et al. 2010), consistent with the structural relationships originally identified by (Holm and Sander 1997). The central β-barrel forms the catalytic core surrounded by α-helices, creates a hydrophobic pocket that stabilizes the pterin substrate through hydrogen bonding with side chains of conserved residues. Similarly, structural modeling of pterin deaminase from Agrobacterium tumefaciens revealed high structural similarity to cytosine deaminase, with a core RMSD below 2.5 Å, confirming its evolutionary relationship within the amidohydrolase clade (Dhanapal et al. 2023). The predicted tertiary fold includes a catalytically essential zinc ion coordinated by histidine and aspartate residues, which facilitates polarization of the amide group and promotes nucleophilic attack on the substrate’s C2–NH₂ bond (Fan et al. 2013a, b; Jayaraman et al., 2016).

In contrast, sepiapterin deaminase although classified within the amidohydrolase superfamily family (AHS), exhibits distinct structural constraints due to its high substrate specificity toward sepiapterin. Based on comparative structural studies of insect enzymes, sepiapterin deaminase is believed to maintain the TIM-barrel scaffold, but possesses a narrower substrate-binding cavity tailored for the rigid bicyclic structure of sepiapterin (Tsusue 1971; Matsubara et al. 1963). The conformational arrangement in Bombyx mori sepiapterin deaminase involves aromatic residues forming π-π stacking interactions with the pterin ring system, aiding precise orientation during catalysis. This tighter substrate fit likely restricts its activity spectrum compared to the more flexible binding pocket of pterin deaminase.

Collectively, these observations indicate that the broader catalytic pocket of pterin deaminase supports activity towards multiple pterin substrates, whereas the spatially constrained active site of sepiapterin deaminase has evolved to preferentially bind sepiapterin.

Metal-binding sites and catalytic residues

The active sites of both pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase are metallohydrolase-type catalytic centers, yet their metal-binding configurations differ subtly. Pterin deaminase primarily utilizes Zn2⁺ as a catalytic cofactor, which is stabilized by a conserved His-Asp-His triad (Hall et al. 2010; Fan et al. 2013a, b). This catalytic arrangement activated a water molecule to perform nucleophilic attack, resulting to hydrolytic deamination of the substrate’s exocyclic amine group. Site-directed mutagenesis and docking studies have confirmed that substitution of His or Asp residues results in complete loss of activity (Jayaraman et al., 2016), highlighting their crucial role in metal coordination and catalysis.

In addition to zinc, certain pterin deaminase homologs exhibit affinity for Fe2⁺ or Mn2⁺ ions, particularly in thermophilic bacteria, which may enhance thermal stability (Takikawa et al. 1979). The metal coordination geometry remains octahedral, providing the appropriate orientation for activation of a water molecule and proton transfer.

Sepiapterin deaminase, while less structurally characterized, is also inferred to be a zinc-dependent amidohydrolase. Unlike pterin deaminase, sepiapterin deaminase possesses a Cys-His-Glu motif in its active site, which likely coordinates Zn2⁺ with a distinct geometry and contributes to its narrower substrate specificity. Inhibition of sepiapterin deaminase by p-chloromercuricbenzoate (Tsusue 1971) confirms that the cysteine residue is catalytically essential, distinguishing it from the histidine-dominated metal site of pterin deaminase. This substitution reflects a trade-off between catalytic speed and substrate selectivity, illustrating how minor residue changes can reshape enzyme specificity and explaining why pterin deaminase acts on a broad range of pterins, whereas sepiapterin deaminase is strictly specific for sepiapterin and isosepiapterin (Fan et al. 2013a, b; Gerlt et al. 2017; Ribeiro et al. 2019).

Active-site dynamics and substrate recognition

The active-site architecture of pterin deaminase has been studied extensively through in silico docking (Fan et al. 2013a, b; Dhanapal et al. 2023).The catalytic residues His237, Asp314, and Lys164, identified from conserved motifs of the amidohydrolase superfamily and modeled based on Agrobacterium radiobacter and related bacteria, are proposed to form a network stabilizing the transition state during amide hydrolysis. In contrast, the structural features of the sepiapterin deaminase active site have not yet been resolved experimentally and therefore inferred mainly from homology modeling and docking studies. Sepiapterin deaminase’s active site is proposed to include aromatic residues, such as tyrosine and phenylalanine, which may participate in substrate recognition through interactions with the side chain of sepiapterin. Such interactions are proposed to impose steric constraints that favor precise substrate positioning during deamination, potentially accounting for the narrower substrate specificity reported for sepiapterin deaminase relative to pterin deaminase.

Both enzymes displayed convergent evolution in maintaining the amidohydrolase fold while diverging in active-site loop composition. Holm and Sander (1997) reported that adaptive shortening of loop regions near the metal-binding pocket alters substrate size tolerance in amidohydrolases, a pattern also observed when comparing pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase models. Such loop modifications influenced both enzyme stability and substrate access, which explains why sepiapterin deaminase remains structurally rigid while pterin deaminase accommodates diverse pteridine substrates.

Collectively, structural comparison reveal that both pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase share a common catalytic scaffold but diverge in local geometry and residue composition, enabling distinct substrate preferences. Pterin deaminase functions as a versatile enzyme for pterin degradation, whereas sepiapterin deaminase’s specialized configuration facilitates precise control in the BH₄-dependent metabolic and pigment pathways.

Although pterin deaminase structures have been resolved at atomic resolution, sepiapterin deaminase remains poorly characterized hindering definitive conclusions about its catalytic residues. Future crystallographic studies are required to validate current homology based assumptions.

Catalytic activity comparison of pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase

Catalytic mechanism

Pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase share a common hydrolytic deamination mechanism characteristic of the amidohydrolase superfamily. Both utilize a Zn2⁺ ion to activate a water molecule for nucleophilic attack on the C2 carbon, resulting in the formation of a tetrahedral intermediate and the release of ammonia (Seibert and Raushel 2005).

Their profound functional divergence, however is rooted in their substrate scope. Pterin deaminase exhibits broad catalytic versatility, acting on a wide array of oxidized pterins such as pterin, xanthopterin, isoxanthopterin, and even folic acid, to yield various lumazine derivatives (Fan et al. 2013a, b; Hall et al. 2010). In contrast, sepiapterin deaminase represents a model of specificity, exclusively targeting sepiapterin and isosepiapterin to produce the yellow pigment sepialumazine (Tsusue 1971; Matsubara et al. 1963).

Kinetic parameters and metal dependence

Kinetic analyses further highlight the distinct physiological roles of these enzymes. Pterin deaminase from Agrobacterium tumefaciens exhibits high catalytic efficiency for pterin (Km = 3.4 × 10⁻4 M; Vmax = 2.8 μmol min⁻1 mg⁻1) and remains active under alkaline conditions (pH 7.8–8.2) and with various divalent metal ions (Zn2⁺ > Mn2⁺ > Fe2⁺), underscoring its robustness (Dhanapal et al. 2023; Jayaraman et al., 2016).

In contrast, Sepiapterin deaminase from Bombyx mori displays a comparable Km for sepiapterin (5.9 × 10⁻4 M) but operates under stricter catalytic control (Tsusue 1971). Its activity is highly dependent on specific metal cofactors (Zn2⁺ or Mn2⁺) and is abolished by Fe2⁺ or Mg2⁺, reflecting a more specialized and finely tuned active site configuration.

Inhibitors and regulatory control

The regulatory profiles of pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase reflect their unique active-site chemistries. Pterin deaminase is inhibited by pterin analogs like 8-azaguanine and amethopterin, which compete for the zinc-binding site, and is inactivated by metal chelators (EDTA, 1,10-phenanthroline) (Hall et al. 2010; Fan et al. 2013a, b). Inhibitor assays on purified pterin deaminase show complete inhibition by EDTA and 1,10-phenanthroline at 10 mM, while DTT and PMSF produced only partial inhibition at 10 mM, consistent with a metalloenzyme mechanism (Jayaraman et al.,2016). Additionally pterin deaminase experiences product inhibition by lumazine derivatives.

In contrast, sepiapterin deaminase is uniquely sensitive to thiol-targeting agents like p-chloromercuribenzoate (p-CMB) and Hg2⁺, confirming the functional importance of a cysteine residue in its metal-binding motif a key distinction from the His-Asp-His triad of pterin deaminase (Tsusue 1971). Accumulation of its product, sepialumazine, also provides feedback inhibition, ensuring tight metabolic regulation within the pigment and BH₄ salvage pathways.

Physiological role of pterin deaminase

Pterin deaminase plays a significant role in regulating the intracellular concentration of pteridines in microorganisms. The enzyme catalyzes the hydrolytic deamination of oxidized pterins, including pterin, xanthopterin, and isoxanthopterin, thereby maintaining pteridine homeostasis within cells (Fan et al. 2013a, b; Hall et al. 2010).In bacteria such as Agrobacterium tumefaciens, Micrococcus luteus, and Pseudomonas fluorescens, pterin deaminase activity contributes to pterin degradation and recycling, preventing accumulation of potentially reactive or inhibitory intermediates (Dhanapal et al. 2023; Jayaraman et al., 2016).

In prokaryotic organisms, pterin deaminase is believed to be involved in one-carbon metabolism through the structural modification of pteridine derivatives related to folate cofactors. By deaminating and transforming surplus pterins into lumazine derivatives, pterin deaminase had an indirect effect on the availability of folate analogs that are crucial for nucleotide biosynthesis and maintaining redox balance. Additionally, bacterial pterin deaminase enzymes, such as those identified in Alcaligenes faecalis, convert natural pterins like 6-hydroxypterin and 7-hydroxypterin into 6,7-dihydroxylumazine (DHL), demonstrating their function in producing lumazine pigments that might act as photo protective substances or secondary metabolites in soil ecosystems (McNutt 1963).

In eukaryotes, pterin deaminase activity has been observed in insects, slime molds, and mammalian tissues, where it contributes to modulating pterin-related signaling and immune functions. Studies in rats indicated that pterin deaminase activity in the liver affected lymphocyte proliferation and immune response regulation, linking pterin metabolism to immunological functions (Sabarathinam et al. 2011) Thus, pterin deaminase appeared to act as a metabolic regulator rather than a pigment-forming enzyme, maintaining the balance of biologically active pterins and folates across different taxa.

Physiological role of sepiapterin deaminase

In contrast, sepiapterin deaminase exhibits a more specialized biological role. The enzyme was primarily associated with pigment formation and cofactor turnover in insects. Sepiapterin deaminase catalyzes the deamination of sepiapterin to sepialumazine, a yellow pigment that accumulates in the cuticle, eyes, and epidermal tissues of insects such as Bombyx mori (Matsubara et al. 1963; Tsusue 1971). This enzymatic step was crucial for color expression in insect pigmentation, influencing visible phenotypic variations in mutants such as the lemon strain of the silkworm (Yan et al. 2009).

Biochemically, sepiapterin deaminase also functions within the pterin and tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) metabolic network. Although sepiapterin itself is not a canonical intermediate in the BH₄ biosynthetic pathway, sepiapterin deaminase’s activity diverted it into lumazine derivatives, preventing its conversion into BH₄ via sepiapterin reductase (SR). This diversion is thought to help maintain pterin equilibrium and prevent excess accumulation of sepiapterin, which could otherwise disrupt cofactor balance and cellular redox homeostasis (Ferre 2024). In this way, sepiapterin deaminase acted as a metabolic control point within the BH₄ salvage pathway, fine-tuning pigment biosynthesis and cofactor metabolism in insects. (Tsusue 1971; Ferre 2024).

Comparative metabolic significance of pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase

Pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase illustrate contrasting physiological roles shaped by evolutionary adaptation. Pterin deaminase functions as a metabolic generalist, regulating intracellular pterin levels in microorganisms, supporting folate-dependent metabolism, and potentially modulating immune and signaling processes in eukaryotes. In contrast, sepiapterin deaminase acts as a specialist in insects, converting sepiapterin to sepialumazine for pigmentation while maintaining BH₄ cofactor and redox balance. These distinctions highlight how structurally related enzymes can diverge to fulfill either broad metabolic regulation or highly specific physiological functions. Together, pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase exemplify the evolutionary balance between enzymatic versatility and specialization in pterin metabolism across taxa. Recent advances and structural insights into pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase

Recent advances have considerably advanced our understanding of pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase in terms of structure, function, and physiological significance. Pterin deaminase has been classified within the metallo-dependent amidohydrolase superfamily, possessing conserved metal-binding motifs crucial for catalytic function. It participates in purine and pyrimidine metabolism, influencing nitrogen turnover and redox regulation. Studies indicate that pterin deaminase exhibits high thermostability and catalytic efficiency, supporting its potential use as an industrial biocatalyst for pterin biotransformation and lumazine-derivative synthesis which are valuable intermediates in riboflavin biosynthesis, fluorescent compound production, and pterin-based fine chemical manufacturing. Novel pterin deaminase producing microbes such as Agrobacterium radiobacter K84 and Micrococcus luteus demonstrate substrate diversity and environmental adaptability (Barzilay et al. 2025; Ferre 2024).

In contrast, sepiapterin deaminase has drawn interest for its role in tetrahydrobiopterin (BH₄) metabolism, impacting neurotransmitter synthesis and vascular homeostasis. It exhibits strict substrate specificity for sepiapterin and its derivatives and is predominantly found in brain and hepatic tissues with high BH₄ demand. Despite predictive homo tetrameric models suggesting structural similarity to other amidohydrolases, definitive crystallographic data for both pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase remain unavailable (Delbreil et al. 2024; Boulghobra and Bonose 2022). Recent advances in clinical therapies for metabolic disorders associated with BH₄ deficiency have further underscored the importance of enzymes involved in sepiapterin metabolism and salvage pathways. Dysregulation of BH₄ homeostasis is linked to impaired neurotransmitter synthesis and vascular complications, prompting therapeutic strategies that emphasize modulation of pterin turnover rather than cofactor replacement alone. This approach is exemplified by pharmacological developments such as sepiapterin (Sephience), designed to enhance BH₄ pathway function (Lamb 2026).

Emerging studies also link altered pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase activity with cancer progression, neurotransmitter imbalance, and pigmentation regulation in insects, underscoring their therapeutic and biotechnological potential (Zeng et al. 2024; Wang and Gao 2024). Future research should focus on high-resolution structural characterization, genetic regulation, and therapeutic modulation of these enzymes to unlock their full potential in biotechnology and medicine.

Conclusion

This review concludes that pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase, while sharing a conserved amidohydrolase scaffold, have evolved distinct catalytic and physiological identities driven by minor but critical residue substitutions in their metal-binding motifs. Pterin deaminase functions primarily as a broad-specificity metabolic regulator of pterin and folate derivatives, supporting redox balance and one-carbon metabolism. In contrast, Sepiapterin deaminase exhibits narrow substrate specificity, coupling pigment biosynthesis and BH₄ cofactor turnover in insects and higher organisms.

Beyond their biochemical characterization, the expanding research on pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase highlights their translational potential in multiple fields. Pterin deaminase’s thermostability and substrate versatility make it a promising biocatalyst for green chemistry, pharmaceutical synthesis, and diagnostic biosensors. Its regulatory role in pterin turnover also provides insights for targeting metabolic and redox-related diseases, including cancer and neurodegenerative disorders. Similarly, sepiapterin deaminase’s pigment-modulating activity in insects suggests novel applications in pest management and sericulture, where enzyme-based modulation could influence coloration, stress adaptation, and breeding outcomes. Moreover, bacterial sepiapterin deaminase like enzymes implicated in oxidative stress and biofilm regulation offer new strategies for antimicrobial or antibiofilm drug design.

Together, these enzymes exemplify how structural conservation can coexist with functional diversification within enzyme superfamilies. Their biochemical versatility pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase can be harnessed in therapeutic enzyme design, metabolic engineering, and biosensor development for disease diagnostics. Their redox-linked mechanisms may aid in targeting oxidative stress disorders and neurodegenerative diseases. Moreover, their structural adaptability offers scope for bioengineering novel catalysts and developing enzyme-based biotechnological tools in life science research. For further studies, this review establishes pterin deaminase and sepiapterin deaminase as model enzymes illustrating how minor structural adaptations can generate major functional diversity, linking enzymatic evolution to metabolic specialization.
