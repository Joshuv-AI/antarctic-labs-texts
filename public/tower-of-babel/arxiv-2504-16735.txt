An Expressive Coalgebraic Modal Logic for Cellular Automata
Henning Basold, Chase Ford, Lulof Pirée

Abstract
Cellular automata provide models of parallel computation based on cells, whose connectivity is given by an action of a monoid on the cells. At each step in the computation, every cell is decorated with a state that evolves in discrete steps according to a local update rule, which determines the next state of a cell based on its neighbour’s states. In this paper, we consider a coalgebraic view on cellular automata, which does not require typical restrictions, such as uniform neighbourhood connectivity and uniform local rules. Using the coalgebraic view, we devise a behavioural equivalence for cellular automata and a modal logic to reason about their behaviour. We then prove a Hennessy-Milner style theorem, which states that pairs of cells satisfy the same modal formulas exactly if they are identified under cellular behavioural equivalence.

Introduction

Cellular automata provide a model for computations that are distributed in space

,

Besides being an immediate model for parallel computing, cellular automata find applications in optimisation

Cellular automata (CA) comprise a collection of cells that hold a state and interact with other cells in their neighbourhood in order to update their state.
In this paper, we will focus on synchronous cellular automata that update cell states in discrete rounds, and where the parallelism only stems from spatial distribution.
A cellular automaton starts from an initial configuration, that is, an assignment of states to each cell, and evolves by means of local rules that compute the next state of the cell from the states of its neighbours.
The spatial neighbourhood of a cell is given by a collection of elements of a monoid, which in turn acts on the cells to provide the spatial connectivity.

There a various ways of implementing leader election
[
27
]
, which can be modelled with cellular automata.
However, correctness proofs for distributed algorithms can be tedious.
In this paper, we propose a
modal logic to reason about the behaviour of cellular automata
, in which properties such as periodicity and nilpotency (existence of fixed points) can be expressed.
Our main result is a Hennessy-Milner style theorem, showing that the logical equivalence induced by our logic captures precisely behavioural equivalence of cellular automata.

In order to formulate behavioural equivalence of cellular automata, we also contribute a
coalgebraic description of cellular automata
in the class of, what is traditionally referred to as, synchronous, non-regular and non-uniform cellular automata
[
8
]
.
Understanding simulations and bisimulations for such models is generally challenging
[
55
]
, but our coalgebraic perspective provides a clear understanding of functional bisimulations between cellular automata via a small extension of the standard notion of coalgebra homomorphisms
[
33
]
to, what we call,
cellular morphisms
.
We show that cellular morphisms are precisely what is required to preserve and reflect the global behaviour of cellular automata, and that they induce the natural behavioural equivalence that corresponds to logical equivalence in our logic.

Related work

We refer to Bhattacharjee et al.
[
8
]
for a comprehensive account and comparison of the various classes of cellular automata and, in particular, for an overview of notions of non-uniformity in models of cellular networks.
A more historical account is given in Sarkar
[
57
]
.
Nishio
[
48
,
49
]
provides an analysis of the relative expressive power between uniform cellular automata and those with non-uniform neighbourhood structure.
Many notions of behavioural equivalence and simulation orders for specific cellular automata classes have appeared in the literature, typically involving a fixed monoid and binary state set
[
20
,
50
]
.
Our notion of behavioural equivalence generalises these notions, but does currently cover simulations.

Modal logics capable of describing patterns in the evolution of configurations in cellular automata have been described by Delivorias et al.
[
18
]
and Hagiya et al.
[
23
]
.
Ishida et al.
[
30
,
29
]
investigate the use of propositional logic as a framework for specifying the local rules of binary cellular automata; Zhang and Bölcskei
[
67
]
provide an extension thereof using propositional Łukasiewicz logic.
Das and Chakraborty
[
17
]
present a deduction system capable of computing the global rule of a fixed uniform cellular automaton.
These works are somewhat orthogonal to ours and, in particular, do not include an analogue of our Hennessy-Milner style theorem (
Theorem

27
).

There have been a number of approaches to modelling cellular automata network structure via category-theoretic machinery.
Capobianco and Uustalu
[
12
,
13
]
model synchronous uniform cellular automata as morphisms in the Kleisli category of the cowriter comonad on the category of uniform spaces and uniformly continuous maps.
Widemann and Hauhs
[
62
]
employ a combination of bialgebras and distributive laws to model two-dimensional uniform cellular automata.
In contrast, we provide a coalgebraic description of cellular automata enabling us to extract our notion of cellular behavioural equivalence.

Staton
[
60
]
describes and relates various notions of bisimulation native to coalgebras of a given type;
a careful comparison with our notion of behavioural equivalence on cellular automata is left for future work.
Basic modal logic
[
9
]
has been extended to the level of coalgebras in the seminal work of Moss
[
47
]
.
Subsequently, Pattinson
[
51
,
52
]
introduced predicate liftings
as a framework for establishing expressiveness results linking logical equivalence and behavioural equivalence on the level of coalgebras
[
38
,
58
]
. For an overview, see Kupke and Pattinson
[
42
]
and Cîrstea et al.
[
16
]
.
We note that thise framework does not directly yield our expressiveness result (
Theorem

27
) as our notion of cellular morphism modifies the usual coalgebra homomorphisms. It would be interesting to understand whether our description of cellular automata could be understood in the context of Joyal et al.
[
34
]
.

2
Preliminaries

We assume familiarity with the basic concepts
from category theory
[
2
,
44
]
.
We briefly gather the necessary background on
(universal) coalgebra
[
33
]
and
comonads
[
7
]
.

Unless mentioned otherwise, we work
over the category
\mathbf{Set}
of sets and maps which
is complete and cocomplete. We fix a terminal
object
1=\{\star\}
, and we write
!_{X}
for the
unique map
X\to 1
. Furthermore,
\mathbf{Set}
admits
the structure of a
Cartesian closed category

[
2
, Ch. VII]
; for each pair of sets
Y,Z
,
the
internal hom

[Y,Z]
is just the set of all
maps from
Y
to
Z
. Cartesian closure means that
there is natural bijection

\mathbf{Set}(X\times Y,Z)\cong\mathbf{Set}(X,[Y,Z]).

From right to left, it maps
f\colon X\to Z^{Y}
to its
transpose

f^{\sharp}\colon X\times Y\to Z
given by
f(x,y)=f(x)(y)
.

2.1
Coalgebra

(Universal) coalgebra
[
33
,
31
,
1
]
is a
framework for the uniform analysis of (state-based)
transition structures. We first give the general definition
before providing a specialized discussion of coalgebras
on
\mathbf{Set}
.

Definition 1
.

Let
F\colon\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{C}
be an endofunctor on a
category
\mathbf{C}
. An
(
F
-)coalgebra
is a
pair
(X,\gamma)
given by an object
X\in\mathbf{C}
and
a morphism
\gamma\colon X\to FX
.
A
homomorphism
from
(X,\gamma)
to
(Y,\xi)

is a morphism
h\colon X\to Y
in
\mathbf{C}
such that

Fh\cdot\gamma=\xi\cdot h
.
We write
\Coalg(F)
for the category of coalgebras
and their homomorphisms, and we denote by

|-|\colon\Coalg(F)\to\mathbf{C}
the faithful forgetful
functor that maps a coalgebra to its carrier.

Throughout the paper, we will follow the established cellular
automata terminology:
cells
are elements of the carrier of
coalgebras, while
states
are labels assigned to those cells
by coalgebras. The intuition is that cells generate the spatial
layout of cellular automata, while the labelling represents the
current state of a cells that accumulate into the global computational
state of a cellular automaton. This deviates from the common
terminology in coalgebra, where the elements of the carrier
are typically referred to as states, thereby borrowing intuition
from automata theory. However, this terminology does not align
well with that of cellular automata because the state of a system
arises only from the labelling. For instance, in the game of life,
cells provide a 2-dimensional grid layout, which has no computational
content without a labelling of “dead” and “alive” cells
[
20
]
.

Example 2
.

Let
M
be a set.
By Cartesian closure, we obtain a functor
[M,-]\colon\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}

that acts by post-composition, that is,
[M,f](m)=f\cdot m
.
We will denote the functor
[M,-]
by
M_{\ast}
and call it the
co-writer
functor, see
Section

2.2
.
An
M_{\ast}
-coalgebra is a set
X
equipped with a map
\gamma\colon X\to[M,X]
.
Writing
x\xrightarrow{m}y
for
x,y\in X
and
m\in M
with
\gamma(x)(m)=y
, we can see
\gamma
as a
deterministic
M
-labelled transition system
.
A map

3
Cellular automata

In this section, we introduce our coalgebraic definition of cellular automata and discuss several examples.
Cellular automata consist of cells arranged in a spatial structure, while their behaviour is determined by the evolution of cell states over time.
For example, in Conway’s game of life, the cells are arranged in the discrete plane
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and each cell can be in one of the states dead or alive.
The state of a cell evolves by means of local rules that determine, given the state of each cell in the neighbourhood of the cell, its next state.
For the game of life, the neighbourhood, which rules take into account, comprises the direct neighbours (horizontal, vertical and diagonal) of a cell.
In fact, we can see the cells of the game of life as being generated from a chosen cell by taking steps up/down and left/right.
Such moves are precisely captured by the monoid
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, while the neighbours are reached via moves in the subset
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\;|\;x,y\in\{1,0,-1\}\}
.
More generally, cellular automata are based on a monoid
(M,\bullet,\mathbf{1})
that describes possible moves between cells and a set
N\subseteq M
that describes the neighbourhood of local rules.
A cellular automaton then comprises a set of cells, a description of the cell connectivity via a monoid action
\alpha\colon M\times X\to X
and for every cell
x\in X
a local rule
\gamma_{x}
that computes the next state of
x
when given the states of the cells in the neighbourhood
N
of
x
.

There is a minor complication when it comes to local rules which does not appear in the game of life.
We may hope that
\gamma_{x}
is a map

, where a map

corresponds to the configuration of states in the neighbourhood
N
of

.
However, this fails for cellular automata whose action is not free, which means that there are
m,n\in M
with
m\neq n
and
\alpha(m,x)=\alpha(n,x)
.
For example, equipping the mod-2 group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
with its natural action is not free because, for instance,
0
can be reached by adding any odd number to
1
:
(2k+1)+1=0\mod 2
.
Thus, if we want to inspect the two neighbours via
N=\{-1,1\}
, then we need to ensure that the state assignment

additionally fulfils
f(-1)=f(1)
.

5
Modal logic over cellular automata

We proceed to develop a modal language
\ML
for specifying properties in the configuration traces of cellular automata obtained by (iterative) application of the global rule from a chosen initial configuration and base cell.
Thus, our language will effectively be interpreted in based and reachable cellular automata equipped with an initial configuration.
Our language involves a collection of
standard modalities
indexed by elements of the monoid
M
as well as an distinguished
update modality
.
Intuitively, standard modalities allow access to the states of cells reachable from the given one in the current configuration.
In contrast, the update modality enables access to cell states in the configurations obtained by application of the global rule to the initial configuration.
The combination of these modalities result in a language for expressing properties of the local and global behaviour of cellular automata.

The syntax of our modal language, which extends the standard syntax of modal logic
[
9
]
by
infinitary disjunctions and the update modality mentioned above, is defined in the following.

Definition 15
.

Let
\ML
denote the proper class generated by the following grammar:

\displaystyle\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\mathop{:}}=

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c}s&\neg\varphi&\tbigvee\displaylimits_{i\in I}\varphi_{i}&\lozenge_{m}\varphi&\hourglass\varphi\end{array}

where
s\in S
is a state symbol,
m\in M
, and
I
is an index set.
An element
\varphi\in\ML
is called a
modal formula
.
For each regular cardinal
\kappa
(i.e. an infinite cardinal which is not
cofinal with any smaller cardinals), we write
\ML_{\kappa}
for the fragment of
\ML
obtained by restricting to index sets of cardinality strictly below
\kappa
. We refer to
\varphi\in\ML_{\kappa}
as
\kappa
-ary modal formulas
. A
modal theory
is a set
\Phi
of modal formulas.

We emphasize that our syntax is given by a proper class of modal formulas.
For each modal formula
\varphi
, let
\kappa_{\varphi}
denote the least regular cardinal such that
\varphi\in\ML_{\kappa_{\varphi}}
(such a cardinal exists).
In particular, each modal theory
\Phi
sits in
\ML_{\lambda}

where

\lambda=\sup\{\kappa_{\varphi}\;:\;\varphi\in\Phi\}.

Notation 16
.

We employ the following shorthands in the examples:

\bot:=\tbigvee\displaylimits_{i\in\varnothing}\varphi_{i},\qquad\qquad\tbigwedge\displaylimits_{i\in I}\varphi_{i}:=\lnot\tbigvee\displaylimits_{i\in I}\lnot\varphi_{i},\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\varphi\to\psi):=\lnot\varphi\lor\psi,

where
\varnothing
is the empty set. Note that
\ML
is non-empty as
\bot\in\ML
.

As indicated, modal formulas are meant to be interpreted in based cellular automata together with an initial configuration.
We detail the semantics of modal formulas below.

.

Let

\displaystyle(\gamma,x,c)\Vdash s

\displaystyle(\gamma,x,c)\Vdash\lnot\varphi

\displaystyle(\gamma,x,c)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\hfil\displaystyle/\hfil$\crcr$\displaystyle\Vdash$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\hfil\textstyle/\hfil$\crcr$\textstyle\Vdash$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\hfil\scriptstyle/\hfil$\crcr$\scriptstyle\Vdash$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\hfil\scriptscriptstyle/\hfil$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\Vdash$\crcr}}}}\varphi

The identity map is a logical transport and logical transports are closed under composition.

Proof.

Obviously, we have that
(\gamma,x_{0},c)\Vdash\varphi

(\gamma,x_{0},\mathsf{id}(c))\Vdash\varphi
holds.
Given logical transports

we obtain

6
A Hennessy-Milner style theorem

In this section, we establish a correspondence between cellular morphisms and logical transport: we show that two cells are cellular bisimilar precisely when they satisfy the same formulas.
This should be compared with the seminal result of Hennessy and Milner
[
25
]
: given a pair of states in finitely-branching labelled transition systems (of the same similarity type) which are not bisimilar, there is a modal formula which distinguishes them.

We start with the easy direction, which is proven by induction on formulas.

Theorem 26
.

If
(f,s)
is a based cellular morphism
(\gamma,x_{0})\to(\delta,y_{0})
between cellular automata, then
s
is a logical transport
(\gamma,x_{0})\rightsquigarrow(\delta,y_{0})
.

This allows us to define a functor
\mathbf{CA}_{\bullet}^{r}\to\mathbf{LogTrans}
by projecting
(f,s)
on
s
.
In the other direction, we show that each logical transport induces a cellular morphism, at least whenever the underlying monoid action of its domain is free. This additional criterion should be compared with the finite-branching condition of the classical Hennessy-Milner Theorem.

Theorem 27
.

Suppose that
t\colon(\gamma,x_{0})\rightsquigarrow(\delta,y_{0})
is a logical transport with
\gamma_{1}
free.
Then there exists a unique a based cellular morphism
\bar{t}\colon(\gamma,x_{0})\to(\delta,y_{0})

with
\pi_{2}(\bar{t})=t
.

We mention two use cases of
Theorem

27
. First, we reemphasize that in order it to prove that two cells are inequivalent under cellular bisimilarity, it suffices to exhibit a modal formula from
\ML
which distinguishes them (i.e. a formula which is satisfied in one structure and falsified in the other).
In another direction, our result enables one to show the non-axiomatisability of classes of cellular automata using familiar techniques from model theory.
We illustrate the non-axiomatisability of one-way cellular automata (cf.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We have described a novel coalgebraic model of cellular automata that, unlike the traditional notion of cellular automata, permits non-uniformity in the local rules and neighbourhood structures of individual cells.
One of the primary benefits of our coalgebraic model is that it supports a robust concept of cellular morphism between cellular automata, which, to the best of our knowledge, has been absent from the literature until now.
We have introduced a modal logic for reasoning about the global behaviour of cellular automata.
Our language involves two types of unary modalities: standard modalities indexed by monoid elements provide access to reachable cell states within a given configuration, while our update modality allows access to cell states after application of the global rule.
Thus, our language is meant to express dynamic properties of cellular automata such as periodicity or nilpotency.
We provide a Hennessy-Milner style theorem (
Theorem

27
) which relates logical and behavioural equivalence.
In particular, we show that cells are identified under cellular morphisms precisely if they satisfy the same modal formulas.

We mention several directions for future work.
We first note that careful inspection of the construction of our coalgebraic type functor suggests that our model of cellular automata might be adapted to base categories with finite limits and Cartesian closed structure, or perhaps even symmetric monoidal closed structure.
This more general landscape includes, for example, the symmetric monoidal closed category of
1
-bounded metric spaces and non-expansive maps.
Extending our framework there might afford a quantitative comparison of cellular automata in the form of a behavioural pseudo-metric
[
6
]
.
We would then also be able to capture linear or continuous
[
8
]
cellular automata.

Our coalgebraic perspective on cellular automata also opens up the possibility of various extensions.
In particular, it should be possible to integrate computational effects by adding a monad component
[
32
,
59
]
.
This would give access to, for example, probabilistic behaviour that can be used to solve the leader election problem without identifiers
[
5
]
.

An interesting aspect of the global rule is its definition in terms of an evaluation map given in
Equation

3.2
.
This map can be either seen as a distributive law of the constant functor over
C
and then potentially related to bialgebras
[
39
]
.
However, evaluation functions also appear in coalgebraic approaches to behavioural metrics
[
6
]
and it would be beneficial to understand if metrics and global behaviour are formally related.

Notions of behavioural equivalence have traditionally been characterized not only in dedicated modal logics, as we do in the current paper, but also in terms of behavioural equivalence games
[
61
]
.
We hope to employ recent work on coalgebraic behavioural equivalence games
[
22
,
41
]
in the development of a game-theoretic perspective on cellular behavioural equivalence.

Finally, our logic features infinitary operations but often fixed point logics, such as modal
\mu
-calculi
[
11
]
, suffice.
Devising fixed point logics for cellular automata in terms of our coalgebraic framework may, with slight modifications to take the global behaviour into account, give access to powerful model checking techniques
[

,

]
.
