Undifferentiated embryonal sarcoma of the liver presenting as a complex mixed solid-cystic hepatic lesion in a young adult: A case report
Authors: Emily Leung, Shabnam Islam, Matthys Van Wyk, Zi Qin Ng, Soo Yeon Kim, Tze Khor, Lingjun Mou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Undifferentiated embryonal sarcoma of the liver (UESL) is a rare and aggressive primary hepatic mesenchymal malignancy, predominantly affecting children aged 6-10 years but occasionally arising in young adults. Diagnosis is clinically challenging owing to paradoxical and nonspecific imaging features that vary across modalities, contributing to misdiagnosis rates of up to 23.5%. We report a 23-year-old woman who presented with a 6-week history of right upper quadrant pain, normal liver function tests, and unremarkable tumor markers. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated a solid-appearing, heterogeneously echoic hepatic mass, while computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging revealed a predominantly cystic lesion with enhancing internal septations, serpiginous peripheral vessels, intertumoral hemorrhage, fluid-fluid levels, and restricted diffusion—a characteristic cross-modal discordance typical of UESL. Following multidisciplinary team review, the patient underwent right hemi hepatectomy without preoperative biopsy. Histopathology confirmed UESL with classic spindle and stellate cells in a fibro myxoid background, diastase-resistant periodic acid-Schiff-positive eosinophilic globules, and an immunohistochemical profile consistent with primitive mesenchymal origin. Adjuvant chemotherapy with ifosfamide and epirubicin was administered, and 12-month surveillance imaging demonstrated no recurrence. This case illustrates that cross-modal imaging discordance in a young adult with a large complex hepatic lesion and normal tumor markers should raise suspicion for UESL, and supports upfront surgical resection as the cornerstone of management.

Introduction

Undifferentiated embryonal sarcoma of the liver (UESL) is a rare and aggressive mesenchymal tumor, first recognized as a distinct entity by Stocker and Ishak in 1978 [1]. It is the third most common primary malignant liver tumor in children, after hepatoblastoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, typically affecting those aged 6-10 years [2,3]. UESL rarely occurs in adults, with a mean age of 24 years in reported cases, and carries a more aggressive course and poorer prognosis due to delayed diagnosis and therapeutic challenges [4,5]. Imaging plays a critical role in diagnosis, but features are often nonspecific and discordant across modalities, leading to misdiagnosis in up to 23.5% of cases [6]. This case report highlights the multimodal imaging characteristics of UESL in a young adult and emphasizes the importance of multidisciplinary evaluation for timely management.

Case report

A 23-year-old woman, otherwise fit and well, presented with a 6-week history of right upper quadrant pain. Physical examination revealed tender hepatomegaly without jaundice or signs of chronic liver disease. Laboratory investigations, including liver function tests and tumor markers (alpha-fetoprotein [AFP], CA19-9, carcinoembryonic antigen), were unremarkable. Serology for hepatitis, Entamoeba histolytica, and Echinococcus was negative.

Abdominal ultrasound showed a well-circumscribed 83 mm × 80 mm heterogeneously echoic, solid-appearing mass in the right hepatic lobe with small peripheral anechoic cystic foci and no intralesional vascularity on color Doppler (Fig. 1A-C). Computed tomography (CT) revealed a hypodense lesion without internal calcification on noncontrast images, with early arterial enhancement of thin serpiginous peripheral vessels and progressive enhancement of internal septations on postcontrast phases (Fig. 1D-F). Although a laparoscopic approach was initially considered, an open right hemihepatectomy was ultimately performed due to concerns regarding potential cyst rupture. Intraoperatively, a large cystic–solid mass was identified within the right hemiliver, with no evidence of peritoneal disease. The procedure was undertaken using combined Glissonean and anterior approaches. The gallbladder was removed to facilitate exposure.

Both the right anterior and right posterior Glissonean pedicles were carefully dissected and transected separately using 35-mm Echelon vascular staplers, with the staple lines reinforced using 5-0 PDS sutures. Parenchymal transection was performed in the standard manner with CUSA, Harmonic scalpel, ligaclips, Hem-o-lok clips, Vicryl ties, and 4-0 Prolene sutures, following the demarcation line to the anterior surface of the inferior vena cava. The right hepatic vein was subsequently divided with a 35-mm Echelon vascular stapler.

Right liver mobilization was completed by dividing the triangular and coronary ligaments and separating the liver from the inferior vena cava while preserving the right adrenal gland. Postoperatively, the patient made an uncomplicated recovery and was discharged on postoperative day 5. At the Sarcoma Multidisciplinary Meeting, adjuvant chemotherapy and intensive surveillance were recommended. The proposed follow-up schedule included imaging every 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months for the subsequent 2 years, and annually for a further 4 years.

The patient subsequently completed five cycles of adjuvant Ifosfamide and Epirubicin. A 30-month surveillance CT scan demonstrated no evidence of recurrence. Thereafter, she returned to her home country to continue follow-up, as she is an international student completing her university degree.

Discussion

Diagnosis of UESL is challenging due to its paradoxical imaging features: solid or heterogeneous on ultrasound, but cystic or complex solid-cystic with septations, hemorrhage, and variable enhancement on CT and MRI [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. In this case, the discrepancy between ultrasound (solid with peripheral anechoic foci) and CT/MRI (predominantly cystic with enhancing septations and serpiginous vessels) is typical. MRI provided additional insights, such as T1 hyperintense/T2 hypointense zones indicating intratumoral hemorrhage and myxoid content, and fluid-fluid levels.

Although not pathognomonic, a large hepatic mass in a young adult with normal biochemistry and tumor markers, combined with discordant imaging, should raise suspicion for UESL. Differentiation from other cystic lesions (eg, mucinous cystic neoplasm, hydatid cyst, hematoma, pyogenic abscess) is essential (Table 1). Percutaneous biopsy is often avoided due to seeding risks and low yield in myxoid lesions. Differential diagnosis of undifferentiated embryonal sarcoma of the liver (UESL) in young adults.

UESL presents a well-recognized diagnostic challenge owing to its paradoxical cross-modal imaging appearance. In the present case, abdominal ultrasound demonstrated a solid or heterogeneously echoic mass, while CT and MRI characterized it as a predominantly cystic lesion with enhancing internal septations, thin serpiginous peripheral vessels, and T1-hyperintense/T2-hypointense foci reflecting intratumoral hemorrhage and myxoid matrix. This discordance—solid on ultrasound yet cystic on CT and MRI—is regarded as the cardinal imaging feature of UESL and arises because the myxoid, gelatinous tumor content attenuates ultrasound waves differently from its true internal composition [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Fluid-fluid levels, as observed in this case, further reflect prior intratumoral hemorrhage and are a recognized though nonpathognomonic MRI finding in UESL.

DWI demonstrated irregular restricted diffusion within the lesion, with a maximum ADC value of 732 × 10−³ mm²/s at b = 800 s/mm². This is significantly lower than the ADC values typically associated with benign cystic lesions (>2.0 × 10−³ mm²/s) and is consistent with the high cellular density and myxoid stroma of UESL, supporting malignancy. Although DWI findings in UESL have not been systematically characterized in large series, restricted diffusion in this clinical context strengthens the case for a primary hepatic malignancy.

The relationship between UESL and MHL is of pathological and clinical significance. Both entities share chromosomal abnormalities at the 19q13.4 locus, supporting the hypothesis that UESL may arise through malignant transformation of MHL. This genetic continuum means that MHL must be included as an important differential diagnosis in young patients with solid-cystic hepatic lesions, particularly given that distinguishing the two preoperatively on imaging alone may be impossible. In the present case, there were no radiological features favoring MHL—notably the absence of a predominantly cystic, nonenhancing mass without restricted diffusion—and the patient’s age (23 years) was beyond the typical presentation of MHL (under 2 years of age).

On histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation, the diagnosis of UESL was confirmed by the characteristic triad of spindle and stellate cells in a fibromyxoid background, pleomorphic multinucleate giant cells, and diastase-resistant PAS-positive eosinophilic globules (Fig. 4A and B). The immunohistochemical profile—diffuse vimentin and α1-antitrypsin positivity, variable CD68 and Bcl-2 expression, focal CKAE1/3, and negativity for lineage-specific markers including HepPar1, AFP, desmin, myogenin, and S100—is consistent with a primitive mesenchymal tumor of undetermined stem cell origin. The focal CKAE1/3 expression, though unexpected in a mesenchymal lesion, has been previously described in UESL and does not exclude the diagnosis. The absence of myogenin and Myo-D1 effectively excludes rhabdomyosarcoma, and negativity for CD117 and DOG1 excludes gastrointestinal stromal tumor with hepatic involvement.

The decision to proceed with upfront surgical resection without percutaneous biopsy was appropriate and consistent with current management principles. Biopsy of complex cystic or myxoid lesions carries a risk of tumor seeding along the needle tract, and the diagnostic yield in heavily myxoid tumors is low due to sampling of nonrepresentative gelatinous material. In this case, the lesion’s size (>8 cm), complexity, and high index of suspicion for malignancy on multimodal imaging collectively supported resection as both the diagnostic and therapeutic intervention. The patient underwent a right hemi hepatectomy via Glissonean and anterior approaches. The Glissonean approach enables extrahepatic control of hepatic inflow pedicles, and the anterior approach avoids tumor mobilization prior to parenchymal transection, minimizing the risk of intrahepatic dissemination.

Complete surgical resection (R0) is the cornerstone of treatment for UESL, and its importance in determining prognosis cannot be overstated. In a pooled analysis of 308 patients, Wu et al reported a 5-year overall survival of 65.8% in patients treated with surgery combined with chemotherapy, compared with significantly inferior outcomes in those undergoing surgery alone (1-year survival 93% vs 65%; 3-year survival 81% vs 51%). Adults demonstrate lower 5-year survival than children (48.2% vs 84.4%), likely reflecting delayed diagnosis and larger tumor size at presentation [[8], [9], [10],18,21].

The adjuvant chemotherapy regimen employed in this case—ifosfamide and epirubicin—draws from soft tissue sarcoma protocols. Emerging evidence suggests that ifosfamide-containing regimens may have superior efficacy in adult UESL compared to the vincristine/actinomycin D/cyclophosphamide regimens used in paediatric practice, though prospective data are lacking. Recurrence risk remains substantial in adults, with a median time to relapse of approximately 12 months, underscoring the necessity of close postoperative surveillance. The 12-month disease-free outcome observed in the present case is therefore an encouraging but not yet definitive result, and continued follow-up beyond the 24-month mark is essential.

The differentials considered at multidisciplinary team review—mesenchymal hamartoma, mucinous cystic neoplasm, and hepatic hydatid disease—represent the most clinically relevant entities in this demographic and are summarized in Table 1. In this patient, negative echinococcal serology and absent daughter cysts or peri-cyst calcification effectively excluded hydatid disease. Mucinous cystic neoplasm was considered but regarded as less likely given the patient’s age (typically middle-aged women) and right lobe location (MCN predominates in the left lobe/segment IV). The cross-modal imaging discordance, normal tumor markers, young age, and ultimately the histopathology together confirmed UESL as the diagnosis.

This case underscores that awareness of UESL’s radiological hallmarks—cross-modal discordance, enhancing septations, serpiginous peripheral vascularity, intertumoral hemorrhage, fluid-fluid levels, and restricted diffusion—is critical to avoiding misdiagnosis and facilitating timely multidisciplinary surgical planning.

This case illustrates that UESL should be considered in the differential diagnosis of any large, complex hepatic mass in a young adult, even in the absence of specific laboratory abnormalities or tumor marker elevation. The characteristic cross-modal imaging discordance—a solid or heterogeneous appearance on ultrasound contrasting with a predominantly cystic, septate morphology on CT and MRI—is the single most diagnostically useful feature of this entity and should prompt early multidisciplinary discussion. Awareness of this paradox is critical to avoiding the reported misdiagnosis rate of up to 23.5%. Summary characteristics of undifferentiated embryonal sarcoma of the liver (UESL).

In a pooled analysis of 308 patients, Wu et al reported an overall 5-year survival of 65.8%, with 70% for the partial hepatectomy subgroup and 78.9% for liver transplantation recipients [[6], [10], [19]]. For patients undergoing partial hepatectomy, paediatric age, radical resection, and combined chemotherapy were independent predictors of improved survival [7].

Adults, however, carry a higher recurrence risk than children, with a median time to relapse of approximately 12 months, underscoring the importance of close, protracted surveillance and prompt re-treatment at recurrence. This case adds to the growing literature supporting upfront resection without percutaneous biopsy and demonstrates a favorable 12-month disease-free outcome following R0 hepatectomy and ifosfamide-based adjuvant chemotherapy in a young adult with localized UESL [20].
