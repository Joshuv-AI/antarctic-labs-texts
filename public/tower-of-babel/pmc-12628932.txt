Assessing the clinical application value of SNP-array in fetal central nervous system malformations
Authors: Wei Li, Jiasun Su, Weiliang Lu, Xianglian Tang, Chaofan Zhou, Yueyun Lan, Yuan Wei, Limei Huang, Jingrong Luo, Shan Ou, Shuyin Tan, Shengkai Wei, Yaoyao Jiang, Jingyu Su, Liang Wang, Minpan Huang, Jingsi Luo, Peng Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSingle nucleotide polymorphism array (SNP-array) has been introduced for prenatal diagnosis. This study aims to evaluate the clinical utility of SNP-array in the prenatal central nervous system (CNS) malformations.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on 437 prenatal cases involving CNS malformations and other structural abnormalities detected by ultrasound. Samples were categorized into three groups: Single CNS malformation, Multiple CNS malformations, and CNS malformations with multiple system malformations. SNP-array and karyotype analysis were simultaneously performed on the Fetal samples. Comparative analysis of data was conducted using SPSS (χ2).ResultsSNP-array analysis of 437 samples revealed an overall abnormality detection rate of 19.0%, which was significantly higher than the 11.7% positive rate detected by karyotype analysis in 427 samples (χ2 = 8.797, P = 0.003). The positivity rates detected by SNP-array were 11.4%, 43.3%, and 63.0% in the above three groups, respectively, and these differences were statistically significant (χ2 = 83.247, P = 8.379×10− 19). The detection rate of clinically significant copy number variants (CNVs) (pathogenic/likely pathogenic, P/LP) was also statistically significant among the three groups (χ2 = 42.000, P = 9.127×10− 11). In pathogenic CNVs, dose-sensitive regions or genes that occur more frequently include the 4p16.3 terminal region, the 17p13.3 region, the 22q11.2 recurrent region, and DLL1, TGIF1, EBF3. We also did an analysis for pregnancy data of advanced maternal age (AMA) and found that the ratio of chromosomal abnormalities in samples was 18.8% tested by SNP-array. The postnatal follow-up data were analyzed. Compared to the group with normal chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) result and isolated CNS malformation, the proportions of termination of pregnancy (TOP)/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion (27.2% vs. 69.6%, P < 0.0001), liveborn infants requiring medical attention (6.3% vs. 21.7%, P < 0.01), infants being alive and well at birth (66.5% vs. 8.7%, P < 0.0001) were all statistically significant in the group with normal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation. The proportions of TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion (27.2% vs. 76.7%, P < 0.0001), infants being alive and well at birth (66.5% vs. 23.3%, P < 0.0001) were also statistically significant in the group with abnormal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation compared to the same group mentioned above.ConclusionsThis study evaluated the application of SNP-array in the prenatal CNS malformations in terms of detection of positive rates, advantages in prenatal diagnosis, AMA, genotype-phenotype correlations, pregnancy outcomes and follow-up data, which could assist in the clinical diagnosis and assessment of fetal CNS malformations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00843-5.Keywords: Central nervous system malformations, SNP-array, Prenatal diagnosis, Chromosomal abnormality

Background

Fetal CNS malformations are a common congenital abnormalities, with an incidence of 0.14–0.16% among live births and 3–6% among stillbirths [1]. It is second only to cardiac malformations in their frequency of occurrence [2]. There are many factors involved in the formation of the CNS, the most important ones are the proliferation of neural cells, neuronal migration, postmigrational cortical organization and connectivity. Abnormalities in these factors can lead to problems in the CNS: neural tube defects, holoprosencephaly, microcephaly or megalencephaly, lissencephaly, focal cortical dysplasias and polymicrogyria [3]. In addition, disorders of processes that regulate brain development in utero can cause specific congenital CNS anomalies [3]. CNS malformations can lead to developmental delay, psychomotor developmental abnormalities, intellectual, motor and behavioral disorders, seizures and even death in children [4]. It is strongly influencing the future of the children and their families. As CNS malformations account for a substantial proportion of miscarriages, stillbirths and infant deaths, prenatal detection of CNS malformations crucial for clinical counseling.

Ultrasound examination is used as the first choice modality to detect fetal CNS malformations. The accuracy of ultrasound detection varies from 92% to 99.7% for CNS abnormalities [5]. However, numerous studies have shown that CNS anomalies are not adequately characterized by ultrasound. Euro-fetus study suggests ultrasound is 88% sensitive for CNS malformations. When ultrasound examination is inconclusive or when a further investigation is required, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is used as a third-level diagnostic tool after prenatal ultrasound [6]. CNS abnormalities are the most common clinical indication for fetal MRI. It constitutes 80% of all examinations requests. The diagnostic accuracy of prenatal cerebral imaging with disagreement between pre and postnatal imaging being observed in about 9% of cases [7]. Fetal MRI has limitations in the early gestational age, such as sensitivity regarding the early detection of cortical dysplasia and diagnosis of Dandy-Walker malformation [6].

Fetal structural abnormalities are often caused by genetic factors and chromosomal structural abnormalities, so the integration of prenatal ultrasound and genetic examination technology is a critical approach for preventing birth defects. With the advancement of genetic detection technology, imaging detection technology has been assisted. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology and oligonucleotide microarray have been used to search for genetic variants of nervous system abnormalities, analyze genes related to embryonic development of theCNS, and analyze genotype-phenotype correlations for diseases of CNS malformations [8–11]. Karyotype analysis and CMA were routinely applied to prenatal diagnosis. While routine karyotype analysis is the “gold standard” for detecting chromosomal aberrations in prenatal diagnosis, CMA takes less time and detects more submicroscopic CNVs that trigger clinically relevant abnormalities [12]. When ultrasound diagnosis indicates fetal abnormality, but karyotype analysis is normal, we need to use CMA technology as the preferred detection method [12]. Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) has become the third generation of genetic markers, which refers to a single nucleotide variation at the same position in the genomic DNA sequence. SNP-array technology uses a special method to fix a large number of SNP site sequences on silicon chips, obtain a high-density SNP microarray (300,000 sites, more than 400 genes), and then obtain data results through sample incubation hybridization, laser scanning, software analysis, which is one of the common methods for chromosome chip analysis at present [13]. Due to its significantly higher resolution (25–50 times) compared to karyotype analysis, SNP-array enables the detection of microduplications and microdeletions across the entire genome [14]. Studies have demonstrated that incorporating SNP-array into prenatal diagnosis can enhance the positive diagnosis rate of normal karyotype samples by 3.6%, regardless of the indication [15]. Moreover, SNP-array requires a smaller sample size (as low as 50ng) compared to Karyotype analysis [14]. Given these advantages, SNP-array plays a crucial role in prenatal diagnosis. Furthermore, when integrated with traditional chromosome typing technology, this novel molecular genetics approach enhances the detection rate of fetal chromosome abnormalities while reducing abortion rates and providing a foundation for genetic counseling and prenatal diagnosis [16].

In this study, we retrospectively reviewed a cohort study of 437 pregnancies to evaluate the clinical utility of SNP-array analysis in prenatal CNS diagnosis and to explore the improvement of diagnostic yield with SNP-array compared to karyotype analysis. In addition, the positivity rate generated by SNP-array was found to be statistically significant among the three subgroups: Single CNS malformation, Multiple CNS malformations, and CNS malformations with multiple system malformations. The distribution of the AMA cohort across different ages and the pregnancy outcomes of the enrolled population in this study were also explored. By analyzing dosage sensitive regions and genes identified in positive cases, this study explored the correlation between genotype and phenotype, providing a reference for the clinical application value of SNP-array in prenatal CNS malformations (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Samples

This study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics committee of Maternal and Child Health, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, and written informed consent was obtained from each patient. All pregnant women who chose to participate in this study received genetic counseling and provided written informed consent before undergoing invasive prenatal test. A total of 437 samples, including chorionic villi, amniotic fluid, and cord blood, were collected and analyzed in our center from June 2014 to October 2022. The characteristics and indications for invasive prenatal diagnosis of all samples were listed in Table 1. The mean age of the pregnant women was 29.7 ± 4.98 years (range:17–46 years). This study was conducted in strict accordance with interventional prenatal diagnostic puncture technique and the management measures for prenatal diagnosis, and amniocentesis was performed under ultrasound guidance. Ten mL of the obtained 30 mL amniotic fluid was tested by SNP-Array, and 20 mL of amniotic fluid was analyzed by chromosome karyotype. Amniotic fluid samples taken for this study were centrifuged immediately after receipt to collect amniocytes. Villi samples were rinsed multiple times with normal saline and separated under an anatomical microscope using a disposable sterilized medical blade.

Karyotype analysis

The obtained amniotic fluid was routinely cultured, fixed, dissected, G-banding, staining and other steps. Thirty division phases were selected for each case, and 5 karyotypes were analyzed. If the karyotype was found to be chimera, 50 ~ 100 division phases were added for chromosome karyotype analysis. G-banded Karyotype analysis was performed for samples according to the principle of ‘An International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature, ISCN2013’ as described previously [17].

SNP-array analysis

The SNP-array analysis was conducted on the Illumina iScan platform. SNP-array were performed for 437 samples using a HumanCytoSNP-12 v2.1 DNA Analisis BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, California, United States), which contains approximately 3,00,000 SNP markers. Then genomic DNA of chorionic villi and amniotic fluid were extracted by TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH (BEIJING)CO.,LTD), and genomic DNA of cord blood was extracted by QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany). About 200 ng of genomic DNA was amplified, fragmented, denatured, hybridized on beadchip, single base extension and staining. Then SNP-array were scanned by iScan system. The data from the captured images were analyzed using Illumina’s KaryoStudio Module based on the reference human genome (hg19/GRCh37). CNVs were interpreted and classified as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variants of uncertain significance (VOUS), likely benign (LB) or benign (B), according to the standards and guidelines released by the American College of Medical Genetics [18]. Some databases were used as reference for classification, including ClinGen Dosage Sensitivity Map (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/dbvar/ clingen), ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), Database of Genomic Variants (DGV) (http://projects.tcag.ca/ variation), DECIPHER (http://decipher.sanger.ac.uk/), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) (http://www.omim.org), and literatures of PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Only P or LP CNVs and VOUS were reported based on the laboratory policy at the time. Microdeletions less than 200 Kb or microduplications less than 1 Mb in size classified as VOUS were not reported unless they contained genes known to be associated with disease. Additionally, loss of heterozygosity (LOH) with size greater than 10 Mb were reported.

Pregnancy outcome

The routine postnatal assessments were conducted either at birth or via telephone interviews facilitated by the hospital’s clinical follow-up center. Data collected included pregnancy complications, pregnancy outcome (TOP, miscarriage, preterm delivery and stillbirth), gestational age at delivery, birth weight/length, newborn assessment (infant alive and well or liveborn but requiring medical attention).

Statistical methods

IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analyses. Count data was expressed as percentages. The Pearson Chi-square test was used to compare the results between groups for categorical variables. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Data summary of single-nucleotide polymorphism array

A total of 437 prenatal samples, diagnosed with CNS malformations by prenatal ultrasound at Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, were enrolled in this study. These samples were successfully analyzed by SNP-array from June 2014 to October 2022, and 83 samples of which yielded 112 abnormal results (83/437, 19.0%), including 25 abnormal chromosome numbers (25/112, 22.3%), 16 LOH (16/112, 14.3%) and 71 CNVs (71/112, 63.4%) (Table 2). Among 25 anomalies with chromosome numbers, trisomy 13 were the most common type (12/112, 10.7%), followed by trisomy 18 (6/112, 5.4%), triploidy (3/112, 2.7%), trisomy 21 (2/112, 1.8%), trisomy 9 (1/112, 0.9%) and sex chromosome aneuploidy (1/112, 0.9%). Among 53 cases with CNVs, P CNVs were detected in 40 cases (40/437, 9.2%). The CNVs, classified only as LP or LP/VOUS, were found in 3 fetuses (3/437,0.7%), while CNVs with only VOUS were found in 10 fetuses (10/437, 2.3%). Seventy-one CNVs anomalies were identified in 53 cases, of which 1 CNV was detected in 35 cases, 2 CNVs were detected in 14 cases, 4 CNVs were detected in 1 case, 1 CNV with 1 aneuploidy were detected in 1 case, and 1 CNV with 1 LOH were detected in 2 cases.

Discrepancy between karyotype analysis and SNP-Array

Meanwhile, traditional karyotype analysis was performed on 427 samples (427/437, 97.7%) of our cohort (Table S1). Among 427 cases, 50 samples (50/427, 11.7%) had abnormal karyotypes, including 28 cases with abnormal structure (28/427, 6.6%), 19 cases with abnormal chromosomal numbers (19/427, 4.4%) and 3 cases with abnormity of chromosomal numbers and structure (3/427, 0.7%). There were 5 samples with chimeric abnormality in 50 cases of abnormal karyotypes (5/427, 1.2%). In 50 cases with abnormal karyotypes, 4 samples were not detected by SNP-array, including 2 cases with balanced translocation, 1 case with robertsonian translocation and 1 case with marker chromosomes.

In 377 cases with normal karyotypes, 33 cases (33/377, 8.8%) were detected with abnormal results by SNP-array, including 17 cases with P CNVs (17/377, 4.5%), 1 case with LP CNV (1/377, 0.3%), 9 cases with VOUS CNVs (9/377, 2.4%) and 7 cases with LOH (7/377, 1.9%) (Table 3). Among the 33 SNP-array anomalies, CNVs accounted for the largest proportion (27/377, 7.2%), followed by LOH (7/377, 1.9%). Parental verification was performed in 8 families. Parental analysis showed that five of these cases were de novo and three of these were inherited from father or mother. The CNVs of cases 20G06150, 2018G4786, 22G04659, 2016G0276 and 2017G7567 are de novo. The CNVs of cases 21G01127 and 20G10189 are maternal, the CNVs of case 20G04462 is paternal. Of the 13 cases classified as P or LP whose postnatal outcome had been investigated, eleven of the pregnant women chose to terminate the pregnancy, one fetus died during the perinatal period and one woman continued the pregnancy with a full-term live birth. Of the 9 cases classified as VOUS whose postnatal outcome had been investigated, seven of them chose to terminate the pregnancy and the other two continued the pregnancy with live birth. Of 7 cases with LOH, the outcomes of 5 fetuses were live births.

The combined utilization of karyotype analysis and SNP-array yielded a total positive detection rate of 19.4% (83/427). The positive detection rate by SNP-array alone, and karyotype analysis alone were 19.0% and 11.7%, respectively, and the difference between the two methods was statistically significant (χ2 = 8.797, P = 0.003).

Comparison of positive SNP-array results in different groups

Fetal CNS malformations were divided into 15 simple types depending on the phenotype (Table 2). In this study, the prenatal samples with CNS malformations were categorized into three subgroups according to ultrasonographic abnormalities: Single CNS malformation, Multiple CNS malformations, CNS malformations with multiple system malformations (Table 2). In the subgroup with single CNS malformation, forty-one cases were reported as positive results in 361 samples (positivity rate of 11.4%). A total of 57 chromosomal abnormalities were reported out of 41 positive reports. Among chromosomal abnormalities, CNV accounted for the highest proportion (34/57, 59.6%), followed by LOH (13/57, 22.8%), abnormal chromosome numbers (10/57, 17.5%). Among the cases with CNVs, P CNVs occupied the largest proportion (19/361, 5.3%). Septum pellucidum dysplasia was the most common anomaly (128/361, 35.5%) with 9.4% (12/128) chromosomal abnormalities, followed by agenesis of corpus callosum (38/361, 10.5%) with 5.3% (2/38) chromosomal abnormalities. In the subgroup with multiple CNS malformations, 13 cases were reported as positive results in 30 samples (positivity rate of 43.3%). Among the 13 positive reports, a total of 18 chromosome abnormalities were identified, including 17 CNVs (17/18, 94.4%) and 1 LOH (1/18, 5.6%). Among the abnormalities with CNVs, eight cases were classified as P (8/30, 26.7%), followed by 4 cases classified as VOUS (4/30, 13.3%), and one case classified as LP (1/30, 3.3%). In the subgroup with CNS malformations combined with multiple system malformations, 29 positive results were reported among 46 samples (positivity rate of 63.0%). Among 37 chromosome abnormalities of the 29 positive results, CNVs still occupied the highest proportion (20/37, 54.1%), followed by abnormal chromosome numbers (15/37, 40.5%) and LOH (2/37, 5.4%). Among the classification categories of CNVs, P was the most frequent category, accounting for 13 out of 46 cases (28.3%), while the remaining were VOUS (1/46, 2.2%). The difference in positivity rate among the three subgroups was statistically significant (χ2 = 83.247, P = 4.639×10− 19). In addition, the difference in detected rate of CNVs with clinical significance (including P/LP CNVs) among the three subgroups was also statistically significant (χ2 = 42.000, P = 9.127×10− 11).

SNP-array analysis was performed for a total of 69 pregnant women with AMA (≥ 35 years old), and the average age of this group is 37.33 ± 2.29 years (range: 35–46 years). The 69 samples with AMA were further divided into seven subgroups according to the maternal age (Table 5). The group of 35 years old was the largest age bracket (17/69, 24.6%), followed by groups of 37 years old (12/69, 17.4%), 36 years old (11/69, 15.9%), 38 years old (11/69, 15.9%), at least 41 years old (8/69, 11.6%), 40 years old (6/69, 8.7%), and 39 years old (4/69, 5.8%). No positive results presented in the group of 37 years old. With the increase of age, the number of cases tested showed a downward trend (Fig. 2). The rate of positive results in different age groups was illustrated in Fig. 3, revealing the positivity rate had a slight increasing trend between the ages of 35 and 40. Thirteen samples were detected with 16 chromosome abnormalities (13/69, 18.8%), the proportion of CNV was the highest (7/16, 43.8%) in reported chromosomal abnormalities, followed by Aneuploidy & Triploidy (6/16, 37.5%), LOH (3/16, 18.8%) (Fig. 4). Postnatal follow-up

Clinical follow-up was available for 310 cases (Tables S1). There were 159 cases of live and healthy births, 124 cases of TOP, 3 case of perinatal mortality, 1 case of spontaneous abortion, 1 case of stillbirth and 22 cases of live births requiring medical attention. Among these, the overall rates of infant being alive and well at birth, TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion and infant liveborn but requiring medical attention, in cases with normal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation, were 66.5% (149/224), 27.2% (61/224) and 6.3% (14/224), respectively. In the group with normal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation, the rates for the above three categories were 8.7% (2/23), 69.6% (16/23), and 21.7% (5/23), respectively. Meanwhile, there were 30 cases with abnormal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation. In this group, the overall rates of infant being alive and well at birth, and TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion, were 23.3% (7/30) and 76.7% (23/30) respectively. No cases were infant liveborn requiring medical attention. The rates for the above three categories in the group with abnormal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation were 3.0% (1/33), 87.9% (29/33) and 9.0% (3/33) respectively (Fig. 5).

The proportion of different pregnancy outcomes in 4 groups of different fetuses. In 310 cases with available clinical follow-up, there were 224 cases with normal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation, 23 cases with normal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation, 30 cases with abnormal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation, and 33 cases with abnormal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation. The group exhibiting the highest proportion of fetal outcomes categorized as “liveborn but requiring medical attention” was the group with “normal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation” (5/23, 21.7%). The highest proportion of fetal outcomes classified as “TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion” was found in the group with “abnormal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation” (29/33, 87.9%), which was also the highest among the four groups. The group displaying the highest proportion of fetal outcomes classified as “live and well at birth” was the group with “normal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation” (149/224, 66.5%)

Based on this, we summarized statistically significant comparisons between different groups. Compared to the group with normal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation, the rate of TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion was significantly higher in the group with normal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation (27.2% (61/224) vs. 69.6% (16/23), P < 0.0001). Additionally, the rate of liveborn infants requiring medical attention was also significantly higher in the latter group (6.3% (14/224) vs. 21.7% (5/23) P < 0.01). Conversely, the rate of infants being alive and well at birth was significantly lower in the latter group (66.5% (149/224) vs. 8.7% (2/23); P < 0.0001). Compared to the group with normal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation, the rate of TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion was significantly higher in the group with abnormal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation (27.2% (61/224) vs. 76.7% (23/30); P < 0.0001). However, the rate of infants being alive and well at birth was significantly lower in the latter group (66.5% (149/224) vs. 23.3% (7/30); P < 0.0001). Compared to the group with abnormal CMA results and non-isolated CNS malformation, the rate of infants being alive and well at birth was also significantly higher in the group with abnormal CMA results and isolated CNS malformation (23.3% (7/30) vs. 3.0% (1/33); P = 0.016). The statistical analysis did not reveal any significant differences between groups with normal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation compared to those with abnormal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation. Based on the above analysis results, it suggested that among CNS-associated malformations, both the severity of the deformity and outcome of CMA had a significant impact on pregnancy outcomes.

Discussion

To better illustrate the advantages of SNP-array in prenatal diagnosis of CNS malformation and its impact on pregnancy outcomes, this study included multiple comparison groups, organized into five comparative sections comprising nine distinct comparison groups. These comparisons encompassed: (1) the abnormality detection rate between SNP-array and karyotype analysis; (2) the abnormality detection rate and P/LP CNVs detection rate among fetuses with Single CNS malformations, multiple CNS malformations, and CNS malformations with multiple system malformations; (3) rates of TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion, liveborn infants requiring medical attention, infants being alive and well at birth between the group with normal CMA results and isolated CNS malformation and the group with normal CMA results and non-isolated CNS malformation; (4) rates of TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion, and infants being alive and well at birth between the group with normal CMA results and isolated CNS malformation and the group with abnormal CMA results and isolated CNS malformation; and (5) the rate of infants alive and well at birth between the group with abnormal CMA results and isolated CNS malformation and the group with abnormal CMA results and non-isolated CNS malformation (Figure 6). All nine comparisons showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) (Table S2-S6).

Main comparison map of the study. It mainly consists of 5 comparison items, which is totally composed of 9 comparison projects. And each comparison project has statistical significance (P < 0.05)

Interruption of normal neuroblasts migration is a cause of CNS malformation. The group of cellular migration disorders is composed of: heterotopias of the gray matter, schizencephaly, porencephaly, lissencephaly, agyria, macrogyria, pachygyria, microgyria, polymicrogyria, agenesis of the corpus callosum, and agenesis of the cranial nerves. Multiple CNS anomalies often coexist. Schizencephaly very often coexist with the most common CNS anomalies including partial or complete agenesis of the septum pellucidum (up to 70%), agenesis of the corpus callosum, cortical dysgenesis and polymicrogyria [19, 20]. Our data showed that septum pellucidum dysplasia accounted for the largest proportion (35.5%) among the case with single CNS malformation, follow by agenesis of the corpus callosum (10.5%), microcephaly (8.9%), arachnoid cyst (8.4%), and hydrocephalus (8.1%) (Table 2). It demonstrates that septum pellucidum dysplasia and agenesis of the corpus callosum frequently co-occur with other CNS anomalies and also manifest individually.

The study identified 83 cases out of a total of 437 cases (19.0% detection rate) using SNP-array, which exhibited reported abnormal results, including aneuploidy/triploidy, LOH, and CNVs. The detection rates of abnormalities are marginally higher than those reported in a previous systematic review (17.4%), possibly because the previous study included some ultrasound soft indices, such as “posterior cranial fossa widened” [21]. The published literatures reported a 3.4% detection rate of P CNVs using SNP-array in 535 cases of fetal CNS abnormalities [22], while a cohort of 539 fetuses with CNS malformations showed a 6.5% rate of P/LP CNVs [4]. In our study, it showed 9.2% detection rate of P CNVs and 9.9% detection rate of P/LP CNVs (Table 2), which were higher than those reported in previous publications. The inclusion of multiple soft indicators, such as lateral ventriculomegaly, widened posterior fossa, and choroid plexus cysts in previous studies, which were not included in our study, accounts for the higher detection rate of P CNVs or P/LP CNVs observed in our study. The correlation between the structural malformation indicators and P/LP CNVs was greater than that of single ultrasonic soft indicators.

Xiaorui Xie, et al. found that an abnormality rate of SNP-array appeared higher than traditional chromosome karyotype analysis, but this difference was not statistically significant (χ2 = 2.43, P >0.05) [21]. Jianlong Zhuang, et al. showed that CMA exhibited a 13.04% improvement over karyotype analysis in detection of chromosomal abnormalities [23]. The disparity in detection rates of chromosomal abnormalities between karyotype analysis and CMA exhibited statistical significance (χ2 = 8.797, P = 0.003) in our study. We observed that the disparity in positivity rates among the three subgroups (Single CNS malformation, Multiple CNS malformations, CNS malformations with multiple system malformations) was statistical significance (χ2 = 83.247, P = 8.379×10− 19), and this disparity also existed in detected rate of P/LP CNVs (χ2 = 42.000, P = 9.127×10− 11). The different of P/LP CNVs diagnostic yields between isolated group (isolated CNS structural abnormalities) and extra-CNS group (CNS abnormalities combined with other structural abnormalities) was statistical significance in reported literatures [4, 23]. The result of this study is consistent with the published conclusions, yet the disparity is even more statistically significant. The sample size used in our study is larger than that of the aforementioned two studies, and the samples with only abnormal soft indicators were excluded. Consequently, our findings possess enhanced credibility. The positivity rates and P/LP CNVs diagnostic yields increased when there were multiple CNS malformations. CNS malformations with multiple system malformations had the highest positive rate. This observation strongly suggests that complex B-ultrasound anomalies, even multi-system abnormalities, have a higher likelihood of detecting chromosomal abnormalities compared to single malformations or anomalies.

In the CNVs detected by CMA, there were many dosage sensitive regions and genes included in the ClinGen database, with a HI/TS score of 3. The most frequent regions were 4p16.3 terminal (Wolf-Hirschhorn syndrome) region, 17p13.3 (Miller-Dieker lissencephaly syndrome) region (includes YWHAE and PAFAH1B1), 22q11.2 recurrent (DGS/VCFS) region (proximal, A-B) (includes TBX1), 22q11.2 recurrent (DGS/VCFS) region (proximal, A-D) (includes TBX1) (Table 4). The syndromes (Wolf-Hirschhorn syndrome, Miller-Dieker lissencephaly syndrome, and chromosome 22q11.2 microduplication syndrome) corresponding to the regions mentioned above are related to neurodevelopment. The clinical phenotypes of these syndromes associated with growth and neurodevelopment encompass CNS malformations (such as lissencephaly, brain cortical malformations), and delayed psychomotor development, severe neurologic abnormalities, growth impairment, intellectual disability, learning disability, developmental delays, autism [24–26]. Additionally, the most frequent genes were DLL1, TGIF1 and EBF3 (Table 4). DLL1 deficiency resulting in haploinsufficiency disease is neurodevelopmental disorder with nonspecific brain abnormalities and with or without seizures. Most common features in the cohort were intellectual disability (12/14), variable brain malformations (11/15), autism spectrum disorder (6/14) and seizures (6/14) [27]. The cases we had observed involving DLL1 gene deletion exhibited central nervous system malformations, including cerebellar hypoplasia, reduced brain and cerebellum volume. These findings suggest that the deletion of this gene has a significant impact on phenotypes of patients. The deletion of TGIF1 resulted in the manifestation of holoprosencephaly [28], as observed in our case. Mutations in EBF3 resulted in hypotonia, ataxia, and delayed development syndrome, which is challenging to discern on prenatal ultrasound [29]. A patient in our study exhibited not only a single gene deletion but also deletions and duplications of other genomic fragments. Consequently, additional phenotypic features were observed on ultrasound, including septum pellucidum widening, intracranial cysts, and separation of the right renal pelvis. Furthermore, in the cases of this study, various dosage sensitivity regions or genes are involved, and most of them are associated with the CNS malformations. It serves an auxiliary and guiding function in B-ultrasound examinations when fetal CNS malformations are detected.

Among the positive findings, we think that most cases with clinical manifestations that can be attributed to detectable CNVs are those involving dosage-sensitive regions/genes with a score of 3, and are classified as P. We had summarized the CNVs identified in 15 cases, focusing on dosage-sensitive regions and genes within these CNVs that may contribute to the B-ultrasound phenotypes observed in these cases. Additionally, we described the clinical features associated with these dosage-sensitive regions and genes that closely resemble the ultrasound findings in our cohort. (Table 6). Comparative analysis shows that these genes or regions can lead to clinical phenotypes consistent with the B-ultrasound results of our cases, which further supports that the detected CNVs with dosage-sensitive regions or genes can better explain the clinical phenotypes observed in the cases. For some pathogenic cases, although dosage-sensitive regions or genes are present, the lack of clear phenotypic matching may be attributed to the incomplete manifestation of the phenotypes during fetal development or the synergistic effect of multiple other genes, making it difficult to attribute the observed clinical phenotypes to the duplication or deletion of specific genes. Cases classified as LP or VOUS typically do not contain dosage-sensitive regions or genes but instead harbor multiple known OMIM disease-associated genes. In some instances involving large chromosomal segments, a high number of genes are involved, making it challenging to establish a precise correlation between the detected genotypes and the actual clinical phenotypes.

Among the cases involving only CNVs and classified as VOUS, only five couples came for parental verification (Table 3). Among the cases, 2016G0276 and 2017G7567 were identified as de novo variations. In case 2016G0276, prenatal B-ultrasound diagnosed the fetus with anencephaly, and a 1.2 Mb deletion was detected in the p15p14 region of chromosome 2. According to the ClinGen database, none of the known OMIM pathogenic genes within this deletion segment were directly associated with anencephaly. However, the genes MDH1 and UGP2 were linked to autosomal recessive disorders, with clinical features including CNS abnormalities such as epileptic encephalopathy, intellectual disability, cortical atrophy, and hypogenesis of the corpus callosum. In case 2017G7567, four OMIM pathogenic genes were identified, but none were directly related to the ultrasound findings of spina bifida and cleft lip. Therefore, it was not possible to determine whether these genes could account for the observed ultrasound abnormalities. In cases 21G01127, 20G10189, and 20G04462, the CNVs were all inherited from the parents, who exhibited normal phenotypes. Specifically, cases 20G10189 and 20G04462 both carried duplications of 16p13.11 region. Previous literature has reported that clinical features associated with duplications in this region (chr16:15,511 − 16,291) include developmental delay, behavioral difficulties (such as autism, Asperger syndrome, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder), variable facial dysmorphism, limb abnormalities, and congenital heart defects [30]. However, the penetrance of such duplications is relatively low, estimated at approximately 7% [31]. Given that cases 20G10189 and 20G04462 did not show severe malformations and the CNVs were inherited from phenotypically normal parents, these findings further support the low penetrance of this genomic region and variable phenotype. Case 21G01127 presented with more severe phenotypes, and the CNV was maternally inherited. It is hypothesized that additional genetic variations, such as point mutations of genes, may contribute to the observed clinical features in this case.

In addition, during the analysis of the genotype-phenotype correlation, we identified specific CNVs that may be associated with cerebellar hypoplasia and septum pellucidum dysplasia. Among the CNS malformation types listed in Table 2, recurrent CNVs with similar genomic regions are rare, occurring only in cases of cerebellar hypoplasia and septum pellucidum dysplasia. In all three cases with cerebellar dysplasia, deletions were observed in the q27 region of chromosome 6, which encompasses the haploinsufficient gene DLL1. As previously discussed, haploinsufficiency of DLL1 can lead to variable brain malformations, including cortical dysplasia and cerebellar hypoplasia. The publications have reported fetuses with deletion in the 6q27 region involving the DLL1, presenting with cerebellar hypoplasia, ventriculomegaly, and corpus callosum abnormalities [32]. These findings suggest that in the presence of a prenatal ultrasound phenotype indicative of cerebellar dysplasia, targeted testing for deletions in the 6q27 region should be considered. Furthermore, two cases with septum pellucidum dysplasia exhibited duplications in the q11.21 region of chromosome 22, which includes the recurrent 22q11.2 (DGS) region, a dosage-sensitive region. The phenotypic spectrum associated with 22q11.2 duplications is generally mild and highly variable, ranging from apparently normal to intellectual disability / learning disability, delayed psychomotor development, growth retardation, and/or hypotonia. To date, septum pellucidum dysplasia has not been described in association with this syndrome in the literatures, which may reflect the broad phenotypic variability of this syndrome.

The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) defines an AMA pregnancy as one in which the mother is 35 years of age or older at the time of delivery. The pregnancy rate among older women has declined, while the risks of post-pregnancy abortion, fetal malformations, pregnancy complications, and other related issues have increased [33]. As the pregnancy rate declined, Fig. 2 illustrated a corresponding reduction in the sample size for invasive prenatal diagnosis due to CNS malformations with increasing maternal age. The positive rate exhibited a slight upward trend between the ages of 35 and 40 in this study, with no positive results observed at the age of 37 and the positive rate declined after the age of 41, which may be due to insufficient sample size of AMA (Fig. 3). However, this also suggests that the incidence of fetal abnormalities due to chromosomal anomalies increases with maternal age. Among the positive cases of AMA detected by SNP-array, most are aneuploidies or CNVs, with the majority of CNVs involving large segmental deletions or duplications. Therefore, the combined effects of multiple genes may contribute to fetal CNS malformations in pregnancies with AMA. Some CNVs encompass dosage sensitivity genes or regions. Deletion of TGIF1 is associated with holoprosencephaly, deletion of ZBTB18 is linked to mental retardation [34], and deletion of HNRNPU is associated with complex neurodevelopmental disorders [35]. Additionally, deletion of the terminal 1q43q44 region can result in severe mental retardation and limited or absent speech [36]. Duplication of the 17q12 recurrent (RCAD syndrome) region is also associated with CNS abnormalities, with clinical manifestations including microcephaly, intellectual disability, autism, and structural brain abnormalities [37].

Three hundred and ten cases with clinical follow-up were divided into 4 groups (Fig. 5). The group displaying the highest proportion of fetal outcomes classified as “live and well at birth” was the group with “normal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation” (149/224, 66.5%), while the proportion was lowest in the group with “abnormal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation” (1/33, 3.0%). Conversely, the group displaying the highest proportion of fetal outcomes classified as “TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion” was the group with “abnormal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation” (29/33, 87.9%), followed by “abnormal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation” (23/30, 76.7%) and “normal CMA result and non-isolated CNS malformation” (16/23, 69.6%), and the proportion was lowest in the group with “normal CMA result and isolated CNS malformation” (61/224, 27.2%). From our data analysis, it is evident that fetuses born alive and well at birth mostly occur when the CMA results are normal and there is only a single CNS malformation present. The incidence of TOP or miscarriage was highest among the group with the most severe outcomes, those exhibiting abnormal CMA results and non-isolated CNS malformation. Abnormal CMA results and non-isolated CNS malformation are important factors that can significantly increase the rate of TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion, as demonstrated by our data analysis. Similar to the adverse factors contributing to TOP, research demonstrated that cases with ultrasound malformations, consistent abnormal results between karyotype analysis and CMA/FISH, autosomal chromosomal mosaicism, or a high fraction of chromosomal mosaicism were more likely to result in a TOP [38]. At the same time, in cases of abnormal CMA, the incidence of TOP remained high, whether the CNS malformation was singular or complex (Fig.5). The results of CMA have a substantial influence on pregnancy outcomes.

In the aforementioned four groups, a number of fetuses were born alive and without apparent abnormalities at birth. Notably, this proportion was particularly high in the group with isolated CNS malformations, such as microcephaly, abnormal cavum septum pellucidum, arachnoid cyst. It has been documented that certain CNS malformations of cases normalized during the later stages of pregnancy or were not present anymore at postnatal follow-up. The interpretation of prenatal variants is complex due to the intricacies of neurologic embryogenesis. Certain CNS malformations are challenging to detect via prenatal ultrasound, and neurological structures continue to develop during the period when prenatal ultrasound examinations are conducted [39]. Therefore, subsequent ultrasounds may reveal changes.

Conclusion

Quality of prenatal ultrasound has significantly increased over the years, but characteristics such as intellectual disability are impossible to assess prenatally based solely on imaging. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate the results combining prenatal diagnostic techniques with prenatal ultrasound. CMA is mainly recommended in pregnancies with abnormal ultrasound findings. CMA can detect aneuploidy, microduplication, and microdeletion across the entire genome with higher resolution compared to karyotype analysis. This study further confirms that SNP-array outperforms karyotype analysis in improving the positive rate of detection, although both techniques should be used complementarily in clinical practice. The inclusion of ultrasound soft markers may potentially reduce detection rate, suggesting that the correlation between CNS structural malformations and chromosomal abnormalities is stronger than that between ultrasound soft markers and chromosomal abnormalities. Both abnormal CMA results and non-isolated CNS malformations are significant factors contributing to increased rates of TOP/perinatal mortality/spontaneous abortion. Additionally, we analyzed the genotype-phenotype correlation and identified several dose-sensitive genes or regions frequently associated with CNS malformations, which aids in the clinical genetic analysis of CNS. Our study has some limitations. Long-term follow-up is necessary to monitor for any emerging abnormal clinical phenotypes, especially neurodevelopmental disorders, as all postnatal cases are still young at the time of writing.
