Multifunctional vesicles in cancer therapy and theranostics: current advances and future challenges
Authors: Fatemeh Araste, Marzieh Akbarzadeh, Maryam Sadat Nabavinia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nanocarriers have revolutionized the field of cancer therapeutics by enhancing the delivery and efficacy of anticancer agents. This paper explores the various types of multifunctional vesicles utilized in cancer treatment, including non-biological vesicles such as liposomes and polymersomes, biological vesicles like extracellular vesicles (EVs), and hybrid vesicles that combine the benefits of both. We provide an in-depth analysis of their applications, highlighting the advantages they offer, such as targeted drug delivery, reduced side effects, and improved drug stability. Additionally, we discuss the potential pitfalls associated with these vesicles, including stability issues, manufacturing complexity, and toxicity concerns. Recent advancements and specific case studies are presented to illustrate the effectiveness of these vesicles in improving therapeutic outcomes. Our findings underscore the need for continued research to address the challenges and fully harness the potential of multifunctional vesicles in cancer treatment.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, with an estimated 12 million cancer-related deaths expected within the next two decades [1]. Despite significant advances in conventional therapies, including chemotherapy, radiation, hormone therapy, and immunotherapy, these treatments often suffer from limitations such as systemic toxicity, poor selectivity, and the emergence of drug resistance. To overcome these challenges, researchers have increasingly turned to nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems, particularly vesicle-based carriers, which offer more precise and effective cancer treatment options [2].

Vesicles are nanoscale, spherical structures composed of one or more lipid, polymer, or inorganic bilayers enclosing an aqueous core. Their unique architecture allows them to encapsulate both hydrophilic drugs (within the coreand hydrophobic drugs (within the bilayer), making them highly versatile carriers for therapeutic and diagnostic agents. Owing to their resemblance to biological membranes, vesicles exhibit excellent biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, which promotes prolonged circulation and facilitates targeted drug delivery [3].

The integration of multiple functionalities into a single vesicle platform has led to the development of multifunctional vesicles that can simultaneously perform several roles in cancer therapy. These vesicles are engineered to enhance targeted drug delivery, enable real-time imaging, and respond to specific biological stimuli for controlled drug release. Multifunctional vesicles improve drug targeting, minimize off-target toxicity, and allow for the precise modulation of therapeutic activity at tumor sites, thereby overcoming many of the limitations of traditional therapies. Additionally, these vesicles can be designed to perform theranostic functions, combining both therapeutic and diagnostic capabilities, which facilitates monitoring of the treatment’s effectiveness in real-time.

While multifunctional vesicles represent a significant advancement in cancer nanomedicine, their clinical translation remains hindered by challenges such as scalability, manufacturing complexities, biological interactions, and regulatory hurdles. Addressing these challenges requires a comprehensive understanding of vesicle design, production methods, and therapeutic applications.

In the following sections, we systematically classify and describe the major categories of multifunctional vesicles used in cancer therapy, including liposomes, polymersomes, natural and hybrid vesicles, and inorganic nano-vesicles. We then highlight key targeting strategies and stimuli-responsive release mechanisms that make these systems particularly effective. Therapeutic applications in chemotherapy, gene delivery, immunotherapy, and theranostics are also discussed. Finally, we address the main challenges in clinical translation and propose future directions for the development of vesicle-based cancer nanomedicine.

Classification of multifunctional vesicles

Various carriers, including dendrimersomes, liposomes, and polymersomes, provide safer and more efficient drug delivery systems, reducing dosage frequency and enabling controlled release [1]. These vesicular structures can adopt hollow, core–shell, spherical, or worm-shaped morphologies, encapsulating therapeutic agents within a hydrophilic inner core surrounded by either a single (unilamellar) or multiple (multilamellar) bilayers. Nanovesicles are broadly classified into inorganic, organic, hybrid, and membrane-based vesicles, each offering unique advantages for targeted drug delivery and imaging applications. These vesicles differ in composition (lipid-based, polymeric, proteinaceous), mechanism of action (passive vs. active targeting), and functions (drug delivery, imaging, theranostics).

Non-biological vesicles

Non-biological vesicles, which can be categorized into hard (inorganic) and soft (organic) nanoparticles, offer distinct advantages in drug delivery due to their tunable physicochemical properties, controlled release mechanisms, and enhanced stability in biological environments [7]. These synthetic carriers offer greater tunability in physicochemical properties, degradation rates, and multifunctionality compared to natural vesicles.

Inorganic nanovesicles

Among inorganic materials, metallic and metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs) have garnered significant attention in cancer therapy and imaging due to their unique optical, electronic, and catalytic properties [7]. These nanoparticles can act as multifunctional platforms, enabling photothermal therapy (PTT), photodynamic therapy (PDT), radiation enhancement, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). A key advantage of metal-based nanovesicles is their ability to degrade under physiological conditions, releasing metal ions and reactive oxygen species (ROS) that selectively exert cytotoxic effects on cancer cells. The tumor microenvironment, characterized by higher metabolic activity and oxidative stress, promotes an increased susceptibility to ROS-mediated apoptosis, making inorganic nanovesicles particularly effective in chemodynamic therapy (CDT) [14]. Recent advancements in gold nanoparticle (AuNP)-based vesicles have demonstrated promising therapeutic outcomes. In a recent study, PEG-PCL (polyethylene glycol–polycaprolactone)-coated AuNPs exhibited enhanced near-infrared (NIR) absorption, leading to improved photothermal conversion efficiency. This system amplified the photoacoustic response, resulting in complete tumor eradication in vivo without recurrence or systemic toxicity [15]. Additionally, studies have shown that hollow AuNP vesicles loaded with chemotherapeutic agents can achieve synergistic effects by combining PTT and controlled drug release, further enhancing treatment efficacy. Beyond gold, other metal-based vesicles, such as iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), have been widely explored for MRI-guided drug delivery [16]. Hybrid SPION-liposome vesicles have recently been engineered to provide dual imaging and therapy capabilities, allowing for real-time tracking of drug distribution and targeted treatment of deep-seated tumors [17].

Liposome

Conventional liposomes, without surface modification, represent the first generation of liposomal carriers. They are recognized by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) in the body and consequently removed from blood circulation. The second generation involves polymer-coated liposomes designed for extended plasma circulation. These liposomes use hydrophilic glycoproteins, synthetic polymers, oligo- and poly-saccharides as a covering shell. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is a commonly used polymer, featured in FDA-approved anticancer formulations such as Caelyx®, Myocet®, and Doxil® [4, 18]. The third generation includes active (targeted) liposomes, incorporating specific antibodies or ligands like sugars, lectins, and proteins on the vesicle surface. This enables precise targeting of tumor antigens or cancerous cell receptors [19]. Such targeted platforms contribute to overcoming drug resistance in cancer cells and enhancing therapeutic efficiency.

Polymersome

Polymersomes are self-assembled vesicles composed of amphiphilic block copolymers, offering superior stability, tunable membrane properties, and enhanced functionalization potential compared to conventional liposomes. Their unique architecture allows precise drug encapsulation and controlled release, making them promising candidates for anticancer therapy. Recent studies have highlighted their ability to improve drug retention and bioavailability, particularly through PEGylated polymersomes, which prolong circulation time and enhance tumor accumulation via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [20, 21]. Advanced polymersome formulations incorporating superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) and chemotherapeutics have demonstrated dual functionality—serving as both therapeutic and imaging agents—offering improved tumor targeting, MRI contrast enhancement, and reduced systemic toxicity in preclinical models.

Synthetic polymersomes

Polymersome construction typically relies on amphiphilic block copolymers, where the ratio of hydrophobic to hydrophilic segments dictates vesicle size, membrane thickness, and overall stability. Recent advancements have explored novel polymer architectures, including dendritic and hyperbranched structures, to achieve highly tunable drug release profiles and multifunctionality [20]. Unlike traditional block copolymers, these advanced designs provide greater control over degradation kinetics, responsiveness to stimuli (e.g., pH, redox, temperature), and co-delivery capabilities [22].

A key development in synthetic polymersomes involves the incorporation of responsive elements, such as disulfide linkages, which facilitate intracellular drug release in reductive environments. Similarly, photo-responsive and enzyme-degradable polymersomes have been engineered to enable spatial and temporal control over drug release, improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects [3].

PEGylation remains a critical strategy for enhancing the pharmacokinetics of polymersomes. Depending on grafting density and molecular weight, PEG chains adopt either a mushroom-like conformation or a brush-like structure, influencing steric stabilization and protein corona formation [23, 24]. Recent studies suggest that zwitterionic polymers, such as phosphorylcholine-based copolymers, may outperform PEG in reducing immune recognition and prolonging circulation time, offering an alternative strategy for next-generation drug delivery systems [25].

Triblock copolymers, configured as ABA or ABC triblocks, allow for precise compartmentalization of hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs within distinct vesicle domains. Recent research has demonstrated that ABC triblock copolymers incorporating polypeptide segments can mimic biological membranes, facilitating cell membrane fusion and intracellular delivery [26]. This biomimetic approach has shown promise in overcoming multidrug resistance (MDR) in cancer cells, offering a new direction for polymersome-mediated chemotherapy [27].

Natural polymersomes

Biopolymer-based polymersomes, constructed from polysaccharides, polypeptides, and naturally occurring polyesters, offer significant advantages in terms of biodegradability, biocompatibility, and reduced immunogenicity. However, their mechanical stability and batch-to-batch variability remain key challenges. Recent studies have addressed these limitations by incorporating cross-linking strategies, such as enzyme-mediated stabilization, to enhance the structural integrity of natural polymersomes in physiological conditions [28, 29]. A notable innovation in this field is the development of starch-functionalized polymethacrylic acid-polysorbate 80 (PMAA-PS80-St) terpolymersomes, which enable dual imaging and therapy. By chelating gadolinium ions (Gd3⁺), this system enhances MRI contrast while simultaneously delivering DOX for chemotherapy. Preclinical evaluations have reported a 3–fivefold increase in T₁ relaxation enhancement compared to Omniscan®, along with pH-responsive drug release, demonstrating significant potential for clinical translation [30].

Polysaccharide-based natural polymersomes, particularly those derived from chitosan and fucoidan (a sulfated polysaccharide from brown algae), have attracted attention for their immunomodulatory properties. These materials can selectively bind to tumor-associated receptors, facilitating targeted drug delivery and immune system activation [31]. Recent work has demonstrated that fucoidan-based polymersomes loaded with paclitaxel enhance tumor apoptosis while reducing systemic toxicity, highlighting their potential as next-generation theranostic platforms [32, 33].

Despite these advancements, challenges remain in ensuring consistent large-scale production and tuning degradation rates for clinical applications. Future research should focus on hybrid polymersomes that combine synthetic and natural components to optimize stability and functionality for biomedical applications.

Biological vesicles

Membrane-based vesicles

Natural vesicles, derived from biological sources such as exosomes, extracellular vesicles (EVs), virus-like particles (VLPs), and protein nanocages, offer unique advantages in drug delivery due to their high biocompatibility, innate targeting capabilities, and minimal immunogenicity. These vesicles are inherently equipped with surface proteins, lipids, and glycoproteins that facilitate cellular uptake, prolong circulation, and promote immune system evasion.

Exosomes, a subtype of EVs, originate from endosomal compartments and range from 30–150 nm in size. They play vital roles in intercellular communication by transferring proteins, RNAs, and lipids between cells. Their ability to naturally home to specific tissues makes them ideal candidates for targeted drug delivery, particularly in cancer therapy. Engineered exosomes loaded with chemotherapeutics, siRNA, or CRISPR components have demonstrated promising results in preclinical cancer models, enhancing delivery efficiency and reducing systemic toxicity [34–36].

Each EV preserves the molecular fingerprint of its parent cell or tissue, facilitating long-distance cell communication and regenerative induction [37]. While mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and dendritic cells (DCs) are primary sources of therapeutic EVs, they have also been derived from epithelial cells, macrophages, cancer cells, and others [38–40].

In addition to biocompatibility and biodegradability, EVs offer distinct advantages such as presenting cell membrane proteins in their natural configuration, intrinsic membrane fusion capability, and inherent signal transduction [41]. Engineered cell membrane vesicles (MVs), incorporating specific active proteins, have been developed through various techniques, including membrane extrusion, surfactant-mediated lysis, ultracentrifugation, or use of viral proteins to mimic viral budding [42–44]. MVs can be functionalized to display specific targeting ligands such as surface antibodies [45]. Several companies, including Codiak BioSciences, Evox Therapeutics, and Exosome Diagnostics, have actively developed EV-based platforms for therapy, targeted drug delivery, and diagnostics (Sect. "Case studies of vesicle-based therapies in clinical translation").

Protein nanocages

Inspired by self-assembling proteins and viruses that target host cells and release cargo in response to signals, protein nanocages are nano-sized structures ranging from a few nanometers to tens of nanometers [46]. Protein nanocages offer advantages such as biocompatibility, water solubility, and low toxicity, and they can be tailored for diverse architectures and applications. However, careful design is necessary to prevent nonspecific aggregation [48].

Non-viral protein cages (NVPNs)

NVPNs are nanocapsules derived from enzymes, the ferritin superfamily, eukaryotic vaults, and heat shock proteins, comprising self-assembling protein subunits ranging from 10 to 100 nm in size [49, 50]. For instance, ferritin proteins in the body can carry up to 4500 iron ions within their hollow structure, which can alternatively accommodate various therapeutic and imaging agents [51].

Viral protein cages: virus-like particles (VLPs)

Most viruses which are formed only by the capsid protein, can be considered as delivery vehicles with biodegradable and biocompatible moiety, monodispersed in size and shape, and in 10–200 nm diameters [52]. Non-pathogenic plant viruses like cowpea chlorotic mottle virus (CCMV) and cowpea mosaic virus (CPMV), as well as bacteriophages such as MS2, Qβ, and P22, are commonly utilized as sources for VLPs due to their safety profile in humans [53].

Hybrid vesicles

Hybrid vesicles are engineered nanocarriers that integrate the structural and functional advantages of both biological and synthetic components. These systems are designed to overcome the limitations associated with individual vesicle types, such as instability, low loading capacity, or limited targeting specificity. By combining liposomes with polymersomes, biological, or inorganic materials, hybrid vesicles can achieve enhanced biocompatibility, prolonged circulation time, and precise drug release profiles [54].

Recent advancements in theranostic applications have also demonstrated the ability of hybrid vesicles to serve both imaging and therapeutic purposes, providing real-time tracking of drug delivery while simultaneously exerting therapeutic effects [55].

One well-studied example includes liposome–biological membtane hybrids, which utilize the inherent targeting capabilities and immune evasion properties of biological membtane, alongside the structural tunability and drug-loading efficiency of liposomes. For example, red blood cell (RBC)-coated polymersomes improve immune evasion and prolong circulation time [56].

Similarly, liposome–polymersome hybrids have shown synergistic effects in encapsulation efficiency and stimuli-responsiveness, especially in systems involving pH-sensitive or redox-responsive block copolymers [57].

Hybrid vesicles can also incorporate inorganic elements such as SPIONs or gold nanoparticles within lipid or polymeric shells. These constructs enable multimodal imaging (e.g., MRI, photoacoustic) combined with therapeutic functions like photothermal or magnetothermal therapy [58, 59]. For example, SPION-polymer hybrids enable MRI-guided drug delivery and magnetic-targeting approaches [60].

Another promising class of hybrids involves virus-like particles (VLPs) modified with synthetic polymers or PEGylated lipid layers to enhance circulation time and minimize immune recognition. These biomimetic constructs are being explored for gene therapy, vaccination, and oncolytic delivery.

Despite their promise, challenges in hybrid vesicle production include reproducibility, scalability, and potential immunogenicity from combined components. Nonetheless, their modular architecture and multifunctionality position them as highly adaptable platforms for next-generation cancer nanomedicine.

Targeting strategies and functionalization

Targeted drug delivery systems are designed to enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity by directing therapeutic agents specifically to tumor tissues. In multifunctional vesicles, targeting strategies are typically categorized into passive and active approaches.

Passive targeting

Passive targeting relies on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect observed in solid tumors. Tumor vasculature tends to be disorganized, leaky, and poorly drained by the lymphatic system, allowing nanoparticles within a size range of 50–200 nm to accumulate selectively in tumor tissues. Vesicles such as PEGylated liposomes and polymersomes exploit this effect to passively concentrate chemotherapeutic agents at the tumor site without needing a specific targeting ligand [20, 21].

Active targeting

The enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, often exploited for passive tumor targeting, is not always reliable due to high interstitial pressure, inefficient vascular permeability, and rapid systemic clearance [61]. Active targeting involves modifying the surface of nanovesicles with ligands that can recognize and bind to specific receptors overexpressed on cancer cells. These ligands include monoclonal antibodies (e.g., anti-HER2), aptamers, peptides (e.g., RGD), folic acid, transferrin, hyaluronic acid, and carbohydrates. Ligand-receptor interactions facilitate cellular uptake via receptor-mediated endocytosis, improving intracellular delivery [62, 63].

Antibody- mediated targeting

Antibody-based targeting strategies utilize monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) or their fragments to achieve high specificity toward tumor-associated antigens [64]. Examples of FDA-approved mAb-conjugated systems include Herceptin®, Bexxar®, and Ibritumomab®. C argets such as HER2, EGFR, and PSMA have been widely explored. Antibody fragments like single-chain variable fragments (scFVs) and nanobodies enhance tumor penetration [65].

Abs are typically conjugated to nanoparticles using physical or chemical methods. Physical interactions rely on ionic absorption, hydrophobic forces, or Van der Waals forces, resulting in fast and simple conjugation. However, these interactions are often unstable, requiring higher Ab concentrations and posing a risk of Ab denaturation [66–68]. Covalent conjugation methods, including carbodiimide, maleimide, and click chemistry, ensure orientation and stability [69].

In a recent study, thiolated anti-MUC18 scFvs were covalently conjugated to liposomes loaded with gold nanocages. These immuno-liposomes achieved a 24-fold increase in cellular uptake by melanoma cells (A375) compared to normal cells, successfully enabling Raman spectroscopy imaging and near-infrared (NIR) photothermal therapy [70].

Advanced systems like biotin-avidin and SpyTag-SpyCatcher facilitate efficient and stable Ab attachment. A novel platform known as Avidin-Nucleic-Acid-Nano-Assemblies (ANANAS) utilizes double-stranded DNA filaments with high affinity for poly-avidin molecules, facilitating targeted drug delivery [71]. Notably, HER2-targeted exosomes engineered from HEK293T cells and CD133-targeted hybrid nanovesicles demonstrate precise tumor homing and therapeutic benefit [72].

Multifunctional CD133-targeted nanovesicles (CD133-grafted Cy5.5/PFOB@P-HVs) were developed with a core–shell structure containing perfluorooctyl bromide (PFOB) and a 3-mercaptopropyltrimethoxysilane (MPTMS) shell. Surface modifications with PEG, CD133 antibodies, and Cy5.5 enabled specific targeting of cancer stem cells (CSCs). In vivo fluorescence and ultrasound imaging confirmed nanovesicle accumulation at tumor sites, peaking 24 h post-administration. Under high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) irradiation, these targeted nanovesicles demonstrated significant tumor suppression [73].

Antibody-targeted nanovesicles remain a powerful tool for selective cancer therapy, with ongoing advancements in conjugation strategies and genetic engineering expanding their clinical potential.

Peptide and protein-targeted nanovesicles

Peptide and protein-targeted nanovesicles are a promising approach in cancer therapy, utilizing ligands that bind to specific receptors overexpressed on tumor cells. To reach a better effect, the stability of targeting peptides and proteins on NP’s surface should be considered not only during the conjugation process, but also during circulation in the body.

For example, the T7 peptide, designed to target the transferrin receptor (TfR), showed significantly improved targeting efficiency when its amino acid sequence was composed of D-amino acids instead of L-amino acids. This modification reduced enzymatic degradation, resulting in enhanced receptor binding and drug delivery [74].

Dual-targeting strategies are also frequently employed, using peptides or proteins to bind multiple receptors present on tumor cells or within the tumor microenvironment. Ligands such as transferrin, RGD peptides, and hyaluronic acid are commonly used due to their broad expression across various cancer types [62].

Protein-targeted nanovesicles

Proteins like transferrin are widely used for targeting nanovesicles to tumor cells. As a key regulator of iron transport, transferrin receptors (TfRs) are often overexpressed on malignant cells, making them ideal targets. Transferrin-modified nanovesicles exhibit enhanced cellular uptake, increased intracellular drug delivery, and improved therapeutic efficacy, particularly in drug-resistant tumors [75, 76].

A notable example is MBP-426, a liposomal formulation of oxaliplatin conjugated with transferrin, currently undergoing clinical trials for gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma. Its TfR-mediated uptake enhances oxaliplatin delivery to cancer cells, improving treatment outcomes [77].

Additionally, transferrin-targeted nanovesicles have been applied to induce ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent cell death. In a study by Xiao et al., cell membrane-derived nanovesicles loaded with Sorafenib (SOR) and Tf-Fe3⁺ were designed to trigger ferroptosis through dual mechanisms: increasing iron accumulation via the TfR pathway and inhibiting antioxidant defenses through Sorafenib. In vivo, these nanovesicles effectively suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival in a hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) mouse model [78].

Peptide-targeted nanovesicles

Peptides are frequently used to target nanovesicles due to their small size, stability, and high specificity. Compared to antibodies, peptides offer better tumor penetration, especially in challenging environments like the central nervous system (CNS) [79].

Tumor homing peptides (THPs) selectively bind to receptors on tumor cells or within the tumor microenvironment. Examples include Angiopep-2, Obatoclax, and Navitoclax, which have been conjugated to nanovesicles for targeted drug delivery. In a recent study, magnetic Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles loaded with Obatoclax (OBX) and coated with melanin were used for tumor suppression. Upon laser irradiation, this system achieved both mild hyperthermia and inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, leading to complete tumor removal in xenograft mouse models [80].

Cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), typically composed of short sequences of amino acids like lysine, ornithine, or histidine, facilitate the cellular uptake of nanovesicles. However, since CPPs lack specificity, they are often combined with THPs for targeted delivery [81, 82]. Notable examples include TAT, Penetratin, and VP22. In one study, DOXIL® liposomes conjugated with transferrin and octaarginine (R8) peptides demonstrated enhanced tumor accumulation, increased nuclear doxorubicin delivery, and improved tumor suppression compared to non-targeted formulations [83].

Cell-penetrating homing peptides (CPHPs) possess both cell-penetrating and tumor-targeting abilities. Peptides like TCP-1, Pep-42, RGD, and F3 are widely used for this purpose. In a recent study, genetically engineered AaLS nanocages displaying RGD4C and SP94 peptides effectively delivered chemotherapy drugs to cancer cells. These nanocages maintained structural stability while selectively targeting tumor cells [84]. Similarly, E2 nanocages conjugated with EGFR-targeting scFvs or folic acid successfully delivered anticancer drugs and fluorescent dyes, achieving efficient tumor imaging and therapy [85].

Peptide and protein-targeted nanovesicles represent a versatile and effective platform for precision cancer therapy. Further advancements in ligand engineering and dual-targeting approaches will continue to enhance their therapeutic potential.

Small molecule-targeted

Small molecules, typically defined as compounds with molecular weights below 500 Daltons, are widely used for targeting nanovesicles due to their excellent tissue penetration and receptor-binding properties [86]. Chemical inhibitor molecules may be more suitable for oral drug delivery formulations, offering enhanced bioavailability [87, 88].

Bind-014, a PLA-PEG nanoparticle conjugated with pentanedioic acid (ACUPA) targeting prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), has advanced to clinical trials for delivering docetaxel (DTX) to prostate cancer cells [89].

Aptamers, single-stranded nucleic acids with high selectivity and affinity for specific targets, are also frequently used for small molecule-based targeting. In one study, liposomes loaded with erlotinib and perfluorocarbon were conjugated with anti-EGFR aptamers. These targeted vesicles significantly reduced EGFR, phosphorylated-EGFR, and HIF-1α expression, induced apoptosis, and suppressed tumor proliferation in both in vitro and in vivo models [90].

Folic acid (FA), a vitamin essential for DNA synthesis and cell proliferation, is another widely used small molecule for targeting nanovesicles. Many tumors overexpress folate receptors, making FA-conjugated vesicles an effective therapeutic strategy [91]. For example, Dai et al. developed multifunctional theranostic liposomes combining photothermal therapy (PTT), photodynamic therapy (PDT), and chemotherapy. FA-conjugation enhanced their targeting capability and treatment efficacy [92].

Carbohydrate-based targeting is another promising approach. Monosaccharides and oligosaccharides act as signaling molecules and selectively bind to lectins on cancer cell surfaces. In a novel approach, ginsenosides were incorporated into the phospholipid bilayer of liposomes instead of cholesterol. Ginsenosides exhibit both anticancer and cancer-targeting properties, while also enhancing liposomal stability and protecting against phagocytosis. When combined with paclitaxel, these ginsenoside-liposomes achieved greater tumor suppression in gastric cancer models compared to commercial formulations like Lipusu® and Abraxane® [94].

The effectiveness of small molecule-targeted nanovesicles is influenced by factors like nanoparticle size, shape, and surface charge, which affect tumor penetration. Smaller nanoparticles, rod-shaped or filamentous structures, and PEGylated formulations generally show improved accumulation in tumor tissues [95, 96]. However, no universal design exists, and optimization remains essential for each therapeutic application.

Furthermore, systemic administration requires nanovesicles with stable structures and tumor-specific release triggers. To address this, multifunctional smart nanoparticles incorporating intrinsic or extrinsic stimuli-responsive systems are increasingly developed for cancer therapy [97]. Functionalization strategies

Surface functionalization not only enables targeting but also contributes to vesicle stability and bioavailability. PEGylation is a commonly used strategy to reduce opsonization and clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), thereby prolonging circulation time. More recently, zwitterionic and stealth coatings have been explored as alternatives to PEG to avoid immune recognition [123].

Ligand conjugation can also be designed to be stimuli-responsive. For instance, pH-sensitive linkers or disulfide bonds allow ligand detachment or vesicle disassembly in the acidic or reductive environment of the tumor. This smart design adds another layer of specificity and control to drug release.

Integration of imaging agents—such as fluorophores, MRI contrast agents, or radionuclides—into targeted vesicles has enabled image-guided therapy. These theranostic vesicles facilitate real-time tracking of biodistribution and treatment monitoring [17, 62, 63].

Stimuli-responsive and smart release systems

The development of stimuli-responsive vesicles enables precise spatiotemporal control over drug release, enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects. These smart vesicle systems are engineered to respond to specific internal (e.g., pH, redox, enzymes) or external (e.g., temperature, light, magnetic fields) stimuli characteristic of the tumor microenvironment or applied externally.

Smart release of cargo and enhanced trigger systems

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is characterized by hypoxia, acidity, elevated redox potential, and overexpression of specific enzymes, which can serve as endogenous stimuli to trigger drug release from nanocarriers. For instance, pH-sensitive vesicles undergo structural changes in acidic environments, leading to controlled drug release within tumors. Similarly, glutathione-responsive nanocarriers leverage tumor-specific redox gradients to trigger drug release in cancer cells [124].

In addition to internal cues, external stimuli such as temperature, ultrasound, magnetic fields, and light irradiation have been employed to enhance drug penetration and precision delivery. For example, photo-responsive nanovesicles release their therapeutic cargo upon exposure to near-infrared (NIR) light, allowing for spatial and temporal control over drug release [125]. Magnetic field-sensitive vesicles, incorporating superparamagnetic nanoparticles, can be remotely activated for targeted hyperthermia and controlled drug release [126].

By integrating smart release mechanisms, multifunctional vesicles improve tumor-specific drug accumulation, therapeutic efficacy, and reduced systemic toxicity, making them a promising strategy for precision oncology.

Endogenous triggers

Ion and pH responsiveness

The acidic nature of the tumor microenvironment (pH ~ 6.5–6.8) and even lower pH in endosomes and lysosomes (pH ~ 5–6) can be exploited for controlled drug release [127]. pH-sensitive vesicles incorporate materials such as poly(histidine), poly(β-amino esters), or acid-labile linkages that undergo conformational changes, swelling, or bond cleavage in acidic conditions, resulting in drug release [128].

Other pH-responsive vesicle systems have been developed for gene therapy and combination treatments. Self-assembled virus-like particles (VLPs), loaded with doxorubicin (DOX) and polyacrylic acid (PAA), relied on pH-triggered charge alterations to achieve controlled drug release in tumor tissues [130].

Hybrid vesicles integrating poly(methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine) (PMPC) and poly(aspartic acid) demonstrated dual responsiveness to pH and glutathione (GSH), providing a versatile theranostic platform for drug delivery [131].

Similarly, Gd-Zn-Cu-In-S/ZnS quantum dots (GZCIS/ZnS QDs) functionalized with EpCAM DNA aptamers effectively suppressed tumor growth in in vitro, in vivo, and ex vivo models [132].

Over the past decade, pH-responsive nanoparticles have shown great promise in advanced applications such as immunotherapy and photothermal therapy. However, only a limited number have successfully transitioned into clinical trials due to challenges in clinical efficacy, safety, and large-scale manufacturing [133]. A major challenge is the complexity of the in vivo tumor microenvironment, where low stimulus levels may impair the efficiency of pH-sensitive drug release [134]. Furthermore, some acid-sensitive chemical bonds require extended cleavage times, slowing down the release process and reducing therapeutic effectiveness [135].

To improve clinical translation, early-stage nanoparticle design must carefully consider the specificity of pH responsiveness, ensuring that the transition pH allows for selective activation in tumor tissues while sparing normal cells [136]. In some cases, developing a library of nanoparticles with varying pH thresholds could optimize responsiveness for different tumor microenvironments [131]. Additionally, optimizing response times is crucial for improving drug delivery precision and molecular imaging at tumor sites [137].

Polyion complex (PIC) vesicles, composed of poly(aspartic acid) and poly(aminopentyl-aspartamide) blocks, were engineered with an outer polyethylene glycol (PEG) layer featuring amidic crosslinks. These vesicles encapsulated FITC-Dex and exhibited a high ionic density, enabling controlled deformation and drug release in response to environmental ion fluctuations. The degree of crosslinking played a critical role in modulating cargo release while maintaining nanoparticle stability during lyophilization, centrifugation, and circulation in the bloodstream [138].

Overcoming these barriers will be key to translating pH-responsive NPs from experimental systems to clinical applications, unlocking their full potential in precision cancer therapy and diagnostics.

Redox responsiveness

Cancer cells exhibit elevated levels of glutathione (GSH) and reactive oxygen species (ROS), making them ideal targets for redox-responsive vesicles [139, 140]. Disulfide, diselenide, and tellurium-based linkages are commonly employed to enable site-specific drug release in the tumor microenvironment [141].

For example, chlorin e6 (Ce6)-conjugated vesicles with disulfide linkers demonstrated glutathione-triggered activation of photodynamic therapy (PDT), preventing premature activation during circulation while ensuring tumor-specific light-triggered cytotoxicity [142] (Fig. 5). Although disulfide linkages are commonly used for redox-responsiveness, diselenide bonds are reported to be more susceptible to oxidation [145]. Moreover, diselenide-contaning linkages are sensitive to both oxidants and reductants [146]. Diselenide-containing micelles, loaded with DOX and stabilized through zinc coordination, exhibited dual responsiveness to pH, GSH, and H2O2, ensuring efficient drug release in oxidative tumor conditions [147]. In another study, metal–organic framework (MOF)-based vesicles with a porphyrin zirconium (PCN-224) core and a diselenide-linked polymer shell enabled laser-induced ROS generation, leading to structural disruption and targeted DOX release for combined chemotherapy and PDT [148].

Additional ROS-cleavable linkages, such as oxalate esters, arylboronic esters, and ferrocenyl derivatives, have also been incorporated into polymeric vesicles to achieve controlled drug release [149, 150]. In another approach, nanovesicles conjugating PEG to hydrophobic corrole (Cor) via disulfide bonds demonstrated a tumor-triggered transformation from photothermal therapy (PTT) to PDT. Upon GSH exposure, vesicle disassembly restored Cor fluorescence and photodynamic activity, leading to precise, stage-specific tumor ablation in vitro and in vivo [152]. Similarly, yolk-shell nanovesicles with PEG-poly(propylene sulfide)-PEG cores responded to intracellular GSH, releasing a USPIO-PAA-DOX complex and enhancing T1 MRI contrast for image-guided therapy [143].

Although redox-responsive vesicles show significant promise, further research is needed to improve their clinical translation. Expanding the application of diselenide bonds, succinimide-thioether linkages, and platinum conjugation beyond micelles and nanogels into liposomal systems could enhance their therapeutic potential [153]. Continued advancements in redox-sensitive nanocarriers will be essential for developing more precise and effective cancer therapies.

Enzyme responsiveness

Enzyme-responsive nanoparticles (NPs) leverage the overexpression of specific enzymes in cancer cells to achieve targeted drug release. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), a family of endopeptidases involved in tumor progression and metastasis, are frequently exploited for site-specific drug delivery [154, 155].

For example, Torchilin et al. designed multifunctional liposomes with dual PEG layers: a short PEG chain conjugated to DSPE and TAT peptides, masked by a long PEG chain linked to an MMP-2-cleavable peptide and monoclonal antibodies. Upon exposure to MMP-2 in the tumor microenvironment, the protective PEG layer was cleaved, exposing TAT for enhanced cellular uptake. Another enzyme-responsive strategy involved MMP-2-cleavable vesicles composed of DOPC, a GSH-sensitive oxaliplatin prodrug (HOC), and PEG conjugated to Pyropheophorbide a (PPa) via a heptapeptide linker. These vesicles accumulated at tumor sites, underwent MMP-2-mediated cleavage for deeper penetration, and subsequently activated the prodrug through endogenous GSH. Incorporating the JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib (Rux) further enhanced immunotherapy by preventing chemoradiotherapy-induced PD-L1 upregulation, promoting tumor regression through immunogenic cell death and T-cell activation [161].

Enzyme-responsive drug delivery systems have been developed for a variety of cancer-specific enzymes, including hyaluronidase ([162], azoreductase [163], urokinase plasminogen activator [164], serine protease (as prostate-specific antigen) [165], elastase [166] and glycosidase [167]. These systems demonstrate strong potential for improving tumor targeting, reducing off-target effects, and enhancing therapeutic outcomes.

However, challenges remain in optimizing enzyme-responsive NPs for clinical translation. Incomplete control over drug release, complex synthesis methods, and high production costs hinder large-scale application [168]. Addressing these limitations will be essential for advancing enzyme-responsive nanocarriers toward more effective and personalized cancer treatments.

Exogenous triggers

Temperature

Temperature-responsive drug delivery systems utilize both intrinsic and extrinsic stimuli to achieve localized drug release. Tumor cells often exhibit elevated temperatures of 40–42°C due to increased metabolic activity, making this a suitable endogenous trigger. Additionally, external thermal stimulation, including mild hyperthermia (42°C) or thermal ablation (above 50 °C), can enhance drug release and tumor cell destruction [169, 170].

One example of temperature-triggered drug delivery involves cationic liposomes containing ammonium bicarbonate. Upon exposure to elevated temperatures, ammonium bicarbonate decomposes into CO₂, forming gas bubbles that disrupt the liposomal membrane and release the drug. These CO₂ bubbles also generate contrast for imaging applications, demonstrating theranostic potential in H460 cancer cells [171].

Multifunctional thermoresponsive liposomes have also been developed by conjugating folate for targeting and gadolinium chelates for MRI contrast. Co-encapsulating DOX and indocyanine green, these liposomes demonstrated triple imaging modes (fluorescence, photoacoustic, and MRI) and enhanced therapeutic efficacy upon near-infrared irradiation-induced temperature elevation [92].

A prominent clinical example is ThermoDox®, a lyso-thermosensitive liposomal doxorubicin formulation developed by Celsion Corporation. Designed to release DOX at temperatures around 40 °C, ThermoDox® has undergone phase III clinical trials (HEAT study) in combination with radiofrequency ablation (RFA) for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [172]. Formulated using dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC) lipids, which exhibit a phase transition at 41.58°C, the liposomes ensure temperature-controlled drug release. Incorporating additional lipids like distearoylphosphatidylcholine (DSPC) further fine-tunes the release profile [173–175].

Preclinical models combining ThermoDox® with high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) demonstrated a 26.7-fold increase in tumor drug uptake [176]. While clinical trials showed no significant overall survival benefit compared to RFA alone, post-hoc analysis suggested potential benefits for specific HCC patient subgroups. Clinical trials have evaluated Thermodox® in combination with RFA for liver cancer [177–180].

In temperature-sensitive polymersomes, the lower critical solution temperature (LCST) or upper critical solution temperature (UCST) of the polymer regulates drug release. Below the LCST, hydrophilic interactions stabilize the structure, while temperatures above the LCST induce collapse and drug release. Polymers like poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAm) are commonly used, with their LCST adjustable through monomer composition and molecular weight [181]. For example, PNIPAm-DOX hydrogels encapsulated within PEG-PTMBPEC polymersomes demonstrated controlled DOX release in response to temperature, reducing tumor growth and toxicity in vivo [182].

Thermosensitive virus-like particles (VLPs) have also been explored. Thong et al. developed VLPs from Macrobrachium rosenbergii nodavirus (MrNVLPs), loaded with DOX and functionalized with folic acid for tumor targeting. These particles exhibited temperature-triggered drug release upon heating from 37° to 43 °C [183].

Inorganic nanoparticles offer another non-invasive thermal approach. Fe2O3 superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) encapsulated in mesoporous silica and coated with PEG were embedded within thermo-responsive polymersomes composed of PNIPAm and PNHMA. Subjected to an alternating magnetic field (AMF), the magnetic nanoparticles generated localized heat, triggering both hyperthermia and DOX release. This combined approach significantly suppressed tumor growth in animal models [184].

Hyperthermia not only facilitates drug release but also enhances vascular dilation and endothelial permeability, increasing nanocarrier accumulation at the tumor site [185]. Furthermore, mild hyperthermia can modulate the immune response, inhibit DNA repair, and sensitize tumor cells to radiation and chemotherapy [186–188].

However, precise temperature control remains a challenge in temperature-responsive drug delivery. Variability in heat diffusion within tumors can create intermediate zones where temperatures are insufficient for complete ablation. While these regions may not reach the threshold for tumor cell death, they can still induce vascular shunting, reducing blood flow and hindering drug delivery [189]. In the case of ThermoDox®, heterogeneous temperature distribution can result in undertreated tumor areas where limited heat prevents effective drug release. Although the total drug dose at the target site may remain consistent across different heating protocols, uneven temperature gradients can significantly impact drug distribution within the tumor. This lack of uniformity can impede the release of ThermoDox® and restrict its therapeutic efficacy [190, 191]. Furthermore, temperatures in intermediate zones that are too low for ablation may lead to slower drug release from thermosensitive liposomes. Vascular shunting in these regions can further disrupt drug transport, limiting access to viable tumor cells [170].

Based on available evidence, it seems that thermal ablation is not the best heating technique to fully utilize the clinical potential of thermosensitive-mediated drug delivery. Instead, combining thermosensitive vesicles with mild hyperthermia seems more effective for drug delivery [192, 193].

In conclusion, temperature-responsive drug delivery systems offer a promising strategy for enhancing cancer treatment. Mild hyperthermia not only facilitates targeted drug release but also improves the efficacy of chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Further research focused on optimizing heat distribution and integrating thermoresponsive carriers into multimodal treatment approaches will be crucial for maximizing their clinical impact.

Ultrasound

Ultrasound has emerged as a versatile tool in cancer therapy, offering applications in imaging, drug release, cytotoxic radical production, and hyperthermia. In 1989, Dr. Langer’s team first demonstrated the use of ultrasound to trigger the localized degradation of polymeric drug carriers for targeted release [194]. More recently, ThermoDox® has been explored in the TARDOX phase I clinical trial, combining ultrasound-induced hyperthermia with temperature-sensitive drug delivery for hepatocellular carcinoma [195, 196].

Ultrasound-responsive vesicles have been designed using various materials to achieve dual-functionality for therapy and imaging. For instance, polymersomes composed of polyethylene oxide-poly(diethyl aminoethyl methacrylate-tetrahydrofuranyl oxyethyl methacrylate) (PEO-P(DEA-TMA)) encapsulated both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. In acidic environments, protonation and crystallization of the polymersome core caused shrinkage, followed by complete disassembly upon ultrasound exposure, facilitating controlled drug release [197].

In another study, double-layered PLGA polymersomes with Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles embedded in the bilayer and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in the hydrophilic core demonstrated effective tumor treatment. Upon ultrasonication, H₂O₂ was released and reacted with Fe₃O₄, generating hydroxyl radicals (•OH) via the Fenton reaction. This not only enabled MR imaging through Fe₃O₄ but also imparted therapeutic effects. Significant tumor suppression was achieved in xenograft-bearing mice within just five minutes of sonication [198].

Ultrasound also induces thermal ablation and cavitation through the formation and collapse of microbubbles. Multifunctional nanovesicles composed of poly(lactic acid) (PLA) and surface-modified with PEGylated gold nanoshells were loaded with perfluorooctyl bromide (PFOB) and superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs). Micro- and nanobubbles, particularly lipid-based bubbles, are highly effective for ultrasound-triggered drug delivery. These bubbles can respond to both low and high ultrasound frequencies and achieve targeted drug release through stable or inertial cavitation [200, 201]. Lipid-shelled nanobubbles containing gaseous cores of air, nitrogen, sulfur hexafluoride, or perfluorocarbons are often combined with drug-loaded vesicles. For example, doxorubicin (DOX)-loaded perfluoropentane (PFP) nanobubbles encapsulated in PEG-PLA vesicles exhibited temperature- and ultrasound-responsive behavior. Upon exposure to ultrasound, the PFP vaporized, enhancing ultrasound visibility and promoting localized DOX release [202].

n a dual-modality study, Şanlıer et al. connected pazopanib and pemetrexed to amine-modified magnetite nanoparticles (NPs) using a peptide linker. The NPs were encapsulated in liposomal nanobubbles, which were guided to the tumor site using an external magnetic field. Ultrasound exposure then triggered the precise release of both drugs. This system demonstrated notable drug delivery accuracy in in vitro, in vivo, and ex vivo models [203].

Hybrid ultrasound-responsive vesicles have also shown significant promise. Researchers combined erythrocyte membrane-derived nanovesicles with DOX-loaded cationic liposomes containing Hematoporphyrin Monomethyl Ether (HMME), a sonosensitizer. Upon ultrasound exposure, ROS generation oxidized unsaturated phospholipids, inducing membrane disruption and controlled drug release. Incorporating folate-targeting groups further enhanced tumor-specific delivery, resulting in superior tumor suppression in H22 tumor-bearing mice [204].

In another innovative approach, a nano-sonodynamic therapy (SDT) platform was developed using liquid fluorocarbon perfluorohexane (PFH). Vesicles were formed from an amphiphilic cationic derivative of heptadecafluorononylamine polyasparagine (C9F17-PAsp-(DET)) and PEG-conjugated protoporphyrin IX (PEG-PpIX). PFH acted as both an oxygen carrier and ultrasound contrast agent, enhancing ROS generation during SDT. This platform achieved effective tumor growth inhibition in both oxygen-preloaded and non-preloaded mice [205].

It is worth noting that while ultrasound-responsive drug delivery systems are still in their early stages compared to traditional drug delivery methods, they hold substantial potential for clinical application. Their ability to provide site-specific drug activation, minimize off-target effects, and enable real-time monitoring makes them valuable tools in cancer therapy. Further research and optimization of ultrasound activation strategies are expected to expand their clinical impact, offering safer and more effective cancer treatments.

Electric field

Electric field-responsive nanoparticles (NPs) offer a promising approach for localized drug delivery, reducing both the required drug dosage and associated side effects [206]. Electro-active materials, characterized by a substantial presence of ionizable groups, undergo reformation or ion release in response to dielectric forces.

Kalia et al. developed electro-responsive liposomes composed of cationic dioleoyl-trimethylammonium-propane (DOTAP) iontosomes and anionic dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-phospho-rac-glycerol sodium (DPPG) reverse micelles. Designed for the buccal delivery of cisplatin and docetaxel, these NPs responded to an iontophoresis-driven electric field by elongating and enhancing cellular penetration. In vitro studies showed a 67.7-fold and 56.6-fold increase in cisplatin and docetaxel deposition, respectively, following a 10-min electrical application [207].

In another study, Ge et al. constructed conductive polypyrrole-based NPs loaded with fluorescein for imaging and daunorubicin for therapy. These NPs were embedded within a temperature-sensitive PLGA-PEG-PLGA hydrogel. Upon reaching body temperature, the hydrogel transitioned from a sol to a gel state, preventing premature drug release. Controlled release was achieved by applying a voltage for 40 s daily. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed effective drug retention and therapeutic efficacy [208]. Despite their advantages, electric field-responsive systems face challenges, including the risk of cell damage and limited penetration in deep tissues [134]. Addressing these limitations through further research and technological advancements will be essential for expanding their clinical applications in cancer therapy.

Therapeutic and theranostic applications

Multifunctional vesicles have emerged as powerful platforms for delivering a variety of therapeutic agents in cancer treatment. Their unique architecture allows for simultaneous encapsulation of hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs, targeted delivery, and responsiveness to tumor-specific stimuli. This section highlights key applications of multifunctional vesicles in chemotherapy, gene therapy, phototherapy, immunotherapy, and combination therapy.

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy remains a frontline treatment in oncology, but its success is limited by systemic toxicity, poor bioavailability, and drug resistance. Multifunctional vesicles have addressed these challenges by encapsulating chemotherapeutics in nanocarriers that offer controlled release and tumor-targeted delivery [7].

Liposomal formulations such as Doxil® (PEGylated DOX-liposomes) and Myocet® have extended drug circulation time and reduced cardiotoxicity in breast cancer and Kaposi sarcoma [4, 18]. Similarly, polymersomes made of PEG-PCL or PEG-PLA copolymers have demonstrated enhanced tumor accumulation via the EPR effect and better stability in circulation [217].

Active targeting improves intracellular delivery by exploiting tumor-specific ligands such as folate, transferrin, and antibodies. Moreover, stimuli-responsive designs, including pH- and redox-sensitive linkers (e.g., disulfide bonds), allow for tumor-specific drug release. For example, disulfide-stabilized vesicles selectively release their content in the reductive environment of tumor cells, minimizing off-target toxicity.

Recent advancements include dual- or multi-drug delivery systems, in which hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs are co-loaded to produce synergistic effects. One study employed a PEGylated liposome co-loaded with erlotinib (EGFR-TKI) and perfluorocarbon (oxygen supplier) to overcome tumor hypoxia and enhance apoptosis via EGFR pathway inhibition [90].

Another example involves dual DOX and verteporfin-loaded polymersomes, designed for combined chemotherapy and photodynamic therapy. These systems demonstrated superior tumor suppression through complementary mechanisms of DNA damage and ROS generation [218].

Other multifunctional vesicles have delivered cocktails such as DOX/cisplatin/5-FU or DOX/paclitaxel, achieving enhanced therapeutic outcomes by simultaneously targeting different pathways involved in tumor growth and resistance [219].

Gene therapy

Nucleic acid therapeutics face delivery challenges such as degradation, poor uptake, and immune recognition. Vesicles provide a non-viral, biocompatible platform to deliver siRNA, miRNA, mRNA, and CRISPR/Cas9 systems to tumor cells [8–10]. Despite its promise, clinical translation is hindered by the instability of nucleic acids in biological fluids, poor cellular uptake, and endosomal entrapment.

A multifunctional hybrid vesicle composed of PCL-PEI with a poly-L-glutamic acid (PGA) shell was developed for the co-delivery of dabrafenib and the immune-regulating microRNA miR-200c. The cationic PEI enabled efficient electrostatic loading of miR-200c, and the PEGylated PGA shell facilitated pH- and redox-responsive release. Phototherapy (PTT/PDT)

Phototherapy uses light to activate photosensitizers or thermal agents for tumor ablation. Phototherapy includes photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT), PDT utilizes photosensitizers (PS) that generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon exposure to light, while PTT employs near-infrared (NIR) absorbing agents to induce localized hyperthermia for tumor ablation [222–224]. Nanocarriers improve phototherapy by increasing solubility, controlled release of photosensitizers or thermal agents, improved tumor accumulation, and reduced damage to healthy tissues [92, 225].

Several multifunctional vesicle-based platforms integrate phototherapy with imaging or chemotherapy. For instance, folic acid-functionalized liposomes co-loaded with graphene quantum dots (GQDs), gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), and doxorubicin demonstrated synergistic phototherapy and multimodal imaging capabilities under single-wavelength NIR light, leading to efficient tumor shrinkage (Fig. 13) [226]. Immunotherapy

Multifunctional vesicles offer unique advantages in cancer immunotherapy by facilitating targeted delivery of immunomodulators, enhancing antigen presentation, and modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME). For example, mannose-functionalized liposomes efficiently targeted alveolar macrophages via CD206-mediated endocytosis, promoting antigen uptake and demonstrating strong potential for innate immune activation [93].

In another study, vesicle-based chemoimmunotherapy was achieved using pH-sensitive liposomes co-loaded with docetaxel and pemetrexed in combination with anti-PD-L1 antibodies. This system induced immunogenic cell death (ICD), enhanced tumor suppression, and significantly reduced systemic toxicity in vivo [233]. Similarly, aptamer-functionalized liposomes (aptamosomes) carrying doxorubicin and IDO1 siRNA targeted both CD44 and PD-L1, reversing local immunosuppression and reducing metastasis through synergistic ICD induction and remodeling of the TME [118].

Engineered protein nanocages based on the E2 domain of pyruvate dehydrogenase have also shown promise as vesicle-based cancer vaccines. These structures can be functionalized with tumor antigens and have been shown to activate dendritic cells, enhance IFN-γ secretion, and elicit antigen-specific immune responses in preclinical models [234–236].

Furthermore, gadolinium-doped mesoporous carbon vesicles loaded with the immune adjuvant R837 achieved dual functions of MRI imaging and immune activation. In vivo studies demonstrated effective inhibition of both primary tumors and metastases, along with the induction of durable immune memory [237].

These findings illustrate how nanovesicles contribute to modern immunotherapy by enabling targeted immune modulation and combination strategies with precise delivery and reduced systemic toxicity.

Theranostic vesicles

Theranostic vesicles represent an advanced class of nanocarriers capable of combining therapy and diagnostic imaging within a single platform. These systems offer real-time monitoring of drug distribution, therapeutic responses, and tumor progression, thereby facilitating personalized cancer treatment [2].

Protein-based nanocages such as lumazine synthase from Aquifex aeolicus (AaLS) have been engineered to deliver chemotherapeutic and fluorescent molecules, enhancing drug cytotoxicity and enabling imaging [84]. Similarly, ferritin nanocages loaded with Lutetium-177 and phosphate ions have been utilized for simultaneous imaging and radiotherapy, demonstrating high tumor specificity [51].

Synthetic polymersomes have also been widely employed. PEG-PCL polymersomes functionalized with the AS1411 DNA aptamer co-encapsulated hydrophilic DOX and hydrophobic gadolinium-based quantum dots, achieving simultaneous MRI imaging and chemotherapy in breast cancer models [217]. Other systems, such as PLA-PEG vesicles functionalized with triphenylphosphonium, enabled mitochondrial targeting, while polypeptide-linked polymersomes facilitated tumor-specific release through MMP-2 cleavage and photoacoustic activation [238–241].

Inorganic vesicles further extend the functionality of theranostics. Ultra-small superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (USPIONs) embedded in redox-responsive copolymers enabled MRI contrast enhancement and triggered DOX release in tumor microenvironments [143]. Gold nanoparticle-coated polymersomes exhibited enhanced NIR absorption and photoacoustic imaging capacity, achieving complete tumor ablation in vivo [15].

Quantum dots (QDs), with their superior optical properties, have enabled deep-tissue, high-resolution imaging alongside photothermal therapy and drug delivery [214, 242].

Natural and hybrid polymer vesicles also contribute significantly. Systems such as starch-functionalized PMAA-PS80 terpolymersomes chelating Gd3⁺ enabled T1 MRI enhancement and pH-responsive DOX release with low cytotoxicity [30]. Lipid–polymer hybrids engineered to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) successfully delivered multi-drug cargo including DOX and mitomycin C to intracranial tumors [243, 244].

Hybrid vesicles combining Fe-Au cores with PEG shells, or liposomes with SPIONs, offered PTT, magnetic guidance, and imaging capabilities in a single construct [70, 99, 232, 237] [245–248].

Membrane-based vesicles such as engineered exosomes and virus-like particles (VLPs) are also gaining prominence. VSVG-expressing HEK293T-derived vesicles, affibody-functionalized exosomes, and recombinant lumazine synthase particles have been used for targeted PDT, chemotherapy, and multimodal imaging [233, 249, 250].

Apoferritin and E2 protein cages functionalized with targeting ligands or MRI contrast agents showed efficacy in both immune activation and in vivo imaging [234–236, 251]. Heat shock proteins (HSPs) and genetically modified VLPs have delivered cocktails of chemotherapeutic agents and imaging probes with high specificity [219, 252].

These platforms highlight the potential of theranostic vesicles in combining therapeutic precision with diagnostic clarity, offering improved tumor targeting, reduced off-target effects, and real-time tracking of treatment efficacy. Combination therapy

Recent advances in nanomedicine have enabled the development of multifunctional vesicles that integrate different therapeutic strategies, such as chemotherapy, phototherapy, and immunotherapy, into a single platform. These vesicles enhance therapeutic efficacy by leveraging multiple mechanisms of action, allowing for improved tumor targeting, drug stability, and controlled release. Their inner compartmentalization facilitates the co-delivery of different anticancer agents while reducing systemic toxicity.

Polymersomes and liposomes have been widely explored for dual drug loading. For instance, bubble-generating polymersomes encapsulating doxorubicin and indocyanine green enabled photo-triggered release and enhanced tumor apoptosis through combined chemotherapy and photothermal therapy [221].

Similarly, anti-EGFR aptamer-functionalized PEGylated liposomes co-loaded with erlotinib and perfluorocarbon successfully delivered both EGFR-TKIs and oxygen to hypoxic tumor environments, reducing HIF-1α expression and restoring therapeutic sensitivity [90].

Inorganic vesicles such as mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) have been used to co-deliver doxorubicin and pro-apoptotic miRNAs, while chitosan-based natural polymers encapsulated both chemotherapeutics and siRNAs to inhibit tumor growth and cell migration [221, 254]. Advanced synthetic polymersomes containing dual hydrophilic-hydrophobic domains have achieved co-delivery of nucleotides, proteins, and chemotherapeutics with high bioavailability and biocompatibility [27, 255–257].

Viral-like particles (VLPs) also serve as efficient combinational carriers. Genetically engineered MS2 VLPs, recombinant PhMV- or P22-derived structures, and heat shock protein-based cages have been loaded with cocktails of imaging agents, cytotoxins, and photosensitizers to enable chemo-, photo-, and gene therapy in a single construct [219, 251, 252, 258].

Methods for preparation of theranostic vesicles: From conventional to innovative approaches

Theranostic vesicles, including liposomes, polymersomes, and inorganic nanoparticles (NPs), can be prepared using top-down or bottom-up approaches. Top-down methods involve breaking down larger molecules through sonication, homogenization, or extrusion to form NPs. In contrast, bottom-up methods construct NPs through molecular assembly using techniques like polymerization, sol–gel reactions, microemulsions, co-precipitation, and hydrolysis.

Preparation methods for nanovesicles can be further classified based on whether they involve two-step or one-step processes. Two-step methods generally include an emulsification step followed by nanoparticle formation, while one-step methods produce nanoparticles directly without emulsification [259].

Emulsification-solvent evaporation is one of the most commonly used two-step techniques, where polymers are dissolved in volatile solvents like ethyl acetate, replacing toxic options like dichloromethane or chloroform [260]. After emulsification in water using surfactants and high shear forces, solvent evaporation under reduced pressure results in nanoparticle formation. While scalable and adaptable, this method is primarily suitable for lipophilic drugs and may result in nanoparticle coalescence [260, 261].

In the emulsification-solvent diffusion method, a water-miscible solvent containing the polymer and drug is emulsified in an aqueous surfactant solution. Diluting with water causes the solvent to diffuse out, leading to nanoparticle formation. This approach offers high yields, reproducibility, and efficient encapsulation rates without requiring high-shear homogenization [262]. However, removing excess water and preventing hydrophilic drug leakage remain challenges [263].

A similar technique, emulsification-reverse salting-out, uses a water-miscible solvent like acetone and an aqueous phase containing a salting-out agent. Dilution with water triggers acetone diffusion and polymer precipitation [264, 265]. This method avoids harmful solvents but is mainly applicable for lipophilic drugs and requires extensive purification to remove salts.

Nanoprecipitation, also known as solvent displacement, is a one-step method where a polymer dissolved in a water-miscible solvent is added to a stirred aqueous phase. Rapid solvent diffusion leads to polymer precipitation and nanoparticle formation [261, 262]. While simple and scalable, this method has low encapsulation efficiency for hydrophilic drugs and often requires optimization of drug-polymer–solvent combinations [266, 267].

Microfluidic techniques are emerging as an efficient alternative, providing precise control over nanoparticle size and distribution. By mixing organic and aqueous phases through microfluidic channels, these systems ensure uniform particle synthesis and reproducibility [268–272]. Microfluidics also enable continuous production, addressing the scalability issues faced by conventional batch processing [273, 274].

Dialysis offers another simple and scalable one-step method. In this approach, polymers are dissolved in an organic solvent, followed by solvent displacement through dialysis against a non-solvent. This method produces nanoparticles with a narrow size distribution, although prolonged dialysis may result in premature cargo release [275].

To minimize the use of harmful solvents, supercritical fluids (SCFs) have emerged as environmentally friendly alternatives. Supercritical carbon dioxide (scCO₂) is particularly advantageous due to its mild critical conditions, low toxicity, and cost-effectiveness [276–278]. SCFs have been widely used in pharmaceutical applications for drug formulation and nanoparticle production. Biological vesicles, such as virus-like particles (VLPs), are typically produced using bacterial, yeast, insect, or mammalian cell systems. While bacterial and yeast systems offer high yields at lower costs, they lack the ability to perform complex post-translational modifications (PTMs) [280]. In contrast, Baculovirus/insect cells (B/IC) excel in executing intricate PTMs but pose purification challenges due to the co-production of enveloped viruses [280]. Mammalian cells, such as Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells, are preferred for producing VLPs with human-like PTMs despite their lower yields [281, 282]. Transient transfection is ideal for small-scale production, while stable cell lines are typically established for large-scale applications [283, 284].

In addition to VLPs, non-viral protein cages (NVPNs) are gaining attention for drug delivery applications. NVPNs are self-assembling protein nanostructures with excellent biocompatibility, structural stability, and ease of functionalization. They can be modified through covalent or non-covalent interactions on their outer or inner surfaces to enhance targeting, cargo loading, and controlled release [285]. Controlled disassembly and reassembly can be triggered using stimuli such as pH, ions, metals, or reducing agents [50, 286].

While various methods offer distinct advantages, selecting an appropriate preparation technique depends on the type of cargo, desired nanoparticle properties, and intended application. For scalable and reproducible production, microfluidics and SCF systems are gaining prominence, while traditional emulsification and nanoprecipitation methods remain suitable for specific applications. Advances in bioproduction systems further expand the potential of biological vesicles in nanomedicine, combining therapeutic and diagnostic functionalities.

Discussion

Vesicle-based drug delivery systems have demonstrated significant potential in improving the stability, bioavailability, and targeted release of therapeutics. By protecting cargo from inactivation, degradation, and rapid clearance, these systems enhance the efficacy of anticancer treatments. Despite substantial progress in preclinical studies, their successful clinical translation remains challenging due to hurdles in scalability, manufacturing, and regulatory compliance.

Clinical translation challenges and preclinical limitations

The successful clinical translation of vesicle-based therapies is hindered by challenges in large-scale manufacturing, tumor heterogeneity, and the limitations of preclinical models. Addressing these issues requires advancements in standardization, patient stratification, and improved in vitro and in vivo testing methodologies to bridge the gap between laboratory research and real-world applications.

Scalability and manufacturing complexities

The large-scale production of vesicles is resource-intensive, often making the return on investment uncertain [287]. Unlike small-molecule drugs, which benefit from well-established manufacturing processes, vesicle production requires precise control over size, charge, and surface modifications to maintain therapeutic efficacy and minimize immune clearance. Batch-to-batch variability, a major bottleneck in large-scale production, can significantly impact drug delivery performance and regulatory approval.

Another key challenge is ensuring vesicle stability during storage and transportation. Liposomal formulations, such as Doxil® (doxorubicin liposomes) and Onivyde® (irinotecan liposomes), highlight the extensive optimization required for clinical success. Maintaining their structural integrity and preventing premature drug leakage demands strict Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) and adherence to evolving nanoguidelines [288]. The use of specialized manufacturing software, such as Aspen, has shown promise in optimizing industrial-scale production while maintaining quality control [289].

Beyond economic and production challenges, the biological complexities of tumor environments also hinder vesicle efficacy [290]. Passive targeting via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, while effective in murine models, has shown inconsistent results in humans, where heterogeneous tumor vasculature limits drug accumulation [291, 292]. Additionally, vesicles are rapidly cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), leading to off-target accumulation in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow, even when stealth modifications such as PEGylation are applied.

Tumor heterogeneity and patient stratification

The biological diversity of tumors presents a major challenge for vesicle-based drug delivery. Tumors vary significantly between individuals and even within the same patient, with differences in vascularization, immune evasion mechanisms, and receptor expression [293]. Effective targeting strategies must account for this heterogeneity to avoid poor drug penetration and uneven distribution within the tumor.

One promising approach is patient stratification based on biomarker expression. For instance, in HER2-positive breast cancer, therapies such as trastuzumab®, pertuzumab®, and ado-trastuzumab emtansine® have demonstrated success due to precise biomarker screening and patient selection [294–296]. Applying a similar precision-medicine framework to vesicle-based therapies could improve efficacy by ensuring that targeted formulations are administered only to patients with high biomarker expression levels.

Additionally, tumor microenvironment factors, such as hypoxia, enzymatic activity, and high interstitial fluid pressure, influence vesicle performance. The dense tumor stroma can hinder vesicle penetration, reducing drug delivery efficiency [297]. To address this, researchers are developing stimuli-responsive vesicles that release their cargo upon exposure to tumor-specific conditions (e.g., pH changes, enzymatic activity, hyperthermia, or magnetic fields) [298].

Targeting circulating tumor cells and cancer stem cells remains difficult; cancer mortality often arises from micro-metastases rather than primary tumors and vesicle-based systems must also be designed for metastatic targeting [299, 300]. The dynamic expression of surface receptors on cancer cells further complicates targeting strategies [301, 302]. Strategies such as dual-targeting vesicles—capable of recognizing both primary tumor markers and metastatic cell signatures—could improve therapeutic outcomes.

Preclinical limitations and emerging testing strategies

Despite promising preclinical results, many vesicle-based therapies fail in human trials due to differences between animal models and human tumors. Mouse models, which dominate preclinical research, often do not accurately replicate human tumor biology, leading to discrepancies in drug distribution, immune responses, and therapeutic efficacy [303].

The EPR effect, a fundamental concept in nanoparticle-based drug delivery, appears to be less pronounced in human cancers than in murine models, raising concerns about its translational relevance [292].

Additionally, there is a growing need for standardized data reporting. Many studies report nanoparticle tumor accumulation without specifying the mass or volume of the tissue analyzed, leading to inconsistent comparisons between studies. More quantitative, standardized methods for reporting tumor drug retention and biodistribution could improve cross-study reliability and accelerate clinical translation [309].

Theranostic vesicles: balancing imaging and therapy

Theranostic vesicles—designed for both drug delivery and imaging—represent an exciting frontier but introduce new challenges. The pharmacokinetics of imaging agents and therapeutic drugs often differ, making it difficult to balance effective therapy with high-quality imaging [289, 310]. Additionally, photo-responsive vesicles, while promising in experimental settings, may lead to phototoxicity and unintended side effects, such as skin sensitivity and local tissue damage [311]. Further refinement of light-sensitive drug delivery mechanisms and the use of alternative imaging modalities could improve the safety and clinical feasibility of these systems.

Comparative analysis of vesicle types

Vesicle-based drug delivery systems vary widely in their composition, stability, and functionality. Each type offers distinct advantages and challenges, influencing their suitability for different clinical applications.

Lipid-based vesicles

Liposomes are the most widely studied vesicle type, demonstrating high biocompatibility and the ability to encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. Clinically approved liposomal formulations, such as Doxil® and Onivyde®, have paved the way for vesicle-based nanomedicines. Additionally, lipid-based vesicles played a pivotal role in the rapid development of SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccines, where lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) were used to protect and deliver mRNA efficiently, showcasing their potential for next-generation vaccine platforms [312]. However, liposomal vesicles face stability challenges, including rapid dissociation and premature drug leakage, which can limit sustained drug release [313]. Their cellular uptake efficiency is also influenced by size and shape, with spherical vesicles under 100 nm exhibiting superior uptake compared to rod-shaped ones [314].

Polymeric vesicles

Polymeric vesicles, or polymersomes, offer enhanced structural stability compared to lipid-based vesicles, making them an attractive alternative for controlled drug release. Their tunable properties allow for precise modifications in degradation rates and cargo loading. However, their widespread clinical use is limited by higher production costs and regulatory challenges due to their synthetic nature [315]. Moreover, polymeric vesicles, while more stable, tend to have higher production costs and are less readily available than synthetic lipid-based vesicles, limiting their widespread clinical use [316]. Standardized protocols for their synthesis and clinical application remain a key challenge.

Metal-based vesicles

Metal-based vesicles hold promise for theranostic applications, integrating both therapeutic and imaging functionalities. Their ability to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) can be leveraged for cancer treatment, yet this property also raises concerns regarding toxicity [317, 318]. Some heavy metals, particularly quantum dots, have been linked to neurotoxicity, oxidative stress, and DNA damage, limiting their clinical translation [319, 320]. Iron-based vesicles, while promising for drug delivery, may accumulate in macrophages, leading to liver toxicity and lipid peroxidation [321]. Additionally, charged metal vesicles may compromise the blood–brain barrier (BBB), raising concerns about neurological effects [322].

Biological vesicles

Biological vesicles, including exosomes and extracellular vesicles (EVs), are naturally derived biological vesicles that facilitate intercellular communication.

These naturally derived carriers possess inherent biocompatibility and the ability to facilitate intercellular communication, making them ideal for transporting therapeutic molecules. Unlike synthetic vesicles, exosomes can cross biological barriers, such as the BBB, enabling efficient drug delivery to previously inaccessible tissues. However, their clinical use is hampered by heterogeneous populations, complex isolation methods, and difficulties in large-scale production [323]. Research is ongoing to optimize exosome purification techniques and enhance targeting capabilities [324].

Hybrid vesicles

Hybrid vesicles, which combine elements of lipid, polymeric, and metal-based systems, represent a promising strategy to overcome the limitations of individual vesicle types. By integrating the stability of polymers, the biocompatibility of lipids, and the functionality of metals, these vesicles can achieve enhanced drug retention, controlled release, and improved tumor targeting. Safety & toxicity considerations

While vesicle-based drug delivery systems offer numerous advantages, their long-term safety and potential toxicity remain key concerns for clinical translation. Several factors, including immune activation, organ accumulation, and unintended off-target effects, must be carefully evaluated.

Immune system activation and inflammation: Nanoparticle accumulation in the spleen and liver can trigger immune system activation, leading to increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines such as IL-6, MCP-1, and GM-CSF in a dose-dependent manner [322]. This immune response, while sometimes beneficial for immunotherapy applications, could pose a risk of systemic inflammation and unintended immune-related side effects. Strategies such as surface modifications (e.g., PEGylation, biomimetic coatings) have been explored to minimize immune recognition and reduce clearance rates.

Pulmonary and cardiovascular risks of inhaled vesicles: For vesicles administered via inhalation, there is growing concern about pulmonary inflammation caused by nanoparticle deposition in the lungs. This can lead to hypercoagulability, increased thrombotic risk, and cardiovascular complications [333]. For example, silver colloidal nanoparticles have been linked to lung, skin, and neurological toxicity when excessively inhaled or ingested [334]. Similarly, MnO₂ nanoparticles have been shown to induce oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, raising concerns about their effects on the central nervous system (CNS) [335].

Blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity and CNS toxicity: High concentrations of charged vesicles may also compromise the integrity of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), potentially leading to neurological side effects [322]. This raises particular concerns for metal-based and polymeric vesicles intended for brain-targeted drug delivery. Researchers are investigating surface engineering techniques and biocompatible coatings to enhance BBB penetration while reducing potential CNS toxicity.

Biodegradability and long-term accumulation risks: The biodegradability of vesicles varies depending on their composition. While lipid-based and extracellular vesicle-derived formulations generally degrade naturally, certain polymeric and metal-based vesicles exhibit slower clearance rates, potentially leading to long-term tissue accumulation. For instance, iron-based vesicles have been observed to accumulate in liver and spleen macrophages, resulting in lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress [321]. To address these concerns, research is ongoing to develop biodegradable polymeric coatings and hybrid vesicles that allow for controlled degradation without residual toxicity.

Conclusion

Multifunctional vesicles represent a significant advancement in the field of cancer therapeutics, offering innovative solutions to enhance the delivery and efficacy of anticancer drugs. Non-biological vesicles, such as liposomes and polymersomes, have demonstrated substantial benefits in targeted drug delivery and reduced systemic toxicity. Biological vesicles, particularly EVs, provide inherent biocompatibility and immune evasion capabilities, making them highly effective for therapeutic applications. Hybrid vesicles combine the strengths of both non-biological and biological materials, resulting in improved stability and multifunctional drug delivery systems.

However, despite these promising developments, several challenges remain. Issues such as vesicle stability, manufacturing complexities, and potential toxicity must be meticulously addressed to ensure the safe and effective clinical translation of these nanocarriers. Continued research and innovation are essential to overcome these hurdles and fully exploit the potential of multifunctional vesicles in cancer therapy.

Looking ahead, emerging technologies could significantly enhance the design, functionality, and therapeutic impact of vesicle-based drug delivery systems. One promising approach is the use of CRISPR-based vesicles, which combine gene-editing capabilities with targeted drug delivery, offering a powerful tool for precision oncology. These vesicles could be engineered to selectively edit oncogenes or enhance immune responses, further improving therapeutic outcomes. Additionally, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning are playing an increasing role in optimizing vesicle design, allowing for more precise control over cargo loading, stability, and biodistribution. AI-driven modeling can accelerate the identification of optimal vesicle formulations, reducing trial-and-error approaches in vesicle engineering and improving efficiency in clinical translation. Machine learning algorithms can also be used to predict vesicle biodistribution and clearance rates, improving the precision of targeted drug delivery. Additionally, AI can facilitate the discovery of novel vesicle formulations with enhanced biocompatibility and reduced immunogenicity, ultimately streamlining the development of next-generation vesicle-based therapies.

Furthermore, bioprinting and microfluidic technologies are emerging as powerful tools for the scalable production of multifunctional vesicles. Microfluidic platforms enable precise control over vesicle size, charge, and cargo loading, ensuring reproducibility and uniformity in vesicle formulations. This advancement could address one of the major bottlenecks in vesicle manufacturing, making large-scale clinical application more feasible.

In conclusion, multifunctional vesicles offer a transformative approach to cancer treatment, with the potential to significantly improve patient outcomes. By addressing the existing challenges and advancing the development of these nanocarriers, we can pave the way for more effective and targeted cancer therapies, ultimately enhancing the quality of life for cancer patients worldwide.
