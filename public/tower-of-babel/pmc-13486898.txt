Equity in health financing in China: a national financing and benefit incidence analysis, 2013–2023
Authors: Tiange Chen, Peng Zhan, Hongqiao Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundEquity in health financing is essential for achieving universal health coverage (UHC), particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). As one of the largest middle-income countries, China has undergone substantial health financing reforms over the past decades and achieved near-universal social health insurance coverage. However, whether these achievements have translated into equitable distribution of health financing burdens and benefits remains unclear. We aim to provide updated national evidence on equity in health financing in China.MethodsWe used data from three waves (2013, 2018 and 2023) of the Chinese Household Income Project, a nationally representative household survey. Financing incidence analysis was conducted to assess the progressivity of major health financing sources, and benefit incidence analysis examined the distribution of realised social health insurance (SHI) benefits. We further conducted counterfactual policy scenario analyses to explore the potential impact of policy reforms to mitigate inequities. All analyses were conducted at the household level and adjusted by adult equivalence. Sensitivity analyses using alternative household ability-to-pay measures, adult equivalence scales, private health insurance imputation approaches, and measures of health need were performed.FindingsThe overall health financing system remained mildly regressive, with Kakwani indices (KIs) of −0.016 (−0.030, −0.002) in 2013, −0.034 (−0.044, −0.023) in 2018, and −0.071 (−0.080, −0.062) in 2023. Among the financing sources, direct tax payments and SHI contributions by employees remained progressive, while indirect tax payments, SHI contributions by non-employed residents, and out-of-pocket payments were regressive. In addition, SHI reimbursements were distributed pro-rich and did not align with measured health need. In 2013, the wealthiest 20% of households accounted for only 7.9% of the health burden but received 55.4% of SHI reimbursements, and this inequity persisted in 2023. Policy simulations suggest that reforming SHI financing mechanisms, capping out-of-pocket payments, and harmonising SHI benefit packages across schemes could improve overall progressivity to varying degrees, but require additional fiscal investments.InterpretationDespite substantial increases in public health expenditure and near-universal health insurance coverage, China has not achieved significant improvements in equity of health financing. Lower-income households continue to bear disproportionately greater financing burdens relative to their ability to pay while receiving fewer SHI reimbursements relative to measured health need. These findings underscore the critical importance of equity-oriented design in health financing mechanisms, offering important lessons for other countries in the Western-Pacific region.FundingKey Project of Philosophy and Social Sciences Research, Ministry of Education of China (24JZD035); Taikang Yicai Public Health and Epidemic Control Fund (XY240927100000); General Project of the National Social Science Fund of China (24BTJ054); Innovation Fund for Outstanding Doctoral Candidates of Peking University Health Science Center (BMU2026BSS001); Fundamental Research Funds for the Central Universities in China.Keywords: Health financing, Equity, Health system, China

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure databases for studies published between Jan 1, 1950, and Dec 31, 2025, using combinations of the terms “China”, “health financing”, “equity”, “progressivity”, “financing incidence”, “benefit incidence”, “social health insurance”, “out-of-pocket”, and “taxation”, without any language restrictions. Over the past decades, the Chinese government has substantially increased public financing for health and achieved near-universal social health insurance coverage. Yet the equity implications of these financing reforms have not been well assessed. Several studies have examined either the distribution of financing burdens or social health insurance reimbursements, generally finding that the overall system was slightly progressive or nearly proportional, while reimbursements were distributed pro-rich. However, most existing evidence relied on provincial-level data, which may overestimate progressivity given significant socioeconomic disparities across China. In addition, many analyses were based on survey data from earlier stages of reform and therefore do not reflect equity in health financing at the current stage. Up-to-date national evidence that simultaneously assesses both financing burdens and benefit distribution in China’s current health financing system remains limited.

Added value of this study

This study provides updated national-level evidence on equity in both health financing burdens and realised SHI reimbursements in China using three waves (2013, 2018, and 2023) of a nationally representative household survey. By simultaneously applying financing incidence analysis and benefit incidence analysis, we find that China’s health financing system remained mildly regressive, with lower-income households bearing disproportionately greater financing burdens. Indirect taxes, flat-rate contributions under residence-based insurance schemes, and out-of-pocket payments were the main sources of regressivity. Meanwhile, social health insurance reimbursements were distributed disproportionately toward wealthier households despite their lower measured health need. Policy scenario analyses as static counterfactual benchmarks further explore how alternative financing and benefit designs, such as reforming flat-rate contributions, capping out-of-pocket payments, and harmonising benefit packages across insurance schemes, would affect inequities.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings suggest that expanding social insurance coverage and increasing public health expenditure alone have not been sufficient to ensure equity in China’s health financing system. The specific design of financing mechanisms matters: flat-rate contribution mechanisms impose disproportionate burdens on lower-income populations, while fragmentation in benefit packages across schemes exacerbates inequities in both financing burdens and reimbursements. These insights are relevant for countries pursuing universal health coverage, underscoring the importance of equity-oriented health financing design.

Introduction

Universal health coverage (UHC) means that all people have access to needed care without financial hardship. Health financing arrangements shape this goal both by determining how resources are raised from households and by influencing how financial protection is realised when households use care. An equitable health financing system requires that financing burdens be distributed in accordance with ability to pay, and benefits be distributed based on health needs.1,2 However, inequities in health financing persist worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where they exacerbate social inequalities, limit access to care, drive catastrophic health expenditures, and undermine overall health system performance.3, 4, 5, 6 Although many LMICs have implemented reforms to address these challenges over the past decades,7, 8, 9 questions remain about the equity implications of different health financing mechanisms. Assessing the distribution of financing burdens and benefits, and examining how alternative financing designs may influence equity, are therefore critical for informing evidence-based reforms toward UHC.

As one of the world’s largest middle-income countries, China provides an important context for examining equity in health financing. Since 2003, China has substantially increased public financing for health, with government health expenditure rising from 17.0% of total health expenditures in 2003 to 26.7% in 2023, and expanded social health insurance (SHI) coverage to more than 95% of the population by 2013.10 This near-universal coverage has been achieved through multiple insurance schemes with fundamentally different financing mechanisms and population coverage. The Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI), established in 1998, primarily covers employed urban residents and is financed through payroll-based contributions shared by employers and employees. In contrast, insurance schemes for non-employed populations, the Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URBMI) and the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme (NRCMS), relied on flat-rate individual contributions that are uniform within each locality, supplemented by government subsidies that account for approximately two-thirds of scheme revenues. URBMI and NRCMS merged into the Urban and Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI) in 2018, though the scheme retained the flat-rate contribution mechanism. Moreover, benefit packages vary substantially across schemes and across provinces, with UEBMI typically offering more generous coverage than URRBMI.10 As a result, China’s health financing system continues to exhibit significant variation in how financial burdens are raised and benefits are distributed across population groups, raising important concerns about equity despite high overall coverage.

Previous research has examined various aspects of equity in China’s health financing system. Several studies have assessed the progressivity of financing burdens, with most reporting that the overall system was slightly progressive or nearly proportional.11, 12, 13, 14 Other studies have examined the distribution of health financing benefits and generally found that wealthier individuals received disproportionately greater benefits.15, 16, 17, 18 However, several limitations remain. First, much of the existing evidence is based on provincial-level survey data, which may overestimate progressivity at the national level given China’s significant cross-provincial socioeconomic disparities and decentralised fiscal system. Second, the majority of analyses relied on data collected before 2013, capturing only an earlier stage of health system reform. Third, and more fundamentally, few studies have simultaneously examined financing burdens and benefit distribution. This gap is important because the two sides are both shaped by the same resource constraints, pooling arrangements, and scheme design. Joint assessment is therefore needed to determine whether inequities are isolated to one side or compounded across the contribution and benefit sides of the same system.19, 20, 21

Guided by this rationale, we adopt a conceptual framework that links equity on the contribution and benefit sides of the financing system to their empirical assessment (Supplementary Fig. S1). This study applies this framework to examine equity in China’s health financing system using nationally representative data from 2013 to 2023. Drawing on three waves of the Chinese Household Income Project (CHIP), we apply financing incidence analysis and benefit incidence analysis to assess financing burdens relative to household ability to pay and benefits relative to ability to pay and measured health need, respectively. We hypothesise that near-universal insurance coverage and increased public financing may not be sufficient to ensure equity in China, where contribution mechanisms, pooling arrangements, and benefit design remain stratified. We further conduct counterfactual policy scenario analyses to evaluate how alternative contribution, out-of-pocket payment, and reimbursement designs may shape these two dimensions of equity. Findings from this study may help Chinese policymakers set healthcare policy priorities and raise international awareness of evidence-based policy adjustments for achieving UHC.

Methods

Data sources

We used the CHIP data as the primary data source to assess equity in health financing in China. CHIP is a nationally representative survey conducted by the China Institute of Income Distribution and the China National Bureau of Statistics (NBS), providing comprehensive micro-level data on household income, expenditures, and wealth.22,23 The survey adopts a multi-stage random sampling design based on the NBS annual household survey framework, covering regions with different social and economic contexts. CHIP has been widely used in research on household savings and expenditures,24 household income and assets,25 as well as health-related issues.26,27

In this study, we used CHIP data from 2013, 2018 and 2023 for analysis. CHIP collects income and expenditure information through diary records, which provides more precise and reliable measurements than end-of-year recall.28,29 We integrated data from the diary records and survey questionnaires. Diary-recorded data were used to construct measures of household income, total and categorized consumption expenditures, and health-related payments and insurance reimbursements. Questionnaire-based data were used to obtain sociodemographic characteristics, health status indicators, and insurance enrollment information. The questionnaire has remained largely consistent across the 2013–2023 waves; in particular, the diary-recorded items and survey questions used in our main analysis are unchanged across the three waves, enabling reliable temporal comparisons. Detailed variable definitions and construction procedures are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

We also used data from the China Household Finance Survey (CHFS) to impute household expenditures on private health insurance premiums, as CHIP records private insurance coverage status but does not collect premium amounts. Households with missing data on key variables required for the estimation of ability-to-pay measures were excluded from the analysis; missing data accounted for less than 1% of the total sample. Further details on the CHIP and CHFS data are provided in Appendix pp 2–4.

Financing incidence analysis

We conducted financing incidence analysis (FIA) to assess how health financing burdens were distributed relative to household ability to pay (ATP). The primary sources of health financing in China included indirect taxes, direct taxes, social health insurance (SHI), private health insurance (PHI), and individual out-of-pocket (OOP) payments. We estimated contributions from each financing source at the household level and measured their progressivity using the concentration index (CI) and Kakwani index (KI). The overall progressivity of the health financing system was estimated by taking the weighted sum of the KI values for each financing source, with weights based on each source’s share in total health expenditures (Supplementary Table S2).30

For each financing source, we estimated household contributions as follows. Indirect taxes consist primarily of value-added tax (VAT), excise tax, and import duties. These indirect tax payments were not directly reported in CHIP and were reconstructed by applying the corresponding tax rates to specific categories of consumption expenditures at the household level. Direct tax paid by households comprises personal income tax, including both comprehensive income tax on wage and salary income, and business income tax on self-employed income. Both follow progressive tax rate structures. A household’s total direct tax payment is the sum of personal income tax for each household member. SHI contributions were also not directly reported in CHIP and were reconstructed at the individual level according to province- and year-specific administrative rules. For UEBMI enrollees, contributions were calculated by applying statutory contribution rates to monthly wages, subject to province-specific minimum and maximum contribution limits. For URRBMI enrollees, we used fixed per-capita contribution amounts set by local governments. Province-specific policy parameters were obtained from provincial government sources for 2013, 2018, and 2023. OOP payments, including expenditures on medical devices, drugs, and medical services, were directly derived from the CHIP data. PHI premiums were mapped from CHFS data according to households’ ATP percentile rank, as the CHIP survey only records coverage status. We also tested alternative approaches (regression-based imputation and excluding PHI premiums) in sensitivity analyses. More details on the calculation of the financing sources are available in Appendix pp 5–15.

To measure the progressivity of each financing source, we calculated both the CI and the KI. The OLS estimate of β is an estimate of the CI.32 Delta method was used to estimate the robust standard error that accounts for the sampling variability of the estimate.33 We also used concentration curves to display this distribution by plotting the cumulative share of healthcare payments against the cumulative share of the households ranked by ATP.

The KI measures the progressivity of a financing source relative to ATP. It is defined as the difference between the CI of healthcare payments and the Gini coefficient of ATP, corresponding to twice the area between the Lorenz curve of ATP and the concentration curve of healthcare payments. The KI ranges from −2 to 1, where positive values indicate progressive financing (the rich pay a larger share of their ATP), and negative values indicate regressive financing.31 These outcome measures are also summarised in Supplementary Table S1.

Benefit incidence analysis

We conducted benefit incidence analysis (BIA) to examine the distribution of realised SHI reimbursements across households ranked by ATP and to assess whether these reimbursements were aligned with health needs. The standard BIA approach multiplies health service utilisation by unit costs to estimate monetary benefits. However, unit costs vary substantially across China’s provinces and healthcare facilities and are not systematically available. Therefore, following Zhang et al.,18 we used SHI reimbursements as a direct measure of the benefit households actually drew from pooled SHI financing, which captures realised monetary transfers received from SHI after service use and claims. The distribution of benefits relative to ATP was assessed using the CI and concentration curves.

To evaluate whether SHI benefits aligned with health care needs, we examined the distribution of SHI benefits and health needs across ATP quintiles. In the main analysis, we measured health need using self-reported health status collected in CHIP, which includes five categories: excellent, good, average, poor, and very poor. Household health need was estimated as the number of household members reporting poor or very poor health status. We also tested two alternative health need proxies in the sensitivity analysis: the number of household members aged 65 years and above, and the number of household members reporting a chronic disease or disability.

Policy scenario analysis

To examine how different health financing mechanisms would affect equity, we conducted counterfactual policy scenario analyses. These analyses estimated how the distribution of health financing burdens and benefits would change under alternative policy designs, holding all other factors constant. We focused on three policy scenarios targeting the primary sources of inequity identified in our baseline analysis. In the first scenario, we simulated reforming URRBMI contributions from a flat-rate mechanism to a proportional structure based on household ATP. The reformed contribution rate was calculated to ensure total URRBMI contributions remained unchanged. In the second scenario, we simulated setting OOP payment caps at 10%, 25%, and 40% of household ATP, following the threshold used in the definition of catastrophic health expenditures,36 with government subsidies covering excess costs above these thresholds. We assumed no behavioural response to the caps, as price elasticity of healthcare demand is low for catastrophic health expenses.37 In the third scenario, we simulated increasing URRBMI reimbursement rate to match UEBMI levels through higher government subsidies. We assumed a price elasticity of healthcare demand of −0.2,38, 39, 40 accounting for the fact that lower out-of-pocket prices (due to higher reimbursement) would induce increases in healthcare utilisation and expenditures. For each scenario, we recalculated the relevant KI and CI values to assess changes in equity. Detailed methods are provided in Appendix pp 17–19.

Sample weights were adjusted in all analyses, including the calculation of concentration index, Kakwani index, and the corresponding delta-method 95% confidence intervals, to ensure national representativeness (see Appendix p 16). All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All P values were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Patient consent and ethical approval were not required for this study because only secondary datasets were used.

The progressivity of health financing

Table 2 presents the CIs and KIs for the overall health financing system and various financing sources across the three survey years. Over the study period, the composition of total health financing shifted, with the share of social health insurance rising and that of general taxes and out-of-pocket payments declining. The overall system remained mildly regressive over time, with KIs −0.016 (95% confidence interval: −0.030, −0.002) in 2013, −0.034 (−0.044, −0.023) in 2018, and −0.071 (−0.080, −0.062) in 2023. This result suggests that poorer households contributed a larger proportion of their income to finance health services compared to wealthier households. The progressivity of health financing within urban areas followed a similar pattern. In rural areas, the overall health financing was more regressive, with larger negative KIs in each respective year. We also estimated the progressivity of health financing by regions. As shown in Supplementary Table S3, the health financing in the eastern provinces was generally more progressive than in central and western provinces.

Distribution of SHI reimbursements

We examined the distribution of SHI reimbursements across households. Supplementary Table S9 presents the proportion of households receiving SHI reimbursements and the amount they received for each ATP quintile. The wealthiest 20% of the population received more than half of reimbursements from SHI in 2013, and this share declined to 43.7% in 2023. This pattern aligns with a decreasing CI, which fell from 0.481 (0.384, 0.578) in 2013 to 0.369 (0.326, 0.411) in 2023; across all three waves, the distribution of realised SHI reimbursements remained substantially pro-rich.

We further examined the distribution of SHI reimbursements by urban/rural residence and insurance scheme. The concentration curves are shown in Fig. 2, and CIs are presented in Supplementary Table S10. Almost all concentration curves lay below the 45° straight line, suggesting that the distribution of SHI reimbursements was pro-rich within each subgroup. The concentration index for urban areas was higher than that for rural areas in both 2013 and 2023. Among different social health insurance types, the CIs for URRBMI were consistently lower than those for UEBMI across all years.

Discussion

This study provides updated national evidence on equity in health financing in China in the context of near-universal insurance coverage. Using nationally representative data from 2013 to 2023, we observe that China’s health financing system remained mildly regressive overall despite substantial increases in government health expenditure and broad population coverage, with poorer households bearing disproportionate financial burdens while receiving fewer SHI reimbursements. These findings on equity of health financing provide important implications for achieving UHC in China, though this study does not directly assess the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure and medical impoverishment.

An important feature of this study is its national scope, which helps explain differences between our findings and some earlier provincial-level analyses that reported more progressive financing patterns.11,13,14 China is characterised by significant cross-provincial disparities in income levels, health spending capacity, and the composition of health financing sources. Within wealthier provinces, health financing tends to be more progressive due to lower out-of-pocket payment shares and higher proportions of employment-based insurance enrollees. However, when aggregated nationally, the distribution becomes more regressive as wealthier provinces account for a smaller share of total health expenditures relative to GDP. Our findings suggest that national-level analysis is important for understanding the full distributional consequences of China’s health financing system in a large and fiscally decentralised setting.

Our analysis suggests that indirect taxes, URRBMI contributions, and OOP payments are major contributors to the regressivity in China’s health financing system. Regarding taxation, indirect taxes remained regressive throughout the study period, consistent with international evidence showing that value-added taxes and consumption taxes impose proportionally higher burdens on lower-income households.8,41,42 By contrast, direct taxes remained progressive, consistent with observations in other countries,2,4,8 largely reflecting recent personal income tax reforms that reduced the tax burden on the poor by raising income thresholds and lowering tax rates for lower-income brackets.43,44 However, indirect taxes continued to account for a dominant share of total tax revenue (63.9% in 2023), far exceeding OECD countries’ average of around 45%, thereby limiting the overall progressivity of tax-based financing in China.

Contributions of URRBMI were consistently regressive throughout the study period. The regressivity of URRBMI contributions was unsurprising given its flat-rate mechanism, where all enrollees pay uniform premiums regardless of income, imposing disproportionate burdens on poorer households. Meanwhile, UEBMI’s payroll-based contributions remained measurably progressive, as they increase with individual wage income. This contrast demonstrates that social health insurance systems are not inherently progressive; their equity impacts depend fundamentally on contribution mechanisms. Similar patterns have been observed in other Asian countries with varied insurance arrangements, such as Indonesia and Japan.42,45

OOP payments represent another regressive financing source in the overall system. This pattern is also closely related to fragmentation in insurance benefit structures across schemes and regions. First, different insurance schemes provide markedly different levels of financial protection. URRBMI typically reimburses approximately 50% of inpatient costs with limited outpatient coverage, while UEBMI reimburses around 70% of inpatient costs and offers more generous outpatient benefits. Because wealthier individuals are more likely to be covered by UEBMI, they tend to bear relatively lower OOP burdens. Second, substantial geographic variation in SHI benefit packages across provinces compounds these disparities. Households in less developed regions are less likely to receive sufficient financial protection, which may help explain why OOP payments disproportionately burden lower-income populations.

Beyond financing burdens, our study indicates that social health insurance benefits are distributed disproportionately toward wealthier households despite their lower health needs measured by self-reported health. This inequitable pattern may reflect several mechanisms. Wealthier households are more likely to utilize healthcare services when ill, partly because they have better physical access and more generous benefit packages.46 When they do seek care, they are also more likely to access higher-level facilities, such as secondary and tertiary hospitals, where per-visit costs are substantially higher. Additionally, as noted above, wealthier individuals are more likely to be enrolled in UEBMI with its more generous benefit package. The combination of higher utilisation probabilities, greater per-visit costs, and higher reimbursement rates may help explain the observed concentration of SHI reimbursements among wealthier households. The distribution of SHI reimbursements remained substantially pro-rich across all three waves. To the extent that the point estimates narrowed over time, this may reflect policy reforms implemented during this period to strengthen financial protection for the poor and rural populations, such as reducing co-insurance rates, raising reimbursement ceilings, and expanding coverage to outpatient services. These reforms likely increased healthcare utilisation among lower-income groups and enabled them to receive a greater share of social health insurance reimbursements than before.18,47

Taken together, our findings highlight that institutional fragmentation in China’s social health insurance system, characterised by divergent financing mechanisms and benefit packages across schemes and regions, plays a central role in shaping inequities in health financing. To examine how alternative financing and benefit designs would reshape the distributional patterns identified above, we conducted three policy scenario analyses as static counterfactual benchmarks. First, as has been proposed in recent literature,48 reforming URRBMI’s flat-rate contribution mechanism to align with ability to pay could substantially improve equity in the health financing system. Yet realising this would require administrative capacity to assess household ability to pay under this scenario, which China is still developing. Building on the means-tested premium subsidies already in place, contributions could initially be differentiated across broad income or household categories, with the schedule refined as income-assessment systems mature. Second, China could follow high-income countries in setting OOP payment caps. Evidence suggests that capping OOP payments, rather than the current approach of capping insurance reimbursements, could offer better protection against catastrophic health expenditures.49,50 In our analysis, the overall progressivity of the healthcare financing improved under this scenario, though it would require additional government investments to cover excess costs exceeding the cap threshold. Considering fiscal feasibility, China could initially set the cap at 40% of household ATP, which would increase the government share of total health expenditures by 2.9 percentage points in 2023. Third, raising URRBMI’s reimbursement rates to match UEBMI levels could address inequities in both financing burdens and SHI reimbursements, echoing longstanding calls to harmonise the two basic social health insurance schemes.47 However, this represents the most fiscally demanding reform, requiring substantially greater increases in government health expenditure than the out-of-pocket cap approach.

We note several limitations. First, several components of the financing incidence analysis are reconstructed from administrative rules and statutory parameters rather than directly observed. Although this rule-based reconstruction is the standard approach in financing incidence analysis,33 the resulting estimates may nonetheless depart from true household payments, reflecting partial statutory pass-through in indirect-tax incidence, variation in contribution compliance, and imputation uncertainty for PHI premiums. Second, our benefit incidence analysis measures realised SHI reimbursements and does not capture benefits from other publicly financed health services, such as the essential public health program and national immunization program. However, as social health insurance accounts for approximately 80% of public health financing, our findings are unlikely to be substantially affected. Third, our health needs measure relies on self-reported health status, which, while widely used in benefit incidence analyses, is socially patterned and may not fully capture clinical need or differential service requirements across households with similar reported health. Fourth, we treated households as independent when computing 95% confidence intervals, because CHIP public microdata does not release cluster identifiers; this may slightly understate sampling variability. Finally, our policy scenario analyses are theoretical exercises based on counterfactual simulations, without addressing implementation challenges such as the measurement of ability to pay, administrative feasibility, or political economy constraints. Rather than serving as prescriptive reform proposals, they should be interpreted as benchmarks for understanding the potential distributional implications of specific policy levers.

These limitations point to several directions for future research. First, more refined measures of health need, drawing on clinical assessments or utilisation-revealed need rather than self-report, would further sharpen tests of benefit-need alignment. Second, decomposing the observed inequity in SHI reimbursements into its underlying drivers (utilisation, provider choice, and scheme generosity) could quantify the relative contribution of each. Third, integrating administrative or claims-based records on tax payments, contributions, and reimbursements would reduce reliance on rule-based reconstruction and survey-reported amounts. Fourth, dynamic policy scenario analyses that incorporate supply-side responses and fiscal feedback would allow the policy scenarios examined here to be assessed as reform-effect estimates rather than static benchmarks.

Despite these limitations, our findings have clear implications for countries pursuing universal health coverage. First, institutional fragmentation in health insurance schemes represents a persistent barrier to financing equity. China’s experience demonstrates that near-universal insurance coverage and rising public health spending can coexist with both regressive contribution structures and a pro-rich distribution of realised SHI reimbursements under such a fragmented system across different population groups and regions. Once such fragmentation becomes embedded, institutional inertia makes it difficult to reverse. Second, the specific design of health financing mechanisms matters. Flat-rate contribution mechanisms not only contribute to regressive health financing patterns but may also raise concerns about the sustainability of coverage among lower-income populations. In recent years, some low-income individuals have opted out of URRBMI due to its high premiums relative to their income. Third, the persistent role of OOP payments in driving inequity highlights the need to reduce OOP reliance. When fiscal space is constrained, strengthening primary care may be a particularly promising strategy for mitigating OOP burdens and containing healthcare expenditure growth for vulnerable populations.51
