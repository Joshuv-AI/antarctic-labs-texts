Dynamic Asset Pricing Theory for Life Contingent Risks
Patrick Ling

Abstract
Although the valuation of life contingent assets has been thoroughly investigated under the framework of mathematical statistics, little financial economics research pays attention to the pricing of these assets in a non-arbitrage, complete market. In this paper, we first revisit the Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing (FTAP) and the short proof of it. Then we point out that discounted asset price is a martingale only when dividends are zero under all random states of the world, using a simple proof based on pricing kernel. Next, we apply Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing (FTAP) to find valuation formula for life contingent assets including life insurance policies and life contingent annuities. Last but not least, we state the assumption of static portfolio in a dynamic economy, and clarify the FTAP that accommodates the valuation of a portfolio of life contingent policies.



A Preprint

1
Introduction

Life contingent contracts are financial agreements where insurance benefits (indemnities) and premium payments depend on the survival of individual(s). Two financial assets are exchanged during contracting: the financial asset of insurance benefits (indemnities), and the premium payments. Both are life contingent financial assets, because cash flows of both assets are contingent upon the health (survival)
state
of individual(s). There is the problem of pricing such life contingent assets. Actuarial business is a regulated business. When actuaries compute the premiums of life contingent policies, they stick to
the Equivalence Principle
:

E[PV(\text{benefits outgo})]=E[PV(\text{premium income})]

(1)

Although this principle can be regarded as extension of the
actuarially fair pricing
concept in insurance economics, simply saying
EPV[\text{benefits outgo}]
is the actuarially fair pricing, or the expected value of insurance indemnity
Z(X)
, or
E[Z(X)]
, is superficial. (
X
: insured loss, or financial loss due to accident;
Z
: a real function that maps loss amount
X
to indemnity amount;
Z(X)
is a random variable.) Such thought ignores the financial perspective of life contingent contracts. These contracts usually have very long
durations
, so it is not advisable to ignore the financial aspect of them. Without a new theoretical framework, it will also be very hard to derive certain results like recursion. Although it is possible to derive these equations thru mathematical statistics, such proof lacks any financial intuition. We deserve an alternative method that displays strong financial intuition.

We can apply the
Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing
to life contingent cash flow of an asset in order to find the valuation. We use
Farkas’ Lemma
to prove the FTAP in a one step model, under the assumptions of non-arbitrage, complete financial market. Then we rewrite the FTAP using the language of conditional expectation. This allows us to extend FTAP to accommodate a multiple step model. Although most financial literature points out that discounted asset prices is a martingale, we show that discounted asset price is a martingale only when no dividends are paid out under all random states. Then, with the help of ordinary differential equations, we derive valuation formulas for life insurance policies and life contingent annuities. Finally, we carefully investigate the details of information set as we price a portfolio of risky assets in a dynamic economy.

2.1
The Dynamic Economy

Assume there are
m

\boldsymbol{p}_{t}=(p_{t}^{1},p_{t}^{2},\dots,p_{t}^{m})

\boldsymbol{p}_{t}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{t}^{j}\theta_{t}^{j}

Assumptions

As of time

,
\{\theta_{s}^{i}\}_{s\leq t}

2.2
Asset Pricing in a Dynamic Economy

2.2.1
Arbitrage Portfolio

A portfolio
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}=(\theta_{t}^{1},\theta_{t}^{2},\dots,\theta_{t}^{m})^{T}
is said to be an
arbitrage opportunity
if

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{t}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 0

(2)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{p}_{t+1}+\boldsymbol{d}_{t+1})\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle\geq 0

(3)

…with at least one of the inequalities to be strict with positive probability.

2.2.2
Farkas’ Lemma

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}
. Then exactly one of the following two statements is true:

Statement 1

- There exists an
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
such that
\boldsymbol{Ax}=\boldsymbol{b}
and
\boldsymbol{x}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}

Statement 2

- There exists a
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}
such that
\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}<0

2.2.3
Payoff Matrix of a Risky Asset

Assume there are
m
financial assets that can be traded. Assume there are
n
random states for the pair
(p_{t+1}^{j},d_{t+1}^{j})
(for
j=1,2,\dots,m
). Define payoff matrix:

\boldsymbol{A}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{t+1}^{1,1}+d_{t+1}^{1,1}&p_{t+1}^{1,2}+d_{t+1}^{1,2}&\dots&p_{t+1}^{1,n}+d_{t+1}^{1,n}\\
p_{t+1}^{2,1}+d_{t+1}^{2,2}&p_{t+1}^{2,2}+d_{t+1}^{2,2}&\dots&p_{t+1}^{2,n}+d_{t+1}^{2,n}\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
p_{t+1}^{m,1}+d_{t+1}^{m,1}&p_{t+1}^{m,2}+d_{t+1}^{m,2}&\dots&p_{t+1}^{m,n}+d_{t+1}^{m,n}\end{pmatrix}

(4)

Define asset cost vector

\boldsymbol{b}=(p_{t}^{1},p_{t}^{2},\dots,p_{t}^{m})^{T}

(5)

Because asset prices
\{p_{t}^{j}\}
is an adapted process, there is no need to model uncertainty of
\boldsymbol{b}
as of time
t
.

2.2.4
Characterizing Arbitrage Portfolio

We first assume statement 2 of Farkas’ Lemma to be true. Then there exists
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}=(\theta_{t}^{1},\theta_{t}^{2},\dots,\theta_{t}^{m})^{T}
so that
\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}
, or:

\displaystyle(p_{t+1}^{1,1}+d_{t+1}^{1,1})\theta_{t}^{1}+(p_{t+1}^{2,1}+d_{t+1}^{2,1})\theta_{t}^{2}+\dots+(p_{t+1}^{m,1}+d_{t+1}^{m,1})\theta_{t}^{m}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle(p_{t+1}^{1,2}+d_{t+1}^{1,2})\theta_{t}^{1}+(p_{t+1}^{2,2}+d_{t+1}^{2,2})\theta_{t}^{2}+\dots+(p_{t+1}^{m,2}+d_{t+1}^{m,2})\theta_{t}^{m}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle(p_{t+1}^{1,n}+d_{t+1}^{1,n})\theta_{t}^{1}+(p_{t+1}^{2,n}+d_{t+1}^{2,n})\theta_{t}^{2}+\dots+(p_{t+1}^{m,n}+d_{t+1}^{m,n})\theta_{t}^{m}

\displaystyle\geq 0

Another implication of statement 2 is
\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}<0
, or:

p_{t}^{1}\theta_{t}^{1}+p_{t}^{2}\theta_{t}^{2}+\dots+p_{t}^{m}\theta_{t}^{m}<0

Above results can be summarized as: under all
n
states, the portfolio
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
has a non-negative payoff at time
t+1
; plus, there is a negative cost associated with constructing such portfolio
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
at time
t
(with probability of 1.) Such
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
is an arbitrage at time
t
.

2.2.5
Assumption: no arbitrage in financial market

We assume that there is no arbitrage opportunity in financial market. This assumption contradicts statement 2 of Farkas’ lemma. Hence, statement 1 must be true. There exists
\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
such that
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{b}
and
\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}
. Let
\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}=

(\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1},\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2},\dots,\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n})^{T}
, then:

\displaystyle(p_{t+1}^{1,1}+d_{t+1}^{1,1})\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1}+(p_{t+1}^{1,2}d_{t+1}^{1,2})\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2}+\dots+(p_{t+1}^{1,n}+d_{t+1}^{1,n})\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n}

\displaystyle=p_{t}^{1}

\displaystyle(p_{t+1}^{2,1}+d_{t+1}^{2,1})\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1}+(p_{t+1}^{2,2}d_{t+1}^{2,2})\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2}+\dots+(p_{t+1}^{2,n}+d_{t+1}^{2,n})\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n}

\displaystyle=p_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle(p_{t+1}^{m,1}+d_{t+1}^{m,1})\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1}+(p_{t+1}^{m,2}d_{t+1}^{m,2})\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2}+\dots+(p_{t+1}^{m,n}+d_{t+1}^{m,n})\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n}

\displaystyle=p_{t}^{m}

Or,

p_{t}^{i}=E_{\pi}[v_{t+1}(p_{t+1}^{i}+d_{t+1}^{i})|I_{t}]

We call
\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}=(\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1},\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2},\dots,\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n})^{T}
the
stochastic discount factor (SDF)
. We call
\{\pi_{t+1}\}
a
risk neutral
or
martingale probability
;
E_{\pi}
is called a
risk neutral
or
martingale expectation
.

2.2.6
Assumption: financial market is complete

We assume that financial market is complete; i.e., the number of linearly independent assets in the payoff matrix is equal to the number of random states of the world. Now that market is complete, the rank of the payoff matrix is
n
:

\begin{pmatrix}p_{t+1}^{1,1}+d_{t+1}^{1,1}&p_{t+1}^{1,2}+d_{t+1}^{1,2}&\dots&p_{t+1}^{1,n}+d_{t+1}^{1,n}\\
p_{t+1}^{2,1}+d_{t+1}^{2,2}&p_{t+1}^{2,2}+d_{t+1}^{2,2}&\dots&p_{t+1}^{2,n}+d_{t+1}^{2,n}\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
p_{t+1}^{m,1}+d_{t+1}^{m,1}&p_{t+1}^{m,2}+d_{t+1}^{m,2}&\dots&p_{t+1}^{m,n}+d_{t+1}^{m,n}\end{pmatrix}

There is a unique solution for stochastic discount factor (SDF)

\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}=(\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1},\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2},\dots,\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n})^{T}

Asset Pricing for Life Contingent Risks

2.3.1
Assumption: risk free rate and risk free asset

For now, we assume that there is a
risk free rate

r=e^{\delta}-1
during the period between
t
and
t+1
. That being said, a
risk free asset
priced
p_{t}^{0}=0
at time
t=0
will generate a payoff of
1+r
with probability of 1 in all states. Payoff matrix and asset cost vector are revised accordingly:

\boldsymbol{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1+r&1+r&\dots&1+r\\
p_{t+1}^{1,1}+d_{t+1}^{1,1}&p_{t+1}^{1,2}+d_{t+1}^{1,2}&\dots&p_{t+1}^{1,n}+d_{t+1}^{1,n}\\
p_{t+1}^{2,1}+d_{t+1}^{2,2}&p_{t+1}^{2,2}+d_{t+1}^{2,2}&\dots&p_{t+1}^{2,n}+d_{t+1}^{2,n}\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots\\
p_{t+1}^{m,1}+d_{t+1}^{m,1}&p_{t+1}^{m,2}+d_{t+1}^{m,2}&\dots&p_{t+1}^{m,n}+d_{t+1}^{m,n}\end{pmatrix}\quad\quad\quad\boldsymbol{b}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
p_{t}^{1}\\
p_{t}^{2}\\
\dots\\
p_{t}^{m}\end{pmatrix}

(6)

Now that there is no arbitrage in the market, there exists
\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}

\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{b}

\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}
. Let
\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}=

(\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1},\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2},\dots,\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n})^{T}
, then:

\displaystyle(1+r)\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1}+(1+r)\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2}+\dots+(1+r)\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle(p_{t+1}^{1,1}+d_{t+1}^{1,1})\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1}+(p_{t+1}^{1,2}d_{t+1}^{1,2})\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2}+\dots+(p_{t+1}^{1,n}+d_{t+1}^{1,n})\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n}

\displaystyle=p_{t}^{1}

\displaystyle(p_{t+1}^{2,1}+d_{t+1}^{2,1})\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1}+(p_{t+1}^{2,2}d_{t+1}^{2,2})\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2}+\dots+(p_{t+1}^{2,n}+d_{t+1}^{2,n})\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n}

\displaystyle=p_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle(p_{t+1}^{m,1}+d_{t+1}^{m,1})\pi_{t+1}^{1}v_{t+1}^{1}+(p_{t+1}^{m,2}d_{t+1}^{m,2})\pi_{t+1}^{2}v_{t+1}^{2}+\dots+(p_{t+1}^{m,n}+d_{t+1}^{m,n})\pi_{t+1}^{n}v_{t+1}^{n}

We still assume that the market is complete;
i.e.

n
. As a result, there is a unique solution for stochastic discount factor (SDF) . Apparently, one solution of SDF is

\boldsymbol{m}_{t+1}=\left(\pi_{t+1}^{1}\cdot\frac{1}{1+r},\pi_{t+1}^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{1+r},\dots,\pi_{t+1}^{n}\cdot\frac{1}{1+r}\right)^{T}

At different times, there are different SDF’s, as payoff matrix and portfolio cost vector both change over time.

3
Dynamic Asset Pricing Theory in a Multiple Step Model

3.1
Pricing of Risk Free Assets

Most financial literature refer to this model as
time value of money
. Although most results are already well established, there is need to highlight some issues with differential equations used in the current literature.

3.1.1
Continuous Discounting

Assuming there is only 1 risk-free asset, whose contract is binding (can be enforced). Dividend payments are made continuously from time
0
to time
t=n
, subject to payment rate function
\delta_{p,t}
. There is risk-free force of interest (FOI) that’s equal to
\delta_{t}
at time
t
.

Between the time
t
and
t+\Delta t
, we can approximate the effective interest rate by

\delta_{t}\times\Delta t

(8)

There is only one random state of the world, so applying FTAP, we have:

p_{t}=\frac{1}{1+\delta_{t}\times\Delta t}(\delta_{p,t}\times\Delta t+p_{t+\Delta t})

(9)

…which can be rewritten into a differential equation

\frac{dp_{t}}{dt}-p_{t}\delta_{t}=-\delta_{p,t}

(10)

The solution to the ODE is the valuation formula for risk-free assets:

p_{0}=\int_{0}^{n}\delta_{p,t}e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{s}ds}dt+p_{n}e^{-\int_{0}^{n}\delta_{t}dt}

(11)

3.1.2
Recursive Valuation Property

We first discount dividend payments between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, and asset price on
t_{2}
, back to
t_{1}
:

p_{t_{1}}=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta_{p,t}e^{-\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\delta_{s}ds}dt+p_{t_{2}}e^{-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta_{t}dt}

Now that we know asset price at time
t_{1}
, we can discount this asset price, along with dividend payments between
t_{0}
and
t_{1}
, back to
t_{0}
:

\displaystyle p_{t_{0}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\delta_{p,t}e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\delta_{s}ds}dt+p_{t_{1}}e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\delta_{t}dt}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\delta_{p,t}e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\delta_{s}ds}dt+\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta_{p,t}e^{-\int_{t_{1}}^{t}\delta_{s}ds}dt+p_{t_{2}}e^{-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta_{t}dt}\right)e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\delta_{t}dt}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{2}}\delta_{p,t}e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\delta_{s}ds}dt+p_{t_{2}}e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{2}}\delta_{t}dt}

3.2
Pricing of Risky Assets

3.2.1
Assumption: adapted processes

We first define adapted process – a concept in probability theory and stochastic process. Then we limit the problem we solve to dynamic economies with asset-related variables that are “adapted” to information structure (uncertainty tree). An informal definition of
“adapted” process

\{X_{t}\}
is that this is (1) a time-dependent sequence of random variables, where you (2) “only know what you’re supposed to know at each point time”.
E.g.
, as of time
t
, you should know the value of the random variables
X_{s},s\leq t
; however, you don’t know what value will be realized for
X_{s},s>t
. In dynamic asset pricing theory, the following processes are assumed to be “adapted”:

•

Asset prices of the
i
-th asset:
\{p_{t}^{i}\}

•

Stochastic discount factors (SDF):
\{m_{t}\}

•

Dividends of the
i
-th asset:
\{d_{t}^{i}\}

In dynamic asset pricing theory, asset prices, SDF’s, and dividends at future times
s\;(s>t)
are considered random variables as of time
t
because their exact values are unknown as of time
t
; however, we assume that the (risk neutral) probability distribution of these random variables is known and can be modeled given the information available at time
t
.

3.2.2
Information Sets

The information set
I_{t}
contains all the information made available on
and
before time
t
, which includes dividend amounts, asset prices, stochastic discoun factors, on and before
t
. Information set
I_{t}
also includes conditional probabilities of getting into any random state of the world in future. Hence, there is information sets at different times that are subject to:

•

For all
0\leq s\leq t
, it must be that
I_{s}\subseteq I_{t}

•

For all
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\dots\leq t_{n}\leq\dots
, it must be that
I_{t_{1}}\subseteq I_{t_{2}}\subseteq\dots\subseteq I_{t_{n}}\subseteq\dots

•

Tower property
– if
I_{1}\subseteq I_{2}\subseteq\dots\subseteq I_{n}
,

E[E[\dots E[X|I_{n}]\dots|I_{2}]|I_{1}]=E[X|I_{1}]

•

A special case is that for
t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\dots\leq t_{n}
,

E[E[\dots E[X|I_{t_{n}}]\dots|I_{t_{2}}]|I_{t_{1}}]=E[X|I_{t_{1}}]

•

“Taking out what is known”
– if information set
I
includes the value of
Z
,

E[ZX|I]=ZE[X|I]

•

If the process
\{X_{t}\}
is adapted process, then

E[X_{t}Y|I_{t}]=X_{t}E[Y|I_{t}]

Probability literature would compare information set
I_{t}
to the
\sigma
-algebra or filtration made available at time
t
. I argue that
I_{t}
doesn’t just include the
\sigma
-algebra, as the information about past dividends specifies the exact event (within
\sigma
-algebra) that is happening at time
t
. Such event is a collection of random states of the world, and the revelation of the exact random state will happen at a future time.

Another reason that information set is different from
\sigma
-algebra is that information set includes conditional probabilities of random states that will could be attained in future.

3.2.3
Pricing Kernel

We may introduce a new process –
pricing kernel
. At time
t
,
a_{t}=m_{1}m_{2}\dots m_{t}
. Because stochastic discount factor process
\{m_{t}\}
is an adapted process, pricing kernel
\{a_{t}\}
is another adapted process –
a_{t}
is known as of time
t
. For any
t
,
a_{t}
is only contingent on the state of the world – this is because all
m_{t}
’s are contingent on the state. Since

p_{t}=E_{\pi}[m_{t+1}(p_{t+1}+d_{t+1})|I_{t}]

(12)

Multiplying
a_{t}
to the two sides

a_{t}p_{t}=a_{t}E_{\pi}[m_{t+1}(p_{t+1}+d_{t+1})|I_{t}]

(13)

Using “taking out what’s known” property, we have:

a_{t}p_{t}=E_{\pi}[a_{t+1}(p_{t+1}+d_{t+1})|I_{t}]

(14)

3.2.4
“Reduced Lottery” Method of Finding Asset Price

By recursively applying FTAP to the multiple step model:

\displaystyle p_{t}

\displaystyle=E_{\pi}[m_{t+1}(p_{t+1}+d_{t+1})|I_{t}]

\displaystyle=E_{\pi}[m_{t+1}(E_{\pi}[m_{t+2}(p_{t+2}+d_{t+2})|I_{t+1}]+d_{t+1})|I_{t}]

\displaystyle=E_{\pi}[m_{t+1}d_{t+1}+E_{\pi}[m_{t+1}m_{t+2}(p_{t+2}+d_{t+2})|I_{t+1}]|I_{t}]

\displaystyle=E_{\pi}[m_{t+1}d_{t+1}+m_{t+1}m_{t+2}d_{t+2}+m_{t+1}m_{t+2}p_{t+2}|I_{t}]

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle=E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{i=t+1}^{T}\prod_{j=t+1}^{i}m_{j}d_{i}+p_{T}\prod_{i=t+1}^{T}m_{i}\vline I_{t}\right]

Multiplying
a_{t}
to the two sides, and using “taking out what’s known” property, we have:

a_{t}p_{t}=E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{i=t+1}^{T}a_{i}d_{i}+a_{T}p_{T}\vline I_{t}\right]

(15)

The above conditional expectations are computed using risk neutral probabilities, that are the probabilities of the reduced lottery. As of time
t
: all random variables (
a_{i},d_{i},p_{i};\;t<i\leq T
) are contingent on the state of the world; however,
a_{i},d_{i},p_{i},i\leq t
are constant (deterministic) numbers.

3.2.5
“Asset Prices Are Discounted Martingales”

Assuming there is no intermediate dividend payouts under all random states, or:

d_{i}=0\quad t<i\leq T

(16)

The above valuation formula reduces to

a_{t}p_{t}=E_{\pi}[a_{T}p_{T}|I_{t}]

(17)

This result is often referred to as “asset prices are discounted martingales”. In this case, the stochastic process
\{a_{t}p_{t}\}
is a
martingale
, adapted to time
t
.

3.3
Pricing of Life Contingent Assets

3.3.1
Force of Mortality

Actuaries are interested in mortality rates. The mortality rate over one year for
(x)
is
q_{x}
:

q_{x}=1-p_{x}=1-\frac{S_{0}(x+1)}{S_{0}(x)}=-\frac{S_{0}(x+1)-S_{0}(x)}{S_{0}(x)}

(18)

The mortality rate over an instantaneous period is called
force of mortality
. Force of mortality for
(x)
is denoted as

3.3.2
Pricing of Whole Life Insurance Policies

In this section, we use such conditional probability as we apply Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing (FTAP) to
continuous whole life insurance policy
.

•

In the whole life insurance – continuous time model,

•

A person can either survive or pass away within a short amount of time

•

We assume force of mortality (FOM) is subject to the function
\mu_{t}

•

We assume there is a limiting age
\omega
(the age at which all survivors pass away)

•

We assume that upon death, there is a one-time death benefit of amount
b_{t}
, paid right at the time of death

•

b_{t}
is actual benefit amount in dollars, not payment rate

•

We assume force of interest (FOI) is subject to the function
\delta_{t}

The conditional probability of death within the short time period of
\Delta t
is
\mu_{t}\times\Delta t
, and the conditional probability of survival within the same time is
1-\mu_{t}\times\Delta t
. Therefore, according to Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing,

p_{t}^{1}=\mu_{t}\cdot\Delta_{t}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\delta_{t}\Delta_{t}}(0+b_{t+\Delta t})+(1-\mu_{t}\cdot\Delta t)\cdot\frac{1}{1+\delta_{t}\Delta t}(p_{t+\Delta t}^{1}+0)

(20)

The upper scripte 1 highlights that we are modeling the asset price under state 1 – the state of survival at all times, until limiting age
\omega
. We do not model asset price of any death state, as price is zero in any of these random states (no dividend payout in future).

The above equation can be rewritten to reveal the average rate of change of asset price w.r.t. time under state 1:

\displaystyle\frac{p_{t+\Delta t}^{1}-p_{t}^{1}}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{t}^{1}\delta_{t}\Delta t-\mu_{t}\Delta tb_{t+\Delta t}+\mu_{t}\Delta tp_{t+\Delta t}^{1}}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle=p_{t}^{1}\delta t-\mu_{t}b_{t+\Delta t}+\mu_{t}p_{t+\Delta t}^{1}

\displaystyle\approx p_{t}^{1}\delta_{t}-\mu_{t}b_{t}+\mu_{t}p_{t}^{1}\quad(\Delta t\to 0)

We develop the ordinary differential equation (ODE)

\frac{dp_{t}^{1}}{dt}=p_{t}(\delta_{t}+\mu_{t})-\mu_{t}b^{t}

(21)

Because there is the assumption of limiting age
\omega
, the latest possible time for
b_{t}
to be paid is
t=\omega
;
in other words
, no dividends will be paid after
t=\omega
under all states of the world. Hence, asset price at
t=\omega
is zero for all states, and state 1 is no exception.
Therefore, we develop the boundary condition

p_{\omega}^{1}=0

(22)

By solving the ODE along with the boundary condition, we have:

\displaystyle p_{t}^{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{\omega}\mu_{u}b_{u}e^{-\int_{0}^{u}\delta_{s}+\mu_{s}ds}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{s}+\mu_{s}ds}du

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{\omega}\mu_{u}b_{u}e^{-\int_{t}^{u}\delta_{s}+\mu_{s}ds}du

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{\omega}\mu_{u}b_{u}e^{-\int_{t}^{u}\delta_{s}ds}e^{-\int_{t}^{u}\mu_{s}ds}du
