Gastrointestinal fate of proteins from commercial plant–based meat analogs: Silent passage through the stomach, oxidative stress in intestine, and gut dysbiosis in Wistar rats
Authors: G. Sánchez‐Terrón, R. Martínez, M. J. Freire, J. Molina‐Infante, M. Estévez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant‐based meat analogs (PBMAs) are common ultra‐processed foods (UPFs) included in the vegan/vegetarian diets as presumed healthy alternatives to meat and meat products. However, such health claims need to be supported by scientific evidence. To gain further insight into this topic, two commercial UPFs typically sold as meat analogs, namely, seitan (S) and tofu (T), were included in a cereal‐based chow and provided to Wistar rats for 10 weeks. A group of animals had, simultaneously, an isocaloric and isoprotein experimental diet formulated with cooked beef (B). In all cases, experimental chows (∼4 kcal/g feed) had their basal protein concentration increased from 14% to 30% using proteins from S, T, or B. Upon slaughter, in vivo protein digestibility was assessed, and the entire gastrointestinal tract (digests and tissues) was analyzed for markers of oxidative stress and untargeted metabolomics. Metagenomics was also applied to assess the variation of microbiota composition as affected by dietary protein. Diets based on PBMAs showed lower protein digestibility than those containing meat and promoted an intense luminal glycoxidative stress and an inflammatory intestinal response. The fermentation of undigested oxidized proteins from T in the colon of Wistar rats likely led to formation of mutagenic metabolites such as p‐cresol. The presence of these compounds in the animal models raises concerns about the potential effects of full replacement of meat by certain PBMAs in the diet. Therefore, future research might target on translational human studies to shed light on these findings.

INTRODUCTION

Consumer awareness on health, environmental sustainability, and ethical issues explains the growing interest in replacing animal‐sourced proteins by alternative sources of protein (Petti et al., 2017). Moreover, meats and processed meats have been classified as a Group 2A (probably carcinogen) and Group 1 (carcinogen), respectively, by the World Health Organization (WHO) and International Agency for Research on Cancer (WHO, 2015), so certain consumers may reject meat consumption for any or all of the aforementioned reasons. However, a significant number of them appreciate its sensory and hedonic properties, and hence, are inclined to consume the so called meat analogs, which are industrially produced using proteins from plant origin (Estévez et al., 2024). In fact, the design, production, and availability of commercial plant‐based meat analogs (PBMAs) have increased remarkably in recent years (Kołodziejczak et al., 2022; Shan et al., 2023). Yet, the nutritional value and health impact of these vegan products are not well understood.

In regard to the nutritional value, it is worth emphasizing that such value may not only be assessed by the content and composition of their components but also by the behavior, fate, and hence, bioavailability of such nutrients in the gastrointestinal tract (GIT). This is particularly relevant for proteins as meat is known as an excellent source of high‐quality proteins. The effective delivery of essential amino acids by dietary proteins depends on their susceptibility to proteolysis after entering the GIT and the efficiency of the transepithelial uptake of peptides/amino acids following digestion (Chen et al., 2022). On this line, the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations is currently recommending the assessment of protein quality and digestibility by the digestible indispensable amino acid score (DIAAS) (Chen et al., 2022; Ishaq et al., 2022). Based on DIAAS values, authors have classified some animal‐ and plant‐derived proteins under different claims of quality. Proteins from animal sources, such as beef, pork, or egg, have been identified as of “excellent quality” (Bailey et al., 2020; Herreman et al., 2020). Proteins from PBMAs, such as seitan (wheat gluten‐based) and tofu (soy‐based), were classified as “low‐quality protein” and “high‐quality protein,” respectively (Herreman et al., 2020; Reynaud et al., 2021). Overall, plant proteins are known to commonly display inferior digestibility compared to their animal counterparts (Khalesi & Fitzgerald, 2021). Moreover, plant materials may contain anti‐nutritional factors and/or compounds such as tannins or polysaccharides that could impair protein digestibility (Kaur et al., 2022). Although scientific literature lacks studies comparing the true digestibility of animal‐ and plant‐source proteins, available data indicate that the latter shows reduced digestibility than the former in both in vitro (Chen et al., 2022) and in vivo (Xie et al., 2022) studies. Yet, the molecular mechanism behind that reduced digestibility is mostly unknown. According to our hypothesis, the severe processing, to which proteins from PBMAs are subjected, could affect their composition, structure, and digestibility. As stated by Shan et al. (2023), the ultra‐processing of certain vegan foods is a two‐edged knife as it is required to achieve desired rheological properties and meaty texture but may seriously affect the digestibility and hence, overall nutritional quality of the plant protein. Given the severe processing undergone by certain PBMAs and the occurrence of a number of additives (Liu & Chang, 2008; Yuliarti et al., 2021), some of these vegan products could be classified as ultra‐processed foods (UPFs) according to NOVA criteria (NOVA‐4) (Monteiro et al., 2017). It is worth noting, however, that tofu and seitan commercially available in mainstream Western supermarket chains differ considerably from those traditionally produced in Asian countries where they have been consumed along with animal‐sourced foods (ASF) for thousands of years (Fukushima, 1981).

The impact of the intake of commercial vegan UPFs on gut health is poorly understood. Although these foods may generally be regarded as healthy alternatives to animal‐based food owing to its plant‐based origin (Miyashiro et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2023), the WHO recently reported health concerns on these UPFs (WHO, 2021). The health organization linked the sustained consumption of plant‐based UPFs with a number of chronic diseases, such as diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer, among others. The extent to which this association is causal and the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain unknown. Understanding the actual impact of these meat analogs on consumer health is of utmost scientific and societal interest. According to our hypothesis, both the poor digestibility of plant‐based UPFs and their toxicological effects may be linked. It is known that gastric and intestinal proteases could fail to recognize and digest severely oxidized proteins (He et al., 2018; Li et al., 2017). Undigested oxidized proteins could reach the colon, where microbiota will use them as a nitrogen source, modifying the relative abundance of different groups of microbial populations (Portune et al., 2016; Sánchez‐Terrón et al., 2023). The environment generated in the colon during the microbial catabolism of undigested proteins could trigger an intense oxidative scene involved in the development of several inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) (Rodríguez‐Romero et al., 2022). However, it is still unknown whether oxidized proteins from UPFs display an impaired digestibility, the extent to which they are fermented, and which are the consequences of such abnormal digestion patterns on gut homeostasis and health.

Little is known about the behavior of proteins from PBMAs during digestive processes. For this reason, our study provides novel insights into the effects of long‐term (10 weeks) intake of PBMAs in experimental animals. Two high‐protein diets (30%) designed with plant‐based proteins (wheat gluten and texturized soy protein) were compared with an isocaloric and isoproteic diet formulated with cooked beef to assess the extent to which these alleged meat analogs compare to the real meat in terms of true digestibility, impact on microbiota, and gut health.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals

External standards of protein carbonyls, namely, α‐aminoadipic and γ‐glutamic semialdehydes (α‐AS and γ‐GS, respectively) were acquired from Sigma Aldrich. The reagents used to metagenomic analyses were provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific. Ultrapure water was prepared using a Milli‐Q water purification system (Millipore Corp.). All other chemicals, solvents, and reagents were acquired from Fisher Scientific.

Meat and meat analogs

Beef loin (M. longissimus dorsi) from industrial genotypes (Charolaise × Limousine) slaughtered at 12 months of age was obtained from a slaughterhouse in Cáceres (Spain). After 10 days of wet aging (2°C), the muscle was sliced into 2.5 cm steaks and then roasted at 220°C for 10 min. Cooked beef was then incorporated into feeds as explained below. Temperature was measured with an infrared thermometer provided by Louis Tellier SAS. Commercial seitan, generally known as vegan meat and produced from wheat gluten, and commercial tofu were obtained from a supermarket chain in Spain (Mercadona). Commercial plant‐based analogs were shaped into slices of similar dimensions to beef steaks, and subsequently, submitted to the same cooking procedure (220°C roasting for 10 min) prior to their incorporation into experimental feeds. The list of ingredients and additives and the nutritional claims of the plant products provided by the supplier are detailed in Table S1.

Animals and feeds

All experimental procedures complied with the Helsinki declaration and the applicable Spanish legal requirements (RD 53/2013). Therefore, the Bioethics Committee of the University of Extremadura (137‐2020) and the Animal Experimentation Ethical Board (EXP20200904) from the Regional Government of Extremadura approved all protocols and procedures described as follows. Twenty‐one male Wistar rats were used in our study. The animals were supplied by the Animal Experimentation Station from the University of Extremadura (Caceres, Spain). The experimental diets of the rats were designed using the rodent's feed Teklad Global Diet 2014, provided by ENVIGO as a basis. The composition of this feed is detailed in Table S2). The different experimental feeds were developed with the aim of increasing the protein content of the basic formulation (i.e., 14%) to 30% in the experimental diets, providing isocaloric and isoproteic chows formulated with proteins from different sources (animal vs. plants). The different high‐protein diets were formulated as follows. The basic feed was milled, and second, mixed with the exact required amounts of cooked beef, seitan, and tofu to reach the target protein concentration in the final feed. To guarantee the homogenous mixture of the basal feed with the protein sources, 200 g of water was added for every 1000 g of feed. Once minced and mechanically blended for 15 min, the mixture was molded to obtain experimental feed pellets (3 cm length × 1 cm width × 1 m height). The experimental pellets were dried under forced air at 25°C for 48 h to reduce the moisture content to approximately 7%. The composition of the experimental feeds was analyzed using official Association of Official Agricultural Chemists (AOAC) methods (AOAC, 1990) by using pellets produced from truly replicated manufacturing processes.

Experimental design

At the beginning of the assay, the rats were individually identified through a perforation code in the auditory pavilion. They were maintained in ventilated cages for a week of adaptation during which water and feed were supplied ad libitum, and the environmental conditions were controlled (20–22°C temperature, 40%–50% humidity, and 12–12 h light/dark cycle).

Once the adaptation period finished, animals were randomly allocated into one of three experimental groups as follows: (i) control group (B), which received the feed made from cooked beef (n = 7); (ii) commercial seitan group (S), which consumed the seitan‐based chow (n = 7); and (iii) commercial tofu group (T), which were fed the tofu‐based chow (n = 7). The assay was conducted for 10 weeks. Both feeds and drinking water were provided ad libitum for all groups until slaughter.

Collection of data on live animals

Animals were checked daily to ensure their well‐being and safety. During the experiment, food and water consumptions were gravimetrically monitored every time they were filled, depending on the demand of the animals (every 2 or 3 days, approximately). Furthermore, bodyweights were registered weekly.

Slaughter, necropsy, and sampling

At the end of the experimental period, the rats were euthanized by exsanguination via cardiac puncture under 5% isoflurane, at an approximate age of 16–17 weeks old and an average weight of 437 g. The stomach, small intestine (jejunum), cecum, and large intestine (distal colon) were aseptically sampled and clamped to avoid loss of intraluminal material (digests). Then, the digests were gently removed, and the emptied tissue from each location was thoroughly cleaned with cold distilled water. The digests and tissues were dispensed in Eppendorf tubes and stored at −80°C until analyses were performed. A portion of intestinal tissues was preserved in 5% formalin for microscopic analyses. Feces from the rectum were also aseptically collected and stored at −80°C until metagenomic analyses were performed.

Assessment of oxidative stress in digests and GIT tissues: analytical methods

Protein carbonylation

The identification and quantification of specific protein carbonyls, that is, α‐AS and γ‐GS, in the feeds, luminal contents, feces, and tissues, were evaluated as previously described by Sánchez‐Terrón et al. (2023). Briefly, 250 mg of the stomach, jejunum, cecum, and colon digests, as well as feces, were individually mixed and homogenized with 1 mL of PBS in Eppendorf tubes in a mixer mill. Likewise, 500 mg of the respective tissues were homogenized with 0.5 mL of PBS. An HPLC analysis attached to a fluorescence detector was carried out, and the results from the quantification of individual semialdehydes were additionally expressed as total primary protein carbonyls (PPCs), all as nmol carbonyl/mg protein. The detailed description of the remaining steps of the chromatographic procedure was reported by Utrera et al. (2011).

Protein glycation

Advanced protein oxidation products (APOPs) were analyzed using fluorescent spectroscopy (PerkinElmer), as reported by Luna et al. (2021). Samples were homogenized and then diluted in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, containing 2 M guanidine chloride. APOPs were excited at 350 nm, and fluorescence emission was detected from 400 to 500 nm. Fluorescence data were normalized using a correction factor (Cf = Pt/Pp), where Pt represents the average protein content from all samples, and Pp is the protein content of each individual sample. Results are showed as arbitrary fluorescence intensity units.

Lipid oxidation

Following the procedure reported by Ganhão et al. (2011), with some modifications, malondialdehyde (MDA) and other thiobarbituric reactive substances (TBARSs) were extracted from feeds, luminal contents, feces, and tissues and subsequently quantified. Samples from homogenates were treated with eight volumes of perchloric acid (3.86%) and 0.5 volumes of butylated hydroxytoluene (4.2% in ethanol) to avoid further peroxidation. Upon a reaction with 0.02 M thiobarbituric acid, samples were placed in a boiling water bath (100°C) for 45 min together with the tubes from the standard curve, which was prepared using 1,1,3,3‐tetraethoxypropane solution in 3.86% perchloric acid. After cooling, the absorbance was measured at 532 nm by spectrophotometry (Shimadzu Model UV‐1800, Shimadzu). Results were calculated as milligrams of MDA per 100 g of the sample.

Protein degradation and digestibility

Moisture content and concentration of proteins were analyzed in the basal feed, digests, and tissues from each compartment from GIT using the official AOAC methods (AOAC, 1990). The Kjeldahl method was performed as previously described by other authors (Etheridge et al., 1998) in order to analyze total nitrogen, water‐soluble nitrogen (WSN) content and non‐protein nitrogen (NPN) in feed and digests. For the WSN, samples were homogenized twice with 5 volumes (w/v) of deionized water and centrifuged at 5000 g and 4°C for 10 min. Combined supernatants were filtered and subsequently subjected to the Kjeldahl method for nitrogen quantification. For the quantification of NPN, an aliquot of the filtrate was mixed with an equal volume of 20% trichloroacetic acid, allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min, centrifuged at 5000 g at 4°C for 10 min, and then filtered. Indispensable variables, such as total protein nitrogen and total dietary nitrogen, were calculated from WSN and NPN to calculate the values of true protein digestibility (TPD), total dietary protein (TDP), and TDP degraded in the digests from the GIT as previously described (Sánchez‐Terrón et al., 2023). For further accuracy, all protein data are shown as dry matter. The combination of TDP degraded at the stomach and at the small intestine was considered digested protein, whereas TDP degraded at both the cecum and the colon was considered fermented protein.

Microscopy analysis of intestinal tissues

Sections from formalin‐fixed intestine samples from treated rats were microscopically assessed by an experienced veterinary pathologist unaware of the animal treatment groups. Paraffin‐embedded specimens were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated with successive washes in an increased gradient of ethanol concentrations. Then, they were stained with hematoxylin 80% (LabKem) and eosin yellowish (Panreac), and the slides were examined using a Leica DFC‐280 light microscope. The Leica Q Win Plus Image Analysis System (Leica Micros Imaging Solutions Ltd) was used for morphometric analysis. The diameter of Peyer's patches (PPs) was measured in small (jejunum) and large intestine (colon) and used as a marker of gut inflammation.

Untargeted MS‐based metabolomic analysis

Metabolites of the different gastrointestinal digests from Wistar rats were extracted and analyzed. The extraction was carried out with both an aqueous and an organic solvent to get most of the metabolites. Briefly, 100 µL of homogenized stomach, jejunum, and colon digests, respectively, were individually homogenized with both 0.5 mL of cyclohexane and 0.5 mL of MilliQ‐water, and subsequently centrifuged at 9000 g at 4°C for 15 min. The two phases obtained were separated into single Eppendorf tubes using 0.22 µm nylon filters. To ensure a correct flux through the column, the aqueous phases were individually mixed with 50 µL of HPLC quality acetonitrile. Samples were analyzed using a Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLC system coupled with a Q‐Exactive high‐resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Analysis conditions, such as column types, phases, injection volume, or run conditions, were similar to those described in previous works carried out by our group (Sánchez‐Terrón et al., 2023). The routine calibration and optimization of the equipment, together with the metabolite extraction method, set Level 2 of the classification levels proposed by the published literature for the identification and characterization of the metabolites (Sumner et al., 2007).

Metagenomics analysis

Microbiota from Wistar rats was analyzed from feces obtained at slaughter using a metagenomics analyses. DNA was isolated using the MagMAX microbiome ultra nucleic acid isolation kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's instructions and the KingFisher Flex Instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Specific primers for V3 and V4 variable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were used to genomic DNA amplification. Once DNA was amplified, sequencing and basic analyses were performed using an Illumina MiSeq platform, the MiSeq Reagent Kit v3, and the 300b paired end. QIIME2 v2021.4 was used for the analysis of the generated raw sequence data. Finally, the operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were classified by taxon using the SILVA database (release 138 QIIME) and trained by a scikit‐learn classifier using the UNITE (release 8.3) database. The results of the raw counts generated by the analysis were expressed as relative frequency (%). Different α‐diversity indices (i.e., dominance, taxa richness, individuals, Shannon index, Simpson index, and evenness) were calculated from genus OTUs’ counts using the software package Past v4.09, and the results were expressed as log2.

Statistical analysis

Parametric and non‐parametric tests were used to conduct the statistical analysis of the raw data using R statistical software (R.4.3.3.) and SPSS 29.0 version. Thus, the relative frequencies of OTUs at the phylum level, as well as the different α‐diversity indices, were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests given the normal and homoscedastic distribution of the data. However, the metagenomic results at the genus level were analyzed using Kruskal–Wallis test. Box plots and line charts were generated using ggplot2 (Wickham, 2011). p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. B‐, S‐, and T‐responsive metabolites were assessed in the MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). Principal component analysis (PCA) as multivariate analyses was used, and the differences between the normalized intensities of the metabolites from each group were evaluated by the ANOVA test as one‐factor statistical method to further analyze the impact of the diets on the metabolome of the different digests from the GIT of Wistar rats. Moreover, we applied MetaboAnalyst 5.0 to obtain a visual heatmap of the top 50 metabolites established as loadings of the multivariate analysis partial least‐squares discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA) and the variable importance in projection (VIP) from PLS‐DA outcomes, of the colonic intraluminal contents of the experimental animals.

RESULTS

Effect of PBMAs on feed consumption and weight of experimental animals

Table 1 shows the nutritional composition and caloric value of the three experimental diets. In accordance with the proposed objectives, all diets had the same protein concentration and provided the same caloric density. The concentration of all other components was influenced by the composition of the protein sources employed for their design. S‐based diet had a significantly higher concentration of carbohydrates than the T‐based diet, whereas B diet had intermediate concentration of this macronutrient. At the contrary, T diet exhibited the highest amounts of lipids, followed by B‐ and S‐based diets, with the latter showing the lowest concentration of this component. The B‐diet provided significantly higher amounts of both saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids than its plant‐based counterparts. Consistently, the plant‐based analogs contributed with significantly higher concentration of polyunsaturated fatty acids. Despite these differences, the weekly mean consumption of feed (Figure S1), and the evolution of bodyweights of the rats, were equivalent for all experimental groups (Figure S2).

Effect of PBMAs consumption on glycoxidative stress on feeds, digests, and feces

Protein glycoxidation in digests at different locations of GIT

Table 2 shows the concentration of PPCs, α‐AS, γ‐GS, and total PPCs (sum of both species) in the feed, digests at each stage of the GIT, and in the feces of the rats. Irrespective of the treatment, there were significant differences among the levels of protein carbonylation in the digests from each stage (p < 0.001). Namely, the concentrations of α‐AS and γ‐GS, as well as the sum of both, were significantly lower in the stomach contents of the animals than in the feeds. The concentration of total PPCs also showed a decrease in the luminal content at the jejunum stage (−47% than those in the stomach content) (p < 0.001). Then, the concentration of these protein oxidation markers gradually increased during the advanced stages of the digestion, with the colon being the compartment where the highest concentration of luminal carbonyls was found regardless of protein‐source of the diets. In fact, the concentration of total PPCs in the intraluminal contents at the colonic stage was twofold higher than the values found in the stomach contents. Interestingly, the concentration of these protein carbonyls decreased in the feces around −62% as compared to the previous colonic stage (p < 0.001). Feeds formulated with PBMAs had a significantly higher concentration of α‐AS, γ‐GS, and total PPCs than B‐diets. The same differences were found among groups for intraluminal contents at the different stages of the digestion and in the feces of the treated animals (p < 0.001). At the colonic stage, PBMA‐digests had the highest values of PPCs. The concentrations of total PPCs in the feces of the animals also showed different trends depending on the diet applied. The highest values of PPCs were found in the feces from S animals and the lowest ones on the feces from B rats.

In addition to the protein oxidation markers described above, Table 2 shows the evolution of APOPs in the digests during their passage along the GIT stages as markers of advanced protein oxidation processes. The intensity of the fluorescence emitted by APOPs increased at the first stages of digestion (stomach/jejunum), whereas these compounds decreased in the intraluminal contents of both cecum and colon, significantly reaching the lowest values of fluorescence intensity in the feces from the animals. Feeds formulated with PBMAs displayed higher amounts of APOPs than the B‐feeds and such differences persisted during all stages of the GIT, as well as in the feces (p < 0.001). The S treatment promoted the highest amount of APOPs in the digests of the stomach, jejunum, colon, and in the feces, compared to B (p < 0.001). T treatment showed the highest values of APOPs in the feeds and in the cecum contents, as compared to B. Both PBMAs diets (T vs. S) behaved similarly in regard to the concentration of APOPs as no significant differences were observed among the levels of these species at the GIT stages under study.

The interaction between the protein source of the diet and the effect of the different GIT stages was statistically significant for all parameters analyzed (p < 0.05), meaning that the effect of PBMAs on the intraluminal oxidative stress is dependent on the location.

Lipid oxidation in digests at different compartments of GIT

Table 2 also shows the concentration of TBARs in feeds, digests, and feces, as an expression of the extent of lipid oxidation. Lipid oxidation increased in the jejunal digests from the GIT of B‐ and S‐rats from basal levels found in the feeds (p < 0.05). The mean values significantly decreased in the contents of the next stages of GIT and in the feces of the animals. T‐based diets had a significant effect on the extent of lipid oxidation, showing higher values of TBARs in the feeds and at the different stages of the GIT as compared to the B‐diet (p < 0.05).

Protein digestibility

Total TPD values are displayed in Figure 1a. Data reveal that the protein digestibility from the feed formulated with beef (B) was significantly higher than the digestibility of proteins from plant‐based diets: 98.12% (beef) vs. 92.03% (seitan) and 92.72% (tofu) (p < 0.01). To gain further insights into the underlying mechanisms, the evolution of TDP was analyzed at the different stages of the GIT and in the feces of experimental animals (Figure 1b). Regardless of the type of diet supplied, TDP decreased as the digests advanced through the different stages of the GIT from the experimental animals. Nevertheless, the trend of the decline was different among the groups. Thus, the concentrations of TDP in the stomach from S rats were higher than those in the stomachs from the B and T counterparts. These results were reflected in the calculation of degraded TDP (Figure 2a), where S rats showed the lowest amounts of luminal degraded proteins at the stomach stage compared to those from B and T rats (7.24% vs. 22.25% and 14.51%, respectively) (p < 0.001). However, at the jejunum stage, an opposite trend was observed. S rats showed the highest amounts of luminal degraded proteins compared to their B and T counterparts. Overall, 75.77% and 74.23% of dietary proteins were digested at the upper stages of the GIT (i.e., stomach and jejunum) from B and S rats, whereas 65.03% of dietary protein were digested in rats supplied with T (p < 0.001). On the other hand, 24.23% and 25.77% of the dietary proteins were degraded at the final stages of the GIT (cecum and colon) from B and S rats, respectively, whereas significant higher amounts of proteins were degraded at the final stages of the GIT from T rats (around 35%) (p < 0.001) (Figure 2b).

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, the current study provides the first in vivo and stage‐by‐stage assessment of the behavior and fate of proteins from diets formulated with two different vegan UPFs along the GIT. The behavior and fate of proteins from these plant‐based analogs are compared to that undergone by proteins from beef. Our results are novel in highlighting a completely alike behavior of different dietary proteins during digestion, which is, in our opinion, very much driven by the degree of digestibility of such proteins in the first compartments of the GIT (stomach/jejunum). A reduced digestibility of plant‐based analogs at these early stages determines the impact of proteins from these UPFs at further GIT locations, including impairment of the microbiota (dysbiosis), the onset of oxidative stress and inflammation, and the formation of potentially toxic compounds. Unlike many other original and/or review papers aimed to establish connection between the intake of UPFs and physiological/health outcomes based on epidemiological correlations, we aim to establish causative connections among the intake of protein from UPFs, the impact of processing on the load of oxidized proteins, the fate of such proteins in the GIT, and the impact on gut health.

Oxidation of dietary proteins before and after ingestion

So far, most scientific and academic literatures assess the nutritional value of dietary proteins based on compositional assessments: quantity and quality (∼amino acid profile). Yet, the impact of dietary proteins on nutrition and health is highly dependent on the mechanisms involved in their fate along the GIT and that, in turn, is dependent on their structure, composition and the extent to which these proteins have suffered oxidative reactions of aggregation and/or degradation prior to consumption (i.e., during industrial processing).

Oxidized proteins and amino acids are formed during food harvesting, handling, storage, and processing (Soladoye et al., 2015). According to our results, the extent of protein oxidation in the vegan UPFs was much higher than in freshly cooked beef. As expected, this degree of oxidation was reflected in the experimental feeds. The levels of protein oxidation markers in the feeds supplied to the experimental animals revealed higher amounts of total PPCs in those made from PBMAs, that is, S (1.48 nmol carbonyl/mg protein) and T (2.26 nmol carbonyl/mg protein), than in their B counterpart (0.63 nmol carbonyl/mg protein), which might be caused by the inherent ultra‐processing to which plant proteins are subjected. Meaningful differences were found for APOPs, which illustrated better than protein carbonyls the extent to which proteins may be aggregated due to advanced oxidative reactions.

Further to the extent of oxidative damage in dietary proteins, these components suffer further oxidative changes during digestion. In fact, several in vitro and in vivo studies illustrate a considerable increase in oxidative reactions during the digestion of dietary proteins (Nieva‐Echevarría et al., 2020; Steppeler et al., 2016; Van Hecke et al., 2015). In this regard, the stomach has been recognized as a bioreactor in which harsh oxidation of biomolecules occurs (Kanner & Lapidot, 2001; Oueslati et al., 2016; Sánchez‐Terrón et al., 2023; Van Hecke et al., 2021). Therefore, the onset of luminal oxidative stress, that is, the occurrence of lipid and protein oxidation in digests, could be a combination of already formed oxidation products from UPFs and the further oxidation of food components during digestion (Estévez & Xiong, 2019). Throughout the compartments of the GIT, the digests from rats fed both S and T showed higher levels of protein oxidation markers than those from B rats, reaching the highest values in the colonic digests of the animals, which would support the associations between the intake of oxidized proteins and increased luminal oxidative stress made by several authors (Estévez & Xiong, 2019). These findings diverge from the reports made by Ahmad et al. (2020), Macho‐González et al. (2020), and Tappel (2007), among others, who hypothesized on the pro‐oxidant nature of heme iron from red meat and its ability to induce severe luminal and postprandial oxidative stress upon intake. Although some of their thoughts may be correct (i.e., pro‐oxidant nature of heme iron), the present study reveals that plant‐based UPFs were, in fact, more susceptible to suffer luminal oxidation during digestion than red meat. The lack of elements of antioxidant protection in the PBMA along with relatively high concentrations of PUFA and sugars/carbohydrates could have promoted their oxidation given the role of the latter in oxidizing proteins through secondary carbonylation (MDA addition) and Maillard reactions, respectively.

Unlike stomach in particular, distant compartments of the GIT, namely, colon, are not so well known for their ability to host oxidative reactions given the lack of oxygen and abundance of microorganisms. Yet, the present results indicate that the colon, in particular, is a key tissue in which both protein oxidation and fermentation occur. The lack of molecular oxygen does not seem to be a limiting factor for protein oxidation. Already reported by Davies (2005) or Estévez (2011), protein carbonylation and aggregations, as expressions of protein oxidation, may occur in the absence of molecular oxygen. This may be one major reason why the extent of lipid oxidation, dependent on molecular oxygen, was neither remarkable nor informative throughout the GIT as measured by TBARs. The colon may not only be witness and host of these biochemical reactions: It may suffer from the shift in microbiota composition, uptake of toxic compounds, and onset of oxidative stress and inflammation, which may be the basis of more serious pathological conditions in the long term. That is why the severity of oxidative stress at this location of the GIT is of relevance.

True digestibility of dietary proteins

When proteins reach the stomach, protein digestion begins, which involves the denaturation and scission of the molecules through the action of gastric proteases and peptidases. The enzymes promote the release of peptides and amino acids, which are absorbed and metabolized mainly in the intestine (Chen et al., 2022). However, the rate and extent of protein digestion are governed by the structural properties of food, including protein solubility, accessibility of digestive enzymes, protein source, and processing history of foods (Kaur et al., 2022). It has been documented that protein oxidation could impair gastric digestion owing to a lack of recognition of oxidized proteins by pepsin and other gastric enzymes (Delgado et al., 2021; He et al., 2018; Snelson & Coughlan, 2019; Van Hecke et al., 2018). This decreased digestion would be partially counteracted by a more intense digestion in subsequent stages (jejunum) because, otherwise, oxidized proteins would remain undigested in the lumen of the GIT and eventually reach the colon (He et al., 2018; Sánchez‐Terrón et al., 2023).

Keeping in mind the different nature of the products, it was not surprising that the digestibility of the proteins derived from PBMAs was lower than that of beef proteins (Figure 1a). In agreement with our results, there are several authors showing how proteins from plant‐based UPFs were less digestible than proteins from roasted beef in their in vitro assays (Chen et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2021). In vivo assays are also consistent such as that performed by Xie et al. (2022), who revealed that PBMAs significantly weakened the function of gastric acid secretion of mice compared with real meat. In agreement, our own results showed higher amounts of undigested protein from seitan in the gastric content from rats together with the lower abundance of several essential amino acids in the corresponding compartments (Figure 2a and 5, respectively). Likewise, Xie et al. (2022) reported low peptide release during digestion of PBMAs, and Chen et al. (2022) showed lower permeation of essential and nonessential amino acids from digested plant proteins in their CACO‐2 in vitro assays as compared to the meat protein digests. Further to the nutritional impact of the production of peptides and amino acids, certain digestive peptides in GIT may affect eating behavior exerting hormone‐like effects of influencing endocrine cell secretion, decreasing satiety sensations (Xie et al., 2023, 2024). Although our experimental animals showed nonsignificant differences in consumption rates (Figure S1), more analysis would have been needed to relate diminished plant‐protein digestibility with impairments in the endocrine gastric responses to the meals. Particularly, our group is currently involved in a human interventional study that evaluates postprandial satiety events after a single‐dose ingestion of either seitan or beef through physiological blood parameters, such as ghrelin, cholecystokinin (CCK), and leptin. The preliminary results suggest that the PBMA (i.e., seitan) promotes higher blood levels of ghrelin, as well as lower levels of both CCK and leptin in the blood of seitan consumers compared with that of beef consumers at 120 min of the ingestion, which suggests that the former provided lower sensations of satiety to volunteers (Arjona et al., 2023). According to the results from this study, meat proteins are digested in the stomach slower but in a more effective way than wheat gluten from seitan, which seem to pass through the stomach fast owing to the quick (peak at 15 min postprandial) and minor response to CKK upon intake (Arjona et al., 2023).

The reduced digestibility of S in the stomach seemed to be partially counteracted in the following compartment of the GIT. Hence, the levels of TDP degraded in the jejunum of S animals showed an increase compared to B and T groups, which would suggest that the lack of gastric protein digestion in the S group would be balanced by an intense protein digestion in the jejunum (Figure 2a). Likewise, authors, such as Chen et al. (2022), evaluated the degree of protein hydrolysis of a PBMAs compared to a real meat counterpart in an in vitro assay and showed a higher hydrolysis at the end of intestinal digestion compared to that at the end of gastric digestion (p < 0.05). However, the composition of the plant‐derived product used by the authors involved combinations of wheat and soy proteins, making difficult the precise comparison of the results with the present ones.

Overall, S and B showed higher digestibility in the upper stages of the GIT (stomach and jejunum), whereas T, which showed an impaired digestion in these first stages, was destined to undergo fermentative reactions at the last stages of the GIT (Figure 2b). In relation to nutritional aspects, these results show that further to the poorer composition of UPF vegan foods in terms of protein quality (amino acid composition) compared to meat, the behavior of the former in the GIT reveals an impaired digestion and that would compromise even more their effective delivery of essential nutrients to humans. Further to this, undigested oxidized proteins from T reach the colon, where they were fermented by the microbiota, increasing the oxidative stress in the compartments and adversely affecting gastrointestinal tissues. In addition to the poor intraluminal protein digestibility associated with protein carbonylation/aggregation, it is known that luminal oxidative stress involves immune and inflammatory responses (Estévez et al., 2021), which are the basis of many currently prevalent intestinal diseases, such as Crohn's disease or colorectal cancer (CRC) (Tian et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2016).

Intestinal oxidative stress, inflammation, and gut health

The present results show that the extent of oxidative stress and tissue damage was particularly intense in animals exposed to the PBMAs reasonably suggesting that luminal oxidative stress may be transferred to the intestinal epithelium. Interestingly, differences were found between dietary proteins and locations, as our results indicated that gluten wheat (mainly digested in jejunum) had a greater impact on the oxidative stress and inflammation in the small intestine, whereas commercial tofu (with higher fermentation rates in colon) appeared to promote tissue damage in the large intestine. Assuming the equivalence factor between the amounts of total PPCs and protein hydrazones suggested by Estévez et al. (2021), the elevated values of total PPCs showed in the colonic tissues from S and T rats (5.12 and 3.88 nmol carbonyls/mg protein, respectively) may closely correlate a PBMAs‐based diet with a hazardous gastrointestinal state directly related to the onset of precancerous lesions based on studies that evaluate the level of protein oxidation of different precancerous states (Mehrabi et al., 2015).

In fact, oxidative stress is recurrently associated with inflammation and allergies at the GIT. This connection was found to be straightforward in our study. Allergic reactions to food components at the GIT are normally triggered by an inadequate immune and inflammatory response in the intestine (Barone et al., 2022). This is the case of celiac disease, triggered by the occurrence and reactivity of wheat protein (i.e., gluten). The reactivity of digested peptides from gluten (namely g‐peptides) activates the intestinal immune system, which is mainly located at the PPs (Mulder & Mulder‐Bos, 2006). The higher PPs diameters in the small intestine from our S group of animals might reflect the inflammatory response of Wistar rats to wheat gluten (Figure 3). In‐line with our results, the presence of a key peptide fragment related to celiac disease was previously identified by other authors after an in vitro digestion of two commercial PBMAs formulated with wheat protein (Yang et al., 2023). Yet, other authors did not find any intestinal inflammatory response from a PBMA elaborated from wheat and soy in a CACO‐2 cell model employed to observe the expression of several cytokines (Chen et al., 2022). Surprisingly, Cruz‐Chamorro et al. (2020) and González‐Montoya et al. (2018) reported anti‐inflammatory properties of peptides from wheat gluten protein hydrolysates and soybean proteins after simulated digestions. However, the differences among the meals and models used (in vitro vs. in vivo) do not enable a straightforward comparison of the results. It is important to note that the dysfunction of PPs may be related additionally to other autoimmune diseases, such as Type I diabetes or Crohn disease (Mulder & Mulder‐Bos, 2006). In the study already reported by Arjona et al. (2023), we found that all 25 healthy human volunteers from both genders suffered a postprandial increase of IL‐6, a marker of food allergy and inflammation, after a single‐dose intake of commercial seitan. Although the reactivity of wheat gluten is known to be dependent on the genetic background of the consumer, it seems obvious that humans and other mammals (i.e., Wistar rats) display certain inflammatory reaction to wheat proteins. It is important to emphasize that the digestion pattern herewith described for wheat gluten (silent passage through the stomach and core degradation in the jejunum) may account for the ability of gluten to induce such reactivity at this very specific location.

To the best of our knowledge, we report for the first time the occurrence of chemical species of great toxicological concern in the colonic contents of rats fed plant‐based UPF, such as p‐cresol or MDA. In the colonic content of rats fed with red meat, the abundance of both toxic compounds was found to appear remarkably reduced compared to the quantities of those in the colonic lumen of PBMAs groups. p‐Cresol is produced by intestinal microbes mainly from undigested proteins, and scientific literature supports a dose‐dependent genotoxic effect of this compound on colonocytes, promoting DNA damage (Andriamihaja et al., 2015; Nowak & Libudzisz, 2006; Wong et al., 2016). p‐Cresol contributes to colon carcinogenesis (Al Hinai et al., 2019), and several authors related its biosynthesis with strict anaerobic bacterial species, such as Escherichia coli (Nowak & Libudzisz, 2006). The higher abundance of species from genus Escherichia–Shigella in the microbiota of T animals, along with the higher concentration of oxidized and undigested proteins at the colon of these animals, strengthens the causal connection among the poor digestibility of tofu proteins, the dysbiosis at the colon, and the generation of fermentation‐derived toxic compounds at the same GIT location.

Given the duality between oxidative stress and CRC (Acevedo‐León et al., 2022; Carini et al., 2017; Perše, 2013), several authors have extensively studied the presence of MDA mainly in serological samples as a direct product of lipid peroxidation and marker of harmful oxidation status (Janion et al., 2022; Nielsen et al., 1997; Zińczuk et al., 2019). However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that a study related increased amounts of MDA in the colonic environment of Wistar rats fed with PBMAs, highlighting that these plant‐based UPFs had the ability to increase the luminal oxidative stress in a more significant extent than red meat. This different behavior led to the occurrence of undesirable and harmful metabolites in the lumen of the GIT of rats fed with vegan UPFs and the occurrence of essential amino acids and other nutrient‐relevant metabolites in the lumen of rats fed the red meat‐based diet.

Colonic microbiota and potential health implications

To gain further insights into the changes on the colonic environment of the rats driven by the experimental diets, a comprehensive study of the microbiota of the rats was performed. Gut microbiota plays a central role in the metabolic and physiologic homeostasis of the host (Huttenhower et al., 2012). It is documented that the intestinal human gut microbiota (from small and large intestine) plays a role in host dietary protein metabolism (Portune et al., 2016). Amino acids contribute to the synthesis of microbial protein, but they can also be fermented as an energy source (Neis et al., 2015). The different metabolomic signature in the colon of the rats from different experimental groups (Figure 6) revealed higher amounts of essential amino acids in the colon of B rats than in those from rats fed the feed produced with PBMAs. Although bacteria may be able to obtain essential amino acids from oxidized proteins from PBMAs, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the concentration in the colon would not be the same as that obtained from an efficient digestion of non‐oxidized proteins made by the host in previous compartments. Alternative fermentation products appeared to be much more abundant and were, in fact, discriminating metabolites in the colonic digests of T‐animals, as aforementioned. It is also reasonable to hypothesize that microbial machinery may be able to obtain more essential amino acids from undigested non‐oxidized proteins of high biological value (cooked beef) even if beef proteins arriving to this distant location were lower than in the case of tofu (severely oxidized). A low concentration of essential amino acids may lead to dysregulation of the metabolic processes of the microbiota and, hence, to environmental changes at this location. Previous studies have documented changes in the microbiota as affected by the amount, nature, and extent of oxidation of dietary proteins (Peled & Livney, 2021; Rodríguez‐Romero et al., 2022; Sánchez‐Terrón et al., 2023). However, the impact of the protein origin on gut microbiota remains unclear, and the published literature shows contradictory results.

Plant‐based diets, proposed as diets rich in dietary fiber, are reported to enhance the increase of Firmicutes phylum of bacteria (Walker et al., 2011), whereas it is documented that animal‐based diet distinctively promotes the abundance of Bacteroidota and reduces Firmicutes phyla (Huang et al., 2016; Peled & Livney, 2021). In the last few years, several authors have investigated gut microbial composition from humans with different dietary preferences, including vegetarian/vegan individuals (Losno et al., 2021; Sakkas et al., 2018; Wong et al., 2018). Increased prevalence of microbes associated with a healthy gut, such as several lactic acid bacteria from both Bacteroidota and Firmicutes phylum, and decreased presence of potential pathogens such as species from Enterobacteriaceae family are reported in the literature as contributions of a plant‐based diet (Huang et al., 2016; Losno et al., 2021). Considering the above references and the benefits ascribed to PBMAs as healthy alternatives to ASF, the results from this study are apparently inconsistent with most of the existing literature. The microbiota from S rats did not show the expected composition at the phylum level as expected from previous research. The occurrence of bacteria from Firmicutes phylum was lower in the microbiota from S rats compared to the microbiotas from B and T counterparts. Bacteria from Bacteroidota phylum significantly increased in the microbiota of S rats as compared to that from T rats. Gluten metabolism, as part of the pathophysiology of celiac disease, and gut microbiota appear to be intimately related. The interaction between the gut microbiota and the mucosal wall seemed to be driven by the same receptors, which directly relates intestinal microbes with immunological response to gluten and intestinal tissue damage (Bascuñán et al., 2020; Collado et al., 2007; Marasco et al., 2016). Several authors proposed that the main bacteria involved in gluten metabolism belong to both Firmicutes and Bacteroidota phylum (Clostridium spp. and Bacteroides spp., respectively) (Bascuñán et al., 2020). The higher abundance of Enterobacter spp. (p < 0.01) or Erysipelotrichaceae UCG‐003 spp. (p < 0.01) revealed by the metagenomic analysis in colonic samples from S group compared to B, and T is consistent with findings from other authors who revealed Enterobacteriaceae family of microorganisms as the dominant bacterial group in microbiota of celiac patients (Collado et al., 2007). Meanwhile, Erysipelotrichaceae family has been related with IgA response from PPs, intimately associated with celiac disorder (Kau et al., 2015; Million et al., 2018). These data are interesting for understanding how microbiota is modified in certain conditions and dietary patterns. Yet, the connection between the microbiota and the onset of celiac disease may not be straightforward as other factors such as the genetic background could be more influential (Mulder & Mulder‐Bos, 2006).

On the other hand, large differences were also found at the phylum level between the microbiota of Wistar rats fed with soy‐based diet (T) compared to that of rats fed the diet in which beef was incorporated (B). Tofu is proposed as a good source of proteins and a probiotic product due to it fermentative processing (Arroyo et al., 2005), increasing gut lactic acid‐producing bacteria and decreasing potential pathogens such as some from Enterobacteriaceae family (Arroyo et al., 2005). Huang et al. (2016) claimed that fermented soy foods have been shown to possess health‐promoting effects, such as anti‐obesity or anti‐inflammation, among others. However, in our study, T diet seemed to promote colonic damage in the animals that consumed this vegan UPF for 10 weeks, assessed through the elevated colonic oxidative markers and the greatest diameter of the colonic PPs compared to B. In fact, the shift in the bacterial population from Bacteroidota and Firmicutes phyla to Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria phyla cannot be regarded as a positive gut health outcome as the latter bacteria have been proposed to be related with IBD (Peterson et al., 2008).The α‐diversity index calculated at the genus level of the microbiota of T animals indicates that tofu diet promoted fewer dominance of species than beef, as well as higher evenness and consequently higher diversity of genus of bacteria. These results are in line with others reporting the effect of vegan diets on microbiota (Losasso et al., 2018; Losno et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2016). The relative abundance of unclassified Muribaculaceae spp. revealed that this was the dominant group in the microbiota of B rats, whereas this genus decreased in the microbiome of T group (p < 0.01). Changes in the relative abundance of this and other microorganisms, such as the increased amount of Escherichia–Shigella spp. in T group (p < 0.05), would explain the results about α‐diversity. In the present study, bacteria from Enterobacteriaceae family increased when T‐diet was consumed, in contrast to the related literature. Hence, it is reasonable to state that increasing diversity does not always mean improved gut health, as several authors associated increased Escherichia–Shigella spp. with some intestinal pathologies (Mu et al., 2016). The beneficial claims made on certain fermented soy products may still be applicable considering that some of those soy‐based foods may differ in terms of composition and properties with the commercial soy‐based UPF used in the present experiment. In fact, the apparent contradictory results from the present study with some of the cited literature may not be such for the following reasons. The studies systematically studying the specific effect of the sustained intake of vegan UPFs as main dietary protein sources (i.e., when used to replaces ASF) are really scarce. Instead, the health‐promoting effects observed for vegetarian/vegan diets may not necessarily be derived from these UPFs but actually from other fresh, unprocessed, or minimally processed fruits and vegetables with proven health benefits. Finally, as aforementioned, the industrially produced tofu and seitan used in the present study were obtained from a Western mainstream market chain and may be considerably different to tofu and seitan produced and regularly consumed in Asian countries where these plant‐based foods are, additionally, consumed in combination with ASF. Yet, the revealing scientific outcomes from this study and from some others to come, such as that anticipated at the 69th International Congress of Meat Science and Technology (Arjona et al., 2023), support the already reported concerns from the WHO on the health impact of vegan UPFs and should warn consumers on the sustained intake of these plant‐based analogs of ASF (WHO, 2021).

CONCLUSIONS

Ultraprocessing seems to seriously affect the oxidative stability of plant proteins, which seems to be related to their impaired digestion, fate in the GIT, and microbiota composition. Oxidized protein from commercial tofu was fermented by the colonic microbiota causing dysbiosis, oxidative stress, and the formation of undesirable harmful metabolites, namely, p‐cresol or MDA, directly related to serious intestinal pathologies. Wheat proteins from commercial seitan appeared to pass unnoticed through the stomach to subsequently suffer an intense degradation in the jejunum where inflammatory and oxidative stress responses were found.

Replacing proteins from red meat such as beef with proteins from commercial plant‐based UPFs may not be recommendable because contrary to their alleged beneficial effects, and they seem to lead to deleterious health outcomes. It is plausible that ultraprocessing protein‐rich ASF (meat/dairy products) would also lead to similar physiological responses, whereas this is yet to be studied.

Some of the nutritional concerns discussed above may only be applicable in Western countries where plant proteins are industrially transformed to produce meat and dairy analogs and consumed to replace the genuine ASF. In opposition, tofu and seitan have been consumed for ages in Asian countries as unique soy and wheat foods along with ASF.
