The relationship between pregnant women’s perception of traumatic birth and their vaginal birth self-efficacy: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ebru Inan Kirmizigul, Cagla Uygun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackroundWomen’s perceptions of traumatic childbirth are associated with their confidence in coping with vaginal birth. This study aimed to examine the relationship between pregnant women’s traumatic childbirth perception and their vaginal birth self-efficacy.MethodsThis cross-sectional correlational study was conducted with 152 nulliparous women attending the antenatal clinic of a private hospital in Turkey. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a Sociodemographic and Obstetric Information Form, the Traumatic Childbirth Perception Scale (TCPS), and the Vaginal Birth Self-Efficacy Scale (VBSES). Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29.0. Independent samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, Pearson correlation analysis, and hierarchical multiple linear regression analysis were performed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 28.20 ± 3.76 years, and the mean gestational week was 21.78 ± 7.86. The mean TCPS score was 63.22 ± 28.67, and the mean VBSES score was 49.34 ± 21.38. Traumatic childbirth perception was negatively correlated with vaginal birth self-efficacy (r = − 0.318, p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, traumatic childbirth perception remained independently associated with lower vaginal birth self-efficacy after controlling for sociodemographic and obstetric variables.ConclusionsPregnant women demonstrated moderate levels of traumatic childbirth perception. Higher traumatic childbirth perception was associated with lower vaginal birth self-efficacy. These findings underscore the importance of identifying and addressing traumatic perceptions during pregnancy in order to support women’s confidence in vaginal birth.

Background

Childbirth, although a normal and physiological process, is a major life event that profoundly affects women both physically and psychosocially. As a process that plays a fundamental role in ensuring generational continuity, childbirth is strongly influenced by women’s perceptions, expectations, fears, and past experiences [1]. While a moderate level of childbirth fear may help women prepare for labour and engage more actively in the birthing process, excessive fear can lead to negative birth perceptions and adversely affect birth performance [2]. In a study by Yildirim and Bilgin, 79.5% of pregnant women reported that they wanted to have a vaginal birth, yet 62.8% stated that they were afraid of it [3]. Negative emotional experiences related to childbirth may lead women to perceive birth as traumatic, which in turn can negatively influence their self-efficacy regarding childbirth [2].

Within the framework of Social Cognitive Theory, Bandura defines self-efficacy as an individual’s belief in their ability to initiate and sustain an action until the desired outcome is achieved [4]. Childbirth self-efficacy refers to a woman’s confidence in her own capacity and skills to cope with the process of labour and birth [5]. According to Bandura, self-efficacy is shaped by four main sources: successful past experiences (mastery experiences), vicarious learning through observing and receiving feedback from supportive others, verbal persuasion, and one’s physiological and emotional state [4]. These factors influence a woman’s awareness of her own body and capabilities, thereby increasing or decreasing her belief in the possibility of a successful vaginal birth [6]. Previous studies have shown that women with higher levels of childbirth self-efficacy cope more effectively with labour pain, require less analgesic medication, and experience fewer birth complications [7]. Conversely, increased anxiety and fear may lead to negative birth expectations and perceptions, resulting in the childbirth experience being perceived as traumatic and associated with lower vaginal birth self-efficacy [8, 9].

Within this theoretical framework, it is important to distinguish between related but conceptually distinct psychological constructs. Fear of childbirth represents an emotional response characterized by heightened emotional arousal, whereas perception of traumatic birth reflects a cognitive appraisal of childbirth as a potentially threatening or overwhelming future event [4, 10]. In line with Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory, emotional arousal such as fear may influence individuals’ cognitive appraisals and interpretations of forthcoming experiences, thereby shaping their perceived capability to cope with the situation [4, 11]. In this context, fear of childbirth may function as an emotional antecedent that contributes to the cognitive evaluation of childbirth as traumatic (“birth will be traumatic”), which in turn may be associated with lower vaginal birth self-efficacy [1]. Accordingly, this pathway is presented as a theoretical framework to guide the interpretation of the findings, rather than as a causal model.

The relationship between pregnant women’s perceptions of traumatic birth and their vaginal birth self-efficacy is a critical issue, as these psychological factors significantly influence birth preferences, experiences, and outcomes [12]. Increased perception of birth as traumatic has been associated with reduced confidence in one’s ability to give birth vaginally, heightened fear of complications, and a greater likelihood of preferring caesarean Sects [8, 13–15]. Current guidance from the World Health Organization emphasizes that caesarean section is a life-saving intervention when medically indicated; however, population-level caesarean section rates above approximately 10–20% are not associated with additional reductions in maternal or neonatal mortality. Rather than endorsing a universal target rate, the WHO recommends focusing on ensuring access to caesarean section for women who need it, while avoiding unnecessary surgical interventions that may pose health risks and economic burdens [16]. Despite this evidence-based perspective, caesarean section rates in Türkiye have continued to rise, reaching 57.5% [17]. Caesarean birth has been associated with increased maternal morbidity, delayed postpartum recovery, impaired breastfeeding initiation, and elevated obstetric risks in subsequent pregnancies, potentially leading to less favorable reproductive outcomes [18]. If negative or traumatic perceptions of childbirth are not addressed, women’s self-efficacy for future vaginal births may be adversely affected, perpetuating a cycle of fear, intervention, and negative birth experiences.

In Türkiye, a “Normal Birth Action Plan” has been developed and implemented to reduce pregnant women’s fear of childbirth and to promote vaginal birth. The primary goal of this national strategy is to encourage normal birth and to decrease caesarean section rates [19]. In this context, the present study aims to explore the relationship between nulliparous women’s perceptions of traumatic birth and their self-efficacy for vaginal birth. Understanding this relationship is expected to contribute to developing effective strategies to better support women during labour and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the relationship between pregnant women’s perceptions of traumatic birth and their vaginal birth self-efficacy.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a cross-sectional and correlational research. It was conducted between November 2024 and May 2025 in the antenatal outpatient clinic of a private hospital in Ankara, Türkiye.

Participant and sampling

During the planning phase of the study, an a priori sample size calculation was not performed because no previous studies with a similar design had examined the relationship between traumatic birth perception and vaginal birth self-efficacy using the same measurement instruments, which limited reliable effect size estimation. Therefore, consecutive sampling was adopted. All eligible nulliparous pregnant women who attended the antenatal clinic during the study period were invited to participate.

Eligible participants were recruited consecutively during routine antenatal follow-up visits at a private foundation hospital. A total of 173 women who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled. Of these, 21 were excluded from the final analysis due to incomplete questionnaires (n = 3), declining participation because of lack of spousal permission (n = 7), or withdrawal due to time constraints related to non-stress test (NST) follow-up (n = 11). The final analytical sample consisted of 152 women. The participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Only complete questionnaires were included in the analyses, and no data imputation methods were applied.

Outcome measures

Data were collected using the Descriptive Information Form, the Perception of Traumatic Childbirth Scale, and the Vaginal Birth Self-Efficacy Scale.

Descriptive information form

This form included questions regarding participants’ sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, education level, income) and obstetric characteristics (e.g., whether the pregnancy was planned, participation in childbirth preparation classes, and fear of childbirth).

Traumatic Childbirth Perception Scale (TCPS)

The scale was developed by Yalnız et al. [20]. It is a single-dimensional, 11-point Likert-type instrument in which each item is scored from 0 (none) to 10 (very much). The total score ranges from 0 to 130, with higher scores indicating a greater perception of childbirth as traumatic. Traumatic childbirth perception levels are classified according to total score ranges as follows: 0–26: Very low, 27–52: Low, 53–78: Moderate, 79–104: High and 105–130: Very high. The original Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.89, while in the present study it was 0.92, indicating high internal consistency.

Vaginal Birth Self-Efficacy Scale (VBSES)

The scale was developed by Chu et al. [21], and its Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Karadeniz and Kavlak [22]. It consists of nine single-dimension items, each rated on an 11-point numerical scale (0–10). The total score ranges from 0 to 90, with higher scores indicating higher levels of self-efficacy toward vaginal birth. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.87 in the original study and 0.91 in the current study.

Data collection

Data were collected using face-to-face interviews. Participants were provided with information regarding the study objectives and procedures, and informed consent was obtained prior to completing the questionnaires. The data collection process took approximately 5–10 min per participant.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values, histograms, and Q–Q plots. Skewness and kurtosis values were within the acceptable range of − 1.5 to + 1.5, indicating approximate normal distribution of the main study variables [23]. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations. Independent samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for group comparisons. When ANOVA results were statistically significant, post hoc comparisons were performed using the Scheffé test if variances were homogeneous and the Tamhane’s T2 test if variances were not homogeneous. Cronbach’s alfa wasused to show the internal consistency coefficient of the scales.

Associations between scale scores were examined using Pearson’s correlation analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses. To examine whether traumatic birth perception independently predicted Vaginal Birth Self-Efficacy Scale (VBSES) scores, a two-step hierarchical multiple linear regression analysis was conducted. In Model 1, sociodemographic and obstetric variables (maternal age, partner’s age, duration of marriage, and gestational week) were entered as control variables. In Model 2, Traumatic Childbirth Perception Scale (TCPS) scores were entered to evaluate their incremental contribution to the explained variance in VBSES scores. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and no evidence of multicollinearity was observed (VIF range: 1.01–2.42).

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Lokman Hekim University Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2024/140, 29 May 2024). All participants provided informed consent prior to data collection. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and ethical guidelines for research publication.

The average duration of marriage was 33.81 ± 18.17 months (range: 5–96), and the mean gestational week was 21.78 ± 7.86 (range: 14–39). A total of 60.5% of the women were university graduates, 65.1% were employed during pregnancy, and 89.5% lived in a nuclear family structure. Similarly, 62.5% of their spouses were university graduates, and 96.7% were employed. Nearly half of the participants (47.4%) reported that their income was equal to their expenses. Regarding health status, 90.8% of the women reported no chronic disease, while 17.1% were smokers. Examination of obstetric characteristics revealed that 82.2% of the pregnancies were planned. 45.4% of the participants had attended childbirth preparation classes, whereas 54.6% had not. Almost half (47.4%) stated that they experienced fear of childbirth, 24.3% reported no fear, and 28.3% were undecided. Additionally, 96.7% of the participants reported receiving support from their spouse, and 89.5% reported support from family or social networks.

The mean total score of the TCPS was 63.22 ± 28.67 (range: 0–130), while the mean total score of the VBSES was 49.34 ± 21.38 (range: 0–90). The distribution of TCPS and VBSES scores according to participants’ sociodemographic characteristics is presented in Table 2. No statistically significant differences were found in TCPS and VBSES scores according to education level, employment status during pregnancy, family type, spouse’s education level, spouse’s employment status, presence of chronic disease, or smoking status (p > 0.05). However, TCPS scores differed significantly across income status groups (F = 3.641, p = 0.029). Participants whose income was lower than their expenses had significantly higher TCPS scores (77.88 ± 31.06) compared to those whose income was equal to (65.06 ± 25.63) or higher than (57.48 ± 30.14) their expenses (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

The distribution of TCPS and VBSES scores according to obstetric characteristics is presented in Table 3. Participants who did not attend childbirth preparation classes had significantly higher TCPS scores (68.51 ± 28.57) compared with those who attended such classes (56.86 ± 27.68) (p = 0.012). Similarly, women who reported fear of childbirth had significantly higher TCPS scores (77.00 ± 24.42) than those without such fear (F = 28.755, p < 0.001). VBSES scores were significantly higher among women who attended childbirth preparation classes (54.04 ± 20.34) than those who did not (45.43 ± 21.55) (p = 0.013). In addition, women who experienced fear of childbirth had significantly lower VBSES scores (46.75 ± 19.70) than women who reported no fear (59.62 ± 22.45) or were undecided (44.84 ± 20.75) (F = 6.154, p = 0.003). No statistically significant differences were found in TCPS and VBSES scores according to whether the pregnancy was planned, or the level of spousal and family support (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

The main findings of our study indicate that pregnant women exhibited a moderate level of traumatic birth perception. This result aligns with existing literature, which reports that women generally possess moderate levels of traumatic birth perception [3, 24, 25]. For instance, in the study by Beydağ and Dal, women’s traumatic birth perception scores were 63.7, indicating a moderate level [25]. Traumatic birth perception is influenced by sociodemographic and economic factors [26]. Literature suggests that single women, those employed in income-generating jobs, and women with lower income levels tend to report higher traumatic birth perception [25]. Consistent with these findings, our study found that traumatic birth perception was particularly higher among pregnant women in the low-income group. Although no statistically significant differences were observed according to educational level, women with higher education exhibited somewhat higher traumatic birth perception. This variation may be attributable to the study being conducted in a private hospital antenatal clinic, where participants’ socioeconomic characteristics were relatively homogeneous.

Although birth self-efficacy is an important determinant of a positive birth experience, it is often an overlooked aspect of maternity care [27]. The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes that effective antenatal care should aim to enhance women’s sense of self-efficacy and autonomy [28]. In our study, the VBSES scores of pregnant women were only slightly above the midpoint, despite the maximum possible score being 90. In contrast, a study conducted with pregnant women in Australia reported high perceived birth self-efficacy [27]. Similarly, Düzbayır et al. found that vaginal birth self-efficacy levels were comparable to our findings and that self-efficacy did not significantly vary according to education level, employment status, family type, place of residence, parity, or history of abortion [6]. In our study, women with lower education (high school or below) and those not employed had slightly higher VBSES scores, although these differences were not statistically significant. Additionally, women with lower income levels had lower self-efficacy scores, but the difference was not statistically significant. This may be explained by the fact that the majority of our sample had university-level education or higher and a certain income level. Socioeconomic and cultural factors may have enhanced women’s self-awareness and influenced their perceived ability to cope with vaginal birth.

Fear of childbirth can lead to perceiving birth as threatening and traumatic. This phenomenon can be explained through the emotional arousal mechanism described in Bandura’s self-efficacy theory [4]. Threatening childbirth-related situations may result in high levels of negative emotional responses, substantially reducing self-efficacy [11, 29]. Fear of childbirth arises from the discrepancy between what individuals expect of themselves and what they believe they can achieve, potentially triggering low self-confidence [11, 30]. High childbirth fear, stemming from uncertainties and indirect information, contributes to the development of a traumatic birth perception [31]. Moreover, Bandura’s theory highlights that past experiences play a critical role in the development of self-efficacy [4]. The nulliparous status of women in our study indicates that, having not previously experienced birth, they lack the most powerful sources of information that could influence their birth perceptions. This suggests that other factors may also play a role in the relationship between traumatic birth perception and self-efficacy. This mechanism may explain why women have difficulty developing effective coping strategies for vaginal birth self-efficacy.

Preference for cesarean birth is associated with higher fear of vaginal birth and lower self-efficacy [9, 32]. In Gemeda-Guteda’s study, it was reported that primiparous women experiencing childbirth fear had negatively affected birth self-efficacy [27]. Similarly, a study conducted in Iran with primiparous and multiparous women found that primiparous women experienced higher levels of childbirth fear, which significantly reduced their birth self-efficacy [15]. In addition to childbirth fear, anticipated interventions during birth (e.g., episiotomy, fundal pressure) can influence traumatic birth perceptions [5, 31]. Ünal and colleagues demonstrated that women with high traumatic birth perceptions were more likely to prefer cesarean birth [33]. In our study, women with childbirth fear had higher traumatic birth perception scores and lower vaginal birth self-efficacy scores. This finding aligns with the literature and highlights that the perception of birth as traumatic is reinforced by individual and environmental factors.

Knowledge acquired through childbirth preparation education plays a critical role in enhancing women’s confidence and coping skills [34]. Previous studies have shown that prenatal education can reduce childbirth fear and traumatic perceptions while increasing birth self-efficacy [9, 27, 35, 36]. However, it is important to note that the content, scope, and accessibility of prenatal education programs may vary considerably. In the present study, participation in prenatal education programs and the specific content of such education were not directly assessed. Therefore, references to prenatal education in this context should be interpreted as implications based on existing literature rather than as findings derived from the current data. These findings suggest that, particularly among nulliparous women, traumatic birth perceptions may be shaped not only by formal education level but also by exposure to indirect, informal, or uncontrolled sources of information, as well as unmet informational needs related to childbirth. This highlights the potential value of structured, evidence-based prenatal education programs that specifically address childbirth-related fears and expectations.

Women with high levels of perceived traumatic birth are less likely to believe they can have a vaginal birth and are more likely to prefer a cesarean delivery [9, 14, 27, 32]. In our study, a negative correlation was observed between vaginal birth self-efficacy and perceived traumatic birth. Additionally, self-efficacy scores were negatively correlated with maternal age, partner’s age, and duration of marriage. In Turkey, 41.9% of births are first births, with the mean maternal age being 29.3 years, and the total fertility rate among university-educated women has decreased to 1.2 [37]. These data indicate that women are postponing childbearing to later ages. Importantly, the association between traumatic birth perception and vaginal birth self-efficacy remained significant after controlling for sociodemographic and obstetric variables in multivariable analysis, indicating that this relationship is not solely explained by age, marital characteristics, or gestational factors. The hierarchical structure of the regression model further demonstrated that traumatic birth perception contributed substantially to the explained variance in vaginal birth self-efficacy beyond basic demographic variables. However, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. It cannot be determined whether higher traumatic birth perception leads to lower vaginal birth self-efficacy or whether lower self-efficacy contributes to perceiving birth as more traumatic. Within this context, identifying traumatic birth perceptions and providing individualized maternity care approaches to enhance vaginal birth self-efficacy may support psychological well-being in subsequent pregnancies [38]. Furthermore, focusing on the development of birth self-efficacy within undergraduate programs and incorporating modules aimed at fostering self-efficacy skills in the education of healthcare professionals such as midwives and nurses may contribute to improved vaginal birth self-efficacy.

Conclusion

This study indicates that nulliparous pregnant women exhibit moderate levels of traumatic childbirth perception and slightly above-average vaginal birth self-efficacy. Traumatic childbirth perception was negatively associated with vaginal birth self-efficacy, and lower self-efficacy was also related to maternal age, partner’s age, and duration of marriage. The findings suggest that traumatic childbirth perception represents an important psychological factor associated with women’s confidence in coping with vaginal birth. Within this context, addressing traumatic perceptions and supporting the development of vaginal birth self-efficacy during pregnancy may be a valuable component of antenatal care. Integrating self-efficacy–supportive approaches into maternity care practices and educational programs for healthcare professionals may help foster women’s confidence in vaginal birth, while acknowledging that these implications should be interpreted in light of the study’s cross-sectional design.

Limitations of research

The findings of this study may yield more robust and generalizable results in larger samples and among populations with diverse socioeconomic backgrounds. Conducting the research with a cohort of women attending a private hospital, characterized by relatively high educational and income levels, limits the direct generalizability of the results to the broader population. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes establishing causal relationships. Therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as directional or causal effects. Fear of childbirth was assessed using a single-item question rather than a validated multi-item scale, which may have limited the ability to capture the multidimensional nature and intensity of this construct. The use of self-reported measures may also introduce the potential for social desirability and response biases. Additionally, some subgroup analyses were based on small sample sizes (e.g., women reporting no partner support), which may limit the statistical stability of these comparisons. Therefore, findings related to these small subgroups should be interpreted with caution. Finally, including only nulliparous women restricts the applicability of the findings to multiparous women.
