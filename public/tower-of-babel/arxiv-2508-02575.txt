Density functional theory of renormalization group in nuclear matter
Yong-rui Chen, Wei-jie Fu, Yang-yang Tan

Abstract
The density functional renormalization group (density-fRG) is proposed to investigate the density fluctuations within the functional renormalization group approach, which allows us to quantify the medium effect and study physics of high densities. This method is applied to the nucleon-meson effective field theory, also known as the Walecka model, to study the properties of nuclear matter at high baryon densities. It is found that both the attractive and repulsive nucleon meson interactions are screened by the high density medium, which results in a stiffer equation of state (EoS) of nuclear matter in the regime of ρ 0 ≲ ρ ≲ 2.5 ​ ρ 0 \rho_{0}\lesssim\rho\lesssim 2.5\rho_{0} , then a softer EoS when ρ ≳ 2.5 ​ ρ 0 \rho\gtrsim 2.5\rho_{0} . Here ρ 0 \rho_{0} denotes the saturation baryon density of symmetric nuclear matter. Furthermore, a new phenomenon called the locking of Fermi surface is found. In the locking of Fermi surface the effective energy of quasi-nucleon is always close to the Fermi surface, which are both running with the renormalization group scale.

In the fRG approach, quantum, thermal and density fluctuations are taken into account successively with the evolution of the renormalization group (RG) scale. The quantum fluctuations are also referred to as the fluctuations in vacuum. In order to simplify the calculations at high baryon densities and in the spirit of density functional theory (DFT), in this work we try to separate the density fluctuations from vacuum fluctuations, and only compute the density fluctuations with the RG evolution. Hereafter we call this method the density functional theory of renormalization group (DFT-RG) or the density functional renormalization group (density-fRG). As a first step, in this paper we would like to apply the density-fRG to the nucleon-meson effective field theory which is also known as the Walecka model
Walecka 1974

II
Nucleon meson effective field theory within the fRG

We begin with the effective action of the nucleon-meson effective field theory (EFT) within the functional renormalization group approach, which reads

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]=

\displaystyle\int_{x}\bigg\{\bar{\psi}_{N}\Big[\gamma_{\mu}\partial_{\mu}+m_{N}-\gamma_{0}\hat{\mu}_{N}-h_{\sigma,k}\sigma T^{0}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{i}h_{\omega,k}\gamma_{\mu}\omega_{\mu}T^{0}+\mathrm{i}h_{\rho,k}\gamma_{\mu}\bm{\rho}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{T}\Big]\psi_{N}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\sigma\partial_{\mu}\sigma+\frac{1}{2}m_{\sigma}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{3}}{3!}\sigma^{3}+\frac{\lambda_{4}}{4!}\sigma^{4}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\omega_{\mu\nu}\omega_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}m_{\omega}^{2}\omega_{\mu}\omega_{\mu}+\frac{1}{4}\bm{\rho}_{\mu\nu}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}m_{\rho}^{2}\bm{\rho}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{\rho}_{\mu}+V_{k}(\sigma,\omega,\rho)\bigg\}\,,

(1)

which is also known as the Walecka model
Walecka 1974
. The subscript
k
denotes the infrared (IR) cutoff, that is, the renormalization group (RG) scale in the fRG. The field
\Phi=(\psi_{N},\bar{\psi}_{N},\sigma,\omega,\rho)
includes the nucleon field, i.e., the isospin doublet of proton and neutron
\psi_{N}=(\psi_{p},\psi_{n})^{\intercal}
, the isoscalar-scalar
\sigma
meson field, the isoscalar-vector
\omega
meson field, and the isovector-vector
\rho
meson field. The field strength tensors of vector mesons read

\displaystyle\omega_{\mu\nu}=

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\omega_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\omega_{\mu}\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}_{\mu\nu}=

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\bm{\rho}_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\bm{\rho}_{\mu}\,.

(3)

In Eq.
1
the Euclidean spacetime has been used with
\int_{x}=\int_{0}^{1/T}\mathrm{d}x_{0}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x
, where
T
is the temperature. The
m_{N}
denotes the nucleon mass in the vacuum and
\hat{\mu}_{N}=\mathrm{diag}(\mu_{p},\mu_{n})
the diagonal matrix of nucleon chemical potentials. The
m_{\sigma}
,
m_{\omega}
and
m_{\rho}
stand for the meson masses in the vacuum for the respective mesons. Moreover, in the vacuum there are self-interactions of cubic and quartic terms for the
\sigma
field with the coupling strength
\lambda_{3}
and
\lambda_{4}
, respectively. Note that all the observables in the vacuum, i.e.,
m_{N}
,
m_{\sigma}
,
m_{\omega}
,
m_{\rho}
,
\lambda_{3}
and
\lambda_{4}
, are
k
-independent.

The nucleons in
1
interact with mesons through the Yukawa couplings with the strength
h_{\sigma,k}
,
h_{\omega,k}
and
h_{\rho,k}
, that are RG scale
k
-dependent. The mesons are in the adjoint representation of the
\mathrm{U}(N=2)
group in the isospin space with

\displaystyle T^{0}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}_{2\times 2}\,,

(4)

and

\displaystyle T^{i}=\frac{\sigma^{i}}{2}\quad(i=1,\,2,\,3)\,,

(5)

where
\sigma^{i}
are the Pauli matrices. It is readily verified that the temporal component of vector fields should be purely imaginary resulting from the requirement of the hermitian property of the effective action.

The last term in
1

V_{k}
is the effective potential arising from density fluctuations, and thus it is vanishing in the vacuum, i.e.,

\displaystyle V_{k\to\infty}=0\,,

(6)

In the density-fRG, quantum fluctuations in the vacuum are separated from the density fluctuations, and only the density fluctuations are taken into account through the evolution of the RG scale
k
. The vacuum case corresponds to
k\to\infty
, and in the other infrared limit
k\to 0
the full density effects are encoded.

III
Flow of the effective potential

In the functional renormalization group approach, the RG scale evolution of the effective action in Eq.
1
is governed by the Wetterich equation
Wetterich 1993
, viz.,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\big(\partial_{t}R_{\bar{\psi}\psi,k}\big)G_{\psi\bar{\psi}}\Big]+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\big(\partial_{t}R_{\phi\phi,k}\big)G_{\phi\phi}\Big]\,,

(7)

where the terms on the right side denote the nucleon and meson loop, respectively. Here the notation
t=\ln(k/\Lambda)
is used, which is usually named as the RG time. The
\Lambda
is a reference scale, for instance the initial scale of the flow equation in the ultraviolet (UV). The flow equation in Eq.
7
is also diagrammatically shown in Fig.
1
. Note that here
\phi=(\sigma,\omega,\rho)
includes all the mesons. The
G
and
R
stand for the respective propagators and regulators. In the fRG the regulator is used to suppress the fluctuations of momenta
p\lesssim k
. As a consequence, density fluctuations of different scales are successively integrated in as the flow equation in Eq.
7
is evolved from the ultraviolet
k\to\infty
to the infrared
k\to 0
. For more details about the concept and application of the fRG method, see recent reviews, e.g.,
Dupuis et al. 2021
;
Fu 2022
.

Figure 1:
Diagrammatic representation of the flow equation of the effective action. The solid and dashed lines stand for the nucleon and meson propagators, respectively, where all the mesons are included. The crossed circles denote the infrared regulators.

Substituting the nucleon-meson effective action in Eq.
1
into the flow equation in Eq.
7
and evaluating both sides on the equations of motion (EoM) for the fields
\Phi_{\mathrm{EoM}}
, i.e., the expectation values of fields in the vacuum or at a finite density, one is able to obtain the flow equation of effective potential. Note that the expectation values of fermionic fields are zero, but those of meson fields could be nonzero. Moreover, for the vector fields one has

\displaystyle\omega_{\mu}\big|_{\Phi_{\mathrm{EoM}}}=

\displaystyle\omega_{0}\delta_{\mu 0}\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mu}^{i}\big|_{\Phi_{\mathrm{EoM}}}=

\displaystyle\rho_{03}\delta_{\mu 0}\delta_{i3}\,,

(9)

which indicates that the expectation value of vector mesons is nonvanishing only when its component is the temporal one. There is another additional constraint that the isospin index should be
i=3
for the
\rho
meson.

After a direct calculation, one arrives at the flow of effective potential,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V_{k}(\sigma,\omega_{0},\rho_{03})=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{3\pi^{2}}k^{4}\Big[\Delta\mathcal{F}_{(1)}(\bar{m}_{N}^{*2};T,\mu_{p}^{*})

\displaystyle+\Delta\mathcal{F}_{(1)}(\bar{m}_{N}^{*2};T,\mu_{n}^{*})\Big]\,,

(10)

where
T
stands for the temperature, and the effective chemical potentials for the proton and neutron read

\displaystyle\mu_{p}^{*}=

\displaystyle\mu_{p}-\frac{1}{2}\Big(h_{\omega,k}\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}+h_{\rho,k}\mathrm{i}\rho_{03}\Big)\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\mu_{n}^{*}=

\displaystyle\mu_{n}-\frac{1}{2}\Big(h_{\omega,k}\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}-h_{\rho,k}\mathrm{i}\rho_{03}\Big)\,,

(12)

respectively. The effective mass of nucleons is given by

\displaystyle m_{N}^{*}=m_{N}-\frac{1}{2}h_{\sigma,k}\sigma\,,

(13)

which is the same for both the proton and neutron. One has
\bar{m}_{N}^{*}=m_{N}^{*}/k
in Eq.
10
. The threshold function
\mathcal{F}_{(1)}
is defined in Eq.
75
and its explicit expression is given in Eq.
76
. Note that in Eq.
10
the vacuum part in the threshold function is subtracted, to wit,

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{F}_{(1)}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)=\mathcal{F}_{(1)}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)-\mathcal{F}_{(1)}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};0,0)\,,

(14)

which indicates that quantum fluctuations are neglected and density fluctuations, i.e., the medium effect of high densities, are included. Note that here the temperature fluctuations are negligible, since we are interested in the case of cold nuclear matter with
T\to 0
. Since the meson propagators do not carry nucleon chemical potentials, they do not contribute to the density fluctuations. This is the reason why the meson loop in Fig.
1
does not contribute to the flow of effective potential in Eq.
10
.

It is straightforward to integrate out the flow equation in Eq.
10
, which yields

\displaystyle V_{k=0}(\sigma,\omega_{0},\rho_{03})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\frac{k^{4}}{E_{k}}\bigg[n_{F}(\bar{m}_{N}^{*2};T,\mu_{p}^{*})+n_{F}(\bar{m}_{N}^{*2};T,-\mu_{p}^{*})

\displaystyle+n_{F}(\bar{m}_{N}^{*2};T,\mu_{n}^{*})+n_{F}(\bar{m}_{N}^{*2};T,-\mu_{n}^{*})\bigg]\,,

(15)

with

\displaystyle E_{k}=\sqrt{k^{2}+m_{N}^{*2}}\,,

(16)

and the fermionic distribution function

\displaystyle n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)=\frac{1}{\exp\bigg\{\frac{1}{T}\Big[k(1+\bar{m}_{f}^{2})^{1/2}-\mu\Big]\bigg\}+1}\,.

(17)

Note that the initial condition for the effective potential in the ultraviolet limit
k\to\infty
in Eq.
6
has been used. Then, one arrives at the thermodynamic potential density,

\displaystyle\Omega=

\displaystyle V_{k=0}+\frac{1}{2}m_{\sigma}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{3}}{3!}\sigma^{3}+\frac{\lambda_{4}}{4!}\sigma^{4}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}m_{\omega}^{2}(\mathrm{i}\omega_{0})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m_{\rho}^{2}(\mathrm{i}\rho_{03})^{2}\,,

(18)

where
\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}
and
\mathrm{i}\rho_{03}
are real-valued, as discussed above. The expectation values of the field
\sigma
,
\omega_{0}
and
\rho_{03}
are determined by their respective equation of motion (EoM), i.e., the stationary conditions,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\sigma}=\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\omega_{0}}=\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\rho_{03}}=0\,.

(19)

Note that if the
k
-dependence of the Yukawa couplings in
Equations

11
,
12
and
13
is neglected and their values are set to be constants, in another word, the Yukawa couplings do not depend on the density, the effective potential in Eq.
10
or Eq.
15
would return to the result obtained in the mean field approximation, which is usually referred to as the relativistic mean field (RMF) calculation in the literature, see, e.g.,
Fu et al. 2008a
;
Fu et al. 2008b
;
Bai et al. 2021
for more details. Our interest is obvious beyond the RMF in this work. In the next section, we will investigate the RG scale dependence of the Yukawa couplings by means of their flow equations, through which the density dependence is encoded self-consistently.

IV
Density dependent Yukawa couplings

As we have discussed above, in the RMF calculations the coupling strengths are constant, independent of densities. The coupling strengths are usually fixed by fitting observables at the saturation density of the nuclear matter
\rho_{0}\approx 0.16\,\mathrm{fm}^{-3}
. This is reasonable if we are only interested in the physics not far away from the saturation density. Nevertheless, if the high density physics is concerned, e.g., those involved in heavy-ion collisions and neutron star where the baryon density can be increased up to two or even several times
\rho_{0}
, a constant coupling strength is obviously insufficient.

In this work, we would like to use the fRG to include the contribution of density fluctuations to the running of Yukawa couplings. Since the running of Yukawa couplings varies at different baryon densities, the density dependence of interaction strengths is included self-consistently via the flows of the Yukawa couplings.

Diagrammatic representation of the nucleon-meson vertex.

The flow equations of the Yukawa couplings can be obtained from the flows of the nucleon-meson vertices as shown in
Figures

2
,
3
and
4
. The flow of nucleon-sigma coupling reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{\sigma,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\Big[\mathrm{Tr}\Big(T^{0}\partial_{t}\Gamma^{(3)}_{\bar{\psi}\psi\sigma}(p_{1}=p_{0},p_{2}=p_{0},p_{3}=0)\Big)\Big]\,,

(20)

where the momentum labels are indicated in Fig.
5
. Here, the nucleon-meson vertex is projected onto the momenta configuration, that the meson momentum is vanishing and the fermion momentum is
p_{1}=p_{2}=p_{0}
with

\displaystyle p_{0}=(p_{0}^{0}=\pi T,\,\bm{p}_{0}=0)\,,

(21)

where the spatial component is zero and the temporal component is located at the lowest-order Matsubara frequency. In Eq.
20
the trace operates both in the Dirac and isospin spaces. Note that the expression in the square bracket is usually complex-valued at finite temperature and chemical potential, and choosing its real part is equivalent to averaging this expression with
p_{0}
and
-p_{0}
. Thus, this expression is real as
T\to 0
.

The flow of the nucleon-omega coupling is obtained by making the projection as follows

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{\omega,k}=-\frac{1}{6}\mathrm{Re}\Big[\mathrm{Tr}\Big(T^{0}\mathrm{i}\vec{\gamma}_{\mu}\partial_{t}\big(\Gamma^{(3)}_{\bar{\psi}\psi\omega}\big)_{\mu}(p_{0})\Big)\Big]\,,

(22)

where
\vec{\gamma}_{\mu}
is the spatial component of the
\gamma
matrix, that is

\displaystyle\vec{\gamma}_{\mu}=(1-\delta_{\mu 0})\gamma_{\mu}\,.

(23)

In Eq.
22
we have projected the vector vertex onto the spatial components. In fact, the Lorentz symmetry is broken at finite temperature and chemical potential. Hence, in principle it is more reasonable to distinguish the temporal and spatial Yukawa couplings for the vector vertices. In this work, we simplify the calculations by using the spatial component and neglecting its difference with respect to the temporal one. This truncation is found to be an appropriate approximation and thus commonly used in literature, see e.g.,
Yin et al. 2019
;
Fu et al. 2020
. The flow of the nucleon-rho coupling reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{\rho,k}=-\frac{1}{6}\mathrm{Re}\Big[\mathrm{Tr}\Big(T^{3}\mathrm{i}\vec{\gamma}_{\mu}\partial_{t}\big(\Gamma^{(3)}_{\bar{\psi}\psi\rho}\big)_{\mu}^{i=3}(p_{0})\Big)\Big]\,,

(24)

where the projection is performed upon the isospin generator
T^{3}
.

The explicit expressions of the flows of Yukawa couplings in
Equations

20
,
22
and
24
are presented in
Appendix

B
, where it can be found that the flows could be expressed in terms of a variety of threshold functions. The threshold functions usually take the form, e.g.,
\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}
, where the subscripts correspond to the numbers of the fermionic and bosonic propagators involved in the threshold function. Similar with the threshold function
\mathcal{F}_{(1)}
in Eq.
14
, it is necessary to separate the vacuum part from the finite density part in
\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}
, since in the DFT one is allowed to investigate the density effect exclusively. The medium part of threshold function is obtained as

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T,\mu,p_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T,\mu,p_{0})

\displaystyle-\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};0,0,0)\,.

(25)

Note that at vanishing temperature and finite chemical potential there is an important property known as the Silver Blaze property, which dictates that the system can reach a finite density only when the chemical potential exceeds a critical value
\mu_{c}

Cohen 2003
. The Silver Blaze property is broken in Eq.
25
, since the Matsubara frequency of the external fermion line in the nucleon-meson vertices is truncated at some value, such as that in Eq.
21
, rather than summed for all its values
Fu and Pawlowski 2015
;
Fu et al. 2016
;
Fu et al. 2020
. In order to recover the Silver Blaze property, we modify Eq.
25
as such

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T,\mu,p_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T,\mu,p_{0})

\displaystyle-\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};0,0,0)\Big]\Big[n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)

\displaystyle+n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,-\mu)\Big]\,,

(26)

where a factor
n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)+n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,-\mu)
comprised of the distribution functions for the fermion and anti-fermion is multiplied with the original expression in Eq.
25
. Note that this modification is motivated by the two-loop calculations in fRG
Fu et al. 2016
, where it is found that this factor always emerges after the summation for the Matsubara frequency of the external fermion line is done. This can be understood readily, since the factor has the following property at
T=0
: the factor is vanishing once the energy of fermion is above the Fermi surface and it is unity when it is below the Fermi surface. In another word, this factor would not change the value of Eq.
25
below the Fermi surface while would suppress it dramatically above the Fermi surface, that is consistent with the expectation of density flows.

We would like to pause here and discuss the reliability of the nucleon-meson effective field theory in Eq.
1
in the regime of high baryon densities. For a crude estimate it is convenient to define a ratio as follows

\displaystyle R=\frac{2r_{N}}{\rho^{-1/3}}\,,

(27)

where
r_{N}
denotes the radius of nucleon, and thus the numerator represents the diameter of nucleon. Here we adopt the value of proton charge radius for
r_{N}
, which was found to be
r_{N}=0.84\sim 0.88
fm through experimental measurement
Lee et al. 2015
;
Bezginov et al. 2019
. The denominator in Eq.
27
represents the average distance between two nucleons. Then, one immediately arrives at
R=0.91\sim 0.96
for
\rho=\rho_{0}
,
R=1.04\sim 1.09
for
\rho=1.5\rho_{0}
,
R=1.15\sim 1.20
for
\rho=2\rho_{0}
,
R=1.24\sim 1.30
for
\rho=2.5\rho_{0}
, and
R=1.32\sim 1.38
for
\rho=3\rho_{0}
. It is remarkable to note that when the baryon density is just about 1.5 times the saturation density, the nucleons begin to overlap with each other. The overlap factor in Eq.
27
grows up to
\sim 1.3
when the density is increased to
\rho=2.5\rho_{0}
, where the substructure of nucleon and the resulting dynamics should begin to play a role, that are missing in the nucleon-meson effective theory. In short, the reliability of calculations in this work is reasonably doubted in the high density regime of, e.g.,
\rho\gtrsim 3\rho_{0}
.

The running Yukawa couplings lead directly to the running effective nucleon mass and effective chemical potentials as well, as shown in Eq.
13
,
11
and
12
, in sharp contrast to the RMF case, where both the effective mass and the effective potential are constants at a fixed value of baryon density. It is expectable that these new properties would give rise to novel effects. In Fig.
7
the effective mass and effective chemical potential are depicted as functions of the RG scale
k

\rho
meson is vanishing in the symmetric nuclear matter. It is found from the left panel of Fig.

k
is lowered down below the threshold value. Moreover, with the increase of the baryon density the effective mass is smaller in the UV, i.e., in the region of large
k
, which is similar with the RMF calculation. However, the effective mass increases more significantly for larger
\rho
and its value is larger in the IR, which is quite different from the RMF. One can see that when the baryon density is increased up to
\rho=2.5\rho_{0}
, the effective nucleon mass at
k=0
is close to the nucleon mass in the vacuum.

In the right panel of Fig.
7
one finds that the effective chemical potential increases as well when the RG scale
k
is below some threshold value. Furthermore, we also compare the energy of quasi-nucleon in Eq.
16
, named the effective nucleon energy hereafter, with the effective chemical potential. It is found that in the region of large
k
the effective energy is larger than the effective chemical potential and there are no flows or particles. When the RG scale
k
is below the threshold value, the effective energy is smaller than the effective chemical potential, but in the subsequent sizable region of
k
, the effective energy is very close to the effective chemical potential. This is a novel phenomenon and has never been observed in the RMF calculations, see Fig.
8
, where
E_{k}-\mu^{*}
is shown, and the fRG and RMF are compared. This phenomenon is not accidental since it takes place for all the results with different baryon densities as shown in the right panel of Fig.
7
and Fig.

.

It is interesting to investigate how this phenomenon takes place. Notice that the running of the Yukawa couplings is possible only when the effective energy is below the effective chemical potential, i.e., below the Fermi surface. In another word, the flows of Yukawa couplings are driven only when the solid line is below the dashed line in the right panel of Fig.
7
. One can see that the Fermi surface is also running with the RG scale
k
. Once the solid line is below the dashed line, the screening effect begins to play a role and it increases the effective mass, so the solid line is pushed toward the dashed line, but cannot be pushed over the dashed line, otherwise, it will cease the running of flows. Consequently, the solid and dashed lines are inclined to stay together. We would like to call this phenomenon the locking between the effective energy and the effective chemical potential, or the locking of Fermi surface concisely.

Further investigation reveals that the vector exchange in the flow of
h_{\sigma,k}
, that is, the second and third diagrams in Fig.

, plays a pivotal role in the screening effect of nuclear medium in the attractive
\sigma

In Sec.

n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)+n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,-\mu)
in the threshold functions in Eq.

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T,\mu,p_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T,\mu,p_{0})

\displaystyle-\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};0,0,0)\Big]\Big[n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,c_{{}_{SB}}\mu)

\displaystyle\Big[n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)+n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,-\mu)\Big]

VII
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, we have proposed the method of density functional renormalization group (density-fRG) to compute the density fluctuations within the functional renormalization group approach. The density-fRG is feasible if the density fluctuations can be separated from the vacuum, i.e., quantum fluctuations. Therefore, one is allowed to deal with the medium effect exclusively and the density fluctuations are taken into account successively through the RG evolution of flow equations, which simplifies the calculations significantly at high densities. The separation of the density and quantum fluctuations is guaranteed by the fact that they dominate in different regimes of RG scale: The density fluctuation plays a role mainly in the regime of
k\lesssim 400\sim 500
MeV, as shown in Fig.
7
and Fig.
8
, while the quantum fluctuation usually dominates above this scale, see the first-principles QCD calculations in, e.g.,
Fu et al. 2020
.

The density-fRG is applied to the nucleon-meson effective field theory, also known as the Walecka model, to study the properties of nuclear matter at high baryon densities. We investigate the running of the Yukawa couplings driven by the density fluctuations in detail. It is found that the medium effect screens both the attractive nucleon-scalar-meson (
N-\sigma
) and the repulsive nucleon-vector-meson (
N-\omega
) interactions. As a consequence of the screening effect and the running Yukawa couplings, a new phenomenon called the locking of Fermi surface is found. In the locking of Fermi surface the effective energy of quasi-nucleon is always close to the Fermi surface, which are both running with the RG scale. Furthermore, the screening effect also leads to that the equation of state (EoS) of nuclear matter is stiffer in the regime of
\rho_{0}\lesssim\rho\lesssim 2.5\rho_{0}
and then becomes softer when
\rho\gtrsim 2.5\rho_{0}
. Note however that the reliability of the nucleon-meson EFT is gradually lost in the region of
\rho\gtrsim(2.5\sim 3)\rho_{0}
.

It is straightforward to extend the studies in this work. The investigation of the symmetry energy of nuclear matter at high densities within the density-fRG is underway, which will be reported in the near future
Chen et al. 2025b
. Moreover, in the density-fRG it is readily to include contributions from the pion meson, which is vanishing in the mean field approximation, but its fluctuations are sizable because the pion mass is significantly smaller than the masses of other mesons. Furthermore, it is also convenient to extend the nucleon-meson effective action in Eq.
1
to include the degrees of freedom of hyperons. The interaction of hyperons and its modification in the high density medium are subjects of high interest and importance in nuclear physics and nuclear astrophysics. Most importantly, it is desirable to combine the theoretical results obtained in the density-fRG or even the density-fRG itself with some phenomenological transport model, such that one is able to compare with experimental data of heavy-ion collisions in the upcoming new facilities of high densities.

Appendix A
Some notations

A.1
Propagators and infrared regulators

The two-nucleon correlation function reads

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(2)}_{\bar{\psi}\psi}(p^{\prime},p)=\frac{\overrightarrow{\delta}}{\delta\bar{\psi}_{N}(p^{\prime})}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]\frac{\overleftarrow{\delta}}{\delta\psi_{N}(p)}\bigg|_{\Phi=\Phi_{\mathrm{EoM}}}\,,

(36)

where the left and right derivatives are used for the fermionic field of nucleons. The
p
and
p^{\prime}
stand for the momenta of anti-nucleon and nucleon, respectively, and their directions are assumed to be along the direction of the fermionic flow, otherwise stated explicitly. Inserting Eq.
1
into Eq.
36
, one arrives at

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(2)}_{\bar{\psi}\psi}(p^{\prime},p)=\Gamma^{(2)}_{\bar{\psi}\psi}(p)(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p^{\prime}-p)\,,

(37)

with

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(2)}_{\bar{\psi}\psi}(p)=\gamma_{0}(\mathrm{i}p_{0}-\hat{\mu}_{N}^{*})+\mathrm{i}\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{p}+m_{N}^{*}\,,

(38)

where the effective chemical potential is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{N}^{*}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{p}^{*}&0\\
0&\mu_{n}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(39)

In this work we adopt the
3d
optimized, i.e., flat IR regulators
Litim 2000
;
Litim 2001
, which are used to suppress fluctuations of modes with momenta smaller than the RG scale, that is,
p\lesssim k
, see, e.g.,
Fu 2022
for details. The fermionic regulator reads

\displaystyle R_{\bar{\psi}\psi}(p)=\mathrm{i}\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{p}\,r_{F}(\bm{p}^{2}/k^{2})\,,

(40)

with the shape function

\displaystyle r_{F}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}-1\right)\Theta(1-x)\,,

(41)

where
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function. Then one arrives at the nucleon propagator with an IR regulator, as follows

\displaystyle G_{\psi\bar{\psi}}(p)=

\displaystyle\Big[\Gamma^{(2)}_{\bar{\psi}\psi}(p)+R_{\bar{\psi}\psi}(p)\Big]^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}G_{p}(p)&0\\
0&G_{n}(p)\end{pmatrix}\,,

(42)

where the proton and neutron propagator read

\displaystyle G_{p/n}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\gamma_{0}(p_{0}+\mathrm{i}\mu_{p/n}^{*})+\mathrm{i}\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{p}\big(1+r_{F}(\bm{p}^{2}/k^{2})\big)+m_{N}^{*}}\,.

(43)

The two-point correlation function for the
\sigma
meson reads

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma}(p^{\prime},p)=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]}{\delta\sigma(p^{\prime})\delta\sigma(p)}\bigg|_{\Phi=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma}(p)(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p^{\prime}+p)\,,

(44)

with

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma}(p)=p^{2}+m_{\sigma}^{2}\,.

(45)

Note that in this work we have not taken into account the density effect on the meson masses, and
m_{\sigma}
in the equation above denotes the
\sigma
mass in the vacuum. This also applies to the
\omega
and
\rho
mesons. This is because the influence of the meson masses is indirect in comparison to the Yukawa couplings. The IR regulator for the
\sigma
meson is given by

\displaystyle R_{\sigma\sigma}(p)=\bm{p}^{2}\,r_{B}(\bm{p}^{2}/k^{2})\,,

(46)

with the bosonic shape function

\displaystyle r_{B}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{x}-1\right)\Theta(1-x)\,.

(47)

Thus, the
\sigma
propagator reads

\displaystyle G_{\sigma}(p)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma}(p)+R_{\sigma\sigma}(p)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{0}^{2}+\bm{p}^{2}\big(1+r_{B}(\bm{p}^{2}/k^{2})\big)+m_{\sigma}^{2}}\,.

(48)

For the vector meson
\omega
we obtain the two-point correlation function as follows

\displaystyle\big(\Gamma^{(2)}_{\omega\omega}\big)_{\mu\nu}(p^{\prime},p)=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]}{\delta\omega_{\mu}(p^{\prime})\delta\omega_{\nu}(p)}\bigg|_{\Phi=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\Gamma^{(2)}_{\omega\omega}\big)_{\mu\nu}(p)(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(p^{\prime}+p)\,,

(49)

with

\displaystyle\big(\Gamma^{(2)}_{\omega\omega}\big)_{\mu\nu}(p)=\Big(p^{2}+m_{\omega}^{2}\Big)\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}(p)+\bigg(m_{\omega}^{2}+\frac{p^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\parallel}(p)\,,

(50)

where the transverse and longitudinal polarization tensors read

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}(p)=

\displaystyle\delta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}{p^{2}}\,,

(51)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\parallel}(p)=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}{p^{2}}\,,

(52)

respectively. Note that in Eq.
50
we have added the gauge fixing term with the gauge parameter
\xi
. The regulator of the
\omega
meson reads

\displaystyle\big(R_{\omega\omega}\big)_{\mu\nu}(p)=\bm{p}^{2}\,r_{B}(\bm{p}^{2}/k^{2})\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}(p)\,.

(53)

Then, one arrives at the propagator of the
\omega
meson

\displaystyle G_{\omega,\mu\nu}(p)=

\displaystyle\Big[\Gamma^{(2)}_{\omega\omega}(p)+R_{\omega\omega}(p)\Big]^{-1}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{0}^{2}+\bm{p}^{2}\big(1+r_{B}(\bm{p}^{2}/k^{2})\big)+m_{\omega}^{2}}\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}(p)\,,

(54)

where we have chosen the gauge parameter
\xi=0
. In the same way, one obtains the propagator of
\rho
meson as follows

\displaystyle G^{ij}_{\rho,\mu\nu}(p)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{0}^{2}+\bm{p}^{2}\big(1+r_{B}(\bm{p}^{2}/k^{2})\big)+m_{\omega}^{2}}\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{\perp}(p)\delta_{ij}\,.

(55)

Appendix C
Threshold functions

We define the dimensionless scalar propagators for the bosonic and fermionic fields

\displaystyle G_{b}(q,\bar{m}_{b}^{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\bar{q}_{0}^{2}+1+\bar{m}_{b}^{2}}\,,

(73)

\displaystyle G_{f}(q,\bar{m}_{f}^{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\bar{q}_{0}+\mathrm{i}\bar{\mu})^{2}+1+\bar{m}_{f}^{2}}\,,

(74)

with
\bar{q}_{0}=q_{0}/k
,
\bar{\mu}=\mu/k
,
\bar{m}_{b}=m_{b}/k
and
\bar{m}_{f}=m_{f}/k
. The Matsubara frequency reads
q_{0}=2n_{q}\pi T
(
n_{q}\in\mathbb{Z}
) for bosons and
(2n_{q}+1)\pi T
for fermions. To proceed, we define the threshold function as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{(n)}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)=\frac{T}{k}\sum_{n_{q}}\big(G_{f}(q,\bar{m}_{f}^{2})\big)^{n}\,.

(75)

A direct calculation by summing up the Matsubara frequencies leads us to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{(1)}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\bar{m}_{f}^{2}}}\bigg(1-n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)

\displaystyle-n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,-\mu)\bigg)\,.

(76)

Furthermore, we also need threshold functions mixing the fermionic and bosonic propagators, and there are three different types in the flows of Yukawa couplings, which read

\displaystyle\mathcal{FB}^{a}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T,\mu,p_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{T}{k}\sum_{n_{q}}\Big(G_{f}(q,\bar{m}_{f}^{2})\Big)^{n_{f}}\Big(G_{b}(q-p,\bar{m}_{b}^{2})\Big)^{n_{b}}\,,

(77)

\displaystyle\mathcal{FB}^{b}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2},x=\bar{\bm{q}}^{2};T,\mu,p_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{T}{k}\sum_{n_{q}}\Big(G_{f}(q,\bar{m}_{f}^{2})\Big)^{n_{f}}\Big(G_{b}(q-p,\bar{m}_{b}^{2})\Big)^{n_{b}}

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69046pt\times\frac{1}{(\bar{q}_{0}-\bar{p}_{0})^{2}+\bar{\bm{q}}^{2}}\,,

(78)

\displaystyle\mathcal{FB}^{c}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2},x=\bar{\bm{q}}^{2};T,\mu,p_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{T}{k}\sum_{n_{q}}\Big(G_{f}(q,\bar{m}_{f}^{2})\Big)^{n_{f}}\Big(G_{b}(q-p,\bar{m}_{b}^{2})\Big)^{n_{b}}

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69046pt\times\frac{1}{(\bar{q}_{0}-\bar{p}_{0})^{2}+\bar{\bm{q}}^{2}}(\bar{q}_{0}+\mathrm{i}\bar{\mu})(\bar{q}_{0}-\bar{p}_{0})\,,

(79)

with
x=\bar{\bm{q}}^{2}=\bm{q}^{2}/k^{2}\in[0,1]
. It is convenient to obtain higher-order threshold functions from the lower-order ones, e.g.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f}+1,n_{b})}(m_{f}^{2},m_{b}^{2})=-\frac{1}{n_{f}}\frac{\partial}{\partial m_{f}^{2}}\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(m_{f}^{2},m_{b}^{2})\,,

(80)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b}+1)}(m_{f}^{2},m_{b}^{2})=-\frac{1}{n_{b}}\frac{\partial}{\partial m_{b}^{2}}\mathcal{FB}_{(n_{f},n_{b})}(m_{f}^{2},m_{b}^{2})\,.

(81)

Therefore, it is sufficient to present the explicit expressions for the lowest-order threshold functions. The mixing threshold function of first type is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{FB}^{a}_{(1,1)}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2},\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T,\mu,p_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k^{2}}{2}\bigg\{-n_{B}(\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\bar{m}_{b}^{2}}}\frac{1}{\Big(\mathrm{i}p_{0}-\mu+k(1+\bar{m}_{b}^{2})^{1/2}\Big)^{2}-k^{2}(1+\bar{m}_{f}^{2})}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\big(n_{B}(\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T)+1\big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\bar{m}_{b}^{2}}}\frac{1}{\Big(\mathrm{i}p_{0}-\mu-k(1+\bar{m}_{b}^{2})^{1/2}\Big)^{2}-k^{2}(1+\bar{m}_{f}^{2})}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,-\mu)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\bar{m}_{f}^{2}}}\frac{1}{\Big(\mathrm{i}p_{0}-\mu-k(1+\bar{m}_{f}^{2})^{1/2}\Big)^{2}-k^{2}(1+\bar{m}_{b}^{2})}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\big(n_{F}(\bar{m}_{f}^{2};T,\mu)-1\big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\bar{m}_{f}^{2}}}\frac{1}{\Big(\mathrm{i}p_{0}-\mu+k(1+\bar{m}_{f}^{2})^{1/2}\Big)^{2}-k^{2}(1+\bar{m}_{b}^{2})}\bigg\}\,,

(82)

where the bosonic distribution function reads

\displaystyle n_{B}(\bar{m}_{b}^{2};T)=\frac{1}{\exp\bigg\{\frac{k}{T}\Big(1+\bar{m}_{b}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\bigg\}-1}\,.

(83)

|\bm{q}|=kx
