Schistosomiasis in Yemen: Focal Persistence, Possible Resurgence, and Challenges to Elimination
Authors: Rashad Abdul-Ghani, Ahmed A Azazy, Abdullah Abdu Al-Mikhlafy, Sami A Al-Haidari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchistosomiasis in Yemen, caused by Schistosoma mansoni and S. haematobium, persists as a focal public health problem despite decades of control based largely on repeated praziquantel (PZQ)-based mass drug administration (MDA). This review synthesizes epidemiological, malacological, clinical, and programmatic evidence to examine why substantial national prevalence reductions have not culminated in sustained progress toward elimination. The evidence indicates a shift from broad endemicity toward geographically uneven persistence and possible resurgence, particularly where unsafe freshwater exposure, inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH), receptive snail habitats, population movement, and surveillance gaps coincide. By 2017, MDA impact evaluation showed that overall prevalence had fallen below the 10% treatment threshold, but recent school-based surveys show persistent or resurgent district-level transmission. PZQ-based MDA has achieved important gains in morbidity control, but elimination will require geographically targeted preventive chemotherapy, supported by updated micro-mapping, species-specific sentinel surveillance, improved diagnostics, WASH strengthening, health education, focal snail surveillance and control, and rapid response to residual or re-emerging hotspots. Integrating these measures within primary healthcare, school health, and humanitarian platforms will be essential for preserving previous gains and preventing localized transmission from expanding.Keywords: schistosomiasis, epidemiology, mass drug administration, control, elimination, Yemen

Introduction

Schistosomiasis is a water- and snail-borne parasitic disease caused by Schistosoma species. In the Middle East, including Yemen, urogenital schistosomiasis is caused by S. haematobium and intestinal schistosomiasis by S. mansoni, with their respective distributions shaped by the ecology of Bulinus and Biomphalaria snail populations, respectively.1 Globally, a 2021 study estimated that schistosomiasis was associated with 151.4 million cases, 12,858 deaths, and 1.75 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs).2 Despite being preventable and curable, schistosomiasis remains a neglected tropical disease (NTD) that disproportionately affects impoverished rural populations in low-income countries.3 Transmission has been reported in 79 countries, and the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that 253.7 million people in 50 moderate-to-high transmission countries required preventive chemotherapy (PC) in 2024, of whom 100.5 million were treated.1

Schistosomiasis morbidity varies by species, infection intensity, disease stage, and affected organs. Acute schistosomiasis or Katayama syndrome may occur after primary infection as a systemic hypersensitivity reaction, especially in non-immune individuals, and can cause fever, eosinophilia, gastrointestinal symptoms, lymphadenopathy, and hepatosplenomegaly.4,5 Chronic disease results from egg-induced granulomatous inflammation, fibrosis, and organ-specific damage.5,6 Intestinal schistosomiasis may cause abdominal pain, diarrhea, bloody or mucoid stool, intestinal polyposis, hepatosplenomegaly, periportal fibrosis, portal hypertension, ascites, variceal bleeding, and cardiopulmonary complications.5,7,8 Urogenital schistosomiasis may cause hematuria, dysuria, bladder lesions, obstructive uropathy, hydronephrosis, kidney damage, genital lesions, and bladder cancer.5,9,10 Both forms are associated with subtle but important childhood morbidities, including anemia, impaired growth, and reduced cognitive development.5,11

Mass drug administration (MDA) with praziquantel (PZQ) remains central to schistosomiasis control because it reduces prevalence, infection intensity, and morbidity.12,13 However, the 2022 WHO guidelines broaden the strategic focus beyond morbidity control toward elimination as a public health problem and, ultimately, interruption of transmission.13 They recommend annual PC with PZQ in endemic communities with a prevalence of ≥10%, targeting all eligible groups aged ≥2 years at ≥75% coverage. Below this threshold, continued monitoring and reduced-frequency treatment or test-and-treat strategies are recommended, while biannual treatment may be considered for persistent hotspots with prevalence ≥10% despite ≥75% annual coverage.13 The guidelines also emphasize integrating PC with water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH), behavior change, environmental management, and focal snail control.13

In Yemen, the 2017 impact evaluation suggested that the overall schistosomiasis prevalence had declined below the WHO 10% PC threshold.14 Nevertheless, later evidence from the 2024 impact evaluation and district-level studies, including 2025 school-based surveys, indicates persistent or possibly resurgent transmission in selected districts.15–20 Moreover, WHO prevalence thresholds are intended for endemic communities or implementation units rather than for national averages; therefore, district- and village-level foci may still require PC even when the national prevalence is below 10%. Despite long-standing endemicity, repeated MDA, and growing concerns about focal persistence or possible resurgence, evidence remains scattered across historical surveys, national mapping exercises, program datasets, and recent field investigations. Therefore, this review synthesizes evidence on schistosomiasis in Yemen and identifies gaps that may have weakened previous gains and continued to hinder elimination.

Literature Search and Narrative Synthesis

This narrative review synthesized peer-reviewed and grey literature on schistosomiasis in Yemen. PubMed and Google Scholar were searched together with WHO documents, program reports, and reference lists of eligible publications. The search covered literature from the early twentieth century to May 5, 2026, using the Boolean strategy: “(Schistosoma OR schistosomiasis OR bilharzia OR bilharziasis) AND Yemen”, without language or study-design restrictions. A small number of early publications were excluded because the abstracts or full texts were unavailable. Non-Yemen studies were included only when they provided interpretive or comparative context. Because the included studies varied widely in design, diagnostic approach, study population, geographic scale, and reporting quality, the findings were synthesized narratively rather than pooled quantitatively.

Programmatic data were obtained from the Administration of NTD Control at the Ministry of Health and Environment in Sana’a. These data comprised Excel-based records from the 2010 baseline mapping, 2014 nationwide follow-up mapping, impact evaluation surveys, and MDA campaign implementation records. Each source was interpreted according to its design, purpose, and geographic scope. The 2010 baseline mapping was used to describe district-level endemicity before the implementation of Yemen’s nationwide Schistosomiasis Control Project (SCP), which delivered multiple rounds of praziquantel-based MDA from 2010 to 2017. The 2014 follow-up remapping was used to assess changes in district endemicity after the SCP scale-up, whereas impact evaluation surveys were interpreted as program-level assessments of post-MDA control progress and as evidence of temporal changes in prevalence, including baseline estimates from 2010 to 2012 and follow-up estimates from 2014, 2017, and 2024. Accordingly, comparisons between district-level prevalence estimates from mapping or impact evaluation datasets and later district-level surveys were made descriptively to identify possible persistence, resurgence, or species-specific shifts in the selected hotspots. Because some programmatic datasets were not publicly available, they were used as aggregate control program data and interpreted cautiously along with published evidence. This approach integrates historical surveys, national mapping data, impact evaluations, and recent district-level findings into a narrative of the changing endemicity, persistent focal transmission, and elimination challenges in Yemen.

Overview of Schistosomiasis in Yemen

Despite decades of control, schistosomiasis has persisted in Yemen as an increasingly focal public health challenge. Both S. haematobium and S. mansoni are endemic, especially among impoverished rural communities and children.15,17–21 The epidemiological pattern has shifted from broad endemicity to focal persistence, with marked geographic and species heterogeneity. Poverty, occupational and domestic water exposure, inadequate WASH infrastructure, and ecological suitability for snail intermediate hosts sustain transmission.15,17–25 In 2022, more than 3.6 million people, including more than 3 million school-age children (SAC), required PC, with 78.7% overall coverage and 74.2% coverage among SAC.26 Yemen remained one of only three countries in the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR) requiring PC for schistosomiasis in 2024, alongside Sudan and Somalia.26

Following the 2010 launch of Yemen’s nationwide SCP, multiple rounds of PZQ-based MDA during 2010–2017 substantially reduced the prevalence and infection intensity.27 Before SCP implementation, baseline risk mapping in 2010 among SAC stratified all 333 districts by endemicity risk using the 2006 WHO prevalence thresholds of <10% for low risk, ≥10% to <50% for moderate risk, and ≥50% for high risk,28 with a program-specific modification defining high-endemic districts at ≥40% rather than ≥50% to expand treatment coverage. Of 333 districts, 275 were classified as known or suspected endemic, including 51 high-endemic, 79 meso-endemic, 35 low-endemic, and 110 suspected endemic districts, while 58 were non-endemic.14 The 2014 follow-up mapping in 332 districts showed a major downward shift in endemicity. High-endemic districts declined to three using a conservative ≥30% threshold to define high endemicity, meso-endemic districts fell to 34, and low-endemic and non-endemic districts increased to 186 and 109, respectively.14

By 2017, the impact evaluation data showed a temporal decline in the prevalence. Compared with the 2010–2012 impact-evaluation baseline prevalence of 16.7% overall and 17.3% among SAC, the prevalence declined to 7.5% overall and 9.5% among SAC in 2014, and further to 6.9% overall and 7.4% among SAC in 2017.14 These estimates indicate national-level control success in reducing prevalence below the WHO 10% PC threshold.13 However, the 2024 impact evaluation suggested a reversal of these gains at the national level, with the overall prevalence among SAC increasing to 20.4%.29 Moreover, recent district-level studies show persistent or renewed focal transmission compared with earlier district-level estimates from mapping and impact evaluation surveys.15–20 These findings indicate that PZQ-based MDA achieved important morbidity control and burden reduction gains, but that these gains were spatially uneven and vulnerable to reversal where ecological exposure, weak WASH infrastructure, population movement, and surveillance gaps persist.

Beyond its direct health effects, schistosomiasis may have substantial socioeconomic consequences in Yemen, particularly in disadvantaged rural communities. However, the extent of this burden remains poorly quantified. Schistosomiasis acts as both a driver and a consequence of socioeconomic deprivation in the country, with fragile infrastructure, protracted conflict, and humanitarian crises further intensifying population vulnerability.30 Schistosomiasis may exacerbate malnutrition and anemia among Yemeni schoolchildren,17 leading to physical and cognitive impairments that reduce school attendance and educational performance. The educational environment itself can also serve as a site of risk, as schools located near streams and other freshwater sources have been reported to have significantly higher infection rates among students.15,21,24 Among adolescents and adults, chronic disease can reduce physical capacity, work productivity, and household earning potential. These consequences are likely to be concentrated in poor rural communities that depend on agriculture and unsafe surface water, thereby reinforcing a cycle of infection, poverty, and social vulnerability. Higher S. mansoni infection rates and childhood wasting among families of low or moderate socioeconomic status further illustrate the close relationship among disease-related morbidity, deprivation, and undernutrition,17 with potentially lasting effects on physical and cognitive development. Nevertheless, the absence of Yemen-specific cost-of-illness and longitudinal socioeconomic studies indicates that the magnitude of these effects remains uncertain, underscoring an important priority for future research.

Historical Insights into Schistosomiasis Endemicity

Early Reports on Schistosomiasis

Early reports from southern Yemen during British colonial rule have documented both urogenital and intestinal schistosomiasis. In 1922, four Yemeni patients diagnosed in Aden hospitals included two cases of S. haematobium and two of S. mansoni, providing early evidence of both forms among Yemenis and one of the earliest records of S. mansoni infection in Asia.31,32 A 1939–1940 survey in the Western Aden Protectorate identified 34 cases, including 10 confirmed S. haematobium and two S. mansoni infections, mainly in inland rather than coastal districts, demonstrating geographic and species-level heterogeneity.33 Later, S. haematobium infection was documented in 73% of boys and 52% of girls in Khanfar district, and six of eight schoolchildren were infected in As-Sa’id district, Shabwah.34

Early reports from northern Yemen under the Mutawakkilite Kingdom rule before 1962 documented both S. haematobium and S. mansoni and showed marked geographic and species-level heterogeneity. In the 1930s, urogenital schistosomiasis was reported in Sana’a, nearby highland villages, and lowland communities, with approximately 200 microscopically confirmed cases over 2 years and an estimated S. haematobium prevalence of 9.5% in lowland populations.32,35 It was also described as common in the middle highlands.36 Surveys in the early 1950s in Hodeidah, Taiz, and Sana’a confirmed the coexistence of both S. haematobium and S. mansoni across coastal, mid-altitude, and highland settings.37 S. mansoni was most prominent in Taiz and related highland foci, whereas S. haematobium appeared more concentrated in Taiz and some lowland or middle-highland areas,32,37 indicating that transmission was already spatially focal rather than evenly distributed. This pattern likely reflects differences in altitude, water ecology, snail habitat, and human movement. It has been suggested that both Schistosoma species may have been introduced or amplified through repeated movement of infected individuals from East Africa, including Eritrea.37 Recent DNA barcoding provides supportive but not definitive evidence for this hypothesis, linking Yemeni S. mansoni and S. haematobium haplotypes to African and coastal East African lineages and suggesting parasite exchange through population movement.38 S. mansoni showed the greatest diversity in Taiz and Ibb governorates, suggesting long-standing foci and possible sources of onward spread to other parts of the country, whereas S. haematobium showed lower diversity and clearer geographic structuring across Taiz, Dhamar, Ibb, and Hodeidah.38 Together, historical and molecular evidence highlights the long-standing focality of schistosome transmission in Yemen and supports molecular surveillance to identify persistent foci and track parasite movement.

Reports Among Yemeni Migrants

Studies among Yemeni migrants provide indirect but important evidence of long-standing endemicity. Among Yemeni agricultural workers in California who emigrated between the early 1950s and 1973, S. mansoni prevalence was 56%, with infection persisting for several years after migration but declining with longer residence abroad.39 In Manchester, United Kingdom, a 1980 screening program identified S. haematobium infection among 4.8% of Yemeni migrants,40 and in the early 1990s, Yemeni male residents in Abha City, southwestern Saudi Arabia, had S. mansoni and S. haematobium prevalence rates of 25.3% and 11.9%, respectively.41 Although some infections could have been acquired after migration, these studies probably reflect substantial historical transmission in Yemen and support cross-border diagnosis, treatment, and surveillance.

Malacological Investigations and Ecological Transmission Patterns

Malacological investigations have shown that schistosomiasis transmission in Yemen depends on localized freshwater habitats that support Bulinus and Biomphalaria intermediate hosts. Early records identified Bulinus snails near Sana’a in areas with S. haematobium cases, Biomphalaria arabica in southern and highland settings, and Bulinus truncatus in the highlands; later experimental work confirmed the susceptibility of Yemeni B. arabica and B. truncatus to S. mansoni and S. haematobium, respectively.35,42,43 Natural infection among Biomphalaria snails from mosque ablution basins in Taiz and later surveys of cisterns, streams, ablution chambers, irrigation canals, and village washing sites showed that transmission was concentrated in small, human-used water bodies rather than across broad landscapes.32,34,44,45 Subsequent surveys in southern governorates further confirmed the presence of several Bulinus species, including B. forskalii, B. beccari, B. reticulatus, and B. sericinus, as well as Biomphalaria rueppelli.34 Natural infection was confirmed through cercarial shedding in B. beccari and B. sericinus, while experimental infection was observed in B. reticulatus.34

Systematic surveys in northern Yemen have clarified the distribution and transmission potential of intermediate host snails. In 1971, B. truncatus and Biomphalaria pfeifferi were detected in various water bodies, mainly cisterns, streams, ablution chambers, and irrigation canals,45 with experimental exposure confirming the susceptibility of B. truncatus to S. haematobium and B. pfeifferi to S. mansoni.45 In the 1980s, three Bulinus species—B. beccarii, B. truncatus, and B. wrighti—and B. pfeifferi were detected in Taiz, with natural infection detected among 1.1% of B. beccarii snails and 31% of B. pfeifferi snails.46 In Amran governorate, north of Sana’a, where urogenital schistosomiasis is highly endemic, Bulinus snails predominated while Biomphalaria snails were rare.47 Water-resource development also created or amplified transmission potential. Reconstruction of the Marib Dam and irrigation expansion generated freshwater habitats in an arid governorate, where both B. arabica and B. truncatus were naturally infected during 1991–1992.48 These malacological observations explain why reinfection and residual focal transmission can persist after MDA when snail habitats and human water contact remain unchanged.

Epidemiological Schistosomiasis Landscape Since the 1970s

This epidemiological landscape traces Yemen’s shift from historically widespread endemicity to more focal, heterogeneous post-MDA transmission, emphasizing districts with persistence, species-specific shifts, or possible resurgence, which are most relevant to elimination planning.

Non-Endemic Governorates: Need for Surveillance Strengthening

The Aden, Al-Mahrah, and Socotra governorates have no documented local transmission of schistosomiasis and were classified as non-endemic in both the 2010 baseline and 2014 follow-up mapping surveys. Rather than treating these governorates as routine MDA priorities, they should be included in risk-based surveillance because migration, displacement, and possible ecological microhabitats can introduce infections. For these low-risk settings, routine MDA is not justified; instead, surveillance should focus on the screening and treatment of imported cases, with ecological assessment where suitable snail habitats or population movement create plausible transmission risk.

Early Large-Scale Surveys

Early large-scale surveys since the 1970s shifted the evidence base from scattered clinical and malacological observations to population-level epidemiology. A 1971 population-based survey in randomly selected villages around major cities across five of eight northern provinces showed widespread but highly heterogeneous transmission, with an overall prevalence of 34% for S. haematobium and 29% for S. mansoni and local rates ranging from 0% to 100%.45 Based on these estimates, the author estimated that more than 1 million people may have been infected among a population of 5–6 million, although the true burden may have been higher because diagnosis relied on relatively insensitive glycerin stool sedimentation and urine sedimentation. S. haematobium was mainly concentrated in Hajjah, Taiz, and Sana’a, whereas S. mansoni predominated in Ibb and Taiz,45 reinforcing the species-specific and geographic focality that remains relevant to elimination planning. Routine laboratory data from 1980 to 1982 also detected S. mansoni in 6% of more than 37,000 stool specimens and S. haematobium in 2% of 25,000 urine specimens submitted to the Central Public Health Laboratory in Sana’a.49 Collectively, these early observations show that Yemen’s schistosomiasis transmission has long been geographically focal and species-specific, explaining why national prevalence reductions may still mask persistent or resurgent district-level transmission.

Regional Pre- and Post-SCP Epidemiological Landscape

The regional landscape was organized across six phases: pre-SCP studies, 2010 baseline endemicity mapping before SCP, studies during SCP scale-up (2011–2013), 2014 follow-up endemicity mapping after SCP scale-up, studies after SCP scale-up (2014–2017), and post-SCP studies from 2018 to the review end date.

Pre- and Post-SCP Epidemiological Landscape in Southwestern Highlands

The southwestern highlands, including Taiz, Ibb, Al-Dhale, and Lahj governorates, remain priority areas because of their long-standing endemicity, rural population density, seasonal valleys, irrigation-linked exposure, and residual post-MDA transmission overlap.

Pre-SCP, Taiz showed intense mixed-species transmission. S. mansoni was reported among 12% and 32.5% of male outpatient children aged 5–9 and 10–14 years, respectively, in 1973.50 During 1980–1982, rural surveys found S. haematobium among 90% of villagers and 37% of schoolchildren, and S. mansoni among 68% of villagers and 64% of schoolchildren.46 Later school-based studies reported 20.8% S. mansoni in five rural areas during 2007–2009,51 and 24.8% overall prevalence in Shara’b Ar-Rawnah district, comprising 18.6% S. haematobium and 31% S. mansoni, in 2008.52 In 2010 baseline mapping, eight districts were high-endemic, 11 meso-endemic, three low-endemic, and one suspected endemic (Table 1). During SCP scale-up, S. haematobium was detected among 36% of children in Mawza and Al-Ma’afer districts in 2012.24 By 2014, three districts were high-endemic, 14 meso-endemic, and six low-endemic (Table 1). Post-SCP, the prevalence of S. mansoni among schoolchildren in Al-Mawasit and Sabir Al-Mawadim districts was 18%, mostly light-intensity infections, in 2019 and 13% during 2019–2020.53,54

Pre-SCP, Ibb predominantly exhibited intestinal schistosomiasis, although both species were present. In 2000, 37% of schoolchildren in Assahul Valley had schistosomiasis, comprising 35% S. mansoni, 5% S. haematobium, and 3% mixed infections.55 In 2010, six districts were high-endemic, five meso-endemic, three low-endemic, and four suspected endemic (Table 1). During SCP scale-up, 30.1% of children had S. mansoni in the As-Sabrah and Al-Udayn districts in 2012.24 By 2014, all districts were low endemic (Table 1). After SCP scale-up, low-level S. mansoni persisted among schoolchildren, including 3.1% in rural and urban areas in 2015,56 6.5% in An-Nadirah district in 2019, with low-to-moderate intensity,21 5.3% among males and 0.4% among females across six districts in 2020,57 and 1.9% in Hubyash district in 2025. In Hubyash, S. haematobium reached 10.6% in 2025 after no infections were recorded in 2014,16 suggesting the possible localized emergence of urogenital schistosomiasis.

In Al-Dhale, no published studies were found before or during the implementation of SCP. In 2010, two districts were high-endemic, four meso-endemic, two low-endemic, and one suspected endemic. However, by 2014, four districts were meso-endemic and five were low-endemic (Table 1). Post-SCP, schoolchildren in Al Husha district showed overall prevalence increasing from 18.8% in 2014 to 20.5% in 2025, with S. haematobium rising from 3.6% to 11.6%, S. mansoni declining from 17.2% to 11.6%, and mixed infections reaching 2.7%.15 In Lahj, no published studies were retrieved. In 2010, three districts were high-endemic, two meso-endemic, two low-endemic, and eight suspected as endemic. By 2014, 10 districts were low-endemic and five were non-endemic (Table 1), but post-SCP status remains uncertain.

Pre- and Post-SCP Epidemiological Landscape in Central and Northern Highlands

The central and northern highlands, spanning Sana’a City and Governorate, as well as Dhamar, Al-Bayda, Amran, Hajjah, Raymah, Al-Mahweet, and Sa’dah governorates, form a heterogeneous zone shaped by agriculture, seasonal rainfall, irrigation systems, wadis, and localized freshwater habitats.

The wider Sana’a area comprises Sana’a City, the capital of Yemen, where urban and peri-urban settings are less suitable for sustained transmission, and the rural Sana’a governorate, where agriculture, irrigation, and surface water may support focal transmission. Pre-SCP, Sana’a mainly showed rural transmission. In the mid-1980s, S. haematobium and S. mansoni each reached 16.5% among rural children, compared with 1.1% and 3.5% among semi-rural children, respectively.58 By the 1990s, S. mansoni predominated among schoolchildren at 18.8% vs 10.1% for S. haematobium,59 with higher rural than urban prevalence, at 15% and 0.5%, respectively.60 In 2010, nine districts were non-endemic and one low-endemic in the city, while five districts were high-endemic, four meso-endemic, three low-endemic, two non-endemic, and two suspected endemic in the governorate (Table 2). During SCP scale-up, low-level S. mansoni persisted, including 0.3% among outpatients in Sana’a City in 2011 and 2.6% among schoolchildren in three rural districts during 2013–2015.61,62 By 2014, all city districts were low-endemic, while governorate districts were low-endemic or non-endemic (Table 2). Post-SCP, S. mansoni was reported in 1.4% and 33.9% of schoolchildren in Bani Mater and Al-Haimah Al-Dakheliah districts, respectively, mostly as light-intensity infections.17 In Al-Haimah Al-Dakheliah, the prevalence declined from 71% in 2010 to 0.7% in 2014, but then increased to 33.9% in 2018, suggesting possible resurgence.

By 2014, all nine districts were low-endemic (Table 2), suggesting reduced endemicity. Post-SCP, however, a 2017 impact evaluation in At-Tawilah district suggested a possible resurgence after earlier gains, as prevalence declined from 34% in 2010 to 7.5% in 2014, and then increased to 23% in 2017, indicating renewed meso-endemicity.

In Sa’dah, pre-SCP prevalence was high among schoolchildren in 1992, with S. mansoni at 76.3% and S. haematobium at 49%.59 However, after four control campaigns over 5 years, the overall prevalence in Sahar district was 5.6% in 2006, comprising 3.3% S. haematobium and 2.3% S. mansoni.68 In 2010, three districts were high-endemic, three meso-endemic, one low-endemic, one non-endemic, and seven suspected endemic. By 2014, all districts had shifted to low endemicity, except for one non-endemic district (Table 2), which suggests a substantial reduction in endemicity. However, the post-SCP status remains uncertain.

Pre- and Post-SCP Epidemiological Landscape in Central-Northeastern Inlands

The central-northeastern inlands, including Marib and Al-Jawf governorates, are mainly arid to semi-arid, but wadis, dams, irrigation schemes, wells, and other water-contact sites can support the focal transmission of schistosomiasis. In 2010, all districts in both governorates were classified as suspected endemic. By 2014, four districts were low-endemic and 10 were non-endemic in Marib, while one district was meso-endemic, 10 were low-endemic, and one was non-endemic in Al-Jawf (Table 3). However, the post-SCP status remains uncertain.

Pre- and Post-SCP Epidemiological Shifts in Coastal Lowlands

The coastal lowlands form a heterogeneous transmission belt extending from Hodeidah governorate westward to the southern and southeastern governorates, including Abyan, Shabwah, and Hadhramout. Transmission is generally lower than in the highlands, but recent S. haematobium hotspots may have persisted or increased in irrigated and wadi-associated settings.16,19 Earlier southern Yemen data showed a higher prevalence of S. haematobium in lowlands than in highlands among children (29.0% vs 13.3%), whereas S. mansoni was highland-confined at 19.2%, suggesting altitudinal and ecological separation of transmission foci.69

No pre-SCP studies have been conducted in Hodeidah. In 2010, three districts were meso-endemic, three were low-endemic, 12 were non-endemic, and eight were suspected to be endemic (Table 4). Post-SCP school-based data from Bura, where the baseline overall prevalence was 31% in 2010, showed an increase in overall prevalence from11.6% in 2014 to 14.3% in 2025; S. haematobium prevalence increased from 2.8% to 5.7%, and S. mansoni prevalence increased from 9.2% to 10%,15 suggesting persistent focal transmission.

Multi-Governorate Surveys

Multi-governorate surveys provide prevalence estimates to interpret schistosomiasis burden beyond individual districts. In 2003, when control relied largely on school-based targeted chemotherapy, S. haematobium was detected in 21.4% of primary schoolchildren in Abyan and Taiz.72 Later multi-governorate surveys confirmed a persistent focal burden despite repeated MDA. During the SCP scale-up in 2012, a community-based survey across 10 rural districts in five governorates reported 31.8% prevalence among children, comprising 23.8% S. haematobium, 9.3% S. mansoni, and 3.9% mixed infections, with substantial moderate-to-heavy intensity.24 In 2025, school-based surveys in six districts across Al-Dhale, Hodeidah, and Amran reported 21% prevalence, comprising 13.5% S. haematobium, 6% S. mansoni, and 1.4% mixed infections,15 suggesting persistent or possibly resurgent focal transmission compared with 2014 mapping estimates in the same districts.

Regional Pre- and Post-SCP Epidemiological Appraisal and Prospects for Elimination

Regional evidence indicates a mixture of persistent, possibly resurgent, and data-deficient foci, with the current status of many districts remaining uncertain because post-SCP data across several governorates are lacking. Southwestern governorates remain among Yemen’s most important schistosomiasis transmission zones. Although SCP scale-up reduced endemicity from high- and meso-endemic levels in 2010 to predominantly low endemicity by 2014, Taiz and Ibb continued to show persistent residual S. mansoni transmission, while Ibb also showed the possible localized emergence of S. haematobium. Al-Dhale showed persistent mixed-species transmission in Al Husha, with increasing urogenital and declining intestinal schistosomiasis. Lahj remains evidence-limited, but its shift following SCP scale-up toward low- or non-endemic status suggests major control gains that still require surveillance in agricultural and valley-based communities.

Evidence from the central and northern highlands shows that national prevalence reductions may coexist with focal persistence or a possible resurgence at the district level. Several districts shifted from high- or meso-endemicity before the SCP scale-up to low-endemicity after the scale-up, suggesting substantial reductions in endemicity. However, later studies documented persistent or renewed transmission, including the possible resurgence of S. mansoni in selected districts of Sana’a and Dhamar and S. haematobium in Amran. Al-Mahweet also showed renewed meso-endemicity in At-Tawilah after an earlier decline, while Hajjah, Sa’dah, Raymah, and Al-Bayda remained ecologically suitable, but lacked post-SCP data.

In central-northeastern inland governorates, transmission appears to be localized, but it remains epidemiologically important because water resource development can create or amplify transmission foci. The current status remains uncertain because of a lack of post-SCP data, but Marib and Al-Jawf should be considered lower-burden, ecologically receptive settings that require risk-based surveillance around dams, irrigation schemes, agricultural areas, and other water contact sites.

Coastal lowlands should be considered focal, heterogeneous transmission belts rather than uniformly low-risk zones. Although coastal governorates generally exhibit lower and more focal endemicity than the highlands, important urogenital schistosomiasis hotspots persist or show increases in irrigated and wadi-associated settings, as illustrated in Amd district, Hadhramout. Abyan also remains epidemiologically important because earlier surveys have documented S. haematobium transmission, although its current status is uncertain because of a lack of post-SCP data. Shabwah is understudied, but its ecological suitability around wadis and irrigation sites justifies sentinel surveillance.

Across Yemen’s regions, evidence indicates that schistosomiasis elimination cannot rely solely on national-level prevalence estimates or repeated MDA. Instead, elimination requires micro-mapping at sub-district levels, sentinel surveillance in historically endemic or ecologically receptive areas, and threshold-based targeted PC in line with the 2022 WHO guidelines.13 PC should be complemented by focal snail surveillance and control, WASH strengthening, behavior change communication (BCC), and rapid investigation and response to residual or resurgent hotspots. This integrated, geographically targeted strategy is essential to preserve earlier MDA gains, detect focal transmission, and prevent localized infection foci from persisting, expanding, or re-emerging. Understanding the persistence or re-emergence of these foci requires moving beyond prevalence trends alone to examine the ecological, behavioral, infrastructural, and programmatic determinants that continue to sustain transmission after PC. Across evidence-limited governorates, including Aden, Lahj, Al-Bayda, Raymah, Al-Jawf, Shabwah, Al-Mahrah, and Socotra, the lack of post-SCP evidence constrains the assessment of current transmission, necessitating risk-based surveillance in previously endemic and ecologically receptive areas.

Determinants of Schistosomiasis Transmission

Historical and Environmental Determinants of Transmission

The regional patterns described above highlight a central elimination challenge in that PZQ can reduce infection prevalence, but transmission may persist where environmental exposure, snail habitats, inadequate sanitation, and frequent water contact remain unchanged. Although early reports on schistosomiasis transmission in Yemen were largely descriptive, they provided important contextual evidence of conditions that historically sustained transmission. Studies from the 1950s linked transmission to poor sanitation, wastewater contamination of nearby water sources, uncovered water conduits, and limited diagnostic and treatment capacity.32,44 Malacological reports consistently identified localized freshwater habitats, including streams, cisterns, pools, drainage canals, ablution chambers, mosque basins, irrigation canals, and village washing sites, as key transmission environments.32,34,35,37,44,45 These habitats were particularly suitable when water was slow-flowing or stagnant, with sandy substrates and aquatic vegetation that supported snail survival and breeding.32 Exposure was further amplified by inadequate WASH services and repeated domestic, agricultural, recreational, and ritual water contact. In communities without piped water, reliance on streams, pools, cisterns, reservoirs, and other open water sources increased contact with potentially infested water.32 In Taiz, childhood infections were linked to swimming and playing in nearby streams and pools,44 while mosque ablution basins contained Biomphalaria snails infected with S. mansoni,35 suggesting that recreational exposure and communal ritual washing could sustain local transmission. Early 1970s surveys around major cities similarly supported rural transmission driven by agricultural and domestic water contact, with high exposure among adolescents and young adults.45 In the 1980s, domestic, agricultural, and recreational contact with snail-infested water was found to sustain high infection incidence among children in Taiz.46 Human mobility probably also contributed to parasite introduction and spread in ecologically receptive areas. In the early 1950s, infections in Hodeidah occurred mainly among recent arrivals from Eritrea or other Yemeni regions,44 while seasonal migration of cotton pickers from Zabid on the western coast to Abyan on the southern coast may have introduced S. haematobium strains compatible with local snail hosts.34 Overall, historical evidence indicates that schistosomiasis transmission in Yemen was sustained by the convergence of poor sanitation, localized snail-supporting freshwater habitats, repeated water exposure, rainfall-driven habitat expansion, and population mobility, all of which remain relevant to elimination planning.

Yemen is among the countries most vulnerable to climate change, while possessing limited institutional, financial, and infrastructural capacity to mitigate its effects or adapt effectively.73 Climate variability and extreme weather events may contribute to persistent or resurgent schistosomiasis transmission in Yemen. Variations in rainfall and temperature can alter the seasonality, abundance, and distribution of snail habitats, while flooding can create or redistribute suitable snail habitats and increase human contact with contaminated water. Prolonged drought may likewise concentrate communities around scarce water sources. In Yemen, recurrent floods have damaged water and sanitation infrastructure, contaminated water supplies, displaced populations, and disrupted access to essential services, potentially amplifying exposure in vulnerable rural communities. These combined effects are particularly important in conflict-affected and underserved rural areas, where adaptive capacity and access to essential services are limited. Despite these risks, the relationship between climate variability and schistosomiasis transmission has not been systematically investigated in Yemen. Climate-sensitive surveillance that integrates meteorological, hydrological, malacological, and epidemiological data is therefore warranted. Incorporating climate projections into surveillance and planning could help identify emerging risk areas and guide timely, locally targeted interventions.74

Schistosomiasis Risk Factors

Since the 1990s, studies have analytically assessed the sociodemographic, behavioral, and environmental risk factors for schistosomiasis in Yemen. Among sociodemographic factors, male sex has been significantly associated with schistosomiasis among children across several governorates,15,18,19,53,55,67,70 and some studies have identified it as an independent predictor. Age ≥10 years has also independently predicted infection among schoolchildren in several settings.15,18,19,24 These associations are consistent with the greater recreational, domestic, and livelihood-related contact with open water among older boys engaged in agricultural activities, water collection, washing, and other tasks that require repeated contact with streams, canals, ponds, or irrigation systems. Other sociodemographic and household-level determinants, including rural residence, larger household size, and low household income, have also been identified as independent predictors.15,24,67 Behavioral and occupational exposure have been consistently implicated in rural communities. Frequent water exposure was significantly associated with infection among children in Al-Mahweet, Ibb, and Abyan,55,67,70 and more recent studies identified high-risk water-contact behaviors as independent predictors, including swimming in ponds, streams, valleys, or other open water sources, water-related activities, and open defecation.18,21,54,70 Environmental and WASH-related factors further modify the risk of infection. Latrine non-use, residence near water bodies or valleys, unsafe water access, absence of household tap water, and ownership of agricultural land have been associated with infection across different settings, with some of these factors identified as independent predictors.17,20,52,55,67,70 Together, these findings indicate that schistosomiasis risk in Yemen is driven by the interaction of poverty, age- and sex-patterned exposure, unsafe domestic water access, inadequate sanitation, proximity to transmission sites, and agricultural livelihoods. Therefore, control strategies should extend beyond PC to include health education, BCC, improved WASH infrastructure, and locally tailored measures to reduce high-risk water contact.

Population mobility likely contributes to the persistence and spread of schistosomiasis. However, its role in schistosomiasis epidemiology has not been formally assessed as an independent risk factor in recent studies from Yemen. Seasonal agricultural migration, internal displacement, rural-to-urban movement, and cross-border migration can relocate untreated infections to areas where transmission is possible, introducing or re-establishing local transmission. A historical report from Yemen has suggested the role of seasonal migration of cotton pickers from the western coast in introducing S. haematobium to Abyan on the southern coast,34 and more recent molecular evidence suggests parasite exchange across Yemeni governorates through movement.38 Following the escalation of armed conflict and humanitarian crisis since March 2015, Yemen experienced an unprecedented increase in internal displacement and population movement. This substantial movement of people may have influenced the spatial distribution and persistence of schistosomiasis. Displaced and mobile populations often lack adequate WASH services and may be missed by MDA campaigns and surveillance, sustaining untreated infection reservoirs. A recent systematic review reported that conflict, displacement, inadequate sanitation, unsafe water, and deterioration of healthcare infrastructure have increased vulnerability to NTDs in Yemen.30 Furthermore, a recent study has also showed that schistosomiasis transmission persists in conflict-affected districts, with prevalence increasing in some areas compared with the 2014 national mapping.15 Elimination strategies should therefore include mobility-sensitive micro-mapping and ensure that migrants, internally displaced persons (IDPs), and other mobile groups are included in PC, diagnostic services, and surveillance near settlements and water contact sites in receptive areas.

Schistosomiasis Morbidity

Persistent focal transmission in Yemen remains clinically important because recurrent or chronic infections may continue to cause intestinal, hepatosplenic, urogenital, and occasionally severe ectopic morbidities or other complications. Although morbidity data are scattered across surveys, hospital reports, migrant studies, and case reports, elimination planning should integrate transmission control with clinical awareness, case detection, and morbidity management in persistent hotspots.

S. mansoni Infection-Associated Morbidity

Acute morbidity has been documented among newly exposed individuals. Katayama syndrome was confirmed serologically and histopathologically in two Saudi schoolboys exposed in Yemen, who presented with systemic and gastrointestinal symptoms, marked eosinophilia, and sigmoidoscopic evidence of acute bilharzial colitis.75 Acute schistosomiasis should therefore be considered in newly exposed individuals with compatible symptoms, as routine parasitological tests may be insensitive during early infection. Chronic infection can persist for years and sustain egg deposition, granulomatous inflammation, and intestinal or hepatosplenic damage, even after migration or local transmission declines.39,76,77

In endemic Yemeni settings, hepatosplenic morbidity appears particularly important, with S. mansoni identified as a contributor to hepatosplenic disease in rural populations, particularly among children in intense-transmission areas.78 In Taiz, early evidence from 1951 showed hepatomegaly in 38.2% and splenomegaly in 60.6% of infected individuals aged <21 years,79 and hepatosplenomegaly was later reported in 40–60% of heavily infected schoolchildren.46 In Ibb, hepatomegaly and splenomegaly were reported in 31% and 5% of schoolchildren, respectively, from villages along Assahul Valley.67 A 2012 survey across five endemic governorates further showed that hepatosplenomegaly was significantly associated with higher S. mansoni infection intensity among children,24 indicating an intensity‑dependent hepatosplenic burden.

Intestinal morbidity has also been reported in several settings, with bloody stool occurring in 33% of S. mansoni-infected schoolchildren in Ibb, and S. mansoni detected in 43% of pediatric bloody-stool cases in Hajjah during 1997–1999.67,80 Similarly, a 1992 survey in Sana’a, Hajjah, and Sa’dah found that 61.7% and 33.3% of S. mansoni-infected children had bloody stool and diarrhea, respectively, although most infections were light.59 Collectively, these findings suggest that intestinal schistosomiasis contributes to both subclinical organ enlargement and overt clinical morbidity in children, even in settings where many infections are light or moderate.

S. mansoni Infection-Associated Nutritional Outcomes

Chronic intestinal schistosomiasis can impair nutrition through intestinal blood loss, inflammation, anorexia, impaired nutrient absorption, anemia, and adverse effects on child growth.81 In Yemen, evidence linking S. mansoni to nutritional outcomes remains limited but suggests a possible association with chronic undernutrition. A 2018 school-based study in rural Sana’a found high levels of stunting (45.8%), wasting (25.0%), and underweight (27.3%), but S. mansoni infection was independently associated with stunting only, suggesting a stronger link with chronic growth impairment than acute malnutrition.17 In contrast, earlier work in Sahar district, Sa’dah, found low post-control schistosomiasis prevalence, including 2.3% S. mansoni, and reported no statistically significant association between infection and nutritional status,68 suggesting limited nutritional impact of S. mansoni in low-prevalence, low-intensity settings. Although anemia is common among children in schistosomiasis-endemic settings in Yemen, its direct association with infection remains unclear. The 2014 nationwide mapping survey found anemia among 30.4% of schoolchildren, with moderate-to-severe public health significance in 62.6% of districts,22 but it did not assess its association with schistosomiasis. In 2012, anemia was prevalent among 39.5% of children from five endemic governorates but was not significantly associated with schistosomiasis,24 a finding also reported among schoolchildren in rural Sana’a in 2018.17 Overall, the nutritional and hematological effects of schistosomiasis in Yemen are difficult to separate from poverty, food insecurity, recurrent infections, other parasitic diseases, and limited healthcare access.

Severe and Atypical Manifestations of S. mansoni Infection

Although S. mansoni infections can be subclinical or mildly symptomatic in endemic areas, rare severe and atypical complications have been documented in Yemen. These cases broaden diagnostic awareness, reminding clinicians to consider schistosomiasis in unexplained gastrointestinal, pulmonary, neurological, or multisystem diseases with a relevant exposure history.

Chronic intestinal S. mansoni infection may produce tumor-like colorectal lesions, as reported in a 20-year-old Yemeni man with tumor-like sigmoid-colon lesions, two 24- and 28-year-old men with rectal bleeding from neoplasia-like colorectal polyps, and a 10-year-old boy who underwent sigmoidectomy for a malignancy-suspected sigmoid mass.82–85 Rare acute abdominal emergencies have also been reported, including small bowel obstruction mimicking abdominal tuberculosis in a 50-year-old man and perforated appendicitis with diffuse peritonitis in a 17-year-old child.86,87

Pulmonary schistosomiasis was documented histopathologically in two 23-year-old Yemeni men, including one with a cavitary lung lesion mimicking tuberculosis and another with secondary spontaneous pneumothorax requiring surgery.88,89 Severe ectopic neurological diseases have also been described, including spinal cord schistosomiasis mimicking a tumor in a Yemeni child, 10 pediatric spinal neuroschistosomiasis cases in Ibb during 2016–2020, mostly among rural boys with myeloradicular manifestations, and a mass-like neuroschistosomiasis lesion in a Korean patient previously exposed in Yemen.90–92

S. haematobium Infection-Associated Morbidity

In Yemen, S. haematobium morbidity varies across endemic settings, from asymptomatic infection to overt urogenital morbidity, mainly hematuria and severe complications. In Amran, during the late 1980s, macrohematuria was not observed in infected schoolchildren despite limited control activities.47 In contrast, a 1992 survey in Sana’a, Hajjah, and Sa’dah documented more overt morbidity, possibly reflecting heavier infections, with hematuria and dysuria reported in approximately two-thirds and one-third of infected schoolchildren, respectively.59 More recent post-MDA studies show that even predominantly light-intensity infections are significantly associated with microhematuria, proteinuria, and dysuria,15,18–20 suggesting that residual infections after repeated MDA may still cause subclinical or mild morbidity detectable through reagent-strip testing or symptom-based screening. These findings indicate that residual urogenital schistosomiasis after repeated MDA can still cause detectable urinary morbidity, and should not be dismissed as clinically irrelevant.

Severe Morbidity and Atypical Manifestations of S. haematobium Infection

Severe obstructive and malignant S. haematobium complications have also been documented in Yemeni patients, including ureteric strictures, hydronephrosis, bladder calcification, renal failure, and malignancy.40,93 Although severe complications are often linked to chronic or heavy-intensity infections, light-intensity infections may still contribute to urinary tract morbidity,94,95 reinforcing the need for early detection and treatment. Ultrasonography documented substantial urinary tract damage in Yemeni patients with urogenital schistosomiasis, and one study reported bladder lesions in 82% of patients and upper urinary tract abnormalities in 54% of patients with bladder lesions, with severity strongly associated with infection intensity.96

Urogenital schistosomiasis has also been recognized as an important cause of acute and chronic obstructive renal disease in Yemen.97–99 Bladder neck obstruction (BNO), although rare and often misdiagnosed, was reported in the late 1990s among eight young Yemeni men with previous or active bilharzial infection, often after treatment for presumed chronic prostatitis.100 The renal burden is further illustrated by a referral-hospital study in Aden that identified 88 renal failure cases from BNO secondary to bilharzial cystitis during 1995–2000,97 with cystoscopy showing bladder-neck hypertrophy in all cases, with frequent calcification and fibrosis.97 Additional referral-hospital data attributed 4.8% of chronic renal failure cases in Hajjah during 1998–2000 and 3.5% of cases in Sana’a during 2013–2015 to S. haematobium.98,99

Chronic urogenital schistosomiasis can involve the genital tract but may be overlooked because it mimics other urological, reproductive, or cutaneous disorders. Testicular schistosomiasis due to S. haematobium was histopathologically confirmed in a 15-year-old Yemeni boy with acute epididymo-orchitis unresponsive to antibiotics, later complicated by testicular torsion requiring orchiectomy.101 Additional reports described intra-testicular schistosomiasis in a 30-year-old man with secondary infertility102 and infertility-associated genital schistosomiasis, in which semen contained S. haematobium eggs despite negative urine findings, and sperm abnormalities resolved after PZQ therapy.103 Atypical vulval cutaneous schistosomiasis was reported in a 9-year-old Yemeni girl with chronic dysuria and an ulcerated vulval nodule, with histopathology confirming S. haematobium despite negative urine and stool examinations.104 Together, these reports show that genital schistosomiasis may cause testicular damage, fertility impairment, and atypical vulval lesions even without typical urinary findings, supporting its consideration in patients from endemic settings with genital lesions, reproductive symptoms, or unexplained urinary complaints. In Sana’a, S. haematobium infection was found in 59% of squamous cell carcinoma cases in a series of 316 bladder cancer cases,105 supporting the relevance of S. haematobium-associated bladder cancer as a severe, chronic complication in endemic settings.

Studies on Schistosomiasis Co-infection with Hepatitis

Co-infection with Schistosoma species and hepatotropic viruses, particularly hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV), can worsen liver disease beyond either infection alone.106 A recent meta-analysis found higher odds of liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma among intestinal schistosome-HBV/HCV co-infected individuals, especially those with HBV co-infection.107 This issue is clinically relevant in Yemen because overlapping hepatic pathology may complicate the attribution of hepatosplenic morbidity to schistosomiasis alone. However, data from Yemen are insufficient to determine the clinical impact of active Schistosoma-HBV/HCV co-infection on hepatic morbidity. Among schoolchildren in Taiz, S. mansoni-HBsAg co-infection was lower than HBsAg positivity alone, at 4% vs 10%, whereas S. haematobium-HBsAg co-infection was higher (17% vs 6%), and anti-HCV was uncommon in both species.51 However, the study was limited to children, did not confirm active HBV/HCV infection, and did not assess hepatic morbidity. A hospital-based case-control study in Sana’a found that a history of schistosomiasis significantly doubled the odds of total anti-hepatitis core antibody (HBcAb) seropositivity.108 Nevertheless, total HBcAb seropositivity reflects HBV exposure but not necessarily active infection. Future studies should therefore integrate community- and hospital-based designs, markers of active hepatitis, and standardized hepatic assessment to clarify co-infection burden, morbidity attribution, clinical impact, and control needs.

Schistosomiasis Diagnosis: Pitfalls and Elimination Prospects

As Yemen moves from morbidity control to elimination, diagnostic sensitivity becomes increasingly important because conventional microscopy, although useful for mapping and routine diagnosis, may miss low-intensity infections after repeated MDA. This limitation affects prevalence estimates and decisions regarding whether districts are non-endemic, controlled, or still experiencing residual transmission.

Routine Laboratory Diagnosis

In Yemen, routine diagnosis in public and private laboratories relies on microscopy. S. mansoni eggs are usually detected in stool sediment using simple saline sedimentation, with formol-ether rarely used, whereas S. haematobium is diagnosed by identifying eggs in centrifuged urine sediment. However, the more sensitive methods of Kato-Katz for S. mansoni and urine filtration for S. haematobium are mainly used by the NSCP for surveys. Although inexpensive and feasible in peripheral laboratories, microscopy has limited sensitivity in low-intensity infections and post-MDA settings.13 In this context, among children from five Yemeni governorates, microscopy detected S. mansoni and S. haematobium in 13.5% and 14%, respectively, whereas PCR detected 16.1% and 16.7%.38 Although modest, this difference may be programmatically important for test-and-treat strategies below the 10% prevalence threshold, as recommended in the 2022 WHO guidelines.13 This strategy may suit many post-MDA settings in Yemen; however, implementation is likely to be constrained by diagnostic capacity, logistics, and surveillance limitations.

Reagent-Strip Microhematuria Screening for S. haematobium Infection

For S. haematobium, urine reagent strips for microhematuria offer a rapid, low-cost field screening tool, especially for school surveys and post-intervention monitoring.109,110 Nevertheless, they may miss light-intensity infections and produce false positives from non-schistosomal hematuria, making them more suitable for initial screening within tiered surveillance rather than as stand-alone confirmation. A meta-analysis of 95 surveys estimated a pooled sensitivity of 81% and specificity of 89% for reagent-strip microhematuria compared with egg-count microscopy.109 In Yemen, reagent-strip microhematuria showed variable performance in detecting S. haematobium infection. In a school-based survey in Amran, urine reagent strips showed limited accuracy compared with urine filtration microscopy among schoolchildren, with a sensitivity of 57%, a specificity of 52.9%, a positive predictive value (PPV) of 68.3%, and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 40.9%.64 However, among schoolchildren in Abyan and Taiz, reagent-strip microhematuria outperformed self-reported hematuria, with 89.9% versus 79.2% diagnostic accuracy, and combined use increased accuracy to 93.4%, with reagent-strip positivity increasing significantly with infection intensity.72 A study from southern Yemen further supports their operational value for rapid screening of S. haematobium infection in endemic communities.111 Evidence that microhematuria can significantly decline after effective PZQ treatment12 supports its use as an indirect indicator of reduced urogenital schistosomiasis burden, although it should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of parasitological cure. In Yemen, repeated pre- and post-MDA reagent-strip surveys could help assess program performance, where conflict, weak diagnostic infrastructure, and poor access limit microscopy. However, reagent strips should complement, not replace, confirmatory testing within tiered surveillance, followed by microscopy or molecular testing, where feasible.

Yemeni S. haematobium Strain Antigens as Immunodiagnostic Targets

Immunodiagnostic research on Yemeni Schistosoma strains remains limited. One study characterized adult Yemeni S. haematobium membrane antigens by immunoblotting and found that patient sera consistently recognized a 19–21 kDa antigen complex, without cross-reactivity with S. mansoni, S. japonicum, and S. mekongi sera.112 These findings suggest potential local immunodiagnostic targets for urogenital schistosomiasis, although further validation is needed.

Molecular Diagnosis and Surveillance in the Context of Elimination

Molecular diagnostics are increasingly relevant to elimination because nucleic acid amplification tests offer higher sensitivity than microscopy and higher specificity than immunological methods for low-intensity infections.13,113 In Yemen, a real-time PCR assay targeting mitochondrial cox1 differentiated S. mansoni from S. haematobium, detected very low Schistosoma DNA concentrations, showed 100% sensitivity versus microscopy, and increased prevalence estimates from 31.8% to 33.3% by detecting microscopy-negative infections.114 Molecular testing could strengthen intervention impact monitoring, confirm suspected low-intensity infections, and support surveillance in persistent or re-emerging foci. However, in Yemen, routine molecular surveillance is constrained by cost, infrastructure, reagent supply, electricity, and technical expertise. Therefore, PCR is more realistic for reference laboratories and sentinel surveillance than for routine field diagnosis. Environmental DNA (eDNA) surveillance is a promising approach for monitoring schistosomiasis transmission by detecting parasites or snail DNA in environmental samples,115 particularly in conflict-affected areas where surveys are difficult. However, eDNA should be viewed as a future complementary surveillance tool rather than an immediate replacement for microscopy or snail surveys in Yemen because its programmatic use requires protocol standardization and technical training.116 Overall, diagnostic strengthening is essential for detecting residual transmission, assessing treatment response, monitoring PZQ efficacy, evaluating intervention impact, and guiding elimination-oriented interventions.

Monitoring PZQ Efficacy: Evidence Gaps

The introduction of PZQ as a free treatment in Yemen in 1983117 marked an important transition in chemotherapy strategies, from case-based treatment toward school-based targeted PC and, later, community-wide MDA. PZQ efficacy monitoring is therefore critical, particularly in settings where transmission persists despite apparently satisfactory MDA coverage.118 The efficacy may vary by parasite species, infection intensity, diagnostic method, timing of follow-up, transmission setting, and previous MDA exposure,119–122 whereas extensive PZQ use has raised concerns about reduced susceptibility or resistance, particularly where laboratory evidence or suboptimal cure rates (CRs) have been reported elsewhere.123–126

Early evidence from Yemen suggests species-specific differences in the impact of PZQ. In Al-Mahweet, annual PZQ treatment in SAC reduced S. mansoni prevalence by 62.5% and lowered infection intensity after 12 months, supporting the value of MDA for intestinal schistosomiasis. In contrast, S. haematobium prevalence and intensity returned to baseline, suggesting rapid reinfection.67 This pattern aligns with a 2022 systematic review reporting S. haematobium reinfection rates of 8–40% within approximately 1-month post-treatment.127 Accordingly, annual MDA may reduce S. mansoni burden but may be insufficient alone for sustained S. haematobium control where exposure persists, and more frequent or higher-dose PZQ regimens may be effective, as shown elsewhere.128 This species-specific limitation observed in Al-Mahweet underscores the need to integrate PC with complementary interventions.

Evidence from another Yemeni setting further showed that PC with PZQ can substantially reduce S. haematobium prevalence and morbidity, particularly when combined with health education. In Amran, a 1997–2001 program combining mass PZQ with culturally tailored weekly health education reduced S. haematobium prevalence from 58.9% to 5.8% community-wide and from 66.9% to 4.3% among schoolchildren.64 Heavy-intensity infections also declined from 40% to 18.9% in the community and from 38% to 14.3% among schoolchildren.64 In Abyan in 2011, single-dose PZQ reduced S. haematobium prevalence among schoolchildren by 51.4% at 3 months and 84.5% at 6 months and eliminated high-intensity infections.70 However, short-term morbidity reduction does not necessarily indicate transmission interruption, particularly if unsafe water contact, environmental contamination, and snail habitats persist.

In Hajjah, treatment-response data highlight the importance of the systematic monitoring of PZQ performance. A 2008–2009 study of microscopy-confirmed infected schoolchildren found that PZQ 40 mg/kg achieved a cure rate of 75.7%, while non-responders were cured after retreatment with 60 mg/kg.66 However, the findings should be interpreted in light of the study’s limited scope and design, including its single-district setting, relatively small sample size, absence of species-specific or infection-intensity-stratified efficacy estimates, and reliance on CR as the sole outcome indicator. Therefore, the low initial cure rate should be interpreted cautiously and should not, by itself, be considered evidence of PZQ resistance. This cautious interpretation aligns with WHO guidelines, which identify the egg reduction rate (ERR) as the preferred efficacy indicator, with an ERR ≥90% considered the reference efficacy.118 WHO recommends PZQ efficacy assessment in areas with high PC coverage for more than 5 years.129 However, no post-MDA efficacy studies have been implemented in Yemen, leaving an important evidence gap in assessing treatment response, reinfection, and possibly reduced drug effectiveness.

Schistosomiasis Control: From Localized Interventions to Nationwide MDA

Schistosomiasis control in Yemen has evolved from fragmented case management and limited subnational activities to school-based chemotherapy and, subsequently, nationwide school- and community-based MDA. However, persistent and possibly resurgent foci show that PC alone cannot achieve elimination unless ecological, behavioral, WASH, and health system drivers are addressed. Although the 2012 WHO roadmap set an ambitious target for eliminating schistosomiasis from WHO EMR countries by 2015,130 transmission has persisted in Yemen. The central challenge is to sustain adaptive, multi-sectoral interventions in a fragile setting affected by conflict, displacement, weak WASH infrastructure, financing gaps, and interrupted surveillance.

School-Based Chemotherapy Before Nationwide MDA

In 2002, the NSCP began to control schistosomiasis among schoolchildren in highly endemic districts. This early strategy relied on parasitological screening followed by PZQ treatment of infected children.131 Although prevalence exceeded 50% in some schools, selective test-and-treat delivery continued until 2007.131 Its limited scale made broad transmission control unlikely; however, it provided programmatic experience and helped prepare the transition from selective chemotherapy to wider PC. Before 2008, control remained fragmented, irregular, and largely case-based, relying on health facilities, private pharmacies, and limited school screening and treatment in highly endemic areas. A major shift occurred in 2008, when the NSCP implemented the first nationwide school-based MDA campaign, delivering PZQ to >2 million children in six governorates.132 To guide scale-up, survey data from 263 endemic districts were compiled as baseline prevalence data, yielding an average overall schistosomiasis prevalence of 19%.131 Building on this initiative, the World Bank supported a six-year nationwide SCP during 2010–2015.131

In December 2009, Yemen piloted community-wide MDA across 10 highly endemic districts in Sana’a, Dhamar, and Hajjah. Delivery used schools for enrolled children, mobile and temporary community sites for non-enrolled children and adults, and routine health facilities for missed or reinfected individuals, supported by training and social mobilization.133 This pilot MDA achieved 83% overall coverage, including 94.8% among children and 73.9% among adults, with comparable coverage by sex.133 This experience showed that community-wide delivery was feasible and helped inform the subsequent nationwide MDA scale-up.

Schistosomiasis Control Project, 2010–2017

Following the successful 2009 pilot MDA,133 schistosomiasis control expanded beyond schoolchildren to include non-enrolled children aged ≥6 years and adults who could contribute to ongoing transmission.14,131 Major progress followed SCP launch and scale-up during 2010–2015, later extended to 2017 due to conflict-related disruptions, through 13 school- and community-based MDA campaigns.14,131 In addition, routine PC was implemented in fixed health facilities and routine outreach services in all endemic districts to treat people suffering from, or suspected to suffer from, schistosomiasis.14,131 The project aimed to reduce overall prevalence to <10% and heavy-intensity infections to <1% among SAC in all endemic or suspected endemic districts by 2015.14,131,133

Baseline Prevalence Mapping and MDA Planning

Before the SCP launch, baseline prevalence mapping was conducted to stratify districts by endemicity level and guide MDA scheduling. In 2010, small-scale surveys covering 120 schools in previously unmapped areas across nine governorates were combined with 2002–2010 mapping surveys and ecological indicators related to schistosomiasis transmission to produce a prevalence risk map.27 Districts were stratified as low-endemic when prevalence was <10%, meso-endemic at 10–39%, high-endemic at ≥40%, and suspected-endemic when unsurveyed but with cases reported through the national surveillance system.27 Of all 333 districts, 275 were classified as endemic and 58 as non-endemic.14 The endemic districts included 51 high-endemic, 79 meso-endemic, 35 low-endemic, and 110 suspected endemic.14 MDA schedules were then tailored by endemicity. Briefly, high-endemic districts were assigned annual school-based MDA throughout years 1–6, supplemented by community-based campaigns in years 1, 2, and 4.14 Meso-endemic and suspected endemic districts received four school-based campaigns in years 1, 2, 4, and 6 plus a community-based campaign in the first year, whereas low-endemic districts received school-based campaigns in years 2 and 5.14

SCP Scale-Up During 2010–2013: Substantial Progress

During the first 3 years, SCP implementation expanded substantially, with approximately 10 million people receiving >18 million PZQ doses during 2010–2013.27 In December 2010, an MDA campaign across 37 districts targeted 2.7 million people, including 1.2 million SAC, achieving 77.6% overall and 85% SAC coverage.27 In 2011, another campaign across 44 districts targeted 2.6 million people, including 1.2 million SAC, achieving 71.1% overall and 78.3% SAC coverage.27 Implementation was interrupted from April 2011 to May 2012 because of political unrest during the Arab Spring. In 2012, two campaigns across 58 districts targeted >3 million people, including about 1.2 million SAC, achieving 70.5–77.1% overall and 76.7–90.2% SAC coverage.27 In 2013, scale-up expanded to 263 districts in 20 governorates, treating 9.6 million people across two campaigns with 74.5% coverage, including 5.4 million SAC with 84.3% coverage, followed by a third campaign treating 1.47 million people in 51 districts, achieving 70% overall and 87% SAC coverage.27 Overall, coverage generally remained above 70%, with consistently higher uptake among SAC, often meeting or approaching the WHO adequate-coverage threshold of ≥75%.13 Moreover, geographic coverage of MDA campaigns was achieved in all 275 endemic or suspected endemic districts.14 Nevertheless, during SCP scale-up, a five-governorate survey found 31.8% of children infected, with heavy-intensity infections comprising 22.1% of S. haematobium and 40.5% of S. mansoni cases,24 suggesting substantial residual transmission despite ongoing MDA.

Nationwide Follow-Up Mapping in 2014: Endemicity Contraction and Low Burden

After the SCP scale-up, nationwide follow-up mapping was conducted from February to May 2014 to assess the decline in prevalence and intensity after repeated MDA, update district-level endemicity, and guide the remaining project period. More than 80,000 schoolchildren aged 10–14 years were surveyed from 2600 schools across 332 districts, with Wald Rabi’ district in Al-Bayda excluded due to insecurity, with the high-endemicity threshold reduced to 30%.22,27 Compared with the 2010 baseline, the 2014 mapping showed a marked contraction in endemicity, with endemic districts decreasing by 18.9%, from 275 to 223. High-endemic districts decreased by 94.1% to three, and meso-endemic districts by 57.0% to 34, whereas low-endemic districts increased by 431.4% to 186, reflecting a marked downward shift in endemicity, although 37 districts remained above the WHO treatment threshold.14

Post-2014 MDA and SCP Extension, 2014–2017

The SCP was initially expected to achieve its objectives by 2015 and close in June 2016,14 in line with WHO NTD targets for eliminating schistosomiasis as a public health problem by 2015.130 However, escalation of conflict in March 2015 disrupted implementation and suspended disbursements, leading to an extension of the closing date to January 2018.14 According to NSCP data, six MDA campaigns were implemented during 2014–2017: one in December 2014 across 78 districts in 14 governorates, achieving 77% coverage among SAC; one in January 2015 across 143 districts in 19 governorates, achieving 89% coverage among SAC; two in April and May 2016 in 57 districts across 13 governorates, achieving coverage of 85% among SAC; and two in January and April 2017 in 76 districts across 25 governorates, achieving coverage of 87% and 89% among SAC, respectively.

SCP Impact Evaluation: Promising Transition Toward Elimination

Independent impact evaluations monitored temporal changes in prevalence and infection intensity during SCP implementation at 36 sentinel sites representing different endemicity levels.27 Each evaluation included 8000 participants, comprising 6500 SAC and 1500 adults.14,27 To measure the impact of SCP, evaluations were conducted at baseline from December 2010 to December 2012, midline in November 2014, and endline in April 2017. These evaluations measured overall schistosomiasis prevalence, with a target of <10%; prevalence of heavy-intensity S. haematobium infections, with a target of 1%; and prevalence of heavy- and moderate-intensity S. mansoni infection, with a target of 2.5%.14 In addition, an impact evaluation was conducted in 2024 to monitor the impact of post-SCP MDA campaigns conducted during 2018–2022.29

According to SCP impact evaluation estimates, the objective of reducing the overall prevalence of SAC to <10% was achieved in 2017, as the overall prevalence declined from 16.7% at baseline to 7.5% at midline and 6.9% at endline, while SAC prevalence fell from 17.3% to 9.5% and 7.4%, respectively. Species-specific prevalence also declined by 2017 compared with baseline, from 8.9% to 3.6% overall and from 9.4% to 4.0% among SAC for S. haematobium, and from 9.2% to 3.6% overall and from 9.4% to 3.9% among SAC for S. mansoni.14 For infection intensity, heavy-intensity S. haematobium infections among SAC declined by approximately 58%, from 3.1% at baseline to 0.8% in 2014 and 1.3% in 2017, approaching the 1.0% target. Heavy-intensity S. mansoni infections declined by approximately 57%, from 0.7% at baseline to 0.4% in 2014 and 0.3% in 2017.14 The prevalence of moderate- and heavy-intensity S. mansoni infections, reported only for the overall population, declined by 75.6%, from 4.1% in 2014 to 1.0% in 2017. The decline in heavy-intensity infections below 1% meets the threshold for the WHO’s 2021–2030 NTD roadmap to eliminate schistosomiasis as a public health problem.134 However, Weigand et al135 argued that the <1% heavy-intensity threshold is inadequate, as it may overlook subtle morbidity and residual transmission.

Post‑SCP MDA and Impact Evaluation, 2018–2022: Setbacks After Earlier Gains

After the SCP ended in 2017, the NSCP continued WHO-supported MDA campaigns during 2018–2022, although implementation was partly disrupted during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) period. Based on NSCP data, five post-SCP campaigns were conducted: April 2018 in 67 districts across 13 governorates, achieving 88% SAC coverage; December 2019 in 98 districts across 11 governorates, achieving 90% SAC coverage; January and April 2021 in 67 districts across 14 governorates, achieving 85% and 71% SAC coverage, respectively; and May 2022 in 40 districts across nine governorates, achieving 82% SAC coverage.

In 2024, a 32-district impact evaluation documented persistent focal schistosomiasis and possible resurgence despite continued post-SCP MDA during 2018–2022.29 Overall prevalence increased from 6.9% in 2017 to 14.9% in 2024, comprising 9.7% S. haematobium and 6.3% S. mansoni. It showed marked spatial heterogeneity, peaking at 31.5% overall in Sana’a, 15.2% for S. mansoni in Taiz, and 27% for S. haematobium in Hadhramout. At the district level, the prevalence was ≥10% in 16 districts, <10% overall with ≥10% village-level foci in eight districts, and <10% in seven districts, while one district showed zero prevalence. Among SAC, the prevalence increased from 7.4% in 2017 to 20.4% in 2024, driven mainly by an increase in S. haematobium infections from 3.8% to 13.5%, while S. mansoni prevalence increased only slightly from 6.1% to 6.9%.29 This pattern suggests that possible post-MDA resurgence was driven predominantly by urogenital schistosomiasis. However, heavy-intensity infections remained relatively uncommon, at 0.4% for S. mansoni and 1.4% for S. haematobium, suggesting that MDA may have continued to limit severe morbidity even as the prevalence rebounded. Overall, these findings indicate fragile and uneven post-SCP gains and underscore the need for updated risk-mapping, species-specific surveillance, and hotspot-focused interventions.

Schistosomiasis Elimination Challenges

Although repeated MDA campaigns had reduced the schistosomiasis burden in endemic districts by 2017,14 elimination in Yemen is still hampered by persistent and possibly resurgent foci that sustain transmission.15,17–21,23 Since 2015, conflict and economic collapse have damaged infrastructure, disrupted basic services, and driven severe humanitarian and displacement crises, making Yemen’s emergency context a major barrier to elimination, despite intensified control efforts. The transition from morbidity control to elimination is constrained by interacting challenges, which include the following.

Gaps in MDA Continuity, Coverage, and Access

Following the completion of the SCP in 2018, MDA campaigns became irregular or interrupted, with additional disruption during the COVID-19 period. Prolonged conflict and humanitarian crises have disrupted MDA through insecurity, displacement, school closures, damaged infrastructure, logistical constraints, fragmented health services, and unreliable population estimates. Moreover, the SCP targeted individuals aged ≥6 years, excluding preschool-aged children aged 2–5 years, who may still be exposed to infection and contribute to ongoing transmission, although their relative contribution to transmission in Yemen remains insufficiently quantified. This exclusion may have limited the population-level impact of MDA, particularly in light of the 2022 WHO guideline,13 which recommends PC for all eligible individuals aged ≥2 years in endemic communities, according to prevalence-based thresholds.

Spatially uneven or incomplete MDA coverage may repeatedly miss IDPs, non-enrolled children, remote rural communities, and residents of conflict-affected districts. Even when national or governorate-level coverage estimates appear adequate, these groups may sustain infection reservoirs and increase the risk of resurgence.136 Evidence also remains limited on treatment adherence, the extent of systematic non-treatment, and the effectiveness of delivery models for maintaining high PC coverage among hard-to-reach populations. Elimination, therefore, requires not only continued PC, but also microplanning to identify and repeatedly reach missed groups. This is particularly important because systematic non-treatment, in which the same individuals repeatedly miss MDA, can substantially reduce the probability of interrupting transmission.137

Conflict-related displacement since 2015 has also moved many IDPs from rural endemic areas into peri-urban and urban settlements, complicating sustained coverage. Mobile populations may be missed by campaigns, whereas overcrowding, poor WASH, and strained infrastructure in informal settlements may introduce, sustain, or expand transmission foci where suitable intermediate host snails are present. Limited post-MDA monitoring, weak surveillance, and missed mobile populations hinder the detection of residual infections and delay responses to resurgence. In Yemen, reliance on outdated 2004 census data,138 together with displacement and migration, weakens target population denominator estimates by inflating reported MDA coverage and masking untreated groups. In conflict-affected settings, inaccurate or outdated population denominators can overestimate coverage,139 making the reported performance appear adequate despite insufficient true reach. Therefore, independent post-MDA coverage surveys are needed to verify reported coverage, identify missed groups, and measure actual population-level uptake.

Gaps in Awareness and Health Education

Community awareness gaps regarding schistosomiasis transmission and prevention hinder elimination efforts by sustaining unsafe water-contact and contamination behaviors.25,53 In 2015, 92% of Yemeni rural respondents were aware of schistosomiasis, but fewer than half identified a transmission route or preventive measure, and only 11.3% recognized snail involvement, while misconceptions about soil contact, dirty hands, and contaminated food were common.25 Similar gaps were reported among rural schoolchildren in Taiz, where two-thirds had heard of schistosomiasis, but fewer than half knew its cause, waterborne transmission, or prevention, and none recognized snail involvement.53 Poor recognition of snail involvement is particularly important because it limits understanding of why specific water bodies remain transmission sites. Although 86% considered schistosomiasis serious and 74.7% considered it locally prevalent, risky practices remained common.53 Because children are both high-risk and central to MDA delivery, these findings support sustained school-based health education. For example, in Amran, weekly health education sessions for 4 months reduced water-source contact from >90% to <10%,64 demonstrating strong potential to improve awareness and modify risky behaviors. Poor awareness can reduce MDA uptake, weaken adherence, sustain misconceptions, and perpetuate risky water contact behaviors. Although PZQ lowers the prevalence and intensity, a limited understanding of transmission and prevention may promote reinfection and residual foci. Culturally tailored BCC should, therefore, be integrated with MDA, WASH, and snail control activities and delivered through schools, community health workers, religious leaders, and local media.

Gaps in Diagnosis and Surveillance

In post-MDA settings, diagnostic limitations become more important because low-intensity infections reduce microscopy sensitivity.140 Although useful for quantifying infection intensity, Kato-Katz and urine filtration have limited sensitivity for post-MDA surveillance,13 where they may falsely suggest interruption of transmission. Surveillance, therefore, requires more sensitive diagnostic tools to detect residual infections, avoid underestimating prevalence, and confirm persistent hotspots or transmission interruption. In the context of elimination, circulating antigen tests could improve the detection of residual active infections after MDA.140 The circulating cathodic antigen (CCA) can detect low-intensity S. mansoni infections but may yield false-positive results in low-prevalence settings,141,142 while circulating anodic antigen (CAA) is more sensitive for active infections with both S. mansoni and S. haematobium but remains limited by cost, laboratory requirements, and limited commercial availability.140,143 In Yemen, CCA could be considered within a tiered diagnostic strategy or sentinel surveillance system rather than as a stand-alone tool, with positive results confirmed by more specific tests. In low-prevalence or post-MDA settings, a two-step diagnostic approach may be needed, using a high-sensitivity screening test followed by a highly specific confirmatory test to improve specificity and avoid overestimating residual transmission.140 In contrast, antibody detection is unsuitable for control program monitoring because it cannot distinguish current infection from past exposure.144 Although molecular assays have been developed and evaluated to detect and differentiate S. mansoni and S. haematobium in Yemen,114 their routine use by the NSCP remains constrained by cost, infrastructure, and technical demands, leaving surveillance reliant on less sensitive methods.

The lack of regular sentinel surveillance in settings with Schistosoma prevalence <10% limits the ability to identify residual transmission or confirm its interruption. WHO recommends close epidemiological monitoring through sentinel-site surveys every 3 years.13 Passive schistosomiasis surveillance in Yemen is conducted through the electronic Disease Early Warning System (eDEWS), a health facility-based system launched in 2013 to strengthen weekly reporting of priority diseases, early epidemic detection, and rapid response across all 333 districts.145,146 However, eDEWS captures only health facility attendees and therefore misses asymptomatic and low-intensity infections in the community. Its usefulness is further limited by reduced microscopy sensitivity in post-MDA settings, where infections are often light, and by incomplete or inaccurate facility reporting. Therefore, eDEWS should complement rather than replace periodic parasitological and sentinel site surveillance. This can help detect unusual clustering, guide field investigations, verify hotspots, and support targeted MDA in conflict-affected and hard-to-reach districts. Overall, insensitive diagnostic methods, limited post-MDA surveillance, and insufficient reassessment of residual hotspots may underestimate the transmission and delay the transition from control to elimination. Yemen’s elimination strategy should also account for species-specific rebound patterns, as reinfection rates and intensities after PZQ may differ between S. haematobium and S. mansoni depending on local transmission dynamics and endemicity.127,147

Gaps in Risk Mapping

Reliable risk mapping is a prerequisite for eliminating NTDs, including schistosomiasis.148 Yemen’s 2014 nationwide mapping-guided MDA data are now outdated because of conflict, displacement, population movement, and ecological changes. Recent hotspot studies and the 2024 impact evaluation15–20,29 indicate that historical maps and aggregate estimates can miss district- and subdistrict-level focal transmission and that these estimates can mask substantial heterogeneity. Updated district- and subdistrict-level prevalence data are lacking for several historically endemic or ecologically receptive governorates, particularly in areas where conflict, displacement, and service interruptions may have altered transmission patterns. The 2014 SAC-focused mapping may have underestimated the residual transmission by excluding preschoolers, IDPs, and hard-to-reach communities. Therefore, post-MDA risk mapping should become a strategic priority, with reassessment at the subdistrict and village levels, inclusion of underserved populations, and use of more sensitive diagnostic tools where feasible. Because schistosomiasis is focal, water-linked, and shaped by snail ecology and human exposure sites, mapping should incorporate geostatistical modeling to predict risk in unsurveyed areas, detect hotspots, and guide targeted PC and complementary interventions.149–151 Such models should integrate environmental and climatic indicators, including temperature, rainfall, vegetation, land use, and altitude; hydrological features, such as water bodies and irrigation systems; WASH indicators; and population-mobility data, including displacement and migration.

Gaps in WASH Infrastructure

Weak WASH infrastructure in rural and peri-urban settings remains a major structural barrier to schistosomiasis elimination in Yemen. Rural populations, estimated at 63.2% of Yemen’s population in 2024,152 are at particular risk because transmission is often concentrated in agricultural communities dependent on surface water. In 2024, an estimated 15.3 million people lacked access to safe WASH services,153 increasing their vulnerability to waterborne diseases, including schistosomiasis. Rural dependence on surface water for agriculture and domestic use, together with unsafe water access and low literacy, further sustain transmission.

Poor WASH conditions undermine MDA by maintaining reinfection pressure after treatment. Conflict, economic collapse, and displacement have degraded water and sanitation systems, causing contamination of freshwater with human excreta and increasing domestic and agricultural exposure to unsafe water. In crowded IDP camps and informal settlements, poor WASH may create transmission microfoci, enabling the spread to new areas or reintroduction into previously controlled areas where suitable snail habitats exist. WHO therefore recommends WASH interventions to support schistosomiasis control and elimination.13 Meta-analysis evidence shows that safe water and adequate sanitation are significantly associated with more than 50% lower odds of schistosomiasis.154 Targeted WASH rehabilitation is therefore needed in persistent transmission foci, prioritizing safe water access, sanitation coverage, hygiene education, and environmental management in areas where MDA has reduced morbidity but not interrupted exposure.

Gaps in Programmatic Integration

A central programmatic gap is the limited integration of PC with complementary interventions beyond campaign-based health education and social mobilization. Although PC reduces morbidity and infection intensity, interrupting transmission requires coordinated action to reduce environmental contamination, limit human contact with infested water, manage snail habitats, and promote sustained behavioral change. However, implementation research remains limited regarding the feasibility, acceptability, cost, and added value of integrating PC with WASH strengthening, health education, focal snail surveillance, and selective mollusciciding in persistent transmission settings. Conflict, funding shortages, and weak infrastructure further constrain this integration.

Snail control has not been operationally integrated into Yemen’s national strategy, leaving the key ecological component of transmission insufficiently addressed. This gap is compounded by outdated and geographically incomplete malacological data, which limits the understanding of active snail habitats, focal transmission ecology, and operational value of snail surveillance and selective mollusciciding. Focal mollusciciding with niclosamide is a recognized component of integrated schistosomiasis control and may support elimination in persistent hotspots.155 Early observations in Yemen suggested that snail habitats were often small and localized, including cisterns, pools, and streams, making interruption through focal mollusciciding and sanitation potentially feasible.45,46 For instance, around Marib Dam, mollusciciding substantially reduced Biomphalaria and Bulinus snail populations by 1988, after 1 year of control activities, as reviewed by Sallam and Wright,156 suggesting that focal mollusciciding substantially reduced snail populations and supported transmission control in localized endemic settings. In Amran, combined PC and mollusciciding in the late 1980s reduced the prevalence in selected villages from 36.3% to an expected 5.1%,47 showing the value of integrated control in reducing the schistosomiasis burden. However, mollusciciding should be guided by malacological surveys, snail ecology, human water contact assessment, and environmental safety evaluation.

A 2014 NSCP technical report on SCP progress documented staff training in snail control approaches, including exposure to the Zanzibar experience, and emphasized its potential role in schistosomiasis elimination.27 However, this capacity building did not translate into operational snail control. The NSCP proposed a pilot intervention using niclosamide molluscicide in snail habitats across 10 districts in six governorates to assess its impact on schistosomiasis prevalence and transmission. However, because funding was not secured, intervention was not implemented. Local evidence is needed to determine whether focal mollusciciding can reduce snail density, interrupt transmission in persistent hotspots, and complement MDA levels in areas with repeated reinfection.

Information, education, and communication (IEC) activities are often limited to MDA campaigns rather than sustained community-based interventions. During campaigns, IEC can improve PZQ uptake, address the fear of adverse events, and encourage adherence. However, elimination-oriented IEC should extend beyond campaigns and be embedded in schools, primary healthcare, community outreach, WASH activities, and local initiatives. Sustained IEC can improve treatment acceptance, promote safer water contact behaviors, improve sanitation and hygiene practices, and strengthen community engagement. Therefore, IEC should be institutionalized as a core component of schistosomiasis control rather than treated as a temporary adjunct to MDA.

Gaps in Financing, Coordination, and Partnerships

Yemen’s NSCP has relied heavily on external financial and technical support, particularly for nationwide MDA. This support largely enabled previous SCP gains, but campaign-focused project funding limits the integration of complementary interventions. Progressing from disease control to elimination requires sustained financing for PZQ procurement, MDA implementation, social mobilization, coverage monitoring, surveillance, impact evaluation, snail surveillance and control, WASH integration, operational research, and program management.

Weak coordination and limited partnerships also constrain elimination. Sustained progress requires collaboration among the NSCP, other NTD programs, primary healthcare, the Ministry of Education, WASH authorities, humanitarian agencies, donors, local communities, academia, and research institutions. WHO’s NTD roadmap emphasizes integrated, country-led, multi-sectoral action as a foundation for eliminating schistosomiasis as a public health problem by 2030.134 Without such coordination, activities are likely to remain campaign-based and geographically uneven. WASH and humanitarian partners can expand safe water and sanitation in endemic and displaced communities, education authorities can sustain school health education, academic institutions can support mapping and operational research, and local communities can identify high-risk water contact sites and promote prevention.

Lessons Learned from Prior Control Efforts

Yemen’s schistosomiasis control efforts yield several insights for advancing from morbidity control to elimination. First, repeated PZQ-based MDA can substantially reduce infection prevalence, intensity, and morbidity when implemented at sufficient scale and frequency, as demonstrated by the gains achieved during the SCP. However, these gains may not be sustained when treatment becomes irregular, surveillance weakens, and the ecological and behavioral determinants of transmission remain unaddressed. Second, national- or governorate-level prevalence and treatment coverage estimates may conceal persistent district-, subdistrict-, or village-level hotspots and populations that are systematically missed. Reported high coverage may therefore coexist with inadequate effective reach, particularly among IDPs, non-enrolled children, remote communities, and residents of conflict-affected areas. Third, reliance on historical risk maps, administrative coverage reports, and passive health facility surveillance is insufficient for a highly focal disease because displacement, infrastructure damage, environmental change, and altered water-use practices can modify transmission patterns over time. Fourth, campaign-based health education and social mobilization may improve short-term treatment uptake but are unlikely to produce sustained behavioral change unless institutionalized through schools, primary healthcare, community structures, and WASH programs. Finally, MDA alone is unlikely to interrupt transmission where unsafe water contact, inadequate sanitation, active snail habitats, reinfection, and weak post-treatment monitoring persist. These lessons support a shift from uniform, campaign-based treatment toward adaptive, geographically targeted, and multisectoral elimination initiatives.

Need for an Integrated National Schistosomiasis Elimination Strategy

Given Yemen’s focal and heterogeneous transmission, elimination requires a selective, adaptive, and multisectoral strategy that preserves PZQ gains while addressing ecological, behavioral, diagnostic, and health system drivers. Building on the lessons from previous control efforts, the NSCP should develop a national schistosomiasis elimination strategy aligned with the WHO 2021–2030 NTD roadmap,134 which calls for country-owned, integrated, and multi-sectoral approaches to accelerate progress from morbidity control toward elimination. This strategy should also follow the 2022 WHO guidelines for schistosomiasis elimination.13

Operationally, this strategy should update district- and subdistrict-level risk maps to identify persistent and possibly resurgent foci, as well as previously endemic and ecologically receptive areas. PC delivery through MDA should then be aligned with the WHO prevalence thresholds for all eligible individuals aged ≥2 years, prioritizing hotspots, historically endemic districts, displaced populations, remote rural communities, and conflict-affected areas. It should be supported by IEC, social mobilization, independent coverage surveys, and updated population denominators to systematically detect untreated groups and correct inflated MDA coverage estimates. PC should also be integrated with WASH rehabilitation, primary healthcare, humanitarian response, school- and community-based health education, and BCC in areas with a high risk of reinfection. In persistent hotspots, snail surveillance and selective focal mollusciciding should be guided by malacological mapping, human water contact patterns, ecological suitability, and environmental safety assessment. Finally, the impact should be monitored, risk classifications updated, district-level strategies refined, and sustained domestic and external financing secured. Operational research should assess reinfection, treatment adherence, diagnostic performance, snail control feasibility, WASH integration, and effectiveness of delivery models for hard-to-reach populations.

Concluding Remarks and Way Forward

Following more than a decade of repeated nationwide PZQ-based MDA, both urogenital and intestinal schistosomiasis remain persistent and increasingly focal public health problems in Yemen. Historically, widespread endemicity has gradually shifted toward a more focal and heterogeneous pattern, with persistent and possibly resurgent transmission foci that continue to challenge elimination efforts. Morbidity evidence also supports retaining schistosomiasis as a clinical health priority because elimination must address residual complications, case management, and clinical awareness in endemic settings. Diagnostic limitations remain a central challenge, as routine microscopy may underestimate low-intensity infections after repeated MDA. The incorporation of more sensitive field-based antigen detection and molecular tools would strengthen post-MDA surveillance, hotspot detection, and elimination monitoring.

Conflict-related disruptions, weak health system capacity, uncertain population denominators for estimating treatment coverage, limited post-MDA monitoring, and insufficient sentinel surveillance further constrain sustained elimination. These barriers make it difficult to measure program impact, identify untreated or systematically missed populations, and detect persistent or re-emerging hotspots. Poor WASH conditions, risky water-contact practices, limited health education, and absence of operational snail control continue to undermine the gains achieved through MDA. Therefore, future strategies should move beyond uniform national PC campaigns toward integrated and locally tailored interventions capable of detecting and responding to residual and re-emerging transmission.

Considering the limited evidence on drug efficacy and long-term program impact in Yemen, periodic assessments of PZQ efficacy, reinfection patterns, treatment adherence, and coverage are needed. Treatment efficacy should be monitored through sentinel PZQ-efficacy studies in persistent or resurgent hotspots using standardized pre- and post-treatment parasitological assessments. These measures should be integrated into a funded, nationally owned schistosomiasis elimination strategy that aligns with the current guidelines on schistosomiasis13 and WHO roadmap for NTDs 2021–2030.134 Priority actions include updating risk maps at district and subdistrict levels; establishing species-specific sentinel surveillance; introducing more sensitive diagnostic approaches in post-MDA settings; independently validating treatment coverage using updated population data; and ensuring PC delivery to all eligible individuals aged ≥2 years in accordance with WHO prevalence thresholds. Microplanning and adaptable delivery through schools, primary healthcare facilities, community health workers, mobile teams, and humanitarian platforms are essential to reach preschool-aged children, non-enrolled children, IDPs, remote communities, and other systematically missed populations.

PC should be integrated with targeted WASH rehabilitation, sustained school- and community-based BCC, primary healthcare and humanitarian services, snail habitat mapping, and selective focal snail control, where supported by malacological evidence, operational feasibility, and environmental safety assessment. BCC should address locally relevant water-contact practices among children, farmers, agricultural workers, livestock herders, displaced populations, and other vulnerable groups. Sustainable implementation will require multisectoral coordination among the national schistosomiasis control program, primary healthcare services, education and WASH authorities, humanitarian agencies, research institutions, donors, and affected communities, supported by domestic and external financing. Operational research should prioritize subdistrict risk remapping, reinfection measurement, treatment adherence and coverage assessment, PZQ efficacy monitoring, validation of sensitive field diagnostics, snail habitat mapping, evaluation of integrated interventions, and assessment of delivery models for hard-to-reach populations.
