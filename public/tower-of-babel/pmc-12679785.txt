Nursing students’ attitudes toward metaverse applications and their online privacy concerns: a correlational study
Authors: Üzeyir Solak, Tuğçe Kabak Solak, Afife Yurttaş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs digital technologies continue to evolve rapidly, interest in incorporating innovative platforms including the metaverse into nursing education has increased. These virtual environments provide students with interactive, real-time learning opportunities, while also introducing concerns related to online privacy. This study examined the relationship between nursing students’ attitudes toward metaverse applications and their online privacy concerns.MethodsThis study was designed as a cross-sectional correlational study. Data were obtained from 257 undergraduate nursing students enrolled at a public university located in the Eastern Anatolia Region of Turkey. Data were collected between September and November 2022 using a structured, self-administered survey. Instruments included a Demographic Information Form, the Metaverse Scale, and the Online Privacy Concern Scale.ResultsThe mean total score on the Metaverse Scale was 51.63 ± 9.56, with subscale means of Technology (24.28 ± 4.88), Digitalization (10.39 ± 2.29), Social (6.31 ± 1.93), and Lifestyle (10.65 ± 2.53). The Online Privacy Concern Scale total mean score was 51.68 ± 11.66, with subscale scores of Email Use (21.21 ± 5.53), Online Trust (22.85 ± 5.23), and Online Payment (7.62 ± 2.01). A positive, statistically significant correlation was observed between nursing students’ attitudes toward metaverse applications and online privacy concern (p = .001). The Social subscale of the Metaverse Scale showed a significant correlation only with the Online Payment subscale of the Privacy Scale, not with Email Use or Online Trust.ConclusionsThe findings reveal a paradox wherein nursing students with greater enthusiasm for metaverse technologies also experience heightened online privacy concerns. This duality presents a critical challenge in nursing education: harnessing the transformative potential of metaverse applications while mitigating privacy anxieties that may hinder student engagement and academic success. Developing secure, transparent, and privacy-respecting virtual learning environments should be prioritized to foster trust and empower students to fully embrace innovative digital tools in their professional development.

Introduction

Scientific and technological advancements continue to evolve rapidly, shaping numerous domains of human life [1]. Over the past few decades, dominant technological paradigms have evolved: “computer communication” defined the 1990s, “web” technologies gained prominence in the 2000s, “mobile” technologies shaped the 2010s, and in the 2020s, the concept of the “metaverse” has moved to the forefront [2]. The term “metaverse” was first introduced in the early 1990s by Neal Stephenson in his novel Snow Crash, where it was used to describe an imagined universe beyond reality [3, 4]. The concept of the metaverse, formed by the combination of the words “meta” and “universe,” is often described as an advanced or extended reality beyond the physical universe [5]. The metaverse is defined as a three-dimensional (3D), internet-based virtual environment in which individuals can perform daily activities using avatars that represent either real or imaginary versions of themselves [6]. The concept encompasses technologies such as augmented reality, virtual reality, and mixed reality [7]. Metaverse-based technologies are being widely adopted across various sectors such as cinema, music, fashion, sports, and the arts. Their integration into strategic fields like public transportation, defense, smart cities, and healthcare is also rapidly expanding [8]. In parallel with this digital transformation, healthcare education is undergoing a significant evolution, particularly in professions like nursing that require both theoretical knowledge and the development of practical and affective competencies. Nursing education, by its nature, aims to cultivate critical thinking, clinical reasoning, and psychomotor skills through experiential learning approaches. The need for innovative instructional strategies that can simulate real-life complexities has become increasingly evident. In this regard, metaverse applications offer interactive, immersive, and scenario-based learning environments that support nursing students in developing professional competencies in safe and controlled settings [9–12]. These platforms not only enhance students’ engagement and motivation but also facilitate the visualization of abstract clinical concepts and promote self-directed learning. Metaverse-based simulations offer persistent, customizable experiences that prepare students for future clinical challenges by replicating realistic scenarios. Within these scenarios, students can interact with virtual patients, observe clinical signs (e.g., bruising, swelling, bleeding), and engage in decision-making processes while wearing appropriate virtual clinical attire. Such immersive environments have been shown to reduce concern and stress levels during clinical practice, improve technical skill accuracy, and strengthen clinical decision-making abilities. Moreover, these technologies contribute to increased intrinsic motivation, professional identity formation, and academic success among nursing students [13–17]. In addition to these educational benefits, the accessibility and convenience of metaverse tools in education promote learners’ engagement and facilitate the adoption of such technologies [18–20]. Nevertheless, as the integration of internet-based platforms becomes more widespread, it also introduces potential risks that warrant critical consideration. The integration of the internet into nearly all aspects of life has led to the rise of cyberbullying, data misuse, identity theft, and other digital security threats. These issues have significant implications, resulting in both individual and societal-level economic losses and disruptions in essential services [18]. Every digital interaction inevitably leaves a “digital footprint,” prompting individuals to become increasingly aware of the traceability of their online behaviors and the possibility of constant monitoring [21]. Such perceptions often give rise to heightened digital privacy concerns—an issue particularly salient in educational contexts where virtual and immersive technologies are rapidly integrated. Within nursing education, the adoption of metaverse applications thus extends beyond questions of technical competence or access; it also encompasses learners’ trust, sense of autonomy, and ethical sensitivity toward data use and surveillance. To conceptually frame these multifaceted dynamics, the present study draws upon two complementary theoretical perspectives: the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [22, 23] and the Privacy Calculus Theory [24, 25]. TAM explains that individuals’ intentions to adopt a technology are shaped by their perceptions of usefulness and ease of use, and it has been widely applied in educational technology research [26, 27]. However, since the metaverse raises new concerns about data protection and personal privacy, explaining adoption solely through TAM is insufficient. The Privacy Calculus Theory complements TAM by suggesting that individuals make decisions about using digital tools by balancing perceived benefits against potential privacy risks and trust-related concerns [28, 29]. Although these frameworks were not directly measured in this study, they provided a conceptual foundation for interpreting the findings. Accordingly, a positive attitude toward metaverse applications may reflect perceived usefulness and ease of use, whereas higher privacy concerns represent risk perceptions that could limit adoption. Considering both frameworks together contributes to understanding how nursing students’ willingness toward digital innovation coexists with their awareness of privacy concerns [29, 30]. While the potential of the metaverse is recognized, and privacy concerns are often mentioned anecdotally, there is a lack of empirical evidence quantifying the relationship between nursing students’ attitudes towards this technology and their specific online privacy concerns. Understanding this relationship is crucial for developing effective implementation strategies. In line with this need, the present study was designed to examine the relationship between nursing students’ attitudes toward metaverse applications and their online privacy concerns.

Research question

“What is the relationship between nursing students’ attitudes toward metaverse applications and their online privacy concerns?”

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a cross-sectional correlational study. The present study was conducted between September and November 2022 at Ağrı İbrahim Çeçen University, a public higher education institution located in Eastern Türkiye, with first-, second-, and third-year undergraduate nursing students. The undergraduate nursing program is offered within the Faculty of Health Sciences and integrates theoretical instruction with supervised clinical practice, following the national nursing education standards.

Sampling

The target population of the study comprised 296 nursing students enrolled in the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd years of a nursing program at a university in Turkey. The required sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 based on a two-tailed correlation analysis with an assumed medium effect size (r = .30), an alpha level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.95. The minimum sample size required for adequate power was determined to be 134 participants. A convenience sample of 257 students who were present on the days of data collection and met the inclusion criteria was recruited.

Data collection

Data for the study were collected using a Descriptive Information Form, the Metaverse Scale, and the Online Privacy Concern Scale. Data collection was conducted prior to class sessions, following an in-class briefing by the researcher about the purpose of the study. Questionnaires were distributed to students who voluntarily agreed to participate and were collected immediately after completion. Completing the surveys took approximately 15–20 min.

Data collection tools

Descriptive Information Form: In addition to the main study variables, a total of thirteen demographic and academic background variables were collected to provide a comprehensive understanding of the sample and to enable potential control in statistical analyses. These variables included: age, gender, marital status, year of education, income level, daily video watching duration, use of the internet for learning, perceived academic success, perceived lack of academic support in online courses, willingness to choose the nursing department, use of online methods in nursing education, awareness of virtual and augmented reality, and grade point average (GPA). These variables were assessed through structured questions embedded in the first section of the questionnaire.

Metaverse scale

The scale was developed by Süleymanoğulları et al. (2022) and consists of four sub-dimensions (technology, digitalization, social, and lifestyle) and has 15 items. The lowest total score that might be obtained from the 5-point Likert scale (1-I disagree; 5-I agree) is 15 and the highest score is 75. As the total score of the scale increases, the level of knowledge and awareness about the metaverse also increases. The cronbach alpha value of the scale was calculated as 0.81 [31]. The cronbach alpha value obtained was calculated as 0.88 in this study.

Online privacy concern scale

The scale was developed by Buchanan et al. (2007) and its Turkish reliability and validity were done by Alakurt (2017). The scale consists of 14 items across three dimensions, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all, 5 = Very much), with no reverse coded items. A high score on the scale indicates high online privacy concern. The cronbach alpha value of the scale was calculated as 0.89 [32, 33]. The cronbach alpha value was calculated as 0.94 in this study.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the SPSS software in a digital environment. Descriptive statistics including frequency, mean, percentage, and standard deviation were calculated. Skewness and Kurtosis values were analyzed to determine whether the measurements showed a normal distribution. The fact that Skewness and Kurtosis values were between + 3 and − 3 indicated that the measurements had a normal distribution. Pearson correlation analysis, appropriate for normally distributed data, was used to examine the relationships between variables.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the “Noninvasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Agri Ibrahim Cecen University” (date: 22/06/2022 no: 186). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Prior to data collection, participants were fully informed about the purpose and scope of the study, and written informed consent was obtained. Participation was entirely voluntary, and the anonymity and confidentiality of all personal data were rigorously protected throughout the research process.

Results

The mean age of the students who participated in the study was 21.59 ± 2.92 years. Among them, 59.9% were female, 98.8% were single, 40.1% were third-year students, and 47.5% reported having less income than their expenses. Additionally, 57.2% of the students engaged in less than 15 min of daily video calls, while 93.4% used the internet to acquire new information. A total of 73.9% perceived their academic achievement as moderate, and 68.1% did not receive academic support in online courses and felt insufficient in terms of academic performance. Furthermore, 59.1% stated that they had voluntarily chosen their department, and 59.5% expressed willingness to use online learning methods in nursing education. While 47.1% reported being aware of virtual or augmented reality, they considered their knowledge insufficient. Finally, 74.7% had a GPA of 2.50 or above (Table 1).

The study sample predominantly consisted of young, single, female nursing students. Most were in their third year of education, reported moderate academic achievement (Table 1).

Discussion

This study identified a significant paradox in nursing education: students who hold more positive attitudes towards the metaverse also report greater concerns about their online privacy. This positive correlation suggests that enthusiasm for innovative technology does not preclude, and may even be coupled with, a critical awareness of its risks.

In the present study, the mean metaverse scale score of the students was found to be 51.63 ± 9.56, the technology sub-dimension mean score was 24.28 ± 4.88, the digitalization sub-dimension mean score was 10.39 ± 2.29, the social sub-dimension mean score was 6.31 ± 1.93, and the lifestyle sub-dimension mean score was 10.65 ± 2.53. In this study, nursing students were found to have generally positive attitudes toward metaverse applications. In this study, it was found that nursing students’ attitudes towards metaverse applications were at a good level. These results are compatible with the studies in the literature [31, 34–36]. Cakir et al. (2022) reported that university students’ attitudes toward the metaverse were at a moderate level [34]. Similarly, Savas et al. (2022) found that male university students had significantly higher levels of metaverse knowledge compared to their female counterparts [35]. In a more recent study, Kim and Kim (2023) identified a positive effect of metaverse use on decision-making self-efficacy among nursing students [36]. In light of the findings, nursing students’ moderately high attitudes toward metaverse applications can largely be attributed to their generational and demographic characteristics, particularly as members of generation Z, often referred to as digital natives [37, 38]. Their early and seamless exposure to digital tools facilitates a greater openness toward emerging platforms such as the metaverse [39]. This alignment between students’ digital fluency and innovations in educational technology may help explain their predisposition to adopt metaverse-based applications in nursing education.

It was also found that the students’ mean online privacy scale score was 51.68 ± 11.66, the use of e-mails sub-dimension mean score was 21.21 ± 5.53, the online trust sub-dimension mean score was 22.85 ± 5.23, and the online payment sub-dimension mean score was 7.62 ± 2.01. based on this, it might be argued that the students who participated in the study had high online privacy concerns. This result is consistent with findings reported in the literature [40–43]. Eroglu (2018) reported that students are aware of what constitutes personal data; however, their lack of awareness and concern regarding the topic remains high [40]. Findings by Yabanci et al. (2018) indicated that university students’ levels of online security anxiety differ significantly based on age [41]. Okumus and Atılgan (2021) found that as students’ digital literacy levels increase, their online privacy concerns also rise [42]. A study by Ozturk et al. (2022) showed that students with online privacy concerns tend to find face-to-face counseling more valuable [43]. Despite being digital natives, the relatively high levels of online privacy concerns observed among nursing students reflect a nuanced paradox. Within the Turkish context, increasing public discourse around digital rights and frequent reports of cybersecurity breaches in both public and private sectors appear to have heightened students’ sensitivity toward data privacy [44]. Consequently, although these students are generally comfortable using digital technologies, they may adopt a cautious approach toward educational platforms particularly when such platforms involve the collection or processing of personal data [45]. This ambivalence may hinder their full engagement in virtual learning environments, potentially impacting their academic participation and performance. Understanding this dynamic is critical for designing safe, transparent, and student-centered digital learning ecosystems within nursing education. In this study, a positive and statistically significant relationship was identified between the total and subdimension scores of the metaverse scale and the online privacy concern scale. Accordingly, individuals with stronger attitudes toward the metaverse tended to exhibit higher levels of privacy concern. This seemingly contradictory relationship, where greater engagement with metaverse technologies correlates with heightened privacy concerns, reflects the complex interplay between technological enthusiasm and digital vulnerability. Subdimension analyses provided a more nuanced understanding of this relationship: while weak but significant correlations were observed between the technology and digitalization subdimensions and all online privacy subscales, the lifestyle subdimension demonstrated moderate correlations with email use, online trust, and online payment concerns. This suggests that students who integrate metaverse technologies into their daily routines may develop a heightened sensitivity to privacy-related implications. In other words, when virtual experiences become embedded within lived realities, users are more likely to recognize and respond to risks associated with data access, transaction security, and institutional trust. In contrast, the social subdimension showed no significant associations with email use or general online confidence, indicating that students may perceive social interaction in virtual environments as less privacy-threatening. This paradox may stem from the generational normalization of digital communication: for generation Z, digital socialization is often regarded as routine rather than risky [46–48].

Shah (2024), in a systematic literature review and interview study, stated that concerns about usability and security, as well as the challenge of balancing the two, constitute the basis of privacy concerns related to the metaverse [49]. Additionally, Tukur et al. (2023) emphasized concerns regarding hardware and software costs, application complexities, digital inequalities, and ethical and moral dilemmas associated with the metaverse [50]. These results emphasize the complex interplay between technological advancement and privacy concerns within the metaverse ecosystem. While our study did not directly measure TAM constructs, the observed positive attitudes suggest that students may perceive the metaverse as having utility. However, the concomitant high privacy concerns likely act as a significant barrier to adoption, consistent with the principles of the Privacy Calculus Theory, a factor that must be integrated into extended technology acceptance models for educational contexts [51–53]. This suggests that minimizing perceived risks is essential to transform favorable attitudes into actual behavioral intentions and sustained usage. In the context of nursing education in Turkey, while students’ digital nativeness enhances openness to innovative technologies, their growing awareness of surveillance, data misuse, and ethical concerns often inhibits deeper engagement with these platforms [54]. Consequently, trust, safety, and ethical awareness emerge as pivotal components in fostering technology-enhanced learning environments within nursing curricula. These findings highlight that successful integration of metaverse technologies in nursing education requires not only technical readiness but also ethical and institutional preparedness. Therefore, it becomes imperative for nursing programs to adopt a dual-focused strategy: advancing the pedagogical integration of immersive technologies while simultaneously implementing robust privacy protocols and fostering digital ethics education. It should also be acknowledged that this study’s findings are limited by its cross-sectional design and the use of a convenience sample from a single institution, which may restrict generalizability. However, these limitations do not diminish the relevance of the observed relationships; rather, they highlight the need for further multi-institutional and longitudinal research in this area.

Limitations of the study

This study was conducted at a single university using a convenience sample, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, and the reliance on self-reported data introduces potential response and social desirability biases. Additionally, the rapidly evolving nature of metaverse technologies may result in variability in participants’ awareness and attitudes.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study identified a paradox among nursing students: those with more positive attitudes toward the metaverse also reported greater concerns about online privacy. While technological familiarity promotes engagement, privacy concerns may limit participation. These findings indicate that effective integration of metaverse technologies in nursing education requires not only technical readiness but also ethical and institutional preparedness.

A dual-focused strategy is therefore essential—advancing the pedagogical use of immersive technologies while strengthening digital ethics and privacy education. Nursing curricula should include structured training covering data protection principles, digital footprints, secure communication in virtual environments, informed consent, and patient confidentiality in metaverse-based simulations.
