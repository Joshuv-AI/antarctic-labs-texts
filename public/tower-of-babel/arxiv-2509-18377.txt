Interactive Real-Time Speaker Diarization Correction with Human Feedback
Xinlu He, Yiwen Guan, Badrivishal Paurana, Zilin Dai, and Jacob Whitehill

Abstract
Most automatic speech processing systems operate in “open loop” mode without user feedback about who said what, yet human-in-the-loop workflows can potentially enable higher accuracy. We propose an LLM-assisted speaker diarization correction system that lets users fix speaker attribution errors in real time. The pipeline performs streaming ASR and diarization, uses an LLM to deliver concise summaries to the users, and accepts brief verbal feedback that is immediately incorporated without disrupting interactions. Moreover, we develop techniques to make the workflow more effective: First, a split-when-merged (SWM) technique detects and splits multi-speaker segments that the ASR erroneously attributes to just a single speaker. Second, online speaker enrollments are collected based on users’ diarization corrections, thus helping to prevent speaker diarization errors from occurring in the future. LLM-driven simulations on the AMI test set indicate that our system substantially reduces DER by 9.92% and speaker confusion error by 44.23%. We further analyze correction efficacy under different settings, including summary vs full transcript display, the number of online enrollments limitation, and correction frequency.

Introduction

Automatic speech recognition and speaker diarization often degrade in multi-speaker conversations, where speaker attribution errors undermine transcription reliability

. Subsequent correction is thus often necessary to obtain high accuracy

A more practical approach in some settings is to let speakers review brief summaries and make lightweight, natural corrections to speaker-attributed text without disrupting the interaction. Recent advances in large language models provide the opportunity for interactive workflows

In this paper, we propose a real-time, interactive correction system (Fig.
1
). The pipeline first performs automatic speech recognition and diarization to produce a speaker-attributed transcript, then uses an LLM to summarize the content for the users. During the conversation, users can provide brief verbal corrections without interrupting the interactions. The system immediately incorporates this feedback, keeping transcripts accurate and trustworthy in the moment, and enabling more effective collaboration in meetings, classrooms, and real-time communication tools.

Figure 1:
Proposed LLM-enabled workflow
: Based on an LLM-generated diarization summary of the previous several utterances, the user gives feedback (with wake-word “Hey Cobi”). This feedback corrects the speech transcript and provides an additional online enrollment for the corrected speaker.

To improve the performance of this system, we introduce two strategies: split-when-merged and online enrollments. Firstly, in multi-speaker scenarios, mainstream ASR pipelines segment by silence, so a single segment may merge utterances from different speakers. Combined with a one-label-per-segment assumption, this limits correction effectiveness. Our split-when-merged (SWM) method detects and splits such mixed segments via window voting with smoothing, enabling corrections to target the proper speaker spans. Secondly, we also seek to harness human feedback not just retroactively to fix mistakes but also proactively to prevent future ones. Hence,
when a user corrects a speaker, we update that speaker’s enrollment list with the corresponding embedding, thus preventing the mismatch between enrollment audio and in-situation speech. Based on these techniques, we conduct simulations on the AMI test set to quantify the benefits of the proposed workflow under different configurations.

Proposed System

We implement an LLM-based interactive speech pipeline (Fig.
1
) for real-time multi-speaker conversation, allowing users to correct the speaker assignment for a specific sentence in the transcript.
The system proceeds in five stages: (1) A streaming ASR produces a transcript for each segment. (2) A speaker embedding model extracts a segment-level embedding, compares it with the enrollments, and assign a speaker label with the closest enrolled embedding. (3) The LLM periodically summarizes the speaker-attribute transcript every
I
segments.
I
is the summarization interval. Then it displays the summary to users. (4) Users review the summary, and provide brief verbal correction feedback to the system starting from the wake word “Hey Cobi”
[
17
]
. (5) The LLM ingests the feedback text, and re-assign the correct speaker for the target sentence, updating the transcript immediately.

3.1
Split-When-Merged (SWM)

In the preliminary experiments, we found the current streaming ASR frontend often merges adjacent turns from different speakers when speakers talk in quick succession, causing the single segment spans multiple speakers. In this case, because the speaker label and corrections are applied at the segment level, this under-segmentation limits the diarization gains from interactive correction. Hence, we add a post-processor split-when-merged (SWM) to the ASR segments.

3.2
Online Enrollments (OE)

With user correction, we also aim to improve the speaker recognition process automatically, improving the subsequent accuracy. Therefore, we add the embedding that the user corrected as a new speaker enrollment. The embeddings generated for the misclassified segments are used for online enrollment for the true speaker and added to the speaker’s pool of embedding vectors. In the following speech process of conversation, all the embeddings in the speaker’s enrollment pool are compared using max cosine similarity as the decision statistic.
The system as a whole is causal, which helps influence the recognition of the subsequent segments, and not any past decisions. As the enrollment pool grows, accuracy can improve in later stages of the meeting.

4
Experiments

4.1
Dataset and Metrics

Dataset
We test our system on the AMI Meeting Corpus, a standard benchmark for multi-speaker ASR and diarization. We use the official Headset-mix test split (16 meetings, about 9 hours; 14 to 50 minutes each; 4 speakers per meeting).
AMI’s frequent interruptions, overlaps, and hesitations make it a realistic meeting benchmark.

Evaluation Metrics

We report the diarization error rate (DER) with pyannote,
including Missed Speech (Miss), False Alarm (FA), and Speaker Confusion (Conf):

\textstyle DER=\frac{T_{miss}+T_{fa}+T_{conf}}{T_{total}}
.
In our experiments, ASR detection is fixed, leaving Miss and FA unchanged. So we report Speaker Conf to isolate effects on speaker attribution. For interpretability, we also report relative improvements ImDER and ImConf over the baseline.

LLM

We use GPT-5o-mini in the proposed system’s summarization and corrections, and also the user feedback simulation in the experiments.
For summarization, the LLM consumes the last
I

For user-simulation in experiments, LLM receives the system-generated summary and the ground truth transcription, and produce a simulated correction message. Fig.

We use Google Cloud Speech-to-Text in streaming mode

For SWM, we use sliding windows with length

4.3
Baseline

Our primary baseline is the streaming ASR and diarization front end that outputs per-segment speaker labels. A second baseline augments this front end with the split-when-merged (SWM) post-processor.
For each baseline, we also report an oracle. For oracle, we hold the segment boundaries fixed to those produced by that system, and reassign each segment’s speaker to the reference speaker with the largest temporal overlap in the ground truth record. These oracles give the minimum DER achievable under fixed boundaries, isolating speaker attribution errors while leaving detection and segmentation effects unchanged.

5.2
Sensitivity Analysis

Number of Online Enrollments

For online enrollments, we add embeddings in speaker enrollment and observe from experiments that the diarization results benefit from it. We wonder how many embeddings are need for the best performance. Figure
3
(a) shows how the DER improvement change along with the number of online enrollments added in three different conditions: No direct correction but only adding embeddings based on user feedback, correction with SWM, and correction without SWM. From the figure, adding the first OE yields a sizable improvement of DER about 3% to 5%. Increasing the OE number from 1 to 2 continues to be helpful when SWM is absent, which can be due to mixed speaker segment contamination. Beyond 2 OEs, all curves plateau or show a slight decline. Hence, 1 OE suffices with SWM-based correction, and a 2-OE setting is robust for other conditions.

Figure 3:
(a) DER Improvement vs Online Enrollments Number.
(b) DER Improvement vs Correction Frequency.

Correction frequency

In interaction, users do not want to be constantly distracted by correction requests. Our goal is to limit user interventions while preserving improvements in speaker attribution. We control correction frequency with two settings: (1) the summarization interval
I
, which means the system show a conversation summary after every
I
segments, and (2) the maximum correction limit; once the limit is reached, further prompts are suppressed.
Fig.
3
(b) shows the DER improvement vs the average correction frequency: more frequent prompting does not automatically yield larger gains. Improvements are highest at moderate frequency, about 2 to 5 minutes, while frequent correction every one minute offers less benefit. For a fixed summary interval like 10, raising the correction limit requires more feedback from user but improves the DER. The best setting is 15 as the summary interval and a correction limit of 30.

Conclusion

We presented a real-time, LLM-assisted framework for interactive correction of speaker-attributed transcripts, integrating ASR, diarization, and lightweight verbal feedback. The framework incorporates two key components: Split-when-Merged, which splits mixed segments, and online enrollments, which update enrollments based on user corrections to improve effectiveness. On AMI, the system reduces DER by 9.92% and speaker-confusion errors by 44.23%. These results demonstrate that real-time feedback, combined with structural and adaptive refinements, delivers practical gains for multi-speaker transcription.
