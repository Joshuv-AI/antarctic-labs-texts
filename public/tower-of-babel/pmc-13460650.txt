Spontaneous Achievers, Responders and Non‐Responders: Aligning Treatment With the Natural History of Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia in Preterm Infants
Authors: Chad C. Andersen, Michael J. Stark
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine how early pulmonary phenotype influences treatment response and the interpretation of trial outcomes in bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD).MethodsA narrative review of early respiratory patterns in very preterm infants and their relationship to treatment response, using published trials of postnatal corticosteroids as the primary example.Results BPD is defined at 36 weeks' postmenstrual age by treatment requirements, reflecting clinical practice rather than the underlying disease biology. The clinical phenotype is evident much earlier. Some infants stabilise with supportive care alone, while others develop persistent pulmonary disease consistent with emerging BPD, with variable responses to treatment. When therapies are applied to broad early populations, spontaneous achievers dilute the apparent treatment effect. As postnatal age increases and populations are enriched for persistent disease, treatment effects become larger and more consistent. Findings from postnatal corticosteroid trials follow this pattern.ConclusionThus, the effect of disease‐modifying therapy in infants at risk of BPD reflects both the intervention and the interaction between population risk and cohort phenotype and is best understood when treatment and trial design align with the early clinical phenotype.

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) frequently complicates very preterm birth and has lifelong consequences for lung health [1, 2]. Despite advances in care, its incidence has not declined, particularly in more immature infants. Defined by later treatment‐based criteria rather than disease biology, it is, therefore, influenced by centre‐specific clinical practice. Yet clinical differences are evident much earlier. Within the first postnatal weeks, infants diverge in their respiratory phenotype. Some stabilise with supportive care alone (spontaneous achievers), while others develop persistent pulmonary disease (emerging BPD). This separation is consistent with the pulmonary pathology, which precedes the current diagnostic threshold by several weeks. This review examines how early clinical phenotype determines treatment response and how that response, in turn, influences both clinical practice and trial outcomes.

Early Clinical Phenotype

Early descriptions of pulmonary disease from preterm birth emphasised the contribution of oxygen toxicity and ventilator‐associated injury. Initially, Wilson and Mikity described a syndrome of tachypnoea, radiographic changes and pulmonary hypertension with a high rate of mortality following prolonged oxygen exposure [3]. Northway defined bronchopulmonary dysplasia as a clinicopathological consequence of mechanical ventilation and high inspired oxygen concentrations [4]. With improved survival at lower gestational ages due to antenatal steroids and surfactant administration, the clinical phenotype changed again [5, 6]. Jobe described a new BPD characterised by impaired alveolarisation and abnormal pulmonary vascular development arising from injury during critical windows of lung growth [7]. This marked a significant evolution from the original description [8, 9]. Despite these changes, classification remains late in the disease process, uncoupling diagnosis from underlying disease biology [10, 11].

In contrast to the later clinical diagnosis of BPD, individual pulmonary phenotypes become evident much earlier. The respiratory course in the extremely preterm newborn can be divided into two patterns during the first 2 postnatal weeks. Many stabilise with supportive care alone and recover without disease‐modifying intervention (spontaneous achievers). Others develop persistent pulmonary disease early in the postnatal course. Within this group, response to treatment varies. Some improve with intervention (responders), while others remain unstable, with sustained oxygen requirements, ventilatory dependence, or deterioration after initial improvement despite ongoing support (non‐responders). This group of non‐responders becomes progressively distinct and accounts for most infants who later meet BPD diagnostic criteria [12]. The biological basis for this separation lies in the persistence of pulmonary inflammation often initiated by antenatal signalling and magnified by postnatal therapy, beyond the first postnatal week in a vulnerable lung. Tracheal aspirate studies show sustained neutrophilic infiltration, elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines and ongoing protease activity, detectable as early as the third day of life in infants who later develop BPD [13]. Persistence of this inflammatory signal, rather than its early presence alone, defines the population at risk.

Postnatal Corticosteroids

Results from trials of postnatal corticosteroids in very preterm infants at risk of BPD are inconsistent [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. This variability is best explained by population selection and individual phenotypes rather than by a corticosteroid‐mediated effect. When given early, before the clinical separation of pulmonary phenotypes is established, a broad at‐risk population is exposed, including many who will recover with supportive care alone. In this setting, treatment effects are modest, and the potential for harm is greater. As postnatal age increases, the population becomes progressively enriched with infants with persistent pulmonary disease, and treatment effects are larger and more consistent (Figure 1).

The relationship between postnatal age, pulmonary phenotype, and observed treatment effect is illustrated using selected corticosteroid trials. Early trials enrolled heterogeneous populations with a high proportion of spontaneous achievers and showed small treatment effects [16]. As postnatal age increases, the population shifts towards infants with persistent pulmonary disease, with reduced spontaneous recovery and a relative increase in responders [15, 19]. Non‐responders persist across all stages and account for a substantial proportion of infants at risk of later bronchopulmonary dysplasia. The observed treatment effect reflects both the intervention's biological action and the population's phenotypic composition at the time of the study.

Across four trial populations, including women at risk of preterm birth, infants at risk of bronchopulmonary dysplasia, infants with emerging bronchopulmonary dysplasia, and infants with established disease, the rate of spontaneous recovery declines, shifting the population towards a phenotype of emerging BPD (Table 1). In this setting, the proportion of responders rises and treatment effects increase. However, the most striking finding is the high rate of non‐responders. These infants have recalcitrant disease, remain in intensive care, and account for most infants who meet diagnostic criteria for bronchopulmonary dysplasia at 36 weeks.

Implications for Trial Design

Research trials in infants at risk of BPD use gestational age and/or birth weight to define population risk, while the composition of clinical phenotypes is determined by the entry criteria or the timing of enrolment. These two variables determine the proportions of both spontaneous achievers and infants with persistent pulmonary disease (Figure 2).

Sequential enrichment of trial populations by postnatal age. Early populations are heterogeneous and include many infants who recover with supportive care alone. Over time, spontaneous recovery declines, and the remaining population becomes enriched for persistent pulmonary disease. Responders exit once clinical objectives are met, while non‐responders remain and form progressively enriched cohorts for further evaluation, including further randomised intervention.

These differences inform the balance between benefits and harms. Broad enrolment of low‐risk infants likely includes many who will recover without intervention, reducing the treatment effect but also increasing the risk of unnecessary exposure. More selective enrolment, using lower gestation and later time points or clinical criteria, increases the proportion in whom treatment may have an effect.

Lastly, treatment should stop once predefined clinical objectives are achieved to limit harm, while outcomes are retained for analysis. Those who respond continue to contribute to the primary outcome, and those who do not respond remain and define a progressively enriched cohort in which treatment effects can be assessed. In this framework, the treatment effect is evaluated within the relevant phenotype.

Thus, the population defines who is at risk, the cohort composition defines which phenotype is studied, and the balance between benefit and harm for any intervention is determined by their interaction.

Conclusion

BPD is defined at 36 weeks' postmenstrual age, by which time the consequences of developmental immaturity, injury and treatment have already shaped the individual lung phenotype. Yet clinical phenotypic separation is apparent much earlier. Some infants stabilise with supportive care alone, recovering without disease‐modifying intervention. Others develop persistent disease. Within this group, some respond to targeted intervention while others do not. These non‐responders account for most preterm infants who ultimately meet diagnostic criteria for BPD.

This clinical separation has direct implications for research and treatment. When therapy is applied to broad populations before this phenotypic separation is evident, infants who will recover without intervention are included, diluting the therapeutic signal and exposing spontaneous achievers to unnecessary risk. The unmet need lies with infants who do not respond to initial treatment. These non‐responders define the population at highest risk and should be the focus of ongoing intervention and trial design. Ultimately, aligning treatment and trial design with the clinical phenotype rather than gestational age alone will increase the effect size, reduce sample size and improve trial effectiveness by concentrating efforts on the most at‐risk population.
