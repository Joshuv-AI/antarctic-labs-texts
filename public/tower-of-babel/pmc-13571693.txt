Unmasked neuropathic groin pain: risk of reoperation
Authors: Kevin M Klifto, Miguel I Dorante, William D Henderson, David L Brown, Robert R Hagan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdentifying factors associated with unmasked neuropathic groin pain after peripheral nerve surgery may clarify its pathophysiology and risks, reduce reoperations, and improve patient outcomes and satisfaction.MethodsWe retrospectively evaluated adults who underwent neurectomy between June 2006 and January 2025 for groin pain localized to the iliohypogastric nerve (IHN), ilioinguinal nerve (IIN), genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve (GBGFN), or lateral femoral nerve (LFN). Patients were stratified by whether they underwent reoperation for unmasked neuropathic pain in a new anatomical distribution. Outcomes were assessed using Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) and functional scores. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent predictors of reoperation. Minimum follow-up was 6 months.ResultsOf 273 patients, 255 (93.4%) patients did not require reoperations and 18 (6.5%) required reoperations for unmasked pain (median time to reoperation: 133 days). Among patients with unmasked pain, the most common initial pain distribution was that of the IIN (88.8%), whereas the most common unmasked distribution was that of the GBGFN (72.2%). GBGFN resection and muscle resection/transposition were associated with lower odds of reoperation. Lower BMI; pain, tingling, and/or burning in the scrotum, testicles, or labia; higher initial NRS scores for the “lowest” pain; and sharp pain were associated with greater odds of reoperation.ConclusionNeuropathic pain was unmasked at new locations following 6.5% of neurectomy procedures. Surgeons should reassess patients preoperatively for genital GBGFN pathology and consider including this nerve in operative plans when findings are subtle, potentially preventing reoperation. These data may also inform consent discussions.Keywords: Groin, Neuralgia, Neuropathic pain, Pelvic pain, Unmasked pain, Reoperation

Introduction

The primary goals of surgery for the treatment of neuropathic pain are to perform a single operation that eliminates pain while minimizing sensory loss and complications and maximizing postoperative function in activities of daily living.1 However, a small but meaningful proportion of patients may identify new areas of pain postoperatively, a phenomenon known as pain unmasking, which may prompt further evaluation and additional surgery.2

The phenomenon of masked pain explains how a more prominent pain signal prevents a patient from detecting a weaker pain signal carried by a different nerve through a “pain-inhibits-pain” pathway.3,4 This process occurs primarily at the level of the spinal cord.4 Unmasked neuropathic pain is diagnosed when a patient experiences pain in a new nerve distribution after neurectomy,2 with the perception that the original pain has “moved” to a new location.

Identifying factors that contribute to unmasked postoperative neuropathic groin pain may help clinicians better understand its pathophysiology, reduce reoperation rates, and improve patient function and satisfaction. Our objective was to compare the characteristics of patients who underwent surgery for neuropathic groin pain and subsequently required reoperation for recurrent, unmasked groin pain with those of patients who did not require reoperation.

Materials and methods

We performed an Institutional Review Board approved retrospective analysis of all patients at a single center who underwent peripheral nerve surgery for groin pain between June 2006 and January 2025. The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.5

Study population

Patients included in this study were aged 18 years or older, had a diagnosis of neuropathic groin pain, underwent neurectomy for groin pain localized to one or more of the following lumbar plexus peripheral nerves: iliohypogastric (IHN), ilioinguinal (IIN), genital branch of the genitofemoral (GBGFN), lateral femoral (LFN), and had a minimum follow-up of 6 months. The groin was defined as the anatomical region encompassing the inferolateral abdomen, upper thigh, and pubis, with the groin crease forming its central axis.6 Neuropathic pain was defined as paresthesias and/or dysesthesias occurring secondary to a lesion or disease process involving the peripheral nervous system that generated abnormal somatosensory signals (Fig. 1).1 Diagnoses were confirmed before surgery using the constellation of symptoms, pain drawings, physical examination findings, and diagnostic nerve blocks. The operating surgeon performed ultrasound-guided nerve blocks with 0.25% bupivacaine and 1% lidocaine, targeting suspected offending nerves proximal to the painful area or presumed site of injury. Imaging, including radiography, ultrasonography, nerve conduction studies, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging was used selectively, primarily to rule out other causes of pain.

Patients were stratified into two postoperative cohorts: those who underwent one or more reoperations for unmasked neuropathic groin pain and those who did not. Patients in the “unmasked” group developed new pain in a different anatomical nerve distribution after neurectomy. They were subsequently reevaluated using their clinical history, physical examination, and nerve blocks and were diagnosed with an injury to a different nerve. Patients often described their original pain as “not fixed but moved from one location to another.”

Outcomes

Patients were followed postoperatively in the clinic until their course was stable and discharge was considered appropriate. Some patients included in this study were contacted by telephone for additional follow-up to obtain longer-term outcome data.

Pain severity was measured using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), ranging from 0 to 10, with 0 indicating no pain and 10 indicating the worst pain imaginable.7 Functional scores were recorded on a scale from 0 to 100%, with 0% indicating no physical function because of groin pain and 100% indicating full physical function without groin pain. Functional scores of <25% were considered poor; 25–49%, fair; 50–74%, good; and ≥75%, excellent. Patients were asked whether they believed the surgery was successful overall and whether they would recommend it to other patients.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to compare medians, interquartile ranges (IQR), odds ratios (OR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), frequencies, and percentages for variables and outcomes, given the non-parametric distribution of population data and small sample sizes. Differences between patients who underwent reoperation for unmasked groin pain and those who did not were assessed using Fisher exact tests for dichotomous variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous and ordinal variables. Univariate analyses were followed by multivariate stepwise logistic regression with forward selection to identify independent predictors of reoperation for unmasked neuropathic groin pain. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with the threshold for statistical significance set at an α value of 0.05. Analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Version 30.0.0.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

Results

A total of 273 patients met the study criteria, were diagnosed with neuropathic sources of groin pain, and underwent surgery involving 543 peripheral nerves (LFN=171, IIN=160, IHN=132, GBGFN=80). Eighteen patients (6.5%) underwent reoperation for unmasked neuropathic groin pain in a new location.

The anatomical distributions of groin pain at the initial evaluation were compared between patients who subsequently developed unmasked pain and those who did not (Table 1). Among patients with unmasked pain, the most common pain distribution at initial presentation was that of the IIN (n = 16/18, 88.8%), whereas the most common distribution of unmasked pain was that of the GBGFN (n = 13/18, 72.2%)(Fig. 2A and Fig. 2B).

Multivariate analyses were performed using significant findings to identify independent predictors of pain unmasking. Surgical resection of the GBGFN (OR: 0.031, 95%CI: 0.003–0.289; p = 0.002) and muscle resection/transposition (OR: 0.040, 95%CI: 0.002–0.667; p = 0.025) predicted lower odds of reoperation. Lower BMI (OR: 1.29, 95%CI: 1.05–1.59; p = 0.014), sharp pain (OR: 11.81, 95%CI: 1.101–138.45; p = 0.049), pain, tingling, or burning in the scrotum, testicles, or labia (OR: 89.76, 95%CI: 5.74–1403; p = 0.001), and higher initial NRS scores for the “lowest pain within the last month” (OR: 1.58, 95%CI: 1.09–2.28; p = 0.014) predicted greater odds of reoperation for unmasked groin pain.

Discussion

Unmasked pain reflects an interaction between overlapping peripheral nerve territories, nociceptive modulation within the central nervous system, and physiological changes associated with peripheral nerve injury. In this 20-year retrospective cohort, 6.5% of patients who underwent surgery for neuropathic groin pain developed unmasked neuropathic pain in a new nerve distribution that required reoperation. Although these data are limited to patients with groin pain, the phenomenon represents an important clinical consideration in the evaluation, surgical planning, and management of neuropathic pain in any anatomical region. Restricting the evaluation to one anatomical region also provided a more homogeneous population for comparison.

The groin is particularly prone to diagnostic complexity because multiple sensory nerves from the lumbar plexus converge within a small anatomical region. The IHN, IIN, and GBGFN share overlapping dermatomal distributions across the lower abdomen, groin crease, proximal medial thigh, and genital regions.6 Pathology involving multiple nerves may coexist, although patients may perceive only the dominant nociceptive generator. In the present cohort, most patients who ultimately developed unmasked pain initially presented with symptoms in the IIN or IHN distributions, whereas postoperative unmasked pain often occurred in the GBGFN distribution. These findings suggest that concomitant GBGFN pathology may frequently be present but clinically overshadowed by stronger nociceptive signals from adjacent nerves. Given the number of patients affected, prophylactic resection of the GBGFN in patients with IIN or IHN pathology may be overly aggressive despite its low morbidity. However, these data support maintaining a high preoperative index of suspicion for GBGFN involvement and considering resection when clinically indicated.

The neurophysiological basis of this phenomenon may be explained by diffuse noxious inhibitory controls (DNIC).3,4 Experimental studies have demonstrated that, through this pathway, a strong nociceptive stimulus suppresses competing, weaker nociceptive signals via descending inhibitory pathways within the central nervous system.3,4 At the dorsal horn, convergent nociceptive neurons receive input from multiple peripheral sources, and intense stimulation from one source can inhibit transmission from others.3 When the dominant pain generator is surgically eliminated, these inhibitory interactions resolve, allowing previously suppressed nociceptive signals to become perceptible.3 This mechanism may explain the shift in a pain distribution among patients who develop unmasked neuropathic pain after peripheral nerve surgery.2

Peripheral nerve injuries are common after surgical interventions and may contribute to iatrogenic pain. Transection, traction, thermal injury, ligation and/or postoperative scarring of sensory nerves can produce ongoing aberrant electrophysiological activity through sodium channel upregulation, ectopic impulse generation, and neuroma formation.8 Injured axons may develop spontaneous firing and cross-excitation between neighboring fibers, amplifying nociceptive signaling within the peripheral nervous system.9 In anatomically dense neural regions such as the groin, where multiple sensory branches traverse shared fascial planes, these interactions may facilitate communication between adjacent injured nerves.6 Eliminating a dominant pain generator may therefore expose signals from other sensitized nerves that were previously masked.2

Central sensitization likely represents another mechanism contributing to unmasked neuropathic pain. Persistent nociceptive input from peripheral nerve injuries can induce long-term plastic changes in dorsal horn neurons, including increased synaptic efficacy, expansion of neuronal receptive fields, and heightened responsiveness to sensory input.10 These changes may persist even after the primary peripheral stimulus is removed. Patients with long-standing neuropathic groin pain may therefore develop sensitized spinal circuits capable of amplifying signals from secondary peripheral nerves.3 Removal of the dominant nociceptive source may subsequently reveal additional sensory inputs that fall within these expanded receptive fields. Our cohort had low final NRS pain scores despite the need for reoperation, which may suggest that interventions were performed before central sensitization developed.

Our multivariate analysis of surgical outcomes supports this pathophysiological framework. Sharp pain and genital sensory disturbances were strongly associated with reoperation for unmasked pain. These symptoms are characteristic of GBGFN involvement, suggesting that subtle concomitant GBGFN pathology may be present at initial presentation but masked by dominant pain signals from the IHN or IIN. Higher baseline NRS scores for the “lowest within the last month” also predicted unmasking, potentially reflecting greater peripheral and central sensitization that predisposes patients to perceive additional pain generators after treatment of the primary lesion.

Surgical resection of the GBGFN was associated with significantly lower odds of reoperation for unmasked neuropathic groin pain. One possible explanation is that early identification and treatment of GBGFN pathology eliminates a common secondary pain generator that might otherwise become clinically apparent after treatment of adjacent nerves. Because the genital branch innervates highly sensitive structures of the scrotum and labia, even subtle pathology may become symptomatic after competing nociceptive signals are removed. Lower BMI was also associated with greater odds of unmasked pain. Although the explanation for this finding is unclear, patients with less subcutaneous tissue overlying the inguinal region may have less mechanical protection of small sensory nerves, potentially increasing their susceptibility to irritation, traction, or postoperative scar entrapment. Although unmasked neuropathic groin pain occurred in 6.5% of this surgical population, most patients experienced durable symptom improvement and high overall satisfaction after surgical treatment.

These findings have important implications for the surgical management of neuropathic groin pain and may be applicable to neuropathic pain in other anatomical regions. Surgeons should recognize that successful treatment of a dominant peripheral pain generator may reveal additional neuropathic sources within overlapping sensory territories of the lumbar plexus. Comprehensive preoperative evaluations that include detailed pain mapping, nerve-specific physical examinations, and targeted diagnostic nerve blocks may help identify multiple contributing nerves before surgery. Although experienced peripheral nerve surgeons performed meticulous preoperative evaluations in this cohort, unmasking still occurred. Counseling patients about the possibility of unmasked pain is important for informed consent, establishing realistic postoperative expectations, and maintaining patient satisfaction.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design. The relatively small number of patients who developed unmasked pain and unequal cohort sizes limited statistical power and produced wide confidence intervals in multivariate analyses. Prospective validation is therefore needed. Among the surgical patients, 273 had at least 6 months of follow-up, whereas 130 had more than 12 months of follow-up. Of those with more than 12 months of follow-up, 15 developed unmasked pain and 115 remained pain-free. Long-term follow-up was difficult to obtain for patients whose pain resolved during the early postoperative period because many had little incentive to return. In addition, neuropathic pain is influenced by complex peripheral and central mechanisms that cannot be fully captured using retrospective clinical data.

Advanced strategies, such as sequential selective nerve blocks during the same evaluation visit, high-resolution ultrasonography, and improved sensory mapping, may better identify concomitant nerve pathology in adjacent areas. In our current practice, the IIN and IHN are blocked first, regardless of whether GBGFN involvement is suspected. The GBGFN is then reexamined and assessed separately to determine whether pain or tenderness persists. If pain resolves after the IIN and IHN are blocked, only these nerves are excised. Some patients may subsequently require GBGFN excision. Because these patients often experience substantial pain relief after IIN and IHN blocks, pain signals from the GBGFN may not initially be perceived. In patients with persistent pain after IIN and IHN excision, the GBGFN may later be blocked, evaluated, and excised. A similar approach is used for the LFN. Overlapping sensory territories may reduce block specificity; consequently, an abnormality in a secondary nerve may remain clinically masked until after definitive treatment.

Conclusions

Neuropathic groin pain was unmasked following 6.5% of surgical neurectomy procedures. Lower BMI; preoperative sharp pain; tingling and/or burning in the scrotum, testicle, or labia; and higher initial NRS scores for the “lowest pain within the last month” predicted greater odds of reoperation for unmasked pain. In contrast, initial neurectomy of the GBGFN and muscle resection/transposition predicted lower odds of reoperation. These findings support meticulous diagnostic evaluation and a low threshold for considering additional concomitant neurectomy; they may also improve informed consent discussions, reduce reoperations, and improve outcomes.
