Introduction

The human brain exhibits rich and complex electromagnetic signals, which include a variety of rhythmic patterns known as neural oscillations. These are integral to various cognitive processes [1], as well as perceptual processes, such as the processing of auditory stimuli [2–4]. These oscillations represent a complex interplay of frequencies, where harmonicity emerges as a key organizing principle [5–7]. Understanding this harmonic structure can yield profound insights into the brain functionality, revealing a unique perspective on the neural correlates of cognitive processes [8–10]. These oscillatory harmonic patterns have recently been associated with cognitive functions such as semantic memory integration [11], as well as auditory attentional capture and stream segregation [12]. Recent work has also demonstrated that harmonic phase coupling can link physiological rhythms such as respiration to neural oscillations measured by electroencephalography (EEG), revealing new avenues for exploring brain–body integration [13]. Furthermore, decomposing brain dynamics into harmonic modes has revealed distinct spatiotemporal reorganizations during altered states of consciousness [14, 15], suggesting the importance of harmonic frameworks—including recent advances in complex harmonics decomposition [16]—for understanding neural mechanisms underlying higher-order cognitive phenomena.

We propose that music theory offers a valuable framework for applying its principles to the processing of biosignals. Models of harmony and rhythm can be useful for both artistic creation and for uncovering significant features in biosignals, enhancing their scientific study and interpretation [17, 18]. They offer novel methods to interpret the intricate patterns and structures within biosignals. For instance, the concept of consonance and dissonance in music may offer a compelling framework for understanding the harmonic relationships in brain oscillations. Consonance, or harmonicity, refers to the pleasantness of a musical interval, often measured through the simplicity of integer ratios. Dissonance corresponds to the opposite concept. These principles could potentially capture or characterize meaningful patterns in neural activity, raising the question of whether specific harmonic structures in oscillations correspond to distinct functional states or cognitive processes. Specifically, Biotuner allows for the automated extraction and quantification of harmonic templates (integer ratios, consonance/dissonance) in physiological signals, enabling new lines of inquiry into the neural basis of harmonicity, cross-modal oscillatory coupling, and the dynamics of brain–body integration across diverse contexts.

Emerging at this crossroads of neuroscience and music theory, the Biotuner Toolbox is an innovative python toolbox designed to harness the principles of music theory for the analysis of biosignals. The underlying philosophy of the Biotuner is that the theory of musical harmony provides a distinctive lens through which to view tension-resolution patterns in any dynamical system. For neuroscientists, Biotuner offers a gateway to explore novel signal processing methodologies inspired from music theory. It allows for the extraction of harmonic components, the computation of rhythmic structures, and the application of harmonicity metrics to biosignals, thereby enabling a deeper insight into their harmonic nature. Conversely, for artists and musicians, Biotuner serves as a tool for inspiration and creative exploration, enabling intuitive auditory perception of natural phenomena. By translating biological rhythms and patterns into musical elements, it provides a unique source for composition and sound art. Beyond a technical resource, Biotuner is a platform for exploration and discovery. This symbiotic relationship between science and art fosters creativity and enriches our understanding of both fields.

This toolbox paper is organized into four key sections. We begin with a Related Work section, providing a comparison with existing toolboxes for neural, audio, and music-theoretic signal analysis, and highlighting Biotuner’s unique contributions. The Design and Implementation section presents the Biotuner Object, the central component of the toolbox, which facilitates harmonic analysis by integrating various methods for extracting peaks and computing harmonicity metrics. Additionally, higher-level objects are presented to compute transitional harmony, assess harmonic connectivity between signals, and analyze the harmonic spectrum. The Results section demonstrates the toolbox’s capabilities—frequency peak extraction, harmonicity metrics, transitional harmony, and harmonic connectivit—using both simulated EEG and electrocardiography (ECG) data, as well as real EEG recordings. Lastly, the Practical Applications section showcases how Biotuner can be applied across various domains, including cognitive neuroscience, biosignal sonification, and the interpretation of both human and non-human biological data.

Related work

A variety of open-source toolboxes exist for the analysis of neural and physiological signals, each with distinct methodological approaches and target applications. Here, we compare Biotuner with the most relevant existing platforms (see Table 1). NeuroDSP is a Python library designed for rhythm and oscillatory analysis in neural time series. Notably, methods such as sliding_window_matching and compute_lagged_coherence are used for detecting recurring temporal motifs and quantifying phase or rhythmic consistency across frequencies [19]. These tools are powerful for characterizing temporal rhythmicity and phase dynamics. However, they do not analyze the relationships between spectral peaks in terms of musical intervals or harmonic structure. Librosa is a popular Python toolkit for music information retrieval and general audio analysis. It provides a comprehensive suite of functions for extracting spectral, temporal, and rhythmic features, including peak detection and onset analysis [18]. While Librosa excels at audio feature extraction, it does not quantify the harmonic relationships between spectral peaks (such as consonance or dissonance), nor is it tailored for the analysis of biosignals. FOOOF (Fitting Oscillations and One Over F) is a specialized toolbox for separating periodic (oscillatory) and aperiodic components of neural power spectra, enabling robust detection of spectral peaks [20]. FOOOF has become a standard for analyzing neural oscillations in EEG and related signals. However, it does not provide tools for assessing the harmonic or music-theoretic relationships between frequency components. Importantly, Biotuner integrates FOOOF as one of its peak extraction methods, allowing users to benefit from FOOOF’s robustness while extending the analysis to the quantification of harmonic structure. Pytuning is a Python library for scale construction and musical interval analysis, providing several consonance metrics (e.g., Tenney height, Euler Gradus Suavitatis) and custom tuning systems. Biotuner incorporates these consonance metrics but extends beyond pytuning by enabling direct extraction of spectral peaks from biosignals and adding new methods such as the dissonance curve [21] for scale construction and the subharmonic tension metric [22].

While NeuroDSP and Librosa offer extensive features for time-series and audio analysis, and FOOOF provides state-of-the-art spectral peak identification, none of these toolkits extract or quantify harmonic templates—integer ratios, musical consonance/dissonance—within physiological signals. Pytuning, while offering core consonance metrics and scale construction tools from music theory, is not designed for biosignal analysis or automated feature extraction from physiological data. Overall, existing open-source packages focus on either rhythmicity, generic audio feature extraction, or robust peak detection, but none perform automated extraction and quantification of harmonic templates from physiological data. Thus, conventional performance metrics (e.g., accuracy, run time, sensitivity) are not directly comparable. A major challenge in developing Biotuner was to integrate diverse signal processing approaches and theoretical perspectives—spanning neuroscience, music theory, and audio analysis—into a single cohesive framework. Additionally, we aimed to minimize software dependencies to ensure broad accessibility, while strategically leveraging the robustness of existing toolkits where appropriate, given the highly interdisciplinary scope of the project. Biotuner uniquely bridges these domains by directly extracting spectral peaks from biosignals and applying advanced music-theoretic metrics. It further expands on these metrics by introducing harmonic connectivity, time-resolved harmonicity, and harmonic spectrum analyses—features not provided by any other existing toolbox. This enables novel insights into latent musical structure and harmonic self-organization in physiological signals, opening new possibilities for both scientific and creative exploration.

Design and implementation

Biotuner object

At the heart of the Biotuner is the biotuner_object, which interfaces with various modules for comprehensive harmonic signal analysis (see Fig. 1). It orchestrates the execution of methods for peak extraction, peak extension, metrics computation, time-resolved harmonicity, tuning construction, and rhythm construction, thereby serving as a central hub for the toolbox’s functionality. This object is specifically engineered to process single time series data and is versatile enough for applications in various fields, such as electrophysiology (including ECG, EEG, PPG, organoid, and plants), audio data analysis, seismography, and the study of gravitational waves.

Architecture of the Biotuner Object. The Biotuner Object can process a diverse range of input types. Invoking the peaks_extraction method enables subsequent calls to the peaks_extension, peaks_metrics, and family of scale_construction methods. The time_resolved_harmonicity method can be directly executed, producing a time series of harmonicity values along with spectral chords, which correspond to moments of heightened consonance

Peaks extraction

Brain signals, such as those measured by EEG, reflect the collective electrical activity of large populations of neurons. These signals are composed of various oscillations, each with its own frequency, amplitude, and phase. Peaks in these signals represent points of maximum amplitude in a particular frequency band and are indicative of synchronous neural activity. Understanding these peaks is crucial as their relative proportions often correlate with specific cognitive and perceptual processes. For instance, the frequency ratios between brain spectral peaks typically exhibit a doubling progression during alert wakefulness, with each step increasing by a factor of two raised to the power of some integer, whereas these frequency ratios become irrational during sleep [23]. By analyzing the patterns and distributions of these peaks, we can not only gain insights into how their organization is associated with specific cognitive states, but also derive bio-inspired harmonic structures that could be used for musical composition. The Biotuner implements a variety of peak extraction approaches (see Sect. 2 of supplementary material), each tailored to identify significant frequency components from complex time-series data.

Peaks extension

The peaks_extension method is designed to expand an initial set of peaks identified by the peaks_extraction method. By calculating the harmonics and subharmonics of the frequency peaks, new ones that reflect the shared harmonicity between peaks can be identified. This method facilitates the expansion of a limited set of frequencies into a more comprehensive collection by examining their inter-harmonic concordance. Its utility lies in the creation of complex tunings, where the number of steps of the tuning are dependent on the number of peaks derived from the original signal (see Sect. 3.2. of supplementary material).

Harmonicity metrics

The Biotuner features an array of metrics that quantify the harmonic properties of biosignals based on the peaks extracted from the time-series data. Harmonicity, also known as consonance, refers to the pleasantness of musical intervals, which can be assessed through various methods, often by examining the simplicity of their integer ratios [24]. The primary use of these metrics is to offer novel features for the scientific analysis of biosignals. Additionally, they can be applied to tunings and rhythms derived from spectral peaks, offering a way to quantify their inherent harmonicity. Another application of these metrics is in evaluating consonance over time, which can aid in identifying specific sections of a signal suitable for music generation.

Consonance

The consonance value is computed by adding the numerator and denominator of a frequency ratio and dividing its sum by the product of the two (see Equation 1).

Harmonic similarity

Harmonic Similarity is calculated by averaging the sum of the ratio differences (xi + yi − 1) / (xi * yi) for a set of i = 1 to N frequency pairs (xi, yi), multiplied by 100 (see Equation 2), which quantifies the similarity between a dyad of frequencies and their natural harmonic series based on their greatest common divisor, with simpler integer ratios yielding higher values of harmonic similarity and therefore of consonance [25].

Tenney height

The Tenney height is a mathematical concept used to quantify the consonance or dissonance of musical intervals, chords, or sets of pitches. It is based on the psychoacoustic principle that simpler ratios between frequencies are perceived as more consonant. Higher values indicate increased perceptual dissonance. The Tenney Height computation involves two approaches:

Pairwise Tenney height

This approach calculates the Tenney height for pairs of frequencies by finding the simplest form of their ratio and applying a logarithmic function to the inverse product of the numerators and denominators (see Equation 3). This method effectively weights simpler ratios more heavily, reflecting the psychoacoustic perception that simpler ratios tend to be more consonant [26].

Integrated Tenney height

This extended form of the Tenney height calculation considers a set of frequencies as a whole, rather than in pairs. It integrates the prime factors across all the frequencies in the set, applying a logarithmic transformation to the multiplication of these factors (see Equation 4). This integrated approach provides a more comprehensive measure of consonance for complex chords, where the interplay of multiple frequencies is essential to the perceived harmonicity.

Euler gradus suavitatis

The Euler algorithm [27] calculates the degree of dissonance for a given set of frequencies by first converting them to their simplest form, finding the least common multiple (LCM) of the denominators, and then determining the prime factors of this LCM. The “degree of sweetness” is then calculated by summing the primes, subtracting one from each, and adding one to the total sum (see Equation 5). A higher Euler value suggests greater dissonance, while a lower value suggests greater consonance.

Subharmonic tension

The subharmonic tension metric calculates the degree of dissonance introduced by subharmonic frequencies [22], which correspond to sequences of frequencies that result from inverting the intervals of the harmonic (overtone) series.

The Subharmonic Tension (ST) metric takes into account the temporal discrepancies between the occurrences of subharmonic frequencies in a signal (see Equation 6). It is calculated by summing the absolute differences in time (Δ), measured in milliseconds (ms), between adjacent subharmonics. Each of these differences is then divided by the corresponding subharmonic frequency value (Subi), and the results are averaged over N subharmonics. This average represents the degree of temporal irregularity or ‘tension’ due to the subharmonics. High ST values indicate poor alignment between the subharmonics of the set of frequencies, leading to increased perceived dissonance.

Each metric offers a different perspective on the signal’s harmonic content, some relying on mathematical purity of intervals, alignment with the harmonic series, or the presence of tension due to subharmonic discrepancies. The complementary nature of these metrics is illustrated by their varying distribution across 100 steps from unison to octave (refer to Fig. 2), and by the correlation matrices with Pearson r values ranging from 0.23 to 0.91 (see Fig. 3). Importantly, for the Tenney height, subharmonic tension and Euler metrics, higher values correspond to increased dissonance, clarifying the reason behind the mix of positive and negative correlations. The correlation matrices also highlight that the relationships between different harmonicity metrics are generally consistent when comparing pairs and triplets of peaks. However, an exception is noted in the case of harmonic similarity and consonance, which exhibit a high correlation for pairs of peaks but a lesser correlation for triplets.

Integration with MNE-python

Harmonicity metrics can be computed directly on MNE epochs objects [28], which allows to easily analyze EEG data and compare harmonicity across different conditions. MNE is a widely used open-source Python toolbox for EEG/MEG analysis, providing standardized data structures and preprocessing pipelines that simplify integration with other methods. For that purpose, the biotuner_mne function of the biotuner2d module can be used. To evaluate the harmonic properties of EEG data, we first segment the continuous recording into short epochs using fixed-length event markers. For each epoch, we apply Biotuner’s peak extraction algorithm (e.g., EMD) to identify salient frequency components. Harmonicity metrics are then computed for each set of detected peaks, quantifying the degree of harmonic organization present in the neural signal. The resulting metrics provide a time-resolved measure of harmonicity, facilitating comparison across experimental conditions or subject states. Full implementation details and example code are provided in the online documentation.

Time-resolved harmonicity

The time_resolved_harmonicity method tracks how consonance and dissonance change over time, making it particularly useful for signals with varying harmonic content. This method uses Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD), a technique that decomposes a signal into simpler components called Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs), each representing a specific frequency range. Harmonic relationships are assessed using two complementary approaches. The first involves extracting instantaneous frequencies (IFs) from the IMFs using the Hilbert Transform [29], allowing detailed tracking of harmonicity at each moment in time. The second approach calculates the spectral centroid, or the “center of mass” of each IMF’s frequency content, to quantify harmonic relationships dynamically [30]. By applying the harmonicity metrics introduced earlier, this method provides a detailed, time-resolved view of how harmonic patterns evolve within a signal (see Fig. 4).

Computing harmonicity across time, space and spectrum

Transitional harmony object

Transitional harmony, as implemented in the transitional_harmony class, offers a novel approach to understanding the dynamic evolution of harmonic structures within a time series, making use of the Biotuner Object within a higher-level programming structure. Leveraging subharmonic progressions, this method evaluates the tension between successive temporal windows of data. By extracting subharmonics of spectral peaks and evaluating the closest common subharmonics in consecutive timeframes, transitional subharmonic tension can be quantified [22]. In contrast to stationary harmony which focuses on the properties of individual chords or spectral peaks, transitional harmony provides a computational framework for analyzing the movement and relationship between chords over time. In the context of music, transitional harmony allows for a deeper exploration of chord progressions, enhancing our understanding of how compositions achieve emotional impact through tension and resolution [22]. Here, we demonstrate its application to the analysis of biosignals, suggesting its utility in detecting subtle changes in biological rhythms. This application may enhance our ability to interpret transitions in physiological states.

The methodology offers two distinct modes: one employs overlapping windows with peak extraction for each segment, while the other utilizes instantaneous frequencies (see time-resolved harmonicity subsection) to gauge time-resolved transitional harmony. The flexibility in window overlap and the precision of peak analysis facilitate a granular examination of harmonic relationships.

Harmonic connectivity object

The harmonic_connectivity class extends the application of harmonic analysis to explore the relationships between multiple time series, typically originating from different sensors or electrodes. In neuroscience, understanding network dynamics through connectivity is increasingly seen as key to unraveling complex brain functions [31–33]. This class, also building on the Biotuner Object, offers new connectivity metrics specifically designed for assessing harmonicity between sensors or any set of signals and incorporates previously discussed peaks metrics within a multivariate context.

Rhythmic ratio coupling with imaginary component (RRCi)

This metric provides insights into the temporal coupling between signals by examining the imaginary components of the complex phase relationships at each pair’s peak frequencies. Lower values indicate less complex phase-coupling relationships.

Equation 7 computes the coupling index by calculating the average magnitude of the imaginary component of the complex exponential phase differences between two signals. These phase differences are weighted by their respective rhythmic ratio components, n and m, and averaged across all time points t = 1, …, N, where N denotes the total number of time points. The instantaneous phases of the signals at frequencies f1 and f2 are represented by 1(t) and 2(t), respectively. This approach extends the capabilities of phase-based connectivity analyses by facilitating the examination of phase coupling between non-equivalent frequencies, thereby providing a more detailed understanding of complex interactions in oscillatory networks.

Weighted phase lag index for Cross-Frequency (wPLI_crossfreq)

This phase synchronization metric assesses the phase relationships between different frequency components of two time series. It is an extension of the classical weighted phase lag index [34, 35], a measure that quantifies the consistency of phase differences between signals, with the particularity of being computed between different frequency bands. Similar to RRCi, this metric offers the advantage of tackling polyrhythmic n: m phase-coupling.

In addition to these newly developed metrics, the harmonic_connectivity class can apply all the peaks metrics discussed earlier, such as harmonic_similarity, euler, consonance, tenney, and subharmonic_tension, to the context of multivariate data. These metrics, initially described for individual time series, are adapted to compute the interrelationships between pairs of time series.

Harmonic spectrum object

The harmonic spectrum is a module of the Biotuner that provides a way to assess the harmonic weight of each frequency in a signal, taking into account both its harmonic relationships with other frequencies and its power contribution to the overall spectrum.

The Eq. 8 for the harmonic spectrum determines the harmonicity of each frequency, f, within a signal by considering both its power and its harmonic relationships with other frequencies. To calculate this, for a given frequency, f, the equation sums the dyadic similarity, DyadSim(f, fi), between frequency f and each different frequency fi, and then multiplies this by the power of frequency f and the power of frequency fi. This process is repeated for all unique frequency pairs where f is not equal to fi. The value of H(f) is normalized by dividing it by the total power. The outcome, the harmonic spectrum, can be used to identify significant spectral peaks, and calculate various metrics like averaged harmonicity, spectral flatness, and spectral entropy, giving a nuanced view of the signal’s harmonic complexity. Results

In this section, we present the application of the Biotuner Toolbox across various biosignals, specifically focusing on both real-world and simulated EEG (electroencephalogram) and ECG (electrocardiogram) data. The toolbox’s capabilities are demonstrated through several key analyses: peak extraction, harmonicity metrics, transitional harmony and harmonic connectivity. These methods are applied to both EEG and ECG data under different conditions, including sleep stages and varying levels of noise and aperiodic components, to illustrate the robustness and versatility of the toolbox in handling complex biosignals. Throughout the results, the harmonic and non-harmonic simulated signals are constructed using the following frequencies: 2, 6, 12, and 24 Hz for harmonic signals, and 2.4, 5.8, 13, and 23.1 Hz for non-harmonic signals.

As the noise level increases (SNR decreases), performance diverges: the EMD method begins to decline around − 10 dB, and FOOOF shows a significant drop in performance shortly thereafter, indicating reduced capacity to identify peaks amidst noise. The harmonic_recurrence method and fixed bands maintain robust performance across noise levels, particularly in harmonic signals. At very low SNRs (below − 20 dB), the performance of all methods decreases, reflecting a general limitation in accurately detecting peaks under extreme noise conditions. These results illustrate that while some methods (e.g., harmonic_recurrence) are more resilient to noise, all tested methods are effective in extracting oscillatory peaks within practical noise ranges, confirming their reliability for biosignal analysis.

Harmonicity metrics on real-world and simulated EEG data

We quantified the differences in harmonicity metrics between harmonic and non-harmonic signals across various 1/f slope levels of simulated EEG signals across 100 bootstraps (see Fig. 7). As expected, most metrics, including consonance, harmonic similarity, and euler, effectively distinguish between harmonic and non-harmonic signals across the whole range of slopes, with harmonic signals consistently showing higher harmonicity values. However, two metrics—tenney and subharmonic tension—do not differentiate between the two types of signals at slopes around − 1.25, which is close to pink noise. This finding suggests that these particular metrics may be less sensitive to harmonic differences when the background noise structure approximates pink noise, highlighting the importance of considering the underlying noise profile when interpreting harmonicity metrics.

For musical signals, a sequence following the I-IV-V-I progression was created using the chords ‘C’, ‘F’, and ‘G’. Transitional harmony was calculated across one-second windows with a substantial 90% overlap, enabling detailed temporal analysis. This analysis reveals the nuanced interplay between heart rate variability and transitional harmony, offering insights into the rhythmic patterns that underlie cardiac function. The visualization demonstrates that variations in heart rate are captured by transitional harmony metrics. As expected, the average transitional subharmonic tension is notably higher for ECG signals with greater heart rate variability. Harmonic connectivity

The analysis of connectivity metrics between harmonic-harmonic (H-H) and harmonic-nonharmonic (H-NH) signal pairs in simulated EEG data, generated with a power law slope of -1, segments duration of 5 s, and across 100 bootstraps, reveals significant differences across all metrics (see Fig. 11). Specifically, the subharm_tension and RRCi metrics demonstrated increased tension and decreased coupling, respectively, for H-NH pairs compared to H-H pairs, as expected (p < 0.001). Consistently, the harmsim metric did display a significant difference between the conditions (p < 0.001) with higher harmonicity for the H-H connectivity analysis. This indicates that these metrics effectively capture the increased complexity of harmonic and phase coupling patterns tension introduced by nonharmonic interactions. Interestingly, the wPLI_crossfreq metric did show a statistically significant difference between harmonic-harmonic and harmonic-nonharmonic pairs (p < 0.001), suggesting that wPLI might also be sensitive to the types of cross-frequency interactions present in these specific conditions. The increased wPLI for harmonic-nonharmonic pairs could be explained by the potential for more complex and varied phase relationships that arise when harmonic and nonharmonic frequencies interact. This suggests that wPLI might be sensitive to these specific types of cross-frequency interactions, although further investigation is necessary to confirm this interpretation.

Availability and future directions

Practical applications

The Biotuner Toolbox, with its novel approach of integrating music theory and neurophysiological signal processing, presents a diverse range of applications across fields. These applications could enhance scientific research, while extending into artistic and creative realms. Its functionality encompasses the analysis of human-originated signals, including electrocardiogram (ECG), electromyogram (EMG), and electroencephalogram (EEG), providing detailed insights into cardiac, muscular, and neural activities, respectively. Beyond human data, it can be used to process biosignals from non-human entities, such as the electrophysiological patterns observed in plants and mycelium networks. These capabilities allow for an in-depth exploration of both human and non-human biological processes, enabling systematic comparisons of harmonic organization across human, animal, and plant electrophysiological systems. Its versatility could extend to interpreting geophysical and astrophysical data, including seismic, anemometric, and gravitational wave information. The Biotuner’s capacity to analyze and sonify these diverse sources of data transforms complex natural phenomena into auditory experiences, making them accessible for educational, engagement, and creative purposes. In the realm of oscillatory coupling, the Biotuner could offer insights into the interconnectedness of various physiological systems within an individual, such as heart-brain synchrony. This understanding has significant implications for comprehending health and behavioral patterns. Moreover, the Biotuner’s application in analyzing harmonic interactions between individuals opens new possibilities for probing social dynamics and collective behaviors, not only in humans but potentially in other species as well. Another crucial application lies in extending the methodologies of cognitive neuroscience. It introduces new harmonicity metrics that provide a fresh lens through which various states of consciousness can be examined, offering new insights into brain function during different cognitive and emotional states. Furthermore, these metrics can be correlated with phenomenological measures, paving the way for a deeper understanding of the neural basis of subjective experiences.

Conclusion

The Biotuner Toolbox, an innovative open-source Python toolbox, embodies the intersection of neuroscience, music theory, and signal processing. Its core functionality lies in its ability to apply musical concepts such as harmony, rhythm, and scale construction to the analysis of biosignals. This unique approach offers a fresh perspective on the study of brain oscillations and their harmonic properties. The toolbox’s structure is meticulously designed to handle various signal types, from neural recordings to environmental and physiological data, through its versatile biotuner_object. The Biotuner Toolbox exemplifies the significance of transdisciplinary work in fostering innovation and generating new research fields. By integrating principles from music theory into neuroscience, it opens untapped possibilities for analyzing and interpreting complex biosignals. This integration not only enhances scientific research but also extends its utility to artistic realms, enabling creators to translate natural rhythms and patterns into musical compositions. Such a cross-disciplinary approach is crucial for driving forward both academic and creative explorations, broadening the horizons of how we perceive and interact with the world around us.
