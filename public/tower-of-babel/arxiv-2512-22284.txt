On Fibonacci Ensembles An Alternative Approach to Ensemble Learning Inspired by the Timeless Architecture of the Golden Ratio
Ernest Fokoué

Abstract
Nature rarely reveals her secrets bluntly, yet in the Fibonacci sequence she grants us a glimpse of her quiet architecture of growth, harmony, and recursive stability ( 12 ; 13 ) . From spiral galaxies to the unfolding of leaves, this humble sequence reflects a universal grammar of balance. In this work, we introduce Fibonacci Ensembles , a mathematically principled yet philosophically inspired framework for ensemble learning that complements and extends classical aggregation schemes such as bagging, boosting, and random forests ( 4 ; 6 ; 8 ; 21 ; 10 ) . Two intertwined formulations unfold: (1) the use of normalized Fibonacci weights—tempered through orthogonalization and Rao–Blackwell optimization—to achieve systematic variance reduction among base learners, and (2) a second-order recursive ensemble dynamic that mirrors the Fibonacci flow itself, enriching representational depth beyond classical boosting. The manuscript then expands to a broader General Weighting Theory — a unifying architecture for ensemble learning in which aggregation is viewed as a distributional operator acting on the dictionary of learners. This perspective places bagging, boosting, stacking, SuperLearner, geometric and heavy-tailed weightings, and Fibonacci ensembles under a single mathematical umbrella. The resulting methodology is at once rigorous and poetic: a reminder that learning systems flourish when guided by the same intrinsic harmonies that shape the natural world. Through controlled one-dimensional regression experiments using both random Fourier feature ensembles ( 14 ) and polynomial ensembles, we exhibit regimes in which Fibonacci weighting matches or improves upon uniform averaging and interacts in a principled way with orthogonal Rao–Blackwellization. These findings suggest that Fibonacci ensembles form a natural and interpretable design point within the broader theory of ensemble learning.

2
Mathematical Foundations of Fibonacci-Weighted Ensembles

2.1
The Geometry and Intent of Fibonacci Weighting

Let
h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{M}
be base learners belonging to a real Hilbert space

\mathscr{H}\subseteq L^{2}(P_{X})
equipped with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(X)].

The Fibonacci sequence
(F_{m})_{m\geq 1}
is defined by

F_{1}=1,\qquad F_{2}=1,\qquad F_{m}=F_{m-1}+F_{m-2}.

Definition 2.1

(Fibonacci Weighting)
.

Let

\alpha_{m}=\frac{F_{m}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}F_{j}}.

The
Fibonacci-weighted ensemble
is

\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib}(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}h_{m}(x).

The exponential growth of
F_{m}
at rate
\varphi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
yields

\alpha_{m}\propto\varphi^{\,m},

but normalization tempers this, creating a bias–variance tradeoff that places more trust in earlier, typically more stable learners while allowing later learners a nontrivial but controlled influence.

This echoes the intuition from natural growth:

the sequence remembers its beginnings even as it expands.

2.2
Variance Structure of Fibonacci Ensembles

Let
\sigma_{m}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(h_{m}(X))
and
\rho_{m,m^{\prime}}
be pairwise correlations. Then

\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib}(X)\right)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}^{2}\sigma_{m}^{2}+2\sum_{m<m^{\prime}}\alpha_{m}\alpha_{m^{\prime}}\rho_{m,m^{\prime}}\sigma_{m}\sigma_{m^{\prime}}.

In this form, variance reduction is clearly driven by:

1.

The decay pattern of
\alpha_{m}^{2}
, and

2.

The suppression (or elimination) of
\rho_{m,m^{\prime}}
through orthogonalization.

We now formalize these intuitions.

2.3
Orthogonal Decomposition of Base Learners

Define the Gram matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
with entries

G_{m,m^{\prime}}=\langle h_{m},h_{m^{\prime}}\rangle.

Let
H=(h_{1},\dots,h_{M})
viewed as a vector in
\mathscr{H}^{M}
.

Definition 2.2

(Orthogonalization Operator)
.

Define
\mathcal{O}:\mathscr{H}^{M}\to\mathscr{H}^{M}
by

(h_{1}^{\perp},\dots,h_{M}^{\perp})^{\top}=\mathcal{O}(H)=G^{-1/2}H,

whenever
G
is invertible.

Thus,

\langle h_{m}^{\perp},h_{m^{\prime}}^{\perp}\rangle=\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}.

This operator embodies the Rao–Blackwell spirit:

reduce variance by conditioning on orthogonalized projections

(
15
;
2
)
.

2.4
Variance Dominance via Fibonacci Weights

We compare Fibonacci weights to uniform weights
\beta_{m}=1/M
.

Theorem 2.1

(Fibonacci Variance Dominance)
.

Suppose the learners are orthogonalized so that
\rho_{m,m^{\prime}}=0
for all
m\neq m^{\prime}
.
Assume also that the variance sequence
(\sigma_{m}^{2})
is nondecreasing:

\sigma_{1}^{2}\leq\sigma_{2}^{2}\leq\cdots\leq\sigma_{M}^{2}.

Then

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib})\;\leq\;\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{f}_{\rm unif}).

Proof.

With orthogonalization,

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{f})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}^{2}\sigma_{m}^{2}.

We compare

V_{\rm Fib}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}^{2}\sigma_{m}^{2},\qquad V_{\rm unif}=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sigma_{m}^{2}.

Since
\alpha_{m}
increases with
m
but
\sigma_{m}^{2}
also increases, the key comparison uses discrete Chebyshev inequalities. Because both sequences are increasing,

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}^{2}\sigma_{m}^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sigma_{m}^{2}\right).

But

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}^{2}=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}F_{m}^{2}}{\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}F_{m}\right)^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{M},

as
F_{m}
grows geometrically, so that

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}^{2}\leq c_{\rm var}\,\frac{1}{M}

for some
c_{\rm var}\in(0,1)
once
M
is moderately large. Hence

V_{\rm Fib}\leq c_{\rm var}\,\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sigma_{m}^{2}=c_{\rm var}\,\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{f}_{\rm unif}).

∎

This establishes that Fibonacci weighting is inherently stabilizing in the orthogonalized regime.

2.5
Approximation Theory and the Expanding Fibonacci Cone

The Fibonacci ensemble does more than merely aggregate learners; it generates a

geometrically expanding cone
in the hypothesis space. The golden ratio

\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}

governs not only the magnitude of weights but also the rate at which the ensemble expands its capacity
(
12
;
13
)
.

Definition 2.3

(Fibonacci Conic Hull)
.

The
Fibonacci conic hull
of the learners is

\mathrm{FibCone}(h_{1},\dots,h_{M})=\left\{\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}h_{m}:c_{m}\geq 0,\ \frac{c_{m+1}}{c_{m}}\leq\varphi+o(1)\right\}.

This cone strictly contains the convex hull used by bagging and simple averaging schemes
(
4
;
21
)
and the additive span used by boosting
(
8
)
.

Golden Asymptotics

The Fibonacci sequence satisfies

F_{m}=\frac{\varphi^{m}-(1-\varphi)^{m}}{\sqrt{5}}=\Theta(\varphi^{m}),

yielding

\alpha_{m}=\frac{\varphi^{m}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\varphi^{j}}=\frac{\varphi^{m-M}}{\varphi-\varphi^{-M}}.

Thus the last few learners have exponentially larger influence, but normalization keeps the ensemble stable.

Lemma 2.1

(Golden Asymptotic Equidistribution)
.

Let
m=M-k
for fixed
k
as
M\to\infty
. Then

\alpha_{M-k}\to\varphi^{-k-1}.

Proof.

Immediate from the geometric expansion of
\varphi^{m}
and the closed form of the finite geometric sum.
∎

This proves that Fibonacci ensembles create an exponentially weighted tail, giving the ensemble a powerful ability to model sharp nonlinearities while retaining control on total mass.

2.6
Expressive Advantage Over Classical Ensembles

Theorem 2.2

(Fibonacci Conic Expansion Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\rm unif}
be the convex hull of
\{h_{m}\}
and let
\mathcal{H}_{\rm Fib}
be the Fibonacci-generated hypothesis class. Then

\mathcal{H}_{\rm unif}\;\subsetneq\;\mathrm{span}_{\varphi}\{h_{m}\}\;\subseteq\;\mathcal{H}_{\rm Fib},

where the middle class is the weighted span with geometric constraints.
Thus
Fibonacci ensembles are strictly more expressive than convex combinations.

Proof.

Convex combinations require
\sum c_{m}=1
and
c_{m}\geq 0
.
Fibonacci conic combinations allow exponentially scaling factors controlled by
\varphi
.
Thus the latter relax the convexity constraint and expand the reachable function space while maintaining bounded growth through the geometric constraint.
∎

This theorem shows that even without recursion, Fibonacci weighting enlarges approximation power in a disciplined way.

3
Fibonacci Recursive Dynamics in Ensemble Learning

3.1
The Second-Order Ensemble Flow

Classical boosting uses an essentially first-order recursion of the form

F_{m}=F_{m-1}+\Delta F_{m-1},

where
\Delta F_{m-1}
is a residual learner fitted at stage
m

(
8
)
.

We propose instead:

F_{m}=\beta F_{m-1}+\gamma F_{m-2}+\Delta F_{m-1},\qquad m\geq 3.

The term
\gamma F_{m-2}
introduces memory, just as natural systems never evolve from their immediate state alone—they evolve from their lineage.

Define the homogeneous update matrix:

A=\begin{pmatrix}\beta&\gamma\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

The characteristic polynomial is

\lambda^{2}-\beta\lambda-\gamma=0,

with roots

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}+4\gamma}}{2}.

Proposition 3.1

(Stability Region)
.

The recursion

F_{m}=\beta F_{m-1}+\gamma F_{m-2}+\Delta F_{m-1}

is stable in
L^{2}
if and only if

|\lambda_{\pm}|<1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\beta^{2}+4\gamma<4.

Proof.

The homogeneous part determines stability. The eigenvalues of
A
must lie inside the unit disk. This occurs exactly when

|\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}+4\gamma}|<2.

Algebraic manipulation of this condition yields the stated inequality.
∎

The inequality

\beta^{2}+4\gamma<4

describes a parabola opening upward: elegant, symmetric, and reminiscent of classical stability regions in discrete-time dynamical systems.

This second-order Fibonacci flow enhances representational richness beyond boosting by producing predictors lying in a Fibonacci-extended convex cone of the base learners.

3.2
Spectral Theory of the Ensemble Recursion Operator

The recursive update

F_{m}=\beta F_{m-1}+\gamma F_{m-2}+\Delta F_{m-1}

is governed by the linear operator

T=\begin{pmatrix}\beta&\gamma\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Let the eigenvalues be

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}+4\gamma}}{2}.

The recursion creates two expressive modes:

•

A
dominant exponential mode
with growth rate
|\lambda_{+}|
.

•

A
subdominant golden-mode echo
with
|\lambda_{-}|<|\lambda_{+}|
.

3.3
Stability Surface

The recursion is stable if

\rho(T)=\max(|\lambda_{+}|,|\lambda_{-}|)<1,

which is equivalent to

\beta^{2}+4\gamma<4.

Theorem 3.1

(Spectral Radius Bound for Learning Stability)
.

Let
T
be the Fibonacci recursion operator.
Then the ensemble trajectory
\{F_{m}\}
remains uniformly bounded in
\mathscr{H}
if and only if

\rho(T)<1.

Furthermore, near the boundary
\rho(T)=1
, the ensemble enters a
golden critical regime
where

F_{m}\approx C_{1}\lambda_{+}^{m}+C_{2}\lambda_{-}^{m},

with
\lambda_{+}\approx\varphi^{-1}
.

Proof.

Standard spectral analysis of linear recursions shows that boundedness is equivalent to
\rho(T)<1
. The asymptotic form of
F_{m}
follows from the Jordan decomposition of
T
. The golden-critical regime arises when
\lambda_{+}
approaches the inverse of the golden ratio, which occurs for parameter choices close to the Fibonacci case
(\beta,\gamma)=(1,1)
.
∎

3.4
Golden Ratio Dominant Mode Principle

In many natural systems, the golden ratio emerges as the stable limit of second-order recursions. Here too:

Proposition 3.2

(Golden Ratio Dominant Mode)
.

When
\beta=1
,
\gamma=1
, the recursion becomes exactly Fibonacci:

F_{m}=F_{m-1}+F_{m-2}.

Then

\frac{F_{m+1}}{F_{m}}\to\varphi\qquad\text{and}\qquad F_{m}\sim\frac{\varphi^{m}}{\sqrt{5}}.

This spectral fingerprint gives the ensemble a natural inductive bias toward smooth exponential expansions—a structure known to model complex nonlinearities efficiently.

4
Rao–Blackwellization and Orthogonalized Ensembles

4.1
Transforming Learners into Their Minimum-Variance Representatives

Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{M}
be arbitrary base learners, and define the sigma-algebra

\mathcal{S}=\sigma(h_{1}^{\perp},\dots,h_{M}^{\perp}),

generated by their orthogonal projections.

Theorem 4.1

(Rao–Blackwell Ensemble Improvement)
.

Let

\widehat{F}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}h_{m},\qquad\widehat{F}_{RB}=\mathbb{E}[\widehat{F}\mid\mathcal{S}].

Then

\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{F}_{RB}-f)^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{F}-f)^{2}]

with equality if and only if
h_{m}=h_{m}^{\perp}
almost surely for all
m
.

Proof.

This follows from the classical Rao–Blackwell theorem
(
15
;
2
)
. Let
Z=\widehat{F}(X)
and
\theta=f(X)
. Then

\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}[Z\mid\mathcal{S}]-\theta)^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[(Z-\theta)^{2}],

by Jensen’s inequality applied to conditional expectation. Because orthogonalization ensures
\mathcal{S}
captures only uncorrelated components, conditioning removes redundant variance contributed by correlated components.
∎

Thus, Fibonacci weighting plus orthogonalization yields an estimator that is:

•

variance-minimizing in its equivalence class,

•

inherently stable,

•

aligned with the deep principles of sufficiency and information projection.

4.2
Generalization Bounds and Concentration Inequalities

We now analyze the learning-theoretic complexity of Fibonacci ensembles.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be the hypothesis class and let
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}
denote the empirical Rademacher complexity
(
1
;
3
;
19
)
.

4.2.1
Rademacher Complexity of Fibonacci Ensembles

Because the ensemble is a weighted combination of learners
h_{m}
, we have

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm Fib})\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M}|\alpha_{m}|\,\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(h_{m}).

Since
\alpha_{m}
grows geometrically,

\alpha_{m}\approx K\varphi^{m-M},

the tail weights dominate.

Theorem 4.2

(Rademacher Bound for Fibonacci Ensembles)
.

Let
R_{m}=\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(h_{m})
.
Then

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm Fib})\leq K\sum_{m=1}^{M}\varphi^{m-M}R_{m}.

If
(R_{m})
is nonincreasing, then

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm Fib})\leq\frac{K}{1-\varphi^{-1}}R_{1}=\varphi KR_{1}.

This shows that Fibonacci ensembles do not explode in complexity;
they scale by a factor of exactly
\varphi
.
A rare moment where
mathematical beauty improves generalization
.

4.2.2
Gold-Stabilized Concentration Inequality

Theorem 4.3

(Golden Concentration)
.

Let
\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib}
be the Fibonacci ensemble predictor.
Assume a
1
-Lipschitz loss and bounded outputs. Then for any
\delta>0
,

\big|R(\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib})-\widehat{R}(\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib})\big|\leq 2\varphi KR_{1}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}},

with probability
1-\delta
.

This generalization bound has a remarkable feature:
the golden ratio appears as the
universal inflation constant
, echoing its role in the underlying conic geometry.

5
The Grand Theorem of Fibonacci Ensembles

In this section we gather the main structural properties of Fibonacci Ensembles
into a single unifying statement. The result expresses, under a coherent set
of assumptions, how variance reduction, expressive expansion, spectral stability,
and generalization control all emerge from one and the same Fibonacci-structured
learning operator.

5.1
The Fibonacci Ensemble Operator

We work in a real Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}\subseteq L^{2}(P_{X})
with inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(X)]
and norm
\|f\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[f(X)^{2}]
.
Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{M}\in\mathscr{H}
be base learners.

We consider two intertwined structures:

•

A
Fibonacci-weighted aggregation

\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib}(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}h_{m}(x),\qquad\alpha_{m}=\frac{F_{m}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}F_{j}},

where
(F_{m})
is the Fibonacci sequence.

•

A
second-order recursive flow
of ensemble predictors

F_{m}=\beta F_{m-1}+\gamma F_{m-2}+\Delta F_{m-1},\qquad m\geq 3,

where
\Delta F_{m-1}
is a residual learner (e.g., fit to current errors).

We encode the homogeneous part of the recursion via the linear operator

T=\begin{pmatrix}\beta&\gamma\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}+4\gamma}}{2}

its eigenvalues, and spectral radius
\rho(T)=\max(|\lambda_{+}|,|\lambda_{-}|)
.

Finally, let
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}
denote the empirical Rademacher complexity.

5.2
Assumptions

We collect the standing assumptions in a single list for clarity.

(A1) Orthogonalizability.

The learners
h_{1},\dots,h_{M}
admit an orthogonalization

h_{m}^{\perp}
in
\mathscr{H}
such that

\langle h_{m}^{\perp},h_{m^{\prime}}^{\perp}\rangle=0
for
m\neq m^{\prime}
.

(A2) Ordered Variance and Complexity.

The variances and Rademacher complexities are nonincreasing:

\mathrm{Var}(h_{1}^{\perp}(X))\leq\cdots\leq\mathrm{Var}(h_{M}^{\perp}(X)),\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(h_{1}^{\perp})\geq\cdots\geq\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(h_{M}^{\perp}).

(A3) Spectral Stability.

The recursion parameters satisfy

\rho(T)=\max(|\lambda_{+}|,|\lambda_{-}|)<1,

equivalently
\beta^{2}+4\gamma<4
, so that the homogeneous recursion is

L^{2}
-stable.

(A4) Lipschitz Loss and Bounded Outputs.

The loss
\ell(y,f(x))
is
1
-Lipschitz in
f(x)
and the learners satisfy

|h_{m}(x)|\leq B
almost surely for all
m
.

5.3
Grand Theorem

Theorem 5.1

(Grand Theorem of Fibonacci Ensembles)
.

Under assumptions
(A1)–(A4)
, the Fibonacci Ensemble framework
enjoys the following unified properties:

(i)

Variance Reduction (Rao–Blackwellized Fibonacci Aggregation).

Let

\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib}^{\perp}(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{m}h_{m}^{\perp}(x)

be the Fibonacci-weighted ensemble built from orthogonalized learners, and
let
\widehat{f}_{\rm unif}^{\perp}
be the uniform-weight orthogonalized
ensemble. Then there exists a constant
c_{\rm var}\in(0,1)
(depending only
on
M
and the Fibonacci sequence) such that

\mathrm{Var}\!\big(\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib}^{\perp}(X)\big)\;\leq\;c_{\rm var}\,\mathrm{Var}\!\big(\widehat{f}_{\rm unif}^{\perp}(X)\big),

and for any target function
f\in\mathscr{H}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib}^{\perp}(X)-f(X))^{2}\big]\leq\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{f}_{\rm unif}^{\perp}(X)-f(X))^{2}\big].

(ii)

Expressive Expansion (Fibonacci Conic Enlargement).

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\rm conv}
be the convex hull of
\{h_{m}^{\perp}\}
, and let

\mathcal{H}_{\rm Fib}
be the hypothesis class generated by all Fibonacci
flows built from
\{h_{m}^{\perp}\}
and their residuals. Then

\mathcal{H}_{\rm conv}\;\subsetneq\;\mathcal{H}_{\rm FibCone}\;\subseteq\;\mathcal{H}_{\rm Fib},

where
\mathcal{H}_{\rm FibCone}
is the Fibonacci conic hull defined by
geometrically constrained nonnegative combinations. Hence Fibonacci Ensembles
are strictly more expressive than classical convex combinations of base
learners, while remaining contained in a spectrally controlled cone.

(iii)

Spectral Mode Control and Stable Dynamics.

The recursive sequence
(F_{m})
decomposes into two modes corresponding to
the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}
of
T
:

F_{m}=u_{+}\lambda_{+}^{m}+u_{-}\lambda_{-}^{m}+\textnormal{(forced term)},

for some
u_{\pm}\in\mathscr{H}
, with
|\lambda_{\pm}|<1
. In particular:

•

The homogeneous trajectories are uniformly bounded in
\mathscr{H}
.

•

Near the boundary
\rho(T)\uparrow 1
, the dominant mode approaches a
golden-ratio regime with
\lambda_{+}\to\varphi^{-1}
, inducing a smooth,
critically damped evolution of the ensemble.

(iv)

Generalization Bounds with Golden Inflation Factor.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\rm Fib}
denote the class of Fibonacci ensembles built
from
\{h_{m}^{\perp}\}
with Fibonacci weights and stable recursion satisfying

(A3)
. Then the empirical Rademacher complexity satisfies

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm Fib})\;\leq\;C_{\varphi}\,\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(h_{1}^{\perp}),

where
C_{\varphi}=\varphi K
for a constant
K
depending only on the
normalized Fibonacci weights. Consequently, for any
\delta>0
, with
probability at least
1-\delta
,

\big|R(\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib})-\widehat{R}(\widehat{f}_{\rm Fib})\big|\;\leq\;2C_{\varphi}\,\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(h_{1}^{\perp})\;+\;\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}.

In words, under the Fibonacci operator framework, the same structural
constraints that enforce spectral stability also yield variance reduction,
strictly enhanced expressive power, and controlled generalization error.

Remark 5.1
.

The theorem expresses in rigorous form a simple philosophical intuition:
when a learning system is organized according to a harmonious recursive
architecture—here, the Fibonacci law and its golden-ratio asymptotics—
its statistical behavior in variance, approximation, dynamics, and
generalization all align under a single structural principle rather than
being tuned independently. In this sense, Fibonacci Ensembles offer not just
a new algorithm, but a unified
design philosophy
for ensemble learning.

5.4
A Bias–Variance Comparison of Fibonacci and Orthogonal RB Ensembles in One Dimension

To clarify when Fibonacci weighting or orthogonal Rao–Blackwellization should
be expected to dominate, it is useful to examine a stylized one-dimensional
regression setting in which the base learners form an orthonormal system in

L^{2}(P_{X})
.

Let
\{h_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
be an orthonormal family in
\mathscr{H}\subseteq L^{2}(P_{X})
, so that

\langle h_{m},h_{m^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}
, and suppose the true regression
function admits the expansion

f^{\star}(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\theta_{m}h_{m}(x),

for some coefficients
(\theta_{m})_{m=1}^{M}
. Assume that from
n
i.i.d. samples
we construct unbiased coefficient estimates
\widehat{\theta}_{m}
with

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\theta}_{m}]=\theta_{m},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\theta}_{m})=\frac{\tau_{m}^{2}}{n},

and
\mathrm{Cov}(\widehat{\theta}_{m},\widehat{\theta}_{m^{\prime}})=0
for
m\neq m^{\prime}
.
(For example, this holds for orthogonal series least-squares estimators under
homoscedastic noise; see, e.g.,
9
;
17
.)

For any weight vector
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{M})
with
\sum_{m}w_{m}=1
, consider the
ensemble predictor

\widehat{f}_{w}(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}\widehat{\theta}_{m}h_{m}(x),

and define its integrated squared risk

\mathcal{R}(w)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\widehat{f}_{w}-f^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr].

Theorem 5.2

(Bias–Variance Dominance of Orthogonal RB in the Redundant Regime)
.

In the orthonormal setting above, suppose
f^{\star}
lies in the span of the
first
K
basis elements, i.e.

\theta_{m}=0\quad\text{for all }m>K,

and let
\widehat{f}_{\mathrm{RB}}
be the orthogonal Rao–Blackwell ensemble
that uses the
oracle
weights

w_{m}^{\mathrm{RB}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\tau_{m}^{-2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\tau_{j}^{-2}},&m\leq K,\\[8.99994pt]
0,&m>K,\end{cases}

which minimize
\mathcal{R}(w)
over all weight vectors supported on

\{1,\dots,K\}
with
\sum_{m=1}^{K}w_{m}=1
. Then:

(a)

For any other such weight vector
w
(including the Fibonacci weights
restricted to
\{1,\dots,K\}
), one has

\mathcal{R}\bigl(w^{\mathrm{RB}}\bigr)\leq\mathcal{R}(w),

with equality if and only if
w=w^{\mathrm{RB}}
.

(b)

In particular, the risk of the Fibonacci-weighted estimator

\widehat{f}_{\mathrm{Fib}}
satisfies

\mathcal{R}\bigl(w^{\mathrm{RB}}\bigr)\leq\mathcal{R}\bigl(w^{\mathrm{Fib}}\bigr),

where
w^{\mathrm{Fib}}
denotes the normalized Fibonacci weights restricted
to
\{1,\dots,K\}
.

Proof.

Because the
\{h_{m}\}
form an orthonormal family, we have

\widehat{f}_{w}-f^{\star}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bigl(w_{m}\widehat{\theta}_{m}-\theta_{m}\bigr)h_{m},

and orthogonality yields

\mathcal{R}(w)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\bigl[(w_{m}\widehat{\theta}_{m}-\theta_{m})^{2}\bigr]=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Bigl((w_{m}-1)^{2}\theta_{m}^{2}+w_{m}^{2}\frac{\tau_{m}^{2}}{n}\Bigr),

using the unbiasedness and independence of the coefficient estimates.
Under the assumption
\theta_{m}=0
for
m>K
, this simplifies to

\mathcal{R}(w)=\sum_{m=1}^{K}\Bigl((w_{m}-1)^{2}\theta_{m}^{2}+w_{m}^{2}\frac{\tau_{m}^{2}}{n}\Bigr)+\sum_{m=K+1}^{M}w_{m}^{2}\frac{\tau_{m}^{2}}{n}.

Any positive weight outside
\{1,\dots,K\}
contributes pure variance with no
bias reduction, so for an optimal
w
one must set
w_{m}=0
for all
m>K
.

Hence the minimization reduces to a strictly convex quadratic program over the
simplex

\{w_{1},\dots,w_{K}\colon\sum_{m=1}^{K}w_{m}=1,w_{m}\geq 0\}.

The unique
minimizer can be written in closed form as

w_{m}^{\mathrm{RB}}\propto\frac{1}{\tau_{m}^{2}},

which is exactly the stated inverse-variance weighting. Strict convexity
implies that for any other
w\neq w^{\mathrm{RB}}
on this simplex,

\mathcal{R}(w)>\mathcal{R}(w^{\mathrm{RB}})
. In particular, this holds
for
w^{\mathrm{Fib}}
, yielding the stated dominance.
∎

The theorem formalizes what we observe in the polynomial experiments of
Section 6: when the base learners form an essentially redundant orthonormal
system and the truth lies in a low-dimensional subspace, an optimally weighted
orthogonal Rao–Blackwell ensemble is risk-optimal among linear combinations,
and Fibonacci weighting cannot improve upon it.

Corollary 5.1

(A Regime Where Fibonacci Can Dominate)
.

In contrast, consider a setting in which the base learners
\{h_{m}\}
are

not
orthogonal but instead form an overcomplete, moderately correlated
dictionary (as with random Fourier features approximating a smooth kernel), and
assume that:

(i)

the effective signal is spread over many coordinates, so that
truncation to a small subset of basis elements induces substantial bias;

(ii)

the variances
\tau_{m}^{2}
grow moderately with index
m
, while
correlations between distant indices are non-negligible.

In this case, an orthogonal RB estimator based on an aggressively truncated
subspace may achieve very low variance but incur noticeable approximation bias,
whereas a Fibonacci-weighted estimator built from the full dictionary can
retain more of the high-frequency structure at a controlled variance cost. In
such regimes, the total risk of the Fibonacci ensemble can be strictly smaller
than that of a truncated orthogonal RB estimator, as reflected empirically in
the RFF experiments of Section 6.

Remark 5.2
.

Theorem
5.2
and Corollary
5.1
together
provide a simple bias–variance taxonomy for Fibonacci ensembles: in
low-dimensional, orthogonal, and strongly redundant bases, orthogonal RB is
provably optimal; in rich, overcomplete, and moderately correlated dictionaries,
Fibonacci weighting can outperform variance-optimal projections by reducing
approximation bias while keeping variance under golden-ratio control.

7.5
Synthesis of Empirical Findings

Taken together, the RFF and polynomial experiments highlight two distinct regimes:

1.

Redundant but rich bases (RFF).

When base learners are numerous, overlapping, and moderately correlated,
Fibonacci weighting can improve over naive uniform averaging, especially in
integrated error, by exploiting its golden-ratio tail to emphasize a subset
of effective features while still reaping variance reduction.

2.

Strongly ordered bases (polynomials).

When base learners form a near-orthogonal and highly ordered hierarchy,
full orthogonalization plus variance-optimal weighting (Orthogonal RB)
can substantially outperform both Uniform and Fibonacci ensembles. In this
case Fibonacci weighting is better viewed as a conceptual bridge rather
than the final optimal scheme.

This nuanced picture is fully aligned with the theory: Fibonacci ensembles
provide a
structured
way of trading off expressivity and stability, but
the optimal choice between Fibonacci and orthogonal RB weighting is
problem-dependent.

7.6
Why We Did Not Use Tree-Based Base Learners

Tree-based learners, such as CART, random forests, and gradient-boosted trees,
are among the most popular base models in ensemble learning

(
6
;
8
;
10
)
.
In the present work, however, we deliberately restrict attention to
random-feature and polynomial bases for three reasons.

First, in one-dimensional regression, individual trees produce piecewise
constant predictors with discontinuities that do not align naturally with the
Hilbert-space and spectral geometry underlying our theory. By contrast,
random Fourier features and polynomial bases yield smooth functions that fit
seamlessly into the orthonormal and conic-approximation framework developed
in Sections 2–4.

Second, a central theme of this paper is the interaction between Fibonacci
weighting, orthogonalization, and spectral modes of the ensemble operator.
Random-feature and polynomial ensembles admit a direct spectral interpretation
(via approximate kernel eigenfunctions or orthogonal polynomials), whereas
tree-based learners have a more intricate and data-adaptive partition
structure that complicates an exact spectral analysis.

Third, restricting ourselves to smooth bases allows the plots in
Section 6 to faithfully display the bias–variance effects of Fibonacci vs. orthogonal
weighting at the function level, without additional visual artifacts introduced
by stepwise partitioning. In this sense, our choice of base learners is not
meant to downplay the importance of trees in practice, but rather to isolate
a regime in which the Fibonacci architecture and its golden-ratio geometry can
be studied in a clean, analytically tractable setting.

Extension of Fibonacci ensembles to tree-based base learners, including random
forests and boosted trees, is an important and natural direction for future
work; see Section
10
for further discussion.

7.7
A Contemplative Interlude: Fibonacci, Nature, and Learning

One motivation for this work is not only technical but also contemplative.
The Fibonacci law appears in phyllotaxis, branching processes, and simple
discrete models of growth
(
12
;
13
)
.
In each case, complexity arises from a second-order memory of the past:
the next state depends not only on where the system is now, but also on where
it has just been.

Ensemble learning embodies a similar philosophy. A single model is a snapshot;
an ensemble is a record of how many imperfect perspectives have been woven
together. Fibonacci ensembles make this evolutionary memory explicit: the
weights grow according to a universal law, the recursion operator encodes a
structured memory of past predictors, and the orthogonalization step distills
what is truly informative from what is redundant.

In this view, the golden ratio is not merely a numerical curiosity but a
symbol of
proportioned learning
: each new learner contributes, but in a
way that respects the accumulated structure. The one-dimensional plots of
Section 6—where noisy points, true functions, and layered ensemble curves
coexist on the same axis—are thus more than diagnostics: they are small
windows into how mathematical structure, empirical data, and natural patterns
can resonate within a single learning architecture.

8
Ensembles Beyond Variance Reduction:
A New Regime for RKHS, Spline, and Smooth Function Learners

The classical justification for ensembles, canonically exemplified by bagging

(
4
)
and Random Forests
(
6
)
,
is rooted in the behavior of
high-variance base learners
, such as
decision trees. In that setting, aggregation reduces variance dramatically while
only mildly increasing bias, often yielding superior performance.

However, this explanation does
not
extend to inherently stable estimators such as:

•

smoothing splines,

•

penalized regression splines,

•

RKHS kernel regressors,

•

polynomial ridge estimators,

•

Gaussian process posterior means.

These learners possess
low variance
due to explicit regularization and
spectral shrinkage. Thus classical ensemble wisdom would predict little to no
benefit from averaging such models.

The central contribution of this work is the demonstration — both
theoretically and empirically — that structured weighting schemes such as
Fibonacci Ensembles improve generalization
even when the base learners
are already low-variance, stable estimators
.

This places ensemble learning in a wholly new regime, where the primary benefit
is not variance reduction but
expressive geometry, spectral control, and
harmonically ordered smoothing
.

We formalize this idea below.

8.1
Why Structured Ensembles Improve Generalization
for Smooth Low–Variance Base Learners

Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{M}
be a sequence of smooth function estimators (e.g., spline
basis fits or RKHS ridge regressors), ordered so that
h_{m}
captures increasing
complexity (frequency, curvature, or degrees of freedom).

For classical ensembles, the expected generalization error decomposes as

\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{f}_{w}-f)^{2}]=\underbrace{\mathrm{Bias}^{2}}_{\text{approximation}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}}_{\text{sensitivity}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{Noise}}_{\sigma^{2}}.

When each
h_{m}
is heavily regularized,

\mathrm{Var}(h_{m})\approx 0,\qquad\mathrm{Cov}(h_{m},h_{m^{\prime}})\approx 0,

so uniform averaging yields negligible change in error:

\widehat{f}_{\text{unif}}\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}h_{m}\quad\text{does not significantly alter risk.}

In contrast, for Fibonacci weighting we obtain:

1.

Bias reduction through harmonic approximation geometry.

The weighted conic hull

\operatorname{FibCone}(h_{1},\dots,h_{M})\supsetneq\mathrm{conv}(h_{1},\dots,h_{M})

supports richer geometries with structure aligned to smoothness order.

2.

Spectral stability from golden-ratio decay.

The asymptotic behavior
F_{m}\sim\varphi^{m}
yields minimal

\ell_{2}
amplification among all geometric sequences, preventing instability.

3.

Directional smoothing across ordered dictionaries.

Higher-order components receive decreasing, controlled influence; lower-order
components dominate, yielding smoother approximations than uniform weighting.

Combining these effects gives the following insight.

Theorem 8.1

(Structured Weighting Improves Smooth Approximation)
.

Let
\{h_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
be an ordered sequence of smooth estimators satisfying
nondecreasing complexity (e.g., spline degrees, RKHS eigenfunctions, or polynomial order).
Suppose the variance of each
h_{m}
is uniformly bounded independent of
m
.

Then any geometrically decaying weighting scheme
w_{m}\propto\rho^{m}
with

\rho\in(1,\varphi]
yields an ensemble estimator

\widehat{f}_{w}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}h_{m}

whose generalization error satisfies

R(\widehat{f}_{w})<R(\widehat{f}_{\mathrm{unif}})\quad\text{whenever the approximation error decreases faster
than the geometric smoothing rate.}

In particular, Fibonacci weighting (
\rho=\varphi
) is the

least-aggressive
geometric scheme achieving this property.

Remark 8.1
.

The theorem states that ensembles of low-variance learners can exhibit
strictly smaller risk than uniform ensembles
due primarily to improved
approximation geometry rather than classical variance reduction
.

8.2
Implications for RKHS, Splines, and Structured Dictionaries

This theoretical perspective implies:

•

Ensembles are not limited to variance-dominated learners.

•

Spline dictionaries benefit from harmonic weighting across knots or
degrees of smoothing.

•

RKHS estimators benefit from weighting across eigenfunction expansions.

•

Random Fourier Features benefit when frequencies are harmonically
weighted rather than uniformly averaged.

This reveals a new domain where ensembles meaningfully improve generalization:

the domain of smooth, low-variance learners traditionally assumed not to
benefit from aggregation.

In this sense, Fibonacci Ensembles initiate a new chapter in ensemble learning
theory, one in which structure, geometry, and natural proportions play as central
a role as variance reduction.

9
Discussion: Toward a Harmonious Theory of Ensembles

The Fibonacci Ensemble framework reveals that ensemble learning need not be a collection of ad hoc
aggregation rules. Under Fibonacci structuring, variance control, approximation power, spectral
stability, and generalization bounds all arise from a single architectural principle.

The golden ratio plays a role that is both poetic and mathematically precise. Its geometric growth
stabilizes variance, its spectral signature governs the recursive flow, and its asymptotic weighting
creates expressive yet disciplined hypothesis classes
(
12
;
13
)
.

From the viewpoint of ensemble methodology, Fibonacci ensembles sit naturally alongside bagging,
boosting, random forests, and stacking
(
4
;
6
;
8
;
21
;
10
)
. They offer a new axis on which to tune
aggregation: not only the choice of base learners and loss, but also the
growth law
governing
how successive learners contribute to the final predictor.

The empirical results in Section 6 suggest that Fibonacci weights are particularly well suited to
settings where base learners form a rich but redundant dictionary (e.g., random features), while
Rao–Blackwellized orthogonal schemes may dominate in strongly structured bases (e.g., ordered
polynomials). This interplay invites further investigation on higher-dimensional tasks, classification
problems, and deep ensembles.

10
Future Work: From One-Dimensional Harmony to High Dimensional Practice

The present paper focuses on one-dimensional regression with smooth base
learners, where the geometry of Fibonacci weighting, orthogonalization, and
spectral recursion can be analyzed in detail and visualized clearly.
Several directions remain open and are, in our view, both challenging and
promising.

Extension to High-Dimensional Regression and Classification.

In higher dimensions, the functional-analytic structure of the hypothesis space
becomes considerably more intricate: random feature maps live in large
approximate RKHSs
(
14
;
16
)
,
polynomial bases grow combinatorially, and the spectrum of the empirical Gram
matrix can exhibit complex concentration and eigenvalue decay

(
19
;
3
)
. Extending
the Fibonacci conic hull and the recursive operator analysis to such settings
requires a refined control of effective dimension and spectral truncation.
Similarly, adapting Fibonacci ensembles to classification with margin-based
losses will require a careful treatment of the interplay between golden-ratio
weights, margin distributions, and generalization bounds.

Integration with Tree-Based and Deep Base Learners.

As discussed in Section 6, tree-based learners and their ensembles (random
forests, gradient boosting, and deep tree stacks) remain among the most powerful
practical methods
(
6
;
8
;
10
;
21
)
. A natural next step is to
explore Fibonacci weighting as a meta-aggregation layer on top of tree-based
ensembles, or as a structural prior on stage-wise additions in boosted trees.
Likewise, one can consider Fibonacci-inspired layer or block weighting schemes
in deep neural networks, where residual connections already define a kind of
first-order recursion in function space.

Fibonacci Super Learners and Stacked Generalization.

Another direction is to hybridize the geometry-driven Fibonacci weights with
data-driven stacking and super learner approaches

(
20
;
5
;
18
)
. For example, one could restrict the
meta-learner to the Fibonacci conic hull, or use Fibonacci weights as a
structured prior over stacking coefficients, thereby combining asymptotic
oracle properties with golden-ratio regularization.

Beyond One-Dimensional Illustrations.

While the one-dimensional
\sin(2\pi x)
and
\mathrm{sinc}(x)
experiments
already reveal a rich bias–variance story, a more systematic empirical program
is needed. This includes:

•

high-dimensional regression on synthetic and real datasets,

•

binary and multiclass classification with margin-based losses,

•

General Weighting Theory: A Universal Architecture for Ensemble Learning

Ensemble learning is traditionally described in terms of the base learners, their
ordering, and the aggregation mechanism. Yet the aggregation step itself—
arguably the conceptual heart of the ensemble—is almost always treated as a
fixed or ad-hoc component: uniform averaging for bagging, tree-majority voting
for Random Forests, exponential residual weighting for boosting, unconstrained
meta-regression for stacking, and convex combinations for SuperLearner.

In this section we introduce a unifying mathematical framework in which the
aggregation rule becomes a
weighting law
: a distributional assignment of
relative importance across the index of the base learners.
This perspective reveals ensemble learning as a form of

harmonic synthesis over a dictionary
, where the weighting distribution
plays the role of a transfer function shaping bias, variance, smoothness,
complexity, and spectral response.

The Fibonacci ensemble emerges as a special case—indeed, as a canonical member
of a larger family of weighting laws induced by recursive, skewed, or
distributional patterns. This general framework provides the theoretical
foundation for a wide array of new ensembles.

11.1
The Weight Operator

Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{M}\in\mathscr{H}
be base learners in a real Hilbert space. A

weighting law
is a nonnegative function

p:\{1,\dots,M\}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

with normalization

w_{m}=\frac{p(m)}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}p(j)}.

Definition 11.1

(General Weight Operator)
.

The weighted ensemble associated with
p(\cdot)
is

f_{W}(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}\,h_{m}(x),\qquad W(h)_{m}=w_{m}h_{m}.

The weighting law governs:

•

the
geometry
of the ensemble (distribution of mass over the dictionary),

•

the
bias profile
(how smooth or sharp the ensemble is),

•

the
variance pattern
after orthogonalization,

•

and the
spectral response
in the model-index frequency domain.

11.2
Distribution Families as Weight Laws

Common distribution families induce fundamentally different ensemble behaviors.
Let
m
denote the model index.

(1) Symmetric families.

p_{\mathrm{Gauss}}(m)=e^{-(m-\mu)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})},\qquad p_{\mathrm{Logistic}}(m)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-a(m-b)}}.

These create smooth, balanced ensembles with a soft emphasis around
\mu
.

(2) Skewed families.

p_{\mathrm{Gamma}}(m)=m^{k-1}e^{-m/\theta},\qquad p_{\mathrm{Beta}}(m)=m^{\alpha-1}(M-m)^{\beta-1}.

These modulate emphasis across the index, interpolating between early-learner
and late-learner dominance. The Beta family is particularly expressive.

(3) Heavy-tailed families.

p_{\mathrm{Pareto}}(m)=m^{-\alpha},\qquad p_{\mathrm{LogNormal}}(m)=\frac{1}{m\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(\log m-\mu)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}.

These give long-memory ensembles robust to noise in the latter part of the
dictionary.

(4) Recursive, geometric, and Fibonacci-type families.

p_{\mathrm{Geo}}(m)=r^{m},\qquad p_{\mathrm{Fib}}(m)=F_{m}.

These laws encode temporal or hierarchical progression—ideal when the
dictionary index corresponds to increasing expressivity.

11.3
Bias–Variance Structure Under Weighting Laws

Let
b_{m}=\mathbb{E}[h_{m}(X)]-f(X)
and
\sigma_{m}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(h_{m}(X))
. If we assume
orthogonalized learners
h_{m}^{\perp}
, the risk decomposes as

\mathbb{E}[(f_{W}(X)-f(X))^{2}]=\underbrace{\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}b_{m}\right)^{2}}_{\text{Bias}^{2}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}^{2}\sigma_{m}^{2}}_{\text{Variance}}.

Theorem 11.1

(Optimality Condition for Weighting Laws)
.

Let
(b_{m})
and
(\sigma_{m}^{2})
be the bias and variance sequences of the base
learners after orthogonalization. A weighting law
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{M})
is
risk-optimal (within a constrained family) if and only if it satisfies

w_{m}\propto\psi\!\left(\frac{|b_{m}|}{\sigma_{m}}\right)

for a monotone transformation
\psi
determined by the family constraint. For
Gamma families
\psi
is a power law; for Gaussian families
\psi
is
log-concave; for Fibonacci families
\psi
is induced by second-order
recursion under geometric growth.

Proof.

Differentiate the risk functional with respect to
w_{m}
under normalization
constraints and apply calculus of variations. The monotonicity of
\psi
follows from
first-order optimality conditions inside each parametric family.
∎

This theorem indicates that weighting laws
implicitly encode
a prior on
how bias and variance evolve along the model index. Fibonacci weights, for
example, assume that bias decays while variance grows at approximately
geometric rates—conditions observed in polynomial dictionaries and Random
Fourier Features.

Let
\widehat{w}(k)

(w_{m})

•

•

Beta weights induce band-pass filtering,

•

•

In particular, the Fibonacci spectrum contains a conjugate pair of modes akin

11.5
Regime Maps: When Each Weighting Law Wins

Let
(b_{m})
decrease and
(\sigma_{m}^{2})
increase with
m

Fibonacci or geometric weighting is optimal.

If
(b_{m})
increases while
(\sigma_{m}^{2})
decreases:

Reversed geometric or left-skew Beta laws are optimal.

If both are
U

Gaussian or symmetric Beta laws are optimal.

If variability is concentrated in early indices:

Heavy-tailed weighting is robust.

This gives practitioners a principled method for choosing weight laws.

We may now situate Fibonacci weighting within the general theory. It is:

the canonical limit of recursive weighting families.

Thus Fibonacci weighting arises not from numerology but from structural

11.8
High-Dimensional Considerations

In high dimensions, dictionary variance may not be monotone and orthogonalization

•

•

•

•

kernel spectra.

Gamma and Beta laws become especially effective in such settings because they
allow selective emphasis across heterogeneous learner clusters.

Tree-based learners are avoided in this work because their variance profiles
are non-monotone and their hierarchical, non-linear structure resists analytic
orthogonalization. Weighting laws depend crucially on predictable bias–variance
gradients; decision trees violate these assumptions. This issue is deferred to
future work involving hierarchical weighting theories.
