Spermatogenesis, hormonal fluctuations, and testicular gene expression changes in male blue catfish, Ictalurus furcatus, throughout pubescent and early adult stages of development
Authors: Kaylan A Martin, Brian G Bosworth, Samitha SN Liyanage, Kyle R Wood, Jason W Abernathy, Benjamin H Beck, Timothy J Bruce, Luke A Roy, Rex A Dunham, Ian AE Butts
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMale blue catfish (Ictalurus furcatus) mature at 4–7 years and cannot be hand-stripped, making sperm collection a lethal procedure. Sperm are also highly variable in quality/quantity. We monitored blue catfish males for 34 months (2.3 to 4.9 years old; y/o) to quantify changes in body morphometrics, plasma androgens, osmolality, ions, testicular development, and gene expression. Body weight and length of fish ranged from 0.6 ± 0.2 to 3.2 ± 0.2 kg and 35.2 ± 2.1 to 58.9 ± 1.9 cm, respectively. Testes weight and gonadosomatic index (GSI) were highly variable within/between sampling points. Maximum testes weight was 6.95 g (4.7 y/o) and GSI was 39.9% (3.7 y/o). Age-associated changes in testicular development were significant, with highest spermatogenic maturity values (0.31–0.73) reported for 3.11 to 4.9 y/o fish and lowest for 2.3 (0.13) and 2.11 (0.12) y/o fish. Spermatozoa were observed in 3.3 and 4.2 to 4.9 y/o fish. The highest ratio of spermatozoa was detected at 4.2 y/o (70%). Androgens spiked at 3.11 y/o, coinciding with reproductive season. An increase in osmolality (mOsm/kg) was observed for 3.11 to 4.5 y/o fish, then decreased. K+, Na+, and Cl− were highest ions throughout development. Transcriptomic analysis revealed 6,317 differentially expressed genes between 2.9, 3.1, 3.9, and 4.2 y/o fish. Most genes were differentially expressed in 3.1 (2,348) and 4.2 (2,689) y/o fish, when compared to 2.9 y/o. Genes related to spermatogenesis and sperm development (cfap61, hsf5, rsph9, dmrt2a, LOC128611646, usp21, gstt1b) were differentially expressed based on age and season. These findings improve our understanding of the reproductive process for an important farm animal.Keywords: Assisted reproductive technology, Biomarker, Gamete quality, Maturation, Paternity, Sperm quality

Introduction

United States (U.S.) ranks 19th in global aquaculture production with an estimated total production of over 300 M kg, valued at $1.7 billion USD [1]. Freshwater aquaculture accounts for the bulk of U.S. production, with the major species produced being trout (Oncorhynchus spp.), crawfish (Cambarus spp.), and catfish (channel catfish, Ictalurus punctatus and the channel catfish × blue catfish, I. furcatus hybrid) [1]. The U.S. catfish industry produced 146 M kg of catfish in 2023, with the top producing states being Mississippi, Alabama, Texas, and Arkansas [2, 3]. The hybrid offspring of female channel catfish and male blue catfish constitute more than 50% of the harvest [4] due to several factors including better tolerance of low oxygen [5], better disease resistance [6], higher fillet yield [7], and superior performance in intensive pond-based production systems, such as intensively aerated ponds and split ponds [4], compared to purebred channel catfish.

Hybridization does not occur naturally, therefore in-vitro fertilization is necessary for production of hybrid catfish [8]. Unfortunately, male blue catfish, unlike many other aquaculture species, cannot be hand-stripped for milt, resulting in lethal extraction and maceration of testes [9]. The same is not true for female channel catfish, whose eggs can be readily stripped for in-vitro fertilization and spawned again in future seasons [10]. Additionally, male blue catfish do not reach sexual maturity until 4 to 7 years of age, compared to 2 to 3 years for the female channel catfish [10, 11]. High variability in both sperm quality and quantity of male blue catfish sperm [12], coupled with the long time to maturation and lethality of sperm collection, also lead to inefficiency in hybrid catfish production. Thus, a thorough understanding of the physiological and molecular mechanisms that regulate testicular development and sperm quantity/quality is beneficial to obtain high reproductive efficiency and to ensure lower costs for breeders.

To address this issue, we first need to histologically quantify the progression of the annual reproductive cycle during growth (spermatogenesis) and maturation phases (spermiation). During spermatogenesis, spermatogonial stem cells transform into spermatogonia, which experience meiotic divisions to become primary spermatocytes, and, through phases of meiosis, will begin the change of development into secondary spermatocytes, spermatids, and finally mature spermatozoa [13, 14]. To allow researchers to assess which spermatic cell types are present at different life stages, histological images are taken of testis tissue. For blue catfish, this should be initiated at the onset of puberty and continue throughout development. Catfish producers report a substantial decline in the proportion of blue catfish males with well-developed testes late in the spawning season (mid to late June), which hinders production [15]. Similar research has already been performed for salmonids (family Salmonidae) [16], zebrafish (Danio rerio) [17], tilapia (Oreochromis spp.) [18], and other commercially important fish species.

In general, there is a relationship between gonadal maturity (via histology) and sex steroid hormone profiles. Androgens are sex steroid hormones that are important in the regulation of development and operation of the male reproductive system [19]. In fishes, the androgen testosterone (T) converts to its 11-oxygenated form of 11-ketotestosterone (11-KT), which serves as a major androgen for reproduction [20]. In blue catfish, correlations between sexual maturity, plasma T levels, and sperm quality have been demonstrated [21]. Meanwhile, in other species, sex steroid hormones (T and 11-KT) have been shown to play a vital role in spermatogenesis, social interactions, male-to-male aggression, and in the development of sexual characteristics [22, 23]. Seemingly, T and 11-KT can experience seasonal changes, with peak levels found in plasma at the start of spawning season [19, 20]. Additionally, T and 11-KT have been observed to increase as fish age, making both androgens key indicators of sexual maturation [24]. As such, it is necessary to quantify these endocrine factors and determine their physiological roles for regulating spermatogenesis in blue catfish and by implication in other Ictalurus spp.

Transcriptomics is now employed to identify biomarkers associated with spermatogenesis and gonadal development. Using such analysis in blue catfish will allow us to better understand interlinked molecular processes and advance our male diagnostic toolbox. Through transcriptomics, RNA is transcribed from the genome and allows us to understand the mechanisms of genes at a certain stage or condition of an organism, based on their level of expression [25]. Testis tissue presents high expression levels of specific genes, providing ample information regarding changes within the testis during spermatogenesis [26]. Thus, research on gene expression during testicular development in blue catfish would help establish requirements for spermatogenesis and may provide potential biomarkers for male fertility. Therefore, RNAs deserve to be the focus of long-range investigations, especially in conjunction with histology, sex steroid hormonal profiles, and sperm quality assessments.

In the present study, we quantified seasonal and age-related changes in male blue catfish body morphology, sex steroid hormones, and testis histology throughout 34 months of development from pubescent to early adult stages of development. Then, we analyzed the transcriptome profile of testes from 4 timepoints [2.9, 3.1, 3.9, and 4.2 year old (y/o) fish], based on varying developmental stages calculated by histological analysis and the spermatogenic maturation index (SMI). This combined approach allows us to better understand the mechanisms regulating spermatogenesis and gonadal development of male blue catfish to improve the sustainability of the hybrid catfish industry.

Materials and methods

All experiments were performed at the Auburn University E.W. Shell Fisheries Center in Auburn, Alabama, USA (32.6524° N, 85.4860° W). Husbandry was approved and performed via the Auburn University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (AU-IACUC #PRN 2020–3710 and PRN 2023–5229).

Broodstock and testes collection

Broodstock were transferred on 1 March 2021 from Jubilee Farms (Jubilee Farms, MS, U.S.) to the E.W. Shell Fisheries Center, Auburn, AL, U.S. (32.6524° N, 85.4860° W) and distributed into four 0.1-acre earthen ponds. All broodstock were fed a 32% protein diet (The Feed Lot, AL, U.S.). Temperature was recorded daily and ranged from 14 to 29 °C in 2021, 9 to 28 °C in 2022, 10 to 29 °C in 2023, and 8 to 11 °C for the months sampled in 2024 (Fig. 1). Mean dissolved oxygen levels ranged from 4.9 to 11.3 mg/L throughout the experimental sampling period (Fig. 1). Male blue catfish were collected bi-monthly using a seine net (75’×8’×1’). Sampling dates and corresponding age of fish were as follows: fish were 2.3 y/o in Aug 2021, 2.5 y/o in Oct 2021, 2.7 y/o in Dec 2021, 2.9 y/o in Feb 2022, 2.11 y/o in Apr 2022, 3.1 y/o in Jun 2022, 3.3 y/o in Aug 2022, 3.5 y/o in Oct 2022, 3.7 y/o in Dec 2022, 3.9 y/o in Feb 2023, 3.11 y/o in Apr 2023, 4.2 y/o in July 2023, 4.3 y/o in Aug 2023, 4.5 y/o in Oct 2023, 4.7 in Dec 2023, and 4.9 y/o in Feb 2024. Four to eight males were selected at random [total n = 90 males (2.3 to 4.9 y/o)] and total body weight to the nearest 0.05 kg was taken of each fish and recorded. Before testes extraction, blood samples (5 to 10 mL) were taken from the anal fin region using 10 mL syringes with 20-gauge needles for T and 11-KT analyses. Blood was immediately placed on ice. Males were subsequently euthanized by blunt force trauma to the head, following industry and AU-IACUC guidelines.

Daily dissolved oxygen (A) and temperature (B) measured in ponds at the Auburn University E.W. Shell Fisheries Center in Auburn, Alabama, U.S. (32.6524° N, 85.4860° W) from Aug 2021 to Mar 2024. The black line represents the monthly mean

Fish were dissected and testes surgically removed using forceps, then weighed to the nearest 0.001 g. Testes were placed in 15 mL tubes with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; reverse osmosis deionized water with 8 g/L NaCl, 0.4 g/L KCl, 0.16 g/L CaCl2 × 2H2O, 0.2 g/L MgSO4 × 7H2O, 0.12 g/L Na2HPO4 × 7H2O, 0.06 g/L KH2PO4, 0.35 g/L NaHCO3, 1 g/L glucose) [27] to prevent pre-activation of sperm cells. A small fraction of tissue (~ 0.05 to 1 g) was cut off and placed in 10% phosphate buffered formalin for histological analysis. An additional testis sample (~ 0.05 to 0.5 g) was collected in triplicate from each male to be snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80℃ for RNA extraction. Gonadosomatic Index (GSI) was calculated for each male by subtracting the mass of gonad (MG) from total body mass (MT) to obtain somatic mass (MS), then dividing MG by MS and multiplying by 100 to obtain a percentage value [MT – MG = MS; (MG/MS) × 100 = GSI (%)].

Histological analysis and spermatogenic maturation index

Testes tissue samples [total n = 90 males (2.3 to 4.9 y/o)] were processed at the Scott-Ritchey Research Center, Auburn University College of Veterinary Medicine via a routine 12 h cycle program. Tissue samples were dehydrated in ethanol solutions that gradually increased from 70 to 100%. They were then embedded in StatLab Parapro 360 paraffin (PP360, StatLab Medical Products, Inc, TX, U.S.). Samples were step-sectioned at 4 μm, placing 32 μm between sections, with three sections mounted on each slide. Slides were then air dried at room temperature before being baked in an incubator at 60°C for 10 min. Afterwards, slides were stained using hematoxylin and eosin. Images of slides were taken using a Zeiss Imager.A2 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, LLC, NY, U.S.) with a 40× objective (A- Plan 40×/0.65 Ph2) and an Axio-cam 305 camera. Zen Pro v.6.1 imaging software was used to capture images (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Testes were sampled in triplicate, with three histological sections placed on one slide. Nine images were obtained for each male, resulting in a total of 810 images for histological analysis, which ranged in age and developmental stage. Images were analyzed by Image J (v. 1.53), where a 48-point grid was placed on the image to determine the area fraction of different cells within testicular tissue using the plugin “Analyze”. Development of spermatogenesis was analyzed using SMI [28], where the tissue was fractioned by cell type and the area fractions per category were weighted by a factor ranging from 0 to 1 [testicular somatic cells (Ts) = 0, spermatogonia (Sg) = 0.25 (> 9 μm), spermatocytes (Sc) = 0.5 (3 to 9 μm), spermatids (St) = 0.75 (1.5 to 2 μm), and spermatozoa (Sz) = 1 (< 1.5 μm)].

Sex steroid hormone analyses

Blood samples were stored overnight at 4 °C in 15 mL centrifuge tubes [total n = 90 males (2.3 to 4.9 y/o)] (VWR, PA, U.S.). Initial serum collection was obtained and placed into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. Blood was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to separate the remaining serum for further collection. Sera samples were stored at −80 °C until hormone analyses were performed. Serum T and 11-KT levels were quantified using Cayman ELISA kits (11-KT ELISA, 582751, Cayman Chemical and T ELISA, 582701, Cayman Chemical, MI, U.S.) via manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were performed in triplicate for each male. Intra-assay variation was 13.29% for T and 7.32% for 11-KT. Mean assay sensitivity (80% B/B0) for T was 57.04 pg/mL, in the range of 2.40 to 123.70 pg/mL. Mean assay sensitivity (80% B/B0) for 11-KT was 29.88 pg/mL, in the range of 0.70 to 99.20 pg/mL. Cross-reactivity for the T assay included 19-nortestoserone (140%), 5 α-dihydrotestosterone (27.4%), 5β-dihydrotestosterone (18.9%), methyltestosterone (4.7%), androstenedione (3.7%), 11-KT (2.2%), 5-androstenediol (0.51%), epi-testosterone (0.2%), progesterone (0.14%), testosterone enanthate (0.11%), androsterone (0.05%), androsterone sulfate (0.04%), testosterone sulfate (0.03%), dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) sulfate (0.02%), estradiol (< 0.01%), and testosterone glucuronide (< 0.01%). For 11-KT, cross-reactivity within the assay included adrenosterone (2.9%), 4-androsten-11β,17β-diol-3-one (0.01%), 5α-androstan-17β-ol-3-one (0.01%), 5α-androsten-3β,17β-diol (0.01%), and T (< 0.01%). Spectrophotometric readings were received with Cytation 3 (Biotek, VT, U.S.) at 412 nm.

Osmolality and ionic analyses

Frozen-thawed (−80 °C) sera samples were used to measure osmolality with a vapor pressure osmometer (Vapro 5520, Wescor Inc., UT, U.S.). The osmometer was calibrated every three samples. A serum sample (10 µL) was pipetted onto a piece of filter paper, placed on the osmometer, and analyzed in duplicate for males with enough blood sera [n = 53 males (2.9 to 4.9 y/o)]. A subset of the sera, based on quantity per male, were shipped on dry ice to the Waters Agricultural Laboratories, Inc. (GA, U.S.) for ion analyses [n = 69 males, (2.9 to 4.9 y/o)]. Ions included Ca2+, Na+, K+, Mg2+, and Cl−. Serum samples were digested with 6 mL of HCL and 4 mL of HNO3 before being analyzed for Ca2+, Na+, K+, and Mg2+ ions using an iCAP™ PRO ICP-OES elemental analyzer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Dilutions for each ion ranged between 200 and 500, depending on sample volume. The Cl− ion was analyzed using Chloride QuanTab® test strips (Hach, CO, U.S.) with dilutions ranging between 2 and 10, also depending on sample volume.

Sperm kinematic analyses

Sperm kinematics, including velocity (VCL; µm/s) and percent motility were analyzed for males (n = 5) during the spawning season (July 2023; 4.2 y/o) using Computer Assisted Sperm Analysis (CASA; CEROS II, Hamilton Thorne Biosciences, MA, U.S.) software, according to Myers et al. [29]. Sperm solution (diluted to 1 × 109 cells/mL with HBSS) was pipetted (0.1 µL) into an 80 μm 2×-CEL chamber (Hamilton Thorne Biosciences.) and activated with 10 µL of 0.5% bovine serum albumin (Merck Millipore, MA, U.S.) dissolved in distilled water (DH2O). Sperm cell activity was recorded at 10 s post-activation. Sperm samples were activated and recorded in triplicate. Samples and activation media were kept at 4 °C in an Echotherm™ chilling/heating dry bath (Torrey Pines Scientific, CA, U.S.).

Transcriptomics

RNA extraction and processing

For RNA extraction, 15 males were used based on maturity status from SMI analysis and separated into 4 groups: group 1 (n = 4) were 2.9 y/o (SMI = 0.17; Ct: 43%, Sg: 46%, Sc: 13%, St: 0%, Sz: 0%) and sampled in Feb 2022, group 2 (n = 4) were 3.1 y/o (SMI = 0.24; Ct: 35%, Sg: 31%, Sc: 33%, St: 0%, Sz: 0%) and sampled in Jun 2022, group 3 (n = 3) were 3.9 y/o (SMI = 0.28; Ct: 38%, Sg: 35%, Sc: 6%, St: 21%, Sz: 0%) and sampled in Feb 2023, and group 4 (n = 4) were 4.2 y/o (SMI = 0.49; Ct: 23%, Sg: 0%, Sc: 2%, St: 5%, Sz: 70%) and sampled in Jul 2023. Equipment used during extractions was cleaned using RNase AWAY (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, U.S.). Tissue (~ 50 mg) was homogenized in 600 µL DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research Corp., CA, U.S.) using a Mini Bead Mill Homogenizer (VWR International, PA, U.S.). This was performed using two cycles of 30 s with a 1 min cooldown in between. Extraction was performed using the Quick-RNA Miniprep Plus Kit (Zymo Research Corp.), and followed the manufacturer’s protocol. Concentration and purity of the RNA was measured with Nanodrop OneC spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). Integrity and quality of RNA was measured using RNA screen tape kit with Agilent 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, CA, U.S.). Samples with 260/280 ≥ 1.9, RIN ≥ 4, and concentration > 100 ng/µL were sent to Azenta Life Sciences (NJ, U.S.) for library preparation and sequencing.

Library preparation, sequencing, and quality control

Sample libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Preparation Kit (New England Biolabs, MA, U.S.), according to manufacturer’s protocol. Indexes were added for sequence attributions for each sample. Sequencing was performed with the Illumina platform using 2 × 150 base-pair (bp), paired-end (PE) configuration. Raw, demultiplexed files at a minimum of 26 M PE reads/sample were provided in FASTQ format. Raw reads were quality control checked using FastQ (v. 0.11.9). Reads were trimmed of low-quality reads and adapter sequences with TrimGalore (v. 0.6.10).

Alignment of genome and estimation of gene expression

The blue catfish (Ictalurus furcatus) genome was retrieved from NCBI (Genome assembly Billie_1.0; accession # GCA_023375685.2). Using high-quality reads obtained after quality control and trimming, alignment was performed using STAR aligner (v. 2.7.5). Post-mapping quality control was analyzed using MultiQC (v. 1.10.1). BAM files were used to create gene-level count data matrices for all samples using the HTSeq software (v.0.11.1). For HTSeq, the quantification level was set to feature type exon, the strand specificity set to non-strand specific, and the overlap mode set to union.

Differential gene expression (DGE) and GO analyses

Data were normalized using the Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) method and expression of differentiated genes (DEG) was determined with edgeR software (v. 2023.12.1). The statistical significance of DEGs were determined based on fold change (significance at |fold change| >1) and adjusted P < 0.05, according to Benjamini and Hochberg method [30]. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed to determine the biological function of DEGs. The GO analysis was performed to assign biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions terms using both the Fisher’s Exact test against the blue catfish annotated transcriptome and Gene Set Enrichment Analyses (GSEA) in OmicsBox software (v.3.3) [31, 32].

RT-qPCR validation of differently expressed genes

Extracted RNA from samples used for transcriptomic analysis (n = 15 males) were used for cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR validation. Testis RNA samples were diluted to 50 ng/µL before performing cDNA synthesis using an iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, U.S.), according to manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA samples were synthesized using a PTC Tempo 96 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, U.S.). Conditions were set at 25 °C for 5 min, 46 °C for 20 min, and 95 °C for 1 min. Following the reaction, the cDNA was stored at −20 °C. Genes (n = 5) selected for RT-qPCR were rsph9, slc661a, synop1b, cfap61, and cnn1b. Reference genes included actb and ef1a. Primer sequences were designed using GenBank accession numbers with the NCBI primer designing tool using an amplification size < 200 bp (Table 1). All primers were retrieved from Eurofins (Eurofins Genomics LLC., KY, U.S.). A test PCR was performed to verify all genes were complemented within the expected PCR product sizes by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA products were diluted to 1.25 ng/µL and readied for the RT-qPCR reaction using the SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Super Mix (Bio-Rad, CA, U.S.) on the CFX Opus 384 real-time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Singapore). The PCR reaction was performed in a 10 µL mixture containing 5 µL of master mix, 0.5 µL each of forward and reverse primer (10 µM stock), and 4 µL of diluted sample cDNA. Each PCR was performed using two technical replicates. Cycling conditions were set for 2 min at 95 °C followed by 15 s at 95 °C, 15 s at 60 °C, and 30 s at 72 °C for 40 cycles. A melt curve analysis was done to ensure the product specificity following a protocol of 5 s holding starting at 65 °C, followed by a 0.5 °C rise until reaching 95 °C. Relative quantification of each gene (ΔCt) was acquired through normalization against the two reference genes (actb and ef1a). The relative expression of the target genes was obtained using the 2−∆∆Ct method, with the 2.9 y/o (Feb 2022) fish used as the control group [33].

Statistical analyses

Body morphometrics, blood hormones, osmolality, ions, and spermatogenic maturation index (SMI)

Data were analyzed with SAS statistical analysis software (v.9.4), IBM SPSS (v.30), and R software (v.2024.09.1 - Build 394). Graphs were created in R software and SigmaPlot (v.15). Residuals were tested for both normality using Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance using the plot of residuals vs. predicted values. Data were log10 transformed or arcsine square-root transformed to meet the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity, when needed. Tukey’s HSD multiple comparisons procedure was used for post-hoc analyses. Values were expressed as least square means ± standard error. Alpha was set at 0.05.

Two statistical approaches were used to analyze male reproductive performance indices related to pubescent and early adult stages of development. First, all data were analyzed using a series of one-way ANOVA models. This allowed us to analyze differences among the 16 sampling points. Secondly, changes in body weight, total length, testis weight, GSI, and SMI data were fitted (a-priori) with a linear or quadratic regression model against Julian days, which are represented as integers that can dictate any instant in time [34]. Final equation selection (linear or quadratic) was based on an F-statistic: dfj(R2j − R2i)/(1 − R2j), where: R2i = the R2 for the ith order, R2j = the R2 for the next higher order, dfj = the degrees of freedom for the higher-order equation with j degrees of freedom in the numerator and dfj = n − j − 1 degrees of freedom in the denominator [35].

RT-qPCR validation

Real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) data were collected using the Bio-Rad CFX Manager Software. Cycle quantification (Cq) values from two replicates were averaged for each sample. The relative abundance of cDNA was analyzed by subtracting the averaged Cq values from the geometric means of the housekeeping genes (ΔCq) and then calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Relative cDNA abundances were compared between age groups using one-way ANOVA. Post-hoc analyses were created using Tukey’s HSD multiple comparisons procedure, setting alpha at 0.05. Individual gene expression levels obtained by Read Per Kilobase of transcript, per Million mapped reads (RPKM), were compared with expression levels gathered from RT-qPCR for validation.

Results

Body morphometrics

Morphometric data were collected from 2.3 to 4.9 y/o fish. There was a significant positive quadratic relationship between Julian days and log10 body weight (R2 = 0.70, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2A). Over the developmental timeline, mean body weight of fish ranged from 0.58 ± 0.19 kg at 2.7 y/o to 3.24 ± 0.17 at 4.7 y/o. The fish slowly increased in weight from 2.3 to 3.9 y/o, with no significant increase in weight after reaching 4.2 y/o (F15,72 = 10.03, P < 0.001; Fig. 2B). A similar relationship was observed for Julian days and log10 total length (R2 = 0.54, P < 0.001; Fig. 2C), which showed no significant increase in size after fish were sampled at 3.11 y/o (F15,72 = 16.32, P < 0.001; Fig. 2D).

Testes weight and GSI were both highly variable for the 2.3 to 4.9 y/o fish (Fig. 3). There was a significant positive linear relationship between Julian days and log10 testes weight (R2 = 0.42, P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). As fish aged, testes weight increased (F15,72 = 6.04, P < 0.001; Fig. 3B), with testes reaching up to 6.95 g (4.7 y/o). A positive linear relationship was also detected for GSI (R2 = 0.17, P < 0.001; Fig. 3C) with significant changes in GSI detected between 2 and 4 y/o fish (F15,72 = 3.29, P = 0.0003; Fig. 3D).

Histological analysis and spermatogenic maturation index (SMI)

Histological stains and digital images were processed for 90 males ranging from 2.3 to 4.9 y/o (Fig. 4), which showed distinct cell types and how they progressed throughout pubescent and early adult stages of development. As fish aged, changes in SMI were significant (F15,72 = 9.59, P < 0.0001; Fig. 5B). Highest mean SMI values were reported from 3.3 y/o and 3.11 to 4.9 y/o fish. Low SMI values were shown in 2.3 and 2.11 y/o fish. There was also a significant positive linear relationship between SMI and Julian days (R2 = 0.17, P < 0.001; Fig. 5A).

Sperm kinematics

Sperm samples were collected from 4.2 y/o males during the spawning season. Mean (± SEM) values for VCL and percent motility were 96.08 μm/s and 22.11%, respectively.

Transcriptomics of testis

Transcriptome analysis was conducted on four different groups of testicular tissue, based on SMI values at 2.9 y/o (0.17), 3.1 y/o (0.24), 3.9 y/o (0.28), and 4.2 y/o (0.49). Fifteen libraries were created and sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq platform to observe genes related to spermatogenesis. We discovered 589,000,590 raw reads, which provided an average of 39,266,706 reads per sample. Reads < 36 bases with adaptor contamination and low-quality regions (average Qscore < 20) were removed, which resulted in a total of 352,455,043 cleaned reads (59.8%) for all samples. Cleaned reads (345,308,532) were mapped to the blue catfish genome (Billie_1.0).

Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis

Mapped reads were used for DGE analysis to form pairwise comparisons among the four age groups (FDR < 0.05; |log2FC| >1). Higher log2FC values correlate with greater expression of genes while lower FDR values indicate statistical confidence. The comparison of each group revealed that there were significant DEGs found between 3.1 and 2.9 y/o fish, 3.9 and 2.9 y/o fish, 4.2 and 2.9 y/o fish, 3.9 and 3.1 y/o fish, and 4.2 and 3.9 y/o fish. Gene ontology (GO) analysis

Analysis of GO terms classifies genes and describes their roles in cellular, molecular, and biological processes. Both Fisher’s exact test (FET) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were used to observe the significance of GO terms between groups after mapping. The FET analysis indicated 11 overrepresented and eight underrepresented GO terms between 3.1 and 2.9 y/o fish. Overrepresented terms included motile cilium (GO:0031514), sperm flagellum (GO:0036126), and cilium movement (GO:0003341). Underrepresented terms were related to regulation of gene expression (GO:0010468) and cell communication (GO:0007154), among others. There were five overrepresented GO terms between 3.9 and 3.1 y/o fish, such as cilium movement and cilium-dependent cell motility (GO:0060285), and two underrepresented GO terms, which were signal transduction (GO:0007165) and protein-coupled receptor activity (GO:0004930). Between 4.2 and 2.9 y/o fish, there were 21 overrepresented terms, including DNA helicase activity (GO:0003678), motile cilium, and long-chain fatty acid transport (GO:0015909), and one underrepresented GO term, Golgi stack (GO:0005795). Only two overrepresented GO terms were expressed between 4.2 and 3.9 y/o fish, which were related to general chromosomes (GO:0005694) and metaphase chromosome alignment (GO:0051310). The FET analysis showed no significant GO terms between 3.9 and 2.9 y/o fish.

GSEA results showed eight top and two bottom GO terms between 3.1 and 2.9 y/o fish. Within those top eight included terms related to sperm flagellum, as well as cilium movement and cilium-dependent cell motility, which correlated with FET. Only six bottom GO terms were represented between 3.9 and 3.1 y/o fish, which included motile cilium (GO:0031514) and cilium movement. Discussion

Catfish are the dominant finfish in the U.S. freshwater aquaculture sector, contributing over 130 M kg of production annually [1]. The crossing of female channel catfish and male blue catfish to produce hybrid offspring has replaced over 50% of production that previously was channel catfish [10, 36]. Production of hybrid catfish, although successful overall, has reproductive bottlenecks related to egg quality of female channel catfish and variability of sperm quality and quantity of male blue catfish [37]. As such, development of biomarkers affiliated with spermatogenesis and maturation in blue catfish is a key step to improve hybrid production. This study identified changes in morphometric traits, plasma hormones and ions, and testes development throughout pubescent and early adult stages. Transcriptomic analysis of testes also allowed measurement of changes in gene expression associated with growth and seasonal variation. Key developmental periods were determined, by age, in the male blue catfish lifespan, such that: (i) growth parameters, like fish weight, length and testes weight gradually increased as fish aged; (ii) GSI remained variable throughout all ages (2.3 to 4.9 y/o), both increasing and decreasing; (iii) androgen levels (T and 11-KT) peaked at 3.11 y/o, then T levels decreased after 4.3 y/o and 11-KT levels remained steady; (iv) both SMI and ratio of mature spermatozoa heightened when fish were nearing 4 y/o; (v) genes associated with spermatogenesis and maturation were expressed between 3.1 vs. 2.9 y/o fish and 4.2 vs. 2.9 y/o fish; and (vi) pathways related to cilium movement and axoneme functions were expressed between 3.1 vs. 2.9 y/o fish and 4.2 vs. 2.9 y/o fish.

Morphometric growth is a standard marker for aging and determining maturity in many organisms [38]. To understand how and when sexual maturation occurs in commercially farmed male blue catfish, we measured morphometric parameters, such as body weight, total length, testes weight, and GSI. The first two parameters, body weight and total length, showed steady increases as fish aged from 2.3 to 4.9 y/o. Previous studies have shown 5 y/o male blue catfish ranged from 1.68 to 2.54 kg in weight and 51.20 to 59.00 cm in length [15]. Another study noted that the mean length and weight for 2 y/o male blue catfish was 35.50 cm and 0.60 kg, respectively [39]. From ages 2 to 3 y/o, we observed a mean body weight of 0.68 kg. At ages 3 to 4 y/o, the mean body weight was 1.34 kg. Beyond 4 y/o, body weight was, on average, 2.60 kg. Body weights of 2 to 3 y/o fish from our study are comparative to those of the immature fish, mentioned above [39, 40]. Additionally, fish starting at 4.2 y/o and beyond had similar weights and lengths of the 5 y/o fish, previously reported [15]. Weight for mature male blue catfish > 5 kg and length of > 70 cm has been observed but no age data was recorded [12, 29, 41]. Despite these findings, when comparing such research, we still need to be cognizant of potential confounding factors like pond sizes (we used 0.1-acre ponds), stocking densities [7], environmental conditions [42, 43], diet [44, 45], genetic stains [15], and associated gene × environmental interactions [46].

There has been a correlation between body size and initiation of testes development in male blue catfish, where, in 5 y/o fish, larger body size coincided with a larger GSI [15]. In other species, it has been shown that an increase in testes weight corresponded with the onset of maturation [47]. In blue catfish, 2 to 3 y/o males had testes that weighed 0.33 to 0.80 g [39, 40]. This is comparable to weights measured from our study during the same age. Another study observed a mean testis weight for mature male blue catfish (5 to 8 y/o) of 6.6 g [8]. Our highest mean testis weight was 4.84 g, observed at 4.5 y/o. However, we did sample one male at 4.7 y/o with a comparable testis weight of 6.95 g. We also reported GSI, which is used as an indicator of sexual maturity in many organisms [48]. One study conducted on different maturity stages in male blue catfish noted that most mature males had a mean GSI of 0.158 [21]. Our study observed very similar values at 3.11 and 4.5 y/o (0.14 and 0.18, respectively). The least mature males from the previously mentioned study had a mean GSI of 0.008, which was also the value we observed at 2.3 y/o [21]. Thus, we conclude the majority of the fish were immature up to ~ 4 y/o. Variation in both testis weight and GSI throughout the study also suggested that males were experiencing pubescent and early adult stages of development until they reached ~ 4 y/o. It is also notable to mention the effect of genetic strain on testes development in blue catfish, as it has been shown that D&B strain males typically have larger testes that contain more sperm over Texas and Missouri strains [15].

The androgens T and 11-KT play an important role in spermatogenesis, especially as 11-KT serves as the initiator of sperm cell proliferation, which is a key step in sperm cell maturation and development of spermatozoa [20]. For male fish, increases in androgen levels indicate reproductive readiness [19]. This was shown in Steindachneridion melanodermatum, a South American species of catfish, whose plasma androgen levels coincided with GSI and spawning capability [49]. In blue catfish, a previous study found a correlation between T levels and maturity, where T levels increased as fish aged from 2 to 5 y/o [21]. Our results indicated T levels increasing at 3.11 y/o, with the highest peak at 4.2 y/o. Afterwards, levels began to decrease again, though they remained higher than fish at 3.9 y/o and younger. The same trend was observed for 11-KT, however, after 4.2 y/o, levels remained steady. These results correspond with the onset age of sexual maturity in male blue catfish (4 y/o) [10]. A similar study conducted on African catfish (Clarias gariepinus) found comparable results, whereas levels of T and 11-KT were highest in maturer fish [50]. The seasonal timing at which androgen levels rise and fall should also be noted. Spawning season for blue catfish typically begins in April and ends in June, depending on latitude and corresponding water temperature. One study showed increased levels of both T and 11-KT for mature male blue catfish from March to July, however age was not reported [51]. The first significant increase for both T and 11-KT in our study was during April 2023 (3.11 y/o). Levels were especially high during July (4.2 y/o) and August (4.3 y/o) and declined in October (4.5 y/o), when environmental conditions are not suitable for spawning (< 21 °C) [52]. Compared to the previously mentioned study, we found similar trends in age related and seasonal effects on hormone levels as mature fish starting at 3.11 y/o.

Blood osmolality can change as fish develop, as shown by a study on the progression of zebrafish from larvae to adults [53]. Additionally, osmolality and ion concentrations can change seasonally, as shown in Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua) [54]. Osmolality is an important factor in sperm activation, as freshwater is usually lower in osmolality than fish internally [55]. Our study found a significant increase in osmolality only in fish 3.11 to 4.5 y/o. In salmonids, blood osmolality was similar to that of seminal plasma [56]. The same trend was shown in African catfish that ranged in ages 1 to 3 y/o, where average seminal plasma and blood osmolality differed by only 2 mOsm/kg [57]. This could explain why blood osmolality increased during summer months in our study, as it is also spawning season. Along with osmolality, we noticed changes in ions within the blood as fish aged and developed, specifically Na+ and K+, which increased in summer months. Such findings relate to ion levels in mature channel catfish, where Na+ was one of the most abundant [58]. More importantly, K+ and Na+ are described as predominant in seminal plasma and heavily influence sperm activity [55], which corresponded when increased levels occurred during spawning season in our study.

Histology is widely used as an indicator of maturity for many species, including fish [59]. In male fish, onset of maturity correlates with the progression of spermatogenesis, which can be observed through histological images [14]. A previous study conducted histological analysis on testis tissue of three different life stages in male blue catfish (~ 3 y/o, 2 y/o, and 4 y/o) and observed the following: at the youngest age, only spermatogonia and germ cells were observed, spermatocytes were prominent in the 2 y/o fish, and spermatids were found in the 4 y/o fish [60]. We found similar results in 2 y/o fish in our study, however in our 4 y/o fish, spermatozoa were present and highly abundant. This was most likely due to the timing of sampling for 4 y/o fish, as it was performed during blue catfish spawning season.

Initiation of puberty in fish is characterized by the initial presence of spermatozoa within fish testis [14]. The spermatogenic maturation index (SMI) is a function of both cell types present, and their abundance within the testis tissue, where values are a direct indicator of spermatogenic development [28]. Using images taken from histology, we can calculate SMI to show the progression of gametes and sperm production, as seen in the European eel (Anguilla anguilla), where SMI values increased linearly throughout testicular development [28]. We also observed a linear increase in SMI values throughout pubescent and early adult stages of development in male blue catfish, and in fish 3.11 to 4.9 y/o the highest values were reached. Previous work calculated SMI for mature male blue catfish, and reported an average value of 0.62 [61], while we observed a nearly identical value of 0.61 in 4.3 y/o fish (0.61). This is also the age where spermatozoa were abundant within the tissue. On the contrary, SMI values remained low until fish reached 3.1 y/o, where prior to this, connective tissue and spermatogonia were most abundant. We noticed that increases in SMI also correlated with rises in sex steroid hormone levels, indicating a relationship between hormone fluctuation and sperm cell development [14].

We observed upregulation of genes related to spermatogenesis and sperm development highly expressed in 3.1 and 4.2 y/o fish, both of which were sampled in the summer season (Table 3). Genes expressed were related to functions of cilia and flagella movement, as well as heat shock protein factors, especially during spermatogenesis. Expression of spermatogenic-related genes expressed at 3.1 y/o suggests that this age signifies the beginning of sexual maturation in blue catfish.

The gene cfap61 was upregulated in both 3.1 and 4.2 y/o fish. This gene functions in assembling sperm flagellum and structuring the radial spokes on the flagellum’s axoneme [62]. Previous research has shown that deletions of cfap61 can be detrimental to sperm and ultimately lead to male infertility [63]. A particular study on mice concluded that males with a homozygous cfap61 allele were infertile and sired no pups when used for breeding [64]. This study also observed that a homozygous cfap61 allele did not block spermatogenesis but rather mutated the length of sperm flagellum [64]. It can be concluded, then, that the presence of cfap61 could indicate sperm development as early as 3.1 y/o. In addition to cfap61, we observed four other genes within the same family downregulated in 3.9 y/o fish when compared to 3.1 y/o fish suggesting sperm development ceases out at the end of spawning season as expected for male blue catfish. Thus, detecting upregulation of cfap61 in 3.1 y/o fish could aid in predicting sexual maturation, as well as males with viable sperm for fertilization.

In addition to cfap61, HSF5 was highly upregulated in both 3.1 and 4.2 y/o fish. This heat shock transcription factor is important for spermatogenesis and progression of meiotic prophase in spermatocytes [65]. Without meiosis prophase progression of spermatocytes, cells will not divide and differentiate into mature spermatozoa [66]. In zebrafish, mutant individuals without HSF5 were deemed infertile and displayed reduced sperm count, inflated sperm heads, and sperm tail deformities [67]. Another member of the heat shock protein family, dnajb13, was found to be downregulated in 3.9 y/o fish when compared to 3.1 y/o fish. This protein is produced primarily in the testis and is found on the axoneme in sperm flagella [68]. One study found the presence of dnajb13 to be affiliated with spermatogenesis and mature spermatozoa in mice [69]. Thus, the absence of dnajb13 in 3.9 y/o fish compared to 3.1 y/o fish suggests that the latter group experienced spermatogenesis during the spawning season of 2022 but had already regressed sexually by February 2023.

There was significant upregulation of rsph9, a radial spoke head protein gene, in 3.1 and 4.2 y/o fish. This gene is associated with normal motility in sperm cilia and flagella [70]. Cilia and flagella share a structure known as the 9 + 2 axoneme that regulates their beating, with one of its associated protein complexes being the radial spokes [71]. Knockdowns of this gene in zebrafish resulted in dysmotility of cilia within the sperm [70]. Upregulation of this gene in our study points towards proper cilia and flagella movement within blue catfish sperm as early as 3.1 y/o.

No notable genes associated with spermatogenesis, sperm or testicular development, or sexual maturation were upregulated in 2.9 or 3.9 y/o fish. This would suggest that 2.9 y/o fish were not sexually mature enough, and neither of these groups were influenced by seasonal spawning cues. We did, however, observe the downregulation of sfrp1b in 3.9 y/o fish, as well as 4.2 y/o fish, compared to 2.9 y/o fish. This gene functions in Wnt-protein binding; the Wnt pathway consists of proteins involved in embryonic development [72]. Most research involved with sfrp1b is related to human health, though one study performed on zebrafish expressed sfrp1b at the tip of the yolk of an embryo [73]. It is unclear why this gene would be expressed in the testis of male blue catfish; thus, future research should investigate whether sfrp1b has any impacts on spawning and fertilization capability.

We also observed downregulation of dmrt2a and usp21 in 3.9 y/o fish. The gene dmrt2a has shown to be expressed through the development of gonads [74]. As such, downregulation of dmrt2a in 3.9 y/o fish suggests that testes were already developing and differentiated. The gene usp21 acts as a deubiquitylate, which removes ubiquitin molecules and stabilizes proteins [75]. In frogs (Xenopus laevis), usp21 was used to remove H2AK119ub1, present in spermiogenesis and embryonic development, from sperm chromatin before fertilization. Eggs fertilized with the usp21 sperm resulted in poor development [76]. The significance of usp21 downregulated in 3.9 y/o fish could allude to sperm not being mature and capable of fertilizing successfully, though future research should expand on this.

Upregulation of gstt1b in 3.9 y/o fish was also observed. This gene belongs to the glutathione-s-transferases (GST) family, whose role in antioxidation helps mitigate against toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) within a cell [77]. Too much ROS causes oxidative stress, which negatively impacts sperm quality [78]. Studies on human males have observed infertility and irregular sperm movement when gstt1b is mutated or knocked out [77, 78]. The process of sperm maturation results in increased polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) found within the plasma membrane, making sperm more susceptible to ROS [79]. Because gstt1b was upregulated in 3.9 y/o fish (Feb 2023), we can speculate that sperm were in the process of maturing and differentiating, therefore protection against oxidative stress within the cell was needed.

Gene ontology enrichment analysis (GO analysis) has been widely used among different studies, including those interested in spermatogenesis and sexual maturation in large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea) [80], turbot (Scophthalmus maximus) [81], and gilthead seabream (Sparus aurata) [82], among others. Most terms expressed by GO analysis in our study were shown in 3.1 and 4.2 y/o fish, when compared to 2.9 y/o fish. The common term amongst both groups is motile cilium; previous work done on blue catfish sperm also noted the expression of motile cilium function as related to sperm motility [41]. Other cilium-related terms (cilium movement and cilium-dependent cell motility) were only observed in 3.1 y/o fish. Thus, it can be suggested that the initial presence of these related genes at 3.1 y/o indicates onset of sexual maturity and sperm development. Oddly enough, we observed a very low number of genes related to cilium movement in 3.9 y/o fish compared to 3.1 y/o, which should be further investigated to determine their presence in out-of-season fish. Additionally, two axoneme-related terms were also expressed in 3.1 y/o fish. The axoneme functions in the formation of the sperm tail, which is important for proper sperm motility [83]. Again, the presence of these terms and the genes affiliated with them suggest the start of sperm development at 3.1 y/o and during summer seasons.

Conclusions

Our data provides the most comprehensive assessment of relationships between morphometric measurements, plasma composition, testes histology and gene expression in blue catfish males as they move through the maturation process. Fish were sampled over 34 months from 2.3 to 4.9 y/o (Aug 2021 to Feb 2024). Over the duration of this project, we employed a multi-dimensional approach, where we quantified physiological, cellular, and molecular indices. We showed that seasonality and age directly impacted body metrics, testes morphology, and sex steroid hormones. Our data also showed various genetic and molecular trends, with several genes and pathways related to spermatogenesis expressed in 3.1 and 4.2 y/o fish. Together, these data provide a better understanding of mechanisms underlying reproduction, and gonadal development at cellular, molecular, organ system (testes), and whole-animal levels. Knowledge gained may be used to improve reproductive efficiency for an economically important farm-raised species (catfish) by considering not only age of maturity, but key phenotypic and genotypic biomarkers related to maturation.
