Comparative impacts of myco-synthesized nanoparticles against strains of Aspergillus spp. causing biodeterioration of stored cocoa beans in Nigeria
Authors: Olayinka Oluyemi Oluranti, Babatunde Ayodeji Ogundeji, Samuel Bukola Orisajo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Silver nanoparticle solutions (AgNPs) of some mushrooms: Pleurotus ostreatus, Agaricus bisporus and Agaricus campestris were prepared and characterized using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis and Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy. Each of the myco-sythesized AgNPs was plated against strains of Aspergillus flavus and A. ochraceous, at 5, 10 and 15% concentrations. Colour change from light yellow to orange, yellowish-brown, and reddish brown was observed after overnight incubation (at 28 °C), in the P. ostreatus, A. bisporus, and A. campestris synthesized AgNPs respectively. TEM analysis showed a spherical shape with an average size of 15.25 to 45.85 nm, 9.22 to 52.60 nm and 10.24 to 17.66 nm in P. ostreatus, A. campestris and A. bisporus AgNPs respectively. EDX spectrum showed absorption peaks of silver in the ranges of 0.8–1.4 keV, 6.2–6.6 keV, and 0.8–1.2 keV, and XRD analysis confirmed the crystalline structure of the biosynthesized AgNPs, while FTIR results revealed O–H, N–H, C=O, and C=N as the prominent functional groups. Mycelial inhibitions against A. flavus strains D28AF and D42AF ranged between 43.86–52.73% and 33.83–57.07% respectively, and were not significantly different (P ≤ 0.05) from the standard (copper sulphate). Inhibitions produced against A. ochraceous strains AOD40 and AOD45 ranged between 34.64–52.36% and 37.43–53.56% respectively and also showed similar trend in relation to the standard. This study showed that the myco-synthesized AgNPs were effective against A. flavus and A. ochraceous infecting cocoa beans at storage. They however need to be further improved for future use in the control of cocoa beans pathogens.

Introduction

Nanoparticles (NPs) are nanomaterials (of zero-dimensions) which exhibit diverse morphologies, dimensions, and chemistry. They have found wide applications and can be generated through several physical, chemical, and other techniques. However, in the quest to design environmental friendly procedures for the synthesis of NPs, the focus of researchers has been greatly shifted from chemical methods to biological systems. Due to their minute sizes, nanoparticles, like viral particles can easily pass through common filters and can only be viewed under electron or laser microscopes [1].

Mushroom-derived inorganic nanoparticles can be synthesized from both the fruiting body and the mycelium of mushrooms. Metallic compounds such as silver nitrate (AgNO3), zinc sulfide (ZnS), cadmium sulfide (CdS), ferrous sulfate (FeSO4), etc. have been used in the synthesis of inorganic NPs from edible and medicinal mushrooms. Mycosynthesis of AgNPs, which involved the role of proteins of the spent mushroom substrate (SMS) for synthesis was first reported by Vigneshwaran et al. [2]. Varying shapes, sizes, and topographies of the AgNPs have been reported from mycelial and fruit body extracts of various popular edible and medicinal mushrooms like Pleurotus florida, Pleurotus sajor-caju, Agaricus bisporus, Tricholoma crassum, Tricholoma matsutake, Ganoderma neo-japonicum, Ganoderma lucidum, Inonotus obliquus, and Calocyble indica. The use of Agaricus bisporus, Pleurotus sapidus, Inonotus obliquus, Ganoderma spp. and Flammulina velutipes for the mycosynthesis of AuNPs has also been reported [3].

Volvariella volvacea extract has been used for the extracellular synthesis of Au, Ag, and Au–Ag NPs in water. The mushroom-derived AgNP suspensions exhibit better stability which can be attributed to the capping action of the mushroom macromolecules. Since the dawn of nanotechnology, AgNPs have been lauded for excellent antimicrobial activities. Most reports have consequentlybacked the evaluation of the synthesized AgNPs against a wide range of plant and human pathogens [3].

Fungi are important group of microorganisms involved in the breaking down of organic matter, thereby playing a key role in balancing the ecosystems. They are responsible for about 5–10% of all food waste and loss, especially in developing countries.The organisms are also involved in the contamination of foods, thereby causing spoilage and undesirable effects like the production of toxic metabolites (mycotoxins), thus impairing the health of consumers, especially those individuals whose immunity has been compromised [4].

Fungi have been discovered in stored dried cocoa beans. Aspergillus niger, A. flavus, Botrydiplodiatheobromae, Fusarium spp., Mucor spp., Neurospora spp., Penicillium spp., Phytophtora palmivora,and Rhizopus spp. were isolated from cocoa beans during storage in Ekiti State, Nigeria [4]. According to Copetti et al. [5], fungi isolated from cocoa beans at various stages of processing include Absidia corymbifera, Aspergillus sp., A. flavus, A. parasiticus, A. nomius, A. carbonarius, A.ochraceus, Penicillium paneum, and yeasts. Highest occurrences of the fungal isolates were recorded in the cocoa beans samples during drying and storage.

Mycotoxin contamination of cocoa beans as well as other foods and feeds in Nigeria has become a major concern to scientists. Consumption of either processed/fermented or raw cocoa beans contaminated with such heat-stable microbial secondary metabolites poses health hazards to the consumers, as it can result in some chronic mycotoxicoses, acute poisoning, and death. Besides this, cocoa beans and other food items with high levels of mycotoxins suffer outright rejection and economic loss to Nigerian farmers in the international market. Though sun-drying has been an effective method of controlling mycotoxin production in dried cocoa beans, it has however been found not to be practicably effective especially during wet seasons. Hence, some farmers’ resort to the use of synthetic chemicals (which are known to deposit dangerous residues in the beans).

Although in order to salvage the prevailing situation, some authors have in the recent past, experimented the use of some extracts of certain medicinal plants against toxigenic moulds of cocoa beans, none of the plants used can be said to be suitable for in vivo application on the beans because of the odour they are naturally able to impact on the commodity, thereby seriously lowering the latter’s flavour quality.

In view of the above, there is an urgent need to scout for suitable, safe, and effective means of controlling toxigenic fungal attacks on fermented, dried/stored cocoa beans. Hence, the use of green-synthesized nanoparticles of selected edible/medicinal mushroom species, to prevent undesirable odour on the cocoa beans when used in vivo. This study therefore aimed at determining the impact of mushroom (myco)-synthesized silver nanoparticles against strains of Aspergillus spp. associated with the biodeterioration of stored cocoa beans in Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Preparation of mushroom extracts

Samples of mushrooms: Pleurotus ostreatus (oyster mushroom), Agaricus bisporus and Agaricus campestris were obtained from a grocery shop in Ibadan, Nigeria. They were separately cut into small pieces with a sterile knife, oven-dried at 50 ± 2 °C for 24 h, and ground to a fine powder with the aid of a blender. Four grammes (4 g) of each of the ground powdered samples were separately weighed and dispersed in 100 ml of sterile distilled water. The mushroom suspensions obtained were then heated in the water bath for 15 min at 50–55 °C. The heated aqueous mushroom extract suspensions were thereafter filtered through Whatman filter paper No.1 and the filtrates were preserved at 4 °C until further use [6].

Myco-synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs)

Five millilitres (5 ml) of the prepared aqueous mushroom extracts (filtrates) was mixed with 95 ml of 0.01 M silver nitrate (AgNO3) laboratory grade solution. The mixtures were then stirred and kept in the dark overnight at ambient temperature (28 °C). The colour changes in the mushroom extract-AgNO3 solutions were observed and noted after the overnight incubation period.

Characterization of myco-synthesized AgNPs

Characterization of the mushroom-mediated (myco-synthesized) silver nanoparticles was done using Debnath et al. [6] methods. Morphological characterization of the biosynthesized AgNPs was done using the Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis with the aid of a JEM-ARM200F-G TEM microscope. A drop of solution containing synthesized AgNPs on a carbon-coated copper grid was used for TEM analysis. Composition of elements in the biosynthesized AgNPs was determined using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX).

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed on each of the samples for the evaluation of the presence of possible functional groups in the mushroom extract responsible for the capping and efficient stabilization of the synthesized AgNPs. Pellets of AgNPs were prepared by centrifuging the reaction mixture at 10,000 rpm for 15 min and dried. FTIR spectrum was recorded in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 and KBr pellet was used in the spectral method. A Nicolet iS10 FT-IR Spectrometer was used for FTIR analysis.

X-ray diffraction analysis

The nano-particle samples were pelletized and sieved to 0.074 mm. They were later taken in an aluminum alloy grid (35 × 50 mm) on a flat glass plate and covered with paper. Wearing hand gloves, the samples were compacted by gently pressing them with the hand.

Each sample was run through the Rigaku D/Max-IIIC X-ray diffractometer developed by the Rigaku Int. Corp. Tokyo, Japan and set to produce diffractions at a scanning rate of 2 0/min in the 2 to 500 at room temperature with a CuKa radiation set at 40 kV and 20 mA. The diffraction data (d value and relative intensity) obtained was compared to that of the standard data of minerals from the mineral powder diffraction file, ICDD which contained and includes the standard data of more than 3000 minerals.

Determination of the antifungal activity of myco-synthesized AgNPs

Antifungal potentials of the synthesized AgNPs were studied against two strains of Aspergillus flavus (AFD28 and AFD42) and A. ochraceous (AOD40 and AOD45) earlier isolated from stored cocoa beans at Plant Pathology (Mycology) Laboratory, Cocoa Research Institute of Nigeria (CRIN), Ibadan, Nigeria. Each of the strains was separately inoculated/cultured on freshly prepared potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates. One milliliter, 2 ml, and 3 ml of each of the prepared AgNPs were also separately incorporated into 20 ml freshly prepared, sterile but cooled (45 °C) PDA plates [7]. Copper sulphate solution (fungicide fondly used by cocoa store farmers) was also prepared at 1% concentration (as recommended by the manufacturers) and 1 ml of it was separately incorporated into 20 ml PDA plates. Each of the pour-plated plates was gently rocked to ensure even distribution of the incorporated solutions in the growth medium.

The plates were allowed to set. Immediately after, agar plugs of each of the actively growing isolates were obtained with the aid of a sterile cork borer (8 mm diameter) and were separately inoculated into the poisoned plates. Some PDA plates without any nanoparticle or copper sulphate solution incorporated, were also inoculated with agar plugs of the isolates.

The inoculated plates and their controls were incubated at 30 ± 2 °C and the mycelia growth diameters of the isolates were taken once in two days until the fungi growths covered the surfaces of the untreated control plates. TEM study also revealed that some of the synthesized silver nanoparticles (particularly those of P. ostreatus and Agaricus campestris) were agglomerated. The Energy Dispersive X-Ray (EDX) spectrum of green synthesized AgNPs analyzed the elementary compositions of the extract samples (Figs. 7, 8, 9). Discussion

The colour change observed in this study with P. ostreatus agrees with the findings of Debnath et al. [6], who synthesized silver nanoparticles from extracts of wild edible mushroom, Pleurotus giganteus with a colour change from light yellow to yellowish brown. The similarity in the colour change might be because the two mushrooms belong to the same genus (Pleurotus). Part of the findings from this work partially agrees with Narasima et al. [8] who reported a dark brown colour change (indicating the synthesis of silver nanoparticles) in an aqueous Agaricus bisporus extract silver nitrate mixture. The changes in the colour of the reaction were indications of the formation of silver nanoparticles. The active molecules present in the mushroom basidiocarp extracts were responsible for the reduction of the silver metal ions into AgNPs. The change of colour of the reaction mixture occurred due to the surface plasmon resonance excitation (SPR) of metallic silver nanoparticles (AgNPs); SPR band arises due to the combined vibration of free electrons of AgNPs in resonance to light wave [9]. The absorbance peak at 440 nm may belong to finding the silver nanoparticles. The mechanism of the synthesis of AgNPs is not known, but from recent studies, intracellular synthesis of AgNPs relates tonitrate reductase which releasesan electron from NADH. This electron reduces Ag + to Ag 0 and formsNAD + .

The AgNPs formation was confirmed by the maximum absorption peak at 450 nm. Also, the width of this peak is a sign of poly-dispersed nanoparticles. The secondary structure of proteins of Pleurotus ostreatus is stable because of their interaction with Ag + or the nanoparticle and the number of aggregates. P. ostreatus—NPs had spherical shapes and sizes reached approx. 45 nm. The functional groups of carboxylic acids, alcohols and aldehydes present in the silver nanoparticle sample may be responsible for reducing AgNO3 to AgNPs. The maximum peak showed absorbance at 425 nm for AgNPs of glucan of grey oyster mushrooms. P. florida due to SPR of P. florida—AgNPs. These nanoparticles have a size reaching 2.4 nm [9]. Sometimes, mushroom-mediated synthesis of AgNPs had crystalline structures in a spherical or similar spherical shape with sizes near 100 nm. While Sujatha et al. [10] referred to the synthesis of silver nanoparticles from 560 to 710 nm using oyster mushrooms (P. ostreatus). From another hand, the biosynthesized AgNPs exhibited forceful antimicrobial activities toward different pathogenic fungi and bacteria.

The agglomeration observed in the transmission electron microscopy of some of the synthesized silver nanoparticles is in partial variance from the findings of Singh et al. [11] and Debnath et al. [6] who carried out Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis on silver nanoparticles synthesized from a wild edible mushroom, Pleurotus giganteus. The synthesized AgNPs observed by the authors were mostly spherical in shape and not agglomerated; thus indicating the uniformity of the particle size. Findings from this research however agree with the discovery of Narasimha et al. [8] who worked on the synthesis of silver nanoparticles from Agaricus bisporus and noticed a few agglomerated silver nanoparticles of the mushroom extract in the course of their research.

Results of the EDX peak obtained in this study partially agree with the findings of Aina et al. [12] and Islam et al. [13], who discovered a strong signal peak around 1.5 keV. Aina et al. [12] specifically got this peak (1.5 keV) from the silver nanoparticles synthesized from a common edible mushroom sample (Lentinus squarrosulus). Islam et al. [13] have similarly reported observing peaks within the range of 1.5–5.0 keV from AgNPs synthesized from the mushroom extracts. The appearance of copper and carbon peaks in the EDX spectra wasbecause of the carbon-coated copper grid base used for the analysis. Along with silver, the appearance of other metals such as potassium (K), iron (Fe), carbon (C), oxygen (O) and nitrogen (N) in the graph might be due to their presence in the mushroom extract [6]. Some, apart from C and O might however be impurities [12].

The discovery of N–H, C=O, and C=N biomolecules in Agaricus campestris and A. bisporus during this study are in perfect agreement with the findings of Narasimha et al. [8] who also carried out similar FTIR analysis on A. bisporus silver nanoparticles, and discovered the prominence of amine (N–H), carbonyl (C=O) and nitro compounds in the mushroom biosynthesized silver nanoparticles. The three functional groups are mainly responsible for the reduction of the silver ions to metallic silver and acted as capping agents for the stabilization of synthesized AgNPs [14]. In the present investigation, the obtained XRD pattern of biosynthesized AgNPs agrees with the findings of Narasimha et al. [8]. The XRD pattern in the mushroom samples used in this study also shows peaks in the whole spectrum of 2θ values which fell within the ranges of 15–65, 10–55, and 15–62 discovered in P. ostreatus, A. bisporus and A. campelloris synthesized nanoparticles respectively by Narasimha et al. [8].The exact pure crystalline nature of the silver nanoparticles formed was deduced from the XRD spectrum of the samples [8]. A comparison of our XRD spectrum with the standard confirmed that the silver particles formed were in the form of nanocrystals, as evidenced by the peaks at 2θ values.

Findings from this research revealed that both the Pleurotus and Agaricus extracts—synthesized silver nanoparticles showed moderate antimicrobial effect (42–56% mycelial inhibition) in some favourable competition with the reference fungicide/standard (copper sulphate solution) against toxigenic fungi strains used in this study. This moderate antimicrobial efficacy is in agreement with the findings of Al-Bahrani et al. [15], which suggested that synthesized P. ostreatus AgNPs showed moderately effective or better antibacterial activity compared to the efficacy of antibiotics such as streptomycin, ampicillin, and gentamycin. Hence, their potential usefulness in the health sectors.

The antimicrobial activities observed in the AgNPs used in this study may be the result of a loss of replication activity that inactivates the cellular proteins and enzymes of the pathogens. The antimicrobial potentials can also be increased by modifying the surface with the mediations of various extractions or different combinations with antimicrobials [16]. Results obtained from this research agree with the findings of Akpinar et al. [16] who in an experiment to test for the efficacy of selected AgNPs against Fusarium spp., got high mycelia inhibition range of between 40 and 98%, thus serving as a confirmation of antimicrobial potentials in metal-based nanoparticles, and that increase in the concentration of AgNPs increases antifungal potentials. Although the average size ranges of AgNPs generated from mushrooms used in this study vary in this order: Agaricus bisporus < A. campestris ≤ P. ostreatus, the resulting antifungal effects against the Aspergillus flavus and A. ochraceous strains were generally within the same range (33.83–57.07%). These findings run contrary to the assertion of Akpinar et al. [16] that the sizes of silver nanoparticles affect their antimicrobial efficiencies against Fusarium spp.

Conclusion

Pleurotus ostreatus, Agaricus campestris and A. bisporus synthesized silver nanoparticles used in this study contained mainly alcohols/phenols, carboxylic acids, benzene, amine and carbonyl groups (as revealed by the FTIR spectra), which were mainly responsible for the moderate inhibition of the mycelia growth/extension of some strains of A. flavus and A. ochraceous that cause biodeterioration of cocoa beans at storage. Thus, indicating their effectiveness against the storage pathogens in vitro. The myco-syhthesized AgNPs however need to be further studied in vivo and improved for future use, in the control of cocoa beans storage pathogens.
