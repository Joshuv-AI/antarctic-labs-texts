Hajime Otsuka and Yutaka Sakamura, *[20pt] Department of Physics, Kyushu University,744 Motooka, Nishi-ku, Fukuoka, 819-0395, Japan

Abstract




Abstract

We investigate the response of the Kaluza-Klein (KK) mass spectrum to various deformations of the rugby-ball background
in 6-dimensional supergravity.
We derived the mode equations that contain the 3-dimensional scale factor and the lapse function.
By solving these, we numerically evaluate the KK masses for a bulk scalar and a spinor
when the background has a nontrivial dependence on the position in the compact space.
We clarify some qualitative features of the spectrum deformation for some perturbations of the rugby-ball background.
For perturbations of a supersymmetric background,
we find that the mass-splitting between bosonic and fermionic modes
is much smaller than the deviation from the values of the supersymmetric mass eigenvalues.

Introduction

Models with large extra dimensions have been attracted some attention
for various reasons
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
Some of them predict a small cosmological constant, which is identified with the Casimir energy for the compact space,
and are discussed in the context of the cosmological constant problem.
Besides, such large extra dimensions affect the cosmological history at early times.
If the temperature in the radiation-dominated era
T_{\rm rad}
is much higher than the compactification scale
m_{\rm KK}
,
the Kaluza-Klein (KK) modes affect the expansion rate of the universe in that era
because they contribute to the energy density and the pressure of the radiation.

In our previous papers
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
,
we pursue the time evolution of the space and the background values of the moduli
in a six-dimensional (6D) model compactified on a sphere
S^{2}
by numerically solving the field equations.
The model is basically the Salam-Sezgin setup
[
8
]
,
which is based on the gauged 6D
{\cal N}=(1,0)
supergravity (SUGRA)
[
9
,
10
]
.
We found that if
T_{\rm rad}\gg m_{\rm KK}
, the expansion rate for the three-dimensional (3D) non-compact space deviates
from the usual four-dimensional (4D) cosmology.
We also found that the moduli oscillation is induced by the pressure for the two-dimensional (2D) compact space,
even if the moduli are stabilized at the initial time.
In these works, we considered a case that there are no branes and the compact space has a spherical symmetry, for simplicity.
In order to construct a realistic model, however, we need to introduce a 3-brane on which the standard model particles live.
1
1

1

Since we consider large extra dimensions, the standard model cannot live in the bulk because the gauge coupling constants are suppressed
by the large volume of the compact space.

In this paper, we introduce two 3-branes that are set at antipodal points, and analyze the KK mass spectrum.
They break the spherical symmetry, and the background can have a nontrivial dependence on the position in the compact space.
This makes the analysis of the KK spectrum complicated.
In the 6D SUGRA, the number of hypermultiplets
n_{H}
and that of vector multiplets
n_{V}
are constrained
by the anomaly cancellation condition
n_{H}-n_{V}=244

[
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
This indicates the existence of many hypermultiplets in the bulk.
As a representative of them, we consider a 6D scalar and a 6D spinor to analyze the KK mass spectrum.
When the background has the 4D Lorentz symmetry,
the KK masses are determined solely by the information on the compact space geometry.
In contrast, for cosmological discussions, the background does not have such a symmetry
and the scale factor for the 3D non-compact space often
has a nontrivial dependence on the position of the extra dimensions.
In such a case, the 3D scale factor also affects the KK masses.
The information on the KK spectrum is indispensable for calculating the energy density and the pressures of the radiation,
or the Casimir energy for the compact space.
In the model we consider, the curvature of the compact space and the magnetic flux threading the extra dimensions also contribute
to the moduli stabilization, in addition to the scalar potential.
Such a situation is often encountered in string theory.
Hence our setup can also be regarded as a simplified version of the string-theoretical models.

3
KK mass spectrum of bulk scalar

In this section, we discuss the Kaluza-Klein (KK) mass spectrum of a bulk scalar field
\Phi
.
This information is necessary to calculate the Casimir energy or the energy density and the pressure of the radiation in the early universe.
When we consider the radiation contribution to the time evolution of the universe,
we assume that the bulk relativistic particles are in the thermal equilibrium achieved by fast motion of the particles
compared to the time scale of the background space expansion.
In such a case, we can neglect the time-dependence of the background.
Thus, we treat the background fields

,
a
,
b
,

\sigma

as functions of only

\displaystyle S_{\rm scalar}

\displaystyle=-\int d^{6}x\;\sqrt{-g^{(6)}}{\cal D}^{M}\Phi^{\dagger}{\cal D}_{M}\Phi+\cdots,

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{M}

\displaystyle\equiv\partial_{M}-is_{\rm b}A_{M},

\displaystyle E_{M}^{\;\;A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}n&&&\\
&a\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{3}&&\\

5
Summary

We derived the mode equations for a bulk scalar and a bulk spinor,
including the lapse function and the 3D scale factor, and evaluate the KK masses for backgrounds
that have nontrivial dependence on the position in the compact space.
In particular, we consider several perturbations of the rugby-ball background in 6D SUGRA
[
15
,
2
,
16
]
.
By integrating the mode equations, we obtain the function
{\cal F}_{q}(\lambda_{q})
whose zeros are the KK mass eigenvalues.
Roughly, the KK masses are determined by the volume of the compact space.
However, when the 3D scale factor (or the lapse function) depends on the extra-dimensional coordinates
y^{m}
(
m=1,2
),
it also affects the KK masses.
In fact, after the moduli stabilization, the scale factors generically depend on
y^{m}

[
19
,
20
]
.
In order to understand the effects of various background deformations on the spectrum,
we numerically evaluate the KK masses by solving
{\cal F}_{q}(\lambda_{q})=0
, and obtain the following results.

•

The lower KK modes are less sensitive to a nontrivial
\theta
-dependence of the extra-dimensional scale factor
b
(or
c
).

•

The effect of the 3D scale factor
a
on the spectrum is small compared to that of the extra-dimensional one,
but the lower KK modes may receive a non-negligible contribution.

•

From the mass formulae, the SUSY background only allows R-neutral hypermultiplets (i.e.,
s_{\rm b}=0
).

•

For a deformation of the (SUSY) rugby-ball background,
the mass-splitting between a boson and a fermion is much smaller than the deviation from (
4.35
).

In the evaluation of the KK masses
m_{p,q}
, we have dropped the
t
-dependence of the background.
In the cosmological evolution, the lapse function
n
and the scale factors
a,b,c
change from moment to moment.
Thus, the time dependence of
m_{p,q}
is taken into account through the time evolution of the functions
\{n,a,b,c\}

in the mode equations.

The function
{\cal F}_{q}(\lambda_{q})
defined in (
3.29
) or (
4.24
) is also useful
when we sum up all the contributions of the KK modes by using the analytic continuation and the contour integral for the complex plane
[
21
]
.
In fact, the energy density and the pressure of the radiation in the early universe, or the Casimir energy for the compact space can be
calculated by such a method.
Those quantities play important roles in cosmological discussions.
In our subsequent works, we will investigate the scale-factor dependencies on them,
and pursue their time evolutions by numerically solving the 6D Einstein equations.

Appendix A
Flux quantization

From (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), the Lagrangian terms involving the field strength are
[
22
]

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm FF}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-g^{(6)}}\left[-\frac{e^{\sigma}}{4g_{\rm gc}^{2}}F^{MN}F_{MN}+\xi_{+}e^{\sigma}\epsilon^{mn}F_{mn}\delta^{(2)}(y-y_{+})+\xi_{-}e^{\sigma}\epsilon^{mn}F_{mn}\delta^{(2)}(y-y_{-})\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-g^{(6)}}\left[-\frac{e^{\sigma}}{2g_{\rm gc}^{2}b^{2}c^{2}}F_{\theta\phi}^{2}+\frac{2\xi_{+}e^{\sigma}}{bc}F_{\theta\phi}\delta^{(2)}(y-y_{+})+\frac{2\xi_{-}e^{\sigma}}{bc}F_{\theta\phi}\delta^{(2)}(y-y_{-})+\cdots\right].

(A.1)

From (
3.4
), the field strength
F_{\theta\phi}
has the following profile in the bulk.

\displaystyle F^{\rm(bulk)}_{\theta\phi}(\theta)

\displaystyle=C_{\theta\phi}\frac{b(\theta)c(\theta)}{n(\theta)a^{3}(\theta)}e^{-\sigma(\theta)}.

(A.2)

Due to the brane-localized flux terms,

also has the contributions proportional to the delta functions.
From (
A.1
), they are identified as

\displaystyle F_{\theta\phi}

\displaystyle\equiv F^{\rm(bulk)}_{\theta\phi}-2g_{\rm gc}^{2}bc\left[\xi_{+}\delta^{(2)}(y-y_{+})+\xi_{-}\delta^{(2)}(y-y_{-})\right].

(A.3)

Here, the 2D delta function

is defined so that

\displaystyle\int_{{\cal A}_{\pm}}d^{2}y\;\sqrt{\det(g_{mn})}\delta^{(2)}(y-y_{\pm})

\displaystyle=1,

(A.4)

where
{\cal A}_{\pm}
is a region that contains

, and the anti-symmetric tensor
\epsilon^{mn}
is defined as

\displaystyle\epsilon^{12}

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{21}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(g_{mn})}}=\frac{1}{bc},

\displaystyle\epsilon^{11}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{22}=0.

(A.5)

Here let us consider a region
{\cal A}_{+}=\left\{(\theta,\phi)|0\leq\theta\leq\theta_{*},0\leq\phi<2\pi\right\}
,
where
\theta_{*}
is a constant smaller than
\pi
.
From the Stokes theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\;A_{\phi}^{(+)}(\theta_{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\cal A}_{+}}d^{2}y\;F_{\theta\phi}

Since

is independent of
\phi

Using (

), the right-hand-side is

(RHS)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\cal A}_{+}}d^{2}y\;F_{\theta\phi}^{\rm(bulk)}-2g_{\rm gc}^{2}\xi_{+}=2\pi\int_{0}^{\theta_{*}}d\theta\;C_{\theta\phi}\frac{b(\theta)c(\theta)}{n(\theta)a^{3}(\theta)}e^{-\sigma(\theta)}-2g_{\rm gc}^{2}\xi_{+}.

(A.7)

We have used (
3.4
) in the second equation.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle A_{\phi}^{(+)}(\theta_{*})

\displaystyle=C_{\theta\phi}\int_{0}^{\theta_{*}}d\theta\;\frac{b(\theta)c(\theta)}{n(\theta)a^{3}(\theta)}e^{-\sigma(\theta)}-\frac{g_{\rm gc}^{2}\xi_{+}}{\pi}.

(A.8)

Similarly, for a region
{\cal A}_{-}\equiv S^{2}\setminus{\cal A}_{+}=\left\{(\theta,\phi)|\theta_{*}<\theta\leq\pi,0\leq\phi<2\pi\right\}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle A_{\phi}^{(-)}(\theta_{*})

\displaystyle=-C_{\theta\phi}\int_{\theta_{*}}^{\pi}d\theta\;\frac{b(\theta)c(\theta)}{n(\theta)a^{3}(\theta)}e^{-\sigma(\theta)}+\frac{g_{\rm gc}^{2}\xi_{-}}{\pi}.

(A.9)

Note that

and

are related through the R gauge transformation,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(+)}(x)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(is_{\rm b}\vartheta(x)\right)\Phi^{(-)}(x),

\displaystyle A_{\phi}^{(+)}(x)

\displaystyle=A_{\phi}^{(-)}(x)+\partial_{\phi}\vartheta(x).

(A.10)

From (
A.8
) and (

), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\phi}\vartheta

\displaystyle=A_{\phi}^{(+)}-A_{\phi}^{(-)}

\displaystyle=C_{\theta\phi}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\;\frac{b(\theta)c(\theta)}{n(\theta)a^{3}(\theta)}e^{-\sigma(\theta)}-\frac{g_{\rm gc}^{2}}{\pi}\left(\xi_{+}+\xi_{-}\right),

(A.11)

which is a constant.
Therefore, the transition function
\vartheta(x)
is identified as

\displaystyle\vartheta(\phi)

\displaystyle=\phi\left[C_{\theta\phi}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\;\frac{b(\theta)c(\theta)}{n(\theta)a^{3}(\theta)}e^{-\sigma(\theta)}-\frac{g_{\rm gc}^{2}}{\pi}\left(\xi_{+}+\xi_{-}\right)\right]+\mbox{(constant)}.

(A.12)

From the single-valuedness of a scalar field
\Phi
in the overlapped region of the coordinate patches, we obtain the quantization condition,

\displaystyle s_{\rm b}C_{\rm\theta\phi}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\;\frac{b(\theta)c(\theta)}{n(\theta)a^{3}(\theta)}e^{-\sigma(\theta)}-\frac{s_{\rm b}g_{\rm gc}^{2}}{\pi}\left(\xi_{+}+\xi_{-}\right)

\displaystyle=k_{\rm b},

(A.13)

where

\displaystyle s_{\rm f}C_{\rm\theta\phi}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\;\frac{b(\theta)c(\theta)}{n(\theta)a^{3}(\theta)}e^{-\sigma(\theta)}-\frac{s_{\rm f}g_{\rm gc}^{2}}{\pi}\left(\xi_{+}+\xi_{-}\right)

where

\displaystyle\simeq c_{1}J_{0}\left(\hat{\lambda}_{q}(\pi-\theta)\right)+c_{2}Y_{0}\left(\hat{\lambda}_{q}(\pi-\theta)\right)

\displaystyle\simeq\mbox{constant}.

(C.13)

In summary,
f(\theta;q)
behaves near
\theta=\pi
as

\displaystyle f\left(\theta;q\right)

\displaystyle\simeq{\cal O}\left(\left(\pi-\theta\right)^{-\absolutevalue{\alp_{\rm b}}}\right).

(C.14)

C.2
Spinor case

We first consider the behaviors near
\theta=0

From (
3.5

C.1
),
{\cal D}_{q}(\theta)
defined in (

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{q}(\theta)

\displaystyle\simeq-s_{\rm f}r_{0}D_{0}\theta^{2}+r_{0}\zeta_{\rm f},

(C.15)

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{\rm f}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{r_{0}}\left(q+\frac{1}{2}+s_{\rm f}\tilde{\xi}\right),

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{\theta}^{2}+\frac{\zeta_{\rm f}}{\theta^{2}}+s_{\rm f}D_{0}+\tilde{\lambda}_{q}^{2}-\frac{\left(\zeta_{\rm f}-s_{\rm f}D_{0}\theta^{2}\right)^{2}}{\theta^{2}}\right]\tilde{h}_{\rm R}(\theta;q)

\displaystyle\simeq 0,

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{\theta}^{2}-\frac{\zeta_{\rm f}}{\theta^{2}}-s_{\rm f}D_{0}+\tilde{\lambda}_{q}^{2}-\frac{\left(\zeta_{\rm f}-s_{\rm f}D_{0}\theta^{2}\right)^{2}}{\theta^{2}}\right]\tilde{h}_{\rm L}(\theta;q+1)

\displaystyle\simeq 0,

where
\tilde{\lambda}_{q}\equiv b_{0}\lambda_{q}/n_{0}

whose solutions are

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{\rm R}(\theta;q)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{\rm L}(\theta;q+1)

c_{1}
