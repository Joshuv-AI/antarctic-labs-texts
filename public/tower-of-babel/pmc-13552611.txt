Functional alginate-chitosan packaging nanofilms enhanced with calcium chloride and in situ synthesized ZnO@MgO nano-heterostructures
Authors: Samy Selim, Samiah Hamad Al-Mijalli, Amr H. Hashem, Mohammed Aladhadh, Emad M. Abdallah, Mohammed H. Alruhaili, Hattan S. Gattan, Alaa A. Kashmiry, Ashwag Jaman Alzahrani, Mohamed S. Hassanin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of biodegradable materials in food packaging has gained increasing attention as a sustainable alternative to conventional plastics. In this study, calcium chloride-loaded nanofilms were developed using sodium alginate (NaAlg) as the primary polymer matrix, with chitosan (Ch) added at varying concentrations, resulting in three formulations (NF1–NF3). The films were further reinforced with bimetallic ZnO@MgO nanoparticles to enhance functionality. Comprehensive characterization was conducted to evaluate structural, morphological, thermal, and mechanical properties. FTIR analysis confirmed strong interactions between NaAlg and Ch, indicating successful formation of a blended polymer network, while characteristic bands verified nanoparticle incorporation. SEM observations showed that increasing chitosan content resulted in denser, rougher film surfaces, suggesting enhanced intermolecular interactions. Thermal analysis demonstrated improved stability of the nanofilms compared to neat polymers, likely due to crosslinking effects and the presence of inorganic nanoparticles. Mechanical testing revealed enhanced tensile properties across all formulations compared with pure alginate films. Biocompatibility assessment using Vero and Wi-38 cell lines confirmed low cytotoxicity, with cell viability remaining above safe limits. Antimicrobial activity significantly increased with higher chitosan and nanoparticle content, with NF3 showing the strongest inhibition against the tested microorganisms. Additionally, antioxidant activity improved progressively, reaching 87.50% in NF3. Moreover, biodegradation of the formulated films in soil was rapid. Calcium and bimetallic ion migration test exhibited controlled release in the food simulation system. In conclusion, the developed alginate–chitosan nanofilms reinforced with ZnO@MgONPs exhibited enhanced physicochemical, mechanical, antimicrobial, and antioxidant properties, highlighting their strong potential as safe and effective biodegradable materials for active food packaging applications.

Introduction

Food packaging plays a crucial role in preserving food quality, extending shelf life, and reducing postharvest losses.1 Calcium chloride (CaCl2) is utilized to preserve the freshness of fresh-cut fruits and vegetables.2 In addition, CaCl2 is used to stabilize and fortify the membrane system against fungal infections.3 CaCl2 is a highly effective desiccant, absorbing moisture to protect goods from moisture-related damage.4 It sometimes absorbs several times its weight in water and is widely used in industrial, shipping, and food packaging to prevent corrosion, mold, and spoiling.5,6

Packaging materials based on nanofilms offer functionality and efficiency, enhancing strength, barrier properties, and antimicrobial activity.7 Packaging nanofilm-based, low-cost, and high-performing materials have long been used for this purpose; however, the search for sustainable alternatives has intensified due to their poor biodegradability and the growing environmental burden associated with plastic waste.

Biopolymers' renewability, biocompatibility, and ability to form films that enable biodegradation have resulted in them garnering growing interest as environmentally acceptable packaging materials. Because of their availability, non-toxicity, and utility in food packaging applications, sodium alginate and chitosan stand out among these materials. Because of its biocompatibility and gel-forming properties, sodium alginate (NaAlg), a naturally occurring anionic polysaccharide composed of α-l-guluronic acid and β-d-mannuronic acid residues linked by 1 → 4 glycosidic linkages, is widely used in culinary and medicinal applications.8 Despite its intrinsic biodegradability and film-forming properties, pure NaAlg films are limited in food packaging applications due to subpar barrier performance, insufficient mechanical strength, and limited antibacterial activity.9 To overcome these constraints, NaAlg has been combined with complementary substances, such as gelatin 10, collagen,11 cellulose,12 and chitosan,10 to create composite films with improved functional properties.

Chitosan (Ch), a cationic polysaccharide, is derived from chitin 13. It is the second-most prevalent polysaccharide in the natural world. The deacetylation of chitin from the cell walls of fungi and bacteria, as well as the exoskeletons of crustaceans and insects, produces this biodegradable, cationic hydrocolloid with antimicrobial activity and the ability to form films.14

Consequently, the opposing charges of NaAlg and Ch enable electrostatic interaction, forming polyelectrolyte complexes.15 These complexes can improve the strength, durability, and blocking capabilities of films. Adding inorganic nanoparticles to biopolymer matrices is another effective way to strengthen them and endow them with new functions, alongside polymer blending. Researchers have extensively studied metal oxide nanoparticles, such as ZnO and MgO, because they can improve the thermal, mechanical, and bacterial resistance of polymeric films.16 Bimetallic nanoparticles are particularly interesting because the two metal oxides may interact, making them more active and stable than systems with a single metal.17 When added to alginate/chitosan matrices, ZnO@MgO nanoparticles may help make the structure more compact, more resistant to heat, and better for active food packaging. Calcium chloride can also be a key crosslinking agent for alginate chains. This helps create a more stable three-dimensional network, thereby making the resulting nanofilms stick together more strongly. This study aims to produce calcium chloride-loaded NaAlg/Ch nanofilms containing bimetallic ZnO@MgONPs for potential use in food packaging. NaAlg was used as the main matrix polymer, and Ch was used at different concentrations to prepare the nanofilms. This was carried out to examine the role of the nanofilms composition in the formulation of the in situ bimetallic. The structure was examined via mechanical, physicochemical, and morphological properties to determine how the biopolymers, calcium chloride, and bimetallic nanoparticles interact. The main goal of this work is to help design biodegradable nanocomposite films with improved physical and chemical properties that serve as eco-friendly alternatives to traditional food packaging materials. This study aimed to develop biodegradable alginate–chitosan nanofilms reinforced with bimetallic ZnO@MgO nanoparticles. It further aimed to evaluate their physicochemical properties, biosafety, and functional performance, including antimicrobial and antioxidant activities.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sodium alginate, chitosan of medium molecular weight, glycerol, and Calcium chloride dihydrate were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Zinc acetate dihydrate and Magnesium acetate tetrahydrate were purchased from Loba-Chem, India.

Methods

Formulation of nanofilms

The nanofilms were formulated in a green method, and bimetallic nanoparticles (ZnO@MgONPs) were generated in situ. The polymer matrix was formulated using biopolymers, including NaAlg at 10% (w/v) in distilled water and Ch at 1, 3, and 5% (w/v) based on the dry weight of NaAlg; the nanofilms were designated NF1, NF2, and NF3, respectively. Chitosan was dissolved in 1% acetic acid solution. In detail, the nanofilms were formulated by adding the Ch to the NaAlg solution and stirring for 3 h at 70 °C. The metal salts were added at a fixed concentration of 1% in all nanofilm solutions, based on the sodium alginate dry weight, and stirred for overnight at 70 °C according to our previous work with minor modification.18,19 The nanofilms were ultrasonicated in an ultrasonic water bath after adding 1 mL of glycerol as a plasticizer. Nanofilms were washed in absolute ethanol and distilled water using a centrifuge. The nanofilms were cast and left to dry in open air. The nanofilms were soaked in a solution 1% w/v calcium chloride for 3 h. Nanofilms were washed with absolute ethanol three times and, for the last time, with distilled water and left to dry in the open air for further use.

Characterization of nanofilms

A Universal testing machine with a high-temperature chamber (3366, INSTRON) was used to test mechanical qualities at 25 °C, 50% humidity, 5.0 mm min−1, and a loading cell of 500N. Tensile strength (MPa) and elongation (mm) were among the mechanical characteristics. The data were recorded as mean ± SD after the samples were examined in triplicate. A BRUKER ALPHA Platinum apparatus (Germany) was used to perform Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) in the 4000–400 cm−1 range with 32 scans and a resolution of 2 cm−1. Thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a TGA-DTA Q600 SDT (TA Instruments, USA) in argon at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. A TESCAN MIRA field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) fitted with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (OXFORD Xplore) was used to get the surface morphology. Gold was used as a coating on the samples.

Cytotoxicity of the prepared nanofilms

The cytotoxicity of all films produced was assessed against the Wi38 normal cell line using a modified MTT assay.20 Sterile films of precise dimensions were excised and placed on the bottom surface of six-well tissue culture plates, which were seeded with 1 × 105 cells per mL and cultured at 37 °C for 24 hours. The growth medium was added; the cell monolayer was rinsed twice with wash media and incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours. The cells were subsequently examined for any physical indicators of toxicity, including partial or total loss of the monolayer, rounding, shrinkage, or cell granulation. 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg mL−1 in PBS) was applied to each well and agitated for 5 minutes at 150 rpm, followed by incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1–5 hours to facilitate MTT metabolism, after which the medium was removed, and the plate was dried. The formazan was re-suspended in 200 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and agitated at 150 rpm for 5 minutes, after which the optical density was measured at 560 nm.

Antimicrobial of the prepared nanofilms

The antimicrobial efficacy of all prepared films was assessed against Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853), Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 and Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6051), unicellular fungi (Candida albicans ATCC 90028), and filamentous fungi (Aspergillus brasiliensis ATCC 16404). All bacterial and fungal strains were cultivated on nutrient agar and malt extract and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h for bacteria and at 28 °C for 2–4 days for fungi. All prepared films were sectioned into specific discs (8 mm); these discs were positioned on the surface of nutrient agar for bacterial cultures and Malt extract agar for fungal cultures, which had been individually inoculated with all microbial strains, and subsequently incubated. Upon completion of the incubation periods, the inhibition zone surrounding each disc was assessed and quantified.

Antioxidant of the prepared nanofilms

The 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay was employed to evaluate and distinguish the free radical-scavenging ability of the prepared nanofilms, following the method of21 with modifications. DPPH radical solution (1 mM) was prepared in 95% ethanol. 800 µl of DPPH solution was mixed with 200 µl of the concentration, then vigorously shaken, and finally kept for 30 min at 25 °C in darkness. After this time, centrifugation was performed at 13 000 rpm for 5 min.

Biodegradation of the prepared nanofilms

Biodegradation was tested by decomposing samples in soil, following the investigations by ref. 22–24. The soil was removed from the upper stratum, and all non-reactive elements were discarded to obtain a uniform substance. Around 100 grams of soil was deposited into a plastic jar to a depth of roughly 3 cm. The specimens, each 1 × 1 cm, were measured and desiccated for 24 hours at 50 °C, then entombed in containers to a depth of 1 cm after weighing (g). Irrigation was conducted daily to sustain humidity levels. The specimens were weighed weekly for 4 weeks after being rinsed with distilled water and dried at 50 °C for 24 hours. Migration test

In accordance with the stipulations outlined in the prevailing regulation,25,26 10 × 5 cm samples of each film were cleaned, submerged, and maintained in 100 mL of a food packaging simulation system prepared as an acetic acid-free metal solution (4% v/v) at 60 °C for a duration of 30 minutes. Upon completion of this treatment, the solution was examined for heavy metal levels using a flame atomic absorption spectrometer (Agilent Technologies 200 Series AA; Santa Clara, CA, USA) for calcium, magnesium, and zinc.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were carried out using Minitab 17 software. Differences among the nanofilm formulations were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Symbols: a, b, c, d, e, etc., are referred to as the power of significance.

Results and discussion

The nanofilms were formulated via a green pathway, and the bimetallic nanofilms were synthesized in situ using the functional groups of NaAlg and Ch as stabilizing, capping, and reducing agents. In addition, the calcium chloride solution acted as a crosslinker for the formulated nanofilms, as well as a firming agent, preservative, and quality enhancer. Calcium chloride is essential to the packaging and postharvest handling of fresh food, especially fruits, vegetables, and fresh-cut produce. Fortifying cell walls and controlling moisture help preserve the structural integrity of produce, delay senescence (aging), and increase shelf life.

Characterization of the nanofilms

The mechanical properties of the nanofilms compared to the pure sodium alginate film (NF0) are shown in Fig. 1. NF0 had low tensile strength (8 ± 0.9 MPa) and elongation (59 ± 11%), indicating that the neat NaAlg film is neither very strong nor very flexible;.27,28 After adding Ch, both tensile strength and elongation improved, indicating that the polymer matrix was strengthened. The tensile strength increased from NF0 to NF1. The NF1 recording 12 ± 2.2 MPa and 124 ± 12% for low tensile strength and elongation, respectively, and NF2 having the highest mechanical value (tensile strength 17 ± 1.9 MPa and elongation 149 ± 14%). This suggests that this composition achieved the best balance of intermolecular interactions, likely due to effective electrostatic complexation between NaAlg and Ch, bimetallic nanoparticle reinforcement, and calcium-induced crosslinking. NF3 still had a higher tensile strength (14 ± 2.5 MPa) than NF0 and NF1, but it was lower than NF2. This suggests that excessive Ch may have reduced stress transfer efficiency, possibly because the matrix became more heterogeneous or partially aggregated. On the other hand, elongation increased steadily from NF0 to NF3 (159 ± 13%). This shows that adding more Ch made the nanofilm more flexible and bendable. The fact that NF3 had the highest elongation means that its network is more flexible, even though it was weaker than NF2. In general, the results show that NF2 exhibited the best mechanical resistance, while NF3 showed the greatest. This shows that the film's composition significantly affected the balance between strength and elasticity.

Mechanical properties of the formulated nanofilms compared with pure NaAlg film (NF0).

The FTIR spectra of the formulated nanofilms, compared with those of their neat materials, are shown in Fig. 2. The FTIR spectrum of NaAlg observed an OH stretching vibration band at 3271 cm−1, and an aliphatic C–H stretching vibration was assigned at 2943 cm−1.29 The strong band at 1633 cm−1 corresponded to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the carboxylate group.30 The symmetric stretching vibration of the carboxylate group was noted at about 1444 cm−1. The glucosidic linkage band was assigned at 1072 cm−1.31 Additionally, the spectrum of chitosan was presented as a pure chitosan-like spectrum with a broad band at 3318 cm−1, attributed to the overlap of the N–H and O–H stretching vibrations. In addition, the C–H group stretching vibration was assigned at 2867 cm−1.32 The bands at 1651, 1561, 1369, and 1018 cm−1 correspond to amide I, amide II, amide III, and glucosidic linkage stretching vibrations, respectively.33,34 The spectra of the developed nanofilms (NF1–NF3) showed enhanced intermolecular interactions due to structural modifications. The hydroxy group band was assigned at 3283, 3284, and 3294 cm−1 for NF1, NF2, and NF3, respectively, with NF3 nanofilm showing the highest frequency. The C–H group stretching vibration was presented in nanofilms as two small bands at about 2885 and 2945 cm−1. The asymmetric stretching vibration of the carboxylate group of NaAlg and the amide I band of Ch overlapping was assigned at around 1607 cm−1. The inorganic oxide bands of bimetallic nanoparticles were clearly assigned in the nanofilm NF3 at 738, 660, 529, and 477 cm−1. The formation of a nanofilm is supported by the overall spectral shifts observed across NF1–NF3, indicating improved intermolecular interactions and structural alterations with increasing chitosan concentration.

The FTIR spectra of the formulated nanofilms and their neat materials.

The thermal gravimetric analysis of the formulated nanofilms and their neat materials is presented in Fig. 3. Because of their composition, thermal degradation occurred in multiple phases. As the temperature drops below about 150 °C, all samples initially lose weight. This is due to the hydrophilic nature of the biopolymers and the nanofilms (NF1–NF3), which causes physically adsorbed moisture to escape.35 Afterward, the degradation stage occurs, during which the polymer backbones thermally decompose between 200 and 350 °C. This includes the depolymerization and disintegration of the polysaccharide rings of biopolymers. However, the Ch showed more rapid and pronounced weight loss, indicating that it is less thermally stable than NaAlg.36,37 Whereas the addition of the growing of the bimetallic in the nanofilms leads to decomposing more slowly and retaining more mass at high temperatures, the nanofilms (NF1–NF3) exhibit superior thermal stability. This is because ZnO@MgONPs strengthen the nanofilms, and NaAlg and Ch form a polyelectrolyte complex. Among all the nanofilms, NF3 had the highest char residue and heat stability. This is most likely due to its higher chitosan content and more robust intermolecular interactions. The presence of inorganic metal oxides is confirmed by the greater residue at temperatures above 600 °C. This demonstrates that ZnO@MgONPs were successfully added to the polymer matrix.38 The TGA results demonstrate that the nanocomposite films are more heat-resistant than the simple polymers.

Biosafety of the prepared films

The biosafety of the developed films (NF0–NF3) was evaluated using the normal cell lines Vero and Wi-38, to assess their suitability for safe applications. The results demonstrated that all nanofilms exhibited acceptable cytocompatibility, with cell viability remaining within safe limits (Fig. 6). NF0 (biopolymer matrix without nanoparticles) showed the highest cell viability in both Vero and Wi-38 cell lines, confirming the well-established biocompatibility of sodium alginate and chitosan. These natural polymers are widely recognized for their non-toxic, biodegradable, and biocompatible nature, making them suitable for biomedical and food-contact applications.39,40

Cytotoxicity of NF0–NF4 toward Vero and Wi-38 normal cell lines.

Upon incorporation of ZnO@MgO nanoparticles (NF1–NF3), a slight reduction in cell viability was observed in a concentration-dependent manner. However, cell viability values remained above the accepted cytotoxicity threshold (≥70%), indicating that the nanofilms are non-cytotoxic. The slight decrease in viability with increasing nanoparticle content can be attributed to the release of Zn2+ ions and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which may induce mild oxidative stress at higher concentrations.41 Nevertheless, the presence of chitosan plays a protective role by stabilizing nanoparticles, reducing their aggregation, and controlling ion release. This encapsulation effect limits direct nanoparticle–cell interactions, thereby reducing cytotoxicity.42–44 It was also noted that Wi-38 cells exhibited slightly higher sensitivity than Vero cells, which may be attributed to their human fibroblast origin and greater responsiveness to oxidative stress. Despite this, no significant cytotoxic effects were observed, further supporting the biosafety of the developed films. The cytotoxicity results strongly support the biosafety of the developed nanofilms for food packaging applications. Materials intended for food contact must demonstrate minimal toxicity to ensure consumer safety. The high cell viability observed in both Vero and Wi-38 cell lines confirms that these nanofilms meet essential safety requirements.

Thus, the cytotoxicity evaluation using Vero and Wi-38 normal cell lines confirmed that the developed ZnO@MgO-loaded alginate–chitosan nanofilms are biocompatible and non-cytotoxic under the tested conditions. Although a slight decrease in cell viability was observed as nanoparticle content increased, all films remained within the safe limit. These findings demonstrate that the nanofilms are suitable for food packaging applications, where both functionality and safety are essential.

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of the developed nanofilms (NF1–NF3) clearly demonstrated that incorporating ZnO@MgO bimetallic nanoparticles and increasing chitosan concentration significantly enhanced microbial inhibition (Table 1 & Fig. 7). The control film (NF0), composed of sodium alginate and chitosan without metal oxides, exhibited weak or negligible antimicrobial activity. This limited effect is consistent with previous findings that alginate lacks inherent antimicrobial properties. At the same time, chitosan alone shows moderate activity due to electrostatic interactions with negatively charged microbial cell membranes.45 In contrast, nanocomposite films (NF1–NF3) exhibited pronounced antimicrobial activity, following a clear concentration-dependent trend (NF3 > NF2 > NF1 > NF0). This enhancement is mainly attributed to the synergistic interaction between chitosan and ZnO@MgO nanoparticles. Chitosan acts as a polycationic biopolymer that disrupts microbial membranes, leading to increased permeability and leakage of intracellular components.46 Among all formulations, NF3 exhibited the highest antimicrobial efficacy, highlighting its potential as an effective active food packaging material for improving food safety and extending shelf life. Inhibition zone of NF3 film toward E. coli, P. aeruginosa,B. subtilis,S. aureus, and C. albicans was 13 ± 1.0, 10.7 ± 0.40, 25.3 ± 0.58, 20.6 ± 1.15, and 9.1 ± 0.17 mm, respectively. On the other hand, the NF3 film showed no activity against A. brasiliensis.

The incorporation of ZnO@MgONPs further amplified antimicrobial performance through multiple mechanisms. ZnO nanoparticles are known to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), release Zn2+ ions, and directly interact with microbial membranes, resulting in oxidative stress, protein denaturation, and DNA damage.47 The addition of MgO to the bimetallic system enhances these effects by increasing surface reactivity and promoting electron transfer, leading to stronger antimicrobial activity than in monometallic systems.48 Furthermore, the progressive increase in antimicrobial activity from NF1 to NF3 can be attributed to the higher chitosan content, which improves nanoparticle dispersion, reduces aggregation, and increases the availability of active antimicrobial sites. The preparation method (in situ synthesis, ultrasonication, and CaCl2 crosslinking) also contributes to uniform nanoparticle distribution and structural stability, ensuring consistent antimicrobial performance across the film matrix. Similar observations have been reported for chitosan/ZnO nanocomposite films, in which improved dispersion enhances antimicrobial efficiency.49

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of the prepared nanofilms (NF0–NF3) is shown in Fig. 8, with measured values of 21.83%, 64.23%, 78.66%, and 87.50% for NF0, NF1, NF2, and NF3, respectively. The results clearly demonstrate a substantial and progressive enhancement in antioxidant capacity with increasing chitosan content and the incorporation of ZnO@MgONPs. NF0, representing the control film composed of sodium alginate and chitosan without metal oxides, exhibited relatively low antioxidant activity (21.83%). This limited activity can be attributed to the modest radical scavenging ability of the polymeric matrix. Although chitosan contributes to antioxidant activity through hydrogen donation and metal ion chelation, its effect alone remains insufficient for strong antioxidative performance. A sharp increase in antioxidant activity was observed upon incorporation of ZnO@MgONPs in NF1 (64.23%), confirming the critical role of nanoparticles in enhancing free radical scavenging. This improvement can be explained by the ability of ZnO and MgO nanoparticles to act as electron donors or acceptors, facilitating neutralization of free radicals. In addition, nanoparticles provide a high surface area that promotes redox interactions and improves the overall antioxidant efficiency of the film matrix.50 Further increases were recorded in NF2 (78.66%) and NF3 (87.50%), indicating a concentration-dependent enhancement correlated with higher chitosan content. The improved performance at higher chitosan ratios can be attributed to better dispersion and stabilization of ZnO@MgONPs within the alginate matrix, thereby increasing the availability of active sites. Moreover, chitosan enhances intermolecular interactions, reduces nanoparticle aggregation, and contributes synergistically to radical scavenging through its amino and hydroxyl functional groups.51,52 The superior antioxidant activity of NF3 (87.50%) highlights the strong synergistic effect between chitosan and bimetallic nanoparticles. The presence of MgO alongside ZnO likely enhances electron transfer processes and increases surface reactivity, resulting in more efficient quenching of reactive species. Similar trends have been reported in recent studies, in which chitosan/ZnO nanocomposite films exhibited significantly improved antioxidant activity due to enhanced dispersion and interfacial interactions within the polymer matrix.53 Additionally, bimetallic oxide systems have been shown to outperform single-metal nanoparticles due to their improved catalytic and redox properties.54 Herein, these results support the potential application of NF3, in particular, as an effective active food packaging material with strong antioxidant properties.

Biodegradation

The soil burial experiment showed that all alginate–chitosan packaging films biodegraded slowly during the 4 weeks incubation period (Table 2). The percentage of weight loss increased with burial time for all formulations, confirming the susceptibility of the biopolymer matrix to microbial degradation in soil. During the first two weeks, the control film (NF0) showed significantly higher rates of biodegradation (21.31 and 31.37%, respectively) compared to the films with nanoparticles (NF1–NF3), indicating that the incorporation of inorganic nanoparticles improved the structural integrity of the polymer matrix initially, while limiting water penetration and microbial accessibility.55 This behavior can be attributed to the formation of stronger intermolecular interactions between the nanoparticles and the alginate-chitosan matrix, which limited the mobility of moisture and extracellular enzymes that promote polymer hydrolysis.56

Migration test

The migration analysis showed that the alginate–chitosan packaging nanofilms released small amounts of calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), and zinc (Zn) into the food simulant, indicating excellent structural integrity and low ionic leaching of the produced films as tabulated in Table 3. The control film (NF0) showed the highest calcium migration (5.09 ± 0.24 µg mL−1), which gradually decreased to 3.19 ± 0.25 µg mL−1 as the nanoparticle loading increased in NF3. This reduction is due to enhanced binding of Ca2+ ions to the alginate structure and improved polymer–nanoparticle interactions that prevent the release of Ca2+ from the crosslinked matrix. On the contrary, migration of Mg and Zn increased continuously from NF1 to NF3, indicating better incorporation of Mg- and Zn-bearing nanoparticles in the film matrix. However, the maximum Zn migration measured for NF3, 3.35 ± 0.21 µg mL−1, was significantly below the specific migration limit (SML) of 5 mg kg−1 set by current European legislation on food contact materials, demonstrating compliance with food safety requirements.25,59 Furthermore, calcium and magnesium are important dietary minerals with no specific migration limits under the same regulatory framework, provided that their migration does not adversely affect food quality or consumer safety. The low release of these mineral ions relative to the amount of initial incorporation indicates that the nanocomposite films retain a sufficient matrix integrity, allowing only limited ion diffusion and therefore confirming their suitability as active biodegradable food-packaging materials.60 Additionally, the controlled release of Zn and Mg can confer beneficial antimicrobial properties without exceeding safety standards, thereby prolonging food shelf life and protecting consumer health.61

Conclusion

The present work showed that NaAlg and Ch nanofilms doped with bimetallic ZnO@MgO nanoparticles and loaded with calcium chloride were successfully formulated as biodegradable materials for food packaging. The prepared films exhibited strong interaction between the polymer components and the inorganic phase, as evidenced by their morphological, thermal, and mechanical properties. Compared with the pure NaAlg film, the nanofilms were generally stronger and more stable, indicating the positive effect of Ch, calcium chloride, and bimetallic nanoparticles on nanofilm formation. Among the tested formulations, the higher Ch level gave a more compact film structure and better overall properties. The developed ZnO@MgO-loaded alginate–chitosan nanofilms demonstrated excellent biosafety, maintaining acceptable cytocompatibility in normal cell lines despite slight nanoparticle-related effects. Functional evaluation confirmed a clear enhancement in antimicrobial and antioxidant activities as nanoparticle and chitosan content increased, with NF3 showing superior performance. The observed improvements are driven by synergistic effects, improved nanoparticle dispersion, and enhanced surface reactivity. Importantly, all films satisfied safety requirements for food-contact materials. Herein, this is supported by the biodegradation behaviour of the formulated nanofilms, as well as by the control of metal migration in the metal-containing bioactive films. These findings highlight the potential of the prepared nanofilms as effective active packaging systems that can improve food safety, extend shelf life, and provide multifunctional protection against microbial contamination and oxidative degradation.
