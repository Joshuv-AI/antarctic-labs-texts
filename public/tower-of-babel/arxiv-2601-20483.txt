Nonequilibrium noise emerging from broken detailed balance in active gels Preprint: APS/123-QED
Ashot Matevosyan, Frank Jülicher, Ricard Alert

Abstract
In thermodynamic equilibrium, the fluctuation-dissipation theorem links thermal fluctuations and dissipation. Biological systems, however, are driven out of equilibrium by internal processes that produce additional, active fluctuations. Despite being relevant for biological functions such as intracellular transport, predicting the statistical properties of active fluctuations remains challenging. Here, we address this challenge in a minimal model of an active gel as a network of elastic elements connected by transient crosslinks. The crosslinkers’ binding and unbinding rates break detailed balance, which drives the system out of equilibrium. Through coarse-graining, we derive fluctuating hydrodynamic equations including an active noise term, which emerges explicitly from the breaking of detailed balance. Finally, we provide predictions for the stochastic motion of a tracer particle embedded in the active gel, which enables comparisons with microrheology experiments both in synthetic active gels and in cells. Overall, our work provides an explicit link between the statistical properties of active fluctuations and the molecular breaking of detailed balance. Thus, it paves the way toward complementing the fluctuation-dissipation theorem with a fluctuation-activity relation in active systems.

Introduction

Living matter is active:
It is internally driven by irreversible processes such as the action of molecular motors. Over the last two decades, the theory of active matter has been developed to capture this internal activity.
The existing hydrodynamic theories for active materials have been fruitfully applied to both synthetic and biological systems
ramaswamy2010mechanics
;
prost2015
;
marchetti2013
;
julicher2018
;
kruse2005generic
. However, these theories mostly focus on the
average

For example, the theory can predict how the collective action of molecular motors produces an average active stress and drive flows of the cell cortex
mayer2010
;
prost2015
.

Yet, beyond these average effects, active processes generate prominent fluctuations, which are fundamentally different from equilibrium thermal fluctuations.
Active fluctuations can be stronger than thermal fluctuations, and they can impact biological processes such as intracellular transport.
For example, active fluctuations can enhance the diffusivity of macromolecules, vesicles, and biomolecular condensates
brangwynne2009
;
parry2014
;
guo2014
;
fakhri2014
;
shu2024
and speed up motor-driven transport
ezber2020
;
wolgemuth2020
;
ariga2021
. At the cellular scale, active fluctuations may enable cellular sensitivity to weak magnetic fields
matevosyan2021nonequilibrium
, promote bleb nucleation
alert2016
;
turlier2016
;
turlier2019
, and help to position the cell nucleus
almonacid2015nucleus-positioning
;
rupprecht2018
.

Despite their relevance, active fluctuations have been largely overlooked in active matter theories so far. In part, this is due to the fact that, unlike in the case of thermal fluctuations, we lack a general theoretical framework to predict the statistical properties of active fluctuations. For example, there is no fluctuation-dissipation theorem to relate active fluctuations to dissipation
gnesotto2018
.
Given this limitation, previous works took a phenomenological approach and proposed active noise terms with spatiotemporal correlations consistent with experimental data for particular systems

lau2003
;
basu2008thermal
;
ben-isaac2011
;
fodor2015
;
fodor2016
;
ahmed2018
;
bernheim2018
;
abbasi2023non
.

Here, we seek to relate the active noise to the underlying molecular processes. To this end, we start with a minimal model of an active gel as a network of elastic elements bound by molecular crosslinkers. At the subcellular level, these elastic elements can be cytoskeletal filaments, such as actin in the cell cortex or microtubules in the mitotic spindle. At the tissue level, the elastic elements can be cells crosslinked through cell-cell junctions.
We introduce activity at the molecular level by breaking detailed balance in the binding kinetics of the crosslinkers.

Through explicit coarse-graining, we then derive the fluctuating hydrodynamic equations of the active gel. These equations include a nonequilibrium noise term consisting of active noise, which arises from the breaking of detailed balance, and passive noise, which encompasses both equilibrium thermal fluctuations and externally driven contributions such as an imposed shear. This latter contribution indicates that even externally driven passive systems can exhibit nonequilibrium noise within their hydrodynamic descriptions.

Overall, our results establish a fluctuation-activity relation that links the statistics of active noise at the mesoscopic scale to the breaking of detailed balance at the molecular scale. We also predict properties of the fluctuations of a tracer particle embedded in an active gel, which is often measured in microrheology experiments on cytoskeletal networks
mizuno2007
;
brangwynne2008
;
toyota2011
and cellular cytoplasm

Motivated by the cytoskeleton, the active gel is represented as a network of elastic elements, such as cytoskeletal filaments, connected by transient crosslinkers (
Fig.

1
a,b). Each crosslinker is modeled as an elastic macromolecule, which can stochastically bind and unbind from the network. To reach a certain binding site, a linker generally has to stretch and reorient. Thus, a linker can be in multiple bound states, characterized by two symmetric and traceless tensors: its mesoscopic strain
{\mathbf{u}}
and orientation
\mathbf{q}
(
Fig.

1
b).

To account for the dynamics of the linkers, we introduce
n({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}
as the fraction of bound linkers with strain
[{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{u}}\!+\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}]
and orientation
[\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\!+\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}]
at time
t
.
The evolution of this distribution is described by the following advection-reaction equation
oriola2017
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}+v_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial n}{\partial u_{\alpha\beta}}+\dot{Q}_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial n}{\partial q_{\alpha\beta}}=(1-\phi){k}_{\text{b}}-n\,{k}_{\text{u}}.

(1)

This equation expresses that
n({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q},t)
evolves due to two processes: The left-hand side accounts for the change in the strain and orientation of bound linkers under externally-imposed deformations, including a uniform shear rate
v_{\alpha\beta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\dot{u}_{\alpha\beta}}}\right\rangle
and change in global orientation
\dot{Q}_{\alpha\beta}
, where
Q_{\alpha\beta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle q_{\alpha\beta}}}\right\rangle
. For example, a crosslink initially bound at state
({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})
will deform to
({\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{v}}t,\mathbf{q}+\dot{{\mathbf{Q}}}t)
after time
t
, provided it remains bound and ignoring its diffusion.

The right-hand side of
Eq.

1
accounts for binding and unbinding events, which we assume to be independent Poisson processes.
Individual linkers stochastically bind and unbind with rates
{k}_{\text{b}}
and
{k}_{\text{u}}
respectively, which can depend on the strain
{\mathbf{u}}
and orientation
\mathbf{q}
of the linker in the bound state. The total binding rate is proportional to the fraction of unbound linkers,
1-\phi
, where

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\int n({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}

(2)

is the fraction of bound linkers. Respectively, the total unbinding rate is proportional to the fraction of linkers bound to a specific state (c.f. last term in
Eq.

1
).
The integrals
\int\dots\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}
are over all possible bound states; see
Appendix

A
for the definition and evaluation of these integrals.

At thermodynamic equilibrium, the binding and unbinding rates of individual linkers must fulfill detailed balance. In active systems, however, detailed balance is locally broken, which can be generically expressed as
oriola2017
;
chen2020
;
julicher1997

\displaystyle\frac{{k}_{\text{b}}}{{k}_{\text{u}}}=\mathrm{e}^{\beta\epsilon_{0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta F}+\Omega}}\right)\,.

(3)

Here,
\beta\equiv({\rm k}_{\rm B}T)^{-1}
is the inverse temperature,
\epsilon_{0}
is the chemical part of the binding energy, while
F({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})
represents the mechanical free energy difference per linker between the bound state
({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})
and the unbound state due to stretching and reorientation of the linker (
Fig.

1
c). The term
\Omega
quantifies the departure from detailed balance, which we refer to as molecular activity. It is an a priori unknown function of the system parameters, the coordinates
{\mathbf{u}}
and
\mathbf{q}
of the bound state, and the chemical potential difference
\Delta\mu
of ATP hydrolysis, with
\Omega\propto\Delta\mu
near equilibrium
julicher1997
.

At this stage, for each specific system, one should introduce the appropriate dependence on the unbinding rate
{k}_{\text{u}}
on the crosslinker’s bound state
{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q}
. For simplicity, here we assume a state-independent unbinding rate. This assumption implies that the activation energy of the unbinding process is the same for all bound states (see
Fig.

1
c)
walcott2010
, which is a good approximation if the binding energy is dominated by the chemical contribution
\epsilon_{0}
rather than the mechanical contribution
F
. Additionally, for convenience, we incorporate the factor with the chemical energy
\epsilon_{0}
in
Eq.

3
into
{k}_{\text{u}}
. Thus, we have:

{k}_{\text{b}}({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})={k}_{\text{u}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta F({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})}+\Omega({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})}}\right).

(4)

Furthermore, we consider that the system has an isotropic linear elastic response, both to strain and to nematic ordering
lubensky2002
.
We now switch to a continuum description for a system with a density
\rho
of linkers with free energy density
f({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})=\rho F({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})
given by

f({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})=\frac{\mu}{2}u_{\alpha\beta}u_{\alpha\beta}+Du_{\alpha\beta}q_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{\chi}{2}q_{\alpha\beta}q_{\alpha\beta},

(5)

where Greek indicates denote spatial coordinates,
\mu
is the shear elastic modulus,
D
is the elastonematic coefficient, and
\chi
is the inverse nematic susceptibility
lubensky2002
.
With these choices, the model is fully specified.

Hereafter, we work with dimensionless quantities by rescaling energy by
\beta^{-1}
, time by
{k}_{\text{u}}^{-1}
, length by
(\beta\mu)^{-1/3}
. Therefore, pressure, stress and energy density are all in units of
\mu
.

3
Constitutive relation for the average stress

In continuum descriptions, a constitutive relation specifies how thermodynamic forces – here the stress tensor – depend on the relevant thermodynamic variables and their gradients
mazur
;
landau-stat
. It closes the balance equations by encoding the material-specific response. In passive viscoelastic media this yields, for example, a viscous stress proportional to the strain-rate and an elastic stress derived from a free energy. In our case, detailed balance is broken and nematic order provides additional symmetry-allowed couplings, so the stress contains both dissipative terms and active, non-potential contributions such as a term proportional to the nematic order parameter
Q_{{\alpha\beta}}

ramaswamy2010mechanics
;
prost2015
;
marchetti2013
;
julicher2018
;
kruse2005generic
. While such constitutive laws are typically derived phenomenologically, here we derive them from an explicit microscopic model of stochastic binding-unbinding linkers, thereby expressing the effective transport coefficients in terms of microscopic rates and elastic couplings
oriola2017
.

Our goal is to obtain an equation for the stress tensor of the active gel, which fluctuates due to the stochastic linker dynamics.
The stress exerted by a bound linker in a coarse-graining region of volume
V
is given by

\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{\text{linker}}({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{V}\frac{\partial F({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})}{\partial u_{\alpha\beta}}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\frac{\partial f({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})}{\partial u_{\alpha\beta}},

(6)

where
N=\rho\,V
is the number of linkers in volume
V
.
Summing over linkers, the total stress in this volume is:

\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{\text{linker}}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i})\;\mathds{1}_{i}(t),

(7)

where the hat indicates fluctuating quantities, and
\mathds{1}_{i}(t)
is the indicator function

\mathds{1}_{i}(t)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if linker $i$ is bound at time $t$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(8)

that counts only the bound linkers in
Eq.

7
. When
\mathds{1}_{i}(t)=1
,
(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{i})
specifies the bound state of linker
i
.

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(t)=

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\,\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(t)\,}}\right\rangle_{\sim n}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\int n({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q},t)\sigma^{\text{linker}}_{\alpha\beta}({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int n({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q},t)\frac{\partial f({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})}{\partial u_{\alpha\beta}}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q},

where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle~}}\right\rangle_{\sim n}
denotes the average over the distribution
n({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q},t)
. We consider identical linkers; therefore, the bound state of each linker follows the same distribution, with

To derive a hydrodynamic equation for the average stress, we multiply

1
by
\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{\alpha\beta}}
and integrate over all bound states. Using that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{{k}_{\text{u}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}}}\right)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}=2\eta\,v_{\alpha\beta}-\nu\,\dot{Q}_{\alpha\beta}+\zeta Q_{\alpha\beta}.

(10)

This result shows that the system behaves like an active nematic viscoelastic fluid with relaxation time
{k}_{\text{u}}^{-1}
. The shear viscosity

The scalars

\displaystyle\textstyle\int u_{\alpha\beta}\,\Omega({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}=\Omega_{u}Q_{\alpha\beta},

(12)

These quantities arise from the breaking of detailed balance and give rise to the active stress term

As the integrands are tensors, the integral is proportional to

The coupling coefficients (

Here, we decomposed the duty ratio into the thermal and active contributions (c.f.

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\text{th}}=\int\mathrm{e}^{-\beta f/\rho}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q},\qquad\Omega_{0}=\int\Omega\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}.

(15)

The first integral is evaluated in

6.1
Thermal, driven, and active contributions

In thermodynamic equilibrium, where both the activity and the external drivings vanish (
\Omega=0
,
{\mathbf{v}}=\dot{\mathbf{Q}}=0

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\text{thermal}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}=4\eta{\rm k}_{\rm B}T~\mathsf{T}^{\text{(iso)}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}},

(30)

where
\mathsf{T}^{\text{(iso)}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\beta\mu}+\delta_{\alpha\mu}\delta_{\beta\nu})-\frac{1}{d}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\mu\nu}
is the 4
th
-rank isotropic tensor,
{\rm k}_{\rm B}T
is the thermal energy, and
\eta
is the equilibrium viscosity given by
Eq.

11

\phi=\phi_{\text{eq}}=\phi_{\rm ss}(\Omega=0)
. In equilibrium, the noise and dissipative coefficient
\eta
must satisfy the Fluctuation-Dissipation theorem (FDT)
landau-stat
;
mazur

30

\Omega=0

The external driving puts the system out of equilibrium while preserving detailed balance at the mesoscopic scale, so that the system remains passive, but driven.
In our model, this corresponds to
\Omega=0
and
{\mathbf{v}},\,\dot{\mathbf{Q}}\neq 0

Interestingly, this external driving leads to additional noise with amplitude

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\text{driven}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}=

\displaystyle\frac{2\phi_{\rm ss}}{\rho{k}_{\text{u}}^{3}}(\mu v_{\alpha\beta}+D\dot{Q}_{\alpha\beta})(\mu v_{\mu\nu}+D\dot{Q}_{\mu\nu})

(31)

Thus, our model predicts a nonequilibrium contribution to the noise in passive driven systems. While the external drivings are held constant in our model,

When activity is introduced (

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\text{active}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}=

\displaystyle\,-4\eta{\rm k}_{\rm B}T\;\mathsf{T}^{\text{(iso)}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}\;\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\mathcal{Z}}

\displaystyle\hskip-25.83325pt+\frac{2}{\rho{k}_{\text{u}}(1+\mathcal{Z})}\!\int\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu u_{\alpha\beta}\!+\!Dq_{\alpha\beta}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu u_{\mu\nu}\!+\!Dq_{\mu\nu}}}\right)\Omega\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q},

6.2
Symmetries of the noise amplitude tensor

The noise amplitude
\Lambda^{\sigma}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}
in
Eq.

28
is a fourth-rank tensor with 81 entries. However, it has symmetries that strongly reduce its number of degrees of freedom. These symmetries arise from two facts: (i) that
\Lambda^{\sigma}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}
is defined in
Eq.

28
as the product of the noise tensor
\hat{\xi}_{\alpha\beta}
, which is itself a second-rank traceless and symmetric tensor;
and (ii) that we assume a uniaxial nematic, such that the system is invariant under rotations around the axis of nematic order.

The rank-4 tensors
\mathsf{T}
that satisfy above symmetries have the following properties:

•

Traceless with respect to the first and last pairs of indices:
\mathsf{T}_{{\xi\xi}\mu\nu}=0,~\mathsf{T}_{\alpha\beta{\xi\xi}}=0
,

•

Symmetric with respect to the first and last pairs of indices:
\mathsf{T}_{{\alpha\beta}\mu\nu}=\mathsf{T}_{{\beta\alpha}\mu\nu},~\mathsf{T}_{\alpha\beta{\mu\nu}}=\mathsf{T}_{\alpha\beta{\nu\mu}}
,

•

Symmetric under the exchange of the first and last pairs of indices:
\mathsf{T}_{{\alpha\beta}\mu\nu}=\mathsf{T}_{\mu\nu{\alpha\beta}}
,

•

Rotationally invariant around the nematic director
{\bm{n}}
:
R_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}R_{\beta\beta^{\prime}}R_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}R_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}\mathsf{T}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}=\mathsf{T}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}
,
where
R
is an arbitrary rotation matrix
around the nematic director axis
{\bm{n}}
,

where the first three properties follow from (i) and the last property follows from (ii).

The linearly independent tensors that satisfy these requirements are (see
for derivation)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}^{\text{(0)}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\beta\mu}\!+\!\delta_{\alpha\mu}\delta_{\beta\nu})\!-\!\frac{1}{3}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\mu\nu}\equiv\mathsf{T}^{\text{(iso)}}_{{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}},

(34a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}^{\text{(1)}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta_{\alpha\mu}N_{\beta\nu}+\delta_{\beta\nu}N_{\alpha\mu}}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta_{\alpha\nu}N_{\beta\mu}+\delta_{\beta\mu}N_{\alpha\nu}}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta_{\alpha\beta}N_{\mu\nu}+\delta_{\mu\nu}N_{\alpha\beta}}}\right),

(34b)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}^{\text{(2)}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}=

\displaystyle N_{\alpha\beta}N_{\mu\nu}.

(34c)

where

is the orientation tensor, with

The superscripts denote the order in

(35a)

6.3
Orientation-selective activity: a minimal illustrative case

Here, we illustrate the properties of the active noise by taking a simple specific form of the molecular activity:

\Omega({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})=\Omega({\mathbf{u}})\;\delta(\mathbf{q}-\bm{Q}).

(39)

This choice corresponds to activity that selectively promotes binding to a prescribed alignment
{\mathbf{Q}}
, enforced here through a Dirac delta function.
In practice, however, the delta function should be understood as an idealization. Real systems exhibit a finite width in alignment selectivity. This is particularly important when the activity is negative (
\Omega({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})<0
), corresponding to suppressed binding: in that case, the binding rate
{k}_{\text{b}}
in
Eq.

3
must remain positive, which can only be satisfied by a sharply peaked but finite-width function instead of
\delta(\mathbf{q}-\bm{Q})
. For clarity and analytical simplicity, we will continue to use the delta function representation in what follows.

Additionally, we assume that the strain part
\Omega({\mathbf{u}})
in
Eq.

39
is invariant under any rotation.
With this symmetry, the first moments of the activity defined in
Eq.

12
evaluate to

\displaystyle\Omega_{u}=0,\qquad\Omega_{q}=\Omega_{0}.

(40)

These quantities measure the departure from detailed balance, and they appear as factors in the active stress coefficient
\zeta
in
Eq.

11
.

Respectively, here we found that the second moments of the molecular activity determine the active noise strength. Using their definition in
Eq.

35
, we obtain

\Omega^{(0)}_{uu}=A\Omega_{0},\quad\Omega^{(2)}_{qq}=\Omega_{0}|{\mathbf{Q}}|^{2}.

(41)

with all other coefficients being zero (note that
Q_{\alpha\beta}Q_{\mu\nu}=|{\mathbf{Q}}|^{2}\mathsf{T}^{(2)}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}
). Here we introduced a standard-deviation-like parameter
A>0
, which characterizes the width of the function
\Omega({\mathbf{u}})
.

Ignoring contributions from the external driving in
Eq.

29
, the total noise magnitude for this simple choice of activity is given by

\Lambda^{\sigma}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}=4\eta{\rm k}_{\rm B}T\frac{1}{1+\Omega_{0}/\mathcal{Z}_{\text{th}}}~\mathsf{T}^{\text{(iso)}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}\\
\frac{2\Omega_{0}}{\rho{k}_{\text{u}}(1\!+\!\mathcal{Z}_{\text{th}}+\Omega_{0})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A\mu^{2}\mathsf{T}^{\text{(iso)}}_{{\alpha\beta}{\mu\nu}}+D^{2}Q_{\alpha\beta}Q_{\mu\nu}}}\right)

(44)

In the activity-dominated regime, where
\Omega_{0}\gg\mathcal{Z}_{\text{th}}
, the thermal contribution becomes negligible. Beyond this limit, two distinct active contributions remain. The first one is a direct analogue of the thermal term. In the activity-dominated regime, the viscosity takes the form

\eta=\frac{\mu\phi}{2{k}_{\text{u}}}=\frac{\mu\Omega_{0}}{2{k}_{\text{u}}(1+\Omega_{0})}
,
and the parameter
A
, defined in
Eq.

41

\frac{\rho{\rm k}_{\rm B}T}{\mu}
in the Boltzmann factor of
Eq.

4
. Thus, with effective temperature
T_{\text{eff}}=\frac{A\mu}{\rho k_{\rm B}}
, the first term in the second line of
Eq.

44

.

The second term, proportional to
Q_{\alpha\beta}Q_{\mu\nu}
, represents a genuinely nonequilibrium active contribution that has no analogue within the equilibrium thermodynamic framework. This term arises because molecular activity
\Omega
biases crosslink binding toward specific orientations characterized by
{\mathbf{Q}}
. Owing to the elastonematic coupling coefficient
D
, crosslinks preferentially bind with local strain fields
{\mathbf{u}}\sim D{\mathbf{Q}}
. Consequently, this mechanism introduces anisotropy in both the stress fluctuations and the corresponding noise amplitude.

Overall, the specific choice of molecular activity in
Eq.

39
significantly simplifies the results, reducing the number of independent activity parameters from twelve to only two. In the following section, we investigate the dynamics of a tracer particle embedded in an active gel described by this choise of molecular activity.

7
Fluctuations of a tracer particle

In the previous sections, we derived the noise term in the constitutive relation for the stress tensor of an active gel. Now, we discuss how this noise affects a tracer particle.
Specifically, we calculate the fluctuation spectrum and the response function of a tracer particle immersed in the active gel. In experiments, these quantities are measured by passive and active microrheology, respectively, and comparing them can reveal departures from the FDT that indicate the presence of active fluctuations.

If the tracer particle experiences a displacement
\mathbf{R}(t)
when subject to an external force
{\mathbf{F}}^{\text{ext}}(t)
, the response function
\chi^{R}
is defined, in Fourier space, by

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{\alpha}(\omega)=\chi^{R}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\tilde{F}^{\text{ext}}_{\beta}(\omega).

(45)

Respectively, the fluctuation spectrum is defined as the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function of the particle’s position:

\displaystyle{\rm S}^{R}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\hat{R}_{\alpha}(t)\hat{R}_{\beta}(0)}}\right\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\mathrm{d}t.

(46)

In thermodynamic equilibrium, these two quantities are related by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT):

\displaystyle{\rm S}^{R,\text{eq}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=-\frac{2{\rm k}_{\rm B}T}{\omega}\chi^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega),

(47)

where
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\beta}
is the imaginary part of the response function.
Below, we calculate
\chi^{R}
and
{\rm S}^{R}
for a tracer particle in an active gel, and demonstrate the FDT violation due to broken detailed balance in the active gel.

To this end, we first write the fluctuating hydrodynamic equations for an incompressible active viscoelastic fluid that follows the constitutive relation
Eq.

17
. Here, we focus on a situation with a constant and uniform nematic orientation
{\mathbf{Q}}
, and in the low-Reynolds limit characteristic of intracellular flows. Thus, the fluctuating hydrodynamic equations are

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}\hat{\sigma}^{\text{tot}}_{\alpha\beta}=0\,,\quad\hat{\sigma}^{\text{tot}}_{\alpha\beta}=\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}\!-\!\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\hat{p}\,,\quad\partial_{\alpha}\hat{v}_{\alpha}\!=\!0\,,

(48a)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{{k}_{\text{u}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}}\right)\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}=2\hat{\eta}\,\hat{v}_{\alpha\beta}-\hat{\zeta}\,Q_{\alpha\beta}+\hat{\xi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}.

(48b)

The total stress tensor
\hat{\sigma}^{\text{tot}}

\hat{\sigma}

\hat{p}

The strain rate tensor is defined as

As before, the symbol ‘

(i)

(ii)

Rather than taking an imposed shear

(iii)

In contrast,

Discussion and conclusion

In summary, we derived the properties of nonequilibrium fluctuations of an active gel, which we modeled as a network of elastic elements connected by transient crosslinks. Our results show explicitly how active noise emerges from nonequilibrium, irreversible processes at the molecular scale. In particular, we obtained the fluctuation spectra of both the stress tensor of the gel and the position of a tracer particle. The results show how the breaking of detailed balance for the crosslinker binding kinetics leads to departures from the fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT). Thus, our work extends the FDT to active systems by providing a fluctuation-dissipation-activity relation, where the activity is given in terms of the function
\Omega
that characterizes the breaking of detailed balance.

Appendix A
Definition and evaluation of integrals involving tensors

In the main text, we encounter integrals over all possible binding states, characterized by traceless and symmetric tensors
{\mathbf{u}}
and
\mathbf{q}
. In
d
dimensions, each tensor has
d^{2}
entries, but the constraints of tracelessness and symmetry reduce the number of independent entries to
d(d+1)/2-1
. Therefore, the space of all possible binding states has a dimension of
d(d+1)-2
.

Working in Cartesian space simplifies the interpretation of the integral over all
2d^{2}
entries of the tensors
{\mathbf{u}}
and
\mathbf{q}
. To this end, we define a density of states
g({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})
, which is non-zero only when
{\mathbf{u}}
and
\mathbf{q}
are symmetric and traceless. Specifically, we choose:

\textstyle g({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})=\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{ii}}}\right)\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{ii}}}\right)\\
\textstyle\times\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}\delta(u_{ij}-u_{ji})\delta(q_{ij}-q_{ji}).

(64)

The first two delta functions ensure that
{\mathbf{u}}
and
\mathbf{q}
are traceless, while the remaining delta functions enforce their symmetry.

To ensure that binding events occur only in valid states, we modify the binding rate in
Eq.

4
as follows:

\displaystyle{k}_{\text{b}}({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})={k}_{\text{u}}\;g({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\beta}{\rho}f({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})}+\Omega({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})}}\right].

(65)

This modification guarantees that linkers are bound exclusively to valid states at any given time.

A.1
Evaluation of the thermal component of the duty ratio

In this section, we evaluate the thermal component of the duty ratio, denoted by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\text{th}}=\int g({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta}{\rho}f({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q},

(66)

where the free energy density
f({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})
is defined in
Eq.

5
.

This integral represents the ratio of the binding rate to any bound state,
\int{k}_{\text{b}}({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})g({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}
, to the unbinding rate
{k}_{\text{u}}
in a passive system, where
\Omega\equiv 0
.

Using the rotational symmetry of the free energy, the integral
Eq.

66
can be factorized as follows:

\mathcal{Z}_{\text{th}}=\;\mathcal{I}_{1}^{\;d(d-1)/2}\;\mathcal{I}_{2},

(67)

where

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\int\mathrm{d}u_{12}\mathrm{d}u_{21}\mathrm{d}q_{12}\mathrm{d}q_{21}\;\delta(u_{12}\!-\!u_{21})\delta(q_{12}\!-\!q_{21})\\

Appendix B
Derivation of the constitutive relations

In this appendix, we derive the constitutive relation

10
as presented in Ref.
oriola2017
. To obtain an equation for the average stress, we multiply

1
by
\frac{\partial f({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})}{\partial u_{\mu\nu}}
and integrate over all bound states:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{\mu\nu}}n\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}+v_{\alpha\beta}\int\frac{\partial n}{\partial u_{\alpha\beta}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{\mu\nu}}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\\
+\dot{Q}_{\alpha\beta}\int\frac{\partial n}{\partial q_{\alpha\beta}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{\mu\nu}}\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}=\\

Note that

D.1
Derivation: method of integrating factors

In this appendix, we solve the equation derived from
Eq.

1
by setting
\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}=0
and
\phi=\frac{\mathcal{Z}}{1+\mathcal{Z}}
:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({k}_{\text{u}}+v_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{\alpha\beta}}+\dot{Q}_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{\alpha\beta}}}}\right)n_{\rm ss}({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})=\frac{{k}_{\text{b}}({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})}{1+\mathcal{Z}}.

To simplify the notation, we introduce generalized variables
\mathbf{x}=({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})
and
{\mathbf{J}}=({\mathbf{v}},\dot{{\mathbf{Q}}})
(1d vectors containing the components of the tensors), transforming the equation into:

\displaystyle({k}_{\text{u}}+{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\nabla)n_{\rm ss}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{{k}_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{x})}{1+\mathcal{Z}}.

(111)

Multiplying both sides by the integrating factor

, we obtain:

\displaystyle{k}_{\text{u}}\mu(\mathbf{x})n(\mathbf{x})+(\mu(\mathbf{x}){\mathbf{J}})\cdot\nabla n(\mathbf{x})=\frac{{k}_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{x})}{1+\mathcal{Z}}\mu(\mathbf{x}).

(112)

If
\mu(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\nabla\mu(\mathbf{x})={k}_{\text{u}}\mu(\mathbf{x}),

(113)

Eq.

112

\displaystyle{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu(\mathbf{x})n(\mathbf{x})}}\right)=\frac{{k}_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{x})\mu(\mathbf{x})}{1+\mathcal{Z}}.

(114)

Defining

and
g(\mathbf{x})=\frac{{k}_{\text{b}}(\mathbf{x})\mu(\mathbf{x})}{1+\mathcal{Z}}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x}).

(115)

This is a directional derivative. Writing
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{0}+{\mathbf{J}}\tau

\mathbf{x}_{0}
and

\displaystyle{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}f(\mathbf{x}_{0}+{\mathbf{J}}\tau)=g(\mathbf{x}_{0}+{\mathbf{J}}\tau).

D.2
Probabilistic interpretation of steady-State distribution

In
Section

5
, we argued that the steady-state distribution can be expressed as:

\displaystyle n_{\rm ss}({\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{q})=\phi_{\rm ss}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\frac{{k}_{\text{b}}({\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}\tau,\;\mathbf{q}-\dot{\mathbf{Q}}\tau)}{{k}_{\text{u}}\mathcal{Z}}}}\right\rangle_{\tau},

(121)

where the average is taken over the observed lifetime
\tau
of the linker. The observed lifetime
\tau
is the duration for which the linker has been bound, if one just observed that it is currently bound.

We now argue that the observed lifetime
\tau
follows an exponential distribution with rate
{k}_{\text{u}}
. When observing a linker, there is a bias towards those that remain bound for longer periods, while linkers that detach quickly are less likely to be observed in the bound state. To derive the distribution of the total lifetime
s
of an observed linker (
\tau
plus the time time that the linker remains bound after the observation), denoted as
p(s\,|\,\text{bound})
, consider that if a linker has been bound multiple times with lifetimes
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},\dots
, the probability that a randomly chosen bound state had lifetime
s_{i}
is
({s_{i}p(s_{i})})/({\sum_{k}s_{k}p(s_{k})})
.
For the continuous case with an exponential lifetime distribution
p(s)={k}_{\text{u}}\mathrm{e}^{-{k}_{\text{u}}s}
, this becomes:

\displaystyle p(s\,|\,\text{bound})=\frac{s\mathrm{e}^{-{k}_{\text{u}}s}}{\int s^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{-{k}_{\text{u}}s^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}}={k}_{\text{u}}^{2}\,s\,\mathrm{e}^{-{k}_{\text{u}}s}.

(122)

The distribution of the observed lifetime
\tau
is then:

\displaystyle p(\tau\,|\,\text{bound})=\int_{0}^{\infty}p(\tau\,|\,s,\text{bound})\,p(s\,|\,\text{bound})\mathrm{d}s,

(123)

where
p(\tau\,|\,s,\;\text{bound})
is the conditional distribution of the observed lifetime
\tau
given that the linker has a total lifetime
s
. This conditional distribution is:

\displaystyle p(\tau\,|\,s,\;\text{bound})=\frac{1}{s}\quad\text{if}\quad\tau<s,\quad\text{otherwise}\quad 0.

(124)

Thus, the distribution of the observed lifetime
\tau
is:

\displaystyle p(\tau\,|\,\;\text{bound})=\int_{\tau}^{\infty}{k}_{\text{u}}^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-{k}_{\text{u}}s}\,\mathrm{d}s={k}_{\text{u}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-{k}_{\text{u}}\tau}.

(125)

Performing the average in
Eq.

121
with respect to this distribution yields the steady-state distribution (
26
).
