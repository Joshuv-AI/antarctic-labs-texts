Enantio-selective vibronic coupling revealed via dissymmetry of upconverted emission in double-resonance excitation of chiral polymer
Authors: Guang S He, Cecilia LAV Campos, Jojo P Joseph, Alexander S Baev, Paras N Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDouble-resonance dual-beam spectroscopy, involving vibrational and electronic transitions, is a powerful tool allowing to resolve vibronic coupling in complex molecular systems. Here, introducing resonant three-photon excitation with mid-infrared beam in vibrational resonance, near-infrared beam to connect to a two-photon resonant electronic state, and endowing both beams with circular polarization, we demonstrate enantio-selective vibronic coupling resulting in deep amplitude modulation of coherent upconverted emission. Quantum chemical calculations confirm that helical supramolecular organization of the polymer leads to strong coupling of the local nuclear motion at the chiral sidechains with the delocalized electronic motion of molecular π-system upon its resonant excitation. Time-resolved measurements reveal that local vibrational relaxation is not sensitive to the beam polarization state, which leads to lower dissymmetry of incoherent upconverted fluorescence. Our findings highlight the importance of chiral control of vibronic coupling for achieving deep enantio-selective modulation of coherent upconverted emission for various applications in chiral photonics.Subject terms: Nonlinear optics, Polymers

Introduction

Two-dimensional double-resonance spectroscopy is an advanced (nonlinear) spectroscopic technique in which two resonances are coupled to reach an excited state of an electronic system or to connect to a virtual state by a multiphoton process. In infrared-visible double-resonance studies, one of the vibrational levels of the electronic ground state is usually chosen as the first excited state with the excitation wavelength in the mid-infrared (MIR) spectral range, whereas an excited electronic state is the final state that can be reached by an excitation wavelength either in the visible, for one-photon absorption, or near-infrared (NIR) range, for two-photon absorption.

The resonant excitation of the targeted vibrational level allows for a highly selective means to study vibronic coupling, giving valuable information about the nuclear dynamics of the system under investigation. Most of the previous reports have been concerned with a two-step, two-photon excitation approach1–19, wherein the vibrational excitation is usually applied via a continuous-wave (cw) MIR beam. The detected signals comprise either two-photon absorption (2PA) induced attenuation of the second (probe) beam4,5,8–10,17,18, or the 2PA-induced upconverted emission1,2,7,14,16. In some studies, the two-step/two-photon excitation-induced enhancement or depletion of coherent Raman emission was detected6,11,15,19. In other works, three-photon absorption (3PA) induced upconversion emission20–23 was reported, with a special focus on the vibrational resonance-enhanced three-photon excitation24–28. The resonantly-enhanced three-photon sum-frequency generation has also been employed for investigating the nuclear dynamics of surfaces and adsorbates, as well as for molecular sensing29–32.

In the above-referenced reports, linearly polarized light beams were employed. Imposing circular polarization (spin angular momentum—SAM) to one or all beams represents an important advancement of the double-resonance technique, allowing for probing of enantio-selectivity in vibronic coupling in chiral systems. Probing the chiral components of the nonlinear susceptibility tensors is also possible by using different combinations of linear polarization (s- or p-polarized waves) in the double-resonance sum-frequency generation (SFG) technique33. Nonlinear chiral materials and nanostructures have recently gained increased attention because of their great potential for many practical applications, such as fast enantio-selective optical switching, optical data processing, chiral imaging, and bio-sensing34–36. Various nonlinear spectroscopic techniques have been employed to characterize these materials, including open aperture Z-scan37,38, second-harmonic generation (SHG)39–43, third-harmonic generation (THG)44–46, four-wave frequency mixing (FWFM)47, and the THz beam-induced optical activity48.

In our work, we probe enantio-selectivity in vibronically-coupled nonlinear optical processes, which are schematically shown in Fig. 1a–d. These are based on double resonance-enhanced three-photon excitation of the sample material by light with SAM (circular polarization), and the detected signal is therefore frequency-upconverted emission. Shown in Fig. 1a is a direct three-photon absorption (3PA) process that involves simultaneous absorption of two photons of one handedness from a pulsed laser beam of NIR wavelength and of one photon with the same or opposite handedness from a pulsed MIR laser beam, which is tuned to the resonance with a specific vibrational level of the ground electronic state. As a result, part of the molecular ensemble in the ground state will be excited to a higher electronic-vibrational state, followed by non-radiative relaxation to the lowest vibrational level of the excited electronic state, finally emitting frequency-upconverted light. In contrast, Fig. 1b shows a two-step (3PA) process in which the resonant excitation by a circularly polarized MIR beam leads to enantio-selective buildup of the vibrational level population. A molecule in this state can further absorb two photons with SAM from the NIR beam and reach the final electronic state. Although the spectral appearance of the upconversion emission for the above-mentioned two processes can be the same, the direct 3PA requires the pulses from the two incident beams to impinge on the sample at the same time, whereas the two-step 3PA can still take place even when the NIR pulse arrives at the sample later than the MIR pulse, provided the time delay between the two incident pulses is shorter than the lifetime of the vibrational energy level. This allows for probing of vibrational relaxation. Finally, Fig. 1c, d shows a coherent three-photon excitation process - the three-photon-induced sum-frequency generation. The common feature of all presented nonlinear processes is double-resonance enhancement, which boosts the light-matter interaction and allows for deep enantio-selective modulation of the observed upconverted emission.

It must be noted that apart from using resonance-enhanced light-matter interaction, allowing for stronger coupling and, therefore, clearly resolved signals, the nonlinear chiro-optical response of molecular, and especially polymer-based materials, can be significantly amplified by employing molecular design approaches, such as increasing the conjugation length or introducing push-pull-type building blocks. These approaches are generally known to work well for amplifying the optical nonlinearities of (achiral) organics49–56. When applied to organic chiral systems, these design principles are expected to provide amplification of the targeted nonlinear chiro-optical properties as well. For example, increasing the conjugation length along the helical backbone boosts magnetic coupling due to an increase of the induced magnetic dipole, by analogy with a classical conducting coil, whose magnetic field increases with the number of turns (highly delocalized electron density plays the role of such a conducting coil)37,54. Introduction of symmetric push-pull motifs (donor-π-donor, acceptor-donor-acceptor, etc.) into the structure leads to a sizable increase of the electric transition dipoles and re-distribution of the energies of the excited states, which in combination with the increased magnetic coupling of conjugated systems can result in an enhancement of mixed electric-magnetic hyperpolarizabilities governing nonlinear chiro-optical response37,51,52. Another way to manipulate linear and nonlinear chiro-optical response is to employ post-processing, for example, annealing, that results in the formation of supramolecular helicity through the entire length of polymeric fibrils57,58. Here, the three-photon excitation-generated upconverted emission is not just greatly enhanced due to the double-resonance condition but also reveals substantial modulation due to the vibronic coupling that is extremely sensitive to the circular polarization states of the two incident laser beams.

Results and discussion

Linear optical characterization

Figure 2a shows the FTIR absorption spectra measured for the PF8TTS polymer (from now on P*, see the SI for the details of synthesis and film preparation) film sample on a glass plate and from a plain glass substrate, respectively. From this figure, one can see that in the measured spectral range, there is one strong polymer-related absorption peak at 2852 cm−1, corresponding to the vibrational transitions around 3.5 μm. This transition can be assigned to sp3 C–H bond stretching of the chiral methyl group residing on the side chains and is chosen for the vibrational resonance-enhanced nonlinear circular dichroism study. Figure 2b shows the UV-visible linear absorption spectrum of the same film sample, indicating a broad and strong absorption band peaking at ~470 nm wavelength, with a fading tail in the 600-650 nm range. The circular dichroism (CD) spectrum of thin P* film shows a strong response within the spectral region of the absorbance band (Fig. S4a). Notably, only minimal linear dichroism (LD) is registered (Fig. S4b), indicating that the observed ellipticity mainly results from the polymer’s inherent chiral properties. Vibrational circular dichroism (VCD) spectrum of P* film (Fig. S5a) showed a clear signal in the mid-infrared (MIR) range between 2800 and 3100 cm−1, indicating IR-active chiral vibration of aliphatic side chains. Figure 2c shows the measured fluorescence spectral curves of the film sample excited by a ~ 400 nm laser beam and, separately, by a ~ 1300 nm laser beam. The beams were generated by a ~ 100-fs and frequency-doubled laser system and an associated optical parametric oscillator (OPO) system, respectively. The nearly identical spectral curves in Fig. 2c reveal that the fluorescence originates from the same excited state of the polymer under one-photon ( ~ 400 nm) and three-photon (~ 1.3 μm) excitations, resembling the emission behaviors of some other material systems (including organic dyes and semiconductor nanoparticles) under one-photon and multi-photon excitation conditions22,23. It has also be noted that the emission generated by three-photon absorption of 1300 nm beam only is much weaker than that generated by 400 nm excitation and resonant two-beam action.

Upconverted emission

We first explore the effect of vibrational resonance on the three-photon excited upconverted emission of the polymer. Two linearly polarized pulsed laser beams with the wavelengths of λ1 ≈ 1.3 μm and λ2 ≈ 3.5 μm are used to excite the polymer sample and generate frequency-upconverted emission. Here, the wavelength, λ2, of the MIR beam is tuned to the resonance with the strong vibrational transition ~2852 cm−1 of the polymer. Based on the observed emission spectra, we could identify the following contributing mechanisms: 1. Instantaneous (direct) three-photon absorption, leading to the transition of polymer molecules from their ground state to an excited electronic state, followed by fluorescence emission (Fig. 1a); 2. Two-step three-photon absorption, i.e., one-photon absorption of the λ2 ≈ 3.5μm beam in the first step and subsequent two-photon absorption of the λ1 ≈ 1.3 μm beam in the second step (Fig. 1b); 3. Three-photon-induced sum-frequency generation (3 P SFG), which can generate coherent emission at a few different wavelengths, depending on the energy of the combined photons (for example, two 1.3 μm photons and one 3.5 μm photon or two 3.5 μm photons and one 1.3 μm photon). Note that the resonance enhancement related to the λ2 ≈ 3.5 μm beam plays the same key role in all the above-mentioned mechanisms.

Figure 3a shows the measured spectral profile of the upconverted emission from the polymer sample, which clearly implies two different contributions: coherent emission with two peaks at ~550 nm and ~749 nm due to three-photon sum-frequency generation, and a broad incoherent fluorescence emission in the spectral range between these two spectral peaks. In this figure, the broken curve is directly adapted from the spectral profile of fluorescence emission shown in Fig. 2c. Note that under our experimental conditions, the coherent emission at ~550 nm may also contribute to fluorescence emission via its reabsorption inside the film material.

The data shown in Table 1 indicate that the overall upconversion signal intensities are strongly dependent on the polarization state of each beam, which directly reveals circular polarization-(SAM) sensitive coupling of nuclear and electronic motions of the chiral polymer. The measured maximum intensity ratio is ILR/ILL = 0.4:1. Here ILR represents the case when the 3.5 µm beam is in left-handed circular polarization state, while the 1.3 µm beam is in the right-handed circular polarization state, and ILL represents the case when both beams have the same left-handed circular polarization. At other polarization combinations, the intensity ratios varied from ILR/IRR = 0.49:1 to IRR/IRL = 0.95:1. The broad range of the values reflects the fact that the polymer was synthesized and purified with enantiomeric excess close to 100% and so the weak coupling of the NIR light with the handedness opposite to that of the enantiomer to the electronic degrees of freedom may result in inefficient generation of the coherent signal, which lowers the contrast between the signals with the opposite handedness of MIR light.

It should also be mentioned that, given the coherence of the 3P-SFG process, there is a chance that polarization-dependent propagation effects may come into play, therefore affecting the observed dissymmetry of upconverted emission signals. To rule out this possibility in our experiments, we collected all upconverted light by an open-aperture PMT detector, so possible angular dispersion effects can be neglected. Next, the fluorescence and the three-photon sum-frequency generation are both relatively broadband signals (Fig. 3), hence the possible interference fringes effect would be smoothened out. Finally, Fresnel reflection loss on the film surface is ~4% which is insensitive to the minor refractive-index changes.

Figure 4 shows the upconversion emission spectra for two combinations of the incident polarization states. showing the deepest modulation in the emission signal: LR and LL. One can clearly see that the upconverted emission yield for both beams having the same left-handed circular polarization is significantly larger than that with the two beams having opposite circular polarizations. It is important to note that the signals of the coherent emission, originating in sum-frequency generation, show much deeper intensity modulation than that of the incoherent fluorescence. We argue that the main reason for this discrepancy is the key role that the phase-matched coupling between the electronic and nuclear motion plays in the generation of a coherent signal. Also note the peak at ~795 nm for LR combination, which may be related to the difference-frequency generation (DFG) process, when the energy of 3.5 µm photon is subtracted from the combined energy of two 1.3 µm photons.

Since the dissymmetry is observed for the upconverted emission, the most intuitive quantification of the effect, which can be introduced to relate our study to the vast body of work on chiral systems, would be the upconversion dissymmetry ratio: gUC=2ILL−ILRILL+ILR. This can be obtained for either the SFG process or the 3PA-induced upconversion process. For example, gUC = 0.75 for SFG at 550 nm. This quantity would be methodologically closer to the conventional absorption dissymmetry ratio gabs, than the circularly polarized luminescence ratio glum, because of the implicit dependence of the upconverted emission on the polarization-dependent resonantly enhanced excitation process. In the spectral region located between the SFG-generated peaks, gUC is perhaps equivalent to gabs, if it were to be estimated for the nonlinear absorption, instead of conventional linear CD.

To reveal some mechanistic understanding of the studied phenomenon, we performed the quantum chemical calculations of the molecular orbitals giving the largest contributions to the dipole-allowed transition from the ground to the first excited electronic state of the monomer, dimer and tetramer sequences (see Fig. S8 of the SI). The dipole-allowed transition happens to be of π→π* character, as clearly witnessed by the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied (HOMO and LUMO) molecular orbitals (MOs, Fig. S8d–f). The electron density of these MOs is delocalized over the structural core of the molecules, comprising fluorene and thiophene rings, out of their molecular plane. In this case, the in-plane stretching of C–H bonds, residing on the sidechains, cannot couple to the electronic π→π* transition, which would result in the emission signal independent of the polarization state of the 3.5 µm beam. This, however, would only hold if HOMO → LUMO transition were the only one contributing to S0 → S1 electronic state transition of the molecule, which is the case with the monomer. The underlying reason is the symmetry of the MOs of the flat in-plane geometry of the monomer (Fig. S8a). The situation starts changing, going from the monomer to the dimer, and further to the tetramer (and, by induction, to the whole polymeric sequence). Because of the steric hindrance of the aliphatic chiral sidechains, the previously flat geometry of the monomeric units acquires both intra- and inter-unit twists (Fig. S8b, c). This results in the MOs with symmetries other than that of HOMO and LUMO contributing to S0 → S1 transition. Specifically, HOMO-1 and HOMO−2 MOs of the tetramer sequence have their contributions to the electronic transition compared to that of HOMO (Fig. S8g). A closer look at the HOMO-2 orbital reveals part of its electron density residing on the side chains. Vibrations of local C-H bonds of the sidechains are now coupled to the electronic motion (S0 → S1 transition) because of the overlap between the electron density localized on the sidechains and the electron density delocalized over the conjugated backbone of the polymer. Driving, via applying MIR circularly-polarized light, the helical displacement of this density in- (with the circularly-polarized NIR light of the same handedness) or out-of-phase (with the circularly-polarized NIR light of the opposite handedness) with the displacement of the electron density residing on the fluorene and thiophene structural core units results in strong modulation of the coherent emission signal. Thus, the supramolecular helical organization of the polymer appears to be the “driving force” behind the vibronic enantiomeric selectivity, resulting in the dissymmetry of the observed upconverted emission.

In the context of future studies, the most important criterion for a chiral system to exhibit the reported dissymmetry of the upconverted emission would naturally be a sizable overlap of the electron densities involved in MIR and electronic transitions. This translates either into helicity of the aromatic backbone, when the manifold of the local stretching vibrations of, for example, C–C bonds would lack the inversion symmetry, or to relative proximity of the chiral bonds of the sidechains to the abovementioned conjugated backbone. In small non-flat organic molecules, the latter is naturally much easier to devise; however, the effect could be much weaker simply because of the smaller value of the nonlinear susceptibility of the third order that governs the process of the three-photon sum-frequency generation. To increase this value, one needs to either design a system with a very high degree of electronic delocalization, similar to that of aromatic conjugated systems, or utilize the large density of intermediate states of asymmetric twisted π-electron system chromophores (zwitterionic systems). The latter may be of great interest to explore as they possess very high values of optical nonlinearities60. In conclusion, the interplay between the overlaps of the electron densities associated with a specific molecular vibration and an electronic transition, and the optical nonlinearity of a molecular system, which itself can be related to a specific electron density configuration, will determine the efficiency of enantio-selective nonlinear MIR-NIR upconversion.

Two-beam cross-correlation measurements

To shed light on the possible effect of the vibrational relaxation dissymmetry on the observed dissymmetry of the upconverted emission, we performed two-beam cross-correlation analysis. Assuming both input beams have the same symmetric pulse shape, one can expect that the direct two-beam 3PA excitation and the three-photon sum-frequency generation will both lead to a symmetric cross-correlation curve, which can be obtained by smoothly changing the time delay between the two pulses and synchronously recording the variation of the upconverted emission signal. Conversely, the two-step 3PA is not an instantaneous process, but instead allows the 1.3 μm pulse to arrive at the sample later than the 3.5 μm pulse, given a delay shorter than the lifetime of the excited vibrational level. Therefore, considering that all of the above-mentioned processes contribute to the upconverted emission signal, the observed two-beam cross-correlation curve should manifest an asymmetric feature that may reveal some useful information about the relaxation of the excited vibrational level.

Figure 5a shows a boxcar (gated integrator) system-recorded cross-correlation (CC) curve with an asymmetric tail at positive delay times, while Fig. 5b shows deconvolution of the CC function into a symmetric Gaussian function, representing the direct 3PA and three-photon SFG processes and an asymmetric contribution. The latter is due to the two-step 3PA process. The dash-dot curve of the asymmetric contribution (Fig. 5b) essentially represents the population dynamics of the vibrational level, with the buildup of the population due to the resonant MIR excitation followed by its decay after the departure of the MIR pulse. At time delay P the temporal overlapping of the two pulses is absent, so only the two-step 3PA can contribute to the cross-correlation signal. Therefore, the decay after this point solely represents the relaxation of the molecules excited to the vibrational level. The extracted decay time of τ′ = 140 fs (± 10 fs) can therefore be recognized as the lifetime of the vibrational level reached by ~2852 cm−1 transition (see Fig. 2a). On the other hand, since the spectral linewidth of the ~2852 cm−1 vibrational band is ~140 cm−1 (Fig. 2a), the lifetime of the corresponding vibrational level should be in the range of ~200 fs according to the uncertainty principle. Our measured result of τ′ value is quite close to the above expected value.

Additionally, our cross-correlation measurements revealed no dependence of the lifetime of the excited vibrational level on the circular polarization states of the two beams. This is consistent with our observation of lower dissymmetry of the upconverted emission in-between the blue and red edges of the emission spectrum, where the three-photon absorption-induced fluorescence dominates, compared to that due to three-photon sum-frequency generation.

In conclusion, we report vibrational resonance-enhanced three-photon excitation employed for studying the effect of vibronic coupling on the nonlinear chiro-optical properties of a chiral medium. In our experiment, one mid-IR pulsed laser beam at 3.5 µm and one near-IR laser beam at 1.3 µm were applied to generate three-photon excitation-induced upconverted emission from the sample. Both pulsed beams had the same pulse duration of ~100 fs, and the mid-IR beam was set to be in resonance with the strong vibrational transition of the sample material at ~2852 cm−1. Our study reveals the following key points:

(i) The two-beam double-resonance-enhanced three-photon excitation generates upconverted emission signals that can be at least one order of magnitude stronger than those created separately by every single beam.

(ii) A deep modulation of the upconverted emission can be achieved via de-phasing the coupling between the electronic and nuclear degrees of freedom through varying the polarization states of the incident laser beams, with the measured maximum intensity ratio of the upconverted signals 0.4:1. To the best of our knowledge no other experimental observations of enantio-selective vibronic coupling in nonlinear optical processes were reported before.

(iii) The two-beam cross-correlation measurements reveal the information on the lifetime of the vibrationally excited level, which is insensitive to the polarization state of the mid-IR wave.

(iv) The electronic structure calculations reveal that an overlap of the electron density delocalized over the structural core of the polymer and that, residing on the chiral sidechains, is promoted by the supramolecular organization of the monomeric units into a helical structure of the polymer.

Methods

Sample preparation

The chiral polymer, P* (PF8TTS_30K, molecular weight 30,000 Da), was synthesized using a polycondensation reaction between bis-pinacolato-boron-substituted fluorene and 5,5’-dibromo-2,2’-bithiophene, which was catalyzed by tetrakis(triphenylphosphine)-palladium (0) (Fig. S1, Scheme 1).57,58 The reaction was performed in a basic environment using a mixed solvent system of 1,4-dioxane and water, with Aliquat 336 acting as a phase transfer catalyst to improve reaction efficiency. After the synthesis, the polymer was purified through multiple steps of reprecipitation and Soxhlet extraction using methanol, acetone, and chloroform. The final product films were produced by spin-casting polymer solution in toluene onto glass substrates to analyze the linear absorption and circular dichroism properties. The films were annealed at 120 °C in a vacuum oven for three hours (Fig. S1, Scheme 2).

Experimental setup

The pulsed 3.5 μm and ~1.3 μm beams were sourced from an optical parametric oscillator (OPO, Opera SoLo, from Coherent) pumped by a ~ 800 nm and ~100 fs laser oscillator and amplifier system (Astrella HE from Coherent) working at 1 kHz repetition rate (Fig. S6a). The spatially separate two pulsed beams propagated along different optical paths and were finely focused on the same surface position of the film sample. An adjustable optical delay line either ensures a perfect temporal overlapping of the pulses from the two beams or provides a continuous change of the delay between the pulses from the two beams for cross-correlation measurements. The three-photon absorption excited upconversion fluorescence emission, and the 3PSFG signals were detected together by a photomultiplier (PMT) that was connected either to a 500-MHz oscilloscope or a Boxcar (Gated Integrator) system. UV-Vis, fluorescence, and CD spectra were measured on a Shimadzu UV-3101PC spectrometer, RF-5301PC, and JASCO J-1700 CD spectropolarimeter (scanning rate of 100 nm min−1, bandwidth of 1 nm, response time of 1 s, with a single accumulation), respectively. FTIR/ATR spectra were obtained using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two Fourier Transform Infrared system across a wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm−1. Vibrational circular dichroism (VCD) data for the chiral PF8TTS polymer film were collected using a JASCO VCD spectrometer, the high-performance FVS-6000 model.
