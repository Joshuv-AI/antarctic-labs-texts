Terminal singularities of the moduli space of curves on low degree hypersurfaces and the circle method
Jakob Glas, Matthew Hase-Liu

Abstract
We study the singularities of the moduli space of degree e e maps from smooth genus g g curves to an arbitrary smooth hypersurface of low degree. For e e large compared to g g , we show that these moduli spaces have at worst terminal singularities. Our main approach is to study the jet schemes of these moduli spaces by developing a suitable form of the circle method.

The interplay between geometry and number theory has had a long and rich history. Examples like Deligne’s resolution of the Weil conjectures

Occasionally, this flow of information can be reversed and tools from analytic number theory can be used to investigate objects of geometric interest. For instance, building on ideas of Ellenberg and Venkatesh, Browning and Vishe

Of course, instead of investigating the quality of the singularities, one may ask the related question of whether the singular locus of
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}(X,e)
is large. Because terminality only appears in at least codimension three, our results give a weak lower bound on the codimension of the singular locus of
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}(X,e)
. When
d\geq 3
and
n\geq 2d
, Harris, Roth and Starr
[
13
]
showed that
\mathcal{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is generically smooth for generic
X
. In an unpublished note, Starr and Tian moreover proved a lower bound for the codimension of the singular locus for general hypersurfaces of low degree using a bend-and-break approach. For an arbitrary smooth hypersurface
X
, Browning and Sawin
[
5
]
used the circle method to give stronger bounds on the codimension of the singular locus of
\mathcal{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
at the expense of an exponential lower bound on

in terms of
d
. Recently, in
[
19
]
, Lehmann, Riedl, and Tanimoto used ideas from developments related to geometric Manin’s conjecture to give a non-explicit linear lower bound on the codimension of families of non-free curves that do not arise from accumulating maps for even the higher genus setting and an arbitrary Fano variety
X
.

Outline

We now give a brief overview of the main steps in the proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
. Instead of working with
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}(X,e)
directly, it suffices to work with the moduli space
\Mor(C,X,L)
of degree
e
morphisms from a fixed curve
C
to
X
corresponding to a specific line bundle
L
. We explain this reduction in Section
2.1
.

More precisely, recall there is a natural map
\Mor_{e}\left(C,X\right)\to\Pic^{e}\left(C\right)

given by pulling back
\mathcal{O}_{X}\left(1\right)
to
C\times T
along

C\times T\to X\times T
for a
T
-point of
\Mor_{e}\left(C,X\right)
. Denote by
\Mor(C,X,L)
the fiber above a closed point
[L]\in\Pic^{e}(C)
. Our proof relies on studying the jet schemes
J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))
as well as the iterated jet schemes
J_{1}\left(J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))\right)
for any integer
m\geq 0
. The link to terminal and canonical singularities is provided by a result due to Mustaţă
[
22
]
, which relates terminality and canonicity of local complete intersection varieties to geometric properties about their (iterated) jet schemes, namely that for all
m
, the schemes
J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))
and
J_{1}\left(J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))\right)
are irreducible local complete intersection varieties.

More precisely, we will deduce Theorems
1
and
2
from the following results.

Proposition 4
.

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve of genus
g
over the complex numbers. Under the assumptions of Theorem
1
, for any
m\geq 0
, the jet schemes
J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))
and iterated jet schemes
J_{1}(J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L)))
are irreducible and of the expected dimensions
(m+1)\dim\Mor(C,X,L)
and
2(m+1)\dim\Mor(C,X,L)
, respectively, for all
[L]\in\Pic^{e}(C)
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve of genus
g
over the complex numbers. Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
, the jet schemes
J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))
are irreducible of the expected dimension
(m+1)\dim\Mor(C,X,L)
for all
m\geq 0
and for all
[L]\in\Pic^{e}(C)
.

Remark 6
.

It seems plausible, though extremely tedious, to prove results similar to Propositions
4
and
5
for iterated jet schemes of the form
J_{1}(J_{1}(\cdots J_{1}(J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L)))\cdots))
by the same strategy that we present in this paper. More precisely, if
u
is the number of
J_{1}
’s in this iterated jet scheme, by appropriately modifying the proof of Theorem 3.3 in
[
11
]
, it seems likely that one should be able to show for
n
sufficiently large compared to
d^{u}2^{d}
and
e
large compared to
d
,
u
, and
g
, the discrepancies of
\Mor(C,X,L)
are all larger than
u
.

It would also be interesting to investigate the behavior of iterated higher-order jet schemes of the form
J_{m}(J_{m^{\prime}}(\Mor(C,X,L)))
for arbitrary
m,m^{\prime}
, for which generalizations of Mustaţă’s results relating jet schemes and canonical singularities are not known. See
[
23
]
for further questions and discussions about generalized jet schemes.

Let us now discuss our approach in more detail, restricting to the case
C=\mathbb{P}^{1}
for simplicity. Our strategy follows the path paved by Browning and Vishe
[
6
]
. More precisely, after performing a spreading out argument, it suffices to prove the corresponding results over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. By appealing to the Lang–Weil estimate this is equivalent to understanding the number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-points on
J_{m}(\Mor_{e}(C,X))
and
J_{1}(J_{m}(\Mor_{e}(C,X)))
as
q
goes to infinity. At least for
C=\mathbb{P}^{1}
, these correspond to
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t][s]/s^{m+1}
-points and
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t][s,r]/\left\langle s^{m+1},r^{2}\right\rangle
-points of degree
e
in
t
on
x
. We solve this counting problem by developing a suitable version of the circle method. When
C=\mathbb{P}^{1}
, this counting problem can be interpreted as counting the number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
-points of bounded height on a system of
m+1
equations (resp.
2(m+1)
) in
(n+1)(m+1)
(resp.
2(n+1)(m+1)
) variables. Classical applications of the circle method require the number of variables to grow roughly quadratically in the number of equations. In particular, since for
n,d
and
e
we want to count points for
all

m\geq 0
, this approach seems hopeless and in fact Browning and Sawin
[
5
]
even wrote in their work that it does not seem possible to prove that
\mathcal{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
has canonical singularities using the circle method. Nevertheless, we succeed using a modified version of the circle method, as we shall now explain.

The key point is that we do not treat the equations for
J_{m}(\Mor_{e}(C,X))
as a system of equations over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
, but keep working with it as a single equation over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t][s]/s^{m+1}
. Our situation may thus be compared to that over number fields: if one attempts to count solutions to a single equation over a number field, but uses Weil restriction to consider it as a system of equations over
\mathbb{Q}
, then a naive application of the circle method requires the number of variables to grow quadratically in the degree of the number field. However, as demonstrated by Skinner
[
29
]
, if things are set up correctly, one can essentially still treat the system as a single equation and obtain completely analogous results to those of
\mathbb{Q}
.

In our approach we use a geometric interpretation of harmonic analysis over
(\mathbb{F}_{q}[s]/s^{m+1})(\!(t^{-1})\!)
following
[
14
]
, which is particularly useful in the higher genus setting. Then, we continue along the standard lines of attack of the circle method. This includes a division into minor and major arcs followed by a suitable form of Weyl differencing to bound the exponential sums that arise in this process.

The aforementioned results on irreducibility and expected dimension for moduli spaces of rational curves on smooth low degree hypersurfaces have been generalized to the setting of smooth complete intersections of the same degree by Browning, Vishe and Yamagishi
[
7
]
. More precisely, given
{p_{1},\dots,p_{b}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}}
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{b}\in X
, they extended their method to deal with the spaces
\Mor_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X,p_{1},\dots,p_{b};y_{1},\dots,y_{b})
of degree
e
morphisms
f\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\to X
satisfying
f(b_{i})=p_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,b
. Their approach is based on a function field version of Rydin Myerson’s work
[
26
,
27
]
that allows one to handle systems of forms with the circle method for which the number of variables only grows linearly in the number of equations. It seems plausible that our work extends to the setting of
[
7
]
and it would be interesting to see whether Rydin Myerson’s approach could be useful in the study of terminal singularities.

2  Preliminaries

2.1
Reduction to maps out of a fixed curve

In this subsection, we explain how to deduce a statement about terminal singularities of the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}(X,e)
from that of
\Mor(C,X,L)
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a flat and finite type morphism
of integral algebraic stacks locally essentially of finite type over

\mathbb{C}
. Suppose
\mathcal{Y}
is regular and that all closed fibers
of
f
have at most terminal (resp. canonical) singularities. Then,
\mathcal{X}
has at
worst terminal (resp. canonical) singularities.

Proof.

This argument is due to Takumi Murayama (see
[
21
]
). We first reduce to the case of schemes. Let
V\to\mathcal{Y}
be
a smooth atlas and consider the fiber product
V\times_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{X}
.
Then, let
U\to V\times_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{X}
be a smooth atlas
as well. Note that the composition
U\to V\times_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{X}\to V

satisfies the conditions of the lemma, since
V
is regular over

\mathbb{C}
, and the closed fibers, because they are smooth over the closed
fibers of
f
, have at worst terminal (resp. canonical) singularities by Proposition 2.15 of
[
17
]
(having at worst terminal or canonical singularities is a smooth-local property). So we may assume
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are schemes.

Replacing
\mathcal{Y}
with
\Spec\left(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y},y}\right)

for
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, we can reduce to the case of
\mathcal{Y}

the spectrum of a regular local ring, say with
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}

a regular system of parameters.

For
d=0
, there is nothing to prove. Assume the claim is true inductively
for smaller
d
. By the induction hypothesis,
f^{-1}\left(V(x_{d})\right)
has at
worst terminal (resp. canonical) singularities. Since
f^{-1}\left(V(x_{d})\right)

is a Cartier divisor and small deformations of terminal (resp. canonical) singularities
are also terminal (resp. canonical) by Proposition 4.15 of
[
20
]
, it follows that the singularities in a neighborhood
of
f^{-1}\left(V(x_{d})\right)
are at worst terminal (resp. canonical).

By Corollary 4.18(i) of
[
20
]
, every fiber of
f
has at worst terminal (resp. canonical) singularities. Then, the complement of
f^{-1}\left(V(x_{d})\right)
also has at worst
terminal (resp. canonical) singularities by applying the induction hypothesis to the
restriction of
f
to
\Spec\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{Y},y}\left[x_{d}^{-1}\right]
,
since the latter has dimension less than
d
.
The result follows.
∎

Corollary 8
.

Let
g\geq 0
be an integer. For all smooth projective curves
C
over
\mathbb{C}
of genus
g
and all
[L]\in\Pic^{e}(C)
, suppose
\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)
is a non-empty local complete intersection of the expected dimension with at worst terminal (resp. canonical) singularities. Then,
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}\left(X,e\right)
is also a local complete intersection with at worst terminal (resp. canonical) singularities.

Proof.

Consider the forgetful morphism
f\colon\mathcal{M}_{g,0}\left(X,e\right)\to\mathcal{M}_{g}
, which on points forgets the map to
X
. We first claim that
f
is flat.

To verify this, we invoke miracle flatness, i.e. checking that the source is Cohen–Macualay, the target is regular, and the fibers are of the same dimension.

In general, for a surjective map of varieties or Deligne–Mumford stacks
X\to Y
, we have
\dim X_{y}\geq\dim X-\dim Y
for any point
y\in Y
. Since every fiber
\Mor_{e}\left(C,X\right)
of
f
has the expected dimension, the total space
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}\left(X,e\right)
must also be of the expected dimension, i.e. it is a local complete intersection Deligne–Mumford stack cut out by
de-g+1
equations in the smooth Deligne–Mumford stack
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}(\mathbb{P}^{n},e)
by Proposition 3 of
[
15
]
.

In particular,
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}\left(X,e\right)
is Cohen–Macaulay,
\mathcal{M}_{g}
is always smooth, and the fibers are of the same (expected) dimension, so we conclude that
f
is flat.

The same argument shows that the map
\Mor_{e}(C,X)\to\Pic^{e}(C)
is flat with closed fibers
\Mor(C,X,L)
. By assumption on the singularities of
\Mor(C,X,L)
, Lemma
7
then implies that
\Mor_{e}(C,X)
has at worst terminal (resp. canonical) singularities. Then, applying Lemma
7
again to the map
f
implies that
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}(X,e)
has at worst terminal (resp. canonical) singularities.
∎

Remark 9
.

For
g=0
, we can argue more directly as follows:
\mathcal{M}_{0,0}\left(X,e\right)

can be realized as the stack quotient
\left[\Mor_{e}\left(C,X\right)/\PGL_{2}\right]
,
and since
\PGL_{2}
is smooth, we have
\Mor_{e}\left(C,X\right)\to\mathcal{M}_{0,0}\left(X,e\right)

is an atlas, and hence we can simply use the fact that having at worst terminal (resp. canonical) singularities
is smooth-local.

2.2
Properties of jet schemes

Following
[
22
]
, let us recall some basic facts about jet schemes. Fix a field of arbitrary characteristic
k
and let
Y
be a scheme of finite type over
k
.

For each integer
m\geq 0
, the
m
th jet scheme
J_{m}(Y)
is defined as the scheme whose representable functor of points
F
in the category of finite type
k
-schemes satisfies

F(A)=Y\left(A[t]/t^{m+1}\right),

where
A
is any finitely-generated
k
-algebra. For instance, there are canonical isomorphisms
J_{0}(Y)\cong Y
and
J_{1}(Y)\cong\mathcal{T}Y
, the total space of the tangent bundle of
Y
.

The various jet schemes are equipped with canonical maps
\phi_{m}\colon J_{m}\left(Y\right)\to J_{m-1}\left(Y\right),
which on the level of points are defined by modding out by
t^{m}.
Let us write
\pi_{m}\colon J_{m}\left(Y\right)\to Y
to denote the canonical projection (alternatively, this is simply the composition
\phi_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{m}
).

The construction of jet schemes is compatible with open immersions, and it is hence possible to describe them explicitly by reducing to the affine case. From this construction, one can show that if
Y
is a smooth, connected variety of dimension
n
, then for every
m
, the morphism
\pi_{m}
is an affine bundle whose fibers are
mn
-dimensional. Moreover,
J_{m}\left(Y\right)
is smooth, connected, and
(m+1)n
-dimensional.

In general, for any finite type
k
-scheme
X
, the
expected dimension
of
J_{m}(X)
is
(m+1)\dim X
.

In Theorem 1.3 of
[
10
]
and Proposition 1.7 of
[
22
]
, Ein and Mustaţă establishes the following criteria for having canonical and terminal singularities.

Theorem 10
.

Let
Y
be a local complete intersection variety. Then

(i)

Y
has at worst terminal (resp. canonical) singularities
if and only if the jet schemes
J_{m}(Y)
are normal (resp. irreducible) for every
m
,

(ii)

J_{m}(Y)
is a local complete intersection variety if
J_{m}(Y)
has the expected dimension, and

(iii)

Y
is normal if
J_{m}(Y)
is irreducible for some

m\geq 1
(equivalently for
m=1
).

Assuming the validity of Propositions
4
and
5
, Ein and Mustaţă’s criteria implies the main theorems:

Proof of Theorems
1
and
2
, assuming Propositions
4
and
5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
, it follows that
J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)
is irreducible of the expected dimension for all
m
, which by Ein and Mustaţă’s criteria implies that
\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)
has at worst canonical singularities. Then, by Corollary
8
,
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}(X,e)
also has at worst canonical singularities.

Similarly, under the assumptions of the Theorem
1
, the schemes
J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)
and
J_{1}\left(J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)\right)
are both irreducible of the expected dimension for all
m
, which by Ein and Mustaţă’s criteria implies that
J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)
is normal for all
m
, which again by Ein and Mustaţă’s criteria implies that
\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)
has at worst terminal singularities. Then, by Corollary
8
,
\mathcal{M}_{g,0}(X,e)
also has at worst terminal singularities.
∎

So it remains to prove Propositions
4
and
5
.

2.3
Spreading out and reduction to point-counting

Let us recall the spreading out process as described in Section 2 of
[
6
]
. For every
m
, note that both
J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)
and
J_{1}\left(J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)\right)
are finite type and hence can be defined over a finitely-generated
\mathbb{Z}
-algebra
\Lambda
(by using the explicit equations cutting out these schemes). More precisely, for fixed
m
, we may assume
C,

X,

J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)
, and
J_{1}\left(J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)\right)
, before base-changing to an algebraic closure, are the generic fibers of the finite type morphisms
\mathcal{C}\to\Spec\Lambda
,
\mathcal{X}\to\Spec\Lambda
,
Y_{m}\to\Spec\Lambda
, and
Y_{1,m}\to\Spec\Lambda
, respectively.

By Zariski’s lemma, for any maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of
\Lambda
, the quotient
\Lambda/\mathfrak{m}
is a finite field. Then, irreducibility of
J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)
and
J_{1}\left(J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)\right)
will follow from irreducibility of the fibers
Y_{m}\times\Spec\Lambda/\mathfrak{m}
and
Y_{1,m}\times\Spec\Lambda/\mathfrak{m}
.

Also, by possibly enlarging
\Lambda
, we may assume the fibers
\mathcal{C}\times\Spec\Lambda/\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathcal{X}\times\Spec\Lambda/\mathfrak{m}
are still smooth. By inverting
d!
in
\Lambda
, we may also assume that the residue field of any maximal ideal has characteristic larger than
d
.

As established in the proof of Theorem 1 of
[
14
]
for
g\geq 1
and Theorem 1.1 of
[
5
]
, the space of morphisms
\Mor(C,X,L)
for any
[L]\in\Pic^{e}(C)
is irreducible and of the expected dimension, i.e. are local complete intersections, under the assumptions in either Proposition
4
or
5
.

Using the affine bundle structure of the projection maps
\pi_{m}
of jet schemes of the smooth loci, it follows that the jet schemes in consideration always have at least the expected dimension.
By Chevalley’s theorem on constructible sets, there is a non-empty open subset
U\subset\Spec\Lambda
such that the dimension of the generic fiber of
Y_{m}\to\Spec\Lambda
(resp.
Y_{1,m}\to\Spec\Lambda
) is at most the dimension of any fiber above a closed point in
U
.
As a consequence, it suffices to show that
J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)
and
J_{1}\left(J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)\right)
are irreducible and of the expected dimension when
C
and
X
are defined over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

By the Lang-Weil bounds, this is equivalent to the following:

\lim_{q\to\infty}q^{-(m+1)\mu}\#J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}\right)\leq 1

(2.1)

and

\lim_{q\to\infty}q^{-2(m+1)\mu}\#J_{1}\left(J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)\right)\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}\right)\leq 1,

(2.2)

where
\mu=\dim\Mor(C,X,L)=(n+1)(e-g+1)-(de-g+1)-1.

More precisely, to prove Proposition
4
, it suffices to establish (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) (under the assumptions of Proposition
4
). Similarly, to prove Proposition
5
, it suffices to establish (
2.1
) (under the assumptions of Proposition
5
).

Let
F\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
be a form of degree
d
defining
X
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.
The rational points of
\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)
can be described explicitly as tuples of the following form:

\left\{\left(s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\right)\in H^{0}(C,L)^{n+1}\colon F\left(s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\right)=0,s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\text{ globally generate}\right\}/\mathbb{G}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

Denote by
M\left(C,X,L\right)
the cone of
\Mor\left(C,X,L\right),
which we mean to denote the variety whose rational points are the same as those of
\Mor\left(C,X,L\right),
except without the quotient by
\mathbb{G}_{m}.

Lemma 11
.

An
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
-point of
M\left(C,X,L\right)

is given by a tuple
{\vec{x}\in H^{0}(C,L)^{n+1}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}}
such that
\vec{x}\bmod t

globally generates and
F\left(\vec{x}\right)=0
.

Similarly,
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t,r]/\left\langle t^{m+1},r^{2}\right\rangle
-points of
M\left(C,X,L\right)
are given by

\vec{x}\in H^{0}(C,L)^{n+1}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t,r]/\left\langle t^{m+1},r^{2}\right\rangle
such that
\vec{x}\bmod\left\langle t,r\right\rangle

globally generates and
F\left(\vec{x}\right)=0
.

Proof.

We will only prove the first claim; the second goes through identically. An
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
-point of
\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)

is simply a morphism
C\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}\to X\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
with corresponding line bundle isomorphic to
L\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
,
which is a morphism
C\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}\to\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}}^{n}

that factors through
X\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
.

This is given by an
(n+1)
-tuple of global sections
\vec{x}
of

H^{0}\left(C\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1},L\otimes_{k}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}\right)
, which is isomorphic to
H^{0}\left(C,L\right)\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
,
that globally generates and factors through
X\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
. Suppose that
\vec{x}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
.
To globally generate means that the map of sheaves

\left(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\right)\colon\mathcal{O}_{C}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}\to L\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}

is surjective.

Suppose
\vec{x}\bmod t
globally generates. Then, working on the level of stalks, we may assume
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\in R[t]/t^{m+1}
where
R=\mathcal{O}_{C,p}
at some point
p\in C
such that
\left\langle x_{0}\bmod t,\ldots,x_{n}\bmod t\right\rangle=R.
Since
C
is smooth, note that
R
is a discrete valuation ring.

This implies that we can find
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}\in R
such that

a_{0}x_{0}+\cdots+a_{n}x_{n}=1+tr^{\prime}
for some
r^{\prime}\in R
. However,
1+tr^{\prime}

is invertible in
R[t]/t^{m+1}
and hence
\left\langle x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\right\rangle=R[t]/t^{m+1}
, as desired.
∎

In particular, from the lemma, it follows that the rational points of
J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)
comprise tuples of the form

\left\{\left(s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\right)\in\frac{H^{0}(C,L)^{n+1}[t]}{\left\langle t^{m+1}\right\rangle}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}F\left(s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\right)=0,\\
s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\text{ glob. gen. mod $t$}\end{array}\right\}/\mathbb{G}_{m}\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}\right),

and the rational points of
J_{1}\left(J_{m}\left(\Mor\left(C,X,L\right)\right)\right)
comprise tuples of the form

\left\{\left(s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\right)\in\frac{H^{0}(C,L)^{n+1}[t,r]}{\left\langle t^{m+1},r^{2}\right\rangle}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}F\left(s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\right)=0,\\
s_{0},\ldots,s_{n}\text{ glob. gen. mod $t,r$}\end{array}\right\}/\mathbb{G}_{m}\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}[t,r]/\left\langle t^{m+1},r^{2}\right\rangle\right),

where
H^{0}(C,L)^{n+1}[t]/\left\langle t^{m+1}\right\rangle
is simply the tensor product
H^{0}(C,L)^{n+1}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
and likewise for
H^{0}(C,L)^{n+1}[t,r]/\left\langle t^{m+1},r^{2}\right\rangle
.

Then, in the same vein as for
M(C,X,L)
, for any
[L]\in\Pic^{e}(C)
, we denote by
M_{m}(C,X,L)
and
M_{1,m}(C,X,L)
the cones of
J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))
and
J_{1}(J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L)))
, respectively, i.e. whose rational points are the same except without the quotient by
J_{m}(\mathbb{G}_{m})
, respectively
J_{1}(J_{m}(\mathbb{G}_{m}))
.

Finally, then, to verify inequalities (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), it suffices to show the following:

\lim_{q\to\infty}q^{-(m+1)(\mu+1)}\#M_{m}\left(C,X,L\right)\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}\right)\leq 1

(2.3)

and

\lim_{q\to\infty}q^{-2(m+1)(\mu+1)}\#M_{1,m}\left(C,X,L\right)\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}\right)\leq 1.

(2.4)

3  Setup of the circle method

Let us recall from
[
14
]
the geometric interpretation of the circle method that will allow us to work more naturally in the setting of smooth projective curves with arbitrary genus.

Fix a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and a line bundle
L
on
C
of degree
e
. Throughout this paper, we assume that
e\geq 2g-1
so that
L
is always non-special. For any non-negative integer
r
and effective divisor
Z\subset C
, let us write
P_{re,C}
to denote
H^{0}\left(C,L^{\otimes r}\right)
,
P_{re,Z}
to denote
H^{0}\left(Z,L^{\otimes r}|_{Z}\right)
, and
P_{re-Z,C}
to denote
H^{0}\left(C,L^{\otimes r}(-Z)\right)
. Moreover, if
m\geq 0
is an integer, let
P_{re,C,m}
denote
P_{re,C}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
,
P_{re,Z,m}
denote
P_{re,C}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
, and
P_{re-Z,C,m}
denote
P_{re-Z,C}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
.

The “circle” in the circle method is given by elements of
P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}\cong P_{de,C}^{\vee}\otimes\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
. To relate this to the perspective of harmonic analysis over function fields, we refer the reader to Section 6 of
[
14
]
, which uses a Serre duality argument to show that linear functionals on
P_{de,C}
are the same as a certain quotient of the completion of
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
with respect to the infinite place.

We say
\alpha\in P_{de,C}^{\vee}
factors through an effective divisor
Z\subset C
if
\alpha
is determined by its restriction to
Z
, i.e.
\alpha
lies in the image of the canonical map
P_{de,Z}^{\vee}\to P_{de,C}^{\vee}
. We denote this by
\alpha\sim Z
. For general
m\geq 0
, we say
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
factors through
Z
if the restriction of
\alpha
to
P_{de,C}^{\vee}
factors through
Z
, i.e. if
\alpha\bmod t
factors through
Z
. Again, we write
\alpha\sim Z
. We also define
\deg\alpha
to be the smallest integer
b
for which there exists an effective divisor
Z
of degree
b
and
\alpha\sim Z
.

The analogue of Dirichlet’s approximation theorem in the geometric setting is given by Lemma 9 of
[
14
]
:

Lemma 12
.

Any
\alpha\in P_{de,C}^{\vee}
factors through an effective divisor of degree at most
de/2+1
.

Let us write
\alpha\sim_{\min}Z
if
\alpha
factors minimally through an effective divisor
Z
.

Lemma 10 of
[
14
]
implies the following:

Lemma 13
.

There is at most one effective divisor
Z
such that
\alpha\sim_{\min}Z
and
\deg Z\leq e-2g+1
.

Note that we can view the
form
F
whose zero locus is the hypersurface
X
as a function

F\colon H^{0}\left(C,L\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}\to H^{0}\left(C,L^{\otimes d}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}

given by the cup product, and the composition with
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
is simply

H^{0}\left(C,L\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}\to H^{0}\left(C,L^{\otimes d}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}.

Moreover, if
\alpha=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}t+\cdots+\alpha_{m}t^{m}

and
y_{0}+y_{1}t+\cdots+y_{m}t^{m}\in H^{0}\left(C,L^{\otimes d}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}
,
then we define

\alpha(y)\coloneqq\alpha_{0}\left(y_{0}\right)+\left(\alpha_{0}\left(y_{1}\right)+\alpha_{1}\left(y_{0}\right)\right)t+\cdots+\left(\alpha_{0}\left(y_{m}\right)+\cdots+\alpha_{m}\left(y_{0}\right)\right)t^{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}.

Finally, let us fix
\psi_{m}\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]/t^{m+1}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
to be the non-trivial
additive character given by

a_{0}+\cdots+a_{m}t^{m}\mapsto\prod_{i=0}^{m}\psi\left(a_{i}\right),

where
\psi
is a non-trivial additive character on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

3.1
Setup for
J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))

In the classical circle method, we express our counting question in terms of an integral of exponential sums, parametrized by the circle. Using the language of the geometric interpretation, for a given
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
, we define the associated exponential sum as follows.

Let

S(\alpha)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,m}^{n+1}\\
\vec{x}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi_{m}\left(\alpha\left(F(\vec{x})\right)\right).

(3.1)

The condition “
\vec{x}
globally generates” is shorthand for the condition “
\vec{x}\bmod t
globally generates
L
.”

Orthogonality of characters then gives

\sum_{\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}}\psi_{m}\left(\alpha\left(F(\vec{x})\right)\right)\\
=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }F(\vec{x})\neq 0,\\
\#P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}&\text{else,}\end{cases}

which, by switching sums, implies

\sum_{\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}}S(\alpha)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,m}^{n+1}\\
\vec{x}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}}\psi_{m}\left(\alpha\left(F(\vec{x})\right)\right)=\#P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}\#M_{m}\left(C,X,L\right)(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

Rearranging and applying Riemann-Roch, we obtain

\#M_{m}\left(C,X,L\right)(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q^{-\left(m+1\right)\left(de-g+1\right)}\sum_{\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}}S(\alpha),

which, according to our goal (
2.3
), implies that it suffices to verify

q^{-(m+1)(n+1)(e+1-g)}\sum_{\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}}S(\alpha)\to 1.

(3.2)

Let us refine this goal slightly. In the classical circle method, the circle is divided into “major” and “minor” arcs reflecting the expected size of the exponential sums. In particular, we will show the following.

Proposition 14
.

Let
m\geq 1
. Then,

q^{-(m+1)(n+1)(e+1-g)}\sum_{\deg Z\leq e-2g+1}\sum_{\alpha\sim_{\text{min}}Z}S(\alpha)=q^{-m(n+1)(e+1-g)}\sum_{\alpha\in P_{de,C,m-1}^{\vee}}S(\alpha).

Proposition 15
.

Let
m\geq 1
and suppose that
n,d,e
and
g
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
2
. Then,

q^{-(m+1)(n+1)(e+1-g)}\sum_{\deg\alpha>e-2g+1}S(\alpha)\to 0\quad\text{as }q\to\infty.

Motivated by this decomposition above, we say
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
is
major
if
\deg\alpha\leq e-2g+1
and
minor
otherwise. We will prove Propositions
14
and Propositions
15
in Sections
4.1
and
6.1
, respectively. Assuming the validity of the results, we can verify (
2.3
) and hence complete the proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We will proceed by induction on
m
. The case
m=0
holds by
[
5
]
for
g=0
and by
[
14
]
for
g\geq 1
under even weaker assumptions on
n
and
e
. So suppose
m\geq 1
and that (
3.2
) holds for
m-1
. We then have

\displaystyle q^{-(m+1)(n+1)(e+1-g)}\sum_{\alpha\in P^{\vee}_{de,C,m}}S(\alpha)

\displaystyle=q^{-(m+1)(n+1)(e+1-g)}\left(\sum_{\deg Z\leq e-2g+1}\sum_{\alpha\sim_{\text{min}}Z}S(\alpha)+\sum_{\deg\alpha>e-2g+1}S(\alpha)\right),

where we used Lemma
13
to show that any major
\alpha
factors through a unique effective divisor. Proposition
15
shows that the contribution from
\deg\alpha>e-2g+1
tends to
0
as
q\to\infty
and by Proposition
14
we have

\displaystyle q^{-(m+1)(n+1)(e+1-g)}\sum_{\deg Z\leq e-2g+1}\sum_{\alpha\sim_{\text{min}}Z}S(\alpha)

\displaystyle=q^{-m(n+1)(e+1-g)}\sum_{\alpha\in P_{de,C,m-1}^{\vee}}S(\alpha)\to 1

as
q\to\infty
by the induction hypothesis.
∎

3.2
Setup for
J_{1}(J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L)))

The setup for
J_{1}(J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L)))
is completely analogous to that of
J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))
.
For given
\alpha,\beta\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
, we define

S\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}_{0},\vec{x}_{1}\in P_{e,C,m}^{n+1}\\
\vec{x}_{0}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi_{m}\left(\alpha\left(F\left(\vec{x}_{0}\right)+\beta\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{x}_{0}\right)\right)\right).

(3.3)

Here, the condition “
\vec{x}
globally generates” is again shorthand for the condition “
\vec{x}\bmod t
globally generates
L
.”

By orthogonality of characters, we have

\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}}\psi_{m}\left(\alpha\left(F\left(\vec{x}_{0}\right)+\beta\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{x}_{0}\right)\right)\right)=\begin{cases}\left(\#P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}\right)^{2}&\text{if }F\left(\vec{x}_{0}\right)=\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{x_{0}}\right)=0,\\
0&\text{else,}\end{cases}

which, by switching sums, implies

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}}S\left(\alpha,\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}_{0},\vec{x}_{1}\in P_{e,C,m}^{n+1}\\
\vec{x}_{0}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\begin{cases}\left(\#P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}\right)^{2}&\text{if }F\left(\vec{x}_{0}\right)=\vec{x}_{1}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{x}_{0}\right)=0,\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\left(\#P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}\right)^{2}\#M_{1,m}\left(C,X,L\right)(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

To justify the second step, observe that if

if and only if

.

In particular, by Lemma

\#M_{1,m}\left(C,X,L\right)(\mathbb{F}_{q})=q^{-2\left(m+1\right)\left(de-g+1\right)}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}}S\left(\alpha,\beta\right).

According to our goal (

(3.4)

as

. More precisely, we will show the following.

Let

\displaystyle\sum_{\deg Z\leq e-2g+1}\sum_{\deg W\leq e-2g+1}\sum_{\alpha\sim_{\min}Z}\sum_{\beta\sim_{\min}W}S\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=q^{2(n+1)(e+1-g)}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in P_{de,C,m-1}^{\vee}}S(\alpha,\beta).

Let

and suppose that

and

is

is

. The proof of Propositions

and

Proof of Theorem
1
.

It suffices to show that (

) holds. We will proceed by an induction on

. Note that when

, then

) is the same as (
3.2
) with

. The assumptions of Theorem

We have

\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}}S(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{\deg W,\deg Z\leq e-2g+1}\sum_{\alpha\sim_{\min}Z}\sum_{\beta\sim_{\min}W}S(\alpha,\beta)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}\\

4.1
Major arcs for
J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))

Let us first deal with the proof of Proposition
16
.
Suppose
\alpha\sim_{\min}Z
for
\deg Z\leq e-2g+1
and write
\alpha=\alpha_{0}+t\alpha_{1}
with
\alpha_{0}\in P_{de,C}^{\vee}
and
\alpha_{1}\in P_{de,C,m-1}^{\vee}
. For
\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,m}^{n+1}
, let us write
\vec{x}=\vec{y}+t^{m}\vec{z}
with
\vec{y}\in P_{e,C,m-1}^{n+1}
and
\vec{z}\in P_{e,C}^{n+1}

Then, using Taylor expansion we obtain

\displaystyle S(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,m}^{n+1}\\
\vec{x}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi_{m}\left(\alpha\left(F(\vec{x})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{y}\in P_{e,C,m-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\vec{z}\in P_{e,C}^{n+1}}\psi_{m}\left(\alpha\left(F\left(\vec{y}\right)+t^{m}\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{y}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{y}\in P_{e,C,m-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi_{m-1}\left(\alpha_{1}\left(F\left(\vec{y}\right)\right)\right)\psi_{m}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(F\left(\vec{y}\right)\right)\right)\sum_{\vec{z}\in P_{e,C}^{n+1}}\psi_{m}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(t^{m}\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{y}\right)\right)\right).

Let

T\left(\alpha_{0}\right)=\sum_{\vec{z}\in P_{e,C}^{n+1}}\psi_{m}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(t^{m}\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{y}\right)\right)\right)\\
=\sum_{\vec{z}\in P_{e,C}^{n+1}}\psi\left(\alpha_{0}\left(\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{y}\bmod t\right)\right)\right).

Lemma 18
.

Let
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
and suppose that
\alpha\sim_{\min}Z
with
\deg Z\leq e-2g+1
. Then, with the notation above, we have

T\left(\alpha_{0}\right)=\begin{cases}q^{\left(n+1\right)\left(e-0-g+1\right)}q^{\left(n+1\right)0}=q^{\left(n+1\right)\left(e-g+1\right)}&\text{if }Z=0,\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
\alpha_{0}\sim Z
and
\deg Z\leq e-2g+1
, using the exact sequence
0\to H^{0}\left(C,L\left(-Z\right)\right)\to H^{0}\left(C,L\right)\to H^{0}\left(Z,L|_{Z}\right)\to 0
, and applying Riemann-Roch, we may write

T\left(\alpha_{0}\right)=q^{\left(n+1\right)\left(e-\deg Z-g+1\right)}\sum_{\vec{z}\in P_{e,Z}^{n+1}}\psi\left(\alpha_{0}\left(\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{y}\bmod t|_{Z}\right)\right)\right),

viewing
\alpha_{0}
as an element of
P_{de,Z}^{\vee}
. By orthogonality of characters we have

T(\alpha_{0})=\begin{cases}q^{(n+1)(e-g+1)}&\text{if }\alpha_{0}(\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F(\vec{y}\bmod t_{|Z}))=0\text{ for all }\vec{z}\in P_{e,Z}^{n+1},\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Moreover, since
\alpha_{0}\sim_{\min}Z
, it follows that
\alpha_{0}\left(\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{y}\bmod t|_{Z}\right)\right)=0

identically in
\vec{z}
if and only if
\alpha_{0}\left(z_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_{i}}\left(\vec{y}\bmod t|_{Z}\right)\right)=0

for
0\leq i\leq n
, which holds if and only if
z_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_{i}}\left(\vec{y}\bmod t|_{Z}\right)=0

for
0\leq i\leq n
. If this holds for every
\vec{z},
then
\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_{i}}\left(\vec{y}\bmod t|_{Z}\right)=0

for
0\leq i\leq n
.

Since
F
is smooth by assumption, the radical of the ideal
\left\langle\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_{0}}\left(\vec{y}\bmod t\right),\ldots,\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_{n}}\left(\vec{y}\bmod t\right)\right\rangle
is given by the ideal
\left\langle y_{0}\bmod t,\ldots,y_{n}\bmod t\right\rangle
,
from which it follows that
y_{0}^{M}\bmod t,\ldots,y_{n}^{M}\bmod t

are all in
\left\langle\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_{0}}\left(\vec{y}\bmod t\right),\ldots,\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_{n}}\left(\vec{y}\bmod t\right)\right\rangle

for sufficiently large
M
. Since
\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_{i}}\left(\vec{y}\bmod t|_{Z}\right)=0

for
0\leq i\leq n
, it follows that
y_{0}^{M}\bmod t|_{Z},\ldots,y_{n}^{M}\bmod t|_{Z}=0
.
This means
MV\left(y_{0}\bmod t,\ldots,y_{n}\bmod t\right)-Z
is
effective. However, since
y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}
globally
generate modulo
t
, we must have
V\left(y_{0}\bmod t,\ldots,y_{n}\bmod t\right)=0
. Hence,
\alpha_{0}\left(\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F\left(\vec{y}\bmod t|_{Z}\right)\right)=0
identically
if and only if
Z=0
.

∎

Having Lemma

at our disposal, it is now straightforward to prove Proposition

Proof of Proposition

.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\deg Z\leq e-2g+1}\sum_{\alpha\sim_{\min}Z}S\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{1}\in P_{de,C,m-1}^{\vee}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{y}\in P_{e,C,m-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi_{m-1}\left(\alpha_{1}\left(F\left(\vec{y}\right)\right)\right)\sum_{\deg Z\leq e-2g+1}\sum_{\alpha_{0}\sim_{\min}Z}\psi_{m}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(F\left(\vec{y}\right)\right)\right)T\left(\alpha_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{1}\in P_{de,C,m-1}^{\vee}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{y}\in P_{e,C,m-1}^{n+1}\\

where the penultimate identity follows from Lemma

∎

Remark 19
.

We will now carry out the proof of Proposition

.

Suppose that

and

for

and write

.

\displaystyle S\left(\alpha,\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}_{0},\vec{x}_{1}\in P_{e,C,m}^{n+1}\\

5  Minor arcs

In this section we establish upper bounds for the exponential sums
S(\alpha)
and
S(\alpha,\beta)
defined in (
3.1
) and (
3.3
) via Weyl differencing, which will then be used to estimate the contribution from the minor arcs.

As before, let
F\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
be a homogeneous degree
d
form defining the smooth hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
, given by

F(\vec{x})=\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}=0}^{n}a_{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}}x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{d}}

where the
a_{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
are symmetric in the indices. A key role in our estimates is played by the multilinear forms

\Psi_{j}\left(\vec{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\vec{x}^{(d-1)}\right)=d!\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d-1}=0}^{n}a_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d-1},j}x_{j_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots x_{j_{d-1}}^{(d-1)},

(5.1)

for
j=0,\dots,n
, where multiplication should be interpreted via the cup product. Given
\vec{y}\in P_{e,C,\ell}^{n+1}
and a function
G\colon P_{e,C,\ell}^{n+1}\to P_{de,C,m}
for some
\ell
, we define the differencing operator
D_{\vec{y}}
via its action on
G
by

D_{\vec{y}}(G)(\vec{x})=G(\vec{x}+\vec{y})-G(\vec{x}).

For
k\geq 2
, we define recursively
D_{\vec{y}_{1},\dots,\vec{y}_{k}}(G)=D_{\vec{y}_{k}}(D_{\vec{y}_{1},\dots,\vec{y}_{k-1}}(G))
. The key property of the differencing operator is that if
G
is a polynomial, then it lowers the degree of
G
. In the special case of a homogeneous form
F
as above, we have for
G(\vec{x})=\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F(\vec{x})
the identity

D_{\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-2)}}(G)(\vec{y}^{(d-1)})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}z_{i}\Psi_{i}(\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-1)})

(5.2)

where
\Psi_{0},\dots,\Psi_{n}
are the multilinear forms associated to
F
defined in (
5.1
).

5.1
Weyl differencing for
J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L))

We begin with the treatment of
S(\alpha)
. If we write
m^{\prime}=\lceil(m+1)/2\rceil
, then assuming
m\geq 1
any
\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,m}^{n+1}
can be written uniquely as
\vec{x}=\vec{y}+t^{m^{\prime}}\vec{z}
for some
\vec{y}\in P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1}
and
\vec{z}\in P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}^{n+1}
, so that we have
\psi_{m}(\alpha F(\vec{x}))=\psi(\alpha(F(\vec{y})+t^{m^{\prime}}\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F(\vec{y})))
. Therefore, we have

S(\alpha)=\sum_{\vec{z}\in P^{n+1}_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}}T(\alpha),

where

T(\alpha)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{y}\in P^{n+1}_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}\\
\vec{y}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi(\alpha((F(\vec{y})+t^{m^{\prime}}\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F(\vec{y}))),

where global generation of
\vec{y}
means global generation after reducing modulo
t
. We now continue with the usual process of Weyl differencing. For
\vec{z}\in P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}^{\vee}
, let
G^{\prime}(\vec{y})=F(\vec{y})+t^{m^{\prime}}\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F(\vec{y})
and
G(\vec{y})=\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F(\vec{y})
. Squaring gives

|T(\alpha)|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{y}_{1}\in P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}_{1}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{y}_{2}\in P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}_{2}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi(\alpha(G^{\prime}(\vec{y}_{1})-G^{\prime}(\vec{y}_{2}))).

After making the change of variables
\vec{y}^{(1)}=\vec{y}_{2}
and
\vec{x}=\vec{y}_{1}-\vec{y}_{2}
, then the condition that
\vec{y}_{1}
and
\vec{y}_{2}
globally generate transforms into
\vec{y}^{(1)}
and
\vec{y}^{(1)}+\vec{x}
globally generate. We obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}|T(\alpha)|^{2}&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{y}^{(1)}\in P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}^{(1)}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}^{(1)}+\vec{x}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi(\alpha(D_{\vec{y}^{(1)}}(G^{\prime})(\vec{x})))\\
&\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{y}^{(1)}\in P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}^{(1)}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}^{(1)}+\vec{x}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi(\alpha(D_{\vec{y}^{(1)}}(G^{\prime})(\vec{x})))\right|\end{split}

(5.3)

Inductively, an application of Cauchy–Schwarz yields

\displaystyle|T(\alpha)|^{2^{d-2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left((\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1})^{2^{d-3}-(d-2)}\sum_{\vec{y}^{(1)}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}^{(d-3)}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}^{(1)}+\cdots+\vec{y}^{(d-3)}+\vec{x}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi(\alpha(D_{\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-3)}}(G^{\prime})(\vec{x})))\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\#P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1})^{2^{d-2}-(d-1)}\sum_{\vec{y}^{(1)}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}^{(d-3)}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1}\\
\vec{y}^{(1)}+\cdots+\vec{y}^{(d-3)}+\vec{x}\text{ glob. gen.}\end{subarray}}\psi(\alpha(D_{\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-3)}}(G^{\prime})(\vec{x})))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\#P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1})^{2^{d-2}-(d-1)}\sum_{\vec{y}^{(1)}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}^{(d-2)}}\sum_{\vec{y}^{(d-1)}}\psi(\alpha(D_{\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-2)}}(G^{\prime})(\vec{y}^{(d-1)}))),

where for each

\vec{y}^{(i)}\in P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1}
is restricted to those such that

globally generates and the last inequality follows from a change of variables as in (

).

D_{\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-2)}}(G^{\prime})(\vec{y}^{(d-1)})=D_{\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-2)}}(F)(\vec{y}^{(d-1)})+t^{m^{\prime}}D_{\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-2)}}(G)(\vec{y}^{(d-1)})
. Applying Cauchy–Schwarz
d-2
times gives

\left|S(\alpha)\right|^{2^{d-2}}\leq(\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}})^{2^{d-2}-1}\sum_{\vec{z}\in P^{n+1}_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}}\left|T(\alpha)\right|^{2^{d-2}}.

(5.4)

As
D_{\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-2)}}(F)(\vec{y}^{(d-1)})
is clearly independent of
\vec{z}
and recalling (
5.2
), interchanging the order of summation and taking absolute values yields

\displaystyle\left|S(\alpha)\right|^{2^{d-2}}

\displaystyle\leq(\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}^{n+1})^{2^{d-2}-1}(\#P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1})^{2^{d-2}-(d-1)}

\displaystyle\phantom{aa}\times\sum_{\vec{y}^{(1)}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}^{(d-1)}}\left|\sum_{\vec{z}\in P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}^{n+1}}\psi(\alpha(t^{m^{\prime}}\sum_{i=0}^{n}z_{i}\Psi_{i}(\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-1)})))\right|

\displaystyle\leq(\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}})^{2^{d-2}}(\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1})^{2^{d-2}-(d-1)}N^{(m^{\prime}-1,m+1-m^{\prime})}(\alpha),

where for
k_{1}\geq k_{2}-1
we have defined

N^{(k_{1},k_{2})}(\alpha)=\#\left\{(\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-1)})\in P_{e,C,k_{1}}^{(n+1)(d-1)}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha(\Psi_{i}(\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-1)})y)\equiv 0\bmod s^{k_{2}}\\
\text{for all }0\leq i\leq n\text{ and }y\in P_{e,C,k_{2}-1}\end{array}\right\}.

Note that here we dropped the condition about globally generating, which makes the counting function only larger. For any
k_{1}\geq k_{2}-1
, we have
N^{(k_{1},k_{2})}(\alpha)=\#P^{(n+1)(d-1)}_{e,C,k_{1}-k_{2}}N^{(k_{2}-1,k_{2})}(\alpha)
with the convention that
P_{e,C,-1}=\{0\}
, so that for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq-1}
we shall write
N^{(k)}(\alpha)=N^{(k,k+1)}(\alpha)
from now on. As
\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}\#P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}=\#P_{e,C,m}
and
\#P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{-1}\#P_{e,C,2m^{\prime}-m-2}=\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}^{-1}
, we have the proved the following result.

Lemma 20
.

Let
\alpha\in P^{\vee}_{de,C,m}
and suppose that
m\geq 1
. Then,

|S(\alpha)|^{2^{d-2}}\leq(\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m})^{2^{d-2}}(\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}^{n+1})^{-(d-1)}N^{(m-m^{\prime})}(\alpha).

Remark 21
.

It might seem more natural to apply Weyl differencing to
S(\alpha)
directly rather than to the sum
T(\alpha)
. The reason for not doing so is that we are working with globally generating sections in the definition of
S(\alpha)
. This would then cause problems when evaluating the resulting linear exponential sum using orthogonality of characters.

5.2
Weyl differencing for
J_{1}(J_{m}(\Mor(C,X,L)))

Next, we begin with the analysis of
S(\alpha,\beta)
.
For any
\vec{z}\in P_{e,C,m}^{n+1}
, the combination of Cauchy–Schwarz and Weyl differencing arguments from the previous subsection can also be applied to the sum

S^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{\vec{y}\in P_{e,C,m}^{n+1}}\psi_{m}(\alpha F(\vec{y})+\beta\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F(\vec{y})),

where the summation is restricted to elements that globally generate
\bmod\penalty\ t
. Indeed, the final outcome of Weyl differencing only depends on the top degree part of
\alpha F(\vec{y})+\beta\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F(\vec{y})
as a polynomial in
\vec{y}
, which after summing trivially over
\vec{z}
thus leads to the estimate

|S(\alpha,\beta)|^{2^{d-2}}\leq(\#P^{2(n+1)}_{e,C,m})^{2^{d-2}}(\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}^{n+1})^{-(d-1)}N^{(m-m^{\prime})}(\alpha).

(5.5)

Alternatively, we can first apply Weyl differencing to the sum over
\vec{y}
only
d-2
times and then bring the sum over
\vec{z}
inside. Beginning with
d-2
applications of Cauchy–Schwarz yields

|S(\alpha,\beta)|^{2^{d-2}}\leq\#(P^{n+1}_{e,C,m})^{2^{d-2}-1}\sum_{\vec{z}\in P^{n+1}_{e,C,m}}|S^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)|^{2^{d-2}}.

(5.6)

Arguing as in the lines after (
5.4
), we obtain

|S^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta)|^{2^{d-2}}\leq\#(P^{n+1}_{e,C,m})^{2^{d-2}-(d-1)}\sum_{\vec{y}^{(1)}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}^{(d-1)}}\psi_{m}(D_{\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-2)}}(\alpha F+\beta\vec{z}\cdot\nabla F))(\vec{y}^{(d-1)}).

We can now insert this estimate into (
5.6
) and take absolute values to get

\displaystyle\begin{split}|S(\alpha,\beta)|^{2^{d-2}}&\leq\#(P^{n+1}_{e,C,m})^{2^{d-1}-d}\sum_{\vec{y}^{(1)}}\cdots\sum_{\vec{y}^{(d-1)}}\sum_{\vec{z}}\psi_{m}(\beta\sum_{i=0}^{n}z_{i}\Psi_{i}(\vec{y}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{y}^{(d-1)}))\\
&=\#(P^{n+1}_{e,C,m})^{2^{d-1}-d+1}N^{(m)}(\beta),\end{split}

(5.7)

where we dropped the condition on the
y^{(i)}
about global generation of a suitable translate. Combining (
5.5
) with (
5.7
) and observing that
\#P_{e,C,m}=\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}\#P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}
, we arrive at the following result.

Lemma 22
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
and suppose that
m\geq 1
. Then,

|S(\alpha,\beta)|^{2^{d-2}}\leq(\#P^{2(n+1)}_{e,C,m})^{2^{d-2}}(\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}})^{-(d-1)}\min\left\{N^{(m-m^{\prime})}(\alpha),(\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1})^{-(d-1)}N^{(m)}(\beta)\right\}.

Remark 23
.

An alternative approach to estimate
S(\alpha,\beta)
is to treat
F(\vec{x})=\vec{y}\cdot\nabla F(\vec{x})=0
as a complete intersection of two forms and then use the standard approach in the circle method. Ignoring that globally generating sections cause difficulties here, that is to say, via Weyl differencing one can relate
S(\alpha,\beta)
to certain multilinear forms associated to the pencil generated by
F(\vec{x})
and
\vec{y}\cdot\nabla F(\vec{x})
. However, our approach is more efficient and exploits the special shape of the equations with the result that we save one iteration of differencing. In the case
m=0
, this is explained in more detail in the paragraph after (5.3) in
[
5
]
.

5.3
Shrinking lemma

Our next goal is to establish a suitable form of Davenport’s shrinking lemma. This will be achieved by reducing to the case
m=0
. We recall the following (slightly more general) result from Proposition 22 of
[
14
]
:

Lemma 24
.

Let
L_{0},\ldots,L_{M}
be linear forms in
n+1
variables
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}

whose coefficients belong to
P_{(e-s)(\ell-1)+e(d-\ell-1),C}
. Also, let

\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{M^{\prime}}\in P_{de,C}^{\vee}
and
I_{0},\ldots,I_{n}

be subsets of
\{0,\ldots,M\}\times\{0,\ldots,M^{\prime}\}
. Then, for
\vec{x}\in P_{e,C}^{n+1}
,
we can think of
\sum_{(i,j)\in I_{k}}\alpha_{i}\left(L_{j}\left(\vec{x}\right)(-)\right)

as a linear functional
P_{e+(\ell-1)s,C}^{\vee}
; for
\vec{x}\in P_{e-s,C}^{n+1}
,
we can think of
\sum_{(i,j)\in I_{k}}\alpha_{i}\left(L_{j}\left(\vec{x}\right)(-)\right)

as a linear functional
P_{e+\ell s,C}^{\vee}
.

Write

K_{\ell-1}\left(\alpha\right)=\#\left\{\vec{x}\in P_{e,C}^{n+1}:\sum_{(i,j)\in I_{k}}\alpha_{i}\left(L_{j}\left(\vec{x}\right)(-)\right)=0\in P_{e+(\ell-1)s,C}^{\vee}\text{ for all }k\right\}

and

K^{\prime}_{\ell}\left(\alpha\right)=\#\left\{\vec{x}\in P_{e-s,C}^{n+1}:\sum_{(i,j)\in I_{k}}\alpha_{i}\left(L_{j}\left(\vec{x}\right)(-)\right)=0\in P_{e+\ell s,C}^{\vee}\text{ for all }k\right\}.

Then,

\frac{K_{\ell-1}(\alpha)}{K^{\prime}_{\ell}(\alpha)}\leq\begin{cases}q^{\left(n+1\right)s}&g=0\text{ and }s\geq 0,\\
q^{\left(n+1\right)s}&g=1\text{ and }s\geq 2,\\
q^{\left(n+1\right)s}&g\geq 2,s\geq\max\left(2g-1,2\right),\text{ and }\ell\geq 2,\\
q^{\left(n+1\right)\left(s+\left(g+1\right)/2\right)}&g\geq 2,s\geq\max\left(2g-1,2\right),\text{ and }\ell=1.\end{cases}

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as that of Proposition 22 in
[
14
]
, which in turn is a geometric reinterpretation of the classical shrinking lemma.

The idea is to construct a certain vector bundle
E
over
C
(which should be understood as the geometric analogue of a lattice and choice of norm) such that the cardinalitites of
H^{0}\left(C,E\right)
and
H^{0}\left(C,E\left(-s\infty\right)\right)
are precisely
K_{\ell-1}(\alpha)
and
K_{\ell}^{\prime}(\alpha)
, respectively.

In Section 7 of
[
14
]
, the construction of the vector bundle
E
works identically if “
\alpha\gamma
” in the definition of the transition matrix
\Lambda
is replaced with the linear combination
\sum_{(i,j)\in I_{k}}\alpha_{i}\left(L_{j}\left(\vec{x}\right)\right)
.

Then, we can bound
\log_{q}\left(K_{\ell-1}(\alpha)/K_{\ell}^{\prime}(\alpha)\right)
by bounding
h^{0}\left(C,E\right)-h^{0}\left(C,E\left(-s\infty\right)\right)
. This, in turn, can be achieved by using Riemann-Roch for vector bundles and the slopes of the Harder-Narasimhan filtration of
E
(the latter should be viewed as successive minima of the corresponding lattice). In particular, the rest of the argument in Section 7 (and Proposition 22) of
[
14
]
goes through identically.

∎

It is now straightforward but notationally somewhat cumbersome to generalize Lemma
24
to
P_{e,C,k}
when
k\geq 1
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
L_{0},\ldots,L_{n}
be linear forms in
n+1
variables
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}

whose coefficients belong to
P_{(e-s)(\ell-1)+e(d-\ell-1),C,k}
. Also, let

\alpha\in P_{de,C,k}^{\vee}
. Then, for
\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,k}^{n+1}
,
we can think of
\alpha\left(L_{j}\left(\vec{x}\right)(-)\right)

as a linear functional
P_{e+(\ell-1)s,C,k}^{\vee}
; for
\vec{x}\in P_{e-s,C,k}^{n+1}
,
we can think of
\alpha\left(L_{j}\left(\vec{x}\right)(-)\right)

as a linear functional
P_{e+\ell s,C,k}^{\vee}
.

Write

K^{(k)}_{\ell-1}\left(\alpha\right)=\#\left\{\vec{x}\in P_{e,C,k}^{n+1}:\alpha L_{j}\left(\vec{x}\right)=0\in P_{e+(\ell-1)s,C,k}^{\vee}\text{ for all }j\right\}

and

K^{(k)}_{\ell}\left(\alpha\right)=\#\left\{\vec{x}\in P_{e-s,C,k}^{n+1}:\alpha L_{j}\left(\vec{x}\right)=0\in P_{e+\ell s,C,k}^{\vee}\text{ for all }j\right\}.

Then,

\frac{K^{(k)}_{\ell-1}(\alpha)}{K^{(k)}_{\ell}(\alpha)}\leq\begin{cases}q^{(k+1)\left(n+1\right)s}&g=0\text{ and }s\geq 0,\\
q^{(k+1)\left(n+1\right)s}&g=1\text{ and }s\geq 2,\\
q^{(k+1)\left(n+1\right)s}&g\geq 2,s\geq\max\left(2g-1,2\right),\text{ and }\ell\geq 2,\\
q^{(k+1)\left(n+1\right)\left(s+\left(g+1\right)/2\right)}&g\geq 2,s\geq\max\left(2g-1,2\right),\text{ and }\ell=1.\end{cases}

Proof.

We can write

L_{i}\left(\vec{x}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}t^{j}L_{i,j}\left(\vec{x}\right)

for
0\leq i\leq n
, where each
L_{i,j}
is a linear form in the
n+1

variables
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
with coefficients in
P_{(e-s)(\ell-1)+e(d-\ell-1),C}
.

Write
\vec{x}=\vec{x}_{0}+t\vec{x}_{1}+\cdots+t^{k}\vec{x}_{k}

with each
\vec{x}_{i}\in P_{e,C}^{n+1}
for all
i
or each
\vec{x}_{i}\in P_{e-s,C}^{n+1}

for all
i
. Let

\widetilde{L}_{i,k}\left(\vec{x}_{0},\ldots,\vec{x}_{k}\right)=\sum_{j+\ell=k}L_{i,j}\left(\vec{x}_{\ell}\right),

so that each
\widetilde{L}_{i,k}
is a linear form in
\left(k+1\right)n

variables. We can then write

L_{i}\left(\vec{x}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{k}t^{k}\widetilde{L}_{i,k}\left(\vec{x}_{0},\ldots,\vec{x}_{k}\right).

Let us also write
\alpha=\alpha_{0}+t\alpha_{1}+\cdots+t^{k}\alpha_{k}
,
so that we have

\alpha L_{i}\left(\vec{x}\right)=\left(\alpha_{0}\widetilde{L}_{i,0}\right)+t\left(\alpha_{0}\widetilde{L}_{i,1}+\alpha_{1}\widetilde{L}_{i,0}\right)+\cdots+t^{k}\left(\alpha_{0}\widetilde{L}_{i,k}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}\widetilde{L}_{i,0}\right).

and it follows for each
i
that
\alpha L_{i}\left(\vec{x}\right)=0\in P_{e+(\ell-1)s,C,k}^{\vee}

if and only if
\alpha_{0}\tilde{L}_{i,j}+\cdots+\alpha_{j}\tilde{L}_{i,0}=0
for
j=0,\dots,k
. Thus we can re-write
K_{\ell-1}\left(\alpha\right)
and

K_{\ell}\left(\alpha\right)
as counting problems for which
k=0
in

(k+1)(n+1)
variables. The result then follows from Lemma
24
.
∎

Let
k\geq 1
and
s\geq 0
be integers. Given
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
, where
m\geq k
let us define

N^{(k)}_{s}(\alpha)\coloneqq\#\left\{\left(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(d-1)}\right)\in\left(P_{e-s,C,k}^{n+1}\right)^{d-1}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha(\Psi_{j}(\vec{x}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{x}^{(d-1)})x)\equiv 0\bmod t^{k+1}\\
\text{ for all }x\in P_{e+(d-1)s,C,k}\text{ and }0\leq j\leq n\end{array}\right\}.

We thus have
N^{(k)}(\alpha)=N^{(k)}_{0}(\alpha)
in the notation of Sections
5.1
and
5.2
. The previous result applied
d-1
times immediately gives the following result.

Lemma 26
.

Let
\alpha\in P_{de,C,k}^{\vee}
. For
g=0
, we have

\frac{N^{(k)}(\alpha)}{N^{(k)}_{s}\left(\alpha\right)}\leq q^{\left(k+1\right)\left(d-1\right)\left(n+1\right)s}

for all
s\geq 0
.

For
g\geq 1
and
s\geq\max\left(2g-1,2\right)
, we have

\frac{N^{(k)}(\alpha)}{N^{(k)}_{s}\left(\alpha\right)}\leq\begin{cases}q^{\left(k+1\right)\left(d-1\right)\left(n+1\right)s}&\text{if }g=1,\\
q^{\left(k+1\right)\left(n+1\right)\left(\left(d-1\right)s+\left(g+1\right)/2\right)}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

We now aim at choosing the parameter
s
in such a way that if
(\vec{x}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{x}^{(d-1)})
is counted by
N^{(k)}_{s}(\alpha)
, then this forces
\Psi_{j}(\vec{x}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{x}^{(d-1)})=0
for
0\leq j\leq n
.

Lemma 27
.

Let
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
and
0\leq k\leq m
. Suppose
\alpha
factors through
Z
minimally and
(\vec{x}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{x}^{(d-1)})\in(P_{e-s,C,k}^{n+1})^{d-1}
is given. If

s>\max\left(\frac{\deg Z-e+2g-2}{d-1},e-\frac{\deg Z}{d-1}\right)

and
\alpha\left(\Psi_{j}\left(x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(d-1)}\right)x\right)\equiv 0\bmod t^{k+1}
holds for all
x\in P_{e+(d-1)s,C,k}
and
0\leq j\leq n
, then in fact

\Psi_{j}\left(x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(d-1)}\right)\equiv 0\bmod t^{k}
for all
0\leq j\leq n
.

Proof.

Write
\Psi_{j}\left(x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(d-1)}\right)=\rho_{0}+\cdots+t^{k}\rho_{k}

and
\alpha=\alpha_{0}+\cdots+t^{k}\alpha_{k}
, and note that then
\alpha\rho\equiv 0\bmod t^{k+1}

is equivalent to

\alpha_{0}\rho_{0}=\alpha_{0}\rho_{1}+\alpha_{1}\rho_{0}=\cdots=\alpha_{0}\rho_{k}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}\rho_{0}=0.

By Lemma 23 of
[
14
]
, we have
\alpha_{0}\rho_{0}=0

implies
\rho_{0}=0
under the assumptions on
s
. It then follows that

\alpha_{0}\rho_{1}=0
, and so we may apply the same reasoning to
conclude that
\rho_{1}=0
, and so forth, until we obtain
\rho_{k}=0
.
This implies
\rho=0
, as desired.
∎

Next, we bound the dimensions of the jet schemes of the variety cut out by the
\Psi_{i}
.

Lemma 28
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{(n+1)(d-1)}
be the variety defined by
\Psi_{0}=\cdots=\Psi_{n}=0
and
J_{k}(V)
its
k
th jet scheme. Then,

\dim J_{k}(V)\leq\left(k+1\right)\left(d-1\right)\left(n+1\right)-\left(n+1\right)\left(\left\lfloor\frac{k}{d-1}\right\rfloor+1\right).

Proof.

We identify
x^{(i)}\in\mathbb{A}^{(k+1)(n+1)}
for
1\leq i\leq d-1
with
x_{0}^{(i)}+tx_{1}^{(i)}+\cdots+t^{k}x_{k}^{(i)}
, where
x_{j}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}
. Since
V\subset\mathbb{A}^{(d-1)(n+1)}
, we have
J_{k}(V)\subset J_{k}(\mathbb{A}^{(d-1)(n+1)})=\mathbb{A}^{(k+1)(n+1)(d-1)}
. Under this description, we have

J_{k}(V)=\{({x}^{(1)},\dots,{x}^{(d-1)})\in\mathbb{A}^{(k+1)(n+1)(d-1)}\colon\Psi_{i}({x}^{(1)},\dots,{x}^{(d-1)})\equiv 0\bmod t^{k+1}\text{ for }0\leq i\leq n\}.

Let now

\Delta=\{({x}^{(1)},\dots,{x}^{(d-1)})\in\mathbb{A}^{(k+1)(n+1)(d-1)}\colon{x}^{(1)}=\cdots={x}^{(d-1)}\}

be the diagonal, which has dimension
(k+1)(n+1)
. Suppose that
({x},\dots,{x})\in\Delta\cap J_{k}(V)
. We must have
\nabla F({x})\equiv{0}\bmod t^{k+1}
. If
{x}\neq 0
, we can write
{x}=t^{l}{x}^{\prime}
with
{x}^{\prime}\neq{0}\bmod t
. Then as
F
is non-singular, this implies
l(d-1)\geq k+1
, as otherwise the reduction of
{x}^{\prime}
modulo
s
would produce a singular point of
F
. This is equivalent to
{x}_{0}=\cdots={x}_{\left\lfloor\frac{k}{d-1}\right\rfloor}=0
and hence

\dim\Delta\cap J_{k}(V)\leq(n+1)\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{d-1}\right\rfloor\right).

Therefore, observing that the intersection
\Delta\cap J_{k}(V)
is non-empty because
\Delta
and
J_{k}(V)
are cut out by homogeneous polynomials, we obtain

\displaystyle\dim J_{k}(V)

\displaystyle\leq\dim(\Delta\cap J_{k}(V))+(k+1)(n+1)(d-1)-\dim\Delta

\displaystyle\leq(n+1)\left(k-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{d-1}\right\rfloor\right)+(k+1)(n+1)(d-2),

from which the result follows.
∎

Lemma 29
.

Let
V
be a variety in
\mathbb{A}_{K(C)}^{N}
, and let
D
be a divisor on
C
. Then, the number of
K(C)
-points of
V
such that each coordinate satisfies the norm conditions defined by
D
is bounded above by
Cq^{\dim Vh^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}(D))},
where
C
depends only on
N
and the degree of
V
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Lemma 3.6 in
[
3
]
.

We may assume
V
is geometrically irreducible. The case
\dim V=0
is trivial. Suppose
V
has positive dimension, then, and note that we can find some index
1\leq i\leq N
such that
V
intersects the hyperplane
H_{\alpha}\colon x_{i}=\alpha
properly for every
\alpha
.

Let
M_{V}(D)
denote the quantity we want to bound. Then, we have

M_{V}(D)\leq\sum_{\alpha\in K(C)\colon\alpha\in H^{0}\left(C,\mathcal{O}(D)\right)}M_{V\cap H_{\alpha}}(D).

By the induction hypothesis, each
M_{V\cap H_{\alpha}}(D)=O\left(q^{(\dim V-1)h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}(D))}\right)
, so the result follows.
∎

Combining Corollaries
28
and
29
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 30
.

Let
k\geq 0
and
0\leq s\leq e
be integers. We have

\#\left\{\vec{x}\in(P_{e-s,C,k}^{n+1})^{(d-1)}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}\Psi_{j}(\vec{x}^{(1)},\dots,\vec{x}^{(d-1)})=0\\

5.4
Exponential sum estimates

We now have everything at hand to present our main estimates for the sums
S(\alpha)
and
S(\alpha,\beta)
defined in (
3.1
) and (
3.3
) respectively.
Before we state our main results, we need to introduce some notation. Given
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
such that
\alpha
factors through
Z
minimally, we define

s_{\alpha}=\begin{cases}\max\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\deg Z-e+2g-2}{d-1}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor e-\frac{\deg Z}{d-1}\right\rfloor\right)+1&\text{if }g=0,\\
\max\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\deg Z-e+2g-2}{d-1}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor e-\frac{\deg Z}{d-1}\right\rfloor,2g-2,1\right)+1&\text{if }g\geq 1.\end{cases}

(5.8)

In addition, for a non-negative integer
g
we set

f(g)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }g\in\{0,1\},\\
(g+1)/2&\text{if }g\geq 2.\end{cases}

(5.9)

Proposition 31
.

Let
m\geq 1
and
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
. Suppose that
\alpha\sim_{\text{min}}Z
. For
s=s_{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{|S(\alpha)|}{\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m}}\right)^{2^{d-2}}\ll(\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}^{n+1})^{-(d-1)}

\displaystyle q^{(m-m^{\prime}+1)(n+1)((d-1)s+f(g))}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle q^{h^{0}(C,L(-s\infty))(n+1)((m-m^{\prime}+1)(d-1)-(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1))}

In particular, if
e-s\geq 2g-1
, then

\left(\frac{|S(\alpha)|}{\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m}}\right)^{2^{d-2}}\ll q^{(m-m^{\prime}+1)(n+1)f(g)-(n+1)(e-s-g+1)(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1)},

where in both cases the implied constant is independent of
q
.

Proof.

With our choice of
s
, it is clear that Lemmas
26
and
27
are applicable. In particular, successively applying Lemmas
20
and
26
with
k=m-m^{\prime}
gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\frac{|S(\alpha)|}{\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m}}\right)^{2^{d-2}}&\leq(\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}^{n+1})^{-(d-1)}q^{(m-m^{\prime}+1)(n+1)((d-1)s+f(g))}N^{(m-m^{\prime})}_{s}(\alpha).\end{split}

(5.10)

By Lemma
27
and Corollary
30
we have

N_{s}(\alpha)\ll q^{h^{0}(C,L(-s\infty))(n+1)((m-m^{\prime}+1)(d-1)-(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1))},

(5.11)

which once combined with (
5.10
) verifies the first assertion of Proposition
31
. For the second, note that if
e-s\geq 2g-1
, then
h^{0}(C,L(-s\infty))=e-s-g+1
by Riemann–Roch. Similarly,
h^{0}(C,L)=e-g+1
, yielding
\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}=q^{(e-g+1)(m-m^{\prime}+1)}
. After canceling some of the terms, (
5.10
) together with (
5.11
) easily give the second assertion of Proposition
31
.
∎

Proposition 32
.

Let
m\geq 1
and
\alpha,\beta\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
. Suppose that
\alpha\sim_{\text{min}}Z
and
\beta\sim_{\text{min}}W
. Then

\left(\frac{|S(\alpha,\beta)|}{\#P_{e,C,m}^{2(n+1)}}\right)^{2^{d-2}}\ll q^{(n+1)R(\alpha,\beta)},

where
R(\alpha,\beta)
is defined to be the minimum of the quantities

(m-m^{\prime}+1)((d-1)(s_{\alpha}-(e-g+1))+f(g))+h^{0}(C,L(-s_{\alpha}\infty))\left(\left(m-m^{\prime}+1\right)\left(d-1\right)-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\right\rfloor+1\right)\right)

and

(m+1)((d-1)(s_{\beta}-(e-g+1))+f(g))+h^{0}(C,L(-s_{\beta}\infty))\left(\left(m+1\right)\left(d-1\right)-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{m}{d-1}\right\rfloor+1\right)\right).

In particular, if
\max(e-s_{\alpha},e-s_{\beta})\geq 2g-1
, then

\left(\frac{|S(\alpha,\beta)|}{\#P_{e,C,m}^{2(n+1)}}\right)^{2^{d-2}}\ll q^{(n+1)((m+1)f(g)-M(\alpha,\beta))},

where
M(\alpha,\beta)
is defined to be

\begin{array}[]{ll}m^{\prime}f(g)+\left(e-s_{\alpha}-g+1\right)\left\lceil\frac{m-m^{\prime}+1}{d-1}\right\rceil&\text{if }e-s_{\alpha}\geq 2g-1>e-s_{\beta},\\
\left(e-s_{\beta}-g+1\right)\left\lceil\frac{m+1}{d-1}\right\rceil&\text{if }e-s_{\beta}\geq 2g-1>e-s_{\alpha},\\
\max\left\{m^{\prime}f(g)+\left(e-s_{\alpha}-g+1\right)\left\lceil\frac{m-m^{\prime}+1}{d-1}\right\rceil,\left(e-s_{\beta}-g+1\right)\left\lceil\frac{m+1}{d-1}\right\rceil\right\}&\text{if }e-s_{\alpha},e-s_{\beta}\geq 2g-1.\end{array}

Proof.

By Lemmas
22
and
26
we have

\left(\frac{|S(\alpha,\beta)|}{\#P^{2(n+1)}_{e,C,m}}\right)^{2^{d-2}}\leq(\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}^{n+1})^{-(d-1)}\min\left(N^{(m-m^{\prime})}(\alpha),(\#P_{e,C,m^{\prime}-1}^{n+1})^{-(d-1)}N^{(m)}(\beta)\right).

(5.12)

Successively applying Lemma
26
, Lemma
27
and Corollary
30
gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}N^{(m-m^{\prime})}(\alpha)&\leq q^{(m-m^{\prime}+1)(n+1)((d-1)s_{\alpha}+f(g))}N_{s_{\alpha}}^{(m-m^{\prime})}(\alpha)\\
&\ll q^{(m-m^{\prime}+1)(n+1)((d-1)s_{\alpha}+f(g))+h^{0}(C,L(-s_{\alpha}\infty))(n+1)((m-m^{\prime}+1)(d-1)-(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1))}.\end{split}

(5.13)

The same reasoning applied to
N^{(m)}(\beta)
yields

N^{(m)}(\beta)\ll q^{(m+1)(n+1)((d-1)s_{\beta}+f(g))+h^{0}(C,L(-s_{\beta}\infty))(n+1)((m+1)(d-1)-(\lfloor\frac{m}{d-1}\rfloor+1))}.

(5.14)

Upon observing that
\#P_{e,C,m}=q^{(m+1)(e-g+1)}
and
\#P_{e,C,m-m^{\prime}}=q^{(m-m^{\prime}+1)(e-g+1)}
, the first assertion now follows after rearranging some of the terms in the exponents. For the second, suppose that
e-s_{\alpha}\geq 2g-1
. Then by Riemann–Roch, we have
h^{0}(C,L(-s_{\alpha}\infty))=e-s_{\alpha}-g+1
, so that (
5.12
) and (
5.13
) give

\displaystyle\left(\frac{|S(\alpha,\beta)|}{\#P^{2(n+1)}_{e,C,m}}\right)^{2^{d-2}}

\displaystyle\ll q^{(n+1)((m-m^{\prime}+1)(-(e-g+1)(d-1)+(d-1)s_{\alpha}+f(g)+(e-s_{\alpha}-g+1)(d-1))-(e-s_{\alpha}-g+1)(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1))}

\displaystyle=q^{(n+1)((m-m^{\prime}+1)f(g)-(e-s_{\alpha}-g+1)(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1))}.

Similarly, when
e-s_{\beta}\geq 2g-1
, then by Riemann–Roch we have
h^{0}(C,L(-s_{\beta}\infty))=e-s_{\beta}-g+1
. Thus, by (
5.12
) and (
5.14
) we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{|S(\alpha,\beta)|}{\#P^{2(n+1)}_{e,C,m}}\right)^{2^{d-2}}

\displaystyle\ll q^{(n+1)((m+1)(-(e-g+1)(d-1)+(d-1)s_{\beta}+f(g)+(e-s_{\beta}-g+1)(d-1))-(e-s_{\beta}-g+1)(\lfloor\frac{m}{d-1}\rfloor+1))}

\displaystyle=q^{(n+1)((m+1)f(g)-(e-s_{\beta}-g+1)(\lfloor\frac{m}{d-1}\rfloor+1))}.

The second claim now readily follows by comparing these last two estimates with the definition of
M(\alpha,\beta)
.
∎

6.1
Canonical singularities

Given
n,d,g,e\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
2
, our goal is to show that

\#P_{e,C,m}^{-(n+1)}\sum_{\deg Z\geq e-2g+2}\sum_{\alpha\sim_{\text{min}}Z}|S(\alpha)|\to 0\quad\text{as }q\to\infty.

(6.1)

By Lemma
12
any
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}
factors through some effective divisor
Z\subset C
of degree at most
de/2+1
. As there are
O_{d,e,g}(1)
possibilities for integers
D
satisfying
e-2g+2\leq D\leq de/2+1
, it suffices to consider the contribution from
\deg Z=D
for a fixed
D
in (
6.1
). We shall denote this contribution by
N(D)
.

Let

s=\begin{cases}\max\left(\left\lfloor\frac{D-e+2g-2}{d-1}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor e-\frac{D}{d-1}\right\rfloor\right)+1&\text{if }g=0,\\
\max\left(\left\lfloor\frac{D-e+2g-2}{d-1}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor e-\frac{D}{d-1}\right\rfloor,2g-2,1\right)+1&\text{if }g\geq 1\end{cases}

(6.2)

and observe that the first term in the maximum dominates the second if
de/2-g+1<D
and the second dominates the first if
D\leq de/2-g+1
. Suppose that
e-s\geq 2g-1
. Under this assumption, Proposition
31
hands us the estimate

\frac{|S(\alpha)|}{\#P^{n+1}_{e,C,m}}\ll q^{(n+1)((m-m^{\prime}+1)f(g)-(e-s-g+1)(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1))/2^{d-2}}

(6.3)

whenever
\alpha
factors through
Z
minimally with
\deg Z=D
. The number of
\alpha\in P_{de,C,m}
that factor through a given
Z
is
\#P^{\vee}_{de,C,m-1}
times the number of
\alpha_{0}\in P_{de,C}^{\vee}
that factor through
Z
, so that in total there are
O(q^{D+m(de+1-g)})
available
\alpha
. In addition,
C
is a smooth curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and hence has
O(q^{D})
effective divisors of degree
D
. In particular, combining these observations with (
6.3
) shows that

N(D)\ll q^{2D+m(de+1-g)+(n+1)((m-m^{\prime}+1)f(g)-(e-s-g+1)(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1))/2^{d-2}}.

In particular, if we define

A\coloneqq 2^{d-2}\frac{2D+m(de+1-g)}{(e-s-g+1)(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1)-(m-m^{\prime}+1)f(g)},

(6.4)

and
e-s\geq 2g-1
holds, then to establish Proposition
15
it suffices to show that
n+1>A
.

6.1.1
d\geq 3
,
g=0
and
1\leq e\leq d-2

Recall that in this case we assume
n+1>2^{d-2}d(2d+1)
when
e=1
and
n+1>2^{d-1}(d-1)(de+2)
when
e\geq 2
. Note also
e-s=\lceil D/(d-1)\rceil-1\geq 2g-1
, so that

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=2^{d-2}\frac{2D+m(de+1)}{\left\lceil\frac{D}{d-1}\right\rceil(\left\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\right\rfloor+1)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-2}\frac{2D+m(de+1)}{\max(1,D/(d-1))\max(1,m/(2(d-1)))},

since
\lfloor(m-m^{\prime})/(d-1)\rfloor+1=\lceil(m-m^{\prime}+1)/(d-1)\rceil\geq\lfloor(m+1)/2\rfloor/(d-1)\geq m/(2(d-1))
and
\lceil D/(d-1)\rceil\geq\max(1,D/(d-1)
. For any
D
, the last fraction is increasing in
m
when
1\leq m\leq 2(d-1)
and decreasing when
m>2(d-1)
. Therefore, it is maximal at
m=2(d-1)
and hence

A\leq 2^{d-1}\frac{D+(d-1)(de+1)}{\max(1,D/(d-1))}.

(6.5)

When
e=1
, then we assume that
d\geq 4
, implying
D\leq d/2+1\leq d-1
. In this case we thus have

A\leq 2^{d-2}(d+2+2(d-1)(d+1))=2^{d-2}d(2d+1),

which is sufficient. When
e\geq 2
, then (
6.5
) is maximal at
D=d-1
, so that

A\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)(de+2),

which is again satisfactory.

6.1.2
d\geq 3

Recall that if
d\geq 3
and
g\geq 1
, we assume that
e>e_{0}
, where

e_{0}\coloneqq(g-1)(2^{d-1}(d-1)^{2}(2d-1)+2)+(d-1)(g+(d-1)f(g))(2^{d-1}(d-1)(d^{2}-d+1)+1),

(6.6)

while we assume
e\geq d-2
when
g=0
. We begin by showing that if
d\geq 3
, then in fact

s=\max\left(\left\lfloor\frac{D-e+2g-2}{d-1}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor e-\frac{D}{d-1}\right\rfloor\right)+1

also holds for
g\geq 1
. First, suppose
D\leq de/2-g+1
. Then

\left\lfloor e-\frac{D}{d-1}\right\rfloor\geq e-1-\frac{de/2-g+1}{d-1}=\frac{e(d/2-1)-d+g}{d-1}.

Our assumption on
e
implies that
e\geq 2^{d-1}(d-1)^{2}(1+f(g))
and a straightforward computation reveals that

\frac{2^{d-1}(d-1)^{2}(1+f(g))(d/2-1)-d+g}{d-1}\geq\max\{2g-2,1\}.

Second, if
de/2-g+1<D
, then

\left\lfloor\frac{D-e+2g-2}{d-1}\right\rfloor\geq\frac{e(d/2-1)+g-1}{d-1}\geq\frac{e(d/2-1)+g-d}{d-1}\geq\max\{2g-2,1\}

by what we have shown in the first case and hence completes the verification of the claim.

Next, we show that

e-s\geq 2g-1.

(6.7)

If
D\leq de/2-g+1
, then we have

e-s=\left\lceil\frac{D}{d-1}\right\rceil-1\geq\frac{e-2g+2}{d-1}-1.

(6.8)

If
g=0
, then
e\geq 1
and the right hand side is at least
3/(d-1)-1\geq-1
, which is sufficient. If
g\geq 1
, then (
6.6
) implies that
(e-2g+2)/(d-1)\geq 2^{d-1}(d-1)(1+f(g))\geq 2g-1
, which is also satisfactory. When
D>de/2-g+1
, then

e-s=e-\left\lfloor\frac{D-e+2g-2}{d-1}\right\rfloor-1\geq e-\frac{e(d/2-1)+2g-1}{d-1}-1=\frac{ed/2+2g-d}{d-1}.

When
g=0
, this is at least
-1
because
d\geq 2
. When
g\geq 1
, then
2g\geq 3-2g
holds, which together with
d\geq 3>2
implies

e-s\geq\frac{e-2g+2}{d-1}-1,

which is precisely the inequality (
6.8
). We have thus already shown that this implies
e-s\geq 2g-1
.

In particular, to establish (
6.1
) it is enough to show
n+1>A
, where
A
is defined in (
6.4
).

Case I:

D\leq de/2-g+1
. Note that we are always in this case when
g=0
. Here,
e-s+1=\lceil D/(d-1)\rceil
and hence

\displaystyle\begin{split}A&\leq 2^{d-2}(d-1)\frac{2D+m(de+1-g)}{(D-g(d-1))(\lfloor(m-m^{\prime})/(d-1)\rfloor+1)-(m-m^{\prime}+1)(d-1)f(g)}.\end{split}

(6.9)

Let us first treat the case
m\leq 2(d-1)
. This implies
(m-m^{\prime})/(d-1)=(\lfloor(m+1)/2\rfloor-1)/(d-1)<1
and hence
\lfloor(m-m^{\prime})/(d-1)\rfloor=0

m\leq 2(d-1)
, the inequality (
6.9

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)\frac{D+(d-1)(de+1-g)}{D-g(d-1)-(m-m^{\prime}+1)(d-1)f(g)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)\frac{D+(d-1)(de+1-g)}{D-g(d-1)-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}.

(6.10)

Note that the last expression is decreasing in

and hence is maximal at
D=e-2g+2
, which implies that

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)\frac{e-2g+2+(d-1)(de+1-g)}{e-2g+2-g(d-1)-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}.

(6.11)

When

, then the right hand side is strictly increasing in

, whence

A<2^{d-1}(d-1)(d^{2}-d+1).

(6.12)

Thus,

when

. If

, then the right hand side of (

) is strictly decreasing in

and one can check that when evaluated at

defined in (
6.6
)

2^{d-1}(d-1)(d^{2}-d+1)+1
. Of course, here we have chosen
e_{0}
to ensure this identity. As we assume that

, which is satisfactory.

Next, let us treat the opposite case

. As
\lfloor(m-m^{\prime})/(d-1)\rfloor+1\geq(m-m^{\prime}+1)/(d-1)
, from (

) we get

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-2}(d-1)^{2}\frac{2D+m(de+1-g)}{(m-m^{\prime}+1)(D-g(d-1)-f(g)(d-1)^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)^{2}\frac{2D+m(de+1-g)}{m(D-g(d-1)-f(g)(d-1)^{2})},

where we used that
m-m^{\prime}+1=\lfloor(m+1)/2\rfloor\geq m/2
. The last expression is strictly decreasing in

. In particular,
m>2(d-1)
implies that

A<2^{d-1}(d-1)\frac{D+(d-1)(de+1-g)}{D-g(d-1)-f(g)(d-1)^{2}}.

This is precisely the term in (
6.10
) and we have already seen that this implies

when we treated the case

.

Case II:

de/2-g+1<D\leq de/2+1
. In this case we have
e-s+1=e-\lfloor(D-e+2g-2)/(d-1)\rfloor
, so that

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=2^{d-2}\frac{2D+m(de+1-g)}{(e-\lfloor\frac{D-e+2g-2}{d-1}\rfloor-g)(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1)-(m-m^{\prime}+1)f(g)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-2}\frac{de+2+m(de+1-g)}{(e-\frac{de/2-e+2g-1}{d-1}-g)(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1)-(m-m^{\prime}+1)f(g)}

\displaystyle=2^{d-2}(d-1)\frac{de+2+m(de+1-g)}{(de/2-2g+1-g(d-1))(\lfloor\frac{m-m^{\prime}}{d-1}\rfloor+1)-(m-m^{\prime}+1)f(g)(d-1)}.

(6.13)

Let us first assume that
m\leq 2(d-1)
. Then

h(e)

. As we assume that

Next, we turn to the case

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-2}(d-1)^{2}\frac{de+2+m(de+1-g)}{(m-m^{\prime}+1)(de/2-2g+1-g(d-1)-f(g)(d-1)^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)^{2}\frac{de+2+m(de+1-g)}{m(de/2-2g+1-g(d-1)-f(g)(d-1)^{2})},

(6.14)

where we used that

. The term in (

, and as we assume that

As we have already shown that

6.1.3

Recall that we only treat

when

, in which case we assume

(6.15)

Note that when

simplifies to

6.2
Terminal singularities

Given
n,d,g,e\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
1
, our goal is to show that

\#P_{e,C,m}^{-2(n+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in P_{de,C,m}^{\vee}\\
\max(\deg\alpha,\deg\beta)\geq e-2g+2\end{subarray}}|S(\alpha,\beta)|\to 0\quad\text{ as }q\to\infty.

(6.17)

Let us write
\deg(\alpha)=D_{\alpha}
and
\deg(\beta)=D_{\beta}
. By Lemma
12
we have
D_{\alpha},D_{\beta}\leq de/2+1
. Recall the definitions of
s_{\alpha}
and
s_{\beta}
in Proposition
32
] and assume that
\max(e-s_{\alpha},e-s_{\beta})\geq 2g-1
. Then, by Proposition
32

we have

\frac{|S(\alpha,\beta)|}{\#P_{e,C,m}^{2(n+1)}}\ll q^{(n+1)((m+1)f(g)-M(\alpha,\beta))/2^{d-2}}.

There are at most
q^{D_{\alpha}+m\left(de-g+1\right)}
linear
functionals on
P_{de,C,m}
that factor through any specific degree

D_{\alpha}
effective divisor and
O\left(q^{D_{\alpha}}\right)
such effective divisors
(likewise for
D_{\beta}
). As there are
O_{d,e}(1)
possibilities for
D_{\alpha}
and
D_{\beta}
, to verify (

) it suffices to show

which, if we set

A^{\prime}=2^{d-1}\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{M(\alpha,\beta)-(m+1)f(g)},

is equivalent to

n+1>A^{\prime}.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1
, we will show that we always have
\max(e-s_{\alpha},e-s_{\beta})\geq 2g-1
and verify the inequality

. This will therefore complete our proof of Theorem
1
.

6.2.1
d\geq 4
,
g=0
and
1\leq e\leq d-1

Note that for

, we have

\displaystyle M(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\max\left(\left\lceil\frac{D_{\alpha}}{d-1}\right\rceil\left\lceil\frac{m-m^{\prime}+1}{d-1}\right\rceil,\left\lceil\frac{D_{\beta}}{d-1}\right\rceil\left\lceil\frac{m+1}{d-1}\right\rceil\right)

\displaystyle\geq\max\left(\left\lceil\frac{D_{\alpha}}{d-1}\right\rceil\max\left(1,\frac{m}{2(d-1)}\right),\left\lceil\frac{D_{\beta}}{d-1}\right\rceil\max\left(1,\frac{m+1}{d-1}\right)\right).

In particular, if
\max(D_{\alpha},D_{\beta})\leq d-1
, then we obtain

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+m(de+1)}{\max(1,(m+1)/(d-1))}.

Let us first deal with the case
e=1
and
d\geq 4
. For
D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}\geq d+1=de+1
, the right-hand side of the above inequality is maximized at
m=d-2
, where it is equal to

2^{d-1}(D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+(d-2)(d+1)).

Note that
\max(D_{\alpha},D_{\beta})\leq d/2+1,
so
D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}\geq d+1
precisely when
D_{\alpha}=D_{\beta}=(d+1)/2
for
d
odd and when
(D_{\alpha},D_{\beta})\in{(d/2+1,d/2),(d/2,d/2+1),(d/2+1,d/2+1)}
for
d
even.

In either case, for
D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}\geq d+1
, it follows that

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}(d+2+(d-2)(d+1)).

For
D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}<d+1
, the right-hand side is instead an increasing function of
m
, so

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)(d+1).

So, for
e=1
and
d\geq 4
, we have

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}d^{2}\leq 2^{d-2}d(2d+1)<n+1

by our assumption on
n
in this case.

Note that

\min(de/2+1,d-1)=\begin{cases}d/2+1&\text{if $d\geq 4$ and $e=1$}\\
d-1&\text{else.}\end{cases}

We shall therefore assume
e>1
from now on. Then, if
\max(D_{\alpha},D_{\beta})\leq d-1
, we again have

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+m(de+1)}{\max(1,(m+1)/(d-1))}\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)(de+1)\eqqcolon B_{0},

since
D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}\leq 2d-2<de+1
.

Now, suppose
\max(D_{\alpha},D_{\beta})\geq d
. This gives

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+m(de+1)}{\max\left(D_{\alpha}\max(1,m/(2(d-1))),D_{\beta}\max(1,(m+1)/(d-1))\right)}.

When
1\leq m\leq d-2
, then we get

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)\frac{2\max(D_{\alpha},D_{\beta})+(d-2)(de+1)}{\max(D_{\alpha},D_{\beta})}\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)(2+(d-2)(de+1)/d)\eqqcolon B_{1}.

(6.18)

While if
d-1\leq m\leq 2d-2
, then we obtain

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+m(de+1)}{\max\left(D_{\alpha},D_{\beta}\frac{m+1}{d-1}\right)}.

The right-hand side is increasing in
D_{\beta}
when
D_{\beta}\leq(d-1)D_{\alpha}/(m+1)
and decreasing otherwise. In particular, it is maximal at
D_{\beta}=(d-1)D_{\alpha}/(m+1)
. Note that this implies
D_{\alpha}\geq D_{\beta}
and hence
D_{\alpha}\geq d
. Thus,

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)\frac{D_{\alpha}(1+(d-1)/(m+1))+m(de+1)}{D_{\alpha}}\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)((1+(d-1)/(m+1))+m(de+1)/d).

The last quantity is increasing in
m
and hence maximal at
m=2(d-1)
. We obtain

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)(1+(d-1)/(2d-1)+2(d-1)(de+1)/d)\eqcolon B_{2},

(6.19)

say. Next, we consider the case
2d-1\leq m
. Here we have

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}(d-1)^{2}\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+m(de+1)}{\max\left(D_{\alpha}m/2,D_{\beta}(m+1)\right)}.

The quantity is maximal when
D_{\beta}=D_{\alpha}\frac{m}{2(m+1)}
and
D_{\alpha}\geq D_{\beta}
, so that again
D_{\alpha}\geq d
must hold. We get

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d}(d-1)^{2}\frac{D_{\alpha}(1+m/(2(m+1)))+m(de+1)}{D_{\alpha}m}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d}(d-1)^{2}\frac{d(1+m/(2(m+1)))+m(de+1)}{dm}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d}(d-1)^{2}\frac{d(1+(2d-1)/(4d))+(2d-1)(de+1)}{d(2d-1)},

where we used that the expression in the second line is decreasing in
m
. A straightforward computation reveals that we always have

2^{d}(d-1)^{2}\frac{d(1+(2d-1)/(4d)+(2d-1)(de+1)}{d(2d-1)}<B_{2}

and also
B_{0},B_{1}<B_{2}
. It thus transpires from (
6.18
) and (
6.19
) that we have

A^{\prime}\leq B_{2}=2^{d-1}(d-1)\left(1+\frac{d-1}{2d-1}+\frac{2(d-1)(de+1)}{d}\right)<n+1,

by our assumption on
n
. This concludes our treatment of this case.

\displaystyle=2^{d-1}\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{M(\alpha,\beta)-(m+1)f(g)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\alpha}/2+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(\left\lceil\frac{D_{\alpha}}{d-1}\right\rceil-g\right)\left\lceil\frac{m-m^{\prime}+1}{d-1}\right\rceil-\left(m-m^{\prime}+1\right)f(g)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3D_{\alpha}/2+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(D_{\alpha}-(d-1)g\right)\left\lceil\frac{m-m^{\prime}+1}{d-1}\right\rceil-(d-1)\left(m-m^{\prime}+1\right)f(g)}.

If
m\leq 2\left(d-1\right)
, we obtain

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3D_{\alpha}/2+2(d-1)\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(D_{\alpha}-(d-1)g\right)-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}.

(6.20)

The right hand side is decreasing in
D_{\alpha}
, so it is at most

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\coloneqq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3\left(e-2g+2\right)/2+2(d-1)\left(de-g+1\right)}{e-2g+2-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}

\displaystyle=2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\left(\frac{3}{2}+2d(d-1)+\frac{(d-1)\left(4d^{2}g+\left(4d^{3}-8d^{2}+7d-3\right)f(g)+4d(g-2)-g+4\right)/2}{e-2g+2-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}\right).

Let
T=-(d-1)\left(4d^{2}g+\left(4d^{3}-8d^{2}+7d-3\right)f(g)+4d(g-2)-g+4\right)/2,

so that

E=2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\left(\frac{3}{2}+2d(d-1)-\frac{T}{e-2g+2-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}\right).

For
g=0
, we have
T=(d-1)(4d-2)>0
for
d\geq 3
. So
A^{\prime}\leq E<2^{d-2}\left(d-1\right)\left(4d^{2}-4d+3\right)
.

For
g\geq 1
, we have
T<0
for
d\geq 3
, and note that

2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\left(\frac{3}{2}+2d(d-1)-\frac{T}{e_{0}-2g+2-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}\right)<2^{d-2}\left(d-1\right)\left(4d^{2}-4d+3\right)+1

for
e_{0}=2^{d-2}\left(d-1\right)^{2}\left(4d^{2}g+\left(4d^{3}-8d^{2}+7d-3\right)f(g)+4d(g-2)-g+4\right)-1+(d+1)g+(d-1)^{2}f(g).

If
m>2(d-1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3D_{\alpha}/2+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(D_{\alpha}-(d-1)g\right)\left\lceil\frac{m-m^{\prime}+1}{d-1}\right\rceil-(d-1)\left(m-m^{\prime}+1\right)f(g)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)^{2}\frac{3D_{\alpha}/2+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(m-m^{\prime}+1\right)\left(D_{\alpha}-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)^{2}\frac{3D_{\alpha}/2+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(m/2\right)\left(D_{\alpha}-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)\right)}

\displaystyle=2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3D_{\alpha}\cdot\frac{d-1}{m}+2(d-1)\left(de-g+1\right)}{D_{\alpha}-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3D_{\alpha}/2+2(d-1)\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(D_{\alpha}-(d-1)g\right)-(d-1)^{2}f(g)},

but this is the same as (
6.20
).

III.2.2:
D_{\alpha}/2\leq D_{\beta}

Next, consider the case where
D_{\alpha}/2\leq D_{\beta}
, where
we use the
\beta
estimate instead. Then, we have

M(\alpha,\beta)\geq\left(e-s_{\beta}-g+1\right)\left\lceil\frac{m+1}{d-1}\right\rceil=\left(\left\lceil\frac{D_{\beta}}{d-1}\right\rceil-g\right)\left\lceil\frac{m+1}{d-1}\right\rceil.

By the assumptions on
e
, we have
e-s_{\beta}\geq 2g-1
.
so

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2^{d-1}\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{M(\alpha,\beta)-(m+1)f(g)}3D_{\beta}\frac{d-1}{m+1}+\frac{m}{m+1}\left(de-g+1\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\frac{3D_{\beta}+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(\left\lceil\frac{D_{\beta}}{d-1}\right\rceil-g\right)\left\lceil\frac{m+1}{d-1}\right\rceil-\left(m+1\right)f(g)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3D_{\beta}+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(D_{\beta}-(d-1)g\right)\left\lceil\frac{m+1}{d-1}\right\rceil-(d-1)\left(m+1\right)f(g)}.

If
m\leq d-2
, we obtain

A^{\prime}\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3D_{\beta}+(d-2)\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(D_{\beta}-(d-1)g\right)-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}.

(6.21)

Note that
D_{\beta}\geq D_{\alpha}/2\geq e/2-g+1
. The RHS is decreasing
in
D_{\beta}
, so it is at most

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\coloneqq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3\left(e/2-g+1\right)+(d-2)\left(de-g+1\right)}{e/2-g+1-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}

\displaystyle=2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3\left(e-2g+2\right)+2(d-2)\left(de-g+1\right)}{e-2g+2-2(d-1)g-2(d-1)^{2}f(g)}

\displaystyle=2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\left(3+2d(d-2)+\frac{2\left(\left(2d^{2}-4d+3\right)\left(d-1\right)^{2}f(g)+2d^{3}g-4d^{2}g-2d^{2}+2dg+5d-g-2\right)}{e-2g+2-2(d-1)g-2(d-1)^{2}f(g)}\right).

Let
T=-2\left(\left(2d^{2}-4d+3\right)\left(d-1\right)^{2}f(g)+2d^{3}g-4d^{2}g-2d^{2}+2dg+5d-g-2\right),

so that

E=2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\left(3+2d(d-2)-\frac{T}{e-2g+2-2(d-1)g-2(d-1)^{2}f(g)}\right).

For
g=0
, we have
T=-2\left(-2d^{2}+5d-2\right)>0
for
d\geq 3
.
So
A^{\prime}\leq E<2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\left(2d^{2}-4d+3\right)
.

For
g\geq 1
, we have
T<0
for
d\geq 3
. Furthermore, for
e\geq e_{0}
,
we have
E<2^{d-2}\left(d-1\right)\left(4d^{2}-4d+3\right)+1
by
computer verification.

If
m>d-2
, we obtain

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3D_{\beta}+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(D_{\beta}-(d-1)g\right)\left\lceil\frac{m+1}{d-1}\right\rceil-(d-1)\left(m+1\right)f(g)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)^{2}\frac{3D_{\beta}+m\left(de-g+1\right)}{\left(m+1\right)\left(D_{\beta}-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)^{2}\frac{\frac{3D_{\beta}}{m+1}+\frac{m}{m+1}\left(de-g+1\right)}{D_{\beta}-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}.

For
g=0
and
d=3
, we have
3D_{\beta}\geq de-g+1
. Thus the right hand side
is decreasing as a function of
m
and

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)^{2}\frac{\frac{3D_{\beta}}{d-1}+\frac{d-2}{d-1}\left(de-g+1\right)}{D_{\beta}-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}

\displaystyle=2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{3D_{\beta}+\left(d-2\right)\left(de-g+1\right)}{D_{\alpha}-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)},

which is precisely (
6.21
).

For
g\geq 1
or
d\geq 4
, since
D_{\beta}\leq e-2g+1
, we have
3D_{\beta}<de-g+1
,
which means that

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle<2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{de-g+1}{D_{\beta}-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\frac{de-g+1}{\frac{e}{2}-g+1-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}

\displaystyle=2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\left(2d-\frac{T}{\frac{e}{2}-g+1-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}\right),

where
T=-1-2d\left(-1+f(g)\right)-2d^{3}f(g)+d^{2}\left(4f(g)-2g\right)+g.

For
g=0
and
d\geq 4
, we have
T>0
, so
A^{\prime}<2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\left(2d\right)<2^{d-2}\left(d-1\right)\left(4d^{2}-4d+3\right).

For
g=1
and
d\geq 3
, we have
T<0
. For
e\geq e_{0}
, we have

2^{d-1}\left(d-1\right)\left(2d-\frac{T}{\frac{e}{2}-g+1-(d-1)g-(d-1)^{2}f(g)}\right)\leq 2^{d-2}\left(d-1\right)\left(4d^{2}-4d+3\right)+1

by using a computer algebra system.

6.2.3
d=2
and
g\geq 1

Note that in this case
(\alpha,\beta)
belongs to the minor arcs precisely when
e-2g+2\leq\max(D_{\alpha},D_{\beta})\leq e+1
. Let
\gamma\in\{\alpha,\beta\}
and suppose that
e-2g+2\leq D_{\gamma}\leq e+1
. Then
s_{\gamma}
simplifies to

s_{\gamma}=\begin{cases}2g&\text{if $D_{\gamma}=e+1$},\\
2&\text{if $D_{\gamma}=e$ and $g=1$},\\
2g-1&\text{if $e-2g+2\leq D_{\gamma}\leq e$ and $g\geq 2$}.\end{cases}

(6.22)

Our assumption that
e>29g-5+14f(g)
readily implies
e-s_{\gamma}\geq 2g-1
in these cases, so that it suffices to show
A^{\prime}<n+1
.

I:
e+2-2g\leq D_{\beta}\leq e+1

Note that by (
6.22
) we have
M(\alpha,\beta)\geq(e-3g+1)(m+1)
, so that

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+m(2e-g+1)}{(m+1)(e-3g+1-f(g))}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{2(e+1)+m(2e-g+1)}{(m+1)(e-3g+1-f(g))}.

The last term is decreasing in
m
and hence maximal at
m=1
, yielding

A^{\prime}\leq\frac{4e+3-g}{e-3g+1-f(g)}.

This is decreasing in
e
and with a computer algebra system one can check that when evaluated at
e=e_{0}
is strictly smaller than 12, which is sufficient.

II:
2g\leq D_{\beta}\leq e+1-2g

Note that when
D_{\beta}<e+2-2g
, then
e-s_{\beta}+1=D_{\beta}
and we still have
e-s_{\beta}\geq 2g-1
, since
D_{\beta}\geq 2g
. As
D_{\beta}\leq e+1-2g
implies
D_{\alpha}\geq e+2-2g
, we have

M(\alpha,\beta)\geq\max(D_{\beta}(m+1),m^{\prime}f(g)+(e-3g+1)(m-m^{\prime}+1))\eqcolon B.

We obtain

A^{\prime}\leq 2\frac{D_{\alpha}+D_{\beta}+m(2e-g+1)}{B-(m+1)f(g)}.

One can check that the last term is decreasing in
D_{\beta}
if
B=D_{\beta}(m+1)
and increasing if
B=m^{\prime}f(g)+(e-2g+1)(m-m^{\prime}+1)
. Thus it is maximal when the two terms in the definition of
B
agree, in which case
D_{\alpha}\leq e+1
yields

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{e+1+(m^{\prime}f(g)+(e-3g+1)(m-m^{\prime}+1))/(m+1)+m(2e-g+1)}{(m-m^{\prime}+1)(e-3g+1-f(g))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{e-3g+1-f(g)}\left(\frac{e+1}{m-m^{\prime}+1}+\frac{f(g)}{m}+\frac{e-3g+1}{m+1}+2(2e-g+1)\right),

where we used that
m^{\prime}/((m+1)(m-m^{\prime}+1))\leq m
. The last term is decreasing in
m
and hence maximal at
m=1
. Thus

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(e+1+f(g))+e-3g+1+2(4e-2g+2)}{e-3g+1-f(g)}

\displaystyle=\frac{11e+7+12f(g)-7g}{e-3g+1-f(g)}.

One can check that when evaluated at
e=29g-5+14f(g)
this is strictly less than 12, which is sufficient.

III:
D_{\beta}<2g

In this case we have
M(\alpha,\beta)\geq m^{\prime}f(g)+(e-3g+1)(m-m^{\prime}+1)
. Using
m-m^{\prime}+1=\lfloor(m+1)/2\rfloor
and
D_{\beta}\leq 2g-1
, we get

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{2g-1+D_{\alpha}+m(2e-g+1)}{\lfloor\frac{m+1}{2}\rfloor(e-3g+1-f(g)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{2g+e+m(2e-g+1)}{\lfloor\frac{m+1}{2}\rfloor(e-3g+1-f(g))}.

The last expression is decreasing as
m
runs through even positive integers and increasing for odd values of
m
. Plugging in
m=2
and evaluating the limit as
m\to\infty
for odd
m
, we obtain

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{e-3g+1-f(g)}\max(4e-2g+2,5e+2)

\displaystyle=2\frac{5e+2}{e-3g+1-f(g)}.

Evaluating this expression at
e=29g-5+14f(g)
again shows that
A^{\prime}<12
, which is sufficient.
