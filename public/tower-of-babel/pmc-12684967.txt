Dynamic obstruction of right coronary ostium by a papillary fibroelastoma: a case report on alternative causes of chest pain and cardiac imaging assessment
Authors: Luis Manuel Garcia-Linacero, Eduardo Casas Rojo, Antonio Piris, Miren Martin-Garcia, Covadonga Fernandez-Golfin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPapillary fibroelastomas (PFEs) are one the most common primary cardiac tumours. Although they are benign, their location involves a high risk of adverse events, which have been reported mainly in the form of embolic events, but angina pectoris is another possible, less frequent manifestation.Case summaryWe present a case of a 72-year-old woman with chest pain symptoms, who was found to have a PFE. As part of pre-operatory study a computed tomography (CT) scan was performed which documented dynamic obstruction of the right coronary artery. Surgery was brought forwards and mass was resected with histology confirming PFE.DiscussionThe interest of our case lies into the documentation of the obstruction with the scan, highlighting the role and advancements of this diagnostic technique and the importance of a prompt assessment and management of these patients, since previous clinical evidence has shown that ischaemia due to PFEs could present with acute coronary syndromes or even sudden cardiac death.

Introduction

Papillary fibroelastoma (PFE) is one of the most common primary cardiac tumours. Although traditionally myxomas are considered the most frequent primary cardiac masses, some studies based on improving imaging techniques1 suggest that PFE may be most common. Papillary fibroelastomas have no sex predilection and are most frequently diagnosed in 7th to 8th decades of life, though they can be found at any age.2,3 These benign masses have an average size of 1 cm in reported series and are composed of fibroelastic tissue covered by endocardium.1,2 They are described as ‘sea anemone-like’ due to their pedunculated morphology with branching structures. They predominantly attach to heart valves, most commonly the aortic (35%–65% of cases, depending on the series), followed by mitral, tricuspid, and pulmonary valves (10%–55%, 6%–15%, and 1%–8%, respectively).2,3 Diagnosis relies on echocardiographic imaging, with transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) offering greater sensitivity and specificity2–4 than transthoracic (TTE) approach.

Although up to 30% of cases are asymptomatic2 and diagnosed incidentally, PFE can lead to severe complications, mainly embolic events like transient ischaemic attack or embolic strokes,2,3 or less common but equally severe, like myocardial infarction, angina, syncope, or sudden cardiac death.2,4–7 We present a case of a PFE which presented with chest pain.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old woman with no cardiovascular risk factors was referred from Primary Care to our centre for evaluation of chest discomfort. She described a stabbing chest pain, sometimes lasting for hours. The pain was unrelated to exertion and was accompanied by dizziness and palpitations. Her past medical history was unremarkable except for a prior palpitations’ study with a 72-h Holter monitor which showed no abnormalities. Physical examination revealed stable vital signs, regular cardiac auscultation, normal lung sounds, and no signs of heart failure. Baseline electrocardiogram had no significant abnormalities.

To further investigate the patient’s symptoms, a stress echocardiogram was scheduled. Baseline resting TTE images obtained priorly revealed a mobile, pedunculated mass measuring 11 × 7 mm attached to the aortic valve. Stress study was cancelled and TEE was scheduled confirming a mass attached to the free edge of the non-coronary cusp, visible on the aortic side throughout the cardiac cycle. Mass was elongated, of intermediate echogenicity, mobile, homogeneous, and non-calcified. No valvular dysfunction was observed. Corresponding images are shown in Figure 1. A blood sample was analysed with no relevant findings, troponin or inflammatory markers were within normal limits and there was no leucocytosis.

Mass’ differential diagnosis included primary cardiac tumour and valvular thrombus. Infective endocarditis (IE) was not considered in the absence of clinical criteria (no fever, negative microbiological findings, and no additional Duke’s criteria). A thrombus was deemed less likely given the morphology of the mass, with a distinct pedicle attached to the valve, although it could not be completely excluded. Cardiac metastasis was a less likely diagnosis with the patient not having any systemic symptoms, so despite patient’s age pre-test probability was considered low. With this differential, main suspicion was a primary cardiac tumour. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was not deemed necessary for the study. Case was discussed in a multidisciplinary clinical meeting, and surgical resection was recommended because of the lesion’s embolic risk. As part of pre-operative assessment, patient underwent a computed tomography (CT) scan to rule out coronary artery disease. Scan revealed no coronary atherosclerosis but did demonstrate an intermittent, dynamic obstruction of the right coronary ostium caused by the mass. Images from this study are presented in Figure 2. In light of this finding, surgical intervention was expedited. Blood analysis was repeated day before surgery showing no changes with the previous one.

A gelatinous mass measuring 10 × 7 mm shown in Figure 3 was resected. Procedure was uneventful, and patient was discharged a few days later with a normal post-operative echocardiogram. Histopathological examination shown in Figure 4 revealed a mass with avascular fibroelastic tissue and arboriform fronds covered by a single layer of endocardium, consistent with PFE. Final diagnosis was thus established. Timeline of the events are summarized in supplementary material.

The patient was followed up at 1, 3, and 8 months after surgery. Further echocardiogram assessment demonstrated no findings with absence of residual masses. The patient did not report recurrence of chest pain.

Discussion

In order to correctly evaluate and manage an aortic valve mass detected on initial TTE and to establish an appropriate differential diagnosis that supports optimal decision-making, some key aspects must be considered.

First, a detailed clinical history should be obtained, with attention to prior conditions or comorbidities and to the presence of key symptoms or signs such as fever, neurological deficits, or embolic phenomena. Subsequently, careful imaging evaluation is required. Following TTE, the first invasive diagnostic test should be a TEE.4,8,9

Infective endocarditis should be the first entity to be excluded given its clinical relevance. Duke’s criteria remain fundamental for its diagnosis. Infective endocarditis lesions are often located upstream relative to the valve and are typically associated with valve destruction and dysfunction (regurgitation and/or stenosis). Non-IE commonly presents with multiple filamentous strands.9

Lambl’s excrescences are mostly seen in elderly patients and typically appear as thin, thread-like structures.9

Regarding cardiac tumours, myxomas do not usually affect the aortic valve. When a mass is located on the aortic valve, PFE can be the most frequent primary tumour. Secondary cardiac tumours or metastases are common cardiac masses but usually do not present as isolated lesions involving the aortic valve.9

Thrombi affecting cardiac valves may closely resemble PFEs both morphologically and on imaging. Careful evaluation is essential to differentiate them. Advanced imaging modalities such as cardiac magnetic resonance can be useful for tissue characterization in selected cases, although their limited temporal resolution may reduce accuracy in assessing valvular masses.9

With respect to treatment, current consensus documents recommend surgical removal of aortic PFEs, particularly when the tumour measures >10 mm or when clinical manifestations such as embolic events are present.4 Some advocate resection of any PFE, because even small tumours may cause embolic complications.8 However, in high-risk surgical candidates or in cases of small, minimally mobile lesions, close monitoring may be an alternative.4 Antiplatelet therapy has been proposed in medically managed cases, but there is no clear evidence of benefit.4,8 Patients require long-term follow-up after surgery, as recurrence rates of up to 16% at 10 years have been reported.1,8,10,11

Papillary fibroelastomas causing angina or myocardial infarction have previously been reported, mostly in case reports and small series, typically in the setting of masses affecting the aortic valve.2,5–7 These symptoms appear to result from dynamic obstruction of coronary ostia. Sudden cardiac death may occur as the initial presentation due to complete coronary obstruction.2,6,7

This evidence suggests that the presence of angina in patients with PFEs should be regarded as an alarming symptom that warrants prompt evaluation and timely management.

Conclusion

We present a detailed and comprehensive case of a PFE obstructing a coronary ostium, causing angina, documented in with striking and illustrative CT scan images. This case emphasizes the value of multimodality imaging in the diagnosis and risk assessment of cardiac masses and highlights that angina can be a clinical manifestation of PFE. Such a presentation should raise concern and prompt early treatment to prevent serious complications.
