A web-based two-stage prediction model for postoperative pulmonary complications using preoperative and intraoperative data
Authors: Anran Dai, Hao Liu, Yuqi Dai, Po Shen, Jian Chen, Jiayong Zhang, Chen Chen, Yuping Hu, Yanna Si, Jianjun Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost existing models for predicting postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) rely solely on preoperative variables and lack integration of intraoperative data or clinical tools for application.MethodsThis study developed and externally validated logistic regression and machine learning (ML) models for predicting PPCs in non-cardiothoracic surgical patients undergoing general anesthesia. A cohort of 997 patients was randomly divided into training and test sets in a 7:3 ratio. The stepwise regression method was adopted to conduct feature screening on the preoperative dataset and the combined preoperative and intraoperative dataset, and a variety of ML algorithm models were developed. Model performance was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), brier score, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score. External validation was performed on an independent cohort of 286 patients from a different institution. The features of the optimized model were interpreted by introducing SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method.ResultsThe AUROC value of the logistic regression model using only preoperative variables in the test set was 0.828. After including intraoperative variables, the CatBoost model demonstrated superior performance, with an AUROC value of 0.927. In the preoperative model, C-reactive protein (CRP), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, age, and smoking status were the most significant predictors. However, operative duration, CRP, age, and intraoperative blood loss were the dominant predictors of postoperative outcomes. External validation confirmed general applicability, with preoperative logistic regression and the Assess Respiratory Risk in Surgical Patients in Catalonia Tool (ARISCAT) yielding AUROC values of 0.827 and 0.796, respectively. The postoperative CatBoost model achieved an AUROC of 0.865, AUPRC of 0.809, sensitivity of 0.787, specificity of 0.921, and the lowest Brier score (0.132).ConclusionsThe preoperative model enabled early risk stratification, while the postoperative model provided improved accuracy. A publicly accessible web-based calculator was developed to support clinical implementation and facilitate prospective validation.Keywords: Postoperative pulmonary complications, Non-cardiothoracic surgery, Machine learning, Risk factors, ARISCAT

Introduction

Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) account for 2–19% of non-cardiothoracic surgeries.1 Mild PPCs can also impose a significant economic burden on healthcare systems, with affected patients incurring an average increase of $48,168 in hospitalization costs, primarily due to extended length of stay (LOS), ICU care, and mechanical ventilation. Moreover, PPCs correlate with surgical and anesthetic risks.2, 3 Therefore, assessing the risk of PPCs and implementing effective preventive measures are crucial for reducing postoperative complications, improving patient outcomes, and lowering healthcare costs.

At present, digital technology is profoundly transforming the medical field. However, the risk assessment of PPCs commonly relies on traditional scoring systems, such as the Assess Respiratory Risk in Surgical Patients in Catalonia Tool (ARISCAT),4 the Score for Prediction of Postoperative Respiratory Complications (SPORC)5 and the multifactorial risk index developed by Arozullah AM et al.6 These systems mainly use preoperative data, which limits their predictive accuracy, especially when accounting for the dynamic changes that occur during surgery. While the SPORC-2 score improves prediction to some extent by incorporating intraoperative data, such as ventilation parameters and anesthesia depth, it still relies on static, predefined rules and thresholds, which cannot adapt in real time to rapid changes in a patient’s condition during surgery.7 Furthermore, traditional models often depend on manual data input and clinical judgment, which can lead to inconsistencies or missed opportunities for early intervention, especially in high-risk or complex patients.

Given these limitations, there is a pressing need to develop novel predictive models that enhance both accuracy and clinical applicability. Machine learning (ML) models, particularly gradient boosting frameworks, offer a promising approach. Gradient boosting is an ensemble technique that progressively constructs weak learners via gradient descent to minimize a differentiable loss function. This additive, error-correcting process enables models to automatically learn complex nonlinear decision boundaries and high-order feature interactions without explicit feature engineering.8 Additionally, ML models excel at processing dynamic intraoperative data. These data points evolve over time and exhibit high heterogeneity (e.g., cumulative blood loss, hemodynamic fluctuations, surgical duration). Unlike traditional scoring systems reliant on static thresholds or linear combinations, ML models treat intraoperative variables as continuous or cumulative features. They adaptively capture temporal patterns and sequence dependencies, thereby refining risk predictions in real-time as new data emerges throughout the surgical procedure. Recent theoretical and empirical studies indicate that in tabular data scenarios (common in perioperative medicine), boosting-based methods outperform linear models and even deep neural networks in certain cases when sample sizes are moderate.9, 10 Existing studies have shown that in high-risk surgical fields such as acute gastrointestinal surgery and acute cerebral hemorrhage, ML models have demonstrated relatively excellent predictive capabilities for PPCs.11, 12, 13 Further studies suggest ML frameworks incorporating intraoperative variables outperform traditional scoring systems in predictive performance.14 Thus, we hypothesize that ML models incorporating intraoperative characteristics may provide more accurate personalized PPCs prediction.

This study aimed to develop highly applicable models for dynamically predicting the occurrence of PPCs at two time points: preoperatively and postoperatively, following non-cardiothoracic surgery. The performance of these models was compared with the ARISCAT risk score. To assess generalizability, the models were externally validated using an independent patient cohort from a separate medical center, confirming robust performance across institutions. Furthermore, an online risk calculator was developed to enhance clinical decision-making by providing a more efficient and accessible tool for clinicians.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study included two independent cohorts of non-cardiothoracic surgical patients who underwent invasive ventilation under general anesthesia. The development cohort was derived from Nanjing First Hospital (Jiangsu, China), with cases collected from March 2023 to April 2024, while the external validation cohort was obtained from the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Jiangsu, China), spanning from February 2023 to December 2024.

Patient inclusion criteria were identical across both centers. Exclusion criteria were: (1) minors (under 18 years of age); (2) patients undergoing obstetric surgery and organ transplantation; (3) patients with incomplete medical records; (4) outpatient procedures (who had a LOS in hospital less than 24 h); (5) patients undergoing mechanical ventilation in the previous 30 days.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of both hospitals: Nanjing First Hospital (Protocol code: KY20220621–04-KS-01) and the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Protocol code: 20230322-k106), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of the study and absence of personally identifiable information, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the IRBs.

Data collection

The characteristics to be considered in this study were selected based on the existing literature reports and the experience of clinicians. Preoperative variables comprised demographics, disease history (e.g., hypertension, diabetes mellitus, asthma), laboratory measurements (e.g., C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell (WBC)), preoperative incidents (e.g., preoperative anemia, respiratory infection within 30 days), etc. Intraoperative factors included surgery duration (incision to end of surgery), intraoperative hypotension, use of nasogastric tube, perioperative fluid management (e.g., intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative crystalloid infusion), and intraoperative medication (e.g., opioid, muscle relaxant), etc. Among them, the preoperative peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2) was measured using a pulse oximeter in the resting state. Intraoperative hypotension was defined as MAP < 65 mmHg.

Outcome

The elemental outcome was the occurrence within the first seven postoperative days of one or more PPCs, defined according to the European Perioperative Clinical Outcome (EPCO). PPCs included: respiratory infection, respiratory failure, pleural effusion, atelectasis, pneumothorax, bronchospasm, and aspiration pneumonitis.15 Patients were assessed daily during this period by trained nurse anesthetists through either bedside evaluation or telephone follow-up for those discharged early.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of patients with and without PPCs were compared using statistical analysis. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to determine if continuous variables had a normal distribution. All continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) and analyzed with an independent sample t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. The Chi-square test was applied to evaluate all categorical variables, which were represented in terms of numbers and percentages. It was deemed statistical significance when the p < 0.05 (2-sided). The statistical analysis was performed using R 4.3.1 software.

Datasets pre-processing

Patients were randomly split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets, maintaining a similar PPCs distribution in both sets. Variables with imbalance ratios < 100:1 or missing rates > 20% were excluded to reduce overfitting. The overall proportion of missing data among the retained variables was low. Missing values were imputed using a K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) method,16 with K set to 5, a value widely recommended for clinical datasets of comparable size and demonstrated to yield robust imputation performance across varying missing data patterns.17 KNN imputation was chosen for its capacity to preserve local data structures without assuming a specific distribution. Given the low missing rate in our dataset, the impact of imputation on original data distributions was expected to be minimal. To confirm this, we compared the means and standard deviations of continuous variables and the frequency distributions of categorical variables before and after imputation, and no clinically meaningful shifts were observed (Table S1). Furthermore, before inclusion in the algorithm, all continuous variables underwent Z-Score normalization, whereas categorical variables undertook One-Hot encoding.

Feature selection

Logistic regression analysis was performed to factors that predicted PPCs, and the results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and any factors with VIF greater than 5 were promptly removed from the analysis. Stepwise multivariable logistic regression was performed using bidirectional elimination based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Variables were entered at p < 0.05 and removed at p > 0.10. Feature selection was performed twice on the training set, first including only preoperative variables and then combining preoperative and intraoperative variables.

Machine learning algorithms

The 5 classifier algorithms were conducted to predict PPCs using the training set: logistic regression (LR), random forest classifier (RFC), extreme gradient boosting (XGB), light gradient boosting machine (LGBM), and categorical boosting (CatBoost). Class imbalance was addressed via cost-sensitive learning (weighted loss functions in ML algorithms), prioritizing minority class without oversampling to avoid synthetic data artifacts. The candidate hyperparameter search ranges were determined based on commonly adopted configurations in the literature and preliminary exploratory runs, and optimized via grid search with 10-fold cross-validation. ML models were conducted using Python’s “sklearn 1.0.2” and “catboost 1.1.1” packages.

An internal testing set was applied to evaluate the models’ generalization capability, and the optimal model was chosen from each dataset. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was mainly utilized to evaluate model performance. The area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) was used to quantify the trade-off between precision and recall at different threshold values of the model’s output score, which can more truly reflect the model’s ability to recognize minority classes.18 Calibration was assessed using creating a calibration plot and calculating the calibration slope. The Brier score was also used to assess the accuracy of each model, and the value of 0 indicates a perfect prediction, while 1 indicates an inferior prognosis. Then, the performance of the preoperative models was compared with that of the ARISCAT risk score. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was plotted to evaluate the clinical benefits and applicability net benefits of the model with the best diagnostic value.

Model interpretation

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was a game theoretic approach to visualize the importance of each variable and explain the individual predictions. The relationship between predictors and PPCs can be understood by considering the cumulative effects of variable attributions on the overall risk for each observation. The SHAP values were calculated using Python's “SHAP 0.40.0” package. Higher SHAP values suggest an increased probability of PPCs.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 1581 patients were initially screened in the development cohort from Nanjing First Hospital. After applying exclusion criteria, 997 eligible patients were included in the final study population for model development. These patients underwent major surgical procedures, classified as abdominal/pelvic (e.g., gastrointestinal, hepatobiliary, urological) or nonabdominal/pelvic (e.g., orthopedic, neurological) surgeries, among whom 131 (13.1%) developed postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs). The top three complications were respiratory infection (6.9%), pleural effusion (6.8%), and atelectasis (5.8%). The baseline characteristics and intraoperative outcomes of the development cohort were detailed in Table 1 and Table 2. Patients with PPCs were significantly older than those without, and no significant difference was observed in gender. Moreover, the demographic and clinical variables were well balanced between the training and internal testing sets (Table S2).

To validate the prediction models from the derivation cohort, an independent cohort of 426 patients was retrospectively collected from the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between February 2023 and December 2024. After applying the same inclusion and exclusion criteria, 286 eligible patients were included in the external validation set. Of them, 83 (29.0%) developed PPCs. The clinical characteristics of this cohort were summarized in Table S3. Variables such as age, CRP, surgery duration, intraoperative blood loss, preoperative SpO₂, and ASA physical status showed significant differences between PPC and non-PPC groups. These trends were largely consistent with those observed in the development cohort, supporting the robustness of the identified risk factors.

Model performance

The complete search grid and corresponding optimal values for the five models we constructed are provided in Table S5. By comparing the performance of these models on the test set, we found that CatBoost, when incorporating intraoperative variables, significantly outperformed the other models, particularly in terms of AUROC, AUPRC, accuracy, F1 score, sensitivity, and specificity. However, XGB, LGBM, and RFC also performed well and showed promising results, but they were not as effective as CatBoost in handling intraoperative data (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of patient enrollment in this study.

As shown in Fig. 2, when using only preoperative variables, the LR model achieved an AUROC of 0.834 (95% CI: 0.751–0.905) on the training set and 0.828 (95% CI: 0.752–0.916) on the test set, while CatBoost yielded 0.873 (95% CI: 0.834–0.912) and 0.814 (95% CI: 0.748–0.875). However, when both preoperative and intraoperative variables were used concurrently, CatBoost’s AUROC significantly improved to 0.927 (95% CI: 0.887–0.967). Moreover, the XGB model and the LR model yielded the next highest AUROC values of 0.924 (95% CI: 0.878–0.970) and 0.924 (95% CI: 0.887–0.961), respectively.

Fig. S1 displayed the Precision-Recall curve (PRC) for the five models. In the preoperative model, the LR model achieved the highest AUPRC of 0.402 (95% CI: 0.245–0.582) on the testing set. In contrast, in the postoperative model, the CatBoost model demonstrated a significant improvement with an AUPRC of 0.662 (95% CI: 0.479–0.807) on the testing set.

Model calibration was assessed using the Brier score and the calibration plot (Fig. S2). Among models based solely on preoperative features, the CatBoost model achieved the lowest Brier score of 0.076 (compared to 0.082 for LR, 0.082 for RFC, 0.079 for XGB, and 0.088 for LGBM). The calibration plot of the CatBoost model showed good fit, with a slight overestimation in the moderate-to-high risk group (predicted probability 0.4–0.8), where the actual positive proportion was lower than the predicted values. After incorporating intraoperative variables, models generally exhibited greater volatility, particularly in the high-risk group. This volatility is normal due to sample sparsity in extreme risk stratification, where more features are also more susceptible to capturing noise. Nevertheless, the lower Brier scores confirm improved probability reliability, with CatBoost’s calibration significantly improving to 0.061 - still the lowest overall.

The diagnostic performance of the models at their optimal cutoff points is shown in Table S6. For the preoperative model, the LR achieved the highest F1-Value (0.561) on the testing set. In the postoperative model (which included both preoperative and intraoperative variables), the CatBoost model demonstrated the best sensitivity (0.903) and the highest F1-Value (0.664) on the testing set.

We evaluated the predicted performance of the ARISCAT risk score. The AUROC was 0.801(95%CI: 0.745–0.856) in the training set and 0.834 (95%CI: 0.752–0.916) in the testing set (Fig. S3), respectively. The sensitivity and F1-Value were 0.733 and 0.378 on the testing set (Table S7). The results of DCA showed that the net benefit of the LR model and the Catboost model was greater than the range of the ARISCAT risk score (Fig. S4).

External validation and comparison

The predictive performance of the five models was further evaluated using an independent external cohort (n = 286), as illustrated in Fig. 3 and summarized in Table 4. In the preoperative-only setting, the LR model achieved the highest AUROC (0.827) and AUPRC (0.669), outperforming the ML counterparts in distinguishing patients at risk of PPCs. However, its calibration curve showed moderate deviation from the ideal line, with a Brier score of 0.165. As shown in Fig. S5, the ARISCAT score achieved an AUC of 0.796 and an AURPC of 0.588, which were slightly lower than those of our LR model. When intraoperative variables were added, all models demonstrated notable improvements in discrimination, precision-recall balance, and probabilistic calibration. The CatBoost model yielded the best overall performance, with the highest AUROC (0.865), AUPRC (0.809), sensitivity (0.787), specificity (0.921), and F1-score (0.646), along with the lowest Brier score (0.132), indicating strong generalizability and robustness. These findings validate the external applicability of the proposed models and emphasize the clinical relevance of incorporating intraoperative data for dynamic perioperative risk prediction.

Model interpretation

The SHAP summary plots for the LR and CatBoost model were presented in Fig. 4, illustrating the ranked contributions of each variable to the predicted risk of PPCs. In the LR model based solely on preoperative variables (Fig. 4A), CRP, ASA physical status, and age emerged as the most influential predictors. In contrast, in the CatBoost model incorporating both preoperative and intraoperative features (Fig. 4B), surgery duration, CRP, and age were identified as the top contributors, highlighting the added prognostic value of intraoperative information in the postoperative risk assessment.

Construction of the web calculator

The preoperative model (LR) and postoperative model (CatBoost), which demonstrated the best performance, were selected and integrated into the risk web calculator accessible at https://prediction-of-ppcs.streamlit.app/ (Fig. S6). The workflow comprises: (1) Selecting the model (preoperative or postoperative); (2) Entering key variables; (3) Clicking “Predict.” Real-time calculations are then performed using the deployed models, outputting PPCs probability, risk classification, and SHAP-based feature contribution waterfall plots.

Discussion

In this study, we applied ML and LR models to predict the occurrence of PPCs in non-cardiothoracic patients and achieved dynamic prediction at both preoperative and postoperative time points. Our preoperative model showed comparable predictive performance to the ARISCAT risk score, highlighting the model’s clinical relevance and competitiveness. Notably, the postoperative models exhibited significantly enhanced predictive capability compared with preoperative models, underscoring the critical value of immediate postoperative risk prediction. To evaluate the generalizability of our models, we conducted external validation using an independent cohort from a different medical center. The models maintained stable predictive performance, with the CatBoost model achieving the highest AUROC and AUPRC after incorporating intraoperative variables. These results support the robustness and external applicability of our prediction framework.

In addition to providing individualized risk predictions, understanding how the model generates its output is equally important for clinical adoption. Therefore, we sought to enhance model interpretability by identifying clinically meaningful predictors. SHAP summary plots were employed to assess the relative importance of input features. Among all predictors, CRP emerged as one of the most important features, ranking first in the preoperative model and second in the postoperative model. It is a rapidly expressed protein responding to inflammatory stimuli such as microbial invasion or tissue damage. Xiang et al. reported that hip fracture patients with high preoperative CRP were more likely to develop postoperative pneumonia.19 Elevated CRP levels were identified as a possible factor for acute exacerbation of interstitial lung disease after non-pulmonary surgery in the study by Furuya et al. and subsequently as an independent risk factor in Takao et al.20, 21 A recent study reported that perioperative use of methylprednisolone was considered an independent risk factor for PPCs after hip fracture in older people. One of the efficacy of methylprednisolone is to suppress inflammatory response and significantly reduce CRP levels.22 Our study may indirectly explain the reason for this corticosteroid as a risk factor. Currently, CRP is an accepted biomarker for identifying cardiovascular risk,23, 24 but based on these results, the predictive ability of CRP for PPCs should also be of interest and further investigated.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that intraoperative blood loss is a significant independent risk factor for PPCs, making its inclusion in intraoperative prediction models essential.25, 26 The SHAP analysis results in this study further support this view, with intraoperative blood loss ranking fourth in importance. Significant blood loss can lead to hypovolemia and hemodynamic instability, thereby impairing pulmonary tissue perfusion and oxygenation capacity. This, through ischemia-reperfusion injury mechanisms, increases the risk of ventilator-associated lung injury and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).27, 28 Failure to promptly and adequately replenish volume deficits caused by blood loss and urinary output may exacerbate postoperative complications through sustained intraoperative hypoperfusion.29 Although perioperative fluid administration variables were excluded from this model, prior studies indicate fluid management strategies significantly influence PPCs occurrence. Multiple evidence sources demonstrate that varying fluid infusion volumes and net perfusion rates substantially impact surgical outcomes.27, 28, 29, 30 Blum et al. reported an association between intraoperative fluid and blood product transfusions and ARDS occurrence.27 Tandon et al. described the risk of transfusion-related acute lung injury.28 Besides, multiple studies confirm that personalized, goal-directed perioperative fluid therapy strategies help reduce postoperative morbidity and mortality.31, 32 The International Fluid Optimization Group also recommends fluid management protocols based on preset physiological targets (such as cardiac output and oxygen delivery) to achieve precise perioperative volume optimization.33

In addition, age, preoperative anemia, respiratory infection within 30 days, surgery duration, and preoperative SpO2 have been confirmed by multiple studies as independent risk factors for PPCs,4, 25, 34, 35 and the findings of this study are highly consistent with these reports. Among these, surgery duration ranked highest in SHAP importance analysis. Prolonged surgery may exacerbates atelectasis through prolonged mechanical ventilation, leading to alveolar collapse, regional hypoventilation, and release of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-a). This impairs pulmonary surfactant function and significantly increases PPC risk.30, 36 Diabetes, as a chronic metabolic disorder, causes cumulative damage to multiple organ systems through prolonged hyperglycemia. Ramachandran et al. demonstrated that diabetes is an independent predictor of unexpected early tracheal intubation after non-cardiac surgery.37 Kor et al. also confirmed a strong association between diabetes and postoperative lung injury.38 Furthermore, a history of cerebrovascular accidents may result in residual neurological deficits (such as diminished cough reflex and impaired airway clearance capacity), thereby weakening airway protective mechanisms and increasing aspiration risk.6, 36

Our web-based risk calculator can be considered a more flexible and convenient tool. Clinicians can utilize the preoperative model, incorporating baseline patient factors (e.g., age, CRP, ASA status), to assess a patient’s risk of PPCs. This enables early risk stratification during the preoperative evaluation period, thereby facilitating targeted interventions such as respiratory muscle training or adjustments to intraoperative ventilation strategies.39 In contrast, the postoperative model incorporates intraoperative variables (e.g., operative duration, blood loss) to provide refined risk prediction immediately after surgical closure. This supports timely, personalized postoperative management, including enhanced respiratory monitoring, early mobilization, prophylactic noninvasive ventilation, or ICU admission planning for high-risk patients.20 This two-stage approach bridges static preoperative tools with dynamic postoperative optimization, aligning with evidence-based perioperative care pathways. Meanwhile, we consider the current risk calculator to represent an initial, practical application of ML models in clinical settings. Looking ahead, as artificial intelligence becomes more deeply integrated with healthcare systems, the development of ML or even deep learning frameworks capable of continuously and automatically monitoring electronic health records (EHRs) will become inevitable. The true value of such systems lies in their capacity for self-learning and continuous evolution, adapting to newly acquired data over time to improve predictive accuracy and clinical relevance.

Recent studies have applied ML to predict PPCs. Xue et al.40 developed ML models using preoperative and intraoperative data from a large multicenter cohort, achieving AUROC values of 0.83–0.91 for multiple postoperative complications (including respiratory events), confirming the value of dynamic intraoperative features, though not specifically targeting PPCs. Kiatinkul et al.13 constructed a general ML tool to predict postoperative respiratory failure, integrating intraoperative variables to achieve an AUROC of 0.93. However, the model is not PPC-specific and lacks interpretability tools such as SHAP. Li et al.14 developed an interpretable gradient-boosting model for predicting PPCs, achieving an AUROC of 0.86 and providing SHAP explanations, with relevant features more focused on intraoperative respiratory variables. Compared to these studies, our research offers three advantages: First, a clinically intuitive two-stage framework enables preoperative risk stratification and postoperative real-time optimization, aligning with actual decision-making timelines. Second, it combines a publicly accessible web calculator with SHAP-based interpretation, providing transparent, patient-level explainability for clinical applications. Furthermore, it explicitly compares the preoperative stage with the widely recognized ARISCAT score.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, due to its retrospective design, there is an inherent risk of selection bias. Although the observed incidence of PPCs in our cohort aligns with previously reported epidemiological data, the possibility of over- or under-representation of specific patient subgroups cannot be excluded. Second, our model was constructed using single-center data. Although externally validated, both cohorts originated from hospitals in Nanjing, spanned a similar time period, and had relatively small sample sizes, potentially limiting its national or international applicability. Future studies should be conducted across regions or in a multicenter setting, extending the time span, expanding the sample size, and incorporating subgroup analyses to assess the model’s robustness across different surgical types, thereby further enhancing the generalizability of the findings. Third, the study lacked detailed intraoperative ventilation parameters, such as peak airway pressure and driving pressure, which may influence the risk of PPCs. Due to data unavailability, these factors were not included in model development.41 Moreover, the role of lung-protective ventilation strategies in reducing PPCs remains controversial.42 Future prospective studies should incorporate comprehensive intraoperative respiratory data to better assess the impact of ventilation practices on postoperative outcomes and to further enhance the model’s predictive performance.

Conclusions

We successfully developed dynamic prediction models for PPCs at both the preoperative and postoperative stages in non-cardiothoracic surgical patients. The preoperative model achieved predictive performance comparable to the ARISCAT risk score, while the incorporation of intraoperative variables led to significantly enhanced accuracy in the postoperative model. To evaluate the generalizability of our framework, we conducted external validation using an independent cohort from a different institution. The models maintained stable performance, with the CatBoost model demonstrating the best overall results after incorporating intraoperative data. Furthermore, the development and deployment of a web-based risk calculator provide a user-friendly clinical tool, supporting real-time individualized risk assessment and facilitating prospective external validation in broader clinical settings.
