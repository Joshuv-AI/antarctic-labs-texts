Extracellular vesicles from breast cancer cells carry proliferation-associated miRNA-mRNA networks under nutrient stress
Authors: Dhananjay B. Alagundagi, Raushan Kumar Chaudhary, Athira C. Rajeev, Mahima Rachel Thomas, Amod Kulkarni, Praveenkumar Shetty, Prakash Patil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) from any cell type carry diverse cargo, including proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, metabolites, and organelles, which exert multifaceted functions in recipient cells. The present study investigated changes in EV-associated miRNAs in breast cancer (BC) cells under serum starvation and explored potential miRNA-mRNA regulatory relationships. The small RNA sequencing dataset from plasma-derived EVs of BC patients (GSE270497) was analyzed for differentially expressed miRNAs (DE-miRNAs). Target mRNA prediction, protein-protein interaction network analysis, hub gene identification, and validation of public transcriptomic datasets were employed to prioritize candidate regulatory molecules. EVs were isolated from the MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 cell lines. Selected miRNAs and target transcripts were evaluated by RT-qPCR in BC cells and their EVs under normal and serum-starvation conditions.We identified 25 DE-miRNAs and 3720 predicted target genes. In silico analyses prioritized PRKACB and ESR1 among the hub genes. The enriched pathways included MAPK, PI3K-Akt, and Ras signaling. Neither PRKACB nor ESR1 showed any significant association with patient survival. Hsa-miR-548v and hsa-miR-601 displayed variable expression patterns across cancer types. Nanoparticle tracking analysis showed EV particle sizes ranging from 150 to 210 nm. RT-qPCR analysis showed relatively abundant levels of hsa-miR-548v and PRKACB transcripts in EVs from both BC cell lines. In contrast, hsa-miR-601 showed higher intracellular abundance, whereas ESR1 transcripts were not detected in cells or EVs.Overall, our findings indicate differential sorting and abundance of selected miRNAs and mRNAs in EVs under serum-starvation conditions and identify hsa-miR-548v, hsa-miR-601, and PRKACB as candidate components of an EV-associated regulatory network.

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membrane-bound nanovesicles secreted by most cell types and play a crucial role in intercellular communication. EVs transport diverse bioactive cargo molecules, such as proteins, metabolites, lipids, DNA, messenger RNAs (mRNAs), and regulatory non-coding RNAs, including microRNAs (miRNAs), which can modulate gene expression and signaling pathways in recipient cells upon their uptake.1, 2, 3 In cancer, EV-mediated communication contributes to tumor proliferation, metastasis, immune evasion, and therapy resistance by transferring oncogenic molecules between tumor cells and components of the tumor microenvironment.[4] Breast cancer (BC)-derived EVs have been reported to transfer RNA species and protein cargo to recipient cells, thereby influencing their transcription and signaling pathways.5, 6 EV-associated miRNAs have garnered significant interest owing to their ability to regulate gene expression by binding target mRNAs. Dysregulation of miRNA expressions in BC is associated with its subtypes, progression, and therapeutic response.7, 8 Previous studies suggest that miRNAs incorporation into EVs is not a passive process, but a selective and regulated mechanism. Melo et al. (2014) showed that EVs from multiple BC cell lines (4 T1, MDA-MB-231, and MCF-7) are enriched in miRNA cargo compared with EVs from normal breast epithelial cells. Moreover, metastatic BC cell lines showed higher miRNA levels in their EVs than those from less aggressive cells. BC patient-derived and cell-derived EVs contained components of miRNA biogenesis, such as Dicer, Ago2, and TRBP, indicating that EVs may carry elements for processing and regulating miRNAs.[9] Additional studies have shown that different miRNAs can be selectively sorted into distinct EV populations, supporting the existence of active cargo-sorting mechanisms.10, 11, 12 Although selective RNA sorting into EVs is well established, the molecular determinants governing the differential fate of individual miRNAs under cellular stress remain incompletely understood. Therefore, identifying stress-associated patterns of differential EV enrichment provides an important first step toward understanding how specific regulatory RNAs are selectively partitioned between cells and EVs.

Cancer cells face metabolic stress within their tumor microenvironment due to restricted nutrient availability, hypoxia, and competition for metabolic resources.[13] Under such conditions, cells activate adaptive regulatory mechanisms to maintain their survival and proliferation. Several studies have reported that nutrient deprivation or serum starvation can alter the expression and activity of specific miRNAs that regulate target mRNAs involved in cell-cycle control, apoptosis, autophagy, and metabolic adaptation.14, 15 Through these regulatory networks, miRNAs help cancer cells activate signaling pathways and sustain growth under unfavourable conditions. In addition, stress conditions have been shown to influence miRNA-mRNA regulatory interactions and the cellular machinery involved in miRNA function, further highlighting the dynamic nature of these networks during metabolic stress.[14] Despite these reports, most studies have focused either on profiling EV-associated miRNAs or examining intracellular miRNA expression independently. Therefore, it is unclear whether coordinated miRNA-mRNA regulatory networks present within cancer cells are also reflected in EV cargo. Because miRNAs regulate specific mRNAs, EVs may transmit coordinated regulatory modules rather than isolated RNA molecules. Cancer cells frequently encounter microenvironmental stressors, such as nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, or metabolic competition. Under such conditions, EV-mediated communication has been proposed to facilitate adaptive responses by redistributing signaling molecules between cells.[16] In this regard, EVs may serve as reservoirs of signaling molecules by preserving proliferation-associated transcripts and regulatory RNAs that can be utilized for cellular adaptation.

Our previous study provided evidence that BC-derived EVs can undergo autologous uptake, raising the possibility that EV-associated regulatory miRNAs may contribute to communication between EVs and parental cells.[17] It also raises the possibility that EV cargo loading may be selectively organized to support signaling requirements within the same cell population. If EV uptake by parental cells is favored, EVs could serve as reservoirs of regulatory RNA molecules that can be reintroduced into the originating cells during periods of cellular stress, thereby reinforcing proliferation-related signaling pathways. Based on this, we hypothesized that BC cells may selectively package miRNA-mRNA regulatory networks into EVs, thereby enabling the coordinated transfer of required RNA under nutrient-stress conditions. Therefore, the present study aims to identify differentially expressed miRNAs (DE-miRNAs) in plasma-derived EVs from BC patients, characterize their predicted regulatory networks, and determine whether selected miRNA-mRNA components show differential abundance between BC cells and their corresponding EVs under normal and serum-starvation conditions. The study explores contrasting patterns of intracellular retention and EV enrichment of the individual miRNAs, which may provide clues for regulating the EV cargo selection. However, the molecular determinants of such differential sorting have not been directly investigated and remain an important area for future mechanistic study.

Methodology

Data acquisition and miRNA expression analysis

A publicly available GSE270497 dataset was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database at NCBI for miRNA expression profiles of plasma-derived EVs from BC patients and healthy controls.[18] The raw miRNA read counts were downloaded and processed in R (version 4.3) using the Bioconductor package edgeR (v3.40). To reduce background noise and improve the reliability of results, lowly expressed miRNAs were filtered to retain only those with at least 10 reads in at least 50% of samples in either the BC or healthy control group. Normalization was then performed using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method implemented in edgeR to account for differences in sequencing depth and library composition across samples. DE-miRNA analysis between BC patients and healthy controls was conducted using the quasi-likelihood generalized linear model framework in edgeR (glmQLFit followed by glmQLFTest), with contrasts defined as BC versus healthy. MiRNAs with an absolute log₂ fold change ≥2.0 and a false discovery rate (FDR)–adjusted p-value <0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction) were considered differentially expressed. Volcano plots and MA plots were generated to visualize differential expression patterns, and heatmaps of the most significant miRNAs were created using logCPM-normalized values to validate sample clustering. In the present study, we employed multiple publicly available datasets from the GEO database to provide independent validation layers for comparing patient-derived EV miRNA profiles with transcriptomic data from BC cell lines and their EVs.

Prediction and filtering of miRNA target genes

Putative target mRNAs of the DE-miRNAs were predicted using the miRWalk database (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/). mRNA targets with predicted miRNA binding sites in their 3′ untranslated region (UTR), 5′ UTR, or coding sequence were retrieved. Further, targeted predictions were cross-validated using the miRDB database to enhance the prediction reliability. Only target mRNAs predicted by both platforms were considered for further analyses.

Construction of protein-protein interaction network and hub gene identification

All predicted target genes were pooled, duplicates were removed, and the resulting gene list was imported into Cytoscape (v3.10.1)[19] to construct a PPIN using the STRING plugin. The confidence score threshold was set to 0.7, while other parameters were kept at their default values. The hub genes from the PPIN were identified using the cytoHubba plugin as previously described.[20] Briefly, the top 10-ranked genes from each cytoHubba algorithm were extracted, their overlap was assessed using a Venn diagram, and genes ranked in at least 6 algorithms were considered hub genes.

Functional enrichment and pathway analysis of the network

Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis of the PPI network were performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID). Gene Ontology (GO) terms for biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF), as well as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, were analyzed to characterize the biological roles of the target genes.

In silico validation of hub genes in EVs of breast cancer cell lines

In this study, we used transcriptomic datasets of BC cell lines (GSE159661) and EVs (GSE188385) as orthogonal validation to determine whether the identified hub genes from plasma-derived EV miRNA networks demonstrate enrichment in cells and their EVs. Both datasets were log2-transformed to ensure cross-platform comparability and quantile-normalized using the preprocessCore package in R.21, 22 The normalized data were exported as CSV and Excel files for further analysis. Relative gene enrichment was calculated as the difference between the normalized log2 expression values for EVs and the BC cell line, with positive values indicating enrichment in EVs.

Differential expression and clinical relevance of hub genes and regulatory miRNAs in breast adenocarcinoma

The expression of hub genes at the mRNA and protein levels was evaluated using publicly available online tools, GEPIA2 and UALCAN. Among these, the GEPIA2 tool assessed mRNA expression in breast adenocarcinoma (BRCA) tumors compared with normal tissues, whereas UALCAN examined mRNA and protein expression across BRCA molecular subtypes. The clinical significance of the hub genes was determined using overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) analyses from GEPIA2. Briefly, patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression value. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival plots were generated, and statistical significance was assessed using log-rank tests (p < 0.05). Additionally, the ‘TCGA miRNA’ module in UALCAN was used to assess miRNA regulatory expression and clinical relevance.

Pan-cancer expression of regulatory miRNAs and binding with target mRNA

The expression patterns of regulatory miRNAs were evaluated across multiple carcinomas using the ‘miRTarget’ module of UALCAN. miRNA expression in tumor and normal tissues was compared across cohorts to construct a pan-cancer landscape of miRNA dysregulation. Further, the binding of these miRNAs with their target mRNAs was assessed using the Target Scan Human 7.2 (https://www.targetscan.org/) tool.

In vitro cell culture and EVs isolation

BC cell lines MCF7 (luminal) and MDA-MB-231 (aggressive, triple negative) were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1000 U/mL penicillin, and 1000 U/mL streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and were serum-starved for 48 h before harvesting the spent media for EV isolation. These serum-starved cells were also harvested for RNA isolation. The spent media were then subjected to differential ultracentrifugation (300 ×g for 10 min, 3000 ×g for 20 min, 10,000 ×g for 30 min, and 100,000 ×g for 120 min) as reported by Eswaran et al. (2025).[23] Isolated EVs were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline and characterized for particle size and concentration using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; NanoSight LM10, Malvern Instruments, UK) as previously described.[24]

In vitro validation of miRNA-mRNA regulatory network using quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction

The total RNA was isolated from MCF7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines in normal and serum-starved conditions (NC and SSC), and their corresponding EVs, using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) according to the supplier's instructions. Isolated RNAs were reverse-transcribed using a commercial cDNA synthesis kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Japan) on a QuantStudio QS3 system (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc., USA).[25] Gene expression levels were quantified using the comparative Ct (2-ΔΔCt) method, with GAPDH and U6 snRNA used as endogenous controls for mRNA and miRNA analyses, respectively. Results

Differential expression of miRNAs in breast cancer plasma-derived vesicles

The GSE270497 dataset included expression profiles of 2655 miRNAs. Among these, 25 were DE-miRNAs in plasma-derived EVs from BC patients (n = 80) compared to healthy controls (n = 120) (Supplementary Table 1). Of the 25 DE-miRNAs, 23 were upregulated and two were downregulated. The expression patterns of miRNAs were visualized using volcano plots (Fig. 1) and further supported by hierarchical clustering shown in the heatmap (Supplementary Fig 1), which demonstrated clear separation between BC and control samples. DE- miRNAs were further used to predict potential target mRNAs.

Hub gene identification and functional enrichment

MiRWalk predicted 3720 target mRNAs for DE-miRNAs (Supplementary Table 2). These targets were then mapped using STRING, and a high-confidence PPIN comprising 2582 nodes and 10,552 edges was constructed (Supplementary Fig 2). The PPIN of hub genes was shown in Fig. 2A. CytoHubba analysis identified epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), tumor protein 53 (TP53), protein kinase cAMP-activated catalytic subunit beta (PRKACB), protein kinase cAMP-activated catalytic subunit alpha (PRKACA), calmodulin 3 (CALM3), signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MAPK1), and estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1) as hub genes (Supplementary table 3). The functional analysis of predicted targets revealed significant enrichment in several GO and KEGG pathway terms (Supplementary Table 4). The top-5 enriched GO and KEGG terms were shown in Fig. 2B. As per Fig. 2, the top enriched BP terms included both positive and negative regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, indicating the dynamic control of gene expression essential in cancer progression. Additionally, signal transduction and regulation of DNA-templated transcription were highly enriched, highlighting the involvement of target genes in crucial signaling and transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. For MF terms, the enriched categories involved protein binding, metal and zinc ion binding, identical protein binding, and ATP binding. Further, cellular localization of targets revealed that most were found in the cytosol, cytoplasm, nucleus, plasma membrane, and other membrane compartments. This distribution aligns with their functional roles in signaling and transcriptional regulation. The pathway enrichment analysis underscored the biological relevance of genes in oncogenic pathways that are crucial to cancer development and progression. Our results revealed enrichment of major signaling pathways, including the MAPK and PI3K-Akt pathways, as well as pathways related to human papillomavirus infection and Ras signaling.

Selective enrichment of hub genes in EVs

The enrichment of hub genes in BC cells and EVs was assessed using normalized expression profiles from transcriptomic datasets. As shown in Table 2, EGFR, PRKACB, and ESR1 exhibited higher relative abundance in EVs from MDA-MB-231, while CALM3 and STAT3 were moderately enriched. In case of MCF7-derived EVs, PRKACB and ESR1 were highly enriched, whereas CALM3 and PRKACA were moderate, and STAT3, TP53, and EGFR showed minimal differences. Expression of miRNA-mRNA network in breast cancer

After in silico enrichment validation, we assessed PRKACB and ESR1 for their expression patterns and potential clinical relevance in BRCA using bioinformatics tools. As shown in Fig. 3, PRKACB mRNA levels were modestly but consistently reduced in tumor tissues compared with normal tissue samples across the luminal, HER2-enriched, and triple-negative (TNBC) subtypes; similar trends were observed at the protein level. However, its expression was not significantly associated with OS (p = 0.86) or DFS (p = 0.72). In contrast, ESR1 exhibited clear subtype-specific expression, with high levels in luminal tumors and minimal expression in HER2-enriched and TNBC subtypes, consistent with its established role as an estrogen receptor (ER) marker. Expression of hsa-miR-548v and hsa-miR-601 in pan-cancer

Pan-cancer analysis of miRNA expression showed variable expression of hsa-miR-548v across tumor types, with marked downregulation in BLCA, CESC, COAD, LUAD, LUSC, STAD, THYM, and UCEC, and upregulation in CHOL, PAAD, and PCPG. In contrast, hsa-miR-601 expression remained relatively stable across most cancer types, with downregulation in THYM and UCEC and upregulation in COAD, CHOL, PCPG and READ. In the case of BRCA, both miRNAs clustered near the center of the distribution, indicating minimal or no altered expression (Fig. 6). Overall, hsa-miR-548v and hsa-miR-601 exhibited variable expression patterns across the analyzed cancer types, suggesting that their abundance is context-dependent rather than uniformly altered across cancers. These findings provide broader cancer-context information but do not establish cancer-type-specific regulatory functions. Further, TargetScan analysis revealed the binding site and patterns of hsa-miR-548v and hsa-miR-601 with their target genes, PRKACB and ESR1. Further, the concentration of MDA-MB-231-EVs was 3.43 × 109 particles/mL, while that of MCF7-EVs was 2.44 × 109 particles/mL. Validation of miRNA-mRNA signaling components in EVs

To examine whether EV cargo reflects coordinated regulatory organization rather than random RNA export, we performed RT-qPCR analysis of the regulatory miRNA-mRNA axis in MCF7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines under NC and SSC conditions, as well as their corresponding EVs. As shown in Fig. 9A, intracellular hsa-miR-601 abundance was higher in MDA-MB-231 cells under SSC compared to NC; however, this was not reflected in EVs, where hsa-miR-601 abundance was very low, indicating preferential intracellular retention of hsa-miR-601 by TNBC cells. In contrast, MCF7 cell lines exhibited low intracellular abundance of hsa-miR-601 in both SSC and EVs. The low abundance of hsa-miR-601 in EVs relative to cellular levels is consistent with preferential intracellular retention and reduced EV association of this miRNA. In case of hsa-miR-548v, SSC induced an increase in abundance, which was further high in EVs, whereas in MCF7 cells, intracellular abundance was less under SSC, but EVs exhibited abundant hsa-miR-548v levels compared to NC (Fig. 9B). Further, we checked the relative abundance of predicted targets of these miRNAs. Among them, PRKACB exhibited higher abundance in EVs from both cell lines, indicating its packaging as active cargo (Fig. 9C). Although our PPIN analysis identified ESR1 as a hub gene, its abundance was undetectable in our experimental setup. Our results demonstrate that hsa-miR-601, a well-known tumor suppressor in BC, was retained in both cell lines, whereas its target mRNA, PRKACB, along with another regulatory miRNA, hsa-miR-548v, was preferentially enriched in EVs. These non-parallel patterns in cellular abundance and EV loading support the hypothesis that EVs package coordinated signaling networks rather than passively and randomly export intracellular RNA.

Discussion

BC progression is driven by complex intercellular communication mediated by EVs, which serve as regulated carriers of bioactive cargo rather than a cellular waste-disposal system16, 26. EVs helps tumor cells to communicate with other cells by transporting proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. The tumor cells transfer the bioactive cargo via EVs to recipient cells, which may influence the signaling in them, thereby contributing to changes in cellular behavior. The differential expression of miRNAs and their predicted target in the present study, indicates the possibility that these cargos may participate in EV-mediated communication under nutrient-restricted conditions. However, we have not examined the direct EV uptake and cargo transfer in this study.

Although our previous study demonstrated that BC cell-derived EVs are preferentially internalized by their parent cells and enhance proliferation, suggesting the existence of an autologous EV signaling loop.[17] However, the key molecules underlying this phenomenon were unclear. In this study, we combined patient-derived EV miRNA profiling with network-based analyses and experimental validation in luminal and TNBC models to determine whether EV cargo reflects coordinated regulatory signaling networks. This integrated analysis identified PRKACB and ESR1 as central hubs, driven by hsa-miR-548v and hsa-miR-601. An experimental validation revealed selective packaging of hsa-miR-548v and PRKACB in EVs derived from both cell lines. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that miRNAs and other RNAs can be selectively sorted into EVs through interactions with RNA-binding proteins such as hnRNPA2B1, YBX1, and others.12, 27, 28, 29 However, the molecular determinants driving this differential distribution of hsa-miR-548v and hsa-miR-601 were not explored in the present study. The preferential EV enrichment of hsa-miR-548v and the relative retention of hsa-miR-601 within cells, suggests that these miRNAs may be differentially recognized by components of the RNA-sorting machinery. One of the explanations could be the influence of serum starvation that changes the abundance, localization, or RNA-binding activity of specific RBPs, thereby altering the packaging of individual miRNAs. Conversely, miRNAs' sequence- or structure-dependent features may also contribute to their differential recognition. However, the current datasets fail to distinguish among these possibilities; therefore, a direct mechanistic investigation is required. The current study identifies a differential EV cargo phenotype and provides candidate miRNA-mRNA components for further investigation, rather than establishing the upstream molecular machinery responsible for their sorting.

Among the identified regulatory miRNAs, the relative abundance of hsa-miR-548v was higher in EVs than in both cell types. Previous studies reported varied functions of the miR-548 family; some members are tumor suppressors (e.g., miR-548b-5p), while others are oncomiRs (e.g., miR-548j).30, 31 However, the role of hsa-miR-548v is comparatively less explored in BC. Our findings show a high abundance of hsa-miR-548v in EVs despite intracellular variations during serum deprivation in both BC cell lines. Given that hsa-miR-548v is predicted to target PRKACB and ESR1, its selective abundance suggests that EV-mediated export could influence intracellular regulatory balance. Conversely, hsa-miR-601 is well-established as a tumor suppressor in several cancers, including BC. Its downregulation is reported to be associated with increased proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.32, 33, 34, 35 However, our findings show limited sorting into EVs, although starvation altered its intracellular levels. The contrasting EV-associated and intracellular abundances of hsa-miR-548v and hsa-miR-601 support a non-uniform distribution of RNA cargo between BC cells and EVs and are consistent with regulated cargo selection.

In addition to miRNAs, we also assessed the relative abundance of their target mRNAs in EVs. PRKACB encodes the catalytic subunit of protein kinase A (PKA), which is a key regulator of cAMP signaling that controls cellular proliferation, metabolism, and transcriptional regulation. Activation of the cAMP-PKA pathway can further influence downstream signaling pathways, such as the MAPK pathway, in cancer. The elevated relative abundance of PRKACB transcripts in EVs suggests a requirement for proliferative regulation in cells. Although PRKACB expression levels were relatively low across BC subtypes in public datasets, our in silico and in vitro analyses demonstrated its abundance in EVs. Previous studies reported that EV cargo packaging and export is a regulated sorting mechanism that influences donor and recipient cells by altering gene expression.[36] Our findings are consistent with earlier studies demonstrating that EVs selectively export oncogenic mRNAs and miRNAs to promote tumor progression and adaptation.37, 38, 39 Although intracellular miRNA and mRNA levels were altered under serum starvation, we observed that EV-associated transcripts remained elevated, suggesting that EVs may preserve proliferation-associated signaling molecules under stress conditions.

GO and KEGG analyses of the DE-miRNA targets revealed enrichment of the MAPK, PI3K-Akt, and Ras signaling pathways. These pathways regulate proliferation, cell survival, metabolism, and cellular responses to stress. Under nutrient-restricted conditions, BC cells undergo metabolic and signaling adaptations for maintaining cellular homeostasis and survival. Further, in the case of BP, transcription-associated processes, such as the regulation of RNA polymerase II-dependent transcription, suggest that the predicted miRNA targets may participate in broader changes in gene expression regulation. Although these findings are based on enrichment analysis of predicted targets, they provide insight into the implications of key pathways under serum starvation.

Additionally, we observed an important discrepancy for ESR1 between the public transcriptomic analysis and our experimental validation. Despite being identified as a hub gene through the network analysis and showing EV enrichment in the public dataset, ESR1 mRNA was not detectable by RT-qPCR in either the BC cells or their corresponding EVs. This highlights an important limitation of interpreting cross-dataset EV enrichment as directly transferable to specific experimental cell models. Differences in cellular phenotype, culture conditions, transcript abundance, transcript isoforms, and assay platform may all contribute to differences in detection. In addition, hub-gene identification is based on network topology and does not necessarily indicate that the gene will be abundantly expressed or experimentally detectable in every biological model. Therefore, ESR1 should be considered a computationally prioritized candidate in the present study rather than a validated EV-associated transcript. This discrepancy also supports the need for experimental validation of computationally prioritized EV cargo candidates before assigning them a functional role.

The high abundance of hsa-miR-548v and PRKACB transcripts in the EVs indicates their co-enrichment. However, further experimental validation is warranted. Our previous study provided evidence for autologous EV uptake in BC cells; however, uptake was not directly investigated in the present study. Therefore, the possibility that the identified EV-associated miRNAs may be transferred back to parental cells and contribute to intracellular regulatory processes remains a testable hypothesis rather than an experimentally established mechanism in the current study.[17] In this context, PRKACB and hsa-miR-548v in EVs hint toward a potential re-internalization of EVs to reinforce proliferation in parent BC cells. Combining both studies, our findings suggest that BC cells internalize their own EVs, potentially to recycle oncogenic signaling cargo under stress, and that packaging such cargo may meet cells functional requirements for adaptive signaling or intercellular communication. In this way, our findings highlight the importance of studying EV cargo separately from its cellular profiles.

Despite these interesting findings, the present has certain limitations. Firstly, EV characterization was primarily based on NTA. The analysis of EV markers and ultrastructure was not assessed due to a lack of the required facilities and resources. Next, although we have used serum starvation as an experimental model of nutrient stress, we did not employ different starvation durations, graded starvation conditions, or nutrient-restoration experiments. The molecular components responsible for the differential recognition and sorting of hsa-miR-548v and hsa-miR-601 in EVs were not investigated. Further, the direct EV uptake and functional cargo transfer were not examined in the present study. Although our previous work demonstrated autologous EV uptake, the current study did not include EV-tracking or uptake-inhibition experiments. Similarly, direct regulation of PRKACB by hsa-miR-548v or hsa-miR-601 was not confirmed using miRNA manipulation, rescue, or reporter assays. Overall, the present study provides evidence for differential EV-associated RNA profiles under serum-starvation conditions and identifies candidate regulatory relationships, but direct validation of EV identity, cargo sorting, uptake, molecular interaction, and functional consequences is required in future studies.

Conclusion

Overall, this study identifies a differential EV-associated RNA pattern involving hsa-miR-548v and PRKACB in BC cell models under serum-starvation conditions. The contrasting distribution of hsa-miR-548v and hsa-miR-601 between cells and EVs suggests that EV-associated RNA cargo is not uniformly represented and may be subject to regulated cargo selection. EVs act as reservoirs of proliferation-associated regulatory molecules that can reinforce signaling in parent BC cells upon internalization. However, further investigation of the mechanisms governing selective EV cargo loading, their differential distribution and uptake may provide deeper insight into tumor adaptation.
