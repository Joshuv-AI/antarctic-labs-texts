Pseudogap in electron-doped cuprates as a thermal precursor to magnetism
Authors: Emmanouil K. Kokkinis, Andrey V. Chubukov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We study pseudogap behavior in a metal near an antiferromagnetic instability and apply the results to electron-doped cuprates. We associate pseudogap behavior with thermal magnetic fluctuations and compute the fermionic self-energy along the Fermi surface in a weak pseudogap regime, which we justify. We analyze the spectral function as a function of frequency (energy distribution curves, EDC) and momentum (momentum distribution curves, MDC). We show that the EDC display pseudogap behavior with peaks at a finite frequency at all momenta. On the other hand, MDC peaks disperse within the pseudogap, ending at a gossamer Fermi surface. We analyze magnetically-mediated superconductivity and show that in a weak pseudogap regime thermal fluctuations almost cancel out in the gap equation. We favorably compare our results with recent ARPES studies.

Introduction

The origin of pseudogap behavior observed in cuprates and other materials1–12 remains one of the most challenging problems in the physics of strongly correlated electron systems. The existing theories of the pseudogap can be broadly divided into two categories. In the first, pseudogap is viewed as a state with some order, either a conventional one, bilinear in fermions (e.g., a charge-density-wave13–15, loop current16 or a pair density wave17–19), or a topological one, which breaks Luttinger theorem without a conventional order20–25. Within this scenario, pseudogap behavior is the result of order-induced reconstruction of the Fermi surface26. In the second category, pseudogap is viewed as a precursor to either superconductivity27–31 or density-wave order, like antiferromagnetism32–46 or pair-density-wave47. In this scenario, the Fermi surface is not reconstructed and the spectral weight is finite at all Fermi momenta, but a portion of the spectral weight is transformed away from the lowest energies, creating a depletion of the density of states around the Fermi energy.

Theories from both categories have been applied to hole-doped cuprates. There is experimental evidence in favor of a true reconstruction of the Fermi surface (see e.g., ref. 48 and references therein), but some data like Fermi arcs can be interpreted as evidence in favor of a reduction of the spectral weight near the corners of the Brillouin zone without Fermi surface reconstruction (see e.g., refs. 49,50). The data analysis in hole-doped cuprates is further complicated by the presence of strong charge-density-wave correlations, stripes, which replace a commensurate (π, π) magnetism51,52, and B1g phonons53.

The situation looks less complicated in electron-doped (n-type) cuprates, like Nd2−xCexCuO4 (NCCO). Here, a plethora of experimental data shows10,11,54,55 that a magnetic order is commensurate, with momentum Q = (π, π), and it holds up to much larger dopings than for p-type cuprates. This also agrees with previous theoretical analyses34,56,57. Charge-density-wave fluctuations and effects from phonons are weaker, so it is likely that (π, π) magnetic fluctuations play the major role. At small x, the data in the (π, π) ordered state show10,58,59 that electronic excitations are reconstructed into electron pockets around (π, 0) and (0, π). In between, fermionic excitations are gapped. At x = 0.15, which is close to the end point of an antiferromagnetic order, angle resolved photoemission (ARPES) data show superconductivity at T ≈ 25 K and pseudogap behavior at higher temperatures60–63, i.e., superconductivity emerges out of a pseudogap state.

From theory perspective, the dominance of (π, π) magnetic fluctuations still allows two scenarios for the pseudogap: a topological order, whose effect on the electrons mimics that from a true (π, π) order and gives rise to Fermi surface reconstruction into small electron and hole pockets26, and a precursor to antiferromagnetism32–46.

In this communication we consider the precursor scenario. We conjecture that pseudogap emerges due to thermal antiferromagnetic fluctuations—the ones that destroy long-range antiferromagnetic order in quasi-2D systems at temperature TN well below the exchange energy J, and consider how thermal magnetic fluctuations above TN affect fermionic spectral function, and how spectral weight redistribution in the normal state affects superconductivity. We perform controlled perturbative calculations in a weak pseudogap regime, where the pseudogap is smaller than vF ξ −1(T), where ξ(T) is a magnetic correlation length.

One of our goals is to understand the results of the recent extensive ARPES study of NCCO at x = 0.15 by Xu et al.60 (see also ref. 64). The authors of60 collected ARPES data along several cuts parallel to the Brillouin zone diagonal (see Fig. 1) and extracted from the data the fermionic spectral function A(k, ω) as a function of frequency at a fixed momentum (Energy Distribution Curves (EDC)) and as a function of momentum at a fixed frequency (Momentum Distribution Curves (MDC)). This study found two fundamental features. First, above the superconducting Tc, data show that the maximum of the EDC spectra remains at a finite frequency at all momenta k, including those where the Fermi surface would be located in the absence of antiferromagnetic fluctuations (we label these momenta as kF). Xu et al. interpreted this result as evidence for Fermi surface reconstruction without a (π, π) order. At the same time, the peak in the MDC data in each cut disperses inside the EDC gap and at vanishing frequency is located very close to kF. This is what one would expect in a metal with an unreconstructed Fermi surface (gossamer Fermi surface, in the terminology of Xu et al.). Second, below Tc, Xu et al. found a d—wave superconducting gap, with a maximum at the hot spot k = kh (one of 8 Fermi surface points for which kh + Q is also on the Fermi surface), where the suppression of the low-energy spectral weight in the EDC data is the largest. We argue that both sets of results are reproduced within the precursor scenario based on thermal antiferromagnetic fluctuations35,38,44,44. We recall that within this scenario, Fermi surface is not reconstructed, but some portion of the spectral weight gets transferred to a finite frequency. We show that in the magnetic pseudogap phase, the EDC spectral function Ak(ω) has a maximum at a finite frequency, which is the largest at kF in each cut. This gives rise to re-entrant behavior of the dispersion extracted from EDC, when k varies through kF. The largest density of states depletion among all cuts is at a hot spot kh. Both results are consistent with the data in ref. 60. Next, we show that MDC spectral function Aω(k) taken at a frequency within the gap, has a peak, and the peak position disperses towards kF as ω gets smaller. This is again consistent with60. We argue that such behavior is a generic feature of a precursor regime, where in a finite time interval, probed by EDC, the system behaves in the same way as if it had a long-range SDW order, while over a larger time, probed by MDC at small ω, the system visits different ordered states and displays the averaged behavior, consistent with a metal. Finally, we analyze superconductivity out of a pseudogap state and argue that there is no paradox. Namely, we show that thermal fluctuations almost cancel out in the gap equation in a manner similar to cancellation of non-magnetic impurities. This effect has been discussed before within the Eliashberg theory which assumes a factorization of momentum integration between bosons and fermions65. We show that it holds if we compute the one-loop self-energy exactly, without factorization. As a result, thermal depletion of the low-energy spectral weight near a hot spot does not affect the angular dependence of the superconducting gap, which remains nearly the same as if only quantum (π, π) fluctuations were present. In the last case, at weak and moderate couplings, superconducting gap has the largest value at a hot spot66. This is fully consistent with the data in ref. 60.

Results

Thermal antiferromagnetic fluctuations

The input for our studies is the work by Ye et al.44,45, which in turn is based on earlier works by Vilk, Tremblay and Senechal32–34. Ye et al. considered fermions with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t,{t}^{{\prime} }$$\end{document}t,t′ dispersion, bandwidth W = 8t and Hubbard interaction U ≥ W, and analyzed the effect of thermal antiferromagnetic fluctuations for a fermion at a hot spot in a temperature range between a low T < TN, when long-range (π, π) order is present in a quasi-2D material, and higher T > TN, when magnetic order disappears. They argued that there are two distinct pseudogap regimes at T > TN, which they identified as strong pseudogap and weak pseudogap. In the strong pseudogap regime, the pseudogap scale ΔPG is set by Mott physics and is approximately U. Simultaneously, the chemical potential strongly shifts from its free-Fermion value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu }_{0} \sim {t}^{{\prime} }$$\end{document}μ0~t′ to μ = − U/2 + O(J) (J = 4t2/U), such that both EDC and MDC ARPES intensities (the products of the spectral functions and the Fermi function nF(ω)) have peaks at ω ~ − J. To describe this regime diagrammatically, one needs to sum up an infinite series of terms for the fermionic self-energy going beyond self-consistent one-loop, and an infinite set of terms for the magnetic polarization35,36,38,44,44. A weak pseudogap behavior emerges when λth exceeds a threshold λth,c ≈ 0.5, but is not large (this has to be verified in explicit calculations at a given λth > λth,c). Strong pseudogap behavior in turn holds for large λth. For dopings x ≤ xc (the end point of an antiferromagnetic order), strong pseudogap behavior holds only in a narrow T range immediately above TN(x), while the bulk of the pseudogap range falls into a weak pseudogap regime.

The data from60 for NCCO at x = 0.15 show that (i) the pseudogap energy ΔPG is below 100 meV, smaller than vFξ−1(T) ≥ 100 meV, and (ii) the gossamer Fermi surface crosses the magnetic Brillouin zone boundary approximately at the same kh as for free fermions. The combination of these two facts strongly indicates that the data is obtained in the weak pseudogap regime. We assume that this is the case and compute EDC and MDC spectral functions within one-loop approximation along six cuts parallel to the Brillouin zone diagonal shown in Fig. 1. Specifically, we compute the thermal self-energy Σth(k, ω) as the convolution of the static antiferromagnetic propagator χ(q + Q) ∝ 1/(q2 + ξ−2(T)) and a propagator of a free fermion G0(k + q + Q, ω). We emphasize that we must use the bare fermionic Green’s function rather than the full one (i.e., use perturbative rather than self-consistent one-loop approximation), because for thermal fluctuations self-energy and vertex correction insertions have to be treated on equal footings. In the weak pseudogap regime, both corrections can be neglected, in the strong pseudogap regime, both have to be included32–36,44,67,68. We explicitly verified (see Supplementary Note 2) that in our case (λth = 0.85) the two-loop diagrams with self-energy insertion into the propagator of an intermediate fermion (a member of self-consistent one-loop series) and with a vertex correction almost cancel each other, i.e., a perturbative one-loop approximation is quite accurate.

For a more accurate treatment, one should keep the quantum self-energy. The latter is well described within Eliashberg theory and predominantly depends on frequency, Σqm ≈ Σqm(ω) (see, e.g., ref. 70). At small frequencies, ω − Σqm(ω) ≈ ω(1 + λqm). This renormalization rescales frequency, but other than this the results remain the same as without Σqm(ω). We note in this regard that even at strong coupling, quantum self-energy for a dispersing boson (our case) gives rise to an incoherent non-Fermi liquid behavior, but does not give rise to pseudogap behavior. We re-iterate that to obtain λth,c and the pseudogap behavior at λth > λth,c, we need to use perturbative rather than self-consistent one-loop approximation. We discuss Σth(k, ω) in more detail in Supplementary Notes 1 and 2. In Supplementary Note 1, we obtain Σth(k, ω) analytically in a self-consistent one-loop approximation and show that then the spectral function does not display a pseudogap behavior at any λth. In Supplementary Note 2 we compute Σth(k, ω) at two-loop order by combining the contributions from the diagram with the self-energy of an intermediate fermion (part of diagrammatic series for a self-consistent one-loop) and the diagram with the vertex correction. We show that the two almost cancel each other at small frequencies and hence have almost zero effect on the value of λth,c. An indication of some cancellation between self-energy and vertex corrections has also previously been identified in ref. 71, where the authors analyze the pseudogap phase in the two-dimensional Hubbard model using an extended two-particle self-consistent approximation. For completeness, in Supplementary Note 3 we discuss full series for the thermal self-energy and show that vertex correction diagrams are essential for the description of the pseudogap behavior. Superconductivity

We next discuss superconductivity out of a pseudogap phase. We recall that Xu et al. found the largest value of a superconducting gap at a hot spot, where the spectral weight above Tc is mostly depleted, and called this a paradox of spin-mediated pairing. We argue that within our theory these results are actually the expected ones as thermal fluctuations almost cancel out in the equation for the pairing vertex (the gap equation for brevity), such that the reduction of the low-energy spectral weight induced by thermal spin fluctuations does not influence the momentum dependence of a superconducting gap along the Fermi surface. Without thermal fluctuations, the pairing gap has a maximum at a hot spot66,74,75.

A near-cancellation of the contributions from thermal fluctuations have been reported before65,66,76–80, for the gap equation obtained within the Eliashberg approximation, when one factorizes the momentum integration between fermions and the pairing boson (the exact cancellation holds when one additionally neglect the momentum dependence of the pairing vertex near a hot spot). The physical argument is that for spin-singlet pairing, thermal fluctuations mimic non-magnetic impurities, and their near-cancellation is essentially the realization of the Anderson theorem81. In our case, it is crucial that thermal self-energy is obtained by performing the momentum integration exactly, without factorization. Still, we demonstrate that thermal self-energy nearly cancels out.

We measure the pairing vertex Φ(k, ω) in units of vFξ−1, like other variables and measure momentum in units of ξ−1. We consider the momentum right at the hot spot, k = kh, where the depletion of the spectral weight above Tc is the largest. To simplify the presentation, we first neglect quantum self-energy, as we did before, and consider the linearized gap equation for infinitesimally small Φ(k, ω). We then show that the near-cancellation of the thermal contribution holds when we include Σqm and analyze a non-linear gap equation.

Discussion

In this communication, we reported the results of our study of the pseudogap behavior in a metal near an antiferromagnetic instability under the assumption that pseudogap behavior is caused by thermal magnetic fluctuations. We specifically analyzed weak pseudogap regime (pseudogap energy is smaller than vFξ−1), where we computed fermionic self-energy along the Fermi surface. We demonstrated that EDC and MDC spectral functions behave differently: EDC displays a pseudogap behavior with peaks at a finite frequency at all momenta, which gives rise to a depletion of the density of states at the lowest energies, the largest one at a hot spot. At the same time, MDC peaks disperse within the pseudogap, and end up at zero frequency at a gossamer Fermi surface, which is close to the Fermi surface of free fermions. We analyzed magnetic-mediated superconductivity and showed that thermal fluctuations almost cancel out in the gap equation despite the fact that the thermal self-energy is obtained without factorization of the momentum integration between fermions and spin fluctuations. As the consequence, the gap function retains the same form as if thermal fluctuations were absent and has a maximum at a hot spot. Our results reproduce a number of features found the in the ARPES study of NCCO at x = 0.15 (ref. 60). The reduction of the spectral weight at small energies should also affect transport properties of electron-doped cuprates. Given a good agreement of our results with ARPES, we believe that the one-loop thermal self-energy can be also used for the computation of transport characteristics, e.g., optical resistivity.

An intriguing question is whether the same scenario applies to hole-doped cuprates. These systems are certainly more correlated than electron-doped cuprates, and the pseudogap energy at small hole doping is larger than vFξ−1, which places weakly doped hole cuprates into a strong pseudogap regime. The results of some numerical studies39–43,46 have been interpreted as evidence for pseudogap induced by thermal spin fluctuations. The analytical study, however found44,45 that a strong pseudogap behavior it is not guaranteed to hold if the ground state is not antiferromagnetically ordered. An alternative scenario for a pseudogap, supported by magnetotransport measurements82, is that it is a phase with a topological order and full reconstruction of the Fermi surface into pockets (see ref. 83 for recent detailed review of this and other scenarios for pseudogap in hole-doped cuprates). More studies are needed to settle on the origin of pseudogap in hole-doped cuprates.
