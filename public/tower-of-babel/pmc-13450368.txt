Continuous contributions of the dorsolateral striatum to movement initiation and execution
Authors: Jiaming Cao, Yujie Zhao, Jianing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spikes in the dorsolateral striatum (DLS) of the basal ganglia (BG) correlate with forelimb and whole-body movements, but the extent to which they contribute to movement remains unclear. Using behavior-timed optogenetic inhibition in rats performing a lever-release reaction-time task, we found that DLS photoinhibition delayed the initiation of forelimb reaching and lever release; when delivered during ongoing reaching, it also interrupted execution. Inhibition during reward retrieval impaired locomotion, especially turning, reducing movement speed and resulting in less efficient trajectories. By contrast, inhibition before the reward-triggering nose poke had little effect despite substantial preceding DLS activity. These effects were accompanied by bidirectional changes in firing rates in the substantia nigra pars reticulata, a BG output nucleus, and were much weaker when the dorsomedial striatum was perturbed. Together, these findings suggest that execution of many, but not all, learned movement patterns depends continuously on DLS activity.

INTRODUCTION

Behavior has been defined as the total movement produced by an animal (1). Learned movement sequences, in particular, involve repeated execution of actions with high precision, low variability, and speed. Despite fine-scale dissections of the brain areas that may contribute to movement generation—including neural circuits in the cortex and basal ganglia (BG) (2–4), the roles of different neural structures in producing movements, and whether these roles generalize across movement types, remain unresolved (5). The dorsal striatum, the major input structure of the BG, is widely implicated in the learning and execution of procedural skills (6–8). Striatal neural activity correlates strongly with behavioral events during procedural tasks and changes with learning (9–13). Proposed roles of the striatum include action selection (14–16), encoding kinematic parameters of movements (17, 18), controlling movement vigor (i.e., speed and force) (13, 19–21), and storing or chunking motor sequences when behaviors become automatic (9, 10, 22).

Although striatal spikes have been functionally correlated with various behaviors, the extent to which they influence movement execution remains elusive—particularly whether they contribute moment-to-moment, and whether any movement phase or type exists where they are dispensable. Furthermore, prior work using lesions or perturbations has typically focused either on precisely controlled forelimb movements (2, 12, 19, 23–27) or on whole-body locomotion (13, 20, 27, 28) but rarely on both within the same study. Whether striatal activity contributes uniformly across movement types therefore remains unclear.

To fill this gap, we performed striatal optogenetic inhibition across multiple phases of a procedural task involving both forelimb and whole-body movements: a simple reaction time (SRT) task in which rats performed a conditioned, tone-cued lever release for reward (21, 29, 30). Rats pressed and held a lever, released it upon hearing a trigger tone, and then made a whole-body locomotor movement (including turning) to the reward port on the opposite wall, followed by an operant nose poke for water—both forelimb and whole-body movements being learned operant responses chained into a motor sequence. We first characterized how these movement types were represented in the dorsolateral striatum (DLS) using chronic multisite recordings, including Neuropixels probes; we then optogenetically activated inhibitory interneurons to suppress striatal projection neurons and examined how behavior-coupled DLS photoinhibition affected movement initiation and execution; last, recording from the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr), a BG output nucleus, during DLS photoinhibition let us link these behavioral effects to changes in SNr activity. Our results show that event-coupled DLS spikes influence execution of both forelimb and whole-body movements—delaying or interrupting forelimb actions and producing slower, less efficient whole-body locomotion—whereas comparable dorsomedial striatum (DMS) perturbations have only minor effects.

RESULTS

Event-locked DLS activity across SRT task phases

Rats were trained to perform an SRT task (21, 29–33). In a single session, the rat moved between a spring-loaded lever and a reward port in a custom behavioral chamber (Fig. 1A; fig. S1, D and E; and movie S1). To start a trial (Fig. 1B), the rat was required to voluntarily initiate a lever press, without any external cue, and hold it for a variable duration (750 or 1500 ms), known as the foreperiod (FP). At the end of the FP, a brief, 250-ms tone sounded, and the rat released the lever within a 600-ms response window to obtain a reward. Premature or late responses were not rewarded. The reward port was located on the opposite side of the behavioral box, where a nose poke triggered water drops after a correct lever release. Well-trained rats exhibited stable, reactive lever releases across sessions (Fig. 1C), with low premature responses and a rapid increase in response probability immediately after the stimulus (fig. S1A). Rats formed stereotypical locomotor patterns in the operant box, moving between the reward port and the lever (Fig. 1D). Forelimb reaches were also stereotyped. After training, rats (n = 26) released the lever in response to the trigger tone. The median correct response ratio was 73.1% [interquartile range (IQR): 70.1 to 79.7%] for short FP trials (750 ms) and 68.4% (IQR: 61.1 to 70.6%) for long FP trials (1500 ms) (fig. S1B). The median reaction time was 0.34 s (IQR: 0.27 to 0.37 s) for short FP trials and 0.29 s (0.25 to 0.33 s) for long FP trials (fig. S1C).

We performed chronic multichannel recordings in the DLS of 10 rats using microwire arrays or silicon probes, of which two were implanted with Neuropixels probes (Fig. 1E). Units with low interspike interval (ISI) violations were analyzed (see the “Inclusion criteria” in Materials and Methods). Both wide-spike (0.26 ± 0.07 ms, mean ± SD, n = 673) and narrow-spike (0.11 ± 0.06 ms, n = 102) waveforms were observed (Fig. 1, F to H). We focused our analysis on the wide-waveform units (labeled in green in Fig. 1, F and G), a population dominated by putative spiny projection neurons (SPNs). To further exclude non-SPNs, we retained only wide-waveform units that fired phasically, defined as units in which the cumulative duration of ISIs > 2 s exceeded 30% of total recording time (n = 548; Fig. 1I) (34). The remaining wide- or narrow-waveform units likely originated from interneurons or en passant axons (34–36).

A generalized linear model (GLM) with task events as predictors (Eq. 1) identified 478 putative SPNs (87.2%) whose firing was significantly modulated by at least one task event (Fig. 1, J to L), including lever approach/press, lever holding, trigger/release, and reward retrieval (example units in Fig. 1J). Most task-modulated SPNs (455 of 478, 95.2%) showed task-related increases in firing rate (Fig. 1K).

To capture the modulation of population activity by behavior, we assembled a pseudopopulation using the soft-normalized average firing rates of 478 task-modulated putative SPN units and performed principal components analysis (PCA; Materials and Methods). Firing rates were partitioned into two nonoverlapping segments: (i) from lever approach (2.5 s before press) to lever release and (ii) from lever release to 1 s after nose poke (during reward consumption). A separate PCA subspace was constructed for each segment. For visualization (Fig. 1M), we projected population activity onto the top 4 principal components (PCs) of each subspace, which together captured 73.1 and 76.0% of the variance in the press/release and retrieval/poke subspaces, respectively. Individual PCs typically encoded overlapping combinations of behavioral features: within the press/release subspace, lever approach and forepaw lifting (PCs 2 and 3), holding (PCs 1 to 3), and release (PCs 1 to 4); and within the retrieval/poke subspace, modulation peaking near the nose poke (PCs 1 to 3). Using thresholds on the PC loading coefficients (Materials and Methods), we selected units that contributed substantially to each PC. These units exhibited average firing patterns tied to specific behavioral events: lifting/lever press (L), holding (H), trigger/release (T), and reward retrieval (R) (Fig. 1, N and O, and fig. S2, B and C). To compare the two subspaces in a basis-independent way, we computed the four principal angles between them (37): 56°, 65°, 86°, and 89°. The first two principal directions showed partial alignment between the two behavioral segments, likely reflecting a shared orienting component (toward the lever or the reward port), while the remaining two were essentially orthogonal. Together, these results indicate that largely distinct DLS neuron subsets are recruited across behavioral phases, spanning forelimb movements during the SRT task and whole-body movements during reward retrieval.

DLS activity tracks task structure and motor kinematics

Using chronic Neuropixels probes, we observed sequential recruitment of DLS neurons tracking task events among simultaneously recorded units within single sessions (rat 1, fig. S3, A to F; rat 2, fig. S3, G to L). In an example session from rat 1, a total of 59 putative SPNs were recorded. Of these, 44 units were modulated by behavioral events, the majority of which (40, 90.9%) showed positive modulation. These units were modulated according to the task’s trial structure, with distinct behavior-relevant units intermixed along the dorsal-to-ventral axis of the striatum (fig. S3A). These neurons exhibited repeated phasic activation across trials (fig. S3, B and D; see movie S1), which was coupled to the animal’s movements, including selective firing during lever approach, reaching, and reward retrieval (fig. S3, E and F). Similar patterns were observed in a session from a different rat (rat 2, fig. S3, G to L, and movie S1).

To examine the coupling between DLS activity and motor kinematics at a fine timescale, we used Poisson GLMs (Eqs. 3 and 4, and Fig. 2) (17). In two rats implanted with Neuropixels probes, we analyzed side- and top-view videos from nine sessions and selected 259 unique single units (150 units from five sessions in rat 1 in fig. S3, A to F, and 109 units from four sessions in rat 2 in fig. S3, G to L). We applied a cross-day neuron-matching method to identify recurrences of the same neuron across sessions and included each neuron only once to ensure that the 259 units were distinct (38).

We constructed two complementary encoding models. In the side-view model (Fig. 2, A to E), spikes were fitted using kinematic features from the contralateral forepaw (contralateral to the implant) and from one ear (used as a proxy for head position). Both positional state variables and movement-related action variables were included. To capture nonlinear relationships between body position and spike rate, positions were represented over two-dimensional spatial bins spanning the field of view (Fig. 2C), such that for each 30-ms time bin, the body part position was assigned to a single spatial bin (1 for the occupied bin, 0 otherwise) (39). Movement was described by horizontal and vertical velocity components together with absolute speed. Because a minimal number of 100 spikes was required for modeling, only 122 of the 259 units were fitted; the remaining units did not fire enough spikes for reliable model fitting (Fig. 2J). Trial-shuffled controls were constructed to examine whether behavioral stereotypy alone contributes to apparent spike-kinematics coupling (Fig. 2J).

The fitted coefficients revealed different forms of tuning across units (fig. S4, A to D and I to M). Across units, positional variables generally explained variance better than action variables alone (Fig. 2J). We used pseudo-R2 (pR2) as the goodness-of-fit metric (Eqs. 5 and 6 and Materials and Methods). Units with pR2 > 0.15 in the action-only or state-only models were mainly those that fired during the lever-approaching and paw-lifting phases (Fig. 2P).

The above models (Fig. 2J) were based on zero-lag relationships using behavioral variables at the same time point as neural activity. To examine the temporal relationship between DLS spiking and kinematics, we introduced temporal offsets by shifting spike times relative to behavioral variables (40). Positive lags correspond to neural activity preceding kinematics, consistent with a role in driving motor responses (41), whereas negative lags indicate activity following behavior, consistent with a role in reflecting behavioral consequences, such as somatosensory feedback. For each unit, we performed 10 repeated train/test splits. Within each split, we fit the model (full = state + action, state-only, or action-only) across a predefined set of candidate lags (±30, ±60, …, ±240 ms) and evaluated performance on held-out test data. This allowed us to identify the preferred time lag for each unit (Fig. 2K) and construct an average tuning curve for each model type (Fig. 2L). On average, tuning curves were centered near zero lag, with no systematic bias toward positive or negative lags.

We selected units for which the zero-lag (action-only or state-only) model performed relatively well (pR2 > 0.15) and plotted their spike density functions (SDFs) together with their lag-dependent tuning curves (Fig. 2P, center and right). Individual units exhibited diverse lag-dependent profiles, which collectively produced the near-zero–centered population average shown in Fig. 2L. Units that were coupled to lever press—that is, those exhibiting transient firing preceding the press, such as units 1 and 2 in Fig. 2B—tended to show peak model performance at positive lags (arrows in Fig. 2P), suggesting that their activity is more strongly correlated with future kinematics.

Notably, this spike-kinematics lead relationship does not necessarily imply movement initiation. In our task, many behavioral transitions involve brief changes in velocity or direction, such as a transient forelimb acceleration followed by deceleration before lever press or turning during reward retrieval, which often consists of a brief increase in lateral speed followed by a subsequent increase in forward movement. Thus, positive-lag activity is consistent with a role in shaping upcoming kinematic changes, rather than initiating movement per se.

We performed a similar analysis using head kinematics from top-view videos (Fig. 2, F to I). In these models, regressors included state variables such as head location and head direction and action variables such as forward and lateral head motion and speed. These variables jointly predicted DLS spiking (219 units; Fig. 2, F to I and M). Models with state-only variables (head location and head angle) were sufficient to predict spikes of a subset of units (76 units, pR2 > 0.15; Fig. 2Q). Action-only models predicted the spikes in a smaller subset of units (35 units), particularly those that were locked to the trigger-release period, corresponding to modulation of DLS activity by head movement (i.e., turning toward the port during lever release; see unit 3 in fig. S4, N to Q).

State-only models also captured the spiking patterns of many units that fired during the reward retrieval period (e.g., units near 60 to 76 in the state model in Fig. 2Q), whereas action-only models did not (Fig. 2Q). Thus, activity during reward retrieval is not well explained by movement variables alone but is better captured by variables related to body configuration and spatial context.

A similar time-shift analysis was performed on top-view models (Fig. 2, N and O). The time-lag preference revealed a slight bias toward positive lags, particularly for action models. Consistent with this, units that were coupled to the trigger-to-release period showed right-shifted pR2 tuning curves (e.g., units near 20 in the action model in Fig. 2Q).

Overall, a subset of DLS neurons exhibited activity coupled to ongoing kinematics—both action- and state-related—throughout the task (13, 17, 42), with some units showing activity that preceded kinematic changes around critical time points such as lever reaching or release.

In freely moving animals, behavior is often stereotyped, producing strong correlations among movements of different body parts (43)—e.g., downward head tilting coupled with forepaw reaching or lever release coinciding with head turning—making it difficult to disentangle whether DLS spikes reflect a single body part’s movement or coordinated whole-body posture (44). Nevertheless, the close correspondence between DLS spiking and movement/body state does not establish whether these spikes are functionally involved in movement. Prior lesion studies show behavioral deficits (17, 20, 21) but cannot attribute them to specific activity patterns. We therefore performed acute, millisecond-timescale, closed-loop optogenetic inactivation of the DLS.

Optogenetic inhibition of the striatum by activating interneurons

The striatum contains local inhibitory interneurons that regulate the flow of information through its circuits (45, 46). Activating these interneurons rapidly suppresses SPNs within 2 to 3 ms (47, 48). To suppress task-related phasic activation of DLS neurons, we used an inhibitory interneuron-specific enhancer, mDlx, to introduce Channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) into striatal inhibitory interneurons (Fig. 3A) (49, 50). The mDlx enhancer drives inhibitory interneuron-selective expression in the striatum with high fidelity (50). After injecting a recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) encoding mDlx and ChR2 into the DLS, colabeling between Foxp1-expressing neurons [an SPN-specific marker (51)] and green fluorescent protein (GFP)–expressing neurons was minimal: 25 of the 1813 GFP+ neurons (1.4%) expressed Foxp1, while 2 of the 3526 Foxp1+ neurons (<0.1%) expressed GFP (data from two rats, 18 slices). To validate this approach physiologically, we performed in vivo optrode recordings from three rats implanted with 16-channel opto-microwire arrays (Fig. 3B). We performed electrophysiological recordings while rats engaged in the SRT task and delivered laser pulse trains (40 Hz, 5-ms pulse width, 2200-ms duration) to the DLS at random task phases in a small proportion of trials (<25%), such as during lever approach or lever press. Putative inhibitory interneuron units were activated by laser pulses, showing elevated firing rates throughout the stimulation period (Fig. 3, C to E). Some units (e.g., unit 3 in Fig. 3D) exhibited more complex patterns, likely caused by interactions between inhibitory interneurons [see (52)].

Most units were suppressed during stimulation, with latencies of less than 10 ms [note that measurable membrane potential hyperpolarization occurred around 3 to 4 ms using a similar approach in the cortex (53)]. In recordings during SRT behavior, the behavior-related activation was reduced (PhotoInh/NoStim spike rate ratio: median = 0.09, IQR: 0.03 to 0.32, n = 96 units with NoStim spike rate > 0.5 Hz; Fig. 3, F and G).

To further characterize the spatial and temporal extent of inhibition, we combined Neuropixels recordings with optogenetics in one rat performing a simplified press-release-poke task without a FP or reaction-time requirement (Fig. 3H). Striatal units within the fluorescence region were nearly completely suppressed, those outside its perimeter only partially so, and cortical units were unaffected. Suppression persisted throughout the stimulation period, including for later events such as reward-port poking (Fig. 3, I to K). These results confirm the spatial specificity and temporal persistence of inhibition, letting us define its range from post-mortem histology (fig. S5, A and B) and interpret the behavioral effects below. Photoinhibition duration was fixed at 2200 ms to span the targeted behavioral events; we did not analyze post-offset behavior to avoid rebound-activity confounds. Optogenetic experiments are summarized in tables S1 and S2.

DLS inhibition delays forelimb reaching

To examine how DLS inhibition affects the initiation and execution of a lever press, we installed an infrared sensor above the lever. Once triggered, the sensor initiated delivery of a 2200-ms, 40-Hz pulse train (5-ms pulses) to the DLS in a small proportion (10%) of trials (Fig. 4A). As shown in the example, bilateral DLS photoinhibition (PhotoInh) delayed movement initiation compared to uninhibited trials (NoStim) (Fig. 4, B to D, and see movie S2; more examples in fig. S5, C to H). The press ratio, defined as the proportion of trials in which the rat pressed the lever within a 2200-ms window after approach detection, decreased by 0.141 [median paired difference; 95% confidence interval (CI): [0.100, 0.185]; p < 0.001, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 rats; Fig. 4G].

We measured the time from approach detection to lever press (ta2p) and fitted a linear mixed-effect (LME) model (Eq. 7) with stimulation type (StimType: NoStim or PhotoInh) as a fixed effect. The model included random intercepts and random slopes for StimType by rat to account for individual variability. Bilateral DLS photoinhibition significantly increased ta2p (β1 = 0.83 ± 0.09 s, mean ± SEM, t24 = 9.42, P < 0.001, n = 25 rats; Fig. 4H). When successfully initiated, lever-reaching trajectories during PhotoInh resembled those in NoStim trials (Fig. 4, B, C, and E). To assess forepaw movement speed, in a subset of rats with unobstructed forepaw tracking (n = 16), we fitted an LME model with forepaw speed as the response and StimType as a fixed effect (Eq. 8). The estimated marginal mean speed was 27.07 ± 1.02 cm/s for NoStim (mean ± SEM), comparable to 26.02 ± 1.15 cm/s for PhotoInh. The coefficient (β1) for StimType was −1.05 ± 0.98 cm/s (t14 = −1.07, P = 0.301), suggesting a small and inconsistent population-level effect of DLS photoinhibition on forepaw speed.

For each trial, we partitioned ta2p into two durations: from approach to lift peak (ta2pk) and from lift peak to lever press (tpk2p). We then fitted an LME model to assess the interaction between the effect of photoinhibition (StimType) and duration type (DurationType; Eq. 9). A strong StimType × DurationType interaction was observed (β3 = −0.97 ± 0.02 s, t8127 = −51.81, P < 0.001, n = 16 rats), indicating that photoinhibition affected these two durations differently. The increase in ta2p (0.83 ± 0.05 s) was entirely attributable to the increase in ta2pk (0.90 ± 0.03 s), but not the subsequent tpk2p (−0.07 ± 0.03 s; Fig. 4J). Lever movement was monitored with a piezo sensor, and lever position was reconstructed (Fig. 4F). Once aligned to the highest paw position, lever movements were indistinguishable between NoStim and PhotoInh trials (Fig. 4F), suggesting that similar force was applied to the lever. Thus, DLS photoinhibition delayed forepaw lifting. The effect persisted over repeated stimulation, with modest adaptation (∼15% reduction; first 60 stimulations: Δta2p=0.84 s, 95% CI [0.66, 1.01]; subsequent stimulations: 0.71 s, [0.54, 0.88]; n = 21 rats).

The eventual recovery of forepaw lifting during ongoing photoinhibition could reflect recovery of DLS spike rates or compensatory activity elsewhere. The former is unlikely, given the robust inhibition from activating local interneurons (Fig. 3, J and K): During optrode recordings, two units with prelifting activity remained suppressed at the time of paw lifting (fig. S6, A to H).

The results above were obtained with bilateral DLS photoinhibition. In 16 rats, we tested unilateral photoinhibition (contralateral or ipsilateral to the lever-pressing paw) and compared its effects with those of bilateral photoinhibition. Press ratio was analyzed in all 16 rats; ta2p and paw trajectories were analyzed in a subset of 10 rats with paw tracking (Eq. 10 and fig. S7, A to E). Unilateral photoinhibition also significantly decreased the press ratio and increased ta2p relative to NoStim. Overall, bilateral photoinhibition had the strongest effect, followed by contralateral and then ipsilateral inhibition. These findings support a role for the DLS in initiating the lever-pressing actions, suggesting that phasic prelift DLS activity contributes functionally to movement initiation (e.g., unit 3 in Fig. 2, B to I and unit 2 in fig. S4, B to E).

DLS inhibition interrupts ongoing execution of forelimb reaching

From the perspective of action selection (14, 16), forelimb lever reaching can be viewed as a single action chosen among alternatives, such as standing still, turning away, or using the contralateral paw. A subset of units fired selectively during the lever-reaching process, which reflects encoding of movement kinematics (e.g., units 1 and 2 in Fig. 2, B and E and unit 1 in fig. S4B). We next asked whether DLS photoinhibition affects the ongoing execution of a lever-reaching movement once it has been initiated.

To achieve this, we implemented real-time, high-speed video-based pose detection with online estimation computed within 10 ms per frame at 50 Hz (Fig. 5A) (54, 55). In a subset (10%) of trials, paw lift triggered bilateral DLS photoinhibition. Lift-triggered and approach-triggered photoinhibition trials were often interleaved within the same session.

As shown in the example, DLS photoinhibition at lift onset interrupted reach execution, causing the paw to drop and then hover mid-air for variable durations (Fig. 5, B to D, and see movie S3). The paw-drop ratio increased by 0.499 (median paired difference; 95% CI: [0.353, 0.683]; P < 0.001, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 16 rats; Fig. 5G). Rats often reinitiated reaching during PhotoInh (Fig. 5, C to E), but with a slight speed reduction (10.6 ± 5.9% decrease, mean ± SEM; Fig. 5H), likely reflecting mid-trajectory restarts. Despite these interruptions, once reaching resumed, subsequent lever pressing was largely unimpaired (Fig. 5F).

As above, we partitioned the lift-to-press duration (tl2p) into the lift-to-lift-peak (tl2pk) and lift-peak-to-press (tpk2p) segments and fitted an LME model (Eq. 12). The model revealed a significant StimType × DurationType interaction (β3 = −0.27 ± 0.02 s, mean ± SEM, t5444 = −12.55, P < 0.001), indicating that photoinhibition affected the two durations differently. Photoinhibition increased tl2p (0.42 ± 0.08 s; Eq. 11) primarily because of a delay in tl2pk (0.34 ± 0.04 s), with only a small effect on tpk2p (0.07 ± 0.04 s) (Fig. 5I).

The results above were based on bilateral DLS photoinhibition. Compared with bilateral photoinhibition, unilateral photoinhibition had weaker effects on the paw-drop rate and on tl2p (Eq. 13 and fig. S7, F to J). Relative to the lifting paw, contralateral photoinhibition had a larger effect than ipsilateral photoinhibition.

DLS inhibition prolongs tone-triggered lever release

A prominent component of DLS neural dynamics was associated with lever-release responses following the trigger stimulus (e.g., trigger-release units in Figs. 1N and 6, A and C, and fig. S2B). This trigger/release-aligned activity was stronger during correct, trigger-instructed lever releases than during self-initiated releases in the absence of the trigger (Fig. 6, A and C). Self-initiated releases included anticipatory (premature) responses and responses during a small (∼10%) subset of “probe” trials in which no trigger stimulus was presented. In probe trials, lever release was neither rewarded nor penalized.

Among DLS units that exhibited increased activity following the trigger cue (hereafter, trigger-responsive units; median onset = 175 ms; IQR: 125 to 275 ms; n = 51; Fig. 6E), 27 units (53%) showed significantly stronger activity during trigger-instructed responses than during self-initiated responses (Fig. 6, F to H). Units without significant differences did not form an obvious timing-defined subgroup, as both significantly and nonsignificantly modulated units showed distributed peri-release activity patterns (Fig. 6, F and G). Nonsignificant units should not be interpreted as having identical activity across trial types: The significance criterion required a same-direction difference (larger responses in instructed trials) across at least three consecutive 50-ms bins within a window spanning 600 ms before to 200 ms after release (Materials and Methods). These results indicate that a substantial subset of trigger-responsive DLS units distinguishes trigger-instructed from self-initiated actions, even when activity is aligned to lever release in both trial types (56, 57).

Notably, action-only top-view models accounted for some of the firing-rate variance in these units (pR2 values for units 1 to 4 in Fig. 6, A and C: 0.13, 0.30, 0.40, and 0.09, respectively). In this task, correct trigger-instructed releases were typically rapid and accompanied by head/body turning toward the reward port. By contrast, premature and probe releases were often slower or more hesitant and were less consistently followed by orienting movements toward the port (Fig. 6, B and D). Thus, differences in movement kinematics may partly contribute to the differences in neural activity between correct and premature/probe trials. Example trials from representative units in two rats are shown in movie S4.

To assess the functional role of trigger/release-aligned DLS activity, we suppressed it by coupling laser onset to the trigger stimulus in 25 rats (Fig. 6I) and examined the effects of DLS photoinhibition on reaction time (lever release; see movie S5) and on the subsequent reward retrieval process (see below).

As illustrated by an example rat (Fig. 6J), trigger-coupled bilateral DLS photoinhibition prolonged reaction times (β1 = 0.69 ± 0.1 s, mean ± SEM, t63.5 = 7.25, P < 0.001, n = 25 rats; LME model in Eq. 14; Fig. 6K). Initially, reaction times increased by 2.27-fold for the 750-ms FP and 2.57-fold for the 1500-ms FP (Fig. 6L; Early Stim). This slowing effect attenuated with repeated inhibitions, reducing to 1.47-fold and 1.62-fold (Fig. 6L; Late Stim), suggesting partial adaptation. Compared to bilateral DLS photoinhibition, unilateral photoinhibition had inconsistent effects (fig. S8). Delayed lever release occurred while DLS spiking remained inhibited (fig. S6, J to M). Overall, DLS activity associated with trigger-guided release is important for initiating rapid reactive lever-release responses.

DLS photoinhibition was also applied when the lever was pressed down (fig. S9) to assess whether sustaining lever holding depends on DLS activity; laser duration was fixed at 2200 ms to cover the longest FP (1500 ms) and response window (600 ms). Initially (early phase), press-coupled photoinhibition delayed reaction times and reduced premature responses in some rats; with repeated photoinhibition (late phase), the effect reversed, significantly increasing premature responses (fig. S9, B to D, and see note S1)—a pattern resembling the persistent premature-response increase after bilateral DLS lesions (data replotted in fig. S9E) (21), possibly because animals shift toward earlier responding over repeated trials as photoinhibition initially slows reactive responses.

DLS inhibition reduces locomotor efficiency and motor vigor during reward retrieval

In the behavioral chamber, after a correct lever release, rats retrieved water rewards from a reward port on the wall opposite the lever, requiring body turning, locomotion, and a self-initiated nose poke (Fig. 1, A and D, fig. S1, D and E). Reward retrieval duration was defined as the time between lever release and nose poke at the reward port. Reward size was fixed and did not depend on retrieval duration. To assess the impact of DLS photoinhibition on reward retrieval, we coupled the onset of photoinhibition to lever release and tracked head coordinates and direction using a top-view camera (Fig. 7A).

Bilateral DLS photoinhibition prolonged the reward retrieval durations, with trajectory frequency maps showing longer, more outward paths and reduced speed (Fig. 7, D to F; see Y position and speed in Fig. 7F; movie S6). Using an LME model with fixed effects including StimType (NoStim and PhotoInh) and StimSide (bilateral, ipsilateral, and contralateral to turning direction) and random effects for animals (n = 6; Eq. 15), we found that bilateral photoinhibition significantly increased path length (β1 = 1.91 ± 0.23 cm, t7.5 = 8.34, P < 0.001), reduced speed (β1 = −9.16 ± 0.81 cm/s, t6.6 = −11.3, P < 0.001), and prolonged reward retrieval duration (β1 = 0.93 ± 0.06 s, t7.4 = 15.28, P < 0.001), indicating impaired movement efficiency (larger outward turns) and reduced movement vigor (slower speed). Compared to bilateral inhibition, unilateral inhibition had similar but weaker effects, with ipsilateral inhibition producing a larger effect than contralateral inhibition (fig. S10). The slowing in retrieval duration persisted over repeated photoinhibition, with mild adaptation (first 60 stimulations: Δ = 0.77 s, 95% CI [0.47, 1.08]; subsequent stimulations: Δ = 0.56 s, [0.25, 0.87]; n = 5 rats).

When DLS photoinhibition was instead coupled to the trigger stimulus as in Fig. 6, rats, despite longer reaction times, still sometimes released the lever within the response window, allowing us to test whether the subsequent reward retrieval process was affected (fig. S11; same example rat as in Fig. 7C). The effects were similar: Trigger-coupled DLS photoinhibition also increased the reward retrieval duration, particularly with bilateral inhibition (β1 = 0.52 ± 0.04 s, t24.6 = 12.42, P < 0.001, n = 25 rats; fig. S11, B and C). Movement tracking in a subset of rats (n = 11; fig. S11, D to J) showed increased path length and reduced speeds similar to Fig. 7 (C to I) (movie S7).

We further analyzed the relationship between reaction time and retrieval duration under NoStim and trigger-coupled PhotoInh conditions, in trials where rats released the lever despite photoinhibition. Retrieval duration was positively correlated with reaction time under NoStim in most rats (21 of 25); in these rats, this correlation was significantly reduced during photoinhibition (fig. S11, K and L), suggestive of a role for the striatum in coordinating movement vigor across operant responses and reward retrieval (20, 58, 59), although the analysis is limited by truncation at long reaction times (slow-reaction trials yielded no reward and thus no retrieval data).

Last, using an infrared sensor near the reward port, we applied bilateral photoinhibition near the end of retrieval locomotion, as rats began to decelerate (Fig. 7M) and prepare for the nose poke, but before the poke itself (n = 5 rats; Fig. 7B and see movie S8). Photoinhibition had little effect on the remaining trajectory (Fig. 7, J to M), with only a slight increase in path length to the nose poke (β1 = 0.43 ± 0.1 cm, t4 = 4.17, P = 0.014; Fig. 7N) and no significant change in movement speed (β1 = −0.17 ± 0.13 cm/s, t3.6 = −1.28, P = 0.278), suggesting normal deceleration. The duration from detection to nose poke onset increased slightly (β1 = 0.16 ± 0.02 s, t4.6 = 7.68, P < 0.001; Fig. 7, O and P). Thus, DLS photoinhibition subtly delayed nose poke behavior, in contrast to its much stronger effects on forelimb lifting (Figs. 4 and 5): nose poke–related striatal spikes, despite their abundance (e.g., PC2 in the reward/poke subspaces was almost entirely driven by nose poke near the reward port; Fig. 1M), appear to contribute only mildly to the nose poke movement itself.

Control experiments

To minimize the visual confounds from laser stimulation, as in previous optogenetics studies (60), all optogenetic experiments in this study were conducted with a masking blue light-emitting diode (LED) flickering light (20 Hz, 4 to 6 s) mounted on the ceiling of the box, applied during both NoStim and PhotoInh trials and beginning at lever approach. Control experiments were performed in rats (n = 4) injected with an AAV-expressing GFP but lacking ChR2. These rats underwent identical training, behavioral testing, and analysis (fig. S12). Sham photoinhibition coupled to lever approach did not alter approach-to-press duration (fig. S12, A to E); coupled to the trigger tone, it slightly shortened reaction time (fig. S12, F and G), likely from the added visual cue coinciding with the tone. Sham stimulation had no effect on whole-body locomotion toward the reward port (fig. S12, H to N), confirming that the observed effects of DLS photoinhibition reflected modulation of DLS and downstream activity.

DMS–DLS dissociation

In addition, we conducted parallel experiments and analyses in the DMS using the same methodology and pipeline (n = 10 rats; Fig. 8, A and B). DMS photoinhibition produced no detectable change in approach-to-press duration, indicating that forelimb movement initiation was unimpaired (Fig. 8, C to I, and fig. S13). Lift-coupled photoinhibition was not performed in DMS. When photoinhibition was coupled to lever release, whole-body movement speed slowed and reward retrieval duration increased, but these effects were substantially smaller than those with DLS photoinhibition (Fig. 8, J to P; Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, DMS versus DLS: P < 0.001 for each of path length increase, speed reduction, and retrieval duration increase).

DLS inhibition results in mixed responses in BG output (SNr)

The behavioral effects of striatal photoinhibition ultimately manifest as changes in BG output via structures such as the SNr (61, 62), with downstream effects on the motor thalamus, superior colliculus, and brainstem (63–67). Increased BG output delays forelimb movement initiation (63) or suppresses repetitive licking (68), while decreased BG output slows reaching in monkeys (25)—so perturbing BG output in either direction can impair movement.

To examine the effects of DLS inhibition on BG output, we performed tetrode recordings from the SNr of nine rats during the SRT task. In six of these rats, we bilaterally implanted optical fibers in the DLS to enable simultaneous SNr recordings during DLS photoinhibition (Fig. 9A). Spike waveforms recorded in the SNr exhibited a continuum of widths and shapes, which we roughly grouped into two types (1 and 2) based on spike width (type 1: 0.1 ± 0.02 ms, n = 246; type 2: 0.18 ± 0.03 ms, n = 64; mean ± SD) and their projections onto the first principal component (Fig. 9, B and C). These unit types were spatially intermixed across recording depths and exhibited similar baseline firing rates (Fig. 9, C and D), possibly corresponding to genetically defined cell types such as parvalbumin (PV)–positive and non-PV neurons (64, 69) or those projecting to specific targets (65). Using the same criteria applied to DLS units, including ISI violations and event modulation, we selected 243 (type 1) and 60 (type 2) SNr units for further analysis (Fig. 9, I to M).

Consistent with earlier studies (12, 63, 70, 71), SNr neurons showed heterogeneous firing patterns, with behavior-relevant increases or decreases in firing rate from a high baseline, modulated by lever approach, press, holding, release, and reward retrieval (Fig. 9, E to J); both type-1 (narrow) and type-2 (broad waveform) neurons were modulated. PCA identified subspaces capturing SNr population variance modulated by forelimb responses or reward retrieval, with projections qualitatively similar to DLS (Fig. 9K), and the first four principal angles between forelimb- and retrieval-related subspaces (59°, 70°, 80°, and 86°) were comparable to DLS, indicating a similar degree of separation. As in Fig. 1N, we used these subspaces to guide unit selection by loading (Fig. 9, L and M). Notably, SNr units showed more mixed selectivity: 85.4% of retrieval units overlapped with lift/hold/trigger units versus 33.3% in DLS [fig. S2, D and G; χ2(1) = 68.1, P < 0.001]. Thus, although forelimb- and retrieval-related subspaces were similarly separated in SNr and DLS, individual SNr units showed greater mixed selectivity than DLS units.

In a subset of SNr recordings (n = 124 units), DLS photoinhibition was applied in a small fraction of trials. To measure the effect of DLS photoinhibition on SNr activity, we specifically examined press-aligned activity because, although premature responses increased with DLS photoinhibition, they typically emerged only after repeated stimulation and increased with longer holding durations, rather than immediately after the press (fig. S9). We selected trials in which the holding duration was at least 500 ms for both NoStim and PhotoInh conditions, ensuring that the first 500 ms of behavior was matched across conditions to allow for direct comparison of neural activity (Fig. 10, A to D). As shown in these examples, DLS photoinhibition induced either an increase or a decrease in the firing rate of single SNr neurons (Fig. 10, E and F), and the distribution of change directions did not differ significantly between type 1 and 2 neurons [χ2(2) = 0.22, P = 0.895, chi-square test; Fig. 10G]. The change occurred ∼14 ms (time at half maximum from photoinhibition onset) for neurons with decreasing firing rates and 8 ms for neurons with increasing firing rates (Fig. 10F). The complex and diverse effects of DLS inhibition on SNr neurons observed here are consistent with previous studies involving striatal activation or inactivation (61, 62).

The modulation of SNr units by DLS photoinhibition was largely sustained throughout the stimulation period, with only modest adaptation (Fig. 10F). However, behavior following a defined time point (e.g., lever press) can differ substantially between NoStim and PhotoInh conditions because of trial-type variation (short versus long FP) and outcome (more premature responses during photoinhibition; fig. S9), contributing to firing-rate differences across conditions (Fig. 10, B and D). To assess whether the photoinhibition effect on SNr spikes evolves over time—informing the mechanism of behavioral recovery (e.g., delayed lever release after trigger-coupled photoinhibition)—we compared behaviorally matched, correct-trial time points: Immediately after photoinhibition onset (0 to 300 ms; press- or trigger-coupled) and just before lever release (−300 to 0 ms), while photoinhibition was still ongoing (see fig. S15 for trigger-modulated SNr units). For each unit, we computed the spike-count difference (PhotoInh − NoStim) at these two points; a difference that decreases toward zero would indicate adaptation, whereas a stable difference would not. The 2200-ms stimulation duration was sufficient to encompass both windows for correct trials in both stimulation designs.

This analysis showed that the effect of photoinhibition was largely maintained at the later time point, with the regression slopes slightly below unity indicating partial attenuation (press: slope = 0.72, r = 0.76, P < 0.001; trigger: slope = 0.84, r = 0.97, P < 0.001; Fig. 10H). Together, these results suggest that the behavioral recovery observed during DLS photoinhibition (e.g., lever release) is unlikely to be explained by a recovery of BG output alone and instead reflects contributions from parallel circuits outside the BG.

DISCUSSION

Task- and kinematics-related activity in DLS has been described for decades (9), but its functional contribution to ongoing behavior remains unclear. Our closed-loop optogenetic experiments revealed that DLS activity contributes throughout the execution of well-learned forelimb and whole-body movements. Photoinhibition delayed movement initiation, including lever reaching and lever release, and disrupted ongoing reaches. Rats moved more slowly toward the reward port and followed less efficient trajectories. The nose-poke movement, by contrast, appeared largely insensitive to photoinhibition.

Neural activity in DLS tracks movement kinematics, reflecting both position and movement variables

In our task, the lever and the reward port were placed on opposite sides of the behavioral box (32 or 35 cm apart), naturally segregating lever-related forelimb movements from reward-oriented whole-body locomotion. Individual DLS neurons were coupled to one or more discrete behavioral events, including lever approach, paw lift (reach), lever press and hold, lever release, and reward retrieval.

This event-aligned activity was partially captured by Poisson GLMs using kinematic predictors such as body-part velocity and speed (action) and position (state). State-only GLMs typically performed better than action-only models, accounting for spiking in a larger fraction of neurons (Fig. 2, J and M). Thus, DLS spiking appears to track kinematic variables, particularly body position, with some components potentially arising from somatosensory feedback and others reflecting motor-related signals, such as motor commands or their corollary discharge (13, 17, 40–42, 72–74).

Interpreting the functional role of neural activity during behavior remains challenging. For example, in rare cases (movie S9) where rats held the forepaw in the air for extended periods before lever press, sustained spiking over seconds (unit 1 in Fig. 2B) suggested position tracking, but these spike-kinematics correlations alone cannot disentangle somatosensory feedback, posture-maintaining motor commands, or corollary discharge (42). Disambiguating these will likely require passive perturbations, such as externally imposed limb or head movements in awake animals (75–77).

By shifting spike times relative to behavioral variables across temporal lags, we examined whether DLS spikes primarily “lead” or “follow” kinematic changes [see (41)]. This analysis revealed no systematic bias toward positive (leading) or negative (lagging) lags overall, although a small subset of units coupled to lever reaching or release preferred positive lags, suggesting that their activity precedes and may contribute to kinematic changes.

Together, these results do not support a purely control or purely monitoring role for DLS activity. Instead, they suggest that the temporal relationship between spiking and kinematics varies across neurons, with some units leading and others lagging kinematic changes. Thus, DLS activity can both reflect and, in some cases, precede kinematic variables (Fig. 2, P and Q). Understanding these relationships will likely require asking whether time-lag preferences are stable across days and how they emerge and evolve with motor learning—questions that will require longitudinal tracking of identified neurons across sessions (38).

Continuous contributions of DLS in ongoing motor behaviors

Regardless of its functional interpretation, DLS activity is involved in ongoing behavior. When applied before movement onset, DLS photoinhibition delayed forelimb reaching or tone-triggered lever release, consistent with a role in action selection and initiation (16, 78). After reach initiation, DLS photoinhibition interrupted ongoing movement, indicating that the DLS also contributes to the progression and completion of an initiated movement. It is possible that the body-state representation in the DLS provides a continuous feedback signal to the brain, such that its sudden removal causes movement impairment. Despite the delay or interruption, lever pressing or release nevertheless resumed during photoinhibition, although DLS units were still in an inhibited state (Fig. 3 and fig. S6) and BG output remained perturbed (Fig. 10H). Thus, DLS is not absolutely required for these movements, but it is actively involved in efficient movement generation during ongoing behavior. Consistent with this, DLS lesions, despite impairing SRT behavior, did not abolish lever pressing (79) or reaction-time performance (21). Since there are many parallel circuits sending descending inputs to spinal cord circuits, movement without DLS is expected (80–82).

In comparison, whole-body locomotion did not stop completely during photoinhibition: Rats continued toward the reward port at lower speeds and via larger circles, indicating reduced movement vigor [defined as the “speed, amplitude, or frequency” of a movement (19)]. However, the effect was not limited to a reduction in speed; the movement trajectory also differed substantially (e.g., longer retrieval arc length). We interpret this result as reduced efficiency of whole-body movement, particularly during turning. Since a small subset of DLS units fire in relation to limb movements during locomotion (13, 77), and some units track turning-related kinematics (Fig. 2Q), it is possible that these kinematic representations contribute to the efficient coordination of whole-body turning. In line with this interpretation, bilateral DLS lesions also persistently reduce retrieval speed and alter movement trajectories in the same task (21).

Last, not all striatal spikes are directly involved in a given movement. In our task, nose-poking into the reward port, the final step of a behavioral trial, was represented by a substantial fraction of DLS spikes; however, the action was largely preserved during DLS photoinhibition: Animals could perform the nose-poke movement with little deficit in initiation or vigor during DLS photoinhibition. The function of these spikes is thus unlikely to be directly related to the movement itself. Previously, activity associated with the final step of a behavioral trial, often also coupled to reward retrieval, has been reported to increase with learning (9). Although the emergence of such activity has been associated with habit formation or skill acquisition, its function has not been fully resolved. Our photoinhibition experiments do not support a primary role of such activity in producing the reward-collection motor response. Further investigations are needed to understand the role of DLS representations of reward collection during operant behaviors; for example, how reward-related spikes emerge during outcome-driven operant learning and whether they are necessary for learning rather than execution.

Much of our understanding of BG function in movement control comes from behavioral observations following disease or experimental perturbation, but animal lesion and inactivation studies, including ours, cannot directly access the subjective experience underlying these deficits. In human stroke patients with BG damage, clinical reports describe autoactivation deficit, in which patients remain motionless for extended periods despite responding when prompted (83, 84). DLS photoinhibition may transiently produce an analogous state in rats, impairing self-initiated movements (lever reaching, reward retrieval) while externally triggered lever release still occurs, albeit more slowly. That said, the rats’ internal state was unavailable to us, and the impairments could arise from multiple mechanisms. Nevertheless, these observations support the view that the BG are critically involved in enabling normal movement initiation and execution, not simply in generating motor commands.

Comparisons with previous work

In head-fixed mice, striatal inactivation with archaerhodopsin-3 (ArchT) during joystick movement reduced movement velocity while leaving displacement unchanged (23). This parallels our findings that DLS photoinhibition applied during forepaw lift caused animals to drop the paw mid-air and then reinitiate the reaching movement moments later (Fig. 5H). Notably, paw dropping was not reported in the joystick task, possibly because the joystick served as a support that prevented it.

Lever-press studies have reported forelimb initiation deficits following DLS photoinhibition: ArchT-mediated inactivation coupled to lever approach increased approach-to-press duration (27), and halorhodopsin-mediated photoinhibition increased fixed-ratio completion time via longer interpress intervals (85), both suggesting impaired initiation. We similarly observed an increase in approach-to-press duration and quantitatively analyzed the forepaw movement, showing that the increased duration resulted from a delay in lift initiation rather than a general slowing of forelimb movement during lever reaching or difficulty pressing the lever (Fig. 4J). We further coupled inhibition to ongoing reaching via online pose tracking to test whether DLS is also required for completing initiated movements (Fig. 5), an approach that, to our knowledge, had not been previously used.

Previous studies similarly report reduced whole-body movement vigor after striatal inhibition or lesions: Halorhodopsin-mediated DLS photoinhibition increased trial duration in a T-maze decision task (86), dorsal striatum lesions slowed treadmill movement during timing learning (20), and direct-pathway SPN inhibition increased “choice time” (movement duration between ports) in an interval categorization task (87)—consistent with our finding of reduced vigor during reward retrieval (Fig. 7).

Our work extends these studies in several important ways. First, we used a robust method to inactivate DLS neurons, achieving a median of 90% suppression of spiking in putative DLS SPNs. Our approach did not require the opsin to be expressed in DLS SPNs because inhibitory interneuron activation can inactivate nearby projection neurons broadly. Second, we performed side-by-side comparisons of the effect of DLS photoinhibition across distinct movement types, allowing us to establish the functional influence of DLS spikes on forelimb and whole-body movements and the relative lack of effect on the nose poke movement. Third, we quantitatively tracked the effect of DLS photoinhibition on movement trajectories, a feature largely absent from previous studies. Fourth, by combining DLS photoinhibition with SNr recordings, we directly linked striatal perturbations to changes in BG output, providing a circuit-level account of how continuous DLS activity shapes ongoing behavior. Last, we compared the effects of DLS and DMS inhibition and found a dissociation in their behavioral consequences: DLS photoinhibition strongly affected forelimb initiation and whole-body movement speed, whereas DMS-targeted photoinhibition lacked these effects—with residual speed reductions likely attributable to viral spread into DLS rather than DMS inhibition per se.

Limitations

Rats are well suited for reaction-time experiments: Their forelimb and whole-body movements are quantifiable in freely moving paradigms, their lever-press behaviors become stereotyped once performance stabilizes, and their size accommodates large neural recording implants with minimal impact on behavioral performance, allowing stable longitudinal Neuropixels recordings in freely moving animals over months (38), which remains challenging in mice. Our optogenetic approach, which drives inhibitory interneurons to suppress the firing rates of projection neurons (49, 88), does not isolate cell type– or pathway-specific effects, as both the direct (striatonigral) and indirect (striatopallidal) pathways are suppressed. This is an important limitation. Future work could leverage transgenic rats (89) or molecular tools (90) to perturb these pathways selectively. Alternatively, downstream circuits could be targeted directly, including the external globus pallidus (91), or DLS outputs could be silenced using axonal-silencing approaches (92). The current approach and its variants (e.g., activating striatal PV-positive interneurons) produce robust suppression of SPN spiking, functionally akin to a precisely timed lesion, and can guide more targeted perturbations. Moreover, pathway-specific manipulations are complicated by lateral interactions: Increasing or decreasing the activity of one cell type inevitably alters the activity of the other (45, 91).

During the reward retrieval phase, rats turned toward the reward port. DLS photoinhibition markedly slowed this movement and altered the trajectory (Fig. 7D). One possibility is that turning, relative to straight-line locomotion, relies more heavily on the DLS, which could account for much of the observed slowing in whole-body movement. Turning is indeed strongly represented in the DLS [Fig. 2Q and see also (11, 28)]. However, our behavioral box was not large enough to fully assess straight-line locomotion; rats typically reached the reward port shortly after turning and began to decelerate. Future experiments in a large maze, e.g., a T-maze (9, 86, 93) or a treadmill (13, 20), that combines turning with straight-line locomotion could directly address this question.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the animal care standards set forth by the US National Institutes of Health and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University (LSC-YuJN-1). Male adult Brown-Norway rats were obtained from Vita River Laboratories (Beijing, China). Rats were older than 3 months and weighed 250 to 400 g before water restriction and behavioral training. Rats were housed individually on a 12-hour reverse light-dark cycle. During water restriction, water was earned during behavioral sessions; if intake was less than 12 ml, supplemental water was provided at least 3 hours after the session. Body weights were maintained at ∼80% of their free-feeding level. Training was conducted 6 days per week, with unrestricted water access on the seventh day.

Apparatus and behavioral training

Behavioral training protocols were detailed in a prior study (21). Briefly, custom operant chambers integrated lever-based response modules (Med Associates, Fairfax, VT, USA) with Bpod state machines (Sanworks LLC, Rochester, NY, USA) to precisely control reward delivery and event timing. Within each chamber, a retractable response lever and reward port were positioned on opposite walls, separated by 32 cm (optogenetics experiments) or 35 cm (Neuropixels recordings). Behavioral programs were written in MedState Notation by M. Caetano and M. Laubach. SRT behavioral performance was shaped progressively across six stages: (i) autoshaping, (ii) lever press, (iii) lever release, (iv) lever hold (FP increased from 0 to 1.5 s) with a wide release response window (2 s), (v) hold with rapid release incentives (response window reduced from 2 to 0.6 s), and (vi) full SRT schedule (0.75, 1.5 s FP, 0.6-s response window). The lever remained extended throughout the sessions (except for autoshaping), with trials self-initiated by lever presses. Correct responses triggered illumination of an LED within the reward port, cueing the delivery of a 60-μl, 10% (w/v) sucrose solution upon nose poke entry. Error responses (premature or late release) were penalized with a timeout (4 to 10 s), with the lever light and house light turned off (lever not retracted). The imperative trigger stimulus was a 250-ms tone (2 kHz, 70-dB SPL, sound pressure level) delivered via a speaker mounted above the lever. An infrared LED, synchronized to tone onset, provided a signal for aligning video timestamps with behavioral events. A side-view camera (100 fps; MER-160-227U3M, Daheng Imaging, Beijing, China) was positioned externally to record forelimb kinematics during lever pressing. An overhead camera (100 fps; MER-160-227U3M) captured top-view images of locomotor behavior.

In closed-loop optogenetic experiments, a transparent acrylic plastic block was placed directly in front of the lever to constrain the movement angle and posture of the animals, thereby facilitating the detection of lever-approach and paw-lifting behaviors. A 40-mm rectangular gap allowed access for lever pressing, reducing presses from extreme side angles.

Stereotaxic surgery

Before surgery, rats were given unrestricted access to water for at least 2 days. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction: 4%; maintenance: 1 to 2% in O2) and mounted in a stereotaxic frame (MTM-3; World Precision Instruments). Rectal temperature was maintained at 37.5°C using a feedback-controlled heating pad. Eyes were lubricated with erythromycin eye ointment (Qilu Pharmaceutical, Jinan, China). Fur was removed using an electric clipper, and the scalp was sterilized with iodine tincture followed by 75% ethanol (3×). The scalp was incised along the midline, and the pericranium was reflected using a blunt scalpel. The skull surface was further cleaned with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution. Craniotomies were drilled at the target coordinates, and the dura was carefully incised using a 30-gauge needle before injection or implantation.

Virus injection

Virus injections and optical fiber implantation (described below) were performed on separate days. Injections were performed using glass pipettes (Drummond Wiretrol II, Broomall, PA, USA) mounted on a custom-made injector system (Stereotaxic Injector System, Janelia) or a motorized microsyringe pump (UMP3; World Precision Instruments) coupled to a 10-μl precision syringe (701 RN; Hamilton, Reno, NV, USA) fitted with a removable 33-gauge, 12-mm needle (Hamilton). AAV2/9-mDlx-hChR2(H134R)-EGFP-WPRE-pA, or a control virus, AAV2/9-mDlx-EGFP-WPRE-pA (titer: 3 × 1012 vg/ml; Shanghai Taitool Bioscience, Shanghai, China) was injected bilaterally into the DLS [relative to bregma; first site: anterior-posterior (AP) = +1.3 mm, medial-lateral (ML) = ±3.6 mm; second site: AP = +0.3 mm, ML = ±4.0 mm] or DMS (first site: AP = +1.5 mm, ML = ± 2.5 mm; second site: AP = +0.5 mm, ML = ±2.7 mm). Each site included injections at two depths [dorsal-ventral (DV) = −4.6 mm, −4.0 mm, relative to the brain surface], and 250 to 300 nl of virus were injected at each depth at a rate of 50 nl/min. After each injection, the pipette was left in place for 10 min before slow retraction. Craniotomies were sealed with Kwik-Cast silicone sealant (World Precision Instruments) after all injections at a site were completed. The scalp was sutured, and rats were allowed to recover in their home cages. Flunixin meglumine (2.5 mg/kg, subcutaneously (s.c.); QianShou Bio, Wuhan, China) was administered immediately postsurgery and daily for 3 to 5 days for analgesic effects, alongside ceftriaxone sodium (30 mg/kg, s.c.; Yuekang Duozhi, Beijing, China) to prevent infection. Body weights were monitored daily until full recovery.

Optical fiber implantation

After a minimum of 2 weeks of recovery, the scalp incision was reopened, and the skull surface was cleaned as described for virus injections. Four stainless-steel anchor screws (1.2-mm diameter; Yuyan Bio, Shanghai, China) were inserted into the skull for stability. Fiber-optic cannulae [200-μm diameter, numerical aperture (NA) = 0.37; InPer Technologies, Hangzhou, China] were bilaterally implanted in the DLS (AP = +0.8 mm, ML = ±4.0 mm, DV = −3.9 mm relative to bregma) or in the DMS (AP = +1.0 mm, ML = ±2.3 mm, DV = −3.9 mm). The craniotomies were sealed with Kwik-Cast, and the ferrules were secured to the skull using Super-Bond C&B dental adhesive (Sun Medical Co. Ltd., Shiga, Japan). A protective layer of dental cement (Jet Denture Repair; Lang Dental, Wheeling, IL, USA) was applied over the exposed skull and screws. Rats were allowed to recover under a heated lamp (35° to 37°C) until fully ambulatory (15 to 30 min).

Electrode implantation

DLS recordings used multiple electrode configurations across animals, combining previously reported and newly acquired datasets. A subset (eight rats), previously described in (21), is reanalyzed here: custom-made 16-channel fixed microwire arrays (MWAs; nickel-iridium; BioSignal, Nanjing, China) in four rats; 16-channel fixed MWAs (Hong Kong Plexon, China) integrated with a 200-μm optical fiber, for combined optogenetic/electrophysiological recordings, in three rats (fig. S2B); and 32-channel silicon probes (Diagnostic Biochips) on a movable drive (Micro Linear Drive, Janelia) in two rats, with coordinates reported previously [figure 2 in (21)].

New datasets were also collected from two rats implanted with Neuropixels 2.0 probes (IMEC, Leuven, Belgium) following a published protocol (94); unit locations from a representative session in each rat are shown in fig. S3, and probes were not advanced after implantation.

Surgical procedures followed standard stereotaxic methods, as described above. Two ground screws, each with a silver wire soldered to it, were implanted bilaterally in the skull above the cerebellum, ensuring contact with brain tissue for reference and grounding. Target electrodes were implanted contralateral to the preferred paw at the following coordinates (relative to bregma): AP = +0.8 mm, ML = 4.0 mm. Fixed arrays were inserted to a final depth of −4.0 to −4.5 mm (relative to the brain surface), with adjustments made during implantation when large-amplitude spikes (>100 μV) were observed on the recording system. The silicon probe was lowered to an initial depth of 2.6 mm (relative to the brain surface) and subsequently advanced in 50-μm increments after each recording session. The Neuropixels probes, mounted on a reusable drive (3Dneuro) (94), were inserted to a depth of 5.5 mm (relative to the brain surface) at a 6° angle in both the AP and ML planes and remained immobile thereafter. The craniotomy and the electrode were sealed with petroleum jelly (Vaseline) and bone wax. Neural recordings were performed after 1 week of post-implantation recovery.

For SNr recordings, movable tetrode arrays (Kedou Biotech, Hangzhou, China; stainless steel, 12.5-μm-diameter polyimide-coated wires) were used in an additional cohort of rats. These consisted of 16-channel (four tetrodes) or 32-channel (eight tetrodes) configurations mounted on a microdrive. Target coordinates (relative to bregma) were: AP = −5.2 mm, ML = 2.4 mm. Electrodes were initially lowered to a depth of −7.3 mm (relative to the brain surface) and then advanced in 50-μm increments after each recording session to optimize yield.

Multichannel recordings from the MWA, tetrode, or silicon probes were conducted using a digital amplifier (Cereplex Direct, Blackrock Neurotech, Salt Lake City, UT, USA). Neuropixels recordings were conducted using a PXIe-based system (National Instruments, Austin, TX, USA) and acquired with the SpikeGLX software environment (Janelia Research Campus).

Neural data analysis

Inclusion criteria

All chronic recordings were screened to exclude duplicate units recorded across multiple sessions. For Neuropixels data, 10 to 11 sessions per animal were included. An automated algorithm (38), followed by manual curation, was used to identify and remove repeated units across sessions, ensuring that only units deemed unique after this procedure were included in the population analyses.

Units were excluded if more than 1% of spikes violated the refractory-period criterion, defined as ISIs < 3 ms, and if the estimated false-positive rate, computed following previous work (95), exceeded 0.1. Putative SPNs were further required to exhibit silent periods, quantified as the fraction of recording time occupied by ISIs > 2 s greater than 0.3 [cf. (34)]. For DLS SPNs, the median ISI violation rate, defined as the percentage of spikes with ISIs <3 ms per unit, was 0.1%, with most units falling within 0 to 0.8% (5th to 95th percentiles; n = 478 putative SPNs). For SNr units, the median ISI violation rate was 0.3%, with most units falling within 0 to 1.1% (5th to 95th percentiles; n = 303 units).

Spike waveform analysis

Spike waveforms were inverted so that negative potentials were oriented upward (Fig. 1F). Spike width was quantified as the full width at half height (FWHH). To distinguish units with distinct waveform shapes, PCA was applied to the waveforms of all units within a given brain region (DLS or SNr), treating the voltage value at each time point as a feature. The average waveform of each unit was then projected into the resulting PCA space for low-dimensional visualization. A scatter plot of the projection onto the first PC (PC1) versus FWHH was used to separate putative SPNs from non-SPNs. Units were clustered into two groups using the density-based method HDBSCAN (96). Unclustered data points were assigned to the nearest cluster based on proximity. Putative SPNs were identified as the cluster exhibiting broader spike widths (Fig. 1G).

Spike rate modulation by behavioral events (GLM)

To investigate how behavioral events modulated neuronal spike rates, spike counts were quantified within pre- and post-event time windows relative to key behavioral events using a GLM (see fig. S2A). The events and their respective time windows were (in s): (i) approach/lift, [−2, −1] (pre) and [−1, 0] (post) relative to lever press; (ii) press, [−0.5,0] (pre) and [0,0.5] (post) relative to lever press; (iii) trigger/release, [−0.25,0] (pre) and [0,0.25] (post) relative to trigger stimulus; (iv) poke, [−1, 0] (pre) and [0, 1] (post) relative to port nose poke. Events with fewer than 20 total spikes across trials were excluded to ensure reliable parameter estimation.

Spike count (SpikeCountij) was treated as a random variable from a Poisson process: Poisson(μij). The goal was to determine whether μ was modulated by the event time point (post relative to pre) for each event. A GLM with a log link was fitted to each event separately using the “fitglm” function (MATLAB, MathWorks). For an event, if either the pre- or post-window had zero counts in >50% of trials, we added 1 to both windows to stabilize fitting. The model included a fixed effect for event time point (pre: 0; post: 1) and an offset for window durationlog(μij)=β0+β1TimePointij+log(Durationi)(1)where the response variable SpikeCountij follows a Poisson(μij) distribution; i indexes the time window (pre: i = 1, post: i = 2) for trial j; β0 represents the fixed intercept; β1 is the fixed-effect coefficient for the event time point; TimePointij is the indicator variable for pre or post time window (pre: 0, post: 1); and Durationi is the duration (in seconds) of the respective time window. The coefficient β1 represents the log fold change in spike rate between post- and pre-event windows.

A contrast on β1 was evaluated using a two-sided Wald test (“coefTest” function) to test the null hypothesis: β1 = 0, yielding a P value that indicates whether that unit’s spike rate changed between post- and pre-windows for that event. To control the false discovery rate at 0.05, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (97) was applied across all units and events within a brain region (DLS or SNr) to derive a critical threshold; unit-event pairs with P values below the threshold were deemed significantly modulated (e.g., Fig. 1L).

SDF and warping

Spike times were convolved with a Gaussian kernel (σ = 50 ms) to obtain a smoothed SDF. SDFs were aligned to event times to obtain a peri-event SDF, with 95% CIs derived from bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations). To average trials with different reaction times and reward retrieval durations, SDFs were linearly warped to target times (median reaction time or reward retrieval duration across all trials at the animal level and across animals for population averages) (98). Let t=t1,t2,…,tn be the time points of the SDF f(t) for the current trial, and let the target time points be t′=t1′,t2′,…,tk′. The scaling factor s was applied to warp the SDF, yielding SDFwarp(t′), denoted as fwarp(t′), where t′=t1′,…,tk′, as followsfwarp(ti′)=(⌈i⋅s⌉−i⋅s)⋅f(⌊i⋅s⌋)+(i⋅s−⌊i⋅s⌋)⋅f(⌈i⋅s⌉)(2)where s=tn−t1tk′−t1′ and ⌈⋅⌉ and ⌊⋅⌋ are the ceiling and floor functions, respectively.

Units were classified as positively modulated if the maximum of their mean-subtracted average SDF exceeded the absolute value of the minimum; otherwise, they were classified as negatively modulated. For heatmaps showing ranked activity of DLS or SNr neurons (Figs. 1K and 9I), the warped SDF, SDFwarp(t), was mean-subtracted and soft-normalized by division by (r + c), where r was the minimum-maximum range and c was a constant (c = 5) (99). To order units by response timing, data from correct trials in the 750-ms FP condition were used to order units by the first time each unit’s mean-subtracted SDF crossed a threshold (75% of the maximum for positively modulated units or 75% of the minimum for negatively modulated units). The same order was then applied to the 1500-ms FP condition.

Principal components analysis

Neural population activity was analyzed using PCA to identify low-dimensional manifolds that capture the dominant patterns of variance across units. Unlike prior work (21), to avoid mixing different behavioral regimes, PCA spaces were constructed separately for periods dominated by forelimb movements (lever press, hold, release; from 2.5 s before lever press to lever release) and whole-body movements (reward retrieval; from lever release to 1 s after nose poke). For each of the two behavioral epochs, soft-normalized SDFs from included units were stacked into a matrix of size time points × units, and PCA was then applied using MATLAB’s “pca” function, treating each unit (column) as a covariate, yielding PCs ordered by explained variance. Population activity was then projected onto the top four PCs for visualization (Fig. 1M and 9I), which revealed that individual PCs aligned with distinct behavioral features, such as forelimb lift initiation or reward retrieval movement.

To identify subpopulations contributing substantially to behaviorally coupled PCs (Figs. 1, N and O and 9, L and M), units were selected on the basis of the magnitudes of their PCA loadings (weights wi; i = 1,2,3,… indexes the PCs) in the coefficient matrix. Thresholds were empirically pre-specified for different PCs. Loadings were taken from PCA on activity aligned to lever press for the lift/hold/trigger phases, and to nose poke for retrieval:

1) Lift units (selective for prepress paw-lifting movement): DLS: w2 > 0.05; SNr (positively modulated): w2 > 0.05; SNr (negatively modulated): w2 < −0.05.

2) Hold units (selective for sustained press): DLS: w1 > 0.05; SNr (positively modulated): w1 > 0.05; SNr (negatively modulated): w1 < −0.05.

3) Trigger/release units (selective for trigger stimulus and lever release): DLS: w2 < 0.025 and w3 > 0.05; SNr (positively modulated): w3 < −0.05; SNr (negatively modulated): w3 > 0.05.

4) Retrieval units (selective for post-release reward retrieval): DLS: w1 < −0.075 or w2 > 0.075 or w3 > 0.075; SNr (positively modulated): w1 < −0.075 or w2 > 0.075 or w3 < −0.075; SNr (negatively modulated): w1 > 0.075 or w2 < −0.075 or w3 > 0.075.

This yielded partially overlapping unit subsets per phase, each enriched for the associated behavioral events, with overlaps (indicating multievent modulation) illustrated as Venn diagrams (fig. S2, D and G).

GLM analysis of body kinematics and spikes

Following alignment of video frames to behavioral timestamps, video clips were extracted around each lever press: −5 to +5 s for top-view videos, and −2 to +2 s for side-view videos. Body-part pose models were trained on selected frames, and videos were tracked using DeepLabCut (100). Tracked body parts were then manually inspected and curated via a custom MATLAB GUI to ensure high-quality trajectories (e.g., excluding segments with occlusion or low-confidence tracking). For each video clip, the x-y coordinates of the tracked parts were stored in a table with each row representing a frame. To reduce frame-to-frame noise, tracked coordinates were smoothed using an 11-frame moving-average filter. Coordinates in pixels were converted to centimeters.

For the side-view analysis, we selected movement blocks in which at least two body parts, the contralateral forepaw and the ear, were reliably and continuously tracked in individual trials. For the side-view model, coordinates were transformed into a lever-centered reference frame. In this coordinate system, positive x was defined as horizontal displacement toward the lever, and positive y corresponded to vertical displacement upward from the ground toward the lever. Velocities were computed from these transformed coordinates using a ±3 frame difference and were further smoothed with a 5-frame moving-average filter. Speed was defined as the magnitude of the velocity vector.

To capture nonlinear relationships between body position and spike rate, positional variables were represented using a one-hot encoding of spatial location, where each time bin was assigned to a single bin on a regular two-dimensional grid [1 for the occupied bin, 0 otherwise; cf. (39)]. This representation allows the GLM to capture arbitrary spatial tuning without assuming a specific functional form (e.g., firing rate increases/decreases monotonically with x or y).

The Poisson linear-nonlinear GLM model (101, 102) is constructed as followsyt∼Poisson(μt)(3)logμt=β0+xt⊤β(4)

Here, yt is the spike count in the tth 30-ms bin, μt is the expected spike count in that bin, β0 is the intercept, and xt is the kinematic feature vector for that same bin. For the side-view model, xt included positional variables for the contralateral paw and ear, together with horizontal velocity, vertical velocity, and speed magnitude for each body part. For the top-view model, xt included head position, head angle represented by cosθ and sinθ, forward speed, lateral speed, and speed magnitude. Predictors were taken from the same time bin.

Model fitting was performed using MATLAB’s lassoglm function with an elastic-net penalty (103). The elastic-net mixing parameter was fixed at α = 0.9. Only time bins with valid tracking data were included in the analysis, and units with fewer than 100 spikes across valid bins were excluded from fitting. Train/test partitioning was performed at the level of whole trials rather than individual time bins to avoid temporal leakage across training and test sets. For each fit, trials were randomly divided into a training set (75% of trials) and a held-out test set (25% of trials). Predictor variables were standardized using the mean and SD computed from the training set only, and the same transformation was then applied to the test set.

For each training set, the regularization parameter λ was selected by fivefold cross-validation performed on the training trials only. A sequence of 50 candidate λ values was generated automatically, and cross-validation performance for each λ was quantified using pR2. For each unit, the value of λ that maximized the mean validation pR2 across folds was selected, and the model was then refit on all training rows using this λ. To simplify the computation, for each unit, we fixed λ at the value identified from a single split and reused this value across repeats, variants of models (e.g., with only state or action variables), and models with shuffled data (see below).

Model performance on held-out test trials was quantified using a likelihood-based Poisson pseudo-R2 (pR2), which measures the improvement of the model in describing the firing-rate variance over a null model that predicts the mean spike count of the training set for every bin (104, 105). The Poisson log-likelihood was defined asL(y∣μ)=∑t[ytlogμt−μt−log(yt!)](5)and pR2 was computed aspR2=Lmodel−LnullLsat−Lnull(6)where Lmodel is the log-likelihood of the fitted model, Lnull is the log-likelihood of a null model, and Lsat is the log-likelihood of the saturated model.

To summarize the fitting results shown in Fig. 2 (J and M), we repeated the random trial-based train/test split 10 times and reported the mean held-out pR2 across repeats for each unit and model variant.

Shuffled data were constructed by reassigning spike times across trials while preserving their timing relative to behavioral events (lever press and reward port poke). Specifically, spike times from trial i were aligned to the behavioral events of trial j, thereby preserving the overall temporal structure of the task while disrupting the trial-by-trial correspondence between neural activity and kinematics. Shuffled analyses were performed only for the full model. A total of 10 independent shuffles were performed, and for each shuffle, 10 repeated train/test splits were used to compute the mean shuffled pR2.

To examine the temporal relationship between DLS spiking and kinematics, spike trains were shifted relative to kinematic predictors by lags from −240 to +240 ms in 30-ms increments, with spike counts recomputed for each lag; positive lags correspond to spikes preceding kinematics, so better performance at positive lags indicates that spikes are more predictive of future kinematics [this convention is opposite to that in (41)].

For each unit and model (full, state, and action), we evaluated lag-dependent performance using repeated train/test splits (75/25%, 10 random splits), fitting Poisson GLMs per candidate lag on training data and evaluating pR2 on held-out test data; a zero-lag model was fit and evaluated on the same splits for paired comparison. This yielded lag-dependent performance curves per unit and model (Fig. 2, K to M and O), with example tuning curves shown in the gray colormaps in Fig. 2 (P and Q).

For each unit, lag-dependent performance was first averaged across splits to obtain a tuning curve. To reduce noise, the tuning curve was smoothed using a moving average across three adjacent lag bins. The preferred lag was then defined as the lag at which the smoothed tuning curve reached its maximum. The zero-lag performance was taken directly from the same smoothed curve at 0 ms.

Trigger/release coupled activity

Trigger/release-mediated positive modulation should increase firing in 750-ms FP trials after the trigger compared with 1500-ms FP trials at the same elapsed time after lever press, when no trigger had yet occurred. Thus, to identify neurons with trigger-related modulation (Fig. 6E), spike counts were computed in 50-ms bins within a fixed 0- to 750-ms analysis window after the 750-ms time point. For trials with a 750-ms FP, this window started at the trigger. For trials with a 1500-ms FP, the same window started 750 ms after lever press, corresponding to the period before the later trigger. This comparison allowed activity following the 750-ms trigger to be contrasted with activity at the same elapsed waiting time in trials without a trigger. Only trials with valid trigger times were included. For each neuron and each time bin, spike counts were compared between the two FP conditions using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Significant modulation was defined as a cluster of at least three consecutive bins with P < 0.05 and the same direction of change. The onset of trigger-related modulation was defined as the bin-center time of the first bin in the earliest significant cluster. Because spike counts were computed in 50-ms bins, onset estimates were discretized at this temporal resolution (Fig. 6E). Neurons without a significant cluster in the analysis window were considered not significantly trigger-modulated.

To compare activity around trigger-instructed and self-initiated lever releases, spike counts were computed in 50-ms bins from 600 ms before to 200 ms after lever release. Correct trials were used for trigger-instructed releases. Premature and probe trials with hold durations of at least 500 ms were used for self-initiated releases. For each neuron and time bin, spike counts were compared between the two release types using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Significant release-related differences were defined as clusters of at least three consecutive bins with P < 0.05 and the same direction of change. For visualization, release-aligned activity in the two release types was soft-normalized together for each neuron using a soft-normalization constant of c = 2 Hz, so that activity patterns in the two conditions could be compared directly.

Optogenetic stimulation during lever approach

Hardware and experiment pipeline

An infrared sensor (FQD-31NO, Luo Shida Sensor, Dongguan, China) was positioned above the lever to detect the approach of the rats. Once detected, in a small proportion of trials (<10%), laser stimulation (473 nm) was triggered. The stimulation consisted of a 2200-ms train at 40 Hz with 5-ms pulses (DPSS laser, MBL-FN-473, Changchun New Industries Optoelectronics Technology Co. Ltd., Changchun, China). Laser light was delivered to the brain through an optical fiber (250 μm; NA = 0.37). Laser power was calibrated at the tip each day before the experiment and was set to 20 mW peak, yielding an average power of 4 mW during the pulse train. To minimize visual confounds, a masking blue LED flash (20-Hz, 5-ms pulses for 4 to 6 s), triggered upon approach, was presented in both NoStim and PhotoInh trials.

Forelimb movement was recorded using high-speed video at 100 fps (camera: MER-160-227U3M, Daheng Imaging, China; acquisition: StreamPix 7, NorPix, Montreal, Canada). Post-acquisition alignment was performed using an infrared signal locked to the trigger stimulus. Forelimb movement was tracked using DeepLabCut (100).

A polyvinylidene fluoride vibration sensor (LDT0-028 k, TE Connectivity) was affixed to the end of the lever. The electrical signal was amplified with an amplifier module (PVA103, Shenzhen Vkinging Electronics Co. Ltd., China), digitized with the multifunction data acquisition device PCIe-6341 (National Instruments), and stored using WaveSurfer (Janelia). Using simultaneously recorded video-derived lever position, a linear model converted the sensor’s voltage signal to lever position (R2 > 0.98 on testing data; fig. S16).

Approach-to-press duration analysis ( ta2p ; Fig. 4H)

The approach-to-press duration (ta2p) was computed per trial as the elapsed time between approach detection and the lever press time recorded by Bpod. Outliers were excluded if ∣ta2p−median(ta2p)∣>5×MAD(ta2p), where MAD denotes the median absolute deviation. The effect of DLS photoinhibition on ta2p was evaluated using an LME model with StimType (0 = NoStim, 1 = PhotoInh) as a fixed effect and random intercepts/slopes for StimType by animal (anm): ta2p∼StimType+(1+StimType∣anm). Optogenetic stimulation during forepaw lifting

A second side camera (MER-160-227U3M, Daheng Imaging, China) positioned next to the main side camera captured the paw-lifting process within a 20 cm–by–15 cm field of view at 50 fps. Frames were streamed to Bonsai (55), where a smaller 7 cm–by–4 cm region was analyzed. Bonsai integrated DeepLabCut-Live (54) to estimate the coordinates and detection likelihood of the selected forepaw (left or right). When the paw position exceeded a predefined threshold and the detection likelihood was >75% for three consecutive frames, Bonsai transmitted a trigger signal to the Bpod state machine as a SoftCode event. Bpod then determined whether to initiate laser stimulation based on a predefined probability (<10%).

Drop ratio analysis (Fig. 5G)

A drop event was counted when the vertical paw position fell below a predefined threshold (50 to 70% of the height from baseline to the global peak position) after the lift’s local peak. The drop ratio (pdrop) was computed for each condition (StimType per animal). Paired differences between NoStim and PhotoInh conditions were assessed using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the pdrop.

Optogenetic stimulation coupled to trigger stimulus

For trigger-coupled photoinhibition (Fig. 6), the trigger stimulus activated the Bpod’s scheduled waves. Laser stimulation was applied in <25% of trials per session, randomly assigned to different events, such as approach, lever-press, or trigger, within the same session.

Reward retrieval duration and locomotor behavior

A top-view camera operating at 100 fps captured videos with StreamPix 7 to monitor locomotion. DeepLabCut was used to track body parts and determine the rat’s head location and direction. Movement paths were derived from the positions of the ears, with head direction defined as the vector orthogonal to the ear-to-ear line and pointing toward the nose. The instantaneous velocity of the head for each frame was calculated as the Euclidean displacement between the current head location and the head location one frame ahead, divided by the time interval (10 ms). The velocity time series was smoothed with a moving-average filter (centered window of 25 samples or 250 ms). In release-coupled photoinhibition, the mean velocity for each trial was computed as the average instantaneous velocity from lever release to 1 s afterward, capturing the period of active movement. For near-poke-coupled photoinhibition triggered by an IR detector near the reward port, mean velocity was computed as the average from detection to 0.5 s afterward. Movement path length was calculated as the cumulative Euclidean distance from lever release (or IR detection) to the water-port nose poke. Photoinhibition was applied bilaterally or unilaterally (ipsilateral or contralateral to the turning direction) in a randomized, interleaved order across sessions.

These release- or near poke–coupled photoinhibition experiments had a smaller sample size (n = 6) than the forelimb reaching experiments (n = 25 tested and n = 16 tracked), as the initial focus was on the forelimb movement. Whole-body locomotion tests were added later after observing prolonged reward retrieval duration under trigger-coupled photoinhibition.

Three LME models with the same formula [Metric ∼ StimType × StimSide + (1 + StimType|anm)] were applied to path length, velocity, and retrieval duration, measured across multiple trials and sessions per rat, with StimType and StimSide (bilateral, ipsilateral, and contralateral to turning direction; bilateral as reference) as fixed effects and the StimType slope varying by animal. The model for each measurement y is given byyij=β0+β1StimTypeij+β2StimSideipsi,ij+β3StimSidecontra,ij+β4(StimTypeij×StimSideipsi,ij)+β5(StimTypeij×StimSidecontra,ij)+u0j+u1jStimTypeij+εij(15)where yij is the metric (path length, mean velocity, or retrieval duration) for trial i in animal j; all other terms are defined as in the previous models. The exact model specification applies to both release- and near poke–coupled photoinhibition (Fig. 7, G to I and N to P).

A similar model was fitted for reward retrieval duration under trigger-coupled photoinhibition (fig. S11C). A simplified model excluding StimSide (bilateral only) was used for kinematics analysis during trigger-coupled photoinhibition (fig. S11, H to J).

Statistics

LME or linear models were analyzed using R (“lme4” and “lmerTest” packages). Significance of the fixed effect (e.g., β1 in Eq. 7) was assessed using a t test with Satterthwaite approximation for degrees of freedom. Estimated marginal means for the PhotoInh − NoStim contrast (effect size) were computed using the “emmeans” package, with SEM derived from the model. P values were adjusted using Holm’s method when multiple tests were performed. Effect sizes were reported for all statistical tests, typically as estimated marginal mean differences PhotoInh − NoStim with SEM. The GLM-based analysis of spike count modulation was conducted using MATLAB, as detailed above.
