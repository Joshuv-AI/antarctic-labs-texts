Exploring the Applicability of the Lattice-Boltzmann Method for Two-Dimensional Turbulence Simulation
Raquel Dapena-García, Vicente Pérez-Muñuzuri

Abstract
The Lattice-Boltzmann method is a mesoscopic approach for solving hydrodynamic problems involving both laminar and turbulent fluids. Although the suitability for the former cases is supported by a myriad of studies, turbulent flows always give rise to additional challenges that need to be addressed properly. In this paper, we estimate the accuracy of the simulation results obtained via a custom implementation of a Lattice-Boltzmann solver for a two-dimensional turbulent flow. To this end, a two-dimensional flow field filled with randomly located rigid disks was simulated, and the von Karman vortex street generated after the wake of such obstacles was studied.

1
Introduction

Turbulence plays a crucial role in natural flows. It is ubiquitous in nature, shaping a vast array of physical processes in the atmosphere, oceans, rivers, and even in astrophysical environments
(
Pope 2000
)
. In the atmosphere, turbulent mixing drives the redistribution of heat, moisture, and momentum, significantly influencing weather patterns and climate dynamics
(
Vallis 2017
)
. Most notably, the chaotic motion of turbulent eddies in clouds enhances the mixing of air masses, leading to cloud formation and precipitation. In rivers and streams, turbulence enhances oxygen transfer and sediment transport, which are vital for aquatic ecosystems and the geomorphological shaping of landscapes.

Extending the scope beyond Earth, turbulence is also central in astrophysical and planetary processes. In the formation of stars and galaxies, turbulent gas flows within interstellar clouds controlling the rate at which matter collapses to form new stars. Similarly, in the oceans
(
Galperin et al. 2010
)
, turbulence near the surface and at boundaries drives nutrient mixing, supporting marine life by enabling photosynthesis and biological productivity. Turbulence also manifests at smaller scales, with such flow patterns observed in the larynx and nasal cavities
(
Davidson 2015
)
, as well as in major arteries like the aorta, potentially signaling abnormalities in the valves function
(
Stein & Sabbah 1976
)
. While turbulence introduces complexity and unpredictability to fluid systems, its presence is essential for efficient mixing, energy transfer, and the self-organization of natural systems. Without turbulence, many vital processes in nature would be drastically slowed down or rendered ineffective.

Turbulence in three-dimensional (3D) and two-dimensional (2D) flows differs fundamentally in how energy and structures evolve over time
(
Tabeling 2002
,
Boffetta & Ecke 2012
)
. In 3D turbulence—as commonly seen in atmospheric flows, oceans, and pipe systems—energy cascades from large scales to smaller ones, a process known as the forward energy cascade. This leads to the breakdown of large eddies into progressively smaller vortices, where viscous forces eventually dissipate the energy as heat. Thus, energy moves towards the largest wave numbers. This behaviour is central to the chaotic and dissipative nature of 3D turbulence and is key to understanding energy loss and mixing in natural and engineered systems
(
Kolmogorov 1941
)
.

In contrast, 2D turbulence, such as that observed in thin layers of atmosphere, soap films
(
Sommeria & Moreau 1982
,
Paret & Tabeling 1997
)
, Faraday waves
(
von Kameke et al. 2011
)
or planetary atmospheres like Jupiter’s, exhibits an inverse energy cascade. In this case, energy injected at small or intermediate scales tends to move toward larger scales, causing small vortices to merge into larger, more coherent structures. This explains the persistence of large-scale features like Jupiter’s Great Red Spot
(
Marcus 1993
)
. The difference arises from constraints on vortex stretching: in 3D, vortices can stretch and twist, enabling energy to move to smaller scales; in 2D, such stretching is absent, changing the dynamics entirely. These distinctions are not just theoretical—they fundamentally alter how we model and predict fluid behaviour in different dimensions.

Mathematically, turbulent flows are characterized by their energy spectrum, which is obtained from the Fourier transform of the velocity field

E=\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}|\vec{u}|^{2}d\vec{r}=\int E_{k}dk,

(1)

along with the enstrophy and its corresponding spectral distribution

Z=\frac{1}{2}\int_{D}|\vec{\omega}|^{2}=\int k^{2}E_{k}dk=\int Z_{k}dk,

(2)

where
\vec{\omega}=\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{u}
is the vorticity and
k
is the wave vector. Kraichnans theory of forced two-dimensional turbulence predicts the development of a double energy cascade
(
Kraichnan 1967
)
. When the flow is forced at some wavelength
\lambda_{f}
the energy is passed on to larger wavelengths generating an inverse energy cascade with the same scaling as in three dimensional turbulence (
E_{k}\propto k^{\gamma}
,
Z_{k}\propto k^{2}E_{k}=k^{\gamma+2}
, with
\gamma=-5/3
for
k<k_{f}
). In contrast, for
k>k_{f}
enstrophy is passed to smaller wavelengths and in this range energy scales as
E_{k}\propto k^{-3}
, while
Z_{k}\propto k^{-1}
. In 2D turbulence then, energy transfers form small to large scales (opposite to the behaviour observed in 3D turbulence), while the enstrophy moves towards higher wavenumbers (corresponding to smaller wavelengths); thus, energy and enstrophy propagate in opposite directions through the spectrum.

In computational fluid dynamics (CFD), the simulation of turbulent flows relies on a hierarchy of models that balance accuracy and computational cost. At the highest fidelity, Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) resolves all relevant scales of motion, capturing the full complexity of turbulence without modeling assumptions; however, its computational demands make it impractical for most real-world applications. Outside of DNS, there are two main families of models, ordered by decreasing complexity and accuracy
(
Wilcox et al. 1998
)
: LES (Large Eddy Simulation), which solves the full problem from the mean flow down to the larger turbulent eddies (which are the most energetic), while excluding the smallest scales from the numerical simulation and introducing a model to account for the influence these small scales have on the rest of the flow; and RANS (Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes equations), which are based on approximating the apparent Reynolds stresses (or Favre stresses in the case of compressible flows). The choice among these models depends on the required balance between accuracy and computational cost. In addition to these traditional approaches, the Lattice-Boltzmann Method (LBM)
(
Succi 2001
,
Krüger et al. 2017
,
Mohamad 2019
)
has emerged as a valid alternative framework for simulating hydrodynamic problems.

Over the past decades, the LBM has evolved enough to compete with classical tools in solving complex fluid flows problems. Essentially, LBM adopts a mesoscopic perspective, modelling the fluid as a system of particle distribution functions whose collective dynamics approximate macroscopic flow behaviour.

In this paper, we simulate a two-dimensional turbulent flow using the Lattice-Boltzmann method to compare the energy and enstrophy Fourier spectra with those predicted by the theory. This will further the understanding of the capabilities of the LBM to solve flows in this regime, while also serving as a gentle introduction to turbulent flows for students in higher educations due to the relative straightforward implementation of the algorithm compared to Navier-Stokes solvers.

2
The Lattice Boltzmann method for hydrodynamic problems

When tackling a fluid dynamics problem, a numerical discretisation of the Navier-Stokes equations is necessary. One of the most wide-spread techniques for this purpose is the Finite Volume method, included in the comprehensive suites of most CFD softwares.

Another valid approach to solve such problems is to shift the focus to a different point of view, where instead of focusing on the macroscopic properties of the systems (such as the velocity or density of the fluid on each region of the domain) the collective behaviour of its molecules is studied, for which a mesoscopic study of the system is required. This is the regime in which the LBM operates. Rather than solving the Navier-Stokes equations directly, the LBM is based on the Boltzmann equation, which describes the behaviour of a system of particles moving under the influence of forces such as collisions and external fields. The continuous fluid domain is discretized onto a lattice, and the Lattice-Boltzmann equation is solved using a set of discrete velocities
(
Aidun et al. 1998
)
. This allows for the efficient simulation of complex flow systems, including turbulent ones. One of the advantages of LBM is that it can easily handle complex geometries and boundary conditions, and it is well-suited for parallelisation
(
Krüger et al. 2017
)
.

In this paper we implement a standard Lattice-Boltzmann solver with a D2Q9 (2-dimensional 9-velocities) lattice to describe an incompressible Newtonian fluid. We further assume that the dynamics of fluid can be described by a single relaxation time (SRT), modelled by the BGK collision operator
(
Bhatnagar et al. 1954
)
. The resulting equation is the following:

f_{i}(\bm{x}+\bm{c}_{i}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)=f_{i}(\bm{x},t)-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\left(f_{i}(\bm{x},t)-f_{i}^{eq}(\bm{x},t)\right)

(3)

where
f_{i}(\bm{x},t)
represents the distribution function of the discrete set of velocities
\bm{c}_{i}
at position
\bm{x}
and time
t
,
\tau
is the relaxation parameter, and
f_{i}^{eq}
represents the local equilibrium distribution function
(
Qian et al. 1992
)
described by:

f_{i}^{eq}=\rho w_{i}\left(1+\frac{\bm{c}_{i}\cdot\bm{u}}{c_{s}^{2}}+\frac{\left(\bm{c}_{i}\cdot\bm{u}\right)^{2}}{2c_{s}^{4}}-\frac{\bm{u}\cdot\bm{u}}{2c_{s}^{2}}\right)

(4)

with
w_{i}
being the weight associated to each
c_{i}
and
c_{s}
the lattice speed of sound,
c_{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}
. Lastly,
\rho
and
\bm{u}
are the local density and velocity (respectively) of the fluid, related to the mesoscopic distributions through the expressions:

\rho(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{i}f_{i}(\bm{x},t)\quad\rho(\bm{x},t)\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{i}\bm{c}_{i}f_{i}(\bm{x},t)

(5)

It can be proven that under the right limit the LBM recovers the macroscopic behaviour described by the Navier-Stokes equations (Chapman-Enskog analysis), with the kinematic fluid viscosity being directly related to the lattice parameters as,

\nu^{*}=c_{s}^{*2}\left(\tau^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

(6)

where the
*
represents quantities given in lattice (i.e non-dimensional) units. Henceforth, to avoid cluttering the notation, we assume that all variables are given in lattice units and we will omit this symbol.

However, for turbulent flows, some modifications to the basic LBM algorithm have to be made
(
Mohamad 2019
)
. In this study, we follow the approach proposed by Smagorinsky in 1963
(
Smagorinsky 1963
)
, which introduces the eddy viscosity
\nu_{T}
to complement the kinematic viscosity:

\nu_{T}=C_{s}^{2}\sqrt{2\sum_{i,j}S_{ij}S_{ij}}

(7)

where is
S_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)
is the strain-rate tensor and
C_{s}=0.1
is the Smagorinsly coefficient.

Following Equation
6
, this new addition would imply:

\tau=3(\nu+\nu_{T})+0.5

(8)

\tau
then must be calculated at each time step and for each domain cell increasing the computational cost.

Results

The vortical structures that develop on the wake of the set of disks are shown in Fig.
2
. Note that, in the vicinity of the array of disks, several von Karman vortex streets develop and interact with each other, while further downstream these vortices start to blend together into bigger swirling patterns. As expected, the size of the vortices increases with the disk radius, with larger disks producing larger vortices. An increase in the Reynolds number leads to higher velocities and stronger vortex interactions in the array wake. This behaviour, where small structures merge into larger ones, is characteristic of how turbulence works in two-dimensional flows.

Spatio-temporal energy and enstrophy averages calculated downstream of the set of disks are shown in Fig.
3
as a function of the disks’ radius. In this simulation,
u_{max}
decreases as
R
increases, since the Reynolds number and viscosity remain constant. For this reason, to compare results for the time average, we choose the same time period after the vortex street is fully developed in the domain. Note that both magnitudes decrease with increasing disk size, but increase with the number of disks. Increasing the radius of the disks (while keeping the number fixed), leads to a blockage effect, thus reducing the available space for the fluid to accelerate. This causes a reduction in velocity fluctuations and, consequently, leads to lower kinetic energy. Additionally, the blockage suppresses vortex shedding, resulting in a net decrease in small-scale vorticity. In contrast, increasing the number of disks (while keeping the radius fixed) generates more vortex wakes that interact, therefore facilitating fine-scale mixing and vortex merging, which in turn increases both the kinetic energy and the enstrophy. These differences are larger for smaller disk radii.

4
Conclusions

In this paper we explore the capability of the Lattice Boltzmann method to simulate a two-dimensional turbulent flow that develops in the wake of a set of disks. The exponents predicted for two-dimensional turbulent flows for the energy and enstrophy spectra have been calculated for different Reynolds numbers and sets of disks of varying initial configurations, with the values reported being close to those predicted by the theory;
\gamma\approx-3
and
\gamma\approx-1
, respectively.

As the front velocity increases with the Reynolds number we considered an eddy viscosity function of the strain tensor to better account for the turbulent effects. The results obtained show no qualitative differences using a simplified model with a constant value for
\tau
.

The model presented in this paper serves as a low-cost toy model for the study of two-dimensional turbulent flows, allowing students to gain a deeper understanding of the underlying concepts and governing equations. To support this learning process, we have provided the necessary code to reproduce the previously discussed results, thus facilitating the learning and teaching of this method.
