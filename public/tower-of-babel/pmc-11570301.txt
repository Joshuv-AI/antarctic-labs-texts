Bond behavior of confined rebar in recycled brick aggregate concrete using bending pullout test
Authors: Tanvir Hassan Tusher, Khondaker Sakil Ahmed, Saleh Bin Ibrahim Sarker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The pertinence of recycled brick aggregate (RBA) in concrete structures largely depends on their bonding ability with the rebars. Considering the practical detailing of concrete structures where main rebars are usually confined by stirrups, this study conducted bending pullout tests on 12 beam specimens to study the influence of concrete confinement on the bond behavior of rebar in RBA concrete. The investigated variables include RBA%, rebar diameter (d), embedment length (le), and the ratio of concrete cover to rebar diameter (c/d). Bond stress-slip relationships were acquired, analyzed, and average bond strengths were compared to the AS 3600, CEB-FIP codes, and existing MacGregor's model. The average bond strength of confined specimens was nearly double that of the unconfined specimens. A progressive increase in bond strength was noticed with the presence of RBA regardless of confinement. The confined specimens also exhibited higher deformability, ductility, and toughness values than those of the unconfined beams. At 15% RBA content, the maximum toughness value of the confined specimen was 3078.1 J, while the unconfined specimens reached a value of 278.1 J, only. Similarly, the ductility of the confined beam was found in a range of 1.21–1.60 whereas the value is only 1.04–1.10 for the unconfined cases. Moreover, the confined specimens exhibited a significant number of cracks during their failure, while the unconfined specimens experienced a split type brittle failure. Nevertheless, these findings may contribute to the further development of the bond behavior of rebar in RBA-based concrete structures and thus play a role in the sustainability of construction materials.

Introduction

The quest for sustainable construction materials has become increasingly prominent worldwide in recent decades. Innovative strategies that balance ecological responsibility and structural integrity are required in the building industry. One such approach comprises the incorporation of recycled materials in construction elements as a possible alternatives of natural aggregates. The incorporation of recycled aggregates (RA) in construction, derived from sources like crushed concrete, mitigates the need for primary resources and eliminates the environmental impacts correlated to the extraction of natural stone aggregates. Notably, the rapid expansion of urban development is evident in the global consumption of 20 billion tons of concrete each year [1] whereas, the annual destruction of old concrete structures results in 12 billion tons [2] of construction and demolition (C&D) debris, which is approximately 30–40% of the total solid waste [3]. Moreover, due to the scarcity of natural stone, South Asia produces approximately 310 billion bricks annually [4]. India alone single-handedly produces over 240 billion bricks every year [5] whereas Bangladesh and Nepal manufactured 27 billion and 5 billion bricks, respectively [6]. Moreover, multiple recent studies demonstrated that the European Union (EU) annually generates 0.531 billion tons of construction trash whereas the United States (US) produces 0.191 billion tons [7]. Regrettably, the bulk of this garbage ends up in landfills, with only around 5% being recycled [8]. Notably, over 75% of waste concrete has the potential for recycling, offering a solution to the global scarcity of materials when used in new construction [9]. To guarantee the structural integrity and safety of these sustainable building components, it is crucial to investigate the bond and structural behavior of concrete incorporating RBA.

A good number of studies has been conducted on the physical and mechanical attributes of normal (NBA) and recycled brick aggregates (RBA) [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Qoja et al. [14] examined the unit weight of lightweight concrete made with brick aggregates, which varied between 1685 kg/m3 and 1760 kg/m3. The unit weight of brick aggregate concrete (BAC) falls between the range of normal-weight concrete and lightweight concrete, indicating that brick aggregate yields concrete with a moderate unit weight. In previous studies, crushed BAC is noted to exhibit a compressive strength of approximately 61% of that of natural aggregate concrete (NAC), presuming both have comparable workability [14]. Furthermore, when compared to stone chips concrete (SCC), BAC exhibited a reduced elastic modulus and an enhanced tensile strength of 10%–15% in comparison to that of SCC [16]. However, the flexural strength of BAC is noticed roughly 70% of that of NAC, which is further reduced for RBA [14]. On the contrary, while examining the recycled stone and brick aggregate in new concrete, researchers observed a noticeable decline in mechanical properties, such as compressive strength, flexural strength, elastic modulus, and splitting tensile strength [13,[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Liu et al. [18] performed a regression analysis to investigate the correlation between the 28-day compressive strength, flexural strength, and splitting tensile strength, and the amount of broken brick present. Moreover, Mohammed et al. [10] noticed that the substitution of virgin first-class brick aggregates (VBA) with RBA at levels of up to 50% did not result in any noteworthy influence on the compressive strength of concrete. In addition, Kou et al. [12] demonstrated that RBA exhibits more porosity compared to VBA, resulting in an increased demand for water in concrete and making recycled brick aggregate concrete (RBAC) less workable.

The bond behavior of reinforcing bars in concrete is a critical parameter for the overall performance of new concrete and post-installed rebar constructions that incorporate recycled concrete (RC) [21,22]. Haque et al. [23] claimed a contrasting bond strength behaviors between BAC and SCC, including lower modulus of elasticity, reduced compressive strength, and higher absorption capacity [11] in BAC. Moreover, the reduced weight of brick aggregate in concrete leads to a lighter material with lower compressive and bond strengths compared to SCC [24]. Furthermore, a number of researchers have also looked into how the maximum size of the coarse aggregate, the crushing index, the aggregate replacement ratio, and the compressive strength of the aggregate affect the bond strength of RBAC [[25], [26], [27]]. Among them, one of the major structural properties, the residual cementing property of recycled brick aggregate, was studied by Amin et al. [28], and they noted that RBAC appears to have an enhanced gain in compressive strength than those from SCC. Such enhancement in compressive strength might lead to the augmentation of structural capacity. Therefore, for the same percentage of the RA, the bond strength and slip can also be influenced by the use of an internal hinge as well as due to the inclusion of confinement [29,30].

Pullout tests are widely accepted technique to understand the bond behavior of rebar in concrete [[30], [31], [32], [33]] and the fibers in composite at any scale [34,35]. In addition, pullout bending test using beam specimens are also a popular and established technique where the steel bar's longitudinal flexural bond behavior in concrete beams are investigated. The ACI 408R-03 [29] encompasses four bond behavior test methods: pull-out, beam-end, splice beam, and beam anchorage. However, despite their simplicity, pull-out tests are not recommended due to their inability of offering a qualitative description of bond behavior [36]. Furthermore, the RILEM RC5 bending pullout test on beam specimens is also reported in prior studies [37], along with all four ACI 408R-03 [29] test methods. Although the pullout bending test entails a greater financial cost and involves the intricate production of specimens, it accurately reflects the bond behavior under flexure, which is representative of the stress state experienced by a structural component [30]. In bending pullout investigation, Dong et al. observed that recycled aggregate concrete (RAC) is demonstrated to have 1%–21% lower bond strength than NAC [31,38]. Moreover, the findings obtained from a comprehensive half-beam test reveal a notable decrease of 21% in the bond strength of RAC sourced from various origins when compared to conventional concrete [32].

Moreover, further research demonstrates that confinement is one of the primary determinants that can influence the bond behavior of beam specimens [29]. During the investigation, the effect of confinement can be ensured either by the reinforced concrete beam being in direct contact with support or by ensuring the presence of internal stirrups. Zhao et al. [30] noted that the arrangement of stirrups has the potential to alleviate the impact on bond strength caused by the replacement ratio of RAC. Malvar [39] and La-Borderie et al. [40] found that confined specimens have higher bond stress than unconfined ones, which increases with confinement. Additionally, confinement prevents cracking-related expansion and preserves the interlock between steel and concrete [39,40]. Moreover, confinement can aid in increasing the beam bond's ductility and can contribute to decreasing the rate of load drop post-peak [41]. Furthermore, confinement prevents the appearance of splitting cracks until the concrete components are crushed and the bar fails in a pull-out mode [41].

Based on prior investigations, it is evident that the bond behavior of concrete is influenced not only by concrete strength, embedded length, cover thickness, and rebar diameter, but also by the presence of recycled aggregate and extent of stirrup confinement. Therefore, this study aims to employ the bending pullout test using four-point loading arrangement to investigate the bond behavior of rebar in confined and unconfined concrete beam containing RBA.

Research significance

The incorporation of RBA in contemporary structures has gained notable significance due to its potential to cut energy consumption through lowering the dependence on natural stones or clay burnt brick virgin materials and offering lower expenses in the construction sector. In order to ascertain its appropriateness for structural purposes, it is important to assess its bonding ability with the reinforcement bars particularly in confined situation. While the rebars are placed in concrete elements in a confined situation, there is a notable gap in the literature regarding the influence of confinement on the bond strength of RBAC. Existing equations proposed by Darwin et al. [42], Zou et al. [43], Chapman et al. [44], and code provisions such as ACI-318 [45], AS 3600 [46], CEB-FIP [47], and ACI-408 [29] are appropriate for only normal concrete and there is no established code for RBAC in the literature to date. However, considering the growing emphasis on environmental sustainability and economic viability, it is crucial to study the bonding characteristics of rebar in RBA at confined condition for focusing their application in concrete structures. With these earlier limitations, this study predominantly aims to investigate the bond capacity and failure mechanism of both confined and unconfined beam specimens, with varying proportions of RBA as a replacement for coarse aggregate. Furthermore, the study intends to contribute to the advancement of code regulations of the bond strength of RBAC.

Test program

Specimen preparation

The aim of the research was to ascertain the bond characteristics of RBAC and steel rebar in a beam bond arrangement. The test program consisted of twelve rectangular concrete beams. Each beam had a cross-section measuring 100 × 180 mm and a length of 800 mm, as depicted in Fig. 3(a–d). The selection of the beam specimen size was based on the UTM's capacity and previous studies conducted in our laboratory. Among the twelve specimens six confined and six unconfined specimens were prepared to investigate the confinement effect on bond behavior. The confined beams are comprised of a pair of concrete blocks that are interconnected through steel reinforcement in tension and a steel hinge in compression. The steel hinge is positioned at the top between two concrete blocks to deliver a rolling motion when applying load. In contrast, the unconfined beam was constructed as a monolithic unit, incorporating solely the primary reinforcement. In order to assess the impact of different factors on the bond behavior between rebar and RBAC, variations were made in the thickness of the concrete cover and the diameter of the bars.Fig. 3(a) Slump test, (b) Steel caging for concrete confinement, (c) Reinforcement arrangements of confined and unconfined beams, and (d) Casting of beams.Fig. 3

(a) Slump test, (b) Steel caging for concrete confinement, (c) Reinforcement arrangements of confined and unconfined beams, and (d) Casting of beams.

To mitigate the risk of premature concrete fracture in the edge zone, a deliberate measure was taken to ensure that the bar in the front section of each block remained unbonded over a length of 50 mm. To optimize the confinement effect, it was necessary to incorporate 8 mm diameter steel stirrups at a spacing of 80 mm. This strategic measure was implemented to mitigate the risk of shear failure made in the thickness of the concrete cover and the diameter of the bars. The designated embedment length for confined specimens was determined to be 375 mm, whereas, for unconfined beams, it was established at 400 mm for each block based on prior research. Moreover, to ensure optimal resistance against corrosion, a concrete cover of 42 mm was thoughtfully provided for the 16 mm rebar embedded specimens. Similarly, a concrete cover of 40 mm was meticulously implemented for the 20 mm embedded specimens. This strategic approach aims to safeguard the rebar and enhance its durability in the face of potential corrosive elements.

Test setup, loading protocol and data acquisition techniques

The beam bond test was performed utilizing a displacement-controlled four-point bending method, with INSTRON-manufactured Universal Testing Machine (UTM) capacity of 1000 kN. Fig. 4(a and b) showcases a comprehensive depiction of a test setup along with its schematic layout. The experimental configuration consisted of a robust steel framework fabricated using plate girders, a hydraulic actuator for controlled motion, a servo-motor power supply unit, and steel hinged supports for stability. To evenly distribute the total load P across each half-beam, a spreader steel profile was employed. The measurement of the applied load on the specimen during the test was conducted using a load cell integrated within the UTM.Fig. 4(a) Schematic diagram of test setup and (b) actual test setup for beam test.Fig. 4

(a) Schematic diagram of test setup and (b) actual test setup for beam test.

The vertical displacement at the center of the beam during the loading phase was directly obtained from the UTM. Moreover, two linear variable differential transformers (LVDTs) were positioned strategically at the free ends of the beam specimens in order to measure the slip between the main reinforcing bar and the terminal surface of the beam specimens. A consistent loading rate of 1 mm per minute was consistently maintained during the testing process. The tests were concluded either when the beam specimens experienced splitting failure or when the load-midspan deflection curve reached a nearly horizontal position due to a decrease in residual resistance. As a result, considering the correlation between the applied load and the length of the bond, the bond stress was ultimately calculated.

Crack patterns and failure modes

Numerous investigations have documented diverse failure modes exhibited by concrete beam specimens while performing bond tests on beams. Beam specimens subjected to flexural bond testing may fail in one of three ways: (i) rebar pullout refers to the phenomenon where the rebar, or reinforcing bar, is pulled out of the surrounding concrete due to applied forces. (ii) Concrete spitting occurs when the concrete surface breaks or cracks, resulting in small fragments being expelled. (iii) rebar rupture refers to the failure of the rebar, often due to excessive tensile forces, resulting in a break or separation of the bar. Among these different failure modes, rebar pullout failure occurs when the rebar is firmly restricted by lateral reinforcement. Concrete splitting occurs when the reinforcing bar is not adequately restrained or when the strength of the concrete is insufficient. Conversely, rebar rupture happens when the adhesive force between the reinforcement and concrete is very high. The failure pattern of each tested specimen was observed meticulously. In this investigation, all the beam specimens exhibited failure through splitting of concrete, while the longitudinal reinforcement did not reach the point of yielding upon failure, as illustrated in Fig. 5(a–d). The occurrence of flexural cracks has been noted to initiate in close proximity of the location experiencing the greatest moment, subsequently leading to the development of cracks between the load cell's points. As the applied load intensified within the load cell, a structural failure in the form of bond failure, specifically splitting failure, occurred.Fig. 5(a) Failure modes of all specimens, (b) Splitting failure of confined beam, (c) Splitting failure of unconfined Beam, and (d) Splitting failure of confined beam.Fig. 5

While examining the confined beam specimens, it was noticed that failure is predominantly distinguished by the occurrence of concrete splitting in the vicinity of the steel hinge positioned at the midpoint of the beams. In this study, the containment is effectively established through the implementation of stirrups, which serve to optimize the connection between the reinforcing bars and the concrete, particularly when subjected to compressive and shear forces. The confinement mechanism implemented in this design effectively mitigates the risk of lateral expansion and cracking in the adjacent concrete. As a result, the reinforcement bars are able to maintain their secure anchorage and exhibit remarkable load-bearing capacity. Enhanced ductility, resilience, and load-bearing capacity are achieved, rendering it a crucial factor to be taken into account in the design of high-performance concrete structures. Prior to reaching their ultimate load capacities, these confined beams exhibit the occurrence of multiple cracks, with a majority of them displaying significantly wider dimensions compared to the cracks observed in unconfined specimens.

On the contrary, unconfined beams were devoid of stirrups and lacked the inclusion of a steel hinge at the precise midpoint of the beam. Hence, when subjected to significant loads, the beams primarily demonstrate a tendency towards brittle behavior. Consequently, the structural deficiencies observed in the unconfined beams were predominantly manifested through the development of singular cracks, exhibiting a progressive increase in depth as the applied loads increased. After reaching their maximum load capacity, the specimens experience a critical point where the concrete is unable to sustain the compressive load exerted on the upper surface of the beams. Consequently, the concrete collapses to failure through rupture. Simultaneously, beams demonstrate the occurrence of bond failure, while the reinforcement tends to undergo bending when subjected to increasing loads. This ultimately leads to a displacement at the free ends, which was quantified by employing a LVDT.

Experimental results and discussions

Mechanical properties of RBA concrete

An assessment of the compressive and split tensile strengths of concrete cylinders from each batch was conducted at various time intervals in order to analyze their structural performance. The quasi-static strength test was performed with a loading rate of 0.25 MPa per second. The mean value of three cylindrical samples from every batch was analyzed, and Fig. 6 demonstrates the findings. The provided illustration emphasizes a clear and direct relationship between the percentage of RBA and the subsequent enhancement in compressive strength. The experimental specimens for RBA with 0%, 15%, and 30% content exhibit compressive strengths of 30.7 MPa, 31.1 MPa, and 30.9 MPa, respectively. Moreover, Fig. 6 additionally illustrates the measured tensile strength of hardened concrete after a period of 28 days. The experimental specimens for RBA with 0%, 15%, and 30% content exhibit tensile strengths of 2.59 MPa, 2.49 MPa, and 2.19 MPa, respectively. The experimental results demonstrated that the tensile strength declines more significantly for a 30% RBA compared to an RBA of 15%. Moreover, it is evident from Fig. 6 that the compressive strength shows enhancement after the inclusion of RBA, while the tensile strength is noticed to decline from the control specimen. The contrasting behavior in bond strength compared to compressive and tensile properties may be attributed to the specific effects of RBA. The enhancement in compressive strength may be attributed to its residual cementing capacity that enhances the adhesion within the concrete matrix. In contrast, the tensile strength declines as the RBA content rises because the cement paste becomes less efficient in bonding due to the increasing water demand as well as workability concerns. Additionally, the irregular shape, dimensions, and the presence of micro cracks or pre-existing flaws in RBA compromise its ability to resist tensile stresses, leading to premature failure and further contributing to the decline in tensile strength. However, based on the findings from this investigation, it can be concluded that the best combination for the bond strength of concrete, including RBA, is obtained by incorporating a mixture encompassing 15% RBA.

Bond-slip relationship of beam specimens

The bond stress-slip relationship apparent in Fig. 7(a) demonstrates the characteristic bond-slip behavior and showcases the bond activity in reinforced concrete elements. These correlations have been used in the study of cracking [59,60] and in the finite-element method (FEM) [61]. The bond-slip response often consists of two distinct phases: an initial rise in bond stress resulting from the adhesion between the deformed bar and concrete, followed by a rapid decline that ultimately leads to failure. The observed rise in bond stress may be ascribed to the process of mechanical interlocking, wherein the tension progressively accumulates until it achieves its maximum level of stress. However, as the slip continues, the impact of adhesion progressively decreases, resulting in the development of a pattern defined by separation in the bond-slip curves. After reaching a certain slip, a consistent level of adhesive strength is seen until it eventually fails due to pull-out. Furthermore, the deformed rebar exhibits a greater number of clearly defined bond-slip curve phases and a higher level of frictional bond in comparison to plain round rebar.Fig. 7(a) Typical bond stress-slip curve [62], and (b–g) Bond-Slip relationship of different RBA% and rebar diameter for confined and unconfined beam specimens.Fig. 7

(a) Typical bond stress-slip curve [62], and (b–g) Bond-Slip relationship of different RBA% and rebar diameter for confined and unconfined beam specimens.

The bond stress versus slip response at the midpoint of all the beam specimens are presented in Fig. 7(b–g), respectively. From these Bond-Slip relationships of this investigation it is observed that, regardless of the RBA%, type of beam (Confined or Unconfined), and bar diameter, the bond stress of the specimen increases with increasing degree of slip. Among all the cases, the specimens with 15% RBA show substantially higher bond strength than the specimens with 30% RBA in all circumstances. Throughout this investigation, a maximum 6.87 MPa bond strength is observed, whereas the maximum mid-point slip is 22.12 mm. Besides, it is observed that when RBA is increased from 0 to 30 percent, beam specimens with embedded 16 mm rebar exhibit bond strength increases of up to 8.24% and bond slip increases of 23.3% relative to the control sample. Furthermore, the increase in 20 mm diameter rebar can be up to 16.24% for bond strength and 44.22% for bond slip when RBA moves to 0–30%.

The residual cementing property is the primary factor that accounts for the bond's rise from the control sample. Amin et al. [28] have noted the residual cementing property of concrete containing recycled brick material. During the hydration process between fresh cement and water, the adhering mortar in RBA can self-activate. In essence, this serves as additional cement, which reduces the water-to-cement ratio and can increase the bond strength in newly produced concrete by enhancing compressive characteristics. However, this improvement only becomes apparent once a certain amount of RBA has been replaced with new concrete. On the contrary, a declining pattern in bond strength could be evident for higher percentage replacement.

Effect of confinement in bond-slip relationship of beam specimens

The effect of confinement on the bond-slip relationship in beam specimens of reinforced concrete is substantial. In this investigation, confinement is achieved through the application of stirrups throughout the beam specimens. The bond behavior between the reinforcing bars and the surrounding concrete is significantly enhanced by confinement. In this investigation, the same characteristics of the bond and slip are observed between the confined and unconfined beam specimens which is depicted in Fig. 7(b–g). From Fig. 8(a) it is evident that, while contrasting with confined specimens, a reduction of 41–44% in bond strength was observed for 16 mm diameter rebar embedded specimens while retaining the RBA%, whereas it is 48%–53.5% for 20 mm diameter rebar embedded specimens. Furthermore, the final slip and ductility exhibit the same trend of decline. On the contrary, peak slip is seen to be reduced by up to 83% for unconfined beam specimens of 16 mm diameter rebars, and the reduction can move by up to 50.3% for 20 mm diameter rebar embedded specimens.Fig. 8(a) Bond strength variation, (b) Ratio of bond strength, (c) Toughness variation with confined and unconfined beam specimens.Fig. 8

Parameter dependency

Influence of rebar diameter

The diameter of the rebar possesses an impact on the strength of the bond in both NAC and RAC. Several studies indicate that larger bar diameter results in a higher pullout force [22] but diminishes the bonding strength while using the same concrete blend [63]. From the bond-slip relationship illustrated in Fig. 7(b–g), it becomes apparent that the bond strength of both confined and unconfined beams displays diverse fluctuations in response to changes in the rebar diameter. Upon increasing the diameter of the confined beam from 16 to 20 mm while maintaining a constant RBA%, a notable decrease in bond strength of 12.31% is observed. However, the bond strength of unconfined specimens decreases by 26.84% when the diameter fluctuates from 16 to 20 mm. The observed outcome can be attributed to the fact that as the diameter of the bar rises, there exists a linear growth of the contact surface area between the steel and concrete and a drop in the ratio of ribs per unit length, eventually resulting in a reduction in mechanical interlocking and bond strength [64].

Influence of embedment length

Similar findings are observed for the inclusion of embedment length, which is comparable to the diameter of the rebar. Numerous studies indicate that embedment length improves the pull-out force [72] and the act of embedding diminishes the strength of bonding [21]. This study comprises an examination of a confined beams, characterized by an embedment length of 375 mm, as well as an unconfined beam, which possesses an embedment length of 400 mm. Table 4 depicts the relationship between embedment length and bond strength for all specimens. It is evident that increasing the embedment length, while maintaining a consistent RBA% and rebar diameter, results in a decrease in bond strength from 41.4% to 53.6%. The decline in bond strength can be mostly attributed to the clear cover-to-embedment ratio (c/le), as an increase in embedment length leads to a decrease in the clear cover-to-embedment ratio, impeding the proper connection and adhesion between concrete and rebar, ultimately leading to a reduction in strength.

Influence of concrete cover

The bond strength primarily relies on the thickness of the concrete cover or the magnitude of the concrete volume that affects it. The present study involves the use of specimens with a diameter of 16 mm for the rebar, which are provided with a concrete cover of 42 mm. Additionally, specimens with a diameter of 20 mm are provided with a concrete cover of 40 mm. Table 4 reveals that there is an increase in bond strength ranging from 8% to 26.84% when the clear cover is changed from 40 to 42 mm. This phenomenon can be clarified through the fact that when the cover is increased, there is a corresponding increase in the volume of concrete. As a result, the effort required to take out an object from the concrete can be spread out over a broader area, leading to an increase in the bond strength.

Conclusion

An experimental investigation is performed to understand and compare the bond behavior of confined and unconfined beam specimens containing RBA and NBA through beam bending tests. Key bonding parameters such as rebar diameter, embedment length, concrete cover, and confinement effects are also taken into consideration for this study, alongside the influence of various RBA proportions. This experimental study comprises confined and unconfined beam specimens of a target concrete strength of 30 MPa for two different rebar diameters (16 mm and 20 mm), two different embedment lengths (375 mm and 400 mm), and different RBA percentages. Based on the experimental findings, the following conclusions can be made.(a)The inclusion of RBA increased the bond strength, with the effect being more pronounced in confined concrete. An enhancement of up to 16.24% was observed in RBA compared to that with 100% NBA and the enhancement can be attributed to the residual cementing properties of RBA.(b)The bond stress is noticed to be increased with the degree of slip regardless of the RBA content, with a maximum slip of 22.12 mm. However, the increase in bond strength and slip diminish at higher replacement levels.(c)The confined beam specimens exhibited 1.7–2.15 times higher bond strength than that of unconfined beam specimens. Moreover, the confined rebar ensured significantly higher deformability, ductility, and toughness than that of the unconfined beams.(d)Splitting failure was observed for both confined and unconfined beam specimens without any sign of rebar fracture. Multiple wider cracks were observed for confined specimens whereas unconfined specimens failed in a single crack in a brittle manner.(e)The rebar diameter and the embedded length significantly impacts its bond strength. Increasing the rebar diameter from 16 to 20 mm and the embedment length from 375 to 400 mm resulted in a reduction in bond strength by up to 26.84% and 53.6%, respectively.(f)The experimental bond strength of confined beam specimens aligns closely with the MacGregor model, with a deviation of only 10%. In contrast, unconfined beam specimens exhibit lower values compared to the codes, likely due to the lack of confinement and their reduced bond strength.

This study focuses on the bond strength of concrete beam specimens composed of RBA for both confined and unconfined concrete. The commercial utilization of RBA in the construction industry may be aided by the findings of this study. In future, a reliability analysis can be conducted to assess the uncertainty and variability in RBA bond strength predictions. This analysis will consider factors such as material properties, loading conditions, and environmental influences to develop more robust bond strength formulas and enhance their applicability in practical structural design.
