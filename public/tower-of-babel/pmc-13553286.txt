A reality check for mHealth sleep apps: bridging the empirical void in digital sleep health
Authors: Nicola M Ludin, Christopher J Gordon
Published: 2026

While the clinical sleep medicine world has focused on clinical efficacy, therapeutic protocols, and evidence-based interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), consumers have often been more interested in accessibility and convenience. Individuals experiencing regular sleep disturbance often seek self-help approaches rather than engaging with health systems that have insufficient sleep specialists, long wait times, and clinical protocols that fail to provide sufficient individualization [1]. As such, the commercial sleep wellness market has experienced exponential growth, with consumers increasingly engaging with mobile health (mHealth) applications and consumer sleep trackers [2, 3]. Millions of adults now routinely rely on self-directed mHealth apps to manage sleep health. However, this widespread adoption largely exists in an empirical void, lacking the rigorous clinical evaluations required to demonstrate therapeutic efficacy. There is an obvious asymmetry in evidence between the use of commercial sleep wellness apps and the rigorous evaluation required for clinical sleep therapeutics. This empirical gap becomes highly problematic given the global burden of sleep disturbance, which exceeds the capacity of traditional clinical health systems.

With up to 40% of the global adult population regularly experiencing sleep disturbance, the accessibility and scalability of mHealth platforms offer a potential avenue for public health to manage sleep health outside the traditional clinical practice paradigm. Yet, while meta-analytic data show that mHealth apps can improve sleep quality and reduce insomnia severity, the quality of trials remains highly variable [4]. Accordingly, there is a critical need for high-quality, rigorous clinical trials to establish the efficacy of sleep-related mHealth apps on sleep disturbance in the general population.

A particularly salient and much-needed example is the work of Vazzaz et al., [5] whose large-scale trial provides a rigorous evaluation of the three categories of standalone, audio-based sleep interventions. This work represents a direct, thoughtful scaling of their previous pilot trial [6], which demonstrated high acceptability and feasibility among adults with self-reported poor sleep. Crucially, the architecture of this trial addressed a gap in contemporary sleep medicine. While CBT-I remains the internationally recognized first-line treatment for chronic, clinical insomnia, its intensive framework leaves out millions who experience non-disorder-specific, sub-clinical sleep disturbances, and do not meet formal clinical diagnostic thresholds. By purposefully targeting a working-community population experiencing these early-stage disturbances, Vazzaz et al. have focused efforts where scalable digital interventions can be valuable as a prevention tool.

The primary finding of the trial was that targeted mHealth-delivered audio sleep interventions did not demonstrate statistically significant improvements over the active control. Historically, digital trials have relied on waitlist controls, and concerns about inflated treatment effects are not new. Methodological artefacts, particularly expectancy effects and inadequate control conditions, can exaggerate apparent efficacy in psychiatric trials. This critique aligns with more recent meta-analytic evidence that wait-list controls systematically inflate effect sizes compared with usual-care comparators [7–9]. In contrast, Vazzaz et al. utilized an active, attention-matched control, which separated the specific content of the audio tracks from the artefacts of digital engagement.

While the sleep audio did not outperform the control, the within-group improvements observed across all arms offer an important clinical insight. Being actively enrolled in a study appears to create psychological scaffolding, emphasizing that the context of digital delivery may be an active ingredient in itself. For many individuals, what presents as a sleep problem may actually be a manifestation of an underlying stress problem. Because sleep and circadian rhythm disruption, and stress share a bidirectional relationship [10], the trial’s true efficacy may not lie in the intervention’s content. Rather, by engaging in the study, regardless of which arm they were in, participants prioritized sleep and enhanced their well-being. Actively focusing on improving sleep and well-being served as a behavioral circuit breaker, potentially mitigating the broader stress cycle they might experience. The Reliable Change Index, a self-report measure, showed low rates of symptom deterioration, confirming that the audio interventions for sleep and the daily boost control are safe.

Admittedly, the final sample size fell short of the pre-registered target of 520 participants, due to escalating screening costs and budgetary constraints, leaving the trial technically underpowered to detect very small between-group effect sizes. However, while large-scale digital health protocols can sometimes achieve large cohorts [11], a sample of nearly 500 in a highly structured active-controlled design remains a valuable feat.

Consequently, the clinical implications of this study might be important for primary care. When a patient presents to a General Practitioner (GP) with early-stage sleep complaints, the clinician’s management options are often constrained by a limited toolkit. Knowing that CBT-I pathways are bottlenecked and lacking evidence-based alternatives, many GPs default to prescribing short-term pharmacotherapy, such as benzodiazepines [12]. While these medicines can improve sleep duration, they fail to address the underlying etiology and often carry risks of dependence; they should only be used for short-term management.

While the null result of the current trial means these mHealth apps cannot be prescribed for sleep disturbance, the study advances the rigorous evaluations required for GPs to confidently prescribe digital therapeutics in the future. To facilitate this transition, primary care would benefit from clinical registries or digital formularies that classify mHealth apps by scientific merit.

Ultimately, if we are able to manage and stabilize the chronic and common experience of sleep disturbance in modern populations, particularly among young adults, we must continue to champion rigorous research into accessible, ostensibly consumer-level digital interventions. Vazzaz et al. have conducted a large randomized controlled trial, and whilst previous research has shown that the mHealth audio tools are acceptable, safe and highly scalable, the results from this trial indicate that these are not clinical interventions. We encourage future research on mHealth apps for sleep and circadian disturbances, as digital health will be increasingly used in public health strategies.
