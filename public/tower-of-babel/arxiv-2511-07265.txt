When Intelligence Overloads Infrastructure: A Forecast Model for AI-Driven Bottlenecks
Gamal Refai-Ahmed 1 , Mallik Tatipamula 2 , Victor Zhirnov 3 , Ahmed Refaey Hussein 4 , and Abdallah Shami 5

Abstract
The exponential growth of AI agents and connected devices fundamentally transforms the structure and capacity demands of global digital infrastructure. This paper introduces a unified forecasting model that projects AI agent populations to increase up to ∽ \backsim 100× between 2026 and 2036+, reaching trillions of instances globally. In parallel, bandwidth demand is expected to surge from ∽ \backsim 100 EB/day in 2026 to ∽ \backsim 8,000 EB/day by 2036 ( ≈ \thickapprox 81x) in a single decade. Through this growth model, we identify critical bottleneck domains across access networks, edge gateways, interconnection exchanges, and cloud infrastructures. Simulations reveal that edge and peering systems will experience saturation as early as 2030, with more than 70% utilization of projected maximum capacity by 2033. To address these constraints, we propose a coevolutionary shift in compute-network design, emphasizing distributed inference, AI-native traffic engineering, and intent-aware orchestration. Security, scalability, and coordination challenges are examined with a focus on sustaining intelligent connectivity throughout the next digital decade.

III
Expected Challenges in the Emerging AI-Connected Ecosystem

Despite the promising growth in intelligent connectivity outlined in recent forecasts, the anticipated expansion of devices, AI agents, and bandwidth usage poses significant technical and systemic challenges. These problems stem from inherent limitations in today’s Internet architecture, compute distribution, and protocol design, all of which must evolve to accommodate the demands of a hyperconnected, AI-native digital ecosystem.

III-A

Scalability Bottlenecks in Internet Infrastructure

The current global Internet is designed primarily to support human-driven traffic and moderately scalable services. However, the shift toward multi-trillion-scale AI agents and tens of billions of connected devices introduces unprecedented stress on addressability, routing protocols, and Quality of Service (QoS) guarantees. IPv6 adoption has yet to achieve a uniform deployment globally, and legacy NAT-dependent systems continue to inhibit direct, low-latency, peer-to-peer communication among intelligent agents.

In addition, the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP), the backbone of today’s inter-domain routing, was not designed for the fluid, dynamic, and context-sensitive traffic patterns emerging from AI-to-AI communication. Its limitations in convergence speed, scalability, and trust make it ill-suited for the real-time orchestration of distributed intelligent agents across wide-area networks.

III-B

Latency and Bandwidth Constraints for Distributed Intelligence

The exponential rise in agent-driven services, ranging from digital twins and real-time sensor fusion to autonomous coordination, demands ultralow latency and deterministic bandwidth. Current best-effort packet delivery models struggle to support round-trip latencies below 5 ms, which are necessary for applications such as autonomous vehicle coordination, industrial robotics, and federated learning at the edge .

Furthermore, the projected increase in data volumes (up to 8,100 EB/day by 2036) will require a fundamental redesign of the data plane to support elastic throughput, intelligent caching, and programmable traffic segmentation. Without such advancements, the backbone and metro networks will experience frequent congestion, jitter, and service degradation.

III-C

Compute-Connectivity Misalignment

A key structural problem lies in the growing misalignment between compute availability and network intelligence. Although hyperscale data centers and edge platforms are expanding rapidly, their integration with the network layer remains rudimentary. This results in suboptimal resource utilization, poor intention translation, and high coordination overhead .

Emerging paradigms such as intent-based networking, AI-native infrastructure, and federated orchestration require deeper coordination between compute and connectivity. For example, AI agents should be dynamically placed based on real-time network telemetry, power availability, and workload urgency, but current systems lack this end-to-end context awareness.

III-D

Security and Trust in Autonomous Interactions

The increasing autonomy and scale of agent-based interactions expose the internet to novel threat vectors, including adversarial coordination, identity spoofing, and federated model poisoning
[
13
]
. Traditional perimeter-based security models are insufficient in a topology in which agents independently negotiate, transact, and adapt.

To ensure reliable and secure operation, future networks must embed zero-trust principles, decentralized identity frameworks, and AI-based threat detection directly into the network fabric. In addition, AI agents must be endowed with policy-driven governance to prevent unintended behaviors or malicious collusion.

III-E

Urgency for a Co-Evolutionary Infrastructure Shift

Taken together, these challenges point to a critical need for a co-evolution of computing and networking platforms. The internet of today, designed for resilience, openness, and universality, must be reimagined to support scale-aware intelligence, intent-based coordination, and hyperdistributed infrastructure. If left unaddressed, the anticipated gains from connected intelligence can be offset by systemic fragility, inefficiencies, and vulnerabilities. Hence, this forecast quantifies future demands and underscores the architectural shift required to sustain the next decade of intelligent connectivity.

The projection framework is grounded in a combination of macroeconomic growth modeling and biologically inspired system dynamics, offering multiple perspectives on how connected ecosystems evolve. Initially, the number of connected devices is modeled using the well-established
Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) assumption

Specifically, the number of devices connected to the Internet grows multiplicatively over time, starting from 25 billion in 2026 and increasing at a compound annual growth rate of 5 percent. t

In parallel, total daily bandwidth consumption is projected using an

Although the Gompertz curve also approaches a multi-trillion-agent scale, it does so more gradually, highlighting the nuanced phases of AI ecosystem maturity. Collectively, these three models provide a robust spectrum of projections. The exponential model outlines an upper bound scenario, while the logistic and Gompertz models offer more realistic bounded forecasts that consider adoption barriers and infrastructure saturation. This diversity of models supports strategic planning under both optimistic and constrained technological growth trajectories.

VI
Future Research Directions and Solution Pathways

This section translates the forecasted bottlenecks into a targeted research agenda spanning access/edge, peering/IXP interconnection, and cloud/storage domains.
We prioritize energy-aware scaling, secure multi-agent orchestration, and compute–network co-design, coupling lightweight, deployable mechanisms (e.g., wake-up control planes, predictive traffic engineering, O-RAN energy xApps/rApps, and RIS-assisted links) with the existing modeling presented herein. For each topic, we provide concrete solution pathways.

VI-A

Trillion-Agent Addressability and Routing

Problem:
Section III-A highlights routing and addressability stress under multi-trillion AI agents and tens of billions of devices.

Directions:

(i) Agent-centric addressing overlays with hierarchical scoping and late binding to handle ephemeral/migratory agents;
(ii) Path-aware and compute-aware inter-domain routing that exposes latency/energy/queue state as first-class constraints;
(iii) Control-plane compression and fast-convergence schemes for bursty agent churn.

Solutions to Explore:
Locator/identifier split at the agent overlay, programmable data planes (P4) for agent-flow classification, and incremental deployment via SDN policy offload at peering/IXP edges.

VI-B

Deterministic Low-Latency Fabrics for AI-to-AI

Problem:
Section III-B notes difficulty meeting
<\!5
ms round-trip for coordination workloads.

Directions:

(i) Deterministic paths with bounded jitter using segment routing and time-sensitive queuing at metro/edge;
(ii) Intent-class-aware traffic engineering that separates control microflows (wake-up, coordination) from bulk dataflows.

Solutions to Explore:
Per-intent queueing and pacing, in-network aggregation for telemetry summaries, multi-link aggregation across Wi-Fi 7/6G with fast path reselection.

VI-C

Compute–Network Co-Placement and Orchestration

Problem:
Section III-C underscores misalignment between compute placement and network state.

Directions:

(i) Joint placement policies that consider link congestion, energy budget, and Service Level Objectives (SLOs);
(ii) Closed-loop orchestration integrating RAN/edge/cloud telemetry.

Solutions to Explore:
Model predictive control or reinforcement learning policies embedded in edge controllers and O-RAN Radio Intelligent Controller / near-RT apps / non-RT apps to co-optimize placement, batching, and in-network offload.

VI-D

Peering/IXP Congestion and Asymmetry Management

Problem:
Section IV-B and Fig. 4 forecast rising peering stress due to asymmetric AI-to-AI patterns.

Directions:

(i) Predictive interconnection traffic engineering using short-horizon demand forecasting;
(ii) Policy reconciliation for bursty microservice egress.

Solutions to Explore:
Congestion prediction models at IXPs, admission control for large fan-out agent swarms, and QUIC-based transport tuning for microburst smoothing.

VI-E

Access/Edge Saturation and Radio Co-Design

Problem:
Fig. 4 shows steep risk at Edge & Access under dense agent deployments.

Directions:

(i) Aggressive duty-cycling, wake-up radios, and micro-beacon control channels for agents;
(ii) Edge-side content/function caching for repeated inference paths.

Solutions to Explore:
Two-plane design (ultra-light control vs. elastic data), on-device/near-device inference with partial result caching, and local consensus primitives to defer cloud round-trips.

VI-F

Secure Autonomy, Identity, and Policy

Problem:
Section III-D enumerates adversarial coordination, spoofing, and poisoning threats.

Directions:

(i) Decentralized identity (DID) and verifiable credentials for agents;
(ii) Signed intents with policy constraints enforced at admission points;
(iii) Robust federated learning aggregation and attested execution.

Solutions to Explore:
Intent signing and verification at edge gateways; TEE-backed (or DICE/TPM) attestation for model/version provenance; robust FL aggregators (trimmed mean, median-of-means) and anomaly scoring for gradient updates; control-plane rate limiting with proof-of-resource for swarm join.

VI-G

Storage, East–West Traffic, and Disaggregation

Problem:
Section IV-C and Fig. 4 indicate rising cloud & storage risk approaching saturation.

Directions:

(i) Data tiering and inference-aware caching to reduce hot-path storage I/O;
(ii) Compute/memory disaggregation with congestion-aware fabric scheduling.

Solutions to Explore:
Popularity-aware caches for model shards and embeddings; admission control that factors storage queue depth; programmable NIC offload for shuffle/aggregation.

VI-H

Sustainability and Energy-Aware Scaling

Problem:
Growth trajectories can become energy- rather than capacity-limited (Discussion & Fig. 4 trend).

Directions:

(i) Energy-neutrality constraints in placement and traffic shaping;
(ii) Joint energy–information co-design with renewable and storage awareness.

Solutions to Explore:
Energy budgets exposed to schedulers; energy-credit admission for agent spawning; RIS-assisted links to reduce transmit power; predictive deferral of non-urgent flows to renewable-rich windows.

VI-I

Benchmarking & Interoperability

Directions:
Establish public, privacy-preserving AI–agent traffic traces that distinguish control and data planes and capture burstiness and fan-out; define cross-layer benchmark suites that jointly exercise network, compute, and storage SLOs; specify intent schemas and policy portability across vendors/domains; and enable inter-domain telemetry exchange to support predictive traffic engineering and shared security signals.

VII
Discussion and Conclusion

Although global core infrastructure is unlikely to reach saturation, systemic bottlenecks may still arise in several critical regions of the network. These include edge and access networks experiencing high-density AI deployments, inter-cloud transfers among hyperscalers that generate substantial east-west traffic, radio access layers in 5G/6G environments constrained by limited spectrum availability, and large-scale multi-agent orchestration that depends heavily on centralized inference engines. To address these risks, various mitigation strategies have been proposed, such as the adoption of federated learning frameworks, the deployment of on-device inference capabilities, and the implementation of AI-aware traffic engineering mechanisms, including network slicing and advanced transport protocols like QUIC. This paper introduced a projection framework for the AI-connected ecosystem, validated against historical data. Identified key infrastructure bottlenecks and proposed strategies rooted in decentralization, real-time orchestration, and protocol optimization. The future digital infrastructure must evolve to align with the demands of AI on scale in computational, communication, and autonomy.
