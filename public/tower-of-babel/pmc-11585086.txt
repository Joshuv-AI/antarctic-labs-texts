The diagnositic value of dynamic contrast-enhanced ultrasound for evaluation of tissue oxygen status in rat hepatoma model
Authors: Bo Jiang, LuDa Song, Xiang Fei, JiaNing Zhu, LianHua Zhu, QiuYang Li, YuKun Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypoxia is a characteristic of solid tumors, but whether significant hypoxia exists in the hepatocellular carcinoma remains unclear. This animal study aims to explore the value of dynamic contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) quantitative parameters to evaluate the oxygen status in two rat hepatoma models.Materials and methodsN1S1 and McA-RH7777 S-D rat orthotopic hepatoma models were established. Once the tumors reached a diameter of 10–15 mm, CEUS and oxygen partial pressure (pO2) polarography were performed. Immunohistochemical staining for HIF-1α and pimonidazole was conducted after euthanizing the rats. Correlation between quantitative CEUS parameters, pO2, and the immunohistochemical integrated optical density (IOD) was analyzed to assess the predictive ability of CEUS quantitative parameters for the tissue oxygen environment.ResultEleven N1S1 models and ten McA-RH7777 models were established successfully. There was no significant difference in pO2 (35.5 mmHg vs 32.2 mmHg, P = 0.917), IOD of HIF-1α (13.4 vs 20.0, P = 0.159) and pimonidazole (0.70 vs 1.30, P = 0.926) between the tumor and the peritumoral liver tissue. The pO2 values were correlated with CEUS quantitative parameters including mean time-intensity curves (mTIC) (P = 0.003), peak intensity (PKI) (P = 0.010), area under the curve (AUC) (P = 0.009), area under the wash-in curve (WiAUC) (P = 0.006), and arrival time (AT) (P = 0.033). The IOD of HIF-1α correlated with AUC (P = 0.022), WiAUC (P = 0.009), ascending slope (AS) (P = 0.044), and falling time (FT) (P = 0.009). Multiple linear regression indicated that the “short AT” was an independent protective factor for hypoxia (β = -2.347, 95% CI: -4.948, -0.394, P = 0.022), and CEUS had the ability to predict the tumor pO2 (P = 0.003).ConclusionNo evidence of significant hypoxia was identified in two rat orthotopic hepatoma models. Quantitative CEUS parameters correlated with the oxygen status of the tumor, which could be utilized to predict the tumor tissue pO2.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-024-03523-1.Keywords: Liver neoplasm, Ultrasonography, Hypoxia

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the digestive cancers with high morbidity and mortality rates [1]. Patients with early-stage HCC usually do not exhibit obvious symptoms; therefore, a considerable number of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage. For advanced HCC, comprehensive immune-targeted therapy is the primary treatment [2]. Though immune-targeted therapy can improve the prognosis and survival rates [3], drug resistance may limit the effectiveness of drugs due to the generation of resistant cells. One of the most important reasons for drug resistance may be the hypoxic tumor microenvironment [4, 5].

Hypoxia is a characteristic of solid cancers, which can lead to changes in biochemical metabolism, promoting tumor angiogenesis, increasing the possibility of metastasis, and mediating resistance to anti-tumor drugs [6–8]. Nevertheless, it is still controversial whether there is significant hypoxia in HCC which has abundant blood supply [9]. When a tumor has developed necrosis, hypoxia is inevitably present, and the area of necrosis can be observed by enhanced imaging. However, it is controversial whether hypoxia occurs in tumors early in development when there is no obvious necrosis, and therefore assessment of intratumoral oxygen content may be more valuable.

Severely reduced tumor oxygenation results in aggressive disease, tumor immune evasion, and reduced therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, the characterization of hypoxia (partial pressure of oxygen) is of great importance. At present, the partial pressure of oxygen (pO2) polarography is the gold standard for evaluating and measuring tissue pO2. Traditionally, the Clark's electrode has been used for oxygen measurement in tissue. The initial study to report on the measurement of pO2 in liver cancer indicated that the pO2 in normal liver tissue is approximately 30 mmHg, whereas in tumors it is only 6 mmHg [10]. However, the tumors observed in this study were colorectal cancer liver metastases, rather than HCC. In addition, a study found that the pO2 in a rat model induced by diethylnitrosamine, is not lower than that in normal tissues [11]. Therefore, no convincing evidence of hypoxia in HCC has been obtained using the pO2 polarography method. Moreover, there are few studies investigating the relationship between pO2 and the hypoxic microenvironment in orthotopic hepatoma in animal models by using the pO2 polarography method.

Imaging modalities are also commonly used to assess hypoxia. Blood oxygenation level dependent-MRI (BOLD-MRI) and tissue oxygenation level dependent-MRI (TOLD-MRI) can evaluate the oxygen status by detecting the paramagnetic properties of deoxyhemoglobin in blood or tissues, but their clinical applications are relatively rare [12]. Photoacoustic imaging is a technique that measures the total hemoglobin and oxygen saturation index of tissues by detecting the spectral absorption characteristics of HbO2 and Hb [13]. However, due to the limited penetration ability of light, it is currently mainly used for superficial tissues [14, 15].

Microcirculation is the foundation of tissue blood and oxygen supply. Microcirculation perfusion is the determining factor in the uptake, exchange, and oxygen status of tissue [16]. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is an ultrasonic technology that is now commonly used clinically for differential diagnosis of focal lesions in the liver, and it is also recommended by several guidelines (such as LI-RADS, AASLD, APASL) for a good diagnostic value in HCC [17–19]. Although the value of CEUS for the diagnosis of HCC remains controversial in different guidelines, CEUS can demonstrate microcirculation perfusion with high resolution in real time by using a pure blood pool contrast agent [20]. Therefore, CEUS may have the potential to evaluate the tissue oxygen environment [21–23]. Meanwhile, the CEUS is extensively applied in the clinic, offering the advantages of convenience, cost-effectiveness, and the absence of kidney toxicity. At present, there has been no study on the evaluation of hypoxia status of liver cancer tissues by CEUS. This study aims to investigate the potential of dynamic CEUS for assessing oxygen status in two rat hepatoma models.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Rat N1-S1 and McA-RH7777 hepatoma cells (HonSun, Shanghai, China) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (WelGENE, Daegu, South Korea) and a 1% penicillin/streptomycin mixture (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. Prior to tumor implantation, a trypan blue staining procedure was conducted to confirm the cell viability exceeded 90%.

Animal model

This animal study was approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of the First Medical Center of PLA General Hospital (No. 2023-X19-90). Thirty male Sprague–Dawley rats weighing approximately 240 g (bought from the Animal Experiment Center of the First Medical Center of PLA General Hospital) were randomly divided into two groups: the N1S1 group (n = 15) and the McA-RH7777 group (n = 15). To establish immune tolerance, Cyclosporin A (20 mg/ kg/ day) was subcutaneously injected from one day before to four days after modeling [24]. After anesthesia with 1% sodium pentobarbital (intraperitoneal injection), a suspension of hepatoma cells, comprising N1-S1 or McA-RH7777 cells (5 × 106 cells prepared in 100 µL of medium) and 100 µL of Matrigel (Bioseth, Zhenjiang, China) was prepared. Under the guidance of ultrasound, the hepatoma cells suspension was injected percutaneously (the injection time was approximately 30 s) to a depth of 5 mm below the capsule of the left liver lobe. The vascular structures were avoided as much as possible during the injection. After injection, the needle remained in situ for 1–2 min. Following needle withdrawal, the puncture site was pressed for 30 s to prevent outflow of liquid and bleeding [25]. The procedural sequence is depicted in Fig. 1.

Hepatoma monitoring

From the seventh day after cell injection, the growth of the tumors was monitored every three days with B-mode ultrasound (M9, Mindray, China). Once the diameter of tumor growth had reached between 10 and 15 mm, the rats underwent subsequent CEUS procedures.

Dynamic contrast-enhanced ultrasound

All the ultrasound examinations were performed by a radiologist with more than 7 years of experience in liver ultrasound (J.B.) using an ultrasound machine (Resona 9, Mindray, China) with a linear array probe (L11-3U) [26, 27]. After anesthesia as described above, B-mode ultrasound was performed to localize the lesion before CEUS.

CEUS was performed after conventional ultrasound examination. We used sulfur hexafluoride-filled microbubbles (SonoVue; Bracco, Milan, Italy) as the contrast agent. It was prepared as specified (mixed with 5 ml normal saline and shaken as a suspension) then manually bolus injected with 0.1 ml via the caudal vein, and followed by 0.5 ml of a normal saline injection to flush the catheter. The mechanism index was 0.08. Gain and depth were adjusted properly. Images were continuously recorded from the time of contrast agent injection and for 90 s.

Image analysis

For B-mode ultrasound, we observed the tumor and recorded its diameter (cm), volume (V = length × width × height × π/6), echogenicity, margin, shape, peripheral halo, CDFI grade (Adler grade), and location.

For CEUS, the tumor enhancing intensity (hyperenhancement, isoenhancement, hypoenhancement), enhancing direction (overall rapid enhancement, centripetal enhancement), enhancing heterogeneity, non-enhancing area, washout time, and enhancing shape (regular, irregular) were observed and recorded.

We set the normal liver adjacent to the tumor at the same depth as the reference. The region of interest (ROI) of the tumor was manually drawn. According to the size and shape of the tumor, different small ROIs were drawn within the tumor. The quantitative parameters of CEUS were analyzed by the software embedded in the ultrasound device. We used the auto-tracking function in the software to solve the problem of tissue movement. The mean time intensity curve (mTIC), peak intensity (PKI), area under curve (AUC), wash-in area under curve (WiAUC), wash-out area under curve (WoAUC), arrival time (AT), rising time (RT), time to peak (TTP), ascending slope (AS), descending slope (DS), slope ratio (AS/DS, SR), falling time (FT) and mean transition time (MTT) were automatically calculated from the time-intensity curves of the ROIs. The definition of the quantitative CEUS parameters is shown in Table S1. The graphical definition and example of quantitative CEUS parameters were shown in Figure S1 and 2A-E.

Tissue partial oxygen pressure measurement

After calibrating the instrument as specified, the pO2 polarography (UOE-04 T, Unique Medical, Tokyo, Japan) which is connected to the device (POG-301, Unique Medical, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted under the guidance of ultrasound into the corresponding small ROI points of CEUS (Fig. 2F). Tissue temperature was also compensated during the measurement. After waiting for the logging data to stabilize, each point was measured 5–10 times. Results were considered reliable if the standard deviation was less than 30% of the mean, and the mean was used as the final result.

Sampling and pathological examination

After CEUS and measurement of tissue pO2, pimonidazole solution (concentration 20 mg/ml) was intraperitoneally injected at a dose of 60 mg/kg. Ninety minutes after the injection, the rats were deeply anesthetized and sacrificed by decannulation. The liver lobe containing the hepatoma was harvested, which roughly corresponded to the section observed by CEUS according to the location of the liver. The tumor was split according to the section observed by CEUS. Tissue samples were immersed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for fixation and underwent standard pathological processing procedures. Immunohistochemical staining for pimonidazole, HIF-1α, and hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining were performed.

Pathological analysis

All pathology slides were scanned into electronic pathology whole slide images (WSIs). Quantitative pathology analyses were conducted using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. After appropriate exposure adjustments, the whole slide was initially scanned at low magnification. Regions with specific staining of both tumor and surrounding normal liver tissue were selected. Images of tumor immunohistochemical staining were preserved at a magnification of 400 × . Following calibration of the images using the software, regions of interest were selected for the integrated optical density (IOD) measurement. The average of three measurements was taken as the final value for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0 software. If the data accorded with a normal distribution, they were expressed as mean and standard deviation, and Student t test was used for comparison between two groups. If the data did not follow a normal distribution, they were expressed as median and interquartile range, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparison between two groups. When compared between central and peripheral parts of the tumor, and between the tumor and peritumoral liver parenchyma, Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. Spearman's correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between quantitative parameters of CEUS and pO2 values, as well as immunohistochemical IODs. Parameters showing linear correlation with pO2 underwent multicollinearity testing, and multiple linear regression was used to investigate the ability of CEUS quantitative parameters to predict tumor pO2. A p-value < 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered as statistically significant.

Result

Hepatoma model baseline

Twenty-one rat hepatoma models were successfully established, with a success rate of 70% (21/30), including 11 N1S1 model rats and 10 McA-RH7777 model rats. Nine rats were excluded, including two that died accidentally, four failed in tumorigenesis, three were larger than 1.5 cm in diameter, with extensive extrahepatic metastasis and severe abdominal adhesion.

After modeling, the tumor growth time of N1S1 group was longer than that of McA-RH7777 group (P < 0.001), therefore the body weight of rats in N1S1 group was larger than that of McA-RH7777 group (P = 0.003). The tumor volume in the McA-RH7777 group was larger than that in the N1S1 group (P = 0.041). The baseline characteristics of the two hepatoma models are shown in Table 1. Correlation between CEUS quantitative parameters and tumor oxygen partial pressure

The average pO2 of all points measured in the tumor was 34.2 ± 12.3 mmHg, and the median pO2 of all tumors was 35.5 (21.3, 42.3) mmHg, while the median pO2 of all peritumoral liver parenchyma was 32.2 (26.8, 46.7) mmHg. There was no significant difference in pO2 between tumor and peritumoral liver parenchyma (P = 0.917), nor between central (32.3, IQR: 25.4–38.2 mmHg) and peripheral parts of the tumor (41.0, IQR: 22.3–46.4 mmHg) (P = 0.508). In the N1S1 and McA-RH7777 groups, the median pO2 of all tumors was 36.8 (26.4, 44.3) mmHg and 35.4 (16.7, 44.9) mmHg, respectively, while the median pO2 of all peritumoral liver parenchyma was 27.8 (23.1, 42.2) mmHg and 32.6 (30.2, 47.0) mmHg, respectively. There was no significant difference in pO2 between tumor and peritumoral liver parenchyma (P = 0.673, P = 0.441). The pO2 of tumor and peritumoral liver parenchyma was shown in Fig. 3.

Quantitative parameters of CEUS predict tumor oxygen partial pressure

CEUS quantitative parameters of mTIC, PKI, AUC, AT, and WiAUC were tested for multicollinearity. Finally, the mTIC was excluded, and the other four parameters were included in the multiple linear regression analysis. As is shown in Table 4, the multiple linear regression analysis suggested that the early tumor AT was the independent protective factor for tissue hypoxia (β = −2.347, 95% CI: −4.948, −0.394, P = 0.022), while PKI (β = 0.281, P = 0.090), AUC (β = 0.176, P = 0.220), and WiAUC (β = −0.178, P = 0.859) did not show statistical significance. The prediction formula for tissue oxygen pressure was pO2 (mmHg) = 27.8 + 0.420 × PKI + 0.004 × AUC—0.001 × WiAUC—2.671 × AT. After model testing, the residual variance was homogeneous, and no multicollinearity (VIF > 0.1) and outliers (the observed value with the residual greater than 3 standard deviations) were found. Finally, the predictive model composed of the above four parameters was statistically significant (F = 4.474, P = 0.003).

There was no significant difference in HIF-1α and pimonidazole expression between the tumor and peritumoral liver parenchyma (P = 0.159, P = 0.926) (Fig. 4E-H).

Discussion

In this study, we found that there was no extensive and obvious hypoxia in two rat orthotopic hepatoma models by using pO2 polarography and pathological methods. In addition, we found that the quantitative parameters of CEUS were correlated with tumor oxygen partial pressure, HIF-1α expressions, and may have the ability to predict the oxygen partial pressure of liver tumor tissue.

Currently, the N1S1 and McA-RH7777 models are the most widely used orthotopic liver cancer models in rats. The cell lines of the models are carcinogen-induced primary HCC cells with typical human HCC characteristics, and their inoculation in rat livers can better mimic the growth of human HCC. The modeling is stable and reproducible, which is why it applied in this study.

To make a more comprehensive comparison and increase the reproducibility of the experiment, two common rat hepatoma models were selected to be explored. The results of baseline analysis showed that McA-RH7777 tumor grew faster than N1S1 tumor, so when the size of the tumor met the conditions for successful modeling, McA-RH7777 tumor was larger. Meanwhile, rats in the N1S1 groups were fed for a longer time, so the weight in the N1S1 model was larger. After successful modeling, when comparing CEUS quantitative parameters of these two models, the N1S1 model was more likely to show fast washout performance (exhibiting a larger descending slope), and the other quantitative parameters were basically similar. Therefore, we considered that the CEUS performance of these two models was similar.

In this study, we used the pO2 polarography method to directly measure the oxygen partial pressure of tumor and surrounding liver tissue. We did not find a significant decrease in pO2 in the tumor compared with the surrounding normal liver tissue, which was similar to the former study [11]. This may be due to the tumor size of successful modeling was 1.0–1.5 cm. Firstly, the tumors were small and still in the growth stage, and the tumors were in a highly vascularized stage with abundant arterial blood supply. Therefore, the blood supply was still able to meet the needs of tumor growth, so the oxygen partial pressure did not show a significant decrease. Secondly, necrosis (non-enhancing zone) was rarely detected within the tumor. Although the central part of the tumor had begun to show a trend of decrease in pO2, it still did not show a statistical difference.

Because the pO2 polarography method can only detect the pO2 in a small area of tissue around the electrode, ultrasound-guided puncture was used. This ensured that the ROIs of CEUS were generally consistent with the pO2 measuring area to ensure the comparability of the two methods. It was found that tumor pO2 was positively correlated with mTIC, PKI, AUC, WiAUC, and negatively correlated with AT. It is well known that tumor perfusion consists mainly of arterial perfusion, and to a lesser extent portal venous perfusion [28]. The parameter mTIC and AUC reflect the overall condition of tissue perfusion and washout, while the PKI and WiAUC reflect the tumor perfusion. The increase of tissue blood perfusion can provide more oxygen to the tissue, thus increasing the tissue oxygen partial pressure, which is conducive to the growth of tumors. Meanwhile, AT reflects arterial pressure [29]. An earlier AT representing higher arterial pressure and the faster the rate of oxygen delivery, and finally resulting in the higher oxygen content of the tumor.

In this study, multiple linear regression analysis showed that late arrival time of contrast agent in the tumor was an independent risk factor for tumor hypoxia. Previous studies have shown that AT represents the speed of blood flow and may predict the prognosis of patients after immune-targeted therapy [30]. The result in this study highlighted the significance of contrast agent arrival time (arterial pressure) in reflecting tumor tissue oxygen partial pressure. To some extent, the number of arteries and arterial pressure, reflected by the arrival time, determine the oxygen status within the tumor. In addition, the perfusion related parameters (AUC, PKI, WiAUC) reflect the amount of blood supply to the tissue, playing a role in affecting the oxygen status of the tissue [31].

Although the gold standard "pO2 polarography method" is accurate, it has the limitation of having a small measurement range. Therefore, in this study, immunohistochemical analysis was performed to analyze the distribution and severity of tumor hypoxia. We found that HIF-1α and pimonidazole were mildly expressed as markers of hypoxia only in a few necrotic peripheral areas and near the edge of the hepatic capsule. The above results suggested that no significant hypoxia occurred in most regions of the tumor. Meanwhile, the location of HIF-1α and pimonidazole expression suggests that chronic hypoxia is more likely to occur during the gradual development of the tumor. However, it should also be taken into account that the tumors are still in the growth stage.

No clear correlation was found between the expression of pimonidazole and the quantitative CEUS parameters, which may be due to the low expression of pimonidazole. On the other hand, the expression of HIF-1α was correlated with the AUC, WiAUC, and AS, which were similar to the results of previous studies [21, 22]. At the same time, it was correlated with the DS. It was considered that this parameter may reflect the perfusion-drainage short-circuits in the tumor. The earlier the time of washout, the more perfusion-drainage short-circuits there are in the tumor. As a result, the blood supply to the tissues is diverted before adequate oxygen exchange, leading to the increase of ineffective perfusion, which might aggravate the hypoxia of the tumor tissues to a certain extent.

In this study, we assessed and predictively calculated the intra-tumoral oxygen partial pressure using CEUS quantitative parameters for the first time. We also found that tumors may not be significantly hypoxic if there is an adequate blood supply and without significant necrosis.

This study still had three limitations. Firstly, although the Clark electrode was arranged under the guidance of ultrasound and pathologic sections were taken within the ultrasound surveillance, it was still difficult to ensure absolute consistency among all three methods. Moreover, the Western Blot was not performed to quantify hypoxia markers in tissues, and the obtained IOD values may not be comparable in similar studies. At last, animal hepatoma models are still different from HCC in humans, and the experimental evidence derived from animal may not be applicable to human.

In conclusion, no evidence of extensive significant hypoxia within two rat hepatomas was found by tissue oxygen partial pressure measurements and pathological immunohistochemical examination. The prediction of partial oxygen pressure can be achieved by using quantitative CEUS analysis. This not only answered the question of whether hypoxia exists in tumors at the early stage of tumor formation and development, but also laid the foundation for future assessment of the tumor oxygen environment. Furthermore, it provides an early warning of resistance to immunotargeted therapies.
