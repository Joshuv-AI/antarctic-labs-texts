Acute TGFα drives OA-associated transcriptional and metabolic remodelling in primary chondrocytes
Authors: Emily L. White, Emily A. Day, C. Thomas Appleton, Matthew W. Grol, Frank Beier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesArticular cartilage degeneration is a hallmark of osteoarthritis (OA); however, underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. Transforming growth factor alpha (TGFα) and chondrocyte metabolism have been independently implicated in OA pathogenesis; in this study, we investigated whether TGFα affects chondrocyte metabolism.DesignImmature murine articular chondrocytes (iMACs) were isolated from 4-6-day-old CD1 mice and treated with TGFα or vehicle control (ddH2O) for 24–72 h. Global transcriptional changes were assessed at 48 h by bulk RNA sequencing (n = 4) followed by gene set enrichment analysis. Gene expression was validated by qPCR (n = 4). Mitochondrial mass and membrane potential were assessed using MitoTracker Green and JC-1 (n = 6), respectively. Seahorse XFe analysis assessed changes in cellular metabolism following TGFα treatment, in the presence and absence of OA synovial fluid (n = 4).ResultsTGFα treatment led to differential expression of 1643 genes (1305 upregulated, 338 downregulated), with enrichment in processes related to Response to Reactive Oxygen Species, Glycolysis, and Inflammatory Response. TGFα increased the expression of genes involved in antioxidant defence/ROS production (Sod3, Prdx1, Ucp2, Nox4), mitochondrial quality control (Bnip3), metabolism (Ldha), and inflammation (Tlr4), while inducing depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential in chondrocytes. TGFα enhanced extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) in the presence of OA synovial fluid.ConclusionAcute TGFα exposure induces transcriptional changes associated with ROS, glycolysis, and inflammatory signalling. TGFα decreases mitochondrial membrane potential, while increasing glycolysis in the presence of OA synovial fluid, supporting a link between TGFα signalling and metabolic reprogramming.

Introduction

TGFα, a potent ligand of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), is an important regulator of cartilage physiology and OA pathogenesis [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. TGFα is elevated in OA synovial fluid and in OA cartilage of preclinical post-traumatic disease models [1,5,6], and multiple genome-wide association studies have linked the TGFΑ gene locus to OA susceptibility in humans [7,8]. Importantly, TGFα/EGFR signalling has complex and context-dependent effects. Young Tgfα null mice, but not older, are protected from OA following destabilization of the medial meniscus surgery, indicating an age-dependent destructive role for TGFα [4]. Mechanistically, TGFα reduces expression of SRY-Box Transcription Factor 9 and extracellular matrix (ECM) genes, including type II collagen (Col2a1) and aggrecan (Acan) [1], while inducing expression of catabolic and pro-inflammatory mediators such as matrix metalloproteinase 13 (Mmp13) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (Tnfα)[1]. Interestingly, Wei et al. (2021) showed TGFα-conjugated nanoparticles reduced cartilage damage after destabilization of the medial meniscus surgery in mice, indicating a complex role for TGFα/EGFR signalling in cartilage and the need for more mechanistic studies [2]. Emerging evidence indicates that EGFR regulates cellular metabolism across diverse tissues; however, whether TGFα/EGFR signalling alters metabolic pathways in OA remains undefined.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and dysregulated cellular metabolism have become increasingly important topics in OA-related cartilage degradation [[9], [10], [11]]. Observed metabolic abnormalities in OA articular chondrocytes include reduced oxidative phosphorylation, increased glycolysis, oxidative stress, increased mitochondrial content and inflammatory responses [9,10,12]. While mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of OA cartilage degeneration [13], the upstream molecular signals that initiate and sustain these bioenergetic changes remain poorly defined.

EGFR signalling regulates mitochondrial function, ROS production, and cellular metabolism across multiple tissues and diseases [[14], [15], [16], [17]], yet it remains unknown whether TGFα/EGFR signalling modulates metabolic homeostasis in articular chondrocytes. We therefore hypothesized that TGFα would alter transcriptional programs governing metabolism and mitochondrial function in chondrocytes. To test this, we treated primary chondrocytes with TGFα and then analyzed transcriptomics, mitochondrial mass and membrane potential as well as metabolism. TGFα induced transcriptional changes in metabolic, oxidative stress, and inflammatory pathways while promoting mitochondrial membrane depolarization and increased extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), consistent with enhanced glycolysis. Together, these findings identify a previously unrecognized link between TGFα/EGFR signalling and metabolic reprogramming in chondrocytes, implicating altered cellular metabolism in TGFα-induced OA pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Primary chondrocyte culture

Primary immature murine articular chondrocytes (iMACs) were isolated from the femoral heads, femoral condyles, and tibial plateaus of 4-6-day-old CD1 mice (Charles River, Laval, Quebec) as previously described [18]. All mouse handling procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee at Western University (Protocol 2023-057).

To obtain biological replicates, litters from 2 to 3 pregnant mice were combined, and pups were divided equally into 2 groups for tissue extraction. As such, each biological n value represents an independent primary chondrocyte isolation. Following isolation, cells were resuspended in culture medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, 2 mM l-glutamine, 50 U/ml penicillin, 0.05 mg/ml streptomycin) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ThermoFisher) and seeded for experiments in the appropriate culture vessel. Cells remained in serum-supplemented culture media for 24 or 48 h. Twenty-four hours before treatment, culture media was replaced with serum-free media. On day 3 or 4 of culture, treatment was carried out in serum-free culture media with vehicle control (double-distilled H2O (ddH2O)) or recombinant human TGFα (Peprotech, #100-16A-100UG). In studies emulating a synovial joint microenvironment with symptomatic knee OA, treatment was carried out in the presence of 5% OA synovial fluid pooled from six participants in the Western Ontario Registry for Early Osteoarthritis Knee Study with radiographic and symptomatic knee OA, and moderate-to-severe synovitis confirmed by musculoskeletal ultrasound (Supplemental Table 1). Participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by Western University’s Research Ethics Board for Health Sciences Research Involving Human Subjects (HSREB #109255). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

RNA extraction and isolation

Isolated iMACs were seeded at a density of 8.3 × 103 cells/cm2 in 6-well Nunclon™ Delta Surface plates (ThermoFisher, Roskilde, Denmark), and cultures were treated with 20 ng/mL TGFα or vehicle (ddH2O) for 24, 48 or 72 h (n = 4) in serum-free media. Cultures were re-treated with TGFα or vehicle control after 48 h. Following treatment, total RNA was extracted using 750 μL of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, #15596018). RNA isolation was performed using the Zymo Research Direct-zol RNA miniprep kit (ThermoFisher #501977076) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific).

Bulk RNA-Sequencing

Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed at the London Regional Genomics Centre. RNA concentration and quality were assessed using a NanoDrop (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies Inc., Palo Alto, CA), respectively. Libraries were prepared using the Vazyme VAHTS V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina with rRNA depletion (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), pooled, and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 as a 1 × 76 bp single-end run, using a High Output v2 kit (75 cycles).

Reads were aligned to the Mus musculus genome (mm39) using STAR 2.7.8a and annotated using Ensembl release 112. Features with fewer than 14 reads were excluded, and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA, v4.3.3) was performed using unfiltered gene lists and the Gene Ontology (GO) biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), molecular function (MF), and Hallmark collections from the Molecular Signatures Database [19,20]. Gene sets with a false discovery rate < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Volcano plots, bubble plots, heat maps, and upstream regulator analyses were generated using VolcaNoseR, RStudio/ggplot2, Partek Flow, and Searchlight2, respectively [[21], [22], [23]].

Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted and isolated for qPCR following 24, 48, or 72 h of treatment with TGFα or vehicle (ddH2O) (n = 4) and reverse transcribed following the manufacturer’s instructions (iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit, Bio-Rad #1708891). Real-time PCR (qPCR, thermocycler conditions, Supplemental Table 2) was performed using the 2x SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad #1725200) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative expression was determined using the ΔΔCt method. Briefly, data were normalized to the geometric means of two housekeeper genes, ribosomal protein S29 (Rps29) and hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase, and individual samples were subsequently normalized to the average ΔCt of the vehicle control group at 24 h. Primer sequences and commercial assay IDs are provided in Supplemental Tables 3 and 4.

Determination of mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial mass

Following isolation, iMACs were seeded in triplicate at 1.9 × 104 cells/cm2 in 96-well black bottom PolymerBase plates (ThermoFisher, Rochester, New York), and treated with 5 ng/mL, 10 ng/mL or 20 ng/mL of TGFα or vehicle (ddH2O) in serum-free media for 48 h.

To assess mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial mass, cultures were incubated post-treatment with 30 μM JC-1 (Abcam, ab113850) for 10 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in serum-free media, or 150 nM MitoTracker Green (Invitrogen, #M7514) for 30 min under the same conditions, followed by PBS washes. Live cells were imaged at 40x magnification in serum-free medium supplemented with TGFα or vehicle control using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI 6000B with a Leica DFC460 FX camera). Three images were taken per well, with identical exposure settings for the treatment and control groups within each biological replicate. For JC-1 imaging, exposure times ranged from 700 to 1000 ms (RFP) and 2000–3000 ms (GFP). For MitoTracker imaging, GFP exposure times ranged from 2000 to 3000 ms. Exposure times varied between biological replicates but were equivalent within replicates. JC-1 fluorescence was quantified in ImageJ as the ratio of red-to-green integrated density, with higher ratios indicating increased mitochondrial membrane potential [24]. Mitochondrial mass was quantified using Corrected Total Cell Fluorescence (CTCF), which was calculated from integrated density measurements normalized to cell area and mean background fluorescence in Image J.

Measurement of cellular metabolism by seahorse assay

The Seahorse XFe MitoStress Test (Agilent Technologies) was used to assess oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and ECAR. Isolated iMACs were seeded in duplicate (1.0 × 105 cells/cm2) in XF24 microplates (Agilent Technologies) and treated for 48 h with 20 ng/mL TGFα or vehicle control (ddH2O) in the presence or absence of 5% pooled OA synovial fluid (Supplemental Table 1) in serum-free media. Following treatment, cells were incubated for 1 h in Seahorse XF assay medium supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM l-Glutamine, and 10 mM glucose (Agilent Technologies) prior to analysis. Baseline OCR and ECAR were measured before sequential injections of 1.5 μM oligomycin A (75351, Sigma-Aldrich), 1.5 μM carbonyl cyanide-4 (trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP) (C2920, Sigma-Aldrich), and 2.5 μM Antimycin A plus 1.25 μM Rotenone (A8674, Sigma-Aldrich). Data were analyzed using Agilent XF Seahorse Analytics.

Confirmation of cell viability

The Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK-8; Abcam ab228554) was used to assess cell viability. Isolated iMACs were seeded in triplicate (7.5 × 104 cells/cm2) in 96-well Falcon tissue culture plates (Corning Incorporated) and treated for 48 h with 20 ng/mL TGFα or vehicle (ddH2O) in the presence or absence of 5% pooled OA synovial fluid in serum-free media. Following treatment, 10 μL of WST-8 solution in 90 μL of serum-free culture media was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Absorbance was measured at 460 nm using the FlexStation 3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices) in conjunction with the SoftMax Pro 7 Software. The absorbance values of blank wells were subtracted from the total absorbance values.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as individual biological replicates with the mean ± 95% confidence interval, with descriptive statistics reported as mean ± SD in the Supplementary Tables. N values represent biological replicates from independent primary chondrocyte isolations and subsequent cultures. All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 11. For experiments in which multiple conditions were assessed within the same biological replicate, mixed-effects models fitted by restricted maximum likelihood were used with biological replicate/cell isolation specified as the matched factor. QPCR data were analyzed using two-factor mixed-effects models with treatment and time as fixed effects, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. JC-1 and MitoTracker data were analyzed using one-factor mixed-effects models followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, while Seahorse data were analyzed using one-factor mixed-effects models followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05.

Results

TGFα treatment results in enrichment of pathways related to metabolism, oxidative stress and inflammatory response

Previous studies have examined the effects of TGFα on cartilage using targeted approaches [1]. To define the broader transcriptional response, we performed bulk RNA-seq on primary iMACs treated with 20 ng/mL TGFα or vehicle for 48 h under serum-free conditions. Differential expression analysis identified 1643 genes altered by TGFα compared to controls (1.5-fold change, FDR <0.05), including 1305 upregulated and 338 downregulated transcripts (Fig. 1A). GSEA revealed 414 enriched GO BP terms in the TGFα-treated group compared to vehicle-treated cells (FDR <0.05), with the top 50 gene sets ranked by normalized enrichment score shown in Supplemental Table 5. Cartilage-related gene sets were negatively enriched following TGFα treatment (Fig. 1B and C), whereas pathways related to glycolysis, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and inflammatory signalling were positively enriched (Fig. 2A–D). Additionally, upstream regulator analysis identified TGFα-induced enrichment and activation of several transcription factors, such as STAT1, NFKB1, RELA and JUN (Fig. 2E and F), consistent with increased inflammatory markers observed in RNA-Seq. TGFα increases expression of genes related to oxidative stress, metabolism, and mitochondrial processes

To examine time-dependent effects of TGFα on chondrocyte gene expression, we analyzed individual genes identified by bulk RNA-seq, focusing on cellular metabolism, oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and inflammation. Complete effect estimates, 95% CIs of difference, and adjusted p values are provided in Supplemental Tables 9 and 10. Treatment with TGFα increased expression of the inner mitochondrial membrane protein uncoupling protein 2 at 48 h (adjusted p = 0.0031) and 72 h (adjusted p = 0.0159). Expression of antioxidant enzymes superoxide dismutase 3 and peroxiredoxin 1 was increased at all time points following TGFα treatment (all adjusted p < 0.05) (Fig. 3; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10); however, no significant time-dependent differences in superoxide dismutase 3 expression were observed within either treatment group. Expression of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate Oxidase 4 (Nox4), a ROS-generating enzyme, was significantly increased only after 72 h of TGFα treatment (adjusted p = 0.0305) (Fig. 3; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10). In vehicle-treated controls, Nox4 expression was also higher at 72 h than at 24 h (adjusted p = 0.0134) or 48 h (adjusted p = 0.0430) (Fig. 3; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10). Expression of lactate dehydrogenase A, a key glycolytic enzyme, was increased 48 h following TGFα treatment (adjusted p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10).

With respect to mitochondrial and inflammatory regulators, BCL2/adenovirus E1B 19 kDa protein-interacting protein 3 (Bnip3) was increased at 48 h (adjusted p = 0.0290) and 72 h (adjusted p = 0.0189) of TGFα treatment, with expression higher at 48 than 72 h within the TGFα-treated group (adjusted p = 0.0054) (Fig. 3; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10). Toll-like receptor 4 expression was increased following TGFα treatment at all timepoints (all adjusted p < 0.05). Within TGFα-treated cells, expression was also greater at 72 h than at 24 h (adjusted p = 0.0183) (Fig. 3; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10). TGFα also increased mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase-6 (Mt-nd6) expression at 72 h (adjusted p = 0.0156) (Supplemental Fig. 1; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10). Although TGFα treatment increased neuronal pentraxin 1 and Prostaglandin E Receptor 4 expression at select timepoints (all adjusted p < 0.05), transcript levels for treated and untreated groups were low (Ct > 35) (Supplemental Fig. 1; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10).

Expression of the catabolic mediator Mmp13 was increased with TGFα treatment at 24 h (adjusted p = 0.0385) and 72 h (adjusted p = 0.0492), but not at 48 h. Within TGFα-treated cells, Mmp13 expression was lower at 72 h than at 24 h (adjusted p = 0.0343) (Fig. 4; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10). In contrast, expression of the cartilage ECM components aggrecan (Acan) and type II collagen (Col2a1) was reduced following TGFα treatment, with Acan decreased at 24 h and Col2a1 decreased at 24 and 48 h (all adjusted p < 0.05). Col2a1 expression also decreased over time in vehicle controls, with lower expression at 48 h (adjusted p = 0.0234) and 72 h (adjusted p = 0.0077) compared with 24 h (Fig. 4; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10).

We next examined pro-chondrogenic transcription factors and components of EGFR signalling. Sox9, a master transcription factor of chondrogenesis, was suppressed by TGFα treatment only at 24 h (adjusted p = 0.0110). Importantly, in vehicle-treated controls, Sox9 expression declined at 48 h (adjusted p < 0.0001) and 72 h (adjusted p = 0.0083) relative to 24 h (Fig. 4; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10). Creb5, another chondrogenic transcription factor, demonstrated increased expression following TGFα treatment at all three timepoints (all adjusted p < 0.05) with no significant differences across time within TGFα-treated cells (Fig. 4; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10). Finally, as expected, the endogenous EGFR inhibitor Mig6 was increased in response to TGFα treatment at 24 h (adjusted p = 0.0056) and 48 h (adjusted p = 0.0052), but not at 72 h (Fig. 4; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10).

TGFα induces depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential in chondrocytes

Following our transcriptomic findings, we assessed mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial mass, two parameters indicative of mitochondrial function and dynamics [25,26], following TGFα treatment. Complete effect estimates, 95% CIs of difference, and adjusted p values are provided in Supplemental Tables 11 and 12. TGFα reduced mitochondrial membrane potential at 20 ng/mL, marked by increased green fluorescence (depolarized) and reduced red fluorescence (polarized) (Fig. 5A) [24]. Quantification of the red:green ratio confirmed a significant decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential at 20 ng/mL TGFα (adjusted p = 0.0439) (Fig. 5B; Supplemental Tables 11 and 12). Next, we assessed mitochondrial mass using MitoTracker Green [27] and observed no significant differences between TGFα-treated and control groups (Fig. 6A and B; Supplemental Tables 11 and 12). TGFα enhances ECAR in an OA microenvironment

To further investigate the effects of TGFα on chondrocyte metabolism, OCR and ECAR were measured using the Seahorse XFe Analyzer. Complete effect estimates, 95% CIs of difference, and adjusted p values are provided in Supplemental Table 13. Synovial fluid alone increased ECAR (adjusted p = 0.0468), which was further enhanced by TGFα (adjusted p = 0.0062), whereas TGFα alone had no effect (Fig. 7A; Supplemental Table 13). There was no significant overall treatment effect on basal OCR (p = 0.0879), although basal OCR was lower following combined TGFα and synovial fluid treatment compared with vehicle control (adjusted p = 0.0442) (Fig. 7B; Supplemental Table 13). No differences were detected for maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, or non-mitochondrial respiration (Supplemental Fig. 2; Supplemental Table 13). Discussion

Dysregulated energy metabolism and TGFα/EGFR signalling have been independently identified as important players in cartilage degradation in OA; however, a link between the two has yet to be established. In this study, we show that TGFα treatment of isolated iMACs leads to coordinated transcriptional changes in genes regulating oxidative stress, metabolism, and inflammation. In addition to these transcriptional observations, we observed mitochondrial membrane depolarization at 20 ng/mL TGFα without changes in mitochondrial mass, alongside increased ECAR following combined TGFα and OA synovial fluid treatment. The magnitude of these functional changes was substantial, with an approximately 68% reduction in the JC-1 red/green ratio at 20 ng/mL TGFα and an approximately 47% increase in basal ECAR when TGFα was added in the presence of OA synovial fluid. Together, these findings support acute TGFα/EGFR signalling as a regulator of chondrocyte metabolic state and suggest that metabolic effects of TGFα may be enhanced by the OA joint microenvironment.

Our bulk RNA-seq and qPCR data recapitulated established effects of TGFα/EGFR signalling on cartilage homeostasis [1,4,28]. Importantly, while a prior RNA-seq study of TGFα-treated murine chondrocytes reported similar regulation of matrix genes to our study, metabolic pathways were not examined [29]. In this regard, transcriptomic profiling of TGFα-treated iMACs revealed for the first time enrichment of genes involved in ROS production and antioxidant responses, mitochondrial quality control and mitophagy, cellular metabolism, and inflammation, consistent with metabolic reprogramming observed in OA chondrocytes [1,30,31]. Together, these findings identify metabolic regulation as a previously underappreciated consequence of TGFα signalling in chondrocytes.

Mitochondrial membrane potential is critical for ATP production and the maintenance of mitochondrial function, and sustained depolarization is a recognized feature of mitochondrial dysfunction, as reported in human OA chondrocytes. [10,26]. In this study, TGFα induced mitochondrial membrane depolarization in murine articular chondrocyte cultures at 20 ng/mL; however, basal OCR was not significantly altered following 48 h of TGFα treatment. Increased mitochondrial mass has been reported in human OA chondrocytes and proposed as a compensatory response to mitochondrial membrane depolarization [9]; however, as with basal OCR, no changes in mitochondrial mass were detected following TGFα treatment. The absence of significant changes in OCR or mitochondrial mass, despite mitochondrial depolarization, may reflect the acute nature of our experimental design, whereas chondrocytes within OA cartilage experience chronic stress [12]. Thus, TGFα-induced mitochondrial depolarization may represent an early metabolic adaptation that precedes the mitochondrial dysfunction and compensatory increases in mitochondrial content observed in established OA. Future studies examining prolonged TGFα treatment or alternative mitochondrial measures, such as mtDNA-to-nuclear DNA ratios [32], might further clarify these findings.

Interestingly, we found that glycolytic metabolism, measured as ECAR, was increased by OA synovial fluid and was further increased when TGFα was added to the OA synovial fluid condition, whereas TGFα alone had no effect. This finding is further supported by enrichment of glycolytic gene sets and increased expression of glycolytic genes, such as lactate dehydrogenase A. Together, these data suggest that the metabolic response to TGFα is context-dependent and may preferentially enhance glycolytic metabolism in an OA-like inflammatory environment. Although the overall effect of treatment on basal OCR did not reach statistical significance, combined TGFα and OA synovial fluid treatment produced a significant post hoc reduction relative to vehicle control; however, given the nonsignificant omnibus test, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, these findings are consistent with increased glycolysis reported in OA chondrocytes and with the ability of EGFR signalling to promote glycolytic metabolism in other cell types [12,14].

Inflammation is a central feature of OA pathogenesis [33], and TGFα has previously been shown to promote inflammatory signalling in chondrocytes [1]. Inflammatory cytokines can also enhance glycolysis and disrupt mitochondrial function [31,34], providing a potential link between the inflammatory and metabolic responses observed in our study. The selective enhancement of ECAR by TGFα in the presence of OA synovial fluid therefore suggests crosstalk between growth factor, inflammatory, and metabolic signalling. While inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways were enriched in our transcriptomic data, future studies should determine whether downstream inflammatory mediators and ROS contribute to TGFα-induced metabolic reprogramming in chondrocytes.

An important experimental consideration is the TGFα concentration used in this study. We observed maximal effects on mitochondrial membrane potential in our chondrocyte cultures at 20 ng/mL and used this concentration in all other experiments, where we also replicated established effects of TGFα on cartilage marker expression [1]. Although this dose falls within the range commonly used to study TGFα signalling in chondrocytes (10–50 ng/mL) [1,35], physiological concentrations of TGFα within OA joints remain unknown, limiting extrapolation of these findings to in vivo exposure. A second limitation is that TGFα responses are age-dependent, and our iMAC model uses cells derived from 4–6-day-old mice; therefore, these findings may not fully reflect responses in aged or chronic OA cartilage [4]. Finally, the functional and qPCR studies used relatively small numbers of independent cell isolations, limiting precision and power, particularly for effects with substantial biological variability. Consequently, nonsignificant findings should be interpreted as an absence of statistically significant evidence rather than evidence of equivalence.

Taken together, this study expands our understanding of TGFα/EGFR signalling in cartilage by identifying its role in regulating chondrocyte metabolic programs. While prior work focused on the role of TGFα on cartilage ECM homeostasis, our integrated transcriptomic and functional analyses demonstrate that TGFα induces metabolic gene reprogramming and depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential, establishing a novel link between growth factor signalling and metabolic reprogramming in chondrocytes. By connecting TGFα to chondrocyte metabolism, these findings provide new mechanistic insight into pathways potentially relevant to OA progression and identify metabolic pathways downstream of EGFR as candidates for further investigation as therapeutic targets.
