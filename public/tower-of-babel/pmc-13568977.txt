Multifunctional Thiolated Hyaluronic Acid‐graft‐PEDOT Dressing With Antifouling, On‐Demand Drug Delivery and Electrical Stimulation for Wound Healing
Authors: Jingwen Yang, Jiwon Hong, Anthony Phillips, Bicheng Zhu, Jenny Malmstrom, Devon T. Bryant, Alireza Akbarinejad, Lisa I. Pilkington, Jadranka Travas‐Sejdic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent advances in electro‐responsive wound dressings have enabled on‐demand drug delivery or electrical stimulation (ES) to accelerate healing; however, integrating both functions within a single platform remains challenging. Here, we report a multifunctional dressing based on a conductive, porous substrate comprising a thiolated hyaluronic acid (THA)‐grafted PEDOT copolymer, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA). The material is fabricated by copolymerizing 3,4‐ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT) and S‐((2,3‐dihydrothieno[3,4‐b][1,4]dioxin‐2‐yl)methyl)ethanethioate (EDOT‐SAc) on flexible Au‐coated electrospun PCL fiber mats, followed by electrochemical activation to generate thiols for THA conjugation. The conjugated THA can be released in a controlled manner via reductive cleavage of disulfide bonds (−0.8 V vs. Ag/AgCl). THA incorporation enhances hydrophilicity and conductivity, while forming a hydrated antifouling interface that resists protein adsorption and cell adhesion. In vitro studies using NIH 3T3 fibroblasts show that electrochemically released THA retains bioactivity, promoting cell proliferation, migration, and wound closure comparable to free THA. Furthermore, sequential THA release and ES induce reversible cell detachment without cytotoxicity and enhance intercellular F‐actin polymerization. Collectively, this platform functions as a dynamic biointerface integrating antifouling capability, controlled biomolecule delivery, and electrical stimulation, highlighting its potential as a multifunctional wound dressing for advanced therapeutic applications.

Introduction

Wound healing is a dynamic and highly regulated process involving four phases: hemostasis (stopping of the bleeding), inflammation (cleaning of the wound), proliferation (building new tissue through granulation and reconstitution of skin layers), and remodeling (or scar maturation) [1]. Throughout this process, the body produces a diverse array of growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines, which coordinate tissue repair and regeneration [2]. However, the process of wound healing does not always proceed as expected or desired. The impact of various illnesses or conditions, such as diabetes [3], burns, skin cancer [4] and infection [5], may cause abnormal wound repair. Chronic wounds that remain unhealed for months or even years are extremely burdensome on the individual and their families, affecting quality of life and the healthcare system. Consequently, various approaches have been proposed to address the challenges of chronic wound healing, including wound dressings [6], skin regeneration scaffolds [7], pharmacological treatments [8], electrical stimulation [9], gene therapy [10], and photodynamic therapy [11]. These therapeutic strategies operate through distinct mechanisms and are designed to target specific stages of the healing process. Despite the development of numerous technologies that utilize a range of individual methods, these do not fulfill all the needs for effective and efficient wound healing.

In recent years, electrical stimulation (ES) has been demonstrated as a promising adjunctive therapy for wound healing, particularly for chronic wounds [12]. ES is a non‐invasive technique that utilizes low‐level electrical currents (20–200 µA) [12, 13, 14] to promote tissue regeneration and expedite the wound healing process. ES has been shown to accelerate wound closure and reduce infection [15], enhance angiogenesis and modulate cell migration and proliferation through various mechanisms [16, 17]. For example, ES has been reported to promote keratinocyte migration and re‐epithelialization [18], stimulate endothelial cell alignment and angiogenic activity [19], and modulate immune cell behaviour [20], contributing to improved inflammatory regulation. These findings indicate that ES exerts coordinated regulatory effects across multiple key cellular components involved in the wound healing process. Accordingly, ES has been investigated in a range of clinically relevant wound types, including diabetic wounds [21], burns [22] and repeatedly infected or deep wounds [23], where impaired healing is often associated with disrupted cellular coordination. However, the effectiveness of ES therapy on its own in wound healing is limited due to the complexity of different wound sizes, types and patient characteristics [24]. A recent systematic review [25] also emphasized that while ES improves wound healing, it achieves optimal outcomes when combined with other interventions, such as drug delivery.

On‐demand drug delivery systems have attracted significant attention for wound healing applications [26] as they can improve tissue regeneration and reduce the risk of complications. Smart materials, which respond to various stimuli, such as near‐infrared, UV light, magnetic, ultrasonic, and electrical signals, are the basis of advanced on‐demand drug delivery systems [26]. Among these, electro‐responsive materials have unique advantages in that they enable precise control of drug delivery using either constant or pulsating potentials applied to the drug‐containing substrates [27, 28].

Conducting polymers (CPs) are the ideal electro‐responsive materials for both ES therapy and drug delivery in biomedical applications due to their properties, including tunable electrical responsiveness, biocompatibility and processability [29], as well as the capability to load and release therapeutic agents through volume changes driven by electrochemical redox reactions [30]. In CP‐based drug delivery systems, drug loading capacity is often accomplished by a doping process; small, charged molecules, proteins, enzymes and antibodies have been used as dopants to CPs [31, 32]. Poly(3,4‐ethylenedioxythiophene) (PEDOT) is one of the most commonly used CPs in electroactive drug delivery applications [33]. Although PEDOT has been well studied for drug delivery, challenges remain for its practical applications because of its limited drug‐loading capacity [34]. To address this issue, CPs, including PEDOT, can be electrochemically deposited onto conductive substrates that have tailored micro‐ or nanostructures, such as electrospun fiber mats, featuring high surface area and porosity to enhance electrochemical activity and facilitate rapid ion exchange [35]. The parameters that directly control the morphology or thickness of CP layers during the electrochemical polymerization process also directly facilitate the drug release profile [27]. Covalent binding of a drug to the substrate may improve drug delivery by enabling controlled release through ES‐triggered cleavage of the drug from the surface, resulting in more uniform release and extended circulation time compared to conventional carriers and formulations [36]. Moreover, covalent binding of a payload to the CP may circumvent the limitations associated with drug charge and molecular size typically required for dopant‐based loading strategies. In parallel, advances in implantable electrochemical devices enable localized, self‐sustained therapy [37], opening the way to combine ES with controlled drug delivery in one system.

We have recently reported three studies [38, 39, 40] utilizing a novel functionalized conducting copolymer of 3,4‐ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT) and S‐((2,3‐dihydrothieno[3,4‐b][1,4]dioxin‐2‐yl)methyl) ethanethioate (EDOT‐SAc), for efficient capture and release. In addition, we demonstrated conjugation and subsequent controlled electrochemical release of an antisense oligonucleotide drug [40].

In wound healing applications, the antifouling properties of dressings are a critical consideration. Biofoulant adhesion can trigger adverse biological responses that compromise dressing performance, delay tissue repair, and necessitate frequent replacement to prevent hypoxia, thereby increasing the risk of secondary injury [41]. Electro‐responsive CP‐based substrates, such as PEDOT, are prone to nonspecific protein adsorption and unwanted cell adhesion, which can trigger immune responses and impair device sensitivity [42]. Ashraf et al. [43] reported that poly(oligo(ethylene glycol)methyl ether methacrylate)‐grafted fiber mats exhibited excellent antifouling properties towards bovine serum albumin (BSA) but also reduced human microvascular endothelial cells (HMEC‐1) viability. Another strategy involves incorporating bioactive, non‐adhesive molecules into the CP matrix. For example, hyaluronic acid (HA) coatings on polypyrrole electrodes suppressed fibroblast adhesion while maintaining the cellular activities of normal human dermal fibroblasts (nHDF) [44]. This dual functionality is particularly advantageous, as HA is not only antifouling but also intrinsically involved in wound healing. HA is found extensively in the extracellular matrix (ECM) of connective tissues in both humans and animals, playing a critical role in wound healing by mediating anti‐inflammatory responses, reducing pain, regulating cellular functions, and promoting angiogenesis [45, 46]. Importantly, HA regulates fibroblast proliferation, migration, and extracellular matrix production [47], while being predominantly synthesized and remodeled by fibroblasts during wound healing, positioning these cells as central mediators of HA‐driven tissue repair [48]. Low‐molecular‐weight (20–300 kDa) HA penetrates the stratum corneum more effectively than its high‐molecular‐weight counterpart [49] and results in positive intra‐ and extracellular effects as well as ECM reorganization in fibroblasts [50]. In the meantime, multiple studies [51, 52, 53, 54] have demonstrated that thiolation of polysaccharides enhances cell signaling pathways, thereby supporting key cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, spreading and differentiation, which are advantageous for tissue engineering and wound healing. Consequently, thiolated hyaluronic acid (THA) has been applied in various biomedical contexts, including as interface materials for contact lenses [55], implantable electrodes [56], and contact materials for wounds [57]. In contrast, studies on THA as a drug for wound healing, or the therapeutic effects of delivered THA from a material via electrochemistry, have not yet been reported.

In this work, we demonstrate a multifunctional, antifouling substrate that integrates electrochemical THA conjugation and its on‐demand controlled electrochemical release with ES, in a wound‐healing context. Our highly porous, CP‐based, electro‐responsive, drug‐delivery platform comprises thiolated P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) deposited on electrospun polycaprolactone (PCL). Electrochemical reduction of SAc generates surface thiols that undergo disulfide coupling with THA, yielding P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA). The grafted THA confers hydrophilicity to the substrate, thereby allowing it to resist protein adsorption and reduce fibroblast adhesion under static conditions, while the reductive cleavage of disulfide bonds enables controlled, on‐demand release of THA. In parallel, the same substrate functions as a dynamic biointerface under ES, actively regulating cellular activity.

Compared with previously reported functional dressings, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) offers several advantages: the use of EDOT‐SAc allows electrochemical conjugation and tunable THA loading, providing a high density of binding sites; its highly porous architecture efficiently transduces electrical signals to cells, and the presence of THA imparts hydrophilicity and antifouling performance, even in complex media. We report the design, fabrication, and systematic evaluation of this multifunctional substrate, including its composition, electrochemically triggered THA release profiles, and in vitro fibroblast (NIH 3T3) responses to ES and THA delivery. The wound‐healing efficacy of the released THA was assessed in vitro.

Results and Discussion

The platform (Scheme 1a) consists of electro‐responsive and thiolated hyaluronic acid‐graft‐P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc), termed ‘P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)’, deposited on electrospun porous mats of PCL. The grafted THA establishes a hydrophilic, antifouling surface layer that resists protein adsorption and minimizes fibroblast adhesion. The conjugation of THA is enabled by EDOT‐SAc, where SAc is electrochemically reduced to a thiol, then the thiol undergoes electrochemical oxidative coupling with thiol‐functionalized drug molecules to form a covalent disulfide bond. The disulfide bond is then electrochemically cleaved at ‐0.8 V to afford controlled THA release when desired for enhanced wound healing (Scheme 1b). At the same time, the substrate can deliver ES of +0.1 V to cells to regulate their activities.

Fabrication of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)

The fabrication of our smart, porous wound healing dressing starts with the electrospinning of PCL as the matrix substrate for the electrodeposition of electroactive CP. Optimized electrospun PCL fiber mats were fabricated from 10% wt PCL solution in tetrahydrofuran (THF): dimethylformamide (DMF), 1:1 (vol:vol), as these fiber mats exhibited the most uniform morphology when 9%–14%wt PCL solutions were tested (Figure S1a,b). While the fiber diameters in such fiber mats ranged from 200 to 500 nm, electrospinning of 9% wt PCL solution resulted in fibers with beads and breakages, and concentrations above 10% wt produced thicker fibers and less uniform fiber diameter distribution. Notably, the electrospun fiber mats prepared from a 10% wt PCL solution exhibited the highest electrical conductivity (0.6 ± 0.1 S cm− 1) after sputter‐coating with a 30 nm gold layer (Au‐PCL) (Figure S1c).

P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) was then electrochemically deposited onto the Au‐PCL from a solution containing a total concentration of 50 mM of monomers (with EDOT: EDOT‐SAc = 9:1 mol/mol), affording doped P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc). The thickness of the EDOT: EDOT‐SAc coating was estimated to be ca. 3 µm (Figure S2). After electropolymerization, the thioacetate groups of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) were reduced to give P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SH) by applying a potential of −0.8 V (vs. Ag/AgCl), where the thioacetate esters were electrocatalytically reduced into thiol [58]. In the following step, the THA was electrochemically oxidatively coupled to the thiols on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SH) in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), through application of a positive potential of +1.0 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) to afford P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) (Scheme 2). It should be noted that the THA is expected to be grafted onto the accessible thiol groups mainly on the surface layers of the substrate and likely not fully throughout the whole P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SH) layer. The details of the THA synthesis and the synthesis of EDOT‐SAc are provided in the Figures S3 and S4, respectively.

Reaction schemes for: (top row) the synthesis of THA and (bottom row) electrochemical copolymerization of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) onto the Au‐PCL fiber mats, electrochemically controlled conjugation of THA to the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SH) (oxidation) and subsequent cleavage of THA (reduction).

Morphology of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)

The scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of the Au‐PCL fiber mat (Figure 1a) reveal a uniform and highly porous morphology. This porous structure is expected to facilitate efficient nutrient and oxygen exchange, while also promoting effective waste removal during the wound healing process [59]. The SEM images of the fiber mat with electrodeposited P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) (Figure 1b) indicate the successful electro‐polymerization of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) on the Au‐PCL fibers and substantially increased roughness of the CP‐coated surface. The estimated real surface area of the fiber mat electrode (projected area of 0.25 cm2, 0.5 × 0.5 cm) increased from 2.98 cm2 for Au‐PCL fiber mats to 7.57 cm2 after the electrodeposition of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) (Figure S5), i.e., by a factor of ca. ∼2.5. The increased surface area of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) is expected to provide a large number of active sites for the grafting of THA. The SEM images of the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) after THA grafting (Figure 1c,d) clearly show THA agglomerates dispersed on the surface, appearing as bright white spots within the size range of 0.1 – 1.2 µm2. Energy dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis of the surface indicated a substantial increase in the proportion of oxygen compared to P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) (Figure S6). The Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT–IR) spectrum of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) showed two additional peaks compared to P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) (Figure S7) at 1720 and 1620 cm−1, associated with the C═O stretching and ─NH bending vibrations from THA, respectively. The presence of these signals, alongside the SEM images, indicated THA had been successfully conjugated to the substrate to form P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA). Last, SEM images of the THA‐released substrate (Figure 1e,f) showed a clean surface, which is clearly different from the substrate with THA attached and not released (Figure 1c,d).

To further investigate the distribution of THA on the surface of the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), 2D Raman mapping was conducted. The optical microscopy (OM) image, Figure 1g, shows the region mapped by the Raman scanning, covering an area of 300 × 200 µm, with the measurements conducted using a laser spot size of 1 × 1 µm at 28 µm intervals. The Raman spectrum of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) is given in Figure 1h. A signal at 560 cm−1 on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐THA) corresponds to the disulfide linkage [60], also mapped as shown in Figure 1iI. Meanwhile, the peaks ∼1000 cm−1 are assigned to various bending vibrations, including C─C, C─O, C─OH and CH groups. This region is characteristic of polysaccharides and their glycosidic linkages [61]. The peak at 1359 cm−1 corresponds to the amide III band, related to C─N stretching and N─H bending vibrations, attributed to N‐acetylglucosamine units. This peak was used to map the areal distribution of THA, Figure 1iII. The intensity values were found to range from 205.0 (red) to 1385 (blue), indicating the presence of THA distributed across the surface, albeit in a non‐uniform manner, in agreement with the observations from the SEM images. The nonuniformity may originate from different electro‐polymerization rates of EDOT and EDOT‐SAc monomers [62]. The Raman mapping analysis further confirmed successful THA conjugation to the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) surface via electrochemical oxidative coupling.

Conductivity and Electrochemical Stability of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)

Efficient charge transfer and long‐term electrochemical stability are vital for electronics used on skin for ES, as this enables precise control of cellular responses and ensures consistent therapeutic outcomes during extended use [63, 64]. Deposition of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) onto Au‐PCL significantly enhanced the conductivity relative to the Au‐PCL substrate alone (from 0.6 ± 0.1 to 13.7 ± 1.2 S cm− 1). The attachment of THA only slightly reduced the conductivity (9.9 ± 0.5 S cm− 1) (Figure 2a).

(c) Specific capacitance obtained from the integrated area enclosed by the CV curves at the 10th and 100th cycle. (d) Specific capacitance of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) obtained from GCD curves with a current density of 0.2 mA cm−2 over 1000 charge–discharge cycles. Insert: GCD measurements in the first 5 cycles (left), and the last 5 cycles (right). All data represent the mean ± standard deviation, n = 3 independent samples.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was employed to assess the electrochemical activity of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA). The CVs of Au‐PCL, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) and P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) are presented in Figure 2b. P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) exhibited the highest capacitance of 19.5 ± 7.8 mF cm− 2 at a scan rate of 100 mV s− 1 after 100 CV cycles, compared to 12.9 ± 4.5 mF cm− 2 for P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) (Figure 2c). The superior conductivity and capacitance of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) are expected to enhance delivery of ES to the wound site.

The typical galvanostatic charge‐discharge (GCD) behavior of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) is shown in Figure 2d. From the GCD curves, the specific capacitance of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) was calculated [65] as 10.8 ± 3.5 mF cm−2 at a current density of 0.2 mA cm−2 (compared to 19.5 ± 7.8 mF cm−2 obtained by CV at 100 mV s− 1), with a maximum energy density of 5.4 µW h cm−2. P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) demonstrated excellent stability over 1000 charge‐discharge cycles, retaining 82.7% of its initial specific capacitance. These results suggest that THA grafting enhances both the electrical and electrochemical properties of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), substantiating its use in conductive dressing applications.

The mechanical property of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) was investigated through a cyclic bending test (Figure S8). During the test, the specimen was connected to a potentiostat to monitor current variations in real‐time. A noticeable drop in current was observed upon bending, which recovered to its original value once the film returned to its initial flat state. After ≈20 bending cycles, no significant change in current was detected, indicating that the film on the substrate maintained stable conductivity throughout the test. These results demonstrated that the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) exhibits certain flexibility and mechanical resilience, highlighting its potential for use in flexible wound dressings.

Antifouling Performance of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)

Hydrophilic coatings that support hydration layers maintain biocompatibility and antifouling properties under physiological conditions, making them ideal for wound healing [66]. The antifouling properties of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) were evaluated by their resistance to the adsorption of proteins and cells. To assess the hydrophilicity changes of the substrate after THA conjugation, water contact angles were measured for Au‐PCL, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc), and P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) (Figure 3a). The water contact angle exhibited a decreasing trend from 129.8 ± 4.5° on Au‐PCL to 78.9 ± 0.6° on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) and further to 14.8 ± 0.7° on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA). Furthermore, the protein resistance of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) was assessed by quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation (QCM‐D), allowing in situ characterization of protein adsorption dynamics on the polymer surface (Figure 3b). BSA protein (42 mg/mL) in PBS (pH 7.4) was used to simulate the physiological conditions of human serum [67]. According to standard convention, the frequency was normalized by the overtone number and the Sauerbrey equation [68] was applied to calculate the mass change from frequency shifts. After rinsing with PBS, the amount of BSA remaining on the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) surface was 1.65 ± 0.27 µg/cm2, which was approximately 9× greater than that observed on the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) surface (0.19 ± 0.05 µg/cm2). This demonstrates excellent antifouling performance of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA). In addition, the changes in dissipation (ΔD) of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) are shown in Figure S9. These results corroborate with literature reports of the antifouling properties of the long‐chain polysaccharide structure of (T)HA that provides a physical barrier for the adhesion of proteins [69, 70, 71].

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) was utilized to investigate the antifouling performance of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) in complex protein environments. P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) was incubated in complete culture media for 10 days at 37°C, as well as in PBS for comparison. The Nyquist plots (Figure 3c), obtained for the substrates in the complete culture media, showed slightly increased charge transfer resistance (R ct) of 252.6 ± 7.4 Ω compared to that in PBS (222.7 ± 18.1 Ω). Similarly, the capacitance value showed a minimal change: 9.6 ± 0.8 mF/cm2 in the complete culture media, compared to 8.6 ± 2.2 mF/cm2 in PBS.

Finally, cell adhesion was examined by culturing NIH 3T3 on the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) and on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) for 3 days. NIH 3T3 embryonic mouse fibroblast cells are commonly used in wound research, as they are well characterized and more easily cultured than primary cells [72, 73]. SEM and fluorescence imaging were employed to investigate the adhesion and morphology of NIH 3T3 fibroblast on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) (Figure 4a–c). The SEM results revealed very few cells adhered to P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), in contrast to the cell covering the entire surface of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) (Figure 4a). Similar trends were observed from fluorescence images and quantification of the stained area of DAPI and phalloidin (Figure 4b,c). On P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), only a few round‐shaped NIH 3T3 cells were detected, consistent with the significantly reduced stained area. In contrast, a dense, flat and well‐spread cell morphology was observed on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc). These findings suggest that the antifouling properties of the THA‐grafted surface limited fibroblast adhesion, likely by reducing protein adsorption required for integrin‐mediated cell attachment [74]. However, the uneven distribution of THA across the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) substrate (Figure 1i), together with the porous nanofiber structure (Figure 1c), might have facilitated some cell attachment as the nutrient exchange was not limited and cell protrusions to extend into the matrix were possible [75]. Notably, the NIH 3T3 cells cultured on the 50% THA pre‐released P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) showed the results in midpoint between P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) and P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) (Figure 4b,c), suggesting dose‐dependent antifouling activity of THA. Importantly, in this study antifouling refers specifically to resistance to protein adsorption and mammalian cell adhesion, rather than antibacterial or antibiofilm activity. This distinction is critical, as all in vitro experiments were conducted under sterile conditions with antibiotic–antimycotic‐supplemented media, precluding bacterial growth and associated evaluation. Future study that directly evaluates bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) would be required to more comprehensively assess their potential in wound healing applications.

THA Release P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)

The THA release mechanism in this disulfide‐based electrochemical platform is governed by redox‐mediated thiol–disulfide chemistry established in our prior work for sensing, capturing, and drug‐delivering applications [39, 40, 58, 76]. In these systems, the thioacetate groups of EDOT‐SAc in the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) are electrochemically reduced to thiols that form surface‐confined disulfide bonds with thiolated molecules, followed by their reductive cleavage under negative potentials (RS–SR + 2e−→2RS−) to induce release. Zhu et al. directly evidenced the thioacetate‐to‐thiol conversion and thiol‐mediated coupling via x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy and differential pulse voltammetry, respectively [58]. The subsequent studies demonstrated that the efficiency of capture and release is controlled by thiol density, interfacial accessibility, and electron transfer through the conducting polymer matrix [39]. These studies also confirmed that the released species, including antibodies, aptamers, cells, and oligonucleotide drug molecules, retain structural integrity [38, 40]. Accordingly, the THA delivery observed here follows this established mechanism, where electrochemical reduction at −0.8 V triggers disulfide bond cleavage for release.

To investigate controlled delivery of THA, electrochemical release of THA was performed by applying a potential of −0.8 V across the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) in PBS for 350 s. Under these reductive conditions, cleavage of disulfide bonds linked to THA was expected to occur, resulting in detachment of THA from the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA). The released THA was quantified by measuring the fluorescence intensity of the release solution, where fluorescent adducts of THA with o‐phthalaldehyde (OPA) and DL‐alanine (ALA) were formed, similarly as described by Lee et al. [76] (Figure 5a, Section S1.6, and Figure S10). The ‘release’ when 0 V was applied (taken as ‘OFF state’) was also recorded as a control, with no fluorescent signal observed, suggesting that THA release is electrochemically triggered rather than diffusion driven.

The concentration of THA released from P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) in PBS as a function of copolymer composition is shown in Figure 5b. P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) was polymerized from feed solutions of varying fractions of EDOT‐SAc (2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5 and 15 mol%), with EDOT concentration correspondingly reduced to maintain an overall monomer concentration of 50 mM. Although some nonspecific adsorption of THA onto the substrates was observed after incubation in PBS without applied potential (that could be washed subsequently) (Figure S11), this contribution is negligible compared to electrochemically triggered release. The substrate containing 10 mol% and 12.5 mol% EDOT‐SAc exhibited the highest THA release capacity of 76.9 ± 6.1 and 63.5 ± 7.0 µg/cm2, respectively. The release capacity decreased to 38.2 ± 2.2 µg/cm2 for 15 mol% of EDOT‐SAc (Figure 5b). The increase in THA release efficiency as the fraction of SH‐containing monomer initially increases is attributed to the increase in the proportion of available thiol functionalities to form the disulfide bonds with THA. However, the further increase in density of thiol functionalities on the substrate surface likely results in oxidative coupling of thiol groups on the surface, competing with the thiols from THA [77]. These experiments emphasize that investigating copolymer composition is essential for optimizing the drug‐release capacity of the substrate.

Next, we optimized the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) deposition time (Figure 5c). The composition of EDOT‐SAc was fixed to 10 mol% as this composition showed the highest release efficiency, as discussed above. The deposition times were 40, 45, 50, 55 and 60 s, corresponding to charges of 0.0250, 0.0275, 0.0300, 0.0325 and 0.0350 C, respectively, being delivered. Increased charge corresponds to the increased thickness of the deposited P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) [78]. The highest concentration of the released THA was found when a deposition time of 45 s was used.

The optimum potential for P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) deposition was also investigated over a range from 0.9 to 1.3 V (Figure 5d), with the proportion of EDOT‐SAc kept at 10 mol% and a constant charge of 0.0275 C (45 s deposition time). THA release capacity increased to 91.7 ± 12.4 µg/cm2 for P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) deposited by applying 1.2 V, which was not significantly different from when 1.3 V was used. There is evidence that the higher potential of 1.3 V could possibly lead to overoxidation and degradation of the PEDOT backbone [79]. Therefore, the potential of 1.2 V was used for P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) deposition for the following steps.

The oxidative electrochemical coupling time for P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SH) and THA was investigated over a range from 40 to 120 s (Figure 5e). The coupling time was shown not to significantly influence the degree of subsequent THA release, suggesting that coupling was completed within 40 s; thus, a coupling time of 40 s was considered sufficient for this process.

With the optimized parameters of THA release determined, we next investigated on‐demand THA delivery from P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA). The cumulative THA release over 350 s is shown in Figure 5f. In the first 100 s, 83.7 ± 13.3 µg/cm2 (77.3% ± 10.3%) of THA was released. The total amount of THA electrochemically released on demand at the end of 350 s, by applying a potential of −0.8 V, was 98.6 ± 10.0 µg/cm2, implying an efficient (91.1% ± 0.1%) and rapid electrochemical release of THA. This biphasic release profile is consistent with a mechanism involving (i) fast reductive cleavage of surface‐accessible disulfide bonds, followed by (ii) a transition to a diffusion‐ or depletion‐limited regime as the availability of conjugated THA decreases. A small amount (9.6 ± 1.1 µg/cm2) of THA was also seen to be in the release solution when a potential of 0 V (OFF state) was applied for 350 s, suggesting there was some residual, non‐specifically adsorbed THA “released” from the substrate. The on‐demand, electrochemical delivery of THA from P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) was shown to be controlled through on–off pulses of potential (Figure 5g). When 0 V was applied (OFF, orange), THA exhibited very limited passive diffusion from the substrate, followed by the rapid THA release upon application of −0.8 V potential (ON, blue), indicating efficient electrochemical cleavage of the disulfide bond [80]. The on–off switching behavior suggests that THA release is governed by electrochemical activation of disulfide bond cleavage, rather than diffusion‐controlled leaching. The proposed mechanism is consistent with previously reported studies [38, 40]. The structural integrity of the released THA was verified by 1H NMR (Figure S12), demonstrating preservation of molecular structure following electrochemical cleavage.

If P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) is to be used in real wound healing applications, the THA release behaviour would be expected to be influenced by the wound microenvironment. Alkaline pH (typically > pH 8.5) could promote thiolate formation and accelerate disulfide exchange [81], while temperature affects electron transfer and mass transport [82]. Endogenous thiols, such as glutathione and cysteine, may further facilitate bond cleavage via exchange reactions, whereas oxidative species could favour disulfide reformation, establishing a dynamic redox balance [83]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the conductive P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) exhibited controllable on‐demand delivery of THA. However, the THA release kinetics in a physiologically relevant wound environment warrant further investigation.

Effect of Released THA on Fibroblasts

Although the role of HA in wound healing is well established [84], the pharmacological activity of THA remains underexplored, and clarifying its pharmacodynamics is crucial to determine its cellular effects and optimal therapeutic dose. Emerging research suggests an important role for the extracellular thiol/disulfide redox environments in cell proliferation. Therefore, here we first investigated the effect of different concentrations of THA (0.039, 0.078, 0.156, 0.313, 0.625, 1.25, 2.5, and 5 mg/mL) on NIH 3T3 cells. The cells were incubated with THA in the complete culture medium for 6 days, and their biological responses were tested on Days 2, 4, and 6 (Figure 6a). A PrestoBlue assay showed statistically significant differences in cell viability after a 2‐day treatment with THA concentrations between 0.625 and 5 mg/ml, but not between 0.039 and 0.313 mg/mL. THA concentrations between 0.625 and 5 mg/mL continue to promote cell viability on Day 4, with cells demonstrating progressive adaptation to the presence of THA. A substantial increase in viability was observed at THA concentrations of 1.25 and 2.5 mg/mL on Day 6, while neither the highest nor the lowest concentration of THA (5, 0.039 mg/mL) showed an impact or significantly negative effect on cell viability on Day 6.

Based on the above results, a concentration of 1 mg/mL was selected for subsequent studies, as it falls within the effective range identified and is consistent with concentrations commonly employed in the literature (0.1–1 mg/mL) for in vitro investigations of fibroblast responses to HA [85, 86]. At this concentration, supplementation of HA (1 mg/mL) into the complete culture media showed no significant change to NIH 3T3 growth curve over 6 days, compared to the cells in complete culture media without HA. In contrast, the addition of 1 mg/mL THA to the complete culture medium notably improved NIH 3T3 proliferation at Day 3 (Figure S13). The observation is consistent with previous studies, where the proliferation of normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDF) remained largely unchanged in the presence of 1 mg/mL HA [85, 86]. Nevertheless, HA has been reported to modulate key fibroblast functions including migration, collagen production, and gene expression associated with wound healing processes [85, 87]. The enhancement of cell proliferation after 1 mg/mL THA treatment may be associated with the modulation of the extracellular thiol/disulfide redox balance, which has been reported to play a critical role in regulating fibroblast proliferation [88]. Notably, this study first reports the examination of the biological effects of THA on NIH 3T3 proliferation and therefore no direct literature comparison is currently available. It should also be recognised that such biological responses may vary depending on cell type and origin, culture conditions and cellular growth state.

Based on above results, a scratch wound assay was then performed to assess wound closure under treatment of 1 mg/mL THA, either by directly adding THA into the complete culture medium or by adding THA released from P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), denoted as THAR. Both treatments were compared against complete culture media only as a control (Figure 6b,c). NIH 3T3 cells in the control group exhibited the slowest wound closure, while the cells in both THA and THAR‐treated groups readily migrated to the scratch area and achieved full wound closure on Day 3. The percentage of wound closure was quantified at Day 1, 2, and 3 (Figure 6b). Notably, by Day 2, the wound closure was significantly enhanced by both the THA (77.2% ± 5.0%) and THAR‐treated groups (82.6% ± 8.0%), compared to the control (50.3% ± 5.6%). The therapeutic performance of THA and THAR suggests their similar biological activity on Day 3, with wound closure reaching 100%, while the control was at 77% closure. Importantly, the quantification of THA electrochemically released from P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) (Figure 5f) showed 98.6 µg/cm2 under the application of −0.8 V for 350 s. When translated to a clinically relevant wound dressing area (5–10 cm2), this corresponds to an estimated delivered dose of 0.5–1 mg THA per dressing. Unlike in vitro conditions where release occurs into 1 mL of medium, the actual wound–dressing interface involves a much smaller fluid volume, suggesting that the effective local THA concentration at the wound interface would be substantially higher.

Collectively, these results indicate that THA promotes cellular migration and proliferation, thereby facilitating faster wound closure. Moreover, the THA released from P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) maintained its bioactivity, showing comparable effects to THA addition. This supports the feasibility of electrochemically controlled THA delivery as a therapeutically relevant strategy for wound healing applications.

Electrical Stimulation by P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)

In Section 2.4, the adhesion of fibroblasts cultured on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) was analyzed, and in Section 2.6, the effect of released THA on cells in the scratch wound assay was evaluated. In this section, we investigated the effect of ES on NIH 3T3 fibroblast cells, applied from the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) substrate, and the possibility of THA delivery and ES from the same substrate. The schematic of the experimental setup for ES of NIH 3T3 is shown in Figure 7a,b. A LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity assay was conducted to evaluate the effect of ES on the NIH 3T3 cell viability on Au‐PCL, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc), and P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) (Figure 7c–e). Extensive cell adhesion was observed on bare Au‐PCL and P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) surfaces without applying potential (OCP), whereas significantly reduced cell adhesion occurred on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), consistent with the results above (Figure 4). Notably, most cells found on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) were viable (stained in green fluorescence by calcein‐AM) and showed healthy cell morphology. With the ES application at a positive potential of + 0.1 V, the cells adhered to P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) and exhibited clustered cell organization rather than even distribution (Figure 7c). Based on the chronocoulometric data (Figure S14a), a total charge of 3.024 × 10− 3 C was delivered over 300 s under +0.1 V stimulation. This corresponds to an average current of 10.08 µA and, when normalized to the electrode surface area (0.25 cm2), yields a current density of approximately 40.3 µA/cm2. This value falls within the microcurrent regime commonly reported in both clinical and experimental wound‐healing studies [89, 90, 91], where current densities on the order of 10–100 µA/cm2 have been shown to promote fibroblast migration, proliferation, and tissue regeneration without inducing electrochemical damage. The application of −0.8 V resulted in substantial cell detachment from P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), likely reflecting efficient THA release and conversion to P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SH). In contrast, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) still retained a high number of cells, where the cells showed a more elongated spindle‐like phenotype and directional alignment in groups, which is significantly different from the cell morphology without applying potential (OCP) or the cell morphology on Au‐PCL or P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA). In addition, the chronoamperometric profiles recorded at −0.8 V (Figure S14b) show transient current responses that rapidly decay to low steady‐state values, indicating that charge transfer is dominated by short‐lived interfacial processes rather than sustained faradaic reactions [92, 93]. This behavior supports a controlled electrochemical cleavage mechanism and minimizes prolonged electrochemical perturbation to the cellular microenvironment. Consistently, pH measurements of the culture media after 3 days of ES (Figure S15) showed that both positive and negative potentials resulted in a slightly more basic environment (pH 7.5–8.0) compared to the OCP group (pH ∼7.0). Notably, in the presence of THA, the culture media exhibited a relatively lower pH, particularly under −0.8 V stimulation (pH ∼7.5), suggesting that electrochemically released THA may contribute to partial stabilization of the local pH environment. Among all substrates, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) exhibited the highest number of viable cells, as indicated by the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of Calcein AM (Figure 7d), whereas P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) had the lowest dead cell MFI across all ES conditions (Figure 7e). The Au‐PCL substrate, in contrast, resulted in a relatively higher level of cell death. This behavior is likely related to the soft, hydrated interface that THA provides, which is capable of reducing mechanical mismatch for the cells and improving the abiotic‐biotic interface for charge transfer [94, 95].

The cells appeared widely dispersed across the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) instead of forming dense local clusters when a combined potential of +0.1 V for 5 min followed by −0.8 V for 20 s was applied. Notably, the quantitative MFI analysis from the LIVE/DEAD assay confirmed that the NIH 3T3 fibroblasts detached from the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) into the inner chamber under both −0.8 V and combined ES remained highly viable (Figure S16). Overall, although the antifouling properties of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) suppress nonspecific protein adsorption and cell adhesion at the dressing interface, the system should not be considered biologically inert. Rather, the THA‐grafted surface functions as a dynamic biointerface. Under ES, electrochemically released THA and applied potentials act on cells in the surrounding environment, rather than those adhered to the dressing, enabling modulation of cellular responses. The presence of THA introduces this dynamic biointerface, characterized by enhanced cellular response to positive potential at +0.1 V, and THA release and cell detachment at −0.8 V [96, 97].

ES has been shown to influence wound healing by modulating cell alignment and promoting migration [14]. Cell migration has been reported to be closely associated with the expression and polymerization of F‐actin [98, 99]. Furthermore, it was shown in several studies that low molecular weight HA fragments ( < 250 kDa) play a crucial role in F‐actin polymerization by activating specific signaling pathways [100, 101]. These findings collectively suggest that F‐actin polymerization can serve as an indicator of cellular functionality (motility) following THA and ES treatment. F‐actin polymerization upon adding THA of varying concentrations (50–5 mg/mL) was initially evaluated by flow cytometry. As the concentration of THA increased from 50 to 5 mg/mL, a corresponding elevation in F‐actin polymerization was observed (Figure S17). The F‐actin polymerization of the NIH 3T3 on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) substrates after three days of ES was subsequently investigated. In parallel, an equivalent amount of released THA was added to the culture medium of cells maintained under identical conditions without substrates, designated as the THA group. The extent of F‐actin polymerization in NIH 3T3 cells was compared between untreated control (no ES nor THA treatment), those with a direct addition of THA alone, and in combination with ES (THAR+ES), wherein stimulation was applied across P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) with the potential of +0.1 V for 5 mins followed by −0.8 V for 20 s in complete culture medium for 3 days. The amount of THAR released was estimated to be 24.9 ± 0.6 µg/cm2 on each day (Figure 5f). Compared with the untreated group, the group with THA treatment showed 1.2‐fold in F‐actin polymerization of the fibroblasts, but without statistical difference (Figure 7f,g). Notably, the THAR+ES group resulted in a substantial enhancement, yielding approximately a 1.8‐fold increase in F‐actin polymerization of fibroblasts (p value < 0.05).

In summary, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) provided a biocompatible interface that effectively delivers ES to the cells. Application of a +0.1 V potential on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) enhanced fibroblast cellular responses, whereas applying −0.8 V triggered the electrochemical release of THA and induced cell detachment. This process enabled a dynamic cell detachment accompanied by THA release, without eliciting any detectable cytotoxic effects. The observed upregulation of intercellular F‐actin expression potentially suggests enhanced cellular functionality resulting from the favorable effects of THA release and ES.

Conclusion

This study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first demonstration of a CP‐based substrate capable of integrating antifouling property, electrochemically triggered release of covalently substrate‐conjugated drug with ES, for potential wound healing applications. A highly porous, conductive P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) substrate with an antifouling THA layer was developed to achieve the functionalities of on‐demand delivery of THA and ES to the cells.

Compared to conventional electroresponsive CP‐based wound dressings, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) demonstrates several distinct advantages. First, surface grafting of THA significantly enhances hydrophilicity and antifouling properties, effectively resisting protein adsorption and minimizing cell adhesion. Second, the high THA release efficiency (98.6 ± 10.0 µg/cm2, 91.1% ± 0.1%) stems from the combined effects of the optimized substrate composition and release conditions and the porosity of the conductive substrate. Thirdly, the electroresponsive redox behavior of the disulfide bond, regulated via electrochemistry, enables the precise and controllable release of THA without compromising the bioactivity of THA. Finally, the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) maintains high fibroblast viability under both the positive potential of ES (+0.1 V) and negative potential (−0.8 V) at which THA is released, accompanied by cell detachment. The combined application of THA release and ES therapy exhibited a pronounced positive effect on intercellular F‐actin polymerization, which potentially indicates enhanced fibroblast motility.

These features demonstrate that P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) functions as a multifunctional wound dressing, integrating antifouling behavior, redox‐controlled drug release and electrical stimulation. The THA‐grafted surface minimizes cell adhesion at the dressing–tissue interface, enabling atraumatic removal, while electrochemically released THA and ES modulate cellular responses in the surrounding environment. This system operates as a dynamic biointerface, transitioning from a low‐adhesion state to a cell‐responsive interface under stimulation.

The current study is limited to NIH 3T3 mouse fibroblast model and does not capture the full complexity of wound healing processes in the body, including re‐epithelialization, angiogenesis, and inflammatory regulation. Validations, such as (1) additional, more physiologically relevant cell types, particularly human dermal fibroblasts [102] and keratinocytes [18], (2) co‐culture of two or more wound‐relevant cell types [103], (3) ex vivo skin wound models [104], and (4) an in vivo wound model [105], would further strengthen the clinical translation possibilities. This study focused on establishing material design principles, electrochemical functionality, and initial biological responses of the system. Future studies will extend to in vivo wound models to evaluate therapeutic efficacy and biocompatibility in a more physiologically relevant context.

Experimental Methods and Materials

Chemical Reagents

PCL (MW 90 kDa), epichlorohydrin, 5,5'‐dithiobis (2‐nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), and lithium perchlorate (LiClO4), PBS tablet (pH 7.2–7.6) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Merck, MO, US). 3,4‐Dimethoxythiophene (EDOT), p‐toluene sulfonic acid, thioacetic acid, sodium hyaluronate (HA, M w 50–200 kDa), cysteamine hydrochloride (CSA•HCl), 1‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl)‐3‐ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDCl), N‐hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), ALA and OPA were purchased from AK Scientific (CA, US). BSA (standard grade) was purchased from pH Scientific (Auckland, NZ). Solvents, including deuterium oxide (D2O), acetonitrile, DMF, THF, were analytical reagent grade and procured from Sigma–Aldrich (Merck, St. Louis, MO, US). All chemicals were used as purchased without any further purification, unless specified.

Synthesis and Characterization of THA

The synthesis of THA was carried out with some modifications to the previously reported method [106]. HA (400 mg) was dissolved in deionized water (DI water, 80 mL) at room temperature. EDCl and NHS (1000 and 600 mg, respectively) were added, and the pH of the mixture was adjusted to 5.5 with HCl (0.1 mol). Subsequently, CSA•HCl (500 mg) was added to the mixture, the pH was adjusted to 4.5, and the mixture was then reacted for 48 h. The reaction mixture (100 mL) was dialyzed in 1 L acidic aqueous solution (pH 3.5, adjusted with 0.1 M HCl), including NaCl (0.1 M) for 72 h using dialysis sacks (M w 12 kDa cut‐off; Merck, MO, US). The dialysis was carried out at 25°C, and the aqueous solution was changed every 24 h. Finally, the dialysis solution was lyophilized for 48 h to obtain THA (220.6 mg, yield 55.1%). The THA was then stored at 4°C until further use.

Solutions (5 mg/mL) of THA and pure HA were independently prepared in D2O and analyzed with 1H NMR using a 400 MHz Bruker Avance 400 spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). Ellman's assay [107, 108] was used to quantify the sulfhydryl group content in THA, which is detailed in Section S1.1. The calibration curve was prepared using cysteamine (0.1–1.2 mM) as the standard. The 1H NMR spectrum of THA and the substitution degree of sulfhydryl groups on THA obtained from the calibration curve were 17.5% ± 2.5%, shown in Figure S3.

Fabrication of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)

The fabrication and optimization of the Au‐PCL fibers using 10%wt–14%wt PCL solution is detailed in Section S1.2, with the SEM‐imaged morphology, fiber diameter distribution, and conductivity of the resulting fiber mats presented in Figure S1. Based on the results, a 10% wt PCL solution in DMF and THF (1:1) was employed to fabricate the fiber mats by electrospinning. The conductive porous substrate, Au‐PCL, was formed by sputter‐coating a 30 nm gold layer on the 10% wt PCL fiber mats on both sides of the substrate at a speed of 8 nm min−1 using a sputter coater (Q150RS, Quorum Technologies, UK). Prior to electrochemical deposition, Au‐coated PCL electrospun fiber mats were cleaned by sonication in an ethanol/DI water mixture (1:1, v/v) for 5 min.

S‐((2,3‐Dihydrothieno[3,4‐b][1,4]dioxin‐2‐yl)methyl) ethanethioate (EDOT‐SAc) was synthesized according to the published methods [58], also outlined in Section S1.3, with the synthetic scheme and 1H NMR of the product shown in Figure S4. The electrochemical copolymerization of EDOT and EDOT‐SAc, 0.05 M in total, was carried out on cleaned Au‐PCL (5 × 10 mm), using a potentiostat CHI660 (Metrohm, Herisau, Switzerland) in 0.1 M LiClO4 in water: acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). Electrochemical deposition was performed with a three‐electrode system, where the Au‐PCL mat was used as the working electrode, platinum mesh (MW‐1033, Basi, USA) as the counter electrode and Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode (MF‐2052, Basi, USA). The copolymerization potential was +1.0 V applied for 45 s. All potentials are referenced against Ag/AgCl unless otherwise stated. The resultant substrate, with a deposition area of 5 × 5 mm, is termed “P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc)” herein.

The acetylthiomethyl groups on EDOT‐SAc monomers were reductively converted into EDOT‐SH (‘activated’) by application of −0.8 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) for 60 s in PBS. The activated surface was exposed to an oxidation potential of +1.0 V for 60 s, in a freshly prepared THA solution (5 mg/mL THA in PBS, 2 mL) to enable the coupling of THA to the substrate. The substrate was then rinsed with copious amounts of water to remove the unbonded THA molecules and incubated in 2 mL PBS before THA release [39, 58].

Characterization of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)

A SEM (FEI Quanta 600F, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) connected with an EDX (Octane Elect SDD, JEOL, Japan) detector (SEM–EDX) was used to observe the morphology of substrates. The size of the projected area of the THA molecule grafted on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), as shown in the SEM figures (Figure 1), was analyzed by Image J software (version 2.1, Fiji). FT‐IR (Vertex 70, Bruker, USA) was used to confirm the composition of the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), and Raman spectroscopy mapping was carried out to detect the THA distribution using a LabRAM HR Evolution Raman system (Horiba, Tokyo, Japan) coupled with an external diode laser source (532 nm, 80 mW) and a confocal microscope. A 4‐point probe conductivity meter (RM3000, Jandel Engineering Limited, UK) was used to measure the conductivity of the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) before and after THA‐grafting. The apparent water contact angles of the substrate surfaces were measured using an Attension Theta Optical Tensiometers (Biolin Scientific, Gothenburg, Sweden). Real‐time conductivity changes of P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) during mechanical deformation were also monitored by performing I‐t measurements using a two‐electrode setup connected to a potentiostat CHI660. The antifouling properties and the THA conjugation‐release processes were monitored by Quartz Crystal Microbalance with Dissipation (QCM‐D, Q‐sense analyzer instrument, Biolin Scientific, Gothenburg, Sweden). Details of the methodology are given in Section S1.4. All the electrochemical characterization method measurements (CV, GCD, EIS) are detailed in Section S1.5.

Electrically Controlled THA Release

THA was released from the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) synthesized with different molar proportions of EDOT‐SAc (2.5–15 mol% in 0.05 M), with different P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) deposition potentials (0.9–1.3 V) and polymerization times (40–60 s). The optimized composition was identified as 10 mol% EDOT‐SAc (90% EDOT) with electro‐polymerization at +1.2 V for 45 s. Additionally, the incubation time with THA was varied between 40 to 120 s using P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SH) to determine the optimal conditions for maximum release up to 350 s. The working electrode was P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), the counter electrode was platinum mesh (MW‐1033, Basi, USA) and the reference electrode was Ag/AgCl (MF‐2052, Basi, USA) using PBS (2 mL) as the electrolyte.

The on‐demand delivery profile of THA released from the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) (Figure 5) was characterized by applying a constant potential of −0.8 V over a total cumulative release duration of 400 s, in 50 s intervals. The PBS containing released THA (2 mL) was collected and replaced with fresh PBS at each interval. The amounts of the THA released were determined by measuring the fluorescence intensity in the collected PBS at λex/λem = 330/440 nm using a fluorescence spectrometer (LS‐50B, Hopkinton, MA, USA). The THA quantification protocol is detailed in Section S1.6, and the calibration curve for THA is shown in Figure S10.

Solutions (49.3 µg/mL) of THA released from the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) were freeze‐dried overnight and reconstituted in 1 mL D2O and analyzed with 1H NMR using a 400 MHz Bruker Avance 400 spectrometer (Bruker, Germany).

Bioassay Reagents

Mouse NIH 3T3 fibroblasts from ATCC (Cat# CRL1658) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) from Moregate BioTech (New Zealand) were used. Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; high glucose, L‐glutamine, phenol red), Antibiotic‐Antimycotic solution (100×), sterile‐filtered PBS (pH 7.4), UltraPure water, TrypLE Express Enzyme (phenol red), trypan blue solution, PrestoBlue Cell Viability Reagent, DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole), Alexa Flour (AF) 568‐Phalloidin and LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit (Cat# L3224; containing calcein‐AM and ethidium homodimer‐1), were all purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (MA, US).

Cell Subculture

NIH 3T3 fibroblasts were cultured in a T75 culture flask (Greiner) supplemented with complete culture media consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and Antibiotic‐Antimycotic solution (1×) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. After cells reached 90% confluency, the media was removed entirely, and the cells were washed once with pre‐warmed PBS. After PBS, the cells were dissociated with TrypLE Express Enzyme, centrifuged (200 × g, 10 min), and then the resuspended cells were counted on a hemocytometer (Marienfeld, Germany) by trypan blue exclusion and subcultured in a new T75 culture flask.

Cell Adhesion on P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)

P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) and P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)‐coated substrates (deposition area 5 × 5 mm) were initially washed with 70% ethanol and then washed with PBS supplemented with Antibiotic‐Antimycotic (1×) three times. Finally, both sides of the substrates were sterilized by ultraviolet (UV) light in a biosafety cabinet for 30 min [109]. A substrate was placed by sterilized forceps in each well of PhenoPlate 96‐well microplates (black, optically clear bottom, tissue culture‐treated) (Revvity, Inc., NY, US), and NIH 3T3 cells were seeded on the substrate in 200 µL/well at a density of 2 × 104 cells/mL and cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. After 3 days of culture without changing media, the substrates with cells attached were washed three times with 37°C pre‐warmed PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (diluted from 16% formaldehyde in water; Electron Microscopy Sciences, PA, US) for 20 min, rinsed with 200 µL UltraPure water per substrate and dehydrated in sequential ethanol gradient (50, 75 and 100%; 30 min each) at room temperature. The morphology of NIH‐3T3 fibroblasts was analyzed by fluorescent microscopy and SEM.

The 50% THA pre‐released P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) was prepared in a three‐electrode electrochemical system by applying a constant potential of −0.8 V in PBS (2 mL) for 175 s, corresponding to charges of −0.014 C being delivered. Then the substrates were placed into fresh PBS and re‐oxidized at +1.0 V (0.0275 C). The working electrode was the P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA), the counter electrode was a platinum mesh (MW‐1033, Basi, USA), and the reference electrode was Ag/AgCl (MF‐2052, Basi, USA). The resulting substrates were washed with copious amounts of water, subsequently sterilized, and cultured with cells following the same procedure described above.

For fluorescent microscopy, each paraformaldehyde‐fixed substrate with cells was stained at room temperature with 100 µL AF568‐Phalloidin (λex/λex = 578/600 nm) for 40 min (16.5 nM in PBS containing 1% BSA to minimize nonspecific background staining) to visualize F‐actin filaments. The substrates were then washed three times with PBS and counterstained with 100 µL DAPI (λex/λex = 358/461 nm) for 10 min (1 µg/mL in PBS) for nuclei visualization. After washing twice with PBS, the substrate samples were placed onto microscope slides (76 × 26 mm, Marienfeld Superior) and covered with glass coverslips (22 × 22 mm, Citotest Scientific). NIH‐3T3 cells were then imaged under a Nikon Ti‐E inverted microscope (Tokyo, Japan), 10× magnification, with NIS‐Elements software. A minimum of two images was randomly acquired for each sample using a Nikon D610 camera (Tokyo, Japan) and processed using the ImageJ software.

Lastly, those AF568‐Phalloidin/DAPI‐stained samples were dehydrated through a series of ethanol concentrations (35%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%, 20 min each) at room temperature. After complete drying in air and gold sputtering (10 nm), the samples were visualized with SEM as described above.

THA Dose‐Response in NIH 3T3

NIH 3T3 cells were seeded at 2 × 104 cells/mL in 100 µL per well in the PhenoPlate 96‐well plate and cultured for 24 h. The supernatant was removed and treated with THA solutions prepared by 2‐fold serial dilutions (5, 2.5, 1.25, 0.625, 0.313, 0.156, 0.0781, 0.0391 mg/mL) in the complete culture medium. Two controls were included: (1) the cells with fresh complete culture medium without THA (negative control); (2) the complete culture medium alone without any cells (blank control). The cell viability in response to the drug was analyzed on Days 2, 4, and 6 (on separate plates) without changing the complete culture media, using PrestoBlue Cell Viability Reagent as indicated by the manufacturer. This reagent quantifies metabolic activity in viable cells by measuring the reduction of resazurin to resorufin, with the resulting color or fluorescence change directly correlating with the number of viable cells. After 10 min incubation, fluorescence intensity in each well was measured from the bottom using a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader at λex/λem = 560/590 nm. Mean fluorescence value of blank control wells was subtracted from that of each well in each plate as a background, and the cell viability of each test group was reported as % fluorescence relative to the negative control on Days 2, 4, and 6, respectively.

Similarly, 1 mg/mL THA or HA was incubated with NIH 3T3 cells for comparison over 1–6 days. The same two controls described above were included. Again, the mean blank control fluorescence was subtracted from each well value as background. The results were presented as background‐subtracted fluorescence intensity.

In Vitro Scratch Wound Assay

NIH 3T3 cells were seeded in 100 µL per well, at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL in PhenoPlate 96‐well plate in complete culture media and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. A wounding tool was made based on a reported design [110], which included a self‐leveling pin and slit panel. The wounding tool and the slit plate were sterilized with 70% ethanol for 5 min and dried completely in a biosafety cabinet before use. Then, a scratch wound was created using the tool in the middle of the surface of each well. Detached cells and debris were immediately washed away (2 × 100 µL PBS/well).

To test the effect of THA for wound closure, the scratched cell monolayers were incubated for 4 days with 100 µL of complete culture medium containing 1 mg/mL THA or THAR (where THAR stands for THA released from substrate), along with a negative control containing 10% PBS, no THA or THAR. Those conditioned media were prepared by first dissolving THA in PBS or releasing THAR from the substrate into 1 mL PBS, freeze‐drying, and reconstituting in complete culture medium to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL (containing 10% PBS). After 4 days of culture, the wound closure was imaged by the Operetta CLS high‐content analysis system with Harmony v5.3 software (PerkinElmer, MA, USA). The images taken under ×20 magnification over 6 adjacent positions were stitched and analyzed by ImageJ. The scratch area for all samples was consistent and amounted to 4.2 ± 1.3 mm2 in the tested area. Wound closure (%) was evaluated by measuring the cell‐free gap area between the advancing boundaries of the scratch, analyzed by ImageJ.

Electrical Stimulation of Fibroblasts

Au‐PCL, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc) and P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA) substrates, 5 × 5 mm each, were first washed with 70% ethanol three times, dried completely, and placed into each well of 24‐well plates (Corning Costar tissue culture‐treated, NY, US). A polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) chamber (outer dimensions 8 × 8 × 12 mm, with a central hole 5 × 5 × 12 mm) was directly placed over each substrate; the setup is referenced from the work reported by Tomaskovic‐Crook et al. [111]. Some modifications were made to accommodate the specific requirements of this study. Specifically, each substrate was inserted into a well of a 24‐well plate as the working electrode, held in place by a PDMS chamber rather than loaded on a glass slide, and then a plate lid was placed on top to minimize potential contamination during the experiment. Before use, the whole setup was sterilized by UV light in a biosafety cabinet for 60 min.

NIH 3T3 fibroblasts were seeded onto each substrate (n = 3 independent samples, with three repeat experiments) within the PDMS chamber at a density of 3 × 105 cells/mL in 200 µL/well. An additional 800 µL of complete culture medium was added to the well outside the PDMS chamber to prevent medium evaporation during the experiment. After culturing for 24 h, a positive ES potential of + 0.1 V [112, 113] with 1 s‐pulse potential for 5 min (during which a total charge of 3.024 × 10− 3 C was delivered, corresponding to 40.32 µA/cm2 over a projected surface area of 0.25 cm2), and −0.8 V constant potential for 20 s were applied to NIH 3T3 cells co‐cultured substrates (Au‐PCL, P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐SAc), and P(EDOT‐co‐EDOT‐g‐THA)) for the next three days, both individually and in combination. The ES was applied by a potentiostat (CHI660, Metrohm, Herisau, Switzerland) using the bespoke ES setup. The open‐circuit potential (OCP) served as a no‐ES control. After three days of ES, 100 µL of inner culture medium from the PDMS chamber of each sample well was transferred into a clean PhenoPlate 96‐well plate to assess the cells detached from the substrates (floating cells). Meanwhile, 100 µL of outer culture medium outside the PDMS chamber in the plate well, together with the cell‐cocultured substrates from the PDMS chamber, were separately transferred into another clean PhenoPlate 96‐well plate after ES (attaching cells). Two 96‐well plates were subsequently stained using the LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit (as described above), and the 96‐well plate with floating cells was evaluated on SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader at λex/λem = 494/517 nm for calcein‐AM and λex/λem = 528/617 nm for ethidium homodimer‐1. To visualize the cells, the substrates with cell attachment were carefully placed onto clean glass slides covered with glass coverslips and imaged using a Ti‐E inverted microscope at 10× magnification. A minimum of two images were randomly acquired for each sample using a Nikon D610 camera (Tokyo, Japan). ImageJ was used to quantify fibroblast viability on each substrate. The pH of the culture media after 3 days of ES was also measured by a pH meter (IC‐HI5522, Hanna Instruments, US) using a micro pH electrode (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, US).

Following the cell detachment with TrypLE Express and centrifugation (200 × g, 10 min), 1 × 105 cells of each group (untreated, THA‐treated, and THA+ES) were resuspended were resuspended in 200 µL fresh complete culture media and mixed with 1 mL of pre‐chilled fixing solution (2 parts PBS pH 7.4, 1 part 16% formaldehyde, and 1 part 75% glycerol in water) [114] and centrifuged (500 × g, 5 min). Fixed fibroblasts were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 200 µL PBS containing 16.5 nM AF568‐Phalloidin and stained in the dark for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were centrifuged (500 × g, 5 min) and resuspended in 1 mL PBS for flow cytometry analysis (5000–20000 events were analyzed). Flow cytometry data were acquired with SpectroFlo v3.3.0 software (Cytec Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo v10.10 software (BD Life Sciences).

Statistical Analysis

Details of the replication for each assay are included in the reporting of results. All analyses were repeated at least three times with independent cell passages and performed on three independent samples every time. Biological replicates were averaged over three repeated measurements prior to statistical analysis. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed by two‐tailed t‐test, one‐ or two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's Honest Significant Difference (HSD) multiple comparisons test, unless stated otherwise. GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0, GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, US) was used for both graphs and statistical analysis. Differences were considered significant if p value < 0.05: *p value < 0.05, **p value < 0.01, ***p value < 0.001, and ****p value < 0.0001.
