BOLD-based cerebrovascular reactivity is influenced by baseline cerebral blood volume and oxygen metabolism in humans
Authors: Gabriel Hoffmann, Lena Schmitzer, Jan Kufer, Matthias JP van Osch, Lena Václavů, Jan Kirschke, Christian Sorg, Stephan Kaczmarz, Christine Preibisch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) is a key indicator of vascular health. It is typically assessed using a vasoactive stimulus such as hypercapnia while recording perfusion changes using blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data or measuring cerebral blood flow (CBF) directly, for example, with arterial spin labelling (ASL). Commonly used BOLD–CVR estimates, however, may be biased by differences in (baseline) cerebral blood volume (CBV). To investigate this, we acquired data from 50 participants at 3 T, obtaining BOLD–CVR, dynamic CBF–CVR, time-encoded CBF–CVR, relative CBV (rCBV) and relative oxygen extraction fraction. Additionally, dynamic simulations of BOLD signal changes were conducted. Our results showed a pronounced contrast between grey matter and white matter in BOLD–CVR, whereas CBF–CVR was similar across tissues. Both inter-subject and intra-subject analyses indicated that this grey matter/white matter contrast in BOLD–CVR primarily reflects differences in rCBV. Other potential factors, such as variations in arterial transit time or baseline CBF, did not show significant correlations. The simulations further supported these findings and, interestingly, pointed to a slow decrease of oxygen metabolism during hypercapnia. In conclusion, BOLD–CVR strongly depends on CBV, a relationship highly relevant for studies in pathologies with altered CBV, such as cerebrovascular diseases or brain tumors.

Introduction

Cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) is considered to be an important marker of vascular health in various brain diseases, such as cerebrovascular diseases,1–5 multiple sclerosis 6 and glioma. 7 CVR generally describes the vasodilatory reserve of cerebral blood vessels and can be tested by a vasodilatory stimulus monitored by a physiology-sensitive readout, including blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD), arterial spin labelling (ASL) or phase contrast (PC) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).8,9 Hypercapnia (i.e. elevated levels of inspired CO2) or acetazolamide are frequently used to induce changes in vasodilation.10,11 BOLD–MRI and ASL provide voxel-wise values with whole-brain coverage, whereas PC–MRI is limited to measuring flow velocity in major brain feeding arteries. Due to its comparably high signal to noise ratio (SNR), high temporal resolution and decent image quality on a single subject level, BOLD–MRI-based CVR (BOLD–CVR) is generally preferred over cerebral blood flow (CBF)-based CVR (CBF–CVR) from ASL.12,13

The vasodilation-related BOLD signal change, however, does not only depend on the perfusion increase, as actually desired, but also on baseline physiology. 14 These complex dependencies have recently been investigated theoretically using a steady-state BOLD signal model. 9 Herein, the authors particularly propose that cerebral blood volume (CBV) is a major influencing factor in BOLD-based CVR measurements, a fact that has already been described in early investigations of BOLD-signal changes. 15 This may also explain study reports of higher CVR in grey matter (GM) than white matter (WM).16–18 Common BOLD-signal models also incorporate baseline oxygen extraction fraction (OEF) as a contributor to signal changes.19–21 Therefore, it appears plausible that BOLD-signal amplitudes—and consequently BOLD–CVR—may be biased by differences in baseline CBV and OEF. Lastly, it is still debated whether CO2, frequently regarded as an isometabolic stimulus, 22 especially in calibrated functional MRI (fMRI) experiments, may actually cause changes in cerebral oxygen metabolism. 14

To study these complex relations, we experimentally compared BOLD–MRI- and ASL-based CVR measurements and investigated the dependence of BOLD–CVR on baseline physiology, in particular CBV and OEF. The presented analyses were conducted across two separate datasets: the first to explore relationships between BOLD–CVR and dynamic CBF-CVR (dynCBF–CVR), as well as general signal characteristics, and the second to specifically investigate the dependence of BOLD–CVR on baseline physiology, including CBV and OEF. In addition, we performed dynamic BOLD signal simulations to investigate potential dependencies on baseline physiology and explore the dependence of temporal characteristics of hypercapnia-induced BOLD signal changes on cerebral oxygen metabolism. We hypothesized that BOLD–CVR is significantly influenced by baseline CBV as well as baseline OEF.

Material and methods

Study population

We acquired data from 50 healthy young and elderly subjects. For comparing CVR derived from BOLD–MRI (BOLD–CVR) and dynCBF–CVR, we scanned 21 subjects in dataset 1 (age = 29.1 ± 8.6 years, 10 female). Three subjects had to be excluded from the analyses due to incomplete data acquisition; three further subjects were excluded due to apparent image artefacts. To evaluate the dependency of BOLD–CVR on CBV and OEF, we further acquired data from 29 subjects in dataset 2 (age = 71.2 ± 7.5 years, 18 female). In this elderly cohort, three subjects had to be excluded due to incidental findings. Also, we had to exclude four subjects with incomplete data acquisition (due to claustrophobia during MRI or CO2 intolerance). Lastly, we excluded two subjects due to non-optimal CO2 application (i.e. no effective seal of the face mask) and strong imaging artefacts (i.e. motion). The study design was approved by the local ethics committee (TUM Ethics Committee, TUM School of Medicine and Health, approval numbers 472/16S for dataset 1 and 293/16S for dataset 2) and was in line with the Helsinki declaration. All subjects gave written consent to participate. Data processing

Processing of all data was performed with custom-built MATLAB programs (version 2024b; The Math Works, Inc., Natick, MA, USA) and Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12; Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK). Additionally, the FMRIB Software Library (FSL) was used for evaluation of teASL data. Spatial preprocessing generally involved rigid body motion correction using SPM12 and registration of the Atlas of Intrinsic Connectivity of Homotopic Areas (AICHA) 27 to the BOLD, CBF and rCBV image space. Structural data were segmented into tissue probability maps, which were used to generate brain masks and subsequently co-registered into individual image spaces. GM and WM masks were defined using a threshold of p > 0.7. Additionally, the three bottom and top slices from each image were excluded from analyses. Together with the tissue masks, this facilitated the calculation of parenchymal signal and reduced the contribution of venous signal, especially to BOLD–CVR. Statistical evaluations

Statistical evaluations were performed in MATLAB. Prior to statistical analyses, we calculated subject-wise VOI-average values for all parameters and tested for normal distribution across subjects using the Anderson–Darling Test, assuming p > 0.05 indicating a normal distribution of the respective parameter values. Statistical testing employed paired Student’s t-tests for normally distributed parameter values and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed parameters. Correlation analyses used Pearson’s correlation and Spearman’s correlation, respectively, and corresponding linear regression lines are shown in all plots. Statistical significance is assumed at p < 0.05 for all analyses. Paired scatterplots show subject-wise VOI-average values as dots, which are connected with solid lines between parameter values, and means are indicated as red dashed lines. Whisker plot boxes indicate 25th and 75th percentiles, median values are shown as horizontal lines, and outliers are visualized by crosses. Data were analyzed both on an inter-subject level using subject-wise VOI-average values (Figures 3–6) as well as on an intra-subject level by evaluating values across AICHA VOIs within individual subjects (Figure S4). For evaluating GM–WM contrast, we compared ΔCVRBOLD and ΔCVRCBF (according to equation (3)) as well as ΔCRVBOLD and ΔrCBV (equation (4)). BOLD- and dynCBF–CVR signal characteristics were assessed by means of temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR), which was calculated voxel-wisely across the first baseline periods of dynamic acquisitions as the quotient of the temporal signal mean and standard deviation. Correlation of the full time courses with etCO2 (for R and p) and CVR was calculated as described above. Correlation analyses were performed to investigate the agreement of both CVR methods (BOLD and CBF) and assess the potential impact of ΔATT and baseline CBF. Lastly, we correlated ΔSBOLD, as a proxy for BOLD–CVR, with rCBV and rOEF. Here we used ΔSBOLD to facilitate comparison with simulations. In addition to the above-described inter-subject investigations we also correlated rCBV and baseline CBF within single subjects (intra-subject analysis) and compared R and p values of those correlations.

Simulations

We simulated the dependence of the BOLD signal on hypercapnia-induced CBF and CMRO2 changes, as well as baseline CBV and OEF, using a previously published BOLD signal model 19 based on the balloon model from Buxton and co-workers.21,37 Using measured etCO2 and dynCBF time courses as driving inputs, we first varied baseline CBV and OEF to explore the dependency of ΔSBOLD on baseline physiology. In a second step, we varied the characteristic time constant (τm) and amplitude (m1) of ΔCMRO2 within physiologically plausible ranges to achieve an optimal fit of the measured ΔSBOLD. Further details and constant definitions can be found in the Supplementary Material. To facilitate comparability between simulations and experimental data in Figure 6, we normalized simulated ΔSBOLD to the mean difference in etCO2 of dataset 2 (∆etCO2 = 16 mmHg) to yield BOLD–CVR in %/mmHg.

Interestingly, the best fit of the measured ΔSBOLD (Figure 7(d)–(f), red curves) and the simulated BOLD signal (green curves) was found when a slow (τm ~15 s) decrease in CMRO2 of ~20% (m1 = 0.8) was allowed in response to 5% CO2 (Figure 7(c) and (f) and see Figure S2 for more details regarding the determination of optimal fit parameters for temporal characteristics of the CMRO2 change). Discussion

In this study, we investigated the influence of several physiological factors, including perfusion and metabolism, on BOLD-based CVR measurements. As hypothesized, both our experimental and simulation results revealed a significant dependence of BOLD–CVR on (baseline) cerebral blood volume and OEF, while no correlation was found with baseline CBF.

Regarding practical applicability, we first evaluated how BOLD- and ASL-based CVR compare in terms of data quality. As expected, BOLD–CVR yielded significantly higher tSNR and correlated better with etCO2—a fact that seems plausible with regard to previous investigations.12,38 Likewise, absolute values of quantitative BOLD–CVR in GM of 0.22% ± 0.06%/mmHg and dynCBF–CVR = 3.5% ± 1.0%/mmHg in young HC are in excellent agreement with literature (BOLD–CVR ~0.17%–0.31%/mmHg,18,39–41 CBF–CVR ~2.8%–4.5%/mmHg12,40–42).

Examining subject- and VOI-average signal time courses and parameter maps (Figure 2), a clear GM–WM contrast was observed for BOLD–CVR, which resembles contrast in rCBV parameter maps. In comparison, CBF–CVR is similar in GM and WM, both facts that we confirmed with a statistical analysis of ΔCVR and ΔrCBV (Figure 3). This finding also agrees well with previous studies, where similar CBF–CVR was found in GM and WM, both from single PLD 17 and teASL, 43 while BOLD–CVR differences between GM and WM have also been reported.16–18 However, there was a good correlation of BOLD–CVR and both dynCBF–CVR and teCBF–CVR, findings that likewise agree with previous work.17,41,44 In addition to GM–WM contrast, BOLD–CVR maps showed bright edges along the borders of the brain, which were largely excluded from analyses because they most likely arise from superficial draining veins and large vessels such as the superior sagittal sinus. This concords with venous architecture 45 and previous BOLD–CVR studies41,44 and has been described as especially prominent for GE-EPI acquisitions. 46

Regarding the dependence of BOLD–CVR on baseline physiology, we examined several factors. First, we investigated whether baseline perfusion, i.e., CBF, and perfusion delays, i.e., ATT, had an impact on BOLD–CVR. Interestingly, BOLD–CVR appears to be independent of baseline CBF, despite CBF being significantly larger in GM compared to WM, due to differences in vessel density.47–49 This finding is supported by both our inter- (Figure 5) and intrasubject analyses (Figure S4), and has implications for clinical imaging, as BOLD–CVR appears to add additional diagnostic value to a baseline CBF scan. In the context of this work, this result indicates that differences in baseline CBF do not explain the apparent GM–WM difference in BOLD–CVR. Furthermore, we did not observe any dependence on ΔATT, thus indicating stability across perfusion delays in BOLD–CVR.

Considering blood volume, we confirmed the hypothesized dependence of BOLD–CVR on baseline rCBV (Figure 6). This also very well explains the strong GM–WM contrast of BOLD–CVR and agrees with previous findings from PET 50 and MRI studies,51–53 demonstrating substantially larger CBV in GM than WM. Our findings also align with the theoretical considerations by Schulman et al. 9 who hypothesized a similar GM–WM contrast for BOLD–CVR without including experimental data. It also agrees with the analytical model of the BOLD signal by Yablonskiy and Haacke that describes the BOLD signal as dependent on (venous) CBV. 33 To some extent, it would also appear plausible that GM–WM differences of BOLD–CVR may also depend on differences of hypercapnia-induced CBV changes between GM and WM, considering that PET studies reported larger CBV reactivity in GM than WM. 54 However, we could not investigate this because we lack CBV measurements during hypercapnia.

With respect to oxygen metabolism, a weak relationship between BOLD–CVR and baseline rOEF appears plausible given theoretical considerations. 9 Here, we speculate that this relation might be stronger than presented in our data, since, compared to baseline rCBV and CBF, rOEF is particularly noisy because it is derived from a total of three independent measurements, namely T2, T2* and rCBV. 26 From those, T2* is particularly susceptible to motion and susceptibility artefacts,35,36 and, in our cohort, T2 was additionally affected by an apparent fat shift artefact.

Reassuringly, both dependencies on baseline rCBV and rOEF are supported by the dynamic BOLD signal simulations when using physiologically realistic model parameters as informed by our measurements of (baseline) physiology and employed in previous studies. 19 Our simulations predict a relationship between BOLD–CVR and both baseline CBV and OEF (see Figure 6). Interestingly, our simulations also revealed that an optimal fit of the measured and simulated BOLD data appears to be aided by allowing for a slow reduction in CMRO2 by ~20% (see Figure 7 and Figure S2). In particular, this explains a steady increase of the BOLD signal across the etCO2 plateau, that is, not found for dynamic CBF measurements. This simulation finding contradicts the frequently used isometabolic assumption in calibrated fMRI experiments.14,22,55,56 And, it at least qualitatively agrees with a number of previous studies reporting a CMRO2 decrease of ~13% using MRI by means of T2-relaxation under spin-tagging (TRUST) and PC–CBF 57 as well as TRUST and ASL–CBF, 58 but also applying near infrared spectroscopy and Doppler ultrasound. 59

Limitations

Our study is subject to a few limitations. Due to technical reasons (i.e. the use of a static protocol file for controlling the gas mixer), we used a fixed scan order, while randomizing scans would have been beneficial to balance potential saturation effects of the BOLD signal and CBF responses to CO2. However, we did not find any significant differences in etCO2 values between scans of dataset 1 (Table S2). In addition, BOLD signal as well as CBF changes were comparable in the first and second CO2 blocks for both BOLD–CVR and dynCBF–CVR time courses (see Figure 2(a) and (c)). Thus, we are confident that a slightly extended period of 5 min CO2, as used for teCBF–CVR to accommodate the time-encoded acquisition, does not compromise teCBF–CVR results. With regard to OEF, the applied mqBOLD method is restricted to cortical GM, since T2, T2* and DSC–MRI-based CBV measurements exhibit directional dependencies in WM, 60 and iron deposition in deep GM also compromises R2′ estimates.26,61 Regarding our two different data sets, the subject groups exhibit a considerable age difference. Nevertheless, despite the fact that CBF 62 and CVR decrease with age,40,63 we assume that the underlying relationships are generally valid. Notably, since all conducted analyses yielded similar results in both datasets (i.e. Figures 2(a) and (b), 3(a) and (b) and 5(a) and (c)), we assume that our assumption is reasonably valid.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results indicate that BOLD–CVR is strongly dependent on baseline CBV and may also be influenced by baseline OEF in pathologies that considerably affect brain oxygen metabolism. In healthy subjects, the dependence on baseline CBV mainly manifests as a clear GM–WM contrast, which is not present in CBF–CVR. These findings also agree with our dynamic BOLD-signal simulations in response to a hypercapnic challenge. Interestingly, our simulation results are consistent with a clear decrease of CMRO2 under hypercapnia. Thus, our results do not just provide a deeper understanding of BOLD-based CVR and calibrated fMRI experiments, but may also have clinical implications, especially in patients with altered CBV and OEF, such as in cerebrovascular diseases or brain tumors.64–68
