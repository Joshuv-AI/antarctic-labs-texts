Factors associated with healthcare seeking behavior from private clinics among patients in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Roa Altaweli, Mamdouh M Shubair, Rawan Ahmed Alshagha, Badr F Al-Khateeb, Bandar Aleissa, Khalid Ahmari, Mohamed Faisal Alanazi, Khadijah Angawi, Samah Saad Salem, Noof Alwatban, Paivi Toivola, Ashraf El-Metwally
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare-seeking behavior is a key factor in how well a health system performs and how fair it is. In Saudi Arabia, public healthcare services are free, yet many people still choose private healthcare, especially in cities like Riyadh. It is important to understand why people seek care from private clinics to help shape health policies, distribute resources better, and improve services across the healthcare system.ObjectivesThis study aimed to examine the frequency of private healthcare use, defined as the reported usual or concurrent use of private healthcare services, and to identify sociodemographic, behavioral, and health-related factors associated with this choice among adults in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was carried out in Riyadh from March to July 2023 using a multistage cluster sampling method. We randomly selected 48 government primary healthcare centers and invited adults aged 18 and older who visited these centers to participate. We collected data electronically with a validated questionnaire that covered sociodemographic details, patterns of healthcare use, reasons for choosing private healthcare, behavioral risk factors, and existing health conditions. We used multivariate logistic regression analysis to find independent predictors of private healthcare use, reporting adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsOf 14,239 participants, 72.4% reported using private healthcare services either as a usual source of care or alongside public services. The multivariable analysis revealed several factors to be positively related to private healthcare utilization. Those who were married were more likely to use private healthcare services (AOR 1.23, 95% CI 1.11–1.36). Those with insurance coverage were threefold higher odds of private healthcare use (AOR 3.51, 95% CI 3.13–3.94). Smokers were more likely to seek private healthcare (AOR 1.60, 95% CI 1.45–1.77) than non-smokers, and those who exercised reported increased utilization (AOR 1.83, 95% CI 1.67–2.00). Obesity was also positively related to private healthcare utilization (AOR 1.38, 95% CI 1.12–1.71), and those with heart disease had substantially higher odds of using private healthcare services (AOR 2.09, 95% CI 1.59–2.76).ConclusionPrivate healthcare use in Riyadh is common and associated with insurance coverage, marital status, behavioral factors, and certain chronic conditions. These findings provide descriptive insights into factors related to private healthcare utilization among public PHC attendees in Riyadh, without implying causal effects or policy recommendations beyond the scope of the data.Keywords: Healthcare-seeking behavior, Private healthcare utilization, Health insurance, Saudi Arabia, Primary healthcare

Introduction

Healthcare-seeking behavior is a key factor in how health systems operate and how well people can access and use available services [1]. It reflects individual choices about when, where, and from whom to get medical care. These choices are influenced by a mix of personal, socioeconomic, cultural, and system-level factors [2, 3]. In Saudi Arabia, healthcare offers a unique dual-system environment: public healthcare services are offered free to citizens via governmental hospitals and primary healthcare centers, whereas private healthcare services function on a fee-for-service or insurance-based model [4]. Even with many free public services available, a significant number of patients still choose to go to private clinics, even in cities like Riyadh where public facilities are easy to reach [5, 6].

The Saudi healthcare system has undergone recent transformation reforms aimed at improving quality, safety, and patient-centeredness. These reforms emphasize enhancing organizational climate, service efficiency, and accountability in both public and private sectors, which may shape patients’ preferences for care [7–9]. Understanding what drives this choice is important for shaping policy changes, improving resource use, and ensuring fairness and efficiency in the healthcare system.

Evidence from various studies shows that healthcare-seeking behavior is affected by individual demographic and socioeconomic traits [2, 3, 10]. Factors like age, gender, education level, job status, and income greatly influence patients’ healthcare decisions [2, 3, 10, 11]. People with higher education and better financial resources are more likely to choose private healthcare services. They often believe these services provide better quality care, shorter wait times, and a more comfortable environment [12–14].

In addition to demographic and economic determinants, patients’ perceptions of quality, safety, organizational climate, and provider engagement play a major role in private healthcare utilization. Research has shown that safety training, workplace environment, and management commitment indirectly influence care quality and patient trust, which may affect decisions to seek care in private clinics [7–9]. The perceived quality of care is one of the main reasons patients seek private healthcare. Several studies show that patients link private clinics with better medical equipment, improved facilities, greater privacy, trust in healthcare providers, patient satisfaction, effective communication with doctors, and more attentive staff [15–19]. Additionally, private clinics frequently offer flexible appointment times, shorter waits, and more convenient locations, which many patients feel justifies the extra cost or use of insurance [15–20].

Similar to many healthcare systems, seeking care in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, is affected by several factors. At the patient level, these factors include education, income, insurance status, and gender. At the provider level, they involve perceived quality, convenience, access to specialists, privacy, and communication. However, despite the relevance of these factors, evidence from Saudi Arabia remains limited, and the extent to which they shape patients’ preference for private clinics over government facilities in the local context is still not well understood. In particular, few studies have examined healthcare-seeking behavior in Riyadh by focusing specifically on the factors associated with choosing private clinics within a health system where public services are widely available at no direct cost to citizens. Moreover, much of the existing literature from Saudi Arabia has emphasized healthcare utilization more broadly [5, 6], without clearly distinguishing the determinants of private clinic use from those of public-sector care. Despite its importance, there has been little research on what distinguishes users of private clinics from those who go to government hospitals in Saudi Arabia. This represents an important knowledge gap in the Saudi context, where understanding why patients bypass or supplement public services with private care may help inform policies aimed at improving service responsiveness, patient satisfaction, and the efficient distribution of healthcare resources. Addressing this gap is essential for improving the health system, increasing the use of public healthcare, and developing strategies that focus on perceptions of care quality and service delivery in government health facilities.

Materials & methods

Study design and setting

We undertook a cross-sectional study in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, from March to July 2023. It aimed to examine factors related to healthcare-seeking behavior in the private sector and understand why patients prefer private clinics. Riyadh, the capital and largest city of Saudi Arabia, has a wide network of government primary healthcare centers and a rapidly growing private healthcare sector. This makes it a good location for studying healthcare usage patterns. However, the sampling frame was limited to adults attending selected government primary healthcare centers; therefore, the study population represents public primary healthcare attendees in Riyadh rather than all residents of the city. As a result, individuals who rely exclusively on private healthcare services and do not attend government primary healthcare centers may not have been captured.

Sampling strategy

We used a multistage cluster sampling technique to gather a representative sample of healthcare users. In the first stage, we randomly selected 48 primary healthcare centers from a total of 103 government facilities in Riyadh. These centers acted as clusters. In the second stage, we invited a random sample of patients visiting the selected centers during the study to participate. We collected samples throughout the day to minimize selection bias due to different attendance times. At each selected center, participant recruitment was performed using a systematic selection approach. Based on patient flow at the facility, a sampling interval was determined, and every kth eligible attendee was invited to participate after a random starting point was selected. This procedure was applied across different clinic hours to improve representativeness of attendees present at varying times of the day.

Eligibility criteria and study population

The study participants eligible for consideration were those adults of age 18 years or above who had presented themselves at the selected primary healthcare service points for their health service needs during the data collection period. Only individuals who were themselves seeking or receiving healthcare services at the selected centers were included in the study. Accompanying family members or other visitors who were present only in a supportive role and were not receiving care were not included in the final sample. On the other hand, healthcare professionals, administrative staff, and employees working at the selected centers were excluded so as not to have the results biased by the professionals’ perspective. In total, 14,239 participants who met the eligibility criteria were included in the final analysis, which gave adequate statistical power to detect associations between the predictor variables and private healthcare utilization.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated to ensure adequate precision for estimating the prevalence of private healthcare use, the primary outcome of this study. Assuming an expected prevalence of private healthcare use of approximately 30–35% based on prior regional data, a 95% confidence level, and a margin of error of 1%, the minimum required sample size was approximately 8,100 participants. To account for potential non-response and enable subgroup analyses, a larger sample was targeted. The final sample of 14,239 participants exceeded the minimum requirement and provided sufficient statistical power for both prevalence estimation and analyses of factors associated with private healthcare utilization.

Questionnaire development and validation

The data collection instrument was a structured, standardized questionnaire which was prepared to measure the frequency of private healthcare usage and the reasons that lead to such behavior. The data collection instrument was a structured, standardized questionnaire developed based on an extensive literature review and previously validated instruments assessing healthcare utilization and related behaviors. Items related to healthcare utilization, including private healthcare use, were adapted from previously published surveys and contextualized to the Saudi healthcare system. The questionnaire used in this study is previously published [21]. Initially, the questionnaire was drafted in English based on an extensive literature review and previously validated instruments. After that, it was translated into Arabic, and an English back translation was performed to verify linguistic accuracy and conceptual equivalence.

To establish content and face validity, a focus group meeting was convened with 20 key informants, including clinical experts and practitioners. These experts evaluated the questionnaire for clarity, relevance, and representativeness across key domains, including sociodemographic characteristics, healthcare utilization patterns, behavioral risk factors, and comorbid conditions. The Content Validity Index (CVI) for individual items ranged from 0.87 to 1.00, and the overall scale-level CVI (S-CVI) was 0.94, indicating excellent content validity.

The questionnaire was piloted in Hail, Saudi Arabia, with 100 participants to check the clarity, understanding, and appropriateness of the questions. The comments and suggestions from the pilot study were utilized to improve the wording of the questions and the answer choices. The updated questionnaire was then examined by a panel of 20 experts from public health and healthcare delivery sectors to confirm the content validity.

Construct validity was supported through expert evaluation and consistency with established theoretical domains; however, formal factor analysis was not performed, as the questionnaire was not intended to measure latent constructs but rather to capture observable behaviors and characteristics.

The reliability of the instrument was further confirmed through a test–retest procedure with 100 participants, in which the questionnaire was administered twice with a two-week interval. Reliability assessment covered key domains including healthcare utilization (private healthcare use), behavioral factors (smoking, physical activity), and self-reported comorbidities. The resulting Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.83, indicating high temporal stability.

The final questionnaire absorbed information on sociodemographic variables (age, gender, marital status, education level, employment status, and health insurance coverage), healthcare utilization patterns, motives for selecting private healthcare services, behavioral risk factors (smoking status, fast food consumption, physical activity, obesity), and self-reported comorbid conditions (diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease). All behavioral variables were self-reported. Physical activity was assessed as engagement in any regular exercise or physical activity and was recorded as a dichotomous yes/no variable. Smoking status was categorized based on self-report and classified as current smoker versus non-current smoker. Fast food consumption was assessed by asking participants whether they consumed fast food and was recorded as a yes/no variable. These measures were included as simple behavioral indicators within the survey instrument rather than as detailed frequency-based or scale-derived measures. Obesity was based on self-report rather than direct anthropometric measurement. Participants were classified as obese if they self-reported obesity (Yes/No). The primary outcome, private healthcare use, was defined based on participants’ self-report of whether they usually sought healthcare from private clinics as a source of care. This measure was intended to reflect usual healthcare-seeking behavior at the time of the survey rather than lifetime ever-use or only the most recent visit. Participants could therefore report private clinic use even if they also attended public primary healthcare centers, reflecting possible dual use of services.

Data collection procedures

We collected data using electronic devices such as tablets or mobile phones and data collection was facilitated by trained data collectors. We approached study participants in waiting areas where they were informed about the study objectives, procedures, and voluntary nature of participation. The data collectors also obtained a written informed consent from all study participants before collecting the data. Confidentiality of study participants was maintained by anonymizing all responses and limiting access to the dataset exclusively to the research team.

The questionnaire consisted of sociodemographic variables such as age, gender, marital status, education level, employment status, and insurance coverage. We also collected data on healthcare utilization patterns such as frequency of seeking care from private healthcare facilities. In addition, the data was collected on behavioral and health-related factors such as smoking status, physical activity, obesity, and comorbidities including cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and diabetes.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized with frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SD). First, univariate logistic regression was conducted to assess the unadjusted association between each independent variable and private healthcare use. Variables demonstrating a p-value < 0.20 in the univariate analysis, or those identified as clinically relevant based on prior literature, were entered into the multivariable logistic regression models. We reported adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Model 1 was specified to examine sociodemographic determinants and included age, gender, education, marital status, employment status, and insurance coverage. All variables in this model were entered simultaneously and mutually adjusted to estimate their independent associations with private healthcare use. Model 2 was specified to evaluate behavioral risk factors and clinical comorbidities and included age, gender, obesity, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, smoking status, physical activity, and fast-food consumption. The final regression models helped identify the most important factors associated with private healthcare utilization among residents of Riyadh. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and pseudo-R² statistics (Cox & Snell R² = 0.001; Nagelkerke R² = 0.002), indicating acceptable model calibration. We analyzed the data using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Study results

Socio-demographic and health-related profile of the study participants

Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic and health-related characteristics of the 14,239 study participants. The sample included adults across a wide age range, with nearly half aged 50–75 years, 34% under 50 years, and 17% aged 75 or older. Most participants were married (65.3%), and there was an almost equal split between employed (51.4%) and unemployed (48.6%) individuals. A majority did not have health insurance coverage (75.7%). Educational attainment varied, with 4% having primary education, 27.6% up to high school, 51.5% college/university, and 16.8% other forms of education. Regarding health-related characteristics, 27.7% were current or former smokers, 60.7% engaged in some physical activity, and 5.2% were classified as obese based on self-reported height and weight. The relatively low obesity prevalence (5.2%) reflects self-reported height and weight data rather than measured values. This likely underestimates the true prevalence, consistent with known limitations of self-reported anthropometric data. Diabetes mellitus and hypertension were present in 12.4% and 11.1% of participants, respectively. Overall, 72.4% reported using private healthcare services, either as a usual source of care or in addition to public services.

Findings of multivariable analysis

Table 3 presents the adjusted associations from the multivariable logistic regression. After adjustment, age was no longer a significant predictor of private healthcare use. Male sex remained associated with lower odds (AOR 0.71, 95% CI 0.65–0.77). Education up to high school or college/university was associated with higher odds, though not statistically significant, while the “other” education category had lower odds (AOR 0.50, 95% CI 0.41–0.61, p < 0.001). Married participants (AOR 1.23, 95% CI 1.11–1.36) and those with insurance coverage (AOR 3.51, 95% CI 3.13–3.94) had higher odds of using private healthcare. Unemployed individuals were less likely to use private care compared to employed participants (AOR 0.66, 95% CI 0.60–0.72).

Findings of univariate analysis

As shown in Table 3, several behavioural risk factors and co-morbidities showed a statistically significant association with PHC use in a univariate Analysis. Smokers had a significantly higher odds of utilising PHC than non-smokers (OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.80–2.16, p < 0.001). Those who reported exercising had a higher odds of PHC use than those who did not (OR 1.88, 95% CI 1.74–2.02, p < 0.001). “Fast food” consumption was positively associated with PHC use (OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.13–1.33, p < 0.001). For patients with diabetes, however, they had a lower odd of PHC use (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.76–0.95, p = 0.004) compared to patients without diabetes. Patients with hypertension showed a slightly lower odds of utilizing PHC than patients without hypertension (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.86–1.09), though this was not statistically significant (p = 0.60). Obesity was significantly, positively associated with PHC use (OR 1.88, 95% CI 1.55–2.28, p = 0.001), as was hypercholesterolaemia (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.51, p < 0.001). Lastly, patients with heart disease had a higher odd of using PHC than patients without heart disease (OR 2.89, 95% CI 2.25–3.72, p < 0.001).

Findings of multivariable analysis

In the multivariable analysis, smoking remained an important predictor of private health care (AOR 1.60, 95% CI 1.45–1.77, p < 0.001). Exercise was also significant (AOR 1.83, 95% CI 1.67-2.00, p < 0.001) predictor of private health usage. Fast food intake was found to have inverse association with private health care usage in the multivariable model (AOR 0.77, 95% CI 0.70–0.85, p < 0.001). Having diabetes continued to be a significant predictor for lower likelihood of using private health care than those without diabetes (AOR 0.75, 95% CI 0.66–0.85, p < 0.001). Obesity continued to be positively associated with private health care usage (AOR 1.38, 95% CI 1.12–1.71, p = 0.001). The multivariable analysis found hypercholesterolemia was not a significant predictor of private health care usage as it had been in the univariate analysis (AOR 1.15, 95% CI 0.98–1.34, p = 0.08). Having heart disease remained a strong predictor of private health care usage (AOR 2.09, 95% CI 1.59–2.76, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This paper presents evidence of the multiple factors influencing patients’ use of private clinics in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Nearly 75% of respondents reported using private services either as a usual source of care or alongside public services, highlighting the widespread reliance on the private sector even within a system offering free public healthcare. The findings underscore that healthcare-seeking behavior is complex, shaped by sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral risk factors, comorbidities, and enabling resources such as health insurance. Socio-demographic factors strongly influenced private healthcare use. Although age was significant in univariate analyses, it was no longer an independent predictor after adjustment, suggesting that associations with age may be mediated by insurance coverage, employment status, or health conditions.

Gender differences were notable, with women more likely than men to use private healthcare services. This aligns with previous research showing women’s higher utilization for preventive and family care, and greater emphasis on features such as continuity, privacy, and provider choice [22–25]. Additionally, workplace conditions and provider burnout in public facilities may influence perceived quality, indirectly shaping patient preferences for private settings [26]. These findings highlight the importance of adopting gender-oriented approaches and improving provider working conditions to enhance service responsiveness in public facilities.

Education was positively associated with private healthcare use in univariate analysis; however, this association was attenuated and did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for other factors. Therefore, education does not appear to be an independent predictor of private healthcare utilization in this study. Unemployed people utilized private health services less often than employed people; employment was found to be the most predictive factor of health service use. This supports earlier findings showing that unemployed individuals tend to seek care from the public health sector [27–29]. Providing an income and employer-supplied health coverage, employment means less cost associated with seeking private health care. Additionally, people who are employed typically have a higher level of health care priorities to minimize their time spent seeking care by taking advantage of the shorter waiting times and ease of making appointments offered by many private clinics.

Having health insurance was found to be another key factor in the level of private service use, a finding consistent with prior studies as well [27, 30, 31]. Those with health insurance were more than three times more likely to seek private healthcare services compared to those without insurance, highlighting the central role of financial access in shaping healthcare-seeking behavior. Other studies also indicate that people with health insurance are significantly more likely to seek private health services than those without insurance [27, 30–32]. Therefore, in summary, insurance is the most significant factor in reducing the out-of-pocket expense associated with obtaining private health services and reducing financial barriers to care, increasing patients’ access to private providers [33]. From a policy perspective, this finding is particularly relevant in the context of ongoing healthcare reforms in Saudi Arabia, including initiatives under Vision 2030 that aim to expand private sector participation and insurance coverage. Increased access to private insurance may further shift utilization toward private providers, potentially reducing pressure on public healthcare facilities. However, this trend also raises important considerations for health system equity, as individuals without insurance may continue to rely disproportionately on public services. Balancing the expansion of private insurance with the strengthening of public healthcare services will be critical to ensure equitable access to care. Policymakers should consider strategies that maintain high-quality public services while regulating private sector growth to avoid widening disparities in access and utilization. Furthermore, understanding how insurance coverage influences patient choice can inform policies aimed at optimizing resource allocation across both sectors.

The use of private healthcare services was found to be strongly correlated with behavioral risk factors. Smoking still persisted as a major predictor in the multivariate analysis, which was consistent with the literature [34]. This finding may indicate that individuals with higher perceived health risk or greater healthcare needs are more likely to seek care in the private sector. However, the reasons underlying this association cannot be determined from the present cross-sectional data, and interpretations related to stigma, consultation style, or patient preference should therefore be made cautiously. Similarly, the positive association between physical activity and private healthcare utilization may reflect greater health awareness and preventive care-seeking, but it may also be influenced by unmeasured socioeconomic differences or health-related confounders, since individuals with greater resources may be both more likely to engage in exercise and more able to access private healthcare services. This finding implies that illness may not solely drive private healthcare utilization but it may also reflect proactive health-seeking behaviors among individuals who prioritize preventive services and wellbeing.

The association between dietary behavior and private healthcare usage was rather intricate. On one hand, the consumption of fast food was shown to have a positive association at the univariate level, while on the other hand, it became negatively correlated after the multivariable adjustment. This change in direction should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect confounding by other sociodemographic or behavioral factors included in the adjusted model. It is also possible that the observed reversal is influenced by residual confounding, measurement limitations in self-reported dietary behavior, or correlations with other lifestyle characteristics not fully captured in the analysis. Therefore, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct relationship between fast food consumption and private healthcare use.

Differences in patterns of care use were observed for different chronic conditions. Obesity and heart disease were both associated with an increased likelihood of utilizing the private sector, suggesting that people with complex or severe health issues may choose to access private clinics for specialized services, quicker patient flow and a better overall experience compared to the public sector. Heart disease has the highest positive association, which indicates that this chronic condition may benefit from prompt and specialized care. The results of our study are consistent with what has been previously reported in the literature, where individuals with previous chronic conditions have been shown to have an increased likelihood of using private health care [27, 30, 35, 36].

Conversely, diabetes was inversely associated with private healthcare use, consistent with prior studies [27]. This may reflect the structured public chronic disease programs in Saudi Arabia, which provide regular follow-up, laboratory monitoring, medication provision, and continuity of care. Patients with diabetes may therefore rely more on public services for ongoing management. Integrating evidence on self-management care and shared decision-making in type 2 diabetes supports this explanation [37]. Hypercholesterolemia was not independently associated with private healthcare use, suggesting that other coexisting conditions or lifestyle factors may drive patient preferences rather than this condition alone.

From a policy perspective, the findings highlight the need for ongoing healthcare reforms under Saudi Vision 2030. Efforts to improve public PHC quality, patient-centered care, provider working conditions, and preventive services may help balance healthcare utilization between public and private sectors. Addressing workforce burnout and enhancing the organizational climate in public facilities may improve perceived quality and patient trust, reducing unnecessary reliance on private clinics [26, 37].

Strengthening chronic disease programs, particularly for heart disease and obesity, may ensure continuity of care in public settings and improve health equity. Gender-specific strategies, financial protection mechanisms, and patient-centered services can further promote equitable access across the Saudi healthcare system.

Strengths and limitations

One considerable strength of this research lies in its large sample of public primary healthcare attendees, which improves statistical power and supports the robustness of the analyses within the study population in Riyadh. In addition, multistage cluster sampling and a carefully validated questionnaire were employed, which is a further rationale for the dependability of the results. The diversity of sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical variables studied is another plus as these different types of factors that may explain healthcare seeking behavior have been looked at thoroughly.

Nevertheless, the study also has some limitations. First, because of its cross-sectional design, temporal relationships between exposure variables and private healthcare use cannot be established, and therefore causal inferences should not be drawn from the observed associations. Second, the sample was drawn from adults attending selected government primary healthcare centers rather than from the general population of Riyadh; therefore, the study should not be considered population-based. This sampling approach may have introduced selection bias, as individuals who exclusively rely on private healthcare services and do not attend public primary healthcare centers were less likely to be captured, and the findings may therefore be more reflective of healthcare-seeking patterns among public PHC attendees than the broader Riyadh population. Behavioral factors and obesity were assessed using self-reported questionnaire data rather than objective measurements or detailed validated scales; therefore, some degree of misclassification or recall or social desirability bias may be possible. Since the study was carried out in Riyadh, the results may not be entirely extrapolated to the countryside or other cities with a different healthcare setup. Additionally, because this study was based on secondary data analysis, the selection of variables was limited to those available in the dataset, including sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral factors, and comorbidities. Although these domains are broadly consistent with factors commonly considered in healthcare-seeking behavior research, the dataset did not include all constructs needed to fully operationalize a formal theoretical model such as Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Services Use. Therefore, the analysis was structured according to the variables available in the dataset rather than an explicitly predefined conceptual framework. This should be considered when interpreting the findings, and future studies may benefit from prospectively collecting data guided by established theoretical models. Also, since the participants were recruited from government primary healthcare centers, the findings may be more reflective of healthcare-seeking patterns among public PHC attendees than the general population of Riyadh. In addition, individuals who exclusively rely on private healthcare services may have been underrepresented. In addition, some potentially important determinants of healthcare-seeking behavior, such as income and other detailed measures of socioeconomic position, were not available in the dataset; therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Lastly, the research did not evaluate the qualitative aspects of patient experiences that could have further shed light on the inclination towards private healthcare.

Conclusion

This research reveals that the factors determining the healthcare, seeking behavior of people in Riyadh involve a complicated interaction of sociodemographic characteristics, different types of risky behavior, the extent of chronic diseases, and the availability of resources like insurance coverage. The association between having insurance and utilizing private healthcare services highlights the key role of financial accessibility in determining the healthcare decisions of individuals who participated in the survey. To address these factors, it is necessary to implement targeted policy measures, strengthen the public healthcare system, and increase insurance coverage, which will pave the way for a more fair, efficient, and patient, focused healthcare system in Saudi Arabia.

Implications of findings

The results point to the necessity of targeted endeavors in the public healthcare sector and a more equitable distribution of healthcare services. The results imply that healthcare policy and system planning in Saudi Arabia will have to take healthcare into account. Upgrading the quality of public healthcare, decreasing the time spent waiting, and giving the possibility of having more specialists are some of the ways that public healthcare can be changed so that it can compete with private healthcare. The insurance coverage can be expanded and at the same time patient, centered care models can be implemented which in turn will ensure that everyone will have equal access to healthcare. Healthcare policymakers should be guided by these revelations when implementing healthcare reforms that are in harmony with the expectations of the patients and the needs of the population.

The public’s preference for using the private sector for healthcare is largely due to the influence of insurance coverage, employment, the speed of access and chronic conditions the patient has. Hence, the demand side plays an important part in how people utilize health services. To assist with some of the strain placed on the private sector and to create a more equal playing field, the provision of services in the public sector must provide issues surrounding waiting times, appointment availability for doctors, continuity of care, and communication between patients and providers. Increasing the number of people with health insurance, especially for those who are unemployed and low-income earners will help alleviate the barriers to obtaining private health services for these groups. Patient-centred care should be the primary focus for both these sectors. By having longer consultation times with patients, healthcare providers improving their communication skills and utilizing patient feedback to assist in designing their services, trust in public healthcare facilities can be restored and overuse of the private sector reduced.
