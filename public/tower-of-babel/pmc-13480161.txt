Exacerbations and Healthcare Burden of COPD with Type 2 Inflammation: US Claims Analysis
Authors: Dharani K Narendra, Rosirene Paczkowski, Jihye Park, George Mu, Stefanie Kolterer, Emmeline Burrows, Stephen G Noorduyn, Shibing Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUp to 40% of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) have type 2 inflammation, characterized by an elevated blood eosinophil count (BEC), with limited real-world evidence of disease burden.MethodsRetrospective analysis using Optum’s Clinformatics® Data Mart Database, covering over 28 million lives of de-identified medical and pharmacy claims data in the US (commercial insurance and Medicare Advantage). Eligible patients required ≥2 COPD diagnosis claims (≥30 days apart) from January 2021 to December 2023, ≥40 years old at index date (last COPD diagnosis claim), continuous insurance coverage for ≥12 months both before (baseline) and after (follow-up) index date, triple therapy for ≥1 day and eligible BEC (≥14 days since last systemic corticosteroid use, if applicable) at baseline. Patients were categorized into three groups: with ≥2 moderate or ≥1 severe exacerbations (EXAC) and BEC ≥150 or ≥300 cells/µL at baseline (“EXAC-EP-150” and “EXAC-EP-300”, respectively), and a reference group (BEC ≥150 cells/µL but not EXAC). Outcomes included COPD-related exacerbations, healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) and medical costs.ResultsThere were 13,840 eligible patients (6,389 EXAC-EP-150; 3,100 EXAC-EP-300; 7,451 reference). During 12-month follow-up, the mean (95% confidence interval) rates of any COPD-related exacerbations were similar in the EXAC-EP-150 and EXAC-EP-300 groups, both higher versus the reference group (1.58 [1.53, 1.63] and 1.56 [1.50, 1.63] vs 0.52 [0.50, 0.54]). Similarly, COPD-related HCRU and medical costs were higher in the EXAC-EP-150/300 groups than the reference group.ConclusionPatients with COPD and type 2 inflammation experiencing exacerbations despite triple therapy have high exacerbation rates, HCRU, and medical costs, highlighting the need for improved management of this patient population.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous and progressive disease, that causes airway inflammation leading to persistent airflow obstruction.1,2 Many patients with COPD still experience exacerbations despite receiving maximum inhaled triple therapy (inhaled corticosteroid [ICS]/long-acting β2-agonist [LABA]/long-acting muscarinic antagonist [LAMA]). These events are associated with an increased risk of further future exacerbations, disease progression and hospitalization, with long-lasting negative effects on clinical outcomes, health-related quality of life and prognosis, thus increasing healthcare resource utilization (HCRU), morbidity (including cardiovascular risks) and mortality.2–8 Up to 40% of patients with COPD have type 2 inflammation, characterized by an eosinophilic phenotype.9–13 While treatment guidelines refer to raised blood eosinophil count to identify patients at higher risk of exacerbations and more likely to benefit from ICS therapy,14 evidence has shown an increasing risk of frequent COPD exacerbations and higher HCRU and healthcare costs starting from blood eosinophil count ≥150 cells/µL.15–18 A threshold of blood eosinophil count ≥150 cells/µL is also recommended to identify patients with underlying type 2 inflammation in other diseases, such as asthma,19 granted different characteristics and background inflammatory mechanisms may be at play in COPD.

Currently, there is a lack of real-world data assessing exacerbations and HCRU among patients with uncontrolled COPD with type 2 inflammation in the United States (US). This US claims database analysis aimed to describe differences in exacerbation rates, HCRU and healthcare costs among patients with uncontrolled COPD (indicated by a history of moderate/severe exacerbations) with type 2 inflammation (characterized by either blood eosinophil count ≥150 or ≥300 cells/µL), compared with those with a blood eosinophil count ≥150 cells/µL but without a history of moderate/severe exacerbations.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a real-world, retrospective cohort study using data from Optum’s de-identified Clinformatics® Data Mart Database (Optum CDM) (GSK ID: 218961). The Optum CDM database is a large de-identified administrative claims database containing healthcare claims covering over 28 million lives of medical and pharmacy claims data in the US.20 The database comprises both commercial insurance and Medicare Advantage (Part D). The study period ran from 1 January 2020 to 24 December 2024, with an index period of 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2023. The index date was that of the last recorded diagnosis of COPD (defined as medical claims with a COPD diagnosis code in any position) within the index period. The baseline period was defined as 12 months pre-index (including the index date) and the follow-up period was defined as 12 months post‑index (excluding the index date).

Study Participants

Eligible patients had to have at least two COPD diagnoses ≥30 days apart during the index period, must have been ≥40 years of age at index date, receiving triple therapy (ICS/LABA/LAMA) for at least 1 day during baseline, and had continuous medical and pharmacy insurance coverage ≥12 months pre-index (baseline) and ≥12 months post-index (follow-up). Furthermore, patients must have had eligible blood eosinophil count at baseline (measures within 14 days of systemic corticosteroid use, if applicable, were considered ineligible). Patients with a diagnosis of other selected respiratory diseases were excluded, including interstitial lung disease, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, lung cancer, cystic fibrosis or active respiratory tuberculosis.

This study did not mandate any direct contact or primary collection of individual human patient data. Personal identifiers and personal identifiable information were removed by the database provider. Study results are in tabular form and aggregate analyses that omit subject identification; therefore, informed consent, ethics committee, or institutional review board approval were not required.

Methods and Analysis

Patients were stratified into three groups: 1) Patients with ≥2 moderate or ≥1 severe exacerbations in the 12 months pre-index (EXAC), with an eosinophilic phenotype (defined as blood eosinophil count ≥150 cells/µL; “EXAC-EP-150”); 2) A subset of “EXAC-EP-150”, which included patients with ≥2 moderate or ≥1 severe exacerbations in the 12 months pre-index (EXAC), with an eosinophilic phenotype (defined as blood eosinophil count ≥300 cells/µL; “EXAC-EP-300”); 3) A “reference group” of patients with a blood eosinophil count ≥150 cells/µL but not meeting the exacerbation history (EXAC) criteria.

Demographic and clinical characteristics were gathered during the baseline period. Outcomes measured included COPD-related exacerbations and COPD-related HCRU and cost during the 12-month follow-up. COPD exacerbations included moderate and severe COPD exacerbations, defined as recommended by the Mapel et al validated HCRU-based algorithm.21 Moderate exacerbations were defined as an outpatient (OP), emergency department (ED) or <2-day inpatient (IP) visit for COPD with dispensing of systemic corticosteroid and/or guideline-recommended antibiotics ≤14 days after the healthcare visit. Severe exacerbations were defined as ≥2-day IP hospitalization for COPD. COPD-related ED and OP visits were defined as encounters with a recorded diagnosis of COPD. COPD-related hospitalizations (“IP visits”) were defined as admissions in which COPD was identified as the primary diagnosis. The total costs incurred during a COPD-related healthcare encounter were categorized as COPD-related costs. All findings were analyzed and are reported descriptively. For categorical variables, the number (n) and proportion (%) of patients are reported. Summary statistics (mean, standard deviation [SD], geometric mean, logSD) are reported for continuous variables, as appropriate. No inferential analyses were conducted.

Exacerbations

During the 12 months post‑index, the mean (95% CI) number of any COPD-related exacerbations was similar in the EXAC-EP-150 and EXAC-EP-300 groups, and both were higher than in the reference group (1.58 [1.53, 1.63] and 1.56 [1.50, 1.63] vs 0.52 [0.50, 0.54], respectively). Moderate exacerbations were the most frequent type of exacerbations, with similar trends for the EXAC-EP-150 and EXAC-EP-300 groups versus the reference group (1.40 [1.36, 1.44] and 1.39 [1.33, 1.46] vs 0.45 [0.43, 0.47], respectively). Severe exacerbations trended similarly, although with lower frequency (0.18 [0.17, 0.19] and 0.17 [0.15, 0.19] vs 0.06 [0.06, 0.07], respectively) (Figure 1). Healthcare Costs

During the 12 months post‑index, the mean (95% CI) total COPD-related medical costs were higher for the EXAC-EP-150 and EXAC-EP-300 groups versus the reference group ($12,796 [11,999, 13,593] and $12,287 [11,136, 13,439] vs $5,800 [5,341, 6,260]). Costs for specific visit types trended similarly, with the highest costs resulting from IP visits ($6,285 [5,640, 6,929] and $6,064 [5,099, 7,029] vs $2,536 [2,164, 2,909], respectively), followed by OP visits ($5,097 [4,824, 5,370] and $4,812 [4,456, 5,169] vs $2,777 [2,582, 2,973], respectively), and ED visits ($1,414 [1,323, 1,506] and $1,411 [1,284, 1,538] vs $487 [449, 525], respectively) (Figure 3). Discussion

This study of US medical claims in the Optum CDM database showed that patients with COPD and type 2 inflammation characterized by either blood eosinophil count ≥150 or ≥300 cells/µL alongside a history of moderate or severe exacerbations, are more likely to experience exacerbations and incur a greater healthcare resource burden and costs than those with blood eosinophil count ≥150 cells/µL but without exacerbation history. Overall, HCRU and total costs were approximately two-fold greater among patients with versus without a history of exacerbations.

The present study contributes to the growing real-world evidence that patients with COPD and type 2 inflammation suffer from an increased disease burden. Indeed, a retrospective medical claims analysis of the Practice Fusion database linked to Symphony Health Solutions found that patients with blood eosinophil count ≥220 cells/µL were more likely to experience moderate or severe COPD exacerbations and had greater costs of care than those with blood eosinophil count <220 cells/µL.17 Another retrospective study of insurance claims data from the Reliant Medical Group found that patients with blood eosinophil count ≥150 cells/µL had numerically higher HCRU and healthcare costs than those with blood eosinophil count <150 cells/µL, and these differences were exacerbated when patients had blood eosinophil count ≥150 cells/µL and a history of exacerbations.18 The current study expands upon these data to highlight that this economic burden is greater in patients who have reached an uncontrolled stage of the disease (ie, history of exacerbations), compared to those who still have a controlled disease stage (ie, no exacerbations), while leveraging data from a larger patient population.

In 2050, the total number of COPD cases in North America is estimated to reach 45.1 million, with a prevalence of 13.5%.22 The World Population Review estimates the US make up 56.3% of the population of North America.23 Thus, assuming a homogenous prevalence of COPD between the countries that make up North America, we can expect the number of COPD cases in the US to potentially reach 25.4 million in 2050. This forecast, along with the findings of the present study, illustrate the significant healthcare and financial burden that COPD may pose to the US healthcare system, especially among patients with blood eosinophil count ≥150 cells/µL and a history of moderate/severe exacerbations, if treatment paradigms do not shift and new disease‑management guidelines are not implemented by 2050.

This study highlights the unmet needs among patients with COPD with type 2 inflammation who continue to exacerbate despite eligibility for triple inhaler therapy. It is well established that previous exacerbations are a predictor of future exacerbations.7,8 However, the current study expands upon this knowledge in patients with a blood eosinophil count of ≥150 cell/µL, indicative of type 2 inflammation, to demonstrate the economic burden of continuing exacerbations in this population. On average, these patients had two clinically significant exacerbations per year and incurred substantial burden to the healthcare system, with most of them receiving Medicare Advantage coverage (~95%). Targeted therapies, such as biologics, may be a possible option to address this unmet medical need. Two Phase III trials of dupilumab, a human monoclonal antibody that targets the interleukin-4Rα receptor, showed greater efficacy versus placebo in patients with blood eosinophil count ≥300 cells/µL and frequent exacerbations.24 Additionally, a prespecified meta-analysis of the METREX and METREO Phase III trials of mepolizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody specifically targeting interleukin-5, showed a significant reduction in moderate or severe exacerbations among patients with blood eosinophil counts ≥150 cells/μL at screening or ≥300 cells/μL in the prior year.25,26 These results were later confirmed by the MATINEE Phase III trial, which demonstrated the efficacy of mepolizumab in significantly reducing moderate/severe exacerbations in patients with COPD, frequent exacerbations and type 2 inflammation characterized by blood eosinophil count ≥300 cells/µL.27 Dupilumab and mepolizumab were therefore approved by the US Food and Drug Administration as add-on therapies in patients with poorly controlled COPD with an eosinophilic phenotype,28,29 and are recommended in the most recent update of the GOLD guidelines, alongside azithromycin and roflumilast, in patients who continue to exacerbate despite triple therapy.30

Interestingly, our data identified differences in the baseline characteristics between patients with a history of exacerbations (ie, EXAC-EP-150 and EXAC-EP-300) and those without. Firstly, comorbidities, including cardiovascular disorders, depression, gastro-esophageal reflux disease, and asthma, were more common among patients with a history of exacerbations. This aligns with previous data demonstrating a significantly increased frequency of exacerbations among patients with COPD exhibiting these comorbidities, and a significant increase in the frequency of severe exacerbations among patients with comorbid asthma.8 Secondly, there was a slightly higher proportion of female patients among the groups with a history of exacerbations than in the reference group (Table 2). This may be partly explained by the higher prevalence of comorbid asthma in the same groups, which is more commonly observed among female patients than male patients,19 and data suggesting that female patients may have a higher risk of having COPD exacerbations than male patients.8

The strengths of this study include the relatively large sample size, providing a degree of generalizability in the US context, especially to patients receiving Medicare Advantage coverage. This study may be limited by the retrospective nature of claims data, collected primarily for reimbursement processing rather than clinical research, which can be impacted by coding inaccuracies with respect to identifying eligible patients, defining COPD-related exacerbations and estimating HCRU and associated costs. For example, while 33% of patients in this claims-based analysis had comorbid asthma, consistent with the numbers observed in the overall COPD population,31 this may partly reflect misdiagnoses or mis-categorizations of asthma. Laboratory values are available for less than half of patients within Optum CDM, which could have introduced selection bias. The current study enrolled patients with ≥1 day of triple therapy exposure, which was likely insufficient to capture or represent those patients who were stable on maintenance therapy. Therefore, it is possible that patients remained uncontrolled and continued to exacerbate due to poor adherence to maintenance therapy, which was not assessed in the current study. Furthermore, although our analysis excluded blood eosinophil count measurements taken near the time of an OCS dispensing, the recorded blood eosinophil count levels used for patient stratification may still have been influenced by prior corticosteroid use or recent exacerbations. Generalizability may be affected by the fact that these results only apply to commercially insured US employment-based adults.

Conclusions

To conclude, this analysis demonstrates the high exacerbation rate and HCRU in US patients with COPD with type 2 inflammation (characterized by either blood eosinophil count ≥150 or ≥300 cells/µL) who continue to exacerbate despite eligibility for triple therapy, the majority of whom have Medicare Advantage coverage (~95%). These results highlight an unmet therapeutic need, reflecting the current effort to identify enhanced targeted therapeutic options, such as biologics, for patients with uncontrolled COPD and an eosinophilic phenotype (with blood eosinophil counts ≥150 cells/µL).
