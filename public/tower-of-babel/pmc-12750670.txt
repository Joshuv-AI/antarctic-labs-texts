Geosocial inequalities in walking for leisure at older ages
Authors: Yang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWalking for leisure is a key contributor to healthy ageing, yet participation remains uneven across older populations. Addressing these inequalities requires approaches that capture how physical and social environments jointly shape opportunities for walking. Integrating the COM-B (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behavior) model’s emphasis on behavioral opportunity, this study examines how the combinations of geographic and social domains intersect to shape walking in later life.MethodsConceptually grounded in the geosocial framework, which posits that territories (e.g., parks, walkways, libraries), social networks (e.g., social trust and bonds), and social categories (e.g., age and gender) intertwine to shape health and well-being, multilevel models were analyzed to examine the association between interlocking geosocial environments and walking using data from the National Health Interview Survey for adults aged 55 or older (n = 14,149).ResultsCollectively the three geosocial domains were interdependent, while individually territorial characteristics showed the strongest associations with walking for leisure, followed by social network and categorical characteristics.ConclusionsBy integrating the geographic and social opportunity domains of the COM-B model through the geosocial framework, this study quantitatively operationalizes the built, network, and categorical domains as interacting structures at a fine level of granularity to inform precisely targeted physical activity interventions among older populations.Keywords: Ageing, Environment, Geospatial, Health equity, Precision intervention, Physical activity, COM-B

Introduction

Robust evidence documents the benefits of physical activity for health and quality of life for older adults [1, 2]. Research shows that walking reduces the risk of dementia [3] and mortality [4] among older adults, and enhances cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, psychosocial health, and overall quality of life [5, 6]. Yet, the guidelines on physical activity for older adults by the World Health Organization and the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services have rarely been met [7]. In 2018, just 46% of U.S. adults aged 65–74 engaged in levels of leisure physical activity meeting the federal guidelines, while the share of women aged 65–74 fulfilling the same guidelines was even lower at 42% [8].

To promote physical activity in the older population, precisely targeted approaches are a viable intervention pathway [9–11]. These approaches emphasize contextual precision that aligns physical activity guidance with each person’s lived environment and social situation, acknowledging inter-individual heterogeneity in physiological responses as well as environmental and ecological influences. For example, an older woman residing in a rural area with limited socialization or transportation options likely requires a different set of supports and opportunities for walking compared to a middle-aged man living in an urban area with ample parks, walkways, public transportation, and socialization opportunities.

Yet, extant literature has rarely explored the intertwined role of the social and built environmental influences on physical activity in later life, particularly using nationally representative data, despite substantial evidence linking both domains to older people’s walking behaviors. Research on the built environment highlights how features such as walkability, access to green space, and neighborhood design affect mobility and physical activity [2, 12, 13]. Complementary evidence shows that social environments, such as trust, social cohesion, and supportive networks, also play a decisive role [14–17]. Although emerging research shows that neighborhood walkability and social conditions can interact to influence activity [18–20], integrative evidence remains limited. This constrains the understanding of potential intervention mechanisms to promote physical activity, as each individual is situated within unique combinations of social and physical conditions that may interact to influence walking.

An integrative conceptual approach is needed to articulate how built and social structures jointly shape behavioral ‘opportunity’, a key component of the COM-B model of behavior [21], which proposes that Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation interact to generate Behavior. While COM-B has been widely used to frame health behavior interventions, the ‘opportunity’ (O) domain—comprising both physical and social opportunity—has rarely been operationalized through frameworks that treat social and physical environments as intertwined. This calls for research that integrates the social and physical opportunity domains to help the development of synergistic interventions that address the interdependent environments in which older adults live and move, which is examined next through the geosocial framework.

Geosocial framework

The geosocial framework helps to understand and improve the opportunity (O) domain of the COM-B model for targeted PA interventions at the intersection of the social and built environments. The geosocial framework (Fig. 1) posits that individual health behaviors and outcomes are conditional upon contextual processes consisting of territories (e.g., parks, walkways, libraries), social networks (e.g., social trust and bonds), and social categories (e.g., age and gender) that interact to shape health and well-being [22]. This framework advances the understanding of place and space to integrate built infrastructure such as sidewalks with social spaces for interactions and support, and social networks that mirror the interactions and trust between people, where these domains are additionally shaped by memberships in social categories (e.g., age, gender).

In the case of walking, the geosocial framework suggests that contextual barriers and motivators of leisure walking would be a combination of territorial, network, and categorical factors. For example, older people residing in environments with greater social supports are likely to engage in more regular walking relative to environments where social resources are limited, despite comparable profiles of walkability from built structures [23]. Research demonstrates that older men are more physically active than older women in advanced ages [24], regularly walking to social spaces or local services to engage in social activities, despite mountainous terrains with challenging topography [25]. Although the built environment has been extensively researched [2, 12] as barriers and motivators of walking, little is known about their interactions with social network and social categories in shaping older adults’ leisure walking activity [26]. The geosocial framework explicitly addresses how these domains intersect to shape leisure walking in older adults.

The present study

The study aims to quantitatively operationalize the built, network, and categorical factors as interacting domains of physical and social opportunity within the COM-B model at a level of fine granularity to inform precisely targeted interventions for physical activity among older populations. Based on the geosocial framework, territories are defined as accessible and experienced material, social, and natural infrastructure, social networks refer to interpersonal relations and experiences, and social categories indicate intergroup relations and experiences. This study addresses two focal questions: (1) To what extent are disparities in walking explained by geosocial environments at the intersection of territories, social networks, and social categories? (2) How much explanatory power does each geosocial domain have, relative to an analysis without any intersectional geosocial domain? The study identifies the individual geosocial domains with the highest predictive power for walking, as well as the collective intersectional geosocial combinations where leisure walking is more likely for older people, offering evidence for precision interventions to promote leisure walking. Pioneered in social epidemiology, the innovative approach of precision interventions delivers “the right intervention to the right person” [27] and is shown to effectively narrow health disparities [28].

Methodology

Data

The 2015 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) provided data for the present study. The NHIS is a nationally representative survey of the non-institutionalized civilian population of the U.S [29]. Data on walking for leisure and environmental characteristics were procured from the section on Physical Activity of the Cancer Control Supplement (not repeated at the time of this writing). Respondents were asked about their walking for leisure in the prior 7 days in addition to characteristics relating to walkability of communities – an innovative feature of the 2015 National Health Interview Survey [29]. Data for demographic covariates were drawn from the Sample Adult core file of the NHIS. For the 2015 NHIS, sampling weights were based on the 2010 Census estimates [29]. The analytic sample included 14,149 adults aged 55 or older, after < 0.5% of the sample were dropped due to missing information on variables of interest [30]. The Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics and the U.S. Office of Management and Budget approved the NHIS [29]. All NHIS respondents gave oral consent ahead of participation [29].

Measures

Outcome

Walking for leisure refers to at least one 10-minute session of walking that does not include walking for transportation in the 7 days prior to the survey [31] and was assessed using the question: “During the past 7 days, did you walk for at least 10 minutes for fun, relaxation, exercise, or to walk the dog?” Affirmative responses to this question were treated as leisure walking [32].

Geosocial domains

The three dimensions of the geosocial framework were separately assessed: territorial, categorical, and network domains [22]. Territorial dimension was gauged using four questions: “Where you live, are there roads, sidewalks, paths or trails where you can walk?”; “Are there bus or transit stops that you can walk to?”; “Are there places like movies, libraries, or churches that you can walk to?”; “Are there places that you can walk to that help you relax, clear your mind, and reduce stress?” [33]. Categorical dimension was gauged using indicators of age (55–64, 65–74, 75+) and gender (female = 1, male = 0). Network dimension was measured using two survey questions: whether “people in the neighborhood can be trusted” and whether “people in the neighborhood help each other” [34]. Responses were coded dichotomously: 1= “Yes”, 0= “No”/“Don’t know” for indicators of the territorial dimension, and 1= “Somewhat agree”/“Definitely agree”, 0= “Somewhat disagree”/“Definitely disagree”/“Don’t know” for indicators of the network dimension, following Omura and colleagues [35].

Covariates

Educational attainment and race/ethnicity were controlled for, based on research evidence that these attributes were consistently associated with physical activity at older ages [2, 12, 36, 37].

Analytic strategy

To examine the explanatory power of geosocial intersections on leisure walking, multilevel analysis of individual heterogeneity and discriminatory accuracy was applied to partition total variance into between- and within-strata components [38, 39]. This technique nests each respondent within an intersection (i.e., stratum) and estimates random effects at the level of intersections. The relative explanatory power of an intersectional dimension was evaluated using the proportional change in variance between the null model and the model including the intersectional dimension of interest, where a larger proportional change in the random intercepts’ variance indicates higher explanatory power of the added dimension [40]. In total, 384 geosocial intersections (3 × 2^7) were constructed through the cross-classification of seven dichotomous attributes representing gender, territorial, and social environments, and three age groups (55–64, 65–74, 75+). On average, each intersection contained about 40 respondents. Analyses were performed in Stata following the procedures outlined in prior literature [41]. Respondents with missing data on walking or key covariates (< 0.5%) were excluded. Sensitivity checks including low-frequency strata yielded consistent estimates. This innovative method was used given its advantages over conventional approaches in analyzing interlocking contextual attributes, including ease of interpretation, model parsimony, precise and reliable estimates [38]. The empirical implementation and theoretical foundations of this approach are described in detail in prior literature [38–40].

Results

Table 1 reports the characteristics of leisure walking and the geosocial dimensions among sample respondents. About 48% of the sample participated in at least one 10-minute duration of walking for leisure in the 7 days prior to the survey. In terms of the territorial dimension, 78% of respondents reported having access to roads, paths or trails for walking; 42% reported having pedestrian access to bus or transit stops; 37% reported having pedestrian access to movies, libraries, or churches; 63% reported having pedestrian access to places (e.g. parks) that help them relax and reduce stress. In terms of the categorical dimension, 54% were women; 46% were men; 47% aged 55 to 64; 31% aged 65 to 74; 22% aged 75+. For the network dimension, 83% indicated that residents in the neighborhood can be trusted, while 81% reported that people in the neighborhood offer help to each other. Table 1 also reports demographic control characteristics. About 42% of the sample attained education at the level of high school or below, while 58% attained college education or higher. Approximately 76% of the sample were non-Hispanic white, 10% non-Hispanic black, 4% non-Hispanic Asian, 1% non-Hispanic other race, and 9% Hispanic (any race).

Table 2 provides results for multilevel analyses on intersecting geosocial dimensions and walking for leisure. Individually, territorial dimension showed the highest predictive power for leisure walking, as its inclusion in the model reduced the random intercept’s variance by 70% (Model 2), followed by network (9%, Model 4) and categorical (3%, Model 3) dimensions, referencing the null model (Model 1) where the additive effects of geosocial dimensions were not taken into account. Collectively, the combination of all three geosocial dimensions showed the highest predictive power as it reduced the random intercept’s variance by 88% (Model 8), followed by the combinations of, respectively, territorial + network (83%, Model 7), territorial + categorical (74%, Model 5), and categorical + network (14%, Model 6), referencing to the null model (Model 1). This indicates that 12% of the between-strata variation remains unexplained by additive effects but was reflected in non-additive interaction, evidence of the high levels of variability in leisure walking across interdependent geosocial environments.

Each row in this table represents an intersectional geosocial environment, where a shaded cell indicates ‘yes’ to the corresponding attribute. For the purpose of illustration, ten intersecting environments each with the highest and lowest predicted values are shown. At the top of the table are the geosocial environments with the highest predicted values of walking: predominantly persons aged 55–74 in environments with ample territorial features (trails, transit stops, libraries, and parks) and strong social network. By contrast, the bottom of the table shows the geosocial environments with the lowest predicted values of walking: primarily persons aged 65 + in environments with zero territorial feature and weak social network. These attributes (or lack thereof) co-constitute the geosocial environments where older people are least likely to engage in leisure walking and are logical interventions targets.

Discussion

The present study sought to quantitatively examine geosocial inequalities in walking for leisure at older ages. Drawing from the geosocial framework, the study investigated the combined role of territories, social networks, and social categories in walking for leisure by positioning individuals within intersecting geosocial domains. Findings show that the three geosocial domains are interdependent, while individually territorial characteristics exhibited the greatest explanatory power for leisure walking, followed by social network and categorical characteristics. These results support the notion that physical and social opportunity domains, which are central to the COM-B model, should be considered jointly when promoting behavior change in older adults, particularly in contexts where socioeconomic disadvantage or limited neighborhood infrastructure may constrain opportunities [18, 19].

While a growing body of research has examined how built and social environments shape physical activity in later life, few studies have operationalized these factors as intersecting domains of physical and social opportunity within the COM-B model at a level of fine granularity to inform precisely targeted interventions. In the present study, the observed role of social networks in motivating walking aligns with evidence on the importance of social cohesion, trust, and supportive networks [14, 16, 17], including qualitative “walk-along” interviews [42], highlighting that older adults are incentivized to walk through social exchanges beyond access to built infrastructure. Similarly, the observed geosocial interactions for leisure walking echo findings that neighborhood walkability and social conditions jointly influence physical activity [20], and that built environment features such as green spaces and sidewalks support mobility [2, 12, 13]. Together, these findings indicate that future research and intervention design should more deliberately integrate physical and social opportunity dimensions – consistent with COM-B model – and consider their interaction with motivation and capability to better support sustained engagement in walking among older adults [21].

The study identifies the specific intersectional geosocial environments where older adults are most likely to walk for leisure, offering granular evidence for targeted interventions to promote physical activity in later life. Simply put, persons aged 55–74 in places with ample territorial features (trails, transit stops, libraries, and parks) and strong social network show the highest likelihood of leisure walking. However, the finding that persons aged 65 + in places with poor territorial infrastructure and weak social network are the least likely to engage in walking offers evidence on intervention targets, as these likely represent individuals in areas where a motorized vehicle would be needed for most daily activities. In some rural areas, the opportunity for social exchange is particularly cherished by older people who have diminishing social contact following retirement [43, 44].

A few study limitations merit discussion. Given data availability, the environmental conditions in this study are perceived and self-reported conditions for walking, and thus may not objectively reflect the actual conditions. The territorial indicators primarily capture the quantity of environmental features rather than their quality (e.g., aesthetics or maintenance) which recent reviews indicate are crucial for older adults’ engagement in walking [18, 19]. It is acknowledged that other covariates may influence the outcome but could not be controlled for, including health, frailty status or physical function, previous falls, self-efficacy and additional indicators of socioeconomic status. Data permitting, future research should investigate other aspects of territories and social network by utilizing objectively measured attributes to examine the role of geosocial interactions in shaping late-life walking. Future research should additionally investigate the role of other domains, for example the digital environment as it intersects with physical and social environments in predicting physical activity. No causal interpretations should be made from the present study.

Despite these limitations, this study establishes the extent to which geosocial environments predict late-life leisure walking, and identifies specific geosocial environments with the highest and lowest probability of walking in the older population. The study draws insights from the geosocial framework, which posits that social and geographic resources jointly shape health and health behavior. Given the wide range of health benefits associated with walking, alongside the emerging precision prescriptions to promote physical activity by identifying internal and external influences specific to the individual, this research offers evidence for tailored and synergistic interventions to enhance leisure walking. Additionally, this study represents a methodological contribution to the research on the multilevel antecedents of health behavior by capturing the ecological connections between individuals and their geosocial environment.

Conclusion

Evidence on the combined role of territorial, network, and categorical characteristics in predicting leisure walking activity facilitates the development of synergistic and precision interventions to promote physical activity in the older population. Programs that are designed to enhance the built or territorial structures (e.g. sidewalks, parks) may gain additional efficacy for older people in social settings that incentivize physical activity, such as reliable social network (e.g. trust). Conversely, efforts to promote walking through fostering social exchanges may benefit from enhancements to the territorial infrastructures, including well-maintained trails and sidewalks, as well as accessible transit stops. Considerations may also be given to categorical characteristics including age and gender when designing physical activity interventions to individuals based on scientific evidence of the prevalence of physical activity and health outcomes within relevant population segments.
