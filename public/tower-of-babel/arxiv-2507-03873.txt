RateCount: Learning-Free Device Counting by Wi-Fi Probe Listening
Tianlang He, Zhangyu Chang, and S.-H. Gary Chan

Abstract
A Wi-Fi-enabled device, or simply Wi-Fi device, sporadically broadcasts probe request frames (PRFs) to discover nearby access points (APs), whether connected to an AP or not. To protect user privacy, unconnected devices often randomize their MAC addresses in the PRFs, known as MAC address randomization. While prior works have achieved accurate device counting under MAC address randomization, they typically rely on machine learning, resulting in inefficient deployment due to the time-consuming processes of data cleaning, model training, and hyperparameter tuning. To enhance deployment efficiency, we propose RateCount, an accurate, lightweight, and learning-free counting approach based on the rate at which APs receive PRFs within a window. RateCount employs a provably unbiased closed-form expression to estimate the device count time-averaged over the window and an error model to compute the lower bound of the estimation variance. We also demonstrate how to extend RateCount to people counting by incorporating a device-to-person calibration scheme. Through extensive real-world experiments conducted at multiple sites spanning a wide range of counts, we show that RateCount, without any deployment costs for machine learning, achieves comparable counting accuracy with the state-of-the-art learning-based device counting and improves previous people counting schemes by a large margin.

A Wi-Fi-enabled device, or simply
Wi-Fi device
in this paper, actively manages its wireless connection by sporadically broadcasting probe request frames (PRFs) to the access points (APs) in its proximity.
The frames are broadcast no matter whether the device has been connected to an AP, with an interval generally ranging from seconds to minutes.

II-B

Window-averaged Device Counting

Fig. 5:

The window-averaged device count is 2.5, with two devices (

Given the widespread use of Wi-Fi devices, the number of devices in an area could be used to estimate the population of the area, which is crucial for many IoT applications. For example, knowing the variation of device counts near bus stations is beneficial for scheduling public transportation. This knowledge is also useful for reflecting the queuing situation in various public venues such as canteens, banks, hospitals, toilets, which facilitates crowd management. To enable these applications, we study device counting, specifically, the counting of the number of devices present at a specific time instant in an area.

Device counting based on Wi-Fi probing has two challenges. First, since a probing burst is transient, it is difficult to know the device count at each time instant. Second, due to MAC address randomization, APs could receive more distinct MAC addresses than the actual number of devices, which further complicates the counting problem. To overcome the issues, previous approaches employ machine learning to associate the probing bursts with their broadcast devices and assume the probing intervals as the
dwell time
of the device in the counting area. To illustrate this, we present a counting example in Fig.
5
, showing the dwell time of three devices and their probing bursts that have been associated with their broadcast devices. Based on the association results, the three devices (
A,B
and
C
) are correctly counted at time instant

, because the probing intervals of the three devices overlap at
t_{1}
.
Although effective, as mentioned earlier, the system deployment of this approach could be costly and inefficient due to the PRF association based on machine learning. Also, it may under-count the devices, as the probing interval is expected to be shorter than the actual device dwell time (see the counting example at
t_{2}
).

In this paper, we propose a lightweight counting approach, which approximates the device count using the number of devices time-averaged over a window, namely,

. As illustrated in Fig.
5
, the window-averaged device count from

to

and

C

and

t

\overline{N}=\frac{1}{w}\int_{0}^{w}N(t)dt,

(3)

where the window extends from time

to
w

In the following, we discuss how to unbiasedly estimate the window-averaged device count (
\overline{N}
) under MAC address randomization without using machine learning and unveil how window size (

III-A

Rate Model

As mentioned earlier, a counting area is the joint coverage of one or more APs. Given a window of size
w
during which
B
probing bursts are received by the APs, we design a rate model to estimate the device count within the window. Our observation is that APs typically receive the bursts at a
higher
rate when
more
devices present in the area.

First, we define the rate at which APs receive probing bursts in the window as the
aggregated probing rate
, which is calculated as

R=\frac{B}{w},

(4)

and the total number of probing bursts (
B
) is the sum of the number of probing bursts from all the devices ({
b_{n}
}), i.e.,

B=\sum_{n=1}^{\mathbf{N}}b_{n}.

(5)

Intuitively, the aggregated probing rate quantifies how frequently Wi-Fi probing occurs in the window.

Second, we define the average rate that a single device probes in a counting area as the
mean probing rate
, which is calculated as

\overline{r}=\frac{1}{\overline{\tau}},

(6)

where the
mean probing interval
, denoted as
\overline{\tau}
,
is derived from the distribution of the probing interval (
\tau
) given this counting area (
v\in V
)
from (
2
), expressed as

\overline{\tau}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau Pr(\tau\mid v)d\tau.

(7)

Intuitively, the mean probing rate quantifies how frequently each device probes in a counting area on average.

Given the above, the rate model counts devices by the
ratio
of the aggregated probing rate to the mean probing rate, which is given as

\widehat{N}=\frac{R}{\overline{r}}=\frac{B}{w\overline{r}}=\frac{B\overline{\tau}}{w}.

(8)

We argue that
\widehat{N}
is an unbiased estimator of the ground truth
\overline{N}
when the device count (
\overline{N}
) and window size (
w
) are sufficiently large.

Next, we explain that
\widehat{N}
approximates the ground truth

[

Approximation of Ground Truth

III-C

Error Model

We have demonstrated that RateCount approximates the ground truth. In the following, we deduce an error model to reveal how the lower bound of its counting error varies with the window size, the number of bursts in the window, and the interval variance of a single device probing.

The mean squared error (MSE) of the rate model consists of the count variance, denoted as
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{N})
, and the counting bias, denoted as
\mathrm{Bias}(\widehat{N},\overline{N})
. The error of the rate model is computed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{N}-\overline{N}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{N}-\mathbb{E}\left(\widehat{N}\right)\right)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\widehat{N}\right)-\overline{N}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left(\widehat{N}\right)+\mathrm{Bias}^{2}\left(\widehat{N},\overline{N}\right).

Since the rate model is unbiased with sufficiently large
\overline{N}
and
w
, we neglect the bias term and estimate MSE using the count variance, i.e.,

\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{N}\right)\approx\mathrm{Var}\left(\widehat{N}\right),

(17)

where
\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{N})\geq\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{N})
.

Given that
b
and
w
are constants, the count variance depends on the variance of the mean probing interval, given as

\mathrm{Var}\left(\widehat{N}\right)=\mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{B\overline{\tau}}{w}\right)=\frac{B^{2}}{w^{2}}\mathrm{Var}\left(\overline{\tau}\right),

(18)

where
\mathrm{Var}(\overline{\tau})
represents the variance of the mean over a group of samples with a sample size of
B
, corresponding to the number of bursts.

Since
\tau
is an IID random variable, its variance contributes to the random error. Therefore, the
lower bound
of the count variance depends on the variance of its sample mean, i.e.,

\mathrm{Var}(\overline{\tau})\geq\frac{\sigma_{\tau}^{2}}{B},

(19)

where
\sigma_{\tau}
is the standard deviation of
\tau
, i.e., the standard deviation of the distribution in (
2
).
By combining (
18
) and (
19
), the error model is given as

\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{N}\right)\approx\mathrm{Var}\left(\widehat{N}\right)\geq\frac{B\sigma_{\tau}^{2}}{w^{2}}.

(20)

The error model conveys three key messages regarding the counting system. First, it shows a tradeoff in selecting the window size, where a large window could reduce the errors but may delay the counting results, and vice versa. Second, the counting area has an impact on the counting error as the variance of probing intervals contributes to the count variance. Third, the error model indicates that the count variance increases with the number of bursts (
B
). In fact,
B
does not have a direct impact on the count variance. Rather, the ground-truth number of devices could explain their correlation. That is, more devices lead to both more bursts and larger counting variance.

To clarify the third point, given that
\overline{N}\approx B\overline{\tau}/w
from (
8
) and (
9
),
we present the MSE relative to the ground-truth device count (
\overline{N}
) in normalized root-mean-square error (NRMSE), expressed as

\mathrm{NRMSE}\left(\widehat{N}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}\left(\widehat{N}\right)}}{\overline{N}}\approx\frac{\sigma_{\tau}}{\sqrt{\overline{N}w\overline{\tau}}}=\frac{\sigma_{\tau}}{\overline{\tau}\sqrt{B}}.

(21)

As indicated by NRMSE, the counting is more accurate relative to the ground-truth device count (
\overline{N}
).
In other words, RateCount tends to be more accurate in the more crowded scenarios in terms of relative counting accuracy.

IV-A

Multimodal Calibration Scheme

Given the popularity of Wi-Fi devices today, the device count is often used to estimate crowd density. However, in some scenarios, it is necessary to determine the exact number of people, regardless of whether they have Wi-Fi devices. To address this need, we demonstrate a calibration scheme that extends RateCount to people counting.

The gap between the number of devices and people lies in how many Wi-Fi devices a person typically carries, referred to as the

We define the device-to-person ratio rigorously as the mean number of devices a person carries in a counting area, calculated as

\alpha=\frac{\mathbf{N}}{\mathbf{M}},

(22)

where
\mathbf{N}
and
\mathbf{M}
are the numbers of devices and people in the counting area, respectively.

Given this ratio, we can estimate the window-averaged people count in any subarea of the counting region without a camera, given as

\displaystyle\overline{M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w}\int_{0}^{w}M(t)dt

(23)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{w}\int_{0}^{w}\frac{N(t)}{\alpha}dt=\frac{\overline{N}}{\alpha},

where

, and
\overline{N}
is estimated by RateCount.

We estimate the device-to-person ratio from the calibration region by

\alpha\approx\frac{\overline{N}}{\overline{M}},

(24)

where
\overline{N}
is estimated by RateCount, and
\overline{M}
is estimated by the camera-based people counting.

Next, we explain (
24
) by considering the dwell time of devices and people.
Recalling from (
10
), the total dwell time of all devices is the sum of the dwell time of each individual device.
On the other hand, the dwell time of devices equals their users’ dwell time
(devices that do not have an associated user can be excluded by using the method in
[
22
]
).
Therefore, the dwell time of all devices is given as

D=\sum_{n=1}^{\mathbf{N}}d_{n}=\sum_{m=1}^{\mathbf{M}}p_{m}\alpha_{m},

(25)

where
p_{m}
is the dwell time of the
m
th person who has
\alpha_{m}
as the number of devices in total.
8
8

8

Rigorously,
\alpha_{m}
represents the device-to-person ratio. Since it is specific to one person (the
m
th person), its value can be interpreted as the number of devices that the person carries.

Here, we consider a general case where a person’s dwell time is independent of how many devices it carries.
Therefore, with a sufficiently large number of people,
we have

\sum_{m=1}^{\mathbf{M}}p_{m}\alpha_{m}\approx\frac{\mathbf{N}}{\mathbf{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{\mathbf{M}}p_{m},

(26)

recalling that
\mathbf{N}/\mathbf{M}
is the mean number of devices carried by a person.
By combining (
25
) and (
26
),
we get

\frac{\overline{N}}{\overline{M}}=\frac{\frac{1}{w}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathbf{N}}d_{n}}{\frac{1}{w}\sum_{m=1}^{\mathbf{M}}p_{m}}\approx\frac{\frac{\mathbf{N}}{\mathbf{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{\mathbf{M}}p_{m}}{\sum_{m=1}^{\mathbf{M}}p_{m}}=\alpha.

(27)

This indicates that we can estimate
\alpha
using (
24
).

From the above analysis, the ratio is preferably calculated from a counting area with large crowds, such as a campus atrium or the entrance of a shopping mall, and a large window size is also preferred.

IV-C

Error Analysis

In this section, we analyze the counting error of the people counting scheme. In particular, we consider the error of camera-based people counting based on the error model discussed in Section
III-C
.

Let
\widehat{M}_{c}
and
\widehat{N}_{c}
denote the people count and device count estimations in the calibration region, respectively, and
\widehat{N}
denote the device count estimation in the counting area.
The people count estimation in the counting area, denoted by
\widehat{M}
, is given as

\widehat{M}=\frac{\widehat{M}_{c}}{\widehat{N}_{c}}\widehat{N}.

(28)

According to error propagation
[
23
]
, the NRMSE of the people count estimation, denoted by
\mathrm{NRMSE}{(\widehat{M})}
, is given as

\sqrt{\mathrm{NRMSE}^{2}{\left(\widehat{M}_{c}\right)}+\mathrm{NRMSE}^{2}{\left(\widehat{N}_{c}\right)}+\mathrm{NRMSE}^{2}{\left(\widehat{N}\right)}}.

In our implementation, we empirially obtain the error of camera-based people counting (
\mathrm{NRMSE}(\widehat{M}_{c})
).

\widehat{y}
represent the counting result,
\overline{y}
represent the (near) ground truth and
J
represent the number of observations in the experiment. We use the following metrics:

•

Root-Mean-Square Error
(RMSE):

\mathrm{RMSE}\left(\widehat{y}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{y}_{j}-\overline{y}_{j}\right)^{2}}.

(30)

(31)

In other words, RMSE calculates the counting error in terms of the number of devices or people, and MAPE and NRMSE reflect the error relative to the ground truth. Also, RMSE and NRMSE calculate the error based on second-order norm, which is more sensitive to large errors than MAPE.

In this section, we review the related works in Wi-Fi-based device counting and the existing sensing technologies for people counting.

VII
Conclusion

For network discovery, Wi-Fi devices sporadically broadcast probe request frames (PRFs) with randomized MAC addresses to the nearby access points (APs). While prior works have achieved accurate device counting under MAC address randomization, the counting relies on machine learning to associate the PRFs, leading to inefficient and time-consuming system deployment involving training data curation and cleaning, model training, and hyperparameter tuning. To enable deployment efficiency, we study a lightweight and learning-free approach to count Wi-Fi devices under MAC address randomization. Observing that more Wi-Fi devices lead to a higher PRF rate, we propose a counting approach named RateCount, which counts devices by a simple and effective closed-form expression that measures the rate of APs receiving PRFs within a window. We further prove that the rate model gives an unbiased estimator of the device count time-averaged over the window and deduce the lower bound of the counting error in terms of the window size, number of PRFs in the window, and the variance of the probing interval. In addition, we demonstrate how to extend RateCount to people counting via a multimodal calibration scheme. We have implemented RateCount and conducted extensive experiments of device and people counting in various environments, specifically, in a lab room, a building entrance, a campus atrium, and a bus station. The results show that our learning-free RateCount achieves comparable counting accuracy with the SOTA learning-based device counting and significantly improves the previous people counting by cutting 66% errors. Furthermore, our error model has demonstrated sound estimations of the counting error.

Appendix A
Proof of the Unbiasedness of RateCount

We provide a proof of
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{N})=\overline{N}
below by presenting that the whole system is equivalent to a

renewal process
. In a renewal process, we consider the summation of a sequence of positive IID random variables
X_{k}
given as

S_{K}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}X_{k},

(32)

where
\mathbb{E}(X_{k})=\overline{X}
.
We define a
counting random variable

L(S)
as the maximum number of IID random variables (
X_{k}
)
such that their summation
S_{L(S)}\leq S
.
Namely, we have
\sum_{k=1}^{L(S)}X_{k}\leq S
and
\sum_{k=1}^{L(S)+1}X_{k}>S
.

An important property of the renewal process is the
Elementary Renewal Theorem
,
which states that

\frac{\mathbb{E}(L(S))}{S}=\frac{1}{\overline{X}}

(33)

when
S
is sufficiently large.

We elaborate that our system is equivalent to a
renewal process
using the single-device example shown in Fig.
2
. In this figure, the APs received
b_{n}=2
bursts separately at
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, and the device enters the area at time
x
and leaves at
y
.
The dwell time of this device is calculated as

d_{n}=y-x=\beta_{1}\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\beta_{3}\tau_{3},

(34)

where
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{3}
represent the fraction of
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{3}
with
0<\beta_{1},\beta_{3}<1
. Since the device enters and leaves the area largely independent of the probing, with equal probabilities,
x
could be any value between
t_{0}
and
t_{1}
, and
y
could be any value between
t_{2}
and
t_{3}
. Therefore,
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{3}
are IID
uniform random variables
with
\mathbb{E}(\beta_{1})=\mathbb{E}(\beta_{3})=1/2
.

When computing the total dwell time (
D
), we use
T_{F}
to denote the set of
fractional
intervals
\{\beta_{i}\tau_{i}\}

where only part of
\tau_{i}
is summed up (e.g.,
\beta_{1}\tau_{1}
and
\beta_{3}\tau_{3}
),
and
T_{I}
to denote the set of
integral

\{\tau_{i}\}

\tau_{i}

\tau_{2}
).
It is important to note that both

and

are sets of IID random variables

\mathbb{E}(\beta_{i}\tau_{i})=\overline{\tau}/2

\mathbb{E}(\tau_{i})=\overline{\tau}

Generally speaking,
\mathbf{N}

B

(35)

We let

for
\tau_{i}\in T_{I}

Clearly, we have

\frac{\mathbb{E}(B-\mathbf{N})}{D_{I}}=\frac{1}{\overline{\tau}}

(36)

(37)

when

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{N})
