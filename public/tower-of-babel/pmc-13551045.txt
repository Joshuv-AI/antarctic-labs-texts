Social Determinants of Diabetic Retinopathy Severity at Initial Presentation: A Cross-Sectional Study of Data from an Urban Safety Net Hospital
Authors: Carolyn H Brandt, Sriranjani P Padmanabhan, Kieran S O’Brien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDiabetic retinopathy (DR) is the leading cause of blindness among working-age adults in the US We aimed to assess relationships between social determinants of health and indicators of DR severity at initial presentation at a public safety net hospital.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study using electronic health records among patients presenting to Zuckerberg San Francisco General (ZSFG) with diabetic retinopathy. Outcomes included DR severity (non-proliferative vs proliferative) and visual acuity (VA) at initial presentation. Multivariable modified Poisson and linear regressions were used to assess the relationship between sociodemographic and clinical predictors and these outcomes.ResultsWe identified 2044 patients with DR at initial presentation to (1851 with complete VA data) who had a mean (SD) age was 55.8 (11.6) years. Two hundred eighty (13.7%) patients had proliferative DR at initial presentation to ZSFG and 449 (24.3%) had some level of visual impairment or blindness (logMAR ≥ 0.30). Predictors increasing the risk of severe DR included experiencing housing or economic problems (adjusted Risk Ratio (aRR) 1.35, 95% CI 1.0 to 1.71, P=0.01), other race and ethnicity (aRR 1.94, 95% CI 1.06 to 3.54, p=0.03), former smoking status (aRR 1.28, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.64, P=0.05). Predictors associated with worse visual acuity at presentation included Medicare (adjusted Mean Difference (aMD) 0.05 logMAR, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.10) and Medicaid (aMD 0.06 logMAR, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.10, P =0.01) insurance status, hypertension (aMD 0.03 logMAR, 95% CI 0.002 to 0.06, P =0.04), age (aMD 0.02 logMAR, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.03, p=0.01), and Cantonese language (aMD 0.04 logMAR, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.09, p=0.05).ConclusionsGiven the high burden of severe DR and vision loss in this population, identifying approaches to target high-risk groups with early diagnosis and treatment among vulnerable populations is needed.

Introduction

In the United States, diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the leading cause of blindness among working-age adults.1 The current prevalence of 9.6 million cases is projected to grow due to the increasing burden of diabetes mellitus, particularly among youth and adults.2 Noticeable symptoms can be minimal in early stages (mild non-proliferative DR), but if left untreated, progression to later stages including proliferative DR and diabetic macular edema can threaten vision.3,4 Up to 95% of severe outcomes resulting from DR progression are preventable with early detection, routine screening, and appropriate, timely treatment.4,5 National guidelines recommend comprehensive, dilated DR examinations annually or biannually among patients with diabetes, yet adherence to these guidelines ranges from just 15–77%.6

Disparities across the diabetes care continuum have been documented, leading to preventable inequities in clinical outcomes among racial and ethnic minority groups, lower-income individuals, and underinsured individuals.7,8 Previous studies have found that DR examination rates are lower among racial and ethnic minority groups, and there are greater barriers in accessing care among Hispanic and Black individuals compared to non-Hispanic White individuals.6,9 Social determinants of health (SDoH) are also influential, with poor healthcare access and housing conditions associated with worse adherence to regular DR screening.6 Individuals with type 2 diabetes who experience housing or food insecurity are also less likely to visit eye care practitioners.10 Health insurance also plays a role, with one study finding that patients insured through Medicaid were less likely to follow-up with eye care providers after a proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) visit,11 and another finding that Medicaid patients experience worse baseline visual acuity at DME treatment initiation compared with Medicare and private insurance patients.12

Although risk factors for adherence to DR examinations and of progressing to severe DR are relatively well established,6,9,11,12 less evidence exists on the relationship between social determinants of health and severity of DR at initial presentation. Previous studies have assessed data from large population surveys and datasets,9,12–14 but there is minimal evidence among primarily underserved populations. Preliminary data suggest these patients face a higher risk of progression to severe DR and worse visual outcomes than higher-income, more homogenous patient populations.12 To address these gaps, this study aimed to identify risk factors for late presentation for eye care among patients with DR in an urban safety‑net hospital in San Francisco.

Methods

Study Design

This was a cross-sectional study using electronic health record (EHR) data from Zuckerberg San Francisco General (ZSFG) hospital, a large, safety‑net hospital serving low-income patients across the San Francisco Bay area. The Zuckerberg San Francisco General Hospital and Trauma Center (ZSFG) is a public safety‑net hospital in the city of San Francisco that serves as the only Level 1 trauma center for San Francisco and San Mateo counties. The hospital primarily provides care for diverse patients who are publicly insured, uninsured, or otherwise underserved, including a large homeless and housing insecure population. The Ophthalmology and Optometry services at ZSFG collectively perform over 20,000 individual patient encounters per year in addition to nearly a 1000 surgeries per year.

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of California, San Francisco (22–36540) provided ethical approval for the survey, including a waiver of consent. Data were extracted from the electronic health records of eligible patients from ZSFG by Academic Research Services at the Clinical and Translational Science Institute. Protected health information was removed and data was deidentified. The ZSFG EHR system transitioned to Epic in August 2019 and the data were accessed in September 2023.

Participants

We extracted all available electronic health record data from 2014–2023 and included cases with a first diagnosis of DR during that period. DR was identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) codes. To ensure inclusion of incident cases, we only included data from a patient’s first available date of DR diagnosis. In addition, patients with the same first diabetes and DR diagnoses date were excluded to reduce the likelihood that they already had a DR diagnosis from outside of ZSFG. Patients with greater than 5 years between DR diagnosis date and the most recent clinic visit date with sociodemographic and clinical risk factor data were excluded to reduce potential exposure misclassification. Patients with ICD codes not referring to retinopathy, those with “unspecified” DR, and missing DR were also excluded. If a patient had different DR severity diagnoses in each eye, the eye with the worse severity was retained for DR severity analyses.

Variables Collected

Two primary outcomes were considered: DR severity and visual acuity at initial presentation. DR severity at initial presentation was identified using ICD 10 codes (Supplementary Table 1) and categorized by severity into two groups: non-proliferative (including mild, moderate, and severe), and proliferative. ICD codes which referred to cataract and not retinopathy were removed (Supplementary Table 1). A chart review was performed on 88 observations in which the ICD code designating DR severity was unclear, and observations identified as being placeholder values for further charting were excluded, while 2 observations were correctly categorized by severity based on information available in the chart.

Visual acuity at initial presentation was defined as the best corrected value in the better-seeing eye. Data were extracted as Snellen values and converted to logMAR. The dichotomous visual impairment variable was defined using the World Health Organization ICD-11 classification as no visual impairment (logMAR < 0.30) and visual impairment or blindness (logMAR ≥ 0.30) for descriptive analyses.

Independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics available in the EHR: sex (male and female), age, race and ethnicity (Non-Hispanic Asian, Non-Hispanic Black or African American, Hispanic, Multi-race or ethnicity, Other (including American Indian or Alaska Native), Non-Hispanic Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, and Non-Hispanic White). American Indian or Alaska Native individuals were grouped with those with “Other” noted in their EHR record due to small numbers which may potentially identify patients. Individual-level social determinants of health included preferred language (Cantonese, English, Other, Spanish), housing or economic circumstances problems (yes, no), and insurance type (Medicaid, Medicare, Other, and uninsured). “Other” insurance type included pseudo-private employer-based insurance types, as well as Healthy workers HMO, because it is tied to employment even though it is run by city of San Francisco. “Healthy SF” is a local public health coverage program and was categorized in the uninsured category, since only uninsured individuals are eligible for the program. Behavioral factors included smoking status (current, former, never, unknown) and alcohol consumption (current, former, never, unknown, missing).

Individual clinical level characteristics relating to diabetes management included: HbA1c score, hypertension (yes, no), and hyperlipidemia (yes, no). Patient-level social determinants of health data had a high degree of missingness in the EHR and so we used patient zip code data to link to the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI).15 SVI is based on US census data at the census tract level and ranks tracts by social vulnerability based on social factors including poverty, lack of vehicle access, and crowded housing, with values ranging from 0 (least vulnerable) to 1 (most vulnerable).16 We weighted the tract values to create a zip code community-level social vulnerability index value predictor for our population. Supplementary Table 2 lists all variables considered in analyses.

Sample Size

The sample size was fixed at 2044 patients based on the number of initial DR visits in the EHR time periods available. We calculated the minimum detectable effect based on the age predictor, where 942 patients were younger than the mean age of 55.8 years and 1101 were older. We had 80% power to detect an absolute difference in the prevalence of proliferative DR at presentation of 6% between these age groups, corresponding to a detectable relative effect size of 1.096, assuming a two-sided alpha of 0.05.17

Statistical Methods

Continuous sociodemographic, individual social determinants of health, clinical level characteristics, and community-level predictors were assessed descriptively as mean and standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, overall, and stratified by DR severity status and visual acuity status. Unadjusted models were first examined, and predictors which were found to be significantly associated with DR severity at initial presentation (P-value less than 0.05) were then included in a multivariable modified Poisson regression model with robust standard errors. Predictors found to be significantly associated with visual acuity at initial presentation (P-value less than 0.05) were included in a multivariable linear regression model. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the impact of design choices on outcomes, including using pinhole vision instead of presenting, examining those that were excluded for having the same DR diagnosis and clinic visit date, and examining type 1 and type 2 diabetes separately. Analyses were conducted using R software (Version 2024.12.1+563).

Data and Resource Availability

The datasets generated during and analyzed during the present study are not publicly available due to preserving patient confidentiality but are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

The final analysis included 2044 patients diagnosed with DR. Among these, 1851 had complete visual acuity data (Figure 1). Two hundred eighty (13.7%) patients had proliferative DR at initial presentation to ZSFG and 449 (24.3%) had some level of visual impairment or blindness (logMAR ≥ 0.30). Supplementary Table 3 describes the sociodemographic, social determinants, and clinical characteristics of eligible patients, overall and by DR severity and Supplementary Table 4 describes the same characteristics by visual acuity mean logMAR value. Overall, the mean age of the included population was 55.8 years (SD 11.6) and 57.1% were male. For insurance coverage, 850 (41.6%) had Medicaid insurance, 622 (30.4%) had Medicare, 375 (18.4%) were uninsured, and 196 (9.6%) had employer-based insurance. 20.8% indicated that they faced housing or economic circumstances difficulties. This population faced high levels of comorbidities relating to diabetes management, with 78.2% and 67.7% experiencing hypertension and hyperlipidemia respectively. In univariable models, factors associated with a greater risk of initial presentation with severe DR included male sex, Medicaid or uninsurance, experiencing housing or economic insecurity, former smoking or alcohol use, and identifying as non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, multiple-race and ethnicity, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, or other race and ethnicity (Supplementary Table 3). Speaking Cantonese, having hyperlipidemia, and younger age at initial presentation were protective in a patient’s risk of being diagnosed with severe DR. In the adjusted multivariable model (Figure 2), age, housing problems, former smoking, unknown alcohol use, other race and ethnicity, and hyperlipidemia status remained significant predictors of a patient’s risk of severe DR. Patients experiencing housing or economic insecurity experienced a 35% increased risk of presenting with proliferative DR than those without housing or economic insecurity (aRR 1.35, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.71, P=0.01). For every 10 years of increasing age, the risk of presenting with proliferative DR decreased by 26% (aRR 0.74, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.82, P<0.0001). Patients who indicated that they were former smokers experienced a 28% higher risk of presenting with proliferative DR than those who never smoked (aRR 1.28, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.64, P=0.05). Patients who were other race and ethnicity (including American Indian or Alaska Native and not specified) experienced a 94% higher risk of presenting with proliferative DR than non-Hispanic Asian patients (aRR 1.94, 95% CI 1.06 to 3.54, P=0.03). In univariable models, worse vision at initial presentation was found among older patients, (Mean difference (MD) 0.02 logMAR, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.03, P<0.0001), patients speaking Cantonese (MD 0.04 logMAR, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.08, P=0.05), those who had Medicare insurance (MD 0.06 logMAR, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.11, P=0.01), and those with hypertension (MD = 0.05 logMAR, 95% CI=0.02 to 0.08, P=0.002; Supplementary Table 4). In an adjusted multivariable model (Figure 3), age, language, insurance status, and hypertension status remained significant predictors of visual acuity at initial presentation. For every 10 years of age mean visual acuity increased by 0.02 logMAR (approximately one letter worse), (aMD 0.02 logMAR, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.03, P<0.0001). Patients who spoke Cantonese also had worse visual acuity compared with patients speaking English (aMD 0.04 logMAR, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.09, P=0.05). Patients with Medicare and Medicaid insurance had worse visual acuity than patients with other employer-based insurance types (Medicare aMD 0.05 logMAR, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.10, P=0.03; Medicaid aMD 0.06 logMAR, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.10, P=0.01). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the impact of design choices on outcomes. Overall, we observed similar effect sizes to the main analyses (Supplementary Tables 5–7), with some differences, likely due to smaller sample sizes. When assessing the relationship between predictors and pinhole visual acuity, age and Medicaid insurance were no longer significant predictors (Supplementary Table 5). When including only patients with the same DR diagnosis and clinic visit dates, age and hypertension were not significant predictors of presenting visual acuity (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). Of 2044 patients with a classifiable ICD-10 code, 1924 (94.2%) had type 2 diabetes and 36 (1.8%) had type 1 diabetes. Restricting the fully adjusted model to the type 2 subgroup did not change the association between age and proliferative retinopathy (risk ratio [RR] per 10-year increase in age, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.66–0.84), and adding diabetes type as a covariate to the full model (n=1960 with an unambiguous type 1 or type 2 code) showed a similar age association (RR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.68–0.87) along with an independent association between type 1 diabetes and higher risk of proliferative retinopathy (RR, 1.86; 95% CI, 1.07–3.26; P=0.03).

Discussion

This analysis of electronic health record data aimed to identify predictors of severity of DR and vision loss at initial presentation in a population of 2044 DR patients from a safety net hospital. Overall, this population was found to experience a large burden of severe of DR and visual impairment. The prevalence of severe DR among in this study was close to 14%, which is higher than the 5% seen nationwide among those with diabetes in 2021.2 Similarly, 24% had visual impairment or blindness, which is higher than the overall US diabetes population self-reported visual impairment prevalence of 20.7%.18 Nearly 42% of patients had Medicaid insurance coverage and 18% were uninsured in our study, compared to 21% and 8% in the overall US population in 2024, respectively.19,20 The present analysis found housing insecurity, age, race, smoking status, alcohol use, and hyperlipidemia to be independently associated with severity of DR at initial presentation and age, language, insurance status, and hypertension to be independently associated with worse visual acuity, suggesting these populations might benefit from earlier access to care.

Housing conditions have previously been found to be associated with adherence to diabetic eye examinations,6 with diabetic patients experiencing poor housing conditions (overcrowding, inadequate heating) less likely to have had the recommended eye examination.6 Poor housing conditions are linked to poverty, and studies similarly found that lower income is associated with higher rates of visual impairment.14 Those struggling with housing insecurity may face additional barriers in attending doctors’ appointments and preventative care to manage their diabetes and complete DR screening, leading to delayed diagnosis.21 Similarly, housing insecurity may include periods of homelessness, which present additional barriers in accessing healthcare services, including transportation.22

We also found that patients with public insurance coverage (Medicaid and Medicare) experienced worse vision at their initial DR diagnosis compared to those with employer-based insurance. Eligibility for Medicaid is primarily based on low income, which is known to be associated with lower access to healthcare, including ophthalmic care.23 Previous studies have found that patients with public insurance types experience worse vision. Malhotra 2021 found that among a large, EHR-based population, privately insured patients presented with a better baseline visual acuity than Medicaid patients when initiating treatment for diabetic macular edema.12 In another study, private, employer-based insurance was protective against visual impairment compared with Medicare.14 Our findings are similar in that we found Medicare patients experienced worse visual acuity (independent of age) compared to other employer-based types, which may suggest that these groups are facing barriers to obtaining medical care to identify vision changes early.

The study also assessed relationships between race and ethnicity and language with severity of DR and visual acuity at presentation. Cantonese-speaking patients were found to have lower risk of proliferative DR, but higher risk of worse visual acuity outcomes compared to English-speaking patients, which may reflect different distributions of ocular disease. Overall, we did not find race and ethnicity to be a significant predictor of initial presentation with proliferative DR or visual acuity. Previous studies have found non-White race to be associated with a higher prevalence of low vision,14 and that Hispanic and Black individuals with DR face greater delays and barriers to receiving healthcare and are more likely to progress to severe diabetic retinopathy compared to non-Hispanic White individuals.9,24 Race is a complex social construct and is often a proxy for structural and social factors, including economic disadvantage. Our analysis included assessment of structural determinants of health such as housing insecurity, as well as insurance status, which is related to income.

Housing insecurity and Medicaid insurance status were significantly associated with initial proliferative DR diagnosis and visual acuity and may better measure the negative health impacts of such structural determinants than EHR- reported race and ethnicity in this dataset and context. Despite this, we did see that those with other race and ethnicity and that Cantonese speakers experienced worse vision at their initial DR diagnosis compared to non-Hispanic Asian individuals and English speakers, respectively which may indicate groups facing additional barriers outside of those we measured.

Two inverse associations in our data may appear counterintuitive. Younger age at diagnosis was associated with greater risk of proliferative DR, despite risk generally increasing with longer disease duration. A similar pattern has been reported in type 1 and type 2 diabetes and attributed to longer effective disease duration and delayed diagnosis among younger patients.25,26 The association persisted within the type 2 subgroup and after adjustment for diabetes type. As we used the first electronic health record diagnosis date as a proxy for onset, we cannot exclude a longer undiagnosed interval among younger patients in this safety-net population, which would inflate their observed risk at presentation. A hyperlipidemia diagnosis was also associated with lower risk, which we do not interpret as a protective effect of dyslipidemia. The diagnosis more likely indicates treatment with lipid-lowering therapy, including fenofibrate, which slows retinopathy progression independent of lipid lowering,27,28 or more frequent clinical contact that leads to earlier detection. Both would produce an apparent inverse association through confounding by treatment and health-care access, which our data do not capture, rather than a causal effect.

This study has several limitations. The study population was limited to patients seen at the ZSFG hospital in San Francisco, California. The findings thus reflect experiences of an urban, underserved population at a safety net hospital and as such, they may not be generalizable to the broader US population with diabetes. Several limitations are related to the use of electronic health record data. Outcome misclassification was possible. We aimed to analyze predictors of severity of DR at initial presentation, but it is possible some of the included patients had previously been diagnosed with DR. We attempted to limit the population to incident DR by ensuring each included case had a prior diabetes diagnosis and excluding those with initial diabetes diagnoses at the same time, but it is possible that initial diagnoses happened outside of the ZSFG system. In addition, for 88 patients ICD codes could not be accurately categorized by DR severity, and after performing chart review, it became evident that the majority of these codes (n=86) served as placeholders in the medical chart rather than reflecting a clinical diagnosis of DR. Exposure misclassification was also possible in this study. To reduce this risk, we excluded patients whose SDoH data were collected more than five years before their DR diagnosis, although while the SDoH considered here are generally stable there might still have been changes within that timeframe. Certain variables like race and ethnicity may also have been assigned in the EHR without asking the patient. In general, we were limited to variables that were available without missingness in the EHR, which does not fully capture the SDoH and factors which may be influencing patient’s DR severity and visual acuity at their primary diagnosis.

This study utilized electronic health records from a safety net hospital serving low-income patients to investigate predictors of the severity of DR and visual acuity at the time of initial DR diagnosis. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate disparities in diabetic retinopathy severity at initial diagnosis, including a higher risk of initial severity among individuals experiencing housing insecurity, as well as age and insurance status being risk factors for having worse vision. Each of the risk factors identified may warrant a different type of intervention. Future research should be conducted to further elucidate the barriers contributing to seeking preventative care and screening to inform the development of targeted interventions aimed to reduce negative outcomes from delayed presentation and diagnosis.
