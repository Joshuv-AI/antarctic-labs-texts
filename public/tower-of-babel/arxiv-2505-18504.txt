Higher Order Bell Symmetric Functions
Milo Bechtloff Weising

Abstract
. We study symmetric function analogues of the higher order Bell numbers. Their construction involves iterated plethystic exponential towers mimicking the single variable exponential generating functions for the higher order Bell numbers. We derive explicit recurrence relations for the expansion coefficients of the Bell functions into the monomial and power sum bases of the ring of symmetric functions. Using the machinery of combinatorial species, the Bell functions are proven to be the Frobenius characteristics of the permutation representations of symmetric groups on hyper-partitions of certain orders and sizes. In the order 1 case, we are able to give more details about the expansion coefficients of the Bell functions in terms of vector partitions and divisor sums as well as give a recurrence relation analogous to the well known recursion for the Bell numbers. Lastly, we use Littlewood’s reciprocity theorem and the Hardy-Littlewood Tauberian theorem to prove that the Schur expansion coefficients of the order 1 Bell functions are certain asymptotic averages of restriction coefficients.



1.
Introduction

The operation of plethysm plays an important role
in the study of symmetric functions. Often, plethystic compositions are used to algebraically encode complicated combinatorial or representation-theoretic symmetric functions in a simpler way. Despite the ubiquity of plethysms in algebraic combinatorics, we do not yet understand plethsytic substitutions combinatorially. One of the longest standing open problems in combinatorics may be phrased in this manner. The
plethysm problem
, which remains largely open, asks for a combinatorial interpretation for the Schur expansion coefficients of the Schur function plethysms
s_{\lambda}(s_{\mu}(X)).
Other problems like the
restriction problem
have a similar interpretation.

Schur positive
symmetric functions are central to the study of symmetric functions. This is, in part, due to their representation theoretic importance as combinatorial analogues for characters of representations of symmetric groups. All of the main operations of symmetric functions (addition, multiplication, Kronecker product, plethysm, etc.) preserve the set of Schur positive symmetric functions. As such, it is easy to define families of symmetric functions using these operations which are manifestly Schur positive, but hard to understand combinatorially. In this paper, we study one such family of symmetric functions. In particular, we study the
plethystic exponential towers

\Omega(X),\Omega(\Omega(X)-1),\Omega(\Omega(\Omega(X)-1)-1),\ldots.

These are generalizations of the single-variable exponential towers
\exp(t),\exp(\exp(t)-1),\ldots.
When interpreted as exponential generating functions, the latter power series encode the
higher order Bell numbers

b_{n}^{(m)}
. As such, the homogeneous components
B_{n}^{(m)}(X)
of the plethystic exponential towers are symmetric function analogues of the higher order Bell numbers.

This paper is structured as follows. First, we review the basics of symmetric functions and some other combinatorial definitions. Next, we define the higher Bell functions
B_{n}^{(m)}
and prove some immediate results relating the
B_{n}^{(m)}
to the Bell numbers
b_{n}^{(m)}
and showing their Schur positivity. Then we use the machinery of combinatorial species to prove that the
B_{n}^{(m)}
are the Frobenius characteristics arising from the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
action on the order
m

hyper-partitions
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}.
This explains both the connection with the Bell numbers
b_{n}^{(m)}
and the Schur positivity. Afterwards, we algebraically determine recursive formulae for the monomial and power sum expansion coefficients of the Bell functions. We then focus entirely on the
m=1
case of the Bell functions
B_{n}^{(1)}
, giving more detail on their structure, and proving a recursive formula relating
B^{(1)}_{n+1}
to
B^{(1)}_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq n
. In the last section, we show that the Schur expansion coefficients of the functions
B_{n}^{(1)}
are asymptotic averages of certain
restriction coefficients
.

1.1.
Symmetric functions

Here we briefly introduce some notation and results related to symmetric functions which we will need later in the paper. We refer the reader to Macdonald’s book
[
Mac15
]
for a more complete introduction to symmetric functions.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Lambda
denote the graded completion of the
ring of symmetric functions
over
\mathbb{Q}.
As an abuse of terminology, we will call elements of
\Lambda
symmetric functions. Elements of
\Lambda
have the form
\sum_{n\geq 0}F_{n}(X)
where
F_{n}(X)
is a homogeneous symmetric function of degree
n
. We will write
\Lambda_{0}
for all
\sum_{n\geq 0}F_{n}(X)\in\Lambda
with
F_{0}=0
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
for the subalgebra of all symmetric functions
\sum_{n\geq 0}F_{n}(X)
where
F_{n}(X)=0
for all but finitely many
n.
Define
m_{\lambda},h_{\lambda},p_{\lambda},s_{\lambda}
respectively for the
monomial, complete homogeneous, power sum, and Schur functions
indexed by partitions
\lambda\in\Par.
A symmetric function
F\in\Lambda
is
Schur positive
if it has non-negative integer coefficients when expanded in the Schur basis
s_{\lambda}.

Symmetric functions appear naturally in the study of representations of the
symmetric groups

\mathfrak{S}_{n}
over
\mathbb{C}.

Definition 1.2
.

For
n\geq 1
let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the group of bijections of the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}.
Given a finite dimensional complex representation
V
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
define the
Frobenius characteristic
of
V
as

\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(V)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}_{V}(\sigma)p_{\lambda(\sigma)}(X)\in\Lambda

where
\lambda(\sigma)
is the cycle-type partition of
\sigma.

Definition 1.3
.

The
Hall inner product

\langle-,-\rangle:\Lambda^{\prime}\times\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
is the pairing defined by

\left\langle\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}p_{\lambda},\sum_{\mu}b_{\mu}p_{\mu}\right\rangle:=\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}b_{\lambda}z_{\lambda}

where

z_{\lambda}:=\prod_{j\geq 1}\left(m_{j}(\lambda)!j^{m_{j}(\lambda)}\right)

and
m_{j}(\lambda)
denotes the number of
j
’s appearing in the partition
\lambda.

Throughout this paper, we will use
plethystic substitution
of symmetric functions. For the purposes of this paper, we will define plethystic substitution as follows:

Definition 1.4
.

Let
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots
be a set of commuting free variables. For a formal series
A\in\mathbb{Q}[[t_{1},t_{2},\ldots]]
, say
A=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}t^{\alpha}
and
n\geq 1
define

p_{n}(A)=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}t^{n\alpha}.

Now we define the plethystic substitution
F\mapsto F(A)
as the (non-unital)
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra homomorphism
\Lambda_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}[[t_{1},t_{2},\ldots]]
uniquely determined by
p_{n}\mapsto p_{n}(A).
For
A(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[[t_{1},t_{2},\ldots]]
with
A(0)=0
we may also define the substitution
F\mapsto F(A)
as the unique algebra homomorphism extending the plethystic substitution
\Lambda_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}[[t_{1},t_{2},\ldots]]
by mapping
1\mapsto 1.
In particular, this defines plethsytic substitution for symmetric functions as a map
\Lambda\times\Lambda_{0}\rightarrow\Lambda.
We may also define substitutions of the form
\Lambda^{\prime}\times\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda
using the identity
p_{n}(1+A)=1+p_{n}(A)
and extending accordingly.

Remark 1
.

We will be careful throughout this paper to avoid any plethystic substitutions of the form
F(G)
where
F\notin\Lambda^{\prime}
and
G\notin\Lambda_{0}.
We will leave such substitutions undefined.

Definition 1.5
.

Define the
plethystic exponential
as

\Omega(X):=\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}(X).

It will be useful to define

\Omega_{0}(X):=\Omega(X)-1=\sum_{n\geq 1}h_{n}(X).

We will be interested in repeated plethystic substitutions of the plethystic exponential
\Omega_{0}(X)
.

Definition 1.6
.

We define the symmetric functions
\Omega^{(m)}_{0}(X)\in\Lambda_{0}
inductively by

\Omega_{0}^{(0)}(X):=h_{1}(X)

and for
m\geq 0

\Omega_{0}^{(m+1)}(X):=\Omega_{0}\left(\Omega_{0}^{(m)}(X)\right).

Importantly, note that
\Omega^{(m)}(X)
is indeed well-defined as a symmetric function. If instead we had considered plethystic substitutions of the form
\Omega(\Omega(X))
, we would not obtain a well-defined symmetric function since
\Omega(1)
itself is not well-defined. The following are well-known results regarding the plethystic exponential.

Lemma 1.7
.

The plethystic exponential satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\Omega(X)=\prod_{i\geq 1}(1-x_{i})^{-1}=\exp\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{p_{n}(X)}{n}\right)

(2)

\Omega(X+Y)=\Omega(X)\Omega(Y)

(3)

\Omega(XY)=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{p_{\lambda}(X)p_{\lambda}(Y)}{z_{\lambda}}=\sum_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(X)s_{\lambda}(Y).

We will need the following specialization map.

Definition 1.8
.

Define the homomorphism
\psi:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}[[p_{1}]]
by
\psi(p_{m}):=0
if
m>1
and
\psi(p_{1}):=p_{1}.

The specialization
\psi
satisfies the following properties:

Lemma 1.9
.

\psi(\Omega(X))=\exp(p_{1})

Proof.

This follows from the identity

\Omega(X)=\exp\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{p_{n}}{n}\right).

∎

Lemma 1.10
.

For
F\in\Lambda
and
G\in\Lambda_{0}
,

\psi(F(G(X)))=\psi(F)(\psi(G)(p_{1})).

Proof.

This follows from the identity
p_{n}(p_{m}(X))=p_{nm}(X)
for all
n,m\geq 1.

∎

1.2.
Bell numbers

Definition 1.11
.

[
Bel38
]

For
m\geq 0
define the power series
E^{(m)}(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[[t]]
by
E^{(0)}(t)=\exp(t):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}
and
E^{(m+1)}(t):=E^{(m)}(\exp(t)-1)
for
m\geq 0.
For
n,m\geq 0
the
higher order Bell numbers

b_{n}^{(m)}
are defined by

E^{(m)}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{b_{n}^{(m)}}{n!}t^{n}.

For
m=1
the
b_{n}^{(1)}
are the usual Bell numbers counting the number of set partitions of
\{1,\ldots,n\}.
It is known that the higher order Bell numbers
b_{n}^{(m)}
count the number of order m hyper-partitions of
\{1,\ldots,n\}

[
SK19
]
.

2.
Higher order Bell functions

2.1.
Construction

In this section, we will construct symmetric function analogues of the higher order Bell numbers and discuss some of their properties.

Definition 2.1
.

For
m\geq 0
define the
higher order Bell symmetric functions

B_{n}^{(m)}(X)\in\Lambda
by

\Omega\left(\Omega_{0}^{(m)}(tX)\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}B_{n}^{(m)}(X)t^{n}.

In essence, we are simply replacing each series
\exp(t)
along with the usual power series composition with the plethystic exponential
\Omega(X)
and plethystic substitution. The
m=1
case of the higher order Bell symmetric functions have occurred previously in the literature, for example in Gessel’s paper
[
Ges90
]
, but to the author’s knowledge have not been studied directly in their own right as symmetric functions.

Remark 2
.

The
0
-th order Bell functions are the complete homogeneous symmetric functions
B^{(0)}_{n}=h_{n}
and the corresponding Bell numbers are simply
b_{n}^{(0)}=1.
Note that for all
m\geq 0,

B_{0}^{(m)}=1.

We may directly relate the higher order Bell symmetric functions to the higher order Bell numbers as follows:

Proposition 2.2
.

For all
n,m\geq 0
,

b_{n}^{(m)}=\langle B_{n}^{(m)},p_{1^{n}}\rangle.

Proof.

Using Lemmas
1.9
and
1.10
inductively, we see that

\Psi\left(\Omega\left(\Omega_{0}^{(m)}(X)\right)\right)=E^{(m)}(p_{1})=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{b_{n}^{(m)}}{n!}p_{1}^{n}
so that

\Psi(B_{n}^{(m)})=\frac{b_{n}^{(m)}}{n!}p_{1}^{n}.
Note that for
F
homogeneous of degree
n
,
\langle F,p_{1}^{n}\rangle=\langle\Psi(F),p_{1}^{n}\rangle.
Therefore,

\langle B_{n}^{(m)},p_{1^{n}}\rangle=\langle\Psi(B_{n}^{(m)}),p_{1^{n}}\rangle=\langle\frac{b_{n}^{(m)}}{n!}p_{1^{n}},p_{1^{n}}\rangle=b_{n}^{(m)}\frac{\langle p_{1^{n}},p_{1^{n}}\rangle}{n!}=b_{n}^{(m)}.

∎

2.2.
Schur positivity

Here we will show that the Bell functions
B_{n}^{(m)}
are Schur positive. We will first do so algebraically and then later we will show that the
B_{n}^{(m)}
are Frobenius characteristics of certain
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
representations. We first need the following:

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
n,m\geq 0,

B_{n}^{(m+1)}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\prod_{j\geq 1}h_{m_{j}(\lambda)}(B_{n}^{(m)}).

Proof.

We proceed by direct computation:

\displaystyle\Omega\left(\Omega^{(m+1)}_{0}(tX)\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega(\Omega_{0}(\Omega_{0}^{(m)}(tX)))

\displaystyle=\Omega((\Omega(\Omega_{0}^{(m)}(tX))-1))

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}B_{n}^{(m)}(tX)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n\geq 1}\Omega\left(B_{n}^{(m)}(tX)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}h_{k}\left(B_{n}^{(m)}(tX)\right)\right).

Lastly, we expand this product into a series

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},\ldots\\
k_{n}=0~a.e.\end{subarray}}\prod_{n\geq 1}h_{k_{n}}\left(B_{n}^{(m)}(tX)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2},\ldots\\
k_{n}=0~a.e.\end{subarray}}t^{\sum_{n\geq 1}nk_{n}}\prod_{n\geq 1}h_{k_{n}}\left(B_{n}^{(m)}(X)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}t^{|\lambda|}\prod_{n\geq 1}h_{m_{n}(\lambda)}\left(B_{n}^{(m)}(X)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}t^{n}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\prod_{j\geq 1}h_{m_{j}(\lambda)}\left(B_{j}^{(m)}(X)\right).

∎

The Bell functions
B_{n}^{(m)}
are manifestly Schur positive.

Proposition 2.4
.

For all
n,m\geq 0,
the higher order Bell function
B_{n}^{(m)}
is Schur positive.

Proof.

The proof follows by induction on the order
m\geq 0.
For
m=0,

B_{n}^{(0)}=h_{n}=s_{(n)}
is clearly Schur positive. Now assume for some
m\geq 0
that every
B_{n}^{(m)}
is Schur positive. Using Lemma
2.3
, we see that for all
n\geq 0

B_{n}^{(m+1)}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\prod_{j\geq 1}h_{m_{j}(\lambda)}(B_{j}^{(m)}).

By assumption, each
B_{n}^{(m)}
is Schur positive so that the plethysms
h_{m_{j}(\lambda)}(B_{n}^{(m)})
are also Schur positive and thus the products
\prod_{j\geq 1}h_{m_{j}(\lambda)}(B_{j}^{(m)})
are Schur positive. Thus each
B_{n}^{(m+1)}
is Schur positive as required.
∎

Remark 3
.

The Schur expansion coefficients of the Bell functions appear to satisfy monotonicity with respect to the
dominance ordering
. It would be interesting to see if this in fact holds in general. Furthermore, it is an interesting problem to find combinatorial formulas for these coefficients. From Proposition
2.4
we see that if we knew combinatorial formulas for plethysm coefficients
s_{\lambda}(s_{\mu})
(which we do not), then we could recursively determine the Schur expansions of the
B_{n}^{(m)}
albeit in a roundabout manner.

2.3.
Combinatorial species and hyper-partitions

Using the machinery of
combinatorial species
, we may identify specific representations whose Frobenius characteristics are the higher order Bell functions. Here we give an abridged introduction to combinatorial species. We refer the reader to Bergeron–Labelle–Leroux’s book
[
BLL98
]
.

Definition 2.5
.

Define
\mathbb{B}
to be the category whose objects are finite sets and whose morphisms are bijective functions. A
species
is a functor
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}.
For all
n\geq 0
we use the shorthand
\mathcal{F}[n]:=\mathcal{F}(\{1,\ldots,n\}).
Define the species of sets
\mathcal{S}
as
\mathcal{S}(U):=\{U\}
and for a bijection
f:U\rightarrow V,

\mathcal{S}(f)(\{U\}):=\{V\}.
Define the species
\mathcal{S}_{+}
of non-empty sets to agree with
\mathcal{S}
for all finite non-empty sets and
\mathcal{S}_{+}(\emptyset):=\emptyset.
Define the species of set partitions
\mathcal{P}
by

\mathcal{P}(U):=\{\pi=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}\}~|~W_{i}\neq\emptyset,~\sqcup_{W\in\pi}W=U\}
and for a bijection
f:U\rightarrow V
define
\mathcal{P}(f)(\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}\}):=\{f(W_{1}),\ldots,f(W_{k})\}.

The main operation on species which we will be interested in is composition.

Definition 2.6
.

If
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
are species, then we define the composition species
\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{G}
by

(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{G})(U):=\bigcup_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(U)}\left(\mathcal{F}(\pi)\times\prod_{W\in\pi}\mathcal{G}(W)\right)

and for a bijection
f:U\rightarrow V
define
(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{G})(f):(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{G})(U)\rightarrow(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{G})(V)
using
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{P}(f))
and
\mathcal{G}(f|_{W})
accordingly.

As an example, it is instructive to check that
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{S}\circ\mathcal{S}_{+}.
Every species defines a symmetric function in the following way:

Definition 2.7
.

For a species
\mathcal{F}
define the
cycle index series

Z_{\mathcal{F}}\in\Lambda
as

Z_{\mathcal{F}}:=\sum_{\lambda\in\Par}\frac{\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}[n],\lambda)}{z_{\lambda}}p_{\lambda}(X)

where
\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{F}[n],\lambda)
is the number of fixed points in
\mathcal{F}[n]
of any permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with cycle type
\lambda.

For all
n\geq 1
the homogeneous degree
n
component of
Z_{\mathcal{F}}
is the Frobenius characteristic of the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
representation determined by the group action of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
on
\mathcal{F}[n].
As an example, we have that
Z_{\mathcal{S}}=\Omega(X)
and
Z_{\mathcal{S}_{+}}=\Omega_{0}(X).

Definition 2.8
.

For
m\geq 0
define the species of order
m
hyper-partitions
\mathcal{P}^{(m)}
by
\mathcal{P}^{(0)}:=\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{P}^{(m+1)}=\mathcal{P}^{(m)}\circ\mathcal{S}_{+}
for all
m\geq 0.
We will write
\pi^{(m)}_{n}:=\mathcal{P}^{(m)}[n]
for the set of order
m

hyper-partitions
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}.

Lemma 2.9
.

[
BLL98
]

If
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are combinatorial species with
\mathcal{G}(\emptyset)=\emptyset,
then

Z_{\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{G}}=Z_{\mathcal{F}}(Z_{\mathcal{G}}).

The main result in this section is the following:

Theorem 2.10
.

For all
n,m\geq 0
,
B_{n}^{(m)}(X)
is the Frobenius characteristic of the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
action on the set
\pi_{n}^{(m)}
of order
m
hyper-partitions of
\{1,\ldots,n\}.

Proof.

By induction using Lemma
2.9
, we see that
\Omega(\Omega^{(m)}_{0}(X))
is the cycle index series for the species
\mathcal{P}^{(m)}
. The result follows.
∎

2.4.
Monomial expansion

We will provide a recursive formula for the coefficients in the monomial expansions of the Bell functions.

Definition 2.11
.

Define
Q
as the subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\infty}
consisting of finite support sequences. Let
Q^{\prime}:=Q\setminus\{0\}.
We consider
\mathbb{Y}\subset Q
as the set of weakly decreasing vectors. Define
\rho^{(m)}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{Q}
by

B^{(m)}_{n}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\rho^{(m)}(\lambda)m_{\lambda}.
Extend
\rho^{(m)}
to
\alpha\in Q
by setting
\rho^{(m)}(\alpha):=\rho^{(m)}(\lambda)
where
\lambda
is the weakly decreasing rearrangement of
\alpha.

Note that since
B^{(0)}_{n}=h_{n}
for all
n\geq 0,
we know that
\rho^{(0)}(\lambda)=1
for all
\lambda\in\Par.
The numbers
\{\rho^{(m)}(\lambda)\}_{m\geq 0,\lambda\in\Par}
satisfy the following recursion:

Proposition 2.12
.

For all
m\geq 0
and
\lambda\in\Par\setminus\{\emptyset\},

\rho^{(m+1)}(\lambda)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:Q^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\sum_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\phi(\alpha)\alpha=\lambda\end{subarray}}\prod_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\binom{\phi(\alpha)+\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)-1}{\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)-1}.

Proof.

By direct computation,

\Omega\left(\Omega^{(m+1)}_{0}(X)\right)=\Omega\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}B_{n}^{(m)}(X)\right)=\Omega\left(\sum_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)x^{\alpha}\right)=\prod_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\left(1-x^{\alpha}\right)^{-\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)}.

Now we use the standard power series identity

(1-t)^{-N}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\binom{n+N-1}{N-1}t^{n}
to see that

\displaystyle\prod_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\left(1-x^{\alpha}\right)^{-\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}\binom{n+\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)-1}{\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)-1}x^{n\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:Q^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\phi(\alpha)=0~\text{a.e.}\end{subarray}}x^{\sum_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\phi(\alpha)\alpha}\prod_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\binom{\phi(\alpha)+\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)-1}{\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)-1}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{\beta\in Q^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:Q^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\sum_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\phi(\alpha)\alpha=\beta\end{subarray}}\prod_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\binom{\phi(\alpha)+\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)-1}{\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)-1}\right)x^{\beta}

\displaystyle=m_{\emptyset}(X)+\sum_{\lambda\in\Par\setminus\{\emptyset\}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:Q^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\sum_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\phi(\alpha)\alpha=\lambda\end{subarray}}\prod_{\alpha\in Q^{\prime}}\binom{\phi(\alpha)+\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)-1}{\rho^{(m)}(\alpha)-1}\right)m_{\lambda}(X).

∎

We may interpret the values
\rho^{(m)}(\lambda)
in terms of hyper-partitions using the following result.

Theorem 2.13
.

Suppose
M
is a finite set on which
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts by permutations. Then the Frobenius characteristic
\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\mathbb{C}[M])
has the monomial expansion

\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\mathbb{C}[M])=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}|M/\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}|m_{\lambda}(X)

where
M/\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}
denotes the set of orbits in
M
of the Young subgroup
\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}=\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda_{\ell}}.

Proof.

This follows from
Frobenius reciprocity
between
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and the subgroups
\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}.
We refer the reader to Dotsenko’s article
[
Dot08
]
.
∎

As an immediate consequence, we find that the numbers
\rho^{(m)}(\lambda)
have the combinatorial interpretation

\rho^{(m)}(\lambda)=|\pi_{|\lambda|}^{(m)}/\mathfrak{S}_{\lambda}|.

This implies that the recursion Proposition
2.12
should be combinatorially meaningful. It would be interesting to give a fully combinatorial description of this formula.

2.5.
Power sum expansion

Here we will find a recursive formula for the power sum expansion coefficients of the Bell functions
B_{n}^{(m)}.
It will be convenient to introduce the following notation.

Definition 2.14
.

For
\lambda,\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(n)}\in\Par
and
\lambda=\mu^{(1)}\cup\cdots\cup\mu^{(n)}
define

\binom{\lambda}{\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(n)}}:=\prod_{j\geq 1}\frac{m_{j}(\lambda)!}{m_{j}(\mu^{(1)})!\cdots m_{j}(\mu^{(n)})!}.
Define
\beta^{(m)}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{Q}
as

B_{n}^{(m)}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{\beta^{(m)}(\lambda)}{z_{\lambda}}p_{\lambda}.

Note that by Theorem
2.10
we know that for all
\lambda\neq\emptyset,

\beta^{(m)}(\lambda)=\mathrm{fix}(\pi_{|\lambda|}^{(m)},\lambda)
, i.e., the number of order
m
hyper-partitions of
\{1,\ldots,|\lambda|\}
fixed by any permutation with cycle type
\lambda.
In particular,
\beta^{(m)}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.
We will now prove the following recursive formula:

Proposition 2.15
.

For all
m\geq 0
and
\lambda\in\Par\setminus\{\emptyset\},

\beta^{(m+1)}(\lambda)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu^{(1)}\cup\cdots\cup\mu^{(n)}=\lambda\\
\mu^{(i)}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\binom{\lambda}{\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(n)}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mu^{(i)})}d^{\ell(\mu^{(i)})-1}\beta^{(m)}(\mu^{(i)}/d)\right).

Proof.

We proceed by induction:

\displaystyle\Omega\left(\Omega_{0}^{(m+1)}(X)\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\sum_{\mu\neq\emptyset}\frac{\beta^{(m)}(\mu)}{z_{\mu}}p_{\mu}(X)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}\left(\sum_{\mu\neq\emptyset}\frac{\beta^{(m)}(\mu)}{z_{\mu}}p_{\mu}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{\mu\neq\emptyset}\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mu)}\frac{\beta^{(m)}(\mu/d)}{dz_{\mu/d}}\right)p_{\mu}\right)

It may be checked directly that for all
\mu\in\Par
with
d\mid\gcd(\mu),

z_{\mu/d}=\frac{z_{\mu}}{d^{\ell(\mu)}}.
Thus,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{\mu\neq\emptyset}\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mu)}\frac{\beta^{(m)}(\mu/d)}{dz_{\mu/d}}\right)p_{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{\mu\neq\emptyset}\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mu)}d^{\ell(\mu)-1}\beta^{(m)}(\mu/d)\right)\frac{p_{\mu}}{z_{\mu}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}\left(\sum_{\mu\neq\emptyset}\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mu)}d^{\ell(\mu)-1}\beta^{(m)}(\mu/d)\right)\frac{p_{\mu}}{z_{\mu}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(n)}\neq\emptyset}\frac{p_{\mu^{(1)}\cup\cdots\cup\mu^{(n)}}}{z_{\mu^{(1)}}\cdots z_{\mu^{(n)}}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mu^{(i)})}d^{\ell(\mu^{(i)})-1}\beta^{(m)}(\mu^{(i)}/d)\right)

Lastly, it may be checked that For
\lambda,\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(n)}\in\Par
with
\lambda=\mu^{(1)}\cup\cdots\cup\mu^{(n)}
,

\binom{\lambda}{\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(n)}}=\frac{z_{\lambda}}{z_{\mu^{(1)}}\cdots z_{\mu^{(n)}}}.

Therefore, we arrive at

1+\sum_{\lambda\neq\emptyset}\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu^{(1)}\cup\cdots\cup\mu^{(n)}=\lambda\\
\mu^{(i)}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\binom{\lambda}{\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(n)}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mu^{(i)})}d^{\ell(\mu^{(i)})-1}\beta^{(m)}(\mu^{(i)}/d)\right)\right)\frac{p_{\lambda}}{z_{\lambda}}.

∎

Remark 4
.

Interestingly, since the numbers
\beta^{(m)}(\lambda)
are integers, the denominators
\frac{1}{n!}
in the recursion in Proposition
2.15
must cancel. Clearing the denominators gives

\ell(\lambda)!\beta^{(m+1)}(\lambda)=\sum_{1\leq n\leq\ell(\lambda)}\frac{\ell(\lambda)!}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu^{(1)}\cup\cdots\cup\mu^{(n)}=\lambda\\
\mu^{(i)}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\binom{\lambda}{\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(n)}}\prod_{1\leq i\leq n}\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\mu^{(i)})}d^{\ell(\mu^{(i)})-1}\beta^{(m)}(\mu^{(i)}/d)\right)

where both sides of the equation are non-negative integers. It would be interesting to give a combinatorial interpretation for this recursion.

3.2.
Recurrence relation

The Bell numbers
b_{n}
are well-known to satisfy the recursion

b_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}b_{k}
for all
n\geq 0.
This may be seen by applying the chain rule to the power series composition
\exp(\exp(t)-1).
In this section, we will prove an analogous statement for the Bell functions
B_{n}.
We will need to introduce the following auxiliary symmetric functions.

Definition 3.6
.

For
n\geq 1
define

H_{n}:=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{\sigma_{\ell(\lambda)-1}(\gcd(\lambda))}{z_{\lambda}}p_{\lambda}.

The
H
functions arise in the following expansion:

Lemma 3.7
.

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)=\sum_{n\geq 1}H_{n}t^{n}

Proof.

First, expand the left hand side as

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}\left(\sum_{\lambda\neq\emptyset}t^{|\lambda|}\frac{p_{\lambda}(X)}{z_{\lambda}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
\lambda\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}t^{n|\lambda|}\frac{p_{n\lambda}(X)}{nz_{\lambda}}=\sum_{\lambda\neq\emptyset}\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\lambda)}\frac{1}{dz_{\lambda/d}}\right)t^{|\lambda|}p_{\lambda}(X).

Lastly, using
z_{\lambda/d}=\frac{z_{\lambda}}{d^{\ell(\lambda)}}
we see that
\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\lambda)}\frac{1}{dz_{\lambda/d}}=\frac{\sigma_{\ell(\lambda)-1}(\gcd(\lambda))}{z_{\lambda}}
as required.
∎

The
H
functions have a simple monomial expansion. Recall that
\sigma_{-1}(n):=\sum_{d\mid n}\frac{1}{d}.

Proposition 3.8
.

For all
n\geq 1,

H_{n}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\sigma_{-1}(\gcd(\lambda))m_{\lambda}.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.7
we may expand
\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)
as

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}\left(\sum_{\lambda\neq\emptyset}t^{|\lambda|}m_{\lambda}(X)\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
\lambda\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{t^{n|\lambda|}m_{n\lambda}(X)}{n}.

Rearranging this series gives

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
\lambda\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{t^{n|\lambda|}m_{n\lambda}(X)}{n}=\sum_{\lambda\neq\emptyset}\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(\lambda)}\frac{1}{d}\right)t^{|\lambda|}m_{\lambda}(X)=\sum_{\lambda\neq\emptyset}\sigma_{-1}(\gcd(\lambda))t^{|\lambda|}m_{\lambda}(X).

∎

H_{n}
depends heavily on the divisors of

In particular, for a prime
p

H_{p}=\frac{1}{p}m_{p}+h_{p}.

Proposition 3.9
.

For all
n\geq 0,

B_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n-k+1}{n+1}H_{n-k+1}B_{k}.

Proof.

We begin by applying the chain rule:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Omega\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\exp\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)\right)\frac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}p_{n}\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)\left(\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{n\geq 1}H_{n}(X)t^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}nH_{n}(X)t^{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}B_{n}t^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)H_{n+1}(X)t^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}(n-k+1)H_{n-k+1}(X)B_{k}(X)\right)t^{n}

On the other hand,

\frac{d}{dt}\Omega\left(\Omega_{0}(tX)\right)=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{n\geq 0}B_{n}(X)t^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)B_{n+1}(X)t^{n}

and therefore,

(n+1)B_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(n-k+1)H_{n-k+1}B_{k}.

By dividing by
n+1
we get the result.
∎

3.3.
Relation to restriction coefficients

In this last section, we show that the Schur expansion coefficients of the Bell symmetric functions
B_{n}
are directly related to the
restriction coefficients
.

Definition 3.10
.

For a partition
\lambda
and
n\geq 0
let
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}\mathbb{C}^{n}
denote the
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
module obtained by applying the Schur functor
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}
to the standard representation
\mathbb{C}^{n}
of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. We embed
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\subset\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
as the set of
permutation matrices
. For
|\mu|=n
let
V_{\mu}
denote the irreducible Specht module of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
over
\mathbb{C}
. For
\lambda,\mu\in\Par
with
n=|\mu|
define the restriction coefficient
r_{\lambda}^{\mu}
as

r_{\lambda}^{\mu}:=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Hom_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(V_{\mu},\mathrm{Res}^{\GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}\mathbb{C}^{n}\right).

Define the (single-variable)
restriction series

R_{\lambda}(z)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}[[z]]
by

R_{\lambda}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}r_{\lambda}^{(n)}z^{n}.

Note that
r_{\lambda}^{(n)}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
is the dimension of the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-invariants of
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}\mathbb{C}^{n}.
For
|\mu|=n
let
c_{\mu}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{S}_{n}]
denote Young symmetrizer corresponding to
\mu
. Then for
|\lambda|=k
,
r_{\lambda}^{(n)}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}c_{1^{n}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k}c_{\lambda}
where
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts on the left and
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
on the right of
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k}.
For details regarding the special case of the restriction coefficients of the form
r_{\lambda}^{(n)}
we refer the reader to Narayanan–Paul–Prasad–Srivastava
[
Nar+21
]
. The restriction coefficients
r_{\lambda}^{\mu}
may be understood algebraically using Littlewood’s reciprocity theorem:

Theorem 3.11

(Littlewood
[
Lit58
]
)
.

For
\lambda,\mu\in\Par,

r_{\lambda}^{\mu}=\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\mu}(\Omega(X))\rangle.

Using Littlewood’s reciprocity result, we find a convenient algebraic formula for the restriction series
R_{\lambda}(z):

Lemma 3.12
.

\Omega\left(z\Omega(X)\right)=\sum_{\lambda}R_{\lambda}(z)s_{\lambda}(X)

Proof.

Using Theorem
3.11
we find

\displaystyle\Omega\left(z\Omega(X)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}(z\Omega(X))

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}h_{n}(\Omega(X))

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}\sum_{\lambda}r_{\lambda}^{(n)}s_{\lambda}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}r_{\lambda}^{(n)}\right)s_{\lambda}(X).

∎

We will use the following notation for the Schur expansion coefficients of the Bell functions
B_{n}.

Definition 3.13
.

Define
A_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
via

B_{n}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}A_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}.

In order to relate the Schur coefficients
A_{\lambda}
to the restriction coefficients
r_{\lambda}^{\mu}
, we will need to prove a simple result regarding the restriction series
R_{\lambda}(z).

Proposition 3.14
.

For all
\lambda\in\Par,

(1-z)R_{\lambda}(z)
is a polynomial with degree at most
|\lambda|
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.12
we see that

R_{\lambda}(z)=\langle s_{\lambda}(X),\Omega\left(z\Omega(X)\right)\rangle.

We will use the expansion
s_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu\vdash n}K^{(-1)}_{\lambda,\mu}h_{\mu}
where
K^{(-1)}_{\lambda,\mu}
are the
inverse Kostka numbers
and instead compute
\langle h_{\mu}(X),\Omega\left(z\Omega(X)\right)\rangle
. Following similar to the proof of Proposition
2.12
,

\displaystyle\Omega\left(z\Omega(X)\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\sum_{\alpha\in Q}zx^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\alpha\in Q}\frac{1}{1-zx^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-z}\prod_{\alpha\in Q\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{1-zx^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-z}\sum_{\alpha\in Q}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:Q^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\sum_{\beta\in Q\setminus\{0\}}\phi(\beta)\beta=\alpha\end{subarray}}z^{\sum_{\beta\in Q\setminus\{0\}}\phi(\beta)}\right)x^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-z}\sum_{\mu}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:Q^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\sum_{\beta\in Q\setminus\{0\}}\phi(\beta)\beta=\mu\end{subarray}}z^{\sum_{\beta\in Q\setminus\{0\}}\phi(\beta)}\right)m_{\mu}(X).

Define the series

f_{\mu}(z):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:Q^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\sum_{\beta\in Q\setminus\{0\}}\phi(\beta)\beta=\mu\end{subarray}}z^{\sum_{\beta\in Q\setminus\{0\}}\phi(\beta)}.

Note that
f_{\mu}(z)
is a polynomial with degree
|\mu|.
The leading coefficient of
z^{|\mu|}
is always
1
corresponding to the vector partition
\mu=\mu_{1}(1,0\ldots)+\mu_{2}(0,1,0,\ldots)+\ldots+\mu_{\ell(\mu)}(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots)
. Now we see

\Omega\left(z\Omega(X)\right)=\sum_{\mu}\frac{f_{\mu}(z)}{1-z}m_{\mu}(X).

This means that

\langle h_{\mu}(X),\Omega\left(z\Omega(X)\right)\rangle=\frac{f_{\mu}(z)}{1-z}.

Putting this all together,

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}(z)=\langle s_{\lambda}(X),\Omega\left(z\Omega(X)\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\vdash|\lambda|}K^{(-1)}_{\lambda,\mu}\langle h_{\mu}(X),\Omega\left(z\Omega(X)\right)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\vdash|\lambda|}K^{(-1)}_{\lambda,\mu}\frac{f_{\mu}(z)}{1-z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\mu\vdash|\lambda|}K^{(-1)}_{\lambda,\mu}f_{\mu}(z)}{1-z}.

It is clear that
\sum_{\mu\vdash n}K^{(-1)}_{\lambda,\mu}f_{\mu}(z)
is a polynomial with degree at most
|\lambda|.

∎

The Schur coefficients
A_{\lambda}
are the
z=1
residues of the restriction series
R_{\lambda}(z).

Proposition 3.15
.

A_{\lambda}=\lim_{z\rightarrow 1}(1-z)R_{\lambda}(z)

Proof.

Notice that

\Omega(z\Omega_{0}(tX))=\Omega(z\Omega(tX)-z)=(1-z)\Omega(z\Omega(tX)).

The left hand side limits to

\Omega(\Omega_{0}(tX))=\sum_{n\geq 0}B_{n}(X)t^{n}

whereas the right hand side is given by

\sum_{\lambda}\left(\lim_{z\rightarrow 1}(1-z)R_{\lambda}(z)\right)s_{\lambda}(X)t^{|\lambda|}.

∎

The main result of this section is an application of a weak version of the Hardy–Littlewood
Tauberian theorem
which we now review.

Theorem 3.16

(Hardy–Littlewood)
.

Suppose
a_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\geq 0
and
\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}\sim\frac{1}{1-z}
as
z\uparrow 1.
Then as
n\rightarrow\infty
we have

\sum_{k\leq n}a_{k}\sim n.

We may apply the Tauberian theorem directly.

Theorem 3.17
.

A_{\lambda}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\leq n}r_{\lambda}^{(k)}

Proof.

Suppose first that
A_{\lambda}\neq 0.
We have that

R_{\lambda}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}r_{\lambda}^{(n)}z^{n}
so by Proposition
3.15
we know
R_{\lambda}(z)\sim\frac{A_{\lambda}}{1-z}
as
z\uparrow 1.
Therefore, by Theorem
3.16
we find that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\leq n}r_{\lambda}^{(k)}\sim A_{\lambda}
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
In the case that
A_{\lambda}=0,
the polynomial
(1-z)R_{\lambda}(z)
has
z=1
as a root and hence
R_{\lambda}(z)
must itself be a polynomial. But then,
r_{\lambda}^{(k)}
is only nonzero for finitely many
k
and thus
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\leq n}r_{\lambda}^{(k)}=0=A_{\lambda}
as required.
∎

Notice that
\rho(\mu)=f_{\mu}(1)
so
A_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu\vdash|\lambda|}K_{\lambda,\mu}^{(-1)}\rho(\mu).
This sum has a (signed) combinatorial expansion using the results of Eğecioğlu–-Remmel on the inverse Kostka numbers
[
ER90
]
. This leads to the pleasant formula

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\leq n}r_{\lambda}^{(k)}=\sum_{\mu\vdash|\lambda|}K_{\lambda,\mu}^{(-1)}\rho(\mu).

Remark 5
.

We may generalize the restriction series
R_{\lambda}(z)
to a symmetric function
R_{\lambda}(Z)=R_{\lambda}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots)
by defining

R_{\lambda}(Z):=\langle s_{\lambda}(X),\Omega(Z\Omega(X))\rangle.

The symmetric series
R_{\lambda}
have previously been considered by Lee
[
Lee24
]
. Using Lee’s conventions,
R_{\lambda}(Z)=\mathcal{F}\{s_{\lambda}\}(Z)
where
\mathcal{F}\{-\}
is the
Frobenius transform
. It is straightforward to check that
R_{\lambda}(Z)=\sum_{\mu}r_{\lambda}^{\mu}s_{\mu}(Z)
and furthermore,
\Omega(-Z)R_{\lambda}(Z)=\langle s_{\lambda}(X),\Omega(Z\Omega_{0}(X))\rangle
is an inhomogeneous symmetric function with maximal degree at most
|\lambda|.
In particular,

\Omega(-Z)R_{\lambda}(Z)=\sum_{\mu\vdash|\lambda|}K_{\lambda,\mu}^{(-1)}F_{\mu}(Z)
where

F_{\mu}(Z):=\langle h_{\mu}(X),\Omega(Z\Omega_{0}(X))\rangle.
For all
\mu
,
F_{\mu}(Z)
is explicitly given by

F_{\mu}(Z)=\sum_{\Phi\in Y(\mu)}z^{|\Phi|}

where

Y(\mu)
is the set of all
\Phi:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\times Q\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
\Phi_{i}(\alpha)\neq 0
