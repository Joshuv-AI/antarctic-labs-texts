Comparative Efficacy of Recombinant C1 Inhibitor Versus Sebetralstat for On-Demand Treatment of Hereditary Angioedema Attacks: A Matching-Adjusted Indirect Treatment Comparison
Authors: John Anderson, Nihal Narsipur, Douglas Jones, Andrew Smith, Anurag Relan, Emily Aiello, Neil Roskell, Adam Gough, Sakshi Jindal, Ketsia Habimana, Hannah Kilvert, H Henry Li, Amanda Harrington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPrompt and effective on-demand treatment is critical in hereditary angioedema (HAE). Although multiple on-demand therapies are available, including recombinant human C1 esterase inhibitor (rhC1-INH) and sebetralstat, no head-to-head efficacy trials exist.MethodsA series of indirect treatment comparisons evaluated the relative efficacy of US Food and Drug Administration-approved doses of intravenous rhC1-INH (50 U/kg for patients weighing < 84 kg; 4200 U otherwise) vs oral sebetralstat (600 mg) for the on-demand treatment of HAE attacks. Patient-level data from rhC1-INH trials (C1 1310, C1 1205, and C1 1304 [placebo only]) were reweighted to match aggregate baseline characteristics (i.e., prophylaxis use, pooled attack location) from the sebetralstat KONFIDENT trial; matching variables were identified from a validated, clinician-informed study of treatment effect modifiers. In the primary analysis, time to complete resolution (TTCR) and redosing were compared using unanchored matching-adjusted indirect comparisons. TTCR subgroup analyses were performed for variables not matched for in the primary analysis due to population differences (i.e., attack severity, time to treatment). Scenario and sensitivity analyses were performed to support TTCR results; a scenario analysis was performed to support redosing results. Hazard ratios (HRs) and odds ratios (ORs), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were estimated using a weighted Cox proportional hazards model and a weighted logistic regression model, respectively.ResultsIn the primary analysis, rhC1-INH was associated with a statistically significant 4.5-fold increased likelihood of achieving complete resolution (HR 4.52; 95% CI 2.84–7.18) and a significant 82% reduction in redosing (OR 0.18; 95% CI 0.06–0.53) vs sebetralstat. Similar results for TTCR were observed in subgroup analyses of patients with severe/very severe baseline attacks and patients receiving earlier treatment (within the median time to treatment). Scenario and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings.ConclusionsrhC1-INH provides significantly faster symptom resolution and lower redosing rates vs sebetralstat.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03605-4.Keywords: Hereditary angioedema, Indirect treatment comparison, On-demand therapies, Redosing, rhC1-INH, Sebetralstat, Time to complete resolution

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Because no head-to-head trials have evaluated on-demand therapies for hereditary angioedema (HAE), variability in study designs and outcome definitions across clinical trials limits direct interpretation of relative efficacy, including time to complete resolution (TTCR) and the need for redosing.This indirect treatment comparison evaluated the US Food and Drug Administration-approved doses of recombinant human C1 esterase inhibitor (rhC1-INH) and sebetralstat for outcomes related to TTCR and the need for redosing in HAE acute attacks using a matching-adjusted indirect treatment comparison.What was learned from the study?Complete symptom resolution within 24 h was 4.5 times more likely (hazard ratio 4.52; 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.84–7.18) with rhC1-INH than sebetralstat 600 mg; the odds of redosing were 82% lower (odds ratio 0.18; 95% CI 0.06–0.53) with rhC1-INH compared with sebetralstat.When clinically meaningful differences between trials were appropriately addressed, rhC1-INH demonstrated a significantly shorter time to complete attack resolution and reduced odds of redosing compared with sebetralstat across multiple analytic approaches.These results underscore the importance of robust comparative efficacy assessments of on-demand HAE therapies to inform clinical decision-making and support future evidence-based evaluations.

Introduction

Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare, autosomal dominant disorder characterized by recurrent and unpredictable episodes of localized, nonpruritic swelling that can affect the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and upper airways [1]. Attacks can be debilitating, with potentially fatal outcomes when the oropharyngeal-laryngeal region is affected [1]. Thus, patients with HAE require constant access to effective on-demand therapies [1]. The primary therapeutic goals in managing acute HAE attacks are to rapidly halt symptom progression and to completely resolve the attack, thereby minimizing morbidity, preventing life-threatening complications, and limiting disruption to patients’ lives [1].

Several on-demand therapies have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the on-demand treatment of acute HAE attacks: ecallantide, icatibant, plasma-derived C1 esterase inhibitor, and recombinant human C1 esterase inhibitor (rhC1-INH) [2]. rhC1-INH has been evaluated in several phase 2/3 and phase 3 clinical trials (NCT00225147, NCT00262301, NCT01188564) [3, 4]. A post hoc analysis of these trials, using data aligned with the FDA-approved dose of rhC1-INH (50 U/kg for patients weighing < 84 kg or 4200 U for patients weighing ≥ 84 kg), reported a median time to onset of symptom relief (TOSR) of 45 min (equivalent to 0.75 h; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.55–1.50) and a median time to complete resolution (TTCR) of 4.5 h (95% CI 3.55–5.50) [5, 6]. More recently, sebetralstat, an oral plasma kallikrein inhibitor, received FDA approval based on the phase 3 KONFIDENT trial (NCT05259917), with a median TOSR of 2.0 h (95% CI 1.5–2.8) within 12 h of the first dose (600 mg) and a median TTCR of > 24.0 h; less than 50% of patients receiving sebetralstat 600 mg and placebo had attack resolution within 24 h [7, 8]. However, no head-to-head trials have been conducted comparing these on-demand therapies [9].

In the absence of direct comparative trials, an indirect treatment comparison (ITC) provides a validated approach to estimate relative treatment effects across heterogeneous trials [9–11]. For clinical trials evaluating interventions in the same therapeutic area, an ITC of efficacy and safety can be conducted by summarizing the evidence with a systematic literature review (SLR) and performing a network meta-analysis of results under equivalent conditions [12]. Because head-to-head trials are not required in clinical research for market approval, ITCs of separate clinical trials are an effective way to provide comparative efficacy and safety data and inform clinical decision-making [12]. Following established frameworks and guidelines can mitigate biases and enhance reproducibility of ITCs; for transparency, all key assumptions and limitations must be shared [9–17].

To date, no ITC has evaluated the FDA-approved dosages of rhC1-INH and sebetralstat or compared outcomes related to TTCR and the need for redosing. To address these gaps, a comprehensive ITC using patient-level data from the pivotal rhC1-INH trials and aggregate data from the sebetralstat KONFIDENT trial was conducted. The objective of this study was to evaluate the relative TTCR and redosing of rhC1-INH versus sebetralstat for the on-demand treatment of acute HAE attacks.

Methods

Systematic Literature Review

The SLR search strategy was developed using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist [18] to identify randomized clinical trials (RCTs) evaluating on-demand treatments for HAE attacks. The SLR was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines, guidelines from the Cochrane Collaboration, and the principles and standards for health technology assessment [14–17, 19], and was designed using predefined population, intervention, comparison, outcome, study design, and timing (PICOS-T) criteria (Table 1). The inclusion criteria focused on published phase 2 or 3 RCTs evaluating treatments for acute HAE attacks in patients aged ≥ 12 years, with the outcomes of TTCR and redosing rates. TTCR was defined as the time from treatment administration to complete resolution of HAE symptoms within 24 h, defined as “none” using the Patient Global Impression of Severity (PGI-S) scale. Redosing was defined as the administration of a second dose of an investigational product within the study-defined observation period. The full details of the SLR have been reported previously [20].

Feasibility Assessment

A feasibility assessment was conducted to identify major differences in the study designs of the included trials and to create a framework for executing the most robust ITC possible. To systematically evaluate the feasibility of conducting a valid ITC, we applied the Cope framework [21], which outlines a structured four-part process for assessing clinical and methodological comparability across trials. This included visualizing heterogeneity in treatment and outcome definitions, evaluating study and patient characteristics for potential treatment effect modifiers (TEMs) and prognostic variables (PVs), assessing baseline risk across trials, and examining observed treatment effects for consistency [21]. Study design tables were developed to compare selected trials, and network diagrams were used to evaluate comparable outcomes across those trials. The Cope framework ensured that assumptions underlying the ITC were transparent and that bias due to cross-trial differences were systematically addressed.

Statistical Methods

Primary and Scenario Analyses

The primary aim of this analysis was to perform a matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC). An MAIC is a specific ITC method that reweights individual patient data from one trial to match those reported in a comparator trial based on patient and trial characteristics that are identified as potential TEMs or PVs, thereby adjusting for cross-trial differences. Effective sample size (ESS) was calculated to assess the impact of reweighting. Weighted Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for TTCR; logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) for redosing.

The choice between anchored and unanchored MAIC as the primary analysis was determined based on the equivalency of the placebo groups of the studies being compared. When one approach was selected as the primary analysis, the alternative approach was prespecified as the first scenario analysis (i.e., if the unanchored MAIC was the primary analysis, the anchored MAIC was conducted as a scenario, and vice versa). An additional scenario analysis explored the implications of an alternative anchoring strategy, by anchoring through different common comparator placebo groups. This structured approach was designed to evaluate the robustness of findings for both the anchoring assumption and the choice of common comparator.

Subgroup Analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted for the identified clinically meaningful TEM and PV [22] variables for which there was limited overlap between the trials. These analyses were designed to explore potential confounding from variables that would not be informative to match in the MAIC due to major reductions in ESS.

Additionally, subgroup analyses can only be conducted when data are available from all trials included. Subgroup data presented in the form of median (interquartile range) were modeled based on an exponential distribution, assuming a constant event rate within the subgroup over the observed time period; subgroup data presented in the form of Kaplan–Meier curves were digitized to generate simulated patient-level data. For rhC1-INH, subgroups were identified using patient-level data for patients meeting the subgroup criteria.

Sensitivity Analysis

The PICOS-T criteria prespecified TTCR as an outcome of interest. For rhC1-INH, TTCR was derived using a mapping algorithm based on a visual analog scale (VAS; scale 0–100 mm) for symptom severity, which introduced a level of uncertainty inherent to the mapping process. Further information on the mapping study can be found in Riedl et al. and Manning et al. [5, 23]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the upper- and lower-bound threshold values for achieving complete resolution based on the VAS, where the lower-bound VAS threshold was 5 mm for all attack locations, and the upper-bound threshold was 15 mm for abdominal attacks, 25 mm for peripheral attacks, and 25 mm for head and neck attacks.

Ethics Approval

Ethics approval was not required because this study was based on previously published clinical trials and did not involve any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Feasibility Assessment

Of the 12 clinical trials identified in the SLR, the outcomes of TTCR and redosing could only be compared in a pooled analysis of three rhC1-INH trials (C1 1310, NCT01188564; C1 1205, NCT00225147; C1 1304, NCT00262301) and the KONFIDENT (sebetralstat) trial (NCT05259917) [3–5, 8]; therefore, only the rhC1-INH pooled analysis and the KONFIDENT trial were included for synthesis (Fig. 1). The original trial protocols were approved by an institutional review board or ethics committee, and the trials were each conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All trial participants provided written informed consent [3, 4, 8].

The C1 1205 and C1 1304 trials did not report redosing and were excluded from the redosing analysis [20]. Patient-level data were pooled from the rhC1-INH trials [C1 1310, C1 1205, and C1 1304 (placebo group only)] for the FDA-approved dose of rhC1-INH (50 U/kg for patients weighing < 84 kg or 4200 U for patients weighing ≥ 84 kg) in the pooled analysis [5] and compared with aggregate data derived from the FDA-approved dose of sebetralstat 600 mg from the KONFIDENT trial.

There were major differences between the rhC1-INH trials and the KONFIDENT trial (Table 2). The rhC1-INH trials were conducted in a clinical trial site where participants needed to arrive at a study center within 5 h of attack onset, whereas the KONFIDENT trial was conducted in a home setting and participants were instructed to treat immediately upon attack symptom recognition. The rhC1-INH trials required a minimum attack severity (VAS score ≥ 50 mm), whereas the KONFIDENT trial had no minimum severity requirement. Patients originally assigned to receive placebo in the KONFIDENT trial were redosed with placebo, thereby maintaining an intervention-restricted placebo arm. In comparison, patients originally assigned to receive placebo in the rhC1-INH C1 1310 trial were redosed with rhC1-INH, resulting in an intervention-permissive placebo arm.

Eligibility criteria for redosing differed between the trials (Table 2, Table S1 in the Supplementary Material). Redosing eligibility began 3 h after the initial sebetralstat dose in the KONFIDENT trial and 4 h after the initial rhC1-INH dose in the C1 1310 trial. The KONFIDENT trial did not permit redosing for laryngeal attacks; instead, it required the use of conventional on-demand treatment for redosing at any time following the first dose of investigational product. The rhC1-INH trials allowed redosing with rhC1-INH prior to the 4-h waiting period for life-threatening oropharyngeal-laryngeal cases (Table S1 in the Supplementary Material).

Patient-level demographics across the four trials were broadly similar: the majority of patients were white (86.0–95.8%) and with a median age of 38–39 years (Table 3). There were more female (60.2–68.8%) than male (31.2–39.8%) patients. Common attack locations were peripheral (41.7–62.4%) and abdominal (35.0–45.2%) across the trials. However, baseline attack severity differed substantially: 100% of patients in the rhC1-INH trials had severe or very severe attacks, compared with only 17.5% of patients in the KONFIDENT trial. Treatment timing also varied due to study design. In the rhC1-INH trials, patients were required to present at a clinical site for treatment, resulting in delayed administration (median time from attack onset to treatment 242–267 min) (Table 3). In contrast, the KONFIDENT trial was conducted at home, with instructions to treat immediately upon attack recognition (median time from attack onset to treatment 41–51 min). Therefore, the variables of attack severity and time from attack onset to treatment were shown to have minimal overlap between the two trials.

Selection of TEMs and PVs to serve as matching variables was informed by the results of a clinician survey evaluating TEMs and PVs in HAE [22], and a univariate regression analysis of rhC1-INH data was performed. In this ITC, clinically relevant variables that were statistically significant in univariate regression analyses included baseline attack severity (P = 0.028), attack location (P = 0.006), time from attack onset to treatment (P = 0.015), and use of long-term prophylaxis (P = 0.034) [22]. Variables with sufficient overlap across trials (attack location and long-term prophylaxis use) were included in matching analyses, whereas variables with limited overlap across trials (baseline attack severity and time from attack onset to treatment) were addressed via subgroup analyses to maintain adequate effective sample size.

To support selection of the primary approach, network diagrams were constructed to visually map trial connections, guide the analytical approach, and identify study design differences that could affect indirect comparisons (Fig. 2). Anchored analyses were limited by differences in the redosing protocols of the placebo groups between trials (Table 2, Table S1 in the Supplementary Material). Thus, unanchored MAIC analyses were conducted in the primary analysis to provide the most robust estimate for comparison (Fig. 2a) for TTCR and redosing using individual patient-level data from rhC1-INH trials reweighted to match key TEMs and PVs (attack location and prophylaxis use) of the KONFIDENT trial population [22].

Scenario analyses explored the impact of using different common comparators to support the primary analysis. Unlike the unanchored MAIC used in the primary analysis, an anchored MAIC compares rhC1-INH and sebetralstat through their respective placebo arms. In the scenario analyses, the anchored MAIC represents an intervention-permissive placebo comparator set (Fig. 2b). Additionally, rhC1-INH and sebetralstat were compared using a Bucher ITC based only on the placebo arm of the KONFIDENT trial [13]. The Bucher ITC represents an intervention-restricted placebo comparator set; treatment data for rhC1-INH and sebetralstat were compared with the intervention-restricted placebo group of the KONFIDENT trial, in which there was no censoring of redosing and no redosing with active treatment (Fig. 2c).

Subgroup unanchored MAIC analyses were performed to explore confounding from variables that could not be matched in the primary analysis due to minimal overlap between the rhC1-INH and sebetralstat populations (baseline attack severity and time from attack onset to treatment). In the KONFIDENT trial, median TTCR was reported for patients with severe or very severe HAE attacks, which allowed the overall pooled rhC1-INH population to be compared with the subgroup of patients with severe or very severe HAE attacks from the KONFIDENT trial, assuming a constant event rate within the subgroup over the observed time period (24 h). In the earlier treatment subgroup analysis, patients treated within the median time from attack onset to treatment in the rhC1-INH studies [5] were compared with the overall sebetralstat 600-mg population from the KONFIDENT trial. Subgroup analyses for redosing were not performed due to lack of available subgroup data for this endpoint from the KONFIDENT trial.

Primary Analysis

An unanchored MAIC was conducted as the primary analysis to estimate comparative treatment effects for TTCR and redosing. A total of 48 and 43 patients met the matching criteria for the TTCR and redosing analyses, respectively. In the primary analysis, the unanchored MAIC showed that rhC1-INH was 4.5 times more likely to achieve TTCR within 24 h compared with sebetralstat (HR 4.52; 95% CI 2.84–7.18), a statistically significant outcome (Fig. 3a). The median (95% CI) time to complete attack resolution was 4.25 (2.98–5.03) h (Fig. 3b). The corresponding restricted mean survival times at 24 h were 6.36 [standard error (SE), 1.14] h and 16.37 (SE, 0.93) h for rhC1-INH and sebetralstat, respectively.

Scenario Analyses

To further support the primary analysis, scenario analyses examined the impact of using a common comparator with an intervention-permissive placebo comparator set (anchored MAIC) or an intervention-restricted placebo comparator set (Bucher ITC) (Table 4, Fig. 2b, c). rhC1-INH was 1.4 times more likely than sebetralstat to achieve TTCR within 24 h in the intervention-permissive placebo comparator set (HR 1.41; 95% CI 0.63–3.18) and 9.8 times more likely to achieve TTCR in the intervention-restricted placebo comparator set (HR 9.76; 95% CI 4.94–19.29). rhC1-INH had a 68% reduction in the odds of redosing compared with sebetralstat (OR 0.32; 95% CI 0.08–1.27) in the intervention-permissive placebo comparator set and a 90% reduction in the odds of redosing compared with sebetralstat in the intervention-restricted placebo comparator set (OR 0.10; 95% CI 0.03–0.32). The scenario analyses for both TTCR and redosing in the intervention-permissive placebo comparator set demonstrated statistical significance.

As a result of minimal overlap between rhC1-INH and sebetralstat in baseline attack severity and treatment timing, these variables could not be included as part of the matching analyses. To explore any possible confounding from variables, unanchored MAICs for TTCR in these subgroups were performed (Fig. 5). The unanchored MAICs showed that rhC1-INH was 5.4 times more likely than sebetralstat to achieve TTCR within 24 h (HR 5.39; 95% CI 2.61–11.12) in patients with severe or very severe attacks at baseline (Fig. 5a), and 5.5 times more likely to achieve TTCR within 24 h than sebetralstat in the earlier treatment subgroup (HR 5.53; 95% CI 3.22–9.50) (Fig. 5b), both statistically significant outcomes.

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using unanchored MAIC to explore alternative endpoint definitions for TTCR; upper-bound and lower-bound estimates were based on varied thresholds for TTCR using results from a post hoc analysis of TTCR in rhC1-INH trials [5, 23]. rhC1-INH was 5.1 times more likely than sebetralstat to achieve TTCR within 24 h based on the upper-bound threshold of TTCR (HR 5.11; 95% CI 3.12–8.38) and 2.8 times more likely based on the lower-bound threshold of TTCR (HR 2.82; 95% CI 1.76–4.50) (Table S2 in the Supplementary Material). The results from both sensitivity analyses were statistically significant.

Discussion

This ITC provides evidence that rhC1-INH demonstrates statistically significantly favorable efficacy compared with oral sebetralstat for the on-demand treatment of HAE attacks. Using MAIC methodology and patient-level data from pivotal rhC1-INH trials, this ITC showed that rhC1-INH was associated with a statistically significant faster time to complete resolution of symptoms and lower redosing rates than sebetralstat.

In the overall population, complete symptom resolution within 24 h was 4.5 times more likely with rhC1-INH than with sebetralstat in the primary analysis (HR 4.52; 95% CI 2.84–7.18). Additionally, rhC1-INH demonstrated an 82% reduction in the odds of redosing compared with sebetralstat (OR 0.18; 95% CI 0.06–0.53). These positive results with rhC1-INH were consistent across scenario, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses.

To our knowledge, this is the first ITC to compare the outcomes of TTCR and redosing in patients with HAE. Although the primary endpoint of HAE on-demand clinical trials is typically TOSR, complete attack resolution is inherently the most meaningful outcome to patients, enabling them to return to normal daily activities [1, 20, 24]. A patient may achieve onset of symptom relief and meet the trial endpoint yet subsequently regress and require redosing before reaching complete resolution. Therefore, TTCR and redosing represent more decision-relevant and interpretable endpoints for indirect comparison of on-demand HAE treatments.

A 2025 ITC by Li et al. found no statistically significant difference between sebetralstat and rhC1-INH in TOSR (HR [95% credible interval] 0.96 [0.42–2.15] to 1.19 [0.58–2.45]) [9]. However, there were key methodological limitations, including assumptions about the similarity of outcomes, reliance on an anchored MAIC, and matching on noninformative variables, which may have obscured clinically meaningful differences. The ITC by Li et al. also assumed that the outcomes of treatment effect questionnaire (TEQ) and Patient Global Impression of Change (PGI-C) are equivalent; however, the TEQ comprised two questions and required no worsening, whereas the PGI-C consisted of one question and allowed for worsening. Further, the analysis relied on anchored MAIC methodology, which is limited due to differences in the placebo groups, as previously described. Additionally, the analysis by Li et al. did not use matching or subgroup analysis to account for any of the clinically validated TEMs and PVs, such as attack severity, attack location, treatment timing, or prophylaxis use [22]. Instead, matching was performed on noninformative patient-level characteristics such as patient age, sex, and race. Matching on noninformative variables may not only fail to address heterogeneity between trials but may also inadvertently redistribute key TEMs and PVs differently across comparison groups. Finally, the ITC by Li et al. compared the FDA-approved dose of rhC1-INH (50 U/kg) dose with the sebetralstat 300-mg dose rather than the FDA-approved 600-mg dose. Our study compared the FDA-approved doses of rhC1-INH and sebetralstat and aligned outcomes and endpoints; given substantive differences in the placebo arms, an unanchored MAIC was selected as the primary analysis. Furthermore, all clinician-validated TEMs and PVs were accounted for through MAIC reweighting and subgroup analyses, thereby enabling a more robust comparison across heterogeneous trial populations.

As with all indirect comparisons, this analysis is subject to several assumptions and limitations. The validity of MAIC relies on the assumption that all relevant TEMs are identified and appropriately adjusted for in the analysis [25]. Although attack severity, attack location, treatment timing, and prophylaxis use were accounted for, other unmeasured confounders not identified in the clinician survey may remain. The majority of patients enrolled in these trials were female; however, the clinical survey informing the TEMs and PVs for this analysis did not identify patient sex, age, and race as meaningful modifiers of treatment response for HAE [22]. Accordingly, these factors were not incorporated into the adjustment set. Nonetheless, some uncertainty may remain, as the potential for unmeasured effect modification cannot be fully excluded.

Differences in trial design posed additional challenges. The KONFIDENT trial and the rhC1-INH trials enrolled patients based on different eligibility requirements for HAE attacks. All patients in the rhC1-INH trials had a severe or very severe attack at baseline based on the PGI-S scale [3, 4], whereas patients enrolled in the KONFIDENT trial had a range of baseline attack severities, with only 17.5% of patients having severe or very severe attacks at baseline [8]. Because this difference in baseline attack severity could not be accounted for in our model, we performed a subgroup analysis. Patients with severe and very severe attacks were 5.4 times more likely to achieve complete resolution with rhC1-INH than with sebetralstat (HR 5.39; 95% CI 2.61–11.12), a statistically significant result consistent with the overall analysis, and of greater magnitude. These results suggest that if sebetralstat had been studied using the trial design of rhC1-INH, even greater differences between treatments may have been observed.

The rhC1-INH trials required clinic visits for assessment and treatment, resulting in delayed rhC1-INH administration (median 242 min) [3, 4], whereas the KONFIDENT trial allowed for self-administration of sebetralstat (median 41 min) [8]. Given the inability to account for differences in treatment timing within the model, a subgroup analysis was performed. Patients in the earlier treatment population were 5.5 times more likely to achieve complete resolution with rhC1-INH compared with sebetralstat (HR 5.53; 95% CI 3.22–9.50), a result consistent with—and more pronounced than—that of the overall analysis. Despite a delay in treatment administration incurred by study design, rhC1-INH demonstrated improved outcomes in TTCR with statistical significance. These data suggest that even greater efficacy may be observed in real-world settings where earlier administration is feasible.

The rhC1-INH C1 1310 trial allowed redosing of oropharyngeal-laryngeal attacks, whereas laryngeal attacks were not eligible for redosing in the KONFIDENT trial [3, 8]. This may have led to an underestimation of the sebetralstat redosing rate, as laryngeal attacks were included in the total number of attacks (i.e., attacks at risk) but were excluded from the number of attacks with an event (i.e., redosing). This study design difference could not be accounted for because the number of nonlaryngeal attacks was not estimable in the KONFIDENT population, as patients could report multiple baseline attack locations.

To understand the relative effect of the differences between placebo arms, we performed a scenario analysis using an intervention-permissive placebo comparator set (anchored MAIC) and an intervention-restricted placebo comparator set (Bucher ITC). In these scenario analyses, the HRs for TTCR were 1.41 (95% CI 0.63–3.18) and 9.76 (95% CI 4.94–19.29). The ORs for redosing were 0.32 (95% CI 0.08–1.27) and 0.10 (95% CI 0.03–0.32). The variability observed in the effect estimates across anchoring scenarios indicates that the results are sensitive to the choice of placebo comparator, highlighting important limitations of placebo-anchored analyses in the context of these trials. These results further support our choice to use unanchored analyses, which were prioritized to remove the impact of the differences in study design across placebo arms.

Additionally, outcome definitions varied across trials. TTCR was derived using different patient-reported outcome (PRO) instruments (VAS vs PGI-S), requiring mapping algorithms to ensure comparability. Although the use of different PRO instruments may introduce uncertainty, variability was explored using the lower-bound and upper-bound thresholds for TTCR derived from the mapping algorithm [23]. rhC1-INH demonstrated a shorter TTCR compared with sebetralstat across lower- and upper-bound thresholds for TTCR, with an HR of 2.82 (95% CI 1.76–4.50) and 5.11 (95% CI 3.12–8.38), respectively. Across thresholds for TTCR, the observed magnitude of effect was consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the primary TTCR HR of 4.52 (95% CI 2.84–7.18) in the unanchored MAIC. Regardless of the TTCR threshold applied, rhC1-INH demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in TTCR compared with sebetralstat.

Despite these limitations, the consistency and magnitude of the efficacy differences observed across subgroup, sensitivity, and scenario analyses support the primary analysis findings and demonstrate the clinical value of rhC1-INH as an effective on-demand treatment for acute HAE attacks. These findings may inform clinical decision-making and treatment guidelines, particularly in settings where redosing is not ideal and rapid and complete symptom resolution is critical.

Whereas this analysis focused on efficacy outcomes, real-world treatment decisions in HAE extend beyond time to symptom resolution. Factors such as cost, patient perception of severity, access to on-demand medication, convenience, and route of administration may influence adherence [26–28]. Although some patients may perceive oral administration to be more convenient than intravenous administration, the relationship between administration route and adherence is nuanced [26]. A US Hereditary Angioedema Association survey of patients with HAE receiving injectable on-demand treatments (i.e., icatibant, rhC1-INH, plasma-derived C1 esterase inhibitor, and ecallantide) found that patients who were anxious about using injectable treatments were more likely to delay or forego treatment of HAE attacks [27]. However, more than 50% of patients were either not anxious or reported only mild anxiety. Additionally, 58% of patients who were not anxious still did not treat all HAE attacks, suggesting that factors other than route of administration may influence whether patients treat every HAE attack [27].

Conclusions

In the primary analysis, this ITC found that rhC1-INH 50 U/kg (for patients weighing < 84 kg or 4200 U for patients weighing ≥ 84 kg) was associated with statistically significantly faster TTCR and lower redosing rates compared with oral sebetralstat 600 mg for the on-demand treatment of HAE attacks. Supportive analyses provided similar results, with 100% favoring rhC1-INH and 75% achieving statistical significance. These findings were consistent across multiple analyses, including subgroups with severe attacks and those receiving earlier treatment, and remained robust in sensitivity and scenario analyses. Although limitations inherent to cross-trial comparisons exist, these results support rhC1-INH as an effective option for achieving rapid and sustained symptom control in acute HAE management.
