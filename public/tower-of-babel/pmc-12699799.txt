Financial toxicity in pediatric cancer: lived experiences and coping strategies of parents
Authors: Yan Liu, Pengfei Li, Qian Gao, Boyu Liu, Yuantao Qi, Weimin Guan, Nan Zhang, Youhua Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeParents and other caregivers of pediatric cancer patients often experience severe financial toxicity due to prolonged treatment durations and substantial out-of-pocket expenses, compounding both physical and psychological burdens. Despite increasing recognition, a comprehensive understanding of these experiences remains limited. This study explored the lived experiences and coping strategies of parents confronting financial toxicity in the context of pediatric cancer in China.MethodA descriptive phenomenological approach was employed to examine parents’ lived experiences. Between April and May 2024, semi-structured, in-depth interviews were conducted with 20 parents of children with cancer, recruited through purposive sampling from three tertiary hospital in Shandong Province, China. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using Giorgi’s phenomenological method, assisted by NVivo 12.0.ResultsAnalysis of the interview data identified four major categories encompassing twelve subcategories. Financial toxicity stemmed from diverse sources, including cancer-related factors, limited household economic resilience, societal expectations, and gaps in insurance coverage. Consequences included asset depletion, ongoing financial hardship, and restricted access to optimal care. Parents reported substantial psychological distress—such as anxiety, depression, and social isolation—and primarily employed passive and unsustainable coping strategies, including borrowing, asset liquidation, cutting essential expenditures, and informal employment.ConclusionPediatric cancer imposed a heavy financial burden that influenced treatment decisions, parental mental health, and overall family well-being. Inadequate insurance and limited social support exacerbated this burden. Targeted financial assistance and policy reforms are urgently needed to mitigate the impact and improve health outcomes for affected parents.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13760-z.Keywords: Financial toxicity, Pediatric cancer, Parents, Phenomenological study, Coping strategies, China

Introduction

Cancer is one of the major global public health issues, characterized by prolonged treatment duration, complex therapeutic modalities, and substantial healthcare costs [1]. It imposes multidimensional burdens on patients and their parents and other caregivers, including physical suffering, psychological distress, and significant economic strain [2]. In 2013, Zafar first introduced the concept of financial toxicity (FT), referring to the economic adverse effects experienced by cancer patients as a consequence of high direct or indirect medical costs and reduced income during treatment [3]. Financial toxicity includes both objective financial burdens and subjective financial distress, leading to diminished quality of life, lower treatment adherence, and even discontinuation of treatment [4].

According to the most recent national estimates from the National Center for Pediatric Cancer Surveillance, approximately 121,145 children and adolescents (aged 0–19 years) were diagnosed with cancer in China between 2018 and 2020, corresponding to an incidence rate of 126.48 per million (122.86 per million among children aged 0–14 years) [5]. Parents and other caregivers of children with cancer often face unique and more prolonged financial challenges compared with adult cancer patients, largely due to extended treatment courses, intensive caregiving needs, and limited financial protection for pediatric care [6]. Since pediatric patients do not directly bear financial responsibilities, financial toxicity primarily manifests at the family level, with parents, as primary caregivers, often experiencing severe financial strain and psychological distress due to substantial medical expenditures and long-term caregiving responsibilities. In China, the cancer care system relies mainly on the Basic Medical Insurance (BMI) scheme, which provides coverage to approximately 95% of the population [7]. However, reimbursement rates vary among different population groups, and children as well as rural residents generally receive lower levels of coverage. In addition, high-cost or innovative treatments—including targeted and immunotherapies—are often only partially reimbursed or excluded from insurance benefit lists [8]. Commercial health insurance remains underdeveloped, contributing less than 4% to total health expenditure and leaving families exposed to considerable out-of-pocket costs during extended treatment periods [9].

Although an increasing number of qualitative studies have explored cancer-related financial hardship, most have focused on adult patients and have been conducted in high-income countries such as the United States, Canada, and Western Europe. In contrast, qualitative investigations addressing the experiences of parents of children with cancer—particularly in low- and middle-income contexts like China—remain very limited [10]. Specifically, qualitative investigations into financial toxicity experienced by parents of children with cancer remain scarce. While existing literature provides valuable insights into the financial burdens and coping mechanisms among adult cancer patients, there remains a significant gap in understanding the specific features, impacts, and coping strategies associated with financial toxicity in pediatric oncology populations [11]. This underscores the necessity for systematic qualitative studies addressing financial toxicity among children with cancer and their families.

This study aimed to qualitatively investigate the financial toxicity experienced by parents of children with cancer and their coping strategies. We hoped to generate empirical evidence to inform targeted interventions and policies might alleviate the financial and psychological challenges faced by parents.

Methods

Approach, study design and setting

We employed a descriptive phenomenological approach, using semi-structured interviews to explore parents’ lived experiences of financial toxicity, including its psychological impacts and related coping responses. The study was conducted in the paediatric oncology departments of three tertiary hospitals in Shandong Province, China: Qilu Hospital of Shandong University, Shandong Provincial Hospital, and the First Medical University of Shandong Affiliated Cancer Hospital. This setting provided direct access to parents of children receiving active cancer treatment, enabling the collection of rich, context-specific accounts of financial experiences.

Participants and recruitment

Direct parents accompanying children during hospitalisation were selected as study participants, as paediatric patients were too young to participate in interviews themselves. Purposive sampling was used to recruit parents who met the inclusion criteria. Eligible participants were: (i) parents of a child aged 0–14 years with a histopathologically confirmed cancer diagnosis; (ii) aged ≥ 18 years; and (iii) able to communicate in Mandarin. Parents with language or cognitive impairments, psychiatric conditions, or who were unable to allocate time for the interview were excluded. Potential participants were approached face-to-face in the paediatric oncology wards by a member of the research team, who provided study information and invited them to participate. All parents who were approached agreed to participate, and none refused or withdrew from the study. Data collection continued until data saturation was reached, when no new descriptions emerged.

Data collection

An interview guide was developed based on a comprehensive literature review and group discussions. Pilot interviews with five parents were conducted to refine question clarity, flow, and cultural appropriateness; these data were excluded from the final analysis. The revised guide was used for formal semi-structured interviews (Table S1).

Interviews were conducted by the first three authors (Yan Liu, Pengfei Li, and Qian Gao) between April and May 2024. All three were full-time researchers specialising in oncology and public health, each holding a postgraduate degree in medicine or public health, with experience in cancer prevention, health services research, and qualitative methods. The interview team comprised two male researchers and one female researcher, which supported rapport-building with parents of different genders. Each parent took part in a single semi-structured interview lasting 20–30 min to allow in-depth discussion while minimising participant burden. Interviews were conducted in private hospital meeting rooms or at the bedside in a quiet ward area, with only the interviewer and participant present to ensure privacy and reduce external influence. All interviews were audio-recorded and supplemented with field notes. Before each interview, researchers introduced themselves, outlined their roles, informed parents of the study objectives, and obtained written informed consent. This process helped establish a safe and trusting interview environment that encouraged open sharing.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

No prior relationship existed between interviewers and participants. The interviewers recognised that their professional background in oncology services might influence their perspectives on financial hardship. To uphold a phenomenological stance, they engaged in ongoing reflexivity and bracketing throughout data collection to minimise preconceptions and potential bias.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Medical University of Shandong Affiliated Cancer Hospital (Approval No. SDTHEC-2023009018). The study adhered to institutional and national ethical standards and followed the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating parents; no minors were interviewed. Confidentiality was ensured, and parents were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Interview transcripts were not returned to parents for checking or correction. To enhance reporting transparency, this study followed the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) guidelines, and the completed checklist was uploaded as a related file (not for publication).

Qualitative analysis

All interview data were transcribed verbatim within 24 h and imported into NVivo 12.0 for management and coding. Two researchers independently coded the initial transcripts, discussed discrepancies to reach consensus, and refined the coding framework, which was then applied to subsequent transcripts. A third researcher reviewed all coded materials to ensure consistency and accuracy of interpretation. An initial coding framework was developed and iteratively refined as new data had been analysed.

Data analysis was conducted using Giorgi’s phenomenological method. The analysis steps were as follows: (1) Transcription and familiarisation: The audio recordings and transcripts were carefully reviewed to gain an overall understanding of the data. (2) Meaning unit identification: The data were segmented, focusing on the experiences of parents, and key “meaning units” were identified. (3) Organisation and description: Repetitive meaning units were removed, relationships between units and their contexts were clarified, and a systematic description was produced. (4) Structural description: Meaning units were integrated to reveal the essential structure of individual experiences. (5) Synthesis of general structure: The individual structural descriptions were synthesised into a comprehensive conceptual structure. For example, statements describing parents’ struggles to afford immunotherapy were identified as meaning units during data segmentation, later integrated into the subcategory “Limited Treatment Options,” and finally represented within the broader category “Severe Economic Damage.” This illustrates how Giorgi’s steps were applied to move from raw transcripts to abstracted descriptive structures.

Risk factors for financial toxicity

Among the four major descriptive categories, factors contributing to financial toxicity were the most frequently reported, discussed by nearly all parents. Differences in tumor type, disease stage, and timing of diagnosis were closely associated with variations in treatment costs and overall financial burden. The heterogeneity of pediatric cancers appeared to influence both the intensity and duration of required care.

Personal traits

Differences in tumor type, disease stage, and timing of diagnosis were closely associated with variations in treatment costs and overall financial burden. The heterogeneity of pediatric cancers appeared to influence both the intensity and duration of required care.

Our child has hepatoblastoma. It’s large and multifocal but not metastatic, so costs were moderate. (P14)

Because of delayed diagnosis, our child went through surgery, transplantation, and immunotherapy. After remission, the cancer recurred—every stage added more costs. (P1)

Due to our child’s poor health, he spends less than half a month at home, the rest of the time is spent in the hospital, which results in higher medical expenses, and there will be a significant cost afterward. (P5)

Family economic resilience to risks

Parents described that having limited financial reserves made it hard to manage the costs of care. They shared that high treatment expenses—especially for advanced therapies not covered by insurance—were beyond what their household income could support, particularly when earnings were unstable.

Our financial condition is quite stable… we don’t face much economic pressure… We had purchased insurance early and could afford the 300,000 RMB proton therapy out-of-pocket. (P9)

We used all our savings for a school-district apartment. When she got sick, we had nothing left. (P10)

The greatest difficulty lies in financial aspects… because both my child’s father and I are quite young, having only worked a few years after graduation before getting married and having children, which also involved significant expenses. (P3)

Parents shared that unstable income and caregiving arrangements made it difficult to cope financially. Many described having to reduce working hours, take long leave, or leave their jobs entirely to care for their child, which significantly reduced household income.

His illness occupies all my time. I’ve lost my income entirely. (P18)

Both of us had to quit our jobs to care for her around the clock. (P16)

Single-parent families and those lacking broader family support bore more intense financial strain. In contrast, households with healthy and involved elders benefited from additional caregiving support, which helped reduce indirect costs.

Ours is a single-parent family, and I have always been the sole parent. After my child fell ill, I had to quit my job to take care of him here. (P20)

Both of us are here with the sick child. His grandmother takes care of the older one at home. (P11)

His grandmother is also here. It’s sometimes inconvenient to care for the child alone, so my mother-in-law helps me take care of him. (P8)

Parents shared that ongoing financial commitments, such as mortgage payments and supporting more than one child, were already straining their household budget. After the child’s diagnosis, several parents described that these existing pressures became harder to manage.

We don’t have much in savings… We just had our second child, haven’t been working for many years, and we still have to pay the mortgage. We have no money and are also in debt. (P4)

I also have another child who just started college this year. Both children require financial support. (P17)

Deeply held cultural values and the emotional drive to save one’s child often motivated families beyond financial limits. Parents expressed a willingness to take on debt or sell assets to pursue the best possible treatments, regardless of the costs.

We’re young—it’s okay to be in debt. I’d sell everything for my child. (P3)

In Beijing, everything was expensive. We had to borrow money just to afford the trip and rent. (P5)

Social determinants of financial toxicity

The concentration of pediatric cancer resources in major cities led many families to seek care far from home, incurring substantial costs for travel, accommodation, and living expenses. These non-medical expenditures significantly compounded financial toxicity.

We stayed in Shanghai for almost a year—monthly rent was 4–5,000 RMB, plus food and high-speed train tickets. (P1)

In Beijing, the costs are particularly high, mainly because the rent is very expensive… Our financial situation was not good to begin with, and the money for taking the child to the doctor was all borrowed. (P4)

Social support played an important role in alleviating families’ financial stress, although the extent and type of support varied substantially among parents.

Financially, my parents and friends have given some support; they directly gave us money. Actually, everyone helped, so the financial pressure isn’t that great. (P13)

We didn’t want to publicize our child’s condition online, so we just asked relatives. (P8)

Others were worse off than us, so we didn’t raise money. We wanted to leave help for those who really need it. At least we are capable of working, but many others are not able to work… We thought about accumulating some good fortune for our child by leaving the care and assistance to those more in need than us. (P10)

Health insurance constraints and financial toxicity

Inter-provincial treatment was common due to uneven distribution of pediatric cancer services, but lower reimbursement rates outside patients’ home regions appeared to increase out-of-pocket costs and stress.

We travel to Shandong for treatment, but the reimbursement rate is lower than at home. It adds to our burden. (P11)

Commercial insurance was perceived as helpful in reducing financial burden, but its high cost limited accessibility for low-income parents and contributed to existing disparities in protection. In contrast, parents with early coverage experienced less financial strain.

We bought a high-limit commercial plan. After using the New Rural Cooperative Scheme, the rest is covered. It really eased our pressure. (P16)

Severe economic damage

Significant disruption to family finances

Financial toxicity was closely associated with serious disruption to family finances. In several cases, children were aware of their parents’ economic difficulties, which led to emotional conflict between their wish to continue treatment and concern about the financial burden on their household.

He sometimes says: ‘Let’s stop treatment and go home—we shouldn’t sell the house.’ But then he says, ‘I still want to live. (P15)

The parents had depleted their savings, relied on borrowed funds, and listed their house for sale, yet still struggled to afford ongoing care.

Limited treatment options

Financial toxicity was reported to limit parents’ access to certain recommended therapies. Several parents described delaying or forgoing immunotherapy because of cost, even when doctors suggested its potential benefits. Some parents believed that such financial constraints contributed to disease progression or less favorable treatment outcomes.

We couldn’t afford immunotherapy. We thought, if it doesn’t work, we’d have nothing left. (P19)

The financial pressure is too overwhelming; otherwise, we would have done immunotherapy long ago. We have been delaying it for two years, and now it’s too late—the cancer has already metastasized to the bones. (P2)

We had no commercial insurance, so we could only afford chemotherapy and targeted drugs. Now it’s relapsed, and he’s too weak for surgery or immunotherapy. Chemo has damaged his heart and kidneys. (P12)

Mental health issues

Direct medical financial pressure on caregivers

High treatment costs led to persistent financial stress for parents, regardless of income level. The burden of funding care, especially advanced therapies like immunotherapy, contributed to overwhelming anxiety and psychological strain.

For everyone, undergoing immunotherapy feels like a massive mountain pressing down, suffocating us, though it’s one of the more effective treatment methods available. It gives hope—but most families just can’t afford it. (P5)

If the treatment level in our country is insufficient, we considered going abroad. However, if it involves moving to developed countries, it would cost at least three to five million, which is a tremendous financial burden. (P9)

I care for him alone now. The pressure is crushing—sometimes I feel like I’ll break down. (P15)

Indirect mental health issues

Beyond direct financial pressures, parents experienced emotional strain associated with social judgment, secrecy, and isolation. Some parents chose to conceal the illness from relatives to protect them from distress, while others reported feelings of stigma and a lack of social support.

Our older child is still in kindergarten, and we need to keep up with the child’s nutritional needs while adults try to save wherever possible. Sometimes when his grandmother takes the older child to the supermarket to buy some food, there are many gossips in the village, saying, ‘Look, the younger one is ill, and yet they are still spending money on the older one,’ which is typical in rural areas. (P11)

We hid the diagnosis from the grandparents for over a year—to protect them from worrying. (P10)

Now I’m the only one caring for him. The pressure is overwhelming—my husband hasn’t returned to work since. (P15)

Passive coping behaviors

Faced with unaffordable treatment costs, many parents resorted to short-term strategies such as borrowing, selling assets, or reducing essential spending. These responses provided temporary relief but placed further strain on household finances.

Borrowing money

Faced with unaffordable treatment costs, parents often resorted to short-term strategies like borrowing, selling assets, or cutting essential spending. These responses were typically unsustainable and often deepened financial vulnerability.

My parents still farm, and I do odd jobs. But the relatives we borrowed from now avoid us. If you keep asking, they talk behind your back. (P20)

Since my child got sick, everything changed. Even relatives and friends now avoid us. (P15)

Selling assets

Selling property was often a desperate but necessary step to cover treatment costs. Some parents gave up essential assets like homes, leading to long-term financial insecurity.

Immunotherapy costs over a million yuan. We sold our house near a good school. There was no choice—treatment came first. (P8)

We sold our house and were planning to sell his grandmother’s too. Now we live on my parents’ pension. (P15)

Cutting daily expenses

To prioritize the cost of the child’s treatment, parents were often forced to drastically reduce spending on daily necessities. This reallocation of resources could temporarily relieve financial strain but significantly compromised the quality of life for other family members, especially siblings of the patient, who often endured material deprivation and emotional neglect.

We used to buy things freely. Now, except for the sick child, we don’t buy anything unless it’s necessary. (P13)

We used to live comfortably, but now we save wherever we can. (P8)

We can hardly provide for our daughter—food, clothes, everything is minimized. She’s clearly affected. (P4)

Seeking additional income

To maintain basic financial stability, some parents sought flexible work near hospitals. Retired grandparents returned to work or took on physically demanding tasks, while parents engaged in informal jobs such as food delivery to support ongoing treatment.

I’m an only child. After our child got sick, his retired grandparents went back to work—his grandmother chopped firewood, and his grandfather drove and carried goods. His father started delivering food near the hospital to stay close and earn money. (P3)

We used to work on construction sites. After she got sick, I stayed home to care for her, and my husband stopped working too. Now he delivers food to make ends meet. (P12)

Discussion

We conducted qualitative interviews to investigate the financial toxicity experienced by parents of children with cancer in China and the coping strategies they employed throughout the treatment journey. Our findings indicated that financial toxicity in this population is both multifaceted and profound, described by parents across several areas of their lives, including disease-specific factors, household economic resilience, the adequacy of social support networks, and structural limitations in health insurance coverage [11]. While some of these patterns resembled what has been reported in studies involving adult cancer populations, this study also highlighted experiences that appear unique to the pediatric context—particularly the fact that children were not financially responsible for themselves, thereby transferring the entirety of economic stress to their parents. These findings contributed to a more nuanced understanding of financial toxicity in pediatric oncology and underscored the need for comprehensive, child-centered support mechanisms.

Specifically, financial toxicity in parents of children with cancer arises from diverse sources, such as the complexity and duration of treatment, limited household income, insufficient or temporary social support, and gaps in public insurance coverage [12]. The absence of financial independence among pediatric patients places the full burden of costs on parents—an issue particularly pronounced in single-parent households and those with weak economic foundations [13, 14]. Within traditional Chinese family structures, grandparents sometimes assume caregiving and financial responsibilities, which may temporarily alleviate stress but can also exacerbate burdens when elder caregivers face health problems themselves [15, 16]. In addition, current insurance schemes often fail to cover high-cost therapies such as proton or immunotherapy, leaving parents with substantial out-of-pocket expenses [17]. For those seeking care outside their home province, complex reimbursement procedures and limited cross-regional compensation further intensify financial strain [18]. Commercial insurance offers partial relief but remains inaccessible to many low- and middle-income parents due to high premiums, thereby widening existing disparities in healthcare protection [19]. These findings suggest the need for policy action—specifically, expanding insurance coverage for pediatric oncology, simplifying interprovincial reimbursement mechanisms, promoting inclusive commercial insurance options, and enhancing sustainable social support systems to alleviate long-term financial burdens.

The impacts of financial toxicity were both severe and multidimensional. Economically, parents face substantial risks, including asset depletion, rising debt, and even bankruptcy, as parents often lose income due to long-term caregiving responsibilities. These effects extend beyond the patient, disrupting the daily lives and developmental opportunities of other family members, particularly siblings [20]. Critically, the financial toxicity associated with pediatric cancer is not a one-time crisis but a prolonged and cumulative hardship that can persist for years. Treatment decisions frequently involve difficult trade-offs between pursuing the best possible care “at any cost” and adhering to what is financially sustainable. Even parents with relatively stable income may experience distress if the cost of treatment consumes a disproportionate share of disposable income, reflecting the importance of evaluating affordability in relative, not absolute, terms [12].

Financial toxicity also placed a heavy psychological toll on parents, many of whom reported symptoms of anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal. These psychological burdens arise not only from economic hardship but also from prolonged emotional stress, inadequate institutional support, and the moral distress of choosing between the needs of the sick child and the well-being of the rest of the family [21]. Feelings of guilt, helplessness, and uncertainty about the future are particularly acute among single parents or those in economically fragile households [22]. Social stigma and limited public awareness further exacerbate the sense of isolation. Moreover, the emotional consequences of financial toxicity tend to be long-lasting and often concealed, potentially affecting both the mental health of parents and the stability of the family as a whole [23].

In response to financial toxicity, parents typically adopt passive and short-term coping strategies. These included borrowing money, selling assets, cutting back on essential household expenditures, and engaging in informal or flexible labor near hospitals [24]. While these measures may offer temporary relief, they are often unsustainable and may compromise long-term financial security. Borrowing from relatives or social networks may strain interpersonal relationships, while asset liquidation diminishes future economic resilience [25]. Reducing expenditures frequently leads to the sacrifice of basic needs for other family members. Some parents take on gig work to generate supplementary income, but these jobs tend to be low-paying, physically demanding, and unstable [11, 26]. Compared with parents of adult cancer patients, those caring for pediatric patients demonstrate a more passive pattern of response, often due to a lack of preparedness and limited institutional support. These patterns underscore systemic gaps in economic assistance and preventative mechanisms within the current healthcare framework [27].

Addressing the multifaceted nature of financial toxicity may require consideration of comprehensive and targeted policy interventions. Expanding health insurance coverage for pediatric cancer treatment, simplifying reimbursement processes for cross-provincial medical care, and promoting affordable and accessible commercial insurance are essential steps [28]. Additionally, long-term social support systems—including psychological counseling, peer support programs, and dedicated financial aid—should be strengthened to help parents move from passive coping to proactive resilience [29]. Such interventions not only alleviate the immediate financial pressures but also contribute to the long-term stability and well-being of both parents and patients.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it draws on a geographically diverse sample of participants from across China, which enhances the richness and representativeness of the data. Such diversity supports the generalizability of the findings to a broader population of parents affected by pediatric cancer. Second, the study adopts a multidimensional perspective, examining not only the economic burden but also the psychological consequences and treatment-related decision-making, thereby providing a comprehensive understanding of the lived experiences associated with financial toxicity. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. Because it relied on semi-structured interviews, the findings may reflect some degree of researcher subjectivity during questioning and interpretation. Although efforts were made to ensure consistency and neutrality, some degree of interviewer bias may still exist [30]. In addition, participants’ self-reported narratives may be affected by recall bias or emotional framing, particularly given the sensitive and stressful nature of the topic. The study was conducted in three hospitals within Shandong Province, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare systems. Finally, while this qualitative study provides valuable insights into the lived experiences of parents affected by pediatric cancer, it does not quantify the prevalence or severity of financial toxicity, highlighting the need for future research using mixed-methods designs to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant financial toxicity faced by parents of pediatric cancer patients in China, which compromises treatment decisions, quality of life, and mental health. High medical costs, inadequate insurance, and limited social support intensify this burden. Targeted policies and support systems should be developed to alleviate these pressures. Future research should explore disparities across socioeconomic groups to inform more equitable interventions.
