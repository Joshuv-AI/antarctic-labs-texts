Development and internal validation of a prediction model for sleep apnea syndrome treated with continuous positive airway pressure based on claims and health checkup data linked to personal health records
Authors: Tatsuya Muraki, Tsuyoshi Ueda, Chihiro Hasegawa, Hiroshi Usui, Hiroshi Koshimizu, Kenichi Ariyada, Kunio Kusajima, Yasuhiro Tomita, Masashi Yanagisawa, Masao Iwagami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo develop and validate a prediction model for sleep apnea syndrome (SAS) treated with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) in the general population.MethodsUsing claims and health checkup data held by JMDC Inc. linked to personal health records (Pep Up), we developed and internally validated a prediction model for SAS treated with CPAP, defined as a SAS diagnosis and reimbursement records of CPAP. Every 3 months from January 1, 2022 to July 1, 2024 (11 timepoints), we identified eligible individuals with available data both 1 year before and after that timepoint to define the presence/absence of SAS treated with CPAP, along with 279 predictor variables. We developed a LightGBM model for the training and tuning datasets and evaluated its performance on the validation dataset.ResultsOverall, 18,692,873 observations (mean age: 44.8 ± 11.3 years; female, 37.5%) were obtained from 1,858,566 individuals; of these observations, 300,868 observations (1.6%) were found to have SAS treated with CPAP. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.898 (95% confidence interval 0.895–0.901). The positive predictive values among observations in the top 1% and 10% of predicted risk were 28.3% and 10.3%, respectively. According to the SHapley Additive exPlanations plots, male sex was the most important predictor, followed by age, body mass index, and waist circumference. Moreover, personal health records significantly improved the predictive performance.ConclusionWe developed a prediction model to identify individuals at high risk of SAS treated with CPAP and encourage them to undergo polysomnography or related tests.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11325-026-03725-9.Keywords: Sleep apnea syndrome, Continuous positive airway pressure, Obstructive sleep apnea, Prediction model, Personal health records

Introduction

Sleep apnea syndrome (SAS), mainly obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), is a common disease [1–3]. For example, the estimated prevalence of SAS, defined as apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) ≥ 15, is around 9.0% in male and 2.8% in female individuals in Japan [4]. SAS is associated with a variety of morbidities and mortalities [5–7]. Thus, early detection and intervention are important for improving patient prognosis [8–10]. However, many individuals with SAS remain undiagnosed [11–14]. This may be because they find it difficult to recognize the condition themselves. Even if they have symptoms such as daytime sleepiness, their capacity for and access to polysomnography (PSG) are limited in most countries, including Japan, preventing timely diagnosis and treatment, such as continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) [14–16].

Therefore, identifying or predicting individuals at high risk of SAS from any available health information is warranted to encourage them to undergo PSG or related tests, such as a pulse oximeter and an airflow sensor at home [17] or portable encephalography [18]. Many diagnostic prediction models for SAS or OSA have been developed and validated based on electronic health records, claims data, and/or personal health records (PHRs) obtained from mobile apps and wearable or home medical devices [19–22].

However, in most previous studies, the study population consisted of individuals receiving PSG or related tests [19–22]. Consequently, the sample size was often small. Moreover, the study populations did not represent the general population, and the established models cannot be used to suggest who should be prioritized for PSG or related tests. For an SAS prediction model to be widely used in the community, the study population should be broadly representative. Further, predictor variables should ideally be obtained from routinely collected health data, such as medical claims and PHRs.

In Japan, JMDC Inc. has collected medical claims data and health checkup data of individuals with employee insurance since 2005. Further, PHRs from mobile apps and wearable or home medical devices have recently been linked from willing members within the system of “Pep Up” [23, 24]. Using these data, we aimed to develop and internally validate a prediction model for SAS treated with CPAP in the general population.

Methods

Data source

This study utilized data obtained from Pep Up [23, 24], an information and communications technology service provided by JMDC Inc. for society-managed health insurance members, based on the JMDC payer database [25]. As of July 2025, there were approximately two million Pep Up users.

Informed consent was obtained from individuals in the Pep Up system to use their lifelog data (as detailed below), claims data, and annual health checkup results in the JMDC payer database for research. Under the universal insurance system in Japan, claims data include diagnoses (Japanese diagnostic codes, corresponding to International Classification of Disease-10 [ICD-10] codes), prescriptions and dispensations (Japanese drug codes, corresponding to the World Health Organization-Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical [WHO-ATC] codes), and Japanese procedure codes. In this system, doctors or medical staff may sometimes first designate a diagnosis as “suspected.” This flag may later be removed in some cases and not in others i.e., they remain in the system as “suspected” diagnoses.

The data were pseudonymized and processed by JMDC Inc.

Study design and population

This study aimed to establish a cross-sectional “diagnostic” prediction model for the presence or absence of SAS treated with CPAP, in contrast to a “prognostic” prediction model for the future SAS incidence [26]. However, because of the nature of routinely collected real-world data, a certain span (instead of a single day) is required for health information to be recorded and used to define the presence or absence of outcome and predictor variables. Therefore, we first determined the index dates for prediction, such as January 1, 2022 (Fig. 1). We used information recorded both 1 year before and after the index date to define the outcome. Thus, for this index date, we identified and included individuals registered with Pep Up during the entire period of 2 years. We repeated this process every 3 months from January 1, 2022 to July 1, 2024 (11 timepoints) to create the analysis dataset. Thus, each person contributed to the analysis dataset from a minimum of once up to a maximum of 11 times.

Outcome

The outcome was SAS treated with CPAP, defined as a diagnosis (excluding “suspected” diagnoses) of SAS, recorded as Japanese diagnostic codes corresponding to ICD-10 code G47.3, and use of CPAP, recorded as Japanese procedure codes C107-2, C171-2, and C165 in Japanese insurance claims during the 2 years. Notably, in Japan, CPAP for SAS is covered by health insurance and is reimbursed if the AHI is ≥ 20 by PSG or is ≥ 40 by a simple test using a pulse oximeter and airflow sensor at home [27]. The government constantly monitors whether an indication is appropriate to prevent fraudulent or false claims, meaning that the specificity of CPAP for SAS is expected to be extremely high.

Predictor variables

Using the claims data and annual health checkup results in the JMDC payer database and the PHRs in Pep Up, we defined 279 predictor variables (Online Resource 1). These included the following: sex and age at the index date; 50 disease diagnoses in the medical claims data, for which the number of months during the 1 year before the index month (not including the index month itself) was counted, providing a number from 0 to 12; 94 types of drug prescriptions (corresponding to WHO-ATC 3-digit codes) in the medical claims data, for which the number of months during the 1 year before the index month (not including the index month itself) was counted, providing a number from 0 to 12; health checkup results (23 variables) at the closest day before the index date during the 2 years before the index date; lifelog data (110 variables) recorded in Pep Up, linked to devices including home blood-pressure monitors, smartwatches, and body composition monitors, or manual inputs by the users, from 90 days to 1 day before the index date.

Statistical analysis

After summarizing the distribution of the predictor and outcome variables in the analysis dataset, we randomly split the participants (regardless of number of contributions to the analysis dataset) into training, tuning, and validation datasets in a ratio of 6:2:2. The split was performed at the person level, meaning that observations (records) from the same person belonged only to the training, tuning, and validation datasets. We then developed a prediction model in the training and tuning datasets and assessed its predictive performance in the validation dataset.

The model was created using LightGBM, a gradient-boosting decision tree, owing to its high predictive performance and computational efficiency [28]. We used log-loss as the loss function. Hyperparameters were determined via a grid search to minimize log loss in the tuning data. The search ranges were set as num_leaves = 32, 128, and 512; min_child_samples = 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000; and feature_fraction = 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0. The n_estimators were determined by early stopping. To ensure clinical interpretability, we applied monotonic constraints to the predictor variables (Online Resource 1). Missing values were input into the LightGBM model. In tree-based algorithms, the model learns the optimal branch directions to handle missing values during training.

As a metric of model performance, we estimated the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Additionally, we estimated the positive predictive value (PPV), which is the proportion of observations with SAS treated with CPAP (i.e., true cases) among observations in the top 1% and 10% of predicted risk, respectively. Finally, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) function was used to interpret the model via a SHAP plot (beeswarm plot) of the top 20 predictor variables [29]. This plot visualizes the distribution of the SHAP values for each feature, demonstrating the magnitude and direction of their contribution to the model output on the log scale.

A sex-stratified subgroup analysis was conducted. As a sensitivity analysis, we first assessed the predictive ability of the model in the validation datasets split at 11 timepoints (Fig. 1) separately to confirm that prediction ability is not affected by year or season. Second, we developed and validated a prognostic prediction model (in contrast to a diagnostic prediction model in our main analysis) for the new incidence of SAS treated with CPAP within 1 year after the index date. We excluded individuals with any SAS diagnosis (i.e., both confirmed and “suspected” SAS diagnosis, with or without CPAP) during the 1 year before the index date to avoid any leakages in prediction.

As an additional analysis, to quantify the contribution of PHRs in Pep Up (110 variables) to the prediction, we compared the original model based on all 279 predictor variables and the model without PHRs in Pep Up in terms of AUROC. We also estimated the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), which is the improvement in the differences between the average predicted probabilities of cases and non-cases [30]. For this purpose, we restricted the study population to (i) individuals with at least 1 non-missing variable (among 110 variables) in PHRs, suggesting that they are “light users” of Pep Up, and (ii) individuals with ≥ 30-day recordings (among 90 days before the index date) of systolic and diastolic blood pressure, resting heart rate, body weight, body fat percentage, steps, and sleep duration, suggesting that they are “heavy users” of Pep Up.

All data management and statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12.9).

Results

From January 1, 2022 to July 1, 2024, 1,858,566 individuals contributed to the analysis dataset at least once and a maximum of 11 times, resulting in 18,692,873 observations. Their mean age was 44.8 (standard deviation: 11.3) years, and the proportion of female sex was 37.5%. The distribution of the predictor variables is shown in Online Resource 2.

Of the 18,692,873 observations, 300,868 observations (1.6%) were found to have the outcome (i.e., SAS treated with CPAP). The prevalences according to sex, age, and body mass index (BMI) are shown in Fig. 2. The AUROC was estimated as 0.898 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.895–0.901; Fig. 3). The PPVs among observations in the top 1% and 10% of predicted risk were 28.3% and 10.3%, respectively. According to the SHAP plot, male sex was the most important predictor, followed by age, BMI, and waist circumference (Fig. 4).

In the subgroup analysis, the prevalences of SAS treated with CPAP were 2.4% and 0.2% in male and female observations, respectively; the corresponding AUROCs were 0.860 (95% CI 0.856–0.864) and 0.906 (95% CI 0.890–0.922). The PPVs among observations in the top 1% and 10% of predicted risk were 32.7% and 13.3% in male and 7.2% and 1.7% in female observations, respectively. The SHAP plots are shown in Online Resource 3.

In the first sensitivity analysis, separate validation datasets at 11 timepoints yielded almost the same AUROC (Online Resource 4). In the second sensitivity analysis to develop a prognostic prediction model for the new incidence of SAS treated with CPAP, among 18,326,135 observations, 39,906 observations (0.22%) were found to have new SAS treated with CPAP during the 1 year after the index date. The AUROC was 0.845 (95% CI 0.838–0.851).

Among 2,300,547 observations obtained from 460,632 light users of Pep Up (individuals with at least 1 non-missing variable among 110 variables in PHRs), 42,076 observations (1.8%) were found to have SAS treated with CPAP. The AUROCs were 0.896 (95% CI 0.888–0.903) and 0.895 (95% CI 0.887–0.902) in the models with and without PHRs in Pep Up, respectively. Thus, the use of PHRs in Pep Up increased the AUROC by 0.001 (95% CI 0.0003–0.002). The IDI was 1.07% (95% CI 0.93–1.22), implying that the prediction model improved by 1.07% using PHRs in Pep Up. The top 20 ranking of the SHAP plot was generally similar to that of the original model in the main analysis, but the mean sleep duration during the three months recorded in Pep Up ranked at 14th (Fig. 5).

Among the 8,865 observations obtained from 2,441 heavy users of Pep Up (individuals with ≥ 30-day recordings of systolic and diastolic blood pressure, resting heart rate, body weight, body fat percentage, steps, and sleep duration), 491 observations (5.5%) were found to have SAS treated with CPAP. The AUROCs were 0.836 (95% CI 0.684–0.938) and 0.826 (95% CI 0.670–0.935) in the models with and without PHRs, respectively. Thus, the use of PHRs in Pep Up increased the AUROC by 0.010 (95% CI -0.004–0.024). The IDI was 4.57% (95% CI 2.22–6.67), implying that the prediction model improved by 4.57% using PHRs in Pep Up. Regarding the SHAP plot, 5 predictor variables based on PHRs in Pep Up were ranked between 9th and 17th (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Using claims and health checkup data linked to PHRs from mobile apps and wearable or home medical devices, we developed and internally validated a prediction model for SAS treated with CPAP. The model demonstrated a high predictive performance, with an AUROC of 0.898. In our sensitivity analysis at 11 separate timepoints, prediction ability was not affected by year or season. Although the prevalence of SAS treated with CPAP was 1.6% in the present study, the PPVs among observations in the top 1% and 10% of predicted risk were 28.3% and 10.3%, respectively, indicating that approximately 3 and 1 in 10 individuals would have SAS treated with CPAP, respectively. Considering the limited availability and high cost of inpatient PSG in many countries, including Japan [14–16], it seems efficient to encourage individuals with relatively high prediction scores to undergo PSG or related tests, such as a pulse oximeter and an airflow sensor at home [17] or portable encephalography [18].

Identifying patients with SAS through routine clinical practice or health checkups remains challenging. OSA is frequently underrecognized in primary care, largely because its cardinal symptoms, such as fatigue, insomnia, and uncontrolled hypertension, are non-specific and easily attributed to coexisting chronic conditions. Even patients who regularly visit primary care clinics may remain undiagnosed despite exhibiting a high pre-test probability of OSA [31]. In this context, a population-level prediction model that leverages routinely collected health data can provide valuable support for early diagnosis and timely referral within primary care workflows.

Well-known risk factors for SAS including male sex, older age, higher BMI, and higher waist circumference [19–22], were good predictors in our model. This study also quantified the contribution of PHRs to prediction in addition to claims data and health checkup results. Among light users of Pep Up, the improvements in AUROC and IDI were not large, though they were statistically significant. Only mean sleep duration during the 3 months was ranked among the top 20 predictor variables in terms of SHAP values. However, among heavy users of Pep Up, the improvements in AUROC and IDI became larger, and five predictor variables obtained from PHRs ranked. This indicates that the contribution of PHRs to the prediction of SAS treated with CPAP can be larger if users of PHR services use them more consistently. PHRs are a relatively new service, and the number of both light and heavy users of Pep Up has gradually increased. Further assessment is warranted after more PHRs accumulate in the Pep Up system.

More than 80% of individuals with moderate-to-severe OSA in the community are estimated to remain undiagnosed [32], underscoring the limitations of relying solely on sleep specialty clinics for case detection. A key strength of the present model is its ability to stratify risk using only health checkups and claims data, without imposing additional testing burdens. This makes it particularly suitable for large-scale implementation in corporate and municipal health programs. Moreover, integrating PHR data enables the model to extend its utility to individuals with limited access to outpatient care, including those receiving home-based medical services. This enhances opportunities to remotely identify individuals with potential OSA in underserved populations.

This study has some limitations. First, the outcome of the study was SAS treated with CPAP, which is the most severe form of SAS. Indeed, the prevalence of SAS treated with CPAP in the present study was only 1.6% (2.4% and 0.2% in male and female observations, respectively), lower than the expected prevalence of any SAS type in the community. For example, the prevalence of SAS defined as AHI ≥ 15 is estimated to be around 9.0% in male and 2.8% in female individuals [4]. The present study indicates that only a small proportion of individuals with SAS have access to CPAP treatment, particularly female individuals. It is possible that some individuals without SAS treated with CPAP in the study population had undiagnosed or untreated SAS. As individuals with higher healthcare access are probably more likely to be diagnosed as having SAS and are treated by CPAP, the prediction ability of our model including variables associated with health care access in claims data might have been overestimated. We expect that risk factors, and therefore, predictive models, would overlap between severe SAS and any SAS including undiagnosed or untreated SAS. However, a different study may be needed to test our model performance against any SAS. Notably, this study suggests that the underdiagnosis of SAS was more severe in female than in male individuals. This may have resulted in male sex being a dominant predictor. To avoid bias arising from such an algorithmic fairness, using sex-specific models may be recommended in real-world practice, given that AUROCs were still high enough in male (0.860 [95% CI 0.856–0.864]) and female participants (0.906 [95% CI 0.890–0.922]), respectively, in our subgroup analysis. Second, despite being drawn from the general population, Pep Up users mainly consisted of company employees and their family members, who may be more health-conscious than non-users or the average Japanese population. Thus, the generalizability of these findings to other populations in Japan remains unknown. Similarly, the applicability of these findings to populations in other countries should be carefully examined. Third, there is still room for improvement of our prediction model, by including additional variables such as body position on the bed, which is known to affect AHI [33]. Finally, there are many missing values for lifelog data among the overall studied population. This is because not every Pep Up user used devices, including home blood-pressure monitors, smartwatches, and body composition monitors. PHRs significantly contributed to the prediction model in light and heavy users of Pep Up, yet it is expected that more lifelog data will accumulate in the Pep Up system.

OSA represents a substantial public health burden [32], and significant disparities in access to sleep medicine services have been well documented. A recent analysis in the US estimated that approximately 45.8 million individuals reside in areas with limited access to sleep healthcare [34]. By integrating PHRs, health checkup data, and claims data, the proposed prediction model may mitigate such disparities by identifying high-risk individuals earlier before they reach specialized care. This has important implications not only for clinical practice but also for community health initiatives, occupational health programs, and regional public health planning. We expect that using PHRs for “nudging” or encouraging medical tests within an automated system can generally contribute to more timely diagnosis and treatment of SAS, reducing the disease burden in the community. However, it should also be carefully monitored whether these new technologies create a disparity between those who are more tech-savvy, relative to those who are not.

In conclusion, we developed a prediction model for SAS treated with CPAP based on claims data and health checkup data linked to PHRs, showing good predictive ability. This model could be useful to provide feedback and encourage individuals with high prediction scores to receive PSG or related tests for definitive diagnosis and treatment.
