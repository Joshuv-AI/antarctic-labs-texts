Effectiveness of a novel pork belly simulation model versus traditional training for ultrasound-guided TAP block in anesthesiology residents
Authors: Sipei Cheng, Helin Zeng, Yanming Kang, Lan Wu, Shouming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe developed a novel, low-cost pork belly model for simulation training in ultrasound-guided transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block among anesthesiology residents. This study aimed to investigate its effectiveness in clinical teaching.MethodsThirty anesthesiology residents underwent training for ultrasound-guided TAP block. They were allocated to a traditional training group (Group A, n = 15) or a pork belly model simulation training group (Group B, n = 15). Residents in both Group A and Group B received identical theoretical training on ultrasound-guided TAP block. However, residents in Group B additionally underwent simulation training using a pork belly model. Subsequently, all residents performed two TAP block procedures on surgical patients under the supervision of clinical instructors. Finally, each resident was required to independently perform one TAP block procedure, and their entire operational process was evaluated using a standardized scoring scale by instructors who were unaware of the group assignments. Additionally, before and after the training session, all residents were required to complete a subjective questionnaire using a Likert scale to self-assess their general knowledge of abdominal ultrasound, their understanding of TAP blocks, and their confidence in performing TAP blocks.ResultsGroup B demonstrated significant advantages over Group A, especially in objective performance measures.The standardized scoring results, which revealed that Group B residents exhibited superior ultrasound image quality (p < 0.001) and needle visualization (p < 0.001). Moreover, Group B achieved significantly shorter procedure times (201s vs. 276s, p = 0.002) and a notably higher success rate for independently performing the TAP block procedure (93.3% vs. 26.7%, p < 0.001). For the subjective self-assessment items, Group B showed notable within-group improvements in some areas (abdominal image discrimination, needle guidance, injectate spread assessment; overall confidence, all p < 0.002083), while Group A showed no significant change except in abdominal image discrimination after training. However, the between-group differences did not reach statistical significance after correction; only abdominal image discrimination remained significant after correction (p < 0.002083).ConclusionsIntegrating this novel pork belly model into a simulation‑based training strategy is associated with improved technical proficiency and success rate of ultrasound-guided TAP block among resident physicians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09546-4.

Introduction

The transverse abdominal plane (TAP) block under ultrasound guidance has become a commonly used method for postoperative pain management in abdominal surgery, significantly reducing the level of postoperative pain [1]. However, ultrasound visualisation technology remains a critical and challenging focus in anesthesiology residency training. The conventional didactic model of “see one, do one, teach one” is not only time-consuming but also frequently leads to suboptimal training results [2].

Simulation training serves as a highly effective method for teaching ultrasound - guided regional anaesthesia [3]. Although numerous simulation-based training courses are currently available, most are limited to having students identify neurosonographic anatomy on volunteers [4] without allowing actual hands-on needle manipulation. In actual procedures, however, challenges for novice learners lie in how to find the needle tip and maintain a complete view of the needle trajectory. Furthermore, essential skills such as probe sliding, tilting, and rocking during needle advancement, as well as the ability of hand-eye coordination, cannot be adequately replicated through such simulation-based training. A variety of models have been developed to simulate regional nerve block training, including gelatin-based phantoms, hydrogel models, and water-based setups [5]. However, the TAP block remains difficult to replicate realistically. Park et al. [6] reported using an anaesthetised porcine model for ultrasound-guided TAP block simulation. Although the effect of training is effective, it requires specialised equipment and a place to anaesthetise the piglets, which involves considerable expense. In a similar context, Chuan et al. [7] used segmented fresh-frozen cadaveric specimens in the simulation-based nerve block courses. Despite the anatomical fidelity offered, this method may evoke discomfort among trainees and poses challenges regarding the repeatability and preservation of specimens.

We have developed a novel, high-fidelity, low-cost, easy-to-access, and store pork model for simulating TAP blocks. The model consists of a full section of pork belly (approximately 15 × 10 cm), which includes skin, subcutaneous fat, external oblique, internal oblique, and transversus abdominis muscles, providing tissue imaging characteristics highly close to those of humans. If the pig skin is relatively thick, subcutaneous tissue may be difficult to visualize under ultrasound, so the skin can be removed; alternatively, it is also possible to just turn the internal to outward and not remove the skin, which does not affect the simulation training and still provides comparable ultrasound images [8]. Storable at 4 °C, the model supports repeatedly puncture practice at multiple sites, thereby effectively enhancing trainees’ ultrasonographic manipulation skills and hand-eye coordination.

Despite the advantages of the pork model, its training efficacy remains unproven. We therefore conducted a trial to assess whether it enhances ultrasound‑guided TAP block training.

Methods

Study participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University. From May 2023 to May 2024, a total of 32 anesthesiology residents with no prior experience in ultrasound-guided TAP block or any other ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve block were assessed for eligibility. Among them, two refused to participate. The remaining 30 residents were randomly allocated through a computerised number generator to either the traditional training group (Group A) or the pork belly model simulation training group (Group B). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Ultrasound-guided TAP block training

All participants were required to complete a questionnaire before the TAP block training. An anesthesiologist provided a brief didactic session for both groups, covering fundamental ultrasound physics and the sonographic identification of abdominal wall muscles and fascial planes. Then the teacher explained the steps for performing a TAP block using a long-axis, in-plane approach. The teacher emphasised how to achieve hand-eye coordination, especially focusing on how to find the needle tip and maintain a clear visualisation of the entire needle trajectory during advancement.

Group A students transitioned to clinical practice after the didactic session. They were required to perform TAP blocks on the patient under the direct supervision of an attending anesthesiologist. The instructor would reiterate and demonstrate key procedural points at the bedside and intervene to take over if any difficulties or errors occurred. After completing two supervised cases, students were asked to perform the TAP block independently, with the instructor providing a score using a standardised feedback scoring system (Supplementary Table 1). The scoring system comprised the assessment of four key components: (1) ultrasound selection and materials preparation; (2) the quality of sonographic visualisation for the abdominal wall muscles; (3) the assessment of the needle trajectory and tip visibility; and (4) the level of operational proficiency, including procedure time and autonomous success rate.

Group B students practised the TAP block using the pork belly model, which provided clear sonographic identification of imaging characteristics highly close to those of humans. Besides, the model supported repeated puncture practice at multiple sites. Under the guidance of anesthesiology staff, Group B students performed a minimum of three simulated blocks. Feedback was provided throughout the training by the anesthesiology staff. After completing the simulation training, they also performed two TAP block procedures on surgical patients. Group B students were also evaluated by a standardised feedback scoring system when they performed TAP blocks on actual patients.

Self-assessment questionnaire

After the end of the course, students in both groups all finished the questionnaire again. The questionnaire aimed to assess their overall knowledge and confidence in performing the TAP block. According to a 5-point Likert scale, the students graded each of the following questions from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables and ordinal categorical variables (Likert scale items) were summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages. For between-group comparisons, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous and ordinal variables, and Fisher’s exact test was used for binary or unordered categorical variables. For within-group comparisons (pre- vs. post-training), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to the Likert scale scores. SPSS software, version 23.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA), was used for all analyses.

Bonferroni correction was applied to the 24 tests involving the eight Likert items (8 items × 3 comparisons in Table 2: Group A pre vs. post, Group B pre vs. post, and Group A post vs. Group B post). The corrected significance level was set at α = 0.05/24 ≈ 0.002083; only p-values below this threshold were considered statistically significant. For the six objective outcomes in Table 3, Bonferroni correction was similarly applied (α = 0.05/6 ≈ 0.00833).

Results

The remaining 30 participants were randomly allocated to Group A (traditional training, n = 15) or Group B (pork belly simulation training, n = 15). All 30 were included in the final analysis. (Fig. 1). There was no difference in gender between the two groups of residents, with an average age of 24 years and an average of 2 years of training in anesthesiology (Table 1).

Practical skills

Both groups demonstrated significant improvement in abdominal image identification (Group A: pre 3.0, post 4.0, p = 0.001; Group B: pre 3.0, post 5.0, p = 0.001), with Group B scoring higher than Group A after training (p = 0.002) (Table 2). Similarly, Group B significantly improved in needle guidance (pre 3.0, post 4.0, p = 0.001) and injectate spread assessment (pre 3.0, post 4.0, p = 0.001), Group A did not show such improvements in these areas (needle guidance p = 0.641; injectate spread p = 0.382). Although Group B scored higher than Group A post-training, the between-group differences post-training did not reach statistical significance after correction for needle guidance (p = 0.012) or injectate spread assessment (p = 0.037).

Self-confidence in performance

Regarding psychological state (Table 2), Group B showed a significant increase in overall confidence in performance TAP block (pre 3.0, post 4.0, p = 0.002), while Group A remained unchanged (p = 0.855); the between-group difference did not reach significance (p = 0.058). However, regarding anxiety levels, no statistically significant differences were observed within or between groups.

Standardized assessment of procedural competency

A standardized and objective scoring system for resident performance allows for a direct assessment of the comprehensive competency of residents in both groups during ultrasound-guided TAP block procedures (Table 3). In terms of equipment preparation, both groups achieved similar scores. However, Group B outperformed Group A significantly in image quality (p < 0.001) and needle guidance (p < 0.001). Furthermore, procedural proficiency was markedly higher in Group B compared to Group A (p < 0.001). Group B completed the TAP block procedure in significantly less time (201.0 s) than Group A (276.0 s) (p = 0.002). Most notably, the success rate was substantially higher in Group B (93.3%, 14/15) compared to Group A (26.7%, 4/15) (p < 0.001).

Discussion

Our study employed a randomized controlled design to systematically evaluate, for the first time, the significant advantage of the pork belly simulation model in developing and training students’ abilities for the TAP block. After rigorous multiple comparison correction (Bonferroni method), Group B outperformed Group A in core skills of TAP block, as Group B had a 27% reduction in procedure time and a 3.5-fold higher rate of independent procedural success. The objective score indices showed meaningful within-group improvements in subjective self-assessments. Students in Group B reported within-group improvements in practical skill self-ratings and confidence; however, after correcting for multiple comparisons, most between-group subjective differences were not statistically significant. It may suggest that pork belly simulation’s most robust effects are on objectively measured procedural competence.

The effectiveness of the pork belly model may be summarised as several key advantages. Its high fidelity offers sonographic characteristics close to human tissue, which is superior to some previously reported models used for ultrasound training, such as water-based phantoms, gelatin models, and animal carcasses [9–11]. Simultaneously, in contrast to specialised alternatives like anaesthetised porcine models [6] the pork belly model is low-cost and readily available, ensuring TAP block simulation training can be conducted regularly. Furthermore, the risk-free training environment supports repetitive practice without clinical pressures, aligning with established principles of skill acquisition [12]. The splitting modular training design also minimises cognitive load by isolating technical components, allowing focused refinement of fundamental skills before integration.

Regarding the observed decrease in equipment preparation self-ratings in Group A, this is not a sign of a decline in ability, but rather a result of a calibration of self-perception. In traditional clinical training, attending anesthesiologists often set up the ultrasound machine and prepare the equipment themselves out of considerations for efficiency and patient safety, while the residents focus mainly on needle manipulation and image identification. After observation of experienced operators from the sidelines, residents may realize that they were not as proficient in independent operation as they had imagined. Therefore, they gave more conservative post-training self-ratings. This is a normal psychological process of transitioning from “unconscious incompetence” to “conscious incompetence” in skill acquisition theory - overestimating oneself before training and realizing one’s shortcomings after training. In contrast, the pork belly model required residents to perform all steps independently, including equipment preparation. Repeated hands-on practice built their self-efficacy, which resulted in an improvement.

From a psychological perspective, Group B showed notable within-group improvements in overall confidence after training. The traditional “see-one, do-one, teach-one” approach still exposes novice trainees to large psychological pressure when performing procedures directly on the patient, despite being under the supervision of a teacher. Those experiences may adversely affect long-term learning and performance [13]. In contrast, by providing a low-risk learning setting, the pork belly model allows trainees to build self-efficacy through repetitive successful practice, which is critical for foundational skill development among early-stage learners [14].

These results indicate that the pork belly simulation-based training strategy can effectively improve the technical performance of the TAP block. It should be noted that the additional hands-on practice inherent to simulation likely contributed to the observed benefits, and the study does not isolate the effect of the model itself from that of increased practice volume. We propose a “simulation-first” educational framework wherein trainees acquire foundational operational skills using pork belly models prior to advancing to the clinical TAP block. This progressive may approach enhances both educational outcomes and patient safety.

Study limitations and future directions

This study has some limitations. A formal power analysis was not performed prior to initiating this study. The sample size was first decided by the number of eligible residents available during the recruitment period at a single center. Therefore, the sample size should be considered a feasibility sample. Despite the small sample size, we also observed significant differences between-groups (p < 0.00833) in core objective measures. Post-hoc power analysis indicated the power to detect a between-group difference exceeded 80%, with the current sample size (n = 15 per group). Nonetheless, these findings require confirmation in larger, multi-center studies.

An inherent limitation of this study is that the total training duration was not strictly matched between the two groups. Group B underwent at least three additional simulated puncture exercises compared to Group A, which may have partially amplified the intervention effect. Nevertheless, there is an inherent difference between the two teaching methods in the real-world. One of the fundamental distinctions between the traditional “see-one, do-one, teach-one” approach and simulation-based training is that the latter allows beginners to engage in repetitive, deliberate practice without clinical pressure. Future studies could employ a crossover design or include an active control group (e.g., equal-time training with a low-fidelity model) to further separate the contributions of practice volume versus model fidelity to learning outcomes.

We only conduct short-term follow-up, while the focus remained primarily on initial skill acquisition in novice trainees, long-term skill retention can also be assessed. Additionally, a systematic examination of how training intensity and frequency impact skill acquisition would help optimise educational efficiency.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a simulation-based training strategy incorporating the pork belly model is associated with improved technical proficiency and success rate among anesthesiology residents performing ultrasound-guided TAP blocks. Given the unequal training exposure between groups, the observed benefits cannot be attributed solely to the model itself but rather to the broader simulation-based strategy, which includes additional hands-on practice. Nevertheless, these findings support the educational value of integrating such a low-cost, high-fidelity model into a ‘simulation first’ curriculum.
