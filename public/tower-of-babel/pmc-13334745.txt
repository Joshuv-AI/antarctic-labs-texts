Shaping my own future: a latent profile analysis of how career resources influence future work self-salience among Chinese vocational students
Authors: Chen Sun, Hongyu Ma, Xiangping Zhan, Hanying Tang, Chunyan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFuture work self-salience is critical for vocational college students in China to navigate the school-to-work transition effectively. However, there is limited research on the distinct profiles of career resources that shape these students’agency in envisioning and pursuing their professional identities.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify latent profiles of vocational college students based on their configuration of career resources (i.e., psychological, identity, and social resources) and to examine differences in future work self-efficacy and the mediating role of personal growth initiative across the identified profiles.MethodsThe present study involved 1,093 first- and second-year vocational college students in China. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) was conducted in Mplus to classify career resource profiles. The BCH method was applied to examine differences in future work self-salience across identified subgroups. Finally, relative mediation effect analysis was performed to assess the mediating role of personal growth initiative.ResultsThe LPA results indicated that vocational college students can be classified into four distinct subgroups based on career resources: the deficient group, the relatively high external employability group, the sufficient group, and the balanced group. The sufficient group demonstrated the highest levels of personal growth, initiative, and future work self-salience. Moreover, personal growth initiative mediates the relationship between different subgroups and future work self-salience.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the active role individuals play in translating resources into a clearer future work self. Despite limitations, this study contributed to the literature by offering implications for supporting the developmental transition of vocational students entering the workforce.Keywords: Career resources, Future work self-salience, Personal growth initiative, Latent profiles analysis, Vocational college students

Introduction

The school-to-work transition (STWT) is a defining developmental milestone in emerging adulthood (Arnett 2000), representing the psychological and physical shift from education to the labor market (Blokker et al. 2023). This process generally comprises three stages: career planning, job search, and organizational entry. During the initial stage—while still in education—students begin forming their future work identities (Blustein 1994; Guan et al. 2017). Adopting the perspective of Strauss et al. (2012), this study defines work identity during STWT as the future work self—a mental representation of a desired professional identity (Fang & Saks 2022). Both theoretical and empirical research indicate that the motivational power of a future work self depends heavily on its salience (i.e., clarity, accessibility, and centrality) rather than merely its content (Oyserman 2015; Strauss et al. 2012). Navigating the uncertainties of the contemporary labor market and the complex challenges of the school-to-work transition (STWT) requires vocational college students to access a diverse array of career resources (Parola & Marcionetti 2025). This requirement is particularly critical in China, where the educational track bifurcates early, directing high achievers to universities and others to vocational colleges. Although essential to national development, these vocational students often confront unique psychological barriers—such as diminished self-expectations, transition anxiety, and a pervasive sense of diminished hope (Feng & Jiang 2016). These internal vulnerabilities can severely restrict their ability to envision a clear future work identity. In light of this context, the present research aims to understand how specific configurations of career resources empower vocational students to overcome these psychological obstacles and successfully chart their future careers.

Hirschi (2012) identified four core resources within the self-directed career management (SDCM) framework, which builds upon the work of Sullivan, Carden, and Martin (1998). SDCM outlines four essential resources for career development: human capital, social resources, psychological resources, and identity resources. Each resource is considered fundamental to career development (Ng & Feldman 2014a, 2014b). Importantly, to capture the unique realities of vocational college students facing the school-to-work transition, we chose four highly contextualized constructs: readiness for career transition to represent psychological resources, self-perceived employability to represent human capital resources, self-identity to represent identity resources, and career-related teacher support to represent social resources. These four constructs correspond directly to SDCM domains and form the foundation for students’ active career exploration, planning, and development (Hirschi 2012). Previous research has been extensively examined empirically. Existing literature provides substantial evidence that resources within these specific domains are linked to students’ future work self-salience (FWSS). Specifically, psychological and human capital resources, such as readiness for career transition and self-perceived employability, empower students with the confidence and cognitive preparedness needed to articulate a clear future career vision (Ma et al. 2024; Magnano et al. 2021; Peng & Zhang 2025). Furthermore, a well-developed self-identity serves as the cognitive anchor for crystallizing desired work roles (Strauss et al. 2012; Guan et al. 2017), while career-related teacher support provides the external guidance and validation that further sharpen this future work self (Kao et al. 2022; Parola et al. 2024; Zhang, Yuen, & Chen 2018; Zhang, Yu, & Wang 2025).

Although these studies establish an essential link between such resources and FWSS, most adopt a variable-centered perspective and often overlook a fundamental premise of the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory (Hobfoll 1989): resources rarely exist independently. Consequently, there is a pressing need for an integrative, person-centered perspective to capture how complex combinations of resources uniquely shape the future work self; yet, this critical line of inquiry remains largely unexplored. Furthermore, as posited by the Self-Directed Career Management (SDCM) framework (Hirschi 2012), the mere possession of resources does not guarantee their effective use; rather, students must actively engage in self-directed behaviors to leverage them. However, the specific proactive mechanisms—such as Personal Growth Initiative (PGI)—through which distinct resource profiles are mobilized and translated into future work self-salience remain insufficiently explored.

To systematically address these gaps, our study adopts a person-centered approach to answer two primary Research Questions (RQs):

RQ1: Do distinct latent subgroups (profiles) of career resources exist among vocational college students?

RQ2: How do these distinct profiles differ in their relation to personal growth initiative (PGI) and future work self-salience (FWSS)?

By addressing these questions, our study offers a nuanced understanding of how resources co-occur within individuals, providing new theoretical insights into the developmental mechanisms of vocational students and targeted practical guidance for educational interventions.

Literature review

Theoretical background

SDCM (Hirschi 2012) delineates four primary resource domains essential for career development: human capital (such as education, expertise, and information access), social resources (including parental support and mentoring), psychological resources (such as proactive personality, resilience, and emotions), and identity resources (including goal clarity and self-awareness). Conservation of resources (COR) theory challenges variable-centered approaches by asserting that resources cluster, interact, and produce synergistic effects within “resource caravans” (Hobfoll et al. 2018). Resources tend to reinforce one another, so the accumulation of one resource facilitates the acquisition of others (Hobfoll 2002). Empirical work indicates that these four resources are positively related and function as multi-component constructs (Hirschi, Niles, & Akos 2011). Examining how co-occurring resources form distinct latent profiles is therefore essential for understanding students’ developmental advantages (Antonio & Chiesa 2024; Parola & Marcionetti 2025). We selected self-identity, career readiness, self-perceived employability, and career-related teacher support for two reasons. First, they provide compact yet complete representations of the four SDCM domains, thereby avoiding redundancy while capturing all resource types. Second, unlike static traits, these measures reflect contextualized, “state-like” resources that are pertinent during vocational school-to-work transitions, summarize the specific developmental challenges vocational students face, and, most importantly, are malleable capacities that can be directly fostered through targeted educational interventions (Hofmann et al. 2014; Janeiro 2014; Koen et al. 2012).

While COR theory explains the structural aggregation of resources, the SDCM describes how those resources become dynamically activated. As Hirschi (2012) noted, merely possessing career resources is insufficient; they remain dormant until mobilized by self-directed action. Personal Growth Initiative (PGI)—conceived as intentional action toward self-change (Robitschek et al. 2012)—epitomizes the self-directed behaviors emphasized by SDCM. By actively engaging in PGI, students convert latent resource caravans into tangible career adaptability and, ultimately, a salient future work self (FWSS) as a concrete outcome of resource gain.

Above all, our study integrates the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory (Hobfoll 1989) and the Self-Directed Career Management (SDCM) framework (Hirschi 2012) to elucidate the mechanisms of career development among vocational college students. The following paragraphs provide more details on the career resources examined in this research.

Career resources within the SDCM framework

Self-identity

Self-identity is defined as the ongoing perception of one’s abilities, beliefs, and development status across time and contexts (Ochse & Plug 1986). It was selected as the representative factor of identity resources, given that vocational college students are in a critical stage of identity development, a period characterized by active exploration and commitment to personal roles (Arnett 2000; Erikson 1968). A positive self-perception not only supports but also reinforces the formation and consolidation of role-related identity, thereby facilitating adaptive psychosocial functioning (Deng et al. 2018; Robitschek & Cook 1999). At this stage, an individual’s ability to identify with their professional role directly influences the stability of their career choice and the clarity of their career objectives. Together, these elements form a critical internal psychological foundation that helps vocational college students navigate the inherent uncertainties of their future career paths.

Self-perceived employability

Employability concerns the extent to which individuals possess the requisite skills to secure and sustain desired employment. Self-perceived employability (SPE) is an individual’s cognitive assessment of their own employability, reflecting their subjective perception of the alignment between personal skills, experiences, and labor-market demands (Rothwell & Arnold 2007). It encompasses both internal and external facets: internal employability is individuals’ confidence in their ability to transition between careers, whereas external employability is their optimistic outlook on labor market prospects (Rothwell et al. 2008, 2009). Drawing on the classic articulation by Fugate et al. (2004), self-perceived employability functions as a vital social and human capital that enables individuals to navigate turbulent career transitions successfully. For vocational college students, this capacity is a crucial career resource. Viewed through Hirschi (2012), the external facet of SPE serves as a direct psychological proxy for human capital resources, capturing employers’ perceived currency of their technical training. Moreover, the internal facet of SPE, which captures confidence in navigating career transitions, aligns perfectly with the psychological resource domain. Previous research has indicated that future work self-salience among Chinese junior college students positively predicts self-perceived employability through career exploration. However, the potential reciprocal relationship between these constructs remains unexplored (Ma et al. 2024; Ma & Bennett 2021).

Readiness for career transition

Readiness for career transition is a critical psychological resource available to individuals during the STWT period (Magnano et al. 2021); it benefits not only those entering the workplace but also those considering future career changes. More recently, the National Association of Colleges and Employers stated that career readiness prepares people “for a successful transition into the workplace” (National Association of Colleges and Employers [NACE], 2021, p.1). Research indicates that providing career-oriented courses and implementing STEM project-based learning can significantly enhance students’ readiness for career transitions, thereby supporting them in making informed and effective career decisions and long-term plans (Mattern 2015; Stebleton et al. 2020).

Career-related teacher support

The Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT; Lent & Brown 1996; Lent et al. 1994, 2000) emphasizes the importance of external factors in career development, highlighting that teachers serve as critical influencers in students’ career development by providing role modeling, skill development opportunities, enhancing self-efficacy, and offering proximal support throughout the career exploration and decision-making process. Therefore, career-related teacher support is selected as the primary social resource for vocational college students during their academic tenure. Teacher support has been shown to enhance students’ confidence in completing career decision-making tasks, thereby promoting positive expectations regarding future career outcomes (Garcia et al. 2015). It also influences the career preparedness of diverse adolescent groups in urban areas of the United States (Perry et al. 2010). Moreover, a qualitative study conducted in secondary schools in Hong Kong, China, indicates that teachers play a pivotal role in helping students pursue their career aspirations (Wong et al. 2022). In sum, these four constructs of career resources do not operate in isolation; instead, they interact synergistically to constitute a dynamic support system that fosters a cumulative advantage, thereby generating sustained momentum for the career development of vocational college students.

The mediating role of personal growth initiative

The unpredictable and dynamic professional landscape requires vocational college students to move beyond merely possessing career resources and actively using them. According to Self-directed Career Management (SDCM), these resources must be intentionally managed through proactive behaviors (Hirschi 2012). Personal Growth Initiative (PGI) perfectly captures this proactive orientation. Defined as a conscious effort toward self-improvement (Robitschek 1998), PGI provides the essential competencies for growth. It includes knowing when to change (readiness), making adjustments (planfulness), seeking help (resource use), and taking action (intentional behavior) (Robitschek et al. 2012). In essence, PGI represents the active drive needed to navigate modern career transitions.

Grounded in the COR theory (Hobfoll et al. 2018), individuals use existing resources to pursue personal goals. When vocational students possess a strong structure of positive self-identity, transition readiness, perceived marketability, and strong support from their teachers, they are psychologically and structurally prepared to start self-improvement. Previous studies have confirmed that personal and social resources, such as psychological capital, self-esteem, hope, parenting styles, and social support, are significantly related to the level of personal growth initiative (Kwon & Yang 2020; Dordi & Purandare 2018; Hirata & Kamakura 2018; Cai & Lian 2022). With support from both internal and external sources, these resource caravans advance the goal-setting and systematic planning central to PGI. Once activated, this drive directly shapes students’ career management. From the SDCM perspective, crystallizing a future work self is not a passive outcome of resource possession but a direct reward of active career management. Empirical evidence consistently links high PGI to adaptive career behaviors, including clearer goal-setting, effective resource mobilization, and a greater willingness to embrace developmental challenges (Dweck 2012; Luyckx & Robitschek 2014; Sharma & Rani 2013; Shorey et al. 2007). For college populations in particular, this intentional engagement translates into highly tangible outcomes. Notably, PGI serves as a robust predictor of both career adaptability and the crystallization of a prominent future work self-salience (FWSS) (Li 2024; Zou 2022).

Therefore, combining COR and SDCM theories, we propose that personal growth initiative serves as a key mediating variable linking various latent profile types of career resources to future work self-salience.

Hypotheses and conceptual model

To our knowledge, previous studies have predominantly examined the impact of internal or external resources at the individual level on future work self-salience. In addition, existing scholarship has not yet fully addressed the relationship between career resources and future work self-salience through an integrative lens, leaving a critical gap in understanding how these factors interact to shape individuals’ career development, especially among vocational students in the Chinese context. To this end, the person-centered approach was considered the most appropriate methodological approach, particularly given the increasing individualization of the career process. This approach enables the identification of profiles that differ both quantitatively (in the level of dimensions) and qualitatively (in profile configuration) (Spurk et al. 2020). Whereas the variable-centered approach assumes uniform manifestations of resources and personal adjustment across individuals, the person-centered approach allows that these resources may emerge in distinct configurations.

Hence, our study aims to conduct a latent profile analysis (LPA) of career resources and to examine the psychological mechanisms that may mediate the relationship between distinct latent classes of career resources and future work self-salience within Hirschi’s (2012) integrated career resource framework. The reason for using LPA to classify types is that it is a person-centered analysis method that addresses the limitations of traditional variable-centered statistical research and reduces the researcher’s subjective judgment compared to cluster analysis (Vermunt & Magidson 2002). Method

Participants and procedure

In April 2025, an online questionnaire was administered to first- and second-year students at a vocational college in JX Province using cluster sampling, yielding a representative sample of the target population. This vocational college ranks among the upper tier of the 1544 Chinese vocational colleges in terms of size, academic quality, and career education. All participants were enrolled voluntarily and informed about the study’s purpose, the confidentiality of their data, and their right to withdraw at any time.

Data were collected anonymously via a paper self-report questionnaire; completion took approximately 10–15 min. The online questionnaire instructions require respondents to provide answers based on their genuine feelings or actual behaviors. Regarding rules of thumb, the simulation study by Nylund et al. (2007) concluded that a minimum sample size of approximately 500 is sufficient to identify the correct number of latent profiles accurately. One week after the distribution, a total of 1,211 questionnaires were collected. Following a rigorous screening process based on predefined criteria—namely, insufficient response time, repetitive answering patterns, and duplicate submissions from the same participants—118 invalid responses were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 1,093 valid questionnaires and an effective response rate of 90.3%.

The demographic characteristics of the final sample were as follows: participants’ gender ratio was 1:1.06, with 530 males and 563 females. The distribution across grades was as follows: 1 st grade = 791, 2nd grade = 302. The distribution of students by part-time work experience status was: Yes = 773; No = 320. The Ethics Review Committee of Central China Normal University approved this study. All participants provided informed consent, and the collected data were anonymized and handled in strict accordance with confidentiality protocols.

Measures

To ensure the reliability and validity of the scales, we employed the “translation-back” translation method (Brislin 1970). We used validated measures from authoritative Chinese and international journals to adapt the original scale accurately.

Self-identity

We measured self-identity using a 19-item scale (Ochse & Plug 1986). Sample questions include “I wonder what sort of person I really am,” rated from 1 (never) to 4 (very often). We reversed the scores for 12 items and then summed them. Higher scores indicate a stronger sense of self; lower scores indicate someone is still forming their identity. The scale was reliable (Cronbach’s α = 0.76) and matched our data well: χ2/df = 3.31, RMSEA = 0.05, CFI = 0.90, TLI = 0.90, SRMR = 0.06.

Readiness for school-to-work transition

We used the 13-item readiness subscale of the 40-item “Career Transitions Inventory Scale” (Heppner et al. 1994) to measure readiness for the school-to-work transition. Each item was rated on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). An item sample included “I feel as though I have a driving force within me to work on my career (school-to-work) transition right now”. In this study, the scale demonstrated acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.94) and a satisfactory model fit: χ2/df = 3.97, CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.04, SRMR = 0.03.

Self-perceived employability

We measured self-perceived employability using a scale developed by Rothwell et al. (2008). This 16-item instrument employs a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree) to measure two components: self-perceived external employability (10 items, e.g., “People in the career I am aiming for are in high demand in the external labor market”) and self-perceived internal employability (6 items, e.g., “I am generally confident of success in job interviews and selection events”). This measure has shown sufficient reliability in studies of Chinese university students (e.g., Cheung & Cheung 2018; Xie et al. 2017). In this study, Cronbach’s α for the two subscales was 0.86 and 0.94. The total scale showed a good two-factor model fit: χ2/df = 4.79, RMSEA = 0.06, CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.92, SRMR = 0.04, and Cronbach’s α = 0.95.

Career-related teacher support

We measured career-related teacher support using a scale developed by Zhang et al. (2021) comprising 16 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (never) to 5 (always). These items are distributed across three dimensions: enhancement of self-exploration (6 items; e.g., “Help me identify my strengths and weaknesses”), informational support (5 items; e.g., “Explain to me the prospects of my profession”), and emotional support (5 items; e.g., “Hope that I can have good career prospects”). In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for these three subscales were 0.91, 0.95, and 0.93, respectively. The combined 16 items yielded a good three-factor model fit: χ2/df = 3.74, RMSEA = 0.05, CFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.95, SRMR = 0.03, and Cronbach’s α = 0.97.

Personal growth initiative

We measured personal growth initiative using a scale developed by Robitschek et al. (2012) and Yang & Chang (2014). Specifically, this 16‐item instrument employs a 6‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree) to measure four components: readiness for change (e.g., “I know when it is time to change specific things about myself”), planfulness (e.g., “I set realistic goals for what I want to change about myself,”), using resources (e.g., “I use resources when I try to grow”), and intentional behavior (e.g., “I actively work to improve myself”). In terms of reliability, the Cronbach’s α for the four subscales was 0.87, 0.91, 0.86, and 0.86, respectively. The total scale yielded a good four-factor model fit: χ2/df = 3.73, RMSEA = 0.05, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.94, SRMR = 0.03, Cronbach’s α = 0.96.

Future work self-salience

We measured future work self-salience with the Chinese version (Guan et al. 2014) of the Strauss et al. (2012) scale. This scale assesses clarity regarding one’s future professional identity, using four items (e.g., “I am very clear about who and what I want to become in my future work.”). Participants rate their agreement on a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). In our analysis, the one-factor model for this scale showed a good fit to the data: χ2/df = 3.97, CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.04, SRMR = 0.03, Cronbach’s α = 0.84.

Control variables

Since the data were not normally distributed (Table 1), we used the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test to assess between-group differences in future work self-salience. Sophomores scored significantly higher than first-year students (Z = −2.07, p < 0.05), and students with part-time work experience scored significantly higher than those without (Z = −3.67, p < 0.001), but no significant difference for gender(Z = −1.354, p = 0.176). In addition, gender is not significantly associated with any of the examined variables. Consequently, only grade (1 = 1st grade, 2 = 2nd grade) and part-time work experience (1 = Yes, 2 = No) were included as controls in our final model.

Data analyses

All data were processed and subjected to preliminary analyses using SPSS 27.0, and descriptive statistics were performed to summarize the data. Although the absolute skewness and kurtosis coefficients of the variables were within the general acceptable thresholds of 2 and 7, respectively (Finney & DiStefano 2013; West et al. 1995), we employed the robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimator to account for any potential deviations from multivariate normality (e.g., the peaked distribution of CRTS). As all questions are mandatory, the resulting data contains no missing values.

Subsequently, latent profile analysis (LPA; Muthén, 2002) was conducted in Mplus8.3 to identify resource profiles. To account for the varying response scales across measures, all variables were standardized using z-scores prior to the analysis. In line with person-centered methods (Morin et al. 2018), we represented the four career resources using nine indicators: single standardized scores for self-identity and readiness for career transition, as well as subdimension and total scores for self-perceived employability (three indicators) and career support (four indicators). Keeping these dimensions separate, rather than aggregating them into composite scores, prevents the “masking effect” and ensures that meaningful subgroup differences in both the level and shape of resources remain observable (Bandalos 2002).

The default Mplus parameterization (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017) allows indicator means to vary freely across profiles while constraining variances to equality and fixing within-profile covariances at zero to assume local independence. To determine the optimal number of profiles, model fit indices were evaluated based on lowest values on the AIC (Akaike Information Criterion; Akaike 1987), BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion; Schwarz 1978), and SABIC (adjusted BIC for sample-size; Sclove 1987), with lower values indicating better model fit. Additionally, significant p-values (p < 0.05) for the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LRT) and the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) indicate that a k-profile model fits the data significantly better than a (k-1)-profile model (Dziak et al. 2014). Further, an optimal model should exhibit an entropy value above 0.80, reflecting good classification quality (Nylund-Gibson & Choi 2018), and its smallest latent class should comprise at least 5% of the total sample (Nylund, Asparouhov, & Muthén, 2007). Throughout model selection, simplicity and interpretability were considered (Wang & Wang 2019). Additionally, differences in personal growth initiative and future work self-salience across the latent profiles were examined using the Bolck-Croon-Hagenaars (BCH) method (Bolck et al. 2004). For model interpretability, we estimated standardized mean differences (Cohen’s d) for all pairwise mean comparisons as an estimate of class separation, following guidelines that Cohen’s d values greater than 1.5 reflect strong separation between classes on a given indicator (Tein et al. 2013) and values less than 0.85 reflect low separation (Masyn 2013).

Finally, we evaluated the mediation mechanism of PGI using a multigroup mediation approach (Hayes & Preacher 2014). Since our independent variable comprises four categorical profiles (k = 4), we estimated relative mediation effects by comparing each profile with the reference group using SPSS 27.0 PROCESS macro v2.16 (Model 4; Hayes 2013), with 5,000 Bootstrap samples to calculate the 95% BCCI. Following the recommendations of Wen et al. (2023) and Fang et al. (2017), we adopted a “classify-and-analyze” approach for mediation testing. We conducted both omnibus and relative mediation analyses to capture the comprehensive and specific effects of profile membership on future work self-salience. These relative indirect effects are used to determine whether the mediation mechanism operates “transformatively” across profiles and to assess whether the mediation mechanism differs substantially across resource groups.

Result

Common method bias assessment

Given the self-reported nature of the data, we first examined potential standard method bias (CMB). An exploratory factor analysis was performed on all items from the six questionnaires using Harman’s single-factor test with an unrotated principal component analysis. The results indicated 12 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor accounted for 27.78% of the total variance, below the threshold of 40% (Zhou & Long 2004). We further evaluated CMB by incorporating a single unmeasured latent method factor (ULMC; Podsakoff et al. 2003). Before including this factor, the initial 12-factor model exhibited adequate fit: χ2/df = 2.274, CFI = 0.905, TLI = 0.900, RMSEA = 0.034, SRMR = 0.055. After adding the common method factor (CMV), fit indices remained comparable (χ2/df = 2.265, CFI = 0.907, TLI = 0.901, RMSEA = 0.034, SRMR = 0.042). Changes in fit were minimal, with ΔCFI and ΔTLI both less than 0.010, and ΔRMSEA and ΔSRMR both less than 0.050 (Wen et al. 2018). Considering these test results together, the data do not indicate a serious common-method bias problem.

Latent profile analysis of career resources

To clarify which indicator values are above or below the sample means, we used z-standardized mean-scale scores for self-identity, career transition readiness, self-perceived employability (two dimensions), career-related teacher support (three dimensions), and conducted an LPA based on these nine indicators. To identify the optimal number of latent classes, models with 2 to 6 latent classes were sequentially estimated and compared, beginning with the single-class solution. The model fit indices are presented in Table 3. As the number of profiles increased, the model fit indices (LL, AIC, BIC, SABIC) generally decreased. Except for the non-significant LMR value for the six-profile solution, the LMR and BLRT p-values for the other models were statistically significant. The entropy values consistently exceeded 0.80 across all solutions, indicating high classification accuracy and supporting the superiority of the current model over previous alternatives. Model accuracy reached 90% in all cases. However, the entropy was highest at 0.972 when there were four profiles, with probabilities of 0.98, 0.99, 0.98, and 0.98, respectively. Therefore, given its simplicity and interpretability (Ferguson et al. 2020), we adopted the four-profile model as the optimal model (Fig. 2), thereby addressing H1.

Figure 2 depicts the four-profile solution from our distributional similarity model. Our smallest group, Profile 1 (n = 63, 5.76%), was characterized by significantly low levels of identity, psychological, and social resources. This may indicate that individuals in this profile were in the early stages of developing their career resources. Therefore, we labeled this profile as the Deficient Career Resources profile.

Profile 2 (n = 593, 54.25%) was characterized by slightly low levels of self-identity, career readiness, internal–external self-perceived employability, and moderately low levels of enhancement of self-exploration, information support, emotional support, and the total of career-related teacher support. Owing to the relative prominence of external self-perceived employability among various career-related factors, even though absolute scores remained below the sample mean, we labeled this group the Relatively High External Employability Profile.

The highest levels of all career resources were observed in profile 3 (n = 108, 9.90%). Therefore, we designated this profile the Sufficient Career Resources profile, in which vocational college students reported receiving the highest level of career-oriented instructional support. This label indicates that these emerging adults benefit from comprehensive teacher support, encompassing both informational resources (e.g., internship opportunities, industry insights, and job-seeking skills) and substantial emotional support (e.g., encouragement, affirmation, and care). This high-intensity support facilitated self-exploration and enhanced career transition readiness among students in this subgroup, creating a resource augmentation effect characterized by a “support-exploration-growth” dynamic.

Profile 4 (n = 329,30.10%) was characterized by moderately high levels of all career resources. Individuals in this profile exhibited balanced levels of career resources. Therefore, we labeled this profile as the Balanced Career Resources profile.

Next, we used the BCH method (Bolck et al. 2004) to examine mean differences across the four profiles in personal growth initiative and future work self-salience. As shown in Table 4, the overall Wald χ2 test indicated significant differences in personal growth initiative (χ2 = 214.47, p < 0.001) and future work self-salience (χ2 = 49.53, p < 0.001), thus supporting H2. Specifically, the “Sufficient Career Resources group” scored significantly higher on both personal growth initiative (M = 4.94) and future work self-salience (M = 3.33) than the other three profiles. Based on Cohen’s d criteria (d > 1.5 for large effects and d < 0.85 for small effects; Tein et al. 2013; Masyn 2013), the substantial Cohen’s d values indicate that these gaps represent meaningful differences rather than mere statistical artifacts. These results suggest a notable divergence in students’ vocational development between the “Sufficient” and “Deficient” profiles.

Mediated effects of personal growth initiative

We used the SPSS 27.0 PROCESS macro to test mediation with a multivariate independent variable. We created three dummy variables (D1, D2, D3) through indicator coding to represent four career resource profiles. The deficient career resource profile served as the reference group and was assigned a score of “0” on all variables. The high external employability, sufficient, and balanced career resources profiles were coded as “100”, “010”, and “001”, respectively. Finally, we tested a mediation model in which personal growth initiative served as the mediator and future work self-salience as the dependent variable.

In accordance with Hayes and Preacher’s (2014) recommendation, we applied 1-α/(k−1) in place of the conventional (1-α) criterion for significance tests to control the Type I error rate. Consequently, a significance level of p < 0.017 was adopted for testing the relative indirect, direct, and total effects. The results showed that the omnibus total effect, F(3,1089) = 23.22, p < 0.001, indicating that the three relative total effects are not all zero. The omnibus direct effect F (3,1088) = 1.98, p = 0.116, and the omnibus indirect effect’s Bootstrap 95% CI = [0.049, 0.094], suggesting that the three relative indirect effects were significant. Therefore, following the procedure for testing relative indirect effects, further relative mediation analyses were warranted (Fang, Wen, & Zhang 2017).

The results indicated that, with the deficient career resource group as the reference, the Bootstrap 95% CI for the relative indirect effects of the Relatively High External Employability (RHEE) group, the Sufficient Career Resources (SCR)group, and the Balanced Career Resources (BCR) group were [0.086, 0.237], [0.365, 0.610], and [0.223, 0.407], respectively, none of which included zero. This suggests that the relative indirect effects were significant (a1b = 0.16, a2b = 0.47, a3b = 0.31). That is, compared to the deficient career resource group, students with higher self-perceived external employability, stronger teacher support, and more balanced career resources demonstrated higher levels of personal growth initiative and future work self-salience (a1 = 0.43, a2 = 1.31, a3 = 0.85, b = 0.36). Meanwhile, only the relative direct effect of the sufficient career resource subgroup was significant (p = 0.016, Bootstrap 95% CI [0.043,0.041]), while the direct effects of the other two subgroups were not (see Table 5).

Discussion

Overview of findings

The self-directed career management and the construction of a future work self among vocational college students require support from career resources, which should be explored from a broader, more integrative perspective. Based on a relatively large sample, this study employed latent profile analysis to investigate the enabling mechanisms of future work self-salience among vocational college students, not only by examining the combined patterns of identity resources, psychological resources, and social resources and their associations with future work self-salience but also by exploring the pathways through which different types of career resources influence future work self-salience from the perspective of personal growth initiative. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the career development mechanisms in vocational students and provide an empirical basis for targeted career interventions.

This study identified four latent profiles of career resources among vocational college students: deficient, relatively high external employability, sufficient, and balanced. These profiles illustrate how resources integrate, acting as a “resource caravan” (Hobfoll 2011). In this caravan, individual resources are not lone travelers; they form supportive teams that either thrive or struggle together. In the sufficient career resources profile, students with peak levels of self-identity and teacher support have a robust internal and external foundation. Specifically, teacher support emerged as the most prominent factor for this group. Moreover, this profile demonstrated significantly higher scores in personal growth initiative and future work self-salience than the other three profiles, underscoring the “gain spiral” effect created by their integrated resource advantage. This suggests that strong teacher support serves as a vital catalyst, fostering the autonomy needed to explore personal interests and clarify professional goals (Parola et al. 2024; Perry et al. 2010; Wong et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2018). Furthermore, these psychological mechanisms are significantly amplified within the Chinese cultural context. Rooted in Confucian values, Chinese society places a profound emphasis on respecting teachers (Zun shi Zhong dao). Educators are regarded as authorities and cherished life mentors for students, and support and affirmation carry special psychological value, enabling them to ease students’ career worries. Findings from this subgroup further demonstrate that Faculty career guidance needs to be strengthened in vocational colleges.

In contrast, the relatively high external employability profile reveals a “fragile caravan”. While these students packed their bags with labor market skills (Strauss et al. 2012), they lack the internal psychological pillars (e.g., identity) to sustain them. This “mismatch” aligns with SCCT (Lent & Brown 1996; Lent et al. 1994, 2000), suggesting that external assets require internal self-efficacy to drive effective career development. Without this internal belief, skills alone cannot sustain long-term progress. Conversely, the deficient career resources profile reflects a “loss spiral”. These students had the lowest levels of psychological and social resources, likely lacked the confidence and support needed to navigate their future career paths.

An important finding of this research was that the effects of relatively high external employability/sufficient/balanced career resources profiles on future work self-salience were partially mediated by personal growth initiative. The structural disparities across career resource subgroups may relate to future work self-salience via either a direct-and-indirect pathway or a fully indirect one. Individuals with relatively high perceived external employability and a balanced profile of career resources typically possess strong self-efficacy and robust social support networks (Zikic & Klehe 2006), enabling them to actively integrate internal and external assets to clarify their career direction (Hirschi 2012). When confronted with uncertainty, they are more inclined to perceive it as a developmental opportunity rather than a threat, thereby sustaining engagement in self-exploration and competency enhancement. This positive psychological orientation fosters a sense of personal growth initiative (Robitschek & Cook 1999), which significantly strengthens their identification with their future work selves (Strauss et al. 2012). This mechanism not only enhances the clarity of career goals but also allows individuals to maintain a prospective outlook and adaptability within a dynamically evolving labor market. Consequently, they are more effective in connecting current actions with long-term development, facilitating sustained career progression (Savickas 2005, 2013). In contrast, individuals lacking adequate career resources often experience a deficiency in external support and heightened self-doubt (Hobfoll 2002), leading to a state of passive waiting. This impedes the initiation of effective career construction behaviors. Their constrained personal growth initiative leads to vague, less realistic conceptions of their future selves in the workplace.

Practical implications

Beyond theoretical contributions, the current findings offer specific, actionable implications for educational practice. From a life-cycle perspective, vocational students are navigating a critical stage of career exploration (Super 1980), necessitating a shift in institutional objectives from mere skill acquisition to the holistic cultivation of career resources. To be truly “student-centered,” schools can use the profiles we identified as a practical screening tool. By understanding the resources students already have upon enrollment, counselors can move away from a “one-size-fits-all” approach. Instead, they should offer highly specific interventions for students in the low-resource group. For example, counselors could first provide targeted psychological coaching to help these students overcome early career anxiety. More importantly, since our data showed that part-time work experience significantly boosts students’ future work self-salience, schools should proactively strengthen partnerships with local companies. In turn, colleges can provide these vulnerable students with stable, low-threshold part-time jobs or internships, paving a clear professional path for them to accumulate required work experience.

Meanwhile, vocational colleges should develop structured mentoring programs that encourage faculty to do more than provide basic job-search advice. Moreover, institutions should invest in teachers’ professional development through systematic training and incentive systems. Such training should strengthen educators’ competencies in career counseling, psychological support, and industry-relevant knowledge. With these capabilities, teachers can effectively help students grow confidence and remain central to their career readiness. Ultimately, this authentic, relationship-oriented support empowers students to take charge of their career trajectories proactively.

Limitations and future research

The current study is subject to several methodological and sampling limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design, while providing preliminary insights, limits our ability to draw causal conclusions about the relationships among career resource profiles, personal growth initiative, and future work self-clarity. Given that career resources are theorized to operate through dynamic, reciprocal processes over time (Hirschi 2012), future research should adopt longitudinal designs—such as cohort-sequential approaches combined with latent transition analysis—to capture developmental trajectories and subgroup transitions better. Secondly, our sample was limited to first- and second-year vocational students and did not include third-year students. This omission was due in part to practical challenges in data collection during the internship period, when some students were off-campus. Future studies should aim to incorporate graduating cohorts to provide a more complete picture of career development across the full vocational education cycle. Thirdly, the use of a convenience sample from two vocational colleges in Jiangxi Province may affect the generalizability of the findings. To enhance external validity, subsequent research should broaden geographical and institutional diversity, incorporating vocational students from multiple regions across China.

Conclusion

Drawing on Hirschi’s (2012) integrative career resources framework, this study identified four distinct profiles of career resources among vocational college students: (a) deficient; (b) relatively high external employability; (c)sufficient, and(d) balanced. The career resource-sufficient subgroup scored highest on personal growth initiative and future work self-salience. Moreover, the structural differences among career resource subgroups were linked to future work self-salience via either a direct and indirect pathway, further substantiating the mediating role of personal growth initiative.
