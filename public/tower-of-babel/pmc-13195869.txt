A comparative study on the outcomes of post-placental intrauterine contraceptive device insertion between preterm and term deliveries: a hospital-based observational study
Authors: Soni Kumari, Sarita Singh, Aditi Chawla, Abhishek Lachyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndia is the most populous country in the world, with over 1.4 billion people, facing significant challenges in maternal and reproductive health services. Family planning is a key strategy to prevent unintended pregnancies and optimize spacing between births. Post-placental intrauterine contraceptive device (PPIUCD) insertion is a safe, long-acting, reversible, and cost-effective method of immediate postpartum contraception. This study aimed to compare continuation rates, complications, and expulsion rates of PPIUCD insertion between preterm and term deliveries, and to explore factors influencing outcomes.Settings and designHospital-based observational study conducted at VMMC & Safdarjung Hospital, New Delhi.Materials and methodsAmong 2000 antenatal women counseled for immediate postpartum IUCD insertion, 240 (12%) consented and were enrolled. Participants were divided equally into preterm (n = 120) and term (n = 120) groups. CuT 380 A devices were inserted immediately post-placentally. Follow-up was conducted at 3, 6, and 12 months to assess continuation, satisfaction, expulsion, and complications. Counseling was provided antenatally and postnatally using standardized IEC materials, models, and checklists.Statistical analysisCategorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Chi-square tests and t-tests were applied where appropriate using SPSS 24.0, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 26.9 ± 3.2 years. Continuation rates at 6 and 12 months were significantly higher in term deliveries (96.6% and 85.0%) than in preterm deliveries (88.3% and 63.3%; p < 0.05). Spontaneous IUCD expulsion was higher in preterm women at 6 months (7.5% vs. 1.6%) and 12 months (15.3% vs. 6.4%; p < 0.05), potentially due to smaller uterine size, underdeveloped uterine cavity, and variations in uterine involution. Satisfaction rates were high and comparable between groups at 12 months (81% preterm vs. 85% term; p > 0.05). Acceptance rates were low overall (12%), highlighting the need for structured counseling, provider training, and peer education to improve uptake.ConclusionPost-placental IUCD insertion is safe, effective, and cost-efficient in both preterm and term deliveries. Higher expulsion rates in preterm deliveries warrant focused counseling and follow-up. Structured antenatal and postnatal counseling, combined with provider confidence and patient education, is essential to increase acceptance and continuation of PPIUCD, particularly in populations at higher risk of early postpartum fertility.Keywords: Post-placental IUCD, Preterm delivery, Term delivery, Continuation rate, Expulsion, Complications

Introduction

“India is currently most populous country in the world, with a population exceeding 1.47 billion, highlighting the urgent need for effective family planning strategies to optimize birth spacing, reduce unintended pregnancies, and improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes.” Family planning (FP) is a crucial intervention for controlling population growth, as it enables appropriate spacing of pregnancies and reduces unintended childbirths [1]. Counseling regarding contraceptive methods should ideally begin during the antenatal period and continue immediately postpartum, as 10–44% of women may become pregnant within the first year after delivery [2].

Intrauterine contraceptive devices (IUCDs) are a safe, long-acting, reversible, and cost-effective method of contraception, with established high satisfaction (approximately 80%) and continuation rates (around 84%) [3]. Prevention of unintended pregnancies through effective postpartum contraception can also reduce maternal mortality by 20–35% and infant mortality by approximately 20% [4].

Post-placental IUCD (PPIUCD) insertion, performed immediately after delivery, offers the advantage of convenience, early contraception, and reduced risk of loss to follow-up. However, acceptance rates can be variable due to socio-cultural factors, patient awareness, and provider confidence in counseling. While several studies have examined outcomes in term deliveries, data on PPIUCD insertion in preterm births are limited, particularly regarding continuation, expulsion, and satisfaction rates.

The present study aimed to compare the continuation rates, satisfaction levels, and complications of PPIUCD insertion between women delivering preterm and those delivering at term, with a focus on understanding factors influencing expulsion and acceptance. This study also highlights the importance of structured counseling and provider-led interventions to improve uptake of immediate postpartum contraception.

Materials and methods

This hospital-based observational study was conducted in the Department of Obstetrics & Gynaecology, Vardhman Mahavir Medical College (V.M.M.C) & Safdarjung Hospital (SJH), Delhi, over a period of 24 months, from December 2020 to December 2022. A total of 240 women were enrolled after being counseled regarding postpartum contraception using standardized Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) materials, including pictorial flip charts, demonstration models, and checklists aligned with the Government of India’s Family Planning Programme. Counseling was provided antenatally and reinforced at delivery to ensure informed decision-making. Women who consented to participate were screened post-delivery according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and divided into two groups based on gestational age at delivery: preterm and term. Only women who accepted post-placental intrauterine contraceptive device (IUCD) insertion were included.

Inclusion criteria were women under medical eligibility category 1 or 2 for IUCD, those who delivered vaginally, were not planning pregnancy within the next 12 months, and were willing to adhere to follow-up visits. Exclusion criteria included chorioamnionitis in the current pregnancy, pelvic infection, prolonged rupture of membranes (> 18 h), hemoglobin < 8 g/dL, Wilson’s disease, postpartum hemorrhage, suspected uterine rupture, retained placenta, history of ectopic pregnancy or uterine anomaly, cervical cancer or untreated high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion, thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 100,000/cu mm), fever (> 38 °C), and women undergoing second-trimester abortion.

IUCD insertion (CuT 380 A) was performed immediately post-placentally following standard hospital protocols. Women were observed in-hospital for 48 h post-insertion and subsequently followed up at 3, 6, and 12 months. At each follow-up visit, a detailed clinical evaluation was performed through history and pelvic examination. Severity of lower abdominal pain and menstrual pain was assessed using a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) ranging from 0 to 10, and satisfaction with IUCD usage was measured using the Likert scale at 6 and 12 months (Flowchart 1).

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24.0. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) depending on distribution. Quantitative variables were compared using unpaired t-tests or Mann–Whitney tests, and qualitative variables were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and non-parametric tests were applied when normality was rejected. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and uncorrected chi-square statistics were used where appropriate.

The study protocol, including counseling procedures and informed consent, was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC/VMCC/SIH/THESIS/2020-11/CC-182, dated 10.12.2020). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion, emphasizing voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time.

Results

Over the 24-month study period, a total of 2000 antenatal women were counseled regarding postpartum IUCD insertion during their visits to the antenatal OPD or labor room. Among them, 240 women (acceptability: 12%) consented and were enrolled, highlighting that overall acceptance remained low despite structured counseling. These participants were equally divided into term (n = 120) and preterm (n = 120) groups. The mean age of the study population was 26.9 ± 3.2 years. During the antenatal period, 53.3% of women had mild anemia and 46.7% had moderate anemia. At 12 months follow-up, hemoglobin levels improved significantly in both term and preterm groups compared to baseline (p < 0.05), indicating that IUCD use did not adversely affect menstrual blood loss. The mean Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score for pain during IUCD insertion was 2 in 75% of women, suggesting minimal discomfort during the procedure.

Table 1 summarizes the socio-demographic and obstetric characteristics of the participants. Both groups were comparable in terms of age distribution and parity. The majority of participants were aged 26–30 years (preterm: 55.0%, term: 50.8%), followed by 21–25 years (preterm: 31.7%, term: 31.7%), while a smaller proportion were younger than 20 years or older than 30 years. These findings indicate that the study population predominantly comprised women in the mid-reproductive age group, which is consistent with previous studies on postpartum contraception in India.

Table 1 shows that both groups were comparable in terms of age and parity. However, a significant difference was observed in educational status, with a higher proportion of women in the term group having intermediate or higher education compared to the preterm group (p = 0.001).

During IUCD insertion, the string was visible in 3.3% of women (7 women between 28 and 31 + 6 weeks, none between 32 and 36 + 6 weeks; p < 0.05). At 12 months follow-up, 78% of women had resumed menses (93% in the preterm group and 85% in the term group). In the term group, there was a statistically significant difference in menstrual pattern, menstrual pain, and duration before and after IUCD insertion at 12 months.

Overall IUCD removal on request was reported in 1.3% at 3 months, 1.7% at 6 months, and 6.7% at 12 months. At 12 months, 87% of women were satisfied with IUCD insertion according to the Likert scale. There was no significant difference in satisfaction between the two groups (p > 0.05).

Table 2 demonstrates that continuation rates were significantly higher in the term group compared to the preterm group at both 6 and 12 months (p < 0.05). Satisfaction rates were high and comparable between groups at both time points (p > 0.05). Expulsion rates, however, were significantly higher in the preterm group at both follow-up intervals (p < 0.05).

Discussion

There are limited studies comparing continuation, satisfaction, and complication rates of post-placental intrauterine contraceptive device (PPIUCD) insertion between preterm and term deliveries. In our study, the mean age of participants was 26.9 ± 3.2 years, comparable to previous reports by Mishra et al. [4] (20–29 years) and Peipert et al. [3] (25.8 ± 5.9 years). Age did not significantly influence PPIUCD acceptance in either group. Women and their spouses in the term group had higher educational levels than those in the preterm group, highlighting the positive association between education and contraceptive acceptability [4].

The low acceptance rate (12%) despite structured counseling highlights persistent socio-cultural barriers, misconceptions regarding IUCD use, fear of complications, and limited partner involvement. Additionally, effective counseling requires adequate provider training, communication skills, and confidence in addressing patient concerns. Regular reinforcement through structured training programs, peer education, and integration of contraceptive counseling into routine antenatal care can significantly improve acceptance rates. Strengthening provider capacity and ensuring consistent, high-quality counseling are essential for increasing demand generation and uptake of PPIUCD.

Discontinuation was primarily due to bleeding, infection, abdominal pain, and desire for conception, consistent with Mishra et al. [4] and Khurshid et al. [10]. Enhanced pre-insertion counseling, early follow-up, and partner involvement are essential to improving continuation rates. In preterm deliveries, string visibility was higher in women delivering at 28–31 + 6 weeks compared to 32–36 + 6 weeks, suggesting that gestational maturity influences post-placental IUCD outcomes.

Multigravida women demonstrated higher acceptance rates, aligning with findings from Gungan Goswami et al. [5], likely because PPIUCD is a safe, long-acting reversible contraceptive and a preferred alternative to sterilization. At 12 months, hemoglobin levels were significantly improved in both groups, indicating that IUCD insertion does not substantially increase menstrual blood loss, consistent with Borghei et al. [6].

Pain during insertion was minimal, with 69.2% of term and 80.8% of preterm women reporting VAS scores of 2. The open cervical os post-delivery likely reduces discomfort compared to interval IUCD insertion, as reported by Kumar et al. [7]. String visibility immediately after insertion was absent in 100% of term deliveries, significantly higher than preterm deliveries (93.3%). Among preterm women, string visibility improved with increasing gestational age, supporting the relationship between uterine size and string placement [8].

At 6 and 12 months, term women experienced significant reductions in menstrual flow and pain, whereas preterm women did not show notable changes. Expulsion rates were higher in preterm deliveries, and continuation rates were lower, with overall rates of 92.5% at 6 months and 74% at 12 months. The higher expulsion rate observed in preterm deliveries may be attributed to several physiological factors. The uterine cavity in preterm deliveries is relatively smaller and less distended, which may hinder optimal fundal placement of the IUCD. Additionally, altered uterine involution dynamics, reduced uterine tone, and increased uterine contractility in the early postpartum period may contribute to device displacement. Variations in cervical dilation and rapid anatomical changes following preterm delivery may further influence IUCD retention.

No perforations or pregnancies were reported during follow-up [3, 8, 9, 10] Satisfaction rates remained high at 6 months (preterm 89%, term 93%) and 12 months (preterm 81%, term 85%), reflecting reduced fear of unintended pregnancy during lactation [3, 11–13]. Satisfaction was objectively assessed using the Likert scale and verified by clinical examination and ultrasound, rather than self-reporting.

Limitations and recommendations

This study was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The follow-up period was restricted to 12 months, preventing assessment of longer-term outcomes. Additionally, the effectiveness of counseling and acceptance of post-placental IUCD may vary across different populations and healthcare settings.

We recommend conducting multi-center studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up to gain a more comprehensive understanding of long-term continuation, satisfaction, and complication rates associated with immediate post-placental IUCD insertion, particularly in women delivering preterm.

Conclusion

Post-placental IUCD is a safe, long-acting, reversible, and well-tolerated method of contraception. Acceptance was notably higher among multigravida women and those with higher educational levels, highlighting the importance of education in promoting contraceptive use. Structured counseling and systematic follow-up significantly improve both acceptance and continuation rates. Although preterm deliveries exhibited slightly higher expulsion rates, IUCD insertion remains a feasible and effective option with minimal complications at 12 months.Strengthening provider training and integrating structured, repeated antenatal counseling can play a pivotal role in improving acceptance and continuation of PPIUCD services.

Future directions

Future studies should evaluate outcomes beyond the first year, particularly in preterm populations where expulsion rates are higher. Large, multi-center trials can provide stronger evidence to inform policy and clinical practice. Integrating IUCD counseling into routine antenatal and postnatal care, complemented by community awareness programs and healthcare provider training, can enhance both acceptance and adherence. Policy initiatives focusing on contraceptive education, especially in underserved and rural areas, will be critical to expanding the safe and effective use of post-placental IUCDs.
