What do women experiencing domestic violence expect from healthcare professionals? A qualitative study in Sri Lanka
Authors: Tharuka Silva, Abey Rathnayake, Maggie Evans, Duleeka Knipe, Thilini Rajapakse, Thilini Agampodi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesFor healthcare professionals (HCPs) to provide optimal support for women experiencing domestic violence (DV), it is important to understand what type of support women expect from HCPs. The objective of this study was to explore what kind of support women who experienced DV in Sri Lanka expect from HCPs.DesignA qualitative descriptive design was used.SettingThis study was conducted in two gender-based violence centres (Mithuru Piyasa centres) and a toxicology unit, situated in hospitals in the Central Province of Sri Lanka.ParticipantsTwenty women who had experienced DV and presented to health services were purposefully recruited using the maximum variation sampling technique, ensuring diversity of age and socio-economic variables. In-depth interviews were conducted with women who consented.ResultsWomen who have experienced DV expect varying assistance from HCPs, including changing their abusive partner’s behaviour, resolving their issues or restoring family and marital harmony. Women expect HCPs to intervene in different ways, such as by conversing with both parties or offering support to help the abusive partner overcome alcohol or drug addiction. Women also recommend community-based programmes that concentrate on enhancing marital and family cohesion. Financial and instrumental social support also emerged as key expectations. They anticipate emotional support from HCPs, whom they expect to maintain confidentiality, exhibit empathy, listen attentively and give more importance to DV disclosures. A few women requested referrals to other support services.ConclusionsA multisectoral approach including a proper referral system and social support between health and other social services is a timely recommendation, given the women’s requests for financial and instrumental social support, which go beyond the health sector. The women’s requests for HCPs to talk with both women and their partners to resolve their issues is a more complex and new finding, which warrants further exploration.

Introduction

Domestic violence (DV) is a major global public health problem that affects the health and well-being of women as well as their children and families.13 DV refers to violence by an intimate partner or by other family members regardless of where the violence has taken place and the form of violence.4 Globally, 26% of women aged 15 years and older have experienced physical and/or sexual violence from a current or former husband or male intimate partner at least once in their lifetime. South Asia has a higher prevalence (35%) of intimate partner violence, particularly among women aged 15–49 years, compared with high-income countries (20%).5 A significant prevalence of DV is also evident in Sri Lanka, a South Asian country, where two in every five women (39.8%) reported having experienced any kind of violence by a partner at some point in their lifetime.6

In Sri Lanka, the Prevention of Domestic Violence Act was introduced in 2005 to address the issue legally.7 However, healthcare professionals (HCPs), including physicians, nurses and midwives, also play a major role in supporting women experiencing DV, as they can be the first point of contact for the physical and psychological consequences related to DV.8 9 HCPs can address the issue of DV by identifying women experiencing DV, offering referrals to community agencies and health and social services and providing assistance for physical and psychological health conditions.10 Furthermore, the health system can respond to DV through counselling women, mother-child interventions, responses to perpetrators, child protection notifications and system-level responses such as making a referral system and documentation of DV in medical records.11

In Sri Lanka, the Family Health Bureau of the Ministry of Health introduced gender-based violence centres named ‘Mithuru Piyasa’ to provide optimal support for DV survivors in 2007. Mithuru Piyasa centres are first-contact, walk-in centres situated within the hospitals in most districts of the country. The nurse and/or doctor in the Mithuru Piyasa centre provides emotional support and counselling to women and may see them several times on an outpatient basis. The Mithuru Piyasa centre staff may also direct the women to other in-hospital services (such as referrals to psychiatrists, gynaecologists or surgeons) and/ or out-of-hospital services (referrals to legal aid and financial aid) through a system of referrals.12 13 In addition, a national action plan for the health sector was developed to improve the health sector response to gender-based violence including DV.13

However, despite having an established service centre in the Sri Lankan health system to support women experiencing DV, the number of women experiencing DV who seek help from HCPs is still relatively low,5 12 due to barriers at individual, HCPs and societal level.14 Therefore, to overcome these barriers and improve women’s access to healthcare settings, it is important to understand what HCPs can do to support women experiencing DV. Furthermore, in order to understand how best to provide care, it is crucial to explore what sort of support women require from HCPs, particularly in a lower-middle-income setting. Little data are available in this regard. In order to take a person-centered approach to support women experiencing DV, we used qualitative approach to gain insights into what kind of support women would like from their HCPs. Given that, the main purpose of this study was to describe what women who experience DV expect from HCPs. The findings of this study can help to inform interventions at the individual, community and healthcare levels, to minimise the harm and burden of DV in Sri Lanka. Moreover, in Sri Lanka, there is no specific official training for HCPs in general to work with DV survivors.15 Thus, it is anticipated that the findings of this study will help develop training for HCPs in order to provide the best care for women experiencing DV.

Methods

This paper depicts a part of a large research project exploring the help-seeking behaviour of women who experienced DV. The barriers to help-seeking from HCPs are published elsewhere.14 The aspect of women’s expectations from HCPs for DV, which is described in this paper, is a unique component that sheds light on modifiable interventions for the improvement of health services for women facing DV. Hence, both papers include findings from data collected from the same sample of women,14 and we are presenting them in a nutshell in this paper.

Study design

This was a qualitative descriptive study. Qualitative descriptive research enables an in-depth investigation of participants’ experiences and perspectives on a particular phenomenon. This method allows the researcher to delve deeply into the lived experiences of women who experienced DV. Furthermore, as women’s personal preferences might differ from one to another, this approach helps the researcher better understand the different types of support each woman expects from HCPs.15

Study setting

The study took place in the Central Province of Sri Lanka, where a relatively high prevalence of DV has been reported (19%–72%).1618 The Central Province has a population of 2.6 million and is made up of three main districts: Kandy, Matale and Nuwara Eliya. The population is a mixture of Sinhalese (66%), Tamil (23.8%), Sri Lankan Moors (9.9%) and other ethnic groups (0.3%).19 For this study, women with a current or history of DV were recruited from two settings, Mithuru Piyasa centres at the National Hospital Kandy and Base Hospital Gampola, as well as from the toxicology unit (ward 17) at the Teaching Hospital Peradeniya, Kandy. Women experiencing DV may choose to go to the Mithuru Piyasa centre after self-identifying the need for support, or after being referred by an HCP within the hospital.12 Furthermore, a high prevalence of DV has been reported among women admitted to the toxicology unit for medical management of deliberate self-poisoning.18 Therefore, we recruited women from these two settings in order to easily identify women who have experienced DV.

Sampling

A purposeful sample of women who have experienced or are experiencing DV was included. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) 18 years or over, (2) self-reported current or past history of DV perpetrated by a partner or household member and (3) presentation to the Mithuru Piyasa centre or the toxicology unit between August 2019 and January 2020. Women presenting to the toxicology unit were included only if they screened positive for DV after the initial screening for exposure to DV, using the ‘Humiliation, Afraid, Rape and Kick 4-item questionnaire’,20 which has been previously used for research in Sri Lanka.18 Exclusion criteria were participants who reported having a diagnosis of a current psychiatric disorder based on their self-report to avoid potential distress for women from discussing a highly sensitive topic. Women who experienced DV were recruited using the maximum variation sampling technique. Maximum variation sampling is the purposeful selection of study participants with a broad range of variance on dimensions of interest. It guarantees that the sample includes individuals with various backgrounds, viewpoints and perspectives.21 Interviewing people with different backgrounds, opinions and perspectives is crucial to gaining a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon being studied. Thus, participants were purposefully selected to ensure variation in age and socio-economic variables.

Participant recruitment

The detailed procedure of participant recruitment and data collection is discussed in the previous publication14 and is provided here in brief. Twenty-two women who presented to the two Mithuru Piyasa centres were approached and four declined to participate. Five women were approached in the toxicology unit and two of them were invited to take part in the interviews after they screened positive for DV exposure.

The interviewer approached the participants in a friendly and empathetic manner. Women were given a detailed explanation of the purpose of the study, what was expected from them and how the confidentiality and anonymity of collected data would be maintained during and after the study. They were also informed that participation in this study was completely voluntary and that refusal or withdrawal during or after the study would not affect their treatment in the hospital in any way. Thus, respecting their decision, the women were not asked why they refused to participate in the study.

Women were either presented to the Mithuru Piyasa centres directly from the local community or presented to the hospital and referred to the Mithuru Piyasa centres. There was no prior association between the women and the Mithuru Piyasa centres. The researcher was introduced to the Mithuru Piyasa centres and their staff, in order to carry out the project and had no direct involvement with the centres before. The researcher also did not have any prior relationship with the women and was not involved in any treatment or therapy they received.

Data collection procedure

The lead author, who is a graduate nurse, conducted all the in-depth interviews in the native language. Sociodemographic data were collected before starting the in-depth interviews using a brief questionnaire created for the study. The in-depth interviews were conducted using an interview guide designed for the study (online supplemental file 1). The topic guide was modified as needed after the initial interviews. During the interview, women were asked what type of support they expect from HCPs for their DV issues and what they think HCPs can do to support women experiencing DV. The women were interviewed face-to-face, confidentially in a private room, either at the Mithuru Piyasa centre or at the toxicology unit, with only the interviewer present. The researcher ensured that the woman’s participation in the study did not increase her risk of being subject to violence by not interviewing the woman in the presence of her husband or any other family member. If a woman became distressed during the interview, she was offered support, and if needed she was also referred to the local psychiatric service for further help.

Each interview lasted between 20 and 60 min. The anticipated sample size was 25 and data collection was stopped at 20 interviews as data saturation was reached, which was determined when no new information was observed in the interviews.22 All the interviews were audio recorded. Interviews were transcribed by an external transcriber into the local language and checked for veracity by the lead author. Each participant record was assigned a code in order to link their interview and transcript while maintaining anonymity and confidentiality. This link between the participant’s identity and the code was only known by the main researcher. No one other than the research team of the study had access to paper or electronic data.

Data analysis

Interviews were analysed using thematic analysis, which took place in parallel with data collection.21 23 Two investigators independently coded the initial transcripts to ensure verifiability. A preliminary codebook was designed with agreement between the two investigators. The code book was revised and re-coded as necessary while analysing the transcripts with consensus. Themes and subthemes were identified using an inductive approach.24 25 To avoid data loss in translation, analysis was conducted using the local language transcripts. Selected quotes that were translated into English by the author, who is a native bilingual speaker, were checked for accuracy by another native bilingual speaker.

Quality assurance

Two investigators independently coded all the transcripts to increase credibility. The entire analysis process was reviewed by the wider study team, including qualitative experts, to ensure the dependability and confirmability of the findings. The coded transcripts and codebook were reviewed for accuracy by qualitative experts after the two investigators completed independent coding and designed the codebook.26 In order to prevent the researcher’s preconceived assumptions from being considered, the researcher entered the field with an open mind and limited prior assumptions or notions regarding the phenomenon being studied.27 In addition, the researcher engaged in reflective activities such as writing memos, which helped to identify the participants’ actual interpretations and avoid the researcher’s interpretations during the data collection and analysis.27 28 In addition, respondent validity was demonstrated by the interviewer summarising the data obtained from the interview at the end of each interview and reconfirming what the participant said.29 This also helped to avoid the influence of the researcher’s preconceived assumptions on the findings.

Patient and public involvement

Before designing this research study, we held several informal stakeholder meetings with HCPs working in the field of DV in Sri Lanka, to get their insights and experiences with women facing DV. Many HCPs highlighted the issue of women’s economic dependence and that women expect varying types of support. This information was useful in identifying the need to explore what support women experiencing DV actually expect from HCPs. Another study, in which some of the authors were involved, was conducted with local stakeholders such as national government officials (Department of Health, Education, Social Services and Welfare), social workers and community leaders/members to identify possible solutions to prevent suicide attempts among high-risk individuals. Findings from this study suggested that relationship and conflict resolution skills training programmes should be carried out in the community and school settings as interpersonal conflicts were one of the major reasons for suicide attempts.30 These factors also influenced the importance of exploring ways to minimise the DV incidence in Sri Lanka and therefore this study was designed to address the current research gap.

Findings

Participants were between the ages of 21 and 52 years (mean=37 years). Most of the participants were Sinhalese (95%, n=19) and were educated to secondary level (90%, n=18). Seven women (35%) were employed, and 13 (65%) were unemployed. Most women (95%, n=19) were married and nearly half of them (53%, n=10) were living with their partner. Almost all (95%, n=19) of the women had children. The current or former husband/or partner was the main perpetrator for all the cases. Therefore, the findings of this study represent what women who have experienced DV by a partner expect from HCPs. In addition, in line with the study objectives, these findings reflect women’s subjective experiences and their expectations from HCPs. Therefore, no comparisons were made to compare the experiences of women with different demographic characteristics and recruited from Mithuru Piyasa centres and toxicology departments.

Four major themes were identified: (1) mediation, (2) financial and instrumental support, (3) expected personal qualities of HCPs and (4) referral to counselling services and gender-based violence centres. The major theme ‘mediation’ was further categorised into three subthemes (figure 1).

Women’s expectations from healthcare professionals (HCPs). This figure depicts women’s diverse expectations from HCPs for domestic violence (DV), including mediation, financial and instrumental support, personal qualities such as paying more attention to DV, being confidential and empathetic, being a good listener and providing emotional support, as well as referrals to counselling services and gender-based violence (GBV) centres.

Mediation

Most women wanted assistance from HCPs to help them navigate their relationships and provide a type of mediation support to tackle their relationship problems. A majority of women wanted support to keep their family together. Women discussed different ways that HCPs could mediate for this problem. They expected mediation at couple, family and community levels. We identified three subthemes based on women’s expectations.

Requesting HCPs to mediate family cohesion

In order to keep their family together and reduce the DV, some women wanted HCPs to talk and advise both the women and their husbands about how to lead ‘a good family life’. A housewife in her early 20s mentioned:

Healthcare providers can give advice and explain things to both of us [husband and wife]. Now, when he [husband] gets angry, I stand up for myself… I can’t just stay silent when he scolds me for no reason… we argue often. It would be helpful if healthcare providers could guide us on how to behave and have a peaceful family life. We need to move forward! We want to be happy! (Woman 03)

Furthermore, some women wanted HCPs to advise their husbands not to abuse their wives and to warn them of the possible repercussions. A housewife in her mid-30s said:

I want healthcare providers to talk to my husband. They [HCPs] should take women’s side and advise their husbands. Men often use their power and women feel helpless. I would like the doctors to firmly tell my husband to stop this behavior and warn him that he could face consequences if he continues. (Woman04)

Many women discussed how HCPs in sexual and reproductive health services setting could support women who experience DV. Some women suggested that the public health midwives (PHMs) could arrange discussion sessions for families with DV issues to discuss their problems with HCPs and talk to their abusive husbands. They mentioned that PHMs could do this better because they are the ones who are more familiar with the village and its people, and they often visit houses.

The midwife often visits our village and knows us all very well. She could ask couples with family problems to meet with her once or twice a month to talk about their issues. There might be only a few families in the village with these problems. She could ask them to visit her regularly, even if they don’t have any problems at that time. During these visits, she could ask about the past month, if they faced any problems, and give advice or help them solve their issues. (Woman05)

Mediation to overcome mental health problems of the perpetrator

Women believed that their husband had mental health problems and that this was the main reason for his abusive behaviour. Therefore, they expected HCPs to treat their husbands. Excessive sexual desires and recurrent doubts about the wife’s fidelity were considered manifestations of a mental health problem by some women.

My husband always wanted to have sex with me, and he would beat me if I refused. It didn’t matter if it was day or night, even if I was cooking. If I didn’t go to him, he would get angry and beat me. He had a strange sexual desire. I wanted to give him some medicine to control his feelings, so I went to the doctor. I wanted to make him better and live happily with my family. Even though we were not financially stable, I needed him for my children. (Woman12)

My husband always doubts me, no matter what I do. This is the reason for all our problems. I believe he will get better if he takes some medicine. I’m sure of it. If he takes the medicine, he will be okay. So, I want the healthcare providers to tell him to come to the hospital. Then the doctors can give him the medicine. (Woman08)

Some women pointed out that their husband’s alcohol/drug addiction was the main reason for DV. These women expressed the idea that the abuse only happens when the husband is under the influence of alcohol/drugs, and that ‘at other times he behaves well’. They had the opinion or hope that if the husband could get treatment for these addictions, he would become a ‘good person’ and the situation would get better. Therefore, women wanted HCPs’ support to change their husbands’ abusive behaviour by supporting them to stop their alcohol/drug addiction.

I would be very happy if HCPs could help treat my husband’s alcohol addiction and stop his drinking. I don’t know how many more years I will live, so I want him to become a better person through treatment, and I want to live happily with him for the rest of my life. That’s all I need. (Woman06)

All these problems come from alcohol. So, HCPs could help women by stopping men from drinking and advising them to live peacefully with their families. (Woman09)

Mediate community programmes

Some women suggested that HCPs could organise DV-related programmes at the community level. They believed that these programmes would be helpful for them to lead a ‘good family life’ and to increase women’s awareness about DV and available DV support services in healthcare settings.

It would be great if HCPs could run programs for women in the villages. They could do this on clinic days when many women are already there. This way, HCPs could help women learn more about domestic violence and encourage them to disclose it to HCPs. They could also educate women about the available support services, such as Mithuru Piyasa. I really believe that Mithuru Piyasa could help me solve my problems. (Woman13)

Another woman explained that community programmes should be organised specially for men to provide sex education and it will be very helpful to decrease men’s perpetration of DV. She believed that men often tend to use violence against their wives when the wife refuses to have sex with them.

I know a lot of women suffer because of their husbands‘ excessive sexual desires. They go through so much pain and keep quiet for the sake of their children. HCPs should really step in to help stop this. Even though they run community programs for women or both women and men, they hardly ever have programs just for men to talk about these issues. I think it would be really helpful if HCPs could create programs just for men to discuss their sexual problems and provide sex education. Men don’t like talking about these things in front of their wives. If HCPs gave men a chance to talk openly about their sexual problems, it could make a big difference. They [HCPs] could ask men how they feel and why they act this way. HCPs should advise men to stop this and make them aware of how their actions can negatively impact their children too. I believe running such programs would be very helpful. (Woman12)

Another woman discussed the importance of increasing awareness about DV among school children too because it would be helpful for them to lead a ‘good family life’ in the future.

I think HCPs could organize programs to raise awareness about domestic violence even among school children. Not for very young kids, but for 18 or 19-year-old students. Before they start a family, they should learn what causes domestic violence and how it can harm their children. Married life is not as beautiful as dating. Nowadays, some kids get married at a very young age and start a family without any knowledge. So, HCPs could educate them about domestic violence and give them advice on how to have a good family life. (Woman14)

Expecting financial and instrumental support

Financial and instrumental support to find a job, a place to live and support to educate their children were prominent concerns expressed by many women. Some of the expectations represent the complex social issues underpinning the family context. Poverty is a major underlying cause of many social problems and the direct and indirect requests for financial aid. A working woman in her late 40s said:

The only thing I expected from the HCPs was their help to find me a better-paying job. I have children. My son doesn’t work, he always hangs out with his friends. His wife is pregnant now too. I’m the one who feeds all of them. So, I thought it would be better if I had a better job. That was the only thing I expected. (Woman19)

Another woman said:

I want to take care of my children and make their future better. So, I expect some financial help from the HCPs. If they can help me buy the things my children need, it would be a great support. It would be really helpful if they [HCPs] could arrange something like giving a parcel with essential food items and other necessities. If not, I would appreciate it if they could provide me some financial assistance monthly to buy books and other educational materials for my children. (Woman12)

Expected personal qualities of HCPs

Women said that the ability of HCPs to provide support depends on their personal qualities. Many women discussed the importance of HCPs paying more attention to DV, such as being confidential, empathetic and listening when disclosed. They expected a person who is sensitive to their issues and who would offer support with kindness while maintaining privacy and confidentiality. A housewife in her early 20s said:

I told the doctor that my husband hit my pregnant belly and I was afraid something might happen to my baby. Doctors and nurses know better than me what happens when a pregnant mother is hit like that. They should have talked to my husband and told him to stop, but they didn’t. I think they shouldn’t be like this and should pay more attention to this problem [DV]. (Woman05)

Some women mentioned that HCPs should also encourage women to disclose DV by asking directly about DV experiences. A working woman in her early 30s said:

Sometimes women don’t open up on their own. But if a doctor asks them questions about their family life, if they are having any problems at home, or how their children are doing, they might share their issues. Then, the doctors can offer them the right support. So, it’s helpful if HCPs ask women first. (Woman09)

Women stressed the importance of HCPs maintaining confidentiality and protecting women’s privacy. A working woman in her late 30s said:

Sometimes HCPs ask about our problems in front of other patients and other people. No matter who is around, they ask even if there are a lot of people around. I think HCPs shouldn’t do that. (Woman12)

Many women also expected emotional support from the HCPs for their problems. They said they needed a person who could confidentially listen to their story and thereby help them get a sense of relief from their problems.

I want to talk about my pain with the HCPs and free my mind. It will help me feel better. I would really appreciate it if they [HCPs] could help me get rid of this pain. (Woman06)

Some of the participants suggested strategies to share the pain and find relief.

I hope that HCPs will help me make up my mind. I hope they will talk to me in a way that makes me feel better. It would also be helpful if they could bring women who experience abuse, like me, together in one place and talk to them. That way, we could all feel like we’re getting support and can clear our minds. (Woman10)

Referrals to counselling services and gender-based violence centres

Counselling and women support services are not common in the current study setting. However, some participants expected that the HCPs could refer women to counselling centres. A housewife in her early 20s said:

Once when I was pregnant, my husband hit me, and I ended up in the hospital. I told the doctors what happened and hoped they would refer both me and my husband to counseling. But they didn’t. This time, I asked the HCPs in the ward to refer us both to counseling, and they referred us to Mithuru Piyasa. (Woman05)

In particular, another woman suggested establishing Mithuru Piyasa centres in rural hospitals to increase the referral options for women experiencing DV in rural areas. She said:

Even though HCPs treat our illnesses, they don’t pay enough attention to domestic violence when we’re in the hospital. This time, I was referred to Mithuru Piyasa only because I was transferred to the Kandy hospital. If I had been at the village hospital, they wouldn’t have been able to refer me to Mithuru Piyasa. So, I think it would be very helpful if Mithuru Piyasa centers were set up in village hospitals too. That way, HCPs could refer us there, and we could get the help we need. (Woman13)

Discussion

This qualitative study explored the expectations of women experiencing DV. This study identified that women who experience DV expect various support strategies from HCPs including improved favourable communication, building family cohesion, financial and other material support, organisation of community programmes and proper referral mechanisms to overcome their problems informing the necessity of multisectoral approaches in control and prevention of DV.

A majority of women expected help to stop the violence and improve family cohesion. Although this finding was raised by some other studies from both high-income countries and lower-middle-income countries such as the USA and Lebanon,31 32 this has not been previously reported in Sri Lanka. The fact that women expressed the wish to keep their family together despite the abuse, may be due to multiple complex factors. On one hand, it may represent the socio-cultural constructs of honour and shame seen in Sri Lanka as well as in other Asian countries.5 33 34 In Asian communities, including Sri Lanka, the woman’s role is often considered as being a ‘good wife’, maintaining the reputation of her family and the marriage.3537 Another key factor is likely to be a lack of economic independence on the part of the women. Due to women’s economic dependence on their husbands, they may have no other financial resources, no place to go and no way to care for their children, if they leave their abusive husbands.38 39 This is likely to be an important factor that contributes to the women’s request that the HCPs should intervene with the couple, to help reduce the DV and help the family stay together without violence. Shelters or safe-homes for women facing DV are extremely limited or not available in most areas of the country, further contributing to this problem.

Some international studies from high-income countries, including Australia and the Netherlands, show that women are more likely to request HCPs’ support to leave the abusive partner, rather than expecting HCPs to ‘fix their problem’,40 41 which is in contrast to what we have described. Other studies report contrasting findings, where women have said that couple counselling was not successful for DV issues.42 The appropriateness of mediation/couple counselling needs careful further consideration in Sri Lanka—not all women who we interviewed requested that the HCP speak to the couple. The safety and appropriateness of this kind of intervention would need to be evaluated on a case-by-case basis, always ensuring that it does not increase the risk to the women. HCPs would also need training on how to approach such discussions. Further research is warranted regarding situations where counselling of the couple would be relevant and safe, and the best ways in which to approach this kind of discussion.

Women in this study said they preferred to disclose DV to HCPs in the context of a confidential, non-judgmental, empathic and caring consultation, and they would expect emotional as well as instrumental support, in line with previous findings.4244 Many of the women saw HCPs at all levels as persons who would be in a position to counsel them, reflecting the social status they attribute to the healthcare profession. HCPs, including doctors, are expected to be good counsellors.45

Many women in our study expected HCPs to provide medical treatment to the perpetrator for their drug abuse, alcohol addiction and psychiatric problems. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time this has been reported locally. This reflects the concepts of empathy and love that inhibit women’s decision to leave their abusive relationship, as well as their lack of financial independence or other options, and their belief or hope that the husband will change.5 46 47 This finding contradicts studies from high-income countries, such as the Netherlands and the USA, which reported that women seek medical treatment for drug/alcohol addiction, depression, anxiety and physical injuries/ symptoms for themselves rather than their partners.41 42 Despite wanting the perpetrator to get treatment from HCPs, women expressed difficulty in getting their partner to attend. Therefore, it would be worthwhile to explore innovative ways in which HCPs can offer support to the woman and the husband (perpetrator) when she requests it, while ensuring the women’s safety and preventing further risk of violence.

In the current study, only some women asked for referrals, whereas international studies from the USA have shown that almost all women who experience DV expect referrals to and information on available support services such as police, a social worker, shelter homes, counselling and mental health specialists.48 49 This may be due to the lack of knowledge about what support services are available or an awareness that the provision of support such as shelters, or employment assistance, is realistically minimal in Sri Lanka. Some women in this study did wish for financial help and instrumental support to find a job, a place to live and educate their children, similar to other international studies conducted in the USA and the Netherlands, but many of these needs are beyond the role of HCPs.48 50 This highlights the importance of linking health to other services, such as social services, that are better placed to provide financial aid and employment opportunities for women. The combination and complex interactions of social determinants including poverty, low level of education, social isolation and lack of social support when combined with alcohol and violence lead to adverse outcomes such as suicides in rural Sri Lankan settings.51 Hence, it would be propitious to consider HCP contact as an early window of opportunity to link women for other support services.

The suggestion to implement community programmes aimed at increasing awareness of DV and the services provided by HCPs was raised in this study as well as in previous studies. Women have previously suggested that HCPs place posters, pamphlets, flyers and hotline numbers of DV support services at doctors’ offices, emergency departments, toilets and examination rooms to increase awareness of services available.42 52 In the present study, women also proposed the idea of providing sex and gender education separately for groups of men and women. Community education and awareness of gender, shifting gender norms and sex education emerge as key areas to consider in the prevention of DV. SASA! is a successful community mobilisation approach developed by Raising Voices for African communities and currently used in many countries around the world to prevent violence against women, aimed at changing social norms and attitudes leading to a power imbalance between women and men.53 These programmes can be adapted to the local setting in Sri Lanka. Young people of both genders should be targeted if attempting to reduce DV among the next generation. As an example, the RESPECT women framework by WHO and United Nations suggests that programmes can be arranged for young people in universities, schools and communities to promote gender equality and gender-sensitive education.54 In keeping with this, some higher educational authorities in Sri Lanka have already suggested organising seminars and programmes on gender, masculinities and services and gender-based violence in the universities,55 and such endeavours could be further promoted.

Research, practice and policy implications

Findings from the present study suggest broader intervention approaches that would be helpful in improving HCPs’ responses to DV in Sri Lanka. The main suggestion is to develop holistic interventions to improve the communication skills of HCPs coupled with family counselling, appropriate psychiatric or medical referrals and referrals for social support services. It is important to enhance the favourable attitudes and skills of HCPs in managing women and families with DV. This should be done while ensuring that the women are not placed at increased risk of violence and should be evaluated with further research. Counselling training for primary healthcare officers such as PHMs in the current study setting will also be beneficial as PHMs are more likely to be in contact with women and their families than other HCPs. Enhancing the skills of HCPs would be highly beneficial as the study findings reveal the importance of building trust and improving soft skills. Previous studies also show that communication skills training for HCPs can help improve communication about intimate partner violence.56 Furthermore, creating policies such as mandating continuous professional development programmes that focus on effective communication skills, empathy and women-centred care is highly recommended.

One of the major factors associated with DV in our study was substance/alcohol misuse and/or mental health issues in the partner. Therefore, the minimisation of substance use disorders, particularly alcohol and drug misuse, should be prioritised, and rehabilitation services for people with substance misuse or dependency should be made available throughout the country, at community levels.57

Some of the expectations of women are beyond the scope of HCPs, and this highlights the importance of creating and strengthening referral pathways between the healthcare setting and other support services. Therefore, it is crucial to implement policies that mandate referral pathways to access additional support services such as social services.

The empowerment of women is another important aspect to be addressed. RESPECT Women: Preventing violence against women-Implementation package by WHO and the United Nations for policymakers suggests interventions such as psychosocial support and psychological interventions for women experiencing DV, combining economic and social empowerment programmes, working with couples to improve communication and relationship skills and group-based participatory education with both women and men to generate critical reflections about unequal gender power relationships.54 These empowerment approaches can be adapted and implemented in the local context to address the issue of DV.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study in Sri Lanka to explore what types of support women experiencing DV would like from HCPs. The qualitative nature of the study provides in-depth insight that will aid in the detailed planning of intervention programmes to improve HCPs’ responses to DV. In addition, using independent coding of interview transcripts, codebook design and identification of themes by two investigators for verification provided a deeper understanding of participants‘ experiences.

Limitations

In this study, we only interviewed women who were already in contact with health services. The views and expectations of women who are not in contact with these services may be quite different from those who have made contact with healthcare services. Therefore, the findings of this study may not be generalisable to all women in the community and women in other geographic areas. In addition, the sample over-represented Sinhalese women and married women, limiting the diverse perspectives and expectations of women from different cultures and experiences. Furthermore, the two Mithru Piyasa centres may not be representative of all Mithru Piyasa centres in Sri Lanka due to geographical limitations, limited resources and population diversity.

Conclusions

Women expected HCPs’ active involvement in responding to DV. The gap between women’s expectations and the support they actually receive from HCPs should be bridged by implementing interventions at the community level and HCPs level, as well as policy interventions to provide optimal support for women experiencing DV in Sri Lanka. Training HCPs to help them develop knowledge and skills to respond appropriately and provide optimal support for women facing DV is a timely recommendation. Innovative methods, such exploring strategies and situations where HCPs could talk safely with the women and their partners to help reduce conflict, will help meet some of the women’s expectations. Involvement in multiple sectors of support, particularly social services, is essential. Advocating for policies to minimise the burden of alcohol and other substance abuse is another major concern to be addressed. Furthermore, strategies such as introducing shelter homes, hotlines for seeking help and providing practical assistance with aspects such as employment in South Asian and low- and middle-income countries like Sri Lanka will also be helpful in empowering women.
