Safety Profile of the Non-steroidal Anti-inflammatory Drug Celecoxib in the Short-Term Management of Acute Non-cancer Pain: A Systematic Review with Meta-analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials
Authors: Carlos Mesa-Castrillon, Ye Tian, Jessica Hughes, Paula Beckenkamp, Michelle Hall, Dimitri Papadimitriou, Paulo Ferreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo summarise the literature regarding the safety of short-term use of the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) celecoxib.Study DesignSystematic review with meta-analysis of randomised trials. Participants comprised individuals of all ages with acute non-cancer pain. Interventions included celecoxib at 200–400 mg/day for up to 10 days. The comparators were placebo, other NSAIDs (including cyclooxygenase-2 [COX-2] inhibitors and non-selective NSAIDS [nsNSAIDS]), or opioids.Data SourcesFive databases were searched from inception to April 2025: Embase, Web of Science, MEDLINE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Scopus. Additionally, a registry was searched: ClinicalTrials.gov.Data SynthesisMeta-analyses using Mantel-Haenszel and random-effects model were used to calculate risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for severe cardiovascular, respiratory, and gastrointestinal adverse events and secondary outcomes. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for randomised trials (RoB-2) was used to assess bias risk. The Grading of Recommendation Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) was conducted to assess the certainty of evidence of each reported outcome.ResultsTitle/abstract and full text screening comprised 3976 and 273 studies, respectively. Fifty studies were included with 10,693 participants. The RRs for adverse events were no different between celecoxib and placebo for severe events (3 studies) (RR 0.44 [95% CI 0.10–2.03]), cardiovascular (3 studies) (RR 0.84 [95% CI 0.24–2.92]), respiratory (4 studies) (RR 1.23 [95% CI 0.29–5.26]), and gastrointestinal events (33 studies) (RR 0.96 [95% CI 0.64–1.43]). There was no difference between celecoxib and NSAIDS for gastrointestinal adverse events, RR 0.89 (95% CI 0.68–1.17). Celecoxib had a lower risk compared to opioids for gastrointestinal events, RR 0.34 (95% CI 0.14–0.86), and showed a lower risk of nausea compared with placebo, RR 0.75 (95% CI 0.60–0.93), and nsNSAIDS, RR 0.80 (95% CI 0.64–0.99). Most studies had some risk of bias concerns, and the overall certainty of evidence for most outcomes was very low. Celecoxib appears to be safe for acute non-cancer pain when compared to placebo, NSAIDS, and opioids. It had a lower risk compared to opioids for gastrointestinal adverse events in general, nausea and vomiting, as well as a lower risk for nausea adverse events when compared to placebo and nsNSAIDS.RegistrationPROSPERO—CRD42025642152.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40265-026-02377-z.

Introduction

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDS) encompass both nonselective NSAIDS (nsNSAIDS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) selective inhibitors, also known as coxibs. Celecoxib is a COX-2 selective inhibitor with demonstrated efficacy for analgesia in acute pain following surgery, musculoskeletal and/or soft tissue injury [1]. The toxicity of celecoxib is largely consistent with other NSAIDS, including cardiovascular toxicity [2]. The acute toxicity reported at a 400-mg daily dose is mostly related to chronic use (e.g., more than 12 weeks), where celecoxib has a favourable gastrointestinal safety profile relative to nsNSAIDS [3, 4]. Furthermore, adverse effects such as hypertension, heart failure and renal toxicity are comparable with other NSAIDS [5]. Although celecoxib has a well-characterised safety profile relative to other NSAIDS, most available evidence relates to long-term use. The safety of short-term celecoxib use at commonly prescribed doses (200–400 mg/day) for acute non-cancer pain remains less defined.

Celecoxib was made available in the Australian market in 2025 without a medical prescription but under pharmacist consultation in tablets or capsules of 200 mg or less, in a primary pack not containing more than 10 dosage units for the short-term treatment (e.g., up to 5 days) of acute pain due to primary dysmenorrhea, musculoskeletal, or soft tissue injuries in adults [6]. Australia is the first country in which celecoxib has been made available without a prescription (implemented in February 2024), but under the supervision of a pharmacist to provide benefit to the public through increased access for short-term use (e.g., up to 5 days). In contrast, celecoxib use still requires a prescription in the USA, Canada, the UK, Ireland, and New Zealand [7].

In terms of safety, celecoxib has a well-established safety profile similar to other NSAIDS, and any risk factors such as adverse effects, interactions, and contraindications are known, identifiable and manageable by a pharmacist [6]. The comparative safety profile in long-term treatment has been extensively examined [2, 8]. A Cochrane systematic review with meta-analysis [9] published in 2013, described the effectiveness and tolerability of a single dose of celecoxib for acute postoperative pain; however, there is a paucity of evidence reviewing its comparative safety profile in a short-term setting (e.g., up to 10 days). Given differences in exposure duration and clinical context, a focused evaluation of short-term safety is warranted. This study aimed to summarise the literature regarding the safety of celecoxib at 200–400 mg/day for acute non-cancer pain (e.g., post-surgery, primary dysmenorrhea, musculoskeletal or soft tissue injuries) in short-term treatment (e.g., up to 10 days).

Methods

The protocol for this systematic review with meta-analysis was registered in PROSPERO – CRD42025642152 (Available from https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42025642152). This systematic review was reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Appendix 1) [10]. The followings amendments to the protocol were performed, before the search strategy was set up and the screening process started: 1) Two additional databases were included (e.g., Web of Sciences and Embase); 2) Information about the funder name and date of award was updated, 3) JH was added as an author for contributing to the development of the search strategy and set up of Covidence for the screening, 4) Opioids were added as a study outcome comparator, 5) DP was added as an author for contributing to the conceptualisation, methodology, and review. The review also followed the good Publication Practice guidelines [11].

Search Strategy

The literature research was conducted from inception to 2 April 2025 in five bibliographic databases, including Embase, Web of Science, MEDLINE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Scopus. Additionally, a registry was searched: ClinicalTrials.gov (grey literature). The search strategy (Appendix 1 in the supplementary material) was developed in collaboration with The University of Sydney Library and included a combination of medical subject headings terms and keywords related to celecoxib and pain. References of the final selection of articles were also checked for any additional relevant studies. No date or language restrictions were applied; however, the search was limited to human subjects and randomised controlled trials.

Study Selection

The eligibility criteria included randomised controlled trials reporting on the safety profile of celecoxib. The population included people of all ages with or without non-cancer pain (e.g., including but not limited to healthy individuals, headaches, menstrual cramps [dysmenorrhea], muscle aches, toothaches, sprains and strains, osteoarthritis, arthritis flare-ups, pre- and post-surgical pain, back pain, joint pain, tendonitis, bursitis, fibromyalgia). Conference abstracts, presentations, and studies without accessible full texts were excluded due to insufficient methodological detail and lack of complete outcome reporting, which precluded eligibility assessment, risk-of-bias evaluation, and data extraction.

Studies reporting counts of adverse events (AEs) instead of the number of individuals reporting AEs were excluded. Similarly, we excluded studies that did not report person-level binary outcomes (e.g., the number of participants with AEs) and the total number of participants in each group.

Covidence®, a literature review support software, was used to screen the studies. We used the Cochrane RCT classifier within Covidence, a validated machine-learning tool that identifies records with a high probability of being randomised controlled trials based on text-mining algorithms. This tool was used to assist in prioritising screening; however, all records were independently reviewed by the study investigators to confirm eligibility. Duplicates were removed manually and automatically via Covidence. Two independent reviewers (CMC and YT) completed the screening of titles and abstracts and the full-text screening. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Data extraction was performed in Covidence and before data charting, two reviewers (CMC and YT) piloted a random sample of two publications and necessary amendments were made. Once formal data extraction commenced, no further alterations were made. Inter-rater reliability was assessed through a correlation coefficient (e.g., random agreement probability) and Cohen’s Kappa [12]

Intervention

The intervention focused on celecoxib (capsules, tablets or liquid) at 200–400 mg/day in short-term use (e.g., up to 10 days), regardless of the frequency. The comparators were placebo, other NSAIDs (including non-selective NSAIDs, abbreviated as nsNSAIDs and/or other COX-2 selective inhibitors), and opioids. We excluded studies that combined celecoxib with other medications to limit the potential for confounding and isolate the safety profile of celecoxib.

Sub-group analysis of the AEs was performed according to the comparator type (e.g., celecoxib versus placebo group, celecoxib vs NSAIDs, and celecoxib vs opioids). When a study presented data on more than two arms, data were combined into a single group based on the comparator drug class (i.e., other NSAIDs or opioids) when appropriate (e.g., rofecoxib and ibuprofen groups were combined for the other NSAIDs group).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the main AEs (e.g., cardiovascular and/or cerebrovascular, gastrointestinal, renal, other, severe AEs), reported by the participants of different studies, followed up to 10 days after the randomisation. We adopted the original trial authors’ classification of events. Secondary outcomes included withdrawals from the clinical trial, constipation, diarrhea, epigastric pain, headache, nausea, skin symptoms, vomiting, and any AEs (e.g., other than those specified).

Two independent reviewers (CMC and YT), performed independent data extraction from the selected studies including the following information: title, lead author and contact details, the country in which the study was conducted, publication date, study funding sources, conflicts of interest, total sample size, patient characteristics, disease/condition characteristics (e.g., location, number of joints affected), treatment characteristics (e.g., drug used, comparator), raw data (number of events) for outcomes of interest including cardiovascular/cerebrovascular AEs (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke), gastrointestinal AEs (e.g., adverse reactions occurring in the gastrointestinal tract), other AEs (e.g., nervous system AEs, ocular AEs—visual impairment, renal AEs), severe AEs (medically significant but not immediately life-threatening), serious adverse effects (life-threatening AEs, disability or permanent damage, or death).

When several time-points were available, we extracted the total number of individuals reporting unique AEs, for example, summing the cases of all time-points available. Where studies did not describe the time of endpoint assessment of AEs, the last endpoint reported was used. Adverse events related to the study drug were preferred over AEs reported as not related to the study drug. Whenever possible, data from intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses were used.

Risk of Bias (Quality) Assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for randomised trials (RoB-2) was used to assess bias risk [13]. This tool evaluates the studies on specific areas of bias related to trial design, conduct, and reporting. Parallel and crossover trials were assessed separately using their respective assessment form. Each area has a set of questions to gather relevant information about trial characteristics that could affect bias risk. Based on the answers, an algorithm provided an assessment of the risk of bias for each area, categorised as "low", "some concerns", or “high”. Two independent reviewers (CMC and YT) conducted the risk of bias assessment. Any disagreement was resolved by discussion.

Certainty of Evidence

The Grading of Recommendation Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) was conducted to assess the certainty of evidence of each reported outcome [14]. The overall evidence for each outcome was downgraded from high by one level (high, moderate, low and very low certainty) based on (1) limitations in study design; (2) study limitations, when more than 25% of the participants were from studies with a high risk of bias; (3) inconsistency of results through heterogeneity was based on the similarity of point estimates (poor similarity suggest heterogeneity), the extent of overlap of confidence intervals (CIs) (poor overlap suggest heterogeneity), test of heterogeneity (Cochran Q test where low values < 0.05 suggest heterogeneity ), and I2 test (where high values > 50% suggest heterogeneity); (4) indirectness, when > 25% of studies had a poor representation of the outcome (when definitions of the outcome varied) or use a non-general population); (5) imprecision when there were fewer than 2000 patients in the pooled analysis and the 95% CI included no effect (i.e., CI around risk ratio [RR] excludes 1.0); and (6) publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots, in which the effect estimates from individual studies are plotted against a measure of their precision (e.g., standard error). According to the Cochrane Handbook, a symmetrical, inverted funnel shape suggests the absence of publication bias, whereas asymmetry may indicate potential publication bias, such as the non-publication of smaller studies with negative or non-significant results [15, 16].

Statistical Analysis

A meta-analysis was performed using the Review Manager software version 5.3 [17]. A random-effects model was employed to pool the number of individuals reporting AEs from the included studies. The Cochrane Handbook recommends the use of a random-effects model when heterogeneity is anticipated, as it assumes that the true effect size may differ across studies rather than being fixed [16]. Person-level binary outcomes (e.g., the number of participants with AEs) and the total number of participants in each group were used to analyse outcome data. Mantel-Haenszel was the statistical method used to calculate the RR, 95% CI, and generate forest plots. Heterogeneity was assessed statistically using the I2 statistic. A higher I2 value (typically over 50%–90%) indicates substantial heterogeneity [18].

The tables were presented into the following main categories: severe AEs (medically significant but not immediately life-threatening), cardiovascular/cerebrovascular AEs (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke); respiratory AEs (e.g., upper respiratory tract infection), gastrointestinal AEs (e.g., adverse reactions occurring in the gastrointestinal tract); renal AEs, other AEs (e.g., nervous system AEs, ocular AEs—visual impairment); serious adverse effects (life-threatening AEs, disability or permanent damage, congenital anomaly/birth defect or death). Studies were included and pooled irrespective of their specific follow-up duration, provided that outcome assessment fell within the predefined 10-day follow-up period.

When grouping person-level binary outcomes of related events for pooling (e.g., vomiting and nausea within gastrointestinal disorders), a standard procedure was followed: first, the outcome with the most severe clinical prediction was selected over outcomes with less severe clinical prediction (e.g., hypertension and sinusitis were selected over palpitation and nasal congestion, respectively), to avoid the risk of double-counting. Second, if both outcomes were deemed equally severe, then the outcome with the higher number of events was selected.

Results

The search yielded 10,501 studies, and after removing duplicates, 3976 studies were screened, with 3650 of them excluded at the title/abstract level. The complete list of studies excluded after full-text review can be accessed in Appendix 2. In the full text stage, 326 studies were sought for retrieval, with 53 excluded because the full text was not available. A substantial proportion of these records corresponded to clinical trial registrations without available results, often in early phases of recruitment. Finally, 273 studies were screened at the full-text level, with 223 studies being excluded, mostly because of the wrong publication type (30%), wrong timeframe (17%), and not reporting AEs (14%). Fifty randomised trials were included for meta-analysis. Appendix 3 presents the PRISMA flowchart of the screening process. Appendix 4 lists the included studies and Appendix 5 describes their characteristics, which included a total sample of 10,693 participants, 7466 (70%) women, and 44 (88%) parallel trials. More than half of the studies (56%) were published before 2015, and only 7 (14%) were published in the last 5 years. Appendix 6 shows the description of the interventions, including 39 (78%) studies with surgical procedures, 22 (44%) studies that prescribed a single dose of celecoxib, and 26 (52%) studies that had a treatment duration of one day, with an average of 3 days (range 1 to 7 days). Regarding the risk of bias, most of the parallel trials (54%) had “some concerns”, while 34% and 12% had low and high-risk bias, respectively. Similarly, most of the crossover trials (67%) had “some concerns”, while 16% and 17% had low and high-risk bias, respectively. Overall, 28 (56%) studies had at least “some concerns”, and more than half of the studies, 32 (64%), were rated down because of the absence of a clinical trial number registration or protocol publication (Appendix 7). All primary outcomes showed a very low overall certainty of evidence, except for celecoxib versus NSAIDs on gastrointestinal AEs, which showed a low overall certainty of evidence (Appendix 8). Overall, publication bias is strongly suspected across all outcomes, except for celecoxib versus NSAIDs on gastrointestinal AEs (Appendix 8).

Severe AEs

Three studies [19–21] reported a total of six severe AEs among 680 participants, and only one study [20] reported a description of the severe events that included alveolar osteitis, dizziness, flushing, headache, incisional site pain, pallor, post-procedural site swelling, and vomiting. Thirty-three studies [19–51] reported gastrointestinal AEs, which included faecal occult blood positive, vomiting, diarrhea, epigastric pain, and dyspepsia. Three studies reported serious or moderate AEs. Manvelian et al [20] reported 9 events from 51 participants for the celecoxib group, while 11 events from 100 participants for the diclofenac group, and four in the placebo group (51 participants). Langford et al [19] reported one serious event among 206 participants in the celecoxib group versus one event in the opioid group (206 participants), and no events in the placebo group (102 participants). Cheung et al [22] reported one case of rhabdomyolysis among 57 participants in the celecoxib group, the serious event was related to the intervention. However, several pre-study and pre-surgical medications were also described for the participant involved. Only one study [24] reported renal AEs (i.e., urinary retention), one event in both the celecoxib and placebo groups from 38 and 36 participants, respectively.

Appendix 9 shows the secondary analyses of celecoxib versus placebo, NSAIDs, and opioids on trial withdrawals, any AEs, constipation, diarrhoea, epigastric pain, headache, nausea, skin symptoms, and vomiting. Overall, there were no statistical differences between intervention and comparators except for a lower risk for celecoxib versus placebo, nsNSAIDs and opioids on nausea AEs, RR 0.75 (95% CI 0.60–0.93); RR 0.80 (95% CI 0.64–0.99) and RR 0.46 (95% CI 0.27–0.79), respectively.

Discussion

This systematic review with meta-analysis summarises the safety of celecoxib for acute non-cancer pain in short-term treatment (up to seven days). The main findings include no differences in the risk of most AEs when celecoxib was compared to placebo, NSAIDs, and opioids, although with a very low certainty of evidence and a high rate of risk of bias mainly due to lack of clinical trial registration. Celecoxib showed a significantly lower risk compared to opioids for gastrointestinal AEs, as well as nausea and vomiting (very low certainty of evidence). Additionally, celecoxib had a significantly lower risk of nausea AEs compared to placebo and nsNSAIDs (very low certainty of evidence).

This study provides very low or low certainty of evidence on the safety profile of celecoxib for acute non-cancer pain in short-term treatment and at the recommended dose of 200–400 mg/day. The findings are in line with a Cochrane systematic review of randomised trials [9], which showed that adults prescribed a single dose of celecoxib for acute postoperative pain experienced a similar proportion of AEs with no significant difference between celecoxib at 400 mg (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.84–1.17) or 200 mg (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.63–1.29) compared to placebo [3].

Additionally, our findings are in line with the literature that celecoxib is safer compared to nsNSAIDs such as naproxen (HR 0.51; 95% CI 0.32–0.81), and ibuprofen (HR 0.43; 95% CI 0.27–0.68) in terms of gastrointestinal adverse effects in the long term. A key message of the Australian and New Zealand College of Anaesthetists and Faculty of Pain Medicine includes that short-term use (5–7 days) of a COX-2 inhibitor such as celecoxib results in gastric ulceration rates similar to placebo and lower than nsNSAIDs [52]. A Bayesian meta-analysis showed that with use for one to seven days, the cardiovascular risk of myocardial infarction for celecoxib (OR 1.24; 95% CI 0.91–1.82) compared with non-use of NSAIDs, was comparable to traditional NSAIDs but appeared modestly lower than naproxen (OR 1.58; 95% CI 1.07–2.17), ibuprofen (OR 1.50; 95% CI 1.06–2.04), and diclofenac (OR 1.53; 95% CI 1.07–2.33), each compared with non-use of NSAIDs [53].

Clinicians should also consider the Australian Medicines Handbook [54] which states that both COX‑2 inhibitors and nsNSAIDs are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events; meanwhile, COX‑2 inhibitors are commonly associated with a lower risk of gastrointestinal AEs compared to nonselective NSAIDs. Similarly, the Australian Therapeutic Guidelines Rheumatology [55], indicates that individual characteristics (e.g., age and history of cardiovascular events) and factors such as dose and duration of medication could increase the risk of AEs independently of the type of NSAIDs.

Our findings are broadly consistent with existing guideline recommendations, which suggest that COX-2 inhibitors such as celecoxib are associated with a lower risk of gastrointestinal AEs compared with nsNSAIDs, while cardiovascular risks remain a concern. However, most guidance is based on evidence from longer-term use or mixed indications. The present study extends this evidence by evaluating short-term use (≤10 days) at commonly prescribed doses (200–400 mg/day) in acute non-cancer pain. These results provide targeted evidence to inform short-term prescribing decisions, suggesting that while gastrointestinal safety may be favourable, careful consideration of patient-specific cardiovascular risk remains warranted.

We did not find studies comparing AEs between celecoxib and NSAIDs or opioids for serious, cardiovascular, and respiratory AEs. Regarding secondary outcomes, we did not find studies reporting AEs between celecoxib and opioids for diarrhoea, epigastric pain, and headache.

The findings of this study support the current policy to allow the supply of celecoxib for short-term use under the supervision of a pharmacist and add to the body of evidence regarding the safety of celecoxib in short-term use [7]. However, AEs might be mitigated through appropriate use, following the supervision of a pharmacist and the therapeutic indications, contraindications, warnings, and precautions for use.

Limitations

Although the study has some important strengths, limitations should be acknowledged. Misclassification of AEs in original studies could affect the precision of the results; for example, some studies reported events such as nausea, headache, or vomiting as severe AEs. Most studies had some concerns regarding the risk of bias, primarily due to not reporting a clinical trial registration number, which introduces bias in the selection of the reported results. Additionally, the certainty of evidence of most of the primary outcomes was assessed as very low, because of risk of bias, small number of participants and AEs (affecting precision of the CIs), and publication bias. Pharmaceutical companies funded most of the included studies (42%), while 26% of studies did not report funding, 22% of the studies were funded with public, competitive, or university grants, and 10% of the studies declared had no funding. Another limitation was the lack of data stratified by sex and gender, which prevented sub-analysis. In the assessment of publication bias, we did not perform trim-and-fill analyses because the number and size of included studies for most of the primary outcomes were limited [16], reducing the reliability and interpretability of such methods in this context; therefore, conclusions about publication bias may vary. Similarly, funnel plot asymmetry should be interpreted cautiously, as it may also arise from other factors, including heterogeneity, methodological differences, or chance, particularly when the number of studies is fewer than 10 [16]. Another limitation of the study concerns sensitivity analyses, which were planned in the protocol study to be carried out according to the follow-up time (time-point); however, due to the limited number of studies within each outcome, sensitivity analyses were not possible. Finally, we acknowledge that the very low number of events is reflected in wide CIs and limits the interpretability of comparisons. This constraint is inherent to the available evidence base, and we have taken a cautious approach in interpreting these findings in the manuscript. However, to address this issue, we also ran the Peto odds ratio statistical model, which can be advantageous in meta-analyses involving rare events. The results showed no substantial difference from the current results (Appendix 10).

In conclusion, this systematic review with meta-analysis found that celecoxib at 200–400 mg/day may be safe for acute non-cancer pain in short-term treatment when compared to placebo, NSAIDs, and opioids, although with a very low certainty of evidence. Additionally, celecoxib had a lower risk compared to opioids for gastrointestinal AEs in general, as well as nausea and vomiting, with a very low certainty of evidence. Finally, celecoxib showed a statistically significantly lower risk for nausea AEs when compared to placebo and nsNSAIDs.
