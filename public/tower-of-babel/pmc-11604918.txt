Differential regulation of brain microvessel transcriptome and brain metabolome by western and heart-healthy dietary patterns in Ossabaw pigs
Authors: Gloria Solano-Aguilar, Gregory Matuszek, Nirupa R Matthan, Alice H Lichtenstein, Xuedi Wang, Sukla Lakshman, Kathryn Barger, Joseph F Urban, Aleksey Molokin, Rachel E Bennett, Bradley T Hyman, Stefania Lamon-Fava
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiet is a potentially modifiable neurodegenerative disease risk factor. We studied the effects of a typical Western diet (WD; high in refined carbohydrates, cholesterol and saturated fat), relative to a heart-healthy diet (HHD; high in unrefined carbohydrates, polyunsaturated fat and fiber, and low in cholesterol) on brain microvessel transcriptomics and brain metabolomics of the temporal region in Ossabaw minipigs. Thirty-two pigs (16 male and 16 females) were fed a WD or HHD starting at the age of 4 months for a period of 6 months. The WD and HHD were isocaloric and had a similar macronutrient content but differed in macronutrient quality. Within each dietary group, half of the pigs also received atorvastatin. Relative to HHD-fed pigs, WD-fed pigs had 175 genes differentially expressed (fold change > 1.3, FDR < 0.05) by diet, 46 upregulated and 129 downregulated. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis identified 22 gene sets enriched in WD-fed pigs, comprising pathways related to inflammation, angiogenesis, and apoptosis, and 53 gene sets enriched in the HHD-fed pigs, including cell energetics, neurotransmission, and inflammation resolution pathways. Metabolite analysis showed enrichment in arginine, tyrosine, and lysine in WD-fed pigs, and ergothioneine and S-adenosyl methionine in HHD-fed pigs. Atorvastatin treatment did not affect gene expression. These results suggest a likely contribution of diet to brain pathologies characterized by neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81321-1.Keywords: Neuroinflammation, Brain microvessels, Transcriptomics, MetabolomicsSubject terms: Mechanisms of disease, Cellular signalling networks, Data acquisition, Data integration, Data mining, Functional clustering, Cell biology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Molecular biology, Neurology

Introduction

Vascular dementia and Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) are the most common forms of dementia in older adults and represent a major public health burden1. It has been projected that the number of people worldwide with AD will triple by 20501. AD is characterized by the presence of extracellular β-amyloid plaques, composed of abnormally folded Aβ, and neurofibrillary tangles, composed of hyperphosphorylated tau filaments, in the brain2. These abnormalities are associated with loss of neurons and neuronal synapses, with progressive memory loss and impairment in cognitive function1,2. Both environmental and genetic factors play an important role in the initiation and progression of AD3. Epidemiological and clinical evidence suggests a strong link between cardiovascular disease (CVD) and AD, supported by shared risk factors such as chronic low-grade inflammation, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and presence of the apoE4 allele4–8. AD, like CVD, is a chronic disease that becomes symptomatic after decades of sub-clinical progressive pathological alterations and, while much has been learned about the macro- and micro-pathologic brain alterations in fully developed AD, less is known about the initial molecular and metabolic changes. A better understanding of early and potentially modifiable factors may lead to the development of strategies for the prevention of AD-associated neurodegeneration or mitigation of its progression. One such modifiable factor may be diet quality, particularly early in life. The causative role of a Western-type Diet (WD) on CVD has long been known9, and poor diet quality has also emerged as a risk factor for AD3,10. A typical WD is characterized by high dietary intake of saturated fats, cholesterol and refined carbohydrates, accompanied by a low intake of fruit, vegetables and fiber. In contrast, heart-healthy dietary patterns, including the Mediterranean diet, are characterized by low saturated fat and either high polyunsaturated or monounsaturated fatty acids, with higher intake of whole grains, fruit and vegetables, and have been associated with benefits in terms of both CVD and AD risk11–13. Preclinical studies have shown that, relative to a low-fat diet, a high-fat, Western-type diet causes brain vascular alterations in mice14,15, and similar findings have been observed with high glycemic index diets16.

Cerebrovascular inflammation has been suggested as one of the early pathophysiologic alterations in neurodegenerative diseases and AD. Specifically, dysfunction of the blood brain barrier (BBB), characterized by inflammation and increased permeability of the small brain vessels and capillaries, may initiate and promote microglia activation and brain inflammation leading to β-amyloid plaque formation17. Moreover, relative to controls, AD patients present with alterations in brain metabolism, including metabolites in TCA cycle and markers of arginine and glutathione metabolism18. Alteration in brain TCA cycle metabolites have also been demonstrated in mice fed a WD relative to control diet19.

Understanding the brain vascular changes associated with consumption of a WD, relative to a heart-healthy diet (HHD), is relevant to uncovering the early neurovascular dysfunction potentially contributing to AD. We have previously documented that the Ossabaw minipig, an established animal model of diet-induced obesity and metabolic syndrome, develops atherosclerosis when fed a typical humanized WD20. In the current study, we used the Ossabaw minipig to test our hypothesis of a significant role of dietary patterns relevant to human health in early brain alterations by comparing the gene expression of the microvessels in the brain temporal region and brain metabolites in response to WD and HHD. The brain temporal region was selected because reductions in blood flow are pronounced in this region in AD patients21.

To test our hypothesis of a differential effect of diet on brain microvessels, we compared the gene expression of microvessels isolated from the temporal region of pigs fed the WD and HHD, irrespective of statin treatment (n = 15/diet group). We identified 175 differentially regulated genes (fold change > 1.3 and FDR < 0.05), of which 46 were upregulated and 129 were downregulated in WD-fed pigs relative to HHD-fed pigs (Supplementary Table 1). Differentially regulated genes were mostly related to neuronal functions (e.g.: TNN, ADORA2A, GPR88, FOXJ1, FGF13, DNAI1, SORL1, EPHB2, SNCG), immune functions (e.g.: NMT1, OASL, GNLY, GZMA), and metabolism (e.g.: SEC23B, HKDC1, PRF1). There were no genes differentially expressed by statin nor diet by statin interactions. To gain a better insight into the overall biological effects of the diets, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and identified 22 gene sets enriched in WD-fed pigs and 53 gene sets enriched in HHD-fed pigs (FDR < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2). Most of the 22 gene sets enriched in the WD group were overlapping in function and could be grouped into 3 different families: inflammation, extracellular matrix/angiogenesis, and cell death (Fig. 1). The inflammatory pathways included the interferon (IFN) α, β, and γ pathways, cytokine signaling, and the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) pathway via the TNF binding receptors and associated factors, leading to NF-kB activation (Fig. 2A). An enrichment in genes related to immune cell interactions was also observed in WD. Within the extracellular matrix family of gene sets, there was activation of both the synthesis and degradation of collagen pathways (Fig. 2B), and an activation of PDGF (platelet-derived growth factor) signaling. Necrosis, cell death and apoptosis pathways were also enriched by WD (Fig. 2C). The 53 gene sets enriched in the HHD fed pigs could be grouped into 4 major families: cell metabolism, neurotransmitters and synaptic transmission, intracellular trafficking, and immune functions (Fig. 1). Activation of glucose metabolism and mitochondrial functions, represented by the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, the respiratory electron transfer and mitochondrial protein transport gene sets, were observed in the HHD (Fig. 3A). In addition, synaptic and postsynaptic transmission pathways, including the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor pathway, were enriched in HHD (Fig. 3B). Intracellular organelle communication, such as the retrograde Golgi to ER transport which requires coat protein complex I (COPI), was also enriched in the microvessels of pigs fed the HHD relative to the WD (Fig. 3C). In addition, HHD was associated with an enrichment in the anti-inflammatory IL-10 synthesis pathway (Fig. 3D) and pathways of phagocytosis and autophagy. The direction and strength of the associations between the differently regulated pathways are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, where network connections among several pathways upregulated in the WD as well as among those upregulated in HHD were observed.

Integrated multi-omics analysis was used to identify related genes and metabolites. Ergothioneine, EPEA and 13-HODE (a derivative of linoleic acid), which were higher in pigs fed the HHD than WD, were negatively associated with the expression of inflammatory genes (MYADLM, IRF2BP3, MX2, and CAVIN2) and the amino acid transporter SLC3 gene (Fig. 6). A positive association between 5-HETE (a pro-inflammatory derivative of arachidonic acid) and the expression of these genes was observed. Among the complex lipids, PC38:5 and PC40:5, which were significantly elevated in WD-fed pigs, were positively correlated with the inflammatory genes and SLC3, while the TG that were elevated in HHD-fed pigs were inversely correlated with the expression of these genes (Fig. 6).

Four GSEA pathways were enriched in pigs receiving statin treatment and 12 in those without statin treatment, independent of diet (Supplementary Table 3). The pathways increased by statin were mostly related to IFN α, β and γ signaling, while the pathways enriched in non-statin brain microvessels related to synaptic activation and cell interactions. The PLS-DA analysis showed good metabolite separation between the statin and non-statin groups, but no clear lipid metabolite separation (Supplementary Fig. 2). Metabolites did not show a significant diet by statin interaction.

Immunohistochemistry of the temporal region did not identify significant differences in amyloid precursor protein (APP), β-amyloid peptide (Aβ), ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba-1) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) immunostaining between WD and HHD, with or without statin treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 4). A non-significant trend was observed for greater Aβ in WD than HHD (P = 0.076).

Discussion

Our study shows that changing the quality, without changing the quantity, of macronutrients in the diet by substituting polyunsaturated fatty acids, unrefined carbohydrates and high fiber with saturated fatty acids, cholesterol, refined carbohydrates and low fiber, leads to significant differences in microvessel gene expression and brain metabolomics in the temporal brain region in Ossabaw pigs.

The link between CVD and AD has been known for a long time, with several studies showing that established CVD risk factors such as age, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, apo E4, obesity and diabetes promote age-related cognitive decline and AD22,23. Furthermore, vascular pathologies are present in a high percentage of AD patients. It has long been debated whether the cerebrovascular alterations found in AD precede the onset of the characteristic neurodegenerative features of AD or are the consequence of it. Early evidence that vascular abnormalities may function as a first hit, causing hypoperfusion, permeability of the BBB, inflammation and, subsequently, Aβ deposition and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, supports the vascular hypothesis of AD24. Reduction in cerebral blood flow has been documented in the temporal and parietal cortex even in mild AD and has been associated with altered brain metabolism21. Small vessels with spiral or tortuous shape, which may impact blood flow, are typical vascular abnormalities in AD21. The cause of these vessel abnormalities is not known, but aberrant angiogenesis may be one of the contributing factors.

Our data show that consumption of the WD was associated with genes related to biological processes including inflammation, vascular remodeling, and apoptosis and cell death. All these pathways are typically enhanced in angiogenesis25. The WD group also showed an enrichment of biological processes related to the activation of IFN, TNF signaling and NF-kB activation, indicating that the WD leads to inflammation of the small brain blood vessels, which may potentially disrupt the BBB and subsequently lead to changes in the clearance of Aβ26.

Relative to WD, pigs fed the HHD showed activation of cell metabolic processes such as the TCA cycle and electron transport chain, indicative of better mitochondrial function and/or number. Functional differences in TCA cycle enzymes and alterations in cell metabolism and number of mitochondria between AD and controls have been reported27,28. Mitochondria play an important role in the brain by providing adequate metabolic energy to support neuronal activity and prevent excessive reactive oxidative species production29. Moreover, in immune cells, a more efficient TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation is tied to a pro-resolution of inflammation phenotype30. Therefore, a more efficient glycolysis, TCA cycle and respiratory electron transfer in HHD-fed pigs than WD-fed pigs may suggest better mitochondrial functions and cell energetics related to a lower inflammatory status.

Interestingly, several neurotransmission pathways were enhanced in HHD relative to WD fed pigs. Among these, there was an increase in GABA receptor activation. Several studies have shown an age-associated decline in brain GABA concentrations and specifically somatostatin expressing (SST+) GABAergic interneurons, correlated with impaired motor performance and poor cognitive functioning31. Reduced SST + neuron numbers have been commonly observed in AD32,33. In addition, NMDA receptor activation and Ca2+-dependent NO generation have been shown to regulate the tone and to dilate small arteries in the brain34. The HHD was associated with enhanced processes of intracellular trafficking, including the retrograde transport from Golgi to ER. This process, which involves the COPI among others, has been implicated in innate immune function35. Finally, pathways related to the resolution of inflammation and protection from bacterial and viral infections, such as increased IL-10 synthesis, phagocytosis and aggrephagy36,37, were enhanced in the HHD fed pigs.

A broad range of metabolites related to primary metabolism, biogenic amines and several lipid species were measured in the brain temporal region of pigs in our study. PLS-DA demonstrated good separation of the WD and HHD for these metabolites. Among the primary metabolites and biogenic amine metabolites differentially regulated by diet, the amino acids arginine and tyrosine were higher in WD pigs, while ergothioneine was higher in HHD pigs. Evidence of altered brain arginine metabolism in AD is compelling. Several studies have demonstrated higher levels of arginine in the brain of humans with AD38. A study conducted in a mouse model of AD showed significant increases in brain arginine compared to WT mice at 13 weeks of age39. Arginine is metabolized to several molecules with bioactive functions in the brain, including the vasodilator nitric oxide (NO). Molecular studies have suggested a reduction in the capillary expression eNOS, the enzyme catalyzing the synthesis of NO, in patients with AD38. Whether the dysregulation in arginine metabolism is a potential contributor to or a consequence of AD is not currently known. However, our list of 175 DEGs did not include genes affecting NO production or metabolism. Tyrosine is the precursor of the neurotransmitters epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine, but it is not known if the increase in its concentration in WD pig brains is due to altered neurotransmitter synthesis. HHD pigs had higher brain levels of ergothioneine, a potent antioxidant compound that is synthesized by some fungi and bacteria (Lactobacillus reuteri) but not humans and which has been found to be lower in aging and AD brains40. In vivo and in vitro studies have suggested that ergothioneine acts on the gut-brain axis by protecting neuronal cells from oxidative stress and Aβ toxicity41,42. In our study, ergothioneine was negatively correlated with the expression of several inflammatory genes. SAMe and L-cysteine-glutathione disulfide, a ubiquitous glutathione derivative, were also higher in HHD pigs. In a metabolomic study conducted in patients with dementia and normal controls, these metabolites were found to be lower in the blood of dementia patients, thus associating redox reactions and methylation to the pathogenesis of AD43. As expected due the different lipid composition of the diets, the PLS-DA of brain lipids showed a good separation of the WD and HHD pigs. Many of these lipids are complex lipids in the phospholipid and triglyceride families. In addition, the endocannabinoid EPEA was higher in HHD brains. This metabolite of the ω-3 fatty acid EPA can act on cannabinoid receptors and activate anti-inflammatory pathways. Indeed, in our multi-omics analysis, EPEA was inversely associated with genes involved in inflammation. Overall, the altered metabolic profile observed in pigs fed the WD is reminiscent of the alterations observed in AD and other neurodegenerative disorders18. Studies have shown that low saturated fat and low glycemic index diets lead to slower decline in cognition and reduced AD markers44.

Statin treatment had no significant effect on brain microvessel transcriptomics and brain lipids, possibly due to fact that atorvastatin, the statin used in this study, does not cross the BBB.

A limitation of our study was the small number of pigs in each diet/intervention group, which did not allow us to dissect potential differential effects of the diets by sex. The study was originally powered to assess diet-induced atherosclerosis. In addition, pigs received the diets for only six months and were sacrificed at the relatively young age of 10 months, which may have limited our ability to detect potential downstream effects on astrocyte and microglia activation and Aβ deposition. Although attempted, we were unable to measure BBB permeability using an immunohistochemistry-based perivascular IgG assessment approach due to constraints in the accuracy of the methodology. A strength of our study was the isocaloric feeding design, which minimized differences in body weight and body fat distribution between the WD and HHD fed pigs20.

Conclusions

Our data contribute to the current literature by providing evidence of a significant impact of diet on the brain vasculature. Novel findings are that changes in the quality of fat and carbohydrates in the diet, without changes in the quantity of these macronutrients, are associated with vascular pro-inflammatory, pro-angiogenesis and metabolic effects in pig brains. These results are reminiscent of observations in mouse models of AD and of small vessels isolated from AD brains45,46, and support the hypothesis that diet contributes to, and potentially exacerbates, the vascular alterations observed in the aging brain and in AD. Because diet is a modifiable risk factor, these results provide further support for vascular directed interventions in diseases thought of as primarily neurodegenerative. More research is needed to further assess the impact of dietary interventions with different fat and carbohydrate quality on neurodegeneration and cognition in this animal model over an extended period of time, so as to recapitulate the decades of diet exposure in humans. This would also allow for the assessment of potential sex-specific biological mechanisms of neurodegeneration and Alzheimer’s disease. Moreover, understanding the molecular mechanisms of gene expression regulation by diet in the initiation and progression of neurodegenerative diseases may provide further insights into targeted therapeutic approaches.

Methods

Animals and study design

This study was designed to compare the effects of a WD to a HHD, with or without a statin (S), on cardiovascular disease in Ossabaw minipigs20. Briefly, at the age of two months, 32 pigs were randomly stratified by weight and sex into 4 groups (8/group, 4 boars and 4 gilts)—a Western diet (WD) without atorvastatin (WD − S), a WD with atorvastatin (WD + S), a Heart Healthy diet (HHD) without atorvastatin (HHD − S), and an HHD with atorvastatin (HHD + S)—according to a 2 × 2 factorial design. During the first month, pigs were acclimatized and fed a preweighed amount of a “grower” diet containing 20% protein, 4% fat, and 6.5% fiber and supplemented with minerals and vitamins customized to the requirements of growing miniature pigs (Lab Mini-Pig Chow, Labdiet; PMI Nutrition). Over the next month one third of this diet was gradually replaced with an equivalent amount of the WD or HHD until complete diet replacement was done by four months of age. The pigs were subsequently fed the full respective diets for 6 months. Pigs were fed ad libitum and the energy (mean ± SD) consumed by the pigs in all groups was gradually increased from 2174 ± 25 to 5633 ± 12 kcal/d over the feeding period. One pig in the WD died during the acclimatization period and one pig in the HHD died at the baseline blood draw, therefore 30 pigs completed the study. The study protocol was approved by the Tufts Medical Center/Tufts University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

The WD and HHD diets were designed to be similar to typical human diets and have similar macronutrient content, consisting of 38% energy as fat, 47% energy as carbohydrates, and 15% as protein (Supplementary Table 5)20. However, the two diets differed in macronutrient quality, with the WD high in saturated fatty acids, cholesterol and refined carbohydrates and low in fiber, and the HHD high in unsaturated fatty acids, unrefined carbohydrates and fiber, low in cholesterol, and containing a freeze-dried fruit and vegetable mix (Futureceuticals, Momence, IL) (Supplementary Table 5)20. Pigs assigned to the HHD were also supplemented with omega-3 fatty acids (one capsule containing 550 mg EPA and 200 mg DHA 3 times/week). Pigs assigned to statin treatment were given 20 mg of atorvastatin (Lipitor; Pfizer, New York, NY) each day for the first 3 months of the dietary intervention and 40 mg atorvastatin each day during the last 3 months.

Pigs were euthanized at the end of the study by intravenous injection with Euthasol (50 mg sodium pentobarbital/kg body weight; Virbac Animal Health, Inc.)20. Blood was obtained at sacrifice and serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at -80 °C until analyzed. Serum total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and glucose concentrations were measured using standard enzymatic methods using reagents from Roche Diagnostics (Indianapolis, IN)20. The concentration of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was calculated using the Friedewald formula47. Plasma insulin was measured by ELISA (Human, Canine, or Porcine Insulin Quantikine ELISA kit; R&D Systems)20.

Harvesting of brain tissue

After euthanasia, thoracotomy was performed, and the pericardial sac was opened to expose the heart. A 12 F catheter was inserted in the left ventricle to infuse the vascular system with several liters of saline. Following perfusion, the brain was removed, and the left and right hemispheres were carefully separated. The left hemisphere was fixed in 10% formalin. The hippocampus region of the right hemisphere was dissected, and the hemisphere sliced into 10 separate coronal sections, including the frontal, temporal, parietal and occipital regions. Each section was immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C.

Isolation of brain microvessels

Blood microvessels were isolated from the right brain temporal region using an established method45,46,48. This method is designed to purify neurovascular units which comprise small blood vessel and associated pericytes and perivascular astrocyte end-feet48. Briefly, a 150–200 mg frozen slice of the temporal region was minced in sterile HBSS, 10 mM HEPES (buffer B1), homogenized in a Dounce homogenizer, and centrifuged at 2,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet, comprising microvessels and myelin, was resuspended in ice-cold buffer B2 (B1 containing 18 g dextran from Leuconostoc spp/100 mL). After centrifugation at 4,400 g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant and myelin were discarded and the microvessel pellet resuspended in ice-cold buffer B3 (B1 containing 1% BSA), filtered through a 20 μm mesh membrane (Millipore, Burlington, MA), and recentrifuged. The pellet, containing purified brain microvessels was immediately processed for RNA isolation.

RNA isolation and transcriptomic analysis

RNA was isolated from the purified brain microvessels using the Qiagen RNAesy mini kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. TURBO DNA-free kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) was used to remove residual DNA. RNA quality was assessed using an Experion RNA StdSens Analysis kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). All samples had an RNA Quality Indicator greater than 8.

Sample libraries were constructed using Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit v2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Hercules, CA) according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Library quantification was performed using KAPA Library Quantification kit (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA) and library fragment size was determined using Experion DNA 1 K Analysis kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). RNA sequencing was performed on NextSeq 500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) using NextSeq 500/550 Output kit v2.5 (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Unaligned FASTQ files generated from sequencing were imported into CLC Genomics Workbench version 11.0 (QIAGEN, Bioinformatics, Redwood City CA) where they were trimmed to remove low quality reads and adapter sequences. Reads were mapped to non-redundant (NR) (NR_11-13-19) transcript library (GEO repository, accession number GSE263935)49. The sequences for these genes are found in the Porcine Translational Research Database maintained by the Beltsville Human Nutrition Research Center, Diet, Genomics, and Immunology Laboratory and contain only protein coding genes50. Mapped reads for each sample were used to generate gene level expression counts that were used as input for differential gene expression (DGE) analysis.

Metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomics analysis of the brain temporal region was performed by West Coast Metabolomics. Primary metabolites (consisting of a panel of carbohydrates and sugar phosphates, amino acids, hydroxyl acids, free fatty acids, purines, pyrimidines, aromatics, and exposome-derived chemicals) and biogenic amines (comprising acyl-carnitines, TMAO, cholines, betaines, nucleotides and nucleosides and methylated and acetylated amines) were measured as previously described51–53. Complex lipids (ceramides, sphingomyelins, cholesteryl esters, lyso- and phospholipids, mono-, di- and tri-acylglycerols, galactosyl- and glucuronyl-lipids) and oxylipins (polyunsaturated fatty acid-derived lipids by enzymatic action of cyclooxygenase, lipoxygenases, and cytochrome P450) were measured as previously reported54. Brain temporal region fatty acid composition was assessed by gas chromatography and expressed as molar percent of total fatty acids as previously described55.

Immunohistochemistry

Sections of the left hemisphere temporal region were formalin- fixed, paraffin-embedded and subjected to immunohistochemistry with the following primary antibodies: APP (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Invitrogen; cat# CT695), β-amyloid (Biolegend, San Diego, CA; anti-β-amyloid 17–24, cat# 800708), GFAP (Agilent Technologies Cedar Creek, TX; cat# Z0334), and Iba-1 (Fujifilm Wako Chemicals, Richmond, VA; cat# 019-19741).

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Two-factor ANOVA with the main effects of diet, statin, and diet by statin interaction was used to assess differences in variables, including plasma lipids, gene expression and metabolite concentrations. No diet by statin interaction was observed for any of the variables tested, therefore statistical analyses assessing the diet effect were conducted comparing all animals fed the WD (WD -S and WD + S; n = 15) to those fed HHD (HHD -S and HHD + S, n = 15). Similarly, statin effects were assessed by comparing animals treated with statin (WD + S an HHD + S; n = 16) to no statin (WD -S and HHD -S; n = 14).

Differential gene expression and pathway analysis

Differential gene expression analysis of microvessels was performed using the R Bioconductor package DESeq256. Low count genes were removed using a minimum count of 10 in at least 7 samples representing the smallest treatment group size. Differential expression analysis was performed using the single step function, DESeq. Pairwise comparisons for diet effect (WD vs. HHD) or statin effect (statin vs. non-statin) were generated using the results function56. A fold change > 1.3 and FDR-adjusted p-value cutoff of 0.05 were used for classifying differentially expressed genes.

Enrichment analysis of the brain microvessel transcriptomic data was conducted using GSEA version 4.2.157. The expression dataset consisted of 4352 genes, consisting of all genes after low expression genes were removed. The reactome database version 7.5.1 was used for the underlying gene set database, and gene sets were restricted to those containing a minimum of 15 and a maximum of 500 genes to ensure accurate normalization using the built-in normalization methods. This restriction reduced the total number of queried gene sets from 1615 in the full reactome dataset to 531 gene sets. Enriched gene sets were identified based on a combination of FDR cutoff of 0.05 and nominal P value < 0.1 and were ranked based on the normalized enrichment score (NES).

Metabolomics

A total of 764 metabolites were measured from the temporal regions. Of these, 166 were identified and, after filtering out metabolites with missing values, 90 primary metabolites were analyzed. Complex lipids (n = 137) and oxylipins (n = 55) were measured by both positive and negative phases of the mass spectrometer, and fatty acids (n = 43) by gas chromatography. For each lipid, the more sensitive measurement was retained. The negative phase was used for GlcCer, and for LPC, PC, PE, and SM lipids the positive phase values were used. Each dataset was median normalized and log-transformed prior to statistical analysis. Partial Least Squares – Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was performed on the primary metabolite and biogenic amines subset as well as the lipid subset. PLS-DA models were constructed for WD vs. HHD comparison as well as the statin vs. non-statin comparison. For each PLS-DA model, a sparsity function was incorporated to reduce the number of metabolite features to the set that most strongly differentiated between groups. A range of features from 2 to 150 were evaluated using 5-fold 100 repeat cross-validation with the maximum distance used as the distance measure. Each model was evaluated using balanced error rates (BER) and the sparsity function was tuned using BER for selected features to identify maximum model performance. Final sparse PLS-DA models were constructed using the tuned parameters for the optimal number of components and features. Feature stability was assessed over 100 repeat runs and metabolite loadings plots were constructed for each component. For each model, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the strength of the final model to categorize metabolites based on the diet or statin comparison being evaluated.

Integrated multi-omics analysis

To assess the aggregated changes across transcriptomics, metabolites and lipids between the WD and HHD, and statin and non-statin, a block PLS-DA method (DIABLO) was performed. An initial 3 × 3 design matrix was established with correlation values for each pairwise comparison (mRNA x lipids, mRNA x metabolites, lipids x metabolites) set to 0.5 to prioritize strong correlations that are discriminative between the treatment groups. The number of components for each data type are calculated using an initial diablo model using 10-fold cross validation repeated 10 times. As with the PLS-DA model described above, the optimal number of features to include from each dataset is calculated using a tuning function and a pre-specified range for each dataset. For this model we used a range from 9 to 30 for each of the three datasets (mRNA, primary metabolites, lipids and biogenic amines). This tuning block sparse PLS-DA model was performed with 10-fold cross validation and repeated 5 times. The low number of repeats for each model, 10 for component selection and 5 for feature selection are due to the required run-time to calculate. The final diablo model is run using the optimal number of components and features for each dataset calculated above using the design matrix. Correlations between each pairwise comparison in this multi-block sPLS-DA (mRNA x lipid, etc.) are calculated along with 95% confidence intervals. Correlations between features in each dataset are calculated along with contribution scores of each feature to the components.

Model performance is evaluated using balanced error rates and weighted votes. Centroids distance is used to calculate distance between clusters of correlated features and ROC and AUC curves are used to assess the strength of the classifier.
