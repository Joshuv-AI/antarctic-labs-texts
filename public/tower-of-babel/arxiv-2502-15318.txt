Constructing a Tutte polynomial for graphs embedded in surfaces
Iain Moffatt

Abstract
There are several different extensions of the Tutte polynomial to graphs embedded in surfaces. To help frame the different options, here we consider the problem of extending the Tutte polynomial to cellularly embedded graphs starting from first principles. We offer three different routes to defining such a polynomial and show that they all lead to the same polynomial. This resulting polynomial is known in the literature under a few different names including the ribbon graph polynomial, and 2-variable Bollobás–Riordan polynomial. Our overall aim here is to use this discussion as a mechanism for providing a gentle introduction to the topic of Tutte polynomials for graphs embedded in surfaces.

Introduction

If you find yourself in need of a version of the Tutte polynomial for
graphs embedded in surfaces
, rather than for graphs in the abstract, then you will be met with a variety of options in the literature, see

;

;

The best known polynomial among these is the
Bollobás–Riordan polynomial

BR01
;
BR02
which is a 4-variable polynomial of embedded graphs. However, most of the results about these topological Tutte polynomials concern a 2-variable specialisation of this polynomial, which is known variously as the

2-variable Bollobás–Riordan polynomial

Tutte polynomial of a cellularly embedded graph

;
Goodall_2020
;
maya
.)
While as a community we agree what the Tutte polynomial of a graph is, there is less clarity around what the Tutte polynomial of an embedded graph should be.

In this paper we consider the problem of constructing a Tutte polynomial for embedded graphs. We approach this problem by
going back to first principles and considering how to construct a polynomial for graphs in surfaces that, just as the classical Tutte polynomial, has a recursive deletion-contraction definition with a base case given by edgeless graphs.
We detail three approaches for doing this: an approach through the dichromatic polynomial, a state-sum approach, and an approach based upon the deletion-contraction cases of the classical Tutte polynomial. All three approaches result in the same polynomial.

Our discussion here builds upon the work on topological Tutte polynomials in
HUGGETT_2019
, which in turn built upon work on Tutte polynomials for Hopf algebras in
Krajewski_2018

3.4
and
3.5

3.2
and
3.3
is new to this paper. We also give a quasi-tree expansion for our polynomial in Section
3.6
and give an overview of its properties in Section
3.7
.

Background on embedded graphs

This section provides an overview of cellularly embedded graphs, ribbon graphs, and some of their standard constructions and terminology. We refer the reader to
zbMATH05202336
for additional background on graph theory,
Ellis_Monaghan_2013
for background on embedded graphs and ribbon graphs as we use them here,
zbMATH04006288
for standard background on embedded graphs, and
HUGGETT_2019
for further discussion of deletion and contraction for embedded graphs.

We assume a familiarity with surfaces and the classification of surfaces, but remind the reader of a few relevant facts here. Additional background can be found in many standard topology or topological graph theory texts, for example
(
zbMATH03716424
, Chapter 1)
.
We use the term
surface
here to mean a compact Hausdorff topological space in which every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open 2-dimensional disc. A
surface with boundary
here means a compact Hausdorff topological space in which every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to either a 2-dimensional disc or the upper half-plane. Its
boundary
is the set of all points
p
that have a neighbourhood homeomorphic to the half-plane with the homeomorphism mapping
p
to the origin.
Note that here surfaces and surfaces with boundary need not be connected.
A surface or surface with boundary is
nonorientable
if it contains a subset homeomorphic to a Möbius band, and is
orientable
otherwise.
A connected orientable surface is homeomorphic to a sphere or the connected sum of tori, with its
genus
being the number of tori. A connected nonorientable surface is homeomorphic to the connected sum of projective planes, with its
genus
being the number of projective planes.
Up to homeomorphism a connected surface is uniquely determined by its genus and orientability.
A connected surface with boundary
\Sigma
consists of a connected surface
\Sigma^{\prime}
with the interiors of
k
discs removed, with
k
giving its number of boundary components. The genus of
\Sigma
is defined as the genus of
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
Up to homeomorphism a connected surface with boundary is uniquely determined by its genus, orientability, and number of boundary components.
The genus of a non-connected surface with or without boundary is the sum of genus of each of its connected components.

2.1
Graphs cellularly embedded in surfaces

We say
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
is a
graph embedded in a surface

\Sigma
(or an
embedded graph
for brevity) if it consists of a set
V
of points on
\Sigma
, called
vertices
, and another set
E
of simple paths in
\Sigma
, called
edges
. The ends of the edges must lie on vertices, only the ends of edges may meet vertices, and two edges may only intersect where they share a vertex. Note that we allow the possibility that both ends of an edge meet the same vertex. We do not require
\Sigma
to be connected, but we do insist that there is at least one vertex in each connected component of the surface. Note that the embedded graph
\mathbb{G}
has an
underlying graph

G
with vertex set
V
, edge set
E
and incidences determined by the paths in the obvious way. Abusing notation slightly, we say that
\mathbb{G}
is an
embedding
of its underlying graph
G
.
Two embedded graphs are
equivalent
if there is a homeomorphism between the surfaces they are embedded in that induces an isomorphism of the underlying graphs. If the surfaces are orientable, the homeomorphism must be orientation preserving. We consider embedded graphs up to equivalence.

Deleting the subset of
\Sigma
formed by the vertices and edges of
\mathbb{G}
results in a collection of surfaces with boundary called the
regions
of
\mathbb{G}
. (Visualise using a pair of scissors to cut the surface up by snipping along the graph. The result of this gives the regions.) If each region is homeomorphic to a disc then the regions are called
faces
and
\mathbb{G}
is said to be
cellularly embedded
. For example, Figure
1(a)
shows a graph that is cellularly embedded in a torus, while Figure
1(b)
shows a graph that is not cellularly embedded in a torus (one region is a disc but the other is an annulus). Here we are principally interested in cellularly embedded graphs.

Defining deletion and contraction of edges
of a cellularly embedded graph requires a little care as we need to ensure that these operations result in a graph that is also cellularly embedded.
Let
\mathbb{G}
be a graph cellularly embedded in a surface

\mathbb{G}\ba e

2.2
Ribbon graphs

When working with polynomials of graphs embedded in surfaces it is common to describe embedded graphs as ribbon graphs.
A
ribbon graph

\mathbb{G}=\left(V,E\right)
is a surface with boundary, represented as the union of two sets of discs: a set
V
of
vertices
and a set of
edges

E
with the following properties.

1.

The vertices and edges intersect in disjoint line segments.

2.

Each such line segment lies on the boundary of precisely one vertex and precisely one edge. In particular, no two vertices intersect, and no two edges intersect.

3.

Every edge contains exactly two such line segments.

See Figures
7(a)
and
8
for some pictures of ribbon graphs.

Two ribbon graphs are
equivalent
if there is a homeomorphism (which should be orientation-preserving if the ribbon graphs are orientable) that sends vertices to vertices, and edges to edges. We consider ribbon graphs up to equivalence.
In particular equivalence preserves the vertex-edge structure, adjacency, and cyclic ordering of the edges incident to each vertex.
It is worth emphasising that ribbon graph equivalence demands more than that the underlying surfaces with boundary are homeomorphic.

It is well-known that ribbon graphs are just descriptions of graphs cellularly embedded
in surfaces (see for example
Ellis_Monaghan_2013
, or
zbMATH04006288
where ribbon graphs are called reduced band decompositions). If
\mathbb{G}
is a cellularly embedded graph, then a ribbon
graph representation results from taking a small neighbourhood of
the cellularly embedded graph
\mathbb{G}
, and deleting its complement. On the other hand, if
\mathbb{G}
is a
ribbon graph, then, topologically, it is a surface with boundary. Capping off the holes, that is, ‘filling in’ each hole by identifying its boundary component with the boundary of a disc, results in a ribbon graph embedded in a surface from which a graph embedded in the surface is readily obtained. Figure
7
shows a cellularly embedded graph and its corresponding ribbon graph.
Two ribbon graphs are equivalent if and only if they describe equivalent cellularly embedded graphs.

For an embedded graph or ribbon graph

•

We use
V(\mathbb{G})
to denote the vertex set of

•

•

•

3
A Tutte polynomial for graphs embedded in surfaces

In this section we consider the problem of defining a Tutte polynomial for embedded graphs. We detail three approaches for defining it and show that all three approaches result in the same polynomial. We then give a summary of properties of this polynomial.

3.1
A reminder of the classical Tutte polynomial of a graph

Our staring point is the classical Tutte polynomial of a graph. We refer the reader to
zbMATH07553843
for additional background on the Tutte polynomial. The
Tutte polynomial
,
T(G;x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
, of a graph
G=(V,E)

is the polynomial uniquely defined by the following recursive
deletion-contraction relations
:

T(G;x,y):=\begin{cases}x\,T(G/e;x,y)&\text{if $e$ is a bridge,}\\
y\,T(G\backslash e;x,y)&\text{if $e$ is a loop,}\\
T(G\backslash e;x,y)+T(G/e;x,y)&\text{if $e$ is neither a bridge or loop,}\\
1&\text{if $G$ is edgeless}.\end{cases}

(2)

Thus
T(G;x,y)
is computed by applying the first three relations in (
2
) to express
T(G;x,y)
as a
\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
-linear combination of Tutte polynomials of edgeless graphs, each of which evaluates to 1 by the fourth relation in (
2
). It is not immediate that
T(G;x,y)
, as we have defined it, is independent of the choice of the order of the edges in which we apply the relations in (
2
). The standard way of proving that it is indeed independent of this choice is to show that

T(G;x,y)=\sum_{A\subseteq E}(x-1)^{r(E)-r(A)}(y-1)^{|A|-r(A)},

(3)

where
r(A)
denotes the rank of the spanning subgraph
(V,A)
of
G=(V,E)
. The right-hand side of (
3
) is clearly well-defined and hence so is
T(G;x,y)
(details can be found in
zbMATH01179517
).

3.2
Defining a polynomial through deletion-contraction relations

We are interested in finding an analogue of Equation (
2
), and hence a version of the Tutte polynomial, for graphs embedded in surfaces.
We start by trying to make the most obvious changes to Equation (
2
). First we replace graphs with embedded graphs (so we replace each
G
in the equation with
\mathbb{G}
), then our deletion and contraction operations “
\ba e
” and “
/e
” become the embedded graph versions, rather than the graph versions. In the graphs case, Equation (
2
) is split into cases according to whether
e
is a bridge, loop or neither. It does not make sense to consider these three cases in our topological version.
1
1

1

It is not hard to see that using the cases in Equation (
2
) for the topological polynomial results in an embedded graph polynomial that equals the Tutte polynomial of the underlying graph.

So far we have that our deletion-contraction relations for embedded graphs should be split into cases, but it is not at all obvious what these cases should be.
Our approach is to avoid the cases in Equation (
2
) altogether, starting by rewriting the Tutte polynomial of a graph so that it treats every edge in exactly the same way.

The
dichromatic polynomial

Z(G;u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}[u,v]
of a graph
G=(V,E)
can be defined as

Z(G;u,v):=\sum_{A\subseteq E}u^{|A|}v^{k(A)}.

By expanding exponents in Equation (
3
), it can be shown that the dichromatic polynomial and Tutte polynomial are equivalent, with

T(G;x+1,y+1)=x^{-k(G)}y^{-v(G)}Z(G;y,xy).

(4)

The significance of
Z(G;u,v)
for us here is that its deletion-contraction relations treat all edges in the same way:

Z(G;u,v)=\begin{cases}Z(G\ba e;u,v)+u\,Z(G/e;u,v)&\text{if $e$ is an edge,}\\
v^{v(G)}&\text{if }E(G)=\emptyset.\end{cases}

(5)

Thus we may may proceed in the obvious way by defining an analogue of the dichromatic polynomial for graphs embedded in surfaces by replacing the graphs in Equation (
5
) with embedded graphs and setting

Z(\mathbb{G};u,v):=\begin{cases}Z(\mathbb{G}\ba e;u,v)+uZ(\mathbb{G}/e;u,v)&\text{if $e$ is an edge,}\\
v^{v(\mathbb{G})}&\text{if }E(G)=\emptyset.\end{cases}

(6)

It is worth emphasising that Equation (
5
) uses graph deletion and contraction whereas Equation (
6
) uses embedded graph deletion and contraction. Although easily overlooked due to the similarity in the notation, this difference is crucial here.

Example 1

Figure

shows two calculations using Equation (

). Figure

shows
Z(\mathbb{G};u,v)
where

consists of one vertex and two edges embedded in the torus computed in terms of embedded graphs. This gives

3.3
Approach 1: Deducing a Tutte polynomial from
Z(\mathbb{G})

One difficulty of this approach is that we have defined an analogue of the dichromatic polynomial rather than the Tutte polynomial. So what should the Tutte polynomial be?
We know that the Tutte polynomial is equivalent to the dichromatic polynomial as in Equation (
4
). Thus, by analogy, we should expect our Tutte polynomial
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
of embedded graphs to be obtained from
Z(\mathbb{G};u,v)
by a change of variables. But which change of variables exactly?

To decide this we need to consider what properties
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
should have. Here we impose the reasonable condition that when
\mathbb{G}
is an embedding of a graph
G
in the plane or sphere (or more generally when
g(\mathbb{G})=0)
) we have

T(\mathbb{G};x,y)=T(G;x,y),

(9)

i.e., the Tutte polynomial of a graph and of an embedded graph coincide for graphs embedded in the plane.
With this we have

\displaystyle T(G;x+1,y+1)

\displaystyle=x^{\,-k(G)}y^{\,-v(G)}Z(G;y,xy)\quad\text{(by~\eqref{eq:tzgraphs})}

\displaystyle=x^{\,-k(G)}y^{\,-v(G)}\sqrt{xy}^{\,v(\mathbb{G})}\;Z(\mathbb{G};y/\sqrt{xy},\sqrt{xy})\quad\text{(by~\eqref{eq:zzplane})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{x}^{\,e(\mathbb{G})-b(\mathbb{G})}\sqrt{y}^{\,-v(\mathbb{G})}\;Z(\mathbb{G};\sqrt{y/x},\sqrt{xy})\quad\text{(by~Euler's formula).}

It is then natural to propose the following definition.

Definition 1
(Tutte polynomial — first definition)

The
Tutte polynomial
,
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
, of an embedded graph
\mathbb{G}
is defined as

T(\mathbb{G};x+1,y+1):=\sqrt{x}^{\,e(\mathbb{G})-b(\mathbb{G})}\sqrt{y}^{\,-v(\mathbb{G})}\;Z(\mathbb{G};\sqrt{y/x},\sqrt{xy}).

(10)

Example 3

;
and when

Notice that the first example is a polynomial in

and

, but the second is a polynomial in

and
\sqrt{y-1}
. We shall see later that this is related to orientability.

Thus in this definition of
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
we have been naturally led to a graph polynomial that:

1.

coincides with the classical Tutte polynomial on plane graphs; and

2.

can be defined by deletion-contraction relations in which edgeless graphs give the terminal forms, just as with the classical Tutte polynomial of a graph. (The arising deletion-contraction appears later in Definition
3
.)

Although we reached this definition of
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
by taking the obvious direction at each stage, the precise form on the right-hand side of Equation (

3.4
Approach 2: A state-sum approach to the Tutte polynomial of an embedded graph

In this section we discuss an approach based upon the state-sum expression for the Tutte polynomial of a graph where we modify the rank function so that it records some topological information about an embedded graph.

Recall the state-sum definition of the classical Tutte polynomial of a graph from
Equation (
3
):

T(G;x,y)=\sum_{A\subseteq E}(x-1)^{r(E)-r(A)}(y-1)^{|A|-r(A)},

(11)

where the rank
r(A)
equals
v(A)-k(A)
.

The rank
r(\mathbb{G})=v(\mathbb{G})-k(\mathbb{G})
records no topological information about an embedded graph
\mathbb{G}
(in the sense that different embeddings of the same graph will always have the same rank). Hence (
3
) does not directly result in a satisfactory topological graph polynomial. Let us modify it so that it does.

We start by setting

\rho(\mathbb{G}):=r(\mathbb{G})+\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma(\mathbb{G}).

Following our standard notational conventions, for a subset of edges
A
, we set
\rho(A):=\rho(\mathbb{G}\ba(E-A))
and so

\rho(A)=r(A)+\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma(A).

We can make use of Euler’s formula to write

\rho(A)=\tfrac{1}{2}(|A|+v(A)-b(A)).

With
\rho(A)
we define a Tutte polynomial for embedded graphs by replacing
r(A)
in (
11
) with
\rho(A)
.

Definition 2
(Tutte polynomial — second definition)

The
Tutte polynomial
,
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
, of an embedded graph
\mathbb{G}
with edge set
E
is defined as

T(\mathbb{G};x,y):=\sum_{A\subseteq E}(x-1)^{\rho(E)-\rho(A)}(y-1)^{|A|-\rho(A)}.

(12)

Note that if
\mathbb{G}
is an embedding of a graph
G
in a disjoint union of spheres,
\gamma(A)
is always 0. In this case it follows that
\rho(A)=r(A)
and therefore
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
coincides with the classical Tutte polynomial for plane graphs.

3.5
Approach 3: deletion-contraction, duality and the Tutte polynomial of an embedded graph

In this section we approach a definition of the Tutte polynomial for embedded graphs by adapting the deletion-contraction relations for the classical Tutte polynomial of a graph shown in (
2
). The approach given here is extracted from the results of
HUGGETT_2019
, although that reference comes to the polynomial through a slightly different route and so, along with
Krajewski_2018
, gives a fourth approach to defining our Tutte polynomial.

We need some additional background on geometric duals.
The construction of the
geometric dual

\mathbb{G}^{*}
of a graph
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
embedded in a surface
\Sigma
is well known:
V(\mathbb{G}^{*})
is obtained by placing one vertex in each face of
\mathbb{G}
, and
E(\mathbb{G}^{*})
is obtained by embedding an edge of
\mathbb{G}^{*}
between two vertices whenever the faces of
\mathbb{G}
in which they lie are adjacent. For example, the geometric dual of a 3-cycle embedded in the sphere is a theta-graph embedded in the sphere.

Geometric duality has a particularly neat description in the language of ribbon graphs. Let
\mathbb{G}=(V(\mathbb{G}),E(\mathbb{G}))
be a ribbon graph. Recalling that a ribbon graph is a surface with boundary, we cap off the holes using a set of discs (i.e., take a disc for each boundary component, and identify the boundary component with the boundary of the disc), denoted by
V(\mathbb{G}^{*})
, to obtain a surface without boundary. The
geometric dual
of
\mathbb{G}
is the ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}^{*}=(V(\mathbb{G}^{*}),E(\mathbb{G}))
. The edges of
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{*}
correspond.
We shall use
e^{*}
to denote the edge of
\mathbb{G}^{*}
that corresponds to an edge
e
of
\mathbb{G}
. (Additional examples and discussion can be found in, for example,
Ellis_Monaghan_2013
.)

We initially defined the Tutte polynomial
T(G;x,y)
of a (non-embedded) graph
G
through deletion-contraction relations in Equation (
2
). The difficulty in defining a Tutte polynomial for embedded graphs directly from these deletion-contraction relations is that it is not at all clear what the cases in the relations should be. However, rewriting Equation (
2
) offers a route to determining the cases.

Let
G
be a graph embedded in the sphere (better still, for readers familiar with matroids, let
G
be a matroid).
Writing
T(G)
for
T(G;x,y)
, Equation (
2
) may be written as

T(G)=\begin{cases}p(e)\cdot T(G\ba e)+q(e)\cdot T(G/e)&\text{for an edge $e$,}\\
1&\text{if $G$ is edgeless.}\end{cases}

(13)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}p(e)=\begin{cases}x-1&\text{if $e$ is a loop in $G^{*}$,}\\
1&\text{if $e$ is not a loop in $G^{*}$; and}\end{cases}\\
\\
q(e)=\begin{cases}y-1&\text{if $e$ is a loop in $G$,}\\
1&\text{if $e$ is not a loop in $G$.}\end{cases}\end{array}

(14)

To see why this is the same as Equation (
2
) note that
e
is a loop in a graph embedded in the sphere if and only if
e^{*}
is a bridge in the dual. (This is not true for graphs embedded in higher genus surfaces.)

To deduce a Tutte polynomial
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
for embedded graphs from this we can immediately write down an analogue of Equation (
13
):

T(\mathbb{G})=\begin{cases}f(e)\cdot T(\mathbb{G}\ba e)+g(e)\cdot T(\mathbb{G}/e),&\text{for an edge $e$,}\\
1&\text{if $\mathbb{G}$ is edgeless.}\end{cases}

(15)

where we write
T(\mathbb{G})
for
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
, and where suitable
f(e)
and
g(e)
are yet to be found.
It remains to find the analogue of (
14
).

The key observation here is that there are two different types of loop in an embedded graph. As with graphs an edge of an embedded graph or ribbon graph is a
loop
if it is incident to exactly one vertex. A loop of a graph embedded in a surface is said to be
orientable
if it has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an annulus, and it is said to be
nonorientable
if it has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an Möbius band. Correspondingly, a loop in a ribbon graph is said to be
orientable
if together with its incident vertex it forms an annulus, and it is said to be
nonorientable
if it forms a Möbius band. See Table
1
.

Thus, for some suitable terms
a,b,c,a^{*},b^{*},c^{*}
, we set

\begin{array}[]{l}f(e)=\begin{cases}a^{*}&\text{if $e^{*}$ is an orientable loop in $\mathbb{G}^{*}$,}\\
b^{*}&\text{if $e^{*}$ is a nonorientable loop in $\mathbb{G}^{*}$,}\\
c^{*}&\text{if $e^{*}$ is a not a loop in $\mathbb{G}^{*}$;}\end{cases}\\
\text{and}\\
g(e)=\begin{cases}a&\text{if $e$ is an orientable loop in $\mathbb{G}$,}\\
b&\text{if $e$ is a nonorientable loop in $\mathbb{G}$,}\\
c&\text{if $e$ is not a loop in $\mathbb{G}$.}\end{cases}\end{array}

(16)

Let us consider what
a,b,c,a^{*},b^{*},c^{*}
can be. First we would like to
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
to coincide with
T(G;x,y)
whenever
\mathbb{G}
is plane. Thus we must have

a^{*}=x-1,\quad c^{*}=1,\quad a=y-1,\quad\text{and}\quad c=1.

For
b^{*}
and
b
we recall that we need the value of
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
to be independent of the order upon which (
15
) is applied to the edges of an embedded graph.
Figure
10(a)
shows a ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}
and Figure

\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{*}

e

\mathbb{G}

e^{*}

\mathbb{G}^{*}

Theorem 3.1
(Universality)

Let
\mathcal{G}

U:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}[w,\sqrt{a^{*}},\sqrt{c^{*}},\sqrt{a},\sqrt{c}]

\begin{array}[]{l}f(e)=\begin{cases}a^{*}&\text{if $e^{*}$ is an orientable loop in $\mathbb{G}^{*}$,}\\

c^{*}&\text{if $e^{*}$ is a not a loop in $\mathbb{G}^{*}$;}\end{cases}\\
\text{and}\\
g(e)=\begin{cases}a&\text{if $e$ is an orientable loop in $\mathbb{G}$,}\\
\sqrt{a}\sqrt{c}&\text{if $e$ is a nonorientable loop in $\mathbb{G}$,}\\
c&\text{if $e$ is not a loop in $\mathbb{G}$,}\end{cases}\end{array}

Moreover,

U(\mathbb{G})=w^{v(\mathbb{G})-\rho(\mathbb{G})}c^{\rho(\mathbb{G})}(c^{*})^{e(\mathbb{G})-\rho(\mathbb{G})}T\left(\mathbb{G};\frac{wa^{*}+c}{c},\frac{wa+c^{*}}{c^{*}}\right).

discovered independently in

Theorem 3.2
(Duality)

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an embedded graph. Then

Proof

Define a function

from embedded graphs to polynomials in variables

\displaystyle T^{*}(\mathbb{G};x,y)=T(\mathbb{G}^{*};x,y)

\displaystyle=f(e^{*})T((\mathbb{G}/e)^{*};x,y)+g(e^{*})T((\mathbb{G}\ba e)^{*};x,y)

\displaystyle=g(e^{*})T^{*}(\mathbb{G}\ba e;x,y)+f(e^{*})T^{*}(\mathbb{G}/e;x,y),

where we have used that

and

(see, for example

By rewriting

satisfies the deletion-contraction relations for

(For example,

if

is an orientable loop in

It follows from the Universality Theorem that

We say that a ribbon graph

3.6
Quasi-trees

In this section we show that
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
can be written as a sum over spanning quasi-trees, a result that is analogous the fact that the classical Tutte polynomial of a connected graph can be written as a sum over spanning trees.
An activities expansion for the Bollobás–Riordan polynomial of orientable ribbon graphs was given in
zbMATH05960754
. This was quickly extended to non-orientable ribbon graphs in
Vignes_Tourneret_2011
and, independently, in an unpublished manuscript
dewey
.
An extension to the Krushkal polynomial followed in
Butler_2018
. Each of these expansions expresses the graph polynomial as a sum over quasi-trees, but they all include a Tutte polynomial of an associated graph as a summand. Most recently,
Morse_2019
gave a spanning tree expansion for the 2-variable Bollobás–Riordan polynomial of a delta-matroid which specialised to give one for the 2-variable Bollobás–Riordan polynomial of a ribbon graph. The approach we take here is a special case of
(
HUGGETT_2019
, Section 5)
which gives expressions for the Tutte polynomials of several types of topological graph.

Equations (
2
) and (
3
) gave the two most common definitions of the Tutte polynomial of a graph. However there is a third standard definition, often called the
spanning tree expansion
or
activities expansion
, that expresses the Tutte polynomial of a graph as a sum over spanning trees.
For completeness we include a description of the definition; however, a reader unfamiliar with it may safely skip the details in the next paragraph.

Let
G
be a connected graph and fix a linear order of its edges.
Suppose that
T
is a spanning tree of
G
and let
e
be an edge of
G
.
If
e\notin T
, then the graph
T\cup e
contains a unique cycle, and we say that
e
is
externally active
with respect to
T
if it is the smallest edge in this cycle.
If
e\in F
, then we say that
e
is
internally active
if
e
is the smallest edge of
G
that can be added to
T\ba e
to recover a spanning tree of
G
.
Then the
spanning tree expansion
of the Tutte polynomial is

T(G;x,y)=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}x^{IA(T)}y^{EA(T)},

(22)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the set of spanning trees of
G
, and where
\mathrm{IA}(T)
(respectively
\mathrm{EA}(T)
) denotes the number internally active (respectively, externally active) edges of
G
with respect to
T
and the linear ordering of
E
. (Additional details and examples can be found in, for example,
(
Ellis_Monaghan_2022_C
, Section 2.2.3)
).
We can obtain a similar expression for the Tutte polynomial of an embedded graph, but instead of summing over spanning trees we sum over spanning quasi-trees as follows.

For convenience, in the rest of subsection we shall work in the language of ribbon graphs and restrict our discussions to connected ribbon graphs.

We begin by rewriting Equation (
5
).
An edge in a ribbon graph is a
bridge
if deleting it increases the number of components of the ribbon graph.
A loop

in a ribbon graph is said to be
interlaced
with a cycle

if when travelling around the boundary of the vertex incident to

we see edges in the cyclic order
e\,c_{1}\,e\,c_{2}
where
c_{1}
and

are edges of

A loop is

if it is not interlaced with any cycle.
With this terminology we can use Equation (

Z(\mathbb{G};u,v)=\begin{cases}(1+uv)Z(\mathbb{G}\ba e;u,v)&\text{if $e$ is a trivial orientable loop,}\\
(u+v)\,Z(\mathbb{G}/e;u,v)&\text{if $e$ is a bridge,}\\
Z(\mathbb{G}\ba e;u,v)+u\,Z(\mathbb{G}/e;u,v)&\text{if $e$ is not a bridge}\\
&\qquad\text{nor a trivial orientable loop,}\\
v^{|V|}&\text{if }E(G)=\emptyset.\end{cases}

(23)

connected every ribbon graph arising in a computation of

Lemma 1

If
\mathbb{G}
is a connected ribbon graph then the set of spanning quasi-trees of
\mathbb{G}
is in one-one correspondence with the set of leaves of the resolution tree of
Z(\mathbb{G};u,v)
computed from Equation (
23
). Furthermore, the correspondence is given by deleting the set of edges of
\mathbb{G}
that are deleted in the branch that terminates in the node.

Proof
(Sketch)

If you start with a connected ribbon graph the only way you can create an additional component is by deleting a bridge or contracting a trivial orientable loop. Thus the branches in a resolution tree correspond to every possible way to delete and contract edges without creating additional components. Next, since the order of deletion and contraction of edges does not matter, the ribbon graph at leaf is a vertex obtained as
\mathbb{G}\ba D/C
, for some sets of edges
C
and
D=E-C
. Since contraction does not change the number of boundary components, it follows that
\mathbb{G}\ba D
has exactly one boundary component and is hence a spanning quasi-tree of
\mathbb{G}
. The 1-1 correspondence follows. (A more detailed proof can be found just after
(
HUGGETT_2019
, Lemma 46)
.)∎

In light of Lemma
1
we see that if we can read off the product of the labels on a branch directly from its corresponding spanning quasi-tree then we can express

as a sum over spanning quasi-trees.
The edges of the given spanning quasi-tree correspond to those edges contracted along the branch, and edges outside the quasi-tree are deleted. Thus to establish how much the branch contributes, we just need to know how many edges we see as a bridge and how many edges we see as a trivial orientable loop when we delete and contract edges as specified by the branch.

Recall that the edges of

3.7
Further properties

We conclude by listing some key properties of the Tutte polynomial of embedded graphs.

1.

T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
is determined by the delta-matroid of
\mathbb{G}
. See
CMNR-JCT
. This is analogous to the well-known fact that the classical Tutte polynomial of a graph is determined by the cycle matroid of the graph.

2.

If
\mathbb{G}
is a plane embedding of
G
, then
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)=T(G;x,y)
. See
BR01
;
BR02
.

3.

If
\mathbb{G}
is an embedded graph and
H
a graph such that
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)=T(H;x,y)
, then
\mathbb{G}
is plane. See
(
Ellis_Monaghan_2022_B
, Corollary 3.11)
.

4.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an orientable embedded graph. Then
T(\mathbb{G};x,y)
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
(or
\mathbb{C}[x,y]
) if and only if
\mathbb{G}
is non-separable. See
(
Ellis_Monaghan_2022_B
, Theorem 1.1)
.

5.

T(\mathbb{G}\sqcup\mathbb{H};x,y)=T(\mathbb{G};x,y)\cdot T(\mathbb{H};x,y)=T(\mathbb{G}\vee\mathbb{H};x,y)
. (See
BR01
;
BR02
.)

6.

T(\mathbb{G}^{*};x,y)=T(\mathbb{G};y,x)
. See
Ellis_Monaghan_2011
;
Moffatt_2008
. This expression was extended to a partial duality formula for a multivariate version of the dichromatic polynomial in
zbMATH05569114
;
Moffatt_2010_PDBR
;
Vignes_Tourneret_2009
.

7.

An analogue of the classical result that two graphs related by Whitney flips have the same Tutte polynomial holds (see, for example,
(
zbMATH07680519
, Theorem 6.14)
for details on this classical result).
If two embedded graphs are related by vertex joins, vertex cuts, or mutation, as defined in
zbMATH07336895
, then their Tutte polynomials are identical. See
(
zbMATH07336895
, Corollary 3)
for details.

8.

A expression for the Tutte polynomial of the
k

k
-summands was given in
(
HMsplit
, Theorem 1)
. This is an analogue of Negami’s splitting formula for the classical Tutte polynomial of a graph

.

9.

An expression for
