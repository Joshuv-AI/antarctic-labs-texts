Impact of magnetic water treatment on the properties of high-performance concrete
Authors: Malathy Ramalingam, Karuppasamy Narayanan, G K Arunvivek, Pramod Kumar, Anasuya Sahu, Bamidele Charles Olaiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh Performance building materials are continuously developed by civil engineering society. The process of hydration and workability mainly rests on the water quantity existing in concrete. Currently, a vast amount of potable water has been exploited by construction for numerous purposes. Using magnetized water (MW) in concrete has numerous advantages if it is used as mixing water. This study explores the effects of ready-mix concrete (RMC) using MW with the addition of superplasticizer (SP) and storage time on the workability and hardened properties using a permanent magnet with 0.9 Tesla field intensity. Seven distinct MW mixes were prepared with different substitutions of SP dosage (0.15%, 0.25%, 0.35%, and 0.45%) and storage times (1 h, 2 h, and 3 h) for M30 mix with W/C of 0.38. The performance of concrete was assessed through mechanical properties. The characterization of MW concrete in RMC is assessed through morphology and spectral analysis. The findings indicate that MW as a hydration boosting agent significantly enhances the fresh and hardened concrete properties. Results show a 14.2% enhancement in workability and a 10.85% improvement in Compressive load compared to normal water (NW) with 0.45% SP. MW after 60 min of storage time shows better performance than Normal Water Concrete. Hydrated products of concrete mix with MW were confirmed through X-ray diffraction and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy. The microstructure analysis shows that MW forms a smaller Calcium Hydrate crystal and complete hydration products after 28 days.Keywords: Magnetic water, Read mix concrete, Super plastizer, Storage time, XRD, SEMSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Water is one of the prime constituents of the concrete mix. It controls the microstructure, strength, and workability of concrete. The civil engineering construction sector uses large quantities of potable freshwater for its various activities. To produce one cubic meter of concrete mix, nearly 150–200 L of water are used1. The quality and amount of water in the concrete mix determine how the concrete will hydrate and how workable it will be. The civil engineering industry is one of the major freshwater-consuming industries for infrastructure development2. New technology has been implemented to prepare a concrete mixture using magnetic field-treated water (MFTW). Locally offered water can be magnetized for making concrete3,4. In 1962, researchers started investigating the use of MW in concrete mix intended for military infrastructure, such as jetties and airports5. When NW is passed to a magnetic field, it turns into MW. Due to magnetization, clusters in water are disintegrated to form small clusters; the molecular framework of water molecules is refined as a result the characteristic properties of water are improved6–8. Figure 1 presents the structure of water molecules before and after magnetization9.

Structure of water molecules before and after magnetization.

Concrete production is one of the most water- and energy-intensive processes in the construction industry. When water is introduced into the concrete mixture, hydration occurs, and the hydration products tend to settle on the surface of cement particles, forming barriers that hinder further hydration10. However, using magnetized water (MW) during mixing enhances the penetration of water into cement particles and promotes more complete hydration. This enhanced hydration improves the mechanical and durability characteristics of concrete11. The utilization of MW is an effective strategy to achieve a higher degree of cement hydration, reduce porosity, and boost overall concrete strength. The magnetization process lowers the surface tension of water and modifies its physicochemical behavior, leading to more resilient molecular bonding with cementitious materials12,13. Consequently, MW-treated mixes exhibit improved microstructural integrity and enhanced interfacial transition zones (ITZ)14. Experimental findings indicate that MW treatment at magnetic field intensities between 6000 and 9000 Gauss can yield notable enhancements in concrete performance. For instance, several studies reported approximately a 35% increase in slump and a 20% improvement in compressive strength, allowing cement content reduction by up to 7.5% without compromising strength or durability15. Furthermore, magnetostatic induction at intensities of 19.07–25.37 Gauss has been shown to accelerate hydration, shorten setting times, and enhance the mechanical properties of cement pastes by forming denser, low-porosity microstructures16. Mixes prepared with MW exhibited up to 10% strength gain and a 5% reduction in cement usage17. Wang et al.18 observed that MW enabled a more complete hydration process and reduced bleeding and segregation, while the use of mixed poles during magnetization resulted in a 50% strength increase compared to normal water concrete (NWC)18.

Similarly, when cement was partially replaced with copper slag up to 30%, compressive, flexural, and tensile strengths improved by 4–18%, 6–17%, and 5–10%, respectively19. Salehi and Mazloom20 found that MW in self-compacting lightweight concrete (SCLC) improved total fracture energy by 18% and initial fracture energy by 21%, as MW clusters strengthened the ITZ and cement paste20. Wei et al.21 further reported that MW enhanced the resistance of concrete to early-age shrinkage, demonstrating superior dimensional stability compared to mixes prepared with normal water21.

These findings align closely with the body of sustainable materials research advanced by Olaiya et al.22–24 which explores alternative, eco-efficient approaches in concrete production. Similarly, Iro et al.25 optimized bagasse ash concrete using response surface methodology to enhance mechanical performance, while Olaiya et al.26 addressed sustainability practices for clinical laboratories, emphasizing energy and resource optimization. Together, these studies reinforce the premise that integrating MW into sustainable mix design frameworks can achieve both performance optimization and carbon reduction in construction materials. Furthermore, previous work by Olaiya, Lawan, and Olonade (2023)27 on the utilization of sawdust composites in construction supports the ongoing transition toward sustainable and circular material use. Incorporating MW technology within such systems offers a complementary mechanism to improve hydration efficiency, minimize cement dependency, and promote environmentally responsible concrete production. Finally, while conventional high-strength concrete relies heavily on chemical admixtures such as superplasticizers (e.g., Aura Mix 200-inbng 20) to achieve workability and compaction28–31, the use of MW presents a green and energy-efficient alternative. By naturally enhancing hydration and bonding characteristics, MW can reduce the need for chemical additives while contributing to the global agenda of sustainable and low-carbon concrete innovation.

This paper focuses on studying the mechanical behaviour of MW as a water reducing agent (WRA) instead of admixtures in RMC plants. Here, a 0.9 T field intensity is used to magnetize the water through PERMAG (N407), and fresh concrete specimens were made by adjusting the dose of SP (0–0.45% by cement volume). Also, MW with different prolonged mixing times on the fresh state and hardened state were optimized. To assess the hydration of Calcium Silicate Hydrate crystal with NW-RMC mix and MW-RMC mix, this study also performed microstructural tests on concrete samples.

Characterisation of magnetic pumpable concrete

FT-IR spectrum

The quantity of hydroxide (OH) groups in cement particles is measured with an FT-IR spectrophotometer. FT-IR spectrometers from Singapore-based PerkinElmer with a resolution of 0.5 cm−1 were used to analyse the FT-IR data in this study for the sample-prepared NW-RMC and MW-RMC. The concrete sample powder was passed through the 75-micron screen and analysed in ATR mode using wavelengths between 8200 and 350 cm1 With less interpretation and more accuracy in the number of materials being inspected, this characterization technique has more advantages than one45.

XRD analysis

An XRD investigation was conducted on powder samples to measure phases of cement (or) clinker to forecast the concrete performance46. Differences of X-ray with MW-RMC and NW-RMC samples were analyzed with a 0.02-degree scan length, and a Cu-anode ceramic tube power XRD from Bruker was used. Using the method outlined, a sample magnetized with Gauss was inserted into a device for examination. The graph was produced with the intensity series on the y-axis and the 2θ range on the x-axis, where 2θ represents the angle between the transmitted and reflected beams of the concrete samples.

Microstructure study

The microstructure of the NW-RMC and MW-RMC after 28 days of curing was studied using SEM analysis. SEM images of samples were made by scanning the surface using focused electron beams. The NW-RMC and MW-RMC samples of M30 concrete are categorised by SEM. SEM images give a clear idea about the internal bonding characteristics of different ingredients in the concrete matrix. The bonding between the cement phase and aggregate phase in the transition zone plays a dynamic role in strength attainment47–49.

Results

Marsh cone test

The Marsh cone test was conducted to determine the optimum dosage of SP in the NW-RMC concrete mix. The purpose of this test was to find out the compatibility between cement and SP and to find out the most favorable dosage that would result in good flowability of the concrete mix. The time taken to collect 500 mL of cement paste through the Marsh cone nozzle in a beaker was noted. From the observation, it is noted that at 0.45% SP dosage, saturation of cement paste was realized in respect of NW, whereas in the case of MW, without the addition of SP, flowability on par with the flowability of 0.45% SP normal water cement paste was obtained. It is considered that without the addition of SP, MW can influence the absorbing ability of the liquid–gas interface and consequently increases the solubility and dispersion of the cement particles. Thus, MW-RMC concrete mix requires zero amount of SP dosage to spread the water molecules without any hindrance in the concrete mix and to enhance the strength of the concrete50,51.

Fresh concrete properties

The qualities of fresh concrete are evaluated based on compaction and workability. Concrete possessing good compaction increases the density of and reduces the void space in concrete. To determine the workability of fresh concrete, the slump cone test and compaction factor test were carried out. In the current research, the properties of fresh M30 grade concrete were optimised through the adoption of different dosages of SP (0 to 0.45%) in the concrete mix, distinct storage periods (1 h, 2 h, and 3 h) for MW. All the said tests were carried out following the guidelines presented in IS: 1199-195943. The NW after magnetization changes the physical and chemical water properties and its effect on concrete properties12.

Effect of MW on fresh properties

Slump cone tests are an easy and widely used method of evaluating the workability of concrete. A frustum-shaped mold (a cone with a truncated top) made of metal. The standard dimensions are: Bottom Internal Diameter: 200 mm (8 inches); Top Internal Diameter: 100 mm (4 inches); Height: 300 mm (12 inches). Dampen the slump cone and base plate (but ensure no free water remains). Place the base plate on a level, stable surface and position the slump cone on it, holding it firmly down with your feet on the footrests. Fill the cone in three equal layers. Each layer is rodded 25 times with the tamping rod. The strokes should be distributed uniformly across the cross-section and penetrate the underlying layer. Carefully and vertically lift the cone straight up within 5–10 s, without any twisting or lateral motion43. IS 456-2000 recommends that concrete have a 75–100 mm slump for medium workability, particularly for areas that are severely reinforced36. The outcomes indicate that the slump value of MW mixed concrete shows superior results than that of NW mix for different substitutions of SP dosage. Notably, an increase in slump value was obtained with an increase in SP dosage for both NW-RMC and MW-RMC concrete mixes. The slump value increased from 140 to 160 mm with the MW-RMC mix for a 0.45% SP dosage. This notable increase was claimed to be because of the easier propagation of MW molecules. As a result, water molecules were able to enter cement particles more easily, requiring less water overall, increasing the workability of concrete52. The NW with 0.15% SP (98 mm) can be achieved in MW without SP (90 mm) through a slump test. Good workability was realized for M30 grade MW-RMC mix without the addition of SP, i.e., 0% SP.

The compaction factor test is performed to determine the workability of a fresh concrete mix. It gives more accurate results than the slump cone test. The compaction factor test is more suitable for concrete mixes having low slump values. It is particularly carried out in respect of a concrete mix that needs compaction through vibration. In the current study, adopting the procedure contained in IS: 1199-1959, the compaction factor test was carried out on the NW-RMC concrete mix and the MW-RMC concrete mix. The compaction factor value for MW-RMC concrete mix indicated that the workability of MW-RMC concrete mix was better than that of NW-RMC concrete mix. Figure 3(b) shows the values of compaction factor with respect to concrete mixes, namely NW-RMC and MW-RMC, with different substitution of SP dosage. Higher compaction factor values were obtained for MW-RMC as compared to the workability value of NW-RMC. Applying MW increased the compaction factor up to 9.3%, without SP dosage.

Discussions

Influences of MW on admixture

Effect of MW on fresh properties

Water in its nano state, which is present in clusters and whose size is determined by the water molecules dominant force, is the cause of the increase in the slump in the absence of SP dose. Water’s molecular cluster breaks in response to magnetic fields because of its macroscopic properties, which lowers the binding angle between hydrogen atoms from 104.5° to 103 °C54. MW seeps through the middle portion of the cement composition during hydration, enveloping the concrete’s strength10. This is because the MW may initiate the hydration without segregating or bleeding concrete by using a smaller volume of water surrounding the cement particles. It is observed from the fresh state properties that MW mixing without SP dosage provides the desired workability for the RMC plant mix.

Effect of MW on hardened properties

The average 28-day strength increase rate of MW concrete without SP dosage was 10.85%. Hassan (2008) explained the improvement in compressive strength of the MWC specimen as follows: MW in the concrete mix shortens the length of setting time of cement paste, increases the solubility of cement paste, and prompts the formation of calcium silicate hydrate (CSH) gel (i.e., accelerates the hydration process to give rise to CSH gel). More molecules are available for the hydration process owing to the small MW clusters. When the hydration process is completed, the magnetic concrete gets denser than the non-magnetic concrete55. Additionally, MW serves as a super lubricant to create a uniform mixture and enable cement to fully hydrate. When MW is used in concrete, the strength and quality of the NW are enhanced. The utilisation of MW instead of NW helps to produce sustainable concrete with zero addition of chemical admixtures. According to previous studies, magnetic concrete’s mechanical strength increases with MW mixing 56. Figure 4(b) and (c) show the performance of MW-RMC without the substitution of SP for different curing spells. Adequate cement hydration is responsible for the strength improvement in MW-RMC; this process fills the pores in the concrete paste and increases the density of the concrete packing. It is observed that instantaneous MW does not require any SP. MW itself can serve as a water-reducing admixture (WRA) in the place of a SP and can be very well used in the making of concrete mixes 57.

Influences of MW on prolonged mixing

Effect of MW on fresh properties (pro-longed mixing)

It is observed from Fig. 5(a) that the slump cone test results using MW (kept in storage for more than 1 h), the effect of MW diminishes, and the workability mix decreases. Magnetization fortifies the electrical charges of water molecules. The slump value is increased due to the application of MW. MW improves the interaction of water with cement particles in the concrete mix. In general, MW tends to lose its characteristics once it leaves the magnetic field. Furthermore, if kept under storage for longer periods, MW becomes weak in its power. This means that with an increase in storage time, MW loses its power. This finding was also endorsed by Abdel-Magid (2017)58. Ramalingam et al.10 reported that MW kept in a reservoir for a longer period may lose its properties before its application in the concrete mix. Kumar et al.59 described that the property attained by water through magnetisation is a temporary feature and, in some instances, water after magnetization could not acquire distinct characteristics readily due to magnetization.

Effect of MW on hardened concrete properties (pro-longed mixing)

It transpires that MW enhanced the strength and workability of the MW-RMC mix, and without the addition of SP, even after a one-hour storage period after instant magnetization, good strength performance was realized for the MW-RMC concrete mix. Over extended durations of curing (ageing), a steady increase in concrete strength is achieved. MW mix accelerates the hydration process, which results in the creation of CSH gel, shortens the cement paste’s setting time, and boosts the paste’s solubility60. MW not only minimizes the size and quantity of capillary pores in the mix but also keeps the mix from discontinuous packing19. Gholizadeh and Arabshahi5 and Reddy et al.7 reported that MW produces more small clusters in water and reduces the formation of CaCO3 scales. More cement hydration fills the pores within the concrete, increasing the density of the concrete packing and contributing to the rise in MW-RMC strength61. It has been found that MW functions as a super lubricant during prolonged resting and affects the electrical load of the cement particles, which results in the separation of charges from one another. As a result, the MW more readily enters the interior of the cement particles and extends the lubricating effect (up to one hour). The hardened concrete test results show a reduction in strength with an increase in storage duration after one hour for all curing days, which implies that MW properties are lost after one hour of storage time. Song and Zhao 62 reported that MW, when kept in storage for a longer period, loses its power and characteristic features acquired through magnetization and behaves almost like normal water (non-MW).

XRD analysis in Fig. 8(b), the major peaks of quartz, C3S, C2S, and CSH were observed with NW-RMC after 28 days. Figure 8(a) illustrates the XRD plots of samples obtained from MW-RMC in 28 days. The XRD analysis projects the quartz preponderance peaks during sample appraisal. The magnetic action causes the clusters of water inside the molecules to disintegrate, causing the water molecules to enter the cement particles more quickly, starting hydration and increasing the strength of concrete compared to NW-RMC. Non-observation’ of CSH and CH is due to their poorly crystalline, nano-crystalline, or amorphous nature, which leads to broad, low-intensity scattering that is often obscured by the background or the sharper peaks of crystalline aggregates (like quartz in the sand)6,64–68.

SEM analysis

Figure 9(a) and (b) displays the SEM-taken microscopic images of the hardened concrete specimens that were made using NW and MW. Looking into the morphology of CSH gel in MW-RMC, it is inferred that the CSH becomes denser and less porous due to the influence of MW. CSH gel’s morphological pattern shows the cement’s hydration product and is responsible for the development of the concrete’s mechanical strength47. Since cement reacts more readily with smaller molecules of magnetic water, CSH forms more quickly and completely17,48,69–73. Figure 9b illustrates a larger amount of CH crystal using MW in a smaller and unique pattern. This may be responsible for MWC’s strength parameter46,74,75. Referring to Fig. 9(a) and (b), it is gathered that partially hydrated CH crystals and more pores are present in NW-RMC, whereas completely hydrated CH crystals and a smaller number of pores are present in MW-RMC, respectively.

The present research on the utilization magnetized water concrete on RMC plant can be reproducibility and consistency of magnetization effects over time, especially in large scale applications in terms of i) Structural Efficiency- Allows for the potential use of less cement to achieve the same target strength. ii) Enables the production of higher-grade concrete more reliably, which is crucial for high-rise buildings, long-span bridges, and other critical infrastructure. iii) Promote a greener concrete industry by reducing reliance on chemical admixtures. iv) Lead to more durable and resilient infrastructure with a longer service life.

Practical considerations and feasibility for ready-mix application

From the laboratory test results, magnetized water was found to be a substitute for super plasticizers in enhancing workability and strength parameters, but some practical challenges were addressed for adoption in ready-mix plants. Firstly, scaling the magnetization process requires industrial-scale equipment capable of treating large volumes of water (several cubic meters per hour) with consistent magnetic flux density. The optimal placement of such a unit, whether on the main water line or on a dedicated loop, presents an engineering challenge. Secondly, the variability in source water quality (e.g., dissolved ion content) between different plant locations could influence the effectiveness of magnetization, necessitating site-specific calibration. In future studies, techno-economic analysis must be carried out. In addition, future work will focus on a cost–benefit analysis comparing the capital and operational expenditure of a magnetic treatment system against the long-term savings from reduced super plasticizer consumption.

Conclusions

The findings confirmed that the application of a magnetic field to water improves its quality, and it has the potential to decrease the water hardness range while mixing for RMC.
