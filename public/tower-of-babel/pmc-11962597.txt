QOT: Quantized Optimal Transport for sample-level distance matrix in single-cell omics
Authors: Zexuan Wang, Qipeng Zhan, Shu Yang, Shizhuo Mu, Jiong Chen, Sumita Garai, Patryk Orzechowski, Joost Wagenaar, Li Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-cell technologies have enabled the high-dimensional characterization of cell populations at an unprecedented scale. The innate complexity and increasing volume of data pose significant computational and analytical challenges, especially in comparative studies delineating cellular architectures across various biological conditions (i.e. generation of sample-level distance matrices). Optimal Transport is a mathematical tool that captures the intrinsic structure of data geometrically and has been applied to many bioinformatics tasks. In this paper, we propose QOT (Quantized Optimal Transport), a new method enabling efficient computation of sample-level distance matrix from large-scale single-cell omics data through a quantization step. We apply our algorithm to real-world single-cell genomics and pathomics datasets, aiming to extrapolate cell-level insights to inform sample-level categorizations. Our empirical study shows that QOT outperforms existing two OT-based algorithms in accuracy and robustness when obtaining a distance matrix from high throughput single-cell measures at the sample level. Moreover, the sample level distance matrix could be used in the downstream analysis (i.e. uncover the trajectory of disease progression), highlighting its usage in biomedical informatics and data science.Keywords: single-cell genomics, optimal transport, Wasserstein distance, Gaussian Mixture Model, quantization

Introduction

Single-cell technologies have enabled the opportunity to explore the molecular mechanism through high-dimensional gene analyses. Numerous computational frameworks have recently been developed to address the heterogeneity between cells: Levine et al. [1] developed PhenoGraph to study the single-cell heterogeneity. Wang et al. [2] developed SIMLR to improve the clustering performance of single-cell sequencing data and provide better visualization and interpretability.

Pathomics enhances molecular omics approaches by providing quantitatively measured, structure-focused details on histological formations, emphasizing objective, tissue-related data. Bülow et al. [3] and Hölscher et al. [4] investigated extracting and analysing computer-generated measurements from digitized images in histopathology and revealed that morphological alterations are associated with different diseases.

To address the challenges posed by sample variation in single-cell datasets, our method effectively captures the diverse and complex conditions encountered in various experiments. Each of these conditions represents a high-dimensional space consisting of heterogeneous cell populations, with each cell characterized by numerous gene measurements. Our framework extends from cell-level comparisons [5–7] to sample-level comparisons [8–10], enabling a comprehensive understanding of collective cellular responses across different samples. This approach ensures that we can accurately interpret the biological complexities at both the cell and sample levels, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of cellular behaviors under varied experimental setups.

Perturbation studies at the cell-level are crucial for understanding the impacts of genetic and environmental interventions on cellular states. For instance, scPerturb, introduced by Peidli et al. [5], uses E-distance to measure the impact of different perturbations among cell populations in single-cell datasets, helping to quantify similarities between perturbations and predict drug effects. This enhances our understanding of how these interventions influence cellular behavior. Furthermore, Replogle et al. [6] demonstrate the use of Energy distance tests to identify perturbations that induce altered transcriptional states in genome-scale experiments.

Building on the insights gained from cell-level studies, the sample-by-sample distance matrix further enhances our understanding by revealing variations within subpopulations, such as distinct cell profiles and/or gene expression patterns. PhEMD (phenotypic Earth mover’s distance) leverages the Earth mover distance (EMD) on diffusion-based spaces to explore patient-to-patient variation and perturbation effects [8]. Similarly, PILOT (Patient level distance with Optimal Transport) investigates the sample-based analysis in multi-scale single-cell and pathomics data using optimal transport [9]. Flores et al. [10] utilize multicellular factor analysis to infer latent spaces that encapsulate coordinated transcriptional changes across cell types, offering a tissue-centric view of biological samples. As a result, the computed sample-level distance matrix measures and compares cellular responses across various conditions, such as disease states and perturbations. This analysis identifies patterns and trajectories linked to disease progression or cellular changes, enabling clear interpretation of complex biological data at the sample level.

Optimal transport is a mathematical concept initially proposed by Monge [11] and subsequently reformulated in a more computationally-friendly manner by Kantorovitch [12]. This concept has garnered considerable interest from theoretical and practical perspectives [13, 14]. In recent times, its applicability has been rediscovered and extended to fields such as image processing [15–17], shape analysis [18], generative modeling [19], and fairness [20]. At its core, OT addresses the challenges of comparing empirical distribution, ensuring mass preservation while minimizing associated costs. The application of Optimal Transport has recently been extended to similarity learning to find the distance between two high-dimensional distributions [21, 22]. In particular, with the advancement of single-cell omics data such as RNA-seq and cytometry, OT has been used in trajectory identification, cell-cell similarity inference, and single-cell data auto-gating [7, 23, 24].

In this study, we propose Quantized Optimal Transport (QOT), a fast and robust method of learning the distance matrix between samples from single-cell datasets using the OT-based Wasserstein distance. We summarize our main contributions as follows:

Related work

The field of single-cell analysis has traditionally employed methods such as pseudo-bulk [10] and proportion analyses to quantify differences at the sample level. Pseudo-bulk analysis sums the gene expression counts for all cells in a sample, while proportion analysis measures the distribution of cell types (clusters) per sample. Furthermore, advanced techniques such as PhEMD [8] and Multiscale PHATE by [25] have been developed, utilizing diffusion maps and multi-resolution representation for more detailed analysis. These methods presuppose cell differentiation and activation through pseudo-time estimates, an assumption that may not be applicable to the heterogeneous cell populations often found in whole organ samples.

Another recent method is PILOT, which generates a sample-level distance matrix in single-cell and pathomics data by calculating the Wasserstein distance between samples. The cost matrix in this method is determined based on predefined cell types [9]. However, PILOT, assumes that single-cell experiments have been previously pre-processed and clustered, which can pose limitations when cell-type labels are unavailable. Furthermore, PILOT aggregates gene expression data from multiple samples to establish a common framework for analysing each cell type. This method uses a uniform gene expression (location) for each cell type across different patients, treating the cell type’s distribution similarly in all samples. Such an approach does not assume uniformity in the mass of cell types across patients but applies the same gene expression (location) parameters, which might mask individual variations in gene expression specific to each patient.

In our proposed method, we address the limitations of existing single-cell analysis techniques by utilizing a flexible, non-assumptive framework. Our approach does not rely on predefined cell types or prior data clustering. Our approach dynamically adapts to each sample’s unique data profile, enabling the capture of distinct gene expression patterns that highlight the specific biological characteristics of each sample.

To accurately obtain a sample-level distance matrix from single-cell data, it is essential to meet two conditions. First, an appropriate metric is needed to address variations in cell counts across samples and the proportions of different cell types, often used as indicators of pathological changes or developmental differences. The Wasserstein distance is suitable in this context as it encompasses both aspects. Second, given the large single-cell datasets, the computational demand for calculating the Wasserstein distance should be carefully managed, as it suffers from the time complexity and the curse of dimensionality . Our approach begins with quantization, simplifying the complex data into a more structured, discrete format to address this issue. Following this, QOT method transforms cell-by-gene expression matrices into parametric Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs), facilitating efficient Wasserstein distance computations between samples.

Two primary steps are involved in the proposed QOT method for generating sample level distance matrix: (1) Acquisition of Gaussian Mixture Representations for each sample (Fig. 1, B-2). (2) Computation of Wasserstein distances based on these Gaussian Mixture Representations (Fig. 1, B-3).

Quantized Optimal Transport

In order to obtain the sample level distance matrix, we compute the Wasserstein distance between two samples and then loop through all the samples to obtain the sample level distance matrix. A typical method to compute the Wasserstein distance between GMMs for two samples is sampling the discretized GMMs and solving the OT problem on discrete distributions. However, the complexity of computing such an OT problem can increase based on the discretization resolution. To alleviate this computation challenge, we provide two designs of cost matrix that focus on different perspectives of the data, and the choice of cost matrix leaves as a hyperparameter in the application.

The first design of the cost matrix is to compute the Wasserstein distance of two GMMs by restricting the set of possible coupling measures also to be Gaussian mixtures, i.e. we compute the optimal transport on the submanifold of Gaussian mixture distributions [27, 28]. QOT-Full and QOT-Cosine offer contrasting approaches for approximating distances between GMMs. QOT-Full provides a more theoretically rigorous approach by focusing on the overall distribution shapes of GMMs and directly calculating the Wasserstein distance. In contrast, QOT-Cosine provides a computationally efficient alternative by focusing on the similarity between GMM centroids. This dual approach allows researchers to select the method that best suits their specific dataset, computational constraints, and the importance of distribution shape in their datasets. The Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) evaluates how well different methods maintain connections between samples linked to their labels. We performed unsupervised clustering on a k-nearest neighbor graph created among the samples, utilizing the Leiden algorithm for this process. The predicted labels and the true labels are used to calculate ARI.

The downstream analysis uses the sample level distance matrix to generate the disease trajectory. For trajectory generation, we feed the sample-level distance matrix from our QOT method into the PHATE algorithm [29]. PHATE then uses the pre-defined matrix to construct a diffusion operator that connects data points based on their proximities, effectively mapping the manifold structure of the data. We analyse the trajectories formed along the first PHATE axis, which represents the main gradient of disease development. The positioning of samples along this axis allows us to define the disease progression order. We calculate the Area Under Precision-Recall curve (AUPRC) and Spearman Correlation to quantify whether the generated trajectory follows the disease progression order. We only considered disease vs. non-disease cases in the calculation of AUPRC. The ordered value from the trajectory is used to calculate the Spearman correlation. For datasets with two classes, we use the label 0 for a control sample and 1 for the disease sample. For the dataset with three classes, we use 0 for the control sample, 1 for the mild sample, and 2 for the severe sample.

We applied the Friedman–Nemenyi test, a non-parametric method, to compare multiple algorithms across different datasets. The Friedman test identifies overall performance differences by ranking the algorithms, while the Nemenyi test pinpoints specific pairs with significant ranking disparities.

Experimental setup

Parameter settings

The QOT method has two hyperparameters: the number of Gaussian components per cell type (1–9) and the type of cost matrix (Full or Cosine Similarity). The input features are the first 50 principal components for all the tested single-cell datasets. For all the tested pathomics datasets, the input features are original pathomics measurements.

We benchmarked our method against PILOT and PhEMD, using the same real-world datasets previously employed in the PILOT study. Since PILOT was tested against three other methods—Pseudo-bulk, Proportions, and Multiscale-PHATE—and outperformed them, our analysis primarily focuses on comparing it to PILOT alone.

In simulation datasets, for the PhEMD, we downsample the number of cells for each sample to be 2000 in simulation datasets (1/10 of the entire datasets). The downsample strategy is used due to the scalability of PhEMD. Using the full size of the dataset leads to memory outrage.

In Table 2, QOT did not perform as well for the follicular lymphoma and kidney datasets. Here, we discuss these cases briefly. For the follicular lymphoma dataset, analysing just the proportions of each cell type has been shown to effectively distinguish the disease, as it is significantly characterized by the proportion of CD4 cells. However, when considering more detailed distributions of gene expression, performance may be impacted due to the overlap in cell type distribution. This suggests that methods focusing primarily on cell-type proportions may yield more effective outcomes for this dataset, while those that account for detailed distributional differences may face challenges due to this overlap. PILOT, which assumes variations only in the mass of cell types while maintaining the same distribution across all samples, might show ideal results. However, this approach can be misleading. When considering the gene expression profiles of different patients, it becomes clear that the distribution should be specific to each individual sample rather than uniform across the entire dataset. PhEMD and QOT share a similar hypothesis in defining distributions, but their effectiveness is somewhat reduced by the overlap in cell clusters. Addressing the issue of distribution overlap while preserving the effectiveness of proportion analysis will be a primary focus in our future research. For the Kidney dataset, its smoothed gene expression profiles are well-suited to the pseudo hypothesis used in PhEMD, where QOT still ranks second. This indicates that while QOT may not always outperform in every scenario, its utility in handling diverse datasets with varying characteristics is evident.

Sample subgroup detection

In real-world applications, sample-level distance matrices help detect sample subgroups in single-cell omics data. A similar experiment has also been conducted in PILOT [9]. We applied a graph-based clustering algorithm [37] at multiple resolutions: higher resolutions yielded more communities, while lower resolutions resulted in fewer. We calculated the silhouette score for each resolution using the predicted group labels and the sample-level distance matrix to determine the optimal clustering. The clustering with the highest silhouette score was selected as it best aligns with the underlying sample-level distances in an unsupervised manner.

Figure 3 illustrates the results of our subgroup detection. We identified three subgroups with a hierarchical structure. The first group, labeled as 0, comprises 11 control samples. The remaining two groups are associated with PDAC (pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma) samples, consisting of 15 samples classified as Tumor 1 (label 1) and 9 samples as Tumor 2 (label 2). Based on the predicted subgroup labels, we analysed differences between the groups at the cell proportion level. Since our primary interest lies in the sub-disease groups, we focus on the Tumor 1 and Tumor 2 groups for the remainder of the analysis.

Next, we show how the choice of hyperparameters will affect the actual runtime. Appendix Figure A.6 showcases the end-to-end runtime for QOT, PhEMD, and PILOT on the Myocardial Infarction dataset, where we downsample the total number of input cells to 20 000 for PhEMD for scalability reasons.

In Figure A.6, the runtimes for PhEMD and PILOT appear as straight lines because the number of Gaussian components per cell type does not influence their performance. PILOT is the most efficient method due to its simpler cost matrix design, while QOT with Cosine similarity is the second most efficient. PhEMD, limited by its scalability, only utilizes 1/5 of the dataset and ranks third in efficiency. QOT with the Full cost matrix is the least efficient, as it calculates the Wasserstein distance from the perspective of all cells. However, it is essential to note that despite being the least efficient, QOT Full estimates the discrete Wasserstein distance between two samples with the Euclidean cost matrix across all available cells among datasets, which will be almost impossible by using EMD [42].

Conclusion

Our study introduces an effective method for analysing large-scale single-cell data through a non-assumptive, flexible framework. Unlike PILOT, which relies on predefined cell types and assumes consistent cell distributions across samples, our approach dynamically adjusts to capture unique gene expression patterns across samples. It avoids the pseudo-time assumptions common in diffusion-based methods, offering a data-driven approach that is not constrained by model-imposed limitations, thus allowing for a more accurate analysis of cellular data structures. We tested our method on 3 simulated and 11 real-world datasets, demonstrating its superior performance over established methodologies. Additionally, we examined how hyperparameters affect runtime and accuracy.

The following are a few interesting future directions. First, we acknowledge that a straightforward application of fitting GMMs directly to each sample may not always yield optimal results, primarily due to the challenge of selecting the appropriate number of components. While we currently use a GMM for each cluster in each sample, exploring more efficient and accurate methods for data quantization could be valuable. Second, the choice of cost matrix is crucial for calculating the Wasserstein distance. Future work will focus on developing a model that learns an appropriate cost matrix while maintaining computational efficiency. Finally, we will explore more use cases and biological insights about the sample-level distance matrix.
