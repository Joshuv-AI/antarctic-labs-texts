Abstract
. We establish that the Grothendieck–Teichmüller conjecture, which posits an isomorphism between the Grothendieck–Teichmüller group G ​ T ^ \widehat{GT} and the absolute Galois group Gal 𝐐 \operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}} of 𝐐 {\bf Q} , holds in the category of profinite spaces. We establish this result by demonstrating that both G ​ T ^ \widehat{GT} and Gal 𝐐 \operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}} are homeomorphic to the Cantor set, thereby unveiling a unifying topological framework for their study. This identification enables a recursive encoding of Gal 𝐐 \operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}} via an explicit algorithm (the Cubic Matrioshka construction), that associates to each element of Gal 𝐐 \operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}} (and similarly to G ​ T ^ \widehat{GT} and its GT-shadows) a unique infinite binary sequence. Such a combinatorial parametrization provides a new computational approach to the structure of these fundamental groups. To recover the arithmetic content lost in the passage to topology, we introduce a novel invariant: the Galois Grothendieck Path Integral. Inspired by the Feynman Path Integral, this construction encapsulates deep arithmetic information through correlation functions, offering a new perspective on the interplay between Galois symmetries, deformation theory, and quantum structures. As an application, we explore its potential role in a quantum model at the Planck scale.

1.2.
Results and Discussions

•

In this work, we demonstrate that the Grothendieck–Teichmüller Conjecture holds in the category of profinite spaces. Our approach is framed within the context of Polish group theory, wherein we establish that both
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
and
\widehat{GT}

•

We stipulate that via this homeomorphism every element of

(and of

(Sec.

–

•

This topological framework provides the essential tools to define a Path Integral for

•

We introduce an algorithm — the Cubic Matrioshka Algorithm (CMA) — a concrete construction that enables an explicit characterization of automorphisms in terms of their profinite topology, applicable to both

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
, Sec.

1.4.
Significance and Limitations

Topological, Geometric
\&
Combinatorial Insights

A Cantor set is compact, metrizable, totally disconnected, and perfect meaning that it contains no isolated points. It is also second-countable, which implies that it has a countable basis for its topology. Establishing that the Grothendieck–Teichmüller group

\widehat{GT}
is homeomorphic to a Cantor set consequently shows that
\widehat{GT}
is a compact, totally disconnected, and perfect topological space.
The total disconnectedness implies the absence of nontrivial connected components, consistent with its profinite structure. Moreover, this property ensures that every point in
\widehat{GT}
is a limit point, reflecting the complexity of
\widehat{GT}
as a space of automorphisms or geometric actions.

Additionally, the metrizability of
\widehat{GT}
implies that its topology can be described using a metric, a crucial feature. This metric allows us to define a notion of distance between automorphisms, facilitating the formulation of an approximation criterion for automorphisms, which can be approached using explicit combinatorial methods. We will particularly highlight the connection between this metric and the Hamming metric, typically used in binary codes and hypercubes.

Since the absolute Galois group is naturally equipped with the profinite topology, making it compact and totally disconnected, the homeomorphism between

\widehat{GT}
and the absolute Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
reveals that both groups share the same topological structure. In particular, topologically, we can view the elements of
\widehat{GT}
and
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
as arising from a recursive construction, where each automorphism corresponds to a vertex in an infinite-dimensional hypercube. We will show that, in the case of

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
, the construction of each element as an infinite binary word encodes the precise arithmetical data that defines each automorphism.

This topological investigation has allowed us to introduce a Path Integral for
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
(the Galois Gorthendieck Path Integral), which allows to regain the arithmetical information which initially was unavailable, from the topological homeomorphism viewpoint.

The absolute Galois group encodes profound arithmetic information about the field of rational numbers and its algebraic extensions. In contrast, the
\widehat{GT}
group emerges from the study of the profinite completion of fundamental groups of moduli spaces and is deeply rooted in geometric and combinatorial settings. By establishing a homeomorphism between the profinite spaces corresponding to those groups, we create a bridge that allows techniques and intuitions from geometry and combinatorics to be applied to classical problems in number theory, in line with Grothendieck’s vision in anabelian geometry. In particular the group embedding of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}

Profinite Groups, Profinite Spaces and Cantor Sets

2.1.

We recall some well known facts concerning profinite groups. A profinite group is a pro-object in the category of finite groups. It is a cofiltered limit (inverse limit) of finite groups, where transition maps are surjective homomorphisms.
The category
ProFinGrp
of profinite groups is equivalent to the pro-category of finite groups,
Pro(FinGrp)
. This is another way of stating that profinite groups are “formal” cofiltered limits of finite groups. This category of profinite groups is complete and products, inverse limits are constructed as in the category of topological groups, with the product/limit topology.

The profinite completion functor is left adjoint to the forgetful functor:

{\bf Grp}\to{\bf ProFinGrp},\quad U:{\bf ProFinGrp}\to{\bf Grp}.

2.1.1.

Furthermore, profinite groups are Stone spaces (compact, Hausdorff, totally disconnected). This property allows to connect them to Boolean algebras via Stone duality. The Stone duality establishes a contravariant equivalence of categories:

{\bf StoneSpace}\simeq{\bf BoolAlg}^{op}

where:

•

StoneSpace
is the category of Stone spaces (compact, Hausdorff, totally disconnected spaces) with continuous maps

•

BoolAlg
is the category of Boolean algebras with homomorphisms.

Under this equivalence, a Stone space
X
corresponds to the Boolean algebra
Clopen(X)
of its clopen subsets, and vice versa
[
27
,
15
,
13
]
.

2.1.2.

A Galois category is a category equipped with a fiber functor to finite sets, whose automorphism group is profinite. For example, the category of finite étale covers of a scheme
X
forms a Galois category, with
\pi_{1}^{et}(X)
, the étale fundamental group as its profinite automorphism group. An important statement shows that the fundamental group of a Galois category is a profinite group, and every profinite group arises this way.
In fact, by
[
31
, Thm. 3.3.2]
, every profinite group is isomorphic (as a topological group) to some Galois group.

There are several properties that we mention because of their importance.

•

Closed subgroups of profinite groups are profinite.

•

Continuous homomorphic images of profinite groups are profinite.

•

A profinite group is topologically finitely generated if it has a dense finitely generated subgroup.

2.2.

A profinite space is a cofiltered limit (inverse limit) of finite discrete spaces. Profinite spaces form a well-defined category with continuous maps as morphisms and is denoted
ProFin
.

In particular, the Cantor set is a profinite space, which explicitly can be expressed as:

\mathcal{C}\cong\varprojlim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{0,1\}^{n},

where the bonding maps
\pi_{n}:\{0,1\}^{n+1}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
are projections dropping the last coordinate. This identifies

\mathcal{C}
as an object in
ProFin
, the category of profinite spaces.

2.2.1.

In the category
CPTD
of compact, perfect, totally disconnected, metrizable spaces the Cantor set
\mathcal{C}
is the unique object in
CPTD
up to homeomorphism. This property enjoys a universal property: for any object
X\in{\rm Ob}({\bf CPTD})
, there is a unique (up to isomorphism) continuous surjection
X\to\mathcal{C}
.

Consider the following inverse system (diagram) in the category of topological spaces, where objects are finite discrete space
\{0,1\}^{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Morphisms are projection maps
p_{n}:\{0,1\}^{n+1}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
that drop the last coordinate:

p_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\cdots,x_{n+1})\to(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\cdots,x_{n})

and this forms a tower of spaces and maps.
The inverse limit (or projective limit) of this diagram is the space of infinite binary sequences:

\ \varprojlim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{0,1\}^{n}.

2.2.2.

Going back to the Stone duality, the Cantor set corresponds to the countable atomless
1
1

1

\forall a\in\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\},\ \exists b\in\mathbb{B}\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }b\leq a\text{ and }b\neq a.
Boolean algebra
\mathbb{B}
, which is unique up to isomorphism:

\mathcal{C}\cong{\rm Spec}(\mathbb{B})

where
{\rm Spec}
is the Stone space functor. Here
\mathbb{B}
has no minimal elements (atoms) and is uniquely determined up to isomorphism.
The space
\mathcal{C}
is perfect (no isolated points) and universal for compact metrizable Stone spaces.

Therefore, the Cantor set is a profinite space—an inverse limit of finite discrete spaces it is
compact, Hausdorff, and totally disconnected. It is additionally a perfect (no isolated points) and metrizable.,

2.2.3.

A cylinder set

Z_{s}=\{x\in\mathcal{C}\,;\,x\quad\text{starts with}\,s\},

for a fixed word
s\in\{0,1\}^{n}
of length
n
which
corresponds to the Boolean algebra element
a_{s}\in\mathbb{B}
. In particular, the union and intersection operations are given by

Z_{s}\cup Z_{t}\leftrightarrow a_{s}\vee a_{t},\quad Z_{s}\cap Z_{t}\leftrightarrow a_{s}\wedge a_{t}

The topology on
\mathcal{C}
is generated by cylinder sets
Z_{s}
and the cylinder sets
Z_{s}
are clopen.

2.2.4.

The Cantor space being metrizable it is equipped with a standard metric defined as follows:

(1)

d(x,y)=\frac{1}{2^{\min\{k\,|x_{k}\neq y_{k}\}}}

where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots)
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots)
are sequences in
\mathcal{C}
. This is an ultrametric, meaning that:

d(x,z)\leq\max\{d(x,y),d(y,z)\}.

2.2.5.

The Cantor set, (the set of infinite binary sequences with the product topology) plays a significant role in model theory, particularly in the study of types, theories, and topological dynamics.

More precisely, points in
\mathcal{C}
correspond to models of propositional theories, where each bit decides the truth value of a propositional variable. This is important in proposition logic. In propositional Logic, the Cantor space is homeomorphic to the space of truth assignments for a countable set of propositional variables. Each sequence
(a_{i})\in\mathcal{C}
assigns

a_{i}
to the
i
-th variable. In particular the Cantor space is the space of models of the theory of

\mathbb{B}
, with clopen sets encoding finitary properties.

Finally, the Cantor space is also central to studying automorphism groups of structures (e.g., the automorphism group of a countable dense linear order) and we will see that it plays a central role in Grothendieck’s conjecture.

3.
The Absolute Galois group

We consider a specific object in the category
ProFingrp
: the absolute Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{{\bf Q}}/{\bf Q})
over the field of rational numbers
{\bf Q}
.
Given a field
k
, the Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(K/k)
of an algebraic extension
K
of
k
is defined as the group of all automorphisms of
K
that fix every element of
k
. In case of
k={\bf Q}
, the absolute Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
consists of all automorphisms of the algebraic closure
\overline{{\bf Q}}
that fix
{\bf Q}
.

The group
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is a profinite group (
[
31
, Prop. 3.1.1]
), meaning it is compact, totally disconnected, and is the inverse limit of finite groups.
It can be described as the inverse limit of the finite Galois groups
\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q})
, where
L
runs over all finite Galois extensions of
{\bf Q}
, i.e., the set of all subfields of
\overline{{\bf Q}}
that are finite Galois extensions of
{\bf Q}
. Explicitly, we have:

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}=\varprojlim\limits_{L/{\bf Q}\atop\text{finite Galois}}\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q}),

where the projective system is indexed by finite Galois extensions
L/{\bf Q}
.

Since
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is the limit of the finite Galois groups
\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q})
it inherits, as such, a natural topology, referred to as the profinite topology, in which the open sets correspond to the cosets of open normal subgroups in the finite Galois groups
\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q})
. This topology is compact and totally disconnected.

3.1.
The Clopens of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}

For every finite Galois extension
L/{\bf Q}
, there is a natural continuous surjective homomorphism:

\pi_{L}:\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}\to\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q}),\quad\sigma\mapsto\sigma|_{L}

where every automorphism
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
restricts to an automorphism of
L
, since since
L/{\bf Q}
is Galois. Such maps are compatible over nested extensions. These homomorphisms are well defined and continuous in the profinite topology.

3.1.1.

Specifically, for a finite extension
L
, the kernel

N_{L}=\ker(\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}\to\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q}))

consists of all automorphisms of
\overline{{\bf Q}}
fixing
L
pointwise, i.e.,

N_{L}=\{\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}\,;\,\sigma|_{L}=id_{L}\}=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{{\bf Q}}/L).

The subgroup
N_{L}
is clopen (i.e. open and closed) and normal in
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
, because
L/{\bf Q}
is Galois.

3.1.2.

A
neighbourhood
of an automorphism
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is a coset of
N_{L}
in
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
defined as:

\sigma\cdot N_{L}=\{\sigma\cdot\nu\,;\,\nu\in N_{L}\},

where
\nu
fixes
L
. Precisely, this contains all automorphisms
\tau
, that can be written as
\tau=\sigma\cdot\nu
for some
\nu\in N_{L}
and since
\nu
fixes
L
, they agree with
\sigma
on
L
, i.e.:

\tau|_{L}=(\sigma\cdot\nu)|_{L}=\sigma|_{L}\cdot\nu|_{L}=\sigma|_{L}\cdot id|_{L}=\sigma|_{L}.

The cosets
\sigma\cdot N_{L}
for
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
partition
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
into finitely many disjoint clopen sets, each corresponding to an element of the finite quotient
\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q})
. This decomposition refines as
K
increases, forming the inverse limit topology.

The group
N_{L}
is itself a profinite group:

\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{{\bf Q}}/L)=\varprojlim\limits_{K/L\atop\text{finite Galois}}\operatorname{Gal}(K/L).

We refer to

3.1.3.

For nested finite Galois extensions

, there is a restriction map:

\operatorname{res}_{L/K}:\operatorname{Gal}(K/{\bf Q})\to\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q}),

which fits into the inverse limit diagram.

In particular for nested Galois extensions the following fact occurs:
If
L\subset K
, then
N_{K}\subset N_{L}
and the coset

is a smaller neighborhood of
\sigma
, refining the action to agree on the larger field

3.1.4.

We illustrate the above construction on an example. Take for instance the tower of cyclotomic extensions
{\bf Q}(\zeta_{p^{n}})
for a fixed prime
p

{\bf Q}\subset{\bf Q}(\zeta_{p})\subset{\bf Q}(\zeta_{p^{2}})\subset{\bf Q}(\zeta_{p^{3}})\subset\cdots

where
\zeta_{p^{n}}
is a primitive
p^{n}
-th roots to unity. The Galois group of each extension
{\bf Q}(\zeta_{p^{n}})/{\bf Q}
is given by

\operatorname{Gal}({\bf Q}(\zeta_{p^{n}})/{\bf Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}

the multiplicative group of integers modulo
p^{n}
.

The tower
{\bf Q}(\zeta_{p^{n}})
forms a directed system of extensions. The absolute Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
projects onto each finite Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}({\bf Q}(\zeta_{p^{n}})/{\bf Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})^{\times},
via reduction modulo

. The inverse limit over all natural numbers

gives the profinite completion

\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}=\varprojlim\limits_{n}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z}),

which is a quotient of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
.

The projection map from
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is given by the natural surjective homomorphism :

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times},

defined by restricting an automorphism
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
to act on the
p

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}
is a closed normal subgroup of

.

3.2.
Discrete vs. Profinite Topologies

It is noteworthy to remark that the profinite absolute Galois group is endowed with the profinite topology in contrast with the finite Galois groups, which occur in the definition

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}=\varprojlim\limits_{L/{\bf Q}\atop\text{finite Galois}}\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q})

and which, individually, have a discrete topology. We recall below the standard notion of an
isolated point
, which is taken from topology.

Let
X
be a topological space, and let
x\in X
. The point
x
is said to be isolated if the singleton
\{x\}
is an open set in
X
. Applying this concept in the context of Galois groups, we say that an elements
\sigma
is isolated if
\{\sigma\}
is an open set in the Galois group. This implies that the element
\sigma
is uniquely determined by its action on some finite Galois extension
K/{\bf Q}
.

In a finite Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q})
, every singleton set is open. This makes every element isolated. In the context of the previous example, where
L={\bf Q}(\zeta_{p^{n}})
, let us consider a Frobenius automorphism
\operatorname{Frob}_{p}\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Topologically, it is considered as isolated, because the group
\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q})
is a finite abelian group and endowed with the discrete topology, where every singleton subset is clopen. The Frobenius automorphism (unramified in this extension) acts on
\zeta_{p^{n}}
via

F:\zeta_{p^{n}}\mapsto\zeta_{p^{n}}^{p},

lifting the residual Frobenius
x\mapsto x^{p}\mod p^{n}
.
In particular, this means that
\operatorname{Frob}_{p}
has no limit points in the group.

However, if we consider
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
its profinite topology (meaning it is compact, Hausdorff and totally disconnected) is not discrete. Specifically, this implies that it has no isolated points. In particular, the Frobenius lifts
\operatorname{Frob}_{p}
discussed above, in
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
, are
no longer
isolated. Any open neighborhood of
\operatorname{Frob}_{p}
contains infinitely many other automorphisms that agree with
\operatorname{Frob}_{p}
on
L
but differ on larger extensions. Frobenius elements are dense in
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
, by Chebotarev’s density theorem.

4.
The Absolute Galois Group from the Polish (group) Perspective

4.1.
The category of Polish Spaces
PolSp

One can define as a full subcategory of the category of topological spaces the category of Polish spaces
PolSp
. In this category objects are all Polish spaces and morphisms are continuous maps between Polish spaces. We refer to
[
2
]
, §6 Chap. IX and
[
16
,
17
]
for more information on Polish spaces.

A Polish space is a topological space that is separable and completely metrizable. That is, there exists a metric on the space that induces the topology and makes it a complete metric space, and the space has a countable dense subset.

4.2.
The category of Polish Groups
PolGr

A Polish group is a topological group which is also a Polish space, with continuous group operations. Polish groups are widely studied because they arise naturally in many areas of mathematics, including descriptive set theory, dynamical systems, and topological group theory. Polish groups also form a category
PolGr
where objects are Polish groups and morphisms are continuous group homomorphisms between Polish groups. These morphisms must preserve the group structure and be continuous with respect to the topology.

4.3.
The full subcategory of t.d.l.c Polish groups

A particularly interesting class of Polish groups are the totally disconnected locally compact (t.d.l.c) Polish groups. These are Polish groups (separable, completely metrizable topological groups) that are additionally totally disconnected and locally compact.

This is a subclass of Polish groups, satisfying the subcategory requirement for objects. The morphisms are continuous group homomorphisms between t.d.l.c. Polish groups. These morphisms are identical to those in the parent category (the category of Polish groups), restricted to the subclass of t.d.l.c. Polish groups.

The t.d.l.c. Polish groups form a full subcategory because the morphisms between them are exactly the same as in the parent category (there are no additional restrictions imposed).

Such groups include profinite groups (inverse limits of finite groups with the discrete topology), automorphism groups of countable structures, and certain groups of homeomorphisms. The structure of these groups is deeply connected with harmonic analysis,
p
-adic analysis, and the theory of locally compact groups.

4.4.
Classification of t.d.l.c Polish groups

According to
[
14
]
, any totally disconnected locally compact Polish group
G
must be homeomorphic to one of the following:

(1)

A discrete space of at most countable size.

(2)

The Cantor set
\mathcal{C}
.

(3)

The space
\mathcal{C}\times\mathbb{N}
with the product topology.

We shortly discuss this.

•

If
G
is countable, then it must have the discrete topology (since a countable, totally disconnected Polish space is discrete). This corresponds to case (1).

•

If
G
s uncountable, but compact, then it is homeomorphic to
\mathcal{C}
. This follows from the well-known characterization of compact, totally disconnected, metrizable spaces as homeomorphic to a closed subspace of

\mathcal{C}
, and for Polish spaces, this must be exactly
\mathcal{C}
if it is uncountable.

•

If
G
is non-compact, then since it is locally compact and totally disconnected, it can be expressed as a countable union of compact open sets. It follows from a classical theorem of van Dantzig that such a group has a basis of compact open subgroups. Moreover, the space of cosets of a compact open subgroup in a locally compact totally disconnected Polish group behaves like a countable discrete space. Thus, one can show that

G
is homeomorphic to
\mathcal{C}\times\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}=Gal(\overline{{\bf Q}}/{\bf Q})

be the absolute Galois group of
{\bf Q}
, endowed with its natural profinite topology. Then
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is a compact, totally disconnected topological group. Moreover,
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is a Polish group.

Proof.

Recall that
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is the inverse limit of the finite Galois groups
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}(L/{\bf Q})
as
L
ranges over the finite Galois extensions of

{\bf Q}
. Each
\operatorname{\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}}(L/{\bf Q})
is finite and equipped with the discrete topology, and hence the inverse limit

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
inherits the profinite topology. In particular,

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is compact, Hausdorff, and totally disconnected.

The open neighborhoods of the identity in

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
are given by the clopen normal subgroups

N_{L}=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{{\bf Q}}/L),

where
L/{\bf Q}
is a finite Galois extension. Since every finite extension of

{\bf Q}
is defined by the roots of a polynomial with rational coefficients, and the set of such polynomials is countable, there are only countably many finite Galois extensions of
{\bf Q}
. Hence, the family
\{N_{L}\}
forms a countable basis for the topology of

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
.

Being a compact Hausdorff group with a countable basis,

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is second countable. By Urysohn’s metrization theorem, it follows that
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is metrizable. Moreover, every compact metric space is complete and separable. Therefore,
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is a Polish group.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{{\bf Q}}/{\bf Q})

denote the absolute Galois group of
{\bf Q}
, considered as an object in the category
\mathbf{ProFin}
of profinite spaces. Then
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is perfect; that is, under the canonical forgetful functor
\mathbf{ProFin}\to\mathbf{Top}
, the underlying topological space of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
has no isolated points.

Proof.

Recall that the absolute Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is endowed with the profinite topology, which is defined as the inverse limit of the finite Galois groups

\operatorname{Gal}(L/{\bf Q})
for finite Galois extensions
L/{\bf Q}
. In this topology, a fundamental system of open neighborhoods of any element
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is given by the cosets

\sigma N_{L},\quad\text{where}\,N_{L}=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{{\bf Q}}/L).

This subgroup consists of all automorphisms in
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
that act trivially on
L
.

Each subgroup
N_{L}
is a clopen normal subgroup. Since
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is infinite (indeed, it is not a discrete space), no such subgroup
N_{L}
is trivial. Therefore, every open coset
\sigma N_{L}
contains more than one element.

We demonstrate by contradiction that no element in
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
can be isolated.

Assume for contradiction that
\sigma
is an isolated point in
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
. Then there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
\sigma
with
U=\{\sigma\}.

But, by the profinite topology,

must contain a coset of the form

for some finite Galois extension
L/{\bf Q}
. Since
N_{L}
is nontrivial, the coset
\sigma N_{L}
contains at least two distinct elements, contradicting
U=\{\sigma\}
.

Therefore, no element in
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is isolated, which implies that
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is a perfect space.
∎

Finally, we deduce that since
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is non-discrete, metrizable and profinite group it is homeomorphic to the Cantor set:

Let

This is a direct application of

5.
\widehat{GT}
as a Cantor Set

5.1.
The meta-profinite fundamental group

5.1.1.

We will show that a key aspect of the topological structure of
\widehat{GT}
, which has significant implications for its arithmetic and geometric properties, is that it can be identified, in the context of profinite spaces, to a Cantor set. This statement mirrors a similar structure in the absolute Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
, showing that in the category of profinite spaces, these two objects are isomorphic.

5.1.2.

The meta-profinite fundamental group associated with the Teichmüller tower of moduli spaces refers to an inverse system of profinite fundamental groups of moduli spaces of curves, encoding the asymptotic and universal Galois-theoretic properties of these spaces. It is a conceptual refinement of the classical profinite fundamental group, capturing the structure of the entire Teichmüller tower.

Consider the tower of moduli spaces
\mathcal{M}_{0,n},
where

\mathcal{M}_{0,n}
is the moduli space of genus zero curves with

n
marked points. Each of these spaces has an étale fundamental group

\pi_{1}^{et}(\mathcal{M}_{0,n})
, which arises as the profinite completion of the topological fundamental group

\pi_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{0,n})
.

Definition 5.1
.

The meta-profinite fundamental group, denoted by
\Pi_{meta}
, is then defined as the inverse limit of the system of these profinite fundamental groups over the entire Teichmüller tower:

\Pi_{meta}=\varprojlim_{n\geq 4}\widehat{\pi}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{0,n}).

Here, the inverse system is structured by natural maps between different moduli spaces, coming from forgetful:

\mathcal{M}_{0,n+1}\to\mathcal{M}_{0,n}

and gluing operations.
One of the central ideas in Grothendieck’s
Esquisse d’un Programme
was to study the absolute Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
via its action on fundamental groups of algebraic varieties, particularly the fundamental groups of moduli spaces.

A key result is that the automorphism group of this meta-profinite fundamental group defines the Grothendieck–Teichmüller group:

\widehat{GT}={\rm Aut}(\Pi_{meta}),

and captures a universal symmetry of the Teichmüller tower. It contains
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
as a subgroup. The Grothendieck–Teichmüller group

\widehat{GT}
is conjectured to be (essentially)
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
.

5.2.
The Grothendieck–Teichmüller Group

The profinite Grothendieck-Teichmüller group
\widehat{GT}
is a universal profinite symmetry group governing
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
-equivariant deformations of structures tied to the geometric étale fundamental group (e.g., outer automorphisms, associators). Its defining relations encode obstructions to coherence, ensuring compatibility with the arithmetic of moduli spaces of curves and braided tensor categories. As a profinite group,
\widehat{GT}
is compact, totally disconnected, and contains the absolute Galois group. Its elements act on étale fundamental groups and moduli spaces, mediating deformations constrained by the pentagon and hexagon equations.

To demonstrate this conjecture in the context of profinite spaces, we first show that the Grothendieck–Teichmüller group

\widehat{GT}
is an object of the category
PolGr
.

The Grothendieck–Teichmüller group can be more prosaically included in a short exact sequence involving automorphism groups of braid and free profinite groups:

1\to\widehat{GT}\to{\rm Aut}(\widehat{B}_{4})\to{\rm Out}(\widehat{F}_{2}),

where
\widehat{B}_{4}
is the profinite braid group on four strands and
\widehat{F}_{2}
is its profinite completion of the free group
F_{2}
in two generators. The map to
{\rm Out}(\widehat{F}_{2})
(outer automorhisms) factors through
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
.

\widehat{GT}
acts on
\widehat{F}_{2}
, by automorphisms that preserve profintie versions of the pentagon and hexagon equations. It ensures the coherence with the action of the absolute Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
on etale fundamental groups. This action being faithful (injective), this means that
\widehat{GT}
embeds into
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
.

Therefore formally,
\widehat{GT}
is a subgroup of the automorphism group of
\widehat{F}_{2}
, i.e.
\widehat{GT}\subset{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
(see
[
19
, Ch.5, Sec. 5.3]
).

5.3.
The group
\widehat{F}_{2}
is Polish

We first show that
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
is a Polish group.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{ProFin}
denote the category of profinite groups with continuous homomorphisms, and let
\mathbf{PolGrp}
denote the category of Polish groups with continuous group homomorphisms. The profinite completion functor

\widehat{(\cdot)}:\mathbf{Grp}\to\mathbf{ProFin}

applied to the free group
F_{2}
on two generators produces the profinite group
\widehat{F}_{2}
. Then, under the natural inclusion functor

\mathbf{ProFin}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{PolGrp},

the group
\widehat{F}_{2}
is an object in
\mathbf{PolGrp}
, i.e., it admits a compatible Polish topology, making it a Polish group.

Proof.

A Polish group is a topological group that is separable and completely metrizable. We will show that
\widehat{F}_{2}
satisfies these conditions.

Consider the profinite completion of the free group
F_{2}
. There is a natural homomorphism
i_{F_{2}}:F_{2}\to\widehat{F}_{2}
, which is defined by the universal property of profinite completions.
\widehat{F}_{2}
is the inverse limit of all finite quotients of
F_{2}
.
Since
F_{2}
is residually finite and finitely generated, its profinite completion is compact, Hausdorff, and totally disconnected, satisfying the definition of a profinite group.

For a finitely generated group such as
F_{2}
the set of finite-index normal subgroups is countable. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are finitely many normal subgroups of
F_{2}
with index
n
. Moreover, the union over all
n
forms a countable set. To be more specific,
[
31
, Lem 4.1.2]
states that if a profinite group is generated by a finite set, then for any integer
r
, there are at most
r^{r^{2}+2}
open normal subgroups of index
r
in
\widehat{F}_{2}
. Therefore, it follows that
\widehat{F}_{2}
has only countably many open normal subgroups.

The profinite topology on
\widehat{F}_{2}
has a basis of open sets derived from these finite quotients. Since there are countably many finite quotients, the topology is second-countable. Therefore, applying
[
31
, Prop. 4.1.3]
(a profinite group with only countably many open normal subgroups has a countable base of open sets) implies that
\widehat{F}_{2}
is second countable.

Finally, by Urysohn’s metrization theorem, a second-countable, compact Hausdorff space is metrizable.
By the above arguments,
\widehat{F}_{2}
is compact Hausdorff and second-countable, so it is metrizable. Moreover, compact metric spaces are complete. Any metric on a compact space is naturally complete. Since

\widehat{F}_{2}
is compact and metrizable, it is completely metrizable.

Since we have already shown that
\widehat{F}_{2}
is second countable, completely metrizable profinite group, it follows that
\widehat{F}_{2}

is a Polish group.
∎

5.4.
The group
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
is Polish

We prove the following standard fact from profinite group theory.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathbf{ProFin}
denote the category of profinite groups with continuous homomorphisms, and let
\mathbf{PolGrp}
denote the category of Polish groups with continuous group homomorphisms. Consider the automorphism group functor

\operatorname{Aut}(-):\mathbf{ProFin}\to\mathbf{Grp}.

For the profinite free group
\widehat{F}_{2}
, the group
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
naturally inherits a topology as an inverse limit of automorphism groups of finite quotients. This gives rise to a functorial assignment

\operatorname{Aut}(-):\mathbf{ProFin}\to\mathbf{ProFin}\cap\mathbf{PolGrp},

meaning that
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
is both an object in
\mathbf{ProFin}
and in
\mathbf{PolGrp}
. In particular, it is a Polish and profinite group.

Proof.

We establish that the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
of the profinite free group
\widehat{F}_{2}
on two generators is both a profinite group and a Polish group. This is achieved by demonstrating that
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
can be expressed as the inverse limit of the automorphism groups of its finite quotients, thereby inheriting a natural topology that is compact, totally disconnected, and metrizable. Consequently,

\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
resides in the intersection of the categories of profinite and Polish groups.

A profinite group is a topological group that is isomorphic to the inverse limit of an inverse system of finite discrete groups. Such groups are compact, totally disconnected, and Hausdorff. A Polish group is a topological group that is separable and completely metrizable. The intersection of these categories consists of groups that are both profinite and Polish, implying compactness, total disconnectedness, metrizability, and separability.

Consider the profinite free group
\widehat{F}_{2}
on two generators. By definition,
\widehat{F}_{2}
is the inverse limit of its finite quotients:

\widehat{F}_{2}=\varprojlim_{N\,\,\triangleleft\,\,\widehat{F}_{2}}\widehat{F}_{2}/N

where the limit is taken over all open normal subgroups
N
of
\widehat{F}_{2}
. Each quotient
\widehat{F}_{2}/N
is a finite group, and its automorphism group
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2}/N)
is also finite. The automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
can thus be expressed as the inverse limit

\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})=\varprojlim_{N\,\,\triangleleft\,\,\widehat{F}_{2}}{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2}/N).

In this inverse limit,
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
inherits the inverse limit topology, making it a closed subgroup of the product
\prod\limits_{N}{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2}/N)
.

Since each
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2}/N)
is finite and discrete, their product, equipped with the product topology, is compact and totally disconnected. Therefore
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
, as a closed subgroup of this product, is also compact and totally disconnected, satisfying the definition of a profinite group.

To establish that
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
is a Polish group, we note that it is metrizable due to its construction as an inverse limit of finite discrete groups. Moreover,
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
is separable, as it contains a countable dense subset arising from its topological generation by a finite set. Compactness implies complete metrizability. Thus,
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
is a Polish group.

The functorial assignment

\operatorname{Aut}(-):\mathbf{ProFin}\to\mathbf{ProFin}\cap\mathbf{PolGrp},

is valid, and
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
resides in the intersection of the categories of profinite and Polish groups.

∎

Note that for non-finitely generated profinite groups the situation can be more complicated.

5.5.
Is
\widehat{GT}
a Polish Group?

The fact that
\widehat{GT}\in{\rm Ob}({\bf ProFin})
implies that
\widehat{GT}
is endowed with the
profinite
topology.

A crucial question is whether
\widehat{GT}
is second-countable (i.e., has a countable basis for its topology). We establish in this section that
\widehat{GT}
is a Polish group.

Lemma 5.3
.

Any compact profinite subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
is a Polish group.

Proof.

Recall that a profinite group is compact, Hausdorff, and totally disconnected. For a profinite group to be Polish, it must also be second-countable (i.e., its topology has a countable base) and metrizable.
The automorphism group
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
being a Polish group, is second-countable and completely metrizable.

Closed subgroups of Polish groups are Polish. Since

{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
is Polish, any closed subgroup of it is also Polish.

Compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces (like Polish groups) are closed. Thus, a compact profinite subgroup of

{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
is necessarily closed and inherits the Polish properties.

We now explain why the subgroup is Polish.
As a closed subgroup of the second-countable group

{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
, the subgroup is second-countable.
Furthermore, profinite groups are metrizable if they are second-countable. The subgroup inherits a compatible metric from

{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
. Finally, compactness ensures completeness with respect to the metric.

Therefore, any compact profinite subgroup of

{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
is a closed, second-countable, and completely metrizable topological group, hence Polish. This aligns with the general principle that compact subgroups of Polish groups are Polish.
∎

Proposition 5.1
.

The profinite group
\widehat{GT}
is a Polish group.

Proof.

First, the profinite
\widehat{GT}
is a subgroup

\widehat{GT}

From the definition of a profinite group it follows that
\widehat{GT}
is

In any Hausdorff topological group—which all Polish groups are—every compact subgroup is closed. Profinite groups are by definition inverse limits of finite groups and are therefore compact. Thus, when a profinite group is embedded as a subgroup in a Polish group, it is necessarily a closed subgroup because its compactness guarantees that property in a Hausdorff space.
Therefore,
\widehat{GT}
is closed in the metrizable Polish space
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})

Now, a Polish group is a topological group whose topology is given by a complete metric that is separable. So, every closed subgroup of a Polish group is itself a Polish group (see for instance
[

In particular, such a closed subgroup inherits the following properties:

•

Completeness:
In any complete metric space, every closed subset is complete with respect to the induced metric. Therefore, given a Polish group (a complete metric space) the closed subgroup will also be complete.

•

Separability:
A subspace of a separable space is also separable. Since Polish groups are separable, the closed subgroup will inherit separability from the larger group.

•

Group Structure:
Since the subgroup is closed and the group operations are continuous, the subgroup with the subspace topology remains a topological group.

∎

The Grothendieck–Teichmüller group is therefore a
second-countable profinite group
. This follows from its construction as a closed subgroup of second-countable (Polish) automorphism groups and the countability of its defining conditions. Its second-countability aligns with the broader framework of Galois theory and profinite group theory.

This is summarized as following statement.

Corollary 5.1
.

The Grothendieck–Teichmüller group is a second-countable profinite group.

By virtue of Theorem

6.2.
Cubic Matrioshka Construction

From a computational perspective, a natural approach is to approximate an infinite binary sequence (representing an automorphism in

or

n

6.2.1.
Recursion

This construction is recursive and gives rise to a bipartite graph. Specifically, the

Q_{n}^{(2)}

Q_{n}^{(1)}
(resp.
Q_{n}^{(2)}

6.2.2.
Distance

The Hamming distance between two finite binary words provides a combinatorial measure of their difference. In the context of the Polish groups

and

p

Remark 6.1

When considering the infinite binary sequences (rather than finite approximations), the Hamming distance becomes irrelevant, and the Cantorian metric, as defined in

6.3.
Cantorian Coordinates for the Automorphisms in

By virtue of Theorem

thereby establishing a bijective correspondence between the elements of

In our setting, the homeomorphism

This explicit encoding provides a

•

The Cantor set structure implies that

•

The finite approximations given by the projections

•

The encoding thereby provides an effective tool for comparing two automorphisms: on one side using the metrizablility (induced from the Polish group property) and secondly via the Hamming metric, which exists naturally on finite binary words that is for the approximations.

In summary, the homeomorphism

as established in Theorem

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
and also encapsulates the complete data of its finite, discrete quotients

In other words, this provides a novel coordinate system for each element of

in the Cantor set.

Such a coordinate system can be also attributed to the profinite Grothendeick–Teichmüller group

6.4.
Interpretation for
\widehat{GT}

One can apply exactly the same reasoning for
\widehat{GT}
. Suppose that there exists an effective encoding

\Phi\colon\widehat{GT}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}},

which is a homeomorphism onto its image, so that each element of
\widehat{GT}
is represented by a unique infinite binary sequence. For each

\pi_{n}\colon\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}^{n}

the projection onto the first
n

A subset of
\mathcal{C}
, where a finite number of coordinates is fixed (and the rest is unrestricted) is usually called a

.

Proposition 6.1

(Hypercube and Cylindrical Structure for Finite Approximations)
.

Assume that automorphisms in
\widehat{GT}
are encoded by binary sequences as above. Then for each fixed
n\geq 1
:

(1)

The set
\{0,1\}^{n}
naturally carries the structure of an
n

n
-bit words.

(2)

Two vertices

are adjacent (i.e., connected by an edge) if and only if they differ in exactly one coordinate. In particular, the Hamming distance

d_{H}(w,w^{\prime})=\#\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}:w_{i}\neq w^{\prime}_{i}\}

is equal to 1 if and only if
w
and
w^{\prime}
are neighboring vertices in the hypercube.

(3)

Consequently, the Hamming distance between the finite approximations
w_{n}(\sigma)
and
w_{n}(\tau)
of two automorphisms

provides a discrete measure of their difference in the combinatorial (and, by continuity, the profinite) topology.

Proof.

The identification
\{0,1\}^{n}
with the vertices of an
n
-hypercube is classical. By definition, two
n
-bit words differ in exactly one coordinate if and only if their Hamming distance is 1. Since the encoding
\Phi
is assumed to be a homeomorphism (or at least continuous with respect to the profinite topology on
\widehat{GT}
and the Cantor topology on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
), the finite projections

capture the local combinatorial behavior of
\tau
. In particular, if
d_{H}(w_{n}(\sigma),w_{n}(\tau))=1
, then
\sigma
and
\tau
differ by a minimal change in their binary approximations, which is reflective of a corresponding neighboring relationship in the profinite topology. This completes the proof.
∎

7.
The Cubic Matrioshka Algorithm for
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
and
\widehat{GT}

We establish a bijective construction between the set of elements in the absolute Galois group
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
over
{\bf Q}
and the set of infinite binary sequences
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
through a recursive, algorithmic approach. Beyond
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
and
\widehat{GT}
, one can adapt our construction for
p
-adic integers
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
(see
[
25
]
) and, more generally, for any profinite group which is homeomorphic to a Cantor set.

Remark 7.1
.

Note that the construction is purely topological and that the encoding need not respect algebraic operations. Furthermore, it is very important to highlight that the coding depends on how we define this homeomorphism.

7.1.
The Idea

•

In order to introduce our algorithmic construction, let us first take a countable basis of clopen sets
\{U_{s}\}_{s\in\{0,1\}^{n}}
(we recall that is
s
is a binary word) which shall be recursively partitioned into two clopen subsets.

•

Encoding/Decoding: We map en element
x\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
to a sequence
(a_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
by
a_{n+1}=0
if
x\in U_{s0}
else 1.

•

One recovers
x
via
\cap_{n}U_{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}}
, which converges by compactness.

This is a homeomorphism if the clopen partitions respect the Cantor space’s self-similar structure.

7.2.
The Cubic Matrioshka Algorithm

Step 1: Properties of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}

We recall the key topological properties of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
:

•

Compactness:
Every open cover of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
has a finite subcover.

•

Total Disconnectedness:
The only connected subsets of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
are singletons.

•

Polish:

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is separable and completely metrizable.

Due to its compactness and total disconnectedness,
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is homeomorphic to the Cantor space
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Step 2: Basis of Clopen Sets

Being a compact, totally disconnected, and metrizable space,
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
admits a countable basis of clopen sets. We refine this basis into a recursive binary tree structure of clopen partitions that we call a cubic Matrioshka:

•

The root
: The entire space
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
.

•

Level 1:
Partition
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
into two disjoint clopen subsets
U_{0},U_{1}
.

•

Recursive Step
: For each clopen set
U_{s}
at level
n
(labeled by a finite binary string
s\in\{0,1\}^{n}
), partition it into two clopen subsets
U_{s0},U_{s1}
.

Step 3: Mapping Elements to Binary Sequences

Given an automorphism
g\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
, we associate to
g
a binary sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
where:

a_{n}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }g\in U_{s0}\text{ at level }n,\\
1&\text{if }g\in U_{s1}\text{ at level }n,\end{cases}

and
s
represents the binary string of length
n-1
. This procedure defines a unique binary sequence for each element
g\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
.

Step 4: Inverse Mapping

For a given binary sequence
(a_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the corresponding element
g\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
by:

g=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}U_{a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}}.

By the compactness of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
, the intersection of these nested clopen sets contains exactly one point, which corresponds to a unique automorphism
g\in\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
.

Step 5: Bijection

The maps
g\mapsto(a_{n})
and
(a_{n})\mapsto g
are mutually inverse, thus establishing a bijection:

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}\leftrightarrow\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}.

Remark 7.2
.

For other groups like
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
(the
p
-adic integers), the clopen partitions encode
p
-adic expansions instead of binary, but the same logic applies with adjustments for the basis. See
[
25
]
, where one shows that
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\,\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny homeo}}}{\simeq}}\,\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and its clopen subgroups form a binary tree structure compatible with the encoding.

Our statement below allows to attribute to every element of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
a unique representation as an infinite binary sequence. It is important to note that the map is not canonical in the sense that the coding (and the decoding) of an element in
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
as a binary code depends on how we have defined the clopens, during the steps of the algorithm.

Proposition 7.1

(
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
as a Cantor Set)
.

Let
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
denote the absolute Galois group over rational numbers
{\bf Q}
. Then:

(1)

The topological space underlying
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
is homeomorphic to the Cantor set.

(2)

Consequently, every element of
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
admits a unique representation as an infinite binary sequence.

Proof.

This follows from the theorem Thm
6.1
and the construction outlined above.
∎

Theorem 7.1

(Subcube Structure of Automorphism Approximations)
.

Let

\Phi\colon\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{{\bf Q}}/{\bf Q})\longrightarrow\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

be a fixed homeomorphism identifying the absolute Galois group with the Cantor set, and for each
n\geq 1
, let

\pi_{n}\colon\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{0,1\}^{n}

denote the projection onto the first
n
coordinates. Suppose that for two automorphisms

\sigma,\tau\in\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{{\bf Q}}/{\bf Q})
the associated
n
-bit approximations

w(\sigma)=\pi_{n}(\Phi(\sigma))\quad\text{and}\quad w(\tau)=\pi_{n}(\Phi(\tau))

satisfy

w(\sigma)_{i}=w(\tau)_{i}=s_{i}\quad\text{for }i=1,2,\dots,p,

where

s=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{p}\in\{0,1\}^{p}

is a fixed binary word. Then both
w(\sigma)
and
w(\tau)
lie in an
(n-p)
-dimensional hypercube.

Proof.

By hypothesis, for each
i=1,\dots,p
we have

w(\sigma)_{i}=w(\tau)_{i}=s_{i}.

Thus, the
n
-bit sequences
w(\sigma)
and
w(\tau)
can be written as

w(\sigma)=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{p},t_{p+1},\dots,t_{n})

and

w(\tau)=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{p},u_{p+1},\dots,u_{n}),

where
t_{j},u_{j}\in\{0,1\}
for
j=p+1,\dots,n
. Hence, every such sequence with fixed initial segment
s
lies in the set

\{s_{1}\}\times\{s_{2}\}\times\cdots\times\{s_{p}\}\times\{0,1\}^{n-p},

which is naturally identified with the
(n-p)
-dimensional hypercube. This completes the proof.
∎

Therefore, we can define a certain distance between two families of automorphisms in
\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}
defined by a binary sequence of length
n
, via the Hamming distance on the hypercube.

The Cubic Matrioshka Algorithm: Applications to
\widehat{GT}

As previously, we present a canonical description of any element in the Grothendieck–Teichmüller group, denoted by
\widehat{GT}
, as an infinite binary sequence.
We refer to
[
11
,
19
,
26
]
for a detailed study of the profinite version of
\widehat{GT}
.
Our construction exploits the embedding

\widehat{GT}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2}),

where
\widehat{F}_{2}
is the profinite completion of the free group on two generators
F_{2}=\langle x,y\rangle
. By recursively partitioning
\widehat{GT}
into clopen subsets and encoding the corresponding data, we obtain a natural bijection between elements in
\widehat{GT}
and the elements of the Cantor space
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Our attempt to make our construction for
\widehat{GT}
as explicit as possible relies on the fact that it is a subgroup
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
and results concerning congruence subgroup properties of
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
in
[
3
,
10
]
.

Step 1. A Hierarchy of Congruence Subgroups

For each
k\geq 1
, let
N_{k}
denote the
k
th congruence subgroup of
\widehat{F}_{2}
, defined as the kernel of the natural projection

\widehat{F}_{2}\to\widehat{F}_{2}/\Gamma_{k},

where
\Gamma_{k}
is a descending chain of finite-index subgroups of
F_{2}
that induce finite quotients in
\widehat{F}_{2}
. Each
N_{k}
is a normal subgroup, and the quotient
\widehat{F}_{2}/N_{k}
is a finite group.

Step 2. Partitioning
\widehat{GT}
into Clopen Sets

At level
k
, partition
\widehat{GT}
into clopen sets. For each
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2}/N_{k})
, define

U_{k,\sigma}=\{\,\tau\in\widehat{GT}\,;\,\tau\equiv\sigma\ (\mathrm{mod}\,N_{k})\,\}.

The sets
U_{k,\sigma}
are clopen in
\widehat{GT}
and form a basis for its profinite topology.
The encoding
\Phi(\tau)
uniquely identifies
\tau
by its action at each level.

Step 3: Multi-Binary Encoding

\bullet

Determine the Number of Automorphisms

At each level
k
, compute the order of the automorphism group:

m_{k}=|{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2}/N_{k})|.

\bullet

Calculate Required Bits

Define the number of bits needed to encode elements of
{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2}/N_{k})
:

b_{k}=\lceil\log_{2}m_{k}\rceil

\bullet

Define Coherent Bijections
: for each
k
, fix a bijection:

\varphi_{k}:{\rm Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2}/N_{k})\to\{0,1\}^{b_{k}}

ensuring that for all
\tau\in\widehat{GT}
, the projection satisfies:

trunc_{b_{k-1}}\bigg(\varphi_{k}(\tau\mod N_{k})\bigg)=\varphi_{k-1}(\tau\mod N_{k-1}),

where
trunc_{b_{k-1}}
truncates the binary string to the first
b_{k-1}
bits.

\bullet

Encode Elements of
\widehat{GT}
.

For
\tau\in GT
, define its multi-bit coordinate at level
k

\Phi_{k}(\tau)=\varphi_{k}(\tau\mod N_{k})\in\{0,1\}^{b_{k}}.

\bullet

Adjust for Cantor Space Homeomorphism

If
b_{k}
is bounded, pad each
\Phi_{k}(\tau)
with zeros to a fixed length
\max_{k}b_{k}
.

If
b_{k}
is unbounded, use variable-length blocks and metrize

\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\{0,1\}^{b_{k}}

as a Cantor-compatible space (e.g., via dyadic intervals).

The full encoding is the concatenation:

\Phi(\tau)=(\Phi_{1}(\tau),\Phi_{2}(\tau),\dots)\in\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\{0,1\}^{b_{k}}.

Thus, the assignment

\Phi(\tau)=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\dots)\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

yields a well-defined bijection between
\widehat{GT}
and the Cantor set.

By exploiting the profinite topology of
\widehat{GT}
and its embedding into
\operatorname{Aut}(\widehat{F}_{2})
, we have established a rigorous recursive partitioning of
\widehat{GT}
into clopen sets. Thus, every element of
\widehat{GT}
may be uniquely encoded as an infinite binary sequence.

Theorem 8.1

(Profinite Grothendieck–Teichmüller Group as a Cantor Set)
.

The profinite Grothendieck–Teichmüller group
\widehat{GT}
is a universal profinite symmetry group governing

\operatorname{Gal}_{{\bf Q}}

\widehat{GT}\quad\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny homeo}}}{\simeq}}\quad\mathcal{C},

.

It is important to note that the map

\Phi\colon\widehat{GT}\longrightarrow\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

is non-canonical, which means that
different choices of
\Phi
yield different binary representations of the same element

Proof.

This statement follows from the construction depicted above and Thm.
6.1

∎

The explicit computation of the bijection, however, remains a challenging open problem, inviting further advances in the interplay between profinite group theory and deformation theory.

Remark 8.2

In the scope of an explicit construction of

N_{1}\supset N_{2}\supset\cdots\supset N_{k}\supset\cdots

We refer to
[
