Energy spectrum-based correction of washout rates in dual-isotope 123I-BMIPP/201Tl imaging
Authors: Kenichi Nakajima, Shotoku Makida, Masato Okamura, Masa-aki Kawashiri, Takayuki Shibutani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial metabolism and perfusion in patients with ischemic heart disease or cardiomyopathy can be simultaneously assessed using 123I-β-methyl-iodophenyl-pentadecanoic acid (BMIPP) and 201Thallium (Tl) dual-isotope images. However, cross-contaminated counts between these tracers complicate quantitative analyses, particularly of 123I-BMIPP washout rates (WRs). We developed a simplified method to determine WRs corrected for energy spectral crosstalk from dual-isotope images.MethodsOur correction method was based on fixed energy-spectrum spill-in ratios (iRatio for 123I and tRatio for 201Tl) that were empirically determined from phantom and patient datasets. These ratios were applied to separate 123I and 201Tl counts in 38 studies of 36 patients with ischemic heart disease using dual-energy planar and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) images. We calculated WRs before and after correction using early and late anterior planar images, as well as mean tomographic counts derived from polar maps. Thresholds of 5% (planar) and 10% (SPECT) were used to assess classification agreement between corrected and uncorrected WRs.ResultsThe range of iRatios was 0.57–0.73 and that of tRatios was 0.10–0.12. Higher iRatio values were associated with greater body-weight patients, whereas tRatios were relatively stable regardless of body weight. The corrected 123I-BMIPP WR depends primarily on tRatio and, by formulation, is not directly affected by iRatio. Using a fixed tRatio of 0.12, the correction method revealed strong correlations between corrected and uncorrected WRs for planar imaging (R² = 0.969, p < 0.0001) and SPECT (R² = 0.964, p < 0.0001). Corrected 123I-BMIPP WRs were comparable to uncorrected values for both planar imaging (mean difference, − 1.4%) and SPECT (mean difference, 0.8%). Among the 38 studies, classification of tentative normal versus abnormal WRs remained concordant in 35 (92%) planar studies and 36 (95%) SPECT studies.ConclusionOur simplified energy spectrum–based method demonstrated that correction of 123I-BMIPP WRs is feasible under dual-isotope conditions. Although uncorrected WRs may provide approximate estimates, correction is important for reliable quantitative use of dual-isotope datasets, where spectral cross-talk limits accuracy. Introduction

Dual-isotope SPECT with 123I-β-methyl-iodophenyl pentadecanoic acid (123I-BMIPP) and 201Tl-chloride (201Tl) [1–3] has been used to simultaneously evaluate myocardial fatty acid metabolism and perfusion in patients with coronary artery disease and heart failure. Previous visual assessments of matched and mismatched uptake patterns between these tracers have provided critical insights into myocardial viability, especially during the early phase after acute ischemic events. Early mismatches between preserved perfusion (201Tl) and reduced fatty acid uptake (123I-BMIPP) are associated with a viable but ischemic myocardium and may predict functional recovery [4, 5]. Despite the clinical value of dual-tracer imaging for viability assessment, most applications remain qualitative, with limited consideration of quantitative parameters such as 123I-BMIPP washout rates (WRs).

Accurate quantitation of myocardial dual-isotope radiotracer clearance, typically expressed as WRs between early and late images, is important for diagnostic assessment and for understanding myocardial pathophysiology [6, 7]. However, the physical characteristics of these radionuclides complicate quantitative analysis. Whereas 123I emits a principal gamma photon at 159 keV, 201Tl emits characteristic mercury X-rays around 70 keV and additional gamma photons at 167 keV. The 167 keV component of 201Tl partially overlaps the 123I 159 keV photopeak, particularly when energy windows such as 15%–30% are relatively wide. Despite this crosstalk, practical and standardized correction strategies applicable to routine clinical imaging remain limited.

Washout rates of 123I-BMIPP images are key quantitative parameters for diagnosing triglyceride deposit cardiomyovasculopathy (TGCV), for which a WR threshold of 10% is a common diagnostic criterion [8, 9]. Although WRs can be calculated in various ways [10–12], the specific impact of dual-isotope acquisition on WR accuracy has not been systematically evaluated. This limitation is particularly relevant in routine dual-tracer imaging, where spectral overlap between 123I and 201Tl can bias measured WRs.

The primary aims of this study were to evaluate the feasibility of determining 123I-BMIPP WRs under dual-isotope acquisition conditions, to develop a simple energy spectrum–based method for separating tracer signals, and to quantify the bias introduced by dual-isotope imaging. We further propose a practical correction approach to estimate WRs for 123I-BMIPP and to explore its applicability to 201Tl using planar and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) images.

Methods

Data acquisition

Syringes containing 123I-BMIPP and 201Tl solutions (111 MBq each) were placed in a cylindrical acrylic phantom (diameter, 13 cm) designed to simulate the human neck and an upper insert to represent the thyroid gland. Energy spectra were acquired using a dual-head gamma camera (NM830, GE Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with extended low-energy general-purpose (ELEGP) collimators. Energy spectra of 123I, 201Tl, and dual-tracer (123I + 201Tl) were acquired for 3 min. These datasets were used to characterize energy-spectrum crosstalk and to establish spill-in ratios for correcting dual-isotope images.

Patients

We acquired 201Tl and 123I-BMIPP images from 36 consecutive patients (38 studies; mean age, 69 ± 10 y; male, n = 22) with ischemic heart disease. Baseline diagnoses included acute myocardial infarction after percutaneous coronary intervention (n = 12; 14 studies), old myocardial infarction (n = 7), vasospastic angina (n = 2), myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (n = 2), cardiomyopathy (n = 2), heart failure (n = 1), and atypical or typical chest discomfort (n = 10). All data were anonymized before analysis, and identifiable patient information was neither accessed nor stored. The Ethics Committee at Kaga Medical Center approved this technical evaluation of dual-tracer correction using anonymized clinical data (Approval No.: study 2025 [R7]-7). The need for patient consent was waived due to the retrospective study design.

Planar and SPECT imaging

Planar and SPECT images were acquired from all patients at 20 min (early) and at 3 h (late) after intravenous injections of 201Tl and 123I-BMIPP (111 MBq each). Anterior planar images were acquired using a 128 × 128 matrix for 5 min per frame under a dual-energy acquisition protocol. Energy windows were centered at 71 keV (30%) for 201Tl and 159 keV (20%) for 123I. Subsequent SPECT images were acquired over a 180° arc. Projection data were acquired using a 64 × 64 matrix, and tomographic images were reconstructed using filtered back projection (FBP) with a Butterworth pre-filter (cutoff 0.45 cycles/cm, order 10) and ordered-subset expectation maximization (OSEM; 2 iterations, 10 subsets, Butterworth post-filter) algorithms. Energy spectra were extracted from quality control (QC) XML files of a subset of seven patients, at early and late time points, to confirm spectral consistency in vivo.

Creation and calculation of separate and combined energy spectra

Separate 123I and 201Tl energy spectra were acquired to simulate combined dual-radionuclide conditions. The relative intensities were adjusted to match approximate peak counts at 71 keV (201Tl) and 159 keV (123I) to mimic clinical acquisition conditions (Fig. 2A). The main energy windows were defined as ± 10% (20% total width) and ± 15% (30% total width) for 123I and 201Tl, centered at 159 and 71 keV, respectively, consistent with standard clinical protocols.

Calculation of counts in clinical study

Myocardial counts from anterior images were measured for planar analysis. A circular region of interest (ROI) was placed over the cardiac region on 201Tl images acquired in the 71 keV window. The same ROI was then copied to the corresponding 123I-BMIPP image acquired in the 159 keV window to ensure spatial consistency. Results

Energy spectra of 123I and 201Tl in phantom and patient studies

Energy spectra were acquired under single-tracer conditions in phantom experiments and clinical patient studies to characterize spectral overlap between 123I and 201Tl. Distinct photopeaks for 123I (159 keV) and 201Tl (≈ 71 keV) were observed in phantom spectra, confirming appropriate energy window settings. Based on the phantom spectra, the spill-in iRatio and tRatio were 0.33 and 0.10, respectively (Fig. 1B).

The overall spectral profiles remained consistent at early and late time points in clinical data derived from three patients (six studies). Shoulder counts slightly increased in patient spectra compared with the phantom (Fig. 1C), which likely reflected scatter and Compton contributions. Nonetheless, peak positions and shapes remained stable among all six studies (Fig. 1D). These findings supported the reproducibility of energy distribution in vivo.

Scatter fractions were higher in energy spectra obtained under dual-nuclide conditions for 123I and 201Tl in patients weighing 43, 63, 68, 73, and 81 kg (Fig. 2) than in phantom spectra. The scatter contribution of 201Tl in the intermediate energy range between the 201Tl and 123I peaks increased with body weight, resulting in a weight-dependent increase in spill-in from 123I into the 201Tl window. The iRatio range was accordingly 0.57–0.73. In contrast, spill-in from 201Tl into the 123I window was relatively stable, with a tRatio of 0.10–0.12. Figure 2D shows that tRatio remained approximately constant (~ 0.12), whereas iRatio increased with body weight. These empirically determined ratios, derived from clinical data, were applied in all subsequent analyses to separate tracer counts and calculate corrected WRs in planar and SPECT images under dual-isotope conditions.

Threshold-based discrepancy analysis

We assessed the potential clinical impact of spectral cross-talk on WR classification using the threshold criteria of 5% (planar) and 10% (SPECT) to classify 123I-BMIPP washout as normal or abnormal [10]. Classification agreement was evaluated before and after spill-in correction.

Corrected and uncorrected planar WR values resulted in discordant classification in only 3 (8%) of 38 studies. All discrepancies occurred near the 5% cutoff, indicating that crosstalk correction may influence interpretation primarily in borderline cases. A comparison of corrected planar and SPECT WRs based on their respective thresholds revealed discordant classification in 3 (8%) of 38 studies, again clustered near the threshold values.

Discussion

We developed a practical reproducible method for correcting spectral cross-talk between 123I and 201Tl in dual-isotope imaging. Fixed spill-in ratios derived from energy spectra (iRatio for spill-in of 123I into the 201Tl window and tRatio for spill-in of 201Tl into the 123I window) allow estimation of tracer-specific counts and corrected WRs that could be calculated without requiring additional hardware or repeated patient-specific energy spectral acquisition. This approach is particularly relevant in clinical settings where accurate 123I-BMIPP WR quantitation is required, such as TGCV evaluations and myocardial metabolic impairment assessments after ischemia. The simplicity and robustness of our proposed method support wider clinical implementation of dual-tracer protocols.

Combination of 123I-labeled radiopharmaceuticals and 201Tl

Dual-isotope imaging with 123I-labeled radiopharmaceuticals and 201Tl has been widely applied in Japan to evaluate ischemic heart disease, as 123I-BMIPP determines myocardial fatty acid metabolism and 201Tl defines cardiac perfusion. The high sensitivity of 123I-BMIPP allows the detection of metabolic abnormalities in acute coronary syndrome and spasm-related ischemia [13, 14]. During the acute phase particularly after successful coronary intervention, mismatch patterns characterized by preserved 201Tl uptake and reduced 123I-BMIPP uptake indicate viable, but stunned myocardium, and may predict subsequent functional recovery. Similarly, BMIPP defects in vasospastic angina often correspond to vasospastic territories and normalize together with recovered left ventricular contractility after appropriate therapy [15, 16].

The Japanese Circulation Society (JCS) guidelines support the application of 123I-BMIPP SPECT with Class I or IIa indications for diagnosing myocardial ischemia, evaluating vasospastic angina, and assessing myocardial damage in patients undergoing hemodialysis [17]. Although separate acquisitions for metabolism and perfusion are feasible, simultaneous dual-isotope imaging with 123I-BMIPP and 201Tl remains clinically attractive because it enables efficient assessment of mismatch patterns in time-sensitive settings.

Subtraction-based approaches and multi-window correction methods have been applied to isolate tracer activity to address photon energy overlap between 123I and 201Tl. While these methods support visual interpretation, their impact on quantitative indices such as WRs has not been systematically validated. We addressed this gap by quantifying the influence of spectral crosstalk and correction on WR measurements in phantom and clinical datasets.

Dual-isotope imaging has also been applied to combinations of 123I-meta-iodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) and 201Tl, enabling assessment of perfusion–innervation mismatch. This mismatch has clinical value for risk stratification in heart failure, particularly for predicting sudden cardiac death and ventricular arrhythmias [18, 19]. Therefore, the proposed correction framework may be applicable to other 123I/201Tl dual-isotope protocols such as 123I-MIBG studies, although radionuclide- and system-specific determination of spill-in ratios would be required.

Late 123I-BMIPP images and washout rates

Late-phase 123I-BMIPP images are typically acquired 3 h after a radiolabeled tracer is injected into a patient. This approach was initially explored during early clinical investigations in the 1990s but was not widely adopted into routine practice. This was largely due to the predominant clinical emphasis on detecting mismatch patterns between fatty acid metabolism (123I-BMIPP) and perfusion [1, 20, 21], rather than focusing on quantitative indices such as WRs. Consequently, clinical use of late BMIPP imaging gradually declined in most institutions.

However, interest in WR quantitation has been revitalized with the recognition of TGCV as a distinct clinical entity [9, 22–24]. A viable myocardium rapidly takes up and incorporates 123I-BMIPP into the intracellular triglyceride pool, where it is subsequently cleared over time. Therefore, WRs provide a quantitative measure of the dynamic status of myocardial lipid metabolism.

Proposed correction method using energy-spectrum analysis

Our proposed approach assumes that spill-in ratios between radionuclides remain sufficiently stable under clinical imaging conditions to allow mathematical decomposition of measured counts using fixed coefficients. This assumption is supported by the finding that overall energy-spectral patterns are reproducible in vivo. In this study, the tRatio (spill-in of 201Tl into the 123I window) remained relatively stable among patients, whereas iRatio (spill-in of 123I into the 201Tl window) varied with body habitus. This variability is likely attributable to differences in scatter conditions, which are influenced not only by body weight but also by stature and attenuation geometry, as shown by 201Tl spectral variation (Fig. 2B). Although alternative contamination indices such as CT, wI/CI, wI or CI, wT/CT, wT ratios could also be defined, they were not adopted because they depend on relative administered activities and myocardial 201Tl and 123I-BMIPP uptake, which may substantially vary among patients. In contrast, the spill-in ratios derived from energy spectra in our model were less sensitive to inter-patient variability in tracer uptake.

Spill-in ratios were derived from clinical energy spectra rather than phantom measurements. The apparent 201Tl peak detected around 71 keV is a composite, rather than a single discrete photon energy, reflecting characteristic mercury X-rays (Kα = 70.8 keV, 73.7%; Kβ = 80.3 keV, 20.4%) as well as additional characteristic X-rays generated by interactions with the lead collimator (Pb Kα ≈ 75 keV; Pb Kβ ≈ 85 keV). Therefore, peak positions and spectral shapes may differ slightly between phantom and patient conditions. However, because spill-in ratios were consistently derived from clinical data, these variations are considered unlikely to significantly affect correction performance.

Optimal iRatios and tRatios might differ among imaging systems and protocols, including detector type (Anger vs. cadmium-zinc-telluride [CZT]), collimator design, energy window settings, and reconstruction parameters. Although agreement between FBP and OSEM reconstructions was excellent, newer CZT cameras with improved energy resolution might further reduce spectral overlap compared with conventional Anger systems, and may improve quantitative accuracy in dual-isotope imaging, including protocol combinations of 123I-BMIPP with perfusion tracers [25, 26].

The purpose of this study was not to define universal spill-in ratios, but rather to provide a systematic and clinically practical correction framework. The method can be locally optimized at any institution using simple phantom measurements and a few representative patient spectra. This spill-in ratio–based approach allows dual-isotope protocols to retain both qualitative and quantitative utility, including more accurate WR calculation, even in the presence of overlapping radionuclide energy spectra.

Corrected versus uncorrected washout rates

This study showed that 123I-BMIPP WRs corrected for spectral crosstalk in dual-isotope imaging strongly and consistently correlated with uncorrected values. Corrected WRs derived from planar images were slightly lower on average than apparent WRs, while maintaining a high correlation, supporting the internal consistency of the correction approach. A similarly strong correlation was observed for SPECT-derived WRs. Thus, uncorrected WRs may provide reasonable estimates in many clinical situations. However, in cases near diagnostic thresholds (e.g., TGCV vs. normal), even small differences may influence classification. Therefore, the correction method is valuable for improving diagnostic accuracy and reducing the risk of misclassification in borderline cases. In contrast, 201Tl WRs were not sufficiently stable when using simple fixed spill-in ratios and were therefore not further evaluated for quantitative WR analyses. Moreover, variability in iRatio could not be sufficiently compensated by simple body weight–based adjustment.

In clinical settings such as acute coronary syndrome, angina pectoris, and myocardial infarction, WRs near 0% might suggest significantly impaired myocardial fatty acid clearance and possible TGCV. By reducing bias due to spectral overlap, our proposed correction method improves confidence in WR interpretation and enhances the reproducibility of quantitative assessment using dual-isotope 123I-BMIPP/201Tl protocols.

Washout rates in TGCV

Defective intracellular lipolysis in TGCV results in low 123I-BMIPP clearance (nearly zero), indicating severely impaired fatty acid utilization and metabolic trapping [11, 23]. A 123I-BMIPP WR of < 10% has been proposed as part of the diagnostic criteria for TGCV when supported by other characteristic findings such as myocardial triglyceride accumulation on computed tomography or magnetic resonance images, along with angiographic evidence of diffuse concentric coronary narrowing [9, 24, 27]. These elements form the basis of the TGCV diagnostic algorithm established through Japanese clinical research.

The Japanese Society of Nuclear Cardiology (JSNC) and the Japanese Society of Nuclear Medicine (JSNM) have recommended single-isotope acquisition for 123I-BMIPP WR analysis to support accurate WR measurements [11]. Although dual-isotope imaging is clinically attractive for simultaneous metabolism and perfusion assessment, it has not been recommended for WR quantitation because spectral cross-talk between 123I and 201Tl can introduce substantial bias in tracer separation and WR calculations.

Nevertheless, some institutions have applied dual-isotope 123I-BMIPP and 201Tl protocols during the early phase of TGCV research, before correction methods were standardized. The present findings provide a reproducible mathematical framework for separating 123I and 201Tl counts using empirically derived energy spectra, which allows estimation of 123I-BMIPP WRs under dual-isotope conditions. This approach may improve the interpretability of archived dual-isotope datasets and also support quantitative analysis in settings where dual-isotope acquisition is used for clinical efficiency.

To assess potential clinical impact, we applied previously proposed cutoff values for abnormal WR (5% for planar imaging and 10% for SPECT) [10]. Classification discrepancies between corrected and uncorrected WR values were uncommon when the 10% threshold was used. However, misclassification may occur primarily near decision thresholds, indicating that correction may influence interpretation mainly in borderline cases. Moreover, when dual-nuclide studies are performed in patients with coronary artery disease or heart failure, simultaneous calculation of corrected WRs may obviate the need for an additional 123I-BMIPP study. The proposed method also provides a practical means to re-evaluate previously acquired datasets, thereby preserving their clinical and research utility, particularly in the context of TGCV.

Finally, although several studies in this cohort had 123I-BMIPP WRs < 10%, TGCV cannot be inferred from WR alone. Diagnostic confirmation requires multimodal imaging and clinical correlation. Because clinical experience with 123I-BMIPP WR remains limited, further evaluation across diverse clinical and pathophysiological conditions is warranted.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This study, conducted at a single-center, obtained all energy spectra, spill-in ratios, and validation analyses using a single gamma camera system with fixed acquisition and reconstruction protocols. Therefore, generalizability to other institutions, camera models, and imaging conditions remains to be established.

Our proposed correction for 123I-BMIPP WR relies primarily on tRatio in both planar and SPECT imaging. Because SPECT reconstruction introduces depth-dependent attenuation and scatter effects, spill-in might vary spatially within the myocardium. Although agreement between FBP and OSEM reconstructions was excellent, other reconstruction approaches (e.g., resolution recovery, attenuation correction, or more sophisticated scatter correction models) might differently influence spill-in.

In addition, 201Tl WR could not be reliably quantified using fixed iRatio values, and iRatio variability related to body habitus was not evaluated in patients weighing > 81 kg. Although iRatio was associated with body weight, scatter within the body is influenced by multiple factors beyond body weight alone, which may contribute to its instability. This may explain why body weight–based adjustment did not sufficiently improve the reliability of 201Tl WR. In contrast, tRatio (spill-in from 201Tl into the 123I window) was relatively stable and can be used for calculating 123I-BMIPP WRs.

For a more rigorous comparison, data from both single-isotope and dual-isotope studies would be desirable. However, in clinical practice, it was not feasible to perform repeated acquisitions using both protocols in the same patients because of ethical and logistical constraints, including additional radiation exposure.

Although patient spectra were generally consistent in our cohort, spectral characteristics may differ among institutions depending on detector type (Anger vs. CZT), collimator design, energy resolution, and energy window settings. These factors might affect the applicability of fixed spill-in ratios when implemented under various clinical conditions.

Finally, while the present study demonstrates the feasibility of a practical correction framework, further investigation is required to validate and optimize the approach across diverse imaging systems and patient populations. Multicenter studies are needed to establish system-specific correction factors and to develop practical recommendations for routine clinical implementation of dual-isotope protocols.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that correcting spectral crosstalk between 123I and 201Tl in dual-isotope imaging using fixed, energy spectrum–derived spill-in ratios is feasible and robust. The proposed method enables practical WR quantitation under dual-tracer acquisition conditions without requiring patient-specific spectral measurements. This approach supports reliable quantitative assessment, particularly in threshold-based interpretation, and may facilitate broader clinical use of dual-isotope protocols for myocardial ischemia and TGCV.
