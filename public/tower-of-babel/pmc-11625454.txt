Terminal Care Performance of Nurses Caring for COVID‐19 Patients: A Cross‐Sectional Descriptive Study
Authors: Juyeon Oh, Dong‐Hee Kim, Yujin Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo identify the effects of moral distress and terminal care stress on the terminal care performance of nurses who cared for COVID‐19 patients.DesignThis study was cross‐sectional descriptive research.MethodsUsing a convenience sampling method from June to October 2022, nurses who provided terminal care for COVID‐19 patients in a Korean tertiary hospital within the last 6 months from the time of the study were selected as participants. Socio‐demographic and work characteristics of the participants were collected, and COVID‐19 moral distress, terminal care stress and terminal care performance were assessed. A total of 140 self‐report questionnaires were distributed and returned (response rate: 100%).ResultsIn the terminal care performance, it was found that nurses performed relatively well in the physical area, but less well in the emotional and spiritual areas. Moral distress and terminal care stress were both positively correlated with terminal care performance for nurses who cared for COVID‐19 patients. The predictors of terminal care performance were moral distress (β = 0.27, p = 0.003), gender (β = 0.20, p = 0.015) and experience in a dedicated COVID‐19 unit (β = 0.22, p = 0.020).ConclusionsThe study identified moral distress as the most significant factor influencing terminal care performance among nurses caring for COVID‐19 patients. To enhance terminal care performance, it is crucial to address moral distress and provide clear guidelines and support, especially in the emotional and spiritual care domains. Future research should explore additional factors that may influence terminal care performance in pandemic situations.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.

Introduction

The first Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) case was reported in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China, in December 2019, and in South Korea, the first COVID‐19 case was reported in January 2020. For almost 3 years, COVID‐19 has continued to mutate, resulting in a staggering global toll of 771,820,937 cumulative cases and 6,978,175 cumulative deaths as of 8 November 2023. In South Korea alone, there have been 34,571,873 cases and 35,934 deaths, emphasising the urgency of the ongoing fight against the virus (World Health Organization 2023). Since patients with COVID‐19 were isolated in hospitals according to infection prevention guidelines (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency 2022), nurses were the only individuals who provided terminal care for patients with COVID‐19.

While families of COVID‐19 patients wished to be with the patient at end of life, they could only watch their loved one dying via video calls due to the COVID‐19 guidelines that did not allow entry by outsiders who are not part of the medical staff, which caused severe stress (Galazzi et al. 2023). Some families were not even able to watch their loved one dying through video calls if the hospital was not equipped with a system to enable such calls or if the caregiver was at an age at which these were not easily accessible (Galazzi et al. 2021). It is recommended that the body of the deceased COVID‐19 patient be placed in a single‐layer body bag after postmortem care and that double‐layer body bags be used in cases of excessive fluid exposure, transportation of the body over a long distance or for handling large numbers of bodies (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency 2022). Facing situations where human dignity is threatened, nurses experience heartbreak while providing terminal care (Hughes and Rushton 2022). In situations where terminal care performance is limited by the systems put in place to prevent the spread of infection, it is important to carefully examine the terminal care performance of nurses who care for COVID‐19 patients.

Backgrounds

Terminal care performance is a holistic care process that involves providing physical, emotional, social and spiritual support to patients and their families at the end of the patient's life. The goal is to help patients achieve a peaceful and dignified passing while maintaining a high quality of life during their remaining time (Noh et al. 1995). However, during COVID‐19, providing continuous care while wearing protective clothing is impossible, resulting in reduced patient contact time (Kong 2021). In addition, because caregivers' access to the COVID‐19 ward was restricted, nurses were required to complete the tasks that would normally be completed by the caregivers (Yeo 2022). Tasks such as inspecting patient items in the isolation room and managing machine malfunctions were therefore added to nurses' other duties, resulting in additional burdens on the nursing staff. (Oh and Lee 2021). Terminal care performance could be limited due to the need to comply with COVID‐19 guidelines and the burden on nursing staff.

Specifically, the various measures for preventing the spread of the COVID‐19 infection increase nurses' moral distress, including having to inform a patient's near‐death status over the phone and watching a dying patient's loneliness in the absence of family members because of restrictions in visitation (Romero‐García et al. 2022). Moral distress refers to an uncomfortable and distressing psychological reaction experienced by nurses when they know what the ethically correct decision is in patient care but cannot execute it due to various constraints (Jameton 1984). Not being able to provide care because of COVID‐19 while watching patients in pain and dying places a psychological burden on nurses (Lesley 2021). Therefore, as various unethical situations arise to with the aim of preventing the spread of COVID‐19, the relationship between moral distress and terminal care performance must be examined in depth.

Nurses who care for terminal patients experience greater levels of stress compared to those who care for general patients. This is due to making significant terminal decisions and addressing patients' and their families' complex palliative needs (Parola et al. 2018). Caring for dying patients causes work overload for nurses, and even when administrative procedures are delayed, nurses experience high terminal care stress (Woo, Kim, and Kim 2013). In addition, as workload increases, terminal care stress increases (Son and Park 2021). In COVID‐19, nurses can experience increased stress as they must wear personal protective equipment (PPE) to provide terminal care (Holland et al. 2021). Nurses' workload increases as COVID‐19 prevalence and the number of dying patients increase (Chung, Seong, and Park 2022). As delays are expected even after death as a result of complex funeral guidelines (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency 2022), it is necessary to measure the level of terminal care stress among nurses who cared for patients who died as a result of COVID‐19 infection.

New infectious diseases emerge every few years, such as severe acute respiratory syndrome (hereinafter ‘SARS’) in 2003, influenza A (hereinafter ‘H1N1’) in 2009, Middle East respiratory syndrome (hereinafter ‘MERS’) in 2015, and COVID‐19 in 2019. However, there is a lack of research on terminal care performance for patients with SARS, H1N1 or MERS. Research on the terminal care for individuals with COVID‐19 is limited to qualitative studies (Hanna et al. 2021) or investigations into the availability of caregivers during the terminal process (Strang et al. 2020), with a limited focus on the performance of nurses in providing terminal care.

Nursing college education on terminal care in Korea is not mandatory; therefore, instead of providing independent terminal care courses, death‐related education is included in adult or geriatric nursing (Kim 2021). During clinical practice, between 45.1% and 58.6% of nursing students experienced the death of a patient (Jang and Kim 2019; Yun, Moon, and Park 2023). However, due to concerns about the spread of the COVID‐19 infectious disease, nursing students' clinical practice is limited and they become new nurses without any experience (Jeong and Han 2023). Due to the nature of Korea's nursing curriculum, there may be further limitations in performing terminal care, and nurses may face significant difficulties in performing terminal care if new infectious diseases arise, so understanding these limitations is necessary.

Furthermore, this study aims to identify the difficulties nurses face in performing terminal care during an infectious disease epidemic and identify the related factors that can be used as the basis for educational programmes to help nursing students and new nurses effectively perform terminal care. Against this backdrop, this study aims to identify the effects of moral distress and terminal care stress on the terminal care performance of nurses who cared for COVID‐19 patients and to provide a basis for future research on the terminal care performance of patients affected by emerging infectious diseases.

The research questions of this study are as follows: What are the levels of moral distress, terminal care stress and terminal care performance among nurses who cared for terminal COVID‐19 patients?What is the association between socio‐demographic (e.g., gender, age, religion, marital status and educational level) and work characteristics (e.g., position, nursing department, years of nursing experience and experience in a COVID‐19 unit), moral distress, terminal care stress and terminal care performance of nurses who cared for COVID‐19 patients?

Methods

Study Design

This study is cross‐sectional descriptive research.

Study Participants and Methods

This study used convenience sampling to select nurses who provided terminal care to COVID‐19 patients at tertiary care hospitals in South Korea. Participants were recruited by posting recruitment notices in relevant four Korean tertiary hospitals and nurse communities. The inclusion criteria were as follows: nurses who provided terminal care for COVID‐19 patients in a Korean tertiary hospital within the last 6 months and who understood the purpose of the study and voluntarily agreed to participate. Participants who did not work in a Korean tertiary hospital or did not provide terminal care for COVID‐19 patients within the last 6 months were excluded. The G‐power 3.1.9.7 was used to calculate the appropriate number of participants (Faul et al. 2009). Based on previous research (Lee and Ahn 2019), with a moderate effect size of 0.15, significance level (α) of 0.05 and power of 0.80, with 11 predictor variables, a sample size of 123 was developed. Considering a dropout rate of approximately 10%, 140 participants were selected.

Data Collection

Data were collected from June 2 to October 23, 2022. There are a total of 45 tertiary general hospitals in Korea. The researchers called all tertiary general hospitals, explained the study and sent the research proposal and approval request to the hospital if they asked for it. Four tertiary general hospitals approved the research. Recruitment notices were posted in groupware and the bulletin boards of the wards. A recruitment notice was also posted on a nurse community site. Participants were allowed to participate in the study only if they read the posted recruitment notice, understood the purpose and method of the study through the URL link provided, and gave their consent to participate.

Ethical Considerations

We obtained approval from the REDACTED. After submitting the research protocol and request for approval to the nursing department of a Korean tertiary hospital and obtaining consent, recruitment notices were posted on groupware, the nursing department and ward bulletin boards of four tertiary hospitals that wished to participate in the study. Additionally, a recruitment notice was posted on a nurse community site. Participants who expressed interest in participating were directed to a Google Forms survey URL link, where they were informed about the study's purpose, assurance of anonymity and the ability to withdraw from the study at any time. They were asked to provide their consent to participate in the study.

Measurement

Socio‐Demographic and Work Characteristics

Based on previous studies, variables found to be related to the dependent variable were used, including gender, age, religion, marital status, educational level, position, nursing department, years of nursing experience and experience in a COVID‐19 unit (Kim, Nam, and Kwon 2017; Lee and Ahn 2019).

Moral Distress

The COVID‐19 Moral distress scale (COVID‐MDS), developed by Lake et al. (2022) to measure moral distress, was used in this study. The translation and back‐translation of the questionnaire were carried out with permission from the original authors. COVID‐MDS consists of nine questions that reflect traditional moral distress situations faced by nurses, such as ‘Attempting to deliver a high standard of care with limited time, supplies and resources’, and four questions that reflect the COVID‐19 situation, such as ‘Caring for patients who must experience hospitalisation without family presence’. Each item enquires about the frequency of the participants' experience and the level of distress they felt. The frequency of experience was measured on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 for ‘Never’ to 3 for ‘Frequently’, and the severity of distress was measured on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 for ‘None’ to 3 for ‘Severe’. The two values were multiplied to produce an itemised moral distress scale ranging from 0 to 9. Higher scores indicate higher moral distress. The Cronbach's α was 0.88 in Lake et al.'s (2022) study and 0.80 in this study, indicating good reliability.

Terminal Care Stress

Terminal care stress was measured using the terminal care stress tool developed by Lee et al. (2007) for Korean nurses. This tool presents various situations that may cause stress during terminal care, and each nurse evaluates the level of stress experienced in these situations during terminal care. The responses are rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘Not at all’) to 5 (‘Very much’), with higher scores indicating higher levels of stress. The tool consists of 40 items across seven subdomains. The subdomains and sample items are as follows: (a) Negative attitudes towards death by patients and their families (e.g., when a terminal patient cannot accept death), (b) Difficulty allocating time to terminal patients (e.g., when required to care for another patient before mourning a death), (c) Burden of end‐of‐life care (e.g., witnessing the dying process of a terminal patient), (d) Work overload (e.g., when end‐of‐life care leads to excessive workload), (e) interpersonal conflicts with terminal patients (e.g., when the patient's circumstances are particularly heartbreaking), (f) Lack of professional knowledge and skills (e.g., difficulty providing systematic care for terminal patients), (g) Conflict over medical limitations (e.g., when there is little that can be done for a terminal patient). The Cronbach's α for this tool was 0.93 at the time of development and 0.95 in this study. The tool was used with the developer's permission.

Terminal Care Performance

Terminal care performance was measured using the terminal care performance instrument developed by Park and Choi (1996) for Korean nurses. This tool measures the terminal care nurses provide to patients and consists of 22 questions and three subfactors: eight questions in the physical domain, such as ‘I changed positions regularly if possible’, eight questions in the emotional domain, such as ‘I stayed with patients when they wanted’ and six questions in the spiritual domain, such as ‘I prayed with patients’. This is scored on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 for ‘Not at all’ to 4 for ‘Very much so’. The range is 22 to 88 points, with higher scores indicating better terminal care performance. The Cronbach's α of the tool was 0.93 when it was developed and 0.82 in this study, indicating good reliability. The tool was used with the developer's permission.

Data Analysis

The collected data were analysed using the SPSS Statistics 24.0 program. All collected data were used in the analysis without any missing values. Kolmogorov–Smirnov was used to check whether the assumptions were met. The normality was confirmed for moral distress (p = 0.88), terminal care stress (p = 0.64) and terminal care performance (p = 0.55). Participants' socio‐demographic and work characteristics, including moral distress, terminal care stress and terminal care performance, were analysed as means and standard deviations, frequencies as well as percentages. Differences in the levels of moral distress, terminal care stress and terminal care performance according to the socio‐demographic and work characteristics of the participants were analysed using t‐test, ANOVA and post hoc tests such as the Scheffe test. Correlations between moral distress, terminal care stress and terminal care performance were analysed using Pearson's correlation coefficient. To identify factors affecting the participants' terminal care performance, a regression model was constructed with gender, work experience in the COVID‐19 unit, moral distress and terminal care stress, which were shown to be related to terminal care performance, as independent variables. Gender and work experience in the COVID‐19 unit were converted to dummy variables and input, and multiple regression analysis was performed using the Enter method. To test the independence of the dependent variable, a Durbin–Watson test without autocorrelation was performed, and the tolerance limit was confirmed. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was tested to check for multicollinearity.

Differences in Moral Distress, Terminal Care Stress and Terminal Care Performance by Participants' Socio‐Demographic and Work Characteristics

The moral distress of the participants was statistically significantly different according to age (F = 3.24, p = 0.0.42) and nursing experience (F = 6.04, p = 0.003). An analysis conducted previously exhibited that the level of moral distress did not differ by age (p = 0.057) but was higher among those with > 5 years and < 10 years of nursing experience than those with < 5 years (p = 0.017).

Statistically significant differences were found in participants' terminal care stress based on gender (t = −2.36, p = 0.020), department (t = 2.80, p = 0.006) and work experience in the COVID‐19 unit (F = 3.24, p = 0.024). Women reported higher levels of terminal care stress than men (p = 0.020), general ward nurses reported higher levels of stress than ICU nurses, and those with > 1 year and < 2 years of work experience in the COVID‐19 unit reported higher levels of stress than those with < 6 months of experience(p = 0.044).

There were statistically significant differences in terminal care performance by gender (t = −2.91, p = 0.004) and work experience in the COVID‐19 unit (F = 3.34, p = 0.021). Women had higher terminal care performance than men(p = 0.004), and those with > 1 year and < 2 years of work experience in the COVID‐19 unit exhibited higher performance than those with < 6 months (p = 0.042) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study was conducted to identify factors affecting the performance of terminal care by nurses who provided care for patients who were dying due to COVID‐19. The level of terminal care performance was lower than the average level, indicating that terminal care was not performed well, and factors influencing terminal care performance were moral distress, working experience in the COVID‐19 unit, and gender. The level of terminal care stress was medium to high, and there were significant differences by gender, work department and work experience in the COVID‐19 unit. Moral distress differed depending on clinical experience, and moral distress and terminal care stress were positively correlated with terminal care performance.

The level of terminal care performance in this study contradicts the results in most studies on nurses who provided care for general patients (Son and Park 2021), which found that the level of terminal care performance was higher than the average level. In the subcategories, it was found that nurses performed relatively well in the physical area, but less well in the emotional and spiritual areas. This is because caregivers are often unable to be present in the isolation wards of patients with COVID‐19, so nurses are forced to do everything, from dressing and changing sheets to oral care (Yeo 2022). Meanwhile, nurses working in COVID‐19 wards typically provide direct patient care for approximately 5 hours per shift while wearing PPE, which has led to significant physical discomfort and injury among many nurses (Bambi et al. 2021). Consequently, due to the difficulty of providing prolonged care while wearing PPE, the amounts and quality of emotional and spiritual care may have decreased because of the increased burden of non‐nursing tasks (Kong 2021). In particular, as a result of the nature of the pandemic, outsiders, including those connected to a religion, are not allowed to enter isolation wards and patients are not permitted to go outside (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency 2022). Hence, it is inevitable that the spiritual domain score was low. Institutional measures, such as providing books, tapes and articles related to religion, need to be implemented to enhance spiritual terminal care performance. In addition, extraneous work other than nursing should be reduced through accurate work guidelines and job assignments during the COVID‐19 situation to allow emotional and spiritual care to be provided. Nursing students in Korea often learn about terminal care as a part of adult and geriatric nursing courses, and as a result, they often experience terminal care during their practical training (Jung 2023; Yun, Moon, and Park 2023). However, the likelihood of experiencing terminal care decreased due to the lack of practical training for nursing students caused by COVID‐19. Additionally, new nurses believe that terminal nursing education is necessary, but only 23.1% of them have received terminal nursing education (Park and Seo 2019). This suggests a need to promote terminal care training starting with nursing students and to supplement such training for new nurses to enhance terminal care performance.

The most influential factor in terminal care performance was moral distress. It is known that moral distress occurs due to various restrictions in the COVID‐19 situation (Kong 2021). The results of this study seem that nurses' psychological discomfort caused by moral distress leads to overcompensation of performance, and Wilkinson (1989) reported similar results. The performance of terminal care increases depending on work experience in the COVID‐19 unit, indicating that as nurses gain more experience, their terminal care performance improves (Koo and Min 2023). The COVID‐19 unit nurses struggled with basic preparation steps, such as familiarising themselves with the isolation unit's layout and PPE (Wilson et al. 2022). However, as nurses become more familiar with the guidelines and become used to wearing PPE and providing care over time (Chung, Seong, and Park 2022), which means they have more experience in the COVID‐19 unit, their level of terminal care performance increases. In addition, the fact that women appear to be better at performing terminal care is related to the universal gender norm that women are the subjects of care (Gott, Morgan, and Williams 2020). According to a recent US survey, over 75% of all caregivers are women and may spend as much as 50% more time providing care than men (Family Caregiver Alliance 2016). Several studies in Korea also showed that the proportion of female nurses ranged from 90% to 95.4% (Lee and Ahn 2019; Kim, Nam, and Kwon 2017). This suggests that medical service providers are not free from gender bias (Wong and Phillips 2023). Although terminal care stress was not influential in terminal care performance, it was positively correlated because an appropriate stress level provided proper stimulation for work performance (Son and Park 2021). Meanwhile, the explanatory power of this model was 17%, meaning that other variables influenced the terminal care performance of COVID‐19 patients. Therefore, future studies would need to examine additional factors influencing the terminal care performance of such nurses.

The results regarding the level of terminal care stress in this study are similar to those from Son and Park (2021), which examined nurses caring for general patients. Personal conflicts with dying patients were the highest among the subdomains, likely due to the stress of having to watch a COVID‐19 patient die without their family being present (Anderson‐Shaw and Zar 2020) and the inability to provide direct care due to the use of personal PPE (Kong 2021). The emotional challenges faced by nurses caring for patients suffering from COVID‐19 have been identified as a significant issue in other studies, highlighting the need for support to maintain the emotional health of nurses (Bisesti et al. 2021). Therefore, it is important to develop and apply a stress‐coping programme to help such nurses effectively manage terminal care stress.

The higher level of terminal care stress in women is related to the fact that women have higher levels of mental health problems, such as stress, anxiety and depression, than men. (Gott, Morgan, and Williams 2020). The level of terminal care stress was found to be lower in COVID‐19 intensive care units than in general wards, possibly as a result of the differences in the nature of the care provided in these settings. This may be because it is possible to make quick and accurate interventions in such settings (Kim, Kim, and Yun 2019). In addition, when a patient with COVID‐19 dies, they will have a different terminal and body disposal process than other patients (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency 2022), and nurses with more experience in caring for dying COVID‐19 patients are more likely to suffer a higher level of terminal care stress.

Among the subcategories, moral distress, and specifically the factor of COVID‐19, was higher than the findings in a study about hospital nurses (Lake et al. 2022). The higher average rating of COVID‐19‐specific factors compared to the overall average rating of moral distress is consistent with the above research results (Lake et al. 2022). COVID‐19 inpatient units are isolation facilities where patients are admitted or die without their families (Ben‐Jacob and Peterson 2022). Nurses feel a significant level of moral anguish when they watch patients receiving treatment and dying alone without any family (Lake et al. 2022). Nurses working in these units are exposed to environments that may increase their risk of contracting COVID‐19 and spreading it to their families (Hughes and Rushton 2022). They are also assigned to unfamiliar environments and must follow unfamiliar guidelines (Donkers et al. 2021), which can lead to high levels of distress. The more clinical experience a nurse has, the higher their level of moral distress. This appears to be due to the experience that nurses experienced the hospitalisation or death of patients, which was different from their previous situation. (Je et al. 2022).

Strengths and Limitations

This study is significant as it identified the level of moral distress, terminal care stress and terminal care performance of nurses who cared for COVID‐19 patients, filled in a research gap and identified moral distress as the most significant factor affecting their terminal care performance.

Two limitations of this study should be noted. First, the surveys were voluntary. Hence, the results may not represent all nurses, as only those interested participated. Second, while this study measured the levels of moral distress, terminal care stress and terminal care performance of nurses who cared for COVID‐19 patients, the instruments used for measuring terminal care stress and terminal care performance may not fully reflect the unique circumstances of COVID‐19.

Clinical Implications

As a result of this study, the COVID‐19‐specific factor among the moral distress subcategories was higher than in previous studies. Lake et al.'s (2022) study targeted all nurses, while this study only surveyed those who provided care for patients with COVID‐19. In other words, the moral distress of nurses who provided care for patients with COVID‐19 was higher. Therefore, to alleviate the moral distress of nurses caring for patients with new infectious diseases, it is necessary to create regular meetings within the hospital to share and resolve their moral distress. Additionally, active efforts are needed to develop and apply moral distress relief programmes at the organisational level.

Since the stress of terminal care has been found to be high during the COVID‐19 pandemic, it is necessary to develop and apply a stress‐coping programme to effectively respond to the stress of terminal care if a new infectious disease was to arise. Additionally, terminal care stress must be continuously monitored and managed at the organisational level.

Nurses caring for patients with COVID‐19 responded that they were lacking in terminal care performance. To improve the performance of terminal care, the segregation of duties for each occupation must be clearly defined, and tasks outside of the nursing job should be minimised. Due to the nature of new infectious diseases, individuals such as religious figures who are not medical staff cannot enter hospitals; therefore, the practice of spiritual terminal care needs to be improved by providing books, tapes and items related to religion on the ward at the institutional level.

Moral distress, gender and COVID‐19 work experience were influential factors in terminal care. Based on this, it is necessary to establish hospital guidelines to improve terminal care performance while reducing moral distress with minimal restrictions without deviating from the nation's new infectious disease guidelines. In addition, it is necessary to develop and apply an education programme about terminal care that reflects gender and to develop education that can be indirectly experienced through simulation in preparation for new infectious diseases. Furthermore, establishing an ethics training system for nurses with limited experience is also necessary to improve their terminal care performance in preparation for future infectious diseases.

Conclusion

No study has examined the performance of terminal care for patients with COVID‐19 during the pandemic. This study examined factors affecting terminal care performance among nurses caring for COVID‐19 patients. The factors were moral distress, gender and experience in the COVID‐19 department. In addition, it was revealed that the nature of COVID‐19 may have limited the terminal care performance. Therefore, to increase the performance of terminal care in the case of new infectious diseases, it is necessary to reduce tasks that are not nursing‐related and provide accurate guidelines for terminal care. Programmes are needed to measure moral and terminal care stress and appropriately reduce them. In addition, nurses' performance in terminal care is expected to improve if a simulation programme is prepared to experience terminal care in a new infectious disease situation. Based on these findings, we recommend developing and validating an instrument to measure terminal care stress and the terminal care performance of nurses caring for patients with emerging infectious diseases such as COVID‐19. Future research would also benefit from identifying other related factors of terminal care performance for nurses caring for COVID‐19 patients.
