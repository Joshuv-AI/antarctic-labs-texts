Comparison of nasolabial changes following Le Fort Ⅰ maxillary advancement versus Medpor implantation in skeletal class Ⅲ patients
Authors: Xin LV, Jingyi LI, Jun CAO, Yuxin WANG, Xudong YANG, Qian ZHANG
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNasolabial depression is a main aesthetic concern in patients with skeletal Class Ⅲ malocclusion, for which Le Fort Ⅰ maxillary advancement or paranasal Medpor implantation is the major treatment choice. However, the aesthetic changes following these two treatments are unclear. This study aimed to compare the nasolabial modifications following Le Fort Ⅰ advancement and Medpor implantation to provide a reference for in surgical planning.MethodsTwenty skeletal Class Ⅲ patients undergoing Le Fort Ⅰ advancement or paranasal Medpor implantation were retrospectively enrolled (10 in the advancement group and 10 in the implantation group). Spiral CT data were acquired one week preoperatively (T1) and at least 6 months postoperatively (T2) for study. For detailed comparison, the nasolabial region was divided into paranasal and subnasal regions. A total of 26 landmarks were marked, from which 10 linear segments,4 angles, corresponding regional volume and curvature were measured.ResultsThe average moving distance in advancement group was 5.29 ± 0.53 mm, and the average thickness of the Medpor implant was 6.51 ± 0.72 mm. After surgery in both groups, most of the parameters in the paranasal and subnasal regions increased significantly (P ≤ 0.05), except for nasal length (N’-SN). In the advancement group, the postoperative nasal length (N’-SN) demonstrated a significant reduction compared to preoperative values, whereas in the implantation group, no significant difference was observed in nasal length (N’-SN) after surgery.In the paranasal region, alar width and alar base width showed significantly greater changes in the implantation group than in the advancement group. In the subnasal region, four linear segments, one angle and the corresponding volume and curvature showed significantly greater changes in the advancement group than in the implantation group.ConclusionBoth Le Fort Ⅰ advancement and Medpor implantation can significantly improve paranasal depression. However, compared with Le Fort Ⅰ advancement, Medpor implantation provides limited improvement in subnasal depression but affects more in alar width.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13005-026-00620-2.Keywords: Nasolabial region, Le Fort Ⅰ advancement, Medpor implantation, Orthognathic surgery

Introduction

Improving nasolabial depression is a major esthetic appeal in patients with skeletal Class Ⅲ malocclusion [1]. Nasolabial depression is an obvious characteristic of midfacial concavity and manifests as a flat nasal dorsum, a flat nasal tip, a protruded upper lip, and a deep nasolabial fold [2]. Orthognathic surgery is a common procedure that provides both functional and aesthetic benefits [3]. Through maxillary Le Fort Ⅰ advancement, midfacial concavity is augmented and nasolabial depression is subsequently improved [4]. Apart from orthognathic surgery, for patients demanding minimally invasive treatments, paranasal augmentation using alloplastic or autologous implants has become an alternative treatment to camouflage nasolabial depression [2]. The implant materials employed in paranasal augmentation include costal cartilage, polyetheretherketone (PEEK), polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), and porous polyethylene (Medpor) [5–8].

Anatomically, the nasolabial region comprises two distinct subregions: the paranasal and subnasal areas [9, 10]. The paranasal area refers to the broad concavity extending from the alar base to the adjacent cheeks, while the subnasal area is beneath the nostrils and columella [11]. Due to the complex anatomical structure and the highly variable soft tissue thickness, predicting soft tissue changes in the nasolabial area remains challenging compared with other aesthetic units [12]. Ruppertti et al. analyzed the sagittal changes in soft tissue following maxillary advancement and found the correlation rates in subnasal point, soft A point and upper lip were ranged from 57% to 73% [13]. Lei Hou et al. compared prediction accuracy after Le Fort Ⅰ advancement among diverse face regions, showing that the accuracy in lateral buccal regions was significantly less than 2.0 mm while that in the upper lip was significantly more than 2.0 mm [12]. In addition, although implants have been extensively applied for augmenting midfacial depression, quantitative studies on postoperative soft tissue changes are lacking [14, 15].

This study employed three-dimensional(3D) imaging techniques to quantitatively assess soft tissue changes in the nasolabial region following Le Fort Ⅰ maxillary advancement versus Medpor implantation in skeletal Class Ⅲ patients. By analyzing linear, angular, volumetric, and curvature parameters, we aimed to characterize the regional effects and degree of improvement for each approach, providing objective evidence for individualized treatment planning.

Materials and methods

Patient information

This retrospective study enrolled 20 skeletal Class Ⅲ patients (5 males and 15 females. BMI: 16.10–26.42 kg/m². Age: 18–33 years) who underwent treatment at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Trauma, Orthognathic and Plastic Surgery, Nanjing Stomatological Hospital, between February 2022 and June 2025. Patient demographics are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) skeletal Class Ⅲ malocclusion primarily attributed to maxillary hypoplasia with evident paranasal depression; (2) age < 35 years; (3) body mass index (BMI) between 16 and 27 kg/m²; (4) maxillary vertical and transverse displacement < 1 mm with three-dimensional rotational angle < 1°.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) jaw deformity secondary to infection or trauma; (2) history of craniofacial surgery or filler injection; (3) prior camouflage orthodontic treatment.

Surgical treatment

Patients were allocated into two groups on the basis of the surgical approach: 10 patients underwent Le Fort Ⅰ advancement combined with bilateral sagittal split ramus osteotomy (BSSRO) (advancement group), and 10 patients underwent Medpor implantation combined with BSSRO (implantation group). All surgical procedures were performed by the same senior surgical team to ensure consistency in surgical planning and technique.

Advancement group: Surgery was performed through an intraoral maxillary vestibular incision. After subperiosteal dissection, a standard Le Fort Ⅰ osteotomy was performed to completely separate the maxillary complex from the cranial base. The mobilized maxilla was advanced to the predetermined position and stabilized with titanium plates and screws at the piriform rim and zygomaticomaxillary buttress (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). BSSRO was performed via the intraoral approach with osteotomies of the medial ramus, external oblique ridge, and buccal cortex, followed by ramus splitting and rigid fixation with titanium plates and screws. We measured the sagittal advancement of the bilateral piriform rim osteotomy lines as the surgical displacement in the advancement group (Supplementary Fig. 1C-D). Average maxilla advancement was 5.29 ± 0.53 mm.

Implantation group: Bilateral intraoral vestibular incisions were made in the canine region. Subperiosteal dissection was performed to create an implant pocket in the paranasal region around the piriform aperture. Medpor implants were positioned and secured within this pocket (Supplementary Fig. 1E). The BSSRO was performed as in the advancement group. We used the same size Medpor implants for all the patient. No personalized adjustment to the implants was performed either preoperatively or intraoperatively. We scanned the implant with a structured light scanner (D2000, 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) preoperatively to obtain the implant model. The implant was divided into upper, middle and lower sections, and the maximum thickness of the two cross sections was measured. The mean thickness of the two cross sections was taken as the implant thickness [7](Supplementary Fig. 1F-G). All measurements were performed independently by two examiners in duplicate, with results expressed as means ± standard deviations. The implant thickness was 6.51 ± 0.72 mm.

All patients underwent alar base cinch suturing, with bilateral nasalis muscles secured to the soft tissue inferior to the anterior nasal spine to control postoperative alar widening. Patients were admitted to the general ward for three days postoperatively and subsequently monitored by scheduled follow-up appointments. We have reviewed the postoperative complications among all patients, including respiratory issues, infections, exposure of the implant or titanium plate, poor bone healing/non-union, osteonecrosis, apical root damage, and pulp necrosis. In the implantation group, no postoperative complication was observed. In the advancement group, there was 1 patient who developed local infection at the maxillary surgical incision one week after the surgery. After rinsing with normal saline and anti-inflammatory therapy, the wound healed normally. Each group achieved normal jaw healing.

Data process

Spiral CT data and facial scanning data were acquired within one week preoperatively and at least 6 months postoperatively using a 64-slice spiral CT scanner (Siemens AG, Germany) with 1 mm slice thickness and 3dMDface System (3dMD Inc., Atlanta, GA, USA). DICOM data were imported into Mimics (Research 21.0, Materialise Dental, Belgium) for three-dimensional reconstruction of craniofacial hard and soft tissues, and exported as STL files for analysis in 3-Matic (Research 13.0, Materialise Dental, Belgium).

To evaluate nasolabial soft tissue morphological changes, pre- and postoperative 3D models reconstructed from spiral CT data and facial scanning data were registered with a root mean square (RMS) accuracy requirement of < 0.5 mm (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 2) [16]. In this study, the FH plane was defined as the horizontal plane. The plane passing through the bilateral porion(P) points and perpendicular to the FH plane was defined as the coronal plane. The plane passing through the hard tissue nasion(N) point and perpendicular to both the coronal plane and the horizontal plane was defined as the sagittal plane. A coordinate system was established with the Frankfort horizontal plane as the X–Y plane, the midsagittal plane as the Y–Z plane, and the coronal plane as the X–Z plane. The soft and hard tissue landmarks are marked (Figs. 1B–C; Table 1). For detailed comparison, the nasolabial region was divided into bilateral paranasal regions and the subnasal region [16, 17] (Figs. 1B–C). Linear segments, angles, volume and curvature were measured to evaluate nasolabial soft tissue (Fig. 1D–J; Table 2). All indicators were measured directly on the 3D models reconstructed from spiral CT data, except for the rate of curvature change. The comparison of pre-and post-operative soft tissues was cheeked through the facial scan data for better image distinction.

In order to obtain the postoperative changes of soft tissue volume in bilateral paranasal regions and the subnasal region, we first used the “Split Surface” function in the 3-matic software to extract the pre- and post-operative surface patches (Fig. 1F). The “Create Bridge” function in 3-matic can virtually enclose the lateral surfaces between the pre- and post-operative patches and form a complete cuboid (Fig. 1G). The volume of the cuboid is the increase volume after the surgery.

To obtain the pre- and post-operative curvature of the soft tissue in bilateral paranasal regions and the subnasal region, we used the “Analyze” function in the 3-matic software to obtain the frequency histogram and the heatmap of the curvature. Then, we selected the first peak valley as the boundary, extracted the curvature values of the first peak, and calculated the average as the curvature average of this area.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). Continuous variables are expressed as means ± standard deviations. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Paired t-tests were used for intragroup comparisons to evaluate pre- and postoperative differences and bilateral soft tissue symmetry. Independent samples t-tests were used to compare soft tissue changes between the two groups. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Since the comparisons reached 20 items, we performed Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction to all between-group parameters. The results show 9 of the 10 originally significant results remained statistically significant. The rate of curvature change in the subnasal region (original P = 0.0454; adjusted P = 0.0908) no longer held significance after correction. The complete FDR-adjusted P values are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

All landmark identifications and measurements were performed independently by two examiners in duplicate to ensure high reproducibility and reliability of the methodology.

Results

Nasolabial changes following Le Fort Ⅰ advancement and Medpor implantation

The advancement group: As shown in Supplementary Table 3, in the paranasal region, significant postoperative increases were observed in alar width (AL(L)-AL(R)), alar base width (AC(L)-AC(R)), alar prominence (AL-CP), alar base prominence (AC-CP), cheek prominence (CK-CP), ∠1, ∠2 and curvature. The unilateral volume increased by 1297.00 ± 540.40 mm³, with curvature change rate of 0.23 ± 0.07. In the subnasal region, significant postoperative increases were observed in subnasal height (SN-LS), subnasal prominence (SN-CP), soft tissue A’ point prominence (A’-CP), labrale superius prominence (LS-CP), upper nasolabial angle (∠3), lower nasolabial angle (∠4) and curvature, while nasal length (N’-SN) decreased. The volume increased by 2258.00 ± 615.00 mm³, with a curvature change rate of 0.36 ± 0.20.

The implantation group: In the paranasal region, significant postoperative increases were observed in alar width (AL(L)-AL(R)), alar base width (AC(L)-AC(R)), alar prominence (AL-CP), alar base prominence (AC-CP), cheek prominence (CK-CP), ∠1, ∠2 and curvature. The unilateral volume increased by 1488 ± 444.20 mm³, with a curvature change rate of 0.26 ± 0.07. In the subnasal region, significant postoperative increases were observed in subnasal height (SN-LS), subnasal prominence (SN-CP), soft tissue A’ point prominence (A’-CP), labrale superius prominence (LS-CP), upper nasolabial angle (∠3), lower nasolabial angle (∠4) and curvature, while nasal length (N’-SN) showed no significant change compared to preoperative values. The volume increased by 1142.00 ± 585.40 mm³, with a curvature change rate of 0.19 ± 0.13.

Discussion

The nasolabial area serves as a visual focus and plays pivotal role in midfacial aesthetics [19]. Due to its complex anatomical structure, predicting the postoperative soft tissue changes in the nasolabial area is more challenging than other aesthetic units, including the mandibular zone and malar zone [11]. In this study, we partitioned the nasolabial area into distinct paranasal and subnasal regions and quantified the different soft tissue changes following Le Fort Ⅰ advancement and Medpor implantation.

In the paranasal region, both procedures effectively improved soft tissue projection. After Le Fort Ⅰ advancement, the alar prominence (AL-CP) increased by approximately 82% of the bone advancement, which is consistent with the 60–90% soft tissue-to-hard tissue ratios reported in previous studies [20]. We found that the Medpor implantation (6.5 mm thickness) achieved paranasal augmentation by 54–66%, which is relatively lower than the 68–74% reported by Kwon et al. using 4.5 mm implantation [7]. Notably, our study and previous data (Supplementary Table 5) consistently suggest that the soft tissue response ratio decreases as the magnitude of hard tissue displacement increases [20–25]. This observation indicates that the relationship between hard and soft tissue changes in the nasolabial region is nonlinear rather than proportional. Biomechanical modeling studies have demonstrated that facial soft tissue behaves as a viscoelastic material whose deformation characteristics can be approximated by mass-spring and finite element models [26, 27]. In such models, soft tissue resistance increases progressively with larger displacements due to the inherent strain-stiffening properties of skin and subcutaneous tissue [28], the tethering effect of perioral muscles [29], and the constraints imposed by fascial attachments [30]. These findings collectively support the hypothesis that larger skeletal advancements encounter greater viscoelastic resistance from the overlying soft tissue envelope, resulting in a diminished response ratio which need further study. Furthermore, we found that the implantation group showed greater increase in alar base width compared to the advancement group. The alar base increased 2.70 ± 0.92 mm on average after Le Fort Ⅰ advancement (5.29 ± 0.52 mm) compared with 3.91 ± 1.19 mm after Medpor implantation (6.51 ± 0.72 mm). Relevant literature reveals that Le Fort Ⅰ advancement (5.6–7.3 mm) results in an alar base widening of (1.7–2.5 mm), and thinner Medpor implantation (4.5 mm) results in an alar base widening of 2.2 mm [7, 31, 32]. The findings of previous studies are broadly consistent with our findings, suggesting that particular attention should be paid to alar base width management after Medpor implantation.

In the subnasal region, we found that the soft tissue augmentation was significantly greater in the advancement group than that in the implantation group. Recent studies are consistent with our results. Keldal et al. and Susarla et al. reported that soft tissue augmentation in the subnasal region is obvious (ratios of soft and hard tissue: 0.83–0.9) following Le Fort Ⅰ advancement [33, 34].But Jeon et al. and Wang et al. reported that the soft tissue changes are limited (-0.17–0.30 mm) following implantation [1, 35]. These findings can be attributed to Le Fort Ⅰ advancement, which induces comprehensive advancement of the entire maxilla, thereby enhancing overall midfacial prominence and resulting in a more pronounced improvement of the subnasal concavity. In contrast, the Medpor implant is constrained by its morphology and dimensions, resulting in limited improvement to areas beyond the paranasal region [36].

The present study highlights that Le Fort Ⅰ advancement and Medpor implantation each possess distinct advantages and limitations, and the selection should be guided by a comprehensive assessment of individual patient needs. As part of bimaxillary orthognathic surgery, Le Fort Ⅰ advancement enables simultaneous correction of dental occlusion and 3D repositioning of the maxillomandibular complex. This allows for comprehensive improvement of both skeletal function and facial aesthetics, including the paranasal, subnasal, and upper lip regions [4]. For patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion accompanied by significant maxillary deficiency and functional impairment, orthognathic surgery remains the standard of care [4]. However, orthognathic surgery entails greater surgical trauma, higher costs, and a prolonged treatment course that typically requires long time for preoperative orthodontic preparation [37]. In comparison, Medpor implantation is a minimally invasive procedure that can be performed without preoperative orthodontic preparation. It offers shorter operative time, reduced recovery period, and lower overall cost [38]. For patients present with isolated paranasal depression and acceptable occlusal function, Medpor implantation can effectively camouflage the midface concavity. However, as demonstrated in our study, its augmentation effect is largely confined to the paranasal region, with a tendency toward more obvious alar widening.

The present findings are based on a limited, predominantly female sample constrained by the retrospective study design and institutional case volume; perioperative weight fluctuations may additionally introduce variability in soft tissue measurements. Future prospective studies with larger, sex-balanced, and weight-controlled cohorts are warranted to confirm and extend these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, both Le Fort Ⅰ advancement and Medpor implantation can effectively improve nasolabial depression, but can lead to different esthetic changes. Le Fort Ⅰ advancement demonstrates pronounced augmentation in both the paranasal region and subnasal region, and is the prior choice for skeletal Class Ⅲ malocclusion patients with maxillary deficiency and midface depression. Medpor implantation shows significant improvement in paranasal region but limited effect in subnasal region. It can serve as a supplement for single jaw surgery for mild skeletal Class Ⅲ malocclusion and is an effective choice for improving paranasal depression, but special attention should be paid to the management of the alar width.
