Beyond Experience Retrieval: Learning to Generate Utility-Optimized Structured Experience for Frozen LLMs
Xuancheng Li, Haitao Li, Yujia Zhou, Yiqun Liu, Qingyao Ai

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are largely static and often redo reasoning or repeat mistakes. Prior experience reuse typically relies on external retrieval, which is similarity-based, can introduce noise, and adds latency. We introduce SEAM ( S tructured E xperience A dapter M odule), a lightweight, executor-specific plug-in that stores experience in its parameters and generates a structured, instance-tailored experience entry in a single forward pass to guide a frozen LLM executor. SEAM is trained for utility via executor rollouts and GRPO while keeping the executor frozen, and can be further improved with logged-success SFT after deployment. Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks show consistent accuracy gains across executors with low overhead. Extensive ablation and analysis further elucidate the mechanisms underlying SEAM’s effectiveness and robustness. 1 1 1 We release our code at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/SEAM .



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved strong performance across diverse tasks, including machine translation, information retrieval, and mathematical reasoning
(
1
;
20
;
24
)
.
Yet, they remain largely
static
: when confronted with new problems, LLMs often reason from scratch, re-explore familiar solution paths, and repeat avoidable mistakes
(
10
;
8
;
36
)
.
In contrast, humans distill procedural experience from past problem solving and apply it to new situations, improving efficiency and avoiding prior failures
(
7
;
14
;
27
)
.
Ideally, an agent should also accumulate reusable experience for fast adaptation to novel problems.

Effectively leveraging prior experience requires answering two questions: how to extract useful experience from interactions and how to apply it to new problems.
A common approach is to maintain an explicit experience library and consult it at inference time via retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
(
3
;
9
;
39
)
.
However, a key limitation is that retrieval is typically optimized for surface-level similarity rather than practical utility to the executor, and similarity does not imply effectiveness. Even when semantically close, retrieved entries may fail to yield meaningful improvements. They can miss decisive constraints or critical checks, or add noise that distracts reasoning and destabilizes solving. In addition, external libraries add noticeable inference overhead by requiring retrieval-time computation and often extra LLM calls for summarization or refinement, resulting in higher latency.

In this paper, we take a different approach: instead of maintaining a structured experience library as an external store, we encode experience in the parameters of a lightweight model and use it to guide a frozen downstream LLM executor. Accordingly, we introduce
SEAM
(
S
tructured
E
xperience
A
dapter
M
odule), a compact, trainable, and executor-specific plug-in. We view each executor as a distinct problem solver with its own strengths, inductive biases, and failure modes; SEAM therefore stores executor-tailored experience in its parameters and, at inference time, generates a structured, instance-tailored experience entry in a single forward pass, steering the frozen executor without modifying its parameters. This parameterized design eliminates the need for complex external curation and organization, since these choices are learned implicitly during training. More importantly, unlike prior RAG-style experience retrieval that relies on similarity-based selection, SEAM is optimized for utility—it is trained to generate experience entries that actually improve the frozen executor’s task performance.

We train SEAM with a forward learning procedure consisting of three training steps: (i) forward exploration, where SEAM samples multiple schema-constrained experience entries per instance; (ii) rollout-based evaluation, where a frozen executor solves each instance conditioned on each candidate and we score candidates by task success; and (iii) parametric library evolution, where we compute group-relative advantages from candidates’ returns and update SEAM under the GRPO objective while keeping the executor frozen. At test time, SEAM synthesizes a task-relevant experience entry in a single forward pass to steer the executor. Optionally, SEAM can be further improved after deployment by logging successful entries and periodically applying SFT on accumulated successes.

We conduct experiments on diverse mathematical reasoning benchmarks to assess SEAM.
Across multiple benchmarks and executors, SEAM improves accuracy with low additional overhead.
We further conduct a series of analyses to investigate SEAM’s internal mechanisms and dynamics.

Related Work

Learning from Experience.

Learning from experience enables LLM-based agents to reuse prior knowledge for new tasks, improving autonomy, efficiency, and performance in dynamic settings
(
26
;
38
;
30
;
17
)
. Most prior work instills this capability by directly training the agent (e.g., reinforcement learning, imitation learning, and multi-agent learning)
(
5
;
34
;
9
)
, but often suffers from limited use of negative examples, weak cross-task generalization, and catastrophic forgetting. As an alternative, RAG-based frameworks extract structured experience from past rollouts, store it in a memory bank, and retrieve relevant snippets to augment prompts for new tasks
(
25
;
19
;
3
)
. Despite their flexibility, these pipelines rely on surface-level similarity matching that can yield low-utility prompts
(
6
;
23
)
, introduce nontrivial latency and compute overhead
(
29
;
22
)
, and depend on hand-crafted storage templates that require continual maintenance as tasks evolve
(
31
)
.

LLM and Reinforcement Learning.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is increasingly used to move beyond supervised fine-tuning and enable LLMs to learn from interactive feedback. RLHF aligns outputs with human preferences
(
21
)
, and recent work adopts GRPO to improve long-horizon reasoning and tool use under environment interaction signals. For example, Search-R1 trains models to issue web queries to maximize answer correctness
(
13
)
, Tool-N1 learns when to invoke external tools
(
37
)
, and RAGEN extends GRPO to multi-turn self-evolution
(
28
)
. In learning-from-experience settings, RL is typically applied either to directly optimize the executor
(
18
)
or to train a memory agent within RAG to extract and inject memories
(
32
)
. In contrast, SEAM applies GRPO to train a lightweight, executor-specific guidance policy that internalizes experience in parameters and generates problem-conditioned structured experience guidance to steer a frozen executor.

3
Method

3.1
Problem Formulation

Once deployed, LLMs operate in a fundamentally stateless manner: let
\mathcal{D}
denote a distribution over problem instances
s
, where each instance may be associated with a verifiable target
a^{\star}(s)
.
Given a new instance
s
sampled from
\mathcal{D}
, a frozen executor LLM
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
typically generates its output
\hat{a}
without conditioning on accumulated experience:

\hat{a}\;\sim\;\pi_{\phi}(\hat{a}\mid s).

(1)

This limitation arises because LLMs are bounded by a finite context window, which prevents them from retaining
and reusing information across long interactions or evolving task sequences.
Consequently, each new instance must be reasoned through from scratch, leading to redundant exploration of
solution paths already discovered and frequent repetition of past errors.

This motivates us to equip LLMs with an experience module
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
that maintains experience and
provides guidance to the frozen executor. Formally, for an instance
s
sampled from
\mathcal{D}
and a specific executor

\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
, the experience module produces an auxiliary structured experience prompt
z
of limited length:

z\sim\pi_{\theta}(z\mid s,\mathcal{E}_{\phi}),

(2)

The executor
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
then solves the task conditioned on this experience:

\hat{a}\sim\pi_{\phi}(\hat{a}\mid s,z).

(3)

The design of the experience module
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
can thus be formulated as the following constrained
optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\max_{\pi_{\theta}}&\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\,s\sim\mathcal{D},\;z\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s,\mathcal{E}_{\phi}),\;\hat{a}\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid s,z)}\Big[R\big(\hat{a},a^{\star}(s)\big)\Big]\\
\text{s.t.}&\lVert z\rVert\leq L.\end{array}

(4)

where
R(\cdot)
denotes a reward measuring task correctness,
a^{\star}(s)
is the ground-truth (or verifiable) target for instance
s
, and
L
is a predefined maximum length of the generated experience.

Most existing work instantiates
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
with the RAG pipeline: a LLM is first prompted to retrospectively
summarize its trajectories into explicit experience entries, which are stored in an external bank.
At inference time, the experience policy performs a search step that retrieves
k
candidates from this store,
followed by a generation step that rewrites the selected items into the prompt
z
.
However, two key limitations remain: (i)
Similarity
\neq
utility
—retrieval is optimized for surface relevance, so retrieved items can be noisy or miss decisive constraints/checks, yielding little improvement or even destabilizing the solver; (ii)
Inference overhead
—external libraries add noticeable latency due to retrieval-time computation and often extra LLM calls for refinement.

3.2
Architecture

To address this limitation, we adopt a fundamentally different paradigm: instead of maintaining an external experience store and retrieving entries via a RAG pipeline, we
parameterize
the structured experience library by encoding it in a lightweight generator, which can be accessed with a single forward pass. We introduce
SEAM
(
S
tructured
E
xperience
A
dapter
M
odule), a compact and trainable plug-in that implicitly maintains a structured experience library in its parameters. Given a new problem instance
s
for a downstream LLM executor
\mathcal{E}{\phi}
, SEAM synthesizes a schema-constrained experience entry
z
in one forward pass, and
\mathcal{E}{\phi}
solves the task conditioned on
z
.

This design shifts experience management away from explicit retrieval systems into a lightweight generative module and has several key characteristics.

(1) Efficient parametric experience access.

SEAM internalizes a structured experience library in its parameters and produces guidance via a single forward pass.
This avoids the high cost of RAG-style pipelines, notably external storage, online retrieval, and retrospective summarization by large models, yielding low overhead and low latency at inference time.
Moreover, this parameterized design eliminates the need for complex external curation and organization (e.g., indexing, clustering, deduplication, and retrieval policies), as these choices are absorbed into SEAM and learned implicitly during training.

(2) Utility-oriented guidance instead of surface-level retrieval.

At test time, SEAM does not retrieve entries based on embedding similarity.
Instead, it
synthesizes
an instance-tailored experience entry under strict schema constraints and is trained using the executor’s task success as a direct learning signal.
As a result, SEAM learns to generate structured experience with actionable utility for solving the current instance, rather than returning superficially relevant but low-utility snippets that may dilute or destabilize the solver.

(3) Executor-specific experience libraries.

We adopt an
executor-specific
perspective: different downstream agents behave like different problem solvers, with distinct strengths, inductive biases, and failure modes.
Accordingly, SEAM maintains a dedicated parametric experience library for a particular frozen executor and generates
dedicated
, customized, and structured guidance for that executor, without modifying the executor’s parameters.

(4) Lightweight, pluggable modular adaptation.
We train SEAM while keeping the downstream executor
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
frozen, which reduces training and deployment costs, enables hot-swapping across domains, and preserves
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
’s general competence and stability. Moreover, operational rollouts can be readily logged and recycled as feedback, enabling continual training and steady evolution of SEAM without modifying
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
.

As illustrated in Figure
1
, SEAM provides the executor with a
structured experience entry

z
as a textual prompt. Each entry follows a fixed schema with three complementary components:

•

Problem analysis
— a concise assessment of the instance
s
, highlighting intrinsic difficulty, salient substructures, and likely failure modes;

•

Experience highlights
— executor-aligned insights distilled from prior rollouts, specifying reusable strategies, heuristics, or cautionary checks that help
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
solve
s
;

•

Reference plan
— an
illustrative
step-by-step decomposition procedure that demonstrates a reliable workflow, rather than revealing instance-specific shortcuts or the final answer.

This schema-bound design encourages SEAM to produce guidance that is simultaneously diagnostic (problem analysis), prescriptive (experience highlights), and procedural (reference plan), providing a stable and controllable interface for steering the frozen executor.

3.3
Training of SEAM

We train SEAM with a forward learning procedure that treats SEAM as an executor-conditioned guidance policy and improves it using observable outcomes from running a frozen executor.
SEAM is not a surrogate reasoner; it provides procedure-level, schema-constrained guidance that biases the executor’s exploration toward more reliable solution trajectories. Concretely, training iterates over three steps: (i)
forward exploration
, where SEAM samples a group of schema-constrained candidate experience entries for each instance; (ii)
rollout-based evaluation
, where we condition the frozen executor on each candidate and score its utility by downstream task success; and (iii)
policy evolution
, where we update SEAM (e.g., via GRPO) to increase the likelihood of higher-utility entries.
Throughout training, the executor remains frozen and only SEAM is updated.

Step 1: Forward exploration (candidate generation).

For each training instance
s\sim\mathcal{D}
, SEAM samples a group of
K
candidate experience entries to guide the specific executor
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
:

\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s,\mathcal{E}_{\phi}).

(5)

Step 2: Rollout-based evaluation (guidance utility).

To evaluate the guidance utility of each candidate for the target executor
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
, we condition the frozen executor on each
z_{j}
and perform
M
stochastic rollouts.

\hat{a}_{j,m}\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid s,z_{j}),\qquad m=1,\dots,M.

(6)

We score each rollout using a binary reward, which jointly reflects task success and structural completeness:

\mathcal{R}(\hat{a},z;s)\;=\;\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\hat{a}$ is correct and $z$ is structurally complete},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

We aggregate rollout outcomes by averaging rewards across rollouts for the same candidate:

\widetilde{R}_{j}\;=\;\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{R}(\hat{a}_{j,m},z_{j};s).

(8)

Step 3: Parametric library evolution (GRPO update).

We update SEAM using GRPO with the group rewards
\{\widetilde{R}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
.
Let
\pi_{\text{old}}
denote the behavior policy used to sample
\{z_{j}\}
(a snapshot of SEAM before the update),
and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
be a fixed reference policy (a copy of the initial SEAM).
We compute group-relative advantages by normalizing rewards within the group:

A_{j}=\frac{\widetilde{R}_{j}-\bar{R}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\big(\widetilde{R}_{i}-\bar{R}\big)^{2}}+\delta},\qquad\bar{R}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\widetilde{R}_{i},

(9)

and perform a PPO-style clipped update over experience candidates:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\min\!\Big(\rho_{j}A_{j},\,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{j},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,A_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s,\mathcal{E}_{\phi})\,\big\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid s,\mathcal{E}_{\phi})\Big),

\displaystyle\rho_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(z_{j}\mid s,\mathcal{E}_{\phi})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(z_{j}\mid s,\mathcal{E}_{\phi})}.

(10)

Crucially, gradients are backpropagated only through SEAM; the executor
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
remains frozen. From a high-level perspective, we optimize one model using the observable execution behavior of another: we treat
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
as a fixed solver that provides behavioral signals, and train SEAM to generate the structured guidance that
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
can best exploit. In this way, SEAM is not a surrogate reasoner; instead, it supplies procedure-level, schema-constrained cues that bias the executor’s exploration toward more reliable solution trajectories. This improves guidance and end-to-end effectiveness without backpropagating through
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
. Such a decoupled paradigm enables SEAM to continually improve from execution feedback without modifying
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
’s parameters, thereby preserving the executor’s general competence while enhancing robustness and overall performance.

Optional: Deployment-time evolution via logged SFT.

As an optional extension, SEAM can further improve during deployment by logging successful experience entries that actually guide the frozen executor to correct solutions. When enabled, we store these pairs
(s,z^{\star})
in a buffer
\mathcal{B}
and periodically fine-tune SEAM with teacher forcing:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{(s,z^{\star})\sim\mathcal{B}}\Big[\log\pi_{\theta}(z^{\star}\mid s,\mathcal{E}_{\phi})\Big].

(11)

This update again modifies only SEAM while keeping the executor frozen, progressively internalizing effective experience from concrete past episodes into the parametric library.

4.2
Main Results

Table
1
shows across four math-reasoning benchmarks and two executors, SEAM achieves the best performance on all datasets, demonstrating the effectiveness and generality across different executors.
Relative to directly training the executor, SEAM attains superior overall performance, indicating that internalizing the experience in a small parameterized generator that provides task-conditioned test-time guidance is effective and can even surpass directly optimizing the executor itself. This likely reflects an inductive-bias difference: directly training the executor updates a global behavior policy and aggregates credit across heterogeneous instances, whereas SEAM learns instance-conditioned guidance that injects procedural priors and reshapes the executor’s search space on a per-problem basis without perturbing the executor’s general reasoning capabilities.

Compared with RAG-based approaches, SEAM delivers substantially stronger performance, indicating that its mechanism of implicitly parameterizing experience and directly generating task-specific guidance addresses key limitations of RAG such as low-quality experience summarization and weak downstream guidance.
Regarding task difficulty, the largest performance gains of SEAM emerge on the more challenging AIME problems, whereas on the comparatively saturated MATH500 and GSM8K benchmarks, SEAM still provides modest improvements. This pattern suggests that experience-based guidance becomes increasingly critical and effective for the executor when tackling harder tasks. Collectively, these findings affirm the robustness and effectiveness of our design across diverse models and datasets.

Results and discussion.

shows that training is essential beyond prompting and experience formatting.
SFT-only provides a modest improvement over no training, but remains less effective than GRPO, suggesting that directly maximizing the likelihood of logged successful experiences is insufficient to reliably optimize guidance utility under a frozen executor.

5.2
Model Pairing

We systematically examine the pairings of SEAM’s different lightweight models with various executors in Table
4
.
On the side of smaller lightweight models, Qwen3-0.6B yields some improvements, but its limited generalization and generation quality lead to experience entries that lack stability and coverage in complex tasks, resulting in only modest gains. In contrast, Qwen3-1.7B exhibits stronger generalization and more structured generation, thereby providing more stable and significant improvements for the executor on harder tasks. On the side of executors, stronger LLMs are already capable of solving a substantial portion of problems correctly on their own, which reduces the marginal benefit brought by external experience, making the improvements from SEAM less pronounced. Overall, there exists a complementary relationship between the scale of SEAM and the capability of the executor: given a fixed executor, enhancing the generative capacity of SEAM effectively amplifies the value of external experience; whereas when the executor itself is already sufficiently strong, the gains from external experience saturate, in which case further strengthening SEAM (e.g., with a more powerful base model or improved training) may be necessary to achieve additional benefits.

6
Conclusion

We presented
SEAM
, a lightweight, executor-specific module that parameterizes experience and produces structured, problem-conditioned guidance in a single forward pass to steer a frozen LLM executor. SEAM is trained with a three-step pipeline: (1) generate multiple schema-constrained experience candidates; (2) evaluate each candidate via executor rollouts and task success; and (3) update SEAM with GRPO using group-relative advantages to internalize the most effective experiences.
SEAM can further evolve at deployment time by applying SFT on accumulated successes. Experiments and additional analyses demonstrate SEAM’s effectiveness and robustness across different frozen executors, and position it as a practical alternative to retrieval-based experience reuse. We hope this work motivates a broader paradigm of learning from experience beyond RAG.

Limitations

Despite the promising results, our approach still has several limitations:

Limited scope across domains and executors.

Our current evaluation is primarily centered on mathematical reasoning and a small set of executors, leaving the generality of SEAM in broader domains and with more diverse executor families insufficiently characterized. We plan to validate SEAM across a wider range of tasks, domains, and executors to better understand its robustness and applicability.

Lack of a unified, adaptive SEAM for cross-model and cross-domain transfer.

SEAM is currently trained in an executor- and domain-specific manner, and we do not yet provide a principled mechanism for a single SEAM instance to adaptively transfer across heterogeneous models and domains. We will explore parameter-management strategies (e.g., modularization, routing, or lightweight adapters) to enable scalable cross-model and cross-domain transfer.

Continual evolution remains underexplored.

While SEAM can be updated using logged feedback, we do not yet address robust continual learning under distribution shift, noisy or delayed supervision, and catastrophic forgetting. As future work, we will design robust continual-learning algorithms that allow SEAM to evolve its experience library reliably over time.

Ethical Considerations

Our work studies how to improve a frozen large-language executor by training a lightweight guidance model (SEAM) from execution feedback. We discuss potential ethical risks and mitigations below.

Intended use and misuse.

SEAM is designed to generate structured, task-conditioned guidance that improves reliability and efficiency for benign problem-solving tasks (e.g., mathematical reasoning).
Like other LLM-based systems, however, it could be repurposed to assist with harmful activities by providing step-by-step procedural guidance.
We do not propose SEAM as a safety mechanism, and our method should be deployed with standard safeguards (e.g., content filtering, policy enforcement, and monitoring) when used in real-world applications.

Data and privacy.

Our experiments use public datasets and do not involve personal data collection.
In the optional deployment-time evolution setting, the system may log successful interactions for supervised fine-tuning.
If applied beyond our experimental setup, such logging could capture user-provided sensitive information.
Deployments should therefore minimize data retention, anonymize or redact sensitive fields, and obtain appropriate user consent; access controls and secure storage should be used for any retained logs.

Bias and fairness.

SEAM internalizes patterns from the executor’s rollouts and the training data distribution.
As a result, any biases present in the executor or datasets may be propagated or amplified in generated guidance.
While our benchmarks are primarily technical, future applications in open-domain settings should include bias assessment and mitigation, such as evaluating guidance quality across user groups and enforcing constraints on unsafe or discriminatory content.

Robustness and over-reliance.

Generated guidance may be incorrect, incomplete, or misleading, which could cause downstream errors if users or systems rely on it uncritically.
Our design partially mitigates this by keeping the executor frozen and by training SEAM to optimize downstream task success, but this does not guarantee correctness on all inputs.
For higher-stakes deployments, additional measures (e.g., verification, calibration, or human oversight) are necessary.

Environmental impact.

Training SEAM requires additional computation beyond using a fixed executor alone.
However, SEAM is lightweight compared to the executor and can reduce time-to-correct at inference by improving solution success rates.
We report efficiency metrics to support transparent accounting of computational costs.

Responsible release.

We release our code in the supplementary material and recommend accompanying it with clear documentation on intended use, limitations, and safety considerations, as well as default configurations that disable deployment-time logging unless explicitly enabled by users.

B.2.1
General protocol and fairness controls

If a method produces longer memory (e.g., iterative curation), we truncate by keeping higher-priority sections first

B.2.2
Mem-0
(graph-based long-term memory)

Adaptation to math reasoning.

Mem-0
is originally designed for incremental conversational memory.
We adapt it by treating each processed training/evaluation instance as a single interaction unit:
(user message = problem statement; assistant message = model solution).
The external memory stores structured “facts” that can help future problems, such as reusable lemmas,
problem templates, common transformations, and frequent error patterns.

Two-stage pipeline: extraction and update.

We follow the standard
Mem-0
pipeline consisting of:
(i)
extraction
, which extracts a set of salient candidate memories from the latest interaction
given (a) a global summary of the history and (b) a recency window of recent interactions;
(ii)

ADD
,
UPDATE
,
DELETE
, or
NOOP
for each candidate memory, to maintain consistency and avoid redundancy.

Hyperparameters.

We use a recency window
m=10
interactions and retrieve
s=10
similar memories during the update phase.
All similarity search uses dense embeddings with cosine similarity.
After the memory is updated, we retrieve the top-
r
memories most relevant to the current problem (default
r=10
),
serialize them into text, and prepend them to the executor prompt as additional context
(while respecting the 4k-token memory budget).

B.2.3
Dynamic-Cheatsheet
(DC-RS)

Method overview.

We adopt the DC-RS (retrieve-and-synthesize) variant, which maintains a persistent text cheatsheet (memory)
and updates it
before
solving each new problem.
At step
i
, DC-RS performs:
(1) retrieve top-
k
most similar past inputs and their model-generated outputs,
(2) update the cheatsheet by a
curator
conditioned on the previous cheatsheet and retrieved pairs,
and (3) solve the current problem by a
generator
conditioned on the updated cheatsheet.

Retrieval and prompts.

We set
k=3
for retrieval.
The retriever ranks historical inputs by embedding cosine similarity and returns the top-
k
input–output pairs.
We follow DC’s prompt separation between a generator (problem solver) and a curator (memory editor):
the generator prompt contains a
[[CHEATSHEET]]
placeholder and a
[[QUESTION]]
placeholder;
the curator prompt takes the previous cheatsheet and retrieved notes and outputs an updated cheatsheet.
We keep the cheatsheet as a structured document (e.g., reusable strategies / heuristics) and enforce the global 4k-token memory budget.

Online update without labels.

Consistent with DC, the curator does not use ground-truth answers.
After each problem is answered, we append the (problem, model output) pair to the retriever store;
the next step may retrieve it to refine the cheatsheet.

B.2.4
Memento
(case-based reasoning with a case bank)

Adaptation to single-step math problems.

Memento
is proposed as a planner–executor agent with a growing case bank.
For math benchmarks without tool interaction, we instantiate the core case-based reasoning (CBR) component
in a single-step setting:
each problem is treated as a state
s

a

r

Case bank (Write/Read).

We use the
non-parametric

Write
appends each completed case
(s,a,r)
to the case bank.

Read
retrieves top-
K

and stored past states.

Hyperparameters and prompting.

We set

to control context length and match the retrieval scale of other baselines.
Retrieved cases are formatted as short exemplars (problem statement + key steps + final answer, optionally with the reward tag),

We again enforce the 4k-token memory budget for the concatenated exemplars.

Why we use non-parametric CBR.

While

also supports a parametric retrieval policy (online-updated Q-function),
we use the non-parametric variant to avoid introducing an additional trainable module and to keep the comparison focused on retrieval-augmented reuse.
