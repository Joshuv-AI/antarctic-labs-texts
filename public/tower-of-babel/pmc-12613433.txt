Exploring the impact of pan-immune-inflammation-value and other traditional inflammation-based markers on coronary collateral circulation in patients with chronic total occlusion
Authors: Kenan Toprak, Mesut Karataş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary collateral circulation (CCC) plays a pivotal role in mitigating the adverse effects of chronic total occlusion (CTO) by providing an alternative blood supply to ischemic myocardium. In recent years, there has been a growing interest in understanding the relationship between inflammation and coronary collateral development. However, the predictive role of pan immune inflammation value (PIV), a new and expanded inflammatory response marker, on CCC has not yet been investigated. This article aims to provide a detailed comparison of the effect of PIV and other traditional inflammation-based markers on CCC in patients with CTO.Methods467 patients who underwent coronary angiography due to non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and were detected to have CTO in epicardial coronary artery were included in the study. The patients were divided into two groups as poor CCC and good CCC according to the Rentrop classification, and the inflammation-based marker values of the groups were compared.ResultsThe PIV values of NSTEMI patients were significantly higher in the poor CCC group than those in the good CCC group. High PIV values were determined as independent predictors for poor CCC (OR: 0.832, CI: 0.791–0.876, p < 0.001). With a 79% sensitivity and 78% specificity at the optimal cut-off value of 628.8, PIV demonstrated good predictive ability for identifying poor CCC (AUC: 0.785, CI: 0.744–0.826, p < 0.001).ConclusionPIV was found to be superior to traditional inflammation-based markers in discriminating pooc CCC in NSTEMI patients.Keywords: Pan-immune-inflammation value, Coronary collateral circulation, Inflammation-based markers, Non-ST segment elevation myocardial infarction, Chronic total occlusion

Introduction

Chronic total occlusion (CTO), characterized by complete obstruction of a coronary artery for an extended period, poses a significant challenge in the management of coronary artery disease [1]. The development of collateral vessels around the occluded artery represents a natural adaptive response to maintain myocardial perfusion [2]. Coronary collateral circulation (CCC), comprising small blood vessels that develop as an adaptive response to chronic ischemia, holds significant physiological, clinical, and prognostic implications in the context of coronary artery disease (CAD) [3]. Physiologically, these vessels sustain myocardial perfusion, acting as a natural bypass system and regulating pressure gradients across obstructed arteries.4 Clinically, well-developed collaterals alleviate angina symptoms, reduce infarct size during acute coronary events, and enhance exercise tolerance [4–7]. Moreover, patients with robust collateral circulation exhibit improved survival rates, experience fewer cardiovascular events, and demonstrate better outcomes following revascularization procedures [8, 9]. Risk stratification models consider collateral development, emphasizing its crucial role in assessing disease severity and predicting patient prognosis, making it an integral factor in the comprehensive management of CAD [10].

CTO presents a significant challenge in coronary artery disease management, and the intricate interplay between inflammation and CCC is crucial in understanding the pathophysiology of this condition [1–3]. Inflammation has been recognized as a pivotal player in atherosclerosis progression and the regulation of collateral vessel formation [11, 12]. Traditional inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein/albumin ratio (CAR) [12], neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio (NLR) [13], platelet/lymphocyte ratio (PLR) [14] and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII)17 have been studied extensively on CCC development, but the introduction of Pan Immune Inflammation Value (PIV) has sparked interest due to its comprehensive evaluation of the immune-inflammatory status [15, 16]. Understanding the comparative effectiveness of these markers is crucial for advancing our knowledge of the intricate interplay between inflammation and coronary collateral development, ultimately contributing to improved clinical outcomes for patients with CTO.

PIV is a novel inflammatory marker that integrates various immune and inflammatory parameters, providing a more comprehensive assessment of the overall inflammatory status [15, 16]. By considering a wide array of immune cells and their interactions, PIV offers a nuanced perspective on the inflammatory processes underlying chronic diseases [17]. Previous studies have demonstrated that PIV is associated with adverse outcomes in a variety of disease processes highlighting its prognostic value across diverse inflammatory and neoplastic conditions [15–17]. This broad applicability supports its potential as a robust marker for assessing systemic inflammation in cardiovascular diseases as well.

PIV’s unique approach considers both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory components, potentially providing a more comprehensive assessment of immune-inflammatory interactions [18, 19]. The intricate relationship between inflammation and coronary collateral circulation in CTO patients is a complex puzzle [3]. Traditional markers like CAR, NLR, PLR and SII have provided valuable insights, but the introduction of PIV allows for a more nuanced exploration of the inflammatory landscape [12–14, 20].

Inflammation undeniably exerts an adverse influence on coronary collateral circulation in patients with CTO [12–14, 20]. In this context, the comparison of PIV and traditional inflammatory markers becomes crucial. PIV’s comprehensive evaluation of immune-inflammatory parameters may provide a more nuanced understanding of the inflammatory landscape, potentially paving the way for improved strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of inflammation on coronary collateral circulation in CTO patients. This article provides a detailed exploration of how inflammation adversely affects coronary collateral circulation and offers a comprehensive comparison between the novel PIV and traditional inflammatory markers in the context of CTO.

Materials and methods

Study population

In this retrospective cross-sectional study, 467 consecutive patients who underwent coronary angiography for the first time due to non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and were detected to have CTO in at least one epicardial coronary artery between January 2017 and July 2023 were included in the study to evaluate the development of CCC. According to guidelines from the European Heart Journal, a combination of the following factors was used to diagnose NSTEMI: typical ischemic chest pain, a troponin-I level of >0.01ng/ml, changes in the 12-lead ECG without persistent ST-segment elevation but with persistent or transient ST-segment depression, transient ST-segment elevation, inverted or flat T-wave, pseudo-normalization of T-wave, or normal electrocardiography [21]. Patients with moderate-to-advanced liver or renal dysfunction, previous history of CAD, history of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, malignancy, hematological and endocrinological disorders, coronary artery myocardial bridge or congenital coronary artery malformation, acute or chronic infective disease, and patients with missing data were excluded from the study. Also, patients whose coronary arteries had less than 95% of their lumen narrowed were not allowed to participate in the investigation because this level of constriction was insufficient to induce the formation of CCC in the arteries [22]. Of the initially screened patients, 1% were excluded due to missing laboratory or clinical data. Since the proportion of excluded cases was minimal and no significant differences in demographic or clinical characteristics were observed between included and excluded patients, this exclusion was considered unlikely to affect the study’s validity or introduce selection bias.

The local ethics committee approved the study, which was conducted in accordance with the 2008 Declaration of Helsinki version.

Clinical definitions and measurements

Postprandial glucose > 200 mg/dL, fasting blood glucose > 126 mg/dL, or the use of anti-diabetic medication were considered indicators of diabetes mellitus (DM). Evidence of hypertension was defined as a diastolic blood pressure of at least 90 mm Hg, a systolic blood pressure of at least 140 mm Hg, or the use of antihypertensive drugs. Taking any lipid-lowering medicine or having serum total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dl, triglycerides ≥ 200 mg/dl, low density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥ 130 mg/dl, or high density lipoprotein cholesterol < 35 mg/dl were all considered indicators of dyslipidemia. Patients who had smoked for at least half of the preceding year were classified as smokers. Heart failure was defined as having heart failure symptoms and signs together with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of less than 50%. The formula for calculating body mass index (BMI) was weight (kg) divided by height (m)2. During the hospital stay, the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was assessed by two experienced cardiologists using an echocardiography device (Philips Epiq 7 device, Andover, MA, USA) via the modified Simpson method.

Angiographic procedure and evaluations

Using the Judkins approach, either a femoral or radial access was used for coronary angiography (CAG), depending on the cardiologist’s preference. All patients who were recruited in the study had their coronary angiography images digitized for quantitative analysis using DICOM viewer (MedCom GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany). The final decision was taken after at least two experienced, independent invasive cardiologists reviewed the CCC analysis. Lesions lasting longer than three months that exhibit either minimal contrast penetration into the lesion without distal vascular opacification or full blockage of antegrade blood flow on angiography were classified as coronary total occlusions [1]. CCC was graded using the Rentrop classification method [23]. This classification is based on the following factors: the absence of any collateral vessels (grade 0); filling in side branches through collateral vessels without the ability to see the epicardial segment (grade 1); partial filling of the major epicardial artery through collateral circulation (grade 2); and full filling of the major epicardial artery through collateral circulation (grade 3). The study involved the division of patients into two groups: good CCC (well-developed CCC: grades 2–3) and poor CCC (poorly-developed CCC: grades 0–1). In cases where multiple CCCs were present, the highest Rentrop grade was employed for analysis. The SYNTAX score (SS), which was used to determine the degree of coronary artery severity and extensity, assessed by using an online calculator (https://syntaxscore.org/calculator/start.htm). All coronary lesions resulting in about 50% stenosis in arteries with a diameter greater than 1.5 mm were taken into account when calculating the SYNTAX score. Investigators evaluating CCC were blinded to the patients’ laboratory data. For CCC and SYNTAX score evaluation, the intra-observer variabilities were 2% and the inter-observer variabilities were 3%.

Laboratory analysis and calculations

Antecubital venous blood samples were taken into tripotassium EDTA-based anticoagulated tubes at admission to the cardiology clinic, prior to CAG. After a 12-hour fast, blood samples were taken in the morning after 20 min of rest. An automated blood cell counter (Coulter LH 780 Hematology Analyzer, Beckman Coulter Corp, Hileh, Florida) was used to assess the complete blood count (CBC). Using an automated biochemical analyzer (Roche Diagnostic Modular Systems, Tokyo, Japan), all standard biochemical tests were carried out. Based on the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation formula, the estimated glomerular filtration rate (e-GFR) was determined [24]. PIV was calculated using the formula: (absolute neutrophil count × absolute monocyte count × absolute platelet count)/absolute lymphocyte count. The CAR was calculated as the ratio of serum CRP level to serum albumin level. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was calculated using the following formulas: absolute platelet count x absolute neutrophil count/absolute lymphocyte count, monocyte/HDL-C ratio (MHR) derived from dividing the absolute number of monocytes by serum HDL-C, neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio (NLR) derived from dividing the absolute neutrophil count by the absolute lymphocyte count, and platelet/lymphocyte ratio (PLR) derived from dividing the absolute platelet count by the absolute neutrophil count.

Statistical analyses

The analyses were performed using the statistical programs SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and MedCalc (trial version 12.7.8, Mariakerke, Belgium). Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to check if the distribution of continuous variables was normal. The Student’s t test or the Mann-Whitney U test were used to compare continuous variables, depending on the distribution. The median (interquartile range) or mean ± standard deviation (SD) were used to represent continuous data. For regularly distributed variables, the mean (SD) was utilized, and for nonnormally distributed data, the median (interquartile range [IQR]) was employed. Categorical variables were represented by numbers (percentages), and comparisons were made using Fisher’s exact or the χ2 test as needed. Spearman or Pearson rho correlation coefficient was used to determine the relationship between inflammation based markers and Rentrop classification. The study employed univariate and multivariate regression analyses to ascertain putative independent factors that contribute to the formation of CCC. The multivariate logistic regression analysis included the baseline variables for which the univariable analysis had clear significance (p < 0.1). In logistic regression analysis, the multivariate model’s goodness of fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow and Nagelkerke R2 tests. To avoid multicollinearity, the components that make up the inflammation based markers were not included in the regression model. The regression model did not include variables with multicollinearity (variance inflation factor > 3 or tolerance < 0.1) revealed by logistic regression analysis. Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to estimate the predictive values of PIV, SII, CAR, NLR, PLR and MHR. The Youden J index was used for determining the point’s sensitivity and specificity. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) values of each mentioned marker were compared pairwise using the Delong test through the MedCalc statistical program.

ROC curve analyses and DeLong tests were performed separately for each inflammation-based marker (PIV, NLR, PLR, CAR, and SII) to compare their discriminative power for identifying poor CCC. No additional clinical variables were included in these ROC models, as the aim was to evaluate the standalone predictive ability of each marker. However, multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of poor CCC after adjusting for potential confounding variables such as age, gender, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking, and lipid parameters.

The regresssion model’s outcomes were presented as p-values, an odds ratio (OR), and a 95% confidence interval (CI). A two-sided p-value lower than 0.05 was accepted as statistically significant.

Results

A total of 467 patients (mean age: 57.4 ± 12.8 and 63.4% male) who underwent coronary angiography due to NSTEMI and were detected to have CTO in at least one epicardial coronary artery after exclusion criteria were included in this study (Fig. 2). The patients were categorized into two groups according to the Rentrop classification: 195 (41.7%) with poor CCC and 272 (48.3%) with good CCC. Baseline demographic, clinical and laboratory data of the study population are presented in Table 1, and angiographic data are presented in Table 2. The frequency of diabetes mellitus (DM) and metabolic syndrome was significantly higher in patients with poor CCC compared to patients with good CCC (28.2% vs. 20.2%, p = 0.045; 55.9% vs. 46.0%, p = 0.034, respectively). Although the frequency of dyslipidemia was more common in the poor CCC group than in the good CCC group, it was not statistically significant (56.4% vs. 47.4%, p = 0.055). There was no significant difference between the hemodynamic characteristics and previous medications of the groups (p > 0.05, for all). While uric acid, CRP, triglyceride levels and the number of neutrophils, monocytes and platelets were higher in the poor CCC group than in the good CCC group; albumin, HDL-C, hemoglobin, hematocrit levels and lymphocyte count were significantly lower (p < 0.05, for all). CAR (0.18 [0.06–0.60] vs. 0.09 [0.02–0.28], p < 0.001), MHR (0.018 [0.012–0.026] vs. 0.016 [0.011–0.024], p = 0.013), NLR (4.3 ± 2.1 vs. 3.3 ± 1.9, p < 0.001), PLR (172 ± 86 vs. 154 ± 86, p = 0.026), SII (814 [602–1110] vs. 478 [420–871], p < 0.001) and PIV (674 ± 48 vs. 616 ± 37, p < 0.001) levels were significantly higher in the poor CCC group than in the good CCC group (Fig. 3). The SYNTAX score level was slightly higher in the poor CCC group than in the good CCC group (21.1 ± 6.7 vs. 19.6 ± 7.7,p = 0.032). In correlation analysis, P showed a stronger negative correlation with the level of CCC development indicated by the Rentrop classification than other inflammation-based markers (r: −0.3019, 95% Confidence Interval(CI): −0.3822 to −0.2171), p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1). In multivariate regression analysis, the presence of metabolic syndrome (Odds Ratio [OR]: 1.606, Confidence Intervale [CI]: 1.040–2.479, p = 0.033), high uric acid (OR: 0.502, CI: 0.364–0.994, p = 0.021), hematocrit (OR: 1.061, CI: 1.019–1.104, p = 0.004), SYNTAX score (OR: 0.964, CI: 0.936–0.993, p = 0.017), MHR (OR: 0.962, CI: 0.941–0.984, p = 0.001), SII (OR: 0.792, CI: 0.708–0.885, p < 0.001) and PIV levels were determined as independent predictors for poor CCC (Table 3). PIV was well predictive of detecting poor CCC with 79% sensitivity and 78% specificity at the best cut-off value of 628.8 in ROC curve analysis (Area Under the Curve [AUC]: 0.785, CI: 0.744–0.826, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Additionally, the discriminative value of PIV for poor CCC was found to be significantly higher than other inflammation-based markers (p < 0.05, for all pairwise comparisons).

Discussion

​This article delves into a comprehensive comparison of the effect of Pan Immune Inflammation Value (PIV), calculated as platelet count multiplied by monocyte count multiplied by neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count, and traditional inflammation-based markers on CCC in patients with CTO. We demonstrated that high PIV values in NSTEMI patients with CTO were more closely related to the level of CCC development compared to other traditional inflammation-based markers. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the predictive values of PIV, a new inflammation-based marker, with other traditional inflammation-based markers for the development of CCC in patients with NSTEMI in whom CTO was detected.

When the native vessel does not have enough blood supply, coronary collaterals, also known as “natural bypasses,” are anastomotic connections between segments of the same coronary artery and between other coronary arteries. Complete coronary occlusion or recurrent severe myocardial ischemia and the pressure gradient between the coronary bed of arteries initiate the adaptive mechanism known as CCC development and so they serve as a vital backup blood supply.3,27,28 In CAD, preserving myocardial viability, reducing myocardial ischemia, and averting infarction depend critically on the appropriateness of CCC. Consequently, the mortality risk and occurrence of cardiovascular complications is lower for patients with well-formed CCC than for those with poorly developed CCC [4–10, 25]. Therefore, CCC formation plays a pivotal role in clinical outcomes, especially in NSTEMI patients who cannot achieve optimal revascularization.

Although coronary ischemia is the main factor that triggers CCC formation, many studies have shown that physiological, metabolic and molecular differences play an active role in this process [3–6, 11, 25]. Many recent studies have revealed that proinflammatory status plays a decisive role in the process of CCC formation [12–14, 20]. The assessment of inflammation in the context of coronary collateral circulation in CTO patients is a multifaceted endeavor. The intricate interplay between coronary collateral development and inflammation underscores the dynamic molecular and cellular processes that contribute to the intricate pathophysiology of cardiovascular diseases. The orchestrated response of coronary collateral development in the context of inflammation involves a complex cascade of molecular events, including the activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, endothelial dysfunction, and recruitment of immune cells, all of which collectively influence the arteriogenic and angiogenic processes pivotal in adapting to ischemic conditions within the coronary vasculature [3, 4, 25–28–30]. Inflammation contributes to endothelial dysfunction, impairing the ability of blood vessels to dilate and form collateral connections and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines disrupt the balance between angiogenic and inflammatory signaling pathways [26–28]. Many studies have shown that formulations created by bringing together the pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory components of the blood in various fractions (such as SII, CAR, NLR, and PLR) can reflect the pathophysiological processes related to the inflammatory process, such as the development of CCC, better than each of the components forming the formulations [12–14, 20].

Traditional inflammatory markers such as SII, NLR, and PLR, CAR, MHR provide valuable information on the pro-inflammatory process, but PIV, which considers the combined effect of platelets, monocytes, neutrophils, and lymphocytes, offers a more nuanced understanding of the immune-inflammatory landscape [15–19]. In this study, the superiority of PIV’s ability to predict the development of CCC compared to other inflammation-based markers may be due to the fact that PIV reflects a broader inflammatory response landscape with the synergistic effect of the components that make up the formula, compared to traditional predictors, when considered in the context of the close relationship between CCC development and inflammation [15–19].

​In patients with CTO, inflammatory markers such SII, MHR, and PIV seem to be good predictors of the development of CCC, which may be related to the systemic inflammatory response. When PIV was included in the basic model of traditional risk factors for CCC formation, the predictive value of the model could be increased. Further investigation on PIV’s additive synergistic effect with other predictors may contribute to improvements in predictive value, therapeutic relevance, and applicability.

In this study, PIV remained a strong predictor of the development of CCC in patients with NSTEMI after adjusting for relevant covariates. Metabolic syndrome, uric acid, hematocrit level and SYNTAX score are independent predictors of CCC development, and their predictive values need to be further investigated.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First of all, this study is a retrospective and observational study, which may be subject to selection bias and limit generalizations. Secondly, it was conducted with a relatively small sample size and no long-term follow-up was performed. Third, we collected data from blood samples taken only after patients were admitted to the hospital and did not account for changes in serial measurements. Fourthly, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, a definitive causal relationship between PIV and CCC development cannot be defined, and this relationship can be revealed more clearly in future longitudinal studies. Fifth, the Rentrop classification is only subject to macroscopic visual assessment and is inadequate for assessing microvascular CCC formation. Sixth, unaccounted confounding factors that may affect the development of CCC, such as physical exercise, may have affected the results of the study. Finally, another limitation of our study is that the variables constituting the PIV (neutrophil, platelet, monocyte, and lymphocyte counts) are acute-phase reactants that may fluctuate over time depending on the stage of inflammation or treatment interventions. As our study was cross-sectional, serial or longitudinal PIV measurements were not performed. Future studies incorporating longitudinal assessment of PIV dynamics could provide more comprehensive information about the temporal relationship between inflammation and coronary collateral formation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the intricate relationship between inflammation and CCC in patients with CTO, particularly in the context of NSTEMI. The novel inflammatory marker, PIV, emerges as a promising predictor, outperforming traditional inflammation-based markers such as CAR, NLR, PLR, MHR and SII. High PIV values were found to be significantly associated with poor CCC, establishing PIV as an independent predictor for CCC development. The comprehensive evaluation provided by PIV, considering a wide array of immune components, contributes to its superiority in discriminating CCC status. The study underscores the pivotal role of inflammation in CCC formation and highlights the potential of PIV as a valuable tool for risk stratification and clinical management in CTO patients. Further research with larger cohorts and longitudinal designs is warranted to validate these findings and explore the clinical implications of PIV in the context of coronary artery disease.
