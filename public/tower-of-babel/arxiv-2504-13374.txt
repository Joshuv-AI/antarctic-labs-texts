The generalized scalar auxiliary variable applied to the incompressible Boussinesq Equation
Andreas Wagner, Barbara Wohlmuth, Jan Zawallich

Abstract
This paper introduces a second-order time discretization for solving the incompressible Boussinesq equation. It uses the generalized scalar auxiliary variable (GSAV) and a backward differentiation formula (BDF), based on a Taylor expansion around t n + k t^{n+k} for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . An exponential time integrator is used for the auxiliary variable to ensure stability independent of the time step size. We give rigorous asymptotic error estimates of the time-stepping scheme, thereby justifying its accuracy and stability. The scheme is reformulated into one amenable to a H 1 H^{1} -conforming finite element discretization. Finally, we validate our theoretical results with numerical experiments using a Taylor–Hood-based finite element discretization and show its applicability to large-scale 3-dimensional problems.

Introduction

The Boussinesq system
zeytounian2003joseph
for an incompressible fluid models the temperature evolution when gravity and buoyancy are the dominant forces on the fluid. For engineering applications, it is a vital component to describe cooling, ventilation, or the dispersion of heat
de1983natural
;
ward2000application
;
BAKER1994261
;
lube2008stabilized
.
Aside from that, it is used in various scientific fields:
It is integral for geophysical applications like earth-mantle convection, though here, the infinite Prandtl number approximation leads to an omission of the inertia terms in the fluid equation
gassmoller2020formulations
. Further, it is also applied to describe pollutant dispersion in environmental engineering
MADALOZZO20145883
or to model the convection in stars
spiegel1971convection
in astrophysics.
All in all, the applications are various, and hence, finding efficient discretizations for the Boussinesq system is of interest.

Formally, the system can be stated in a non-dimensionalized form as follows: For the temperature
\theta\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the
velocity
{{\bf u}}\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
and the pressure
p\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}

on the bounded domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with time interval
[0,T]
, the incompressible Boussinesq system
zeytounian2003joseph
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta+({{\bf u}}\cdot\nabla)\theta-\frac{1}{\textmd{Re Pr}}\Delta\theta=

\displaystyle\,0

\displaystyle\textmd{ in }[0,T]\times\Omega,

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{{\bf u}}+({{\bf u}}\cdot\nabla){{\bf u}}-\frac{1}{\textmd{Re}}\Delta{{\bf u}}+\nabla p=

\displaystyle\,\textmd{Ri }\theta{\bf e}_{g}

\displaystyle\textmd{ in }[0,T]\times\Omega,

(2)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{{\bf u}}=

\displaystyle\,0

\displaystyle\textmd{ in }[0,T]\times\Omega,

(3)

with the Reynolds number
Re
, Prandtl number
Pr
, and Richardson number
Ri
as material parameters, and where
{\bf e}_{g}
is the vector describing gravity. We use the boundary conditions
{{\bf u}}=0
, and
\partial_{n}\theta=0
on
[0,T]\times\partial\Omega
, and the initial conditions
{{\bf u}}(0,\cdot)={{\bf u}}^{0}
,
\theta(0,\cdot)=\theta^{0}
, for the purely spatial fields
\theta^{0}
, and
{{\bf u}}^{0}
.
The model generally neglects temperature-induced density changes and only incorporates buoyancy effects due to gravity.

Over the years, several time-stepping schemes have been proposed:
In
davis2002operator
, a multiple-step time integrator is used for the fluid equation, while a backward Euler and a Crank-Nicolson scheme are applied to the energy equation. Both, fluid and energy equations use semi-implicit advective parts.
A first-order convergent consistent projection scheme was proposed in
yang2023error
, including stability proofs and an error analysis incorporating the spatial discretization.
In
jiang2019pressure
, a similar analysis is carried out for an ensemble scheme, including a particle mean velocity.
Moreover, additional first and second-order IMEX schemes with stability proofs have been introduced in
chen2023unconditional
.
A fully implicit scheme, including a nonlinear approximation, strategy has been proposed in
damanik2009monolithic
.
In
ding2024optimal
, a second-order scheme including temperature-dependent viscosity and heat conduction coefficient has been introduced.
In
takhirov2021direction
, the Boussinesq Equation on the spherical shell is solved with a direction splitting scheme using a domain-decomposition approach.
Similarly,
paszynski2020massively
uses a splitting scheme and presents a massively parallel simulation for atmospheric flows.
A mostly explicit method with an implicitly treated pressure term is presented in a high-performance context in
castillo2024explicit
with an analysis for the fluid-dynamic parts in
kaya2024error
.
In
pan2022monolithic
, a staggered scheme for the momentum and energy equations is used, also considering additional temperature-related changes in the density and viscosity.
A scheme with proofs for nonsmooth data was presented in
zhang2018decoupled
.
Further, various schemes have been introduced when just the steady state is of interest
hou2022decoupled
;
hawkins2024analysis
;
pollock2021acceleration
.

More recently, a scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) approach
shen2018scalar
;
shen2018convergence

and more specifically, its extension to multiple scalar auxiliary variables (MSAV)
cheng2018multiple
, was applied to a Crank-Nicolson leap-frog scheme and a BDF2 scheme in
jiang2023unconditionally
in combination with stability terms for the spatial discretization.
The unconditional stability was proven, and the second-order convergence was shown numerically.
Though the scheme succeeds in linearizing the Boussinesq system, it still requires inverting two saddle point problems for the velocity and pressure, which typically requires advanced iterative linear solvers.
In
zhang2024error
, a first-order SAV scheme based on a projection method is derived, including a stability and error analysis with remarks on how to generalize it to a second-order scheme.

Recently, building on the SAV approach, a generalized scalar auxiliary variable (GSAV) approach
huang2022new
has been introduced for dissipative systems and successfully applied to the Navier–Stokes equation, obtaining a
first-order in time consistent splitting scheme
li2023error
and a second-order scheme
huang2023stability
based on a BDF scheme using a time-discretization at
t^{n+5}
.
These schemes require the inversion of two generalized Poisson equations per time step for the velocity and pressure, which is significantly easier to achieve, and computationally less expensive.
Hence, in this paper, we build on the scheme introduced in
huang2023stability
to achieve a more efficient discretization for the Boussinesq equation.
We thereby introduce a new auxiliary variable for stability and include temperature dependence in our error analysis.
In addition, we justify the approach’s applicability for general Taylor expansions around
t^{n+k}
for
k\geq 3
, instead of limiting it to
k=5
.
In the following, we want to present our time discretization for the Boussinesq equation, heavily relying on the analysis and time-stepping scheme of the incompressible Navier–Stokes in
huang2023stability
.
We extend this scheme in Sec.
2
to the incompressible Boussinesq equation by including a bidirectional coupling to a temperature discretization.
We will introduce two equivalent schemes, one having higher regularity requirements for the discretization inspired by
huang2023stability
, while for the other scheme, the ideas of
shen2007error
are used to rewrite our scheme in a feasible way for a finite element discretization.
Our main theoretical result, an a priori error estimate for the time-discrete algorithm for
d=2
, is introduced in Sec.
3
, with a rigorous proof in Sec.
4
.
Finally, in Sec.
5
, we show numerical experiments validating our theoretical results, as well as 2D and 3D results for the Marsigli flow.

Time discretization and weak formulation

We will consider a slightly more general problem than the instationary Boussinesq system. This problem is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta+({{\bf u}}\cdot\nabla)\theta-\kappa\Delta\theta=

\displaystyle\,g

\displaystyle\textmd{ in }[0,T]\times\Omega,

(4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{{\bf u}}+({{\bf u}}\cdot\nabla){{\bf u}}-\nu\Delta{{\bf u}}+\nabla p=

\displaystyle\,{{\bf f}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\textmd{ in }[0,T]\times\Omega,

(5)

Also, we assume appropriate initial conditions at

, and

In the following derivation, we will assume that all fields are sufficiently smooth and later specify more restrictive assumptions. Our main goal is to decouple temperature, velocity, and pressure into independent equations.
First from
\nabla\cdot{{\bf u}}=0
and the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, we have
{\langle\partial_{t}{{\bf u}},\nabla q\rangle}=0
, for all

\displaystyle{\langle\nabla p,\nabla q\rangle}

\displaystyle={\langle{\bf{}f}(\theta)-({{\bf u}}\cdot\nabla){{\bf u}}-\nu\nabla\times\nabla\times{{\bf u}},\nabla q\rangle},

(6)

where
{\langle u,v\rangle}=\int_{\Omega}u\cdot v\,\textmd{d}x

In our time discretization, this equation replaces the incompressibility constraint and allows us to decouple pressure and velocity, yielding a
consistent splitting scheme

Further, we will use the GSAV method to stabilize our system. For this, we define the energy

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\theta(t),{{\bf u}}(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\lVert{{\bf u}}(t)\rVert}^{2}+\frac{\bar{\alpha}^{2}}{2}{\lVert\theta(t)\rVert}^{2},

where

{\lVert{{\bf u}}(t)\rVert}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}|{{\bf u}}(t,x)|^{2}\textmd{d}x

and

{\lVert\theta(t)\rVert}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}|\theta(t,x)|^{2}\textmd{d}x

denote the standard norm for square-integrable functions,
and
\bar{\alpha}>0
is some constant chosen later, linearly depending on the Lipschitz constant

{\bf{}f}_{2}

The energy evolution follows the dissipation law

\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}=-\nu{\lVert\nabla{{\bf u}}\rVert}^{2}+{\langle{{\bf f}}(\theta),{{\bf u}}\rangle}-\kappa\bar{\alpha}^{2}{\lVert\nabla\theta\rVert}^{2}+\bar{\alpha}^{2}{\langle g,\theta\rangle},

where we plugged in Eqs. (
4
), and (
5
) for the time derivative, utilized
{\langle({\bf u}\cdot\nabla)\theta,\theta\rangle}=0
, and
{\langle({\bf u}\cdot\nabla){\bf u},{\bf u}\rangle}=0

We define the auxiliary variable as

where
\bar{C}
is sufficiently large.
The idea is now to solve the auxiliary variable with a separate ordinary differential equation (ODE). We save the ratio between the energies from the ODE and partial differential equation (PDE) in the variable
\xi

\eta=1-(1-\xi)^{2}

To derive an ODE for

\dot{r}=\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}(\theta,{{\bf u}})

Just multiplying the right-hand side with one gives the differential equation

\dot{r}=\frac{r}{\mathcal{E}(\theta,{{\bf u}})+\bar{C}}\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}(\theta,{{\bf u}}),

which has the solution

\displaystyle r(t^{n+1})=\exp\left(\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\frac{\nicefrac{{d\mathcal{E}}}{{dt}}(\theta,{{\bf u}})}{\mathcal{E}(\theta,{{\bf u}})+\bar{C}}\,\textmd{d}t\right)r(t^{n}).

(7)

This reformulation has the advantage that finding a bound for the exponential term is straightforward. In practice, we will apply a simplistic quadrature formula to approximate the integral.

3
Assumptions and main result

In the following, we are in a standard functional analytic setting in 2-dimensions:
We denote the space of measureable functions with finite
p
-th moment by
L^{p}(\Omega)
with the norm
{\lVert\cdot\rVert}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
.
As mentioned in the previous section, we abbreviate the norm for
L^{2}(\Omega)
by
{\lVert\cdot\rVert}
and denote its inner product by
{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle}
.
Further, we write
L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
for all square-integrable functions with zero average, which is the natural space where the pressure resides.
The function space of
k
-times weakly differentiable functions with square integrable derivatives is denoted by
H^{k}(\Omega)
, and its norm by
{\lVert\cdot\rVert}_{k}
.
With
H^{k}_{0}(\Omega)
we denote the functions in
H^{k}(\Omega)
with zero trace on the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
Similarly, the function spaces for vectorial fields are given by
(L^{2}(\Omega))^{2}
,
(H^{k}(\Omega))^{2}
, and

,

by

. Hence, using

\displaystyle{\lVert{{\bf h}}^{n}\rVert}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C+\alpha^{2}C{\lVert\delta^{k+1}\theta^{n}\rVert}^{2}+{\lVert\delta^{k+1}{{\bf u}}^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}{\lVert\nabla\delta^{k+1}{{\bf u}}^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{L^{4}(\Omega)}

summing over the entire equation from

to

6
Conclusion

We have introduced a time-stepping scheme for the Boussinesq equation based on the discretization in
huang2023stability
for the Stokes equation using BDF(k) and GSAV to stabilize
the algorithm.
We have reformulated it to be suitable for a finite element discretization and presented error estimates for the time-stepping scheme showing second-order convergence in time for all BDF(
k
) schemes with
k\geq 3
.

Further, we have verified these rates numerically and demonstrated the scheme’s applicability to the Marsigli flow in 2D and 3D.
Even though the asymptotic behavior is independent of
k
, small values in
k
lead to smaller error constants.
Finally, we demonstrated that GSAV alone is not enough for a truly robust algorithm, though spatial stabilizations can improve the algorithm’s stability.
Enlarging
k
hereby yields additional stability.
Hence, there is a trade-off between choosing
k
as large as possible for maximum stability and making it as small as possible for a minimal error constant.

With a Taylor expansion around
t_{n+k}
, we immediately obtain the integral remainder terms

\displaystyle\zeta_{n+1,k}(v):=

\displaystyle k\int^{t^{n+1}}_{t^{n+k}}\partial^{2}_{t}{v}(s)(t^{n+1}-s)ds-(k-1)\int^{t^{n}}_{t^{n+k}}\partial^{2}_{t}{v}(s)(t^{n}-s)ds

\displaystyle\Xi_{n+1}(v):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left((2k+1)\int^{t^{n+1}}_{t^{n+k}}\partial^{3}_{t}v(s)(t^{n+1}-s)^{2}ds-4k\int^{t^{n}}_{t^{n+k}}\partial^{3}_{t}v(s)(t^{n}-s)^{2}ds\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+(2k-1)\int^{t^{n-1}}_{t^{n+k}}\partial^{3}_{t}v(s)(t^{n-1}-s)^{2}ds,\right)\quad\textmd{and}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{n+1}(r):=

\displaystyle\int^{t^{n}}_{t^{n+1}}\partial^{2}_{t}{r}(s)(t_{n}-s)ds.

For the integral estimate, we start with the first term
\zeta_{n+1,k}(v)
and get

\displaystyle\zeta_{n+1,k}(v)=\int^{t^{n+1}}_{t^{n+k}}\partial^{2}_{t}{v}(s)(t^{n+k}-s)ds-(k-1)\int^{t^{n}}_{t^{n+1}}\partial^{2}_{t}{v}(s)(t^{n}-s)ds.

Hence, applying the norm, using Cauchy-Schwarz and Young, on both terms yields

\displaystyle{\lVert\zeta_{n+1,k}(v)\rVert}^{2}_{V}\leq\frac{2}{3}\tau^{3}(k-1)^{3}{\lVert\partial^{2}_{t}v\rVert}^{2}_{L^{2}((t^{n+1},t^{n+k}),V)}+\frac{2}{3}(k-1)^{2}\tau^{3}{\lVert\partial^{2}_{t}v\rVert}^{2}_{L^{2}((t^{n},t^{n+1}),V)}.

Further, summing up over n and using that the integrals of the first term overlap at most
k-1
times yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}{\lVert\zeta_{n+1,k}(v)\rVert}^{2}_{V}\leq\frac{2}{3}\tau^{3}(k-1)^{2}\max((k-1)k,2){\lVert\partial^{2}_{t}v\rVert}^{2}_{L^{2}((0,T^{*}),V)},

where we further used
((k-1)^{2}+1)\leq(k-1)k
for
k\geq 2
.
The proof for
\Xi_{n+1}(v)
works analogously.
We note that smaller upper bounds can be obtained by not independently bounding the terms. However, we omit this as the constants are of minor importance.

Proof
(
Proof of
Lemma

5
)

We first show the equality

\displaystyle({\textmd{D}}^{k}v^{n+1},\delta^{k+1}v^{n+1})_{V}=

\displaystyle a({\lVert v^{n+1}\rVert}^{2}_{V}-{\lVert v^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{V})+({\lVert bv^{n+1}-cv^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{V}-{\lVert bv^{n}-cv^{n-1}\rVert}^{2}_{V})+

\displaystyle\quad+d{\lVert v^{n+1}-2v^{n}+v^{n-1}\rVert}^{2}_{V}+2{\lVert v^{n+1}-v^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{V},

where the constants
a,b,c
and
d
are given by

\displaystyle a=\frac{3}{2(k+1)},\quad c=\sqrt{k^{2}+\frac{k}{2}-\frac{1}{2}},\quad b=c^{-1}\left(k^{2}+\frac{3k}{2}-1\right),\quad\textmd{and}\quad d=c^{2}.

Expanding and collecting the terms of the left-hand side, we get

\displaystyle 4k^{2}{\lVert v^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{V}+\left(-2k^{2}+k\right){\langle v^{n-1},v^{n}\rangle}_{V}+\left(2k^{2}+k-1\right){\langle v^{n-1},v^{n+1}\rangle}_{V}+

\displaystyle+\left(-6k^{2}-5k\right){\langle v^{n},v^{n+1}\rangle}_{V}+\left(2k^{2}+3k+1\right){\lVert v^{n+1}\rVert}^{2}_{V},

while the right-hand-side becomes

\displaystyle\left(-c^{2}+d\right){\lVert v^{n-1}\rVert}^{2}_{V}+\left(-a-b^{2}+c^{2}+4d+2\right){\lVert v^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{V}+\left(2bc-4d\right){\langle v^{n-1},v^{n}\rangle}_{V}+

\displaystyle+2d{\langle v^{n-1},v^{n+1}\rangle}_{V}+\left(-2bc-4d-4\right){\langle v^{n},v^{n+1}\rangle}_{V}+\left(a+b^{2}+d+2\right){\lVert v^{n+1}\rVert}^{2}_{V}.

By comparing the individual terms, a straightforward matching shows

\displaystyle{\langle\delta^{k}v^{n+1},\delta^{k+1}v^{n+1}\rangle}_{V}=\hat{a}{\lVert\delta^{k+1}v^{n+1}\rVert}^{2}_{V}+\hat{b}{\lVert v^{n+1}+v^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{V}+\left(\hat{d}+\hat{f}\right){\lVert v^{n+1}\rVert}^{2}_{V}-\hat{d}{\lVert v^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{V},

where

\displaystyle\hat{a}=\frac{4k^{2}-1-\epsilon}{4k^{2}+4k+1},\quad\hat{b}=\frac{k+1/2+k(1-\epsilon)}{(2k+1)^{2}},\quad\hat{d}=\frac{k+\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon k}{2k+1},\quad\hat{f}=\epsilon.

Expanding the left-hand side terms yields

\displaystyle{\langle\delta^{k}v^{n+1},\delta^{k+1}v^{n+1}\rangle}_{V}={\lVert v^{n+1}\rVert}^{2}_{V}k\left(k+1\right)+{\langle v^{n+1},v^{n}\rangle}_{V}\left(1-2k^{2}\right)+{\lVert v^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{V}k\left(k-1\right),

while the right-hand side gives

\displaystyle{\lVert v^{n+1}\rVert}^{2}_{V}\left(\hat{a}k^{2}+2\hat{a}k+\hat{a}+\hat{b}+\hat{d}+\hat{f}\right)+{\langle v^{n+1},v^{n}\rangle}_{V}\left(-2\hat{a}k^{2}-2\hat{a}k+2\hat{b}\right)+{\lVert v^{n}\rVert}^{2}_{V}\left(\hat{a}k^{2}+\hat{b}-\hat{d}\right).

Again, the result follows from a one-to-one comparison of the coefficients.
