Rational Normal Curves in Weighted Projective Space
Caitlin M. Davis, Aleksandra Sobieska

Abstract
. This article aims to extend classical homological results about the rational normal curves to analogues in weighted projective spaces. Results include determinantality and nonstandard versions of quadratic generation and the Koszul property.

1.
Introduction

This article aims to extend classical homological results about the rational normal curves to their weighted analogues. The rational normal curves provide a rich source of examples of many homological properties. In particular, the defining ideals of these curves are determinantal (
[
Eis05
, Proposition 6.1]
) with a quadratic Gröbner basis (
[
Stu
,
ERT94
]
,
[
CDNR13
, Theorem 3.1.1]
), and their coordinate rings are Cohen-Macaulay and Koszul. We consider certain analogues of the rational normal curves in weighted projective spaces, and we show that (weighted variants of) all of these properties hold for these curves.

There has been great interest in recent years in extending homological properties to the nonstandard graded and multigraded settings, where we use nonstandard graded to refer to rings with a nonstandard
\mathbbm{Z}
-grading. Since the generalization by Maclagan-Smith of Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity to multigraded polynomial rings (
[
MS04
]
), there have been many results on multigraded regularity:
[
BC17
,
BHS21
,
BHS22
,
CH22
,
CN20
,
SVTW06
]
. There has also been much work on multigraded syzygies:
[
BE23
,
BE24a
,
BE24b
,
BES20
,
BKLY21
,
BS24
,
BCN22
,
Cob24
,
EES15
,
HNVT22
,
HS04
,
HSS06
,
SVT06
,
Yan21
]
. In particular, the generalization of the rational normal curve studied in this article comes from Brown and Erman in
[
BE23
]
.

Despite this interest in the multigraded setting, few geometric examples have been examined thoroughly. Existing work concerning geometric examples focuses primarily on sets of points:
[
HNVT22
,
SVT06
]
. Part of what makes the rational normal curves so important in the literature is that they can be understood from so many different perspectives. They provide an important class of examples for understanding geometric, algebraic, and homological topics, and they are useful
for testing out new definitions and conjectures. Our aim is to develop an analogous class of examples that could play a similar role for the nonstandard graded setting as that theory continues to expand. With this in mind, we will not focus on the most general possible class of analogues, but rather work to build a family that can be as fully understood as the classical rational normal curves. To be specific: the Brown-Erman construction embeds a smooth curve based on additional data
(L,D,e)
where
L
is a line bundle,
D
is a base locus divisor, and
e
is a positive integer. In this article, we study closely the two-parameter family of rational curves where
L={\mathcal{O}}(d)
varies over all line bundles and
e
varies over all positive integers, but where
D
is always simply the point at infinity. The following example illustrates some of the properties which these curves share with the usual rational normal curves.

Example 1.1
.

When
d=3
and
e=2
, the weighted rational curve (see Definition
2.1
) is obtained by the map
\psi:\mathbb{P}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,2,2)
given by
[s:t]\mapsto[s^{3}:s^{2}t:st^{2}:st^{5}:t^{6}]
. Note that the coordinate ring
R\cong\mathbbm{k}[s^{3},s^{2}t,st^{2},st^{5},t^{6}]
is not normal; for example,
\frac{st^{5}}{st^{2}}=t^{3}
is integral, but is not in
R
.

However, this curve shares many other properties with the usual rational normal curve. For instance:
R
is Cohen-Macaulay; the defining ideal

is generated by the

\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}&x_{2}^{2}&y_{0}\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&y_{0}&y_{1}\end{bmatrix};

these generators are a Gröbner basis for

with respect to some monomial order (see Section

); and

is resolved by an Eagon-Northcott complex.

In fact, the observations made in the example above hold for all weighted rational curves.

Theorem A
.

Let
R=S/I
be the coordinate ring of the weighted rational curve of type
d,e
. Then we have the following:

(1)

R
is Cohen-Macaulay (Proposition
2.4
)

(2)

I
is determinantal in the following sense:
I
is generated by the
2\times 2

2\times(d+e)
matrix, almost all of whose entries are linear (See Theorem
3.1
for a more precise version.),

(3)

R
is resolved by the Eagon-Northcott complex (Corollary
3.4
), and

(4)

I
has an initial ideal of quadratic monomials (Theorem

).

Note that (2) and (4) are the key points. Statement (3) follows from (2) and the observation that

has the expected codimension for an ideal of minors. Statement (1) was first proven in
[
BE23
]
for curves of arbitrary genus, but our methods provide an alternate proof in genus 0 which more closely parallels the algebraic proofs for rational normal curves. Ongoing work of Banks–Ramkumar adds to these parallels by showing that these weighted rational curves are of minimal degree, just as in the classical setting (cf.
[
Eis07

).

Theorem

shows how weighted rational curves broadly behave as rational normal curves do, yet it conceals the fact that the internal details are quite different.

Example 1.2

Consider the weighted rational curve with

Said another way, one could not predict this Betti table entirely from the Hilbert function, which is a contrast with what happens for rational normal curves.

Another important property of the usual rational normal curve is Koszulness. The Koszul property, normally defined as

.

Taken together, Theorems

Philosophy 1.4
.

In the study of nonstandard graded and multigraded syzygies, many classical results have multigraded analogues. However, finding these analogues can often require subtle deformations of the classical statements, techniques, or definitions. Key examples of this phenomenon can be found in
[
BES20
]
,
[
MS04
]
, and
[
BE24b
]
.

More specifically, we see how things change: in Theorem
A
(2), the linear matrix is replaced by one which is “almost” linear; in Theorem
A
(4), the Gröbner basis is no longer quadratic in the usual sense, but the initial terms remain quadratic; in Theorem
B
, classical Koszulness of
R
is replaced by Koszulness of the associated graded ring. Since we will allow the case of weighted rational curves of type
d,1
(that is, usual rational normal curves), it is also worth noting both that these changes are meaningful only when
e\neq 1
and that the proofs included here do apply to the usual rational normal curves.

This paper is organized as follows: In §
2
, we define the main object of study, the weighted rational curves. In §
3
, we prove that the defining ideal of each of these curves is determinantal. In §
4
, we establish that the generators of these ideals are in fact Gröbner bases with respect to a certain monomial order. In §
5
, we consider a nonstandard version of the Koszul property and show that it holds for the weighted rational curves.

2.
Background

As a way to understand nice embeddings into weighted projective space, Brown and Erman
[
BE23
]
describe weighted analogues of linear series and of complete linear series. Their analogue of a complete linear series is a “log complete series” and it depends on three pieces of data: a line bundle
L
, an effective divisor
D
, and a positive integer
e
. We refer the reader to Section 3 of their paper for details. As stated in the introduction, we will focus our attention on one specific case of their main definition in order to develop a deep understanding of a concrete class of examples in the nonstandard graded setting. Here we describe this special case: the case where
D
is the point at infinity, which will be the only case of interest throughout this paper.

We first recall a compact notation for certain weighted projective spaces. We use exponents to indicate the number of weights of a particular degree. For example, we write
\mathbb{P}(1^{3},2^{2})
to denote the weighted projective space
\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,2,2)
from Example
1.1
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
Z=\mathbbm{P}^{1}
,
D=[0:1]
, and
L=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(d)
for some
d\geq 1
. The corresponding log complete series for a fixed
e\geq 1
is

W={\langle s^{d},s^{d-1}t,\ldots,st^{d-1},s^{e-1}t^{de-(e-1)},\ldots st^{de-1},t^{de}\rangle}.

This induces a closed immersion
\psi_{W}\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(1^{d},e^{e})
given by

\psi_{W}\colon[s:t]\mapsto[s^{d}:s^{d-1}t:\ldots:st^{d-1}:s^{e-1}t^{de-(e-1)}:\ldots:st^{de-1}:t^{de}].

For
W
and
\psi_{W}
as above, we call the image
\psi_{W}(\mathbbm{P}^{1})
the
weighted rational curve
of type
(d,e)
.

Note that in the case that
e=1
, this recovers the usual rational normal curve of degree
d
.

Define
S=\mathbbm{k}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{d-1},y_{0},\ldots,y_{e-1}]
with the weighted grading where
\deg(x_{i})=1
and
\deg(y_{i})=e
, so that it is the coordinate ring of
\mathbbm{P}(1^{d},e^{e})
. The immersion
\psi_{W}
induces a corresponding ring map
\varphi_{W}:S\rightarrow\mathbbm{k}[s,t]
given by
x_{i}\mapsto s^{d-i}t^{i}
and
y_{i}\mapsto s^{e-1-i}t^{ed-(e-1)+i}
.

We will refer to the coordinate ring of the weighted rational curve as
R
and its defining ideal as
I=\ker\varphi_{W}
, so that
R=S/I
. While
R
and
I
typically depend on
W
, the choice of
W
will be fixed throughout the paper, and so we omit this dependence from the notation.

Example 2.2
.

Let
d=3
and
e=2
, as in Example
1.1
. In this case,
W=\langle s^{3},s^{2}t,st^{2},st^{5},t^{6}\rangle
.

The following observations follow easily from
[
BE23
]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

[
BE23
, Remark 3.15]
The coordinate ring
R
of a weighted rational curve of type
(d,e)
is not integrally closed for
e\geq 2
.

Proof.

The monomial
t^{d}
is in the field of fractions of
R
since it can be written as

t^{d}=\frac{s^{e-1}t^{de-(e-1)}}{(st^{d-1})^{e-1}}.

Furthermore,
t^{d}
is integral over
R
. In particular, it is a root of the monic polynomial
X^{e}-t^{de}
. However,
t^{d}
is not in
R
since
e\geq 2
, so
R
is not integrally closed.
∎

The above proposition implies that the weighted rational curve is not projectively normal in the usual sense. However, Brown and Erman also show that
R
is “normally generated” in the sense of their Definition 3.13, which parallels Mumford’s definition (
[
Mum70
]
), which algebraically implies the following.

Proposition 2.4
.

[
BE23
]

R
is Cohen-Macaulay.

This is a combination of Remark 3.14(1) and Theorem 1.4 in
[
BE23
]
. We will offer an alternative proof of Cohen–Macaulayness in Corollary
3.5
.

3.
Determinantality

In this section, we show that the weighted rational curve is defined by the maximal minors of a certain matrix, as in the case of the usual rational normal curve. In the case of the rational normal curve, this is a classical result; see
[
Eis05
, cf. Proposition 6.1]
for a proof. In line with Philosophy
1.4
, we will see how the classical result gets deformed: in the case of a (classical) rational normal curve of degree
d
, the minors come from a
2\times d
linear matrix; in the type
d,e
weighted case, we have a
2\times(d+e-1)
matrix, all of whose entries are linear, with a single exception. To be precise, we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
I_{2}
be the ideal of
S
generated by the
2\times 2
minors of the following
2\times(d+e-1
) matrix:

(1)

M=\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}&\ldots&x_{d-2}&x_{d-1}^{e}&y_{0}&\ldots&y_{e-2}\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&\ldots&x_{d-1}&y_{0}&y_{1}&\ldots&y_{e-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Recall that
I
is the defining ideal of the weighted rational curve of type
d,e
.
Then
I=I_{2}
.

Note that for any column of
M
, the ratio of the entries after applying
\varphi
(recall that
\varphi
was defined in Section
2
) is
\frac{t}{s}
. Thus, each minor of
M
maps to
0
under
\varphi
, so we know that
I_{2}\subseteq I
. To prove that these ideals are in fact equal, we will prove that the Hilbert series of
S/I_{2}
is equal to that of
S/I
. This relies on the following two results.

Proposition 3.2
.

The Hilbert function and Hilbert series of
R
are

h_{R}(a)=da+1-a\bmod e\ \text{ and }\ HS_{R}(z)=\frac{1+(d-1)(z+z^{2}+\ldots+z^{e})+(e-1)z^{e}}{(1-z)(1-z^{e})}.

Proposition 3.3
.

The Hilbert series of
S/I_{2}
is:

HS_{S/I_{2}}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{d}(1-z^{e})^{e}}\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}(-1)^{k}kz^{k+1}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}{{d-1}\choose{k+1-i}}{e\choose i}z^{i(e-1)}\right).

Before proving these two propositions, we will show how they combine to yield a proof of Theorem
3.1
, the main result of this section. The difficulty lies in proving that the two Hilbert series above are, in fact, equal. In particular, as we observed in Example
1.2
, the Hilbert series for
S/I_{2}
has redundancy which is obscured in that for
R
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Since
I_{2}\subseteq I
, there is a surjection
S/I_{2}\twoheadrightarrow R
. If this map is an isomorphism in each degree, then it is an isomorphism of rings. To show this, we will argue that
S/I
and
S/I_{2}
have the same Hilbert series.

In the Hilbert series for
S/I_{2}
, we can rewrite the double sum by substituting in
k+1-i+i-1
for
k
:

\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{k}((k+1-i)+i-1)\binom{{d-1}}{{k+1-i}}z^{k+1-i}\binom{e}{i}(z^{e})^{i}.

Distributing over
(k+1-i)+i-1
splits this sum into the following three pieces, each of which we will handle separately:

\displaystyle HS_{S/I_{2}}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{d}(1-z^{e})^{e}}\Bigg(1

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{k}(k+1-i)\binom{{d-1}}{{k+1-i}}z^{k+1-i}\binom{e}{i}(z^{e})^{i}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{k}i\binom{d-1}{k+1-i}z^{k+1-i}\binom{e}{i}(z^{e})^{i}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{k+1}\binom{d-1}{k+1-i}z^{k+1-i}\binom{e}{i}(z^{e})^{i}\Bigg).

We will refer to the three sums as
p_{1}(z)
,
p_{2}(z)
, and
p_{3}(z)
, respectively.
The first sum,
p_{1}(z)
, can be handled as follows:

\displaystyle p_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{k}(k+1-i)\binom{{d-1}}{{k+1-i}}z^{k+1-i}\binom{e}{i}(z^{e})^{i}.

By the identity
(k+1-i)\binom{d-1}{k+1-i}=(d-1)\binom{d-2}{k-i}
and dropping the non-contributing
i=k+1
term, this becomes:

\displaystyle=(d-1)z\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{k}\binom{d-2}{k-i}z^{k-i}\binom{e}{i}(z^{e})^{i}.

By splitting
(-1)^{k}
across
z^{k-i}
and
(z^{e})^{i}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle=(d-1)z\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-z)^{k-i}\binom{d-2}{k-i}(-z^{e})^{i}\binom{e}{i}.

Finally, observe that, if the outer sum started at
k=0
, the double sum would be
(1-z)^{d-2}(1-z^{e})^{e}
on the nose. So the sum becomes:

\displaystyle=(d-1)z\left[(1-z)^{d-2}(1-z^{e})^{e}-1\right].

By a similar argument (now using that
i\binom{e}{i}=e\binom{e-1}{i-1})
), we can rewrite
p_{2}(z)
:

\displaystyle p_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{k}i\binom{d-1}{k+1-i}z^{k+1-i}\binom{e}{i}(z^{e})^{i}

\displaystyle=ez^{e}\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-z)^{k-i}\binom{d-1}{k-i}(-z^{e})^{i}\binom{e-1}{i}

\displaystyle=ez^{e}\left[(1-z)^{d-1}(1-z^{e})^{e-1}-1\right].

Similarly, for
p_{3}(z)
we have:

\displaystyle p_{3}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d+e-2}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{k+1}\binom{d-1}{k+1-i}z^{k+1-i}\binom{e}{i}(z^{e})^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{d+e-1}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-z)^{k-i}\binom{d-1}{k-i}(-z^{e})^{i}\binom{e}{i}

\displaystyle=(1-z)^{d-1}(1-z^{e})^{e}-(1-(d-1)z-ez^{e}).

Putting this together and canceling appropriately, we have

\displaystyle HS_{S/I_{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{d}(1-z^{e})^{e}}\left(1+p_{1}(z)+p_{2}(z)+p_{3}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-1)z(1-z)^{d-2}(1-z^{e})^{e}+ez^{e}(1-z)^{d-1}(1-z^{e})^{e-1}+(1-z)^{d-1}(1-z^{e})^{e}}{(1-z)^{d}(1-z^{e})^{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+(d-1)(z+z^{2}+\ldots+z^{e})+(e-1)z^{e}}{(1-z)(1-z^{e})}.

This is equal to
HS_{S/I}(z)
, completing the proof.
∎

3.2.
Proofs of Propositions
3.2
and
3.3

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

We directly compute the size of the monomial basis for a given degree. It will be convenient to view
R
as the semigroup algebra

\mathbbm{k}[\Lambda]=\mathbbm{k}[s^{d},s^{d-1}t,\ldots,st^{d-1},s^{e-1}t^{de-e+1},s^{e-2}t^{de-e+2},\ldots,st^{de-1},t^{de}].

Note that, by the usual normalization from the degree-
d
Veronese map, monomials in
\mathbbm{k}[\Lambda]
of total degree
da
correspond to monomials in
R
of degree
a
.

By virtue of the monomials in the log complete series, every monomial
s^{u}t^{k\cdot de-u}
is in
R
as in the usual rational normal curve, so
\dim_{\mathbbm{k}}(R)_{qe}=dqe+1
. To compute
\dim_{\mathbbm{k}}R_{qe+r}

Gröbner bases

The matrix minors which define the usual rational normal curve are in fact a Gröbner basis for the defining ideal with respect to a graded reverse lexicographic order (

,

]

.

Recall that

is the defining ideal of the weighted rational curve of type

Remark 4.2

5.
Koszulness

The coordinate ring of the usual rational normal curve is Koszul (
[
Stu
,
ERT94
]
,
[
CDNR13
, Theorem 3.1.1]
). That is, the minimal graded free resolution of
\mathbbm{k}
over this ring is linear. In the case of a weighted rational curve, the resolution of
\mathbbm{k}
over
R
cannot possibly be linear, at least in the traditional sense. For instance, the first steps of the resolution will look like:

0\leftarrow\mathbbm{k}\leftarrow R\leftarrow[R(-1)^{d}\oplus R(-e)^{e}]\leftarrow\cdots

Nonetheless, it turns out that
R
satisfies a generalized notion of Koszulness, introduced in
[
HRW98
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

The ring
(R,{\mathfrak{m}})
is nonstandard Koszul.

Before defining “nonstandard Koszul,” we motivate the definition and result by examining the resolution of
\mathbbm{k}
over
R
. Although not linear, this resolution appears well-behaved upon computational inspection and has a linear part which resolves
\mathbbm{k}
over a related ring.

Example 5.2
.

Take

and

\mathbbm{k}
over

\begin{bmatrix}-x_{1}&-x_{2}&-y_{0}&-y_{1}&0&0&0&0&0&0&0&0&-y_{0}&0&0&0\\

Naively taking only the linear entries of the above maps gives a resolution of

In other words, the resolution of
\mathbbm{k}

\begin{bmatrix}-x_{1}&-x_{2}&-y_{0}&-y_{1}&0&0&0&0&0&0&0&0&-y_{0}&0&0&0\\

This matrix is almost identical to the one above: the only changed entries are designated in bold, which are the ones previously containing

However, this new map is linear with respect to the standard grading.

Additionally, we have seen that it is possible to take an initial ideal of

which is “quadratic” in the sense that it is generated by twofold products of variables, though these are not degree-
2
terms in our grading. Taken together, these two observations suggest another instance of Philosophy
1.4
here. Namely, the coordinate rings of weighted rational curves satisfy a Koszul property, but to understand this analogue, we need to deform the notion of Koszulness (in the manner of
[

Definition 5.3
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{n})
be a (graded) local ring. The
associated graded algebra

\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{n}}(A)
of
A
with respect to the ideal
\mathfrak{n}
is the graded ring

A/\mathfrak{n}\oplus\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^{2}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{2}/\mathfrak{n}^{3}\oplus\cdots.

For any element

of

, the
initial form of

f
, written
\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{n}}(f)
, is the image of
f
in
\mathfrak{n}^{j}/\mathfrak{n}^{j+1}
where

is the greatest number such that

. For an ideal

of

\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{n}}(J)
is the ideal of
\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{n}}(A)
generated by

.

Definition 5.4
.

(
[
HRW98
, cf. Definition 5.1]
)
A ring

which is either Noetherian local or
\mathbbm{N}^{d}
-graded is

if its associated graded ring

Note that if

is standard graded, then

\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{n}}A\cong A
.

As a first example where

Example 5.5

Let

Theorem 5.6

The

x_{1}&x_{2}&\ldots&x_{d-1}&y_{0}&y_{1}&\ldots&y_{e-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Theorem 5.7
.

The associated graded algebra
{\operatorname{gr_{\mathfrak{m}}}(R)}
is isomorphic to
S/I_{0}
, where
S
has the standard grading.

Proof.

We know that
\operatorname{gr_{\mathfrak{m}}}(S/I)=\operatorname{gr_{\mathfrak{m}}}S/\operatorname{in}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(I)
. We also know that
\operatorname{gr_{\mathfrak{m}}}S
is
S
with the standard grading. Therefore, we need to show that
S/\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)=S/I_{0}
.

We will first argue that
I_{0}\subseteq\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)
. It suffices to show that each generator of
I_{0}
is the initial form of an element of
I
. In fact, it turns out that every generator of
I_{0}
is the initial form of a minor of
M
. The initial forms of these minors are:

•

\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{i}x_{j+1}-x_{j}x_{i+1})=x_{i}x_{j+1}-x_{j}x_{i+1}
,

•

\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{i}y_{j+1}-y_{j}x_{i+1})=x_{i}y_{j+1}-y_{j}x_{i+1}
,

•

\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{m}}(y_{i}y_{j+1}-y_{j}y_{i+1})=y_{i}y_{j+1}-y_{j}y_{i+1}
,

•

\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{i}y_{0}-x_{d-1}^{e}x_{i+1})=x_{i}y_{0}
, and

•

\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{m}}(x_{d-1}^{e}y_{i+1}-y_{0}y_{i})=-y_{0}y_{i}
.

Each generator of
I_{0}
is of one of these forms, so
I_{0}\subseteq\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)
. This means that we have a surjection
S/I_{0}\twoheadrightarrow S/\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)
. To see that this is in fact an isomorphism, we will argue that
S/I_{0}
and
S/\operatorname{in}_{\mathfrak{m}}(I)
have the same Hilbert series.

To compute
HS_{S/I_{0}}(z)
, consider
S^{\prime}=S[v]/I_{v}
where
S[v]
is now standard graded, and
I_{v}
is again the ideal generated by
2\times 2
-minors of
M_{v}
. We will make use of the isomorphism
S/I_{0}\cong S^{\prime}/{\langle v\rangle}
. Since
S^{\prime}
is an integral domain, we have the following short exact sequence:

0\longleftarrow S/I_{0}\longleftarrow S^{\prime}\longleftarrow S^{\prime}\longleftarrow 0,

where the rightmost map is multiplication by
v
. Since Hilbert series are additive over short exact sequences, we have
HS_{S/I_{0}}(z)=(1-z)HS_{S^{\prime}}(z)
.

We know the Hilbert series of
S^{\prime}
since
S^{\prime}
is the coordinate ring of a rational normal scroll (
[
MS20
, Proposition 3.6]
):

HS_{S^{\prime}}(z)=\frac{1+(d+e-2)z}{(1-z)^{3}}.

Then we have

HS_{S/I_{0}}(z)=\frac{1+(d+e-2)z}{(1-z)^{2}},

which is the Hilbert series of
{\operatorname{gr_{\mathfrak{m}}}(R)}
.
∎

We recall some further definitions from
[
HRW98
, p. 56]
.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
A
be a Noetherian standard
\mathbb{N}
-graded
\mathbbm{k}
-algebra. The
multiplicity
of
A
is
e(A)\coloneqq P(1)
where the Hilbert series of
A
is written as
HS_{A}(z)=\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}
with
\gcd(P(z),Q(z))=1
. For an
\mathbb{N}^{d}
-graded ring
(A,\mathfrak{n})
, the
multiplicity
of
A
,
e(A)
, is defined to be
e(\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{n}}(A))
. By a result from
[
Abh67
]
,
e(A)\geq\dim_{\mathbbm{k}}\mathfrak{n}/\mathfrak{n}^{2}-\dim A+1
if
A
is Cohen–Macaulay. If equality holds, then
A
has
minimal multiplicity
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

We will apply Theorem 5.2 of
[
HRW98
]
. We know
R
is Cohen–Macaulay, so we need to show it has minimal multiplicity. We have

HS_{\operatorname{gr_{\mathfrak{m}}}(R)}(z)=\frac{1+(d+e-2)z}{(1-z)^{2}}.

In the notation from above,
P(z)=1+(d+e-2)z
, so
e(R)=d+e-1
. One can check that the rank of the Jacobian
J
is
0
at the origin and use
\dim_{\mathbbm{k}}{\mathfrak{m}}/{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}=(d+e)-\operatorname{rank}J|_{0}
(
[
Har77
, Theorem 5.1]
) to see that
\dim_{\mathbbm{k}}{\mathfrak{m}}/{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}=d+e
. Therefore, we have

\dim_{\mathbbm{k}}{\mathfrak{m}}/{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}-\dim R+1=(d+e)-2+1=d+e-1.

So
R
has minimal multiplicity, and therefore is nonstandard Koszul by
[
HRW98
, Theorem 5.2]
.
∎

We note that there is an alternative proof of Theorem

The minors of
M_{0}
form a Gröbner basis for
I_{0}
with respect to the term order
<_{\mathbf{w}}

Proof.

Note that whether a set of generators is a Gröbner basis does not depend on grading, so we can consider
I

as ideals of the same ring. As in Theorem

, let
G={\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell}\}}

M
, and let
J={\langle\operatorname{in}_{<_{\mathbf{w}}}g_{1},\ldots,\operatorname{in}_{<_{\mathbf{w}}}g_{\ell}\rangle}

We know that

Proposition 5.14
.

If
{\mathbf{F}}
is the minimal free resolution of
\mathbbm{k}[v]
over the coordinate ring
S^{\prime}
of the scroll
{\mathcal{S}}(d-1,e)
, then
{\mathbf{F}}\otimes_{S^{\prime}}R
gives a resolution of
\mathbbm{k}
over
R
. Similarly,
{\mathbf{F}}\otimes_{S^{\prime}}{\operatorname{gr_{\mathfrak{m}}}(R)}
gives a resolution of
\mathbbm{k}
over
{\operatorname{gr_{\mathfrak{m}}}(R)}
.

Proof.

As observed in the proof of Proposition
3.3
,
R
can be viewed as the quotient
S[v]/(I_{v}+{\langle v-x_{d-1}^{e}\rangle})
, where
I_{v}
is the ideal of
2\times 2
minors of

M_{v}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}&\ldots&x_{d-2}&v&y_{0}&\ldots&y_{e-2}\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&\ldots&x_{d-1}&y_{0}&y_{1}&\ldots&y_{e-1}\end{bmatrix},

while
S^{\prime}\coloneqq S[v]/I_{v}
is simply the coordinate ring of the scroll
{\mathcal{S}}(d-1,e)
. Note that with this notation,
R\cong S^{\prime}/{\langle v-x_{d-1}^{e}\rangle}
. We will also use
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
to denote the sets of variables
x_{0},\ldots,x_{d-1}
and
y_{0},\ldots,y_{e-1}
respectively.

Let
{\mathbf{F}}
be the minimal free resolution of
S^{\prime}/{\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle}
over
S^{\prime}
, which can be obtained as a variant of the resolution of
S^{\prime}/{\langle{\mathbf{x}},v,{\mathbf{y}}\rangle}
over
S^{\prime}
presented in
[
MS20
, Section 4]
. More precisely, one can construct the resolutions of
{\langle{\mathbf{x}}\rangle}
and
{\langle{\mathbf{y}}\rangle}
using the same blocks shown in
[
MS20
]
, then zip them together with a mapping cone for a resolution of
{\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle}
, where the chain maps follow the same pattern with
y_{0}
playing the role of
v
. We will show that
{\mathbf{F}}\otimes_{S^{\prime}}S^{\prime}/{\langle v-x_{d-1}^{e}\rangle}
is also exact, and thus resolves

as desired.

To this end, we check
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{S^{\prime}}(S^{\prime}/{\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle},S^{\prime}/{\langle v-x_{d-1}^{e}\rangle})
. Because
\operatorname{Tor}
is balanced, this can be computed from
S^{\prime}/{\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle}\otimes_{S^{\prime}}{\mathbf{G}}
, where
{\mathbf{G}}:0\leftarrow S^{\prime}\xleftarrow{\cdot v-x_{d-1}^{e}}S^{\prime}\leftarrow 0
is the resolution of
S^{\prime}/{\langle v-x_{d-1}^{e}\rangle}
over
S^{\prime}
. Because
S^{\prime}/{\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle}\cong\mathbbm{k}[v]
,

is simply

, which only has homology at the

is injective. Therefore,

is exact except at the zeroth spot, where its homology is

, as desired.

The same argument, using

, gives the second statement.

An immediate consequence of the Poincaré series in Corollary
5.11
is the fact that
R
is Golod.

Corollary 5.15
.

The ring
R
is Golod.

Proof.

With the computations already done, it is straightforward to check the defining inequality for Golodness:

\operatorname{Poin}_{\mathbbm{k}}^{R}(z)=\frac{(1+z)^{d+e}}{1-z^{2}\operatorname{Poin}^{S}_{I}(z)}.

The Betti numbers for the resolution of
I
over
S
are given by a coarsened (shift of) the Eagon–Northcott count from Corollary
3.4
:
\beta_{i}^{S}(I)=(i+1)\binom{d+e-1}{i+2}
for
i=0,\ldots,d+e-3
. We can thus compute:

\begin{array}[]{ll}1-z^{2}\operatorname{Poin}^{S}_{I}(z)&=1-z^{2}\left(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d+e-3}(i+1)\binom{d+e-1}{i+2}z^{i}\right)\\
&=1-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{d+e-1}(i-1)\binom{d+e-1}{i}z^{i}\\
&=1-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{d+e-1}i\binom{d+e-1}{i}z^{i}+\sum\limits_{i=2}^{d+e-1}\binom{d+e-1}{i}z^{i}\\
&=1-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{d+e-1}(d+e-1)\binom{d+e-2}{i-1}z^{i}+\left[(1+z)^{d+e-1}-1-(d+e-1)z\right]\\
&=-(d+e-1)z\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d+e-2}\binom{d+e-2}{i}z^{i}+(1+z)^{d+e-1}-(d+e-1)z\\
&=-(d+e-1)z[(1+z)^{d+e-2}-1]+(1+z)^{d+e-1}-(d+e-1)z\\
&=(1+z)^{d+e-2}[1-(d+e-2)z]\end{array}

The equality follows immediately.
∎
