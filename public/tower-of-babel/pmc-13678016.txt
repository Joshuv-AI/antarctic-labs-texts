What do we talk about when we talk about spatial determinants of health? A systematic review
Authors: Predrag Duric, Smiljana Rajcevic, Jelena Djekic Malbasa, Danica Mulic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSpatial determinants of health are increasingly recognised as important because the uneven distribution of risks, resources, and exposures across geographic space shapes health outcomes and inequalities, highlighting the need for consistent definitions and operationalisation in public health. The objective of this systematic review is to synthesise how spatial determinants of health (SpDoH) are defined, what their components are, and how they are conceptually positioned relative to other health determinants in the published literature.Study designSystematic review.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in EBSCOhost (including CINAHL, MEDLINE and PsycInfo databases), Scopus, and PubMed on 1 September 2025, supplemented by citation chaining and targeted Google Scholar searches. Eligible studies included peer-reviewed publications addressing human health or related topics including empirical studies and non-empirical formats such as editorials, commentaries, letters, conference papers, and book chapters. Inclusion criteria required explicit discussion of SpDoH or related concepts and/or description of their components and/or relationships with other health determinants. Studies were excluded if SpDoH could not be clearly distinguished as a separate determinant. Due to the conceptual nature of the field, no formal risk-of-bias tool was applied; instead, publication type and source were used as proxies for evidential robustness. A total of 59 publications were included, and data were synthesised narratively. The study was not registered in any registries. It was funded by the Provintial Secretariat for Higher Education and Scientific Research of the Autonomous Province of Vojvodina, Serbia.ResultsOnly three (5.1%) studies explicitly defined spatial determinants of health. Spatial factors were most often embedded within social, environmental, or economic determinants rather than treated as a distinct category. In some cases, they were conceptualised as geographic variation rather than determinants with inherent spatial properties. Three key domains were identified: spatial structures and urban configuration, accessibility, mobility and connectivity, and neighbourhood context. However, substantial heterogeneity in terminology and framing indicated a lack of conceptual coherence across the literature. The review was limited to academic, English-language sources, potentially excluding relevant grey literature and non-English conceptual work. Variability and implicit reporting of SpDoH across studies constrained extraction and synthesis, likely underrepresenting spatial factors not explicitly framed as such and contributing to observed heterogeneity.ConclusionsThe absence of consistent definitions and frameworks for SpDoH limits cumulative knowledge development, comparability across studies, and translation into policy and practice. Clearer conceptualisation of SpDoH as a distinct domain may support improved measurement, theory development, and integration into public health research.Keywords: Spatial determinants of health, Determinants of health, Public health, Conceptual framework, Social determinants of health

Introduction

In epidemiology, space was initially conceptualised as the result of interactions between organisms and nature, independent of human perception. [1] Classical geography treated space as the substrate of natural phenomena, including climate, topography, and vegetation, while later work challenged approaches restricted to biological dimensions and generic and structural determinants, emphasising the individual and subjective dimensions of space and its influence on health. [1] Public health, particularly epidemiology, seeks to identify a geographical area within which the health–disease process can be understood broadly, including its social determinants of health, while aiming to establish the most homogeneous scale possible. [2]

Debates around the concept of space often link it to materialism, production and capitalism, power, knowledge, representation, performativity, and hyperreality. Modern developments, such as changes in production, mobility, and information technologies, have contributed to intense time–space compression, creating new experiences of space and time. [3] These transformations in how space is experienced and organised have implications for human health, as they shape environmental exposures, mobility patterns, and access to health-related resources, which, in turn, influence health outcomes and equity in service utilisation. [4]

Space and place are sometimes used interchangeably in public health research. However, while we acknowledge the ongoing debates about how space and place should be defined and what distinguishes them, in the simplest terms, space may be regarded as a dimension within which matter is located, or as a grid within which substantive entities are contained, whereas place, as a formalised subset of space, refers to a physical location or its occupation within space, shaped by wider relationships, local social structures, and people's experiences and well-being within it. [5]

The World Health Organization (WHO) conceptualises the spaces to which people have access as part of social infrastructure, enabling them to participate fully in social, civic and economic life without barriers and highlights the evidence that indicates that government investment in social infrastructure can improve living standards and health outcomes, while also helping to reduce poverty and inequality. [6] In this context, spatial inequality analysis has emerged as an increasingly important tool for understanding how the determinants of health equity are geographically distributed, clustered and compounded, and how they interact across places, [6] such as the presence and accessibility of natural environments and green spaces which provide important ecosystem services that support human health and well-being. 7] Spatial determinants of health (SpDoH) and health equity require greater recognition and understanding, underpinned by robust health literacy and effective leadership. [8] This is essential to ensure that these issues are appropriately prioritised within political and policy agendas and translated into meaningful action.

The role of space in health and disease has been studied since ancient times, beginning with Hippocrates' description of the environmental effects of air, water, and places on individuals and populations. [2,9,10] This was followed by John Snow's mapping of cholera cases in London in 1854 to investigate its causality, with research continuing throughout the 20th century on the role of space in infectious disease transmission and the relationship between disease and the geographical, physical, and biological characteristics of places. [2,9,10]

Spatial perspectives in health research examine population-level variation in risk exposure, health status, healthcare provision and utilisation, employing qualitative, quantitative, and geographic information system (GIS) methods. [9] Dynamic mapping is opening up new possibilities for space–time analysis in epidemiology, while advances in digital technologies continue to enhance the collection, processing, visualisation, and analysis of spatio-temporal data. [10] This interdisciplinary approach involves physicians, geographers, social scientists, epidemiologists and public health experts, as well as healthcare institutions that use regional data to identify spatial disparities in health and guide interventions. [9]

More recently, research has moved beyond simplistic associations between health and physical environments towards explaining why place matters, including the roles of social context, lived experience, and access to infrastructure. [11] Incorporating a spatial perspective into health research enables researchers to examine determinants that extend beyond individual genetic, biological, and behavioural factors. GIS, spatial data, and analytical techniques can be used alongside the increasingly available information on social and built environments to investigate previously unanswered questions. [11]

Determinants of health represent an important effort to highlight the factors that influence health maintenance, disease progression, treatment outcomes, and the inequalities that may emerge over time. The study of health determinants began in the 1970s, driven by the need to understand health beyond a purely biological perspective. [12] By the late 20th and early 21st centuries, the focus had shifted toward social determinants of health (SDoH). [13,14] The proposed SDoH framework drew broad attention to these determinants and initiated significant research and policy activities aimed at either better understanding the causes of health inequalities or proposing solutions. As the growing recognition of structural and socioeconomic inequalities has highlighted the vulnerabilities of a depoliticised SDoH framework, [[15], [16], [17], [18]] there has been increased interest in other ‘non-SDoH’ determinants of health (or 'super-SDoH' or 'determinants od determinants'). The main purpose of these initiatives, including the definition and investigation of political, commercial, and digital determinants of health, among others, may be interpreted as a need to expand the SDoH framework by including or further highlighting other factors that are relevant to health and that were either relatively neglected until recently in public health research (e.g. political and commercial determinants) or whose role was not sufficiently conceptualised within a broader framework (e.g. ecological and environmental determinants). [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]

Spatial determinants of health have emerged as a concept relatively recently, perhaps influenced by these dynamics and by growing interest in the fact that health risks, resources, and exposures are unevenly distributed across geographic space. These spatial differences influence access to services, environmental exposures, mobility patterns, and neighbourhood conditions, all of which can shape health outcomes and health inequities. [[26], [27], [28]] While research on the relationship between health and space is not new, defining spatial influences as determinants of health and positioning them within a broader public health framework, particularly in relation to SDoH and other determinants, is relatively recent. Although some studies have considered geographic differences in incidence and mortality, a systematic theoretical discussion of the links between space and health determinants remains lacking [9]. The lack of consensus on the definition and classification of determinants of health limits the ability to identify research priorities and develop targeted interventions. [29] Clear definitions of determinants of health are essential for improving health response and research aimed at reducing health inequalities and achieving better health outcomes for people of all ages. [30]

According to Lilford, long-term conceptual coherence requires harmonisation of definitions across countries, although the need for such harmonisation varies depending on the intended purpose. [31] While Lilford was specifically discussing definitions of slums, the same logic may apply to definitions of SpDoH. Local policy, management, and advocacy may not demand a common definition; however, a shared conceptualisation is highly desirable for scientific studies of spatial determinants of health, for evaluating interventions across countries, and for measuring international progress in improving health equity. Consistent standards are essential for meaningful cross-country comparisons. [31] Such harmonisation could be supported through consensus-based conceptual statements, clearer reporting standards requiring authors to specify how spatial determinants are defined and operationalised, and explicit differentiation between spatial determinants and spatial analyses of other determinants.

The study of space and health has moved beyond the traditional boundaries of health geography, with spatial perspectives increasingly integrated into disciplines such as public health, epidemiology and health economics, and this broader approach uses diverse methods and data to examine population health, healthcare provision and utilisation, allowing researchers and institutions to identify spatial inequalities and areas of need for targeted interventions. [9] Given the growing recognition that spatial factors influence health, but the limited conceptualisation, our study aims to systematically review how SpDoH are defined and positioned relative to other health determinants in the literature. We focus on three objectives: (i) identifying explicit definitions of SpDoH, (ii) examining how these determinants are conceptually linked to other categories of health determinants, and (iii) synthesising the main domains through which spatial factors influence health. By highlighting both the presence and absence of explicit definitions, our review provides a structured foundation for conceptual clarity and supports future research, policy, and practice.

Importantly, our study does not aim to define spatial epidemiology, as recently attempted in related work. [32] Instead, we focus specifically on the language, definitions, and conceptual framing of spatial determinants of health in the literature, examining how they are described, classified, and related to other determinants.

Methods

Search strategy and study selection

To explore the conceptualisation of SpDoH, a systematic review was conducted following PRISMA 2020 guidelines. [33] The review team comprised three reviewers, all engaged in each stage of the review.

Literature searches were performed in EBSCOhost (including CINAHL, MEDLINE and PsycInfo databases), Scopus, and PubMed. Detailed search strategies are provided in Table 1. The final search was conducted on 1 September 2025. No date restrictions were applied. Records without an English full text were excluded at the full-text screening stage.

Search results were imported into Rayyan, a web-based systematic review tool designed to support screening and deduplication. Two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts to ensure retrieval of relevant studies; for sources without abstracts, executive summaries or tables of contents were used (Supplementary File A). To ensure consistent application of the inclusion criteria and improve inter-rater reliability, the two primary reviewers initially screened a random sample of 10 records based on title and abstract. Any discrepancies were discussed in detail, and consensus was reached on the precise interpretation of the inclusion criteria. At this stage, all eligible publications that mentioned SpDoH (or its synonyms, e.g. spatial ‘drivers’, ‘factors’, ‘aspects’ of health) in the title or abstract were considered eligible for full-text review. Following this calibration, the reviewers independently screened the remaining titles and abstracts. Any emerging discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted if necessary. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were assessed, and authors were contacted when access was not available.

Additional publications were identified through backward citation chaining (i.e. examining the reference lists of the included publications) and searches of Google Scholar (the first ten pages of results) (Supplementary File B).

Eligible studies included peer-reviewed publications addressing human health or related topics (e.g. pandemic resilience, breastfeeding practices, air pollution), including empirical studies, editorials, commentaries, letters, conference papers, and book chapters. Inclusion criteria were:

Studies were excluded if SpDoH could not be clearly distinguished as a separate determinant (e.g. “socio-spatial” determinants, “spatial environmental” determinants, etc.), (Supplementary File C).

Data extraction

A coding template in Microsoft Excel recorded author(s), year, publication type, study design, country of corresponding author, SpDoH definitions, components, and relationships with other determinants. Verbatim definitions, component discussions, and descriptions of relationships with social or other determinants were extracted.

For full-text review, the two primary reviewers initially screened a sample of 10 publications to ensure consistent application of the inclusion criteria, which at this stage focused on the presence of explicit or clearly implied definitions of SpDoH, descriptions of their components, or discussion of relationships with other health determinants. Any discrepancies in interpretation were discussed, and consensus was reached on how to apply the criteria consistently across all studies. The remaining full texts were then independently screened. Any further discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted as necessary. The template was refined iteratively to generate themes and domains. Results were reported in line with the PRISMA 2020 checklist.

Definitions of SpDoH, components (types) of SpDoH and descriptions of their relationships with other determinants of health were extracted when explicitly stated, i.e. identified by the authors as SpDoH or when it was clear from the authors' wording that they were treated as such. Explicit definitions were considered as those where authors directly referred to a concept as a spatial determinant of health, provided a formal definition, or described the construct in terms of components that could be unambiguously linked to health outcomes. Where such statements were not provided, closely related formulations or clearly implied descriptions in the authors' text were recorded, without extending beyond the scope of the authors' expressed interpretations. Closely related formulations were those that referenced spatial characteristics or processes impacting health, in language clearly proximal to SpDoH, but not formally labelled as such. This conservative extraction strategy was adopted to avoid imposing external definitions of SpDoH and to preserve fidelity to authors' explicit conceptualisations. A less conservative strategy may risk including reviewer-imposed interpretations, reducing validity and transparency.

Data analysis

While the definitions of SpDoH and the relationships with other DoH were analysed narratively, we used an inductive thematic analysis to identify and organise the components of SpDoH reported across the included publications. The analysis was guided by Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase framework, which provides a systematic yet flexible approach to qualitative analysis. [34] We first familiarised ourselves with the material and then generated initial codes.

During the initial coding phase, we extracted all components explicitly identified in the publications (see Supplementary File E). To minimise redundancy and prevent the resulting framework from becoming unnecessarily extensive, components that were closely related or substantially overlapping were subsequently merged (e.g., different aspect of access to healthcare). We then organised the resulting components into conceptually coherent themes, grouping those that reflected similar underlying concepts, mechanisms or pathways through which they may influence health (e.g., different geographic dimensions). In a subsequent stage, related themes were consolidated into broader domains where appropriate, thereby reducing conceptual overlap, enhancing analytical clarity, and capturing higher-order patterns within the SpDoH.

Quality assessment

As SpDoH is an emerging conceptual field, conventional risk-of-bias tools were considered unsuitable, as the primary aim was not to evaluate outcomes but to map and synthesise conceptualisations. [35] Rather than assessing methodological quality, the review focused on capturing the range of conceptualisations and explicitly stated relationships. Publication type and source were used as a proxy for evidence reliability.

The study was not registered in any registry. It is part of a series of systematic reviews that aim to identify definitions, components, and relationships among different determinants of health.

Results

Overview of included publications

The database search retrieved 190 records. Following screening of titles and abstracts, 64 articles were selected for full-text review. Application of the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria resulted in 55 eligible publications. A further 25 records were identified through citation chaining and targeted Google Scholar searches. After deduplication and assessment for eligibility, a total of 59 publications were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1, Supplementary File D).

The temporal distribution of the included literature indicates a marked growth in publication activity over time. Most studies were published recently, with 41 publications (69.5%) appearing between 2020 and September 2025, when the final search was conducted. A further 17 publications (28.8%) were published between 2010 and 2019. Earlier work was scarce, with only one publication (1.7%) dating back to before 2010 (Fig. 2). This distribution likely reflects both broader recent interest in spatial determinants of health, the expansion of spatially informed health research, and the influence of social determinants of health frameworks, rather than a bias in search or selection. Older studies may also be less accessible or not indexed in the databases used.

Publication characteristics

The majority of included publications were original research articles (53 publications; 89.8%). The remaining publications consisted of two reviews (3.4%), one framework paper (1.7%), one book chapter (1.7%), one editorial (1.7%) and one conference paper (1.7%). This distribution suggests that research engaging directly with the conceptual foundations of SpDoH remains comparatively limited, with definitional and theoretical clarification receiving less explicit attention than empirical applications (Table 2).

Definitions of spatial determinants of health

Only studies that explicitly or clearly implied SpDoH definitions were included; this review does not impose external definitions (e.g. defining spatial epidemiology). Only three publications (5.1%) provided a definition of SpDoH. Davies et al. conceptualise these factors as area-level determinants, [36] whereas Pilkington et al. frame them as contextual determinants spanning multiple dimensions, including contributors to spatial inequalities and the geographic patterning of all-cause mortality at the departmental (subnational) scale. [37] By contrast, Pandey et al. is the only publication to provide a formal definition, describing SpDoH as “social, infrastructural, and environmental features of a place that shape infectious disease”. [38]

Components of spatial determinants of health

The thematic structure presented here reflects how the authors of the included studies conceptualised, defined, and operationalised spatial determinants, rather than an externally imposed classification. Domains, themes, and specific SpDoH components were derived inductively from the reviewed publications. Decisions were made during the synthesis process to enhance coherence while aiming to minimise subjective interpretation. The identified specific SpDoH represent only those explicitly named or clearly implied in the authors’ text.

All included publications reported at least one component or illustrative example of a spatial determinant of health (Supplementary File E). Across the reviewed studies, a total of 84 distinct topics were identified, organised into 18 thematic categories, and consolidated into six overarching domains plus a residual “Other” category: Geographic and environmental context; Spatial structure and urban configuration; Accessibility, mobility and connectivity; Socio-demographic and economic context; Residential and neighbourhood context; Healthcare context; Other (Table 3). Geographic and environmental context

This domain comprises three closely related themes: Geography, Environment, and Climate. Geography includes characteristics such as geographic location, spatial configuration, slope, and elevation. Environment encompasses physical and living environments and, within these, more specific settings such as green spaces, blue spaces, and “third spaces”. [39] Green spaces refer to natural environments, parks, school grounds, open spaces, and residual green areas, while blue spaces include seas, lakes, ponds, and rivers. Following Fisher, [39] “third spaces” relate to urban design and landscape, i.e. “spaces in between”, including the notion of therapeutic landscapes. Climatic components considered across studies include temperature, humidity, atmospheric pressure, and rainfall.

Spatial structure and urban configuration

As described by Tiwari et al., [40] the living environment is understood as a lens for examining qualitative aspects of urban living — including slums, congestion, pollution, and the availability and access to social amenities — which reflects how social, economic, and spatial factors jointly shape people's lived experiences and health contexts. This domain further captures the outcomes of interactions between physical environments, living environments, and human activities, including air and water pollution and climate change.

Measures of density are a central component of this domain. Lakes et al., drawing on McFarlane's conceptualisation, describe density in multiple ways: the number of people living in an area; the number of individuals per household (often referred to as overcrowding); concentrations of people at specific sites; and the movement of people through space, including transport systems and streets. [41] Commonly used indicators include residential, urban, and service density. In this review, overcrowding was classified under the Housing and Household theme. Other authors distinguish between population density, urban density, and residential density, with Kroesen and van Wee, for example, defining residential density as average address density per square kilometre, [42] while Lakes et al. define urban density as the number of people living in an urban area. [41]

Accessibility, mobility, and connectivity

Accessibility, mobility, and connectivity emerged as the most frequently cited theme, with distance appearing in 25 publications. This theme includes distance to essential services and amenities such as grocery stores, general practitioners, healthcare facilities, and other commercial, social, and community resources. Abdulla and Puthuvayi define distance as the distance to the nearest grocery store, transit centre, or city centre measured in kilometres. [43] Davis et al. focus on distance to community support centres, [36] while Holman et al. consider residence in areas with limited public transport or a lack of services within walking distance. [44] Other studies emphasise proximity to natural water bodies, such as rivers and lakes, or access to healthcare institutions.

This theme is closely linked to transport and commuting, which includes indicators such as street length, transport infrastructure, spatial connectivity, cycling facilities, and commuting patterns. Overall, accessibility-related measures were most consistently operationalised across disease areas, making them a common entry point for empirical studies addressing spatial influences on health.

Socio-demographic and economic context

Several publications highlight socio-demographic and economic characteristics as key spatial determinants. Abdulla and Puthuvayi, for example, include age, occupancy, income, residential density, proximity to services, and spatial interaction as factors influencing pandemic resilience. [43] In this synthesis, socio-demographic characteristics and socio-economic conditions were treated as related but distinct elements within the same domain. Across the literature, commonly examined factors include social inequalities, cultural conditions, demographic structure, and other demographic attributes, alongside economic indicators such as household income, poverty, employment status, and working conditions.

Residential and neighbourhood context

Neighbourhood-related determinants were frequently examined, particularly in relation to deprivation. Other neighbourhood characteristics included diversity, ethnic composition, evidence of gangs, criminal activity and vagrancy, racial segregation, and community-based stigma. In some cases, additional indicators such as night-time light intensity or activity space size were used as proxies for neighbourhood conditions.

Housing and household characteristics were also commonly reported and are closely interrelated. Housing-related factors included housing costs, average housing age, housing tenure, owner-occupancy ratios, and general housing conditions. Household-level indicators encompassed asset ownership (e.g. radio, television, mobile phone, vehicles), access to electricity and gas, sky view factor, access to drinking water, sewage connections, toilets, and waste collection services.

These determinants affect health via exposure pathways, stress, service access, and living conditions, illustrating the spatial dimension of health inequities.

Healthcare context

While access to healthcare services was addressed under the Accessibility, Mobility, and Connectivity domain, this category captures broader healthcare-related determinants. These include the availability of health services, health insurance coverage, healthcare supply (density of general practitioners, nurses, and pharmacies), and health budgets as enabling conditions, as well as individual health conditions and health behaviours, which some publications discussed as spatially patterned or context-dependent factors.

Other

This residual category includes topics that could not be clearly assigned to the above domains. Examples include geographic variation between census tracts, spatial clustering, autocorrelation, population displacement, and globalisation.

Relationships between spatial determinants of health and other determinants of health

While several publications treat socio-spatial determinants as a single concept, few explicitly examine interactions between SpDoH and other determinants, such as social, political, or commercial factors. Meng et al. emphasise the interdependence of spatial and social determinants, arguing that their interaction shapes patterns of health inequality. [45] Kroesen et al. propose a model in which spatial and demographic factors influence behavioural determinants, subsequently affecting health outcomes. [42] Bencekry et al. identify inequality as an economic determinant with clear spatial dimensions. [46]

Augustin et al. argue that virtually all determinants of health possess a spatial component, with the partial exception of politics and decision-making processes, which cannot always be clearly attributed to a specific spatial level. [9] They note that determinants operating at closer spatial proximity tend to exert a stronger influence on individual health, whereas determinants acting at broader spatial scales may affect health both directly and indirectly, often becoming more influential as their spatial reach or impact increases. Building on this reasoning, the authors propose a conceptual framework that integrates spatial dimensions of health across multiple scales. The framework begins with global driving forces that shape policy and governance, which in turn influence sociodemographic and economic conditions, the living and physical environment, healthcare services, and cultural and working conditions. These factors interact with individual characteristics to determine health outcomes. [9]

At the base of the conceptual framework proposed by Augustin et al. is the micro level, encompassing households and neighbourhoods, corresponding to living and physical environments and cultural and working conditions. Above this lies the meso level, which includes local and regional contexts, such as sociodemographic and economic conditions and healthcare services. At the top of the framework is the macro level, comprising national and global influences, including global driving forces as well as policies and governance. [9]

Although only a small number of studies have directly addressed these interactions, the available evidence suggests that spatial and other determinants are mutually reinforcing, emphasising the importance of integrated, context-sensitive approaches.

Discussion

Our review found that a standard definition of SpDoH is largely lacking. Most publications either do not define the term or assume it is widely understood. Importantly, this absence of explicit definitions is not merely a methodological difficulty but represents a central finding of this review.

A closer look at the types of SpDoH examined reveals a broad range of topics. In many studies, the term SpDoH is used primarily to indicate spatial analyses comparing other determinants—social, epidemiological, or environmental—across different geographic or census units. This usage likely reflects the influence of spatial epidemiology, which Liu defines as “the description and analysis of geographic patterns and variations in disease risk factors, morbidity, and mortality in relation to demographic, socioeconomic, environmental, health behaviour, and genetic factors over time”. [47] When spatial epidemiology is applied within the framework of influential determinants of health, it can create terminological ambiguity, with SpDoH sometimes used merely to describe spatial variations in other health determinants rather than as a distinct determinant category. This lack of conceptual distinction may lead to conceptual broadening, with any geographically patterned variable treated as spatial, which weakens the explanatory specificity of the concept.

Recently, Takenaka et al. proposed a definition of socio-spatial determinants of health as “interlocking and mutually reinforcing arrangements of social, economic, political, intergenerational, and ecological advantages and disadvantages where people are born, live, work, play, and age that enact processes shaping embodied health realities across objective and subjective place over time”. [48] Their framework draws on ecosocial and intersectionality perspectives, effectively reframing the existing social determinants of health framework. While this definition enriches the conceptual debate by explicitly embedding spatiality within structural processes, it also broadens the concept considerably, potentially reducing the analytical distinctiveness of spatial determinants as a separate category. If nearly all determinants are conceptualised as inherently socio-spatial, the added value of distinguishing SpDoH as an independent group may become less clear. Moise and Kolak highlight that adopting a geographic perspective is essential for theoretical, analytical, and technological approaches to SDoH, noting that “a spatial perspective rooted in place considers the influence of structural determinants on place-based policies, resource allocation, and spatially heterogeneous public health effects”. [49]

Overall, the broad range of topics identified, together with the publications reviewed that explicitly or implicitly considered these topics as spatial determinants of health, supports the thesis that there is a lack of common understanding of SpDoH and that they often consider only the spatial variations of ‘other’ determinants of health, mainly social and environmental. This may indicate a positive transition from health geography and spatial epidemiology research on spatial influences on health towards public health. This transition, which could potentially also explain the recent increase in the use of the term ‘spatial determinants of health’ and attempts to conceptualise SpDoH frameworks, [9] may be understood as reflecting the broader relevance of the concept of determinants of health (particularly SDoH) and related frameworks beyond the public health community. Indeed, such dynamics may further enrich research on determinants of health. However, the variety of SpDoH components identified in the review may also highlight a broader lack of conceptualisation of determinants of health and weaknesses in existing frameworks.

Analysis of the domains, themes, and components of SpDoH in our review shows considerable overlap between SpDoH and other determinants of health, including environmental, socioeconomic, built environment, natural environment, health behaviours, healthcare, geographical, and social determinants. [9,50] As Augustin et al. emphasise, all determinants of health have a spatial dimension, and they are sometimes referred to as contextual determinants. [9] Systematic literature reviews of other determinants of health, particularly ecological (EclDoH) and environmental (EnvDoH) determinants, found similar overlaps. For example, the review of ecological determinants identified geography, climate, housing and urbanisation as frequent themes situated within EclDoH, as well as population density. [24] Similarly, a review of EnvDoH components identified climate, the physical and natural environment as common themes/domains of EnvDoH. [25] Particularly commonly identified EnvDoH relate to the built environment, including distance, housing density and neighbourhoods. Overlaps with other determinants of health have also been identified for political and digital determinants of health. [22,23] These findings further support the broader lack of a common understanding of different determinants of health, which is particularly reflected in SpDoH as potentially the least conceptualised category of determinants.

There is an open question as to whether SpDoH should be considered a distinct group of determinants or merely as spatial variations. Based on the findings of this systematic review, as well as reviews of other determinants of health, we would suggest three approaches. First, a mere analysis of spatial variations in ‘other’ determinants of health or health indicators should avoid using the term ‘spatial determinants of health’, as such analyses do not straightforwardly reflect the active role of space as a determinant of health. Second, given the importance of space, we would suggest that SpDoH remain a distinct category, potentially as a subset of EnvDoH, and include density, distance and mobility. In other words, taking into account the aforementioned ambiguity between space and place, we would suggest that determinants related to place be considered environmental determinants of health (either as part of the physical or built environment) or EclDoH (e.g. natural resources and climate), while socio-demographic factors should be considered part of SDoH.

Introducing greater clarity and clearer boundaries between different determinants of health may further improve their operationalisation. This is particularly important if we expect greater integration across different disciplines and consideration of determinants of health beyond what is traditionally considered public health. This leads us to the final proposed approach: the evolving concept of space (and place) [5] often remains outside the public health arena. [1,2] Therefore, we believe that introducing geographical, sociological and philosophical approaches to, and debates about, space in public health may enrich these debates and contribute to a better understanding of space within public health.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It is restricted to academic sources, and valuable contributions from grey literature may be missing. Grey literature, including governmental spatial strategies, urban planning documents, and reports from international organisations, might place greater emphasis on governance, land-use regulation, infrastructure distribution, or spatial justice perspectives, potentially enriching or challenging the academic conceptualisations identified in this review. Despite efforts to cover the most relevant publications, the search strategy and inclusion/exclusion criteria may have limited findings. Furthermore, only publications with English full texts available were assessed, potentially under-representing conceptual work from non-English-speaking research traditions. Extracting definitions, types, and relationships of SpDoH was sometimes challenging, as many studies did not state these explicitly. As noted above, this lack of explicit definition constitutes a fundamental finding of the review and directly influenced the synthesis, as only determinants clearly identified or described as spatial determinants of health were extracted. Consequently, spatial factors that authors may have implicitly considered relevant to health outcomes, but did not explicitly frame as SpDoH, were not captured. This approach may therefore result in an underrepresentation of the full range of spatial determinants assumed within individual studies. The absence of common definitions also limits comparability across studies and may partly explain the heterogeneity observed in the identified themes and domains. This variability may also reflect disciplinary fragmentation, suggesting that SpDoH research is evolving without a shared theoretical core.

Conclusions

Spatial determinants of health offer a valuable perspective for understanding the spatial factors that shape health and disease. However, there remains considerable misconception regarding what SpDoH entail, and they are rarely positioned clearly relative to other determinants. While some authors treat SpDoH simply as spatial variations of other determinants, our findings suggest that certain spatially structured properties of place—such as accessibility, mobility and connectivity, urban configuration, and residence and neighbourhood context—can be conceptualised as distinct relational determinants that condition how other determinants operate. This conceptualisation could support a novel framework to guide future research, policy, and multisectoral approaches.
