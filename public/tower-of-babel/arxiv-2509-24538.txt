Large and Moderate Deviations for Entries of Orthogonal Matrices and the Stiefel Manifold
Philipp Tuchel

Abstract
Let A N A_{N} be distributed according to the Haar probability measure on the orthogonal group 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ) \mathcal{O}(N) for each N ∈ ℕ N\in\mathbb{N} . It is well-known that the upper left m N × k N m_{N}\times k_{N} block of N ​ A N \sqrt{N}A_{N} with m N ​ k N = o ⁡ ( N ) m_{N}k_{N}=o(N) converges in total variation distance to a matrix of same size consisting of i.i.d. standard normal entries as N → ∞ N\to\infty . In this work, we characterize this convergence on the scale of large deviations. More precisely, we show that under the same condition m N ​ k N = o ⁡ ( N ) m_{N}k_{N}=o(N) the empirical measure of entries of this block satisfies a large deviation principle with speed m N ​ k N m_{N}k_{N} and rate function given by the relative entropy with respect to the standard normal distribution. Further, we complement the large deviation principle (LDP) obtained by Kabluchko and Prochno in [Large deviations for random matrices in the orthogonal group and Stiefel manifold with applications to random projections of product distributions, Annales de l’Institut Henri Poincaré. 60 (2024), 990 – 1024] for the whole block A N A_{N} with a moderate deviation principle (MDP). Concretely, we show an MDP for the sequence of matrices β N ​ A N \beta_{N}A_{N} in the product topology, where β N → ∞ \beta_{N}\to\infty is a sequence of real numbers such that β N = o ⁡ ( N ) \beta_{N}=o(\sqrt{N}) . Here, in contrast to the LDP, the Gaussian behavior of the entries is reflected in the rate function. Keywords : Empirical Measure, Joint Convergence, Large Deviation Principle, Moderate Deviation Principle, Orthogonal Group, Random Matrix, Stiefel Manifold. 1
Introduction

The study of high-dimensional random matrices is a central topic in probability theory and geometric functional analysis and has a wide variety of applications, such as in data analysis, machine learning and statistical physics. In 1933 in
[
12
]
Alfréd Haar established the existence of an invariant probability measure on certain compact (matrix) groups, now called Haar measure, making such structures accessible to probabilistic tools. Soon after, uniqueness of such measures and generalizations have been discovered (see for instance
[
8
]
and
[
27
]
). Since then, a lot of research has been done in this area across a variety of different fields.

In this work, we consider the following sets of matrices that also carry such Haar measures: for integers
m\leq N
, the
Stiefel manifold

\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
is the set of all
m\times N
real matrices with orthonormal rows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{m,N}\;:=\;\Bigl\{\,V\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}\;:\;V\,V^{\top}\;=\;\operatorname{Id}_{m}\Bigr\}.

(1)

Here,
\operatorname{Id}_{m}
denotes the
m
-dimensional identity matrix. When
m=N
, this coincides with the
orthogonal group

\mathcal{O}(N)
, which is the set of all
N\times N
real matrices satisfying
AA^{\top}=A^{\top}A=\operatorname{Id}_{N}
. For these sets (which have a group structure only in special cases, e.g.
m=N
, and
m=1
), there is a unique invariant probability measure, called Haar measure, that we denote by
\Unif(\mathbb{V}_{m,N})
and
\Unif(\mathcal{O}(N))
in case of
m=N
. Its characterizing property is that for any fixed
A\in\mathcal{O}(m)
and
B\in\mathcal{O}(N)
, the distributions of
AVB
and
V
coincide for
V
being distributed as
\Unif(\mathbb{V}_{m,N})
, denoted by
V\sim\Unif(\mathbb{V}_{m,N})
. In
[
13
]
James connected these measures to known matrix distributions such as the Wishart distribution. For a modern, comprehensive treatment of random matrix theory on classical compact groups, we refer the reader to the monograph by E. Meckes
[
20
]
. The Haar measure provides a natural framework for the study of the typical behavior of such sets of matrices. In particular, the distribution of the entries of random orthogonal matrices in high-dimensions has attracted considerable attention and is an active research area in probability and statistics today. Roughly speaking, as each row (and column) of a matrix in
\mathcal{O}(N)
has norm
1
, a typical entry of a random matrix in
\mathcal{O}(N)
is of order
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
. Over a century ago Poincaré and later Maxwell and Borel (see
[
2
]
and
[
7
, Chapter 6]
for historical context) observed that any individual coordinate of a sphere, rescaled by
\sqrt{N}
, is approximately Gaussian for large
N
. This classical result is a precursor to the study of collective convergence of coordinates to i.i.d. Gaussian random variables. In
[
7
]
, Diaconis and Freedman proved that
p_{N}
entries of a random uniform vector on the sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
converge jointly in total variation to
p_{N}
i.i.d. Gaussian random variables provided
p_{N}=o(N)
. A notable result in this direction is by Jiang who proved a threshold behavior for the more general case of the orthogonal group
\mathcal{O}(N)
(see
[
14
]
). More precisely, for
A_{N}\sim\Unif(\mathcal{O}(N))
and let
Z_{N}=A_{N}^{m_{N},k_{N}}
be the
m_{N}\times k_{N}
upper-left block of
A_{N}
. Then, the joint distribution of the
m_{N}k_{N}
entries of
\sqrt{N}Z_{N}
converges in total variation to the joint distribution of
m_{N}k_{N}
independent standard normal random variables as
N\to\infty
if
m_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
and
k_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
. On the other hand, he proved that if
m_{N}=\lfloor x\sqrt{N}\rfloor
and
k_{N}=\lfloor y\sqrt{N}\rfloor
for fixed
x,y>0
, this convergence does not hold. In recent years, more progress has been made in this direction. For instance, in
[
15
]
Jiang and Ma proved that the convergence even holds under the condition
m_{N}k_{N}=o(N)
but not if
m_{N}k_{N}=cN
for some constant
c>0
. Moreover, the convergence to Gaussian entries under the regime
m_{N}k_{N}=o(N)
holds not only in total variation but also for more distances including the Kullback-Leibler distance and the Hellinger distance. Notably, in the Euclidean distance, the convergence holds if
m_{N}k_{N}^{2}=o(N)
but not if
m_{N}k_{N}^{2}=cN
for a positive constant
c
. Recently, in
[
3
, Theorem 1]
, the authors provide the explicit convergence rate
\frac{m_{N}^{2}k_{N}(k_{N}+1)}{4N^{2}}(1-o(1))
for the Fisher information between
\sqrt{N}Z_{N}
and an
m_{N}\times k_{N}
block of i.i.d. standard Gaussians.

A framework for a more quantitative study of the asymptotic behavior beyond the scale of Gaussian approximation is the theory of large deviations. Originated in the work of Cramér on sums of i.i.d. random variables and formalized by Varadhan and others in the 1960s, large deviation principles (LDPs) have become a cornerstone in modern probability theory (see
[
4
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
26
]
and for a book
[
6
]
). In rough terms, LDPs characterize probabilities of rare events on an exponential scale in a quantitative way including the speed of convergence and a so-called rate function that captures the, often subtle, dependence on the event (see Definition
3.1
for a precise definition). Classical examples include Cramér’s LDP for sample means of random variables, Sanov’s theorem for random empirical measures and the Gärtner-Ellis Theorem (see
[
6
, Chapter 4 and 6]
). In cases where, for some sequence of random variables
X_{N}
, the sequence
X_{N}
satisfies an LDP and
\sqrt{N}X_{N}
satisfies a central limit theorem (CLT), often the sequence
\beta_{N}X_{N}
, for some
\beta_{N}\to\infty
,
\beta_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
, also satisfies an LDP with a certain speed and a rate function that reflects the CLT behavior. Such LDPs are also called moderate deviation principles (MDPs) and provide convergence behavior on this intermediate scale.

In the context of random matrices, large deviation results have been mostly studied in the context of spectral properties, such as the empirical eigenvalue distributions of Wigner and Wishart matrices (see for instance
[
1
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
29
]
and
[
30
]
).

In contrast, large deviations for the entries of such matrices is a more recent pursuit. One result in this direction is given by Kim and Ramanan (
[
19
, Theorem 2.8]
) who showed that the empirical measure of scaled columns of an element from the Stiefel manifold satisfies an LDP. More precisely,
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\sqrt{N}v_{i}}
, where
V_{N}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})\sim\Unif(\mathbb{V}_{m,N})
, satisfies an LDP in the
q
-Wasserstein topology for
q\in(0,2)
(for background, see
[
23
]
) with speed
N
and good (strictly) convex rate function
I
given by

\displaystyle I(\nu)=\begin{cases}H(\nu|\gamma^{\otimes m})+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\mathcal{C}(\nu))&\text{if }\mathcal{C}(\nu)-\operatorname{Id}_{m}\text{ is positive semidefinite},\\
+\infty&:\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(2)

Here
H
is the relative entropy,
\gamma^{\otimes m}
is the Gaussian product measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathcal{C}(\nu)
is the covariance operator of
\nu
. Note that the rate function is, in this case, not just the relative entropy which would be expected from a purely Gaussian limit. This statement is a core element in the proof of their results about large deviations for random projection of certain high-dimensional random vectors (
[
19
, Theorem 2.4 and Theorem 2.6]
), illustrating the significance of these studies in asymptotic geometric analysis. Random projections of vectors or convex bodies are a common application of random elements from the Stiefel manifold and an active research area (see
[
25
]
for a recent survey and the references therein). Another related result was obtained in
[
18
]
. The authors consider, for a certain class of random vectors
X_{N}
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the empirical measure of the projection coordinates:

\frac{1}{m_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\delta_{(A_{N}X_{N})_{i}}.

Here,
A_{N}
is the upper left
m_{N}\times N
block of a random orthogonal matrix and
A_{N}X_{N}
can be seen as the projection of
X_{N}
onto a random subspace. They prove an LDP for this sequence with different regimes for
m_{N}
(constant, sub-linear and linear). A key ingredient in this proof is an LDP for the empirical measure of
m_{N}
coordinates of a uniform random element from the sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, i.e. for
\frac{1}{m_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\delta_{\sqrt{N}X_{i}}
, where
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\sim\Unif(\mathbb{V}_{1,N})
. This gives another point of view on the central limit theorem for
m_{N}
coordinates of a uniform random element from the sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
obtained by Diaconis and Freedman in
[
7
]
in the context of large deviations and empirical measures.

In addition to the limiting behavior of the entries scaled by
\sqrt{N}
, there has been some progress on the limiting behavior of the unscaled entries of
A_{N}\sim\Unif(\mathcal{O}(N))
. Kabluchko and Prochno
[
16
]
(see also Kabluchko, Prochno and Thäle
[
17
]
) quantified the rate of this convergence on the scale of large deviations motivated by applications in random projections of product distributions. They showed (
[
16
, Theorem B]
) that the sequence
A_{N}
(embedded in
[-1,1]^{\infty\times\infty}
by padding with zeros) satisfies, for
N\to\infty
, an LDP with speed
N
and good convex rate function
I:[-1,1]^{\infty\times\infty}\to[0,\infty]
, given by

\displaystyle I(T):=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\left(\operatorname{Id}_{\infty}-TT^{\top}\right)&:T\in\mathscr{S}_{2}\text{ and }\|TT^{\top}\|_{op}<1,\\
+\infty&:\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(3)

where
\|\cdot\|_{op}
denotes the operator norm and
\mathscr{S}_{2}
is the Hilbert space of Hilbert–Schmidt operators on
\ell_{2}
:
\mathscr{S}_{2}:=\{T=(t_{i,j})_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty\times\infty}:\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}t_{i,j}^{2}<\infty\}
. Here, the determinant is defined as
\det\left(\operatorname{Id}_{\infty}-TT^{\top}\right):=\lim_{k\to\infty}\det\left(\operatorname{Id}_{k}-T_{k}T_{k}^{\top}\right)
, with
T_{k}
being the top-left
k\times k
block of
T
. Note that this rate function is well defined because
\|AA^{\top}\|<1
holds if and only if
\operatorname{Id}_{m}-AA^{\top}
is positive definite, i.e. the determinant is positive. Similarly, according to
[
16
, Theorem A]
, for fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
V_{N}\sim\Unif(\mathbb{V}_{m,N})
, the sequence
V_{N}
satisfies an LDP with speed
N
and a good convex rate function
J:[-1,1]^{m\times\infty}\to[0,\infty]
that has a similar structure to the rate function
J
above.

The purpose of this work is twofold. First, we characterize the normal approximation of an
m_{N}\times k_{N}
block in a new way, by an LDP for the empirical measure of the scaled entries of a random orthogonal matrix, where the Gaussian behavior is captured in the relative entropy in the rate function. In fact, as a consequence we find that the empirical measure converges, under certain assumptions, almost surely to the standard Gaussian measure in the weak topology. This LDP complements and extends the empirical measure LDPs by Kim, Liao and Ramanan (
[
19
]
,
[
18
]
) and gives a quantitative interpretation of the Gaussian approximation on the scale
m_{N}k_{N}=o(N)
. At the heart of the proof lies a new uniform local limit result for densities, which might be of independent interest. Next, we examine the asymptotic behavior of the entire matrix block not on the large but on the moderate deviation scale. This means that for any sequence
\beta_{N}\to\infty
and
\beta_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
, we show that the sequence
\beta_{N}A_{N}
satisfies an MDP with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and a rate function that reflects the Gaussian limit of the individual entries, which is not seen in the unscaled regime. This closes the gap between the matrix scaled by
\sqrt{N}
, studied by Jiang and Ma in
[
15
]
, and the unscaled matrix, studied by Kabluchko and Prochno in
[
16
]
.

2
Main Results

Our first result is a large deviation principle (LDP) for the empirical measure of the scaled entries of a random orthogonal matrix. The rate function reflects the Gaussian behavior of this empirical measure.

Theorem A

(Empirical Measure LDP for Scaled Entries)
.

Let
X_{N}=A_{N}^{m_{N},k_{N}}
be the
m_{N}\times k_{N}
upper left block of
A_{N}
where
A_{N}
is Haar-distributed on
\mathcal{O}(N)
. Assume that
m_{N},k_{N}
are sequences of natural numbers such that
p_{N}:=m_{N}k_{N}\to\infty
and
p_{N}=o(N)
. Let
y_{i,j}^{(N)}=\sqrt{N}(X_{N})_{i,j}^{(N)}
be the scaled entries for
1\leq i\leq m_{N},1\leq j\leq k_{N}
. Define the random empirical measure by

\nu_{N}:=\frac{1}{p_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{N}}\delta_{y_{i,j}^{(N)}}.

Then the sequence
\nu_{N}
satisfies an LDP on the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, equipped with the weak topology, with speed
p_{N}=m_{N}k_{N}
and good convex rate function
I
given by the relative entropy with respect to the standard Gaussian measure
\gamma
:

I(\mu)=H(\mu|\gamma):=\begin{cases}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\left(\frac{d\mu}{d\gamma}\right)d\mu&:\mu\ll\gamma,\\
+\infty&:\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The proof of the theorem, as well as the proofs of most other results below, is given in Section
4
. Roughly speaking, the theorem asserts that the probability that the empirical measure of the scaled entries of a random orthogonal matrix deviates from the standard Gaussian measure is given by

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{p_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{N}}\delta_{y_{i,j}^{(N)}}\not\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\gamma)\right)\approx\exp\left(-p_{N}\inf_{\mu\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\gamma)}H(\mu|\gamma)\right)

for
\epsilon>0
,
\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\mu)
being the
\epsilon
ball around
\mu
(in the Lévy–Prokhorov metric) and where "
\approx
" means asymptotic equivalence up to subexponential terms (note that
H(\mu|\gamma)=0
if and only if
\mu=\gamma
).

Remark 2.1
.

The condition
p_{N}=o(N)
cannot be improved without changing the rate function. As we have seen in the introduction, by
[
19
, Theorem 2.8]
,
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\sqrt{N}v_{i}}
, where
v_{i}
are the columns of
V_{N}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})\sim\Unif(\mathbb{V}_{m,N})
, satisfies an LDP in the
q
-Wasserstein topology for
q\in(0,2)
(and hence in the weak topology) with speed
N
and good rate function given by Equation (
2
). Using the contraction principle (see Proposition
3.7
below), for
p_{N}=kN
(
k
fixed), the sequence
\nu_{N}
satisfies an LDP with speed
N
and a rate function that is not just given by the relative entropy but also involves the covariance operator of
\nu_{N}
.

The theorem leads to the following corollary:

Corollary 2.2
.

In the setting of Theorem
A
with
p_{N}
such that
p_{N}=N^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the following holds with probability
1
: the sequence of empirical measures
\nu_{N}
converges to the standard Gaussian measure
\gamma
as
N\to\infty
in the weak topology.

Proof.

Let
B
be an open neighborhood of
\gamma
in the weak topology in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
. Then,
H(\cdot|\gamma)\not=0
on
B^{c}
and since
B^{c}
is closed
\inf_{x\in B^{c}}H(x|\gamma)\geq c>0
for some
c>0
. The LDP in Theorem
A
implies that
\mathbb{P}(\nu_{N}\not\in B)=O(e^{-p_{N}c/2})
and hence
\sum_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}(\nu_{N}\not\in B)<\infty
. By the Borel-Cantelli Lemma, with probability 1,
\nu_{N}\not\in B
for only finitely many
N
. This means that
\nu_{N}
is eventually in
B
with probability 1. Since this holds for any open neighborhood
B
of
\gamma
, this implies the almost sure convergence.
∎

The proof of Theorem
A
uses the following result on the uniform convergence of density functions which might be of independent interest. Let
m=m_{N}
,
k=k_{N}
and
N\geq m+k
. Define

Z_{N}:=\bigl(Z_{ij}^{(N)}\bigr)_{1\leq i\leq m,\;1\leq j\leq k}

to be the upper–left
m\times k
block of a Haar–distributed matrix in
\mathcal{O}(N)
. By
[
14
, Lemma 2.5]
, the block
Z_{N}
has, for
N\geq k+m
, the density
f_{N}:[-1,1]^{m\times k}\to[0,\infty)
,

f_{N}(A)=\frac{\Gamma_{m}\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{mk}{2}}\Gamma_{m}\left(\frac{N-k}{2}\right)}\operatorname{det}\left(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-AA^{\top}\right)^{\frac{N-k-m-1}{2}}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\left\|AA^{\top}\right\|_{op}<1\right\}},\quad A\in[-1,1]^{m\times k},

where
\Gamma_{m}
denotes the multivariate gamma function

\Gamma_{m}(x)=\pi^{\frac{m(m-1)}{4}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Gamma\left(x-\frac{i-1}{2}\right),

where
x\geq\frac{1}{2}(m-1)
(see
[
11
, Theorem 1.4.1]
). Here
\|AA^{\top}\|_{op}
is the operator norm of
AA^{\top}
. For
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
define

g_{N}(B):=N^{-\frac{mk}{2}}\,f_{N}\left(\frac{B}{\sqrt{N}}\right),\qquad\phi_{N}(B):=(2\pi)^{-\frac{mk}{2}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|B\|_{F}^{2}\Bigr),

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. Note that
g_{N}
is the density of
\sqrt{N}Z_{N}
while
\phi_{N}
is the density of the standard Gaussian
m\times k
block. We prove the following result:

Proposition 2.3

(Uniform Local Limit)
.

Assume
m,k\geq 1
with
m=m_{N}=o(N)
and
k=k_{N}=o(N)
. Let
R_{N}
be a sequence such that
R_{N}=o(N^{1/2})
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all sufficiently large
N
,

\sup_{B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}:\|B\|_{F}\leq R_{N}}\Bigl|\log\frac{g_{N}(B)}{\phi_{N}(B)}\Bigr|\;\leq\;C\Bigl(\frac{R_{N}^{4}}{N}+\frac{mk(m+k)}{N}+\frac{(k+m)R_{N}^{2}}{N}\Bigr).

Remark 2.4
.

Under stronger conditions, specifically if
mk(m+k)=o(N)
and
R_{N}=o(N^{1/4})
, the error term on the right hand side tends to zero as
N\to\infty
. This implies that
g_{N}(\cdot)/\phi_{N}(\cdot)\to 1
uniformly on
\{B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}:\|B\|_{F}\leq R_{N}\}
.

The next main results are the MDP for scaled orthogonal and Stiefel matrices, which complements the LDP results in
[
16
, Theorem A and B]
.

Theorem B

(MDP for the Stiefel Manifold)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed and
V_{m,N}
be chosen uniformly (i.e. with respect to the Haar measure) in
\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
for every
N\geq m
. Furthermore, let
\beta_{N}
be a sequence of positive numbers such that
\beta_{N}\to\infty
and
\beta_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
. Then, the sequence
\beta_{N}V_{m,N}
satisfies an LDP on the space
\mathbb{R}^{m\times\infty}
with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and good convex rate function

I:\mathbb{R}^{m\times\infty}\to[0,\infty],\quad I(A)=\frac{1}{2}\|A\|_{F}^{2}.

A similar result is true for the orthogonal group.

Theorem C

(MDP for the Orthogonal Group)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed and
A_{N}
be chosen uniformly in
\mathcal{O}_{N}
for every
N
. Furthermore, let
\beta_{N}
be a sequence of positive numbers such that
\beta_{N}\to\infty
and
\beta_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
. Then, the sequence
\beta_{N}A_{N}
satisfies an LDP on the space
\mathbb{R}^{\infty\times\infty}
with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and good convex rate function

J:\mathbb{R}^{\infty\times\infty}\to[0,\infty],\quad J(A)=\frac{1}{2}\|A\|_{F}^{2}.

These theorems show the asymptotic behavior below the CLT level. Deviations from the zero matrix are governed by a Gaussian-like quadratic rate function and speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
which interpolates between the LDP speed
N
and the CLT.

3
Preliminaries

We introduce some notation. The space of probability measures in
\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
. We write
\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}_{m})
for the standard
m
-dimensional Gaussian distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
\operatorname{Id}_{m}
is the
m
-dimensional identity matrix. We denote the Dirac measure at some point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by
\delta_{x}
. For a random vector
X
that is distributed according to a probability measure
\mu
, we write
X\sim\mu
. The mean of
X
is denoted by
\mathbb{E}[X]
and the variance by
\Var[X]
. An open ball of radius
r>0
centered at
x
in a metric space is denoted by
\mathbb{B}_{r}(x)
. The
weak topology
is the coarsest topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
for which the maps
\mu\mapsto\int fd\mu
are continuous for all
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
, the space of bounded continuous functions. As mentioned above,
\|\cdot\|_{F}
and
\|\cdot\|_{op}
are the Frobenius norm and operator norm, respectively. Depending on the context, we use
\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)
for
\mathbf{1}\{x\in A\}
for the indicator function over some set
A
. For sequences
a_{N}
,
b_{N}
, we write
a_{N}=o(b_{N})
and
a_{N}=O(b_{N})
for
|\frac{a_{N}}{b_{N}}|\to 0
and
|\frac{a_{N}}{b_{N}}|\to C\geq 0
, respectively (as
N\to\infty
). We use
a_{N}\sim b_{N}
to denote asymptotic equivalence, i.e.
\frac{a_{N}}{b_{N}}\to 1
. For a square matrix
A
,
\Tr{A}
denotes the trace of
A
and for a measurable set
B
,
\vol_{m}(B)
is the
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
B
.

3.1
Large and Moderate Deviation Principles

In this section, we collect some preliminary definitions and results that are used in the proofs of the main results. We start with the concept of a large deviation principle. Let
\mathbb{X}
be a topological space. A function
I:\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty]
is a
rate function
if it is lower semi-continuous, i.e. its sublevel sets
\{x\in\mathbb{X}:I(x)\leq y\}
are closed for all
y\geq 0
. If those sets are, in addition, compact, we call
I
a
good rate function
.

Definition 3.1

(Large Deviation Principle)
.

A sequence of random variables
X_{N}
taking values in a topological space
\mathbb{X}
satisfies a
Large Deviation Principle (LDP)
with
speed

s_{N}\to\infty
and
rate function

I:\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty]
if
I
is a rate function and

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{N}\in O)

\displaystyle\geq-\inf_{x\in O}I(x)\quad\text{ for all open }O\subseteq\mathbb{X},

(4)

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{N}\in C)

\displaystyle\leq-\inf_{x\in C}I(x)\quad\text{ for all closed }C\subseteq\mathbb{X}.

(5)

We say that a sequence satisfies a
weak LDP
with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
I
if it satisfies the assumptions of an LDP with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
I
except that the upper bound in (
5
) is only assumed to hold for compact sets. Next, we introduce the notion of a moderate deviation principle which formally is just a special kind of LDP.

Definition 3.2

(Moderate Deviation Principle (MDP))
.

Let
Y_{N}
be a sequence of random variables. Let
\beta_{N}
be a sequence such that
\beta_{N}\to\infty
and
\beta_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
. We say that
\beta_{N}Y_{N}
satisfies a
Moderate Deviation Principle (MDP)
with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and rate function
I
if it obeys an LDP with the same speed and rate function.

Since the speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
tends to infinity faster than
\sqrt{N}
but slower than
N
, the MDP scale is typically between the central limit theorem (CLT) scale and the LDP at speed
N
. In the MDP case, the sequence still satisfies an LDP but the CLT behavior is often reflected in the rate function. We can see this in the Theorems
B
and
C
as the rate function has a quadratic form that reminds of the exponent in the Gaussian density.

Definition 3.3

(Exponential Tightness)
.

A sequence of random variables
X_{N}
with values in a topological space
\mathbb{X}
is
exponentially tight
with respect to speed
s_{N}
if for every
a>0
, there exists a compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{X}
such that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{N}\notin K)\leq-a.

One main tool in our proofs is the following version of the Gärtner-Ellis theorem which can be found for instance in
[
6
, Theorem 4.4.13]
.

Proposition 3.4

(Gärtner-Ellis Theorem)
.

Let
X_{N}
be a sequence of random variables in
\mathbb{X}
, where
\mathbb{X}
is assumed to be a metric space or a Hausdorff topological vector space, and let
I
be a good rate function. Denote by
C_{b}(\mathbb{X})
the space of continuous and bounded functions on
\mathbb{X}
with values in
\mathbb{R}
. Then,
X_{N}
satisfies an LDP with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
I
if and only if for every
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{X})
the limit

\Lambda(f)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(s_{N}f(X_{N})\right)\right]

exists and

\displaystyle\Lambda(f)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\left\{f(x)-I(x)\right\}.

We continue with some standard tools in LDP theory. The following result is taken from Lemma 1.2.18 in
[
6
]
and the remark before.

Proposition 3.5

(Equivalence of LDP and weak LDP & exponential tightness)
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a locally compact space or a Polish spaces. Then a sequence of random variables in
\mathbb{X}
satisfy an LDP with speed
s_{N}
and good rate function
I
if and only if it satisfies the corresponding weak LDP and is exponentially tight.

The next proposition is adapted from
[
6
, Lemma 4.1.11 and Lemma 4.1.18]
.

Proposition 3.6

(Reformulation of weak LDP)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a base of the topology in a metric space
\mathbb{X}
. Let
X_{N}
be a sequence of random variables with values in
\mathbb{X}
and assume
s_{N}
is some sequence with
s_{N}\to\infty
. If for every
x\in\mathbb{X}
,

I(x):=-\inf_{A\in\mathcal{T}:x\in A}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{N}\in A)=-\inf_{A\in\mathcal{T}:x\in A}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{N}\in A),

then
X_{N}
satisfies a weak LDP with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
I
. Conversely, if
X_{N}
satisfies a weak LDP with speed
s_{N}
and rate function
I
, then the above identities hold.

The next proposition, known as the Contraction Principle, can be found in
[
6
, Theorem 4.2.1]
.

Proposition 3.7

(Contraction Principle)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
be continuous, where
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
are Hausdorff spaces. If
X_{N}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{X}
with speed
s_{N}
and good rate function
I
, then
f(X_{N})
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{Y}
with speed
s_{N}
and good rate function
J(y)=\inf_{x\in f^{-1}(y)}I(x)
(with the convention that
\inf_{x\in\emptyset}I(x)=+\infty
).

We also need a result that allows us to lift an LDP from a coarser topology to a finer topology if the coarser topology is Hausdorff and the sequence is exponentially tight with respect to the finer topology. The statement can be found in
[
6
, Corollary 4.2.6]
. The result is given for an exponentially tight sequence, but it follows immediately from the proof of Theorem 4.2.4 in
[
6
]
that the weak LDP is a consequence without exponential tightness.

Proposition 3.8
.

Assume
X_{N}
satisfies an LDP on a set
\mathbb{X}
equipped with some Hausdorff topology
\tau_{2}
. Let
\tau_{1}
be a topology on
\mathbb{X}
which is finer than
\tau_{2}
. Then,
X_{N}
satisfies the corresponding weak LDP with respect to the topology
\tau_{1}
.

We introduce the notion of projective limits which we will need for the proofs of Theorems
B
and
C
(projective limits where also used in the proof of
[
16
, Theorem A]
). A
projective system

\left(\mathbb{Y}_{j},p_{ij}\right)_{i\leq j}
consists of Hausdorff topological spaces
\mathbb{Y}_{j},j\in\mathbb{N}
, and continuous mappings
p_{ij}:\mathbb{Y}_{j}\rightarrow\mathbb{Y}_{i}
such that for all
i\leq j\leq k
, we have
p_{ik}=p_{ij}\circ p_{jk}
and
p_{jj},j\in\mathbb{N}
is the identity mapping on
\mathbb{Y}_{j}
. Then, the projective limit
\mathbb{Y}
of this system is given by

\mathbb{Y}:=\Big\{y=\left(y_{j}\right)_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\prod_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Y}_{j}\,:\,y_{i}=p_{ij}\left(y_{j}\right),\forall i<j\Big\}.

We denote by
p_{j}:\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{Y}_{j}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
the canonical projection, i.e., for
y\in\mathbb{Y}
, we have
p_{j}(y)=y_{j}
in the notation above. The following theorem by Dawson and Gärtner, see
[
5
, Theorem 3.3]
, allows inferring an LDP on
\mathbb{Y}
given an LDP on
\mathbb{Y}_{j}
for each
j
.

Proposition 3.9

(Dawson – Gärtner)
.

Let
\mathbb{Y}
be the projective limit of the projective system
\left(\mathbb{Y}_{j},p_{ij}\right)_{i\leq j}
. Assume that
X_{N}
is a sequence of random variables in
\mathbb{Y}
such that for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
p_{\ell}(X_{N})
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{Y}_{\ell}
with speed
s_{N}
and good rate function
I_{\ell}
. Then
X_{N}
satisfies an LDP with speed
s_{N}
and good rate function
I:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
given by

I(x):=\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}I_{\ell}\left(p_{\ell}(x)\right).

4
Proofs

In this section, we provide the proofs, starting with the proof of the LDP for the empirical measure of scaled entries of an orthogonal matrix, followed by the proofs of the MDPs for scaled orthogonal and Stiefel matrices.

4.1
Proof of Theorem
A

We start with the following lemma that will be essential in the proof of Proposition
2.3
. It quantifies the difference in the exponents of both densities. Recall that we compare the density
g_{N}(B)=N^{-mk/2}f_{N}(B/\sqrt{N})
with

f_{N}(A)=\frac{\Gamma_{m}\!\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{mk}{2}}\,\Gamma_{m}\!\left(\frac{N-k}{2}\right)}\,\det\bigl(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-AA^{\!T}\bigr)^{\frac{N-k-m-1}{2}}\;\mathbf{1}_{\{\|AA^{\!T}\|_{op}<1\}},

with the Gaussian density
\phi_{N}(B)=(2\pi)^{-mk/2}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|B\|_{F}^{2})
.

Lemma 4.1

(Log-determinant expansion)
.

Let
X:=\tfrac{1}{N}\,BB^{\top}
where
B
is a real
m\times k
matrix. For
\|X\|_{op}<1
, the following expansion holds:

\frac{N-k-m-1}{2}\,\log\det(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-X)=-\frac{\|B\|_{F}^{2}}{2}+\frac{k+m+1}{2N}\,\|B\|_{F}^{2}-\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\bigl((BB^{\top})^{2}\bigr)}{4N}+R_{N}^{\prime}(B).

If
\|B\|_{F}\leq R_{N}
for a sequence
R_{N}
with
R_{N}=o(N^{1/2})
, the remainder term is bounded by

|R_{N}^{\prime}(B)|\;\leq\;C\left(\frac{(k+m)R_{N}^{4}}{N^{2}}+\frac{R_{N}^{6}}{N^{2}}\right)

for some constant
C>0
.

Proof.

We start with an identity for
\log\det(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-X)
. By
[
24
, Equation (512)]
, we have
\det(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-X)=\exp(\mathrm{Tr}(\log(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-X)))
and thus
\log(\det(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-X))=\mathrm{Tr}(\log(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-X))
. Since
\|X\|_{op}<1
, we get by a series expansion of the logarithm and linearity of
\mathrm{Tr}
,

\log\det(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-X)=-\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})}{r}.

Let
C_{N}=\frac{N-k-m-1}{2}
and note that
\mathrm{Tr}(X)=\|B\|_{F}^{2}/N
. Then

\displaystyle C_{N}\log\det(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-X)

\displaystyle=C_{N}\left(-\mathrm{Tr}(X)-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(X^{2})-\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})}{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}(1-\tfrac{k+m+1}{N})\left(-\tfrac{1}{N}\|B\|_{F}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2N^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}((BB^{\top})^{2})-\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})}{r}\right)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\|B\|_{F}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4N}\mathrm{Tr}((BB^{\top})^{2})+\tfrac{k+m+1}{2N}\|B\|_{F}^{2}+\tfrac{k+m+1}{4N^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}((BB^{\top})^{2})-C_{N}\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})}{r}.

The remainder term
R_{N}^{\prime}(B)
is therefore given by

R_{N}^{\prime}(B)=\tfrac{k+m+1}{4N^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}((BB^{\top})^{2})-C_{N}\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})}{r}.

We bound the two terms. Let us start with the first term,
\tfrac{k+m+1}{4N^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}((BB^{\top})^{2})
. We use the inequality
\mathrm{Tr}((BB^{\top})^{2})\leq(\|B\|_{F}^{2})^{2}=\|B\|_{F}^{4}
. To see this, let
\sigma_{i}\geq 0
be the eigenvalues of the
m\times m
matrix
BB^{\top}
. Then
\mathrm{Tr}(BB^{\top})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}=\|B\|_{F}^{2}
, and
\mathrm{Tr}((BB^{\top})^{2})=\sum\sigma_{i}^{2}\leq(\sum\sigma_{i})^{2}
. Thus, this first term of
R_{N}^{\prime}(B)
is bounded by:

D_{1}\frac{(k+m)\|B\|_{F}^{4}}{N^{2}},

where
D_{1}>0
is some constant. Next, we analyze the second term. We have
\|X\|_{op}=\frac{1}{N}\|BB^{\top}\|_{op}\leq\frac{1}{N}\|B\|_{op}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{N}\|B\|_{F}^{2}
by standard properties of the operator norm. Since
\|B\|_{F}\leq R_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
, we have
\|X\|_{op}=o(1)
. Next, for the sum term
-C_{N}\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})}{r}
, we use the inequality
\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})\leq\|X\|_{op}^{r-2}\mathrm{Tr}(X^{2})
for
r\geq 2
. This holds because
\|X\|_{op}=\lambda_{1}
and for
r\geq 2
,
\lambda_{i}^{r}=\lambda_{i}^{r-2}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq\lambda_{1}^{r-2}\lambda_{i}^{2}=\|X\|_{op}^{r-2}\lambda_{i}^{2}
where
\lambda_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{m}\geq 0
are the eigenvalues of
X
. Summing over
i
gives
\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{r}\leq\|X\|_{op}^{r-2}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}=\|X\|_{op}^{r-2}\mathrm{Tr}(X^{2})
. Thus,

\Big|C_{N}\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})}{r}\Big|\leq C_{N}\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{|\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})|}{r}\leq C_{N}\mathrm{Tr}(X^{2})\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{\|X\|_{op}^{r-2}}{r}.

Using the geometric series and the fact that
\|X\|_{op}=o(1)
, we get
\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{\|X\|_{op}^{r-2}}{r}\leq\frac{1}{3}\frac{\|X\|_{op}}{1-\|X\|_{op}}
.
This leads to the overall bound for the sum term:

\Big|C_{N}\sum_{r\geq 3}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(X^{r})}{r}\Big|\leq C_{N}\mathrm{Tr}(X^{2})\frac{\|X\|_{op}}{3(1-\|X\|_{op})}.

We have established above that
\|X\|_{op}\leq N^{-1}\|B\|_{F}^{2}
and
\mathrm{Tr}(X^{2})=N^{-2}\mathrm{Tr}((BB^{\top})^{2})\leq N^{-2}\|B\|_{F}^{4}
. Since
\|X\|_{op}=o(1)
, we have
1/(3(1-\|X\|_{op}))=o(1)
. Substituting these approximations, we get

C_{N}\mathrm{Tr}(X^{2})\frac{\|X\|_{op}}{3(1-\|X\|_{op})}=O\left(\frac{\|B\|_{F}^{6}}{N^{2}}\right).

Combining these gives

|R_{N}^{\prime}(B)|\leq C\left(\frac{(k+m)\|B\|_{F}^{4}}{N^{2}}+\frac{\|B\|_{F}^{6}}{N^{2}}\right).

Since
\|B\|_{F}\leq R_{N}
, we get the desired bound

|R_{N}^{\prime}(B)|\leq C\left(\frac{(k+m)R_{N}^{4}}{N^{2}}+\frac{R_{N}^{6}}{N^{2}}\right).

∎

We continue with a technical lemma about the asymptotics of the multivariate gamma function. This compares the normalizing constants of both densities.

Lemma 4.2

(Gamma quotient estimate)
.

Let
\Gamma_{m}(x)=\pi^{\frac{m(m-1)}{4}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\Gamma\bigl(x-\tfrac{j-1}{2}\bigr)
for
x>(m-1)/2
be the multivariate gamma function. Assume
k=k_{N}=o(N)
and
m=m_{N}=o(N)
. Then

\log\frac{\Gamma_{m}(\tfrac{N}{2})}{\Gamma_{m}(\tfrac{N-k}{2})}-\frac{mk}{2}\,\log\!\Bigl(\tfrac{N}{2}\Bigr)=O\!\Bigl(\frac{mk(m+k)}{N}\Bigr).

The proof of this result involves asymptotic expansions of the polygamma functions. While these objects are studied in the literature, we could not find an exact reference and will provide a self-contained proof here.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

For the first term on the left-hand side, we get (after an index shift and applying logarithm rules) the term

\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\left(\log\Gamma\left(\tfrac{N}{2}-\tfrac{l}{2}\right)-\log\Gamma\left(\tfrac{N-k}{2}-\tfrac{l}{2}\right)\right).

Let us define
x_{l}:=\tfrac{N-k}{2}-\tfrac{l}{2}
. We are interested in an expansion of
\log\Gamma(x_{l}+h)-\log\Gamma(x_{l})
for
h:=k/2
and
h=o(x_{l})
. Let
f(x)=\log\Gamma(x)
. We use the Taylor series expansion of
f(x_{l}+h)
around
x_{l}
:

f(x_{l}+h)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(x_{l})}{n!}h^{n}.

Rearranging, we get:

\log\Gamma(x_{l}+h)-\log\Gamma(x_{l})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(x_{l})}{n!}h^{n}.

The derivatives of
\log\Gamma(x)
are the polygamma functions (see for instance
[
22
, Chapter 5.15]
): For instance
f^{\prime}(x)=\psi(x)
is the digamma function,
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\psi^{\prime}(x)
the trigamma function; and
f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)
, the tetragamma function. The idea is now to use the Taylor expansion and the known expansions for the polygamma functions in order to get the asymptotic behavior of this difference. Substituting into the Taylor expansion gives:

\log\Gamma(x_{l}+h)-\log\Gamma(x_{l})=h\psi(x_{l})+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\psi^{\prime}(x_{l})+\frac{h^{3}}{6}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x_{l})+\frac{h^{4}}{24}\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(x_{l})+\dots

For large
x_{l}
, the pentagamma function has the asymptotic behavior
\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(x_{l})\sim 2x_{l}^{-3}
(see
[
22
, Chapter 5.15]
for asymptotics of polygamma functions). This mean that we can write

\log\Gamma(x_{l}+h)-\log\Gamma(x_{l})=h\psi(x_{l})+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\psi^{\prime}(x_{l})+\frac{h^{3}}{6}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x_{l})+O\left(\frac{h^{4}}{x_{l}^{3}}\right).

We now use the known asymptotic expansions for the digamma and trigamma functions for large
x_{l}
:

\psi(x_{l})=\log x_{l}-\frac{1}{2x_{l}}-\frac{1}{12x_{l}^{2}}+O(x_{l}^{-3})

and

\psi^{\prime}(x_{l})=\frac{1}{x_{l}}+\frac{1}{2x_{l}^{2}}+O(x_{l}^{-3}).

Substituting these into the Taylor expansion gives

\displaystyle\log\Gamma(x_{l}+h)-\log\Gamma(x_{l})

\displaystyle=h\left(\log x_{l}-\frac{1}{2x_{l}}-\frac{1}{12x_{l}^{2}}+O(x_{l}^{-3})\right)+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{x_{l}}+\frac{1}{2x_{l}^{2}}+O(x_{l}^{-3})\right)+\frac{h^{3}}{6}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x_{l})+O(hx_{l}^{-3})+O(h^{2}x_{l}^{-3})+O(h^{4}x_{l}^{-3}).

For large
x_{l}
,
\psi^{\prime\prime}(x_{l})\sim-x_{l}^{-2}
. This means
\frac{h^{3}}{6}\psi^{\prime\prime}(x_{l})
is of order
O(h^{3}x_{l}^{-2})
. This leads to the simplified expression

\log\Gamma(x_{l}+h)-\log\Gamma(x_{l})=h\log x_{l}-\frac{h}{2x_{l}}+\frac{h^{2}}{2x_{l}}+O\left(\frac{h+h^{2}+h^{3}}{x_{l}^{2}}\right).

We have, using the series expansion of
\log(1-x)
and the definition of
x_{l}
,

\log x_{l}=\log\left(\frac{N-k}{2}\right)+\log\left(1-\frac{l}{N-k}\right)=\log\left(\frac{N-k}{2}\right)-\frac{l}{N-k}-\frac{l^{2}}{2(N-k)^{2}}+O\left(\left(\frac{l}{N}\right)^{3}\right).

In the same way, we have

\log\left(\frac{N-k}{2}\right)=\log\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)+\log\left(1-\frac{k}{N}\right)=\log\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)-\frac{k}{N}-\frac{k^{2}}{2N^{2}}+O\left(\left(\frac{k}{N}\right)^{3}\right).

Hence

\log x_{l}=\log\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)-\frac{k}{N}-\frac{l}{N-k}+O\left(\frac{k^{2}}{N^{2}}+\frac{l^{2}}{N^{2}}\right).

So, for each
l
(remember
h=k/2
):

\displaystyle\log\Gamma(x_{l}+h)-\log\Gamma(x_{l})

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{2}\left(\log\Bigl(\frac{N}{2}\Bigr)-\frac{k}{N}-\frac{l}{N-k}\right)-\frac{k}{4x_{l}}+\frac{k^{2}}{4x_{l}}+O\left(\frac{k+k^{2}+k^{3}}{(N-k-l)^{2}}+\frac{k^{3}+kl^{2}}{N^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{2}\log\Bigl(\frac{N}{2}\Bigr)-\frac{k^{2}}{2N}-\frac{kl}{2(N-k)}+\frac{k(k-1)}{4x_{l}}+O\left(\frac{k^{2}}{N}\right).

Now, we sum over all
l
:

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\left(\log\Gamma(x_{l}+h)-\log\Gamma(x_{l})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{mk}{2}\log\Bigl(\frac{N}{2}\Bigr)-\frac{mk^{2}}{2N}-\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\frac{kl}{2(N-k)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\frac{k(k-1)}{4x_{l}}+O\left(\frac{mk^{2}}{N}\right).

To evaluate the sum
\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\frac{k(k-1)}{4x_{l}}
, recall that
x_{l}=\frac{N-k-l}{2}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\frac{k(k-1)}{4x_{l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k(k-1)}{4}\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\frac{2}{N-k-l}=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{N-k-l}.

Using the approximation (and denote
\sim
for the fraction tending to one),

\frac{1}{N-k-l}=\frac{1}{N-k}\left(1-\frac{l}{N-k}\right)^{-1}\sim\frac{1}{N-k}\left(1+\frac{l}{N-k}\right)

for
l=o(N)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{N-k-l}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{N-k}\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\left(1+\frac{l}{N-k}\right)=\frac{1}{N-k}\left(m+\frac{m(m-1)}{2(N-k)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{N-k}\left(1+\frac{m-1}{2(N-k)}\right)=\frac{m}{N-k}\left(1+O\left(\tfrac{m}{N}\right)\right).

Therefore,

\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\frac{k(k-1)}{4x_{l}}\sim\frac{k(k-1)}{2}\cdot\frac{m}{N-k}\left(1+O\left(\tfrac{m}{N}\right)\right)=\frac{mk(k-1)}{2(N-k)}\left(1+O\left(\tfrac{m}{N}\right)\right).

In addition,

\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\frac{kl}{2(N-k)}=\frac{km(m-1)}{4(N-k)}.

Substituting these into the main sum:

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}\left(\log\Gamma(x_{l}+h)-\log\Gamma(x_{l})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{mk}{2}\log\Bigl(\frac{N}{2}\Bigr)-\frac{mk^{2}}{2N}-\frac{km(m-1)}{4(N-k)}+\frac{mk(k-1)}{2(N-k)}\left(1+O\left(\tfrac{m}{N}\right)\right)+O\left(\frac{mk^{2}}{N}\right)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{mk}{2}\log\Bigl(\frac{N}{2}\Bigr)-\frac{mk^{2}}{2N}-\frac{km(m-1)}{4N}+\frac{mk(k-1)}{2N}+O\left(\frac{mk^{2}}{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{mk}{2}\log\Bigl(\frac{N}{2}\Bigr)+O\left(\frac{mk^{2}}{N}+\frac{m^{2}k}{N}\right).

The error after subtracting
\frac{mk}{2}\log(\frac{N}{2})
is
O(\frac{mk(m+k)}{N})
.
∎

Now, we combine both lemmas to prove Proposition
2.3
.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

First, we confirm that the indicator is
1
for large
N
, i.e.
\|AA^{\top}\|_{op}<1
holds for
\|B\|_{F}\leq R_{N}
. With
B=\sqrt{N}A
we have
AA^{\top}=\frac{1}{N}BB^{\top}
and thus
\|AA^{\top}\|_{op}=\frac{1}{N}\|BB^{\top}\|_{op}
. Using
\|BB^{\top}\|_{op}\leq\|B\|_{F}^{2}
and the assumption
\|B\|_{F}\leq R_{N}
, we have

\|AA^{\top}\|_{op}\leq\frac{1}{N}\|B\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{R_{N}^{2}}{N}=o(1).

Hence
\mathbf{1}_{\{\|AA^{\top}\|_{op}<1\}}=1
for large enough
N
. Using logarithm rules,

\log g_{N}(B)=-\frac{mk}{2}\log N+\log\Gamma_{m}\!\bigl(\tfrac{N}{2}\bigr)-\log\Gamma_{m}\!\bigl(\tfrac{N-k}{2}\bigr)-\frac{mk}{2}\log\pi+\frac{N-k-m-1}{2}\,\log\det(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\tfrac{BB^{\top}}{N}),

and

\log\varphi_{N}(B)=-\frac{mk}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\,\|B\|_{F}^{2}=-\frac{mk}{2}\log 2-\frac{mk}{2}\log\pi-\frac{1}{2}\,\|B\|_{F}^{2}.

Hence, with
X=\tfrac{1}{N}BB^{\top}
,

\displaystyle\log\frac{g_{N}(B)}{\varphi_{N}(B)}

\displaystyle=\left[\log\frac{\Gamma_{m}(\tfrac{N}{2})}{\Gamma_{m}(\tfrac{N-k}{2})}-\frac{mk}{2}\log N+\frac{mk}{2}\log 2\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\left[\frac{N-k-m-1}{2}\log\det(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-X)+\frac{1}{2}\|B\|_{F}^{2}\right].

By Lemma
4.2
,

\log\frac{\Gamma_{m}(\tfrac{N}{2})}{\Gamma_{m}(\tfrac{N-k}{2})}=\frac{mk}{2}\log(\tfrac{N}{2})+O\left(\frac{mk(m+k)}{N}\right),

so the first term in brackets becomes

\frac{mk}{2}\log(\tfrac{N}{2})+O\left(\frac{mk(m+k)}{N}\right)-\frac{mk}{2}\log N+\frac{mk}{2}\log 2=O\left(\frac{mk(m+k)}{N}\right).

By Lemma
4.1
, the second term is

\frac{k+m+1}{2N}\|B\|_{F}^{2}-\frac{\mathrm{Tr}[(BB^{\top})^{2}]}{4N}+R_{N}^{\prime}(B).

Therefore, for
\|B\|_{F}\leq R_{N}
, using
\mathrm{Tr}[(BB^{\top})^{2}]\leq\|B\|_{F}^{4}\leq R_{N}^{4}
again, for large
N
,

\displaystyle\Bigl|\log\frac{g_{N}(B)}{\varphi_{N}(B)}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\frac{mk(m+k)}{N}+\frac{k+m+1}{2N}R_{N}^{2}+\frac{R_{N}^{4}}{4N}+|R_{N}^{\prime}(B)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\frac{mk(m+k)}{N}+C_{2}\frac{(k+m)R_{N}^{2}}{N}+C_{3}\frac{R_{N}^{4}}{N}+C_{4}\left(\frac{(k+m)R_{N}^{4}}{N^{2}}+\frac{R_{N}^{6}}{N^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{5}\left(\frac{R_{N}^{4}}{N}+\frac{mk(m+k)}{N}+\frac{(k+m)R_{N}^{2}}{N}\right)

for positive constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4},C_{5}
and
R_{N}=o(N^{1/2})
.
∎

We now show the main ingredient for the proof of Theorem
A
: the limit of the logarithmic moment generating function. The proof uses the uniform density estimate in Proposition
2.3
on a certain growing set inside the expectation. Even though outside this set, the densities do not converge uniformly, concentration results compensate for this error.

Lemma 4.3
.

With the notation of Theorem
A
, let
Z_{N}=(g_{i,j})_{i,j}
be a sequence of
m_{N}\times k_{N}
matrices with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries. Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded function. Then, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{N}}f(y_{i,j}^{(N)})\right)\right]=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{N}}f(g_{i,j})\right)\right]=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}].

Proof.

Let
Y_{N}=(y_{i,j}^{(N)})_{i,j}
,
i=1,\ldots,m_{N}
,
j=1,\ldots,k_{N}
be an
m_{N}\times k_{N}
matrix of scaled entries of the orthogonal group from Theorem
A
, and let
G_{N}=(g_{i,j})_{i,j}
,
i=1,\ldots,m_{N}
,
j=1,\ldots,k_{N}
be a matrix of i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries of the same size. Let
p_{N}=m_{N}k_{N}
. The second equality in the lemma states that,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{N}}f(g_{i,j})\right)\right]=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}].

This follows directly from the i.i.d. property of the random variables
g_{i,j}
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{N}}f(g_{i,j})\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i,j}e^{f(g_{i,j})}\right]=\prod_{i,j}\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{i,j})}]=\left(\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}]\right)^{p_{N}}.

Thus,
\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\left(\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}]\right)^{p_{N}}=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}]
. Now, we prove the first equality in the statement. Let
R_{N}
be some sequence such that
R_{N}=o(N^{1/2})
,
p_{N}=o(R_{N}^{2})
as
N\to\infty
and
\frac{R_{N}^{4}}{Np_{N}}\to 0
(this can be achieved for example by choosing
R_{N}=N^{1/8}p_{N}^{3/8}
). By denoting
\mathbb{B}(R_{N}):=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{N}\times k_{N}}:\|A\|_{F}\leq R_{N}\}
and
g_{N}
for the density of
Y_{N}
and
\phi_{N}
for the density of
G_{N}
, we can write using
[
6
, Lemma 1.2.15]

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i,j}f(y_{i,j}^{(N)})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\max\Big\{\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\int_{\mathbb{B}(R_{N})}\exp\left(\sum_{i,j}f(y_{i,j})\right)g_{N}(y_{i,j})\,dy_{i,j},

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\int_{[-\sqrt{N},\sqrt{N}]^{m_{N}\times k_{N}}\setminus\mathbb{B}(R_{N})}\exp\left(\sum_{i,j}f(y_{i,j})\right)g_{N}(y_{i,j})\,dy_{i,j}\Big\}.

Notice that for
\|A\|_{F}\leq R_{N}
, by Proposition
2.3
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\Big|\log\left(\frac{g_{N}(A)}{\phi_{N}(A)}\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{R_{N}^{4}}{Np_{N}}+\frac{p_{N}(m+k)}{Np_{N}}+\frac{(k+m)R_{N}^{2}}{p_{N}N}\right)=C\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{R_{N}^{4}}{Np_{N}}+o(1)\right)=0

where we used the assumption on
R_{N}
. Using this calculation, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\int_{\mathbb{B}(R_{N})}\exp\left(\sum_{i,j}f(y_{i,j})\right)g_{N}(y_{i,j})\,dy_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\textbf{1}\{G_{N}\in\mathbb{B}(R_{N})\}\exp\left(\sum_{i,j}f(g_{i,j})\right)\right]\leq\log\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}].

For the lower bound in the last line we use the boundedness of
f
with
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f(x)=:f_{sup}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textbf{1}\{G_{N}\in\mathbb{B}(R_{N})\}\exp\left(\sum_{i,j}f(g_{i,j})\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i,j}f(g_{i,j})\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\textbf{1}\{G_{N}\not\in\mathbb{B}(R_{N})\}\exp\left(\sum_{i,j}f(g_{i,j})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}]^{p_{N}}-e^{f_{sup}p_{N}}\mathbb{P}(G_{N}\not\in\mathbb{B}(R_{N})).

By standard concentration results (see e.g.
[
21
, Proposition 2]
), we have
\mathbb{P}(G_{N}\not\in\mathbb{B}(R_{N}))=\mathbb{P}(\|G_{N}\|_{F}^{2}>R_{N}^{2})\leq e^{-cR_{N}^{2}}
for
N
large enough, where
c
is some constant. So the expression is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}]^{p_{N}}-e^{f_{sup}p_{N}}e^{-cR_{N}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}]^{p_{N}}-e^{(f_{sup}-\log(\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}]))p_{N}-cR_{N}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}]^{p_{N}}\left(1-o(1)\right)

where the last step follows from
\frac{R_{N}^{2}}{p_{N}}=\infty
. Combining this with the upper bound, we get the desired equality:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\textbf{1}\{G_{N}\in\mathbb{B}(R_{N})\}\exp\left(\sum_{i,j}f(g_{i,j})\right)\right]=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}].

Now, we evaluate the second term from the maximum (denoting
B=(b_{i,j})
):

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\int_{[-\sqrt{N},\sqrt{N}]^{m_{N}\times k_{N}}\setminus\mathbb{B}(R_{N})}\exp\left(\sum_{i,j}f(b_{i,j})\right)g_{N}(B)\,dB

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\int_{\mathbb{B}(R_{N})^{c}}\exp(p_{N}f_{sup})g_{N}(B)\,dB

\displaystyle=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\left(p_{N}f_{sup}+\log\mathbb{P}(A_{N}\not\in\mathbb{B}(R_{N}))\right)=f_{sup}+\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}(\|A_{N}\|_{F}\geq R_{N}).

Here
A_{N}
is an upper left
m_{N}\times k_{N}
block of a an element that is distributed according to the Haar measure on
\mathcal{O}(N)
multiplied by
\sqrt{N}
. By the concentration of Stiefel matrices (see
[
21
, Proposition 10]
), we have
\mathbb{P}(\|A_{N}\|_{F}>R_{N})\leq e^{-cR_{N}^{2}}
for
N
large enough and some constant
c>0
. This implies

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{P}(A_{N}\notin\mathbb{B}(R_{N}))\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}-c\frac{R_{N}^{2}}{p_{N}}=-\infty,

since
R_{N}^{2}/p_{N}\to\infty
. The proof is complete.
∎

The next proof is the proof of Theorem
B
. We use the previous lemma and the Gärtner-Ellis Theorem to show the LDP for the empirical measure.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

We apply the Gärtner-Ellis Theorem (Theorem
3.4
) to the sequence of empirical measures
\nu_{N}
in the Polish space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
equipped with the weak topology. For
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
the logarithmic moment generating function is

\Lambda(f)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{N}}f(y_{i,j}^{(N)})\right)\right].

By Lemma
4.3
, this limit exists for any bounded function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, and is given by

\Lambda(f)=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}].

where
g_{1,1}\sim N(0,1)
. Since
f
is bounded,
e^{f(g_{1,1})}
is integrable with respect to the Gaussian measure, so
\Lambda(f)
is finite for all
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
. As we have seen in Lemma
4.3
, the same limit occurs when we replace the
y_{i,j}^{(N)}
with i.i.d. standard Gaussians
g_{i,j}
. By Sanov’s theorem
[
6
, Theorem 6.2.10]
, the sequence
\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{N}}f(g_{i,j})\right)\right]
satisfies an LDP with speed
p_{N}
and a good rate function. By Lemma
4.3
,

\displaystyle\Lambda(f)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{N}}f(y_{i,j}^{(N)})\right)\right]=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{p_{N}}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{N}}f(g_{i,j})\right)\right]=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{f(g_{1,1})}].

The LDP in Sanov’s theorem
[
6
, Theorem 6.2.10]
implies that
\Lambda(f)
satisfies the assumptions of the Gärtner-Ellis Theorem
3.4
and hence
\nu_{N}
satisfies the same LDP.

∎

4.2
Proofs of Theorems
B
and
C

We continue with the proof of Theorem
B
. In parts, we can follow the lines of the proof of
[
16
, Theorem B]
. In other parts, the scaling
\beta_{N}
causes some technical difficulties that need to be addressed carefully.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

We start by proving a weak LDP for the block
\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}
, where
V_{m,N}^{(k)}
denotes the
m\times k
matrix consisting of the first
k
columns of
V_{m,N}
. After that, we need to prove exponential tightness to get the full LDP, since unlike in the LDP case, the random variables are no longer contained in a compact set. The claim for the entire matrix then follows in the end by the Dawson-Gärtner Theorem
3.9
.

Recall that by
[
14
, Lemma 2.5]
, the block
V_{m,N}^{(k)}
has, for
N\geq k+m
, the density
f_{N}:[-1,1]^{m\times k}\to[0,\infty)
,

f_{N}(A)=\frac{\Gamma_{m}\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{mk}{2}}\Gamma_{m}\left(\frac{N-k}{2}\right)}\operatorname{det}\left(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-AA^{\top}\right)^{\frac{N-k-m-1}{2}}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\left\|AA^{\top}\right\|<1\right\}},\quad A\in[-1,1]^{m\times k}.

By a transformation of the density with the function
V\mapsto\beta_{N}V
, the block
\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}
has density
\tilde{f}_{N}:[-\beta_{N},\beta_{N}]^{m\times k}\to[0,\infty)
given by

\tilde{f}_{N}(A)=\beta_{N}^{-mk}f_{N}\left(\frac{A}{\beta_{N}}\right)=\frac{\Gamma_{m}\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)}{\beta_{N}^{mk}\pi^{\frac{mk}{2}}\Gamma_{m}\left(\frac{N-k}{2}\right)}\operatorname{det}\left(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}AA^{\top}\right)^{\frac{N-k-m-1}{2}}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\left\|AA^{\top}\right\|<\beta_{N}^{2}\right\}},

for
A\in[-\beta_{N},\beta_{N}]^{m\times k}
.

Step 1: Weak LDP
Define
\mathbb{B}:=\{M\in[-\beta_{N},\beta_{N}]^{m\times k}:\enskip\|MM^{\top}\|<\beta_{N}^{2}\}
and its closure by
\overline{\mathbb{B}}:=\{M\in[-\beta_{N},\beta_{N}]^{m\times k}:\enskip\|MM^{\top}\|\leq\beta_{N}^{2}\}
. We want to prove a weak LDP for
\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}
with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and rate function

I_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}\to[0,\infty],\quad I_{k}(V)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|V_{i}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|V\|_{F}^{2},

i.e. we need to show the two inequalities according to Proposition
3.6
:

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\mathbb{P}\left(\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}\in B_{r}(A)\right)\leq-I_{k}(A),

(6)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\mathbb{P}\left(\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}\in B_{r}(A)\right)\geq-I_{k}(A),

(7)

where
B_{r}(A)
denotes a Euclidean ball of radius
r>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
around
A
(these sets clearly form a base of the topology). We always have
A\in\overline{\mathbb{B}}
if
N
is large enough for any fixed
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
. For large
N
,

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\mathbb{P}\left(\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}\in B_{r}(A)\right)=\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\int_{B_{r}(A)}\beta_{N}^{-mk}f_{N}\left(\frac{D}{\beta_{N}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}D

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\int_{B_{r}(A)}e^{\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\beta_{N}^{-mk}f_{N}\left(\frac{D}{\beta_{N}}\right)\right)}\textup{d}D\leq\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\int_{B_{r}(A)}e^{\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\sup_{C\in B_{r}(A)\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\beta_{N}^{-mk}f_{N}\left(\frac{C}{\beta_{N}}\right)\right)}\textup{d}D

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\vol_{mk}(B_{r}(A)\cap\mathbb{B})\right)+\sup_{C\in B_{r}(A)\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\beta_{N}^{-mk}f_{N}\left(\frac{C}{\beta_{N}}\right)\right).

The first term goes to
0
as
N\to\infty
because
\vol_{mk}(\mathbb{B})\leq(2\beta_{N})^{mk}
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\beta_{N}^{-mk}f_{N}\left(\frac{C}{\beta_{N}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\frac{\Gamma_{m}\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)}{\beta_{N}^{mk}\pi^{\frac{mk}{2}}\Gamma_{m}\left(\frac{N-k}{2}\right)}\right)+\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\frac{N-m-k-1}{2}\log\left(\det\left(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}CC^{\top}\right)\right).

We can argue as in the proof of Lemma 3.1 in
[
16
]
, that the first term goes to
0
since the argument in the logarithm grows with polynomial speed. More formally, using Stirling’s approximation, we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\frac{\Gamma_{m}\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)}{\beta_{N}^{mk}\pi^{\frac{mk}{2}}\Gamma_{m}\left(\frac{N-k}{2}\right)}\right)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{mk}\pi^{\frac{mk}{2}}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{N}{2}+\frac{1-j}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{N}{2}+\frac{1-j}{2}-\frac{k}{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\frac{1}{o(N^{\frac{mk}{2}})\pi^{\frac{mk}{2}}}\left(\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)^{\frac{mk}{2}}(1+o(1))\right)\right)=0.

The second term can be written as

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{C\in B_{r}(A)\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\frac{N-m-k-1}{2}\log\left(\det\left(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}CC^{\top}\right)\right)

(8)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{C\in B_{r}(A)\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2}\log\left(\det\left(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}CC^{\top}\right)\right).

(9)

Let
C_{1},...,C_{m}
denote the rows of
C
. Notice that
\det(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}CC^{\top})
is a polynomial in the entries of
C
with one term (the diagonal term) being
\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-\frac{\|C_{i}\|^{2}}{\beta_{N}^{2}})
and the other terms
p_{2},...,p_{m!}
are of order at least
O(\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{4}})
. This can, for example, be seen using the Leibniz formula for the determinant. If one diagonal factor is missing, then there is necessarily a second non-diagonal factor in this summand. Remember the sequence representation of
e
and, in particular,
\alpha_{N}\log(1+\frac{x}{\alpha_{N}})\to x
for
\alpha_{N}\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
. We factor out the diagonal term of the determinant and calculate

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{C\in B_{r}(A)\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2}\log\left(\det\left(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}CC^{\top}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{C\in B_{r}(A)\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2}\log\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{\|C_{i}\|^{2}}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\right)\right)+\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{C\in B_{r}(A)\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\sum_{i=2}^{m!}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}p_{i}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{\|C_{j}\|^{2}}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2}\log\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{\inf_{C\in B_{r}(A)}\|C_{i}\|^{2}}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\right)\right)+\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\sum_{i=2}^{m!}\sup_{C\in B_{r}(A)}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}p_{i}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{\|C_{j}\|^{2}}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\inf_{C\in B_{r}(A)}\|C_{i}\|^{2}.

For
N
large enough, we have
B_{r}(A)\subset\mathbb{B}
, and therefore, the supremum is not
N
-dependent. The second term vanished because of the sequence representation of
e
, since
\beta_{N}^{2}p_{i}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
and because
(\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-\frac{\|C_{j}\|^{2}}{\beta_{N}^{2}}))^{-1}
is upper bounded by a positive number for
r
being small enough. Consequently,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\mathbb{P}\left(\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}\in B_{r}(A)\right)\leq-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\inf_{C\in B_{r}(A)}\|C_{i}\|^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|A_{i}\|^{2},

by continuity in
C
. The upper bound (
6
) is satisfied.

For the lower bound, note that
\|AA^{\top}\|<\infty
and therefore
A\in\mathbb{B}
for
N
being large enough. If one replaces
\sup_{C\in B_{r}(A)\cap\mathbb{B}}
with the infimum (and vice-versa), one can argue exactly as in the upper bound after the estimate

\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\mathbb{P}\left(\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}\in B_{r}(A)\right)\geq\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\int_{B_{r}(A)}e^{\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\inf_{C\in B_{r}(A)\cap\mathbb{B}}\log(\beta_{N}^{-mk}f_{N}\left(\frac{C}{\beta_{N}}\right))}\textup{d}D.

Finally,
I_{k}
is continuous and therefore lower-semi continuous. Also

\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}:\enskip I_{k}(A)\leq y\}

is compact as a closed subset of the compact set
[-y,y]^{m\times k}
, which makes
I_{k}
a good rate function.

We proved that the sequence
\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}
, for uniform random
V_{m,N}
in
\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
, satisfies a weak LDP with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and good rate function
I_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}\to[0,\infty]
,
I_{k}(A)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|A_{i}\|^{2}
.

Step 2: Exponential tightness

We need to show that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
such that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log(\mathbb{P}(\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}\in K^{c}))\leq-\epsilon.

Note that the entries of
\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}
are not bounded. We set
K:=[-\sqrt{2m\epsilon},\sqrt{2m\epsilon}]^{m\times k}
. Notice, that for
N
large enough, we have
K\subset[-\beta_{N},\beta_{N}]^{m\times k}
. By replacing
B_{r}(A)
with
K^{c}
in the above calculation for the weak LDP upper bound, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}\in K^{c}\right)\right)\leq\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\vol_{mk}\left(K^{c}\cap\mathbb{B}\right)\right)+\sup_{C\in K^{c}\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\beta_{N}^{-mk}f_{N}\left(\frac{C}{\beta_{N}}\right)\right).

Again, the first term tends to
0
as
N\to\infty
because
\vol_{mk}(\mathbb{B})\leq(2\beta_{N})^{mk}=2^{mk}o(N^{\frac{mk}{2}})
grows at polynomial speed. Similar to the proof for the weak LDP, we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{C\in K^{c}\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{N}\log\left(\beta_{N}^{-mk}f_{N}\left(\frac{C}{\beta_{N}}\right)\right)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{C\in K^{c}\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2}\log\left(\det\left(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}CC^{\top}\right)\right).

Denote by
(\lambda_{i}^{N})_{i=1}^{m}
the eigenvalues of
CC^{\top}
in order
\lambda_{1}^{N}\geq...\geq\lambda_{m}^{N}
. We know that
0\leq\lambda_{i}^{N}
for every
i
since the Gram matrix is always positive semi-definite. Also, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{N}=\operatorname{Tr}(CC^{\top})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|C_{i}\|^{2}
. By definition of
K
at least one entry of
C
is outside of
K
. Thus, we get
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|C_{i}\|^{2}\geq\sqrt{2m\epsilon}^{2}=2m\epsilon
. This shows the lower bound
\lambda_{1}^{N}\geq 2\epsilon
for the largest eigenvalue. Since
\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}CC^{\top}
has eigenvalues
\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{i}^{N}}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\right)_{i=1}^{m}
, we end up with

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{C\in K^{c}\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2}\log\left(\det\left(\operatorname{Id}_{m}-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}CC^{\top}\right)\right)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{C\in K^{c}\cap\mathbb{B}}\frac{\beta_{N}^{2}}{2}\log\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{i}^{N}}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\left(\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{\inf_{C\in K^{c}\cap\mathbb{B}}\lambda_{i}^{N}}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\right)^{\beta_{N}^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{2\epsilon}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\right)^{\beta_{N}^{2}}\right)=-\epsilon.

This concludes the proof of the exponential tightness. We proved that the sequence
\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}
for uniform random
V_{m,N}
in
\mathbb{V}_{m,N}
, satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and good rate function
I_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}\to[0,\infty]
,
I_{k}(A)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|A_{i}\|^{2}
.

Step 3: Projective limit
We want to uplift the LDP to the entire sequence
\beta_{N}V_{m,N}
. The projective system can be defined by
\left({Y}_{j},p_{ij}\right)_{i\leq j}
, with
{Y}_{k}=\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
, and define for
i\leq j
,
p_{ij}(V)=V^{(i)}
(i.e. it removes the columns
i+1,...,j
). This clearly forms a projective system. Then, the projective limit is
{Y}:=\mathbb{R}^{m\times\infty}
, with projection maps
p_{k}(V)=V^{(k)}
. We proved that the sequence
p_{k}(\beta_{N}V_{m,N})=\beta_{N}V_{m,N}^{(k)}
satisfies an LDP with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and good rate function
I_{k}
for every fixed
k
. By the Dawson-Gärtner Theorem
3.9
, the sequence
\beta_{N}V_{m,N}
satisfies an LDP with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and good rate function
I:\mathbb{R}^{m\times\infty}\to[0,\infty]
,
I(V)=\sup_{k}I_{k}(p_{k}(V))
. The monotonicity
I_{k}(V)\leq I_{k+1}(V)
shows that the rate function reduces to
I(V)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|V_{i}\|^{2}
. The convexity of this rate function is clear. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
C
.

Recall that
\mathbb{V}_{N,N}=\mathcal{O}(N)
. We define the projective system
\left({Y}_{j},p_{ij}\right)_{i\leq j}
, by
{Y}_{m}=\mathbb{R}^{m\times\infty}
, and define for
i\leq j
,
p_{ij}(V)=V^{(i,\infty)}
(i.e. it removes the rows
i+1,...,j
). The projective limit is
{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{\infty\times\infty}
, with projection maps
p_{k}(V)=V^{(k,\infty)}
. Let
A_{N}
be chosen uniformly from
\mathcal{O}(N)
for each
N
. Then
A_{N}^{(k,\infty)}
is distributed uniformly in
\mathbb{V}_{k,N}
. By Theorem
B
, the sequence
p_{m}(\beta_{N}A_{N})=\beta_{N}A_{N}^{(m,\infty)}
satisfies an LDP with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and good convex rate function
I_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times\infty}\to[0,\infty]
,
I_{m}(A)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|A_{i}\|^{2}
. By the Dawson-Gärtner theorem
3.9
, the sequence
\beta_{N}A_{N}
satisfies an LDP with speed
\frac{N}{\beta_{N}^{2}}
and good convex rate function
J(A)=\sup_{m}I_{m}(p_{m}(A))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|A_{i}\|^{2}
, which proves the claim.
∎
