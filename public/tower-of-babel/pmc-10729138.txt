Planning for healthcare services during the COVID-19 pandemic in the Southeast of England: a system dynamics modelling approach
Authors: Abraham George, Peter Lacey, Padmanabhan Badrinath, Alex Gray, Paul Turner, Chris Harwood, Mark Gregson
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo develop, test, validate and implement a system dynamics model to simulate the pandemic progress and the impact of various interventions on viral spread, healthcare utilisation and demand in secondary care.DesignWe adopted the system dynamics model incorporating susceptible, exposed, infection and recovery framework to simulate the progress of the pandemic and how the interventions for the COVID-19 response influence the outcomes with a focus on secondary care.SettingThis study was carried out covering all the local health systems in Southeast of England with a catchment population of six million with a specific focus on Kent and Medway system.ParticipantsSix local health systems in Southeast of England using Kent and Medway as a case study.InterventionsShort to medium ‘what if’ scenarios incorporating human behaviour, non-pharmaceutical interventions and medical interventions were tested using the model with regular and continuous feedback of the model results to the local health system leaders for monitoring, planning and rapid response as needed.Main outcome measuresDaily output from the model which included number infected in the population, hospital admissions needing COVID-19 care, occupied general beds, continuous positive airway pressure beds, intensive care beds, hospital discharge pathways and deaths.ResultsWe successfully implemented a regional series of models based on the local population needs which were used in healthcare planning as part of the pandemic response.ConclusionsIn this study, we have demonstrated the utility of system dynamics modelling incorporating local intelligence and collaborative working during the pandemic to respond rapidly and take decisions to protect the population. This led to strengthened cooperation among partners and ensured that the local population healthcare needs were met.

Introduction

The emergence of the COVID-19 marked the first global pandemic in a century.1 Effectively responding to the pandemic necessitated timely and accurate epidemiological analysis and modelling. Given the novelty of this virus with limited testing availability on the outset, initially, there was scarcity of data for intelligence-led planning and response. In the UK, academics from the Imperial College2 authored reports that provided estimates of the virus’s transmissibility and infection fatality ratio. In addition, Scientific Pandemic Infections Group on modelling (SPI-M-O) developed modelling3 to inform Scientific Advisory Group for Emergencies, who, in turn, offered guidance on policy development.4 The outputs from centralised sources were disseminated to local systems which were considered by public health, National Health Service (NHS) and care sector leaders to plan for and respond to the pandemic as it progressed. However, it soon became evident that nationwide modelling was inadequate for informing localised policy actions. This was primarily due to variations in population demographics and dynamics across different regions of the country, resulting in varying rates of epidemic spread. Accordingly, the government responded by introducing tiered systems as one of the interventions to contain the epidemic.5 Hence modelling at different spatial scales including nations, regions or even smaller geographical areas such as local authority or integrated care systems (ICSs) became increasingly useful.6

Kent and Medway (K&M), located in the Southeast of England and encompassing a population of approximately 2 million, covers a large geographical area. There has been criticism of some national models in that they more often produced doomsday scenarios and rarely captured turning points in the pandemic due to various interventions.7 Moreover, outputs from the centralised national models frequently experienced delays likely attributable to the government decision-making bureaucracy. Use of simulation modelling was already in place prepandemic to help make decisions across Kent relating to acute hospital bed demand, demand for mental health beds, workforce planning and forecasting chronic disease prevalence for local authority-led public health needs assessment.8 Building on this previous work, the local NHS took the initiative to commission 'The Whole System Partnership', a consultancy specialising in strategic development of the health and care sector.9 Their objective was to build a susceptible exposed infected recovered (SEIR) model using system dynamics (SD) modelling approach.10 This model was intended to serve as a guiding tool for local decision-making and actions. SD modelling uses stocks and flows to reflect the progression of entities, in aggregate, along a pathway or between different states. The flows between states, driven by such things as risk factors (in the case of disease progression) creates the dynamics of the model, that is, behaviour over time. An SEIR model reflects four states of being, or segments of a population (figure 1). At the beginning of the pandemic, the whole population was considered susceptible. Exposure could result in infection, and subsequent recovery or death depending on virus’s characteristics and the policies implemented to manage the COVID-19 outbreak.11 ‘Therefore, SD modelling is a powerful tool for assessing the impact of multiple interventions within a complex and dynamic system’.8 Several attempts have been made globally to use modelling to inform decision-making during the COVID-19 pandemic. For example, in Singapore,12 they used an agent-based modelling approach as a basis for their SEIR epidemiology model. In Europe, mathematical modelling was used to compare various regions across the continent to understand the impacts of the various public health measures had on the various populations,13 and in the USA modelling was used to address the challenges in accurately predicting COVID-19 morbidity and mortality in the New York City metropolitan region.14 These examples are not exhaustive, and there is a plethora of international examples where modelling has been used to support decision-making. Many of these models used a similar SEIR structure, however, the modelling method varied, and included multiple network, mathematical and regression models.12–14 SD was chosen in this study, as this method has previously been used to model infectious disease outbreaks such as influenza15 and has demonstrated effective simulation of dynamic systems.

The above figure shows a simplified SEIR structure (susceptible, exposed, infected, recovered). This design is often used to model infectious disease within a population.

This study aims to provide an overview of the development, refinement, validation and use of an SD to simulate the COVID-19 pandemic in the Southeast of England and how the outputs were instrumental in local decision-making and the applicability of the approach to other systems and countries.

Methods

K&M is one of six ICSs in the Southeast region of England and initially this work began there. Other ICSs gradually became involved and during 2021 the work extended across each of these ICSs across the entire region.

Model specification: We modelled the rate of COVID-19 infections using a modified SEIR framework16 and adopted a SD approach.17 The original model was based on a SD prototype developed by iSee systems.10

The SEIR framework serves as a valuable tool for comprehending the evolving distribution of individuals within a population across its four key stages over time. At the outset of a pandemic, everyone is initially susceptible. As the pandemic unfolds, the population transitions through the remaining stages, and there is a possibility of returning to a susceptible state. This dynamic progression is pivotal in generating the demand that forms the basis of the SD model. To effectively simulate service demand throughout the course of a pandemic, such as for hospital beds, community care and mortuary spaces, it becomes essential to incorporate the supply side, effectively modelling and planning for capacity at each stage of the pandemic.

The population was divided into distinct risk groups, using sociodemographic factors, including deprivation, which were derived from prior research conducted in Kent as a component of the Joint Strategic Needs Assessment (JSNA).8 This segmentation was carried out in a manner consistent with the evolving body of evidence concerning the risk of severe disease. The risk groups were defined as low, moderate and high based on categorisation of morbidity and frailty and grouped into 10-year age bands (online supplemental table 1).18 19 Additionally, adjustment was made for deprivation of the health system catchment population (online supplemental table 2).20 21 The population of care homes in each of the included Integrated Care Partnership (ICP) was also included in the model. As a result of patients being infected and recovering from COVID-19 the model was developed to adjust the impact of COVID-19 demand on acute care based on the groupings described above and associated mortality rates.

Movement of population through the SEIR framework was initiated by the rate of potential viral transmission contacts for each ‘age group’, and also the ‘setting’ which included home, school, work and other (online supplemental table 3).22 23The SD model software employed featured a versatile functionality allowing for the optimisation and sensitivity testing of variables. This technique was initially applied in the early stages to evaluate the rate of contact as the pandemic evolved. These assessments were subsequently validated with empirical evidence18 and fine-tuned using Google Mobility data.24 To validate our model, we calculated the reproductive number over time (R(t)) using our simulated new cases and compared it to the published regional data25 for the average duration. The initial calibration of the model was carried out using the initial reproductive number (R(0)). Furthermore, the total number of modelled community cases was validated against publicly available data26 27 on a daily basis during the initial phases of the pandemic and transitioned to a weekly validation process as the pandemic advanced.

The model was initiated using the earliest date on which a wild strain of the virus was detected in sufficient numbers to result in significant community spread for each of the ICPs. The wild strain was assigned specific transmissibility and severe infection rates, and on introduction of the vaccine, a rate of vaccine evasion. As the virus evolved into new variants and sub lineages the model was expanded to include the most recent variants of concern and their progressive dominance. For each new variant, an initial date and number infected were determined and ratios of transmissibility, severe infection and vaccine evasion were arrived at compared with the wild strain (online supplemental table 4).28 This was derived from using nationally published reports28 29 relating to the above-mentioned characteristics of the variant.

The modelled demand from transmission was adjusted to reflect non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), which included government lockdown measures, introduction of population tiers,30 social distancing guidelines, mask wearing, working from home directive and school closures in the early stages of the pandemic. Furthermore, super spreading events were factored into the model (online supplemental tables 5 and 6). Subsequently, the COVID-19 vaccination programme was integrated into the model. The following variables were incorporated, namely, the weekly number of vaccinations categorised by age and risk group, the percentage of individuals receiving a second dose and the booster vaccination rates. These data were sourced from the National Immunisation Management System, which is not publicly accessible.31 Additionally, the model factored in the disease severity attenuation attributed to vaccination, as per the consensus of local experts. These adjustments reflected the age and risk profile in order to dynamically simulate the spread and impact from COVID-19, for example, the effect of discharging hospital patients into care homes.

The individuals within the recovered group had a degree of immunity from either natural infection or vaccine induced. The strength of this immunity declined over time, with the rate of decline depending on how the immunity was acquired, the specific variant in circulation, as well as the individual’s age and risk group. Once the immunity had waned, this population became susceptible and re-entered the SEIR model. This allowed for the modelling of the pandemic’s progress in the long run. Since the advent of the Omicron variant the risk of reinfection was also included in the model (online supplemental table 7).

The model’s supply side reflected the care and facilities that were available within the local health systems. As patients progressed through the SEIR stages, the more severely affected cases were modelled as hospital admissions entering the hospital section of the model. Within this section, the model simulated the care requirements in line data and assumptions gathered at the time. Three levels of care were modelled, each necessitating an acute hospital bed, with variations in the level of resources required at each stage. These levels included general acute, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) and the intensive care unit (ICU). It is important to note that the progression through these care levels was not linear, and patients could (and still can) experience fluctuations in their care needs, including step-up and step-down elements within their acute phase of care. In addition to modelling acute bed demand, mortality rates within the hospital were also considered, as well as the demand for discharge pathways into community care. We also simulated the potential impact that improvements in the care delivered (including innovation) had on the various levels of need, mortality rates and discharge. These improvements encompassed pharmaceutical enhancements such as the administration of dexamethasone.

The modelled rate of admission and occupancy of hospital beds was validated to locally published data which was circulated to local partners. Bed availability was provided by each hospital in order to compare against projected demand and identify times of potential over occupancy. Similarly, care home capacity was provided by local public health officials to identify any gap between capacity and demand.

By using these supply and demand factors, users of the model were able to formulate scenarios to generate valuable insights for planning their operational healthcare responses to COVID-19. For instance, they could determine the number of fully operational COVID-19 beds required based on the disease’s transmission and the existing or planned social restrictions. Additionally, they could ascertain when and where additional placements would be needed.

Model structure and parameter fitting: Figure 2 provides a conceptual diagram of the model. The entire population initiates in a susceptible state, represented in orange. This population is further stratified into 10-year age groups and risk categories determined by the likelihood of experiencing severe disease. The risk of severe infection is influenced by these two groups and index of multiple deprivation. The susceptible population flows to the exposed state (in yellow) influenced by factors such as rate of contact, the implementation and scale of NPIs and the emergence of new variants and sub lineages. The exposed population moves to an infected state (in green) and subsequently enters either an asymptomatic or presymptomatic phase. Those who are asymptomatic recover (in blue) and develop natural immunity. Following the presymptomatic phase, they progress to either mild/moderate or severe infection state. Mild/moderate infections result in recovery without the necessity for hospital admission and develop natural immunity. In contrast, severe infections require hospital admission, which can be either in a general acute bed, CPAP bed or ICU bed. The modelled outcome is they either recover or die. Those who recover can then follow one of the three discharge care pathways, determined by their levels of community care need. Pathway 0/1 (P0/1) is for patients who need no care or home care, pathway 2 (P2) is patients requiring care in a community hospital bed and pathway 3 (P3) is in a care home bed. These patients subsequently recover and develop natural immunity.

A simplified overview of the model with the SEIR structure (susceptible, exposed, infected, recovered). The model within the grey area represents the pandemic within the community, and the green section represents the in-hospital. The model also includes step V, where the population is vaccinated, which flows population from susceptible to recovered with a degree of immunity, without the need for infection. SD, system dynamics. IMD, Index of Multiple of Deprivation.

As natural immunity wanes, individuals in the recovered group become susceptible once more. Vaccination can be administered to the susceptible stock to provide induced immunity. In figure 2, the model diagram within the grey background reflects the progress of the pandemic in the community and the section of the diagram within the green background denotes the demand of the pandemic for hospital care.

The model as described above was built with SD licensed software.32 The formalised fitting of the model outputs to actual data ensured robust parameter calibration. This process was conducted on a daily basis at the start of the pandemic and weekly as the pandemic progressed. The model was initialised from 1 January 2020 generating daily outputs currently up to April 2023. An example of the full model outputs can be found in online supplemental table 8.

Qualitative validation: In order to gain insights into the value of the engagement process with local systems as a contribution to the overall benefits from the work, we conducted a brief online survey of those that we met with regularly. The survey took place in February 2023 at a time when the significance of COVID-19 for service planning was becoming less important, and when two of the six systems with whom we had engaged had ceased being a regular part of the network. The survey sought to ascertain how, and with what benefit, people had engaged with the modelling work. There were three ways that people could engage, each being covered by the survey, namely by people accessing the model directly in a secure online environment with the ability to run scenarios and download model outputs; second through engagement in the debate based on model outputs held regularly at virtual meeting; and finally, by using the reports and model outputs circulated to the group on regular occasions. The survey design gathered some simple demographic data and then posed questions to elicit a response on a scale of 1–10 as to the relevance, usefulness, timeliness and responsiveness of the modelling team.

The survey recipients included colleagues from acute hospitals, commissioners, clinicians, senior analysts and public health consultants who during the pandemic used the outputs to plan and mount an effective response. The survey was distributed using ‘Survey Monkey’33 to 30 colleagues and followed up with two reminders to maximise the response rate. For open-ended questions, responders could input up to three answers. Average scores were derived from scaled questions. For survey questions, please refer to online supplemental table 9, and for full survey responses, please refer to online supplemental tables 10–14. The questions included relevance, usefulness, timeliness, value for money, responsiveness and their support for local modelling. We also sought information on how the model outputs were being used in the local system and the added value on top of the national modelling outputs. The survey was undertaken as part of the job roles of the respondents who took part, and the authors, which was a collaborative piece of work. As this was undertaken as a service activity to inform healthcare planning and delivery ethical approval is not required as per our institutional guideline.

Qualitative survey

A survey was distributed to 30 users, resulting in 10 responses received. Respondents represented several ICSs including Hampshire and Isle of Wight, Buckinghamshire, Oxfordshire and Berkshire, Surrey Heartlands, and K&M ICSs, with notable concentration of responses from K&M, where 6 of the 10 responses originated (online supplemental table 10).

Online supplemental figure 1 presents the average scores from the scaled outputs of the survey, while comprehensive results can be found in online supplemental table 11. Across all questions, the average scores ranged from 7.7 to 9.1. The model’s relevance and usefulness received scores of 8 and 7.7, respectively, underscoring the strength of the model outputs. Notably, the support for local modelling received a score of 8.7, indicating the strong consensus among local systems regarding the necessity of local modelling efforts.

Open-ended questions provided additional insights into how modelling outputs were utilised, primarily for capacity and resource planning, but also for enhancing communication throughout the pandemic (online supplemental table 12). Respondents highlighted a key advantage of this modelling approach, in addition to the centralised models, was the ability to use local data and its relevance to the local situation (online supplemental table 13). Furthermore, respondents suggested aspects that could be included in future modelling efforts, such as influenza and wider health determinants (online supplemental table 14).

Discussion

We successfully developed an SD model based on the SEIR framework, calibrated to local ICPs using locally sourced data. Model outputs included critical information that clinicians and health system leaders’ activity sought throughout the pandemic. These outputs encompassed metrics such as, number of community cases, new admissions to hospitals, acute hospital bed requirements by type (general acute, CPAP, ICU), discharge pathways and deaths in hospitals. Notably, the model exhibited strong concordance with real world data (figure 3A). The models ran daily, with outputs routinely shared with system leaders on a weekly basis, enabling them to make informed decisions regarding resource planning and deployment in response to the ongoing pandemic.

A significant strength of this study lay in the utilisation of real time local data and intelligence, enabling the formulation of local assumptions to refine and enhance the model’s relevance and usefulness locally. This was particularly vital as national level assumptions often proved less suitable. Moreover, during the initial stages, challenges arose due to inconsistencies in data submissions from local hospital provider organisations. However, the very act of modelling and regular sense checking with local stakeholders against actuals led to further improvements. The use of SD enabled us not only to incorporate standard quantitative variables such as the R(o) but also include complex system factors, for example, human behaviour. We did not use R(t) as an input but rather estimated it over time as a model output. We did, of course, need an initial R(o). This was a major strength in the modelling as alternatives available at the time ran what-if scenarios based on different R(t) without reference to the progress of the pandemic, while our modelling kept a record of the balance of SEIR populations, and therefore, could estimate R(t) quite effectively given R(o). A limitation of this study was that data systems collecting information on key aspects of COVID-19 response such as details of hospital management of COVID-19 cases, were separate and could not be integrated due to technical and information governance limitations. Due to the novel nature of this virus little pre-existing information was available on various aspects. In these regard, attempts were made to construct reliable assumptions from local experts. In any new situation, the required data are not collected initially and the only option available is to use local experts which is common practice.35 The immediate solution is to rapidly replace the real data as it becomes available and use an iterative process to derive at the best estimate.

A second limitation is that the model does not include incidental admissions with COVID-19 for other conditions or hospital-acquired COVID-19 cases. This situation can be attributed, in part, to inconsistencies in reporting and a divergence in perspective. The modelling effort primarily focused on exploring admissions and bed requirements for patients admitted for COVID-19. Whereas locally hospitals accounted for COVID-19-occupied beds, encompassing cases acquired within the hospital setting and incidental admissions. In the pre-Omicron period, the additional occupied beds seen in hospitals was relatively small (subject to good infection control) and identified hospitals where this might be a challenge, leading to informed local action. The growth in incidental admissions with the arrival of Omicron transformed the position from December 2021 where beds occupied by people with COVID-19 reflected underlying severe disease and greater swings in asymptomatic or mild disease that was not the cause of admission but did need to be planned for. In our view, all models are oversimplification of real-life situations and are ‘broadly right but precisely wrong’ which needs to be considered in any interpretation. It is likely that we underestimated the number of COVID-19 hospitals beds needed as we did not include nosocomial COVID-19 and those who admitted to non-COVID-19 reasons and tested positive we referred to as incidental admissions in our modelling. We performed external analysis outside of the model to ascertain both incidental and nosocomial COVID-19 cases. In future, researchers need to understand detailed epidemiology of any new pathogen and include all potential ways of acquiring the given infection.

Another limitation of our SD model approach includes reliance on local data which was often inconsistent and incomplete, and we try to overcome this by triangulation of various data sources. Data quality and completeness will continue to be an issue and researchers need to be aware of this and methods such as triangulation as appropriate. The other limitation of this modelling approach is the complexity of the assumptions leading to challenges in aligning actual experience with model outputs in real time.

In any modelling approach, some of the assumptions could be complex and it is essential to continually monitor the model performance against actuals and adjust the assumptions as the modelling progresses. This did not reflect in detail the variability of the transmission dynamics of individual contacts and clustering of outbreaks. The variability could be better tested using other modelling methods such as agent-based modelling. There is always the potential that our modelling underestimated the variability of the epidemic spread as it did not include the impact of individual contacts and clustering of outbreaks. The way to overcome this limitation is to use another modelling approach, for example, agent-based modelling.36

Chung and Chew12 developed an SEIR multiplex network model to study the virus in Singapore and were able to produce outputs which corresponded well with the actuals and allowed scenario testing, however, the model required use of a simplified population, and impact of demographics could not be considered. Similarly, Wang et al37 developed a well-fitting multiscale modelling framework to investigate the relationship between the spread of COVID-19 within and between hosts, however, the within-host model assumes all beings are average and does not account for demographics. A key advantage of using SD is that this framework allows the model to be arrayed by demographic factors such as age and gender. Additionally, work previously conducted for the Kent JSNA8 was used to determine risk stratification of the population, which was included as an additional demographic factor. Dai et al38 employed a variation of the SEIR model, including a loop to return recovered individuals to the susceptible population. Their model demonstrated ability to accurately predict historical trends. However, outputs were focused on an overall trend, making it very useful for informing policy, but less useful for day-to-day healthcare planning. One notable advantage of the SD approach is the flexibility it affords in choosing the modelling frequency. This allows for modelling of daily new cases and corresponding healthcare capacity needs.

All national health systems across countries and continents are organised differently but local health systems are key in any healthcare system. Modelling done at national level may not be the answer for local action. The applicability of our study for other countries and systems is that we have clearly demonstrated that using local collaboration, data and intelligence it is possible to model outputs that are relevant at a local level for action decision-making. When compared with national modelling initiatives, local modelling projects, such as ours can produce outputs that are more finely tuned, nuanced and directly applicable. They also offer the advantage of being subject to regular validation and peer review. The feedback we received from clinicians and policy-makers underscores their strong trust and enthusiasm for such localised modelling efforts. As a result, there is a clear need to bolster the modelling capabilities within local health and care systems, rather than solely relying on centralised approached entirely. Using the same approach to modelling, it is possible to scenario generate the post pandemic effects of COVID-19 particularly the burden of Long COVID-19 and other underlying comorbidities in the population, and concurrent effect of seasonal influenza. From our research, we suggest the following five policy recommendations. (1) Modelling done at national level may not be appropriate or relevant for local health systems. Policy-makers need to provide the necessary resources for local modelling using local experts and data for local action. (2) Results of the local modelling need to be incorporated into all decision-making at local level. (3) It is important to choose the right level and scale for local modelling as it is resource intensive. Our study demonstrates the population catchment for local modelling should be at least one million. (4) Policy-makers need to appreciate the need for better joined up local health information systems to improve capture of critical data which will allow better SD modelling, and this needs prioritising. (5) Timeliness of national modelling did not track to changes in a fast-paced emerging pandemic. National assumptions were not always in sync with the emerging evidence, for example, the national projections about the demand for intensive care beds did not emerge. Hence policy-makers should encourage the development of local modelling initiatives to complement any national modelling work.

Conclusion

In this study, we have demonstrated the utility of SD for the local health system planning and response, incorporating intelligence and collaborative working during the pandemic to respond rapidly and take decisions to protect the population.
