Beyond predictive accuracy: A case for mechanism-informed, uncertainty-aware machine learning in food microbiology
Authors: Youssef Ezzaky, Elgin Ee Lin Yap, Wei Ning Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFoodborne illness and avoidable food waste both depend on decisions about how microorganisms behave in a food. Machine-learning (ML) models can achieve strong predictive accuracy within a defined data domain, but a held-out test error alone does not establish reliability for food-safety decisions. Conventional predictive-microbiology models are usually empirical rather than fully mechanistic, although their parameters have biological interpretation; ML offers flexible learning of complex, high-dimensional relationships. Mechanism-informed hybrids can incorporate kinetic or physiological constraints within a learning workflow. Such constraints may improve plausibility, data efficiency, and interpretability within a declared applicability domain, but they do not confer universal or unrestricted extrapolation. We outline four hybrid design patterns and propose a food-specific validation framework that includes context-relevant external validation, calibrated predictive uncertainty, and decision-focused evaluation.

Introduction

Foodborne illness is one of the most significant preventable burdens of disease worldwide.1,2,3 In 2021, contamination of foods led to approximately 866 million illnesses and 1.5 million deaths, with young children under 5 representing almost one-third of the caseload, despite accounting for only 9% of the total world population.4 The problem of disease caused by food is compounded by the disposal of 1.05 billion tonnes of food in a retail/consumer context per year.5 Both issues share a common technical deficiency: an inability to predict how microbes will behave in a given food product under relevant conditions.

Predictive microbiology aims to support precisely these decisions. Primary models describe changes in a microbial population over time, including quantities such as lag time, growth rate, and population density. Secondary models relate those parameters to factors such as temperature, pH, and water activity (aw). Tertiary tools implement such models in accessible decision-support software; established examples include ComBase,6 the USDA Pathogen Modeling Program,7 Sym’Previus,8 and MicroHibro.9 These tools differ in scope and underlying databases. Most classical primary and secondary models are empirical in form, not fully mechanistic. Their value is that several parameters have explicit biological interpretation (for example, lag time, maximum specific growth rate, cardinal temperatures, and minimum aw), allowing expert scrutiny and physiological plausibility checks. In this perspective, “mechanism-informed” refers to a hybrid design that uses established biological relationships or constraints; it does not imply that the hybrid itself is a complete mechanistic model.

In practice, predictions are never just values; someone must act on them, and the cost of an error varies significantly. An operator must decide whether to release or withhold a batch; a developer must decide how to change the salt content, acidity, or aw of a food product; a retailer must decide on the expiration date of the product; a regulator must sign off on a hazard analysis critical control point (HACCP) plan; these are all instances where a prediction must inform a decision. Crucially, accuracy measured by error on a train-test split is not sufficient: dependability and interpretability are what support a safety decision.

Random forest models, gradient boosting models, neural network models, and other ML techniques typically demonstrate comparable or better performance than the standard mechanistic two-step or global models in selected benchmark comparisons.10,11,12 Reported ML performance gains have not by themselves translated into routine use. Literature often frames the issue as a contest between conventional empirical predictive models and ML, with the latter judged by controlled benchmark performance. That framing is inadequate. A low root-mean-square error (RMSE) after a random train-test split does not show that a model is calibrated, fit for regulatory or operational use, or informative about uncertainty. We instead propose judging models by whether they support a specified decision under uncertainty. Empirical parameter-based models contribute to interpretable structure; ML contributes flexible learning of complex interactions. Hybrid approaches may combine these strengths, but their value must be demonstrated for a stated application domain rather than assumed from their architecture.

Empirical parameter-based and data-driven models: Complementary rather than competing

Classical empirical predictive microbiology models and data-driven ML are sometimes presented as antagonistic approaches, yet both address the same problem: how a microbial population changes over time under stated conditions. Classical primary and secondary models provide interpretable kinetic parameters and relationships with temperature, pH, and aw.13,14,15 Their biological interpretation makes assumptions visible to domain experts, but it does not make them fully mechanistic or universally extrapolative. ML fits flexible functions that can capture interactions not represented in a specified empirical form but can also yield misleading predictions when inputs or food matrices fall outside the training distribution.16 Neither type of empirical model should be assumed to predict reliably in conditions it has not adequately represented. A mechanism-informed hybrid can encode known relationships, retain interpretable outputs, and provide explicit applicability-domain and uncertainty checks. Taken together, these studies show that point-prediction gains are context-dependent and do not, by themselves, justify replacing empirical parameter-based models. Most comparisons emphasize point-error metrics; uncertainty calibration and decision-specific performance are reported less consistently. The evidence therefore supports systematic comparison and integration where justified: an empirical component can preserve interpretable kinetic relationships, while ML may represent residual nonlinearities or high-dimensional inputs. Whether that combination adds value must be assessed against relevant empirical and ML comparators under the intended validation conditions.

First, there is in-sample optimism. In nearly all studies that report performance gains, improvement in prediction error comes from random train-test splits of a single dataset. In reality, records from the same experiment are strongly correlated, leaking training data to the test set and favoring flexible models that reward interpolation over systematic improvement.16,24,25,26,27 In fact, external validation, i.e., results obtained using entirely separate sites, seasons, or instruments, is very limited.27

Second, distribution shift is common. Reformulation, new protein sources, process changes, and variation in cold-chain conditions can create inputs or food matrices outside the training range.28,29 Neither empirical parameter-based models nor ML models should be presumed reliable in such settings. Known biological relationships can expose implausible behavior, but they do not guarantee safe extension beyond the evidence base; explicit domain checks and external validation remain necessary.

Third, there is uncertainty. Food decisions (e.g., use-by dates, batch release) require a stated level of safety, not merely a point estimate. Typically, reports are based on error metrics like R2, RMSE, and accuracy factors; uncertainty metrics like prediction interval coverage and calibration are rare.30,31

Fourth, a prediction must usually support hazard analysis, HACCP plans, regulatory submission dossiers, or action plans. Interpretability is not just a stylistic preference; it enables the embedding of model predictions in complex procedures and is a prerequisite for adoption. It also favors designs that maintain the meaning of biological parameters.32,33

Finally, there is the issue of data scarcity and reproducibility. Challenge studies are expensive and time-consuming. Publicly available datasets are sparse and fragmented. Samples are often small, and critical metadata (e.g., aw and pH) is often missing.34,35,36,37,38 This is precisely the scenario in which purely data-driven models are most prone to overfitting and in which a mechanism is most needed.

None of these concerns implies that pure ML approaches are fundamentally inappropriate. In data-rich environments with abundant labels and weak or unavailable prior structures, such as some sensor streams, binary spoilage classifications, or sequence-based identification tasks, flexible algorithms may be the appropriate choice. Where established biological relationships are relevant, data are limited, uncertainty must be communicated, and outcomes are consequential, a mechanism-informed hybrid is a candidate to evaluate rather than a universally preferable solution.

Four hybrid architectures for mechanism-informed ML

One approach is to use established biological relationships as constraints or priors in ML,39 rather than treating either a fully data-driven or a fully specified empirical model as the only option. This does not make a hybrid universally preferable. Its value depends on whether the constraint is relevant, accurately specified, and useful for the stated food-safety decision. Hybrid architecture formalizes this option by combining interpretable kinetic relationships with modern learning capacity. Our contribution is to link the selection of hybrid architecture to the decision it must support and to the validation and uncertainty requirements developed below.

Perhaps the most direct application of these architectures is the parameter network, where the weights of an ML model are trained to predict parameters of an interpretable kinetic model. For example, neural networks have been used to replace pre-assigned mechanistic forms by learning cell diffusivity and logistic growth functions in cell migration models, to calibrate maximum specific growth rates, yields, and internal resistance based on Monod kinetics for bioelectrochemical systems, or to learn Koopman operators for modeling microbial fermentation processes.40,41,42 Such a network consists of a final output layer that maintains its biological relevance, while allowing the relationships between each input and each parameter in the model to be learned. In a formal sense, this is similar to a one-step global fit; however, in place of a determined secondary model, a function is substituted whose form is chosen by ML.

A second structure is the residual corrector, in which an empirical kinetic model provides an initial prediction and an ML component learns the residual difference between prediction and observation. This retains the explicit kinetic assumptions while allowing correction within the range represented by the training data; it does not validate the residual correction outside that range. A third approach is the physics-informed neural network, in which governing equations or biologically justified constraints are incorporated as soft terms in the training loss. A fourth design is a Bayesian hybrid, in which parameters of an empirical kinetic model are updated with data to provide predictive distributions alongside point estimates, subject to the assumptions of the model and available evidence.43,44,45

To argue the case for such architecture requires empirical evidence. In fields beyond food microbiology, incorporating mechanistic structure has been shown to frequently outperform simple ML methods.17,18 Moreover, hybrid mechanistic machine-learning models have successfully improved predictive capacity in applications like fermentation and inactivation modeling. For example, improvements have been achieved by integrating kinetic equations with physics-informed neural networks,46 utilizing sparse regression techniques like sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics to identify governing equations,47 and integrating random forest algorithms with empirical models like the Weibull model.48 The application in food microbiology is, by comparison, immature, and extensive empirical validation studies that consider genuine extrapolation are needed before strong claims about hybrid architectures can be made.

Considering a cardinal-temperature relationship governing microbial growth, a parameter-network hybrid can retain this biological constraint while learning residual variation from data (Figure 1). This may improve physiological plausibility near the boundary of the represented domain, but it does not establish validity for unrepresented formulations or conditions. The model should therefore state its applicability domain, flag out-of-domain inputs, and widen or withhold uncertainty-qualified predictions when supporting evidence is insufficient. Its purpose is disciplined decision support under defined uncertainty, not the presentation of forecasts as validated beyond the declared domain.

A validation framework for decision-support models in food microbiology

For a model intended to support an operational food-safety decision, held-out accuracy should be complemented by a prespecified, food-specific validation strategy (Figure 2). Existing tools can contribute pieces of that strategy: one-step/global modeling, shared data resources such as ComBase,6 and multilevel approaches for characterizing variability and uncertainty. We use transparent reporting of a multivariable prediction model for individual prognosis or diagnosis, adaptation for artificial intelligence (TRIPOD + AI) and prediction model risk of bias assessment tool (PROBAST)49,50,51 only as structured reporting and bias-assessment resources, not because clinical prediction and food microbiology are equivalent. The first criterion is validation by design, beginning with a clearly defined target decision and applicability domain. Temporal validation asks whether performance transfers across production periods or supply conditions when those factors are relevant to use. Geographic or laboratory validation asks whether performance transfers across sites, instruments, enumeration protocols, and operators. Domain validation asks whether it transfers across a new strain, matrix, or formulation. These are distinct questions, not universal tests that every model must always pass. Model developers should select the axes relevant to the proposed use, justify any omitted axis, and prespecify the validation split before evaluation. External validation addresses optimism and distribution shift; uncertainty calibration addresses the absence of usable intervals; and decision-focused evaluation addresses whether a performance measure maps to the food-safety action at stake.54,55

These dimensions are not arbitrary imports from clinical practice; each represents a concrete route by which a food model can fail even when its recorded inputs, such as temperature, pH, and aw, are held fixed. Temporal or seasonal validation is relevant only when the intended use spans supply periods in which unmeasured factors can vary, including raw-material microbiota, strain composition, or background flora. Seasonality and geographic origin have been associated with shifts in raw-milk microbiota,56 and regional and seasonal microbiome differences have been reported in retail poultry.57 If the intended setting is tightly controlled and the relevant causal variables are measured, a seasonal split may add little and can be waived with justification. Laboratory validation matters where instruments, enumeration protocols, and operators differ systematically; domain validation matters where strain, matrix, or formulation changes the biological response at identical nominal set points.

Published applications show why validation must extend beyond a single error metric. Ezzaky et al.21 developed a random-forest model for Salmonella inactivation in a model hybrid alternative-protein matrix. In the development setting, the model achieved R2 = 0.91, RMSE = 0.52, Bf = 1.09, and Af = 2.24. When evaluated using a commercially available hybrid-protein substrate, for which the authors observed a protective matrix effect, performance decreased to R2 = 0.75 and RMSE = 1.04. This is the type of matrix-domain validation that a food-safety model should report: performance in the development matrix did not transfer unchanged to the commercial matrix.

Gowda et al.58 provide a complementary safety-oriented example. Their Listeria monocytogenes model for ready-to-eat meats achieved a validation RMSE of 0.074 for μmax, Bf = 0.95, Af = 1.50, and 81.03% of relative errors within the acceptable simulation zone. Nevertheless, 7% of lag-time predictions were biased in the fail-dangerous direction. The lesson is not that the model is invalid; rather, aggregate fit and acceptable-prediction percentages do not reveal every error relevant to a food-safety decision. Validation should therefore report the direction of error, the food matrix and intended use, and, where an uncertainty method is available, empirical prediction interval coverage on an independent or deliberately shifted dataset.

The calibrated-uncertainty criterion should be reported with equally concrete quantities. When a model supplies prediction intervals for microbial concentration, validation should report their empirical coverage, average interval width, and the proportion of safety thresholds correctly classified on an independent or deliberately shifted dataset. Low coverage relative to the nominal interval level indicates overconfidence, whereas very wide intervals may have good coverage but limited operational value. These checks are directly applicable to growth and inactivation models and avoid relying on a clinical analogy.31

Biological variability and uncertainty should be separated explicitly. Variability refers to real heterogeneity, for example among strains, batches, or individual cells; uncertainty reflects imperfect knowledge of parameters, model structure, measurements, or future inputs. Classical statistical practice can represent these quantities separately through prediction and confidence intervals, so the concern is not that classical models necessarily mix them. The practical problem is that reports and downstream decisions often retain only a point forecast. A decision-grade model should state which sources are included in each interval, propagate them where feasible, and report calibration on relevant independent data.30,45,52

Two further elements complete the framework. First, models should be evaluated against the decision itself, using prespecified safety thresholds, asymmetric costs of false-safe and false-alarm errors, and, where relevant, time to detection. Second, distribution-shift stress tests should use explicit scenarios such as a novel matrix, extended storage condition, or altered formulation. The validation plan should describe these scenarios before performance is examined, reducing the scope for selecting favorable data splits after the fact. Clinical-prediction reporting frameworks are useful here only at the level of transparent reporting and bias assessment. Food-microbiology models face different data structures: challenge studies can be small and costly; microbial outcomes can be batch—rather than person-specific; and applicability depends strongly on organism, matrix, process, and intended use. Its scientific rationale is the food-specific failure modes above, and its requirements should be proportionate to the intended decision and available independent data.53,59

Applications of mechanism-informed hybrid prediction models along the food supply chain

Deployment of a mechanism-informed hybrid requires more than selecting an architecture. Before use, developers must define the decision and unit of prediction; specify the input-data and metadata requirements; identify an applicability domain; preserve the interpretable constrained component; provide calibrated uncertainty; and establish monitoring, version control, and recalibration triggers. These are hybrid-deployment requirements because the relationship between the learned component and the biological constraint must remain traceable after deployment (Figure 3).Figure 3Potential applications of mechanism-informed hybrid prediction models across the food supply chainAt each stage, deployment requires defined scope, input-quality checks, applicability-domain monitoring, calibrated uncertainty, and governed updating; dynamic prediction is not unique to hybrids.

At each stage, deployment requires defined scope, input-quality checks, applicability-domain monitoring, calibrated uncertainty, and governed updating; dynamic prediction is not unique to hybrids.

Any model expressible as a function of time can generate dynamic predictions. The distinction is therefore not dynamic versus static. For a hybrid, the added design objective is to combine a traceable biological constraint with a learned correction or parameterization, then test whether that combination improves decision performance and uncertainty calibration in its stated domain. Neither property should be presumed from the word “hybrid” alone. The applications below identify where those deployment requirements change the decision, rather than claiming that hybrids alone can model dynamic conditions.

A prominent application is the cold chain and dynamic shelf life. ML regression using electronic-nose, hyperspectral, gas-sensor, and time-temperature data can update a remaining-life estimate.28,61,62,63,64,65 For deployment, the model must specify sensor quality-control rules, missing-data handling, the temperature-history range represented during training, and the action rule associated with its uncertainty output.

In a dynamic shelf-life setting, a mechanism-informed hybrid could use a kinetic relation for temperature-dependent microbial response while learning residual, sensor- or matrix-specific effects. Its deployment value must be demonstrated, not assumed: compared with an appropriate conventional dynamic model and a purely data-driven comparator, it should be evaluated on independent temperature histories and matrices for threshold classification, interval coverage, and the waste-versus-risk trade-off. When an input history lies outside the supported range, the system should flag that condition and follow a predefined conservative workflow rather than presenting an apparently precise remaining-life value.

At primary production, processing, retail, and consumer stages, deployment also requires traceable data provenance, explicit microbial and matrix scope, and a procedure for handling missing or newly observed factors. A hybrid may combine a known process relation with high-dimensional sensor, sequence, or environmental data, but its learned component must be monitored for drift and its biological constraints documented. These requirements are distinct from the generic ability of a model to generate a time-dependent prediction.

Almost all predictive microbiology and ML studies focus on single organisms, yet any commercial decision requires integrating multiple hazards. QMRA offers an integrative framework, although to date most ML application of this has been for outbreak tracing66 and source attribution, rather than for prediction; models generally predict the occurrence of only a single pathogen.67 Forward prediction across multiple hazards is constrained more by biological than by computational limitations; assessing several competing populations often depends on combined stochastic models of pathogens and spoilage flora68 or models that explicitly deal with competition between the target pathogens/spoilage flora and background flora.69

For multi-hazard or QMRA applications, a hybrid must also state how uncertainty is passed between modules, how dependencies among hazards are represented, and which component is recalibrated when new evidence arrives. Some biological interactions can be described with explicit models, such as Lotka-Volterra-type competition70 or Jameson-effect constraints, while a learned component may capture residual high-dimensional variation. The limitation remains data availability: measurements involving multiple pathogens are scarce, and resources such as ComBase focus mainly on single-species responses. A shared foundation of FAIR data and public benchmarks is therefore a prerequisite, not an optional enhancement.71

Building the foundations: Data, benchmarks, and reporting standards

The data and reporting deficit described above cannot be fixed by improving individual models in isolation. It requires a shared infrastructure, and the progress of general ML benchmarking suites is a useful example, although the specifics need adaptation for food systems.25,26,72

The first priority is FAIR data and shared benchmarks.35 A recent review identifies limited data availability and inadequate metadata as major barriers to using ML in food microbiology.34 ComBase is currently the closest food-microbiology analog to resources such as ImageNet or OpenML,26 but it lacks standardized metadata, particularly for aw, formulation composition, and inoculum preparation. Its coverage is also restricted to a relatively narrow range of food matrices compared with the breadth of biological-image and general tabular-data benchmarks. A versioned, machine-readable extension of ComBase with harmonized metadata could support direct model comparisons and provide a common resource for external validation.

The second priority is the development of reporting standards. Table 3 adapts relevant TRIPOD + AI and PROBAST principles for food-microbiology prediction models. The additions include declaring the decision supported by the model, reporting relevant external-validation results alongside internal error, presenting calibration curves and prediction-interval coverage where uncertainty is modeled, and documenting the conditions for which the model has and has not been evaluated. These are food-specific adaptations, not an assertion that clinical and food models should be assessed identically.

The third priority is implementation infrastructure. Parameter networks, physics-informed neural networks, and Bayesian hybrids require transparent software, versioned model specifications, reproducible training data, and interfaces with established predictive-microbiology resources. Related open-source implementations are emerging,46,73,74 but food-microbiology deployment also requires validation against relevant data resources such as ComBase and compatibility with existing tertiary tools.

A deployed model will encounter conditions absent from its original training data, including novel formulations, shifts in raw-material microbiota, and changed handling practices. As operating distributions shift, performance can deteriorate.75 The response should be one of governed change control rather than unmonitored automatic modification: model versions, training data, external validation, calibration, and retraining triggers should be documented. Biological constraints can provide a plausibility check during updates, but they do not guarantee validity. Prediction intervals and drift-monitoring signals can prioritize data collection where evidence is most needed, provided their limitations and potential sampling biases are managed.76,77

This is not a risk-free process, as biased data collection, derived from actively querying true labels only for the model’s uncertain outputs, distorts the data distribution and can confound drift detection mechanisms.76 Continuous deployment therefore requires structured machine learning operations (MLOps) pipelines with explicit drift-monitoring triggers and retraining protocols.78 Procedures for modifying algorithms need to be governed as stringently as the models themselves are monitored, with corresponding adaptation of reporting standards; in particular, the date and training dataset for any model retraining event must be reported, alongside the impact on both external validation and calibration data,34 highlighting that the lack of available, well-documented data will continue to be the primary constraint to this process.

Conclusion

Predictive microbiology should move beyond the question of whether ML or conventional empirical modeling is universally superior. The relevant question is whether a model is appropriately constrained, uncertainty-aware, and validated for the decision and applicability domain in which it will be used. Hybrid approaches are promising where established biological relationships can be combined with flexible learning, but they are not a substitute for independent validation or a route to unrestricted extrapolation.

One counterargument deserves a direct answer. The deficit of trust described here is a necessary explanation for slow adoption, but not a sufficient one. The institutional barriers are just as real: weak commercial pull, no regulatory requirement, no budget for ongoing model maintenance, and incumbent methods that are already accepted and good enough. These cannot be waved away. This Perspective addresses the trust half of the problem; the institutional half will yield only to sustained engagement among modelers, regulators, and industry, which lies beyond our scope here but is no less urgent.

We therefore propose a concrete agenda: report external validation and calibrated uncertainty whenever feasible; select empirical, ML, or hybrid architectures according to the decision and evidence available; build FAIR, machine-readable microbial-response data and public benchmarks; adapt transparent reporting and bias-assessment principles to food microbiology; and work with regulators and food-chain partners to agree on decision-relevant evaluation criteria. Together, these steps would anchor the field to predictive trust rather than held-out accuracy alone.
