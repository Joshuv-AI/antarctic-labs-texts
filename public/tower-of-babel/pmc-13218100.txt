The changing impact of non-pharmaceutical interventions on COVID-19 transmission across different pandemic stages in 12 Asian countries: an ecological study
Authors: Xiaona He, Hui Liu, Fanyan Zeng, Wei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the impact of non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) on COVID-19 transmission in different pandemic stages across 12 Asian countries.DesignThis was an ecological study of publicly available data. This study used the Stringency Index from the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (OxCGRT) as a composite measure of implementation strictness of non-pharmaceutical interventions.SettingData were obtained from Our World in Data and OxCGRT (January 2021 to September 2022).Participants12 countries were included in the study: Azerbaijan, Turkey, Bahrain, Israel, Lebanon, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Cambodia, and Indonesia.Main outcome measureThe instantaneous reproduction number (Rt). Rt is defined as the expected number of secondary infections occurring at time t, divided by the number of infected individuals, each scaled by their relative infectiousness at time t (an individual’s relative infectiousness is based on the generation interval and time).ResultsThree different pandemic development patterns were identified: Cluster 1 countries (marked by distinct fluctuation), Cluster 2 countries (characterised by smaller fluctuation) and Cluster 3 countries (featuring a peak between July and September). An increase in the Stringency Index was associated with a significant decrease in Rt during warmer seasons in both Cluster 1 and 2 (both p values < 0.05). For Cluster 1, the accumulated local effects (ALEs) of the Stringency Index reached a maximum magnitude of approximately 1.80 during April–June, declining from 1.55 (95% CI 1.36 to 1.75) to −0.25 (95% CI −0.33 to −0.17). This was followed by a secondary ALE magnitude of about 0.47 during July–September, decreasing from 0.15 (95% CI 0.12 to 0.18) to −0.32 (95% CI −0.39 to −0.25). Similarly, in Cluster 2, the ALE of the Stringency Index peaked at a magnitude of about 1.25 during July–September, dropping from 0.65 (95% CI 0.54 to 0.76) to −0.60 (95% CI −0.70 to −0.51). In Cluster 3, during July–September, once the Stringency Index reached a certain threshold, the Rt value initially declined but subsequently increased again.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that the effectiveness of NPIs varies with seasonal changes and pandemic patterns. Therefore, to improve the efficiency of public health responses, policymakers should tailor NPI strategies based on seasonal variations and local socio-structural factors. The findings provide new insights for future research on the impact of NPI implementation during pandemics, which plays a critical role in pandemic management.Keywords: COVID-19, Vaccination, Public health

Background

COVID-19 is an infectious disease caused by SARS-CoV-2. As of 30 April 2022, there were over 765 million confirmed cases of COVID-19 and over 6.9 million related deaths worldwide.1 Non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) are important strategies for managing the spread of COVID-19.2,4 NPIs include a series of public health measures, such as school closures, workplace closures, cancellations of public events, restrictions on gathering sizes, closures of public transport, stay-at-home requirements, restrictions on internal movement, restrictions on international travel and public information campaigns.5

The implementation of NPIs significantly decreased the incidence and the number of deaths of COVID-19.6 7 The simultaneous implementation of two or more types of NPIs may be more effective for containing the spread of COVID-19.6 The impact of NPIs on the pandemic might be varied in vaccination rates and seasons.8,10 Understanding the changing impact of NPIs on COVID-19 transmission in the context of vaccination coverage and seasons may inform future pandemic preparedness, providing the best implementation of these NPI strategies.

When vaccination was not available, NPIs served as the primary strategies for controlling the transmission of COVID-19.11,14 Yet, NPIs also play key roles in reducing COVID-19 infections even after the rollout of vaccinations against COVID-19.1215,18 This is due to the mutations of SARS-CoV-2, the immune escape characteristics of its variants and the low vaccination coverage in the early stages. Previous studies have shown that the implementation of strict NPIs, along with the rapid rollout of mass vaccination, resulted in a significant reduction in COVID-19 cases in early 2021 in Israel.19 Conversely, the relaxation of NPIs could lead to a significant increase in COVID-19 cases, even during the rollout of vaccines.20,23 Some studies have indicated that the impact of NPIs in mitigating the pandemic was also influenced by seasonal changes.8 16 21 24 However, evidence regarding the real-world effects of implementing NPIs in the context of the COVID-19 vaccine and varying seasons remains unclear, especially in Asian countries.

This study investigated the impact of NPIs on COVID-19 transmission during different periods following the rollout of vaccines. This exploratory analysis may provide new insights for future research on the impact of NPIs on the management of similar respiratory infectious diseases.

Data and methods

Data sources

The data analysed for the COVID-19 pandemic in Asian countries were obtained from the Our World in Data and the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (OxCGRT).5 25 The two databases harmonise global data sources and ensure comparability of key indicators across the selected countries. We extracted (1) daily new confirmed COVID-19 cases per million people (7-day moving average), (2) the daily estimates of the instantaneous reproduction number (Rt) and (3) the percentage of people fully vaccinated from the Our World in Data global COVID-19 database.25 Additionally, we obtained the Government Response Stringency Index from OxCGRT, which is a comprehensive measure of the strictness of NPIs.5

The study included 12 countries with a substantial amount (≥50%) of vaccination data to ensure a robust analysis and to avoid unreliable estimates that can arise from overly sparse vaccination data. The 12 countries included Azerbaijan, Turkey, Bahrain, Israel, Lebanon, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Cambodia and Indonesia. The geographic distribution of these countries across West Asia, Southeast Asia and East Asia makes it representative of the region’s diversity in key aspects such as socioeconomic development and public health capacity, thereby aligning with the study’s focus on regional characteristics. For the small number of remaining missing values, the proportion of missing data in the 2021 datasets was very low, at 0.04%(n=2) for daily new cases per million people and 2.1%(n=94) for vaccination coverage. The missing values were assumed to be missing at random. We employed linear interpolation to impute the missing values. Additionally, the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method was also employed to conduct a sensitivity analysis.

Definition of outcome

Instantaneous Rt

The main outcome was the instantaneous Rt. The Rt values for each country were obtained from Our World in Data, as contributed by Arroyo-Marioli et al.26 Rt is defined as the expected number of secondary infections occurring at time t, divided by the number of infected individuals, each scaled by their relative infectiousness at time t (an individual’s relative infectiousness is based on the generation interval and time). As a crucial epidemiological parameter, Rt plays a vital role in evaluating epidemic control and was used to study the impact of NPIs.

Daily new confirmed COVID-19 cases per million people (7-day moving average)

The 7-day rolling average of daily new confirmed COVID-19 cases per million people is widely used by policymakers to evaluate the progression of COVID-19. This metric helps mitigate daily fluctuations that may result from reporting or counting errors, thereby offering a clearer picture of actual infection trends. We used this metric to represent the daily infection rates. The indicator was selected based on the assumption that it is the best direct indicator to reflect the COVID-19 trajectory in the real world, without relying on some assumed parameters.

Definition of explanatory variable

Stringency Index

The OxCGRT project calculates a Stringency Index, which is a composite measure of nine of the response metrics.5 The nine metrics used to calculate the Stringency Index are school closures, workplace closures, cancellation of public events, restrictions on public gatherings, closures of public transport, stay-at-home requirements, public information campaigns, restrictions on internal movements and international travel controls.5 Since government policies may differ by vaccination status, a stringency index is calculated for three categories: those who are vaccinated; those who are unvaccinated; and a national average which is weighted based on the share of people who are vaccinated. In the present study, we used a weighted average index value using the non-vaccinated/vaccinated index based on the proportion of the population who are vaccinated with a complete initial protocol. The calculation method for this index is shown follow:

Stringency Index=[(indexv∗Wv)+(indexnv∗Wnv)]/100 (1）

In the formula, v is the index for the vaccinated, nv is the index score for the non-vaccinated, Wv is the weight of population vaccinated (ie, % vaccinated) and Wnv is the weight of population who are non-vaccinated (ie, % non-vaccinated).

The index is scored on a scale from 0 to 100, where a higher score indicates a stricter response (ie, 100=the strictest response). In cases where policies differ at the subnational level, the index reflects the strictest response level among the sub-regions. It’s important to note that this index solely records the stringency of government policies and does not measure or imply the appropriateness or effectiveness of a country’s response. A higher score does not necessarily mean that a country’s response is better than those with lower scores.

We used a 7-day lag for the stringency index to account for the delayed effects of response measures in controlling the spread of COVID-19. Considering that 2021 was the period when NPIs were primarily implemented, our model focuses on investigating the impact of NPIs on COVID-19 transmission in 2021.

The percentage of people fully vaccinated, as referred to as vaccination coverage

Vaccination coverage refers to the number of people who have received all doses prescribed by the vaccination protocol per 100 people in the total population.25 Considering the sufficient antibodies produced in the body after receiving the second vaccine dose,27 we used the value of this indicator with a 7-day lag to analyse its impact on COVID-19 transmission.

Previous cumulative infection cases

Our study used the cumulative cases reported over the previous 8 weeks as a proxy for recently acquired immunity.28 29 This metric represents the cumulative total of daily new confirmed COVID-19 cases per million people over an 8-week period.

Study periods

The study encompasses a total of 21 months, from 1 January 2021 to 30 September 2022. The daily infection rates among the 12 countries were also presented in online supplemental figure S1. The data from these 21 months were included in the cluster analysis to obtain the most representative clusters. Regarding the assessment of the impact of NPIs, our study specifically focuses on analysing the data from the year 2021.

Statistical analysis

Unsupervised clustering method was employed to identify patterns in the development of the pandemic, allowing for the consideration of potential geographical factors and other national characteristics in the subsequent Generalised Additive Models (GAMs) analyses.30 GAMs are powerful data-driven non-parametric regression models that effectively capture both linear and nonlinear relationships between independent and dependent variables.31 Given the complex relationship between NPIs and COVID-19 transmission, GAMs are a more suitable choice than linear models for this study.

Unsupervised clustering analysis

We employed unsupervised hierarchical cluster analysis to examine the infection rate trajectories of 12 countries from 1 January 2021 to 30 September 2022.32 Hierarchical cluster analysis is an effective unsupervised machine learning method used to reveal patterns or characteristics of the pandemic and to identify countries with similar and dissimilar patterns or features.30 33 Before conducting the cluster analysis, we normalised the infection rate using z-score transformation, ensuring comparability of changes in the trajectories across countries.

The establishment of clusters is data-driven, based on the dissimilarity and distance of COVID-19 trajectories across different countries. We adopted a Pearson correlation-based distance as a measure of dissimilarity and applied Ward’s method to measure the distance between clusters.30 34 35 This enabled us to obtain a group of countries with similar epidemic trajectories, irrespective of the magnitude of the actual cases.

GAM analysis

GAMs were employed to explore the association between the stringency index and Rt. GAMs can capture the complex relationships between the stringency index, vaccination coverage and Rt.31 In the present study, the Gaussian distribution family and restricted maximum likelihood were used to estimate the model coefficients and smoothing parameters in GAMs.36 37 For brevity in GAMs, we included only the stringency index, vaccination coverage, the interaction term between the stringency index and vaccination coverage, previous cumulative infection cases and country variables as random effects. The goodness of fit of the GAMs was evaluated based on the deviance explained and the correlation between observed Rt and predicted Rt.

Accumulated local effects (ALEs) are popular methods for visualising black-box models by framing them as GAMs.38 In our study, the effects of model terms (stringency index, vaccination coverage and previous cumulative infection cases) in GAMs were quantified using ALE plots. The ALE plots provide unbiased estimates of the effects of predictor variables in GAMs compared with other methods for quantifying feature importance (eg, partial dependence, partial effects).39 The value of the ALE curve in the ALE plots can be interpreted as the deviation from the mean prediction. For example, if the ALE equals 0.2 at a stringency index of 0.8, it indicates that, at this stringency index, the Rt is 0.2 higher than the average predicted value of Rt.

Before conducting the GAMs analysis, we transformed the original values of all dynamic variables into relative change values to ensure accurate estimation of each variable’s impact and to eliminate any time trends in the data.28 The relative change reflects the changes observed in various countries during a given week. When assessing the impact of NPIs, changes observed at the weekly level may have greater practical significance. In online supplemental figure S2, we provide a visual representation of the dynamic variables, showing both their transformed and untransformed states.

All analyses were conducted using R software (V.4.3.1). The ‘pheatmap’ package was employed to perform hierarchical cluster analysis.40 The ‘mgcv’ package was used to fit GAMs.41 ALE plots were generated using the ‘ALEPlot’ package.39 A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Sensitivity analysis

Recognising the underreporting of cases in many countries, we also used estimates of infection rates from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME)42 to perform the same cluster analysis. Additionally, we conducted cluster analysis using daily and weekly cumulative infection rates, and we adjusted the calculation of the distance matrix by applying Euclidean distance. For all main models, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis by re-running all main models using the upper and lower bounds of the 95% interval for Rt.

Results

Different patterns of pandemic development in 12 Asian countries

As shown in figure 1, the dendrogram reveals that Azerbaijan, Turkey, Bahrain, Israel, and Lebanon cluster together, indicating a high degree of homogeneity among them. Similarly, Japan, Singapore, and South Korea share the same grouping, while Malaysia, Thailand, Cambodia, and Indonesia form another group. Therefore, the 12 Asian countries were identified as three clusters based on the unsupervised machine learning methods. Cluster 1 consists of five West Asian countries (Azerbaijan, Turkey, Bahrain, Israel, and Lebanon), which exhibited a specific pattern of pandemic development, characterised overall by distinct fluctuations. Therefore, we describe its characteristics as the distinct fluctuation cluster of 2021. Cluster 2 includes three developed countries (Japan, Singapore, and South Korea), which were characterised by smaller fluctuations in 2021. Cluster 3 comprises four Southeast Asian countries (Malaysia, Thailand, Cambodia, and Indonesia), characterised by a peak in July–August 2021. In addition, Clusters 1 and 3 exhibit distinct regional characteristics.

The changing impact of stringency index on Rt across different patterns of pandemic development and periods

We evaluated the goodness-of-fit of the GAMs based on the deviance explained and the correlation between observed Rt and predicted Rt. The deviance explained for all models ranged from 47.2% to 91.1%. Specifically, during the third quarter of 2021 (July–September), the models exhibited higher deviance explained percentages. The deviance explained percentages were 84.8%, 91.1% and 89.5% for Cluster 1, Cluster 2 and Cluster 3, respectively. In addition, the model for Cluster 1 also exhibited higher deviance explained percentages (89.1%) during the first quarter of 2021 (January–March). The correlation in all GAMs ranged from 0.66 to 0.94, exhibiting strong positive correlations. See Supplementary Materials for further details (online supplemental table S1,table S2). These results present compelling evidence of the effectiveness of the models in capturing and predicting Rt.

As shown in figure 2, a significant nonlinear association between the stringency index and Rt was observed in clusters 1, 2 and 3, except for cluster 3 in the second quarter (April–June) and the fourth quarter (October–December) of 2021 (p<0.01). However, the direction of these nonlinear associations was not consistently the same across the different clusters and periods. Additional details are provided in online supplemental table S3.

Discussion

In the present study, three clusters of countries with different patterns of pandemic development were identified: Cluster 1 (five West Asian countries), Cluster 2 (Japan, Singapore and South Korea) and Cluster 3 (four Southeast Asian countries). In these three clusters of countries, the NPIs in reducing the spread of COVID-19 appear to be more effective during warmer seasons when taking into account vaccination coverage, the interaction term between the stringency index and vaccination coverage and previous infection cases, particularly in Cluster 1 and Cluster 2. This study used an unsupervised clustering approach to identify different pandemic patterns across Asian regions. Then, through powerful data-driven non-parametric regression models, we captured the impact of NPIs on COVID-19 transmission across these patterns and seasons.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, our data were collected at the country level and did not capture the timing and intensity of NPIs at sub-national levels, which may be important and require a more conservative interpretation of the results. Second, it is important to note that the present study did not consider the influence of multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants and their varying transmissibility. However, by conducting our analysis across different periods, we aimed to partially address this limitation. Third, it is essential to acknowledge the relationship between NPIs and Rt is very complex; in addition to the influences noted in this study, it is also affected by factors not considered in other research, such as adherence to social distancing. Additionally, our study has a limited scope, including only 12 Asian countries. Based on the above limitations, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

There are notable regional distinctions in Cluster 1 and Cluster 3. Geographic factors significantly influence the pattern of pandemic development, which is consistent with previous research.30 Geographically, while Singapore is not close to Japan and South Korea, all three countries are developed and have high public compliance with their governments’ responses to COVID-19.12

During warmer seasons, the impact of the NPIs implemented by these clusters of countries in mitigating the pandemic appears to be enhanced, irrespective of vaccination rates and previous infection cases. According to the findings of a recent study conducted in six temperate Northern European countries, it is suggested that the observed effect of NPIs may be attributed to the seasonality of the coronavirus.9 The warmer temperatures and increased UV radiation during summer are likely to support public health measures in controlling the spread of COVID-19.43 Although seasonal variations alone cannot stave off the subsequent surge in COVID-19 cases,29 it is crucial to adjust the implementation of NPIs based on these variations to avoid trade-offs between benefits and negative consequences. Moreover, in our study, the enhanced effect of NPIs on Rt during the warmer seasons may be stronger in a group of West Asian countries and in a group of developed countries (Japan, Singapore and South Korea). The larger effect of NPIs in the warmer seasons in the group of countries may partly explain the inconsistencies noted in previous research regarding why COVID-19 case numbers decrease in some countries during the warmer seasons, while in some Asian countries, the case numbers remain high during the same seasons.

Our study finds evidence that the effectiveness of NPIs in reducing COVID-19 transmission is associated with both seasonal variations and distinct pandemic patterns, demonstrating notably stronger impacts during warmer seasons and in certain national contexts. The findings provide novel insights for future research on the impact of NPIs implementation during a pandemic, which plays a significant role in managing pandemics. Future research should take into account the implementation of appropriate NPIs earlier in warmer seasons to yield greater containment benefits at relatively lower socioeconomic costs. Furthermore, it is also important to develop frameworks for tailoring NPIs based on local contexts.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that the effectiveness of NPIs in reducing COVID-19 transmission was influenced by both seasonal variations and pandemic patterns. Notably, stronger impacts were observed during warmer seasons and within certain national contexts. To improve the efficiency of public health response, policymakers should tailor NPI strategies according to seasonal conditions and local socio-structural factors. This study provides new insights to inform public health decision-making in addressing future pandemic threats.
