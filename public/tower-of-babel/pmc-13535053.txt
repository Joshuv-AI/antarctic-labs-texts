Timing of lactated Ringer’s administration relative to Sepsis-3 diagnosis and 28-day mortality: A retrospective propensity score-matched cohort study
Authors: Yujie Huang, Jiayue Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBalanced crystalloids are preferentially recommended over saline for sepsis resuscitation; however, the optimal timing of administration remains to be defined.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database to analyze adult patients meeting Sepsis-3 criteria. Patients were stratified by Lactated Ringer’s (LR) administration timing relative to documented Sepsis-3 onset: Group A (LR initiation ≤24 h after onset), Group B (LR initiation ≤24 h before onset), Group C (LR initiation 24-48 h after onset), and Group D (reference: no LR exposure during intensive care unit [ICU] admission). The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. To address confounding, Group B patients were 1:1 Propensity-Score Matched (PSM) with Group D counterparts, followed by multivariable Cox regression and sensitivity analyses.ResultsPSM generated a balanced cohort of 3,876 patients (1,938 in Group B vs. 1,938 untreated Group D counterparts). Multivariable Cox regression analysis showed that pre-onset LR exposure remained associated with lower 28-day mortality after adjustment for clinical covariates. In additional post-PSM sensitivity analyses, this association persisted after further adjustment for elective surgery and total recorded fluid volume during the 24 hours preceding Sepsis-3 onset. To address timing-related bias, we also compared the ICU admission-to-SOFA time interval and performed a 24-hour ICU-admission landmark analysis; the association remained in the landmark cohort. Kidney outcomes were mixed: any acute kidney injury (AKI) was more frequent in Group B, whereas Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) stage 3 AKI and renal replacement therapy were less frequent.ConclusionPre-diagnosis LR exposure within 24 hours before Sepsis-3 onset was associated with lower 28-day mortality in adult patients with sepsis. These observational findings are hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as evidence supporting prophylactic LR administration before Sepsis-3 onset.

1. Introduction

Sepsis, a life-threatening syndrome characterized by organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response to infection, causes over 11 million annual deaths and accounts for 20% of global in-hospital mortality.1–4 Fluid resuscitation--a cornerstone of sepsis management--aims to restore effective circulating volume, optimize tissue oxygen delivery, and acts as first-line intervention for hemodynamic stabilization. However, two critical debates remain unsettled in clinical practice: the comparative efficacy of balanced crystalloids (e.g., LR solution) versus normal saline (NS), and the optimal timing of intervention to maximize therapeutic benefit.5,6

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) guidelines recommend crystalloids as first-line resuscitation fluids and suggest balanced crystalloids rather than normal saline for adults with sepsis or septic shock, but the latter recommendation remains weak and based on low-quality evidence. 7 The guidelines also acknowledge insufficient evidence to recommend a restrictive versus liberal fluid strategy during the first 24 hours after initial resuscitation. 7 Recent multi-omics work further suggests that septic shock subgroups may differ in their response to restrictive and liberal fluid strategies, 8 reinforcing the need for individualized and time-sensitive investigations of fluid administration.

Despite its historical role as a common resuscitation fluid, normal saline contains a supraphysiologic chloride concentration of 154 mmol/L. In experimental sepsis, hyperchloremic acidosis has been associated with increased inflammatory mediators and lower arterial pressure,9,10 while crystalloid composition has influenced microcirculatory perfusion and short-term survival.11,12 In critically ill adults, chloride-liberal fluid strategies have also been associated with kidney injury. 13

Balanced crystalloids, including LR solution and Plasma-Lyte A, more closely approximate the electrolyte composition of plasma. Growing clinical evidence supports their association with reduced AKI incidence and lower mortality compared with saline.13,14 Prespecified secondary analyses of the SMART (Isotonic Solutions and Major Adverse Renal Events Trial) reported lower 30-day in-hospital mortality among patients with sepsis treated with balanced crystalloids rather than saline (adjusted OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.59-0.93; P=0.01), fewer major adverse kidney events (MAKE), and decreased vasopressor dependence. 6

At the same time, clinical equipoise remains. 15 The BaSICS (Balanced Solution in Intensive Care Study) trial reported comparable 90-day mortality between balanced solution and saline in the overall critically ill population. 16 Consistent with this uncertainty, the 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines suggest balanced crystalloids rather than saline for adults with sepsis or septic shock, but the recommendation is weak and based on low-quality evidence. 7 Thus, no definitive mortality-benefit consensus exists for saline versus balanced crystalloids in sepsis management.

Using the MIMIC-IV v2.0 database, this retrospective cohort study examines the association between timing of balanced crystalloid administration and clinical outcomes among patients with sepsis, seeking to resolve existing controversies through real-world evidence.

2. Methods

2.1. Data source

This secondary analysis used the publicly available, de-identified MIMIC-IV v2.0 database, which contains ICU admissions from 2008 to 2019. This period was used because it represents the complete observation window available in MIMIC-IV v2.0, the database version used to develop the prespecified cohort and analysis. The creation and sharing of the MIMIC-IV resource were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, which waived the requirement for written informed consent (IRB protocol number 2001P001699). Because the present study used only de-identified data from this approved resource, no additional institutional ethical approval or individual written informed consent was required. Author YJH completed the required CITI Program training (Certification ID 9244195) and data use agreement before accessing the database. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 17

2.2. Study population

Eligible patients met the following criteria: (1) Sepsis and septic shock (Sepsis-3) as defined by the Third International Consensus Definitions, i.e., suspected or documented infection with an increase of at least 2 points in the total Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score 1 ; (2) Aged ≥18 years at ICU admission; (3) Sepsis onset within 24 hours of ICU admission; (4) Exclusion criteria included >20% missing critical variables or incomplete records of LR solution administration; (5) Analysis was restricted to the first ICU admission for patients with multiple ICU encounters. For all included patients, we retrospectively extracted baseline demographics, serial laboratory values, ICU therapeutic interventions, hospitalization metrics, and mortality outcomes.

2.3. Exposure and outcomes

The primary exposure was intravenous administration of LR solution during ICU hospitalization. Fluid exposure and volume data were extracted from prespecified MIMIC-IV medication and ICU fluid input records. LR exposure was defined according to the original cohort-defining medication records, using the recorded starttime and stoptime fields for Lactated Ringers; unlike laboratory variables, no additional time-shift or delay correction was applied to LR timing. Participants were stratified into four mutually exclusive cohorts according to LR timing relative to Sepsis-3 onset (SOFA time): Group A (LR initiation within 24 hours after sepsis onset), Group B (LR administration within 24 hours before sepsis onset), Group C (LR initiation 24-48 hours after sepsis onset), and Group D (no recorded LR exposure).

The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, ICU length of stay, and kidney-related outcomes. Kidney outcomes included any acute kidney injury (AKI), KDIGO stage 2 or 3 AKI, KDIGO stage 3 AKI, and renal replacement therapy (RRT) within 48 hours and within 7 days after SOFA time.

2.4. Data extraction

Data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database using Structured Query Language (SQL), including patient demographics, severity scores, vital signs, laboratory measurements, comorbidities, mechanical ventilation, norepinephrine use, elective surgery, LR administration, ICU fluid input records, KDIGO stages, RRT, and clinical outcomes. Severity scores included SOFA, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), which were defined according to established scoring systems.18–21

2.5. Fluid-volume characterization

To address total fluid exposure in the matched comparison groups, we performed an additional post-PSM fluid-volume analysis anchored to Sepsis-3 onset (SOFA time). The primary window was the 24 hours preceding SOFA time. Fluid exposure and volume variables were derived from prespecified MIMIC-IV medication and ICU fluid input records. LR exposure was retained from the original cohort-defining medication records to remain consistent with cohort assignment. Non-LR crystalloids and colloid/blood-product/other IV fluids were summarized from recorded ICU fluid input data and classified using structured item labels. For records crossing the analytic window boundary, included volume was prorated by overlap duration: recorded volume multiplied by the proportion of infusion duration overlapping the 24-hour window preceding SOFA time. Fluid categories were summarized as total recorded fluid volume, recorded non-LR crystalloid volume, and recorded colloid/blood-product/other IV fluid volume.

2.6. Statistical analysis

For this retrospective cohort study, data extracted from the MIMIC-IV database were analyzed as follows: Outliers (defined as values outside the range Q1−1.5×IQR to Q3+1.5×IQR, where IQR=Q3−Q1) were recoded as missing values. Missing data were imputed using the random forest-based “missRanger” package (Figure S1). All variables had variance inflation factors (VIF) <5, confirming no significant multicollinearity.

Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (%) and analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests; continuous variables were reported as mean +/- standard deviation or median (25th-75th percentiles) and compared via Student's t-tests, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, or ANOVA, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P<0.05.

Additional post-PSM sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the matched-cohort findings. These models used the covariates retained in the primary multivariable Cox model and then incorporated clinically important factors that could plausibly confound the association. Elective surgery status was compared between Group B and Group D and explicitly included in an additional Cox proportional hazards model. To address potential confounding by resuscitation intensity, a second sensitivity Cox model further adjusted for total recorded fluid volume during the 24 hours preceding SOFA time.

To examine whether the Group B association could be explained by immortal time bias or differences in the timing of sepsis recognition, we performed two additional post-PSM analyses. First, we descriptively compared the interval from ICU admission to Sepsis-3 onset (SOFA time) between Group B and Group D using the Mann-Whitney U test and compared the proportions of patients whose SOFA time occurred within 6, 12, and 24 hours after ICU admission. Second, we performed a 24-hour ICU-admission landmark Cox analysis that included patients whose SOFA time occurred within 24 hours after ICU admission and who remained at risk at 24 hours; follow-up was re-anchored at 24 hours after ICU admission. The landmark Cox model adjusted for covariates selected by the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) analysis reported in the manuscript: weight, hematocrit, anion gap, blood urea nitrogen, respiratory rate, SAPS II, OASIS, and norepinephrine use.

2.7. Propensity score matching

To minimize confounding, we used 1:1 PSM to evaluate associations between LR solution administration and sepsis outcomes. Propensity scores were estimated via multivariable logistic regression that included clinically validated covariates22,23: age, race, sex, weight, OASIS, SOFA, SAPS II, and GCS. LR solution-exposed patients (Group B) were matched to controls without recorded LR exposure (Group D) using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit-transformed propensity score, without replacement, to reduce selection bias. Adequate post-matching covariate balance between groups was confirmed by standardized mean differences (SMD) <0.20 24 (Figures S2 and S3).

2.8. Inverse probability of treatment weighting

We performed sensitivity analyses using Inverse Probability Treatment Weighting (IPTW) on the pre-matched cohort to validate the PSM results. IPTW creates a pseudopopulation by weighting patients to balance covariate distributions between LR solution-exposed and unexposed groups. Stabilized weights—derived from propensity scores estimated via logistic regression with the same covariates as the PSM model (age, race, sex, weight, OASIS, SOFA, SAPS II, GCS)—were applied using the R package “WeightIt” to minimize the impact of extreme weights and optimize efficiency.

3. Results

3.1. Patient selection

Figure 1 outlines the cohort selection process. Of 34,899 patients with sepsis (meeting Sepsis-3 criteria), 32,316 fulfilled inclusion criteria. Participants were stratified by timing of LR solution administration: Group A (LR solution initiation ≤24h post-diagnosis; n=2,303), Group B (LR solution initiation ≤24h pre-diagnosis; n=1,939), Group C (LR solution initiation 24–48h post-diagnosis; n=587), and Group D (no LR solution exposure; n=27,487).Figure 1.Flow diagram for patient selection.

Flow diagram for patient selection.

1:1 PSM generated an analytic cohort of 3,876 patients (1,938 in Group B vs. 1,938 in Group D) for outcome analysis. Robustness was evaluated using IPTW applied to Groups B and D, which achieved covariate balance (post-IPTW SMD <0.20 for all variables) and confirmed result stability in sensitivity analyses.

3.2. Fluid exposure in the matched cohort

During the 24 hours preceding SOFA time, Group B had higher total recorded fluid volume than Group D (median 3598.6 [IQR 1066.4-4801.0] mL vs. 189.8 [4.9-2054.1] mL; P<0.001; Supplementary Table 1). Because LR exposure itself defined cohort assignment, the cohort-defining LR order volume was not presented as a separate quantitative fluid-volume endpoint. Group B also had greater recorded non-LR crystalloid volume (1748.4 [20.5-3012.0] mL vs. 29.4 [0.0-701.0] mL; P<0.001). Recorded colloid/blood-product/other IV fluid volume was 336.5 [12.6-687.2] mL in Group B and 1.6 [0.0-550.0] mL in Group D (P<0.001). These estimates represent recorded fluid volume captured in prespecified MIMIC-IV medication and ICU fluid input records and should not be interpreted as complete bedside fluid administration.

3.3. Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier analysis of 28-day all-cause mortality among 32,316 eligible patients demonstrated significantly better survival in Group B (LR solution administered ≤24h before sepsis diagnosis) versus Group D (no LR solution; log-rank P<0.001; Figure 2). Groups A (LR solution ≤24h after diagnosis) and C (LR solution 24–48h post-diagnosis) showed smaller survival benefits. To identify independent prognostic factors, a Cox proportional hazards model was used with a rigorous analytical approach: first, 30 candidate variables were screened via univariate regression (inclusion criterion: P<0.05); then, multivariable Cox analysis was restricted to variables meeting proportional hazards (PH) assumptions. Six variables (SAPS II, OASIS, hematocrit, anion gap, respiratory rate, norepinephrine) showed significant time-dependent effects (P<0.05 for PH assumption violation), requiring inclusion of time-varying coefficients via multiplicative interaction terms with log-transformed survival time. In additional post-PSM sensitivity analyses, elective surgery was more frequent in Group B than in Group D (285/1938 [14.7%] vs. 74/1938 [3.8%]; OR 4.32, 95% CI 3.32-5.62; P<0.001). After explicit adjustment for elective surgery, Group B remained associated with lower 28-day mortality (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.33-0.57; P<0.001), while elective surgery itself was not independently associated with 28-day mortality (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.48-1.88; P=0.888). The association also persisted after additional adjustment for total recorded fluid volume during the 24 hours preceding SOFA time (Group B HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.38-0.68; P<0.001; Supplementary Table 2).

In the post-PSM timing-bias analysis, the interval from ICU admission to SOFA time differed between groups: Group B had a longer median interval than Group D (5.2 [IQR 3.9-7.1] hours vs. 2.2 [IQR 1.0-4.6] hours; P<0.001). SOFA time occurred within 6 hours after ICU admission in 64.1% of Group B and 82.1% of Group D patients, and within 12 hours in 91.3% and 94.4%, respectively; all matched patients in both groups had SOFA time within 24 hours after ICU admission. In the 24-hour ICU-admission landmark cohort (n=3,850; Group B n=1,932, Group D n=1,918; 295 events), Group B remained associated with lower 28-day mortality (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.35-0.61; P<0.001; Supplementary Table 3).

3.6.2. Kidney-related outcomes

Kidney outcomes showed a mixed pattern (Supplementary Table 4). Within 48 hours after SOFA time, any AKI was more frequent in Group B than Group D (1401/1938 [72.3%] vs. 1171/1938 [60.4%]; OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.49-1.95; P<0.001), whereas KDIGO stage 3 AKI (172/1938 [8.9%] vs. 258/1938 [13.3%]; OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.52-0.78; P<0.001) and RRT (55/1938 [2.8%] vs. 118/1938 [6.1%]; OR 0.45, 95% CI 0.33-0.63; P<0.001) were less frequent in Group B. Similar patterns were observed within 7 days after SOFA time: any AKI was more frequent in Group B, but KDIGO stage 3 AKI and RRT were less frequent.

3.8. Association between LR solution volume and 28-day mortality

To evaluate the dose-mortality relationship, we analyzed weight-adjusted LR infusion volumes (ml/kg) within the first 24 hours in Group B. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis identified an optimal threshold for mortality discrimination at 15.05 ml/kg (specificity of 0.75, sensitivity of 0.51; Figure 10). Kaplan-Meier analysis stratified by this threshold demonstrated significantly worse survival in patients receiving >15.05 ml/kg compared to those with <=15.05 ml/kg (P=0.004; Figure 11). 4. Discussion

Our findings suggest that LR administration within 24 hours prior to Sepsis-3 onset was associated with lower 28-day all-cause mortality (adjusted HR = 0.44, 95% CI 0.34-0.58). This association was observed in the matched cohort and persisted in additional sensitivity models that explicitly adjusted for elective surgery and total recorded fluid volume during the 24 hours preceding SOFA time. The additional timing-bias analyses showed that ICU admission-to-SOFA time differed between groups, supporting the possibility that pre-onset LR exposure may be related to earlier recognition, perioperative monitoring, or differences in clinical trajectory. Nevertheless, the association remained in the 24-hour ICU-admission landmark cohort. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously as associations rather than as evidence that LR administration itself caused the lower mortality.

4.1. Relationship to prior evidence

Our findings can be considered alongside prior studies comparing balanced crystalloids with saline. In a secondary analysis of SMART, Brown et al. reported lower 30-day in-hospital mortality among patients with sepsis who received balanced crystalloids rather than saline (26.3% vs. 31.2%; adjusted OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.59-0.93; P=0.01). 6 Tseng et al. similarly found lower mortality with balanced crystalloids than with saline (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.74-0.95) or low-molecular-weight hydroxyethyl starch (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.69-0.95) in a network meta-analysis of patients with sepsis. 25 The European Society of Intensive Care Medicine guideline also recommends balanced crystalloids over saline for sepsis resuscitation. 26

Our study is consistent with this broader literature, but it extends it only as real-world observational evidence focused on timing before recorded Sepsis-3 onset. The present analysis should not be interpreted as proving that LR itself caused the lower mortality, because treatment timing, clinical recognition, and patient selection may be correlated with outcomes even after PSM and multivariable adjustment.

4.2. Advantages of balanced crystalloid solutions

The mechanisms underlying the clinical advantage of balanced crystalloids over 0.9% sodium chloride remain incompletely understood. Current evidence indicates that chloride-mediated pathways are central to this difference: normal saline’s supraphysiological chloride concentration (154 mmol/L versus plasma levels of ∼94–111 mmol/L) induces hyperchloremia, which may trigger a range of adverse effects:

4.2.1. Metabolic acidosis

Infusion of normal saline introduces a supraphysiological chloride load (154 mmol/L Cl- versus plasma levels of 94–111 mmol/L), which may directly promote metabolic acidosis through chloride-mediated mechanisms. Consistent with Stewart’s physicochemical framework, elevated chloride concentrations significantly reduce the strong ion difference (SID), thereby lowering plasma buffering capacity and increasing free H+ concentration. This disturbance precipitates hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis—a clinically meaningful alteration that is particularly detrimental in sepsis, where acid-base balance is already impaired.27–29

4.2.2. Enhanced inflammatory response

Experimental evidence indicates that hyperchloremic acidosis can amplify inflammatory signaling and shift cytokine profiles toward a pro-inflammatory state.9,30 Acidic conditions may also enhance nitric oxide (NO) production, which can contribute to hypotension and organ injury. 31 These mechanisms may partly explain adverse physiological responses to chloride-rich fluids in sepsis, although they were not measured in the present study.

4.2.3. Hemodynamic instability

Experimental sepsis data link hyperchloremic acidosis to hemodynamic instability. Kellum et al. showed that hydrochloric acid-induced hyperchloremic acidosis lowered mean arterial pressure in septic rats. 10 In a separate rodent study, Pedoto et al. found that acidosis increased nitric oxide production and reduced arterial pressure. 32 Together, these findings provide a mechanistic link between chloride-associated acidosis and impaired vascular tone.

4.2.4. Impaired renal function

Elevated chloride exposure can promote renal vasoconstriction and reduce renal perfusion, thereby increasing the risk of AKI. Clinical evidence suggests that balanced crystalloids, including lactated Ringer’s solution, may reduce kidney complications compared with saline in some settings. Zhang et al. found that maximum and mean chloride concentrations before AKI were independently associated with AKI development. 33

4.2.5. Gastrointestinal and hematological alterations

Wilkes et al.'s randomized trial in elderly surgical patients showed that saline-based fluids produced a larger increase in chloride and a greater decline in base excess than balanced fluids. Two-thirds of patients receiving saline-based fluids developed postoperative hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, whereas none receiving balanced fluids did; gastric mucosal perfusion was also better preserved with balanced fluids. 34

Clinical cohort data also associate worsening hyperchloremia with mortality in septic ICU patients. Neyra et al. found that higher chloride concentrations at 72 hours and greater within-patient increases in chloride were independently associated with hospital mortality. 35 Pro-inflammatory cytokines can suppress erythropoietin production, 36 although a direct link between chloride exposure and this pathway has not been established.

4.3. Implications for clinical practice

Large pragmatic trials suggest modest renal composite benefits from balanced crystalloids in some clinical settings. In SMART, 14.3% of critically ill adults assigned to balanced crystalloids experienced a major adverse kidney event within 30 days, compared with 15.4% assigned to saline (marginal OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.84-0.99; P=0.04). 37

In SALT-ED, the primary outcome of hospital-free days did not differ between balanced crystalloids and saline. However, major adverse kidney events within 30 days were less frequent with balanced crystalloids (4.7% vs. 5.6%; adjusted OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.70-0.95; P=0.01). 38 This finding suggests a possible renal benefit while acknowledging the neutral primary outcome.

In patients with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), using balanced crystalloids versus normal saline for treatment resulted in a shorter time to DKA resolution (HR = 1.68, 95% CI = 1.18–2.38; P = 0.004) and a shorter duration of insulin infusion (HR = 1.45, 95% CI = 1.03–2.03; P = 0.03), supporting the efficacy and safety of balanced crystalloids in this population. 39

In deceased-donor kidney transplantation, BEST-Fluids found a lower incidence of delayed graft function (DGF) with Plasma-Lyte 148 than with saline (30% vs. 40%; adjusted RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.66-0.84; P<0.0001). 40 These findings support a potential renal benefit of balanced crystalloids in the transplantation setting.

While normal saline remains widely used in initial resuscitation due to its low cost and ready availability, its association with hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis and related complications warrants continued evaluation. Beyond acid-base and renal effects, fluid composition and volume may also influence microcirculation, endothelial function, glycocalyx integrity, and hemostasis. Experimental and clinical studies have linked crystalloid therapy to peripheral perfusion and microcirculatory flow in sepsis, endothelial glycocalyx degradation, and coagulation/platelet function.11,41–44 Although these variables were not directly measured in the present study, they provide additional biological plausibility for the observed association and warrant further investigation.

5. Limitations

Our study has several important limitations. First, as a retrospective observational study, its findings remain vulnerable to residual confounding and unmeasured factors despite PSM, multivariable modeling, IPTW sensitivity analysis, and the additional landmark analysis. Confounding by indication is particularly important: pre-diagnosis LR exposure may reflect earlier recognition, perioperative monitoring, clinician behavior, or resuscitation intensity rather than a direct protective effect of LR itself. We therefore interpret all estimates as associations rather than causal treatment effects.

Second, timing misclassification may exist in MIMIC-IV medication and fluid input records. LR exposure was defined using recorded starttime and stoptime fields from the cohort-defining medication records, and no additional delay correction was applied. For the new fluid-volume analysis, recorded ICU fluid input data crossing the 24-hour window preceding SOFA time were included proportionally by overlap duration, but recorded fluid data may still incompletely capture all clinically administered fluids. Third, no a priori sample size calculation was performed because this was a retrospective database study including all eligible MIMIC-IV patients who met the prespecified inclusion criteria; the final analytic sample was therefore determined by database availability, cohort definitions, missing-data handling, and propensity score matching. Fourth, the kidney findings were not uniformly protective: any AKI was more frequent in Group B, whereas KDIGO stage 3 AKI and RRT were less frequent. These mixed outcomes may reflect differences in surveillance, case mix, baseline risk, or early resuscitation context. The study does not support prophylactic LR administration before sepsis is clinically suspected; rather, it suggests that early balanced crystalloid exposure may be a marker of early recognition and resuscitation practices associated with outcomes.

Fifth, because MIMIC-IV v2.0 contains admissions from 2008 to 2019, subsequent changes in sepsis recognition and fluid-management practices may limit the applicability of the findings to current clinical care. Thus, caution is needed when generalizing these findings to populations in other countries with different healthcare systems, demographics, or clinical practices.

6. Conclusion

Our findings suggest that LR exposure within 24 hours before Sepsis-3 onset was associated with lower 28-day mortality, lower in-hospital mortality, and shorter ICU length of stay in the post-PSM cohort. This association persisted after additional adjustment for elective surgery and total recorded fluid volume, but the observational design precludes causal inference. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether early balanced crystalloid strategies improve outcomes in patients at high risk for sepsis.
