Predictive Utility of In-Hospital Screening for Postdischarge Depression Symptoms Among Survivors of Critical Illness
Authors: Isabella Shubert, Emily Poehlein, Deepshikha C. Ashana, Maragatha Kuchibhatla, John A. Gallis, Maren K. Olsen, Laura S. Porter, Marc Moss, Catherine L. Hough, Christopher E. Cox
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Although postdischarge symptoms of depression are highly prevalent after critical illness, the best timing and strategies for screening are unclear.RESEARCH QUESTION:What is the value of using the 2-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2) depression scale in the hospital to predict elevated postdischarge depression symptoms?STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS:This was a secondary analysis from a randomized factorial trial designed to optimize a postdischarge distress intervention among survivors of cardiorepiratory failure managed in ICUs in 3 states. The primary outcome was the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) score measured immediately after discharge, whereas the key predictor was in-hospital PHQ-2 score. Performance metrics of the PHQ-2 across different score cut points were compared to identify the optimal threshold value for predicting clinically important postdischarge symptoms. Linear and logistic regression models examined the PHQ-2’s performance as a continuous and binary variable alone, as well as with the addition of sociodemographic and clinical characteristics.RESULTS:Among 164 participants, clinically important depression symptoms were present after discharge among 72%, as defined by a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 5, and among 39%, as defined by a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 10. Compared with a PHQ-2 score threshold of ≥ 2, a value of ≥ 1 provided higher sensitivity (78% vs 58%), although lower specificity (61% vs 80%) among those with a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 5; metrics were similar with the use of a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 10. Compared with models with binary PHQ-2 scores alone, continuous PHQ-2 scores provided better discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC], 0.61–0.69 vs 0.74, respectively), but similar prediction accuracy (Brier score, 0.17–0.19 vs 0.17, respectively). The addition of sociodemographic and clinical characteristics improved the PHQ-2’s discrimination (AUC range, 0.61–0.74 vs 0.77–0.83, respectively) and amount of variance explained (R2 range, 0.18–0.36 vs 0.37–0.48, respectively).INTERPRETATION:Our results show that the in-hospital PHQ-2 demonstrated moderate predictive performance as a screening tool for postdischarge depression symptoms among survivors of critical illness. The addition of sociodemographic and clinical variables improved performance.CLINICAL TRIAL REGISTRATION:ClinicalTrials.gov; No.: NCT04038567; URL: www.clinicaltrials.gov

Many of the millions of people hospitalized annually in the United States for acute critical illness experience not only long-term physical, social, financial, and cognitive sequelae, but psychological challenges as well.1 For example, > 30% of survivors report significant symptoms of depression after discharge.2–6 However, because formal screening programs for depression are uncommon after serious illness,7 few patients with depression symptoms after discharge are identified, and even fewer access relevant clinical therapies.8–10 This identification gap also is relevant for researchers seeking strategies to determine efficiently which patients are eligible for clinical trials that address depression symptoms after discharge. For example, although many behavioral health interventions generally begin after hospitalization, they typically require in-hospital informed consent as well as the extra step of assessment after discharge to determine if depression symptoms are elevated sufficiently to warrant trial inclusion.11,12 Ideally, in-hospital screening would predict significant postdischarge symptoms quickly and reliably so that clinical care and research could focus efficiently on the patients with true-positive findings who have the highest risk of persistent depression symptoms after discharge. However, few data exist to guide the optimal timing and location of screening, the screening measure, and the threshold value for positive screening evaluation results.7

To address this gap, we performed a secondary analysis of data from a clinical trial conducted among ICU survivors to assess the predictive performance of the inpatient 2-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2) depression symptom survey for postdischarge depression symptoms measured soon after discharge using the more complete 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) depression scale survey.13 The PHQ-9 is relevant because its validity is supported by successful use in several thousand studies across numerous clinical settings as well as its excellent psychometric properties, including responsiveness to change, simple interpretation of symptom severity, and brevity. We hypothesized that the PHQ-2 may serve as an efficient inpatient screening strategy given its brevity, excellent sensitivity, and moderate specificity in community settings and its omission of the PHQ-9’s suicidality item, which can present a care management challenge. We also examined whether additional sociodemographic and clinical characteristics could improve the PHQ-2’s predictive performance.

Study Design and Methods

Study Design

We used secondary data from 2 time points (in the hospital and within approximately 1 week of discharge) from the prerandomization or preintervention phase of a factorial randomized clinical trial conducted between 2019 and 2023 at Duke University, Oregon Health & Science University, and the University of Colorado.

The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT04038567) and was approved by the central Duke Institutional Review Board (Identifier: 00119897).

Participants, Recruitment, and Data Collection

The parent trial’s eligibility criteria are reported elsewhere.12 Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, acute cardiorespiratory failure (mechanical ventilation duration ≥ 12 hours; noninvasive ventilation, high-flow nasal cannula, or face mask oxygen for ≥ 4 hours in a 24-hour period; use of vasopressors or inotropes for ≥ 1 hour; use of IV pulmonary vascular vasoactive medications; or use of aortic balloon pump for cardiogenic shock for ≥ 1 hour), admission to an ICU for at least 24 hours, absence of cognitive impairment and severe mental illness, as well as English fluency sufficient to complete study surveys. Exclusions were hospitalization for a serious illness within 3 months of admission, hospital admission from or discharge to a location other than home (eg, nursing facility), expectation of requiring complex medical care soon after discharge (eg, surgery), inability to complete study procedures, and lack of reliable smartphone or Wi-Fi access.

Timing of Outcomes Assessment

Within 48 hours of discharge home, participants self-completed the PHQ-9 survey via a digital platform, which was used to determine eligibility for the main trial as well as to report sociodemographic data. The PHQ-9 and other study surveys also were self-completed 1 and 3 months later. After approximately 30% of the trial’s target population had been enrolled, the PHQ-2 was added for self-completion at the time of consent when patients had just transferred from the ICU to the ward. This was carried out because we noted that nearly 40% of patients who gave consent were ineligible for the main trial because of a low PHQ-9 score (< 5) after hospital discharge.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the PHQ-9 score evaluated as both a continuous variable (range, 0–27) and a binary variable (< 5 vs ≥ 5, a score representing at least mild depression symptoms).14

Predictors

The primary predictor of interest was the in-hospital PHQ-2, a brief screening tool for depression symptoms that consists of the first 2 PHQ-9 items: “little interest or pleasure in doing things” and “feeling down, depressed, or hopeless.” PHQ-2 scores range from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating a greater burden of depression symptoms.

Patient-reported sociodemographic characteristics included in multivariable models were sex (female vs male), age, ethnicity (non-Hispanic or non-Latino vs Hispanic or Latino), race (White vs American Indian or Alaska Native, Black, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, other, and ≥ 2 racial groups), marital status (not partnered vs partnered), education level (some college or more vs high school, trade school, or less), financial stress (short on money vs barely enough money vs enough money for extras vs completely comfortable), as well as Activities of Daily Living (ADL) and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scores before admission (e-Table 1). Clinical characteristics abstracted from the electronic health record included the number of comorbidities, length of ICU stay, ICU type (medical vs surgery), use of mechanical ventilation during admission, surgery during admission, use of antidepressants at admission, and use of pain medications at admission.

Statistical Analyses

An expanded version of the statistical analysis procedures is provided in the e-Appendix 1.

Optimization of PHQ-2 Cut Points for the Prediction of Depression Symptoms After Discharge:

In outpatient settings, a PHQ-2 value of ≥ 3, a common screening threshold, has a sensitivity of 83% and a specificity of 92% for a diagnosis of major depressive disorder as determined by an interview with a mental health professional.15 Therefore, we explored the accuracy of using this in-hospital PHQ-2 threshold as well as others (≥ 1 and ≥ 2) to predict clinically important depression symptoms after discharge defined as a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 5.16 We also included sensitivity analyses using a PHQ-9 score threshold of ≥ 10 as well as an exploration of performance restricted to those who reported not receiving mental health professional care for depression in the 3 months before hospital admission. For each PHQ-2 threshold, we calculated sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and Youden’s statistic.17

Evaluation of the Predictive Performance of the PHQ-2:

Evaluation of PHQ-2 Performance Alone:

To assess the predictive performance of in-hospital depression symptoms (ie, PHQ-2 score) on depressive symptoms after discharge (ie, PHQ-9 score), we evaluated several logistic and 4 linear regression models examining different combinations of predictor and outcome definitions.

Logistic regression models (models 1A-1D) were used to predict clinically important postdischarge depression symptoms defined as a binary PHQ-9 score of ≥ 5. Model 1A used a binary PHQ-2 score with the traditional cutoff (≥ 3), Models 1B and 1C included lower PHQ-2 cutoffs (≥ 2 and ≥ 1, respectively) because they were the thresholds that maximized Youden’s index (ie, value closest to 1) to a similar degree,18,19 whereas model 1D used the continuous PHQ-2 score. Predictive accuracy was assessed using the Brier score.20 Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), which represents the model’s ability to distinguish individuals with vs without the outcome.21,22 In these analyses, we defined Brier scores as reflecting poor (> 0.25), moderate (0.10–0.25), or good (< 0.10) predictive accuracy. Similarly, the discrimination performance of AUC scores was defined as poor (< 0.7), moderate (≥ 0.7 to < 0.8), or good (≥ 0.8).

Linear regression models (models 2A-2D) were used to predict continuous PHQ-9 scores. Models 2A, 2B, and 2C used the 3 different binary PHQ-2 score cutoffs, and model 2D used a continuous PHQ-2 score. Predictive performance was assessed using root mean squared error (RMSE; emphasizes sensitivity to outlier values), mean absolute error (MAE; a measure that reflects average error magnitude and is robust to outliers and interpretable in the same units as the original data), and coefficient of determination (R2; measures the proportion of variance explained by a model).23,24 Lower RMSE and MAE values and higher R2 values represent better predictive performance.25 In these analyses, R2 values were defined as poor (< 0.3), moderate (0.3–0.7), and good (> 0.7).

Tenfold cross-validation was used to evaluate the out-of-sample performance of all models.26 The prediction metrics outlined herein then were calculated based on all out-of-fold predictions across the 10 folds, allowing us to report the potential external validity of the model or how well the model performs on out-of-fold data. We report model performance characteristics for both derivation (on all observed data only) and cross-validated samples to convey both internal and external validity, respectively.

Evaluation of PHQ-2 Performance With the Addition of Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics:

To evaluate whether the addition of sociodemographic and clinical characteristics improved the predictive performance of PHQ-2 scores, we fit the same 8 models above (models 1A-2D) with the addition of variables shown in e-Table 1. To reduce the risk of overfitting because of the low events-per-variable ratio, we used a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator penalized regression method for each of the 8 models, using 10-fold cross-validation to find the optimal penalty parameter.27 Multicollinearity was examined using a generalized variance inflation factor and was not found.28 Penalized coefficients are reported in a heat map comparing each of the models, representing the magnitude and direction of associations. Additionally, calibration of these models was assessed using calibration slopes, calibration in-the-large intercepts, and associated binned calibration plots.

All model assumptions were assessed using partial residual plots for linearity, residuals vs fitted values for homoskedasticity, and quantile-quantile plots for normality. Patients missing key predictors of interest were excluded from respective analyses. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1 software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Because of the prediction-focused nature of the analysis, P values were not calculated.

Results

Participants

Of 247 participants randomized in the parent trial, 164 participants (66.4%) completed an in-hospital PHQ-2 survey. The median age of the study cohort was 56 years (interquartile range [IQR], 43–64 years), and most patients were male (n = 97 [59%]), White (n = 132 [81%]), and non-Hispanic or non-Latino (n = 150 [92%]). The median ICU length of stay was 3 days (IQR, 2–6 days) (Table 1).29

The median (IQR) and mean (SD) postdischarge PHQ-9 scores were 7 (0–11) and 7.9 (5.3), respectively, and 118 patients (72%) demonstrated clinically important depression symptoms based on a score of ≥ 5 (e-Tables 2, 3, e-Fig 1). Compared with patients with a PHQ-9 score of < 5, those with a score of ≥ 5 were more likely to be unpartnered (n = 58 [49.2%] vs n = 15 [32.6%]) and to have financial distress (n = 87 [73.7%] vs n = 21 [45.7%]) and were less likely to have undergone a surgical intervention as the main factor determining ICU eligibility (n = 68 [57.6%] vs n = 33 [71.7%]) (Table 1).

The prevalence of positive PHQ-2 score results by threshold was 110 (67%) for ≥ 1, 77 (47%) for ≥ 2, and 41 (25%) for ≥ 3 (Table 2). The median (IQR) and mean (SD) in-hospital PHQ-2 scores were 1 (0–2.3) and 1.7 (1.6), respectively (e-Table 2).

Optimization of PHQ-2 Cut Points in the Prediction of Postdischarge Depression Symptoms

Table 2 shows the performance characteristics of using 1 of 3 different PHQ-2 threshold scores (≥ 1, ≥ 2, or ≥3) to screen for clinically important postdischarge depression symptoms defined by PHQ-9 scores. The standard PHQ-2 threshold of ≥ 3 was present among only 41 patients (25%); all but 4 of these patients showed true-positive results as defined by a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 5, resulting in a positive predictive value of 90%. However, among all 118 patients with clinically important postdischarge PHQ-9 scores of ≥ 5, 81 patients (69%) showed a PHQ-2 score of < 3, consistent with the metric’s low (31%) sensitivity (e-Table 3). Based on Youden’s index, PHQ-2 thresholds of ≥ 1 and ≥ 2 seemed to be similarly optimal; however, the threshold of ≥ 1 showed higher sensitivity (78% vs 58%), whereas the threshold of ≥ 2 showed higher specificity (80% vs 61%) (Table 2).

Compared with a PHQ-9 score threshold of ≥ 5 to define clinically important symptoms, using a PHQ-9 score threshold of ≥ 10 improved the PHQ-2’s sensitivity slightly, although this diminished its specificity (Table 2). Using a PHQ-9 score threshold of 5, the PHQ-2’s performance among the 141 cohort members who were not receiving any therapy for depression at admission was similar to that of the larger cohort (Table 2).

Evaluation of the Predictive Performance of the PHQ-2

PHQ-2 Alone:

PHQ-2 scores alone generally demonstrated moderate predictive performance for postdischarge depression symptoms (ie, PHQ-9 scores ≥ 5). Continuous PHQ-2 scores provided better discrimination (AUC, 0.74) and prediction accuracy (Brier score, 0.17) compared with binary PHQ-2 categorization at all 3 threshold values (AUC range, 0.61–0.69; Brier score range, 0.17–0.19) (Table 3). In comparison with AUC values for the derivation models predicting the binary PHQ-9 outcome, the poor to moderate discriminative ability of cross-validated models (AUC range, 0.54–0.71) suggested potential overfitting, whereas Brier scores were comparable between the evaluation types (range, 0.17–0.19) (e-Table 4).

PHQ-2 scores also demonstrated a moderate ability to predict continuous PHQ-9 scores after discharge. Compared with binary PHQ-2 models, those using continuous PHQ-2 scores demonstrated more favorable performance metrics (RMSE, 4.23; MAE, 3.19; R2 = 0.36) (Table 3). Cross-validated results also revealed that use of the continuous PHQ-2 score provided a lower prediction error (RMSE, 4.17; MAE, 3.22) and highest explained variability (R2 = 0.42) than binary PHQ-2 classifications (e-Table 4).

PHQ-2 Score Plus Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

The PHQ-2’s predictive performance improved from a range of poor to moderate to a range of moderate to good with the addition of sociodemographic and clinical variables across all models predicting PHQ-9 scores of ≥ 5 (models 1A-1D) based on AUCs ranging from 0.77 to 0.83 and Brier scores ranging from 0.15 to 0.17 (Table 3). Cross-validated metrics declined across all models, with lower AUCs (0.61–0.67) and higher Brier scores (0.18–0.20), suggesting reduced generalizability to new data (e-Table 4). Models using continuous PHQ-2 scores showed slightly better predictive ability compared with models using binary PHQ-2 scores.

The inclusion of sociodemographic and clinical characteristics also led to improvements for Models 2A through 2D predicting continuous PHQ-9 scores (Table 3). Derivation model ranges of RMSE (3.83–4.23) and MAE (3.04–3.41) indicated that predicted PHQ-9 scores differed on average by 3 to 4 points from observed scores. Derivation model R2 values (0.37–0.48) suggested that the added covariates explained slightly more variation in continuous PHQ-9 scores compared with PHQ-2 alone, but did not markedly improve models’ overall explanatory power. Performance declined with cross-validation, indicating limited external validity, and continuous PHQ-2 scores provided slightly improved prediction compared with using a binary cutoff (e-Table 4).

Calibration also was assessed across all multivariable models. Models did not systematically overpredict or underpredict the probability of depression symptoms (all calibration intercepts around 0), but slopes were > 1, indicating that the models tended to underestimate risk among high-risk patients and overestimate risk for low-risk patients (e-Figs 2, 3).

Figure 1 shows that based on penalized coefficients from each of the 8 least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression models, higher PHQ-2 score and greater financial stress were identified consistently as key predictors of worse depression symptoms, whereas surgical diagnosis (vs medical) as the reason for ICU admission predicted fewer depression symptoms.

Discussion

Depression symptoms after critical illness can impair recovery. Unfortunately, although these symptoms are relatively common, few screening approaches tested to date are both accurate and feasible for in-hospital use. We found that the 2-item PHQ-2 screening survey applied in a hospital setting overall showed a moderate predictive accuracy for identifying clinically important postdischarge depression symptoms.

These results provide potentially useful information to guide clinical screening approaches. A PHQ-2 score of ≥ 2, similar to the value used in outpatient settings, seems to be a threshold that best balances sensitivity and specificity. However, the superior sensitivity of a ≥ 1 cutoff score might be a more practical initial screening step to flag those who may benefit from subsequent testing. The performance of both cutoff scores was stable across different definitions of postdischarge symptom severity. It should be noted that increasing the complexity of the screening assessment by adding sociodemographic and clinical characteristics improved the PHQ-2’s performance. This moved AUCs into the good predictive performance range in the case of a binary PHQ-9 outcome, a more logical clinical application. It could be feasible for some centers to add this enhanced capability by integrating PHQ-2 scores with an automated, electronic health record-integrated screening system.

It is important to consider key contextual information when interpreting the relevance of these analyses. First, the PHQ-2 is only a 2-item scale and 1 of its items may not be particularly relevant to the immediate post-ICU time frame (“little interest or pleasure in doing things”). Second, patients may have been at different points in the trajectory of illness given the variable duration of hospitalizations. Third, it is possible that some patients in fact were in an emotional upswing with an improvement in their condition that permitted transfer out of the ICU. This may have contrasted with a reality after discharge of caregiving needs, concerns about return to work, and other factors leading to comparably higher levels of distress. Finally, the population’s high prevalence (72%) of depression symptoms impacts performance measures such as positive and negative predictive value.

We believe that this study’s findings make a novel addition to the medical literature. First, we aimed not merely to determine associations, but also to test an inpatient screening approach widely used in outpatient settings for an outcome measured soon after discharge. Although it is valuable to consider the importance of predicting longer-term symptoms, past epidemiologic studies show that most patients’ symptoms improve or remain stable, rather than worsen.30 Therefore, predicting a baseline level of symptomatology is important for early identification of those who could benefit from proactive therapies. Second, these findings have important relevance for research teams because no past work to our knowledge has quantified sensitivity, specificity, or accuracy of any particular screening approach in this population. Finally, these data add to the already strong association between financial distress and postdischarge psychological distress and the apparently higher risk among medical compared with surgical patients on average.31–33 Despite the informative nature of these characteristics, prediction metrics indicated that other stronger unmeasured characteristics are likely that could be represent modifiable processes.

It is useful to consider these results in the context of past research. Summarizing the important work of others in this area is challenging because of wide variation in the surveys and score cutoff values used to define outcomes, predictor variables measured and the timing of their measurement, prevalence of symptoms within cohorts, and timing of outcome ascertainment. To our knowledge, no reports have been published on the accuracy of in-hospital screening surveys for ascertaining depression symptoms after discharge among ICU survivors. Systematic reviews also have failed to demonstrate consistently associations of symptoms after discharge with easily measurable sociodemographic and clinical characteristics such as sex, age, and illness severity.34–36 However, researchers have reported statistically significant associations between other variables and postdischarge psychological distress symptoms measured at 2 months (number of comorbidities, children < 18 years of age in household, and previous psychological symptoms),37 3 months (lack of social support, PHQ-2 score, and presence of traumatic memories38; and frailty, education level, strength, and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale before hospitalization),39 as well as 6 months (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale score before discharge and recall of ICU experiences).40

This work has limitations to consider. These data were derived from an intervention trial. However, the focus of our analyses was on a period preceding the deployment of an intervention. Also, the lack of formal power considerations and moderate sample size may have added imprecision to the results. The framing of the PHQ-2’s items as symptoms that occurred during the past 2 weeks may have reduced precision, given patients’ different durations of hospitalization. Also, the fact that the PHQ-2’s items are contained within the PHQ-9 could have inflated sensitivity and distorted specificity somewhat. Although administering the PHQ-9 itself during hospitalization may be considered a more complete approach, we focused on a simple strategy and one that did not include the management of those reporting suicidal ideation through the PHQ-9’s final item. Although participants were enrolled from 3 states representing 3 distinct regions of the country, these results may not be generalizable broadly to all areas. Finally, the PHQ-9 does not provide a clinical diagnosis of major depressive disorder.

Interpretation

Although the PHQ-2’s performance in the hospital for predicting clinically important depression symptoms after discharge among survivors of critical illness can be impacted by symptom prevalence and outcome definitions, a threshold score of ≥ 1 may represent a reasonable first screening step to identify those who might benefit from further evaluation. The PHQ-2’s performance was improved with the addition of sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, although this substantially increases the complexity of the screening process.
