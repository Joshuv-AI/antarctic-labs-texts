Atypical manifestation of pulmonary lymphatic perfusion syndrome treated with percutaneous lymphatic embolization
Authors: Melika Rezaee, Andreas M Loening, Stanley Rockson, Matthew Hung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a case of a 62-year-old male with a history of obesity, COPD, diabetes, and smoking, who presented with progressive dyspnea on exertion. CT chest imaging demonstrated interlobular septal and peribronchovascular thickening in a lymphatic distribution, lung biopsy demonstrated dilation of the pulmonary lymphatics with lobular septal fibrosis and he was diagnosed with diffuse pulmonary lymphangiomatosis. A pedal MR lymphangiogram failed to visualize the thoracic duct, prompting further evaluation with intranodal MR lymphangiography, which revealed markedly dilated pulmonary lymphatics and retrograde lymphatic flow. The patient underwent successful thoracic duct embolization using N-butyl cyanoacrylate and coils. Follow-up imaging showed resolution of interstitial thickening, and the patient reported significant symptomatic improvement. This case underscores the diagnostic value of intranodal MR lymphangiography over pedal techniques and highlights an uncommon imaging and clinical presentation of pulmonary lymphatic perfusion syndrome. Early identification and targeted embolization of lymphatic conduction anomalies can offer significant clinical benefit.

Letter

The patient is a 62-year-old male with obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, and 5 pack-year smoking history presenting with worsening dyspnea on exertion (DOE). He was evaluated at an outside hospital 15 years prior to presentation for shortness of breath and at that time CT chest demonstrated diffuse interlobular septal thickening as well as marked peribronchovascular thickening suggestive of pathology in a lymphatic distribution (Fig. 1A). This was of unclear etiology, so he underwent a surgical lung biopsy that demonstrated dilation of the pulmonary lymphatics with lobular septal fibrosis, and he was given a diagnosis of diffuse pulmonary lymphangiomatosis. Work-up for his current presentation was notable for cardiomyopathy with ejection fraction of 35–50 % and CT chest findings similar to prior. His pulmonary function tests were notable for forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) of 62 %, forced vital capacity (FVC) of 74 %, and diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) of 64 %, consistent with restrictive physiology. He underwent pedal MR lymphangiogram to further investigate his pulmonary lymphatic pathology at an outside hospital. Although the lymphatic channels of the injected limbs were opacified, the thoracic duct was not able to be visualized. After evaluation by pulmonology and cardiology at the outside hospital, the patient was told his condition could only be managed medically, and he was offered treatment with sirolimus. He presented to our institution for a second opinion, and we pursued further workup with an intranodal MR lymphangiogram. Intranodal MR lymphangiogram demonstrated markedly dilated pulmonary lymphatic channels with retrograde lymphatic flow from the thoracic duct toward lung parenchyma (Fig. 2).

A) Initial CT chest demonstrated diffuse interlobular septal thickening (black arrow) and marked peribronchovascular thickening (black circle) suggestive of pathology in a lymphatic distribution. B) Follow up CT chest 1 month after embolization demonstrated complete resolution of interlobular septal thickening and peribronchovascular thickening seen on prior imaging.

Intranodal MRI lymphangiogram demonstrated refluxing lymphatic channel with filling of markedly dilated pulmonary lymphatics (white arrows). There is retrograde filling of the right hilar lymphatics (dash white arrow).

The patient returned for direct lymphangiography and lymphatic embolization. Intranodal lymphangiography was performed using standard technique [1]. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the cisterna chyli was accessed percutaneously using a 21G needle. After access was obtained, a 2.0 Fr TruSelect microcatheter (Boston Scientific, Natick, Massachusetts) was advanced over a 0.018 inch V18 guidewire (Boston Scientific) into the thoracic duct and lymphangiography was performed. Lymphangiogram demonstrated a dominant refluxing lymphatic channel into the dilated left pulmonary lymphatics (Fig. 3) consistent with diagnostic intranodal MR lymphangiogram findings. The thoracic duct and dominant refluxing left pulmonary lymphatic channel were embolized using N-butyl cyanoacrylate (TruFill: Codman and Shurtleff, Raynham, Massachusetts) and coils (Fig. 4).

Intranodal fluoroscopic lymphangiogram demonstrated refluxing lymphatic channels into the dilated left pulmonary lymphatics (black arrow). There is retrograde filling of the right hilar lymphatics (dash black arrow). These findings match those identified on the intranodal MR lymphangiogram.

Post-embolization spot image demonstrated coils and glue cast filling dominant refluxing lymphatic channel and thoracic duct.

The patient was seen in follow-up 1 month after the procedure. Repeat CT demonstrated resolution of the previously seen interlobular septal thickening and peribronchovascular thickening (Fig. 1B). In addition, patient reported improvement in his dyspnea, particularly the ability to take deep breaths. He still had some DOE, likely attributed to his heart failure and he is currently undergoing medical optimization of heart failure.

We hypothesize that the patient's symptomatic improvement, including his ability to now take deep breaths, is due to the resolution of lymphatic edema within the pulmonary interstitium, leading to improved lung compliance.

Central conducting lymphatic anomalies can manifest as lymphatic leaks in various body compartments and produce significant morbidity. In the thorax, this is termed pulmonary lymphatic perfusion syndrome and may manifest as chylothorax, chylopericardium and/or plastic bronchitis (PB) [2]. This case highlights the utility and increased sensitivity of intranodal MR lymphangiogram over pedal MR lymphangiogram for lymphatic disorders in the chest, abdomen, or pelvis. A pedal lymphangiogram cannot get as much contrast into the lymphatics as an intranodal lymphangiogram, and the small molecular weight gadolinium based contrast agents typically used may also leak out of the lymphatics as the contrast progresses through the limbs, resulting in suboptimal opacification of the central lymphatics. Once identified, prior studies such as Dori et al. showed that interruption of the abnormal lymphatic flow resulted in significant improvement (88 %) of symptoms in patients with pulmonary lymphatic perfusion [2,3].

This case also highlights an uncommon imaging presentation of pulmonary lymphatic perfusion syndrome. O'Leary et al. studied CT patterns in patients with plastic bronchitis and demonstrated that ground-glass opacities were present in 91 % of patients with lymphatic plastic bronchitis [4]. This patient presented with interstitial thickening without ground-glass opacities, and clinically presented with dyspnea on exertion in the absence of chyloptysis, chylothorax or chylopericardium.

In summary, our case suggests that in patients with CT findings suggestive of a lymphatic process, further work-up should be pursued with an intranodal MR lymphangiogram to search for a lymphatic conduction anomaly, which can be treated successfully with embolization.
