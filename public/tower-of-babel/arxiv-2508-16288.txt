Curvature at the infinity of asymptotically flat Einstein manifold
Bing Wang, Hao Yin

Abstract
. In this paper, we construct coordinates at the infinity of an asymptotically flat end of a Ricci-flat manifold ( M m , g ) (M^{m},g) as long as the L m / 2 L^{m/2} norm of the curvature is finite in this end. As applications, we can define a Weyl tensor at the infinity of M M and a version of renormalized volume following Biquard and Hein to ALE Ricci-flat manifolds/orbifolds of any dimension.



1.
Introduction

Suppose that
(M,g)
is an
m
-dimensional Riemannian manifold and
\Omega
is an end of
M
satisfying

(1)

(M,g)
is Ricci-flat on
\Omega
;

(2)

Vol(B(o,r)\cap\Omega)\geq Cr^{m}
, for some fixed
o\in M
and
C>0
and any
r>0
;

(3)

\int_{\Omega}\left|Rm\right|^{m/2}dV_{g}<\infty
.

It was proved in a famous paper of Bando, Kasue and Nakajima
[
BKN89
]
that this end is ALE of order
\tau=m-1
in the sense that there exists a compact subset
K\subset M
,
R>0
, a finite subgroup
\Gamma\subset O(m)
acting freely on
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B(0,R)
, a diffeomorphism
\mathcal{L}
from
\Omega\setminus K
to
(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B(0,R))/\Gamma
such that if

\tilde{g}:=(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\circ{\rm proj})^{*}g

where
{\rm proj}
is the projection from
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B(0,R)
to
(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B(0,R))/\Gamma
, then

\tilde{g}_{ij}(z)=\delta_{ij}+O(\left|z\right|^{-\tau})

and for any positive integer
k

(1.1)

\partial_{k}\tilde{g}_{ij}(z)=O(\left|z\right|^{-\tau-k}).

The estimates on the derivatives follow from the Ricci-flat condition and the fact that the coordinate obtained in
[
BKN89
]
is a harmonic coordinate system.

Moreover, in the case
m=4
, or
(M,g)
is Kähler, this
\tau
can be improved to be
m
(see Theorem 1.5 of
[
BKN89
]
). Later, it was observed by Cheeger and Tian
[
CT94
]
that as long as
\Gamma
is not trivial,
\tau
can be taken to be
m
in general. Kröncke and Szabó recently
[
KS24
]
pointed out a gap in
[
CT94
]
and fixed it as a corollary of their main theorems. It is worth noting that both
[
CT94
]
and
[
KS24
]
discuss the more general setting of asymptotically conical end.

While
\tau=m
is optimal, various authors continue to improve our understanding towards the regularity of metric at the infinity. For example, Chen
[
Che20
]
proved a full expansion of
\tilde{g}_{ij}
in the coordinate constructed in
[
BKN89
]
. Recently, more precise expansion for harmonic functions and harmonic one-forms were obtained by Chen and Yan
[
CY25
]
.

For
m=4
, Biquard and Hein conducted a detailed analysis of the leading term
h_{0}
in the expansion of
\tilde{g}_{ij}
. In particular, in Section 2 of
[
BH23
]
, it was proved that
h_{0}
is the sum of harmonic gauge terms and Kronheimer terms. While the former could be removed by a further coordinate change, the latter was shown to be exactly the leading term of the metric expansion for the family of ALE Kähler Ricci-flat four manifold due to Kronheimer. Although not explicitly mentioned, the computation in Proposition 2.6 in
[
BH23
]
actually established an isomorphism between the space of Kronheimer terms and the space of Weyl tensors on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
(See also Appendix A of
[
Ozu24
]
).

1.1.
The main results

In this paper, we revisit the problem of establishing optimal decay rates and characterizing the leading terms in general dimensions. We need the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

For a function
f
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B(0,R)
, it is said to be in
O(\left|x\right|^{-\tau})
if there exist constants
C(k)
such that

\left|\partial^{(k)}f\right|(x)\leq C(k)\left|x\right|^{-\tau-k}\qquad\text{for}\quad k=0,1,2,\cdots

on
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B(0,R)
.

Our first result is

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(M,g)
be
m
-dimensional Riemannian manifold and
\Omega
is an end satisfying

(1)

(M,g)
is Ricci-flat on
\Omega
;

(2)

Vol(B(o,r)\cap\Omega)\geq Cr^{m}
, for some fixed
o\in M
and
C>0
and any
r>0
;

(3)

\int_{\Omega}\left|Rm\right|^{m/2}dV_{g}<\infty
.

Then there is a compact set
K
in
M
such that there is a diffeomorphism
\Psi
from the universal cover of
\Omega\setminus K
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B
that gives a coordinate system satisfying

(I) it is Bianchi, in the sense that if
g_{e}
is the flat metric given by the coordinates, then

B_{g_{e}}g:=\delta_{g_{e}}g-\frac{1}{2}d({\rm Tr}_{g_{e}}g)=0.

Moreover, this is equivalent to say that
\Psi
is a harmonic map from
g_{e}
to
g
.

(II) the metric tensor has an expansion

(1.2)

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+W_{iklj}\frac{x_{k}x_{l}}{\left|x\right|^{{m}+2}}+O(\left|x\right|^{-m-1}).

Here
W_{ikjl}
is a Weyl tensor.

Remark 1.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be the fundamental group of
\Omega\setminus K
. In previously mentioned results such as
[
CT94
]
,
[
KS24
]
, it is necessary to assume
\Gamma\neq\left\{1\right\}
. This is not needed for Theorem
1.2
. The key point is that instead of focusing on one coordinate, we use an intertwined process of finding new coordinates and improving the regularity of the metric. Indeed, in the rest of the paper, we always assume that
\Omega\setminus K
is simply connected. In case
\Gamma\neq\left\{1\right\}
, it is easy to pass to the universal cover and work with
\Gamma
-equivariant spaces in the proofs.

Remark 1.4
.

There is a harmonic coordinate version of the above theorem, with (I) replaced by: (I’) the components of
\Psi
are harmonic functions. This could be proved similarly. Instead of repeating, we will indicate at various places how the proofs can be modified for this purpose.

Given Theorem
1.2
, it is natural to ask if the Weyl tensor
W
is independent of the choice of coordinate. Or equivalently, is it an intrinsic geometric property of an end of manifold satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
? Our next theorem provides a positive answer.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(M,g)
and
\Omega
be as in Theorem
1.2
. Assume that there are two coordinate systems
(x_{k})
and
(y_{k})
defined on (the universal cover of)
\Omega\setminus K
such that in each of these coordinate systems, we have Weyl-type tensor
W
and
\tilde{W}
respectively satisfying

g=(\delta_{ij}+W_{iabj}\frac{x_{a}x_{b}}{\left|x\right|^{m+2}}+O(\left|x\right|^{-(m+1)}))dx_{i}\otimes dx_{j}

and

g=(\delta_{ij}+\tilde{W}_{iabj}\frac{y_{a}y_{b}}{\left|y\right|^{m+2}}+O(\left|y\right|^{-(m+1)}))dy_{i}\otimes dy_{j}.

Then there exists an
A\in O(m)
such that

W_{ijkl}=\tilde{W}_{abcd}A_{ai}A_{bj}A_{ck}A_{dl}.

Remark 1.6
.

In Definition
1.1
, we have assumed a bound for derivatives of any order. This is not necessary for the validity of Theorem
1.5
. We have chosen to keep the exposition simple.

The coordinate in Theorem
1.2
in spite of possessing good properties is not unique (up to rotation). Hence, it is natural to ask the following question (with the exponential map and the normal coordinate in mind): is it possible to define a better coordinate that is unique (up to rotation) and whose metric expansion contains more geometric information?

We then move on to discuss the renormalized volume. On noncompact manifolds, the total volume is often infinity. However, under reasonable assumptions on the geometry of the manifolds, it is often useful to compare this infinity (more precisely the growth rate) with some standard example and the difference is usually called the renormalized volume. It was first defined for asymptotically hyperbolic Einstein metrics by Graham
[
Gra00
]
. Later, Anderson
[
And01
]
found an explicit formula for the renormalized volume in this case. Brendle and Chodosh
[
BC14
]
defined a renormalized volume in a different setting for asymptotically hyperbolic metrics. For the asymptotically Euclidean (or flat) case, the renormalized volume was defined by Biquard and Hein
[
BH23
]
for ALE Ricci-flat four-manifolds/orbifolds. In this case, they proved that the renormlized volume is nonpositive and established the rigidity for the equal case.

In this paper, using the coordinate above, we generalize the definition of Biquard and Hein to higher dimensions. First, we note that Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.5
are about the geometry of an end. Therefore, we may allow
M
to be an orbifold as long as its end satisfies the assumptions. Both Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.5
are still valid. Let
x
be the coordinate defined on
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B
given in Theorem
1.2
. Set

B_{r}=M\setminus\left\{\left|x\right|>r\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{B}_{r}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}|\,\left|x\right|<r\right\}/\Gamma.

The renormalized volume
\mathcal{V}
is defined to be the limit

(1.3)

\lim_{r\to\infty}V_{g}(B_{r})-V_{g_{e}}(\tilde{B}_{r}).

Following the discussions of
[
BH23
]
, we have

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(M,g)
be
m
-dimensional ALE Ricci-flat manifold/orbifold such that the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
hold for its end
\Omega
. Using the above notations, the limit in (
1.3
) exists and is independent of the choice of the coordinate that satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
. Moreover,
\mathcal{V}\leq 0
. The equality holds if and only if
(M,g)
is
\mathbb{R}^{m}/\Gamma
.

In the case of
m=4
, the renormalized volume
\mathcal{V}
coincides with the one defined in
[
BH23
]
. In
[
BH23
]
, the uniqueness of CMC foliation (see
[
CEV17
]
) is used in order to justify that
\mathcal{V}
is an intrinsic geometric quantity. Here, we take a different approach. Our proof relies on the fact that any two coordinates satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
are the same up to high order.

1.2.
Idea of the proofs

To illustrate the idea used in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we start from the well known expansion of any Riemannian metric
g_{ij}
in its normal coordinate system,

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}R_{iabj}x_{a}x_{b}+O(\left|x\right|^{3}).

This formula is discussed in many textbooks on Riemannian geometry. The popular proof uses Jacobi fields. There is another direct proof due to Riemann (as explained by Spivak in
[
Spi79
]
, see Volume 2, Chapter 4). There is also an interesting and informative discussion of the history of this formula available at StackExchange
[
Bry14
]
. Among the answers posted there, there is one due to Bryant that is very relevant to the proofs in this paper, which we summarize briefly as follows.

Since
g_{ij}
is smooth, one starts with the Taylor expansion

(1.4)

g_{ij}=a_{ij}+b_{ij,k}x_{k}+c_{ij,kl}x_{k}x_{l}+\cdots

It is easy to see that we can use a linear coordinate change to make
a_{ij}
equal to
\delta_{ij}
, in which sense (
1.4
) is simplified. The idea is that we may continue to do so and look for better and better coordinate in which
g_{ij}
becomes as ”simple” as possible. By considering a coordinate change

\varphi(x)_{i}=x_{i}+A_{jk,i}x_{j}x_{k},

we can eliminate the linear term
b_{ij,k}x_{k}
if
A_{jk,i}
is chosen correctly (by solving some linear equations). Finally, one may consider one more coordinate change

\varphi(x)_{i}=x_{i}+B_{jkl,i}x_{j}x_{k}x_{l}

to reduce the
c_{ij,kl}
to a curvature tensor. To the best of our knowledge, we do not know a complete proof in the literature. Hence, we provide the full details of this proof in Section
4
with the linear algebra formulas proved in Section
2
.

A part of Theorem
1.2
amounts to an adaptation of the above argument to the infinity of an asymptotically flat Ricci-flat metric. We take an intertwined argument of algebra (to eliminate/simplify the coefficients in the expansion) and analysis (to produce the next nontrivial leading term in the expansion using Einstein equation). More precisely, we use
[
BKN89
]
as our starting point(hence our argument is then independent of later progresses), i.e. a harmonic coordinate at infinity in which (by elliptic regularity of the Einstein equation) the metric is

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+A_{ijk}\frac{x_{k}}{\left|x\right|^{m}}+O(\left|x\right|^{-m}).

Some algebraic lemma allows us to find a new coordinate in which
A_{ijk}\equiv 0
. This is a feature of the present argument in the sense that we do not assume
\Gamma\neq\left\{1\right\}
to show the vanishing of
A_{ijk}
, instead, we find a new coordinate. Then, we solve the harmonic map equation to turn the new coordinate into a Bianchi coordinate while keeping the expansion. Then elliptic regularity of the Einstein equation again gives an expansion

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+A_{ijkl}\frac{x_{k}x_{l}}{\left|x\right|^{m+2}}+O(\left|x\right|^{-m-1}).

Another algebraic argument implies the existence of a new coordinate in which this
A_{ijkl}
is reduced to a Weyl tensor. Finally, we solve the harmonic map equation again to find the coordinate claimed in Theorem
1.2
.

Now, we explain the proof of Theorem
1.5
. The identity map is certainly a harmonic map. If we use coordinate
x
in the domain and coordinate
y
in the target, the identity map is given by
\varphi(x)=y
for some smooth function
\varphi
. Given that
g_{ij}
is approximately
\delta_{ij}
in both
x
-coordinate and
y
-coordinate, it is obvious that when
x
goes to infinity,
\varphi
gets closer and closer to an orthogonal transformation plus a translation. The key to the proof is to develop the regularity of
\varphi
using the harmonic map equation and the assumed expansion of metric tensor in both coordinates. For simplicity, if we assume the orthogonal transformation and the translation at the infinity is trivial, the regularity analysis yields

\varphi(x)_{i}=x_{i}+B_{ij}\frac{x_{j}}{\left|x\right|^{m}}+\cdots

for some two-tensor
B\in(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes 2}
.
Formal computation shows that it is this two-tensor
B_{ij}
that is responsible of transforming
W
to
\tilde{W}
. However, some linear algebra result (see Lemma
5.4
) implies that
W=\tilde{W}
as claimed by the theorem.

Given the existence and the “almost” uniqueness of a good coordinate at the infinity, it is natural to use it to define various geometric quantities. Besides the Weyl tensor given in Theorem
1.5
, we follow Biquard and Hein
[
BH23
]
for the definition of the renormalized volume (
1.3
). Our uniqueness result implies that it is an intrinsic geometric quantity. The rest of Theorem
1.7
is proved using a result of
[
Ros87
]
(as suggested by Remark 3.1 of
[
BH23
]
).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we discuss some linear algebra results needed later. In Section
3
, we prove a theorem about the existence of Bianchi coordinate. In Section
4
, we give precise details to the local result outlined by Bryant that serves as a precursor to Theorem
1.2
, and strengthen it a little by asking the coordinate to be Bianchi. The result in this section is not logically necessary for the proof of the main theorems in this paper. We simply hope they are interesting in its own right. In Section
5
, we prove the two main theorems. Finally, in Section
6
, we discuss the concept of renormalized volume and show that in the special case of dimension four, it agrees with what is known in
[
BH23
]
.

2.
Some algebraic facts

In this section, we define some maps between tensor spaces and discuss a few properties that are useful to the proof of our main results. The maps defined in Section
2.2
and the their properties may look strange at first, but they will be shown to be useful later.

2.1.
Notations

To start, we fix notations. Throughout this paper, we are interested on various tensors on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. With the standard inner product of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
in mind, we make no distinction between covariant and contravariant tensors. If
A
is a
4
-tensor, its components are denoted by
A_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}}
. For simplicity, we write
A_{(1234)}
for
A_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}}
. We denote by
[A_{(1234)}]
the tensor whose components are
A_{(1234)}
. Please note that using this notation,
[A_{(2134)}]
is different from
[A_{(1234)}]
. The same convention applies to
2
-tensors and
3
-tensors.

We use
\delta=[\delta_{(12)}]
for the Kronecker delta. We also assume the summation convention that repeated indices are summed over. We use
{\rm Tr}_{(13)}
for the trace over the first and the third indices of a tensor. For example, if
A
is a
4
-tensor, by taking
{\rm Tr}_{(13)}
, we obtain a
2
-tensor.

{\rm Tr}_{(13)}([A_{(1234)}])=[A_{(a1a2)}].

We also use
S_{(12)}
for the symmetrization of the first and second indices of a tensor. More precisely,

S_{(12)}([A_{(123)}])=\frac{1}{2}[A_{(123)}+A_{(213)}].

S_{(234)}
is understood similarly.

S^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
is the space of symmetric
k
-tensors.
S^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
is the traceless symmetric
2
-tensors and
\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
is the skew-symmetric
2
-tensors. These spaces arise naturally in our paper. For example, when we consider the Taylor expansion of a metric
g_{ij}
, the linear term is
a_{ijk}x_{k}
where
[a_{(123)}]
is a tensor in
S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Finally,
\mathcal{C}
is the space of curvature tensors and
\mathcal{W}
is the space of Weyl tensors. We define a map
s
on
\mathcal{C}
by

s([R_{(1234)}])=\frac{1}{2}([R_{(1342)}+R_{(1432)}])\in S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\qquad\text{}\quad\forall R\in\mathcal{C}.

It is well known that
s
is injective on
\mathcal{C}
. We denote
s(\mathcal{C})
by
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
and
s(\mathcal{W})
by
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The map
s
is related to the so-called curvature operator of the second kind, which we refer to Section 2.3 of
[
BK78
]
for its definition.

Remark 2.2
.

As a corollary of the first Bianchi identity, for any tensor
A
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
, we have

A_{(1234)}+A_{(1342)}+A_{(1423)}=0.

2.2.
Maps between tensors

Using these notations, we write down a few important maps between tensors that will be used in later proofs.

(1)

{\mathbf{R}}:S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathcal{C}
defined by

(2.1)

{\mathbf{R}}(A)=[A_{(2413)}-A_{(2314)}-A_{(1423)}+A_{(1324)}].

For the meaning of this map, we notice that if the metric is
g_{(12)}=\delta_{(12)}+A_{(1234)}x_{(3)}x_{(4)}
for
A\in S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, then the curvature tensor at the origin is
{\mathbf{R}}(A)
.

(2)

\tilde{\mathbf{R}}:S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
defined by

\tilde{\mathbf{R}}(A)=-\frac{1}{6}[{\mathbf{R}}(A)_{(1342)}+{\mathbf{R}}(A)_{(1432)}].

Given
g
as above, this is the tensor that appears as the second order term in the expansion of
g
in the normal coordinate system.

(3)

\Psi_{1}:S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}\to S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by

\Psi_{1}(B)=-2[B_{(312)}+B_{(321)}].

This is related to the coordinate change of the linear term in the expansion of
g_{ij}
.

(4)

\Psi_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes S^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
defined by

\Psi_{2}(B)=-3[B_{(1234)}+B_{(2134)}].

This is related to the coordinate change of the quadratic term in the expansion of
g_{ij}
.

(5)

\Psi_{3}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by

\Psi_{3}(B)=-(m-2)[\delta_{(13)}B_{(2)}+\delta_{(23)}B_{(1)}].

This is related to the coordinate change of the
-(m-1)
order term in the expansion of an asymptotically flat metric at infinity.

(6)

\Psi_{4}:(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\otimes 2}\to S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
defined by

\Psi_{4}(B)=[B_{(12)}\delta_{(34)}+B_{(21)}\delta_{(34)}-\frac{m}{2}(B_{(13)}\delta_{(24)}+B_{(14)}\delta_{(23)}+B_{(23)}\delta_{(14)}+B_{(24)}\delta_{(13)})].

This is related to the coordinate change of the
-m
order term in the expansion of an asymptotically flat metric at infinity.

(7)

The Bianchi operator acting formally on the
-(m-1)
order term of the metric expansion motivates

{\mathbf{B}}_{1}:S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}\to S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}

{\mathbf{B}}_{1}(A)=[2A_{(3aa)}\delta_{(12)}-m(A_{(312)}+A_{(321)})-A_{(aa3)}\delta_{(12)}+\frac{m}{2}(A_{(aa2)}\delta_{(13)}+A_{(aa1)}\delta_{(23)})].

(8)

The Bianchi operator acting formally on the
-m
order term of the metric expansion motivates

{\mathbf{B}}_{2}:S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes S^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{m})

{\mathbf{B}}_{2}(A)=S_{(234)}[4A_{(a1a4)}\delta_{(23)}-2(m+2)A_{(3124)}-2A_{(aa14)}\delta_{(23)}+(m+2)A_{(aa24)}\delta_{(13)}].

2.3.
Decomposition of tensor spaces

In this subsection, we collect a few properties for the maps defined in the previous subsection. Those maps can be regarded as morphisms of representations of
O(m)
. Hence, they may be understood via a decomposition of the domains and the images into irreducible representations. However, we refrain from using any abstract theory, instead we prove only results that are needed in later proofs using elementary and explicit methods.

Proposition 2.3
.

The map
\Psi_{1}:S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}\to S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

\Psi_{1}
is a linear map between vector spaces of the same dimension. Hence it suffices to show that
\Psi_{1}
is injective. Namely, for any
[B_{(123)}]\in S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying

B_{(312)}=-B_{(321)},

we need to show that
[B_{(123)}]=0
. In fact, since
B
is symmetric in the first two indices and skew-symmetric in the last two indices, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{(123)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{(213)}=-B_{(231)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-B_{(321)}=B_{(312)}=B_{(132)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-B_{(123)},

which implies that
B=0
.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

There is the direct sum decomposition

S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})=\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}\oplus\Psi_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes S^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{m})).

More precisely, for any
A\in S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes S^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
,

A=\tilde{\mathbf{R}}(A)+\Psi_{2}(B)

where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes S^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
is defined by

(2.2)

B=-\frac{1}{18}[2A_{(1234)}+2A_{(1324)}+2A_{(1423)}-A_{(3412)}-A_{(2314)}-A_{(2413)}].

Proof.

The fact that
A=\tilde{\mathbf{R}}+\Psi_{2}(B)
can be proved by straightforward computation. In fact, we have

\tilde{\mathbf{R}}(A)=-\frac{1}{6}[A_{(2314)}+A_{(2413)}-2A_{(3412)}-2A_{(1234)}+A_{(1432)}+A_{(1324)}]

and

\Psi_{2}(B)=\frac{1}{6}\left[4A_{(1234)}-2A_{(3412)}+A_{(1324)}+A_{(1423)}+A_{(2314)}+A_{(2413)}\right],

whose sum is exactly
A
.

To see that this is a direct sum, assume that we have
C\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes S^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that

C+\Psi_{2}(B)=0.

By Remark
2.2
,
C
and hence
\Psi_{2}(B)
satisfy the first Bianchi identity,

(\Psi_{2}(B))_{(1234)}+(\Psi_{2}(B))_{(1342)}+(\Psi_{2}(B))_{(1423)}=0,

which is (using the definition of
\Psi_{2}
)

(2.3)

3B_{(1234)}+B_{(2134)}+B_{(3124)}+B_{(4123)}=0.

Here in the above computation, we have used the symmetry of
B
in its last three indices. We do a permutation of the four indices in the above equation and sum them up to see

B_{(1234)}+B_{(2134)}+B_{(3124)}+B_{(4123)}=0,

which (together with (
2.3
)) implies that
B=0
. Therefore
C=0
and the proof of the lemma is done.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

We have the following direct sum decomposition

3.
Bianchi coordinate at infinity

The aim of this section is to show how we can turn an almost Bianchi coordinate into a Bianchi one. This amounts to solving a harmonic map equation using the Schauder fixed point theorem.

We assume that a natural coordinate system is given for
\Omega_{R}:=\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let
g_{e}
be the flat metric given by this coordinate. A map
\varphi
from
\Omega_{R}
to
\Omega_{R}
is a vector-valued function. It is harmonic from
(\Omega_{R},g_{e})
to
(\Omega_{R},g)
when

\triangle\varphi_{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(\varphi(x))\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial x_{a}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{k}}{\partial x_{a}}=0,\qquad\text{for}\quad i=1,\cdots,m

Here
\Gamma
is the Christoffel symbol of the metric
g
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given a Riemannian metric
g
defined in
\Omega_{R}
satisfying

(3.1)

g_{ij}-\delta_{ij}\in O(\left|x\right|^{-(\tilde{n}-2)})

for some
\tilde{n}>2
, assume the identity map from
\Omega_{R}
to itself is approximately a harmonic map from
(\Omega_{R},g_{e})
to
(\Omega_{R},g)
in the sense that

(3.2)

\Gamma^{i}_{aa}(x)=O(\left|x\right|^{-\tilde{n}}).

Then for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is some
R^{\prime}
sufficiently large and a
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued function
u
defined on
\Omega_{R^{\prime}}
such that
u
solves

(3.3)

\triangle u_{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(x+u(x))\left(\delta_{ja}+\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{a}}\right)\left(\delta_{ka}+\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial x_{a}}\right)=0

and

u\in O(\left|x\right|^{-(\tilde{n}-2-\varepsilon)}).

In particular,
\varphi(x)=x+u(x)
is a harmonic map from
(\Omega_{R^{\prime}},g_{e})
to
(\Omega_{R},g)
.

The proof requires some estimates for the Poisson equation in weighted Schauder spaces, which will be discussed in Section
3.1
and Section
3.2
. After that, we prove Theorem
3.1
.

3.1.
Weighted function space

For
R>0
, recall that
\Omega_{R}=\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{R}
and set

S_{r}:B_{2}\setminus B_{1}\to B_{2r}\setminus B_{r}

to be the map

S_{r}(x)=rx.

For functions defined on
\Omega_{R}
, we define the weighted Hölder norm (
\beta>0
and
\beta\notin\mathbb{Z}
)

\left\|v\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})}=\sup_{r\geq R}r^{\beta}\left\|v\circ S_{r}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1})}.

We denote the corresponding Banach space by
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})
. When there is no ambiguity, we often omit
\Omega_{R}
and write
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}
only.

Remark 3.2
.

Here in the above definitions, we allow
\alpha
to be any positive real number other than integers. For example, by
C^{5/2}
, we mean the set of functions whose derivatives up to second order are Hölder continuous with exponent
1/2
.

Remark 3.3
.

The exact value of
\alpha
is not important for the proofs in this paper. Later, for technical convenience, we may require
\alpha
to be bigger than some dimensional constant so that we can use the fact that: a
C^{\alpha}
function on
S^{m-1}
has its spherical harmonic expansion converge uniformly. See
[
Kal95
]
.

We discuss a few properties of the weighted functions.

For the derivative, we have

(3.4)

\left\|\partial_{x_{i}}v\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\beta+1}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\left\|v\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})}\qquad\text{}\quad\forall i=1,\cdots m.

To see this, for any
r\geq R
,

\displaystyle r^{\beta+1}\left\|(\partial_{x_{i}}v)\circ S_{r}\right\|_{C^{\alpha-1}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{\beta+1}\left\|r^{-1}\partial_{x_{i}}(v\circ S_{r})\right\|_{C^{\alpha-1}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r^{\beta}\left\|v\circ S_{r}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1})}.

For the multiplication, we have

(3.5)

\left\|fg\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\min(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}(\Omega_{R})}\left\|g\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}}(\Omega_{R})}.

The proof is omitted.

We will also need the following result about the composition of weighted functions.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume that
f=O(\left|x\right|^{-l})
for some
l>0
on
\Omega_{R/2}
. For
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued function
u
on
\Omega_{R}
, set

\varphi(x)=x+u(x).

If for any
\beta>0

\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})}\leq 1,

then as long as
R
is larger than some universal number, we have

\left\|f\circ\varphi\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,l}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C.

Proof.

For
r\geq R
, we need to bound

r^{l}\left\|f\circ\varphi\circ S_{r}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1})}.

By the assumption of
f
,

r^{l}\left\|f\circ S_{r}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1})}\leq C\qquad\text{for all}\quad r>R/2.

Since
f\circ\varphi\circ S_{r}=f\circ S_{r}\circ S_{r}^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ S_{r}
, it remains to show

(3.6)

S_{r}^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ S_{r}:B_{2}\setminus B_{1}\to B_{4}\setminus B_{1/2}

is bounded in
C^{\alpha}
. In fact, we have

S_{r}^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ S_{r}(x)=x+r^{-1}u(rx)

and (
3.6
) follows from our assumption for
u
, in particular,

\left\|u\circ S_{r}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1})}\leq 1.

∎

3.2.
Poisson equation on
\Omega_{R}

The aim of this subsection is to prove

Theorem 3.5
.

There is a bounded linear map

\Psi:\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R})\to\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})

such that

\triangle\Psi(f)=f\qquad\text{on}\quad\Omega_{R}

for any
f\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R})
. Moreover, there is a constant
C
independent of
R
such that

\left\|\Psi(f)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R})}.

This result should be well known. For completeness, we include a proof.

Proof.

It is important to note that throughout the proof, the constants are independent of
R
.

Given
f\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R})
, we first extend its domain to
\Omega_{R/2}
while keeping the norm bounded. Denote the extended function by
\tilde{f}
, then we have

\left\|\tilde{f}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R/2})}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R})}.

By using some cut-off function to further extend the domain of
\tilde{f}
, still denoted by
\tilde{f}
, to the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we get a
C^{\alpha-2}
function on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying

\left|\tilde{f}\right|(x)\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R})}\left|x\right|^{\beta+2}.

Given the decay and
\beta>0
, convolution of the Green’s function gives us a solution
u
to the Poisson equation

\triangle u=\tilde{f}\qquad\text{on}\quad\mathbb{R}^{m}

and

\left|u\right|(x)\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-2,\beta+2}(\Omega_{R})}\left|x\right|^{\beta}.

Define

\Psi(f)=u|_{\Omega_{R}}.

The desired bound on
\left\|\Psi(f)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega_{R})}
follows from the elliptic estimates.
∎

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Since
\tilde{n}
is assumed to be larger than
2
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon
is very small so that we may set
\beta=\tilde{n}-\varepsilon>2
and choose
\beta^{\prime}\in(\beta,\tilde{n})
.
For simplicity of notation, we set

F[u]_{i}:=\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(x+u(x))\left(\delta_{ja}+\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{a}}\right)\left(\delta_{ka}+\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial x_{a}}\right).

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
\delta>0
, there is
R
sufficiently large (depending on the metric and
\delta
,
\beta^{\prime}
,
\tilde{n}
) so that for any

with
\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta-2}(\Omega_{R})}\leq 1
, we have

\left\|F[u]\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\beta^{\prime}}(\Omega_{R})}\leq\delta.

Proof.

The proof consists of two steps.

Step 1.

There exists some constant
C
such that

(3.7)

\left\|F[u]\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\tilde{n}}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C.

For the proof, we compute

F[u]_{i}=\Gamma^{i}_{aa}(x+u(x))+2\Gamma^{i}_{ja}(x+u(x))\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{a}}+\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(x+u(x))\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{a}}\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial x_{a}}.

Given the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, we have

\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(x)=O(\left|x\right|^{-(\tilde{n}-1)}),\qquad\Gamma^{i}_{aa}(x)=O(\left|x\right|^{-\tilde{n}}).

Lemma
3.4
implies that

\left\|\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(x+u(x))\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\tilde{n}-1}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C

and

\left\|\Gamma^{i}_{aa}(x+u(x))\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\tilde{n}}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C.

Using (

) and the assumption of

, we have

\left\|\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{a}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\beta-1}(\Omega_{R})}\leq C.

Noting that
\beta-1>1

).

Step 2.

With (

), the proof of the lemma is done by taking

and the constant

in (

\left\|F[u]\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\beta^{\prime}}(\Omega_{R})}\leq\left\|F[u]\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\tilde{n}}(\Omega_{R})}(R)^{(\beta^{\prime}-\tilde{n})}.

The above inequality is a direct consequence of the definition of the weighted norm. In fact, for any function
w\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\tilde{n}}(\Omega_{R})
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|w\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\beta^{\prime}}(\Omega_{R})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{r\geq R}r^{\beta^{\prime}}\left\|w\circ S_{r}\right\|_{C^{\alpha-1}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(R)^{\beta^{\prime}-\tilde{n}}\sup_{r\geq R}r^{\tilde{n}}\left\|w\circ S_{r}\right\|_{C^{\alpha-1}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(R)^{\beta^{\prime}-\tilde{n}}\left\|w\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\tilde{n}}(\Omega_{R})}.

∎

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

For the constant
C
in Theorem
3.5
, choose
\delta=\frac{1}{C}
. Let
R^{\prime}
be the

given by Lemma
3.6
. Consider the unit ball
\mathcal{B}
in the Banach space
\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta-2}(\Omega_{R^{\prime}})
. For each

, Lemma
3.6
implies that

\left\|F[u]\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha-1,\beta^{\prime}}(\Omega_{R^{\prime}})}\leq\delta.

Theorem
3.5
gives some

satisfying

\left\|v\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\beta^{\prime}-2}(\Omega_{R^{\prime}})}\leq 1.

Consider the natural embedding

\iota:\mathcal{X}_{\alpha+1,\beta^{\prime}-2}(\Omega_{R^{\prime}})\to\mathcal{X}_{\alpha,\beta-2}(\Omega_{R^{\prime}}).

For
\beta^{\prime}>\beta
,

is a compact linear operator satisfying

By defining

we obtain a compact operator from

to itself. Then the Schauder fixed point theorem implies the existence of

in

with

4.
Expansion of metrics at a smooth point

In this section, we study special coordinates around a smooth point of any smooth Riemannian manifold. We
do not claim
any result of this section to be new. Indeed, the first part of Theorem
4.3
below is well documented in any Riemannian geometry textbooks and the proof we used here is nothing but the one outlined by Bryant in
[
Bry14
]
with the algebraic details worked out. The second claim in Theorem
4.3
is rather routine in PDE. It shows that we can have a harmonic/Bianchi coordinate that is as good as the normal coordinate in the sense that we have the same expansion of the metric.

4.1.
Definitions and results

Given a smooth Riemannian manifold, around a fixed point,
the geodesic coordinate
is defined via exponential map and it has many advantages, in particular, the metric tensor has an expansion in this coordinate where the Riemannian curvature tensor appears as the coefficients of the second order term.

(4.1)

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}R_{ipqj}x_{p}x_{q}+\cdots.

Besides this, one can define a
harmonic coordinate
in a neighborhood, where the coordinates are harmonic functions with respect to the metric. The advantage of this harmonic coordinate is that in this coordinate, the leading term of Ricci curvature is nothing but the Laplacian of the metric tensor (see for example
[
DK81
]
)

R_{ij}=-\frac{1}{2}\triangle g_{ij}+\cdots

It is natural to ask if there is any coordinate that has both the features mentioned above.

There is also a notion of
Bianchi coordinate
, which is defined by

(4.2)

B_{g_{e}}g=0

where
g_{e}
is the flat metric defined by the coordinates and
B_{g}(h)=\delta(h-\frac{1}{2}({\rm Tr}_{g}h)g)
for any symmetric two-tensor
h
. This coordinate serves a similar purpose as harmonic coordinate in making the Ricci equation elliptic, while its definition allows global considerations. It is widely used in the study of deformation of Einstein metrics.
We have the following interpretation of the Bianchi coordinate

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that
(M,g)
is an
m
-dimensional Riemannian manifold and
\mathrm{x}\in M
,
U
is a neighborhood on which
\varphi:B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\to U
defines a coordinate system. Then it is Bianchi if and only if
\varphi
is a harmonic map from
B
(with the flat metric of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
) to
(U,g)
.

Proof.

If we write
\tilde{g}
for
\varphi^{*}(g)
as a metric on
B
, the Bianchi coordinate assumption means

(4.3)

-\partial_{j}\tilde{g}_{ij}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}(\sum_{j}\tilde{g}_{jj})=0.

Let
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}
be the Christoffel symbols of the Levi-Civita connection of
\tilde{g}
.
For the identity map to be a harmonic map from
(B,g_{e})
to
(B,g)
, we need

(4.4)

\triangle_{g_{e}}x^{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{jk}\frac{\partial x_{j}}{\partial x_{l}}\frac{\partial x_{k}}{\partial x_{l}}=0,

which is equivalent to

\Gamma^{i}_{jj}=0.

By the definition of
\Gamma
,

\Gamma^{i}_{jj}=g^{il}\left(\frac{\partial g_{jl}}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial g_{jj}}{\partial x_{l}}\right).

Since
g^{il}
is invertible, we find that (
4.4
) is equivalent to (
4.3
).
∎

Remark 4.2
.

There is another way of looking at the above lemma.
Since the map
\varphi
is a diffeomorphism, then a variation of
\varphi
is equivalent (by the invariance of Dirichlet energy under the action of diffeomorphism group) to a variation from the domain. Hence, in the terminology of harmonic maps, for such maps, being harmonic is equivalent to being stationary harmonic. The divergence free property of the Stress-energy tensor in this case is exactly the same as the Bianchi condition (
4.2
).

The main result of this section is

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
(M,g)
be any Riemannian manifold of dimension
m
and
\mathrm{x}\in M
be any fixed point. Then there is a coordinate around
\mathrm{x}
such that

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}R_{ipqj}x_{p}x_{q}+o(\left|x\right|^{2}),

where
R_{ipqj}
is the Riemannian curvature tensor at
\mathrm{x}
.
Moreover, if
Ric(\mathrm{x})=0
, the above coordinate can be chosen so that it is harmonic or Bianchi.

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
4.3

For a vector-valued function
u:B(0,\varepsilon)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, consider the equation

(4.5)

\triangle u=F(x,u,\nabla u)

where
F
is a smooth function in its arguments. We are interested in the “local solvability”, which is the topic of Section 14.3 of
[
Tay97
]
. Note that the author discussed more general elliptic equation of order
m
.

The
k=0
case of the following result is exactly Proposition 3.3 (for our equation) in
[
Tay97
]
, which was presented as a “refinement” of the local solvability theorem (Theorem 3.1 therein). As an interesting historic note, the author remarked after Proposition 3.3: “The desirability of having this refinement was pointed out to the author by R. Bryant.”

Lemma 4.4
.

If
u_{0}
is a smooth function on
B(0,\varepsilon)
that satisfies (
4.5
) at
0
up to order
k
, namely,

\left(\triangle u_{0}-F(x,u_{0},\nabla u_{0})\right)^{(l)}(0)=0

for
l=0,1,\cdots,k
. Then there is a solution
u
to (
4.5
) defined on some smaller neighborhood
B(0,\delta)
such that

(u-u_{0})^{(l)}(0)=0

for any
l=0,1,\cdots,k+2
.

The generalization from
k=0
to general
k
is routine. Since the initial approximate solution is better, the formal solution in Lemma 3.2 could be chosen better. Namely,
m+1
in (3.13) could be improved to
m+1+k
. The rest of the argument could be modified accordingly. We refer the details of this proof to Section 14.3 of
[
Tay97
]
. We also note that a similar version of the weighted function space and an application of the Schauder fixed point theorem as in Section
3
could also provide a proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Now, let’s start the proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Find any local coordinate around
\mathrm{x}\in M
. Since the metric is smooth, there is the Taylor’s expansion. By making a rotation, we may assume that

g_{ij}(x)=\delta_{ij}+O(\left|x\right|).

By doing so, we have simplified the constant term in the expansion. The next lemma deals with the linear term.

Lemma 4.5
.

There is another smooth coordinate centered at
\mathrm{x}
such that

(4.6)

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+O(\left|x\right|^{2}).

Proof.

In the original coorindate,

(4.7)

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+A_{ijk}x_{k}+O(\left|x\right|^{2})

where
A_{ijk}=A_{jik}
.
By Proposition
2.3
, we can find
B_{ijk}
satisfying

(4.8)

A_{abk}+2B_{kab}+2B_{kba}=0,\qquad\text{}\quad\forall k,a,b=1,\cdots,m.

Consider a coordinate change

\varphi(x)_{k}=x_{k}+B_{ijk}x_{i}x_{j}.

The pullback metric is

(\varphi^{*}g)_{ab}=\left(\delta_{ij}+A_{ijk}\varphi_{k}\right)\left(\delta_{ia}+2B_{kai}x_{k}\right)\left(\delta_{jb}+2B_{kbj}x_{k}\right)+O(\left|x\right|^{2}).

Thanks to (
4.8
), we obtain

(\varphi^{*}g)_{ab}=\delta_{ab}+O(\left|x\right|^{2}).

∎

The next lemma deals with the second order terms in the expansion of
g_{ij}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

There is another smooth coordinate centered at
\mathrm{x}
such that

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}R_{ipqj}x_{p}x_{q}+O(\left|x\right|^{3})

where
R_{ipqj}
is the Riemann curvature tensor at
x=0
.

Proof.

Since
g
is smooth and satisfies (
4.6
), we have an expansion

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+A_{ijkl}x_{k}x_{l}+O(\left|x\right|^{3}).

Here
A_{ijkl}
satisfies
A_{ijkl}=A_{jikl}=A_{ijlk}
.

By Proposition
2.4
, there are
\tilde{R}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes S^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that

A=\tilde{R}+\Psi_{2}(B).

Consider a coordinate change

\varphi(x)_{i}=x_{i}+B_{ijkl}x_{j}x_{k}x_{l}.

The pullback metric is

\displaystyle(\varphi^{*}g)_{pq}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial x_{p}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial x_{q}}\left(\delta_{ij}+A_{ijkl}\varphi_{k}\varphi_{l}+O(\left|\varphi\right|^{3})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{pq}+3B_{qpbc}x_{b}x_{c}+3B_{pqbc}x_{b}x_{c}+A_{pqbc}x_{b}x_{c}+O(\left|x\right|^{3}).

By the definition of
\Psi_{2}
in Section
2.2
,

(\varphi^{*}g)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\tilde{R}_{ijpq}x_{p}x_{q}+O(\left|x\right|^{3}).

By the isomorphism between
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathcal{C}
, we have
R\in\mathcal{C}
such that

(\varphi^{*}g)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{6}(R_{ipqj}+R_{iqpj})x_{p}x_{q}+O(\left|x\right|^{3}).

Given this expansion, we verify by direct computation that
R
is the curvature tensor of
\varphi^{*}g
at
x=0
.
∎

This proves the first claim in Theorem
4.3
.

For the second part, take the coordinate given by Lemma
4.6
and we plan to solve

(4.9)

\triangle u_{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(x+u(x))\left(\delta_{ja}+\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{a}}\right)\left(\delta_{ka}+\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial x_{a}}\right)=0

for
u
in a neighborhood of
0
such that

(4.10)

u=\nabla u=\nabla^{2}u=\nabla^{3}u=0.

(
4.9
) implies that
\varphi(x)=x+u(x)
is a harmonic map, i.e. the coordinate given by
\varphi
is a Bianchi coordinate and it follows from (
4.10
) that the expansion in this new Bianchi coordinate remain the same.

To solve (
4.9
), we use Lemma
4.4
with
u_{0}=0
. Therefore, it remains to check that

\Gamma^{i}_{aa}(0)=0;\quad\partial_{l}\Gamma^{i}_{aa}(0)=0.

The first equation is trivial because there is no linear term in the expansion. For the second term, we compute

\displaystyle\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3}\left(R_{jkbi}+R_{jbki}+R_{kjbi}+R_{kbji}-R_{jibk}-R_{jbik}\right)x_{b}+\cdots

Hence,

\partial_{l}\Gamma^{i}_{aa}(0)=\frac{2}{3}(Ric)_{li}(0)=0,

where we have used the assumption
Ric(\mathrm{x})=0
.

This finishes the proof of Theorem
4.3
in the Bianchi coordinate case. For the harmonic map case, it suffices to replace (
4.9
) with

\triangle u_{i}-\Gamma^{j}_{aa}(x)\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}=0.

The rest of the proof is the same.

5.
Coordinate at infinity of asymptotically flat Einstein metric

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.5
. We start by recalling the famous result of Bando, Kasue and Nakajima
[
BKN89
]
. Let
(M,g)
be as in Theorem
1.2
. It was proved in
[
BKN89
]
that there is a compact set
K
and a coordinate defined on the universal cover
\Omega\setminus K
in which the metric satisfies

\left|g_{ij}(x)-\delta_{ij}\right|\leq C\frac{1}{r^{m-1-\varepsilon}}\qquad\text{on}\quad\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{R}.

Remark 5.1
.

Throughout this section, we assume that
\Omega\setminus K
is simply connected for simplicity. In fact, we work on
\Omega_{R}
and in case the fundamental group
\Gamma
is not trivial, we should insist that every function/tensor be
\Gamma
invariant.

Moreover, the coordinate function
x_{i}
is harmonic, i.e.

\triangle_{g}x_{i}=0.

Therefore, the Ricci-flatness implies

(5.1)

\triangle g=Q(g,\partial g)

where
Q
is quadratic in
\partial g
.

Due to (
5.1
) and the elliptic estimate, we know for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left|\partial^{(k)}g_{ij}(x)\right|\leq C(k)\frac{1}{\left|x\right|^{m+k-1-\varepsilon}}.

By Definition
1.1
, we may write

(5.2)

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+O(\left|x\right|^{-(m-1-\varepsilon)}).

Hence, the right hand side of (
5.1
) is in
O(\left|x\right|^{-2(m-\varepsilon)})
. By Theorem
3.5
, we know the existence of a matrix of harmonic functions
h_{ij}
such that

g_{ij}=h_{ij}+O(\left|x\right|^{-2(m-\varepsilon)+2}).

As harmonic functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{R}
,
h_{ij}
has a well-known expansion. By comparing with (
5.2
), we obtain a set of coefficients
A_{ijk}
(with
A_{ijk}=A_{jik}
) such that

(5.3)

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+A_{ijk}\frac{x_{k}}{\left|x\right|^{m}}+O(\left|x\right|^{-m}).

As discussed in the introduction, when the metric
g
admits some nontrivial isometric action of a finite group
\Gamma
, it is known that
A_{ijk}
must vanish. Here we do not assume this additional symmetry. Instead of proving the vanishing of
A_{ijk}
directly, we show the existence
another
coordinate in which the metric expansion has vanishing
m-1
order term.

5.1.
A coordinate with vanishing
m-1
order term

Using notations given above, we prove

Proposition 5.2
.

There is a Bianchi coordinate, denoted by
\tilde{x}
, defined on
\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{R^{\prime}}
(for some
R^{\prime}>R
) such that the metric has an expansion

\tilde{g}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+O(\left|\tilde{x}\right|^{-m+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{for small}\quad\varepsilon>0,

where
\tilde{g}_{ij}
is the metric matrix of
g
in coordinate
\tilde{x}
.

Proof.

Since the coordinate functions
x_{i}
’s that we use in (
5.3
) are harmonic, the coefficients
A_{ijk}
satisfy some additional requirements. More precisely, the harmonic function equation
\triangle_{g}x_{i}=0
implies that

g^{kl}\Gamma^{i}_{kl}(x)=0.

Plugging (
5.3
) into the above equation and ignoring all terms that decay faster than
\left|x\right|^{-m}
, we obtain

2\partial_{x_{a}}\left(A_{iab}\frac{x_{b}}{\left|x\right|^{m}}\right)-\partial_{x_{i}}\left(A_{aab}\frac{x_{b}}{\left|x\right|^{m}}\right)=0,

which is equivalent to

(2A_{iaa}\delta_{bc}-2mA_{ibc}-A_{aai}\delta_{bc}+mA_{aab}\delta_{ic})x_{b}x_{c}=0.

By the definition of
{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
in Section
2.2
, the above equation becomes

{\mathbf{B}}_{1}(A)=0.

Lemma
2.6
implies the existence of
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\Psi_{3}(B)=-A
, i.e.

A_{ijk}=(m-2)(\delta_{ik}B_{j}+\delta_{jk}B_{i}).

With this
B
, we consider a coordinate change
\varphi
given by

(\varphi(x))_{i}=x_{i}+\frac{B_{i}}{\left|x\right|^{m-2}}.

Note that when
\left|x\right|
is large,
\varphi
is a diffeomorphism. The pullback metric
\varphi^{*}(g)
has an expansion

(5.4)

\begin{split}(\varphi^{*}g)_{pq}&=g_{ij}(\varphi(x))\left(\delta_{ip}-(m-2)\frac{B_{i}x_{p}}{\left|x\right|^{m}}\right)\left(\delta_{jq}-(m-2)\frac{B_{j}x_{q}}{\left|x\right|^{m}}\right)\\
&=\delta_{pq}+\left|x\right|^{-m}\left(A_{pqk}x_{k}-(m-2)B_{q}x_{p}-(m-2)B_{p}x_{q}\right)+O(\left|x\right|^{-m})\\
&=\delta_{pq}+O(\left|x\right|^{-m}).\end{split}

To finish the proof of Proposition
5.2
, we need to apply Theorem
3.1
. Using the new coordinate given by
\varphi
(still denoted by
x
), the metric
g
satisfies

(5.5)

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+O(\left|x\right|^{-m}).

Hence,

\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(x)=O(\left|x\right|^{-(m+1)}).

It then follows that the identity map
\varphi(x)=x
satisfies approximately the harmonic map equation in the sense that

(5.6)

\triangle\varphi_{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(\varphi)\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial x_{a}}\frac{\partial\varphi_{k}}{\partial x_{a}}=O(\left|x\right|^{-(m+1)}).

Theorem
3.1
then implies that there is a solution
\tilde{\varphi}(x)
to the above equation such that

\tilde{\varphi}(x)=x+O(\left|x\right|^{-(m-1-\varepsilon)}).

Using a computation similar to (
5.4
) again, (
5.5
) remains true in the coordinate given by
\tilde{\varphi}
. The new coordinate now is Bianchi.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

There is a similar version of Proposition
5.2
that gives a harmonic coordinate instead of a Bianchi one. Here we give an indication of how to modify the proof. The proof before (
5.5
) remains intact. We observe that

\begin{split}dV_{g}=&\sqrt{{\rm det}g}dx\\
=&\left(1+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(W_{ijkl}\frac{x_{j}x_{k}}{\left|x\right|^{m+2}})+O(\left|x\right|^{-(m+1)})\right)dV_{g_{e}}\\
=&(1+O(\left|x\right|^{-(m+1)}))dV_{g_{e}}.\end{split}

Hence, for any
s>r>0
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B_{s}\setminus B_{r}}dV_{g}-\int_{\tilde{B}_{s}\setminus\tilde{B}_{r}}dV_{g_{e}}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\int_{\tilde{B}_{s}\setminus\tilde{B}_{r}}O(\left|x\right|^{-(m+1)})dx}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left|\int_{r}^{s}\frac{1}{t^{2}}dt\right|.

The proof of the claim is done.

To justify the definition, we need to show that if
y
is another coordinate at the infinity satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
, then

(6.3)

\lim_{r\to\infty}V_{g}(B_{r}^{x})-V_{g_{e}}(\tilde{B}_{r}^{x})=\lim_{r\to\infty}V_{g}(B_{r}^{y})-V_{g_{e}}(\tilde{B}_{r}^{y}).

By Corollary
5.9
, we have an orthogonal matrix
A
and a translation vector
c
such that

y=Ax+c+e_{\alpha_{1}+2,m-\varepsilon}

for some
e_{\alpha_{1}+2,m-\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{X}_{\alpha_{1}+2,m-\varepsilon}
. A rotation does not change either
B_{r}
or
\tilde{B}_{r}
, hence we may assume that
A
is the identity matrix. In order to show that the definition of the renormalized volume is the same for coordinate
x
and coordinate
y
, it suffices to show

(6.4)

\lim_{r\to\infty}V_{g}(B_{r}^{x})-V_{g}(B_{r}^{y})=0.

Define
\tilde{y}=y-c
. We first claim that

\lim_{r\to\infty}V_{g}(B_{r}^{x})-V_{g}(B_{r}^{\tilde{y}})=0.

In fact, by definition of
\tilde{y}
, we have

\left|x\right|-C\left|x\right|^{-(m-\varepsilon)}\leq\left|\tilde{y}\right|\leq\left|x\right|+C\left|x\right|^{-(m-\varepsilon)}

when
\left|x\right|
is sufficiently large. It follows that

B^{x}_{r-Cr^{-(m-\varepsilon)}}\subset B^{\tilde{y}}_{r}\subset B^{x}_{r+Cr^{-(m-\varepsilon)}},

which implies that

\lim_{r\to\infty}V_{g}(B_{r}^{x})-V_{g}(B_{r}^{\tilde{y}})=0.

Hence, the claim is proved. The proof of (
6.4
) is reduced to

\lim_{r\to\infty}V_{g}(B_{r}^{\tilde{y}})-V_{g}(B_{r}^{y})=0,

or equivalently,

(6.5)

\lim_{r\to\infty}V_{g}(B_{r}^{\tilde{y}}\setminus B_{r}^{y})-V_{g}(B_{r}^{y}\setminus B_{r}^{\tilde{y}})=0.

Given that
\tilde{y}=y-c
, for sufficiently large
r
, if
g_{e}
is the Euclidean metric determined by
y
(or
\tilde{y}
) outside a compact set, then

V_{g_{e}}(B_{r}^{\tilde{y}}\setminus B_{r}^{y})+V_{g_{e}}(B_{r}^{y}\setminus B_{r}^{\tilde{y}})\leq Cr^{m-1}

and

V_{g_{e}}(B_{r}^{\tilde{y}}\setminus B_{r}^{y})-V_{g_{e}}(B_{r}^{y}\setminus B_{r}^{\tilde{y}})=0.

Given these elementary facts, (
6.5
) follows from (
6.2
). This concludes the proof of the first claim in Theorem
1.7
.

6.2.
Non-positivity of the renormalized volume

The aim of this subsection is prove the remaining part of Theorem
1.7
. The proof uses a result of
[
Ros87
]
(see Remark 3.1 of
[
BH23
]
). For
B_{r}
defined above, let
H_{g}
be the mean curvature of
\partial B_{r}
and
dA_{g}
be the volume form of
\partial B_{r}
with respect to the metric
g
. Since the Ricci curvature of
g
is non-negative, Theorem 1 in
[
Ros87
]
implies

\int_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{1}{H_{g}}dA_{g}\geq mV_{g}(B_{r}).

Let
H_{e}
be the mean curvature of
\partial\tilde{B}_{r}
with respect to the flat metric
g_{e}
, which is just
1/r
. Let
dA_{e}
be the standard volume form on
\partial\tilde{B}_{r}
. Then we have trivially

\int_{\partial\tilde{B}_{r}}\frac{1}{H_{e}}dA_{e}=mV_{e}(\tilde{B}_{r})

To show that
\mathcal{V}\leq 0
, it suffices to show

(6.6)

\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{\partial\tilde{B}_{r}}\frac{1}{H_{e}}dA_{e}-\int_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{1}{H_{g}}dA_{g}=0.

To see this, we need to study the asymptotics of
\int_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{1}{H_{g}}dA_{g}
up to high order. Via the coordinate map, we identify
\partial B_{r}
with
\partial\tilde{B}_{r}
. They are the same hypersurface. However, the unit normal, the volume form and the mean curvature depend on the ambient metric. In order to compare these quantities, we start with
\nabla_{g}r
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{g}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(g^{ij}\partial_{i}r)\partial_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\delta_{ij}-W_{iabj}\frac{x_{a}x_{b}}{r^{m+2}}+O(r^{-(m+1)}))\frac{x_{i}}{r}\partial_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{g_{e}}r+O(r^{-(m+1)}).

Here in the last line above, we have used the first Bianchi identity of
W
.
Taking the squared norm, we have

\displaystyle\left|\nabla_{g}r\right|_{g}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{ij}(\frac{x_{i}}{r})(\frac{x_{j}}{r})+O(r^{-(m+1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+O(r^{-(m+1)}).

Denote the unit normal vector of
\partial B_{r}
(with respect to
g
) by
n_{g}
and that of
\partial\tilde{B}_{r}
(with respect to
g_{e}
) by
n_{g_{e}}
. Since
\nabla_{g}r
is perpendicular to
\partial B_{r}
, we have

(6.7)

n_{g}=\frac{\nabla_{g}r}{\left|\nabla_{g}r\right|}=n_{g_{e}}+O(r^{-(m+1)}).

Recall that we have proved
dV_{g}=(1+O(r^{-(m+1)}))dV_{g_{e}}
.
Using the formula

dA_{g}=(\iota_{n_{g}}dV_{g})|_{\partial B_{r}};\quad dA_{g_{e}}=(\iota_{n_{g_{e}}}dV_{g_{e}})|_{\partial\tilde{B}_{r}},

we obtain

(6.8)

dA_{g}=(1+O(r^{-(m+1)}))dA_{g_{e}}.

The vector field
X=x_{i}\partial_{i}=rn_{g_{e}}
generates a one-parameter family of diffeomorphism
\varphi_{t}(x)=e^{t}x
. For any subdomain
\Sigma
of
\partial B_{r}
, the first variation of area gives

\int_{\Sigma}(m-1)\vec{H}_{g}\cdot XdA_{g}=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}\int_{e^{t}\cdot\Sigma}dA_{g}.

Here
\vec{H}_{g}=H_{g}n_{g}
is the mean curvature vector. Note that for any function
w\in O(r^{-(m+1)})
, by the definition of
O
, we have

\frac{d}{dt}w=X(w)=r\partial_{r}w\in O(r^{-(m+1)}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}(m-1)rH_{g}(n_{g}\cdot n_{g_{e}})dA_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}\int_{e^{t}\cdot\Sigma}(1+O(r^{-(m+1)}))dA_{g_{e}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}(m-1)+O(r^{-(m+1)})dA_{g_{e}}.

For the left hand side, by (
6.7
) and (
6.8
), we obtain

\int_{\Sigma}rH_{g}dA_{g_{e}}=\int_{\Sigma}1+O(r^{-(m+1)})dA_{g_{e}}.

By the arbitrariness of
\Sigma
, we have

H_{g}=\frac{1}{r}+O(r^{-(m+2)})=H_{g_{e}}+O(r^{-(m+2)})

and therefore

(6.9)

\frac{1}{H_{g}}=\frac{1}{H_{g_{e}}}+O(r^{-m})=\frac{1}{H_{g_{e}}}(1+O(r^{-(m+1)})).

It is obvious that (
6.6
) follows from (
6.8
) and (
6.9
).

In case the equality of (
6.6
) holds, the proof above implies that

(6.10)

\lim_{r\to\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{1}{H_{g}}dA_{g}-mV_{g}(B_{r})\right)=0.

We need to prove that
M
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{m}/\Gamma
. The following proof is adapted from the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
Ros87
]
. There was a solution
u_{r}
satisfying

\begin{cases}\triangle_{g}u_{r}=1&\text{in}\quad B_{r}\\
u_{r}|_{\partial B_{r}}=0.&\end{cases}

Claim 1.
Setting

E_{r}=\frac{1}{m}(\triangle_{g}u_{r})^{2}-\left|\nabla_{g}^{2}u_{r}\right|^{2}\leq 0,

we claim that

\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{B_{r}}E_{r}dV_{g}=0.

Claim 2.
Setting

\tilde{u}_{r}=u_{r}+\frac{1}{2m}r^{2},

we claim that
\tilde{u}_{r}
converges locally smoothly to a limit
\tilde{u}_{\infty}
that is defined on
M
and that the traceless Hessian of
\tilde{u}_{\infty}
vanishes everywhere.

Assume the claims for now. By definition,
E_{r}
remains the same with
u_{r}
replaced by
\tilde{u}_{r}
. Taking the limit, we obtain

\frac{1}{m}(\triangle_{g}\tilde{u}_{\infty})^{2}-\left|\nabla_{g}^{2}\tilde{u}_{\infty}\right|^{2}=0\qquad\text{on}\quad M.

Moreover, by the equation of
u_{r}
, we have

\triangle_{g}\tilde{u}_{\infty}=1.

In summary, if
X=\nabla_{g}\tilde{u}_{\infty}
, we have just proved that

\mathcal{L}_{X}g=\frac{2}{m}g.

Namely,
X
is a conformal Killing vector field. Then
(M,g)
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{m}/\Gamma
.

Proof of Claim 1.

Using the Reilly’s formula

\int_{B_{r}}(\triangle_{g}u_{r})^{2}-\left|\nabla_{g}^{2}u_{r}\right|^{2}dV_{g}=\int_{\partial B_{r}}(m-1)H_{g}\left|\partial_{n_{g}}u_{r}\right|^{2}dA_{g},

we have

\frac{m-1}{m}V_{g}(B_{r})+\int_{B_{r}}E_{r}dV_{g}=(m-1)\int_{\partial B_{r}}H_{g}\left|\partial_{n_{g}}u_{r}\right|^{2}dA_{g}.

Following
[
Ros87
]
,

\displaystyle V_{g}(B_{r})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\partial B_{r}}\partial_{n_{g}}u_{r}dA_{g}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{r}}H_{g}\left|\partial_{n_{g}}u_{r}\right|^{2}dA_{g}\cdot\int_{\partial B_{r}}\frac{1}{H_{g}}dA_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{m}V_{g}(B_{r})+\frac{1}{m-1}\int_{B_{r}}E_{r}dV_{g}\right)\left(mV_{g}(B_{r})+o(1)\right).

Here in the last line above, we used (
6.10
).
Claim 1 follows from the above inequality and the fact that
E_{r}\leq 0
.
∎

Proof of Claim 2.

While the coordinate is only defined at the infinity, we can extend the domain of the function
\frac{1}{2m}\left|x\right|^{2}
smoothly inside
M
. Denote the new function by
u_{a}
. Given the expansion of the metric at the infinity, we may compute

(6.11)

\triangle_{g}u_{a}=1+O(\left|x\right|^{-(m+\varepsilon)}).

Hence, for any
r
sufficiently large, we have

(6.12)

\begin{cases}\triangle_{g}(\tilde{u}_{r}-u_{a})=1-\triangle_{g}u_{a}=O(\left|x\right|^{-(m+\varepsilon)})&\text{in}\quad B_{r}\\
(\tilde{u}_{r}-u_{a})=0&\text{on}\quad\partial B_{r}.\end{cases}

Multiplying both sides by
\tilde{u}_{r}-u_{a}
and integrating by parts, we get

\int_{B_{r}}\left|\nabla_{g}(\tilde{u}_{r}-u_{a})\right|^{2}dV_{g}=\int_{B_{r}}(\triangle_{g}u_{a}-1)(\tilde{u}_{r}-u_{a})dV_{g}.

Since we have uniform Sobolev inequality for
B_{r}
(due to the Ricci lower bound and non-collapsing volume), we use the Hölder inequality to get

\left\|\tilde{u}_{r}-u_{a}\right\|_{L^{\frac{2m}{m+2}}(B_{r})}\leq C\left\|\triangle_{g}u_{a}-1\right\|_{L^{\frac{2m}{m-2}}(B_{r})}\leq C.

Here in the last inequality above, we have used (
6.11
). Note that the above estimate holds uniformly and the constant
C
is independent of
r
. Together with (
6.12
), we obtain uniform higher order estimate on any compact set for
\tilde{u}_{r}
so that Claim 2 holds.
∎

6.3.
Compatible with the known definition in dimension four

Finally, when
m=4
, we show that the renormalized volume defined here is the same as the one defined in
[
BH23
]
. In fact, it suffices to show the CMC coordinate that was used to define the renormalized volume there satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
.

In Theorem B of
[
BH23
]
, there is a coordinate in which

g=g_{e}+h_{0}+O(\left|x\right|^{-5})

where
h_{0}
is some symmetric two-tensor defined on
\mathbb{R}^{4}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
satisfying

(6.13)

\mathcal{L}_{r\partial_{r}}h_{0}=-2h_{0},\partial_{r}\llcorner h_{0}=0,{\mathrm{t}r}_{g_{e}}h_{0}=0,{\rm div}_{g_{e}}h_{0}=0,\triangle_{g_{e}}h_{0}=0.

In Proposition 2.6 therein, the dimension of all possible
h_{0}
satisfying the above conditions was proved to be
10
. It is well known that the dimension of the space of Weyl tensors (
m=4
) is also
10
. Moreover, it is easy to verify directly that if

h_{0}=W_{iabj}\frac{x_{a}x_{b}}{\left|x\right|^{6}}

for some
W\in\mathcal{W}
, then (
6.13
) holds. Therefore, the CMC coordinate satisfies the assumption of Theorem
1.5
and hence defines the same renormalized volume.
