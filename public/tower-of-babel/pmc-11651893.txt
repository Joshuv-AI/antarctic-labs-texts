Weak-form inference for hybrid dynamical systems in ecology
Authors: Daniel Messenger, Greg Dwyer, Vanja Dukic
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpecies subject to predation and environmental threats commonly exhibit variable periods of population boom and bust over long timescales. Understanding and predicting such behaviour, especially given the inherent heterogeneity and stochasticity of exogenous driving factors over short timescales, is an ongoing challenge. A modelling paradigm gaining popularity in the ecological sciences for such multi-scale effects is to couple short-term continuous dynamics to long-term discrete updates. We develop a data-driven method utilizing weak-form equation learning to extract such hybrid governing equations for population dynamics and to estimate the requisite parameters using sparse intermittent measurements of the discrete and continuous variables. The method produces a set of short-term continuous dynamical system equations parametrized by long-term variables, and long-term discrete equations parametrized by short-term variables, allowing direct assessment of interdependencies between the two timescales. We demonstrate the utility of the method on a variety of ecological scenarios and provide extensive tests using models previously derived for epizootics experienced by the North American spongy moth (Lymantria dispar dispar).Keywords: multi-scale model, hybrid systems, data-driven modelling, system identification, parameter estimation, WSINDy

Introduction

The ability to estimate model parameters and select appropriate models based on data is of central interest in ecological applications but is often complicated by the presence of irregularly observed sparse observations [1]. Fortunately, the widespread availability of high-performance computing and the development of sophisticated nonlinear optimization algorithms have made it possible to fit a vast range of nonlinear ecological models to data [2]. Many applications of model-fitting have focused on human diseases, for which an extensive knowledge base makes it possible to write down at least basic models using only information from the literature [3]. A very large set of ecological problems, however, instead involve natural systems about which so little is known that there is often a great deal of uncertainty about the model structure [1].

In such cases, the standard approach is to write down a set of competing models, each comprising what is believed to be a reasonable model structure, to fit each model separately to the data and then to use statistical model selection to choose between the models [4–6]. Because in such cases, the models in question are typically based on well-studied ecological models from the literature, the use of model selection results in a model that has a well-understood connection to the biology of the system being studied. Model selection can then offer important insights into the biology of the system [7].

A very important disadvantage of the application of model selection to ecological problems, however, is that it typically requires the implementation of complex nonlinear fitting routines in high-performance computing environments, and this implementation must be repeated for each model under consideration [8]. Model selection in ecology therefore faces daunting computational obstacles when even moderate numbers of models are considered, which is probably why it has played only a modest role in the development of the field.

Here, we therefore propose a novel alternative approach based on weak-form equation learning [9–11] to accomplish the joint parameter estimation and model selection task, which we will jointly refer to throughout as ‘model identification’ in this work. By simultaneously comparing a very large number of models to data at once, our model identification algorithm is able to carry out model selection far more rapidly than the standard information-criterion model selection process. Moreover, our algorithm produces models that can very accurately reproduce data.

The models that we consider consist of host–pathogen models that allow for seasonality. Although classical host–pathogen models typically neglect seasonality [12], host–pathogen interactions in nature are often very strongly affected by seasonality [13]. Moreover, standard models typically incorporate seasonality in the form of sinusoidal variation in a model parameter, often host reproduction. But this model structure is deeply unrealistic for most host–pathogen systems.

For most host–pathogen systems, there is instead typically a period during which pathogen transmission exhausts or nearly exhausts the supply of available hosts. Transmission then falls to zero or nearly so until host reproduction replenishes the supply of susceptible hosts. As briefly summarized by Andreasen & Dwyer [14], this pattern occurs in host–pathogen systems for which hosts are all classes of vertebrates, most if not all terrestrial phyla of invertebrates, and many different plants, while the pathogens include viruses, bacteria and fungi. Our algorithm is customized to adapt to this specific structure by carrying out model identification using a combination of short-term disease dynamics and long-term population fluctuations.

We consider, in particular, the case of baculovirus pathogens of forest insects, which often drive long-period, large-amplitude cycles in the population densities of their insect hosts [15]. Because only larvae are susceptible to baculoviruses, virus epizootics (epidemics in animals) only occur during the larval season. Larvae that survive to adulthood reproduce, while the virus overwinters on the insect’s eggs. The epizootic is then restarted in the following spring when some larvae become infected as they hatch. Because many outbreaking host insects have only one generation per year [16], and because the larval period typically lasts for only two months [17], our models consist of continuous-time epizootic models that are nested inside discrete-generation host-reproduction/virus-overwintering models.

These multi-scale hybrid models of insect-pathogens were introduced in the ecological literature by Briggs & Godfray [18] and have been extended to include evolutionary change [17,19–21], variation in the foliage quality of insect host trees [22] and weather effects [7]. Hybrid models have been extensively applied to host–pathogen dynamics more generally [23–25] and to consumer–resource theory [26,27], sometimes drawing fundamentally different ecological implications compared with purely discrete or purely continuous models.

Inference and learning in dynamical systems

When the model system equations are of known form, the unknown system parameters and initial conditions are usually inferred from the data using statistical methods. However, when the model itself is unknown (in addition to having unknown parameters), the problem of statistical inference becomes much more difficult. We discuss both situations next.

Parameter Inference

In spite of the availability of sophisticated optimization algorithms and high-performance computing, parameter estimation and inference (and inverse problems in general) in nonlinear dynamical systems remain a challenging problem [28]. Many of the existing parameter estimation methods in practice, such as nonlinear least squares and likelihood-based methods, still use optimization (cost) functions which are based on comparisons of data to the solution of the system. This results in iterative schemes that use a forward-solver-based optimization routine to minimize the nonlinear least squares residual at every iteration. Furthermore, the likelihood functions commonly resulting from nonlinear differential equations often exhibit multiple modes and ridges, with different combinations of parameter values yielding similar state values. In most scenarios, converging to globally optimal and unique parameter values can be challenging, requiring long run times and careful diagnostic monitoring [29–31].

Several alternative approaches have therefore been put forward in an attempt to reduce or altogether avoid using forward-based solvers, with varying levels of success: via likelihood-free sequential Monte Carlo methods [32], local approximations [33] and manifold-constrained Gaussian processes [34]. Sometimes a purely statistical approach (such as spline and kernel regression methods in [35]) is used instead to model a smooth temporal pattern. With the exception of the Gaussian-process-based approach in [34], none of the above methods are guaranteed to solve the underlying dynamical system; our weak-form approach in contrast guarantees that. Moreover, the Gaussian-process approach in [34] requires Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling to do so and thus it takes notably longer to produce results than the weak-form algorithm presented here.

Model equation learning

As we described earlier, the standard approach to the model learning problems in ecology is to write down a small candidate set of models based on a general understanding of the organisms in question, and then to fit the parameters in each model as if that model was the true model. Statistical model selection is then performed using an information criterion (such as Akaike (AIC), Bayesian (BIC), Watanabe (WAIC), etc.) [6,7,17,36–39]. This process requires a great deal of knowledge of the biology and sizeable computational resources and is particularly challenging when the models under consideration have either high parameter dimensions or high-dimensional states, or both in the case of hierarchical and random differential equation models [22,39], or in the case of strong seasonality and multiple coupled models, like the discrete-continuous hybrid framework that we consider here.

Our approach is to instead write down very general expressions for the model structure, and then to use data to choose the actual model structure from a large library 𝕃 consisting of |𝕃| possible equation terms (where |𝕃| in our examples is allowed to reach 562 through adaptive expansion), resulting in 2|𝕃| possible candidate models for consideration. Such large model spaces cannot be explored in their entirety, but instead rely on guided methods for sequential exploration, such as sequential thresholding [40,41]. The equation learning methodology presented here thus results in the identification of interpretable forms of dominant mechanisms governing a hybrid discrete-continuous dynamical system for population dynamics, among combinations of hundreds of possible linear and nonlinear terms in the library, without the need to separately fit different models to the data.

Previous methods for model equation learning for hybrid dynamics have generally handled parameter inference only after the final form of the model has been chosen. Such methods include combined sparse identification and clustering [42,43], neural networks [44,45] and online learning [46]. Ecological hybrid modelling calls for new tools, particularly to address complex dependencies between the discrete and continuous modelling stages. In addition, in contrast to engineering applications that are the focus of [42], in many ecological settings, the switching times between continuous stages are seasonal and therefore known a priori. This implies that many existing methods may be unnecessarily complex, providing non-optimal or inefficient estimators for ecological hybrid models.

Here, we develop a novel data-driven method that performs weak-form ‘equation-learning’ methodology simultaneously with parameter inference [9–11], in the context of seasonal hybrid dynamical systems. Our method can be seen as a weak-form extension of the sparse hybrid system identification method [42] with statistically sound parameter estimation and special adaptations for seasonal hybrid systems, where each ‘generation’ has a fixed length, a common setting in ecology. A core technique in the inference of seasonal hybrid systems is our novel employment of the sparse parametric modelling technique in [47], whereby we alternate between interpreting the discrete variables as parameters in the continuous dynamics, and interpreting the (final-time) continuous variables as parameters in the discrete dynamics. This method is made particularly effective by incorporating parameter inference as in [9], which accurately models noise in the data and thereby enables quantification of parameter uncertainty.

Our weak-form method is highly efficient as it does not employ forward-solvers during model identification, requiring of the order of 30 s on a modern laptop to complete (§4.2 describes performance measures for hardware specifications). Efficiency is granted by (i) employing the WENDy algorithm for parameter estimation (weak-form estimation of nonlinear dynamics), which was demonstrated in [9] to be orders of magnitude faster than nonlinear least squares-based methods, and by (ii) selecting models using sparse regression with adaptive library expansion instead of an exhaustive search. The latter enables consideration of a large set of models (of the order of 2|𝕃|, although efficient elimination of inaccurate models leads to explicit testing on only a small fraction of this total). Moreover, compared with other sparse equation learning methods that employ the strong form, weak-form sparse regression methods as employed here have high noise tolerances (e.g. [10,11]), which demonstrate that the noise level may be increased by at least an order of magnitude without sacrificing speed or accuracy). The method we introduce here also has relatively low data requirements. A sparse set of intermittent observations is sufficient to identify a coupled system of short-term continuous dynamics and long-term discrete dynamics, each a generalization of the annual epidemics and overwintering effects common to seasonal host–pathogen systems. This enables a variety of inference tasks common to ecology to be performed efficiently, such as prediction of future boom and bust cycles. In fact, as we will show in §4, data on 18 non-consecutive generations can enable accurate prediction for upwards of 40 generations with our method. The sampling strategy, however, does impact performance, as noted in previous SINDy-type studies [10,48,49], and we confirm in §4.6 that peak sampling, the approach most commonly conducted in field studies,1 is significantly more efficient than random sampling.

Our method learns a parametrization of the continuous-time model by the discrete-time variables (and vice versa) using the weak-form model learning algorithm WSINDy (weak-form sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics [10]), an extension of the SINDy algorithm [40] and is therefore a novel approach to modelling hybrid systems [52,53]. In this way, our method can be directly applied to general hybrid systems with known event times (i.e. seasonality), as arise in a variety of disciplines including epidemiology [12], endocrinology [54], oncology [55] and even security for cyberinfrastructure [56]. Furthermore, learning a system of differential equations using WSINDy, jointly with the recently developed weak-form parameter inference method WENDy [9], constitutes a novel approach to model learning (see §3.1 for an overview of WSINDy and WENDy). In addition, both WSINDy and WENDy are novel to ecology applications. WENDy has been shown to lead to a substantial improvement over both traditional nonlinear least-squares optimization [9] and Bayesian methods [34,57]. The resulting improvements in our ability to identify the dominant mechanisms in both short- and long-term modelling components have the potential to improve our understanding of the underlying ecology, to produce better model-based predictions and to better inform future control strategies. The weak-form equation-learning methods presented here therefore have the capability to allow model identification and inference for a broad class of dynamical systems that express some form of seasonality, and that therefore lie outside of the current capabilities of equation learning.

In §4, we perform a series of numerical experiments to assess the performance of the method (§§4.4 and 4.6), and in addition, we provide a detailed roadmap of how to interpret, build upon and utilize the results of the algorithm in practice to make predictions and quantify uncertainty (§4.5). Motivation

In order to motivate the model class relevant to this paper, in §2.1, we review a simplified model for the dynamics of insect–baculovirus interactions, which will form the basis of our numerical experiments in §4. The general class of seasonal discrete-continuous models is presented in §2.2.

Seasonality in host–pathogen systems

Animal and plant host–pathogen systems commonly have a high degree of seasonality that depends on the timing of host reproduction and pathogen transmission (see [14] and references therein). In many animal hosts, notably invertebrates and amphibians, pathogen transmission can only occur during the larval stage, while in plants host transmission often only occurs during the growing season. Meanwhile, transmission in many bird and mammal hosts often ends because of a lack of susceptible hosts. In either type of host–pathogen interaction, long periods of disease transmission strongly reduce the susceptible host population, after which short periods of host reproduction replenish the susceptible host population. The ubiquitous effect of seasonality in ecological systems demands a robust modelling and inference framework, which we argue is met by hybrid discrete-continuous models and the inference method that we present here.

To introduce the hybrid discrete-continuous modelling framework, and to demonstrate its applicability to strongly seasonal ecological systems, we consider a model for insect–baculovirus dynamics [19]. As we described earlier, baculoviruses represent a large group of viral pathogens of arthropod hosts [58]; in many insects in particular, baculoviruses are transmitted when uninfected larvae that are feeding on the foliage of their host plant accidentally consume infectious occlusion bodies released from the cadavers of hosts killed by the virus [59]. Baculoviruses must thus kill their host to be transmitted [60]. For many species of forest insects, baculoviruses decimate outbreaking populations [50,61], and therefore appear to drive long-period, large-amplitude predator–prey type cycles in populations of their hosts [62]. The ability of mathematical host–pathogen models to reproduce these cycles provides support for this hypothesis [15,19].

Outbreaking forest insects typically have only one generation per year [16]. Because only larvae can become infected, and because host reproduction therefore occurs after each year’s viral epizootic is over, we first construct a continuous-time model of baculovirus dynamics during the larval period, using a modification of a standard susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR) model from theoretical epidemiology [12]. Seasonal discrete-continuous hybrid models: the general case

Consistent with the ecological literature, with a specific focus on insect-pathogen systems, we define the seasonal hybrid model class to be composed of three sub-models: (i) an initialization function mapping the discrete variables Xn at generation n to the continuous variables Yn(0) at time t = 0 of generation n, (ii) a continuous-time ODE model for Yn(t) parametrized by Xn, and (iii) a discrete-time dynamical system mapping Xn to Xn+1, parametrized by the final-time continuous-time variables Yn(T). Methods

The main result of this article is that the discrete-continuous seasonal dynamics (2.3)–(2.6) can be identified from data with moderate measurement noise using the sparse weak-form equation learning approach proposed in this section. The proposed method is an extension and merger of two existing nonlinear dynamics identification algorithms, WSINDy [10,11] and WENDy [9]. The method is suitable for incomplete and irregularly collected data, with gaps in observations sometimes stretching over several years, as commonly seen in ecology given the inherent difficulties of collecting field data.

In what follows, we provide an overview of weak-form inference in §3.1, including the WSINDy and WENDy algorithms [9–11]. Specific advancements of these methods for the current setting of inference for hybrid discrete-continuous models of the form (2.4)–(2.6) is included in §3.2. We refer readers to appendix A, which contains several notation tables with mathematical symbols used throughout.

Weak-form equation learning and parameter inference

The equations (2.3)–(2.6) are known as the strong form of the differential equations, a form that is characterized by specifying that the equation holds pointwise in time, for all t. In contrast, the weak form of a differential equation specifies that the equation holds when integrated in time with respect to an arbitrary function referred to as the test function. The crux of weak-form methods relies on choosing the right test functions to achieve a given objective. For example, when the objective is inference from noisy data, smoothness of the test functions will be an important point. We discuss this in detail in the next subsections.

Weak-form inference methods have origins in parameter estimation dating back to the 1950s (e.g. [65]). In some disciplines, this is known as the modulating function technique [66]. Weak-form equation learning, which combines parameter estimation with model selection, is a more recent pursuit, and together with works published by authors of the current article [10,11,67–69], has seen many new developments in recent years [47,70–77].

Another recent pursuit in the context of parameter estimation is to successively improve weak-form estimates by iteratively incorporating the action of the test function basis Φ into the covariance structure for the weak-form residual. This is known as the WENDy method, or weak-form estimation of nonlinear dynamics, and has been shown to be advantageous over forward solver-based parameter estimation (such as nonlinear least squares) in a variety of ODE models of biological relevance [9].

In the current work, we combine the WENDy and WSINDy methods for the first time, arriving at an efficient inference framework for simultaneously selecting model terms and estimating parameters. Below, we review the WENDy and WSINDy methods.

The choice of an appropriate trial function library 𝕃 is highly dependent on the system under consideration. Automatic selection of 𝕃 is an ongoing research area [78,79]. Polynomial libraries are often an excellent initial choice due to their computational efficiency, interpretability, expressivity (i.e. all sufficiently smooth functions can be approximated by polynomials) and ubiquity in models of natural phenomena. On the other hand, low data quality may lead to multi-collinearity between library terms (including polynomial terms), preventing reliable selection of terms. In this case, expert knowledge about the system at hand is necessary to curate the trial function library [49]. Motivated by their frequent appearance in models of population dynamics, we employ polynomial libraries in this work, together with an automated library incrementation step as outlined in the following text.

The WENDy algorithm [9] is a forward-solver-free method for estimating parameters in nonlinear dynamical systems for which the model form is known. The only requirement is that the systems are linear in parameters (as in (3.2)), agreeing with the WSINDy construction just described. That is, the sparsity aspect of WSINDy is removed. Weak-form sparse identification for seasonal discrete-continuous models

Our proposed approach to identify dynamical systems of the general seasonal hybrid form (2.4)–(2.6), which we will refer to as the WSINDy-Eco algorithm, is presented in algorithm 1. Given data of the form 𝐗=(Xn,Xn+1)n∈I and 𝐘=(Yn(𝐭n))n∈I for a finite set of generations I and time-grids (𝐭n)n∈I, WSINDy-Eco outputs weight vectors (𝐰^IC,𝐰^Y,𝐰^X) as well as libraries (𝕃IC,𝕃Y,𝕃X) to define the learned model, estimates (𝐂^IC,𝐂^Y,𝐂^X) for the covariances of the residuals for each sub-model, and parameter covariance estimates (𝐒^IC,𝐒^Y,𝐒^X). Crucially, the parameter covariances allow for uncertainty quantification in a variety of predictive scenarios, as described in §§4.3 and 4.5. Choices of hyperparameters for WSINDy-Eco are presented in appendix C.2. Note that superscripts {IC,Y,X} refer to the initial conditions map (2.4), the continuous (Y) dynamics (2.5) and the discrete (X) dynamics (2.6), respectively (see appendix A for a full list of notations used).

A main component of WSINDy-Eco is the application of MSTLS-WENDy to parametrized libraries, dubbed MSTLS-WENDy-Par, described below in §3.2.1 with pseudocode given in algorithm 3 in the appendix. MSTLS-WENDy-Par utilizes recently developed methods for identifying parametric dependencies in PDEs [47]. However, our approach employs parametrized libraries to learn dependencies between the discrete and continuous variables in each of the sub-models (2.4)–(2.6).

In order to learn the discrete dynamics (2.6), an estimate of the continuous variables at time T, Y^n(T), is required, provided Yn(T) is not directly observed (which is often the case and is assumed here). In WSINDy-Eco, this is handled by simulating the learned continuous dynamics given by (𝐰^Y,𝕃Y) using the learned initial conditions map given by (𝐰^IC,𝕃IC), to obtain state estimates 𝐘^T=(Y^n(T))n∈I. These estimates are then used in conjunction with the observed data (Yn(𝐭n))n∈I to inform a covariance estimate Σ^𝐘^T for use in the final MSTLS-WENDy-Par stage to approximate the discrete dynamics. This process is encompassed in the algorithm ForwardSim (algorithm 4). This is the only stage of WSINDy-Eco requiring forward solves (and is unique to the hybrid model framework); in particular, no forward solves are needed during model identification.

Numerical experiments

We now examine the performance of WSINDy-Eco applied to seasonal host–pathogen dynamics. To simplify exposition of the inference method, we restrict our examples to the epizootic models (2.1) and (2.2), which captures the key components of a seasonal hybrid model. An additional two-pathogen ecological example is included in appendix C, and guidelines for application to more general problems can be found in the code repository https://github.com/MathBioCU/wsindy_eco (e.g. a forced spiking neuron example utilizing the FitzHugh–Nagumo model).

Below we describe the data generation process, several performance metrics and methods of uncertainty quantification, before diving into results concerning long-term prediction (§4.4). We show that the method predicts accurately over many cycles of the continuous-time variables (despite requiring accurate updates and reinitializations in terms of the discrete variables at every generation n). We then describe a human-in-the-loop modelling framework that leverages the uncertainty information provided by the WENDy regression approach in §4.5. Finally, we evaluate the performance of the method under different sampling strategies in §4.6, revealing that peak sampling (data collection during outbreaks, as is customary in field studies), provides a clear advantage over random sampling in terms of predictive accuracy.

Uncertainty quantification

WSINDy-Eco outputs a distributional model with asymptotically multivariate normal parameters

Collectively, we will refer to the full model output as 𝐰∼N(𝐰^,𝐒^). The simplest uncertainty metrics we can readily quantify are the confidence intervals around each parameter, obtained from 𝖽𝗂𝖺𝗀(𝐒^). We will, however, also estimate the uncertainty in predictions using parametric bootstrap sampling: in §4.4, we quantify uncertainty in population peak times and amplitudes, and in §4.5, we present a human-in-the-loop modelling approach using various uncertainty quantification (UQ) metrics. In each case, we make 200 independent parameter draws 𝐰(ℓ)∼N(𝐰^,𝐒^), ℓ=1,…,200. Then, for each draw, the resulting model is constructed using the output libraries 𝕃IC,𝕃Y,𝕃X and simulated from the same observed initial conditions. At each generation n=0,…,80, we compute histograms for the discrete host densities {Nn(ℓ)}ℓ=1200 to visualize the growth of uncertainty over time (figures 4–6). Similarly, peaks P1(ℓ),P2(ℓ),… are computed for each draw ℓ=1,…,200, and prediction regions for each peak location and magnitude are found based on marginal sample order statistics, from 2.5th quantile to 97.5th quantile (figures 1 and 2).

Human-in-the-loop modelling

A desirable quality of equation learning combined with covariance information (as afforded by the current method) is that models can be vetted by experts, through direct examination of the learned model terms, their respective coefficients and their corresponding covariance structure and confidence intervals. For example, figure 4 displays confidence intervals for the model described in figure 2 and shows that in this case each confidence interval contains the true value of the parameter (green star). Note that, normally we would expect 95% of our confidence intervals to contain the true values over repeated sampling. When confidence intervals are large, however, and include 0, this allows for a discussion about whether those terms should be excluded from the final model, as long as multiple testing strategies are employed.

In practice, however, there is no ground truth model, and it is quite possible that the learned model could be improved upon by further inspection from a researcher with expertise in the ecological systems being studied. It is also possible that low data quality and high noise can lead to suboptimal sparse recovery (see §4.6). In particular, if the model goodness of fit is not diminished in any meaningful way with the removal of terms, then by Occam’s razor the simpler model should be used. In the current framework, this can be tested immediately. Overall, this process reveals a procedure by which expert knowledge can be used to modify equations and quickly assess hypotheses using the modified equations, as well as assess the uncertainty in the learned model and its predictions. We reiterate that the entire learning algorithm, including assessment and modification of the learned model as just described, occurs with no more than several minutes of computation time on a modern laptop.

Sampling strategies

Data collection (sampling) strategies are an important consideration in ecological applications, due in part to limited resources and challenges associated with field work. Specific questions may include when to sample, how many samples to collect, and during which years to collect samples. Several recent studies have sought to quantify the effect of sampling strategies on the performance of SINDy-type methods, concluding in some cases that sampling near fast dynamics is more informative than near slow dynamics [10,48], and that interpolation onto finer grids may be employed to overcome sparse measurements [49]. In this section we focus on realistic sampling strategies from the perspective of ecological field work. Namely, we discuss quantitative performance results from two different collection strategies, random sampling and peak sampling. The random sampling strategy involves sampling the generation set I uniformly at random from the first 40 generations, with |I|∈{12,16,20}. Peak sampling, which is the strategy typically employed in studies of outbreaking organisms, refers to sampling generations around host population peaks. Letting ni denote the location of the ith host population peak, we include {ni−2,ni−1,ni,ni+1}⊂I for each of the sampled peaks. We consider peak sampling from the first three, four or five peaks, and perform a direct comparison with the same corresponding randomly sampled number of generations |I|.

Figure 7 top row presents violin plots summarizing the method performance when using random sampling, as data quantity (the number of generations sampled) increases, over 2000 simulated datasets for each |I|. Similarly, the bottom row of figure 7 presents violin plots summarizing the method performance when using peak sampling, as data quantity (the number of peaks sampled, with each peak having four generations) increases, over 500 simulated datasets for each number of peaks (|I|/4). The first column of the figure shows TPR (defined in equation (4.6)), with higher values conveying better performance at learning the correct model. The middle column of figure 7 presents coefficient relative error (defined in equation (4.7)), with higher values corresponding to worse performance. Finally, the right column of figure 7 presents the number of generations accurately predicted (metric defined in equation (4.9)), with higher values corresponding to greater number of accurately predicted generations and better method performance.

It can be observed from the left plots that sampling three peaks only is sufficient to identify the dynamics in the vast majority of cases with average TPR above 0.8, whereas random sampling average TPR is below 0.6 even with the equivalent of five peaks worth of data. Correspondingly, the right plots show that peak sampling with only three peaks doubles the accurate prediction length to 20 generations on average, compared with the equivalent data quantity random sampling with 12 generations.

In the low peak-sampling regime, when only three peaks are observed, the learned models on average still robustly identify the discrete map (TPRX≈1, bottom left plot), compared with random sampling, which yields TPRX≈0.3 on average (top left). Recall that correct identification of the discrete map relies on accurate identification of both the initial conditions and the continuous map (see algorithm 1), making TPR⁣X a good indicator of accurate identification of the full model. This directly correlates with the observed prediction accuracy, as measured by n0.5(𝐰^) (right plots), whereby three peaks are sufficient to predict up to 20 generations (with a long tail extending up to 80 generations), nearly twice that of random sampling. When five peaks are observed, results improve slightly, in particular n0.5(𝐰^) on average increases to approximately 30 for peak sampling and approximately 20 for random sampling. Additional performance metrics for the initial conditions and continuous dynamics are shown in figure 14 of the appendix, showing similar trends.

In summary, the bottleneck in predictive accuracy of the learned model is found to be identification of the correct model terms (e.g. as measured by the TPR). Peak sampling provides a clear advantage by offering sufficient data quality for the sparse regression routines to be effective even when few total generations are observed, indicating that host peak samples are more informative for long-term prediction. We conjecture that random sampling is a less effective strategy due to the higher probability of sampling in the slow, out-of-peak phase of the dynamics. Because host peaks rise and fall in relatively few generations, uniformly-at-random generation sampling is less likely to sample during the (highly dynamic) host peaks. This at least agrees with previous studies in which samples during fast dynamics are found to produce better results than samples during slow dynamics [10,48]. Overall, however, the proposed approach is able to work effectively with intermittent generation samples and data gaps, especially when peak sampling is employed, despite the log-normal noise model falling outside of the normality assumptions inherent to regression via WENDy thanks to the consistency properties of the embedded GLS routine which depend on only the first two moments [84–86].

Discussion

Summary

We present a general framework for rapidly learning hybrid discrete-continuous dynamics driven by strong seasonality with relevance to ecological systems. The resulting WSINDy-Eco algorithm runs in seconds on a modern laptop. Sparse model selection identifies dominant mechanisms in the dynamics which can easily be interpreted by the explicit model expressions, including complex coupling between the discrete and continuous variables. The method is well suited for a variety of sampling designs, including data with irregular sampling characterized by missing data for several generations. Distributional information about the WENDy parameter estimators afforded by the methodology [9] enables uncertainty quantification which can be used to provide confidence around model predictions, and to easily modify learned models, as described in §4.5. Moreover, the framework is not restricted to models in ecology, but can easily be applied to general hybrid systems featuring coupled discrete and continuous variables.

Extensions

A major benefit of the general weak formulation and library-based model learning is that libraries and test functions can also be tailored to suit different problems. Even though the library employed in this work is large, the terms in it were realistic for the problem at hand. For example, when we consider genetic drift and stochasticity in future work, to complement previous ecological studies [17,19,64], we will append additional terms to the library. We note also that our method need not be restricted to ecological settings and is directly applicable to seasonal hybrid systems, a ubiquitous modelling framework across the life sciences [12,54,87]. In our code repository (https://github.com/MathBioCU/wsindy_eco), we include resources for applications to other disciplines, including an additional example of a forced spiking neuron. In addition, different test functions for discrete-time inference may be used, reflecting different correlation structures if so desired.

Data quality and sparse regression

The data we considered here is meant to match realistic field studies, often limited to sampling a few times per week, often with 10–20 generations of data on hand [88–91]. Results did not vary significantly when the number of intergenerational samples M and sampling rate Δt were varied outside of the fixed values (4.4), with ΔtM∈(0.5T,T) and M≥24. Furthermore, we note that for the system (4.1)–(4.3), the coarsest sampling needed to identify the true model under zero-noise conditions is M=8 samples per generation, corresponding to roughly one sample per week, over nine (not necessarily consecutive) generations, which leads to all parameters estimated to within 0.08% relative error.

As in general, we will not know a priori what the noise distribution is, we have presented a general framework that we tested on a misspecified likelihood by treating log-normal noise using a GLS estimator within WENDy. Instead of tailoring to a specific likelihood (e.g. log-normal), which would require additional knowledge, we have relied on consistency properties of GLS, which depend only on correct specification of the first two moments, which our algorithm was able to handle well.

In the low-data/high-noise limit, sparse regression is not expected to return perfect results, as the sparsity threshold adapts to the current noise level (see appendix B.2 and figure 8 for visualization of sparsity threshold selection under different noise levels). In figure 15 in the appendix, we include a treatment with 10% noise, displaying limitations in forward prediction accuracy. This is due to the unavoidable high coefficient errors imparted by large noise and low sampling rates, which limits the selectivity of sparse regression routines.7 In such cases, making use of the uncertainty as detailed in §4.5 is expected to faithfully quantify predictive loss.

Visualization of sparsity threshold selection in MSTLS. Left to right: selection of thresholds for the initial conditions map, continuous dynamics and discrete dynamics. Top and bottom: 1% noise and 5% noise. There is a clear relationship between the selected threshold and the level of noise in the data, with higher noise levels corresponding to the need for (and the selection of) a larger threshold κ^, and consequently less complex models. In the case of the continuous dynamics (middle plots) the loss function becomes flatter, indicating that there are potentially multiple models describing the data equally well. In these examples all sub-models were identified correctly with the exception of the continuous dynamics at 5% noise, yet the recovered model is accurate enough to enable identification of the discrete dynamics (recall this depends on the outputs of the simulated learned continuous dynamics).

In such data-poor regimes, there are several ways to improve results, such as limiting the number of terms in the model libraries to reduce the size of the model space, or structuring the candidate models such that certain terms are favoured (e.g. including them directly in the model a priori). For example, if intraspecific competition is known to be important, density-dependent survival or reproduction terms can easily be added to the models. In addition, some recent works have sought to overcome highly sparse samples of continuous-time dynamics using interpolation [49]. We leave a full investigation of techniques for handling such highly sparse regimes to future work.

We also note that measurement noise in the discrete (X) variables has not been considered, as it is more realistic that measurement errors are found in the continuous variables (which in reality are typically stochastic). A trade-off exists between noise in continuous and discrete variables, and indeed the present sampling regime is sensitive to noise in the discrete variables, due to the low number of observations and the fact that we are also estimating initial conditions for the continuous (Y) variables at each generation (noise in the discrete variables is amplified by the noise in the initial conditions). Data with |I|=12 generation pairs corresponds to linear systems with only 12 rows when identifying the initial conditions map and discrete dynamics, which is often insufficient to accurately select terms in the presence of noise in both X and Y. This could be alleviated by the introduction of a Bayesian framework to explicitly model the interplay between noise in X, noise in Y and model parameters, as in [8,29,30,92–95], and through the use of literature and experiment-derived prior knowledge as a way to regularize the estimation and provide more efficient inference. We defer the Bayesian extensions to future work.

B.2. WSINDy-Eco hyperparameters

Throughout we set the WENDy iteration tolerance to τ=10−4, typically reached in under 10 iterations, and we use MaxIts=5. The library, test function and data covariance estimation choices are described below, together with a discussion about the sparsity parameters.

Trial function libraries. In this work, we let each library 𝕃 be of power–law type, with functions fj(u)=u1p1⋯udpd for pi∈ℝ. These trial functions are chosen for their ability to model any dynamical system if pi is chosen large enough, as well as their computational efficiency, and their widespread appearance in models of natural phenomena. The parameter libraries 𝕃(p)IC,𝕃(p)X are initialized to only contain the constant function {1}, while 𝕃(p)Y is initialized to contain the terms {1,Nn−V}. They are then incremented by increasing the total polynomial degree by 1 in each incrementation step, up to a total degree of 4. The state libraries 𝕃(s)Y,𝕃(s)X are fixed with total degrees 3 and 2, that is, one degree greater than that appearing in the true model (or what we think the true model is); in addition the term Sn1+V is added to 𝕃(s)Y. Altogether this leads to a total of 562 possible terms across all equations in all sub-models.

Test function basis. For the continuous dynamics, we employ piece-wise polynomial test functions of the form

which are supported on the time intervals (tk−mtΔt,tk+mtΔt), where Δt is the within-epidemic sampling rate and mt is the test function radius. The power η controls the highest order of differentiability, and thus the degree of accuracy of numerical integration in the absence of noise. The radius mt is largely responsible for variance reduction and mitigating high-frequency noise. The parameters η,mt are chosen from the observed data 𝐘 using the method detailed in [11, appendix A], which interprets the weak form as an approximate low-pass filter.

Data covariance estimation. The covariance matrix Σ𝐘 for the observed continuous data 𝐘=(Yn(𝐭n))n∈I is taken to be diagonal and is estimated by assuming that each value in the time series Yn(𝐭) has a constant variance σn, which we approximate using a high-order filter (see [97, appendix G]). The covariance matrix ΣIC for the initial condition data is then taken to be diagonal with variance σn for Yn(0) and variance 0 for Xn. For the covariance matrix Σ𝐗 of the data (Xn+1,Xn,Yn(T)), we assume that Yn(T) is random but Xn,Xn+1 are non-random (have zero variance). We set the variance estimate for each Yn(T) to be the empirical variance of the observed data Yn(𝐭n) around computed values Y^n(𝐭n), as described in algorithm 4.

Sparsity thresholds. We use equally log-spaced candidate thresholds log10⁡κ= 𝚕𝚒𝚗𝚜𝚙𝚊𝚌𝚎(−4,0,50), seen to work well in previous studies utilizing MSTLS [10,11]. The ultimate sparsity threshold κ used in the each final model is determined by the MSTLS loss function [11]. We note that there is a clear relationship between the selected threshold and the level of noise in the data, with higher noise levels corresponding to the need for (and the selection of) larger thresholds, and consequently less complex models. Figure 8 displays this trend for two exemplary cases, using peak sampling with three peaks at 1% and 5% noise, respectively. The selected threshold is seen to adapt to the noise level; however, at larger noise it may be challenging to select the correct model, as many models fare equally well according to the given loss (bottom middle plot).

C.2. Results

Figures 11–13 display the results of 100 random trials applying WSINDy-Eco to data from the two-pathogen system for each of the noise levels σNR∈{0.002,0.01,0.02,0.05}. We first note that the initial conditions map (top left set of equations in table 5) was recovered accurately in each case, with correct terms (TPR = 1) and relative coefficient errors (E2) below the data noise level σNR.

Similar to the results in the main text, WSINDy-Eco recovers each of the continuous dynamics (S˙,P˙1,P˙2,ν˙1,ν˙2), as well as the discrete dynamics (N,Z1,Z2), robustly at low noise levels (e.g. 0.2%, see figures 11 and 12). At 1% noise, recovery of the equation for ν˙1 begins to fail (figure 11, fourth column), while the remaining equations are robustly identified, with median TPR equal to 1. This is because ν1 rapidly reaches steady state in the first few time steps, leaving very little information about its dynamics (see figure 10, bottom row). At 2% noise, Z2 also begins to lack robust recovery, and at 5% noise most equations are challenging to identify. It should be noted that the discrete dynamics are identified from a highly underdetermined system of just 60 rows and 210 columns of possible terms.

Figure 13 displays the prediction accuracy of the learned models as measured by n0.5(𝐰^) using in-sample testing and out-of-sample testing. In-sample testing (left plot of figure 13) refers to simulating the learned model from the same initial conditions X0=(N0,Z1,0,Z2,0) as the training data. In a slight abuse of terminology, out-of-sample testing (middle plot of figure 13) refers to simulating the learned model on five random initial conditions drawn from a uniform distribution with mean X and variance 0.25X2, where X is the average of the respective X∈{N,Z1,Z2} values observed in the training data.

As can be seen in figure 13, in-sample testing is highly sensitive to noise. This is due to the low initial pathogen 2 density (P2,0) leading to many learned models predicting that P2 will die out in the first few generations. For example, for data with 1% noise, we record 75% of learned models, each trained on data with a different independent noise sequence, predicting that P2 will die out in the first five generations. This is exactly the scenario in figure 9 (top five plots), in which the learned model from data with 1% noise predicts that P2 dies out in the third generation (as shown, without halting conditions that learned model eventually leads to negative P2 values). This sensitivity manifests as a numerical instability in the discrete update equation for Z2, but can also be readily interpreted from an ecological perspective. Coefficient errors as low as 1% in the Z2 update, even with correct equation identification (TPR = 1), can result in non-positive initial P2,n+1(0) values when P2,n(T) is sufficiently low, from which P2 density cannot recover. Put another way, for sufficiently low density, the second pathogen is not able to survive the winter.

For comparison, out-of-sample testing performs surprisingly well, with only 12% of models predicting that P2 dies out in the first five generations at 1% noise. The bottom five plots of figure 9 show an out-of-sample test that uses the same learned model as the in-sample test in the plots above it. Despite misidentifying the equation for ν1, the model predicts the next four host outbreaks to within a year on this out-of-sample dataset. From this we conclude that the ‘pathogen 2 invasion’ case examined here is particularly challenging to model, but it nevertheless provides rich enough data to WSINDy-Eco to learn models that generalize well to a variety of cases.

In the rightmost plot of figure 13, motivated by the challenges associated with identifying the Z2 dynamics as exhibited in figure 12, we further examine the out-of-sample testing case but for learned models that identified the Z2 equation correctly with TPR = 1. The learned models exhibit significantly increased prediction lengths across each noise level. This result, together with the sensitivities of in-sample testing discussed in the previous paragraphs, emphasizes the importance of quantifying the effective over-wintering survival rate of competing pathogen strains; reassuringly, the necessary experiments are logistically challenging but feasible [21]. Moreover, correct identification of the Z2 dynamics does not correlate with correct identification of the dynamics of ν˙1 which is the other equation that turned out to lack robust recovery (figure 11 column 4). We therefore conjecture that this model is not particularly sensitive to the ν˙1 dynamics.

In summary, in this additional analysis we have shown that the algorithm presented in the main text can be used to infer higher dimensional and thus harder problems. The problem that we have studied is characterized by fast changing state dynamics which makes it more challenging to model and learn in the case in which there are only a few terms. Indeed, the magnitude of the problem is even more severe for other methods, for example parameter-inference-only methods with known model structure, for which sophisticated statistical parameter estimation techniques such as MCMC tend to be very slow for anything but very simple versions of the models that we use here [21].
