Tocilizumab for the Management of Osimertinib-Induced Pneumonitis in a Critically Ill Patient
Authors: Caroline Hénin, Ludovic Gerard, Alexis Werion, Farah Tamirou, Frank Aboubakar, Virginie Montiel, João Pinto Pereira
Published: 2026

Introduction

Targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors have fundamentally transformed treatment of cancer—reshaping outcomes with sometimes lasting responses while also generating distinct toxicity profiles that differ from those of traditional chemotherapy. Discontinuation of targeted therapy or immunotherapy and administration of corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of management for most high-grade immune-related adverse events (irAEs) and usually induce a response within 72 hours. Refractory cases may warrant additional immunosuppression using agents such as infliximab, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), or tocilizumab.

Moreover, in the case of checkpoint inhibitor–related pneumonitis, retrospective data suggest that higher-grade toxicities are more frequently steroid-resistant.

Conversely, there are no robust data regarding the treatment of adverse effects associated with epidermal growth factor (EGFR)–targeted therapies. We present a case of osimertinib-related steroid-refractory pneumonitis that responded favorably to the off-label use of tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor inhibitor, enabling the patient to be weaned off mechanical ventilation and discharged from the intensive care unit.

Case

A 67-year-old woman was admitted to the emergency department with a 4-day history of progressively worsening dyspnea accompanied by a dry cough. Her medical history included depression and metastatic EGFR L858R–mutated non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with osimertinib treatment initiated just before 2 months.

On admission, she was severely hypoxemic with an oxygen saturation of 59% on room air and tachycardic at 130 beats per minute. Laboratory investigations revealed an elevated C-reactive protein at 18.5 mg/L, a pro-brain natriuretic peptide of 677.5 ng/mL, and mild renal impairment with a creatinine of 1.14 mg/dL corresponding to an eGFR of 46 mL/min/1.73 m2. White blood cell count, including eosinophils, was within normal limits. Contrast-enhanced chest CT demonstrated extensive bilateral crazy-paving opacities, parenchymal consolidations, and interlobular septal thickening distributed along an anterior-posterior and apical-basal gradient (Fig 1).

PaO2/FiO2 ratio evolution from admission (Day 0) to ICU discharge (D12). Tocilizumab was given on Day 4. ICU, intensive care unit; P/F, PaO2/FiO2.

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) with a working diagnosis of severe osimertinib-associated pneumonitis. Corticosteroid therapy was initiated with methylprednisolone at 1 mg/kg/day, following management principles similar to those applied for immune checkpoint inhibitor–related pneumonitis. As a comprehensive infectious workup (including a well-tolerated bronchial wash fluid analysis) was negative, no empiric antibiotic therapy or other specific treatments were initiated. To minimize the risk of respiratory deterioration, only 60 mL was instilled.

Because of worsening oxygenation, the patient requires endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. The PaO2/FiO2 (P/F) ratio was 60 mmHg on intubation, consistent with severe hypoxemia. Despite an initial favorable response to prone positioning during the first 48 hours, the patient remained severely hypoxemic, with FiO2 requirements between 0.60 and 0.80 and a P/F ratio of approximately 150 mmHg (Fig 2).

(A) Transverse view of contrast-enhanced chest CT on Day 0 (admission). (B) Frontal view of the contrast-enhanced CT scan on day 0 (admission).

Four days after ICU admission, because of persistent deterioration despite under high-dose systemic corticosteroids, tocilizumab was administered at 8 mg/kg, once. This was followed by a gradual improvement in respiratory status, permitting progressive ventilatory weaning. The patient was successfully extubated on day 12 and placed on high-flow nasal oxygen, which was ultimately weaned on day 14. She was discharged from the ICU on day 16 with 3 L/min of supplemental oxygen. By hospital day 30, supplemental oxygen had been fully discontinued, and the patient was deemed fit for discharge. The daily dose of methylprednisolone was tapered from 64 mg to 32 mg on day 10, maintained at 32 mg for 2 weeks, and then further reduced to 16 mg until discharge. A control CT was performed a month later (Fig 3) and revealed no residual signs of pneumonitis.

Discussion

Osimertinib is a third-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitor that selectively and irreversibly binds to mutant forms of the EGFR including exon 19 deletions and exon 21 L858R. Its efficacy and favorable safety profile established it as first-line treatment in EGFR-mutated metastatic non–small cell lung cancer. Common adverse effects include digestive intolerance, diarrhea, rash, and fatigue. Less frequently, patients may develop a pulmonary toxicity. In the FLAURA trial,1 pneumonitis occurred in 4% of the patients, whereas some other series report incidence of drug-induced lung toxicity/pneumonitis in up to 18% of the patients. In a fraction of those, lung toxicity may be life-threatening.2

Most cases occur within the first 3 months of therapy, with a median time to onset of approximately 2 months,3 consistent with the timeline observed in this case.

The differential diagnosis of acute respiratory failure in patients receiving osimertinib includes infectious pneumonia, cardiogenic pulmonary edema, acute eosinophilic pneumonia, lymphangitic carcinomatosis, and drug-induced interstitial lung disease (ILD).

In this case, the radiologic findings of diffuse bilateral crazy-paving, interlobular septal thickening, and consolidation are consistent with inflammatory process, whereas the negative infectious workup and the absence of cardiac dysfunction support the diagnosis of osimertinib-induced pneumonitis. The absence of a precipitating factor, pleurisy, or fever, combined with the lack of response to corticosteroids in this older patient, argues against a diagnosis of acute eosinophilic pneumonia.

Current management of EGFR TKI–associated ILD remains largely empirical as dedicated evidence is limited. In practice, therapeutic strategies are often extrapolated from the management of immune checkpoint inhibitor–related pneumonitis, despite these entities being mechanistically distinct. High-dose systemic corticosteroids constitute the cornerstone of treatment for EGFR TKI–associated ILD. However, evidence regarding the management of severe or refractory cases remains scarce.

By contrast, in immune checkpoint irAEs, treatment algorithms are better established, with escalation to second-line immunosuppressive therapy recommended for corticosteroid-refractory cases. Commonly used agents in this setting include infliximab, vedolizumab, mycophenolate mofetil, IVIG, and cyclophosphamide. Emerging evidence also supports the use of tocilizumab as a second- or third-line option, for selected irAEs, with clinical benefit reported in up to 73% of treated patients.

While such approaches may inform management considerations in EGFR TKI–associated ILD, their applicability remains uncertain given the differing underlying pathophysiology.4,5

Early cytokine upregulation, including elevated circulating IL-6 levels, appears to play a role in EGFR-TKI toxicities and poorer tumor response to targeted therapy. Recent data indicate that IL-6 is a key driver in EGFR-mutant tumors, affecting both tumor behavior and therapeutic response.6

Building on these observations, preclinical studies have explored the complex mechanisms involving IL-6 pathway inhibition (eg, tocilizumab or JAK inhibitors) with EGFR TKIs as a strategy to overcome or delay therapeutic resistance. However, the clinical relevance of this approach remains uncertain as it has not yet been validated in prospective studies involving patients treated with osimertinib.7

In the present case, early initiation of methylprednisolone and standard supportive measures failed to improve the patient's respiratory status. The temporal association between tocilizumab administration and clinical improvement raises the possibility that cytokine-mediated inflammation, including IL-6–mediated signaling, may contribute to the pathogenesis of TKI-associated pneumonitis. However, serum IL-6 levels were not measured, representing an important limitation and precluding direct mechanistic confirmation of IL-6 pathway activation in this patient.

Although evidence supporting IL-6 receptor blockade for drug-induced pneumonitis remains anecdotal, recent reports of immune checkpoint inhibitor–related pneumonitis and COVID-19–associated acute respiratory distress syndrome have suggested potential benefit in hyperinflammatory presentations.8,9 Some preclinical data also support combining IL-6 pathway blockade (eg, tocilizumab or JAK inhibitors) with EGFR-TKIs to overcome or delay resistance. However, this strategy has not yet been validated in clinical trials for osimertinib-treated patients.

Tocilizumab has additionally been explored as secondary prophylaxis during checkpoint inhibitor rechallenge, without apparent impairment of antitumor efficacy or interference with corticosteroid tapering.

While a growing body of literature supports treatment rechallenge under steroid coverage for certain toxicities, there are currently no data regarding osimertinib reintroduction after grade 4 osimertinib-induced pneumonitis. Consequently, permanent discontinuation is advised in such cases, canceling out the otherwise long overall survival of these patients with NSCLC while also posing a significant challenge for patients with brain metastases for whom therapeutic alternatives are limited.10-12

Overall, this case illustrates a severe osimertinib-induced pneumonitis refractory to early high-dose corticosteroids and requiring aggressive respiratory support. The clinical improvement observed after tocilizumab administration raises the hypothesis that IL-6–mediated inflammation may contribute to the pathogenesis of TKI-associated pneumonitis. While this single observation does not establish causality, IL-6 receptor blockade may represent a potential rescue strategy in carefully selected steroid-refractory cases, provided that its known toxicity profile—including infection risk and hepatotoxicity—is carefully weighed. Prospective studies are warranted to define the role, optimal timing, and safety of this approach.
