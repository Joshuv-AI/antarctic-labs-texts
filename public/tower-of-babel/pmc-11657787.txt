Clinical significance of serum Helicobacter pylori antibody cytotoxin-associated gene A levels in patients with unstable angina
Authors: Haizhen Jia, Huajun Fan, Jinhe Zhao, Jun Peng, Yunhong Pan, Hongyan Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe investigated the clinical significance of serum Helicobacter pylori cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA) antibody levels in 768 patients with unstable angina (UA).MethodsSerum CagA levels were measured using ELISA. Demographic data, serum biomarkers, and SYNTAX scores were collected. Patients were followed up for 1 year for major adverse cardiac events (MACE).ResultsCagA-positive UA patients had higher total cholesterol and hsCRP levels, and SYNTAX scores. CagA levels correlated positively with TC, hsCRP, and SYNTAX scores. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed shorter MACE-free survival in CagA-positive UA patients. CagA levels predicted MACE occurrence within 1 year, along with SYNTAX scores.ConclusionsSerum H. pylori CagA antibody positivity is associated with worse prognosis in UA patients. CagA levels correlate with lipid metabolism and inflammatory markers.Keywords: Helicobacter pylori, Cytotoxin-associated gene A, Unstable angina, SYNTAX

Introduction

Coronary syndrome is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, reportedly causing nearly 7 million deaths and 129 million disabilities each year [1, 2]. Unstable angina (UA) is a type of acute coronary syndrome characterized by chest pain or discomfort at rest or with minimal exertion. It is considered a medical emergency due to its association with a high risk of myocardial infarction or cardiac death. UA affects a significant number of individuals worldwide and poses a substantial burden on healthcare systems [3–5]. Understanding the underlying mechanisms and potential biomarkers associated with UA can greatly contribute to improving risk stratification and management strategies for affected patients.

In recent years, increasing evidence suggests that chronic infections and inflammation play a crucial role in the development and progression of cardiovascular diseases, including UA [6–8]. One such infection that has gained attention is Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori). H. pylori is a gram-negative bacterium that colonizes the gastric mucosa of approximately half of the world’s population [9]. H. pylori infection has been associated with various gastrointestinal diseases, such as gastric ulcers and gastric cancer [10]. However, emerging studies have also implicated H. pylori infection in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases, including UA. This bacterium has been shown to induce chronic low-grade inflammation, which can trigger a cascade of events leading to endothelial dysfunction, plaque instability, and thrombosis, ultimately contributing to the development of UA [11, 12]. One of the virulence factors produced by H. pylori is the cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA) protein. CagA is injected into the host gastric epithelial cells, where it interferes with cellular signaling pathways and promotes persistent inflammation. In response to H. pylori infection, the host immune system generates antibodies against CagA protein, which can be detected in the serum. Quantification of serum CagA antibody levels has become a valuable tool for assessing the exposure and immune response to H. pylori infection [13, 14]. It has been shown that CAGA positivity is significantly associated with the development of coronary heart disease [15]. And mechanistically, it has also been demonstrated that CAGA promotes vascular cell inflammatory response and atherosclerosis [16]. However, the clinical significance of serum CagA antibody levels in patients with UA remains largely unexplored.

In this prospective observational study, we aim to investigate the clinical significance of serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels in patients with UA. By analyzing the relationship between CagA antibody levels and the severity of UA, we hope to gain insights into the potential role of H. pylori infection and associated immune response in the pathogenesis of UA. These findings could potentially contribute to the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for managing UA patients.

Methods

Subjects

This prospective cohort study included 768 unstable angina (UA) patients who were treated at our hospital from October 2021 to February 2023. The diagnostic criteria for UA included [17]: (1) typical symptoms of angina pectoris, (2) typical ischemic electrocardiographic changes (new or transient ST depression ≥ 0.1 mV or T-wave inversion ≥ 0.2 mV), and (3) normal levels of cardiac biomarkers (cTnT, cTnI, or CK–MB). Exclusion criteria included: (1) stable angina, myocardial bridge, coronary artery anomalies, cardiomyopathy, valvular heart disease, or congenital heart disease, and heart failure patients; (2) patients who had previously undergone percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG); (3) patients who had received treatment with steroids, anti-inflammatory drugs, antibiotics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or immunosuppressive agents within the past month; (4) patients with rheumatic or hematological diseases; (5) patients with severe infection, severe liver or kidney disease, malignancies, or cardiovascular dysfunction. This study did not intervene in or standardize the subsequent treatment of H. pylori infection in these patients. All patients received treatment according to the relevant Chinese guidelines. The entire study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (TY2021-09-03).

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was determined using a standard formula for cohort studies evaluating binary outcomes. The following formula was used: N = 2(Zα/2 + Zβ)2[P1(1 − P1) + P2(1 − P2)]/(P1–P2)2. Based on the study design and available literature, we assumed an event rate of 18% in the CagA-positive (exposed) group (P1) and 10% in the CagA-negative (non-exposed) group (P2). With a significance level of 0.05 and a power of 80%, resulting in a total sample size of 768 patients. This sample size is sufficient to detect a significant difference in outcomes between the two groups with adequate statistical power.

ELISA

All study subjects were asked to provide a fasting (8 h) antecubital venous blood sample of 5 mL in the morning. The concentration of Helicobacter pylori CagA in the serum was then measured using the CagA–IgG assay kit and the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method. The serum samples were centrifuged at 2000 g for 15 min, and then tested using a commercially available kit (CagA, MBS2611711, 20 ng/ml–0.312 ng/ml, MyBioSource, California, USA). In brief, ELISA plates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 100μL of the target protein solution, washed with PBS buffer, and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h. After further washing with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20, the bound antibodies were detected by sequential dilutions of the target (culture supernatant, antibody) and incubation at room temperature. After additional washing, binding antibodies were detected using sheep anti-mouse IgG conjugated to HRP. One hundred microliters of TMB substrate were added, incubated for 10–20 min, and then 50μL of stop solution (1NH2SO4) was added. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured. Antibody concentrations were determined based on a specific standard curve targeting the CagA protein. We divided the patients into high CagA antibody level and low CagA antibody level groups based on the median value of CagA antibody concentration within our cohort (6 ng per milliliter) [18].

Data collection and scale scoring

Clinical data, including age, height, weight, and comorbidities, were directly extracted from the patients' medical records or obtained through direct patient questioning. Smoking and alcohol consumption data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered to the patients. Laboratory parameters, including serum biomarkers, were measured through standard clinical testing procedures. This approach ensured that all relevant patient data were systematically gathered for analysis. The synergy between percutaneous coronary intervention with taxus and cardiac surgery (SYNTAX) score was calculated as follows: Two experienced interventional cardiologists used the SYNTAX score calculator to calculate the SYNTAX score based on coronary angiography. By visually assessing each independent lesion with a vessel diameter ≥ 1.5 mm and a stenosis diameter ≥ 50%, the individual scores of all these lesions were added up to obtain the total score. In addition, a whole blood analyzer was used for complete blood testing, measuring lipid-related indicators such as total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC), inflammatory markers white blood cell count (WBC), hs-C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), as well as creatine kinase MB (CK-MB), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). Furthermore, all patients were followed up for 1 year, and those who experienced one of the following during the follow-up period: cardiac death, heart failure, cardiogenic shock, recurrent myocardial infarction, arrhythmia with hemodynamic disorders, were defined as having major adverse cardiac events (MACE) indicating poor prognosis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The normal distribution of data was confirmed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (range). Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney test or Student’s t test. Proportions were compared using the chi-square test. Sparman’s correlation analysis was performed to assess the correlations between serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels and clinical characteristics of UA patients. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to determine the predictive value of serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels for the prognosis of UA patients. Logistic regression analysis was employed to identify risk factors for poor prognosis of UA patients. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics of all participants

This prospective observational cohort study included 768 patients with UA. Based on the levels of H. pylori CagA antibody, the patients were divided into the high CagA antibody level group (n = 392) and the low CagA antibody level group (n = 376). Comparing the demographic and clinical data between the two groups, we found that the UA patients in the high CagA antibody level group had significantly higher levels of serum TC and hs-CRP. Furthermore, we also observed a significantly higher SYNTAX score in the high CagA antibody level group. No other clinical characteristics showed significant differences between the two groups (Table 1).

Correlation between serum Helicobacter pylori CagA antibody levels and clinical factors in UA patients

Next, we conducted Spearman’s analysis to assess the correlation between serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels and clinical factors in UA patients. As shown in Table 2, serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels were positively correlated with serum TC and hsCRP in UA patients. In addition, the Spearman’s coefficient between serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels and SYNTAX score in UA patients was 0.388, P < 0.001, indicating a positive correlation. This suggests an association between serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels and clinical factors as well as the severity of UA in patients.

Kaplan–Meier curve analysis of MACA-free survival time in UA patients

MACE occurred in a total of 91 UA patients during the 1-year follow-up period. Next, we performed Kaplan–Meier curve analysis to evaluate the MACA-free survival time within 1 year in UA patients stratified by CagA positivity and negativity. As shown in Fig. 1, compared to low CagA antibody level UA patients, high CagA antibody level UA patients had significantly shorter MACA-free survival time within 1 year (P = 0.021).

Predictive value of serum Helicobacter pylori CagA antibody levels in adverse outcomes of UA patients

Furthermore, we conducted ROC curve analysis to assess the predictive value of serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels on the occurrence of MACE during the 1-year follow-up in UA patients. The results demonstrated that serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels can be used to predict the occurrence of MACE within 1 year in UA patients, with an AUC of 0.633, a cutoff value of 6.49 ng/ml, sensitivity of 70.3%, and specificity of 57.0% (Fig. 2).

Risk factors for the occurrence of MACE within 1 year in UA patients using logistic regression analysis

Finally, we performed logistic regression analysis to identify the risk factors for the occurrence of MACE within 1 year in UA patients. For logistic regression analysis, we employed a multivariable binary logistic regression using the enter method. Two models were established, including Model 1 (Age, BMI, SYNTAX, TC, TG, HDLC, LDLC) and Model 2 (WBC, Hs-CRP, PCT, CK-MB, BUN, LDH, CagA). The results indicated that the SYNTAX score and serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels were risk factors for the occurrence of MACE within 1 year in UA patients (Table 3).

Discussion

Coronary artery disease is responsible for up to 17 million deaths worldwide each year [19], while UA can lead to serious complications such as myocardial infarction and sudden cardiac death. Therefore, further understanding of risk factors for adverse outcomes in patients is needed. In this study, we investigated the clinical significance of serum Helicobacter pylori CagA antibody levels in UA patients. The results demonstrated important associations between serum Helicobacter pylori CagA antibody levels and clinical factors, severity, and prognosis in UA patients.

In this study, approximately 50% of unstable angina patients were classified as high CagA antibody level, based on the median serum CagA antibody level. This differs from reports indicating that over 90% of H. pylori-positive individuals in China are CagA-positive. The discrepancy arises from our methodology, which used the median value to categorize patients, rather than a fixed diagnostic threshold. This approach was chosen to explore how varying levels of CagA antibodies influence clinical outcomes. In contrast, other studies often use predefined thresholds to detect CagA-positive strains, which can vary depending on the diagnostic tools and population studied. Differences in study design, population characteristics, and diagnostic criteria likely contribute to the variation in reported CagA positivity rates. Our method highlights the importance of considering antibody levels in understanding CagA's clinical implications, offering a different perspective from studies using binary classifications.

SYNTAX is a coronary angiography scoring system that reflects the complexity and burden of coronary artery disease (CAD). According to the 2018 European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (ESC/EACTS) guidelines on myocardial revascularization, the SYNTAX score can be used to assess the severity of coronary artery stenosis [20]. Anatomical SYNTAX score has been proven to predict the occurrence rate of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) and long-term prognosis in stable CAD patients undergoing coronary artery revascularization [21]. Xu et al. also demonstrated that a high SYNTAX score is an independent predictor for MACE in UA patients after medical treatment [22]. In this study, we similarly found that SYNTAX score was a risk factor for adverse outcomes in UA patients. Furthermore, we observed that the high CagA antibody level group of UA patients had significantly higher SYNTAX scores compared to the low CagA antibody level group. This finding suggests that CagA positivity may be associated with more severe coronary artery disease and may indicate a higher risk of adverse cardiovascular events in these patients.

In addition, we also found significant differences in lipid metabolism and inflammatory markers between the high CagA antibody level and low CagA antibody level groups. In other disease types, Yang et al. found that the CagA–IgG positive group had significantly higher levels of TC, TG, and LDLC compared to the CagA–IgG negative group [23]. Huang et al. demonstrated that patients with coronary heart disease in the Helicobacter pylori CagA group had significantly elevated levels of total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein, lipoprotein B, serum hsCRP, and oxLDL, as well as a significant increase in the severity of coronary artery atherosclerosis [24]. The observed higher total cholesterol levels in high CagA antibody level patients may suggest a link between H. pylori CagA positivity and dyslipidemia. This aligns with previous studies that have reported associations between CagA-positive H. pylori strains and adverse lipid profiles [24]. The pro-inflammatory effects of CagA may contribute to alterations in lipid metabolism, further increasing cardiovascular risk in these patients. In this study, we found that UA patients in the high CagA antibody level group had significantly higher levels of serum TC and hs-CRP compared to the low CagA antibody level group. However, there was no significant difference in LDLC levels between the two groups. In clinical practice, it is generally desired to have lower LDLC levels in UA patients [25]. The discrepancy between this study and previous research findings could be attributed to variations in inclusion/exclusion criteria, sample size, different patient characteristics, and comorbidities. This suggests that further studies are needed to validate our findings. H. pylori has been shown to induce chronic low-grade inflammation, which can trigger a cascade of events leading to endothelial dysfunction, plaque instability, and thrombosis, ultimately contributing to the development of UA [26]. Mechanistically, Li et al. confirmed that CagA promotes plaque growth in early stages of atherosclerosis in apolipoprotein E-deficient mice and enhances the expression of adhesion molecules and inflammatory cytokines in mouse aortic endothelial cells (MAECs) [16]. Our findings support previous research indicating an association between serum Helicobacter pylori CagA antibody positivity and lipid metabolism as well as inflammatory response.

Previous studies have suggested a potential association between CagA positivity and lipid metabolism, particularly with respect to the impact of H. pylori eradication therapy on lipid profiles. Several reports have indicated that CagA-positive H. pylori strains may influence lipid metabolism by promoting chronic inflammation, which is known to affect lipid regulation and atherosclerosis development. In particular, an increase in TC and LDL-C levels has been observed following successful H. pylori eradication therapy, likely due to the reduction of chronic systemic inflammation induced by the infection [24, 27]. This phenomenon may also be influenced by the reduction in bacterial toxins that suppress lipid metabolism. While this study did not directly explore the effects of H. pylori eradication or detailed lipid changes, the observed associations between CagA antibody levels and unstable angina suggest that lipid metabolism could be an important factor to consider in future studies. Further research is needed to clarify the mechanisms by which CagA-positive strains may affect lipid profiles and cardiovascular risk in patients with unstable angina, especially in the context of H. pylori eradication.

In addition to the above associations, this study demonstrated a positive correlation between CagA antibody levels and SYNTAX scores. Higher CagA antibody levels were associated with more severe coronary artery disease, indicating a potential role of CagA antibodies as a biomarker for disease severity in UA patients. In other studies, CagA antibodies have also been used as biomarkers for certain diseases. For example, Shiota et al. demonstrated a positive correlation between the presence of anti-CagA antibodies and gastric cancer, suggesting that anti-CagA antibodies can serve as a biomarker for gastric cancer in East Asian countries [28]. Lu et al. showed that CagA-positive expression is associated with the severity and prognosis of gastric adenocarcinoma, indicating that CagA expression could be a promising biomarker for metastasis and prognosis in gastric adenocarcinoma, as well as a potential therapeutic target [29]. Moreover, we found that CagA antibody levels could predict the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) within 1 year in UA patients. This highlights the potential utility of CagA antibody levels as a prognostic indicator for UA patients.

Our findings that Helicobacter pylori CagA positivity is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with unstable angina align with emerging evidence on the role of H. pylori in vascular pathology. Recent studies have shown that H. pylori infection can impair endothelial function through an exosome-mediated mechanism, where exosomes derived from infected cells carry virulence factors like CagA, leading to endothelial dysfunction, a key precursor to atherosclerosis [30]. In addition, it has been demonstrated that the CagA protein itself can induce vascular calcification in coronary artery smooth muscle cells, further contributing to the pathogenesis of coronary artery disease [31]. Moreover, another study revealed that exosome-transmitted miR-25, induced by H. pylori infection, promotes vascular endothelial cell injury by targeting the KLF2 gene, a crucial regulator of endothelial integrity [32]. These studies collectively support the notion that H. pylori, particularly CagA-positive strains, may play a significant role in promoting vascular damage and increasing the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, which is consistent with the associations observed in our cohort.

There are several limitations to this study. First, it is a single-center study, which may limit generalizability; however, our sample size of 768 patients and diverse patient demographics provide broad representation. Second, only a subset of clinical factors and serum markers was measured. Third, not all patients underwent endoscopic examination, restricting analysis of gastric conditions like gastritis severity. In addition, this study did not explore the mechanisms underlying the association between CagA positivity and UA, requiring further research to validate our findings. Finally, while CagA-positive H. pylori strains are common in East Asia, the incidence of UA is not higher than in Western countries, indicating that other factors, such as genetics, lifestyle, and healthcare access, also influence UA risk. Future studies should explore these interactions across diverse populations.

Conclusion

This study revealed that UA patients with serum Helicobacter pylori CagA antibody positivity have worse prognosis. In addition, serum H. pylori CagA antibody levels were associated with clinical factors such as lipid metabolism and inflammatory markers. These findings may have implications for the development of improved treatment and prevention strategies for UA patients, aiming to reduce the occurrence of MACE and improve patient prognosis and quality of life.
