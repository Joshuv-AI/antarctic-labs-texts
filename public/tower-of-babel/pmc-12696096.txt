Ultrahigh current output performance in piezoelectric energy harvesters enabled by phase boundary–bandgap synergistic engineering
Authors: Yan Lin, Yanqi Wu, Mupeng Zheng, Bo Wu, Chunlin Zhao, Chaofeng Wu, Ke Wang, Yudong Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the rapid expansion of the Internet of Things, piezoelectric energy harvesting has become essential for sustainable self-powered microsystems. However, achieving sufficient output current under operating conditions remains a persistent challenge. Herein, a phase-boundary and bandgap co-engineering strategy is implemented for potassium sodium niobate-based ceramics by incorporating BiFeO3 into 0.97(K0.5Na0.5)(Nb0.96Sb0.04)O3-0.03(Bi0.5Na0.5)0.85(Li0.5Nd0.5)0.15ZrO3. This induces a rhombohedral-tetragonal phase boundary that establishes nanoscale multiphase coexistence alongside an elevated average polarization displacement, enabling an exceptional piezoelectric coefficient. Simultaneously, ultralow resistivity is achieved through the lower bandgap resulting from BiFeO3 incorporation. This design achieves ultrahigh current density (50 μA/cm2) and power density (860 μW/cm3). Atomic-resolution structural analyses reveal that continuous rotation of the polarization vector, promoted by multi-directional polar states and high-density multiscale nanodomains, suppresses polarization anisotropy and domain-wall pinning, thereby synergistically amplifying piezoelectric responses. Concurrently, narrow-bandgap BiFeO3 doping elevates oxygen-vacancy concentration, lowering the conduction-band minimum and narrowing the bandgap, further reducing resistivity.

Introduction

In recent years, driven by the rapid development of the Internet of Things (IoT), self-powered technology has emerged as a crucial research direction to meet the energy demands of micro-powered devices1–3. Piezoelectric energy harvesters (PEHs) can directly convert ambient vibrations, such as human movement, mechanical oscillation, and acoustic waves, into electric energy (Fig. 1a). This capability enables low-power, miniaturized devices (e.g., wireless sensor nodes in the IoT) to achieve self-powered operation, thereby garnering widespread attention from both academia and industry4. However, PEHs typically generate high output voltages (tens to hundreds of volts), which far exceed the operating voltage of conventional batteries (generally below 5.0 V). Therefore, achieving high current output in PEHs requires ceramics with maximized d33. The focus of current studies on high-performance piezoelectric ceramics for PEHs remains primarily directed toward lead-based systems hold a major share in the market7–10. However, the growing emphasis on environmental protection has led to stricter regulations regarding the use and content of toxic elements such as Pb in electronic devices. Consequently, this has spurred the rapid development of lead-free piezoceramics as a more environmentally friendly alternative11.

Potassium-sodium niobate (KNN)-based lead-free piezoelectric ceramics are considered promising candidates for lead-free PEHs, owing to their combination of excellent electromechanical properties, a high Curie temperature, and environmental compatibility12,13. The presence of polymorphic phase boundaries is critical, as it induces a state of strong spontaneous polarization that significantly facilitates the reorientation of ferroelectric domains under an applied electric field. This intrinsic mechanism is responsible for the notably high electromechanical performance observed in these materials14,15. Nevertheless, the piezoelectric performance of the pure KNN system remains constrained, thereby falling short in meeting the demands of PEHs (Fig. 1b). To enhance the piezoelectric properties of KNN, extensive research has focused on phase boundary engineering, primarily through the introduction of multiple elements16. Significant breakthroughs have been achieved via novel phase boundaries, yielding piezoelectric coefficients ≥400 pC/N in KNN-based ceramics14,17,18. These results demonstrate the substantial potential of KNN-based lead-free piezoceramics for high-performance PEHs. Consequently, constructing new phase boundaries (NPBs) has become a key strategy for enhancing piezoelectric coefficients, leading to significantly improved PEH power output, as shown in Fig. 1c. For example, Huan et al. successfully constructed the rhombohedral-orthorhombic-tetragonal (R-O-T) three-phase coexisting phase boundary at room temperature by doping CaZrO3 into the composition 0.964(K0.52Na0.48)(Nb0.96Sb0.04)O3-0.036(Bi0.5Na0.5)ZrO3, which yielded a high d33 value of 354 pC/N and a substantial d33×g33 value of 5508 × 10−15 m2/N, which enabled the achievement of a high current of 19.3 μA19. Recently, Xi et al. enhanced energy harvesting by constructing entropy-engineered polycrystalline phase transitions in KNN-based materials, which promoted lattice distortion and high-density nanodomains. Their approach doubled the output power density to 819 μW/cm3 versus low-entropy counterparts20. Despite these advances, piezoelectric performance enhancement in KNN-based ceramics has reached a bottleneck, making significant short-term improvements unlikely. Consequently, alternative material modification strategies beyond conventional frameworks are urgently needed to advance the piezoelectric energy harvesting.

Beyond phase-boundary engineering, internal resistance critically governs PEH performance. Excessive resistance impedes current output and causes substantial energy dissipation within devices. Reducing resistivity in piezoelectric materials effectively increases the output current of PEHs, which is another key factor for optimizing overall performance21. For instance, Zhao et al. introduced highly conductive Ag particles into Ba0.85Ca0.15Zr0.1Ti0.9O3 (BCTZ) ceramics, forming ceramic-metal piezoelectric composites22, in which the optimal matching load of PEH reduced from 400.0 to 300.0 kΩ, and the short-circuit current increased by approximately 30%. Similarly, An et al. improved current output by 42% in BCTZ PEH by using low-resistivity BaSnO3 to substitute BaZrO323. However, incorporating conductive phases to reduce resistivity often severely compromises piezoelectric properties. This performance trade-off fundamentally limits the practical viability of such approaches.

The aforementioned limitations reveal a fundamental dilemma: While phase boundary engineering approaches the physical ceiling for piezoelectric enhancement, resistivity reduction via conductive phases incurs unacceptable compromises in piezoelectric properties. To transcend this trade-off, we propose a co-design paradigm simultaneously manipulating polarization mechanisms and charge transport through dual-functional modifications that both construct phase boundaries to enhance piezoelectric coefficients and reduce resistivity via narrow-bandgap engineering, as schematically demonstrated in Fig. 1d. Such synergistic control of microstructure and electronic structure reconciles competing performance metrics by elevating poling efficiency through phase boundary design while lowering charge activation barriers via bandgap reduction. This strategy is implemented through BiFeO3 doping in the (K0.5Na0.5)(Nb0.96Sb0.04)O3-0.03(Bi0.5Na0.5)0.85(Li0.5Nd0.5)0.15ZrO3 (KNNS-BNLNdZ-xBF) system, which has been extensively demonstrated to exhibit exceptional piezoelectric properties, with a d33 ranging from 400 to 520 pC/N and rhombohedral-tetragonal (R-T) phase boundaries at room temperature24–26. BF acts as an effective phase structure modulator, establishing new phase boundaries without compromising the Curie temperature27,28. Its narrow bandgap (2.7 eV) enables simultaneous piezoelectric enhancement and resistivity reduction in KNNS-BNLNdZ ceramics29–31. The results demonstrated that the KNNS-BNLNdZ-0.6BF (0.6-KNN) system simultaneously achieves a remarkably high d33 of 470 pC/N and a low resistivity of 3 × 1010 Ω·cm. These improvements further lead to exceptional output performance in BF-doped KNN PEHs, compared to the undoped sample (0.0-KNN).

Results

Chemical modification designed to achieve high piezoelectricity alongside low resistivity

All the ceramics exhibit a typical perovskite structure without detectable impurity phases, indicating that BF has been successfully incorporated into the KNN lattice (Supplementary Fig. S1). A primary objective of introducing BF is to shift the rhombohedral-orthorhombic (R-O) and orthorhombic-tetragonal (O–T) phase transition temperatures of KNN towards room temperature. To assess the effectiveness of BF doping for this phase engineering, the temperature dependence of the dielectric permittivity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon }_{33}^{T}$$\end{document}ε33T/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon }_{0}$$\end{document}ε0, abbreviated as εr) is presented in Supplementary Fig. S2. As the BF content increases from 0.0 to 0.6 mol%, the O–T transition temperature (TO-T) of the KNNS-BNLNdZ-xBF ceramics decreases from approximately 70 °C to near room temperature. Concurrently, the temperature of the R-O phase transition (TR-O) gradually increases, finally converges with that of the TO-T. This convergence leads to the coexistence of R and T phases near room temperature in the 0.6-KNN sample, resulting in an R–T phase boundary. With further increases in the BF content, the temperature of the R-T phase transition (TR-T) ultimately shifts below room temperature. This intricate phase evolution is further corroborated by the phase diagram (Supplementary Fig. S3) and Rietveld refinement of X-ray diffraction data (Supplementary Fig. S4 and Table S1). The observed shift in phase transition temperatures can be attributed to the unequal substitution of BF ions, which generates localized stress fields. This localized stress dynamically modifies the phase boundary, consistent with observations in other KNN-based ceramics13,32,33.

Bipolar polarization (P-E) and strain (S-E) hysteresis loops of the KNNS-BNLNdZ-xBF ceramics were measured at room temperature and 1 Hz (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. S5a, c). All samples exhibit symmetrical and saturated P-E loops, confirming sufficient ferroelectric domain switching. The maximum polarization (Pmax) and remnant polarization (Pr) show a tendency to increase and then decrease with increasing x, with both values reaching a maximum in the 0.6-KNN sample. The coercive field (Ec) is minimized at this composition, while both the positive strain (Spos) and negative strain (Sneg) reach their maximum values in the 0.6-KNN sample. These observations are significant because Pr characterizes the residual polarization after field removal, Sneg arises primarily from non-180° domain reorientation, and Spos originates from the combined intrinsic piezoelectric lattice strain and extrinsic reversible domain switching strain, all of which collectively reflect domain dynamics28,34. Therefore, the concurrent minimization of Ec and maximization of Spos/Sneg in the 0.6-KNN sample collectively indicate that the phase engineering significantly enhances domain activity. To evaluate the impact of BF doping on charge transport efficiency, we assessed the resistivity and impedance of 0.0-KNN and 0.6-KNN specimens, investigating the effects of BF doping on bulk resistance in KNNS-BNLNdZ-xBF ceramics. At room temperature, the 0.6-KNN sample exhibits significantly lower AC impedance (Z) and DC resistivity (ρ) than the 0.0-KNN sample (Fig. 2e, f). Additionally, the ρ of both 0.0-KNN and 0.6-KNN samples was measured from 300 to 400 °C. Both exhibit Arrhenius behavior, with activation energies (Ea) derived from the Arrhenius equation shown in the inset of Fig. 2f. The Ea for the 0.0-KNN sample is 1.01 eV, consistent with values reported for oxygen vacancy migration in perovskite oxides42,43. Owing to BF doping, the Ea of the 0.6-KNN sample decreases to 0.70 eV. This reduction implies an increased concentration of oxygen vacancies in KNNS-BNLNdZ-xBF. Further insight was gained using AC impedance spectroscopy, a powerful tool for analyzing bulk defect structures44. As the characteristic complex impedance semicircle is resolvable only at elevated temperatures, measurements were performed at 380 °C (Fig. 2g). Typical Cole-Cole-type semicircular arcs observed in the impedance spectra indicate a point-defect-dominated conduction mechanism at elevated temperatures. Notably, the semicircle size decreases significantly with BF doping, reflecting reduced bulk impedance due to enhanced carrier mobility45–47. The experimental Cole-Cole plots were fitted using two series-connected equivalent circuits, where R (resistance) and CPE (constant phase element) represent the contributions of grain and grain boundary, respectively48. The fitting results are presented in Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table S3. The grain resistance (Rg) plummets from 376.0 kΩ (0.0-KNN) to 38.6 kΩ (0.6-KNN), a ~ 90% decrease, while the grain-boundary resistance (Rgb) drops from 192.0 kΩ to 40.2 kΩ, a ~ 79 % reduction. The more dramatic decline in Rg is attributed to BF-induced lattice charge imbalance, which generates charge-compensating oxygen vacancies predominantly within the grains.

To further confirm this speculation, the oxygen (O) elements in the 0.0-KNN and 0.6-KNN samples were examined using XPS spectra (Supplementary Fig. S7). The spectra can be fitted with three distinct peaks within the binding energy range of 528–534 eV, which are assigned to lattice oxygen (OLattice), oxygen vacancies (ODefect), and the adsorbed oxygen (O(-OH)). It is evident that the concentration of oxygen vacancies significantly increases with BF doping, thereby further corroborating our hypothesis of the decreased Ea and Rg. Fig. 2h compares the ρ of the 0.6-KNN sample with other KNN-based systems reported in the literature. The 0.6-KNN ceramics exhibit significantly lower ρ than these KNN lead-free counterparts49–54. In summary, as shown in Fig. 2i, BF incorporation into the KNNS-BNLNdZ system enables the 0.6-KNN ceramic to simultaneously achieve a high piezoelectric coefficient and a low resistivity, which represent a 70% increase in d33 and a 70% reduction in ρ compared to the 0.0-KNN ceramic.

Revealing the structural origins of high piezoelectricity alongside low resistivity

To elucidate the structural origins of the high piezoelectric response in the 0.6-KNN ceramic, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atomic-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) were employed to resolve its domain architectures and local atomic configurations. TEM images reveal a complex domain architecture in the 0.6-KNN ceramic that spans several distinct morphologies-striped, curved, watermark-like, short-segment, and tweed-like-together with embedded nanodomains (Fig. 3a–c). Notably, finer nanodomains (10–20 nm) are ubiquitously nested within the larger striped variants (80–100 nm), forming a self-similar, fractal-like hierarchy. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-.field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) was employed to resolve the atomic structure along the [100] axis. The distribution of the polarization vector was determined by quantifying the relative displacement of A-site cations relative to B-site cations using 2D Gaussian fitting. These quantitative displacement measurements directly reveal the local polarization states. The resulting polarization vectors, represented by yellow arrows, are superimposed on the atomic-resolution image in Fig. 3d. Fig. 3d1 shows a [100]-projected schematic of the unit cell resolving the perovskite structure, while Fig. 3d2 defines the arrow directions distinguishing the R and T phases. Therefore, the coexistence of R and T phases within short-range disordered regions facilitates rotation of the polarization vector. This smooth rotation suppresses anisotropy and enables facile polarization switching—a well-established mechanism for enhancing piezoelectric performance16. Fig. 3e, f present angular and displacement heat maps of polarization vectors along the [100] direction, respectively. Their statistical distributions are quantitatively summarized in the accompanying histograms (Fig. 3g). The results show that the coexistence of R and T phases features multiplexity of polarization angles, which the homogeneous angular distribution spanning from 100° to 200° signifies a progressive nanoscale phase transition, fostering a proliferation of nano-domains and facilitating the facile switching of local polar domains57,58. Moreover, the average polarization displacement reaches 18 pm, underscoring an exceptional piezoelectric response. In short, the multi-phase coexistence structure brings more polarization directions and multiscale nanodomains, reduces the polarization anisotropy and the energy of the domain wall, increasing the polarization intensity, which collectively underpins the exceptionally high d33 in 0.6-KNN ceramic.

To clarify the origin of reduced resistivity, we examined the band structure of BF-doped KNN. As BF is a narrow-bandgap semiconductor, its incorporation into the insulating KNN matrix narrows the overall bandgap. This is confirmed by UV-visible absorption spectra (Supplementary Fig. S8a), which show a pronounced red shift of the absorption edge. Tauc-plot analyses (Supplementary Fig. S8b) quantify this reduction, yielding a direct bandgap of 3.14 eV for 0.0-KNN and 3.05 eV for 0.6-KNN (Fig. 3h). The ~90.00 meV bandgap reduction upon BF incorporation directly explains the observed resistivity decrease59. To elucidate the underlying mechanism of this bandgap tuning, first-principles calculations were employed to investigate how BF incorporation modifies the electronic structure of the representative KNbO3 (KN) system. Eight inequivalent K positions relative to the Fe dopant were considered (Supplementary Fig. S9). The total densities of states for KN, BF, and the seven BF-substituted models are compared in Supplementary Fig. S10, while the K2 configuration is highlighted in Fig. 3i. Irrespective of the substitution site, the average bandgap narrows relative to the pristine host (Fig. 3j). Notably, when K sits midway between the upper and lower cell edges (K2), a sub-band emerges inside the conduction band of KNBF, depressing the conduction-band minimum and shrinking the gap to 1.06 eV. This computational result aligns with the experimentally observed, pronounced bandgap narrowing upon BF doping, which directly lowers the resistivity of KNN ceramics.

Ultrahigh Power generation performance

To intuitively evaluate the energy-harvesting capabilities of 0.0-KNN and 0.6-KNN ceramics, cantilever-beam PEHs were fabricated, and a schematic diagram of the test system is shown in Fig. 4a. Measurements were carried out at the resonant frequency (89 Hz, Supplementary Fig. S11) of the cantilever beam and 1.0 g acceleration, and all the following tests were performed at the 89 Hz. The PEH fabricated with 0.6-KNN delivers a peak-to-peak short-circuit current of 30.0 µA (corresponding to a current density of 50 µA/cm2), nearly doubling the 18.0 µA obtained from the 0.0-KNN device, as depicted in Fig. 4b. As shown in Fig. 4c, d, the output current (Iout) decreases monotonically with increasing load resistance, while the output power initially rises, peaks at the optimal load (impedance matching point), and subsequently declines. Under matched impedance conditions, the 0.6-KNN PEH demonstrates superior power generation performance, achieving an optimal output power of 41 µW with a corresponding power density of ~860 µW/cm3 and marking a 90% enhancement over the 0.0-KNN PEH. Figure 4f schematically depicts the self-powered charge-discharge platform for evaluating PEH performance. When powering the system, the 0.6-KNN PEH charged a 47 μF commercial capacitor to 4.5 V in 40 s – nearly twice the 2.5 V achieved by the 0.0-KNN PEH under identical conditions. Fig. 4g further shows that the 0.6-KNN PEH responds linearly to vibration acceleration: it short-circuits current density rises from 20 µA/cm2 at 0.5 g to 50, 70, and 115 µA/cm2 at 1.0 g, 1.5 g, and 2.0 g, respectively. These demonstrations underscore the compelling value of 0.6-KNN PEHs for next-generation, high-performance lead-free energy harvesters in self-powered microelectronic systems.

In summary, a strategy to simultaneously increase ISC and output power in the KNN PEH is established. This strategy shifts the TR-O and TO-T of the KNN system to room temperature, facilitating the formation of a multiphase boundary while narrowing the bandgap via controlled BF incorporation, thus a high d33 of 470 pC/N and a low resistivity of 3 × 1010 Ω·cm are obtained. Continuous rotation of polarization vectors, promoted by multi-directional polar states, together with high-density multiscale nanodomains, suppresses polarization anisotropy and domain-wall energy, collectively amplifying the piezoelectric response. Concurrently, the narrow-bandgap BF dopant lowers the conduction-band minimum, further diminishing resistivity. This dual mechanism synergy drives a paradigm shift in PEH performance. This work offers a clear materials-design paradigm—phase boundary coupled with bandgap engineering—that paves the way for next-generation, high-performance lead-free energy harvesters.

Methods

Sample preparation

The piezoelectric ceramics with a chemical formula of (0.97-x)(K0.5Na0.5)(Nb0.96Sb0.04)O3-0.03(Bi0.5Na0.5)0.85(Li0.5Nd0.5)0.15ZrO3-xBiFeO3 (abbreviated as KNNS-BNLNdZ-xBF, x = 0–0.8%, molar ratio) were prepared by the conventional solid-state reaction method. Raw chemicals Na2CO3 (99.80%, Sinopharm, China), K2CO3 (99.00%, Sinopharm, China), Li2CO3 (98.00%, Sinopharm, China), Nb2O5 (99.99%, Sinopharm, China), Sb2O3 (99.00%, Sinopharm, China), ZrO2 (99.00%, Sinopharm, China), Bi2O3 (99.00%, Sinopharm, China), Nd2O3 (99.99%, Sinopharm, China) and Fe2O3 (99.00%, Sinopharm, China) were weighed stoichiometrically. The raw materials were first mixed with zirconia balls and ethanol in a planetary ball mill for 24 h. The resulting powder was then dried and calcined at 850 °C for 6 h. Following this, the calcined powder was subjected to a second milling process for an additional 12 h under the same conditions as the initial milling. The dried powder was ground and granulated by adding 8.0 mol% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and then pressed into a mold with a diameter of 10 mm under a uniaxial pressure of 10 MPa. The green bodies were held at 600 °C for 12 h to remove PVA, and then the deboned ceramic pieces were placed on an alumina crucible lid for sintering at 1085 °C for 3 h.

Structure characterizations

Structural characterization was performed using a Bragg-Brentano geometry X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany). Detailed crystallographic information was obtained through Rietveld refinement analysis. The UV-Vis diffuse reflectance spectra were recorded using an ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (UV-3600 plus, Shimadzu, Japan). The valence states of the elements were identified using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Observations of the sub-nanometer scale domain characterization were conducted using a spherical aberration-corrected bright-field transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Tecnai F30, FEI). Atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) images were obtained using an atomic-resolution scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM; Titan Themis G2, FEI). The accurate polarization vectors within the STEM images were determined through 2D Gaussian peak fitting.

Properties measurement

Before testing the electrical properties, all ceramic samples were ground to a thickness of approximately 1 mm. Subsequently, platinum electrodes were sputtered onto the samples’ surface, followed by a stress-free annealing process at 400 °C. The temperature-dependent dielectric permittivity (εr) was measured over a temperature range from −100 °C to 450 °C using a precision LCR meter (Agilent E4980A, Santa Clara) coupled with an automatic temperature controller. The polarization-electric field hysteresis loops and bipolar strain curves were measured using the ferroelectric tester (aixACCT, TF Analyzer 2000E). The poling process was conducted at a direct current electric field of 40 kV/cm and 30 °C for 30 min in a silicone oil bath. After a 24-h aging period, the piezoelectric charge coefficient (d33) was determined using a commercial piezoelectric meter (ZJ-6A system, Institute of Acoustics, Academic Sinica, China). Impedance spectroscopy of the samples was conducted using a broadband impedance spectrometer (Novocontrol Technologies, Montabaur, Germany) equipped with a high-temperature heating system. The resistivity measurements were performed using an insulation resistance meter (TH2683A, Tonghui, China). The piezoelectric energy harvesting performance was evaluated using a self-built cantilever beam-type energy harvesting setup (Fig. 4a). For detailed information regarding this test system, please refer to our previous work62.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculation

A 2 × 2 × 2 supercell of KNbO3 was constructed with partial substitution of K by Bi and Nb by Fe, corresponding to a chemical composition of (K0.875Bi0.125)(Nb0.875Fe0.125)O3. This structure was studied using first-principles calculations, which were performed with the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP) based on density functional theory (DFT). The projector augmented wave (PAW) method was adopted, and the exchange-correlation interactions were treated within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional. A plane-wave energy cutoff of 500.0000 eV was used throughout all calculations. The Brillouin zone was sampled using a Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh of appropriate density depending on the size of the unit cell. Spin-polarized calculations were performed by setting the initial magnetic moments of Fe atoms to ± 4.0000 μB (for spin-up and spin-down, respectively), while the initial magnetic moments of all other atoms were set to 0.0000. After self-consistent calculations, the absolute value of the magnetic moment on Fe atoms converged to approximately ± 4.0820 μB.

Structural optimization was carried out using the conjugate-gradient method until all forces acting on atoms were below 0.0010 eV/Å. The on-site Coulomb interactions for the d-orbitals of Nb were treated using the DFT + U method with U = 3.0000 eV and J = 1.0000 eV, according to the Dudarev approach. For the density of states (DOS) analysis and bandgap estimation, the tetrahedron method with Blöchl corrections (ISMEAR = 0) and a Gaussian smearing width of 0.0500 eV was applied. All structures were fully relaxed before static calculations for electronic properties.
