Abstract
. We investigate the evolution of strictly convex hypersurfaces driven by the k k -Hessian curvature flow, subject to the second boundary condition. We first explore the translating solutions corresponding to this boundary value problem. Next, we establish the long-time existence of the flow and prove that it converges to a translating solution. To overcome the difficulty of driving boundary C 2 C^{2} estimates, we employ an orthogonal invariance technique. Using this method, we extend the results of Schnürer-Smoczyk [ 25 ] and Schnürer [ 23 ] from the second boundary value problem of Gauss curvature flow to k k -Hessian curvature flow.



1.
Introduction

The evolution of convex hypersurfaces under curvature flows is a fundamental topic in differential geometry, with significant progress made over the past few decades. Prominent examples include flows driven by the mean curvature
[
2
,
13
,
19
]
and the Gauss curvature
[
6
,
5
,
8
,
14
,
27
]
, which have yielded profound insights into the evolution and asymptotic behavior of hypersurfaces. For more results on curvature flows, see
[
4
,
3
,
12
,
13
]
and references therein. However, the analysis becomes intricate when boundary conditions are involved. Various boundary value problems for curvature flows, including Dirichlet and Neumann conditions, have been explored
[
1
,
20
,
24
]
. Second boundary condition, corresponding to prescribed gradient images, naturally arises in optimal transport
[
10
,
11
,
22
]
and has been studied in relation to Hessian equations
[
21
,
26
,
28
,
29
]
as well as curvature equations
[
7
,
30
]
. Nonlinear parabolic equations with second boundary conditions have also been addressed
[
15
,
31
]
.

Schnürer and Smoczyk
[
25
]
studied the second boundary value problem for Hessian flow equation:

(1.1)

u_{t}=\log F(D^{2}u)-\log g(x,u,Du),\quad\text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,T),\\

subject to second boundary condition
Du(\Omega)=\Omega^{*}
and initial data
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}
. Here
F
is a Hessian function of a special class, and
g(x,u,Du)
is a positive function with
g_{z}>0
. It yields the logarithmic Gauss curvature flow:

u_{t}=\log\det u_{ij}-\frac{n+2}{2}\log(1+|Du|^{2})-\log g_{1}(x,u).

which describes the evolution of a hypersurface as graph
u
, with the ”vertical” velocity equals to the difference of the logarithms of the actual Gauss curvature for
u
and the prescribed Gauss curvature
g_{1}(x,u)
.
They established the existence of smooth, strictly convex solutions that converge to a solution
u_{\infty}
of the elliptic second boundary value problem

(1.2)

\begin{cases}F(D^{2}u_{\infty})=g(x,u_{\infty},Du_{\infty}),\quad\text{in}\ \ \Omega,\\
Du(\Omega)=\Omega^{*}.\end{cases}

Here, (
1.2
) includes the prescribed Gauss curvature equation:

\frac{\det u_{ij}}{(1+|Du|^{2})^{\frac{n+2}{2}}}=g_{1}(x,u).

Schnürer
[
23
]
further considered the second boundary value problem for curvature flow:

(1.3)

\begin{cases}X_{t}=-(\log F-\log f)\nu\quad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,T),\\
\nu(M(t))=\nu(M(0)),&\\
X|_{t=0}=X_{0},\quad&\text{in}\ \Omega,\end{cases}

where
X=(x,u(x,t))
is a family of convex graphic hypersurfaces defined in bounded domain
\Omega
, and
M(t)=\text{graph}(-u)|_{\Omega}
. Since the unit normal
\nu
acts as the Gauss map,
\nu(M(t))=\nu(M(0))
is equivalent to a second boundary value condition
Du(\Omega)=Du_{0}(\Omega)=\Omega^{*}
. Schnürer proved that the solution converge to translating solutions. In this flow, the hypersurface
X
evolves such that its normal velocity is determined by the logarithmic difference of a curvature function
F
, from a special class, and a positive function
f
, where
F
includes, in particular, the Gauss curvature. In related work, Huang
[
15
]
investigated the second boundary problem for Lagrangian mean curvature flow, establishing flow convergence.

This paper aims to investigate the second boundary value problem for a more general curvature flow: the
k
-Hessian curvature flow, extending the results of
[
23
,
25
]
to a broader class of curvature flows. While the entire Hessian curvature flows in Minkowski space have been studied in
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
, these flows are not under second boundary conditions.
We consider the second boundary value problem for the evolution of strictly convex hypersurfaces
M_{u}(x,t)
with normal velocity given by the
k
Hessian curvature

(1.4)

\frac{\partial X}{\partial t}=\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}(\kappa[M_{u}])N,\quad\text{in}\ \Omega,

where
1\leq k\leq n
,
N
is the upward unit normal vector of
X(x,t)
.
The convex graphic hypersurfaces
M_{u}(x,t)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is described by the embeddings
X:\Omega\times[0,T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
as

M_{u}(x,t)=\left\{X(x,t)=(x,u(x,t)):\,(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T)\right\}.

Here,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a strictly convex bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
and
\kappa[M_{u}]=(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\cdots,\kappa_{n})
denotes the principal curvatures of
M_{u}
. The Hessian curvature
\sigma_{k}
is defined by the
k
-th elementary symmetric polynomial in the principal curvatures:

\sigma_{k}(\kappa)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}\kappa_{i_{1}}\kappa_{i_{2}}\cdots\kappa_{i_{k}}.

Thus, the graph function
u(x,t)
of convex hypersurface
X(x,t)
, evolving by (
1.4
), satisfies the following evolution equation:

(1.5)

\dot{u}=\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}(\kappa[M_{u}]),

where
\dot{u}=u_{t}
denotes the time derivative of
u
and
Du
represents the gradient with respect to the spatial variable
x
.

We consider the evolution starting from the initial hypersurface
M_{u}(x,0)
, given by

X(x,0)=X_{0}(x),

and subject to the second boundary condition

(1.6)

Du(\Omega)=\Omega^{*}.

Here
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
are smooth unifromly convex domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Given
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
X_{0}
, we study the following curvature flow problem:

(1.7)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &X_{t}=\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}(\kappa[M_{u}])N,&\text{in }\Omega\times[0,T),\\
&Du(\Omega)=\Omega^{*},&\\
&X(x,0)=X_{0}(x),&\text{in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

The curvature flow problem (
1.7
) can be reformulated as the following second boundary value problem for the graph function
u(x,t)
:

(1.8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}=\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}(\kappa[M_{u}]),&\text{in }\Omega\times[0,T),\\
&Du(\Omega)=Du_{0}(\Omega)=\Omega^{*},&\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&\text{in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Our goal is to prove the existence of a smooth, strictly convex solution
u(x,t)
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. Moreover, as
t\to\infty
, the solution converges to a translating solution
u(x,t)=u_{\infty}(x)+at
of (
1.4
), where
u(x)
satisfies

(1.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}(\kappa[M_{u_{\infty}}])=\frac{a}{\sqrt{1+|Du_{\infty}|^{2}}},&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&Du(\Omega)=\Omega^{*}.&\end{aligned}\right.

We summarize our main result in the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
be bounded strictly convex domains with smooth boundaries in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, there exists a unique smooth, strictly convex graphical solution
u(x,t)
to (
1.8
) for all
t\geq 0
. Moreover, as
t\to\infty
,
u
converges to a translating solution with a specified velocity.

The
k
-Hessian curvature flow (
1.4
) in Theorem
1.1
, including the mean curvature flow (
k=1
) and the Gauss curvature flow (
k=n
), generalizes the results of Schnürer’s flow(
1.3
) to the broader class of
k
-Hessian curvatures
1\leq k\leq n
. In
[
17
]
, we studied
k
-Hessian curvature hypersurfaces with prescribed Gauss images, using a boundary
C^{2}
estimate technique based on orthogonal invariance to extend Urbas’ work
[
30
]
from the Gauss curvature equation to the
k
-Hessian curvature equation. In this paper, we apply an orthogonal invariance method to obtain boundary
C^{2}
estimates for the flow. A key technique, using the maximum principle on the initial surface, enables the derivation of a double tangential boundary derivative estimate. By adapting these boundary estimation techniques, we extend the study of
k
-Hessian curvature equation
[
17
]
to the corresponding
k
Hessian curvature flow.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe some geometric notations, define the support function and the Legendre transformation, derive the corresponding equations, and state other relevant concepts and lemmas that will be used in the subsequent sections, respectively. In Section, we discuss the translating solution for the flow. Section 4 and 5 provide the
C^{0}
and
w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}
estimates. Section 6 addresses the strict obliqueness of the boundary condition. In Sections 7, we establish the
C^{2}
estimates, including global and boundary
C^{2}
estimates, where a special tangential direction is constructed in double tangential estimate. Finally, in Section 8, we complete the proof of the main theorem, establishing the long-time existence and convergence of the flow.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Geometric notations

We begin by introducing the notations used in this paper, which mainly follows the conventions of
[
17
]
and
[
34
]
. Unless otherwise stated, we employ the Einstein summation convention with repeated Latin indices summed from
1
to
n
. The letter
C
denotes a positive constant that may vary in different contexts but remains independent of the essential parameters in our analysis.
We use both
u_{t}
and
\dot{u}
to represent the time derivative of
u
, i.e.

u_{t}=\dot{u}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}.

We denote the spatial derivatives of
u
as

u_{i}=D_{i}u=\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},\ u_{ij}=D_{ij}u=\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}},\ |Du|=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|D_{i}u|^{2}}.

The upward-pointing unit normal vector to
X
is given by

N(X)=\frac{(-Du,1)}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}.

In the standard coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the induced metric
g_{ij}
and its inverse
g^{ij}
on
M_{u}
are

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+D_{i}uD_{j}u

and

g^{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{D_{i}uD_{j}u}{1+|Du|^{2}}.

where
1\leq i,j\leq n
.
The second fundamental form
h_{ij}
of
M_{u}
is

h_{ij}=\frac{D_{ij}u}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}.

The principal curvatures
\kappa
of
M_{u}
are the eigenvalues of the Weingarten matrix
(h^{j}_{i})=(h_{ik}g^{kj})
. According to
[
9
]
, these eigenvalues are the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix

A=(a_{ij})=\left(\frac{1}{w}b^{ik}(D_{kl}u)b^{lj}\right),

where
w=\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}
and

b^{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{D_{i}uD_{j}u}{w(1+w)}

is the square root of
g^{ij}
. Let
b_{ij}
be the inverse of
b^{ij}
, given by

b_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\frac{D_{i}uD_{j}u}{1+w}.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the space of symmetric
n\times n
matrices, and define

\mathcal{S}_{+}=\{A\in\mathcal{S}:\lambda(A)\in\Gamma_{n}\},

where
\Gamma_{n}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lambda_{i}>0\text{ for all }i\}
is the convex cone, and
\lambda(A)=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
represents the eigenvalues of
A
. Define the function
F
by

F(A)=F\left(\frac{1}{w}b^{ik}(D_{kl}u)b^{lj}\right)=\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}(\lambda(A)),\quad A\in\mathcal{S}_{+}.

Note that the function
F
is well-defined for strictly convex hypersurfaces.
Set

F^{ij}(A)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial a_{ij}}(A),\quad F^{ij,kl}=\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial a_{ij}\partial a_{kl}}.

Thus, using above notations, evolution
(
1.5
)
can be rewritten as

(2.1)

u_{t}=\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}F\left(\frac{1}{w}b^{ik}(D_{kl}u)b^{lj}\right)=wF.

2.2.
Support function

The support function
v
for the convex hypersurface
X
is defined as

v:=-\langle X,N\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\left(\sum_{i}x_{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}-u\right).

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard Euclidean inner product.

Let
\{\textbf{e}_{1},\cdots,\textbf{e}_{n}\}
be an orthonormal frame on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and let
\nabla
be the standard Levi-Civita connection of
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.
Then the spherical Hessian of
v
is

(2.2)

\Lambda_{ij}=\nabla_{ij}v+v\delta_{ij},

where
\nabla_{i}v,\nabla_{ij}v
are the first and second covariant derivatives with respect to the standard spherical metric.
From convex geometry theory, we have the following

\nabla_{i}v=-\langle X,\textbf{e}_{i}\rangle,\ \ X=-\sum_{i}(\nabla_{i}v)\textbf{e}_{i}-vN.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle g_{ij}=\sum_{k}\Lambda_{ik}\Lambda_{kj},\ \ h_{ij}=\Lambda_{ij}.

This implies that the eigenvalues of the spherical Hessian are the curvature radii of
M_{u}
. Specifically,
if the principal curvatures of
M_{u}
are
(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}),
then the eigenvalues of the spherical Hessian
are
\left(\lambda_{1}^{-1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}^{-1}\right).

Therefore, flow equation (
1.4
) can be written as

(2.3)

v_{t}=-\frac{1}{F^{*}(\Lambda)},\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+},

where
F^{*}(\Lambda)=\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n-k}}(\lambda(\Lambda))\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}
.

2.3.
Legendre transform

The Legendre transform of
u
, denoted by
u^{*}:\Omega^{*}\times[0,T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, is defined by

u^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}-u,\ \ y_{i}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}.

From the definition, we obtain the following properties:

\dot{u}^{*}=-\dot{u},\quad\frac{\partial u^{*}}{\partial y_{i}}=x_{i},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}u^{*}}{\partial y_{i}y_{j}}=u^{ij},

where
u^{ij}
is the inverse of Hessian
u_{ij}
and
y_{i}
is considered time-independent.
From the theory of convex bodies and boundary condition (
1.6
), we get

(2.4)

Du^{*}(\Omega^{*})=\Omega.

It is also known that

\left(\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\right)=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}u^{*}}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}\right)^{-1}.

Using the coordinate
(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})
, the first and the second fundamental forms can be rewritten as:

g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+y_{i}y_{j},\ \ h_{ij}=\frac{u^{*ij}}{\sqrt{1+|y|^{2}}},

where
\left(u^{*ij}\right)
denotes the inverse matrix of
(u^{*}_{ij})
and
|y|^{2}=\sum_{i}y_{i}^{2}
. Let
W
denote the Weingarten matrix of
M_{u},
then we have

(W^{-1})_{ij}=\sqrt{1+|y|^{2}}\sum_{k}g_{ik}u^{*}_{kj}.

Then, we obtain the evolution equation for
u^{*}
:

(u^{*})_{t}=-\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}\frac{1}{F^{*}(\lambda^{*})}=-w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1},

where
w^{*}=\sqrt{1+y^{2}}
, and

(2.5)

F^{*}(\lambda^{*})=F^{*}\Big(w^{*}\sum_{k,l}b^{*}_{ik}(D_{kl}u^{*})b^{*}_{lj}\Big)=\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n-k}}(\lambda^{*})\right)^{\frac{1}{k}},

where
\lambda_{i}^{*}=1/\kappa_{i}
.
Thus, we derive the following dual evolution problem for
u^{*}
:

(2.6)

\begin{cases}(u^{*})_{t}=-w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1},\ \text{in }\Omega^{*}\times[0,T),\\
Du^{*}(\Omega^{*})=\Omega,\\
u^{*}(y,0)=u^{*}_{0}(y),\ \ \quad\text{in}\ \Omega^{*},\ \ \end{cases}

where
u^{*}_{0}=x\cdot Du_{0}-u_{0}
.

2.4.
Second boundary conditions

For convenience of boundary estimate, we introduce a
defining function
h
for convex domain
\Omega^{*}
to reformulate the boundary condition
Du(\Omega)=\Omega^{*}
to

(2.7)

h(Du)=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ \partial\Omega.

Here
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth, strictly concave function such that

\Omega=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ h(p)>0\}\ \text{and}\ |Dh|\neq 0\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega^{*}.

Without loss of generality, we may assume

|Dh|=1\ \ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega^{*}.
Note that
h(Du)
is positive in
\Omega
and zero on
\partial\Omega
.

Similarly, we define the defining function
h^{*}
for
\Omega
and have

(2.8)

h^{*}(Du^{*})=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ \partial\Omega^{*}.

2.5.
Previous results

We recall some basic results and lemmas from previous work
[
17
]
that will be used in our
C^{2}
estimates.

Lemma 2.1
.

(Lemma 5.1 of
[
17
]
) Let
(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})
be the rectangular coordinate of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose
\nabla
is the standard Levi-Civita connection on the unit sphere. Let

e_{i}=\sum_{k}w^{*}\,b_{ik}^{*}\partial/\partial y_{k}.

Then
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\}
is an orthonormal frame on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Moreover, for any function
v
defined on some subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we get

(2.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l}w^{*}\,b_{ik}^{*}D_{kl}v\,b_{lj}^{*}=\nabla^{2}_{ij}\frac{v}{w^{*}}+\frac{v}{w^{*}}\delta_{ij}.

Proposition 2.2
.

(Proposition 5.2 of
[
17
]
) Given some boundary point
y_{0}\in\partial\Omega^{*}
and some tangential vector
\xi=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n})
of
\partial\Omega^{*}
at
y_{0}
. We can construct a vector field
\mathcal{T}=\sum_{m}\mathcal{T}_{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{m}}
near
y_{0}
. It satisfies

(2.10)

\mathcal{T}_{m}(y_{0})=\sqrt{1+|y_{0}|^{2}}\xi_{m}

and

(2.11)

|\mathcal{T}(y)|^{2}=\sum_{m}\mathcal{T}_{m}^{2}(y)\leq 1+|y|^{2}\text{ for any }y,

where the equality of (
2.11
) holds when
y=y_{0}
.

3.
Translating Solutions

In this section, we establish the existence of a translating solution to the
k
-Hessian curvature flow (
1.8
). A translating solution
u(x,t)
takes the form

u(x,t)=u(x)+at,

where
a
is a constant. Substituting this into the flow equation (
1.5
), we obtain the equation for
u(x)
:

(3.1)

\frac{a}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}=\sigma^{\frac{1}{k}}(\kappa(M_{u})).

By Theorem 1.3 in
[
17
]
, we know that there exists a unique solution
u(x)
to

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}(M_{u})=aN\cdot E,&\text{in }\Omega\\
&Du(\Omega)=\Omega^{*},&\end{aligned}\right.

where
a
is a constant determined by
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
,
N(X)=\frac{(-Du,1)}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}
, and
E=(0,\cdots,0,1)
.
This shows the existence of a solution to (
3.1
) subject to the boundary condition, thus establishing the existence of translating solution to (
1.9
).

Applying the Legendre transform to the function
u(x,t)
, we have

u^{*}(y,t)=x(t)\cdot Du(x,t)-u(x,t)=u^{*}(y)-at.

This leads to the equation for
u^{*}
:

\frac{a}{\sqrt{1+|y|^{2}}}=(F^{*})^{-1}

or equivalently,

F^{*}\left(w^{*}b^{*}_{ik}D_{kl}u^{*}b^{*}_{lj}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{1+|y|^{2}}}{a}.

Thus, we also have the existence of translating solution to the dual problem.

4.
C^{0}
Estimates

In this section, we first derive an estimate for
\dot{u}
, then obtain the
C^{0}
estimate for
u(x,t)
and
u^{*}(y,t)
. Let
\nu
and
\nu^{*}
denote the inner unit normal of
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
, respectively.

We first have the following inequality for the
\sigma_{k}
and
\sigma_{1}
curvatures:

\sigma_{k}^{1/k}(\kappa)=F\left(\frac{1}{w}b^{ik}(D_{kl}u)b^{lj}\right)\leq C\sigma_{1}\left(\frac{1}{w}b^{ik}(D_{kl}u)b^{lj}\right)+C.

Integrating this inequality over
\Omega
yields:

\int_{\Omega}F(a_{ij})dx\leq\int_{\Omega}D\left(\frac{Du}{w}\right)dx+C|\Omega|=C|\Omega|-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{D_{\nu}u}{w}\leq C,

where
a_{ij}=\frac{1}{w}b^{ik}(D_{kl}u)b^{lj}
and
\nu
is the unit normal to
\Omega
. Here, the boundedness of
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}

ensures that the integral is bounded:

(4.1)

\int_{\Omega}F(a_{ij})\,dx\leq C.

By the boundedness of
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
, we can derive the following
C^{1}
-estimate:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
be bounded domains. For a smooth convex solution
u(x,t)
of (
1.8
), the gradient of

u
satisfies:

|Du|\leq C,\ \text{for all}\ t\geq 0,

where
C
is a constant depending on
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
.

Integrating both sides of the graph equation
u_{t}=\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}F(a_{ij})
over
\Omega
, and
using (
4.1
) and the
C^{1}
bound, we obtain:

\int_{\Omega}u_{t}dx\leq C.

Next, integrating this inequality with respect to time from
0
to
t
, we have

\int_{\Omega}u(x,t)dx-\int_{\Omega}u(x,0)dx=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}u_{t}\,dx\,d\tau\leq Ct.

Thus, we conclude that:

\int_{\Omega}u(x,T)\leq CT+C.

Therefore, there exists a point
\tilde{x}\in\Omega
such that
u(\tilde{x},T)\leq C(\Omega,T)
. By
|Du|\leq C
, we get

u(x,T)\leq C(\Omega,T).

Since
u_{t}>0
, it follows that:

u(x,t)-u(x,0)>0.

Thus, we have

|u(x,T)|\leq C(\Omega,T).

Moreover, we can also derive the bound for
u^{*}=x\cdot Du-u
:

|u^{*}|\leq C(\Omega,\Omega^{*},T).

We have established
C^{0}
estimate for
u
and
u^{*}
.

5.
Estimate of
w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}

From the dual flow equation (
2.6
), we deduce the evolution of
(F^{*})^{-1}
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(F^{*})^{-1}

\displaystyle=-(F^{*})^{-2}F^{*ij}(w^{*}b^{*}_{ik}D_{kl}\frac{d}{dt}(u^{*})b^{*}_{lj})

\displaystyle=(F^{*})^{-2}(F^{*})^{ij}\cdot\left[w^{*}b^{*}_{ik}D_{kl}(w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1})b^{*}_{lj}\right],

where
w^{*}=\sqrt{1+|y|^{2}}
and
b^{*}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\frac{y_{i}y_{j}}{1+w^{*}}
.

This gives the evolution equation for
w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}
:

\frac{d}{dt}\left(w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}\right)=w^{*}(F^{*})^{-2}(F^{*})^{ij}\left[w^{*}b^{*}_{ik}D_{kl}(w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1})b_{ij}^{*}\right].

Thus,
w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}=-\dot{u}^{*}
satisfies a parabolic equation. Applying the maximum principle, we can conclude that
w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}
attains its maximum or minimum on the parabolic boundary. By the Hopf maximum principle, at the maximum or minimum point of
\dot{u}^{*}
, we have

\dot{u}^{*}_{\nu^{*}}<0,\quad(\text{or}>0),

unless
\dot{u}^{*}
is constant.

On the other hand, by the boundary condition
h^{*}(Du^{*})=0
, we get

(5.1)

h^{*}_{p_{k}}\dot{u}^{*}_{k}=0\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega.

According to Lemma 3.1 of
[
23
]
, as long as the solution exists, the boundary condition is strictly oblique, i.e.

(5.2)

\left<\nu(x),\nu^{*}(Du(x,t))\right>>0,\ \ x\in\partial\Omega,

where
\nu
and
\nu^{*}
denote the inner unit normal of
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
, respectively.
Moreover, by boundary condition
h(Du)=0
on
\partial\Omega
and
|Dh|=1
, we know that the interior unit normal of
\partial\Omega^{*}
is given by

\nu^{*}(p)=Dh(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})=\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{1}},\cdots,\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{n}}\right).

Thus we get a contradiction by the strict obliqueness of of
h^{*}_{p_{k}}
.
Therefore, we conclude the following estimate for
\dot{u}^{*}=-w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}
:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
u^{*}
be a smooth convex solution of the dual flow (
2.6
). As long as the smooth convex solution
u^{*}
exists, we always have

\min\left\{\min_{t=0}(-\dot{u}^{*}),0\right\}\leq-\dot{u}^{*}\leq\max\left\{\max_{t=0}(-\dot{u}^{*}),0\right\}.

6.
Estimates of Strict Obliqueness

In this section, we quantify the strict obliqueness of the boundary conditions. The proof integrates ideas from
[
28
]
and
[
29
]
. We adapt Lemma 4.2 in
[
17
]
from elliptic equations to the flow. A brief outline of the proof is provided below.

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose
\Omega
,
\Omega^{*}
are bounded strictly convex domains with smooth boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For a smooth solution of (
2.6
), we have the strict obliqueness estimate:

(6.1)

\langle\nu^{*}(Du(x,t)),\nu(x)\rangle\geq c_{0}>0,\ x\in\partial\Omega,

where
\nu
and
\nu^{*}
are the inner unit normal of
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
and
c_{0}
is some constant independent of time.

Proof.

Let
h(Du)
be the defining function of
\Omega^{*}
giving
\nu^{*}=Dh=(h_{p_{1}},\cdots,h_{p_{n}})
. Define

\chi(x,t)=\langle\nu^{*}(Du(x,t)),\nu(x)\rangle=h_{p_{k}}(Du(x,t))\nu_{k}(x),

where
h_{p_{k}}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_{k}}
and
\nu=(\nu_{1},\cdots,\nu_{n})
.
According to
[
29
]
, we can get
\chi\geq 0
and

(6.2)

\displaystyle\chi=\sqrt{u^{ij}\nu_{i}\nu_{j}(D_{kl}u)h_{p_{k}}h_{p_{l}}},\ \ \text{ on }\ \ \partial\Omega.

To show that there exists a positive lower bound for
\chi
for
(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times[0,T]
, it suffices to prove
u_{kl}h_{p_{k}}h_{p_{l}}
and
u^{ij}\nu_{i}\nu_{j}
have uniform positive lower bounds.

Suppose
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\partial\Omega\times[0,T]
is the point where
\chi
attains its minimum on
\partial\Omega
. Define

\phi(x,t)=\chi(x,t)+Ah(Du(x,t))=h_{p_{k}}(Du(x,t))\nu_{k}(x,t)+Ah(Du(x,t)),

where
A
is a positive sufficiently large constant to be determined. Following the argument in
[
29
]
, to prove that
u_{kl}h_{p_{k}}h_{p_{l}}
has a uniform positive lower bound, it is sufficient to show

(6.3)

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(x_{0},t_{0})\geq-C,

where
e_{n}
is the unit outward normal of
\partial\Omega
at
x_{0}
.

Suppose
h^{*}(x)
is the uniformly concave function of
\Omega
, giving
\nu=Dh^{*}
. Define

\chi^{*}(y,t)=\langle\nu^{*}(y),\nu(Du^{*}(y,t))\rangle,

then
\chi^{*}(y,t)=\chi(x,t)
, where
y=Du(x,t)
. Similarly, to prove the lower bound for
u^{ij}\nu_{i}\nu_{j}
, define

\phi^{*}(y,t)=\chi^{*}(y,t)+A^{*}h^{*}(Du^{*}(y,t)),

it suffices to show:

(6.4)

\displaystyle\phi^{*}_{n^{*}}(y_{0},t_{0})\geq-C,

where
(y_{0},t_{0})
is the minimum point and
e_{n}^{*}
is the unit outward normal of
\partial\Omega^{*}
at
y_{0}
.

Therefore, proving (
6.3
) and (
6.4
) yields the desired strict obliqueness estimate (
6.1
).

We first prove (
6.3
). Denote
G(Du,D^{2}u)
by:

G(Du,D^{2}u)=wF\left(\frac{1}{w}b^{ik}(D_{kl}u)b^{lj}\right)=w\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}(a_{ij}).

For the evolution equation
u_{t}=G=wF
, we define the operator
\mathcal{L}
as:

(6.5)

\mathcal{L}f=-\dot{f}+\left(G^{ij}D_{ij}f+G^{s}D_{s}f\right),

for any function
f
, where

G^{ij}=\frac{\partial G}{\partial u_{ij}}=wF^{kl}\frac{\partial a_{kl}}{\partial u_{ij}}=\sum_{p,q}F^{pq}b^{ip}b^{qj},

and

\displaystyle G^{s}=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial u_{s}}F+wF^{kl}\frac{\partial a_{kl}}{\partial u_{s}}=\frac{u_{s}}{w}F+wF^{ij}u_{kl}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{s}}\left(\frac{1}{w}b^{ik}b^{lj}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{u_{s}}{w}F-\frac{u_{s}}{w}F^{ij}a_{ij}-2wF^{ij}a_{ik}b^{lj}\frac{\partial b_{kl}}{\partial u_{s}}=-2wF^{ij}a_{ik}b^{lj}\frac{\partial b_{kl}}{\partial u_{s}}.

Differentiating
\dot{u}=wF=G
with respect to
x_{l}
yields

\dot{u}_{l}=G^{ij}u_{ijl}+G^{s}u_{sl}.

We also have

\phi_{s}=h_{p_{k}p_{l}}u_{sl}\nu_{k}+h_{p_{k}}\nu_{ks}+Ah_{p_{k}}u_{ks}

and

\phi_{ij}=u_{jm}(h_{p_{k}p_{l}p_{m}}\nu_{k}+Ah_{p_{l}p_{m}})+2G^{ij}h_{p_{k}p_{l}}u_{li}\nu_{kj}+G^{ij}h_{p_{k}}\nu_{kij}.

Thus we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\phi=

\displaystyle-\dot{\phi}+\left(G^{ij}D_{ij}\phi+G^{s}D_{s}\phi\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G^{ij}u_{il}u_{jm}(h_{p_{k}p_{l}p_{m}}\nu_{k}+Ah_{p_{l}p_{m}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2G^{ij}h_{p_{k}p_{l}}u_{li}\nu_{kj}+G^{ij}h_{p_{k}}\nu_{kij}+G^{s}h_{p_{k}}\nu_{ks}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(h_{p_{k}p_{l}p_{m}}\nu_{k}+Ah_{p_{l}p_{m}}+\delta_{lm})G^{ij}u_{il}u_{jm}+C\sum_{i}G^{ii}+C\sum_{s}G^{s}.

In Section 5, we have proved an estimate for
w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}
. Specifically, the estimate
w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}=wF=G
and bound of
|Du|
provide an upper bound for
G
and
\sum_{s}G^{s}
. Since
D^{2}h\leq-\theta I
for some positive constant
\theta
, we can choose sufficiently large
A
such that:

h_{p_{k}p_{l}p_{m}}\nu_{k}+Ah_{p_{l}p_{m}}+\delta_{lm}<0.

Using the fact that
\sum G^{ii}\geq C>0
, we derive

(6.6)

\mathcal{L}\phi\leq C\sum_{i}G^{ii}.

Next, we shall construct a barrier function and apply the maximum principle to establish the desired lower bound. The argument follows similarly to the proof of Lemma 4.2 in
[
17
]
, with the only modification of the auxiliary function.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
x_{0}
is the origin and the positive
x_{n}
-axis corresponds to the interior normal direction to
\partial\Omega

at the origin. Suppose near the origin, the boundary
\partial\Omega
is given by

(6.7)

x_{n}=\rho(x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha<n}\kappa^{b}_{\alpha}x^{2}_{\alpha}+O(|x^{\prime}|^{3}),

where
\kappa^{b}_{1},\kappa^{b}_{2},\cdots,\kappa^{b}_{n-1}
are the principal curvatures of
\partial\Omega
at the origin and
x^{\prime}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,

x_{n-1})
.
Denote a neighborhood of
x_{0}
in
\Omega
by

\Omega_{r}:=\{x\in\Omega:\rho(x^{\prime})<x_{n}<\rho(x^{\prime})+r^{2},|x^{\prime}|<r\},

where
r
is a small positive constant to be chosen.

Define a barrier function as follows:

(6.8)

\varphi(x)=-\rho(x^{\prime})+x_{n}+\delta|x^{\prime}|^{2}-Kx^{2}_{n},

where
\delta=\frac{1}{6}\min\{\kappa^{b}_{1},\kappa^{b}_{2},\cdots,\kappa^{b}_{n-1}\}
and
K
is some large undetermined positive constant. Note that
-\varphi
is strictly convex when
r
is sufficiently small. Similar to Lemma 4.2 of
[
17
]
, we have

(6.9)

\mathcal{L}\varphi\leq-C\sum G^{ii},\,\,x\in\Omega_{r},

by choosing
K
sufficiently large and
r
sufficiently small. Moreover, we have

\varphi\geq 0,\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega_{r}.

Now we consider the following auxiliary function

\Phi(x,t)=\phi(x,t)-\phi(x_{0},t_{0})+B\varphi(x),

where
B
is a constant to be determined.

By (
6.9
), we get

\mathcal{L}(\Phi(x))\leq C(1-B)\sum_{i}G^{ii}<0

for large
B
.

By letting
K
being large enough we conclude that

(6.10)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \triangle(\Phi(x,0))&\leq 0,\ \ &&x\in\Omega_{r},\\
\Phi(x,0))&\geq 0,\ \ &&x\in\partial\Omega_{r}.\end{aligned}\right.

By the maximum principle, we get

(6.11)

\Phi(x,0)|_{\Omega_{r}}\geq 0.

Then we can derive

(6.12)

\Phi(x,t)|_{\Omega_{r}\times[0,T)}\geq 0.

Combining with
\Phi(x_{0},t_{0})=0
, we have
\partial_{n}\Phi(x_{0})\geq 0
, which proves (
6.3
).

The proof for the second inequality
(
6.4
)
follows similarly. Here we use the linear operator
\mathcal{L}^{*}
:

\mathcal{L}^{*}f=-\dot{f}+(w^{*})^{2}(F^{*})^{-2}(F^{*})^{ij}b^{*}_{ik}(D_{kl}f)b^{*}_{lj},

for dual equation
u^{*}_{t}=-w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}
. We refer to
[
17
]
for rest of the proof.

Thus, we derived the strict obliqueness estimate (
6.1
).

∎

7.
C^{2}
estimates

In this section, we derive
C^{2}
a priori for
u^{*}
. We assume the existence of flow (
2.6
) over the time interval

[0,T]
. Notably, the estimate will not depend on
T
.

7.1.
Global
C^{2}
estimates

Let
v=-X\cdot N
be the support function of hypersurface
X(x,t)
,
where
N
is the unit normal. Suppose
\{\textbf{e}_{1},\cdots,\textbf{e}_{n}\}
is an orthonormal frame on
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
. The spherical Hessian is given by:

\Lambda_{ij}=\nabla_{ij}v+v\delta_{ij},

and satisfies the following properties:

\nabla_{k}\Lambda_{ij}=\nabla_{j}\Lambda_{ik}

and

\nabla_{jj}\Lambda_{ii}-\nabla_{ii}\Lambda_{jj}=-\Lambda_{jj}+\Lambda_{ii}.

Let
\tilde{\Omega}=P^{-1}(\Omega^{*})
. Then we get the evolution equation for
v

(7.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{t}=-(F^{*})^{-1}(\Lambda_{ij}),\ x\in\tilde{\Omega},\\
&v(\cdot,0)=u_{0}^{*}x_{n+1}.\end{aligned}\right.

Consider the problem

\sup_{\bar{\tilde{\Omega}}\times[0,T]\atop\eta\in T_{y}(\partial\Omega^{*}),|\eta|=1}\Lambda_{\eta\eta}(x).

Without loss of generality, we can assume at
(x_{0},t_{0})
,
\textbf{e}_{1}
are the maximum point and direction of the above problem. Moreover, we assume

\Lambda_{ij}
is a diagonal matrix at
x_{0}
.
Consequently, we have

\Lambda_{\eta\eta}\leq\Lambda_{11}(x_{0},t_{0}).

Now, consider the test function

\varphi=\Lambda_{11}.

We have

\varphi_{t}=(\Lambda_{11})_{t}=(v_{11}+v)_{t}=(v_{t})_{11}+v_{t}.

The first and second derivative of
v_{t}
are calculated as follows:

(v_{t})_{1}=(F^{*})^{-2}F^{*ij}\Lambda_{ij1}

and

\displaystyle(v_{t})_{11}

\displaystyle=(F^{*})^{-2}\left(F^{*}\right)^{ij}\Lambda_{ij11}+(F^{*})^{-2}(F^{*})^{ij,pq}\Lambda_{ij1}\Lambda_{pq1}-2(F^{*})^{-3}((F^{*})^{ij}\Lambda_{ij1})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(F^{*})^{-2}\left(F^{*ii}\Lambda_{11ii}-\sum_{i}F^{*ii}\Lambda_{11}+F^{*ii}\Lambda_{ii}\right)-2(F^{*})^{-3}(F^{*ii}\Lambda_{ii1})^{2}.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}

\displaystyle\leq(F^{*})^{-2}(F^{*ii}\varphi_{ii}-\varphi\sum_{i}F^{*ii}+F^{*})

\displaystyle-2(F^{*})^{-3}(F^{*ii}\Lambda_{ii1})^{2}-(F^{*})^{-1}.

Therefore,

(7.2)

\varphi_{t}-(F^{*})^{-2}F^{*ii}\varphi_{ii}\leq-\varphi(F^{*})^{-2}\sum F^{*ii}.

If the maximum value of
\varphi
is attained at an interior point
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\tilde{\Omega}\times(0,T]
, then from (
7.2
) implies a contradiction. We get the maximum is achieved on boundary. Hence, we conclude that:

\sup_{\bar{\tilde{\Omega}}\times[0,T]}\Lambda_{\eta\eta}\leq C+\sup_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}\times(0,T_{1}]\cup\tilde{\Omega}\times\{0\}}\Lambda_{\eta\eta},

Therefore, we obtain

\sup_{\bar{\Omega}^{*}\times[0,T]}\sum_{k,l}w^{*}b_{ik}^{*}D_{kl}^{2}u^{*}b_{jl}^{*}\leq C+\sup_{\partial\bar{\Omega}^{*}\times[0,T]}\sum_{k,l}w^{*}b_{ik}^{*}D_{kl}^{2}u^{*}b_{lj}^{*},

which implies

(7.3)

\sup_{\bar{\Omega}^{*}\times[0,T]}|D^{2}u^{*}|\leq C\left(1+\sup_{\partial\Omega^{*}\times[0,T]}|D^{2}u^{*}|\right).

7.2.
Boundary
C^{2}
estimates

We now proceed to estimate the second derivatives of
u^{*}
on
\partial\Omega^{*}\times(0,T]
.

For the dual problem, the boundary condition is given by:

(7.4)

h^{*}(Du^{*})=0,\quad x\in\partial\Omega^{*},

where
h^{*}
is the defining function of
\Omega
. Let
\beta=(h^{*}_{p_{1}},\ldots,h^{*}_{p_{n}})
. Denote
\nu
as the interior unit normal of
\partial\Omega
. Thus, we have
\beta(y)=\nu(Du^{*}(y))
. By the strictly obliqueness of the boundary condition, it follows that
\beta\cdot\nu^{*}>0
, where
\nu^{*}
is the interior unit normal of
\partial\Omega^{*}
.

Consider a fixed point on
\Omega^{*}\times[0,T]
, any direction at this point can be decomposed as

a\tau+b\beta,

where
\tau
is a tangent direction to
\partial\Omega^{*}
. Here the constants
a
and
b
are uniformly bounded due to the strict obliqueness estimates. Therefore, it suffices to estimate the following second derivatives:

u^{*}_{\tau\beta},\ u^{*}_{\beta\beta},\ u^{*}_{\tau\tau}.

\bullet

Estimate for
u^{*}_{\tau\beta}
:

Differentiating the boundary condition
h^{*}(Du^{*})=0,\quad x\in\partial\Omega^{*}
along the tangential direction
\tau
, we have

(7.5)

u^{*}_{\tau\beta}=h^{*}_{p_{i}}u*_{i\tau}=0\quad\text{on}\ \partial\Omega^{*}.

\bullet

Estimate for
u^{*}_{\beta\beta}
:

Denote
H^{*}=h^{*}(Du^{*})
. Note that
H^{*}
is positive in
\Omega^{*}
and zero on
\partial\Omega^{*}
. We compute its derivatives:

D_{i}H^{*}=h_{p_{k}}^{*}D_{ik}u^{*}

and

D_{ij}H^{*}=h^{*}_{p_{k}}D_{ijk}u^{*}+h_{p_{k}p_{l}}^{*}u^{*}_{ik}u^{*}_{jl}.

Recall the linear operator
\mathcal{L^{*}}
for dual equation:

\mathcal{L}^{*}f=-\dot{f}+w^{*}(F^{*})^{-2}(F^{*})^{ij}w^{*}b^{*}_{ik}(D_{kl}f)b^{*}_{lj},

for any function
f
.

Differentiating the equation
(u^{*})_{t}=-w^{*}(F^{*})^{-1}
with respect to
y_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{u}^{*}_{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{y_{k}}{w^{*}}(F^{*})^{-1}+w^{*}(F^{*})^{-2}F^{*ij}\frac{y_{k}}{w^{*}}b^{*}_{ip}D_{pq}u^{*}b_{qj}^{*}

\displaystyle+2w^{*}(F^{*})^{-2}w^{*}\frac{\partial b_{ip}^{*}}{\partial y_{k}}D_{pq}u^{*}b_{qj}^{*}F^{*ij}+w^{*}(F^{*})^{-2}F^{*ij}w^{*}b^{*}_{ip}D_{pqk}u^{*}b_{gj}^{*}.

Then we have

\mathcal{L}^{*}u_{k}^{*}=\frac{y_{k}}{w^{*}}(F^{*})^{-1}-w^{*}(F^{*})^{-2}F^{*ij}\frac{y_{k}}{w^{*}}b^{*}_{ip}u_{pq}^{*}b_{qj}^{*}-2w^{*}(F^{*})^{-2}F^{*ij}w^{*}\frac{\partial b_{ir}^{*}}{\partial y_{k}}(b^{*})^{rs}b^{*}_{sp}u_{pq}^{*}b_{qj}^{*}

Noting that

\frac{\partial b^{*}_{ir}}{\partial y_{k}}=\frac{y_{i}\delta_{kr}+y_{r}\delta_{ik}}{1+w^{*}}-\frac{y_{i}y_{r}}{(1+w^{*})^{2}}\frac{y_{k}}{w^{*}},\quad(b^{*})^{rs}=\delta_{rs}-\frac{y_{r}y_{s}}{w^{*}(1+w^{*})}

and
|\sum_{p,q}F^{*ij}w^{*}b^{*}_{sp}u^{*}_{pq}b_{qj}^{*}|
is uniformly bound, we derive that

(7.6)

\left|\mathcal{L}^{*}u_{k}^{*}\right|\leq C,

by the convexity of
u^{*}
.

Direct computations yield

\mathcal{L}^{*}H^{*}=-\dot{H}^{*}+w^{*}(F^{*})^{-2}F^{*ij}w^{*}b^{*}_{ik}(D_{kl}H^{*})b_{lj}^{*}

and

\dot{H}^{*}=h_{p_{k}}\dot{u}^{*}_{k}.

Thus

\mathcal{L}^{*}H^{*}=h_{p_{k}}\mathcal{L}^{*}u_{k}^{*}+\sum h^{*}_{p_{k}p_{l}}F^{*ij}w^{*}b^{*}_{ip}u^{*}_{pk}u^{*}_{ql}b_{qj}^{*}.

Utilizing the following identity

\sum_{p,q}F^{*ij}w^{*}b_{ip}^{*}u^{*}_{pk}u^{*}_{ql}b_{qj}^{*}=\sum_{p,q,s,n}F^{*ij}w^{*}b_{ip}^{*}u^{*}_{ps}b_{st}^{*}(b^{*})^{tk}(b^{*})^{lm}b^{*}_{mn}u^{*}_{nq}b_{qj}^{*},

we can derive

(7.7)

\left|\sum_{k,l,p,q}h^{*}_{p_{k}p_{l}}F^{*ij}w^{*}b_{ip}^{*}u^{*}_{pk}u^{*}_{ql}b_{qj}^{*}\right|\leq C\sum_{i}F^{*ii}\lambda_{i}^{2},

where
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}
are eigenvalues of the matrix
(\sum_{k,l}w^{*}b_{ik}^{*}u^{*}_{kl}b_{lj}^{*})
, which also corresponds the principle radii of curvature of
M_{u}
. Thus we obtain

\left|\mathcal{L}^{*}H^{*}\right|\leq C\left(1+\sum_{i}F^{*ii}\lambda_{i}^{2}\right).

Since
F^{*}=(\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n-k}})^{\frac{1}{k}}
, by
[
29
]
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we further get

\left|\mathcal{L}^{*}H^{*}\right|\leq C\left(1+(C(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon M)\sum_{i}F^{*ii}\right),

where
C(\varepsilon)
is a constant depending only on
\varepsilon
and

(7.8)

M=\sup_{y\in\Omega^{*}}\{\lambda_{1}(y),\cdots,\lambda_{n}(y)\}.

Since (
2.6
) is invariant under rotations in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we may assume the positive
y_{n}
-axis is the interior normal direction to
\partial\Omega^{*}
at
\hat{y}=(\hat{y}_{1},\hat{y}_{2},\cdots,\hat{y}_{n})
.
Near
y_{0}
, the boundary
\partial\Omega^{*}
is given by

\bar{y}_{n}=y_{n}-\hat{y}_{n}=\rho^{*}(y^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha<n}\kappa^{*}_{\alpha}(y_{\alpha}-\hat{y}_{\alpha})^{2}+O(|y^{\prime}|^{3}),

where
\kappa^{*}_{1},\kappa^{*}_{2},\cdots,\kappa^{*}_{n-1}
are the principal curvatures of
\partial\Omega^{*}
at
\hat{y}
and
y^{\prime}=(y_{1}-\hat{y}_{1},y_{2}-\hat{y}_{2},\cdots,y_{n-1}-\hat{y}_{n-1})
.
Let
\Omega^{*}_{r}
be a neighborhood of
y_{0}
in
\Omega^{*}
, defined as

\Omega^{*}_{r}:=\{y\in\Omega^{*}:\rho^{*}(y^{\prime})<\bar{y}_{n}<\rho^{*}(y^{\prime})+r^{2},|y^{\prime}|<r\},

where
r
is a small positive constant to be chosen. We now construct a barrier function
\varphi^{*}
defined as follows:

(7.9)

\varphi^{*}(y)=-\rho^{*}(y^{\prime})+\bar{y}_{n}+\delta^{*}|y^{\prime}|^{2}-K^{*}\bar{y}_{n}^{2},

where
\delta^{*}=\frac{1}{6}\min\{\kappa^{*}_{1},\kappa^{*}_{2},\cdots,\kappa^{*}_{n-1}\}
and
K^{*}
is some large, undetermined positive constant. The function
\varphi^{*}
is designed to satisfy the following properties:

\mathcal{L^{*}}\varphi^{*}\leq-C\sum_{i}F^{*ii}\quad\text{in}\ \Omega^{*}_{r}\ \ \text{ and }\quad\varphi^{*}\geq 0\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{r}^{*}.

For any boundary point
\hat{y}\in\partial\Omega^{*}
, we can define a neighborhood
\Omega^{*}_{r}
of
\hat{y}
and barrier function
\varphi^{*}
. Next, we construct the following test function:

\Phi=H^{*}-\frac{B}{2}(C(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon M)\varphi^{*}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{r}^{*},

where
B
) is a positive constant.

At
t=0,

\Phi(y,0)=H^{*}(y,0)-\frac{B}{2}(C(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon M)\varphi^{*}(y)\leq 0.

On the boundary
\partial\Omega^{*}
, it holds that:

\Phi\leq 0.

Moreover, we compute:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}\Phi

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{*}H^{*}-\frac{B}{2}(C(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon M)\mathcal{L}^{*}\varphi^{*}

\displaystyle\geq-C(C(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon M)\sum F^{*ii}+B(C(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon M)\sum F^{*ii}-C\sum F^{*ii}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

If
r
is chosen sufficiently small, we have
\Phi\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega_{r}^{*}
and
\Phi(y_{0})=0
. It follows that
\Phi
achieves its maximum value at
\hat{y}
. Consequently, at
\hat{y}
, we drive

D_{\nu^{*}}H^{*}\leq C\left(C(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon M\right).

Since
\hat{y}
is arbitrary, combining with (
7.5
), we conclude

(7.10)

0\leq D_{\beta\beta}u^{*}\leq C(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon M\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega^{*}.

\bullet

Estimate of
u^{*}_{\tau\tau}
:

In estimating the double tangential derivative
u^{*}_{\tau\tau}
, we encounter the additional negative terms that cannot be eliminated directly. To address this, we adopt a strategy similar to that in
[
17
]
for the elliptic curvature equation. By using the orthogonal invariance of the hypersurfaces, we are able to differentiate along specific tangential vector fields, which allows us to establish the estimate for
u^{*}_{\tau\tau}
.

Let
\{A_{s}\}
be a family of orthogonal matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, where
s\in[0,\varepsilon_{0}]
and
\varepsilon_{0}
is a small positive constant. Define

(7.11)

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(y)=PA_{s}^{-1}P^{-1}(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
P:\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the projection map and
P^{-1},A_{s}^{-1}
are inverse maps of
P,A_{s}
respectively.

Following Lemma 5.2 of
[
17
]
, we can prove

Lemma 7.1
.

Let

u_{s}^{*}(y,t)=u^{*}(\sigma_{s}(y),t)

and

(7.12)

\tilde{u}_{s}^{*}(y,t)=\frac{\sqrt{1+|y|^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+|\sigma_{s}(y)|^{2}}}u_{s}^{*}(y,t).

Then, the evolution equation for
u_{s}^{*}(y,t)
