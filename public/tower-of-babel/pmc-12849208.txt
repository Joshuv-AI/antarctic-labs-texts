Relatives’ experiences of end-of-life care and organ donation after controlled circulatory death: a national prospective multicenter study
Authors: Matthieu Le Dorze, Anne Renet, Romain Barthélémy, Virginie Souppart, Nancy Kentish-Barnes, Gerard Audibert, Gerard Audibert, Marie-Ange Azais, Dorothée Carpentier, Charles Cerf, Gaëlle Cheisson, Renaud Chouquer, Martin Cour, Vincent Degos, Philippe Gouin, Pierre-Grégoire Guinot, Marion Fresco, Florence Lallemant, Fabien Lambiotte, Benjamin Lebas, Anne-Claire Lukaszewicz, Julien Mayaux, Emmanuelle Mercier, Bruno Megarbane, Jérôme Morel, Laurent Muller, Erika Parmentier-Decrucq, Pierre Perez, Sébastien Prin, Benjamin Rohaut, Armine Rouhani, Jean-Christophe Venhard, Mathieu Willig, Hodane Yonis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe development of controlled donation after circulatory determination (cDCDD) - a promising strategy to improve access to transplantation - raises unique ethical, relational and communication challenges. We aimed to evaluate the psychological outcomes and lived experiences of relatives of potential cDCDD donors.MethodsWe conducted a prospective multicenter study in 32 French ICUs. Relatives of patients identified as potential cDCDD donors following a decision to withdraw life-sustaining measures (WLSM) were recruited. One relative per patient, preferentially the most involved in decision-making, was followed up by structured phone interviews at 3 and 6 months. Outcomes included PTSD symptoms (IES-R, primary endpoint), anxiety and depression (HADS), experience of dying and death (CAESAR), prolonged grief (PG-13), and a study-specific questionnaire on perceptions of end-of-life and organ donation (OD) processes. Two subgroups were defined: OD+, when WLSM was initiated while OD was still considered possible, and OD−, when OD was no longer considered at the time of WLSM.ResultsA total of 159 relatives were included (119 OD+, 40 OD−). Follow-up was completed in 118 relatives at 3 months and 109 at 6 months. At 3 months, one third reported PTSD symptoms, half reported anxiety, and one third depression, with persistence at 6 months for a substantial proportion. Around 10% described a distressing experience of dying and death, and fewer than 10% met criteria for prolonged grief disorder. Psychological outcomes did not differ between OD + and OD − groups. Most relatives felt involved in WLSM and OD discussions, though 80% perceived being asked to authorize donation. Emotional responses were ambivalent, combining reassurance and distress.ConclusionRelatives of potential cDCDD donors experience high levels of psychological distress, but OD discussions did not exacerbate burden. These findings support that cDCDD can be implemented without additional risk for families, while underscoring the need for improved shared decision-making, communication, and emotional support in the ICU.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT05041023 (First registration date: 2021-09-02).

Background

High quality end-of-life care and effective communication with relatives of dying patients in the intensive care units (ICUs) are essential to mitigate the risk of post-ICU burden, including post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, depression, and prolonged grief among bereaved relatives [1–3]. An increasing proportion of deaths in the ICU now occur following a decision to withdraw life-sustaining measures (WLSM) [4]. Supporting patients and their families during the decision-making and dying process is a core mission of ICU teams [5–9].

Organ donation (OD) is now increasingly considered a routine part of end-of-life care planning in the ICU [10–12]. Among the different OD pathways, controlled donation after the circulatory determination of death (cDCDD) is emerging as one of the most promising strategies to expand access to transplantation [13–16]. However, the development of cDCDD within the ICU setting raises multiple challenges – technical, logistical, legal, ethical, and relational [12, 15]. Ensuring that this end-of-life pathway evolves under conditions of transparency and trust requires a better understanding of how patients, relatives, and ICU healthcare professionals (HCPs) experience and perceive it [17–19].

To date, there is limited data on how families experience the dying of a loved one in the ICU when cDCDD is considered. Most existing studies have focused on identifying factors associated with relatives’ refusal of OD, rather than exploring their lived experience of the dying process itself [20, 21]. Few studies have investigated the potential impact of cDCDD on the psychological burden of relatives in the months following the patient’s death [22–29]. In parallel, some studies have explored public attitudes toward cDCDD, but these surveys primarily reflect general opinions rather than the lived experiences of families directly involved in the process [30–32]. To our knowledge, no study has specifically compared the experiences of relatives of patients who died after being identified as potential cDCDD donors, depending on whether OD ultimately occurred or not.

In this context, we conducted a national, multicenter, prospective study to explore how relatives perceived and experienced end-of-life care and communication in situations where cDCDD was part of the end-of-life care planning. Our aim was to identify both the challenges and the opportunities to improve support for families facing these highly sensitive circumstances. In France, where this study was conducted, organ donation is regulated under a soft opt-out system and cDCDD is authorized under a single national protocol [16].

Methods

From October 2021 to February 2024, we conducted a prospective, observational study in 32 ICUs across France. The study was approved by the French ethics committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes Île-de-France XI, IDRCB 2021-A00397-34).

Objective

The primary objective was to assess whether relatives of patients identified as potential cDCDD donors were at increased risk of developing post-traumatic stress symptoms when the OD was pursued (OD + group), as compared with situations in which OD did not ultimately proceed (OD − group). The primary outcome was the IES-R score at 3 months. Secondary outcomes included HADS and CAESAR scores at 3 months, understanding of end-of-life and OD processes, and the IES-R, HADS, and PG-13 scores at 6 months.

Recruitment

Relatives from ICU patients identified as potential cDCDD donors were recruited in 32 ICUs. Eligibility required that the patient die in the ICU following a WLSM decision, under the following three conditions: (1) a formal WLSM decision had been made and the relatives were informed and in agreement; (2) the patient was evaluated by the OD team and identified as a potential cDCDD donor; (3) the relatives were approached for consent and authorization to OD. Screening of eligible cases occurred during the family interview regarding OD. Both situations where OD was ultimately performed and those where it was not were included. Two subgroups were defined: (1) the OD- group included cases in which OD was no longer considered at the time of WLSM, due to one of the following reasons: medical unsuitability, patient refusal, relative’s opposition, legal barriers, logistical constraints, or hemodynamic instability; (2) the OD + group included cases in which WLSM was initiated while OD was still considered possible, regardless of whether donation ultimately occurred (e.g., due to prolonged warm ischemia or failure of normothermic regional perfusion).

For each potential cDCDD donor, a single relative was included. Among relatives, the invitation to participate in the study was preferentially addressed to the one most involved in the decision-making process. At the end of the family interview regarding OD, the study was presented by an ICU physician. They were told that their decision to participate or not would have no consequences on future discussions, OD processes, and EOL care. An information letter was given to the family members, who discussed among themselves and/or with the ICU team or the OD team. The participating relative was informed that he/she would be called by a psychologist/nurse at 3 and 6 months after the patient’s death and that he/she could interrupt participation at any time. At each call, the psychologist/nurse checked if the participant was willing to continue. At the end of the 3-month interview, some relatives were also invited to take part in a separate complementary qualitative study, which is reported elsewhere.

Study outcomes

Participating relatives were followed up by phone at two time points: 3 and 6 months after the patient’s death. All telephone interviews were conducted by the same psychologist (AR) and the same nurse (VS), with extensive interviewing experience. These structured interviews comprised a combination of validated psychometric tools and a study-specific questionnaire.

At 3 months, the follow-up included: 1- the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R), a valid and reliable screening tool for post-traumatic stress-disorder (PTSD), measuring subjective distress caused by traumatic events. Scores range from 0 (no symptoms) to 88 (severe symptoms), with a threshold of ≥ 26 indicating clinically significant symptoms [2, 25]; 2- the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), a valid and reliable tool to assess symptoms of anxiety and depression. Subscale scores ≥ 8 were considered to indicate clinically meaningful symptoms of anxiety and depression [1, 25, 33]; 3- the CAESAR scale, developed to assess the relatives’ experience with the dying and death of a family member in the ICU. Scores ≤ 59 were considered indicative of a distressing experience, scores 60–68 of a fair experience, and scores ≥ 69 of a good experience [3, 34]; (4) a structured questionnaire (appendix) of approximately 60 items exploring key domains such as the end-of-life support, communication about OD, understanding of decision-making processes, and the overall ICU experience.

At 6 months, the follow-up included: (1) the IES-R, as above; (2) the HADS, as above; (3) the Prolonged Grief Disorder-13 questionnaire (PG-13) scale, designed to assess symptoms of complicated grief. A score ≥ 30 was used to identify prolonged grief disorder [35–37].

Sample size calculation and statistical considerations

The sample size calculation was based on the primary hypothesis that the prevalence of post-traumatic stress symptoms, assessed with the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R) at 3 months, with a cutoff score ≥ 26 indicating clinically significant symptoms, would be significantly higher in the OD + group compared with the OD − group. Assuming a two-sided test with 5% types I error (α) and 80% power (1 − β), the estimated number of relatives required for analysis was 116. Anticipating a 30% loss to follow-up at 3 months, the total target sample size was set at 151 relatives over the entire inclusion period.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software. Quantitative variables were described as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as numbers (%). All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To explore factors associated with PTSD symptoms, we performed univariate and multivariable logistic analyses in two populations: all relatives assessed at 3 months and the OD + subgroup. PTSD was dichotomized using the validated IES-R threshold (≥ 26 vs. < 26). Twenty major questionnaire items were tested, and categorical variables were recoded into two levels to increase statistical power.

Results

Study population

Between October 2021 and February 2024, a total of 159 relatives were included across the 32 participating ICUs (Fig. 1): 119 in the OD + group and 40 in the OD- group. Recruitment was heterogeneous across participating ICUs: 4 centers did not include any relatives, whereas 14 centers enrolled ≥ 5 relatives each. Among the 119 OD + situations, 110 resulted in actual cDCDD donors. Donation could not proceed in one case because the agonal phase exceeded the 180-minute limit defined by the national procedure, and in eight cases due to excessive warm ischemia time related to NRP dysfunction or hypoperfusion. At 3 months, follow-up data were available for 118/159 (74%) relatives (89/119 (75%) in the OD + group, 29/40 in the OD − group). Among them, the median age was 48 years (IQR 37–60). Most participants were female (73%). The relationship to the deceased was most frequently as son/daughter (33%) or spouse/partner (32%), followed by siblings (15%) and parents (13%) (supplemental Table 1). Baseline characteristics of potential cDCDD donors and relatives did not differ between those with completed 3-month follow-up and those lost to follow-up. At 6 months, follow-up was completed for 109/159 (69%) relatives (82/119 (69%) in the OD + group, 27/40 (68%) in the OD − group).

Sociodemographic characteristics of patients are shown in Table 1. The main reason for ICU admission was cardiac arrest (46%). The median interval between ICU admission and WLSM decision was 6 days (IQR 4–15) in both groups. Time from WLSM to circulatory arrest was markedly shorter in the OD + group (22 [16–32] vs. 85 [21–308] minutes, p < 0.001). No tensions or conflicts were reported between relatives and the ICU team during end-of-life decision-making or the EOL and donation process (dedicated items in the standardized questionnaire administered during follow-up interviews).

Psychometric outcomes

Psychometric outcomes at 3 and 6- months are presented in Table 2. At 3 months, the prevalence of PTSD related symptoms (approximatively one third of relatives), anxiety (approximatively 50%), and depression (approximatively one third) was similar in the OD + and OD − groups. At 6 months, one in five relatives still presented PTSD related symptoms, one third reported anxiety, one in five depression, and fewer than 10% fulfilled criteria for prolonged grief disorder, with no differences between the two groups. Across the two populations (all relatives and OD + subgroup), none of the twenty questionnaire items tested in univariate analysis was significantly associated with PTSD symptoms (IES-R score ≥ 26), and no multivariable model met criteria for further interpretation.

Relatives’ perceptions of WLSM decision-making

Among the 118 relatives who completed the 3-month follow-up, 90% (n = 106) recalled that a WLSM decision had been made. The perceived median level of involvement in the decision-making process (0–10 scale) was 7 (IQR 3–10), reflecting a relatively high degree of participation, with no difference between the OD- and OD + groups. 65% (n = 76) reported that the final decision itself had been made by the ICU team alone. Regarding the timing of the WLSM decision, 57% judged it appropriate, 27% too late, and 8% too early. Most relatives (89%) considered the information provided about WLSM appropriate. The physician was identified as the main source of clear and appropriate information (82%), followed by the nurse (10%) (Supplemental Table 2).

Relatives’ perceptions of OD discussions

Table 3 summarizes relatives’ perceptions of OD discussions. These most often occurred during (36%) or after (42%) the WLSM announcement, and less frequently beforehand (11%). In most cases, donation was first mentioned by the ICU team (65%), less often by relatives (19%) or the OD team (14%). Discussions were generally perceived as normal, although more than half of relatives believed donation would not be feasible in this context, and 46% were unaware of the patient’s prior wishes. In such cases, views were shaped by the patient’s personality or character (36%), social values such as solidarity (19%), their own opinion (17%), and, less frequently, spiritual or religious references (4%). The purpose of the OD discussion was variably interpreted: 80% perceived it as a request for their authorization, while 17% considered it a verification of a prior opposition expressed by the patient. Most relatives reported strong involvement, adequate support from both their relatives and the HCPs, and appropriate information - particularly from the OD team. Emotional responses, however, were mixed, with 41% reporting negative feelings and 54% positive ones.

Relatives’ perceptions of the interval between WLSM decision and its implementation

Nearly 90% of relatives reported visiting their loved one during the interval between the WLSM decision and its implementation. This period was described as painful by 40%, but as constructive or acceptable by 60%, often characterized as “necessary”, “important”, “neutral”, or “reassuring”. Interactions with HCPs were generally perceived as meaningful, and communication during this time was positively evaluated (Supplemental Table 3).

Relatives’ perceptions of WLSM implementation

Three-quarters of relatives were present at WLSM, and 83% remained until death, with similar proportions across groups. Among the 27 relatives who were absent at withdrawal, 37% reported that other family members were present; this was described as “important” (26%) or “reassuring” (15%). Only 15% expressed regret about their absence. Table 4 summarizes relatives’ experiences during WLSM. Nearly 90% considered the number of HCPs in the room appropriate, and more than 80% described the atmosphere as calm. Two-thirds reported receiving support from HCPs during withdrawal, whereas one-third did not; among the latter, most (68%) felt missing such support. Presence at the bedside was generally perceived as reassuring (68%), though one-quarter found it stressful.

Relatives’ experiences of the organ procurement process (OD + group)

Only a small minority (13%) remained at the hospital during procurement. Although most relatives (85%) were subsequently informed that procurement had occurred, 15% were not. More than half (58%) saw the body again, most often to reflect or pray (63%), to say goodbye (48%), or to ensure that respect has been maintained (19%). Discussion

In this large, national multicenter study – the first to systematically investigate this population - we found that one third of bereaved relatives of potential cDCDD donors experienced significant PTSD-related symptoms at 3 months, with persistence at 6 months for a substantial proportion. At 3 months, half of the relatives experienced symptoms of anxiety and one third symptoms of depression, both of which remained frequent at 6 months. Approximately one in ten reported a distressing experience of dying and death, and fewer than one in ten met criteria for prolonged grief disorder.

Compared with previous studies, IES-R score and the proportion of relatives with PTSD-related symptoms in our cohort were lower than those reported in bereaved relatives of brain-dead organ donors [25] or of patients dying in the ICU [3, 34, 37]. Anxiety and depression were similar to those reported previously [3, 25, 34, 37]. Median CAESAR scores were comparable to those reported in relatives of ICU patients outside the context of OD, though fewer reported a distressing experience [34]. Complicated grief was markedly lower than that reported in bereaved relatives of brain-dead organ donors [25]. Several hypotheses may explain these findings. First, the dying process in DCDD is often more gradual, allowing time for preparation and participation in decision-making, whereas brain death is sudden and difficult to comprehend [38]. Second, relatives included likely represent a selected subgroup, as cDCDD requires specific eligibility and context free of conflicts over WLSM. Recruitment may have further accentuated this selection bias. Third, cDCDD programs develop only in units with supportive ethical climate, structured protocols, strong interprofessional collaboration, and careful communication [39]. These conditions may mitigate burdens. Importantly, psychological outcomes did not differ between OD- and OD + groups. Overall, our findings suggest that accompanying families through the entire process of end-of-life care and a potential organ donation in cDCDD does not appear to increase relatives’ psychological distress.

Beyond psychometric outcomes, our study sheds light on several important aspects of relatives’ experiences. First, most reported involvement in WLSM and OD discussions and felt well informed and supported. These findings suggest that ICU teams are engaging in shared decision-making around OD, even within a French opt-out system that does not legally require family authorization. Notably, 80% perceived that they were asked to authorize donation, reflecting families’ de facto decision-making role, especially when unaware of the patient’s prior wishes. This perception, often meaningful rather than burdensome [40], nonetheless raises ethical and communication challenges, underscoring the need to balance adherence to legal norms with recognition of the relational and emotional dynamics that shape how families understand and engage in organ donation decisions. Second, the emotional experience from WLSM to OD was often ambivalent [41], combining relief and reassurance with distress and emotional conflict, in line with prior studies [28, 29, 40]. One in four relatives perceived the interval between decision and implementation as too long, and 40% described it as painful, reflecting the strain of waiting in awareness of imminent death. For professionals, these findings confirm their concerns [18] and highlight the need for emotionally attuned communication, careful timing, and sustained support to help families navigate this challenging period. Third, presence at WLSM was frequent and supported by HCPs, helping families understand the dying process and find meaning [42]. Yet in our cohort, it was often experienced as stressful, underscoring the tension between benefits and emotional costs and the need for better preparation and support. Once again, our complementary qualitative approach helps to better understand families lived experience during EOL in this specific context. Interestingly, the time to circulatory arrest after withdrawal of life-sustaining measures was significantly shorter in the OD + group than in the OD − group, although both groups included comparable end-of-life ICU patients initially identified as potential cDCDD donors, with similar a priori likelihood of death occurring within the national protocol’s time limits (< 180 min). This difference was not expected, was not part of the study’s primary objectives, and represents the first quantitative observation of its kind in this population. As such, it warrants careful interpretation and further investigation. In the French regulatory framework, once life-sustaining measures are withdrawn, all measures that artificially maintain life must be discontinued, and continuous and deep sedation until death must be implemented (unless the patient had previously expressed opposition to it). Within this framework, several mechanisms may hypothetically contribute to a shorter dying process when organ donation is anticipated: continuous and deep sedation may be initiated earlier or at higher intensity; terminal extubation may be preferred over terminal weaning; and these practices may, intentionally or not, hasten the occurrence of circulatory arrest. These hypotheses echo our earlier qualitative findings highlighting the perceived “pressure to succeed” among ICU teams when donation is envisaged [18]. This unexpected result should now be explored through dedicated research combining objective clinical data with the lived experience and perceptions of clinicians and relatives, as it has major implications for transparency and trust in cDCDD end-of-life practices.

Strengths of our study include its prospective multicenter design, systematic follow-up at two time points, and the use of validated psychometric instruments alongside detailed assessments of relatives’ perceptions. Limitations include both loss to follow-up, potential recall bias, and restriction to the French context, where an opt-out donation system may not be generalizable to other countries. In addition, we included only one relative per patient, which may not fully capture the diversity of family perspectives. Another important limitation is the absence of systematic data on the number of eligible patients and recruitment rates across ICUs, which prevents accurate assessment of selection bias and may limit the generalizability of our findings. Finally, the OD- group was heterogeneous, including cases where OD was not pursued for medical, logistical, or relational reasons. This heterogeneity may have influenced relatives’ experiences differently, particularly when donation was declined by patients or families.

Conclusions

This study shows that relatives of potential cDCDD donors experience high levels of psychological distress, similar to those of relatives not approached for donation. OD discussions, when conducted within established frameworks, did not exacerbate post-bereavement complications. However, gaps in shared decision-making and emotional support persist, underscoring the need for structured interventions to improve end-of-life care and family support in the ICU.
