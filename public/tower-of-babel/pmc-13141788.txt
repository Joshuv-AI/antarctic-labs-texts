A porous andesite-stabilized nZVI (CMC) composite for efficiently removing Cd(II) and Pb(II) from electroplating wastewater
Authors: Zheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe treatment of electroplating wastewater containing toxic Pb(II) and Cd(II) requires efficient adsorbents. To address the agglomeration and oxidation of nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVI), we developed a novel composite, AM-nZVI-CMC, by stabilizing nZVI with carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) on a porous acid-modified andesite matrix (AM). Characterization confirmed uniform dispersion of nZVI particles (23–72 nm). Batch adsorption was rapid and followed the Langmuir model, with maximum capacities of 62.37 mg g−1 for Pb(II) and 57.04 mg g−1 for Cd(II). It outperformed commercial activated carbon in real wastewater, achieving >82% removal for both metals, and retained >85% capacity after 5-week storage. Column tests and cost analysis support its practical potential. This work presents AM-nZVI-CMC as a dual-stabilized, cost-effective composite that integrates physical confinement and chemical protection to overcome the inherent limitations of nZVI. Its superior performance in real wastewater, excellent storage stability, and favorable economics highlight its potential as a practical alternative for heavy metal remediation.Subject areas: Electrochemistry, Applied sciences, Water resources engineering

Introduction

As an essential process in modern industrial systems, electroplating is widely employed in mechanical manufacturing, electronics, aerospace, and related sectors.1 Nonetheless, the industry annually produces more than 10 billion tons of wastewater contaminated with heavy metals.2 If inadequately treated, such effluent can enter the environment and accumulate through the food chain, resulting in long-term risks to ecosystems and public health. It is estimated that nearly 35% of global heavy metal contamination in surface waters is directly linked to electroplating wastewater discharges.3 In light of increasingly strict environmental policies—exemplified by China’s Water Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan and the European Union’s Industrial Emissions Directive—the pursuit of efficient, economical, and eco-friendly technologies for heavy metal removal has become a central focus in environmental engineering research.4,5 Promoting such technologies is of strategic importance for supporting the green transformation and sustainable development of the electroplating industry.

In the complex pollutant system of electroplating wastewater, the removal of heavy metal ions such as cadmium (Cd) and lead (Pb) is particularly critical. Cd(II), classified as a group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), induces severe toxic effects including renal failure and osteomalacia.6 Pb(II) exhibits marked neurotoxicity, with even low-concentration exposure linked to intellectual developmental disorders in children.7 Studies have demonstrated that the detected concentrations of these two heavy metals in electroplating wastewater typically exceed the limits specified in the Discharge Standard of Pollutants for Electroplating (GB 21900-2008) by 2–3 orders of magnitude.8 More critically, conventional treatment processes achieve less than 40% removal efficiency for low concentrations of Cd(II)/Pb(II) below 0.8 mg L−1, failing to meet increasingly stringent discharge requirements.9 Consequently, the development of novel advanced treatment technologies is urgently required to enable targeted prevention and control of heavy metal pollution.

Current treatment technologies for Cd(II) and Pb(II) primarily include chemical precipitation, ion-exchange, and adsorption methods. Although chemical precipitation is cost effective, it generates substantial amounts of heavy metal-laden sludge (with treatment costs reaching 100–150 dollar/ton), posing risks of secondary pollution.10,11,12 Ion-exchange resins exhibit good selectivity, but their regeneration processes require excessive consumption of acid-base reagents, resulting in poor economic viability.13 In contrast, adsorption has garnered significant attention due to its operational simplicity and reusability. In recent years, nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVI) has demonstrated unique advantages in heavy metal remediation.14 Among various adsorbents, nZVI has garnered significant attention for heavy metal remediation due to its high specific surface area, strong reducing capacity (E0 = −0.44 V), and versatile removal mechanisms including adsorption, reduction, and co-precipitation. The characteristic core-shell structure of nZVI, comprising a zero-valent iron core and an iron oxide/hydroxide shell, provides abundant active sites for the immobilization of toxic metal ions.15 However, nZVI particles are prone to agglomeration via van der Waals forces and magnetic interactions, leading to reduced active sites and diminished reaction efficiency (experimental studies indicate that agglomeration can decrease removal rates by over 60%).16 This inherent agglomeration, coupled with its rapid surface oxidation in aerobic and aqueous environments, drastically diminishes the reactivity, longevity, and mobility of nZVI, posing a fundamental challenge to its practical application in engineering-scale wastewater treatment. Therefore, developing effective strategies to stabilize nZVI is a prerequisite for harnessing its full potential.17 To mitigate these limitations, two primary stabilization strategies have been extensively explored: (1) immobilization onto porous supports (e.g., activated carbon, biochar, clay minerals, and porous silica) to provide a dispersive scaffold that minimizes particle contact, and (2) surface modification or coating with stabilizers (e.g., carboxymethyl cellulose [CMC], polyacrylic acid, or starch) to introduce electrostatic repulsion and/or steric hindrance. As a low-cost, non-toxic, and anionic polyelectrolyte, CMC has proven particularly effective.18 Its carboxyl groups can chelate with iron ions, facilitating uniform nZVI nucleation during synthesis and forming a protective coating that hinders agglomeration and retards oxidation by acting as a physical barrier and a radical scavenger. Despite these advances, achieving simultaneous and synergistic enhancement of nZVI’s dispersion stability, oxidation resistance, accessible reactivity, and cost effectiveness for real wastewater treatment remains an ongoing challenge. Merely loading nZVI onto a carrier or coating it with a polymer often leads to compromises, such as pore blockage reducing accessibility or insufficient protection against long-term oxidation.19,20

While individual stabilization strategies—either immobilization on porous supports or surface coating with polymers—have been extensively explored, each approach in isolation presents inherent limitations. Porous supports alone can mitigate nZVI agglomeration but offer limited protection against oxidative passivation; conversely, polymer coatings provide some oxidation resistance but cannot completely prevent nanoparticle migration and aggregation in the absence of a robust scaffold. Moreover, many commonly used carriers (e.g., synthetic mesoporous silica and functionalized biochars) are associated with relatively high production costs, which hinder their scalability for real-world wastewater treatment. To address these multi-faceted challenges, a synergistic strategy that concurrently provides physical confinement and chemical stabilization—while maintaining economic viability—is urgently needed.

In this study, we propose and demonstrate such a strategy by constructing a hierarchically stabilized composite, AM-nZVI-CMC, which integrates physical confinement (porous andesite) and chemical protection (CMC). This dual-stabilization design represents a conceptual advance over previously reported single-method stabilizations, aiming to synergistically enhance both the reactivity and longevity of nZVI. To realize this design, we selected natural andesite—an igneous rock with inherent macroporosity—as the matrix. Unlike commonly investigated carriers such as biochar or manufactured porous silica, the novelty of using andesite lies not merely in its role as a support but also in its engineered transformation through a simple acid pretreatment. This process selectively leaches soluble components, creating a highly porous, silica-rich scaffold (the “andesite-based material”, hereinafter referred to as AM) that is both mechanically robust and chemically active. Crucially, the raw material cost of andesite is negligible (<$7.5/ton), offering an unmatched economic advantage over synthetic alternatives. More importantly, our approach moves beyond simple physical loading. We constructed a hierarchically stabilized composite, AM-nZVI-CMC, through a synergistic design: (1) The three-dimensional porous network of AM provides physical confinement, limiting nZVI particle migration and aggregation; (2) Simultaneously, sodium CMC acts not only as a dispersant during synthesis but also forms a protective, oxygen-resistant hydrogel layer around the nZVI particles, significantly retarding their surface oxidation. This dual stabilization (physical + chemical) is hypothesized to be key for enhancing both the reactivity and long-term stability of nZVI. Therefore, the novelty of this work is articulated in three dimensions: first, in material sourcing, utilizing and engineering an ultra-low-cost, natural mineral to create a high-surface-area carrier; second, in stabilization strategy, implementing a combined physical-chemical method to comprehensively address both agglomeration and oxidation; third, in performance validation, rigorously testing the composite not only in idealized aqueous solutions but also against authentic, complex electroplating wastewater, with a dedicated evaluation of its storage stability—a critical yet often overlooked parameter for practical deployment. We systematically characterized this novel composite, elucidated its adsorption mechanisms for Pb(II) and Cd(II), and demonstrated its superior efficacy and cost effectiveness compared with a benchmark adsorbent (activated carbon [AC]) in real-world conditions. This work aims to provide a practically viable and economically compelling solution for the advanced treatment of heavy metal-laden industrial wastewater.

Results

Preparation and characterization of AM-nZVI-CMC

Pretreatment of andesite matrix

The natural andesite ore was crushed using a crusher (RT-N13SF, Benchen Technology, Shijiazhuang, China) and sieved to obtain particles with a size range of 80–100 mesh. The collected particles were mixed with deionized water and subjected to ultrasonic cleaning (ODI-CS02, OIDIRE, Foshan, China) for 1 h to remove non-mineral impurities. After cleaning, the andesite particles were treated with 2 mol L−1 HNO3 under continuous stirring, using a magnetic stirrer (MHS4Pro, Qunan, Ningbo, China) for 30 min. Subsequently, the particles were rinsed thoroughly with deionized water, dried, and thereby yielded the porous andesite-based material (AM), which was stored for further use.

Preparation of stabilized nZVI material loaded on porous andesite

The synthesis protocol involved the following steps: (1) The AM matrix was combined with a 0.2 mol L−1 FeSO4 aqueous solution (200 mL) and anhydrous ethanol (200 mL) in a three-necked flask under nitrogen atmosphere, maintaining a Fe-to-AM mass ratio of 1:2. The mixture was subjected to combined magnetic stirring (500 rpm) and ultrasonic dispersion (40 kHz) for 30 min to ensure homogeneity; (2) 0.4 mol L−1 NaBH4 solution was introduced dropwise (2 mL min−1) into the suspension until gas evolution ceased, indicating complete reduction of Fe(II) ions. The resulting precipitate was collected via vacuum filtration, thoroughly rinsed with deionized water, and dried under nitrogen flow to obtain the intermediate product AM-nZVI. (3) For stabilization, CMC was blended with AM-nZVI at a mass ratio of 1:3, using vortex mixing (2,000 rpm, 10 min). The homogenized slurry was snap frozen at −20°C for 24 h to yield the final composite AM-nZVI-CMC. All procedures were conducted under strict anaerobic conditions to prevent the oxidation of nZVI.

Figure 2 displays the appearance and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization of the raw andesite material, the acid-modified (AM) matrix, and the AM-nZVI-CMC composite. As depicted in Figure 2A, the natural andesite, which did not undergo any pretreatment, exhibited a homogeneous mixture of various mineral constituents, with virtually no porous structure on the particle surfaces. Figure 2C illustrates the AM-nZVI-CMC composite, where CMC acts as a dispersing and stabilizing agent, facilitating the uniform distribution of zero-valent iron (Fe0) nanoparticles on the AM substrate without noticeable aggregation.22 Moreover, visual assessment indicated that the majority of the Fe0 particles were less than 100 nm in diameter, confirming their nanoscale dimensions.

Appearance and SEM images of andesite, AM, and AM-nZVI-CMC

As revealed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization (Figure 3A), the distribution of Fe0 particles on the surface of the AM matrix was distinctly visible. A statistical analysis was performed on 50 randomly selected Fe0 particles from the TEM images (Figure 3B). The results indicated that the particle sizes range from 23 to 72 nm, with the majority (34.16%) falling within the 50–60 nm interval, representing the most frequent size fraction.

TEM images of AM-nZVI-CMC and Fe0 particle size distribution statistics

Figure 4 presents the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the AM matrix, AM-nZVI, and AM-nZVI-CMC. The AM sample exhibited a single prominent diffraction peak at 22.56°, which is characteristic of amorphous SiO2, indicating that the acid-treated AM substrate primarily consists of silica.23 Significant changes were observed in the diffraction profile after the incorporation of zero-valent iron (Fe0). In the case of AM-nZVI, additional peaks corresponding to Fe were evident alongside the SiO2 peak. Specifically, the main peak of Fe0 appeared at 44.72°, consistent with the standard pattern of α-Fe0. In addition, 30.12°, 35.44°, 57.04°, and 62.60° are the characteristic peaks of Fe3O4 or γ-Fe2O3, which is the most common oxidation form of nZVI during its preparation and storage, and the peak positions of the two are highly overlapping and difficult to distinguish. The characteristic peaks of α-Fe2O3 are 24.12°, 49.40°, and 54.04°, among which the peak at 24.12° is covered by a large peak at 22.56° (SiO2).24 The above analysis indicates that although the characteristic peaks of nZVI have emerged, the oxidation of AM-nZVI that has not been stabilized is relatively severe. For the AM-nZVI-CMC composite, the positions of the Fe0-related peaks remained largely unchanged and the strength increased. Meanwhile, a concomitant peak appeared at 65.04. The emergence of this peak position indicates that the crystallinity of nZVI was significantly increased compared to the baseline, and the Fe0 loading was also increased. In addition, the characteristic peaks of Fe oxides (including Fe3O4, γ-Fe2O3, and α-Fe2O3) were significantly reduced.25 The above results indicate that CMC effectively prevents the crystal growth and agglomeration of nZVI, as well as plays a certain inhibitory role in the oxidation of the material. That is, the stability of AM-nZVI-CMC is significantly enhanced compared with AM-nZVI.

XRD patterns of AM, AM-nZVI, and AM-nZVI-CMC

The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis of the AM matrix, AM-nZVI, and AM-nZVI-CMC composites is presented in Figure 5. In the spectrum of the AM matrix (Figure 5A), a stretching vibration corresponding to Si–O–Si was observed at 1,071 cm−1. Peaks at 970 and 1,632 cm−1 were assigned to the bending and in-plane deformation vibrations of –OH groups, respectively. A broad absorption band centered around 3,428 cm−1 was associated with the stretching vibrations of both –OH groups and adsorbed water molecules.26 No additional prominent peaks were detected, suggesting that the acid treatment primarily preserved the SiO2 framework of the andesite, in agreement with XRD findings. Moreover, HNO3 treatment activated the silica surface, leading to the presence of surface hydroxyl groups.

FTIR spectra of the AM, AM-nZVI, and AM-nZVI-CMC

In the FTIR spectrum of AM-nZVI (Figure 5B), distinctive peaks related to iron species were evident. The absorption at 3,388 cm−1 may be assigned to O–H stretching vibrations associated with iron oxyhydroxides, which indirectly enhances the tensile vibration peak intensity of –OH on the surface of the AM matrix and attached water molecules. In addition, characteristic peaks at 1,282 and 1,436 cm−1 correspond to –OH bending and in-plane deformation vibrations in Fe(OH)3, respectively; the characteristic peaks at 582 and 771 cm−1 correspond to the stretching vibration of Fe-O in Fe3O4; and the characteristic peaks at 647 and 512 cm−1 correspond to the stretching vibration of Fe-O in γ-Fe2O3.27,28 These results confirm that zero-valent iron (Fe0) is deposited on the AM substrate, accompanied by partial surface oxidation.

With the incorporation of CMC, the FTIR profile of AM-nZVI-CMC (Figure 5C) exhibited notable alterations. In the spectrum of AM-nZVI-CMC, the characteristic peaks of the AM matrix (e.g., Si–O–Si at 1,071 cm−1) remained unchanged, while new features emerged in the region of 800–900 cm−1 and 2,800–3,000 cm−1, which can be attributed to the C–O stretching from CMC and potential Fe–O–H interactions, respectively. The overlap of peaks suggests close interfacial interactions between CMC, nZVI, and the AM support. The enhanced intensity of the peak at 1,436 cm−1 suggests stabilization of certain functional groups, potentially due to interaction between nZVI and CMC. Weak bands observed at 2,983 cm−1 and 2,873 cm−1 might have arisen from Fe–O–H bonding facilitated by carboxyl groups from CMC, implicating a surface complexation mechanism.29 Collectively, these changes verify that CMC is effectively coated on the composite and influences the deposition behavior of Fe0.

To provide a more quantitative perspective, we analyzed the intensity ratios of key peaks, using the Si–O–Si vibration (∼1,071 cm−1) as an internal reference. The appearance of C–O stretching at 886 cm−1 in AM-nZVI-CMC—a peak absent in both AM and AM-nZVI—confirmed the successful incorporation of CMC. The intensity ratio of this C–O peak to the Si–O–Si reference was calculated as 0.27, providing a quantitative measure of CMC loading. Additionally, the weak C–H stretching bands at 2,983 and 2,873 cm−1, attributed to the CMC backbone, showed a combined intensity ratio of 0.12 relative to the reference.

Furthermore, the intensity ratio of the Fe–O vibration (∼582 cm−1, characteristic of iron oxides) to the Si–O–Si reference decreased from 0.41 in AM-nZVI to 0.23 in AM-nZVI-CMC. This quantitative decrease indicated that the CMC coating effectively suppressed surface oxidation of the nZVI particles, consistent with the XRD results showing reduced iron oxide peaks in the stabilized composite.

In-depth mechanistic analysis of the FTIR spectra

The FTIR analysis provides critical evidence for the successful stabilization and elucidates the interaction mechanisms between CMC, nZVI, and the AM matrix.

Interaction between CMC and the AM matrix: In the spectrum of AM-nZVI-CMC, the intense Si–O–Si stretching vibration at 1,071 cm−1 was preserved, indicating the integrity of the silica framework of andesite after compositing. The CMC chains are likely anchored to the carrier via a hydrogen-bonding network between their hydroxyl groups and the abundant silanol groups on the acid-treated AM surface.

Stabilization and interaction mechanism of CMC with nZVI: Key evidence lies in: (1) Appearance of characteristic peaks: the presence of the C–O stretching vibration (primary alcohol) at 886 cm−1 and the weak C–H stretching vibrations at 2,983 and 2,873 cm−1 directly confirmed the existence of the CMC organic phase. (2) Peak shifts and changes: compared to pure CMC, the asymmetric stretching vibration of the carboxylate group (typically near 1,590 cm−1) may shift or overlap with iron oxide peaks (e.g., near 1,632 cm−1). This strongly suggests surface complexation or ion exchange between the –COO− groups of CMC and iron species (Fe2+/Fe3+). This interaction is key to CMC’s dual stabilizing role: first, during synthesis, the negatively charged carboxylates prevent nZVI particle aggregation via electrostatic repulsion. Second, in subsequent storage and use, the formed CMC-Fe coating acts as a physical barrier, effectively slowing the contact of the nZVI core with oxygen and moisture, thereby inhibiting its oxidative passivation (consistent with the weakened oxide peaks in XRD and the long-term stability results).30,31

Formation of the composite interface: The enhanced peak at 1,436 cm−1 may be related to the symmetric stretching of COO− or CH2 deformation, and its change could reflect the conformational alteration of CMC chains in the iron-silica composite interface environment. The Fe-O vibration peaks in the 580–771 cm−1 range confirmed the inevitable presence of a partial oxide layer (Fe3O4/γ-Fe2O3) on the nZVI surface, which itself is an important active phase for heavy metal adsorption.32

In summary, the FTIR analysis revealed that AM-nZVI-CMC is not a simple physical mixture. Instead, through molecular-level interactions—hydrogen bonding between CMC and AM, and coordination between CMC and nZVI—a stable “organic-inorganic” composite interface is constructed. This is the chemical foundation for high dispersibility and anti-passivation capability of AM-nZVI-CMC.

Study on the effect of pH on the adsorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II) by AM-nZVI-CMC

As demonstrated in Figure 8, the adsorption behavior of AM-nZVI-CMC toward heavy metal ions was strongly influenced by solution pH. Under highly acidic conditions, partial corrosion of Fe0 occurred, leading to diminished adsorption efficiency of AM-nZVI-CMC for metal ions. Consequently, within the pH range of 4.50–5.50, the equilibrium adsorption capacity remained below 30 mg g−1 for both Pb(II) and Cd(II), accompanied by a notable increase in pH following adsorption. In the case of Pb(II) adsorption, the higher standard electrode potential of Pb2+/Pb0 compared to that of Fe2+/Fe0 enabled the reduction of Pb(II) to metallic Pb0 by Fe0, with subsequent adsorption of Pb0 onto the iron oxide layer. XPS spectra of AM-nZVI-CMC, AM-nZVI-CMC-Pb, and AM-nZVI-CMC-Cd

The high-resolution XPS spectra of the Pb 4f region for the Pb-laden AM-nZVI-CMC are presented in Figure 10A. This area contains two main peaks. The first peak, Pb 4f7/2 peak located at binding energies (BEs) of 136.8 eV, could be unambiguously assigned to metallic lead (Pb0). The second peak, with peak at 143.8 eV, corresponds to oxidized lead (Pb(II)) species, likely in the form of PbO, Pb(OH)2, or surface complexes with iron (oxy)hydroxides.40,41 Quantitative analysis based on the integrated peak areas indicated that approximately 62% of the adsorbed Pb exists in the metallic state (Pb0), with the remaining 38% present as oxidized Pb(II) species (e.g., PbO, Pb(OH)2, or surface complexes). This provides direct and conclusive evidence that a significant portion of Pb(II) was reduced to Pb0 by the zero-valent iron in the composite, confirming the reductive immobilization pathway proposed earlier.

The deconvolution of Pb 4f and Cd 3d spectra

The Cd 3d spectrum of the Cd-laden composite (Figure 10B) showed two spin-orbit components: Cd 3d5/2 at 405.1 eV and Cd 3d3/2 at 411.9 eV, both characteristic of Cd(II) in an oxidized state (e.g., Cd(OH)2 or CdO).42 Both peaks originate from the same chemical species (Cd(II)), and their relative intensities (approximately 79% for Cd 3d5/2 and 21% for Cd 3d3/2) reflect the intrinsic spin-orbit coupling ratio. Importantly, the quantitative analysis confirmed that no detectable signal corresponding to metallic Cd0 (expected around 404.8 eV) was present, indicating that reduction is not a significant removal pathway for Cd(II). Instead, adsorption and surface complexation/precipitation are the predominant mechanisms, consistent with the less favorable reduction potential of Cd2+/Cd0 compared to Pb2+/Pb0.43

The XPS results provide definitive experimental evidence for the pH-dependent mechanisms. At pH 6.5, Pb(II) removal is governed by a combination of reduction to Pb0 and surface complexation/adsorption of Pb(II). At pH 6.0, Cd(II) is primarily removed through surface complexation and co-precipitation as hydroxides, facilitated by the corrosion products of nZVI. These findings move the mechanistic discussion from literature-based supposition to direct proof.

Based on the pH optimization results discussed above, the relatively appropriate pH values of 6.5 for Pb(II) and 6.0 for Cd(II) were selected as the fixed experimental conditions for all subsequent batch adsorption studies aimed at evaluating the maximum adsorption performance of AM-nZVI-CMC. Specifically, these pH conditions were uniformly applied in the kinetic studies, adsorption isotherm experiments, and thermodynamic investigations presented in the following sections. This differentiated pH design ensured that the adsorption capacity of the composite for each target metal ion is evaluated under its respective optimal conditions, thereby providing a more accurate and scientifically valid comparison of removal mechanisms and performance.

In-depth mechanistic interpretation of the dual-model fitting for Pb(II) adsorption

The concurrent high correlation with both pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models for Pb(II) adsorption is not merely indicative of a “combination of processes.” Instead, it can be directly deconvoluted based on the unique hierarchical structure of AM-nZVI-CMC.

Initial rapid adsorption phase (pseudo-first-order kinetics dominant): This phase primarily reflects the rapid diffusion and physical adsorption of Pb(II) ions within the macroporous network. The three-dimensional interconnected meso/macro-pore channels of the porous andesite matrix (AM), with an average pore size of 173.88 nm, offer minimal mass transfer resistance. This allows Pb(II) to quickly diffuse into the material’s interior and be adsorbed by the vast specific surface area (306.91 m2 g−1). As this process does not involve strong chemical bonding, it is dominated by concentration gradient-driven diffusion, characteristic of pseudo-first-order kinetics.

Subsequent slow equilibrium phase (pseudo-second-order kinetics dominant): As Pb(II) approaches the surface modified by nZVI and CMC, chemisorption and reduction become the rate-limiting steps. This includes the following: (1) surface complexation: specific coordination of Pb(II) with carboxylate groups (–COO−) from CMC and –OH groups on the iron oxide/hydroxide surfaces; (2) reductive deposition: the standard electrode potential of Pb2+/Pb0 (−0.126 V) is higher than that of Fe2+/Fe0 (−0.44 V), making the reduction of Pb(II) to metallic Pb0 by nZVI thermodynamically favorable. The resulting Pb0 deposits on or embeds within the surface oxide layer. This electron transfer process is typical of chemisorption, where the rate depends on the number of active sites on the adsorbent surface, thus aligning better with the pseudo-second-order model.

Therefore, the dual-model fitting fundamentally reflects the multi-stage adsorption mechanism of AM-nZVI-CMC, transitioning from “macroscopic carrier diffusion” to “nanoscale active site reaction.” The andesite skeleton is responsible for efficient capture and transport, while the surface-loaded nZVI and CMC are responsible for selective fixation and transformation.

Figure 11B presents the fitting outcomes of the intra-particle diffusion model, which is employed to elucidate the diffusion mechanism in the adsorption process. The adsorption process comprises three distinct stages: rapid adsorption, internal diffusion, and adsorption equilibrium. During the internal diffusion stage, intra-particle diffusion serves as the primary rate-limiting factor, influenced mainly by the adsorbent’s pore size and the concentration gradient between the interior and exterior surfaces.48 In the current study, the intra-particle diffusion model exhibited an excellent fit (R2 = 0.9924) during the internal diffusion stage, indicating strong agreement between the selected data points and the model. However, the substantial C value (29.01), reflecting a pronounced deviation from the origin, suggested limited intra-particle diffusion efficiency and suboptimal transport of reactants within the particles. According to prior pore size characterization results, AM-nZVI-CMC possesses a pore size of 306.91 nm, significantly larger than the adsorbate’s particle dimensions, implying that pore channel structure is not responsible for the observed diffusion constraint. Instead, the low diffusion effect may be attributed to the relatively low concentration of Pb(II) in the solution, which fails to establish a sufficient intra-pore concentration gradient.

Figure 12 displays the modeling outcomes for the adsorption process of Cd(II), with corresponding parameters provided in Table 2. Figure 10A, which compares the pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and Elovich models, illustrates that the pseudo-second-order kinetic model yields a superior correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.9982). Moreover, the theoretical adsorption capacity calculated from this model aligns more closely with the experimental qe value, suggesting that the pseudo-second-order model offers a more accurate description of the Cd(II) adsorption behavior on AM-nZVI-CMC. This consistency implies that the adsorption process is likely governed by chemical adsorption. Additionally, the linear fit of the intra-particle diffusion model (Figure 10B) does not intersect the origin, indicating that intra-particle diffusion is not the dominant mechanism controlling the adsorption kinetics—a conclusion analogous to that drawn for Pb(II) diffusion within the material’s porous structure.

Proposed removal mechanisms

Based on the collective experimental evidence, the removal mechanisms of Pb(II) and Cd(II) by AM-nZVI-CMC are proposed, as illustrated in Figure 18. The processes differ due to the distinct electrochemical properties of the two metals.

Mechanism schematic diagram: (1) the dual stabilization of nZVI by AM and CMC, and (2) the removal of Pb(II) and Cd(II) by the AM-nZVI-CMC composite

For Pb(II), the high standard reduction potential of Pb2+/Pb0 (−0.13 V) relative to Fe2+/Fe0 (−0.44 V) makes reductive precipitation thermodynamically favorable. The XPS analysis confirming the presence of metallic Pb0 provides direct evidence for this pathway. Concurrently, Pb(II) ions can also be adsorbed onto the composite surface through the formation of inner-sphere complexes with the iron (oxy)hydroxide shell or the carboxylate groups of CMC. Therefore, Pb(II) removal is attributed to a combined mechanism of reductive precipitation and surface complexation.

For Cd(II), with a much lower reduction potential (Cd2+/Cd0 = −0.40 V), the driving force for reduction by nZVI is minimal. This is corroborated by the XPS results, which showed no metallic Cd0. Instead, Cd(II) removal primarily proceeds via surface complexation and co-precipitation. Cd2+ ions likely form complexes with surface hydroxyl groups on the iron oxides or with the functional groups of CMC. Additionally, the corrosion products of nZVI (e.g., FeOOH and Fe3O4) can facilitate Cd(II) uptake through adsorption and subsequent (co)precipitation as Cd(OH)2 or Cd–Fe mixed hydroxides, especially at the optimal pH.

Throughout these processes, the porous AM matrix acts as a dispersive scaffold, preventing nZVI aggregation and providing abundant channels for mass transfer. The CMC stabilizer plays a dual role: (1) its anionic chains promote nanoparticle dispersion during synthesis via electrostatic repulsion, and (2) the resulting polymeric coating acts as a barrier, significantly retarding the oxidation of the nZVI core and thereby preserving its reactivity for metal removal.

Consistent with the proposed removal mechanisms, the regeneration of spent AM-nZVI-CMC can be achieved through acid washing. For metal ions bound via surface complexation or precipitated as hydroxides/oxides—such as the majority of Cd(II) and a portion of Pb(II)—treatment with dilute HCl promoted desorption through proton competition (H+ displacing metal ions from binding sites) and the dissolution of hydroxide phases. However, the fraction of Pb(II) that was reduced to metallic Pb0 and deposited on the nZVI surface was not readily releasable under mild acid conditions, contributing to the irreversible capacity loss observed after regeneration.

Regeneration performance and its mechanistic interpretation

The regeneration experiments revealed that after a single acid washing cycle (0.1 mol L−1 HCl, 3 min stirring), the AM-nZVI-CMC composite retained 71.2% of its initial adsorption capacity for Pb(II) and 69.4% for Cd(II) (Table 6). While these values indicate that the material can be partially regenerated and reused, they also reflect an inevitable loss of approximately 30% of the original capacity.

This capacity loss can be rationalized by considering the distinct removal mechanisms for the two metal ions (as discussed in “proposed removal mechanisms” and evidenced by XPS results in Figure 10).

For Pb(II), the removal involved both reductive precipitation to metallic Pb0 and surface complexation/co-precipitation. The fraction of Pb that was reduced to Pb0 could be irreversibly immobilized on the nZVI surface and could not be released under mild acid washing conditions. This irreversibly bound fraction accounts for a significant portion of the observed capacity loss.

For Cd(II), although reduction was not involved (no metallic Cd0 detected by XPS), the strong complexation with iron oxyhydroxides and CMC functional groups, as well as potential incorporation into the iron oxide structure during co-precipitation, might have rendered a portion of the adsorbed Cd(II) resistant to complete desorption by a brief acid wash.

Additionally, the acid washing process itself may induce further oxidation of the nZVI core or partial disruption of the CMC stabilizing layer, both of which could contribute to the reduction in available active sites for subsequent adsorption cycles.

While the regeneration efficiency was moderate, the material exhibited excellent single-use performance and outstanding storage stability (retaining >85% of initial capacity after 5 weeks of dry storage, as shown in Figure 20). This suggests that AM-nZVI-CMC is particularly well suited for applications where the material is used in limited cycles (e.g., 1–2 times) combined with efficient initial treatment, rather than in processes requiring dozens of regeneration cycles. The economic implications of this partial regeneration were incorporated into our techno-economic analysis (Table 7), where we estimated an amortized cost of $1.01/kg·cycle based on one regeneration cycle, which remains competitive with that of commercial AC ($1.69–$1.95/kg for single use).

We have made a preliminary budget for material costs based on the prices of the reagents and the market conditions. It is important to acknowledge that the above analysis represents a preliminary estimate based on laboratory-scale synthesis and assumptions about industrial-scale costs. To comprehensively address the long-term stability required for practical application, a 5-week (35-day) study was designed to evaluate AM-nZVI-CMC under two distinct scenarios: (1) control group (fresh material), (2) dry ambient storage, simulating shelf life during transportation and warehousing, and (3) immersion in a simulated wastewater matrix, assessing its chemical stability under continuous exposure to a harsh, ionic environment. This design evaluates the stability for both Pb(II) and Cd(II) removal, providing a holistic view of the material’s durability.

Adsorption performance testing after storage

The adsorption performance of all retrieved samples (both dry storage and wet aging groups) was evaluated using standardized batch tests to ensure comparability.

Testing procedure: For each retrieved aliquot, two separate adsorption experiments were conducted in parallel, using 100 mL of the metal solution, respectively. One experiment was optimized for Pb(II) removal (pH 6.5, initial concentration = 80 mg L−1), and the other for Cd(II) removal (pH 6.0, initial concentration = 80 mg L−1). All other parameters (adsorbent dosage, temperature, and contact time) strictly followed the optimal conditions established in kinetic study and isotherm study of this work.

Data presentation: The stability is presented as the percentage of initial capacity retained, calculated as: (qe of aged sample/qe of fresh control sample) × 100%. This metric clearly visualizes performance decay over time for both metals under both storage conditions.

The long-term stability results are presented in Figure 20. After 5 weeks of dry storage, AM-nZVI-CMC exhibited excellent shelf stability, retaining >90% of its initial adsorption capacity for Pb(II) (20a) and >85% for Cd(II) (20b). This minimal decline confirmed the effectiveness of CMC stabilization and the porous andesite matrix in protecting nZVI from rapid atmospheric oxidation during storage, addressing a key practical concern.

In contrast, continuous immersion in the simulated wastewater matrix (wet aging group) presented a more challenging environment, leading to a gradual decrease in performance. After 5 weeks, the capacity retention for Pb(II) and Cd(II) was 59.1% and 43.5%, respectively. The slightly more pronounced decline for Cd(II) may be attributed to its removal mechanism being more reliant on surface complexation with iron (oxy)hydroxides, which could be subtly altered during prolonged aqueous aging. The observed decay is attributed to the slow, continuous oxidation of the nZVI core and potential surface passivation in the ionic solution.

Crucially, even after 5 weeks of wet aging, the material retained the majority of its functionality. These results define the operational boundaries of AM-nZVI-CMC, suggesting it is best suited for cyclic treatment processes (as supported by the regeneration data in “practical application study of AM-nZVI-CMC”), rather than permanent immersion. The combined data from dry and wet stability tests provide a robust foundation for assessing its practical deployment logistics and lifespan.

Laboratory-scale continuous flow column tests

To provide a preliminary yet distinct evaluation of the dynamic adsorption performance for Pb(II) and Cd(II) under flow-through conditions, separate laboratory-scale, fixed-bed column experiments were conducted for each target metal. This approach eliminates inter-metal competition, allowing for a clear assessment of each metal’s specific breakthrough behavior and dynamic capacity on the AM-nZVI-CMC composite.

Column setup: Two identical glass columns (ID: 1.0 cm) were wet-packed with a homogeneous mixture of 0.5 g AM-nZVI-CMC and acid-washed quartz sand (40–60 mesh). The final bed height was 15 cm; operation mode: up-flow mode using a peristaltic pump at a constant flow rate (Q) of 1.0 mL min−1, resulting in an empty bed contact time (EBCT) of 11.8 min; sampling: effluent was collected via a fraction collector at fixed volume intervals (every 25 mL).

Specific feed solutions and experimental runs

Column A: Pb(II) removal. This column was fed with a solution containing 0.75 mg L−1 Pb(II) in a background electrolyte (0.01 M NaNO3, pH adjusted to 6.5, the optimal pH for Pb determined in batch studies).

Column B: Cd(II) removal. This column was fed with a solution containing 0.92 mg L−1 Cd(II) in the same background electrolyte, with pH adjusted to 6.0, the optimal pH for Cd.

Each column experiment ran continuously. The effluent from column A was analyzed only for Pb(II) concentration, and effluent from column B was analyzed only for Cd(II) concentration. Each experiment was terminated when the respective effluent concentration reached about 95% of its influent value. It should be noted that these column experiments, while providing valuable initial insights, were conducted at laboratory scale with single-metal solutions. Further validation using pilot-scale columns with real, mixed electroplating wastewater under long-term cyclic operation is necessary to fully assess the material’s engineering potential.

Comparative assessment and practical merits of AM-nZVI-CMC

While the maximum adsorption capacities of AM-nZVI-CMC for Pb(II) (62.37 mg g−1) and Cd(II) (57.04 mg g−1) are within the moderate range compared to some specialized high-capacity adsorbents reported in literature (e.g., certain engineered biochars or MOFs that can exceed 200 mg g−1), the primary merit of this work lies in its integrated design for practical applicability and cost-effectiveness, rather than pursuing ultimate capacity alone.

First, the material is built upon an ultra-low-cost and naturally abundant matrix (andesite). The raw material cost is negligible (<$7.5/ton), providing a fundamental economic advantage over many synthetic carriers. Second, the dual stabilization strategy (porous AM confinement and CMC coating) effectively addresses the inherent issues of nZVI agglomeration and rapid oxidation, granting the composite exceptional shelf stability (see “study on the adsorption stability of AM-nZVI-CMC for Pb(II) and Cd(II)”) and maintaining reactivity in aqueous environments. Third, and most importantly, its performance has been validated in real electroplating wastewater (see “practical application study of AM-nZVI-CMC”), where it outperformed commercial AC—a benchmark material—in terms of removal efficiency for both target metals.

Therefore, the value proposition of AM-nZVI-CMC is not as a “record-breaking” adsorbent in idealized pure solutions, but as a robust, cost-effective, and readily scalable material that demonstrates excellent performance under complex, real-world conditions. This balance between adequate capacity, high stability, low cost, and proven efficacy in real wastewater represents a significant step toward practical implementation, which is the ultimate goal of environmental remediation technologies.

Discussion

This study developed a novel AM-nZVI-CMC composite via a dual-stabilization strategy—physical confinement by porous andesite (AM) and chemical protection by CMC coating—which effectively mitigated nZVI agglomeration and oxidation.

The conceptual advancement of this work lies in the integration of physical confinement and chemical protection within a single composite, leveraging the natural porosity of acid-treated andesite and the stabilizing functionality of CMC. This dual-stabilization approach simultaneously addresses the two primary challenges of nZVI technology—agglomeration and oxidative passivation—that have been tackled only separately in most prior studies. Moreover, by employing an ultra-low-cost natural mineral as the carrier, we demonstrated that high-performance nZVI composites need not rely on expensive synthetic materials, thereby enhancing the economic feasibility for large-scale application. The subsequent validation in real industrial wastewater, combined with assessments of storage stability and regeneration, further distinguishes this study from those that remain confined to idealized laboratory conditions. Collectively, these elements represent a meaningful step toward bridging the gap between advanced materials research and practical environmental remediation.

Critical interpretation of key findings

XPS analysis confirmed distinct removal pathways: 62% of adsorbed Pb was found to exist as Pb0, evidencing reductive immobilization by nZVI. In contrast, no Cd0 was detected, indicating surface complexation as the sole mechanism for Cd(II). This difference, due to reduction potentials, explains the selective Pb adsorption in binary systems (KPb/Cd = 4.34).

Practical viability is supported by multiple tests. In real wastewater, AM-nZVI-CMC outperforms AC (>82% vs. ∼72% removal) and settles rapidly (∼20 min vs. >25 min). Storage stability is excellent (>85% retention after 5 weeks dry), though wet aging reduces the capacity to ∼40%–60% after 5 weeks.

Comparison with nitrogen-doped carbons

Recent studies on nitrogen-doped and modified carbon materials have demonstrated promising adsorption capabilities for heavy metals. Nitrogen-doped porous carbons, for instance, have achieved high Pb(II) capacities (>200 mg g−1), attributed to their nitrogen-containing functional groups that act as effective coordination sites. Similarly, modified carbon adsorbents functionalized with oxygen- or sulfur-containing groups have shown enhanced affinity for Cd(II) in batch systems. These materials highlight the potential of surface functionalization for enhancing adsorption capacity.56,57,58,59 However, their synthesis is often energy intensive, and validation in real wastewater is limited. Our AM-nZVI-CMC, with moderate capacities (Pb: 62.37 mg g−1; Cd: 57.04 mg g−1), offers practical advantages: ultra-low cost (<$7.5/ton), proven real-wastewater efficacy, excellent storage stability, and rapid settleability—making it a robust, cost-effective alternative for field applications.

Single-cycle regeneration (∼70% retention) reflects irreversible Pb0 binding (62%) and partial Cd complexation/nZVI oxidation, positioning the material for high-efficiency single use or limited cycles. Economic analysis confirms competitiveness: $1.01/kg·cycle vs. $1.69–$1.95/kg for single-use AC.

In conclusion, the developed adsorbent balances the cost, stability, and field-validated performance. Its value lies in robust, real-world applicability enabled by a sustainable, low-cost design.

Limitations of the study

(1) This study has advanced beyond initial batch tests to include competitive adsorption and separate, preliminary continuous-flow column experiments for Pb(II) and Cd(II). However, a key limitation for immediate engineering translation remains: the study has not yet performed an integrated, large-scale fixed-bed column test using real, mixed electroplating wastewater under long-term, cyclic (adsorption-regeneration) operation. Such a test is necessary to determine consolidated process parameters like the selective breakthrough order in mixed streams, hydraulic pressure drop, long-term column fouling, and the efficiency of in situ regeneration protocols. Our future research will prioritize pilot-scale column studies with complex feeds to address these specific engineering design and economic viability questions.

Characterization techniques

The morphological features of both AM and AM-nZVI-CMC were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; model SEM5000Pro, Guoyi Quantum, Hefei, China) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; model H-7500, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The crystalline structures of the materials were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a powder diffractometer (XtaLAB PULSAR, Bruker, Zweibrücken, Germany) operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. Chemical functional groups were identified via Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy using a Nicolet Summit spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Specific surface area and pore characteristics were determined with a NOVA4200e surface area and porosity analyzer (Quantachrome Instruments, California, USA). The Brunauer Emmett Teller (BET) method was applied to calculate the specific surface area, while pore size distribution was derived from the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model.
