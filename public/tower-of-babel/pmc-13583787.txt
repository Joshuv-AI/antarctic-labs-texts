Psychotropic-associated sudden death: a multifactorial challenge
Authors: Ana Beatriz Andrade, Anabela Pereira Neves, Rosa Henriques De Gouveia
Published: 2026

More than 970 million individuals are estimated to live with a mental illness, corresponding to a prevalence of approximately 29% of the world’s population, with projections of further increase in the future. As a result, psychotropic medications, especially antidepressants and antipsychotics, have become increasingly integrated into both primary care and specialised clinical settings.1

Psychotropic medication occupies a significant role in the treatment of various mental health disorders, providing impactful therapeutic benefits by improving quality of life and reducing psychiatric morbidity. However, increasing evidence has drawn attention to their association with sudden death, particularly sudden cardiac death (SCD), raising relevant questions regarding their cardiovascular (CV) implications and safety, requiring careful consideration in everyday clinical practice.1

SCD accounts for a relevant contribution to CV mortality, accounting for 10%–20% of deaths in Europe and presenting commonly as the first and last sign of a heart disorder, since by definition, SCD corresponds to a “non-traumatic, unexpected fatal event occurring instantly or within 1 hour of the onset of symptoms in an apparently healthy subject. If death is not witnessed, the definition applies when the victim was in good health 24 hours before the event”.2

SCD has a substantial impact on both familial and psychosocial components, as well as on healthcare systems. Family doctors have a major role when such unexpected deaths happen, both in the follow-up of grieving families and in the investigation of the underlying medical causes. In fact, family doctors are the first, constant and lifelong health support of patients, being the guardians of their psycho-familial-somatic histories.3 SCD remains highly unpredictable, and the frequent coexistence of various morbidities, especially in the older population, can hinder the identification of the main contributing mechanisms or triggering factors for these fatal events.2

Various discussions on psychotropic-associated sudden death focused on isolated electrophysiological mechanisms, which could potentially precipitate this outcome, particularly QT interval prolongation and Torsades de Pointes.1 4 5 While this reflection has improved awareness of arrhythmogenic risk, looking through these lenses may oversimplify a far more complex reality.

This article defends that psychotropic-associated sudden death should not be understood as an isolated pharmacological event but rather as a multifactorial clinical challenge, requiring longitudinal and individualised holistic risk assessment across healthcare settings, including primary care.

Psychotropic-associated sudden death rarely reflects a single causal pathway. Instead, most cases result from the combined effects of psychotropic medication and several individual patient factors, such as genetic predisposition to arrhythmogenic conditions, abnormal levels of essential minerals in the body, such as potassium and sodium (electrolyte imbalance), multiple coexisting illnesses, polypharmacy and fragmented care.1 4 5

This distinction is especially important to primary care and community health. Primary care physicians frequently deal with patients presenting chronic psychiatric conditions, often prescribing psychotropics to patients with multimorbidity, complex therapeutic regimens and concomitant conventional CV risk factors.

Individuals with severe mental illness, for example, schizophrenia, are prone to reduced life expectancy compared with the general population, largely because of an increased burden of CV disease. This high CV risk reflects the combined effects of conventional risk factors, including hypertension and diabetes, and unhealthy lifestyle behaviours, such as heavy smoking, substance use, higher food intake, weight gain and physical inactivity.1 5

Long-term psychotropic treatment is also associated with metabolic consequences, and patients taking atypical antipsychotics or tricyclic antidepressants were reported to have a higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome, a major CV risk factor defined by the presence of at least three of the following: abdominal obesity, hypertension, insulin resistance, elevated triglyceride levels and low HDL-cholesterol levels, all of which are independent CV risk factors.5

As improvements in psychiatric care have enabled more patients with mental health disorders to reach older age, the cumulative impact of these CV risk factors and prolonged medication exposure contributes to a high prevalence of acquired structural and ischaemic heart disease, which remains the leading substrate for SCD in older and multimorbid individuals.5 6 Therefore, CV safety should not be regarded as a static assessment performed solely before treatment initiation but as a continuous reassessment across the course of treatment, to better weigh up the dynamic nature of CV risk.

Some contributing factors for this premature and elevated mortality include higher rates of metabolic disorders, reduced adherence to medical treatments, barriers to healthcare access and adverse lifestyle choices.1 5 Therefore, psychotropic medication exposure is less likely the single, isolated cause of death, but a potential trigger capable of interacting with pre-existing vulnerability. For example, when a genetic variant results in an altered protein function; exposure to a drug may further disrupt the same biological pathway, exceeding the capacity of compensatory mechanisms, pushing the status quo over the cliff edge and leading to a dismal clinical outcome.

The most recognised mechanism underlying the association between psychotropic medication and sudden death is cardiac arrhythmogenesis, particularly the development of ventricular arrhythmias. Among these, Torsades de Pointes secondary to QT interval prolongation is considered one of the most clinically significant mechanisms. According to recent guidelines, to be recognised as an increased risk for Torsades de Pointes, corrected QT needs to exceed 500 ms or increase more than 60–70 ms from its baseline after initiating a new pharmacological agent.1 2

Prolongation of the QT interval, related to some psychotropic substances, occurs through dose-dependent inhibition of the rapid component of the delayed rectifier potassium current, resulting in heterogeneous lengthening of ventricular repolarisation, consequently creating an electrophysiological substrate that may predispose and increase susceptibility to the development of malignant arrhythmias.1 4

Nevertheless, not all drugs capable of prolonging the QT interval result in clinically significant arrhythmias. The likelihood of this happening varies depending on drug properties and presence of additional risk factors, such as the CV risk factors mentioned prior, further supporting the notion that psychotropics alone may be insufficient to explain extreme outcomes and that the presence of additional risk factors frequently determines whether electrophysiological changes may become clinically consequential.1 4 5 These observations reinforce the need for risk models that account for multifactorial vulnerability rather than isolated electrophysiological changes alone.

In addition, some psychotropic medications may inhibit sodium and calcium currents potentially unmasking or exacerbating channelopathies, for example, Brugada syndrome and long QT syndrome. Individuals harbouring silent or subclinical phenotypes are particularly vulnerable, as genetic defects create a pro-arrhythmogenic substrate. In this context, pharmacological exposure may function as a second hit, transforming mild repolarisation abnormalities into potentially lethal arrhythmias.2 4

This concept has significant implications for clinical practice, as patients with known or unrecognised genetic arrhythmia syndromes may inadvertently be prescribed pharmacological substances that are not recommended in their condition. Corroborating this concern, a nationwide Danish study from 2023 found that more than one-third of patients with Brugada syndrome received at least one non-recommended drug after diagnosis, with psychiatric disorders and non-recommended drug use before diagnosis identified as important and independent predictors of such practice.7 Jespersen et al also mention this in their paper,7 and another study of the group on the use of non-recommended drugs in patients with congenital long QT syndrome also found that nearly 60% of patients used a non-recommended drug after diagnosis.

These findings highlight that preventing adverse effects from psychotropic exposure extends beyond establishing a diagnosis. Patients with a known (or undiagnosed) inherited arrhythmia syndrome should not be actively prescribed agents that are contraindicated in their condition, leading primary-care professionals to a new action point, like checking resources such as brugadadrugs.org or other similar cardiological guidelines, when approaching patients with relevant personal or family history.7 Ultimately, and only if deemed necessary by the physician responsible or by the multidisciplinary team, genetic testing might be advised.2

Effective risk reduction requires ongoing communication between cardiologists, psychiatrists and primary care physicians to ensure that contraindicated drugs are recognised and avoided when possible. Regular medication reconciliation and reassessment are important in all patients, but careful attention should be given to patients with a personal or family history suggestive of genetic arrhythmia syndromes. When potentially proarrhythmic drugs cannot be avoided, treatment decisions should be individualised, measuring psychiatric and CV risks while consulting evidence-based prescribing resources and considering case discussion in multidisciplinary meetings to achieve the best care possible.7 8

Diagnostic overshadowing represents an additional challenge in patients with mental health disorders receiving psychotropic drugs. This phenomenon describes the tendency to misattribute physical symptoms to an existing illness, potentially delaying or preventing appropriate medical evaluation.9 Consequently, CV manifestations, including chest pain, palpitations, dyspnoea or syncope and unspecific symptoms, may be interpreted as anxiety, somatisation or medication-related effects rather than prompting further investigation. Similarly, sudden death occurring during psychotropic treatment may be prematurely attributed to the underlying psychiatric disorder, suicide or intentional overdose, potentially limiting comprehensive post-mortem examination. Such misclassification may reduce opportunities to identify possible genetic causes of death that would otherwise precipitate cascade screening of at-risk relatives. Recognising and addressing diagnostic overshadowing is therefore essential to improve both antemortem assessment and post-mortem identification of potential causes of death, ultimately contributing to more accurate diagnosis and preventive strategies.9

Post-mortem genetic testing, also known as ‘molecular autopsy’, has become an essential complement to the traditional autopsy examination, particularly when macroscopic and histological findings are negative or inconclusive, and when toxicological analysis reveals residual or therapeutic levels of psychotropics. Its growing use has transformed previously unexplained sudden deaths into opportunities for cascade screening, family counselling and preventive intervention among first-degree relatives because it allows the identification of a possible genetic and/or inherited arrhythmogenic condition. Recognition of this possibility further emphasises the importance of genetic predisposition within this multifactorial framework and highlights the implications of sudden death beyond the individual patient.6

This concept accentuates the importance of comprehensive clinical evaluation in everyday prescribing practices. It suggests that conventional approaches focused exclusively on drug classification and theoretical pharmacological safety and tolerability may fail to identify those patients at highest risk. Instead, physicians should weigh cumulative vulnerability, integrating individual biological predisposition with evolving clinical context.

Emerging insights into the neurocardiac axis further support the multifactorial nature of these fatal outcomes and its imperative need for more integrated physiological understanding. The bidirectional relationship between central autonomic regulation and cardiac electrophysiology suggests that mental illness itself, through complex pathophysiological mechanisms, alongside brain injury, seizure activity, emotional stress, inflammatory signalling and pharmacological modulation, may influence arrhythmogenic risk. Although chronic sympathetic overactivity and parasympathetic withdrawal are more commonly associated with ventricular arrhythmogenesis, autonomic dysfunction is more complex. Impairment or overactivity of either autonomic branch may influence cardiac electrophysiology and contribute to arrhythmia susceptibility, depending on the underlying cardiac substrate and clinical context. Because of this, psychotropics may affect CV outcomes not only through direct ion channel interactions but also through autonomic mechanisms.1 10

Acknowledging the true multifactorial nature of psychotropic-associated sudden death (figure 1) requires a shift in medical daily-practice philosophy. CV and arrhythmogenic risk assessment should not occur only at the time of psychotropic initiation but throughout the treatment course. Periodic reassessment is essential to ensure continued treatment safety. The dynamic nature of arrhythmogenic risk should always be considered because risk evolves over time and is influenced by intermittent conditions, such as acute illness and dehydration, as well as by age-related physiological decline and evolving comorbidities, which may temporarily or permanently increase susceptibility, resulting in the need for therapeutic adjustments and closer monitoring. This kind of ‘continuity care’ places primary care physicians in a uniquely advantageous position to identify these situations and intervene before vulnerability escalates and catastrophic outcomes develop.

From a practical standpoint, a comprehensive approach should include baseline review of CV history, concurrent medications (including over-the-counter drugs) and identify potential arrhythmogenic risk factors and/or conditions. When clinically appropriate, baseline electrocardiographic evaluation and referral to a cardiologist should be taken into consideration. Equally important is periodic reassessment especially after dose escalation, introduction of possible interacting pharmacological agents or acute clinical deterioration. Shared decision making remains crucial, balancing the extensive psychiatric therapeutic benefits vs individualised CV risk, rather than blindly relying on commonly used avoidance strategies.1 2

A balanced perspective is critical. Fear-driven prescribing restrictions may increase undertreatment of mental illness, worsening the already relevant treatment gap (more than 70% of individuals with mental illness not receiving adequate treatment nor support)1 and inadvertently increasing morbidity and mortality through clinical deterioration. Our goal is not to discourage psychotropic use, but to promote safer prescribing habits through vigilance and multidisciplinary collaboration involving primary care physicians, psychiatrists, cardiologists, geneticists and clinical pharmacologists among other health professionals.

Concluding, psychotropic-associated sudden death should not be interpreted as a simple adverse drug reaction, but as the consequence of complex interactions between pharmacological effects, biological vulnerability, autonomic dysfunction, genetic predisposition and evolving clinical context. Although the absolute risk remains relatively low, susceptible individuals may face substantially increased vulnerability in the presence of additional arrhythmogenic factors.

Consequently, CV safety in psychopharmacology requires a shift away from static and reductionist risk models toward more integrated, longitudinal and individualised approaches. Primary care physicians are uniquely positioned to contribute to this process through continuity of care, periodic reassessment and multidisciplinary collaboration.

As advances in molecular autopsy, neurocardiac axis and genetic testing continue to expand our understanding of these mechanisms, future strategies should aim not to discourage psychotropic use but to move beyond fragmented and reactive risk assessment, improve risk stratification and preserve the balance between therapeutic benefits and CV safety.
