PUST Cafeteria Food Image Dataset: Real-world Bangladeshi meal-platter images with bounding-box and polygon annotations
Authors: Marufa Yeasmin, Md. Razu Ahmed, Protiva Rani Kundu, Jannatul Mauya, Md. Shamim Reza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The PUST Cafeteria Food Image Dataset is a real-world image resource developed for object detection and instance segmentation of mixed Bangladeshi cafeteria meals. The source collection comprises 720 meal-platter photographs acquired between 1 August and 5 September 2025 at the Central Cafeteria of Pabna University of Science and Technology, Bangladesh. Images were captured using a Realme GT Master Edition and an OPPO A92 during routine cafeteria operations without standardized illumination, camera position, camera-to-platter distance, background, plate configuration, or food arrangement. Each identifiable food instance was manually annotated in Roboflow using a bounding box and polygon vertices delineating its visible region. Following auto-orientation, resizing, selective augmentation, light class balancing, and quality review, 1,728 images containing 6,297 annotated food instances across 16 classes were retained in the final processed dataset. The dataset was organized into training, validation, and test subsets comprising 1,512, 108, and 108 images, respectively. Annotations are provided in YOLO-compatible TXT and COCO JSON formats, supporting reuse across object-detection and instance-segmentation frameworks. The dataset can be reused for regional food-image analysis, comparative benchmarking, transfer learning, domain adaptation, and intelligent food-service research.

Background

Food-image datasets support the development and evaluation of computer-vision methods for food classification, localization, and segmentation. Food-101 established a widely used benchmark comprising 101,000 images across 101 food categories; however, it was developed primarily for image-level classification and does not provide object-level spatial annotations for identifying individual foods within multi-item meal scenes [1]. Culturally representative datasets are also important because food composition, preparation, appearance, and presentation vary across regional cuisines. Several datasets have improved the representation of Bangladeshi foods in computer-vision research. The Traditional Bengali Food Image dataset contains 2835 images across seven traditional food categories and was developed for image-level classification [2]. A subsequent Bangladeshi dessert dataset provides 4118 original images representing 27 dessert categories, but it similarly focuses on classification within a restricted food group [3]. More recently, FoodBD introduced 3523 smartphone-captured Bangladeshi meal images across 67 food categories with polygon-based food-region annotations; 1837 images additionally include expert-estimated nutritional information [4]. These resources address important aspects of regional food recognition, although their acquisition settings, annotation structures, and intended applications differ from those of a focused university-cafeteria dataset providing corresponding annotations for object detection and instance segmentation. Spatial annotations are particularly important for mixed-meal images because adjacent food items may have irregular boundaries, similar colours and textures, direct physical contact, overlap, and partial occlusion. UNIMIB2016 comprises 1027 canteen-tray images containing 3616 food instances across 73 classes, with individual food regions manually delineated using polygonal boundaries [5]. VIPER-FoodNet provides approximately 15,000 images across 82 food categories with food-object bounding boxes and class labels [6], whereas UEC-FoodPix Complete contains 10,000 food images with segmentation masks, including 9000 masks manually refined from the preceding dataset version [7]. These resources demonstrate the value of spatially annotated data for analyzing complex food scenes, but their cuisine coverage and acquisition environments do not specifically represent mixed meals served in Bangladeshi university cafeterias.

The PUST Cafeteria Food Image Dataset [8] was developed as a complementary, setting-specific resource for this context. It was derived from 720 original meal-platter photographs acquired under natural conditions at the Central Cafeteria of Pabna University of Science and Technology, Bangladesh. Following image preprocessing, selective augmentation, light class balancing, quality review, and dataset refinement, the final processed dataset contains 1728 images with 6297 annotated food instances across 16 commonly served cafeteria food classes. Each distinguishable food instance is represented by both a bounding-box annotation for object detection and a polygon-based instance annotation for segmentation, provided in YOLO-compatible formats. This paired annotation structure enables the development and comparative evaluation of object-detection and instance-segmentation models using a common image collection, annotation framework, and unified class taxonomy. The dataset complements existing food-image resources by providing spatially annotated, real-world Bangladeshi cafeteria meal scenes captured under natural acquisition conditions.

Data Description

Dataset composition and contents

The PUST Cafeteria Food Image Dataset [8] is a real-world image resource developed for object detection and instance segmentation of mixed Bangladeshi cafeteria meals. The source photographs were collected under natural operating conditions at the Central Cafeteria of Pabna University of Science and Technology, Bangladesh, and include both single-item and multi-item meal platters representing realistic cafeteria serving scenarios. The source collection consisted of 720 original meal-platter photographs. Each image was manually annotated in Roboflow, where every visually distinguishable food item was assigned a class label and represented using both bounding-box annotations for object detection and polygon-based annotations for instance segmentation. The processed images were auto-oriented and standardized to 640 × 640 pixels. To ensure reliable evaluation and prevent data leakage, the dataset was partitioned at the original image level before augmentation. The original collection was divided into three independent subsets: 504 training images, 108 validation images, and 108 test images. Data augmentation and selective light class balancing were subsequently applied only to the training subset. Therefore, augmented derivatives of training images were restricted exclusively to the training partition, while validation and test images remained unchanged. Following preprocessing, selective augmentation, light class balancing, quality review, and dataset refinement, the final processed dataset comprises 1728 images with 6297 annotated food instances across 16 cafeteria food classes. The final dataset was organized into 1512 training images, 108 validation images, and 108 test images. The processed images are provided in JPG format, while corresponding annotations are available in YOLO-compatible TXT and COCO JSON formats. Each food instance in the dataset is associated with a class identifier, bounding-box coordinates, and polygon vertex coordinates describing the visible food region. Images containing multiple food items include separate annotation records for each distinguishable instance. Food-class taxonomy and instance distribution

The dataset contains 16 food classes representing staple foods, curries, side dishes, snacks, beverages, and other prepared items commonly served at the PUST Central Cafeteria. The class taxonomy was developed to represent visually distinguishable food items present in real-world cafeteria meals. Several class labels are transliterated Bengali food names; therefore, brief English descriptions are provided to improve accessibility for international users. These descriptions serve as explanatory metadata and do not replace the original class labels used in the annotation files. Annotation files and data formats

The dataset package includes processed meal-platter images and corresponding annotations for object detection and instance segmentation. All processed images are provided in JPG format at a standardized resolution of 640 × 640 pixels. The package contains three parallel data branches: YOLO object detection, YOLO instance segmentation, and COCO-format annotations. Each branch is organized into training, validation, and test subsets. In the YOLO object-detection branch, each annotated food instance is represented by a numerical class identifier and normalized bounding-box coordinates. In the YOLO instance-segmentation branch, each instance is represented by a class identifier followed by normalized polygon vertex coordinates delineating the visible food region. Images containing multiple food items include a separate annotation record for each distinguishable instance. The COCO branch contains the corresponding images and a partition-specific _annotations.coco.json file. Each JSON file organizes image, category, and instance-annotation records in a structure compatible with COCO-based computer-vision workflows. The three branches are organized using the same 16-class food taxonomy and training, validation, and test partition structure, supporting reuse across different object-detection and instance-segmentation frameworks. Dataset organization and directory structure

The dataset is organized into three primary components: 01_raw_original_images, 02_processed_dataset, and 03_metadata, together with the accompanying README.md and LICENSE.txt files. This structured organization facilitates transparent dataset reuse, annotation accessibility, and reproducible experimentation. The 01_raw_original_images directory contains the 720 original meal-platter photographs collected under natural cafeteria conditions. These images are provided in JPG format and represent the unprocessed source collection used for annotation and subsequent dataset preparation. The 02_processed_dataset directory contains the final processed dataset comprising 1728 images with 6297 annotated food instances across 16 classes. The processed dataset is organized into three parallel annotation branches: coco, yolo_detect, and yolo_seg. These branches represent alternative annotation formats of the same processed image collection and should not be interpreted as separate datasets. Each annotation branch follows the same partition structure consisting of train (1512 images), valid (108 images), and test (108 images) subsets, where valid denotes the validation subset. The coco branch contains partition-specific image directories and corresponding _annotations.coco.json files, providing COCO-compatible image, category, and instance-level annotations for object detection and instance segmentation workflows. The yolo_detect branch provides YOLO-compatible bounding-box annotations, including separate images and labels directories for each partition and a data.yaml configuration file defining dataset paths and the 16-class taxonomy. The yolo_seg branch contains corresponding polygon-based instance segmentation annotations in YOLO segmentation format, with parallel images and labels directories. The 03_metadata directory contains supporting documentation and machine-readable metadata files, including class_names.csv, dataset_summary.csv, image_source_mapping_final.csv, instances_before_augmentation.csv, instances_after_augmentation.csv, and device_information.txt. These files provide information regarding class taxonomy, dataset statistics, image-source lineage, augmentation-related instance distribution, and image-acquisition details to improve dataset interpretation and reproducibility. The accompanying README.md file provides detailed information about dataset contents, annotation formats, directory organization, preprocessing workflow, and usage instructions. The LICENSE.txt file specifies the conditions for dataset reuse and distribution. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The PUST Cafeteria Food Image Dataset [8] was developed through six sequential stages: image collection, food-instance annotation, original-image-level dataset partitioning, image preprocessing and training-only augmentation, light class balancing and dataset refinement, and preparation of the released annotation formats. The 720 original meal-platter photographs were partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets before augmentation was applied. Augmentation and subsequent light class balancing were performed only within the training subset, while the validation and test subsets remained unchanged. The complete dataset-development workflow is summarized in Fig. 7, and the corresponding procedures are described in 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5.Fig. 7Workflow used to prepare the PUST Cafeteria Food Image Dataset.Fig 7 dummy alt text

Image collection and acquisition conditions

The 720 original meal-platter photographs were collected between 1 August and 5 September 2025 at the Central Cafeteria of Pabna University of Science and Technology, Pabna-6600, Bangladesh. Images were captured during routine cafeteria operations using the rear-facing cameras of two smartphones: Realme GT Master Edition and OPPO A92. Permission for data collection was obtained from the cafeteria authority. No controlled imaging setup or standardized capture protocol was applied during image acquisition. Variations in camera position, viewing angle, camera-to-platter distance, illumination conditions, background, plate configuration, and food arrangement were intentionally retained to represent realistic cafeteria environments rather than laboratory-controlled conditions. The collected images included both single-item and multi-item meal platters, containing adjacent, overlapping, and partially occluded food instances. These natural variations introduce realistic challenges for food detection and segmentation tasks while improving the practical relevance of the dataset. Details of annotation, preprocessing, augmentation, dataset partitioning, and quality-control procedures are described in 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5.

Food-instance annotation procedure

The 720 original meal-platter photographs were manually annotated using the Roboflow platform by two annotators. Each identifiable food item was assigned one of the 16 numerical class identifiers listed in Table 2. For multi-item meal platters, each visually distinguishable food component was annotated as an individual food instance. Two complementary annotation formats were generated: bounding-box annotations for object detection and polygon annotations for instance segmentation. Bounding boxes were drawn around the visible extent of each food instance, while polygon vertices were manually placed along the visible boundaries of the corresponding food regions. In images containing adjacent, overlapping, or partially occluded food items, separate annotations were created whenever individual instances could be visually distinguished. Polygon masks represented only the visible portion of each food item without estimating hidden regions. The annotation process involved independent manual labeling followed by cross-checking between the two annotators to ensure consistency in class assignment, bounding-box placement, polygon delineation, and image-label correspondence. Ambiguous or unclear food regions were reviewed carefully before final inclusion. The finalized annotations were verified prior to generating the released YOLO-compatible TXT and COCO JSON files. Representative examples of bounding-box and polygon-based annotations are presented in Fig. 5. Further dataset verification, preprocessing, augmentation, and partitioning procedures are described in 4.3, 4.4, 4.5.

Image preprocessing, training-only data augmentation, and light class balancing

The images were auto-oriented using the available image metadata and resized to 640 × 640 pixels to standardize the input dimensions. Data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training subset to increase geometric and photometric variability while preventing data leakage. The applied augmentation operations included horizontal and vertical flipping, random zooming/cropping, rotation, brightness and saturation adjustment, Gaussian blur, and salt-and-pepper noise. Following augmentation, selective class balancing, annotation verification, and quality refinement, the final processed dataset contained 1728 images with 6297 annotated food instances across 16 classes, consisting of 1512 training images, 108 validation images, and 108 test images. The validation and test subsets were kept unchanged throughout the process to ensure unbiased evaluation and prevent data leakage.

Final dataset organization and annotation-format preparation

The final processed dataset containing 1728 images with 6297 annotated food instances across 16 classes was prepared in three parallel annotation formats: COCO, YOLO_detect, and YOLO_seg. These formats represent different annotation representations of the same dataset and follow the same train, valid, and test structure. The YOLO_detect branch contains bounding-box annotations in TXT format with a data.yaml configuration file, while the YOLO_seg branch provides polygon-based instance segmentation annotations in TXT format. The COCO branch includes JSON annotation files containing image, category, and instance-level information for object detection and segmentation tasks. Quality control and dataset verification

Manual quality control was performed throughout the annotation and dataset refinement processes. Each image-annotation pair was reviewed to ensure correct food-class assignment, accurate bounding-box placement, proper polygon delineation of visible food regions, and consistency between image files and annotation records. Images containing adjacent, overlapping, or partially occluded food items were further examined to confirm that visually distinguishable items were annotated as separate instances. After preparing the final annotation formats, the dataset structure was verified to ensure the correct organization of the COCO, YOLO_detect, and YOLO_seg branches. Each branch was checked for consistent train, valid, and test partitions, with corresponding image and annotation files properly aligned. The YOLO branches were verified for image-label consistency and the presence of the required configuration files, while COCO annotations were checked for valid image, category, and instance records. The final dataset records were cross-validated to confirm consistency between images, annotations, and class taxonomy. The released dataset contains 1728 images with 6297 annotated food instances across 16 classes, maintaining identical class identifiers across all annotation formats.

Limitations

Although the PUST Cafeteria Food Image Dataset [8] provides a realistic benchmark for food recognition, object detection, and instance segmentation, several limitations should be considered. The dataset was collected from a single university cafeteria within a limited acquisition period using two smartphone cameras. Therefore, the visual characteristics of the dataset reflect the specific food items, serving practices, plate arrangements, and environmental conditions of that cafeteria. Models trained on this dataset may require further validation and adaptation before deployment in other food-service environments with different menus, cultures, or imaging conditions. The dataset includes 16 food classes with a markedly non-uniform class distribution. Although selective training augmentation and light class balancing were applied to improve the representation of less frequent classes, substantial frequency disparities remain. In the final released dataset, majority classes such as salad (1550 instances) and rice (1486 instances) are considerably more represented than several minority classes. This imbalance may affect model evaluation because aggregate performance measures can be dominated by majority classes, whereas class-specific precision, recall, F1-score, and related metrics for minority classes may be less stable because of their limited representation. Therefore, model performance should be evaluated using both overall and class-wise metrics, and results for minority classes should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Additionally, augmented samples increase image variability but cannot fully replace the diversity obtained from independently collected meal scenes. For instance segmentation, polygon annotations represent only the visible regions of food items and do not estimate hidden areas affected by overlap or occlusion. Although annotation quality was manually reviewed, an independent multi-annotator agreement analysis was not performed. Furthermore, the dataset provides visual annotations only and does not include additional information such as food weight, portion size, ingredients, preparation methods, pricing, or nutritional composition. Therefore, applications involving automated billing, portion estimation, calorie prediction, or dietary assessment require integration with external validated information sources. Future work may focus on expanding the dataset across multiple locations, increasing class diversity, and incorporating additional food-related metadata.
