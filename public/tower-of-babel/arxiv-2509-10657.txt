Boosting Sparsity in Graph Decompositions with QAOA Sampling
George Pennington, Naeimeh Mohseni, Oscar Wallis, Francesca Schiavello, Stefano Mensa, Corey O’Meara, Giorgio Cortiana, Víctor Valls

Abstract
We study the problem of decomposing a graph into a weighted sum of a small number of graph matchings. This problem arises in network resource allocation problems such as peer-to-peer energy exchange, and it is challenging to solve with current classical algorithms even for small instances. To address this problem, we propose a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm, E-FCFW, based on the Fully-Corrective Frank-Wolfe (FCFW) algorithm. In particular, E-FCFW extends FCFW by incorporating a matching-sampling subroutine that can be carried out classically or with a quantum approach. We show how to design such a subroutine using QAOA, which aims at solving a constrained discrete optimisation problem approximately to obtain solution-variety. We benchmark our approach on complete, bipartite, and heavy-hex graphs, conducting experiments using the Qiskit Aer state-vector simulator (9–25 qubits), the Qiskit Aer MPS simulator (52-76 qubits) and on IBM Kingston (52-111 qubits), demonstrating performance at a utility-scale quantum hardware level. Our results show that E-FCFW with QAOA consistently yields sparser decompositions (mean and median) than the other methods (random sampling and simulated annealing) for small complete and bipartite graphs. For large heavy-hex graphs with 50 and 70 nodes, E-FCFW with QAOA also outperforms the other methods in terms of approximation error. Our findings highlight a promising role for quantum subroutines in classical algorithms.

Introduction

Quantum optimisation harnesses the principles of quantum mechanics to solve optimisation problems that are beyond the reach of classical algorithms

. A key example is quantum adiabatic evolution
FGGS 00
, which aims to minimise a cost function that is hard to optimise classically (e.g. non-convex, discrete) by encoding its solution as the ground state of a Hamiltonian.
These characteristics have generated significant interest in applying quantum optimisation to real-world problems such as portfolio optimisation

, network design

, peer-2-peer energy trading
BBC+ 24

The typical approach in quantum optimisation has been to model a problem of interest and solve it directly using a quantum algorithm

;
DPSP 19
. However, the scale of many real-world problems often exceeds the capabilities of current quantum hardware. For instance, practically-relevant instances of Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimisations (QUBOs) are usually challenging to solve classically when the number of decision variables is over a hundred thousand
GHG+ 21
;
MIP 17
, which is orders of magnitude larger than what today’s quantum computers can handle.

An alternative to solving a problem directly with a quantum approach is to use quantum optimisation to carry out a specific subroutine within a classical algorithm. That is, use quantum computing to solve a smaller yet crucial subproblem within a classical algorithm—e.g. computing a gradient in first-order methods
Bec 17
.
Such an approach is appealing because the subproblem might be smaller than the main optimisation task, making it more suitable for current or near-term quantum computers. Also, by integrating quantum optimisation into the workflow of a classical algorithm, it is possible to tackle a broader range of problems than what purely quantum approaches allow today.

In this paper, we explore the use of quantum optimisation to solve a subproblem within a heuristic algorithm designed to solve a combinatorial problem. In short, the mathematical problem consists of decomposing a weighted graph into a small collection of graph matchings (subgraphs where no two edges share a common node), and it is a generalisation of the Birkhoff Decomposition problem for bipartite graphs
Bru 82
;
DC 17
. The Birkhoff Decomposition problem is known to be NP-hard when the objective is to find decompositions with the minimum number of matchings
DU 16
. Notably, the problem can be computationally challenging for classical algorithms, even for graphs with just 10 nodes

.

The problem is motivated by scheduling problems in networks with operational constraints

The motivation for considering quantum for this problem is twofold.
First, algorithms addressing this type of problem typically rely on a
subroutine
that computes graph matchings, which can be encoded as bitstrings. This encoding aligns well with existing quantum algorithms and is compatible with current quantum devices. The second motivation stems from the observation that classical algorithms often struggle to identify matchings that yield highly sparse decompositions. This issue is illustrated in Fig.

This behaviour is puzzling, particularly because it happens across all instances and there is a large gap between the solutions found and the best-known solution (red line).

Hence the question: how can quantum optimisation help in computing graph matchings? Existing classical algorithms often employ a greedy strategy to identify a single matching with maximum-weight; however, this may not be the most effective approach when the goal is to minimise the number of matchings required to fully decompose a graph. In contrast, quantum algorithms are capable of exploring a broader and more diverse set of solutions, potentially offering better coverage of the solution space. This, however, introduces several challenges such as designing an algorithm that can efficiently handle multiple matchings, and developing subroutines capable of computing these matchings with current quantum hardware. II.1
The problem of decomposing an undirected graph as a small collection of matchings (model-independent formulation)

Let

be an undirected graph with
V

to denote that an edge

v

e(u,v)=0

has a weight

associated to it, where
w(e)=0
if there is no edge connecting nodes

and
v

e(u,v)=0

Let

be a collection of subgraphs of

. That is, if an edge

connects two nodes

Let
\alpha_{M}\geq 0
be a weight associated with every matching

. The optimisation problem we want to solve is the following:

\underset{\alpha_{M}}{\text{minimise}}

\displaystyle\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}(G)}\alpha_{M}^{0}

subject to

\displaystyle w(e)e(u,v)=\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}(G)}\!\!\!\!\!\alpha_{M}e_{M}(u,v),\ \forall u,v\in V

where
0^{0}=0
. That is, we want to minimise the number of weights
\alpha_{M}
that are non-zero subject to the constraint that
w(e)e(u,v)=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}(G)}\alpha_{M}e_{M}(u,v)
. The variable

can be regarded as assigning the

in the original subgraph.

We will always assume that the problem is feasible in the sense that for some given weights

, there exists a collection of matchings and weights such that the constraints

can be satisfied for all

II.3
Related models and classical algorithms

The problem and mathematical model presented are closely connected to the Birkhoff decomposition problem
Bru 82
. As mentioned in Sec.
I
, our problem can be viewed as a generalisation of the Birkhoff decomposition problem, where we aim to decompose a general graph rather than a bipartite graph. The mathematical model we use is also related since we seek to decompose a doubly substochastic matrix as the weighted sum of symmetric and binary substochastic matrices. This can be viewed as a generalisation of the Birkhoff problem goal of decomposing a doubly stochastic matrix as the weighted sum of permutation matrices, reflecting the fact that, for general graphs, it may not always be possible for matchings to include every node.

The special characteristics of our problem (e.g. symmetry) do not allow algorithms for decomposing doubly stochastic matrices to work, e.g.
LML+ 15
;
BVAV 16
;
VIT 21
. Those algorithms rely on the fact the convex hull of permutation matrices is equal to the set of doubly stochastic matrices. However, that is not the case for symmetric doubly stochastic matrices since
the convex hull of symmetric permutation matrices is only a
subset
of the set of symmetric doubly stochastic matrices

Cru 75
. In the next section, we will present a Frank-Wolfe (FW) algorithm, which is a more polyvalent type algorithm for decomposing an object (e.g. a matrix) as the convex combination of different objects, which is often known as Carathédory’s problem
CP 23
.

III.1
FW background and motivation

In short, FW is an algorithm devised for solving convex programs where the objective is a smooth convex function and the set is a polytope—such as the convex hull of a finite set of discrete elements. In our graph decomposition problem, these discrete elements are symmetric binary substochastic matrices (
\mathcal{P}
) and
D^{*}
is an element in their convex hull. The objective function we will use is

\displaystyle f(X):=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left\|D^{*}-X\right\|_{F}^{2},

(4)

where
X
is a matrix in the convex hull of symmetric binary substochastic matrices, and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm.

The essence of the FW algorithm is to iteratively build solutions as convex combinations of the current approximation by selecting discrete elements from the feasible set. For example, let
\mathbb{M}
denote a set of
k
graph matchings. Then, we can write

\displaystyle X_{k}:=\sum_{M\in\mathbb{M}}\alpha_{M}M

for some weights
\alpha_{M}\geq 0
, which can be selected so that
X_{k}
is close to
D^{*}
.

FW selects how to add a new matching to set
\mathbb{M}
by solving the following optimisation problem

\displaystyle\hat{M}\in\arg\min_{U\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\nabla f(X)_{ij}U_{ij}.

(5)

This update step is equivalent to finding a
maximum-weight matching
on the graph defined by the matrix
(D^{*}-X_{k})
—that is, finding the matching
\hat{M}\in\mathcal{P}
that maximises the total weight of the selected edges in
(D^{*}-X_{k})
. See Sec.
II.2
where we map a graph to a matrix. The update can also be interpreted geometrically as finding a matching that aligns best with the negative direction of the gradient (see Fig.
4
).

With update in Eq. (
5
), Frank-Wolfe finds a collection of weights such that
X_{k}\to D^{*}
as
k
grows. The standard FW selects weights (also known as the learning rate) using a predefined rule. However, for our problem, and since we know the optimal solution
D^{*}
, it will be more convenient to recompute the weights with

\displaystyle(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{|\mathbb{M}|})=\underset{u\in\Delta_{|\mathbb{M}|}}{\arg\min}\left\|D^{*}-\sum_{M\in\mathbb{M}}u_{M}M,\right\|_{F}^{2}

where
\Delta_{|\mathbb{M}|}
is the
|\mathbb{M}|

III.2.1
E-FCFW Steps (Algorithm
1

In the first step of the optimisation, Algorithm

, with

\mathbb{M}
.

The second step is to find a set of weights for the matchings in
\mathbb{M}
that minimises the decomposition error, while using at most

k
is the current iteration count. Here, when we refer to a matching being “used", we mean that it is assigned a non-zero weight. Limiting the number of matchings used to
k+1
is important because it enables the algorithm to correct poor decisions made in earlier iterations. For example, suppose that at some iteration
k

using

. Now suppose that by considering

, we can attain the same (or better) approximation using a

This means we can effectively discarded suboptimal matchings from the approximate decomposition, freeing the algorithm to select better ones in future iterations, which may lead to an even lower error. Moreover, by restricting the number of matchings per iteration, we ensure a fair comparison with the vanilla Fully Corrective Frank-Wolfe (FCFW) algorithm, for which

and only a single maximum-weight matching is sampled in each iteration.

The third step stores the weights, and computes the new approximate decomposition and the corresponding approximation error.

Once the desired approximation error

with non-zero weights, and their corresponding weights.

The main part of the subroutine that computes matchings (Algorithm

Quantum Subroutine for
Computing Matchings

In this section, we present a QAOA subroutine for sampling matchings. Sec.

motivates the use of QAOA in our problem. Sec.

presents the problem of finding maximum-weight matchings as a discrete optimisation problem, which we use in the QAOA model in Sec.

IV.1
Motivation

We employ QAOA, a heuristic quantum algorithm that leverages quantum fluctuations to explore the search space. Our motivation is inspired by the observation that, while QAOA at low circuit depths is typically regarded as limited in solution quality, this apparent drawback may become an advantage in our setting. Since our application benefits from diversity near optimality rather than exact optimality, the approximate solutions generated by low-depth QAOA are not only acceptable but particularly valuable.

IV.2
QUBO Formulation for Maximum-Weight Matchings

To apply QAOA to finding the maximally-weighted matchings of a
graph, we need a formulation that aligns with the underlying structure of the algorithm. Since QAOA can be interpreted as a parameterized quantum walk over the solution space, it is natural to encode edges as qubits rather than vertices. We formulate the problem as the following constrained binary optimisation model:

maximise

\displaystyle\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\!\!\!\!w_{uv}x_{uv}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in\Gamma(u,v)}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!x_{uv}x_{u^{\prime}v^{\prime}}=0,\quad\hfill\forall(u,v)\in E

x_{uv}\in\{0,1\},\quad\hfill\forall(u,v)\in E

Here,
x_{uv}
is a binary variable that takes the value
1
if edge
(u,v)
is included in the matching and zero otherwise.
w_{u,v}
represents the weight of edge
(u,v)
and
\Gamma(u,v)
is the set of edges adjacent to edge
(u,v)
.
The constraint ensures that if an edge
(u,v)
is included in the matching, all adjacent edges
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in\Gamma(u,v)
are not included.
With this encoding, every subgraph is represented as a unique bitstring, and every matching is a bitstring satisfying the constraint. For instance, in a graph with four edges, the bitstring
0101
represents a subgraph that includes only the second and fourth edges.

We transform the constrained binary optimisation problem into an unconstrained problem by introducing a cost function, to be minimised, including the primary objective along with a penalty term representing the constraint:

\displaystyle C(x)=-\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\!\!w_{uv}x_{uv}+\lambda\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,v)\in E\\
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in\Gamma(u,v)\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!x_{uv}x_{u^{\prime}v^{\prime}}

(9)

where
x\in\{0,1\}^{|E|}
. In this formulation, bitstrings violating the constraint in Eq. (
IV.2
) incur a cost penalty of
\lambda
. A crucial aspect of this method is the choice of the penalty term. It must be large enough to effectively enforce the constraints, ensuring that invalid matchings receive a significant penalty. However, if the penalty is too large, it can overshadow the primary objective, making optimisation difficult by biasing the search too heavily toward feasibility rather than optimality. Provided that
\lambda
is chosen appropriately, the maximum-weight matching minimises Eq. (
9
).

Note that alternatively, we could incorporate constraints by embedding them directly into the mixing Hamiltonian, which restricts the search space to only feasible solutions by ensuring that quantum evolution remains within the valid subspace
HWO+ 19
.
However, this method typically requires multi-qubit gates,
which are resource-intensive to decompose into two-qubit gates. In contrast, incorporating constraints into the penalty is more resource-efficient.

IV.3
QAOA Model

We use a standard QAOA model with one layer to implement the problem in Eq. (
9
)

FGG 14
. The Hamiltonian that encodes the cost function is

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{C}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j\in E}w_{j}\left(\frac{1-\hat{Z}_{j}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda\sum_{j\in E}\sum_{k\in\Gamma(j)}\left(\frac{1-\hat{Z}_{j}}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1-\hat{Z}_{k}}{2}\right),

(10)

where
\hat{Z}_{j}
is the Pauli-Z operator acting on qubit
j
and
w_{j}\geq 0
is the weight assigned to edge
j\in E
. Recall from Sec.
IV.2
that we encode edges to qubits.
The system is initially prepared in the state
|+\rangle^{\otimes N}
where
N:=|E|
corresponds to the number of edges in the graph. Then, we apply evolution under the cost and mixing Hamiltonians
\hat{U}_{C}(\gamma)=\exp(-i\gamma\hat{H}_{\rm C})
and
\hat{U}(\beta)=\exp(-i\beta\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{X}_{j})
where
\hat{X}_{j}
is the Pauli-X operator acting on qubit
j
, and
\gamma\in[0,2\pi]
and
\beta\in[0,\pi]
are variational parameters. This generates the following variational quantum state

\displaystyle|\psi({\beta},{\gamma})\rangle=\hat{U}_{M}(\beta)\hat{U}_{C}(\gamma)|+\rangle^{\otimes N}.

The original cost function, Eq. (
9
), is given by the expectation value of the cost Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle C({\gamma},{\beta})=\left\langle\psi({\gamma},{\beta})\right|H_{C}\left|\psi({\gamma},{\beta})\right\rangle,

where a classical computer is then used to search for the optimal parameters
\left({\gamma}^{*},{\beta}^{*}\right)=\arg\min_{{\gamma},{\beta}}\ C({\gamma},{\beta})
.
Once the optimal parameters
\left({\gamma}^{*},{\beta}^{*}\right)
are found, the variational circuit is executed multiple times with these fixed parameters to sample bitstrings
x\in\{0,1\}^{|E|}
from the quantum state
\left|\psi\left({\gamma}^{*},{\beta}^{*}\right)\right\rangle
. Each sampled bitstring corresponds to a candidate matching, and a few ones with the highest weight are selected as the final output.

Circuit scaling.
The number of two-qubit gates in the QAOA ansatz increases rapidly with the number of edges in the graph. To illustrate this, consider a complete graph with
n
nodes. In such a graph, each node has degree
n-1
and is adjacent to
2(n-2)
other edges. Since our QAOA cost function, Eq. (
9
), requires the implementation of two-qubit
Rzz
gates between adjacent edges, this means the number of non-local gates in the QAOA ansatz, and in turn the circuit depth, can quickly become too large for current current devices. For example, in the 9-node graphs we will consider in Sec.
V.3.1
, the two-qubit gate depth of the QAOA circuits ranges from 120 to 222 (after transpilation for IBM Sherbrooke), despite only requiring 20 to 25 qubits.

Demand matrices.
For all of our experiments, we use a consistent approach for constructing the demand matrices for a given graph topology. For a given underlying topology (complete, bipartite, heavy-hex), a demand matrix for a graph with
n
nodes is constructed as follows:

1.

Sample
n
matchings in the underlying topology uniformly at random and map them to
n
binary substochastic matrices
\{M_{1},\dots,M_{n}\}
. The matchings may not necessarily be unique. This can be viewed as picking random matchings in a graph containing every possible allowed edge in the desired topology.

2.

Select weights
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
uniformly at random such that
0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq 1
for all
i=1,\dots,n
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}=1
, and construct a convex combination with the selected permutation matrices, i.e.
D^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}M_{i}
.

This approach guarantees that
D^{*}

lies within the convex hull of the graph matchings and that a decomposition with at most
n
matchings exists. We generate 10 demand matrices per graph topology.

VI
Conclusions

This paper has studied the problem of decomposing a graph as the weighted sum of a small number of matchings. To address this problem, we have proposed a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm, E-FCFW, which extends the FCFW algorithm to allow it to use a matching-sampling subroutine. We showed how to design such a subroutine using QAOA to solve a constrained discrete optimisation problem that aims to find high-weight matchings in a graph. The motivation behind using QAOA is to find matchings with high-weight that use different edges in the graph.

We benchmark our approach on complete, bipartite, and heavy-hex graphs, conducting experiments with the Qiskit Aer state-vector simulator (9–25 qubits), the Qiskit Aer MPS simulator (52-76 qubits) and on IBM Kingston (52-111 qubits). Our results show that E-FCFW with QAOA consistently yields sparser decompositions (mean and median) than the other methods for small complete and bipartite graphs. For large heavy-hex graphs with 50 and 70 nodes, E-FCFW with QAOA outperforms the other methods in terms of approximation error, and QAOA using MPS simulation outperforms QAOA executed on quantum hardware, likely due to hardware noise.

We observe that the QAOA sampling method performs poorly for the 100-node graphs considered in Appendix
B
. We think this is likely due to hardware noise, but it could also be affected by our parameter setting strategy, and the single-layer QAOA ansatz used. Potential future research avenues to address this issue could include: using more sophisticated parameter setting strategies for QAOA, using deeper QAOA ansätze, and executing reduced-depth circuits on quantum hardware with higher qubit connectivity.

Another interesting research direction could be to explore different post-processing techniques for selecting the matchings in each iteration of the E-FCFW algorithm. For instance, instead of discarding bitstrings which violate the constraints, we could remove edges in the corresponding subgraphs until they correspond to valid matchings. Or, we could explore different selection criteria, rather than always selecting the highest-weight matchings. Such a selection criterion could be informed by overlaps between matchings, as discussed in Sec.
V.3.1
.

Appendix B
100-node results

Figure 12:
A plot showing the decomposition error vs decomposition length for three 100-node heavy-hex topology graphs using the FCFW and E-FCFW algorithms. For the E-FCFW algorithm, we compare three different sampling methods for the matching subroutine (Algorithm
2
), namely QAOA, random sampling, and simulated annealing. For QAOA, we execute all circuits on IBM Kingston. The black dashed line indicates desired decomposition error
\epsilon=10^{-6}
. The median two-qubit gate depths of the QAOA circuits were, from left to right, 68.5, 69, and 72.

In this section, we present results for 100-node graphs with heavy-hex topology (introduced in Sec.
V.3.3
). The experimental setup is identical to the 50 and 70-node experiments in Sec.
V.3.3
, only without MPS simulation results. Fig.
12
shows the decomposition approximation error as a function of decomposition length for the various methods, for three 100-node heavy-hex graphs. Unlike the case of 50 and 70-node graphs, here the E-FCFW algorithm with simulated annealing sampling reaches the lowest decomposition error of all the methods, and QAOA performs poorly.

This could be due to a variety of reasons, including sub-optimal QAOA parameters, low expressivity of the single-layer QAOA ansatz, or increased noise due to larger circuit-depths. Since we observe better performance when using MPS simulation for 50 and 70-graphs when using the same parameters and ansatz (see Fig.
10
), we suspect that hardware noise is the dominant cause of the poor QAOA performance at 100-nodes.

The QAOA circuits of the 100-node graphs are deeper than those of the 50 and 70-node graphs, and likely more prone to errors as a result. The median two-qubit gate depths of the QAOA circuits were 38, 42.5, and 39.5 for the 50-node graphs, and 68.5, 69, and 72 for the 100-node graphs. This is due to there being more terms in the Hamiltonian, and limited routing options during transpilation as the number of qubits approaches the hardware limit (156). Furthermore, particularly noisy qubits or two-qubit connections are be harder for the transpiler to avoid for larger circuits.

It would be interesting to see if the performance of QAOA improves when executed on quantum hardware with lower error rates and higher qubit connectivity, such as the upcoming IBM Nighthawk processor.
