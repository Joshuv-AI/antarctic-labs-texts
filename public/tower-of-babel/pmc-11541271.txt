Immunotherapy in the Fight Against Bone Metastases: A Review of Recent Developments and Challenges
Authors: Zhonghui Peng, Wei Huang, Ziyu Xiao, Jinge Wang, Yongzhe Zhu, Fudou Zhang, Dongqiang Lan, Fengjiao He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Opinion StatementBone metastasis, a frequent and detrimental complication of advanced cancers, often triggers bone deterioration events that severely compromise patient quality of life and prognosis. The past few years have witnessed the emergence and continuous advancements in immunotherapy, ushering in innovative therapeutic prospects for bone metastasis. These advancements include not only the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), both as standalone and combined treatments, but also the investigation of novel targets within immune cells residing in bone metastases. These breakthroughs have instilled fresh optimism for effectively managing patients with bone metastasis. This article endeavors to present an exhaustive review of the recent progress made across a spectrum of immunotherapeutic strategies and targeted therapies specifically designed for individuals battling bone metastasis from malignant tumors. By doing so, it seeks to offer insights that can inform clinical practices and guide further medical research in this domain.

Introduction: The Significance of Immunotherapy for Bone Metastasis

The characteristic tropism, or tendency, of cancer cells for distant metastasis, is well-established in clinical practice. It is now known that one of the most frequent locations for malignant tumors to metastasize is the bone. Bones have a unique microenvironment where tumors preferentially colonize, allowing for the growth and prosperity of metastatic tumor cells [1–4]. Bone metastases exhibit a predilection for specific malignancies, including breast, prostate, lung, and melanoma. Their incidence reportedly increases with advancing disease stages at the time of diagnosis [5, 6]. Traditional anti-tumor treatments, including external beam radiation therapy, cytotoxic chemotherapy, and targeted biologics, play a role in managing metastatic bone disease. Bisphosphonates and denosumab have become cornerstone medications in this area [7]. However, bone metastases typically signify a poor prognosis and are seldom curable. Bone destruction is frequently accompanied by the occurrence of skeletal-related events (SREs) such as nerve impingement, spinal cord compression, and severe pain. Pathological fractures are also prevalent symptoms in these cases [5]. These SREs significantly diminish patient quality of life and physical function.

In recent years, immunotherapy has shown promising potential across various cancer treatments. It dynamically regulates or rejuvenates the immune system of the body to effectively target and eliminate cancer cells through various pathways. This serves as an additional effective tool in the battle against cancer, complementing surgical procedures, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies [8, 9]. Currently, cancer immunotherapy can be broadly classified into four main categories: ICIs, cellular immunotherapies, tumor vaccines, and non-specific immunomodulators [10].

Breakthroughs in tumor immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockade, have revealed substantial potential value for patients with bone metastases. This article aims to provide a comprehensive overview of these advancements and their application for patients with bone metastasis from malignant tumors.

The Immune Microenvironment of Bone Metastases

The distinctive immune microenvironment within bone tissue contributes to the unique characteristics of bone metastasis, differentiating it from primary tumors and other types of metastases. Bone metastasis often involves bone destruction, disrupting the equilibrium between osteoblast and osteoclast activities, thereby leading to alterations in the immune microenvironment.Recently, a new concept of osteoimmuno-oncology (OIO) has been introduced to the community. OIO emphasizes the importance of interactions between tumor, immune, and bone cells in promoting tumor growth in bone metastases, and it can be used to reveal the most promising targets for bone metastases [11]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the interplay between various immune cell subsets and the tumor microenvironment within the bone context is essential to unraveling the paramount importance of immunotherapy in relation to the management of malignant bone metastases.

Bone Colonization Progress

The colonization of bone by tumor cells is a sequential series of occurrences, where circulating tumor cells in the bloodstream and disseminated tumor cells within the bone marrow represent the clinical process of spread and colonization throughout the bone metastasis process [12]. This process can be segmented into three essential stages: seeding, dormancy, and growth. This perspective is rooted in Stephen Paget's seminal "seed and soil" hypothesis, which proposes that cancer cells (seeds) from their primary site invade and establish metastatic lesions (soil) at distant sites [13].

Tumor cells must interact with the microenvironment of a specific host organ to generate metastases, such as IL-1α in the microenvironment, which can promote tumor growth. This fact further emphasizes the important role of tumor cell-bone microenvironment interaction in the formation of bone metastases [14, 15]. This suggests that the preference of tumors to metastasize to bone may be attributed to the unique features of the bone milieu.

The skeletal system possesses a distinct immune microenvironment compared to other organs, making bone metastasis different from primary tumors and other types of metastasis. Moreover, as a regulator of systemic immunity, the bone environment also impacts the efficacy of both conventional treatments and immunotherapies. Thus, understanding the specific immune microenvironment of bone is vital for gaining insights into the dynamics of bone metastasis development and treatment response.

Immune Cells' Involvement in the Formation of Tumor Bone MetastasisTumors and Bone tissues Engage in a Vicious cycle

Tumor-secreted factors stimulate bone cells typically stimulate the proliferation and maturation of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, leading to a net increase in osteoclast mediated bone destruction. The absorption of bone matrix leads to the release of factors that stimulate tumor growth. The local enrichment factors around tumor cells not only promote tumor growth, but also inhibit tumor growth and alter their phenotype, making them resistant to standard cytotoxicity and anti-tumor therapy [16–18]. This interaction involves the joint regulation of hematopoiesis, osteogenesis, and bone resorption. It leads to spatial constraints that disrupt the equilibrium between osteoblasts and osteoclasts, triggering changes within the immune microenvironment [19]. Research has found that (1) bone metastasis only forms in the area of hematopoietic active red bone marrow [20], and (2) bone metastasis disrupts the biological processes responsible for bone structure, including osteolysis and osteogenesis. A considerable amount of research has focused on the unique ability of certain cancers to grow in the bone by disrupting the calcified matrix, while other cancers seem to grow by producing new bone tissue [6]. The result is a vicious cycle that occurs within the bone microenvironment. Immune-related factors, including nuclear factor-kappa-B receptor activator (RANK) and RANKL, interleukins, and tumor necrosis factor, play significant roles in the biology of osteoclasts and osteoblasts [21–23]. For instance, RANKL inhibitors are widely used in the therapy of bone metastasis and for mitigating the risk of SREs. RANKL acts as a critical mediator in the differentiation and proliferation of osteoclasts [24]. When malignant tumors metastasize to the bone, they induce osteoblasts to secrete RANKL, which leads to the production of parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP) and other hormones that mediate osteoclast formation systemically [25]. As a result, when RANK is expressed by osteoclasts and their precursors due to increased RANKL secretion, the interaction of RANKL to RANK triggers signaling pathways that regulate osteoclast maturation and differentiation [26, 27].

Preclinical studies have demonstrated the presence of a variety of immune cells within the bone microenvironment. These include different subsets of T and B lymphocytes, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, which interact primarily with osteoblasts and osteoclasts. For example, CD4 + T lymphocytes encompass distinct subtypes such as helper T cells (Th) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) [28], while CD8 + T lymphocytes are the primary effector cells accountable for killing tumor cells [29]. Upon arrival in the bone microenvironment, tumor cells can promote the proliferation of specific T and B lymphocyte subsets [30]. This phenomenon is notably associated with the latency period of tumor metastasis. Functionally, activated CD4 + T cells release interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and other factors that inhibit osteoclast activation [31]. Infiltrating plasmacytoid dendritic cells (PDCs), on the other hand, continuously secret Th2 signals. These signals suppress CD8 + T cells, facilitate the maturation of regulatory T cells, and contribute to dormant tumor progression [32]. Similarly, M1-type macrophages secrete inflammatory cytokines like IL-23, IL-1, IL-6, and IFN-γ, which activate CD8 + T cells and NK cells, facilitating tumor cell clearance [33–35]. Conversely, M2-type macrophages are known to produce elevated levels of TGF-β and IL-10, which suppress the activity of CD4 + T lymphocytes [36]. Furthermore, the interaction between tumors and distant metastatic sites has been linked to the activity of extracellular vesicles (EVs) and tumor-derived exosomes (TDEs) [37–39]. However, the immune function within the bone is relatively suppressed. A majority of the immune cells residing in the bone marrow face challenges in effectively controlling the proliferation of cancerous cells. Such suppression may stem from the prevalence of numerous immature and suppressive immune cells within the pre-metastatic niches [40]. These include substantial populations of immunosuppressive cells such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and Tregs, both of which serve to attenuate the function of cytotoxic lymphocytes crucial to tumor immune surveillance by secreting corresponding cytokines [41]. This dampening effect reduces the capacity to eliminate cancer cells. Contributing further to this "vicious cycle" are cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). CAFs can be upregulated by TGF- β Secretion and recruitment of macrophages to activate PD-1 and other factors induce inhibition of the immune microenvironment [42, 43]. Moreover, The bone microenvironment consists of immunosuppressive microenvironment consisting of bone metastases with immunological heterogeneity. The substances in the microenvironment may affect the efficacy of immunotherapy for bone metastases [15, 44–46].

The bone microenvironment in metastasis presents a complex challenge. While a small but significant population of cytotoxic cells exists, they are outnumbered by immature or suppressive immune cells. This interplay between osteoclasts, osteoblasts, and these immune cells fosters immune evasion by tumor cells, ultimately hindering the efficacy of immunotherapy. Therefore, a deep understanding of the unique bone immune microenvironment is crucial to improving immunotherapy for treating bone metastasis.

The Application of Immunotherapy in Tumor Bone Metastasis

Cancer immunotherapeutic strategies encompass ICIs and adoptive cellular therapies (ACTs), have prolonged survival time for advanced cancer patients. This progress has opened new avenues for treating tumor bone metastasis. We have therefore explored the application of immunotherapy in this specific context.

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors

ICIs represent a potent form of cancer immunotherapy. They work by blocking inhibitory signals that suppress T-cell activation. This allows T cells that recognize and target tumors to overcome these regulatory mechanisms and unleash a formidable anti-tumor immune response [47]. While the use of ICIs for treating bone metastasis presents some uncertainties, such as lower response rates observed in some patients [48], Despite their limitations, they continue to be a promising treatment option, particularly for patients with surgically-intractable bone metastases. It has been observed that immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have the potential to induce long-lasting immune responses in certain patients [49]. ICIs typically target immune checkpoints, such as PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4, which participate in the process of tumor immune evasion. By impeding these signals, ICIs can effectively reactivate the immune system's response [50].

Monotherapy with ICIs

Widely used in treating various malignant tumors, ICIs have demonstrated efficacy in patients suffering from liver and brain metastases. However, the clinical benefit of monotherapy with ICIs for patients with bone metastases remains inconclusive, with most reports on PD-1 inhibitors in this context being case studies.

Within the bone microenvironment, PD-1 is frequently expressed at high levels by activated T cells [49]. T-cell activation can induce PD-1 expression [49]. This protein is highly expressed on Treg cells, and in the presence of its ligands, it can enhance Treg cell proliferation. Since many tumors are infiltrated with Treg cells that suppress the immune response, blocking the PD-1 pathway represents a potential approach to enhance anti-tumor immunity by reducing Treg cell abundance or activity within tumors [49, 51]. These observations suggest that interventions targeting the bone microenvironment could offer specific therapeutic benefits.

Furthermore, a Phase II clinical trial (KEYNOTE-199) demonstrated antitumor activity and an acceptable safety profile of pembrolizumab monotherapy within a subset of patients diagnosed with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC). This subgroup also showed encouraging overall survival (OS) outcomes [52]. Additionally, studies by Xue Chao revealed that the expression of PD-1 and PD-L1 in patients suffering from breast cancer bone metastasis (BCBM) suggests potential therapeutic benefits of immune checkpoint inhibitors in certain BCBM cases [53].

PD-1 Combined with CTLA-4 Therapy

Although, numerous clinical studies have reported unsatisfactory outcomes. [9, 54–57], further research is warranted to address challenges such as improving efficacy, determining the optimal tolerated dose, and identifying the best timing and duration of treatment. Additionally, future studies are likely to focus on developing optimal combination regimens involving ICIs and refining strategies for managing and treating adverse reactions.

Among ICIs, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) are the most studied. Targeting these proteins has proven effective in activating anti-tumor immune responses [58]. There are experimental reports that dual blockade of PD-1 with LAG-3 or CTLA-4 has been shown to synergistically enhance T cell effector function, thereby delaying the growth of mouse ovarian tumors [59]. The combined blockade of PD-1 and CTLA-4 can increase the infiltration of effector T cells (Teff), resulting in a highly favorable ratio of Teff and regulatory T cells to the tumor [60, 61]. It is now understood that CTLA-4, a transmembrane glycoprotein receptor on regulatory and memory T cells, suppresses the immune system when it binds to CD80/86 on antigen-presenting cells like DCs [62]. Both PD-1 and CTLA-4 are upregulated on T cells under chronic antigen stimulation or inflammation. This helps reduce tissue damage from autoimmune responses, highlighting their role in suppressing autoimmunity [63]. Conversely, CTLA-4 acts as an anti-osteoclast molecule, directly binding to osteoclast precursor cells and suppressing their differentiation [64]. Blocking CTLA-4 function can alleviate the suppression of T cell-mediated immunity, potentially promoting immune-mediated tumor regression [65]. In this context, interventions in the bone microenvironment may yield specific therapeutic effects on the disease. Interestingly, CTLA-4 and PD-1 affect different components of the immune response, with CTLA-4 playing a major role in the activation of the CD4 effector region, particularly inducing the amplification of the Inducible T cell CO-Stimulator(ICOS) + Th1-like CD4 effector, whereas PD-1 mainly regulates the proliferation of CTLs [60, 61].

Combining PD-1 inhibitors (PD-1i) with CTLA-4 inhibitors (CTLA-4i) has shown significantly increased response rates and longer median survival in cancer patients compared to monotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors, including in cases of hepatocellular carcinoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and melanoma [66]. An initial experimental study demonstrated the safety and tolerability of combining Tremelimumab and Durvalumab in patients with bone metastatic mCRPC, even exhibiting antitumor activity in a subset of patients [67]. Moreover, the use of PD-1i, or the combination of Nivolumab and Ipilimumab, alongside Denosumab has shown promising efficacy with manageable safety profiles in patients with bone metastases [68]. CTLA-4 and PD-1 have been found to play a crucial role in mediating anti-tumor immune efficacy. In patients with metastatic osteosarcoma, they effectively limited the progression of tumors and significantly improved survival rates. Inhibitors of CTLA-4 and PD-1 have become an attractive method to enhance the immune efficacy against malignant tumors and improve the prognosis of cancer patients [69]. In summary, combining CTLA-4 and PD-1 blockers effectively enhances response rates and survival for specific types of tumor bone metastases, overcoming limitations of traditional therapies and chemotherapy while offering the benefits of combination treatment.

Data from recent studies suggest promise for ICI-based treatment of bone metastasis, with potential benefits including lower recurrence rates, durable antitumor effects, and a reduced risk of severe side effects. However, Clinical data on both monotherapy and combination therapy suggests that the effectiveness of ICIs in treating and eliminating bone metastasis remains limited, highlighting a substantial unmet medical need. Large-scale clinical trials are urgently needed to better assess the response rates and long-term efficacy of single-agent and combination ICI regimens, while also seeking to minimize the frequency and severity of adverse events.

Adoptive Cell Therapy

Adoptive cell therapy is a type of immunotherapy with potential benefits for patients who struggle with first-line treatment options for bone metastasis. ACT offers a distinct approach compared to conventional therapies. Instead of directly attacking cancer cells with external agents, ACT leverages the patient's own immune system by expanding and activating immune cells in the lab and then infusing them back into the patient to target and eliminate the cancer [70]. Four types of ACT are currently undergoing significant research advancements in clinical trials: tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy, engineered T-cell receptor (TCR) therapy, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, and natural killer (NK) cell therapy.

Tumor Infiltrating Lymphocyte Therapy

TIL therapy can precisely identify and eliminate tumor cells while exhibiting minimal cytotoxicity to healthy tissues. Additionally, it can be effectively targeted to tumor sites following intravenous administration [71]. Clinical trials are currently exploring how to "tailor" TIL therapy for patients with bone metastases. A preliminary study compared patients with various bone metastatic cancers who received TIL immunotherapy to those who received only standard therapy. The study showed a 30% effectiveness rate for TIL therapy, and combining it with standard therapy significantly increased the median survival time of patients [72].

Despite its potential, TIL therapy for bone metastasis requires further development to improve its effectiveness and broaden its use. Ongoing clinical trials are exploring ways to enhance its anti-tumor activity and increase the number of TILs available for treatment, ultimately aiming to overcome current limitations and fully realize the clinical potential of TIL therapy in this setting. In a study, through univariate analysis of relevant clinicopathological factors affecting bone metastasis in patients with PCa, it was found that PSA, Gleason score, tumor stage, nodal metastasis, CD4 TILs infiltration, and NSD2 expression were related to bone metastasis (P < 0.05), while age and CD8 TILs infiltration were not (P > 0.05). In addition, univariate and multivariate logistic stepwise regression analysis suggested that PSA and CD4 TILs infiltration level are independent risk factors affecting the bone metastasis of PCa [73]. Some research results have confirmed a new mechanism. The breast cancer model has proved that bone metastasis contains tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL), which provides a basic principle for the use of immunotherapy, because the activation of T cells will increase their anti-cancer and osteoclast properties. Therefore, using immunotherapy to activate T cells can not only help T cells kill cancer cells, but also prevent the formation of osteoclasts and bone loss associated with bone metastasis in cancer patients [5, 74].

Engineered T-cell Receptor Therapy

The function of TCR therapy is to isolate, activate, and enrich T cells in the patient's body. These cells then utilize polyclonal T cells of abnormally present cancer antigens to endow themselves with new receptors, enabling them to recognize unique epitopes presented by cancer antigens [75, 76]. TCR-T cells possess a larger library of targeted antigens due to its ability to recognize both intracellular and extracellular antigens [77]. Moreover, only a small amount of antigens are required to activate TCR-T cells to interact with tumor cells. Benefiting from these advantages, TCR therapy has emerged as a promising treatment for tumors and many clinical trials have been reported to show positive results in the cure of different cancers [78–80].

Screening for new antigens and improving TCR binding affinity to enable TCR-T cells to accurately and efficiently target most tumor cells are important for the development of TCR therapy [81].

However, despite the promise of TCR therapy as a cellular immunotherapy modality, several challenges regarding its safety and efficacy in treating bone metastatic tumors remain.

Chimeric Antigen Receptor Cell Therapy

Driven by the urgent need for novel therapies against advanced cancers, researchers are exploring the promising potential of "potentially curative" cell-based treatments like CAR-T therapy, marking an innovative epoch in the field of immunotherapy [82]. One study demonstrates that mCRPC treatment with γδ-enriched CAR-T cells resulted in rapid and significant tumor regression, extended survival rates, and alleviated bone disease associated with cancer [83]. A Phase 1 clinical trial by Dorff investigated a 4-1BB-co-stimulatory CAR-T cell therapy targeting PCCA. While responses were limited to stable disease due to concurrent bone metastasis, the treatment still showed some clinical efficacy [81]. Based on the current understanding, this research is the first to assess the use of γδ T cells expressing CAR for the treatment of bone metastatic prostate cancer. The Vγ9Vδ2 subset is particularly well-suited for CAR expression due to its high abundance in peripheral blood, simple protocols for ex vivo expansion, and potential in vivo synergism with ZOL. A study has shown that Vγ9Vδ2 CAR-T cells are highly effective in reducing the burden of bone metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) and in preventing cancer-induced bone disease. Additionally, the data indicates that combining γδ CAR-T cells with the bisphosphonate ZOL enhances these responses, leading to dual activation characterized by increased cytotoxicity and cytokine secretion. These findings provide strong justification for advancing γδ CAR-T cell therapy to clinical trials for the treatment of men with bone mCRPC who have not responded to available chemotherapy and/or hormonal therapy [83].

A recent study by Zhang et al. developed CAR-T cells carrying both anti-PSMA chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) and dominant-negative TGF-β type II receptors. This approach aims to overcome resistance to TGF-β-mediated inhibition of cytotoxic T-cell function and holds promise for patients with bone mCRPC [84]. While CAR-T cell therapies hold significant therapeutic potential, further research is crucial to definitively determine their ability to reduce or eliminate bone metastases in upcoming clinical trials. Developing more comprehensive metastatic models is essential to gain a clearer understanding of this treatment's true effectiveness. Additionally, mitigating post-treatment recurrence remains a central focus of ongoing investigations into CAR-T cell therapy.

Natural Killer Cell Therapy

Natural killer cells prevent metastasis by eliminating circulating cancer stem cells in the absence of NK cells and IFN- γ The treatment of neutralizing antibodies and cancer models with CD39 deficiency have demonstrated their anti metastatic potential [85]. Their ability to survey the body for threats independent of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) has garnered significant attention, but their involvement in bone physiology and metastasis remains under investigation [86]. Studies on prostate cancer (PCa) bone metastasis suggest a role for NK cells, as demonstrated by increased NK cell activity upon overexpression of IRF7 [87]. Additionally, a breast cancer bone metastasis model showed that inhibiting TGF-β signaling markedly diminished tumor burden in bone and enhanced NK cell anti-tumor activity, leading to the inhibition of bone metastasis formation [88].

While extensive research is needed to fully understand if enhanced NK cell immunosurveillance can be effectively harnessed to prevent or treat bone metastasis in cancer patients, NK cell therapy presents an innovative and promising avenue with significant potential for future development.

Adoptive cell therapy has witnessed a surge in research activity and groundbreaking discoveries in recent years. Its perception has gradually shifted from "non-traditional treatments" to "complementary adjunct therapies alongside standard modalities". These innovative therapies can be effectively combined with the three mainstays of cancer treatment—chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and surgery—to improve survival rates for patients with metastatic disease. Researchers are relentlessly exploring ways to harness and modulate the immune response, utilizing next-generation cellular immunotherapies to achieve clinical benefits and offering renewed hope for improved patient outcomes.

Tumor Vaccines

Tumor vaccines work by mimicking cancer cells. Once injected, the body's immune system identifies these simulated cells, triggering the production of antibodies and a T-cell response against the actual cancer cells [89]. This approach shows promise in hindering the dissemination of tumor cells to various organs, including the bones, with minimal impact on healthy tissues. It has demonstrated benefits in treating metastatic cancers like non-small cell lung cancer, prostate cancer, and breast cancer [90]. For example, an genetically modified tumor cell vaccine called GVAX are used to express Granulocyte–Macrophage Colony-Stimulating Factor (GM-CSF) which can achieve anti-tumor efficacy [91]. Prostate GVAX is composed of two cell lines, LN-CaP from prostate cancer lymphonode metastases and PC-3 from prostate cancer bone metastases, to provide specific prostate cancer antigens [91]. Simons et al. found that patients who used the GVAX vaccine survived longer than those who received chemotherapy [92].

Some studies support the potential of tumor vaccines in reducing bone metastasis. Shijie Ma's team's research suggests that mPLA/mRNA tumor vaccine (mLPR) effectively targets bone metastasis in lung cancer by promoting a robust immune response. After nasal administration, this vaccine enhances the maturation of dendritic cells and reprograms M2 macrophages to M1, thereby activating both innate and adaptive immune systems. These immunological changes result in increased secretion of IFN-γ and IL-12 cytokines and activate natural killer cells, which contribute to significant reductions in bone metastases through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. This approach highlights the potential of mRNA vaccines in managing metastatic progression in lung cancer patients [93]. Similarly, DC vaccine therapy has also shown benefits for cancer patients with multiple bone metastases [94]. These studies collectively highlight the potential pathways through which tumor vaccines exert their effects.

Tumor vaccines, while not universally successful yet, offer a potentially gentler approach to managing bone metastases in cancer patients compared to chemotherapy and radiotherapy. These conventional treatments often cause severe side effects because they target both cancerous and healthy, rapidly dividing cells. In contrast, cancer vaccines act more precisely, triggering the immune system to specifically target and attack cancer cells. This tailored approach has the capability to significantly enhance the quality of life for patients by offering a less aggressive treatment experience.

Non-Specific Immune Modulators

Non-specific immunotherapy plays a complementary role in oncological treatment alongside surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. It utilizes specialized immune modulators to stimulate the immune system and induce a more robust anti-tumor reaction. These modulators, which can be immune enhancers, suppressants, or bidirectional regulators, affect substances that activate or suppress the immune system [95].

Over the past years, several studies have explored the potential of non-specific immune modulators for bone metastasis. Juárez P. demonstrated that blocking TGF-β signaling with immune modulators effectively treats and prevents bone metastasis while increasing bone mass [96]. Within the context of treating bone metastasis from breast cancer, Tulotta C. et al. proposed that targeting IL-1B signaling in breast cancer might keep dormant tumor cells within the bone, preventing overt metastasis [97]. Similarly, Buijs JT. found that BMP7 could inhibit local and bone metastasis of breast cancer by blocking TGF-β signaling [98]. Compared with breast cancer, prostate cancer progression and metastasis are closely linked to inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. Studies have shown that the IL-1 family, which is highly expressed in PCa patients with bone metastasis, can serve as a critical biomarker for predicting disease stage and prognosis [99]. This suggests that the IL-1 family exerts a considerable influence in the progression and bone metastasis of many cancers.

However, limitations like small sample sizes and inconsistent effectiveness currently confine the use of immune modulators primarily to adjunctive therapy. More extensive, prospective clinical trials are needed to definitively assess their efficacy and solidify their role in clinical practice.

Despite promising results from current immunotherapy and combination therapies for bone metastasis, their use remains limited, primarily playing a supportive role. Additionally, many combination therapies in clinical trials lack a clear scientific basis, relying on trial-and-error approaches. Further research is essential to establish well-defined strategies and methodologies for these combinations. This requires addressing challenges like determining appropriate efficacy evaluation methods and understanding the potential side effects of combined therapies.

Application of Immunotherapy Combined with Other Treatments in Tumor Bone Metastasis

Surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy can rapidly eliminate a significant multitude of tumor cells and reduce the tumor burden in bone metastasis. Immunotherapy is also showing promise in this area. However, no single treatment is completely effective in eliminating all cancer cells.

To mitigate the effects of malignant bone metastases and the incidence of SRE events, comprehensive treatment plans are often preferable to single-modality therapy [100]. Accordingly, the combination of immunotherapy with conventional treatments like surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy holds promise. By combining these approaches, clinicians aim to achieve longer-lasting removal of residual tumor cells and potentially improve the body's immune function, which can be impaired by radiation and chemotherapy.

Immunotherapy Combined with Radiotherapy in Bone Metastases

In clinical management of bone metastases, the combination of radiotherapy (RT) with immunotherapy has drawn considerable interest from researchers.Bone RT directly induces apoptosis in tumor cells, releases tumor antigens, and triggers immune activation. The integration of immunotherapy with RT is premised on radiation-induced cell death leading to the localized release of these antigens, thereby fostering cross-presentation by dendritic cells and the establishment of long-lasting immune memory in T cells [101, 102]. This approach harnesses the ability of RT to activate the immune system in concert with immunotherapy, eliciting synergistic effects. Radiotherapy further generates immune-mediated antitumor responses that can lead to regression of non-irradiated distant metastases beyond the primary irradiation site, overcoming mechanisms that allow tumors to evade immune recognition and enhancing both local and systemic immune responses [103].

Moreover, multiple studies have demonstrated synergistic effects when immunotherapy is combined with palliative bone radiotherapy [104]. Studies by Deng et al. showed that radiotherapy increases PD-L1 expression, a protein that can inhibit the immune system. Combining radiotherapy with anti-PD-L1 drugs, which block this protein, can enhance the body's anti-tumor immunity and improve treatment effectiveness compared to either therapy alone [105]. Similarly, research by Yang Liu explored how immunotherapy, particularly ICIs, can further strengthen the systemic anti-tumor effects of radiotherapy [106].

Studies support the potential benefits of combining immunotherapy with other therapies for bone metastases. For instance, a controlled trial involving melanoma patients with bone metastases demonstrated that the best overall survival occurred in those receiving both immunotherapy and palliative radiotherapy [107]. In another study, the radiopharmaceutical samarium-153-EDTMP used for bone metastases was shown to potentially synergistic effect on immunotherapy to enhance tumor cell susceptibility to immune attack [108]. Similarly, research by Huiping Qiang suggests the effectiveness of combining systemic immunotherapy with palliative bone radiotherapy for disease control and improved survival in patients suffering from advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) bone metastases [109]. Their study also highlighted that patients receiving concurrent bone-targeted therapy or palliative bone radiotherapy alongside immunotherapy experience additional benefits. Multifactorial analysis also plays a significant role in predicting progression-free survival (PFS) [109]. Radium-223 (Ra-223) has shown promising results in extending survival and improving symptoms for men with mCRPC and symptomatic bone metastases [110]. Future research directions are exploring its combination with immunotherapy, with ongoing trials like NCT03093428 investigating the potential of combining Ra-223 and pembrolizumab for this patient population.

The cases presented above demonstrate that combining immunotherapy with radiotherapy often leads to better outcomes compared to single therapies, showing promise in specific areas. This finding provides valuable guidance for treating patients with bone metastases. However, the most effective order and timing for administering radiotherapy alongside immunotherapy remain unclear and require further clinical trials to establish the best approach.

Combining immunotherapy with chemotherapy regimens presents promising prospects as a prospective treatment approach for metastatic bone disease, as evidenced by several proposed trials showcasing clinical benefits. Studies in NSCLC with bone metastases have shown promising results with combination therapies. Combining pembrolizumab with standard chemotherapy and platinum-based agents significantly improved PFS and OS compared to chemotherapy alone [120]. Similarly, a study by Howard West et al. demonstrated that atelizumab plus chemotherapy offered a significant improvement in PFS compared to chemotherapy alone [121]. Xue Wang's research further supports this, indicating a favorable survival benefit for the combination therapy group in patients with NSCLC bone metastases [122]. Moving beyond NSCLC, research in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer suggests that combining dendritic cell-based immunotherapy (DCVAC/PCa) with chemotherapy is safe and effective. This approach did not increase bone metastases and even demonstrated longer-than-expected survival rates [123]. IL-1B can cause immune suppression, therefore it has been found that combining immunotherapy to suppress this cytokine can promote anti-tumor immunity, and chemotherapy can treat bone metastasis. Therefore, Claudia Tulotta and others proposed a future treatment plan for bone metastasis of breast cancer: combining IL-1B signal suppression with standard chemotherapy to reduce bone metastasis [124–126].

These clinical trials suggest combining immunotherapy with chemotherapy may improve patient outcomes for bone metastases from various tumors. Optimizing treatment regimens for individual patients to maximize the effectiveness of these combination therapies will be crucial for developing personalized and potentially curative approaches in the future.

Immunotherapy Combined with Bone-Targeted Drug Therapy in Bone Metastasis

Combining immunotherapy with bone-targeted drug therapy has emerged as a promising approach in the treatment of bone metastasis. Bone-targeted therapy (BTT), which includes drugs like denosumab and bisphosphonates, is widely accepted as the standard treatment for bone metastases caused by malignant tumors. These drugs function by inhibiting osteoclasts, the cells responsible for bone breakdown, thereby preventing further bone destruction and improving patient outcomes [127, 128]. Bisphosphonates address this issue by inhibiting osteoclast formation and growth, strengthening bones and reducing fractures and maintaining calcium levels [129, 130]. Denosumab, a monoclonal antibody of human origin, operates via a distinct mechanism. It targets the RANK/RANKL pathway, a key signaling mechanism for osteoclast development. By blocking this pathway, denosumab prevents osteoclast formation and reduces the risk of bone-related complications like fractures in patients with osteoporosis and bone metastases [131]. Osteoblasts and bone marrow stromal cells produce RANKL, a protein that attracts cancer cells expressing RANK. This attraction triggers specific signaling cascades, particularly the MAPK pathway, causing cancer cells to aggregate, migrate, and ultimately form bone metastases while degrading surrounding bone tissue [132]. Research indicates that a protein known as soluble PD-L1 (sPD-L1) can bind to PD-1 on osteoclast precursors. This binding leads to the release of CCL2, a molecule that further promotes osteoclastogenesis induced by RANKL [133]. Given these interactions, studies have shown that combining immune checkpoint inhibitors with drugs that target RANKL is more effective at suppressing metastatic activity compared to using either type of drug alone [134]. These findings indicate that combining immunotherapy with BTT might be a more effective treatment approach for patients who are experiencing bone metastases.

Studies imply that the combination of immune checkpoint inhibitors and denosumab, a bone-targeted therapy, may have synergistic effects. For instance, a prospective Italian study found that NSCLC patients diagnosed with bone metastases who received ICIs alongside BTT experienced a higher overall survival rate contrasted with those receiving ICIs alone [145]. Similarly, a Chinese clinical trial demonstrated that denosumab, when used with ICIs in patients suffering from advanced NSCLC and bone metastases, offered synergistic antitumor efficacy without increased toxicity [136]. Further research supports this approach. Yohei Asano's study showed that combining ICIs with denosumab in advanced NSCLC patients led to longer overall survival compared to ICI therapy alone. This study also suggests that pabolizumab combined with denosumab may be the most effective treatment for both primary lung lesions and bone metastases [146]. Additionally, Yenny Angela's team found that combining PD-1 inhibitors with denosumab for metastatic melanoma and bone metastases appears feasible and safe, with promising early signs of efficacy [68]. A smaller study involving 10 melanoma patients presenting with bone metastases treated with denosumab and ICIs reported a 60% objective remission rate and an 80% disease control rate, with an average treatment duration of 9.8 months [24].

While preliminary studies suggest promise, large-scale randomized clinical trials are still crucial for assessing the effectiveness and safety of combining denosumab with ICIs for treating bone metastases definitively. Obtaining more clinical data on this combination therapy would offer significant hope for patients with advanced bone metastases who currently have limited treatment options.

Adverse Effects of Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy offers promising advancements for treating bone metastases in cancer, but various approaches face challenges and uncertainties that require further research. Notably, these treatments can cause side effects, which are crucial concerns for clinical practice to manage and address.

Patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors for bone metastases often experience immune-related adverse events (irAEs). These can include common irAEs like skin reactions, digestive issues, lung problems, and hormonal imbalances [147]. Furthermore, patients may encounter symptoms related to bones and muscles, such as arthritis, muscle weakness, loss of bone density, and an elevated risk of fractures [5, 148–151]. Despite these potential side effects, many are manageable through close monitoring, symptom control, and continued treatment with ICIs in most cases.

Conclusions and Perspectives

Immunotherapy offers hope for treating bone metastases previously considered untreatable, given that the immune system inside the tumor microenvironment plays a key part in how bone metastases develop. Researchers are exploring new approaches that target these immune cells directly within the bone metastases. Additionally, they are combining immunotherapy with other treatments like radiation, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies to see which patient groups benefit most. This multi-pronged approach aims to improve early diagnosis and potentially stop cancer progression.

However, bone metastases remain a significant challenge. Studies like the one by Angel Qin suggest that patients with bone metastases who receive ICI therapy may have shorter survival times and respond less favorably than those without bone involvement, highlighting the need for further research [54]. Additionally, patients with bone metastases often develop resistance to ICIs and tend to have worse outcomes overall [152]. The impact of bone metastases on immunotherapy effectiveness remains an ongoing area of investigation, with ongoing clinical trials aiming to clarify this relationship.
