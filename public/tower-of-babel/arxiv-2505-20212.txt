R. H. D. Townsend, R. V. Kuenzi, J. Christensen-Dalsgaard

Abstract
Stellar oscillation codes are software instruments that evaluate the normal-mode frequencies of an input stellar model. While inter-code comparisons are often used to confirm the correctness of calculations, they are not suitable for characterizing the numerical error of an individual code. To address this issue, we introduce a set of tools — ‘error measures’ — that facilitate this characterization. We explore the behavior of these error measures as calculation parameters, such as the number of radial grid points used to discretize the oscillation equations, are varied; and we summarize this behavior via an idealized error model. While our analysis focuses on the GYRE code, it remains broadly applicable to other oscillation codes.

Introduction

Recent space photometry missions, including

can leverage these
data into quantitative constraints on the global
properties (mass, radius, age, metallicity, etc.) and detailed
interior structures of the stars observed.

A key ingredient in asteroseismic modeling is a stellar oscillation

of an input stellar model. Many such codes are described in the
literature; examples include MAD
[

,

[

, PULSE
[

,

[

,

, NOSC
[

,

, filou
[

,

[

, and

[

,
54
,
25
,
50
]
. Given
the community’s increasing reliance on these complex software
packages, it is important to verify that they yield results that are
not only precise but also accurate.

Historically, a number of papers have examined the problem of code
verification. In considering the oscillations of polytropic models,

Mullan & Ulrich [36]
employ a convergence criterion that the change in
mode frequencies, upon increasing the number of radial grid points used to
discretize the oscillation equations, drops below a prescribed
threshold. Such an approach can ensure that frequencies are precise,
but does not by itself also guarantee accuracy. In a subsequent paper,

Christensen-Dalsgaard &
Mullan [16]
expand on this work by undertaking
a comprehensive comparison between the polytrope-model frequencies
evaluated by a pair of independent oscillation codes. More recently,

Moya et al. [35]
compare many of the codes cited above, as applied
to realistic stellar models.

Inter-code comparisons provide confidence that oscillation codes are
yielding reliable results. However, their utility is limited when it
comes to characterizing the numerical error of an individual code —
for instance, diagnosing the various mechanisms that contribute toward
this error, and exploring whether these mechanisms behave as expected
from theoretical considerations. Such characterizations can
potentially highlight hidden bugs, and are key to deciding if and how
the code can further be improved.

Within this context, the present paper introduces a set of tools for
characterizing the numerical error of oscillation codes. We introduce
these tools — ‘error measures’ — in Section
II
, and
demonstrate their application to the GYRE code in
Section
III
. We summarize our findings in
Section
IV
, and discuss some of the practicalities of
using the tools in the context of asteroseismic studies.

II
Error measures

The numerical error of an oscillation code can in principle be
quantified by comparing the code’s outputs (eigenfrequencies,
eigenfunctions, etc.) against exact solutions. In practice, however,
the latter are known only for the simple and quite artificial case of
the homogeneous compressible sphere
[
42
, see]
. To
proceed, therefore, we heed these words from

Christensen-Dalsgaard &
Berthomieu [14]
: “The only practical way of
estimating the numerical error in the computed frequencies is to
compare results obtained with different numbers of mesh [grid]
points”.

The general process is straightforward: run a code multiple times
while varying the number of radial grid points
N
, and explore how
its outputs change with
N
. As a straightforward example, one might
follow
Mullan & Ulrich [36]
and focus on how the frequency
\sigma
of
a given mode depends on
N
. However, as we already mention above,
this approach ensures precision but does not guarantee accuracy. The
problem lies with the choice of
\sigma
as the output to monitor; by
itself, it furnishes no indication that it lies close to the
correct value. To address this, we introduce the concept of an error
measure
\mathcal{E}
, fulfilling the following criteria:

(i)

\mathcal{E}
must be calculable from the outputs of the oscillation
code;

(ii)

\mathcal{E}
must be insensitive to non-physical quantities such as
the normalization of eigenfunctions;

(iii)

\mathcal{E}
must approach zero as numerical solutions approach exact
solutions of the oscillation equations.

The last criterion is key to ensuring that precision and accuracy go
hand-in-hand. In the subsections that follow, we introduce three
specific definitions of the error measure. To keep the presentation
compact, we defer much of the detailed mathematical formalism to
Appendix
A
.

II.1
Frequency error measure

As discussed in Appendix
A.2
, the frequency of a mode can be
evaluated from various integrals over its eigenfunctions. For
numerical solutions, this ‘integral’ frequency will not agree exactly
with the eigenfrequency output by the oscillation code, and we
introduce the frequency error measure

\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}\equiv\frac{\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}-\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}}{\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}}

(1)

to quantify their relative difference. Here,
\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}
is the
eigenfrequency output by the code, and
\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}
is the
integral frequency evaluated using equation (
A8
);
throughout, the subscripts
n
and
\ell
denote the radial order and
harmonic degree, respectively, of the mode.

II.2
Orthogonality error measure

As discussed in Appendix
A.3
, the displacement
eigenfunctions of a pair of oscillation modes with the same harmonic
degree but different radial orders (
n,n^{\prime}
) are orthogonal if the
surface term (
A6
) satisfies
\mathcal{S}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{n^{\prime},n,\ell}^{\ast}
. Numerical solutions will not satisfy this
property exactly, and we introduce the orthogonality
error measure

\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{P}}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}\equiv\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}}{\left(\mathcal{P}_{n,n,\ell}\,\mathcal{P}_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime},\ell}\right)^{1/2}},

(2)

to quantify the discrepancy; here, the inner product

\mathcal{P}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}
is defined in equation (
A4
). In
accordance with criterion (
ii
), above, the denominator
ensures that this measure remains independent of the arbitrary
normalization of eigenfunctions.

II.3
First-Integral Error Measure

As shown by
Takata [52]
, the oscillation equations for radial
(
\ell=0
) and dipole (
\ell=1
) modes admit first integrals that
vanish everywhere for solutions that remain regular at the
origin. Once again, numerical solutions to the equations will not
satisfy this property exactly, and we introduce the first-integral
error measure

\mathcal{E}^{I}_{n,\ell}\equiv\frac{\max(I_{n,\ell})-\min(I_{n,\ell})}{\mathcal{P}_{n,n,\ell}^{1/2}}

(3)

to quantify the discrepancy. Here,
I_{n,\ell}
(
\ell=0,1
) are the
first integrals defined in equations (42) and (43) of

Takata [52]
for radial and dipole cases, respectively; and
the
\min()
and
\max()
operators denote the minimum and maximum
value attained over all points of the radial grid. As with
equation (
2
), the denominator ensures
that this measure is independent of eigenfunction normalization.

III
Calculations

For a variety of stellar models, we explore how the error measures
introduced above behave as a function of the number of radial grid
points
N
. We use release 8.0 of the GYRE code, which
includes support for the evaluated models discussed in
Section
III.1
. At the stellar surface
r=R
we impose
the zero-pressure mechanical boundary condition

\delta\tilde{P}_{n,\ell}\equiv\tilde{P}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}-g\,\rho\,\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n,\ell}=0,

(4)

together with the potential boundary condition

\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\tilde{\Phi}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}}}{\operatorname{d}\!{r}}+\frac{\ell+1}{r}\tilde{\Phi}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}=-4\pi\,G\,\rho\,\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n,\ell}

(5)

[
18
, see, e.g.,]
. Together, these boundary conditions
mean that
\mathcal{S}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{n^{\prime},n,\ell}^{\ast}
(see
equation
A14
), and thus eigenfunctions
should be orthogonal. We briefly discuss the impact of other
boundary-condition choices in Section
IV
.

With the exception of a few special cases that we highlight when
necessary, the grids employed by GYRE consist of
N=6K+1
points
uniformly distributed in fractional radius
0\leq x\equiv r/R\leq 1
, where
K
is an integer. This choice allows us to
evaluate integrals (for instance, those appearing in
equations
A8
and
A13
) using composite 7-point
Newton-Coates quadrature
[
43
]
across
K

sub-intervals. The motivation here is to ensure that the quadrature
truncation error remains small in comparison to the other sources of
numerical error arising in the calculations.

Evaluated models

We first consider stellar models where the internal structure can be

[

The analytic stellar models considered so far are helpful for
illustrating the behavior of the error measures, but they are not
representative use-cases for oscillation codes. More typically, a
model is supplied to a code as a tabulation of various structural
quantities throughout the interior of the star. In general, the radial
grid of this tabulation does not match that used to discretize the
oscillation equations, and some kind of interpolation is therefore
required. These interpolated models are the focus of the present
section.

III.2.1
n_{\rm poly}=3
polytrope

We first consider an
n_{\rm poly}=3
polytrope tabulated on a uniform

M
-point radial grid. The tabulation consists of three set of
values:
\{\xi_{j}\}
(
j=1,2,\ldots,M
) representing the
independent variable in the Lane-Emden equation,
\{\vartheta_{j}\}

representing the corresponding dependent variable, and

\{\vartheta^{\prime}_{j}\}
representing its first derivative. These data are
calculated by applying the
LSODAR
differential-equation
solver from ODEPACK
[
27
]
to the Lane-Emden
equation, with a relative tolerance parameter
10^{-15}
. They are
used by GYRE to construct piecewise cubic Hermite interpolants for
both
\vartheta(\xi)
and
\vartheta^{\prime}(\xi)
, allowing the stellar
structure to be evaluated at locations that fall between the
tabulation points.

Figure
8
plots the three error measures as a function
of
N
, for tabulations composed of
M=11
, 101 and 1001 points. All
three error measures initially decay rapidly, asymptoting toward the
expected
\mathcal{E}\sim N^{-6}
scalings, before plateauing at constant
values. This stalled convergence is a consequence of the model
interpolation: although the cubic interpolants exactly satisfy the
Lane-Emden equation at the tabulation points, elsewhere they do
not. Therefore, the model is slightly inconsistent with the stellar
structure equations.

To illustrate the inconsistencies, we introduce the defects

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{c_{1}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\ln c_{1}}}{\operatorname{d}\!{\ln x}}-\left(3-U\right),

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{U}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\ln U}}{\operatorname{d}\!{\ln x}}-\left(-\frac{V}{\Gamma_{1}}-A^{\ast}-U+3\right)

(here,
V
,
A^{\ast}
,
U
and
c_{1}
are the coefficients appearing
in the dimensionless oscillation equations; see, e.g.,

57
). These defects are derived from equations (20)
and (21), respectively, of
Takata [52]
, in such a way that
they both vanish when the equilibrium stellar structure locally
conserves mass. Figure
9
plots the defects as
a function of
x
, for the
n_{\rm poly}=3
polytrope tabulated with
M=11

points. With the exception of the tabulation points (where

\mathcal{D}^{c_{1}}=\mathcal{D}^{U}=0
by construction), it is generally the
case that the defects are non-zero, highlighting the breakdown of mass
conservation.

The net effect of this breakdown is to introduce additional terms on
the right-hand side of the error
measures (
1
–
3
), that are
independent of
N
but decrease with increasing
M
because finer
tabulations reduce the magnitude of both
\mathcal{D}^{c_{1}}
and

\mathcal{D}^{U}
. For the data shown in the figure, switching from
M=11

to
M=101
, and likewise from
M=101
to
M=1001
, yields a
\approx 10^{-4}
reduction in the error-measure plateaus, indicating that the
errors from interpolation inconsistency scale as
\sim M^{-4}
.

IV
Summary & Discussion

In the previous sections we introduce three measures,

\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}
,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{P}}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}
, and
\mathcal{E}^{I}_{n,\ell}
,
as tools for characterizing the numerical error in solutions to the
oscillation equations. For a variety of stellar models, we explore how
these error measures respond to changes in the number of radial grid
points
N
used to discretize the oscillation equations, and/or the
number of points
M
in the tabulation of the stellar structure. To
summarize the results from this investigation, we propose an idealized
model for the behavior of the error measures as a function of
N
and

M
:

|\mathcal{E}|\approx a_{\rm d}N^{-b_{\rm d}}+a_{\rm q}N^{-b_{\rm q}}+a_{\rm c}N^{-b_{\rm c}}+a_{\rm r}N+a_{\rm i}M^{-b_{\rm i}}.

(7)

Each term on the right-hand side represents a particular source of
numerical error, with the
a
coefficients setting the magnitude of
the error and the
b
coefficients determining its scaling with
N
or

M
. In detail:

•

the
a_{\rm d}N^{-b_{\rm d}}
term represents the truncation error
arising from the discretization of the oscillation equations. For
a discretization scheme with local error
O(h^{i+1})
,
b_{\rm d}=i
.

•

the
a_{\rm q}N^{-b_{\rm q}}
term represents the truncation error
arising from the quadrature used to evaluate the integral terms in
the definitions of
\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}
and

\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{P}}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}
. For a quadrature scheme with local error

O(h^{i+1})
,
b_{\rm q}=i
. The
\mathcal{E}^{I}_{n,\ell}
error
measure does not require quadrature, and so has
a_{\rm q}=0
.

•

the
a_{\rm c}N^{-b_{\rm c}}
term represents the error arising
from the lowest-order discontinuity (or discontinuities) in the
equilibrium stellar structure. If this discontinuity has an order

q
, then
b_{\rm c}=q
.

•

the
a_{\rm r}N
term represents the accumulated round-off
error.

•

the
a_{\rm i}M^{-b_{\rm i}}
term represents the
inconsistencies arising from interpolation of tabulated
models. Evaluated models do not require interpolation, and so have

a_{\rm i}=0
.

This model does not account for calculation
mistakes
— for
instance, neglecting non-zero surface terms when evaluating

\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}
(see the dot-dashed curves in the left-hand panels
of Figs.
5
). Moreover, it is intended to be
descriptive rather than prescriptive — a way of understanding the
behavior of error measures, rather than predicting this behavior

a priori
.

Although our calculations focus exclusively on the GYRE code, other
oscillation codes can benefit from a similar exploration of the error
measures’ functional dependence on
N
and
M
. In addition to
verifying basic code correctness, such exercises can furnish a
mechanism for selecting
N
,
M
values that strike an appropriate
balance between computational cost and accuracy. In this context, we
expect smaller
\mathcal{E}
to correspond to more-accurate solutions;
however, to place this connection on a more quantitative footing, it
behooves us to consider the semantics of each error measure — what
actually is being measured?

To clarify the meaning of the frequency error measure, we write the

\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}
and
\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}
terms appearing on the
right-hand side of equation (
1
) as the sum of the
exact frequency
\sigma_{n,\ell}
and a numerical error:

\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}=\sigma_{n,\ell}+\Delta\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell},\qquad\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}=\sigma_{n,\ell}+\Delta\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}.

(8)

Then, equation (
1
) becomes

\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}=\frac{\Delta\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}-\Delta\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}}{\sigma_{n,\ell}}

(9)

where we have neglected terms that have a combined error order of
second or beyond. From this, we can establish the inequality

|\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}|\leq 2\max\left(\left|\frac{\Delta\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}}{\sigma_{n,\ell}}\right|,\left|\frac{\Delta\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}}{\sigma_{n,\ell}}\right|\right),

(10)

and so the frequency error measure sets a lower bound on the magnitude
of the relative error in
\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}
or
\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}
,
whichever is greater. Turning this statement around, if it is required
that the relative error in the eigenfrequency be less than some
threshold
E
, then it is necessary that
|\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}|\leq 2E
. Unfortunately, this condition is not also sufficient;
circumstances situations can potentially arise where

|\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}|
is significantly smaller than
E
, but the
relative errors in both
\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}
and
\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}

remain larger than
E
.

The situation is somewhat improved in cases where the surface term
satisfies
\mathcal{S}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{n^{\prime},n,\ell}^{\ast}
— for
instance, due to adoption of the zero-pressure boundary
condition (
4
). The variational principle then
predicts that
(\Delta\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}/\sigma_{n,\ell})\sim(\Delta\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}/\sigma_{n,\ell})^{2}
. In the limit

|\Delta\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}/\sigma_{n,\ell}|\ll 1
, the second term
in the numerator of equation (
9
) can be neglected,
and equation (
10
) then reduces to

|\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}|\approx\left|\frac{\Delta\sigma^{\rm e}_{n,\ell}}{\sigma_{n,\ell}}\right|,

(11)

indicating that the frequency error measure
is
the relative
error in the eigenfrequency. However, a word of caution here

[
15
, echoing the remarks in section 4
of]
:
\Delta\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}

includes a contribution from the truncation error that arises when
evaluating the integrals in equation (
A8
), and this
contribution is not subject to the variational principle. Therefore,
neglecting
\Delta\sigma^{\rm i}_{n,\ell}
requires that a sufficiently
accurate quadrature scheme be used.

Interpreting the orthogonality error measure is rather more
straightforward: it represents the cosine of the angle in the Hilbert
space defined by the inner products
\mathcal{P}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}
. This
information is useful when expanding solutions to certain problems in
a superposition of eigenfunctions — for instance, when calculating
the response of a star to tidal forcing by a companion object

[
10
,
23
, see, e.g.,]
. Then,

\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{P}}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}
can be used to quantify the level of numerical
leakage between terms in the expansion.

The meaning of the first-integral error measure is perhaps the most
elusive. The first integrals derived by
Takata [52]
should
vanish everywhere when a star is isolated from any outside
gravitational influence. Therefore,
\mathcal{E}^{I}_{n,\ell}\neq 0

indicates the contamination of numerical eigenfunctions with
additional perturbations that arise from an external distribution of
mass. Knowing what this distribution is seems to have little practical
value; however, knowing
where
in the star the resulting
contamination occurs is certainly useful. We demonstrate this in
Figs.
11
and Fig.
12
, where the jumps in the

I_{0}
and
I_{1}
first integrals allows us swiftly to diagnose
features in the
\mathcal{N}^{2}
profile that are causing difficulties for
GYRE.

With these considerations, we return now the question of selecting
appropriate values for
N
and
M
. For asteroseismic optimization
studies
[
2
, e.g.,]
, where the goal is to construct a
(tabulated) stellar evolutionary model whose numerical
eigenfrequencies match a set of observed frequencies to within some
relative tolerance
E
, we propose the following course of action:

(i)

For a model with a given
M
, create plots of
\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}

as a function of
N
for modes of interest, and identify the regimes
where the various terms in equation (
7
) are
dominant.

(ii)

If interpolation errors mean that
|\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}|>E
for
all
N
considered, then construct a new model with a larger
M
and
repeat step (i). As part of this process, inspecting the defects

\mathcal{D}^{c_{1}}
,
\mathcal{D}^{U}
defined in equation (
6
)
may prove useful in identifying where additional tabulation points
should be placed.

(iii)

If
|\mathcal{E}^{\sigma}_{n,\ell}|<E
once
N
exceeds some threshold

N_{\rm thr}
, then adopt the eigenfrequencies at
N_{\rm th}
. If a
smaller
N_{\rm th}
is desired (for instance, in the interests of
computational efficiency), then step (i) can be repeated using
higher-order discretization or quadrature schemes, or a distribution
of grid points that’s optimized to resolve discontinuities (the
choice here depending on which error term is dominant). Richardson
extrapolation
[
43
,
16
, e.g.,]

can also be applied in tandem with these approaches, to achieve
further reductions in
N_{\rm th}
.

Step (ii) will likely be the costliest in this procedure, as the
computational cost of stellar evolution calculations is usually much
larger than oscillation calculations. Therefore, future work should
prioritize finding ways (other than increasing
M
) to reduce the
impact of interpolation errors — for instance, by devising a set of
interpolants that are locally consistent with the stellar structure
equations.

There also remains a need to examine the influence of outer boundary
conditions on the error measures. Our choice of the zero-pressure
mechanical boundary condition (
4
) and the
potential condition (
5
) is primarily motivated by
simplicity, but also leads to favorable mathematical outcomes (e.g.,
that
\mathcal{S}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{n^{\prime},n,\ell}^{\ast}
, implying the
orthogonality of eigenfunctions). However, this choice may sacrifice
some degree of physical realism: stars possess atmospheres that extend
beyond their formal surfaces, and boundary conditions should
adequately account for the fact that oscillations can and do penetrate
out into these superficial layers.

In this regard,
Unno et al. [57, their section 18.1]
present a
more-general formulation of the surface boundary conditions, that
approximates the oscillation equations in the atmosphere as having
spatially constant coefficients
2
2

2

The older

Dziembowski [20]
boundary condition results from expanding
this formulation to first order in
V^{-1}
.
. When adopting this
formulation, it is appropriate to extend the upper bounds of the

\mathcal{W}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}
integrals
(equations
A5
and
A4
) to include the atmosphere;
this can be done by leveraging the explicit solutions to the
constant-coefficient equations
[
57
, see, e.g., equation 18.36
of]
. However, we anticipate that additional
contributions to the error measures will arise due to the
approximations made in treating the atmosphere. We plan to explore
this matter in detail in a future paper.

On a closing note, we highlight that the tools and analysis presented
here address only one part of the asteroseismic code verification
problem. It is equally important to explore the numerical errors
arising in the stellar evolution calculations that feed into
oscillation codes. The recent work by
Li & Joyce [32]
is an important
step in this direction.

Appendix A
Mathematical Formalism

In this appendix we lay out the formalism that provides the basis for
the error measures introduced in Section
II
. The goal is to
provide sufficient detail that there can be no ambiguity in their
definition. Throughout, we assume a context of linear, adiabatic
oscillations of spherical, non-rotating stars.

A.1
Perturbation Forms

The linearized fluid equations describing small departures from the
equilibrium state of a star can be separated in all three
spherical-polar coordinates
(r,\theta,\phi)
and in time
t
. For a
mode with radial order
n
, harmonic degree
\ell
and azimuthal order

m
, we express the fluid displacement perturbation vector in the form

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n,\ell,m}(\mathbf{r};t)=\left\{\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n,\ell}(r)\,\mathbf{e}_{r}+\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm h};n,\ell}(r)\left[\mathbf{e}_{\theta}\,\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{\theta}}+\mathbf{e}_{\phi}\,\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\,\frac{\partial{}}{\partial{\phi}}\right]\right\}\,Y^{m}_{\ell}(\theta,\phi)\,\exp{(-\mathrm{i}\,\sigma_{n,\ell}\,t)}.

(A1)

Here,
\mathbf{r}
denotes the position vector;
(\mathbf{e}_{r},\mathbf{e}_{\theta},\mathbf{e}_{\phi})

are the spherical-polar basis vectors at
\mathbf{r}
; and
Y^{m}_{\ell}

is a spherical harmonic. The accompanying Eulerian perturbations to
the pressure
P
and self-gravitational potential
\Phi
take the form

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{n,\ell,m}(\mathbf{r};t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{P}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}(r)\,Y^{m}_{\ell}(\theta,\phi)\,\exp{(-\mathrm{i}\,\sigma_{n,\ell}\,t)},

(A2)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}_{n,\ell,m}(\mathbf{r};t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Phi}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}(r)\,Y^{m}_{\ell}(\theta,\phi)\,\exp{(-\mathrm{i}\,\sigma_{n,\ell}\,t)},

respectively.

In these expressions, the functions with tilde accents
(
\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};\ell,n},\tilde{P}_{\ell,n}^{\prime}
, etc.) encapsulate the radial
dependencies of perturbations. They are the eigenfunctions of a
two-point boundary value problem (BVP) constructed from a coupled
system of ordinary differential equations and boundary conditions

[
57
, see, e.g., section 14.1 of]
; the corresponding
eigenvalue is
\sigma_{n,\ell}^{2}
, with
\sigma_{n,\ell}
being the
angular frequency of the oscillation mode.

A.2
Integral Expression for Mode Frequencies

As demonstrated by many authors
[
22
,
11
,
26
,
46
,
18
,
12
,
31
,
57

e.g.,]
,
the frequency of an oscillation mode can be expressed in terms of
integrals over the mode’s eigenfunctions. Most derivations of this
relationship make specific assumptions about the boundary conditions
applied at the stellar surface; here, we present a somewhat
more-general approach.

We begin from equation (14.18) of
Unno et al. [57]
, which originates
in the linearized momentum equation. Adapting this equation to our
notation, and performing the integrals over
\theta
and
\phi
, we
obtain the relationship

\sigma_{n,\ell}^{2}\,\mathcal{P}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\mathcal{W}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}+\mathcal{S}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell},

(A3)

where

\mathcal{P}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\int_{0}^{R}\left[\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n,\ell}\,\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n^{\prime},\ell}^{\ast}+\ell(\ell+1)\,\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm h};n,\ell}\,\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm h};n^{\prime},\ell}^{\ast}\right]\,\rho\,r^{2}\,\operatorname{d}\!{r},

(A4)

defines an inner product between the displacement perturbation eigenvectors
for a pair of modes with indices
(n,\ell)
and
(n^{\prime},\ell)
,

\mathcal{W}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\int_{0}^{R}\left\{\frac{1}{\rho^{2}c^{2}}\tilde{P}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}\tilde{P}_{n^{\prime},\ell}^{\prime\ast}+\mathcal{N}^{2}\,\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n,\ell}\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n^{\prime},\ell}^{\ast}-\frac{1}{4\pi G\rho}\left[\frac{\partial{\tilde{\Phi}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}}}{\partial{r}}+\frac{\ell+1}{r}\tilde{\Phi}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}\right]\left[\frac{\partial{\tilde{\Phi}_{n^{\prime},\ell}^{\prime}}}{\partial{r}}+\frac{\ell+1}{r}\tilde{\Phi}_{n^{\prime},\ell}^{\prime}\right]^{\ast}\right\}\,\rho\,r^{2}\,\operatorname{d}\!{r},

(A5)

defines a generalized weight integral
3
3

3

We used this
terminology because, when
n^{\prime}=n
, the integral reduces to the standard
weight integrals appearing in the literature
(
31
, e.g.,
, as corrected by

55
)
for the same pair of modes, and

\mathcal{S}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\left\{r^{2}\left[\left(\tilde{P}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}+\rho\,\tilde{\Phi}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}\right)\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n^{\prime},\ell}^{\ast}+\frac{1}{4\pi G}\tilde{\Phi}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}\left(\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\tilde{\Phi}_{n^{\prime},\ell}^{\prime}}}{\operatorname{d}\!{r}}+\frac{\ell+1}{r}\tilde{\Phi}_{n^{\prime},\ell}^{\prime}\right)^{\ast}\right]\right\}_{r=R},

(A6)

is a surface term, with
R
the stellar radius. In these expressions,

c^{2}\equiv\frac{\Gamma_{1}P}{\rho},\qquad\mathcal{N}^{2}\equiv\frac{g}{r}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\ln P}}{\operatorname{d}\!{\ln r}}-\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{\ln\rho}}{\operatorname{d}\!{\ln r}}\right),

(A7)

are the squares of the adiabatic sound speed and Brunt-Väisälä frequency,
respectively,
\rho
is the density,

the gravitational

\Gamma_{1}

Setting
n^{\prime}=n
in equation (

(A8)

which constitutes our formulation of the integral expression for mode

[

The integrals appearing in equations (

and (

\mathcal{S}_{n,n,\ell}
is evaluated at the same location.

A.3
Orthogonality of Eigenfunctions

We now consider the conditions under which oscillation eigenfunctions
are mutually orthogonal. Observing that
\mathcal{P}_{n^{\prime},n,\ell}=\mathcal{P}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{W}_{n^{\prime},n,\ell}=\mathcal{W}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}^{\ast}
, it follows from
equation (
A3
) that

\left(\sigma_{n,\ell}^{2}-\sigma_{n^{\prime},\ell}^{2\ast}\right)\mathcal{P}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}-\mathcal{S}_{n^{\prime},n,\ell}^{\ast}.

(A9)

When
n^{\prime}=n
, this reduces to

\left(\sigma_{n,\ell}^{2}-\sigma_{n,\ell}^{2\ast}\right)\mathcal{P}_{n,n,\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{n,n,\ell}-\mathcal{S}_{n,n,\ell}^{\ast}.

(A10)

If
\operatorname{Im}(\mathcal{S}_{n,n,\ell})=0
, then the right-hand side of this
equation vanishes,

\left(\sigma_{n,\ell}^{2}-\sigma_{n,\ell}^{2\ast}\right)\mathcal{P}_{n,n,\ell}=0;

(A11)

given that non-trivial solutions have
\mathcal{P}_{n,n,\ell}>0
, this
indicates that the eigenvalues
\sigma_{n,\ell}^{2}
are real.

Returning to equation (
A9
), if
\mathcal{S}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{n^{\prime},n,\ell}^{\ast}
then

\left(\sigma_{n,\ell}^{2}-\sigma_{n^{\prime},\ell}^{2}\right)\mathcal{P}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=0.

(A12)

Assuming that no degeneracies exist among the eigenvalues
[see
section 14.1 of
57
, for a brief discussion of this
assumption]
, the term in parentheses is non-zero when
n^{\prime}\neq n
and so

\mathcal{P}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=0,

(A13)

indicating that eigenfunctions of modes with differing radial orders
but the same harmonic degree are orthogonal to each other. This is
equivalent to the statement that the oscillation BVP is self-adjoint,
and leads to the well-known result

[
11
,
33
, e.g.,]
that a
variational approach can be used to refine its eigenvalues.

With the potential boundary condition (
5
), the
condition
\mathcal{S}_{n,n^{\prime},\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{n^{\prime},n,\ell}^{\ast}
necessary for
orthogonality simplifies to

\left(\tilde{P}_{n,\ell}^{\prime}\,\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n^{\prime},\ell}^{\ast}\right)_{r=R}=\left(\tilde{P}_{n^{\prime},\ell}^{\prime\ast}\,\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n,\ell}\right)_{r=R}

(A14)

This is satisfied by the zero-pressure boundary
condition (
4
), but can also be satisfied by
other boundary conditions such as the rigid-lid condition
\tilde{\xi}_{{\rm r};n,\ell}=0
.
