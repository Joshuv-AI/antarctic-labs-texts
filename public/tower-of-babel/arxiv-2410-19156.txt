Kuramoto Osciilators With Asymmetric Coupling
Leonard M. Sander

Abstract
We study a system of coupled oscillators of the Sakaguchi-Kuramoto type with interactions including a phase delay. We consider the case of a coupling matrix such that oscillators with large natural frequencies drive all slower ones but not the reverse. This scheme is inspired by Hebbian learning in neuroscience. We propose a simple model which is partly solvable analytically and shows many unusual features. For example, for the case of phase delay π / 2 \pi/2 (a symmetric phase response curve) with symmetric coupling there is no synchronous behavior. But in the asymmetric coupling case, there are phase-locked states, i.e., states where many oscillators have the same frequency, but with substantial phase differences.



I
Introduction

In this paper, we look at a simplified model of coupled oscillators motivated by treatments of neuron interaction with learning. A numerical approach to some of the results has recently appeared
[
1
]
(ASZ). Here, we explore in depth some of the unexpected results that arose in ASZ because they took the coupling between oscillators to be asymmetric: i.e. oscillator
j
drove oscillator
i
with a different strength than
i
drove
j
. Such an asymmetry is generic in systems of neurons coupled by synapses. In ASZ it was the result of using a model for learning.

Our work is based on the Sakaguchi-Kuramoto (SK)
[
2
]
scheme of
N
coupled oscillators with a phase delay,
\alpha
:

\dot{\theta}_{i}=\omega_{i}-\sum_{j}K_{ij}\sin(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}+\alpha)

(1)

Here,
\theta_{i}(t)
is the phase of the
i^{th}
oscillator at time
t
,
\omega_{i}
is its natural (uncoupled) frequency, and
K_{ij}
is the coupling matrix. SK took the
K_{ij}
to be a multiple,
K/N
, of the unit matrix, i.e., all-to-all coupling.

The phase delay,

\alpha
, with
0\leq\alpha\leq\pi/2
, tunes between antisymmetric and symmetric drive of the
i^{th}
oscillator by the
j^{th}
. For
\alpha=0
(the original Kuramoto model
[
3
,
4
]
) complete synchrony of some or all of the oscillators occurs if
K
is large enough. For
\alpha=\pi/2
, synchrony is absent for all
K
.

The change in the nature of the driving function is reminiscent of the change in behavior of neurons according to their phase response curves (PRC)
[
1
]
. Neurons with Type 1 PRC’s act like the
\alpha=\pi/2
case, and Type 2 are like the
\alpha=0
case.
Type 1 neurons cannot synchronize, but Type 2 neurons can.
They are sometimes called integrators and resonators in the neuroscience literature.

In most treatments of the SK model (and its forebearer, the Kuramoto model
[
3
,
4
]
) the coupling constants,
K_{ij}
, are a symmetric matrix. However,
asymmetric
couplings are common in actual neuron systems. In particular, a well-known form of learning, spike-time dependent plasticity
[
5
,
6
]
, tends to result in strong synapses when fast neurons (large
\omega_{i}
) drive slower ones, but weak synapses in the reverse direction.

In ASZ it was shown that this case the general behavior described in the previous paragraph changes completely. In both Type 2 and Type 1 cases, we found frequency locking, namely entrainment of the frequencies of some of the oscillators to the fastest one. The relative phases of the frequency-locked oscillators differ substantially.

There is a large literature about a form of frequency locking that occurs for
identical
oscillators, i.e.
\omega_{i}\equiv\omega
in Eq. (
1
); see, for example,
[
7
]
and references therein. In many situations this gives rise to
splay states
, frequency-locked states with equally spaced phases. In our case we take the frequency spacing to be non-zero. In fact, we take it to be a fraction of
K
in Eq. (
1
) (they have the same units). The resulting relative phases are not equally spaced, but, in the case of Type 1, the spacings do not vanish as the coupling increases, as they would for true synchrony.

In this paper we explore, both by numerics and analysis, a very simplified version of Eq. (
1
), far simpler than the ASZ treatment. In our toy model, we assume
fully
asymmetric coupling such that fast -to-slow drive is present, but the reverse is completely absent. The toy model has remarkable (and remarkably simple) features that we have been able to explain, in part, analytically using elementary techniques.

II
Model Definition

In our toy model we use Eq. (
1
) with:

\displaystyle K_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K\quad\omega_{i}<\omega_{j}

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\quad\omega_{i}>\omega_{j}

We number the oscillators
i=0,...,N-1
such that lower indexes correspond to larger
\omega_{i}
. Further, we suppose that the
\omega
’s are
equally spaced
, and put:

\omega_{i}=\omega_{0}-i\Delta,

(3)

where
\Delta
is the frequency spacing. Of course, the numerical value of
\omega_{0}
plays no real role in the results, since we can shift to a rotating frame. So, without loss of generality, we can take
\omega_{0}=N\Delta
.

Now we can divide the equation by
\Delta
and redefine time as
\tau=\Delta t
. We redefine the ’dot’ notation to mean
d/d\tau
. Then Eq.(
1
) becomes:

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}-k\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\sin(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}+\alpha)

(4)

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N,N-1,N-2,...,1;\quad i=0,..,N-1

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K/\Delta

Note that these equations are in the form of a recursion relation. For
i=0
, we simply have
\theta_{0}=\Omega_{0}t+\phi_{0}
. Next, we use this result to find
\theta_{1}(t)
. Then
\theta_{0}(t)
and
\theta_{1}(t)
determine
\theta_{2}(t)
, and so on. Put another way, the faster oscillators act as an external drive to the slower ones.

In what follows, we will focus on two cases,
\alpha=\pi/2
and
\alpha=0
, that is, Type 1 and Type 2 oscillators. In these cases the term inside the sum in Eq.(
4
) becomes

\cos(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}),\sin(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}),

respectively.

In the original Kuramoto
[
3
]
and SK
[
2
]
cases, the approach was to let
N\to\infty
with fixed
K/N
. We are concerned with a different limit here. We suppose
N
to be finite and vary
k
.

III
Numerical Results

In this section, we explore the model numerically for Type 1 and Type 2 oscillators.

III.1
Type 1:
\alpha=\pi/2

For Type 1 coupling, the equations are:

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}

(5)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}-k\cos(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{2}

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}-k(\cos(\theta_{2}-\theta_{0})+\cos(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}))

\displaystyle.....

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{j}-k\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\cos(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}).

Type 2,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}-k(\sin(\theta_{2}-\theta_{0})+\sin(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}))

\displaystyle.....

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{j}-k\sum_{j<i}\sin(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}).

IV
Analytical Treatment

In this section, we attempt to understand the numerical results by analyzing Eqs. (
5
) and (
8
). We have a significant result for Type 1, namely a proof of Eq. (
6
). We have made very little progress for Type 2.

IV.1
Type 1:
\alpha=\pi/2

If there is frequency locking, in Figure (
1
) we are led to the following
ansatz
for
i=0,1,...,N_{e}
:

\theta_{i}(t)=\Omega_{0}t+\phi_{i},

(9)

where
\phi_{i}
are the phases of the oscillators relative to the leading one.
The phase of the fastest oscillator,
\phi_{0}
, is arbitrary.

Inserting this form into Eqs. (
5
.) we find for
i\leq N_{e}
:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})

(10)

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\phi_{2}-\phi_{0})+\cos(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})

\displaystyle.....

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\cos(\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}).

The value of
N_{e}
is determined by finding how many of these equations have a solution for a given
k
.

Clearly, on examining the first line of Eqs.(
10
), we see that we need
k\geq 1
to determine
\phi_{1}
. Thus, the first threshold is at
k=1
, and, at threshold,
\phi_{1}=\pi
. (We have put
\phi_{0}=0
). It is easy to show explicitly that for
k\geq 2
, we have two equations with solutions, namely:
\phi_{1}=\pm 2\pi/3,\phi_{2}=\pm 4\pi/3
, As we will see later, the negative solutions are stable, and the positive ones unstable. For
k
just above 3, the stable solution is:
\phi_{1}=\cos^{-1}(-1/3)=-1.911,\phi_{2}=-\pi
, and
\phi_{3}=-\pi+\phi_{1}
. These explicit results, i.e., thresholds at
k=1,2,3
, agree with Eq. (
6
). They also have the symmetry property of Figure (
3
).

IV.1.1
Thresholds

Let us suppose that the
l^{th}
equation of the set in Eqs. (
10
) that has a solution. Then for it, and any earlier equation, we can write:

\displaystyle-l/k

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\phi_{l}-\phi_{0})+\cos(\phi_{l}-\phi_{1})+...

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\phi_{l})[\cos(\phi_{0})+\cos(\phi_{1})+..]

\displaystyle\quad+\sin(\phi_{l})[\sin(\phi_{0})+\sin(\phi_{1})+..]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\phi_{l})\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\cos(\phi_{i})+\sin(\phi_{l})\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\sin(\phi_{i})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle f_{l}\cos(\phi_{l})+g_{l}\sin(\phi_{l}),

where:

f_{l}=\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\cos(\phi_{i});\quad g_{l}=\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\sin(\phi_{i}).

(12)

Now introduce new variables,
r_{l},\psi_{l}
as follows;

f_{l}=r_{l}\cos(\psi_{l});\quad g_{l}=r_{l}\sin(\psi_{l}).

(13)

or,

r_{l}^{2}=f_{l}^{2}+g_{l}^{2};\quad\tan(\psi_{l})=g_{l}/f_{l}.

(14)

The phase angle
\psi_{l}
depends on the earlier phases in a complicated way.

Now inserting Eq. (
13
) into Eq. (
11
), we have:

-\frac{l}{kr_{l}}=\cos(\phi_{l}-\psi_{l}).

(15)

This equation has a solution when
l/kr_{l}\leq 1
.

This is a useful exercise because we can compute
r_{l}
from Eqs. (
10
). First we note that:

\displaystyle r_{l}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{l}^{2}+g_{l}^{2}

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\cos(\phi_{i})\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\sin(\phi_{i})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}[\cos(\phi_{i})^{2}+\sin(\phi_{i})^{2}]

\displaystyle+2\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}[\cos(\phi_{i})\cos(\phi_{j})+\sin(\phi_{i})\sin(\phi_{j})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle l+2\sum_{n=1}^{l-1}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\cos(\phi_{n}-\phi_{m})

The second term (the cross terms in the squares of the sums in Eq. (
16
)) can be evaluated by summing Eqs. (
10
) up to index
l-1
:

-\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\frac{i}{k}=-\frac{l(l-1)}{2k}=\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\cos(\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}).

(17)

Therefore:

r_{l}^{2}=l-\frac{l(l-1)}{k}=\frac{l}{k}(k+1-l).

(18)

Now Eq. (
15
) reads:

-\left(\frac{l/k}{k+1-l}\right)^{1/2}=\cos(\phi_{l}-\psi_{l}).

(19)

The threshold condition is:

\displaystyle\frac{l/k}{k+1-l}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1\quad\rm{thus}

(20)

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle k.

This proves Eq. (
6
).

Stability

Investigating stability in a coupled system normally requires calculating the eigenvalues of a Jacobian. This case is much simpler because of the recursive nature of Eq. (
4

To this end we consider the substitution:

\theta_{l}(t)=\Omega_{0}t+\phi_{l}+\delta(t),

in Eq. (
5

\delta<<1

We assume the phases are known for

\displaystyle\dot{\delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\delta\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\sin(\phi_{l}-\phi_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\delta\left(\sin(\phi_{l})\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\cos(\phi_{j})-\cos(\phi_{l})\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\sin(\phi_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\delta\left(f_{l}\sin(\phi_{l})-g_{l}\cos(\phi_{l})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle kr_{l}\delta\sin(\phi_{l}-\psi_{l}).

We have used the definitions of
f_{l},g_{l}
from Eq. (

For Type 2 we have made essentially no progress analytically. We can proceed a bit, and put

V
Discussion

Figure 7
:

Phases versus time for
N=61,N_{e}=30
. Note the second phase-locked group.

The model we have presented is deceptively simple, and, at least for Type 1, has many unexpected and remarkable features. We have been able to understand the most striking result,
N_{e}=\rm{int}(k)
, but there is still much to be learned.

Viewed as a mathematical exercise, the model is quite attractive: it is easy to write down, and it does not seem to require advanced techniques. The reader is invited to try to derive, for example, the striking symmetry and scaling properties of Figures (
3
) and (
4
).

Further, there are many extensions that could be made. For example, we have only looked at
\alpha=\pi/2
and
\alpha=0
. What about the mixed case, e.g.
\alpha=\pi/4
? The numerical results of ASZ seem to indicate that in this case, phase locking persists. Also, suppose
K_{ij}
is not fully one-directional, but has a symmetric part. Once more, ASZ find echos of the extreme case.

We have not said anything about the oscillators that are not phase-locked. In Figure (
7
), we show a phenomenon that we often encounter: in addition to the leading group, there can be phase-locking to other frequencies so that there are groups of entrained oscillators.

Whether this model is relevant to actual neuroscience is a much harder question. It is certainly true that in the brain there are many asymmetric connections. After all, axons and dendrites are quite distinct. However, this author is not aware of brain behavior that could be related to this approach, but there certainly could be. In other applications of coupled oscillators there also could be asymmetry so that this kind of model could be relevant.
