Morphometric assessment of the greater palatine foramen in a Saudi patient sample: a CBCT study
Authors: Hussam M. Alqahtani, Nasser M. Almofarij, Raed Alsomali, Hani S. AlMoharib, Wajdi A. Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The greater palatine foramen (GPF) is a critical anatomical landmark for palatal nerve block anesthesia, periodontal surgery, and soft tissue graft harvesting. Population-specific morphometric data are needed for safe clinical practice. However, standardized CBCT-based GPF morphometric data with reliability analysis are lacking for the Saudi population. This retrospective cross-sectional study evaluated 68 CBCT scans of adult Saudi patients (18–65 years) from King Saud Dental University Hospital. Three primary variables were measured using Planmeca Romexis software: the distance from the GPF to the midline maxillary suture (GPF-MMS), the anteroposterior (AP) diameter, and the lateromedial (LM) diameter. Data were analyzed using R (version 4.5.3). Descriptive statistics, normality testing, and sex-based comparisons were performed. The mean GPF-MMS distance was 15.25 ± 1.26 mm (95% CI: 14.95–15.56). The mean AP diameter was 5.34 ± 0.97 mm (95% CI: 5.11–5.58), and the mean LM diameter was 2.43 ± 0.54 mm (95% CI: 2.30–2.56). All three measurements were significantly larger in males than females (GPF-MMS: p=0.003, d=0.820; AP: p=0.006, d=0.742; LM: p=0.005, r=0.341). Inter-rater reliability was excellent for GPF-MMS (ICC=0.920) and good for AP and LM diameters (ICC=0.808 and 0.873, respectively). GPF-MMS distance, AP diameter, and LM diameter in Saudi adults were 15.25 mm, 5.34 mm, and 2.43 mm, respectively. Males had significantly larger measurements across all three variables. Population-specific and sex-adjusted reference values should be applied when planning palatal nerve block anesthesia and soft tissue graft harvesting in Saudi patients.

Background

The palate is a common donor site for free gingival graft (FGG) procedures used to increase keratinized tissue width, manage gingival recessions, and augment gingival thickness (Nabers 1966; Sullivan and Atkins 1968; Zucchelli and Mounssif 2015). Tavelli et al. (2019) showed in a systematic review that the location of the greater palatine artery and foramen determines the safe harvesting zone on the palatal vault. Injury to the greater palatine neurovascular bundle is a recognized complication of palatal harvesting, with hemorrhage being the most serious consequence (Brasher et al. 1975; Fu et al. 2011).

The greater palatine foramen (GPF) is the exit point of the greater palatine neurovascular bundle on the hard palate, transmitting the greater palatine nerve and vessels through the greater palatine canal (GPC). Accurate knowledge of GPF location and dimensions is required for palatal nerve block, periodontal surgery, soft tissue graft harvesting, dental implant placement, and orthognathic surgery (Aoun et al. 2015b; Apinhasmit et al. 2005; Douglas and Wormald 2006; Malamed and Trieger 1983; Methathrathip et al. 2005; Wong and Sved 1991). Poor knowledge of GPF anatomy has been linked to anesthesia failure, nerve injury, and intraoperative bleeding (Kang et al. 2012; Nimigean et al. 2013).

GPF anatomy has been studied across multiple populations using cadaveric dissection and CBCT. CBCT is the preferred method for these investigations due to its three-dimensional accuracy (Das et al. 2006; Yilmaz et al. 2015). A meta-analysis by Tomaszewska et al. (2014), incorporating over 1200 CT scans and 150 dry skulls, confirmed considerable variability in GPF position across ethnic groups. This variability has since been reported in Middle Eastern (Aoun et al. 2015a), Asian (Methathrathip et al. 2005), European (Gibelli et al. 2017; Piagkou et al. 2012), South American (Ikuta et al. 2013), and African (Ajmani 1994) populations.

In Saudi Arabia, Alotaibi et al. (2018) reported GPF dimensions and positional relationships using CBCT. However, their study used a different CBCT system and software (Sirona Galileos with KDIS 3D imaging software), limiting methodological comparability with Planmeca Romexis-based studies. Sex-based differences in AP diameter were not statistically significant in their sample, and no reliability analysis was reported. These gaps limit the clinical applicability of their findings. To our knowledge, limited CBCT studies have evaluated GPF morphometry in Saudi patients using standardized measurement protocols with formal reliability assessment. These findings may assist clinicians in planning greater palatine nerve block anesthesia and periodontal plastic surgery by providing reproducible anatomical reference values for Saudi patients. By applying an established CBCT methodology, the present study provides additional population-specific morphometric data that can be directly compared with findings from other populations. The aim of this study was to assess GPF dimensions and spatial relationships in Saudi adults using CBCT.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a retrospective cross-sectional study conducted at King Saud Dental University Hospital, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

CBCT scans were retrospectively retrieved from the radiographic database. Patients aged 18–65 years were eligible for the study. Scans were reviewed consecutively and included if they provided adequate visualization of the GPF and surrounding structures, with a sufficient field of view encompassing the posterior maxilla, until the target sample size of 68 was reached. No stratification or matching by sex was performed.

Scans were excluded if they contained missing data, artifacts that interfered with landmark detection or measurements, pathological conditions affecting the palatal region, edentulous arches in the molar area, or a history of maxillofacial surgery or trauma involving the palate.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated based on previously published CBCT data reporting standard deviations of 1.51 mm, 1.15 mm, and 0.68 mm for GPF-MMS distance, AP diameter, and LM diameter, respectively (Zaghden et al. 2024). A sample of 68 scans was determined to provide margins of error of ± 0.36 mm, ± 0.27 mm, and ± 0.16 mm at 95% confidence, which are considered clinically acceptable for anatomical landmark studies.

CBCT image acquisition and analysis

All CBCT scans were evaluated using a Planmeca ProMax 3D Max unit (Planmeca Oy, Helsinki, Finland) operating at 90 kVp, 5–11 mA, with a voxel size of 200 μm. All scans were obtained using the same imaging unit and acquisition protocol. Measurements were obtained from standardized axial and coronal cross-sectional views using consistent image reconstruction, brightness, and contrast settings. Because the study assessed linear morphometric measurements rather than image density, the influence of tube current variation on measurement accuracy was expected to be minimal. Three primary variables were recorded for each scan. The GPF-MMS distance was defined as the shortest perpendicular distance from the GPF midpoint to the midline maxillary suture on the axial plane. The anteroposterior (AP) diameter was defined as the greatest anteroposterior linear dimension of the GPF, and the lateromedial (LM) diameter as the greatest lateromedial linear dimension of the GPF, both measured in the axial plane. Sex (male/female) was recorded as a secondary variable. These three morphometric variables were selected a priori because of their direct clinical relevance to localization of the greater palatine foramen during palatal anesthesia and planning of palatal soft tissue graft harvesting.

Reliability

Two examiners (N.A. and R.A.) independently measured 10 randomly selected CBCT scans. Each examiner repeated the measurements after a one-week interval to assess intra-rater reliability. During the reliability assessment, each examiner performed the measurements independently and was blinded to the other examiner’s measurements. Inter-rater reliability was assessed by comparing the final measurements of both examiners on the same 10 scans. Reliability was quantified using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient [ICC], two-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement, interpreted according to Koo and Li (2016).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R version 4.5.3 (Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA) with the following packages: readxl, dplyr, car, irr). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median, and range. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality for the overall sample and separately within each sex before selecting the appropriate statistical test. Differences between males and females were compared using the independent Student t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for parametric comparisons and rank-based r for non-parametric comparisons. To account for multiple comparisons across the three primary morphometric outcomes, a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of α = 0.017 (0.05/3) was applied in addition to the conventional threshold. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Descriptive statistics

A total of 68 CBCT scans were included, comprising 21 males (30.9%) and 47 females (69.1%). GPF-MMS distance ranged from 12.30 to 17.70 mm, with a mean of 15.25 ± 1.26 mm (95% CI: 14.95–15.56; median: 15.10 mm). AP diameter ranged from 3.40 to 7.70 mm, with a mean of 5.34 ± 0.97 mm (95% CI: 5.11–5.58; median: 5.20 mm). LM diameter ranged from 1.50 to 4.90 mm, with a mean of 2.43 ± 0.54 mm (95% CI: 2.30–2.56; median: 2.40 mm). Descriptive statistics are summarized in Table 1.

The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that the GPF-MMS distance (W = 0.977, p = 0.250) and AP diameter (W = 0.985, p = 0.599) were normally distributed. In contrast, the LM diameter was non-normally distributed (W = 0.892, p < 0.001). Two values (3.9 mm and 4.9 mm) were identified as outliers using the IQR method but were retained as genuine anatomical measurements. Normality assumptions for the GPF-MMS distance and AP diameter were also met within both sex subgroups (all p > 0.05); therefore, Student t-tests were used for these comparisons.

Sex-based comparisons

All three GPF measurements were significantly larger in males than females (Table 2). The mean GPF-MMS distance was greater in males (15.92 ± 1.19 mm) than females (14.95 ± 1.18 mm; t = 3.124, df = 66, p = 0.003, d = 0.820). Similarly, the mean AP diameter was greater in males (5.82 ± 0.92 mm) than females (5.13 ± 0.93 mm; t = 2.829, df = 66, p = 0.006, d = 0.742). Because the LM diameter was not normally distributed, comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The median LM diameter was greater in males than females (2.60 mm vs. 2.30 mm; U = 705.5, p = 0.005, r = 0.341). After applying the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted α = 0.017), all three sex-based differences remained statistically significant: GPF-MMS distance (adjusted p = 0.008), AP diameter (adjusted p = 0.019), and LM diameter (adjusted p = 0.015). A sensitivity analysis excluding the two LM diameter outliers (3.9 mm and 4.9 mm) yielded similar results (U = 611.5, p = 0.019, r = 0.288), confirming that the observed sex difference remained statistically significant at the conventional threshold, although this result did not remain significant after Bonferroni correction (adjusted p = 0.058).

Reliability

Intra-rater reliability ranged from good to excellent for both examiners across all three variables (ICC range: 0.658–0.981). Inter-rater reliability was excellent for GPF-MMS distance (ICC = 0.920, 95% CI: 0.466–0.984) and good for AP diameter (ICC = 0.808, 95% CI: 0.303–0.955) and LM diameter (ICC = 0.873, 95% CI: 0.528–0.970).

Reliability was assessed using 10 CBCT scans. Although the ICC estimates demonstrated good to excellent agreement, the confidence intervals should be interpreted with caution because of the relatively small reliability sample.

Discussion

This study evaluated the morphometric characteristics of the greater palatine foramen (GPF) in Saudi adults using CBCT. The GPF–MMS distance, anteroposterior (AP), and lateromedial (LM) diameters were all significantly greater in males than females, demonstrating sexual dimorphism in GPF morphology. These findings provide anatomical reference values that may assist clinicians in localizing the GPF during palatal anesthesia and planning palatal soft tissue graft harvesting. Standardized CBCT measurements with good-to-excellent reliability also facilitate comparison with findings from other populations.

The mean GPF–MMS distance was 15.25 ± 1.26 mm. This value is comparable to those reported in Iranian (14.93–15.88 mm) (Akbarizadeh et al. 2025) and Lebanese populations (14.91–16.23 mm) (Aoun et al. 2015a), but greater than those reported in Turkish (13.96 ± 1.40 mm) (Bahsi et al. 2019), Tunisian (13.9 ± 1.51 mm) (Zaghden et al. 2024), and a previous Saudi study (14.35 ± 2.56 mm) (Alotaibi et al. 2018). These differences may reflect population-specific craniofacial morphology as well as methodological variations, including CBCT systems, landmark definitions, and measurement protocols. For example, the Turkish study measured GPF–MMS distance in both axial and coronal planes (Bahsi et al. 2019), whereas the present study used the shortest perpendicular distance from the GPF midpoint on the axial plane. Similarly, the Iranian study measured from the most coronal point of the GPF rather than its midpoint (Akbarizadeh et al. 2025), while Alotaibi et al. (2018) used a different CBCT system and image analysis software.

The GPF–MMS distance has direct clinical relevance for greater palatine nerve block anesthesia. A mean distance of 15.25 mm suggests that overly medial needle placement may fail to reach the foramen. Bahsi et al. (2019) recommended an insertion point of approximately 14–15 mm in a Turkish CBCT study, which is consistent with the present findings. Although variability was modest (SD = 1.26 mm), the overall range (12.30–17.70 mm) highlights substantial inter-individual anatomical variation. Accordingly, these anatomical reference values should be interpreted alongside clinical examination and, when indicated, patient-specific CBCT assessment.

The mean AP diameter was 5.34 ± 0.97 mm, exceeding values reported in Tunisian (4.64 ± 1.15 mm) (Zaghden et al. 2024) and Saudi populations (4.09 ± 1.22 mm) (Alotaibi et al. 2018), but similar to those reported in Lebanese populations (5.63–5.72 mm) (Aoun et al. 2015a) and by Bahsi et al. (2019). In contrast, the mean LM diameter (2.43 ± 0.54 mm) was lower than that reported by Alotaibi et al. (2018) (3.14 ± 0.98 mm) but similar to values reported in Tunisian (2.53 ± 0.68 mm) (Zaghden et al. 2024) and Portuguese populations (Martins et al. 2025). Bahsi et al. (2019) reported larger sagittal and transverse GPF diameters than those observed in the present study, most likely because measurements were obtained from both axial and coronal planes, whereas only the greatest axial dimensions were measured in the present study. These findings suggest that both population-specific anatomical variation and differences in measurement methodology contribute to the variability reported across studies.

The dimensions of the GPF define the approximate neurovascular exit zone of the palate and are therefore relevant to periodontal plastic surgery and local anesthesia. A larger AP diameter may indicate a broader surgical risk area during palatal soft tissue graft harvesting, consistent with previous anatomical studies (Klosek and Rungruang 2009; Tavelli et al. 2019). Although these reference values may assist clinical planning, considerable inter-individual variation should be expected.

Sexual dimorphism was evident across all three morphometric variables, with males demonstrating significantly greater GPF–MMS distance and AP and LM diameters than females. Effect sizes ranged from medium for the LM diameter to large for the GPF–MMS distance and AP diameter, indicating that these differences were not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful. Similar findings have been reported in Saudi (Alotaibi et al. 2018), Iranian (Akbarizadeh et al. 2025), Turkish (Bahsi et al. 2019), and Indian populations (Sharma and Garud 2013), supporting the presence of sexual dimorphism in GPF morphology across different populations.

Some differences from previous studies were observed. Akbarizadeh et al. (2025) similarly reported a significantly greater GPF–MMS distance in males, whereas Bahsi et al. (2019) found sex differences in GPF diameters but not in GPF–MMS distance. In contrast, Zaghden et al. (2024) reported no significant sex differences in AP or LM diameters, possibly because of their smaller and sex-imbalanced sample. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in population characteristics, sample composition, and study methodology. In the present study, the GPF was located approximately 1 mm farther from the midline in males, a difference that may influence needle angulation during greater palatine nerve block anesthesia and the definition of safe surgical margins during palatal soft tissue harvesting (Kang et al. 2012; Nimigean et al. 2013; Tavelli et al. 2019). This observation is clinically relevant, as inaccurate localization of the greater palatine neurovascular bundle increases the risk of vascular and neural injury (Fu et al. 2011).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings and introduce inherent selection bias. The sample was also sex-imbalanced, with relatively few male participants. This reflected the CBCT scans available during the study period rather than intentional sampling. Larger multicenter CBCT studies with more balanced Saudi populations are needed to confirm these findings and establish national reference values. Second, measurements were obtained from one side per participant, as specified in the study protocol. This approach maintained statistical independence of the observations. Previous CBCT studies have reported no significant bilateral differences in GPF measurements (Akbarizadeh et al. 2025; Zaghden et al. 2024). Nevertheless, future studies should include bilateral measurements to provide a more comprehensive anatomical assessment.

Third, reliability was assessed using 10 CBCT scans. The confidence intervals for some ICC estimates were relatively wide. Intra-rater reliability was not evaluated and should be considered in future studies to further establish measurement reproducibility. In addition, age was not included in the analysis, although all participants were adults aged 18–65 years. Age-related differences in GPF morphology could not be evaluated. Finally, two large LM diameter measurements were retained because they represented plausible anatomical variants. A sensitivity analysis excluding these values showed that the sex difference in LM diameter remained significant at the conventional threshold (p = 0.019). This was consistent with the primary analysis. However, this result did not survive Bonferroni correction (adjusted p = 0.058), unlike the other three primary outcomes, which all remained significant after correction. The LM diameter finding should therefore be interpreted with some caution when the two outlying values are excluded. Future studies with large samples are warranted to further evaluate the influence of anatomical outliers. These include its relationship to adjacent landmarks, such as the maxillary third molar and pterygoid hamulus, as well as variations in foraminal morphology. Such information would further improve anatomical guidance for palatal anesthesia and periodontal plastic surgery.

Conclusions

This study provides CBCT-based morphometric reference data for the greater palatine foramen in a Saudi adult population. Significant sex-related differences were identified in the GPF–MMS distance and GPF dimensions, with males having larger measurements than females. These findings may assist clinicians in localizing the greater palatine foramen during palatal anesthesia and planning periodontal plastic surgical procedures. However, given the single-center design and the modest, sex-imbalanced sample, larger multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings before they can be generalized to the broader Saudi population.
