Fixed point results and the Ekeland variational principle in vector B B -metric spaces
Radu Precup, Andrei Stan

Abstract
. In this paper, we extend the concept of b b -metric spaces to the vectorial case, where the distance is vector-valued, and the constant in the triangle inequality axiom is replaced by a matrix. For such spaces, we establish results analogous to those in the b b -metric setting: fixed-point theorems, stability results, and a variant of Ekeland’s variational principle. As a consequence, we also derive a variant of Caristi’s fixed-point theorem.

1.
Introduction

The concept of a
b
-metric space arises as a natural generalization of a
metric space, where the triangle inequality axiom is relaxed by introducing
a constant
b\geq 1
on its right-hand side. Early ideas in this direction
can be traced back to the notion of ”quasimetric” spaces, as discussed in

[
1
]
. However, the formal definition and terminology of
b
-metric
spaces are widely attributed to Bakhtin
[
2
]
and Czerwik
[
3
]
. Notably, one of the earliest works to introduce a mapping
satisfying the properties of a
b
-metric dates back to 1970 in
[
4
]
, where such a mapping was referred to as a ”distance”. A concept
related to that of a
b
-metric is the notion of a quasi-norm, which can be
traced back to Hyers
[
5
]
and Bourgin
[
6
]
, who
originally used the term ”quasi-norm.” For a survey on
b
-metric spaces we
send the reader to
[
7
,
8
]
.

Various results from the classical theory of metric spaces have been
extended to
b
-metric spaces, including fixed-point theorems (see, e.g.,

[
9
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
12
,
14
]
), estimations
(see, e.g.,
[
15
,
16
]
), stability results (see, e.g,
[
17
,
18
]
), and variational principles (see, e.g.,
[
19
,
20
]
). In
[
21
]
, the metric was allowed to take vector
values, and results analogous to those for
b
-metric spaces were
established, with matrices converging to zero replacing the contraction
constants, but not the constant
b
from the triangle inequality axiom.

In this paper, we introduce the concept of a vector
B
-metric space, where
the scalar constant
b
in the triangle inequality is replaced by a matrix
B
. This generalization introduces new challenges in establishing results
analogous to those for classical
b
-metric spaces. To the best of our
knowledge, this concept, along with the corresponding results presented
here, is novel. Notably, some of the results appear to be new even in the
scalar particular case where the matrix
B
is reduced to a constant.

Throughout this paper, we consider
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued vector metrics (
n\geq 1
) on a set
X
, i.e., mappings
d:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
.
In the scalar case (
n=1
), we use the special notation
\rho
to denote
a standard metric or a
b
-metric.

The classical definition of a
b
-metric reads as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X
be a set and let
b\geq 1
be a given real number. A mapping
\rho:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is said to be a
b-metric
if for all
x,y,z\in X
the following conditions are satisfied:
\rho(x,y)\geq 0
,
\rho(x,y)=0
if and only if
x=y
,
\rho(x,y)=\rho(y,x)
and
\rho(x,z)\leq b\left(\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z)\right)
. The pair
(X,\rho)

is called a
b
-metric space.

In case the mapping
\rho
is allowed to be vector-valued and one replaces
the constant
b
by a matrix
B
, we obtain our definition of a vector
B
-metric space.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
X
be a set,
n\geq 1
and let
B\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
be an arbitrary matrix. A mapping

d=(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n})\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
is called a
vector
B
-metric if for all
u,v,w\in X,
one has

(positivity)
:
d(u,v)\geq 0
and
d(u,v)=0
if and
only if
u=v
;

(symmetry)
:
d(u,v)=d(v,u)
;

(triangle inequality)
:
d(u,w)\leq B\left(d(u,v)+d(v,w)\right)
.

The pair
(X,d)
is called a vector
B
-metric space.

2.
Preliminaries

In this paper, the vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are looked as column matrices and ordering between them and, more
generally, between matrices of the same size is understood by components.
Likewise, the convergence of a sequence of vectors or matrices is understood
componentwise.

The spaces of square matrices of size
n
with real number entries and
nonnegative entries are denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
and
\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}\right),
respectively. An element of
\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}\right)
is refereed as a
positive matrix
, while a matrix
M\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
is called
inverse-positive
if it is invertible and its
inverse
M^{-1}
is positive.

A positive matrix
M
is said to be
convergent to zero
if its power

M^{k}
tends to the zero matrix
0_{n}
as
k\rightarrow\infty.

One has the following characterizations of matrices which are convergent to
zero (see, e.g.,
[
22
,
23
]
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}\right)
and let
I
be the identity matrix of size
n.
The following
statements are equivalent:

(a):

M
is convergent to zero.

(b):

The spectral radius
r\left(M\right)
of matrix
M
is less
than
1,
i.e.,
r\left(M\right)<1.

(c):

I-M
is invertible and
\left(I-M\right)^{-1}=I+M+M^{2}+\ ....

(d):

I-M
is inverse-positive.

The following proposition collects the various properties equivalent to the
notion of an inverse-positive matrix (see, e.g.,
[
23
,
24
]
).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right).
The following statements are equivalent:

(a):

M
is inverse-positive.

(b):

M
is monotone, i.e.,
Mx\geq 0

\left(x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)
implies
x\geq 0.

(c):

There exists a positive matrix
\overline{M}
and a real number
s>r\left(\overline{M}\right)
such that the following representation
holds:
M=sI-\overline{M}.

Clearly, if
M
is inverse-positive, from the representation
M=sI-\overline{M},
we immediately see that all its entries except those from the diagonal
are
\leq 0;
also the matrix
\frac{1}{s}\overline{M}
is convergent to
zero. If a matrix
M
is both positive and inverse-positive, using the
representation
M=sI-\overline{M}
we deduce that
M
must be a diagonal
matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries.

A mapping
N:X\rightarrow X
defined on a vector
B
-metric space
\left(X,d\right)
is said to be a
Perov contraction mapping
if there
exists a matrix
A
convergent to zero such that

(2.1)

d\left(N\left(x\right),N\left(y\right)\right)\leq Ad\left(x,y\right)

for all
x,y\in X.

The next proposition is about the relationship between vector
B
-metrics
and both vector and scalar
b
-metrics.

Proposition 2.3
.

(1
0
) Any vector-valued
b
-metric
d
can be identified with a vector
B_{b}
-metric, where
B_{b}
is the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries
are all equal to
b.

(2
0
) If
d
is a vector
B
-metric with an inverse-positive matrix
B,

then
d
is also a vector
\underline{B}
-metric with respect to the
diagonal matrix
\underline{B}
that preserves the diagonal of
B,
as well
as a vector-valued
\tilde{b}
-metric with
\tilde{b}=\max\left\{b_{ii}:\ 1\leq i\leq n\right\}.
Here
B=\left(b_{ij}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

(3
0
) If
d
is a vector
B
-metric with a positive matrix
B,
then to
each norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
one can associate a scalar
b
-metric, for example:

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}(x,y),

\displaystyle\text{is
a }b_{1}\text{-metric,}

\displaystyle b_{1}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\max_{1\leq j\leq n}b_{ij},

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}d_{i}(x,y),

\displaystyle\text{is a }b_{\infty}\text{-metric,}

\displaystyle b_{\infty}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij},

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}(x,y)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\text{is a }b_{2}\text{-metric,}

\displaystyle b_{2}:=\left(\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}b_{ij}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Thus, to any vector
B
-metric, one can associate different (scalar)
b
-metrics, depending on the chosen metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
However, as shown in
[
22
]
, working in a vector setting with
matrices instead of numbers is more accurate especially when a connection
with other matrices is necessary. It will also be the case of this work
where some conditions or conclusions will connect the matrix
B
with the
matrix
A
involved in (
2.1
).

If
Y
is a nonempty subset of a vector
B
-metric space
\left(X,d\right),
we define the
diameter
of the set
Y
by

\mathrm{diam}_{d}(Y):=\sup\{\rho_{1}\left(x,y\right)\,:\ x,y\in Y\}=\sup\left\{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\left(x,y\right)\,:\ x,y\in Y\ \right\}.

From this definition, it follows immediately that if
\ \mathrm{diam}_{d}\left(Y\right)=a,
then
d\left(x,y\right)\leq ae
for all
x,y\in Y,
where
e=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Conversely, if
d\left(x,y\right)\leq ae
for all
x,y\in Y,
then
\mathrm{diam}_{d}(Y)\leq na.

Although a
b
-metric does not generate a topology (see, e.g.,
[
25
]
), several topological properties can still be defined in terms of sequences
(e.g., closed sets, continuous operators, or lower semicontinuous
functionals).

We conclude this section by two examples of vector
B
-metrics.

Example 2.4
.

Let
\ d\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
be given by

d(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}|x_{1}-y_{1}|^{2}+|x_{2}-y_{2}|\\
|x_{2}-y_{2}|,\end{pmatrix},

for
x=(x_{1},x_{2}),\ y=(y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.
Then,
\left(\mathbb{R}^{2},d\right)
is a vector
B
-metric space, where

B=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Here, the matrix
B
is inverse-positive, but not positive.

Example 2.5
.

We present an example of a vector-valued mapping
d
which is a vector
B
-metric with respect to a positive matrix, but for which no inverse-positive
matrix exists such that
d
remains a vector
B
-metric. Let

S=\left\{(t,t)\,:\ \,t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

and let
\ d\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
be given by

d(x,y)=\begin{cases}(0,0)&\text{if }x=y,\\
\left(|x-y|^{2},|x-y|\right)&\text{if }x,y\in S,\\
\left(|x-y|,|x-y|^{2}\right)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\left|z\right|=|(z_{1},z_{2})|=|z_{1}|+|z_{2}|
is a norm
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Note that
d
is a vector
B_{0}
-metric, where

B_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

Let us show that
B_{0}
is the smallest matrix for which the triangle
inequality holds for
d
. To this aim, let
B=(b_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
be
any matrix for which the triangle inequality is satisfied. Then, for
x,y\in S
and
z\notin S
, we have

(2.2)

\begin{pmatrix}|x-y|^{2}\\
|x-y|\end{pmatrix}\leq\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}\left(|x-z|+|z-y|\right)+b_{12}\left(|x-z|^{2}+|z-y|^{2}\right)\\
b_{21}\left(|x-z|+|z-y|\right)+b_{22}\left(|x-z|^{2}+|z-y|^{2}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

Let
t,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, and set
x=(t,t)\in S,

y=(0,0)\in S
and
z=(\alpha,0)\notin S
. The first
inequality in (
2.2
) yields,

4t^{2}\leq b_{11}\left(|t-\alpha|+|t|+\left|\alpha\right|\right)+b_{12}\left((|t-\alpha|+|t|)^{2}+\alpha^{2}\right).

Clearly, taking
\alpha=t
and the limit as
t\rightarrow\infty
, this
inequality holds only if
b_{12}\geq 2
. Similarly, from the second
inequality, we obtain

2\left|t\right|\leq b_{21}\left(|t-\alpha|+|t|+\left|\alpha\right|\right)+b_{22}\left((|t-\alpha|+|t|)^{2}+\alpha^{2}\right).

Setting
\alpha=\frac{t}{2}
, we find that

2\left|t\right|\leq 2b_{21}\left|t\right|+5b_{22}\frac{t^{2}}{2},\quad\text{or equivalently,}\quad 5b_{22}\frac{t^{2}}{2}+2\left|t\right|(b_{21}-1)\geq 0.

Clearly, this inequality required for all
t
implies
b_{21}\geq 1
. To
determine the values of
b_{11}
and
b_{22}
, we apply the triangle
inequality with
x,y,z\in S\ \,(x\neq y\neq z\neq x)
, which gives

\begin{pmatrix}|x-y|^{2}\\
|x-y|\end{pmatrix}\leq\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}\left(|x-z|^{2}+|z-y|^{2}\right)+b_{12}\left(|x-z|+|z-y|\right)\\
b_{21}\left(|x-z|^{2}+|z-y|^{2}\right)+b_{22}\left(|x-z|+|z-y|\right)\end{pmatrix}.

Similar arguments as above imply that
b_{11}\geq 2
and
b_{22}\geq 1.

Thus,
B\geq B_{0}
as claimed.

3.
Fixed point theorems in vector
B
-metric spaces

In this section we establish some fixed point results in vector
B
-metric
spaces, analogous to the well-known classical results.

3.1.
Perov type fixed point theorem

Our first result is a version of Perov’s fixed point theorem (see,
[
26
,
27
]
) for such spaces.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete vector
B
-metric space, where
B

is either a positive or an inverse-positive matrix, and let
N\colon X\rightarrow X
be an operator. Assume that there exists a convergent to
zero matrix
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that

(3.1)

d(N(x),N(y))\leq Ad(x,y),\quad\text{for all }x,y\in X,

i.e.,
N
is a Perov contraction mapping. Then,
N
has a unique fixed point.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in X
, and recursively define

x_{k}=N(x_{k-1}),\ \ \ \text{for\ \ }k\geq 1.

Since the matrix
A
is convergent to zero, for each
\alpha>0
, there
exists
k_{0}=k_{0}\left(\alpha\right)
such that

A^{k_{0}}\leq\Lambda,

where
\Lambda
is the square matrix of size
n
whose entries are all
equal to
\alpha.
Let
k,p\geq 0
and
k_{0}
be such that
A^{k_{0}}\leq\Lambda
, for some
\alpha>0
to be specified later.

Case (a):
B
is inverse-positive
.
The triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle B^{-2}d(x_{k},x_{p})

\displaystyle\leq B^{-1}d(x_{k},x_{k+k_{0}})+B^{-1}d(x_{p},x_{k+k_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq B^{-1}A^{k}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+d(x_{p},x_{p+k_{0}})+d(x_{p+k_{0}},x_{k+k_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq B^{-1}A^{k}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+A^{p}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+A^{k_{0}}d(x_{k},x_{p})

\displaystyle\leq B^{-1}A^{k}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+A^{p}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+\Lambda d(x_{k},x_{p}),

which gives

(3.2)

(B^{-2}-\Lambda)d(x_{k},x_{p})\leq B^{-1}A^{k}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+A^{p}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}}).

Given that the right-hand side of (
3.2
) is a vector that
converges to zero as
k,p\rightarrow\infty
, our goal is to show that a
linear combination of the components of the vector
d(x_{k},x_{p})
is
bounded above by the corresponding components of the right-hand side of (
3.2
). To this aim, we make the following notations

\displaystyle B^{-2}=(\gamma_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n},\,\

\displaystyle B^{-1}A^{k}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+A^{p}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})=\varphi_{k,p}=(\varphi_{k,p}^{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}.

Hence

(3.3)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{k,p}^{i}\rightarrow 0\ \ \ \text{as\ \ }k,p\rightarrow\infty.

Under these notations, relation (
3.2
) gives

(3.4)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\gamma_{ij}-\alpha)d_{j}\left(x_{k},x_{p}\right)\leq\varphi_{k,p}^{i}\,,\,\,i=1,2,\ldots,n.

Summing in (
3.4
) over all
i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
, we obtain

(3.5)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\gamma_{ij}-\alpha)d_{j}(x_{k},x_{p})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{k,p}^{i}.

Since
B^{-2}
is invertible and positive, the sum of its elements in each
column must be positive, i.e.,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{ij}>0,\text{ \ \ }j=1,2,\ldots,n.

If we denote

\gamma=\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{ij}\,:\ \,j=1,2,\ldots,n\right\},

relation (
3.5
) implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{k,p}^{i}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\gamma_{ij}d_{j}(x_{k},x_{p})-n\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}(x_{k},x_{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{ij}\right)d_{j}(x_{k},x_{p})-n\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}(x_{k},x_{p})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(\gamma-n\alpha)\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}(x_{k},x_{p}).

Choosing
\alpha<\gamma/n
, one has

(3.6)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}(x_{k},x_{p})\leq\frac{1}{\gamma-n\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{k,p}^{i}.

In (
3.6
), we observe that the factor
\frac{1}{\gamma-n\alpha}
depends only on
n
and
B
, whence (
3.3
) yields

\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}(x_{k},x_{p})\rightarrow 0\ \ \ \text{as\ \ }k,p\rightarrow\infty,

so the sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)
is Cauchy.

Case (b):
B
is positive. One has

\displaystyle d(x_{k},x_{p})

\displaystyle\leq Bd(x_{k},x_{k+k_{0}})+Bd(x_{p},x_{k+k_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq BA^{k}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+B^{2}d(x_{p},x_{p+k_{0}})+B^{2}d(x_{p+k_{0}},x_{k+k_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq BA^{k}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+B^{2}A^{p}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+B^{2}A^{k_{0}}d(x_{k},x_{p})

\displaystyle\leq BA^{k}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+B^{2}A^{p}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+B^{2}\Lambda d(x_{k},x_{p}),

which gives

(3.7)

(I-B^{2}\Lambda)d(x_{k},x_{p})\leq BA^{k}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+B^{2}A^{p}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}}).

Note that since
\Lambda^{k}=(n\alpha)^{k-1}\Lambda
, if
\alpha
is
chosen to be smaller than one divided by the greatest element of
B^{2}

multiplied with
n
, the matrix
B^{2}\Lambda
is convergent to zero.
Consequently,
I-B^{2}\Lambda
is invertible and
\left(I-B^{2}\Lambda\right)^{-1}\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}\right).
Hence, (
3.7
) is equivalent to

(3.8)

d(x_{k},x_{p})\leq\left(I-B^{2}\Lambda\right)^{-1}\left(BA^{k}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+B^{2}A^{p}d(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})\right).

As the right-hand side of (
3.8
) converges to zero when
k,p\rightarrow\infty,
we conclude that
\left(x_{k}\right)
is Cauchy.

Therefore, in both cases, the sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)
is Cauchy and
since
X
is complete, it has a limit
x^{\ast},
that is,
d\left(x_{k},x^{\ast}\right)\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty.
Then, from

d\left(N\left(x_{k}\right),N\left(x^{\ast}\right)\right)\leq Ad\left(x_{k},x^{\ast}\right),

it follows that
N\left(x_{k}\right)\rightarrow N\left(x^{\ast}\right)

as
k\rightarrow\infty,
while from
x_{k+1}=N\left(x_{k}\right),

passing to the limit, one obtains
x^{\ast}=N\left(x^{\ast}\right).

Hence
N
has a fixed point. To prove uniqueness, suppose that there exists
another fixed point
x^{\ast\ast}
. Then, from

d\left(x^{\ast},x^{\ast\ast}\right)=d\left(N\left(x^{\ast}\right),N\left(x^{\ast\ast}\right)\right)\leq Ad\left(x^{\ast},x^{\ast\ast}\right),

recursively, we obtain that

d\left(x^{\ast},x^{\ast\ast}\right)\leq A^{k}d\left(x^{\ast},x^{\ast\ast}\right),

for all
k\geq 1.
Since
A^{k}\rightarrow 0_{n}
as
k\rightarrow\infty,

we deduce that
d\left(x^{\ast},x^{\ast\ast}\right)=0,
i.e.,
x^{\ast\ast}=x^{\ast}.

∎

If we are not interested in the uniqueness of the fixed point for
N
, the
condition (
3.1
) can be relaxed and replaced by
a weaker assumption on the graph of
N
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete vector
B
-metric space, where
B

is either positive or inverse-positive, and let
N\colon X\rightarrow X
be
an operator. Assume there exists a convergent to zero matrix
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that

(3.9)

d\left(N(x),N^{2}(x)\right)\leq Ad(x,N(x)),\ \,\,\text{for all }x\in X.

Then,
N
has at least one fixed point.

Proof.

Following the proof of Theorem
3.1
, from any initial point
x_{0},

the sequence
x_{k}=N^{k}\left(x_{0}\right)
is convergent to a fixed
point
x^{\ast}
of
N,
which clearly depends on the starting point
x_{0},
but condition (
3.9
) is insufficient to guarantee the
uniqueness.
∎

The next result is a version for vector
B
-metric spaces of Maia’s fixed
point theorem. The contraction condition on the operator is considered with
respect to a vector
B_{1}
-metric
d_{1},
not necessarily complete, while
the convergence of the sequence of successive approximations is guaranteed
in a complete vector
B_{2}
-metric
d_{2}
in a subordinate relationship to

d_{1}.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
X
be a set equipped with two
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-vector metrics, a
B_{1}
-metric
d_{1}
and a
B_{2}
-metric
d_{2}
,
where
B_{2}
is either positive or inverse-positive, and let
N\colon X\rightarrow X
be an operator. Assume that the following conditions hold:

(i)

(X,d_{1})
is a complete vector
B_{1}
-metric space;

(ii)

d_{1}(x,y)\leq Cd_{2}(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
and some matrix
C\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right);

(iii)

There exists a matrix
A
convergent to zero such that

(3.10)

d_{2}\left(N(x),N(y)\right)\leq Ad_{2}(x,y),\ \text{ for all }x,y\in X;

(iv)

The operator
N
is continuous in
\left(X,d_{1}\right)
.

Then, the operator
N
has a unique fixed point.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in X
be fixed, and consider the iterative sequence
x_{k+1}=N(x_{k})
for
k\geq 0.
For any
k,k_{0},p\geq 0
, applying the
triangle inequality twice and using condition (iii), we derive either

(B_{2}^{-2}-A^{k_{0}})d_{2}(x_{k},x_{p})\leq B_{2}^{-1}A^{k}d_{2}(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+A^{p}d_{2}(x_{0},x_{k_{0}}),

in case that
B_{2}
is inverse-positive, or

(I-B_{2}^{2}A^{k_{0}})d_{2}(x_{k},x_{p})\leq B_{2}A^{k}d_{2}(x_{0},x_{k_{0}})+B_{2}^{2}A^{p}d_{2}(x_{0},x_{k_{0}}),

if
B_{2}
is positive. Arguing similarly to the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we deduce that
(x_{k})
is a Cauchy sequence in
(X,d_{2})
. From (ii),
it follows immediately that
(x_{k})
is also a Cauchy sequence in
(X,d_{1})
, hence
(x_{k})
is convergent with respect the metric
d_{1}
to some
x^{\ast},
that is,

d_{1}(N(x_{k}),x^{\ast})=d_{1}\left(x_{k+1},x^{\ast}\right)\rightarrow 0,\ \ \ \text{as }k\rightarrow\infty,

while the continuity of
N
yields
d_{1}(N(x^{\ast}),x^{\ast})=0
, i.e.,
N(x^{\ast})=x^{\ast}
. To establish uniqueness, suppose that
x^{\ast\ast}
is another fixed point of
N,
i.e.,
N(x^{\ast\ast})=x^{\ast\ast}
.
Then, by (
3.10
), one has

(I-A)d_{2}(x^{\ast},x^{\ast\ast})\leq 0.

Since
A
is convergent to zero, we necessarily have
d_{2}(x^{\ast},x^{\ast\ast})=0
, i.e.,
x^{\ast}=x^{\ast\ast}
.
∎

3.2.
Error estimates

The classical Banach and Perov fixed point theorems are accompanied by some
error estimates in terms of the contraction constant and matrix,
respectively. These estimates allow us to obtain stopping criteria for the
iterative approximation process. It is the aim of this subsection to obtain
such stopping criteria when working in vector
B
-metric spaces.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that all the conditions of Theorem
3.1
hold and let
\left(x_{k}\right)
be a sequence of successive approximations of the fixed point

x^{\ast}.

(1
0
):

If
B
is inverse-positive, then

(3.11)

\left(B^{-1}-A\right)d\left(x_{k},x^{\ast}\right)\leq A^{k}d\left(x_{0},x_{1}\right)\ \ \ \ \left(k\geq 0\right).

If in addition the matrix
B^{-1}-A
is inverse-positive, then

(3.12)

d\left(x_{k},x^{\ast}\right)\leq\left(B^{-1}-A\right)^{-1}A^{k}d\left(x_{0},x_{1}\right)\ \ \ \left(k\geq 0\right).

(2
0
):

If
B
is positive, then

(3.13)

\left(I-BA\right)d\left(x_{k},x^{\ast}\right)\leq BA^{k}d\left(x_{0},x_{1}\right)\ \ \ \ \left(k\geq 0\right).

If in addition
\ I-BA
is inverse-positive, then

(3.14)

d\left(x_{k},x^{\ast}\right)\leq\left(I-BA\right)^{-1}BA^{k}d\left(x_{0},x_{1}\right)\ \ \ \left(k\geq 0\right).

Proof.

(1
{}^{0})
: We have

\displaystyle B^{-1}d\left(x_{k},x^{\ast}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d\left(x_{k},x_{k+1}\right)+d\left(x_{k+1},x^{\ast}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle A^{k}d\left(x_{0},x_{1}\right)+Ad\left(x_{k},x^{\ast}\right),

whence we deduce (
3.11
). The second part is obvious.

(2
0
): We have

\displaystyle d\left(x_{k},x^{\ast}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Bd\left(x_{k},x_{k+1}\right)+Bd\left(x_{k+1},x^{\ast}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle BA^{k}d\left(x_{0},x_{1}\right)+BAd\left(x_{k},x^{\ast}\right),

that is (
3.13
). The additional conclusion is obvious.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Clearly, since
A^{k}
tends to the zero matrix as
k\rightarrow\infty,

formulas (
3.12
) and (
3.14
) provide stopping criteria for the
iterative fixed point approximation algorithm starting from
x_{0},
when an
admissible error is given. It should be emphasized that these estimates are
in terms of matrices
A
and
B
. In contrast, if we make the transition to
(scalar)
b
-metric spaces, as discussed in Section 2, the resulting
estimates will depend on the chosen norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and may vary
across different norms. So, from this point of view, the vector approach not
only unifies the results that can be obtained with the scalar method, but
also provides the best estimates.

3.3.
Stability results

We now present two stability properties of the Perov contraction mappings in
vector
B
-metric spaces.

The first property is in the sense of Reich and Zaslavski and generalizes
the one obtained in
[
18
]
for
b
-metric spaces.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete vector
B
-metric space, and
let
N\colon X\rightarrow X
be an operator such that
(
3.1
) holds with a matrix
A
convergent to zero. In addition
assume that either

(a):

B
and
B^{-1}-A
are inverse-positive;

or

(b):

B
is positive and
I-BA
is inverse-positive.

Then,
N
is stable in the sense of Reich and Zaslavski, i.e.,
N
has a
unique fixed point
x^{\ast}
, and for every sequence
(x_{k})\subset X

satisfying

(3.15)

d(x_{k},N(x_{k}))\rightarrow 0\text{ }\ \ \text{as\ \ }k\rightarrow\infty,

one has

x_{k}\rightarrow x^{\ast}\ \ \ \text{ as \ }k\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

According to Theorem
3.1
the operator
N
has a unique fixed point
x^{\ast}.
In addition, for any sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)
satisfying (
3.15
), in case (a), we have

\displaystyle B^{-1}d(x_{k},x^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq d(x_{k},N(x_{k}))+d(N(x_{k}),x^{\ast})

\displaystyle=d(x_{k},N(x_{k}))+d(N(x_{k}),N(x^{\ast}))

\displaystyle\leq d(x_{k},N(x_{k}))+Ad(x_{k},x^{\ast}),

that is,

d(x_{k},x^{\ast})\leq(B^{-1}-A)^{-1}d(x_{k},N(x_{k})),

while in case (b),

d(x_{k},x^{\ast})\leq\left(I-BA\right)^{-1}Bd(x_{k},N(x_{k})).

These estimates immediately yield the conclusion.
∎

The second stability result is in the sense of Ostrowski and extends to
vector
B
-metric spaces a similar property established in
[
18
]
for
b
-metric spaces.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete vector
B
-metric space, and
let
N\colon X\rightarrow X
be an operator. Assume
N
satisfies
(
3.1
) with a matrix
A
convergent to zero. In addition,
assume that either

(a):

B
and
I-\tilde{b}A
are inverse-positive, where
\tilde{b}=\max\{b_{ii}\,:\,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
;

or

(b):

B
is positive and
I-BA
is inverse-positive.

Then,
N
has the Ostrowski property, i.e.,
N
has a unique fixed point
x^{\ast}
, and for every sequence
(x_{k})\subset X
satisfying

d(x_{k+1},N(x_{k}))\rightarrow 0\text{ \ as \ }k\rightarrow\infty,

one has

x_{k}\rightarrow x^{\ast}\ \ \ \text{ as \ }k\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

As previously established, the operator
N
has a unique fixed point
x^{\ast}
. In case (a), we have

\displaystyle d(x_{k+1},x^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{b}\,d(x_{k+1},N(x_{k}))+\tilde{b}\,d(N(x_{k}),N(x^{\ast}))

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{b}\,d(x_{k+1},N(x_{k}))+\tilde{b}A\,d(x_{k},x^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq\ \ldots

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{b}\sum_{p=0}^{k}(\tilde{b}A)^{p}d(x_{k+1-p},N(x_{k-p}))+(\tilde{b}A)^{k+1}d(x_{0},x^{\ast}),

while in case (b), similar estimation gives

d(x_{k+1},x^{\ast})\leq\sum_{p=0}^{k}(BA)^{p}Bd(x_{k+1-p},N(x_{k-p}))+(BA)^{k}Bd(x_{0},x^{\ast}).

Since
I-\tilde{b}A
is inverse-positive and
\tilde{b}A
is positive in
the first case, and
I-BA
is inverse-positive and
BA
is positive in
the second case, the series
\sum_{p=0}^{k}(\tilde{b}A)^{p}
and
\sum_{p=0}^{k}(BA)^{p}
are convergent. Moreover,
(\tilde{b}A)^{k}
and
(BA)^{k}
converge to the zero matrix as
k\to\infty
.
Therefore, using the Cauchy-Toeplitz lemma (see
[
28
]
), it follows
that
d(x_{k+1},x^{\ast})\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty.

∎

3.4.
Avramescu type fixed point theorem

Our next result is a variant of Avramescu’s fixed point theorem (see
[
29
]
) in vector
B
-metric spaces.

Theorem 3.8

(Avramescu theorem in vector
B
-metric spaces)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete vector
B
-metric space,
D
a
nonempty closed convex subset of a normed space
Y,

N_{1}:X\times D\rightarrow X
and
N_{2}:X\times D\rightarrow D
be two mappings. Assume
that the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

N_{1}\left(x,.\right)
is continuous for every
x\in X
and
there is a matrix
A
convergent to zero such that

d(N_{1}(x,y),N_{1}(\overline{x},y))\leq A\,d(x,\overline{x}),

for all
x,\overline{x}\in X
and
y\in D;

(ii)

Either

(a):

B
and
B^{-1}-A
is inverse-positive;

or

(b):

B
is positive and
I-BA
is inverse-positive.

(iii)

N_{2}
is continuous and
N_{2}(X\times D)
is a relatively
compact subset of
Y
.

Then, there exists
(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})\in X\times D
such that

N_{1}(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})=x^{\ast},\quad N_{2}(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})=y^{\ast}.

Proof.

For each
y\in D
, Theorem
3.1
applies to the operator
N_{1}\left(.,y\right)
and gives a unique
S(y)\in X
such that

(3.16)

N_{1}(S(y),y)=S(y).

We claim that the mapping
S:D\rightarrow X
is continuous. To prove this,
let
y,\overline{y}\in D
. In case (a), we have

\displaystyle B^{-1}d(S(y),S(\overline{y}))

\displaystyle=B^{-1}d\left(N_{1}\left(S(y),y\right),N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),\overline{y}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq d\left(N_{1}\left(S(y),y\right),N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),y\right)\right)+d\left(N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),y\right),N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),\overline{y}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq Ad\left(S(y),S(\overline{y})\right)+d\left(N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),y\right),N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),\overline{y}\right)\right),

which implies

\left(B^{-1}-A\right)d(S(y),S(\overline{y}))\leq d\left(N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),y\right),N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),\overline{y}\right)\right),

while in case (b), one has

\left(I-BA\right)d(S(y),S(\overline{y}))\leq Bd\left(N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),y\right),N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),\overline{y}\right)\right).

Since
B^{-1}-A
and
I-BA
are inverse-positive, respectively, in case (a),
we deduce that

(3.17)

d(S(y),S(\overline{y}))\leq\left(B^{-1}-A\right)^{-1}d\left(N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),y\right),N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),\overline{y}\right)\right),

and in case (b),

(3.18)

d(S(y),S(\overline{y}))\leq\left(I-BA\right)^{-1}Bd\left(N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),y\right),N_{1}\left(S(\overline{y}),\overline{y}\right)\right).

Then, for any convergent sequence
\left(y_{k}\right)\subset D,

y_{k}\rightarrow y^{\ast}
as
k\rightarrow\infty,
the continuity of
N_{1}\left(S\left(y^{\ast}\right),.\right)
together with relations (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) implies that
d(S(y_{k}),S(y^{\ast}))\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty.

Thus,
S
is continuous, and since
N_{2}
is continuous, the composed
mapping

N_{2}(S(.),.):D\rightarrow D

is continuous too. Since its range is relatively compact by condition (iii),
Schauder’s fixed point theorem applies and guarantees the existence of a
point
y^{\ast}\in D
such that

(3.19)

N_{2}\left(S(y^{\ast}),y^{\ast}\right)=y^{\ast}.

Finally, denoting
x^{\ast}:=S(y^{\ast}),
from (
3.16
) and (
3.19
)
we have the conclusion.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

Without the invariance condition
N_{2}\left(X\times D\right)\subset D,
a
similar result holds if

is a closed ball

centered at the origin
and of radius

In particular, for scalar

-metric spaces, conditions (a) and (b) from

3.8
are the same and reduce to the

and the Lipschitz constant

of

is less than one. More exactly, Theorem
3.8
reads as follows.

Theorem 3.10

,

4.1.
Classical results

We first recall for comparison the classical results in metric spaces (see,

Theorem 4.1

(Weak Ekeland variational principle)
.

Let
\ \left(X,\rho\right)
be a complete metric space and let
\ f:X\rightarrow\mathbf{\mathbb{R}}
be a lower semicontinuous function bounded from below. Then, for given
\ \varepsilon>0
and
\ x_{0}\in X,
there exists a point
\ x^{\ast}\in X

such that

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)\leq f\left(x_{0}\right)-\varepsilon\rho\left(x^{\ast},x_{0}\right)

and

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)<f\left(x\right)+\varepsilon\rho\left(x^{\ast},x\right)\ \ \ \text{for all \ }x\in X,\ x\neq x^{\ast}.

Theorem 4.2

(Strong Ekeland variational principle)
.

Let
(X,\rho)
be a complete metric space, and let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a lower semicontinuous function that is bounded from below. For given
\varepsilon>0
,
\delta>0
, and
x_{0}\in X
satisfying

f(x_{0})\leq\inf_{x\in X}f(x)+\varepsilon,

there exists a point
x^{\ast}\in X
such that the following hold:

\displaystyle f(x^{\ast})\leq f(x_{0}),

\displaystyle\rho(x^{\ast},x_{0})\leq\delta,

\displaystyle f(x^{\ast})<f(x)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\rho(x^{\ast},x)\ \,\,\ \text{for all }x\in X,\ x\neq x^{\ast}.

Below, we have a version of Ekeland’s variational principle for scalar

-metric spaces (see,
[

]
).

Theorem 4.3

(

]
)
.

Let
(X,\rho)

.

Ekeland variational principle in vector

First we state and prove a version of the weak form of Ekeland’s variational

Theorem 4.6

(Weak Ekeland variational principle in vector
B
-metric
spaces)
.

Let
\left(X,d\right)
be a complete vector
B
-metric space
such that the
B
-metric
d
is continuous, and let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a lower semicontinuous function bounded from below. Assume that
f

satisfies the following condition:

(H):

For every nonempty closed subset
F\subset X
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a point
x_{\varepsilon,F}\in F
such
that

(4.2)

f(x_{\varepsilon,F})\leq f(x)+\varepsilon e,\quad\text{for all }x\in F,

where
e=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Then, for a given
x_{0}\in X
, there exists a sequence
\{x_{k}\}\subset X

and a point
x^{\ast}\in X
such that
x_{k}\to x^{\ast}
as
k\to\infty
,

(4.3)

f(x^{\ast})\leq f(x_{0})-d(x^{\ast},x_{0}),

and

(4.4)

f(x^{\ast})+d(x^{\ast},x_{k})\geq f(x)+d(x,x_{k})\quad\text{for all }k\geq 0\quad\text{implies }x=x^{\ast}.

Moreover,

(4.5)

f(x^{\ast})\geq f(x)+Bd(x^{\ast},x)+(B-I)d(x^{\ast},x_{k})\quad\text{for all }k\geq 0\quad\text{implies }x=x^{\ast}.

Proof.

Let us fix a sequence
\left(\varepsilon_{k}\right)
of positive numbers
satisfying
\varepsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty.
We now
proceed to construct the sequence
\left(x_{k}\right).
Let

F\left(x_{0}\right):=\left\{x\in X:\ f\left(x\right)+d\left(x,x_{0}\right)\leq f\left(x_{0}\right)\right\}.

Clearly,
x_{0}\in F\left(x_{0}\right)
and
F\left(x_{0}\right)
is
closed because

is continuous and
f

), there exists a point
x_{1}\in F\left(x_{0}\right)

f\left(x_{1}\right)\leq f\left(x\right)+\varepsilon_{1}e\ \ \ \text{for
all }x\in F\left(x_{0}\right).

Define

F\left(x_{1}\right):=\left\{x\in F\left(x_{0}\right):\ f\left(x\right)+d\left(x,x_{1}\right)\leq f\left(x_{1}\right)\right\},

and recursively, having
x_{k}\in F\left(x_{k-1}\right)
with

f\left(x_{k}\right)\leq f\left(x\right)+\varepsilon_{k}e\ \ \ \text{for
all }x\in F\left(x_{k-1}\right),

we define

F\left(x_{k}\right):=\left\{x\in F\left(x_{k-1}\right):\ f\left(x\right)+d\left(x,x_{k}\right)\leq f\left(x_{k}\right)\right\}.

The sets
F\left(x_{k}\right)
are nonempty and closed, and by their
definition form a descending sequence. To apply Cantor’s intersection lemma,
we verify that their diameters tend to zero as
k\rightarrow\infty.

Indeed, for any
y\in F\left(x_{k}\right)\subset F\left(x_{k-1}\right),

one has

f\left(y\right)+d\left(y,x_{k}\right)\leq f\left(x_{k}\right).

Also, from the definition of
x_{k},

f\left(x_{k}\right)\leq f\left(y\right)+\varepsilon_{k}e.

Consequently, using the definition of
F(x_{k})
, we deduce

d\left(y,x_{k}\right)\leq f\left(x_{k}\right)-f\left(y\right)\leq\varepsilon_{k}e,

whence, for every
y_{1},y_{2}\in F\left(x_{k}\right),
we have

d\left(y_{1},y_{2}\right)\leq B\left(d\left(y_{1},x_{k}\right)+d\left(y_{2},x_{k}\right)\right).

As a result, diam
{}_{d}\left(F\left(x_{k}\right)\right)\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty.
Thus, by Cantor’s lemma,

\bigcap\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}F\left(x_{k}\right)=\left\{x^{\ast}\right\}.

From
x^{\ast}\in F\left(x_{0}\right),
one has (
4.3
).

Next, we prove (
4.4
). To this end, we show the equivalent statement: if

x\neq x^{\ast},
then there exists
k=k\left(x\right)\geq 0
such that

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)+d\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)\ngeqslant f\left(x\right)+d\left(x,x_{k}\right),

that is

f_{i}\left(x^{\ast}\right)+d_{i}\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)<f_{i}\left(x\right)+d_{i}\left(x,x_{k}\right)

for at least one index
i\in\left\{1,2,...,n\right\}.

Let
x\in X,\ x\neq x^{\ast}
be arbitrary. Then
x\notin\bigcap\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}F\left(x_{k}\right).
We distinguish two
cases:

\text{(a)\ \ }x\notin F\left(x_{0}\right);\ \ \text{(b)\ \ }x\in F\left(x_{k-1}\right)\ \text{and }x\notin F\left(x_{k}\right)\ \text{for some }k=k\left(x\right)\geq 1.

In case (a), we have
f\left(x\right)+d\left(x,x_{0}\right)\nleqslant f\left(x_{0}\right).
In case (b), we have
f\left(x\right)+d\left(x,x_{k}\right)\nleqslant f\left(x_{k}\right).
Thus, in both cases, there
exists
k=k\left(x\right)\geq 0
such that
f\left(x\right)+d\left(x,x_{k}\right)\nleqslant f\left(x_{k}\right).
This implies that there is
some
i\in\left\{1,2,...,n\right\}
with

f_{i}\left(x\right)+d_{i}\left(x,x_{k}\right)>f_{i}\left(x_{k}\right).

On the other hand, since
x^{\ast}\in F\left(x_{k}\right),
one has
f\left(x^{\ast}\right)+d\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)\leq f\left(x_{k}\right).
In particular, for the index
i
identified above, it holds
that

f_{i}(x_{k})\geq f_{i}(x^{\ast})+d_{i}(x^{\ast},x_{k}).

Then, from these two ineqialities we obtain

(4.6)

f_{i}\left(x^{\ast}\right)+d_{i}\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)<f_{i}\left(x\right)+d_{i}\left(x,x_{k}\right),

which equivalently proves (
4.4
).

In order to establish (
4.5
), we apply the triangle inequality for
d

on the right hand side of (
4.6
), which gives,

f_{i}\left(x^{\ast}\right)+d_{i}\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)<f_{i}\left(x\right)+d_{i}\left(x,x_{k}\right)\leq f_{i}\left(x\right)+\left(Bd\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)\right)_{i}+\left(Bd\left(x^{\ast},x\right)\right)_{i}.

Hence

f_{i}\left(x^{\ast}\right)+d_{i}\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)<f_{i}\left(x\right)+\left(Bd\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)\right)_{i}+\left(Bd\left(x^{\ast},x\right)\right)_{i},

that is,

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)\ngeqslant f\left(x\right)+Bd\left(x^{\ast},x\right)+\left(B-I\right)d\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right).

Thus, (
4.5
) holds.
∎

A version of the strong form of Ekeland’s variational principle in vector
B
-metric spaces is the following one.

Theorem 4.7

(Strong Ekeland variational principle in vector
B
-metric
spaces)
.

Let
\left(X,d\right)
be a complete
B
-metric space such that
the
B
-metric
d
is continuous, and let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a lower semicontinuous function bounded from below and satisfying
condition (H). Then, for given
\ \varepsilon,\delta>0
and
\ x_{0}\in X

with

(4.7)

f\left(x_{0}\right)\leq f\left(x\right)+\varepsilon e\ \ \ \text{for all
}x\in X,

there exists a sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)\subset X
and
x^{\ast}\in X

such that
x_{k}\rightarrow x^{\ast}\ \ \
as
k\rightarrow\infty,

(4.8)

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)\leq f\left(x_{0}\right),

(4.9)

d\left(x^{\ast},x_{0}\right)\leq\delta e,

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}d\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)\geq f\left(x\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}d\left(x,x_{k}\right)\ \ \text{for all }k\geq 0\ \ \text{\ implies \ }x=x^{\ast}.

Moreover,

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)\geq f\left(x\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}Bd\left(x^{\ast},x\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\left(B-I\right)d\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)\ \ \text{for all }k\geq 0\ \ \ \text{implies
\ }x=x^{\ast}.

Proof.

We apply the weak form of Ekeland’s variational principle, Theorem
4.6
,
to the vector
B
-metric
\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}d.
From (
4.3
),
we immediately obtain (
4.8
), while from
x^{\ast}\in F\left(x_{0}\right)
and (
4.7
), we deduce

\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}d\left(x^{\ast},x_{0}\right)\leq f\left(x_{0}\right)-f\left(x^{\ast}\right)\leq\varepsilon e,

whence (
4.9
). The remaining conclusions follow directly.
∎

A consequence of the weak form of Ekeland’s variational principle is the
following version of Caristi’s fixed point theorem (see
[
34
]
) in
vector
B
-metric spaces.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\left(X,d\right)
be a complete vector
B
-metric space such that the

B
-metric
d
is continuous, and let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a lower semicontinuous function bounded from below and satisfying
condition (H). Assume that for an operator
N:X\rightarrow X,
the following
conditions are satisfied:

(4.10)

d\left(N\left(x\right),y\right)\leq d\left(x,y\right)+Bd\left(N\left(x\right),x\right),\ \ \ x,y\in X

and

(4.11)

Bd\left(N\left(x\right),x\right)\leq f\left(x\right)-f\left(N\left(x\right)\right),\ \ \ x\in X.

Then,
N
has at least one fixed point.

Proof.

Assume that
N
has no fixed points. Then, applying Ekeland’s variational
principle to
f
(Theorem
4.6
), from (
4.4
), one has

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)+d\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)\ngeqslant f\left(N\left(x^{\ast}\right)\right)+d\left(N\left(x^{\ast}\right),x_{k}\right)

for some
k.
Therefore, there is an index
i
with

f_{i}\left(x^{\ast}\right)+d_{i}\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)<f_{i}\left(N\left(x^{\ast}\right)\right)+d_{i}\left(N\left(x^{\ast}\right),x_{k}\right).

Using (
4.11
) gives

\left(Bd\left(N\left(x^{\ast}\right),x^{\ast}\right)\right)_{i}\leq f_{i}\left(x^{\ast}\right)-f_{i}\left(N\left(x^{\ast}\right)\right)<d_{i}\left(N\left(x^{\ast}\right),x_{k}\right)-d_{i}\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right),

that is

d_{i}\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)+\left(Bd\left(N\left(x^{\ast}\right),x^{\ast}\right)\right)_{i}<d_{i}\left(N\left(x^{\ast}\right),x_{k}\right),

which contradicts (
4.10
). Consequently,
N
has a fixed point.
∎

4.3.
New versions of the Ekeland variational principle in
b
-metric
spaces

We emphasize that in the scalar case, that is, when
n=1,\ B=b\geq 1
and
d=\rho
is a
b
-metric, our theorems from the previous subsection offer
more natural versions in
b
-metric spaces to the classical results, as
follows.

Theorem 4.9

(Weak Ekeland variational principle in
b
-metric spaces)
.

Let
\left(X,\rho\right)
be a complete
b
-metric space (
b\geq 1
) such that the
b
-metric
\rho
is continuous, and let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a lower semicontinuous function bounded from below. Then, for given
x_{0}\in X,
there exists a sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)\subset X
and
x^{\ast}\in X
such that
x_{k}\rightarrow x^{\ast}\ \ \
as
k\rightarrow\infty,

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)\leq f\left(x_{0}\right)-\rho\left(x^{\ast},x_{0}\right),

and for each
x\in X,

x\neq x^{\ast},
there exists an index
k=k\left(x\right)
with

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)+\rho\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)<f\left(x\right)+\rho\left(x,x_{k}\right).

Moreover, for each
x\in X,

x\neq x^{\ast},
there exists an index
k=k\left(x\right)
with

(4.12)

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)<f\left(x\right)+b\rho\left(x^{\ast},x\right)+\left(b-1\right)\rho\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right).

Theorem 4.10

(Strong Ekeland variational principle in
b
-metric spaces)
.

Let
\left(X,\rho\right)
be a complete
b
-metric space (
b\geq 1
) such
that the
b
-metric
\rho
is continuous, and let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a lower semicontinuous function bounded from below. Then, for given
\ \varepsilon,\delta>0
and
\ x_{0}\in X
with

f\left(x_{0}\right)\leq\inf_{x\in X}f\left(x\right)+\varepsilon,

there exists a sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)\subset X
and
x^{\ast}\in X

such that
x_{k}\rightarrow x^{\ast}\ \ \
as
k\rightarrow\infty,

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)\leq f\left(x_{0}\right),

\rho\left(x^{\ast},x_{0}\right)\leq\delta,

and for each
x\in X,

x\neq x^{\ast},
there exists an index
k=k\left(x\right)
with

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\rho\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right)<f\left(x\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\rho\left(x,x_{k}\right).

Moreover, for each
x\in X,

x\neq x^{\ast},
there exists an index
k=k\left(x\right)
with

(4.13)

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)<f\left(x\right)+b\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\rho\left(x^{\ast},x\right)+\left(b-1\right)\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\rho\left(x^{\ast},x_{k}\right).

Theorem 4.11

(Caristi fixed point theorem in
b
-metric spaces)
.

Let
\left(X,\rho\right)
be a complete
b
-metric space (
b\geq 1
) such
that the
b
-metric
\rho
is continuous, and let
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a lower semicontinuous function bounded from below. If for an operator
N:X\rightarrow X,
one has

(4.14)

\rho\left(N\left(x\right),y\right)\leq\rho\left(x,y\right)+b\rho\left(N\left(x\right),x\right),\ \ \ x,y\in X

and

(4.15)

b\rho\left(N\left(x\right),x\right)\leq f\left(x\right)-f\left(N\left(x\right)\right),\ \ \ x\in X,

then
N
has at least one fixed point.

The last three results reduce to the classical ones in ordinary metric
spaces, i.e., if
b=1.
Thus, (
4.12
) reduces to

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)<f\left(x\right)+\rho\left(x^{\ast},x\right),\ \ \ \ x\neq x^{\ast};

(
4.13
) reduces to

f\left(x^{\ast}\right)<f\left(x\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\rho\left(x^{\ast},x\right),\ \ \ \ x\neq x^{\ast};

assumption (
4.14
) trivially holds, while (
4.15
) becomes the classical
Caristi’s inequality

\rho\left(N\left(x\right),x\right)\leq f\left(x\right)-f\left(N\left(x\right)\right),\ \ \ x\in X.

5.
Conclusion and further research

In this paper, we introduced the concept of a vector
B
-metric space.
Several fixed-point theorems, analogous to those in scalar
b
-metric spaces
as well as their classical counterparts, were presented. Additionally, we
discussed some stability results. Finally, we provided a variant of
Ekeland’s variational principle alongside a version of Caristi’s theorem. It
remains an open question whether the assumption that
B^{-1}-A
or
I-BA
is
inverse-positive can be omitted in Theorems
3.8
,
3.6
and
3.7
. Additionally, one may explore a
variant of Ekeland’s variational principle where Caristi’s theorem holds
without requiring the additional assumption (
4.10
). Lastly, it would be interesting to study the case where the matrix
B
is neither positive nor inverse-positive; for instance, when it has positive diagonal elements but contains both positive and negative entries elsewhere.
