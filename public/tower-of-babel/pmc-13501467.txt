Advanced Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 nanocomposite: a synergistic smart strategy for catalytic functionality in preparation of spiro[indene-2,2′-naphthalene]-4-carbonitriles and antibiotic resistance
Authors: Narjes Sadat Mireei, Pooria Babaei, Somaye Rashki, Aliasghar Mahmoudi Kharazm, Aref Ghasemi-Ghahsareh, Seyyed Mohammad Ebrahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent decades, the increasing prevalence of antibacterial resistance and the growing global demand for efficient, sustainable catalytic systems have emerged as critical frontiers in pharmaceutical and materials science. This pressing necessity has prompted the development of multifunctional nanocomposites with integrated therapeutic and synthetic capabilities. In this study, a novel Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 hybrid composite was designed and prepared as a synergistic, high-performance dual-purpose nanostructure for broad-spectrum antibacterial and highly efficient catalytic functionality. Structural engineering via niobium doping significantly refined the composite topology, reducing the average crystallite size to 25 nm and optimizing the pore diameter to 9.68 nm (SBET = 87.35 m2 g−1), thereby maximizing the accessible active sites. Benefiting from enhanced electronic charge separation and the resulting improvement in interfacial electron transfer, the designed composite exhibited robust antibacterial activity against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, particularly Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecalis, with inhibition zones of 18 and 21 mm, respectively, and a 5-log reduction in viability within 24 h. These empirical findings suggest a potent oxidative-stress-inducing mechanism suitable for combating resistant clinical pathogens. Furthermore, the hybrid composite demonstrated exceptional utility as an outstanding heterogeneous catalyst for the preparation of medicinal substituted spiro[indene-2,2′-naphthalene]-4-carbonitrile derivatives in an ethanol medium, achieving excellent yields (up to 98%) within 20 minutes and retaining high catalytic functionality over five consecutive runs without significant leaching. This study highlights the potential of Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 as a robust dual-purpose composite for antimicrobial applications and sustainable catalysis in green organic synthesis, offering a scalable, environmentally benign paradigm for addressing contemporary biomedical and catalytic challenges.

Introduction

The presence of spirocarbocycle scaffolds in a wide range of pharmacologically active molecules has established them as prominent targets in synthetic organic chemistry (Scheme 1).1 Owing to their diverse therapeutic profiles and biological activities, these frameworks have attracted significant attention from synthetic chemists.2 Consequently, various synthetic methodologies have been developed to prepare spirocarbocycle systems, including alkylation,3 radical cyclization,4 rearrangement,5 and cycloaddition reactions.6 Besides, various catalytic systems such as ethylene glycol,7l-proline,8l-tryptophan decorated Fe3O4@SiO2,9 and Dabco-based cationic surfactant [C18-Dabco][Br]10 have been applied for this purpose. Despite their utility, many of these reported procedures suffer from notable drawbacks, including difficult catalyst recovery, prolonged reaction times, and the use of toxic materials. Thus, developing efficient, green, and recyclable catalytic systems remains highly desirable.

Bioactive compounds with a spiro-skeleton.

In recent decades, metal–organic frameworks or MOFs have emerged as highly attractive materials in coordination chemistry and materials science.11 These highly porous crystalline materials, often classified as multifunctional materials, find applications in diverse fields (e.g., gas storage, drug delivery, and heavy metal adsorbents).12 Furthermore, MOF systems have been increasingly applied as heterogeneous solid catalysts in organic synthesis, including Knoevenagel condensations,13 Biginelli reactions,14 and Sonogashira coupling reactions.15 Zeolitic Imidazolate Frameworks (ZIFs), a subclass of MOF systems,16 are particularly noteworthy due to their exceptional properties, such as extremely high surface area, uniformly distributed active sites, good compatibility in chemical functionalization, and outstanding chemical and thermal stability.17 For instance, Phan et al. successfully utilized ZIF-8 as an efficient catalyst in the Friedel–Crafts acylation reaction of benzoyl chloride and anisole.18 Similarly, Tayebee et al. reported the first phosphotungstic acid-supported ZIF-9(NH2) composite as an eco-friendly catalyst for the preparation of 3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2(1H)-ones.19

Given the clinical importance of developing new antimicrobial agents, ZIF-based engineered nanostructures have also been extensively explored for antibacterial applications. For instance, Zhuang et al. developed Co-ZIF composites that exhibited significant activity against Escherichia coli bacteria.20 Liu et al. also prepared Ag-decorated ZIFs as highly effective antibacterial agents against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.21

In this context, nano-scale ZnO has garnered immense interest due to its biocompatibility, low toxicity, and photocatalytic properties, making it a valuable candidate for biomedical, chemotherapeutic, and catalytic applications.22,23 Furthermore, doping of elements is a well-established strategy to enhance the physicochemical and antimicrobial attributes of nanomaterials significantly. Incorporating suitable transition metals into the nano-scale ZnO lattice not only modifies its electronic structure but also synergistically boosts its bioactivity and catalytic functionality.24,25

Herein, we report the design and preparation of a novel dual-functional hybrid nanocomposite consisting of ZIF-8 modified with Nb-doped ZnO (Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8) via a combined hydrothermal and precipitation technique. The structural and chemical attributes of the prepared composite were systematically characterized using various analytical techniques. The dual-functional efficiency of this novel composite was evaluated by investigating its antibacterial performance against various bacterial strains, alongside its catalytic functionality in the one-pot synthesis of spiro[indene-2,2′-naphthalene]-4-carbonitriles. Experimental procedure

Generation of nano-scale Nb-doped ZnO (Nb-doped ZnO NPs)

Nb-doped ZnO nanoparticles were fabricated through the hydrothermal process based on our previously published study.24 In a nutshell, zinc(ii) sulfate heptahydrate (1 g, 3.47 mmol) was completely dissolved in deionized water (30 mL) and was further stirred to obtain a clear solution within 2 min. Next, ammonia solution (25%, precipitating agent) was added dropwise by hand until the pH reached and stirred for 5 min. To the above mixed solution, niobium(v) chloride (0.01 g, 0.037 mmol, doping agent) was added. Then, stirring was further continued for 10 min. The reaction container was moved to a Teflon autoclave reactor and kept under hydrothermal conditions (12 h, 120 °C). The precipitates were collected and rinsed three times with the solution entailing water: ethanol (3 : 1 ratio) to eliminate the unreacted chemicals. Eventually, the solid product was dried at 45 °C and calcined (500°C/2 h).

Generation of the ZIF-8 scaffold

The ZIF-8 scaffold was fabricated according to the previous literature protocol.18 In short, 2-methylimidazole (0.8 g, 9.74 mmol) was completely dissolved in CH3OH (10 mL). Afterwards, the methanol solution of Zn2+ entailing zinc nitrate hexahydrate (0.6 g, 2.08 mmol in 5 mL) was slowly added to the above solution and then stirred for 30 min under ambient conditions. Thereafter, the as-fabricated white solid was centrifuged and rinsed with methanol and dichloromethane (3 × 20 mL), respectively. The white solid was finally dried in a vacuum overnight.

Generation of the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite

After the fabrication of nano-scale Nb-doped ZnO (see 2.1), the next step is preparation of the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite. Accordingly, the methanol solution of 2-methylimidazole (0.8 g, 9.74 mmol in 10 mL) was prepared. Next, zinc nitrate hexahydrate (0.6 g, 2.08 mmol) was completely dissolved in methanol (5 mL) to form a homogeneous colorless solution. Afterward, the obtained solution was added drop by drop to the primary solution under stirring conditions. After 30 minutes, a white solid was formed. Thereafter, as-fabricated nano-scale Nb-doped ZnO (1 g) was added, and then the mixture was dispersed in an ultrasound bath for 15 min (60 W). This process helps achieve more homogeneity. After that, the mixture was placed in the reactor under hydrothermal conditions (100 °C, 24 h). After one day, the light beige powder was centrifuged and rinsed with methanol and dichloromethane (3 × 20 ml). The prepared beige powder was finally dried in a vacuum overnight to obtain a Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite.

Antibacterial activity assay

Bacterial strains

Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus faecalis, E. coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates were recovered by consecutive samplings from patients admitted to the infectious diseases ward of Shahid Beheshti Hospital of Kashan, Iran, in 2024.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) test protocol

The MICs of samples (1–4) were determined by applying the broth microdilution protocol. First, the microtiter plate wells were charged with 100 µL of Mueller–Hinton broth. The various sample concentrations (S1–S4) were then added to microtiter plate wells. A bacterial suspension with half McFarland concentration was prepared. This suspension (10 µL) was added to each well. The microtiter plates were incubated for a day at 37 °C. The MIC was ultimately established as the lowest concentration that hindered the observable proliferation of the bacteria.26 All experiments were carried out in three separate replicates.

Evaluation of the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

To determine the MBC, ten microliters from every well were collected and cultured on a blood agar environment. Following a 24-hour period, the quantity of colonies in the blood agar environment was tallied, and the sample concentration with the lowest bacterial growth was denoted as MBC.27 All experiments were carried out in three separate replicates.

Agar disk-diffusion route

The antibacterial performance of each sample (S1–S4) was examined using the disk-diffusion protocol. A sterile cotton swab was applied to culture the isolates (1.5 × 108 CFU mL−1) on the surface of a Mueller–Hinton agar plate. Paper discs were then impregnated with samples (S1–S4) and placed on the plate and next incubated for a 24-hour period at 37 °C. To calculate the bacterial inhibition zone, the plate photos were recorded.27 All experiments were carried out in three separate replicates.

Time-kill evaluation

According to Salavati-Niasari et al.'s procedure, the time-kill evaluation was carried out. A concentration of 106 CFU mL−1 was used to fabricate the bacterial suspension. Next, for 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 hours at 37 °C, this suspension was treated with the desired samples. 10 µL of each sample (103 CFU mL−1) was transferred to blood agar and incubated at 37 °C for a day. The number of colonies is finally recorded as log10 CFU mL−1.26 All experiments were carried out in three separate replicates.

Statistical analysis

In this current study, a t test and ANOVA statistical tests were employed to compare between experimental groups. The p-value <0.05 was described as statistically significant. GraphPad Prism (version 8) was employed for statistical analysis. All experiments were carried out in three separate replicates.

General protocol for fabrication of substituted spiro[indene-2,2′-naphthalene]-4-carbonitrile frameworks

In the general experiment, a round flask was charged with malononitrile and cyclohexanone in a 1 : 1 molar ratio and ethanol (5 mL) in the presence of Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite (12 mg) as a catalyst under stirring conditions (40 °C) to produce intermediate (I). To get intermediate (II), 12 mg of catalyst was applied to mix various aryl aldehydes and 1,3-indanedione (1 : 1 molar ratio) in ethanol (5 mL) under continuous stirring (40 °C). Intermediates (I) and (II) were next combined under reflux conditions to obtain the desired product (Scheme 2). At completion (monitored by TLC), the crude mixture was cooled to ambient conditions. To separate the catalyst, chloroform was first added (to dissolve the crude product), and then the catalyst was isolated by simple filtration. After that, the solvent was removed to form the crude product. Next, the crude mixture was first rinsed with cold ethanol and then recrystallized from ethanol to get a pure product. Results and discussion

The Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite characterization data analysis

The primary objective of this study was to develop a novel dual-purpose catalyst. To this end, a novel Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 hybrid composite was rationally designed and successfully fabricated. The formation of the proposed hybrid composite was confirmed using a range of spectroscopic characterization techniques. Furthermore, the physicochemical properties of the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite, including its crystal structure, crystallite size, elemental and chemical composition, surface characteristics, specific surface area, and morphology, were systematically investigated.

The phase structure, crystallite size, and purity of the pristine ZnO, Nb-doped ZnO, pristine ZIF-8, and Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 nanostructures were investigated via the XRD technique. The XRD curves are demonstrated in Fig. 1. Evidently, pristine ZnO and Nb-doped ZnO NP Miller's indices are observed (see Fig. 1a and b); the intensity ratio and all of the positions of peaks that appeared are consistent with those of the reference (JCPDS No. 01-080-0075 hexagonal).22 To compute the average of the crystallite size, the Scherrer equation was applied (D = kλ/βcos θ). The incorporation of Nb5+ ions into the ZnO lattice significantly modulates the physicochemical properties of the resulting nanostructures through the substitutional replacement of Zn sites, as evidenced by recent studies. Owing to the disparity in the ionic radius between Nb5+ (0.70 Å) and Zn2+ (0.74 Å), this substitution induces substantial lattice strain and compressive stress, which effectively inhibits grain growth.28 As evidenced by the data summarized in Table 1, a pronounced reduction in crystallite size is observed, decreasing from 21 nm (pristine ZnO) to 10 nm (Nb-doped ZnO). This contraction in crystallite size, coupled with the introduction of defects at the grain boundaries, serves as a definitive structural reason confirming the successful formation of the Nb-doped nanostructure. The crystallite structure of ZIF-8 is formed from Zn2+ and 2-methylimidazole used as a linker. Fig. 1c shows the XRD graph of the nano-scale ZIF-8 nanostructures. The XRD curve obtained (based on empirical data) is totally consistent with previous research29 and the average crystallite size was calculated to be 34 nm. Approximately all Miller indices of ZIF-8 and Nb-doped ZnO are illustrated in the final XRD graph (Fig. 1d). These peaks obviously reveal that the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composites were successfully fabricated. Moreover, the average crystallite size was reported to be approximately 25 nanometers.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was applied to characterize the elemental constitution and spatial distribution of the Nb-doped ZnO and pristine ZIF-8 nanostructures (Fig. 2). The findings validated the high chemical purity of the as-prepared samples, as evidenced by the absence of impurity signals. Moreover, the estimated material ratios were consistent with the target structure (summarized in the EDS Table). To assess the structural architecture, EDS elemental mapping was conducted, revealing a uniform dispersion of all constituent species across the samples. In the case of the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite, the mapping observations provided definitive evidence for the successful integration of Zn, O, N, C, and Nb within the hybrid framework. The observed homogeneous distribution across the scaffold surface not only confirms the structural integrity of the composite but also suggests effective interfacial interaction between the components (Fig. 3).

As can be observed in Fig. 5a, niobium dopant species were evenly dispersed on the nano-scale ZnO plates. For comparison, pristine ZIF 8 scaffolds (Fig. 5b) exhibited uniform rhombic dodecahedral crystallites with an average size of approximately 150 nanometers. Upon in situ incorporation of Nb doped ZnO nanostructures during ZIF 8 formation, the product morphology evolved markedly. The resulting composite structure predominantly consisted of nanoplates with an average lateral dimension of ∼434 nm (Fig. 5c). This morphological transition suggests a strong influence of Nb-doped ZnO on the crystal growth dynamics of ZIF 8.

FE-SEM photos of Nb-doped ZnO (a), pristine ZIF-8 (b), and the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite (c).

The incorporation of Nb-doped ZnO into the ZIF-8 matrix induces a distinct morphological transition from the conventional rhombic dodecahedral geometry to a plate-like architecture. This structural evolution is attributed to modified crystallization kinetics and heterogeneous nucleation processes at the composite interface. While pristine ZIF-8 typically forms dodecahedra dominated by [110] facets to minimize surface free energy,31,32 the Nb-doped ZnO surface provides a high density of hydroxyl groups and active metal sites that act as preferential nucleation centers. Based on the coordination-modulation mechanism, the interaction between the 2-methylimidazole ligands and the Nb-doped ZnO substrate restricts the isotropic growth of ZIF-8.33 Consequently, this interfacial coupling promotes anisotropic growth along specific crystallographic planes, resulting in hexagonal nanoplates instead of the standard polyhedral morphology.34 Furthermore, the presence of Nb dopants likely perturbs the coordination–deprotonation equilibrium during framework assembly, as reported in related metal-doped MOF systems.35,36 Such morphological evolution is a strong indicator of the successful integration and robust interfacial contact between the Nb-doped ZnO and the ZIF-8 matrix, which is fundamental to the synergistic performance of the resulting hybrid composite.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was applied to scrutinize the unique morphological features of the designed composite, offering a detailed perspective on how the presence of Nb-doped ZnO influences the crystallization and final habit of the ZIF-8 microstructures. The TEM photos confirmed the presence of ordered hexagonal plates consistent with ZIF 8 domains (Fig. 6). Surrounding these plates, spherical nanoparticles were observed, which are attributed to the Nb-doped ZnO phase. The combined FE-SEM and TEM results provide compelling evidence that Nb-doped ZnO was successfully integrated within the ZIF 8 framework, leading to a compositional and morphological synergistic effect in the final composite.

The N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms were applied to evaluate the surface textural parameters of pristine ZIF-8 and the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite. BET diagrams are revealed in Fig. 7. As summarized in Table 2, pristine ZIF-8 displayed a high specific surface area (SBET) of 1177.80 m2 g−1, a total pore volume (Vp) of 0.6137 cm3 g−1 and an average pore diameter of 1.8997 nm, confirming its highly microporous nature. Following the incorporation of Nb-doped ZnO nanoparticles, a significant attenuation in textural attributes was observed. The SBET and Vp of the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite decreased to 87.35 m2 g−1 and 0.1598 cm3 g−1, respectively. This drastic reduction is primarily related to the pore-filling effect and the partial blockage of ZIF-8's micropores by the Nb-doped ZnO nanoparticles. Furthermore, the substantial density difference between the zinc oxide and the porous ZIF-8 framework contributes to the decrease in mass-normalized surface area. Interestingly, the mean pore diameter shifted from 1.8997 nm to 9.6843 nm. This shift suggests that while the internal micropores of ZIF-8 are largely occupied, the designed Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite develops a secondary mesoporous architecture arising from the interstitial voids created between the Nb-doped ZnO nanoparticles and the ZIF-8 support.

Textural and structural attributes of the designed scaffold

Table 2 shows that ZIF-8 acts as a molecular concentrator in addition to being a physical support. Due to its high affinity for organic molecules, ZIF-8 facilitates the rapid adsorption and enrichment of reactants near the active sites of Nb-doped ZnO nanostructures. Even with a lower total surface area, the local concentration of the designed composite at the heterojunction interface is significantly higher than that in the pristine ZIF-8, thereby accelerating the reaction kinetics.37–39 As shown in the BET results, the mean pore diameter increased to 9.68 nm. This could relate to a mesoporous structure that facilitates the mass transfer of larger reactant molecules and improves the diffusion of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can be more beneficial for catalytic performance than the restricted diffusion within the original micropores of pristine ZIF-8.40–42 Moreover, the incorporation of Nb-doped ZnO into the ZIF-8 framework provides an electron acceptor or a stable environment that suppresses the recombination of electron–hole pairs. This efficient charge separation, further enhanced by Nb (as a doping agent), significantly enhances the quantum efficiency of the catalytic process in the designed composite. Furthermore, ZIF-8 serves as a substrate that prevents the Nb-doped ZnO nanoparticles from severe agglomeration. While the total BET area decreases, the accessible active sites are more effectively stabilized and exposed on the surface of the composite compared to Nb-doped ZnO nanoparticles, which tend to aggregate and lose their effective catalytic area.43

Consequently, the observed reduction in SBET is a characteristic feature of ZIF-8-based composites where the guest species (Nb-doped ZnO) occupy the host framework's cavities (ZIF-8). Despite this reduction, the designed composite exhibits superior catalytic functionality, suggesting that the reaction is not solely surface-area-dependent but is controlled by the enhanced charge separation at the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 interface and the improved electron interaction capability provided by Nb-doping.

The thermal stability of pristine ZIF-8 and the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite was evaluated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), as depicted in Fig. 8. The observed thermograms show distinct variations in the thermal resilience and degradation pathways of the two structures. Pristine ZIF-8 (red line) exhibits remarkable thermal resilience, retaining nearly 100% of its weight up to approximately 550 °C. Beyond this point, a steady weight loss is observed as the temperature increases to 1000 °C, leaving a residual mass of about 60%. This reveals the robust nature of the pure framework under high-temperature conditions.

TGA graph of pristine ZIF-8 (red line) and the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite (blue line).

In contrast, the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite (blue line) displays a multi-step decomposition profile with lower thermal stability. An initial weight loss occurs between 150 °C and 220 °C, resulting in a weight drop to approximately 80%. This stage is typically associated with the evaporation of surface-adsorbed species such as water or solvents. The composite remains relatively stable until 480 °C, where a sharp degradation phase begins, concluding at around 580 °C. This major weight loss corresponds to the structural collapse of the ZIF-8 framework, potentially accelerated by the presence of Nb-doped ZnO sites. Finally, the weight stabilizes at approximately 30% from 600 °C up to 1000 °C, representing the thermally stable inorganic residue. Overall, while the integration of Nb-doped ZnO facilitates the formation of stable metal oxide phases, it reduces the onset temperature of the ZIF-8 framework's decomposition compared to its pristine form.

Catalytic application of the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite for generation of substituted spiro[indene-2,2′-naphthalene]-4-carbonitrile frameworks

Over the past few years, several studies have been conducted on catalytic functionality in the fabrication of substituted spiro[indene-2,2′-naphthalene]-4-carbonitrile frameworks. All of these protocols have their advantages for the preparation of these products. Today, given the significance of MOFs due to their tailorable geometry, homogeneous distribution of active sites, uniform pore sizes, and large surface-to-volume ratios, these structures have been recognized as next-generation catalysts. While various catalysts have been employed for spirocarbonyl synthesis, the use of MOFs remains unexplored in fabrication of substituted spiro[indene-2,2′-naphthalene]-4-carbonitrile frameworks, which prompted us to investigate the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite as a nanocatalyst in this field. Hence, to compare the catalytic functionality, the reaction between cyclohexanone, 1,3-indandione, p-nitrobenzaldehyde, and malononitrile (in a 1 : 1:1 : 1 molar ratio) was selected as a model reaction. Table 3 reports the conditions and diverse catalysts in the generation of spiro[indene-2,2′-naphthalene]-4-carbonitriles.7,8,10,44 Accordingly, this procedure avoids catalysts, and it requires high temperatures (100 °C) and a high-boiling solvent (ethylene glycol), resulting in a much longer reaction time (2.5 h) and lower yields (82%) (No. 1).45 From No. 2, while l-proline is an organocatalyst, it requires a relatively high loading (15 mol%). Moreover, methanol was used as a solvent and is toxic and volatile and the reaction takes 3 hours to reach an 89% yield.46 No. 3 reveals that ultrasound irradiation conditions and water (as a green solvent) were applied in the presence of an ionic liquid catalyst. Despite this external energy source, the reaction time (1.5 h) is still much longer than that in our proposed catalytic system. In comparison to case 4, although this nanostructured catalyst uses similar conditions (ethanol/reflux), it requires a higher catalyst dosage (20 mg) and a longer time (30 min) to achieve a 91% yield. The designed Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 catalyst exhibits superior performance (No. 5). It achieves the highest isolated yield (98%) in the shortest reaction time (20 min) using a lower catalyst loading (12 mg). These results highlight the exceptional catalytic functionality and synergetic effect of the Nb-doped ZnO within the ZIF-8 scaffold compared to other reported nanostructured and organic catalysts. Therefore, the present strategy and offered catalyst can be substituted as a viable strategy and powerful catalyst in order to move towards applying the available protocol, moderate conditions and environmentally friendly solvent.

The reaction mechanism proposal

The significant catalytic performance of the designed Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 hybrid composite can be attributed to the synergistic cooperation between its diverse active sites. In this cooperative system, the low-coordinated Zn2+ ions in the ZIF-8 scaffold, along with the highly electrophilic Nb5+ species (characterized by vacant d-orbitals), act as strong Lewis acid sites to polarize and activate the carbonyl groups of the reactants.49 Concurrently, the uncoordinated nitrogen moieties within the imidazolate linkers of ZIF-8 function as basic sites to facilitate deprotonation of the active methylene compounds.50 Based on our control experiments (Table 4) and well-established literature precedents on cooperative MOF-based catalysis, a plausible reaction pathway is illustrated in Scheme 3. The reaction is initiated by the simultaneous activation of the carbonyl functional groups via the Lewis acidic metal sites (Zn2+ and Nb5+). Subsequently, Knoevenagel condensations occur to give intermediates (I) and (II). This is followed by a Michael addition and a subsequent intramolecular nucleophilic addition to form intermediate (III). The desired product was afforded by tautomerization at the final step.

The hot-filtration test

To understand the heterogeneous nature and surface functionality of the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 hybrid composite, a control experiment was conducted on the model reaction. In this regard, 10 minutes after the reaction began, the nano-scale catalyst was separated and the yield of the isolated product was obtained at approximately 78%. The crude reaction was then permitted to continue for 10 minutes without a catalyst for the same duration. It was found that the yield of the isolated product did not remarkably change (∼80%). It was found that the reaction is primarily carried out through the heterogeneous nano-scale catalyst and to achieve high yield, the presence of the nano-scale catalyst must be maintained in the medium until the reaction is complete. This suggests the remarkable surface effect of the nano-scale catalyst (Fig. 9).

Study of the catalyst retrievability

Some parameters of green chemistry, entailing catalyst retrievability and facile workup, are very desired and prominent properties in catalytic systems. In this regard, substituted spiro[indene-2,2′-naphthalene]-4-carbonitriles were applied as substrates in model reaction experiments for the assay of catalytic retrievability. Upon completion, the nano-scale catalyst was first filtered, rinsed with dry acetone to eliminate the impurities, and finally dried under vacuum. The recycling outcomes ascertained that no detectable loss occurred in the catalytic performance up to five runs (Fig. 10a). The chemical structures and microscope shape of the reused catalyst (after the 5th run) were examined by XRD, FT-IR, and FE-SEM techniques to check the catalyst stability. Assessment of antibacterial performance

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) test

The antibacterial efficacy of samples 1–4 was evaluated using the broth microdilution method. As summarized in Table 6, the MIC values for the prepared compounds ranged from 1250 to 2500 µg mL−1 against the tested bacterial strains. These experimental results suggest that the enhanced antibacterial performance observed in the samples is likely attributable to the synergistic effects within the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite. Specifically, Table 6 highlights that the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite exhibited an MIC of 156.25 µg mL−1, representing a significantly superior inhibitory effect compared to Nb-doped ZnO (P < 0.01). The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of nanoparticles that resulted in the complete inhibition of visible bacterial growth, corresponding to an optical density (OD600) of ≤0.05 relative to the growth control. All assays were conducted in triplicate (comprising three biological and three technical replicates), and the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

While further investigation is required to fully elucidate the precise antibacterial mechanisms of the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite, several pathways can be proposed based on its physicochemical properties. The ZIF-8 scaffold and its derivatives exhibit potent antibacterial performance, which is attributed to their high surface area, tunable porosity, and robust thermal stability.51 The significant source of Zn2+ ions, ZIF-8, induces systemic stress in bacterial cells and facilitates membrane disruption through direct interfacial contact. Upon interaction with essential intracellular components (including enzymes, ribosomes, DNA, and proteins), the composite triggers oxidative stress. This process leads to multifaceted cellular damage, such as structural deformation, altered membrane permeability, disruption of electrolytic balance, enzyme inhibition, and protein denaturation, ultimately modulating gene expression and compromising bacterial viability.52

Furthermore, the modification of ZIF-8 with metals, nanoparticles, metal oxides, polymers, or fibers facilitates the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, and superoxide anions. These species induce significant oxidative stress and mechanical damage to the bacterial cell envelope. Concurrently, the liberated ions interact with the bacterial cell membrane, subsequently penetrating the cytosol. These ions then bind to essential functional groups (specifically sulfhydryl, amino, and hydroxyl groups), thereby disrupting metabolic pathways and compromising cellular integrity.53

The synergistic strategy of ZIF-8-based frameworks has been well documented in recent literature. For instance, Weng et al.54 synthesized a dextran-coated ZIF-8 nanocomposite (Ce6@ZIF-8/Dex) and evaluated its efficacy against periodontal pathogens. Their findings indicated that the photosensitizer chlorin e6 (Ce6) acts synergistically with Zn2+ ions released from the framework, providing a dual-action antibacterial mechanism that effectively mitigates the development of bacterial resistance compared to single-agent treatments. Similarly, the functionalization of ZIF-8 with bioactive molecules has shown promising results; in one study, the incorporation of eugenol into a ZIF-8-cellulose composite significantly amplified its antimicrobial activities. This enhancement was primarily attributed to the collective action of eugenol and the ZnO moieties residing within the ZIF-8 structure and the cellulose matrix.55 Besides, Wu et al. engineered an Ag/ZIF-8@ZIF-67 heterostructure, which demonstrated superior antibacterial activity relative to both pristine ZIF-8 and ZIF-8@ZIF-67. Notably, at a concentration of 125 µg mL−1, this composite achieved an inhibition rate of 98.81% against Staphylococcus aureus. This high performance was ascribed to the concurrent release of Co2+ and Ag+ ions, which effectively induced membrane rupture and irreversible cellular damage.56

Agar disk-diffusion approach

In this study, the disk-diffusion approach can be applied to evaluate the antibacterial impact of samples. The approach was evaluated via computing the diameters of inhibition zones of bacterial growth. Based on empirical outcomes (Table 7 and Fig. 11), the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite revealed the highest inhibition zone toward Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecalis. The zone of inhibition values of Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite for S. aureus and Enterococcus faecalis were estimated to be 18 and 21 mm, respectively (P < 0.01). Furthermore, studies have shown that samples 1, 2, and 4 did not present any activity against Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae microorganisms. Based on these empirical findings, it is speculated that the enhanced antibacterial functionality of the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite could be due to the synergistic impact of the Nb-doped ZnO and ZIF-8 hybrid composite.

Conclusion

To sum up, the study successfully introduced a novel Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 hybrid composite as a high-performance and dual-purpose nanocomposite for both organic catalysis and antibacterial applications. Incorporation of Nb-doped ZnO nanostructures into a ZIF-8 scaffold, as a smart strategy, led to favorable structural modifications, notably a decrease in crystallite size to 25 nm and an optimization of the pore diameter to 9.68 nm, facilitating superior mass transfer and active site accessibility. The catalytic functionality of the Nb-doped ZnO/ZIF-8 composite was rigorously confirmed through the preparation of spiro[indene-2,2′-naphthalene]-4-carbonitrile derivatives. The composite achieved an impressive yield (98%) within a remarkably short reaction time (20 min). This performance is significantly superior to that of other previously reported catalysts. Furthermore, the robust stability of the catalyst was confirmed, maintaining its structural integrity and catalytic activity over five consecutive cycles with no detectable loss in efficiency. Moreover, the nanocomposite displayed exceptional antibacterial activities. A notable finding of this research is its effectiveness against Gram-negative strains (E.coli and K. pneumoniae), which were resistant to individual pristine components. The strongest antibacterial performance was observed against S. aureus and E. faecalis, with inhibition zones of 18 mm and 21 mm, respectively, and a 5-log reduction in bacterial viability within 24 hours. The empirical findings of this study highlight a successful synergistic effect: while pristine ZIF-8 acts as a high-surface-area concentrator, the Nb-doped ZnO enhances charge separation and electronic properties. This dual-functional composite represents a significant advancement in materials science, green chemistry, and medical fields.
