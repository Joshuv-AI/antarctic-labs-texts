Centering HVIPs within the community violence prevention ecosystem
Authors: Matt Vogel, Taylor Kaser, MPH
Published: 2026

We applaud Wical and colleagues’ effort to center the voices of participants in Baltimore and DC-area Hospital-based Violence Intervention Programs (HVIPs), along with their loved ones, and program staff, in discussions of program impact on violence prevention.1 The perspectives of those directly affected by firearm violence are surprisingly absent from many assessments of HVIPs. And indeed, these interviews reveal a clear and concerning disconnect between participants’ needs and the services HVIPs are positioned to provide.

We diverge from the authors on where we think these observations should lead. The conclusion that HVIPs should ‘prioritize long term care and social change’, although appealing, generates expectations that are out-of-scope for these programs. Community violence is structural, intergenerational, and deeply entrenched in longstanding patterns of neighborhood disadvantage and racial inequality.2 3 No well-designed, well-intentioned programming can unravel these root causes. To suggest otherwise risks minimizing the more modest, but no less meaningful contributions that HVIPs can and do make.

In this sense, we view Wical and colleagues’ findings not as an indictment of HVIPs writ large, but as a moment for reflection on a maturing field. Perhaps we have been too focused on demonstrating program impact—often narrowly defined as reinjury4—without fully grappling with what these programs are equipped to accomplish. HVIPs are, by design, individually-oriented interventions operating within very real institutional constraints—limited funding, shifting policy priorities, and the logistical realities of hospital-based care.5 These interviews underscore a persistent disconnect between what evaluators and funders tend to quantify as ‘success’ and what participants themselves experience as meaningful support. HVIPs’ strengths lie in meeting immediate needs (including transportation, food assistance, and emergency financial support), navigating complex health systems, and providing continuity of care in the aftermath of injury. Reducing community violence at scale, however, requires a broader constellation of interventions.

Violence prevention is multiplex, necessitating intervention and cooperation across sectors (eg, mental health, housing, law enforcement, to name but a few), with each addressing different components of the problem. The consistent message across these interviews is not that HVIPs are ineffective, but that they are incomplete. Rather than stretching HVIPs beyond capacity, the field would be better served by investing in coordinated partnerships with community-based violence intervention programs already at work where violence is pervasive.6 7 Our takeaway, then, is less about reimagining HVIPs as engines of social change and more about situating them as a component of a larger, collaborative response—one that remains grounded, realistic, and attentive to the lived experiences of those it serves. To be sure, this is consistent with what Wical and colleagues advocate, although their message is, at times, overshadowed by their somewhat more outsized aspirations for program impact.
