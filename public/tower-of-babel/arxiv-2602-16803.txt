A Kinetic Phase-Field Model of Diffusion Bonding: A Nonlocal Approach to Interface Coalescence Preprint: To appear in Journal of Applied Mechanics (DOI: 10.1115/1.4071127 )
Maryam Khodadad, Noel Walkington, Suresh Kalyanam, Matteo Pozzi, Kaushik Dayal

Abstract
Conventional phase-field models often drive solid-solid interfaces to coalesce when in close proximity. This feature limits their use for processes like diffusion bonding, where the interfaces might need to remain distinct under certain thermodynamic conditions. We develop a kinetic phase-field model to address this problem, using an evolution equation based on a geometric conservation law for interfaces, rather than the gradient descent evolution that is typical in phase-field modeling. This formulation enables us to specify complex kinetic laws, and we use this to incorporate a physically-motivated geometric criterion to control interface merging. This criterion, based on nonlocal higher-derivative curvature invariants of the phase field, can be temperature-dependent, allows for a range of behaviors from complete coalescence to the preservation of distinct boundaries. Simulations show controlled bonding kinetics, demonstrating capabilities that are not available with existing methods for modeling interfaces that must remain distinct under given thermodynamic conditions.

Contributions of this paper.

Despite significant advancements, a core challenge persists in applying phase-field models to solid-state diffusion bonding: the inherent tendency of conventional evolution equations (e.g., Allen-Cahn type, where the time-derivative of the order-parameter is proportional to the variational derivative of the free energy) to promote interface coalescence. Driven by free energy minimization, and including gradient energy terms, these models can cause the gradual diffusion and disappearance of unbonded interfaces or thin interlayers over time, even when their persistence is physically expected. While some approaches, such as the concentration-dependent interfacial energies in Kovacevic et al.
KPSM 20
, offer control by creating local energy minima, the phase-field dynamics may favor interface broadening or coalescence if these delicate energy balances are not perfectly maintained or if kinetic parameters are not numerically tuned. True interface preservation or arrested coalescence often relies on such precise energetic balancing or numerical adjustments, rather than an explicit mechanism for interface coalescence in the diffusion bonding process. This identifies a need for phase-field frameworks that intrinsically incorporate conditions for stopping interface motion based on alternative physical or geometric considerations.

Our work addresses this gap by directly introducing this physical effect into the phase-field kinetic law.
Our approach uses a kinetic phase-field model

;
AD15b
that is based on a geometric conservation law for interfaces rather than the typical phase-field gradient descent.
This provides the ability to specify arbitrary complex kinetic laws; in this work, the kinetic equation is designed to activate based on specific, nonlocal geometric criteria of the phase field itself—such as curvature and gradient invariants—that signify a stable, unbonded interface or a persistent thin layer. By modulating the effective interface mobility, it provides a direct mechanism to suppress or arrest phase evolution locally, offering a physically motivated means to control interface coalescence rather than relying purely on complex energy landscapes or tuned mobility parameters.
This geometric approach allows for a more flexible prediction of microstructure evolution, particularly in scenarios where experimental evidence suggests an unbonded interface or a distinct interlayer is the thermodynamically favored or kinetically arrested outcome.
This paper presents the detailed formulation and validation of this geometrically-controlled nonlocal phase-field model.

2
Kinetic Phase-Field Model Formulation

We develop a phase-field model that enables precise control over interface coalescence during diffusion bonding. Our approach introduces physically motivated constraints on interface merging, based on the geometric detection of interfaces, thereby addressing a fundamental limitation in conventional phase-field models where interfaces always merge when in proximity regardless of kinetic favorability.

2.A
Phase-Field Evolution Equation

The evolution of the phase field variable
\displaystyle\phi({\mathbfit x},t)
, which implicitly describes the interface between distinct regions, is typically assumed to be governed by the Allen-Cahn equation:

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=-M_{\phi}\variation_{\phi}\mathcal{P}

(1)

where
\displaystyle M_{\phi}
is a mobility coefficient;
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
is the total free energy functional
AC 79
; and
\displaystyle\variation_{\phi}\mathcal{P}
is the functional derivative of
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
with respect to
\displaystyle\phi
.
This framework is utilized in models such as that by Kovacevic et al.
KPSM 20
for diffusion bonding, where the thermodynamic driving force
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}:=-\variation_{\phi}\mathcal{P}
incorporates chemical, elastic, and gradient energy contributions.

Although the Allen-Cahn equation (
1
) is effective for many phenomena, there are challenges in prescribing complex interface kinetics and, critically, the nucleation of new phases.
This makes calibration challenging and can lead to unphysical scenarios, such as nucleation occurring in a uniform phase driven solely by a large kinetic driving force, without meeting specific nucleation criteria
AK 06
;
DB 06
;
CAW+ 24
.
To overcome these limitations and enable a clear separation between interface motion (kinetics) and interface creation (nucleation), Agrawal and Dayal
AD15a
;
AD15b
proposed an alternative evolution law for
\displaystyle\phi
derived from a geometric conservation principle for interfaces
AD15a
. In their model,
\displaystyle\nabla\phi
is interpreted as an interface density, and the resulting evolution equation is:

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=|\nabla\phi|v_{n}^{\phi}+G

(2)

Here,
\displaystyle v_{n}^{\phi}
is a prescribed interface velocity governing the kinetics of existing interfaces, and can depend on various physical quantities such as stress or the thermodynamic driving force.

\displaystyle G
is a source term that controls the nucleation of new interfaces, designed to activate only under specific, predefined critical conditions.

An important difference in the evolution law in (
2
) compared to (
1
) is the presence of
\displaystyle|\nabla\phi|
in the kinetic response.
This term ensures that kinetic evolution is active only at existing interfaces (where
\displaystyle|\nabla\phi|\neq 0
), effectively preventing the kinetic term from causing nucleation in uniform phase regions (where
\displaystyle|\nabla\phi|=0
). Nucleation is then exclusively controlled by the separate term
\displaystyle G
.

The interface velocity
\displaystyle v_{n}^{\phi}
in (
2
) encapsulates the kinetic response of the interface to thermodynamic driving forces. Generally,
\displaystyle v_{n}^{\phi}
can be an arbitrary function of the driving force
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
, subject only to the constraint of the second law of thermodynamics. For instance, a common assumption is linear kinetics, where
\displaystyle v_{n}^{\phi}=\kappa\mathcal{F}
, with
\displaystyle\kappa
being a kinetic coefficient.

In our proposed model, we adopt the structure of (
2
) but introduce a specific structure for the interface velocity
\displaystyle v_{n}^{\phi}
.
We propose that the kinetic response to the thermodynamic driving force is modulated by a coalescence kinetic function,
\displaystyle g(\phi,\nabla\phi,\dots)
, which accounts for the nonlocal geometric state of the
\displaystyle\phi
field, particularly to detect conditions indicative of interface proximity with thermodynamic thresholds for merging.
The value of
\displaystyle g
can range from
\displaystyle 0
(representing no hindrance to interface motion) to
\displaystyle 1
(representing complete arrest of the interface). The interface velocity is then expressed as:

v_{n}^{\phi}(\mathcal{F},g)=\kappa(1-g)\mathcal{F}

(3)

Substituting this definition into the kinetic part of (
2
), and temporarily omitting the nucleation term
\displaystyle G
to focus on kinetics, yields our central evolution equation for interface motion:

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=-\kappa\left[1-g\left(\phi,\nabla\phi,\dots)\right]\right|\nabla\phi|\variation_{\phi}\mathcal{P}

(4)

Here,
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
represents a Ginzburg-Landau-type free energy, typically:

\mathcal{P}[\phi]=\int_{\Omega}\left(W(\phi)+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{\phi}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\right)\ \mathrm{d}V

(5)

where
\displaystyle W(\phi)
is the chemical free energy density (e.g., a double-well potential establishing distinct phases) and
\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}
is the gradient energy coefficient for the phase field order parameter.
The variational derivative
\displaystyle\variation_{\phi}\mathcal{P}=\partial W/\partial\phi-\kappa_{\phi}\nabla^{2}\phi
is the standard local driving force.

In this formulation (
4
), the term
\displaystyle\kappa(1-g)
can be interpreted as an effective, spatially and temporally varying kinetic parameter.
Crucially, unlike conventional kinetic coefficients or mobilities that are often treated as constants or simple functions of temperature, the factor
\displaystyle(1-g)
depends on the evolving geometry of the phase field.
This direct coupling between the geometric configuration and the kinetic response allows the system to locally modulate or even arrest interface motion when the conditions, as interpreted by
\displaystyle g
, deem coalescence unfavorable.
This offers a distinct mechanism for controlling interface homogenization compared to models like Kovacevic et al.
KPSM 20
.
While Kovacevic et al. employ a standard Allen-Cahn evolution (
1
) with a numerically tuned constant mobility
\displaystyle M_{\phi}
, and achieve control through the coupling of
\displaystyle\phi
to concentration fields and their influence on the free energy, our approach embeds the control into the kinetic law via the geometric functional
\displaystyle g
.

This approach differs from previous phase-field formulations for diffusion bonding.

, we compute the time derivative of
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
(

\frac{\ \mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}}{\ \mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\variation_{\phi}\mathcal{P}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\ \mathrm{d}V

(15)

Substituting (

\frac{\ \mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}}{\ \mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\variation_{\phi}\mathcal{P}\left[-\kappa(1-g)\variation_{\phi}\mathcal{P}|\nabla\phi|\right]\ \mathrm{d}V=-\int_{\Omega}\kappa(1-g)\left(\variation_{\phi}\mathcal{P}\right)^{2}|\nabla\phi|\ \mathrm{d}V

For the second law to be satisfied
\displaystyle\left(\frac{\ \mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}}{\ \mathrm{d}t}\leq 0\right)
, the integrand in (

\displaystyle\kappa>0
,
\displaystyle|\nabla\phi|\geq 0
, and
\displaystyle\left(\variation_{\phi}\mathcal{P}\right)^{2}\geq 0

\displaystyle\frac{\ \mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}}{\ \mathrm{d}t}
reduces to requiring
\displaystyle 1-g\geq 0
. As
\displaystyle g
is defined to be within the interval
\displaystyle[0,1]
, thermodynamic admissibility is satisfied.
Thus, the incorporation of the coalescence function
\displaystyle g
allows for localized modulation of the phase-field kinetics—effectively slowing or arresting evolution in specific regions—while ensuring that the total free energy does not increase, thereby maintaining consistency with the second law of thermodynamics.

2.F
Mixed Finite Element Implementation

The governing phase-field evolution equation (
4
) incorporates
\displaystyle g
, which is a function of nonlocal geometric measures including second-order spatial derivatives of the phase field
\displaystyle\phi
. The presence of such higher derivatives, potentially in nonlinear combinations with first-order terms (e.g.,
\displaystyle|\nabla\phi|
), alongside the Laplacian
\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi
in the thermodynamic driving force, requires a mixed finite element formulation, as the geometric hindering term
\displaystyle g(\phi,\nabla\phi,\nabla^{2}\phi,\dots)
introduces both first and second derivatives whose consistent evaluation in weak form necessitates explicit access to
\displaystyle\nabla\phi
and
\displaystyle\nabla\nabla\phi
.

We introduce an auxiliary vector field
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}:=\nabla\phi
as an independent unknown
BF 91
. This approach recasts the problem into a system of coupled, first-order partial differential equations by providing direct access to both
\displaystyle\nabla\phi
and
\displaystyle\nabla\nabla\phi
, which is essential for evaluating the geometric hindering contributions. While we have implemented simpler discretization strategies like finite differences in one-dimensional contexts, the mixed FEM offers enhanced flexibility and robustness for multi-dimensional problems with unstructured meshes.
This mixed formulation is implemented using FEniCS
LMW 12
;
ABH+ 15
, a versatile open-source platform for solving partial differential equations.

To establish the mixed variational formulation over a domain
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the phase field
\displaystyle\phi
is sought in the Sobolev space
\displaystyle V^{\phi}=H^{1}(\Omega)
, and the auxiliary gradient field
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}
is sought in
\displaystyle V^{\boldsymbol{\psi}}=H(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
. Specifically,
\displaystyle V_{h}^{\phi}
is constructed using second-order (P2) Continuous Galerkin (CG) finite elements, which employ
\displaystyle C^{0}
-continuous quadratic Lagrange basis functions. For
\displaystyle V_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
, we employ first-order (P1) Continuous Galerkin vector elements; this choice is formally conforming as the space of such
\displaystyle C^{0}
-continuous vector elements is a subspace of
\displaystyle(H^{1}(\Omega))^{d}
, and subsequently of
\displaystyle H(\mathrm{div};\Omega)
, ensuring a well-defined divergence.

The mixed weak formulation is then: find the discrete fields
\displaystyle(\phi_{h},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h})\in V_{h}^{\phi}\times V_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
such that for all corresponding test functions
\displaystyle(\hat{\phi}_{h},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{h})\in V_{h}^{\phi}\times V_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
, the following equations hold. If the system is coupled with mechanics, a discrete displacement field
\displaystyle{\mathbfit u}_{h}\in V_{h}^{{\mathbfit u}}
(where
\displaystyle V_{h}^{{\mathbfit u}}
is typically a subspace of
\displaystyle(H^{1}(\Omega))^{d}
) and its corresponding test function
\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbfit u}}_{h}\in V_{h}^{{\mathbfit u}}
are also included (See Section
4
).

\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{h}\,\ \mathrm{d}V-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{h}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{h}\,\ \mathrm{d}V=0

(17)

and

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\phi_{h}^{n+1}-\phi_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}\hat{\phi}_{h}\,\ \mathrm{d}V-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{R}_{\phi}(\phi_{h}^{n+\theta},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}^{n+\theta},g_{h}^{n})\hat{\phi}_{h}\,\ \mathrm{d}V=0

(18)

The weak form comprises (
17
), which enforces the definition of the auxiliary gradient field
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}=\nabla\phi_{h}
in a weak sense, and (
18
).
In the latter, the term
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\phi}
represents the spatial operator obtained from (
4
), which incorporates the geometric term.
In particular, the contributions involving
\displaystyle|\nabla\phi|
and
\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi
generate weak-form terms of the type
\displaystyle\varepsilon\,\nabla\phi_{h}^{n+1}\!\cdot\nabla(\hat{\phi}_{h}\,m_{n})
, which arise from integration by parts of the Laplacian
\displaystyle\varepsilon m\nabla^{2}\phi
; here,
\displaystyle m=|\nabla\phi|(1-g)
is a function of
\displaystyle\phi
but is treated as a known value from the previous step.
The mixed formulation provides consistent approximations to both
\displaystyle\nabla\phi
and
\displaystyle\nabla\nabla\phi
which are required to evaluate the geometric function
\displaystyle g
.

For the temporal discretization, evaluating the term
\displaystyle g_{h}^{n}=g(\phi_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}^{n},\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}^{n},\dots)
using values from the previous time step (
\displaystyle n
) renders the scheme semi-implicit when terms involving
\displaystyle\phi_{h}^{n+\theta}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}^{n+\theta}
(with
\displaystyle\theta>0
, e.g.,
\displaystyle\theta=1
for Backward Euler or
\displaystyle\theta=0.5
for Crank-Nicolson) are treated implicitly. This choice is favored over fully explicit schemes due to the inherent stiffness associated with phase-field equations, which can impose severe restrictions on the time step size for stability
BPR 13
;
HV 13
;
ARW 95
.

In addition to being evaluated explicitly from the previous iterate, the hindering field is updated in a strictly monotone manner. Let
\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{inst}}^{n}
denote the instantaneous geometric hindering computed from
\displaystyle(\phi_{h}^{n},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}^{n},\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}^{n})
. The stored hindering field
\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{pers}}^{n}
is then advanced by

g_{\mathrm{pers}}^{\,n+1}=\max\!\left(g_{\mathrm{pers}}^{\,n},\,g_{\mathrm{inst}}^{\,n}\right),

(19)

so that the effective hindering factor used in the mobility
\displaystyle m_{n}=|\nabla\phi_{h}^{n}|\,(1-g_{\mathrm{pers}}^{\,n})
cannot decrease once activated. This construction parallels the irreversibility condition in phase-field fracture that prevents the unphysical healing of cracks
HMCW+ 25
.

The discretization of (
17
) and (
18
) at each time step
\displaystyle\Delta t
yields a coupled system of non-linear algebraic equations for the discrete unknowns
\displaystyle(\phi_{h}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}^{n+1})
. This system is solved using a Newton-Raphson iterative method.

Phase-field evolution:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{\phi_{h}^{n+1}-\phi_{h}^{n}}{\Delta t}\hat{\phi}_{h}\,\ \mathrm{d}V+\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{\phi}\,\nabla\phi_{h}^{n+1}\!\cdot\nabla\!\big(\hat{\phi}_{h}\,m_{h}^{n}\big)\,\ \mathrm{d}V+\int_{\Omega}m_{h}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial\phi}+\frac{\partial U}{\partial\phi}\right)_{\!n+\theta}\hat{\phi}_{h}\,\ \mathrm{d}V=0

(25)

The auxiliary field
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}
is updated at each time step via the weak constraint (
24
), implemented as an
\displaystyle L^{2}
-projection of
\displaystyle\nabla\phi_{h}
onto
\displaystyle V_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
rather than as a simultaneous unknown in the Newton solve.
The subscripts
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle n+1
denote time levels, and
\displaystyle n+\theta
indicates a semi-implicit evaluation (e.g.,
\displaystyle\theta=1
for Backward Euler for the driving force terms).
The lagged mobility factor is defined as

\displaystyle m_{h}^{n}=(1-g_{h}^{n})
, so that the geometric hindering enters both the local driving force and the diffusive flux through the term
\displaystyle\nabla(\hat{\phi}_{h}m_{h}^{n})
. The resulting scheme corresponds to a staggered mixed formulation in which
\displaystyle({\mathbfit u}_{h},\phi_{h})
are solved implicitly and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{h}
is updated consistently after each phase-field solve.

Discussion

The kinetic phase-field model presented in this work introduces an approach to controlling interface coalescence, particularly relevant to simulating processes like diffusion bonding.
Our approach is to systematically modulate the interface kinetics through a coalescence kinetic function.
This kinetic function depends on nonlocal higher-gradient geometric variables of the phase field (such as its gradient, Laplacian, and Hessian), following similar strategies of using nonlocal gradient terms to describe complex behavior
DBD 22
;
Day 17
.
This allows the arrest of interface motion in regions where specific geometric criteria – indicative of desired interface stability or a barrier to coalescence – are met. These criteria can be linked to macroscopic experimental parameters like temperature or critical interlayer thickness, thereby allowing the model to implicitly reflect the governing thermodynamic conditions for joining or non-joining.
This enables the prediction of quantities such as the final interface thickness and morphology based on energetics and kinetics, without explicitly tracking solute concentration fields or their gradients.

This approach has distinct features when compared to phase-field models that explicitly couple phase evolution with solute transport via, for example, a Cahn-Hilliard framework, such as the model for Ti-ZrC by Kovacevic et al.
KPSM 20
.
Their work relies on carbon concentration as a primary field and its influence on interfacial gradient energy as a driving force for phenomena like critical interlayer thickness, necessitating the solution of a fourth-order Cahn-Hilliard equation alongside the phase-field and mechanical equations.
Our formulation, by omitting the explicit solute field, reduces the number of coupled partial differential equations to be solved. While it does not resolve the detailed solute profiles, it aims to capture the macroscopic structural outcome of phase transformations and interface interactions. This can be computationally advantageous, particularly for scenarios where the detailed concentration pathway is of secondary interest compared to the final interface stability or morphology under given conditions.

The interface identification proposed in this work also offers an approach to simulating systems where interface behavior is strongly dictated by nonlocal geometry and overarching thermodynamic conditions, potentially applicable to a diverse range of joining or other phase transformation problems in intermetallics, functionally-graded materials, and CMCs.
For example, the model could be applied to simulate grain boundary pinning, where the motion is arrested upon geometric contact with a second-phase particle, or arrested crack healing, where a crack interface is preserved until specific thermodynamic conditions are met.

Appendix A
Selection of Model Parameters

This appendix details the rationale behind the selection of key phase-field model parameters used in the numerical simulations.

1.

Gradient Energy Coefficient (
\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}
):

The parameter
\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}
determines the energetic penalty for spatial gradients in
\displaystyle\phi
, thereby influencing the diffuse interface thickness. It is essential that this interface spans several grid cells to be numerically well-resolved. If the physical interface is much thinner than the mesh resolution (
\displaystyle\Delta x
),
\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}
is often chosen based on
\displaystyle\Delta x
. In this study,
\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}
was selected such that the nominal interface width was proportional to
\displaystyle 5\Delta x
to
\displaystyle 10\Delta x
. For instance, with
\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}=0.1
and
\displaystyle\Delta x=0.02
, in dimensionless quantities. This ensures that the diffuse interface is adequately captured by the discrete mesh without being overly diffuse or numerically unstable.

2.

Reference Chemical Energy (
\displaystyle f_{\gamma}
):

One of the bulk phases is typically chosen as an energetic reference. In this work, the
\displaystyle\gamma
-phase (e.g., ZrC in the Ti-ZrC system, represented by
\displaystyle\phi\approx 1
) was set to have zero baseline chemical energy:
\displaystyle f_{\gamma}=0
.
This simplifies the interpretation of
\displaystyle f_{\beta}
as the relative energy difference.

3.

Relative Chemical Energy (
\displaystyle f_{\beta}
):

The chemical energy of the
\displaystyle\beta
-phase (e.g., Ti, represented by
\displaystyle\phi\approx 0
) relative to the
\displaystyle\gamma
-phase was selected in relation to
\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}
and
\displaystyle\Delta f
. For the specific parameters presented (
\displaystyle f_{\beta}=0.05
,
\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}=0.1
,
\displaystyle\Delta f=0.1
), this sets a moderate energy penalty for the
\displaystyle\beta
-phase.

4.

Double-Well Potential Height (
\displaystyle\Delta f
):

The parameter
\displaystyle\Delta f
scales the height of the energy barrier in the double-well potential
\displaystyle G(\phi)
, influencing the energetic cost of intermediate phase-field values (
\displaystyle 0<\phi<1
). It is typically chosen to be of a similar order of magnitude as the interfacial energy density per unit volume (related to
\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}/\ell^{2}
) or the bulk energy differences. Setting
\displaystyle\Delta f
appropriately with respect to
\displaystyle\kappa_{\phi}
(e.g.,
\displaystyle\Delta f=\kappa_{\phi}=0.1
as used in simulations) ensures a clear energetic distinction between the bulk phases and the interfacial region, promoting phase separation.
