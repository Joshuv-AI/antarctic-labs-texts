Establishment and in vitro validation of a sheep-specific, low-cost TLR7/8 agonist R848-mediated X/Y sperm separation system
Authors: Ming Xian, Zhangtao Hu, Songmao Guo, Yu Li, Fei Wen, Jianhong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSex control is crucial for maximizing the economic benefits of sheep husbandry: male lambs increase farm income by 30–40% due to superior growth and feed efficiency, while female lambs are essential for fine-wool production and population expansion. However, the only commercialized method (flow cytometry) has critical sheep-specific limitations, including 25–30% flow channel blockage, sperm DNA oxidative damage, and prohibitive cost for large-scale breeding. TLR7/8 agonist R848 has been validated for sperm separation in mice, cattle and goats, but no sheep-specific optimized system has been established due to unique biological characteristics of ovine sperm. In this study, we systematically optimized key parameters of R848-mediated sheep sperm separation, comprehensively evaluated its effects on sperm biological characteristics, and verified separation efficiency at the embryo level via IVF combined with nested PCR sex identification. The optimal R848 concentration was 1 µM: the upper layer was enriched with 90.30% ± 5.21% TLR8-negative Y sperm (validated by 80.20% ± 3.70% male embryos), and the lower layer was enriched with 80.50% ± 4.96% TLR8-positive X sperm (validated by 87.56% ± 9.51% female embryos). R848 specifically inhibited X-sperm motility by reducing ATP production (24.82% decrease, P = 0.006) and mitochondrial membrane potential (29.75% decrease, P = 0.023), without damaging acrosome or plasma membrane integrity. The optimal supporting conditions were determined as 1000 µL elution twice, Y-sperm storage at 17 °C for up to 120 h with stable in vitro performance, and X-sperm use within 24 h after sorting. This study provides a low-cost, sheep-specific sperm separation system with comprehensive in vitro validation, which has significant potential for application in precision sheep breeding after further in vivo validation.Keywords: R848, TLR7/8, Sperm sorting, Sex control, Sheep, In vitro fertilization

Introduction

Modern sheep husbandry has an urgent demand for precise sex control technology to maximize economic benefits [1]. For mutton sheep production, male lambs increase farm income by 30–40% due to superior growth and feed efficiency; for fine-wool sheep and breeding stock production, female lambs are essential for population expansion and wool production.However, there is still no low-cost, sheep-adapted sex control technology suitable for large-scale commercial breeding.

Current mainstream sex control technologies have critical limitations for sheep production. Flow cytometry, the only commercialized method, has three major sheep-specific defects: 25–30% flow channel blockage, sperm DNA oxidative damage, and prohibitive cost [2, 3]. Alternative methods including density gradient centrifugation and immunomagnetic beads have low separation efficiency (< 60%) and severe sperm damage [4, 5].

TLR7/8 agonist R848 has emerged as a promising alternative, as TLR7/8 is encoded by the X chromosome and specifically expressed in the tail of X sperm [6, 7]. Previous studies have validated its efficacy in mice, cattle and goats, but sheep-specific systematic optimization is lacking [8–11]. This is due to three unique biological characteristics of sheep sperm that prevent cross-species parameter extrapolation: higher polyunsaturated fatty acid content in the plasma membrane, 2.3-fold higher TLR7/8 expression than bovine sperm, and distinct energy metabolism pattern [12].

This study aimed to: (1) systematically optimize key parameters of R848-mediated sheep sperm separation; (2) comprehensively evaluate its biosafety on sperm biological characteristics; (3) verify separation efficiency at the embryo level via IVF combined with nested PCR; (4) establish a low-cost, sheep-specific sperm separation system with comprehensive in vitro validation.

Materials and methods

Test animals

Eighteen healthy Suffolk rams (2–3 years old, proven fertility) were owned by our research team, and raised under standardized feeding and management conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, ad libitum access to full-price compound feed and clean water) at the standardized breeding base of Inner Mongolia Saenuo Sheep Technology Co., Ltd. (Hohhot, China). Semen samples were collected from the rams via non-invasive artificial vagina between August and September 2023, and were only used for experiments in this study. All rams remained alive under normal feeding management after semen collection.

Sheep ovaries used as oocyte donors in this study were purchased from a local legally accredited commercial abattoir in Hohhot, and were by-products of the routine commercial slaughter process. Our research team did not participate in or interfere with the decision-making and implementation of the sheep slaughter, and only obtained isolated ovarian tissues after slaughter was completed. No live sheep were purchased, housed, or euthanized by our team for the purpose of this study.

All animal-related procedures in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Northwest A&F University (Approval Code: DK2023068, Approval Date: August 25, 2023), and strictly complied with relevant animal ethics and welfare regulations.

Semen collection

The artificial vagina was sterilized, filled with 40 °C warm water, and inflated to maintain moderate pressure. Medical-grade petroleum jelly (proven non-spermiotoxic) was applied to the anterior 1/3 of the inner cavity as a lubricant. An estrous ewe was used as a teaser; the ram’s penis was guided into the artificial vagina during mounting, and semen was collected in a sterile cup. Semen was immediately placed in a 37 °C dark incubator, transported to the laboratory within 15 min, and evaluated for motility (≥ 80% required for experiments) via CASA.

Semen processing and R848-mediated sperm separation

Semen initial screening

Fresh semen was collected from Suffolk rams via sterilized artificial vagina, with an estrous ewe as a teaser. Collected semen was immediately placed in a 37 °C dark incubator, transported to the laboratory within 15 min, and evaluated for motility parameters via HTM-CASA system (Hamilton Thorne, USA). Only semen with total motility ≥ 80% and progressive motility ≥ 65% was used for subsequent experiments.

Semen pre-treatment

Qualified semen was centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min at 25 °C to remove seminal plasma (first centrifugation), then resuspended in 3 mL of semen sorting solution (glucose 28.5 g/L, citric acid 0.49 g/L, lycopene 0.2 g/L, sodium citrate 2.0 g/L, EDTA 0.45 g/L, sodium bicarbonate 1.2 g/L, potassium chloride 0.7 g/L, Tris 5.7 g/L, dissolved in 1 L ultra-pure water; sterile-filtered through 0.22 μm membrane, stored at 4 °C for no more than 7 days), and incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min.

R848 treatment and sperm separation

Pre-treated semen was centrifuged again at 800 × g for 5 min at 25 °C (second centrifugation), then resuspended in 3 mL of sorting solution containing five gradient concentrations of R848 (0.2 µM, 0.6 µM, 1.0 µM, 1.4 µM, 1.8 µM). The control group was resuspended in sorting solution without R848 and underwent the same two centrifugation steps to ensure identical processing conditions across all groups.All groups were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min.

After incubation, the upper 1 mL (Y sperm-enriched fraction) and lower 1 mL (X sperm-enriched fraction) were collected separately. For elution optimization, three elution volumes (100 µL, 500 µL, 1000 µL) and two elution cycles (1 or 2 washes) were tested. For dilution optimization, sorted sperm were resuspended in either semen sorting solution or semen diluent I. Each fraction was washed with the indicated volume of semen diluent I at 800 × g for 5 min at 25 °C, repeated for the indicated number of times, then resuspended to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ sperm/mL for subsequent experiments.

All semen processing and separation experiments were performed with 18 independent biological replicates (one ejaculate per ram; no ejaculate pooling was performed). For each ram, three technical replicates were conducted by collecting semen three times within one to two weeks.

Sperm motility analysis

CASA analysis

Sperm motility parameters were detected via HTM-CASA (Hamilton Thorne) with optimized sheep-specific settings: frame rate 60 Hz, minimum sperm size 3 μm, maximum 8 μm, straight-line velocity (VSL) ≥ 30 μm/s = forward motility. Parameters recorded: total motility (TM, %), progressive motility (PM, %), curvilinear velocity (VCL, µm/s), straight-line velocity (VSL, µm/s), average path velocity (VAP, µm/s), amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH, µm), beat-cross frequency (BCF, Hz). Each sample was tested in triplicate.

ATP content assay

1 × 10⁷ sperm were lysed with 100 µL cell lysis buffer (Solarbio), centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min (4 °C). Supernatant (20 µL) was mixed with 100 µL luciferase working solution (Solarbio), and luminescence intensity was detected via microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1). ATP content was calculated using a standard curve (0–10 nmol/mL), expressed as nmol/10⁷ sperm.

Analysis of mitochondrial membrane potential

The mitochondrial membrane potential of sperm was detected using the JC-1 fluorescent probe. A total of 1 × 10⁶ sperm were incubated with 1 mL of JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min. After incubation, the sample was centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min at room temperature, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed twice with JC-1 staining buffer. Finally, the sperm were resuspended in 500 µL of staining buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry. The level of mitochondrial membrane potential was expressed as the ratio of red fluorescence (JC-1 aggregates) to green fluorescence (JC-1 monomers); a higher ratio indicated a higher mitochondrial membrane potential.

Analysis of sperm plasma membrane integrity

Sperm plasma membrane integrity was detected using the SYBR14/PI double-staining method combined with flow cytometry. A total of 1 × 10⁶ sperm were incubated with 10 µL of SYBR14 and 5 µL of PI at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. After incubation, 1 mL of PBS was added and mixed, and the sample was analyzed by flow cytometry. SYBR14 can enter live sperm with intact plasma membranes and emit green fluorescence, whereas PI can only enter dead sperm with damaged plasma membranes and emit red fluorescence. The proportion of sperm with intact plasma membranes was calculated based on two-parameter dot plot analysis.

Analysis of sperm acrosome integrity

Sperm acrosome integrity was detected using FITC-PNA staining. Ten microliters of sperm suspension were smeared onto a glass slide and air-dried, followed by fixation with 95% ethanol for 15 min. The fixed slide was stained with FITC-PNA for 30 min, rinsed with running water, and air-dried. Sperm nuclei were stained with 1 µmol/L DAPI for 3 min, rinsed with water, and air-dried. Fluorescence microscopy was used for imaging and analysis. Sperm with intact acrosomes showed uniform green fluorescence in the head, whereas those with damaged acrosomes exhibited unstained regions or uneven staining in the head. The proportion of sperm with intact acrosomes was calculated.

TLR8 immunofluorescence staining

Core principle: Previous studies have confirmed that TLR8 is encoded by the X chromosome and specifically expressed in the tail of X sperm, with no expression in Y sperm. Based on this, we used TLR8 fluorescence staining as a preliminary screening indicator for the proportion of X/Y sperm: sperm with positive TLR8 fluorescence signal were preliminarily judged as X sperm, and TLR8-negative sperm were preliminarily judged as Y sperm. The final separation efficiency was verified by IVF combined with PCR embryo sex identification at the embryo level.

TLR8 immunofluorescence staining was performed to identify putative X sperm. Briefly, 1 × 10⁶ sperm were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, washed twice with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 30 min, sperm were incubated with anti-TLR8 antibody (Abcam, ab134932, 1:200 dilution) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:500 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 3 min. Fluorescence images were captured using an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the proportion of TLR8-positive sperm was counted in at least 5 random fields per sample (≥ 200 sperm per field).

In vitro fertilization (IVF) and embryo culture

Oocyte maturation

Cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) were aspirated from ovarian follicles (2–6 mm), selected for uniform cytoplasm and intact cumulus layers, and cultured in TCM-199 medium (10% FBS, 10 ng/mL EGF, 0.5 µg/mL LH) at 38.5 °C, 5% CO₂, saturated humidity for 24–26 h.

IVF and embryo culture

Mature oocytes (n = 150 per R848 concentration) were co-cultured with sorted sperm (1 × 10⁶ sperm/mL) in BO fertilization medium (0.6% BSA, 10 mM caffeine) at 38.5 °C, 5% CO₂ for 6–8 h. Zygotes were transferred to SOFaa medium (5% FBS, 0.2 mM glutamine) at 38.5 °C, 5% CO₂, 5% O₂, 90% N₂, with half-medium replacement every 48 h. Cleavage rate (48 h post-fertilization) and blastocyst rate (7 days post-fertilization) were recorded.

Embryo sex identification

In vitro fertilized embryo amplified the SRY gene (Y-specific) and GAPDH (housekeeping gene). Primers:

First round: SRY-0 F: AATACTTTCGCCTTTGGG, R: CTGCTGTGATGCTCCTTT; GAPDH-0 F: TCGGAGTGAACGGATTTG, R: CTTCTGGGTGGCAGTGAT.

Second round: SRY-6 F: CGAAGACGAAAGGTGGCT, R: GCAAGGGATTTCTGTGGC; GAPDH-1 F: GTTCCACGGCACAGTCAA, R: GTCATAAGTCCCTCCACGAT.

PCR conditions: Pre-denaturation 95 °C/3 min; 30 cycles (first round)/35 cycles (second round): 94 °C/30 s, 58 °C (first round)/62°C (second round)/20 s, 72 °C/20 s; final extension 72 °C/5 min. Products were electrophoresed on 2% agarose gel.

Blastocysts (n = 30 per group) were individually lysed in 20 µL of lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 50 mM KCl, 0.5% Tween 20, 0.1 mg/mL proteinase K) at 55 °C for 2 h, followed by 95 °C for 10 min to inactivate proteinase K. Nested PCR was performed using the primers and conditions described above to amplify the SRY gene (Y-specific) and GAPDH housekeeping gene. Male embryos (SRY⁺/GAPDH⁺) showed two bands; female embryos (GAPDH⁺ only) showed one band.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated via G*Power 3.1 software: assuming a 15% difference in blastocyst rate between groups, α = 0.05, power = 0.8, the required number of oocytes per group was 120.

Normality of continuous data was tested via Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and compared via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Non-normal data were expressed as median (interquartile range), and compared via Kruskal-Wallis test. Exact P-values are reported for key results (e.g., optimal R848 concentration effects, ATP content and mitochondrial membrane potential changes); for other comparisons, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and P < 0.01 was considered highly significant. Letter-based significance indicators are defined as follows: different lowercase letters indicate significant differences between groups at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM, USA), and all graphs were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad, USA).

Experimental design

A total of 18 healthy Suffolk rams with proven fertility were used in this study. Each ram was considered an independent biological replicate (n = 18; no data pooling was performed across biological replicates). For each biological replicate, three technical replicates were performed to assess intra-assay variation. All statistical analyses were performed with biological replicates as the primary experimental unit.

Results

Optimization of R848 concentration for sperm separation

Sperm motility parameters

R848 treatment significantly reduced X-sperm (lower layer) motility parameters but had no effect on Y-sperm (upper layer) (Table 1). At 1 µM R848, X-sperm total motility (TM) decreased to 66.75% ± 7.26% (vs. control 82.36% ± 4.23%, P = 0.026), and VCL decreased to 78.26 ± 6.52 μm/s (vs. control 112.45 ± 8.38 μm/s, P = 0.005). Y-sperm TM (81.59% ± 4.56%) and VCL (109.88 ± 7.25 μm/s) were not significantly different from controls (P > 0.05). These results indicate that R848 exerts a dose-dependent, specific inhibitory effect on X-sperm motility.

TLR8 fluorescence staining and X/Y sperm ratio analysis

To investigate the physiological ratio of X/Y sperm in normal sheep semen, and the changes of X/Y sperm proportion in upper and lower layers after R848 treatment, semen samples from control group and R848 treatment groups (0.2 µM, 0.6 µM, 1.0 µM, 1.4 µM, 1.8 µM) were subjected to TLR8 fluorescence staining, then the proportion of X and Y sperm in each layer was calculated.

As shown in Fig. 1, the proportion of X sperm in the control group was 48.38% ± 3.51%, and Y sperm was 51.62% ± 3.51%, presenting an approximately 1:1 physiological ratio. At the optimal R848 concentration of 1.0 µM, the proportion of TLR8-positive X sperm in the lower layer reached 80.50% ± 4.96%, while the proportion of TLR8-negative Y sperm in the upper layer reached 90.30% ± 5.21%. This separation efficiency was significantly higher than that of the 0.2 µM group (67.58% ± 1.87% X sperm in lower layer, 77.21% ± 1.92% Y sperm in upper layer, P = 0.022) and 1.8 µM group (64.22% ± 2.56% X sperm in lower layer, 74.32% ± 5.65% Y sperm in upper layer, P = 0.021). No significant difference was observed between 1.0 µM group and 0.6 µM, 1.4 µM groups (P > 0.05).

These results confirmed that R848 treatment can effectively alter the distribution of X/Y sperm in the upper and lower layers, and achieve efficient separation of X and Y sperm from sheep, with the optimal concentration determined as 1.0 µM.

Effects of R848 addition on sheep sperm motility

As shown in Fig. 2, the addition of R848 caused a decrease in the motility of lower-layer sperm (control group: 82.36%±4.23%, experimental group lower layer: 66.75%±7.26%), with most sperm showing circular motion in place. The movement trajectories of upper-layer sperm were similar to those of the control group, and there was no significant difference in sperm motility (control group: 82.36%±4.23%, experimental group upper layer: 81.59%±4.56%). These results indicate that R848 treatment can affect the motility of X sperm but has no effect on the motility of Y sperm.

Effects of R848 addition on sheep sperm acrosome integrity and plasma membrane integrity

Sperm acrosome integrity and plasma membrane integrity parameters can reflect sperm fertilization ability to a certain extent. Based on this, this study measured the acrosome integrity and plasma membrane integrity of sheep sperm in the R848 addition group and the control group, and statistically analyzed the significance. As shown in Fig. 3, there were no significant differences in acrosome integrity between the upper-layer sperm, lower-layer sperm, and control group sperm in the treatment group. There were also no significant differences in plasma membrane integrity between the upper-layer sperm, lower-layer sperm, and control group sperm in the treatment group. R848 treatment did not affect the acrosome and plasma membrane integrity of sheep sperm, indicating that R848 treatment had no significant effect on the fertilization ability of either X or Y sperm. These findings demonstrate that R848 treatment does not damage the acrosome or plasma membrane integrity of sheep sperm.

Effects of R848 addition on ATP content and mitochondrial membrane potential in sheep sperm

ATP content and mitochondrial membrane potential in sheep sperm treated with R848 addition group and control group were measured, and statistical analysis of significance was performed. As shown in Fig. 4, there were no significant differences in ATP content and mitochondrial membrane potential between upper-layer sperm in the treatment group and control group sperm, while ATP content and mitochondrial membrane potential in lower-layer sperm of the treatment group were significantly reduced. At the optimal 1 µM concentration, the ATP content of lower-layer X sperm decreased by 24.82% (P = 0.006), and the mitochondrial membrane potential decreased by 29.75% (P = 0.023), compared with the control group. These data suggest that R848 specifically impairs energy metabolism in X sperm but not Y sperm.

Effects of R848 concentration on In vitro fertilization efficiency and sex ratio of sheep sex-controlled semen

R848 concentration gradients of 0.2 µM, 0.6 µM, 1.0 µM, 1.4 µM, and 1.8 µM were set for in vitro fertilization experiments, and their cleavage rates, blastocyst rates, and embryo sex ratios were statistically analyzed. As shown in Fig. 5, when the R848 addition amount was 1 µM, the cleavage rate of Y sperm-fertilized eggs was 79.38%±10.25%, the blastocyst rate was 59.26%±10.27%, and the proportion of male embryos was 80.20%±3.70%; the cleavage rate of X sperm-fertilized eggs was 53.46%±4.50%, the blastocyst rate was 32.15%±2.78%, and the proportion of female embryos was 87.56%±9.51%. The in vitro fertilization efficiency and sex ratio were optimal at this R848 concentration. These results confirm that 1 µM is the optimal R848 concentration for efficient sperm separation and IVF.

Effects of storage time of sheep sex-controlled semen on in vitro fertilization efficiency and sex ratio

Using the optimal R848 addition amount of 1 µM, time gradients of 0 h, 4 h, 8 h, 16 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, and 120 h were set for in vitro fertilization experiments, and their cleavage rates, blastocyst rates, and embryo sex ratios were statistically analyzed. As shown in Fig. 6, with the extension of storage time, the cleavage rates and blastocyst rates of eggs fertilized by X and Y sperm both showed a downward trend, while the embryo sex ratio remained basically stable. Within 120 h of storage at 17 °C, Y sperm maintained stable total motility, progressive motility, cleavage rate, blastocyst rate and sex ratio (all P > 0.05 compared with 0 h); X sperm had little influence within 24 h of storage, but showed an unexpected increase in female embryo ratio at 96 h.

Effects of dilution and elution methods of sheep sex-controlled semen on in vitro fertilization efficiency and sex ratio

Using the optimal R848 addition amount of 1 µM, dilution method controls for diluent and sorting solution were set, and gradients of 100 µL, 500 µL, 1000 µL resuspension volume and one or two elution times were set for in vitro fertilization experiments. Cleavage rates, blastocyst rates, and embryo sex ratios were statistically analyzed. As shown in Fig. 7, there was no significant difference in diluting sorted sperm with sorting solution or diluent, and the resuspension and elution method of 1000 µL resuspension and two elutions was the best. These results indicate that 1000 µL elution twice is the optimal post-separation processing method.

Discussion

This study established a systematically optimized in vitro-validated, sheep-specific R848-mediated X/Y sperm separation system, which achieved high-efficiency sex separation without damaging sperm fertilization potential, providing a low-cost, scalable alternative to flow cytometry for sheep precision breeding. The optimal R848 concentration for sheep was consistent with that reported for goats but significantly higher than that for cattle, likely due to the 2.3-fold higher TLR7/8 expression level in sheep sperm [10, 11].

Compared with previous R848-related studies, this study has three core innovations for practical sheep breeding: first, we systematically optimized the full-process parameters of R848-mediated sheep sperm separation, addressed the problem that cross-species parameters cannot be directly applied to sheep in vitro; second, we optimized the elution method and storage conditions for sorted sheep sperm for the first time, which fills the gap of large-scale application parameters in previous sheep-related studies [5]; third, we completed the verification of separation efficiency at the embryo level via IVF combined with PCR sex identification, which significantly enhances the reliability of the system, R848 treatment did not damage the acrosome and plasma membrane integrity of either X or Y sperm, which is a critical advantage over flow cytometry, as it preserves the fertilization potential of sorted sperm, evidenced by the 59.26% ± 10.27% blastocyst rate of sorted Y sperm in this study, which is even higher than the 52.3% reported in goat R848 separation studies [13, 14].

In this study, we observed that R848 specifically reduced ATP content and mitochondrial membrane potential in X sperm but had no significant effect on Y sperm. This phenotype is consistent with the conserved mechanism reported in previous studies in mice, cattle and goats [6, 12, 15, 16]. Specifically, Wen et al. [15] demonstrated that TLR7/8 activation inhibits mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in bovine X sperm via the PI3K/GSK3α/β and NFκB signaling pathways. Our findings further support the evolutionary conservation of this mechanism across ruminant species [7]. However, this study did not directly measure reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, mitochondrial respiration rates, or downstream signaling molecules such as PI3K/GSK3 and NF-κB [10]. The exact molecular mechanism underlying R848-mediated X-sperm inhibition in sheep requires further investigation through proteomic and transcriptomic analyses.

Notably, X-sperm fractions exhibited significantly lower cleavage and blastocyst rates compared with Y-sperm fractions at the optimal R848 concentration. This may be attributed to sustained metabolic suppression induced by R848, which could reduce X-sperm fertilization competence even after elution [17]. Similar observations have been reported in dairy goats, where R848-treated X sperm also showed reduced developmental competence [5]. For practical applications, this reinforces the recommendation that X-sperm fractions should be used within 24 h after sorting to minimize the impact of metabolic inhibition.

A key advantage of this system is its low cost and scalability. Based on our laboratory’s actual reagent and labor costs, the R848-mediated separation method costs approximately $0.5 per dose of sexed semen, which is less than 5% of the cost of commercial flow cytometry-based sexed semen ($15–20 per dose in international markets) [3]. This significant cost advantage makes the system particularly suitable for large-scale commercial sheep breeding, where high input costs are a major barrier to the adoption of sex control technologies.

While this study provides a comprehensive in vitro validation of the R848-mediated sheep sperm separation system and defines the optimal operational parameters, several limitations should be noted. First, only in vitro validation was performed at the sperm functional and embryonic developmental levels; large-scale in vivo artificial insemination experiments under commercial farm conditions are needed to confirm offspring sex ratio, pregnancy rate and lambing performance [18]. Second, separation efficiency was assessed by TLR8 fluorescence staining and embryo sex ratio, but gold-standard methods such as fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or digital PCR (ddPCR) were not used to directly quantify X/Y sperm proportions [4]. Third, the molecular mechanism of R848-mediated X-sperm inhibition was not fully elucidated; ROS levels, mitochondrial respiration kinetics and downstream signaling pathways were not measured in this study [2]. Finally, long-term DNA integrity and oxidative stress status of sorted sperm during storage were not evaluated [1].

Future studies will focus on addressing these limitations to further improve and promote the application of this system. Specifically, we will conduct large-scale in vivo artificial insemination experiments under commercial farm conditions to validate the system’s performance in practical production. We will also use FISH or ddPCR to directly quantify X/Y sperm proportions and refine separation efficiency assessment. In addition, proteomic and transcriptomic analyses will be performed to elucidate the exact molecular mechanism of R848-mediated X-sperm inhibition. Finally, we will evaluate the long-term DNA integrity of sorted sperm during storage to ensure the biosafety of the system for long-term application.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study systematically established and optimized a sheep-specific R848-mediated X/Y sperm separation system, and comprehensively validated its in vitro efficacy and biocompatibility at the sperm functional and embryonic developmental levels. The optimal technical parameters were determined as follows: 1 µM R848 treatment at 37 °C for 30 min, 1000 µL elution twice, Y-sperm storage at 17 °C for up to 120 h with stable in vitro performance, and X-sperm use within 24 h after sorting.

This system achieved efficient separation of X and Y sperm, with 80.20% ± 3.70% male embryos and 87.56% ± 9.51% female embryos via IVF-PCR validation. R848 specifically inhibited X-sperm motility by reducing ATP production and mitochondrial membrane potential, without damaging acrosome or plasma membrane integrity.

This low-cost, biocompatible system addresses key limitations of existing sheep sex control technologies and has significant potential for application in precision sheep breeding after further in vivo validation.
