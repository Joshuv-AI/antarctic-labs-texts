Co-occurrence of native and invasive malaria vectors in anthropogenic habitats in Metehara, Ethiopia: Opportunities for urban malaria control
Authors: Temesgen Ashine, Abena Kochora, Muluken Assefa, Nigatu Negash, Bedasa Gidisa, Elifaged Hailemeskel, Merit Alemayehu, Chaltu Assefa, Mihret Ebabu, Galana Mamo Ayana, Tedros Nigusse, Fikregabrail Aberra Kassa, Endashaw Esayas, Adane Eyasu, Sagni Chali, Adrienne Epstein, Fatou Jaiteh, John Bradley, Patricia Doumbe-Belisse, Alison M Reynolds, Fekadu Massebo, Anne L Wilson, David Weetman, Martin James Donnelly, Endalamaw Gadisa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLocal data are essential to understand the threat posed by invasive Anopheles stephensi and native malaria vectors on urban malaria transmission. This study investigated key bioecological features of invasive and native malaria vectors in Metehara town, Ethiopia. In parallel with a case-control study assessing the impact of An. stephensi on urban malaria transmission, a bioecological assessment was conducted between November 2023 and October 2024. All potential larval breeding habitats were mapped, followed by bimonthly collections of immature and adult mosquitoes from randomly selected locations. Immatures were collected using standard dippers, and adults with CDC light traps, BG Pro traps, and Prokopack aspirators. Adult Anopheles were identified morphologically, while species identification of immatures, adult blood-meal analysis, and sporozoite detection were performed via PCR. Of 767 potential larval breeding habitats, 98.3% (n = 754) were anthropogenic, with the majority (95.2%, n = 730) accessible for oviposition, either fully (73.1%, n = 551) or partially (23.7%, n = 179). More than half were water storage containers for human consumption (37.3%, n = 281) or associated with construction (20.8%, n = 157), while abandoned containers, including discarded tyres, accounted for 22.3% (n = 168). Among anthropogenic habitats positive for Anopheles immatures (55.3%, n = 417), one-third contained both An. stephensi and An. arabiensis. Habitat positivity for An. arabiensis showed significant seasonal variation (likelihood ratio, LR = 46.96, P < 0.01), whereas An. stephensi remained stable (LR = 13.06, P = 0.11). Of 2078 adult catches, An. arabiensis was the most abundant species (63.7%, n = 1323), followed by An. pharoensis (26.4%, n = 549). The human blood index was highest in An. arabiensis (21.8%), compared with An. pharoensis (8.3%) and An. stephensi (1.9%). Sporozoite rate was highest in An. pharoensis (4.2%, 23/548), followed by An. arabiensis (0.4%, 5/1321), while no An. stephensi tested positive (0/173). In conclusion, most breeding habitats were anthropogenic, supporting both native and invasive vectors. Anopheles arabiensis exhibited seasonal variation, whereas An. stephensi remained stable. Integrated vector control targeting anthropogenic larval habitats is recommended. Identification of An. pharoensis from larval pools, given its high sporozoite rate, is critical to guide urban malaria control.Keywords: Anopheles stephensi, Anopheles arabiensis, Anopheles pharoensis, Co-occurrence, Blood meal, Sporozoite rate

Background

Malaria remains a major global public health challenge, with an estimated 263 million cases in 2023, reflecting an increase of 11 million from 2022. Africa bears a disproportionate burden, accounting for approximately 94% of global malaria cases and 95% of related deaths (WHO, 2024a). Although malaria remains predominantly a rural disease, rapid and unplanned urbanization is increasingly contributing to high transmission risks among underserved urban communities (WHO, 2022). Africa is undergoing rapid urban growth, and by 2050, more than 60% of its population is expected to reside in cities (Cobbinah et al., 2015). Alarmingly, the invasion and spread of the urban-adapted malaria vector Anopheles stephensi across the continent (Faulde et al., 2014; Carter et al., 2018; Ahmed et al., 2021; Ochomo et al., 2023; WHO, 2023) may place an additional 120 million people at risk (Sinka et al., 2020). The rise in reported malaria cases in Djibouti city, Djibouti (Faulde et al., 2014; de Santi et al., 2021) and Dire Dawa town, Ethiopia (Emiru et al., 2023) has been linked to An. stephensi, underscoring its emerging public health threat in urban settings.

Ethiopia is a fast-urbanising country (Koroso et al., 2021) endemic for both Plasmodium falciparum and P. vivax, accounting for 70% and 30% of malaria cases, respectively (NMEP, 2020). The country is currently experiencing a significant resurgence of malaria, with more than 7.3 million cases reported in 2024, nearly double the number reported in the previous year (WHO, 2024b). Given the ongoing expansion of the invasive Asian malaria vector An. stephensi in Ethiopia (Balkew et al., 2020, 2021; Ashine et al., 2024b), understanding its contribution to malaria resurgence in Ethiopia is crucial (Jalilian et al., 2024). Despite the implications of An. stephensi during a malaria outbreak in Dire Dawan town in 2022, other data are conflicting with respect to its role in driving malaria transmission. At some sites where An. stephensi was the only vector collected in 2022, the number of malaria cases was lower than that in 2017. Moreover, malaria case trends in urban settings between 2014 and 2019 showed no clear link to An. stephensi presence (Teka et al., 2023). Similarly, a recent nationwide geospatial and temporal trend modelling utilising public health emergency management (PHEM) malaria data for 2013–2022 did not show An. stephensi as a driving factor for malaria risk (Jalilian et al., 2024).

With a relatively higher burden of malaria in rural areas of Africa, malaria vector control and infection prevention efforts have been focused on rural settings (WHO, 2022). In rural settings, malaria vectors are commonly believed to breed in natural habitats (Mnzava et al., 2022; WHO, 2022). However, there is evidence of the adaptation of primary African malaria vectors to anthropogenic habitats in urban settings (Doumbe-Belisse et al., 2021). In Ethiopia, both the primary (An. arabiensis) and secondary (An. pharoensis) vectors have been detected in adult collections across urban centres (Krafsur, 1977; Ashine et al., 2024b), where they are associated with malaria transmission (Krafsur, 1977; Doumbe-Belisse et al., 2021). While the potential impact of An. stephensi on urban malaria cannot be discounted, there is a clear risk of malaria transmission in African urban settings resulting from the adaptation of native vectors (Doumbe-Belisse et al., 2018, 2021). Thus, the ecology of urban malaria in the context of both invasive vector and the adaptation of native vectors needs attention (Taylor et al., 2024).

Evidence on the growing importance of urban malaria in Ethiopia is accumulating. Between 2014 and 2019, urban districts accounted for a notable 15.3% of the national malaria burden (Teka et al., 2023). Similarly, a malaria trend analysis in southern Ethiopian between 2012 and 2019 showed a higher burden in Dilla town than in surrounding rural areas (Molla et al., 2021). However, information on the relative roles of An. stephensi and native vectors in urban malaria transmission, particularly in areas where they coexist, remains limited. To shed light on this, an extensive case-control study was initiated in Metehara town in October 2023 to assess the relative importance of household exposure to An. stephensi and endemic malaria vectors (Ashine et al., 2024a). The Metehara town is malaria-endemic (Mandefro et al., 2024), and the presence of An. stephensi has been reported since 2019 (Balkew et al., 2021; Ashine et al., 2024b). The species has also been found to exhibit resistance to multiple insecticides (Teshome et al., 2023).

To better understand the bioecological characteristics of the invasive An. stephensi and native malaria vectors, we conducted an in-depth entomological investigation between November 2023 and October 2024. This study characterised larval breeding habitats, species composition, blood-meal sources, and Plasmodium sporozoite rates among malaria vectors in Metehara town, Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study area

Metehara town in the Oromia regional state is at an altitude of 947 m above sea level. The town has an estimated population of 47,661 (Municipality unpublished health office data). The town is divided into 2 urban kebeles (smallest administrative units), Metehara and Haro Adi, separated by 3 km (Fig. 1). Across kebeles, housing is predominantly within enclosed spaces called compounds consisting of one or more households. Owing to piped water shortages, communities are obliged to dig pits and to use medium- to large-sized reservoirs to store water for household and/or hotel consumption and other economic activities.

Position of the Metehara town administration within Ethiopia (left panel) and details of the Metehara town kebeles (right panel).

In Metehara, residents depend on service-based industries, commerce (production of construction materials, mainly bricks) and farming, including subsistence farming and a large government-owned sugarcane plantation located south of the town, 6 km from Metehara and 3 km from Haro Adi kebeles. The town is based around the main arterial routes between Ethiopia and Djibouti. The warm and humid climate, alongside the presence of Lake Basaka, creates a suitable environment for mosquito breeding and survival. The main rainy season starts in June and extends to August/September, while the short rainy season runs from March to April/May, although the rainy seasons are increasingly erratic. Malaria transmission is therefore bimodal, with peaks from September to November and from March to May (Mandefro et al., 2024).

Mapping larval habitats and selection of sampling points

Between November and December 2023, a team of two entomologists, together with community leaders and local guides, systematically searched for potential mosquito breeding habitats. These included any aquatic bodies with a storage period of 15 days or more, as identified by owners, local guides or nearby residents in open-field settings. The survey covered compounds (both private and public), construction sites, open fields, and the nearby lakeshore. All potential and productive larval habitats were georeferenced using a global positioning system (GPS).

Each of the town’s two kebeles was divided into 4 quadrants, using town crossroads and main arterial roads. An exhaustive baseline survey identified 265 potential and productive larval habitats (Supplementary file 1: Table S1), of which 100 sampling points, each representing a larval habitat and a 50-m radius, were included for in-depth entomological study. The sampling points were proportionally allocated among quadrants based on the number of potential larval habitats identified at baseline and the number of households in each quadrant. The sampling points were selected from the list of mapped habitats in each quadrant via simple spatial inhibitory random sampling (Chipeta et al., 2016). The selected structures within each sampling point were first visited, and the owner of the habitat/structure or household head was asked to provide informed consent for mosquito/larval collection. In subsequent survey rounds, if the selected larval habitat did not contain water, the nearest larval habitat that contained water within a 50-m radius was used as a replacement.

Immature stage collection

From December 2023 to October 2024, every 2 months, 100 selected sampling points were resampled for immature stages. Immature collections were conducted via a standard dipper (John W. Hock Company, Model 320, cup volume 300 ml) (WHO, 2013). Briefly, the larval habitats were systematically searched, and depending on the size of the larval habitat, 1 to 10 dips were taken to determine the presence or absence of immature stages. The number of dips taken and the number of immatures per dip were recorded and used to calculate the average number of larvae per dip. The collected immature specimens were morphologically identified as anopheline or culicine and then counted and recorded. The specimens identified as anopheline were pooled by larval habitat and preserved in absolute ethanol (96%) in a 2-ml tube, which was labelled with a unique habitat identification number. The samples were maintained at room temperature until they were transported to the Armauer Hansen Research Institute (AHRI) laboratory in Addis Ababa for species identification.

Characterisation of larval breeding habitats

The systematic observation and recording of the description of larval breeding habitats was performed via a pre-prepared and piloted checklist. In general, the nature of the larval habitat was classified as natural if created by rain or on the lake shore or anthropogenic if created by human activities, including but not limited to construction activities, to preserve or generate water for household or hotel consumption for waste management and farming (livestock and plantation) activities. The anthropogenic habitats were further characterised on the basis of their fabric/substrate, land use in the surrounding area, and purpose of water storage. The presence/absence or partial presence of a plant/tree shadow (shade) and any cover that might restrict mosquito access for oviposition and the presence of visible green algae, aquatic vegetation and dry waste were recorded. Estimations (in metres) were also made of the length, width, and depth of the breeding habitat and its distance from the nearest animal shelter and/or human structure (if present). The water temperature and pH were measured via a water quality tester (NOYAFA, EZ-9901, pH meter 3 in 1). The anthropogenic larval habitats were ultimately classified as animal drinking water troughs, locally constructed water reservoirs (made of either concrete or a shallow well with a plastic lining or a deep well/pit), drainage channels, abandoned construction sites/pits, discarded vehicle tyres, water storage drums (either plastic, metal or fibre material) and deep wells/pits (built to store water for household or hotel use).

Adult mosquito collection and morphological identification

Similarly, from December 2023 to October 2024, adult mosquitoes were collected via Prokopack aspirators (Vazquez-Prokopec et al., 2009) to collect resting mosquitoes in and around structures within the compounds and included occupied/unoccupied houses, fences, vegetation, barrows such as mounds, manholes, piles of wood/stones, or edges of aquatic habitats. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) light traps (LTs) were placed indoors (Namango et al., 2022) at the foot of occupied beds, and BG pro traps were placed outdoors (Degener et al., 2021), 5–10 m away from the indoor traps, to collect host-seeking mosquitoes. Mosquitoes were first identified morphologically, and then all anopheline mosquitoes were sorted to the species level using the keys by Coetzee (2020). Feeding and reproductive status (freshly fed, unfed, gravid, and half gravid) were also recorded. The samples were then preserved individually in 1.5-ml Eppendorf tubes with two lid holes and placed in Ziplock bags containing silica gel. Culicines were discarded after recording.

Mosquito DNA extraction

The head-thorax region was dissected from the abdomen of wild-caught females, and the pooled immature samples were dried in a heat block (65 °C for 10 min). The samples were then homogenised (BioSpec Products, Bartlesville, USA) with 200 μl of molecular-grade water and 0.2 mg of 1.0 mm zirconia/silica beads at 3800 rpm for 20 s. After centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 1 min, 150 μl of the supernatant from the adult samples and 200 μl from the immature samples were transferred to separate 1.5-ml microcentrifuge tubes. DNA was extracted from the head-thoraxes and abdomens of adults and immature stages via modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) extraction buffer as described elsewhere (Jangra and Ghosh, 2022). The extracted DNA was stored at −20 °C until PCR analysis.

Molecular identification of Anopheles species

Molecular identification of pooled larval samples was conducted only for An. stephensi and An. arabiensis, as these were previously identified as the dominant species (Ashine et al., 2024b). Separate PCR was conducted using primers that targeted the internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) of An. stephensi (Singh et al., 2023) and the ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene of An. arabiensis (Scott et al., 1993). PCR was performed in a 20 μl reaction volume containing 1 μl of DNA template, 0.25 μM of the ST-F primer, 0.37 μM of the UD2-R primer, and 10 μl of 2× DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo-Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Pub. No. MAN0012704). The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 68 °C for 7 min for denaturation, annealing and extension, respectively; and a final extension step at 68 °C for 7 min. Amplicons were identified via visualisation on a 2% agarose gel (Invitrogen™, Carlsbad, CA, USA; UltraPure™ agarose powder).

Detection of Plasmodium sporozoites

The mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene was targeted (Echeverry et al., 2017) with modifications: DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix was used, and the number of thermal cycling steps was reduced from 40 to 34 cycles. PCRs were conducted in a 25 μl volume, each containing 3 μl of DNA template extracted from the head and thorax, 0.25 μM of each primer, and 12.5 μl of 2× DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix. The thermal cycling conditions involved initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 34 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 60 s, annealing at 62 °C for 60 s, and extension at 72 °C for 90 s. A final extension step was performed at 72 °C for 10 min. The expected size of the positive amplicon was 520 bp. 18S rRNA nested PCR was conducted to identify P. falciparum and P. vivax in cox1 PCR-positive samples as described by Snounou and Singh (2002).

Blood-meal source detection

Blood-meal source identification was carried out following a published protocol (Kent and Norris, 2005), with modifications, reducing the number of thermal cycling steps from 40 to 34 cycles. PCR primers that target the mitochondrial cytochrome b gene for the identification of humans (132 bp), goats (680 bp), dogs (561 bp), and cows (334 bp) were used. PCRs were performed in a 25 μl volume using DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix (2× ) and 0.2 μM of each primer. The thermal cycling conditions included initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 28 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 60 s, annealing at 57 °C (cow or dog) or 62 °C (human or goat) for 60 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 60 s with a final extension step at 72 °C for 7 min. The PCR products were visualised by 2% agarose (Invitogen™ UltraPure™ agarose powder) gel electrophoresis.

Malaria case and climate data

Weekly confirmed malaria case data for Metehara town were obtained from the Oromia Regional Health Bureau. The data were aggregated into monthly totals to correspond with the temporal resolution of the Anopheles habitat positivity data. Corresponding climatic variables, including maximum and minimum temperature, precipitation, and relative humidity for the study period were obtained from the National Meteorology Institute of Ethiopia. The climatic data were also aggregated on a monthly basis to assess their temporal association with mosquito habitat positivity rates.

Data analysis

The data were collected using electronic tablets with REDCap-designed forms (Harris et al., 2009, 2019) and uploaded daily to the AHRI data server. The datasets were downloaded, cleaned and managed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA), and Stata software release 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) was used for analysis. Larval habitat characteristics and positivity, relative abundance of Anopheles species, blood-meal indices, and sporozoite rates were summarised as percentages. The mean number of adult Anopheles per collection method was calculated by dividing the total number of a given species collected by the total number of collection nights or attempts. To identify environmental factors associated with Anopheles habitat positivity, univariate analyses were first conducted for all variables. Variables with P ≤ 0.4 in the univariate analysis (Supplementary file 1: Table S2) were included in a multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression model, using the survey round as a grouping variable to account for repeated sampling. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Immature density across breeding habitats was compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann-Whitney U) test for 2-category variables and the Kruskal-Wallis rank test for variables with 3 or more categories. To assess seasonal variation in species-specific habitat positivity, separate logistic regression models were fitted for each species, with survey date (categorical) as the sole predictor. Each full model was compared to its null model using likelihood ratio (LR) tests. Seasonal patterns and associations between habitat positivity for An. stephensi and An. arabiensis with malaria cases data, and climatic variables, were illustrated using line graphs. Differences in Plasmodium sporozoite rates between An. pharoensis and An. arabiensis were assessed using a chi-square test. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of potential larval breeding habitats

The majority of potential larval breeding habitats were anthropogenic (98.3%, 754/767), including water storage drums (34.2%, 258/754), locally made water reservoirs (32.8%, 274/754), and deep wells/pits (13.9%, 105/754). Notably, 22.3% (168/754) of the larval habitats were abandoned water containers, and discarded vehicle tyres (7.6%, 57/754). Habitat materials included plastic (33.2%, 250/754), concrete (21.2%, 160/754), and earth (no artificial lining) (17.9%, 135/754), including construction pits, deep wells/pits, and metals (13.3%, 100/754) (Table 1). Many of the breeding habitats were located within compounds of occupied houses (68.2%, 514/754). Most water storage containers were used for household and/or hotel consumption (37.3%, 281/754), construction-related activities (20.8%, 157/754) and farming purposes (13.4%, 101/754). Most larval habitats were either fully (72.7%, 541/754) or partially accessible (24.1%, 179/754) for oviposition. The median and interquartile range (IQR) of the larval habitat size, temperature and pH were 1 m2 (0.3–4.0), 28 °C (27–29 °C) and 7.5 (6.9–7.9), respectively.

Larval breeding habitat positivity

Over half (63.4%, 486/767) of all the surveyed larval breeding habitats were positive for any mosquito immatures: Anopheles spp., culicines, or both. Among the positive larval habitats, 28.8% (221/767) were positive only for Anopheles spp., 8.5% (65/767) were positive only for culicines, and 26.1% (200/767) were positive for both. The positivity rate for any mosquito immatures was high in discarded vehicle tyres (93.0%, 53/57), habitats located in the compounds of religious and other institutes (71.2%, 37/52) and within a 50-m radius from animal shelters (65.3%, 231/354), abandoned water storage containers (78.6%, 132/168), habitats fully shaded by trees or plants (with little or no sunlight exposure) (80.0%, 8/10), fully accessible habitats for oviposition (68.0%, 368/541), habitats containing algae (74.7%, 59/79), aquatic vegetation (83.1%, 54/65) and visible dry waste (74.6%, 129/173) (Table 1).

Co-occurrence of An. stephensi with native vectors

Among the Anopheles immature samples (n = 417) pooled by habitat and assayed via species-specific PCR, 93.1% (n = 388) tested positive for at least one Anopheles species. Among the species-specific PCR-positive (n = 388) Anopheles immature samples, most (70.4%, n = 273) were An. stephensi only. Co-habitation by both An. stephensi and An. arabiensis accounted for 28.1% (n = 109) of the samples, whereas a small proportion (1.5%, n = 6) were positive for An. arabiensis only (Fig. 2 and Table 2). In terms of habitat type, abandoned construction pits (58.3%), drainage channels (38.9 %) and locally made water reservoirs (31.9%) were commonly shared by both An. stephensi and An. arabiensis. However, the habitats occupied by An. stephensi alone were most likely to be animal drinking water troughs (84.2%), water storage drums (77.2%) and deep wells/pits (75.0%) (Table 2).

Determinants of Anopheles habitat positivity

Univariate analysis identified several factors associated with Anopheles larval habitat positivity. These included the type of anthropogenic larval habitat (OR = 0.9, 95% CI: 0.9–1.0, P = 0.01), land use of the surrounding area (OR = 0.9, 95% CI: 0.8–1.0, P = 0.01), purpose of water storage (OR = 0.8, 95% CI: 0.7–1.0, P = 0.01), presence of a container cover (OR = 0.6, 95% CI: 0.4–0.8, P = 0.01), green algae (OR = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.2–3.1, P = 0.01), aquatic vegetation (OR = 2.8, 95% CI: 1.6–4.8, P = 0.01), and water temperature (OR = 0.9, 95% CI: 0.9–1.0, P = 0.01). The co-occurrence of An. stephensi and An. arabiensis was influenced by the study quadrant (OR = 1.2, 95% CI: 1.1–1.3, P = 0.01), type of anthropogenic habitat (OR = 0.9, 95% CI: 0.8–1.0, P = 0.01), purpose of water storage (OR = 0.8, 95% CI: 0.7–1.0, P = 0.01) and the presence of green algae (OR = 2.6, 95% CI: 1.5–4.5, P = 0.01) or aquatic vegetation (OR = 2.3, 95% CI: 1.3–4.1, P = 0.01), and water temperature (OR = 0.9, 95% CI: 0.8–1.0, P = 0.01). Habitat positivity for An. stephensi was further associated with kebele (village) (OR = 0.7, 95% CI: 0.5–1.0, P = 0.01), study quadrant (OR = 0.9, 95% CI: 0.9–1.0, P = 0.01), and presence of a container cover (OR = 0.6, 95% CI: 0.4–0.8, P = 0.01) (Supplementary file 1: Table S2).

In multilevel analysis, habitat positivity for Anopheles immatures was also significantly associated with several environmental and anthropogenic factors. These included the fabric of the anthropogenic habitat (OR = 0.9, 95% CI: 0.9–1.0, P = 0.04), purpose of water storage (OR = 0.8, 95% CI: 0.7–0.9, P < 0.01), presence of a container cover (OR = 0.7, 95% CI: 0.5–0.9, P = 0.02), aquatic vegetation (OR = 3.2, 95% CI: 1.5–6.6, P < 0.01), and breeding habitat size (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.9–1.0, P < 0.01) (Table 3).

With respect to the container substrate, habitats composed of earth (OR = 0.6, 95% CI: 0.3–0.9, P = 0.03) and plastic (OR = 0.4, 95% CI: 0.3–0.6, P < 0.01) were less likely to harbour Anopheles immatures than concrete substrates. Seasonality of larval breeding habitat positivity

Anopheles stephensi and An. arabiensis immatures were detected across all collection months. However, the habitat positivity of An. stephensi immatures appeared to be less seasonal than that of An. arabiensis. Statistical testing indicated that this seasonal variation in An. stephensi habitat positivity was not significant (likelihood ratio, LR = 13.06, P = 0.11). In contrast, the habitat positivity of An. arabiensis showed significant seasonal variation (LR = 46.96, P < 0.01). The proportion of habitats positive for An. arabiensis declined between December 2023 and February 2024 before increasing again in April 2024. The observed seasonal peaks in An. arabiensis habitat positivity were closely aligned with patterns of precipitation and relative humidity in the study area, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. The trend in confirmed monthly malaria cases also varied in relation to the habitat positivity of An. arabiensis (Fig. 3B).

Adult Anopheles species composition

The Anopheles mosquito diversity in Metehara town indicates a vector landscape composed of five species: An. arabiensis, An. pharoensis, An. stephensi, An. funestus and An. brumpti. Among the adult Anopheles collected (n = 2078), An. arabiensis (63.7%, n = 1323) was the most abundant species, followed by An. pharoensis (26.4%, n = 549) and An. stephensi (8.3%, n = 173). An. funestus (n = 7) and An. brumpti (n = 1) accounted for 0.4% of the total catches. Most An. arabiensis were collected via CDC LT (63.3%, 95% CI: 60.7–65.9%) compared with those collected via BG pro traps (28.5%, 95% CI: 26.1–30.9%) and Prokopack aspirators (8.2%, 95% CI: 6.8–9.7%). Whereas An. pharoensis was more predominant in the BG pro traps (57.2%, 95% CI: 53.1–61.3%) than in the CDC LT traps (40.4%, 95% CI: 36.3–44.5%). The majority of An. stephensi was collected via Prokopack aspirators (76.9%, 95% CI: 70.6–83.2%) compared with BG pro traps (16.8%, 95% CI: 11.2–22.3%) and CDC LT traps (6.4%, 95% CI: 2.7–10.0%). In general, CDC LTs collect more Anopheles mosquitoes, with a mean catch rate of 2.17 per trap per night. However, Prokopack aspirators outperformed CDC LTs in the collection of An. stephensi (0.27 mean catch per collection attempt) (Table 6).

Blood-meal sources and Plasmodium sporozoite rates

The blood-meal origin of An. arabiensis from cows (Bovine Blood Index (BBI) = 23.4%, n = 71) and humans (human blood meal index (HBI) = 21.8%, n = 66) were comparable. However, cows are the common blood-meal origin for An. pharoensis (BBI = 44.6%, n = 54) and An. stephensi (BBI = 58.5%, n = 31). The human blood-meal index (HBI) was greater in An. arabiensis (HBI = 21.8%, n = 66) compared with An. pharoensis (HBI = 8.3%, n = 10) and An. stephensi (HBI = 1.9%, n = 1) (Table 7). Among the tested Anopheles species, Plasmodium sporozoite DNA was detected in An. arabiensis and An. pharoensis. Plasmodium sporozoite rate was higher in An. pharoensis [4.2%, 23/548 (9 P. falciparum, 13 P. vivax, and 1 mixed infection)] than in An. arabiensis [0.4%, 5/1321 (3 P. vivax and 2 mixed infections)] (χ2(1) = 38.3, P < 0.01). None of the tested An. stephensi were positive for Plasmodium sporozoites (Table 7).

Discussion

This in-depth entomological study uniquely characterised the ecology of urban malaria vector breeding habitats in Ethiopia in the context of both invasive An. stephensi and native vectors. The bioecological traits of Anopheles species were characterised via a year-round bimonthly survey in parallel with an extensive case-control study (manuscript in preparation) to determine the relative contribution of malaria vectors to local transmission. As expected for an urban setting (Thomas et al., 2016; Ashine et al., 2020; Hessou-Djossou et al., 2022; WHO, 2022; Yared et al., 2023; Ndiaye et al., 2024), the predominant immature mosquito habitats were water storage containers, mostly to compensate for local shortages. The main breeding habitats were water storage drums, locally made water reservoirs, deep wells/pits, and discarded vehicle tyres, which were predominantly found inside compounds. As observed in other studies (Ashine et al., 2020), the anthropogenic larval habitat positivity for An. stephensi immatures was high and without clear seasonality, unlike the native An. arabiensis. However, a modelling study indicated a pronounced seasonal dynamics of An. stephensi (Whittaker et al., 2023), which is poorly predicted by rainfall patterns, unlike native African malaria vectors. Interestingly, the native vector An. arabiensis was seemingly well adapted to the urban anthropogenic breeding environment, cooccurring with An. stephensi in one-third of the positive habitats, with varying proportions across seasons. There have been previous reports on the occurrence of native vectors in anthropogenic habitats in African urban settings (Azrag and Mohammed, 2018; Hessou-Djossou et al., 2022; Ndiaye et al., 2024; Machani et al., 2024); however, the present study uniquely characterised the co-occurrence of the invasive An. stephensi with the major malaria vector in Ethiopia, An. arabiensis, providing the opportunity to target and control multiple species.

As noted earlier, urban malaria is becoming more prevalent across Ethiopia (Molla et al., 2021; Teka et al., 2023), although little is known about the ecology, species composition and relative contribution of Anopheles vectors in local malaria transmission. Poor sanitation, open water storage practices, and a lack of proper water drainage systems are associated with an increased risk of malaria transmission and burden in urban areas (De Silva and Marshall, 2012; Duguma et al., 2022; Hassen and Dinka, 2022; WHO, 2022). Our study in Metehara revealed that the An. stephensi co-occurrence with native vectors and the preference for breeding in abandoned water storage containers with aquatic vegetation are noteworthy to guide more tailored interventions.

Our observations underscore the need for improved environmental and water management practices, including removing non-active water containers and making water storage mosquito-proof through proper coverings (WHO, 2022; Kahamba et al., 2024). Since many larval habitats serve as water reservoirs to overcome water shortages, the provision of reliable piped water could help reduce the incidence of mosquito-borne diseases, including malaria (WHO, 2022). The evidence also supports the use of larviciding to complement LLINs in urban areas, including in the presence of insecticide resistance (Maheu-Giroux and Castro, 2014; Antonio-Nkondjio et al., 2021; Teshome et al., 2023; Sigei et al., 2024).

In adult collections, An. arabiensis was the dominant species, followed by An. pharoensis and An. stephensi, similar to our previous work (Ashine et al., 2024b). However, the present study revealed a relatively high sporozoite rate of both P. falciparum and P. vivax parasites in An. pharoensis than An. arabiensis. This contradicts existing evidence (Aschale et al., 2023; Ashine et al., 2024b; Hawaria et al., 2025), where higher sporozoite rates were recorded in An. arabiensis. This variation may be due to unexplored behavioural adaptations of the vector to urban areas or indicative of the changing patterns of the relative contribution to malaria transmission (Msugupakulya et al., 2023). Plasmodium parasites were not detected in An. stephensi in the present study, which is likely related to the low number of adults caught and tested for Plasmodium sporozoites, the relatively zoophagic behaviour of this species and/or relatively less permissiveness than the native vectors. However, a previous study using membrane feedings assays in Ethiopia concluded that An. stephensi is a permissive vector for both P. falciparum and P. vivax, similar to An. arabiensis (Tadesse et al., 2021) or a competent vector under natural conditions, with P. vivax sporozoite rates ranging from 0.3 to 0.5% (Balkew et al., 2021) and 0.5% for P. falciparum sporozoite (Emiru et al., 2023).

The present study highlights that malaria transmission in urban settings could be driven by native vectors, strengthening the reports that indicate An. gambiae complex as the driver of urban malaria in sub-Saharan Africa (Krafsur, 1977; Doumbe-Belisse et al., 2021). This is in line with increasing evidence on the adaptation of native malaria vectors of Africa, An. gambiae (s.l.), An. coluzzi, An. arabiensis and An. funestus, to urban environments (Doumbe-Belisse et al., 2018, 2021; Azrag and Mohammed, 2018), which is accompanied by increased tendency to develop insecticide resistance (Machani et al., 2024). However, the assessment of larval co-occurrence in this study was restricted to An. stephensi and An. arabiensis, as specific molecular markers for other native malaria vectors, particularly for An. pharoensis, were not available. Moreover, pooled larval samples from 29 breeding habitats tested negative for both species. Given that An. pharoensis was the second most abundant species with the highest Plasmodium sporozoite rate in adult collections in the present study, a clearer understanding of its larval ecology is likely to be important. Moreover, species identification of pooled larval samples was conducted using species-specific PCR, which should be taken into account when comparing the results with studies that relied on morphological identification keys. Further systematic investigations, measuring temperature at multiple time-points, are therefore warranted to elucidate the larval ecology of An. pharoensis and other malaria vector species, thereby enhancing knowledge of the malaria vector ecosystem in urban settings and providing an evidence base for tailored and integrated vector control strategies.

Conclusions

In Metehara, anthropogenic water storage containers were the primary breeding habitats for Anopheles mosquitoes. Both An. stephensi and native vector species co-occurred in these habitats, though An. stephensi positivity was less seasonal. Habitat features such as accessibility for oviposition, presence of aquatic vegetation and abandoned containers were associated with higher Anopheles immature positivity. While An. arabiensis dominated adult collections, An. pharoensis exhibited higher Plasmodium infection rates. These findings underscore the need for multisectoral action to enhance access to safe water and improve water storage practices. Strengthening environmental and larval source management, including targeted larviciding, can help to reduce habitat productivity and mosquito density, thereby mitigating the risk of vector-borne diseases including urban malaria.
