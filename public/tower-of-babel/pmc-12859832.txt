Global burden and cross-country inequalities in rabies from 1990 to 2021: a systemic analysis of the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Qin-Yan Zuo, Wen-Wen Lv, Yu Qin, Jian Yang, Yun-Fei Zhang, Guo-Bing Yang, Can-Jun Zheng, Mai-Geng Zhou, Ji-Chun Wang, Shun-Xian Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRabies is a nearly 100% fatal, vaccine-preventable disease. This study provides a comprehensive global analysis of rabies burden from 1990 to 2021, highlighting health inequality, and aims to inform resource allocation and reduce the overall burden.MethodsThe study systematically evaluated the global, regional, and national burden of rabies and its trends from 1990 to 2021 using data from the GBD 2021 database. The Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model was applied to project future disease burden trends, including age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), mortality rate (ASMR), and disability-adjusted life-years rate (ASDR). Frontier analysis was conducted to estimate achievable outcomes across different development levels, and decomposition analysis was employed to identify the driving factors behind changes in disease burden.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, the global burden of rabies showed a significant decline across multiple indicators. Specifically, the ASIR decreased from 0.42 per 100,000 population [95% uncertainty interval (UI): 0.30, 0.55] in 1990 to 0.13 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 0.08, 0.18) in 2021, reflecting a 69.38% reduction [95% UI: −77.61, −61.03; estimated annual percentage change (EAPC): −4.06% (95% confidence interval, CI: −4.32, −3.80)]. Similar reductions were observed in the ASMR, and ASDR, which declined by 69.35% (95% UI: −77.62, −61.02), and 69.39% (95% UI: −79.35, −59.48), respectively. Frontier analysis showed that significant room for improvement in rabies prevention and control remains in several high-SDI countries and regions, such as the United Arab Emirates, Norway, the United States, Latvia, and Greenland, and many low SDI countries.ConclusionDespite progress in reducing rabies burden, low-SDI regions remain a priority. Key strategies include enhancing canine vaccination, post-exposure prophylaxis, stray dog control, and international collaboration. Future research should improve data quality and adapt interventions to local contexts to achieve the 2030 goal of eliminating dog-mediated human rabies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12475-0.

Background

Rabies is an acute and fatal zoonotic disease caused by the rabies virus [1, 2]. It is primarily transmitted through contact with the saliva of infected animals via scratches, bites, or direct exposure to mucous membranes [3, 4]. Dogs are responsible for up to 99% of human rabies cases. Early symptoms include fever, pain, and neuropathic manifestations. Without timely post-exposure prophylaxis, rabies is nearly universally fatal. The disease has a prolonged incubation period, typically ranging from 1 to 3 months, during which prophylactic vaccination is nearly 100% effective at preventing the onset of clinical disease [1, 2, 5].

According to estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO), rabies causes approximately US$8.6 billion in global losses annually, encompassing the loss of life, livelihoods, healthcare costs, and the immeasurable psychological burden. Rabies is present on all continents except Antarctica, with an estimated 59,000 deaths worldwide each year [6]. However, due to underreporting, the documented cases often differ from these estimates. Despite the availability of highly effective human rabies vaccines and immunoglobulins, access remains limited for those in need, often due to affordability [6, 7]. The average cost of post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is estimated at US$108, including travel expenses and lost income, posing a significant economic burden for individuals earning only US$1–2 per day. Globally, over 29 million people receive human rabies vaccines annually [6].

Studying the global burden of rabies is essential for understanding its epidemiology, health impact, and economic burden. As a nearly 100% fatal yet entirely preventable infectious disease, rabies remains a significant public health challenge in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in Asia and Africa [8, 9]. Although previous studies have examined the global burden of rabies [10, 11], they are often limited to specific time periods and regions, lacking comprehensive analyses of major rabies trends and associated health inequalities. In particular, studies utilizing the most recent data from the 2021 Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD 2021) remain scarce. GBD 2021 systematically analyzes and integrates global health and disease data, serving as a critical tool for assessing the global, regional, and national burden of diseases and injuries [12–15].

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the burden of rabies. Health inequalities between countries and regions are evaluated using the slope index of inequality (SII) and concentration index of Inequality (CCI) [16, 17]. Frontier analysis estimates optimal health outcomes based on the sociodemographic index (SDI), while decomposition analysis identifies the main contributors to changes in disease burden [16, 17]. These analyses provide essential evidence for evidence-based rabies control strategies, supporting vaccination, dog population management, and post-exposure interventions to meet the WHO’s 2030 goal of zero human rabies deaths.

Methods

Date source

The GBD 2021 project provides a comprehensive assessment of the global burden of 371 diseases and injuries, including rabies, as well as 88 risk factors. This analysis spans 204 countries and territories, 21 geographical regions, five Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) levels, and several super-regions, based on data derived from 100,983 disease-related resources [12–15]. The analysis systematically adjusted epidemiological data to account for biases arising from variations in data sources, definitions, and measurement methods. These adjustments were implemented through sophisticated statistical models, including Bayesian priors, regularization, and trimming using the stands for meta-regression with Bayesian priors, regularization, and trimming (MR-BRT) framework, as well as DisMod-MR 2.1 (a Bayesian mixed-effects meta-regression modeling tool developed for GBD analyses), ensuring internal consistency across estimates for different regions, age groups, sexes, and time periods. The process employed standardization and calibration steps to minimize the impact of heterogeneity on study outcomes [13, 14]. Mortality data in GBD 2021 primarily originated from national vital registration systems, maternal and child health surveillance networks, and census data. Incidence and prevalence data were derived from disease surveillance systems, national health surveys, and published studies [12–14]. Disability burden estimates were based on data from case notifications, hospital discharge records, household surveys, and cohort studies [12, 13]. Detailed information on study design, data collection, and estimation methodologies is provided in the GBD 2021 documentation [12–14].

The data on rabies were extracted from the GBD 2021 using the GBD results tool on the IHME website (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/), The dataset includes information on rabies for incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs cases, alongside age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), mortality (ASMR), and disability-adjusted life-years rate (DALY) rate (ASDR). including global, regional, and national levels, classified by sex (both sexes, male, and female) and age groups (all ages, five-year intervals, and specific age categories [12–15]. For rabies, the relevant classification code in the WHO’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) is A82 [13].

The SDI is a composite measure used to assess the level of social and demographic development of countries or regions. Developed by the GBD project, the SDI is widely applied in analyzing and comparing health and social development across diverse settings. Countries and territories were classified into five development levels based on SDI thresholds (low: 0–0.4658, low-middle: 0.4658–0.6188, middle: 0.6188–0.7120, high-middle: 0.7120–0.8103, high: 0.8103–1.0000), enabling systematic analysis of the impact of socioeconomic development on health outcomes [12, 13].

Statistical analysis

The disease burden of rabies was assessed using rates and total case counts for incidence, mortality, and DALYs. Rate was expressed as estimates per 100,000 population, reflect the relative burden, while case counts represent the absolute burden. Both metrics are reported with 95% uncertainty intervals (UI, 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of all draws) [10, 16–18].

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; available at https://cran.r-project.org). Detailed descriptions of specific analytical methods, including BAPC models, the SII and CCI of inequality analysis, frontier analysis, and decomposition analysis, will be provided in subsequent sections [10, 16–18].

Percentage change

The percentage change (PC) quantifies the changes in rabies incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs, as well as in ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR, by comparing 2021 values to those of 1990. The calculation for PC is as follows [10, 19, 20]:

PC= (value 2021 −value 1990)/value 2021 x 100%. When the lower bound of the 95% UI for the PC is greater than 0, it indicates an increasing trend. Conversely, if the upper bound of the 95% UI is less than 0, it indicates a decreasing trend [19, 21].

BAPC

The model applies second-order random walk priors to smooth temporal variations in rabies-specific age-standardized rates, effectively capturing underlying patterns while reducing noise from sparse or heterogeneous data. Using Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation for efficient posterior inference, the method provides robust interval estimates for future rabies incidence and mortality without the computational limitations of simulation-based approaches. By generating age-stratified, time-specific projections of rabies metrics, this analysis delivers targeted insights into how changing demographic structures and evolving population risks may influence rabies burden over the coming decade, thereby supporting evidence-based planning for age-specific immunization and surveillance strategies [21, 27]. Inequality analysis

This study employs two indices, the SII and the CCI, to analyze inequality in rabies ASDR. Specifically, the SII is calculated through robust regression of rabies ASDR against SDI, with the independent variable being the midpoint value of the cumulative population distribution sorted by SDI. This approach aims to capture the absolute differences in ASDR across SDI gradients. In contrast, the CCI is calculated based on the relationship between the cumulative population percentage and the corresponding rabies ASDR percentage, with the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of perfect equality determined through numerical integration [16, 21]. This index focuses on identifying whether the disease burden is concentrated in regions with low socio-economic status.

Frontier analysis

To quantitatively assess the potential for reducing rabies burden across different socioeconomic development levels, this study employed frontier analysis to model the theoretically achievable minimum ASDR for rabies relative to SDI. Unlike conventional regression approaches, this method specifically identifies the optimal performance frontier—representing the lowest possible ASDR attainable at each SDI level—based on the best-performing countries or regions. Using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) with multiple smoothing parameters (0.3, 0.4, 0.5) and local polynomial fitting, we constructed a smoothed frontier that captures the nonlinear relationship between SDI and rabies ASDR [16, 21]. The robustness of the frontier was ensured through 1,000 bootstrap iterations. By calculating the absolute distance between each country’s observed ASDR in 2021 and the corresponding frontier value, we quantified the improvement gap in rabies mortality. This approach directly addresses the study’s aim of identifying health-system performance shortfalls and prioritizing efforts to reduce rabies-specific ASDR in underachieving populations [16, 21].

Decomposition analysis

To elucidate the specific drivers behind the temporal changes in global rabies incidence cases from 1990 to 2021, this study applied the Das Gupta decomposition method. This analytical approach quantitatively disaggregates the total change in rabies case numbers into the distinct contributions of three factors: population aging, population growth, and epidemiological change. Unlike associative statistical models, this method allows for the precise quantification of the absolute contribution and proportional share of each factor to the observed trend in rabies incidence. By isolating the independent effect of epidemiological changes—reflecting shifts in rabies transmission dynamics and control efforts—from purely demographic influences, the analysis provides critical, targeted evidence. The findings directly identify whether the observed fluctuations in rabies incidence over the past three decades were primarily driven by changes in population structure or by genuine shifts in the disease’s epidemiological landscape, thereby offering an empirical basis for prioritizing public health interventions [16, 21].

Five SDI

In 2021, the ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR for rabies were highest in low-SDI regions and lowest in high-SDI regions. Between 1990 and 2021, these ASR remained relatively stable in high-SDI regions, while significant declines were observed in the other four SDI categories (Tables 1 - 3). In contrast, the absolute numbers of rabies cases, deaths, and DALYs increased in high-SDI regions but showed substantial decreases across the other four SDI regions (Supplementary Tables S1–3).

Geographical regions

In 2021, the ASIR of rabies was highest in Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa at 0.50 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 0.20, 1.13). Compared to 1990, the ASIR increased in High-income North America and Western Europe by 2021, remained stable in Oceania, Australasia, Southern Latin America, and the Caribbean, and declined in the other 15 regions (Table 1).

Similarly, in 2021, the ASMR of rabies was highest in Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, reaching 0.49 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 0.19, 1.13). Between 1990 and 2021, ASMR increased in High-income North America and Western Europe, showed no significant changes in Oceania, Australasia, Southern Latin America, the Caribbean, and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, but declined in the remaining 14 regions (Table 2).

In 2021, the ASDR of rabies was also highest in Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa at 26.67 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 10.27, 59.74). Compared to 1990, the ASDR increased in Australasia, High-income North America, and Western Europe by 2021, remained stable in Oceania, the Caribbean, and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, and decreased in the other 15 regions (Table 3).

Countries and territories

In 2021, Nepal reported the highest ASIR of rabies, at 1.71 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 0.92, 2.65) (Supplementary Fig S1A). From 1990 to 2021, the ASIR increased in 26 countries, with the largest increase observed in Somalia (AAPC = 0.002, 95% CI: 0.001, 0.003). ASIR remained stable in 16 countries and significantly declined in 162 countries or territories, with Nepal showing the largest decrease (AAPC = −0.149, 95% CI: −0.150, 0.147) (Supplementary Table S4).

In terms of the ASMR, Nepal reported the highest rate in 2021, at 1.69 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 0.91, 2.62) (Supplementary Fig S1B). Between 1990 and 2021, ASMR increased in 13 countries, with the greatest rise in Somalia (AAPC = 0.002, 95% CI: 0.002, 0.003). ASMR remained stable in 27 countries and significantly decreased in 164 countries or territories, with Nepal again exhibiting the largest decline (AAPC = −0.147, 95% CI: −0.149, −0.144) (Supplementary Table S4).

The ASDR of rabies in 2021 was also highest in Nepal, at 81.66 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 45.50, 129.56) (Supplementary Fig S1C). Over the period 1990–2021, the ASDR increased in 15 countries, with the largest rise observed in South Sudan (AAPC = 0.077, 95% CI: 0.041, 0.114). The ASDR remained stable in 34 countries and significantly declined in 155 countries or territories, with Nepal showing the most substantial reduction (AAPC = −7.692, 95% CI: −7.779, −7.605) (Supplementary Table S4).

Age-gender group

In 2021, globally, there were no significant differences in the ASIR, ASMR, or ASDR of rabies between males and females across all age groups. Similarly, the absolute numbers of cases, including incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs, showed no variation between males and females across different age groups(Supplementary Fig S2 A-C). Notably, the ASDR and DALY cases were higher in the age groups under 5 years, 5–9 years, and 10–14 years, while they were comparatively lower in all age groups above 60 years(Supplementary Fig S2 A-C).

Discussion

This study systematically analyzed global and regional trends in the burden of rabies from 1990 to 2021 and projected future trends. The findings revealed that, although ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR of rabies globally showed significant declines exceeding 69%, the disease burden remains disproportionately high in low SDI regions, particularly in Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa. In contrast, certain metrics have increased in high-income regions, such as North America and Western Europe. Furthermore, the study highlighted that the burden of rabies is particularly pronounced among children and adolescents, underscoring the need for future prevention and control strategies to prioritize high-risk populations and regions.

In countries with low socioeconomic development, poor management of dog populations, a high number of stray dogs, and low vaccination coverage make it difficult to effectively control the spread of the rabies virus between animals and humans. Additionally, limited medical resources and the high cost or inaccessibility of post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) prevent many exposed individuals from receiving timely treatment. Public awareness of rabies risks is also low, leading to inadequate wound care and prevention after exposure. In low-income, agriculture-based countries, frequent contact between humans, livestock, and wildlife further increases the risk of infection [8, 31, 32]. In addition, although the number of rabies cases is low in high-income countries, imported cases remain a significant risk, especially in regions with frequent international trade and travel. Wildlife species such as bats and raccoons, which act as rabies reservoirs, continue to pose a threat to public health. Despite advanced healthcare systems, the high cost of rabies treatment significantly contributes to the disease burden [8, 31, 32]. Furthermore, these countries often have well-developed data collection and reporting systems, it allow for more comprehensive tracking and representation of the actual rabies burden.

The study underscores the urgent need for targeted policy interventions to address the disproportionate burden of rabies in low SDI regions, particularly Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa. The findings highlight the critical role of enhancing rabies vaccination programs, not only for humans but also for dogs, to interrupt transmission at its source. This calls for a strategic approach to resource allocation that prioritizes high-burden regions and supports the expansion of both vaccination and PEP services [32–35]. At the national level, governments should strengthen regulatory frameworks for dog population management, promote universal dog vaccination, and improve public awareness campaigns, especially in rural areas where human-animal interactions are frequent [36]. At the international level, our results suggest the need for enhanced collaboration between high- and low-income countries to ensure equitable access to rabies vaccines and diagnostics, particularly in resource-poor settings. Furthermore, the development of cost-effective and accessible vaccines should be a priority for global health initiatives, as this would reduce financial barriers to timely treatment and prevent further economic and social impacts. Finally, integrating comprehensive surveillance systems into national health frameworks is essential to guide informed decision-making, track progress, and optimize the allocation of resources to control and eventually eliminate dog-mediated human rabies by 2030 [32, 37, 38].

This study has several limitations. First, the accuracy of disease burden estimates in the GBD 2021 framework depends on data quality and availability. Despite the use of advanced statistical models such as DisMod-MR 2.1 to address heterogeneity, disparities in data coverage, reporting mechanisms, classification standards, and surveillance quality persist across countries. Underreporting remains a challenge in low- and middle income countries owing to resource constraints, while developed nations with comprehensive surveillance may report lower burdens, introducing potential biases in global estimates [13, 15]. Second, the GBD 2021 database is based on modeled estimates rather than directly observed data, which may result in overestimation or underestimation of the disease burden [13, 15, 39]. Third, the predictive models used in this study, such as the BAPC model, heavily depend on the quality of available data and historical trends. However, factors such as changes in human-dog interactions, variations in canine infection rates, and fluctuations in dog populations significantly influence the occurrence and spread of rabies. Since these variables are not accounted for in the GBD 2021 modeling framework, the projections may not fully capture or accurately reflect future trends in rabies dynamics.

Conclusions

Over the past three decades, the global burden of rabies has markedly declined, though significant disparities remain across regions. Low-SDI areas, especially Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, continue to experience the highest burden, while some high-income regions, such as North America and Western Europe, have seen an increase in certain rabies indicators. Effective control in high-burden regions requires a comprehensive, integrated strategy, with large-scale canine vaccination as the foundation. This should be supplemented by strengthened stray dog control, improved animal management, and mandatory registration and vaccination of dogs. Such measures are critical to reducing rabies globally and advancing public health goals.
