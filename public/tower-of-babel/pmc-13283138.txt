Prognosis of patients with hormone receptor-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative, 1–3 node-positive breast cancer meeting the high-risk criteria of the monarchE trial: a comparison between macrometastasis and micrometastasis
Authors: Takeshi Murata, Masayuki Yoshida, Chikashi Watase, Hinako Maeda, Ayako Nakashoji, Ayumi Ogawa, Natsuko Ohgi, Mengge Chen, Sayako Tsubota, Akiko Maeshima, Eriko Iwamoto, Shin Takayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe monarchE trial enrolled patients with hormone receptor (HR)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative, node-positive, high-risk early breast cancer (EBC). Although both macrometastasis and micrometastasis were eligible, the number of patients with only micrometastasis was not reported, leaving their prognosis uncertain. This study compared outcomes of patients with 1–3 positive axillary lymph nodes (pALNs), including those with only micrometastases and those with macrometastases, according to monarchE high-risk criteria.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 844 patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative/node-positive EBC from a prospectively maintained institutional database and classified them into four groups: Group 1, 1–3 pALNs (including macrometastasis) with grade 3 tumors or tumors ≥ 5 cm; Group 2, 1–3 pALNs (only micrometastasis) with grade 3 tumors or tumors ≥ 5 cm; Group 3, 1–3 pALNs (including macrometastasis) with grade < 3 tumors and tumors < 5 cm; and Group 4, 1–3 pALNs (only micrometastasis) with grade < 3 tumors and tumors < 5 cm. Invasive disease-free survival (IDFS) and distant relapse-free survival (DRFS) were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test. Univariable Cox models assessed associations between clinical factors and outcomes.ResultsGroup 2 showed outcomes comparable to Group 1 (5-year IDFS: 80.7% vs. 83.7%; DRFS: 89.5% vs. 87.9%). In univariate analysis, only tumor size—rather than Group 2 or histological grade—was prognostic for IDFS and DRFS. Sensitivity analyses restricted to patients undergoing axillary lymph node dissection yielded consistent results.ConclusionsAmong HR-positive/HER2-negative EBC, high-risk patients with only micrometastasis demonstrated prognoses similar to those with macrometastasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12282-026-01871-2.

Introduction

Hormone receptor (HR)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer is the most common subtype, accounting for approximately 70% of all breast cancers [1, 2]. While some patients with this subtype have a low risk of recurrence with adjuvant endocrine therapy alone, others have a high risk of recurrence even after chemotherapy, highlighting the need for new treatment strategies for these high-risk patients. Recently, the monarchE trial demonstrated the efficacy of 2 years of abemaciclib combined with adjuvant endocrine therapy in patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative, node-positive early breast cancer at high risk of recurrence, approving it as a new standard of treatment [3, 4]. In the monarchE trial, the criteria for high risk included patients with ≥ 4 positive axillary lymph nodes (ALNs) or 1–3 positive ALNs with histological grade (HG) 3 or tumor size ≥ 5 cm [3]. Patients with both macrometastases (> 2 mm) and micrometastases (< 2 mm) were eligible for enrollment; however, the number of patients with only micrometastases was not reported. Although patients with only micrometastases are generally considered to have a better prognosis than those with macrometastases [5, 6], no study has compared their prognoses based on the monarchE trial’s high-risk criteria.

The present study aimed to compare the prognosis of patients with 1–3 positive ALNs who met the monarchE high-risk criteria, with a particular focus on directly evaluating outcomes between those with micrometastasis only and those with macrometastasis. By concentrating on this high-risk population, the study sought to clarify whether micrometastatic nodal involvement confers a prognosis comparable to that of macrometastasis under the monarchE-defined high-risk conditions.

Patients and methods

A total of 884 patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer and 1–3 positive ALNs, diagnosed at the National Cancer Center Hospital between January 2004 and December 2022, were identified from a prospectively maintained institutional database and included in this retrospective analysis. Exclusion criteria were patients with ≥ 4 positive ALNs, ductal carcinoma in situ at primary breast cancer, stage IV disease at initial diagnosis, absence of ALN involvement, bilateral breast cancer (synchronous and metachronous), and inflammatory breast cancer. Patients with unknown receptor status, HG, nodal metastasis size, or those who received adjuvant cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK 4/6) inhibitor were also excluded. Clinical and pathological data were obtained from the institutional database, including age at initial diagnosis, sex, tumor size, nodal status, HG, histological type, menopausal status, estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) status, HER2 status, Ki-67 index, type of initial surgery, chemotherapy, postoperative radiotherapy, and endocrine therapy. ER and PR were considered positive when > 1% of tumor cells showed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining [7]. An HER2-negative result corresponded to an IHC score of 0 or 1 + or 2 + without amplification on fluorescence in situ hybridization [7–9]. Tumor, node, and metastasis (TNM) staging followed the 7th edition of the Union International Cancer Control staging system [10]. According to current American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) criteria [11], isolated tumor cells (< 0.2 mm) were classified as negative (pN0i+), while micrometastatic deposits of 0.2–2.0 mm were classified as positive (pN1mi). Pathologic nodal staging was determined by the number of metastatic ALNs as follows: pN0, none; pN1 (1–3 lymph node metastases), limited; and pN2–3 (> 3 lymph node metastases), advanced.

Definition of recurrence risk group

Considering the clinicopathological risk factors for recurrence used in the monarchE trial [12], patients were classified into four groups. The high-risk macrometastasis group (Group 1) included patients with 1–3 positive ALNs, including macrometastasis, and either grade 3 disease or tumor size ≥ 5 cm. The high-risk micrometastasis group (Group 2) included patients with 1–3 positive ALNs (only micrometastasis) and either grade 3 disease or tumor size ≥ 5 cm. The low-risk macrometastasis group (Group 3) included patients with 1–3 positive ALNs, including macrometastasis, grade < 3, and tumor size < 5 cm. The low-risk micrometastasis group (Group 4) included patients with 1–3 positive ALNs (only micrometastasis), grade < 3, and tumor size < 5 cm. The Ki-67 index was evaluated using the same method used in the MonarchE trial, measured in all untreated primary breast tumor samples with the Ki-67 IHC assay developed by Agilent Technologies (formerly Dako; Santa Clara, CA).

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the National Cancer Center Hospital Review Board and Ethics Committee (approval no. 2017 − 278). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test or the Chi-squared test, as appropriate. Invasive disease-free survival (IDFS) and distant relapse-free survival (DRFS) among the four groups were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival estimates were compared using the log-rank test. IDFS was defined as the time from the date of initial surgery to the date of first occurrence of ipsilateral invasive breast tumor recurrence, local/regional invasive breast cancer recurrence, distant recurrence, contralateral invasive breast cancer, second primary non-breast neoplasm, or death from any cause. DRFS was defined as the time from the date of initial surgery to the date of distant recurrence or death from any cause, whichever occurred first.

As a sensitivity analysis, we performed univariable Cox proportional hazards models to examine the association between each key baseline factor and both IDFS and DRFS. These analyses were restricted to the high-risk population (Group 1 and Group 2), which represents the primary focus of this study. Specifically, we evaluated tumor size and HG, as these two factors are well-established prognostic indicators. Given the limited number of events and the highly skewed distribution of HG, multivariable adjustment was not attempted to avoid model overfitting.

In addition, because axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) is more frequently omitted in patients with micrometastasis in general, raising the possibility of axillary understaging, we conducted a second sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who underwent ALND and therefore had accurate axillary staging. Within this ALND-only subset, tumor size and HG were again evaluated using univariable Cox proportional hazards models for both IDFS and DRFS, to determine whether the prognostic comparison between high-risk micrometastasis and macrometastasis remained consistent when staging accuracy was ensured.

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA SE version 16 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX). P < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 884 patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative, node-positive breast cancer were included in this study. Clinicopathological findings of all patients are shown in Table 1. Among the 884 patients, 183 patients (20.7%) were in Group 1, 66 (7.5%) in Group 2, 407 (46.0%) in Group 3, and 228 (25.8%) in Group 4. When comparing Group 1 and Group 2, the frequencies of one positive node (54% vs. 89%) and HG 3 (81% vs. 95%) were higher in Group 2. The proportions of tumor size ≥ 5 cm (24% vs. 6%), total mastectomy (64% vs. 44%), ALND (98% vs. 62%), and chemotherapy (84% vs. 59%) were higher in Group 1. No substantive differences were observed in the rates of radiotherapy and endocrine therapy between the two groups.

IDFS

Kaplan–Meier survival estimates of IDFS are shown in Fig. 1. Patients with high risk (Groups 1 and 2) had significantly worse IDFS than those with low risk (Groups 3 and 4) (p = 0.0002; 5-year IDFS: 82.9% vs. 92.7%; Fig. 1a). In contrast, IDFS did not differ significantly between Groups 1 and 2 (p = 0.3201; 5-year IDFS: 83.7% vs. 80.7%; Fig. 1c). Similarly, sensitivity analysis using Cox proportional hazard models revealed no significant difference in IDFS between Group 2 and Group 1 (hazard ratio 1.34, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.75–2.39, p = 0.322). For tumor size, pT2 compared with pT1 was significantly associated with worse IDFS (hazard ratio 3.75, 95% CI 1.46–9.60, p = 0.006), and pT3 compared with pT1 also showed a significant association (hazard ratio 3.74, 95% CI 1.35–10.4, p = 0.011). For HG, the hazard ratio for HG3 compared with HG1/2 was 1.31 (95% CI 0.59–2.90, p = 0.509) (Table 3a).

Patients’ characteristics and recurrent events among those who received ALND are shown in Online Resource Tables 1 and 2. In the sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who underwent ALND, IDFS again showed no meaningful difference between Groups 1 and 2 (p = 0.371; 5-year IDFS: 84.0% vs. 82.6%; Online Resource Fig. 1c). Consistent with this pattern, Cox proportional hazard analysis similarly revealed no significant difference in IDFS between Group 2 and Group 1 (hazard ratio 1.35, 95% CI 0.70–2.61, p = 0.373). For tumor size, pT3 compared with pT1 was significantly associated with worse IDFS (hazard ratio 3.27, 95% CI 1.17–9.17, p = 0.024), and pT2 compared with pT1 also showed a significant association (hazard ratio 3.20, 95% CI 1.24–8.29, p = 0.017). For HG, the hazard ratio for HG3 compared with HG1/2 was 1.21 (95% CI 0.54–2.71, p = 0.643) (Online Resource Table 3a). When the analysis was limited to patients who received chemotherapy, the absence of a significant difference in IDFS between Groups 1 and 2 persisted (p = 0.2149; 5-year IDFS: 85.7% vs. 74.5%; Online Resource Fig. 3a). No group differences were observed in the proportion of patients who received radiotherapy or endocrine therapy, or in the duration of endocrine therapy (Online Resource Table 1). Additional IDFS comparisons between the groups are presented in Fig. 1 and Online Resource Fig. 1.

DRFS

Kaplan–Meier survival estimates of DRFS are shown in Fig. 2. Patients with high risk (Groups 1 and 2) had a significantly worse DRFS than patients with low risk (Groups 3 and 4) (p = 0.0001; 5-year DRFS: 88.4% vs. 96.6%; Fig. 2a). By contrast, DRFS was comparable between Groups 1 and 2 (p = 0.8441; 5-year DRFS: 87.9% vs. 89.5%; Fig. 2c). Likewise, sensitivity analysis using Cox proportional hazard analysis revealed no significant difference in DRFS between Group 2 and Group 1 (hazard ratio 0.93, 95% CI 0.43–1.98, p = 0.844). For tumor size, pT2 compared with pT1 showed a significant association (hazard ratio 2.58, 95% CI 1.26–14.0, p = 0.019), and pT3 compared with pT1 also showed a significant association (hazard ratio 3.88, 95% CI 1.05–14.3, p = 0.042). For HG, the hazard ratio for HG3 compared with HG1/2 was 1.17 (95% CI 0.46–3.03, p = 0.740) (Table 3b).

Discussion

This study investigated the prognosis of patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer with 1–3 positive lymph nodes, based on a combination of the monarchE trial high-risk criteria and lymph node metastasis size. The results showed that patients with 1–3 positive ALNs (only micrometastasis) and either grade 3 tumors or tumors ≥ 5 cm had similar IDFS and DRFS compared with patients with 1–3 positive ALNs (including macrometastasis) and the same features. These trends in IDFS were similar when the analyses were restricted to patients who underwent ALND and patients who received chemotherapy.

The sensitivity analyses restricted to the high-risk population (Group 1 and Group 2) demonstrated that tumor size was a strong and consistent prognostic factor for both IDFS and DRFS. pT2 and pT3 tumors showed significantly worse outcomes than pT1 across both endpoints. In contrast, HG showed an unfavorable but statistically non-significant trend for both IDFS and DRFS. This lack of statistical significance is likely attributable to the limited number of events and the markedly skewed distribution of grade in this cohort, with approximately 90% of patients classified as grade 3, resulting in insufficient contrast to reliably estimate its effect. Despite these imbalances—smaller tumors but higher HG in Group 2—the hazard ratios comparing Group 2 with Group 1 were modest and non-significant for both IDFS (hazard ratio 1.34) and DRFS (hazard ratio 0.93) (Table 3). The similar 5-year outcomes between the two groups may therefore reflect a balance between opposing prognostic factors: the smaller tumor size in Group 2 (favorable) may have partially offset the potential negative impact of higher HG (unfavorable). The consistency of this pattern across both IDFS and DRFS strengthens the interpretation that the overall prognosis of high-risk micrometastasis is comparable to that of high-risk macrometastasis within this cohort. These findings suggest that the prognostic implications of high-risk micrometastasis cannot be fully understood by nodal status alone and should be interpreted in the context of tumor size and HG. Given the limited number of events and the restricted variability in grade, the results should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

In addition to these analyses, a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who underwent ALND was performed to address the possibility of axillary understaging in the micrometastasis group. In this ALND-only subset, no significant difference was observed between Group 2 and Group 1 for either IDFS (hazard ratio 1.35) or DRFS (hazard ratio 1.01) (Online Resource Table 3), consistent with the findings from the overall high-risk population. The associations observed for tumor size were also similar: pT3 and pT2 remained significantly associated with IDFS, and for DRFS, pT2 showed a significant association while pT3 demonstrated a trend toward association. HG again showed no statistically significant associations for either endpoint. The alignment of results between the primary analyses and the ALND-restricted analyses indicates that the absence of significant differences between Group 2 and Group 1 was not attributable to potential inaccuracies in axillary staging. This consistency further supports the interpretation that the prognosis of high-risk micrometastasis is comparable to that of high-risk macrometastasis within this study population.

To our knowledge, no previous reports have examined the prognosis of patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative early-stage breast cancer who had only micrometastases and met the high-risk criteria of the monarchE trial. Generally, patients with breast cancer with only micrometastases have a better prognosis than those with macrometastases [5], with reported 5-year DFS rates ranging from 75.9 to 98.2% [13–16]. In the present study, the 5-year IDFS for patients with only micrometastases (Groups 2 and 4) was 90.0% (Fig. 1b), consistent with previous reports. The novelty of our study is that by dividing patients with only micrometastases into high-risk (Group 2) and low-risk (Group 4) groups, we found that the high-risk group had significantly worse IDFS than the low-risk group (p = 0.0011; 5-year IDFS: 80.7% vs. 93.1%; Fig. 1h). Furthermore, the high-risk micrometastasis group had significantly worse IDFS than the low-risk macrometastasis group (p = 0.0014; 5-year IDFS: 80.7% vs. 92.4%; Fig. 1e), but similar IDFS to the high-risk macrometastasis group (p = 0.321; 5-year IDFS: 80.7% vs. 83.7%; Fig. 1c). Emily et al. [17] reported that among patients with breast cancer with only micrometastasis, those with HG 3 tumors had poorer outcomes than those with HG 1 or 2 disease. In our high-risk micrometastasis group, 63 (95.5%) patients had grade 3 tumors, consistent with that study.

In our study, treatment differences were observed between the high-risk macrometastasis and high-risk micrometastasis groups, particularly in the proportion of patients who received chemotherapy (Table 1). Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that differences in chemotherapy administration may have influenced the outcomes. To explore this issue, we conducted an analysis limited to patients who received chemotherapy (Online Resource Table 4). In this subgroup, no significant difference in IDFS was observed between the high-risk macrometastasis and high-risk micrometastasis groups (Online Resource Fig. 3a), and a similar pattern was seen for DRFS (Online Resource Fig. 3c). These findings indicate that, among patients who received chemotherapy, no clear evidence of worse outcomes was observed in the high-risk micrometastasis group. However, because baseline characteristics still differed between the groups, these results do not establish that the high-risk micrometastasis group received fully adequate treatment or that treatment effects were equivalent. Rather, the findings should be interpreted as showing that, within the treated subgroup, no significant difference in prognosis was detected between the two groups. Overall, patients with high-risk micrometastasis demonstrated outcomes similar to those with high-risk macrometastasis in this cohort, although residual confounding related to treatment selection cannot be ruled out. Therefore, patients with high-risk micrometastasis likely require adjuvant therapy, including CDK4/6 inhibitors, similar to those used for patients with high-risk macrometastasis. Additionally, patients with high-risk micrometastasis tended to experience more recurrences after completing 5 years of endocrine therapy than those with high-risk macrometastasis (Online Resource Table 5), suggesting that extended endocrine therapy may be beneficial for this subgroup.

The prognosis of high-risk patients in our study was more favorable than that reported for the endocrine therapy-alone group in Cohort 1 of the monarchE trial [4]. One possible reason is that our study excluded patients with ≥ 4 lymph node metastases. In practice, patients with ≥ 4 micrometastases are rarely seen (only five patients with three micrometastases were identified in our cohort). Therefore, our analysis focusing on high-risk patients with one to three micrometastases was considered appropriate.

This study has some limitations. First, this study was a single-center retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained institutional database. This retrospective design may have introduced selection bias, including factors influencing treatment decisions and axillary management. In addition, the small number of events limited statistical power and prevented multivariable adjustment, which may have affected the precision of the effect estimates. Second, the sample size was relatively small. Third, the follow-up period was not long enough, considering the high incidence of late recurrence in HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer. In addition, a methodological limitation of this study is the difference in axillary surgical procedures between groups. Because a substantial proportion of patients in the micrometastasis groups underwent sentinel lymph node biopsy alone, the possibility of axillary understaging cannot be completely excluded. Previous evidence, including the IBCSG 23 − 01 trial, has shown that approximately 13% of patients with micrometastasis have additional non-sentinel lymph node metastases [15], indicating that further nodal involvement may remain undetected without ALND. Although the ALND-restricted sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with the primary analyses, residual inaccuracies in nodal assessment may still have influenced the observed outcomes. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution in light of this potential source of bias.

Conclusions

We compared the prognosis of patients with only micrometastasis and those with macrometastasis based on the high-risk criteria of the monarchE trial. We revealed that high-risk patients with only micrometastasis had similarly worse prognoses compared with those with macrometastasis. Therefore, high-risk patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative, node-positive breast cancer should be considered for systemic therapy, including CDK4/6 inhibitors, regardless of the size of lymph node metastasis.
