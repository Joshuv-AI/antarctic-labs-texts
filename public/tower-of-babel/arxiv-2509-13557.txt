MALTA: An Automated CGRA Design Framework
Zesong Jiang, Yuqi Sun, Qing Zhong, Mahathi Krishna, Deepak Patil, Cheng Tan, Sriram Krishnamoorthy, Jeff Zhang

Abstract
Coarse-grained Reconfigurable Arrays (CGRAs) are a promising computing architecture that can deliver high-performance, energy-efficient acceleration across diverse domains. By supporting reconfiguration at the functional unit level, CGRAs efficiently adapt to varying computational patterns and optimize resource utilization. However, designing CGRAs is highly challenging due to the vast design space, independent architectural parameters, and the time-consuming nature of manual design. Fortunately, the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) presents new opportunities to automate this process. In this work, we propose MALTA – an open-source multi-agent LLM-based framework for Hardware/Software (HW/SW) co-design of CGRAs. The framework employs LLM reasoning to generate CGRAs across four stages: HW/SW co-design , Design error correction , Best design selection , and Evaluation & \& Feedback . Furthermore, MALTA iteratively optimizes the generated CGRAs, leveraging agent reasoning and feedback to achieve higher PPA (that is, power, performance, and area) design points for a given domain. In addition, we introduce an LLM self-learning mechanism that employs LLM-driven decision making to select the optimal CGRA to accelerate the design process. We evaluate the framework with state-of-the-art LLM-based methods and manual CGRA design, in terms of performance, power consumption, and area. Experimental results show that MALTA efficiently generates high-quality CGRA architectures, significantly reducing manual design effort and demonstrating the potential of our framework for real-world CGRA design.



I
Introduction

Coarse-grained Reconfigurable Arrays (CGRAs) have emerged as a versatile computing paradigm that bridges the gap between the fixed efficiency of domain-specific accelerators and the flexibility of general-purpose processors. By enabling reconfiguration at the functional unit level, CGRAs can adapt hardware resources to the specific requirements of individual applications, striking a favorable balance among performance, energy efficiency, and architectural adaptability. This capability makes CGRAs particularly effective for compute- and communicate- intensive workloads such as signal processing, machine learning, and scientific computing.

Despite these advantages, designing an efficient CGRA remains a formidable challenge. Architects must explore an extensive combinatorial design space across hardware and software parameters while accounting for application-specific constraints and balancing inherently conflicting objectives such as performance, power, and area. For domain-specific applications, architecture design requires careful consideration of multiple hardware parameters – such as tile size, functional unit allocation, memory capacity, and interconnect topology – which are tightly coupled and jointly determine system performance. Similarly, software-level factors such as compiler strategies also play a critical role, including loop unrolling to reduce execution cycles or enabling vectorization to exploit parallelism in kernels. Together, hardware and software parameters create an enormous joint design space, where the impact on final performance is difficult to predict. Achieving a coordinated selection of these parameters is therefore a central problem in CGRA architecture design. Unfortunately, traditional manual approaches sequentially make design decisions, which are time-consuming, error-prone, and incapable of fully exploring the vast design space, highlighting the need for more automated, intelligent, and scalable design methodologies.

Recently, the emergence of large language models (LLMs)
[
1
]
has brought new opportunities for intelligent HW/SW co-design of CGRAs. With their strong reasoning and knowledge integration capabilities, LLMs can tackle the complexity of CGRA design and generate optimized solutions. However, approaches that rely solely on LLMs equipped with domain knowledge and prompt engineering—common in LLM-based tasks—can produce feasible CGRA architectures but often suffer from reduced performance and design errors due to hallucinations, falling short of ideal outcomes. Meanwhile, enhancing CGRA design performance through data fine-tuning—a common practice in LLM-based tasks—requires generating and synthesizing large, diverse CGRA architecture datasets, rendering the process both costly and time-consuming.

In this work, we propose MALTA, an open-source multi-agent LLM-based framework for HW/SW co-design of CGRAs. By leveraging feedback-driven iterative optimization and a multi-stage design approach, our framework continuously improves the performance of the designed CGRAs and reduces errors caused by LLM hallucinations.
MALTA decomposes the design process into specialized agents responsible for HW/SW co-design, Design error correction, Best design selection, and Evaluation & Feedback. These agents work in coordination to generate complete and valid architectures and iteratively optimize them through feedback, encompassing both
architectural parameters
(tile layout, functional unit allocation, memory sizing, interconnect configuration) and
compilation parameters
(unroll factors, vectorization factors). In addition, we introduce a self-learning paradigm that enables the LLM to progressively acquire knowledge through results feedback and learn to select the optimal design from multiple candidates, thereby achieving fast iteration. Experimental results across diverse workloads demonstrate that the framework efficiently generates high-quality CGRA designs while substantially reducing manual design effort.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose MALTA – the first open-source LLM-based framework for CGRA design, establishing a foundation for automated and systematic CGRA generation. In addition, MALTA integrates software mapper, RTL generation, and synthesis tools to support CGRA HW/SW co-design, allowing our framework to holistically optimize the joint impact of software and hardware on overall efficiency.

•

MALTA employs a multi-agent collaborative methodology, in which LLM-driven agents cooperate across design, verification, correction, and selection stages. This approach decomposes the CGRA design process into individual tasks, enabling the LLMs to complete individual process more efficiently.

•

We introduce a self-learning paradigm that enables the LLM to leverage EDA tool-generated reports to learn effective CGRAs. This enhances the LLM’s ability to select the top architecture from multiple candidates, replacing the need for time-intensive EDA tool-based evaluations.

•

We compared MALTA framework with Qwen (few-shot), Qwen (domain-augmented), and manual design, our method achieves improvements in power optimization, with average reductions of 59.4%, 48.9%, and 27.1% . Meanwhile, MALTA significantly improves average power efficiency, outperforming none HW/SW co-design approaches by 36.8%.

II-A

CGRA Design: Overview and Challenges

CGRAs are a class of programmable hardware accelerators that strike a balance between flexibility and efficiency, making them highly suitable for domains such as signal processing, linear algebra, and machine learning. The typical CGRA design process, as illustrated in Fig. 1, consists of two main components: compiler design and architecture design. A CGRA fabric is organized as a grid of tiles, each integrating functional units (FUs), local memory, and interconnects. The compilation software includes choices such as unrolling factors and vectorization factors, which directly affect kernel mapping efficiency. Therefore, effective CGRA design requires joint consideration of both hardware and software dimensions.

CGRA architects commonly face several design challenges to achieve high computational throughput and energy efficiency while ensuring scalability. First, inherent design complexity: architects must explore a vast design space with coupling effects among parameters that could degrade performance. Second, balancing performance and power efficiency is critical.

Recent advances in LLMs offer an opportunity to tackle these challenges, by leveraging LLMs strong reasoning abilities to design CGRAs and iteratively optimize them through continuous learning of CGRA domain knowledge.

III-A

Overview of the MALTA Framework

As shown in Figure 2, our framework, MALTA, consists of four stages, in which CGRA HW/SW co-design, verification and correction, best design selection, and evaluation are carried out sequentially. Each stage is managed by a dedicated agent that performs its specific task while collaborating with the other agents to complete the overall CGRA design.

•

Stage 1: CGRA Design
– The CGRA co-designer, guided by historical data and HW/SW co-design principles, generates multiple candidate CGRA designs.

•

Stage 2: Validation & Correction
– Using CGRA design tools
[
26
]
, MapFixer validates compilation and structural correctness. Invalid designs are automatically repaired through iterative, report-driven feedback.

•

Stage 3: Multi-Judge Selection
– A hierarchical judging system performs coarse-to-fine filtering. The coarse-grained judge uses reasoning and prior knowledge to eliminate weak candidates, producing the top K designs from the verified candidates. The fine-grained judge, potentially in combination with synthesis tools and experimental metrics (e.g., performance, power, area), selects the best design from these top K candidates.

•

Stage 4: Performance Evaluation & Feedback
– The final designs undergo comprehensive performance profiling using synthesis tools, and the results are stored as historical data to guide subsequent iterations.

Together, these four stages establish a closed-loop iterative design process that reduces manual intervention and significantly accelerates CGRA optimization.

III-B

Design Methods of MALTA Framwork

Our design methodology for the four stages is as follows:

Stage 1: CGRA Design –
CGRA co-designer generates candidate CGRA designs based on user input while iteratively refining designs using historical data, which leverages HW/SW co-design principles to produce a diverse set of architecture candidates. Each candidate encompasses parameters at both the HW/SW levels, including functional unit allocation and loop unrolling factors, ensuring that we can generate effiencicy CGRAs by covering a wide range of design trade-offs.

To produce high-quality CGRA candidates, domain-specific knowledge is embedded into prompts, enabling the agent to effectively balance compilation strategies, mapping efficiency, and hardware architecture optimization.

Stage 2: Design Verification and Correction –
This stage employs MapFixer to invoke simulation tools for validation. As automatically generated designs may contain syntax, compilation, or structural errors, designs that pass validation proceed directly to the next stage, while those that fail are automatically repaired using the LLM’s reasoning capabilities, guided by information from reports and logs. This process ensures that only valid and executable CGRA architectures advance to subsequent stages.

Stage 3: Multi-Judge Selection –
This stage employs a hierarchical, two-level filtering process. At the first level, the coarse-grained judge leverages the reasoning capabilities of LLM along with embedded optimization knowledge to rapidly identify candidates with high performance potential.

At the second level, the fine-grained agent works alongside CGRA design tool and synthesis tool. In the early iterations, synthesis tools provide accurate power and area analysis, while open-source CGRA tool
[
26
]
estimate speedup, enabling the selection of the best-performing design. Meanwhile, the fine-grained judge also makes the best choice and learns from the reports if its selection is incorrect. As iterations progress, the fine-grained agent gradually learns from experimental data, evolving into an autonomous evaluator capable of replacing the tools to make the final CGRA design selection. This process leverages an adaptive-confidence, LLM self-learning approach for CGRA design selection, as outlined in Algorithm 1, which will be discussed in detail in the following sections.

Stage 4: Performance Evaluation and Feedback –
In stage 4, final designs that are not evaluated in Stage 3 undergo comprehensive performance and power assessment using design and synthesis tools. The results are stored as historical data, which are fed back to the agents in Stage 1 to further enhance the CGRA designer’s generation quality. Upon completion of the final iteration, the framework outputs the final design results.

III-C

Adaptive Confidence CGRA Design Selection with LLM Self-learning

In Stage 3, when selecting best design from the top K designs, we consider two approaches: evaluation via synthesis tools and inference via LLM reasoning(Fine-grained Judge). Considering the time-consuming nature of tool-based evaluation and the potential error rate of LLM reasoning, we propose an adaptive confidence CGRA selection method with LLM self-learning to balance accuracy and efficiency.

The specific algorithm is presented in Algorithm 1. Initially, a confidence measure and trust threshold are established. For each iteration, if LLM’s reasoning confidence falls below the threshold, EDA tools (e.g., simulations and synthesis) are used to evaluate the top-k candidate designs, from which the best design is selected. Simultaneously, the LLM engages in contrastive learning against the EDA tool reports and its own choices to enhance its capability. As iterations progress, the confidence score increases based on the consistency between LLM predictions and simulated results. Once the confidence surpasses the threshold, the system transitions to primarily relying on LLM-based reasoning, enabling rapid and accurate design selection. To ensure system stability, simulation-based evaluations are periodically invoked every N iterations.

This approach fully leverages the model’s inference capabilities to enable rapid iterative optimization and selection of CGRA design. Compared with traditional reinforcement learning methods, it offers superior scalability, as it is not restricted to a single learning framework. During subsequent design evolution, when new software compilation parameters or hardware architecture parameters are introduced, the model can retain existing knowledge while continuously learning and integrating new information, progressively refining and enhancing its capabilities.

Conclusion

In this work, we present MALTA, a multi-agent LLM-based framework for CGRA design. By decomposing the design process and performing iterative optimization, the framework effectively identifies optimal CGRA architectures for a diverse range of application kernels.

Experimental results on a comprehensive benchmark demonstrate that MALTA can generate high-quality CGRA architectures with improved performance, efficiency, and resource utilization compared to traditional design approaches. To our knowledge, this is the first work to leverage LLMs in a structured multi-agent setting for CGRA design. Future work will explore extending the framework to support more complex architectures, integrate additional optimization objectives, and incorporate real hardware deployment feedback to further enhance design quality and practicality.
