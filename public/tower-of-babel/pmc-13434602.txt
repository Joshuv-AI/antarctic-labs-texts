Impact of nurse–parent partnership, social support, and uncertainty on parental efficacy among mothers of children hospitalized in pediatric wards in South Korea: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Dan A Kim, Mi-Young Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to identify the relationships among nurse–parent partnership, social support, uncertainty, and parental efficacy among mothers of children hospitalized in pediatric wards.MethodsThis descriptive survey study included 149 mothers of children hospitalized in pediatric wards at 5 hospitals in the Chungcheong region from August 12 to September 17, 2025. Data were collected using structured questionnaires and analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation analysis, and multiple regression analysis.ResultsParental efficacy was positively correlated with social support (r=.35, p<.001) and negatively correlated with uncertainty (r=–.36, p<.001). Multiple regression analysis showed that uncertainty (β=–.26, p=.001) and social support (β=.24, p=.003) were significantly associated with parental efficacy and explained 21.0% of the variance.ConclusionUncertainty and social support were key factors associated with parental efficacy among mothers of children hospitalized in pediatric wards. Nursing interventions to enhance parental efficacy should therefore focus on reducing maternal uncertainty by providing specific, repeated information and fostering a supportive nursing environment that helps mothers recognize and use available social support.

INTRODUCTION

In Korea, previous studies on hospitalized children have consistently identified mothers as the primary caregivers [1], assuming full responsibility for nursing and caregiving during hospitalization. This evidence indicates that they play the central role as primary caregivers in hospital settings, suggesting their critical significance in children’s health recovery and adaptation to hospitalization.

A child’s hospitalization imposes concurrent physical and psychological burdens on mothers. During this period, maternal coping abilities may be associated not only with their own psychological well-being but also with the child’s treatment process and family functioning [2]. In particular, the hospitalization of a child creates significant uncertainty for mothers, leading them to experience negative emotions such as anxiety, helplessness, guilt, and stress [3]. When these negative emotions persist, mothers may lose confidence in caring for their child and experience decreased parental efficacy, which can ultimately have adverse effects on the hospitalized child’s recovery process [4].

Parental efficacy refers to a parent’s perception of their ability to successfully manage parenting and discipline, including the confidence to resolve challenges that may arise while raising their child [5]. As an essential psychological resource, it empowers caregivers to perform parenting tasks with confidence and is closely linked to children’s healthy development and attachment formation [5]. Mothers with high parental efficacy demonstrate positive interactions with their children and actively engage in child-rearing, which is positively associated with child development [6]. Particularly during hospitalization, mothers with high parental efficacy actively participate in medication administration and emergency management, as well as demonstrate proactive and effective coping even in stressful situations [7]. Therefore, parental efficacy is a critical factor in the treatment process and recovery of hospitalized children, and strengthening it among mothers of hospitalized children is essential for achieving positive health outcomes for these children.

Nurse–parent partnerships are collaborative relationships between parents and nurses that are based on shared goals and interactions in the care of hospitalized children [8]. Characterized by reciprocity, professionalism, and collaboration, this study defined the concept specifically as the partnership with nurses as perceived by the mothers of hospitalized children [8]. Collaborative care through these partnerships is associated with enhanced parental understanding of treatment processes and improved nursing care quality [2]. Nurses should alleviate the parental psychological burden and create supportive environments through emotional support and appropriate interventions, while effective communication strengthens parental therapeutic trust, which is related to parental efficacy and treatment outcomes [9]. A collaborative relationship provides mothers with the necessary clinical information and emotional validation, which may bolster their confidence and competence in performing parenting tasks within an unfamiliar hospital environment.

Social support refers to the human resources available within the parenting environment, specifically the assistance available to mothers from their social network, excluding their spouses [10]. It includes emotional and practical assistance from family, friends, and the community, playing a crucial role in promoting emotional stability and alleviating stress among the parents of hospitalized children [10]. Adequate social support is linked to a reduced psychological burden and enhanced parental efficacy [11], whereas insufficient support leads to psychological isolation. This suggests that providing adequate social support can be an important strategy for promoting parental efficacy. By providing practical help and emotional stability, external support systems may help mothers maintain their psychological resilience, thereby sustaining their sense of efficacy in the parenting role even during a child's illness.

Uncertainty refers to a state of being unable to clearly interpret a situation due to unfamiliarity with illness-related events, unpredictability, ambiguity, and a lack of information [12]. This cognitive barrier arises from insufficient disease information and diagnostic complexity, posing a major challenge to effective parental role performance. For parents of hospitalized children, high levels of uncertainty are often triggered by ineffective communication with healthcare providers and unpredictable changes in their child’s clinical condition [12,13]. Persistent uncertainty is associated with feelings of helplessness and decreased role performance and confidence, reducing parental efficacy, which in turn impedes parent–child attachment and negatively affects home caregiving after discharge [14]. When mothers cannot clearly interpret their child's health status or the trajectory of treatment, they experience a diminished sense of control, which may undermine their confidence in caring for their child effectively.

Although previous research has identified nurse–parent partnerships, social support, and uncertainty as factors associated with parental efficacy—primarily among mothers of premature infants in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) and parents of children with chronic or life-threatening conditions—there is a paucity of studies that comprehensively examine the interrelationships among these variables in mothers of children hospitalized in general pediatric wards. Given the contemporary development in healthcare demands and the unique cultural context of South Korean pediatric wards, where mothers provide 24-hour bedside care, it is necessary to re-examine these variables to provide updated, evidence-based nursing strategies.

METHODS

Ethical statements: This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Chungbuk National University (IRB No. CBNU-2025-A-0093). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

1. Study Design

This descriptive correlational study examined the relationships among nurse–parent partnerships, social support, uncertainty, and parental efficacy, and identified the factors influencing parental efficacy in mothers of children hospitalized in pediatric wards. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [15].

2. Participants

The participants were the mothers of children hospitalized in pediatric wards at 5 hospitals in Chungcheong region who understood the study’s purpose and provided informed consent. The required sample size was estimated using G*Power ver. 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf) for multiple regression analysis with 3 predictor variables, a significance level of .05, and a power of .80. The effect size (f2=.715) was calculated from a previous study investigating factors associated with parental effectiveness in mothers of hospitalized children [10], which reported an adjusted R2 of .417. To improve the generalizability and statistical stability of the findings, 153 questionnaires were distributed, accounting for a 10% attrition rate, and 149 questionnaires with complete responses were analyzed.

3. Measurements

1) General characteristics

The assessed maternal characteristics included age, religion, educational level, employment status, perceived economic status, and marital status. The child and hospitalization-related characteristics included the child’s sex, birth order, number of hospitalizations (including current hospitalization), current length of hospital stay (days), and primary diagnosis.

2) Nurse–parent partnerships

Nurse–parent partnerships were measured using the Pediatric Nurse Parents Partnership Scale developed by Choi and Bang [8]. The instrument comprises 34 items across 7 domains: reciprocity (9 items), professional knowledge and skill (7 items), sensitivity (6 items), collaboration (3 items), communication (4 items), shared information (3 items), and cautiousness (2 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 34 to 170. Higher scores indicate a higher level nurse–parent partnership. The reliability of the original instrument was Cronbach’s α=.96 and, in this study, Cronbach’s α=.97.

3) Social support

Social support was measured using the Social Support Scale developed by Park [13] and modified by Kim [16], comprising 10 items on a 5-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 10 to 50. Higher scores indicate higher perceived social support. Cronbach’s α was .94 in the study by Kim [16] and .93 in this study.

4) Uncertainty

Uncertainty was measured using the Parents’ Perception of Uncertainty Scale developed by Mishel [17] and translated and modified by Oh [18]. The instrument comprises 22 items on a 4-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 22 to 88. Higher scores indicate higher uncertainty levels. Cronbach’s α was .83 in the study by Oh [18] and .77 in this study.

5) Parental efficacy

Parental efficacy was measured using the Parenting Sense of Competence (PSOC) scale originally developed by Gibaud-Wallston and Wandersman [19]. While the original PSOC consists of 2 subscales—parental efficacy (reflecting skills and abilities) and parental satisfaction (reflecting affective dimensions such as motivation and frustration)—this study utilized the Korean version of the PSOC adapted by Shin and Chung [20]. In their factor analysis, Shin and Chung [20] identified 3 distinct factors: parental efficacy (9 items), anxiety as a parent (4 items), and others (3 items). For the purpose of this study, which focuses specifically on the mothers’ perceived competence in their parenting role, only the 9-item parental efficacy subscale was employed. Formal approval was obtained to use this specific subscale for research purposes. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 9 to 45. Higher scores indicate higher levels of parental efficacy. The reliability of the parental efficacy subscale was acceptable, with a Cronbach’s α of .78 in the study by Shin and Chung [20], and .79 in this study.

4. Data Collection

Permission to use all measurement instruments was obtained from the original developers, modifiers, and translators by email and text messages. Data collection was conducted from August 12 to September 17, 2025. The researcher contacted the nursing departments of 5 hospitals with pediatric wards in the Chungcheong region, explained the study’s purpose and methods, and obtained approval by submitting a data collection application. Subsequently, the researcher visited each pediatric ward to obtain ward consent and posted recruitment announcements. Participants who voluntarily expressed interest in participating after viewing the announcements were approached directly by the researcher and provided with questionnaires that included an information sheet and a consent form. The researcher fully explained to the participants, both verbally and in writing, the study’s background and purpose, methods, benefits, compensation for participation, potential side effects or risks and discomfort, personal information protection and confidentiality assurance, and their right to voluntary participation and withdrawal at any time without any disadvantage. All participants received a small token of appreciation for their participation. The time required to complete the questionnaire was approximately 20 to 30 minutes. To protect personal information, each participant was provided with a sealable return envelope for the completed questionnaire, which was then directly collected by the researcher and processed anonymously. All collected data were used solely for research purposes, and all personal information was coded to protect participant privacy. The coded data were stored and password-protected on a computer accessible only to the researcher and analyzed accordingly. All data will be destroyed 3 years after the completion of the study.

5. Data Analysis

Collected data were analyzed using IBM SPSS for Windows ver. 31.0 (IBM Corp.) as follows. First, participants’ general characteristics and the levels of nurse–parent partnership, social support, uncertainty, and parental efficacy were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Second, differences in parental efficacy according to participants’ general characteristics were analyzed using independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When the ANOVA results showed significant differences, the Scheffé test was performed for post-hoc comparison. Third, the correlations among nurse–parent partnerships social support, uncertainty, and parental efficacy were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Fourth, to identify the factors influencing parental efficacy, multiple regression analysis was conducted using the enter method. Prior to the regression analysis, assumptions were tested by examining multicollinearity, residuals, and outliers.

RESULTS

1. Differences in Parental Efficacy according to Participants’ General Characteristics

Differences in parental efficacy according to the general characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1. Parental efficacy differed significantly by employment status (t=–2.26, p=.025) and perceived economic status (F=4.49, p=.013). The post-hoc analysis revealed that mothers who perceived their economic status as “sufficient” had significantly higher parental efficacy than those who perceived it as “moderate” (Table 1).

2. Descriptive Statistics for the Main Variables

The levels of the main variables are presented in Table 2. The mean score for nurse–parent partnerships was 4.50±0.48 out of 5. Among the subscales, cautiousness showed the highest mean score at 4.71±0.47, followed by collaboration (4.61±0.52), professional knowledge and skill (4.57±0.52), reciprocity (4.55±0.50), shared information (4.43±0.58), sensitivity (4.42±0.57), and communication (4.24±0.62). The mean scores were 4.04±0.78 out of 5 for social support, 1.92±0.31 out of 4 for uncertainty, and 3.72±0.51 out of 5 for parental efficacy.

3. Correlations among the Main Variables

The correlations among the main variables are presented in Table 3. Parental efficacy showed a significant positive correlation with social support (r=.35, p<.001) and a significant negative correlation with uncertainty (r=–.36, p<.001). Although there was no statistically significant correlation between parental efficacy and the total score of nurse–parent partnerships, 2 subscales—sensitivity (r=.17, p=.035) and shared information (r=.19, p=.023)—showed significant positive correlations with parental efficacy. Meanwhile, nurse–parent partnerships were positively correlated with social support (r=.30, p<.001) and negatively correlated with uncertainty (r=–.26, p=.002).

4. Factors Influencing Parental Efficacy

To identify the factors influencing parental efficacy, multiple regression analysis was conducted with employment status and perceived economic status (i.e., variables showing significant differences in general characteristics) as control variables, and nurse–parent partnerships, social support, and uncertainty as independent variables. Prior to the regression analysis, multicollinearity was examined. The tolerance ranged from 0.30 to 0.92, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) ranged from 1.09 to 3.34, both of which were within the acceptable thresholds (tolerance >.10; VIF <10) [21]. Thus, there were no multicollinearity issues. The Durbin-Watson statistic was 2.32, which is close to 2, confirming that the assumption of independent residuals was satisfied. Regarding the normality of residuals, the skewness and kurtosis values were .11 and .11, respectively, while the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (p=.200) and Shapiro-Wilk (p=.162) tests indicated that the residuals did not deviate from a normal distribution. Finally, the assumption of homoscedasticity was also met.

The regression model was statistically significant (F=6.68, p<.001), with an adjusted R2 of 21.0%. The examination of the factors influencing parental efficacy revealed that uncertainty (β=–.26, p=.001) and social support (β=.24, p=.003) were significant predictors. Specifically, lower uncertainty and higher social support were associated with higher parental efficacy (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the relationships among nurse–parent partnerships, social support, uncertainty, and parental efficacy to identify the factors associated with parental efficacy among mothers of children hospitalized in pediatric wards. The results revealed that uncertainty and social support were significant explanatory variables associated with parental efficacy.

In this study, parental efficacy showed statistically significant differences according to employment and perceived economic status. Specifically, employed mothers demonstrated higher parental efficacy than unemployed mothers, which is consistent with the findings of Chang and Park [22], who reported that maternal employment is positively related to higher parental efficacy. Separately, our finding that mothers with higher perceived economic status reported greater parental efficacy aligns with the broader understanding that economic resources provide a foundational support for caregiving roles [23]. This suggests that while both variables are influential, employment status may specifically be related to efficacy through professional role fulfillment, whereas economic stability acts as a protective buffer against the costs of treatment and caregiving [24]. These findings suggest that the socioeconomic background of the parents of hospitalized children can be significantly associated with their parental efficacy. Therefore, pediatric nurses should assess the socioeconomic background of the parents of hospitalized children and provide tailored nursing care accordingly. Specifically, for families facing economic difficulties, nurses should provide information about medical cost support systems and community resources, as well as enhance education and emotional support to help parents actively participate in the caregiving process and enhance their parental efficacy.

The analysis of the correlations among nurse–parent partnerships, social support, uncertainty, and parental efficacy revealed that parental efficacy had a significant positive correlation with social support and a significant negative correlation with uncertainty. The positive correlation between social support and parental efficacy is consistent with the findings of Huang et al. [25] who reported that a family-focused parenting support intervention significantly improved both perceived social support and parents' sense of competence among parents of preterm infants. While their study focused on parents of children hospitalized in a NICU where the clinical instability is extreme, our findings confirm that this inverse relationship remains consistent in general pediatric ward settings. These findings suggest that enhancing social support and reducing uncertainty are key factors in improving parental efficacy among the mothers of hospitalized children.

Meanwhile, although parental efficacy showed no significant correlation with the total score of nurse–parent partnerships, the subscale analysis revealed that sensitivity and shared information were significantly positively correlated with parental efficacy. This indicates that nurses’ sensitive responses to children’s conditions and parents’ needs, along with the provision of necessary information through continuous communication, can enhance parents’ perception of active participation in the treatment process and their confidence in performing the parental role. These findings echo those of Genna et al. [26], who reported that appropriate responses and interactions from healthcare providers enhance parents’ role awareness and caregiving confidence. Notably, while nurse–parent partnerships showed no direct correlation with parental efficacy, they demonstrated significant correlations with social support and uncertainty. This suggests the possibility that nurse–parent partnerships may contribute to enhancing parental efficacy indirectly by strengthening social support and reducing uncertainty, rather than through a direct effect. However, as this mediating effect was not directly examined in this study, future research using mediation analysis is needed to clarify these pathways.

Multiple regression analysis revealed that uncertainty and social support were significant factors associated with parental efficacy, with uncertainty as the primary predictor with the greatest explanatory power. This finding is consistent with Huang et al. [25], who demonstrated that family-centered support interventions enhanced parental efficacy among parents of preterm infants. The role burden and lack of control experienced by mothers in hospitalization contexts amplify uncertainty, which acts as a major factor inhibiting parental efficacy. Notably, the absolute levels of uncertainty in this study were relatively lower than those typically reported in intensive care settings. This can be attributed to the characteristics of general wards, where disease progression is relatively predictable compared to the NICU. Furthermore, according to the Uncertainty in Illness Theory by Mishel [27], uncertainty is a cognitive state determined by the clarity of clinical cues. In this study, the effective provision of information and sensitive interaction by nurses likely served as sufficient cues, enabling mothers to develop a better understanding of the situation and thus report lower uncertainty. If mothers enhance their understanding of the treatment process and regain a sense of control, uncertainty can be reduced, and parental efficacy can be enhanced. However, Glatz and Lippold [28] cautioned that a unilateral information overload can actually increase parental anxiety and undermine efficacy. Therefore, rather than simply providing abundant information in clinical settings, it is essential to assess parents’ psychological readiness and receptive capacity to establish stepwise and individualized information provision strategies.

In this study, social support was identified as the second major factor associated with parental efficacy. This finding is consistent with those of the study by Jung [29] on the parents of hospitalized children, suggesting that social support is a universal and essential factor that strengthens parental efficacy regardless of the clinical context. Furthermore, the importance of social support has been consistently reported in special caregiving situations as well. In their study of mothers of premature infants, Begjani et al. [30] found that education and support from peer mothers significantly enhanced parental efficacy. These findings confirm that support from others acts as a protective factor during hospitalization by providing psychological stability and boosting confidence in parental role performance. Therefore, in clinical settings, a multilayered approach is needed to strengthen parents’ social support systems. Specifically, healthcare providers should offer emotional support and encouragement to parents, facilitate peer support groups or online support communities among the parents of hospitalized children, and strengthen linkages with family members and community resources. Through this multifaceted construction of social support networks, parental efficacy can be effectively enhanced [31].

In this study, although the total score of nurse–parent partnerships showed no significant correlation with parental efficacy, 2 subscales—sensitivity and shared information—demonstrated significant positive correlations with parental efficacy. However, nurse–parent partnerships were not identified as an independent predictor of parental efficacy in the regression analysis. This finding may be related to the characteristics of the study participants. Most participants were mothers of children with relatively short hospital stays and generally evaluated nurse–parent partnerships positively. However, there may have been temporal constraints preventing the partnerships formed during short-term hospitalization from developing into a sufficiently deep therapeutic relationship to substantially enhance mothers’ parental efficacy. Additionally, considering that nurse–parent partnerships showed significant correlations with social support and uncertainty, partnerships may contribute to parental efficacy indirectly by mediating the pathways of strengthening social support and reducing uncertainty, rather than having a direct effect. The finding that the sensitivity and shared information subscales correlated with parental efficacy is noteworthy. It suggests that parental efficacy is enhanced not simply through the formation of positive partnerships with nurses, but rather through the process of mothers experiencing nurses’ sensitive responses, receiving adequate information, directly participating in childcare, and accumulating successful experiences based on the established partnership. Therefore, for children with relatively short hospital stays, a strategic approach that goes beyond simply forming close relationships is needed to enable mothers to substantially participate in childcare and experience a sense of achievement. Specifically, nurses should respond sensitively to mothers’ needs and emotions, provide clear and understandable information about the child’s condition and treatment, offer opportunities for mothers to participate in daily childcare activities, and reinforce successful experiences through positive feedback. These recommendations are consistent with the systematic review by Hodgson et al. [32], which reported that family-centered interventions for hospitalized children enhance parental participation, knowledge, and overall well-being, and such an approach can serve as a foundation for the substantial enhancement of parental efficacy.

The need for repeated investigation of these relationships is underscored by the shifting dynamics of the contemporary healthcare environment. First, modern advances have altered how parents manage illness-related information; in the current digital era, an information overload paradoxically increases the demand for clear, professional communication to navigate clinical uncertainty. Second, changes in subject characteristics warrant consideration, as millennial and Gen Z mothers pursue more active participation and autonomous decision-making compared to previous generations. Finally, given the unique cultural context of 24-hour rooming-in in South Korean pediatric wards, validating these variables is essential to provide evidence-based nursing strategies that reflect current parental expectations. Thus, this study provides a critical and timely update to existing theories, confirming their relevance in the present-day clinical setting.

The limitations of this study are as follows. First, this study was conducted only with mothers of children hospitalized in pediatric wards in selected regions, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the study only considered mothers as the primary caregivers and did not capture the experiences of fathers or other caregivers. Future research should include diverse regions and a comprehensive range of caregivers. Second, this study included the mothers of children hospitalized in pediatric wards without differentiating by disease or length of stay. This constrained our ability to compare differences in parental efficacy according to disease characteristics or hospitalization duration. Future research should specify target populations by disease groups, acute versus chronic hospitalization, and length of stay. Third, the uncertainty measurement tool may not adequately reflect the changes in caregiver perceptions and current healthcare environments since its development, which may have contributed to lower reliability compared to the original instrument. Future research should revise and validate the instrument to reflect current healthcare contexts and parental perceptions. Furthermore, the parental efficacy instrument was originally developed for general parenting contexts and may not fully assess the specific efficacy required for parents caring for hospitalized children, such as managing a child's illness, participating in treatment processes, and communicating effectively with healthcare professionals. Therefore, future studies should employ or develop a disease-specific parental efficacy scale to better assess the unique obstacles faced by parents in pediatric settings. Fourth, as this study employed a cross-sectional design, clear causal relationships among variables could not be established. In particular, the indirect pathways through which nurse–parent partnerships may influence parental efficacy via social support and uncertainty were not directly examined. Future research using longitudinal designs or structural equation modeling to verify mediating effects is thus needed.

Despite these limitations, this study yields the following implications. By empirically identifying factors associated with parental efficacy among mothers of hospitalized children, this study reaffirms the importance of family-centered nursing practice and contributes to expanding the knowledge base in pediatric nursing. Moreover, the findings can serve as foundational data for developing evidence-based nursing intervention programs for the families of hospitalized children.

CONCLUSION

This study identified the factors associated with parental efficacy among mothers of children hospitalized in pediatric wards, focusing on the roles of nurse–parent partnerships, social support, and uncertainty. The results revealed that uncertainty and social support were significant explanatory variables associated with parental efficacy, suggesting that nurse–parent partnerships may indirectly contribute to parental efficacy through these variables. The importance of this study is supported by the evolving dynamics of the contemporary healthcare environment. First, temporal shifts in information access have transformed how parents manage illness-related data; in the digital era, information overload often necessitates clear, professional communication to alleviate clinical uncertainty. Second, the generational transition among caregivers—specifically millennial and Gen Z mothers in South Korea—reflects an increasing demand for active participation and autonomous decision-making. Finally, considering the unique 24-hour rooming-in culture in Korean pediatric wards, validating these variables provides essential evidence-based insights that reflect current parental expectations. Therefore, this study re-evaluates and confirms the relevance of existing theoretical frameworks within the present-day clinical setting.

Based on these findings, nursing interventions are needed to enhance parental efficacy by reducing maternal uncertainty through the provision of specific, repeated, and tailored information. Furthermore, healthcare providers should create a supportive environment that facilitates the recognition and utilization of social support. Such multifaceted support strategies will ultimately contribute to improving the quality of care and health outcomes for hospitalized children and their families.
