Estimation of Treatment Effects Under Nonstationarity via Truncated Difference-in-Q’s
Ramesh Johari, Tianyi Peng, Wenqian Xing

Abstract
Randomized controlled experiments (“A/B testing”) are fundamental for assessing interventions in dynamic technology-driven environments, such as recommendation systems, online marketplaces, and digital health interventions. In these systems, interventions typically impact not only the current state of the system, but also future states; therefore, accurate estimation of the global average treatment effect (or GATE) from experiments requires accounting for the dynamic temporal behavior of the system. To address this problem, recent literature has analyzed a range of estimators applied to Bernoulli randomized experiments in stationary environments, ranging from the standard difference-in-means (DM) estimator to methods building on reinforcement learning techniques, such as off-policy evaluation and the recently proposed difference-in-Q’s (DQ) estimator. However, all these estimators exhibit high bias when the environment of interest is nonstationary , and potentially high variance as well. In this paper, we address the challenge of estimation in the nonstationary setting. We show that using a simple extension of the DM estimator to use differences in truncated outcome trajectories , rather than just instantaneous outcomes, yields favorable bias and variance in nonstationary Markovian settings, relative to typical approaches. Our theoretical analysis establishes this result by first showing that the truncated estimator is in fact estimating an appropriate policy gradient that can be expressed as a difference in Q Q -values; thus we refer to our estimator as the truncated DQ estimator (by analogy to the DQ estimator). We then show that the corresponding policy gradient is a first-order approximation (in the magnitude of the treatment effect) to the GATE. Combining these insights yields our bias and variance bounds. We validate our results through a synthetic simulation study and conduct sensitivity analysis with respect to the truncation length. Additionally, we study experiments in two realistic simulation environments: (1) a real-world calibrated queueing simulator capturing nonstationary patient arrivals in a hospital emergency department; and (2) a simulation of a large-scale ride-sharing system based on New York City taxi data. Our results make clear that an appropriately calibrated truncated DQ estimator can yield low bias and variance even in nonstationary environments likely to arise in real-world practice.

Introduction

Randomized controlled experiments (“A/B tests”) are essential tools for evaluating the impact of interventions across dynamic, technology-driven environments such as recommendation systems, online marketplaces, and digital health platforms. Typically, these interventions do not merely influence immediate outcomes but also propagate effects over subsequent system states. This leads to a breakdown of the standard assumption of unit-level independence, a phenomenon known as
temporal interference

(
Glynn et al. 2020
)
. For instance, in a digital health platform, randomization of incoming patients to treatment and control affects the workload of clinical providers, which in turn impacts the care experience of subsequent patients. Similarly, in a ride-sharing platform, user assignments to treatment and control affect the availability of drivers: a booking decision by one user influences the system state—e.g., driver distribution or availability—which in turn affects the experience and behavior of subsequent users. In such settings, applying a naive estimator (e.g., the difference-in-means or Horvitz-Thompson estimators
(
Horvitz and Thompson 1952
)
) can result in substantial bias, as it fails to account for temporal dependencies and state dynamics
(
Chamandy 2016
)
.

One way to model this dynamic effect is through a Markovian system, represented by the sequence
(X_{t},Z_{t},Y_{t})
for a finite time horizon
t=1,\dotsc,T
. Here,
X_{t}
denotes the system state at time
t
(e.g., the number and locations of available drivers), and
Z_{t}\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether the user arriving at time
t
is assigned to the control or treatment group. The variable
Y_{t}
captures the reward resulting from the user’s behavior after receiving the assignment (e.g.,
Y_{t}=1
if the user books a trip). Conditioned on the state
X_{t}
and action
Z_{t}
, the system evolves according to:

\displaystyle X_{t+1}\sim P_{Z_{t}}^{t}(X_{t},\cdot),

where
P_{0}^{t}
and
P_{1}^{t}
denote the (unknown) transition kernels under control and treatment at time
t
. The superscript
t
reflects the system’s
nonstationarity
: the transition kernels can change over time, driven by factors such as evolving user preferences, time-dependent driver dynamics, and external shocks (e.g., seasonal trends or viral events). Nonstationarity is a central challenge we address in this work.

Platforms typically want to compare two policies: the
treatment policy
, which assigns
Z_{t}=1
for all
t
, and the
control policy
, which assigns
Z_{t}=0
for all
t
. The estimand of interest is the expected average reward under each policy over a fixed finite time horizon, commonly referred to as the
global average treatment effect
(GATE). Since it is infeasible to observe both policies simultaneously, data are typically collected under an exploratory policy (the
experiment
)—for instance, a Bernoulli randomization policy that assigns each
Z_{t}
independently to
0
or
1
with equal probability, as in a typical A/B test setting.

This yields a challenging causal inference problem:
estimation of the treatment effect from a single trajectory in a nonstationary setting.
A variety of approaches, of differing levels of sophistication, exist for this problem:

•

Naive estimator.
A naive approach is to simply ignore the interference and take the difference in average outcomes between treatment and control groups (e.g., a difference-in means or Horvitz-Thompson estimator
(
Horvitz and Thompson 1952
)
).
Though straightforward, the bias of such an estimator can be as large as the treatment effect itself
(
Glynn et al. 2020
,
Farias et al. 2022
,
Hu and Wager 2022
)
, which motivates the line of research aimed at addressing interference in the first place.

•

Stationary OPE.
Recent research has explored more sophisticated techniques inspired by reinforcement learning, including off-policy evaluation and the recently proposed difference-in-Q’s (DQ) estimator
(
Farias et al. 2022
,
Farias et al. 2023
,
Uehara et al. 2022
,
Shi et al. 2023
)
. However, most of these methods assume a
stationary
environment. When applied to our setting, they tend to exhibit high bias and substantial variance (see Sections
4
and
6
), as they rely heavily on assumptions such as the stationary observability of system states or the availability of multiple independent trajectories—none of which hold in our setting.

•

More complex designs.
An orthogonal line of research investigates more elaborate experimental designs, such as switchback experiments
(
Hu and Wager 2022
)
, to address nonstationarity. However, these designs can be challenging to implement in practice and often require careful tuning of parameters such as interval length. This motivates our investigation into whether improvements are possible within the more commonly utilized Bernoulli randomized design in A/B testing.

Perhaps surprisingly,
we find that a simple modification of the difference-in-means (DM)

The estimator is simple to implement:

Empirical.

In this highly nonstationary environment, we compare its performance to standard OPE methods and a recent switchback-based approach

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the formal framework used throughout the paper.

2.1
A dynamic model of treatment

Notation.
We adopt standard Landau notation, writing
f(x)=\mathcal{O}{\left({g(x)}\right)}
if there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
x
, such that
|f(x)|\leqslant C|g(x)|
for all sufficiently large
x
. We use
[T]
to denote the index set
\{1,\ldots,T\}
, and
\mathsf{Law}(Y)
to denote the probability distribution of a random variable
Y
.

Time horizon.

We consider a discrete, finite time horizon indexed by
t=1,\ldots,T
.

State and action.

We consider a finite state space
\mathcal{X}
and a binary action space
\mathcal{Z}=\{0,1\}
.

Transition kernel.

For
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, we let
P_{z}^{t}(x,x^{\prime})
denote the transition probability from
x
to
x^{\prime}
if action
z
is chosen at time
t
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}=x^{\prime}|X_{t}=x,Z_{t}=z)=P^{t}_{z}(x,x^{\prime})
.

We assume the following
mixing time
property on the transition kernels. A similar uniform mixing-time bound was assumed by
Farias et al. 2022
and
Hu and Wager 2022
in the context of switchback experiments in a Markovian setting. More broadly, mixing time assumptions are common in the literature on bandits and reinforcement learning
(
Van Roy 1998
,
Even-Dar et al. 2004
,
Hu and Wager 2023
)
, as well as in the study of general Markov processes
(
Doukhan 1994
)
.

Assumption 1

(Mixing time)
.

There exists a constant
\gamma
,
0<\gamma<1
, such that, for any time
t\in[T]
, any action
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, and any pair of distributions
f
and
f^{\prime}
over the state space
\mathcal{X}
, the following holds

\left\|f^{\prime}P_{z}^{t}-fP_{z}^{t}\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leqslant\gamma\left\|f^{\prime}-f\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}},

where
fP_{z}^{t}
denotes the distribution on
\mathcal{X}
defined by
(fP_{z}^{t})(x^{\prime}):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)P_{z}^{t}(x,x^{\prime})
,
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
denotes the total variation distance.

Markov process.

We denote the distribution of the initial state
X_{1}
by
\rho
. For all subsequent time steps, we assume the state transition dynamics obey the Markov property.
Formally, for any time
t
and any sequences
\{X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{t-1}=x_{t-1},X_{t}=x\}
of states and
\{Z_{1}=z_{1},\ldots,Z_{t-1}=z_{t-1},Z_{t}=z\}
of actions, we assume the Markov property holds, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}=x^{\prime}|X_{1}=x_{1},Z_{1}=z_{1},\ldots,X_{t-1}=x_{t-1},Z_{t-1}=z_{t-1},X_{t}=x,Z_{t}=z)\\
=\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}=x^{\prime}|X_{t}=x,Z_{t}=z)=P^{t}_{z}(x,x^{\prime}).

Any sequence of actions

\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{T}\}\in\mathcal{Z}^{T}
,
together with the initial distribution
X_{1}\sim\rho
, completely determines the distribution of the (Markov) process
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{T}\}
.

Remark 1
.

Assumption
1
implies that the influence of the current action at time
t
on the future state diminishes over time, i.e., at an exponential rate of
\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{t})
. Informally, despite nonstationarity, this property yields systems that gradually “forget" initial conditions or past actions.

Rewards.

For
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, we let
R(\cdot|x,z)
denote the probability distribution of the
real-valued rewards
obtained when action
z
is taken in state
x
.
We assume
for simplicity that rewards are almost surely bounded over all states and actions, i.e.,
there exists a constant
M
such that
R([-M,M]|x,z)=1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X},z\in Z
. We let
r(x,z)=\int yR(dy|x,z)
denote the expected reward when action
z
is used in state
x
.
For any time
t
and any sequences
\{X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{t-1}=x_{t-1},X_{t}=x\}
of states and
\{Z_{1}=z_{1},\ldots,Z_{t-1}=z_{t-1},Z_{t}=z\}
of actions, we assume the reward obtained at time
t
, denoted
Y_{t}
, is distributed according to
R(\cdot|x,z)
, and is conditionally independent of all other randomness (including the past history) given
X_{t}
and
Z_{t}
.

2.2
Estimand and experimental design

In this section, we describe our estimand as well as a Bernoulli randomized experimental design to compare treatment and control. As we will see, these are naturally modeled through the viewpoint of Markov policies from the theory of Markov decision processes (MDPs).

Markov policies: Treatment, control, and Bernoulli randomization.

Informally, Markov policies are those that choose actions based only on the current state (and not based on history); they can be deterministic or randomized. Since we consider
\mathcal{Z}=\{0,1\}
(binary treatment), any Markov policy can be specified by the probability it chooses
z=1
in each state
x
.

In this paper, we consider a set of Markov policies parameterized by
\theta\in[0,1]
. Given
\theta
, the policy
\pi_{\theta}
chooses
z=1
(resp.,
z=0
) with probability
\theta
(resp.,
1-\theta
) in every state
x
, independent of all other randomness. Note that
\pi_{1}
is the policy that always chooses
z=1
, which we refer to as the
treatment
policy; analogously,
\pi_{0}
is the policy that always chooses
z=0
, which we refer to as the
control
policy. For any
\theta
such that
0<\theta<1
, the policy assigns treatment in each time step according to an i.i.d. Bernoulli(
\theta
) random variable, so we refer to such a
\pi_{\theta}
as a
Bernoulli randomization
policy.

We denote
P_{\theta}^{t}
as the transition kernel under the Bernoulli randomization policy
\pi_{\theta}
at time
t
, i.e.,
P_{\theta}^{t}=\theta P_{1}^{t}+(1-\theta)P_{0}^{t}
.
Similarly, we denote
r_{\theta}(x)
as the expected reward in state
x
under policy
\pi_{\theta}
, i.e.,
r_{\theta}(x)=\theta r(x,1)+(1-\theta)r(x,0)
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{t}
as the marginal distribution of the outcome
Y_{t}
under
\pi_{\theta}
, i.e.:

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{t}=\operatorname{Law}\left(Y_{t}\mid X_{1}\sim\rho,\,Z_{u}\sim\text{i.i.d.~Bernoulli}(\theta)\text{ for all }1\leqslant u\leqslant t\right).

(1)

Remark 2
.

Together with the specification above, given an initial distribution for the state
X_{1}
, every policy
\pi_{\theta}
induces a Markov process as follows: at each time
t=1,\ldots,T
, the action is chosen according to
Z_{t}\sim\textrm{Bernoulli}(\theta)
, and the next state is then chosen according to
X_{t+1}\sim P_{Z_{t}}^{t}(X_{t},\cdot)
. Thus our specification above is a special case of a nonstationary Markov decision process (MDP), with respect to the restricted set of policies defined above; since the transition dynamics vary arbitrarily over time, in general, no stationary distribution will exist for the resulting process (see, e.g.,
Lecarpentier and Rachelson 2019
,
Cheung et al. 2020
,
Hu and Wager 2022
).

Estimand: Global Average Treatment Effect (GATE).

Given an initial state distribution
X_{1}\sim\rho
, recall from (
1
) that
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{t}
(resp.,
\mathcal{L}_{0}^{t}
) represents the marginal distribution of the outcome
Y_{t}
at time
t
under the treatment policy
\pi_{1}
(resp., under the control policy
\pi_{0}
).
With these laws in hand, the treatment effect at time
t
, denoted by
\tau_{t}
, is defined as
\tau_{t}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}
, and the global average treatment effect (GATE)
\tau
over the time horizon
[T]
is then defined as

\tau:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tau_{t}.

Remark 3
.

The estimand
\tau
represents the average treatment effect over the specific time horizon
[T]
under the initial state distribution
\rho
.
In general, since the underlying process follows a nonstationary MDP,
\tau
does not converge to a stationary value.
Nevertheless, estimating
\tau
remains meaningful, as it provides insight into the treatment effect over the experimental horizon—an interval that is typical of intrinsic interest and may be repeated in future deployments (see, e.g.,
Hu and Wager 2022
,
Wu et al. 2024b
).

Data generation and estimation.

The experiment starts from an initial state
X_{1}\sim\rho
, evolves under a uniform Bernoulli randomization policy
\pi_{1/2}
over the time horizon
[T]
, and generates a single data trajectory
{\left\{{(X_{t},Z_{t},Y_{t}):1\leqslant t\leqslant T}\right\}}
. An
estimator
(computed at time
T
) is then any random variable adapted to
\sigma{\left({(X_{t},Z_{t},Y_{t}):1\leqslant t\leqslant T}\right)}
.

3
Estimation under nonstationarity

A naive approach to estimating
\tau
under a Bernoulli randomization policy
\pi_{1/2}
is to use the following difference-in-means (DM) estimator:

\hat{\tau}_{0}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{u=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1\left\{Z_{u}=1\right\}}{1/2}-\frac{1\left\{Z_{u}=0\right\}}{1/2}\right)Y_{t}.

(2)

The DM estimator is unbiased and exhibits low variance when the observations are i.i.d.
(
Horvitz and Thompson 1952
,
Robins et al. 1994
)
. However, in dynamic (e.g., Markovian) environments, the DM estimator has been shown to incur substantial bias due to temporal
interference
across observations
(
Glynn et al. 2020
,
Farias et al. 2022
,
Hu and Wager 2022
)
. Consequently, a variety of estimators have been developed to correct for temporal interference in dynamic settings, including the recently proposed difference-in-Q’s (DQ) estimators
(
Farias et al. 2022
,
Farias et al. 2023
)
and estimators based on off-policy evaluation (OPE) techniques
(
Shi et al. 2023
,
Uehara et al. 2022
)
. Despite these advances, most bias-corrected estimators still rely on stationarity assumptions and can fail in nonstationary environments, where bias correction is inherently more challenging.

A novel estimator: Truncated difference-in-Q’s (DQ).

To address the challenge of estimation under nonstationarity, we propose an estimator that naturally extends the naive DM estimator and, perhaps surprisingly, mitigates temporal interference bias even in nonstationary Markovian environments. Our approach is straightforward to implement: instead of adding up only the immediate outcome
Y_{t}
at each time
t
as in (
2
), we sum the subsequent outcomes over a window of size
k
. Formally, for each truncation size
0\leqslant k\leqslant T-1
, we define the estimator:

\hat{\tau}_{k}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{u=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1\left\{Z_{u}=1\right\}}{1/2}-\frac{1\left\{Z_{u}=0\right\}}{1/2}\right)\sum_{t=u}^{\min(t+k,T)}Y_{t}.

(3)

Note that when
k=0
, the estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
reduces to the naive DM estimator, which completely ignores the nonstationary Markovian dynamics of the environment. In contrast, when
k\geqslant 1
, the estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
captures the Markovian dynamics by incorporating short-term future outcomes into the estimation. We refer to
\hat{\tau}_{k}
as the
truncated difference-in-Q’s (DQ) estimator
with truncation size
k
; the name is chosen because when the truncation size is
k=T
, the estimator becomes the Monte-Carlo DQ estimator introduced in
Farias et al. 2023
.

Remark 4
.

The truncated DQ estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
does not require access to the states, offering a practical advantage over estimators that depend on full state observability (e.g., OPE estimators
(
Uehara et al. 2022
)
). Similar properties have been established for the Monte-Carlo DQ estimator in stationary environments
(
Farias et al. 2023
)
.
Moreover, when the data-generating process is i.i.d. conditional on the current action,
\hat{\tau}_{k}
provides an unbiased estimation of
\tau
under any truncation size
k
.

The main results of this paper establish bias and variance bounds for the truncated DQ estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
in nonstationary Markovian environments, as formalized in Theorem
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, the bias of the truncated DQ estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
with respect to the global average treatment effect
\tau
, for any truncation size
0\leqslant k\leqslant T-1
, is bounded by

{\left\lvert{\mathbb{E}{\left[{\hat{\tau}_{k}}\right]}-\tau}\right\rvert}\leqslant\mathcal{O}\left(k^{2}\delta^{2}M+\frac{\gamma^{k}}{1-\gamma}\delta M\right),

(4)

where
\delta
is the kernel deviation, i.e., the maximum total variance distance between the transition kernels across all states and times:

\delta:=\sup_{1\leqslant t\leqslant T}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|P_{1}^{t}(x,\cdot)-P_{0}^{t}(x,\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

Additionally, the variance of
\hat{\tau}_{k}
is bounded by

\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{k})\leqslant\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{(k+1)^{3}M^{2}}{T}+\frac{\gamma(k+1)^{2}\,M^{2}}{T(1-\gamma)}\right).

(5)

Theorem
1
shows that the bias and variance of
\hat{\tau}_{k}
is jointly governed by the truncation size
k
, the mixing rate
\gamma
, and the kernel deviation
\delta
. In particular, since the DM estimator corresponds to
k=0
, the theorem demonstrates that the bias reduction benefit of the truncated DQ estimator with
k>0
depends on the relationship between
\delta
,
\gamma
, and
k
. By rearranging, we see that the truncated DQ estimator has an advantage for treatment effects that are small relative to the choice of truncation, i.e., when
\delta<(1-\gamma^{k})/((1-\gamma)k^{2})
. This bound reveals that the advantage over DM grows when
\gamma
is smaller as well.

We derive this theorem over the next two sections. We start in Section
4
by first providing an analysis of the untruncated DQ estimator, i.e., the case
k=T
, in the nonstationary environment described in Section
2
. In particular, we present a novel interpretation of the DQ estimator: we show that one can view the untruncated DQ estimator as an estimator of a
policy gradient
evaluated at
\pi_{1/2}
. The policy gradient can be viewed as a first order Taylor approximation to the GATE (i.e., the difference in policy value for
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{0}
). In this way we obtain a bias bound for the untruncated DQ estimator in the nonstationary setting that (1) is dominated by the Taylor error; and (2) unfortunately, scales quadratically in
T
due to nonstationarity.

Our analysis of the truncated DQ estimator is then carried out in a parallel manner, and shows this poor bias behavior can be mitigated. We show in particular in Section
5
that the truncated DQ estimator can
also
be viewed as an estimator of a hypothetical “truncated” policy gradient, that estimates the difference between two policies: first playing
\pi_{1/2}
, and then playing
\pi_{1}
or
\pi_{0}
respectively in the most recent
k
time periods. Because we approximate this gradient using the truncated DQ estimator, a Taylor approximation error is again incurred, similar to the untruncated DQ estimator; this is the first term in the bias bound (
4
) in the theorem, which we refer to as the
Taylor error
. The second term in the bias bound arises by bounding the gap between the hypothetical truncated policy gradient and the GATE, taking advantage of the mixing time assumption Assumption
1
; thus we refer to the second term as the
mixing bias
. Crucially, we observe that due to truncation, the estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
has a bias that scales quadratically in
k
, rather than
T
as for the untruncated estimator.

The theorem further shows that the variance of
\hat{\tau}_{k}
grows cubically with
k
while diminishing as the horizon length
T
becomes larger.
A formal proof Theorem
1
is provided in the appendix.

4
The untruncated DQ estimator: A policy gradient viewpoint

As a starting point in our analysis, we consider the untruncated DQ estimator, i.e., the estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
in (
14
) with
k=T
. We show that this estimator can be naturally interpreted as an approximate policy gradient, and use this interpretation to quantify its bias via a Taylor approximation argument. We start with some preliminary definitions.

Value function.

Define the
value function
under the Bernoulli randomized policy
\pi_{\theta}
as the average outcome over the horizon
[T]
, i.e., in our setting with nonstationary Markovian environments,

J(\theta):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}.

(6)

Note with this definition that the GATE
\tau
is equal to
J(1)-J(0)
.

We then define the
Q
-value of taking action
z
at time
t
under the experimental policy
\pi_{1/2}
as the expected cumulative reward from time
t
to
T
.
Formally, we write:

Q_{{1/2}}^{t}(z):=\sum_{u=t}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}\left[Y_{u}\mid Z_{t}=z\right].

(7)

Note that this definition of the
Q
-value is
state-independent
. Unlike the standard
Q
-value in infinite-horizon stationary MDPs that depends on the state-action pair, our definition reflects the expected value from taking action
z
at time
t
, conditioned on the initial state distribution
X_{1}\sim\rho
.

Proposition 1
.

The policy gradient of
J(\theta)
in (
6
) evaluated at the uniform randomized policy
\pi_{1/2}
exists and is given by the average difference in
Q
-values defined in (
7
) over the horizon, i.e.,

\nabla J(1/2)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(Q_{1/2}^{t}(1)-Q_{1/2}^{t}(0)\right).

(8)

Proposition
1
shows that the policy gradient
\nabla J(1/2)
corresponds to the average
Q
-value difference over the horizon
[T]
.

2

2

In general stationary MDPs,
J(\theta)
is known to be smooth under both average
-
reward and discounted
-
reward formulations (see
Sutton 2018
); our theorem shows it remains smooth for finite-horizon non
-
stationary MDPs.
In fact, the difference-in-Q’s (DQ) estimator proposed in
Farias et al. 2022
can be interpreted as a policy gradient in stationary average-reward MDPs (see the appendix for a detailed discussion).

Along these lines, the key step in our analysis of the untruncated DQ estimator
\hat{\tau}_{T}
is to view it as an estimator for the policy gradient
\nabla J(1/2)
in the nonstationary setting.
Applying a Taylor expansion around
J(1/2)
, the bias of
\hat{\tau}_{T}
relative to
\tau
decomposes as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}_{T}\right]-\tau

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}_{T}\right]-\nabla J(1/2)+\nabla J(1/2)-(J(1)-J(0))

\displaystyle=\underbrace{{\left({\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}_{T}\right]-\nabla J(1/2)}\right)}}_{\text{gradient estimation bias}}+\underbrace{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\nabla^{n}J(1/2)\left[(-1/2)^{n}-(1/2)^{n}\right]}_{\text{Taylor remainder}},

(9)

where the Taylor error corresponds to the higher-order bias in approximating
\tau
using the policy gradient
\nabla J(1/2)
.
Now observe by elementary calculation that the untruncated DQ estimator is
unbiased
for the policy gradient
\nabla J(1/2)
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\tau}_{T}]=\nabla J(1/2)
. Therefore the entire bias is due to the Taylor error. The following proposition provides a general bound on this bias.

Proposition 2
.

The bias of
\hat{\tau}_{T}
with respect to the GATE
\tau
is bounded by

{\left\lvert{\mathbb{E}{\left[{\hat{\tau}_{T}}\right]}-\tau}\right\rvert}\leqslant\mathcal{O}{\left({T^{2}\delta^{2}M+T\delta M}\right)}.

(10)

(In fact, the preceding bound applies for any unbiased estimator
\hat{\tau}
of
\nabla J(1/2)
.)
Moreover, the variance of
\hat{\tau}_{T}
is bounded as
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{T})\leqslant\mathcal{O}{\left({T^{2}M^{2}}\right)}
.

As we can see from the preceding result, the untruncated DQ estimator exhibits both bias and variance with quadratic scaling in the full horizon
T
, which is undesirable when
T
is large. In the next section, we carry out a parallel analysis of the truncated DQ estimator (
14
) with general
k
, and show how we can leverage the mixing time assumption
1
to obtain more favorable bounds on bias and variance.

5
Estimation via truncated policy gradient

In this section, we introduce the truncated policy gradient in nonstationary MDPs and establish its connection to the truncated DQ estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
.
Truncation is a well-established idea in the reinforcement learning and off-policy evaluation literature
(
Thomas and Brunskill 2016
,
Su et al. 2020
,
Poiani et al. 2023
,
Hu and Wager 2023
)
.
These approaches are typically viewed as bias-variance trade-offs: they introduce additional bias, but typically reduce variance, both due to the truncation.
In nonstationary MDPs with the mixing-time property, we show that
\hat{\tau}_{k}
yields variance reduction over
\hat{\tau}_{T}
, as expected; but in addition, truncation
also
can reduce bias relative to using the full trajectory, due to nonstationarity.

We begin by defining the truncated policy value function as follows.

Truncated policy value function.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{t,k}
denote the distribution of the outcome
Y_{t}
when policy
\pi_{1/2}
is followed for the first
(t-k)
states (if
t>k
), and policy
\pi_{\theta}
is followed for the remaining
k
states (i.e., a truncated policy), conditioned on the initial state distribution
X_{1}\sim\rho
, we have:

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{t,k}=\operatorname{Law}\left(Y_{t}\mid X_{1}\sim\rho,\,\{Z_{u}\}_{u=1}^{t-k}\sim\text{i.i.d.~Bern}(1/2),\,\{Z_{u}\}_{u=t-k+1}^{t}\sim\text{i.i.d.~Bern}(\theta)\right).

(11)

The
truncated policy value function

J_{k}(\theta)
is then defined as

J_{k}(\theta):=\frac{1}{T}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{t,k}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}}=\frac{1}{T}{\left({\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}+\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{t,k}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}}\right)}.

(12)

We refer to the gradient of
J_{k}(\theta)
as the
truncated policy gradient
.
For truncation size
0\leqslant k\leqslant T-1
, define the
truncated
Q
-value
as

Q_{{1/2}}^{t,k}(z):=\sum_{u=t}^{\min(t+k,T)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}\left[Y_{u}\mid Z_{t}=z\right].

(13)

Proposition 3
.

The truncated policy gradient
\nabla J_{k}(\theta)
evaluated at the uniform randomized policy
\pi_{1/2}
exists and is given by the average difference in truncated
Q
-values in (
13
) over the horizon, i.e.,

\nabla J_{k}(1/2)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(Q_{1/2}^{t,k}(1)-Q_{1/2}^{t,k}(0)\right).

Moreover, observe that with the truncated DQ estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
defined in (
3
), we have

\mathbb{E}{\left[{\hat{\tau}_{k}}\right]}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(Q_{{1/2}}^{t,k}(1)-Q_{{1/2}}^{t,k}(0)\right)=\nabla J_{k}(1/2).

(14)

This indicates that the truncated DQ estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
can be viewed as an estimation of
\tau
via the truncated policy gradient
\nabla J_{k}(1/2)
. Accordingly, the bias of
\hat{\tau}_{k}
relative to
\tau
decomposes as

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}_{k}\right]-\tau=\underbrace{\nabla J_{k}(1/2)-(J_{k}(1)-J_{k}(0))}_{\text{{Taylor error w.r.t. }}J_{k}(1/2)}+\underbrace{(J_{k}(1)-J(1))+(J(0)-J_{k}(0))}_{\text{mixing bias}},

which includes a Taylor error between
\nabla J_{k}(1/2)
and
J_{k}(1)-J_{k}(0)
, and an additional mixing bias between the original value function
J(\cdot)
and its truncated counterpart
J_{k}(\cdot)
. We note that the Taylor error is of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
, as the treatment probability affects only the most recent
k
states rather than the full trajectory (cf. the
\mathcal{O}(T^{2})
bias of
\hat{\tau}
).

\nabla J(1/2)

\hat{\tau}

\hat{\tau}_{k}
. Specifically, the bias of the truncated DQ estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
involves an additional mixing bias but reduces the Taylor error.
Additionally, the variance of
\hat{\tau}_{k}
can be bounded by applying the mixing-time property in Assumption
1
within a strong
\alpha
-mixing framework
(
Rosenblatt 1956
)
.
Unlike the variance bound for
\hat{\tau}
that grows with
T
, the variance of
\hat{\tau}_{k}
remains bounded in the asymptotic regime as
T\rightarrow\infty
for any fixed truncation size
k
.

Remark 5
.

When the mixing rate and kernel deviation are unknown a priori, the choice of truncation size
k
might be nontrivial.
Post-calibration methods, such as Lepski’s method
(
Lepski et al. 1997
)
, can be used to select
k
in a data-driven manner.
As a simple practical guideline, we recommend starting with the standard DM estimator (
k=0
) and then incrementally increasing
k
(e.g., to 1, 3, 5, etc.). As shown in our numerical results, in most cases increasing
k
corrects the bias of the DM estimator and moves the estimate closer to the ground-truth GATE.

We further adapt a large-scale NYC ride-sharing simulator using real-world data from

. Rider arrivals and destination locations are generated based on historical records provided by New York City’s Taxi and Limousine Commission

In this experiment, mirroring common practice in industry, we implement a Bernoulli switchback design, in which each fixed time interval (e.g., 10 or 20 minutes) is independently assigned to treatment or control with probability

. The system state for each interval is defined as the average number of available drivers.
Figure

presents the results of various estimators for this setting. Our first finding is that the DM estimator can perform well but is highly sensitive to the choice of switchback interval. For example, intervals of 10, 20, and 30 minutes all exhibit large bias, while the 60-minute interval performs well. This means the experimenter must identify the appropriate interval in advance. In contrast, the truncated DQ estimators (which are all extensions of DM)—consistently reduce bias across a wide range of switchback intervals. This suggests that truncated DQ can serve as an effective postprocessing tool to compensate for imperfect interval selection during the design phase, at the cost of slightly increased variance. We also evaluated several state-of-the-art estimators for switchback experiments. Under the 1-hour interval, the BC estimator offers no improvement over the naive DM and shows slight overestimation when the burn-in period
b
is set to 10 or 20 minutes. Meanwhile, the stationary DQ and OPE estimators struggle to learn reliable stationary
Q

This case study also demonstrates that truncated DQ estimators can be applied not only to the naive Bernoulli A/B design but also to more general designs, such as the Bernoulli switchback design. This provides additional flexibility for using truncated DQ in practical systems. It also raises an important question regarding how to co-design the experiment interval (which controls the mixing rate
\gamma
) and the truncation size

k
together to optimize treatment effect estimation. We leave this as a promising open question for future research.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced the truncated DQ estimator for estimation of the GATE in nonstationary Markovian settings; the truncated DQ estimator computes differences in
k
-step reward aggregates. We showed both theoretically and empirically that, under mild mixing-time conditions, this estimator achieves low bias and variance in single-trajectory, nonstationary Markov environments, without requiring prior knowledge of the state space or multiple independent runs.

Limitations include: (1) the need for a data-driven selection of the truncation length
k
; (2) possible future extension to
include pre-treatment covariates, including doubly robust estimation; and (3) future work for a formal comparison to alternative (e.g., adaptive) experimental designs.

Appendix A
Notation and technical lemmas

In this section, we present the key notation along with several technical results and lemmas used throughout the proofs.

A.1
Time-varying transition kernels

We denote by
P_{\theta}^{a\rightarrow b}
the transition kernel induced by the Bernoulli-randomized policy
\pi_{\theta}
from time
a
to time
b
for
1\leqslant a\leqslant b\leqslant T
, defined as

P_{\theta}^{a\rightarrow b}:=\begin{cases}\prod_{u=a}^{b-1}P_{\theta}^{u},&\text{if }a<b,\\
I,&\text{if }a=b,\end{cases}

where
I
denotes the identity matrix. Additionally, note that
P_{\theta}^{a\rightarrow a+1}=P_{\theta}^{a}
for all
a\in[T]
.

A.2
Time-varying kernel deviations

For each time step
t\in[T]
, we define the difference in transition kernels as
\Delta^{t}:=P_{1}^{t}-P_{0}^{t}
, where
P_{1}^{t}
and
P_{0}^{t}
are the transition kernels under the always-treated and always-controlled policies, respectively. We quantify the maximum deviation across time and states using the worst-case total variation norm:

\delta^{t}:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|P_{1}^{t}(x,\cdot)-P_{0}^{t}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}},\quad\delta:=\sup_{1\leqslant t\leqslant T}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|P_{1}^{t}(x,\cdot)-P_{0}^{t}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

Additionally, we define the deviations from the uniform randomization policy
\pi_{1/2}
at each time
t
as
\Delta_{+}^{t}:=P_{1}^{t}-P_{1/2}^{t}
and
\Delta_{-}^{t}:=P_{0}^{t}-P_{1/2}^{t}
, where
P_{1/2}^{t}=(P_{1}^{t}+P_{0}^{t})/2
. It follows directly that

\sup_{1\leqslant t\leqslant T}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\Delta_{+}^{t}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\sup_{1\leqslant t\leqslant T}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\Delta_{-}^{t}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\sup_{1\leqslant t\leqslant T}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\Delta^{t}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\,\leqslant\,\delta.

A.3
Derivative of the transition kernels

Recall that under the Bernoulli-randomized policy
\pi_{\theta}
, the transition kernel, and expected reward at time
t
is given by

P_{\theta}^{t}=(1-\theta)P_{0}^{t}+\theta P_{1}^{t},\quad r_{\theta}=(1-\theta)r_{0}+\theta r_{1}.

Differentiating with respect to the treatment probability
\theta
, we have

\frac{d}{d\theta}P_{\theta}^{t}=P_{1}^{t}-P_{0}^{t}=\Delta^{t},\quad\frac{d}{d\theta}r_{\theta}=r_{1}-r_{0}.

By applying the product rule for matrix-valued functions for all
1\leqslant a<b\leqslant T
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\theta}P_{\theta}^{a\rightarrow b}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\theta}\prod_{u=a}^{b-1}P_{\theta}^{u}=\sum_{u=a}^{b-1}\left(\prod_{v=a}^{u-1}P_{\theta}^{v}\right)\left(\frac{d}{d\theta}P_{\theta}^{u}\right)\left(\prod_{v=u+1}^{b-1}P_{\theta}^{v}\right)=\sum_{u=a}^{b-1}P_{\theta}^{a\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow b},

and
\frac{d}{d\theta}P_{\theta}^{a\rightarrow a}=0
for all
a\in[T]
.

Then the process
\{\Gamma_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is
\alpha
-mixing with the mixing coefficient for any lag
h\geqslant 1
is

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Gamma}(h)

\displaystyle=\sup_{1\leqslant s\leqslant T-h}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\sigma(\Gamma_{1:s})\\
B\in\sigma(\Gamma_{s+h:T})\end{subarray}}\bigl|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{1\leqslant s\leqslant T-h}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{F}_{1}^{s+k}\\
B\in\mathcal{F}_{s+h}^{T}\end{subarray}}\bigl|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)\bigr|=\alpha\bigl(h-k\bigr)\leqslant\gamma^{\max\{0,h-k-1\}}.

Appendix B
DQ estimator in an average-reward MDP

In
Farias et al. 2022
, a
difference-in-Q’s
(DQ) estimator is proposed to estimate the average treatment effect (ATE) in a stationary, average-reward Markovian setting. This approach was later extended to finite-horizon and discounted-reward settings in
Farias et al. 2023
. Recall that in an average-reward MDP, for a state
x
and action
z
, the
Q
-value
Q(x,z|\theta)
is defined as the
excess
reward over the long-run average reward obtained starting from state
x
with initial action
z
, under policy
\pi_{\theta}
. The DQ estimator assumes access to an unbiased estimator of the
Q
-function
Q(\cdot|\theta)
and computes the difference in steady-state
Q
-values between the treatment and control actions.
The DQ estimator has been shown to achieve a favorable bias-variance trade-off in stationary MDPs, especially when
Q
-values can be efficiently estimated without bias (e.g., with LSTD-
\lambda

(
Bradtke and Barto 1996
)
).
By the policy gradient theorem for stationary average-reward MDPs
(
Sutton 2018
)
, when
J(\cdot)
denotes the steady-state value function, its gradient
\nabla J(\theta)
can be written in terms of the policy gradient, which in our case (since actions are binary) can be expressed as a difference in
Q
-values. Thus we can view the DQ estimator as a form of policy-gradient estimator in the stationary setting. We demonstrate this in detail below.

Policy gradient in an average-reward MDP.

In an average-reward MDP, the
Q
-value represents the cumulative excess reward of a state-action pair
(x,z)
relative to the long-run average reward under a Bernoulli-randomized policy
\pi_{\theta}
. Specifically, it is defined as

Q(x,z|\theta):=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left(Y_{i}-\lambda_{\theta}\right)\mid X_{1}=x,Z_{1}=z\right],

where
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}
denotes the expectation under policy
\pi_{\theta}
, and
\lambda_{\theta}
denotes the long-run average return under policy
\pi_{\theta}
, i.e.,
\lambda_{\theta}:=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}{\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{T}Y_{i}}\right]}
. In this setting, the
Q
-value depends on the state and action, but no longer on time.

Under the ergodicity assumption, let
\rho_{\theta}
denote the stationary distribution of the states induced by policy
\pi_{\theta}
. The steady-state value function
J(\theta)
is then defined as

J(\theta):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\rho_{\theta}(x)\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\pi_{\theta}(z|x)r(x,z).

Then, the average treatment effect (ATE) in
Farias et al. 2022
is defined as
\tau:=J(1)-J(0)
.
By the policy gradient theorem for average-reward MDPs (see Page 333 in
Sutton 2018
), we have

\displaystyle\nabla J(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\rho_{\theta}(x)\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\nabla\pi_{\theta}(z|x)Q(x,z|\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\rho_{\theta}(x)\left(Q(x,1|\theta)-Q(x,0|\theta)\right),

which corresponds exactly to the DQ estimator proposed in
Farias et al. 2022
when
\theta=1/2
.

Proof of Theorem

The key idea here is to estimate the global treatment effect at each time using the transition kernel under the experimental policy
\pi_{1/2}

Recall that the treatment effect at each time

\tau_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}.

We begin by approximating
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}^{t}}[Y_{t}]
using the transition dynamics under the experimental policy
\pi_{1/2}
for each time step
t
. Applying the telescoping expansion for the difference of two matrix products, for any
1\leqslant a<b\leqslant T

\displaystyle\rho^{\top}P_{1}^{a\rightarrow b}-\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{a\rightarrow b}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\top}\prod_{u=a}^{b-1}P_{1}^{u}-\rho^{\top}\prod_{u=a}^{b-1}P_{1/2}^{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u=a}^{b-1}\rho^{\top}\left({\prod_{v=a}^{u-1}P_{1}^{v}}\right){\left({P_{1}^{u}-P_{1/2}^{u}}\right)}\left({\prod_{v=u+1}^{b-1}P_{1/2}^{v}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u=a}^{b-1}\rho^{\top}P_{1}^{a\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}_{+}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow b}.

Specifically, by Assumption

\left\|\rho^{\top}P_{1}^{a\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}_{+}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow b}\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leqslant\gamma^{b-u-1}\left\|\rho^{\top}P_{1}^{a\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}_{+}\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leqslant\gamma^{b-u-1}\delta.

Then, it follows that

\rho^{\top}P_{1}^{a\rightarrow b}-\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{a\rightarrow b}\leqslant\sum_{u=a}^{b-1}\gamma^{b-u-1}\delta=\sum_{i=0}^{b-a-1}\gamma^{i}\delta=\frac{(1-\gamma^{b-a})\delta}{1-\gamma}\leqslant\frac{\delta}{1-\gamma}.

Additionally, by a first-order perturbation expansion, we have for any
1\leqslant a<b\leqslant T

\displaystyle P_{1}^{a\rightarrow b}=\prod_{u=a}^{b-1}P_{1}^{u}=\prod_{u=a}^{b-1}\left(P_{1/2}^{u}+\Delta_{+}^{u}\right)=P_{1/2}^{a\rightarrow b}+\sum_{u=a}^{b-1}P_{1/2}^{a\rightarrow u}\Delta_{+}^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow b}+\mathcal{O}\left((b-a)^{2}\delta^{2}\right).

We are now ready to approximate
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}^{t}}[Y_{t}]

\pi_{1/2}
. Specifically, we consider two cases separately: (i) when

and (ii) when

Case (i): when

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\top}P_{1}^{1\rightarrow t}r_{1}-\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow(t-k)}P_{1}^{(t-k)\rightarrow t}r_{1}+\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow(t-k)}P_{1}^{(t-k)\rightarrow t}r_{1}

Moreover, the remainder term can be written as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}=\,\,\,}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow(t-k)}P_{1}^{(t-k)\rightarrow t}r_{1}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow(t-k)}\prod_{u=t-k}^{t-1}\left(P_{1/2}^{u}+\Delta_{+}^{u}\right)r_{1}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow(t-k)}\left(P_{1/2}^{(t-k)\rightarrow t}+\sum_{u=t-k}^{t-1}P_{1/2}^{(t-k)\rightarrow u}\Delta_{+}^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}\right)r_{1}+\mathcal{O}\left(k^{2}\delta^{2}M\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow t}r_{1}+\sum_{u=t-k}^{t-1}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta_{+}^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{1}+\mathcal{O}\left(k^{2}\delta^{2}M\right).

Therefore, for all

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}=\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow t}r_{1}+\sum_{u=t-k}^{t-1}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta_{+}^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{1}+\mathcal{O}\left(k^{2}\delta^{2}M+\frac{\gamma^{k}}{1-\gamma}\delta M\right).

Case (ii): when

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}=\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow t}r_{1}+\sum_{u=1}^{t-1}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta_{+}^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}+\mathcal{O}\left(k^{2}\delta^{2}M\right).

Combining Cases (i) and (ii), we conclude

\displaystyle\Delta_{-}^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{0}

Additionally, noting that

\displaystyle A_{u}

\displaystyle=\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta_{+}^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{1}-\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta_{-}^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{0}

Putting those together, we obtain

\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{t}-\phi_{0}^{t}=\sum_{u=\max(t-k,1)}^{t}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}\left[Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=1\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}\left[Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=0\right]\right).

Summing over the horizon
[T]
, we observe

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\phi_{1}^{t}-\phi_{0}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{u=\max(t-k,1)}^{t}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}\left[Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=1\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}\left[Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=0\right]\right)

where the final equality follows from Proposition

\displaystyle={\left\lvert{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(Q_{{1/2}}^{t,k}(1)-Q_{{1/2}}^{t,k}(0)\right)-\tau}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle={\left\lvert{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\phi_{1}^{t}-\phi_{0}^{t}\right)-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\left({\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{0}^{t}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}}\right)}}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(k^{2}\delta^{2}M+\frac{\gamma^{k}}{1-\gamma}\delta M\right).

We now prove the variance bound.

For any truncation size
0\leqslant k\leqslant T-1
, recall that
\hat{\tau}_{k}
is given by

\hat{\tau}_{k}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{u=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1\left\{Z_{u}=1\right\}}{1/2}-\frac{1\left\{Z_{u}=0\right\}}{1/2}\right)\sum_{t=u}^{\min(t+k,T)}Y_{t}.

For all
1\leqslant t\leqslant T

Then, the variance of the truncated DQ estimator
\hat{\tau}_{k}
can be written as

\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{k})=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\operatorname{Var}(\Gamma_{t})+2\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant T}\operatorname{Cov}(\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j})\right).

We begin by bounding the variance term
\operatorname{Var}(\Gamma_{t})
. For any time
t\in[T]
, we observe

\operatorname{Var}(\Gamma_{t})=\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{t}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}^{2}[\Gamma_{t}],

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Gamma_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{t,k}_{1/2}(1)-Q^{t,k}_{1/2}(0)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}\left[Y_{t}\mid Z_{t}=1\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}\left[Y_{t}\mid Z_{t}=0\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{u=t+1}^{\min(t+k,T)}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{u}}\left[Y_{u}\mid Z_{t}=1\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{u}}\left[Y_{u}\mid Z_{t}=0\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow t}\left(r_{1}-r_{0}\right)+\sum_{u=t+1}^{\min(t+k,T)}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow t}\Delta^{t}P_{1/2}^{(t+1)\rightarrow u}r_{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2M+\sum_{u=t+1}^{\min(t+k,T)}\|\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow t}\Delta^{t}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\gamma^{u-t-1}M

\displaystyle\leqslant 2M+\sum_{u=t+1}^{\min(t+k,T)}\delta\gamma^{u-t-1}M

\displaystyle\leqslant 2M+\frac{\delta M}{1-\gamma},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{t}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[4\,(2Z_{t}-1)^{2}\left(\sum_{u=t}^{\min(t+k,T)}Y_{u}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=4\left[\sum_{u=t}^{\min(t+k,T)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{u}}[Y_{u}^{2}]+2\sum_{t\leqslant i<j\leqslant\min(t+k,T)}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}Y_{j}]\right].

Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{u}}[Y_{u}^{2}]=\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}(r_{1/2})^{2},

and since
Y_{i}
and
Y_{j}
are conditionally independent given
X_{i}
for all
i<j
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}Y_{j}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}Y_{j}\mid X_{i}]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{i}}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{j}}[Y_{j}\mid X_{i}]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r_{1/2}(X_{i})(P_{1/2}^{i\rightarrow j}r_{1/2})(X_{i})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow i}\right)(x)r_{1/2}(x)\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}P_{1/2}^{i\rightarrow j}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)r_{1/2}\left(x^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}D_{r_{1/2}}P_{1/2}^{i\rightarrow j}r_{1/2},

where
D_{r_{1/2}}:=\operatorname{Diag}\left(r_{1/2}(x):x\in\mathcal{X}\right)
is the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries equal to

.

Putting those together, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{t}^{2}]

\displaystyle=4\left[\sum_{u=t}^{\min(t+k,T)}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}(r_{1/2})^{2}+2\sum_{t\leqslant i<j\leqslant\min(t+k,T)}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}D_{r_{1/2}}P_{1/2}^{i\rightarrow j}r_{1/2},\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\left((k+1)M^{2}+k(k+1)M^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=4(k+1)^{2}M^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\Gamma_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{t}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}^{2}[\Gamma_{t}]\leqslant 4(k+1)^{2}M^{2}-2M-\frac{\delta M}{1-\gamma}\leqslant 4(k+1)^{2}M^{2}.

Next, we bound the covariance term
\operatorname{Cov}(\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j})
for
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant T
. By Corollary
1
, the process
\{\Gamma_{t}\}
is
\alpha
-mixing with mixing coefficients satisfying
\alpha_{\Gamma}(h)\leqslant\gamma^{\max(0,h-k-1)}
for any lag
h\geqslant 1
. Then, applying the foundational covariance bound for
\alpha
-mixing sequences in Lemma
1
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\left\lvert{\operatorname{Cov}(\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j})}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\alpha_{\Gamma}(j-i)\|\Gamma_{i}\|_{\infty}\|\Gamma_{j}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 16\gamma^{\max(0,j-i-k-1)}(k+1)^{2}M^{2}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle 2\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant T}\operatorname{Cov}(\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j})

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant T}{\left\lvert{\operatorname{Cov}(\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j})}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{d=1}^{T-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i<j\leqslant T\\
j-i=d\end{subarray}}\left|\operatorname{Cov}\left(\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{d=1}^{T-1}(T-d)\left(16\gamma^{\max(0,d-k-1)}(k+1)^{2}M^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=32\left[\sum_{d=1}^{k+1}(T-d)(k+1)^{2}M^{2}+\sum_{d=k+2}^{T-1}(T-d)(k+1)^{2}M^{2}\gamma^{d-k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 32\left[T(k+1)^{3}M^{2}+T(k+1)^{2}M^{2}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right].

Finally, we conclude the proof by noting that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\operatorname{Var}(\Gamma_{t})+2\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant T}\operatorname{Cov}(\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j})\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T}\left[4(k+1)^{2}M^{2}+32(k+1)^{3}M^{2}+\frac{32\gamma(k+1)^{2}M^{2}}{1-\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(k+1)^{3}M^{2}}{T}+\frac{\gamma(k+1)^{2}M^{2}}{T(1-\gamma)}\right).

∎

yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\theta}J(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{d}{d\theta}\rho^{\top}P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow t}r_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho^{\top}\left[\sum_{u=1}^{t-1}P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}\right]r_{\theta}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho^{\top}P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow t}\left(r_{1}-r_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\rho^{\top}P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow t}\left(r_{1}-r_{0}\right)+\sum_{u=1}^{t-1}\rho^{\top}P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{\theta}\right].

Evaluate at
\theta=1/2
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla J(1/2)

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{d\theta}J(\theta)\right|_{\theta=1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum_{u=1}^{t-1}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{1/2}+\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow t}(r_{1}-r_{0})\right).

Observe that each term
\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{1/2}
corresponds to the difference in the conditional expected outcome at time
t
between taking action
Z_{u}=1
versus
Z_{u}=0
at time
u
, under policy
\pi_{1/2}
and initial state distribution
X_{1}\sim\rho
. Thus, we can express the gradient as

\displaystyle\nabla J(1/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{u=1}^{t}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}[Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=1]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}[Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=0]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{u=1}^{T}\sum_{t=u}^{T}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}[Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=1]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}[Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=0]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{u=1}^{T}\left(Q_{1/2}^{u}(1)-Q_{1/2}^{u}(0)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(Q_{1/2}^{t}(1)-Q_{1/2}^{t}(0)\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2
.

We first show that the untruncated DQ estimator
\hat{\tau}_{T}
is an unbiased estimator of the policy gradient
\nabla J(1/2)
.
Recall that
\hat{\tau}_{T}
is defined as

\hat{\tau}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{u=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1\left\{Z_{u}=1\right\}}{1/2}-\frac{1\left\{Z_{u}=0\right\}}{1/2}\right)\sum_{t=u}^{T}Y_{t}.

Under the experimental policy
\pi_{1/2}
and initial state distribution
X_{1}\sim\rho
, taking expectation gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[{\hat{\tau}_{T}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{u=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}{\left[{\frac{1\left\{Z_{u}=1\right\}}{1/2}\sum_{t=u}^{T}Y_{t}-\frac{1\left\{Z_{u}=0\right\}}{1/2}\sum_{t=u}^{T}Y_{t}}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{u=1}^{T}{\left({\frac{\mathbb{P}{\left({Z_{u}=1}\right)}}{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}{\left[{\sum_{t=u}^{T}Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=1}\right]}-\frac{\mathbb{P}{\left({Z_{u}=0}\right)}}{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}{\left[{\sum_{t=u}^{T}Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=0}\right]}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{u=1}^{T}\left(Q_{1/2}^{u}(1)-Q_{1/2}^{u}(0)\right)=\nabla J(1/2).

where the final equality follows from Proposition
1
. Hence,
\hat{\tau}_{T}
is an unbiased estimator of the policy gradient
\nabla J(1/2)
.

We then prove the bias bound of
\hat{\tau}_{T}
. Recall that the bias of the estimator
\hat{\tau}_{T}
can be decomposed as

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}_{T}\right]-\tau={{\left({\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}_{T}\right]-\nabla J(1/2)}\right)}}+{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\nabla^{n}J(1/2)\left[(-1/2)^{n}-(1/2)^{n}\right]}.

Since
\hat{\tau}_{T}
is an unbiased estimator of the policy gradient, the first term vanishes. Thus, the remaining bias is entirely due to the higher-order Taylor remainder, which can be alternatively expressed in integral form as

{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\nabla^{n}J(1/2)\left[(-1/2)^{n}-(1/2)^{n}\right]}=\int_{0}^{1/2}\theta J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)d\theta-\int_{1/2}^{1}(1-\theta)J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)d\theta,

where
J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)=\frac{d^{2}}{d^{2}\theta}J(\theta)
denotes the second-order derivative.
Taking absolute values and applying the triangle inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle{\left\lvert{\int_{0}^{1/2}\theta J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)d\theta-\int_{1/2}^{1}(1-\theta)J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)d\theta}\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{1/2}\theta\,{\left\lvert{J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)}\right\rvert}\,d\theta+\int_{1/2}^{1}(1-\theta)\,{\left\lvert{J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)}\right\rvert}\,d\theta

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\theta\in[0,1]}{\left\lvert{J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)}\right\rvert}\,{\left({\int_{0}^{1/2}\theta d\theta+\int_{1/2}^{1}(1-\theta)d\theta}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\;\sup_{\theta\in[0,1]}{\left\lvert{J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)}\right\rvert}\,.

It remains to bound
|J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)|
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
. We have

\displaystyle J^{\prime\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{d^{2}\theta}J(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\rho^{\top}\frac{d}{d\theta}P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow t}\left(r_{1}-r_{0}\right)+\sum_{u=1}^{t-1}\rho^{\top}\frac{d}{d\theta}{\left({P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{\theta}}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\rho^{\top}\sum_{u=1}^{t-1}P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}\left(r_{1}-r_{0}\right)+\sum_{u=1}^{t-1}\rho^{\top}\frac{d}{d\theta}{\left({P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{\theta}}\right)}\right]

where

\displaystyle{\left\lvert{\rho^{\top}\sum_{u=1}^{t-1}P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}\left(r_{1}-r_{0}\right)}\right\rvert}\leqslant\sum_{u=1}^{t-1}2\delta M=2(t-1)\delta M.

Additionally, we observe

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\theta}{\left({P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{\theta}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\frac{d}{d\theta}P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\right)\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{\theta}}_{=:A}+\underbrace{P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}\left(\frac{d}{d\theta}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}\right)r_{\theta}}_{=:B}

\displaystyle\quad+\;\underbrace{P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}(r_{1}-r_{0})}_{=:C},

where

\displaystyle\bigl|\rho^{\top}A\bigr|

\displaystyle=\Biggl|\sum_{i=1}^{u-1}\rho^{\top}P_{\theta}^{1\to i}\Delta^{i}P_{\theta}^{(i+1)\to u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\to t}r_{\theta}\Biggr|\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{u-1}\delta^{2}M\leqslant(u-1)\delta^{2}M,

\displaystyle\bigl|\rho^{\top}B\bigr|

\displaystyle=\Biggl|\sum_{j=u+1}^{t-1}\rho^{\top}P_{\theta}^{1\to u}\,\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\to j}\,\Delta^{j}P_{\theta}^{(j+1)\to t}r_{\theta}\Biggr|\leqslant\sum_{j=u+1}^{t-1}\delta^{2}M\leqslant(t-u-1)\delta^{2}M,

\displaystyle\bigl|\rho^{\top}C\bigr|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\rho^{\top}P_{\theta}^{1\to u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\to t}(r_{1}-r_{0})\Bigr|\leqslant\delta\|r_{1}-r_{0}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2\delta M.

Thus, we have

\left|\sum_{u=1}^{t-1}\rho^{\top}\frac{d}{d\theta}{\left({P_{\theta}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{\theta}}\right)}\right|\leqslant(t-1)^{2}\delta^{2}M+2(t-1)\delta M.

Putting those together,

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in[0,1]}{\left\lvert{J^{\prime\prime}(v)}\right\rvert}\leqslant\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\left({(t-1)^{2}\delta^{2}M+4(t-1)\delta M}\right)}=\mathcal{O}{\left({T^{2}\delta^{2}M+T\delta M}\right)}.

Substituting back into the Taylor remainder bound, we conclude

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\tau}_{T}]-\tau\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\sup_{v\in[0,1]}|J^{\prime\prime}(v)|=\mathcal{O}(T^{2}\delta^{2}M+T\delta M),

which completes the proof of the bias bound.

We now bound the variance of
\hat{\tau}_{T}
. Observe that
\hat{\tau}_{T}
can be expressed as

\hat{\tau}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{u=1}^{T}\Gamma_{u},\quad\Gamma_{u}=(2Z_{u}-1)\sum_{t=u}^{T}Y_{t},

where

\Gamma_{u}^{2}=(2Z_{u}-1)^{2}{\left({\sum_{t=u}^{T}Y_{t}}\right)}^{2}\leqslant{\left({\sum_{t=u}^{T}M}\right)}^{2}=(T-u+1)^{2}\,M^{2}\leqslant T^{2}M^{2}.

Similarly, for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant T
, we have

{\left\lvert{\Gamma_{i}\Gamma_{j}}\right\rvert}\leqslant{\left\lvert{2Z_{i}-1}\right\rvert}{\left\lvert{2Z_{j}-1}\right\rvert}\Biggr|\sum_{t=i}^{T}Y_{t}\Biggl|\Biggr|\sum_{t=j}^{T}Y_{t}\Biggl|\leqslant(T-i+1)^{2}\,M^{2}\leqslant T^{2}M^{2}.

Therefore, the variance of
\hat{\tau}_{T}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{T})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}{\left[{\hat{\tau}_{T}^{2}}\right]}-\mathbb{E}{\left[{\hat{\tau}_{T}}\right]}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}{\left[{\frac{1}{T^{2}}{\left({\sum_{u=1}^{T}\Gamma_{u}}\right)}^{2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}{\left[{\frac{1}{T^{2}}{\left({\sum_{u=1}^{T}\Gamma_{u}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant T}{\left\lvert{\Gamma_{i}\Gamma_{j}}\right\rvert}}\right)}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T^{2}}{\left({T^{3}M^{2}+T^{4}M^{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}{\left({T^{2}M^{2}}\right)}

∎

Proof of Proposition
3
.

The truncated policy value function
J_{k}(\theta)
can be written as

J_{k}(\theta)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{t,k}}{\left[{Y_{t}}\right]}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow\max(t-k,1)}P_{\theta}^{\max(t-k,1)\rightarrow t}r_{\theta}.

Then, differentiating
J_{k}(\theta)
yields

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\,\,=}\frac{d}{d\theta}J_{k}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{d}{d\theta}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow\max(t-k,1)}P_{\theta}^{\max(t-k,1)\rightarrow t}r_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow\max(t-k,1)}\Bigg[\sum_{u=\max(t-k,1)}^{t-1}P_{\theta}^{\max(t-k,1)\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{\theta}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{\theta}

\displaystyle\hskip 199.16928pt+\;P_{\theta}^{\max(t-k,1)\rightarrow t}(r_{1}-r_{0})\Bigg].

Evaluate at
\theta=1/2
, one can observe

\displaystyle\nabla J_{k}(1/2)

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{d\theta}J_{k}(\theta)\right|_{\theta=\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow t}\left(r_{1}-r_{0}\right)+\sum_{u=\max(t-k,1)}^{t-1}\rho^{\top}P_{1/2}^{1\rightarrow u}\Delta^{u}P_{1/2}^{(u+1)\rightarrow t}r_{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{u=\max(t-k,1)}^{t}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}\left[Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=1\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{L}_{1/2}^{t}}\left[Y_{t}\mid Z_{u}=0\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(Q_{{1/2}}^{t,k}(1)-Q_{{1/2}}^{t,k}(0)\right).

∎

E.1
Two-state non-stationary MDP

In the experiment with a two-state non-stationary MDP, we consider a finite-horizon setting with state space
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}
, action space
\mathcal{Z}=\{0,1\}
, and horizon length
T=5000
. The environment evolves according to time-varying transition kernels
P_{z}^{t}
for
z\in\{0,1\}
and
t\in[T]
, which are constructed to model non-stationarity through a mean-reverting autoregressive process with additive i.i.d. Gaussian noise.
Specifically, for each state
x\in\{0,1\}
, the control transition kernel at time
t>1
is given by

P_{0}^{t}(x,\cdot)\propto\alpha P_{0}^{t-1}(x,\cdot)+(1-\alpha)\mu_{x}+\varepsilon,

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
denotes the mean reversion rate,
\mu_{x}
is both the long-run and the initial distribution of next-state transitions from state
x
, s.t.
\left\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\gamma
, and
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2})
denotes Gaussian noise. The treatment transition kernel is a shifted version of the control kernel, moving probability toward state 1, i.e.,
P_{1}^{t}(x,\cdot)\propto P_{0}^{t}(x,\cdot)\pm\delta
.
Rewards are generated from a Normal distribution, i.e.,
Y_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(r(X_{t},Z_{t}),\sigma_{r}^{2})
.

In the simulation, we set the mean reversion rate to
\alpha=0.5
, the standard deviations of the Gaussian noise and reward to
\sigma_{\epsilon}=\sigma_{r}=0.1
and the kernel deviation
\delta=0.1
. The initial distribution
\mu_{x}
is uniform randomly chosen to be either
[\frac{1}{2}(1+\gamma),\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)]
or
[\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma),\frac{1}{2}(1+\gamma)]
(one for each
x\in\{0,1\}
), ensuring a total variation distance of
\gamma
between
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. Mean rewards
r(x,z)
are randomly generated for each state-action pair
(x,z)
.
All experiments are repeated over 1,000 independent trials. Figure
6
further shows some examples under different kernel deviations
\delta
.

\delta=0.1,\gamma=0.3

(c)

\delta=0.1,\gamma=0.5

(d)

\delta=0.1,\gamma=0.7

(e)

\delta=0.3,\gamma=0.1

(f)

\delta=0.3,\gamma=0.3

(g)

\delta=0.3,\gamma=0.5

(h)

\delta=0.3,\gamma=0.7

E.2
Nonstationary queueing simulator

In this section we detail our nonstationary queueing simulator, based on a nonstationary estimate of patient arrival rates to an emergency department (both based on day of week, and time of day), obtained using data from the SEEStat Home Hospital database
(
SEE-Center 2013
)
; see Figure 9, Section 5.2, and Appendix B.1 of
Li et al. 2023
for further details.

The patient arrival rates are estimated using data from the Home Hospital database on admissions to an Israeli hospital emergency department over 2004, for every half hour of each day, at the day-of-week level (Sunday-Saturday). Let
b_{d,t}
denote this estimated arrival rate at time
t
, on a day of week
d
. We simulate a four-week experiment, similar to
Li et al. 2023
. The arrival rate to the queueing system in week
w=1,2,3,4
, on day of week
d
, and time
t
, in state
k
, is specified as:

\lambda_{w,d,t,k}=\frac{8-4p}{1+k/5}a_{w}b_{d,t},

where
a_{1}=0.9,a_{2}=1.0,a_{3}=1.1,a_{4}=1.2
. The service rate is
\mu=20
in all states. In treatment,
p=1.75
; in control,
p=0.25
.

To simulate the system and map it to the discrete-time setting in our paper, we
uniformize
time by considering one-minute time steps. This gives rise to
T=40,320
time steps per simulation. We run 500 simulations of each type of experiment (Bernoulli randomized experiment, and switchback experiment).
In the switchback experiment, we set the switchback interval to one hour (i.e., 60 time steps), and switch between treatment and control with probability
1/2
at each interval, following the Bernoulli switchback design of
Hu and Wager 2022
.

E.3
NYC ride-sharing simulator

We adopt a variant of the large-scale NYC ride-sharing simulator from
Peng et al. 2025
for the pricing experiment. The system observes a sequence of “eyeballs” (i.e., potential ride requests), where each rider specifies pickup and dropoff locations and is shown a posted price along with an estimated time of arrival (ETA).
Based on this information, the rider decides whether to accept the trip. If accepted, the nearest available vehicle is dispatched and the system collects the trip fare as a reward; if rejected, no dispatch occurs and the reward is zero. The rider’s acceptance behavior follows a simple logistic choice model:

\mathbb{P}(\text{accept})=\sigma\left(w_{\text{price}}\cdot\text{price}+w_{\text{eta}}\cdot\text{ETA}+w_{0}\right),

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function. Specifically, we set
w_{\text{price}}=-0.3,w_{\text{eta}}=-0.005
, and
w_{0}=4
. The price is computed as
\text{price}=\alpha\cdot\tau(\text{pickup location},\text{dropoff location})
, where
\tau
denotes the travel time matrix derived from real Manhattan traffic data. The ETA is computed as the driver’s earliest arrival time at the pickup location minus the request time.
The pricing coefficient
\alpha
is set to
0.01
under the control policy and
0.02
under the treatment policy. A similar modeling setup is described in Section 6.3 of
Peng et al. 2025
.
