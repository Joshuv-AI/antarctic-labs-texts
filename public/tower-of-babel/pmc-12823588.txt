Lightweight YOLO object detectors for PET and HDPE classification in recycling facilities
Authors: Adnan Miski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Growing volumes of plastic waste highlight the need for reliable sorting technologies. This study examines whether lightweight YOLO object detectors can distinguish polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottles from high-density polyethylene (HDPE) bottles, two common packaging materials that must be separated for recycling. Two public PET and HDPE datasets were augmented offline to simulate the visual variation typical of industrial sorting lines, and eleven compact YOLO models–nano, tiny and small versions across versions 7–12–were fine-tuned on these data. Their accuracy was quantified using mean average precision (mAP) at multiple intersection-over-union thresholds and precision, recall and F1 scores; their efficiency was evaluated by inference time, size and floating-point operations. All models achieved mAP@0.5 above 99.2% and F1 scores exceeding 98% on test data. Among them, YOLOv11n delivered the best trade-off between accuracy and speed, it achieved mAP@0.5:0.95 of 93.7%, processed 640 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 640 pixel images in around 38 ms on a CPU, and required just 5.5 MB of memory. These findings suggest that accurate polymer discrimination is possible on modest hardware, enabling resource-constrained recycling facilities to reduce mis-sorting, trim costs and support a circular economy.

Introduction

The accumulation of discarded plastics ranks among the foremost environmental concerns of the present day. Global output has climbed markedly, reaching about 390 million tonnes in 2021, and if current trajectories persist it is projected to nearly double by 20401. Only a small fraction of this material is recovered as roughly 9% of plastic waste is recycled worldwide, about 19% is incinerated, and close to half is consigned to landfills or discharged into terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems2. Such inefficiencies aggravate pollution of oceans, rivers and soils, where microplastic fragments derived from deteriorated polymers jeopardize biodiversity and human health3,4. In Asia, river systems alone convey millions of tonnes of plastic annually to the sea5. The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) warns that, absent robust policy interventions, environmental leakage of plastics could triple by 2060, further endangering food webs and water quality6. Recent comprehensive reviews highlight that plastic-waste management must be planned through a sustainable lens to move the sector toward a circular economy7.

Within the packaging sector, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and high-density polyethylene (HDPE) dominate the recyclable polymer market. PET bottles are prized for their transparency and barrier qualities, yet the presence of labels, adhesives and residual contents undermines recycling efforts by degrading recovered material8. HDPE, commonly used for milk jugs and detergent bottles, is valued for its strength and chemical resistance; nevertheless, sorting is complicated by differences in pigmentation and the frequent mixing of resins10. Maintaining clean PET and HDPE streams is essential, since cross-contamination diminishes the mechanical properties of recycled plastics and leads to down-cycling rather than true circularity. Economic analyses indicate that inefficiencies in collection and sorting reduce the value of recovered polymers and perpetuate dependence on virgin fossil resources11. Studies suggest that raising PET and HDPE recycling rates could reduce packaging-related greenhouse gas emissions by roughly 20–30%, in line with global sustainability objectives12,13. However, the sorting process is still largely manual, labour-intensive and error-prone, especially in regions where waste collection occurs informally14.

AI and deep learning advancements in waste management

Artificial Intelligence (AI), and deep learning (DL) in particular, has emerged as a powerful tool for improving waste-management systems15. Comprehensive literature surveys show that DL now dominates research in computer-vision–based waste sorting. Lu & Chen’s critical review of solid-waste sorting outlined the shift from traditional computer-vision methods to DL and emphasized the need for realistic, openly shared datasets16. More recently, Fotovvatikhah et al. performed a systematic review of AI-based waste-classification techniques, categorizing almost 100 studies into machine-learning, DL and hybrid approaches and highlighting persistent issues such as class imbalance and limited real-world variability17.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) automate the classification of waste by learning visual features from images and have achieved high accuracies on controlled datasets10,18,19. Transfer learning, fine-tuning pre-trained models on domain-specific collections, further reduces training times and computational demands for plastic identification20,21. When deployed on sorting belts, deep learning models can discern polymer types on the basis of shape, texture and colour cues, enabling real-time separation of materials14,22,23. CNNs have also been used for broader waste-segregation tasks, such as distinguishing organic matter, metals and plastics, which is valuable for municipal solid-waste processing10,24. Yet real-world deployment introduces challenges: variable lighting, occlusions and deformed objects can degrade performance22,25. Edge computing offers a means to alleviate these issues by enabling inference directly on low-power hardware, thereby reducing latency in robotic sorting systems24,26. Recently, researchers have begun applying deep learning to household waste detection, facilitating sustainable practices through instant classification27.

Object-detection architectures mark a significant evolution for vision-based sorting. The You Only Look Once (YOLO) family is notable for delivering real-time performance by predicting bounding boxes and class labels in a single forward pass28–30. Early versions such as YOLOv3 were used to identify ground debris with high precision under varying conditions31. Later iterations like YOLOv5 introduced attention mechanisms to improve feature extraction, making them well suited to detecting small objects amid cluttered scenes typical of recycling lines28,32. Industrial inspections that leverage optimized YOLOv5 variants have reported inference latencies below 10 ms on GPUs, underscoring their potential for high-throughput sorting29,32. In agriculture and environmental monitoring, YOLO models have assisted in identifying waste items from aerial imagery to support clean-up operations28,33. In manufacturing, integrating YOLO with robotic grippers further enhances its utility, enabling objects to be grasped and sorted autonomously on embedded devices with minimal reliance on cloud services24,33,34. Son & Ahn’s comparative study of Mask R-CNN and YOLOv8 for plastic waste sorting found that while Mask R-CNN achieved a slightly higher accuracy (0.912 vs 0.867) it required 200–350 ms per image, whereas YOLOv8 delivered similar mAP with much faster inference of 80–160 ms per image35. Di et al. subsequently proposed ES-YOLOv8, an improved version that incorporates a refined multi-scale detection framework, an Efficient Multiscale Attention module and a Shape-IoU loss function; this model increased mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95 by 5.4% and 6.1% respectively compared with baseline YOLOv8s36.

Despite these advances, fine-grained identification of specific polymer resins remains uncommon; most studies apply object detectors to broad waste classes rather than differentiating PET from HDPE10,18,23. Numerous papers report effective CNN-based approaches for municipal solid-waste segregation using datasets such as TrashNet and WaDaBa10,18. Some visual deep learning methods using transfer learning have achieved accuracies exceeding 95% when distinguishing recyclables from non recyclables10. Studies on depolymerisation of PET and HDPE underscore the importance of upstream sorting to ensure feedstock purity8,9,37. YOLO-based applications are beginning to appear–for example, for defect inspection of recycled composites–yet systematic comparisons of successive YOLO versions are rare20,23,32. Collectively, the evidence suggests that deep learning holds promise for enhancing recycling efficiency, but current research does not sufficiently address lightweight models suitable for deployment in low-resource settings22,25. Moreover, questions remain about how well these systems handle practical challenges such as lighting variability and occlusion in polymer-specific sorting tasks22,25. Recent developments that couple YOLO with robotic arms have demonstrated more than 90% accuracy in e-waste classification on edge devices26,33,38,39, and AI-driven robotics is increasingly recognized as key to improving recycling efficiency and accuracy40. Nonetheless, no comprehensive benchmark yet exists for the latest YOLO variants on finely grained PET versus HDPE detection, leaving a notable gap between advances in AI and the practical needs of polymer recycling.

Research objectives and contributions

Withe the goal of addressing these challenges, this study benchmarks YOLO models from versions 7 through 12, with particular emphasis on the nano/tiny and small variants, for the task of distinguishing PET and HDPE bottles. These compact models were chosen because their streamlined architectures suit edge-computing scenarios in recycling robots or embedded systems where limited power budgets and low latency are critical. The work evaluates not only accuracy metrics–such as mean average precision (mAP), precision, recall and F1-score–but also efficiency indicators (latency, model size and floating-point operations). By providing a systematic comparison of recent YOLO releases on a fine-grained polymer-detection problem, this research aims to enhance sustainability efforts, reduce contamination in PET and HDPE streams, and further circular-economy objectives. Deploying these models on devices ranging from augmented-reality headsets to robotic arms enables real-time sorting, thereby increasing throughput in recycling facilities while curbing energy consumption.

Dataset acquisition and preparation

The experiments were based on two public datasets. First, Drinking Waste Classification Dataset released on Kaggle41. This collection contains roughly 4,816 images of common household waste items–aluminum cans, glass bottles, PET drink bottles and HDPE milk bottles–labeled with bounding boxes for each object class. Several images were missing labels so they were annotated manually as shown in Figure 2. Because the goal is to perform a binary classification of PET and HDPE plastics, images containing aluminum or glass items were removed. Second, to supplement with industrial realism, RGB images from the FORTH/RECLAIM dataset42–captured on conveyor belts with occlusions, variable lighting, and motion were added. This dataset contains 1,240 PET images and 1,362 HDPE images on conveyor belts mimicking recycling facilities sorting lines as shown in Figure 2.

After filtering, this study dataset comprised 4,000 manually annotated images in total, with 2,000 PET images and 2,000 HDPE images, thus maintaining a balanced representation of the two polymers. To improve generalization and emulate the variability encountered in recycling facilities, offline data augmentation was applied using the Roboflow platform43. Model selection and architecture

The benchmarking considered official YOLov7 model and Ultralytics’ YOLO models from version 8 through version 12. For each version we selected the two smallest scales–nano (n) and small (s) except for YOLOv7 which had one comparable small model. In the case of YOLOv9, the smallest variant is labeled tiny (t), which we treated as the nano equivalent. Thus, the comparison involved v7, v8n/s, v9t/s, v10n/s, v11n/s and v12n/s. The focus on these compact variants reflects their suitability for low-power edge deployments such as embedded processors or robotic sorting platforms, where both memory and inference time are constrained. All models were initialized with pre-trained weights from the COCO dataset to accelerate convergence when fine-tuned on the PET/HDPE task.

Each version of YOLO introduces specific architectural innovations. YOLOv7 introduces extended efficient layer aggregation networks (E-ELAN) in the backbone, using group convolutions with shuffle-and-merge operations to increase feature cardinality without disrupting gradient flow. It also proposes trainable “bag-of-freebies” such as planned re-parameterization and a coarse-to-fine lead-guided label assignment, which collectively reduce parameters and computation while improving real-time accuracy45. YOLOv8 builds upon the YOLO family with an improved CSPDarknet backbone and a C2f neck, offering a balanced trade-off between detection accuracy and real-time speed46. YOLOv9 adds Programmable Gradient Information (PGI) and the Generalized Efficient Layer Aggregation Network (GELAN) to better learn feature representations for small objects and complex scenes47. YOLOv10 departs from previous versions by eliminating the non-maximum suppression (NMS) post-processing step through consistent dual label assignments and adopting a holistic efficiency–accuracy driven design48. YOLOv11 further streamlines the architecture by replacing the C2f block with a C3k2 block and introducing a Cross Stage Partial with Spatial Attention (C2PSA) module; this enhanced attention mechanism allows the model to focus on important regions and improves both speed and accuracy49. Finally, YOLOv12 adopts an attention-centric approach with an area attention module (A2) and residual efficient layer aggregation networks (R-ELAN), enabling higher accuracy while maintaining fast inference. Across all versions, the nano models minimize parameters and floating-point operations for ultra-edge devices, whereas the small variants trade a modest increase in resources for higher accuracy50.

Training procedure

Fine-tuning of each YOLO variant was performed via transfer learning using official repository for YOLOv7 and the Ultralytics YOLO library on a Google Colab Pro instance equipped with an NVIDIA A100 GPU (40 GB VRAM). Prior to training, input images were resized to 640 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 640 pixels and organized into mini-batches of 32. Training proceeded for up to 100 epochs, with an early-stopping patience of 50 epochs to mitigate overfitting. Optimization employed the SGD algorithm with an initial learning rate of 0.01 and a weight decay of 0.0005. Because data augmentation had already been conducted offline, no additional on-the-fly augmentations were applied during training, thereby preserving consistent inputs across epochs. Evaluation protocols

Model performance was evaluated along three axes: accuracy, efficiency and robustness. Accuracy was measured on the clean test set using standard object-detection metrics: mean average precision at an intersection-over-union (IoU) threshold of 0.5 (mAP@0.5), mean average precision averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05 (mAP@0.5:0.95), precision (P), recall (R) and F1-score. These metrics were computed for each class (PET and HDPE) and then averaged to gauge the models’ effectiveness on the binary classification task. Efficiency metrics were collected to determine the suitability of each model for edge computing. Baseline performance

All models performed exceedingly well as mean average precision at IoU 0.5 (mAP@0.5) exceeded 0.992 for every variant, indicating near-perfect detection as shown in Figure 4. When averaging across IoUs from 0.5 to 0.95 (mAP@0.5:0.95), performance fluctuated across model generations: v7 achieved 0.904. v8 variants achieved 0.932–0.938.932.938; v9 variants improved to 0.938–0.942.938.942; v10 variants scored 0.936–0.938.936.938; v11 variants reached 0.937–0.940.937.940; and v12 variants attained 0.936–0.938.936.938. Precision ranged from 0.985 to 0.993, recall from 0.990 to 0.996 and F1-score from 0.989 to 0.994, with v8s and v9t posting the highest F1-score of 0.994. These figures demonstrate that even the smallest models can reliably distinguish PET from HDPE bottles.

Each polymer separately was detected with exceptional accuracy; for example, YOLOv9t, YOLOv10n, and YOLOv11n achieved a class-wise mAP@0.5 of 0.995 on PET and HDPE. While YOLOv7 scored a class-wise mAP@0.5 of 0.998 for PET and 0.996 for HDPE. Precision–recall curves for representative models (Figure 5) appear almost rectangular, with areas under the curve approaching one; this shows that precision remains very high even as recall approaches 100%. Likewise, F1–confidence curves (Figure 6) stay above 0.99 across a wide range of confidence thresholds. The confidence value that maximizes F1-score varies by version–around 0.737 for v7, 0.531 for v8n, 0.667 for v9t, approximately 0.558 for v10n, about 0.601 for v11n and back to roughly 0.63 for v12n. Efficiency for edge deployment

Efficient inference and a small memory footprint are essential in real-time recycling applications. The nano versions of YOLO consistently run faster and occupied less memory than their small counterparts. To illustrate, YOLOv11n processes a single image on the CPU in 38 ms, occupies 5.5 MB of memory and performs 6.3 G FLOPs, yet achieves a high mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.937. The corresponding small model, YOLOv11s, nudges mAP@0.5:0.95 up to 0.940, but takes 95.5 ms per image, uses 19 MB of memory and requires 21.3 G FLOPs. Among the nano models, YOLOv11n therefore offers the most favorable compromise between speed, size and accuracy. On the small-model side, YOLOv11s delivers the highest mAP@0.5 among small models and best mAP@0.5:0.95, giving the most balanced performance in that class. These comparisons show that modest gains in detection accuracy from the small variants come at the expense of substantial increases in latency and memory requirements. It is also worth noting that although the small variant YOLOv9s achieves the highest mAP@0.5:0.95 in this benchmark (0.942), it does so with a CPU latency of about 118 ms–more than three times slower than YOLOv11n–underscoring the steep speed–accuracy trade-off across versions. Discussion

The systematic benchmark presented in this study offers clear evidence that modern YOLO architectures can differentiate PET from HDPE bottles with near-perfect accuracy when trained on a balanced dataset. Even the most compact nano models achieve mAP@0.5 values above 0.992 and F1-scores exceeding 0.98, while the larger variants only incrementally increase mAP@0.5:0.95 from roughly 0.904 for YOLOv7 to about 0.942 for YOLOv9s. Such marginal gains suggest that, for a binary classification problem with strong visual cues, architectural refinements across YOLO generations play a secondary role compared with data quality.

Resource efficiency emerged as a key factor for selecting the optimal model. Nano variants such as YOLOv11n offer rapid inference (38 ms on a CPU) and modest memory footprints (5.5 MB) while sustaining high mAP. Small variants increase accuracy by only a few tenths of a point but triple latency and memory consumption. In practice, a sorting robot on a fast conveyor may prioritize speed and select a nano model, whereas a slower line might opt for a small variant to reduce the risk of misclassifications. These results therefore suggest a pathway for reducing economic losses caused by mis-sorting and cross-contaminated bales11. By demonstrating that an ultra-efficient model such as YOLOv11n can deliver near-perfect sorting on commodity CPU hardware, this study shows how automated AI systems could replace or augment manual sorting, improving throughput while lowering labor costs and energy consumption. Advances in model compression–quantization, pruning and knowledge distillation–could further narrow this gap, enabling more accurate models to run on edge hardware without prohibitive overhead.

The findings of this study are better viewed in the context of previous work. Multi-spectral near-infrared sensors combined with machine learning typically achieve mean recognition accuracies around 72.5% for classifying common plastics22. A recent YOLOv12-based general waste detector reached only 73% precision and a mAP of 78%25. Even the best performing models in the WaDaBa benchmark achieve approximately 98% accuracy18. Against these baselines, the performance of the binary classification in this study is exceptional, albeit on a simpler task. The near-perfect mAP values demonstrate that RGB imaging alone can reliably separate PET and HDPE under controlled lighting, whereas spectral methods or broad waste detectors struggle when faced with multiple classes and variable contamination.

Several limitations temper these positive results. The curated dataset used in this work comprises 4,000 unique images and is heavily augmented offline; most samples depict clean bottles photographed against uncluttered backgrounds, with minimal occlusion or deformation. Real recycling environments, however, contain bottles that are crushed, soiled and overlapped with other waste. Studies combining NIR spectroscopy or mechanical handling note substantial drops in accuracy under these conditions22. Future benchmarks should therefore incorporate diverse backgrounds, lighting conditions and object states–including overlapping and damaged containers–to approximate the variability encountered in material recovery facilities.

Furthermore, while RGB imaging suffices for binary PET/HDPE classification under controlled conditions due to its low cost and wide accessibility, it may falter with heavily contaminated or deformed samples where spectral distinctions are critical. PET and HDPE exhibit unique absorption peaks in the near-infrared and short-wave infrared ranges, enabling hyperspectral imaging to achieve sorting accuracies exceeding 95% in recycling plants52,53. This study prioritized RGB data to benchmark visual cues (shape, color, texture) on modest hardware, aligning with edge-computing scenarios in resource-constrained facilities. Future work could integrate NIR/SWIR datasets, potentially via hybrid models combining RGB detectors with spectroscopic sensors, to enhance robustness in multi-polymer streams.

Beyond binary classification, the methodology should be extended to more realistic, multi-class scenarios. Contemporary recycling streams contain a spectrum of polymers and composites, and contamination from PVC or polypropylene can degrade recycled PET and HDPE. Incorporating additional classes such as LDPE, PP and PS would test whether the observed performance persists as inter-class distinctions become subtler. The WaDaBa dataset and other open collections provide a foundation for such multi-class experiments19. Hybrid systems that combine visual detectors with complementary sensors–near-infrared spectroscopy, hyperspectral cameras–could improve differentiation where color and shape cues alone prove insufficient.

Enhancing the purity of PET and HDPE streams increases the economic viability of recycling and can reduce packaging-related greenhouse-gas emissions by roughly 20–30%, according to life-cycle analysis12. Lightweight architectures like YOLOv11n mitigate the energy costs associated with training and deploying deep models. Their modest computational requirements demonstrate that high-accuracy sorting does not require expensive GPU infrastructure. This finding is particularly relevant for equitable access: small or informal facilities in developing regions could feasibly deploy these models on low-cost hardware such as AR smart glasses, narrowing the digital divide in waste-management technology.

Conclusion

A rigorous assessment of PET and HDPE classification is essential if recycling systems are to evolve from labor-intensive manual operations to efficient, closed-loop material recovery. In this study, a benchmark of YOLO lightweight versions showed that even the most compact detectors can discriminate between these two polymers with remarkable accuracy. These results reveal that accurate polymer sorting does not depend on large, resource-hungry architectures; rather, carefully fine-tuned, cost-efficient models can offer near-perfect performance.

Such findings have broad implications for the circular plastics economy. By lowering the computational and financial barriers to automated sorting, ultra-lightweight detectors can enable small or resource-constrained facilities to reduce mis-sorting, improve bale purity and enhance the economic value of recovered materials, which in turn supports greenhouse-gas reductions. Future work should evaluate these models on more diverse and contaminated streams, incorporate additional polymer classes and exploit complementary sensing modalities such as near-infrared or hyperspectral cameras. A comprehensive life-cycle assessment, including the energy costs of training and running these models and the environmental benefits of improved recycling, would further clarify the net impact. By addressing these challenges, researchers can help translate the promise of AI-enabled sorting into scalable, real-world solutions that advance the transition toward a sustainable, circular economy.
