A national growth mixture modeling analysis of county-level COVID-19 incidence rate trajectories and health inequities during three successive pandemic waves in 2020
Authors: Kimberly Dukes, Pengsheng Ni, Julie Alperen, Nina Cesare, Michael LaValley, Tara Tripp, Kevin Lane, Shariq Mohammed, Prasad Patil, Danielle Emig, Michael Winter, Brandeus Davis, Biqi Wang, Abhi Jain, Michael Acker, Michael Rickles
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We applied Growth Mixture Modeling (GMM) to characterize county-level COVID-19 incidence rate (IR) trajectories across three distinct waves in the United States from March 15 to November 2, 2020. GMM enabled the identification of latent subpopulations with shared temporal patterns of disease spread, offering a flexible analytic framework for uncovering both known and evolving disparities. Across the three periods, up to five trajectory groups were identified, revealing substantial geographic and temporal heterogeneity. To support interpretation and reduce multiple comparisons, we developed and validated a 17-item Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) index representing county-level economic and resource access factors. Higher-incidence rate trajectories consistently aligned with lower SDOH scores and greater proportions of Black or African American residents and younger populations. These disparities shifted over time, with patterns of high-incidence rates emerging in resource-limited counties across the South and Midwest in later waves. By further assessing well-documented inequities and expanding understanding of their dynamic expression across space and time, this study demonstrates the utility of GMM for public health surveillance, planning, and equitable response strategies in future outbreaks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25074-5.

Introduction

In the initial months of the COVID-19 pandemic, substantial geographic and temporal variations in rates of infection were observed in the United States (U.S.)1. Numerous sociodemographic and spatial factors have been associated with COVID-19 morbidity and mortality, offering potential explanations for these observed patterns2–4. Prior research suggests that social determinants of health (SDOH), that is, “the circumstances in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age5,” including socioeconomic inequities, housing conditions, and barriers to healthcare access6–10, may influence COVID-19 outcomes. Geographic racial segregation has also played a role, with communities of color in the US experiencing disproportionately high infection rates10–13, and documented disparities in COVID-19 infection extend across age, gender and other SDOH factors8,12–18. Furthermore, transmission patterns differ between rural and urban areas, primarily due to variations in population density and frequency of social interactions10,19. Jurisdictional differences in public health policies designed to mitigate transmission20 further contribute to the observed heterogeneity in disease spread.

Recognizing the complex and multi-faceted nature of COVID-19, we used Growth Mixture Modeling (GMM) to investigate how county-level COVID-19 incidence rate trajectories evolved over three distinct periods, or waves, during the first nine months of the pandemic and further examined the characteristics of subpopulations that experienced the greatest outbreaks. Our primary measure of disease spread is the incidence rate (IR), defined as the number of new cases per 100,000 county residents, to ensure we account for changes in disease burden relative to population size. Growth Mixture Modeling (GMM) offers a flexible and robust framework for identifying spatiotemporal trends in subpopulations. Here, spatiotemporal trends refer to patterns over time and geographic space, where a county is grouped with others of similar trajectory over time. Specifically, GMM enables the classification of counties with similar patterns of COVID-19 incidence rates, allowing for the exploration of unique characteristics and temporal trends associated with disease spread. As a specific approach within the broader Latent Class Trajectory Modeling (LCTM) framework, GMM accounts for individual-level variability within trajectory groups through the inclusion of random effects, unlike Group-Based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM), which assumes homogeneity within groups21,22. By allowing for within-class heterogeneity and non-linear time trends, GMM can uncover temporal dynamics and subgroup patterns that are masked in conventional regression analyses or static mapping approaches that treat the incidence rate as a single continuous outcome. This flexibility makes GMM particularly well-suited for capturing the heterogeneity in COVID-19 incidence rates across U.S. counties over time.

We hypothesized that employing GMM would reveal distinct COVID-19 IR trajectory patterns within each of three waves, and that distinct SDOH, demographic, and geographic characteristics would emerge among trajectory groups over time. The three periods we utilized in 2020 roughly align with those defined by the CDC and include Period 1: March 15 – June 14, Period 2: June 15 – August 14, and Period 3: August 15 – November 223. We limited our investigation to these three periods to reduce the impact of confounding factors, such as holiday gatherings and travel and the introduction of vaccination efforts.

A related application by Vahabi et al.18 used GMM to examine county-level COVID-19 mortality-to-incidence ratios during similar COVID-19 waves in 2020, and other trajectory-based approaches have modeled epidemic curves over short windows10,13. In addition, several county- and state-level studies have examined associations between social vulnerability and COVID-19 outcomes using cross-sectional methods or were limited to early phases of the pandemic8,9,12. However, to our knowledge, our study is the first to (1) use GMM to investigate IR trajectories for all U.S. 3,142 U.S. counties during each of the first three pandemic waves, and (2) integrate a newly developed nationwide county-level SDOH index constructed to align with the key areas identified in the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Healthy People 2020 SDOH framework24, providing more comprehensive subgroup insights than previously available. In addition, the use of a composite SDOH index, which aggregates multiple SDOH factors into a single summary metric, reduces the need for multiple comparisons across individual socioeconomic factors, indirectly helping to control the familywise error rate (FWER) and improving the reliability of our findings.

The rapid evolution of the pandemic, including the emergence of multiple variants25, stagnating vaccination rates26, and strain on the U.S. healthcare system27, underscores the need for adaptable analytic frameworks. By using GMM to document the U.S.-wide spatial and temporal spread of this disease across trajectory classes and in subpopulations using a national SDOH index, we illuminate conditions contributing to unique transmission patterns, provide valuable insights into the pandemic, and offer policymakers tools to design more targeted and effective responses to future disease outbreaks.

Demographic characteristics

Demographically, Black or African American and Hispanic or Latino/a residents constitute an average of 9.0% (SD = 14.4%) and 9.6% (SD = 13.9%), respectively, of residents across all U.S. counties. The average median age across counties is 41.6 years (SD = 5.5). The mean of the median household income across counties is $55,022 (SD = $14,656). In urban counties, 12.0% (SD = 14.2%) of residents are Black or African American; the mean of the median age is 40.1 years (SD = 4.9); and the average median household income is $62,463 (SD = $17,319). In rural counties, the percentage of Black or African American residents is lower at 7.3% (SD = 14.3%); the mean median age is slightly older at 42.4 years (SD = 5.6); and the average median household income is lower $50,700 (SD = $10,706) compared to urban communities. The proportion of Hispanic or Latino/a residents is consistent between urban and rural areas.

Social determinants of health (SDOH) index

The five-factor principal component solution explained 72.6% of variability in the 17 items of the SDOH index. The first five eigenvalues were greater than 1, aligning with the number of hypothesized constructs. Supplemental Table S1 provides the Promax-rotated factor pattern, confirming that each item loaded meaningfully onto its respective component with minimal cross-loadings. The five sub-indices include Healthcare Access, Food Access, Housing and Transportation, Resource Access, and Economic Security.

The results of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supported the hypothesized five-factor structure, demonstrating an adequate model fit. The Comparative Fit Index (CFI) was 0.803, the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) was 0.754, and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) was 0.090. Our results indicate moderate fit (conventional cutoffs for good fit: CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08) (Table S1). The factor loading patterns were consistent with the PCA results, with minimal cross-loading among factors. The normalized residual matrix indicated that absolute residual values remained below 0.2, suggesting the model adequately accounted for the observed data. These findings support the theoretical structure of the SDOH index, reinforcing the conceptual distinction among the five interrelated sub-domains.

For this analysis, we focus on the overall SDOH index, which demonstrated high internal reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.78) and significant concurrent validity (0.37, p-value < 0.0001) with the Well-Being Index (WBI) (Supplementary Table S2). The overall SDOH index represents all 3,142 U.S. counties with a mean of 49.9 (SD = 4.5) and ranges from 34.9 to 76.3, with a higher score indicating greater resources. Urban counties had a slightly higher mean SDOH score of 51.5 (SD = 5.4) as compared to rural counties, with a mean score of 49.0 (SD = 3.6) (Table 1).

There were 315 counties that fell within the lowest SDOH percentile (< 10th) group, with a larger proportion of rural (n = 220, 11.1%) compared to urban (n = 95, 8.2%) designations. In the ≥ 10th to < 50th percentile category, which includes 1,256 counties, rural counties again make up a substantial proportion (n = 900, 45.3%) compared to urban counties (n = 356, 30.8%). Counties in the ≥ 50th to < 90th percentile group are more evenly distributed, with 1,256 counties split between 466 urban (40.4%) and 790 rural (39.7%) counties. The highest SDOH percentile group (≥ 90th), reflecting greatest access to resources, housing stability, and economic security, includes 315 counties. This group is disproportionately urban, encompassing 20.5% of all urban counties compared with only 3.9% of rural counties in the U.S.

Counties in the highest SDOH group (≥ 90th percentile) are generally concentrated around densely populated urban centers and along the coasts of New England and California, with some rural, less-densely populated areas in the western US. COVID-19 incidence rates (IR)

The mean COVID-19 incidence rate (IR) across U.S. counties from March 15 – November 2, 2020, was 2,897 cases per 100,000 population (SD = 1,789), with a median of 2,627 cases per 100,000. The IR ranged widely, from 0 to 17,952 cases per 100,000, reflecting substantial variability across counties and over time (Table 1).

During Period 1 (March 15–June 14), the mean COVID-19 IR was 388 cases per 100,000 (SD = 691), with a median of 178 cases per 100,000 and a range from 0 to 12,903 per 100,000. This period had the lowest IR, indicating a relatively slower spread of the virus. In Period 2 (June 15–August 14), IR increased substantially to a mean of 805 cases per 100,000 (SD = 804), with a median of 538 cases per 100,000 and a range of 0 to 9,072 cases per 100,000. By Period 3 (August 15–November 2), the mean IR had risen sharply to 1,705 cases per 100,000 (SD = 1,275), with a median of 1,423 cases per 100,000 and a range from 0 to 14,592 cases per 100,000. Period 3 represented the highest COVID-19 rates, reflecting wider transmission. Throughout all three periods, some counties reported no COVID-19 cases, underscoring the uneven geographic spread of the virus, particularly during the early months of the pandemic (Table 1).

Growth mixture modeling (GMM)

Model evaluation and selection followed the criteria outlined in the Methods section. Five-knot GMMs provided a good balance between model fit and stability. Across the three periods, fit indices and class-size thresholds supported selection of five, four and four latent classes, respectively (Supplementary Table S3; Tables S4a–S4c; Figures S1a–S1c). For Period 1, a six-class solution produced the lowest SABIC, AIC, and BIC values, but one class contained fewer than 5% of counties; therefore, the five-class model was selected to maintain stability and interpretability. For Periods 2 and 3, although the five-class models yielded slightly lower SABIC, AIC, and BIC values, the four-class models were retained because small classes in the five-class fell below the 5% county threshold (one class in Period 2 and two classes in Period 3). Entropy ranged from 0.69 to 0.79 across the three periods, exceeding the generally acceptable threshold (≥ 0.70) for class separation in large-sample trajectory studies. The fit indices were concordant overall, supporting the final class selection. After determining the number of classes, predicted marginal trajectories (Supplementary Figures S1a–S1c) were examined to confirm the distinctiveness of the retained classes.

As noted, across the three periods, GMM identified up to five distinct trajectory classes of county-level COVID-19 incidence rates (Fig. 2). Five classes were observed in Period 1, and four in Periods 2 and 3; however, for consistency, we retained five-class labeling across periods, aligning colors and trajectory shapes. The light green line (Class 1) represents counties with the lowest IR trajectories. It appears only in Period 1, conceptually merging with Class 2 thereafter. The dark green line, labeled Class 2 in Period 1 and Class 1/2 in Periods 2 and 3, represents counties with consistently low and flat IRs. The yellow trajectory (Class 3) reflects initially low IRs followed by a sharp rise near the end of each period. The orange line (Class 4) displays a steady increase with shape variations: an inverted U-shape in Periods 1 and 2 and a U-shape in Period 3 with a late rise. The purple trajectory (Class 5) captures the highest IRs, showing an inverted U-shape in Periods 1 and 2 and a plateau at high rates in Period 3. Class 1 (light green; 18.6% of counties, 1.9% of the U.S. population) and Class 2 (dark green; 43.3% of counties, 45.8% of the population) had consistently low IRs, though Class 2 was slightly higher. Class 3 (yellow; 8.9% of counties, 3.4% of the population) was low until week 11, when the incidence rate rose. Period 2: June 15 to August 14, 2020

During this 9-week period, four distinct trajectory classes were identified (Fig. 2C, Table 2), consistent with the model-selection criteria described above. Posterior probabilities (Supplementary Table S4b) showed high certainty of class assignment, with values exceeding 0.77 for each class, and predicted marginal trajectories (Supplementary Figure S1b) confirmed that the retained classes captured distinct COVID-19 IR patterns within this period.

Class 1/2 (dark green; 15.3% of counties, 7.9% of the U.S. population) represents counties with consistently low and stable IRs throughout the period (Fig. 2C, Table 2). Class 3 (yellow; 8.2% of counties, 1.7% of the population) began with low IRs that rose after week 5 and included a predominantly rural composition. Class 4 (orange; 40.2% of counties, 40.8% of the population) showed gradual and steady increase in IR across the period, while Class 5 (purple; 36.3% of counties, 49.6% of the population) peaked around week 6 before declining. Together, these four classes capture the nationwide shift in disease dynamics that occurred after the initial wave (Supplementary Table S5, Fig. 2D). Over 85% of counties in the South and in the East North Central Division had high-IR trajectories (Classes 4 and 5), while 88% of New England shifted toward low IR-trajectories (Class 1/2). Counties in the Pacific division, however, were largely in higher-IR classes (68%), indicating a westward shift in disease spread during this period.

During this 11-week period, four distinct trajectory classes were identified (Fig. 2E, Table 2), consistent with the model-selection criteria described above. Posterior probabilities (Supplementary Table S4c) showed high certainty of class assignment, with values exceeding 0.80 for each class, and predicted marginal trajectories (Supplementary Figure S1c) confirmed that the retained classes captured distinct COVID-19 IR patterns within this period.

Class 1/2 (dark green; 4.5% of counties, 2.0% of the U.S. population) represents counties with persistently low and stable IRs throughout the period. Class 3 (yellow; 6.3% of counties, 0.9% of the population) began with low IRs that rose sharply after week 6. Class 4 (orange; 76.0% of counties, 93.2% of the population) showed moderate IRs early in the period followed by steady and substantial increases, while Class 5 (purple; 13.2% of counties, 3.9% of the population) reached the highest IRs overall, peaking late in the period before plateauing.

Geographically, the highest IR trajectories (Classes 4 and 5) were concentrated in the South and Midwest, where nearly all counties in the South Atlantic (95.9%), East South Central (95.6%), and West South Central (94.7%) divisions fell into these higher-IR groups (Supplementary Table S5, Fig. 2F). Class 4 included a mix of both rural and urban counties, reflecting the broad reach of this wave across community types. High-IR trajectories were also dominant across the Midwest, with more than 90% of counties in the East and West North Central divisions classified in Classes 4 or 5. In contrast, resurgence was observed in the Pacific and New England divisions, where approximately 70% and 40% of counties, respectively, shifted into Class 4, suggesting renewed spread in areas that had experienced earlier declines.

COVID-19 trajectories and disparities

In both Periods 1 and 2, counties with the highest IR had higher proportions of Black or African American residents (Supplementary Table S6 and Fig. 3). In Period 1, counties in Class 5 had the highest percentage of Black or African American residents, with the most significant representation in the lowest SDOH group: 40.7% (SD = 24.7%) compared to Class 1 at 4.4% (SD = 11.0%). In Period 2, Class 5 counties had the largest proportion of Black or African American residents, particularly in counties within the bottom 10th percentile of SDOH, with 30.5% (SD = 24.6%) compared to Class 1/2 with 2.3% (SD = 4.0%). Period 3 showed a slightly different trend, with the highest percentage of Black or African American residents in Class 4 counties within the bottom 10th SDOH percentile with 20.4% (SD = 23.5%), compared to Class 1/2 with 5.0% (SD = 9.3%). Class 5 included counties with the second highest Black or African American representation, and these residents were most likely to fall into the bottom 10th SDOH group with 10.8% (SD = 15.0%) compared to the highest with 3.9% (SD = 5.7%). Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to utilize GMM to examine COVID-19 IR trajectories across all U.S. counties during each of the first three pandemic waves, while integrating a newly developed, nationwide county-level SDOH index grounded in the Healthy People 2020 framework. This approach provides unique national-scale, descriptive insights into how incidence trajectories evolved over time and differed by sociodemographic composition and county-level resources. Vahabi et al. (2021)18 also applied GMM to examine COVID-19 trajectories using data from a similar time period in 2020, and their outcome was survival, measured as the mortality to incidence ratio, and their analysis did not include all U.S. counties during the three successive waves being examined. Dupre et al. (2021)10 also used latent class trajectory methods to examine early COVID-19 mortality patterns, though their analysis relied on group-based trajectory models and was limited to the first pandemic wave. In contrast, our use of GMM allowed for within-class heterogeneity and examined incidence rates across all 3,142 counties and three distinct waves. Karmaker et al. (2021)8 examined social risk factors using the CDC’s Social Vulnerability Index, which has a narrower scope than our novel SDOH index developed to align with the Healthy People 2020 SDOH domains. Together, these differences provide a broader and more flexible perspective on incidence rate trajectories and their social patterning.

We descriptively validated our GMM using geographic location, demographics, and our SDOH Index. These validations demonstrate the robustness of our trajectory classifications and the value of our SDOH index in distinguishing counties based on resource access and socio-economic factors. While previous studies have identified broad patterns linking SDOH, demographic characteristics, and COVID-19 outcomes, our analysis provides a more nuanced, temporal perspective, showing how inequities persisted but shifted geographically and demographically over time.

Although not the primary focus of this manuscript, we validated our 17-item overall SDOH index and five sub-indices through descriptive alignment with IR trajectory classes. We found, consistent with prior research, an association between sociodemographic factors, such as race and socioeconomic status, and COVID-19 incidence rates, with greater disease burden in communities with higher proportions of Black or African American residents and in areas with lower SDOH resources8–18. The GMM trajectory patterns extend this literature by showing how disparities persisted yet evolved across pandemic waves rather than remaining static, underscoring the compounded effects of race and SDOH on COVID-19 morbidity, despite changes in disease spread and policy interventions.

Beyond confirming well-established associations, our findings highlight how resource availability and age distributions within counties shaped disease dynamics. Higher SDOH index scores were consistently associated with younger populations, especially in counties with higher IR, suggesting that younger populations tend to be in areas with more resources, and reinforcing the observation that communities with varying levels of resource access may experience differential exposure to the disease. The temporal shift in age distribution within higher IR trajectory classes further illustrates how age was associated with COVID-19 as the pandemic progressed, with younger, more resource-rich communities experiencing greater transmission in later waves. These findings illustrate the value of combining GMM with theoretically grounded indices to reveal dynamics that are often obscured in cross-sectional or single-outcome studies.

Our results also highlight geographic shifts in disease spread over time, with the highest IR trajectories shifting away from the more densely populated Northeast in Period 1 to the South and West in Period 2 and finally largely in the Midwest and Central U.S. in Period 3. Geographic patterns also illustrate the impact of resource access on disease spread, as we observed that many areas with high IR trajectories and higher SDOH in Period 1 had lower IR rates in Period 2, a pattern observed with far less consistency in areas with lower SDOH, suggesting higher SDOH counties were able to mobilize resources to control disease spread.

This approach can produce valuable insights for policymakers tasked with resource allocation during future outbreaks. Given documented racial disparities in access to quality healthcare resources28,29, our finding that the relationship between SDOH and disease burden was most pronounced in higher IR classes with a greater proportion of Black or African Americans residents highlights the vulnerability of communities that are both resource-poor and historically marginalized and underscores the need for targeted public health interventions in these areas. Our findings reinforce the importance of addressing inequities in resource distribution at the county level, as they directly exacerbate disparities in disease burden.

While this study provides valuable insights into the spatiotemporal patterns of COVID-19 incidence and their association with demographic and SDOH factors, it is important to acknowledge the limitations. GMM is an exploratory tool that allows for the identification of distinct trajectory groups based on observed data. Although our analysis included all U.S. counties, GMM model fit and class enumeration are influenced by model specification and parameterization; while multiple fit indices and class-size thresholds guided model selection, results should be interpreted within this context. Our approach focused on descriptive validation, emphasizing the utility of GMM in uncovering spatiotemporal trends and aligning them with known demographic and SDOH patterns. The value of this work is in identifying population-level patterns that can inform public health response; results should not be interpreted as causal or individual-level associations. While these descriptive findings are critical to assessing our SDOH metric and GMM trajectories, future work should integrate multivariable approaches to disentangle the effects of demographic and geographic factors, SDOH resources, and policy interventions.

In addition, concurrent validity of the SDOH index with the Well-Being Index was ρ = 0.37 overall, with weaker subindex correlations. We view this as moderate validation given the size of the dataset and multiple confounding factors but acknowledge that the index should be refined and tested against additional benchmarks.

Our analysis is restricted to the early pandemic phase, prior to vaccines, holiday gatherings, and variant circulation, which reduced confounding but limits generalizability to later pandemic phases. We also did not explicitly model spatial autocorrelation across counties. While spatial autocorrelation (”spillover”) is a recognized issue, we note that trajectory classes were distributed across all US regions (Table 2, Fig. 2). This suggests that our findings are not solely an artifact of local spillover but reflect broader spatiotemporal patterns. Nonetheless, future work could incorporate spatial lag or error structures to account for this dependence.

In addition, the U.S. did not have a centralized public health reporting system in place prior to the pandemic and relied on states and counties to report COVID-19 data. The lack of standardized methods between jurisdictions led to reporting inconsistencies with the potential to impact the quality of publicly available data like that on which we relied, a limitation also acknowledged by the source, the Johns Hopkins University Center for Systems Science and Engineering’s (CSSE) Coronavirus Resource Lab30,31. We were unable to incorporate other county-level external factors such as public health interventions, vaccination rates, or regional policy shifts, which can substantially alter transmission dynamics and may explain why counties with similar sociodemographic profiles followed different trajectories. Future work should integrate such data to better isolate the effects of SDOH from policy responses.

While our findings reflect the U.S. context in 2020, the GMM approach is scalable and adaptable to other countries or future outbreaks provided comparable sociodemographic and incidence data are available. Finally, the availability of data at smaller spatial resolutions would further enhance the precision of these methods and enable more targeted identification of areas requiring intervention.

In conclusion, GMM provides a robust framework for identifying spatiotemporally explicit patterns, which we demonstrated by examining COVID-19 IR patterns at the U.S. county level and identifying unique subpopulation disease trends. By considering both sociodemographic characteristics and SDOH profiles, we provide a more dynamic and targeted approach to discerning the varied impacts of COVID-19, and hence a more nuanced understanding of the pandemic’s spread. Our study also underscores the importance of integrating theoretically grounded indices with flexible modeling approaches to capture how inequities shift across time and space. Further, incorporating the SDOH index reduces the need for multiple comparisons across individual socioeconomic factors, indirectly helping to control the familywise error rate (FWER) and improving the reliability of our findings. This approach will enable public health professionals to make better-informed decisions regarding policies, resource allocation, and interventions for future, similar infectious disease outbreaks, supporting more effective, responsive, and equitable public health response.

Methods

COVID-19 incidence rate

We obtained county-level COVID-19 incidence rate data from the Johns Hopkins University CSSE via ESRI Online29. We used data that corresponded with the early stages of the pandemic, starting shortly after the World Health Organization’s COVID-19 pandemic declaration on March 11, 2020, and extending through early November 2020. We limited our analysis to this period to reduce the impact of potential confounding factors, such as impending holiday gatherings or travel and the introduction of vaccination efforts. All counties had complete incidence data, and no imputation was required.

To examine trends, we divided the timeframe into three discrete periods within 2020: March 15 to June 14, June 15 to August 14, and August 15 to November 223. These periods roughly align with the waves of COVID-19 cases observed in the US during this time. This segmentation enabled a clearer understanding of the evolution of incidence rates across different phases of the pandemic.

Well-being index (WBI)

The Sharecare WBI originated from the Gallup-Healthways Well-Being Index (now known as the Gallup Well-Being Index), a widely recognized tool for assessing well-being across diverse populations which consists of 38 items measuring self-reported well-being across five domains: purpose, social, financial, community, and physical. Since its inception in 2008, it has been featured in over 80 peer-reviewed publications, highlighting its importance in evaluating health risks across various dimensions. Detailed methodology for the development and validation of the index has been published32. In 2019, based on further psychometric evaluation through Item Response Theory, Sharecare developed an updated version of the WBI which reduced to 33 the number of items included across domains.

Since 2018, each year approximately 500,000 respondents across the US complete a digital survey containing WBI items, demographic information, and questions related to comorbidities and other health habits. County-level WBI scores are calculated using statistical methods including multiple-imputation for missing respondent-level demographic characteristics, small area estimation for counties, and direct standardization to county demographic profile. The methods involve respondent-level mixed effects modeling with the WBI outcome, respondent-level demographic predictors, and random intercepts at the county level. Further details about the methodology are available (https://wellbeingindex.sharecare.com/research/sharecare-community-well-being-index-methods/).

For the purpose of this analysis, we use county-level overall scores from the 2020 Sharecare WBI to validate our SDOH index. Research has consistently shown that individuals with higher well-being are more likely to have better access to SDOH resources and economic opportunities33–35.

Social determinants of health (SDOH) index

We developed and validated an SDOH index to quantify U.S. residents’ socioeconomic environment and resource access at the county level, which measures five interrelated, underlying constructs or domains: neighborhood and built environment, health and health care, social and community context, education, and economic stability (Figure S3). Our aim was to create an overall SDOH index and five sub-indices. While conceptually similar to other established SDOH measures36,37, our index was constructed to align with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services’ Healthy People 2020 SDOH framework, which provided a widely accepted conceptual foundation for understanding how structural and environmental conditions shape health outcomes, and guided both the selection of items and the organization of the index into five sub-indices. Our index makes use of national data consistently available at the county level for all 3,142 U.S. counties.

Using data from but not limited to the U.S. Census Bureau, U.S. Department of Agriculture, Health Resources and Service Administration, U.S. Geological Survey, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Institute of Museum and Library Services and Department of Homeland Security, we selected over 100 items for consideration based on expert opinion and literature review that aligned with our hypothesized framework. Candidate items were then evaluated based on their unique content, distributional properties, and availability for all 3,142 U.S. counties. All items were continuous, and bivariate associations among candidate items were assessed using Pearson (parametric) and Spearman rank (non-parametric) to identify redundant metrics (r > 0.8).

We selected 59 non-redundant items that measured our hypothesized constructs and performed PCA to reduce the number of items retained while maximizing variance explained. We forced five components and examined both varimax and promax rotations. For interpretability, we used the promax rotation and excluded measures with loadings below 0.3 across all five components. We reviewed whether each item could be assigned to one component, based on a loading greater than or equal to 0.4 with minimal cross-loading with the other components. This iterative process resulted in the selection of 17 items measured in all 3,142 US counties.

To formally test our hypothesized conceptual model, we used CFA. Model fit was assessed using the chi-square goodness of fit test, Bentler’s Comparative Fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and the normalized residual matrix, ensuring no residuals exceed 0.2. We considered the following conventional cutoffs for good fit: CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08.

To assess scale reliability, we report Cronbach’s Alpha as a measure of internal consistency for the overall index and each sub-index. Sub-index scores were calculated by summing all respective sub-index items and standardized with a mean of 50 and standard deviation of 10 using a t-score (higher score = better resource access and economic factors). The overall SDOH index was calculated by summing all standardized sub-index scores. To assess concurrent validity, we calculated Spearman correlations between the validated WBI and the overall SDOH index, as well as between each of the five sub-indices.

Geographic, demographic, and SDOH characteristics

Census regions and divisions were classified by the U.S. Census Bureau, and urban–rural status was determined using Rural–Urban Commuting Area codes from the Office of Rural Health Policy38. Demographic measures, including race, ethnicity, income, and age, were defined by the U.S. Census Bureau. Descriptive statistics were calculated for categorical and continuous variables. For categorical variables, frequencies and percentages were reported. For continuous measures, the mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values were provided. Analyses included stratification by urban–rural classification, time-period, SDOH group and trajectory class. Additionally, choropleth maps and plots were used to visualize distributions and trends across categories.

For descriptive purposes, counties are grouped into four categories by SDOH percentile (i.e., < 10th, ≥ 10th to < 50th, ≥ 50th to < 90th, ≥ 90th).

Development of county trajectory groups

Within each of the three time periods assessed, we calculated weekly cumulative incidence rates (IR) for COVID-19. To avoid issues with log transformation for zero values, IR was scaled per 100,000 individuals and 0.1 was added to all values. Weeks were numbered sequentially within each time period.

GMM was used to identify latent classes representing distinct COVID-19 incidence trajectories over time. To address the non-normal distribution of incidence rates, an I-splines transformation with three, five, and seven equidistant knots was applied over the observed range of data within each time period. This approach, recommended by Proust-Lima et al.39, allows for the modeling of non-linear trends while maintaining computational efficiency and avoiding overfitting.

The GMM framework assumes a heterogeneous population consisting of G latent classes. Each county i (i = 1, …, N) is assumed to belong to one latent class g (g = 1, …, G). The probability of a county belonging to latent class g is modeled using multinomial logistic regression.

The observed log IR for each county was modeled using an I-splines transformation, which provided flexibility to capture non-linear changes over time. We specified quadratic fixed effects of time and normally distributed random effects of intercepts and slopes, which allowed counties within the same latent class to vary around the class mean trajectory. This choice enabled us to capture within-class heterogeneity, rather than assuming all counties in a class followed the same curve. In this way, our GMM differs from methods such as GBTM used by Dupre et al. (2021), which assume homogeneity within classes. After transformation, the observed log IR was assumed to follow a normal distribution with constant variance40. To reduce the risk of convergence on local maxima, we estimated each model using 100 random sets of starting values and retained the solution with the highest log-likelihood. Convergence was defined as a change in log-likelihood of less than 1e-6 across successive iterations. The number of latent classes in the model was determined using the sample-size adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (SABIC), with additional evaluation of Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and entropy. Models that produced classes with fewer than approximately 5% of counties or with low average posterior probabilities (< 0.70) were excluded41. These criteria are also consistent with prior latent trajectory studies (e.g., Vahabi et al. 202118). The final class solution was selected based on concordance across indices, with lower SABIC AIC, BIC values indicating improved fit, and entropy ≥ 0.70 reflecting acceptable class separation. In instances where competing models showed comparable fit, selection also considered trajectory interpretability and consistency across periods. Finally, the predicted marginal trajectory for each latent class was compared to the individual trajectories of a random subsample of 100 counties within the class to ensure that the model adequately represented the county-level trajectories.
