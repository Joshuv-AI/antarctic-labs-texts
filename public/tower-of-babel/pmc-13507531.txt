Navigating communication barriers in phlebotomy: Strategies and ethical tensions in routine care
Authors: Selina Diep, Lisa Purdy, Amanda VanSpronsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe emotional and ethical experiences of Medical Laboratory Assistants (MLAs) during challenging patient phlebotomy encounters is largely unknown. This study identified verbal and non-verbal strategies used by MLAs and explored how they navigate systemic and interpersonal pressures during patient resistance.MethodsWe held four online focus groups with 12 MLAs working in hospital and community settings in Alberta, Canada. Transcripts were analyzed using a combined inductive and deductive thematic analysis guided by the Framework method.ResultsParticipants described using plain-language explanations, distraction, translation resources, and support to facilitate phlebotomy involving pediatric patients, cognitive impairment, and language barriers. When these strategies failed, restraint was described as a last resort. These accounts suggest that escalation occurred within perceived constraints on authority and under pressure, rather than a preferred course of action.ConclusionPhlebotomy communication challenges are ethically and relationally complex. MLAs describe balancing procedural demands with patient assent under conditions of limited authority, suggesting a need for training and guidance that extends beyond technical proficiency.InnovationThis study moves beyond the traditional technical view of phlebotomy by examining the ethical and relational challenges experienced by MLAs during routine patient care. It identifies experiences consistent with moral distress and constrained clinical agency as underexplored features of communication barriers in phlebotomy practice.

Introduction

Venipuncture is a common invasive procedure that requires a physically close, time-limited interaction between the patient and phlebotomist. Obtaining a high-quality blood sample is critical for accurate laboratory results [1]. Phlebotomy training focuses on proper technique to minimize errors from identification, labeling, order of draw, or tube-mixing steps. Practical skills are often taught using an apprenticeship-style approach starting with simulated arms, and then moving on to supervised situations with actors, patients, or peers. However, interacting with patients also requires essential transferable skills such as communication, empathy, and conflict-management [2]. Despite increased awareness of the importance of effective communication skills [3], formal training programs remain inconsistent [4], with an expectation that these skills will be gained through experience. Additionally, practical training opportunities are constricting in many jurisdictions, in part due to significant workforce shortages [5]. Underdeveloped communication skills can lead to inconsistent practices, negative experiences, and poorer patient health outcomes [6]. There is a growing push to develop rigorous communication curricula and support ongoing professional development across healthcare training programs [7].

Communication challenges can occur within any patient-provider interaction, but are uniquely magnified during brief interactions like phlebotomy. Our study selected three specific patient populations representing distinct, high-frequency axes of communication and consent/assent challenges that medical laboratory assistants (MLAs) encounter. Pediatric care, cognitive impairment, and language barriers were prioritized as they depict clinical archetypes spanning the spectrum of threats to standard informed consent, where patient cooperation is routinely complicated by comprehension, clinical capacity or age. Research reveals that patients with language barriers are subject to unequal treatment including lack of access, misdiagnoses, treatment delays, a higher risk of readmission, and a longer length of stay despite socioeconomic status [8]. Phlebotomy involving patients with cognitive impairment (PCIs) can be complicated by loss of linguistic abilities, lack of response, and sudden changes in behaviour [9]. Finally, pediatric patients often experience heightened anxiety, distress, and resistance to further treatment if they had a negative experience in the past [10]. Studies identify downstream consequences in adulthood including healthcare avoidance, trypanophobia, and heightened pain sensitivity [10], [11]. Outcomes improve when practitioners prioritize strategies that make the experience comfortable [12]. By evaluating this specific triad, our study captures common phlebotomy scenarios in which patient cooperation, comprehension, or assent may be difficult to secure.

Given the frequency and potential impact of phlebotomy, the content and quality of these interactions warrant closer attention. In the Canadian healthcare landscape, the management of challenging patient encounters is governed by provincial consent legislation and professional ethical standards [13]. While legal guardians provide consent for minors or those with cognitive impairments, healthcare providers are ethically encouraged to seek patient assent, which is the active cooperation of the patient. However, a gap exists in the literature regarding the tension between clinical necessity and this ethical ideal for phlebotomists. Existing research on phlebotomy communication tends to focus on phlebotomist-physician interactions [14], while recent qualitative studies examining the patient perspective reveal a desire for higher-quality conversations about blood testing processes [15]. This study offers a novel empirical lens by centering how MLAs navigate ethical and communication challenges during routine phlebotomy encounters. This study addressed the following research questions: (1) How do MLAs experience and manage the relational and ethical complexities during patient-phlebotomist interactions involving language, cognition, or age-related factors?; and (2) How do MLAs perceive and navigate tensions within clinical decision-making related to patient cooperation, consent/assent, and the use of physical restraint during phlebotomy? This is particularly relevant as MLAs perform most of the phlebotomy in Canadian outpatient settings and a significant portion within hospitals, yet their strategic and emotional navigation of these encounters remains largely unexamined.

Materials and methods

We held four online focus groups with a total of 12 MLAs. We recruited participants via Canadian Society of Medical Laboratory Science email lists and snowball sampling. We targeted MLAs with at least two years of phlebotomy experience in either in-patient or out-patient settings. The sample size was deemed appropriate for an exploratory qualitative study based on the concept of ‘information power’, whereby in-depth dialogue among experienced participants yields sufficient insight into a specialized topic [16].

Focus groups were conducted via Zoom, lasting approximately 60–90 min and explored communication barriers, patient comfort, consent, and strategies for approaching patients. Sessions were facilitated by one trained undergraduate researcher (S. D.). A senior researcher (A.V. or L.P.) was present to take field notes. Participants had no prior relationship with the researchers. To support discussion of sensitive topics, the moderator established ground rules of confidentiality and emphasized the study's focus on lived experiences.

Focus groups were audio recorded, transcribed and verified for accuracy. Thematic analysis was conducted using a combined inductive and deductive approach, guided by the Framework Method [17]. A deductive matrix was initially established from the research questions and targeted patient populations (pediatric, cognitive impairment and language barriers). Transcripts were read, coded, and charted into a matrix using Microsoft Excel, with inductive coding used to capture insights that did not fit the initial categories. Iterative comparison across transcripts identified both population-specific and cross-cutting patterns, including communication strategies, restraint, moral distress, and ethical decision-making.

To support credibility, each researcher read all the transcripts. One researcher (S.D.) generated the initial coding framework, which was reviewed and refined by the senior researchers (A.V., L.P.) during regular team meetings. To guard against pre-existing assumptions from backgrounds in medical laboratory education and technology, the team engaged in ongoing reflexivity, intentionally seeking data that challenged a priori categories. Disagreements regarding code application were resolved through consensus-based discussions until a final analytical framework was established. An audit trail was maintained by comparing field notes against transcripts. To support transferability, descriptions of the study context and participant characteristics are provided. Ethical approval was granted by the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board.

Results

Participants had 2–21 years of phlebotomy experience (average of 9 years), and were currently working in Alberta, Canada. Five had exclusive in-patient experience and three had exclusive out-patient experience. The remaining four had experience in both settings. By the fourth focus group, the research team judged that the data provided sufficient depth and variation to address the study aims, with no major new analytic patterns emerging. Overall, participants described experiences related to communication challenges and the strategies they used to mitigate issues to ensure patient comfort and cooperation. Strategies employed during communication challenges

Patients with Limited English Proficiency

In this study, participants characterized these patients as cooperative, often attributing this to heightened attentiveness because of the language barrier. All participants described trying to access translation resources to mitigate the barrier. They mentioned a variety of approaches, including using in-person interpreters, phone-in interpreters, and mobile translation applications.

Several participants expressed a preference for patient-provided interpreters, such as family members or caretakers. Some participants described intentionally including the patient in the dialogue to maintain patient-centredness, noting they may still understand despite the language barrier:

“They generally have someone with them, who can translate so it can be a little bit easier. I like to kind of talk to both [the translator and patient]‚ just because the patient can't communicate back in the language, it doesn't mean they don't understand what you're saying.”

Access to institutional resources was reported as inconsistent. In these cases, participants noted that patients may provide their own:

“A fair amount of patients come with like an app, like a talk to translate app that you can just talk into their phone ... Or they can speak into their phone, and it will translate it to English for me.”

When formal translation methods were unavailable, participants relied on non-verbal cues and body language, such as pointing to the arm:

“I've had a lot of [patients with language barriers], but they usually understand it when I point to my arm. That's like the easiest way to communicate with them nonverbally.”

To facilitate this understanding, participants also utilized physical props (e.g. tourniquets, tubes) and intentionally simplified their speech by avoiding clinical jargon:

“Talking in your own little jargon and then you realize that you're kind of talking different languages, so you need to be able to communicate and put into plain plain language, so you can understand each other.”

Pediatric patients

Participants emphasized the importance of creating a positive first impression to facilitate long-term cooperation and manage pediatric distress. This often involves reassurance:

“I try to make it as interactive and engaging as possible. My goal is to make a positive first experience because they will remember that ... So, lots of encouragement as well.”

Participants identified procedural transparency, while not downplaying the discomfort, as a preferred method for gaining patient assent:

“I just try to talk to them in simple plain words that they know, and I don't lie to them. I don't want them to be unprepared, but I remind them that they're brave, and they've done lots of things that feel way worse like stubbing your toe or falling off your bike.”

To aid comprehension, participants reported using props, doing practice runs, and role-playing. Once the procedure began, distraction techniques were utilized, typically through conversation, toys, or videos on a portable device, to preoccupy patients and prevent premature movement while tubes were filling:

“I find the hardest part is if [the child] lets you poke them and while you're standing there waiting for the tubes to fill up, that's when they go, ‘Okay I'm done.’ So, I ask them ‘what's [your] favorite color? What's [your] sister's name? What grade?’ and I just get them talking, then they aren't just sitting there waiting for me to be done.”

Less frequent strategies included the use of a numbing topical. Participants with experience with numbing topicals strongly advocated for their increased use to lessen the sensory trigger of the procedure:

“Honestly numbing cream should be a requirement for pediatric collections, ... Usually, the kid is 90% of time okay [until] you do the actual poke. Although it only hurts for that one second, if they had the numbing cream, it probably wouldn't hurt at all”.

Complicating factor: parental involvement

Participants identified parental involvement as a variable that can either facilitate or complicate the phlebotomy encounter. Participants noted that parents may inadvertently undermine procedural transparency by deceiving their child or downplaying pain. In these instances, participants noted that the child often enters the encounter with a baseline of distrust, complicating the practitioner's ability to establish a rapport:

“It is very difficult when parents say, ‘you won't feel this, this won't hurt’ because they're going to figure out [really] quick that it does hurt, just for a second. And that, if they were lied to about that, what else are we lying to them about?”

While participants noted the negative impact of this deception, they expressed reluctance to confront parents regarding these misleading statements. Furthermore, participants noted that parental roles were variable, with some parents adopting a bystander role, remaining disengaged from the interaction entirely.

Patients with Cognitive Impairment (PCI)

While participants primarily discussed patients with dementia or age-related cognitive decline, some noted challenges arising from acute cognitive-impairment, such as intoxication. The primary strategy identified for this demographic was collaborative distraction, often requiring the assistance of a nurse, co-worker, or care-aid:

“A lot of the times it's just distraction. I'll have a nurse or someone else, maybe a partner phlebotomist, come in and just talk. [They] say, ‘oh, your hair is lovely, or what? Are these pictures of your kids?’ Things like that. Sometimes distraction works wonders for the confused.”

Another strategy was providing clear, simplified explanations. Many participants noted that they continue to explain the procedure regardless of the patient's apparent ability to comprehend or respond.

Use of restraints

Restraint was mentioned when discussing both pediatric patients and PCIs when primary communication strategies failed. Participants often enlisted help from parents, co-workers, and nurses. Participants reported a complex assessment process, weighing their own ability to apply restraint, the patient's age, and the patient's capacity for assent:

“It depends on if we can restrain them or not, if they're a smaller size let's say six to eight, and with the parents help we can safely perform the thing, we're going to do it.”

In pediatric cases, participants often experienced a tension between the child's verbal refusal and the parent's legal consent. Many reported prioritizing the parents' wishes to continue with phlebotomy:

“Sometimes even with pediatric patients at a certain age, they know what's going to happen they're telling you no, but the parents are like this needs to be done so we'll … apply restraints to make it happen.”

When asked about how they felt during these situations, participants mentioned being uncomfortable and morally conflicted. While those with more experience reported a higher level of clinical comfort or desensitization to the physical act of restraining, they emphasized it remained a last-resort measure. Even among experienced staff, the decision to restrain was viewed as a situational necessity to ensure patient care:

“I find [collecting from pediatric patients against their wishes] can be a little gray because you have the guardian who wants it done, so they'll sit them down and they'll force their arm out. I never feel the most comfortable because you can see how much the kid doesn't want it and I feel like I get in a bit of a sticky situation, ... But then I asked myself, ‘am I really the most comfortable doing this right now?’ Because I can very much clearly see that this kid doesn't want to get done.”

When discussing PCIs, the decision to restrain was mediated by the practitioner's assessment of coherence. If they felt the patient appeared aware, and yet would not consent, then the phlebotomist was reluctant to support restraint. However, if the patient was deemed non-coherent, then they would defer to the nurse's or caregiver's judgment:

“If the patient is fully coherent, I take the patient's word. If they're very confused [and] they don't know what's going on, then I take the caretaker's word for it. They might not know what's best for them at that moment, because they're not coherent at that time.”

When asked how they navigate these decisions, participants reported an internal hierarchy where diagnostic necessity was prioritized over immediate patient cooperation:

“It's really hard to make [patients] feel comfortable. We focus more on getting the blood work and the results, rather than carrying too much of the feeling at that point, especially if they're confused, or if they are pediatric who [do not understand] the importance of the test.”

However, prioritizing treatment over consent did not eliminate the moral distress associated with the encounter. Even when participants rationalized the procedure as being in the patient's best interest, feelings of unease remained:

“I wouldn't say like I'm desensitized ... before I used to be really uncomfortable, like this patient said ‘no,’ but I'm still doing it. [It's] conflicting myself but now, where it's part of patient care, you have to do what's best for them, even if they don't really know it at that time.”

To manage this emotional dissonance, participants described a self-preservation mechanism of ‘emotional distancing’ or ‘turning off’ their responses to focus strictly on technical proficiency:

“My brain turns off, to be honest, I just go into like blank and just focus on the job at hand, you know getting the getting the blood, ... following procedure and moving on”.

Other emotional impacts of communication challenges

Participants reported other emotional challenges that complicate their daily practice. One concern involved procedural delays and frustration arising from a lack of timely resources, such as interpreters. This could force a choice between delaying treatment or proceeding with suboptimal communication:

“I end up having to try and get nursing staff to find interpreters and stuff, but that is a long and hard process for them, and it is not convenient at all. We just sometimes have to wait till the patient's family comes in and can talk to them and explain it. So, sometimes we have to postpone the bloodwork.”

Beyond these logistical frustrations, a significant theme emerged regarding the internalized sense of powerlessness. This was characterized as a form of moral distress occurring when the phlebotomist felt they were violating a patient's autonomy because the patient lacked the cognitive capacity to understand the encounter:

“It's much more difficult. I'd rather have someone refuse or fight me, to be honest, because I feel horrible because this person can't - they have no idea why I'm doing it, or what I'm doing.”

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

This study suggests that phlebotomy encounters are ethically and relationally complex. When communication barriers arise, MLAs make situational judgments that balance procedural necessity, patient cooperation, and perceived responsibility for downstream care. While some strategies align with broader health literature, our findings extend prior work by challenging the view of phlebotomy as a purely technical task. Participants' descriptions of ‘turning off’ emotional responses suggest a coping response in the absence of formal ethical or communication frameworks. Because the brief, episodic nature of phlebotomy leaves little opportunity to build long-term relationships with patients, this coping mechanism may represent a protective response to the tension between procedure completion and respecting patient cooperation or assent.

For patients with limited English proficiency, participants commonly relied on translation to facilitate cooperation and understanding. Although effective language translation is associated with improved patient outcomes [6], this has not been examined specifically among MLAs. Consistent with prior research, participants described frequent reliance on family members as ad hoc interpreters [18] despite documented concerns related to health literacy, confidentiality, and communication of sensitive information [19]. Existing guidelines caution against the use of minors as interpreters except in emergencies [19], [20]. Participants also reported using mobile translation applications, although prior literature raises concerns about accuracy and compliance with privacy legislation [20]. Together, these findings suggest a gap between recommended best practices and the realities of phlebotomy workflows, where interpretation resources may be inconsistently available [8], [18], [20].

Strategies described for pediatric patients align with existing evidence supporting early preparation, honest explanation, and the use of distraction [21], [22], [23]. Participant emphasis on procedural transparency mirrors findings that prepared children experience less pain and distress [22]. MLAs described tailoring explanations to developmental capacity, underscoring that preparation alone often requires complementary strategies such as distraction [21], [23]. Props and role-playing were described by participants but were less prominent in the literature we identified. Previous studies have noted strategies such as the ‘cough trick’ [24], inflating balloons, or distraction devices [25]. None of our participants reported using these tools.

Our findings also support the need for better parent preparation and involvement. Participants described instances where parental deception undermined trust and increased distress. This finding reinforces prior recommendations that parents should honestly prepare their child for procedures [21]. Studies have found that parent involvement can reduce pain and feelings of anxiousness [12], [21], [26]. Existing literature further supports parents acting as distraction coaches [27] and using mindfulness strategies that may reduce distress for both parent and child [28]. Due to their proximity, MLAs may be positioned to support parents in adopting evidence-based preparation and coping strategies.

In our study, restraint was described as a strategy for pediatric patients and PCIs. Importantly, restraint did not emerge as a routine or preferred practice, but rather as a response to the perceived failure of earlier communication efforts and the presence of competing obligations. Participants consistently framed restraint as a last resort, undertaken in contexts of external pressure (parental insistence, nursing direction, diagnostic urgency) and accompanied by their own discomfort or moral distress. Restraints are known to cause anxiety and contribute to fear of future medical procedures, especially in children [21]. There have been calls to reduce or eliminate the use of restraints [29], [30], [31]. MLA descriptions of restraint should also be interpreted within their limited authority and visibility [32] as well as the short, episodic nature of phlebotomy encounters. Taken together, findings suggest that restraint may arise when diagnostic urgency, external pressures and limited authority converge, rather than as a preferred or endorsed practice. These findings also illustrate how external demands can override attempts to secure patient assent.

A patient's autonomy and consent are important components of satisfactory care [33]. Our participants often rationalized patient restraint by considering the phlebotomy necessary for proper treatment. However, participating in restraint was not without emotional impact. Participants reported distress and uncertainty, consistent with findings from other healthcare professions [34]. Phlebotomy encounters also expose a tension between patient-safety ideals and the lived realities of frontline workers. Although “speaking up” is an important safeguard, voice is shaped by hierarchy, anticipated interpersonal costs, and whether raising a concern feels safe and likely to make a difference [35], [36]. This tension may be particularly acute for MLAs, whose proximity to the patients' distress is paired with limited practical authority to challenge parents, nurses or other clinicians. This interpretation aligns with psychological safety literature showing that the ability to voice concerns is uneven across professional roles, and that workers in less institutionally powerful positions may have less room to question decisions despite shared responsibility for safe care [35].

This was a small exploratory qualitative study that focused on MLA accounts of phlebotomy encounters involving heightened communication and consent complexity, with participants drawn from one Canadian province. The data provided sufficient depth to address the study aims, but further study is needed to examine a broader range of communication contexts and patient populations. Focus groups may also introduce response bias, and discussion of uncomfortable topics can be constrained in a group setting. We mitigated these potential sources of bias through interviewer training and attention to confidentiality, but residual effects are possible.

These findings highlight the need for institutional policies that recognize phlebotomy as an ethically and relationally complex clinical interaction. Policies should support evidence-based patient preparation and engagement strategies, including honest explanation, age-appropriate distraction, and parent-supported coping approaches [31]. Clear guidance on restraint use, including de-escalation pathways, and debriefing is needed to support ethical decision-making and reduce moral distress among MLAs [37]. Targeted training in communication and ethical reasoning alongside reliable access to professional interpretation services may help bridge recommended best practices and phlebotomy workflows.

Innovation

Historically, phlebotomy education has focused predominantly on manual proficiency and standard protocols of professionalism, while giving comparatively little formal emphasis on the complexity of phlebotomist-patient encounters. Although the communication strategies described by participants are not unique to phlebotomy, their use within brief, invasive, diagnostically driven procedures have received little attention. This analysis offers a role- and context-specific account of how communication strategies may escalate towards restraint when diagnostic urgency, patient resistance, limited authority, and constrained institutional resources converge. It also documents the emotional labour and experiences consistent with moral distress described by MLAs. These findings align with contemporary understandings of morally salient events giving rise to psychological distress within organizational constraints [38], [39]. These insights open new avenues for education, organizational, and policy-level interventions aimed at supporting both patient-centred care and provider well-being in frontline roles that have historically received limited ethical and structural support [40].

Conclusion

This study shows that phlebotomy encounters require situated judgment, particularly when patient cooperation, diagnostic urgency, and limited MLA authority intersect. Limited training opportunities may leave frontline workers unprepared for patient resistance. High-quality simulation offers a practical pathway for building essential clinical reasoning skills [41], [42], but educational interventions alone cannot resolve these conflicts. When restraint is used, MLAs may absorb a psychological burden. These findings underscore a need for institutional resources that support ethical decision-making, de-escalation, and post-incident emotional processing.
