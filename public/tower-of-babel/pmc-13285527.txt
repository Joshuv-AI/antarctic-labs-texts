The use of hooks in upper instrumented vertebra to prevent proximal junctional complications: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hifza Babar, Dong Li, Jie Li, Zhen Liu, Zezhang Zhu, Yong Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) and proximal junctional failure (PJF) are leading complications after adult spinal deformity (ASD) surgery. Proximal hooks at the upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) may reduce stiffness transition and PJK/PJF risk, but comparative evidence is limited. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluates the association between hook use and PJK/PJF incidence.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Cochrane Library, and SpringerLink from 2000 to 2025. Studies related to the use of hooks versus other fixation methods (e.g., pedicle screws) in preventing PJK/PJF were compared and included. Data were extracted and analyzed. Risk of bias and publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test. Pooled estimates were calculated using random-effect models.ResultsMeta-analysis was conducted on five studies comprising 1,131 patients. For proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK; 2 studies, n = 183), hook fixation showed a trend toward lower incidence (22.0% vs. 29.1%) but did not reach statistical significance (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.46–1.25, p = 0.28). For proximal junctional failure (PJF; 3 studies, n = 948), hooks significantly reduced incidence (6.9% vs. 25.3%; RR 0.32, 95% CI 0.17–0.59, p < 0.001). Combined analysis of all five studies demonstrated a significant overall protective effect of hooks against proximal junctional complications (RR 0.50, 95% CI 0.31–0.82, p < 0.05). No significant publication bias was detected.ConclusionsHook fixation at the upper instrumented vertebra significantly reduced proximal junctional failure (PJF) by 68% (RR 0.32, p < 0.001) compared to pedicle screws alone. For proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK), a non-significant trend favored hooks (RR 0.76, p = 0.28). These findings support hooks as a protective strategy against clinically significant junctional complications, particularly PJF, in adult spinal deformity surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03757-8.Keywords: Proximal junctional kyphosis, Proximal junctional failure, Adult spinal deformity, Hooks, Pedicle screws

Background

Adult spinal deformity (ASD) is a complex, multiplanar spinal alignment disorder with prevalence increasing with age from 32% to 68% [1, 2]. Surgical correction of ASD is increasingly associated with proximal junctional complications, specifically proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) and proximal junctional failure (PJF) that typically develop within the first 2 years [3, 4]. PJK is defined as ≥ 10° increase in proximal junctional angle (PJA) above the upper instrumented vertebrae (UIV), whereas PJF involves structural failure, neurological deficit, or need for revision surgery [3, 5]. Reported incidence varies, with systematic reviews estimating PJK in 17% to 39% and PJF in 1.4% to 19% of ASD patients [6–11].

The etiology is multifactorial, blending mechanical and biological mechanisms. Patient-specific risk factors include elevated BMI (> 30 kg/m²), age (> 65 years), and osteoporosis. Surgical factors include long rigid constructs, disruption of posterior soft-tissue tension band, and combined anteroposterior approach [7–9, 12]. While pedicle screws provide excellent segmental control and pull-out strength, they act as a rigid “hard-stop” at the upper end. The screw heads may damage the crucial supraspinous and interspinous ligaments during insertion, effectively disrupting the primary posterior tension band that resists kyphosis [13]. Furthermore, screws concentrate stress at the UIV within the pedicle and vertebral body, making it a common site for rod fracture [2]. The combination of ligamentous disruption, paravertebral muscle dissection, and a rigid lever arm creates a predisposition for proximal junctional complications.

The use of proximal hooks, particularly transverse process hooks arranged as claws, offers an alternative fixation method. Hooks engage the posterior vertebral elements while preserving the interspinous and supraspinous ligaments, thereby maintaining the native posterior tension band [13–15]. They act as load-sharing devices that distribute forces across the lamina, potentially reducing the risk of vertebral body fracture at the UIV [16, 17]. Biomechanical studies suggest that hooks provide a more gradual motion transition from the rigid instrumented spine to the mobile proximal segments, minimizing sudden stiffness change, a concept termed the “soft landing technique” [8, 11, 17, 18]. Comparative studies have demonstrated that using transverse process hooks, a type of proximal hook, can provide a more gradual motion transition than pedicle screws alone, potentially reducing the risk of PJK/PJF [18–20].

Although these biomechanical advantages are well-described, comparative clinical evidence examining hook versus pedicle screw fixation at the UIV for preventing PJK and PJF remains limited. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluates whether transverse process hooks at the upper instrumented vertebra reduce the incidence of PJK and PJF compared with pedicle screws alone in adult spinal deformity surgery.

Protocol registration

This systematic review was not prospectively registered. Although a protocol was prepared on PROSPERO, registration was not completed prior to manuscript completion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they: (1) compared the use of hooks with other fixation methods (e.g., pedicle screws) at the upper instrumented vertebra in adult spinal deformity; (2) reported on the incidence of PJK and/or PJF; (3) had a minimum follow-up of 1 year. Studies were excluded if they: (1) included predominantly adolescent populations (< 18 years); (2) were case reports, case series without comparison groups, reviews, or cadaveric studies; (3) had follow-up < 12 months; (4) did not report PJK/PJF as an outcome; (5) were duplicate publications of the same cohort.

Data extraction and analysis

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers. Extracted data included patient demographics, surgical details, incidence of PJK/PJF, and clinical and radiographic outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan Web, Cochrane Collaboration). Due to the clinical distinction between proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK, a radiographic finding) and proximal junctional failure (PJF, structural failure requiring intervention), these outcomes were analyzed separately as the primary analyses. An exploratory combined analysis of all proximal junctional complications was also performed to provide a comprehensive overview. However, this combined analysis should be interpreted with caution given the clinical heterogeneity between outcomes and is considered secondary to the outcome-specific analyses.

Pooled risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a random-effects model (Mantel-Haenszel method) to account for anticipated heterogeneity across studies. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with values < 40% considered low. The observed I² = 0% across all analyses indicates that the variation in effect sizes across studies is no greater than what would be expected by chance alone. This low heterogeneity may reflect the consistent direction of effect favoring hooks across studies, but should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of studies, as I² has limited precision with few studies [21].

For studies with zero events in one treatment arm, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to enable calculation of risk ratios. Publication bias was assessed visually through funnel plot symmetry. Although Egger’s test was performed (intercept: -1.72, 95% CI: -3.73 to 0.28, p = 0.191), these results should be interpreted with caution as statistical tests for publication bias are underpowered when fewer than 10 studies are included [22]. Therefore, while no clear evidence of publication bias was detected visually, the possibility of bias cannot be definitively excluded.

Due to the small number of included studies (k = 5), formal leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were underpowered and could not reliably assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate. However, visual inspection of forest plots suggested that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall results, as all studies showed effect sizes in the same direction.

Radiographic outcomes (sagittal vertical axis, lumbar lordosis) and revision rates were synthesized descriptively due to inconsistent reporting across studies. Due to variations in measurement methods, timing of assessment, and summary statistics across studies, quantitative meta-analysis of sagittal vertical axis (SVA) and lumbar lordosis (LL) was not feasible. These parameters are therefore presented descriptively in Table 3 to provide context for the patient populations studied. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Risk of bias and level of evidence assessment

The risk of bias for the included comparative studies was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). The NOS judges studies on three domains, selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and determine outcomes. A star system is used, with a maximum score of 9 indicating the highest quality. The level of evidence for each included study was rated according to the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) levels. Prospective studies were classified as Level II evidence, and retrospective studies as Level III evidence. Any disagreements between reviewers in the assessment of risk of bias or level of evidence were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer.

Results

A total of 430 articles were identified through database searching. After removal of duplicates (n = 44), 386 articles underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 290 articles were excluded as they did not meet the initial eligibility criteria (e.g., case reports, reviews, non-comparative studies, pediatric populations). The remaining 96 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Following full-text review, 91 articles were excluded with reasons (study design not comparative [n = 38], no hook versus screw comparison [n = 29], insufficient follow-up [n = 12], duplicate cohort [n = 7], other reasons [n = 5]). Notably, several cadaveric biomechanical studies and studies in adolescent populations were excluded as they did not meet the clinical comparative criteria, though they are discussed in the narrative review to provide biomechanical context. Ultimately, 5 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis, encompassing 1,131 patients that formed the basis of this report (Fig. 1). The mean follow-up duration was around 36 months.

PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the process of study identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and final inclusion in the systematic review and meta-analysis

Among the five included studies (1,131 patients), two studies (183 patients) evaluated PJK and three studies (948 patients) evaluated PJF. In both analyses, hook fixation was associated with lower complication rates compared to pedicle screws alone (Table 1; detailed characteristics in Table 2). The use of hooks in the upper instrumented vertebra region showed a trend towards lower revision surgery rates and better maintenance of spinal alignment, as indicated by sagittal vertical axis (SVA) and lumbar lordosis (LL) measurements. Due to variations in measurement methods, timing of assessment, and summary statistics across studies, quantitative meta-analysis of sagittal vertical axis (SVA) and lumbar lordosis (LL) was not feasible. Detailed clinical and radiographic postoperative outcomes are presented in Table 3. These metrics provide insights into the immediate and short-term effects of different fixation methods on patient’s health and spinal alignment. Furthermore, it highlights the necessity of evaluating both clinical results and patient-reported quality of life when assessing the efficacy of various fixation techniques in spinal fusion surgeries. According to Matsumura [6] Short Form-36 questionnaire, the physical function for TPH at 2-year after surgery improved significantly from 42.4 to 64.3. Reoperation rate was recorded at 10.2% for PS group and 4.6% for PH group [23].

Risk of bias and level of evidence

The assessment of risk of bias and level of evidence was performed as detailed in the Methods. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale scores ranged from 8 to 9 (Table 4), indicating generally high methodological quality. Detailed justification for each domain score is provided in Table 5. The single prospective study by Line et al. [19] received the maximum score of 9. The four retrospective studies [6, 11, 24, 26] each scored 8, losing one point in the comparability domain due to lack of adjustment for key confounders such as bone mineral density, rod material, or degree of sagittal correction, which are known to influence proximal junctional complications [2, 8, 19, 27]. Notably, while the NOS scores suggest high quality, the retrospective design of four studies means they remain susceptible to selection bias and unmeasured confounding. In terms of level of evidence, one study was prospective (Level II) and four were retrospective (Level III), as summarized in Table 1.

PJK/PJF incidence and comparative analyses

The analysis for PJK and PJF was conducted separately due to the differences in their clinical severity and the available data. Meta-analysis of two studies comprising 183 patients demonstrated a consistent trend toward lower PJK incidence with hook fixation, though this did not reach statistical significance. The pooled incidence was 22.0% (18/82) in the proximal hook group compared to 29.1% (32/110) in the pedicle screw group, with a pooled risk ratio of 0.76 (95% CI 0.46–1.25, p = 0.28) [6, 24]. Matsumura et al. [6] found that transverse process hooks at the UIV were associated with lower incidence of PJK compared to the pedicle screw group (17.6% vs. 27.3%), with significantly smaller changes in the proximal junctional angle in the hook group (5.0° vs. 19.0°, p = 0.04). This suggests that transverse process hooks may prevent vertebral collapse in cases of UIV fracture, offering a potential advantage over pedicle screws.

Hook fixation at the UIV significantly reduced the incidence of PJF, with a pooled incidence of 6.9% (11/159) in the hook group compared to 25.3% (101/399) in the pedicle screw group. The pooled risk ratio was 0.32 (95% CI 0.17–0.59, p < 0.001), indicating a 68% relative risk reduction with hooks [11, 19, 26]. Reported PJF incidence in the literature ranges from 1.4% to 19% [10, 11]. Park et al. [11] identified nonuse of a transverse process hook as a significant independent risk factor for PJF (OR 5.556, 95% CI 1.205–19.621, p = 0.028) in patients who achieved ideal sagittal correction according to age-adjusted alignment targets. Similarly, another paper from Park et al. [26] demonstrated that hook fixation at UIV + 1 (rather than at the UIV) significantly reduced PJF development compared to pedicle screws alone (2.4% vs. 33.1%), suggesting that placing the hook one level above the construct terminus may further optimize load distribution.

Comparative analyses have demonstrated that transverse process hooks at the upper instrumented vertebra provide a more gradual motion transition than pedicle screws, potentially reducing the risk of proximal junctional complications [20, 28]. Colantonio et al. [28] conducted an in vitro biomechanical study comparing bilateral proximal hooks and bilateral pedicle screws at the UIV, finding that hooks provided a “softer” transition between the fused and unfused spine, reducing motion at the proximal junctional level (UIV + 1) after cyclic loading. Cho et al. [29] provided clinical evidence supporting the effectiveness of transverse process hooks when combined with advanced imaging techniques. Using K-means clustering-based stratification of CT Hounsfield units at the UIV to determine optimal hook insertion sites, they observed no PJK cases in the hook group compared to 29.6% in an equivalent pedicle screw group among 47 patients followed for 2 years, indicating that spinal hooks can significantly reduce the likelihood of PJK occurrence when combined with image-guided placement [29–31]. To provide a comprehensive overview despite these distinct outcomes, we also performed an exploratory analysis pooling all five studies to assess overall proximal junctional complications. However, due to the clinical heterogeneity between PJK (a radiographic finding) and PJF (structural failure requiring intervention), this combined analysis should be interpreted with caution and is considered secondary to the outcome-specific analyses presented above.

Meta-analysis

According to the presentation in Fig. 2, a meta-analysis of five studies comparing the incidence of PJK and PJF in patients treated with proximal hooks versus pedicle screws, along with separate data on PJK (Fig. 3) and PJF (Fig. 4) were performed using random effects model with Mantel-Haenzel method to compare the risk ratio. The combined risk ratio of PJK/PJF was recorded 0.50 with 95% confidence interval of 0.31–0.82 (p < 0.05), demonstrating a significant protective effect of hook fixation. For PJF, the pooled risk ratio was 0.32 (95% CI 0.17–0.59, p < 0.001), indicating a 68% relative risk reduction with hooks. For PJK, the pooled risk ratio was 0.76 (95% CI 0.46–1.25, p = 0.28). Heterogeneity was low across all analyses (I² = 0%), indicating that effect sizes across studies were consistent in both magnitude and direction.

Publication bias

The funnel plot (Fig. 5) assesses the publication bias in PJK/PJF studies that are included in the meta-analysis, as well as explaining individual data on PJK in Fig. 6 and PJF in Fig. 7. The symmetrical distribution of points does not indicate a potential publication bias. This is further supported by the Egger’s test, which did not show statistical evidence of funnel plot asymmetry for the combined analysis (intercept: -1.72, 95% CI: -3.73 to 0.28, t: -1.684, p = 0.191) or for the PJF analysis (intercept: -0.99, 95% CI: -2.27 to 0.29, t: -1.518, p = 0.371).

However, with fewer than 10 included studies, statistical tests for publication bias are underpowered, and funnel plot asymmetry cannot be reliably assessed. Therefore, while no clear evidence of publication bias was detected, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as the possibility of bias cannot be definitively excluded.

Radiographic outcomes

The radiographic outcomes suggest that both hooks and pedicle screws are effective in maintaining and improving spinal alignment post-surgery. The slight reduction in PJK/PJF incidence rate has been observed with hooks, which may provide a more gradual motion in transition, potentially reducing mechanical stress at the proximal junctional region [18]. The use of TPH at the UIV or UIV + 1 may be a preventive measure against the development of PJK/PJF, although results are not consistent across all studies. The choice of upper instrumented vertebrae (UIV) implant type is expected to be crucial in mitigating the risk of PJK [13]. Matsumura [6] and Erkilinc [32] found a lower incidence of PJK with PH, whereas Tsutsui [23] found a higher incidence of PJK with PH in elderly patients. Colantonio et al. [28] conducted an in vitro biomechanical study comparing bilateral PH and bilateral PS at UIV. The study found that PH provided a “softer” transition between the fused and unfused spine, reducing motion at the proximal junctional level (UIV + 1) after cyclic loading.

Discussion

Our pooled data demonstrates that placing proximal hooks (primarily transverse process hooks) at the upper instrumented vertebrae (UIV) is associated with reduced proximal junctional complications in adult spinal deformity surgery. In combined analysis of 1,131 patients, hook fixation showed a 50% lower risk of overall proximal junctional complications compared to pedicle screws alone (RR 0.50, 95% CI 0.31–0.82, p < 0.05). This effect was particularly pronounced for PJF, where hooks were associated with a 68% relative risk reduction (RR 0.32, 95% CI 0.17–0.59, p < 0.001). For PJK, a consistent trend favoring hooks was observed (22.0% vs. 29.1%; RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.46–1.25), though this did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the smaller sample size (n = 183) and the radiographic rather than clinical nature of the outcome.

Biomechanical studies have described hooks as providing a “softer” transition between the rigid instrumented spine and the flexible proximal segments [8, 17, 18, 20]. Colantonio et al.‘s cadaveric work revealed that bilateral proximal hooks at UIV decreased angular motion at UIV + 1 after cyclic loading compared with bilateral pedicle screws [28]. Finite-element analyses confirm that hooks lessen flexion-extension stress at the proximal disc and posterior elements [14, 33, 34]. This stress distribution from proximal hooks, rather than maximal fixation strength, appears to be the key mechanism for reducing PJK/PJF risk, particularly when the posterior ligamentous tension band is already compromised [16, 17, 35]. However, it is important to emphasize that hook augmentation represents one component of a multifactorial prevention strategy rather than a standalone solution [8, 19, 36]. Optimal outcomes likely require combining appropriate implant selection with adequate sagittal correction, preservation of posterior ligamentous structures, and patient-specific risk stratification [11, 18, 19, 27]. The higher revision rates with all-hook constructs reported by Kuklo et al. [37] (6.4% vs. 2.4% for screws) reflect hooks used throughout the spine, not selectively at the UIV as in our study. This distinction is critical. Our findings support a hybrid approach where pedicle screws provide robust fixation distally while proximal hooks at the UIV serve as a stress-reducing interface, combining the advantages of both implants without compromising construct integrity [37].

The clinical benefit of “softer transition” appears more significant for long-segment fusions with UIV in the lower thoracic spine (T9-T11) [6, 11]. This region serves as a critical junction between the rigidly instrumented lumbar vertebrae and the mobile thoracic segments, which may derive particular advantage from the stress-distributing properties of hooks. The clinical significance of PJF reduction with hooks cannot be overstated. Hostin et al. [38] reported that 41% of PJF patients required revision surgery within 6 months, and Hart and colleagues [39] emphasized that PJF subjects patients to additional perioperative risks and increased healthcare costs. Our finding that hooks reduce PJF by 68% suggests this low-cost intervention may have substantial clinical and economic implications. While biomechanically plausible, the efficacy of proximal hooks augmentation for constructs ending in the upper thoracic vertebra is less definitively established by the current pool of studies [28, 40].

According to previous studies, pedicle screws provide greater pull-out and torsional resistance, particularly in osteoporotic bone [25, 41–43]. Hackenberg et al. reported nearly double the axial pull-out strength of screws versus hooks in low bone-mineral-density thoracic spines [25]. However, the clinical sequelae of this difference may be less relevant at the transition level. Screws that are “too strong” may create abrupt stress concentration, whereas hooks that are “strong enough” allow more gradual load transfer [16, 17]. Our pooled data support the concept of biomechanical superiority of screws that does not translate into lower complication rates at the UIV. The favorable outcomes with hooks apply mainly to the proximal thoracic or upper lumbar spine, Choi and Jo demonstrated that S1 foraminal hooks used to augment S1 pedicle screws failed to maintain sagittal alignment and exhibited high loosening rates [12]. Therefore, the protective benefit of hooks appears site-specific and cannot be assumed to apply to lumbopelvic constructs [11, 12].

Advanced age (> 65 years), elevated BMI (> 30 kg/m²), and osteoporosis are established risk amplifiers for PJK/PJF [1, 2, 7, 8]. Patient-specific factors including paraspinal muscle quality and bone mineral density have emerged as critical determinants of mechanical complications. Using a machine learning approach, Li et al. [27] identified paraspinal muscle fat infiltration and vertebral bone quality (VBQ) score as top-ranked predictors of mechanical complications in degenerative scoliosis, surpassing traditional radiographic parameters. These findings align with our observation that hook augmentation may be particularly beneficial in patients with compromised bone quality, as the load-sharing properties of hooks reduce intraosseous stress at the UIV [14, 16, 17]. Matsumura et al. noted that the benefit of hooks persisted after adjusting for age and baseline sagittal imbalance [6]. Moreover, Kozaki’s finite-element study suggested that combined claw hooks plus sublaminar wires may further reduce UIV stress in osteoporotic models [14]. These observations imply that high-risk patients may derive the greatest benefit from hook augmentation. Murakami et al. compared pedicle screws alone versus screws augmented by infra-laminar hooks in the thoracolumbar spine and found hybrid fixation improved stiffness without increasing adjacent-level stress [17]. Cho and colleagues analyzed CT Hounsfield units via K-means clustering to determine optimal hook insertion sites and observed no PJK cases, compared with 29.6% in an equivalent pedicle-screw group [29, 30]. Although these studies lie outside our formal inclusion criteria, they underscore the potential of hybrid constructs and image-guided selection to refine patient-specific strategies [27, 33, 34].

Several prophylactic strategies have been proposed to reduce proximal junctional complications, including vertebral cement augmentation, ligament tethers, and hook fixation [8, 18, 19, 36]. A recent meta-analysis by Li et al. [36] demonstrated that cement augmentation at the UIV and UIV + 1 reduced PJF by 36% and revision surgery by 71%, particularly in older patients with osteoporosis. While cement augmentation addresses bone quality directly, hooks preserve the posterior tension band and provide gradual load transfer [13, 15]. The choice between these techniques, or their combined use should be guided by patient-specific factors including bone mineral density, muscle quality, and sagittal alignment [11, 14, 19, 27]. This multifactorial approach addresses the important observation that prevention cannot rely on a single technique.

Addressing the multifactorial nature of PJK/PJF prevention, it is important to recognize that proximal junctional complications increase with rigid rod constructs, large sagittal corrections, and advanced age [2, 8, 19]. Therefore, prevention must be multifactorial. Hooks alone cannot fully prevent PJK or PJF, as evidenced by the residual complication rates in our hook groups (6.9% for PJF, 22.0% for PJK). Patient factors (bone quality, sagittal alignment, age) and surgeon technique (degree of correction, rod contouring, soft tissue preservation) play equally critical roles [7, 8, 13, 27, 35]. The use of hooks in short-segment constructs may not provide the same benefit observed in long fusions, as the mechanical demands at the proximal junction differ substantially [11, 28].

Clinical Implications

Based on our findings, several specific recommendations can be made for surgeons considering hook augmentation at the UIV:

Patient selection: Consider proximal hooks for patients at elevated risk of junctional complications, particularly those with advanced age (> 65 years), osteoporosis (T-score ≤ -2.5), poor paraspinal muscle quality (high fat infiltration), or large preoperative sagittal imbalance (C7-SVA > 50 mm).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the small number of included studies (n = 5) precludes robust publication bias assessment and limits the precision of pooled estimates. Second, critical variables including rod diameter/material, degree of sagittal correction, UIV level, and bone mineral density were not consistently reported or adjusted for across studies. This reflects the inherent limitations of retrospective designs and highlights the need for prospective studies with standardized reporting of surgical details and patient-specific factors. Although NOS scores ranged from 8 to 9, indicating generally high methodological quality, it is important to note that four of five studies were retrospective and thus susceptible to selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Critical variables including bone mineral density, rod diameter/material, and degree of sagittal correction were not consistently adjusted for across studies, which may influence the observed effect sizes. Third, the mean follow-up of 36 months may be insufficient to capture late-onset complications beyond five years [44]. Fourth, surgical techniques and hook configurations varied across studies, not all used transverse process hooks, and some utilized laminar hooks or hybrid constructs. Fifth, inconsistent reporting of patient-specific factors like bone mineral density and muscle quality limited our ability to perform subgroup analyses identifying which patients benefit most from hook augmentation. Finally, while hooks were associated with reduced PJF, residual complications occurred in 6.9% of patients, confirming that no single prophylactic measure can completely prevent these events [18, 19].

Future research

A multicenter, patient-blinded, randomized controlled trial comparing UIV proximal hooks versus pedicle screw fixation is warranted. Key design elements should include: stratification by osteoporosis grade (DXA T-score ≤ -2.5), muscle quality assessment, and age > 65 years. Standardized radiographic protocol with annual PJA and SVA measurements for a minimum of five years. Patient-reported outcome measures (SF-36, ODI) and implant-related adverse events as secondary endpoints and health-economic evaluation accounting for implant cost and revision burden. Until such evidence emerges, our findings provide moderate-quality support for the use of transverse process hooks at the UIV in appropriately selected patients.

Conclusion

In adult spinal deformity surgery, proximal hooks (primarily transverse process hooks) at the upper instrumented vertebra significantly reduce the incidence of proximal junctional failure compared to pedicle-screw-only constructs. For proximal junctional kyphosis, the evidence remains inconclusive, with a non-significant trend favoring hooks. Surgeons should consider using proximal hooks as a simple, low-cost adjunct for patients at high risk of junctional failure, accepting that the evidence for PJK prevention is less definitive.
