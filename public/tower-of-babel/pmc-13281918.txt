Introduction

Coronary artery anomalies are observed in ∼1%–2% of patients undergoing coronary angiography.1 Among these, the anomalous origin of the left circumflex artery (LCx) from the right coronary sinus is a relatively common variant, typically following a retroaortic course. While contemporary classifications distinguish malignant interarterial courses from benign retroaortic variants, ‘benign’ refers specifically to the low risk of sudden cardiac death rather than immunity from atherosclerotic disease.2 As the population ages, acute coronary syndromes (ACS) involving these anomalous vessels are likely to be encountered more frequently.

In most patients, a retroaortic anomalous LCx remains clinically silent and is often discovered incidentally during coronary angiography or computed tomography coronary angiography (CTCA). However, when ACS occurs in these vessels, the atypical origin may delay identification of the culprit lesion, particularly in the setting of inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), where right coronary artery (RCA) occlusion is usually presumed. Recognition of this anatomical variant is therefore critical not only for diagnostic accuracy but also for procedural planning and device selection. We report a case of inferior STEMI caused by a retroaortic anomalous LCx in which vessel geometry and perioperative considerations dictated a tailored, stent-free percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) strategy.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old man with diabetes, hypertension, and current smoking was emergently transferred to our hospital with suspected STEMI. He had been admitted to another hospital 5 days earlier because of semi-obstructive rectal cancer and was awaiting semi-emergent surgery.

Electrocardiography showed ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed infero-posterior wall motion abnormalities. Emergency coronary angiography demonstrated a straight left main trunk continuing into the left anterior descending artery without visualization of an LCx (Figure 2A). The RCA showed no significant stenosis (Figure 2B).

(B) Right coronary angiography demonstrated no significant stenosis in the right coronary artery. (C) Selective angiography revealed a separate ostium of the anomalous LCx arising inferior to the RCA origin within the right coronary sinus. (D) Severe proximal stenosis of the anomalous LCx.

The absence of a culprit lesion in the RCA despite clear electrocardiographic evidence of inferior STEMI prompted reconsideration of the coronary anatomy. Given the straight continuation of the left main trunk into the left anterior descending artery without a visible LCx, an ostial LCx occlusion was initially suspected. To differentiate between an occluded native vessel and an anomalous origin, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) was performed from the left anterior descending artery to the left main trunk. IVUS confirmed complete absence of a left-sided LCx ostium.

Subsequent exploration of the right coronary sinus revealed a separate ostium inferior to the RCA (Figure 2C, Supplementary material online, Video S1), identifying a retroaortic anomalous LCx with severe proximal stenosis (Figure 2D).

PCI was performed using a 6 French SAL 1.0 guiding catheter with guide extension support. IVUS demonstrated a lipid-rich plaque and a marked longitudinal elliptical vessel geometry as shown in Figure 3A and Supplementary material online, Video S2. The ratio of minimal to maximal vessel diameter at the culprit lesion was 0.66. The proximal anomalous LCx followed a relatively straight course after its retroaortic origin, without significant positive remodelling. The vessel lumen appeared consistently non-circular over multiple frames both proximal and distal to the culprit plaque, suggesting that the elliptical configuration represented a constitutive anatomical feature rather than secondary remodelling due to atherosclerosis. No major plaque rupture cavity or extensive thrombus burden was observed.

Percutaneous coronary intervention and intravascular ultrasound findings. (A) Pre-intervention IVUS demonstrated a lipid-rich plaque and persistent elliptical vessel morphology. (B) Final angiographic result after DCB treatment demonstrated TIMI 3 flow without significant dissection.

Lesion preparation was performed using non-compliant and scoring balloons. The scoring balloon was inflated cautiously from low pressure with gradual escalation under repeated IVUS assessment to achieve controlled lesion preparation while minimizing excessive vessel injury prior to drug-coated balloon (DCB) treatment. Adequate lumen gain was subsequently achieved without angiographically significant or flow-limiting dissection. These findings supported a strategy aimed at preserving native vessel architecture. Considering both the atypical vessel geometry and the need for semi-emergent oncologic surgery, stent implantation was avoided. A 3.0 × 30 mm Paclitaxel-coated balloon was inflated at nominal pressure for 60 s and reassessed with IVUS (Figure 3B). Final angiography confirmed TIMI 3 flow (see Supplementary material online, Video S3).

Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor was initiated at the time of PCI. Following the onset of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, the regimen was transitioned after 1 week to a combination of a direct oral anticoagulant and a single antiplatelet agent, considering the upcoming surgery and bleeding risk. Early CTCA confirmed vessel patency (Figure 4A) and laparoscopic rectal resection was successfully performed 6 weeks after PCI. Six-month CTCA again confirmed sustained patency (Figure 4B–D). Over 2 years of follow-up, no recurrent ischaemic events occurred, and no inducible ischaemia was detected on perfusion imaging.

Discussion

This case highlights three critical considerations in managing anomalous coronary arteries in the ACS setting.

First, a retroaortic anomalous LCx can pose a significant diagnostic challenge during inferior STEMI by mimicking RCA occlusion. When the LCx is not visualized from the left coronary sinus, a systematic search is mandatory. Our proactive use of IVUS to confirm the absence of an LCx ostium within the left main trunk was essential to differentiate an anatomical anomaly from a total ostial occlusion, thereby guiding selective engagement of the anomalous vessel.

In addition, left ventriculography (LVG), although not routinely performed in all primary PCI procedures, may provide indirect anatomical clues in such ambiguous scenarios. Contrast opacification along the retroaortic atrioventricular groove during LVG can occasionally suggest the presence of an anomalous LCx, facilitating targeted coronary engagement. In selected cases where angiographic uncertainty persists, LVG may therefore serve as an adjunctive diagnostic modality. Such multimodal reasoning under time pressure is crucial to avoid delayed reperfusion in patients with atypical coronary anatomy.

Second, although the retroaortic course is generally considered benign with respect to sudden cardiac death,3 it remains susceptible to atherosclerotic plaque rupture. As the population ages, clinicians must be prepared for ACS in these variants.4 Yamamoto et al.5 previously described inferior STEMI caused by plaque rupture in a retroaortic LCx confirmed by IVUS, demonstrating that conventional atherosclerotic mechanisms occur in these anomalous vessels. Recognition of this variant is therefore essential for both accurate diagnosis and timely revascularization.

Third, vessel geometry is an important, yet often underappreciated, determinant of PCI strategy. In this case, IVUS revealed a persistent elliptical morphology, likely reflecting anatomical constraint imposed by the retroaortic course. Contemporary coronary stents are engineered for near-circular expansion; therefore, marked eccentricity in such vessels may predispose to suboptimal expansion or asymmetric radial force distribution, factors associated with adverse clinical outcomes.6 Although robust outcome data specific to anomalous vessels are lacking, these geometric considerations influenced our decision-making. Although definitive visualization of plaque rupture was not obtained, IVUS findings were considered consistent with the clinical presentation and ischaemic territory. In the present case, the rationale for avoiding stent implantation was based primarily on concerns regarding scaffold–vessel mismatch and asymmetric expansion in a vessel with persistent elliptical morphology. DCB therapy was selected not to modify vessel geometry itself, but rather to provide local anti-restenotic drug delivery while avoiding permanent metallic implantation. Recent evidence suggests that DCB therapy may achieve comparable clinical outcomes to drug-eluting stents, even in selected de novo and larger coronary lesions.7

Furthermore, the need for semi-emergent oncologic surgery necessitated minimizing the duration of DAPT, consistent with contemporary guidelines for patients at high bleeding risk.8 The absence of recurrent ischaemic events over two years and sustained patency on serial CTCA support the durability of this geometry-guided, individualized strategy.

Conclusion

A retroaortic anomalous LCx can manifest as inferior STEMI and mimic RCA occlusion. In such anatomical variants, where persistent elliptical geometry may compromise optimal stent expansion, a geometry-guided, stent-free strategy using a DCB represents a rational and effective approach particularly when clinical circumstances necessitate a minimized antithrombotic regimen.
