Exploring nurses’ presenteeism from the effort-reward imbalance perspective in the Chinese context: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Siqi Wang, Chaonan Kong, Mo Li, Zhiyu Zhang, Li Gai, Xinying Yu, Yanan Guo, Ling Fan, Fan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to the unique challenges of their work environment and job demands, nurses often work even when ill. Nurses’ presenteeism, defined as working while ill impairs performance or engagement, poses a significant threat to both the quality of care and patient safety. Previous studies have primarily measured nurses’ presenteeism using scale scores or thresholds, an approach that focuses solely on the study variable while neglecting inter-individual differences. Recognising individual differences is the key to conducting personalised analyses and subsequently developing targeted intervention strategies.AimThis study aimed to employ latent profile analysis to identify distinct subgroups and characteristics of nurses’ presenteeism within the Chinese cultural context, and incorporate the effort-reward imbalance model to explore factors associated with presenteeism.MethodsA cross-sectional online study involving 2,871 eligible nurses conducted through the questionnaire platform named “Wen Juan Xing”. The general information questionnaire, the McCloskey/Mueller Satisfaction Scale, the Stanford Presenteeism Scale and the Effort-Reward Imbalance Questionnaire were used as the measurement instruments. SPSS (version 25.0) was used for the descriptive statistics and multicollinearity analysis. Mplus (version 8.0) was used to conduct the common method bias test, latent profile analysis and covariate analysis.ResultsNurses’ presenteeism was classified into three profiles, namely, the “Low presenteeism-Dual Stability Group” (29.5%), the “Medium Presenteeism-Restricted Concentration Group” (20.5%), and the “High Presenteeism-Impaired Task Execution Group” (50.0%). These subgroups demonstrated divergent patterns across the effort-reward imbalance perspective (effort, reward, and overcommitment), individual characteristics (age and gender), and occupational information (years of working experience and job satisfaction).ConclusionsThe three subgroups of nurses’ presenteeism in this study exhibited significant heterogeneity. Different subgroups exhibited distinct characteristics. The significant heterogeneity in the three potential profiles can help nursing managers develop individualised and targeted interventions. Background

As nurses are guardians of health, disseminators of health knowledge, and promoters of health behaviours, their health status is crucial to the healthcare system. However, the physical and mental health of nurses is not satisfactory, as they are in a high-stress, high-demand, and high-load work environment for long periods [1]. The surge in patient volume, prevention, and control work, particularly during epidemics and infectious disease outbreaks, and the considerably high risk of infection increase the workload of nurses and their work pressure and burnout, which seriously threaten their physical and mental health [2–4]. A study revealed that 40.99% of nurses in the sample exhibited mental health issues, while 54.25% reported poor physical health [1]. However, due to staff shortages and social responsibilities, nurses persist in working even when their physical and mental wellbeing is compromised [5]. Nurses, who comprise the largest group in a healthcare team, have a real stake in whether they are fully engaged or adequately perform when working with illnesses for the benefit of nurses, patients, and hospitals. This phenomenon of “working while ill but not fully engaged or adequately perform” is termed presenteeism [6–8]. Nurse presenteeism compromises the quality of care and increases the risk of adverse patient outcomes, such as medication errors or the omission of critical nursing tasks [9, 10].

A key prerequisite for presenteeism is working when unwell. Nurse working when unwell is a global phenomenon, with a higher prevalence rate than in other professions [11, 12]. A meta-analysis incorporating 28 studies from 14 countries revealed that the overall prevalence of nurse working when unwell approached 50% [13]. A cross-sectional survey conducted among healthcare personnel in Switzerland revealed that the prevalence of nurse working when unwell stood at 84.3% [14]. Influenced by traditional thought and culture, working while ill is considered in China an expression of dedication, encouraging employees to sacrifice themselves for the sake of their work [15]. A study conducted in Henan Province, China, revealed that as many as 94.25% of nurses self-reported engaging in working when unwell within the preceding six months [16]. A survey of 3,491 nurses across 14 hospitals in Shandong Province, China, revealed that the prevalence of working when unwell among nurses stands at 70.6% [3]. The high incidence of working when unwell is closely linked to reduced work efficiency and work engagement [9]. Therefore, it is of paramount importance to address the issue of presenteeism within China’s nursing sector.

Nursing is a profession demanding considerable physical, emotional and mental resources. The efforts of nurses are beyond measure, requiring both physical exertion to perform demanding care procedures and emotional investment to comfort patients and their families, while frequently enduring the psychological toll of witnessing suffering and death [17]. However, nurses often face inadequate rewards, such as limited opportunities for advancement and unsatisfactory salaries [18]. As a result, the nursing profession suffers from a severe effort-reward imbalance. A meta-analysis incorporating 60 studies revealed that the incidence of effort-reward imbalance among nurses reached 52.3%, exhibiting an upward trend year on year [19]. An imbalance between effort and reward threatens nurses’ physical and mental wellbeing [20]. As defined, the impairment of nurses’ physical and mental health serves as a prerequisite for presenteeism. This led us to hypothesise that effort-reward imbalance was related to the emergence of presenteeism. We employed the effort-reward imbalance model to test the aforementioned hypothesis. This model not only emphasises the influence of external resources (effort and reward) but also highlights the impact of an individual’s internal resources (overcommitment) [21–23]. However, the effort-reward imbalance model has been under-applied in studies of nurses’ presenteeism [24].

Previous studies have primarily employed scale scores or score thresholds to measure the level of nurses’ presenteeism. This approach considers only the study variable while disregarding inter-individual differences, which are invariably present in the real world. Identifying individual differences is a key to formulating targeted intervention strategies. Latent profile analysis is a method capable of identifying individual differences based on their response patterns to specific variables [25]. Compared to other “individual-centred” approaches such as mean-splitting and cluster analysis, LPA employs model fit metrics alongside practical significance to group research subjects [26].

Therefore, this study aimed to employ latent profile analysis to identify distinct subgroups and characteristics of nurses’ presenteeism within the Chinese cultural context. It incorporated the effort-reward imbalance model to explore factors influencing presenteeism. We hoped to provide researchers and nursing managers with a reference framework for implementing more precise preventive and intervention measures to address the phenomenon of nurses’ presenteeism.

Methods

Study design

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies [27].

Setting and participants

This study was conducted in March 2023 at a Grade A tertiary hospital in Shenyang, Liaoning Province, China. This hospital ranks among the foremost in Northeast China. Convenience sampling was used in this study. Recruitment invitations were extended to nurses meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria by the respective nursing unit managers. Inclusion criteria required nurses to hold a valid qualification certificate, have completed professional registration, and be engaged in clinical nursing practice. Exclusion criteria included nurses who were absent from their posts during the survey period due to vacation or further study. Ultimately, 3,167 eligible nurses took part in the study. Sample size

There is currently no simple, closed-form formula or calculator available for estimating the sample size for LPA. However, an operational guide on LPA recommended a minimum sample size of 300 to 500 [28]. Another study suggested that the minimum sample size for LPA should be between 300 and 1000 [29]. To ensure the reliability of the research, we selected 1,000 as the minimum sample size for the LPA.Given a potential dropout rate of 20%, the minimum sample size required for this study was calculated to be 1200 nurses. The final sample size was 3167 nurses, meeting the requirements for this study.

Measurements

General information questionnaire

The general information questionnaire was a self-designed questionnaire compiled by the research team in conjunction with previous relevant literature, including demographic characteristics (age, gender, education level, marital status, presence of children) and work-related characteristics (years of working experience, job title, nature of work).

The McCloskey/mueller satisfaction scale (MMSS)

The MMSS was developed by McCloskey [30] and revised by Mueller and McCloskey [31]. This scale was used to measure eight components of nurse job satisfaction, including satisfaction with extrinsic rewards (three items), scheduling (six items), family/work balance (three items), collegial relationship (two items), social/interaction opportunities (four items), professional opportunities (four items), praise/recognition (four items), and control/responsibility (five items). Zheng [32] culturally translated and modified the MMSS to generate a Chinese version that deleted the entry “opportunities for part-time work”. A 5-point Likert scale ranging from “very unsatisfied” (1 point) to “very satisfied” (5 points) was used to assess each item. The sum of item scores ranged from 30 to 150 points, with higher scores associated with higher job satisfaction among nurses. In our study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient and the McDonald’s ω coefficient for the total scale were 0.98 and 0.98.

The stanford presenteeism scale (SPS-6)

The SPS-6 was developed by Koopman et al. [33]. The scale was independent of the type of illness and occupation and was among the most widely used, practical and evidence-level presenteeism scales available [34, 35]. Zhao et al. [36] compiled and revised the scale to generate a Chinese version containing two dimensions with six items: work limitation (items 1, 2, 3, and 4) and work energy (items 5 and 6). Work limitation measures the ability to “get the job done” when a health problem arises, implying that illness affects efficiency. Work energy measures the ability to “avoid distractions and concentrate on work” when a health problem arises, implying that illness affects engagement. Each item was assessed by a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “complete disagreement” (1 point) to “complete agreement” (5 points). Items 5 and 6 had reversed response scales and were recoded prior to calculating the total score [36]. The sum of item scores ranged from 6 to 30 points, with higher scores associated with more severe presenteeism. The Cronbach’s α coefficient and the McDonald’s ω coefficient for the overall scale were 0.71 and 0.78 in this study.

The effort-reward imbalance (ERI) questionnaire

The ERI Questionnaire was developed by Siegrist et al. [23] and then translated and revised by Yang et al. [37], with validation among healthcare professionals. The scale consists of three dimensions with 23 items: effort (6 items), reward (11 items), and overcommitment (6 items). The responses were assessed by a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1 point) to strongly agree (5 points). The sum of the scores for items within each dimension constituted the score for that dimension. The scale is also usually analyzed using two indicators: effort-reward ratio and overcommitment. Effort-reward ratio = total score of the effort dimension / (total score of the reward dimension × 6/11), 6/11 was the adjustment factor. Effort-reward ratio > 1, suggesting that effort-reward imbalance occurred. Those with total overcommitment dimension scores in the upper 1/3 are in a state of high overcommitment. Five items (Items 10, 11, 12, 13, and 20) are reversed response scales and are recoded prior to calculating the total score [23]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the dimensions of effort, reward, and overcommitment were 0.89, 0.88, and 0.80 respectively in this study. The McDonald’s ω coefficients for the three dimensions were 0.89, 0.86 and 0.81.

Data collection

This study collected data through an anonymous online questionnaire using the “wenjuanxing” platform (www.wjx.cn). Following standardised training, respective nursing unit managers organised WeChat groups comprising eligible nurses within their respective departments. Nursing unit managers then distributed this study’s basic information, questionnaire completion requirements and the questionnaire link with a QR code to these WeChat groups. Completion of the questionnaire indicated that the participant agreed to participate in the study. To avoid missing data, the questionnaire was configured that all items must be completed before submission was permitted. Participants could complete the questionnaire at any time within one week of its distribution. Nursing unit managers took no responsibility for compiling statistics or enforcing mandatory participation within the department. To ensure the quality of the questionnaires, invalid questionnaires were manually excluded according to pre-set deletion criteria. The deletion criteria were as follows: (1) Repeated responses: Screening was performed based on the employee number provided by the participant. Only one response record was kept for repeated responses, and the rest were considered invalid questionnaires. (2) Total response time was too short: Questionnaires with a total response time of less than 5 min were considered invalid. (3) Errors in basic information: The basic information provided by the hospital (employee’s number, department, date of birth, and time of joining the hospital) was checked against the information filled out in the questionnaire. Questionnaires answered incorrectly in the basic information were considered invalid. We employed a workflow that separates identity verification from data analysis to ensure data confidentiality and participant anonymity. Identity verification stage: After receiving data containing personally identifiable information, the two researchers limited their work to data cleaning. The entire process did not involve highly sensitive information such as names or national identity card numbers. After verifying and excluding invalid questionnaires, the personal information used to identify the nurses was removed, and their Employee’s number were replaced with random codes. The two researchers involved in this stage provided the anonymized, cleaned dataset but did not participate in the subsequent data analysis. Data analysis stage: Data analysis was conducted solely on anonymised datasets, with analysts having no access to any identifiable information. Participants were clearly informed of all the above procedures.

Statistical analysis

We employed SPSS 25.0 and Mplus 8.0 for data analysis. P < 0.05 with a two-sided test indicated that the results of the study were statistically significant.

SPSS 25.0 was used for descriptive statistics and multicollinearity analysis. Descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were reported in the form of frequencies (n) and proportions (%), while continuous variables that follow an approximately normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Multicollinearity analysis. The variance inflation factor (VIF) being 5 or greater indicated the presence of multicollinearity.

Mplus 8.0 was used to conduct the common method bias test, latent profile analysis and covariate analysis. Common method bias test. Controlling for the effects of an unmeasured latent methods factor(ULMC) was used to measure common method bias that may have been introduced into the measurement results during the data collection process of the self-assessment questionnaire [38]. If the addition of a method factor to a CFA model that originally combined multiple scales results in a significant improvement in model fit indices (with increases of more than 0.1 in CFI and TLI, and decreases of more than 0.05 in RMSEA and SRMR), this indicates the presence of severe common method bias [39]. Latent profile analysis. The six-item scores of the SPS-6 scale were used as external indicators. We used the “multiple random starts” setting (Starts = 1000, 250) to ensure the model’s stability. The number of profiles increased sequentially from one. The model fit indicators include the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), Lo-Mendell-Rubin (LMR), Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio test (BLRT), and Entropy [40]. The lower the AIC, BIC and aBIC values, the better the model fits. The P values for LMR and BLRT are < 0.05, indicating that the model of this category has a significant improvement in the fit of the model of the previous category. The closer the value for Entropy is to 1, the higher the model classification accuracy is, and in general, Entropy ≥ 0.8 is acceptable. To ensure that the results are interpretable and substantial, LPA requires that each profile include more than 5% of the sample. The best fit model was selected by combining the model fit indicators and their practical significance. Covariate analysis. We employed the three-step approach (using the R3STEP command) to examine the relationship between the latent profiles and the predictor variables [28]. These covariates included demographic characteristics (age, gender, education level, marital status, presence of children), work-related characteristics (years of working experience, job title, nature of work, Job satisfaction) and effort-reward imbalance (effort, reward, Overcommitment). Sensitivity analysis. To ensure the robustness of the results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using two analytical models of the effort-reward imbalance. Model A comprised three variables: effort, reward and overcommitment. The analysis was based on the scores for these three variables. Model B, on the other hand, included two measures: the effort–reward ratio and overcommitment, both of which were treated as categorical variables in Model B. Model A was presented as the main analysis result.

In total, 296 invalid questionnaires were excluded from this study, resulting in 2,871 valid questionnaires with a valid response rate of 90.7%. After including the common method factor, the model fit indices did not show any significant improvement. The RMSEA remained unchanged, while the CFI and TLI changed by only 0.001, indicating that there is no significant common method bias in this study [39].

In this study, the total score for the SPS-6 scale was 15.61 (± 4.32), the score for the work limitation dimension was 10.07 (± 4.12), and the score for the work energy dimension was 5.53 (± 2.26). On the ERI scale, the effort dimension scored 19.17 (± 5.29), the reward dimension scored 40.74 (± 7.36), and the overcommitment dimension scored 18.71 (± 4.25). Discussion

This study aimed to investigate potential subgroups of presenteeism among registered nurses at a Grade A tertiary hospital in Shenyang, Liaoning Province, China, from the perspective of effort-reward imbalance, and to identify the specific influencing factors for each subgroup. By employing a “person-centred” latent profile analysis, this study identified three distinct subgroups of presenteeism exhibiting significant differences. These subgroups demonstrated divergent patterns across the effort-reward imbalance perspective (effort, reward, and overcommitment), individual characteristics (age and gender), and occupational information (years of working experience and job satisfaction). These findings highlighted the need for nursing managers to develop targeted intervention strategies to address the heterogeneous nature of presenteeism, thereby optimising staff wellbeing and organisational productive capacity.

Current situation of nurses’ presenteeism

In this study, the SPS-6 scale scores of 2,871 nurses were 15.61 (± 4.32), higher than those reported in another study of nurses in Jilin Province, China 12.07 (± 4.8) [41]. This discrepancy might stem from differences in the tier of hospitals selected by the two research institutes, the size of their respective sample populations, and the socio-economic environments of their study subjects. Regrettably, due to variations in the version of the SPS-6 applied and the research methodologies employed, comparing studies across different countries presents considerable challenges. However, working when illness among Chinese nurses may be relatively high for the following reasons. Firstly, under the influence of Confucianism, working while ill is regarded as honourable and noble in China and is strongly praised [5, 15]. Secondly, there is a shortage of nursing staff in China. In large general hospitals in China, the daytime nurse-to-patient ratio is 1:8.0, and the overnight nurse-to-patient ratio is 1:23 [42]. When a nurse is on leave, a low nurse-to-patient ratio makes it difficult for the nurse manager to find a replacement and increases the workload of other nurses [16, 43, 44]. Nurses choose to stick to work because of guilt [12, 45].

Types of nurses’ presenteeism

Using latent profile analysis, this study identified three distinct profiles of nurses’ presenteeism: “Low presenteeism-Dual Stability Group”, “Medium Presenteeism-Restricted Concentration Group”, and “High Presenteeism-Impaired Task Execution Group”. This study was conducted at a Grade A tertiary hospital in Shenyang, Liaoning Province, where a sample of nurses was recruited for the research. This study was conducted in a leading hospital in Northeast China with unique features in terms of patient complexity and service scale, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. The hospital admits a large number of patients with severe and complex conditions, resulting in an extremely high nursing workload and persistent work-related stress. Despite active staffing adjustments, a relative shortage of nurses remains under the long-term heavy workload. Driven by professional responsibility, concerns about increasing colleagues’ workload, and pressure from the performance appraisal system, nurses frequently continue working even when unwell. Over time, working when unwell has become a common workplace phenomenon, which may lead nurses to hide or underreport their health problems.

Characteristics of nurses’ presenteeism

In the “Low presenteeism-Dual Stability Group”, nurses generally exhibited lower levels of presenteeism, as evidenced by lower scores on the dimensions of work limitation and work energy. This indicated that nurses in this group possessed strong resilience against disease interference, effectively balancing work and health to ensure both work efficiency and quality. Nurses in the “Medium Presenteeism- Restricted Concentration Group” scored lowest on the four items within the work limitation dimension, yet scored highest on the two items within the work energy dimension. This indicated that the impact of illness on this group’s work primarily centred on attention. Although work efficiency is maintained, diminished attention heightens the risk of nursing errors, thereby compromising patient safety [7, 45]. The “High Presenteeism-Impaired Task Execution Group” exhibited the highest levels of presenteeism among these nurses. This group of nurses scored highest on the work limitation dimension, but scored at an average level on the two items under the work energy dimension. This indicated that the condition primarily impacted these nurses’ work efficiency, though they retained a degree of concentration. However, the decline in work efficiency increased the workload for other nurses working alongside them [5]. Nursing managers should prioritise the “Medium Presenteeism-Restricted Concentration Group” and the “High Presenteeism- Impaired Task Execution Group”. When it becomes unavoidable to require nurses to work while unwell, nursing managers must allocate duties judiciously according to each nurse’s level of attention and capacity for work.

This population of “High Presenteeism- Impaired Task Execution Group” in this study mainly consists of aged ≤ 35, female, with 11 to 20 years of work experience, experiencing lower job satisfaction, perceiving high effort and low reward, and exhibiting high overcommitment. These factors may be associated with nurses’ presenteeism, in that they are related to a higher likelihood of working while sick. Nurses aged ≤ 35 years situate at a critical juncture for skill acquisition and professional advancement. Associated with aspirations for self‑fulfilment, this group shows a higher likelihood of working while unwell [46]. Furthermore, wage loss associated with sick leave may be related to the higher tendency of young nurses to work while ill [12]. From a physiological perspective, women go through specific stages such as menstruation, pregnancy and the menopause, which are often accompanied by a range of uncomfortable symptoms including abdominal pain, fatigue and mood swings. However, these discomforts, which arise from physiological characteristics, are not generally regarded as valid grounds for taking sick leave [12].A Chinese study has revealed that as many as 62% of female nurses work while unwell [47]. Another Chinese study also found that gender was a significant predictor of nurses’ presenteeism [48]. Nurses with 11 to 20 years of professional experience were at a pivotal stage in their career development. They must fulfil clinical duties while also undertaking departmental management and teaching responsibilities, making them less easily replaceable [5]. Nurses with higher job satisfaction maintain harmonious relationships with colleagues and supervisors, experience a relaxed and pleasant working atmosphere, and perceive greater organisational support and psychological security in their roles. They do not worry about work backlogs or psychological burdens arising from taking sick leave [49]. Moreover, nurses’ satisfaction with employment safeguards such as welfare benefits and career development has to some extent alleviated their sense of job insecurity and occupational stress, which in turn was related to a lower likelihood of working while ill [50]. High effort and low reward indicates effort-reward imbalance. Effort-reward imbalance is a state of high effort-low reward, where the imbalance between the need to pay a lot and the corresponding pay-off is negligible, which can stimulate work-related stress and increase the risk of physical or mental health damage [21, 23]. Occupations provide individuals with a “key social role” and the “social value” that such “key social role” brings to individuals is also considered to be the rewards of work [23]. Nurses also choose to work during sickness to prevent their original social roles from being broken [43]. Moreover, due to staff shortages and heavy workloads, nurses are compelled to persist in working while ill [3]. They over-extend themselves, yet receive neither commensurate remuneration nor the social recognition their efforts deserve. A lack of proportional rewards for their efforts is associated with decreased motivation among nurses, which is linked to lower productivity while physically present at work and higher presenteeism [41]. Overcommitment is an individual’s self-behavioural response pattern in the face of high job demands. Nurses with high overcommitment tend to contribute excessively to work to obtain the expected rewards of social respect, hospital attention, and career development in the face of high job demands [22]. That is, high overcommitment among nurses is associated with a greater tendency to work toward their goals even when in poor health. Additionally, items 19 to 23 in the measure of overcommitment, such as “When I get home, I can easily relax and ‘switch off’ work” reflect a state of psychological detachment in which the individual is spatially and psychologically removed from work during non-work time, free from the distractions of work problems, and ceases to think about work [51]. Working and non-working hours spent on work are associated with excessive depletion of physiological and psychological resources. In the long term, this pattern is related to physical and mental exhaustion and lower productivity among nurses [52].

Therefore, the essence of reducing nurses’ presenteeism is to minimise the opportunities for them to work while unwell. Firstly, measures should be taken to protect nurses’ physical and mental well-being. Nursing managers should allocate tasks reasonably during nurses’ working hours, avoiding excessive workloads and overly demanding tasks that must be completed outside of working hours. Hospitals and departments should also organise recreational activities to help alleviate the stress nurses face in their work. Secondly, when nurses’ physical or mental health is compromised, people-centred management measures should be implemented. This includes establishing a more flexible sick leave policy, streamlining the sick leave application process, and refining the system for wage deductions during sick leave, thereby fostering an organisational culture that prioritises “healthy working”. Finally, it is important to address the concerns of nursing staff. Nurses face heavy workloads, frequent incidents of workplace violence, and generally low pay and social status, resulting in a mismatch between their efforts and the rewards they receive. Hospitals should improve their promotion systems, enhance staff benefits, prioritise nurses’ career development, and provide them with opportunities for self-fulfilment.

Strengths and limitations

This study offered the following strengths. Firstly, this study examined nurses’ presenteeism within the context of Chinese culture, holding cultural significance. Secondly, this study employed latent profile analysis to investigate inter-individual heterogeneity in nurses’ presenteeism within a relatively large sample. This provided a reference for developing precise prevention and intervention measures targeting nurses’ presenteeism in future research. Thirdly, this study offers novel intervention approaches to mitigate or prevent the occurrence of nurses’ presenteeism by examining the imbalance between effort and reward.

Despite its theoretical and practical significance, this study had some limitations. First, this study only investigated nurses in a Grade A tertiary hospital in Shenyang, China. Therefore, the findings may not be applicable to all nurses in China. Future multi-centre studies may be conducted. However, the sample size of this study was adequate, and the surveyed hospital is among the top hospitals in Northeast China, making the sample representative. Second, information collected using convenience sampling method and self-reporting method is subjective and prone to recall and selection biases. Third, each item in the questionnaire must be answered before submission, which may lead to reporting errors. Forth, the involvement of department heads in distributing the questionnaires might increase nurses’ participation, even if unintentionally. However, we required department heads to merely distribute and explain the questionnaires. They must not compel nurses to respond or keep track of responses, so as to ensure, as far as possible, that nurses participate voluntarily.

Conclusions

This study employed a person-centred latent profile analysis approach to reveal the inherent heterogeneity in nurses’ presenteeism. The findings identified three distinct subgroups: the “Low presenteeism-Dual Stability Group” characterized by the ability to maintain focus and complete tasks on time while unwell; the “Medium Presenteeism-Restricted Concentration Group” characterized by the tendency to become easily distracted while working during illness, yet still managing to complete tasks on time; the “High Presenteeism-Impaired Task Execution Group” characterised by persisting at work despite illness, which affects work efficiency but allows for a degree of concentration. This has significant practical implications for nursing management, as it enables nursing managers to accurately identify different subgroups of nurses, thereby laying a solid foundation for implementing more targeted and effective prevention and intervention strategies to reduce nurses’ presenteeism. Furthermore, grounded in the theory of effort-reward imbalance, this study delved deeply into the relationship between this factor and nurses’ presenteeism. Through this exploration, new frontiers have been opened up for subsequent research and practical measures to tackle nurses’ presenteeism, which will ultimately make a significant contribution to elevating nurses’ overall well-being and the quality of healthcare services.
