Spatio-temporal dynamics of malaria incidence in children under five years of age and correlation with meteorological variability in the North and Far North regions of Cameroon between 2015-2022
Authors: Abas Mouliom, Fredline Ilorme, Joël Marcelin Ateba, Germaine Ekoyol, Jean Fotso, Jean Pierre Kidwang, Janvier Tchopwe, Sali Djele, Joel olivier Dzika, Estelle Essangui Same, Lawrence Ayong, Innocent Mbulli Ali, Else Carole Eboumbou Moukoko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChanges in environmental temperature, humidity, and rainfall can affect the survival and behavior of the mosquito vector of malaria parasites, thereby influencing malaria incidence rates. We investigated the spatiotemporal dynamics of malaria incidence in two geo-epidemiological transmission zones (North and Far North regions) of Cameroon and assessed its correlation with various meteorological factors. Malaria and meteorological data were obtained from the local health information system and by remote sensing, respectively. Malaria risk was stratified according to spatial heterogeneity of incidence. The strength of the association between malaria incidence and meteorological variables was measured using distributed lag nonlinear models (DLNM). A generalized additive quasi-Poisson regression model was used to account for over-dispersion in the number of cases. The values observed during the non-epidemic season were used as exposure reference values for the effects predicted by the DLNM.Between 2015 and 2022, the maximum recorded incidence was 190.6 (189.5–191.8) per 1000 person-years for the North region and 243.8 (242.9–244.7) for the Far North region. Stratification of health districts by malaria risk showed that most health districts are located in areas of very low to low transmission. From more than one millimeter rainfall per day (1 mm/day) on average, the risk of malaria transmission was 1.2 times higher (RR = 1.2, 95 % CI: 1.1–1.2) in the North region and 1.4 times higher (RR = 1.4, 95 % CI: 1.3–1.5) in the Far North region and increased with rainfall intensity. Humidity at 70 % increased the risk of transmission 2 times higher (RR = 2, 95 % CI: 1.4–2.8) in the North region and 3.7 times higher (RR = 3.7, 95 % CI: 2–4.6) in the Far North region. Collectively, the data reveal significant year-to-year variations in malaria incidences in the North and Far North regions of Cameroon, and a strong association with meteorological factors such as humidity and rainfall.Keywords: Malaria, Spatio-temporal incidence, Meteorological correlates, Cameroon

Background

Malaria remains one of the deadliest infectious diseases in tropical and subtropical regions, despite being both preventable and curable. This disease continues to pose a major threat to public health, particularly among children under five years of age and pregnant women who remain the most vulnerable to severe forms of the disease (Rudasingwa and Cho, 2020). In sub-Saharan Africa, where healthcare resources are limited, malaria is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality, significantly hindering socioeconomic development efforts (Nzabakiriraho and Gayawan, 2021; Geleta and Ketema, 2016).

According to the latest WHO World Malaria Report (2024), there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases worldwide in 2023, with 597,000 deaths attributable to the disease. The African Region remains the most affected, accounting for approximately 94 % of cases and 95 % of deaths (World Health Organization, 2024). Despite this persistent burden, notable progress has been made since the early 2000s, largely due to vector control interventions, rapid diagnostic testing, and improved access to treatment, which helped avert an estimated 663 million clinical cases between 2000 and 2015 (Achieving the Malaria MDG Target: Reversing the Incidence of Malaria 2000–2015, 2015). In this context marked by both advances and persistent challenges, the WHO has established, through its Global Technical Strategy 2016–2030, an ambitious roadmap aimed at accelerating the reduction of the malaria burden and progressing toward its elimination (Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030, 2015). This strategy adopted is based on four pillars that include rapid and effective case management, targeted chemoprevention, strengthened vector control, and robust epidemiological surveillance. Its application has already shown encouraging results in some Sahelian countries such as Senegal, where seasonal transmission has declined significantly (Wotodjo et al., 2017). However, these gains remain unevenly distributed and fragile due to financial, logistical and human resource constraints (Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030, 2015; Guo-Ding and Cao, 2019).

In Cameroon, malaria remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children under the age of five years. In 2021, it accounted for 29 % of all outpatient consultations in the country (Programme, 2022). Between 2015 and 2021, the number of reported cases increased continuously, reaching 3,069,521 confirmed cases out of 4,513,640 people tested in 2021, 46 % of which were severe cases (Programme, 2022). During the same period, the national incidence rose from 79.5 to 113.4 cases per 1000 inhabitants, and Cameroon now contributes approximately 2.6 % of the overall malaria burden in sub-Saharan Africa (World Health Organization, 2024). This upward trend underscores the need to better understand the drivers of transmission in order to tailor control strategies.

Malaria transmission in Cameroon exhibits marked spatial heterogeneity, structured into three distinct eco-epidemiological zones that are dominated by Plasmodium falciparum, and primarily mediated by vectors of the Anopheles genus, whose distribution varies across regions (World Health Organization, 2024; Programme, 2019; Ndiaye et al., 2021; Otambo et al., 2022). These include the Sahelian zone (Far North), where transmission is seasonal and short (1–3 months), with an intensity of 1 to 100 infectious bites per person per year; the Sudano-Sahelian zone (North and Adamawa), characterized by seasonal transmission lasting 6–9 months and an intensity of 100 to 200 bites; and the equatorial zone (South and forested areas), where transmission is perennial and ranges from 100 to 400 infectious bites per year (Programme, 2019).

This stratification sharply contrasts the northern regions, where transmission is intense and seasonal, with the southern regions, where it is continuous and generally more stable (World Health Organization, 2024; Programme, 2019). The main vectors are Anopheles gambiae s.l., Anopheles funestus, and Anopheles arabiensis, the latter being more common in most sub-Saharan African countries (Ndiaye et al., 2021; Otambo et al., 2022; Kiam et al., 2025; Josse et al., 1987). In the North and Far North regions, A. gambiae s.l. and A. funestus remain the predominant vector species (Josse et al., 1987).

The spatial variability in transmission results from complex interactions among ecological, climatic, and anthropogenic factors (Josse et al., 1987; Ouedraogo et al., 2018). A thorough understanding of these dynamics is essential for tailoring vector control strategies and reducing the malaria burden in the country.

Numerous studies have demonstrated the influence of rainfall, temperature, and humidity on transmission dynamics, often with a time lag of 1 to 3 months (Jaleta et al., 2013). Human-driven environmental changes, such as intensive agriculture or deforestation, can also have ambivalent effects: reducing transmission in some areas while increasing vector populations in others, particularly in semi-arid zones (Chung, 2016). These interactions complicate the disease dynamics and alter the spatial distribution of transmission hotspots, which tend to shift with rainfall patterns and temperature variations (Catry et al., 2018; Ekpa et al., 2023).

In this context, it is crucial to enhance the understanding of the spatiotemporal dynamics of malaria, particularly in the two northern regions of Cameroon where interventions such as seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) are being implemented. This study aims to analyze the geo-epidemiology of malaria and identify the meteorological factors associated with its transmission in the North and Far North regions of Cameroon, to contribute to better intervention planning.

Methods

Study site

The study covered 48 health districts located in the North and Far North regions of Cameroon (Fig. 1). Together, these two landlocked regions cover a total area of 99,822 km2, with 65,576 km2 in the North and 34,246 km2 in the Far North. According to estimates published in 2021 by the Central Bureau of Population Censuses and Studies (BUCREP) and reported by the Ministry of Territorial Administration, the combined population of the two regions was approximately 6.4 million inhabitants, with over 2.4 million in the North and nearly 4 million in the Far North. Furthermore, the official report on priority target populations, published in 2021 by the Ministry of Public Health, indicated that children under five years of age particularly vulnerable to malaria accounted for nearly 1.5 million individuals, including 549,757 in the North and 974,093 in the Far North (Ministry of Public Health (MINSANTE), 2021).

Incidence trends in the two regions from 2015 to 2022.

From a health perspective, each district comprises multiple health facilities that provide primary healthcare services and actively participate in epidemiological surveillance through the national DHIS2 system. Hospital-based malaria morbidity is particularly high, with estimated proportions of 36.1 % in the Far North and 52.1 % in the North, compared to a national average of 39 % (Programme, 2022). These values correspond to the proportion of confirmed malaria cases among all consultations recorded in health facilities, regardless of cause. Similarly, the proportion of deaths due to malaria among all recorded deaths in health facilities is estimated at 30.9 % in the Far North and 37.6 % in the North. This hospital-based mortality attributable to malaria remains higher than the national average of 24.5 % (Programme, 2022).

From an ecological perspective, the two study regions differ in their eco-climatic characteristics. The North region belongs to the Sudano-Sahelian zone, characterized by an average annual rainfall ranging between 900 and 1200 mm, which supports a relatively long malaria transmission season. In contrast, the Far North falls within the Sahelian zone, which is more arid, with average annual rainfall varying between 350 and 900 mm. Overall, both regions are marked by low rainfall, decreasing progressively from south to north (Lavarenne, 2024; World Bank Group, 2022). They also experience a clearly defined alternation between a long dry season lasting six to seven months and a rainy season lasting five to six months. Thermal conditions are also highly variable, with a cool period from November to February, followed by intense heat waves until the return of the rains (Cameroon Meteorological Department, 2023).

The combination of a high malaria burden, a large proportion of vulnerable children, and unique climatic conditions makes the North and Far North regions a priority study site for understanding the determinants of malaria transmission and improving malaria control strategies.

Data sources

Malaria data

For the two study regions, malaria cases due to Plasmodium in children under five years of age, confirmed either by rapid diagnostic test (RDT) or microscopy, were extracted by health district from the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2) for the period from January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2022. These data, collected and reported by health facilities, are consolidated and validated at each level of the health system pyramid to ensure their quality. Monthly malaria incidence was calculated using the following formula: (Confirmed cases × 1000) / (Population × Time), based on demographic data from national projections and programmatic target estimates (National Institute of Statistics (INS). Demographic Projections and Estimates of Priority Targets for Various Health Programs and Interventions, 2021). The 2015 population was extrapolated using simple linear regression, and district-level population figures were obtained from the Ministry of Public Health (Ministry of Public Health (MINSANTE), 2021).

Incidence was calculated based on the health district where the diagnosis was recorded in DHIS2, regardless of the patient's place of residence, reflecting the functioning of the Cameroonian health system where reporting is tied to the healthcare facility that provided care. Although this method ensures national consistency in data aggregation, it may introduce a bias related to patient mobility, as some individuals seek care outside their district of origin. The DHIS2 system has undergone progressive improvements in quality and coverage over the years, thanks to enhancements in the national surveillance system and validation efforts at regional and national levels. To mitigate the impact of variations and biases resulting from uneven reporting completeness, data were aggregated at the district level. Districts showing persistent inconsistencies or missing data for two or more consecutive months were excluded from the analysis. Although this methodological choice may slightly reduce the precision of estimates at a highly localized scale, it provides a robust basis for temporal and regional comparisons over the entire study period.

Meteorological data

Meteorological data were obtained via remote sensing using software developed as part of the POWER project at NASA's Langley Research Center and funded by the Applied Science Program of the NASA Earth Science Directorate (NASA POWER | Prediction of Worldwide Energy Resources, n.d.). The data include average precipitation (millimeters/day), average temperature (°C), surface humidity (%), and wind speed (m/s) by district from January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2022. To obtain regional meteorological data, district values were averaged, except for precipitation. Regional precipitation was obtained by dividing the sum of precipitation per district multiplied by the district area by the area of the corresponding region according to the following formula: \[Region\ precipitation = \displaystyle\sum (District\ precipitation * District\ area) / \displaystyle\sum District\areas \].

Spatio-temporal dynamics of malaria incidence

To describe and analyze the distribution of malaria incidence in health districts and its evolution over time, the malaria incidence rate per 1000 people from 2015 to 2022 was stratified into four categories according to the WHO recommendations for malaria elimination (Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030, 2015). These categories are: very low transmission (fewer than 100 cases); low transmission (between 100 and 250 cases); moderate transmission (between 250 and 450 cases); and high transmission (more than 450 cases).

Relationship between malaria incidence and meteorological factors

We used distributed lag nonlinear models (DLNM) to describe and measure the strength of the association between monthly malaria incidence and different meteorological variables at the regional level (Gasparrini, 2011). This methodology is based on a crossed basis, i.e., a two-dimensional space that specifies the relationships between the predictor dimensions and time lags (here, in months). To create this cross-base, we selected a natural cubic spline function for each meteorological variable and time lag. No nodes were used in the spline parameterization. Based on previous studies that demonstrated the influence of meteorological variables on malaria transmission (Kim et al., 2012; Beck-Johnson et al., 2013), we set the number of lags to six to measure the effect of meteorological variables in the six months preceding the occurrence of malaria cases.

A generalized additive quasi-Poisson model was used to account for over-dispersion of malaria cases (Wood, 2004). The explanatory variable was the number of confirmed cases. The population was offset using the logarithm. Model quality was presented graphically using the distribution of residuals and a Q-Q plot. The DLNM was used to obtain the relative risks of malaria as a function of time lags and the values taken by the meteorological variables. The 95 % confidence intervals (95 %IC) of the relative risks were also presented. A relative risk strictly greater than one corresponded to a higher probability of malaria transmission under given conditions compared to a reference situation.

The exposure reference values for the effects predicted by DLNM were 0 mm for precipitation, 32 °C for temperature, 3 m/s for wind speed, and 20 % for soil moisture. The adequacy of the model is not affected by the choice of reference values, and different values can be used depending on the study assumptions. We decided to use the values observed during the non-epidemic season as reference values (i.e., the months when incidence is falling). We then analyzed the effects of temporal shifts in meteorological variables on the relative risk of malaria transmission for values observed from April to June. These months correspond to those preceding the return of the epidemic season (an increase in cases).

Results

Spatio-temporal dynamics of incidence

A total of 1,481,401 confirmed malaria cases were recorded in the North and Far North regions over the study period. A temporal reversal in incidence trends was observed between the two regions (Fig. 1). Between 2015 and 2018, the mean annual incidence was higher in the North (138.8 cases per 1000 children) than in the Far North (107.0 cases per 1000 children; p = 0.266). However, from 2019 onwards, this trend reversed, with the mean annual incidence becoming higher in the Far North (187.5 cases per 1000 children) compared to the North (131.3 cases per 1000 children; p = 0.049).

Analysis of incidence extremes reveals that the highest annual peak was recorded in 2015 in the North [190.6 cases (95 % CI: 189.5–191.8) per 1000 person-years] and in 2021 in the Far North [243.8 cases (95 % CI: 242.9–244.7) per 1000 person-years]. Conversely, the lowest annual incidences were observed in 2022 in the North [77.8 cases (95 % CI: 77.1–78.5) per 1000 person-years] and in 2017 in the Far North [79.4 cases (95 % CI: 78.8–80.0) per 1000 person-years].

Fig. 2 shows that monthly incidence trends increased from July to October and decreased from October to May–June each year in both regions. During the peak epidemic season, the incidence rate exceeded 200 per 1000 person-years in the Far North region, while remaining mainly between 100 and 200 per 1000 person-years in the North region. Relationship between soil moisture and incidence of malaria

Fig. 8 shows the evolution of monthly surface soil moisture from 2015 to 2022 in the North and Far North regions of Cameroon. As can be seen, soil moisture begins with the rainy season, peaks in September and gradually decreases until December, which is two to three months after the end of rainfall. The overall impact of soil moisture on malaria incidence is shown in Fig. 9, with a baseline of 20 % representing soil moisture levels during the dry season. An exponential increase in malaria transmission risk is observed as soil moisture increases in both regions. This relationship is similar to that observed for rainfall. At a soil moisture threshold of 70 %, the risk of malaria transmission is 2.0 times higher in the North region (RR = 2, 95 % CI: 1.4–2.8) and 3.1 times higher in the Far North region (RR = 3.1, 95 % CI: 2–4.6). Fig. 10 shows the cumulative (Fig. 10A and 10B) and non-cumulative (Fig. 10C and 10D) lag-specific effects on the relative risk of malaria incidence at 40 % soil moisture. When the soil moisture level reaches 40 %, which corresponds to the onset of the rainy season, the risk of malaria transmission progressively increases. After five months, the relative risk is 1.35 times higher in the North region (RR = 1.35, 95 % CI: 1.21–1.51) and 1.61 times higher in the Far North region (RR = 1.61, 95 % CI: 1.42–1.83) (Fig. 10A and 10B). Conversely, we observe a decrease in the malaria transmission risk as the soil moisture decreases, tending toward zero risk when soil moisture reaches 20 % (Fig. 10C et 10D).

Discussions

This study aimed to define the spatiotemporal dynamics of malaria incidence rates in the North and Far North regions of Cameroon, and to evaluate their correlation with various meteorological factors. Incidence was used to stratify malaria risk, as it directly measures the occurrence of new cases and therefore allows for a more accurate assessment of variations related to meteorological factors.

Malaria and meteorological data were obtained from the local health information system and through remote sensing. Malaria risk was stratified according to spatial heterogeneity of incidence. We measured the strength of the association between malaria incidence and meteorological variables using DLNM.

We observed a marked difference in malaria incidence between the two regions, particularly after 2019, even though their rainfall patterns show broad similarities. This divergence suggests that although climatic factors play a major role in transmission dynamics, they alone cannot explain the variations observed between the regions. Other non-climatic determinants such as disparities in the coverage and effectiveness of control interventions (e.g., implementation of seasonal malaria chemoprevention, quality of case management), population density and mobility, or structural differences in the organization and performance of local health systems may have contributed to these gaps. Moreover, the gradual improvement in the quality and completeness of data in DHIS2 starting from 2018 to 2019 could also partly explain the apparent increase in certain areas. Thus, this interregional heterogeneity, despite relatively similar climatic conditions, underscores the need to adopt a multidimensional approach that integrates climatic, epidemiological, operational, and socioeconomic factors to better understand malaria dynamics and guide control strategies.

We also observed an increase in incidence associated with increased rainfall and soil moisture in both regions. These results are consistent with those of numerous studies carried out in similar contexts that have demonstrated a positive linear relationship between these meteorological factors and malaria incidence (Ekpa et al., 2023; Ministry of Public Health (MINSANTE), 2021; Lavarenne, 2024; World Bank Group, 2022; Cameroon Meteorological Department, 2023; National Institute of Statistics (INS). Demographic Projections and Estimates of Priority Targets for Various Health Programs and Interventions, 2021; NASA POWER | Prediction of Worldwide Energy Resources, n.d.; Gasparrini, 2011; Kim et al., 2012; Beck-Johnson et al., 2013; Wood, 2004; Dieng et al., 2020; Gwitira et al., 2020; Djoufack, 2011). Indeed, malaria transmission is closely linked to stagnant water, which is induced by rainfall and essential for the development of mosquito vectors. An increase in rainfall leads to more stagnant water, and consequently, a greater proliferation of mosquitoes that transmit malaria.

According to the malaria risk classification of the Malaria Elimination Framework (World Health Organization, 2017), most of the health districts in our study regions are located in areas with low or very low transmission. A similar trend was reported in Mali (Cissoko et al., 2022), where several districts in the north of the country also exhibited low transmission intensity in a Sahelian context. This observation suggests that in ecological environments characterized by aridity, low rainfall, and marked seasonality, reduced transmission profiles may be observed. Nevertheless, we recognize that isohyets and rainfall patterns differ between Mali and Cameroon; the proposed analogy should therefore be interpreted with caution, not as a strict equivalence but as an illustration of similar dynamics in other Sahelian areas. Finally, low rainfall reduces the intensity of malaria transmission in these areas by limiting the formation of stagnant water, which is essential for the larval development of mosquito vectors. This restriction leads to a decrease in vector density, consequently slowing down the spread of malaria.

Our study was unable to demonstrate a significant relationship between temperature and malaria incidence. Studies by Sissoko et al. in Mali, Ouedraogo et al. and Bationo et al. in Burkina Faso also observed a non-linear relationship between temperature and malaria incidence (Ouedraogo et al., 2018; Ekpa et al., 2023; Cissoko et al., 2020; Sissoko et al., 2017). There was also a reduction in malaria incidence at temperatures above 26 °C. Sissoko et al.'s study in Mali described a progressive increase in malaria incidence with increasing temperature and a significant decrease at higher temperatures (Bationo et al., 2021). This phenomenon occurs because the larvae and adults of Anopheles gambiae, the main vector in this region, die at temperatures above 35 °C as described by Christiansen-Jucht et al. (Christiansen-Jucht et al., 2014).

Our study described an increase in malaria incidence between 2015 and 2018 in our two study regions. WHO and authors have also reported an increase in malaria cases in endemic sub-Saharan African countries (World Health Organization, 2021; Cohen et al., 2012). The disruption in the use of healthcare facilities due to the COVID-19 pandemic has been cited as one of the factors contributing to this increase (Cissoko et al., 2022). In the Far North region, additional contributing factors include flooding and insecurity resulting from attacks by armed groups, as reported by other authors (Cissoko et al., 2022; Okiro et al., 2011; Correa-Salazar and Amon, 2020).

Our study has methodological limitations that require a nuanced interpretation of the results. The main challenge lies in the exclusive use of data from the health information system, limited to confirmed malaria cases among children under 5 years of age. This approach introduces an ascertainment bias, particularly notable due to the fact that patients do not systematically seek care within their district of residence (Rowe et al., 2009; Ashton et al., 2017), which may affect the accuracy of morbidity and mortality estimates. Although these limitations are inherent to retrospective studies based on routine data, the observed trends remain consistent with documented transmission dynamics in Sahelian areas (Cissoko et al., 2022). The strategy of aggregating data at the district level, combined with the exclusion of districts affected by prolonged inconsistencies, helped mitigate the impact of these biases. Similar studies have also shown that, despite certain limitations, routine health facility data can provide reliable estimates of epidemiological trends when appropriately adjusted (Kapesa et al., 2018; Hyde et al., 2021). Future strengthening of surveillance systems through the systematic collection of patient residence data would significantly refine the accuracy of analyses and the stratification of malaria risk.

We deliberately focused on environmental factors, but we acknowledge that other non-climatic determinants significantly influence malaria risk. Coverage of vector control interventions (such as LLINs and IRS), socio-economic conditions, as well as population movements constitute key variables that could modulate the observed associations (Chung, 2016). Although these factors were not included in our model due to data limitations and the initial focus on meteorological drivers, their integration in future analyses would enable a more holistic understanding of transmission determinants. We therefore encourage multidisciplinary research combining environmental, programmatic, and social data to refine control strategies.

Conclusion

Overall, this study shows that the risk of malaria transmission increases exponentially with increasing rainfall and soil moisture in the North and Far-North regions of Cameroon. Given that climate change, economic development and public health interventions are likely to rapidly transform the dynamics of malaria transmission, the evolution of malaria geo-epidemiology in endemic countries such as Cameroon must be properly understood to ensure the success of malaria control strategies. It would be useful to carry out future studies examining the relationship between malaria incidence and other meteorological, environmental and socio-economic factors at different spatial scales.
