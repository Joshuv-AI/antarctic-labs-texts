High-performance proteomics reveals immune, epithelial, and vascular dysregulation underlying lacrimal fluid defects in patients with aniridia
Authors: Marjolaine Willems, Jérôme Vialaret, Mélissa Girard, Nadège Feret, Dominique Bremond-Gignac, Nicolas Chassaing, Jana Kindermans, Laura Fichter, Christophe Hirtz, Caroline Mollevi, Hannah Crowdy, Marie Pequignot, Cécile Delettre, Vincent Daien, Frédéric Michon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital aniridia is a rare disorder presenting as a panocular malformation with variable severity, often complicated by progressive keratopathy. The purpose of this study was to characterise the tear-film proteome in adults with PAX6-related congenital aniridia and to identify dysregulated pathways linked to aniridia associated keratopathy (AAK).MethodsTears were obtained with Schirmer strips from four genetically confirmed patients and four age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers. Peptides prepared with the single-pot, solid-phase–enhanced (SP3) protocol were analysed by data-independent nanoLC-MS/MS. Proteins were identified with a false discovery rate (FDR) <1% during DIA data processing. Differential abundance between controls and patients samples was assessed using an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Proteins with |log₂-fold change| ≥1 were considered significantly expressed. Functional enrichment was evaluated with Enrichr (Gene Ontology, Reactome, JensenExp, Orphanet, TissueExp).ResultsA total of 3 162 proteins were detected; 2 633 showed a valid intensity in every sample of at least one group and were retained for statistical testing. Seventy-three (2.8%) were differentially expressed: 33 were over-expressed and 40 under-expressed in aniridia tears. Down-regulated proteins clustered in lipid homeostasis, epithelial junction integrity and wound-healing modules and included lacritin, secretoglobins and cytoskeletal adaptors, indicating a fragile, poorly repaired surface. Up-regulated species were dominated by neutrophil effectors (CD177 ≈ 50-fold) and reflected heightened innate immunity and abnormal epithelial maturation. Anti-angiogenic processes were significantly over-represented in both under and over-expressed protein sets.ConclusionOur workflow proved highly sensitive, capturing more than 3 000 tear proteins and thus underscoring the robustness of our proteomic approach. The tear film in aniridia reflects dysregulation of various processes, including immunity, lipid and epithelial homeostasis, and vascular remodelling. Our approach highlights novel biomarkers critical for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05562115. Background

Aniridia is a rare autosomal dominant disorder marked by substantial phenotypic variability and most commonly caused by pathogenic variants in the transcription factor PAX6 [1]. The majority of cases arise from PAX6 haploinsufficiency, typically due to loss-of-function variants (nonsense, frameshift, splice-site mutations) or larger deletions involving the 11p13 locus, including contiguous gene deletions associated with WAGR syndrome. By contrast, missense variants, representing approximately 10–15% of PAX6 mutations, are often associated with a broader phenotypic spectrum that depends on the functional domain affected [2].

Although PAX6 accounts for most cases, pathogenic variants in other genes implicated in ocular development and anterior segment morphogenesis have been reported in patients with aniridia or aniridia-like phenotypes, including FOXC1, PITX2, PITX3, SOX2, OTX2, BMP4, SHH, FGFR2, FGFR3, ALX4, GLI3, GJA1, ITPR1, TRIM44, and SMARCA4. While rare, these alternative genetic causes underscore the heterogeneity of aniridia-related phenotypes [2].

Clinically, aniridia is a complex pan-ocular developmental disorder encompassing abnormalities of the retina, optic nerve, iris and iridocorneal angle, as well as lens anomalies. It is frequently complicated by nystagmus, cataract, glaucoma, progressive aniridia-associated keratopathy (AAK), and severe visual impairment [3]. Corneal disease typically progresses from peripheral changes toward central, vascularized opacification, a trajectory thought to result from converging mechanisms that include limbal stem cell deficiency, impaired corneal innervation, and tear-film abnormalities [4]. Consistent with this multifactorial model, PAX6 haploinsufficiency disrupts key processes required for ocular surface homeostasis, including corneal epithelial differentiation, limbal stem cell function, lacrimal gland development, and immune barrier integrity [5–11]. Together, these defects promote epithelial fragility, chronic inflammation, and tear-film dysfunction, central drivers of AAK onset and progression.

More than half of patients with aniridia develop progressive dry-eye symptoms, often beginning in childhood [12]. Mechanistic insights have been provided by animal models, notably the Sey mouse carrying a heterozygous truncating Pax6 mutation, which exhibits lacrimal gland hypoplasia linked to impaired FGF10 signalling and highlights the essential role of PAX6 in lacrimal gland development and function [11]. Current management remains largely symptomatic, relying on preservative-free artificial tears, therapeutic eye drops, punctal occlusion, autologous serum, and scleral lenses [13]. Increasing evidence, however, suggests that qualitative alterations of the tear film, not merely reduced tear volume, may substantially contribute to corneal disease severity and progression.

The tear film is a highly specialized, multilayered fluid system covering the corneal and conjunctival epithelia and is essential for lubrication, nutrition, and protection. Its aqueous component, produced primarily by the lacrimal glands, contains abundant proteins, such as immunoglobulins, growth factors, and enzymes, that support epithelial integrity, homeostasis, and antimicrobial defence [14, 15]. The outer lipid layer, secreted by the meibomian glands, limits evaporation and stabilizes the tear film.

Proteomics offers a powerful means to qualitatively and quantitatively profile protein composition in complex biological matrices, including body fluids [16]. Over the past three decades, tear proteome studies have substantially advanced our understanding of ocular surface physiology and disease [17]. Reported numbers of tear proteins have ranged from fewer than 10 to more than 1,500, reflecting both methodological variability and the intrinsic complexity of tear composition [18]. Importantly, tear proteomics has enabled biomarker discovery in ocular and systemic conditions and has provided mechanistic insight into disease-associated pathways [19]. In contrast, proteomic studies in aniridia remain scarce [10, 20]. The seminal investigation by Ihnatko et al. [20] used two-dimensional gel electrophoresis coupled with LC–MS/MS and identified a limited set of differentially expressed tear proteins in a small cohort. While this work provided early evidence that tear composition is altered in aniridia, its analytical depth was constrained by the technologies available at the time, with only a modest number of proteins quantified and reported as differentially expressed.

Recent advances in high-resolution mass spectrometry and data-independent acquisition (DIA) now enable a markedly more comprehensive and less biased characterization of the tear proteome. Building on prior work, the present study applies state-of-the-art DIA-based proteomics to achieve substantially increased proteome coverage and to define, at unprecedented depth, the molecular pathways associated with tear-film dysregulation in PAX6-related aniridia.

Accordingly, we conducted a prospective observational study comparing tear proteome profiles from four patients with aniridia and four age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Using advanced proteomic workflows, we aimed to identify proteins significantly altered (up- or downregulated) in aniridia. By delineating disease-associated tear signatures, this work seeks to refine our understanding of AAK pathophysiology and to inform the development of targeted therapeutic strategies that address underlying mechanisms and improve long-term management of aniridia-associated ocular surface disease.

Materials and methods

Patients

For this monocentric observational study, patients aged 20 to 65 years were recruited through the Departments of Ophthalmology and Clinical Genetics at Montpellier University Hospital. Participants were instructed to discontinue any ocular treatments, specifically eye drops, 48 hours prior to tear collection to prevent potential confounding effects. Healthy control subjects, matched by age, sex, and ethnic background, were selected from a biocollection of tears held by Montpellier University Hospital. This biocollection is ethically approved by the French Ministry of Higher Education and Research under reference DC-2020–4151.

All the experiments conducted for this study (NCT05562115) were approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Est I (approval No. 22.01698.000081–2022-A00890-43). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

This case-control study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE Statement guidelines for observational studies.

Ophthalmological evaluation

Participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmological evaluations, including slit lamp biomicroscopy. When feasible, advanced imaging techniques such as optical coherence tomography (OCT) was performed.

Tear collection procedure

Tear samples were collected without anesthesia using sterile Schirmer test strips (Madhu Instruments) placed at the lateral canthus into the inferior conjunctival fornix of each eye without contacting the skin. No intentional external stimulation was applied during sampling; however, Schirmer strip collection may itself induce a variable degree of reflex tearing, as is inherent to this method [21]. Three strips were collected from each eye, immediately placed into Eppendorf tubes, and promptly frozen at −80 °C to preserve the protein content for subsequent analysis. A minimum of 5 mm per Schirmer strip was required to perform the analysis. Tear collection time was not recorded.

Proteomic analysis protocol

Analyses were performed independently for each eye of each participant. The Schirmer strips were inserted into spin columns (Thermo Fisher, ref 69725) and incubated in 150 µL of 1% SDS and 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC), pH 8.5, for 3 hours at 4 °C. Subsequently, columns were transferred to LoBind tubes and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 2 minutes. The quantification of proteins prior to the SP3 protocol was done by using the NanoDrop One Microvolume UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, reference ND-ONE-W) at 280 nm. Protein extracts (6.5 µg per sample) underwent reduction, alkylation, and tryptic digestion using an automated single-pot solid-phase-enhanced sample preparation (SP3) protocol on the Bravo 96LT platform (Agilent Technologies). Peptides were then desalted and analyzed in triplicate using the Evosep One liquid chromatography system coupled to a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics). Samples were separated on a PepSep C18 column (15 cm × 150 µm, 1.5 µm particles; Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, USA) employing a predefined 30 samples-per-day gradient. Data acquisition was conducted using Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA), collecting MS1 and MS2 spectra across an m/z range of 100 to 1700.

Data analysis

Protein identification was performed using DIA-NN software (version 1.8.1). Analysis parameters included trypsin as the digestion enzyme, allowing one missed cleavage, a minimum peptide length of seven amino acids, precursor charge states between 1 and 4, precursor m/z range of 300–1800, and a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold set at 1%. The human proteome database from UniProt (Release 2024_03) served as the reference. Sample preparation-induced modifications, such as asparagine deamidation and methionine oxidation, were considered variable modifications, while cysteine carbamidomethylation was treated as a fixed modification, with a maximum of two allowed variable modifications.

Label-free quantification (LFQ) intensities were processed via the FragPipe-Analyst platform. Identified contaminants or redundant proteins were excluded, and proteins without 100% valid data in at least one group across biological replicates were removed. LFQ data underwent log2(x) transformation and were grouped according to genotype (patients with aniridia or controls). Data normalization was conducted using variance-stabilizing normalization techniques to approximate a normal distribution, and missing values were imputed using Perseus-type algorithms. Proteins were identified with a false discovery rate (FDR) <1% during DIA data processing. Because formal statistical comparison between a single patient and a single matched control is not valid in discovery proteomics, differential expression was assessed at the group level. Differential abundance between controls and patient samples was assessed using an adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Volcano plots identified proteins significantly overexpressed (red) or underexpressed (blue) in patients with aniridia compared to controls. To comprehensively interpret the differentially expressed proteins, we performed functional enrichment analysis using Enrichr (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/), selecting GO Biological Process 2025, Reactome Pathways 2024, Tissues Experimental 2025, Jensen DISEASES Experimental 2025, Orphanet Augmented 2021, and, whose complementary coverage of biological functions, signaling pathways, disease associations, and tissue specificity provides an integrative analytical framework. For each database, Enrichr ranks all terms based on the combined score (ln(p-value) × z-score), following Fisher exact test and Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

Results

Corneal defects are constant across the selected patients

Four individuals (two male, two female) carrying heterozygous pathogenic loss-of-function PAX6 variants were enrolled. None presented with extra-ophthalmological features that can accompany PAX6-related disease (e.g., diabetes, neurological abnormalities, anosmia, hearing impairment), and cranial circumference was within the expected range for all participants. Clinically, all patients had bilateral aniridia, associated cataract, and aniridia-associated keratopathy (AAK; grade 2–4) (Fig. 1). Anterior segment OCT confirmed severe corneal involvement (data not shown). The overall tear film proteome shows defects associated with aniridia

In total, 3,162 proteins were initially detected across all samples (range 2,253–3,024 proteins per sample). Of these, 2,633 proteins showed valid intensity measurements in all samples from at least one group (range 2,132–2,494 proteins per sample) and were retained for downstream statistical analyses (Table S1; Figure S1). The number of detected proteins did not differ significantly between aniridia and control samples (data not shown). Unsupervised clustering of the global dataset (heatmap) revealed clear separation between controls and patients with aniridia, consistent with a distinct tear proteome composition in the disease group (Figure S2). The left-eye sample from patient 3 was excluded due to an excessive proportion of missing values, precluding reliable quantification.

Among the 2,633 quantified proteins, 73 (2.8%) were significantly different between groups, including 40 downregulated proteins (Table 2) and 33 upregulated proteins (Table 3). PAX6 mutations reshape the immune response pathway

GO analysis indicated reduced representation of multiple immune defence processes, particularly those related to antiviral activity (e.g., defence response to virus GO:0051607; regulation of viral genome replication GO:0045069), leukocyte/neutrophil activation, and positive regulation of inflammatory responses. Reactome results corroborated these findings, with decreased enrichment of pathways such as interferon alpha/beta signalling.

In contrast, several terms consistent with antibacterial and acute pro-inflammatory activity were enriched among upregulated proteins, including antibacterial humoral response (GO:0019731), antimicrobial humoral response (GO:0019730), and positive regulation of leukocyte degranulation (GO:0043302). Reactome analysis similarly highlighted increased neutrophil degranulation, suggesting a shift toward innate immune activation. Tissue-expression enrichment further indicated that proteins associated with lymphoid lineages (e.g., B cells/lymphocytes/leukocytes) were predominantly downregulated.

PAX6 mutations alter epithelial organization and lipid-related processes

We identified substantial dysregulation of epithelial and lipid-associated pathways, affecting both barrier maintenance and compensatory stress responses. Terms indicative of impaired membrane organization and epithelial renewal were decreased, including membrane organization (GO:0061024) and positive regulation of epithelial cell proliferation involved in wound healing (GO:0060054).

Concomitantly, pathways linked to tear-film lipid biology were perturbed, with decreased enrichment of sterol/lipid metabolic processes and increased enrichment of cellular responses to lipids, consistent with disrupted tear-film composition and adaptive stress signalling.

PAX6 mutations impact vascular remodeling signatures

Enrichment patterns also pointed to altered vascular biology. Downregulated terms included processes related to vascular smooth muscle differentiation/proliferation and negative regulation of VEGF receptor signalling, whereas regulation of sprouting angiogenesis was enriched among upregulated proteins, consistent with pathways implicated in neovascular remodeling.

The aniridia tear signature overlaps with corneal disease-associated proteomic patterns

Disease-enrichment analyses revealed strong associations between upregulated proteins and rare corneal structural disorders, including gelatinous drop-like corneal dystrophy, juvenile glaucoma, ectodermal dysplasia, and ichthyosis, conditions frequently accompanied by corneal involvement. Similar enrichment was observed for bullous dermatological diseases, which can also manifest ocular surface and corneal complications. Collectively, these associations support the clinical relevance of the aniridia-associated tear proteomic signature and its convergence with pathways implicated in corneal pathology.

Discussion

In this study, we performed a comprehensive, untargeted proteomic profiling of tear samples from patients with PAX6-related aniridia using state-of-the-art analytical workflows. Because sex-dependent differences in the tear proteome have been reported, we implemented a balanced sex distribution (1:1) to reduce confounding and to better resolve disease-associated proteomic alterations [22].

Our data demonstrate marked dysregulation of biological processes central to aniridia-associated ocular surface pathology, particularly immune responses, epithelial homeostasis, and vascular remodelling. Across the quantified proteome, we identified 73 significantly differentially abundant proteins (2.8%), revealing candidate biomarkers and pathways with potential translational relevance as therapeutic targets.

Tears were collected using Schirmer strips, which enable high protein recovery while limiting ocular irritation and thereby reducing reflex tearing-related bias [23, 24]. Each eye was analysed as an independent sample for both methodological and biological reasons. In a cohort of this size (n = 4 patients), pooling or averaging both eyes would further reduce the effective number of observations and substantially decrease statistical power. Moreover, AAK frequently exhibits clinically meaningful intra-individual asymmetry, and tear composition may reflect this heterogeneity; treating both eyes as interchangeable could therefore obscure biologically relevant differences. Accordingly, our objective was to identify proteins consistently dysregulated across affected eyes rather than assume bilateral homogeneity. To mitigate the risk of inflated significance due to non-independence, we applied stringent criteria: we required 100% valid intensities within each condition and retained only proteins showing consistent directionality across all biological replicates. A purely subject-level re-analysis was not feasible without loss of critical information, as several patients displayed pronounced left–right asymmetry in clinical severity and/or data completeness.

Recent technological advances in mass spectrometry, particularly high-resolution data-independent acquisition (DIA) on the timsTOF HT platform, enabled detection of more than 3,000 proteins, representing a substantial improvement in sensitivity, reproducibility, and analytical depth compared with earlier studies [25–28]. Notably, 2,633 proteins were robustly quantified across samples, underscoring the reproducibility of our workflow relative to previous tear proteomics studies. This performance compares favourably with a recent large-scale study in diabetes, which detected over 3,000 tear proteins but reliably quantified 794 proteins in at least 90% of samples [29].

Proteins identified as differentially abundant in the group comparison should be interpreted as cohort-level candidate markers. The aniridia tear proteome highlights the convergence of multiple pathological processes. We observed a strikingly discordant immune signature, characterized by downregulated antiviral pathways and interferon signalling together with reduced immunoglobulin components (e.g., IGLV5-37, IGHD), suggestive of impaired humoral immunity [30]. In parallel, innate immune mechanisms—particularly neutrophil degranulation and antibacterial responses—were upregulated, exemplified by an approximately 50-fold increase in CD177, a marker associated with neutrophil activation and chronic inflammation [31]. This increase may also relate to the elevated density of mature dendritic cells reported in aniridic corneas [9], as neutrophils can release chemokines and alarmins that recruit and promote maturation of antigen-presenting cells [32]. Collectively, these findings support a model in which neutrophil-driven innate inflammation and dendritic cell activation participate in an early, self-amplifying inflammatory loop at the aniridic ocular surface.

Among upregulated proteins, CRISP3 may be particularly relevant to ocular surface immunity in aniridia. Lacrimal gland expression has been demonstrated at the transcript level in mice [33], and the protein localizes to peroxidase-negative granules of human neutrophils and eosinophils, where it contributes to innate defence [34]. Its marked overexpression in aniridia tears is therefore consistent with the neutrophil-dominant signature revealed in our dataset and may reflect both altered lacrimal secretory function and neutrophil-mediated immune activation—mechanisms implicated in epithelial fragility and chronic inflammation in AAK. By contrast, the pronounced downregulation of secretoglobin family proteins (e.g., SCGB1D1 and mammaglobin B), which are often elevated in other inflammatory ocular disorders, suggests that the inflammatory mechanisms of AAK may be distinct from those observed in conditions such as juvenile idiopathic arthritis–associated uveitis [35] or vernal keratoconjunctivitis [36].

Enrichment analyses further indicated impaired epithelial renewal, reduced wound-healing capacity, and dysregulated lipid metabolism, all of which are essential for tear-film stability. Lacritin, a key regulator of tear-film homeostasis and epithelial health [37], was reduced by more than fourfold, plausibly contributing to the dry-eye phenotype commonly seen in aniridia and highlighting a potential therapeutic avenue [38]. PLA2G2A was reduced by more than sixfold, in contrast to its reported upregulation in keratoconjunctivitis sicca [39]. Whereas compensatory secretion of PLA2G2A in KCS may help preserve antimicrobial defense, its decrease in aniridia likely reflects a broader PAX6-driven disruption of epithelial and secretory homeostasis, further compromising tear-film lipid stability and innate immunity and thereby contributing to chronic ocular surface inflammation in AAK.

We also observed downregulation of corneal crystallins ALDH3A1 and CRYAB, which are normally abundant in the corneal epithelium and contribute to UV protection, oxidative-stress resistance, regulation of epithelial differentiation, and optical clarity [40–43]. Reduced abundance of these proteins may weaken antioxidant defences and epithelial stability, predisposing to the progressive opacification characteristic of AAK. Conversely, the marked upregulation of BCAP31, an endoplasmic reticulum–resident chaperone involved in membrane-protein quality control, ER homeostasis, and mitochondria–ER communication, suggests heightened ER stress and perturbed epithelial protein handling. Given BCAP31’s roles in regulating epithelial proliferation, migration, and apoptosis, its dysregulation may reflect increased cellular stress in the ocular surface, consistent with chronic epithelial instability in AAK [44]. In addition, analysis of angiogenesis-related proteins indicated disruption of regulatory pathways that may underpin corneal neovascularization, a hallmark of AAK severity and progression [9].

Overall, the proteomic alterations identified here map directly onto established mechanisms driving aniridia-associated keratopathy. Enhanced innate immune activation—reflected by elevated neutrophil-associated proteins—aligns with the increased density of mature dendritic cells described in aniridic corneas [9]. Downregulation of epithelial structural, wound-healing, and antioxidant proteins is consistent with impaired epithelial differentiation and fragility observed in PAX6 haploinsufficiency models [7, 45]. Reductions in tear-film stabilizing and secretory proteins support a component of lacrimal dysfunction which, together with meibomian gland impairment, is likely to amplify the vicious cycle of ocular surface disease in aniridia [46]. Finally, dysregulated angiogenic signalling corresponds to the well-recognized tendency of AAK to progress toward pannus formation and corneal neovascularization [9]. These convergent pathways provide a coherent interpretative framework for the broad tear-film dysregulation observed in our cohort.

Disease-enrichment analyses further revealed substantial molecular overlap with other genetic disorders affecting corneal integrity and transparency, including corneal dystrophies and ectodermal dysplasia, suggesting shared pathogenic mechanisms involving epithelial and extracellular matrix dysfunction.

Compared with earlier aniridia proteomic studies, our untargeted DIA approach provides markedly greater analytical depth. Ihnatko et al. reported 52 detected tear proteins, with only seven described as dysregulated (including zinc-α2-glycoprotein, α-enolase, cystatin S, gelsolin, and peroxiredoxin 6) [20]. In our dataset, most of these proteins were detected in the global tear proteome; however, only α-enolase showed modest downregulation, and the others did not meet significance under our group-level criteria. These discrepancies are likely driven primarily by methodological differences. Two-dimensional gel–based approaches preferentially detect highly abundant tear proteins, whereas DIA workflows aim for robust, proteome-wide quantification and may de-emphasize highly variable components. Likewise, earlier cytokine-focused immunoassay studies reported low-abundance mediators (e.g., VEGF-C and classical pro-inflammatory cytokines) [10], none of which were detected in our dataset—consistent with the limitations of untargeted shotgun proteomics for very low-abundance soluble factors without prior enrichment. Despite differences at the individual protein level, these studies converge on shared biological themes, including inflammation, epithelial homeostasis, and oxidative stress. Together, these comparisons highlight the value of modern non-targeted proteomics while acknowledging that heterogeneity in patient characteristics and disease stage may also contribute to differences across studies.

Collectively, our findings provide mechanistic insight into AAK and nominate candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets aimed at restoring tear-film stability, mitigating inflammation, and preserving corneal integrity. Future work should focus on translating these molecular insights into personalized interventions, including topical therapies targeting key pathways. Although limited by the rarity of the condition and the resulting sample size, our stringent inclusion criteria and matched controls strengthen the validity of the observed signatures. Targeted validation by immunoassay (e.g., ELISA) is a priority next step to orthogonally confirm a subset of the most clinically relevant differentially abundant proteins identified by our discovery DIA workflow. However, such validation is currently constrained by (i) the limited tear volumes obtainable per participant and (ii) the small number of available patients in this rare-disease population. Robust immunoassay confirmation will ideally require an independent validation cohort and additional tear samples collected under tightly standardized pre-analytical conditions (including control of topical treatments and ocular surface status), with sufficient material for technical replicates and quality-control procedures. We therefore plan, through ongoing recruitment and biobanking within the OPHTARA rare-disease network, to expand sample availability to enable targeted immunoassay validation and to assess leading candidate biomarkers across disease stages. Larger longitudinal studies, particularly in younger patients prior to keratopathy onset, should further clarify the temporal evolution of tear-film abnormalities and inform early-intervention strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study advances the molecular characterization of tear-film alterations in PAX6-related aniridia, identifying disease-associated proteins and pathways that provide a rational basis for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at improving ocular surface outcomes and patient quality of life.
