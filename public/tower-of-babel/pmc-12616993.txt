Pan-cancer analysis identifies LMBR1L as a prognostic and immunological biomarker with validation in colorectal cancer
Authors: Song Liang, Weiwei Yuan, Yin Shi, Yongkang Liang, Hengbo Xia, Chen Chen, Chuansi Wang, Pin Jiang, Juhe Zhang, Shenwei Wu, Aman Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The limb development membrane protein 1-like gene (LMBR1L) encodes a transmembrane protein involved in cellular signaling and molecular transport. LMBR1L plays a critical role in immune system development by negatively regulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in lymphocytes. However, its role in the tumor immune microenvironment and its potential as a predictor of immunotherapy response remain poorly understood. We analyzed LMBR1L expression in normal and tumor tissues using data from public databases and clinical samples. Prognostic significance and immune associations were assessed using survival analysis, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), and immune infiltration profiling tools. Additionally, both in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted to assess the effects of LMBR1L knockdown in colorectal cancer (COAD) cells. Elevated LMBR1L expression was observed in multiple cancers. Single-cell sequencing revealed its primary expression in CD8 + T cells, and higher expression was correlated with poorer outcomes. Increased LMBR1L levels were associated with immune cell infiltration, including CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, and correlated with programmed death-ligand-1 (PD-L1) expression, suggesting its involvement in immune checkpoint regulation. LMBR1L knockdown suppressed the proliferation, migration, and invasion of COAD cells, a finding further corroborated by in vivo experiments. Our findings suggest that LMBR1L may be a prognostic biomarker and an immunotherapy predictor, emphasizing its potential as a therapeutic target in cancer treatment. This study provides a foundation for future work on targeted therapy and immunotherapy strategies in cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03404-7.

Introduction

In recent years, cancer has remained the leading cause of death worldwide, with both incidence and mortality rates continuing to rise [1, 2]. Despite progress in early screening and clinical treatment, the emergence of drug resistance, adverse effects, and other complications has led to unsatisfactory patient prognosis and survival outcomes [3]. Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors, has emerged as a promising treatment option [4, 5], significantly prolonging patient survival [6, 7]. However, their effectiveness remains limited due to low response rates and drug resistance [8, 9]. To maximize the synergistic effects of immune responses, further validation of novel immune-related therapeutic targets in malignancies is essential [10, 11]. Therefore, the identification of potential immune biomarkers and the development of targeted immunotherapeutic strategies remain of critical importance.

The limb development membrane protein 1 like (LMBR1L) gene encodes a transmembrane protein primarily involved in signal transduction and molecular transport [12–15]. LMBR1L was initially associated with limb development, and has recently attracted attention for its role in cancer. It has been shown to regulate endothelial cell proliferation and migration via Norrin/β-catenin signaling [15]. It has also been implicated to childhood obesity and immune defects [14, 16–18]. Evidence suggests that LMBR1L interacts with glycoprotein 78 and ubiquitin-associated domain protein 2 to inhibit the maturation of frizzled class receptor 6 and low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 6. This occurs by promoting ubiquitination and stabilization of the “destruction complex” proteins in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), thereby attenuating Wnt signaling in lymphocytes [14].Thus, LMBR1L may influence the tumor microenvironment (TME), modulate immune responses, and impact treatment efficacy, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and immunotherapeutic target. Further investigations into LMBR1L expression could provide new insights and theoretical foundations for optimizing cancer treatment strategies and enhancing the effectiveness of immunotherapy.

In this study, we utilized public databases, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx), the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA), along with clinical samples, to systematically evaluate LMBR1L expression and its prognostic significance across various cancers. We assessed its association with immune infiltration and immune checkpoint expression at both tissue and single-cell levels. Furthermore, we explored the functional role of LMBR1L in oncogenic processes, such as colorectal cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Our findings provide crucial insights and strategies for future research on LMBR1L-targeted anticancer therapies.

Materials and methods

Data sources

We sourced extensive transcriptomic data on LMBR1L expression and related clinical characteristics from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and GTEx databases (https://gtexportal.org/) to analyze LMBR1L expression patterns and their associations with overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) [19]. Additionally, LMBR1L expression across various cell lines was examined using HPA database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). We also accessed several datasets from the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including GSE167297 and EMTAB8107, primarily to validate LMBR1L expression patterns at single-cell resolution, particularly in CD8 + T cells. The HPA database provided key visualizations of LMBR1L protein localization, revealing its distribution in cellular compartments such as the nucleus and ER, supporting our hypotheses on its functional role. We used the Estimation of Stromal and Immune Cells in Malignant Tumors using Expression data (ESTIMATE, https://bioinformatics.mdanderson.org/estimate/), Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER, https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/), and Tumor Immune Prediction databases (TIP, http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/TIP/) to analyze the relationship of LMBR1L with immune infiltration in the TME and quantify its potential immunomodulatory effects [20]. For data processing, we used Perl scripts and the “limma” package in R for differential expression analysis, with further statistical validation provided by Cox proportional hazards models and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. To further substantiate our bioinformatics findings, we conducted immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses across multiple cancer tissues to confirm LMBR1L overexpression. In vivo experiments using RKO cells with LMBR1L knockdown were also performed to validate the role of LMBR1L in tumor progression.

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses were conducted using the R packages “survminer” and “survival” to assess the impact of LMBR1L expression on patient prognosis, including OS, PFI, and DSS [21]. The “Survival” (version 3.2–10) and “survminer” (version 0.4.9) packages were used for statistical analysis and visualization, respectively. The statistical significance of OS, PFI, and DSS between the high and low LMBR1L expression groups in patients with 33 cancer types was analyzed by univariate Cox regression. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Additionally, a meta-analysis of the univariate Cox survival analysis results was performed using the inverse variance method, with the logarithmic hazard ratio (HR) values serving as the primary measurement indicator.

Genomic status and methylation

Single nucleotide variant (SNV) data from 33 cancer types (n = 8663) were collected from the TCGA database. The TCGA SNV data included various mutation types: Missense_Mutation, Silent, 5' flap, 3' UTR, RNA, In_Frame_Del, Nonsense_Mutation, Splice_Site, Intron, 5' UTR, In_Frame_Ins, Frame_Shift_Del, Nonstop_Mutation, 3' flap, Frame_Shift_Ins, and Translation_Start_Site. Silent, Intron, IGR, 3' UTR, 5' UTR, 3' wing, and 5' wing sequences were filtered out for SNV percentage calculation. The SNV mutation frequency (percentage) for each coding region of the gene was calculated using the following formula: number of mutated samples/number of cancer samples. The oncoplot was generated using the maftools package.A radar chart was used to visualize the spearman correlation between various gene set scores and tropomyosin 4 across pan-cancer. Each axis of the radar chart represents a different tumor type, and the values on the axes indicate the correlation coefficients between the respective gene set scores and tropomyosin 4 expression. Asterisks indicate the statistical significance levels of the correlations: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to explore the relationship between different gene set scores and LMBR1L expression across pan-cancer. The results were visualized using a radar chart to display the correlation coefficients and their statistical significance.

GO and KEGG analyses

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analyses were performed on the top 100 proteins interacting with LMBR1L using the “clusterProfiler” package in R to identify significant pathways [22, 23]. We also conducted a pan-cancer Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to assess the involvement of LMBR1L in various signaling pathways. Using data from the Cancer Single-cell functional State Atlas database (CancerSEA, http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CancerSEA/), we performed Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) to evaluate the relationship of LMBR1L with different tumor processes. Furthermore, we analyzed the differential expression of LMBR1L across different phases of the cell cycle and explored its correlation with the Wnt signaling pathway in various cancer types.

Immune landscape analysis

A comprehensive analysis of immune gene expression and immune cell infiltration was conducted. Immune-stimulating gene, immune-suppressing gene, chemokine, and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) gene expression data were obtained and heatmaps were generated to compare differences between the high and low LMBR1L expression groups. The infiltration levels of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells across various cancer types were evaluated using three algorithms: TIMER [24], Estimating the Proportions of Immune and Cancer cells (EPIC, https://epic.gfellerlab.org/) [25], and Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT, https://cibersort.stanford.edu/) [26]. Additionally, ESTIMATE scores and other immune environment metrics were used to evaluate the correlation between LMBR1L expression and immune-related gene expression [27]. The relationship between LMBR1L expression and cancer immune evasion mechanisms was further examined by employing tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE, http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) scores to quantify immune evasion. Finally, Pearson correlation analysis and heatmaps were used to visualize the association between LMBR1L and CD274 [Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1)] expression across various cancer types.

Single-cell analysis

In this study, single-cell RNA sequencing data from the GEO datasets EMTAB8107 and GSE167297 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) were used to analyze LMBR1L expression across colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), and breast cancer (BRCA) tissues. After preprocessing the data using the Seurat R package, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was applied to visualize LMBR1L expression in various cell clusters, with particular focus on immune cells such as CD4 + and CD8 + T cells. Area Under the Curve Cell (AUCell) was used to score biological pathways in LMBR1L-expressing cells, specifically proliferation-related pathways. Spearman’s correlation analysis further explored the relationship between LMBR1L expression and pathway activity. This analysis provided insights into the role of LMBR1L in immune regulation and tumor cell proliferation across different cancer types.

Immunohistochemical analysis and collection of clinical tissue samples

Clinical tissue samples were collected between May 2022 and May 2024 from the Department of Pathology at Lu’an Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. These samples included common cancer tissues along with their corresponding adjacent normal tissues. The extracted proteins and RNA were stored at − 80 °C. For the IHC experiments, fresh tumor and adjacent normal tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, subsequently dehydrated, embedded, sectioned, dried, and stored at room temperature. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed under high pressure. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the samples with a blocking agent for 15 min. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody against LMBR1L (1:300, Immunoway, Beijing, China). The following day, a secondary antibody (Proteintech, Shanghai, China) was applied, and the sections were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (Boster, Wuhan, China) was used as the chromogen to observe the staining under a microscope. The sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min. Dehydration was performed using a graded ethanol series, and the slides were sealed with neutral resin for imaging. LMBR1L expression levels were evaluated using the histochemical scoring system.

Cell proliferation, migration, colony formation, 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) and wound healing assays

Human colorectal cancer cell lines (SW480, RKO, HCT116) and the colonic epithelial cell line NCM460 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The genetic characterization and mismatch repair status of colorectal cancer cell lines are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA), 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The cells were infected with the lentivirus-mediated shRNA expression vector targeting the human LMBR1L gene according to the manufacturer’s instructions (GeneChem, Shanghai, China), with non-targeting shRNA as a control (Supplementary Table S2). Transfection efficiency was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy. Proteins for Western blot (WB) analysis were harvested using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, France), and probed with antibodies against LMBR1L (1:3000, Immunoway, Beijing, China) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:50000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). For quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), RNA was extracted, reverse-transcribed, and amplified using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to evaluate the knockdown of LMBR1L at the mRNA level. The primer pairs were provided in Supplementary Table S2. Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), measuring absorbance at 450 nm. Colony formation ability was determined by seeding cells in 6-well plates (800 cells/well), staining with crystal violet, and counting visible colonies after two weeks. EdU assays (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were performed to evaluate DNA synthesis, with EdU-positive cells visualized under a fluorescence microscope. Wound healing assays were used to assess the migration by creating a scratch in confluent cell layers and measuring migration after 24 h. Finally, the migration capabilities of colorectal cancer cells were assessed using Transwell assays, with the cells migrating toward the chemoattractant medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum.

In vivo evaluation of the effects of LMBR1L knockdown on tumor proliferation and growth in nude mice

Twelve 4-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (12–16 g; GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle and randomly divided into two groups: sh-NC (n = 6) and sh-LMBR1L (n = 6). All animal protocols were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University. Tumor maximum diameter did not exceed 20 mm, and the sizes of all tumors were within the ethical guidelines set by the committee. RKO cells and RKO cells with stable LMBR1L knockdown (RKO shLMBR1L) were suspended at a concentration of 5 × 106 cells in 100 µl sterile phosphate-buffered saline mixed with matrigel and subcutaneously injected into the flanks of the mice. Tumor growth was monitored every other day using a digital caliper, and the tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: volume = (length × width2)/2 After four weeks, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed according to the order of mouse placement. Tumors were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and then deparaffinized. For IHC analysis, the sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and blocking prior to incubation with primary antibodies against LMBR1L (1:300, Immunoway, Beijing, China) and Ki67 (1:100, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies and 3,3'-diaminobenzidine staining. Additionally, the sections were stained with hematoxylin to visualize the nuclei and with eosin to stain the cytoplasm for hematoxylin and eosin staining. Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test, considering P-values < 0.05 as statistically significant, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.0.5. Differences in LMBR1L expression between tumor and normal tissues, as well as between various cancer types, were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test for unpaired samples and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired samples. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method with log-rank tests to compare survival curves, and Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate HRs for OS, DSS, and PFI with 95% confidence intervals. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, and stage of cancer. GSEA and GSVA were utilized to identify the biological pathways associated with LMBR1L expression. GSEA was performed using the fgsea package in R, and GSVA was conducted using the GSVA package, with p-values adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For immune infiltration analysis, Spearman's rank correlation was used to assess the association between LMBR1L expression and the abundance of immune cell populations as determined by the TIMER, CIBERSORT, and EPIC algorithms. The correlation between LMBR1L expression and immune-related gene expression was also evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to assess the diagnostic potential of LMBR1L expression in distinguishing between tumor and normal groups, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated to quantify the accuracy. For analysis of genomic data, such as mutation frequencies and copy number variations (CNVs), chi-square tests or Fisher's exact tests were used depending on the sample size. The relationship between LMBR1L expression and genomic instability measures like aneuploidy and homologous recombination defects was analyzed using linear regression. The meta-analysis of survival data from various studies was performed using the inverse variance method to integrate the HRs, providing a comprehensive assessment of the prognostic significance of LMBR1L across multiple studies.

All the statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Data are presented as mean ± SD for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

The prognostic value of LMBR1L in pan-cancer

To investigate the prognostic significance of LMBR1L expression across 32 different types of cancer, we conducted survival analysis using the Cox proportional hazards model. Our results indicated that higher LMBR1L expression was associated with poorer OS in BRCA, CESC, COAD, KIRC, mesothelioma (MESO), and STAD. Conversely, elevated LMBR1L expression correlated with better prognosis in bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), KICH, LUAD, pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), THCA, Thymoma (THYM), and uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS), as illustrated by the forest plot for patients with high LMBR1L expression in terms of OS (Fig. 3A). Next, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of LMBR1L gene expression in distinguishing tumor groups from normal groups using ROC analysis (Fig. 3B). The results of the ROC curve analysis demonstrated that LMBR1L expression accurately differentiated between tumor and normal groups in pan-cancer, suggesting that LMBR1L is a potential pan-cancer diagnostic gene, then a meta-analysis was performed on the results of univariate Cox survival analysis using the inverse variance method. LMBR1L was found to be associated with OS, DSS, and PFI in COAD (p < 0.01) and identified as a risk factor (HR > 1) (Fig. 3C). Similar associations were observed for STAD (OS, DSS, and PFI, p = 0.05, HR > 1) (Fig. 3D) and BRCA (OS, DSS, and PFI, p < 0.01, HR > 1) (Fig. 3E). Differences in immune subtypes were observed between the high and low LMBR1L expression groups in pan-cancer (Fig. 3F), as well as in several common cancers such as STAD (Fig. 3G). Overall, our findings suggest that LMBR1L expression is significantly elevated in the progression of various cancers and has potential as a prospective pan-cancer prognostic biomarker, particularly for COAD, STAD, and BRCA.

First, patients were divided into four groups (Q1-Q4) based on LMBR1L expression levels, with Q1 representing the top 25% and Q4 representing the lowest 25% of expression. We calculated the average scores for the immune response and genomic status across different groups and visualized the results with a heatmap, revealing the relationship between LMBR1L expression levels and immune infiltration as well as genomic status (Fig. 4A). Next, we analyzed LMBR1L expression across different types of CNVs and found that LMBR1L expression was highest in amplified CNV cases (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, we evaluated the relationship between LMBR1L and mutations in classic oncogenic pathways, finding that tumor protein p53 mutation frequency was generally high across cancers (Fig. 4C), and visualized this using a mutation waterfall plot (Fig. 4D). The results showed that LMBR1L expression was significantly positively correlated with these genomic scores, with higher LMBR1L expression associated with increased chromosomal instability (Fig. 4E). Functional enrichment analysis reveals the role of LMBR1L in tumorigenesis through methylation, cell cycle regulation, and cancer pathways

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms of LMBR1L in tumorigenesis, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the top 100 interacting proteins. GO analysis indicated that LMBR1L participates in macromolecule methylation, DNA/RNA modification, histone remodeling, and regulation of the mitotic cell cycle G1/S transition, processes essential for gene expression, chromatin organization, and cell proliferation (Fig. 5A). Cellular component analysis showed localization at the nuclear periphery, implicating LMBR1L in nuclear signaling and gene regulation, while molecular function analysis linked it to methyltransferase activity. KEGG analysis revealed enrichment in cancer-related pathways, including pancreatic cancer, small cell lung cancer, melanoma, glioma, and prostate cancer, as well as virus-induced carcinogenesis and cellular senescence (Fig. 5B). In pan-cancer GSEA, LMBR1L was associated with multiple signaling pathways (Fig. 5C). In the GSVA, data from the CancerSEA database, which compiles 14 functional states of tumor cells, were used to evaluate pathway activities. We calculated the Pearson correlation between LMBR1L and the scores for various gene sets, finding that in several common cancers, including COAD, STAD, BRCA, and THCA, LMBR1L expression was significantly positively correlated with various tumor processes, such as angiogenesis, apoptosis, differentiation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), hypoxia, inflammation, invasion, proliferation, and stemness (Fig. 5D). We next plotted the differences in LMBR1L expression across different cell cycle phases, revealing that LMBR1L expression is influenced by cell cycle progression (Fig. 5E), and the trends in LMBR1L expression varied at different cell cycle stages (Fig. 5F). The role of LMBR1L in tumor immunity: correlation with immune gene expression, immune cell infiltration, and modulation of the immune microenvironment

Increasing evidence suggests that cancer metabolism not only regulates tumorigenesis and progression but also reprograms immune cell metabolic pathways, leading to impaired antitumor immune responses. Therefore, we investigated the association between LMBR1L and tumor immunity. First, we generated heatmaps of immune-stimulating genes, immune-suppressing genes, chemokines, and HLA expression differences between the high and low LMBR1L expression groups. The results indicated that immune genes were generally upregulated in the high LMBR1L expression group (Fig. 6A). We used three algorithms—TIMER, EPIC, and CIBERSORT—to evaluate immune cell infiltration in individual tumors and assess the correlation between LMBR1L expression and immune infiltration levels. The TIMER algorithm showed a significant positive correlation between LMBR1L expression and CD4 + and CD8 + T cell infiltration across cancers (P < 0.05), and this finding was further confirmed by the EPIC algorithm (Fig. 6B). We then calculated the Pearson correlation between LMBR1L and each immune-related gene, visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 6C). We also assessed the correlation between LMBR1L and ESTIMATE Score, Immune Score, and Stromal Score in several common cancers (Fig. 6D). We subsequently analyzed the Spearman correlation between TIP scores and LMBR1L expression, as well as the inter-correlation between TIP scores. Discussion

Pan-cancer analysis has emerged as a powerful tool for both uncovering shared and cancer-specific molecular features across diverse tumor types, providing new perspectives for identifying prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets [28–30]. Although previous studies have highlighted the critical roles of LMBR1L in lymphoid development and endothelial function [14, 15], its systemic role in shaping the TME, influencing tumor progression, and modulating therapeutic responses remains largely unexplored. In this study, integrating multi-omics datasets with clinical and cellular validation, we reveal that LMBR1L is significantly upregulated in a range of cancers, preferentially localized in CD8⁺ T cells, and closely linked to OS, DSS, and PFI, highlighting its potential as a prognostic marker and a target for immune-based interventions.

Our systematic analysis revealed that LMBR1L is highly expressed in most cancers, but its prognostic impact is highly cancer-type dependent: high expression is associated with poorer survival in cancers such as COAD, BRCA, and STAD, whereas it correlates with better outcomes in LUAD, BLCA, and other cancers. This heterogeneity may arise from intrinsic signaling differences and TME variability among tumors [31]. For instance, Wnt/β-catenin signaling is frequently aberrantly activated in colorectal and gastric cancers [32–34], which may synergize with LMBR1L to promote tumor progression, whereas in KRAS-driven lung cancers, LMBR1L may exhibit distinct functional roles. Moreover, variations in immune contexture may underlie this divergence. “Hot” tumors, characterized by abundant CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and active antitumor immunity [35], may exhibit stronger LMBR1L-mediated immune regulation, whereas “cold” tumors, with limited immune cell infiltration and an immunosuppressed microenvironment [36], may show weaker effects. Tumors with ‘hot’ immunologically active microenvironments and those with ‘cold’ immunologically suppressed ones differ in immune cell infiltration and activity [37, 38], potentially contributing to the cancer-specific regulatory effects of LMBR1L on the TME. Future studies integrating tumor immune subtyping, spatial transcriptomics, and multi-omics profiling are warranted to fully elucidate the context-specific roles of LMBR1L in cancer prognosis.

Functional enrichment analyses (GO and KEGG) indicated that LMBR1L participates in key biological processes, including methylation regulation, cell cycle control, and chromatin remodeling, and is enriched in multiple cancer-related pathways such as pancreatic cancer, small cell lung cancer, and virus-induced oncogenic pathways. Its expression appears to be cell cycle–dependent, suggesting that LMBR1L may facilitate tumor progression through diverse signaling cascades, particularly the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Pearson correlation analysis further revealed that LMBR1L is positively or negatively correlated with CD274 (PD-L1) across different cancer types. Importantly, accumulating evidence indicates that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway can modulate immune evasion by regulating PD-L1 transcription and stability [39, 40], while PD-L1 expression itself may be controlled by β-catenin–dependent transcriptional programs [41, 42]. Given that LMBR1L has been reported to participate in signalosome formation and membrane trafficking [14, 15], it is reasonable to hypothesize that LMBR1L may indirectly regulate β-catenin activation and PD-L1 expression by influencing receptor localization or endocytic recycling. Moreover, LMBR1L depletion has been linked to impaired immune cell development and enhanced β-catenin signaling, suggesting a dual regulatory role that may vary across tumor types. Our study results, together with previous literature [14, 15], suggest that LMBR1L may jointly regulate tumor biology through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and PD-L1–related immune checkpoint signaling. LMBR1L depletion can enhance β-catenin activity, impair immune cell development, and promote EMT, facilitating immune evasion and metastasis; conversely, elevated LMBR1L may serve as a context-specific regulator of β-catenin activity in certain cancers. Notably, the correlation between LMBR1L and PD-L1 is cancer-type dependent: positive correlations are observed in STAD and HNSC, suggesting a potential role in promoting immune checkpoint activation and T cell exhaustion, whereas negative correlations are seen in BLCA, TGCT, LUSC, and PAAD, indicating possible tumor-suppressive effects in these contexts. These observations support the hypothesis that LMBR1L may act as a molecular adaptor linking β-catenin signaling to PD-L1–mediated immune modulation, thereby functioning as a context-dependent switch in immune escape mechanisms. This bidirectional regulatory pattern suggests that LMBR1L may act as a molecular switch, remodeling immune escape mechanisms through distinct pathways depending on the tumor type and immune microenvironment.

The TME plays a central role in tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic response [43, 44], and our results demonstrate that LMBR1L is closely associated with tumor immune regulation across multiple cancer types. Further analysis revealed that LMBR1L expression is tightly linked to tumor immune status, genomic features, and DNA methylation. Patients with high LMBR1L expression often exhibit higher immune cell infiltration, increased TP53 mutation frequency, and greater chromosomal instability, suggesting that LMBR1L may influence tumor progression by modulating both TME composition and genomic stability. Immune infiltration analysis further showed that LMBR1L expression positively correlates with CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and is accompanied by the upregulation of immune-stimulatory genes, chemokines, HLA molecules, and immune checkpoint–related transcripts. These findings suggest that LMBR1L may contribute to the establishment of an immune-active microenvironment by regulating immune cell recruitment and function, thereby participating in the dynamic interplay between immune activation and immune evasion. Collectively, our results highlight the potential role of LMBR1L in shaping the TME and regulating anti-tumor immunity, suggesting its promise as a predictive biomarker for immune checkpoint blockade therapy. Although direct clinical data linking LMBR1L expression to immune checkpoint blockade response are currently lacking, our findings provide a theoretical basis for future validation in larger patient cohorts and single-cell immune lineage studies.

Our study highlights the potential significance of LMBR1L in tumor immunity and prognosis; however, several limitations should be considered. First, despite comprehensive bioinformatic analyses, our findings are primarily based on publicly available datasets, which may be affected by data heterogeneity, selection bias, or incomplete annotations. Second, experimental validation was largely restricted to colorectal cancer cell lines and corresponding animal models, limiting generalizability to other cancer types. Finally, our conclusions lack clinical validation, necessitating prospective studies or clinical trials to establish the diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic utility of LMBR1L in real-world settings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our comprehensive study delineates the multifaceted role of LMBR1L in cancer, emphasizing its significance as a prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target across multiple cancer types. Its role across various signaling pathways and its impact on the immune microenvironment open new avenues for research and therapy, potentially leading to the development of more effective cancer treatments. Further clinical trials and studies are warranted to validate these findings and translate them into therapeutic strategies.
