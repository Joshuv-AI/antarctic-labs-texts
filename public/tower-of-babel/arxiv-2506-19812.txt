1. Introduction

The function of two variables,

\displaystyle f\left(a,b\right)=\frac{\gcd\left(a+b,ab\right)}{\gcd\left(a,b\right)}

is of interest in this paper. We claim the function

f:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\quad\text{where}\quad\left(a,b\right)\mapsto\frac{\gcd\left(a+b,ab\right)}{\gcd\left(a,b\right)}

is surjective and that every natural number (in the codomain) can be attained. Moreover, we aim to characterise which pairs
\left(a,b\right)
produce a given natural number as output.

Proposition 1.1
.

f\left(a,b\right)
is an integer for all
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

By Bézout’s lemma, there exist
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x\left(a+b\right)+yab=\operatorname{gcd}\left(a+b,ab\right)
. Note that there exist coprime
k,l
such that

\displaystyle a=k\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)\quad\text{and}\quad b=l\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle xk\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)+xl\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)+ykl\left(\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)\right)^{2}=\operatorname{gcd}\left(ab,a+b\right).

So,

\displaystyle f\left(a,b\right)=xk+xl+ykl\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)=xk+xl+byk

which is an integer.

\Box

Proposition 1.2
.

f
is a surjection.

Proof.

We effectively wish to show that for any
c\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\left(a,b\right)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
such that
f\left(a,b\right)=c
. Unlike conventional proofs of directly invoking the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, we condition on
c^{2}=a+b\in\mathbb{N}
. Thereafter, we can choose
a=c
and
b=c^{2}-c
so that

\displaystyle f\left(a,b\right)=f\left(c,c^{2}-c\right)=\frac{\operatorname{gcd}\left(c^{2},c^{3}-c^{2}\right)}{\operatorname{gcd}\left(c,c^{2}-c\right)}

Since
c\geq 1
, then
c^{3}-c^{2}\geq 0
and
c^{2}-c\geq 0
. As such,
f\left(a,b\right)=\frac{c^{2}}{c}=c
so
f
is indeed surjective.

\Box

Now, here is the explicit connection to

\gamma\begin{bmatrix}a^{\prime}\\

\gamma^{\text{T}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\

which reduces to
\gamma_{11}+\gamma_{21}=1

. Solving yields

\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}b+1&b\\

While this stabilizer does not preserve positivity, it acts on primitive pairs
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
with fixed sum

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
n=p_{1}p_{2}\ldots p_{k}
be squarefree. For each prime
p_{i}
, define a minimal pair
(s_{i},t_{i})
and gap
g_{i}
as follows:

•

For
p_{i}=2
, the minimal pair is
(s_{i},t_{i})=(2,2)
, as
f(2,2)=\gcd(\gcd(2,2),1+1)=\gcd(2,2)=2
. The next solutions are
(6,2)
and
(2,6)
, so gap
g_{i}=4
.

•

For odd
p_{i}
, the minimal pair is
(s_{i},t_{i})=(p_{i},2p_{i})
, as
f(p_{i},2p_{i})=\gcd(\gcd(p_{i},2p_{i}),1+2)=\gcd(p_{i},3)
. Since
p_{i}>2
,
\gcd(p_{i},3)=1
if
p_{i}\neq 3
, or
3
if
p_{i}=3
. The next solutions are
(p_{i},2p_{i}+p_{i}^{2})

.

The arithmetic progressions for

are
(s_{i}+mg_{i},t_{i})

. For composite

Since

The choice of variation direction for each

generates

families of solutions, covering all pairs.

For a fixed choice of variation directions, the Chinese remainder theorem gives

are integers coprime to

. The conditions ensure

Thus,
f(a,b)=n
. Conversely, if

.

Let

. The pairs

(ii)

Additionally,

Theorem 3.2

(limiting density)
.

Define the limiting density
\rho
to be
\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n}
. Then,

(1)

\displaystyle\rho=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}\left(p+1\right)}\right)\approx 0.88151.

where the product is taken over all primes
p
.

Proof.
Recall that any pair
(a,b)
can be written as
a=da^{\prime}
and
b=db^{\prime}
, where
d=\gcd(a,b)
and
\gcd(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=1
. This yields
f\left(a,b\right)=\gcd\left(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime},d\right)
. We seek the density of pairs
(a,b)
where
f(a,b)=1
, i.e.
\gcd(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime},d)=1
. This means that
d
and
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}
share no common prime factors. The condition
\gcd(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime},d)=1
holds if and only if for every prime
p
,
p
does not divide both
d
and
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}
. By the Chinese remainder theorem, these conditions are independent across primes. Thus, the density
\rho
factors as an Euler product as follows:

\rho=\prod_{p}\left(1-\mathbb{P}_{p}\right)\quad\text{where }\mathbb{P}_{p}\text{ is the probability that }p\mid\gcd(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime},d).

Here,
p
is a fixed prime. We compute
\mathbb{P}_{p}
by considering two dependent events. Let
A
denote the event
p\mid d
(so
p
divides both
a
and
b
) and let
B
denote the event
p\mid(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime})
(given that
p\mid d
).

Note that
p\mid a
occurs with probability
\frac{1}{p}
. Similarly,
p\mid b
occurs with probability
\frac{1}{p}
. The probability that
p\mid d
is the same as the probability that
p
divides both
a
and
b
. By independence, this evaluates to
\frac{1}{p^{2}}
.

We then compute the probability of event
B
given
A
. Given
p\mid d
, we have
a=da^{\prime}
and
b=db^{\prime}
with
\gcd(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=1
. Note that
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
are defined modulo
p
. Since
\gcd(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=1
, the pair
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\mod p
is uniformly distributed over all pairs in
(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{2}
except
(0,0)
. There are
p^{2}-1
possible pairs
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\mod p
. The condition
a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}\equiv 0\mod p
has solutions: for each
a^{\prime}\not\equiv 0
, set
b^{\prime}\equiv-a^{\prime}\mod p
(which is non-zero). There are
p-1
solutions (one for each non-zero
a^{\prime}
).

Thus, the probability that
p\mid(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime})
given that
p\mid d
is

\frac{p-1}{p^{2}-1}=\frac{1}{p+1}.

We then compute the joint probability
\mathbb{P}_{p}
. The probability that both events occur is

\mathbb{P}_{p}=\frac{1}{p^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{p+1}=\frac{1}{p^{2}(p+1)}.

By complementation, the probability that
p
does not divide
\gcd(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime},d)
is:

1-\mathbb{P}_{p}=1-\frac{1}{p^{2}(p+1)}.

As such, we obtain the desired Euler product for
\rho
.

. Indeed, the appearance of the quadratic class number constant in our analysis of
f\left(a,b\right)
highlights a profound connection between Additive Number Theory and Algebraic Number Theory.

From an analytic viewpoint, we can interpret the Euler product in (
1
) as an asymptotic mean (Lemma
3.3
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\varphi\left(n\right)
denote the Euler totient function and
\sigma\left(n\right)
denote the sum of divisors function. Then,

\displaystyle\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}\left(p+1\right)}\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\varphi\left(n\right)\sigma\left(n\right)}{n^{2}}

Proof.

It is a known fact that
\varphi\left(n\right)
and
\sigma\left(n\right)
are multiplicative. Recall that an arithmetic function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be multiplicative if
f\left(1\right)=1
and for any
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)=1
, we have
f\left(ab\right)=f\left(a\right)f\left(b\right)
. Define

(2)

\displaystyle f\left(n\right)=\frac{\varphi\left(n\right)\sigma\left(n\right)}{n^{2}}.

One can directly verify that
f\left(1\right)=1
and
f\left(n\right)
is multiplicative. Also, the quotient by
n^{2}
is said to be
local
since it depends on the prime factorisation of
n
. For a multiplicative function, the mean value can be expressed as an Euler product over primes. Due to this result by Halász and Wirsing
[
3
]
, we have

(3)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(n)=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{g(p^{k})}{p^{k}}.

For a prime
p
and exponent
k\geq 0
, evaluate
g
at
p^{k}
to obtain

\varphi(p^{k})=p^{k}-p^{k-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma(p^{k})=\frac{p^{k+1}-1}{p-1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle g(p^{k})=\frac{\varphi(p^{k})\sigma(p^{k})}{p^{2k}}=1-\frac{1}{p^{k+1}}\quad\text{so}\quad\frac{g\left(p^{k}\right)}{p^{k}}=\frac{1}{p^{k}}-\frac{1}{p^{2k+1}}.

As such,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{g(p^{k})}{p^{k}}=\frac{p}{p-1}-\frac{1}{p^{3}-p}.

Multiplying by
1-\frac{1}{p}
yields the Euler factor so

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\left(\frac{p}{p-1}-\frac{1}{p^{3}-p}\right)=1-\frac{1}{p^{2}\left(p+1\right)}

and the result follows.

\Box

4. Patterns in
f_{n}\left(a,b\right)
for
n\geq 2

Let

\displaystyle f_{r}\left(a,b\right)=\frac{\operatorname{gcd}\left(a^{r}+b^{r},ab\right)}{\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)}.

In particular,
f_{1}\left(a,b\right)=f\left(a,b\right)
. We note that for
r\geq 2
, there is a nice method to factor
a^{r}+b^{r}
, albeit for the
r
even case,
a+b
will not be a factor
[
1
]
. Over
\mathbb{Q}
,
x^{n}-1
factors as follows:

(4)

\displaystyle x^{n}-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_{d}\left(x\right)

where
\Phi_{d}
is the
d^{\text{th}}
cyclotomic polynomial. In general, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
n^{\text{th}}
cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{n}\left(x\right)
is the unique irreducible polynomial with integer coefficients that is a divisor of
x^{n}-1
and is not a divisor of
x^{k}-1
for any
k<n
. Its roots are all
n^{\text{th}}
primitive roots of unity
e^{\frac{2k\pi i}{n}}
, where
k<n
and
\operatorname{gcd}\left(k,n\right)=1
. In other words, the
n^{\text{th}}
cyclotomic polynomial is equal to

(5)

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}\left(x\right)=\prod_{\begin{smallmatrix}1\leq k\leq n\\
\operatorname{gcd}\left(k,n\right)=1\end{smallmatrix}}\left(x-e^{\frac{2k\pi i}{n}}\right).

By the identity
x^{r}+1=\frac{x^{2r}-1}{x^{r}-1}
, (
4
) becomes

(6)

\displaystyle x^{r}+1=\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{d\mid r}\Phi_{2d}(x)}{\displaystyle\prod_{d\mid r}\Phi_{d}(x)}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid 2r\\
d\nmid r\end{subarray}}\Phi_{d}(x).

Upon homogenisation, we have

\displaystyle a^{r}+b^{r}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid 2r\\
d\nmid r\end{subarray}}\Phi_{d}(a,b)\quad\text{where}\quad\Phi_{d}\left(x,y\right)=y^{\deg\left(\Phi_{d}\right)}\Phi_{d}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right).

We see that each irreducible factor
\Phi_{d}\left(a,b\right)
satisfies
\operatorname{gcd}\left(\Phi_{d}\left(a,b\right),a\right)=1
, and similarly
\operatorname{gcd}\left(\Phi_{d}\left(a,b\right),b\right)=1
. In particular, no new prime can divide both
a
(or
b
) and any of the cyclotomic factors other than those primes already dividing both
a
and
b
. Hence, for every prime
p
, we have

\displaystyle p\mid\operatorname{gcd}\left(a^{r}+b^{r},ab\right)\quad\text{if and only if}\quad p\mid a\text{ and }p\mid b.

This implies that
\operatorname{gcd}\left(a^{r}+b^{r},ab\right)
is just
\operatorname{gcd}\left(a,b\right)
. So, for
r\geq 2
, we can evaluate the limiting density
\rho
, which turns out to be

(7)

\displaystyle\rho=\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\approx 0.60793.

The Euler product for the density arises from the independence of divisibility events over primes. For each prime
p
, the probability that
p
divides both
a
and
b
is
\frac{1}{p^{2}}
. Thus, the probability that
p
does not divide both is
1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}
, and the product over all primes gives the density. This aligns with the asymptotic density of coprime pairs in
\mathbb{N}^{2}
.

Coincidentally, the density obtained in (
7
) is precisely the answer to the following question: if two positive integers are randomly chosen, what is the probability that they are relatively prime? We shall use the Dirichlet hyperbola method (Theorem
4.1
) to help us
[
4
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(Dirichlet hyperbola method)
.

Let
f
be a multiplicative function. Define

\displaystyle F\left(n\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}f\left(k\right).

Let
f=g\ast h
, where
\ast
denotes Dirichlet convolution and
g
and
h
are multiplicative functions, so the sum becomes

(8)

\displaystyle F\left(n\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{xy=k}g\left(x\right)h\left(y\right).

Then,

(9)

\displaystyle F\left(n\right)=\sum_{x=1}^{a}\sum_{y=1}^{n/x}g(x)h(y)+\sum_{y=1}^{b}\sum_{x=1}^{n/y}g(x)h(y)-\sum_{x=1}^{a}\sum_{y=1}^{b}g(x)h(y)

Although technically the inner sum in (
8
) runs over all ordered pairs
\left(x,y\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, we can leave our notation as mentioned. On the
xy
-plane, these pairs
\left(x,y\right)
lie on a hyperbola, and when the double sum is fully expanded, there is a bijection between the terms of the sum and the lattice points in the first quadrant on the hyperbolas of the form
xy=k
, where
1\leq k\leq n
is an integer.

Also, the geometric interpretation of the Dirichlet hyperbola method generally involves the principle of inclusion and inclusion. Let

such that

. Then the lattice points

, another region is bounded by

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\varphi\left(n\right)
denote the Euler totient function. For
x>1
, we have

(10)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\varphi\left(n\right)=\frac{3x^{2}}{\pi^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(x^{3/2}).

Proof.

Note that
\varphi=\mu\ast N
, where
N\left(n\right)=n
. By applying the Dirichlet hyperbola method with
f=N
and
g=\mu
, we have

(11)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\varphi\left(n\right)=\sum_{n\leq y}\mu\left(n\right)F\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)+\sum_{m\leq x/y}N\left(m\right)G\left(\frac{x}{m}\right)-F\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)G\left(y\right),

where

(12)

\displaystyle F\left(x\right)=\sum_{n\leq x}N\left(n\right)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(x\right)\quad\text{and}\quad G\left(x\right)=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu\left(n\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(x\right)

Letting
y=\sqrt{x}
, one can deduce that

(13)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\varphi\left(n\right)=\sum_{n\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{\mu\left(n\right)}{n^{2}}\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{O}(x^{3/2}).

By the Möbius inversion formula, we have

(14)

\displaystyle\zeta\left(2\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{2}}=1

so

(15)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{2}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{n>\sqrt{x}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1/2}).

The result follows.

\Box

Now, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
S
denote the set of
\left(a,b\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
such that
1\leq a,b\leq N
. Then, the total number of elements in

is given by

(16)

\displaystyle 1+2\sum_{b\leq N}\varphi(b)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}N^{2}+\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2}),

where we used Theorem
4.2
. This shows that the probability that two randomly chosen positive integers are relatively prime is
6/\pi^{2}
.
