The infraoptic or infrachiasmatic origin of the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) is an exceedingly rare anatomical variant with a prevalence of 0.086% on MR angiography (MRA).1 It was first reported by Robinson in 1959 during the routine dissection of a male cadaver where the right ACA was noted to arise from the right internal cerebral artery (ICA) immediately after it pierced the dura mater.2 This anomalous vessel then passed inferior to the right optic nerve as it coursed medially to emerge between the right and left optic nerves to reach the longitudinal fissure. Robinson postulated that the pathogenesis of this aberrant vessel was likely an enlargement of the anastomosis between the superior hypophyseal arteries and the ACA.2

Since its discovery, approximately 55 cases of this variant have been reported in literature. Of these, only 20 were bilateral. We present the case of a bilateral infraoptic origin of the ACA with an associated de novo aneurysm that was discovered during an elective diagnostic cerebral angiography study. We also present a relevant review of the literature.

Illustrative Case

A 55-year-old female with an apparent history of right cavernous carotid artery aneurysm that had not been followed up in many years was referred by her primary care physician for further evaluation. The aneurysm was initially discovered incidentally more than 10 years ago on routine imaging. She had no known associated risk factors for developing cerebral aneurysm(s). A diagnostic cerebral angiography study was performed at the time by a different physician, and it demonstrated fusiform dilation of the right cavernous carotid artery without any intracranial aneurysm(s). The patient was subsequently lost to follow-up.

An MRA study was eventually performed that demonstrated a new 6-mm aneurysm arising from the right ophthalmic segment of the ICA. At the time of presentation, the patient was asymptomatic and had a normal neurological examination. We performed a diagnostic cerebral angiography study to better characterize this aneurysm.

The angiogram showed findings consistent with the rare infraoptic origin of the ACAs bilaterally. The right ACA originated from the cavernous/ophthalmic segment of the ICA. The right A1 segment was hypoplastic. At the origin of the infraoptic ACA, a focal area of vessel caliber increase or dilation as well as a medially projecting aneurysm measuring 5 mm × 4 mm × 4.7 mm was noted without any high-risk morphological features (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Observations

The infraoptic origin of the ACA, while rarely encountered, is a clinically significant variant that should be recognized. It is characterized by an anomalous origin and course of the A1 segment of the ACA. In the normal anatomy, the A1 segment arises from the ICA terminus and courses anteromedially and superior to the ipsilateral optic nerve to join the anterior communicating artery (ACoA) complex. In the infraoptic variant, the A1 segment arises from the distal cavernous or ophthalmic segment of the ICA, near or at the origin of the ophthalmic artery. It is therefore often referred to as an anomalous carotid-ACA anastomosis. While most cases arise from the intradural segment of the ICA, an extradural origin proximal to the ophthalmic artery has been reported.3

The infraoptic ACA is readily identified on conventional angiography due to its distinct features: 1) the infraoptic ACA presents as an early bifurcation of the ICA, often at the level of the ophthalmic segment; 2) the A1 segment takes a characteristic horizontal-medial course as it passes under the ipsilateral optic nerve before turning superiorly to join the normally positioned ACoA; and 3) injection of the infraoptic ACA shows that it supplies the normal ACA vascular territory.3

The infraoptic ACA can have four different configurations. Type I is considered an ICA-ACA anastomosis with normal bifurcation of the ACA and MCA at the ICA terminus and a normal supraoptic A1 segment; however, an additional collateral has an infraoptic course. Type II is characterized by an infraoptic origin of the ACA without any ipsilateral supraoptic A1. Type III is a unilateral infraoptic ACA without a contralateral A1. The infraoptic ACA supplies bilateral ACA territories. Type IV has an infraoptic accessory ACA that does not anastomose with the supraoptic A1.4 Our case represents the type II configuration.

Several theories have been proposed to explain the pathogenesis of the infraoptic ACA. One theory suggests a failure of regression of the primitive ophthalmic arteries in the developing embryo. During embryonic development, the orbital region is supplied by the ventral and dorsal ophthalmic arteries. The ventral ophthalmic artery (VOA) arises from the future ACA and enters the orbit through the optic canal. The dorsal ophthalmic artery (DOA) originates from the cavernous segment of the ICA and enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure. During development, the primitive VOA and DOA regress to give rise to the definitive ophthalmic artery. A failure of regression of the DOA is therefore postulated to result in the development of the ACA from this segment (the cavernous ICA). Other hypotheses include enlargement of prechiasmatic anastomosis, and persistent anastomosis between primitive maxillary and olfactory arteries. To date, the embryogenesis of this anomaly remains controversial.4–8

The infraoptic ACA variants are often associated with aneurysms, likely due to hemodynamic stress from abnormal morphology and blood flow patterns. Approximately 59% of infraoptic ACAs are associated with aneurysms of the ACoA. Specifically, the frequencies of ACoA aneurysms in type I, II, and III anomalies are reported to be 100%, 46%, and 67%, respectively. In type II, seen in our patient, the hypoplastic A1 leads to increased stress being exerted at the dominant A1, leading to aneurysm formation. Additionally, the right-angled T-shape of the infraoptic ACA increases hemodynamic stress at the ICA bifurcation.4 Other less commonly associated vascular abnormalities include hypoplastic or aplastic contralateral ACA, carotid-basilar anastomosis variants, fused pericallosal artery and plexiform ACoA, carotid agenesis, moyamoya disease, and coarctation of the aorta. Lessons

First, the present case provides a unique look at the potential natural history of this rare anatomical variant. It demonstrates the real risk for de novo aneurysm formation despite the absence of known risk factors, as seen in this patient over a 10-year period, underscoring the importance of close long-term follow-up in these patients. We recommend annual surveillance using CT angiography or MRA to enable early detection of de novo aneurysms. In cases in which noninvasive imaging reveals concerning features, a cerebral angiogram should be obtained. Additionally, the potential contribution of underlying genetic predispositions should be considered, given the frequent association of this variant with other vascular pathologies of known genetic origin.24

Second, this anatomical variant carries significant implications for both endovascular and microsurgical interventions. The existing literature suggests that type I variants, due to the straighter course of the infraoptic A1 segment, may be more amenable to endovascular approaches. In contrast, type II variants may present challenges for surgical clipping, particularly if unrecognized preoperatively, as the aneurysm may be obscured by the optic nerve during a standard pterional craniotomy. In type III configurations, special care must be taken to avoid prolonged temporary clipping or inadvertent occlusion of the infraoptic A1, as this single vessel supplies bilateral distal ACAs.4

In our patient, who exhibited a type II infraoptic A1 variant associated with a posteriorly projecting aneurysm, surgical clipping would likely be high risk due to the potential for injury to the optic chiasm and the need for a more complex surgical approach. Although coil embolization is often considered in such cases, it may pose an unacceptably high risk for untoward complications due to the wide-neck morphology of the aneurysm as well as the significant incorporation of the aneurysm neck into the relatively dysplastic parent vessel. While a flow diverting stent may also be considered, covering large-flow branch vessels such as the ACA and/or MCA can lead to significantly high rates of thromboembolic complications. Following a lengthy discussion with the patient regarding the risks and benefits of treatment versus observation, we elected for close monitoring with serial angiographic imaging. While the literature documents successful management of infraoptic ACA aneurysms, both ruptured and unruptured, using microsurgical and endovascular techniques, meticulous preoperative angiographic assessment remains essential to guide treatment planning based on the patient’s specific vascular anatomy.25–28

In conclusion, infraoptic ACA or carotid-ACA anastomoses represent rare vascular anomalies that are frequently associated with aneurysms and other cerebrovascular abnormalities. Despite the lack of known risk factors, the risk of aneurysmal development de novo is evident. Their recognition is essential to guide appropriate treatment strategies and mitigate the risk of complications. Close follow-up with advanced imaging may be necessary in patients with known infraoptic origins of the ACA.
