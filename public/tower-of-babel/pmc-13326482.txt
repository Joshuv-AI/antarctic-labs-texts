Psychological pathways from school belonging to life satisfaction: the serial mediating roles of resilience and self-efficacy
Authors: Binaz Bozkur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate whether resilience and self-efficacy mediate the relationship between university students’ sense of belonging at school and their life satisfaction. The sample consisted of 507 students (360 females, 147 males) from various universities in Türkiye. Data were collected using the Sense of Belonging Scale, General Self-Efficacy Scale, Life Satisfaction Scale, Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale, and a Demographic Information Form. The correlation and serial mediation analyses were conducted. School belonging showed strong positive correlations with resilience, self-efficacy, and life satisfaction. Both resilience and self-efficacy independently mediated the link between belonging and life satisfaction, and together they formed a significant serial mediation pathway. The results highlight the importance of fostering a strong sense of belonging to enhance students’ resilience and self-efficacy, ultimately promoting higher life satisfaction and overall wellbeing.Keywords: Sense of belonging to school, Life-satisfaction, Resilience, Self-efficacy, University students

Introduction

Enrollment in higher education continues to increase, bringing increasingly diverse students into environments that demand rapid academic, social, and personal adaptation [44]. This process appears particularly challenging in Türkiye, where students report a number of the lowest levels of life satisfaction and school belonging among countries assessed in the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [52]. For instance, while fewer than 4% of students in the Netherlands report low life satisfaction, more than 20% of Turkish students fall into this category. Further insight from the OECD Education Policy Outlook [53] highlight systemic challenges; Turkish students perceive the school climate as less favorable than the OECD average and report lower levels of belonging (index score = –0.14 vs. 0.01). A growing body of research shows that students who view themselves as valued members of the school community, a sense referred to as school belonging (SSB), exhibit greater psychological adjustment and are more likely to persist in their education [29, 68]. Theoretically, SSB is thought to strengthen two key personal resources: resilience, the capacity to rebound from adversity, and self-efficacy, the belief in one’s ability to cope with challenges [34]. Both of these resources have been identified as strong predictors of life satisfaction among university students [41]. The abrupt transition to online learning during the Covid-19 pandemic further underscored the buffering effect of school belonging, as students with higher SSB levels showed better psychological outcomes in the face of social isolation [31]. Building on this literature, the current study tests serial mediation models in which resilience and self-efficacy mediate the effects of SSB on life satisfaction.

A sense of belonging and life-satisfaction

The importance of belonging is well-established in motivational and psychological theories. Maslow’s hierarchy of needs places belonging just above physiological and safety needs, underscoring its foundational role in psychological development [48]. Moreover, the belongingness hypothesis, proposed by Baumeister and Leary in 1995, asserts that people inherently desire to establish and nurture profound, enduring connections with others.

In educational settings, this translates into students needing to feel accepted, supported, and included, an experience conceptualized as SSB. In an educational context, SSB reflects students' perceptions of personal acceptance, respect, and inclusion within their academic environment [28]. At the university level, this concept extends to encompass the social support and connectedness students feel on campus, alongside their experiences of being accepted, respected, and valued by faculty, staff, and peers [69]. SSB reflects how strongly students perceive themselves as essential and valued academic community members [59]. The key aspects of SSB include students’ perceived social support and feelings of emotional safety on campus, as well as the perception of mutual respect, acceptance, and being valued by the university community [69]. The Sense of Belonging construct includes related dimensions reflecting peer and faculty support, which together capture a broader and integrated sense of belonging rather than entirely independent components [37]. Accordingly, this construct has often been operationalized using a total score to represent overall belonging as a unified psychological experience (e.g., [25, 75]. The level of SSB experienced by students is shaped by a variety of factors, including individual characteristics, institutional practices, and the broader social environment [79].

Although SSB is often conceptualized as a broadly beneficial psychological resource, it may not be entirely uniform or context-independent in its formation and effects. Contemporary perspectives emphasize that belonging-related processes are shaped not only by individual differences but also by social-contextual and normative factors. For instance, perceived social norms and expectations regarding interpersonal interactions may influence how individuals interpret social experiences and inclusion-related cues [14]. At the same time, research suggests that individuals may respond differently to experiences of inclusion and exclusion depending on factors such as the strength of their need to belong and identity-related processes [12, 15]. From this perspective, both the level of SSB and its psychological benefits — such as enhanced resilience, self-efficacy, and well-being — may vary depending on these personal and contextual characteristics. Accordingly, SSB is better understood not merely as a universal psychological resource, but as a socially embedded and condition-dependent construct, whose formation and effects may differ across individuals and contexts. In line with the scope of the present study, these contextual and individual variations are acknowledged at a theoretical level rather than explicitly modeled.

Students with a robust SSB demonstrate a higher probability of attaining favorable academic outcomes, including heightened engagement, augmented motivation, elevated retention rates, and diminished intentions to drop out [30, 57, 70]. Conversely, a weak SSB has been shown to have a negative impact on student integration and increases the likelihood of dropping out [36, 74]. A robust SSB has been demonstrated to support academic success, contribute to psychological well-being, and foster more positive university experiences [13, 20]. A comprehensive SSB, therefore, fosters a sense of identity, security, and community, which is crucial for students' psychological, social, and academic development [39]. Consequently, a comprehensive SSB is also closely linked to life-satisfaction.

Life satisfaction, a critical component of overall well-being [56], entails an individual's comprehensive evaluation of their life quality [23]. This evaluation is related not only to temporary emotional states but also to the individual's general perspective on their life. Life satisfaction is not only an indicator of personal well-being, but it is also associated with important predictive outcomes in educational, occupational, and social domains. For example, high life satisfaction has been linked to academic achievement, greater work satisfaction, and performance, the ability to establish and maintain healthy relationships, and improved psychological health [33, 61, 71]. For university students, life satisfaction can affect not only their current well-being but also their emotional resilience, commitment to life goals, and social functioning after graduation [3, 4]. Research consistently demonstrates that students with a strong sense of school belonging (SSB) tend to exhibit higher engagement in academic activities and are more likely to receive social support, which aids in navigating challenges [56]. As a result, these students are more likely to evaluate their lives positively [38]. In this context, understanding the relationship between life satisfaction and the key elements of the university experience, such as SSB, is important not only for evaluating students' present well-being but also for identifying structural and psychological intervention areas that may enhance student success, persistence, and long-term social adaptation.

A comprehensive review of the existing literature indicates that an SSB is associated with several positive outcomes, including enhanced self-esteem, lower levels of anxiety, and better mental health, all of which are vital for overall life satisfaction [5, 28]. Moreover, life-satisfaction is associated with positive learning outcomes [3, 33], and the lifelong positive results of efforts to support life-satisfaction in education are considered effective social investments [4]. In this context, the connection between SSB and life-satisfaction is also worth considering. By exploring these dynamics, we aim to contribute to the literature by highlighting the critical pathways that connect SSB with overall well-being and providing insight into how educational institutions can better support student life-satisfaction.

Resilience and self-efficacy as mediators

Resilience is a multifaceted capacity that enables individuals to maintain or rapidly regain psychological well-being in the face of stressors [27, 46]. The current study is based on the Connor-Davidson Resilience framework (CD-RISC; [19], which consists of the following: (a) personal competence and perseverance; (b) trust in instincts and self-efficacy; (c) positive acceptance of change; (d) perceived control; and (e) spiritual influences. Self-efficacy refers to the belief that one can organize and implement the necessary actions to achieve set goals [11]. Although self-efficacy is represented as a belief, resilience is conceptually broader and reflects a dynamic process of using both internal strengths and external resources to bounce back from adversities [50, 78]. Although resilience is empirically closely related to self-efficacy, it can theoretically be distinguished from self-efficacy beliefs, as self-efficacy can exist even in the absence of stressors [65].

The directionality between resilience and self-efficacy remains an area of scholarly debate [65]. One line of research suggests that successfully coping with challenges fosters increased self-efficacy [47], while another posits that self-efficacy promotes resilience by motivating proactive and persistent coping strategies [18]. These perspectives reflect the dynamic and potentially reciprocal relationship between the two constructs. Despite this, both resilience and self-efficacy are understood as distinct but interrelated psychological strengths that contribute to adaptive functioning [21, 45]. Numerous studies have shown a strong positive correlation between them [43, 80]. However, from a process-based perspective, resilience can be conceptualized as a capacity that enables individuals to effectively manage stressors encountered in a university environment and cope with academic and social challenges. Repeated experiences of effective coping and adaptation are theorized to function as mastery experiences, which represent the most influential source of self-efficacy beliefs within social cognitive theory [11]. In this sense, resilience-related coping successes may gradually strengthen students’ confidence in their ability to manage academic demands and interpersonal challenges, thereby fostering stronger self-efficacy beliefs over time. Supporting this view, Sagone and De Caroli [62] found that adolescents with higher levels of resilience consistently reported stronger perceived self-efficacy across academic and interpersonal domains, and Xu et al. [76] demonstrated that resilience significantly predicted self-efficacy among college students. Accordingly, resilience can be understood as a proximal adaptive process that contributes to the development of self-efficacy through accumulated successful coping experiences. Therefore, in the present study, we prioritized the resilience-to-self-efficacy sequence because belonging experiences are expected to first influence students’ adaptive coping capacity within the university environment before consolidating generalized competence-related beliefs.

Research supports the positive relationship between resilience and school engagement [51, 73]. On the other hand, self-efficacy is positively affected by the sense of belonging to school [2], and studies show a positive correlation between the sense of belonging to school and self-efficacy [8, 66]. Therefore, in this study, resilience and self-efficacy are considered psychological resources that can be positively affected by SSB and potentially play an important role in increasing life satisfaction.

Within this framework, we posit a sequential pathway through which SSB supports resilience, which in turn supports self-efficacy and greater life satisfaction. This sequencing is theoretically grounded in the Self-System Model of Motivational Development [67], which proposes that when school contexts fulfill students' need for relatedness, students develop adaptive coping capacities that progressively consolidate into stronger competence-related beliefs — paralleling the hypothesized SSB → resilience → self-efficacy pathway in the present study. Consistent with this model, school belonging reflects a psychologically safe and socially supportive academic context [28, 69] that may enhance individuals' capacity to adapt to stress (resilience) and their belief in their own abilities. In the specific context of university belonging, this sequencing is particularly meaningful because belongingness provides students with a psychologically safe and socially supportive environment in which they can engage with academic challenges more confidently and persist despite setbacks. Such environments facilitate adaptive coping responses and emotional regulation, which are core components of resilience. As students repeatedly experience successful adjustment within this supportive context, these coping experiences may subsequently translate into stronger beliefs in their capability to manage future academic and social demands, thereby strengthening self-efficacy. Thus, resilience may operate as an intermediate adaptive mechanism through which belonging experiences are internalized into competence-related beliefs.

Previous research suggests that belonging experiences increase emotional security, access to social support, and perceived competence, which in turn support the development of adaptive psychological resources [21, 55]. Although resilience and self-efficacy can be conceptualized as relatively stable traits, they are also considered dynamic and context-sensitive capacities that develop over time, particularly in relational environments such as the school context [45, 50]. Therefore, we modeled them as outcomes of SSB rather than as moderators or antecedents. However, it is also necessary to consider the limitations of the cross-sectional design. Importantly, both resilience and self-efficacy have shown positive and unique associations with life satisfaction in previous research [1, 10], supporting the rationale for examining their sequential role in this context.

Control variables

Gender, age, and class level were included as covariates to account for their potential associations with life satisfaction and related psychological constructs. While demographic factors typically show modest effects on life satisfaction [61], gender-based differences, such as boys reporting higher life satisfaction than girls, have been observed in large-scale studies [52]. Similarly, class level may shape students’ academic and social integration. Prior research has demonstrated that a sense of school belonging can vary significantly across educational stages, often decreasing as students advance and encounter more academic pressure or reduced institutional support [30, 58]. Controlling for class level helps account for developmental variation in students’ belonging and well-being, enabling more accurate testing of the hypothesized mediation pathways. This approach is consistent with previous studies that statistically adjusted for demographic factors in similar models (for example, [21].

The present study

Although previous studies have linked school belonging (SSB) to life satisfaction, the mechanisms underlying this relationship have not been sufficiently explored. College students face a variety of academic, financial, and psychological challenges throughout their education [41]. A key protective factor facilitating their ability to cope with these challenges is a sense of school belonging, which has been associated with higher academic achievement, improved psychological well-being, and greater life satisfaction [16, 21]. While the positive impact of SSB on well-being is well documented, little is known about the psychological processes through which they enhance life satisfaction.

Emerging adulthood, which typically extends from late adolescence to the twenties, is a critical developmental stage characterized by identity exploration, increased autonomy, and increased educational and emotional demands [6]. As students navigate these transitions, psychological resources such as resilience and self-efficacy become particularly important. School belonging experiences may provide a sense of social connectedness that fosters adaptive coping and self-confidence during this formative period. Therefore, understanding how SSB influences life satisfaction through these resources is important, especially in the developmental context of emerging adulthood.

To address this gap, the current study examines the mediating roles of resilience and self-efficacy in the relationship between SSB and life satisfaction among college students. Resilience, generally defined as the capacity to adapt to adversity and maintain well-being, is positively associated with SSB and has been shown to facilitate psychological adjustment in students [50, 51]. Similarly, self-efficacy, defined as an individual’s belief in their ability to do so, has been associated with both resilience and SSB and influences academic and personal development [2, 66]. Although previous research has examined these constructs independently, studies investigating their interrelated, sequential effects are still rare. This study proposes serial mediation models that suggest that a strong sense of school belonging promotes resilience and self-efficacy, which ultimately promote greater life satisfaction.

This study focuses on Turkish university students who face different challenges within the higher education system, including an intensely competitive admissions process, academic pressures, and financial concerns. Reports indicate that approximately 20% of Turkish students experience low life satisfaction, and this group exhibits lower levels of SSB than other countries studied, such as European countries [52]. Given these factors, investigating the protective role of SSB in this specific context is crucial for developing targeted interventions.

By testing this model, the study aims to provide a more detailed understanding of how SSB indirectly increases life satisfaction through resilience and self-efficacy. The findings may contribute to the development of targeted interventions designed to promote a more supportive university environment and ultimately support students’ psychological well-being and academic success. Methods

Participants and procedure

Before commencing the research, a Monte Carlo Power Analysis was conducted to determine the adequate sample size for the serial mediation model under investigation. This involved reviewing the relevant literature and analyzing correlations between variables. An ‘a priori’ power analysis, following the method developed by Schoemann, Boulton, and Short [63], was conducted with 1000 replications and 20,000 Monte Carlo draws per replication at a 95% confidence level. The analysis concluded that a minimum sample size of 161 participants is necessary for the study.

The research population includes 507 students (360 female and 147 male) enrolled in various departments within education faculties in different cities in Turkey. The sample's mean age was 22.20 ± 3.87 years. The majority of the participants (89.9%) were aged twenty-five or younger, with a smaller portion (10.1%) aged over twenty-five, reflecting a typical age range for undergraduate students in Turkey, where most students begin university around the age of eighteen but some may extend their studies or enroll later. The study employed a convenience sampling method, selecting participants based on their availability and willingness to take part. Undergraduate students from different universities in Turkey were specifically approached and invited to participate in the study. To ensure a diverse sample, data were gathered from students enrolled in education faculties at three universities located in the northern, southern, and central regions of Turkey. The departments included Guidance and Psychological Counseling (n = 93, 18.3%); Preschool Education (n = 85, 16.8%); English Language Teaching (n = 68, 13.4%); Special Education (n = 75, 14.8%); Mathematics Education (n = 59, 11.6%); Science Education (n = 64, 12.6%); and Classroom Teaching (n = 63, 12.4%). Class-level distribution was similarly broad, with 19.5% first-year, 20.1% second-year, 21.3% third-year, and 39.1% fourth-year students, ensuring representation across all years of study.

The study was conducted between October and December 2023. Postgraduate and open faculty students were excluded from the study to maintain a focus on the undergraduate experience. The measurement tools for data collection were selected and approved by a state university's ethics committee. The participants were invited to participate through a combination of in-person contact and online distribution. Although all the data were collected by way of an online survey, the researcher visited relevant classes to introduce the study and encourage participation. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. Before beginning the questionnaire, all participants were presented with an informed consent form describing the purpose of the research, confidentiality procedures, and their right to withdraw at any time. The online survey system required participants to actively select the “I voluntarily agree to participate” option before proceeding, and the survey could not be accessed without providing this consent. The study was approved by the Social and Humanities Research and Publication Ethics Board of the university where the author works. The procedures used in this study adhere to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The measures

Sense of belonging scale

The scale was originally developed by Hoffman et al. [37] to measure students' sense of belonging in a university setting and was validated and reliability-tested in Turkish by Tuhanioğlu and Gizir [72]. Although the Sense of Belongingness Scale has a multidimensional structure, the total score was used in the present study to represent students’ overall perceived level of university belonging. This approach is consistent with prior research conceptualizing belonging as a global relational experience and is supported by evidence indicating adequate reliability for the total score in both the original and Turkish adaptation studies. In addition, using the total score helped preserve model parsimony in the serial mediation analysis. The scale is a 5-point Likert-type scale, with responses ranging from ‘Completely Untrue’ (1), to ‘Completely True’ (5). A sample item from the scale is: "I feel that a faculty member really tried to understand my problem when I talked about it.” The Alpha coefficient for this study was 0.85, indicating an acceptable level of internal consistency. The Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) results indicated satisfactory construct validity (χ2 = 799.06, df = 286, RMSEA = 0.06, NFI = 0.96, IFI = 0.97, CFI = 0.97, SRMR = 0.06). The fact that the scale does not show a correlation of 0.80 or above with other scales used in the study is considered as evidence for discriminant validity; see Table 1.

The Connor Davidson resilience scale (short form)

The scale was developed by Connor and Davidson in 2003. Campbell-Sills and Stein [17] then conducted a study to assess the psychometric properties of the short form of the scale. Kaya and Odaci [42] later adapted this scale for use in Turkish. The scale is a 5-point Likert-type scale, with responses ranging from ‘Not True at All’ (1) to ‘True Nearly all the Time’ (5). A sample item from the scale is: 'I am able to adapt to changes’. The abbreviated version comprises ten items and measures resilience in a single dimension. The Alpha coefficient for this study was 0.86, indicating an acceptable level of internal consistency. The CFA results indicated satisfactory construct validity (χ2 = 146.58, df = 33, RMSEA = 0.08, NFI = 0.97, IFI = 0.98, CFI = 0.98, SRMR = 0.04). The fact that the scale does not show a correlation of 0.80 or above with other scales used in the study is considered evidence for discriminant validity. See Table 1.

The general self-efficacy scale

The scale, which was developed by Schwarzer and Jerusalem [64] to assess general self-efficacy, was validated and tested for reliability in Turkish by Aypay [9]. The scale was originally conceived as a unidimensional measure of individuals’ confidence in their ability to cope with challenging situations. However, the Turkish adaptation by Aypay [9] also exhibited a two-factor structure (1. Effort and 2. Ability and Confidence) while enabling researchers to compute a total score. In the present study, the total scale score was utilized in all subsequent analyses. The scale is a 4-point Likert-type scale, with responses ranging from ‘Completely False’ (1) to ‘Completely True’ (4). A sample item from the scale is: ‘I can always manage to solve difficult problems if I try hard enough’. The Alpha coefficient for this study was 0.91, indicating an acceptable level of internal consistency. The CFA results indicate satisfactory construct validity (χ2 = 139.41, df = 32, RMSEA = 0.08, NFI = 0.98, IFI = 0.98, CFI = 0.98, SRMR = 0.04). The fact that the scale does not show a correlation of 0.80 or above with other scales used in the study is considered as evidence for discriminant validity; see Table 1.

The life-satisfaction scale

Diener et al. [24] developed the scale to measure individuals' satisfaction with life. Yetim [81] conducted a validity and reliability study of the scale in Turkish. This unidimensional scale comprises only five items. The scale is a 7-point Likert-type scale, with responses ranging from ‘Strongly Disagree’ (1) to ‘Strongly Agree’ (7). A sample item from the scale is: ‘I am satisfied with my life’. The Alpha coefficient for this study was 0.86, indicating an acceptable level of internal consistency. The CFA results indicate satisfactory construct validity (χ2 = 4.11, df = 4, RMSEA = 0.01, SRMR = 0.01, NFI = 1.00, IFI = 1.00, CFI = 1.00). The fact that the scale does not show a correlation of 0.80 or above with other scales used in the study is considered as evidence for discriminant validity; see Table 1.

Personal ınformation form

This form was developed to collect basic demographic information from participants and was administered at the beginning of the online survey. It included questions about participants’ gender, age, year of study (for example, first-year, second-year and so on), and academic department. Gender and year of study were indicated using categorical response options, while age was entered as a continuous variable. The academic department was selected from a predefined list. No personally identifying information was collected, and all responses remained anonymous.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to examine the relationships among study variables. Serial mediation analyses were conducted using Hayes’ [32] PROCESS macro (Model 6) in SPSS 25.2 to test the theoretically specified sequential indirect effects among sense of school belonging, resilience, self-efficacy, and life satisfaction. Age, gender, and class level were included as covariates in the analyses to control for potential demographic differences in students’ belonging experiences and well-being indicators. Indirect effects were tested using a bias-corrected bootstrap procedure with 5,000 resamples and 95% confidence intervals. Indirect effects were considered statistically significant when the confidence interval did not include zero [32]. Given the relatively high correlation between resilience and self-efficacy, additional diagnostics were conducted to examine discriminant validity. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values ranged between 1.047 and 2.292, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. In addition, heterotrait–monotrait (HTMT) ratios were examined, and all HTMT values remained below recommended thresholds for discriminant validity (HTMT < 0.85 conservative; HTMT < 0.90 acceptable; [35]. The highest observed HTMT value was between resilience and self-efficacy (0.835), supporting the empirical distinguishability of the study constructs within the mediation model.

The findings

Preliminary analyses

Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations are presented in Table 1.

Table 1 shows the mean scores, standard deviations, and correlations among the study variables. All the variables are positively correlated with each other. In particular, resilience and self-efficacy are highly correlated (r = 0.74, p < 0.01). The correlation between SSB and life satisfaction is relatively low (r = 0.28, p < 0.01), but still statistically significant, suggesting a weaker yet meaningful association. These findings confirm the first hypotheses of the present study. Moreover, the observed positive correlations among the variables provide a suitable basis for conducting mediation analyses to examine the potential indirect effects between a sense of school belonging, resilience, self-efficacy, and life satisfaction.

Serial multiple mediation analyses

To assess common method bias in the established model, Harman's single-factor test was employed. The items from the research scales were combined into a single factor without rotation. According to Fuller et al. [26], common method bias is indicated if the unrotated solution accounts for more than 50% of the variance across all the measured items. Our analysis shows that a single factor does not account for the majority of the variance, with the maximum component explaining 27.09% of the total variance. These results suggest the absence of common method bias in the examined model. The following sections detail the outcomes of the mediation analyses. Based on correlation analysis, a serial mediation model (PROCESS Macro Model 6) was estimated to examine whether resilience and self-efficacy sequentially mediated the association between sense of school belonging (SSB) and life satisfaction.

Figure 1 presents the estimated serial mediation model, illustrating the hypothesized indirect associations between SSB and life satisfaction through resilience and self-efficacy. In this model, SSB was positively associated with resilience (β = 0.375, p < 0.001), resilience was positively associated with self-efficacy (β = 0.700, p < 0.001), and both resilience (β = 0.238, p < 0.001) and self-efficacy (β = 0.183, p < 0.01) were positively associated with life satisfaction. The association between SSB and life satisfaction remained significant after accounting for the mediators (β = 0.125, p = 0.004). Overall, the model explains 23.7% of the variance in life satisfaction (R2 = 0.237). As shown in Table 2, the total effect of SSB on life satisfaction was statistically significant (β = 0.281, B = 0.121, 95% CI [0.085, 0.157]). This total effect was decomposed into a significant direct effect (β = 0.125, B = 0.054, 95% CI [0.017, 0.091]) and a significant total indirect effect (β = 0.156, B = 0.067, 95% CI [0.046, 0.090]), indicating that approximately 55% of the total effect was transmitted through the mediators. All three specific indirect pathways were significant based on 5,000 bootstrap samples: via resilience alone (β = 0.089, B = 0.038, 95% CI [0.018, 0.061]), via self-efficacy alone (β = 0.019, B = 0.008, 95% CI [0.002, 0.017]), and via the serial pathway through both resilience and self-efficacy (β = 0.048, B = 0.021, 95% CI [0.008, 0.035]). The largest share of the indirect effect was carried through resilience, underscoring its primary mediating role in the model.

Testing gender differences in the serial mediation model

Given the gender imbalance in the sample (approximately 70% female participants), a moderated serial mediation analysis was conducted using PROCESS Model 92 to examine whether gender moderated the structural pathways in the proposed model. The results revealed that none of the interaction terms reached statistical significance across the three outcome equations. In the equation predicting resilience (M₁), the interaction between school belonging and gender was non-significant (ΔR2 = 0.000, F(1, 503) = 0.024, p = 0.877). Similarly, in the equation predicting self-efficacy (M₂), neither the school belonging × gender interaction (ΔR2 = 0.001, F(1, 501) = 0.676, p = 0.411) nor the resilience × gender interaction (ΔR2 = 0.000, F(1, 501) = 0.011, p = 0.917) were significant. In the equation predicting life satisfaction (Y), the interactions of school belonging × gender (ΔR2 = 0.000, F(1, 499) = 0.087, p = 0.769), resilience × gender (ΔR2 = 0.001, F(1, 499) = 0.809, p = 0.369), and self-efficacy × gender (ΔR2 = 0.003, F(1, 499) = 1.952, p = 0.163) were all non-significant. Furthermore, the indices of moderated mediation were not statistically significant, as all bootstrap confidence intervals included zero. These findings indicate that gender did not moderate either the direct or indirect effects in the proposed serial mediation model.

Discussion

Empirical evidence consistently shows a connection between a sense of school belonging (SSB) and life satisfaction [7, 21, 38]. This association appears particularly important during emerging adulthood, a developmental stage marked by increased autonomy, identity exploration, and vulnerability to stress [6]. University students with a weaker SSB are three times more likely to report lower life satisfaction [52], highlighting the need to understand the psychological mechanisms that mediate this relationship. This study contributes to the literature by examining how two key adaptive capacities, resilience and self-efficacy, operate as serial mediators between SSB and life satisfaction. While previous research has often examined these resources independently, our findings underscore their combined and sequential influence in shaping well-being among university students.

The study reveals that resilience plays a mediating role in the link between an SSB and life satisfaction. This finding aligns with previous research emphasizing resilience as a key factor in overcoming difficulties and adjusting to challenges [51, 73]. Resilience, characterized as the capacity to recover from setbacks while maintaining well-being [27], is reinforced by a strong sense of connection to a supportive community. Social support networks, particularly relationships with caring adults, play a crucial role in fostering resilience by offering both emotional and practical assistance during challenging periods [49, 54]. Among university students, a strong SSB can serve as a social and emotional buffer, strengthening their ability to navigate academic and personal obstacles with confidence and perseverance. Ultimately, this is associated with higher levels of life satisfaction [22]. The study’s findings suggest that enhancing students’ SSB may be considered a valuable strategy for promoting resilience and, in turn, improving their overall well-being.

Similarly, the study demonstrates that self-efficacy mediates the link between SSB and life-satisfaction. The findings are consistent with the literature indicating that students who feel a strong SSB are more likely to experience enhanced self-efficacy [2, 8, 66]. This increased self-efficacy is attributed to the supportive and inclusive environment fostered by a strong SSB, which helps students feel more competent and effective in their academic and personal lives. Higher self-efficacy beliefs empower students to approach challenges with a problem-solving mindset, reducing anxiety and increasing their ability to cope with stress, which in turn contributes to greater life-satisfaction [10, 60].

The study finds that resilience and self-efficacy serially mediate the relationship between school belonging (SSB) and life satisfaction, underscoring these psychological resources as critical in translating the positive effects of belonging into broader well-being outcomes. Resilience, positioned as the first mediator in the model, plays a fundamental role given its conceptualization as a trait [19], a process [46], and an outcome [49]. This multifaceted nature may allow resilience to support students in navigating adversity and potentially foster the development of further psychological strengths, such as self-efficacy; as a trait, it can provide a stable foundation of confidence; as a process, it may foster self-belief through coping experiences; and as an outcome, it might reinforce personal competence after overcoming challenges. The current findings suggest that students with a strong sense of belonging are more likely to feel emotionally and socially secure, which may facilitate the development of adaptive coping mechanisms (resilience) and subsequently more confident beliefs in their own capabilities (self-efficacy). This sequence of mediation highlights the value of cultivating an inclusive and supportive academic environment that may contribute to students’ capacity to adapt and their sense of agency, both of which are associated with life satisfaction [40].

The present findings also carry theoretical implications for models of belonging and psychological resource development. While the Self-System Model of Motivational Development [67] posits that relatedness needs, when fulfilled, progressively foster competence-related beliefs, our findings provide empirical support for this sequence in a university context: SSB predicted resilience, which in turn predicted self-efficacy, ultimately contributing to life satisfaction. This extends the model's applicability beyond K-12 educational settings to emerging adulthood and higher education contexts. At the same time, our findings extend existing accounts of the belonging–well-being relationship by suggesting that, although belonging is directly associated with life satisfaction, this association may also operate through sequential adaptive mechanisms. This indicates that belonging is linked to well-being not only directly but also indirectly through psychological resources such as resilience and self-efficacy.

It is also important to interpret the findings in light of their practical significance. The model explains 23.7% of the variance in life satisfaction, which can be considered a moderate level of explained variance in psychological research, given the multifactorial determinants of life satisfaction. Rather than indicating a comprehensive explanation, these findings suggest that SSB, resilience, and self-efficacy represent important but partial contributors to students’ life satisfaction. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as providing meaningful but limited explanatory insight, and future research may benefit from incorporating additional individual and contextual predictors.

Conclusions, limitations, and recommendations

To conclude, this research sheds light on the complex interplay between SSB, psychological resources, and life-satisfaction among university students in Turkey. Consistent with the existing literature, the findings provide empirical evidence for a positive relationship between a strong SSB and increased life-satisfaction. Furthermore, this study enhances our comprehension by demonstrating the mediating roles of resilience and self-efficacy in this relationship. In a wider context, these findings are significant not only for Turkey but also for other countries with similar educational structures and student profiles, where students report below-average levels of SSB and life satisfaction [52]. The serial mediation model proposed in this paper illustrates how belonging experiences may contribute to a positive cycle of adaptive psychological resources and overall well-being among university students.

While this study provides important insight into the literature, it has certain limitations. The study was conducted with students from Turkey, a country with a below-average level of school belonging and life satisfaction [52]. As outlined in the introduction, Turkish university students face a uniquely demanding environment characterized by an intensely competitive admissions process, high academic pressure, and significant financial strain — factors that may depress baseline levels of life satisfaction independently of belonging-related experiences. In this context, economic and social pressures may constitute structural barriers that attenuate the strength of the SSB → life satisfaction pathway, suggesting that belonging-based interventions may need to be complemented by material and institutional support to be fully effective. Future research should examine how country-level socioeconomic factors moderate the proposed pathways, particularly in contexts characterized by systemic educational inequality.

Additionally, the use of convenience sampling limits the generalizability of the findings. The present sample consisted of students enrolled in education faculties, which may further limit generalizability across different academic disciplines, as disciplinary contexts may shape students’ experiences of belonging and well-being. Conducting intercultural comparison studies in countries with both similar and different school belonging and life-satisfaction characteristics would further enhance the study. Future research should also examine whether the observed relationships generalize across different academic disciplines and student populations. The cross-sectional nature of the data in this study makes it difficult to establish cause-and-effect relationships. To gain deeper insight into these relationships, it is advisable for future research to employ experimental and longitudinal methods, as they allow for a more nuanced understanding of the dynamics at play over time. Furthermore, this study did not include several potentially important demographic factors such as students’ residential status (e.g., living on campus vs. off-campus), number of years spent at school, regional background, ethnic/immigrant status, or socioeconomic status. These variables could meaningfully shape students' experiences of school belonging and may interact with the psychosocial processes examined in the model. Additionally, excluding additional contextual covariates other than age, gender, and class may limit internal validity and the ability to rule out alternative explanations for the observed associations. Future research would benefit from including such diversity-related variables to better understand the contextual and structural influences on students' SSB.

In addition, the present study relied exclusively on self-report measures collected at a single time point, which may introduce common method variance and shared method bias. Although Harman’s single-factor test was conducted as an initial diagnostic, it has well-known limitations and cannot fully rule out common method effects. More rigorous approaches, such as marker variables or CFA-based common latent factor techniques, were not employed due to the analytical approach based on observed variables within the PROCESS macro framework and the absence of a latent measurement model. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as the observed associations may, to some extent, be influenced by shared method variance.

The findings of this research highlight the potential role of initiatives designed to enhance the sense of belonging within educational environments. Although the cross-sectional design of this study limits causal inferences, the results suggest that SSB is associated with well-being-related outcomes. Previous intervention research indicates that SSB can be enhanced through targeted programs (e.g., [77], which may provide useful context for interpreting the present findings. However, implications for policy should be interpreted with caution. Rather than providing direct evidence for policy implementation, the current results may offer preliminary insights for higher education institutions seeking to support students’ psychological well-being. For example, institutions could consider fostering more inclusive environments and strengthening social and psychological support systems. These implications remain tentative, and further longitudinal and intervention-based research is needed before drawing firm conclusions regarding institutional or policy-level applications.

Taken together, these findings suggest that higher education institutions may benefit from attending to both the academic and psychological dimensions of student experience. Fostering inclusive and supportive campus environments could represent a meaningful, though not sufficient, step toward enhancing students' adaptive resources and overall well-being. These implications remain tentative given the cross-sectional nature of the present study, and further longitudinal and intervention-based research is needed to clarify the conditions under which belonging-focused initiatives may most effectively support student resilience, self-efficacy, and life satisfaction.
