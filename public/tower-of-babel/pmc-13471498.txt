Exploring the impact of antenatal stress on maternal-fetal attachment in high-risk pregnancies: an explanatory sequential mixed methods approach
Authors: Seçil Topaloğlu, Aysegul Simsek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe negative effects of perceived stress during pregnancy on mother-infant attachment and fetal health are well known. However, studies that comprehensively investigate this relationship using both quantitative and qualitative data, particularly in high-risk pregnancies, are limited. The aim of the study is to investigate the effects of perceived stress on the antenatal and fetal health in high-risk pregnancies.Materials and methodsThe study is designed as a sequential explanatory mixed-methods approach consisting of quantitative and qualitative stages. Pregnant women were selected from those who visited the hospital’s maternity clinic using convenience sampling. The study was conducted between January 2024 and December 2024. In the first stage, quantitative data were collected using a descriptive information form, the Fetal Attachment Inventory (FAI), the Antenatal Perceived Stress Inventory (APSI), and a semi-structured interview form. In the second stage, open-ended questions were asked to investigate the emotional state of all pregnant women. The data obtained were analyzed using the descriptive phenomenological method. In quantitative analysis, responses were analyzed directly, whereas in qualitative analysis, inferences and interpretations were drawn from them.ResultsThe study was completed with 81 high-risk pregnant women, with an average age of 28.9. The total APSI score was 13.4, and the FAI score was 82.2. A significant negative correlation was found between the inventories (r=-.965; p<.001), indicating a strong association between perceived stress and antenatal attachment, which should be interpreted with caution. Five main themes were identified in the analysis of the interviews: coping with stress, partner support, duration of partner support, sharing feelings and emotions about pregnancy with the partner, and postpartum fear. While physical or psychological support is often emphasized in partner support, the frequency of conversation is reported to be associated with emotional sharing. In general, mothers with low stress levels and high partner support had healthier pregnancy.ConclusionThe findings indicate that perceived stress during pregnancy is associated with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes, while partner support plays a protective role. Although the association between stress and attachment was strong, it should be interpreted cautiously and within the context of a single-center sample of primigravida women in late pregnancy. Strengthening psychosocial support during pregnancy remains essential.Keywords: Attachment, Fetal health, Stress, Antenatal health, Mixed methods approach

Introduction

Pregnancy is one of the most critical periods in a woman’s life, during which she undergoes significant physical and emotional changes [1]. During this process, women begin to adjust to both pregnancy and their future role as mothers. As pregnancy progresses and fetal movements are perceived, maternal–fetal attachment gradually develops, which plays a key role in preparing the woman for motherhood and supporting the growth and development of the baby [2]. This attachment process is initiated during the prenatal period and continues after birth, facilitating maternal role adaptation and promoting healthy biopsychosocial development in the infant [3]. Many factors influencing this adaptation and attachment emerge during pregnancy, and the ability to cope with these factors in a healthy way is essential for both maternal and fetal well-being. The pregnant woman’s health status is one of the primary factors directly affecting pregnancy outcomes and indirectly influencing fetal attachment [4]. Conditions such as advanced maternal age, genetic predispositions, or complications related to previous pregnancies may render the pregnancy high-risk [5, 6]. Pregnancies that shift from a physiological to a pathological process are defined as high-risk pregnancies and are associated with increased risks for both the mother and the fetus [7]. In addition to physical complications, high-risk pregnancies often involve significant psychosocial stressors, which may disrupt the attachment process [8]. Therefore, care in high-risk pregnancies should address psychosocial factors alongside medical risks. The use of stress management strategies—such as relaxation techniques, breathing exercises, physical activity, yoga, massage therapy, social support, and strengthening spirituality—has been shown to be beneficial in this context [9–12]. Accordingly, fetal attachment constitutes a central component of care planning in high-risk pregnancies, with important implications for maternal and fetal health [13].

Identifying stress-related challenges to fetal attachment during pregnancy is crucial, as antenatal attachment continues after birth in the form of mother-infant attachment [14–16]. Early disturbances in this process may have lasting effects on both maternal adaptation and child development. Based on this framework, the present study aimed to determine the effects of antenatal perceived stress and attachment levels on maternal and fetal health in high-risk pregnancies. In addition, the study sought to explore the stress-coping methods used by expectant mothers facing high-risk pregnancy conditions.

Materials and methods

Study design

The research is an explanatory mixed method. The reason for adopting the explanatory mixed method in the study is the assumption that the results will provide a general understanding of the problem and that qualitative data will enrich and illuminate statistical data through in-depth exploration of the participants’ views and, as a result, increase the validity of quantitative data (cross-sectional and exploratory) by interpreting their meaning [17, 18].

The explanatory sequential design consists of two phases, quantitative and qualitative. In the first stage, quantitative data were collected to reveal the relationship between perceived stress and antenatal attachment. This allowed for the identification of general trends regarding stress and attachment levels among pregnant women. In the second stage, semi-structured in-depth interviews with the same pregnant women were conducted to qualitatively examine coping methods, partner support, and attachment experiences. These qualitative findings were used to explain the quantitative results and to ensure a comprehensive interpretation of the data. This sequential structure allowed the qualitative phase to build directly on the quantitative findings, thereby strengthening interpretive integration. The main rationale for choosing a mixed-methods approach was to combine the generalizability of quantitative data with the in-depth context provided by qualitative data to yield stronger answers to the research questions and enhance the validity of the findings.

These phases were conducted between January 2024 and December 2024 with women who applied to the hospital’s pregnant follow-up outpatient clinic. The study was reported in accordance with the EQUATOR checklist Mixed Methods Article Reporting Standards.

Participants

In the calculation of the study’s population and sample, the last 6 months of patient data of the hospital where the study will be conducted were used. Accordingly, the universe consisted of high-risk pregnant women who had been admitted to the hospital in the last six months. In the sample calculation, the sample size calculated with G*Power at 95% reliability, 80% power, and p<.05 was 68 people. Given the possibility of case losses, all high-risk pregnant women who agreed to participate and met the inclusion criteria during the specified time period were included. Convenience sampling was used to include pregnant women in the study. The inclusion criteria were primigravida, being between 36 and 39 weeks of pregnancy according to the last menstrual period, having a risky pregnancy, and not having any psychiatric disease. Exclusion criteria included a language barrier (not speaking the same language), a diagnosis of cognitive impairment, and incomplete answers to the study questions. Both quantitative and qualitative questions were administered to all pregnant women. There were no case losses during the study. The study was completed with 81 high-risk pregnant women.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using a descriptive information form, the Fetal Attachment Inventory (FAI), the Antenatal Perceived Stress Inventory (APSI), and a semi-structured interview form.

Descriptive information form

It was created by the researchers. The questions ask about the respondent’s characteristics (age, educational status, etc.), characteristics related to their husband and marriage (age, educational status, income, length of marriage, etc.), and characteristics related to pregnancy (number of pregnancies, week of pregnancy, health status, etc.). The form consists of 16 questions, including five open-ended and 11 closed-ended questions.

Fetal Attachment Inventory (FAI)

Developed by Muller in 1993 and adapted into Turkish by Duyan and colleagues in 2013 [19]. The inventory is used to measure the level of attachment of pregnant women to their fetuses. The inventory contains 21 questions that measure the pregnant woman’s attachment to the fetus. The questions are on a four-point Likert-type scale (1 = Almost never, 2 = Sometimes, 3 = Most of the time, and 4 = Almost always). There are no reverse items in the inventory. The total score ranges from 21 to 84. The higher the score, the higher the antenatal attachment is said to be. The Cronbach Alpha coefficient of the inventory was reported as 0.90 [19]. In our study, it was calculated as 0.86.

Antenatal Perceived Stress Inventory (APSI)

The inventory was developed in 2013 and adapted to Turkish in 2018 [20, 21]. The inventory aims to assess antenatal stress by identifying stressors among pregnant women. The inventory is a five-point Likert-type. It consists of 12 items and 3 subscales: medical and obstetric risks/fetal health (four items), psychosocial changes during pregnancy (four items), and expectations about childbirth (two items). Items 2 and 6 of the inventories were not included in any factor in the original inventory. The inventory is applied to pregnant women at 36 weeks or more gestation. The APSI score is obtained by summing the inventory item and dividing by the number of items. The lowest score in the inventory is 1, and the highest is 5. The higher the total inventory score, the higher the pregrant woman’s perceived stress level. The Cronbach Alpha coefficient of the inventory was reported as 0.70.20 In our study, it was calculated as 0.79.

Semi-structured interview form

This form consists of open-ended questions designed to understand the pregnancy of those diagnosed with high-risk pregnancies, pregnancy-related stress, and thoughts about their partner and baby. The form was created after a literature review and search of relevant sources. The interview form consists of eight main questions. The answers to the form are open-ended. The Semi-Structured Interview Form was developed and revised based on the opinions of three experts (a midwife academician, a pediatric nursing academician, and a perinatologist).

Data collection

The necessary hospital and ethics committee approvals were obtained prior to the study. The researchers collected data from pregnant women who attended the hospital’s pregnancy clinic for routine medical checkups. Pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria, gave informed consent, and agreed to have their responses recorded were taken to an interview room at the clinic. The interview took place in a closed room. The room had ventilation and contained two chairs and a table. Only the researcher and the pregnant woman entered the interview room. The introductory information form and the inventory questions were administered to the pregnant women through face-to-face interviews. Then, questions on the semi-structured interview form were asked. The answers were recorded on a voice recorder. The questionnaires were completed in 15–20 min on avarage, while the interview lasted approximately 45–50 min.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed in two stages. In the first stage, quantitative data were analyzed. Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS v25) was used to analyze the data. In the first stage, the mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values of continuous data, and the numbers and percentages of categorical data were calculated. Because the variables follow a normal distribution, the Independent Sample T-Test and One-Way ANOVA were used to compare them. Pearson’s test was used for the relationship between variables. Significance was accepted as p<.05 at 95% confidence interval.

In the second stage, the information obtained from the interviews was analyzed using a phenomenological analytical approach. Each interview was transcribed verbatim and read repeatedly to achieve immersion in the data. Initial notes and reflections were recorded in the margins to capture emerging meanings. Short quotations summarizing sections of the text were highlighted in color. Open coding was conducted independently by the primary researcher, followed by grouping codes into categories and overarching themes.

Data saturation was determined when no new codes or themes emerged from consecutive interviews and when existing categories became conceptually stable. Saturation was assessed through continuous comparison across transcripts rather than by a predetermined sample size. Thematic analysis was finalized once thematic redundancy was observed across participants. Qualitative data management and coding were supported using MAXQDA qualitative data analysis software.

To enhance analytical rigor, methodological triangulation was achieved by integrating qualitative findings with quantitative results within the explanatory sequential design. In addition, investigator triangulation was partially addressed through expert review of the interview guide and emerging themes. Formal member checking was not conducted; however, credibility was supported through prolonged engagement with the data, transparent documentation of analytic decisions, and the use of direct participant quotations in the findings.

Bias and control

This study found a higher-than-expected correlation between antenatal perceived stress and fetal attachment. This finding may be related to factors such as sample homogeneity, measurement tool similarity, and common method bias. Selecting participants from the same center may have increased the correlation coefficient by reducing the variance between variables. Furthermore, the self-report nature of both scales carries the potential for measurement overlap due to emotional state or social desirability effects. In the qualitative phase, reflexive notes were maintained throughout the analysis to minimize researcher bias and enhance analytical transparency. To reduce these potential biases, the data collection process was standardized, the interviewer effect was limited, and statistical controls were applied prior to analysis. Supporting these findings with multicenter, and longitudinal studies involving larger, more heterogeneous samples will more accurately reveal the direction and strength of this relationship.

Ethical aspects of the study

Hospital and inventory use permissions were obtained before the study. The relevant ethics committee approval was obtained (date:19.01.2024, number:23–297). Written and verbal informed consents were obtained before data collection. The Helsinki Declaration of Human Rights was complied with in the study.

During and after the interviews, participants’ identity information was kept confidential and anonymized. In the qualitative interview, each pregnant woman was assigned a number before recording, in accordance with the principle of confidentiality, and the audio recording began accordingly. No identifying information was used throughout the recording; all interviews, recordings, and analyses were conducted using the assigned number. The audio recordings were used solely for research purposes and were not shared with unauthorized persons. All data was stored in a secure environment and protected in accordance with ethical guidelines.

Such pregnancies can cause significant levels of stress, anxiety, and emotional distress in the expectant mother. Theme 3: duration of partner support

In high-risk pregnancies, the partner’s active involvement and continuous support help the mother cope with stress, maintain positive treatment progress, and psychological well-being. The duration and continuity of support may accelerate the mother’s physical recovery and ensure secure attachment in the postpartum period. As in the literature, in our study, it was observed that the complications seen because of effective and regular support from the partner decreased, and effective support contributed to the recovery of expectant mothers:

My husband always makes sure not to neglect me. Even when he is at work, he video calls me almost every time he is free to check if I am okay, and it makes me feel his support. Feeling his presence at every moment makes me stronger (Participant 66).

My husband always makes sure not to neglect me. Even when he is at work, he video calls me almost every time he is free to check if I am okay, and it makes me feel his support. Feeling his presence at every moment makes me stronger (Participant 66).

Whenever I feel sad and stressed, he spares all his time for me. We spend almost the whole day together. My stress and anxiety disappear (Participant 7).

Whenever I feel sad and stressed, he spares all his time for me. We spend almost the whole day together. My stress and anxiety disappear (Participant 7).

In high-risk pregnancies, partner support should be continuous throughout the pregnancy, not just for a few weeks or at specific moments. Discussion

In this study, the relationship between antenatal perceived stress during pregnancy and fetal attachment was examined. The findings revealed that perceived stress during pregnancy showed a strong relationship with antenatal attachment. This finding is consistent with the results of another study emphasizing the negative effects of stress during pregnancy on both physical and psychological health [22].

There was a significant and negative correlation between the APSI and the FAI, indicating a very strong association between antenatal stress and fetal attachment. Rather than implying causality, this finding suggests that stress and attachment are closely interconnected within a complex psychosocial context. Similarly, findings have shown that maternal psychological well-being plays a critical role in early attachment processes [14]. Increased antenatal perceived stress may reduce positive maternal emotions toward the fetus, thereby weakening attachment. From the perspective of attachment theory, high stress may diminish maternal sensitivity and emotional responsiveness, potentially delaying secure attachment [9, 13]. However, such an exceptionally high correlation coefficient should be interpreted with caution. Methodological factors, including sample homogeneity, recruitment from a single center, reliance on self-report measures, and the simultaneous assessment of emotionally related constructs, may have contributed to the strength of the observed association. The generalizability of these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the study was conducted in a single clinical center and included only primigravida women between 36 and 39 weeks of gestation with high-risk pregnancies. These sample characteristics may limit the applicability of the results to women at earlier gestational stages, multiparous women, or those in different clinical or cultural contexts, and may also have contributed to the strength of the observed associations. Accordingly, the present findings emphasize relational patterns rather than causal pathways. Similar studies conducted in Turkey also reported inverse associations between antenatal stress and attachment while underscoring the need for longitudinal research designs [23–26]. These findings indicated that temporal sequencing and causality can be more accurately examined through longitudinal studies [27]. Importantly, the qualitative findings of the present study support the quantitative results by illustrating how stress, emotional burden, and relational factors interact during high-risk pregnancies, reinforcing the contextual validity of the observed association.

Factors influencing perceived stress and fetal attachment may originate from both individual and environmental sources. This variability is also reflected in the psychometric properties of measurement tools. Our findings show that the reliability coefficients of the APSI subscales were lower than those of the total score, suggesting that the subscales capture distinct but less stable aspects of stress. These results are consistent with previous validation studies of APSI and other pregnancy-related stress scales [20, 21, 28, 29]. This highlights the importance of interpreting both total and subscale scores cautiously and supports further psychometric evaluation in diverse samples.

The effects of antenatal and postnatal attachment on infant health and development have been widely documented. Strong mother-infant attachment is associated with positive cognitive, emotional, and social outcomes in later life [30, 31]. Children who develop secure attachment tend to exhibit better emotional regulation, stress management, and academic performance [9, 13]. In this context, the present findings extend existing literature by demonstrating that stress-related disruptions in attachment during pregnancy may have implications beyond the antenatal period.

Qualitative findings revealed that expectant mothers employed various strategies -such as yoga, breathing exercises, spirituality, and social support- to cope with stress during high-risk pregnancies. Across interviews, partner support emerged as a central protective factor, contributing to reduced stress and enhanced emotional security [32]. Consistent with World Health Organization (WHO) reports, insufficient partner support was associated with increased emotional burden and vulnerability to postpartum depression [33]. Rather than repeating its effects, partner support can be understood as a stabilizing psychosocial resource that moderates the relationship between stress and attachment [34]. This integrated interpretation aligns with quantitative findings showing lower stress and higher attachment levels among women reporting stronger partner support and more favorable socioeconomic conditions [14].

Postpartum fears were another prominent qualitative theme, reflecting concerns about infant care, maternal competence, health outcomes, and financial stability. These findings are consistent with evidence indicating that high levels of antenatal stress increase vulnerability to postpartum mental health problems [33]. Early identification of stress and timely psychosocial interventions during pregnancy are therefore critical for preventing adverse maternal and infant outcomes.

High levels of perceived stress during pregnancy have also been linked to adverse fetal outcomes, including prematurity, low birth weight, and impaired neurodevelopment [15, 35–38]. These findings underscore the need to integrate stress assessment and management into routine antenatal care, alongside obstetric risk monitoring. Interventions such as psychosocial support programs, relaxation techniques, and stress management training may benefit both maternal well-being and fetal health.

Finally, advances in digital health technologies offer new opportunities to monitor and address antenatal stress. Mobile health applications, tele-monitoring systems, and digital support programs may facilitate early detection and intervention, particularly in high-risk pregnancies [39]. From this perspective, the findings of the present study contribute not only to clinical practice but also to broader discussions on integrating psychosocial care into emerging health technologies.

Limitations and strengths of the study

The study is limited by its single-center design and the inclusion of primigravida women in late pregnancy (36–39 weeks), which may limit generalizability. Future research should include women at different gestational stages and with varying parity levels to capture a broader spectrum of maternal experiences. However, the use of a mixed-methods approach integrating quantitative and qualitative data represents a key strength, providing a comprehensive understanding of antenatal stress and attachment. Comparing and integrating findings from the research design enhances the validity and reliability of the results. Furthermore, it is important that it offers a multidimensional perspective on the research questions, enabling an understanding of both quantitative information and participant experiences.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study found that perceived stress in high-risk pregnancies has a strong and negative effect on the fetal attachment context. Quantitative findings showed that attachment decreased as antenatal stress levels increased; qualitative findings revealed that coping methods, partner support, and concerns about the postpartum period were particularly decisive factors behind this relationship. The results emphasize that not only obstetric risks, but also psychosocial factors must be considered during pregnancy. The presence of partner support strengthens the pregnant woman’s mental well-being and supports a healthy attachment process. Therefore, strengthening psychosocial support mechanisms during high-risk pregnancies will significantly reduce stress and protect maternal and infant health. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as the study was conducted in a single center and included only primigravida women in the late stages of high-risk pregnancy, which may limit the generalizability of the results.

Based on these findings, it is recommended that pregnant women receive support in stress management. In addition, healthcare professionals should identify stress levels early in pregnancy and offer individualized interventions to pregnant women. Education programs to increase spousal support, expand free psychological counseling services, and implement social support programs for pregnant women should be promoted. Stress management training should also be promoted. Furthermore, at the policy level, it is recommended that perceived stress in pregnancy be included in routine screening programs, that psychosocial support components be integrated into national maternal and child health strategies, and that family-centered approaches be promoted. Our findings suggest various directions for future research. Large-scale, multicenter studies should be supported to better understand the long-term effects. Longitudinal studies based on attachment theory should examine the long-term developmental consequences of perceived stress during pregnancy. Future research should aim to replicate these findings using multicenter designs with more diverse samples, including women at different gestational stages and with varying parity. Longitudinal studies are particularly needed to clarify the temporal and potentially causal relationships between perceived stress, partner support, and antenatal attachment across pregnancy and the postpartum period. It is also recommended that studies be conducted on the effects of stress, partner support, and coping strategies on fetal attachment in different socioeconomic groups.

However, such an exceptionally high correlation coefficient should be interpreted with caution. Several methodological factors discussed in the “Bias and Control” section, including sample homogeneity, recruitment from a single center, reliance on self-report measures, and the simultaneous assessment of emotionally related constructs, may have contributed to the strength of the observed association. In addition, common method bias and reduced variability among participants may have inflated the correlation coefficients observed in this study. To minimize these potential biases, the data collection process was standardized, interviewer effects were reduced, and reflexive notes were maintained throughout the qualitative analysis to enhance analytical transparency. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting relational rather than causal patterns and should be confirmed through multicenter and longitudinal studies involving larger and more heterogeneous samples.
