Abstract
The computational power of autonomous mobile robots under the Look–Compute–Move (LCM) model has been widely studied through an extensive hierarchy of robot models defined by the presence of memory, communication, and synchrony assumptions. While the general n n -robot landscape has been largely established, the exact structure for two robots has remained unresolved. This paper presents the first complete characterization of computational power for two autonomous robots across all major models— 𝒪 ​ ℬ ​ ℒ ​ 𝒪 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{OBLOT} , ℱ ​ 𝒮 ​ 𝒯 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{FST\!A} , ℱ ​ 𝒞 ​ 𝒪 ​ ℳ \mathcal{FCOM} , and ℒ ​ 𝒰 ​ ℳ ​ ℐ \mathcal{LUMI} —under the full spectrum of schedulers ( Fsynch , Rsynch , Ssynch , Asynch , and their atomic variants). Our results reveal a landscape that fundamentally differs from the general case. Most notably, we prove that ℱ ​ 𝒮 ​ 𝒯 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{FST\!A} and ℒ ​ 𝒰 ​ ℳ ​ ℐ \mathcal{LUMI} coincide under full synchrony, a surprising collapse indicating that perfect synchrony can substitute both memory and communication when only two robots exist. We also show that both the color complexity and the computation time of the simulator proving the equivalence can be substantially improved. Moreover, We show that ℱ ​ 𝒮 ​ 𝒯 ​ 𝒜 \mathcal{FST\!A} and ℱ ​ 𝒞 ​ 𝒪 ​ ℳ \mathcal{FCOM} are orthogonal: there exists a problem solvable in the weakest communication model but impossible even in the strongest finite-state model, completing the bidirectional incomparability. This yields the first complete and exact computational landscape for two robots, highlighting the intrinsic challenges of coordination at the minimal scale.

Background and Motivation

The computational power of autonomous mobile robots operating through

The weakest standard model,

To understand the individual roles of memory and communication, two submodels of

Preliminaries

2.1
Robots

We consider a set
R_{n}=\{r_{0},\ldots,r_{n-1}\}
of
n>1
mobile computational entities, called
robots
, which move and operate in the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where they are viewed as points. In this paper, we consider the case that
n=2
and the two robots are denoted
a
and
b
.
We simply write
R
instead of
R_{2}
.The robots are
autonomous
(i.e., they operate without central control or external supervision),

identical
(i.e., they are indistinguishable by appearance and do not have unique identifiers), and

homogeneous
(i.e., they execute the same program).
Throughout this paper, we denote the position of a robot
r\in R
at time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by
r(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Each robot is equipped with a local coordinate system (in which it is always at its origin), and it is able
to observe the positions of the other robots in its local coordinate system.
The robots are
variable disoriented
(i.e., that is, there might not be consistency between the coordinate systems of different robots at the same time, or of the same robot at different times
3
3

3

The disorientation is said to be
self-consistency
if it is assumed that each local coordinate system always remains the same.
).
The robots however have
chirality
; that is, they agree on the same circular orientation of the plane (e.g., “clockwise” direction).

A robot is endowed with motorial and computational capabilities. When active, a robot performs a
{\mathit{Look}}
-
\mathit{Compute}
-
{\mathit{Move}}{}
(
\mathit{LCM}
) cycle:

1.

{\mathit{Look}}
: The robot obtains an instantaneous snapshot of the positions occupied by the robots, expressed in its local coordinate system. It cannot detect whether multiple robots are at the same location, nor how many (i.e., there is no
multiplicity detection
).

2.

\mathit{Compute}
: The robot executes its built-in deterministic algorithm, identical for all robots, using the snapshot as input. The result of the computation is a destination point.

3.

{\mathit{Move}}
: The robot moves continuously in a straight line until it reaches the computed destination (i.e., movements are
rigid
).
4
4

4

Movements are said to be
non-rigid
if they may be interrupted by the adversary.

If the destination is the current location, the robot stays still.

In the standard model,
\mathcal{OBLOT}
, the robots are also
silent
:
they have no means of direct
communication of information to other robots;
furthermore, they are
oblivious
: at the start of a cycle, a robot has no
memory of observations and computations performed in previous cycles.

In the other common model,
{\mathcal{LUMI}}
,
each robot
r
is equipped with a persistent register
\textit{Light}[r]
, called a
light
,
whose value—referred to as its
color
—is drawn from a constant-sized set
C
and is visible to all robots.
The color can be set by
r
at the
\mathit{Compute}
operation, and it is not automatically reset at the end of a cycle.
In
{\mathcal{LUMI}}
, the
{\mathit{Look}}
operation returns a colored snapshot; that is, it returns the set of distinct pairs
(\textit{position},\textit{color})

representing the states of the other robots at that time.
Note that if
|C|=1
, this case corresponds to the
\mathcal{OBLOT}
model.

Two submodels of
\mathcal{LUMI}
have been defined and investigated,
\mathcal{FST\!A}
and
\mathcal{FCOM}
, each offering only one
of its two capabilities, persistent memory and
direct means of communication, respectively.
In
\mathcal{FST\!A}
, a robot can only see the color of its own lights; thus, the color merely encodes an internal state. Therefore, robots are
silent
, as in
\mathcal{OBLOT}
, but they are
finite-state
.
In
\mathcal{FCOM}
, a robot can only see the colors of the lights of the other robots;
thus, it can communicate the color of its own lights to others,
but it cannot remember its own state (i.e., its own
k
colors).
Hence, robots are enabled with
finite-communication
but are
oblivious
.

2.2
Schedulers, Events

In this subsection, we define several schedulers—
Fsynch
,
Rsynch
,
Ssynch
,
Asynch
,
and several subclasses of
Asynch
—in terms of the activation schedule of the robots and the duration of their
\mathit{LCM}
cycles.
These schedulers impose different constraints on the adversary, as we shall see,
but they all share a common constraint called
fairness
:
every robot must perform the
{\mathit{Look}}
,
\mathit{Compute}
, and
{\mathit{Move}}
operations infinitely often.

The time it takes to complete a cycle is assumed to be finite and the operations
{\mathit{Look}}

and
\mathit{Compute}
are assumed to be instantaneous.

We assume that each of the
{\mathit{Look}}
,
\mathit{Compute}
, and
{\mathit{Move}}
operations completes in finite time.
Moreover, the
{\mathit{Look}}
and
\mathit{Compute}
operations are assumed to be instantaneous.
In the literature, the
\mathit{Compute}
operation has also been modeled as having some nonzero duration,
at the end of which a robot changes its color (in the
\mathcal{LUMI}
and
\mathcal{FCOM}
models).
However, we can assume without loss of generality that the
\mathit{Compute}
operation is instantaneous:
since the robot’s color always changes exactly at the end of the
\mathit{Compute}
operation,
its duration has no effect on the subsequent execution.

For any robot
r\in R
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, let

t_{L}(r,i)
,
t_{C}(r,i)
,
t_{B}(r,i)
, and
t_{E}(r,i)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

denote the times at which
r
performs its
i
-th Look, Compute, Move-begin, and Move-end operations, respectively.
These satisfy:

t_{L}(r,i)<t_{C}(r,i)<t_{B}(r,i)<t_{E}(r,i)<t_{L}(r,i+1)
.
If
r
decides to move from
p_{B}
to
p_{E}
at
t_{C}(r,i)
, it moves continuously along
[p_{B},p_{E}]
during
[t_{B}(r,i),t_{E}(r,i)]
, with
r(t_{B}(r,i))=p_{B}
,
r(t_{E}(r,i))=p_{E}
, and variable speed: for any
t_{1}<t_{2}
in
[t_{B}(r,i),t_{E}(r,i)]
,

|p_{B}-r(t_{1})|<|p_{E}-r(t_{2})|
if
p_{B}\neq p_{E}
.

In the
\mathcal{LUMI}
and
\mathcal{FCOM}
models, if
r
changes its light from
c_{1}
to
c_{2}
at time
t
and another robot
s
performs Compute at
t
, then
s
observes
c_{2}
.

In the the
synchronous
setting (
Ssynch
), also called
semi-synchronous
and first studied in
[
23
]
, time is divided into discrete
intervals, called
rounds
; in each round, a non-empty set of robots is activated and they simultaneously perform
a single
{\mathit{Look}}
-
\mathit{Compute}
-
{\mathit{Move}}
cycle in perfect synchronization.
The particular synchronous setting, where every robot is activated in every round
is called
fully-synchronous
(
Fsynch
).

We define
Rsynch
as the semi-synchronous scheduler in which,
after an optional finite prefix of fully synchronous rounds,
the remaining rounds activate non-empty subsets of robots
with the constraint that any two consecutive subsets are disjoint. In the case of
n=2
, the phase is a (prefix of) schedule in round-robin (
RR
) where
a
and
b
appear alternately.

In the
asynchronous
setting (
Asynch
), first studied in
[
12
]
, we do not have any assumption—except for the fairness mentioned before—on the timing of each
{\mathit{Look}}
,
\mathit{Compute}
, and
{\mathit{Move}}
operation and the duration of each
{\mathit{Move}}
operation.

In the rest of this subsection, we introduce subclasses of
Asynch
,
classified according to the level of atomicity of the
{\mathit{Look}}
,
\mathit{Compute}
, and
{\mathit{Move}}
operations.

•

{\mathit{LC}}
-
atomic
-
Asynch
:
Under this scheduler, each robot performs its consecutive
{\mathit{Look}}
and
\mathit{Compute}
operations atomically;
that is, no robot obtains a snapshot during the interval between the
{\mathit{Look}}
and
\mathit{Compute}
operations of any other robot
[
11
,
21
]
.
Formally, this scheduler satisfies the following condition:

\forall r,s\in R,\ \forall i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:\ t_{L}(r,i)\notin(t_{L}(s,j),t_{C}(s,j)].

Note that the interval
(t_{L}(s,j),t_{C}(s,j)]
is left-open; hence, this scheduler may allow two or more robots to obtain snapshots simultaneously.

•

M
-atomic-
Asynch
:
Under this scheduler, each robot performs its
{\mathit{Move}}
operation atomically; that is, no robot obtains a snapshot while any other robot is performing its
{\mathit{Move}}
operation
[
11
,
21
]
.
Formally, this scheduler satisfies the following condition:

\forall r,s\in R,\ \forall i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:\ t_{L}(r,i)\notin[t_{B}(s,j),t_{E}(s,j)].

•

{\mathit{CM}}
-
atomic
-
Asynch
:
Under this scheduler, each robot performs its consecutive
\mathit{Compute}
and
{\mathit{Move}}
operations atomically;
that is, no robot obtains a snapshot during the interval between the
\mathit{Compute}
and
{\mathit{Move}}
operations of any other robot.
Formally, this scheduler satisfies the following condition:

\forall r,s\in R,\ \forall i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:\ t_{L}(r,i)\notin[t_{C}(s,j),t_{E}(s,j)].

In the
\mathcal{FST\!A}

\mathcal{OBLOT}

In contrast, in the

-

-

•

\mathit{LCM}
-
atomic
-
Asynch
:
Under this scheduler, each robot performs its consecutive
{\mathit{Look}}
,
\mathit{Compute}
, and
{\mathit{Move}}
operations atomically; that is, no robot obtains a snapshot during the interval between the
{\mathit{Look}}
,

-

In the following, for simplicity of notation, we shall use the symbols

{F}
,
{R}
,

,

, and

,

,

{\mathit{CM}}
-

Throughout the paper, we assume variable disorientation, chirality and rigidity, unless explicitly stated otherwise.

We define an

2.3
Problems and Computational Relationships

Let
{\cal M}=\{\mathcal{LUMI},\mathcal{FCOM},\mathcal{FST\!A},\mathcal{OBLOT}\}
be the set of models under investigation and

{\cal S}=\{F,R,S,A,A_{LC},A_{CM}\}
be the set of schedulers
under consideration.

A
configuration

\mathcal{C}(t)
of
2
robots at time
t
is a function
\gamma:\mathcal{R}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times C
that specifies the location and color of each robot at time
t
.
Each location is given with respect to a fixed global coordinate system (which is unknown to the robots).
Recall that the color set
C
is a singleton (i.e.,
|C|=1
) in the
\mathcal{OBLOT}
model; in this case, the colors carry no information.
A configuration that specifies only the locations (resp. colors) of the robotsis called a
geometric (resp. color) configuration
; it is defined as a function
\gamma_{1}:\mathcal{R}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
(resp.
\gamma_{2}:\mathcal{R}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\to C
).
In the following, for robot
r
, we denote
\gamma_{1}(r,t)
and
\gamma_{2}(r,t)
simply as
r(t)
and
\mathrm{color}_{r}(t)
, respectively.

In the following, to formally define the problem, we introduce predicates that describe the robots’ actions—whether they are stationary or moving—at time
t
;

(1)
Stop
and
Moving
:

\texttt{Stop}_{r}(t)\;:\;\exists\varepsilon>0\;\forall\tau\in(t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon):\ r(\tau)=r(t).\quad

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we completely characterize the computational power of robot models for two robots under the assumptions of variable disorientation (VD), chirality, and rigidity.
To address the parts that could not be separated in previous work, we introduce two new problems,
DMSD
and
RDAH
, which exploit the specific properties of two-robot systems and achieve the same separation results as in the general multi-robot case.
We also show that, under
Fsynch
, the three models other than
\mathcal{OBLOT}
are equivalent. This result provides the first direct-construction proof of an equivalence that had previously been established only via simulation, giving a more constructive and conceptually transparent understanding of the relation between the models.

A remaining open question is whether the same results hold when the above assumptions are relaxed.
In particular, since chirality is unnecessary except for the
SRO
algorithm, it is an interesting problem to determine whether the same hierarchy can be obtained under only VD and rigidity.

Lemma 0

.

No nontrivial deterministic algorithm solves

in
\mathcal{FST\!A}^{A}
or
\mathcal{OBLOT}^{A}
.

Proof.

Let
D=0:=|b(0)-a(0)|
. Assume the algorithm commands a nonzero move when a robot looks at
(a(0),b(0))
. W.l.o.g. robot
a
is activated first and, by similarity invariance in a two-point scene, chooses a destination on the line
\overline{a(0)b(0)}
of the form

A^{\prime}\;=\;(1-\lambda)a(0)+\lambda b(0)\qquad(\lambda\in(0,1]).

Robot
a
starts moving
rigid

A^{\prime}
. At an adversarially chosen fraction
x\in(0,1)
of
a
’s progress, robot
b
is activated and sees

A_{x}\;=\;a(0)+x(A^{\prime}-a(0))\;=\;a(0)+x\lambda v.

b
computes a destination on
\overline{a(0)b(0)}
with the same coefficient
\lambda
applied to the seen configuration:

B^{\prime}\;=\;(1-\lambda)b(0)+\lambda A_{x}.

The adversary adjusts speeds so that the two arrivals occur simultaneously (or simply considers the later arrival time); that time is a “simultaneous stop”. The distance at that time is

\lVert A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}\rVert\;=\;\bigl\lVert\bigl((1-\lambda)-\lambda+\lambda^{2}x\bigr)v\bigr\rVert\;=\;\bigl|\,1-2\lambda+\lambda^{2}x\,\bigr|\,D_{0}.

For any fixed
\lambda\in(0,1]
, the map

(0,1)

\mathbb{Z}[1/2]
is countable and has empty interior, the adversary can pick

\frac{\lVert A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}\rVert}{D_{0}}\;=\;|1-2\lambda+\lambda^{2}x|\;\notin\;\mathbb{Z}[1/2],

violating
DMSD
at a simultaneous stop. The only way to avoid this is the degenerate “never move” policy, which we exclude as trivial.
