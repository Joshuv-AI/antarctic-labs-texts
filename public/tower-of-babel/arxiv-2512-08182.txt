Nonparametric inference with massive data via grouped empirical likelihood
Yongda Wang

Abstract




Abstract

To address the computational issue in empirical likelihood methods with massive data, this paper proposes a grouped empirical likelihood (GEL) method. It divides
N
observations into
n
groups, and assigns the same probability weight to all observations within the same group. GEL estimates the
n\ (\ll N)
weights by maximizing the empirical likelihood ratio. The dimensionality of the optimization problem is thus reduced from
N
to
n
, thereby lowering the computational complexity. We prove that GEL possesses the same first order asymptotic properties as the conventional empirical likelihood method under the estimating equation settings and the classical two-sample mean problem. A distributed GEL method is also proposed with several servers. Numerical simulations and real data analysis demonstrate that GEL can keep the same inferential accuracy as the conventional empirical likelihood method, and achieves substantial computational acceleration compared to the divide-and-conquer empirical likelihood method. We can analyze a billion data with GEL in tens of seconds on only one PC.

KEY WORDS:
distributed computation; estimating equation; nonparametric likelihood; two-sample mean problem.

Introduction

Empirical Likelihood (EL) was proposed by Owen
25
as an alternative to the bootstrap for constructing confidence regions in nonparametric problems. Building on Owen’s foundational work, Qin and Lawless
31
extended EL to general estimating equation frameworks. DiCiccio et al.
8
established that empirical likelihood methods are subject to Bartlett correction for confidence interval construction. EL has found broad applicability in quantile inference
4
;
45
, ROC curve analysis
7
;
30
;
42
;
21
, and Gini index estimation
33
;
28
. More recently, Schennach
34
pioneered the integration of exponential tilting techniques, Chen et al.
5
addressed the challenges of complex survey design with scrambled responses, and Thorne
36
as well as Xu and Chen
41
demonstrated EL’s utility in detecting differential gene expression. Research on two-sample problems using empirical likelihood has also revived. Qin
32
developed a semi-empirical likelihood approach for inferring the difference between two population means. Jing
11
subsequently demonstrated that this two-sample empirical likelihood method for mean differences is Bartlett-correctable. Now, the EL method has become a primary statistical tool for nonparametric inference. This prominence stems from its methodological advantages over conventional approaches, including: minimal parametric assumptions for data distributions; the capacity to construct data-driven confidence regions; and natural incorporation of auxiliary information.

Advances in technology have driven exponential growth in data collection and dataset sizes. Although computing resources are also increasing rapidly, they pale in comparison to the astonishing surge in data volume. It is widely recognized that in large-scale learning, due to the immense computational demands, estimation and inference are the two primary challenges. The standard EL method assigns an individual probability weight to each data point, and estimates them via an optimization problem of maximizing the empirical likelihood ratio, which does not have a closed-form. As a result, EL can be computationally intensive, especially when applied to large datasets. While the empirical likelihood method can be computationally demanding, especially with large datasets, potentially limiting its practical application, its fundamental importance for massive data analysis remains undeniable. This is largely due to its strong theoretical ties with traditional statistical models and its distinct strengths in statistical inference. The challenges posed by massive datasets are not unique to empirical likelihood, other cornerstone nonparametric inference methods also face significant computational hurdles. For instance, scalable methods for the bootstrap, such as the Bag of Little Bootstraps
13
, have been proposed. Consequently, overcoming these computational hurdles is crucial in the context of EL.

A variety of methods have been developed to address the challenges of big data, some of which also incorporate EL. For instance, subsampling-based methods
13
;
23
;
39
, divide-and-conquer approaches
43
;
6
;
18
, and sequential updating algorithms
35
are among the most prominent. In particular, Ma et al.
24
, Liu and Li
20
, and Zhou et al.
44
have proposed combining the divide-and-conquer strategy with EL to enhance scalability and efficiency in large-scale inference.

Note that, for
N
observations, there are
N
probability weights needed to estimate via optimization techniques in the standard EL method. We observe that, in large-sample settings, these probability weights all tend to be close to each other. It seems that we do not need so many parameters to fit the underlying distribution. In this paper, we propose a grouped empirical likelihood (GEL) method, which divides the
N
observations into
n
groups, and assigns the same probability weight to all observations within the same group. These
n
weights are also estimated by maximizing the empirical likelihood ratio. It can be seen that the dimensionality of the optimization problem from
N
to
n
, thereby lowering the computational burden. We prove that the GEL method possesses the same first order asymptotic properties as EL under the estimating equation settings and the classical two-sample mean problem.
A distributed GEL method is also proposed when more than one servers are available.

Numerical simulations demonstrate that, with easy grouping strategies, GEL can keep the same inferential accuracy as EL, and simultaneously achieves substantial computational acceleration compared to both EL and divide-and-conquer EL. For a billion data, We can run GEL in tens of seconds on only one PC. The distributed GEL method can further accelerate the operation. The proposed methods are also applied to real datasets of anthropometric
records and A/B tests.

2
Empirical likelihood

In this section we briefly review the empirical likelihood (EL) method. Some notation and definitions are needed. For two vectors
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})^{{}^{\prime}}
and
\bm{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{d})^{{}^{\prime}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the inequalities
\bm{a}<\bm{b}
and
\bm{a}\leq\bm{b}
are defined to hold if and only if
a_{j}<b_{j}
and
a_{j}\leq b_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,d
, respectively. For a
d
-dimensional random vector
\bm{X}
, let

F(\bm{x})=\Pr(\bm{X}\leq\bm{x})
denote its cumulative distribution function, where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We use
F(\bm{x}-)
to denote
\Pr(\bm{X}<\bm{x})
, and
\Pr(\bm{X}=\bm{x})=F(\bm{x})-F(\bm{x}-)
. Let
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N}
be independent random observations of
\bm{X}
. The empirical cumulative distribution function of
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N}
is defined as

F_{N}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\{\bm{X}_{i}\leq\bm{x}\}

for
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathbb{I}
represents the indicator function. The nonparametric likelihood of any probability measure
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as

L(F)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(F(\bm{X}_{i})-F(\bm{X}_{i}-)),

where
p_{i}=F(\bm{X}_{i})-F(\bm{X}_{i}-)\geq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,N
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=1
.
Define
\mathcal{F}
as the class of all
d
-dimensional cumulative distribution functions. It is known that

\sup\{L(F):F\in\mathcal{F}\}=L(F_{N})=N^{-N}.

(1)

The problem of interest is to make inference on a
p
-dimensional parameter
\bm{\theta}
defined as the unique solution to the following estimating functions

\mathbb{E}[\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta})]=\bm{0},

(2)

where
\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta})=(g_{1}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}),\ldots,g_{r}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}))^{{}^{\prime}}
and
r\geq p
. By (
1
), the EL ratio at
\bm{\theta}
is defined as

R(\bm{\theta})=\sup\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{N}Np_{i}\mid p_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=1,\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})=\bm{0}\right\}.

Using the method of Lagrange multipliers, the above maximization problem yields

p_{i}=\frac{1}{N\{1+\bm{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})\}},

(3)

where
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
is the solution to the following equations

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})}{1+\bm{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})}=\bm{0}.

(4)

By (
3
), the empirical log-likelihood ratio statistic for
\bm{\theta}
is given by

-2\log R(\bm{\theta})=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left\{1+\bm{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})\right\}.

(5)

The maximum EL estimate of
\bm{\theta}
, denoted by
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{EL}
, is defined as the minimizer of
-2\log R(\bm{\theta})
. Under regularity conditions, Qin and Lawless
31
showed that as
N\rightarrow\infty
,

\sqrt{N}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{EL}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{V}),

where
\bm{\theta}_{0}
denote the true value of
\bm{\theta}
and

\bm{V}=\left\{\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{0})}{\partial\bm{\bm{\theta}}^{{}^{\prime}}}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{0})\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{0})^{{}^{\prime}}\right)\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{0})}{\partial\bm{\theta}^{{}^{\prime}}}\right)\right\}^{-1}.

(6)

Moreover, the statistic for testing

H_{0}:\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}:\bm{\theta}\neq\bm{\theta}_{0}

(7)

is
-2\log R(\bm{\theta}_{0})
. If
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{0})\bm{g}^{{}^{\prime}}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{0})\right]
is positive definite and the rank of
\mathbb{E}[\partial\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{0})/\partial\bm{\theta}]
is
p
, then

-2\log R(\bm{\theta}_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\chi^{2}_{p}\quad\text{as }N\rightarrow\infty.

3
Grouped empirical likelihood

When
N
is sufficiently large, the classical EL method often incurs substantial computational costs due to the requirement of solving large-scale nonlinear equations in (
4
). To reduce the costs, we propose the grouped empirical likelihood (GEL) method that involves much fewer unknown parameters.

Under the same setting in the previous section, GEL randomly divides the
N
sample points
\{\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N}\}
into
n
disjointed groups
{\mathcal{G}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
. Let
d_{i}
represent the number of sample points in
{\mathcal{G}}_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
. We require that the sample sizes in each group should be as equal as possible, i.e., they satisfy

\max_{i,k\leq n,i\neq k}\left|d_{i}-d_{k}\right|=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad N=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}.

For simplicity, in the following we assume that the
N
observations

\{\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N}\}

can be evenly partitioned into
n
groups of size
m
. Rewrite them as
\{\bm{X}_{ij}\}_{i=1,\dots,n}^{j=1,\dots,m}
. The GEL method assigns the same probability measure
q_{i}
to all sample points within each group
\mathcal{G}_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,n
. Hence, there are only
n
, instead of
N
, unknown parameters in the GEL method.

For the parameter
\bm{\theta}
satisfying the estimating equations in (
2
), the GEL framework maximizes
\prod_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}^{m}
subject to restrictions

q_{i}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}=\frac{1}{m},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{ij},\bm{\theta})=\bm{0}.

(8)

Let

\mathcal{H}=m\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log q_{i}+t\left(\frac{1}{m}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\right)+\bm{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{ij},\bm{\theta})\right),

where
t
and
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r})^{{}^{\prime}}
are Lagrange multipliers. Taking derivatives with respect to
q_{i}
, we have

\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial q_{i}}=\frac{m}{q_{i}}-t+\bm{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{ij},\bm{\theta})=0,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial q_{i}}=m+t\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}+\bm{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{ij},\bm{\theta})=0.

We can get
t=-nm^{2}
and

q_{i}=\frac{1}{N\left\{1+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})\right\}},

(9)

where
\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{ij},\bm{\theta})/m
,
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}=-\bm{\lambda}/N
, and for given
\bm{\theta}
,
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}
satisfies

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})}{1+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})}=\bm{0}.

(10)

Since
0\leq q_{i}\leq 1/m
, we have
1+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})\geq 1/n
for each
i
. Let
D_{\bm{\theta}}=\{\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}:1+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})\geq 1/n\}
;, which is convex, closed, and bounded if
\bm{0}
is inside the convex hull of the
\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})

31
. Moreover,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})}{1+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})}\right\}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})^{{}^{\prime}}}{\left\{1+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})\right\}^{2}}

is negative definite for
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}\in D_{\bm{\theta}}
, provided that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})\bar{\bm{g}}^{{}^{\prime}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})
is positive definite. By the inverse function theorem,
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}=\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{\theta})
is thus a continuously differentiable function of
\bm{\theta}
.

The GEL function for
\bm{\theta}
is now defined as

L_{G}(\bm{\theta})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{1}{N\left\{1+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})\right\}}\right\}^{m}.

Since
\prod_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}^{m}
is maximized at
q_{i}=N^{-1}
without the constraints from the estimating equation (
2
), the empirical log-likelihood ratio is

-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\theta})=2m\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{1+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta})\right\}.

(11)

We can obtain an estimate
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{GEL}
of
\bm{\theta}
through minimizing
-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\theta})
. Consequently,
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}
can be estimated from (
10
), and the estimate
\hat{q}_{i}
of
q_{i}
follows from (
9
),
i=1\ldots,n
. When
r=p
,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{GEL}
is acturally the unconstrained maximum likelihood estimator, i.e.,
\hat{q}_{i}=N^{-1}
. Algorithm
1
summarizes the procedure for computing
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{GEL}
. Compared with the computation for
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{EL}
in the previous section, the proposed GEL method involves only
n\ll N
parameters, and this significantly reduces the computational complexity.

Two-sample GEL

Let
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be random vectors associated with parameters
\bm{\theta}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\bm{\theta}_{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, respectively. Each parameter is defined as the unique solution to
p
functionally independent unbiased estimating functions:

\mathbb{E}\{\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{x})\}=\bm{0},\quad\mathbb{E}\{\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{\theta}_{y})\}=\bm{0},

where
\bm{g}
is a vector-valued function
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Let
\bm{\pi}=\bm{\theta}_{y}-\bm{\theta}_{x}
be the parameter of interest.

Suppose that
\{\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N_{1}}\}
is an i.i.d. sample from
\bm{X}
and

\{\bm{Y}_{1},\dots,\bm{Y}_{N_{2}}\}
is an i.i.d. sample from
\bm{Y}
, with the two samples mutually independent. Assume that both
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are exact multiples of the block size
m
, i.e.,
N_{1}=m\,n_{1},N_{2}=m\,n_{2},
for some integers
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. We then partition the two samples as
\{\bm{X}_{ik}\}_{i=1,\dots,n_{1}}^{k=1,\dots,m}
and
\{\bm{Y}_{jt}\}_{j=1,\dots,n_{2}}^{t=1,\dots,m}
, respectively.

Define two probability vectors
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n_{1}})^{{}^{\prime}}
and
\bm{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n_{2}})^{{}^{\prime}}
satisfying
p_{i}\geq 0
,
q_{j}\geq 0
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}p_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}q_{j}=1/m
. For each
\bm{p}
, let
\bm{\theta}_{x}(\bm{p})
solve

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}p_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{ik},\bm{\theta}_{x}(\bm{p}))=\bm{0},

(12)

and similarly define
\bm{\theta}_{y}(\bm{q})
by

\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}q_{j}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{Y}_{jt},\bm{\theta}_{y}(\bm{q}))=\bm{0}.

(13)

The two-sample GEL for
\bm{\pi}
is defined as

L_{G}(\bm{\pi})=\sup\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{n_{1}}p_{i}^{m}\Bigr)\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{n_{2}}q_{j}^{m}\Bigr),

subject to the constraints in (
12
) and (
13
).
The corresponding grouped empirical log-likelihood ratio is

-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\pi})=-2m\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\log(N_{1}p_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\log(N_{2}q_{j})\Bigr\}.

Let
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
,
\tau_{1}=N/N_{1}
, and
\tau_{2}=N/N_{2}
. According to
22
, using the method of Lagrange multipliers, we have:

-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\pi})=2m\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\log\left\{1-\tau_{1}({\bm{\lambda}}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})\right\}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\log\left\{1+\tau_{2}({\bm{\lambda}}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})\right\}\right\},

where
\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{ik},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})/m
,

\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})=\sum_{t=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{Y}_{jt},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})/m
, and
(\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*},\bm{\lambda}^{*})
is the solution to the following nonlinear system:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\frac{\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x})}{1-\tau_{1}\bm{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x})}=\bm{0}\\
&\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\frac{\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y})}{1+\tau_{2}\bm{\lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y})}=\bm{0}.\\
&\bm{\pi}=\bm{\theta}_{y}-\bm{\theta}_{x}\end{aligned}\right.

(14)

outlines the steps for computing
-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\pi})
.

5
Distributed GEL

In the previous two sections, we have focused on the implementation of the GEL method on a single machine. However, in many practical scenarios involving massive datasets, data are often stored and processed in a distributed manner across multiple servers. In this section, we extend the GEL framework to such distributed settings.

Suppose the entire dataset of size
N
is distributed across
K
servers. Let
N_{k}
denote the sample size on the
k
-th server with
N=\sum_{k=1}^{K}N_{k}
. For each
k
, the GEL method partitions the data into
n_{k}
groups of size
m_{k}
, with equal weights assigned to observations within each group. Then, we apply the GEL method independently to the local data, obtaining a local parameter estimate
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{k}
and a local log-likelihood ratio statistic
-2\log R_{G,k}(\bm{\theta})
.
The distributed GEL (DGEL) estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{DGEL}
is then computed by averaging the local estimates,

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{DGEL}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{k}.

Similarly, for testing (
7
), the overall log-likelihood ratio statistic can be aggregated as

-2\log R_{DGEL}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}-2\log R_{G,k}(\bm{\theta}).

Under regularity conditions similar to those in Section 3, it can be shown that
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{DGEL}
is consistent and asymptotically normal, and
-2\log R_{DGEL}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
converges in distribution to a chi-square distribution. The DGEL strategy can also be used for the two-sample problem in Section
4
.

7.2
Evaluation of two-sample GEL on the Gross Merchandise Volume Dataset

The two-sample GEL method we proposed can be applied to controlled experiments within internet companies. Controlled experiments, commonly known as A/B tests, allow businesses to assess the impact of product changes by comparing key metrics (e.g., user engagement, revenue, conversion rates) between different treatment groups
15
;
14
;
16

Welch t-test (WT) is commonly used in traditional A/B tests
40
;
12
. However, when dealing with typical heavy-tailed big data commonly encountered in business contexts, such as Gross Merchandise Volume (GMV), we observed that the WT often fails to control Type I error in A/B tests platform. GMV is a key performance indicator (KPI) that measures the total sales volume of goods and services transacted through a company’s platform or marketplace
29
. However, given the current limitations of optimization algorithms, the traditional two-sample EL method often fails when applied to large-scale datasets. The two-sample GEL method we proposed can address the aforementioned challenges.

For two-sample GEL, we fixed the number of equal‐weight partitions at 1,000. With a significance level of 0.05, we conducted 1,000 times A/A tests using the WT method and the two-sample GEL method, producing 1,000 p-values. The WT method yielded a Type I error rate of 0.062, while two-sample GEL held it at 0.047. Although the average runtimes ( 0.1235 s and 0.1277 s) were comparable, two-sample GEL demonstrated superior control over the Type I error.

Conclusion

We have proposed the GEL method for nonparametric inference on massive datasets. It is shown that the proposed methods possess similar statistical properties to EL with much fewer parameters. where
\bm{B}=\bm{S}_{11}^{-1}\left(\bm{I}+\bm{S}_{12}\bm{S}_{21}^{*}\bm{S}_{21}\bm{S}_{11}^{-1}\right)
. Also under
H_{0}
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{G}_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{0})}\bm{G}_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{0})=\bm{0}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}_{0}=-\bm{S}_{11}^{-1}\bm{Q}_{1n}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{0})+o_{p}(1)

and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(1+\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{G}_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\right)=-\frac{n}{2}\bm{Q}_{1n}^{{}^{\prime}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{0})\bm{S}_{11}^{-1}\bm{Q}_{1n}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{0})+o_{p}(1).

Thus

\displaystyle\frac{-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\theta}_{0})}{m}

\displaystyle=n\bm{Q}_{1n}^{{}^{\prime}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{0})(\bm{B}-\bm{S}_{11}^{-1})\bm{Q}_{1n}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{0})+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=n\bm{Q}_{1n}^{{}^{\prime}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{0})\bm{S}_{11}^{-1}\bm{S}_{12}\bm{S}_{21}^{*}\bm{S}_{21}\bm{S}_{11}^{-1}\bm{Q}_{1n}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{0})+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=\left[(-\bm{S}_{11})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{n}\bm{Q}_{1n}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{0})\right]^{{}^{\prime}}\left[(-\bm{S}_{11})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{S}_{12}\bm{S}_{21}^{*}\bm{S}_{21}(-\bm{S}_{11})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\cdot\left[(-\bm{S}_{11})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{n}\bm{Q}_{1n}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{0})\right]+o_{p}(1).

Let
\bm{Z}:=(-\bm{S}_{11})^{-1/2}\sqrt{n}\bm{Q}_{1n}(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and
\bm{M}:=(-\bm{S}_{11})^{-1/2}\bm{S}_{12}\bm{S}_{21}^{*}\bm{S}_{21}(-\bm{S}_{11})^{-1/2}
. Since
\bm{M}
is symmetric and idempotent, with trace equal to
p
, the empirical likelihood ratio statistic
-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\theta}_{0})/m=\bm{Z}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{M}\bm{Z}+o_{p}(1)
converges to
\chi_{p}^{2}
.
∎

A.4
Proof of Theorem 3

Proof.

Define the sample moment functions

\bm{G}_{N}(\bm{\theta}_{x})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}),\quad\bm{G}_{M}(\bm{\theta}_{y})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bm{g}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}).

At the true parameter values
\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}}
and
\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}}
, by the law of large numbers (with Conditions 6 and 7 ensuring uniform integrability), we have

\bm{G}_{N}(\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}})=O_{p}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\quad\bm{G}_{M}(\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}})=O_{p}(M^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

By applying a first-order Taylor expansion of (
14
) around
\bm{\lambda}=\bm{0}
, and using the positive definiteness of the covariance matrices in Condition 5, we can get

\bm{\lambda}^{*}=O_{p}(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

Note that

-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\pi}_{0})=2m\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\log\left(1-\tau_{1}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})\right)+\sum_{i=j}^{n_{2}}\log\left(1-\tau_{2}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})\right)\right\},

expanding the log terms in
W_{E}(\bm{\pi}_{0})
using Taylor series yields

\displaystyle\log\left(1-\tau_{1}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=-\tau_{1}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})-\frac{1}{2}\left(\tau_{1}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})\right)^{2}+r_{1i}

\displaystyle\log\left(1+\tau_{2}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=\tau_{2}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})-\frac{1}{2}\left(\tau_{2}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}({Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})\right)^{2}+r_{2j},

where
|r_{1i}|\leq C_{1}\left|\tau_{1}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})\right|^{3}
and

|r_{2j}|\leq C_{2}\left|\tau_{2}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})\right|^{3}
. Therefore

\displaystyle-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\pi}_{0})

\displaystyle=2m\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\left\{-\tau_{1}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})-\frac{\tau_{1}^{2}}{2}\left((\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})\right)^{2}\right\}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\left\{\tau_{2}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})-\frac{\tau_{2}^{2}}{2}\left((\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})\right)^{2}\right\}+r\right\},

where
r=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}r_{1i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}r_{2j}
. From (
14
), we have

\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\frac{\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})}{1+\tau_{2}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})-\tau_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{\lambda}^{*}+o_{p}(1)=\bm{0},

\Rightarrow\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})=\tau_{2}n_{2}\left\{\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\right\}\bm{\lambda}^{*}=n_{2}\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{2}\bm{\lambda}^{*},

where
\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{2}=\tau_{2}\operatorname{Var}(\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y},\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}}))
. Similarly, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})=-n_{1}\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}\bm{\lambda}^{*},

where
\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}=\tau_{1}\operatorname{Var}(\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}}))
. Let
\widetilde{\bm{V}}=\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}+\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{2}
. The linear term becomes

\displaystyle-2m\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\tau_{1}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})+2m\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\tau_{2}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})

\displaystyle=2N(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\widetilde{\bm{V}}\bm{\lambda}^{*}.

Combining the expressions, we can obtain

\displaystyle-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\pi}_{0})=

\displaystyle 2N(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\widetilde{\bm{V}}\bm{\lambda}^{*}-m(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\cdot\left\{\tau_{2}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}+\tau_{1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\right\}\bm{\lambda}^{*}+r.

By Condition 5, we have:

\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}})\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}})^{{}^{\prime}}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{m}\operatorname{Var}(\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}})),

\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}})\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}})^{{}^{\prime}}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{m}\operatorname{Var}(\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}})).

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle m\tau_{1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}=N\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}+o_{p}(1),

\displaystyle m\tau_{2}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}=N\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{2}+o_{p}(1).

Hence

\displaystyle-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\pi}_{0})

\displaystyle=2N(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\widetilde{\bm{V}}\bm{\lambda}^{*}-(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}(N\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}+N\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{2})\bm{\lambda}^{*}+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=N(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\widetilde{\bm{V}}\bm{\lambda}^{*}+o_{p}(1).

Define

\bar{\bm{G}}_{1}:=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}}),\quad\bar{\bm{G}}_{2}:=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}}).

Perform a Taylor expansion on the first equation in (
14
)

\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})+\left\{\tau_{1}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}-\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}\right\}\bm{\lambda}^{*}+\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}\bm{\lambda}^{*}+O_{p}(\frac{1}{n_{1}})=\bm{0}.

Then we can get

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}^{*}

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*})\right)+O_{p}(\frac{1}{n_{1}})

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}^{-1}\bar{\bm{G}}_{1}+O_{p}(\frac{1}{n_{1}}).

Similarly,

\bm{\lambda}^{*}=\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{2}^{-1}\bar{\bm{G}}_{2}+O_{p}(\frac{1}{n_{2}}).

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}+\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{2})\bm{\lambda}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\bar{\bm{G}}_{2}-\bar{\bm{G}}_{1})+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{N}\left(\tau_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}})-\tau_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\bar{\bm{g}}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}})\right)+o_{p}(1).

Define
\bm{V}_{1}:=\tau_{1}\operatorname{Var}(\bm{g}(\bm{X},\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}}))
,
\bm{V}_{2}:=\tau_{2}\operatorname{Var}(\bm{g}(\bm{Y},\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}}))
. Then we have
\bm{V}_{1}=m\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}
,
\bm{V}_{2}=m\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{2}
. Thus,
\bm{V}=\bm{V}_{1}+\bm{V}_{2}=m\widetilde{\bm{V}}
. Therefore

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{1}+\widetilde{\bm{V}}_{2})\bm{\lambda}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{N}\left(\tau_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{Y}_{jt},\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}})-\tau_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{ik},\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}})\right)+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left(\tau_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\bm{g}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}})-\tau_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}})\right)+o_{p}(1).

Define

\bm{D}_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\tau_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\bm{g}(\bm{Y}_{j},\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}})-\tau_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\bm{g}(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}})\right),

by the Central Limit Theorem (with Condition 8 ensuring the Lindeberg condition), we have

\bm{D}_{N}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}),

where
\bm{\Sigma}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\operatorname{Var}(\bm{D}_{N})=\bm{V}_{1}+\bm{V}_{2}=\bm{V}
. Then we can get

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}^{*}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\bm{V}}^{-1}\frac{\bm{D}_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=m\bm{V}^{-1}\frac{\bm{D}_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}+o_{p}(1).

Therefore

\displaystyle-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\pi}_{0})

\displaystyle=N(\bm{\lambda}^{*})^{{}^{\prime}}\widetilde{\bm{V}}\bm{\lambda}^{*}+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=N\left(m\bm{V}^{-1}\frac{\bm{D}_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\bm{V}}{m}\right)\left(m\bm{V}^{-1}\frac{\bm{D}_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=m\bm{D}_{N}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{D}_{N}+o_{p}(1).

Since
\bm{V}^{-1/2}\bm{D}_{N}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{p})
, it follows that:

\bm{D}_{N}^{{}^{\prime}}\bm{V}^{-1}\bm{D}_{N}=\|\bm{V}^{-1/2}\bm{D}_{N}\|^{2}\xrightarrow{d}\chi_{p}^{2}.

By Condition 4 and the fact that
\bm{\lambda}^{*}=O_{p}(N^{-1/2})
, we can get

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}|r_{1i}|+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}|r_{2j}|=O_{p}(N^{-1/2}).

From Conditions 6 and 7 together with the M-estimation theory, we have

\bm{\theta}_{y}^{*}-\bm{\theta}_{y_{0}}=O_{p}(N^{-1/2}),\quad\bm{\theta}_{x}^{*}-\bm{\theta}_{x_{0}}=O_{p}(N^{-1/2}).

Hence
r=o_{p}(1)
. Therefore

\frac{-2\log R_{G}(\bm{\pi}_{0})}{m}\xrightarrow{d}\chi_{p}^{2}.

∎
