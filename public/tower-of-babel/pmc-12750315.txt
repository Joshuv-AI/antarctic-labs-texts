Development of a real‐time multi‐scenario monitoring method for respiratory motion based on capacitive sensors
Authors: Yongqiang Wang, Bing Li, Xuhua Xiao, Zhaoyang Lou, Kangdong Liu, Hong Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to improve the accuracy of the tumor radiotherapy dose and reduce the irradiation dose to the surrounding organs, by developing a versatile respiratory motion monitoring system with multi‐scenario applications to enhance the clinical efficacy of radiotherapy for thoracic and abdominal tumors.MethodsThe respiratory motion monitoring system comprised an airbag structure integrated with a parallel‐plate capacitive sensor. The airbag comprised a polymer balloon encapsulated in a nonelastic flexible membrane, and had a projected area of 120 mm × 120 mm. The capacitive sensor adopts a concentric configuration of beryllium copper (thickness: 0.1 mm, diameter: 10 mm) and oxygen‐free copper (thickness: 1 mm, diameter: 10 mm) foils. Real‐time monitoring of the respiratory motion was achieved by detecting the capacitance variations corresponding to changes in the internal air pressure within the airbag. The system performance was rigorously evaluated using a dynamic thorax phantom capable of simulating various patterns, including sinusoidal and fourth‐power cosine waveforms.ResultsThe capacitive sensor‐based respiratory motion monitoring system demonstrated a displacement measurement range of up to 10 mm, capable of detecting movements as small as 0.1 mm (signal‐to‐noise ratio: 2.18). Real‐time displacement conversion was performed using the fitted model y = ax+bx 3 (a = 26.105 ± 0.398, b = 285.868 ± 22.147). The system exhibited a high stability, with a standard deviation of only 0.0011 in capacitance measurements over 10 repeated tests with a 5 mm amplitude sinusoidal waveform, and further maintained the amplitude consistency within 2.25%–96.05% of the maximum value throughout 10 min of continuous operation. A reliable performance was confirmed across various respiratory waveforms, including sinusoidal and fourth‐power cosine profiles. Furthermore, the non‐metallic airbag structure enhances the adaptability to multiple clinical scenarios. However, two types of signal distortions were observed, originating from the airbag deformation and the limitations of the capacitive sensor electrode, both of which are thoroughly explained in the manuscript.ConclusionOverall, this study developed a capacitive sensor and airbag‐based respiratory monitoring system that combines the advantages of abdominal pressure belts with infrared‐based monitoring technologies. This integrated approach offers a cost‐effective, structurally simple, and versatile solution for monitoring respiratory motion across multiple clinical scenarios.Keywords: capacitive sensor, gas pressure, motion detection, respiratory motion

INTRODUCTION

Prior studies have demonstrated that monitoring respiratory motion can effectively reduce radiation exposure to the normal tissues, thereby lowering the risk of short‐term toxicity during clinical radiotherapy for chest and abdominal tumors.[ 1 , 2 ] For example, eliminating respiratory motion has been found to substantially increase tumor control probability in liver cancer.[ 3 , 4 , 5 ] The use of an active breathing coordinator (ABC) can further restrain variations or reduce the mean heart dose and decrease the lung dose in patients with left breast cancer patients.[ 6 , 7 , 8 ] Additionally, respiratory‐gated conformal radiotherapy has been shown to reduce toxicities and increase prescribed doses in lung cancers.[ 9 , 10 , 11 ] It is widely acknowledged that stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) delivers highly concentrated radiation doses to tumors in a limited number of sessions, which necessitates precise target margins to protect the surrounding healthy tissues.[ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 ] Therefore, reducing these margins in clinical practice is crucial for sparing normal tissues, and requires accurate tumor localization. However, respiratory motion remains a primary factor causing displacement of thoracic and abdominal tumors,[ 1 , 2 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ] often compelling the use of larger margins to compensate for its effects during radiotherapy. Fortunately, real‐time detection and management of respiratory motion can reduce the required treatment field margins, thereby decreasing the incidence of radiotoxicity and other side effects.[ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ]

In recent decades, various respiratory motion management technologies have been developed to improve the efficacy of radiotherapy,[ 1 , 2 , 12 ] including infrared‐based monitoring systems,[ 27 ] optical surface monitoring systems,[ 28 ] ABC,[ 29 , 30 ] abdominal pressure plates,[ 12 ] and abdominal pressure belts,[ 31 ] among others.[ 32 , 33 ] Infrared‐based monitoring systems acquire signals via an infrared reflective module placed on the patient's body surface; however, variations in patient positioning and module placement may introduce measurement uncertainties. Optical surface monitoring systems use cameras to capture real‐time images of the patient's body surface to extract the respiratory motion signals; however, they cannot actively suppress respiratory motion. Abdominal pressure plates effectively suppress tumor and organ motion by applying pressure to the abdomen, whereas the ABC system enables breath‐holding radiotherapy by monitoring the lung air volume. However, both methods require sufficient respiratory function to sustain the treatment cycle. Consequently, there is a need to integrate the advantages of existing respiratory motion management technologies into a unified system that can adapt to evolving clinical demands. A recently developed airbag‐based respiratory motion monitoring system [ 34 ] demonstrated the potential to combine the benefits of abdominal pressure belts with real‐time monitoring capabilities. The airbag can suppress respiratory motion and convert residual motion into real‐time changes in the internal gas pressure. To achieve more precise detection of subtle pressure changes in the airbag and clarify the monitoring mechanism, a capacitive‐based sensor was designed to measure the airbag pressure for respiratory motion monitoring purposes and to provide guidelines for the safe and effective use of airbags in clinical settings.

Capacitive sensors have been widely applied in industrial, medical, and precise experimental measurements owing to their high sensitivity, fast response, high measurement resolution, and low cost. These sensors have further been employed for the noncontact monitoring of the respiratory motion of the human body.[ 35 ] However, the presence of metal components in capacitive sensors may interfere with the spatial distribution of X‐rays, rendering them unsuitable for direct use in radiotherapy. To address this limitation, a capacitive sensor was designed to measure airbag pressure indirectly through gas catheters. This approach ensures that the metal components remain at a safe distance from the human body and the center of the X‐ray beam, while still providing precise and reliable measurements.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we developed a parallel‐plate capacitive sensor to detect changes in the gas pressure inside an airbag through a gas duct with a specific diameter, forming the core of the respiratory motion monitoring system. To evaluate the performance of the system, a commercial dynamic thorax phantom was employed to generate various waveforms to simulate respiratory motion, and the designed system was applied to perform real‐time measurements of these simulated waveforms. The experimental results indicated that the respiratory motion monitoring system can detect motion displacements as small as 0.1 mm and track motion periods as short as 2 s in real time across various signal profiles. Furthermore, we validated the stability and operational range of the capacitive‐sensor‐based respiratory motion monitoring system. Comparative studies using a commercial respiratory motion management system and in vivo measurements of volunteer respiratory signals further confirmed the feasibility of this system. However, it has been observed that respiratory motion monitoring systems based on capacitive sensors and airbag structures can produce two types of distorted test signals. These distortions have the potential to yield incorrect test results, which can lead to errors in radiotherapy dosage and misleading clinical decisions. To mitigate these issues, a comprehensive theoretical analysis of the two types of distorted signals was conducted herein, providing insights into their origins and implications.

METHODS

Prototype design

The capacitive‐sensor‐based respiratory motion monitoring system comprises three components. The first component was a respiratory motion acquisition airbag placed on the patient's body surface, which converted the patient's respiratory motion into gas pressure fluctuations. This airbag, constructed from a polymer balloon wrapped in a nonelastic flexible material with a 120 mm × 120 mm projection on the horizontal plane, was strategically placed on a carbon fiber base. This component was then fastened to the patient's abdomen using a nonelastic abdominal girdle. The second component was a capacitive sensor that adopted a simple parallel capacitor structure with air as the dielectric medium between the plates. The upper plate of the capacitive sensor was made of oxygen‐free copper, with a thickness of 1 mm and a diameter of 10 mm. Similarly, the lower electrode plate was constructed from a 0.1 mm thick beryllium copper sheet. Both plates were concentrically aligned to ensure that the capacitive sensor maintained an effective cross‐sectional diameter of 10 mm. The third component is the latex gas duct, which has an internal diameter of 4 mm and a length of 900 mm, facilitating the transmission of pressure changes within the system.

Respiratory motion detection system validation

A dynamic thorax phantom, purchased from Sun Nuclear, was used to simulate a patient's respiratory motion in the ventral‐dorsal direction. The motion waveform consisted of a sine wave with amplitudes ranging from 5 mm down to 0.1 mm (5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.3, and 0.1 mm), with a consistent period of 4 s. To evaluate the respiratory motion monitoring system's response to shortness of breath, a sinusoidal waveform with an amplitude of 3 mm and a period of 2 s was simulated by the dynamic thorax phantom. Furthermore, the cosine of the power of four breathing waveforms with an amplitude of 6 mm and a period of 8 s was simulated to assess the resolution of the system across different respiratory curve types. Finally, a real‐time position management system (Model: INIZIO), purchased from Varian, was used to verify the feasibility of the designed test system.

The respiratory motion monitoring system developed in this study was used to monitor the dynamic motion of a thoracic phantom in real‐time. When the dynamic thorax phantom was positioned at the zero point, the respiratory detection system was pressurized using an air supply pump until the capacitance reached a baseline value of 4.25 pF. At this point, the valve was closed to prevent gas leakage. The background pressure, corresponding to a capacitance value of 4.25 pF ensured that the airbag had a sufficient expansion range, thereby preventing erroneous signals during the simulated expiratory movements when the airbag could no longer expand. The real‐time measured capacitance values were subtracted from the baseline value of 4.25 pF to calculate the relative changes in the capacitance values, which were directly correlated with the motion distance of the dynamic thorax phantom. Capacitance values were further measured using a Tonghui TH2810B digital bridge operating at a frequency of 10 kHz. Because all experiments in this study were performed on a commercial phantom and did not involve any patients or tissues, with no clinical validation experiments conducted, informed consent was not applicable.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

A schematic of the real‐time respiratory motion monitoring system is presented in Figure 1. The dashed red box shows a photograph of the self‐developed capacitive sensor. A fully plasticized three‐way conduit connects the capacitive sensor, airbag structure, and air supply pump. The airbag structure, indicated by the blue dashed box in Figure 1, was made entirely of plastic, ensuring that no metallic components were present on the patient's body surface. A valve was incorporated to isolate the air pump from the gas conduit to maintain the airtight integrity of the respiratory motion monitoring system during measurements. By using a gas duct to link the capacitive sensor and the airbag, the sensor was effectively isolated from the direct patient interface. This design prevents any metallic components from interfering with the radiotherapy dose distribution or computed tomography (CT) image quality. The airbag structure is affixed to the human body, as shown on the right side of Figure 1. The patient's respiratory motion drives a carbon fiber plate to move up and down, compressing the airbag and altering its volume, which, in turn, changes the internal gas pressure. These pressure variations are transmitted to the capacitive sensor via a plastic catheter, resulting in measurable changes in capacitance. Capacitance values were detected using a Tonghui TH2810B digital bridge, and data were acquired using a control system for real‐time respiratory motion monitoring. This system modulates the degree of respiratory motion restraint by adjusting the background pressure in the airbag, thereby partially incorporating benefits similar to those of an abdominal pressure belt.

Figure 2 presents the characteristic curve output of the dynamic thorax phantom, along with the test performance and data processing workflow of the respiratory motion monitoring system. The dynamic thorax phantom generated eight sinusoidal signals with different amplitudes and a fixed period of 4 s to simulate respiratory motion. The simulated motion distances in the ventral and dorsal directions are presented in Figure 2a. Figure 2b shows the corresponding capacitance variations resulting from the sinusoidal waveforms of different amplitudes. For example, a 5 mm amplitude motion produces a clearly measurable capacitance change value of 0.1531 pF, with a standard deviation of 0.0011, demonstrating the capability of the sensor to detect displacements of up to 10 mm. As the amplitude decreases, the system continues to reliably detect the signals, even at a motion amplitude of 0.1 mm (Figure 2c). Figure 2c presents the capacitance change and noise levels of the system. The noise level is measured at 0.0033 pF, whereas the capacitance change for the 0.1 mm sine wave is 0.0072 pF, yielding a signal‐to‐noise ratio of 2.18. This confirms the ability of the system to resolve motion distances as small as 0.1 mm.

To investigate the relationship between the motion amplitude and capacitance change, the maximum relative capacitance changes for each amplitude are extracted and plotted (Figure 2d; green dots). This reveals a nonlinear relationship, which is attributed to the circumferential fixation of the beryllium copper electrode‐this fixation induces nonlinear variations in capacitance with displacement. The data were consequently fitted with a cubic polynomial function y = ax+bx 3, where a = 26.105 ± 0.398 and b = 285.868 ± 22.147. The fitted curve (red line in the figure, R2 = 0.99963) allowed the accurate conversion of capacitance changes into physical displacement. The inset in Figure 2d compares the original 3 mm amplitude signal with the converted displacement, showing a peak‐to‐peak value (Vpp) of 0.227 and an RMS error of 0.077 in the worst case, thus confirming the system accuracy.

Figure 3a shows the capacitance variations for 3 mm amplitude sinusoidal motions with periods of 2 and 4 s. The system captured similar capacitance ranges for both periods, with the 2 s signal containing exactly two cycles within the same duration as one cycle of the 4 s signal. The system further accurately measured a power‐of‐cosine signal (4th power) with a maximum displacement of 6 mm and a period of 8 s (Figure 3b). After conversion using the equation y = ax+bx 3, the measured data (black curve) closely matched the phantom output (red curve), with minor deviations near the lowest point of motion, likely owing to system noise. These results confirm that the system maintains high accuracy for respiratory waveforms with periods greater than 2 s across varying waveform shapes.

To further evaluate the system's detection accuracy under accelerated respiratory rates, we subsequently tested the phantom using sinusoidal waveforms with identical amplitudes across 1 ‐ 4 s cycles (Figure 3c). All measured amplitudes were normalized to the maximum value recorded during the 4 s cycle. The results of this experiment demonstrated that the measured amplitude at the 1 s cycle reached 96.7% of that at the 4 s cycle. Given that the average respiratory rate is 14/min,[ 36 ] the temporal response characteristics of the device meet the practical application requirements. Additionally, the system continuously monitored a 4 s sinusoidal signal over 10 min. The normalized capacitance changes consistently remained between 2.25% (maximum at the trough) and 96.05% (minimum at the peak) of the full‐scale value, thereby ensuring precision throughout the radiotherapy session. Data from the first and last 30 s (Figure d) confirmed that the signal retained its sinusoidal characteristics without significant amplitude attenuation, although minor fluctuations were observed owing to the measurement noise.

To determine the specific operating range of the system, the discrepancies between the actual and measured distances obtained from the system were compared. Figure 4a presents the actual motion distance of the phantom and the measured distance converted from the capacitance values acquired using the respiratory motion monitoring system. The green curve in this figure represents the test data from the respiratory motion monitoring system, while the red curve indicates the actual motion distance of the phantom. The two curves show strong agreement within the capacitance variation range of ‐0.156 to 0.196 pF, corresponding to the critical points defined by a 5% deviation from the actual motion. Beyond this range, the test curve deviates significantly from the actual motion distance. Given that the initial calibration capacitance of our system was 4.25 pF, the effective operating range of the developed system was 4.094 ‐ 4.446 pF. Figure 4b illustrates the relationship between the internal pressure of the respiratory motion monitoring system and the capacitance of the capacitive sensor. When the capacitance value was 4.25 pF, the internal pressure of the system was 670 Pa, indicating that a pressure of approximately 670 Pa is applied to the patient's abdomen at this capacitance value.

Key performance characterization of the capacitive sensor‐based respiratory motion monitoring system. (a) The linear operating range of the respiratory motion monitoring system. (b) The correspondence between the capacitance value of the capacitive sensor and the internal pressure.

To validate the performance of this respiratory motion monitoring system, we subsequently conducted a comparative analysis using a commercially available respiratory motion monitoring system (Figure 5a), performing actual measurements of the respiratory motion from a volunteer's abdomen in a standing position (Figure 5b). In Figure 5a, the pink dotted line represents a sinusoidal motion signal (1 mm amplitude, 4 s period) of the phantom measured by the Varian respiratory motion monitoring device. The same signal was simultaneously detected using the designed system, and the results are displayed as blue curves in Figure 5a. A clear discrepancy was observed between the two systems at the peak and trough points of the phantom motion. However, aside from these limitations, our device produced results similar to those of a commercial system. Figure 5b shows the actual respiratory motion signals acquired from a volunteer under different pressure conditions. The measured respiratory signals were normalized relative to the maximum amplitude observed at 670 Pa. The amplitude of the abdominal respiratory motion decreased as the system pressure increased, as shown in Figure 5b, where the blue and red curves represent the respiratory signals at 670 and 770 Pa, respectively. Although the pressure applied by our system was lower than that typically employed in commercial abdominal pressure belts, it nevertheless enabled the real‐time monitoring of residual respiratory motion. This dual functionality, combining mild motion suppression with continuous monitoring, renders the device particularly suitable for patient intolerant to high pressure. Consequently, our approach extended the applicability of respiratory motion management to a broader patient population.

The capacitive sensor‐based respiratory motion monitoring system was further demonstrated to be capable of detecting respiratory motion in real time. However, during measurement, two types of signal distortion which can potentially lead to inaccuracies may occur (Figs. 6a and 6b). The first type of distortion (Figure 6a) was caused by the loss of elasticity in the airbag. When the initial airbag pressure was relatively high, the airbag was already at its maximum inflated volume, even in the free state. As the phantom moved upward, the gas pressure inside the system increased, leading to an increase in capacitance. When the phantom reached its highest position (indicated by the red arrow in Figure 6c), the capacitance change values reaches 0.21 pF. In contrast, when the phantom crossed the zero point and moved downward (blue arrow in Figure 6c), the maximum capacitance change relative to the zero point was 0.0437 pF (Figure 6a). At this stage, the initial airbag pressure caused the airbag to reach its maximum expansion limit. Even if the phantom continued to move downward, the airbag lost its ability to expand further, resulting in no change in the internal system pressure (as illustrated in the lower‐right panel of Figure 6c). This results in a distorted signal, as shown in Figure 6a.

Analysis of signal distortion mechanisms in the capacitive sensor‐based respiratory motion monitoring system. (a) Signal distortion occurring at maximum airbag expansion. (b) Signal distortion caused by limited metal electrode plate deformation, where the respiratory‐driven gas pressure becomes insufficient for further deformation. (c) Schematic diagram of the two distortion mechanisms.

The second type of distortion (Figure 6b) manifests as signal distortion owing to the loss of elasticity in the elastic electrode plate of the capacitive sensor. When the phantom moved upwards to its highest position, the capacitance change was 0.113 pF, whereas when it moved downward to its lowest position, it was 0.139 pF. This asymmetry in capacitance changes occurs because the airbag is compressed during upward movement, causing a continuous increase in the internal pressure. However, the elastic electrode plate of the capacitive sensor had already reached its maximum deformation limit, thus preventing any further changes in the capacitance (as shown in the upper right panel of Figure 6c). This results in a distortion of the test signal.

To ensure the accuracy of test signals during practical use, the following quality control measures must be implemented: Firstly, during operation, the airbag must not be over‐inflated beyond the baseline capacitance value of 4.25 pF in the free state. The airbag should be inflated until completely filled, while the internal pressure should adjusted using a restraint strap to ensure an initial deformation of 3 cm. Second, when adjusting the pressure inside the airbag, the initial capacitance value must fall within the range of 4.094‐4.446 pF, while the capacitance value should remain within this range throughout the testing process. In addition, regular checks of the airtightness of the system are essential. After use, the airbag must be inflated to a capacitance value of 4.25 pF in its free state for a pressure retention test. The capacitance should be monitored for 30 min to ensure that the change remained within 2%. Finally, periodic testing of the relationship between the capacitance change and actual compression distance should be conducted to ensure that the values of parameters a and b remain within acceptable error margins.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, in the present study, we describe the development of a cost‐effective and user‐friendly respiratory motion monitoring system based on capacitive sensing technology that effectively integrates the advantages of abdominal pressure belts and infrared‐based monitoring systems. This system not only suppresses respiratory motion through airbag pressure, but also enables real‐time respiratory motion detection. Further, it successfully isolates metallic components from the patient's body surface, thereby eliminating any potential interference with radiotherapy dose distribution or CT imaging quality. This enhancement significantly broadened the applicability of the system to diverse clinical scenarios.

Our prototype can detect respiratory motions as small as 0.1 mm, and can accurately measure signals from arbitrary waveforms. However, we identified two types of signal distortions that may occur in capacitive‐sensor‐based monitoring systems, the causes of which were thoroughly elucidated in this study. Furthermore, quality control guidelines for proper use are provided to ensure measurement accuracy and reproducibility during the operation. Future research efforts will focus on standardizing the airbag design and expanding the elastic deformation range of the electrodes, both of which are critical for enhancing the system performance. These improvements are expected to facilitate the translation of the respiratory monitoring technology into clinical practice. In summary, this study lays a robust foundation for our concept and sets the stage for advanced clinical trials to evaluate the clinical benefits of the capacitive sensor‐based respiratory motion detection technology.
