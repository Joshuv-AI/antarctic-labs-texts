Genetic diversity and structure of the endemic Tanacetum cinerariifolium as a consequence of multiple processes operating at different temporal scales
Authors: Martina Grdiša, Filip Varga, Zlatko Liber, Jerko Gunjača, Zlatko Šatović
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study on Dalmatian pyrethrum (Tanacetum cinerariifolium (Trevir.) Sch.Bip.), we combined genetic analysis and ecological niche modeling to investigate the intraspecific diversity, genetic structure, historical and contemporary distribution, and factors determining the observed diversification patterns of this endemic species from the eastern Adriatic coast. The study included 12 newly developed microsatellite markers that exhibited high polymorphism allowing the characterization of different populations. The level of genetic diversity (HE) ranged from 0.399 to 0.616, with an average value of 0.534. Genetic differentiation (FST) between populations was 0.178. Most of the genetic diversity was attributed to differences among individuals within populations, but highly significant φST values indicate the existence of genetic differentiation among populations. Bayesian analysis of population structure revealed the existence of two to four genetic clusters, and a correlation was found between environmental conditions and genetic clustering patterns, suggesting that local ecological conditions contributed to the emergence of distinct genetic patterns. The results of ecological niche modeling suggest that the Pleistocene climate has shaped the patterns of demographic history and diversification of this species in accordance with the refugia-within-refugia hypothesis. Introduction

Understanding the historical and contemporary factors that have shaped the genetic patterns of a plant species improves our knowledge of its evolutionary history and ecological adaptations, helps predict future responses to environmental changes, and contributes to the maintenance of genetic diversity, thereby supporting the development of effective conservation strategies1–4. Climatic changes, particularly during the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM), have led to shifts in genetic structure, demographic changes, and the present-day distribution of species5,6. During this period that occurred approximately between 23,000 and 19,000 years ago7, large portions of Europe were covered by ice sheets, and the climate was much colder and drier than it is today8. Most of today’s northern and central Adriatic was land, forming a bridge between the Apennines and the Balkan Peninsula, while the sea was confined to the southern parts9,10. During glacial periods many species retreated to refugia—geographically isolated areas with more favorable microclimates where they could survive11. The Balkan Peninsula, including the Adriatic coast, served as one of the major refugia in southern Europe during the LGM12. The concept of ‘refugia-within-refugia’ suggests that within these larger glacial refugia, smaller microrefugia existed, allowing species to survive in localized pockets of favorable conditions13. Tzedaki14 highlights the significance of the Balkan Peninsula as a major glacial refugium during the Quaternary period, noting that its diverse topography and coastal proximity fostered stable microclimates. These conditions enabled species to endure climatic fluctuations during the ice ages, supporting biodiversity and making the region crucial for the survival of various plant and animal species during glacial periods. As the climate warmed and glaciers receded after the LGM, species from these microrefugia recolonized their former ranges and genetic signatures of these glacial isolations and recolonization events are still detectable today in refugial populations15–17.

The Adriatic region likely harbored refugia for Dalmatian pyrethrum (Tanacetum cinerariifolium (Trevir.) Sch.Bip.; Asteraceae) and thus contributed to its genetic diversity and population structure. The genus Tanacetum L., to which T. cinerariifolium belongs, comprises of 135 species, distributed primarily across Europe, North America, North Africa, and temperate regions of Asia18. In Croatia, T. cinerariifolium is one of five species in the genus, and it is recognized by several local names, reflecting its cultural and ecological importance19. The species is endemic to the eastern Adriatic coast with its range extending from coastal Croatia, including many islands, to southern Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, and Northern Albania19,20. During the flowering stage, it is easily identifiable by its distinctive white ray and yellow disc florets21.

T. cinerariifolium is valued for its production of pyrethrin, a natural insecticide that disrupts insect nervous systems, rendering it one of the most commercially significant botanical insecticides22. In Croatia, cultivation of T. cinerariifolium for insecticide production has historical roots. Commercial cultivation, particularly along the Dalmatian coast and islands, began in the mid-nineteenth century. By the early twentieth century, Croatia was a leading global producer of pyrethrum, but the industry declined after World War I due to competition from other regions, particularly Japan and Kenya. The widespread adoption of synthetic insecticides like DDT during and after World War II further diminished Croatia’s pyrethrum industry23. Today, East Africa and Australia dominate global pyrethrum production24, while Croatia’s historical role in the industry remains a point of agricultural and cultural heritage. The overexploitation of T. cinerariifolium, initially involving harvesting of wild plants, and later usage of wild material for cultivation surely left additional signature in natural populations on the genetic level.

Molecular marker techniques have become indispensable tools for the genetic characterization, conservation, and management of plant germplasm25–27. In population genetics research, microsatellite markers, or simple sequence repeats (SSRs), are widely used due to their high degree of polymorphism, codominance, and reproducibility. SSRs consist of short, repeating DNA sequences and serve as highly informative molecular markers for assessing genetic diversity, population structure, and gene flow28. SSRs can differentiate between homozygotes and heterozygotes, are locus-specific, and provide consistent and reproducible results29. They have proven to be powerful tools in genetic studies of various species within the Asteraceae family30–33.

Recent advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) have greatly facilitated the development of SSR markers by generating large amounts of sequence data quickly and at lower costs, making it easier to develop species-specific markers34. These technological advancements have recently enabled the development of the SSR set for T. cinerariifolium35 despite its relatively large genome size (7.1 Gb) compared to other species in the Asteraceae family36. The only previous genetic study on this species, which used AFLP markers, indicated high genetic diversity across natural populations along the Croatian coast37, emphasizing the need for further investigations using more robust molecular markers like SSRs and also the need to study the demographic history of the species which has not been well explored up to this point. Combining genetic data with ecological niche modeling can reveal the species’ historical distribution and migration patterns, providing a comprehensive understanding of how ecological and genetic factors interact to shape a species’ distribution, evolution and genetic diversity and structure38.

Thus, by integrating genetic analysis with SSR markers and ecological niche modelling (ENM) in T. cinerariifolium, we aimed to: (1) investigate intra- and inter-population genetic diversity; (2) examine the genetic structure; and (3) acquire comprehensive understanding of genetic diversity and structure in relation to historical and contemporary processes. Studying the genetic diversity of this native species, given its economic importance, is essential for guiding sustainable conservation efforts, enhancing climate resilience and selective breeding programs.

Results

Microsatellite diversity

Genetic diversity and structure of 20 populations of T. cinerariifolium (Supplementary Table S1) were analyzed using SSR markers. Of the 12 markers used, TcUniZg006 amplified the largest number of alleles (29 in total), while the smallest number of alleles (5) was amplified by marker TcUniZg005. A total of 139 alleles were generated with an average of 11.58. The polymorphic information content (PIC) was calculated to evaluate the effectiveness of each marker. The PIC values ranged from 0.326 (TcUniZg023) to 0.864 (TcUniZg008), with an average of 0.605. Three loci (TcUniZg006, TcUniZg008 and TcUniZg009) had values above 0.7, indicating their high identification ability. The calculated probability of identity (PI) for the SSR markers ranged from 0.027 (TcUniZg008) to 0.438 (TcUniZg023) while the combined probability of identity including all loci was 7.87 × 10–11 (Supplementary Table S2). The results obtained show that the newly developed genomic SSR markers used are highly informative and can be effectively used to characterize the genetic diversity and structure of T. cinerariifolium. Of the 240 combinations of population and microsatellite loci, 13 (5.42%) of them were found to have null alleles with frequencies ranging from 0.072 to 0.199 (Supplementary Table S3).

Recent and past bottlenecks

We tested the occurrence of recent and past bottlenecks in T. cinerariifolium populations. Heterozygosity-excess test using Two-Phase Model (TPM) was significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.05) in three populations (P06 Žman, Dugi otok, P10 Mt. Biokovo, Kotiški Stanovi and P16 Lovćen), while the Garza-Williamson’s M-ratio (MR) was significant in seven populations: P01 Krk (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.01), P03 Mali Lošinj, P06 Žman, Dugi otok, P07 Telašćica, Dugi otok, P09 Sevid, P11 Vis and P20 Budva (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.05). However, neither past nor recent bottlenecks were confirmed for the T. cinerariifolium populations, as no significant values were obtained for either test after Bonferroni correction (P(MR) Bonf.) (Supplementary Table S4).

Population differentiation and structure

The most differentiated were populations P09 Sevid and P15 Podgorica (FST = 0.419; FST(null) = 0.418) and the least P01 Krk and P03 Mali Lošinj (FST = 0.031; FST(null) = 0.031) (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). No significant differences were observed between FST and FST(null) (PWilcoxon = 0.330). Estimates of the average FST values both with (FST = 0.178) and without (FST (null) = 0.178) correction relative to null alleles showed significant and moderate genetic differentiation between populations39.

To validate and visualize genetic clustering patterns, we performed a Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA). The first two principal coordinates (PCo1 and PCo2) together explained 11.9% of the total variance and revealed a clustering pattern similar to that obtained using STRUCTURE (see below). PCo1 clearly separated the clusters identified in the STRUCTURE solution at K = 2 (clusters A and B). Along PCo2, clusters A1 and A2 were distinctly separated, whereas clusters B1 and B2 showed substantial overlap, indicating a higher degree of admixture (Fig. 2a). These patterns were consistent with those observed in the Neighbor-Joining tree based on Pairwise Cavalli-Sforza and Edwards’ chord distances (Fig. 2b).

The STRUCTURE software provided evidence of population structure in T. cinerariifolium A maximum ΔK value (395.04) was observed at K = 2, followed by the ΔK value (23.57) at K = 4. With the higher K, the average estimates of the likelihood of the data, ln P(X|K), decreased while the variances increased substantially between the different runs for each K and therefore, the ΔK values were notably lower in comparison to those obtained in K = 2 and K = 4 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Based on this information, the proportion of membership of each individual across 20 populations was generated under K = 2 and K = 4 (Fig. 2c). At K = 2, individuals from the northern Adriatic populations (P01 Krk, P02 Cres, P03 Mali Lošinj) and the southern Adriatic populations (P15 Podgorica, P16 Lovćen, P17 Srđ, P18 Trebinje, P19 Grahovo, P20 Budva) were assigned to cluster A (average membership proportions greater than 0.75; except in P20 Budva). Individuals from a North Adriatic population (P04 Senj), central Adriatic populations (P05 Ugljan, P06 Žman, Dugi otok, P07 Telašćica, Dugi otok, P08 Murter, P09 Sevid) and southern Adriatic island populations (P11 Vis, P12 Pelješac, P13 Korčula and P14 Mljet) as well as P10 Mt. Biokovo population were assigned to cluster B (Fig. 2d).

At K = 4 further subdivisions occurred, most populations from cluster A (P01 Krk, P02 Cres, P03 Mali Lošinj, P17 Srđ, P18 Trebinje, P19 Grahovo, P20 Budva) were separated into subcluster A1, while two populations (P15 Podgorica and P16 Lovćen) grouped together into subcluster A2. Cluster B was separated in subcluster B1 (grouping the majority of populations from cluster B: P04 Senj, P05 Ugljan, P06 Žman, Dugi otok, P08 Murter, P11 Vis, P12 Pelješac, P13 Korčula and P14 Mljet) and subcluster B2 found primarily in populations P07 Telašćica, Dugi otok, P09 Sevid and P10 Mt. Biokovo. The most admixed populations were P04 Senj, P07 Telašćica Dugi otok, P08 Murter and P12 Pelješac (Fig. 2e).

The AMOVA revealed that the majority of genetic variation (81.89%) occurred within populations. When populations were divided into two genetic clusters (K = 2), 5.29% of the variation was explained by differences between the clusters, while 14.90% of the variation was within each cluster. However, most genetic variation (79.81%) was still within populations. In contrast, partitioning individuals into four genetic clusters (K = 4) explained more overall genetic variation (8.31%) and resulted in less variation within each cluster (11.70%). Nonetheless, the majority of genetic variation (79.89%) was still within populations. The significant φST values among populations and among populations within clusters (both K = 2 and K = 4) suggest that there is geographic structuring of populations (Table 2).

Isolation by distance (IBD) and isolation by environment (IBE)

As revealed by isolation by distance analysis there was no statistically significant relationship between genetic and geographical distance (r = 0.079; PMantel < 0.111; Fig. 4a).

Isolation by distance (IBD) and isolation by environment (IBE) in 20 Tanacetum cinerariifolium populations: Scatter plots of simple and partial Mantel tests showing the relationships between (a) geographic and genetic distances, (b) environmental dissimilarity and genetic distances and (c) residual environmental dissimilarity and genetic distances, while accounting for geographic distances among populations.

To further analyze possible factors associated with genetic variation, we performed isolation by environment analysis (IBE). The environmental heterogeneity of the species’ sampling sites was described with 19 temperature- and precipitation- related climatic variables (Supplementary Table S7). The variables were highly correlated and out of 171 pairwise correlations, a strong positive correlation (r > 0.70) was found in 32 cases and strong negative correlation was found in six cases (r < − 0.70) (Supplementary Table S8). Based on correlation matrix Principal Component Analysis was performed to examine and visualize environmental differences among the sampling locations. The first two principal components (PCs) had eigenvalues > 1 and jointly explained 83.18% of total variation in the bioclimatic conditions of T. cinerariifolium sampling locations. PC1 explained 57.31% of total variation and was highly positively corelated (r > 0.70, P < 0.001) with two temperature-related variables BIO02 and BIO03, and seven precipitation-related variables (BIO12-BIO14 and BIO16-BIO19). High negative correlation (r < -0.70, P < 0.001) was observed between PC1 and six temperature-related variables (BIO01, BIO06, BIO08-BIO11). A highly significant positive correlation was observed between PC2 and two temperatures (BIO04 and BIO05) and one precipitation-related variable (BIO15) (Supplementary Table S9).

Sampling sites for populations P01 Krk, P02 Cres, P10 Mt. Biokovo, P15 Podgorica, P16 Lovćen, P18 Trebine, P19 Grahovo, P20 Budva were characterized by higher precipitation levels and lower temperatures. Populations (P01 Krk, P02 Cres, P15 Podgorica, and P18 Trebinje) displayed temperature seasonality, while P04 Senj, P10 Mt Biokovo, P16 Lovćen, P19 Grahovo and P20 Budva exhibited noticeable but not extreme temperature variations throughout the year, as indicated by the BIO04 Isothermality variable. Population P17 Srđ was associated with precipitation seasonality. Northern Adriatic population P03 Mali Lošinj, along with central and southern Adriatic island populations (P05 Ugljan, P06 Žman, Dugi otok, P07 Telašćica, Dugi otok, P08 Murter, P09 Sevid, P11 Vis, P12 Pelješac, P13 Korčula, P14 Mljet) were characterized by higher temperatures and lower precipitation levels compared to other populations (Fig. 3a). The analysis revealed that populations P06 Žman, Dugi otok and P07 Telašćica, Dugi otok had the highest similarity in environmental conditions (0.053). Conversely, the populations P10 Mt Biokovo and P15 Podgorica showed the greatest difference in their sampling sites’ environmental conditions, with an environmental dissimilarity of 5.065 (Supplementary Table S10).

A significant correlation was found between genetic distance and environmental dissimilarity (r = 0.539; PMantel = 0.001). After accounting for the effect of geographic distances in a three-way Mantel test, the correlation between residual genetic and residual environmental dissimilarity remained significant (r = 0.539; PMantel = 0.001; Fig. 4b), suggesting that isolation by environment alone explains 29.1% of the observed genetic pattern (Fig. 4c).

Furthermore, the Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test between the two genetic clusters (A and B), as determined by STRUCTURE, was significant (P < 0.05) for 11 out of 19 environmental variables (Supplementary Table S11), including four of the 11 temperature-related variables (BIO01, BIO06, BIO07, BIO11), as well as all precipitation-related variables (BIO12, BIO13, BIO14, BIO16, BIO17, BIO18, BIO19) except BIO15 (Precipitation Seasonality).

Redundancy analysis

Five uncorrelated environmental variables (BIO07, BIO11, BIO13, BIO15, BIO17) were initially selected for redundancy analysis (RDA). The optimal RDA model, determined using the ordiR2step function in vegan, included only one variable: BIO13 Precipitation of the Wettest Month. As a result, the final RDA model contained a single constrained axis (RDA1). This model was highly significant (P = 0.0002) and explained 13.70% of the total variance (R2 = 0.1369), or 8.9% after adjusting for the number of predictors (adjusted R2 = 0.0890). Both the RDA1 axis and the variable BIO13 were highly significant (P < 0.0001) and accounted for the same proportion of the total variance as the model. To create the RDA scatterplot shown in Fig. 5, we used PC1 (i.e. the first unconstrained axis) on the y-axis. This axis represents the residual variance of the model, which accounted for 14.34% of the total variance. As expected, RDA1 clearly separated populations belonging to genetic cluster A from those in cluster B. Populations belonging to subclusters A1 and A2 were separated along the PC1 axis, indicating substantial differences in allele frequencies that could not be explained by the environmental variables analyzed.

Environmental niche modelling

Five variables were selected for ENM based on their intercorrelation which included: BIO7 Temperature Annual Range, BIO11 Mean Temperature of Coldest Quarter, BIO13 Precipitation of Wettest Month, BIO15 Precipitation Seasonality and BIO17 Precipitation of Driest Quarter. The AUCtest value for the optimal model was 0.9327. The optimal model based on the lowest AICc used regularization multiplier of 0.5 and linear and quadratic feature classes. The results of ENM were further processed and visualized using the maximum sensitivity plus specificity threshold of 0.2523 (cells with lower values considered as unsuitable). Current model predicted a suitable environment for the species matching that of a present distribution, spanning the coastal and peri-coastal areas of Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro and Albania. Additionally, the model also predicted the southwestern coast of Apennine peninsula as suitable, although the species has never been observed in this region in the wild The suitable area for the LGM period was localized to the coastal and peri-coastal areas of the paleo-Adriatic Sea which corresponds to present central and southern Adriatic on both eastern and western side with more penetration inland compared to current model (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Moderate genetic diversity and population differentiation in T. cinerariifolium

The analysis of 20 native populations using 12 microsatellite markers revealed moderate genetic diversity levels (HE = 0.534; Table 1)),which is higher than the average reported for endemic taxa (HE = 0.42)40. Since no significant bottleneck was detected, the relatively high genetic diversity observed across populations may reflect some degree of demographic resilience and adaptive potential. However, given the statistical limitations, the results of bottleneck tests should be interpreted with caution41.

Genetic differentiation between populations was moderate (FST = 0.178). At the population level, AMOVA showed that most of the genetic variation occurs within populations, reflecting a genetic structure which is typical for outcrossing plant species40,42.

Refugium ensured the species survival

Detecting areas of environmental suitability that could have acted as refugia during periods of unfavorable climate conditions involves identifying regions that have remained relatively stable and buffered from significant climate changes over time43. The results of ecological niche modeling (ENM) suggest that during the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM), the ideal habitat for T. cinerariifolium was in the coastal and peri-coastal regions of the paleo-Adriatic Sea. This region includes the current central and southern Adriatic coastline on both eastern and western sides, but with a broader inland range in comparison with the current model (Fig. 6). The land bridge between the peninsulas was unsuitable for the species, thus limiting its dispersal from the Balkan to the Apennine Peninsula and vice versa during that period. For the present-day distribution ENM provided accurate results for T. cinerariifolium in the Balkans, but not for the Apennines, where to our best knowledge the species has never been recorded. This discrepancy could be due to the fact that the model relied solely on climate data without including soil type, which is also one of the key determinants of species dispersal44. In its natural distribution range, T. cinerariifolium thrives predominantly in calcaric leptosols45, i.e. shallow, nutrient-poor soils with limited water availability, which is a typical soil type for the eastern Mediterranean region. In the Apennine region, which was considered suitable for T. cinerariifolium, cambisols—nutrient-rich soils with higher water retention are predominant46, while leptosols have a restricted and fragmented distribution47. This probably limited the spread of the species, despite favorable climatic conditions. In addition, cambisol, which is relatively young and naturally more fertile soil than leptosol, often supports more diverse vegetation that increases interspecific competition48, which can hinder the establishment of T. cinerariifolium in certain areas. These findings are not exclusive to T. cinerariifolium but are also observed in other plant species characteristic of typical karst vegetation, e.g. Salvia officinalis L.17.

The results indicate that the southern parts of the studied area provided stable and favorable conditions that supported the survival of T. cinerariifolium during the glaciation and thus acted as refugia. The same was observed for Edraianthus tenuifolius (Waldst. et Kit.) A.DC.49, Cardamine maritima DC.50 and Campanula pyramidalis L.51. We suspect that, following climatic change, individuals from refugial (i. e. southernmost) T. cinerariifolium populations dispersed and colonized new regions, such as the northern part of the eastern Adriatic. This is consistent with the general findings of studies on the post-glacial dispersal of plants, namely that the plant species that survived in southern European glacial refugia during the ice ages (especially during the LGM) subsequently expanded their range northwards as the ice retreated and conditions became more favorable5,6,12,14,52–54. While classical refugial theory predicts higher diversity in southern populations and lower diversity in northern areas due to founder effects5,12,55–57, T. cinerariifolium shows relatively uniform genetic diversity throughout its range. Populations in the northern Adriatic, particularly P03 Mali Lošinj (HE = 0.616), show some of the highest genetic diversity, although ENM suggests that these areas were unsuitable during the LGM. This pattern could be due to genetic mixing of different lineages during colonization to the north58,59 or to common selection pressure between northern and southern populations finding similar environmental conditions.

Our previous genetic study using AFLP markers37 suggested the northern Adriatic as a potential microrefugium for the species, based on the observation of increased gene diversity in northern populations. However, the ecological niche modeling (ENM) in the present study suggests that it is unlikely that the species survived in the northern Adriatic during the LGM. In addition, this study included a more extensive sampling of natural populations, including regions further south, uncovering higher gene diversity levels. Finally, SSRs are typically more reliable and informative than AFLPs for inferring population genetic parameters due to their codominant nature29,60,61.

The genetic structure as the result of past and contemporary processes

The STRUCTURE analysis revealed that grouping the populations into two (K = 2; Fig. 2d) and four (K = 4; Fig. 2e) clusters provided the best explanation of genetic structure, with each level of clustering offering insights into different evolutionary and ecological processes. At K = 2, the grouping of the northern and southern Adriatic populations into one cluster (cluster A), and the central Adriatic populations into another (cluster B), represents an unusual pattern that does not follow a simple latitudinal or isolation by distance model. Instead, the boundaries of these clusters align with the locations of two rivers—the Zrmanja and the Neretva. The role of these two rivers in the formation and maintenance of genetic clusters within different plant species has already been demonstrated and discussed62. Our previous study identified the Zrmanja canyon as a barrier separating northern and southern populations of T. cinerariifolium37. This area also coincides with the phylogeographic split in Helichrysum italicum (Roth) G.Don63 and to some extent in Salvia officinalis64,65. Similarly, Neretva river valley has been associated with genetic and taxonomic divergence in several species, e.g. Cardamine maritima DC66, Edraianthus tenuifolius (Waldst. et Kit.) A.DC.49, Euphorbia myrsinites L.53 and Euphorbia verrucosa L. alliance67. Lakušić et al. (2013)51 suggested that the Neretva valley acts as an ecological rather than a topographic boundary that emerged after the Last Glacial Maximum and contributed to genetic divergence. Thus, the clustering observed at K = 2 likely reflects environmental transitions shaped by the rivers rather than geographic distance alone.

At K = 4, the appearance of additional subclusters shows clearer signals of evolutionary connectivity and ecological divergence between populations. The two southernmost populations, a lowland (61 m a.s.l.) P15 Podgorica and P16 Lovćen, a high-altitude population (1031 m a.s.l.), exhibited distinct genetic ancestry compared to other southernmost populations, forming a separate cluster (cluster A2). Despite their geographical proximity, these populations occupy different microhabitats and as revealed (Fig. 3a, 3b). A similar pattern is obtained in cluster B2 which groups the high-altitude P10 Mt Biokovo population with central Adriatic coastal populations (P07 Telašćica and P09 Sevid). These highland populations occupy ecologically marginal areas of the species’ distribution, where present-day conditions are suboptimal for such thermophilic species and likely reflect shifts in species distribution during warmer climatic phases, such as the Holocene68,69 Such altitudinal differences are known to create ecological barriers that restrict gene flow, reduce pollinator exchange, and promote divergent selection, even over short distances70–72, however, current results do not yet fully reflect these processes, but likely represent the direction of future divergence. The apparent recent common ancestry of populations within clusters A2 and B2 suggests that there has been insufficient time for extensive genetic divergence, reinforcing the idea that evolutionary connectivity persists across geographically separated populations. This pattern is also reflected in the absence of isolation by distance (Fig. 4a), which does not appear to influence the fine-scale population structure of T. cinerariifolium. Instead, the observed genetic patterns probably reflect the historical connectivity of populations and the influence of bioclimatic factors, which appear to be the main determinants of genetic structure in this species. This interpretation is consistent with the results of the isolation-by-environment analysis, which confirms the role of bioclimatic variation along the eastern Adriatic coast in shaping population structure (Fig. 4c). Wilcoxon Rank Sum Tests provided additional support and showed significant differences in almost all precipitation-related variables (except BIO15; Supplementary Table S11) between clusters A and B. This implies that differences in water availability, rather than the seasonal distribution of precipitation, are a major driver of ecological divergence. While temperature-related variables (BIO01, BIO06, BIO07, BIO11) also showed significant differences among clusters, the lower proportion (4 of 11) suggests that temperature plays a minor role in shaping genetic structure, possibly interacting with precipitation to influence ecological pressures. The redundancy analysis (Fig. 5) further confirms the influence of environment on genetic structure, with the model including only BIO13 Precipitation of the Wettest Month explained the largest proportion of genetic variation. The separation of clusters along the first constrained axis closely matches the STRUCTURE clustering, suggesting that populations in the drier central Adriatic (cluster B) are genetically distinct from those in the wetter northern and southern regions (cluster A). The finding that precipitation plays a crucial role in shaping genetic structure suggests that future climate change, particularly in the form of increasing aridity in the Mediterranean region73 could have profound impact on the genetic integrity of T. cinerariifolium. Populations in drier regions (cluster B) are likely to have genetic variants that improve their ability to cope with drought stress and could play a key role in the species’ overall resilience to climatic change. The role of bioclimatic factors in shaping genetic variation has been well-documented in other Mediterranean species. For example, in Helichrysum italicum, a plant species whose distribution partially overlaps with that of T. cinerariifolium, the observed genetic variation and structuring was linked to bioclimatic factors, suggesting an important influence of local adaptation63. Similar environmentally influenced genetic patterns have also been observed in other European tree species, underlining the importance of ecological variables in shaping genetic variation74,75.

While the observed genetic patterns primarily reflect evolutionary and ecological processes driven by bioclimatic factors, it is also important to consider the possible impact of recent human activities that may have shaped the genetic variation and population structure of T. cinerariifolium. Human-mediated dispersal, along with overexploitation and cultivation, must therefore be taken into account when interpreting the genetic patterns observed. These processes along with habitat fragmentation and destruction, and climate change have a significant impact on the geographic distribution and genetic variation within populations76–78. Due to its strong insecticidal properties and the resulting high market demand, T. cinerariifolium was overexploited, but also cultivated in recent history23. Consequently, the genetic admixture observed in certain populations (P04 Senj, P07 Telašćica, Dugi Otok, P08 Murter and P12 Pelješac; Fig. 2c) may reflect historical human interventions, including seed exchange, cultivation practices and selective breeding for desirable traits. Large-scale cultivation was terminated in the 1940s, and it might be that these populations are influenced by cultivated or feral populations, and their genetic structure illustrates the historical anthropogenic impact. Introduction in cultivation and selection, is known to lower the genetic diversity of a species79, however, these populations maintain relatively high levels, likely due to cultivating plants of multiple origins, relatively short cultivation history, and minimal artificial selection pressure. Similar conclusions were given for Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi80.

Baseline for conservation measures and future breeding programs

Overall, T. cinerariifolium exhibits moderate genetic diversity levels, which, together with the high biochemical variability found in previous studies81–84 suggests a great potential of natural populations in terms of adaptability and climate resistance, and resilience as well as a source of breeding material for the creation of cultivars with high pyrethrin content. By comparing the results of this study with those mentioned above dealing with biochemical variability, we observed a certain similarity between the grouping of populations into chemotypes and genetic clusters, suggesting a possible correlation between the chemical and genetic profiles of the populations studied. High biochemical variability of natural populations was explained as the result of continuous adaptation of the species to the local environment84. Thus, the synthesis of the main compound pyrethrin I was associated with stress caused by a wide range of temperature fluctuations throughout the year, as well as higher temperatures and drought stress in the summer months. This characteristic was observed in populations assigned to bioclimatic group E, which in general coincides with populations assigned to cluster B1 (southernmost populations) identified by the STRUCTURE analysis in this study. The populations of the northern Adriatic (assigned to cluster A1) were also characterized by favorable pyrethrin profiles in previous studies. The northern Adriatic population Krk did not differ significantly in total pyrethrin content compared to the southernmost populations (populations of bioclimatic group E) and was among the populations with the highest total pyrethrin content (1.13%). In addition, the populations of Cres and Mali Lošinj also from the northern Adriatic were characterized with a high pyrethrin content or a high content of pyrethrin I and pyrethrin II and assigned to either chemotype P1 or P1/P282. The populations have sufficiently high genetic diversity, which, combined with their favorable pyrethrin content, justifies prioritizing them in breeding programs and implementing special conservation measures. The same treatment should be given for the populations thriving at the edge of the species’ range (e.g. P10 Mt Biokovo, P16 Lovćen), which have maintained relatively high genetic variation, despite present-day isolation from other populations. Plant populations in marginal areas may serve as a potential source of adaptation to climate-induced ecological changes, as they are already adapted to distinct environmental conditions 85,86 and therefore contribute to local biodiversity and to the evolutionary potential of species 87. According to Varga et al.35 population of Mt Biokovo has a unique chemical profile, characterized with exceptionally high pyrethrin II content. As it is adapted to the more extreme conditions at the edge of the species’ ecological range, it should be considered when breeding cultivars adapted to the continental climate. However, this correlation should be considered preliminary as further validation (GWAS, QTL mapping) is required to confirm the associations between genotype and chemotype.

In Croatia, T. cinerariifolium is a strictly protected plant species (Official Gazette no. 144/13)88. At present-day natural populations are no longer endangered by commercial gathering, as is the case for some other medicinal plant species in the eastern Adriatic (e.g. H. italicum63). However, the species faces significant threats from habitat loss and degradation, primarily caused by human activities such as urbanization and habitat conversion. These factors contribute to the decline of natural habitats and pose a significant threat to the survival of the species. Therefore, in situ conservation strategies should be implemented to preserve these natural populations and their desirable characteristics, taking into account the findings presented in this investigation.

Conclusion

This study represents the first characterization of Tanacetum cinerariifolium using microsatellite markers. We observed moderate genetic diversity levels and genetic structuring of populations. Our results suggest that the species may have survived in southern glacial refugia and subsequently migrated northwards. Present-day environmental dissimilarities between the sampling sites, as well as human impact further influence the observed pattern, as they account for a portion of the observed genetic variation in T. cinerariifolium. The research provides a valuable basis for understanding the complex interactions between genetics and environmental conditions and therefore provides valuable knowledge for the breeding programs, aimed at developing cultivars with favorable pyrethrin profiles and adaptation to wide ecological range, as well as development of adequate conservation strategies. Future in-depth studies should focus on identifying specific genes and biochemical pathways that are activated in response to stress conditions. The Mediterranean region is predicted to experience even more drastic, significant temperature fluctuations in the future, as well as reduced rainfall and increasing water scarcity. These factors will contribute to the overall stress for plant species and other organisms in the Mediterranean, so it is crucial to understand and conserve their adaptive potential. Genome-wide SNP genotyping and functional assays will be particularly valuable to uncover adaptive loci underlying pyrethrin biosynthesis and stress tolerance.

Materials and methods

Sampling locations and plant material

The seed (accessions) of twenty T. cinerariifolium populations were sampled across the natural distribution range of the species in Croatia (P01–P14, P17), Bosnia and Herzegovina (P18) and Montenegro (P15–P16, P19–P20). The collected accessions are stored and kept as part of the Collection of Medicinal and Aromatic Plants (available at: https://cpgrd.hapih.hr/) of the National Plant Gene Bank of the Republic of Croatia, at the Department of Plant Biodiversity, Faculty of Agriculture of the University of Zagreb. Voucher specimens were identified by Filip Varga and Martina Grdiša and they are kept in the ZAGR Herbarium Collection, Faculty of Agriculture, University of Zagreb (available at: http://herbarium.agr.hr/). Sampling sites of the studied T. cinerariifolium populations are described in Supplementary Table 1.

DNA extraction and SSR analysis

The study included 394 individuals from 20 natural T. cinerariifolium populations. Total genomic DNA from each individuum was isolated from 25 mg of silica-dried leaf tissue using the GenElute™ Plant Genomic DNA Miniprep Kit (Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Steinheim, Germany). Microsatellite analysis was based on 12 microsatellite loci35. Microsatellite alleles were amplified using a PCR protocol with an initial touchdown cycle (94 °C for 5 min; 5 cycles of 45 s at 94 °C, 30 s at 60 °C lowered by 1 °C at each cycle, and 90 s at 72 °C; 25 cycles of 45 s at 94 °C, 30 s at 55 °C, and 90 s at 72 °C; and 8-min extension step at 72 °C). The 20 μl of PCR reaction mixture contained 0.25 µM forward primer (FAM, NED, VIC, and PET fluorescently labeled) and 0.25 µM reverse primer, 1 × PCR buffer, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 200 µM each dNTP, 0.5 U Taq™ HS DNA Polymerase (TaKaRa™ Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan), and 5 ng template DNA. Fluorescently labeled PCR products were detected on an ABI 3730XL (Applied Biosystems™, Foster City, California, USA) using the Fragment Analysis service Macrogen Europe BV (Amsterdam, The Netherlands). The allele sizes of the PCR products were estimated using the GeneMapper 4.0 software (Applied Biosystems™, Foster City, California, USA).

Genetic analysis

The diversity of each microsatellite locus, including the total number of alleles (Na), polymorphic information content (PIC) and probability of identity (PI) was calculated using Cervus v. 3.0 89 Micro-Checker v. 2.2.3 software 90 was used to check for the presence of null alleles and scoring errors, and null allele frequencies were estimated using the expectation maximisation algorithm in FreeNA91.

Parameters of within-population diversity such as average number of alleles (Nav), allelic richness (Nar), number of private alleles (Npr) and private allelic richness (Npar) were calculated using HP-Rare v. 1.092. Genepop v. 4.7 software93 was used to calculate observed heterozygosity (HO), expected heterozygosity (HE) and inbreeding coefficient (FIS) and to test for deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. Sequential Bonferroni corrections were used to adjust significance levels for multiple testing using SAS v. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Expected heterozygosity [HE(null)] values were calculated from allele frequencies adjusted for the presence of null alleles in FreeNA and compared with the original HE values using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test in SAS. Populations that showed a deficit of heterozygotes relative to Hardy–Weinberg proportions were further tested using INEST v. 2.294. The Bayesian model (i.e. the individual inbreeding model; IIM) was applied to estimate the effects of null alleles and inbreeding. The full model (‘nfb’), which includes the effects of inbreeding and genotypic errors (FIS > 0), was compared with a random model (‘nb’) that assumes the presence of null alleles and genotypic errors (FIS = 0) using the deviance information criterion (DIC). We used 500,000 MCMC cycles with an update every 200 cycles and a burn-in of 50,000.

The signs of recent (heterozygosity-excess test95 and past bottlenecks (the M-ratio test96 were analysed in INEST. For the heterozygosity-excess test, the two-phase model (TMP) was used with the parameter values described in97 the proportion of multi-step mutations, pg = 0.22 and the average multi-step mutation size, δg = 0.31. Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test based on 1,000,000 permutations was used to assess the significance of both tests.

Genetic differentiation among T. cinerariifolium populations was assessed by estimated pairwise FST values and the corresponding P-values (after 10,000 permutations) in FSTAT v. 2.9.3.298. The pairwise FST(null) values were calculated using the Excluding Null Alleles (ENA) correction method in FreeNA and compared with the original FST values using the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test in SAS.

Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on simple matching coefficients between pairs of T. cinerariifolium individuals was performed using DARwin v.6.0.02199. Pairwise Cavalli-Sforza and Edwards chord distances100 between T. cinerariifolium populations were calculated, and an unrooted tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method. The tree was bootstrapped 1,000 times101 over microsatellite loci using PHYLIP v.3.6b102.

The microsatellite data were further subjected to genetic structure analysis using a model-based clustering method implemented in STRUCTURE103. A burn-in period of 200,000 steps was followed by 1,000,000 Monte Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) steps, assuming an admixture model and correlated allele frequencies. The number of clusters (K) was set from 1 to 11. The analysis was performed in thirty runs on the Padobran computer cluster at the University of Zagreb (Croatia), University Computing Centre (SRCE). To determine the optimal number of clusters (K), an ad hoc ΔK statistic104 was applied using the web-based software StructureSelector105, which also allows clustering and merging of the resulting runs according to the CLUMPAK method described in Kopelman et al. (2015)106. The runs with the maximum likelihood were selected and the proportion of membership of each population in each of the clusters was calculated by averaging the estimated membership coefficients of the individuals. Populations were assigned to a particular cluster if their average membership proportion was greater than 0.75 (i.e. 75%)107.

ARLEQUIN v. 3.5.2.2108 was used to perform the analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA)109 by partitioning the total genetic diversity among and within the T. cinerariifolium populations. In addition, a two-way AMOVA was performed including the effect of genetic clusters (at K = 2 and K = 4) as obtained by STRUCTURE (see below). The significance level of the parameters φ was determined after 10,000 permutations.

To analyse isolation by distance (IBD), we calculated the correlation between the matrix of the natural logarithm of geographical distances (in km) and the matrix of pairwise FST/(1—FST) ratios between population pairs, as described by Rousset et al. (1997)110.

To analyse isolation by environment (IBE), we calculated the correlation between the matrix of environmental dissimilarity and the matrix of pairwise FST/(1—FST) ratios between population pairs. The calculation of environmental dissimilarity between populations was based on data from the WorldClim 2 database with a spatial resolution close to a square kilometre111. Principal component analysis was performed on 19 bioclimatic variables of 20 T. cinerariifolium sampling sites using the PRINCOMP procedure in SAS. The same procedure was used to calculate the pairwise Euclidean distances between the population scores of the first two principal components. The pairwise Euclidean distances were thus considered as a measure of environmental dissimilarity between the populations. A three-way Mantel test was performed between the matrix of environmental dissimilarity and the matrix of pairwise FST / (1—FST) ratios, taking into account the geographical distances between populations. Mantel tests were performed with 10,000 permutations in NTSYS-pc v. 2.21L112.

To further explore the relationship between the environmental variables of the sampling sites and the genetic backgrounds of T. cinerariifolium populations, we conducted a Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test (Mann–Whitney U Test) to compare the distributions of environmental variables between two genetic clusters (A and B), as determined by STRUCTURE (at K = 2). The analysis was performed using the NPAR1WAY procedure in SAS, and exact P-values were calculated for each variable.

Redundancy Analysis (RDA) was conducted using the vegan package113 in R114 to evaluate the influence of environmental variables on microsatellite variation among 20 T. cinerariifolium populations. Prior to analysis, environmental variables exhibiting high collinearity (|r|> 0.65) were excluded based on data from 82 T. cinerariifolium occurrences used in Ecological Niche Modeling (ENM; see below). Microsatellite allele frequencies were transformed using the Hellinger method115, as implemented in vegan. The optimal set of explanatory variables was identified through forward selection using the ordiR2step function with 10,000 permutations. The statistical significance of the overall RDA model and individual constrained axes was assessed using permutation tests (10,000 iterations) via the anova.cca function in vegan.

Ecological niche modelling (ENM)

To determine potential geographical range of the species in present time as well as in the last glacial maximum (LGM), environmental niche modelling (ENM) using MaxEnt 3.4.4. was conducted116. An initial dataset of 734 T. cinerariifolium occurrences was created by combining data from our field research with public databases19,117. After removing records with coordinate errors, duplicate records and conducting spatial thinning to 20 km radius to reduce sampling bias using QGIS 3.28.11118, 82 occurrences were left to use in ENM. To characterize the environment, a set of 19 bioclimatic variables were downloaded from PaleoClim database for current climate conditions as well as LGM (CCSM4—The Community Climate System Model Version 4) in resolution of 30 arc-seconds119. Pearson’s correlation coefficients between all variables for current climate were calculated to remove all correlated variables (|r|> 0.65). To prevent model overfitting, ENMeval package 2.0120 was used to optimize maxent models in R 4.3.1121 and spatial block method was used to partition the occurrences in the optimization process as described by Ljubičić et al. (2023)122. The optimal model was selected based on the lowest value of Akaike Information Criteria corrected (AICc)123 and MaxEnt was ran using optimal parameters in 20 replicates with subsample method (70% of data used for training, 30% used for testing). Presence-absence maps for both current and LGM conditions were obtained by transforming continuous suitability maps into binary maps using the maximum sensitivity plus specificity threshold124.
