Osteoporosis Associated with SCN8A Mutation
Authors: Daniyeh Khurram, Kristyna Kupkova, Larry D Mesner, Andrew T Kraftson, Charles R Farber, Gregory A Clines
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 26-year-old man was evaluated for an elevated serum bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BSAP). His medical history was significant for a neurodevelopmental disorder with generalized epilepsy. Symptoms included chronic pain in the ankles, hands, and right ribs, and a progressive decline in physical activity. Vitamin D deficiency was discovered, but the elevated BSAP persisted despite vitamin D replacement. Imaging studies reported diffusely increased scintigraphic uptake in the axial and appendicular skeleton, and low bone mineral density, but no radiographic sclerotic or lytic lesions. Genetic screening revealed a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the sodium voltage-gated channel α subunit 8 gene (SCN8A) (c.2924 T>A; p.L975*). Loss-of-function mutations in SCN8A are responsible for neurologic disease and bone loss. Expression analysis revealed that SCN8A was present in human osteoblasts and osteocytes, but not in osteoclasts, suggesting that the voltage-gated sodium channel, Nav1.6, encoded by SCN8A, may have direct actions in bone. The patient was subsequently prescribed alendronate, resulting in a lower alkaline phosphatase and symptomatic improvement in bone pain.Keywords: elevated alkaline phosphatase, low bone mineral density, osteoblast, SCN8A mutation, epilepsy

Introduction

Elevated serum bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BSAP) and low bone mineral density (BMD) are common conditions managed by endocrinologists. The causes of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) elevation include states of high bone turnover, such as osteomalacia, Paget disease, bone metastasis, skeletal disuse, hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, and early menopause. When an elevated serum BSAP that does not correct with vitamin D replacement and coexists with low BMD, the differential diagnosis can often be narrowed. It is important to consider rare genetic syndromes as potential causes of increased bone turnover and low BMD, particularly when patients present with uncommon combinations of signs and symptoms.

Case Presentation

A 26-year-old man was referred to an academic medical center endocrinology clinic for a long-standing elevation in serum ALP that was first noted at age 8 years. His medical history was significant for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, seizure disorder, and cerebral palsy. The mother's pregnancy was uneventful. However, the birth was complicated by meconium aspiration, bradycardia, and apnea. The patient experienced a delay in developmental milestones, including walking and other gross motor skills. He was diagnosed with epilepsy at age 4 years and was prescribed a range of antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), including phenobarbital, levetiracetam, phenytoin, and carbamazepine; the patient is currently prescribed oxcarbazepine and topiramate and has been taking them for more than 10 years.

The patient had reported chronic bone pain, located in the ankles, hands, and under the right ribs, resulting in a progressive decline in his physical activity, but without reported muscle weakness. His diet mainly consisted of macaroni and cheese, hamburgers, and cola. Calcium intake consisted of 630 mg of calcium citrate daily and one cup of milk daily. There was no history of fractures, kidney stones, or unusual dental abnormalities. The patient did not smoke cigarettes or drink alcohol. On physical examination, he was lean, with a hunched posture, had a normal gait, and no kyphosis or spinal tenderness. The patient did not have observable bone or joint deformities.

Diagnostic Assessment

A metabolic bone disease laboratory evaluation was performed (Table 1), revealing 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OHD) deficiency and an elevated BSAP and total ALP, with values that ranged from 526 to 648 IU/L (SI: 8.77-10.8 μkat/L) (reference range, 46-116 IU/L [SI: 0.77-1.93 μkat/L]) over a 6-year period. The serum marker of bone resorption, C-telopeptide (CTX), was slightly elevated, and the serum marker of bone formation, procollagen type I intact N-terminal propeptide (P1NP), was in the high-normal range. Other laboratory tests, including serum calcium, parathyroid hormone (PTH), phosphate, and testosterone, were normal. Other causes of osteomalacia were investigated, including fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF-23)-related disorders and heavy metal poisoning, but testing was normal. Bone density evaluation was performed using a GE Lunar iDXA (dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry) bone densitometer at age 26 years that reported low bone mass for chronologic age, with the lowest Z-score of −3.6 at the left femoral neck (Table 2). A radiographic skeletal survey did not report osteosclerotic or lytic lesions. A scintigraphic bone scan demonstrated reduced tracer uptake in the soft tissues and kidneys, with diffuse uptake throughout both the axial and appendicular skeleton, consistent with enhanced skeletal scintigraphic uptake and a global increase in bone turnover (Fig. 1). A DXA scan was repeated when the patient was age 29 years, reporting no significant changes in BMD (see Table 2).

Due to concerns about a genetic syndrome, the patient was referred to an academic medical genetics clinic and underwent whole-exome sequencing genetic evaluation. A genetic screen for mitochondrial mutations reported no pathogenic variants. However, a novel heterozygous pathogenic variant in the sodium voltage-gated channel α subunit 8 gene (SCN8A) (c.2924 T>A; p.L975*) was identified. The mutation was located in exon 17 and predicted to cause premature truncation of this 1980–amino acid protein with an expected loss of function in ion channel activity. SCN8A encodes for Nav1.6, a voltage-gated sodium channel that, when mutated, causes a spectrum of neurological diseases [1, 2].

Treatment

The patient was prescribed ergocalciferol 50 000 units every 14 days, along with a recommendation to increase dietary calcium, with a goal of 1200 to 1500 mg/day. Despite these interventions and subsequent repletion of vitamin D, total ALP and BSAP concentrations remained elevated. The patient was then prescribed alendronate 70 mg by mouth weekly at age 32 years (month 84 in Table 1).

Outcome and Follow-up

Soon after the patient was prescribed alendronate, he reported an improvement in bone pain. Nine months after starting alendronate, the serum total ALP was lower at 391 IU/L. The patient continues to be fracture free. Two years after starting alendronate, at age 34 years, a DXA scan reported a significant 10.7% increase in spine BMD and with stable BMD at other locations (see Table 2).

An analysis was performed to investigate the potential functional roles of Nav1.6 in the skeleton. While Nav1.6, the protein encoded by SCN8A, is expressed in neurons, the extent to which Nav1.6 has direct actions in bone was unclear. Using published RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data (Gene Expression Omnibus and ENCODE), SCN8A expression was determined in bone fragments, as well as in the bone-specific cell types—osteoblasts, osteocytes, and osteoclasts. Abood et al [3] reported the transcriptomic profiles from human acetabular bone fragments obtained during hip arthroplasty of 17 individuals. SCN8A was expressed in this dataset, with low expression at the ninth percentile of all transcripts, suggesting that at least a subset of bone cells express SCN8A (Fig. 2). Next, RNA-Seq data from a human osteoblast cell line (hFOB) were queried from Abood et al [3], revealing much higher expression at the 30th percentile of all hFOB transcripts. As expected, osteocytes, mature osteoblasts that become encased in bone matrix, are also expressed SCN8A at the 11th percentile. Hansen et al [4] reported transcriptomic data from isolated monocytes from 8 female donors that were differentiated into functional osteoclasts. Analysis of the reported RNA-Seq data reported no SCN8A expression in this dataset.

For purified bone samples, for which more samples were available, genes were considered expressed if TPM exceeded 0.1 in at least 20% of samples. Mean expression levels were then calculated per gene, and expression percentiles were derived. All analyses were performed in R using the tidyverse package (version 2.0.0).

Discussion

The patient experienced a prolonged elevation in ALP, raising concern for osteomalacia. This condition results from inadequate mineralization of the bone matrix, most commonly due to insufficient calcium and phosphate, which are necessary for the formation of osteoid hydroxyapatite crystals. Vitamin D deficiency is a common cause of osteomalacia, with AEDs being a contributor through increased metabolism of vitamin D by the activation of cytochrome P450 enzymes. The patient's current AEDs are oxcarbazepine and topiramate, both of which have been reported to increase the metabolism of vitamin D but not to the extent of older AEDs such as phenytoin and carbamazepine [5, 6]. On presentation, the patient was vitamin D deficient. However, with prompt normalization of serum 25-OH vitamin D and with normal 1,25 (OH)2 vitamin D concentrations, total ALP and BSAP remained elevated for the next 6 years. Moreover, the lack of hyperparathyroidism suggested that hypovitaminosis D and low calcium availability in the bone matrix were unlikely to account for the sustained elevation in ALP.

The formation marker, P1NP, was not elevated, but CTX, a marker of bone resorption, was only slightly elevated. P1NP and CTX are not as sensitive for detecting osteomalacia as is ALP [7]. Other conditions that cause osteomalacia include FGF-23–related disorders, renal tubular disorders, Fanconi syndrome, type II renal tubular acidosis, chronic kidney disease, aluminum toxicity, and hypophosphatasia [8]. However, no clinical or laboratory evidence supported these diagnoses.

Genetic analyses revealed a mutation in SCN8A, a gene that encodes Nav1.6 and is a member of a family of genes encoding the α subunit of voltage-gated sodium channels. The 9 members of this family share a similar protein structure, composed of 4 large domains, each containing 6 transmembrane segments [2]. The patient described here is heterozygous for a nonsense and loss-of-function mutation that substitutes the leucine at amino acid position 975 with a stop codon, occurring in transmembrane segment S6 of the second domain of Nav1.6 [9]. Since all 4 domains are required to form an intact pore for sodium ions to traverse the cell membrane, the absence of domains 3 and 4 would produce a nonfunctional protein. Nav1.6 functions as a monomer, so loss-of-function mutations are not expected to exert dominant-negative effects on functional Nav1.6 channels produced from the normal allele. SCN8A is expressed in osteoblasts but not osteoclasts, suggesting that Nav1.6 may directly affect osteoblast function, which is consistent with the elevated serum ALP. Rolvien et al [10] described the phenotype of Scn8a genetic knockout mice of reduced trabecular number and thickness, cortical thinning, and an increase in bone resorption. Histomorphometric analyses reported no change in osteoblast or osteocyte number, but a significant increase in osteoclast number, as well as disorganized collagen structure. The function of voltage-gated sodium channels in osteoblasts is unclear, and the extent to which action potentials are necessary for proper cellular function is speculative.

SCN8A was initially identified in 1995 and is highly expressed in central and peripheral neurons, as well as in the dorsal root ganglia. Loss-of-function mutations of this gene cause a wide range of neurologic disorders that include intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, movement disorders, and epilepsy [1]. A de novo SCN8A mutation was identified in a 4-year-old child presenting with epilepsy, developmental delay, and pathological fractures [11]. In a separate report, Rolvien et al [10] described a 13-year-old girl with epileptic encephalopathy with severe juvenile osteoporosis and multiple skeletal fractures who carried an SCN8A mutation (c.1078_1079delTTinsGC; p.Phe360Ala). The bisphosphonate neridronate was prescribed, which led to normalization of the bone resorption, and no subsequent fractures were reported. The patient reported here was also prescribed a bisphosphonate, which resulted in the resolution of bone pain but failed to normalize the elevated ALP. The reason for less bone pain with alendronate is unclear. Osteomalacia is associated with bone pain, likely due to the presence of microfractures and pseudofractures that activate pain fibers located in the periosteum. As evidenced by the increase in BMD with alendronate, it is possible that the patient was experiencing fewer subclinical fractures, which resulted in less bone pain.

In summary, we report a patient with a novel SCN8A [c.2924 T>A; p.L975*] mutation. Bone health in patients with SCN8A mutations should be regularly assessed, and providers should ensure that patients receive adequate amounts of calcium and vitamin D. Bisphosphonate therapy has been shown to reduce osteoclastogenic activity in patients and can prevent morbidity from bone pain and fractures.
