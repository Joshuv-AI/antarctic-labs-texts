Underlying charge-transfer mechanism of visible light emission in guanine crystals
Authors: Aditya Prasun, Ravindra Vishwakarma, Kangkan Sharmah, Ankur Kanti Guha, Ranjit Thakuria, Tridib K. Sarma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Biogenic guanine crystals found in various aquatic and terrestrial animals, show unique optical properties that include structural coloration as well as reflectance and have intrigued researchers for over a century. Besides the highly reflective nature of these organic crystals, guanine-rich parts of certain organisms (fishes, geckos, etc.) show emission in the visible region upon UV light exposure. This fluorescence is used by these organisms for intra-specific signaling. However, there is no available literature regarding the occurrence and origin of visible light emission in guanine crystals. Earlier reports suggest that guanine, when excited at its absorption maxima (254 nm), emit in the UV region (λem = 332 nm). The objective of the present study is to address the fundamental question: How can guanine crystals emit in the visible region. Laboratory crystallization of guanine requires extremely acidic or basic medium due to its limited solubility under ambient aqueous conditions. Herein, we report blue emissive guanine crystals obtained under highly acidic or basic conditions and explore the origin of their fluorescence in the visible region. Guanine solutions and crystals showed charge-transfer UV–vis bands that, upon excitation, produced blue emission. A combination of experimental and theoretical tools was employed to establish the fluorescence mechanism.

Introduction

Biogenic guanine crystals are well-known examples of biomineralization of organic molecules in living organisms1. Similar to inorganic biominerals like nacre, diatoms, bone, teeth, and corals, the mechanisms underlying the biomineralization of organic molecules remain an unresolved question within the scientific community2,3. Organisms lacking the guanase enzyme form guanine as an end product of nucleic acid degradation. These species thus accumulate guanine in a membrane-bound vesicle within specialized cells commonly known as iridophores that results in crystal formation4. The natural abundance of guanine crystals can be realized in species with prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria5,6 and in eukaryotic species, including algae, spiders, fish, lizards, chameleons, and scallops7–16. Biogenic guanine crystals have distinctive optical features, such as iridescence, birefringence, color tunability and both broadband and narrowband reflections, rendering guanine the most thoroughly researched organic biomineral in nature4. Such physicochemical properties tempted researchers worldwide to grow similar crystals under laboratory conditions. However, under these conditions, the crystallization of guanine is challenging due to the compound’s restricted solubility in most organic solvents and water17. Guanine exhibits limited solubility in certain solvents, including formaldehyde, DMSO, and DMF; hence, several instances of β-guanine crystallization from these solvents are reported in the literature1,18–21. Conversely, guanine solubility in aqueous medium increases when the pH of the solution is extremely acidic or basic22,23. Consequently, guanine crystallization as a salt under acidic conditions is reported with various inorganic acids24–30. Recently, guanine crystals in different basic media were also grown for structural elucidation31,32.

In contrast to the aforementioned optical properties, the luminescent characteristics of guanine crystals are rarely investigated. Limited computational and experimental investigations are present discussing the emissive behavior of guanine crystals33,34. Reports assert that guanine solutions and crystals emit only in the ultraviolet region35. Contrarily, in several biological systems, emission in the visible range is reported from the regions that are abundant with guanine crystals. For instance, Michiels et al. claimed guanine crystals to be the main reason behind red fluorescence observed in reef fishes36. In another report, they showed that red fluorescence in T. Delsei fish originates from the stacking of guanine crystals inside the iridophores37. Except fishes, neon green fluorescence in the gecko species was also suspected to be due to iridophores consisting of guanine crystals38. Due to the lack of available reports indicating the emission of guanine crystals in the visible region, it is presumed that the emission arises from an unidentified fluorophore or impurity associated with guanine36–38. Recently, to mimic the red emissive biogenic crystals, guanine crystallization in an aqueous medium consisting of fluorescent dyes as dopant resulted in red emissive guanine crystals. However, the emission was only due to the dye molecules entrapped in the crystal39. Recently, Aizen et al. reported the possibility of intrinsic fluorescence from nucleobase crystals, however no detailed mechanistic study was carried out to understand the origin of this optical behavior40. The environment in which the biogenic guanine crystals are grown in these species are complex and not fully understood, therefore understanding the factors that lead to the emissive behavior in the visible region is a tedious task.

In our work, we set out to address this gap by demonstrating that the guanine crystals grown under the laboratory conditions exhibit blue-colored emission in both its protonated and deprotonated forms, in solution as well as crystalline state. We observed that by increasing the concentration of guanine in the solution, emission intensity increases linearly. Upon crystallization, the emission intensity of these guanine salts increased significantly, accompanied by a slight shift in the emission maxima, suggesting a crystallization-induced emission enhancement (CIEE) mechanism. We also examined the influence of various counterions on the emission characteristics in their solution state and observed that the intercalation of anions/cations dramatically alters the arrangement of guanine molecules within the lattice, leading to fluorescence in both the crystalline and solution states.

Results and discussion

Owing to the poor solubility in neutral aqueous medium, guanine was crystallized under extreme acidic and basic pH conditions. In a typical procedure (Scheme 1), sufficient amount of commercially available guanine powder was dissolved in concentrated acidic/basic aqueous solutions (for details, see “Methods” section). To ensure complete dissolution, solutions were heated and then allowed to cool naturally. Transparent rod-shaped crystals were formed within 30 min in acidic conditions, while larger, polygon-shaped crystals appeared after 15 days under basic conditions. Surprisingly, upon UV irradiation (λex = 365 nm), both the solution and the crystals grown from it, were found to be emissive in the blue region. While guanine crystallization has been extensively studied in both aqueous and organic media previously, no reports have documented visible light emission from the resulting crystals. Photophysical Studies

In neutral aqueous medium, guanine shows two characteristic absorption bands at 245 nm and 275 nm, that can be assigned to π–π* transitions. The position and intensity of both the modes depend on the extent of protonation/deprotonation. In aqueous solution, guanine can exist in five different forms, which are in equilibrium with each other, depending on the pH of the medium. The different guanine moieties can be identified using UV–visible absorption studies, as they exhibit distinct absorption patterns in the 240–300 nm region23. Thus, to investigate the guanine moieties present in variable experimental conditions, UV–visible absorption spectra of diluted guanine solutions (10 μM) were recorded (Fig. 1a). Under extreme acidic conditions (pH ≤ 1), two bands at 248 nm and 271 nm were observed that matches precisely with the monoprotonated guanine species (GuaH+, Fig. 1b). On the other hand, under highly basic conditions (pH ≈ 14), a broad band at 274 nm occurs along with the disappearance of the band at 245 nm, suggesting the formation of the guanine dianion (Gua2-, Fig. 1b) species in NaOH solution (Figs. 1a, S1)23. However, upon further increasing the guanine concentration, a new absorption feature emerges as a shoulder at around 360 nm in the acidic medium. This band became more pronounced with increasing guanine concentration (Fig. 1d, e). In the basic medium, two new absorption bands at 365 nm and 398 nm were observed at high guanine concentration (25 mM) (Fig. 1f). Next, we performed fluorescence measurement of guanine in both acidic and basic medium to understand their emission behavior. In the acidic medium (Fig. S2), at low concentration of guanine (5 mM), the emission maximum was observed at 347 nm (λex = 310 nm). This was consistent with the earlier reports where the acidic guanine solution showed emission at 352 nm, whereas guanine monomers in neutral aqueous solution emit at 335 nm35,43. Surprisingly, at moderate guanine concentration (25 mM), excitation at its charge-transfer band (360 nm) resulted in emission in the visible region (blue) with a maxima at 393 nm (Fig. 2a). For a better understanding, excitation-dependent emission spectra were recorded at 5 nm intervals in the 300–400 nm range (Fig. S3). When the acidic guanine solution was excited at 315 nm, the emission maxima was observed at 350 nm. Subsequently, with an increase in the excitation wavelength, the emission peak at 350 nm diminished, and a new emission peak at 393 nm appeared, suggesting a red-shift. The most intense emission at 393 nm was recorded at an excitation wavelength of 360 nm. Excitation beyond 360 nm resulted in further red-shifting in emission maxima, however, with reduced intensity. Such excitation-dependent red shifts are often attributed to aggregation-induced emission (AIE). This occurs when aggregates of varying sizes form, leading to differences in molecular packing and electronic interactions. To further investigate the effect of aggregation, guanine concentration was varied from 5 to 25 mM and corresponding emission and excitation spectra were recorded (Fig. S4). In Gua-HCl solution, the intensity of the emission mode at 393 nm (λex = 360 nm) increased linearly with enhancement in guanine concentration (Fig. S4 inset). Additionally, the excitation band at 360 nm owing to charge-transfer increased linearly in intensity with increase in guanine concentration (Fig. S4b). These results confirm that a higher degree of aggregation increases the probability of charge transfer. The charge-transfer process is highly sensitive to the nature of the solvent46. To explore this effect, emission spectra were recorded in aqueous mixtures containing relatively less polar protic (methanol) and aprotic (DMSO) solvents (Fig. 2b). A larger red shift in emission was observed in less polar solvents as compared to water. Guanine was further dissolved in 1 M aqueous solution of various commonly available mineral acids (HBr, H2SO4, HNO3, and H3PO4) and in all the cases, blue emission was observed. In H2SO4, HNO3 and H3PO4 solutions (Fig. S5), guanine showed emission spectra similar to HCl, with maxima at 393 nm. Further, the excitation-dependent emission behavior of Gua-H2SO4 solution followed a similar trend as observed in Gua-HCl solution (Fig. S6a). However, in Gua-HBr solution, a broad emission peak with significantly weak intensity was observed with a maxima at 399 nm (Fig. S6b), suggesting heavy atom effect. On the contrary, upon excitation at 310 nm, a highly intense mode at 360 nm was observed (Fig. S6b inset). In the presence of heavier atoms, due to efficient spin–orbit coupling the probability of intersystem crossing increases. This enhances the non-radiative decay pathways which leads to efficient quenching of emission from the singlet state47. To verify this phenomenon, emission spectra (Fig. 2c) were recorded for an equal amount of guanine dissolved in various hydrohalic acid solutions (HCl, HBr, and HI). The emission intensity reduced 6.5-fold in case of HBr as compared to HCl and entirely quenched in HI (λex = 360 nm)48–50.

The photophysical properties of deprotonated guanine were further evaluated using guanine solubilized in 1 M NaOH aqueous solution to make a 25 mM concentration. Two charge transfer bands were observed at 365 nm and 398 nm in the UV-visible spectrum in case of deprotonated guanine. Upon excitation at these two wavelengths, emission bands were observed at 417 nm (λex = 365 nm) and 460 nm (λex = 398 nm), respectively (Fig. 2d). The absorption and emission behavior of deprotonated guanine in their aggregated state was studied by varying the guanine from 25 mM to 150 mM (Fig. S7). It was observed that upon increasing the guanine concentration, the emission maxima shifted gradually from 417 nm to 464 nm, on the other hand, the excitation maxima were also shifted from 365 nm to 398 nm (Figs. S2c,d, S8). A linear increase in the excitation and emission intensities with an increase in the guanine concentration (Fig. S7 inset) suggested similar aggregation-induced emission in deprotonated guanine also. Aqueous guanine solutions were further prepared in KOH (Fig. S9) and ammonia (Fig. S10). Guanine dissolved in 1 M KOH and 1 M ammonia solution showed an emission maximum at 428 nm and 435 nm, respectively (λex = 365 nm). Both the solutions showed intense blue fluorescence, closely resembling the emission profile of guanine in NaOH solution. This consistent blue-region emission across different alkaline environments highlights the robust and reproducible photophysical response of guanine under these conditions.

Furthermore, guanine crystals were grown under laboratory conditions in both acidic and basic medium, and their emissive properties were systematically investigated. Unlike in solution, the counterion plays an important role in emission enhancement in crystals and this is reflected in the fluorescence spectra of crystals grown in different acidic medium. The extent of bathochromic shift in crystals with respect to their solution varied in each case (Figs. 2e, f, S11). In Gua-HCl crystal, while excited at its charge-transfer band (λex = 360 nm), emission band centered at 406 nm appeared (Fig. 2e), showing a red-shift of 13 nm in comparison to its solution state (λem = 393 nm). In Gua-HBr crystal, a broad and red-shifted emission band (λex = 365 nm) centered at 407 nm was observed (Figure S11 a). In Gua-H2SO4 crystal, the red-shifting was of 10 nm with the band centered at 402 nm (λex = 360 nm, Fig. S11 b). However, in the case of Gua-NaOH, a broad emission spectrum having maxima at 420 nm was observed (λex = 360 nm). The distinct red-shift of emission bands upon crystallization arises from enhanced structural rigidity and intermolecular interactions, with the magnitude of the shift governed by counterion-dependent hydrogen bonding and crystal packing51.

The fluorescence lifetime decay profiles of different acid and basic guanine solutions were recorded at excitation wavelength of 371 nm (Fig. S12). The average lifetime was found to be 0.3 ns and 0.6 ns for guanine solution in HCl and NaOH, respectively (Table 1). The relative quantum yield was calculated using quinine sulfate (Φfl = 0.54) as the reference (λex = 360 nm) and found to be 2.6% and 2.8% in the case of HCl and NaOH respectively. Furthermore, the effect of the solvent polarity on emission properties was also investigated in a mixed solvent system. In a mixture of Gua-HCl solution with DMSO or methanol (H2O:DMSO or MeOH was kept at 1:9 v/v), an increased fluorescence lifetime of 0.73 ns (H2O-DMSO) and 0.60 ns (H2O-MeOH) as compared to 0.37 ns in aqueous solution signifies better stabilization of the excited state in relatively less polar solvent (Fig. S13). Owing to the extensive hydrogen-bonded network and presence of charge-transfer mode in the UV–visible spectra, the possibility of excited-state proton transfer was also investigated55. For this, kinetic isotope effect was observed when water was substituted with D2O keeping all other reaction conditions unchanged. In the fluorescence spectrum, only a slight increase in the emission intensity was observed under acidic conditions. Similar observations were obtained in basic conditions as well (Fig. S15). Moreover, the fluorescence lifetime in both acidic and basic D2O was also compared with those in the water medium and only a slight increase was observed (Table S2). Both these observations suggest the absence of excited intramolecular proton transfer mechanism.

Microscopic analysis

The morphology of the guanine crystals depends appreciably on the crystallization conditions (pH, temperature, presence of additives, etc.)19,56, thus microscopic analysis was performed to analyze the effect of various conditions on the morphologies of the grown crystals. First, Field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) images of different crystals were recorded (Fig. S16). In HCl medium, the crystals had rectangular block-like morphology. However, in the case of HBr, hexagonal crystals were obtained. Rod-like morphologies of guanine crystals were obtained during crystallization in aqueous H2SO4 medium. Large polyhedron-shaped guanine crystals were obtained when crystallization was performed in a basic medium using NaOH. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was employed to investigate the emissive properties of these crystals. Differential Interference Contrast (DIC) and confocal images clearly demonstrate that the emission is localized exclusively within the crystals, with no detectable signal from the surrounding background. Crystal structure analysis of the block-shaped crystals obtained from guanine crystallization in concentrated NaOH showed that the complex crystallizes in monoclinic P21/c space group. The asymmetric unit contains one molecule of guanine dianion, two sodium cations and seven water molecules, resulting in the formation of guanine disodium heptahydrate disalt (Fig. S20). The highly basic NaOH solution resulted formation of a dianion upon removal of two protons from the guanine molecule. The two Na+ ions are coordinated to seven water molecules resulting in a coordinated layer with both Na+ having octahedral coordination geometry. Whereas one sodium ion (Na2) is having a nearly regular octahedral coordination, the second sodium ion (Na1) is in a more distorted geometry (Fig. 3k). In 3D, due to the presence of the metal coordinated water system, the guanine dianion unit does not have any possible π–π interactions and are dispersed in a herringbone fashion resulting in the formation of a plum and pudding type model packing arrangement as shown in Fig. 3l.

Single-crystal analysis confirmed that effective π–π stacking was minimized in both protonated and deprotonated guanine crystals, thereby efficiently reducing the non-radiative pathways responsible for the fluorescence quenching. In anhydrous guanine crystals, strong π–π stacking interactions with distances of approximately 3.2 Å between guanine rings are typically observed, resulting in well-defined layers stabilized by such aromatic stacking9. This strong π–π stacking of aromatic molecules often leads to exciplex formation, which facilitates non-radiative energy transfer and results in fluorescence quenching, a phenomenon commonly termed as aggregation-caused quenching57. The crystalline environment in the studied samples presents a markedly different scenario. In all three crystals grown in the acidic medium, single-crystal XRD data reveal that guanine molecules are linked together as centrosymmetrical dimers primarily through hydrogen bonds (Table S3). These dimers further interact through water molecules and corresponding anions, rather than stacking over each other via π–π interactions. The geometry and packing induced by the presence of water and counteranions disrupt the parallel alignment necessary for substantial π–π stacking interactions. Instead, the crystal lattice is dominated by a network of hydrogen bonds, which determines the molecular arrangement more significantly than aromatic stacking forces. Similarly, in the Gua-NaOH crystal, X-ray diffraction analysis shows that the presence of sodium ions and water molecules causes guanine molecules to arrange in an offset herringbone fashion, thus minimizing effective π-π stacking between them. This disrupted stacking arrangement prevents the formation of strongly coupled π-systems that would otherwise facilitate non-radiative decay pathways and fluorescence quenching.

Bravais=Friedel=Donnay=Harker (BFDH) morphology calculations of the single crystals of guanine molecular salts were in accordance with the morphologies observed in FESEM (Fig. S23). Further Hirshfeld analysis58 of all the crystal structures was performed using Crystal Explorer software to understand various possible intermolecular interactions present in the crystal structures (2D fingerprint plots59, Fig. S24). In the case of acid salt crystals, hydrogen-bonding interaction between C–H, N–H, O–H and H–H were found to be the main contributors (summarized in Fig. S24 iii), while in the case of disodium hydrate salt crystal, main contributors towards structural stability are N–H, H–H, and O–H interactions.

Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectra of commercial guanine powder and guanine crystals were recorded and compared (Fig. S25). Significant changes in various peak positions were observed owing to the changes in the electron density upon protonation or deprotonation of guanine. The C=O stretching frequency at 1693 cm−1 was shifted to 1704 and 1701 cm−1 in Gua-H2SO4 and Gua-HBr, respectively. Significant reduction in the C=O stretching peak intensity was observed in Gua-HCl and Gua-NaOH crystals confirming the involvement of carbonyl groups in hydrogen bonding. The -NH2 stretching vibration at 1669 cm−1 in anhydrous guanine sample was shifted to 1649 cm−1 in Gua-HCl, Gua-HBr, and Gua-H2SO4. In Gua-NaOH, the peak was shifted to 1671 cm−1 with significant reduction in the intensity. In the crystals grown in acidic medium, emergence of new broader peaks above 3300 cm−1 confirmed the O–H stretching of water molecules. In Gua-NaOH, a broad hump with multiple peaks between 3600 and 3000 cm−1 was observed that can be attributed to the presence of water channel in the crystal structure. 1H NMR spectra of different acidic and basic guanine solutions were also recorded in D2O (Fig. S26). Under acidic conditions, a downfield shift was observed in the peaks corresponding to the C-H proton of guanine ring due to increased electron withdrawal effect. Conversely, upon deprotonation, an upfield shift confirmed the increase in the electron density on the ring.

Computational studies

Based on the experimental UV–visible spectra, the ground-state geometries of the guanine moieties at various pH values were optimized using density functional theory (DFT). Band gap calculations showed narrowing of the band gap in both protonation (Gua+) and deprotonation (Gua2−) states, as compared to neutral guanine (Fig. S27). The band gap of neutral guanine (5.34 eV) decreased to 4.33 eV upon protonation and to 3.72 eV under basic conditions. Theoretically calculated band gaps are in excellent agreement with the experimentally observed band gaps from UV–Visible spectra. For example, the calculated band gap for the neutral guanine is 5.34 eV while the UV–Vis absorption is at 245 nm corresponding to 5.06 eV. Further, geometry optimization of asymmetric units derived from SCXRD diffraction data of all crystals was carried out using B3LYP functional with 6–31 G(d) basis set60. The absence of imaginary frequencies was confirmed, indicating that the optimized structure corresponds to a true minimum on the potential energy surface. Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) calculations on the optimized structure of Gua-HCl crystal (Fig. 4a) reveal that the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) is predominantly localized on the chlorine atom, whereas the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) is mainly distributed over the purine ring (∆Eg = 3.23 eV). Similarly, in Gua-HBr crystal (Fig. S28), HOMO and LUMO are distributed over bromine and purine ring (∆Eg = 3.39 eV) respectively. Additionally, in Gua-NaOH (Fig. 4b), HOMO is centered on negatively charged Gua2- ring, however, LUMO is distributed over sodium cations and water molecules (∆Eg = 3.90 eV). These results confirm the possibility of a charge-transfer from the halide ions to the protonated purine ring under acidic conditions. On the other hand, in the NaOH system, charge transfer involves the guanine anion donating electron density toward the cations and solvents. This shows different mechanisms of charge transfer occurring depending on the counterion. Further, these optimized geometries were used to perform the reduced density gradient (RDG) analysis using Multiwfn61 and visualized with the help of VMD (visual molecular dynamics) molecular graphics viewer. The Figs. 4c and d display the noncovalent interaction (NCI) isosurfaces, whereas the scatter plots of RDG vs sign (λ2)ρ of Gua-HCl and Gua-NaOH are shown in Figs. 4e and f, respectively. Conclusion

In conclusion, the origin of emissive behavior of guanine in both solution as well as crystalline state is established through a combination of photophysical, crystallographic and computational studies. The guanine crystals grown from highly acidic and basic aqueous medium shows that both protonated and deprotonated guanine crystals showed emission in the visible region through a combination of charge-transfer and crystallization-induced emission pathways. SCXRD analysis revealed that in both cases, the incorporation of ions and water molecules significantly disrupted the π–π stacking arrangement of guanine layers, thereby suppressing the non-radiative pathways responsible for fluorescence quenching. FMO calculations further confirmed the charge-transfer characteristics of these systems. Although it is premature to directly correlate the emission of the laboratory-grown guanine crystals with the biogenic guanine crystals exhibiting visible light emission, the present studies can be vital in providing valuable insights into the fluorescence properties occurring in the bio world. The precise microenvironment of guanine crystals in such organisms remains poorly understood; however, recent reports suggest that strongly acidic conditions influence the early stages of guanine crystal growth in these systems60. In particular, the role of counterions in modulating the emission of guanine crystals in the visible region offers a promising clue toward identifying the underlying mechanisms in natural systems. Beyond their relevance to biogenic systems, these observations open new avenues for the rational design of guanine-based charge-transfer systems in biophotonics and related disciplines, thereby underscoring the broader significance of the present work.

Methods

Material and chemicals

Guanine (>98%) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Hydrochloric acid (36%, Merck), Sulfuric acid (Advent), Hydrobromic acid (SRL), Hydroiodic acid (ResearchLab), Nitric acid (RANKEM), NaOH pellets (Merck), KOH pellets (Merck), Ammonia Solution (25%, Merck), ortho-phosphoric acid (Merck).

All chemicals were used without any further purification.

Synthesis

Gua-HCl: 50 mM Guanine (98%, Sigma) was added to the acidic solution of HCl (1.34 M), and the solution was dissolved by heating at 60°C under constant stirring. Later, once the solution turned clear, it was left undisturbed. The needle-shaped crystals appeared in the solution after some time.

Gua-NaOH: 100 mM Guanine (98%, Sigma) was added to 2 M NaOH solution. After a few seconds, the solution became transparent, which was kept undisturbed until a big block-shaped transparent crystal appeared in the solution after 15 days.

Details of crystallization procedure under other acidic and basic conditions is provided in the supplementary methods section in the supporting information file.

Instrumentation

Ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy

All UV–vis spectra were recorded at room temperature (298 K) on an Agilent Cary 60 UV–vis spectrophotometer using a quartz cuvette (1 × 1 cm).

Fluorescence spectroscopy

Fluoromax-4p spectrofluorometer from Horiba JobinYvon (Model: FM-100) was used to record steady-state emission spectra of crystal and liquid samples. The slit width used was 2 nm × 2 nm for liquid while 5 nm × 5 nm for solid samples unless otherwise specified. All the spectra were corrected for the spectral sensitivity of the instrument. The spectra were recorded at 298 K.

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectra of guanine powder and all crystal samples were recorded using Bruker ALPHA II platinum ATR spectrometer in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 by performing 16 scans.

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM)

Field emission scanning electron microscopy was performed on Supra 55 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). All the samples were vacuum-dried and coated with 5 nm thick gold coating for scanning.

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM)

Confocal images of crystals were recorded with inverted confocal microscope (Olympus fluoView, model FV1200MPE, IX-83) and an oil immersion objective (100, 1.4 numerical aperture) using 405 nm laser source. Emission was recorded in the blue channel (410–490 nm). For imaging, concentrated acidic/basic solutions (10 μL) were drop cast on the glass cover slips and dried at room temperature. Olympus Fluoview (version 4.2) was used for visualization and processing of the images.

Single crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD)

SCXRD data were produced by using the Rigaku Oxford Supernova CCD diffractometer and SCXRD analysis was conducted at 293 K with monochromatic graphite Mo K (0.71073k). CrysalisPro software was used for data recording and reduction. Further, the crystal structure was solved using Olex2 software and visualized in Mercury software. SCXRD data of Gua-NaOH salt were collected on a Bruker SMART APEX II CCD diffractometer equipped with a graphite monochromator and an Mo Kα fine-focus sealed tube (λ = 0.71073 Å). Data integration was done using SAINT. Intensities of absorptions were corrected using SADABS. Structure solution and refinement were carried out using Bruker SHELXTL. The hydrogen atoms were refined isotropically, and all the other atoms were refined anisotropically. N–H and O–H hydrogens were located from difference electron density maps, and C–H hydrogens were fixed using the HFIX command in SHELXTL. Molecular graphics were prepared using X-SEED and Mercury licensed version 4.0. BFDH morphology of the crystal structures was calculated using Mercury licensed version 4.0.

Hirshfield surface58 and 2D fingerprint62 plots were generated using Crystal Explorer63 software to quantify non-covalently interactions involved to stabilize the crystal structure.

Theoretical calculations

All theoretical calculations were performed using Gaussian 09 software using density functional theory. The ground state optimized geometries along with HOMO/LUMO energies were calculated using B3LYP hybrid functional with 6–31 G + (d,p) basis set and water as solvent for guanine molecules at neutral, protonated and deprotonated state, and 6–31 G (d) basis set for crystal structures. The frequency calculations were performed and absence of any imaginary frequencies was verified. Reduced density gradient (RDG) analysis on the optimized geometries of crystals were performed using Multiwfn61 software and visualized with the help of Visual Molecular Dynamics64 (VMD) software.

Time-correlation single photon counting (TCSPC) spectroscopy and quantum yield measurements

The average fluorescence lifetime of guanine crystal samples was determined using a Horiba DeltaFlex™ time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC) spectrometer equipped with a 370 nm pulsed LED excitation source. Saturated aqueous guanine solutions were prepared and drop-cast onto pre-cleaned microscope glass slides. The samples were dried at ambient conditions, resulting in the in-situ formation of crystalline deposits. Fluorescence decay profiles were recorded at room temperature under ambient atmosphere. The instrument response function was measured separately for deconvolution of the decay curves. Lifetime values were obtained by fitting the decay profiles using a multi-exponential model and reconvolution analysis in the HORIBA DAS6 software suite. Owing to instrumental limitations, it was not possible to record the fluorescence lifetime decay for Gua-NaOH crystals. Fluorescence decay profiles of liquid samples were recorded on Horiba instrument (Fluorocube-01-NL) and 371 nm laser source was used for excitation. Curve fitting was performed on Data analysis software to get average lifetime values.
