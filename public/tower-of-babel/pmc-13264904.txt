Relationship of breathlessness with social isolation and loneliness: a nationally representative cohort study of older adults in England
Authors: Keir E J Philip, Feifei Bu, Sara C Buttery, Lisa Jane Brighton, Michael I Polkey, Nicholas S Hopkinson, Daisy Fancourt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBreathlessness is a common and distressing symptom impacting quality of life and limiting activities of daily living. Social isolation and loneliness are associated with increased morbidity and mortality, being problems in themselves and risk factors for poor health. Qualitative studies indicate breathlessness impacts social health; however, quantitative evaluation is limited. We aimed to assess the relationship of breathlessness with social isolation and loneliness in adults.MethodsUsing a nationally representative sample of older adults aged ≥50 years from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (N=6623). The sample had 44% males, mean age of 70 years (SD=10). We examined associations of baseline breathlessness (modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) breathlessness scale) with loneliness (3-item University of California and Los Angeles (UCLA) loneliness scale) and social isolation (low social contact, low community participation, living alone) at baseline and follow-up at 4 and 8 years later, using regression models adjusted for confounders.ResultsAt baseline, breathlessness was associated with higher levels of loneliness (coef.=0.161, 95% CI 0.113 to 0.209) and social isolation, including low social contact (coef.=0.094, 95% CI 0.028 to 0.159), low community participation (coef.=0.169, 95% CI 0.117 to 0.221) and living alone (OR 1.089, 95% CI 1.026 to 1.157). Longitudinally, breathlessness was associated with increasing loneliness and reducing social contact and community participation at 4-year and 8-year follow-up. Breathlessness was not associated with a change in living alone. Findings were independent of identified confounders.ConclusionsBreathlessness is related to increasing social isolation and loneliness, potentially due to limiting the amount and quality of social interactions. These findings suggest important psychosocial impacts of breathlessness requiring holistic management strategies.

Background

Breathlessness (dyspnoea) is a common and distressing symptom which can result from multiple, often coexistent, aetiologies including respiratory, cardiovascular, metabolic, oncological, haematological, psychological and neurological conditions.1 2 It is estimated that over one-third of older adults are affected by breathlessness,3 though prevalence estimates vary depending on population and definitions used. Breathlessness is a strong predictor of all-cause mortality4 and has a profound, multidimensional impact on quality of life and participation in activities of daily living.5 6

Qualitative research also highlights impacts of breathlessness on diverse aspects of social connections including both social isolation and loneliness.79 Social isolation refers to objective numbers of relationships and frequency of interactions with other people,10 while loneliness is the subjective experience of feeling inadequate meaningful connections, resulting from a perceived mismatch between actual and desired social connection.11 12 A substantial body of research now demonstrates credible causal relationships of social isolation and loneliness with increased morbidity and mortality,1214 including within populations where breathlessness is prevalent, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).15 Further, studies of correlates and consequences of isolation and loneliness in conditions where breathlessness is common consistently demonstrate increased risk of poorer physical and psychological health,1618 emergency hospital use1820 and mortality.2124

Breathlessness is likely related to social isolation and loneliness through complex direct and indirect pathways. First, breathlessness can limit an individual’s ability to engage in physical activity, including such activities that include social interaction. Over time, the avoidance of activities that result in the distressing symptom of breathlessness can lead to physical deconditioning, which in turn can worsen breathlessness, creating a downward spiral.25 This highlights the potential for bidirectionality in relation to pathways from breathlessness to poorer social health. Second, breathlessness is associated with poor mental health, including anxiety and depression, which in turn are related to social isolation and particularly loneliness.12 26 Third, factors such as stigma related to breathlessness may affect social confidence and contribute to social anxiety.27 Breathlessness can also contribute to communication difficulties, loss of intimacy in relationships and a feeling of not being understood by others.7 28 As such, breathlessness is relevant to structural factors that shape the amount and type of social activities a person can participate in (social isolation), and to the function of social relationships (loneliness). It is also possible that in addition to direct impacts of breathlessness on social factors, breathlessness could be a marker of chronic illnesses that negatively impact social health through other additional pathways. For example, people with chronic lung disease frequently experience breathlessness, with direct social impacts, but they may also experience cough and cough-related stigma, which may also have impacts on social isolation and loneliness.29

The concept that breathlessness could have detrimental impacts on social isolation and loneliness is supported by qualitative research, in which people living with breathlessness describe how it limits their engagement in social activities and roles, reduces social contact and contributes to restricted freedom and a shrinking lifeworld.8 However, quantitative research linking breathlessness to social isolation and loneliness is limited. A small number of studies have identified social isolation and loneliness as being prevalent in conditions where breathlessness is common, such as COPD30 31 and heart failure.32 In one study using a nationally representative sample of US older adults, those with COPD were more likely to report social isolation and loneliness than the general population.33 Similarly, the prevalence of isolation and loneliness was highest among people with COPD using supplemental oxygen, who are likely to represent some of the most breathless participants. In a study of 57 people with COPD on long-term oxygen therapy, social participation was found to be associated with dyspnoea which was measured using the modified Medical Resarch Council (mMRC) scale.34 However, the small size of the study, its representativeness, and potential for disease-related factors other than breathlessness limit confidence and generalisability of results.

Characterising the impact of breathlessness on social isolation and loneliness is fundamental to the development of evidence-based interventions and policies. Consequently, this study had the following aims:

To investigate the relationship of breathlessness with social isolation and loneliness in a representative sample of older adults in England.

We hypothesised that breathlessness would be related to higher levels of social isolation and loneliness cross-sectionally, and to increases in these factors over time, independent of identified health and demographic confounders.

Methods

We used data from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), which is a nationally representative longitudinal cohort study of community dwelling people aged ≥50 years old and their partners.35 ELSA started in 2002 with data collected every 2 years. Data collection in ELSA primarily consists of computer assisted interviews conducted in participants’ usual place of residence. Relevant variables for this analysis were measured in ELSA data collection waves 2 (2004/2005), 4 (2008/2009), 6 (2012/2013) and 8 (2016/2017). Wave 4 was used as the baseline as this contained all variables of interest, and enabled us to conduct a sensitivity analysis controlling for pre-baseline level of the exposure of interest (wave 2 breathlessness). For these analyses, core participants (n=6623) were used to ensure national representativeness.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research; however, it focuses on patient research priorities identified through the James Lind Alliance.36

Measures

Breathlessness

Breathlessness was measured using questions included in ELSA wave 2 and wave 4 that correspond to the mMRC respiratory questionnaire, one of the most commonly used tools to assess dyspnoea in both clinical practice and research.1 The questions ask what level of exertion results in breathlessness or breathlessness limiting activity. Responses to the questions in ELSA are scored from 0 to 3, with 0 referring to not being breathless except on strenuous exercise (mMRC 0); short of breath when hurrying on level ground or walking up a slight hill (mMRC 1); short of breath walking with other people of their own age on level ground (mMRC 2); having to stop for breath when walking at your own pace on level ground (mMRC 3). The full mMRC scale usually contains mMRC 4, too breathless to leave the house or breathless when dressing/undressing. These participants were identified in the wave 4 ELSA respiratory questionnaire, by responding that they were too breathless to do any of the activities described relating to mMRC 0–3. Due to the small number of participants classified as mMRC 4 in the sample, we collapsed mMRC 3 and 4 into a single category.

Social isolation

Social isolation was measured in three ways as in previous ELSA studies37 38 (low social contact, low community participation and living alone) to assess how breathlessness is related to different aspects of social isolation.

Low social contact

Low social contact was assessed using self-reported frequency of social interactions using various modalities. These were (a) face-to-face interaction, (b) telephone conversations or (c) email or written communication with (1) children, (2) other family members or (3) friends. One point was given per mode of communication with each group of people that an individual did not have contact with at least once per month. This was summed to provide an overall score from 0 to 9 with higher scores indicating lower social contact (more social isolation). The Cronbach’s alpha for this index was 0.784.

Low community participation

Low community participation was assessed using self-reported frequency of participation in community group activities (including political parties, trade unions or environmental groups, tenant groups, resident groups, neighbourhood watch groups, church or other religious groups, charitable associations, education, arts or music groups or evening classes, social clubs, sports clubs, exercise classes or any other organisations, clubs or societies), and engagement with community cultural activities (including going to museums, exhibitions, the theatre, concerts, opera or the cinema). Frequency of engagement with these groups and activities was measured as number in the past 12 months and then recoded as never (score of 4), one or two times a year (score of 3), every few months (score of 2) or monthly or more (score of 1). Frequency of cultural activities was never (score of 4), less than once a year (score of 3), one or two times a year (score of 2) or every few months or more (score of 1). These scores were then summed, providing an overall index of 2–8, with higher scores indicating higher levels of low community participation. The Cronbach’s alpha for this index was 0.778.

Living alone

Living alone was assessed as a binary variable corresponding to whether individuals lived alone or with others.

Loneliness

Loneliness was measured using an adapted 3-item version of the University of California and Los Angeles (UCLA) loneliness scale,39 extensively used in research, with strong psychometric properties.40 41 Respondents were asked how often they (1) felt that they lacked companionship, (2) felt left out and (3) felt isolated from the people around them. Response options were ‘hardly ever or never’ (scored as 1), ‘some of the time’ (scored as 2) and ‘often’ (scored as score of 3). The scores for each measure were summed to provide a loneliness score ranging from 3 to 9, where higher scores indicated higher levels of loneliness. Data for this measure were collected at each wave included in the analysis. The index had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83.

Covariates

Potential confounding variables were identified using directed acyclic graphs and then included as covariates. Demographic covariates included age (continuous), sex (male, female), ethnicity (white vs ethnic minority, grouped together as the number of ethnic minorities were low), educational attainment (no qualifications or National Vocational Qualification (NVQ) level 1, General Certificate of Education/Ordinary (GCE/O) Level, A level or other higher education and degree or above) and employment status (currently working full-time or part-time vs not working). Net non-pension wealth (in quintiles) was included as a robust indicator of socio-economic resources and living standards in the ELSA population.42 We also included health-related covariates, to investigate whether the relationship between breathlessness and social variables is independent of pre-existing health conditions that could have caused the symptoms. The health-related covariates were a binary variable indicating poor cognition; and a comorbidity variable derived from self-reported doctor diagnosis of any of the following: heart failure, heart attack, angina, other heart problems, diabetes, stroke, dementia, arthritis, Parkinson’s disease and chronic lung disease (such as chronic bronchitis and asthma). Baseline data for these covariates were used in the analyses.

Statistical analysis

Ordinary least squares regression models were used to estimate the cross-sectional and longitudinal relationships of baseline breathlessness with social isolation measures and loneliness, except for living alone, for which binary logistic regression was used (Aim 1). Models were built up with sequential addition of covariates. Model 1 included demographic covariates (age, sex, ethnicity, highest educational attainment, current employment status and wealth). Model 2 additionally included health-related covariates (medical diagnoses and cognitive impairment) to investigate Aim 2. Longitudinal analyses also included adjustment for the relevant outcome variable at baseline (Model 3). Missing data were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations using all predictor variables within the analyses from wave 4 (baseline) onwards, which provided 50 imputed datasets. Missingness of data are shown in online supplemental table 6. Results using unimputed models were materially similar (online supplemental table 5), so imputed models were used for greater statistical power. Model assumptions were tested. For linear regressions of breathlessness on loneliness, residuals were positively skewed (right-shifted) and the Breusch-Pagan test suggested heteroskedasticity (p<0.001). For models of breathlessness with low social contact and low community participation, residuals were approximately normal and heteroskedasticity tests were non-significant (Breusch-Pagan p=0.535 and p=0.494, respectively). Across all three linear models, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were <2.5, suggesting no problematic multicollinearity. For the logistic regression assessing the relationship of breathlessness with living alone, the link test suggested no major specification error (squared predicted value p=0.239). Multicollinearity was low (all VIFs <2.5). Model fit was acceptable on the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p=0.065), and discrimination was acceptable with an area under the curve of 0.7555 (95% CI 0.741 to 0.770). Given the non-normality and heteroskedasticity in the loneliness model, we report robust SEs for all regressions to ensure consistent, assumption-robust inference.

Aim 1: breathlessness, social isolation and loneliness

After adjusting for demographic and health-related factors, at baseline, breathlessness was associated with increased loneliness (coef.=0.161, p<0.001) and social isolation, including low social contact (coef.=0.094, p=0.005), low community participation (coef.=0.169, p<0.001) and living alone (OR 1.089, p=0.005) (table 2, figure 1). Longitudinally, also adjusting for baseline level of the outcome of interest, breathlessness was associated with increasing loneliness and reducing social contact and community participation at 4-year and 8-year follow-up. Breathlessness was not found to be related to change in cohabitation status (living alone) in our analyses (table 2, figure 1).

Aim 2: independence of associations from pre-existing health conditions

In models adjusting for self-reported health conditions at baseline (table 2, Model 2), relationships remained statistically significant, though the size of coefficients decreased, suggesting a contribution to the relationships observed from pre-existing health conditions.

Aim 3: independence of associations from baseline breathlessness

When controlling for pre-baseline level of the measure of interest, results were not substantially different, except for the cross-sectional relationship of breathlessness with low social contact and living alone, which was no longer present and change in community participation over time was slightly attenuated (online supplemental table 4).

Aim 4: differential effects by age group and sex

Analysing people aged 65 years and above, and those below 65, separately demonstrated similar results (online supplemental table 2), with some attenuation of results relating to people aged below 65 years for low social contact, low community participation and living alone, likely related to reduced statistical power in the subgroup analysis. Analysing male and female participants separately also provided similar results. For male participants, changes in community participation over time and cross-sectional cohabitation status were slightly attenuated (online supplemental table 3). We used interaction terms to assess if coefficients differed significantly in these subgroup analyses. There was no evidence of effect modification by age or sex in either cross-sectional or longitudinal analyses examining the association between baseline (wave 4) breathlessness and loneliness, low social contact, or low community participation (all interaction p>0.05). For living alone there was no evidence of effect modification cross-sectionally, or from baseline (wave 4) to wave 8. The only exception was a statistically significant interaction by sex for the association between baseline breathlessness and living alone at wave 6 (interaction OR 0.77, p=0.019); however, sex-stratified estimates were similar in magnitude and not statistically significant, suggesting no meaningful difference between groups.

Missingness increases over sequential waves, primarily due to attrition related to loss to follow-up. To explore for potential selection bias, we compared baseline characteristics between people with at least one follow-up measurement (for loneliness) with those with none. Participants lost to follow-up were older, had lower wealth, educational attainment, less likely to be working and were in poorer health, including more breathlessness, cognitive impairment, long-term health conditions and higher levels of loneliness and social isolation (online supplemental table 7).

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship of breathlessness with current and future social isolation and loneliness in a nationally representative sample of older adults in England. We found that higher levels of breathlessness were related to higher levels of social isolation and loneliness cross-sectionally and over time. People who were more breathless were more lonely, had less frequent contact with family and friends and less frequent participation in community activities. Breathlessness was related to living alone cross-sectionally, but not to changes in cohabitation status over time. These findings suggest important psychosocial impacts of breathlessness requiring targeted and holistic management strategies.

To our knowledge, ours is the first study to focus on the relationship of breathlessness with social isolation and loneliness, on this scale, in a representative population sample. Nonetheless, our findings align with existing qualitative research in this area, in which people describe how breathlessness physically restricts their participation in social activities, which has been found here in relation to frequencies of participation assessed in the social isolation measures of social contact and community participation. The associations with loneliness found here may also reflect qualitative research findings relating to breathlessness impacting the perceived quality, or function, of relationships. For example, people with breathlessness describe it restricting their ability to physically participate in social activities,9 how it can contribute to low mood, avoidance and disengagement,8 as well as feeling that the friends, family and public do not always understand what it is like to live with breathlessness.43 These various impacts interact, in what Brighton et al28 describe as a ‘triple threat’ of disruption, incorporating physical restrictions, psychological reactions and societal unawareness.

Our results also show associations between breathlessness and progressive loss of social contacts in particular over time, supporting qualitative research that suggests breathlessness leads to progressive loss of social activities and support, as participants describe losing their role within a family and lose intimacy in personal relationships including sex,6 7 44 45 highlighting impacts on both the amount and quality of interactions. This worsening over time suggests that existing models of care are insufficiently addressing these impacts. This is further supported by a systematic review of qualitative literature exploring experiences of breathlessness highlighted that study participants frequently discussed how breathlessness services often fail to consider or provide for social and psychological needs.7

In analyses adjusting for pre-baseline breathlessness, there was attenuation of the cross-sectional relationship of breathlessness with low social contact and living alone. This suggests these relationships may, in part, reflect other long-standing limitations that are associated with breathlessness in keeping with previous research on disability, chronic illness and social isolation and loneliness.46

The findings of our study likely relate to multiple, complex, inter-related pathways. Established understandings of vicious cycles between breathing, thinking and functioning in chronic breathlessness have previously been described qualitatively.47 As one example mechanism, breathlessness may worsen symptoms of depression and depression may exacerbate perceptions of breathlessness.26 However, in-depth analysis of potential causal pathways, and modulating factors between breathlessness and social outcomes, would be particularly valuable to inform future intervention development. These research findings have important implications for public health policy. Breathlessness could be used as a psychosocial risk indicator as is increasingly the case for pain, multimorbidity and frailty. As such breathlessness, which is easy to measure and highly prevalent in older populations, could be used in public health approaches including screening, targeting holistic interventions and integrated care.

Strengths of our analyses include use of a large, well characterised, nationally representative sample and accounting for a comprehensive range of potential confounders and demonstrating robustness through sensitivity analyses. We have also investigated these relationships over many years, considering loneliness and multiple measures of social isolation, to enable a nuanced understanding of potential social consequences of breathlessness. However, certain limitations should be considered. Causality cannot be assumed given the observational nature of this study. However, as discussed, there are logical potential mechanisms that would support a causal interpretation, supported by previous qualitative research. Our sample is limited to adults aged 50 and above in England, therefore the extent to which findings apply to younger participants, or other countries, is not clear and warrants future exploration. Additionally, although broadly nationally representative, the proportion of ethnically white participants is higher than in the wider English population. Furthermore, accurate data on conditions contributing to breathlessness, or their severity and treatments, are not available in this dataset. Therefore, although relationships were materially similar when comorbidities were controlled for or not, it is not possible to fully separate breathlessness from the underlying conditions causing breathlessness, in relation to social isolation and loneliness. It is possible that psychosocial impacts of breathlessness differ by contributing medical conditions or medications taken, however this data were not available. Understanding these phenomena in participants with specific, well characterised diagnoses would be of value. In addition, established, accepted, objective physiological measures of breathlessness are lacking or limited in their ability to be applied at scale. Breathlessness is a complex and subjective symptom that can be measured in different ways. The mMRC focuses on exercise limitation due to chronic breathlessness, which is important. However, other measures of breathlessness such as the multidimensional dyspnoea profile, or the dyspnoea-12 also assess other constructs such as the affective components of breathlessness. As such, the mMRC is most directly relevant to the impact of breathlessness on structural factors that might impact the amount or type of social connections, reflected in measures of social isolation, but less likely to directly measure the subjective qualities which are potentially more relevant for loneliness. The mMRC also relies on participant recall, rather than assessment during a specific activity or physical task, as such there is potential for inaccuracies in recollection. However, the mMRC is one of the most widely used measures for breathlessness in both clinical and research contexts. Similarly, social isolation and loneliness are also complex, and the measures used in this study are limited in scope. That said, by using three different measures of social isolation enables a slightly more nuanced appreciation of different aspects. The wider social and cultural context in which breathlessness and psychosocial factors interact is likely to be important. The data in this analysis did not include the COVID-19 pandemic, where social distancing and related measures are likely to have contributed to social isolation and loneliness, and through reductions in physical activity and conditions such as long covid, breathlessness may have also increased. Future research on the relationship of breathlessness with social isolation and loneliness during and after the COVID-19 pandemic would be of interest, as would exploring these relationships in different countries.

The relationship between breathlessness with social isolation and loneliness could also be investigated using other statistical approaches such as repeated measures or fixed effects models. However, the mMRC was only assessed in waves 2 and 4 of ELSA, and the main aim was to investigate whether baseline breathlessness was associated with social isolation and loneliness cross-sectionally and at specific time points of follow-up. As such, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression was used in the current study. Future research using other statistical approaches, if appropriate measures were available, may provide additional insights into these relationships.

Conclusions

This study suggests that breathlessness contributes to increased social isolation and loneliness in older adults in England. These relationships are likely to include both structural and functional factors modulating both the ability to attend and participate in social activities, and to the perceived quality of those interactions, with clear and consistent associations observed for both social isolation and loneliness measures. These findings add to our understanding of the psychosocial dimensions of chronic disease symptoms and emphasise the importance of holistic approaches to breathlessness assessment and management. Our findings support the importance of taking a needs-based (rather than diagnosis based) approach in considering models of care for people with breathlessness.48
