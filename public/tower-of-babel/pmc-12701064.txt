Revealing the topological nature of entangled orbital angular momentum states of light
Authors: Robert de Mello Koch, Pedro Ornelas, Neelan Gounden, Bo-Qiang Lu, Isaac Nape, Andrew Forbes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Topology has emerged as a fundamental property of many systems yet mostly limited to low dimensions. Here, we reveal the hidden topology in entangled states carrying orbital angular momentum (OAM), in arbitrary dimensions. For two-dimensional systems, we demonstrate multiple skyrmion topologies and their equivalence to ’t Hooft-Polyakov magnetic monopoles, experimentally connecting them to the Higgs field. In higher dimensions, we use non-Abelian gauge fields of SU(d) Yang-Mills theory to predict a rich tapestry of topological maps and their invariants, which we confirm experimentally for dimensionality up to seven, showing an underlying topology of 48 dimensions and a topological spectrum spanning over 17000 invariants. In addition to inducing robustness to perturbation, the topological spectrum enables probing them, by observing their emergent signatures in its non-topological spaces. The only degree of freedom we use to construct the topology is OAM, breaking away from the optical paradigm of polarisation-based spin-textured fields and forgoing the need for quantum state engineering. Our theoretical framework can be extrapolated to any dimension and degree of freedom, opening a distinct path for finding topologies in light.

Introduction

Topology has proven a powerful tool by which to characterise and understand complex systems. Although often associated with physical objects1, such as the equivalence of coffee mugs and donuts, it can manifest in many diverse systems, including cosmology2, condensed matter3, high-energy physics4,5, quantum states6, acoustic7,8 and water9 waves. An emerging development has been the observation of optical topologies10, observed as Skyrmions in evanescent waves11, spin-textured vectorial light12, non-paraxial light13, and quantum entangled photons14. The topology of all such systems defines a map from one space to another, captured by a topological number, e.g., a Chern number in condensed matter or a Skyrme number in waves, that remains invariant to smooth deformations15,16. This notion of topological resilience has proven very powerful across diverse fields, including information robustness17, storage and transfer18, entanglement protection19, light steering20, as well as finding unintended applications such as the development of neuromorphic computing architectures by mimicking biological synapses21.

Moving beyond low-dimensional systems d < 3 characterised by a single topological number has many benefits22–25. Advances in this direction include three-dimensional knots in crystals26, two-dimensional plasmonic quasicrystals governed by four-dimensional vectors27, axions in three-dimensional photonic crystals28 using three-dimensional space and a free parameter to map to a four-dimensional hypersphere29–31. Though it has been suggested theoretically that combining space and time could facilitate a true four-dimensional topology32,33, this is yet to be realised.

Here we report the discovery of the rich topological structure in orbital angular momentum (OAM) entangled states, where the topology we uncover is from a direct characterisation of the entangled photon state itself, i.e., it is a fundamental feature of the physics of our spatial entanglement. We show that topology can be found in the same degree of freedom of two entangled particles, allowing us to demonstrate the existence of Skyrmion topology using only the OAM of light, and show their equivalence to ’t Hooft-Polyakov magnetic monopoles, experimentally connecting our topological texture to the Higgs field. Thereafter, we harness OAM qudits to generate high- dimensional (d > 2) quantum states that inherently support complex high-dimensional manifolds. We show that these high-dimensional manifolds host independent embedded spheres, each serving a role analogous to the Bloch sphere, with its own distinct wrapping number. We leverage the language of non-Abelian gauge fields in SU(d) Yang-Mills theory to predict topologies in d2 − 1 dimensional manifolds with a spectrum of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(\begin{array}{c}{d}^{2}-1\\ 3\end{array}\right)$$\end{document}d2−13 possible mappings, each associated with a wrapping number. We confirm the theory experimentally, reaching topological manifolds in 48 dimensions, with signatures of beyond 17000 topological numbers, the highest dimensional topological signatures ever reported. We find that the non-topological (trivial) part of the spectrum shows emergent topology in the presence of perturbation, while the topological (non-trivial) part remains largely unchanged, opening an exciting path to simultaneous resilience to, and probing of, complex channels. Our experimental results are complemented by a comprehensive theoretical framework in the Supplementary Information, allowing extensions of this work to other degrees of freedom and particles.

RESULTS

To reveal the topology of high-dimensional spaces, we first express the topology in a single degree of freedom (DoF) with unlimited dimensionality, and then show theoretically and experimentally that beyond two dimensions, single topological numbers no longer suffice and instead the high-dimensional space is characterised by a topological spectrum. We use in our study quantum entangled states in OAM as a means to access these high-dimensional spaces, stressing that the theoretical treatment that follows can be extended to other mechanisms and DoFs with relative ease.

In our first step, we break the paradigm of two DoFs for optical Skyrmions and show that they can be constructed from a single DoF, which we do first with d = 2 dimensional qubit states.

Our analysis, though framed in two-dimensional OAM states, extends naturally to higher-dimensional OAM spaces, where topological charges generalise to a spectrum of charges for SU(d). This parallels Yang-Mills-Higgs theory, where monopole charges arise from distinct SU(2) embeddings within SU(d)37,38. We show that these embeddings correspond directly to the various S2 → S2 mappings in the higher-dimensional topological space of our states.

Topology in high dimensional OAM spaces

Higher-dimensional spaces possess a spectrum of topological invariants arising from the winding numbers of maps to distinct embedded submanifolds. To systematically uncover these submanifolds, we establish a theoretical framework leveraging the Lie algebra of SU(d) as a powerful tool. This approach generalises the Pauli projections used in Stokes measurements for qubits to higher-dimensional analogous Gell-Mann projections for d = 3, and more generally, projections onto the SU(d) basis {Ta} a = 1, 2, ⋯ , d2 − 1 defining a generalised Lie algebra (GLA) vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${m}_{a}=\left\langle \psi ({\bar{r}}_{A})\right\vert {T}_{a}\left\vert \psi ({\bar{r}}_{A})\right\rangle$$\end{document}ma=ψ(r¯A)Taψ(r¯A). Within this framework, the topological structure emerges from an intrinsic manifold of d2 − 1 dimensions. The reason for the appearance of both disks and spheres is that, topologically, the infinite two-dimensional plane can be associated either with a sphere S2 or a disk D2, depending on the class of functions under consideration. If we restrict to functions that approach a fixed value at infinite radius, all points at infinity can be identified, and a one-point compactification maps the plane to a sphere. In contrast, if the functions retain nontrivial θ-dependence even at infinite radius, the boundary cannot be collapsed to a single point, and the topology is that of a disk.

As we saw in the case of the two-dimensional quantum state of the preceding part, the relevant observables are given by the Pauli-spin matrices, which form a basis for su(2) and determine the components of the state vector, whose tip moves along the surface of the Bloch sphere. Similarly, for a d-dimensional state, any triple {mi, mj, mk} selected from the spatially varying GLA vector defines a vector whose tip traces points on an embedded two-dimensional submanifold. Maps between spheres can exhibit non-trivial topology, characterised by a wrapping number N that counts how many times one sphere covers the other. This leads to a spectrum of topological invariants, potentially one per embedded submanifold, with their number growing as the quantum state dimension d increases. Defining the topological invariants reduces to identifying the triples (mi, mj, mk) that generate meaningful submanifolds. With d2 − 1 available components mi,j,k, there are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(\begin{array}{c}{d}^{2}-1\\ 3\end{array}\right)$$\end{document}d2−13 possible mappings, while the subset of non-trivial and independent invariants depends on the specific quantum state. Which submanifolds embedded within SU(d) to select? As argued in the Supplementary Information (See “Topological invariants for Qudit (any d) states" section), this can be answered by leveraging the identity between the Higgs potential in the vacuum sector of Yang-Mills-Higgs theory and the density matrix. This identification arises from the fact that the triplet {mi, mj, mk} defines a Higgs field in the vacuum sector, whose wrapping number precisely matches the magnetic charge of the corresponding ’t Hooft-Polyakov monopole. In Yang-Mills-Higgs theory, monopoles carry a spectrum of charges, each linked to distinct embeddings of SU(2) subgroups within the larger SU(d) gauge group37,38. Under our framework, these different embeddings correspond directly to the many possible S2 → S2 mappings.

Our theory predicts the properties of the topological spectrum for any given state, in any dimension, and posits that complex high-dimensional quantum states can be classified by their topological spectrum. Towards higher dimensions

The predicted growth in the size of the topological spectrum with dimension is dramatic as shown in Fig. 6a. While the specific non-zero and independent invariants depend on the quantum state, this scaling highlights the rapidly increasing complexity of the topological spectrum. We confirm this in Fig. 6b, c where we show experimentally measured spectra corresponding to d = 5 and d = 7, for topological manifolds of dimension 24 and 48, respectively. We observe 2024 candidate invariants for d = 5, rising to 17,296 for d = 7, a clear indication that this approach is highly scalable. These results represent the highest dimensions reported in any topological system.

DISCUSSION

Intriguingly, using OAM entangled photons to access high-dimensional spaces has been extensively explored42, but the topological features seem to have been hidden from view. Here we have shown that ubiquitous spatial entanglement, such as OAM correlations from SPDC, has an underlying topological tapestry that is very rich, with potentially many thousands of signatures to be found. In general, there will be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{2}d(d-1)({d}^{2}-3)$$\end{document}12d(d−1)(d2−3) non-zero topological numbers in a given spectrum, and have deduced that for OAM states, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{4}d(d-1)(d-2)(d+3)$$\end{document}14d(d−1)(d−2)(d+3) of these invariants are independent (see “Topological invariants for Qudit (any d) states" section in the Supplementary Information). Could this exciting potential be harnessed as a topological alphabet (which in general is not orthogonal) for quantum communication? To do so will require the development of quantum operators, gates and detectors that are topology specific, none of which presently exist, a situation that can be likened to the state of OAM as a resource two decades ago. There is potential for sensing and robustness simultaneously, as we have shown in the experimental example of robustness of the topological spectrum to environmental noise while probing for emergent topology in the initially trivial part of the spectrum, the latter due to the introduction of entanglement into new subspaces.

In the quantum context, both dimensionality and topology have attractive benefits. The harnessing of topology in quantum systems is mooted to overcome the fragility of such systems to external perturbation, facilitating the robust sharing and storing of quantum information43,44. Higher dimensions in quantum states too has benefits, such as noise resilience, higher bounds for eavesdropping and security against cloning45. The mutual benefits are very attractive and remain to be explored, particularly in cases where information must be distributed by entanglement as a resource, such as in teleportation, quantum networks, and quantum computers.

It is possible that this work has scope to expand even further, for instance, the existence of other invariants not yet discovered, which could distinguish states beyond those we have identified, and the ability to shape the topological spectrum by SPDC pump shaping46. Note that in our work, the topology is a property of the entanglement itself, the map from the space of one photon to the other, and as such it has no direct resemblance to quantum simulation with synthetic dimensions of condensed matter phenomena3, e.g., by emulating the Harper-Hofstadter Hamiltonian by some lattice and an interaction through a tight-binding model47. Moving to higher dimensions in such systems also gives rise to a spectrum of topological invariants, experimentally observed in d = 4 for additional Chern numbers48, so there are similarities in the underlying physics that deserve to be explored. OAM too has proven useful here, for instance, simulating 2D topological physics with 1D coupled OAM cavities49 and used as a building block for quantum devices50. But in our work, we are not realising interesting physics in an analogue system that would require some interpretation between the two: the topology we uncover is a direct characterisation of the entangled photon state itself, not emulating other systems but rather probing the intrinsic structure of our physical system directly. Further, our work offers a unique approach to accessing Non-Abelian gauge fields51, a powerful tool for studying complex physical systems52. Our direct link of the OAM biphoton’s topology to the monopole charge and Higgs field texture opens different directions to explore this link further.

In conclusion, our report represents a unique realisation of skyrmionic states with just the OAM degree of freedom, derived directly from the spatial entanglement without any quantum state engineering. Our generalisation to high dimensions has shown a unique paradigm to achieve high-dimensional topological states of light, necessitating a new theoretical and experimental framework, while introducing the notion of a topological spectrum, which we have experimentally confirmed with the highest reported dimensions and topological maps to date. The implication is a rich topological signature for identifying high-dimensional quantum states, potentially opening unique paths to communication and sensing with topology.
