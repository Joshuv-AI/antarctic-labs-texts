Study on the synthesis and biological activity assessment of kojic acid sulfide schiff base
Authors: Chao Yang, Junyuan Tang, Dahan Wang, Sheng Tao, Jinbing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, twenty-six novel kojic acid derivatives were designed and synthesized based on the strategy of preserving kojic acid’s active site for interaction with tyrosinase and the principle of active fragment assembly. Among all the synthesis compounds, compound 6a exhibited the most powerful inhibitory effect with an IC50 value of 1.43 ± 0.39 µM, over 18-fold more potent than the parent compound kojic acid (IC50 = 26.09 ± 0.05 µM). The findings from the mechanism of action study suggested that the compound 6a functions as a competitive inhibitor, exhibiting a Ki value of 0.84 µM. The copper ion chelation and fluorescence quenching experiments further demonstrated that 6a interacts with the Cu²+ active center of tyrosinase, thereby influencing its structural conformation and diminishing its intrinsic fluorescence. This phenomenon exemplifies a dynamic quenching mechanism. Moreover, compound 6a displayed superior anti-browning effect on freshly cut potatoes than that of kojic acid and VC. The finally in vitro antioxidant activity experiments showed that compound 6j had good clearance ability for DPPH with IC50 value of 0.57 ± 0.17 µM, and compound 6 h had good clearance ability for ABTS+ with IC50 value of 0.01 ± 0.0004 µM. Most compounds demonstrated stronger α-glucosidase inhibition than the positive control acarbose, and the compound 6d with fluorine atom at para-position showed the best inhibitory activity with an IC50 value of 2.47 ± 1.01 µM. All compounds demonstrated stronger AChE inhibition than the positive control donepezil. The results suggested that the kojic acid sulfide Schiff base derivatives may have potential multi-target inhibitors for various enzymes and further experimental research should be carried out to validate their efficacy by exploring its potential therapeutic applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23523-9.

Tyrosinase (EC 1.14.18.1) is a copper-containing polyphenol oxidase that plays a crucial role in various biochemical processes1. The hydrophobic amino acid residues located at the core of the active site contribute to the formation of a hydrophobic cavity, in which the binuclear copper ion resides at its deepest point, serving as the specific binding site for tyrosinase’s substrate2,3. It is well-known that, tyrosinase is a key enzyme for melanin pigment biosynthesis with the function of catalyzing two different reactions, including the conversion of monophenols to o-diphenols (monophenolase) and the conversion of o-diphenols to o-quinones (diphenolase). Melanin is the pigment responsible for skin and hair colour in mammals4–6. Normal melanin production can not only protect human skin from ultraviolet rays, but also reduce the damage of many toxic and harmful substances. However, the production of abnormal amounts of melanin would cause aesthetic problem in humans and hyperpigmentary disorders, such as postinflammatory, hyperpigmentation, maturational dyschromia, periorbital hyperpigmentation, melasma age spots, freckles and melanoma7,8. In addition, tyrosinase also catalyzes the neuro-melanin production by the oxidation of dopamine to the dopaquinone species and the excessive formation of dopaquinones leads to neuronal damage and cell death, thereby suggesting that tyrosinase is responsible for the neuro-degeneration associated with Parkinson’s and Huntington’s diseases9. Tyrosinase is a crucial enzyme that facilitates the browning reaction in fruits and vegetables, thereby significantly diminishing their nutritional value and commercial viability10. Consequently, inhibiting tyrosinase activity is the most effective strategy to mitigate the degradation of nutritional value in fresh fruits and vegetables11. Finally, recent studies have shown that tyrosinase is also specifically connected to three metabolic processes in insects, namely cuticle sclerotization, protective encapsulation, and melanization of invading organisms as well as wound healing12,13. Considering the key roles of tyrosinase as shown above, the development of tyrosinase inhibitors has attracted great interest from researchers worldwide. Up to now, many tyrosinase inhibitors from both natural and synthetic sources have been reported; however, their application is very limited due to several drawbacks including toxicity, lack of stability, off-odours, and economic unsustainability14,15.

The enzyme alpha-glucosidase (EC.3.2.1.20) holds a significant importance in the hydrolysis of carbohydrates. α-Glucosidase, located in intestine, hydrolysis these oligosaccharides to monosaccharides which enter the blood stream and cause hyperglycemia16. Therefore, inhibiting this enzyme is a key target for developing more powerful and less toxic hypoglycemic agents17,18. Several drugs that have been established in the management of T2DM belong to this class as acarbose, miglitol and voglibose. Nevertheless, the undesirable gastrointestinal side effects that are associated with the existing α-glucosidase inhibitors as flatulence, diarrhea, stomach ache and liver damage, limiting their long term use19,20. Effective methods for treating diabetes are becoming increasingly important. Acetlycholinesterase (AChE, EC 3.1.1.7) catalyzes hydrolyzing the neurotransmitter acetylcholine into choline and acetate, cholinergic receptors are abundant in healthy central nervous system (CNS). The loss of cholinergic neurons in cortex which leads to deficiency in Ach, which is the main cause of neurodegenerative AD. Development of novel cholinesterase inhibitors (AChEIs) is one of the strategies for the treatment of AD 21. Tyrosinase catalyzes the melanin and catecholamine synthesis pathways. Some studies highlight that the accumulation of neuromelanin over time can function as a pool of ROS and may trigger the neuronal immune system, resulting in neurodegeneration21. The reactive oxygen species (ROS) are a group of molecules which can be categorized as free radicals such as hydroxyl radical, cationic radicals, and non-radicals namely hydrogen peroxide, superoxide and nitric oxide. Oxidative stress is generated by ROS and defined as disruption of the balance between antioxidant and oxidant in favor of oxidant side22. They have an important role in a variety of diseases diabetes, cancer, cardiovascular and auto-immune diseases23. Moreover, the excessive ROS leads to serious neurological disorders such as Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD)24. It is recently reported that there is an overlapping pathological pathway relating T2DM and AD which is the insulin resistance, this type of metabolic resistance leads to oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation25. Antioxidants are the substances that can scavenge radicals to cure the damage caused by these free radicals and thus protect from oxidative stress26.

Kojic acid is currently prevalent tyrosinase inhibitor utilized in the cosmetics industry, however, it accompanies with numerous disadvantages including severe skin irritation and demonstrates mutagenic properties in mammalian cells as well as suboptimal therapeutic efficacy and unideal stability of its formulations27,28. Consequently, design and synthesis of kojic acid derivatives or analogues have emerged as a prominent research strategy for the development of novel tyrosinase inhibitors with improved biological activities and minimal side effects29–31. The hydroxyl group at position 5 and the ketone carbonyl group at position 4 of kojic acid serve as critical binding sites within the active site of the tyrosinase enzyme32. A comprehensive review of pertinent literature and an analysis of our prior research findings reveal that compounds featuring thioether and Schiff base moieties demonstrate pronounced inhibitory effects on tyrosinase activity33,34. In addition, Kojic acid derivatives exhibit significant antioxidant activity35, α-glucosidase inhibitory activity36, and anti-browning properties30, along with potential therapeutic effects against Alzheimer’s disease37. Considering this, Thus, we report here the synthesis and biological evaluation of kojic acid sulfide Schiff base derivatives as a multi-functional agent using in food chemistry field and pharmaceutical field. Subsequently, their structure-activity relationships regarding the inhibition of mushroom tyrosinase were primarily discussed. Besides, the inhibition mechanism, the mode of action, the antioxidant activity, α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity, the anti-browning effect were also systematically investigated.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of Kojic acid sulfide schiff base derivatives (6a–6z)

Compound 2 was synthesized by reference method34. A mixture of compound 2, 4-Aminothiophenol, Potassium carbonate in DMF was stirred at room temperature for 8 h. Saturated ammonium chloride solution was added to the above reaction mixture and extracted with ethyl acetate. The organic phase was combined and dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated under reduced pressure. After evaporation, the crude product was purified by the chromatographic column to obtain compound 4 as yellow solid. Compound 4 and different substituted benzaldehyde were dissolved in absolute ethyl alcohol. The resulting mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 h. The mixture was filtered and recrystallized with DCM/PE (VDCM : VPE = 3:7) to obtain the target product. The reaction yields for derivatives 6a–6z ranged from 37.62 to 75.58%. The synthetic route for the target compounds was shown in Scheme 1. The synthesized compounds have been characterized by 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR, HRMS. In 1H-NMR spectrum, =CH proton appeared as singlet at about 8.36–8.76 ppm. Signals in the range of 6.26–8.05 ppm region belong to aromatic, kojic acid or thiazol protons. In 13C-NMR spectrum, the signal appeared at about 193 ppm was assigned to the C = N.

Tyrosinase inhibitory activity

All kojic acid sulfide Schiff base derivatives were assessed for their inhibitory activity against mushroom tyrosinase in vitro. The IC50 values of all derivatives, along with the positive control drug kojic acid, were presented in Table 1. As shown in Table 1, all kojic acid thioether Schiff bases exhibit good tyrosinase inhibitory activity, with IC50 values ranging between 1.43 ± 0.39 µM and 53.28 ± 0.24 µM. Most compounds demonstrated stronger inhibitory activity than the positive control kojic acid. Among them, the unsubstituted compound 6a showed the best inhibitory activity, with an IC50 value of 1.43 ± 0.39 µM, which is 18 times more potent than kojic acid. By comparing the tyrosinase inhibitory activities of all the synthesis compounds, the following structure-activity relationship (SAR) conclusions can be drawn: (1) The presence of substituents on the aromatic aldehyde leads to a decrease in inhibitory activity. (2) When halogen atoms are present on the aromatic ring, the inhibitory activity of ortho-substituted compounds is stronger than that of para- and meta-substituted compounds. Moreover, the inhibitory activities of para- or meta-substituted halogenated compounds are weaker than that of the positive control kojic acid. For compound with other substituent, para-substituted compound exhibit better inhibitory activity than their ortho- or meta-substituted compound. (3) Among the alkyl-substituted compounds, compound 6q shows the best activity. All para-alkyl-substituted compounds exhibit superior inhibitory activity compared to other positions substituted compounds. Comparing the inhibitory activities of compounds 6 m, 6o, and 6q, the activity gradually increases with the increase of substituent group size, which may be related to the hydrophobicity of the compounds. However, compound 6p has the weakest inhibitory activity in this series, suggesting that when the alkyl group is located in the meta position, steric hindrance plays a dominant role. (4) When the hydroxyl group is replaced by an alkoxy group, the inhibitory activity of the corresponding compounds improves, further indicating that hydrophobicity has a significant influence on the tyrosinase inhibitory activity of these compounds. (5) For the compounds with electron-withdrawing groups at the para position of the aromatic ring, the inhibitory activity is better than that of other para-substituted compounds (except for compound 6q). However, ortho-substituted compounds with electron-withdrawing groups exhibit weaker inhibitory activity compared to other ortho-substituted compounds.

Mechanism of tyrosinase inhibition by compound 6a

As shown in Table 1, compound 6a exhibited the strongest tyrosinase inhibitory activity and was therefore selected as the template molecule for further investigation of its inhibition kinetics. The inhibitory effect of compound 6a on mushroom tyrosinase-mediated oxidation of L-DOPA was evaluated using a Lineweaver-Burk double reciprocal plot. The Lineweaver-Burk plots yielded a series of straight lines intersecting in the first quadrant (Fig. 1A). As the concentration of compound 6a increased, the apparent Km value rose, whereas the Vm remained unchanged. Based on the characteristics of the enzyme inhibition reaction curves, compound 6a was identified as a competitive inhibitor, competing with the substrate L-DOPA for binding to the enzyme’s active site to form an inhibitor-tyrosinase complex, thereby inhibiting tyrosinase activity. Secondary plotting of the enzyme inhibition reaction curves (Fig. 1B) allowed the determination of the dissociation constant (Ki) between compound 6a and tyrosinase as 0.1142 µM. These results show that compound 6a binds reversibly to the active site of tyrosinase, directly competing with L-DOPA for occupancy and thereby inhibiting catalytic activity38.

Metal-chelating ability of compound 6a

The ability of compound 6a to form chelating bond with the copper ion located in the active site of tyrosinase was evaluated. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the absorption band of compound 6a changed significantly in the range of 280–400 nm with the increase of copper ion concentration, accompanied by its maximum absorption wavelength shifts to longer wavelength, which indicated that compound 6a engages in chelation reaction with the copper ion, resulting in the formation of corresponding complexes. This result demonstrated compound 6a could complex with the copper ion active center of tyrosinase, which accounts for its inhibition of enzymatic activity. Additionally, absorbance measurements for varying concentrations of compound 6a (Fig. 2B) revealed that absorbance values fluctuated with the change of copper ion concentration, reaching equilibrium when the molar ratio between copper ions and compound 6awas established at approximately 0.5. This result indicates the chelation ratio is approximately 2:139.

Fluorescence quenching of tyrosinase by compound 6a

Tyrosinase, as a biomacromolecule, exhibits strong intrinsic fluorescence. To evaluate the interaction mode between compound 6a and tyrosinase and determine whether it induces conformational changes in the enzyme, we measured alterations in tyrosinase’s intrinsic fluorescence upon the addition of compound 6a. As shown in Fig. 3A, the intrinsic fluorescence intensity of tyrosinase gradually decreased with increasing concentrations of compound 6a, but no significant spectral shift was observed. When the concentration of compound 6a increased from 0 to 50 µM, the recorded fluorescence intensity dropped to 136.71, representing an 80.99% reduction compared to the original fluorescence intensity (0 µM). This result confirms compound 6a interacts with tyrosinase, effectively quenching its intrinsic fluorescence. To characterize the quenching mechanism, further analysis using the Stern-Volmer equation yielded the quenching constant (Ksv) of 9.25 × 104 L·mol-1 and the bimolecular quenching rate constant (Kq) of 3.08 × 1013 L·mol-1·S-1 (Fig. 3B). Since Kq far exceeds the maximum diffusion-controlled quenching rate for biomolecules (2 × 1010 L·mol-1·S-1), the result indicates that the quenching mechanism is static quenching, suggesting the formation of a ground-state complex between compound 6a and tyrosinase. Logarithmic processing of the Stern-Volmer curve (Fig. 3C) provided the binding constant (Ka) and the number of binding site (n) for compound 6a interacting with tyrosinase, the values are 3.73 × 107 L·mol-1 and 1.25 respectively. These findings support the competitive inhibition observed in kinetic studies, reinforcing that compound 6a binds to the tyrosinase at active site38.

Molecular Docking between tyrosinase and compound 6a

To elucidate the binding characteristics of compound 6a with key residues of tyrosinase, this study employed molecular docking technology to analyze its binding mode. The docking results revealed that the thioether linker fragment of compound 6a exhibits significant hydrogen bonding interactions with key amino acid residues Asn260 and His244, with bond lengths of 2.7 Å and 3.1 Å, respectively (Fig. 4A). These hydrogen bond networks further stabilize the structure of the inhibitor-enzyme complex. It is noteworthy that although the aryl Schiff base fragment of compound 6a, due to steric hindrance, fails to penetrate deep into the active pocket, it still contributes to the overall binding of the molecule through effective hydrophobic interactions with amino acid residues at the pocket entrance, such as Arg268. This may also influence the selectivity and affinity of the inhibitor. More importantly, this fragment forms a strong metal-ligand interaction with the copper ions at the active center, with a bond length of 2.4 Å (Fig. 4A). This direct coordination suggests that compound 6a can competitively bind to the active center of tyrosinase against the natural substrate, which is consistent with the conclusion from previous enzymatic kinetic studies classifying it as a mixed-type inhibitor. Furthermore, the molecular docking results revealed that the kojic acid fragment of compound 6a penetrates into the bottom of the active pocket of tyrosinase (Fig. 4B), essentially occupying the binding sites of the positive control drug kojic acid and the native ligand tropolone of the protein (Fig. 4C).

Carbonyl group of Kojic acid forms a strong metal-ligand interaction with the active center of human tyrosinase, with a bond length of 2.7 Å (Fig. 4D). Hydroxy group of Kojic acid forms hydrogen bond with Ser394 (2.2 Å). The Schiff base fragment forms hydrogen bond with Arg321 (2.4 Å). In addition, the kojic acid fragment of compound 6a penetrates into the bottom of the active pocket of human tyrosinase (Fig. 4E), and the best docking conformation of compound 6a binding to tyrosinase was shown in Fig. 4F.

Anti-browning ability of compound 6a

Tyrosinase is a principal factor in the browning of agricultural and sideline products, including fruits and vegetables. In this study, L* and ΔE were employed as key parameters to assess the antioxidant effects of compound 6a on fresh-cut potatoes. The results are illustrated in Fig. 5A-C. Compared to the control group, compound 6a significantly inhibited the browning process of potatoes, but no concentration dependence. Further comparison of changes in L* and ΔE values (Fig. 5A-B) revealed that the anti-browning effect of compound 6a was more sustained than that of positive control agents such as kojic acid and VC at the concentration of 50 µM (Fig. 5C)38.

Antioxidant activities

All kojic acid sulfide Schiff base derivatives were assessed for DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging ability using Vitamin C (Vc) as reference drug. The IC50 values of all of the compounds, along with the positive control drug VC, are presented in Tables 2 and 3. As shown in Table 2, except for compound 6f, all compounds exhibited stronger ABTS scavenging activity than the positive control Vc. Among them, the ortho-bromo-substituted compound 6 h demonstrated the highest ABTS scavenging activity, with an IC50 value of 0.01 ± 0.0004 µM. From Table 2, the following patterns can be observed regarding the influence of substituent type and position on the ABTS scavenging activity of the compounds: (1) When the aromatic ring is substituted with a halogen atom, the ortho-substituted compounds exhibit stronger inhibitory activity than para- or meta-substituted compounds. For ortho-substituted halogens, as the atomic radius of the halogen increases, the ABTS scavenging activity enhances (6b < 6e < 6 h). Conversely, for para-substituted halogens, as the atomic radius increases, the ABTS scavenging activity decreases ( 6 d > 6 g > 6j). (2) When the aromatic ring is substituted with an alkyl group, the para-substituted compounds show stronger ABTS scavenging activity than ortho- or meta-substituted compounds (6 m > 6 L > 6k, 6o > 6n, 6q > 6p). Moreover, as the size of the alkyl group increases, the ABTS scavenging activity improves (6q > 6o > 6k), which may be related to enhanced hydrophobicity. (3) Comparing compounds 6r–6v, meta-substituted compounds exhibit stronger ABTS scavenging activity than those substituted at other positions. Additionally, hydroxyl-substituted compounds display stronger ABTS scavenging activity than alkoxy-substituted compounds at the corresponding positions (6u > 6s, 6v > 6t). (4) The presence of electron-withdrawing groups leads to a reduction in ABTS scavenging activity, and as the electron-withdrawing ability increases, the ABTS scavenging activity further decreases.

As shown in Table 3, all compounds exhibited varying degrees of DPPH radical scavenging activity. Except for compounds 6f and 6i, the other compounds demonstrated stronger DPPH scavenging activity than the positive control Vc. Among them, compound 6a with no substituents on the benzene ring exhibited the strongest DPPH scavenging activity, with an IC50 value of 4.75 ± 0.003 µM. By analyzing the structure-activity relationships of all compounds in Table 3, the following patterns can be obtained: (1) All substituted compounds showed weaker DPPH scavenging activity than the unsubstituted compound 6a. (2) When halogen atoms were introduced to the aromatic ring, the para-substituted compounds exhibited stronger DPPH scavenging activity than ortho-substituted compounds, while the ortho-substituted compounds were more active than meta-substituted ones ( 6 d > 6b > 6c, 6 g > 6e > 6f, 6j > 6 h > 6i). (3) For alkyl-substituted aromatic rings, para-substituted compounds displayed stronger DPPH scavenging activity than ortho- or meta-substituted ones. Moreover, as the size of the alkyl group increased, the DPPH scavenging activity decreased (6 m > 6o > 6q), which may be attributed to increased steric hindrance. (4) Comparing compounds 6r–6v, the same trend observed in ABTS scavenging activity was confirmed: meta-substituted compounds exhibited stronger DPPH scavenging activity than the other positions substituted compounds. Additionally, hydroxyl-substituted compounds showed higher DPPH scavenging activity than the corresponding alkoxy-substituted compounds, indicating that hydroxyl groups are beneficial for the antioxidant activity of these compounds. (5) When electron-withdrawing groups were present on the aromatic ring, ortho-substituted compounds demonstrated stronger DPPH scavenging activity than para-substituted ones (6w > 6x, 6y > 6z). Furthermore, as the electron-withdrawing ability increased, the DPPH scavenging activity improved slightly.

α-Glucosidase inhibitory activity

As shown in Table 4, all of the kojic acid thioether Schiff bases exhibited certain inhibitory activity against α-glucosidase with IC50 values ranged from 2.47 ± 1.01 µM and 45.14 ± 3.85 µM. From the Table 4, most compounds demonstrated stronger inhibition than the positive control acarbose, and the compound 6d with fluorine atom at papa position showed the best inhibitory activity with an IC50 value of 2.41 ± 1.01 µM. Analysis of the α-glucosidase inhibitory activity of all compounds revealed the following trends: (1) Except for compound 6q, all substituted compounds exhibited stronger inhibitory activity than the unsubstituted compound 6a. This result showed the presence of substituents may be beneficial for the inhibitory activity. (2) All halogen-substituted compounds exhibited stronger inhibition than acarbose. When halogen atoms were introduced on the aromatic ring, para-substituted compounds showed higher inhibitory activity than ortho- and meta-substituted compounds. The result suggested that the inhibitory activity may be related to steric hindrance, compounds with less steric hindrance tend to demonstrate enhanced α-glucosidase inhibition, possibly due to improved binding to the enzyme’s active site. (3) For compounds with electron-donating group at aromatic ring, para-substitution reduced inhibitory activity. In addition, inhibitory activity decreased with the group’s steric bulk increased. Among this kind of compounds, compound 6k displayed the best inhibitory activity. (4) When the hydroxyl group was replaced with methoxy group, inhibitory activity was decreased. This suggested that hydroxyl group may be beneficial for enhancing α-glucosidase inhibition. (5) When electron-withdrawing group was presented on the aromatic ring, inhibitory activity decreased as the electron-withdrawing strength increased.

Mechanism of α-glucosidase Inhibition by compound 6d

Among the tested target compounds, compound 6d showed better α-glucosidase inhibitory activity than the other compounds. Therefore, compound 6d was selected for kinetic studies, and the experimental data were analyzed using the Lineweaver-Burk equation. The Lineweaver–Burk plots for the inhibition of α-glucosidase by the compound 6d were obtained with various concentrations of the compound 6d and the substrate (Fig. 6-1a). As shown in Fig. 6-1a, varying the concentrations of the substrate and compound 6d yielded a series of parallel lines in the enzyme kinetic plots. These results indicate that compound 6d acts as an uncompetitive inhibitor, binding exclusively to the enzyme-substrate complex (ES) to inhibit enzymatic activity. The Ki value of the compound 6d was 35.85 mM. Kojic acid fragment of compound 6d had obvious Pi-Pi interaction with PHE-673 of α-glucosidase, and fluorinated benzene ring also exhibited Pi-Pi interaction with PHE-571 of α-glucosidase. The carbonyl group of kojic acid fragment interacted with ASP-564 and ARG-624, and the hydroxyl group of kojic acid exhibited hydrogen bond interaction with HIS-698 of α-glucosidase with a bond length of 1.9Å (Fig. 6− 2).

Fluorescence quenching of α-glucosidase by compound 6d

α-Glucosidase, as a biomacromolecule, possesses intrinsic fluorescence. The interaction mode between compound 6d and α-glucosidase was evaluated by monitoring changes in the enzyme’s fluorescence intensity using a fluorescence spectrophotometer after the addition of compound 6d. As shown in Fig. 7, the fluorescence intensity of α-glucosidase gradually decreased with increasing concentrations of compound 6d. This result suggested compound 6d effectively quenches the intrinsic fluorescence of α-glucosidase. Notably, despite the reduction in fluorescence intensity, no significant red shift or blue shift in the fluorescence emission peak of α-glucosidase after the addition of compound 6d was observed. The results showed that the interaction between the compound 6d and α-glucosidase did not change the microenvironment around the fluorophores of α-glucosidase. When the concentration of compound 6d reached 20 µM, the fluorescence intensity decreased by approximately 52%. The Stern-Volmer figure of compound 6d with α-glucosidase at 289.15 K was shown in Fig. 7b. Stern-Volmer plots exhibited good linear relationship, this result suggested that the quenching type of compound 6d was single. The calculated bimolecular quenching constant was 5.44 × 1012 L·mol-1·S-1, which exceeds the maximum diffusion collision constant for biomolecules (2 × 10¹⁰ L·mol⁻¹·S⁻¹). This result confirms that the quenching mechanism of compound 6d on α-glucosidase is static quenching, indicative of ground-state complex formation.

Among them, the compound 6j with bromo atom at papa position displayed the most potent inhibitory activity, with an IC50 value of 0.57 ± 0.17 µM. Analysis of the structure-activity relationship revealed the following trends regarding the influence of substituent type and position on AChE inhibition: (1) For halogen-substituted aromatic rings: Para-substituted compounds showed stronger inhibition than ortho-substituted compounds, and ortho-substituted compounds exhibited more potent inhibitory activity than meta-substituted derivatives. (2) For the compounds with electron-donating group on the aromatic ring: Para-substituted compounds showed weaker inhibition than compounds with electron-donating group at other positions. With the increase of substituent steric bulk, the inhibitory activity was decreased. (3) Hydroxyl-substituted compounds exhibited stronger inhibitory activity than the unsubstituted compound 6a. Compounds with methoxy substitution showed weaker inhibition than compounds with hydroxyl group. This result indicated that the hydroxyl moiety may be beneficial to enhance AChE inhibition. (4) For compounds electron-withdrawing group on the aromatic ring: Ortho-substituted compounds demonstrated stronger inhibition than para-substituted analogs. Inhibitory activity improved with increasing electron-withdrawing capability.

Mechanism of acetylcholinesterase Inhibition by compound 6j

Compound 6j exhibited the highest acetylcholinesterase inhibitory activity among the tested kojic acid thioether Schiff bases, we proceeded to further explore the inhibition mechanism of compound 6j. All experimental data were systematically analyzed using the Lineweaver-Burk equation and graphically represented in Fig. 8-1 through GraphPad Prism software (Version number: 10.2.3, URL link: https://www.graphpad.com). As illustrated in Fig. 8-1a, the plots of 1/V against 1/[S] yielded a family of straight lines with diverse slopes. Moreover, the slope of the curve exhibited a downward trend as the inhibitor concentration decreased, and all of the straight lines intersected at a single point within the second quadrant. This result strongly suggested that compound 6j functions as a mixed-type inhibitor and the compound can bind to both the free enzyme and the enzyme-substrate complex. To determine the inhibition constant (Ki) and Kis value of the compound 6j, secondary plotting of the Lineweaver-Burk equation was employed. As depicted in Fig. 8-1b and 8-1c, the Ki value and the Kis value of the compound were calculated to be 0.9067 µM and 1.807 µM, respectively. Since Ki < Kis, compound 6j exhibits higher binding affinity for the free enzyme, behaving similarly to a competitive inhibitor. Molecular docking result showed the carbonyl group of kojic acid fragment had obvious hydrogen bond interaction with GLY-121, GLY122 of acetylcholinesterase (Fig. 8− 2).

Conclusions

In conclusion, in this study, a series of kojic sulfide Schiff base compounds (6a-6z) were designed and synthesized. The results of tyrosinase inhibition activity evaluation demonstrated that compound 6a exhibited the highest efficacy in inhibiting tyrosinase activity among the synthesized compounds, with an IC50 value of 1.43 ± 0.39 µM, over 18 times more potent than that of kojic acid (IC50 = 26.09 ± 0.05 µM). The results of free radical scavenging assays indicated that all compounds exhibited potent antioxidant activity against DPPH comparing to VC, as well as notable scavenging effects against ABTS+. Furthermore, compound 6a displayed remarkable anti-browning ability on freshly cut potatoes. All of the kojic acid thioether Schiff bases exhibited certain inhibitory activity against α-glucosidase with IC50 values ranged from 2.47 ± 1.01 µM and 45.14 ± 3.85 µM. All of the synthesis compounds demonstrated stronger AChE inhibition than the positive control donepezil. These findings suggested that the kojic acid thioether Schiff bases might be application in the pharmaceutical, cosmetic and food industries.

In vitro tyrosinase inhibition

The mushroom tyrosinase inhibitory activity was determined according to previous described method40. Briefly, 168 µL of phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 6.8), 10 µL of mushroom tyrosinase (0.5 mg/mL, Sigma Chemical, USA) and 2 µL of the inhibitor solution were placed in the wells of a 96-well micro plate. After pre-incubation for 20 min at 37 °C, 20 µL of 2.0 mg/mL L-DOPA (3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine, Sigma Chemical, USA) was added and the enzyme activity was measured at 475 nm every 60 s for 180 s in a Microplate Reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). Kojic acid and phosphate buffer were respectively used as positive and negative control. The extent of inhibition by the test compounds was expressed as the percentage of concentration necessary to achieve 50% inhibition (IC50). The percentage of inhibition was calculated as follows: Inhibitory rate (%) = [Ac - At)/Ac] × 100. Ac is the absorbance of the negative control and At is the absorbance of the test compound. Each concentration was analyzed in three independent experiments run in triplicate. The IC50 values were determined by the data analysis software GraphPad Prism 8.29

Tyrosinase kinetic analysis

To study the inhibition type of kojic acid sulfide Schiff base derivatives, the investigation of inhibitory type of selected compound 6a on mushroom tyrosinase was carried out according to reported protocol40. Compound 6a was used at the concentrations of 0 µM, 0.72 µM, 1.43 µM and 3.86 µM, respectively. Substrate L-DOPA concentration was between 500µM and 1000 µM in the process of all kinetic study. Preincubation time and measurement time were the same as described in mushroom tyrosinase inhibition assay procedure. Lineweavere-Burk plots of inverse of velocities (1/V) versus inverse of substrate concentration 1/[S] µM−1was used to determine the type of enzyme inhibition29.

Metal-chelating study

Many tyrosinase inhibitors can form chelator with the copper ions activity center of tyrosinase. In order to illustrate the underlying mechanism, metal-chelating ability of compound 6a with Cu2+ was studied by ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectrometry41. Compound 6a was initially dissolved in methanol. Copper (II) ions solution was prepared with CuSO4·5H2O and phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 6.8). The final concentration of the tested compound was 15 µM, and the final concentration of Cu2+ ranged from 0 to 100 µM. The methanol solution of the compound 6a was added with ascending concentrations of Cu2+, and the reaction mixture was incubated for 30 s at room temperature. The UV absorption spectra were determined using UV–vis spectrophotometry (200 nm to 600 nm, room temperature).

Fluorescence quenching of tyrosinase

A fluorescence spectrophotometer was employed to assess the impact of compound 6a on the intrinsic fluorescence intensity of tyrosinase42. The instrument settings were as follows: an excitation wavelength of 280 nm, an emission slit width of 5 nm, and a scanning wavelength range from 300 to 500 nm. Tyrosinase was prepared in a solution at a concentration of 0.05 mg·mL⁻¹ in PBS (pH 7.2–7.4), while the sample was formulated into a mother solution at a concentration of 10 mM using DMSO. The liquid volume remained constant at 3 mL throughout the measurement process, and changes in fluorescence intensity were recorded following the addition of various concentrations of inhibitors. The fluorescence quenching phenomenon was analyzed using the Stern-Volmer equation.

In silico docking simulation of tyrosinase with compound 6a

Molecular docking calculations were performed molecular docking calculations by using the X-ray structure of mushroom tyrosinase in complex with tyrosinase inhibitor (PDB ID: 2Y9X)24. The most potent compound 6a was prepared by conducting Prepare Ligands procedure (Discovery studio 2019, Version number: 19.1.0.18287, URL link: https://www.3ds.com/products-services/biovia/) in its neutral form and preferential conformation was obtained in the CHARMm force field. Choosing one of the eight monomers from the PDB entry, and the protein structure was prepared using the Prepare Protein module in Discovery studio 2019 software, the specific steps included adding missing residues and hydrogen atoms as well as removing water molecules and spectator ions. Then their conformation optimized in the CHARMm force field. The search grid of binding site was identified as center_x: −10.021, center_y: −28.823, and center_z: −43.596 with Radius value of 10. Molecular modeling simulations were performed with CDOCKER protocol (Discovery studio 2019), reporting the top 10 poses for each ligand and the graph generation was done by PyMOL (Schrödinger)29.

Anti-browning experiment

Potatoes were planted in Dingxi of Gansu province, China, and were purchases from a Walmart supermarket in Shaoyang, Hunan province, China. The potatoes were from the same batch having similar shapes, maturity, and without injuries, rot, diseases or pests. Potatoes was cut into thin slices with the same size by using a sharp knife. Then, the fresh-cut slices were then randomly divided into different groups: the blank control group was immersed in ethanol for 10 min at room temperature, the treatment groups were dipped in a solution of compound 6a under the same conditions, potato with dipping in ethano is blank group, the positive groups were dipped in 50 µM kojic acid and 50 µM vitamin C (Vc) under the same conditions. The test samples were air-dried naturally, immediately packaged separately in polyethylene clam-shell packs, and stored at 5 °C for the following experimental measurements29.

According to the reported protocol43, the color of potatoes was tested using a CR-400 Minolta chroma meter instrument (Konica Minolta, Osaka, Japan). Three data of L*, a*, and b* were recorded for each sample. The L* values indicate lightness, the a* values show reddish-greenish, the b* values represent yellowish-bluish. The color measurement was carried out in triplicate by random sampling29.

DPPH free radical scavenging activity assay

The anti-oxidation effect on DPPH radical was determined using a slightly modified version of a previously described DPPH radical scavenging assay44,45. The reaction mixture consisted of 100 µL DPPH (150 µM) and 20 µL test compound at different concentrations, and then the volume was adjusted to 200 µL with DMSO. The tested solution was incubated for 30 min at room temperature (25 °C) in a dark environment. After incubating, a UV-2600 spectrophotometer was used to measure the absorbance at 517 nm. The experiments were performed in triplicate. DMSO was used as a control, and vitamin C was used as an antioxidant standard for comparison. The calculation of DPPH radical scavenging activity was carried out by this formula:

DPPH radical scavenging activity (%) = (absorbance of standard − absorbance of sample)/absorbance of standard × 10029.

ABTS radical cation scavenging activity assay

According to the reported procedure44,45, ABTS free radical scavenging activity was assayed. The test solution was prepared by mixing 2.45 mM potassium persulfate and 7 mM ABTS in an 8:12 (volume ratio). The reaction solution was kept for 12 to 18 h at room temperature in the dark. Before the ABTS radical scavenging test, the reaction mixture was diluted with DMSO. 1 mL diluted ABTS test solution was added to 10 µl DMSO or test sample solution. After mixing, the mixture was kept for 2 to 6 min in the dark at room temperature, and the absorbance at 734 nm was tested. This measurement was performed in triplicate. DMSO was used as a blank control. Vitamin C was used as antioxidant standard to compare the ABTS scavenging activity. The ABTS scavenging activity was calculated by following formula:

ABTS scavenging activity (%) = (absorbance of standard − absorbance of sample)/absorbance of standard × 10029.

α-Glucosidase inhibition assay

The inhibition assay of α-glucosidase was conducted with certain modifications, following a previously reported procedure. A total volume of 200 µL was prepared for the assay, comprising 118 µL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) and 2 µL (10 mM) of the test compound. Subsequently, 20 µL (0.2 U/mL in PBS) of α-glucosidase was added. The contents of the reaction mixture were pre-incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. After incubation, 20 µL of 2.5 mM substrate (p-nitrophenyl glucopyranoside in PBS) was added in each microtiter well containing sample and control. Microtiter plate containing sample was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After this, 40 µL (0.2 M) Na2CO3 was added as terminator for the reaction in each well. The absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a PT-3502 C microplate reader46. All experiments were performed in triplicates and the acarbose was employed as the positive control. The percentage of inhibition was determined using the following formula:

Inhibition (%) = [(absorbance of standard − absorbance of sample)/absorbance of standard] × 100.

The IC50 values were determined by the data analysis software GraphPad Prism 8.

α-Glucosidase inhibitory kinetics assay

A kinetic study was conducted under the above-mentioned reaction conditions to determine the inhibitory mechanism of the most potent compound 6d. The enzyme solution (0.2 U/mL) was incubated with different concentrations (0, 1.25, 2.5, and 5 µM) of the compound 6d for 15 min at 30 °C. By adding increasing substrate concentrations (2–10 mM), the reaction was initiated and the change in absorbance was measured for 20 min at 405 nm using a spectrophotometer47.

Fluorescence quenching of α-glucosidase

Fluorescence quenching method was operated to analyze the interaction between compound 6d and α-glucosidase42.

Acetylcholinesterase inhibition assay

The experiment was carried out in a 96-well microplate using total assay volume of 200 µL/well contained 140 µL of 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.2–7.4), 20 µL (10 mM) of test substances and 20 µL (0.05 units) of enzyme. The contents were incubated at 25 °C for 15 min. Reaction was started by the addition of 10 µL of substrate (acetylthiocholine iodide for AChE) and 10 µL of DTNB which was then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Yellow color formation indicated end point and the absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a PT-3502 C microplate reader48. The eserine (0.5 mM/well) was employed as a standard control. The assays were performed in triplicate. The percentage of inhibition and IC50 values were determined as described above for the α-glucosidase inhibition assay.

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitory kinetics assay

Kinetics assay was conducted according to the above for the α-glucosidase inhibitory kinetics assay.
