Discrete signature tensors for persistence landscapes
Vincenzo Galgano, Heather A. Harrington, Daniel Tolosa

Abstract
Signature tensors of paths are a versatile tool for mathematical data analysis. Recently, they have been applied in the context of vectorisation of persistent homology: after a choice of embedding of barcodes into a space of paths on a vector space, one applies the path signature map, resulting in tensors amenable to statistical and machine-learning methods. Among the different path embeddings, the persistence landscape embedding (PLE) is injective and stable, but PLE is composed with the signature map loses injectivity. Therefore, we address this by proposing a discrete alternative. Persistence landscapes are determined by the time-series of their critical points, of which we compute the discrete signature. We call this composition the discrete landscape feature map (DLFM), and give results on its injectivity, stability and computability. When studying the injectivity, we complete the proof of a general result due to Diehl, Ebrahimi-Fard and Tapia in the higher-dimensional setting. We showcase the DLFM on a knotted protein dataset, capturing sequence similarity and knot depth with statistical significance. We include an appendix with a preliminary study of Chen signatures of persistence landscapes from the point of view of algebraic geometry. Keywords: persistent homology, barcodes, persistence landscapes, feature maps, time-series, time warping, path signatures, tensors, knotted proteins, vectorisation. Introduction

As the scale and complexity of biological data increases, problems such as interpretation, classification and quantification require advanced mathematical methods for investigation. Applying persistent homology from topological data analysis to biological data provides a geometric interpretation of the shapes of data. Here, we propose an alternative approach combining persistent homology and nonlinear algebra.

Persistent homology provides a multiscale geometric descriptor of data that is functorial, stable to perturbations and interpretable: it takes in a filtered simplicial complex that describes the shape of data, and then outputs a persistence module that can be visualised as a
barcode
, i.e. a multiset of intervals. Persistent homology has been applied to complex datasets (e.g. neuroscience and biomedicine
[
26
,
32
,
34
,
7
,
36
]
). In addition to its stability and interpretability, vectorising barcodes is crucial for machine learning integration
[
2
]
. Some approaches consist of studying the analytical and topological properties of certain continuous paths (e.g. landscape embedding, integrated landscape embedding, Betti curves) associated to landscapes (or barcodes)
[
12
,
16
,
27
,
28
]
. For instance, unlike Betti curves, the (integrated) landscape embedding has been proven to be stable and more meaningful, but still computationally inefficient.

In
[
16
]
the authors proposed a new approach to vectorisation in topological data analysis, leading to
feature maps
which associate barcodes to the signature tensors of the aforementioned paths. More precisely, a feature map
\Phi_{\bullet}

\Phi_{\bullet}:\BBar\xrightarrow{\iota_{\bullet}}\BV(V)\xrightarrow{\sigma}\mathbb{T}((V))

is given by the composition of a chosen
path embedding

\iota_{\bullet}:\BBar\rightarrow\BV(V)
from barcodes to bounded-variation paths, with the
Chen signature map

\sigma:\BV(V)\rightarrow\mathbb{T}((V))
associating to a path its signature tensor.
Signature tensors
are an algebraic tool firstly introduced by
[
15
]
with a rich algebraic and combinatorial structure based on Hopf algebras. A more geometrical approach to signature tensors was firstly proposed in
[
3
]
, focusing on related algebraic varieties. Since then, the topic has increasingly attracted the interest of algebraic geometers
[
4
,
25
]
. A discrete version of signature tensors was introduced by
[
19
,
20
]
, called
discrete signature tensor
(or also iterated sum signature in opposition to Chen’s iterated integral signature). It associates tensors to time-series, and its combinatorics is ruled by quasi-Hopf algebras. First results on the geometry of discrete signature tensors appear in
[
6
]
.

Our work is inspired by both feature maps from persistent homology and the discrete signature tensors. Among the feature maps proposed in
[
16
]
the
landscape
and
integrated landscape
path embeddings satisfy desirable stability properties. The main theoretical contribution of this work is a discrete approach to feature maps as an alternative for vectorisation. We introduce the
discrete landscape feature map
(DLFM)

\begin{matrix}\Phi_{I}:&\BBar&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\iota_{\rm{LS}}}}{{\rightarrow}}&\BV(V)&\rightarrow&\TS(V)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Sigma}}{{\rightarrow}}&\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}))\\
&B&\mapsto&\lambda^{B}&\mapsto&\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{B}&\mapsto&\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{B})\end{matrix}

which first associates to a barcode
B\in\BBar
the time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{B}=\{\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n}\}\in\TS(V)
of critical points of the persistence landscape
\lambda^{B}\in\BV(V)
of the barcode, and then the discrete signature tensor
\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{B})\in\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}))
of the time-series (cf. Sec.
2
for precise definitions). The map
\BV(V)\rightarrow\TS(V)
restricted to persistence landscapes is “canonical”, since persistence landscapes are completely determined by their critical points. Of course, this method may generalise to other path embeddings.

We choose the landscape embedding and the discrete signature due to their
stability
properties and their
discriminative
powers. Indeed, the landscape embedding
\iota_{\rm{LS}}
is stable, unlike other embeddings, and injective (cf.
[
16
, Sec. 3.4]
). However, the discriminative power of
\iota_{\rm{LS}}
gets lost in the feature map
\Phi_{\rm{LS}}
, since Chen signature tensors are invariant under tree-like equivalence of paths. We address this by taking a discrete approach, given the discrete nature of the output of persistent homology, and the availability of the software FRUITS (Feature Extraction Using Iterated Sums) proposed in
[
22
]
for efficiently implementing discrete signatures. We show that discrete signature tensors distinguish among tree-like equivalent landscapes, implying that the DLFM
\Phi_{I}
is more descriptive than
\Phi_{\rm{LS}}
. Alternatively, one may solve the non-injectivity of
\Phi_{\rm{LS}}
by adding a time parameter to
\iota_{\rm{LS}}
, but we expect this would have computational limitations.

We start our analysis of the discrete feature map
\Phi_{I}
by first studying the injectivity of the discrete signature map
\Sigma:\TS(V)\rightarrow\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}))
. Such a map is a monoid homomorphism with respect to the shifted-concatenation (cf. equation (
2.9
)) of time-series, and the tensor product of discrete signature tensors. The discrete signature map is known
[
20
]
to be invariant under time-warping and time-translation equivalence
\sim_{twt}
. We prove that this equivalence uniquely determines the (monoidal) kernel of
\Sigma
. This result appeared in
[
21
, Theorem 3.15]
and we fill the gap in their proof from one-dimensional to higher-dimensional time-series.

Theorem

(cf. Theorem
2.3
)
.

Any two time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}}\in\TS(V)
have the same discrete signature
\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}})=\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{y}})
if and only if
\underline{\mathbb{x}}\sim_{twt}\underline{\mathbb{y}}
. In particular, the quotient map
\TS(V)/_{\sim_{twt}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}))
is injective.

We also propose a description of time-warping and time-translation in terms of matrices (cf. Remark
2.1
).

Next, we address the problem of the stability of the discrete feature map
\Phi_{I}
.
We note that it is not continuous on the whole domain
\BBar
, since the discrete signature map is not invariant under time-series refinements. We overcome this obstacle by restricting to consider a finite set of barcodes
D\subset\BBar
, and consider the union of the critical points of all landscapes arising from
D
. This results in the
specialised discrete landscape feature map

\Phi_{I_{D}}
. Such an assumption is reasonable considering that most experiments generate finite datasets.
Moreover, we restrict the image of
\Phi_{I_{D}}
to the subspace
\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}))
of discrete signature tensors truncated up to weight
k
. Again, this restriction is quite natural for applications.

The feature map
\Phi_{I_{D}}
has desirable continuity properties. Indeed, albeit not being stable in the sense of
1
-Lipschitz, we show that
\Phi_{I_{D}}
is uniformly continuous. Here, the metrics involved are the
bottleneck distance
on the space of barcodes
\BBar
(introduced by
[
17
]
and further investigated by
[
13
]
) and the
Bombieri-Weyl distance
on the space of discrete signature tensors
\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}))
, naturally induced from the standard scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\simeq V
.

Theorem

(cf. Theorem
2.14
)
.

Given
D\subset\BBar
a finite set of barcodes, the specialised discrete landscape feature map

\Phi_{I_{D}}^{\leq k}:\BBar\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}))

is uniformly continuous with respect to the bottleneck distance on
\BBar
and the Bombieri-Weyl distance on
\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}))
.

We complement the theoretical study by showcasing the DLFM on a knotted protein dataset previously analysed with persistence landscapes in
[
8
]
. We use the software FRUITS (Feature Extraction Using Iterated Sums) proposed in
[
22
]
for implementing discrete signatures. We show that discrete signatures and structure representative classes (in the sense of amino-acid sequence similarity) are strongly correlated with very high
Adjusted Rand Index
(ARI) and
Normalised Mutual Information
(NMI) scores. We validate the statistical significance of such correlation by running permutation tests. We also show correlation between discrete signature and knot depth via two statistical methods, and we visualise it by applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA). In our analysis we maintain the labeling of representatives analogous to the one in
[
8
]
to allow comparisons. We explain our analysis in a way intended to be accessible to a broad audience.

Finally, we give a closer look at the classical landscape feature maps and at their images in
\mathbb{T}((V))
. In particular, we focus on the varieties of signature matrices of landscape paths. For this pourpose, as standard in algebraic geometry, we move to the complex field
\mathbb{C}
and the projective setting. Our partial description is intended as invitation to algebraic geometers for further study. For instance, since persistence landscape paths are piecewise linear loops, we first describe the signature matrices for loops.

Proposition

(cf. Corollary
4.2
)
.

The projective variety of signature matrices of piecewise linear loops in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
m
maximal segments coincides with the secant variety
\sigma_{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}(Gr(2,d))
of the Grassmannian of planes in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

This work is organised as follows. Sec.
1
contains the preliminary notions and already known results about signature tensors and persistence feature maps. Sec.
2
represents the theoretical core of the manuscript, and contains all constructions and results about the discrete landscape feature map. Sec.
3
highlights the utility of the signature-based analysis of biological structures of knotted proteins with respect to both sequence similarity and knot depth. The Appendix
4
contains the discussion of signature tensors of persistence landscape from an algebro-geometric perspective.

1
Preliminaries

1.1
Signature tensors

Our main references for notions and result appearing in the following are
[
3
,
4
]
. We fix a vector space
V
over
\mathbb{R}
of dimension
d
and a basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})
.

Power series tensor algebra.

Given a real vector space
V
, let
\mathbb{T}((V)):=\prod_{k\geq 0}V^{\otimes k}
be the
power series tensor algebra
over
V
, whose elements are sequences of tensors
\mathcal{T}=\big(T^{(k)}\big)_{k}
such that
T^{(k)}\in V^{\otimes k}
, and whose inner product is given by the tensor product
\mathcal{T}\otimes\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\big(\sum_{i+j=k}T^{(i)}\otimes(T^{\prime})^{(j)}\big)_{k}
. Equivalently, by looking at elements as power series
\mathcal{T}=\sum_{k\geq 0}T^{(k)}
, the inner product is the multiplication of power series. For any
k
we write
T^{(k)}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}
for the
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
-th entry of the tensor
T^{(k)}
with respect to the fixed basis of
V
, so that
T^{(k)}=\sum_{I\in[n]^{k}}T^{(k)}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}e_{i_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes e_{i_{k}}
. We denote the multiplicative subgroup of
\mathbb{T}((V))
of power series with degree–
0
term equal to
1
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{1}((V))

\displaystyle:=\{\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{T}((V))\ |\ T^{(0)}=1\}\ .

Path signature.

Let
\BV([0,1],V)
be the set of bounded-variation continuous paths
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow V
. The
path signature
is the map

\begin{matrix}\sigma:&\BV([0,1],V)&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{T}_{1}((V))\\
&\gamma&\mapsto&\sigma(\gamma)=\left(\sigma^{(k)}(\gamma)\right)_{k}\end{matrix}

(1.1)

such that
\sigma^{(0)}(\gamma)=1
by convention, and
\sigma^{(k)}(\gamma):=\int_{\Delta_{k}}d\gamma^{\otimes k}
is the Riemann-Stieltjes integral of
d\gamma^{\otimes k}:=d\gamma(t_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes d\gamma(t_{k})
over the
k
–dimensional standard simplex
\Delta_{k}
. More practically, the coefficient of
e_{i_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes e_{i_{k}}
in the tensor
\sigma^{(k)}(\gamma)\in V^{\otimes k}
is (cf.
[
15
, Secc. 2,3]
)

\sigma^{(k)}(\gamma)_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\dot{\gamma}_{i_{1}}(t_{1})\cdots\dot{\gamma}_{i_{k}}(t_{k})dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k}\ .

(1.2)

The sequence
\sigma(\gamma)\in\mathbb{T}((V))
is the
signature tensor
of
\gamma
, and its
k
–th entry
\sigma^{(k)}(\gamma)\in V^{\otimes k}
is the
order-
k
signature tensor
of
\gamma
.

The path signature
\sigma
is a multiplicative homomorphism with respect to the concatenation of paths in
\BV([0,1],V)
(which we denote by
\ast
) and the tensor product in
\mathbb{T}_{1}((V))
. This is a consequence of the Chen relation
[
15
, Theorem 3.1]

\sigma(\gamma_{1}\ast\gamma_{2})=\sigma(\gamma_{1})\otimes\sigma(\gamma_{2})\ .

(1.3)

Moreover,
\sigma
quotients to an injective homomorphism

\sigma:\ \BV([0,1],V)/\sim_{tl}\ \hookrightarrow\ \mathbb{T}((V))

(1.4)

with respect to the
tree-like equivalence
, for whose definition we refer to
[
16
, Sec. 4]
. However,
[
29
, Corollary 1.5]
shows that one can take
\gamma_{1}\sim_{tl}\gamma_{2}\iff\sigma(\gamma_{1}\ast\gamma_{2}^{-1})=(1,\mathbf{0},\ldots)
as definition.

Remark 1.1
.

The name
tree-like
equivalence comes from the fact that in a tree (say, path of segments without loops) one can always iteratively retract leaves up to a point, giving a trivial signature.

Remark 1.2
.

The natural action of
\text{GL}(V)
on
V
induces an entry-wise action on each
V^{\otimes k}
, hence on
\mathbb{T}_{1}((V))
, as well as on
\BV([0,1],V)
where
(g\cdot\gamma)(t)=g\cdot(\gamma(t))
. In particular, the path signature map
\sigma
is
\text{GL}(V)
–equivariant, that is
g\cdot\sigma(\gamma)=\sigma(g\cdot\gamma)
. Finally, signatures are invariant under translations and reparametrisations of paths. We will therefore always consider paths based at the origin, i.e.
\gamma(0)=\mathbb{0}\in V
.

Signatures of PWL paths.

We refer to
piecewise linear
(PWL) paths as continuous paths
\gamma:[0,L]\rightarrow V
which are concatenation of finitely many segments
\gamma=\alpha_{1}\ast\ldots\ast\alpha_{m}
, where
\alpha_{j}(t)=\mathbb{a}_{j}\cdot t
for certain
\mathbb{a}_{j}\in V
: in the expression of
\gamma
, the paths
\alpha_{j}(t)
are implicitly shifted so that the concatenation makes sense. The partition
\gamma=\alpha_{1}\ast\ldots\ast\alpha_{m}
is unique if we require that the segments
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
have maximal length possible, or equivalently that
m
is minimal.

Definition 1.3
.

We refer to such partition as the
minimal segment decomposition
(MSD) of the path, and we say that the path
\gamma
is
m
-piecewise linear (
m
–PWL).

Signatures of PWL paths have a particularly nice expression in terms of their segment decomposition. The
k
–th signature tensor of a segment path
\alpha(t)=\mathbb{a}\cdot t
for
\mathbb{a}\in V
is always the symmetric rank–
1
tensor determined by
\mathbb{a}
, that is

\sigma(\alpha)=\left(1,L\mathbb{a},\frac{L^{2}}{2}\mathbb{a}^{\otimes 2},\ldots,\frac{L^{k}}{k!}\mathbb{a}^{\otimes k},\ldots\right)=\exp(L\mathbb{a}).

In fact, segments (up to tree-like equivalence) are the only paths with rank
1
signature. Then by Chen’s relation (
1.3
) the signature tensor of a
m
–PWL path
\gamma=\alpha_{1}\ast\ldots\ast\alpha_{m}
is

\sigma(\gamma)=\exp(L\mathbb{a}_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes\exp(L\mathbb{a}_{m})\ .

(1.5)

1.2
Persistence feature maps

A
barcode
is a multiset of intervals of the form
B:=\{[b_{1},d_{1}),...,[b_{n},d_{n})\}
,
where
b_{i}\leq d_{i}
are non-negative real numbers called
birth
and
death
. Barcodes, or equivalently persistence diagrams, are the standard output of persistent homology, the flagship method in topological data analysis. We refer to
[
35
]
for a basic introduction to the topic. In this paper, we will assume barcodes to be
tame
, that is they have only finitely many intervals, and all intervals are contained in a certain
[0,L]
.

From
[
13
]
we recall that any barcode
B
defines a family of continuous functions
\lambda_{k}^{B}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\lambda_{k}^{B}(t):=\sup\{s\geq 0\,|\,\beta^{t-s,t+s}\geq k\},

(1.6)

where
\beta^{t-s,t+s}
is the number of intervals in
B
that contain
[t-s,t+s]
, and where one sets
\lambda_{k}^{B}(t)=0
whenever the supremum is taken over the empty set (cf.
[
16
, Definition 3.2]
). The function
\lambda_{k}^{B}(t)
is called the
k
–th landscape
of the barcode
B

[
14
]
, or
k
–th level
of
B
.

Remark 1.4
.

A graphical construction of the landscape of a barcode is as follows. Let
B
be a barcode with bars
(B_{1},\ldots,B_{n})
. For any
i\in[n]
, consider the birth-death point
P_{i}:=(b_{i},d_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
B_{i}
is supported on
[b_{i},d_{i}]
. By definition, each birth-death point lies above the diagonal in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and defines a right triangle whose hypotenus lies on the diagonal and whose catheti meet in the point itself. After isometries of the plane, one consider all these hypotenuses lying on the interval
[0,L]
along the
x
–axis. The persistence landscape of
B
is the union of the sides of such triangles.

By construction, levels of a landscape
\lambda^{B}
are PWL paths
\lambda_{j}^{B}:[0,L]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such that

•

they are loops:
\lambda_{j}^{B}(0)=\lambda_{j}^{B}(L)=0
;

•

higher levels dominate lower levels:
\lambda_{i}^{B}(t)\geq\lambda_{j}^{B}(t)
for any
t\in[0,L]
and
i\leq j
;

•

for any
j\in[d]
the segments of
\lambda_{j}^{B}
have slope in
\{0,\pm 1\}
.

We write
\lambda^{B}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
by non specifying
B
at each level when it is clear.

Figure 1.1:
Landscape with
3
levels:
\lambda_{1}
in black,
\lambda_{2}
in blue,
\lambda_{3}
in red.

In the need of vectorising barcodes, several ways of associating paths in a vector space to barcodes have been introduced under the name of
embeddings
. Moreover, in order to obtain a more informative vectorisation, in
[
16
]
the authors composed such embeddings with the Chen signature map (
1.1
): an embedding
\iota_{\bullet}:\BBar\rightarrow\BV([0,L],\mathbb{R}^{d})
defines the
feature map

\Phi_{\bullet}:\BBar\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\iota_{\bullet}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\BV([0,L],\mathbb{R}^{d})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{T}_{1}((\mathbb{R}^{d}))\ .

Note that the feature map
\Phi_{\bullet}
quotients to an injective map under the tree-like equivalence. In the following we focus on two feature maps, but we refer to
[
16
]
for a more complete overview.

The
landscape embedding
is the map associating to each barcode
B
(with at most
d
levels) a path
\lambda^{B}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose
k
–entry is the
k
–th level of
B
, namely

\begin{matrix}\iota_{\rm{LS}}:&\BBar&\longrightarrow&\BV([0,L],\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
&B&\mapsto&\lambda^{B}(t)&=\scriptsize{\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}^{B}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{d}^{B}(t)\end{bmatrix}}\end{matrix}\ .

(1.7)

Such vectorisation is not as descriptive as one would desire. The tree-like equivalence of paths makes impossible to distinguish large classes of landscapes. For example, the empty barcode, a singleton barcode
\{[b,d)\}
and any barcode
\{[b_{1},d_{1}),...,[b_{n},d_{n})\}
such that
b_{i}>d_{i-1}
,
i=2,...,n
, define tree-like paths. Hence, the feature map
\Phi_{\rm{LS}}
maps each of them to the trivial signature
(1,\mathbb{0},\ldots)
.

Figure 1.2:
Uniform sampling, called
Fibonacci sampling
, of two spheres (left) has a one-dimensional associated persistence landscape (right) with trivial Chen signature.

A more descriptive embedding is given by a cumulative version of
\iota_{\rm{LS}}
, which distinguishes more features of barcodes at the cost of an additional integration step. The
integrated landscape embedding
is the map

\begin{matrix}\iota_{\rm{iLS}}:&\BBar&\longrightarrow&\BV([0,L],\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
&B&\mapsto&F^{\lambda^{B}}(t)&:=\small{\begin{bmatrix}\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{1}^{B}(s)ds\\
\vdots\\
\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{d}^{B}(s)ds\end{bmatrix}}\end{matrix}\ .

(1.8)

The key property of the resulting feature map
\Phi_{i\rm{LS}}
is its stability (under perturbations of the barcodes). However, the extra integration step makes it computationally inefficient.

Some initial work has been done to describe invariants of signature tensors
[
25
,
4
]
and it would be interesting to relate these invariants to the topology of barcodes via the feature maps. For instance, the only rank–
1
signature tensors are signatures of segments
[
4
, Corollary 7.3]
. In the case of the landscape feature map
\iota_{\rm{LS}}
, the latter result leads to the following.

Proposition 1.5
.

Given a barcode
B
, the signature tensor
\Phi_{\rm{LS}}(B)=\sigma(\lambda^{B})
has rank
1
—i.e. for any
k\geq 0
the
k
–th signature
\sigma^{(k)}(\lambda^{B})
has tensor-rank
1
in
V^{\otimes k}
— if and only if the barcode
B
is trivial —i.e. it consists of diagonal birth-death points
[b_{i},b_{i}]
.

Proof.

From
[
4
]
, we know that the signature
\Phi_{\rm{LS}}(B)
has rank
1
if and only if
\lambda^{B}
is a segment. But
\lambda^{B}
is a segment if and only if all birth-death pairs of the barcode lie along the diagonal, that is they are of the form
[b_{i},b_{i}]
.
∎

2
Discrete feature maps

A discrete version of signature tensors, replacing paths with time-series, was introduced in
[
19
,
20
]
, and first glimpses of the related geometry appear in
[
6
]
. Before applying such discrete approach to persistent homology, first we recall definitions and propose a matrix description of the equivalence relation for time-warping and time-translation. Finally, we prove that discrete signatures recover time-series
uniquely up to
time-warping and time-translation, fixing a gap in the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 3.15]
.

2.1
Discrete signatures of time-series

Given the polynomial ring
\mathbb{R}[\mathbb{Z}]=\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}]
, consider its quotient by the constant polynomials

\mathscr{V}_{d}={\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}]}/{\mathbb{R}}\ .

As (infinite dimensional) vector subspace,
\mathscr{V}_{d}
is generated by all possible monomials in the
d
variables. We consider the tensor algebra
\mathbb{T}(\mathscr{V}_{d})
and the power series tensor algebra
\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
: note that
\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
can be identified to the dual space
\mathbb{T}(\mathscr{V}_{d})^{\vee}
.

Let
\underline{\mathbb{x}}=(\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n})
be a time-series of
n\geq 1
vectors (we say, length
n
) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We denote the set of all time-series (of any positive length) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The
discrete signature map
is the map

\begin{matrix}\Sigma:&\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))\\
&\underline{\mathbb{x}}&\mapsto&\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}})\end{matrix}

(2.1)

such that, for any time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}}=(\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n})
of length
n
, the
discrete signature

\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}})
is the element of the tensor algebra
\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
defined as follows: for any
\ell\geq 0
and any family of monomials
p_{1},...,p_{\ell}\in\mathscr{V}_{d}
, the coefficient of
p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell}
in
\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}})
is

\langle\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}}),p_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes p_{\ell}\rangle=\begin{cases}\sum\limits_{1\leq i_{1}<...<i_{\ell}\lneq n}p_{1}(\mathbb{x}_{i_{1}+1}-\mathbb{x}_{i_{1}})\cdots p_{\ell}(\mathbb{x}_{i_{\ell}+1}-\mathbb{x}_{i_{\ell}})&\ell\leq n-1\\
0&\ell\geq n\end{cases}

(2.2)

and
\langle\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}}),\epsilon\rangle=1
where
\epsilon
corresponds to the empty word. The above expression is a generalisation of the notion of quasi-symmetric polynomials
[
20
, Remark 3.5]
. For simplicity, we fix the notation

C_{p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell}}^{\underline{\mathbb{x}}}\ :=\ \langle\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}}),p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell}\rangle\ \in\mathbb{R}\ .

The
weight
of an element
p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell}
is the sum of the degrees of the monomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\ell}
, that is

weight(p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell}):=\deg(p_{1})+\ldots+\deg(p_{\ell})\ .

(2.3)

The truncation
up to weight
h
of the discrete signature
\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}})
is

\Sigma^{\leq h}(\underline{\mathbb{x}}):=\sum_{weight(p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell})\leq h}C_{p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell}}^{\underline{\mathbb{x}}}p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell}\ .

An important feature of the (standard) signature map is being invariant under time alteration (i.e. reparametrisation of the curve). By definition, the discrete signature carries a similar property, known as
time-warping invariance

[
20
]
: it is invariant under
consecutive
time-point repetitions, that is

\Sigma((\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{j-1},\mathbb{x}_{j},\mathbb{x}_{j},\mathbb{x}_{j+1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n}))=\Sigma((\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{j-1},\mathbb{x}_{j},\mathbb{x}_{j+1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n}))\ .

We say that a time-series is
time-warping reduced
(TWR) if it does not present consecutive repetitions. It is clear from the definition that any time-series has a unique associated TWR series so that their signatures coincide.

As the signature path (
1.1
) is injective modulo tree-like equivalence of paths, it is quite natural to ask under which equivalence relation the discrete signature (
2.1
) is injective as well. From the above, we know that time-warping is an invariant property. Moreover, again by definition (
2.2
), the discrete signature is also invariant under
time-translation
:

\Sigma((\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n}))=\Sigma((\mathbb{x}_{1}-\mathbb{y},\mathbb{x}_{2}-\mathbb{y},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n}-\mathbb{y}))\ \ \ \ \ ,\ \forall\mathbb{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ .

Let
\sim_{twt}
be the equivalence relation on
\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined by time-warping and time-translation. Let
\{\mathbb{0}\}
be the time-series of length
1
given by the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\underline{\mathbb{y}}\sim_{twt}\{\mathbb{0}\}\ \iff\ \underline{\mathbb{y}}=\{\mathbb{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{y}_{1}\}\ .

Clearly, all the above time-series have trivial discrete signature

\Sigma(\{\mathbb{0}\})=\epsilon\ .

(2.4)

Remark 2.1

(Matrix description of
\sim_{twt}
)
.

To a time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}}=\{\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n}\}\in\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we associate the matrix
\mathbb{X}=(x_{ij})=\left[\mathbb{x}_{1}|\cdots|\mathbb{x}_{n}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
. The time-translation
\underline{\mathbb{x}}\mapsto\{\mathbb{0},\mathbb{x}_{2}-\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n}-\mathbb{x}_{1}\}
corresponds to the right-multiplication below:

\mathbb{X}\cdot{\scriptsize{\begin{bmatrix}0&-1&\cdots&-1\\
&1\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}_{n\times n}}}=\bigg[\mathbb{0}\ \big|\ \mathbb{x}_{2}-\mathbb{x}_{1}\ \big|\ \ldots\ \big|\ \mathbb{x}_{n}-\mathbb{x}_{1}\bigg]\ .

(2.5)

On the other hand, time-warping extensions and contractions are described as follows:

\bigg[\mathbb{x}_{1}\big|\cdots\big|\mathbb{x}_{n}\bigg]\cdot{\scriptsize{\begin{bmatrix}1&\\
&1&1\\
&&&1\\
&&&&\ddots\\
&&&&&1\end{bmatrix}_{n\times(n+1)}}}=\bigg[\mathbb{x}_{1}\big|\mathbb{x}_{2}\big|\mathbb{x}_{2}\big|\mathbb{x}_{3}\big|\cdots\big|\mathbb{x}_{n}\bigg]

(2.6)

\bigg[\mathbb{x}_{1}\big|\mathbb{x}_{2}\big|\mathbb{x}_{2}\big|\mathbb{x}_{3}\big|\cdots\big|\mathbb{x}_{n}\bigg]\cdot{\scriptsize{\begin{bmatrix}1&\\
&1&-1\\
&&1\\
&&1\\
&&&\ddots\\
&&&&1\end{bmatrix}_{(n+1)\times n}}}=\bigg[\mathbb{x}_{1}\big|\mathbb{x}_{2}\big|\mathbb{x}_{3}\big|\cdots\big|\mathbb{x}_{n}\bigg]\ .

(2.7)

Finally, the time-series of consecutive differences
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{x}}
is obtained by:

\mathbb{X}\cdot{\scriptsize{\begin{bmatrix}-1\\
1&-1\\
&1&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&-1\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}_{n\times(n-1)}}}=\bigg[\mathbb{x}_{2}-\mathbb{x}_{1}\ \big|\ \ldots\ \big|\ \mathbb{x}_{n}-\mathbb{x}_{n-1}\bigg]\ .

(2.8)

Similarly to concatenation of paths, one can consider a
shifted
concatenation of time-series: for any two time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}}=\{\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n}\}
and
\underline{\mathbb{y}}=\{\mathbb{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{y}_{m}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, their (shifted)
concatenation
is the time-series of length
n+m

\displaystyle\{\underline{\mathbb{x}}|\underline{\mathbb{y}}\}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\mathbb{x}_{k}&k\in[n]\\
\mathbb{y}_{k-n}-\mathbb{y}_{1}+\mathbb{x}_{n}&k\in[n+m]\setminus[n]\end{cases}

(2.9)

\displaystyle=\{\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n},\mathbb{x}_{n},\mathbb{y}_{2}-\mathbb{y}_{1}+\mathbb{x}_{n},\ldots,\mathbb{y}_{m}-\mathbb{y}_{1}+\mathbb{x}_{n}\}

The shift in the formula (
2.9
) is motivated by the need of emulating the Chen relation (
1.3
). Indeed, for discrete signatures of time-series the following
discrete Chen relation
holds (cf.
[
6
, Theorem 3.6]
):

\Sigma(\{\underline{\mathbb{x}}|\underline{\mathbb{y}}\})=\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}})\otimes\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{y}})\ .

(2.10)

Note that the shifted concatenation of time-series is coherent with the time-warping-translation equivalence. Moreover, the class
\{\mathbb{0}\}
(modulo
\sim_{twt}
) is the identity element in
\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for the shifted concatenation:

\{\{\mathbb{0}\}|\underline{\mathbb{x}}\}=\{\underline{\mathbb{x}}|\{\mathbb{0}\}\}=\underline{\mathbb{x}}\ .

Finally,
\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
monoid
with respect to the shift concatenation with identity element
\{\mathbb{0}\}
, and the discrete signature map (
2.1
) is a
monoid homomorphism
(in light of (
2.10
) and (
2.4
)). Now, determining when two time-series have the same discrete signature is equivalent to determine the kernel of the monoid homomorphism
\Sigma

\ker(\Sigma):=\left\{(\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}})\in\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}\ |\ \Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}})=\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{y}})\right\}\ .

Note that this is different from determining which time-series have trivial discrete signature
\epsilon
. We claim that
\ker(\Sigma)
corresponds to the diagonal in
(\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})/_{\sim_{twt}})^{2}
, and hence the (quotient) discrete signature map
\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})/_{\sim_{twt}}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
is injective.

In the case of the classical Chen signatures, the bijection between tree-like equivalence for paths and the Chen signatures is obtained by exploiting the group structure of paths and the Chen relation (
1.3
) making the signature path map (
1.1
) a group homomorphism. For discrete signature we just have a monoid structure, so determining the (monoidal) kernel
\ker(\Sigma)
requires a different approach. First, we observe that TWR time-series with same discrete signatures must have the same length.

Given a time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}}=\{\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n}\}\in\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we call
difference time-series
the time-series of consecutive differences

\Delta\underline{\mathbb{x}}:=(\Delta\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\Delta\mathbb{x}_{n-1})\ \ \ \text{where}\ \Delta\mathbb{x}_{j}:=\mathbb{x}_{j+1}-\mathbb{x}_{j}\ .

If
\underline{\mathbb{x}}
is TWR, then
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{x}}
has no zero vectors. Observe that two TWR time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}}\in\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with same discrete signature
\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}})=\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{y}})
must have same length: indeed, it is enough to consider the coefficient
C^{\underline{\mathbb{x}}}_{Z_{i_{1}}\otimes Z_{i_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes Z_{i_{n}}}=\Delta x_{i_{1}1}\cdot\Delta x_{i_{2}2}\cdots\Delta x_{i_{n}n}
for
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\subset[d]^{n}
such that
\Delta x_{i_{1}1},\Delta x_{i_{2}2},...,\Delta x_{i_{n}n}
are nonzero entries.

Diehl, Ebrahimi-Fard and Tapia
[
21
, Theorem 3.15]
addressed the injectivity of the discrete signature up to
\sim_{twt}
in a more general context, working over any semiring
\mathbb{S}
with cancellation property. In their proof, they use an elegant inductive argument (based on the cancellation property of the semiring) to show the injectivity for
1
-dimensional time-series.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
21
]
, Theorem 3.15)
.

For any two
1
-dimensional time-series
{\mathbb{x}},{\mathbb{y}}\in\TS(\mathbb{R})
,

\Sigma({\mathbb{x}})=\Sigma({\mathbb{y}})\ \iff\ {\mathbb{x}}\sim_{twt}{\mathbb{y}}\ .

In particular, the quotient map
\TS(\mathbb{R})/_{\sim_{twt}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}_{1}))
is injective.

However, deducing the injectivity for higher dimensional time-series from the one for
1
-dimensional time-series requires an extra necessary step which is not taken into account in the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 3.15]
. Indeed, starting from two TWR time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}}\in\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for any
i\in[d]
the
1
-dimensional time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{i}:=\{x_{i1},\ldots,x_{in}\}
and
\underline{\mathbb{y}}^{i}:=\{y_{i1},\ldots,y_{in}\}
in
\TS(\mathbb{R})
of the
i
–th vector entries might be non-TWR. Then the same argument to prove Proposition
2.2
shows that for any
i\in[d]
the
1
-dimensional difference time-series
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{i}
and
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{y}}^{i}
have the same (ordered) nonzero values, but a priori they can still have zero values appearing at different entries. We fill this gap and prove injectivity for higher dimensional time-series. The following proof works by replacing
\mathbb{R}
with any semiring
\mathbb{S}
with the cancellation property.

Theorem 2.3
.

For any two time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}}\in\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}})=\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{y}})\ \iff\ \underline{\mathbb{x}}\sim_{twt}\underline{\mathbb{y}}\ .

In particular, the quotient map
\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})/_{\sim_{twt}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
is injective.

Proof.

We assume
\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}}\in\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to be TWR and such that
\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}})=\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{y}})
. We know that
\underline{\mathbb{x}}
and
\underline{\mathbb{y}}
must have same length. Write
\underline{\mathbb{x}}=\{\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{x}_{n}\}
and
\underline{\mathbb{y}}=\{\mathbb{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{y}_{n}\}
.

For every
i\in[d]
consider the
1
-dimensional time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{i}:=\{x_{i1},\ldots,x_{in}\}
and
\underline{\mathbb{y}}^{i}:=\{y_{i1},\ldots,y_{in}\}
of the
i
–th vector entries. They can be non-TWR. However, the same argument in the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 3.15]
shows that for any
i\in[d]
the
1
-dimensional difference time-series
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{i}
and
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{y}}^{i}
are equivalent, that is they have the same (ordered) nonzero values, and they can only differ in the order of appearance of the zeroes. In the following we prove that they also have zero values at the same entries, implying that
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{i}=\Delta\underline{\mathbb{y}}^{i}
.

Since the
d
-dimensional time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}}
is TWR, the difference time-series
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{x}}
doesn’t have zero vectors, hence there exists
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\subset[d]^{n}
such that
C^{\underline{\mathbb{x}}}_{Z_{i_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes Z_{i_{n}}}=\Delta x_{i_{1}1}\cdots\Delta x_{i_{n}n}\neq 0
. In particular, each
\Delta x_{i_{j}j}\neq 0
. Since the time-series have the same discrete signatures, it follows
C_{Z_{i_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes Z_{i_{n}}}^{\underline{\mathbb{y}}}=\Delta y_{i_{1}1}\cdots\Delta y_{i_{n}n}
is nonzero too, as well as each
\Delta y_{i_{j}j}
. Now, pick
(\bar{i},\bar{j})\in[d]\times n
such that
\Delta x_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}=0
and substitute in the above tensor-monomials
Z_{i_{\bar{j}}\bar{j}}
with
Z_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}
: one gets

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\Delta x_{i_{1}1}\cdots\Delta x_{i_{\bar{j}-1}\,\bar{j}-1}\cdot\Delta x_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}\cdot\Delta x_{i_{\bar{j}+1}\,\bar{j}}\cdots\Delta x_{i_{n}n}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Delta y_{i_{1}1}\cdots\Delta y_{i_{\bar{j}-1}\,\bar{j}-1}}_{\neq 0}\cdot\Delta y_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}\cdot\underbrace{\Delta y_{i_{\bar{j}+1}\,\bar{j}}\cdots\Delta y_{i_{n}n}}_{\neq 0}

implying
\Delta y_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}=0
. This shows that the
1
-dimensional difference time-series
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{i}
and
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{y}}^{i}
have zeroes at the same entries, hence they coincide. We conclude that
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{x}}=\Delta\underline{\mathbb{y}}
, that is
\underline{\mathbb{x}}\sim_{twt}\underline{\mathbb{y}}
.
∎

Remark 2.4

(Refinement of segment decomposition)
.

For data applications, unlike the classical signature of paths, the discrete signature is not invariant under “refinement of segment decomposition”, which would correspond to an expansion of the time-series: an evidence is given by the discrete Chen relation (
2.10
).
For instance, a segment
[b,d]
and its refinement
[b,\frac{b+d}{2}]\cup[\frac{b+d}{2},d]
give respectively the time-series
\{b,d\}
and
\big\{\{b,\frac{b+d}{2}\}|\{\frac{b+d}{2},d\}\big\}=\{b,\frac{b+d}{2},\frac{b+d}{2},d\}
, whose discrete signatures are such that

\displaystyle C^{\{b,d\}}_{Z^{2}}=(d-b)^{2},

\displaystyle C^{\{\{b,\frac{b+d}{2}\}|\{\frac{b+d}{2},d\}\}}_{Z^{2}}=C^{\{b,\frac{b+d}{2}\}}_{\epsilon}\cdot C^{\{\frac{b+d}{2},d\}}_{Z^{2}}\ +\ C^{\{b,\frac{b+d}{2}\}}_{Z^{2}}\cdot C^{\{\frac{b+d}{2},d\}}_{\epsilon}=\ \textstyle{\frac{(d-b)^{2}}{2}}.

However, the Chen and the discrete signatures are interrelated
[
20
, Proposition 5.5]
: given a bounded variation path
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the limit —over finite partitions
\pi=\{0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}<1\}
— of the discrete signatures of the time-series
\underline{\mathbb{x}}^{\pi}:=\{\gamma(0),\gamma(t_{1}),\ldots,\gamma(t_{n}),\gamma(1)\}
converges to the Chen signature of
\gamma
.

2.2
The discrete landscape feature map

The discrete signature is a well-defined and meaningful descriptor for persistence landscapes (and in general, of PWL paths). Indeed, persistence landscapes come with a “canonical” time-series associated to them, namely the
minimal segment decomposition
(cf. Definition
1.3
).

Each level
\lambda_{k}(t)
of a persistence landscape
\lambda
is a piecewise linear function with slope
1,-1,
or
0
. The
critical points
of
\lambda_{k}
are the values of
t
at which the slope changes, and the
critical points
of the landscape
\lambda
is the union of all the critical points for each
\lambda_{k}
. A persistence landscape is completely determined by its critical points. In fact, the standard implementation of landscape methods keeps track of the critical points rather than the landscape as a function
[
11
]
. One can list the critical points of a landscape in terms of the corresponding barcode as follows.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
14
]
, Lemma 5.8)
.

Given a barcode
B=\{(b_{i},d_{i})\}
, the critical points in the corresponding landscape
\lambda^{B}
consist of

1.

the left endpoints
b_{i}
,

2.

the right endpoints
d_{i}
,

3.

the midpoints
\frac{b_{i}+d_{i}}{2}
,

4.

the midpoints
\frac{b_{i}+b_{j}}{2}
of pairs of bars where
b_{j}<b_{i}<d_{j}<d_{i}
.

Definition 2.6
.

Given a landscape
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},...\lambda_{d})
, we denote by
\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the time-series obtained by evaluating
\lambda
at all of its critical points, i.e.
\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is the sequence of critical values of
\lambda
. An example is given in Example
2.12
.

Remark 2.7
.

In practice, we work with landscapes with a fixed number of levels, say
d
, where we truncate levels, or extend levels that are identically zero as necessary to reach
d
levels.

The assumption in the above remark allows us to associate a time-series to a landscape (hence to a barcode) in a canonical way. We call this
discrete landscape embedding
and denote it by

I:\BBar\rightarrow\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(2.11)

Analogous to the feature maps in Subsection

, we define the

as the composition of the discrete signature and the discrete landscape embedding:

\Phi_{I}:\BBar\xrightarrow{I}\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})\xrightarrow{\Sigma}\mathbb{T}((\mathscr{V}_{d})).

(2.12)

The following naive example illustrates how the discrete feature map distinguish between landscapes that are tree-like equivalent.

Lemma 2.9
.

The signature of a barcode

\lambda^{B}
is completely determined by the persistence of the bars in

Proof.

If
\lambda^{B}
has a single level then there exists a total ordering

of

(d,b)\prec(d^{\prime},b^{\prime})

Corollary 2.10
.

The coefficients corresponding to univariate polynomials

are determined by the persistence.

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{I}(B),Z^{\otimes n}\rangle

\displaystyle=0\hskip 73.97716pt\text{if }n\equiv 1\ (mod\ 2)

.

\displaystyle\langle\Sigma(\underline{\mathbb{x}}),Z_{2}\otimes Z_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}

2.3
Continuity of the discrete landscape feature map

The discrete signature, being a component-wise polynomial map, is continuous. However, the discrete landscape embedding is not, hence the discrete landscape feature map is not continuous either. The key obstruction is illustrated in the following example.

Example 2.13
.

Let
B=\{(b,d)\}
, with
d>b
, and
B^{\prime}=\{(b,d),(\frac{d-b}{4},\frac{d-b}{4}+\delta)\}
for some
0<\delta<<d-b
. Their bottleneck distance (cf. Section
2.4
) is
\dist_{\B}(B,B^{\prime})=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2}}
, and the corresponding persistence landscapes are

B
B^{\prime}

Some coefficients in the discrete signatures are such that no choice of
\delta
will make their difference small. For instance,

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{I}(B),Z_{1}\otimes Z_{1}\otimes Z_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{I}(B^{\prime}),Z_{1}\otimes Z_{1}\otimes Z_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{(d-b)^{3}}{2^{5}}+o(\delta)\ .

The heart of the issue with continuity is the poor behaviour of the discrete signature under refinement of segment decomposition (cf. Remark
2.4
). Indeed, in the above example, the inclusion of a bar with arbitrarily small persistence, which produces a barcode that is close in bottleneck distance, forces a refinement of segment decomposition, whence the discontinuity.

2.4
The specialised discrete landscape feature map

For applications, one may want to compare barcodes using signatures as input for machine learning methods
[
33
]
. In light of this, it makes sense to consider a finite subset
D\subset\BBar
. Then we can modify the discrete embedding map
I:\BBar\rightarrow\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(
2.11
) as follows. Consider the union of all critical points of the landscapes arising from the domain
D

C_{D}:=\{c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{N}\}=\{c\ |\ c\ \text{critical point of}\ \lambda^{B}\text{ for some }B\in D\}\ ,

and define the
specialised discrete embedding map

\begin{matrix}I_{D}:&D&\rightarrow&\BV([0,L],\mathbb{R}^{d})&\rightarrow&\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
&B&\mapsto&\lambda^{B}&\mapsto&(\lambda^{B}(c_{1}),\lambda^{B}(c_{2}),...,\lambda^{B}(c_{N}))\end{matrix}\ .

(2.13)

This restriction allows us to have a consistent segment subdivision of the landscapes across all barcodes in
D
, which then guarantees continuity of the
specialised discrete landscape feature map

\Phi_{I_{D}}^{\leq k}:D\xrightarrow{I_{D}}\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})\xrightarrow{\Sigma^{\leq k}}\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))

(2.14)

where
\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
denotes the space of discrete signature tensors truncated at weight
k
.

For practical purposes, a particularly desirable property of a feature map would be stability, or equivalently being
1
-Lipschitz. A weaker but still desirable condition is uniform continuity. We aim that the specialised discrete landscape feature map
\Phi^{\leq k}_{I_{D}}
is uniformly continuous. Before proving this, we describes explicitly the metrics on all the involved spaces, that is
\BBar
,
\BV([0,L],\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
.

Bottleneck distance.

The
bottleneck distance
was classically introduced for persistence diagrams in
[
17
, Sec. 3]
, and it translates in the following distance between barcodes:

\dist_{\B}(B,B^{\prime}):=\inf_{\tau}\sup_{B_{i}\in B}\left\lVert B_{i}-\tau(B_{i})\right\rVert_{\infty}

where
\tau
runs among the bijections
\tau:B\cup\{(b,b)|b\in\mathbb{R}\}\rightarrow B^{\prime}\cup\{(b,b)|b\in\mathbb{R}\}
allowing matching birth-death points to diagonal points. This makes the definition consistent when the two barcodes have different number of intervals. The distance between landscapes is the
\infty
–norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
, as considered in
[
13
, Sec. 5]
:

\dist_{\infty}(\lambda^{B},\lambda^{B^{\prime}}):=\left\lVert\lambda^{B}-\lambda^{B^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{t}\left\{\left\lVert\lambda^{B}(t)-\lambda^{B^{\prime}}(t)\right\rVert_{2}\right\}\ .

Finally, given two time-series in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the same length
n
, the distance between them that we consider is

\dist_{\TS}(\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}})=\max\left\{\left\lVert\mathbb{x}_{i}-\mathbb{y}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\ |\ i\in[n]\right\}\ .

Bombieri-Weyl product.

The (non-degenerate) scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defining the
2
–norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
naturally induces a scalar product on
\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
, called
Bombieri-Weyl product
. Fix an orthonormal basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
Sym^{\bullet}\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the graded symmetric algebra over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is isomorphic (as graded algebras) to the polynomial ring
\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}]
: one identifies the basis vector
e_{i}
with the variable
Z_{i}
. In particular, the isomorphism restricts to each graded component, so that
Sym^{h}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is isomorphic to the vector space
\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}]_{h}
of homogeneous polynomials in
d
variables of degree
h
. For any
h\geq 0
, the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
extends to a scalar product, called
Bombieri-Weyl product
(BW-product), on
\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}]_{h}
defined on the monomials (and extended by linearity) by

\langle Z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots Z_{d}^{\alpha_{d}},Z_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots Z_{d}^{\beta_{d}}\rangle_{sym}=\binom{h}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ ,

where
\binom{h}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\frac{h!}{\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{d}!}
and the latter symbol is the Kronecker symbol. The BW-product on
Sym^{h}\mathbb{R}^{d}
induces the Bombieri-Weyl norm, also known as
Kostlan norm
: for any degree–
h
monomial
\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=Z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots Z_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}
it holds

\left\lVert\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rVert_{sym}^{2}=\binom{h}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}\ .

One extends the BW-product to the whole algebra
Sym^{\bullet}\mathbb{R}^{d}\simeq\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}]
by imposing orthogonality between monomials with different grades. This defines a scalar product on
\mathscr{V}_{d}
. Again, this extends to any tensor-power of
\mathscr{V}_{d}^{\otimes\ell}
, resulting in the following norm: given
\ell
monomials
\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}},\ldots,\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}
in
\mathscr{V}_{d}
such that each
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=(\alpha_{i,1},\ldots,\alpha_{i,d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
is a partition of the degree
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}|=\alpha_{i,1}+\ldots+\alpha_{i,d}
, it holds

\left\lVert\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}\right\rVert_{BW}^{2}=\left\lVert\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}}\right\rVert_{sym}^{2}\cdots\left\lVert\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}\right\rVert_{sym}^{2}=\binom{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}|}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}}^{-1}\cdots\binom{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}|}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}^{-1}\ .

Analogously, one extends the scalar product to
\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
by imposing orthogonality between tensor-products of different order. In particular, the BW-norm on
\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
is

\left\lVert\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}|+\ldots+|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}|\leq k}C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}\right\rVert_{BW}^{2}=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}|+\ldots+|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}|\leq k}C_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}^{2}\binom{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}|}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}}^{-1}\cdots\binom{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}|}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}^{-1}\ .

This allows to define the following
Bombieri-Weyl distance
on
\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
: given two elements
f,g\in\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
with coefficients
C^{f}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}
and
C^{g}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}
respectively, it holds

\displaystyle\dist_{BW}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}|+\ldots+|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}|\leq k}\left(C^{f}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}-C^{g}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}\right)\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbb{Z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}\right\rVert_{BW}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}|+\ldots+|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}|\leq k}\left(C^{f}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}-C^{g}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}\right)^{2}\binom{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}|}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}}^{-1}\cdots\binom{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}|}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}^{-1}

(2.15)

Uniform continuity.

In our case we start from the standard scalar product
\langle\mathbb{u},\mathbb{w}\rangle=\sum u_{i}w_{i}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defining the
2
–norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
.

Theorem 2.14
.

Given
D\subset\BBar
a finite set of barcodes, the specialised discrete landscape feature map
\Phi_{I_{D}}^{\leq k}
(
2.14
) is uniformly continuous with respect to the bottleneck distance on
\BBar
and the Bombieri-Weyl distance on
\mathbb{T}^{\leq k}((\mathscr{V}_{d}))
.

Proof.

We divide the proof in more steps, showing that each map involved in
\Phi_{I_{D}}^{\leq k}
is uniformly continous.

Claim 1: The discrete embedding
I_{D}
is uniformly continuous.
The map
I_{D}
can be factored as
I_{d}=\mathbf{ev}\circ\lambda
where
\lambda:D\rightarrow\BV(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the persistence landscape map restricted to
D
, and
\mathbf{ev}
is evaluation at all the points in
C_{D}
.

In
[
14
, Theorem 2.4]
, it was shown that
\lambda
is stable, meaning
1
-Lipschitz where
\BV(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has the
\infty
–norm: in particular,
\lambda
is uniformly continuous. On the other hand, the evaluation map
\mathbf{ev}
is evaluation of persistence landscapes (which are piecewise linear functions) at a fixed set of points, so it is also
1
-Lipschitz, hence uniformly continuous.

Claim 2: The discrete signature
\Sigma
is uniformly continuous.
We need to prove that for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
\gamma>0
such that

\dist_{\TS}(\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}})<\gamma\ \implies\left\lVert\Sigma^{\leq k}(\underline{\mathbb{x}})-\Sigma^{\leq k}(\underline{\mathbb{y}})\right\rVert_{BW}<\epsilon\ .

From (
2.15
), and since we are working with a truncation of the discrete signature, it is enough to show the statement for any coefficient
|C^{\underline{\mathbb{x}}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}-C^{\underline{\mathbb{y}}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}|
.

Given
\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}}\in\TS(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of length
n
, consider their difference time-series
\Delta\underline{\mathbb{x}},\Delta\underline{\mathbb{y}}
of length
n-1
. Flatten them in the vectors
\boldsymbol{\Delta_{x}}:=(\Delta\mathbb{x}_{1},\ldots,\Delta\mathbb{x}_{n-1})
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta_{y}}:=(\Delta\mathbb{y}_{1},\ldots,\Delta\mathbb{y}_{n-1})
of length
d(n-1)
. In the polynomial ring
\mathbb{R}[T_{11},\ldots,T_{1d},T_{21},\ldots,T_{n-1\,d}]
consider the polynomial

P(\mathbb{T}):=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\lneq\ldots\lneq i_{\ell}<n}p_{1}(T_{i_{1}\,1},\ldots,T_{i_{1}\,d})\cdots p_{\ell}(T_{i_{\ell}\,1},\ldots,T_{i_{\ell}\,d})\ .

Then it holds

P(\boldsymbol{\Delta_{x}})=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\lneq\ldots\lneq i_{\ell}<n}p_{1}(\Delta\mathbb{x}_{i_{1}})\cdots p_{\ell}(\Delta\mathbb{x}_{i_{\ell}})=C^{\underline{\mathbb{x}}}_{p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell}}\ .

But polynomials (on a compact domain) are uniformly continuous, hence for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\Delta_{y}}\right\rVert_{2}<\delta\ \implies\ \big|C^{\underline{\mathbb{x}}}_{p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell}}-C^{\underline{\mathbb{y}}}_{p_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes p_{\ell}}\big|=\big|P(\boldsymbol{\Delta_{x}})-P(\boldsymbol{\Delta_{y}})\big|<\epsilon\ .

Now, if

\dist_{\TS}(\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}}):=\max\left\{\left\lVert\mathbb{x}_{i}-\mathbb{y}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\ |\ i\in[n]\right\}\leq\frac{\delta}{2(n-1)}\ ,

one gets

\left\lVert\Delta\mathbb{x}_{i+1}-\Delta\mathbb{y}_{i+1}\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\mathbb{x}_{i+1}-\mathbb{x}_{i}-\mathbb{y}_{i+1}+\mathbb{y}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{x}_{i+1}-\mathbb{y}_{i+1}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\mathbb{x}_{i}-\mathbb{y}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\delta}{n-1}

implying that on the flattenings it holds

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Delta_{x}}-\boldsymbol{\Delta_{y}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sum_{j\in[n-1]}\left\lVert\Delta\mathbb{x}_{j}-\Delta\mathbb{y}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\delta\ .

We conclude that it is enough to take
\gamma:=\frac{\delta}{2(n-1)}
so that the hypothesis
\dist_{\TS}(\underline{\mathbb{x}},\underline{\mathbb{y}})<\gamma
implies
|C^{\underline{\mathbb{x}}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}-C^{\underline{\mathbb{y}}}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\ell}}|<\epsilon
. This proves that the truncated discrete signature map is uniformly continuous.
∎

We remark that discrete signatures characterise landscapes, hence barcodes, up to translating nonzero sections of a landscape along regions of the domain where the landscape is identically zero. Indeed, identically zero regions of the landscape produce consecutive zero vectors in the time-series of the critical points, and these can be contracted to a unique zero vector by time-warping.

3
Application to knotted proteins

In this section we apply discrete signatures to a dataset of knotted proteins drawn from KnotProt 2.0
[
18
]
, a curated database of proteins with entangled backbone structures. Knotted proteins have attracted considerable interest across structural biology and computational biology because the presence and localisation of entanglement are closely linked to protein folding pathways, structural stability, and functional behaviour
[
23
,
1
]
. In particular, even small topological changes in the backbone can lead to significant differences in stability and dynamics. Understanding and quantifying such entanglement therefore requires tools that capture both geometric and topological features of protein structure.

We focus on proteins whose backbones form open-ended positive trefoil knots, the most common and well-studied class of knotted proteins. In this setting, the knot type is assigned using standard closure procedures for open curves, as implemented in KnotProt and related software. This provides a consistent topological framework in which to compare protein structures while retaining their geometric information.

The data are obtained from the Protein Data Bank
[
9
]
and curated in KnotProt 2.0, which
consists of the three-dimensional coordinates of the carbon (C-alpha) atoms of each protein backbone, and additional information about the backbone structure (i.e. the ordering of C-alpha atoms along the chain), the full amino-acid sequence, and annotations such as knot type, knot core, and tail regions.

Following
[
8
]
, we interpolate five equidistant points between successive C-alpha atoms in order to obtain a sufficiently dense approximation of the piecewise linear backbone curve. We then compute one-dimensional persistent homology and persistence landscapes truncated at level
15
. The proteins are categorized into structural classes according to amino-acid
sequence similarity
; proteins with sufficiently similar sequences are expected to have similar three-dimensional structures. We adopt the same convention as
[
8
]
and label the class by a representative protein of the class, or “other” whenever there were insufficient similar sequences to define additional classes. In the present analysis, we exclude proteins labelled “other” from the sequence-similarity comparison, leaving
363
proteins and
9
structural classes.

The knot depth is a standard geometric quantity used to quantify how far the knot core is located from the endpoints of the chain
[
5
]
. More precisely, if
N
and
C
denote the N-tail and C-tail, respectively, and
T
denotes the whole chain, then the depth of a protein chain
p
is

D(p)=\frac{l(N)\,l(C)}{l(T)^{2}},

(3.1)

where
l(\cdot)
counts the number of C-alpha atoms in the relevant subchain.

In
[
8
]
the authors compute pairwise Wasserstein distances on persistence diagrams and pairwise
L_{1}
distances on persistence landscapes, and apply Isomap to obtain low-dimensional embeddings of the protein dataset. These embeddings reveal a visible organisation of proteins according to sequence homology class and knot depth, providing qualitative evidence of a correlation between persistent homology features and biologically meaningful structure.

Building on this framework, we move from qualitative visual analysis to a quantitative comparison based on feature vectors associated to persistence landscapes. In addition to the discrete landscape feature map (DLFM), we implement and compare several alternative approaches, including continuous (Chen) signatures of landscapes and integrated landscapes, as well as baseline methods based on direct vectorisation and
L_{1}
distances.

For the computation of discrete signatures, we use the software FRUITS (
Feature Extraction Using Iterated Sums
)
[
22
]
. Continuous path signatures are computed using the
pysiglib
library
[
37
]
, which provides efficient implementations of Chen signatures for piecewise linear paths. The corresponding code for all experiments in this section is publicly available at
https://github.com/dtolosav/discrete-sig-for-landscapes
. We opted for a straightforward implementation of the DLFM, without performing feature selection or dimensionality reduction at the encoding stage.

Note that both path signatures and discrete signatures are amenable to kernelization
[
30
,
31
]
, which has the potential to significantly improve computational aspects while preserving desirable properties.

15
levels and using the same clustering procedure, except for the

Discrete signatures for predicting knot depth.

We now demonstrate that DLFM features can be used more generally as input to standard machine learning methods, beyond simple clustering, by considering a different task: predicting the
knot depth
(
3.1
).

We investigate whether DLFM features encode information about knot depth using two complementary approaches. First, we compute the
Spearman rank correlation
between pairwise distances in the DLFM feature space and differences in knot depth. This non-parametric measure assesses whether proteins that are close in feature space tend to have similar knot depth, and whether increasing distances in feature space correspond to increasing differences in depth. We obtain a correlation coefficient of
0.647
with p-value
<0.001
, indicating a statistically significant positive association between discrete signature features and knot depth.

Second, we evaluate the predictive power of DLFM features using a
random forest
regression model. Random forest is a learning method based on decision trees, capable of capturing nonlinear relationships between features and target variables
[
10

. Using DLFM feature vectors as input, the model achieves a coefficient of determination
R^{2}=0.866\pm 0.048
, meaning that approximately
86.6\%
of the variance in knot depth is explained by the model. Such value of
R^{2}
indicates strong predictive performance and shows that the discrete signature provides a sufficiently rich representation to support accurate regression tasks.

These results illustrate that DLFM feature vectors can be used effectively in a variety of machine learning settings, not only for clustering but also for predictive modelling of geometric properties.

The experiments above demonstrate that the discrete landscape feature map provides a robust and informative representation of persistence landscapes for downstream data analysis. In particular, DLFM consistently outperforms existing signature-based and landscape-based approaches in recovering biologically meaningful structure, while also supporting predictive modeling of geometric quantities such as knot depth. Together with the theoretical results established earlier, this highlights the potential of discrete signatures as a powerful and flexible tool for topological data analysis in applications.

k
–th universal signature variety

\mathcal{U}_{d,k}
is the Zariski closure of the (projectivisation) of the image of
\sigma^{(k)}
inside
\mathbb{P}(V^{\otimes k})
(cf.
[
3
, Sec. 4.3]
).

We focus on the case
k=2
, namely
varieties of signature matrices
. By construction, these lie in the universal signature varieties
\mathcal{U}_{d,2}\subset\mathbb{P}(V\otimes V)
. Given a path
\gamma\in\BV([0,L],V)
, the second signature
\sigma^{(2)}(\gamma)\in V^{\otimes 2}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
is a matrix with entries

\sigma_{ij}^{(2)}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{L}\gamma_{i}(s)\dot{\gamma}_{j}(s)ds\ .

(4.1)

We denote the symmetric and skew-symmetric part of
\sigma^{(2)}
by
\sigma^{(2)}_{\rm{sym}}:=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{(2)}+(\sigma^{(2)})^{\bfT})
and
\sigma^{(2)}_{\rm{skew}}:=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{(2)}-(\sigma^{(2)})^{\bfT})
respectively. Applying integration by parts to (
4.1
) leads to

2\sigma^{(2)}_{\rm{sym}}=\gamma(L)^{\otimes 2}\ .

(4.2)

In fact, signature matrices are precisely those matrices with rank-
1
symmetric part
[
3
, Secc. 2,3]
, that is

\mathcal{U}_{d,2}=\left\{v\otimes v+Q\ |\ v\in V,\ Q\in\textstyle{\bigwedge^{2}V}\right\}\ .

(4.3)

Let
\mathcal{M}_{d,m}
be the variety of signature matrices of
m
–PWL paths in
V
. Then for any
m

[
3
, Theorem 3.4]

\mathcal{M}_{d,m}=\left\{v\otimes v+Q\ |\ v\in V,\ \text{rk}[v\otimes v|Q]\leq m\right\}\ ,

(4.4)

where
[v\otimes v|Q]
is the matrix obtained by attaching the rank-
1
matrix
v\otimes v
and the matrix
Q
. The varieties
\mathcal{M}_{d,m}
define a filtration starting at the Veronese variety
\nu_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{d-1})
and stabilizing at the universal variety of signature matrices
[
3
, Theorem 3.4, Example 4.12]

\nu_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{d-1})=\mathcal{M}_{d,1}\subset\mathcal{M}_{d,2}\subset\ldots\subset\mathcal{M}_{d,d}=\mathcal{U}_{d,2}\subset\mathbb{P}(V^{\otimes 2})\ .

Signature tensors of PWL loops.

Since landscapes are PWL loops, it is quite natural to first describe the signature varieties of such larger class of paths. Since signature tensors are invariant under translation, we consider loops starting and ending at
\mathbb{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We denote by
\operatorname{log\sigma}(\lambda)
the
log-signature
of
\lambda

[
24
]
, that is a sequence of tensors defined by the property that
\sigma(\lambda)=\exp(\operatorname{log\sigma}(\lambda))
, and by
\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(k)}(\lambda)
its
k
-th entry. By definition of the exponential map, the following truncations hold:

\displaystyle\sigma^{(1)}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(1)}(\lambda)\ ,

\displaystyle\sigma^{(2)}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(2)}(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(1)}(\lambda)\right)^{2}\ ,

\displaystyle\sigma^{(3)}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(3)}(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(1)}(\lambda)\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(2)}(\lambda)+\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(2)}(\lambda)\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(1)}(\lambda)\right)+\frac{1}{6}\left(\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(1)}(\lambda)\right)^{3}\ .

Each tensor space
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes k}
admits a decomposition as direct sum in Thrall modules, see
[
4
, Sec. 3]
. Each summand in the right-hand sides of the above equations belongs to a different Thrall module, so all summands are linearly independent.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\lambda
be a PWL path with MSD
\alpha_{1}\ast\cdots\ast\alpha_{m}
where
\alpha_{j}(t)=\mathbb{a}_{j}t+c_{j}
for
\mathbb{a}_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and suitable
c_{j}
in order to make the path conjuction continuous. The following facts are equivalent:

1.

\lambda
is a loop;

2.

\sigma^{(1)}(\lambda)=\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(1)}(\lambda)=0
;

3.

\sigma^{(2)}(\lambda)=\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(2)}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i<j}\mathbb{a}_{i}\wedge\mathbb{a}_{j}
;

4.

\sigma^{(3)}(\lambda)=\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(3)}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

In the above notation, the first signature of the
m
-PWL path
\lambda
is
\sigma^{(1)}(\lambda)=\mathbb{a}_{1}+\ldots+\mathbb{a}_{m}
. The latter sum being the zero vector is equivalent to
\lambda
being a loop. Moreover, the first signature always coincides with the first log-signature, hence
(1)\iff(2)
. The second equality in
(3)
always holds (cf.
[
24
, Example 7.3]
). If the first log-signature is zero, then
\sigma^{(2)}(\lambda)=\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(2)}(\lambda)
. On the contrary, if the first equality in
(3)
holds, then
(\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(1)}(\lambda))^{\otimes 2}=\mathbb{0}
(as matrices) implies
\operatorname{log\sigma}^{(1)}(\lambda)=\mathbb{0}
(as vectors). This proves
(2)\iff(3)
. Finally, the equivalence
(2)\iff(4)
follows from the linear independence among summands lying in different Thrall modules.
∎

The above characterisations of PWL loops in terms of their signature matrices allow to describe the corresponding varieties. We denote the variety of signature matrices of
m
–PWL loops in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
by

\mathcal{M}_{d,m}^{\circlearrowright}:=\overline{\left\{\sigma^{(2)}(\lambda)\ |\ \lambda\ \text{$m$-PWL loop in}\ \mathbb{C}^{d}\right\}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{d,m}\subset\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})\ ,

and the variety of signature matrices of loops in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
(not necessarily PWL) by

\mathcal{U}^{\circlearrowright}_{d,2}:=\overline{\left\{\sigma^{(2)}(\lambda)\ |\ \lambda\ \text{loop in}\ \mathbb{C}^{d}\right\}}\subset\mathcal{U}_{d,2}\ .

Let
Gr(2,d)\subset\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{d})
the Grassmannian of planes in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, corresponding to
d\times d
skew-symmetric matrices of rank
2
, and let
\sigma_{r}(Gr(2,d))\subset\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{d})
be the
r
–th secant variety of
Gr(2,d)
, corresponding to
d\times d
skew-symmetric matrices of rank at most
2r
.

Corollary 4.2
.

In the above notation, it holds:

1.

\mathcal{U}^{\circlearrowright}_{d,2}=\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{2}V)
;

2.

\mathcal{M}_{d,m}^{\circlearrowright}=\sigma_{\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}(Gr(2,d))
, for any
m\leq d
.

In particular,
\mathcal{M}_{d,d+h}^{\circlearrowright}=\mathcal{U}^{\circlearrowright}_{d,2}=\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{2}V)
for any
h\geq 0
.

Proof.

Up to translation, a loop
\lambda:[0,L]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}
is such that
\lambda(0)=\lambda(L)=\mathbb{0}
, hence the signature matrix of
\lambda
has symmetric part
\sigma^{(2)}_{\rm{sym}}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda(L)-\lambda(0))^{\otimes 2}=\mathbb{0}^{\otimes 2}
. This also follows from Proposition
4.1
. Then the two theses follow straightforward from the definitions (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) by cutting the varieties
\mathcal{M}_{d,m}\subset\mathcal{U}_{d,2}\subset\mathbb{P}(V^{\otimes 2})
by
\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{2}V)
.
∎

Signature tensors of landscapes.

By construction, the number
m
of segments in the MSD of a landscape with
d
levels (i.e. a landscape path in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) is
strictly lower bounded
by
d
, that is
d\lneq m
. Thus it makes sense to consider the (universal) variety of signature matrices of landscapes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
regardless of the number of linear pieces in the MSD. We denote such variety by

\mathcal{M}_{d,2}^{\rm{LS}}:=\overline{\left\{\sigma^{(2)}(\lambda)\ |\ \lambda\ \text{landscape path in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{d,2}^{\circlearrowright}=\mathbb{P}\left(\textstyle{\bigwedge^{2}}\mathbb{C}^{d}\right)\ .

Consider a landscape path
\lambda^{B}(t)=(\lambda_{1}(t),\ldots,\lambda_{d}(t))\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with MSD
\lambda^{B}=\alpha_{1}\ast\cdots\ast\alpha_{m}
defined on the partition
[0,t_{1}]\cup[t_{1},t_{2}]\cup\ldots\cup[t_{m-1},L]
such that each segment
\alpha_{j}:[t_{j-1},t_{j}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as
\alpha_{j}(t)=\mathbb{a}_{j}\cdot t+\alpha_{j-1}(t_{j-1})
for a suitable
\mathbb{a}_{j}=(a_{1j},\ldots,a_{dj})^{\bfT}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
. Then each
\lambda^{B}
defines a
d\times m
matrix
X_{\lambda}:=[\mathbb{a}_{1}|\cdots|\mathbb{a}_{m}]
.

The conditions on the levels of a landscape (cf. Sec.
1.2
) impose some semialgebraic conditions, besides the algebraic condition of being a loop: clearly,
\mathcal{M}_{d,2}^{\rm{LS}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{d,2}^{\circlearrowright}=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{d}\right)
. For instance, the non-negativity of sums over each row implies
\mathbb{a}_{1}+\ldots+\mathbb{a}_{k}\geq 0
for any
k\leq m
, while the dominance of higher levels is equivalent to
\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{ij}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{i+1\,j}
. But there are also non semi-algebraic relations, such as the one on the modulus of the entries. We leave the study of the variety
\mathcal{M}_{d,2}^{\rm{LS}}
for future work. Observe that the orbit argument in
[
3
, Example 2.3]
does not apply since the number of segments
m
is greater than
d
.

Observe that the space of
d
-barcodes depends on
2d-1
parameters, as one can choose
2d
values for the
d
birth-death points but initialise the first birth at
0
. By the definition of levels in (
1.6
), it follows that a barcode of
d
intervals defines a landscape path with at most
d
levels. Therefore, via the landscape embedding, a barcode with
d
intervals defines a (skewsymmetric) signature matrix of size
d\times d
. Assuming that such mapping is injective, then the variety
\mathcal{M}_{d,2}^{\rm{LS}}\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{d})
has dimension at most
2d-1
. A dimensional count implies that the variety
\mathcal{M}_{d,2}^{\rm{LS}}
does not fill the ambient space for every
d\geq 6
. It would be interesting to determine such variety.

Conjecture 4.3
.

For
d\leq 5
the variety of signature matrices of landscapes
\mathcal{M}_{d,2}^{\rm{LS}}
coincides with the variety of signature matrices of loops
\mathcal{M}_{d,2}^{\circlearrowright}=\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{C}^{d})
.
