Joint association of risk factor clustering and socioeconomic position with non-communicable disease mortality risk: a prospective cohort study of 576 299 Norwegian men and women in the NCDNOR project
Authors: Knut Eirik Dalene, Simon Lergenmuller, Hein Stigum, Haakon Eduard Meyer, Wenche Nystad, Inger K Larsen, Erik Reidar Sund, Steinar Krokstad, Laila A Hopstock, Ulf Ekelund, Lars Jøran Kjerpeseth, Inger Ariansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWell-known non-communicable disease (NCD) risk factors tend to cluster, forming groups with similar ‘risk factor phenotypes’. However, the characterisation of such clusters and the role of socioeconomic position (SEP) in modifying their associations with NCDs still remain unclear.MethodsWe utilised data on smoking, physical activity, alcohol consumption, body mass index, blood pressure, cholesterol and triglyceride from health examination surveys conducted in Norwegian adults from 1974 to 2019 (n=5 76 299; age 20–65 years; 64% participation; 52% women) linked to registry data on education, income and mortality (follow-up through 2020). We identified risk factor clusters using latent profile analyses and used flexible parametric survival models to estimate differences in mean age at NCD death across sex, different birth years and SEP (education and income levels).ResultsWe identified four distinct clusters containing 44% (‘healthy’), 45% (‘suboptimal’), 4% (‘dyslipidaemics’) and 7% (‘hypertensives’) of the study sample, respectively. During a mean follow-up of 26 years, 61 241 died from an NCD.Compared with the cluster with the healthiest profile, other clusters had markedly shorter survival across sex and different birth cohorts. However, differences were also substantially modified by SEP: we estimated that individuals with primary education or low income died several years earlier than individuals with tertiary education or high income within each of the four risk factor clusters.ConclusionsOur findings show how NCD risk factors cluster and how SEP modifies the association between this clustering and NCD mortality. The substantial influence of SEP highlights the need for public health strategies that focus not only on changing behavioural and metabolic risk factors but also on addressing socioeconomic disparities to effectively reduce the burden of NCDs.Keywords: Effect Modifier, Epidemiologic; Risk Assessment; Preventive Medicine; Public Health; Primary Prevention

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In a cohort of nearly 600 000 Norwegian adults, we identified distinct risk factor clusters using latent profile analysis and examined how their associations with NCD mortality vary across socioeconomic position using flexible parametric survival models. We found large differences in NCD mortality across clusters and observed that socioeconomic position strongly modifies these associations, contributing substantially to differences in survival both within and between risk factor clusters.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD), cancers, chronic respiratory diseases and diabetes cause 80% of non-communicable disease (NCD) deaths globally,1 with modifiable risk factors contributing substantially to NCD morbidity and mortality.2,5 These risk factors often co-occur,6 emphasising the need to better understand how risk factors cluster and impact mortality risk. This understanding is crucial to achieve the United Nations’ goal of reducing premature NCD mortality by one-third by 2030.7

Although several scoring tools that incorporate multiple risk factors to estimate disease risk have been developed, they typically predict single NCD outcomes in individuals.8 Similarly, most epidemiological studies employ a ‘one risk factor and one disease’ approach, adjusting for other risk factors to create ‘all-else-equal’ scenarios. Although valuable for clinical decision-making and isolating effects of individual risk factors, these methods may not capture the complex interplay of multiple risk factors impacting various NCDs at the population level.

An alternative is using statistical methods to identify groups of individuals with similar patterns of risk factor levels. A few studies have used such approaches,9,11 and one previous study examined how risk factor clustering associates with CVD.12 However, when clustering of risk factors is studied, continuous data are often dichotomised (eg, hypertension yes/no), leading to loss of important information.

Socioeconomic position influences both risk factor levels and mortality risks.13 Similar levels of single risk factors are often associated with different mortality risks among individuals with different socioeconomic backgrounds,14 15 something partially attributed to the clustering of risk factors among socially disadvantaged groups.16 However, whether groups with similar risk factor clustering experience different mortality risks across socioeconomic positions, and how different risk factor clustering impacts mortality risks within socioeconomic groups remains largely unknown.

In this study, we used data on both behavioural and metabolic NCD risk factors from nearly 600 000 individuals to identify clustering of NCD risk factors using latent profile analysis (LPA) and examine the joint association of risk factor clustering and socioeconomic position with NCD mortality risk.

Materials and methods

Study design, participants and assessments

This is a prospective cohort study based on harmonised data from Norwegian population-based health examination surveys conducted between 1974 and 2019 linked to the Norwegian Cause of Death Registry, the Cancer Registry of Norway, and data on education, income and other demographics from Statistics Norway.

The health examination surveys included had a combined participation of 63.8% (figure 1) and have been described extensively elsewhere.17,19 We included participants aged 20–65 years at baseline and used self-reported data on smoking pack-years ((cigarettes/day÷20)×smoking years), and on leisure-time physical activity harmonised into four levels: ‘inactive’, ‘somewhat active’, ‘active’ and ‘very active’ (online supplemental tables 1–7). We calculated body mass index (BMI) from measured height and weight (kg/m²). Blood pressure was measured manually two times until the mid-1980s, three times using automatic measurements thereafter, and we used the second manual measurement and the mean of the second and third automatic measurement. If only two automatic measurements were available, we used the last measurement.17 Serum concentrations of total cholesterol and triglycerides were analysed from non-fasting samples.20 For descriptive purposes and additional analyses, we used the following cut-off values for hypertension, hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia: ≥140 mm Hg (systolic) and/or ≥90 mm Hg (diastolic); ≥6.2 mmol/L; ≥4.0 mmol/L.21,23 Self-reported data on alcohol consumption (grams/day) were available in a subsample (online supplemental table 8).

We obtained data on education, income, immigration and emigration from Statistics Norway.24 We categorised highest attained education at baseline using the International Standard Classification of Education 2011: 0–2 (‘primary’); 3–5 (‘secondary’); 6–8 (‘tertiary’), and classified income into year-specific and sex-specific tertiles, using the highest tertile attained at baseline. We also included data on cancers diagnosed ≤5 years before baseline from the Cancer Registry of Norway,25 and self-reported data on a history of angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, stroke, diabetes and use of antihypertensive medications. Participants with these conditions were retained in the main analyses but excluded in sensitivity analyses (see below).

We obtained mortality data from the Norwegian Cause of Death Registry.26 The codes used to identify CVD, cancer, diabetes and chronic respiratory disease deaths are displayed in online supplemental table 9. Participants were followed until emigration, death or follow-up end (31 December 2020).

Statistical analyses

We used unconditional LPA including only data on the included risk factors (pack-years, physical activity (four-ordered levels, treated as continuous), BMI, blood pressure, total cholesterol and triglycerides) to identify risk factor clusters (gsem in Stata/MP V.18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas)). To guide the identification of the optimal number of clusters, we fitted models with increasing number of clusters and evaluated each model in terms of: Akaike Information Criterion, Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Sample Size Adjusted BIC, classification power (model entropy; posterior probability tables) and cluster distribution and relevance.27 28 Once the optimal number was decided, we allocated participants to the cluster for which they had the largest posterior probability. All analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach. See online supplemental materials for details.

Associations between risk factor cluster and NCD mortality were estimated using flexible parametric survival models, with age as analysis time and follow-up starting at age of health examination survey participation (stpm3, gensplines, predict and standsurv packages/commands in Stata/MP V.18.0; see online supplemental methods for details). All models were adjusted for age (analysis time), sex, birth year and cluster×sex and cluster×birth year interactions. To investigate the joint association of SEP and risk factor cluster with NCD mortality, we estimated separate models for education and income because they represent related but distinct indicators of SEP. These models included risk factor cluster, sex, birth year and either education or income, with interaction terms between cluster and sex, cluster and birth year and cluster and the respective SEP indicator. The baseline log cumulative hazard was modelled using a spline with five df. We restricted follow-up until a maximum age of 100 years and used the cluster with the lowest level of risk factor exposure overall as the referent. We relaxed the proportional hazards assumption by estimating time-varying HRs using splines. Because of non-proportionality, and other shortcomings of HRs,29 we present differences in restricted mean survival time (RMST) as our main effect measure,30 31 and HRs plotted against time as online supplemental materials (main analysis only). To ease interpretation of interactions, we present estimates predicted from the model by sex, two birth years (1930 and 1960) and socioeconomic indicator level.

Additional analyses

We conducted several additional analyses: (1) because of known secular changes in individual risk factors, we conducted separate LPAs for periods 1974–1989, 1990–2004, 2005–2019; (2) in the subsample with alcohol data, we examined the impact of including alcohol in the LPA; (3) to address whether pre-existing or subclinical disease influenced risk factors before baseline, we performed landmark analyses with follow-up starting 5 years after health examination survey participation, excluding those with known disease; (4) we explored the potential impact of competing risks (death from non-NCD causes) by also modelling all-cause mortality as the outcome, and by comparing restricted mean failure times (RMFTs) estimated from failure functions and cumulative incidence functions (CIFs). The latter was done by sex and birth year only, since estimation of RMFTs from CIFs in competing risks models was computationally too intensive to also include cluster×education/income interactions; (5) to assess the impact of individual risk factors across education and income strata on joint associations, we fixed the risk factors to the mean value of those with tertiary education/high income within each cluster; (6) we also modelled survival time differences using CVD, cancer, chronic respiratory disease and diabetes as individual outcomes; (7) finally, for comparison, we modelled survival time differences between clusters based on conventional cut-off values for the individual risk factors (online supplemental table 10).

Patient and public involvement

The NCDNOR project includes user partners from national, regional and local authorities as well as patient organisations for non-communicable diseases. These representatives have contributed to identifying knowledge gaps and have provided input on the dissemination and presentation of research findings.32 However, patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct or analysis of this specific study.

Results

We included 576 299 participants (52% women) with a mean age of 41 (SD 5.3) years. The optimal LPA-model identified four clusters (table 1). Cluster 1 (healthy, 44% of the sample) had the lowest risk factor exposure, was predominantly female (70%), had the lowest mean age at baseline (40.0 years (SD 5.4)) and the highest education and income levels. Cluster 2 (suboptimal, 45%) had moderately higher risk factor exposure, notably higher blood pressure, had a male predominance (60%) and had the second-highest education and income levels. Cluster 3 (dyslipidemics, 4%) was mostly male (84%), had the highest exposure to all risk factors except blood pressure, with more than 99% having dyslipidaemia and nearly one-third having BMI ≥30. Cluster 4 (hypertensives, 7%) had substantially higher blood pressure, with all participants hypertensive, and >50% had dyslipidaemia. Cluster 4 was the oldest (mean age 43.3 years (SD 5.9)) and included the highest proportion born between 1920 and 1939.

Clusters 2–4 had noticeably shorter mean survival times than cluster 1 by age 70, with survival time differences widening progressively as the maximum follow-up age increased (online supplemental table 12). Comparisons of 1974–1989, 1990–2004 and 2005–2019 revealed secular changes in all individual risk factors (online supplemental table 14). However, the optimal LPA-models identified four clusters comparable to those in the full sample (online supplemental figures 1–3). The LPA including alcohol (n=1 81 524) also indicated that a four-cluster model was optimal with clusters comparable to those in the full sample (online supplemental figure 4).

Commencing follow-up 5 years after baseline and excluding those with known disease altered estimates only slightly (online supplemental figures 5,6).

Differences in RMFT estimated from failure functions and CIFs were broadly similar (online supplemental figure 7), indicating that competing events (non-NCD deaths) did not materially alter the observed associations. This was also supported when modelling all-cause death as the outcomes (online supplemental figures 8,9).

Fixing the individual risk factors to the mean values of those with tertiary education (online supplemental table 15) attenuated differences somewhat, but the stepwise increase as educational attainment increased within each cluster remained (online supplemental figure 10). Similarly, fixing to the mean values of those with high income (online supplemental table 16) both attenuated and accentuated differences, but the pattern of association remained (online supplemental figure 11).

The pattern and magnitude of differences were roughly similar to the main analysis when CVD death was modelled as the outcome (online supplemental figures 12,13). Similar patterns were observed when modelling cancer and diabetes death as outcomes, although the RMST differences were smaller than for NCD death. For cancer (online supplemental figures 14,15), this attenuation likely reflects that survival-function–based RMST estimates implicitly assume a setting without competing deaths. In contrast, CIF-based sensitivity analyses indicated that competing events may magnify between-group differences (online supplemental figure 16). Survival time differences were small when chronic respiratory disease death was modelled as the outcome (largest difference observed with the CI not including unity was 1 year (95% CI −2.0 to −0.0)).

When participants were grouped based on conventional cut-off values for the individual risk factors and education level, the largest survival time differences for the maximum follow-up ranged from 9.9 (95% CI 11.6 to 8.2 (leisure-time physical activity: very active+tertiary education vs inactive+primary education)) to 13.1 (95% CI 15.5 to 10.6 (BMI: normal weight+tertiary education vs underweight+primary education)) years in men and from 6.9 (95% CI 8.7 to 5.1 (leisure-time physical activity: very active+tertiary education)) to 13.1 (95% CI 14.3 to 12.0 (triglyceride: normal+tertiary education vs high+primary education)) years in women (online supplemental tables 17–22).

Time-varying HRs for the joint associations of risk factor cluster with education level and income level are presented in online supplemental figures 17,18, respectively.

Discussion

Including data on key NCD risk factors from nearly 600 000 men and women, we identified four distinct risk factor clusters. Based on over 60 000 NCD deaths occurring during nearly 15 000 000 person-years of follow-up, we found substantial survival time differences between these clusters. These survival time differences were substantially modified by both education and income level, showing how both risk factor clustering and socioeconomic disparities have considerable impacts on NCD mortality risk.

We chose well-known risk factors based on global NCD prevention strategies,5 but in contrast to most other studies, we combined behavioural and metabolic risk factors. Furthermore, instead of categorising each risk factor using conventional cut-off values (a common practice6 33 34), we utilised the full data distribution for each risk factor, minimising the risk of information loss and misclassification. This approach allowed us to identify underlying clusters of individuals sharing biological and behavioural patterns highly relevant to NCD mortality risk.

Other studies have found strong associations between the number of risk factors per participant and mortality. For example, Ding et al33 in the Australian ‘45 and Up Study’ (n=2 31 048),33 Krogstad et al in the Norwegian HUNT Study (n=37 785)6 and Nguyen et al34 in the US Million Veteran Program (n=2 76 132).34 Nonetheless, these, and the majority of other similar studies,8 rely on dichotomised behavioural risk factors and counting-based approaches, making comparisons with our findings difficult. Some studies have employed model-based approaches. Tegegne et al12 used latent class analysis in the UK Biobank (n=1 74 030) to identify clusters of behavioural risk factors associated with CVD risk.12 Others have investigated the American Heart Association’s ‘Life’s Essential 8’, combining behavioural and metabolic risk factors, as in López-Bueno et al.35 López-Bueno et al using NHANES data (n=23 531).35 However, these studies also categorise risk factors and focused on CVD. To our knowledge, this study is, therefore, unique in combining continuous data on both behavioural and metabolic risk factors to identify risk factor clusters, and in studying how important socioeconomic indicators modify their association with all the four major NCDs. Importantly, we observed similar clustering of risk factors in the data collected in 1974–1989, 1990–2004 and 2005–2019, despite secular changes in the individual risk factors. This may suggest that ‘risk factor clustering phenotypes’ have remained quite stable over time. Understanding these persistent clusters could guide long-term, multifaceted public health interventions aimed at reducing NCD mortality.

Many studies have shown how socioeconomic inequalities associate strongly with the risk of developing and dying from NCDs,13 even in an egalitarian country like Norway with universal healthcare.36 One important underlying mechanism is the clustering of risk factors among those with low education and/or income,16 which we also observed. Notably, we found a stepwise increase in survival time with higher educational attainment and income within each risk factor cluster, even when risk factor levels were fixed. This suggests that SEP may contribute substantially to differences in survival both within and between risk factor clusters, beyond behavioural and metabolic risk factors measured in middle age. These results underscore the need to address socioeconomic disparities alongside risk factor management, as individuals with adverse risk factor clustering and limited resources are particularly vulnerable and may benefit from specific interventions to reduce NCD mortality.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of our study include its large sample, high participation, long follow-up and substantial number of events. This allowed analyses with high statistical power and strengthens generalisability. The prospective design minimises recall bias, and the harmonisation of variables from surveys using similar protocols limits measurement errors.17 We also used registry data on education and income, and outcome data from the Norwegian Cause of Death Registry, which has a high degree of completeness and validity,26 and objectively measured anthropometric data, blood pressure and lipid levels. Furthermore, we employed flexible parametric survival models to estimate RMST differences, which is increasingly recommended for its interpretability and robustness over HRs.29 31 We also conducted extensive sensitivity analyses, supporting the robustness of our findings.

The study has several limitations. First, despite a relatively high participation rate (figure 1), health examination survey participants often differ from the general population. Surveys were also conducted over five decades, with 88% of participants 35–49 years of age at baseline, which may limit the generalisability of results to the current adult population. Second, we were unable to include data on risk factors such as diet and blood glucose. Additionally, while several included risk factors are established risk factors for several cancers, these factors are generally more strongly associated with CVD than with some cancers, which may partly explain the observed differences in cause-specific mortality patterns across clusters. Third, beyond excluding self-reported antihypertensive users in sensitivity analyses, we could not assess the impact of other relevant medications (eg, statins). Since risk factors were measured only at baseline, we cannot assess changes during follow-up, neither due to lifestyle modifications nor treatments. Fourth, measurement errors may have introduced bias. Personal education and income are imperfect proxies for SEP, blood pressure and lipids were measured only once, and blood samples were non-fasting. The behavioural risk factors were self-reported, making them subject to recall error and social desirability bias. Finally, while our main analyses were based on net survival,30 NCD deaths made up nearly 75% of all deaths, with similar proportions of other causes across clusters. RMFT differences from failure functions and CIFs showed consistent patterns and magnitudes, and results were comparable when all-cause mortality was analysed as the outcome. These findings suggest that competing events had little impact on the observed associations.

Conclusions

We provide new insights into how NCD risk factors cluster and how SEP modifies the association between NCD risk factor clustering and NCD mortality. The substantial influence of SEP highlights the need for public health strategies and interventions that focus not only on reducing behavioural and metabolic risk factors but also on addressing socioeconomic disparities to effectively reduce the burden of NCDs. Future research with repeated assessments of NCD risk factors will be valuable to further elaborate on these findings.
