Qualitative phenomenological exploration of lived experiences and perspectives of Chinese hospital administrators engaged in medical dispute management
Authors: Jun Chen, Meng-Xuan Zhang, Yi-Geng Hou, Jie Chen, Jun Zhou, Ying-Ying Luo, Peng Liu, Ai-Yue Mao, Xiao-Man Hou, Hui Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Medical disputes are a significant challenge in healthcare settings worldwide, with hospital administrators playing a pivotal role in managing the conflicts. In China, despite a reported decline, medical disputes continue to raise public and governmental concerns due to factors such as medical accessibility, patient expectations, and staff competency. This study aimed to explore the lived experiences and perspectives of Chinese hospital administrators directly engaged in medical dispute management, to understand the challenges they face and inform the development of targeted training and support systems. Using a qualitative phenomenological approach, we conducted semi-structured, in-depth interviews with 13 hospital administrators from two major regional comprehensive hospitals in Beijing. Participants were selected through purposive sampling and met criteria including active engagement in managing medical disputes and adequate communication skills. Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s thematic analysis method to extract significant themes from the participants’ experiences. Administrators reported four interconnected challenges: how gender shapes authority and perceived safety in disputes; the struggle to maintain an impartial, ‘outsider’ stance; wide variability in the emotional toll of conflict; and a pronounced need for structured, ongoing dispute-management training. The key themes revealed included (1) Gender Dynamics in Conflict Management: female administrators faced challenges in authority recognition and preferred having male colleagues present during intense disputes for perceived safety and effectiveness; (2) Outsider Perspective in Conflict Management: maintaining an impartial and detached stance was crucial for effective mediation, but administrators varied in their ability to achieve this mindset; (3) Variability in Psychological Experiences: administrators reported diverse emotional reactions and psychological impacts. Some struggled to detach emotionally and lacked formal support systems; (4) Need for Specific Training: there was a clear demand for structured, ongoing training programs tailored to the unique challenges of medical dispute management. Chinese hospital administrators face complex challenges including emotional strain, mixed-gender preference in intense encounters, and insufficient training and support. Addressing the issues is essential for enhancing administrators’ effectiveness in conflict resolution, improving patient care outcomes, and promoting organizational efficiency. Medical institutions should develop comprehensive training programs and establish support systems for administrators’ well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26348-8.

Introduction

Medical disputes encompass any conflicts arising from clinical care, including disagreements between patients and providers as well as intra-staff tensions related to case management, communication breakdowns, or administrative procedures. Conflict resolution refers to the processes and strategies used to de-escalate these disputes, facilitate dialogue, and achieve acceptable outcomes for all parties. In Chinese hospitals, medical disputes remain a critical challenge, driven by uneven access to care, rising patient expectations, and occasional lapses in staff competency. Although recent reports indicate a modest decline in high-profile incidents, disputes continue to erode public trust, consume institutional resources, and jeopardize both patient satisfaction and staff safety1–4.

Hospital administrators serve as primary mediators in these conflicts. They assess dispute severity, document incidents, facilitate communication among providers, patients, and families, and engage security or legal teams when necessary5,6. However, administrators typically lack formal authority over clinical decisions and often must de-escalate highly emotional encounters without clear guidelines or structured support. This disconnect places them under considerable stress, contributes to burnout, and underscores the need for targeted training and well-being measures7.

Existing research on medical conflict resolution focuses largely on provider-patient interactions, emphasizing miscommunication, treatment disagreements, and billing disputes8–12. Studies of organizational management acknowledge administrators’ oversight functions but do not explore their firsthand experiences in dispute mediation. Moreover, although gender dynamics can affect perceptions of authority (for example, female staff sometimes face skepticism in high-tension scenarios)13,14, few investigations examine how these dynamics operate for nonclinical staff. In China, cultural norms, such as hierarchical respect and collective harmony, shape conflict processes15,16. However, scholarship has thus far concentrated on doctor-patient relations rather than the administrative perspective. This gap limits understanding of the personal and institutional factors that influence dispute outcomes.

Focusing specifically on administrators is novel and necessary because they navigate both clinical and organizational pressures. Unlike many clinical staff studies, our research foregrounds the administrators’ dual role: balancing institutional policies with real-time de-escalation efforts. By examining administrators’ lived experiences, we can reveal how organizational structures, gender expectations, and cultural norms intersect to affect conflict management. These insights are essential for designing effective training programs, gender-sensitive policies, and support systems.

To address this gap, we conducted a phenomenological study of 13 hospital administrators from two major regional comprehensive hospitals in Beijing. Between March and May 2024, we used semi-structured, in-depth interviews to explore administrators’ experiences managing medical disputes between patients/families and providers, examine the emotional and procedural challenges they face, and identify their training and support needs.

By illuminating administrators’ firsthand accounts, this study aims to inform the development of targeted training programs and institutional policies that reflect the realities of Chinese hospitals. Our findings also seek to enrich theoretical models of conflict management by integrating the role of nonclinical staff, an often-overlooked dimension. Ultimately, improving administrators’ dispute-management capacities promises to enhance patient care, strengthen institutional trust, and promote organizational efficiency across China’s healthcare settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a qualitative phenomenological approach to explore the lived experiences and perspectives of Chinese hospital administrators engaged in the management of medical disputes. This approach enables an in-depth exploration of their subjective experiences and perceptions to provide rich insights into their personal and contextualized understandings of managing medical disputes17.

The research was conducted between March 1, 2024, and May 15, 2024, in two major regional comprehensive hospitals located in Beijing, China. Our sample reflected the high case volumes and complex workflows typical of large urban medical centers. Both institutions handled over 500 medical disputes annually, which provided rich opportunities to observe administrators’ real-world challenges, experiences, and insights. The hospital locations enabled in-person interviewing by the first author, which facilitated rapport and depth of insight.

Recruitment of participants

Participants were recruited from the leaders and staff of the medical affairs offices of the two hospitals. Eligibility criteria included being hospital administrators or office staff aged 25–60 years, actively engaged in managing medical disputes (handling at least three cases per week), and without active severe mental health conditions that could compromise the results of the study. Informed consent was obtained from each participant after a thorough explanation of the study’s purpose, potential impact, and the confidentiality measures to safeguard participant data.

It is worth noting that we did included participants with mental health conditions which did not seem to affect the study’s results, as assessed by the primary investigator (JC, first author). We recognized that the emotional demands of dispute management may precipitate psychological distress and psychological impact comprises an essential experience of participants. We gathered data on their mental-health status with consent. However, as several participants declined to report it in a publication, we decided not to include specific data in the demographic table (Table 1).

Sampling and approaching of participant

A purposive sampling method was employed to enroll participants to select individuals who could provide rich, relevant data18. The lead researcher first assessed potential participants during their regular administrative duties. Eligible candidates were approached with a friendly greeting and casual conversation to informally assess their communication abilities and determine willingness to participate.

If a staff member was deemed suitable, the researcher provided a comprehensive overview of the study, including its objectives and procedures. Interested individuals were asked to sign informed consent forms and were then invited to a designated quiet meeting room for interview. To ensure maximum diversity, we stratified the sampling across several dimensions. Specifically, we selected administrators with varied lengths of experience, educational levels, and roles. We also balanced gender and age categories to capture different perspectives.

Recruitment continued until data saturation. We determined saturation through an iterative coding process: when three consecutive interviews did not produce new codes or themes, we deemed saturation achieved. By the thirteenth interview, no new codes or themes emerged.

Semi-structured in-depth interview and data analysis

A semi-structured interview guide was developed specifically for this study (see Appendix 1). Interviews were conducted using a digital recorder, with prior permission obtained from participants. Each interview began with an open-ended question, such as “What is your role in conflict resolution at the hospital?” followed by probing questions such as “Can you describe a typical situation involving conflict management and your role in it?” Probing techniques, including repetition, silence, and reflective responses, were used to delve deeper into participants’ feelings and experiences.

The questioning approach and interaction style were adapted based on each participant’s role and level of experience, recognizing that different staff members might have varied communication styles and comfort levels. For complex or nuanced topics, questions were occasionally broken down into simpler components to help participants articulate their experiences more effectively.

Interviews were transcribed verbatim by a research assistant within 24 h to ensure accuracy. The transcripts were then reviewed for accuracy. Any uncertainties were clarified with the participants during or immediately after the interview. Disagreements among the research team regarding interpretations were discussed until consensus was reached. If necessary, the lead researcher sought further clarification from the participant by phone.

Colaizzi’s thematic analysis method was employed for data analysis19. All transcripts were first read independently by three researchers (JC, YGH, JC) to achieve immersion in the data. Each researcher then performed line-by-line coding, highlighting any participant utterance that spoke directly to the study objectives, recurred across interviews, or carried particular emotional or cognitive weight. The excerpts were labeled as “significant statements.” Next, each researcher drafted brief analytic memos outlining the perceived meaning of each statement.

During analysis meetings, the team reviewed all memos side by side, compared the identified statements, and discussed their interpretations in detail. Where two or more researchers differed on whether a statement qualified as “significant” or on its preliminary meaning, we revisited the original transcript context and debated until reaching unanimous agreement. If consensus remained elusive, a fourth, senior investigator (HD) adjudicated.

Once all significant statements had been agreed upon, we clustered them into meaning units and developed an evolving codebook with clear definitions for each emerging sub-theme. We continuously refined sub-themes by grouping related codes and checking back against the raw data to ensure consistency. Finally, we synthesized related sub-themes into overarching themes, again confirming that each theme captured the essence of the administrators’ shared experiences.

Verification, reflectivity, and rigor

To enhance credibility and trustworthiness, we implemented several verification strategies. First, member checking was conducted by returning preliminary themes and interpretations to participants for validation. Second, reflexivity was pursued through structured reflection sessions at key milestones: after initial coding, midway through analysis, and when final themes emerged. During each session, researchers reviewed their personal assumptions, discussed potential biases, and documented reflections in a shared audit log.

Third, three researchers independently coded all transcripts and then compared their codebooks; discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and a fourth team member adjudicated unresolved differences. Fourth, peer debriefing occurred with an external qualitative methodologist who audited coding decisions and theme development. Finally, a detailed audit trail recorded every key step of data collection and analysis, including coding iterations, decisions made during discussions, and any adaptations to the analytic framework.

Ethical consideration, informed consent, and measures for privacy protection and data confidentiality

This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital (Approval No. PKUPH-06571). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the competent national and institutional guidelines and regulatory codes for the protection of human participants. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Confidentiality and data security were maintained through a multi-layered approach. First, all demographic data, field notes, and transcripts underwent a systematic de-identification (desensitization) process, where personal identifiers such as names, specific locations, dates, or case details were removed or generalized, and each participant was assigned a unique code (e.g. P1). Second, only de-identified transcripts were used for analysis. Third, all digital files (audio recordings, raw transcripts, analytic data and notes) were stored on a password-protected flash drive, which was kept in a locked drawer in the lead researcher’s (JC) office and was accessible only to members of the research team. Finally, when sharing transcripts with collaborators (e.g. external methodologist for data analysis audit) or providing data upon request, only the desensitized versions were provided.

Main themes

The interviews were 27.3 min in duration on average (range 19–32 min). Four overarching themes were identified from the analysis of the interview transcripts. The themes encapsulated the lived experiences and perspectives of the administrators regarding their roles in managing medical disputes, highlighting both the challenges and essential skills associated with their positions. Appendix 2 summarizes the themes.

Theme 1: gender dynamics in conflict management

The interviews highlighted gender dynamics as a significant aspect of conflict management. Several administrators noted disparities in how conflicts are managed based on gender. Female administrators often expressed a preference for the presence of male colleagues during intense disputes. This might suggest a perceived advantage in authority or ability to de-escalate situations when male staff members were involved. The preference might also stem from concerns about personal safety, as some situations could become physically threatening. This theme demonstrated the complexities of gender roles in the medical setting and how they can impact the effectiveness and safety of conflict resolution strategies:“Sometimes, I feel it’s better to have a male colleague present, as they can better command the situation and help calm the other party down… There is a noticeable difference in how conflicts might play out depending on whether a male staff member is present or not.” (P2).“Having a male colleague there sometimes just changes the patient’s demeanor faster. They (patients and family) calm down, listen more, and the whole situation seems to de-escalate with greater ease.” (P3).

“Sometimes, I feel it’s better to have a male colleague present, as they can better command the situation and help calm the other party down… There is a noticeable difference in how conflicts might play out depending on whether a male staff member is present or not.” (P2).

“Having a male colleague there sometimes just changes the patient’s demeanor faster. They (patients and family) calm down, listen more, and the whole situation seems to de-escalate with greater ease.” (P3).

Our thematic coding revealed two primary drivers for this preference. First, participants believed male colleagues projected greater authority during heated disputes, which facilitated faster de-escalation. Second, concerns about personal safety emerged, as women administrators feared potential aggression without immediate support. Notably, no participant indicated that this preference stemmed from formal institutional policies or broader cultural expectations.

Participants indicated that, while policies did not require a male colleague, existing measures, such as calling security or using a panic button, offered limited protection. The reactive safeguards lacked the immediacy of having another staff member present and improved proactive interventions are needed:“While we do have a panic button and can call security, The measures just aren’t the same as having someone right there with you. The response time is critical, and sometimes it feels too long when you’re in a tense situation.” (P2).“Our emergency protocols involve calling for help or using emergency buttons, but The don’t bring the immediate physical presence of security and other staff that can make all the difference in how quickly a situation is calmed.” (P3).“The existing measures are good, but they’re reactive, not proactive. You’re essentially waiting for help rather than having it right there, which doesn’t always feel sufficient in real-time conflict management.” (P1).

Theme 2: outsider perspective in conflict management

The interviews revealed that maintaining an “outsider” mental state, or “局外人心态”, was crucial for success in the role of hospital administrators managing medical disputes. This theme stressed the importance of detachment and impartiality in conflict resolution. Administrators emphasized the necessity of approaching each situation without personal bias, which allowed them to mediate more effectively between conflicting parties. This outsider perspective aided in ensuring that the solutions were equitable and focused solely on the facts and policies relevant to the dispute.“Staying detached is not about not caring, but about being fair and objective. It helps me to manage conflicts without letting personal feelings interfere with the decision-making process.” (P4).“I try to maintain a neutral stance in every dispute. It’s such as being an outsider looking in, which is crucial for handling The situations without bias.” (P6).“The ability to remain uninvolved personally allows me to focus on the facts and the policies that apply. This perspective is essential for finding solutions that are just and acceptable to all involved.” (P1).

“Staying detached is not about not caring, but about being fair and objective. It helps me to manage conflicts without letting personal feelings interfere with the decision-making process.” (P4).

When participants were asked about developing this mental state and how they achieved it, responses varied significantly. Some admitted that they were still struggling to fully detach themselves. Others shared that their ability to maintain an outsider perspective was honed through experience, particularly after facing several emotionally charged or troubling cases:“At first, it was tough to not get emotionally involved, especially during cases that were really sad or troubling. But over time, I learned my lessons. I had to ‘jump out of it,’ so to speak, to really start seeing things differently and handle my role effectively.” (P3).“It definitely took some time to cultivate this mindset. Each encounter, especially the really challenging ones, led me to rethink our own way of action. There are days when it’s harder to maintain that distance. But I’ve seen progress in myself after some particularly tough cases, where I realized being too involved doesn’t help resolve anything.” (P8).

Theme 3: variability in psychological experiences

Hospital administrators reported wide variation in their emotional responses and psychological impacts while managing medical disputes. Some described feeling overwhelmed by certain cases, whereas others noted an ability to detach quickly and continue work. Differences in innate coping styles, combined with the acute demands of dispute resolution, created a spectrum of resilience and stress, which influenced individual well-being. Furthermore, many administrators observed that the pace of their duties forced them to move forward immediately, often without time to process lingering emotional effects. Finally, participants indicated a lack of formal support systems, which left them responsible for seeking resources on their own:“Every day is different, and each case hits differently. Some days I feel completely overwhelmed, while other times I feel such as I’ve managed to handle everything thrown at me with ease.” (P4).“The psychological toll can be intense. Some conflicts really get to you, no matter how much you try to remain detached. It’s those moments that test your resilience the most.” (P7).“I’ve noticed a huge variance in how we all handle the stress. Some of my colleagues seem to shake it off quickly, while others, such as myself at times, take it home and ruminate on it.” (P1).

Subtheme 1: emotional resilience

Administrators described varying capacities to detach or remain focused amid conflict. Some felt naturally suited to manage disputes, citing an ability to switch off empathy and maintain calm. In contrast, others acknowledged ongoing struggles, even after years in their roles. These differences suggested that individual temperament and prior experience shaped one’s ability to recover from stressful incidents.

Some administrators felt that they were naturally suited for the demands of their roles, effectively managing disputes with a level of inherent skill:“I’ve always been someone who can detach from the empathy quickly; I think it’s just part of who I am, which makes this job a good fit for me.” (P9).“It’s clear that some people are just cut out for this kind of work. They step into a dispute and handle it with a calm that’s almost innate.” (P3).

Subtheme 2: variable impact on Well-Being

Many participants noted that the urgent demands of their roles forced them to move on quickly, regardless of lingering emotional effects. Administrators felt they had little time to process stress because other duties required immediate attention. As a result, unresolved feelings could accumulate, affecting mental health and job performance over time:“My own discomfort is my own problem. First, the matter must be resolved and cannot be pushed aside; this is a rule. Second, it’s not such as this is the only responsibility. I also have other duties, so where’s the time to feel upset?” (P2).“The pace of our work doesn’t really allow for dwelling on any one situation too long, no matter how tough it was.” (P6).

Subtheme 3: institutional support and gaps

Participants emphasized that formal support systems for psychological well-being were lacking. Although some sought informal help, such as talking to a colleague or seeking external counseling, there was no structured program for debriefing or mental health support within the hospital. This gap left administrators to navigate emotional burdens alone and demonstrates a need for institutionally provided resources:“There isn’t really a system in place for us to get professional help easily; it’s something you have to pursue on your own if you feel the need.” (P2).“I had to take it upon myself to find maybe a colleague who could understand the pressures of the job. The hospital didn’t provide special mental counseling… Some of my colleagues might have sought out support groups or therapy on their own.” (P6).

The interviews revealed a strong theme surrounding the need for specific training tailored to the unique challenges of medical dispute management. While some administrators believed that they were naturally suited to handle conflicts due to inherent skills or personality traits, many expressed a clear desire for more structured and comprehensive training programs to enhance their abilities in this critical area:“I sometimes feel out of my depth with complex cases. A systematic training program would be invaluable.” (P7).

Discussion

Our study revealed four key findings. First, in terms of gender dynamics, female administrators preferred the presence of male colleagues during high-tension disputes and perceived that male presence conferred greater authority and a sense of immediate safety. Second, institutional protocols and authority constraints shaped administrators’ reliance on informal strategies. Reactive measures such as panic buttons or security calls seemed slow and prompted administrators to use ad hoc workarounds. Third, variability in psychological experiences was evident. Administrators described a wide spectrum of emotional resilience, ranging from those who detached quickly to others who found disputes emotionally draining, which was compounded by the absence of formal support systems. Finally, substantial gaps in training and support needs surfaced, as administrators lacked targeted de-escalation training and clear guidelines for legal and policy considerations.

Female administrators’ preference for male colleagues in heated disputes likely stems from both traditional gender norms and real-time assessments of conflict dynamics. Administrators explained that male presence often shifted the interpersonal balance, which deterred aggression and facilitated faster de-escalation. This pattern reflects broader cultural expectations in Chinese society, where masculinity is associated with assertiveness and physical protection20–22. Yet participants clarified that this preference did not arise from formal institutional directives or explicit cultural mandates. Instead, it constituted a pragmatic response to perceived differences in conflict style23–25. Women noted that their own empathic approach could be highly effective in many scenarios but sometimes failed to carry sufficient weight when emotions ran high. Existing literature on medical conflict resolution has largely focused on provider-patient interactions26–28, highlighting cultural elements such as “face” and collective harmony in China, but seldom examines how gender influences nonclinical staff’s strategies29–32. Gender-based studies from Western settings show that female providers may be perceived as less authoritative or disadvantaged during confrontations13,33. Our findings extend the insights by demonstrating that nonclinical administrators adopt proxy strategies, such as calling on male colleagues, when formal supports are absent. One approach to address or reinforce administrators’ ability to de-escalate tension in practice may be to provide targeted de-escalation training or assign designated officers who can provide immediate, authoritative intervention34,35.

Administrators described existing institutional protocols, such as panic buttons and security calls as reactive and cumbersome. These measure often fail to address the urgency of unfolding disputes. Because administrators lack formal authority to make clinical decisions, they frequently resort to creative workarounds, such as enlisting senior staff or reorganizing meetings, to bridge procedural gaps. In large urban hospitals with high case volumes, the time required to escalate through formal channels can be prohibitive. Research on organizational conflict management in healthcare also stresses the need for clear escalation protocols and defined roles36–38. In contrast, Chinese hospitals often adhere to hierarchical decision-making structures, which might delay conflict resolution and force administrators to lean on personal influence and interpersonal networks. Informal strategies can provide short-term relief but risk inconsistency and uneven outcomes. To address such constraints, hospitals should establish tiered conflict-management protocols that delineate specific responsibilities for administrators, security representatives, and clinical leaders. Forming dedicated conflict-response teams composed of conflict mediators, legal advisors, and security personnel could streamline decision-making39. Providing administrators with limited clinical discretion, such as authority to convene multidisciplinary huddles, could expedite resolution and reduce dependence on ad hoc methods40.

The study revealed a broad spectrum of emotional resilience among administrators. Some recounted an innate ability to detach and maintain composure, while others described protracted emotional responses lasting days after particularly difficult disputes. The differences often correlated with administrators’ backgrounds. Those with prior exposure to high-stress settings had developed coping mechanisms such as cognitive reframing and emotional distancing, whereas less experienced administrators found disputes more taxing. The relentless pace of administrative duties allowed little opportunity for reflection or emotional processing. As soon as one case concluded, new conflicts emerged, leaving no time to decompress. Literature on burnout and psychological distress among healthcare providers typically focuses on clinical staff41–43. Fewer studies examine nonclinical personnel. A mixed methods investigation from the United Kingdom found hospital managers faced similar stressors, constant high-stakes decisions and emotional labor, yet felt “invisible” within institutional support structures44. In China, research has demonstrated that administrative staff receive fewer mental health resources than frontline clinicians, and collectivist norms emphasizing endurance and harmony can inhibit open discussions of distress45. Our results echo these findings and underscore the urgent need for structured mental health interventions. Institutions should implement accessible support systems, such as regular debriefing sessions led by trained psychologists, confidential counseling hotlines, and resilience-building workshops that integrate mindfulness and stress-management techniques. Additionally, rotating workloads or ensuring designated rest periods following high-intensity cases could mitigate cumulative burnout and protect administrators’ well-being46,47.

Although administrators expressed strong interest in mental health resources, several barriers impede access. Stigma surrounding psychological counseling can be acute in collectivist settings, where seeking help may be perceived as a sign of weakness48,49. To address the barriers, hospitals may consider to embed brief mental health check-ins within routine administrative meetings, which can normalize discussions of emotional well-being. Confidential digital counseling platforms can offer discrete support for administrators hesitant to seek in-person services. Leadership endorsement of mental health initiatives, through formal statements and visible participation, can further reduce stigma. Embedding mental health components into annual performance reviews or linking them to continuing education credits may institutionalize the value of psychological support and encourage proactive utilization.

Participants identified a clear gap in targeted training for medical dispute management. Without specialized training, administrators rely on intuition, senior colleagues’ advice, or ad hoc learning, which resulted in inconsistent approaches. International best practices emphasize simulation-based training, role-playing exercises, and multidisciplinary workshops to build conflict-resolution skills50,51. In China, recent scholarship has called for localized modules that incorporate cultural nuances, such as respect for hierarchy, collective responsibility, and face-saving tactics, into training curricula52–54. Our findings support this call, showing that administrators may benefit from programs that integrate technical de-escalation techniques with cultural competence. To meet the needs, hospitals may develop comprehensive training programs combining didactic instruction with practical simulations. Involving legal experts in workshops can clarify institutional policies and boundaries during disputes. Peer-mentoring schemes and periodic refresher courses can reinforce learned skills and foster a collaborative learning environment.

Implications for practice

Hospital leadership should adopt a multi-pronged approach to strengthen dispute-management capacities. First, establishing dedicated conflict-response teams that include trained de-escalation officers and security liaisons can ensure rapid, gender-neutral support. Second, clarifying escalation protocols and delineating roles for administrators, legal advisors, and clinical leaders may reduce reliance on informal workarounds. Third, developing comprehensive training programs, combining simulation-based exercises, legal policy clarification, and cultural competence, can equip administrators with the skills needed to navigate complex disputes. Fourth, enhancing safety measures, such as optimizing panic-button response times and conducting wearable alert device pilots, can provide administrators with immediate protection. Finally, providing accessible mental health resources, ranging from regular debriefings to confidential counseling hotlines, may address administrators’ emotional challenges.

Limitations and future research

This study’s insights derive from two large urban hospitals in Beijing and may not reflect rural or smaller healthcare settings, where resources and cultural norms can differ substantially. Future research should examine administrators in diverse regional contexts to assess the transferability of our findings. Additionally, our sample focused on medical affairs office staff, excluding perspectives from security personnel, legal counsel, and frontline clinicians who also engage in conflict management. Expanding the participant pool to include these stakeholders would provide a more holistic view of dispute handling. Longitudinal studies could evaluate the impact of implemented training, support programs, and policy changes on administrators’ performance, stress levels, and dispute outcomes over time. Furthermore, comparative research between hospitals with varying resource levels and organizational structures could identify best practices adaptable across different Chinese healthcare environments.

Conclusions

Hospital administrators are integral to managing medical disputes but face significant challenges that impact their effectiveness and well-being. The emotional strain, influenced by the lack of formal authority and the necessity to navigate emotionally charged situations, underscores the need for institutional support. In terms of gender dynamics, female administrators often prefer to have a male colleague present during high-tension disputes because their presence provided an immediate sense of authority and enhanced safety, preventing escalation. Maintaining an outsider perspective is essential but requires support and training to develop. The critical need for specific training and support systems is evident, highlighting an area where medical institutions can make significant improvements. Future research should consider longitudinal designs to assess the sustained impact of the interventions and undertake cross-cultural comparisons to distinguish universal vs. localized conflict-resolution strategies.
