Nurse-Led Evidence-Based Quality Improvement Programme to Reduce Urinary Leakage in Intensive Care Unit Patients with Urinary catheters: A Pre-Post Quasi-Experimental Study
Authors: Jingjing Yan, Qin Sun, Rongxia Zhai, Hong Wu, Zhengyu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndwelling urinary catheters are commonly used in intensive care units. Urinary leakage around catheters may occur in critically ill patients because of mechanical ventilation, sedation and analgesia, constipation, increased intra abdominal pressure, bladder spasm, and altered consciousness. Nurses require adequate knowledge and skills to prevent and manage urinary leakage.AimThis study aimed to reduce urinary leakage among ICU patients by developing and implementing a nurse led, evidence based Urinary Leakage Bundle.MethodsThe Evidence-based Continuous Quality Improvement framework and the Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services framework were used to guide the development, implementation, and evaluation of the Urinary Leakage Bundle. A quasi-experimental study was conducted in a 20-bed comprehensive intensive care unit, with outcomes including the patient urinary leakage rate, nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to urinary leakage, and nurses’ adherence to the bundle interventions.InterventionsTo support the translation of evidence into clinical practice, a multidisciplinary nursing group appraised and selected the evidence and developed the Urinary Leakage Bundle. The protocol included leakage prevention strategies, clinical management flowcharts, efficacy evaluation and quality control to support implementation in routine care.ResultsThe intervention improved ICU nurses’ questionnaire scores for knowledge, attitudes, and practice, increasing from 3.36±0.31 at baseline to 4.40±0.31 after implementation. The urinary leakage rate decreased from 25.4% before implementation to 13.3% after implementation. Adherence to the Urinary Leakage Bundle also increased, with better compliance in key elements such as catheter selection, catheter securement, and management of catheter bypassing.ConclusionThe nurse led, evidence based Urinary Leakage Bundle was feasible in routine ICU care and was associated with improved nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices, higher adherence to care processes, and a lower urinary leakage rate.Relevance to Clinical PracticeThe reductions in urinary leakage and the improved nurse adherence to evidence based interventions suggest that the Urinary Leakage Bundle can be implemented effectively in other clinical settings.

Introduction

Problem Description

Indwelling urinary catheters are widely used in intensive care units (ICUs). A multicenter study reported that noninfectious catheter related complications occurred more frequently than infectious complications, with rates of 55% and 11%.1 Common noninfectious complications include pain, discomfort, hematuria, and urinary leakage, all of which can reduce patient quality of life.1 Urinary leakage has been reported in 20.3% to 67% of patients with an indwelling catheter.1–4 Urinary leakage contributes to moisture-associated skin damage and incontinence-associated dermatitis (IAD), conditions characterized by maceration and erosion of the perineal skin that compromise the cutaneous barrier and increase susceptibility to secondary infection.5,6 Persistent moisture caused by urinary leakage may promote microbial colonization and barrier breakdown, may increase susceptibility to catheter-associated urinary tract infection.5,6 It also increases nursing workload and care costs for families, and in some settings may contribute to patient complaints and disputes.7

Multiple factors among ICU patients may increase the risk of urinary leakage. Common risk factors include frequent coughing, sputum suctioning, constipation, dyspnea, mechanical ventilation, abdominal surgery, sepsis, and intestinal obstruction.8–10 Increased intra-abdominal pressure raises intravesical pressure, activates bladder stretch receptors, and triggers detrusor contraction,11,12 which may lead to urinary leakage. In addition, sedatives are commonly used in ICU patients and may cause generalized muscle relaxation, including reduced tone of the urethral sphincter and pelvic floor muscles. As a result, the urinary catheter may not fit closely within the urethra, allowing urine to leak around the catheter.12,13 Urinary tract infection, prolonged indwelling urinary catheterization, advanced age, inappropriate balloon inflation, and inadequate catheter securement may also increase the risk of urinary leakage.1,12–14 Because many contributing factors are modifiable in clinical practice, nursing interventions to prevent and manage urinary leakage are clinically important.

Available Knowledge

Intravesical botulinum toxin injection can treat persistent bladder spasm and catheter bypass leakage in patients with long-term indwelling catheters.15 Research also suggests that reducing catheter balloon inflation volume helps decrease urinary leakage.16 Although several interventions have been proposed, current approaches are relatively uniform and difficult to apply in ICU patients, where leakage is often associated with multiple factors. Existing guidelines primarily address prevention and management of catheter-associated urinary tract infection, with limited and unsystematic coverage of leakage, and they do not provide a coherent, evidence-based framework. As a result, clinical practice is often fragmented and lacks standardization. A survey found that only 48.0% of institutions had established leakage management protocols.17 Therefore, existing evidence should be translated into practical protocols for leakage management. Evidence-based implementation may narrow the gap between nurses’ knowledge and practice and improve care quality and clinical outcomes.18

Local Problem

In our institution, ICU nurses can recognize peri-catheter urinary leakage, but management strategies remain limited. In routine practice, nurses often first increase the catheter balloon inflation volume to improve apposition. If leakage persists, they may use a larger catheter. These practices reflect a common assumption that leakage mainly results from the catheter being loose within the urethra. Although urinary leakage management is a routine part of catheter care, there remains a clear gap between current practice and evidence.

Specific Aims

The objectives of this study were to develop and implement a nurse-led, evidence-based intervention bundle to reduce urinary leakage. The specific aims were to: (1) reduce urinary leakage in ICU patients; (2) increase nurses’ knowledge and promote a positive professional attitude toward integrating the leakage bundle into ICU practice; and (3) improve nurses’ adherence to the urinary leakage bundle.

Methods

Context

This Quality Improvement (QI) intervention was conducted in a 20-bed comprehensive intensive care unit at Jiangnan University Medical Center (South Campus).The unit primarily admits and manages postoperative patients requiring close monitoring, as well as critically ill patients, including those with respiratory failure, circulatory failure, pneumonia, sepsis, and those recovering from major surgery, such as cranial or abdominal procedures.

Design

This project used a Pre-Post quasi-experimental Study. Data were collected from two patient cohorts: one admitted before implementation of the nurse led, evidence based Urinary Leakage Bundle and one admitted after implementation of the bundle. In this study, implementation refers to the introduction of a structured urinary leakage prevention and management bundle into routine ICU nursing practice. The bundle included catheter selection, catheter securement, obstruction prevention, staff training. A more detailed description of the intervention is provided in the subsequent Methods section. This design allowed comparison of urinary leakage and related outcomes across the two periods to evaluate outcomes associated with the intervention.

Participants

The population of this project included patients and staff. The study population included adults aged ≥18 years who required an indwelling urinary catheter during ICU admission. Exclusion criteria were: (1) male patients with prostatic disease and a current indication for surgery; (2) severe pelvic floor dysfunction, including uterine prolapse or vaginal wall prolapse; (3) a history of urethral obstruction or urethral calculi; (4) urethral trauma or urethral tumors; and (5) the need for surgery due to urinary catheter related complications. The study included 38 ICU nurses. Among them, 63.16% were junior nurses, 28.95% were intermediate nurses, and 7.89% were senior nurses. The mean duration of work experience was 8.18 years (SD = 6.25).

Sample Size

The study’s initial sample size was calculated using PASS 15, setting α=0.05, β=0.1, power=0.8. Based on the literature,19 the anticipated incidence was P1=0.08 in the intervention group and P2=0.21 in the control group. Accordingly, a minimum sample size of 224 catheterized critical illness patients was required. Assuming a 10% attrition rate, the required total sample size was 246 patients.

Rationale

This project used an evidence-based continuous quality improvement framework to guide evidence implementation. It was informed by the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle, evidence-based nursing, and clinical workflows. The Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Service (i-PARIHS) framework was applied to assess implementation barriers and facilitators. The project comprised four stages: (1) reviewing and summarizing the best evidence and developing audit indicators for the clinical setting; (2) auditing current nursing practice and identifying gaps between current practice and best evidence, (3) applying i-PARIHS to assess barriers and facilitators, then developing and implementing evidence-based strategies; and (4) evaluating the effects of the evidence-based practice at the system, nurse, and patient levels.

Interventions

Stage 1: Evidence Acquisition

The clinical question established in this study was: How to implement nurse-led evidence-based interventions to reduce the urine leakage of ICU patients? This research question adheres to the PIPOST model guidelines established by the Fudan University Center for Evidence-Based Nursing. 1: (1) P-Population: ICU patients aged 18 years and older with indwelling catheters; (2) I–Interventions:focused on implementing strategies to prevent and manage urine leakage; (3) P-Practitioners: nurses; (4) O-Outcomes: urine leakage rates; (5) S-Settings: Hospital ICUs; (6) T-Types of evidence: encompassed clinical practice guidelines, systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials, expert consensus, evidence summaries, and clinical decision.

UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, the Joanna Briggs Institute Evidence-Based Healthcare Center Database, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), the National Guideline Clearinghouse (NGC), the Guidelines International Network (GIN), YiMaiTong, the Registered Nurses’ Association of Ontario (RNAO), the American Urological Association (AUA), the European Association of Urology (EAU), the European Association of Urology Nurses (EAUN), the British Association of Urological Surgeons (BAUS), the Chinese Medical Association of Urological Surgery (CAU), the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, SinoMed, and the China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP) were searched. The search covered January 2014 to December 2024.

The inclusion criteria for evidence sources were as follows: (1) evidence sources applicable to ICU, acute care, or hospital settings; (2) evidence sources reporting preventive or therapeutic interventions for urinary leakage, catheter bypassing, catheter blockage, or catheter related complications in adult patients with indwelling urinary catheters; (3) publication types including clinical practice guidelines, systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials, evidence summaries, expert consensus statements, and clinical decision support resources; and (4) publications in English or Chinese.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) publications for which the full text could not be retrieved or those with insufficient methodological detail; (2) conference abstracts, study protocols, or interim reports; (3) studies that did not meet the minimum quality threshold according to validated appraisal tools specific to each evidence type; and (4) superseded guidance for which updated versions were available.

Two reviewers independently screened the evidence sources according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreement was resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Finally, eight studies7,14,19–24 that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and were rated as moderate quality or higher were included: two guidelines,22,23 two clinical decision,20,21 one consensus statement,14 and three randomized controlled trials.7,14,19,24 A total of 23 recommendations were extracted from the included evidence sources.

In February 2025, four ICU nurses participated in stakeholder meetings to appraise the evidence using the FAME framework, focusing on feasibility, appropriateness, clinical significance, and effectiveness.

In addition, one urologist was invited to review the proposed evidence summary, raise concerns, and provide constructive feedback. Ultimately, 15 best evidences were selected, and 21 audit standards were developed.

Analysis of Barriers and Facilitators

Based on the i-PARIHS analysis and feedback from frontline nurses, managers, and relevant stakeholders, an evidence-based practice change strategy was developed across three domains: change content, change recipients, and the organizational environment.

At the level of change content, a user-friendly selection guide for catheter materials and models and standardized diagrams for secondary catheter securement were developed. Where workflow documents were lacking, the documents were developed with reference to the most recent clinical guidelines, consensus statements, and applicable group standards.

At the level of change recipients, the department implemented a three-part training programme. First, structured sessions were integrated into routine departmental education to explain the evidence base and key practice points. Second, simulation workshops provided focused training on critical steps, including catheter model selection and secondary catheter securement. Third, the evidence-based practice team offered one-to-one bedside coaching and supervision to ensure that practice remained consistent with evidence-informed standards.

At the organizational level, reducing the incidence of urinary leakage from indwelling urinary catheters was set as a departmental quality improvement priority, with clear policies, timelines, and resource support. A consumables plan was submitted to the logistics department to ensure the supply of silicone urinary catheters, multiple catheter sizes, and dedicated securement dressings. Relevant standard operating procedures, nursing care routines, and competency assessment criteria were revised in parallel.

Stage 4: Evaluation of Effect

Patient data for 122 cases admitted between May and August 2025 were collected retrospectively as the control group, and 113 cases admitted between September and December 2025 comprised the evidence-based practice group. For both groups, the number of urinary leakage events during indwelling urinary catheterisation was recorded, and the incidence of urinary leakage was compared between groups. ICU nurses with more than 1 year of experience completed a knowledge, beliefs, and practices survey on catheter-associated urinary leakage, and domain scores were compared before and after the evidence-based implementation.

Measures

Primary Outcome Measures

Patient data were collected prospectively for both groups. Patients admitted from May to August 2025 formed the control group and were observed before implementation of the Urinary Leakage Bundle. Patients admitted from September to December 2025 formed the intervention group and were observed after implementation of the Urinary Leakage Bundle.

For each group, urinary leakage was monitored during indwelling urinary catheterisation, from ICU admission or catheter insertion until catheter removal, ICU discharge, ICU transfer, or death, whichever occurred first. Urinary leakage was operationally defined as visible urine leakage around the urethral meatus or wetness of the perineal skin, bed linens, or clothing caused by urine bypassing the indwelling urinary catheter while the catheter remained in place.

During the observation period, bedside nurses identified urinary leakage during routine catheter care, perineal skin care, and daily nursing assessment. When urinary leakage was observed, the bedside nurse recorded the date, time, clinical manifestation, and related nursing observations in the nursing record and reported the event to the researcher. The researcher then reviewed the relevant nursing records and confirmed whether the event met the operational definition of urinary leakage. A patient was recorded as having urinary leakage if at least one confirmed leakage episode occurred during the predefined observation period. The incidence of urinary leakage was then calculated for each group.

Secondary Outcomes

The questionnaire used in this study was developed by the Chinese scholar Chen Hanxi.17 It assesses nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding urinary leakage associated with indwelling urinary catheters in China. The questionnaire contains 27 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater agreement. Seventeen items assess the knowledge domain, six items assess the attitude domain, and four items assess the practice domain. Item analysis, exploratory factor analysis, and confirmatory factor analysis supported the construct validity of the questionnaire. The questionnaire also showed excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.957.

Study of the Intervention and Data Analysis

In this study, data on nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding urinary leakage were collected using a professional online questionnaire distributed via the WeChat platform. All items were set as mandatory to ensure data completeness. The occurrence of urinary leakage was reported by the responsible nurses and verified by members of the research team. All data were entered independently by two researchers and double checked to ensure accuracy.

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test or the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and between-group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t test. Non-normally distributed data were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For comparisons within the same participants before and after the intervention, the paired samples t test was used. All tests were two sided, and a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Ninety-five per cent confidence intervals were calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0.

Discussion

This study applied an evidence based continuous quality improvement model as the framework to promote the integration of evidence based practice, with the primary aim of reducing the incidence of urinary leakage in ICU patients with indwelling urinary catheters. The implementation of the evidence based Urinary Leakage Bundle was associated with a lower urinary leakage rate, with the incidence decreasing from 25.4% before implementation to 13.3% after implementation. These findings are consistent with those reported in previous studies. Yang Jianying19 reported that a key point control approach to improve the water bag pre-filling procedure reduced the incidence of urinary leakage from 21% to 8%. However, the study used 16–18 Fr double-lumen latex urinary catheters and did not include patients requiring bladder irrigation, which may explain the lower incidence compared with the present study. Fletke26 suggested that when tubing kinking, catheter obstruction, and inappropriate positioning of the drainage bag above bladder level have been excluded, and bypass leakage persists after correction, patients may be treated with oral agents used for overactive bladder. This opinion appears to be consistent with the results of the present study on the urine leakage bundle. Fletke26 also noted that silicone catheters are stiffer and may cause greater urethral irritation, and that many patients with long term indwelling catheters prefer latex catheters because of comfort. This differs from the present study, which supports the use of silicone catheters for long term catheterisation. In this study, silicone catheters were considered to reduce biofilm formation at the catheter tip and thereby decrease urinary leakage, whereas Fletke focused on patient comfort.

In this evidence based practice, nurses showed significant improvements in knowledge, attitudes, and practice. Audit indicators developed from the best available evidence were used to assess clinical nurses, identify areas with low compliance, and guide targeted training. The training addressed the causes of urinary leakage, related risk factors, and preventive measures, thereby improving nurses’ knowledge of catheter related leakage. This supported more standardised implementation of prevention and management procedures, closer patient observation, and timely and appropriate management, providing a sound basis for reducing the incidence of urinary leakage.

An observational study27 in Yemen on healthcare workers’ compliance with guidelines for the prevention of catheter associated urinary tract infection emphasised that regular in service education and training, together with adequate access to necessary materials and supplies, are essential. This is similar to the present study, in which an evidence based practice change strategy was developed across three domains based on the i-PARIHS framework and feedback from frontline nurses, managers, and relevant stakeholders. Alex28 also reported that education tailored to clinical context is important for reinforcing nurses’ practice and improving adherence to guidelines. Therefore, implementing evidence based practice for the prevention and management of urinary leakage in patients with indwelling urinary catheters can improve nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practice, and support the standardised prevention and management of urinary leakage.

Implications for Practice

This study suggested that the systematic application of evidence based practice was associated with a lower urinary leakage rate in ICU patients with indwelling catheters. The findings showed that ICU nurses had limitations in management strategies for leakage, particularly in catheter selection and balloon inflation volume. In clinical practice, when leakage occurred, increasing the balloon volume was often used as the first approach. If this was ineffective, a larger catheter size was selected, and no further action was taken when leakage persisted, which increased the risk of skin complications.

These findings suggest that, although ICU nurses have some experience in managing leakage, additional knowledge and practice based training are required to support the effective implementation of evidence based guidelines. Therefore, in this study, the Urinary Leakage Bundle was implemented using the i-PARIHS framework to systematically assess barriers and facilitators. This approach provides a practical model for other institutions to manage urinary leakage in ICU patients with indwelling catheters.

The results also indicate that a nurse-led evidence based leakage management programme may improve care processes and patient-related outcomes. Nurses were involved in the development of the intervention and played a key role in coordinating resources to meet clinical needs, allowing adaptation to different ICU settings and potentially contributing to a lower occurrence of leakage. This study highlights the importance of tailoring interventions to the ICU context and addressing challenges during implementation, which may contribute to improved quality of care and treatment outcomes.

Limitations

The research project had several limitations. First, this was a single centre study conducted in one ICU, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Second, although audit indicators were used to assess practice, some data relied on observation and self report, which may be subject to observation and reporting bias. Third, the study was conducted over a relatively short period, and the long term sustainability of the intervention effects was not evaluated. In addition, patient related factors, such as underlying conditions and severity of illness, were not fully controlled and may have influenced the occurrence of urinary leakage. Finally, although the intervention was implemented using the i-PARIHS framework, differences in organisational context and resource availability across settings may affect its wider application.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the value of an evidence based practice programme in improving the prevention and management of urinary leakage in ICU patients with indwelling catheters. The findings highlight the importance of a nurse led and structured approach, as reflected by improvements in nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practice, as well as more standardised care. The reduction in urinary leakage suggests that the intervention is both effective and feasible in the ICU setting. The application of the i PARIHS framework supported the systematic assessment of barriers and facilitators and facilitated implementation in clinical practice. Further research is needed to examine the sustainability of this intervention and to evaluate its effects in multi centre studies with larger samples and more detailed outcome measures, particularly with respect to different levels of leakage severity.
