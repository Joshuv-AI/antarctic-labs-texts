Intraprocedural tail-first pulmonary artery migration of a leadless pacemaker: a protective sleeve–assisted helix-side retrieval strategy—a case report

Introduction

The Aveir™ leadless pacemaker system (Abbott, Abbott Park, Illinois, USA) is widely used for the treatment of symptomatic bradycardia. Although device dislodgement has been reported rarely,1–7 migration into the pulmonary artery is an extremely rare complication.1–3,5

Standard retrieval is performed using a dedicated retrieval catheter to grasp the docking button at the device tail. However, previously reported cases of pulmonary artery migration were managed using non-dedicated systems with deflectable sheaths and snare catheters. Such approaches may increase the risk of vascular injury due to traction on the exposed helix during retrieval.

We report a case of tail-first migration of an Aveir™ VR leadless pacemaker into the pulmonary artery, in which the docking button was inaccessible. Successful retrieval was achieved using a protective sleeve–assisted technique with helix enclosure.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old woman with bradycardic atrial fibrillation was referred for leadless pacemaker implantation. Her medical history included mitral valve replacement with a 25 mm Epic bioprosthetic valve and tricuspid annuloplasty with a 28 mm Carpentier–Edwards annuloplasty ring. An Aveir™ VR leadless pacemaker was delivered through a 27 Fr introducer sheath via the right femoral vein and positioned at the mid-septal region of the right ventricle. Pre-mapping measurements showed a pacing threshold of 1.5 V at 0.4 ms, an R-wave amplitude of 15.0 mV, and an impedance of 450 Ω. The LP control knob on the delivery catheter was then rotated clockwise under fluoroscopic guidance until the radiopaque chevron marker reached the manufacturer-recommended 1.5-turn endpoint (Figure 1A and B). After screw-in, impedance increased to 560 Ω, a current of injury of ∼3–4 mV was observed, and electrical parameters remained satisfactory (R-wave sensing 5.5 mV and pacing threshold 1.5 V at 0.4 ms). After confirming fixation by the standard deflection test, the device was released (Figure 1C). Immediately after release, fluoroscopy showed a tail-down shift of the device (Figure 1D) accompanied by frequent premature ventricular contractions and non-sustained ventricular tachycardia, suggesting inadequate mechanical engagement. Because of the risk of further migration or ventricular arrhythmia, retrieval and re-implantation were planned.

An Aveir™ retrieval catheter was introduced through the femoral sheath, with the tip positioned just proximal to the tricuspid valve. As the catheter advanced into the right ventricle, the device tail was displaced towards the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT). During the retrieval manoeuvre, the device abruptly migrated into the left pulmonary artery in a tail-first orientation (Figure 1E and F; Supplementary material online, Video S1). Because the docking button was inaccessible, we adopted a protective sleeve–assisted retrieval strategy to protect the exposed helix. With the protective sleeve leading and the retrieval catheter maintained in a retracted position, the system was advanced from the right ventricle through the RVOT into the pulmonary artery under fluoroscopic guidance while maintaining gentle tangential contact with the vessel wall (Figure 1G; Supplementary material online, Video S2).

After optimal positioning, the dedicated tri-loop snare was deployed, the helix was grasped, and the device was enclosed within the protective sleeve (Figure 1H and I). The device was then retrieved from the pulmonary artery.

Visual inspection of the retrieved device and catheter confirmed that the snare had grasped the helix proximal to its tip, with no deformation of the helix or adherent tissue observed. (Figure 2A and B).

Images of the retrieved leadless pacemaker with the helix captured by the snare. (A) Photograph of the retrieved Aveir leadless pacemaker. The device is shown after removal, with the helix securely captured by the snare. (B) Close-up view of the helix, highlighting the engagement point of the snare. The retrieved device is shown with the helix securely engaged by the snare. Direct traction on the exposed helix without enclosure within the protective sleeve of the dedicated retrieval system may result in injury to the vessel wall. Enclosing the helix within the protective sleeve before retrieval may reduce direct contact between the exposed helix and the vessel wall.

The interval from pulmonary artery migration to retrieval was ∼10 min.

A new Aveir™ VR was subsequently implanted at a more apical septal site. Compared with the initial implantation, in which the device was positioned at a nearly horizontal angle, the second implantation was performed with the device oriented at ∼20° from horizontal on the right anterior oblique view and directed more towards the ventricular septum on the left anterior oblique view. The post-fixation pacing threshold was 1.5 V at 0.4 ms, the R-wave amplitude was 5.0 mV, and the impedance was 450 Ω. Unlike the initial implantation, no tail-down shift or ventricular ectopy was observed after release. Post-procedural transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no pericardial effusion.

The present patient had bradycardic atrial fibrillation requiring ventricular pacing and had previously undergone tricuspid annuloplasty. The 2021 ESC Guidelines discuss the use of leadless pacemakers in selected patients and considerations after tricuspid valve surgery. However, they do not specifically address retrieval of a dislodged leadless pacemaker.8 Surgical retrieval may need to be considered when the device has remained in the pulmonary artery for a prolonged period or has migrated into the distal pulmonary artery. In our case, because migration occurred intraprocedurally and the device remained in the proximal pulmonary artery, a percutaneous retrieval strategy was considered appropriate.

Among the previously reported cases, Amin et al. and Goto et al. reported tail-first pulmonary artery migration, and both cases were managed using non-dedicated systems with deflectable sheaths and snare catheters.1–3,5

Non-dedicated approaches may be acceptable in tip-first dislodgement when docking button engagement is feasible. However, in tail-first configurations, traction on the exposed helix may increase the risk of vascular injury. In this setting, the protective sleeve may help prevent direct contact between the helix and the vessel wall during retrieval.

Although a guidewire-assisted approach may facilitate catheter delivery, we avoided this strategy because removal of the guidewire could cause deformation of the protective sleeve, potentially hindering advancement of the retrieval catheter.

A simple bench experiment compared the flexibility of the protective sleeve with a commonly used ablation catheter (ThermoCool SmartTouch SF, Biosense Webster). (see Supplementary material online, Video S3). The ablation catheter bent at ∼100 g under vertical force, whereas the protective sleeve required ∼270 g, indicating greater resistance to deformation. Under oblique force, both devices bent at ∼10–30 g. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because they may not fully reflect in vivo conditions. Nevertheless, the protective sleeve should be advanced tangentially rather than orthogonally against the vessel wall.

The precise mechanism underlying the initial instability remains uncertain. However, the implantation angle, inadequate septal fixation with insufficient helix penetration, and altered right ventricular geometry related to prior tricuspid annuloplasty may have contributed. Despite satisfactory electrical parameters before release, mechanical fixation proved inadequate, suggesting that the decision to release the device should not rely solely on electrical parameters.

In contrast, stable fixation was achieved at a more apical septal site with more direct septal orientation.

The immediate tail-down shift after device release may represent an early fluoroscopic warning sign of inadequate mechanical fixation, warranting careful reassessment before further device migration occurs.

Although the exact mechanism of migration remains uncertain, the device may have remained attached only by the tip of the helix. A change in device orientation during the retrieval attempt may then have resulted in complete disengagement.

Previous cardiac surgery may have reduced the risk of procedure-related pericardial effusion. Therefore, the safety of this approach in patients without previous cardiac surgery remains uncertain. Further clinical experience is needed to confirm its reproducibility.

Conclusion

Tail-first pulmonary artery migration of an Aveir™ VR leadless pacemaker requires careful procedural planning because standard docking button retrieval is not feasible. In this case, a protective sleeve–assisted helix-side retrieval technique provided a feasible bailout option.
