Spatial and landscape-scale variability in global mangrove soil carbon estimates
Authors: Lucy Carruthers, Lukas Lamb-Wotton, Jaxine Wolfe, David Lagomasino, Stuart E. Hamilton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate global mangrove soil carbon estimates are essential, considering the critical role mangroves play in the coastal carbon cycle. However, current global estimates vary widely, ranging from 2.26 to 10.2 Pg C. Large uncertainties in carbon stocks can be challenging for effective policymaking and inaccurately estimate climate mitigation potential. Here, we identify factors driving spatial and landscape variability by comparing five global mangrove soil carbon stock models. Using the ensemble mean, we generate a global soil carbon stock estimate of 4.13 ± 0.89 Pg C (to 1 m depth). Significant (P < 0.001) spatial variability occurred in Africa and Asia, with high standard deviation between models and limited data representation. High standard deviation occurred in specific geomorphic settings, including terrigenous, deltaic, carbonate and open coasts, within microtidal sites and regions of low and high species diversity. Our findings can help guide future research and reduce uncertainties in carbon stock estimates.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51820-4.

Introduction

Mangrove forests cover ~ 147,359 km21,2 of the world’s coastlines and are globally recognised for their capacity to provide crucial ecosystem services valued at $2.7 trillion USD year-13. Among these benefits is their capacity to efficiently store large quantities of carbon relative to their surface area (~ 2834 to 3615 Mg C ha− 1)6–9, with 75% of carbon stored within the soil10. Mangroves store 2.2611 to 10.212 Pg C of carbon which can be 2 to 10 times more than saltmarshes and seagrass meadows8. In addition to carbon storage, mangroves rapidly accumulate organic carbon, with rates ranging from 138.6 to 194 g C m− 2 y-113–15. This high accumulation and storage of carbon in mangroves is shaped by key abiotic and biotic factors including geomorphology11,16, rainfall17, tidal flooding18 and species diversity19,20, which can lead to variations in soil carbon from within individual forests to patterns observed globally.

Despite the benefits of mangroves to climate change mitigation, global mangrove cover has reduced by 30 to 50% over the past century21, driven by climatic change (e.g. tropical cyclones, extreme climatic events)22–24 and anthropogenic activities and conversion25,26. As a result, from 1980 to 2018, the global reduction in mangrove soil carbon stocks due to land-use and land-cover change was estimated to be 54% ± 13%27. Notably, aquaculture conversion significantly disturbs carbon stocks, removing ~ 60% of soil carbon stock relative to undisturbed sites28. As one of the most carbon-rich ecosystems in the tropics6, mangrove loss contributes substantially to global anthropogenic greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions releasing ~ 7.0 Tg CO₂eq yr⁻¹4. This loss corresponds to ~ 0.1% of total global forest emissions between 2001 and 2024 (9.2 Gt CO₂eq yr⁻¹)29. Crucially, mangrove GHG emissions could reach 2391 Tg CO₂eq by the end of the century30.

Given the crucial role of mangroves in the coastal carbon cycle, and their vulnerability to disturbances, accurate global soil carbon estimates are essential to support blue carbon science and policy8,10. To date, many studies have quantified global soil carbon stocks and spatially explicit carbon concentrations. Each study was unique in spatial resolution (30 m to 25 km2), temporal coverage (2000 to 2020), and by handling approaches and upscaling (Table 1)4,5,9,11,12,17,31. One of the first estimates32 was produced by developing a predictive model at 5 arc min resolution based on a global dataset of 932 sediment core records from previous meta-analyses6,33,34. Global soil carbon stock estimates were 5.00 ± 0.94 (± 1 SE) Pg C (1 m depth), with an average of 369 ± 6.8 (± 1 SE) Mg C ha− 1. More recently,9 the largest soil carbon dataset was compiled and spatiotemporal predictive ensemble machine learning was employed to generate global estimates at 30 m resolution for 20201 for a total of 4.6 Pg C (1 m depth) and an average of 350 Mg C ha− 1. Though these values may be similar, the spatial patterns and underlying variable driving carbon differ.

Variability in global soil carbon estimates can lead to challenges for effective policymaking by inaccurately estimating climate mitigation potential35–37. According to the 2013 Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Wetlands Supplement, large uncertainties in carbon stocks make it difficult to determine whether annual carbon emissions and removals are real simply artifacts of measurement uncertainty38. Errors in annual carbon emissions estimates can lead to major economic consequences, given that carbon credits for mangrove conservation range between 3.0 and 13.0 US$ per tCO2 and 4.5 and 18.0 US$ per tCO2 for restoration39. Therefore, assessing and reducing variability in global mangrove carbon stocks estimates is critical for generating robust data that can answer important policy questions with significant climatic and economic implications40,41.

Here, we present a comprehensive global comparison of mangrove soil carbon models and identify important factors driving spatial and landscape variability. We standardised and compared five soil carbon maps from Maxwell et al., 2023 (TM-2023)9, Rovai et al., 2018 (AR-2018)11, Sanderman et al., 2018 (JS-2018)42, Hamilton and Friess, 2018 (SH_DF-2018)31, and Jardine and Siikamäki, 2014 (JS_SJ-2014)32. We included known drivers of soil carbon such as mangrove biophysical typology43, annual rainfall44, tidal amplitude45, and mangrove species diversity46. We utilized sediment core records from the Coastal Carbon Network (CCN)47, an open-source global tidal wetlands database, to compare carbon stock values between field measurements and modelled estimates. Using this information, we outline key spatial and landscape knowledge gaps and offer targeted recommendations, to help reduce variability in future regional and global carbon stock estimates.

Global trends in mangrove soil organic carbon variability

By integrating existing mangrove soil carbon models, we derived a new global mangrove soil organic carbon stock value using the ensemble mean (± 1 standard deviation) of the models in this study. Our global estimate is 4.13 ± 0.89 Pg C (to a depth of 1 m) and range from 2.96 to 5.31 Pg C. We further investigated patterns in spatial variability between the models by determining the standard deviation between the five global models for each grid cell (Pg C/0.25°) (Fig. 1). Across the Americas, we find high levels of standard deviation between models in Florida, including the Everglades (2.78 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°), Cuba (4.26 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°), parts of Mexico (1.78 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°), as well as across the coastlines of Brazil (2.29 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°) and Colombia (1.68 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°) (Fig. 1B). In Africa, we observed high levels of variability across Western parts of the continent including Guinea-Bissau (2.89 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°), Guinea (2.92 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°), and Nigeria (4.67 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°), with Nigeria exhibiting the greatest variability (Fig. 1C). Southeast Asia (Indonesia: 4.74 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°), Papa New Guinea (3.57 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°) and parts of Northern Australia (1.73 × 10− 3 Pg C/0.25°) also revealed high levels of carbon variability (Fig. 1D).

A continent-level assessment showed significant differences in standard deviation between models for both total carbon (df = 5, F value = 32.09, P < 0.001) and carbon density (df = 4, F value = 20.29, P < 0.001). We find that total carbon is significantly higher in Africa (P < 0.001; mean value 3.31 × 10− 4 Pg C/0.25°) compared to other continents (Fig. 2A). Conversely, the standard deviation between models of carbon density was significantly (P = 0.024; mean value 107 Mg C/0.25°) lower in Africa compared to Asia, Oceania, North and South America (Fig. 2B). Prior studies have also found low variability in organic carbon density across Africa50,51, whereby forests in Vanga and Gazi Bay, Kenya, revealed no significant differences between the separate sites51. Conversely, across West Africa, soil carbon stocks (to 1 m) exhibited greater variability in carbon than the continent average, with mean (± 1 SE) values of 240 ± 27 Mg C ha− 1 in Senegal, 342 ± 13 Mg C ha− 1 in Liberia, 345 ± 12 Mg C ha− 1 in North Gabon and 191 ± 31 Mg C ha− 1 in South Gabon52.

Even though Africa contains ~ 20% of the global mangrove coverage2, it only holds 8.8% of the global grid cells containing sediment cores from the Coastal Carbon Atlas database (CCN)47. This means the continent has the least amount of field data representation and therefore training data for scaling carbon estimates, which can be an important factor driving variability across the models. Africa’s diverse climatic and geomorphic settings, spanning arid to tropical zones and poleward mangrove boundaries, likely underlie the high variability in soil carbon density across the continent. Among the grid cells containing mangroves in Africa, approximately 24.2% are in deltas, 44.0%, in estuaries, 11.4% in lagoons and 20.4% along open coasts. This distribution differs from that of field data with the majority in deltas (58.3%) follow by estuaries (29.2%), open coasts (8.3%) and lagoons (4.2%). Moreover, 95.9% of the grid cells fall within tropical zones, 1.0% in arid and 3.1% subtropical, most of the field data exists in tropical regions. This mismatch between the location of field data used in the models and the distribution of settings known to influence carbon stocks can lead to potential geographical bias53 and inaccuracies in carbon stock estimates54. Expanding field data collection across Africa’s diverse geomorphic and climatic settings will improve carbon stock estimates and clarify whether low variability reflects natural conditions or limited sampling.

By comparison, Oceania displayed the highest standard deviation between models for carbon density (132 Mg C/0.25°) and represents ~ 1.6% of the global mangrove area extent with 1.8% of grid cells with field data. Field studies in Oceania reveal striking variability in mangrove soil carbon55, ranging from 582.7 to 874.9 Mg C ha− 16 in Micronesia, to just 20.9 Mg C ha− 1 to 69.4 Mg C ha− 1 across sites in Aotearoa New Zealand56. If large regional variations in soil carbon heterogeneity are not accurately captured, they can introduce substantial variability in model estimates.

We also compared field, and model estimates to evaluate whether variability stems from differences in modelling approaches. Key sources of uncertainty include the training data (e.g. soil carbon database), spatial resolution, or model type. Ideally, model outputs should align closely with field data whereby this analysis was based solely on grid cells where sediment cores from the CCN were available. When comparing field and an ensemble of all modelled data, no significant differences were observed in either total soil organic carbon stocks or density estimates (Fig. 2C,D). Median values for total soil organic carbon for field and ensemble modelled data were 6.27 × 10− 4 and 6.49 × 10− 4 Pg C/0.25°, respectively, while median values for carbon density were 259 and 305 Mg C/0.25°, respectively. This lack of significance between field data and the model ensemble indicates that the ensemble outputs could provide a more accurate representation of measured field data.

Further, we compared total carbon across all individual models, TM-20239, AR-201811, JS-201842, SH_DF-201831, and JS_SJ-20132. We observed a significant difference (df = 5, F value = 94.28, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2E) between individual models rather than between models and field data. These included SH_DF-2018 and JS-2018 (P = 0.038), SJ_JS-2014 and SH_DF-2018 (P = 0.015) and SJ_JS-2014 and AR-2018 (P = 0.049). Importantly, the differences clustered according to the extent maps originally used. The SH_DF-2018 and AR-2018 models were based on a smaller extent map (83,495 km²), while SJ_JS-2014 and JS-2018 used a larger extent map (137,760 km²) which shows the systematic difference caused by the underlying mangrove distribution layers. While all models were standardized to the same extent map for comparison, legacy effects from the original extent maps may still be influencing carbon total results.

To reduce the potential influence of mangrove extent, we assessed differences between field and modelled data based on carbon per unit area (Fig. 2F). We found all individual models differed significantly from field data (df = 4, F value = 57.84, P < 0.001). Significantly higher estimates were generated by SJ_JS-2014 (P < 0.001; median value 367 Mg C/0.25°) and JS-2018 (P < 0.001; median value 333 Mg C/0.25°) models compared to field data (median = 259 Mg C/0.25°) while AR-2018 (P < 0.001; median values 265 Mg C/0.25°) and TM-2023 (P < 0.01; median values 266 Mg C/0.25°) models were lower than field data.

The differences between model and field data may arise from several factors. Soil carbon databases are unlikely to be the source, as TM-2023 largely relied on the database established by JS-2018 while AR-2018 built its database from SJ_JS-2014. Spatial resolution also seems an unlikely factor, given the variation among models, JS-2018 and TM-2023 at 30 m, SJ_JS-2014 at 5 arc-min, and AR-2018 at 0.25°. As no consistent patterns emerged in the regional, climatic, hydrodynamic or mangrove forest variables used and given that each model identified a different primary driver of soil organic carbon, drivers can be discounted as a plausible explanation. One possible explanation for the differences between field and modelled data may lie in the modelling approaches given that JS-2018 and SJ_JS-2018 employed single-learner machine learning models, TM-2023 used an ensemble approach, and AR-2018 applied a statistical model. Consequently, single-learner machine learning models could lead to overestimations in carbon densities due to being more prone to overfitting and overextrapolation9,57.

Landscape scale variability in mangrove soil organic carbon estimates

Sediment origin plays a critical role in shaping variability in mangrove soil carbon, with terrigenous and carbonate systems showing significant (P < 0.001) differences between the model variability (Fig. 3A). Variability in total carbon was higher in terrigenous systems (2.26 × 10− 4 Pg C/0.25°) compared to carbonate systems (8.74 × 10− 5 Pg C/0.25°). This pattern likely reflects the wide spatial extent of terrigenous mangroves, which account for 90% of the global mangrove area43, and span diverse environmental gradients that may contribute to variability in soil carbon estimates. In contrast, when assessing for soil carbon density, variability between models is significantly (P < 0.0001) higher in carbonate systems (117 Mg C/0.25°) relative to terrigenous ones (107 Mg C/0.25°) (Fig. 3B).

These opposing results align with differences in sediment process. Terrigenous systems are influenced by allochthonous inputs of mineral material transported from surrounding catchments, which generate broad-scale gradients but can dampen fine-scale variability58,59. Carbonate systems, by comparison, are dominated by autochthonous sediment, where organic carbon is primarily mangrove-derived peat formed from the buildup of organic matter60. This autochthonous carbon is labile and easily metabolized61, producing soil carbon stocks that are highly site-specific and therefore more variability local and regional scales62. For example, an analysis of 35 sediment cores in the carbonate islands of Karimunjawa, Indonesia, revealed high variability with soil carbon stocks ranging from 3.3 to 366.7 Mg C ha-163 ,while along the karstic coast of the Mexican Caribbean, seven sediment cores varied by an order of magnitude from 286 to 1166 Mg C ha-164.

Across geomorphic settings, we observed significant differences in variability between models for both total carbon (df = 3, F value = 415.9, P < 0.001) and carbon density (df = 3, F value = 64.09, P < 0.001). Deltaic systems, which account for 40.5% of mangrove extent43 displayed the greatest variability in total organic carbon (6.19 × 10− 4 Pg C/0.25°; P < 0.001) compared to other estuarine (2.35 × 10− 4), lagoon (1.59 × 10− 4) and open coasts (6.97 × 10− 5) (Fig. 3C). As deltas are inherently terrigenous, their broad spatial extent and diverse settings may drive much of the variability observed in terrigenous systems.

In contrast, deltas showed one of the lowest variabilities between models of soil carbon density (99 Mg C/0.25°). The variability was significantly (P < 0.05) lower than lagoons (107 Mg C/0.25°) and open coasts (119 Mg C/0.25°). This low variability in soil carbon density has been reported for several deltaic systems around the world. In the Zambezi River Delta, Mozambique soil carbon stocks ranged insignificantly from 274.6 Mg C ha− 1 to 314.1 Mg C ha− 1 across a large spatial extent (30,267 ha)65. For mangrove sites in Brazil, soil organic carbon stocks were similarly consistent values ranging from 180.5 to 226.3 Mg C ha− 112. Deltaic soils are mainly constructed from allochthonous sediment, largely composed of minerals derived from riverine sediments, tidally resuspended muds or biogenic calcareous sediment43,60 characterized by high dry bulk density and low organic carbon content66,67. These properties likely contribute to the lower variability in soil carbon density as the lower concentration of carbon per unit area could lead to lower variation.

At the other extreme, open coasts showed the significantly (P < 0.001) highest variability in carbon density models. Mangroves in these settings typically occupy sheltered sites that can be exposed to moderate wave energies and are subject to frequent or occasional tidal inundation60. These exposed sites can experience higher rates of erosion and deposition, with rates of surface elevation change ranging from − 1.3 to 5.9 mm year-160. This dynamic setting, with greater exposure to sediment resuspension, deposition, and redistribution may contribute to the higher heterogeneity in carbon density observed in this geomorphic setting due to locally derived sources of mineral and organic material.

We did not find clear patterns between annual rainfall and variability between models, as high standard deviation occurred across all rainfall regions (Fig. 3E,F). Mean standard deviation between models for total carbon across arid, subtropical and tropical regions was 2.22 × 10− 6, 4.46 × 10− 5, 1.80 × 10− 4, Pg C/0.25°, respectively, while the standard deviation for carbon density averaged 157, 140 and 119 Mg C/0.25°, respectively. Four out of the five models included rainfall in their global estimates, with one study identifying annual rainfall as the primary driver of soil organic carbon32. Most mangroves occur in tropical regions (96.2% of grid cells), with only 3.7% of grid cells in the subtropics and 0.1% in arid regions. Field data largely mirrors this, with 91.2% of grid cells in the tropical sites, 8.8% in the subtropics, though no field data are yet available from arid regions.

Spatial patterns in model variability were more distinct across tidal ranges (Fig. 3G,H). For total carbon, mean standard deviation was lowest in microtidal sites (1.68 × 10− 4 Pg C/0.25°) and highest in macrotidal sites (5.63 × 10− 4 Pg C/0.25°). In contrast, mean standard deviation for carbon density was highest in micro and mesotidal (119 Mg C/0.25°) environments and lowest in macro (111 Mg C/0.25°) zones. Similar to differences observed between carbon and terrigenous systems, the patterns in variability may reflect sediment origin and processes. Microtidal environments typically favour the build-up of autochthonous soil organic carbon whereas allochthonous sediment is generally found at sites with higher tidal ranges60. Tidal amplitude was well represented and included in four of five models with field data closely matching global tidal zone distribution.

Biodiversity is a known driver of mangrove soil carbon variability50,68–70 with different dominant species storing markedly different stocks. For example, Avicennia dominated forests averaged 166.7 ± 24.4 Mg C ha− 1 compared to 437.2 ± 34.1 Mg C ha− 1 in Bruguiera dominated forests12. Species richness can also influence soil carbon stocks4 as reported across 234 field plots and 30 transects in China, whereby higher species present (9–15) stored the most carbon (537 Mg C ha− 1) compared to the overall mean of all sites (328 Mg C ha− 1)19. We find a bimodal distribution between species diversity and variability between models for both total carbon and carbon density, suggesting that low and high species richness results in more variability between models (Fig. 3I,J). For total carbon, mean standard deviation between models for low, intermediate and high species diversity was 1.91 × 10− 3, 1.13 × 10− 4 and 1.65 × 10− 4 Pg C/0.25°, respectively, while for carbon density values were 119, 114 and 121 Mg C/0.25°, respectively. None of the models in this analysis incorporated species information, and based on our findings, the exclusion of this variable from global estimates may be contributing to the observed variability between models.

Globally, 50.6% of mangrove grid cells fall within low diversity areas, while 7.6% and 41.8% fall within intermediate and high diversity areas, respectively. However, the distribution of field data is disproportionately skewed, with 71.7% of the field plot data located in low diversity areas, while only 2.9% and 25.4% are in intermediate and high diversity zones, respectively. Given the demonstrated link between species diversity and soil carbon variability70, integrating community composition into global blue carbon estimates and improving field representation across species diversity gradients would likely help reduce uncertainties in carbon stock estimates.

Study limitations

While this study offers valuable insights into spatial and landscape-scale variability in global mangrove soil carbon estimates, several caveats must be acknowledged and considered when interpreting the results. Firstly, while cores were standardized to 1 m depth to align with IPCC methodology71 and global model estimates, we acknowledge that such estimates likely represent a conservative lower bound of total ecosystem carbon storage. Secondly, sediment cores without dry bulk density measurements were excluded from this study, however, this exclusion was randomly distributed and does not represent specific soil types of geomorphic settings. Consequently, we believe that systematic bias has not been introduced into our findings. Thirdly, we followed the Blue Carbon Manual72 by applying a global relationship between organic matter (OM) and soil organic carbon (SOC) which yielded a coefficient (0.476*OM) comparable to the one reported in the Blue Carbon Manual (0.415*OM). While we acknowledge regional relationships are typically more accurate as they take into consideration different geomorphic and bioclimatic zones, we adopted the currently accepted standardized global approach currently used within the blue carbon community. Finally, statistically significant differences detected between models should be interpreted with caution, given that shared underlying features (e.g., databases and extent maps) mean the models are not fully independent of one another.

Future needs in global mangrove soil organic carbon estimates

Understanding the drivers of spatial and landscape variability in mangrove soil carbon models are essential for improving global carbon estimates and reducing uncertainty in future projections. Our synthesis identifies specific gaps in both field data coverage and modelling approaches that disproportionately affect certain regions, geomorphic settings, and ecosystem types. The following research priorities provide a path forward for improving global datasets, refining models, and ensuring that underrepresented regions and ecosystem types are more accurately captured in future assessments.

Incorporate geomorphology more explicitly into modelling efforts. Although geomorphology strongly influences carbon storage11,16,73, it is underrepresented in both models and field data, with only two of five models incorporating it. The field dataset reflects the global distribution of terrigenous and carbonate systems, but deltas are notably underrepresented (20.8% versus 40.5% of global extent), while estuaries and lagoons are overrepresented relative to their area. Increasing field data in deltas and explicitly incorporating geomorphic distributions into global models could help reduce uncertainties in future predictions.Greater research emphasis in Africa. Before we begin new data collection efforts, we must strengthen capacity building with Indigenous peoples and local communities as well as local scientists, institutions, and stakeholders in data-poor regions or environments. There may be valuable existing unpublished datasets which may fill in these identified data gaps. Notably, archiving such datasets at the highest resolution (i.e. original measurements of the depth cores series) can help reduce uncertainties in soil organic carbon estimates. We must engage directly with in-country stakeholders to help identify and utilize these resources and support their integration into global open-source networks such as the CCN47. This will help avoid duplication of efforts and ensure that knowledge already generated is acknowledged and integrated into future regional or global scale analyses.

The methodological processes of this study was based on combining five global mangrove soil models, four external primary drivers of mangrove carbon, and a compendium of field collected carbon soil cores (Fig. 4).

Jardine and Siikamaki, 2014

This study created a database of 932 sediment cores from 28 countries based on three previous studies6,33,34. This model used a mangrove extent map of 137, 760 km2 for the year 2000 and had a spatial resolution of 5 arc minute. Soil organic carbon estimates were generated using a machine learning (bag decision tree) model and model performance was tested using out of sample sum of squared errors (SSE; 0.844 × 105) and mean percentage error (MPE; 32.36) from 1000 random divisions of independent studies in the data. Key input variables used to predict organic carbon were mainly regional (i.e. biogeographic) or climatic (i.e. air temperature and precipitation). Global stock estimates (1 ± standard error) were 5.00 ± 0.95 Pg C (in the top 1 m) with mean (1 ± standard error) 369 ± 6.8 Mg C ha− 1. The most important variable found to drive mangrove organic carbon density was annual precipitation.

Hamilton and Friess, 2018

This study was a canopy-adjusted model compiled from a previously conducted machine learning soil carbon database of 932 sediment cores from 28 countries based on a database generated from a previous study32. It used a mangrove extent map of 83, 495 km2 and had a spatial resolution of 30 m2.

Sanderman et al., 2018

This study created a database of 1812 sediment cores from 47 countries. The model used a mangrove extent map of 137, 760 km2 for the year 2000 at 30 m resolution. It employed a machine learning random forest model with model performance assessed by a 5-fold leave location out cross validation procedure where 20% of complete locations were withheld in each model refitting. A variety of regional, climatic, hydrodynamic and forest characteristics variables were used to predict organic carbon. These included vegetation characteristics, digital elevation data, long term averaged (1990–2010) monthly sea surface temperature, tidal amplitude model (M2), averaged monthly total suspended matter, mangrove typology map and SoilGrids 250 m. Global stock estimates were 6.4 Pg C (in the top 1 m) with mean (1 ± standard deviation) 361 ± 136 Mg C ha− 1. The most important variable found to drive mangrove organic carbon density was SoilGrids 250 m.

Rovai et al., 2018

This study created a database by combining databases from two previous studies4,32, though, density values converted from loss on ignition were removed given that this relationship explains ~ 60% of the variability in organic carbon in mangrove soils. This removal of data resulted in a database of 552 sediment cores from 43 countries. The model used a mangrove extent map of 83, 495 km2 and had a spatial resolution of 0.25° decimal degree. Soil organic carbon was estimated using statistical modelling and inputting key climatic and hydrodynamic variables including precipitation, air temperature, tidal range and river discharge data. Global stock estimates were 2.26 Pg C (in the top 1 m). The most important variable found to drive mangrove organic carbon density was tidal amplitude.

Maxwell et al., 2023

This study created a database using a previously generated database5 with five additional studies. The model used a mangrove extent map 147, 760 km2 for the year 2020 and had a spatial resolution of 30 m. Estimates were generated by employing ensemble machine learning models with climatic, hydrodynamic and mangrove forest variables. Key variables included air and land surface temperature, precipitation, digital elevation model, global surface water and enhanced vegetation index. Model performance was tested using spatial validation by spatial blocking (R2 = 0.44). This resulted in a global stock value of 4.6 Pg C with mean values of 350 Mg C ha− 1 (in the top 1 m). The most important variable found to drive mangrove organic carbon density was soil depth.

Global mangrove soil carbon model preprocessing

Global mangrove soil carbon models were selected based on the availability of spatial explicit data (Table 2). We used the mangrove extent map for the year 200048 to define the global spatial extent of our analysis and used a spatial resolution of 0.25° decimal degree, resulting in 7,091 grid cells. We used the year 2000 global mangrove extent map48 as it was explicitly already built into three out of the five of the soil carbon models to define their spatial extent, and it has been shown to be a reliable representation of mangrove cover for the year 200048. Additionally, as our primary objective was to assess spatial and landscape-scale variability, the specific choice of extent map is less critical provided that all models are standardized to the same dataset. We used a 0.25° decimal degree grid as our unit of analysis as this was the coarsest grid of all input databases11, and all other databases could be spatially aggregated to this scale thus avoiding both spatial interpolation and extrapolation. We then projected, or reprojected, the data from the other datasets into EPSG:432611 and we converted all units into total Peta grams of carbon per grid cell (Pg C/0.25°) We then aggregated all other datasets to the 7091 grid cells to give comparable measures across the globe (See Supplementary Material 2). Each dataset now matches a standardized global grid’s extent, spatial reference, and units.

Global mangrove soil carbon model comparison

Across all five models, and all 7091 grid cells we calculated grid cell agreement for each model using simple descriptive statistics such as the mean, standard deviation, and range. We assessed the level of variability at the highly granular grid level and at the continental level to assess if spatial location was driving variability. We employed a one-way ANOVA and a post hoc analysis (Tukey’s HSD) to examine the significant differences between model variability at the continent level. All statistical analysis was performed in R (Version 2024.04.0 + 735.). Our new global mangrove soil carbon value was generated using the mean (± standard deviation) of the models in this study.

Field data comparison

Estimating 1-m carbon stocks using field measurements of mangrove soil carbon

We utilized mangrove soil carbon data from the Coastal Carbon Library and Atlas (v.1.5.0), an open-source, centralized and standardised soil carbon database designed to enable estimates of carbon stocks and fluxes in blue carbon ecosystems47. The database consisted of approximately 250 published and unpublished studies from 49 countries across 5620 individual mangrove sediment cores. We downloaded all available mangrove sediment cores from the Coastal Carbon Atlas on 2025-05-22 to calculate 1 m soil carbon stocks74.

To estimate 1-m stocks, we only used soil cores with max depths > 50 cm, resulting in 4036 cores and 23,468 depth intervals across all cores. After removing all soil cores that did not have an estimate of dry bulk density (g cm− 3), which is needed to convert soil carbon to carbon density, we were left with 3503 soil cores. For core intervals that did not contain a direct measurement of soil carbon, we estimated it based on the linear relationship between organic matter (OM) and soil carbon for all depth intervals that contained both variables (n = 2001; soil carbon = 0.476*OM; p = 2.2 × 10− 16, R2 = 0.98) (Fig. 6). Our slope coefficient of 0.476 indicates that soil carbon is ~ 50% of OM. It is slightly higher than a previously calculated OM to soil carbon calculation (soil carbon = 0.21*OM1.21)75; but is close to the suggested OM to soil carbon conversion factor used for standard blue carbon measurements (soil carbon = 0.415*OM + 2.89)72,76. We used the regression equation between OM and soil carbon to estimate soil carbon for all depth intervals that estimated OM but not soil carbon. We then converted soil carbon to carbon density for each depth interval by multiplying carbon density by dry bulk density, then multiplied carbon density by the length of the depth interval to get the carbon stock of each depth interval. Depth intervals were then summed for each core and converted to Mg C ha− 1 to obtain the total 1-m soil carbon stock for each core.

Field measurements versus carbon model comparison

After generating 1 m soil carbon stock estimates for each core, we calculated average carbon stocks for each grid cell containing sediment cores. We then multiplied these averages by the mangrove extent map48 to create a field-based model. To compare mean soil organic carbon between this field-based model and the five key global soil carbon models we employed a non-paired t-test. We then compared mean soil organic carbon individually across all five models and the field-based model using a one-way ANOVA. To examine the significant differences between each model, we employed a post hoc analysis (Tukey’s HSD). All statistical analysis was performed in R (Version 2024.04.0 + 735.).

Preprocessing

Four global datasets were chosen to capture the primary factors influencing mangrove soil carbon stocks (Table 3). Datasets included in this study have spatial resolution ranging from 10 m to ~ 7 km and hence can all be nested into the 0.25° decimal degree grid. The four datasets selected are geomorphology, mangrove species, precipitation, and tidal amplitude.

Firstly, given the inherent effect of geomorphology on above ground biomass73 and soil carbon stocks11,16, we included a global biophysical typology of mangroves43. In this typology, mangroves were classified into their different sedimentary (i.e. terrigenous or carbonate) and geomorphological (i.e. deltaic, estuarine, lagoonal or open coast) settings. Geomorphological settings were aggregated by spatially assigning each setting to its corresponding grid cell. Where multiple settings occurred within a single cell, the most dominant setting was selected.

Secondly, we used climatologies at high resolution for the earths land surface areas (CHELSA) annual global precipitation dataset44 at 1 km resolution to represent the influence of precipitation on soil carbon17 including annual precipitation amount (Bio 12). Annual precipitation values were aggregated by calculating the mean value within each grid cell.

Thirdly, due to the impact of tides on sediment deposition, decomposition rates, and nutrient levels, which subsequently affects soil carbon stocks11, we used a global hydrodynamic tidal model (FES2022) at ~ 7 km resolution45. This model assimilates altimetry data to represent tidal range at each location. We selected the principal lunar semi-diurnal (M2) tidal component as it is typically the most dominant tidal constituent5,43,77. Tidal amplitude values were aggregated by calculating the mean value within each grid cell.

Finally, as species diversity has been shown to influence soil carbon stocks70,78, we used a species diversity layer created by the International Union Conservation of Nature46. Each of the analysis units was attributed with the number of species present. We used a species density map79, to create species density categories. Low density is defined as 14 or fewer species present, capturing the entire Atlantic Ocean, the Caribbean Sea, and the entire Indian Ocean. Medium density is defined as 15 to 26 species present, capturing the Eastern portion of the Indian subcontinent, much of Australia and Oceania, as well as the South China Sea. High density is defined as greater than 26 species present, capturing the mangrove hotspot in and around the Indo-West Pacific.

Drivers of mangrove soil carbon comparison

Once we accounted for regional variation, we assessed driver variation using the global datasets from Table 3. To assess the level of model variability and mangrove sedimentary setting we employed a non-paired t-test and to understand the level of variability across mangrove geomorphic setting we employed a one-way ANOVA. We then employed a post hoc analysis (Tukey’s HSD) to explore significant differences between geomorphic setting. To visualise trends in model variability and annual precipitation amount, species diversity and tidal amplitude we used hexagonal binning plots. All statistical analysis was performed in R (Version 2024.04.0 + 735.).
