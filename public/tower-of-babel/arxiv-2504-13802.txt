Contractivity of Wasserstein distance and other functionals for the Landau equation
F.-U. Caja-Lopez, M. G. Delgadino, M.-P. Gualdani, M. Taskovic

Abstract
. Following the breakthrough work of Guillen and Silvestre [ 1 ] , that shows that the Fisher information is monotonically decreasing for solutions to the homogeneous Landau equation, we study, for the same equation, the monotonicity properties of other physically relevant functionals. In the case of Maxwellian molecules, we show that the relative L 2 L^{2} norm with respect to the equilibrium decays exponentially fast in time and is monotonically decreasing after some time. Moreover, still for the Maxwellian case, we provide a novel and short quantitative proof of time monotonicity of the entropic Wasserstein metric. For soft potentials, we show that the Wasserstein metric is contractive, conditional to L 1 ​ ( 0 , T , L p ​ ( ℝ 3 ) ) L^{1}(0,T,L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})) bound for the solution. This result provides an alternative proof of the Fournier and Fournier-Guerin uniqueness theorem in [ 2 ] [ 3 ] .



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the space-homogeneous Landau equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f=\textnormal{div}_{v}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\alpha(|v-w|)|v-w|^{2}K(v-w)\left(f(w)\nabla_{v}f(v)-f(v)\nabla_{w}f(w)\right)\,dw,

with
K(z):=\textnormal{Id}-\frac{z\otimes z}{|z|^{2}}
being the orthogonal projection to
\langle z\rangle^{\perp}
and
\alpha:\mathbb{R}_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
The most relevant physical case is
d=3
and
\alpha(r)=r^{-3}
, which corresponds to Coulomb interactions of electrons, and arises in mathematical modeling of electrons in plasma. The Landau equation is one of the most used mathematical models in plasma physics. From a mathematical point of view, the presence of nonlinear and non-local terms makes the analysis quite challenging. After decades of being an open problem, well posedness of (
1.1
) with smooth data has been recently settled in the breakthrough work of Guillen-Silvestre
[
1
]
. The proof relies on a novel a-priori estimate which shows that the Fisher information is non-increasing in time. Subsequently, global well-posedness for less regular initial data was shown in
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. The surprising monotonicity of the Fisher information hints at the possibility of additional functionals that either decay monotonically or stay bounded.

In a preliminary attempt to answer this question, we focus here on three specific functionals. The first one is the relative
L^{2}
distance between any solution to (
1.1
) and the Maxwellian equilibrium. The other two are the classical
2
-Wasserstein distance and the entropic Wasserstein distance between two solutions to (
1.1
). We summarize our main results in three theorems.

The first theorem shows that in the case of Maxwell molecules (
\alpha\equiv 1
), the relative
L^{2}
distance decays exponentially fast in time. Moreover, this distance is monotonically decreasing after a short initial time. This transient period
measures
how far the initial data is from a radially symmetric configuration. If the initial data is
almost
radially symmetric, the relative
L^{2}
norm is monotone decreasing for all times.

Theorem 1
.

Let
f
be a solution to (
1.1
) with
\alpha\equiv 1
and initial data
f_{in}
such that

\int f_{in}(1,v,|v|^{2})\;dv=(1,0,d)
. Let
m
be the corresponding unique equilibrium solution,
m(v):=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|{v}|^{2}}
. Then the relative
L^{2}
-norm

E(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{f}{m}-1\right)^{2}m\;dv

decays exponentially fast in time,

E(t)\leq c_{0}e^{-2(d-1)t}E(0),

where
c_{0}
depends only on the dimension
d
. Moreover,
E(t)
is monotonically decreasing for any
t\geq t_{0}
with
t_{0}=\max\left\{\frac{1}{4d}\log\left(\frac{2\sup_{i}(\lambda_{i}^{in}-1)}{d-1}\right),0\right\}
, where
\lambda_{i}^{in}
are the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbb{P}^{in}_{ij}=\int v_{i}v_{j}f_{in}\,dv
.

Remark.

If
f_{in}
satisfies
\lambda_{i}^{in}\leq\frac{d+1}{2}
for all
i=1,...,d
, then
t_{0}=0
, namely the relative
L^{2}
norm decreases from the initial time. In particular this applies to radially symmetric initial data, which have
{\lambda_{i}^{in}=1}
.

Our second result shows that, still for the Maxwell molecules case, the Landau equation is contractive with respect to the entropic
2
-Wasserstein distance. Before we define the distance, let’s recall the definition of Boltzmann entropy
\mathcal{H}(f)
and relative entropy
\mathcal{H}(f|g)
between two probability measures
f
and
g
as

\mathcal{H}(f):=\int f\log f\;dx,\qquad\mathcal{H}(f|g):=\int\left(\frac{f}{g}\log\frac{f}{g}-\frac{f}{g}+1\right)g\;dx.

The entropic
2
-Wasserstein distance is defined as

(1.2)

\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\Pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\left\{\iint|v-w|^{2}\,d\Pi(v,w)+\varepsilon\mathcal{H}(\Pi|\mu\otimes\nu)\right\},

where
\mu,\nu
are two probability measures over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
stands for the set of transport plans, which are probability measures over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
, see
[
8
]
for further details. Before we state our second main theorem, we recall a useful expression introduced in
[
1
]
for the matrix
|v-w|^{2}K(v-w)
:

|v-w|^{2}K(v-w)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}b_{k}(v-w)\otimes b_{k}(v-w),

with

b_{1}(v-w):=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
w_{3}-v_{3}\\
v_{2}-w_{2}\end{pmatrix},\;b_{2}(v-w):=\begin{pmatrix}v_{3}-w_{3}\\
0\\
w_{1}-v_{1}\end{pmatrix},\;b_{3}(v-w):=\begin{pmatrix}w_{2}-v_{2}\\
v_{1}-w_{1}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

In the following of the manuscript we denote by
\tilde{b}_{k}
the vector

\tilde{b}_{k}:=\begin{pmatrix}b_{k}\\
-b_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

Our second main result is summarized in the following theorem:

Theorem 2
.

Let
f
,
g
be solutions to (
1.1
) for
\alpha\equiv 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then
\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(f,g)
is non-increasing for any
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Moreover, the dissipation can be expressed as

(1.3)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(f,g)=\varepsilon\underbrace{\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(f)}_{\leq 0}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{1}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left|\nabla(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla U)\right|^{2}P\,dv\,dw\,ds\leq 0,

where
P:[0,1]\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
is the Wasserstein geodesic between
F(v,w)=f(v)f(w)
and
G(v,w)=g(v)g(w)
with vector field given by
{\nabla U:[0,1]\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},dP)^{\otimes 6}}
.

The Geodesic
P
solves a continuity equation with
\nabla U
as the driving vector field, and
U
solves a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation. See Section
5
for the precise statements and explicit expression of
U
.

We provide two different proofs of the monotonicity of
\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}
. The first one closely follows the symmetrization argument introduced in
[
1
]
(see Section
4
). The second proof relies on the duality between entropic optimal transport and Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equations; thanks to the duality argument, we are able to find the explicit expression of the dissipation (
1.3
). This approach is developed in Section
5

We remark that the contractivity of the 2-Wassertein distance
\mathcal{T}
for Maxwellian molecules was already known
[
9
,
10
]
. Even more classically, we mention the work of Tanaka
[
11
]
, which first showed the monotonicity of the 2-Wasserstein metric for the Boltzmann equation. We also mention
[
12
]
which produces a tailored 2-Wasserstein like metric on which the Landau equation arises as the gradient flow of the entropy; understanding if the Landau equation is contractive in this natural metric is an interesting open problem. Here we provide a shorter and more intuitive proof of the monotonicity of
\mathcal{T}
. The expression of the dissipation on the right hand side of (
1.3
) is at the authors’ best knowledge new.

The last main result concerns soft potentials and the contractivity of the
2
-Wasserstein
\mathcal{T}(f_{t},g_{t})
distance. Recall that
\mathcal{T}(f_{t},g_{t})
is defined in (
1.2
) with
\varepsilon=0
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
f_{t}
,
g_{t}
be two smooth solutions to (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}
.

(i)
Let
-3<\gamma<0
. Assume that
f_{t},g_{t}\in L_{t}^{1}L_{v}^{p}
for
p>\frac{3}{3+\gamma}
.

There exist constants
C_{\gamma,p}>0
and
0<\beta_{\gamma,p}<1
such that

(1.4)

\mathcal{T}(f_{t},g_{t})\leq\mathcal{T}(f_{in},g_{in})\exp\left(C_{\gamma,p}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left\|f_{s}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\beta_{\gamma,p}}+\left\|g_{s}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\beta_{\gamma,p}}\;ds\right)\right).

(i)
Let
\gamma=-3
. Assume
f_{t},\;g_{t}\in L_{t}^{1}L_{v}^{\infty}
, and initial data such that
\mathcal{T}(f_{in},g_{in})<e^{-4}
. Then there exist constants
C>0
and
\tau_{0}>0
such that for
t<\tau_{0}
:

(1.5)

\mathcal{T}(f_{t},g_{t})\leq\mathcal{T}(f_{in},g_{in})^{e^{-\beta(t)}},\quad\beta(t):=C\int_{0}^{t}\max\big(\left\|f_{s}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\left\|g_{s}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},1\big)\,ds.

The contractive estimates for soft potentials of Theorem
3
match the estimates of Fournier and Guerin
[
2
]
and Fournier
[
3
]
. Indeed, our result shows that uniqueness of solution for (
1.1
) for Coulomb potential holds provided the solution belongs to
L^{1}(0,\tau,L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
for small
\tau>0
. Our proof does not rely on stochastic methods, but solely uses the symmetrization properties of the distance
\mathcal{T}
and of the Landau operator.

It turns out that Theorem
3
holds for a wider range of potentials. More precisely, it holds for any general potential
\alpha(r)
that satisfies

\alpha^{\prime}(r)^{2}\leq Kr^{\gamma-2}\alpha(r),

for some positive constant
K
and some
\gamma\in[-3,0]
.

1
. Theorem
2

2.
The collision operator for Maxwellian molecules

The proof of Theorem
1
(
\alpha\equiv 1
) starts with the Landau equation (
1.1
) rewritten in the following form:

(2.1)

\partial_{t}f=\textnormal{div}\left(\left[\left|v\right|^{2}K(v)+d\,\textnormal{Id}-\mathbb{P}(t)\right]\nabla f+(d-1)vf\right),

where
\mathbb{P}(t)
is the diagonal matrix formed by

(2.2)

\lambda_{i}(t)=\int v_{i}^{2}f(v)\,dv,\qquad\lambda_{i}(t)=1+e^{-4dt}(\lambda_{i}(0)-1).

We briefly show how to derive the expression (
2.1
) assuming

\int f_{\textnormal{in}}(1,v,|v|^{2})\,dv=\int f(1,v,|v|^{2})\,dv=(1,0,d).

This has already been done in
[
14
]
, but we present a slightly modified proof for completeness, as these computations will be used in the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
f\geq 0
such that
\int f(1,v,|v|^{2})\,dv=(1,0,d)
. The matrix

A[f](v,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{K(v-w)}{|{v-w}|^{2}}f(w,t)\,dw

in the Maxwell molecules case
\alpha\equiv 1
can be rewritten as

A[f](v,t)=\left|v\right|^{2}K(v)+d\,\textnormal{Id}-\mathbb{P}(t)

where
\mathbb{P}(t)
is the matrix with entries
P_{ij}(t):=\int w_{i}w_{j}f(w,t)\,dw
.

Moreover, the Landau equation (
1.1
) reduces to

(2.3)

\partial_{t}f=\textnormal{div}\left(\left[\left|v\right|^{2}K(v)+d\,\textnormal{Id}-\mathbb{P}(t)\right]\nabla f+(d-1)vf\right).

Proof.

Direct computations show that

A[f]=\textnormal{Id}\int\left|v-w\right|^{2}f(w)\,dw-\int(v-w)\otimes(v-w)f(w)\,dw.

For the first term we have

\int\left|v-w\right|^{2}f(w)\,dw=\Bigg[\left|v\right|^{2}\int f(w)\,dw-2v\cdot\int wf(w)\,dw+\int\left|w\right|^{2}f(w)\,dw\Bigg]=\left(\left|v\right|^{2}+d\right).

For the second:

\displaystyle\int(v-w)\otimes(v-w)f(w)\,dw

\displaystyle=v\otimes v\int f(w)\,dw-2v\otimes\int f(w)w\,dw+\int\left(w\otimes w\right)f(w)\,dw

\displaystyle=(v\otimes v)+\int\left(w\otimes w\right)f(w)\,dw=(v\otimes v)+\mathbb{P}(t).

Hence we have

A[f]=\left|v\right|^{2}\Bigg(\textnormal{Id}-\frac{v\otimes v}{\left|v\right|^{2}}\Bigg)+d\,\textnormal{Id}-\mathbb{P}(t)=\left|v\right|^{2}K(v)+d\,\textnormal{Id}-\mathbb{P}(t).

This also provides a very simple expression for

\textnormal{div}\left(A[f]\right)=\textnormal{div}\left(\left|v\right|^{2}\textnormal{Id}-v\otimes v\right)=\left[2v-(d+1)v\right]=-(d-1)v.

Since (
1.1
) can be expressed in terms of
A[f]
as

\partial_{t}f=\textnormal{div}\left(A[f]\nabla f-f\,\textnormal{div}A[f]\right),

substituting
A[f]
and
\textnormal{div}\left(A[f]\right)
above we obtain (
2.3
).
∎

Remark.
If the solution is rotationally symmetric
f(t,v)=f(t,\left|v\right|^{2})
, equation (
2.3
) reduces to the classical Fokker Planck equation. Let
F
be an antiderivative of
f
, for each
t
and
i\neq j
we have

P_{ij}(t)=\int w_{i}w_{j}f(\left|w\right|^{2})\,dw=\int w_{i}\partial_{w_{j}}\left(\frac{F(\left|w\right|^{2})}{2}\right)\,dw=0.

For the case
i=j
, due to radial symmetry, the area under
w_{i}^{2}f(\left|w\right|^{2})
is the same for every
i
, so

P_{ii}(t)=\int w_{i}^{2}f(\left|w\right|^{2})\,dw=\frac{1}{d}\int\left|w\right|^{2}f(\left|w\right|^{2})\,dw=1.

This shows that
\mathbb{P}(t)=\textnormal{Id}
. Moreover, we have

A[f]\nabla f(v)=\left(\left|v\right|^{2}K(v)+(d-1)\textnormal{Id}\right)2vf^{\prime}(\left|v\right|^{2})=(d-1)2vf^{\prime}(\left|v\right|^{2})=(d-1)\nabla f(v)

and the Landau equation for Maxwell molecules reduces to

\partial_{t}f=(d-1)\textnormal{div}\left(\nabla f(v)+vf(v)\right).

Next, we show how
A[f]
evolves in time:

Proposition 2
.

Let
f
be a solution to (
2.3
) with
\int f(1,v,|v|^{2})\,dv=(1,0,d)
. The matrix
\mathbb{P}
with entries
P_{ij}(t)=\int w_{i}w_{j}f(w)\,dw
satisfies the ode

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\prime}(t)=-4d(\mathbb{P}(t)-\textnormal{Id}),

with explicit solution

\mathbb{P}(t)=\textnormal{Id}+e^{-4dt}(\mathbb{P}_{in}-\textnormal{Id}).

Proof.

Using the equation for
f
, and
\textnormal{div}\left(A[f]\right)=-(d-1)v
we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int w_{i}w_{j}f(w)\,dw

\displaystyle=\int w_{i}w_{j}\textnormal{div}_{v}\bigg(A[f]\nabla f\,dw-\textnormal{div}_{v}\left(A[f]\right)f\bigg)\,dw

\displaystyle=-\int\left\langle\nabla(w_{i}w_{j}),A[f]\nabla f\right\rangle\,dw+\int\left\langle\nabla(w_{i}w_{j}),\textnormal{div}\left(A[f]\right)\right\rangle f\,dw

\displaystyle=\int\textnormal{div}\left(A[f]\nabla(w_{i}w_{j})\right)f\,dw-(d-1)\int\left\langle\nabla(w_{i}w_{j}),w\right\rangle f\,dw.

The first term simplifies as

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}\left(A[f]\nabla(w_{i}w_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{div}\left(A[f]\right)\cdot\nabla(w_{i}w_{j})+\textnormal{tr}\left(A[f]D^{2}(w_{i}w_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=-(d-1)w\cdot\nabla(w_{i}w_{j})+\textnormal{tr}\left(A[f]D^{2}(w_{i}w_{j})\right).

Note that
D^{2}(w_{i}w_{j})=\Delta_{ij}+\Delta_{ji}
, where
\Delta_{ij}
is the matrix with all zeroes except a one at position
(i,j)
. In general, if
A
is a matrix then
A\Delta_{ij}
has all of its columns equal to
0
except the
j
-th, where you find the
i
-th column of
A
. Therefore, if the matrix
A
is symmetric, we have
\textnormal{tr}\left(AD^{2}(w_{i}w_{j})\right)=2A_{ij}
, which shows:

\textnormal{div}\left(A[f]\nabla(w_{i}w_{j})\right)=-(d-1)w\cdot\left(\vec{e}_{i}w_{j}+\vec{e}_{j}w_{i}\right)+2A_{ij}[f]=-2(d-1)w_{i}w_{j}+2A_{ij}[f].

Similarly, we have

\int\left\langle\nabla(w_{i}w_{j}),w\right\rangle f\,dw=2\int w_{i}w_{j}f(w)\,dw,

which shows

(2.5)

\frac{d}{dt}\int w_{i}w_{j}f(w)\,dw=2\int A_{ij}[f]f(w)\,dw-4(d-1)\int w_{i}w_{j}f(w)\,dw.

Now we use the following expression for
A[f]

\displaystyle A[f]

\displaystyle=\left|v\right|^{2}K(v)+d\,\textnormal{Id}-\mathbb{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\left|v\right|^{2}K(v)+\int|w|^{2}\left(\textnormal{Id}-\frac{w\otimes w}{|w|^{2}}\right)f(w)\,dw,

which we plug into (
2.5
), yielding

\displaystyle 2\int A_{ij}[f](w)f(w)\,dw

\displaystyle=2\int\Bigg(\left|w\right|^{2}K(w)+\int\left|\xi\right|^{2}K(\xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi\Bigg)f(w)\,dw

\displaystyle=4d\,\textnormal{Id}-4\mathbb{P}(t),

which finishes the proof.
∎

Remark.
The previous formula allows one to simplify the expression of
\mathbb{P}(t)
. Let
f_{in}
be a general initial data with second moment matrix
\mathbb{P}_{in}
. Let
R
be the rotation matrix such that
R\,\mathbb{P}_{in}R^{t}=\textnormal{diag}\left(\lambda_{1}^{\textnormal{in}},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{\textnormal{in}}\right)
. Then, the rotated initial data
\tilde{f}_{in}(v):=f_{in}\left(R^{-1}v\right)
has diagonal second moment matrix:

\mathbb{P}_{in}=\int(v\otimes v)f_{in}\left(R^{-1}v\right)\,dv=\int(Rv)\otimes(Rv)f_{in}(v)\,dv=R\,\mathbb{P}(0)R^{t}=\textnormal{diag}\left(\lambda_{1}^{\textnormal{in}},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{\textnormal{in}}\right).

Without loss of generality, in Section
3
we will consider initial data such that
\int w_{i}w_{j}f_{in}\;dw=0
if
i\neq j
. Then
\mathbb{P}_{in}
is a diagonal matrix and, in view of (
2.4
),
\mathbb{P}(t)
is also diagonal with elements
\lambda_{i}(t)=1+e^{-4dt}\left(\lambda_{i}^{\textnormal{in}}-1\right)
. Because of conservation of the second moment we have that for all times
t\geq 0
:

\textnormal{tr}(\mathbb{P}(t))=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(t)=d.

3.
Exponential decay of relative
L^{2}
norm

We start the proof Theorem
1
with the following computation:

Lemma 1
.

Let
f
be a solution of (
2.3
) with initial data such that
\mathbb{P}_{in}
is diagonal. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int\left(\frac{f}{m}-1\right)^{2}m\,dv\leq

\displaystyle-(d-1)\int\left|\nabla\left(\frac{f}{m}\right)\right|^{2}m\,dv

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}(\lambda_{i}(t)-1)\int\left(\left[\partial_{v_{i}}\left(\frac{f}{m}\right)\right]^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{f^{2}}{m^{2}}v_{i}^{2}\right)m\,dv,

where
\lambda_{i}(t)=\int w_{i}^{2}f(w)\,dw=1+e^{-4dt}\left(\lambda_{i}^{\textnormal{in}}-1\right)
.

Proof.

We rewrite (
2.3
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f=\textnormal{div}\left(|v|^{2}K(v)\nabla f\right)+d\,\textnormal{div}\left(m\nabla\left(\frac{f}{m}\right)\right)-\textnormal{div}\left(fv+\mathbb{P}\nabla f\right).

Because of conservation of mass,
\int f^{2}/m\,dv
and
\int\left(f/m-1\right)^{2}m\,dv
only differ by a constant, so

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int\left(\frac{f}{m}-1\right)^{2}m\,dv

\displaystyle=\int\frac{f}{m}\partial_{t}f\,dv

\displaystyle=-\int\left\langle K(v)\left|v\right|^{2}\nabla f(v),\nabla\left(f/m\right)\,dv\right\rangle

\displaystyle-d\int\left|\nabla\left(f/m\right)\right|^{2}m\,dv+\int\left\langle fv+\mathbb{P}\nabla f,\nabla(f/m)\right\rangle\,dv.

Since
K(v)\nabla m=0
, the first term in the last expression is negative:

\left\langle K(v)\left|v\right|^{2}\nabla f(v),\nabla\left(f/m\right)\right\rangle=\left\langle\left|v\right|^{2}K(v)\nabla(f/m),\nabla\left(f/m\right)\right\rangle m\geq 0.

Therefore, we have

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int\left(\frac{f}{m}-1\right)^{2}m\,dv\leq-d\int\left|\nabla\left(f/m\right)\right|^{2}m\,dv+I_{1}+I_{2},

where
I_{1}:=\int fv\cdot\nabla(f/m)\,dv
and
I_{2}:=\int\left\langle\mathbb{P}\nabla f,\nabla(f/m)\right\rangle\,dv
. Expanding
\nabla(f/m)
in
I_{1}
yields

\displaystyle I_{1}=\int fv\cdot\frac{\nabla f+fv}{m}\,dv

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\left|v\right|^{2}}{m}f^{2}\,dv+\int\frac{v}{m}\cdot\overbrace{f\nabla f}^{\frac{1}{2}\nabla(f^{2})}\,dv

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\left|v\right|^{2}}{m}f^{2}\,dv-\frac{1}{2}\int f^{2}\left(\frac{d+\left|v\right|^{2}}{m}\right)\,dv

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{f^{2}}{m}\left(\left|v\right|^{2}-d\right)\,dv.

The identity
\nabla f=m\nabla(f/m)-fv
yields

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int\left\langle\mathbb{P}\left(m\nabla(f/m)-fv\right),\nabla(f/m)\right\rangle\,dv

\displaystyle=\int\left\langle\mathbb{P}\nabla(f/m),\nabla(f/m)\right\rangle m\,dv-\int\left\langle\mathbb{P}v,f\nabla(f/m)\right\rangle\,dv.

We rewrite the last term as

\displaystyle-\int\left\langle\mathbb{P}v,f\nabla(f/m)\right\rangle\,dv

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\mathbb{P}v\cdot\nabla\left(f^{2}/m^{2}\right)\,m\,dv

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{f^{2}}{m^{2}}\left(m\,\textnormal{div}\left(\mathbb{P}v\right)-\mathbb{P}v\cdot vm\right)\,dv

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2}\int\frac{f^{2}}{m}\,dv-\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{f^{2}}{m}\left\langle\mathbb{P}v,v\right\rangle\,dv,

since
\textnormal{tr}(\mathbb{P}(t))=d
. This yields

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{f^{2}}{m}\left(\left|v\right|^{2}-d\right)\,dv+\int\left\langle\mathbb{P}\nabla(f/m),\nabla(f/m)\right\rangle m\,dv

\displaystyle+\frac{d}{2}\int\frac{f^{2}}{m}\,dv-\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{f^{2}}{m}\left\langle\mathbb{P}v,v\right\rangle\,dv

In the next result we show a gaussian weighted Poincare inequality, which is a (slightly) sharper version of the one found in

Lemma 2
.

Let
m(x)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}
be the Gaussian weight on

. Then for any smooth function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x_{i}|^{2}\varphi^{2}(x)m(x)\,dx\leq{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\partial_{x_{i}}(x)\varphi|^{2}m(x)\,dx+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi^{2}(x)m(x)\,dx,

Moreover, if
\varphi
has zero average
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)m(x)\,dx=0
then we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi^{2}(x)m(x)\,dx\leq\int|\nabla\varphi(x)|^{2}m(x)\,dx.

Proof.

We consider the basis of Hermite polynomials
\{H^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
defined by

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{|\alpha|}}{\sqrt{\alpha!}}e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}\partial_{\alpha}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}(x)=\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d}),

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
is a multi-index and
\alpha!=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}!
.
The Hermite polynomials form a complete orthonormal basis with respect to the Gaussian measure
m(x)
. Any smooth function
\varphi
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}H_{\alpha}(x),

where
a_{\alpha}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)H_{\alpha}(x)m(x)\,dx
are the coefficients of the expansion. We start with the first inequality. We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x_{1}|^{2}\varphi^{2}(x)m(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}x_{1}H_{\alpha}\right|^{2}m(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}x_{1}\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d})\right|^{2}m(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}(\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{1}+1}(x_{1})+\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\prime}(x_{1}))\cdots\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d})\right|^{2}m(x)\,dx,

where we have used the identity
x\textnormal{He}_{n}=\textnormal{He}_{n+1}+\textnormal{He}_{n}^{\prime}
. We thus have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x_{1}|^{2}\varphi^{2}(x)m(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{1}+1}(x_{1})\cdots\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d})\right|^{2}m(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\prime}(x_{1})\cdots\textnormal{He}_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{d})\right|^{2}m(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi(x)|^{2}m(x)\,dx+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\partial_{x_{1}}\varphi(x)|^{2}m(x)\,dx.

The Poincare inequality follows in a similar manner, using the identity
\textnormal{He}_{n}^{\prime}=n\textnormal{He}_{n-1}
.
∎

\displaystyle L_{b}L_{u}u

\displaystyle=e\cdot\nabla\left(b\cdot\nabla\left(b\cdot\nabla\left(e\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)\right)-b\cdot\nabla\left([e,b]\cdot\nabla(e\cdot\nabla u)\right)-[e,b]\cdot\nabla\left(b\cdot\nabla\left(e\cdot\nabla u\right)\right).

From here we deduce the expression

\displaystyle\left[L_{e},L_{b}\right]u

\displaystyle=e\cdot\nabla\left(b\cdot\nabla\left([e,b]\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)+[e,b]\cdot\nabla\left(b\cdot\nabla\left(e\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)

\displaystyle+b\cdot\nabla\left([e,b]\cdot\nabla(e\cdot\nabla u)\right)+e\cdot\nabla\left([e,b]\cdot\nabla\left(b\cdot\nabla u\right)\right).

To notice cancellations we rewrite the expressions to be symmetric in
e
and
[e,b]
. Commuting:

\displaystyle\left[L_{e},L_{b}\right]u

\displaystyle=e\cdot\nabla\left(b\cdot\nabla\left([e,b]\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)+[e,b]\cdot\nabla\left(b\cdot\nabla\left(e\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)

\displaystyle+b\cdot\nabla\left([e,b]\cdot\nabla(e\cdot\nabla u)\right)+b\cdot\nabla\left(e\cdot\nabla\left([e,b]\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)

\displaystyle+e\cdot\nabla\left(\left[[e,b],b\right]\cdot\nabla u\right)+\left[e,b\right]\cdot\nabla\left([e,b]\cdot\nabla u\right).

Plugging this into (
5.11
,
5.12
) and using that
[\tilde{e}_{i},\tilde{b}_{k}]=0
yields

\displaystyle\Delta Qu-Q\Delta u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,k}\left[L_{\hat{e}_{i}},L_{\tilde{b}_{k}}\right]u

(5.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,k}\Big(\hat{e}_{i}\cdot\nabla\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\left([\hat{e}_{i},\tilde{b}_{k}]\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)+[\hat{e}_{i},\tilde{b}_{k}]\cdot\nabla\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\left(\hat{e}_{i}\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)\Big)

(5.14)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,k}\Big(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\left([\hat{e}_{i},\tilde{b}_{k}]\cdot\nabla(\hat{e}_{i}\cdot\nabla u)\right)+\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\left(\hat{e}_{i}\cdot\nabla\left([\hat{e}_{i},\tilde{b}_{k}]\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)\Big)

(5.15)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,k}\Big(\hat{e}_{i}\cdot\nabla\left(\left[[\hat{e}_{i},\tilde{b}_{k}],\tilde{b}_{k}\right]\cdot\nabla u\right)+[\hat{e}_{i},\tilde{b}_{k}]\cdot\nabla\left([\hat{e}_{i},\tilde{b}_{k}]\cdot\nabla u\right)\Big).

Expressions (
5.13
) and (
5.14
) vanish after summing in
i
. The third expression (
5.15
) vanishes in a similar manner. For example, observe that for
k=1
we have

\left[[\hat{e}_{2},\tilde{b}_{1}],\tilde{b}_{1}\right]=2[\hat{e}_{2},\tilde{b}_{1}]=-4\hat{e}_{2},\quad\left[[\hat{e}_{3},\tilde{b}_{1}],\tilde{b}_{1}\right]=-2[\hat{e}_{3},\tilde{b}_{1}]=-4\hat{e}_{3},

which shows that with
k=1
fixed

(\ref{eq:vanish_EOT3})=-2\hat{e}_{2}\cdot\nabla\left(\hat{e}_{2}\cdot\nabla u\right)+2\hat{e}_{2}\cdot\left(\hat{e}_{2}\cdot\nabla u\right)-2\hat{e}_{3}\cdot\nabla\left(\hat{e}_{3}\cdot\nabla u\right)+2\hat{e}_{3}\cdot\left(\hat{e}_{3}\cdot\nabla u\right)=0.

A similar cancellation happens for other values of
k
, finishing the proof of
Step 1
.

Step 2.
Now we proceed with the proof of expression (
1.3
) for the dissipation of entropic optimal transport. We use the following dual formulation

\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(f,g)=\varepsilon\mathcal{H}(f)+\sup\left\{\int u_{1}g-\int u_{0}f:\partial_{s}u+\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Delta u=0\right\},

where
\mathcal{H}
denotes the usual entropy. Moreover, the optimal
u
provides an interpolation between
f
and
g
through the equation

(5.16)

\partial\rho_{s}+\textnormal{div}\left(\rho\nabla u\right)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\rho,\quad\rho_{0}=f,\quad\rho_{1}=g.

The interpolant
\rho
can be written explicitly in terms of the Schrödinger potentials and the heat kernel, although we make no use of this relation here. See
[
8
]
for more details. Now, if
\partial_{t}f=q(f)
,
\partial_{t}g=q(g)
are solutions of the Landau equation for
\alpha\equiv 1
then, thanks to Proposition
4
, we have

\displaystyle 2\frac{d}{dt}\left[\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(f,g)\right]

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(F,G),\left(Q(F),Q(G)\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(F)+\iint u_{1}Q(G)-\iint u_{0}Q(F)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(F)+\iint Q(u_{1})G-\iint Q(u_{0})F.

Consider the interpolation between
F
and
G
of the form (
5.16
) and denote it
\rho(v,w,s)
,
0\leq s\leq 1
. Then we have

\displaystyle 2\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(f,g)=-\varepsilon D(F)+\iint Q(u_{1})G-\iint Q(u_{0})F

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(F)+\int_{0}^{1}\iint Q(\partial_{s}u)\rho+Q(u)\partial_{s}\rho\,ds

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(F)+\int_{0}^{1}\iint\left[\nabla Q(u)\cdot\nabla u-\frac{1}{2}Q(\left|\nabla u\right|^{2})\right]\rho\,ds+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\iint\left[\Delta Qu-Q\Delta u\right]\rho\,ds

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(F)-\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{1}\iint\left|\nabla\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla u\right)\right|^{2}\rho\,ds,

yielding the expression for the dissipation (
1.3
) and finishing the proof of Theorem
2
.

∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
3

We now prove the the contractive estimates for soft potentials using the previously derived expression of
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f,g)
in Lemma
4
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

The starting point is the expression for the dissipation of
\mathcal{T}
found in Lemma
4
.
We first consider the second term and apply Young’s inequality. We get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\iint\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{r}\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)\left(\hat{n}\cdot\nabla u\right)\rho\,dv\,dw\leq

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{4}\iint\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)^{2}\frac{\alpha}{r^{2}}\rho\,dv\,dw

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\iint\frac{(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}{\alpha}\left|\hat{n}\cdot\nabla u\right|^{2}\rho\,dv\,dw.

To bound the first term, we make use of the following identity (see
[
1
]
) for functions
{G:\mathbb{R}^{6}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}}
:

\left|\nabla G\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left(\tilde{e}_{i}\cdot\nabla G\right)^{2}+\frac{\left(\hat{n}\cdot\nabla G\right)^{2}}{2r^{2}}+\sum_{k}\frac{\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla G\right)^{2}}{2r^{2}}.

In particular, this shows that

\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\iint\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla u\right)\right)^{2}\frac{\alpha}{r^{2}}\rho\,dv\,dw\,ds\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\iint\left|\nabla\left(\tilde{b}_{k}\cdot\nabla u\right)\right|^{2}\alpha\rho\,dv\,dw\,ds,

Plugging both estimates in (
5.10
) and denoting by

K:=\sup_{r>0}\frac{\alpha^{\prime}(r)^{2}}{\alpha(r)r^{\gamma-2}}

we get

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f,g)\leq\frac{3K}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\iint|v-w|^{\gamma-2}\left|\hat{n}\cdot\nabla u\right|^{2}\rho\,dv\,dw\,ds.

It is now necessary to introduce
\rho(v,w)\equiv\rho(v)\rho(w)
,
u(v,w)\equiv u(v)+u(w)
, which yields the expression

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f,g)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3K}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\iint\left|v-w\right|^{\gamma-2}\left|(v-w)\cdot\left(\nabla u(v)-\nabla u(w)\right)\right|^{2}\rho(v)\rho(w)\,dv\,dw\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3K}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\iint\left|v-w\right|^{\gamma}\left|\nabla u(v)-\nabla u(w)\right|^{2}\rho(v)\rho(w)\,dv\,dw\,ds.

We now focus on the case
-3<\gamma<0
, and we simply bound

\left|\nabla u(v)-\nabla u(w)\right|^{2}\leq 2\left(\left|\nabla u(v)\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla u(w)\right|^{2}\right)

and reverse the role of
v
,
w
in one of the terms to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f,g)

\displaystyle\leq 3K\int_{0}^{1}\iint\left|v-w\right|^{\gamma}\left|\nabla u(v)\right|^{2}\rho(v)\rho(w)\,dv\,dw\,ds

\displaystyle=3K\int_{0}^{1}\int\left|\nabla u(v)\right|^{2}\rho(v)\bigg(\underbrace{\int\rho(w)\left|v-w\right|^{\gamma}\,dw}_{:=(I_{\gamma}\rho)(v)}\bigg)\,dv

\displaystyle\leq 3K\left\|I_{\gamma}\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,1]\right)}\mathcal{T}(f,g).

Next, we bound the singular integral
I_{\gamma}\rho
by splitting the integral in a ball of radius
R
and its complement:

\displaystyle(I_{\gamma}\rho)(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(v)}\left|v-w\right|^{\gamma}\rho(w)\,dw+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R}(v)}\left|v-w\right|^{\gamma}\rho(w)\,dw

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{B_{R}}\left|w\right|^{\gamma p^{\prime}}\rho(w)\,dw\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\left\|\rho\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+R^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{4\pi(p-1)}{p(3+\gamma)-3}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}R^{\gamma+\frac{3}{p^{\prime}}}\left\|\rho\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+R^{\gamma},

where
p>\frac{3}{3+\gamma}
. By choosing
R=\left\|\rho\right\|_{L^{p}}^{-\frac{p^{\prime}}{3}}
we obtain the following bound:

\left\|I_{\gamma}\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,1]\right)}\leq C_{\gamma,p}\left\|\rho\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\beta_{p,\gamma}},\quad C_{\gamma,p}=\left(\frac{4\pi(p-1)}{p(3+\gamma)-3}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}+1,\quad\beta_{p,\gamma}=-\frac{\gamma p}{3(p-1)}.

Recall that the functional
\rho\mapsto\int\rho^{p}\,dv
is convex with respect to the
2
-Wasserstein distance, see
[
19
]
. Interpolation yields:

\displaystyle\left\|\rho_{s}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\left(\int\rho_{s}(v)^{p}\,dv\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\left((1-s)\int f(v)^{p}\,dv+s\int g(v)^{p}\,dv\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-s)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+s^{\frac{1}{p}}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

In summary, we have shown that for
\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}
,
-3<\gamma<0
, and
p>\frac{3}{3+\gamma}
, there exists
C_{\gamma,p}>0
and
0<\alpha_{\gamma,p}<1
such that

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f_{t},g_{t})\leq C_{\gamma,p}K\left(\left\|f_{t}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\beta_{\gamma,p}}+\left\|g_{t}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{\beta_{\gamma,p}}\right)\mathcal{T}(f_{t},g_{t}).

This proves Theorem
3
for
-3<\gamma<0
.

Next, we study the Coulomb case
\gamma=-3
, which requires a special adaptation of the previous computation since
|v-w|^{-3}
is not locally integrable. We need to use the regularity of
u
, which can be expressed through the Hopf-Lax formula as

(6.1)

u_{s}(v)=-\frac{1}{s}\phi_{s}^{*}(v)+\frac{|v|^{2}}{2s},\qquad\phi_{s}(v)=s\phi(v)+(1-s)\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}.

Since
\phi^{*}
is convex, this shows
D^{2}u_{s}\leq\frac{1}{s}\textnormal{Id}
. On the other hand, it is easy to see that
\tilde{u}(s,v)=-u(1-s,v)
also satisfies a similar inequality. In fact, we have the bounds

-\frac{1}{1-s}\textnormal{Id}\leq D^{2}u_{s}\leq\frac{1}{s}\textnormal{Id},

which implies that
\left|\nabla u(v)-\nabla u(w)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\min\left(s,1-s\right)}\left|v-w\right|
.
We consider a parameter
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
to be optimized and note that
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f_{t},g_{t})
is bounded by

\frac{3K}{4}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\min\left(s,1-s\right)\right]^{-2\varepsilon}\iint\left|v-w\right|^{-3+2\varepsilon}\left|\nabla u(v)-\nabla u(w)\right|^{2(1-\varepsilon)}\rho(v)\rho(w)\,dv\,dw\,ds.

Using the inequality
\left(\left|\nabla u(v)\right|+\left|\nabla u(w)\right|\right)^{2(1-\varepsilon)}\leq 2^{1-2\varepsilon}\left(\left|\nabla u(v)\right|^{2(1-\varepsilon)}+\left|\nabla u(w)\right|^{2(1-\varepsilon)}\right)
and swapping the roles of
v
,
w
in the second resulting term, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f_{t},g_{t})

\displaystyle\leq 3K\int_{0}^{1}\left[\min\left(s,1-s\right)\right]^{-2\varepsilon}\iint\left|v-w\right|^{-3+2\varepsilon}\left|\nabla u(v)\right|^{2(1-\varepsilon)}\rho(v)\rho(w)\,dv\,dw\,ds

\displaystyle=3K\int_{0}^{1}\left[\min\left(s,1-s\right)\right]^{-2\varepsilon}\int\left|\nabla u(v)\right|^{2(1-\varepsilon)}\rho(v)(I_{-3+2\varepsilon}\rho)(v)\,dv\,ds,

where
(I_{\gamma}\rho)(v)=\int\rho(w)\left|v-w\right|^{\gamma}\,dw
as before. This yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f_{t},g_{t})

\displaystyle\leq 3K\left\|I_{-3+2\varepsilon}\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,1]\right)}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\min\left(s,1-s\right)\right]^{-2\varepsilon}\int\left|\nabla u(v)\right|^{2(1-\varepsilon)}\rho(v)\,dv\,ds

\displaystyle\leq 3K\left\|I_{-3+2\varepsilon}\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,1]\right)}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\min\left(s,1-s\right)\right]^{-2\varepsilon}\left(\int\left|\nabla u(v)\right|^{2}\rho(v)\,dv\right)^{1-\varepsilon}\,ds.

Next, we use that
(\rho,u)
are the constant-speed geodesic between
f
and
g
in the
2
-Wasserstein distance. In particular, this means that
\int\left|\nabla u_{s}(v)\right|^{2}\rho_{s}(v)\,dv
is independent of
s
and equal to
\mathcal{T}(f,g)
. Then, using that
\int_{0}^{1}\left[\min\left(s,1-s\right)\right]^{-2\varepsilon}\,ds=\frac{4^{1-\varepsilon}}{1-2\varepsilon}
, we are left with

(6.2)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f,g)\leq\frac{12K}{1-2\varepsilon}\left\|I_{-3+2\varepsilon}\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\mathcal{T}(f,g)^{1-\varepsilon}.

Finally, we need to optimize in
\varepsilon
. As a first step, we carefully bound the singular integral with respect to
\varepsilon
. Given
R>0
we have

\displaystyle(I_{-3+2\varepsilon}\rho)(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(v)}\frac{\rho(w)}{\left|v-w\right|^{3-2\varepsilon}}\,dw+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B_{R}(v)}\frac{\rho(w)}{\left|v-w\right|^{3-2\varepsilon}}\,dw

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\int_{B_{R}}\frac{dw}{\left|w\right|^{3-2\varepsilon}}+R^{-3+2\varepsilon}=\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon}\left\|\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}R^{2\varepsilon}+R^{-3+2\varepsilon}.

Setting
R=\left\|\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{-\frac{1}{3}}\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}
yields that

(6.3)

\left\|I_{-3+2\varepsilon}\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\left(2\pi+1\right)\left\|\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{1-\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}}\varepsilon^{-1+\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}}\leq\left(2\pi+1\right)\max\left(\left\|\rho\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},1\right)\varepsilon^{-1}.

If we plug (
6.3
) in (
6.2
) and assume that
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{4}
we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f,g)\leq 24(2\pi+1)K\max\left(\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\left\|g\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},1\right)\varepsilon^{-1}\left[\mathcal{T}(f,g)\right]^{1-\varepsilon}.

We choose
\varepsilon
that minimizes
\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{T}(f,g)^{1-\varepsilon}
:

\varepsilon=\begin{cases}\frac{-1}{\log\mathcal{T}(f,g)},&\textnormal{if }\mathcal{T}(f,g)\leq e^{-4}\\
\frac{1}{4},&\textnormal{if }\mathcal{T}(f,g)\geq e^{-4}\end{cases}.

In the case
\mathcal{T}(f,g)\leq e^{-4}
, the bound is given by

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{T}(f,g)\leq-24(2\pi+1)K\max\left(\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\left\|g\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},1\right)\mathcal{T}(f,g)\log\mathcal{T}(f,g),

and the proof is finished by solving the ode. Moreover
\mathcal{T}(f,g)=0
if
f_{in}=g_{in}
from Osgood uniqueness criteria.
∎
