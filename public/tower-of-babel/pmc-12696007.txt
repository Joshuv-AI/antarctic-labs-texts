A Standardized Semantic Feature Norm for 954 Cantonese Concepts
Authors: Jing Zhong, Keyi Kang, Weike Huang, Ziyi Zhao, Haoyun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study established psycholinguistic norms on Cantonese conceptual semantic features (CanFeat) with 954 concepts and semantic features, including feature frequency, sharing degree, and type of each feature. We provided concept-level variables, including participant count, feature counts (total, distinct, concept-specific, concept-shared), feature proportions, coverage, category labels (superordinate category and subordinate category), familiarity, and concreteness. We validated the reliability, specificity, and categorical structural consistency of the dataset through multiple analyses, including intra-coder reliability checks, gender comparability assessments, semantic category distinctions, and feature-level comparisons with established norms. We also compared CanFeat with norms in other languages, highlighting both similarities and differences. The present norming offers a timely and valuable resource for researchers in psycholinguistics, particularly those focusing on Cantonese production and semantic representation.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Communication

Background & Summary

Semantic features are critical for investigating semantic representation of concepts, particularly in word processing and semantic memory1–6. These features serve as the foundational elements for constructing concept representations7. Semantic feature norms are widely used in psycholinguistic research, but currently only exist for a limited number of languages, primarily English, German, and several others. Despite being a widely spoken language, no such norm is available for Cantonese. This study, therefore, established a Cantonese feature norm, addressing a gap in existing resources for linguistic research.

Computational modeling provides a robust framework for quantifying semantic representations, thereby facilitating a deeper understanding of the architecture of conceptual processing8. Within this framework, semantic space models have emerged as a prominent approach, including three dominant paradigms: network-based models1,9,10, such as WordNet11, representing concepts as nodes interconnected by edges denoting semantic relationships; feature-based models12, relying on human-generated semantic features (e.g., “has wings”, “can fly”, “is a tool”); and distributional-based models13–15, leveraging the co-occurrence frequency of words in large-scale corpus, using methods such as Latent Semantic Analysis16 and Word2Vec17. While distributional and network-based models offer structural or statistical approximations of conceptual knowledge, feature-based models provide a complementary approach by capturing interpretable, human-grounded representations of concepts. In addition, semantic ratings along with prespecified experiential dimensions offer a complementary approach to modeling meaning. Numerous databases utilizing semantic rating approach have been developed for both English and Chinese, with dimensional coverage ranging from single attributes to several dozen18–21. These methods quantify subjective experience by collecting human judgments on attributes such as concreteness, familiarity, imageability, valence, and arousal.

Understanding featural properties is crucial for establishing the semantic organization22. For example, Mansfield23 demonstrated that young children possess conceptual representations sensitive to semantic relationships, such as those indicating sense, antisense, supersense, subsense, and parts-whole relations. These findings suggest that their conceptual systems follow a structured organization based on shared meaning components, supporting the core arguments of the featural approach. While these feature representations are dynamic and influenced by context, culture, and task goals24–27, repeated exposure and activation in specific contexts lead to stable, aggregated feature patterns across individuals, providing the foundation for semantic feature norms.

As proposed in the conceptual structure account28–30, concepts can be represented as sets of features that define their semantic relationships and allow for categorization into broader domains27,31–33. Behavioral production norms offer a primary empirical method for deriving such semantic representations and revealing the underlying conceptual structure and semantic memory structure22,31,34–39. This approach typically involves a feature-generation task, where participants describe concept features under various instructions. Some studies use guided prompts (e.g., “is”, “has”, “does”, “made of”) to structure responses35,37, while others adapt a free description approach, allowing participants to generate features of a concept spontaneously38,40,41. Free description has been shown to provide a more comprehensive and individualized representation of cognitive structure, as it emphasizes the role of the perceiver and their unique experiences and values42. This method offers an effective tool for exploring the subjectivity of semantic representation, aligning with the study aim to capture cognitive diversity and fine-grained semantic details. In contrast, structured prompts may introduce priming effects43, potentially biasing responses toward the structure of the prompt rather than reflecting authentic semantic processing. The responses collected are then analyzed to compute concept-level metrics (e.g., number of features, familiarity or concreteness) and relational metrics (e.g., similarity). The importance of feature-based norms is further underscored by clinical research involving individuals with semantic memory disorders such as dyslexia, highlighting how variables calculated from feature norms (such as word meaning, and concreteness) can significantly affect semantic processing44–46. A core function of semantic memory involves organizing concepts into categories based on shared features47,48. Feature-based similarity measures enable researchers to examine hierarchical categorical structures, capturing relationships between broad (i.e., superordinate) and specific (i.e., subordinate) conceptual categories49–51. Consequently, feature norms have become important tools in psycholinguistic research, providing standardized stimuli for widely used experimental paradigms such as semantic priming52–55, property verification56,57, and concept combination58,59. These norms facilitate precise selection and control of conceptual variables, thereby enhancing the interpretability and generalizability of experimental findings. Moreover, feature-based semantic norms are increasingly recognized as valuable references for understanding the internal representations of large language models, helping bridge the gap between computational models and human semantic memory.

However, publicly available semantic feature norms remain limited, and are primarily available for a few major languages, including English22,34–36,38, German and Italian60, Spanish61, and Mandarin37,62,63. In contrast, no such standardized norms currently exist for Cantonese, which is the dominant language in the Greater Bay Area in China, including Macau and Hong Kong Special Administrative Regions (SAR), as well as Guangdong province64. For example, data from the 2018 Thematic Household Survey by the Hong Kong Census and Statistics Department65 revealed that 87.7% of residents aged 6 to 65 with normal hearing and speech abilities rated their Cantonese proficiency as “very good” or “good”. Beyond China, Cantonese remains widely spoken in overseas Chinese communities, particularly in countries such as Malaysia, Singapore and Canada, due to historical immigration patterns66–68. Despite this broad geographic and demographic reach, Cantonese still lacks comprehensive semantic feature norms, limiting its inclusion in psycholinguistic and neurolinguistic research.

Cantonese differs markedly from Mandarin Chinese in vocabulary, phonology, and cultural nuances64,69,70. One notable phenomenon is semantic misalignment, where identical meanings are expressed through different lexical or grammatical forms in the two languages71,72. For instance, the word for “umbrella” in Cantonese is “遮-ze1”, which also functions as a verb meaning “to cover”. In contrast, Mandarin uses “雨伞-yu3 san3” strictly as a noun referring to the object. Conversely, some words share identical written forms but differ semantically across the two languages73. For instance, “雀-zoek3” in Cantonese refers broadly to the “bird” category, while in Mandarin “雀-que4” typically denotes a specific word “sparrow”. Moreover, Cantonese incorporates a substantial number of English loanwords that are phonetically translated, such as “的士 - dik1 si6” for “taxi” or “士多啤梨 - si6 do1 be1 lei4” for “strawberry”. These distinctive linguistic features highlight the need to establish semantic norms uniquely tailored to Cantonese, rather than relying on resources developed for Mandarin.

To address this gap, the present study established a comprehensive semantic feature norm for Cantonese, referred to as CanFeat. We selected 954 concepts from a previously validated Cantonese picture-naming norm74 and collected free-form spoken feature descriptions from native speakers. The resulting dataset includes detailed features lists for each concept, accompanied by feature-level metrics (e.g., feature frequency, sharing degree), concept-level metrics (e.g., number of features, familiarity and concreteness) and between-concept measures (e.g., cosine similarity). In addition, each concept was classified into broader semantic superordinate categories and subordinate categories. To assess the cross-linguistic consistency of semantic representation, we also compared the CanFeat Cantonese norm with existing datasets in Mandarin and English for overlapping concepts. McRae’s English semantic feature production norms were selected for their foundational influence in semantic research. For Chinese, Deng et al.37 was chosen as it provides the most comparable feature-generation dataset to Cantonese in both linguistic and methodological terms. CanFeat offers a valuable resource for psycholinguistic research in Cantonese and enables meaningful cross-linguistic comparisons in semantic processing.

Methods

Participants

One hundred and twenty-six participants (96 females) were recruited in this experiment, aged from 18 to 39 years (M = 22.9 yrs, SD = 4.1). Participants needed to complete a Language History Questionnaire online before participating in the experiment. All participants were multilinguals whose native language was Cantonese and spoke at least one other language, mainly Mandarin Chinese (N = 119) and English (N = 109). All participants lived at the Greater Bay Area in China including Macau SAR, Hongkong SAR, and Guangdong province, where Cantonese is the most commonly used language. All rated the proficiency in each language on a scale of 1–7 in listening, speaking, reading, and writing, and the average scores were further converted to a 0–1 scale. The Cantonese proficiency (M = 0.86, SD = 0.14) was comparable with the second language proficiency (M = 0.81, SD = 0.17; p = 0.25), which was significantly higher than that of the third language (M = 0.62, SD = 0.16; p < 0.001). All participants were compensated to complete the experiment, and informed consent regarding data collection and sharing was obtained at the beginning of the study. Ethic approval for the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Macau (Approval number: BSERE21-APP008-ICI).

Materials

The current study included all concepts from the Cantonese picture norm previously established by our research group74, originally selected from the McRae, et al.34 study and Duñabeitia, et al.75 MultiPic databases. Along with these pictures, the picture naming norm provided name agreement (i.e. modal response and the H statistics, ranging from 0 to 4.05), concept agreement percentage, target word frequency (i.e. SketchEngine Cantonese frequency counts, ranging from 1 to 17598), concept familiarity (20.40–95.30 on a 100-point scale), and visual complexity (0.43–5.74). These metrics reflect a broad and diverse coverage of concepts across various psycholinguistic domains. For the current study, the modal responses for the naming of 1286 pictures were first selected. Repeated concepts were removed from the initial 1286 concepts, leaving 984 concepts (e.g. The curtains and drapes in Cantonese both refer to “窗簾”, so these repetitive concepts have been merged into one concept). Then, a preliminary familiarity rating was conducted with four Cantonese native speakers, and only items with higher than 60/100 familiarity items were kept (i.e. an exclusion contained 21 items, approximately 2% of the original set). After that, we performed a second round of synonym merging to ensure semantic distinctiveness (removing 9 more items), leaving 954 concepts in the final set. All the concepts are shown in the “CanFeat_info_concepts” document.

Procedure

Data were collected online via verbal responses recorded from Zoom or Tencent meetings. With each participant, instruction was first presented, offering them guidance on different types of features to consider (e.g., physical characteristics, functional properties, concept-related items, and other relevant attributes), along with illustrative examples. The instruction, guidance and exemplars were directly translated from McRae, et al.34 Appendix B, which were generally used in previous norms36. Following the instructions, participants were asked to freely describe the features for each concept without any prompts or interruptions from the experimenter. The audio of the entire experiment was recorded for subsequent transcription while no video recording from participants was kept. Each participant described 240 concepts presented in a randomized order, and a Latin-square design was used across participants. The self-paced task took approximately 2–3 hours per participant, and each concept was described by 29 to 40 participants.

Additionally, a randomly selected subset of 30 individuals rated each concept for familiarity (ranging from 1 = completely unfamiliar to 100 = very familiar) and concreteness (ranging from 1 = highly abstract to 100 = highly concrete), as they are crucial component in language and semantic processing76,77. Specifically, familiarity has been shown to influence conceptual encoding and retrieval processes76, while concreteness is associated with more detailed and perceptually grounded semantic representations77. In the current norm, familiarity was defined as the extent to which the concept was known based on previous experience, while concreteness was defined as the degree to which a concept can be perceived through the senses78. By collecting these ratings, we aim to provide additional semantic variables that can be used by researchers to explore cognitive mechanisms or control for confounding factors in experimental designs.

Within concepts

All concepts and their associated features were compiled into a comprehensive long form list. For each feature, we provided feature frequency, representing the number of participants who said this feature per concept, and sharing degree, representing the number of concepts that include this feature37. Following previous approaches22,34,37,38, idiosyncratic features, defined as those with a frequency of 1 and a sharing degree of 1 (i.e. only mentioned for one concept by one participant) were removed.

For each concept, we reported the number of participants responded, the total number of features per concept (i.e., the sum of frequency of all features per concept), the number of distinct features per concept (i.e., each feature is counted only once for one concept). Features were classified as either concept-shared (sharing degree ≥ 3) or concept-specific (sharing degree ≤ 2). This classification accounts for natural semantic overlap among closely related concepts and avoids overly strict exclusion of features that may still reflect meaningful conceptual associations. We provided the number of concept-specific features, and the number of concept-shared features for each concept. We also reported proportion of concept-specific features and proportion of concept-shared features, which was calculated by dividing the number of each type by the number of distinct features of this concept.

Additionally, the coverage of each concept was calculated to indicate the probability that a concept’s features are known and accessible within the knowledge base. This coverage algorithm, derived from ecological models of rare species79, is applied to semantic feature norms, where features mentioned by only one or two participants indicate rare features. Moreover, familiarity and concreteness ratings for each concept were calculated based on the average rating across all participants.

Between concepts

Similarities among all concepts were also assessed, using cosine similarity and distance. We constructed a matrix of concept and feature frequency, and applied principal component analysis (PCA) to centralize and reduce the number of dimensions from the original feature vector. After global standardization and PCA, 674 dimensions remained, explaining 95% of the variance. Finally, the cosine similarity was calculated using the cosine function from the proxy package in R81,82, and cosine distance was obtained by subtracting the similarity value from 1. The cosine similarity is measured by calculating the cosine of the angle between two vectors in an inner product space83,84. Additionally, it is commonly used in text analysis such as document-based or sentence-based similarities measurements85–87. Moreover, cosine distance measures how different two vectors are in direction84. To evaluate annotation accuracy, we conducted an intra-coder reliability check using a sample of 20 concepts, derived from common concepts across three norms (i.e. CCFD, McRae and CanFeat), including ten with the highest and ten with the lowest number of distinct features in our dataset. This selection strategy was designed to ensure that the sample represented a wide range of feature variability and served as the basis for the completeness validation analysis reported later. The original annotator (i.e., the first author) independently re-coded the semantic features, and cosine similarity scores between the original and re-coded data were compared using Pearson’s correlation and a paired-sample t-test.

Given the gender imbalance in our sample, we conducted a subgroup analysis on 20 selected concepts, those selected for later completeness validation analysis. For each concept, semantic feature responses were divided by gender, and cosine similarity values were calculated separately for male and female participants. We then compared the pairwise similarities across all concepts using both Pearson’s correlation and independent samples t-tests.

Between categories

To develop a detailed semantic representation of concepts, we explored both parallel and nested category membership. Given the lack of a universal standard for concept classification, we first used the Ward’s Minimum Variance Method for hierarchical clustering. These data-driven machine-generated clusters provided a reference classification based on conceptual distance, informing the final creation of semantic categories. Further adjustments were made manually on machine-generated categories. Ultimately, all concepts were organized into superordinate categories and subordinate categories, reflecting a nested relationship among semantic categories. To validate our categorical structure, we cross-checked the animal category against WordNet hierarchies11.

Using the established category structure, we further examined key differences across categories. Specifically, we compared pairwise semantic similarities between concepts within the same category versus those from different categories. Additionally, we conducted Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc analysis to evaluate differences in total number of features per concept, number of distinct features per concept, number and proportion of concept-specific or concept-shared features and the cosine similarity between concept pairs across all superordinate categories and subordinate categories. To evaluate the category validity of our dataset, we examined whether it reproduced classic semantic-category dissociation effects88. Specifically, a subset of concepts had available picture-naming response time (RT) data from our previously established picture-naming norm74. We extracted these RTs and conducted a one-way ANOVA to compare response times across concepts grouped by superordinate category.

Cross-language comparisons

We further investigated the similarities and differences between the current CanFeat norm and the established Mandarin norm CCFD37, and the English norm from McRae34. For Mandarin/English sets respectively, common concepts between Cantonese and the established sets were selected. We compared all available variables between norms, reporting their correlations and t-test differences. Additionally, when comparing between different norms, numerical values of familiarity and concreteness were converted from the current 1–100 to 1–9 (see equation below), corresponding to the scales used in previous norms.

Moreover, similarity matrices for common concepts were first extracted from both norms and subsequently transformed into pairwise long-form lists. Correlational analyses were first conducted on pairwise similarity using Pearson correlation, and the differences among sets were captured through the t-test.

To assess feature completeness, we compared the proportion of distinct features shared between our dataset and two established norms—CCFD37 and McRae34—across a subset of 20 concepts. These concepts were selected from those common to three norms, including ten with the highest and ten with the lowest number of distinct features in our dataset. This metric reflects the extent to which our features capture core semantic attributes identified in prior studies.

Data Overview

Within concepts

After excluding idiosyncratic features, a total of 108,521 descriptions for 954 concepts remained—representing a deletion rate of 13.62% (17,106 features). This variation aligns with the free-generation approach38,40,41, which naturally produces a wide range of responses, including low-frequency items. Detailed information regarding all concepts and features are provided in two files, “CanFeat_info_features.xlsx” and “CanFeat_info_concepts.xlsx”. The former details variables for each individual feature, including Cantonese feature name, feature English translation, feature frequency, sharing degree, and the type (specific or shared). The later file reports concept-level variables, including the number of participants responded, total number of features per concept, number of distinct features per concept, number of concept-specific features, number of concept-shared features, proportion of concept-specific features, proportion of concept-shared features, coverage, superordinate category, subordinate category, familiarity, concreteness. The two files can be easily combined based on common variables including Cantonese concept name, concept English translation, and concept id.

Overall, the 954 concepts have 10143 distinct features. In average, each concept has a total of 113.8 features (SD = 24.3). The mean coverage is 82.98%, the mean familiarity is 89.05 out of 100 (SD = 9.71), and the mean concreteness is 88.80 out of 100 (SD = 9.01). The mean of other variables were also calculated, including the number of concept-specific features (M = 10.2, SD = 4.6), the number of concept-shared features (M = 29.1, SD = 9.5), the proportion of concept-specific features (M = 27.0%, SD = 13.0%), the proportion of concept-shared features (M = 73.0%, SD = 13.0%), and coverage (M = 0.83, SD = 0.06) (see the “CanFeat_info_concepts.xlsx” for details).

Between concepts

A binary concept-feature matrix was also provided, with features as rows, concepts as columns, and cell values representing the frequency of each feature per concept (see “CanFeat_concept_feature_matrix_freq.xlsx” for details). Based on this matrix, cosine distance and cosine similarity were calculated and reported in “CanFeat_cosine_dist_matrix.xlsx” and “CanFeat_cosine_sim_matrix.xlsx”, respectively.

To facilitate category classification, concepts were first grouped using a data-driven hierarchical clustering method, based on pair-wise conceptual distance (see “Hierarchical Clustering Result k = 10.xlsx” in the Results folder on OSF). The machine-generated clusters were further refined. Specifically, the excessive detailed classifications were merged and overgeneralized clusters were decomposed. For instance, the initial clustering grouped robe, nightgown and pajamas into a subordinate category, and high-heeled shoes, shoes and boots into another. Similarly, zip, tie and buckle were initially classified under the “tool” category. Upon review, we reassigned these items back to the more appropriate “dress” category. We also addressed broader issues, such as the machine-generated clusters grouped “artificial objects” such as airport and stadium with “natural objects” like mountain and desert. We manually separated these concepts into different superordinate categories, respectively as “artificial objects” and “natural objects”, and further subdivided “artificial objects” into subordinate categories like “building” and “sports equipment”. This refinement process ultimately resulted in a final organization of all concepts into 9 superordinate categories and 43 subordinate categories (see the “CanFeat_info_concepts.xlsx” for details).

Technical Validation

The intra-coder reliability analysis demonstrated near-perfect consistency (r = 0.99, p < 0.001; t(19) = 0.07, p = 0.94), supporting the accuracy and stability of the annotation process. Moreover, the gender subgroup analysis revealed a strong correlation between male and female participants (r = 0.92, p < 0.001) and no statistically significant difference in semantic similarity scores (t = −1.83, p = 0.07). These results indicate that the gender imbalance in our sample did not meaningfully affect the main findings. The consistency within male and female subgroups also provides additional support for the reliability of feature generation across participants.

To ensure adequate representation of each category in the figure, we randomly selected six concepts from each subordinate category (or all concepts if a subordinate category contained fewer than six). To further examine the integrity of our categorical structure, we compared the concepts within the animal category against WordNet hierarchies. CanFeat’s superordinate categories were largely consistent with those in WordNet, with only one discrepancy among 150 concepts. However, WordNet’s subordinate categories were notably more fine-grained. For instance, lion is classified as a feline in WordNet, whereas in CanFeat it is grouped under the broader category terrestrial animal. These differences reflect varying levels of abstraction rather than classification errors. To further examine potential inconsistencies, we identified 13 concepts with clear mismatches (e.g., platypus categorized as aquatic animal in WordNet but as terrestrial animal in CanFeat). These cases were then cross-validated against the CCFD37, which aligned with our categorization framework, thereby supporting the validity and consistency of our semantic structure.

A clear nested structure is present, such that concepts within the same subordinate category exhibited higher similarity (diagonal positions) than those from different subordinate categories (off-diagonal), confirmed by a t-test (t(25041) = −129.53, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.16). Between categories

ANOVA analyses were conducted on the total number of features per concept, the number of distinct features, proportion of concept-specific features, proportion of concept-shared features and the cosine similarity between concept pairs. Across all superordinate categories, significant differences were found in the total number of features per concept (F(8,945) = 19.40, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.14), the number of distinct features (F(8,945) = 20.53, p < 0.001, η² = 0.15), proportion of concept-specific features (F(8,945) = 61.95, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.34), proportion of concept-shared features (F(8,945) = 61.95, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.34), and cosine similarity (F(8, 134289) = 8302.69, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.33). Following the significant ANOVA results, post hoc Least Significant Difference (LSD) tests with multiple comparison correction were conducted to explore pairwise differences among all categories (detailed results reported in “LSD_posthoc_Results.xlsx”). To summarize, the total number of features per concept in “food” was significantly higher than “artificial object” (i.e. “animal” ≥ “plant” ≥ “transportation” ≥ “food” > “artificial object” ≥ “natural object” ≥ “body part” ≥ “movement” ≥ “human”, “ ≥ ” means no significant difference while “ > ” means significant difference with p < 0.05). Moreover, these analyses reveal significant differences in the number of distinct features across all superordinate categories (Table 1), such that “animal” category has significantly more distinct features than other categories (ps < 0.05), while “natural objects” and “artificial objects” had significantly more distinct features than “body parts”, “human” and “movement” (ps < 0.05). Regarding the proportion of concept-specific features (Table 1 and Fig. 2A), “human” and “movement” showed significantly higher proportion of concept-specific features than “natural objects” (ps < 0.05). “Body part” showed marginally significantly higher proportion of concept-specific features than “transportation” (p = 0.06), “artificial objects” and “food” (ps < 0.01). Conversely, “animals” and “plants” had most shared features and exhibited lower proportion of concept-specific features than others (ps < 0.001). Regarding cosine similarity, the ranking is shown as “transportation” ≥ “animal” ≥ “plant” > “human” ≥ “food” ≥ “movement” ≥ “body part” > “natural object” > “artificial object” (“ ≥ ” means no significant difference while “ > ” means significant difference with p < 0.05). The analysis of RTs revealed a significant main effect of superordinate category, F(7, 286) = 2.08, p = 0.045, consistent with prior findings that lexical access varies by semantic category90,91. This result confirms that our dataset captures established semantic effects, thereby supporting its validity.

Across all subordinate categories (Table 2), significant differences were found in the total number of features per concept (F(42,911) = 5.41, p < 0.001, η² = 0.20), the number of distinct features (F(42,911) = 6.58, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.23), proportion of concept-specific features (F(42,911) = 18.02, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.45), proportion of concept-shared features (F(42,911) = 18.02, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.45), and cosine similarity (F(41, 24723) = 673.38, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.53). Yet, follow-up post-hoc analyses revealed no significant difference in pairwise comparisons among subordinate categories (Fig. 2B). However, regarding cosine similarity, the ranking is shown as “baby product” ≥ “musical instrument” ≥ “tree” ≥ “amphibious animal” ≥ “fruit” ≥ “meat” ≥ “vegetable” ≥ “religious figure” ≥ “bird” ≥ “boat” ≥ “terrestrial animal” > “flower” ≥ “insect” ≥ “race” ≥ “aquatic animal” > “weapon” ≥ “vehicle” ≥ “aircraft” ≥ “award” ≥ “figure” ≥ “fictional character” ≥ “occupation” ≥ “drinks” ≥ “human” ≥ “solid food” ≥ “tableware” ≥ “seasoning” ≥ “dress” ≥ “dessert” ≥ “animal body part” ≥ “movement” ≥ “human body part” > “building” ≥ “furniture” ≥ “toy” ≥ “electric appliance” ≥ “sports equipment” ≥ “artwork” ≥ “object” ≥ “tool” > “natural object” ≥ “personal belongings” (“ ≥” means no significant difference while “ >” means significant difference with p < 0.05).

Cross-language comparisons

To assess cross-linguistic validity, we compared the present Cantonese norms with established semantic norms in Mandarin37 and English34. These comparisons help evaluate the consistency and distinctiveness of semantic representations across languages, and provide evidence for the reliability and generalizability of the current dataset.

The current CanFeat Cantonese Norm and CCFD Mandarin norm shared 350 common concepts. To compare the similarity between the two norms, correlational analyses were first conducted on all common variables. The total number of features, the number of distinct features, proportion of concept-specific features and concept-shared features were highly correlated between two norms (ps < 0.001). Other measures including concreteness, familiarity and coverage were not significantly correlated (ps > 0.1). Pairwise t-tests were conducted to explore the difference between two norms. Results showed that the total number of features, the number of distinct features, proportion of concept-specific features and concept-shared features, concreteness, familiarity and coverage were all significantly different between Cantonese and Mandarin (ps < 0.001).

Beyond concept level, we also compared the pairwise cosine similarity across all common concepts between two norms (Table 3 and Fig. 3). The correlational analysis showed high consistency between the two norms (r = 0.75, p < 0.001), while a paired-sample t test showed significant difference between the two norms, with cosine similarity in the current CanFeat norm being lower than CCFD (t = −3.13, p = 0.002).

Usage Notes

The CanFeat norm is the first publicly available standardized semantic feature dataset for Cantonese, enabling more precise cross-linguistic comparisons of conceptual representation through feature production. The richness of the database allows researchers to flexibly select and manipulate concepts, features, and variables to suit specific experimental needs. Its detailed structure—including feature types (i.e., concept-specific vs. concept-shared), feature frequency, and number of participants per concept—also allows for the computation of semantic diversity, defined as the variability in how different individuals within a language community represent a concept92. This metric can further support analyses of individual and group-level semantic patterns in Cantonese. In addition, because the CanFeat norm is directly linked to a companion picture-naming dataset74, which includes measures of name agreement, it offers a valuable resource for psycholinguistic studies requiring fine-grained control of stimuli. The integrated design supports multimodal investigations of conceptual representation and lexical access. Finally, when used alongside existing norms in Mandarin or English, CanFeat opens new opportunities for cross-linguistic and cross-cultural studies of semantic memory and lexical processing.
