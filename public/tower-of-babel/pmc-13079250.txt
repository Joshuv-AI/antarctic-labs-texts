Technological and molecular profiling of lactic flora in Hamra ewe’s milk cheese from the Algerian steppe
Authors: Abdelkader El Amine Dahou, Malika Bouchibane, Zakaria Meskini, Hakim Aguedal, Nabil Touzout, Walid Elfalleh, Hamdi Bendif, Hichem Tahraoui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explores the technological and functional characteristics of indigenous lactic acid bacteria (LAB) isolated from Hamra ewe’s milk cheese, a traditional semi-refined product from the steppe region of western Algeria. The objective was to identify, characterize, and evaluate the acidifying, proteolytic, and texturizing potential of dominant LAB species contributing to cheese typicity. Thirty-two isolates were obtained and screened using morphological, biochemical, and molecular (multiplex PCR and 16 S rRNA sequencing) methods. Four dominant species were identified: Lactococcus lactis, L. lactis subsp. lactis biovar diacetylactis, Enterococcus faecium, and Lactobacillus acidophilus. Acidification assays revealed that Lactococcus strains were the most active acidifiers, producing 11 to 12.8 g/L of lactic acid with final pH values between 4.17 and 4.36 after 24 h. E. faecium exhibited moderate acidification (9.7 g/L; pH 4.42), while L. acidophilus produced lower acidity (7.2 g/L; pH 4.81). Growth and tolerance tests confirmed adaptation to wide temperature (6 to 55 °C), pH (2.3 to 7.3), and NaCl (1 to 6%) ranges. Coagulation and proteolytic tests indicated that Lactococcus strains ensured rapid milk coagulation (12 h; viscosity 810–980 cP), whereas E. faecium and L. acidophilus displayed notable proteolytic activity, forming lysis zones of 7 to 14 mm. L. acidophilus also produced exopolysaccharides (up to 7.2 g/L on day 10), enhancing texture and firmness. The coordinated microbial succession during fermentation and ripening confirmed the technological potential of these indigenous LAB. Their use as natural starter or adjunct cultures can enhance the sensory quality, authenticity, and typicity of traditional Algerian ewe’s milk cheeses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44463-026-00064-x.

Introduction

Cheese is a nutrient-dense dairy product rich in high-quality proteins, essential amino acids, lipids, vitamins, and minerals (IDF, 2018). To ensure microbiological safety, most modern cheese dairies rely on milk pasteurization (Chedid et al., 2025). However, an artisanal tradition continues to favor the use of raw milk and spontaneous fermentation, valued for their ability to develop authentic and complex sensory profiles (Falih et al., 2024; Mahaut et al., 2017). This traditional approach contrasts with industrial practices, where standardization and the use of commercial starter cultures may attenuate the sensory richness of cheeses (Jeantet et al., 2017). The International Dairy Federation (IDF, 2018) emphasizes the importance of preserving traditional know-how and microbial diversity as key factors underpinning the sensory and nutritional quality of cheese.

The native microflora of raw milk, unaltered by heat treatment, plays a decisive role in flavor and texture development (Bendimerad et al., 2024; Nicosia et al., 2023). Lactic acid bacteria (LAB), for instance, convert lactose into lactic acid, lowering pH and initiating milk coagulation (Wang et al., 2021). Other microbial groups such as propionic bacteria and molds contribute to eye formation and volatile compound production, giving rise to distinctive flavors in cheeses like Swiss and blue-veined varieties (Coelho et al., 2022). The composition of this microbial community, strongly influenced by the animal’s diet, rearing environment, and geographic origin, accounts for the remarkable variability in sensory characteristics among raw milk cheeses (Ma et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2023; Dahou et al., 2015; Montel et al., 2014; Mayo et al., 2021).

Lactic acid bacteria are indispensable to the dairy industry due to their functional roles in fermentation, preservation, and flavor formation (Beresford et al., 2001; Cardin et al., 2022; Ndoye el al., 2011). They are generally classified into two groups: starter and non-starter (secondary) microorganisms (Centeno & Carballo, 2023). Starter LAB, such as Lactobacillus, Lactococcus, Leuconostoc, Pediococcus, and Streptococcus, initiate fermentation by rapidly acidifying milk, thus inhibiting spoilage flora and forming the structural matrix of cheese (Barry & Tamime, 2010). Secondary microorganisms, which include micrococci, brevibacteria, yeasts, and molds, often originate from the raw milk or environment. They interact with starter cultures during ripening, generating proteolytic and lipolytic compounds that enhance aroma and texture complexity.

Recent advances in microbial ecology have highlighted the extraordinary diversity of LAB in raw and traditional cheeses, revealing strains with promising technological and probiotic potential. Investigating this diversity offers opportunities to identify indigenous LAB with unique metabolic profiles suitable for biotechnological and industrial applications.

The present study focuses on the isolation, identification, and technological characterization of indigenous LAB from Hamra ewe’s milk cheese, a semi-refined traditional product from western Algeria. The main objective is to select native strains with desirable acidification, proteolytic, and texturizing properties that could serve as natural starter or adjunct cultures. This work contributes to the valorization of local microbial biodiversity and supports the development of regionally distinctive Algerian cheeses with improved quality and typicity.

Materials and methods

Sampling and cheese manufacturing

The characterization of a traditional Algerian cheese produced according to a specific artisanal protocol from the Sfisifa region (Naâma) was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions. Twelve independent manufacturing trials (experimental replicates) were prospectively performed between April 2024 and March 2025, using exclusively raw milk obtained from local Hamra sheep. This methodological approach, which departs from standardized industrial processing, was adopted to assess the intrinsic physicochemical and microbiological characteristics of the product.

Analytical monitoring was carried out by collecting representative samples (250 mL of fermented milk and 250 g of curd) at five key technological stages: after coagulation and lactic fermentation, at demolding (24 h), after drying (48 h post-salting), and on days 4 and 10 of ripening. Cheese manufacture and all subsequent analyses were performed at the Animal Production Science and Technology Laboratory, Abdelhamid Ibn Badis University (Mostaganem, Algeria), in accordance with an optimized production scheme derived from the traditional Sfisifa method. Microbiological characterization

Enumeration and isolation of Lactic Microflora

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) were enumerated and isolated from milk and curd samples collected at different stages of production (molding, curdling, ripening). Ten grams (cheese) or ten milliliters (milk) of sample were homogenized with 90 ml peptone water (Institut Pasteur, Algeria) using a Stomacher 80 Biomaster (Seward, USA) for 5 min at 200 rpm. Serial dilutions were plated on selective media to quantify Lactococcus and Lactobacillus spp. Cocci were enumerated on M17 agar supplemented with nalidixic acid, and rods on MRS agar (BIOKAR Diagnostics–Solabia, Brazil). Plates were incubated at 37 °C under anaerobic or microaerophilic conditions, respectively. Morphological and biochemical characterization followed standard methods (Elnaz Milani et al., 2016; Powell et al., 2011). Purified isolates were preserved in skim milk (70%) with 0.05% yeast extract, 0.05% glucose, and 30% glycerol at −20 °C. Phenotypic identification employed API 50 CHL and API 20 STREP (bioMérieux, France) according to manufacturer protocols.

Molecular identification

Genomic DNA was extracted using QIAGEN QIAprep kits (Germany) from isolates grown in MRS or M17 media. Multiplex PCR was performed following Cousin et al. (2019) and Ustaoglu et al. (2024) with minor adjustments. Each 10 µl reaction contained 2× KAPA 2G Fast Multiplex Mix, 300 nM of each primer, and 80–100 ng template DNA. The PCR program consisted of an initial denaturation (95 °C, 2 min), 30 cycles of 95 °C (30 s), 58 °C (30 s), 72 °C (30 s), and a final extension (72 °C, 7 min).

Amplicons were visualized by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide (4 µl/100 ml) in 1× TAE buffer at 80 V for 1 h. A molecular weight marker (Promega, USA) was used. Gels were imaged under UV illumination (λ = 321 nm) using a G: BOX SYNGENE system.

Sequencing was performed using the same primers on a SeqStudio Flex Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Sequence identities were confirmed through BLAST comparison with the NCBI 16 S rRNA database using the platform program (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Identification followed Urshev et al. (2024) criteria: ≥ 99% similarity for species, 97–98% for genus, and < 97% for family assignment.

Technological characterization

The technological properties of the isolated lactic acid bacteria (LAB) were evaluated to determine their potential as starter or adjunct cultures in cheese manufacture. The analyses included growth behavior under varying environmental conditions (temperature, pH, NaCl), acidifying capacity, coagulant and proteolytic activity, and exopolysaccharide (EPS) production.

Growth at different temperatures and tolerance to pH and NaCl

Growth responses were evaluated according to Dahou et al. (2021). Each strain was cultured in MRS broth (25 mL) and incubated at 7, 25, 37, and 45 °C under anaerobic or microaerophilic conditions. Growth was monitored by measuring optical density (OD₆₀₀ nm) every 6 h for 48 h.

The assessment of pH and NaCl tolerance was carried out following the method described by Robinson (2002), using MRS broth adjusted to pH values ranging from 2 to 8 (pH 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8) and supplemented with NaCl at concentrations of 2%, 4%, 6%, and 9% (w/v). Each medium was inoculated with 10 µL of an overnight bacterial culture, and microbial growth was quantified by measuring optical density. Optimal growth conditions and tolerance limits were subsequently determined in order to select bacterial strains with potential suitability for biotechnological applications.

Acidifying power

The acidifying capacity of each strain was determined in sterile 10% (w/v) skim milk according to Dahou et al. (2021). Milk samples (50 mL) were inoculated at 1% and incubated at 37 °C. The pH and titratable acidity were measured after 2, 6, and 24 h of incubation. Acidity was expressed in Dornic degrees (°D) using titration with N/9 NaOH and phenolphthalein as an indicator (1 °D = 0.1 g lactic acid·L⁻¹).

Coagulant and proteolytic activity

Coagulation time was determined following the International Dairy Federation method (IDF, 2018) with modifications by Dahou et al. (2021). Milk (12.5% dry matter) was inoculated with 1 µL of each strain and incubated at 37 °C. The coagulation endpoint corresponded to the appearance of the first whey droplets on the gel surface.

Viscosity evolution during lactic coagulation was monitored using a HÖPPLER® KF 3.2 falling-ball viscometer (Rheotest, Germany). Optimal viscosity (850–1100 cP after 15–18 h) was used as an indicator of curd firmness suitable for cutting and curd retention.

Proteolytic activity was determined on PCA agar supplemented with skimmed ewe milk. Sterile filter-paper discs inoculated with 20 µL of overnight culture were placed on agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Proteolysis was assessed by the diameter of clear zones around the discs, indicating casein degradation.

Exopolysaccharide (EPS) production and texturizing ability

EPS production was initially screened by observing colony morphology on Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) hypersaccharose agar (BIOKAR Diagnostics–Solabia, Brazil). The formation of large, mucoid colonies indicated positive EPS production (Korcz & Varga, 2021).

For quantitative analysis, EPS synthesis kinetics were assessed according to Leroy and De Vuyst (2016) and optimized by the LSTPA laboratory. Reconstituted skim milk (500 mL, 50 g·L⁻¹ sucrose) was sterilized by double pasteurization (75 °C, twice) and inoculated with 1.5% of overnight LAB cultures (cell density > 10⁶ cfu·mL⁻¹; OD₆₀₀ > 0.6). Incubation was carried out at 37 °C (fermentation) and 15 °C (ripening). EPS concentration (g·L⁻¹) was determined after acetone precipitation and drying of the curd at successive stages (fermentation, coagulation, demolding, ripening).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data on acidification, coagulation, proteolysis, and EPS production were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel 2019 and verified with one-way ANOVA to assess differences among strains. Results were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Microbiological characterization

Lactic flora count

The bacterial enumeration revealed a distinct dynamic of lactic flora during the manufacturing process of Sfisifa cheese from raw ewe’s milk. The bacillary lactic flora, determined on MRS medium, increased progressively from 2 × 10³ cfu·g⁻¹ during lactic fermentation-coagulation to 3 × 10⁴ cfu·g⁻¹ at demolding, reaching 5 × 10⁵ cfu·g⁻¹ on the fourth day of ripening and 2 × 10⁶ cfu·g⁻¹ at the end of maturation (Table 1).

Similarly, the coccal lactic flora, enumerated on M17 medium, showed 8 × 10⁶ cfu·g⁻¹ at fermentation-coagulation, 1.2 × 10⁷ cfu·g⁻¹ after demolding, 3 × 10⁶ cfu·g⁻¹ at day 4, and 8 × 10⁵ cfu·g⁻¹ at the end of ripening. These results, consistent across three replicates, indicate stable microbial development due to homogeneous sampling and the use of milk from a single Hamra ewe herd.

The population levels were comparable to those reported by Dizdarevi et al. (2023), Gonzalez et al. (2015), Garabal et al. (2008), and Serrano et al. (2024) for cheeses made from thermized milk, but distinct from those produced with pasteurized milk. Microscopic observations revealed Gram-positive, catalase-negative cocci and bacilli, corresponding respectively to Enterococcus (8 isolates), Lactococcus (19 isolates), and Lactobacillus (5 isolates).

During cheese maturation, the proportion of Lactobacillus increased from 18% during fermentation-coagulation to 41% at the end of ripening, while Enterococcus decreased from 33% to 21%. Lactococcus remained relatively stable (49% → 38%). This evolution reflects the shift from acidifying coccal flora to secondary proteolytic flora as ripening progresses, consistent with findings by Montel et al. (2014) and Mahaut et al. (2017). Sfisifa cheese showed a progressive dominance of lactobacilli, accompanied by a decline in enterococci and a relative stability of lactococci, a dynamic typical of traditional raw-milk cheeses.

Isolation and phenotypic identification

A total of 32 isolates obtained from three independent cheese-making trials were phenotypically characterized using API (Analytical Profile Index) galleries. The isolates displayed substantial diversity depending on processing stage, herd origin, and local technological practices characteristic of Sfisifa cheese from Naama, western Algeria.

Eight representative species were identified: two Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis biovar diacetylactis, two Lactococcus lactis, two Lactobacillus acidophilus, and two Enterococcus faecium. During fermentation-coagulation, Enterococcus represented 33% of isolates, Lactobacillus 18%, and Lactococcus 49%. At the end of ripening, these proportions shifted to 21%, 41%, and 38%, respectively (Psoni et al., 2007; Randazzo et al., 2009). Such microbial succession mirrors that reported in traditional raw-milk cheeses (Litopoulou-Tzanetaki and Tzanetakis, 2011; Stefanovic et al., 2017).

API biochemical profiles confirmed these identifications. Lactobacillus acidophilus, homofermentative, thermophilic, fermenting hexoses but not pentoses. Lactococcus lactis and L. lactis biovar diacetylactis, acidifying species tolerant to > 3% NaCl, producing acetoin but inhibited at 45 °C. Enterococcus faecium, proteolytic, citrate-negative, capable of growth at pH 9 and in > 6% NaCl.

These results confirm that the indigenous lactic flora of Sfisifa cheese comprises technologically relevant strains adapted to its ecological niche and traditional manufacturing process.

Molecular identification

Among the 32 isolates, eight representative strains were subjected to 16 S rRNA gene amplification via multiplex PCR. Electrophoretic profiles (Fig. 2) obtained using GelCompar II software matched those of the corresponding reference strains. PCR products exhibited specific fragment sizes: 290 bp for Lactobacillus acidophilus, 390 bp for Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis biovar diacetylactis, 420 bp for Lactococcus lactis, and 730 bp for Enterococcus faecium, consistent with the ATCC reference strains. BLAST analysis of sequenced amplicons (Table 2) confirmed 99–100% homology with the corresponding reference species, validating the phenotypic identifications. The coexistence of Lactococcus, Enterococcus, and Lactobacillus species underscores their functional complementarity in the fermentation and maturation of Sfisifa cheese. These findings align with previous reports (Al-Mailem et al., 2017; Terzić-Vidojević et al., 2021; Urshev et al., 2024) emphasizing that microbial diversity is central to the sensory typicity and regional identity of traditional cheeses.

Technological characterization

Acidifying power

Acidification is a key functional trait of lactic acid bacteria (LAB), as it governs the biochemical transformation of milk and the subsequent development of cheese texture and flavor. The results presented in Fig. 3A and B indicate that all isolated LAB strains demonstrated progressive lactic acid production, accompanied by a corresponding decline in pH values during 24 h of incubation.

Fig. 3Acidifying power of isolated lactic acid strains: (A) Evolution of acidity at ° Dornic after 24 h of incubation. (B) pH changes after 24 h of incubation

Acidifying power of isolated lactic acid strains: (A) Evolution of acidity at ° Dornic after 24 h of incubation. (B) pH changes after 24 h of incubation

Among the isolates, Lactococcus lactis and Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis biovar diacetylactis exhibited the highest acidifying capacity, producing 11 and 12.8 g/L of lactic acid, respectively, after 24 h, with final pH values ranging between 4.17 and 4.36. Enterococcus faecium showed intermediate acidifying potential, yielding 9.7 g/L of lactic acid with a corresponding pH of 4.42, whereas Lactobacillus acidophilus produced lower acidity (7.2 g/L; pH 4.81).

The observed interspecific variability in acidification aligns with previous findings (Mahaut et al., 2017; Nicosia et al., 2023), confirming that differences in acidifying kinetics are intrinsic to each species and even strain-dependent. Such diversity is beneficial in cheese fermentation, allowing for fine modulation of acid development during curd formation and early ripening (Lang et al., 2025).

Growth at different temperatures, and tolerance to pH and NaCl variations

Temperature, pH, and NaCl tolerance are critical determinants of LAB ecological fitness and technological performance. As shown in Fig. 4, Lactococcus lactis and L. lactis subsp. lactis biovar diacetylactis grew optimally between 8 °C and 42 °C, while Enterococcus faecium demonstrated broader thermotolerance (6–55 °C). Lactobacillus acidophilus initiated growth at 32 °C and remained viable up to 55 °C, confirming its thermophilic behavior.

The strains exhibited considerable pH tolerance (Fig. 5). Lactococcus spp. maintained viability between pH 2.8 and 6.8, L. acidophilus showed optimal proteolytic activity at pH 5.6 but remained viable down to pH 2.3, and E. faecium tolerated a pH range from 3.2 to 7.3. Regarding salt tolerance (Fig. 6), Lactococcus strains grew in NaCl concentrations up to 3%, E. faecium tolerated 1–6%, and L. acidophilus sustained growth between 3 and 6% NaCl.

These findings are consistent with those of Coelho et al. (2022), González et al. (2015), and Montel et al. (2014), emphasizing the adaptation of indigenous LAB to the physicochemical constraints of the regional milk substrate. This ecological fitness supports their authenticity and functionality as native starter or adjunct cultures in traditional cheese-making.

Coagulant and proteolytic activity

Monitoring the lactic acid coagulation (LAC) of the four bacterial strains over 24 h at Lactic coagulation monitoring at 37 °C over 24 h revealed distinct strain-dependent behaviors (Fig. 7). The two Lactococcus strains coagulated milk within 12 h, achieving viscosities of 810–980 cP, indicative of efficient acid production and casein destabilization suitable for lactic fermentation (Xue et al., 2025). In contrast, E. faecium exhibited delayed coagulation (> 24 h) with a viscosity of 720 cP, below the typical range (850–1100 cP), suggesting limited coagulant activity but possible value as an adjunct culture. L. acidophilus displayed minimal coagulation capacity (410 cP) after 24 h, indicating a proteolytic rather than coagulating role in cheese ripening.

Proteolytic activity assays confirmed that L. acidophilus and E. faecium exhibited clear zones of casein hydrolysis, with diameters ranging from 12 to 14 mm and 7–9 mm, respectively, consistent with the proteolytic threshold (5–15 mm) proposed by Fox et al. (2005). These results align with previous reports (Kieliszek et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2018; Morea et al., 2007; Terzić-Vidojević et al., 2021) and highlight the contribution of these species to primary and secondary proteolysis during ripening. Proteolytic enzymes liberate peptides and amino acids essential for microbial growth and flavor compound formation, thereby shaping the organoleptic characteristics of mature cheese (IDF, 2018; Mahapatra & Paul, 2024; Dahou et al., 2021).

Texturizing power

The texturizing ability of the isolates, assessed on hypersaccharose agar, revealed EPS production by L. acidophilus, evident from the formation of large, viscous colonies. Quantitative monitoring (Fig. 8) showed a steady increase in EPS concentration from 0.8 g/L on day 1 to 7.2 g/L by day 10, confirming robust polysaccharide synthesis during ripening. This finding corroborates previous studies (Ahmed et al., 2005; Meng et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2021; Korcz & Varga, 2021), demonstrating the pivotal role of lactobacilli in enhancing curd texture and water retention.

EPS-producing LAB improve curd firmness and cohesiveness by reinforcing protein–polysaccharide interactions, leading to superior rheological and sensory qualities (Prete et al., 2021). The sequential dominance of LAB species throughout cheese maturation—beginning with acidifying Lactococcus spp., followed by Enterococcus faecium and proteolytic L. acidophilus—illustrates a well-coordinated microbial succession typical of traditional sheep’s milk cheeses (Centeno & Carballo, 2023; Dahou et al., 2015; Nicosia et al., 2023).

Molecular identification through multiplex PCR further confirmed the authenticity of the isolated strains, whose electrophoretic profiles were identical to ATCC reference strains, supporting their indigenous origin and functional stability. Statistical analysis (p < 0.05) validated the significance of these functional attributes across strains.

Collectively, the results demonstrate that the indigenous LAB consortium of the Hamra ewe’s milk cheese operates in a dynamic and synergistic manner. Lactococcus strains initiate rapid acidification and coagulation; E. faecium contributes to transitional proteolysis and flavor development; and L. acidophilus enhances textural properties via EPS production. This orchestrated microbial interaction underpins the distinct sensory and structural profile of this semi-refined traditional cheese (Zhong et al., 2024).

Conclusion

This study provides comprehensive insight into the technological and functional characteristics of indigenous lactic acid bacteria isolated from Hamra ewe’s milk cheese produced in the steppe region of western Algeria. The isolates exhibited diverse and complementary metabolic properties, including acidifying, proteolytic, and exopolysaccharide-producing activities, which collectively underpin the unique texture, flavor, and typicity of this regional cheese. Lactococcus lactis and L. lactis subsp. lactis biovar diacetylactis were the most efficient acidifiers, ensuring rapid milk coagulation. Enterococcus faecium displayed moderate acidification and proteolytic capacity, acting as a transitional ripening agent, while Lactobacillus acidophilus demonstrated strong proteolytic and EPS-producing activity, contributing to curd texturization and maturation. The statistically significant results (p < 0.05) confirm a structured microbial succession throughout cheese production, reflecting a naturally regulated fermentation ecosystem. These findings highlight the technological potential of the identified indigenous LAB for use as natural starter or adjunct cultures in artisanal cheese manufacture, contributing to the preservation of regional identity and the improvement of product consistency and quality. Future studies should focus on the genomic and metabolomic profiling of these indigenous strains to further elucidate their biochemical pathways and potential application in controlled cheese fermentation systems.
