Abstract
This article addresses the problem of multiple preamble detection in random access systems based on orthogonal time frequency space (OTFS) signaling. This challenge is formulated as a structured sparse recovery problem in the complex domain. To tackle it, the authors propose a new approximate message passing (AMP) algorithm that enforces double sparsity: the sparse selection of preambles and the inherent sparsity of OTFS signals in the delay–Doppler domain. From an algorithmic standpoint, the non-separable complex sparsity constraint necessitates a careful derivation and leads to the design of a novel AMP denoiser. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves robust detection performance and delivers significant gains over state-of-the-art techniques.

Introduction

Many high-dimensional communication problems exhibit sparsity, either due to the physical characteristics of wireless channels or as a result of coding schemes
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
For example, sparse regression coding achieves optimal asymptotic performance
[
4
,
5
]
, while coded compressed sensing formulates unsourced random access as a sequence of sparse recovery tasks
[
6
]
.
Multipath fading also induces sparsity, motivating the use of
OFDM
(
OFDM
) and, more recently,
OTFS
(
OTFS
) signaling
[
7
]
, which exploits sparsity in the
DD
(
DD
) domain.

Sparsity-inducing methods such as the
LASSO
(
LASSO
)
[
8
]
and its algorithmic variants
[
9
,
10
]
, promote element-wise sparsity but do not account for hierarchical structures.
To address this limitation, structured approaches like
GL
(
GL
)
[
11
]
and
SGL
(
SGL
)
[
12
]

enforce both group and intra-group sparsity, providing a better fit for problems with double sparsity, such as multiple preamble detection in
OTFS
-based random access
[
13
]
.
In this setting, only a few preambles are active, and each active signal propagates through a sparse multipath channel.

In parallel,
AMP
(
AMP
) algorithms
[
14
]
have emerged as efficient, theoretically grounded solutions for large-scale sparse recovery.
Extensions to the complex domain, such as
CAMP
(
CAMP
)
[
15
]
, are particularly relevant in wireless applications.
Recently,

has been extended to incorporate structured sparsity, paving the way for powerful new frameworks.

The main contributions of our article are:
(
i
) an

-based random access;
(
ii
) a structured denoiser that jointly exploits group and intra-group sparsity, with a rigorously derived Onsager correction term to enhance convergence; and
(
iii
) extensive simulations demonstrating significant performance gains over state-of-the-art methods.

II
Background and Problem Formulation

This section offers an overview of sparse regression, focusing on structured sparsity in complex-valued settings.
We start with the canonical linear inverse problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{w},

(1)

where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is the received vector,
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times N}
represents the sensing matrix, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is the unknown sparse vector.
Additive noise vector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
has
i.i.d.
(
i.i.d.
) entries, each with complex Gaussian distribution
w_{i}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}
denotes the noise variance.
For the problem we wish to study, (
1
) arises from an
OTFS
-based random access scenario.
We analyze this setting in detail, as it shapes both the sensing matrix and the sparsity exploited in recovery.

II-A

OTFS-Based Random Access Model

OTFS
is a two-dimensional modulation scheme designed for robust transmission over high-mobility, doubly-dispersive channels, mapping signals onto a
DD
grid to exploit channel sparsity
[
16
]
.
We consider an uplink random access scenario with
K
active users, each selecting a preamble
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{\mathrm{dd}}N_{\mathrm{dd}}}
from a common pool of
G
sequences, where
M_{\mathrm{dd}}
and
N_{\mathrm{dd}}
denote the number of delay and Doppler bins, respectively.
Every active user
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
randomly selects an index
i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,G\}
and transmits the corresponding
OTFS
-modulated preamble
\mathbf{s}_{i_{k}}
.
As is typical in random access, only a small fraction of preambles are active at any given time, with
K\ll G
.
The wireless channel between each user and the receiver is modeled as a sparse multipath channel with
L
dominant
DD
components.
Each multipath component is described by its delay
\tau_{k,l}
, Doppler shift
\nu_{k,l}
, and complex gain
h_{k,l}
, for
l=1,\dots,L
.
The received
OTFS
signal at the access point is

\textstyle\mathbf{y}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{L}h_{k,l}\,\mathbf{s}_{i_{k}}^{(\tau_{k,l},\nu_{k,l})}+\mathbf{w},

(2)

where
\mathbf{s}_{i_{k}}^{(\tau_{k,l},\nu_{k,l})}
denotes the preamble
\mathbf{s}_{i_{k}}
shifted in the
DD
domain by
(\tau_{k,l},\nu_{k,l})
, and
\mathbf{w}
denotes additive complex Gaussian noise.
This gives rise to a structured sparse recovery problem: a small number of active preambles, each experiencing sparse multipath propagation.

Interpreting (
1
) in this context, we see that
\mathbf{X}
is a structured sensing matrix whose columns correspond to
OTFS
-modulated preambles shifted over discrete sets of delays
\mathcal{T}
and Dopplers
\mathcal{D}
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=\left\{0,\frac{\tau_{p}}{M_{\mathrm{dd}}},\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{\tau_{\max}M_{\mathrm{dd}}}{\tau_{p}}\right\rfloor\frac{\tau_{p}}{M_{\mathrm{dd}}}\right\},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle=\left\{\!-\!\left\lfloor\frac{\nu_{\max}N_{\mathrm{dd}}}{\nu_{p}}\right\rfloor\frac{\nu_{p}}{N_{\mathrm{dd}}},\dots,0,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{\nu_{\max}N_{\mathrm{dd}}}{\nu_{p}}\right\rfloor\frac{\nu_{p}}{N_{\mathrm{dd}}}\right\}.

(4)

Above,
\tau_{p}
is the delay period,
\nu_{p}=1/\tau_{p}
is the Doppler period, and
(\tau_{\max},\nu_{\max})
denote the maximum delay and Doppler supported by the system.
The Cartesian product
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}
defines the full grid of DD shifts used to construct the columns of
\mathbf{X}
.
For each preamble
\mathbf{s}_{j}
, and for each DD shift
(\tau_{i},\nu_{i})\in\mathcal{S}
, the corresponding column of
\mathbf{X}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{j|\mathcal{S}|+i}=h_{\text{eff},i}\ast_{\sigma}\mathbf{s}_{j},

(5)

where
h_{\text{eff},i}
denotes the effective channel impulse response associated with the DD shift
(\tau_{i},\nu_{i})
, as defined in
[
17
, Eq. (13)]
, and
\ast_{\sigma}
represents the twisted convolution operator.
The unknown vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{C}^{G|\mathcal{S}|}
captures both user activity and channel properties, with two levels of sparsity: group sparsity across preambles and within-group sparsity across DD components.

II-B

Sparse Regression and Proximal Methods

Sparse regression aims to recover
\boldsymbol{\beta}
under sparsity constraints. The original problem

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq K,

(6)

is combinatorial and NP-hard. A common relaxation replaces
\ell_{0}
with the convex
\ell_{1}
norm, yielding the
LASSO
formulation

\operatorname{min}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{1}.

(7)

This multi-objective optimization balances fidelity and sparsity while remaining amenable to proximal methods.
The proximal operator of the
\ell_{1}
norm is

\prox_{\lambda\|\cdot\|_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\argmin_{\mathbf{v}}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1},

(8)

which reduces to elementwise soft-thresholding for real entries

\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda|\cdot|}(\beta)=\begin{cases}\beta-\lambda&\beta>\lambda\\
0&|\beta|\leq\lambda\\
\beta+\lambda&\beta<-\lambda.\end{cases}

(9)

For complex vectors, the
\ell_{1}
norm becomes

\textstyle\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1}=\sum_{i}|v_{i}|=\sum_{i}\sqrt{\Re(v_{i})^{2}+\Im(v_{i})^{2}},

(10)

which is separable across coordinates.
The proximal operator for the complex case become

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\lambda\|\cdot\|_{1}}(v)=\begin{cases}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{|v|}\right)v&|v|>\lambda\\
0&|v|\leq\lambda,\end{cases}

(11)

which preserves the phase of the argument while shrinking its magnitude.
This generalizes the real-valued operator of (
9
).

II-C

Structured Sparsity: Group and Sparse Group LASSO

The standard LASSO promotes sparsity at the level of individual coefficients but disregards structural relationships within the vector.
In many applications, however, the nonzero components of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
naturally cluster into groups (or blocks), motivating the use of
GL
, which enforces group sparsity through

\displaystyle\textstyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{g}\|_{2},

(12)

where
\mathcal{G}
partitions indices into disjoint groups and regularizers
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{g}\|_{2}
encourage low-energy blocks to vanish.
When sparsity occurs both within and across groups, the
SGL
formulation is more appropriate, combining
\ell_{1}
norm (within-group sparsity) and
\ell_{2}
norm (group sparsity)

\displaystyle\textstyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{1}+\lambda_{2}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{g}\|_{2}\,,

(13)

with parameter
\lambda_{1}
controlling sparsity within groups and
\lambda_{2}
promoting group-level sparsity.

II-D

Approximate Message Passing for Sparse Vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}

We turn to
AMP
to design an iterative solution to (
13
).
The
AMP
framework has been used to recover high-dimensional sparse signals from noisy linear measurements.
In its original form
[
14
]
, AMP reconstructs a real-valued sparse vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
from observation
\mathbf{y}
.
At iteration
t
, an effective observation is formed

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}^{(t)}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}+\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{z}^{(t)}

(14)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}
is the current estimate of the sparse vector,
\mathbf{X}^{T}
denotes the transpose of the sensing matrix, and
\mathbf{z}^{(t)}
is the residual vector described below.
At every step, a refined estimate is obtained via element-wise denoising,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t+1)}=\eta(\mathbf{r}^{(t)}),

(15)

with soft-thresholding
\eta(\mathbf{r}^{(t)})=\prox_{\lambda^{(t)}\|\cdot\|_{1}}(\mathbf{r}^{(t)})
as the proximal operator of the
\ell_{1}
norm.
A key feature of the AMP framework is the inclusion of an Onsager correction in the residual computation,

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t+1)}+\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbf{z}^{(t)}\langle\eta^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{(t)})\rangle,

(16)

where
\delta=n/N
is the measurement ratio and

\textstyle\langle\eta^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{(t)})\rangle\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\allowbreak\frac{\partial\eta_{i}(\mathbf{r}^{(t)})}{\partial\mathbf{r}^{(t)}_{i}}

is the divergence.
The latter term helps preserve approximate independence between the estimates and the residuals, which is crucial to derive key asymptotic properties
[
18
]
.
The algorithm is initialized with
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{z}^{(0)}=\mathbf{y}
.

III
AMP for Complex Sparse Group LASSO

Below, we introduce the
CSGL
(
CSGL
)-
AMP
algorithm, extending
AMP
to handle
SGL
in complex-valued settings.
This algorithm addresses structured sparse signals with both group-wise and within-group sparsity through a novel complex-valued denoiser with adaptive thresholds.
While an initial attempt to solve the
SGL
problem using
AMP
in the real-valued case was presented in
[
19
]
, to the best of our knowledge, this work constitutes the first effort to apply such an approach to the complex domain.

III-A

CSGL-AMP Iterative Framework

Consider the complex linear model in (
1
), with sparse vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
partitioned into groups.
Only some groups are active, and there are few non-zeros entries within each active group.
In a manner akin to the approach described in Sec.
II-D
,
CSGL
-
AMP
estimates
\boldsymbol{\beta}
using a composite iterative algorithm,

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}+\mathbf{X}^{H}\mathbf{z}^{(t)}

(17)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\eta(\mathbf{r}^{(t)},\lambda_{1}^{(t)},\lambda_{2}^{(t)})

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t+1)}+\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbf{z}^{(t)}\left\langle\eta^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{(t)},\lambda_{1}^{(t)},\lambda_{2}^{(t)})\right\rangle

(19)

where
\mathbf{X}^{H}
denotes the Hermitian (conjugate transpose) of the complex-valued sensing matrix, and
\eta(\cdot,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is a complex-valued denoiser tailored to the
CSGL
problem.
Thresholds
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are updated adaptively at every iteration through suitable online approximations, allowing the denoiser to dynamically balance sparsity both within and across groups.
The average divergence term appearing in the Onsager correction is obtained by evaluating the Wirtinger derivative of the denoiser
\eta

[
20
]
, whose derivation is detailed below.

III-B

CSGL Denoiser

Denoiser
\eta(\cdot,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is the proximal operator of the complex-valued
SGL
regularization in (
13
).
It is designed to promote double sparsity, as discussed in Sec.
II-C
.
Let
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
denote the input vector, partitioned into
G
disjoint groups as

\mathbf{r}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{r}_{1}^{T}&\mathbf{r}_{2}^{T}&\cdots&\mathbf{r}_{G}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
,
where
\mathbf{r}_{g}\in\mathbb{C}^{p_{g}}
and
\sum_{g=1}^{G}p_{g}=N
.
Here,
p_{g}
denotes the number of elements in the
g
-th group.
Parameters
\lambda_{1}>0
and
\lambda_{2}>0
regulate the levels of sparsity within individual elements and across groups, respectively.
The denoising process is structured into two sequential stages.

III-B
1
Element-wise soft thresholding

Each coefficient is independently processed using the complex soft-thresholding operator.
For group
\mathbf{r}_{g}
, this yields a thresholded vector

\mathbf{s}_{g}=\eta_{\mathrm{st}}(\mathbf{r}_{g},\lambda_{1})

(20)

where the operator is applied element-wise as

\eta_{\mathrm{st}}(r,\lambda_{1})=\begin{cases}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{|r|}\right)r&|r|>\lambda_{1}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(21)

for each
r\in\mathbf{r}_{g}
. This step enforces sparsity within each group by shrinking small-magnitude coefficients toward zero.

III-B
2
Group-wise shrinkage

Next, the resulting vector
\mathbf{s}_{g}
is scaled based on its
\ell_{2}
norm to enforce group sparsity:

[\eta(\mathbf{r}_{g},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})]_{j}=\begin{cases}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{g}\|_{2}}\right)\cdot[\mathbf{s}_{g}]_{j}&\text{if }\|\mathbf{s}_{g}\|_{2}>\lambda_{2}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(22)

for
j=1,\dots,p_{g}
, where
[\mathbf{v}]_{j}
denotes the
j
-th component of vector
\mathbf{v}
.
This step suppresses entire groups whose energy (after element-wise thresholding) is below the
\lambda_{2}
threshold.
An equivalent and compact expression for the overall denoiser, acting on each coefficient
r_{g,j}
in group
\mathbf{r}_{g}
, is

\begin{split}\eta_{g,j}\left(\mathbf{r}\right)&=\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\sqrt{\sum_{\iota=1}^{p_{g}}\left(|r_{g,\iota}|-\lambda_{1}\right)_{+}^{2}}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{|r_{g,j}|}\right)r_{g,j},\end{split}

(23)

where
(\cdot)_{+}=\max(\cdot,0)
.
It explicitly combines the element-wise and group-wise effects into a single scaling expression.

Remark 1
.

Special cases:
\lambda_{2}=0
yields standard complex
LASSO
(element-wise sparsity only);
\lambda_{1}=0
gives
GL
(group sparsity only). Tuning
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
allows the flexible enforcement of both sparsity levels.

III-C

Derivation of the Onsager Correction

In
AMP
, the Onsager correction accounts for correlations introduced by iterative denoising. For complex-valued denoisers, it can be derived via Wirtinger calculus, treating a complex variable and its conjugate as independent. Let
\eta_{g,j}(\mathbf{r})
denote the
j
-th component of the CSGL denoiser for group
g
, with
\mathbf{r}
partitioned as described above.
Its divergence, needed for the Onsager term, is the average of Wirtinger derivatives

\displaystyle\eta_{g,j}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\partial\eta_{g,j}}{\partial\Re r_{g,j}}-i\frac{\partial\eta_{g,j}}{\partial\Im r_{g,j}}\Big).

(24)

Using the derivative of the modulus

\frac{\partial|r_{g,j}|}{\partial\Re r_{g,j}}=\frac{\Re r_{g,j}}{|r_{g,j}|},\quad\frac{\partial|r_{g,j}|}{\partial\Im r_{g,j}}=\frac{\Im r_{g,j}}{|r_{g,j}|},

(25)

and the chain rule on (
23
), one obtains
\partial\eta_{g,j}/\partial\Re r_{g,j}
; a similar expression holds for
\partial\eta_{g,j}/\partial\Im r_{g,j}
. Substituting into (
24
) gives the complex divergence for each nonzero element.

The global Onsager term averages over all components

\textstyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{g}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{g}}\eta_{g,j}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}),

(26)

where
\mathcal{J}_{g}
is the set of nonzero indices in group
g
. After simplification, the closed-form expression reads

\begin{split}\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{a}}\!\Bigg(|\mathcal{J}_{g}|-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{g}}\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2|r_{g,j}|}-\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\frac{2|\mathcal{J}_{g}|-1-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{g}}\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2|r_{g,j}|}}{\sqrt{\sum_{\iota}(|r_{g,\iota}|-\lambda_{1})_{+}^{2}}}\Bigg)\end{split}

where
\mathcal{G}_{a}
is the set of active groups (
\|\mathbf{s}_{g}\|_{2}>\lambda_{2}
).

This expression captures the interplay between
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
, and the residual geometry.
|\mathcal{J}_{g}|
reflects the group size, while other terms adjust for thresholded magnitudes. When
\lambda_{2}=0
, it reduces to the complex soft-threshold divergence (standard LASSO), and when
\lambda_{1}=0
, it reflects pure group-wise shrinkage, consistent with the CSGL structure and the two-stage denoising process in Sec.
III-B
.

V
Conclusions

We presented a novel
CSGL
-
AMP
algorithm for preamble detection in
OTFS
-based random access, which judiciously exploits both group and within-group sparsity to achieve performance gains over existing methods, as shown in simulations under the
Veh-A
channel model.
Future work will extend the framework to collision scenarios and fully grant-free access in the
DD
domain, with joint preamble detection, channel estimation, and data recovery, further demonstrating the potential of structured
AMP
methods to enhance reliability and scalability in massive machine-type communications.
