CLDN18 as a novel junctional biomarker enhancing multi-gene classification of pancreatic cancer
Authors: Jessie Santoro, Lisa Agnello, Andrea Colacino, Ilaria Leone, Joel Verísimo-García, Eleonora Viscusi, Stefano Laudati, Giuseppe Iannaci, Antonio D’Antonio, África González-Fernández, Valentina De Falco, Immacolata Maietta

ABSTRACT
Highlights•CLDN18 improves multi-gene classification accuracy in pancreatic cancer.•CLDN18 is overexpressed in PAAD tissues, cells, and tumor-derived EVs.•CLDN18 correlates with CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6 in PAAD tumors.•Machine learning models identified CLDN18 as a top predictive feature.•CLDN18-associated EVs may support non-invasive PAAD diagnosis.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, Evs, Biomarkers, In silico analysis, Machine learning

Introduction

Claudins are a family of 24 tetraspanins transmembrane proteins essential for the formation and maintenance of tight junctions in epithelial and endothelial tissues. By closing the space between the cells and linking to cytoplasmic scaffolding proteins, claudins not only regulate selective permeability but also coordinate cellular polarity, signaling, and proliferation. Their expression is tissue-specific and highly regulated; however, aberrant expression or mislocalization is frequently observed in epithelial cancers and contributes to tumour progression [1].

Among them, Claudin-18 (CLDN18) has emerged as a therapeutic target in gastrointestinal pancreas malignancies [2,3]. Importantly, CLDN18 comprises two major splice isoforms, CLDN18.1 and CLDN18.2, which differ in their N-terminal extracellular region and show distinct tissue distribution. CLDN18.1 is mainly associated with lung epithelial cells, whereas CLDN18.2 is predominantly expressed in differentiated gastric mucosa. This distinction is clinically relevant because current CLDN18-directed therapeutic strategies primarily target CLDN18.2 rather than total CLDN18. In normal gastric epithelium, CLDN18.2 is largely confined within tight junctions and is poorly accessible to circulating antibodies; however, during malignant transformation, disruption of epithelial polarity and tight-junction architecture can expose CLDN18.2 on the tumor cell surface. This feature has provided the rationale for CLDN18.2-directed approaches, including zolbetuximab, a CLDN18.2-targeted cytolytic antibody, as well as emerging antibody–drug conjugates, bispecific antibodies and CAR-T cell-based strategies. In several malignancies, including gastric and pancreatic adenocarcinoma, aberrant CLDN18/CLDN18.2 expression has been associated with membranous tumor-cell staining and potential therapeutic relevance. Amplification and upregulation of CLDN18 in PAAD highlight its relevance as both a marker and a potential mediator of disease [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]].

Notably, CLDN18 expression has been reported in approximately 20–30% of PAAD cases, displaying variable intensity and distribution. Immunohistochemical analyses reveal heterogeneous patterns ranging from low and focal staining to strong and diffuse membranous positivity, suggesting the existence of biologically distinct subsets and reinforcing its potential value for therapeutic stratification [9]. PAAD is highly lethal due to late diagnosis, dense stroma and therapy resistance [10]. CEACAM5 and CA19–9, a sialyl-Lewis^a glycan antigen, are the most used biomarker but with low specificity [11]. The CA19.9 synthesis depends on the fucosyltransferases FUT3 and FUT6, which catalyze fucose addition to precursor glycans, generating the Lewis antigens of the CA19.9 epitope [12,13]. FUT3, FUT6, and CEACAM5 are often overexpressed in PAAD [[14], [15], [16]]. However, both CEACAM5 and CA19.9 can be elevated in several other tumour types, including colorectal, gastric, and lung cancers, limiting their organ specificity [17,18]. In this context, CLDN18 offers greater discriminatory power; it is normally restricted to gastric and pancreatic epithelial tissue, and its aberrant expression in malignancy provides a tumour-specific marker with tissue-selective enrichment [19]. Its inclusion in multi-marker panels may therefore help distinguish PAAD from gastrointestinal and non-gastrointestinal cancers.

In this scenario, transcriptomic datasets as Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) and Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) identified CLDN18 as one of the most significantly upregulated Claudins in PAAD by using Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) tool. On the other hand, CA19.9 antigen may be helpful in symptomatic patients to confirm a diagnosis and predict prognosis (National Comprehensive Cancer Network 2014); however, it cannot distinguish between cancer and chronic pancreatitis and possibly other disease states with chronic inflammation [20,21]. In this scenario, the translational use of CLDN18 in precision medicine could be evaluated using novel diagnostic biomarkers such as extracellular vesicles (EVs). Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membranous vesicles secreted by cells in both physiological and pathological conditions and are present in various bodily fluids, such as blood, urine, and saliva [22]. EVs play a crucial role in the transfer of their cargo (i.e., nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and other bioactive factors) from an originating cell to different recipient cells and modulate their function [23]. Tumor-secreted extracellular EVs emerge among messengers in tumor progression. Thus, EVs have shown great potential in diagnosing various diseases and as reliable biomarkers for early cancer detection and cancer development [24]. Moreover, it has been shown that EVs levels in serum increase early in the pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia stage, preceding to the formation of primary tumors [25]. Additionally, mass spectrometry analysis of EVs identified several proteins differentially expressed between pancreatic cancer patients and healthy controls, supporting the role of EVs in the diagnosis of pancreatic cancer [26]. Another study reported elevated levels of glypican-1 (GPC1) in EVs from PAAD which have been associated with angiogenesis and metastasis processes [27]. It was demonstrated that presence of circulating GPC1+ EVs in the serum discriminates between healthy donors and PAAD patients, as well as between early or late-stage patients [28]. Therefore, a biomarker such as CLDN18 holds the potential for PAAD early detection and to identify the disease at earlier stages when treatment options are more effective. Moreover, the discovery of CLDN18 transported by PAAD EVs and the different expression in cancer and normal cells is still under investigation.

Therefore, in this study we assessed the diagnostic performance of CLDN18 to validate the differential expression between tumour, normal and metastatic specimens.

Furthermore, machine learning models demonstrated that CLDN18 can be a good biomarker candidate to discriminate between normal and tumoral tissue. In addition, in vitro analysis suggests the use of CLDN18 as a potential biomarker carried by EVs for PAAD early diagnosis, providing valuable information about tumor aggressiveness and enabling exploration of future developments associated with disease progression. Overall, our findings support potential strategies for personalized treatment and patient risk stratification.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

RNA-seq data and sample-level annotations, including clinical staging information, were obtained from the TCGA Pan-Cancer RNA-Seq dataset and from GTEx (via the UCSC Xena Browser: https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/. Xena Browser data was harmonized to allow a direct comparison between TCGA and GTEx data avoiding any batch effect). Ensembl gene IDs were converted into HGNC symbols using GENCODE v45 annotation. Expression matrices were filtered to include only samples derived from pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD). Four genes of interest—CLDN18, CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6—were selected for analysis. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.0). Clinical information of patients of TCGA PAAD dataset is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Differential expression

Gene expression and survival analyses were conducted using GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn), based on TCGA and GTEx RNA-seq data. GEPIA uses a unified preprocessing pipeline to compare cancer data from the TCGA and the normal samples from GTEx normalizing and avoiding any potential batch effect. Claudin (CLDN) family gene expression in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) versus normal pancreatic tissues was analyzed using thresholds of |log₂FC| > 1 and P < 0.01. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed for CLDN genes with Overall Survival (OS) as the endpoint, stratifying patients by median gene expression. On the survival plot is written the hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence intervals and log-rank P-values.

To further evaluate CLDN18 expression, the UALCAN portal (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu) was used. mRNA and protein expression levels were analyzed in PAAD based on tumor grade, stage, and nodal metastasis. Protein expression comparisons included normal vs. tumor tissues and subgroups defined by cancer-related pathway alterations (e.g., MYC, mTOR, Hippo, SWI/SNF, RTK, NRF2, chromatin modifiers).

CLDN18 gene expression across pancreatic cancer cell lines was examined using DepMap (https://depmap.org/portal/). Transcriptomic data (log₂ TPM) were processed in R (v4.3.3) using ggplot2 and dplyr to generate a dot-plot showing CLDN18 expression per cell line, with dot size and color reflecting expression levels.

Gene co-expression and GO enrichment

Genes positively correlated with CLDN18 expression in PAAD were retrieved using the UALCAN portal (TCGA-PAAD). The resulting gene list was analyzed in Metascape for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment across Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). Enriched terms and biological themes were summarized using bar plots and network visualizations. To corroborate these functional annotations, the same gene list was additionally analyzed using the TNMplot GO functional analysis module, and results were visualized as bar plots and dot plots of the enriched GO categories.

TNMplot-Based multi-level gene expression analysis

To investigate the expression patterns of FUT6, FUT3, CLDN18, and CEACAM5 in pancreatic cancer, we used the TNMplot platform. A pan-cancer RNA-Seq dotplot assessed differential expression between tumor and normal tissues across multiple cancer types. Colors represented up- or down-regulation. Targetgram analysis using gene chip data further compared expression in normal, tumor, and metastatic pancreatic tissues. Gene Signature Analysis evaluated combined expression of the four genes, with group comparisons. A correlation matrix based on RNA-Seq data was constructed using Pearson correlation coefficients; significant associations were defined as r > 0.6 and FDR < 0.05. Finally, Multiple Gene Analysis was used to generate density plots and box plots from both RNA-seq and microarray datasets.

Correlation analysis and multivariate regression

Pairwise associations between CLDN18 and the other genes were assessed using Spearman correlation coefficients. A multiple linear regression model was fitted with CLDN18 as the dependent variable and CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6 as predictors. Model assumptions were evaluated using diagnostic plots: residuals vs fitted values (for linearity and homoscedasticity), Q–Q plots (for residual normality), scale-location plots, and residuals vs leverage. Outlier and influential case diagnostics were performed using Cook’s distance and leverage (R functions influence.measures(), and influencePlot(), from the car package v3.1–2). Residual normality was additionally tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To mitigate departures from normality and heteroscedasticity, we evaluated models with a log-transformed outcome (log(CLDN18 + 1)) and fitted robust linear regressions using R function rlm() (MASS v7.3–60).

Logistic regression and performance evaluation

CLDN18 expression was dichotomized into high- and low- expression groups based on the median and modeled using logistic regression. Model performance was assessed using receiver ROC curves, with AUC and 95% confidence intervals calculated using DeLong’s method in pROC (v1.18.4). Precision–recall curves were generated using the PRROC (v1.3.1) and summarized as AUPRC. Optimal ROC thresholds were identified using the Youden index via the coords() function in pROC.

ROC curve analysis and multi-gene prediction models

For biomarker performance assessment, gene expression values were dichotomized into high vs low groups using the median as the cutoff. Each gene was evaluated as a predictor of each binary target using ROC analysis, and pairwise AUCs with 95% confidence intervals were computed. A custom R function was implemented to compute and summarize all predictor–target AUC combinations; statistical evidence of discrimination was assessed using DeLong’s test as implemented in pROC, testing whether AUC differed from 0.5. Results were stored in unified data frames and rendered as tables using gridExtra::grid.table() (v2.3). For PDAC stage discrimination (Fig. 7F), patients were classified as early-stage (stages I–IB) or late-stage (stages II–IV), and ROC analyses were performed using logistic regression models based on individual genes and the combined four-gene panel. Pairwise comparisons between ROC curves were assessed using DeLong’s test.

(A–D) Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves evaluating the predictive performance of CEACAM5, FUT3, FUT6, and CLDN18 in various binary classification contexts. Each gene was tested alone or against others in predicting binary class labels (A) CEACAM5, (B) FUT3, (C) FUT6 and (D) Claudin 18. Logistic regression models were fitted using CEACAM5, CLDN18, and a two-gene multivariable model including both markers to classify tumor vs normal status and stage (using the stage definition/dichotomization described above for binary ROC analysis). ROC curves were generated using pROC, and AUCs (with DeLong confidence intervals) were compared where applicable.

For machine learning models, feature attribution was computed using permutation importance for both SVM and XGBoost, while gain-based importance was computed for XGBoost. Where applicable, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were calculated for the tree-based model. Model behavior and performance were summarized using partial dependence plots and confusion matrices.

Tumor classification and staging models

To evaluate diagnostic and staging utility, logistic regression models were fitted to classify tumor vs normal tissue and to discriminate between early- and late-stage PAAD. Gene-specific and combinatorial models (e.g., CLDN18 + FUT3 + FUT6, CEACAM5 + CLDN18, and full four-gene models) were evaluated. Stage classification used clinical metadata; tumors were grouped as “early” (Stages I–IB) versus “late” (Stages II–IV). ROC curves were generated and AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test (roc.test()).

Machine learning classification

Support vector machine (SVM, radial kernel) and XGBoost classifiers were fitted on the four-gene panel using the caret framework. Models were evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation, with ROC AUC as the primary metric. Variable importance was computed using caret::varImp() for SVM model and XGBoost gain-based importance (xgb.importance()). Confusion matrices and performance metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and precision) were computed from cross-validated predictions and visualized.

Software and packages

All analyses were performed in R v4.3.0 using the following packages: data.table, dplyr, tidyr, ggplot2, caret, xgboost, MASS, pROC, PRROC, car, biomaRt, and gridExtra. Analysis scripts were saved as R/R Markdown files, and figures and summary tables were saved in PDF format to support reproducibility.

Cell culture

PATU8988s cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cells were passaged every 2–3 days and maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Corning, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA)

HPDE cells were kindly provided by the Condorelli Laboratory and were cultured in Keratinocyte Serum Free Growth Medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 50 µg/mL bovine pituitary extract (Gibco, USA), 5 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF) (Gibco, USA) and 1X penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA).

Separation of EVs from pancreatic cells

EVs derived from PATU8988s and HPDE cell lines were separated using differential ultracentrifugation as previously described [29]. Before EVs separation, PATU8988s cells were cultured in exo-free medium for 48 h; whereas HPDE cells were cultured in absence of bovine pituitary extract upon EVs separation. For both cells, the medium was centrifuged twice (300xg and then 2000xg) for 10 min to remove dead cells and debris. Next, larger EVs and remaining cell debris were removed by ultracentrifugation at 10,000× g for 30 min at 4 °C using OPTIMA MAX-XP (Cat: # 393,315, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The conditioned medium was subjected twice to ultracentrifugation at 200,000× g for 70 min at 4 °C to obtain EVs pellets. The last round was performed to wash EVs pellets using 0.22 µm filtered PBS. Finally, the enriched EVs samples were resuspended in 200 µL of 0.22 µm filtered PBS and used immediately for further analysis.

Analysis of particle concentration and size using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

Pancreatic EVs samples were analyzed using NanoSight NS300 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, UK). Specifically, EVs were diluted 1:100 and injected into the NTA system under constant flow conditions (flow rate = 50). Five × 60 s videos were recorded and analyzed using NTA 3.2 software (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, UK). Three biological replicates of each sample were independently analyzed using NanoSight NS300.

Scanning electron microscopy of PAAD EVs

The morphology of cell derived EVs was analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). EVs derived from cell lines were prepared as previously described, with some modifications [30]. Briefly, the metallic stub was coated with gold (7 nm thick) before placing the sample. After ultracentrifugation, 10 μL of EVs pellets were placed on the prepared stub and were dried under a vacuum pump and coated with gold (5 nm thick) before imaging. Gold-coated EVs samples were examined using a PHENOM PROX scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher).

Immunoblotting analysis of PAAD EVs

Pancreatic EVs samples were analyzed by immunoblotting to assess EVs marker expression. Lysed EVs were resolved on a 10% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX Stain-Free™ gel (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA; Cat. #4568,034) at 120 V, and proteins were transferred using the Trans-Blot Turbo™ Transfer System (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Membranes were blocked with 5% milk in TBST (0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-calnexin (1:1000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Cat. #ab10286), anti-syntenin (1:1000; Abcam; Cat. #ab19903). Calnexin was included as a negative EV marker to monitor potential cellular contamination. Membranes were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, including goat anti-mouse IgG (1:2000; Biotech, Alpharetta, GA, USA; Cat. #10,303–05) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000; Biotech; Cat. #4030–05). Signal was detected using Clarity Max™ Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad Laboratories; Cat. #1705,062) and imaged on a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories; Cat. #12,003,154). PAAD cell lysate (CL) was used as positive control.

They were designed using Primer3 software and synthesized by Eurofins. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCT method, normalizing to the expression of ACTIN as the internal control.

Histological examination and immunohistochemistry

Pancreatic primary tumor samples were from “ P.O. Pellegrini” (ASL NA 1 Centro, Naples, Italy). Tumors were classified according to the America Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system. All patients agreed to make their tumor tissues available for research studies. This study was approved by the Internal Reviewing Board. Normal and tumors specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and 4 μm sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin according to standard procedures. CLDN18 was detected by incubating tissue sections with a mouse monoclonal anti-CLDN18 antibody (clone 43–14A) #790–7027; Ventana, Roche). Immunoreactions were visualized using the OptiView 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) immunohistochemistry (IHC) Detection (Ventana, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) on BenchMark IHC/ISH instruments. Negative controls were performed by omitting the primary antibody.

The sections were observed with an optical microscope (LEICA DM2000 LED) and photographed using a digital scanner 3D HISTECH P480 (3D HISTECH) at 40× magnification.

Statistics

All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates and repeated independently. Data distribution was assessed for normality before statistical analysis. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. For non-normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10.2.3 (GraphPad, Inc.). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. We have submitted all relevant data of our experiments to the EV-TRACK knowledgebase (EV-TRACK ID: EV260013) [32].

Results

Expression and prognostic significance of claudin gene family in PAAD

To evaluate the expression of Claudin (CLDN) genes in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), transcriptomic data from TCGA and GTEx were analyzed using the GEPIA database. Boxplot comparisons revealed that several CLDN genes—including CLDN1, CLDN2, CLDN4, CLDN5, CLDN7, CLDN11, CLDN12, CLDN15, CLDN18, and CLDN23—were significantly overexpressed in tumor tissues compared to normal pancreas (P < 0.001, Wilcoxon test). Among these, CLDN18 displayed the most pronounced tumor-associated upregulation, possibly suggesting altered epithelial differentiation and a shift toward a more aggressive molecular phenotype [33,34](Fig. 1A).

Expression and prognostic significance of claudin (CLDN) family genes in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD). A. Boxplots comparing the expression of individual CLDN genes between tumor (red - 179) and normal (black - 171) pancreatic tissues in the PAAD cohort on GEPIA database. Statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (P < 0.05; red asterisks denote significant differences). Each dot represents an individual sample. (B) Kaplan–Meier survival curves showing the overall survival of PAAD patients stratified by high (red) and low (blue) expression of selected CLDN genes. Patients were divided into high and low expression groups based on median transcript-per-million (TPM) values. Statistical significance was calculated using the log-rank test, with P values and sample sizes (n) indicated in each panel.

To assess their clinical relevance, overall survival (OS) was evaluated by Kaplan–Meier analysis, comparing patients with high versus low gene expression (median cut-off), and statistical significance was assessed using the log-rank test. High expression of CLDN1 (P = 0.012), CLDN4 (P = 0.013), and CLDN2 (P = 0.41) was associated with poorer prognosis, particularly CLDN1, where elevated expression correlated with reduced survival within the first 60 months. Although CLDN18, CLDN12, and CLDN23 did not reach statistical significance, no significant differences in overall survival were observed between the high- and low-expression groups (Fig. 1B). In the CLDN18 analysis, the high-expression group showed an approximately 20–30-month shorter median overall survival than the low-expression group, although this difference was not statistically significant (log-rank p = 0.33; HR = 1.2). However, CLDN18 remained a promising candidate biomarker because of its marked overexpression in PAAD.

Overall, this analysis highlights a subset of CLDN genes with elevated tumor expression and potential prognostic implications in PAAD.

UALCAN transcriptomic and proteomic analysis of CLDN18 in pancreatic adenocarcinoma

Considering the transcriptomic profiling data (Fig. 1A), CLDN18 was among the most significantly upregulated claudins in PAAD tissue compared to matched normal pancreas. This pronounced overexpression, despite the absence of a statistically significant association with overall survival in the Kaplan–Meier analysis (Fig. 1B), highlights CLDN18 as a gene of particular interest because of its marked differential expression in PAAD.

To further characterize CLDN18 expression in PAAD, we analyzed mRNA and protein levels using the UALCAN portal. TCGA RNA-seq data showed differences in CLDN18 mRNA expression across tumor grades (Fig. 2A), with the highest levels observed in grade 2 samples. CLDN18 mRNA expression also varied by pathological stage, with higher expression in stage III tumors (Fig. 2B).

Proteomic analysis using CPTAC data showed that when stratified by stage and grade, CLDN18 protein levels showed patterns broadly consistent with the transcriptomic results (Fig. 2C–D).

Finally, CLDN18 protein expression was examined in relation to common molecular alterations in PAAD. Higher CLDN18 levels were observed in tumors with alterations in MYC/MYCN MYC proto-oncogene / MYCN proto-oncogene, bHLH transcription factor) (Fig. 2E). Functional enrichment and pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes in PAAD

To investigate biological functions and pathways associated with transcriptional dysregulation in PAAD, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analyses in Metascape using differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified in UALCAN. Enriched GO Biological Process terms included broad categories such as cellular process (GO:0009,987), response to stimulus, homeostasis, developmental process, and regulation of biological process (Fig. 3A). Additional enrichments highlighted metabolic and structural programs, including lipid biosynthetic process (GO:0008,610), alcohol metabolic processes, redox-related biological oxidations, and actin filament organization (Fig. 3B). This implies metabolic reprogramming and cytoskeletal remodeling, hallmarks of cancer progression. Additional key pathways included the RHO GTPase cycle, epithelial cell differentiation, glycosylation, and glycerophospholipid biosynthesis, all of which are implicated in epithelial tumor behavior. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network further reinforced these findings, revealing densely connected modules focused on lipid metabolism, cytoskeletal organization, and epithelial structure maintenance (Fig. 3C). These clusters indicate transcriptional programs that together affect intracellular signaling, membrane architecture, and vesicular transport systems, all central to tumor behavior in PAAD [[35], [36], [37], [38]]. To characterize biological programs associated with CLDN18, we next compiled genes positively correlated with CLDN18 expression in PAAD using TCGA data (UALCAN/TNMplot, as indicated). GO enrichment of CLDN18-correlated genes revealed significant associations with cellular compartments and structures including the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, Golgi apparatus, actin cytoskeleton, tight junctions, and the apical junction complex (Supp. Fig. 1A–D), consistent with CLDN18 localization and junctional function [39].

A, Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes show significant enrichment, with top terms including cellular response, movement, and developmental processes. B, Enriched GO pathways refined to reduce redundancy, highlighting lipid biosynthetic and immune-related processes. C, Network visualization of non-redundant GO terms where each node represents a biological process, and color coding reflects functional categories.

Interestingly, the presence of cytoskeletal and vesicular elements among enriched categories suggests that CLDN18 and its co-expressed genes may participate in vesicle transport. This aligns with emerging evidence of claudins being secreted in exosomes, which are detectable in serum and hold promise as non-invasive cancer biomarkers. Analogous cases include exosomal CLDN3 and CLDN7 in ovarian and prostate cancers [[40], [41], [42], [43]].

Functionally, CLDN18-associated genes showed enrichment in glycosylation-related processes, including O-glycan biosynthesis and lipid metabolism (Supp Fig 1B-E). Notably, FUT3 and FUT6, key enzymes involved in CA19.9 biosynthesis [12], were among the top positively correlated genes, along with CEACAM5 (Supp. Fig. 1). Molecular Function enrichments included cadherin binding, phospholipase activity, and fucosyltransferase activity (GO:0008,417) (Supp. Fig. 1C–F). Collectively, these analyses associate CLDN18 co-expression with programs linked to epithelial junction organization, membrane/vesicular compartments, and tumor-associated glycosylation.

CLDN18 expression landscape across pancreatic cancer cell lines

To assess the translational relevance of CLDN18 as a potential therapeutic target in PAAD, we examined its expression across pancreatic cancer cell lines using RNA-seq data from the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) portal. Analyses were performed in R, and cell lines were ranked according to CLDN18 transcript abundance.

CLDN18 expression showed marked heterogeneity across PAAD cell lines (Fig. 4). Only a small subset of models—including PATU8988s8988S, 9505BIK, and PANC0504—displayed high CLDN18 expression (log₂ TPM > 6), whereas most cell lines exhibited low or near-undetectable levels.

Expression profile of CLDN18 across pancreatic cancer cell lines. Dot plot represents CLDN18 mRNA expression in a panel of pancreatic cancer cell lines. Expression levels are color-coded from low (blue) to high (red), and the size of each dot reflects the absolute expression value.

Overall, these results indicate that high CLDN18 expression is restricted to a minority of available in vitro PAAD models, in line with the heterogeneous expression observed in patient-derived datasets. This highlights the importance of selecting appropriate preclinical models for mechanistic studies and for evaluating CLDN18-targeted strategies.

Comparative expression analysis of CLDN18 and glycosylation-related genes across normal, tumor, and metastatic tissues

To investigate whether CLDN18 could enhance the diagnostic power of established biomarkers, we explored potential synergistic interactions with carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 5 (CEACAM5) and fucosyltransferases 3 and 6 (FUT3 and FUT6, respectively). CEACAM5 is a glycoprotein frequently overexpressed in epithelial tumors and often used as a diagnostic marker in gastrointestinal malignancies [14]. FUT3 and FUT6 are fucosyltransferases involved in the biosynthesis of Lewis antigens, including sialyl-Lewisⁿ (sLenm), the carbohydrate determinant measured clinically as CA19.9, a widely used biomarker for PAAD [44]. Building on these observations, we performed an in-depth pan-cancer analysis of a glycoepithelial signature comprising CLDN18, CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6 across multiple tumor types.

To explore the pan-cancer landscape of this glycoepithelial signature, we analyzed RNA-seq data from the TNMplot database across 22 cancer types, including normal, tumor, and metastatic tissues. The dot matrix plot (Fig. 5A) showed widespread dysregulation of the four genes, with PAAD displaying the most consistent and significant upregulation. Other gastrointestinal malignancies, including colorectal, gastric, and esophageal cancers, also exhibited coordinated upregulation of the gene set. Dot color indicates the direction of differential expression (red, upregulation in tumors), whereas dot size reflects statistical significance (–log₁₀ adjusted p-value).

Radar plots summarizing expression by tissue type showed coordinated upregulation of the gene set in tumors, with the largest increase observed for CEACAM5 and CLDN18, followed by FUT3 and FUT6 (Fig. 5B). Metastatic samples retained partial upregulation of the signature, albeit at slightly lower levels than primary tumors. At the signature level, a composite score across cancer types was significantly higher in tumors than in normal tissues (P < 0.0001), with metastatic tissues showing intermediate levels (Fig. 5C), indicating that signature expression is highest in primary tumors within this dataset.

Gene expression density plots showed upregulation of all four genes in tumors, with CLDN18 and CEACAM5 displaying bimodal distributions, consistent with subtype- or differentiation-associated expression patterns. In metastatic tissues, all four genes remained elevated relative to normal samples, although at slightly lower levels than in primary tumors (Fig. 5D).

Correlation heatmaps (Fig. 5E) revealed positive associations among the four genes, consistent with coordinated expression. In normal pancreatic tissue, CLDN18 showed modest correlations with CEACAM5 (r = 0.26), FUT3 (r = 0.25), and FUT6 (r = 0.23). In tumor tissue, these correlations were stronger—CEACAM5 (r = 0.48), FUT3 (r = 0.53), and FUT6 (r = 0.42)—indicating tighter co-expression in tumors. Correlations were calculated using Pearson (or Spearman) correlation coefficients.

Collectively, these results identify CLDN18, FUT3, FUT6, and CEACAM5 as a coordinated expression module that is elevated in PAAD tumors and remains increased in metastatic samples. The stage-dependent expression patterns and strengthened inter-gene correlations in tumors support the use of this four-gene set as a candidate multi-marker panel for PAAD characterization.

Strong associations were observed, particularly between FUT3 and FUT6 (ρ = 0.892, p < 0.001), suggesting co-regulation. CLDN18 expression showed positive correlations with FUT3 (ρ = 0.677), FUT6 (ρ = 0.639), and CEACAM5 (ρ = 0.527), all p < 0.001. Density plots revealed a right-skewed distribution for FUT3 and unimodal distributions for CLDN18 and CEACAM5 (Fig. 6A). To further assess influence and data distribution, we performed influence diagnostics using R functions influencePlot() and identified several samples with relatively high leverage and elevated Cook’s D values, including TCGA-2J-AABP-01 and TCGA-3A-A9IR-01. A histogram of CLDN18 expression showed a right-skewed distribution, with most values concentrated between 5 and 10, supporting the use of a log-transformed outcome. Applying a log(CLDN18 + 1) transformation improved residual normality and stabilized variance in the updated diagnostic plots, while reducing the influence of extreme observations (Supplementary Fig. 3A–B).

An OLS multiple linear regression model was fitted with CLDN18 as the outcome and CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6 as predictors. Diagnostic plots indicated overall acceptable model assumptions: the residuals vs fitted plot showed an almost flat LOESS trend, consistent with linearity. The Q–Q plot suggested residuals were approximately normal, with minor deviations at the tails. The scale–location plot showed no marked heteroscedasticity, and the residuals vs leverage plot highlighted a few potentially influential observations (e.g., TCGA-2L-AAQM-01) that did not exceed Cook’s distance thresholds but warranted caution (Supplementary Fig. 3C).

To further mitigate sensitivity to outliers, we fitted a robust linear model using R functions rlm() with M-estimation. Compared to OLS, the robust model down-weighted extreme observations such as TCGA-F2-A7TX-01, improving robustness and reducing the impact of influential points. Additionally, to explore categorical classification of CLDN18, we dichotomized expressions at the median and fitted a logistic regression model with CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6 as predictors. The resulting multigene classifier performed well (Supplementary Fig. 3D).

The ROC curve yielded an AUC of 0.807, indicating good discriminatory performance between high and low CLDN18 expression groups. The precision–recall (PR) curve further supported model performance, with a PR-AUC of 0.821. Notably, the PR curve maintained relatively high precision across increasing recall, which is particularly informative in class-imbalanced settings. A color gradient reflecting predicted probabilities improved interpretability by visualizing decision thresholds across the curve (Fig. 6B–C).

Collectively, these results support a robust association between CLDN18 and glycosylation-related genes in PAAD and provide a predictive framework that may aid in stratifying tumor subtypes.

Diagnostic performance of CLDN18 and glycosylation-related genes in PAAD

To evaluate the diagnostic and predictive potential of CLDN18 and its co-expressed genes (CEACAM5, FUT3, FUT6) in PAAD, we conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses using median-binarized gene expression. This approach assessed how well each gene could predict high or low expression of the others, revealing functional interconnectivity and biomarker relevance.

In addition, FUT3 (AUC = 0.803), FUT6 (AUC = 0.768), and CLDN18 (AUC = 0.753) showed good discriminatory performance, consistent with coordinated regulation within glycosylation-related programs (Fig. 7A). When FUT3 was used as the target, prediction from FUT6 (AUC = 0.93) was strongest, followed by CEACAM5 (AUC = 0.82) and CLDN18 (AUC = 0.81), supporting tight transcriptional coupling (Fig. 7B). Similarly, for FUT6 as the target, FUT3 (AUC = 0.91) remained the top predictor, with CEACAM5 and CLDN18 showing moderate performance (Fig. 7C).

CLDN18-targeted models showed slightly lower but consistent performance, with AUCs of 0.78 (FUT3), 0.75 (CEACAM5), and 0.72 (FUT6) (Fig. 7D). These findings highlight a shared transcriptional network, with CEACAM5 at the center and CLDN18 as a supportive marker of epithelial differentiation.

For tumor–normal discrimination, the single-gene markers CLDN18 (AUC = 0.761) and CEACAM5 (AUC = 0.785) showed good performance, while the two-gene FUT3+FUT6 model achieved an AUC of 0.815. The full four-gene panel yielded a comparable AUC (0.812) (Fig. 7E). Together, these results suggest that most of the discriminatory signal is captured by FUT3 and FUT6. Although CLDN18 did not improve classification performance in this dataset, its coordinated expression with CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6 supports its inclusion as a biologically relevant marker of epithelial differentiation within this transcriptional network.

In stage stratification, CEACAM5 was the strongest single marker (AUC = 0.839), whereas CLDN18 alone showed limited stage resolution (AUC = 0.631). Notably, combining all four genes improved performance (AUC = 0.911) (Fig. 7F). To further evaluate stage discrimination, ROC analysis was performed to distinguish early-stage (stages I–IB) from late-stage (stages II–IV) PDAC. The combined four-gene model (CLDN18, CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6) achieved the highest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.911). DeLong's test showed that the combined model significantly outperformed the CLDN18-only model (p = 0.0259), whereas comparisons with CEACAM5 and FUT3/FUT6 models were not statistically significant (Supplementary Table 12).

Altogether, these findings support a tightly coordinated transcriptional module involving CLDN18, CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6, with potential diagnostic and staging utility in PAAD. CLDN18 adds epithelial structural context, reinforcing its value as part of integrated biomarker panels.

Predictive performance of CLDN18 and glycosylation-related genes in PAAD

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of a four-gene panel (CEACAM5, CLDN18, FUT3, FUT6) in distinguishing pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) from normal pancreatic tissue, we trained and validated two supervised machine learning models—support vector machines (SVM) with a radial basis function kernel and gradient-boosted decision trees (XGBoost)—on transcriptomic profiles derived from TCGA and GTEx cohorts. To address class imbalance, the normal samples were randomly down sampled to match the number of tumor cases prior to model training. Model performance was assessed using 5-fold cross-validation implemented in the caret framework, preserving class proportions across folds. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used as the primary optimization metric, and performance estimates represent cross-validated results.

Both models demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance. XGBoost showed slightly higher average ROC performance and reduced variability across folds compared to SVM, suggesting greater predictive stability (Fig. 8A). ROC analysis of the training dataset yielded AUC values of 0.9993 for SVM and 1.000 for XGBoost (Fig. 8B–C). To provide a more robust estimate of model performance, out-of-fold predictions from 5-fold cross-validation and external validation in two independent GEO cohorts were subsequently performed (Supplementary Table 13). Using out-of-fold predictions, the SVM and XGBoost classifiers achieved AUC values of 0.9975 and 0.9983, respectively, with no significant difference between the two models (DeLong's test, P = 0.625).

The green-shaded region highlights the High-Performance Zone (low false positive rate < 20%, high true positive rate > 80%). d-E. Confusion matrices for SVM (center) and XGBoost (right) predictions on balanced datasets. F-G. To further assess model generalizability, both classifiers were evaluated in two independent external GEO cohorts. The SVM achieved AUC values of 0.8361 and 0.8849 in GSE62452 and GSE28735, respectively, whereas XGBoost achieved AUC values of 0.7378 and 0.7205, respectively (Supplementary Table 13).

Confusion matrix analysis further supported these findings. XGBoost misclassified only a single tumor sample and no normal samples, achieving perfect specificity. In contrast, the SVM model misclassified four normal and two tumor samples (Fig. 8D). Consistently, XGBoost outperformed SVM across key performance metrics, achieving 99.7% accuracy, 99.4% sensitivity, 100% specificity, and 100% precision, compared with 98.3%, 98.8%, 97.8%, and 97.6%, respectively, for SVM (Fig. 8E–F).

Model interpretability was examined using algorithm-specific feature importance measures. For the SVM model, variable importance was estimated using R functions caret varImp() function, which quantifies the relative contribution of each predictor to classification performance. For the XGBoost model, feature importance was computed using gain-based attribution, reflecting the relative improvement in model accuracy associated with each feature across tree splits. CEACAM5 consistently emerged as the most influential predictor in both models, followed by CLDN18. FUT3 and FUT6 showed lower relative importance but likely captured complementary glycosylation-associated transcriptional programs relevant to PAAD biology (Fig. 8G).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that a focused four-gene panel enables near-perfect classification of PAAD using machine learning approaches, with XGBoost offering a marginal but consistent performance advantage. The dominant contribution of CLDN18 reinforces the potential utility as a clinically informative biomarker.

Based on the in silico analysis, we focused our in vitro analysis exclusively on CLDN18.

Experimental validation of CLDN18 expression in pancreatic cells and derived EVs

To experimentally translate the findings obtained by in silico analyses, we took advantage of the data reported in Fig. 4, which highlighted a differential signal associated with the expression of CLDN18 in comparison with CEACAM 5 and FUT3. Guided by the DepMap analysis reported in Fig. 4, which identified PATU8988s among the few pancreatic cancer cell lines with high endogenous CLDN18 expression, we selected this model for experimental validation. HPDE cells, a well-established immortalized human pancreatic duct epithelial cell line, were used as a non-tumoral reference. This experimental setting provided a biologically relevant comparison to assess CLDN18 expression and its enrichment in extracellular vesicles between malignant and non-malignant pancreatic epithelial cells. Discussion

Claudins, a family of transmembrane proteins forming epithelial tight junctions, have long been associated with cancer development, particularly through the loss of polarity and barrier function that promotes tumor invasion and immune evasion [45]. Among them, Claudin-18 (CLDN18) has gained increasing attention as a tumor-specific marker and therapeutic target in gastrointestinal malignancies [46], including pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) [4,47] Our investigation highlighted a transcriptional module comprising CLDN18, CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6. CEACAM5 encodes a glycoprotein that carries sialyl-Lewis antigens, including CA19.9, while FUT3 and FUT6 are fucosyltransferases essential for CA19.9 biosynthesis [12,13]. Interestingly, these genes were only moderately correlated with CLDN18 in normal pancreatic tissue but showed strong and statistically significant correlations in tumor samples (Spearman’s ρ > 0.4), supporting the hypothesis of a tumor-specific glyco-epithelial regulatory network. Among those, CLDN18 emerged as a prominent feature of PAAD biology. Our data, derived from TCGA, GTEx, and CPTAC cohorts, revealed that CLDN18 is one of the most significantly upregulated claudins in PAAD tissue compared to normal tissue. This overexpression was associated with higher tumor grade and advanced clinical stage. However, no statistically significant association with overall survival was observed in the Kaplan–Meier analysis. These findings support the diagnostic relevance of CLDN18, whereas its prognostic value requires further investigation in larger cohorts. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that CLDN18 and its co-expressed genes are involved in pathways related to apical junction complexes, glycosylation, vesicle transport, and epithelial polarity—features linked to tumor differentiation and dissemination. Pathway analyses using Metascape and TNMplot further confirmed enrichment in glycosylation, immune modulation, and tight junction remodeling [45]. The coordinated overexpression of CLDN18, CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6 suggests the presence of a tumor-associated glyco-epithelial program rather than independent biomarker deregulation. While CLDN18 may reflect epithelial junction remodeling, FUT3 and FUT6 contribute to the biosynthesis of fucosylated Lewis antigens, including the CA19.9 epitope, on glycoproteins such as CEACAM5, supporting a potential link between epithelial differentiation, altered glycosylation, and extracellular vesicle-associated biomarker release in PAAD [5,48].

Consistent with this hypothesis, CLDN18 was co-expressed with CEACAM5, FUT3, and FUT6, supporting coordinated regulation within a tumor-associated glyco-epithelial network [5,38,43]. Although CEACAM5 and FUT3/FUT6 individually showed high diagnostic performance (AUC > 0.80), the four-gene panel including CLDN18 achieved a comparable AUC (0.812 vs. 0.815 for FUT3+FUT6), indicating no substantial improvement in classification accuracy. Nevertheless, CLDN18 provides complementary biological information related to epithelial differentiation and consistently ranked among the top predictive features, supporting its value for characterizing the molecular landscape of PAAD beyond diagnostic performance alone. In the literature, combined biomarker strategies for PAAD have focused primarily on CA19.9 and CEA, yet their limited specificity across gastrointestinal tumors has remained a barrier to clinical utility [16,18]. Although CEACAM5 and FUT3/FUT6 emerged as valid predictors by in silico analysis, CLDN18 showed strong transcriptional association with those genes and consistently ranked among the top predictive features. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that CLDN18, may be secreted in EVs, opening opportunities for their inclusion in liquid biopsy approaches [40,43]. CLDN18 has recently gained attention for its aberrant expression in several epithelial malignancies, including pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), where it has been proposed as biomarker and therapeutic target [49]. Therefore, it was considered as the most promising candidate for the in vitro validation, despite the other features. The experimental setup included two cell models, HPDE and PATU8988s. HPDE cells retain key morphological and functional features of normal pancreatic ductal epithelium representing an appropriate control to investigate molecular alterations; whereas PATU8988s retains more epithelial-like features, making it an essential model for studying pancreatic cancer differentiation and metastasis [50]. Therefore, this asset was used to assess, preliminarily, whether CLDN18 could be biologically relevant in a cellular context. The CLDN18 expression was demonstrated at the cellular level focusing on whether cancer-associated alterations could also be detected in EVs derived from pancreatic cells, thereby supporting the diagnostic potential of CLDN18 in EVs-based liquid biopsy strategies. EVs characterization revealed an enrichment of extracellular vesicles, with PATU8988s-derived EV preparations showing a markedly higher concentration than those derived from HPDE cells, consistent with previous literature [51]. Hence, in vitro experiments provided biological validation of these computational findings, which have not been published yet. CLDN18 overexpression was confirmed in PAAD cells compared with non-tumoral pancreatic epithelial cells, supporting its tumor-associated deregulation at the protein level [30]. Remarkably, CLDN18 was also selectively enriched in EVs released by PAAD cells, whereas minimal expression was detected in EVs derived from normal cells. On the other hand, among CEACAM5 and FUT3 expression levels analyzed by RT-qPCR, only CEACAM5 showed the same trend as CLDN18 in PATU8988s EVs and HPDE EVs, which support the prediction reported by in silico analysis. These observations indicate that CLDN18 deregulation is not confined to the cellular compartment but extends to tumor-derived EVs, consistent with emerging evidence that Claudins and CEACAM5 can be actively sorted into EVs [27]. Altogether, these results indicate that CLDN18 is not only overexpressed in PAAD cells but is also enriched in their secreted EVs. Additionally, CLDN18 overexpression was demonstrated also in pancreatic adenocarcinomas tissues compared to the normal counterpart, supporting data observed in vitro.

Our findings suggest that the presence of CLDN18 in EVs warrants further investigation with a view to its promising application as a biomarker in liquid biopsy. However, for future clinical application, validation must be extended to patient-derived EVs.

The data obtained provide a rational basis for further studies on CLDN18 as a component of broader diagnostic panels, aimed at exploring its therapeutic relevance in selected subgroups of PAAD. From a diagnostic perspective, while CEACAM5 and FUT3/FUT6 individually displayed high discriminatory power, the addition of CLDN18 significantly improved tumor–normal classification and disease stage stratification, which was confirmed by in vitro experiments. Machine learning models confirmed the robustness of this four-gene panel, highlighting CLDN18 as a key structural component complementing enzymatic and antigenic markers. As the first study of its kind, our study suggests that CLDN18, as a central node linking epithelial integrity, and glycosylation pathway, holds great promise as potential biomarker in PAAD diagnosis. Overall, CLDN18 represents a promising complementary biomarker that warrants further investigation alongside established markers such as CA19.9 in future diagnostic studies. However, to confirm the role of the features and specifically of CLDN18, future studies will include the validation in patient derived EVs which would complement and support new avenues in pancreatic cancer medical care.

Overall, our results, as suggested by in silico predictions and in vitro analyses, confirm the biological relevance of CLDN18 and identify its presence in EVs; they therefore provide the basis for the use of CLDN18 as a potential biomarker and highlight the potential translational utility of EV-associated CLDN18 as a novel, less invasive biomarker for pancreatic cancer diagnosis.
