“The world does not look the same anymore”: The experiences of patients six months after finishing an intensive trauma treatment program
Authors: Veronica Vaage-Kowalzik, Jeanette Engeset, Marianne Jakobsen, Julie Horgen Evensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrauma-focused treatment is known to be demanding, resulting in considerable drop-out rates. Intensive trauma treatment programs for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) have demonstrated significant and sustained results and appear to be better tolerated. However, how patients experience this treatment has been scarcely explored through qualitative studies, and only in the weeks following the end of treatment. The longer-term effects and experiences of the treatment remain under-investigated.ObjectiveThe current study aimed to explore participants’ experiences of an intensive inpatient trauma treatment program six months after completing it. Our research questions were: How do patients appraise intensive trauma-focused treatment six months after completion? How do they experience change from participating in the treatment program? And, what are their needs for follow-up care or treatment?MethodsSix patients diagnosed with PTSD with multiple traumas and comorbidity, and who participated six months earlier in an intensive two-week (4 + 4 days) inpatient trauma treatment program that included Prolonged Exposure, Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing, and therapist rotation, were interviewed using a semi-structured qualitative interview. The transcripts were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsOur findings indicate that patients found the intensive treatment program challenging but worthwhile, as participants described reduction in PTSD symptoms and reported shifts in self-perception. Patients also described greater emotional awareness and fewer somatic symptoms. Although patients reported that their process of improvement continued post-treatment, they indicated that they could benefit from additional follow-up care.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that participants reflect on their experiences in the intensive treatment program six months post-treatment and report that the treatment changed how they view their trauma history and, possibly as a consequence, how they perceive themselves. Background

PTSD is a psychiatric condition characterized by significant distress caused by symptoms of re-experiencing traumatic events, hypervigilance, and avoidance behaviors that can severely impair overall functioning and quality of life [1]. Trauma-focused treatment (TFT) has been found to be more effective than non-TFT for alleviating PTSD symptoms; however, it also has a higher dropout rate [2]. The challenges of trauma-focused treatment are clearly illustrated by the following quote from a patient undergoing weekly TFT with trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy: “I was very dark and depressed…constantly thinking about it, bringing up suppressed memories…” [3]. In this qualitative study, several participants described how trauma work led to ongoing rumination and low mood between therapy sessions, negatively impacting their social and occupational lives. This study is included in a recent systematic review of qualitative studies examining patients’ experiences of non-intensive TFT. The review emphasizes TFT as taxing, and notes high levels of distress between sessions and difficulty tolerating treatment as reasons for drop-out [4]. Therefore, it is crucial to explore new, better-tolerated methods for delivering TFT. Over the past decade, intensive trauma-focused treatment programs (ITTP) have been developed. A systematic review of ITTPs indicates that these intensive models have a higher treatment completion rate [5] and suggests that intensive treatments for PTSD may offer a more effective and acceptable alternative to traditional methods by mitigating dropout rates. Intensive treatment is operationalized in this review as treatment programs delivering TFT more than twice a week.

The current study models itself on the inpatient ITTP developed at the Psychotrauma Expertise Center (PSYTREC), Netherlands. This program includes daily Prolonged Exposure (PE) and Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) sessions, physical activity (PA), psychoeducation groups (PEG), as well as therapist rotation (TR) [6]. The associated research group has published studies documenting robust treatment results for patients with PTSD with sustained effects of treatment measured up to six-month follow-up [7], including those with comorbid disorders [8, 9], and the dissociative subtype of PTSD [10]. A recent Swedish feasibility study, which used a similar intensive treatment program, and where patients stayed in a hospital hotel facility, reported significant reductions in PTSD symptoms up to three months [11]. Additionally, the Dutch intensive treatment program has been successfully adapted for an outpatient facility in Norway, yielding positive results [12]. A qualitative study conducted by this research group two weeks post-treatment reported that the eight participants interviewed found the program demanding but beneficial for symptom reduction. The treatment’s intensity, as well as TR, were described as important for efficacy. Also, the sense of unity with fellow participants and physical activity were noted as factors that facilitated program completion [13]. In an earlier study, we investigated inpatients’ experiences with a version of the Dutch intensive treatment program 1–2 weeks after treatment completion. In this study, the six participants, a different sample from the current study, reported that the program’s intensity facilitated trauma processing and described benefiting from various elements of the treatment program. They described benefiting from diverse therapeutic interactions with ward staff and therapists in TR, contributing to the experienced change [14]. There is thus emerging evidence from both qualitative and quantitative research indicating that intensive TFT with two treatment modalities is well tolerated by patients and effective in treating PTSD symptoms.

Three qualitative studies to date have examined ITTPs that utilize a single treatment modality. In a written survey of veterans with PTSD conducted immediately after they completed a two-week massed PE treatment program, Sherill et al. found that treatment engagement was sustained through quick and meaningful symptom relief, and that treatment was prioritized while avoidance was limited [15]. Similarly, Kovacevic et al. reported that participants in a one-week massed Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT) program experienced improved PTSD symptoms along with enhanced cognitive and emotional coping skills. Seven patients were interviewed between one week and two months post-treatment [16]. In a recent study on an intensive PE treatment format delivering TFT over five consecutive days followed by three booster sessions, twelve patients were interviewed shortly after treatment completion. This sample described how short-term discomfort and demands contrasted with strength through collective support, and that the experienced benefits extended beyond PTSD symptom relief to include new insights and personal growth [17].

In summary, there is robust evidence supporting the treatment effect of ITTP. However, this treatment has scarcely been investigated through qualitative analysis, and no qualitative study has explored patients’ experiences six months post-treatment. Qualitative explorations of patients’ experiences aim to enrich and diversify the findings on outcome reported in quantitative research. In this qualitative analysis of patients’ post-therapy assessments and descriptions, we aim to illuminate the change processes they may have undergone and identify the subjective aspects that have been helpful in this process. Studies exploring patients’ experiences with treatment may provide valuable information to improve and refine treatment programs and offer a basis for hypotheses about how psychotherapeutic treatments work. This is particularly important for new treatments such as intensive trauma treatment programs. Furthermore, in a treatment program such as the one currently studied, which employs multiple treatment modalities, investigating how patients experience these modalities together is essential. Additionally, interviewing patients six months after completing treatment may yield valuable insights into the longer-lasting changes they experience and highlight the potential need for follow-up care, including what this care could entail.

In the current study, we aimed to explore patients’ experience of a two-week intensive multicomponent inpatient trauma treatment program. Our research questions were: How do patients appraise intensive trauma-focused treatment six months after completion? How do they experience potential change from participating in the treatment program? And, what are their needs for follow-up care or treatment?

Methods

Design and ethics

The current study is part of an intensive inpatient trauma treatment pilot project. The project’s primary objective is to investigate the feasibility of an ITTP in a standard Norwegian public healthcare setting. The study was conducted at a psychiatric clinic in Oslo, Norway, that offers both inpatient and outpatient services. The facility treats patients with a wide range of mental illnesses. It is part of the specialist healthcare system that requires a referral from a doctor or psychologist. Eighteen patients, divided into three groups of six, participated in the pilot study and received treatment between 2021 and 2023. All patients (N = 6) from the third treatment group were invited to participate in the current nested qualitative study. Patients in earlier treatment groups were not interviewed six months post-treatment due to resource constraints. A feasibility study involves a limited number of patients. However, the research questions aiming to narrow the aim of the study, the relative specificity of the sample, the reasonably well-established theoretical framework behind TFTs, the sound quality of the interview dialogue, and the analysis strategy that aimed at presenting selected patterns relevant for the study´s aim, all enhance the sample’s information power, allowing for a smaller sample size [18, 19]. Interviewing six patients was therefore considered acceptable by the authors.

The Central Norway Regional Ethics Health Committee (REC South East 0704/2022) has approved the study. Clinical Trial gov. identifier NCT05342480.

Participants and procedure

To be included in the pilot study, patients had to fulfill diagnostic criteria for a PTSD diagnosis, had >1 traumatic life event, had symptoms lasting >6 months, and had at least one prior psychotherapeutic treatment (>3 months duration). Patients had to be between the ages of 18–65 and be able to speak a Scandinavian language proficiently. Exclusion criteria included having a psychotic or bipolar disorder, current substance abuse disorder, currently in an abusive or life-threatening situation, or having attempted suicide in the three months before treatment. Eligibility for the pilot study was determined diagnostically using the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) [20]. The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Axis II Personality Disorders (SCID-II) [21] was used, on indication, to diagnose personality disorders. Patients were invited to an assessment interview where they received further information about the treatment and the study before providing informed written consent. Participants were six women aged 19–44 (average 34, median 37 yrs.), all spoke a Scandinavian language fluently (five Norwegian, one Swedish), two were not ethnically Norwegian (one from another European country, one Asian). All had previously received therapy for one year or more and had been in contact with public psychiatric health services during childhood or late teens. All reported multiple traumas, including sexual and physical abuse, witnessing serious violence, systematic bullying, and severe parental neglect. Most patients had current comorbid psychiatric diagnoses, including depressive episodes, eating disorder, and social anxiety disorder. One patient had a previous psychotic episode, and one had previous fleeting non-trauma-related psychotic symptoms. All completed the program.

Treatment

The pilot study treatment program, described in detail elsewhere [14], was a two-week, eight-day program (Monday to Thursday). Patients went home for the weekend (Friday to Monday). The program consisted of daily PE and EMDR sessions, between sessions PA, and PEG. Guidance on in vivo exposure (part of PE) was provided by the ward staff. TR was employed for EMDR and imaginal exposure (part of PE). Thus, patients met with 11–12 different therapists during the treatment. PE and EMDR were administered by psychologists and psychiatrists trained in the treatment methods. Therapists and representatives from the ward staff had a daily one-hour meeting to ensure adherence to the treatment protocols [22, 23], to monitor progress, and plan the following sessions. On-site supervision was provided for both PE and EMDR to ensure treatment adherence. Patients remained under the primary care of their outpatient therapists before and after the treatment. The first session with their outpatient therapist was scheduled one week after discharge. Following this session, the outpatient therapists determined the level of follow-up care. They were, however, asked to avoid providing TFT in the six months after treatment. Patients also underwent follow-up evaluations with the treatment program at two- and six-months post-treatment.

Data collection

A semi-structured interview aims to ensure thematic consistency between interviews while also allowing for a flexible exploration of the main themes [24–26]. Our semi-structured interview guide was developed by the pilot study research group and is similar to the interview guide used in an earlier publication [14]. The interview was conducted in Norwegian, a language that all participants understood very well. Participants were free to respond in other Scandinavian languages, understood well by the interviewers. The interview lasted 30 to 45 min and sought to explore patients’ experiences with the treatment. Patients were interviewed 6 to 7 months after completing the treatment program. They were informed that the interview would focus on their experiences, both positive and negative, with the treatment. The interviewers made an effort to create a supportive tone, encouraging participants to elaborate freely on relevant themes, paying special attention to ensure that difficult and/or negative experiences were explored through prompts and follow-up questions. To ensure that patients felt free to report any negative or difficult experiences, the interviewers were independent of the treatment program, treatment ward, and research project. Furthermore, they were specialized psychiatric nurses with experience in conducting qualitative interviews. We believe these efforts have strengthened the quality of the dialogue, thereby strengthening the sample´s information power.

The interview guide included three domains: (1) preparation for treatment, including “Was there anything that could be done to make you better prepared for the treatment?“; (2) experiences during and after treatment, including “How did you experience the treatment you received in the inpatient ward (the overall treatment program and the different treatment modalities and elements of treatment, including TR)? Do you think you changed in any way during or after the treatment? If so, how (changed thought patterns, emotions, bodily experiences, new insights, or altered relationships)? Do you remember a special situation, event, or episode that was particularly important to you?“; and (3) today’s situation and need for further treatment, including “How have you been since you completed the treatment program? Has it been necessary for you to receive any treatment or additional follow-up since discharge?” Domains 2 and 3 were emphasized. The interviewers used clarifying or probing questions when necessary. They encouraged participants to elaborate on examples of experiences during and after treatment, allowing them to explore their individual lived experiences. The interviews were audio recorded, and a research assistant transcribed them verbatim and removed all identifiable details.

Analysis

Our aim was to explore the participants’ experiences of the treatment program, their subjective experiences of change from participating in the treatment program, and their reported needs for follow-up care. We used Braun and Clarke`s reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) [19, 27] to identify recurring themes and patterns in the participants’ perspectives on the treatment program and their meaning-making related to their experiences of change. We employed an inductive approach to identify themes from our interviews that captured significant aspects of participants’ experiences with the treatment program, change, and the need for follow-up care. Our analysis was based within a hermeneutical-phenomenological epistemology, exploring and focusing on the lived experiences of the participants. We conducted the analyses in six phases. Phase 1: All authors thoroughly familiarized themselves with the data, rereading the transcripts specifically seeking both negative and positive experiences of the treatment program, emphasizing the participants’ lived experiences of both the treatment and the possible resulting changes. Initial notes were made on commonalities and variances in the dataset. Phase 2: The first and second authors developed initial codes and discussed their findings with the last author and compared their codes with the last author’s initial codes. Phase 3: Potential themes were searched for. The last author was introduced to the first and second author’s initial themes, and the three authors sought to further refine the themes and subthemes through reflexive discussion. Phase 4: The first, second, and last authors reviewed the themes and subthemes, and reread the interviews comparing the initial coded data to see that the themes coincided and worked with the data. Phase 5: All authors reviewed the themes and subthemes over several meetings, each bringing their own perspectives to enrich the interpretation of the data and discussing their relevance to the research questions and the study. Phase 6: Each theme was described in a report emphasizing the essence of the theme. Participants’ quotes were included to support the discussion and arguments, and to reflect how the themes were grounded in the participants’ descriptions.

In RTA, emphasis is placed on reflexivity, i.e., the awareness and exploration of how the researcher´s background, values, and assumptions influence the process of data analysis and the communication of their findings. As described elsewhere, the authors have different therapeutic orientations [14]. This might have contributed to an openness to explore the limits and downsides to different elements of the treatment program. Introducing new treatment programs may involve prestige and/or an overly optimistic approach to data interpretation. Two of the authors are permanent staff members in the inpatient ward, one is a permanent staff member responsible for coordinating research and is employed clinically in the outpatient part of the clinic, while one was temporarily hired for the program. While being a permanent member of staff could encourage a more protective stance, fearing to engage truly critically with the data and the treatment program, the authors employed outside the treatment ward may be freer to raise critique about the treatment program, and the authors from the ward may be better able to critique the follow-up care. Similarly, three of the four authors held dual roles as therapists and as researchers in the study, making it important to engage in discussions about how this potentially could affect our understanding of the data, and give heed to the member of the author team who did not hold this dual role. We hope that through dialogues about our understanding of and perspectives on the material throughout the process of analysis, our diverse backgrounds have nuanced, broadened, and enriched our interpretation of the data.

Results

We developed themes through reflexive engagement with the data. Four main themes were identified: (1) So tough, but worth it, (2) More adaptive self-perception, (3) Greater emotional and bodily access, and (4) Further treatment gains, but want more. So tough, but worth it

This theme focused on the patient’s overall experience of the treatment six months after completing the program. Our patient sample described how the treatment had been challenging and intense, but ultimately worthwhile, as it reduced PTSD symptoms. Multiple treatment modalities and therapeutic encounters, along with confronting trauma triggers through exposure, were identified as factors contributing to the treatment’s effectiveness.

Less troubled by PTSD symptoms

Participants reported feeling less burdened by PTSD symptoms. However, the individual patients described variable degrees of symptom relief. Gro described how she related differently to her trauma memories: “…that the memories became more that you can sort of access them when you yourself want to and look at them. It feels, yeah, it feels calmer and more, as a matter of fact, organized in the head…”.

Theresa described being less restricted by avoidance behaviors: “…but I feel like my life before, before we had that treatment, compared to now, it’s like two different lives that things aren’t as scary as they were, my life isn’t as limited, that my quality of life has improved …” Patients further described how a better understanding of the symptoms made the PTSD symptoms easier to tolerate.

Intense, but safe

The participants described the treatment weeks as very tough and intense. Most, however, noted that the intensity facilitated the change process. Theresa said: “…if there was a chance to offer this kind of treatment to even more people instead of this long, drawn-out (…) people can be in and out for ten years. Getting something so intensive, yeah, just really getting to the root of it, then I think many people would get, get a whole new life.” Marie described how the intensity and the inpatient treatment broke down her barriers of defense and made her gain new insights: “…you don’t have time to think about anything else, so it really makes the focus on what, like, should be the focus (…) and you are so sheltered from other everyday things that usually take so much energy, so all these safety behaviors or walls or whatever you have they start to fall away bit by bit.” Furthermore, patients expressed how ward staff and therapists` competency, warmth, and professional involvement made the intensity of the treatment bearable.

The value of multiple treatment elements and therapeutic encounters

The patients described how different treatment methods contributed to making the program efficient. Frida described it like this: “… just that combination of how it was set up, one played off the other, different ways of looking at exactly the same thing, coming at it from different angles, and you really feel completely shaken up, but I think that’s exactly what was, just right for me…” Emilie, on the other hand, struggled with imaginal exposure and found listening to recordings of these sessions particularly difficult: “…but to listen to those tapes, I felt it only pulled me down further if I was already down that day.” Emily, however, described good experiences with EMDR and other elements of the treatment program.

Therapist rotation was described as positive by the participants. Marie said: “I was a bit nervous about it, but it went really well, and above all, I was impressed that when you met a new person, it was a new person, but the situation wasn’t new.” Theresa described how different therapists brought various perspectives and emphasized how therapist rotation helped her feel less burdened by her relationship with the therapist: “I got therapists with different perspectives and of different ages, and the various situations they were in, so they could give different feedback (…). I also thought it was really useful, and the fact that you don’t build up such a relationship that makes you feel like you owe the therapist something or, you know, it was very nice not to have to carry that, but rather just get the (trauma) history and not the relationship, yeah, so that it was really interesting.”

Emilie, however, described TR as “really uncomfortable” as she had trouble trusting new people and therapists. She said: “Think I would have preferred to have one psychologist, and have some time to, like, build up for a month before one really dear to say what one thinks and feels…”.

Facing the triggers

The patients in our sample described positive experiences from working directly or indirectly with PTSD triggers. Most of our patients found benefits from directly confronting triggers through in vivo exposure (PE), and some mentioned they continue to use exposure techniques after treatment. Frida said: “…the world doesn’t look the same anymore, you know. Going out and doing exposure therapy was much more motivating and fun because I learned how to do it, yeah, so then I’ve done it a lot, right, “in peacetime” or with not too high SUD (subjective unit of distress).”

The patients who did not find in vivo exposure helpful did, however, describe benefiting from exposure outside the in vivo sessions. One recalled struggling with having a male ward staff member as their contact person, but built trust in this person during the admission. Another feared spending the night in the hospital but found that this did not prevent her from benefiting from treatment.

More adaptive self-perception

This theme focused on patients’ described change in self-perception that seemed to emerge when they developed a new perspective on their trauma history. This more adaptive self-perception could seem to encourage less self-blame, more self-compassion and acceptance, along with an increased ability to set healthy boundaries for themselves and others.

Changed perspective on trauma history

Patients described a shift in how they viewed their trauma history. They no longer minimized their traumatic experiences or the effects those experiences had had on them. The repeated processing of their trauma history, along with the experience of having their story met with acceptance by several therapists and ward staff, changed their perspective and reduced self-blame. Frida said: “…since I worked especially a lot with a rape, I could hardly say that word at all (…), but I understand that that’s what it was, and, yeah, so that’s actually a really, it’s a huge change.” Frida further described how her perspective changed: “… they didn’t break me down, but they broke down my whole reality first and then helped me rebuild it, only it looked completely different afterward. So, what I thought was very reasonable and wise and logical wasn’t (…) I felt so lonely and disgusting and all those things, and then suddenly, you know, there’s a group of people, like, who know my story and each of them has said, ‘I believe you.’ So just that in itself is so massive.”

Acceptance and self-compassion

Participants described how the treatment had made them less self-critical and more accepting of their vulnerabilities. Astrid said she experienced greater acceptance of her vulnerabilities: “… I, like, have become more conscious of my thoughts and reactions (…) and about my vulnerabilities and why I’ve reacted the way I have, really, so it’s given me a greater calm than I had before, in a way, earlier, I just pushed it aside, and that could make me quite tense in a way, I would just keep going, but now I’m more inclined to seek the pause and the stop and the calm than I did before.” Most described how listening to their recorded PE sessions had given them an outside perspective that made it easier to access self-compassion. Theresa described: “It took me a long time to even understand that it was me and it felt almost like turning on a podcast, a sad podcast, so there were several days at the beginning where I didn’t really realize it was me, but I felt really sorry for that girl, you know, I felt a lot of pity for the girl in the podcast.”

Self-value and improved boundaries

Participants described that the treatment helped them be more accepting of their needs and that this had made it easier to set good boundaries with others and in everyday life. Some described how experiencing that their trauma history was accepted and believed by both therapists and ward staff made it easier to stand up for themselves. Gro said: “…It is being believed that is, kind of, very connected to trauma issues, since I have had many experiences of not being believed (…) when there are so many different people and professionals who, like, just show that they believe you or that there’s a kind of, acknowledging environment (…). I was helped to, kind of, settle with the fact that I shouldn’t have contact with my (family member) that I’ve been a caregiver for, so that felt really good.”

Greater emotional and bodily access

This theme highlighted the patients’ experience of accessing a broader range of emotions and being better able to understand their physiological reactions, thus fostering a greater sense of bodily ease and calm, along with fewer somatic symptoms.

Greater emotional access

Participants described that the treatment had helped them access a greater spectrum of feelings. Gro described how when the PTSD symptoms receded, she got more access to her feelings: “It feels like that treatment helped, especially with the very intrusive memories and stuff, that would come at all hours of the day. It feels like that has become less, but in a way, it has also opened up a lot for all those underlying feelings.”

Patients described feeling less shame and guilt. Some described greater access to anger and mentioned that they had a new experience of anger as non-destructive. A sense of release when they got to work therapeutically on the emotional reactions to trauma was also described. Emilie said: “What happened is, kind of, sitting in my body, like what I feel sits in my body. It is the pictures (of trauma), like, it’s the emotions that pull out those pictures. And I had never processed those emotions. So, I thought it was really good when you got to enter into them.”

Less somatic symptoms and a better understanding of physical reactions

Participants described how bodily experiences and physical symptoms, like jumpiness, tension, and pain became more understandable as the symptoms got linked to their trauma history. Patients described a better understanding of their physical response to trauma triggers and reported a more bodily sense of ease and calm. Less bodily tension was also described by the patient sample, and some described fewer headaches. Marie described suffering from fewer migraines and that a better understanding of her trauma history had influenced her physical symptoms. She said: “One of the biggest insights during those weeks was that I realized, oh my god, there’s so much… unprocessed stuff that I thought I had dealt with mentally, but my body hadn’t.”

Further treatment gains, but want more

This theme focused on patients’ experiences after completing the intensive treatment program and their diverse needs for follow-up care.

Continued process

Patients in our sample described how the therapeutic process they began in the treatment program continued after discharge. Frida said: “…and I think it’s very wise what we were told not to engage in any specific trauma treatment or really just to calm down a bit on treatment in general after the stay. Precisely so that things can mature. They said that would happen, and it, it did happen for me …”. Some, however, described that the process they had begun had somewhat stalled because of current life challenges unrelated to their treatment or trauma history.

Needs-tailored follow-up

The program included specified follow-up sessions, and patients remained under the care of their outpatient therapist for at least two months post-treatment. The therapists were asked to avoid TFT but provide supportive therapy sessions if needed. Some patients, however, reported that they could have benefited from a more detailed and specific plan for their follow-up care. Although patients described that their process of improvement continued after discharge, they expressed a wish for some form of follow-up six months after treatment. Some felt they needed more trauma processing. Emilie said: “I understand that it’s an intensive program, so it’s only supposed to be two weeks, but even so, two weeks is very, very short, (…) to me it felt like, yeah, okay, we had started opening up some stuff.” Some said they required additional therapeutic work, particularly focused on interpersonal challenges and/or on the effects of their trauma history on their overall lives. Gro said: “In that sense, I think that’s how I feel now, that those burdens over time and how they affect me in life and in relationships, it feels like I’ve started, or I’ve begun to process it, but there’s still quite a bit left to go.” Some again expressed the need for more supportive follow-up during difficult life situations or when transitioning back to employment.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the experiences of participants who had undergone intensive inpatient trauma treatment six months earlier. Our analysis indicated that patients found the intensive treatment program very challenging but worthwhile, as they experienced a reduction in PTSD symptoms and a more adaptive self-perception. Patients also described greater emotional awareness and fewer somatic symptoms. Participants described how their process of improvement continued after discharge. However, they also expressed that they could benefit from additional supportive or psychotherapeutic treatment.

Experience of treatment intensity and multimodality

The evaluation of treatment as challenging aligns with Thoresen et al. and our previous study, where interviews were conducted shortly after treatment completion [13, 14]. It further mirrors patients’ experiences in intensive treatment programs that only use one treatment modality (PE or CPT) [15–17]. In our earlier study, we also reported that most patients sometimes felt too overwhelmed to benefit from all elements of treatment [14]. In contrast, the current patient sample did not describe the program as too intensive or overwhelming. Similar to previous studies [13, 14], the current patient sample also described the intensity of the treatment as facilitating trauma processing. Furthermore, multiple treatment elements and therapeutic encounters, including TR, appear to have provided patients with opportunities to engage with the program in ways that could benefit them. Consistent with findings on TR from earlier qualitative studies and Van Minnen et al.‘s survey on TR [6, 13, 14], patients find this novel approach to treatment acceptable. They emphasized the benefits of gaining perspectives from several therapists, and some expressed feeling a greater sense of ownership of the therapeutic process.

Experienced change

Similar to what is described in quantitative studies on medium-term follow-up [28], the current sample describes PTSD symptom reduction. As reflected in the title of this paper, “The world does not look the same anymore” (a quote from our patient sample), in vivo exposure may have the potential to consolidate PTSD symptom improvement. Our patient sample also described lasting changes in self-perception, including improved self-acceptance, self-compassion, and a greater ability to set healthy boundaries for themselves and others. These changes may be linked to patients’ reinterpretation of their trauma history and possibly an associated reduction in self-blame. Similar changes in self-perception were reported in our earlier study [14]. The current study suggests that these changes may be enduring six months post-treatment. This aligns with a quantitative study where patients reported decreased negative trauma-related cognitions, including self-blame, six months post-treatment [29]. We propose that when patients process their trauma history and recognize that they are not at fault for the traumatic events they have experienced, they more readily access self-acceptance and self-compassion. Subsequently, they may find it easier to establish appropriate boundaries for themselves and others. In a recent systematic review of qualitative studies on patients’ experiences with TFT, Gjerstad et al. similarly found that patients reported a shift in their perceptions of their trauma history, themselves, and their future. This, in turn, was linked to a reinterpretation of their traumatic experiences and a decrease in self-blame, described as a profound shift in their self-identity [4].

Similar to what we found in an earlier study [14], the current sample also described gaining access to a broader range of emotions. They further reported decreased somatic symptoms, such as headaches and migraines, and increased bodily presence and calm. This aligns with the theory behind the therapy models, since the EMDR protocol and, to a lesser extent, PE encourage patients to derive meaning from physical symptoms and incorporate bodily experiences into the trauma narrative [23, 22].

What do patients need after intensive trauma treatment?

Six months after completing treatment, patients described how their process of improvement continued after discharge. All, however, said they could benefit from more psychotherapeutic treatment, ranging from more trauma processing to working on relational or existential challenges, or getting support while adjusting to changing life situations. This begs the question: In a highly pressured mental health service, what is enough treatment, and where does therapy end? Most of our patients report that the process of improvement continued after discharge and that they are more emotionally and physically engaged, and some continue to practice exposure in their everyday lives. This could indicate that they can continue the process independently. Both our earlier study and this study may suggest that the intensive trauma treatment with TR helps patients own their recovery process [14]. It could thus be that the treatment model makes patients less dependent on working with a therapist in the aftermath of TFT. This may, however, only apply to therapeutic work on PTSD symptoms.

In a recently published study examining how the therapists in our pilot study experienced providing TFT within the framework of the treatment program, some of the therapists voiced concern about the short duration and intensity of the treatment program [30]. They highlighted the need for better planned follow-up care and noted that the complexity of some of the patients’ trauma histories might necessitate longer-term psychiatric treatment. Many patients with complex PTSD and PTSD with comorbidities struggle with relational challenges. Completing a TFT program and achieving PTSD symptom reduction might make these difficulties more evident. A recent qualitative study of patients who had participated in a one-week massed CPT program reported that increased self-reflection helped patients realize that they could benefit from further therapy to address remaining symptoms/problems [16]. In the current study, different patients report different needs post-treatment, including a desire to address relational difficulties. It could seem like standardized treatment is important in the TFT phase of treatment, as this reduces the risk of avoidance in both patients and therapists. In the post-TFT phase, however, patients might need a less standardized, more individually adjusted discharge plan and plan for follow-up care. In the main national and international PTSD treatment guidelines, limited attention is given to patients’ needs post-TFT [31–33]. Recently, an expert panel has attempted to better define this post-TFT treatment phase [34].The phase mirrors the third stage of the three-stage model for treatment of Complex PTSD, which includes (1) stabilization, (2) trauma memory processing, and (3) reintegration [35]. The panel described existing research on reintegration interventions as lacking. They referenced a systematic review of the effectiveness of reintegration interventions that emphasized how most studies either completely overlook the integration phase or find only weak evidence for the effectiveness of these interventions that included yoga, physical exercise, residential treatment, and service dog programs [36]. The expert panel suggested employing reintegration elements in the early phase of treatment (stabilization phase). This is consistent with treatment programs that have focused on integrating elements from phases one and three into TFT programs to improve outcome. The Skills Training in Affect and Interpersonal Regulation (STAIR) program seeks to improve alliance, social adjustment and daily life function by strengthening interpersonal skills and related emotion regulation capacities [37]. However, RCTs comparing TFT alone to TFT preceded by STAIR have shown TFT alone to be non-inferior [38–40]. Our results may suggest that some patients experience a greater need for, and may be more emotionally receptive to, elements from a STAIR program after completing TFT.

Limitations

This study has some important limitations. The sample was from the same treatment group, which may have limited the experiential variation. The sample also consists of female participants and treatment-completers only, with a mainly European ethnic background. Furthermore, our exclusion criteria included having had a recent suicide attempt, ongoing substance use disorders, and psychotic/bipolar disorders. While the relative specificity of the sample allows for a somewhat smaller sample size, the above factors limit transferability. Furthermore, we were only able to conduct six-month post-treatment interviews with the last patient group in the pilot study. The smaller number of participants in the study is, however, offset by the interview format and the fact that the study explores a novel therapy intervention that has received very limited previous qualitative investigation.

Interviewing patients to get their retrospective experience of treatment is important as it can provide valuable insights about patients’ improvement process; however, qualitative interviews conducted months after an intervention may be biased by memory distortions, reinterpretation, and recall bias that could influence how participants’ memory of the treatment or how they attribute change.

Conclusion and future research

In conclusion, our findings suggest that patients found the intensive treatment program challenging yet worthwhile, as they described self-reported reductions in PTSD symptoms and changes in self-perception. Multiple treatment elements and therapeutic encounters, including therapist rotation, seem to have provided patients with multiple opportunities to benefit from the program. Six months after treatment, patients indicated that their process of improvement continued after discharge. However, the sample also expressed that they could benefit from additional follow-up care.

Clinical implications: Our findings suggests that although patients report continued improvement after treatment, they could still benefit from further follow-up care. It appears that standardized treatment during the TFT phase is important because it reduces avoidance in both patients and therapists. However, after the TFT phase, patients may need a less standardized, more personalized follow-up plan. This approach could be especially vital for patients with complex trauma histories and/or high levels of comorbidity.

Future research: Future research should focus on what constitutes appropriate follow-up care after intensive trauma treatment and explore the therapeutic mechanisms that may link reduced negative trauma-related cognitions to enhanced self-compassion and self-care. Furthermore, including the perspectives of male PTSD patients, non-completers, and patients with diverse ethnic backgrounds would provide valuable insights and are paramount to future research.
