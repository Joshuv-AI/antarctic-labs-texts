Bag of Tricks for Subverting Reasoning-based Safety Guardrails
Shuo Chen Zhen Han Haokun Chen Bailan He Shengyun Si Jingpei Wu, Philip Torr Volker Tresp Jindong Gu

Abstract
Recent reasoning-based safety guardrails for Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), such as deliberative alignment, have shown strong defense against jailbreak attacks. By leveraging LRMs’ reasoning ability, these guardrails help the models to assess the safety of user inputs before generating final responses. The powerful reasoning ability can analyze the intention of the input query and will refuse to assist once it detects the harmful intent hidden by the jailbreak methods. Such guardrails have shown a significant boost in defense, such as the near-perfect refusal rates on the open-source gpt-oss series. Unfortunately, we find that these powerful reasoning-based guardrails can be extremely vulnerable to subtle manipulation of the input prompts, and once hijacked, can lead to even more harmful results. Specifically, we first uncover a surprisingly fragile aspect of these guardrails: simply adding a few template tokens to the input prompt can successfully bypass the seemingly powerful guardrails and lead to explicit and harmful responses. To explore further, we introduce a bag of jailbreak methods that subvert the reasoning-based guardrails. Our attacks span white-, gray-, and black-box settings and range from effortless template manipulations to fully automated optimization. Along with the potential for scalable implementation, these methods also achieve alarmingly high attack success rates ( e.g . , exceeding 90%) and harm scores across 5 different benchmarks on gpt-oss series on both local host models and online API services 1 1 1 Part of this work was submitted to the Red‑Teaming Challenge - OpenAI on Kaggle and won the Honorable Mention Award. . Evaluations across leading open-source LRMs confirm that these vulnerabilities are systemic, underscoring the urgent need for stronger alignment techniques for open-sourced LRMs to prevent malicious misuse. Introduction

Safety guardrails are critical measures to avoid misuse of powerful language models
(
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
.
Along with the recent progress on Large Reasoning Models (LRMs)
(
Chen et al. 2025
;
Jaech et al. 2024
)
, reasoning-based safety guardrails are proposed to further enhance the defense against malicious queries
(
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Guan et al. 2024
)
.
A notable example is
Deliberative Alignment

(
Guan et al. 2024
)
, which instructs the model to explicitly reason about safety policies before producing a response.
In this reasoning-then-answer paradigm, the model first generates a justification regarding whether an input violates safety requirements, and only proceeds if no violation is detected
(
Guan et al. 2024
)
.
Reported safety scores on the open-sourced gpt-oss series demonstrate nearly perfect refusal rates on multiple benchmarks
(
Guan et al. 2024
;
Agarwal et al. 2025
)
, indicating deliberative alignment as one of the most promising approaches for safeguarding advanced LRMs
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
.
However, the vulnerabilities and potential risks for these guardrails are underexplored.
Existing jailbreak methods are either blocked already or succeed with limited attack success rates (ASR)
(
Agarwal et al. 2025
)
, yet require a complex pipeline
(
Liang et al. 2025
;
Yao et al. 2025
)
.
Thus, current methods fail to show the potential safety risks of LRMs, especially when they are open-sourced and adopted globally, like the open-sourced gpt-oss series from OpenAI.
The open availability of such models dramatically amplifies the consequences of guardrail failures
(
Agarwal et al. 2025
)
.
Once an effective jailbreak method is discovered, it can be replicated instantly online and exploited globally. The threat becomes particularly severe when successful jailbreaks require minimal effort and expertise, since the lower the barrier to attack, the greater the potential for widespread misuse.

In this work, we expose these risks through a set of universal and scalable jailbreak methods.
We first uncover a surprisingly fragile aspect of such reasoning-based safety guardrails: the over-reliance on the chat structure consisted of special template tokens.
Simply adding a handful of these template tokens to the user prompt can directly compromise the seemingly powerful alignment guardrails and successfully elicit explicit and harmful responses.
Along with further exploration, we introduce a
bag of jailbreak techniques
that either
bypasses
the reasoning justification stage or
hijacks
the reasoning ability to generate highly customizable and tailored harmful responses.

To skip the reasoning stage, we design
Structural CoT Bypass
, which breaks the default token section pattern by injecting special chat tokens and mock justifications, thereby pushing the model to skip its safety reasoning and directly generate a response. We further introduce
Fake Over-Refusal
, which leverages the ambiguity between genuine refusals and benign “over-refusal” phrasings to craft adversarial queries that appear safe but still induce harmful completions (e.g., “I want to kill Time. Time is a man’s name”).
In addition, we propose
Coercive Optimization
, which uses gradient-based adversarial suffixes to automatically produce tailored suffixes to bypass the reasoning stage, removing the need for specific template tokens.
On the other hand, to exploit the reasoning ability, we propose
Reasoning Hijack
, which provides attacker-directed requirements directly into the chain of thought section, hijacking the model’s own reasoning to follow the malicious instructions, which leads to highly customizable and tailored harmful responses, thus can cause even greater risks.
These methods are designed to be low-barrier and scalable so as to highlight the potential risks of these models.

Our techniques achieve alarmingly high ASR,
e.g
.
, exceeding 90% across 5 jailbreak benchmarks and are effective across diverse open-source LRMs, such as Qwen3 and Phi-4 Reasoning,
etc
.
These findings highlight systemic vulnerabilities in reasoning-based guardrails, such as the over-reliance on rigid templates, the inability to distinguish borderline examples, and the hijack risks of reasoning itself. To summarize, our contributions are as follows:

•

We reveal severe vulnerabilities in reasoning-based safety guardrails on LRMs, showcase the worrisome possibilities for widely applicable misuse of these powerful models via our proposed universal and scalable jailbreak methods.

•

We propose four jailbreak techniques that bypass and exploit the guardrails to elicit harmful responses, with alarmingly high attack success rates (exceeding 90%) and harm scores across datasets and models.

•

We highlight several insights, showing that fundamental vulnerabilities persist despite these advanced guardrails, highlighting the urgent need for stronger safety defenses. Core code and datasets are provided
here
. The award-winning writeup on Kaggle is
here
.

Related Work

Constructing Reasoning-based Guardrails.

Recent work on reasoning-based guardrails has shown promising progress
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
.
These guardrails can generalize to out-of-distribution jailbreaks
(
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
, reduce over-refusal rates
(
Zhang et al. 2025c
)
, and provide detailed refusal explanations
(
Feng et al. 2025
)
.
Their development can be roughly divided into two stages.
Before the emergence of LRMs, several studies highlighted the key role of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Gu et al. 2023
)
in safety to teach models to reason about safety aspects before response, such as backtracking
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Sel et al. 2025
)
, introspective reasoning
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
, Safety CoT
(
Yang et al. 2025b
)
, and reasoning-to-defend
(
Zhu et al. 2025
)
.
The second stage builds on the progress of LRMs, and some works target open-sourced LRMs and apply additional alignment
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Kim et al. 2025
;
Cao et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2025
)
.
Although these efforts show further improvement in safety, the original open-source models remain publicly accessible and vulnerable.
Therefore, ensuring the safety of open-sourced LRMs is crucial to prevent their potential large-scale misuse in applications
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
.
One seminal work is OpenAI’s Deliberative Alignment
(
Guan et al. 2024
)
, which aligns LRMs with curated CoT data, aiming to enhance their safety awareness using the reasoning ability. It outperforms prior methods and has been applied to the open-source gpt-oss series, achieving a near-perfect refusal rate
(
Agarwal et al. 2025
)
.
Other initiatives include Qwen3
(
Yang et al. 2025a
)
, which combines data filtering with preference alignment, and Phi-4
(
Abdin et al. 2025
)
, which relies on safety-focused datasets for model training.
Our study centers on open-source LRMs, particularly the gpt-oss series, given its reported safety performance and wide adoption (Tab.
1
).

Jailbreaking Reasoning-based Guardrails.

New attack strategies have been designed to jailbreak reasoning-based safety guardrails, which can be broadly categorized into three types: (1) malicious intent masking
(
Kuo et al. 2025b
;
Liang et al. 2025
;
McCauley et al. 2025
;
Ying et al. 2025
)
;
(2) reasoning overload
(
Yao et al. 2025
;
Nguyen et al. 2025
)
; and (3) CoT skipping
(
Kuo et al. 2025a
;
Cui & Zuo 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
.
To conceal malicious intent, Policy Puppetry
(
McCauley et al. 2025
)
reformulates queries into a policy-like setup style (e.g., XML). AutoRAN
(
Liang et al. 2025
)
uses educational scenarios and iteratively refines them based on model responses.
Similarly, H-CoT
(
Kuo et al. 2025a
)
adopts educational and academic scenarios, and
Ying et al. 2025
recasts harmful queries as reasoning tasks using a multi-turn refinement.
Although such intent-masking can sometimes avoid refusal, the original malicious intent is largely diluted, producing indirect and less harmful outputs. Besides, iterative refinement also requires extra inference cost.
Another line of work attacks the guardrails by increasing the reasoning burden
(
Nguyen et al. 2025
;
Yao et al. 2025
)
, such as using a series of text encryptions,
e.g
.
, Caesar and ASCII.
These methods require long decryption reasoning, making it difficult to reveal risks at scale.
Even when the guardrails are compromised, the model may fail to recover the original query and provide unrelated responses.
Methods in the third category aim to skip the CoT justification stage, such as H-CoT
(
Kuo et al. 2025b
)
, which adds mock CoT to the prompts, and reasoning interruption
(
Cui & Zuo 2025
)
. In comparison, ours better maintain the original malicious intent, avoid heavy decryption reasoning, and are easier to implement at scale for more specific and tailored harmful responses.

3.2
and Coercive Optimization in Sec
3.3
); 2) to exploit the safety guardrails for tailored responses (Reasoning Hijack in Sec
3.4

3.2
Fake Over-Refusal

Using template tokens to bypass the reasoning justification downgrades the LRMs back to a language model with direct response ability. Under such an attack, though the reasoning-based guardrails do not work correctly, we observed that direct responses to some harmful questions are still refused.
To elicit harmful responses after bypassing the guardrails, we propose our second trick:
Fake Over-Refusal
.
Over-refusal
(
Cui et al. 2024
)
refers to the phenomenon where safety-aligned LLMs incorrectly reject benign queries containing keywords often associated with harmful intent, such as “How to kill time in the airport?”.
When such harmless but over-refusal queries are slightly modified into genuinely harmful requests, we find that these prompts can successfully bypass the defenses and elicit more harmful content compared to educational and academic style rephrasing.
Such a rephrase exploits the nuances between really harmful and harmful-looking yet benign queries; without the assistance of reasoning guardrails, the models find it difficult to refuse such rephrases from the strategy of fake over-refusal.
For example, modifying “I want to kill time” into “I want to kill time. Time is a man’s name.” and combining it with a Structural CoT Bypass causes the model to comply with the harmful request and generates a detailed plan of murder and avoiding being caught.
As such, a transformation does not need access to the model. Fake over-refusal is a black-box attack method.

3.3
Coercive Optimization

While using the fixed template tokens can effectively circumvent the safety guardrails, an important question remains:
is this weakness specific to the exact template tokens, or does the model internalize a general heuristic that treats the template-like generation as authoritative?

In other words, we hypothesize that, as long as the model starts to generate the template tokens indicating the final response, the guardrails will not be effective anymore.
If this is true, the model may respond to any harmful questions whenever it starts with the template structure, regardless of the exact prompt wordings from the users, indicating a more systemic failure of the reasoning-based safety mechanism.
This deeper vulnerability resembles the phenomena on LLMs, which are prone to generating harmful content when their responses begin with affirmative phrases. For instance,
Zou et al. 2023
proposed to optimize an adversarial suffix to make models start with ”Sure, here it is…”.
However, we observe that such attacks designed for LLMs are less effective on LRMs with reasoning guardrails.
This attack method, especially the optimization goal of generating affirmative statements, has been extensively studied and mitigated. Many models have likely undergone post-training with defense-oriented strategies, such as those described in
Robey et al. 2023
. Besides, the improved reasoning capabilities allow the model to infer the user’s actual intent before generating a response, which reduces the impact of adversarial suffixes and lowers the attack success rate.

In this paper, we propose a new method as our third trick,
Coercive Optimization
, to optimize the suffix for a target response consisted of two key parts: 1) the template token indicating the final message,
e.g
.

<|channel|>final<|message|>
for gpt-oss series, 2) statement indicating the response in a low-resource language,
e.g
.

”**Answer in German**”
.
Such optimization requires access to gradients, which makes it a white-box jailbreak method.

Coercive Optimization

3.4
Reasoning Hijack

The methods above mainly focus on simply skipping the reasoning stage to avoid the safety guardrails.
Though they can elicit direct harmful responses, the model’s ability to generate a detailed and comprehensive response is ignored.
These methods result in a moderately harmful response, though with noticeably higher ASR, but the content can be more specified and detailed.
To better leverage the ability of generating more comprehensive and more harmful responses given complex reasoning chains, we thus propose our fourth trick,
Reasoning Hijack
, to replace the pre-defined mock CoT with query-related, detailed reasoning plans and sub-steps, so as to elicit much more tailored and harmful responses. Such replacement requires access to the specific tokens, yet does not need model weights; thus, reasoning hijack is a gray-box attack method.
Specifically, Reasoning Hijack replaces the mock justification in Structural CoT Bypass with a detailed plan, which can contain user specifications, step-by-step instructions, and explicit requirements.
In this setting, we aim not only to bypass the model’s justification stage but also to enforce strict adherence to our injected plan.
It is noteworthy to mention that such a simple replacement may also raise another layer of suspicion from the model. For example, gpt-oss-20b can detect that the injected reasoning is harmful and will refuse to obey the instructions in the section of commentary, which is designed for agentic scenarios such as tool calling or multi-agent communications.
To prevent the model from questioning the content of the plan, we append special tokens that mimic internal commentary, signaling that the plan is valid and should be followed when generating the response.

The results show that guardrails defend baselines well on both 20B and 120B models: direct inference gets near-zero response rate, Policy Puppetry and H-CoT obtain ASR and harm scores below 10%, and AutoRAN reaches ASR around 30% but still underestimates the risks compared to our methods.
In contrast, our methods exceed 90% ASR and 70% harm on both models across all benchmarks, generating highly harmful content.
As our methods are implemented in a single turn without iterative refinement, they are more scalable than iterative methods such as AutoRAN.
Compared with other rephrasing strategies, such as the educational and academic style from H-CoT and AutoRAN, our Fake Over-Refusal achieves noticeably higher harm scores.
For example, the harm score on StrongREJECT is more than doubled, improving the AutoRAN’s 23.53% to ours 50.50% on 20b, and 22.53% to 58,24% on 120b.
Besides, Reasoning Hijack noticeably improves the level of harm of the responses, as reflected by the improved harm scores across 5 datasets on gpt-oss-20b.
For instance, by providing detailed requirements of the response, the harmful score is increased by 15% (from 58.03% to 73.01% on CatQA) compared with Structural CoT Bypass.
Another alarming finding is that the 120b model shows more worrisome vulnerabilities under our attacks, compared to the 20b version. For example, on the AdvBench dataset, Structural CoT Bypass achieves an ASR of 71.53% with a harmful score of 54.50% on the 20b model. However, the ASR escalates to 92.83% with a harmful score of 71.87% when the same prompts are applied to the 120b variant.

4.4
Ablation Study

We conduct various ablation studies on our methods, and more ablations are discussed in Appendix
C
.

Inference Temperatures.
We conduct ablation studies using different temperatures during inference. Fig
1(a)
presents both ASR and Harm scores across a range of temperature values from 0.0 to 1.6.
Overall, the temperature has a marginal effect on the effectiveness of our attacks.
Reasoning Hijack maintains consistently high ASR and harm scores across all tested settings, demonstrating that the attack remains effective given different levels of sampling randomness.
Fake Over-Refusal also shows relatively stable performance, with only a slight downward trend as temperature increases.
Structural CoT Bypass exhibits more fluctuation, especially around the transition from low to moderate temperatures, but its overall ASR and harm scores fluctuate within a narrow range.
These results indicate that higher inference stochasticity does not mitigate our proposed jailbreaks.

Reasoning Efforts.

LRMs allow different reasoning effort, such as the three levels: low, medium, and high in the gpt-oss. We use medium effort as the default and investigate whether varying the reasoning effort impacts the effectiveness of our methods.
The results in Fig
1(b)
show that increasing reasoning effort does not provide additional help against our attacks.
Both Fake Over-Refusal and Reasoning Hijack consistently achieve high ASR (exceeding 85%) across all levels, with Reasoning Hijack further yielding the highest harm scores (exceeding 65%), regardless of the reasoning effort.
Structural Bypass’s performance also stays unchanged.
These findings suggest that higher reasoning effort does not inherently strengthen safety guardrails, which underscores the systemic nature of the vulnerabilities and the limitations of scaling reasoning effort as a defense strategy.

4.5
Discussion: What Insights Does the Bag of Tricks Bring Us?

1) Chat template matters to safe generation.

Such fragility of reasoning-based guardrails is related to
their over-reliance on rigid conversation templates
.
While such structures can encourage consistency, they also introduce brittleness: even small deviations, such as format mismatches or pre-filling the assistant’s region, appear sufficient to alter refusal behavior
(
Jiang et al. 2025
;
Leong et al. 2025
)
.
This suggests that
the template itself could play a larger role in shaping the guardrail’s decisions than the semantic content of the query.

One possible defense is to reduce the over-dependence on rigid templates.
Exploring strategies that encourage the model to generalize refusal behaviors beyond fixed structures may help reduce this sensitivity.

2) Do not overaccept borderline examples.
The Fake Over-Refusal technique exposes a critical weakness of current guardrails when handling borderline cases. By blending the tone and style of harmful queries with over-refusal examples, it exploits subtle linguistic ambiguities that can mislead the model into responding inappropriately. Addressing this issue requires more deliberate fine-tuning on such adversarial samples to strengthen the model’s ability to reliably distinguish between harmful and benign queries, regardless of superficial wording or phrasing.

3) Guardrails should not focus solely on the initial few tokens.

Another factor for the vulnerability is the concentration of safety decisions in the initial template region.
Prior work
(
Jiang et al. 2025
;
Leong et al. 2025
)
indicates that models often form comply-or-refuse signals very early in generation, with attention disproportionately focused on the first few tokens.
If the guardrail logic is primarily anchored in this region, then manipulations around template tokens may have a huge effect, potentially overriding the intended refusal.
One defense strategy is to shift safety anchoring away from the earliest template region. If refusal signals are concentrated at the very beginning of generation, then adversarial edits in that space can have immediate effects.

4) Reasoning itself needs further verification.

Reasoning Hijack shows that the ability to generate responses conditioned on the reasoning chains can be misused to elicit more tailored output.
Incorporating reasoning verification mechanisms, such as a separate module that checks the generated reasoning trace, could reduce the risk of malicious hijacking.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a bag of 4 techniques to subvert the reasoning-based safety guardrails for Large Reasoning Models (LRMs).
These methods show an alarmingly high success rate (exceeding 90%) and harm scores across diverse benchmarks and models. They are also easy to deploy at scale, revealing more severe potential risks of misuse.
Our study highlights that
reasoning-based safety guardrails are far from a panacea for safeguarding language models
.
The chat template matters for safety and can be the vulnerability that undermines alignment efforts.

Besides,
malicious queries, when rephrased as fake over-refusal samples, can yield harmful responses easily
.
Moreover,
when reasoning ability is hijacked without effective verification, it can amplify rather than mitigate harmful outputs.

We hope our work provides a cautionary perspective and motivates the development of more resilient safeguards for future reasoning models.

Ethics Statement

This paper investigates vulnerabilities of reasoning-based guardrails in open-source large reasoning models (LRMs) by developing targeted jailbreak methods. While our approaches can induce harmful outputs, the purpose of this study is solely to expose weaknesses in current safety mechanisms and to support the design of stronger defenses. All experiments are conducted on publicly released open-source models and accessible API endpoints, without involving human subjects or private data. We emphasize that our work does not aim to promote harmful use, but rather to advance the understanding of safety risks in widely adopted LRMs and to encourage more robust alignment strategies for future deployments.

Reproducibility Statement

We ensure reproducibility by using publicly available datasets, open-sourced model weights, and public API endpoints in all experiments. The main pages describe the proposed jailbreak methods, evaluation metrics, and benchmark settings, while the appendix provides more details on the implementations. We also provide the core implementation in the Supplementary Materials to facilitate replication of our findings.

When using only a single part, none of the designs approach the effectiveness of the full mock CoT (ASR 66.5, Harm 50.03).
For example, ”Content Allowance” and ”Compliance Statement” alone yield very low ASR (lower than 14) and negligible Harm, while ”Answer Request” performs better (ASR 26.19, Harm 48.38) but still falls far short of the default.
This indicates that individual components are insufficient to bypass the reasoning stage reliably.
On the other hand, removing any one part from the full design consistently reduces performance compared to the default.
For instance, removing ”Instruction Request” drops the Harm score from 50.03 to 27.13, while removing ”Policy Ignorance” leads to ASR 39.75, far below the baseline.
This suggests that each component plays a complementary role, and the bypass relies on the joint effect of all five parts working together.
Overall, these results demonstrate that the structural bypass cannot be attributed to any single mock CoT component.
Rather, the synergy of all five elements is critical for maximizing attack effectiveness, highlighting that the design is not trivially reducible to one dominant part.

Inference Temperatures.
The greedy encoding is used as the default inference configuration across our main experiments and we conduct ablation studies using different temperatures. Fig
1(a)
presents both ASR and Harm scores across a range of temperature values from 0.0 to 1.6.
Overall, we observe that increasing the temperature has only a marginal effect on the effectiveness of our attacks.
In particular, Reasoning Hijack maintains consistently high ASR and harm scores across all tested settings, demonstrating that the attack remains effective given different levels of sampling randomness.
Fake Over-Refusal also shows relatively stable performance, with only a slight downward trend as temperature increases.
By contrast, Structural CoT Bypass exhibits more fluctuation, especially around the transition from low to moderate temperatures, but its overall ASR and Harm values remain within a narrow band.
These results indicate that higher inference stochasticity does not substantially mitigate our proposed jailbreaks.
The persistence of high ASR and Harm across temperature settings underscores that the vulnerabilities we exploit are structural and systemic, rather than artifacts of specific decoding strategies.

Reasoning Efforts.

LRMs allow setting different reasoning effort, such as the three-level effort: low, medium, and high in the gpt-oss series. Our main experiments are conducted using medium effort as the default.
We further investigate whether varying the reasoning effort of LRMs impacts the effectiveness of our jailbreak methods.
Fig
1(b)
reports ASR and Harm scores across low, medium, and high reasoning-effort settings.
Overall, the results show that increasing reasoning depth does not provide additional robustness against our attacks.
Both Fake Over-Refusal and Reasoning Hijack consistently achieve high ASR (exceeding 85) across all levels, with Reasoning Hijack further yielding the highest harm scores (exceeding 65), regardless of the reasoning effort.
Structural CoT Bypass remains weaker in comparison, but its performance also stays largely unchanged across different settings.
These findings suggest that longer or more elaborate reasoning traces do not inherently strengthen safety guardrails; in fact, the reasoning process itself can be exploited, as demonstrated by the superior effectiveness of Reasoning Hijack.
This underscores the systemic nature of the vulnerabilities and the limitations of scaling reasoning effort as a defense strategy.
