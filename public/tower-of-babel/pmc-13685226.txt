FabHLH130-FaSPS1/FaINV positively contributes to the sugar accumulation in strawberry fruits
Authors: Yanhong Song, Lifeng Liu, Luyi Zhao, Wenting Zheng, Pan Song, Xia Zhao, Gang Li, Houcheng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoluble sugars play a crucial role as a carbon and energy source, with their content and composition governing the flavor profile of fruits. Yet how the contents of glucose, sucrose, and fructose are regulated remains poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, we identified FabHLH130 as a positive regulator in the sucrose metabolism of ‘Snow Princess’ fruits. We generated FabHLH130 overexpression lines in the octoploid cultivar ‘Benihoppe’. Transcriptome analysis reveals higher expression levels of FaSPS1 and FaINV in the sucrose metabolism pathway related to WT. Moreover, the electrophoretic mobility shift and dual-luciferase assays validated that FabHLH130 directly binds to the promoters of FaSPS1 and FaINV, leading to the positive regulation of their expression and enzyme activity. Compared to the WT, the transgenic lines displayed higher total soluble sugar, glucose, sucrose, and fructose contents.ConclusionsOur findings provide insights into the mechanism by which FabHLH130 is associated with sugar accumulation in strawberry fruits and provide a theoretical foundation for understanding soluble sugar metabolism in plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09621-4.

Introduction

Strawberry (Fragaria 🞪 ananassa) is widely cultivated and popular worldwide, renowned for its flavor, aroma, and nutritional value. For strawberry breeders, priority fruit-related traits, including yield, sweetness, aroma profile, color, and firmness, are crucial, as these attributes collectively drive consumer preference and meet the rigorous demands of modern supply chains [1]. The soluble sugar content in the strawberry fruit is primarily comprised of sucrose, fructose, and glucose [2, 3]. Beyond its fundamental roles as a primary carbon source and metabolic energy provider, sugar functions as a pivotal signaling molecule that governs a spectrum of developmental processes in both vegetative and reproductive morphogenesis [4–9].

Sugar content and composition are key factors influencing fruit flavor and sweetness, and are highly regulated by sugar transport and metabolism during fruit development. Sucrose is the primary product of photosynthesis in higher plants, and sucrose-6-phosphate synthase (SPS) plays a crucial role in fruit sucrose metabolism. Numerous studies have validated that SPS activity is significantly positively correlated with sucrose accumulation during the ripening of various fruits, as observed in sugarcane [10, 11], citrus [12], and apple [13]. In strawberry, recent studies have revealed that FvNAC073 and FvCMB1L antagonistically regulate sucrose accumulation by competitively binding to the FvSPS1 promoter [14]; However, the question of whether transcription factors activate FaSPS1 to regulate octoploid strawberry fruit sugar content still awaits clarification.

The homeostasis of sucrose depends not only on its synthesis but also on the fine-tuned regulation of its catabolism. Invertase (INV), which catalyzes the irreversible hydrolysis of sucrose into glucose and fructose, serves as the core hub enzyme in the catabolic direction of sucrose metabolism. In crops such as maize [15], apple [16] and tomato [17], even slight fluctuations in INV activity can trigger seed developmental defects or significant alterations in fruit sugar composition, suggesting that the spatiotemporal expression of INV must be under rigorous transcriptional regulation. FaDOF1.7 and its interactor FaDOF1.5 form a nuclear transcriptional complex that binds DOF elements in promoters of sugar-metabolism genes (e.g., FaSUS1/2 and FaCWINV1), cooperatively enhancing their expression and significantly promoting glucose, fructose, and soluble sugar accumulation [18]. Although the synthetic (SPS) and catabolic (INV) sides of sucrose metabolism require precise coordination during fruit development, no transcription factor has been reported to date that simultaneously regulates both in strawberry. Basic Helix-Loop-Helix (bHLH) transcription factors constitute the second-largest family of transcription factors in plants. They play critical and diverse roles in plant growth and development, metabolic regulation, and environmental adaptation [19–25]. Recent studies have consistently indicated that bHLH transcription factors play a direct role in various aspects of fruit development and ripening. Recently, several bHLH members have acted as downstream components of hormone signaling pathways, such as ethylene or ABA, regulating the initiation of ripening [26–29]. Moreover, a growing body of evidence highlights their pivotal role in sugar metabolism. DlbHLH68 proteins have been found to directly bind to and regulate the promoters of DlSPS1 genes, leading to an increase in the sucrose content in transgenic longan fruits [30]. The competition between MdMYB305 and MdMYB10 for binding to MdbHLH33 has been shown to fine-tune the balance between sugar and anthocyanin accumulation during fruit ripening [13]. Thereby, the bHLH transcription factor contributes to controlling sugar import, unloading, and metabolic flux in fruits.

In this study, we identified a transcription factor, FabHLH130, which is specifically expressed in white strawberry fruit. The Luciferase assays and EMSA confirmed that FabHLH130 directly activates the expression of sucrose metabolism genes (FaSPS1 and FaINV), elevates their enzyme activities, and consequently promotes sugar accumulation in strawberry fruit. FabHLH130 is the first transcription factor reported to simultaneously target both a sucrose synthase (SPS) and an invertase (INV) in strawberry, revealing a dual-regulation strategy for sugar accumulation. Our discovery advances the understanding of fruit sugar regulatory networks, highlighting the potential of FabHLH130 as a target for fruit quality improvement.

Results

The expression pattern of the bHLH transcription factor in ‘Snow Princess’ fruits during development

In our previous study, we conducted a genome-wide identification of the bHLH transcription factor family of strawberry [31], providing the basis for further investigation of bHLH function. Based on the comparative transcriptome analysis of ‘Benihoppe’ and ‘Snow Princess’ strawberry fruits [32]. We identified DEGs regulating anthocyanin and sugar accumulation. To study the function of the bHLH transcription factors in ‘Snow Princess’ strawberry fruits, we analyzed the bHLH genes in three development stages: middle green stage (S2), turning stage (S5), and fully ripe stage (S6) (Fig. 1A). A total of 184 bHLH genes were identified as expressed during strawberry fruit development (Fig. 1B; Table S1). Eleven bHLH genes reached their maximum expression at the turning stage, with expression levels decreasing afterwards. Notably, the majority of these genes (132 out of 184, 71.74%) showed a peak expression at the middle green stage, which subsequently declined in later stages. Expression levels of the remaining genes increased as the fruit developed. Among these, 15 bHLH genes were gradually up-regulated during fruit maturation (Fig. 1C). Sugar accumulation in fruits primarily occurs during the late stage of fruit development; therefore, selecting genes that are highly expressed at this stage may help identify key factors involved in sugar regulation. Taken together, the distinct expression profiles strongly suggest that FabHLH3 and FabHLH130 are key regulators of fruit development. FabHLH3 is a homologous gene of AtTT8, a gene known to be involved in the flavonoid biosynthetic process and seed coat development [33]. Collectively, our data identify FabHLH130 as a key regulator in the white-flesh cultivar ‘Snow Princess’ receptacles.

Characterization of FabHLH130 in ‘Snow Princess’ fruits

A comprehensive functional exploration of FabHLH130 was undertaken. The analyses of its expression profile showed that FabHLH130-A (FabHLH130 gene copy from subgenome A) was dominantly expressed in the turning fruits and ripening fruits (Fig. 2A). We also found that FabHLH130 was expressed at a low level in root and stem, and was mostly expressed in ripening fruit (Fig. 2B). Additionally, we investigated the nuclear localization of FabHLH130 using subcellular localization assays. The gene is localized to the nucleus, suggesting that it may function as a transcriptional regulator involved in sugar metabolic pathways (Fig. 2C). The C-terminal (249–292 aa) of FabHLH130 forms the HLH region and functions as a dimerization domain. FabHLH130 exhibits transcriptional activity, with the 1–66 aa region responsible for transcriptional activation (Fig. 2D). In conclusion, FabHLH130 contains N-terminal transcriptional activity and is specifically expressed in the late development stage of strawberry fruits.

FabHLH130 participates in the starch and sucrose metabolism in the strawberry receptacles

To investigate the function of FabHLH130, we performed the overexpression of FabHLH130 in ‘Benihoppe’. Compared with the wild type (WT), two transgenic lines (OE_1 and OE_2) exhibited approximately 152-fold and 62-fold increases in FabHLH130 expression, respectively (Fig. 3A). Transcriptome analysis was performed on the WT and OE_1 to identify expression changes of downstream genes. There were 1809 up-regulated genes and 3826 down-regulated genes between WT and OE_1 (Fig. 3B). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were primarily enriched in the plant hormone signal transduction, DNA replication, and starch and sucrose metabolism. Among the top 20 KEGG pathways, we focus on those associated with fruit-related pathways, such as starch and sucrose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis (Fig. 3C). We targeted the starch and sucrose metabolism KEGG pathway as a critical hub for fruit taste. Our transcriptome data revealed genes involved in cell wall metabolism (e.g., BGL, PME) and starch/sucrose signaling (e.g., T6P, SS), suggesting that FabHLH130 may exert a broader influence on fruit carbohydrate metabolism beyond the core sucrose pathway. However, given that SPS and INV represent the rate-limiting and branch-point enzymes directly controlling sucrose pool size, we focused our mechanistic dissection on these two pivotal nodes. (Fig. 3D; Table S2).

As for the remaining two fruit-related pathways, twelve up-regulated genes were enriched in the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway, and five up-regulated genes in flavonoid biosynthesis. Among the twelve DEGs, FaPFKs, FaHXK1s, and FaGAPDHs were involved in the glycolytic process of converting sucrose into 3-bisphosphoglycerate. In contrast, the down-regulated genes were predominantly involved in the later stages of glycolysis, leading to pyruvate formation, its further metabolism, and gluconeogenesis (Fig.6A; Table S3). Within the flavonoid biosynthesis, the expression of CHS and CHI was significantly up-regulated, whereas the expression of DFR, ANS, and C4H was down-regulated. This fact suggests a channeling of metabolic flux into the upstream flavonoid pathway, potentially leading to an accumulation of intermediate compounds such as naringenin chalcone or naringenin (Fig.6B; Table S4). Taken together, our transcriptomic data suggest that FabHLH130 may regulate FaSPS1 and FaINV involved in sucrose metabolism in strawberry fruits (Fig. 3E).

The expression of FaSPS1 and FaINV is regulated by FabHLH130

FabHLH130, whose C-terminal is a classic member of the bHLH binding domain, is known to bind the E-box motif (-CANNTG) in the target gene promoters. The Fa6Cg02868, a cell wall invertase among the five FaINVs, showed the lowest p-value. Analysis revealed that Fa7Ag02772 had the lowest p-value among the four soluble invertase genes. As a result, we carried out the cis-element analysis of Fa6Cg02868 (FaCWIN), Fa7Ag02772 (FaINV), and Fa2Cg01329 (FaSPS1). The results revealed that there were five, two, and three E-box motifs in the promoters of FaCWIN, FaINV, and FaSPS1, respectively (Fig. S2; Table S5). The dual luciferase (Dual-LUC) assay indicated that FabHLH130 activates the expression of FaSPS1 and FaINV by binding to their promoter motifs (Fig. 4A and B). However, our Dual-LUC assays showed that FabHLH130 did not activate the FaCWIN promoter (Fig. S3). To confirm the direct binding, an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was conducted. Our experiments suggested that FabHLH130 specifically binds to the E-box of FaSPS1 and FaINV promoters (Fig. 4C). The expression of FaSPS1 and FaINV were subsequently validated by qRT-PCR in both OE_1 and OE_2 (Fig. S5), indicating that their upregulation is not an artifact of a single transformation event. To evaluate the effect of FabHLH130 on enzyme properties, one-way ANOVA was performed for each measured enzyme activity. The transgenic lines show higher SPS and soluble acid invertase (S-AI) activity. The OE_1 had significantly 26.09% higher SPS activity compared to the wild type (F2,6 = 137.91, P < 0.001), and showed a 12.53% increase relative to the wild-type control. Similarly, the soluble acid invertase activities of OE_1 and OE_2 were significantly higher than that in the WT (F₃,6 = 22.9, P = 0.002) (Fig. 4D; Table S6). These findings highlight that FabHLH130 promotes the expression of FaSPS1 and FaINV, and further reveal a positive correlation between gene expression level and enzyme activity.

FabHLH130 positively regulates soluble sugar accumulation in strawberry fruits

Having established that FabHLH130 transcriptionally activates key sugar-metabolizing enzymes (SPS and INV), we next sought to determine the physiological consequence of this regulation on the actual sugar composition of the fruit. Consistent with its role in upregulating these biosynthetic and invertase genes, overexpression of FabHLH130 led to a significant increase in sugar accumulation. Indeed, the mature fruits of transgenic lines exhibited markedly higher concentrations of total soluble sugar, sucrose, glucose, and fructose compared to the wild type (Fig. 5; Table S7). Specifically, the average total soluble sugar content increased from 50.71 mg/g FW in WT to 82.50 and 87.30 mg/g FW in OE lines (F2,6 = 570.03, P < 0.001). The concentrations of all three measured component sugars (glucose, sucrose, and fructose) were significantly higher in the transgenic fruits (Fig. 5B and D, Table S7). The two transgenic lines, despite sharing the phenotype of enhanced sugar accumulation, exhibited opposite effects on total acid content (Fig. 5E). Overall, these data suggest that FabHLH130 positively regulates total soluble sugar accumulation in strawberry fruit.

Discussion

As one of the largest transcription factor families in plants, bHLH proteins play pivotal roles in regulating plant growth, development, and stress responses [13, 34–39]. Genome-wide identification of the bHLH gene family has been extensively conducted in various plants, including wheat [40, 41], rice [42, 43], Arabidopsis thaliana [42, 44], tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) [45, 46], and others. Previously, we identified 113 bHLH TFs in the diploid strawberry Fragaria vesca. In addition, the expression profile of 113 bHLH orthologs was analyzed during fruit development and under hormone treatments in three strawberry varieties (‘Benihoppe’, ‘Xiaobai’, and ‘Snow Princess’) [31]. A total of 376 FabHLHs were identified in Fragaria × ananassa cultivar ‘Camarosa’, which were classified into 24 subfamilies [47]. The ‘Yanli’ haplotype-resolved genome presents 131 FabHLH genes (941 gene sequences of the whole octoploid genome), and a total of 15 FabHLH genes are involved in fruit development based on fruit development transcriptomic data [48].

In this study, a total of 184 bHLH genes were identified as expressed during the three fruit development stages of ‘Snow Princess’ (Fig. 1B). According to the expression pattern, these genes were divided into three groups. Genes in group 3 exhibited minimal expression early in fruit development, with their expression peaking during the mid and late stages. This temporal pattern corresponds to the phase of active accumulation of primary metabolites in the fruit. Among these genes, bHLH3 and bHLH130 exhibited the highest fold changes in group 3. FaMYB123 and FabHLH3 function well to regulate the flavonoid biosynthesis during strawberry ripening [48]. Protein kinase FaSnRK2.6 modulates anthocyanin homeostasis in ripening strawberry fruit by phosphorylating transcription factor FabHLH3 [49]. Finally, FabHLH130 was selected as a candidate gene in the development of white-fruit strawberry. Several bHLH transcription factors have been implicated in fruit sugar metabolism and ripening across different species. In apple, MdbHLH3 activates MdPFPβ expression and coordinates carbohydrate allocation to modulate soluble sugar accumulation [50]. In plum, PsbHLH58 positively regulates sucrose accumulation by activating PsSUS4 expression [51]. FabHLH130 simultaneously targets both the synthetic (FaSPS1) and catabolic (FaINV) branches of sucrose metabolism. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a bHLH transcription factor that directly activates two oppositely directed enzymes within the same metabolic pathway in strawberry.

Sweetness is one of the determining factors of fruit flavor and directly influences the eating quality of the fruit and consumer acceptance [52, 53]. In fruit breeding, sweetness is not only a key indicator for evaluating the intrinsic quality of the fruit, but also a crucial basis for determining the market competitiveness and economic value of a variety. In strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa), flavor quality and perceived sweetness are largely governed by the balance between its sugar and organic acid contents [54]. Glucose was the predominant sugar at the whole berry development stages in both ‘Festival’ and ‘Ventana’ cultivars. The fructose and sucrose contents were comparable, both lower than the glucose level [55]. Sugar metabolism, mostly the sucrose metabolism, provides both energy and carbon skeletons for vital cellular processes [56]. The key enzyme controlling sucrose biosynthesis is Sucrose phosphate synthase (SPS) in plants [57]. SPS catalyzes the conversion of fructose 6-phosphate (F6P) and UDP-glucose (UDPG) into sucrose 6-phosphate (S6P). The SPS activity is related to sucrose accumulation in plants [58]. Sucrose content in sweet sorghum stalks was positively correlated with SPS protein expression. As the plant developed, SPS expression increased progressively, paralleling the upward trend in sucrose accumulation [59].

Invertase irreversibly catalyzes the hydrolysis of sucrose into fructose and glucose, which then serve as crucial energy sources and signaling molecules in plant growth, stress response, carbohydrate allocation, and source/sink regulation [60, 61]. Fruit sugar accumulation is a complex and finely regulated process involving hormones, transcription factors, kinases, and other regulatory components, among which WRKY, MYB, NAC, bHLH, and bZIP transcription factors play a crucial role. In pitaya fruit, HpWRKY3 activates the expression of HpINV2 and HpSuS1 to increase sugar accumulation [62]; CmMYB44 represses CmSPS1 transcriptional level and further suppresses sucrose accumulation in oriental melon fruit [63]. FaNAC035 up-regulates the expression of FaSPS1 and FaSuS genes [64]. Our research identified the transcription factor FabHLH130, which upregulates the expression of both FaSPS1 and FaINV, thereby enhancing the accumulation of sucrose, fructose, and glucose in strawberry fruit.

Notably, our previous transcriptome analysis showed that FabHLH130 transcripts were barely detectable in the three stages (S2, S5 and S6) of ‘Benihoppe’ fruits (Table S8), supporting its fruit-specific expression pattern of ‘Snow Princess’. This observation strongly suggests that the regulatory network of FabHLH130 is highly dependent on the specific genetic background and developmental context of strawberries. Given that our functional characterization was conducted using a transgenic overexpression system in the strawberry cultivar ‘Benihoppe’, we acknowledge that such ectopic expression may not fully reproduce the endogenous regulatory circuitry. Due to the relatively low transformation efficiency of the ‘Benihoppe’ strawberry system, we obtained two independent transgenic lines. Nevertheless, the consistent phenotypes and gene expression patterns observed in both lines, together with the altered sugar accumulation resulting from FabHLH130 overexpression line. Future efforts to generate additional transgenic lines in both ‘Benihoppe’ and ‘Snow Princess’ backgrounds will be valuable to further validate these findings.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified FabHLH130 as a potential positive regulator of sugar accumulation in strawberry fruit through transcriptomic analysis of ‘Snow Princess’ at three developmental stages. FabHLH130-A exhibited specific expression in the middle-green fruits and turning fruits. Overexpression of FabHLH130 in ‘Benihoppe’ led to elevated transcript levels of FaSPS1 and FaINV, accompanied by increased SPS and S-AI enzyme activities, as well as significantly higher contents of total soluble sugars, glucose, sucrose, and fructose. Furthermore, we indicated that FabHLH130 directly binds to the promoters of FaSPS1 and FaINV, suggesting a transcriptional regulatory mechanism. Collectively, these findings suggest that FabHLH130 positively regulates sugar accumulation, at least under overexpression conditions. Future studies involving stable overexpression or CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout specifically in the ‘Snow Princess’ background will be required to fully validate its endogenous function and to determine whether this regulatory module is conserved across strawberry cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growing conditions

The ‘Snow Princess’ strawberry was cultivated in a greenhouse between September 2022 and May 2023, with normal water and fertilizer management. In March 2023, we collected the roots, stems, leaves, and three developmental stages of fruits, and then immediately snap-frozen them in liquid nitrogen. All plant tissues were preserved at -80°C for RT-qPCR analysis. The wild type and transgenic line were grown in September 2023 and 2024 with Standard agronomic practices for irrigation and fertilization.

Subcellular localization

We cloned the coding sequences of FabHLH130 lacking the termination codon using specific primers and integrated it into the expression vector pBI121-GFP, namely the construct pBI121-FabHLH130-GFP. The recombinant vector was then transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. The collected bacteria were resuspended in a buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl₂, 150 µM AS) and transiently expressed in tobacco leaf epidermal cells via Agrobacterium infiltration. After 72 h of incubation, the infiltrated tobacco leaves were analyzed using a confocal fluorescence microscope. A detailed list of primers used for vector construction is provided in Supplementary Table S9.

RT-qPCR analysis

We performed real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) using the LightCycler 480 system (Roche) with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression levels were analyzed using the 2–ΔΔCT method. The primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S9.

Transcription activity assay

Transcriptional activation activity of FabHLH130 was assessed using a yeast-based assay system. The coding sequence of FabHLH130 was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector. The recombinant pGBKT7-FabHLH130, along with the negative control (pGBKT7), was transformed into the Saccharomyces cerevisiae reporter strain AH109. Transformants were selected on SD/-Trp medium. The transformants activity was then evaluated by spotting yeast cultures onto selective SD/-Trp and SD/-Trp-His-Ade plates supplemented with X-α-Gal, followed by monitoring cell growth and the production of blue pigment.

Transformation in ‘Benihoppe’

Following the methods of Oosumi et al. [65] and Kim et al. [66], the strawberry cultivar ‘Benihoppe’ sterile seedlings were transformed using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Leaves from one-month-old subcultured strawberry plants were taken as explants and cut into small pieces measuring 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm. The activated Agrobacterium containing the target gene was expanded, then centrifuged at 6,000 r·min⁻¹ for 5 min. The bacterial pellet was collected and resuspended in MS liquid medium supplemented with 100 µmol·L⁻¹ acetosyringone, adjusting the OD600 to approximately 0.4. The prepared leaf pieces were added and gently shaken on a shaker for 30 min, then transferred to co-culture medium (MS 4.43 g·L⁻¹ + TDZ 4.0 mg·L⁻¹ + IBA 0.3 mg·L⁻¹ + sucrose 30 g·L⁻¹) for dark culture. After 2–3 days, they were transferred to selection medium (MS 4.43 g·L⁻¹ +TDZ 4.0 mg·L⁻¹ + IBA 0.3 mg·L⁻¹ + Kan50 mg·L⁻¹ + Carb 250 mg·L⁻¹ + Tim 250 mg·L⁻¹ + sucrose 30 g·L⁻¹) for selective culture, and maintained in dark conditions for 3 weeks until callus formation. Subculturing was performed every 3 weeks until green shoots differentiated. The elongated shoots were transferred to rooting medium (1/2MS 4.43 g·L⁻¹ + IBA 0.1 mg·L⁻¹ + Kan 25 mg·L⁻¹ + Carb 250 mg·L⁻¹ + Tim 250 mg·L⁻¹ + sucrose 20 g·L⁻¹) for further growth.

Genomic DNA was extracted from 0.05 g of leaves of regenerated plants. PCR amplification was performed using the vector-specific primers 35 S and FabHLH130-R. Plants that yielded the target fragment were preliminarily identified as positive transformants.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

we performed RNA-seq on pooled fruit samples from one representative overexpression line (OE_1) and wild-type (WT) controls at the mature stage, with three biological replicates (each replicate consisted of fruits pooled from 3 individual plants). Although only OE_1 was used for transcriptome profiling, OE_2 was reserved for independent validation of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by qRT-PCR (see Figure S5), ensuring that the key regulatory targets (FaSPS1 and FaINV) were confirmed in an independent transgenic line. The purity and concentration of the RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Library sequencing was carried out on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (paired-end 150 bp), aiming for 6 Gb of raw data per sample. The resulting reads were aligned to the strawberry Benihoppe reference genome [67] using Hisat2 with default parameters. Gene expression levels were quantified using Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped fragments (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with the DESeq2 R package (version 1.18.0), applying a screening threshold of P < 0.05 and a fold change > 2. The RNA-seq experiment included three biological replicates per sample.

Dual-luciferase assay

We cloned the coding sequence (CDS) of FabHLH130 into the pBI121-GFP vector and introduced the 1000-base-pair promoter fragment of FaSPS1, FaCWIN, and FaINV into the pGreenII-0800-LUC vector. Subsequently, we transformed pBI121-FabHLH130-GFP into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and pGreenII 0800-FaSPS1/ FaCWIN/FaINV-LUC into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 (with pSoup). We then co-infiltrated these strains into the leaves of 4-week-old tobacco plants. At 72 h post infiltration, we collected samples from the infiltrated sites, with imaging performed on a multifunctional automated chemiluminescence/ fluorescence imaging analysis system.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

The CDS of FabHLH130 was cloned into the pGEX-4T-1 vector to generate a FabHLH130-GST fusion protein. The promoter region of FaSPS1/FaINV was labeled with a biotinylated probe. The FabHLH130-GST protein was incubated with the labeled probe at 25 °C for 30 min, and the resulting complexes were then separated by electrophoresis on a 6% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel in 0.5× TBE buffer. An unlabeled probe was used as a cold competitor, and the results were visualized using a ChemiDoc XRS system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). The primers were listed in Table S9.

Enzyme activity measurement

Sucrose phosphate synthase catalyzes the formation of sucrose phosphate from fructose-6-phosphate. The sucrose phosphate reacts with resorcinol to produce a color change, with a characteristic absorption peak at 480 nm. The enzyme activity is proportional to the color intensity. In this experiment, the Sucrose Phosphate Synthase (SPS) Activity Assay Kit (BC0600, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used for detection. Soluble Acid Invertase (S-AI) catalyzes the degradation of sucrose to produce reducing sugars, which then react with 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid to form a brown-red amino compound. This compound has a characteristic absorption peak at 540 nm, and within a certain range, the rate of increase in absorbance at 540 nm is proportional to S-AI activity. In this experiment, the Soluble Acid Invertase (S-AI) Activity Assay Kit (BC2470, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used for detection. Data were analyzed using R (version 4.3.1). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test was performed using the agricolae package. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Total sugar content

This study used the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method to determine the content of reducing sugars (RS) in strawberry pulp. The principle is that reducing sugars can reduce DNS reagent under alkaline conditions, producing a red-brown product with characteristic absorption at 540 nm, which can be measured using a microplate spectrophotometer. The experiment followed the instructions of the BC2710 kit from Beijing Solarbio: first, a series of glucose solutions with concentrations of 1, 0.8, 0.5, 0.2, and 0.1 mg/ml were used as standards to create a standard curve, which showed good linearity with a regression equation of y = 0.0211x − 0.0088 and a correlation coefficient (R²) of 0.9997. Based on this, the RS content in the samples can be calculated using the absorbance values and the standard curve.

Determination of glucose, sucrose, and fructose contents

The contents of glucose, sucrose, and fructose were determined using the Glucose Assay Kit (GOD-POD method; BC2505, Solarbio, Beijing, China), the Plant Fructose (FT) Content Assay Kit (BC2450, Solarbio, Beijing, China), and the Plant Sucrose Content Assay Kit (BC2460, Solarbio, Beijing, China), respectively. Absorbance was measured with a microplate reader, and the concentrations were calculated based on their respective standard curves.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means of at least three independent experiments or three biological replicates. Statistical analysis of enzyme activities, sugar and total acid contents was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test for multiple comparisons among groups. Significance levels are indicated by different lowercase letters. The statistical significance of the relative expression of genes between WT and transgenic lines was determined using a t-test, with P ≤ 0.05 (*) and P ≤ 0.01 (**).
