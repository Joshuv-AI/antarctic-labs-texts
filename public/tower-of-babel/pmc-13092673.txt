Protective effects of irisin in sarcopenia: a promising treatment
Authors: Yixiao Chen, Guoqing Li, Feng Gao, Ruijiang Li, Minghui Yang, Yufeng Ge, Xinbao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSarcopenia is an age-related progressive muscle degeneration condition characterized by loss of muscle mass, muscle strength, and poor physical function. Its prevalence and mortality rates continue to rise with advancing age, significantly impairing patients' quality of life. The pathogenesis of sarcopenia involves multiple pathophysiological processes, including imbalanced protein catabolism, cell death, mitochondrial dysfunction, and various cellular signaling pathways. Therefore, it is crucial to identify potential therapeutic targets and treatments for sarcopenia. As an exercise-induced myokine, irisin has shown great potential in maintaining skeletal muscle health. In this review, we focus on the relationship between irisin and sarcopenia, delving into existing research to elucidate irisin's mechanisms of action in sarcopenia—including its effects on protein catabolism, cell death, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular signaling, and muscle cell proliferation and differentiation. We provide insights into irisin as a therapeutic intervention for sarcopenia and provide essential evidence to support its clinical application in sarcopenia treatment.The Translational Potential of this Article.This review highlights the mechanism of action of irisin in sarcopenia and its potential as a therapeutic candidate for sarcopenia in the future.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Irisin, Protein metabolic balance, Treatment, Cell death, Signaling pathway

Introduction

Skeletal muscle, constituting approximately 40% of total body weight, serves as the body's primary protein reservoir and plays a pivotal role in locomotion and energy metabolism. Furthermore, as a major energy metabolic tissue, it actively participates in the uptake, utilization, and storage of energy substrates, including glucose, lipids, and amino acids [1]. However, various pathological and physiological conditions - such as aging, starvation, cancer, diabetes, prolonged immobilization, and denervation - disrupt the delicate equilibrium between protein synthesis and degradation in skeletal muscle. This imbalance leads to progressive loss of muscle mass and strength, ultimately resulting in muscle atrophy, clinically termed sarcopenia [2,3]. The aging process particularly disturbs the balance between muscle protein anabolism and catabolism, explaining the characteristic progressive decline in skeletal muscle mass and strength observed during normal aging [4]. Notably, sarcopenia may further exacerbate bone fragility and osteopenia, thereby increasing the risk of osteoporosis (OP). The concurrent diagnosis of sarcopenia and osteopenia/OP has been designated as osteosarcopenia or sarco-osteoporosis [5]. As a result, morbidity (falls, fractures, and hospitalizations) as well as mortality rates are further increase in the elderly [6]. Clinical investigations have established that skeletal muscle density not only serves as an independent predictor for recurrent hip fractures, but also hip muscle size and density are significantly associated with mortality in elderly patients with hip fracture [7,8]. These findings underscore that age-related skeletal muscle deterioration adversely impacts quality of life, and is associated with increased mortality. Current management strategies for sarcopenia encompass non-pharmacological interventions (physical exercise and nutritional supplementation) and pharmacological approaches. However, there is still no effective drug approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of sarcopenia [9]. Therefore, sarcopenia has become an important clinical problem that needs to be solved urgently, along with the development of new targets and drugs to treat sarcopenia.

In their seminal 2012 study, Bostrom et al. identified irisin as a novel exercise-induced myokine that can mediate muscle peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) activation and promotes white adipose tissue (WAT) browning, attenuating diet-induced obesity and improving insulin sensitivity [10]. In addition, irisin has garnered significant attention for its capacity to enhance muscle mass and strength and may be an ideal therapeutic strategy for the treatment of sarcopenia [11]. This bioactive peptide exhibits pleiotropic expression across multiple organ systems, with its secretion being primarily stimulated by exercise and cold exposure [12]. Notably, circulating irisin levels exhibit an age-dependent decline and show significant correlations with multiple pathological conditions, including metabolic disorders (type 2 diabetes) [13], cardiovascular diseases [14], neurological impairments [15],as well as musculoskeletal degenerative conditions such as osteoporosis [16], osteoarthritis [17], and sarcopenia [18]. These effects of irisin may be due to activation of satellite cells and enhancement of protein synthesis [19].

Preclinical evidence demonstrates that FNDC5/irisin deficiency exacerbates age-related muscle atrophy in aged mice, manifesting as impaired grip strength and reduced muscle mass. Conversely, intraperitoneal administration of recombinant irisin effectively reverses sarcopenic phenotypes, restoring muscle strength, mass, and molecular markers [18]. In addition, irisin can also affect cell proliferation and differentiation by regulating a variety of cell signaling pathways, including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), adenosine monophosphate activated protein kinase (AMPK), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) and NF-κB [20]. These findings demonstrated that irisin act as a potent regulator of skeletal muscle development, functional maintenance, and atrophy prevention, highlighting its therapeutic potential for muscle-wasting disorders.

Although relevant reviews have discussed the role of irisin in sarcopenia, few have systematically integrated the multifaceted effects of irisin in sarcopenia, especially the cell death pathways involved in irisin (such as ferroptosis and pyroptosis), and the regulatory role of irisin in protein metabolism, mitochondrial function, signaling pathways, and satellite cell function. This review synthesises current evidence to highlight the potential of irisin not only as a biomarker but also as a multitargeted therapeutic agent. Sarcopenia definition and influence factors

Definition of sarcopenia

Sarcopenia, a term originally coined by Rosenberg in the 1980s, describes age-related muscle wasting [21]. The disease is currently defined as an age-related progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and physical function. It is commonly associated with increased risks of falls, fractures, diminished activities of daily living, loss of independence, and mortality [22]. Muscle mass and strength generally peak in early adulthood followed by gradual decline after age 40, with accelerated deterioration commencing around age 50 [23]. Longitudinal studies reveal that after age 75, women experience an annual muscle mass reduction of 0.64-0.70%, while men lose 0.80-0.98% per year. Notably, strength declines more rapidly, with annual losses of 3-4% in men and 2.5-3% in women [24]. These age-related muscular changes progressively impair quality of life in older adults. To address this, the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) established standardized clinical definitions and diagnostic criteria in 2010, recommending combined assessment of low muscle mass and impaired muscle function (strength or performance) for diagnosis [25]. Subsequently, the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) developed regional diagnostic guidelines in 2014 based on evidence from Asian populations [26]. Sarcopenia gained formal disease status in 2016 with its own ICD-10 code (M62.84) [27]. Recent research advances have significantly improved understanding and diagnostic approaches. The EWGSOP2 subsequently updated its definition and diagnostic strategy through renewed consensus meetings, aiming to facilitate earlier clinical detection and optimize treatment protocols [28]. In 2025, the AWGS updated consensus, which includes expanding diagnostic coverage to adults aged 50 to 64 years, simplifying the diagnostic algorithm, and introducing an enhanced muscle health framework recognizing skeletal muscle as vital for healthy longevity, emphasizing cross-talk with brain, bone, adipose tissue and immune systems [29] (Fig. 2).

Epidemiology and risk factors of sarcopenia

With the global aging population, the number of middle-aged and older adults is steadily increasing. It is projected that by 2050, the population aged 60 years and older will exceed 2 billion worldwide, and the impact of musculoskeletal disorders, including sarcopenia, will place a significant burden on public health systems [23]. The prevalence of sarcopenia is positively correlated with age, increasing notably after 50 years. Among individuals in their 70s, the prevalence ranges from 5% to 13%, while in those aged 80 and above, it rises to 11–50% [1]. Early diagnosis and prevention are therefore crucial in managing sarcopenia. However, variations in diagnostic criteria have led to discrepancies in reported prevalence rates. The highest and lowest prevalence were observed in Oceania and Europe using the EWGSOP and EWGSOP2, respectively [30]. The difference in sarcopenia prevalence between EWGSOP and EWGSOP2 was compared in another observational study. They found that new EWGSOP2 consensus underestimates the prevalence of sarcopenia compared with EWGSOP using conventional cut-off points. The prevalence of presarcopenia with EWGSOP (low muscle mass) was lower than probable sarcopenia (low muscle strength) with the new EWGSOP2 [31]. The study by Fernandes et al. also found that EWGSOP2 diagnosed a reduced prevalence of sarcopenia in the elderly compared with EWGSOP1, and EWGSOP2 appeared to be worse at predicting adverse outcomes [32]. Yuan et al. further highlighted that 10–16% of older adults worldwide suffer from sarcopenia, which is associated with increased mortality and multiple adverse outcomes, including cognitive impairment, osteoporosis, hospitalization, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and liver fibrosis [33].

With the progress of aging, the harm of sarcopenia to public health and the increase of public burden will become increasingly significant. Early detection of risk factors associated with sarcopenia and prevention of sarcopenia have also become critical. A variety of risk factors affect the development of sarcopenia throughout the life cycle: genetic and environmental factors may be the main determinants of muscle strength, and adverse lifestyle also affects muscle mass in later life [23,34]. Pathophysiological mechanisms contributing to muscle loss encompass neuromuscular junction dysfunction, reduced motor unit recruitment, chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [9]. Based on etiology, sarcopenia is classified into primary (age-related) and secondary (disease- or condition-related) forms [35]. Primary sarcopenia, driven predominantly by aging, involves progressive muscle mass and function decline due to mitochondrial dysfunction, hormonal imbalances, dysregulated muscle-fat crosstalk, calcium homeostasis disruption, cellular stress, and impaired protein turnover [36]. Secondary sarcopenia results from underlying conditions such as cancer-related cachexia, diabetes, obesity, chronic kidney disease, cardiopulmonary disorders (e.g., heart failure, COPD), neurological diseases, sepsis, and disuse atrophy [1]. Ultimately, disease progression leads to muscle atrophy due to reduced contractile activity and a negative protein balance (catabolism exceeding anabolism) [1]. In addition, various causes overlap in the elderly, making it difficult to distinguish whether sarcopenia is primary or secondary [25]. Irisin: origin and potential physiological functions

Irisin is named after Iris, the Greek goddess of rainbows. It is a 112-amino acid polypeptide produced by cleavage of protein 5 containing the type III fibronectin domain (FNDC5), which converts white adipose tissue (WAT) into brown adipose tissue and increases energy expenditure [37]. Irisin is mainly produced by muscles including skeletal muscle and heart muscle. But irisin production is not limited to muscle: other tissues such as liver, thyroid, adrenal, WAT, and the central nervous system (CNS) can also express FNDC-5 and release irisin [38]. Skeletal muscle contraction during exercise can activate PGC-1α, promote the transcription and up-regulation of FNDC5, and correspondingly stimulate the production and secretion of irisin. Irisin is then secreted into the circulation and acts on various target organs [12]. The secreted FNDC5 precursor consists of an N-terminal containing signal peptide, a fibronectin III domain, a linker domain, a hydrophobic transmembrane domain, and a cytosolic C-terminal domain [39]. Human FNDC5 can be cleaved between Glu143 and Met144 by an unknown protease, thus separating the fibronectin III domain from the connexin domain. Eventually, the isolated fibronectin III domain is released into the circulation as irisin [5].

Irisin exhibits multi-target and multi-effect actions in the human body. In 2018, Kim, H. et al. identified integrin αVβ5 as a high-affinity receptor for irisin through proteomic screening, providing a potential target for understanding irisin's regulatory mechanisms [40]. Meanwhile, irisin is associated with various metabolic and non-metabolic diseases, making it a promising therapeutic target for these chronic conditions. Furthermore, as a myokine, irisin plays an important role in regulating muscle and bone diseases, such as improving bone quality and preventing bone loss [41]. For instance, irisin can activate ERK/STAT signaling through αV integrin, enhance BMP2 and BMP/SMAD signaling pathways to promote bone formation, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for osteoporosis [42]. Kan et al. demonstrated that irisin can promote fracture healing through osteogenesis and angiogenesis. Their results contribute to the understanding of muscle-skeletal interactions during fracture healing [43]. Another, the work by sun et al. was the first to demonstrate that irisin could reduce bone fracture by facilitating osteogenesis and antagonizing TGF-β/Smad signaling in a mouse model of osteogenesis imperfecta [44]. Irisin improved IL-1β suppressed expression of chondrocyte-specific cartilage markers and glycosaminoglycans. Intra-articular injection of irisin further relieved the symptoms and gait of medial meniscus instability [45]. In skeletal muscle cells, irisin treatment promotes the expression of glucose transport and lipid metabolism, while suppressing those expression of glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, thereby regulating glucose homeostasis [46]. In cardiac, resistance exercise can promote irisin release, which subsequently activates the AMPK-PINK1/Parkin-LC3/P62 signaling pathway, modulates the mitochondrial autophagy, attenuates myocardial oxidative stress, and ultimately improve cardiac function [14]. Based on the effects and functions of such irisin in different diseases mentioned above, Irisins have also become potential therapeutic targets in many diseases models. The present review will specifically focus on elucidating its potential therapeutic applications in muscle-related disorders.

Irisin may serve as a marker for diagnosis sarcopenia

To date, no specific biological marker has been used for the diagnosis for sarcopenia. With increasing understanding of this condition, including the pathophysiology involving many interacting pathways and the impact of related diseases, the research on sarcopenia has been deepened [34]. In recent years, attention has turned to irisin, with multiple studies suggesting its potential as a biomarker for sarcopenia. Chang et al. evaluated 715 community-dwelling Korean adults to investigate irisin's role as a myokine in pathological sarcopenia. Their results demonstrated that circulating irisin levels were positively correlated with muscle mass and strength, albeit with modest correlation coefficients (r = 0.22–0.32). More importantly, the mean irisin level was significantly lower in the sarcopenia group compared to the normal group. Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified low circulating irisin as a strong and independent risk factor for sarcopenia (adjusted odds ratio = 0.20), and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis showed good diagnostic accuracy, particularly in men (AUC = 0.87). Therefore, these findings suggest that irisin may be a potential marker for the diagnosis of sarcopenia and help to predict the onset of sarcopenia [47]. Park et al. aimed to investigate whether there is an association between irisin and sarcopenia and to evaluate irisin as a possible biomarker for sarcopenia in postmenopausal women. They showed that circulating irisin levels were significantly lower in patients with sarcopenia and that the risk of developing sarcopenia was 95% for serum irisin concentrations below 1 ng/ml. The authors concluded that decreased levels of this myokine correlate strongly with sarcopenia in postmenopausal women and could serve as an early diagnostic biomarker [48]. In a comparative analysis of skeletal muscle protein biomarkers, Yen et al. found significantly lower creatine kinase and irisin levels in sarcopenic patients versus non-sarcopenic controls. While myostatin and coenzyme Q10 showed no significant intergroup differences, coenzyme Q10 and skeletal muscle protein biomarker levels exhibited notable associations with sarcopenia risk. Importantly, irisin levels below 118 ng/mL significantly increased the probability of sarcopenia [49]. Contrasting these findings, Baek's study reported no significant differences in serum irisin levels based on sarcopenia status, low muscle mass, weak muscle strength, poor physical performance, or impaired muscle quality. Furthermore, their adjusted analyses revealed no meaningful correlations between irisin levels and sarcopenia parameters [50] (Table 1).

As there is no standardized method for irisin detection, detection discrepancies are inevitable. Currently, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits are the primary tool for irisin quantification; however, many commercial kits exhibit variations in antibody specificity and cross-reactivity, leading to inaccurate measurements [48,54]. In addition, different studies have focused on different groups (e.g., postmenopausal women [48] and age ≥40 years [49], Koreans [47] and Arabs [51]), and there are differences in hormonal status, age and comorbidities in these groups, which may affect irisin levels and their relationship with sarcopenia. Collectively, these findings support a potential link between irisin and sarcopenia, with promising diagnostic implications. Although conflicting results exist, future studies—including the development of standardized detection methods and large-scale population trials—are expected to provide more robust evidence to clarify the exact role of irisin in sarcopenia.

Anabolic and catabolic changes between irisin and sarcopenia

Intracellular protein degradation and proteolytic systems primarily involve four mechanisms: (1) the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), the principal regulatory mechanism of skeletal muscle atrophy; (2) the autophagy-lysosomal proteolytic system, with cathepsins as the major lysosomal proteases; (3) calcium-dependent calpains, non-lysosomal proteases mediating specific substrate cleavage; and (4) cysteine-dependent aspartate-specific proteases [55]. Muscle protein degradation predominantly relies on the UPS and autophagy-lysosomal systems, which we focus on in this discussion.

The UPS degrades most myofibrillar components, while autophagy primarily breaks down mitochondria and other organelles. During various conditions inducing muscle wasting, a common set of transcriptional adaptations is initiated, leading to increased protein breakdown and decreased protein synthesis [56]. UPS comprises ubiquitin-activating enzymes (E1), ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes (E2), ubiquitin-protein ligases (E3), ubiquitin (Ub), and the 26S proteasome. The E1-Ub complex transfers activated ubiquitin to E2, forming a Ub-E2 complex during ubiquitination Ultimately, proteins are transferred to E3 for further processing and recognition. Following multiple ubiquitination rounds, polyubiquitinated proteins are recognized and degraded into small peptides and amino acids by the 26S proteasome [57]. In muscle, specific E3 ligases - muscle ring finger 1 (MuRF1) and muscle atrophy F-box (MAFbx/atrogin-1) - are crucial for protein degradation and serve as key regulators of proteasomal turnover promoting muscle atrophy [58]. Elevated mRNA expression of MuRF1 and MAFbx has been observed in animal models of fasting, diabetes, cancer, denervation, and immobilization [59]. MAFbx overexpression induces atrophy in myotubes, while MAFbx or MuRF1 deficient mice exhibit atrophy resistance [60]. Genetic ablation of MAFbx and MuRF1 prevents muscle loss by up to 56% and 36%, respectively [60]. In addition, irisin downregulates E3 ubiquitin ligases MAFbx and MuRF1 by inhibiting forkhead box O (FOXO) 3α dephosphorylation, demonstrating its capacity to effectively restore dexamethasone-induced skeletal muscle atrophy [61]. When phosphorylated by Akt, FOXOs move from the nucleus into the cytoplasm, hindering E3 ubiquitin ligase expression. When the Akt pathway is blocked or attenuated, FOXOs remain in the nucleus, induce the expression of E3 ubiquitin ligase, activate the UPS, and cause proteolysis [62]. Ma et al. demonstrated an age-dependent decline in FNDC5/irisin expression within the gastrocnemius muscle, while aerobic exercise effectively reduced the expression of MuRF1 and atrogin-1 in sarcopenic mice. Notably, this exercise-induced improvement in skeletal muscle function was abolished in aged FNDC5-knockout mice, providing compelling evidence for the essential role of FNDC5 in mitigating age-related sarcopenia [63]. Similarly, Wang and colleagues revealed that irisin attenuates muscle atrophy in chronic kidney disease (CKD) by suppressing FOXO3α activation and downregulating the MuRF1 and MAFbx [64].

The autophagy-lysosome system represents another critical regulator of muscle catabolism. Autophagy serves as a fundamental catabolic and cytoprotective process that delivers cytoplasmic contents and organelles to lysosomes for degradation, thereby maintaining myofiber homeostasis. Conversely, impaired autophagy promotes myocyte degradation [1]. Mammals possess three distinct autophagic pathways, including macroautophagy, chaperone-mediated autophagy, and microautophagy, with macroautophagy being the predominant mechanism mediating protein degradation in muscle tissue [65]. The autophagy-related genes (ATG) play essential roles in the precise regulation of autophagic processes and associated signaling cascades. The autophagic machinery initiates with ATG7 and ATG12 mediated formation of protein complexes required for phagophore maturation. Subsequently, ATG4 processes the precursor LC3 (a ubiquitin-like protein) to generate LC3-I, which is then conjugated to phosphatidylethanolamine on developing autophagosomal membranes by ATG complexes, forming the autophagosome-associated LC3-II (the ratio of LC3-I to LC3-II is often used to measure the amount of autophagy degradation or autophagic flux) [66]. Both impaired and excessive autophagy have been implicated in age-related sarcopenia. Maintaining optimal autophagic flux is critical for proper organelle turnover, preventing accumulation of dysfunctional proteins, damaged mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum membranes, while simultaneously avoiding excessive proteolysis - all crucial factors in sarcopenia prevention [67]. Ablation of ATG7 in skeletal muscle results in defective autophagosome formation, leading to loss of fiber integrity, muscle atrophy, and functional impairment [67,68]. ATG7 deletion was also manifested as abnormal mitochondrial aggregation, sarcoplasmic reticulum distension, sarcomere disarrangement and formation of aberrant concentric membranous structures, and induced the upregulation of MAFbx and MuRF1 in the UPS [67]. This implies a compensatory increase in protein degradation by the UPS, which further leads to muscle atrophy and reduced muscle mass in old age [59]. Importantly, autophagy inhibition also promotes muscle loss during denervation and fasting conditions [68]. Clinical evidence shows significantly reduced ATG7 protein and LC3II levels in muscle biopsies from sedentary elderly individuals with sarcopenia. Conversely, ATG7 overexpression restores autophagic flux, increases LC3-positive vesicles, and improves muscle mass in sarcopenic mice [68]. Emerging research highlights irisin's ability to modulate autophagy. In C2C12 myocytes, irisin enhances autophagic activity through PGC-1α, as demonstrated by increased LC3 expression and accelerated p62 degradation [69]. Similarly, in angiotensin II-induced cardiomyocyte injury, irisin treatment elevates LC3II levels while reducing p62 accumulation and attenuating apoptosis. Animal studies further confirm that irisin overexpression mitigates cardiomyocyte apoptosis and pressure overload-induced hypertrophy, suggesting its capacity to induce cytoprotective autophagy while suppressing apoptotic pathways [70]. In skeletal muscle, He et al. demonstrated that exercise improves mitochondrial function and reduces apoptosis following chronic limb ischemia through PGC1α/FNDC5/irisin-mediated selective autophagy. Their work additionally established a positive correlation between extracellular irisin levels and the expression of mitochondrial fission and mitophagy markers in cultured myotubes [71]. These collective findings suggest that irisin may preserve muscle homeostasis through precise modulation of autophagic activity, representing a promising therapeutic avenue for sarcopenia intervention.

Role of irisin in regulating cell death in sarcopenia

At present, there are many influencing factors for sarcopenia, among which cell death such as apoptosis, autophagy, pyroptosis and ferroptosis are closely related to sarcopenia [[72], [73], [74]]. Therefore, exploring the relationship between irisin and cell death could help provide important insights for the future treatment of sarcopenia. Irisin and ferroptosis

Ferroptosis represents a distinct form of regulated cell death characterized by iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, differing fundamentally from other known cell death mechanisms such as apoptosis, pyroptosis, necrosis, and autophagy. Morphologically, ferroptosis exhibits unique features including mitochondrial shrinkage, increased membrane density, and reduction or disappearance of mitochondrial cristae [75,76]. The pathogenesis of ferroptosis in skeletal muscle disorders involves several key mechanisms: accumulation of labile iron, elevated lipid peroxides, inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and depletion of glutathione (GSH). The regulatory mechanisms and targets involved also include P53/solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) axis, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) - SLC7A11/GPX4 axis, transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1), ACSL4, PKCα and NOX1/4 [77]. Experimental evidence demonstrates that ferric citrate induces iron accumulation in C2C12 myoblasts, impairing their differentiation into myotubes. Iron overload upregulates P53 expression, which subsequently suppresses the protein level of SLC7A11. This downregulation of SLC7A11 promotes lipid peroxide accumulation and ultimately induces myocyte ferroptosis, suggesting the P53/SLC7A11 axis contributes to sarcopenia pathogenesis and may represent a potential therapeutic target [78]. Satellite cell-specific deletion of transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) leads to irreversible depletion of satellite cells, accompanied by cell-autonomous proliferation and differentiation defects, leading to skeletal muscle regeneration impairment, followed by labile iron accumulation, lipogenesis, and decreased GPX4 and NRF2 protein levels. Thus, satellite cell-specific deletion of TFR1 impairs skeletal muscle regeneration and activates ferroptosis [79]. While the potential role of irisin in modulating ferroptosis in sarcopenia remains unclear, emerging evidence suggests its regulatory capacity in other contexts. In sepsis-associated encephalopathy, irisin inhibits hippocampal ferroptosis via the NRF2/GPX4 signaling pathway, ameliorating neuroinflammation [80]. In acute kidney injury models, irisin suppresses ferroptosis through SIRT1/NRF2 pathway activation, improving iron metabolism and mitochondrial function while attenuating renal damage [81]. Although the precise involvement of irisin in regulating ferroptosis during sarcopenia requires further validation, these findings suggest a novel direction for exploring irisin-based therapeutic strategies targeting this iron-dependent cell death pathway in muscle wasting disorders (Fig. 4).

Irisin and pyroptosis

Pyroptosis is a newly regulated form of cell death discovered and confirmed in recent years, which is characterized by the release of large amounts of proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β and IL-18. As an inflammatory regulatory cell death mode, it has unique characteristics different from apoptosis, such as intact nuclei, DNA laddering, pore formation, cell swelling, and osmotic lysis [82]. The NLRP3 inflammasome serves as a critical mediator of inflammation-induced pyroptosis. Upon activation, NLRP3 cleaves its substrate gasdermin D (GSDMD), leading to plasma membrane rupture, cellular lysis, and release of proinflammatory contents including IL-1β, IL-18 and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), which induces pyroptosis [82,83]. Emerging evidence demonstrates that NLRP3 inflammasome activation contributes to age-related reductions in myofiber size and diminished glycolytic capacity, ultimately leading to muscle dysfunction. However, NLRP3 knockout has been shown to attenuate myopathy and improve muscle function in aged mice [84]. Furthermore, NLRP3 inflammasome activation triggers both pyroptosis and UPS, resulting in upregulated expression of MuRF1 and MAFbx that promotes muscle protein degradation. Genetic ablation of NLRP3 effectively suppresses pyroptosis-related protein expression and prevents C2C12 myotube atrophy, significantly ameliorating muscle wasting [73]. Although the anti-pyroptotic properties of irisin in sarcopenia models remain to be confirmed, its protective effects have been demonstrated in other pathological contexts. In chronic kidney disease, irisin can enhance the autophagy of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and consequently inhibits NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis and proinflammatory responses, ultimately improving VSMC calcification [85]. In osteoarthritis, irisin not only upregulates collagen II expression while downregulating MMP-13 and ADAMTS-5 through inhibition of the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway in chondrocytes, but also suppresses NLRP3/caspase-1 activity to mitigate chondrocyte pyroptosis [86]. However, whether irisin can regulate pyroptosis to ameliorate sarcopenia requires further investigation (Fig. 4).

Irisin and apoptosis

Apoptosis, an evolutionarily conserved cellular suicide program, can be triggered by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors through tightly regulated signaling pathways [87]. The molecular mechanism of apoptosis includes the sequential activation of a series of cysteine proteases called the caspases. Initiating caspases initiate a series of proteolytic processes leading to execution of caspase activation. The resulting executive caspases catalyze apoptotic body formation, membrane blebbing, and inter-nucleosomal DNA fragmentation [5,88]. Emerging evidence suggests that age-related acceleration of myocyte apoptosis may represent a key mechanism driving the initiation and progression of muscle loss [87]. Skeletal muscle exhibits unique apoptotic characteristics due to the multinucleated nature of myofibers, where apoptosis can occur either in individual myonuclei (nuclear apoptosis) or result in complete cellular death [89]. Recent studies have demonstrated the anti-apoptotic properties of irisin in muscle cells. Skeletal muscle fibrosis represents an important pathological feature associated with age-related sarcopenia. Irisin has been shown to exert dual protective effects by attenuating D-galactose-induced fibrosis in skeletal muscle fibroblasts and enhancing caveolin-1 expression to inhibit C2C12 myoblast apoptosis while promoting fibroblast apoptosis [90,91].

Irisin and mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondria serve as the primary organelles responsible for cellular energy production through ATP synthesis, supporting fundamental metabolic processes. Beyond their bioenergetic function, mitochondria form an interconnected network with the sarcoplasmic reticulum and sarcolemma, establishing a critical structural and functional relationship between mitochondrial integrity and skeletal muscle homeostasis [92]. During aging, skeletal muscle mitochondria undergo progressive pathological alterations characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced mitochondrial content and ATP production, increased ROS production, mutant mtDNA accumulation, mitochondrial protein dysfunction, and alterations in membrane potential [93,94]. These age-related mitochondrial changes activate catabolic signaling pathways that induce nuclear translocation of transcription factors, subsequently promoting the expression of muscle atrophy-related genes [95]. Excessive ROS induces REDOX signaling pathways, leading to oxidative damage and mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced ATP production, increased protein degradation, and inhibition of protein synthesis, which further leads to muscle loss and decreased strength [96]. Clinical investigations by Migliavacca et al. utilizing muscle biopsy specimens from elderly populations demonstrated significant impairments in both mitochondrial function and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) biosynthesis in sarcopenic individuals [97]. Emerging evidence highlights the regulatory role of irisin in mitochondrial quality control. Exercise-induced irisin secretion promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and function, enhances the energy metabolism capacity of muscle cells, and thus improves muscle strength and endurance [94]. Furthermore, irisin has been shown to protect skeletal muscle cells from oxidative damage through modulation of mitophagy - a selective autophagy process essential for maintenance of mitochondrial quality control, clearance of dysfunctional organelles and prevention of muscle atrophy progression [94]. In vitro studies have shown that irisin increased PGC-1α protein expression in C2C12 myoblasts and significantly increased mitochondrial content in a dose-dependent manner [98]. Irisin also significantly increased the expression of metabolic genes, including PGC-1α, nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1), mitochondrial transcription factor A, glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4), and mitochondrial uncoupling protein 3 (UCP3), thereby increasing mitochondrial biogenesis [99].

Relationship of irisin to satellite cells and myoblasts

Satellite cells are mononuclear stem cells with myogenic potential that reside in a mitotically quiescent state between the basal lamina and sarcolemma of muscle fibers, exhibiting low metabolic activity [100]. These cells become activated and proliferate in response to stimuli such as exercise, injury, or mechanical stress [100]. For instance, when quiescent satellite cells receive muscle damage signals, they undergo transcriptional reprogramming characterized by upregulation of the myogenic regulatory factors MyoD and myogenin [101]. In the process of muscle injury repair, satellite cells will undergo three processes, from quiescence to activation, enter the cell cycle for proliferation, differentiate and fuse to form multinucleated myotubes and activate protein synthesis [100]. Aging leads to progressive decline in satellite cell number and activity, resulting in diminished proliferative capacity, impaired regeneration, reduced myofiber numbers, and ultimately muscle atrophy [102]. Studies have shown that resistance training not only promotes the activation and proliferation of satellite cells [100], but also increases the production of irisin [39]. Experimental evidence demonstrates that irisin treatment significantly reduces the percentage of quiescent satellite cells (Pax7+/MyoD-) while increasing populations of proliferating myoblasts (Pax7+/MyoD+) and committed myoblasts (Pax7-/MyoD+), indicating its ability to enhance satellite cell activation and proliferation. Furthermore, irisin administration in denervated mice significantly increases muscle mass and attenuates skeletal muscle atrophy [19]. Yang et al. revealed that irisin promotes satellite cell differentiation by upregulating Mst1/2 expression. Importantly, genetic inhibition of Mst1/2 in satellite cells markedly reduces YAP phosphorylation, impairs satellite cells differentiation, and compromises irisin-mediated muscle regeneration [103].

During early muscle differentiation, myoblast fusion (both myoblast-myoblast and myoblast-myotube) constitutes an essential process for mature skeletal muscle fiber formation [3,5]. The C2C12 myoblast cell line serves as a well-established in vitro model of muscle differentiation, capable of proliferation, differentiation, and fusion into mature myotubes [104]. Sarcopenia negatively impacts myogenic processes by impairing skeletal muscle stem cell function and reducing myogenic capacity [105]. Irisin promotes myogenesis through multiple mechanisms: it induces C2C12 myoblast proliferation via ERK-dependent upregulation of CCL7 and increases expression of proliferation markers PCNA, MKI67, and MCM2 [106]. Additionally, irisin suppresses dexamethasone-induced myotube atrophy by inhibiting expression of MAFbx and MuRF1 along with proteasome activity [61]. In vitro studies demonstrate that irisin dose-dependently enhances myoblast proliferation, increases the fusion index, upregulates the expression of myomaker and caveolin-3, and ultimately promotes myotube formation [19].

Irisin and signaling pathway

The molecular alterations in sarcopenic muscle involve complex changes in protein synthesis and degradation signaling pathways, including but not limited to: insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), MAPK, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) complex 1 (mTORC1), forkhead box transcription factors (FOXOs), PI3K/Akt, along with other critical signaling pathways involved in muscle functional metabolism [34,59].

IGF-1 signaling pathway in skeletal muscle and its potential interaction with irisin

The primary anabolic signaling pathway in skeletal muscle is mediated by IGF-1. IGF-1 binds to its receptor (a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor) on the myocyte sarcolemma, inducing intracellular receptor transphosphorylation and creating docking sites for insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1). This receptor activation triggers IRS-1 phosphorylation, subsequently activating the PI3K/Akt pathway [59]. This fundamental signaling cascade serves as a crucial positive regulator of muscle growth, coordinating multiple cellular processes including protein synthesis, protein degradation, cell proliferation, cell survival, glucose uptake, and energy production [3,66]. The critical role of IGF-1 in muscle physiology is evidenced by IGF-1 overexpression inducing muscle hypertrophy, while IGF-1 receptor deficiency impairing early muscle growth and development. These observations establish IGF-1 as an essential mediator of muscle growth [107]. Irisin, which is significantly upregulated following exercise, demonstrates positive correlations with skeletal muscle mass indices and circulating IGF-1 levels [108]. Irisin enhanced insulin-induced PI3K/Akt signaling activity. Activated Akt promotes the translocation of GLUT4 to the cell membrane, leading to the increase of glucose influx into cells. Activated Akt inhibits GSK3 activity, and subsequently activates glycogen synthase (GS) to enhance glycogen synthesis and maintain glucose homeostasis [109]. Furthermore, in skeletal muscle fibroblasts, irisin overexpression attenuates D-galactose-induced cellular senescence, redox imbalance and fibrotic changes. These protective effects are mediated through modulation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [90]. While the precise relationship between irisin and IGF/PI3K/Akt signaling in sarcopenia remains unclear, these collective findings suggest a potential functional connection. This emerging understanding opens new avenues for exploring irisin-based therapeutic strategies targeting the IGF/PI3K/Akt pathway in sarcopenia management.

The mTOR signaling pathway in muscle homeostasis and potential modulation by irisin

In healthy skeletal muscle, the mTOR signaling pathway plays a crucial role in maintaining muscle mass by regulating protein synthesis. This pathway shows specific upregulation under conditions of muscle hypertrophy, while its biomarkers are frequently downregulated in various atrophic states, indicating its direct involvement in the maintenance of muscle fiber size [1]. The mTOR kinase interacts with several proteins to form two distinct complexes: mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) and mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2), which exhibit differential sensitivity to rapamycin [110]. While mTORC2 is associated with glucose and lipid homeostasis, mTORC1 regulates multiple anabolic processes, including protein synthesis, as well as ribosomal and mitochondrial biogenesis [111]. Among them, the two main effectors of mTORC1 promoting protein synthesis are eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1) and S6 kinase [3]. Upstream of mTOR, AKT phosphorylates and negatively regulates the FOXO family of transcription factors that are sufficient and necessary for the expression of the UPS and autophagy systems, further inhibiting protein degradation [66]. Therefore, activation of this pathway uniquely promotes protein synthesis while suppresses protein degradation. However, the relationship between irisin and mTOR signaling in sarcopenia is still unclear, but other studies have been elaborated. In cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury (CIRI), Irisin can prevent mitochondrial fission and increase mitochondrial fusion to reduce apoptosis, and reduce oxidative stress by activating PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis [112]. In doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity, irisin can reduce oxidative stress and activate AKT/mTOR signaling pathway to reduce cardiomyocyte apoptosis [113].

The FOXO family in skeletal muscle metabolism and potential regulation by irisin

The FOXO family of transcription factors plays crucial roles in regulating various cellular processes including cell cycle progression, apoptosis, oxidative stress resistance, and metabolic homeostasis [114]. In skeletal muscle, the FOXO family consists of three main isoforms: FOXO1, FOXO3, and FOXO4. These transcription factors function as downstream effectors of the IGF-1/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, where AKT-mediated phosphorylation negatively regulates their activity to modulate metabolic and stress responses. However, reduced Akt pathway activity leads to a decrease in phosphorylated FOXO levels in the cytoplasm and a significant increase in FOXO levels in the nucleus [65,115]. Nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation of FOXO family members are sufficient to promote MAFbx and MuRF1 expression, resulting in muscle atrophy [3]. Inhibition of FOXO mediated by AKT phosphorylation prevents MuRF1 and MAFbx expression and their catabolic effects in skeletal muscle [55]. Complete rescue of muscle mass in MIGIRKO mice (muscle-specific triple FOXO1/3/4 knockout) through reversal of enhanced autophagy and without altering proteasome activity [107]. In the C2C12 myotube, activation of the IGF-1/PI3K/Akt pathway suppression of atrophy markers MuRF1 and MAFbx. This effect is mediated through inhibition of their upstream regulator FOXO [116]. In dexamethasone-treated myotubes, irisin can regulate FOXO phosphorylation and inhibit the expression of MAFbx and MuRF1 through IGF1-mediated activity, thereby alleviating muscle atrophy signals. Therefore, irisin has a potential role in regulating FOXO signaling.

The MAPK signaling pathway in skeletal muscle and its potential regulation by irisin

The MAPK pathway comprises a central three-tiered signaling cascade consisting of: MAPK kinase kinases (MKKKs), MAPK kinases (MKKs), and MAPKs. This pathway can be activated by various stimuli, including oxidative stress and cytokines, leading to the activation of four well-characterized MAPK subfamilies: extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERKs) 1/2, ERK5, c-Jun NH2-terminal kinases (JNKs), and p38 kinases [117]. Among these, the p38 MAPK pathway has been involve skeletal muscle atrophy and catabolic processes. This is achieved through its role in activating the expression of muscle-specific E3 ubiquitin ligases MuRF1 and MAFbx [118]. For example, activation of p38 MAPK promotes phosphorylation of C/EBPβ and induces upregulation of atrogin1/MAFbx leading to muscle mass loss [119]. Conversely, inhibition of p38 MAPK attenuated the up-regulation of MAFBX/Atrogin-1 and MuRF1 in C2 myotubes [120]. Emerging evidence demonstrates multiple connections between irisin and MAPK signaling [121]. Irisin has been shown to stimulate glucose uptake via AMPKα2 activation in muscle cells, through a mechanism likely involving p38 MAPK-GLUT4 translocation [122]. Furthermore, in insulin-resistant C2C12 myotubes that typically exhibit impaired glucose uptake capacity and reduced GLUT4 expression. Ye et al. demonstrated that irisin not only enhanced mitochondrial function and mitochondrial respiration, but also increased glucose uptake and improved insulin resistance by activating the p38-MAPK-PGC-1α pathway in cells [69]. Although irisin has been shown to regulate MAPK signaling in muscle cells to maintain glucose metabolism, further studies are needed to elucidate its role in sarcopenia.

Summary of signaling pathways regulated by irisin in sarcopenia

The IGF-1/PI3K/Akt/mTORC1 axis, which normally promotes muscle protein synthesis, becomes impaired with aging, while the activity of FOXO transcription factors - key regulators of muscle atrophy genes - becomes elevated. Concurrently, MAPK signaling pathways that modulate muscle growth and differentiation are also significantly altered in sarcopenic muscle. These molecular changes collectively lead to reduced muscle protein synthesis, increased protein degradation, and ultimately the progressive loss of muscle mass and strength observed in sarcopenia.

Other emerging therapeutic strategies for sarcopenia

Although irisin has shown great potential as a therapeutic candidate for sarcopenia, several other emerging strategies are being continuously explored for sarcopenia treatment.

Myostatin inhibitors

Myostatin is a component of the TGF-β superfamily and plays a crucial role in regulating muscle mass and function, influencing the progression of sarcopenia [123]. MSTN inhibition has been widely considered as a potential treatment for muscle wasting diseases. Despite positive results in various animal studies, inhibition of MSTN did not improve muscle function in humans, probably because of the lack of specificity and unexpected off-target effects of MSTN inhibitors [124]. For example, MYO-029 is a recombinant human myostatin specific antibody, but clinical trials have shown that it does not significantly improve muscle strength and function in adult muscular dystrophies [125]. Non-muscle-related adverse side effects of ACE-031, another myostatin inhibitor, including nosebleeds and telangiectasia, led to study interruption [126]. In the future, the research and development of MSTN inhibitors should give priority to specificity to reduce off-target effects and improve their efficacy.

Hormone therapy

IGF-1 is considered the most important mediator of muscle growth and repair [127]. However, the administration of IGF-I to the elderly has led to controversial results in terms of muscle strength and function, which may be attributed to age-related insulin resistance to amino acid transport and protein synthesis or a significant reduction in IGF-I receptor and receptor affinity for IGF-I in muscle with age [128]. Testosterone replacement therapy has become an effective strategy for treating sarcopenia, with significant improvements in muscle mass and strength [123]. For example, testosterone administration significantly increased appendicular lean mass and body lean mass compared to the placebo group [129].However, its use is limited due to side effects, such as increased prostate cancer risk in men, virilization in women and an overall increased risk of cardiovascular events [130]. In addition, studies have shown that growth hormone can increase muscle protein synthesis and increase muscle mass [130], but some studies have concluded that supplementation GH does not improve muscle strength or muscle mass [128]. Furthermore, trials of GH supplementation have reported a high incidence of side effects including soft tissue edema, carpal tunnel syndrome, joint pain, and gynecological mastitis, raising serious concerns especially in the elderly [128].

Natural medicine

In recent years, there has been a great deal of interest in the use of natural medicines for sarcopenia, including polygonatum [131], umbelliferone [132], geniposide [133], and cistanche deserticola extract (along with its active component echinacoside) [134] - all showed significant effects in improving skeletal muscle health. But there is limited data to support their use in humans in terms of efficacy, as well as potential drug interactions and side effects. Therefore, until further studies demonstrate their safety and efficacy in humans, support for the use of these natural medicines for the treatment and prevention of sarcopenia is limited.

In summary, myostatin inhibitors are limited by off-target effects and poor functional efficacy, hormone therapy faces significant safety hazards, and natural medicines lack sufficient clinical validation. In contrast, irisin's major advantages lie in: (1) multitargeted action; (2) endogenous physiological origin and favorable safety profile; (3) simultaneous improvement of muscle mass and function; and (4) breakthroughs in receptor identification and delivery system optimization, which address key translational barriers. These characteristics position irisin as a highly promising candidate for sarcopenia treatment with broad clinical application prospects.

Translational challenges and future perspectives for irisin-based therapy

Although many current studies have pointed out that irisin can be used as a diagnostic marker and may be a promising drug for the treatment of sarcopenia, many challenges still need to be addressed before its clinical application can be achieved. Irisin is secreted through the circulation and reaches target organs, where it acts through αV/β5 integrins [5]. However, the half-life of irisin in plasma is extremely short (only 1 h), which requires frequent administration during treatment. This not only increases the treatment cost but also raises the risk of adverse reactions [135]. In addition, traditional systemic administration methods such as intravenous injection and intraperitoneal injection, due to their poor targeting and inability to address the core issue of a short half-life, further limit the clinical application value of irisin [136]. Therefore, it is still a great challenge to study how to prolong the half-life of irisin and improve its efficacy.

For instance, Zhu et al. developed an albumin-binding domain-fused irisin (ABD-irisin) construct, which prolonged the plasma half-life to approximately 10 h and improved tissue distribution compared with native irisin [135]. To prevent irisin degradation, Xin et al. incorporated irisin into a silk fibroin/calcium silicate/sodium alginate (SCS) composite scaffold coated with polyvinyl alcohol (i/SCS/P). This system enabled sustained irisin release (up to 50% over 7 days) and effectively promoted osteogenic differentiation both in vitro and in vivo [137]. To overcome the disadvantages of off-target effects and insufficient local drug concentration, Shi et al. designed a biodegradable silk fibroin (SF) hydrogel as an irisin delivery system to treat volumetric muscle loss (VML). This study demonstrates for the first time that irisin delivery strategy using biodegradable SF hydrogels effectively enhances VML repair by modulating myogenic regeneration and immune microenvironment, providing a promising therapeutic approach for clinical application [136]. In summary, although irisin holds promise for the treatment of sarcopenia, its clinical translation faces key challenges in terms of pharmacokinetics, delivery, and targeting. In the future, optimizing the preparation process of irisin, evaluating its long-term safety and immunogenicity, and exploring individualized dosing regimen will be the key directions to promote the transformation of irisin from basic research to clinical treatment.

Conclusion and discussion

With the progression of population aging, the prevalence and mortality of sarcopenia are expected to rise further, significantly impairing patients' quality of life and imposing a heavy burden on society. However, there are still no effective drugs available for treating sarcopenia. The pathogenesis of sarcopenia involves multiple factors, including imbalances in protein synthesis and degradation, cell death (ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and apoptosis), mitochondrial dysfunction, and dysregulated signaling pathways. Exercise-induced irisin has shown potential in maintaining muscle homeostasis, but critical research gaps persist.

First, large-scale clinical trials are needed to confirm the diagnostic utility of irisin as a biomarker as well as its therapeutic efficacy in different populations. A standardized irisin detection method should be established to resolve existing discrepancies in detection. Second, the optimal therapeutic dose, route of administration, and dose-response relationship need to be further explored, as well as long-term safety assessments (e.g., immunogenicity, off-target effects) and interaction assessments with comorbidities or drugs. Mechanistically, the regulatory role of irisin in sarcopenia (e.g., ferroptosis and pyroptosis) as well as the related signaling cascades need to be further elucidated. In addition, the synergistic effects of irisin with comprehensive interventions such as exercise, nutritional supplementation, or other treatments have not been fully explored. Clinically, bioengineering strategies (e.g., half-life extension, targeted delivery systems) should be optimized to address the problem of short plasma half-life and poor targeting of irisin. Prioritizing addressing these gaps will advance irisin from preclinical to clinical applications, effectively addressing the human health impacts of sarcopenia.
