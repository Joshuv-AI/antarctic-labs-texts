Identification and Prioritization of Requirements for a Home‐Based Tele‐Rehabilitation Information System for Cerebral Palsy Patients: A Delphi Study
Authors: Faridokht Salahshoori, Majid Jangi, Ebrahim Sadeghi‐demneh, Farhad Fatehi, Alireza Rahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe delivery of consistent and comprehensive rehabilitation care for Cerebral Palsy (CP) patients, particularly in limited‐resource settings, faces significant structural, geographical, and economic barriers. The aim of the current study was to identify and finalize, through expert consensus, the functional and non‐functional requirements of a home‐based telerehabilitation information system for CP patients in the context of Iran, a setting with similar challenges to other low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs).MethodsA two‐round Delphi methodology was employed with a multidisciplinary panel of experts to obtain a consensus regarding the requirements of a home‐based telerehabilitation system. Based on a prior qualitative study and a scoping review, a comprehensive set of requirements was developed. Requirements with a mean score of ≥ 3.75 on a 5‐point Likert scale or ≥ 80% agreement were included in the final framework. The ≥ 3.75 mean score was selected to represent a high consensus level, ensuring the average rating was clearly above the midpoint and toward the “Very Important” category (score of 5).ResultsA total of 142 requirements were identified and classified into 11 functional subsystems, comprising 121 functional and 21 non‐functional requirements. The subsystems with the highest number of endorsed requirements were Remote Exercise Therapy (n = 22), Remote Education (n = 21), and Remote Counseling (n = 13). The most highly‐rated non‐functional requirements focused on Usability (e.g., simple interface), Compatibility (e.g., multi‐device support), and Adaptability (e.g., future feature addition).ConclusionThis study establishes a validated, context‐sensitive framework for the development of a home‐based telerehabilitation system for CP patients in Iran. By incorporating expert consensus across clinical and technical domains, the findings offer a strong foundation for future system design and implementation. The approach highlights the importance of tailoring digital health solutions to local needs and healthcare infrastructures, providing a model for similar LMIC settings.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP), the most common childhood disability of the motor system, impacts approximately 2 to 3 per 1000 live births worldwide [1, 2]. Its pathogenesis results from non‐progressive brain injury or disturbed brain development occurring during prenatal, perinatal, or early postnatal life [3]. The hallmark sign of CP is chronic motor dysfunction, including spasticity, ataxia, dyskinesia, and mixed, frequently characterized by poor control over posture and movement coordination [1, 4]. A wide range of comorbidities, like intellectual disability, speech and language disability, visual and audio disability, and epilepsy, are also frequently observed in these patients [5]. Given the multifaceted and lifelong nature of these impairments, holistic, multidisciplinary rehabilitation services are essential to optimize the development of the motor system and promote the patients’ independence and overall quality of life [6].

In low‐ and middle‐income countries, which face significant structural and economic barriers, access to specialist rehabilitation services is often limited. Many children with CP, particularly those living in rural or underserved areas, face significant challenges in receiving consistent and comprehensive rehabilitation care [7]. These are compounded by a shortage of trained professionals, expensive travel, and limited institutional resources, ultimately causing discontinuity of care and imperfect therapeutic results [7, 8]. In order to overcome the aforementioned limitations, home‐based tele‐rehabilitation, through the application of telecommunication technologies to deliver rehabilitation services within the patient's home, has been viewed as a potential solution [9]. It not only reduces logistical and economic burdens to families but also embeds therapeutic activity into the natural environment of the child, potentially increasing engagement and functional outcomes [9, 10, 11]. Global evidence attests to the feasibility and effectiveness of telerehabilitation interventions to manage CP and other conditions of a neurodevelopmental nature [10, 12, 13, 14]. However, the successful implementation and adoption of such systems require context‐specific requirements that align with local clinical, technological, and sociocultural contexts.

A critical step in the effective implementation of any health information system is identifying and verifying its functional and non‐functional requirements accurately. Inadequate specification of these requirements can lead to systems that are difficult to work with, poorly adopted by stakeholders, and inefficient in delivering anticipated outcomes [15]. Despite the growing interest in telehealth and digital rehabilitation, there is currently no standardized, context‐sensitive framework specifically tailored to the challenges of LMIC settings like Iran to support the development of home‐based telerehabilitation systems for CP patients [16].

To address this gap, it is essential to bring in the perspective of key stakeholders, from clinicians and rehabilitation experts to caregivers, health informatics experts such as clinical informationits, and system designers, into the earlier development phases of the system [17]. The Delphi technique, a structured and iterative consensus‐building method, is recognized to be of use in complex, multidisciplinary domains such as health information system development [18]. This method enables the systematic collection of feedback from a range of specialist groups and provides a basis for prioritizing main system attributes through collective judgment and context‐specific relevance [18, 19].

The current study aimed to identify and validate the functional and non‐functional requirements of a home‐based telerehabilitation information system for CP patients in Iran using a two‐round Delphi method. By consulting experts from relevant fields, it seeks to create a comprehensive, context‐specific foundation for designing digital rehabilitation platforms tailored to the Iranian healthcare system.

Methods

Study Design

A Delphi methodology was employed from October 2023 to January 2024 to gain expert agreement regarding the functional and non‐functional requirements of a home‐based telerehabilitation information system for Iranian children with CP. Using the Delphi method, which is suitable where little empirical evidence exists and where subject expertise has to underpin a decision, and provides a systematic means of combining varied opinions in technological, clinical, and contextual areas. This study was carried out in accordance with international guidelines for consensus development, and involved three sequential phases of work: (i) initial requirement generation through literature review and stakeholder interviews; (ii) development of a draft list of system requirements; and (iii) a two‐round Delphi method to reach expert consensus and finalize the core system characteristics.

Data Collection

Phase 1: Initial Scoping and Requirement Generation

To develop an initial foundation for the functional and non‐functional requirements of a home‐based telerehabilitation information system for children with cerebral palsy (CP), a two‐stage strategy was adopted, consisting of a scoping review of the available literature and consultations with experts. A scoping review was initially conducted to gather and summarize the international evidence to date relating to cerebral palsy, telerehabilitation, and health information systems. The comprehensive review aimed to identify the global modalities of telerehabilitation service delivery, system functionalities, and the specific patient requirements necessary for the successful implementation of these services for individuals with Cerebral Palsy. The major academic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science, were comprehensively searched. The search strategies employed a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary items, including but not limited to: cerebral palsy, telerehabilitation, home‐based care, system usability, functional requirements, and health information technology. Published studies in the Persian, and English languages from inception until September 2023 were considered. The inclusion criteria were focused on peer‐reviewed studies, including systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials, and design/evaluation studies, describing global modalities of telerehabilitation service delivery, system functionalities, and patient requirements for individuals with Cerebral Palsy. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). Parallel to the literature review, a total of 17 experts and 13 individuals from the Target End‐User Group (CP patients or their caregivers) were interviewed. The Expert Group was interviewed employing a semi‐structured approach to elicit experience‐based practical insights not represented in the academic literature. The Expert Group included specialists in medical informatics, rehabilitation (OT/ST/PT), health information management, and experienced physicians. The Target End‐User Group consisted of CP patients or their primary caregivers, providing crucial insights into real‐life needs and usability. The interviews aimed to explore informed views on system functionalities, user requirements, and issues pertinent to Iran's healthcare environment. The semi‐structured approach allowed commonality across interviews, together with the ability to follow up on surfacing themes.

PRISMA flow diagram of the scoping review.

The findings of each of the sources were integrated to develop a list of initial system requirements. The list became the basis of validation and prioritization in the subsequent Delphi rounds.

Phase 2: Drafting the Delphi Tool

Findings from phase1 (scoping review and semi‐ structured interview) were condensed into a set of draft system requirements, classified into functional (e.g., monitoring of the patient, scheduling of therapy) and non‐functional categories (e.g., security, accessibility, scalability). The initial list was refined through a strict process by the multidisciplinary research group, comprising medical informatics experts, rehabilitation specialists (occupational therapy, speech therapy, and physiotherapy), health information management professionals, psychologists, and experienced physicians who had developed and/or implemented telerehabilitation systems, to obtain the required clarity, comprehensiveness, and relevance of each item to home‐based telerehabilitation in CP patients in Iran. Content Review and Refinement by the research team was used to make the wording of items clearer, remove redundancies, and identify if there were any missing requirements. No items were entirely removed from the draft list at this stage; modifications were limited to achieving optimal clarity and eliminating overlap. The refined list was then converted into a formal questionnaire to be applied in the Delphi process.

The questionnaire applied a 5‐point Likert scale to each item, allowing participants to weigh the importance of each requirement from 1 (not at all important) to 5 (very important). This quantitative assessment facilitated systematic appraisal and comparison of the opinions of experts over a standard metric. For the purposes of eliciting richer qualitative data, the questionnaire also provided open‐ended feedback spaces following each item. These spaces prompted participants to make extra comments, explanations, or propose new requirements not yet uncovered. Through this mixed method of using closed‐ended Likert ratings coupled with open‐ended feedback, measurable consensus creation was ensured along with the inclusion of varied experts’ opinions, allowing a stronger and context‐sensitive determination of key system requirements.

Phase 3: Delphi Rounds

To ensure that system development requirements are grounded in expert judgment and context‐specific relevance, a method of purposive sampling was employed to obtain a panel of experts. The experts chosen represented a broad spectrum of professional categories that ranged from clinicians and rehabilitation specialists to nurses and IT health information professionals, through to system developers. The recruitment process is performed through professional networks, academic institutions, and appropriate national organizations (e.g., Iranian Telemedicine Council and Iran Comprehensive Rehabilitation Center) with a special focus on rehabilitation and health informatics. This multidisciplinary process was used to capture a wide range of perspectives across the home‐based tele‐rehabilitation's clinical, technical, and operational areas. Eligibility criteria for participation required individuals to have at least 5 years of work experience in a related field and current engagement with clinical or system‐level rehabilitation services.

Round 1

Participants indicated the perceived importance of each system requirement and also offered qualitative feedback. Those requirements that achieved a mean rating of ≥ 3.75 and ≥ 80% of respondents in agreement were kept. New items or rewording ideas were debated and implemented where needed. New items or rewording ideas were debated and implemented where needed.

Round 2

The updated list, statistical summations, and anonymized group feedback were received by the same panel. Participants re‐evaluated each item. Requirements with ≥ 80% agreement were classified to have achieved a consensus and form part of the set of finalized requirements. A mean score of ≥ 3.75 was selected as a conservative consensus threshold, ensuring that the average importance rating for included requirements clearly exceeded the midpoint and approached the ‘Very Important’ category on the 5‐point Likert scale. This value, alongside the ≥ 80% agreement, signifies a high degree of confidence in the final requirement set.

Data Analysis

Quantitative measures were assessed using descriptive statistics (standard deviation, mean, percentage of agreement). Discrepancies that did not meet thresholds were debated by the research group with a view to possible modification or elimination. Qualitative feedback was inductively thematically analyzed through content analysis to guide item refinement between rounds.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR. MUI. NUREMA. REC.1401.166). Voluntary and informed consent were facilitated through electronic completion. Anonymity and confidentiality were preserved throughout. Moreover, the completion of each survey round was assumed to constitute consent.

Results

Participant Demographics

In the first round of the Delphi study, 30 questionnaires were distributed to experts in medical informatics, health information technology, medical librarianship, occupational therapy, speech therapy, and physiotherapy. Of these, 17 experts responded (71% male and 29% female). The average age of participants was 43 years, and all of them had doctoral degrees in relevant fields and experience with CP and telerehabilitation services. The second group comprised caregivers, particularly parents (mainly mothers), of patients with CP, whose ages varied from 2 to 36 years. Most patients’ conditions were congenital, with diagnoses given either at birth or within the first year of life, underscoring the need for long‐term care within this demographic. Identified Requirements

Based on the results of the qualitative study and the scoping review, the needs of the tele‐rehabilitation system were identified. A total of 11 key subsystems were identified, encompassing a wide range of services critical to a home‐based telerehabilitation system. 142 identified requirements were defined and classified into functional (121) and non‐functional [20] categories. Functional requirements were assigned to 11 subsystems for a home‐based tele‐rehabilitation system for CP patients. Frequency of 121 functional requirements to each subsystem included: Remote Exercise Therapy (22; 15.5%), Remote Education (21; 14.8%), Remote Counseling (13; 9.2%). Other prominent subsystems included Remote Speech Therapy (11; 7.7%) and Remote Measurement (10; 7.0%). The less frequent subsystems included: User Management (8; 5.6%), Medical Data Management (7; 4.9%), Information Dissemination (6; 4.2%), and Support Services (4; 2.8%). Of these, the Remote Education and Remote Exercise Therapy subsystems had the highest number of identified requirements, with 22 and 21 items, respectively. The Support Services subsystem registered the least number of requirements (n = 4) (Table 2).

In addition to the functional subsystems, the 21 non‐functional requirements are classified into seven quality categories: Usability, security, performance, availability, adaptivity, compatibility, and interoperability.

Two‐Rounds Delphi for Requirements Analysis

Two rounds of a Delphi study were carried out to identify and order the functional and non‐functional requirements of a home‐based telerehabilitation information system. The expert panel rated functional and non‐functional requirements of each subsystem using quantitative measures on a five‐point Likert scale. A total of 127 requirements, with an average score of ≥ 3.75, were endorsed and finalized by the study. The 16 requirements with mean scores between 2.5 and 3.75 also moved to a second stage of evaluation. Interestingly, no requirement received a value of below 2.5. Of the subsystems examined, Distance Education, Remote Occupational Therapy, and Therapeutic Exercises were seen to have the most requirements, highlighting the importance of each within the system. In the second round, the 16 requirements that failed to meet the round one threshold of consensus were resubmitted to the same panel of experts along with their average scores and condensed feedback. Following re‐assessment, the 16 requirements also achieved the threshold of consensus through ≥ 80% agreement and were placed on the final list of requirements (The scores for both functional and non‐functional requirements are detailed in Tables A1 and A2 in the Appendix A section).

Discussion

This study aimed to identify and finalize, through expert consensus, the functional and non‐functional requirements for a home‐based telerehabilitation system for CP patients in Iran. Our study successfully identified 142 requirements. 121 functional requirements were assigned to 11 subsystems for home‐based tele‐rehabilitation system for CP patients. 21 non‐functiona requirements identified.

Among the functional subsystems, remote education, remote exercise therapy, and remote occupational therapy proved to be the most important subsystems based on the number of supported requirements [21]. The result highlights the pivotal importance of organized, customized exercise regimens and caregiver‐centered education interventions in the therapeutic processes, emphasizing the high focus on supporting patient independence and caregiver involvement. The high attention on these aspects is in agreement with the results from Chen et al. (2015) and Kairy et al. (2013) studies, which show exercise and patient/caregiver education consistently rank as the highest priority because of their direct effects on functional recovery and empowerment in home‐based care settings [22, 23]. Similarly, educating caregivers is crucial, as they serve as daily facilitators of therapy and can significantly influence outcomes, particularly for children. In this regard, a systematic review by Ogourtsova et al. (2023) emphasized the effectiveness of telerehabilitation for enhanced outcomes in children and adolescents with developmental disabilities and the need for caregiver engagement and tailored interventions [24].

Although this study highlighted the central role of remote education and exercise therapy, the effectiveness of such interventions strongly depends on robust tele‐assessment. In these contexts, expensive advanced equipment like remote sensing and motion analysis sensors takes a lower priority due to a high cost and limited accessibility of such technology to patients. Individualized planning and monitoring cannot be achieved without accurate and reliable assessment. Ünes (2025) stressed that tele‐assessment is foundational in family‐centered CP [25], while Üneş et al. (2025) confirmed the reliability of remote trunk control assessment [20]. Lavorgna et al. (2024) noted the growing role of tele‐assessment across rare neurological disorders, underlining its importance for CP [26]. Moreover, Magalhães et al. introduced the Gross Motor Family Report, showing how family‐reported outcomes can strengthen caregiver involvement [27]. the result reflects a consensus to balance clinical necessity with local technological and economic feasibility, prioritizing core functional requirements over expensive, hardware‐dependent integration. This finding suggests a necessary shift in focus towards feasible tele‐assessment modalities, relying heavily on video‐based evaluations and family‐reported outcome in the LMIC context. therefore, sustainable home‐based telerehabilitation must integrate tele‐assessment as a prerequisite for individualized therapy delivery. Advances in this area complement our requirement framework and underscore the interdependence between tele‐assessment and telerehabilitation.

Patients with CP often require long‐term, repetitive, and adaptive exercises to improve motor function. The ability to deliver these interventions at home, supported by features such as virtual reality‐based training, use of exergames, and interactive educational modules, can greatly reduce the care burden of disease and improve adherence [28]. Furthermore, the large number of requirements in remote counseling, and remote speech therapy demonstrates the comprehensive nature of the rehabilitation of CP, involving a combination of motor, psychosocial, and cognitive interventions within the tele‐rehabilitation platform.

In addition, the non‐functional requirements identified in this research (e.g., usability of the system, multi‐device support, and scalability) resonate with findings from telehealth platform research in broader contexts. Usability and access across different platforms have consistently been cited as important indicators of system adoption and satisfaction [29, 30]. High ratings for these attributes in this study attest to their importance in tele‐rehabilitation systems that need to be used by non‐specialist populations, such as parents and caregivers. In this regard, Yassin et al. (2020) introduced a cost‐effective, portable EMG biofeedback system to facilitate the remote interaction gap between therapists and patients. Their innovation aligns with key priorities identified in our study, including real‐time monitoring, secure communications, and individualized therapy planning. Notably, the system's interactive design, featuring dual smartphone applications, enables clinicians to track muscle activity remotely, thereby supporting the core objectives of remote monitoring and control, remote measurement, and remote therapy subsystems [31].

Similarly, the highest rank in usability, security, and compatibility as major non‐functional requirements is also in agreement with usability research by Parmanto et al. (2016), which highlights the success of remote therapy approaches to extending access and continuity of care and cites ease of use and data security as paramount in ensuring long‐term compliance and system trust [32]. Also, another study revealed that usability, access across multiple devices, and secure exchange of information are critical elements pertaining to user satisfaction and system adoption for pediatric telehealth technology [33]. Moreover, Fiani et al. highlighted the importance of telerehabilitation in the context of the COVID‐19 pandemic as a valid option to replace face‐to‐face treatment, particularly in pain reduction and physical function improvement in patients with spinal and musculoskeletal disorders. Their work also highlights the necessity of accessibility, user‐friendliness, data security, and technological availability as the basic pillars of a sustainable telerehabilitation system [34].

The major focus on non‐functional requirements underscores the recognition that clinical effectiveness alone is insufficient. A system that is difficult to navigate, insecure, or prone to technical instability will ultimately limit user adoption and therapeutic continuity. Given that many caregivers may have limited digital literacy, ensuring a user‐friendly and intuitive interface is critical for successful implementation and sustained use.

While aligned with existing research, our study also varies in some important ways. In contrast to many telehealth protocols developed in developed countries with emphasis on interoperability standards (e.g., HL7, FHIR) and electronic health records (EHR) integration, our panel assigned comparatively more value to aspects promoting user empowerment, including feedback mechanisms, personalized plans, and real‐time engagement. This might also reflect particular contextual requirements in Iran's healthcare setting, where caregiver‐led home‐based care is of particular importance due to limited access to continuous rehabilitation services. Furthermore, the inclusion of culturally sensitive functionalities, such as support for educational content delivery via offline media (e.g., flash drives, CDs), highlights practical challenges in low‐resource or variable‐connectivity settings that are less emphasized in higher‐income countries. This adaptation to local infrastructure and user capacity strengthens the system's applicability in its intended context.

However, this study differentiates itself by providing a subsystem‐based analysis with a validated requirement framework, which previous studies lack. While previous studies often addressed tele‐rehabilitation as an undivided entity, our study offers a component‐based segmentation, with a more practical guideline provided for developers. The addition of lesser‐discussed subsystems, such as Information Dissemination, Support Services, and Monitoring and Control, contributes to the novelty of this model and adds to its comprehensiveness.

Strengths and Limitations

One of the main strengths of the present study is its application of a systematic two‐round Delphi process guided by the CREDES guidelines, which provided an evident and structured method for eliciting expert consensus. The involvement of a multidisciplinary panel, including specialists in medical informatics, rehabilitation science, clinical practice, and health IT, assured the integration of technical and clinical perspectives. Furthermore, the strong engagement rate (57%) and complete consensus achieved after two rounds indicate engagement and clarity of study design. Also, the context‐specific nature of the requirements, based on local needs, healthcare infrastructure, and user capabilities, is another strength, indicating the practical and culturally appropriate nature of the system.

Despite its strengths, the study has several limitations. First, despite involving a multidisciplinary panel of experts, they were geographically confined to Iran, which may limit the generalizability of the data to other healthcare systems. Second, the lack of direct involvement of patients and caregivers in the Delphi process can limit the identification of real‐life experience requirements, usability needs, and practical challenges of these patients. Third, even though the Delphi process evaluates the expert opinion, the feasibility or real‐life effectiveness of the requirements is not assessed, which requires further validation through system prototyping, pilot testing, and clinical evaluation.

Conclusion

This Delphi study identified and finalized (through expert consensus) a comprehensive set of functional and non‐functional requirements for a home‐based telerehabilitation system tailored to Iranian CP patients. The results highlight the importance of user‐centered design, caregiver involvement, and engaging interactive technologies for home rehabilitation. By addressing technical, clinical, and contextual factors, these findings lay a foundation for culturally appropriate system development. Future research should focus on prototyping, usability testing, and clinical validation, actively involving patients and caregivers in the evaluation phase to assess real‐world feasibility and effectiveness.
