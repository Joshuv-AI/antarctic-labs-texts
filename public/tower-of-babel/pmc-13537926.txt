Global Alignment and Proportion (GAP) Score in Predicting Mechanical Complications After Long‐Segment Fixation for Chinese Geriatric Patients With Adult Degenerative Scoliosis: A Mid‐Term Comparative Study
Authors: Xiangyao Sun, Jiang Huang, Yuqi Liu, Siyuan Sun, Li Cao, Juyong Wang, Shibao Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe predictive value of the global alignment and proportion (GAP) score in the Chinese geriatric population, which has unique demographic and degenerative characteristics, has not been validated. Given the high incidence of mechanical complications in this specific cohort, a dedicated mid‐term evaluation is essential to guide surgical planning. To address this gap, this study aimed to analyze the accuracy and characteristics of the GAP assessment system in predicting mechanical complications following long‐segment fixation surgery for geriatric patients with adult degenerative scoliosis (ADS) in a mid‐term follow up period.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted to select eligible ADS patients who had undergone long‐segment fixation surgery at Xuanwu Hospital Capital Medical University between December 2016 and October 2020. Patients were divided into groups with mechanical complications (MC) and nonmechanical complications (NMC) for comparison. The GAP score and its classification were calculated based on the measurements. The association between GAP scores and mechanical complications was examined using logistic regression analysis. Additionally, the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was utilized to evaluate the predictive performance of the GAP score for mechanical complications and to determine the threshold of the assessment system.ResultsA total of 133 ADS patients were included, with 65 in the MC group and 68 in the NMC group. The GAP score (area under the curve, AUC = 0.654) outperformed the GAP categories (AUC = 0.650) in predicting mechanical complications, and it was particularly accurate for proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) (AUC = 0.872). However, both the GAP score (AUC = 0.201) and GAP categories (AUC = 0.181) demonstrated poor accuracy in predicting internal fixation breakage.ConclusionThe GAP score varied in its predictive performance and thresholds for different mechanical complications and internal fixation‐related issues, exhibiting high accuracy for predicting PJK. However, it did not effectively indicate the risk of internal fixation breakage.

Introduction

Adult degenerative scoliosis (ADS) is characterized as a three‐dimensional spinal deformity with a Cobb angle exceeding 10°, with an incidence rate ranging from 1.5% to 29.4% [1, 2, 3, 4]. As the population ages and the demand for a higher quality of life increases, ADS has garnered significant attention [5]. The pathophysiological features of ADS primarily involve asymmetric degeneration of intervertebral discs and facet joints across different segments, leading to an imbalance in the load distribution of the spinal three‐column structure [6, 7]. This asymmetric loading and spinal degeneration serve as driving factors in the progression of ADS, ultimately resulting in a three‐dimensional deformity of the spine [6, 7]. To more accurately characterize ADS, several widely recognized assessment systems are commonly employed. These include the Scoliosis Research Society‐Schwab classification (SRS‐Schwab classification) [8], the Roussouly classification [9], and the Global Alignment and Proportion Score (GAP score) [10].

According to the SRS‐Schwab classification, the surgical goals for deformity correction should be set as follows: pelvic tilt (PT) < 20°, pelvic incidence (PI) minus lumbar lordosis (LL) mismatch (PI‐LL) < 10°, and sagittal vertical axis (SVA) < 4 cm [8, 11]. However, utilizing such classifications for corrective surgery might not adequately address the individualized characteristics of ADS patients, which contributed to a persistently high rate of postoperative complications [12]. As a result, Roussouly et al. [9] established a new system by analyzing the characteristics of spinal‐pelvic parameters in a normal population. Previous studies have indicated that developing surgical strategies for ADS based on the Roussouly classification can reduce the incidence of mechanical complications and the need for revision surgeries [13, 14, 15]. However, the Roussouly classification does not take into account patient age or health‐related quality of life (HRQL) scoring systems, which may limit its accuracy in assessing ADS.

In addition to the Roussouly classification, the GAP score is also utilized for preoperative assessment of ADS [10]. The GAP score evaluates the sagittal plane morphology of the spine using sagittal imaging parameters based on PI. Specifically, the GAP score comprises five components: (1) relative pelvic version (RPV), which assesses the degree of pelvic retroversion; (2) relative lumbar lordosis (RLL), which evaluates the magnitude of lumbar lordosis relative to the ideal value calculated from PI; (3) lordosis distribution index (LDI), which quantifies the distribution of lordosis between the upper and lower lumbar segments; (4) relative spinopelvic alignment (RSA), which measures the global tilt relative to the ideal value; and (5) age, which accounts for the expected degenerative changes in spinal alignment. Each component is scored from 0 to 2 or 0 to 3 points based on deviation from ideal values, with a total score ranging from 0 to 13. Patients are then classified into three categories: proportioned (0–3 points), moderately disproportioned (4–7 points), and severely disproportioned (≥ 8 points). Both the Roussouly classification and GAP score propose the use of ideal LL, ideal PT, and the distribution of ideal LL to achieve optimal spinal‐pelvic alignment [10]. Implementing corrective strategies based on the GAP score prior to ADS corrective surgery has been shown to reduce the incidence of postoperative mechanical complications [16, 17]. However, there is currently a lack of research in China regarding the predictive effectiveness of the GAP score on surgical outcomes for geriatric ADS patients [18, 19]. Therefore, this study was designed to address the following research question: Does the GAP score accurately predict mechanical complications following long‐segment fixation surgery in Chinese geriatric patients with adult degenerative scoliosis? We hypothesized that the GAP score would demonstrate differential predictive accuracy for various types of mechanical complications, with particularly high accuracy for proximal junctional kyphosis but limited accuracy for internal fixation breakage. Therefore, this study aimed to: (i) Analyze the accuracy and characteristics of the GAP score in predicting overall mechanical complications following long‐segment fixation surgery for Chinese geriatric patients with ADS; (ii) Evaluate the differential predictive performance of the GAP score for specific types of mechanical complications, including proximal junctional kyphosis, distal junctional failure, and implant‐related issues; (iii) Determine the optimal thresholds of the GAP score and its components for predicting these complications in this population.

Materials and Methods

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of data from patients with ADS who had undergone long‐segment fixation surgery at Xuanwu Hospital Capital Medical University between December 2016 and October 2020. Data collected included patient demographics such as gender, age, body mass index (BMI), follow‐up duration, blood loss, surgical time and number of fused vertebrae. Clinical outcomes were assessed using three validated instruments: the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), a unidimensional measure of pain intensity ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain) [20]; the Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) score, a disease‐specific functional assessment tool for low back pain evaluating subjective symptoms, clinical signs, and activities of daily living, with a maximum score of 29 points indicating normal function [21]; and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), a self‐administered questionnaire assessing limitations in activities of daily living due to back pain, with scores ranging from 0 to 100 where higher scores indicate greater disability [22]. Inclusion criteria were as follows: coronal Cobb angle (CA) ≥ 20°; SVA ≥ 5 cm; PT ≥ 25°; thoracic kyphosis (TK) ≥ 60°; age ≥ 55 years; a minimum of 4 fused segments; and all patients had at least 12 months of follow‐up. Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with other spinal conditions, autoimmune diseases, infections, tumors, or a history of prior spinal fusion surgery. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval number: [2018]083), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Surgical Procedure

All surgical procedures were performed by senior spine surgeons with extensive experience in adult spinal deformity correction. Patients underwent posterior long‐segment spinal fusion with pedicle screw‐rod instrumentation. The surgical approach involved a standard posterior midline incision with subperiosteal dissection to expose the posterior spinal elements. Pedicle screws were inserted at the appropriate levels using fluoroscopic guidance, with the upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) typically located at T10 or above and the lower instrumented vertebra (LIV) extending to the sacrum or pelvis in most cases. Interbody fusion cages were placed at the L4‐L5 and/or L5‐S1 levels when indicated to restore disc height and enhance anterior column support. Decompression was performed at levels with significant stenosis or neural compression. Corrective maneuvers included rod contouring to the desired sagittal profile, compression‐distraction techniques, and in situ bending to achieve optimal coronal and sagittal alignment. Bone grafting was performed using a combination of local autograft and allograft. The specific fusion levels and use of interbody fusion were determined based on individual patient characteristics, including the extent of deformity, degree of instability, and presence of stenosis. Postoperatively, all patients were mobilized as tolerated with thoracolumbosacral orthosis (TLSO) brace support for 3 months.

Imaging Parameters and Scoring System

Imaging parameters were measured according to the criteria of the GAP score [10], which primarily included the following: CA, PI, PT, sacral slope (SS), relative pelvic version (RPV), TK, LL, L4‐S1 lumbar lordosis (L4‐S1 LL), relative lumbar lordosis (RLL), lordosis distribution index (LDI), global tilt (GT), SVA, and relative spinopelvic alignment (RSA) (Table 1, Figure 1). The GAP score and its categories were calculated based on the measured values [10] (Table 2). Each component is scored from 0 to 2 or 0 to 3 points based on deviation from ideal values, with a total score ranging from 0 to 13. Based on the total score, patients are classified into three categories: proportioned (0–3 points), moderately disproportioned (4–7 points), and severely disproportioned (≥ 8 points). In this study, mechanical complications encompassed proximal junctional kyphosis or failure (PJK or PJF), distal junctional kyphosis or failure (DJK or DJF), as well as other internal fixation‐related complications [10] (Table 1).

In this study, mechanical complications were initially grouped together for primary analysis based on their shared characteristic of requiring revision surgery or causing significant clinical symptoms, consistent with the original GAP score validation study by Yilgor et al. [10]. This approach allows for initial evaluation of the GAP score's overall discriminative ability for adverse surgical outcomes. However, recognizing the heterogeneous mechanisms underlying different complication types—namely junctional failures (PJK/PJF, DJK/DJF) resulting from biomechanical stress at construct endpoints versus implant‐related failures (loosening/breakage) influenced by material properties and bone quality—we subsequently performed subgroup analyses for each specific complication type to evaluate differential predictive performance.

Patients were categorized into two groups for comparison based on whether they experienced mechanical complications: the mechanical complication (MC) group and the nonmechanical complication (NMC) group. Radiographic evaluations were conducted preoperatively and at the final follow‐up visit. All radiographic measurements were performed independently by two experienced spine surgeons using Surgimap software (Nemaris Inc., New York, New York, USA). Prior to data collection, inter‐observer reliability was established, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) > 0.85 for all measured parameters. Any discrepancies between the two observers were resolved through discussion and consensus, with the final values for analysis calculated as the mean of the two measurements. Clinical assessments, including VAS, JOA, and ODI scores, were performed at corresponding time points: preoperatively and at the final follow‐up visit.

Statistical Methods

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Richmond, California, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed to assess the normality of the data. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, and analyzed using Pearson's chi‐squared test. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were analyzed using independent samples t‐test, while those with skewed distributions were examined using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the association between GAP scores and mechanical complications. These analyses were univariable, assessing the GAP score as a single predictor without adjustment for potential confounding factors such as bone mineral density, number of fused levels, upper and lower instrumented vertebrae levels, performance of osteotomy, or specific surgical techniques. The relationship between GAP scores and mechanical complications was discussed through logistic regression analysis. The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was utilized to clearly evaluate the predictive value of GAP scores for mechanical complications and to calculate the threshold of the assessment system. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Demographics

A total of 133 ADS patients were included in this study, comprising 107 females (80.5%) with a mean age of 76.6 ± 2.6 years. The mean body mass index was 26.7 ± 3.7 kg/m2. Surgical characteristics included a mean operative time of 442.7 ± 138.1 min, mean estimated blood loss of 516.4 ± 161.1 mL, and mean number of fused vertebrae of 5.9 ± 1.8 segments. The mean follow‐up duration was 18.9 ± 6.0 months (range: 13–41 months). The overall incidence of mechanical complications was 48.9% (65/133 patients). Overall, mechanical complications occurred at a mean follow‐up of 19.2 ± 4.3 months (range: 13–27 months). Regarding specific complication types, proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) occurred earliest at a mean of 17.3 ± 5.0 months, followed by proximal junctional failure (PJF) at 19.0 months, distal junctional kyphosis or failure (DJK/F) at 20.0 ± 4.6 months, and implant‐related complications at 21.8 ± 6.9 months. Notably, implant breakage occurred latest at 31.7 ± 14.5 months, indicating a distinct late‐onset pattern compared to other complication types. Proximal junctional kyphosis occurred in nine patients (6.8%) and proximal junctional failure in three patients (2.3%). Distal junctional kyphosis or failure was observed in four patients (3.0%). Implant‐related complications were the most common, occurring in 53 patients (39.8%), including implant loosening in 47 patients (35.3%) and implant breakage in six patients (4.5%). ROC Analysis for the Scoring System in Predicting Mechanical Complications

The ROC analysis revealed that the GAP score (area under the curve, AUC = 0.654) significantly outperformed the GAP score categories in predicting mechanical complications (AUC = 0.650). The GAP score was particularly accurate in predicting PJK (AUC = 0.872) (Table 6, Figure 2). However, both the GAP score (AUC = 0.201) and the GAP score categories (AUC = 0.181) demonstrated poor accuracy in predicting internal fixation breakage.

Discussion

Main Findings of This Study

This study investigated the predictive value of the GAP score for mechanical complications in Chinese geriatric patients undergoing long‐segment fixation for ADS. The principal findings were threefold. First, the GAP score as a continuous variable (AUC = 0.654) outperformed the categorical GAP classification (AUC = 0.650) in predicting overall mechanical complications. Second, the predictive performance varied markedly across different complication types, demonstrating high accuracy for proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK; AUC = 0.872) but a poor and inverse predictive ability for internal fixation breakage (AUC = 0.201). Third, logistic regression analysis confirmed that higher GAP scores were significantly associated with overall mechanical complications, PJK, and implant loosening, but not with PJF or DJK/DJF. These findings indicate that the GAP score has differential utility depending on the specific complication of interest.

Overall Predictive Performance of the GAP Score

The GAP score, with its core concept of scoring relative to an ideal value based on PI, initially demonstrated promising predictability for mechanical complications in the work by Yilgor et al. [10]. Subsequent validation studies have reported varying AUCs depending on the population and complication definitions [16, 17, 18, 23]. A recent meta‐analysis by Cho et al. [18] indicated that the GAP score possesses minimal‐to‐moderate predictability for MC following ASD surgery, with a pooled AUC of 0.69. In the present study, the GAP score yielded an AUC of 0.654, which is consistent with the moderate performance reported in other Chinese cohorts [18] and falls within the range of the published meta‐analysis. While this AUC is lower than that reported in the original derivation study [10], it is comparable to values obtained in recent large‐scale and Asian validation studies [16, 24]. This suggests that the GAP score retains a fair discriminative ability in our specific geriatric Chinese population, but its overall predictive accuracy may be influenced by population‐specific characteristics.

Differential Predictive Performance Across Complication Types

A critical finding of this study was the marked variation in predictive accuracy across different types of mechanical complications. The GAP score demonstrated high accuracy for predicting PJK (AUC = 0.872), yet exhibited a poor and inverse predictive ability for internal fixation breakage (AUC = 0.201). This disparity reflects a fundamental mechanistic distinction: PJK is directly related to sagittal malalignment and stress concentration at the proximal junction, which the GAP score is designed to capture, whereas implant breakage is primarily influenced by material fatigue, bone‐screw interface failure, and fixation strategy—factors largely independent of global alignment [12, 15].

The high accuracy for PJK prediction can be attributed to the GAP score's ability to capture the segmental compensatory mechanisms through the lordosis distribution index (LDI) and relative pelvic measurements. As described by Pizones et al. [13] and Bari et al. [15], restoring harmonious lordosis distribution reduces stress concentration at the proximal junction. In our cohort, patients with PJK demonstrated significantly worse postoperative RPV and RLL scores, confirming that these alignment parameters are particularly relevant to junctional failure. PT plays a vital role in compensating for forward sagittal imbalance; as PT increases, the pelvis tilts backward and the sacral endplate becomes more horizontal [25, 26]. By measuring RPV and RSA, the GAP score reflects the degree of pelvic compensation needed to maintain a normal spinal‐pelvic state, which is critical for guiding lumbar‐sacral relationships during surgery and reducing PJK [13]. However, we recognize that this finding was based on a limited number of PJK events (n = 9), and thus should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating pending validation in larger cohorts.

Conversely, the poor prediction for implant breakage underscores a critical limitation of alignment‐based scoring systems. Paradoxically, our data suggest that patients with better alignment may have experienced slightly higher breakage rates, possibly because rigid correction to ideal alignment in osteoporotic bone increases construct stiffness and stress shielding, accelerating implant fatigue. Alternatively, this inverse relationship may reflect selection bias, where surgeons applied more aggressive correction strategies to patients with better baseline alignment. This finding emphasizes that alignment optimization alone cannot prevent implant‐related failures, and complementary assessment of bone quality and construct biomechanics is essential for comprehensive risk stratification.

Previous studies that pooled all mechanical complications into a single endpoint reported only moderate overall predictive performance [18, 23]. Our subgroup analysis demonstrates that the GAP score—being an alignment‐based tool—performs well for junctional kyphosis but poorly for implant failures. This heterogeneity likely dilutes the overall AUC when complications are analyzed together. Future studies should consider stratified primary analyses based on mechanistic categories rather than aggregate mechanical complications.

Continuous Score Versus Categorical Classification

An important methodological observation from this study is that the GAP score as a continuous variable (AUC = 0.654) outperformed the categorical GAP classification (AUC = 0.650) in predicting mechanical complications. This finding aligns with the original design by Yilgor et al. [10] and the observations of Hiltunen et al. [17], who noted that dichotomization leads to information loss and reduced accuracy. A previous meta‐analysis by Cho et al. [18] categorized data based on GAP groups and concluded that the GAP score had difficulty in predicting complications. Our data suggest that this conclusion may have been partly influenced by the analytical approach; using the GAP score as a continuous variable preserves its full variability and provides a more nuanced risk assessment. Consequently, future validation studies should avoid sole reliance on categorical comparisons and instead incorporate continuous measures and ROC analyses.

Furthermore, the GAP score's components demonstrated differential associations with mechanical complications. In our cohort, postoperative RPV, RLL, and GT showed significant differences between the MC and NMC groups, whereas the LDI demonstrated a less pronounced independent correlation. This lack of discrimination by LDI has been similarly observed in other validation studies [27, 28], suggesting a potential intrinsic limitation of this component when applied to populations with different sagittal profiles. The variation in predictive performance across complication types also arises from the distinct mechanisms underlying different complications, resulting in differential impacts from the various GAP score components [24, 29].

Role of Age and Population‐Specific Factors

The GAP score is the only widely used classification system that quantitatively incorporates patient age, providing a clear indicator of how age‐related degenerative changes influence MC risk. This represents a potential advantage over the SRS‐Schwab and Roussouly classifications, which do not account for age [8, 9]. LL gradually declines with age and ADS progression, and TK tends to increase, both affecting the overall spinal profile [30]. Previous studies did not effectively analyze the impact of age on assessment systems, which has influenced predictions of surgical outcomes [8, 11]. In our cohort, the age component score was significantly higher in the MC group, confirming that older age contributes to MC risk independently of radiographic alignment. All patients in this study were aged 55 years and older, and the fair predictive role of the GAP score in these older adults is consistent with the results of Ham et al. [16]. However, elderly patients may tolerate mild sagittal imbalance better due to adaptive compensatory mechanisms, and rigid adherence to ideal parameters may not always translate to proportional clinical benefits in this population [16].

Clinical Implications

The interpretation of postoperative sagittal alignment should integrate quantitative GAP score assessments with qualitative observations. The restoration of lumbar lordosis relative to PI (RLL), the distribution of lordosis (LDI), and the global alignment (GT, RSA) are key aspects to evaluate [10, 16]. Clinically, successful sagittal realignment typically manifests as reduced VAS and ODI scores and improved functional capacity [31, 32, 33]. Glassman et al. [33] found a strong linear relationship between positive sagittal imbalance and back pain severity. However, correcting SVA to standard ranges can lead to overcorrection and increased mechanical complications [12, 34], a notion supported by our 48.9% complication rate despite alignment correction. The calculation of ideal parameters based on PI enables individualized assessment, and the GAP score effectively captures pelvic compensation through RPV and RSA, aiding in the prediction of decompensation risk [13, 16]. However, most current studies remain retrospective with manual measurements. The development of automated GAP scoring systems may enhance efficiency and consistency, representing an important direction for future clinical application.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several strengths. First, it provides a detailed, subtype‐level analysis of mechanical complications rather than treating them as a single entity, revealing differential predictive performance that would have been obscured in a pooled analysis. Second, it focuses on a specific geriatric Chinese population that was previously underrepresented in GAP score validation studies. Third, the direct comparison of continuous versus categorical GAP assessments offers methodological insights for future research.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the participants were predominantly older, and the findings may not be generalizable to younger cohorts. Secondly, the analysis focused solely on GAP score parameters without addressing compensatory roles of paravertebral muscles and lower limbs. When paravertebral muscles are weak and PT is significant, pelvic compensatory capacity may be reduced; failure of lower limb compensation increases the likelihood of sagittal balance failure. In addition, bone mineral density was not systematically measured, precluding adjustment for bone quality—a known independent predictor of screw loosening and PJK. Future prospective studies should include DXA or CT‐based bone‐density metrics and correlate them with GAP parameters. Thirdly, radiographic measurements were performed by two observers through consensus review without formal inter‐ and intra‐observer reliability statistics, limiting reproducibility assessment. Fourthly, the logistic regression models were univariable, without adjustment for key confounders such as BMD, number of fused vertebrae, or surgical technique. These factors may represent independent risk factors and could influence the predictive performance of the GAP score. This analytical approach may overestimate the independent predictive value of the GAP score, and future studies should employ multivariable regression analysis to determine its adjusted contribution. Fifthly, no formal a priori power analysis was performed, and the small event rates for rare complications (PJF, n = 3; DJK/DJF, n = 4; implant breakage, n = 6) render these subgroup analyses underpowered and exploratory. Lastly, the sample size was limited to a single center, and the mid‐term follow‐up might not capture late‐onset complications occurring beyond 2–3 years postoperatively. Larger, multicenter, prospective studies with longer follow‐up are warranted to validate these findings. Despite these limitations, the present study holds considerable reference value as one of the first to validate the GAP score in a Chinese geriatric ADS population.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the GAP score demonstrated moderate predictive performance for the risk of mechanical complications after long‐segment fixation surgery in geriatric ADS patients. The predictive capabilities and thresholds of the GAP score varied significantly across different complication types, exhibiting high accuracy for predicting PJK but limited predictive value for internal fixation breakage. This differential performance reflects the mechanistic distinction between alignment‐dependent junctional failures and implant‐related complications. The use of the GAP score as a continuous variable provides a more accurate risk assessment than categorical classification. Clinicians should therefore apply the GAP score selectively, recognizing its variable performance across complication types, and consider complementary clinical and surgical factors in preoperative risk assessment. Further refinement of the GAP score or integration with additional predictive variables is needed to meet the complex requirements for postoperative assessment in ADS patients.
