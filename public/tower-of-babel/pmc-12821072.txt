Retrograde control of sympathetic neuron-satellite glia interactions by target-derived NGF signaling
Authors: Raniki Kumari, Erica Boehm, Raluca Pascalau, Rebecca L. Pfeiffer, Bryan W. Jones, Emmanouil Tampakakis, Rejji Kuruvilla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYSatellite glial cells (SGCs) are the major glial cells in sympathetic ganglia contacting neuronal cell bodies. The intimate association of SGCs with sympathetic neurons ideally positions these glia as critical regulators of neuronal homeostasis, architecture, and function. However, how these neuron-glia interactions are established remains unclear. Here, we find a contact-mediated pathway triggered by retrograde signaling from innervated sympathetic targets that underlies neuron-SGC interactions, neuronal morphology, and functional output. We show that neuronal expression of a transmembrane protein, Delta/Notch-like EGF-related receptor (DNER), is dependent on signaling by target-derived nerve growth factor (NGF). Neuronal DNER deletion disrupts neuron-SGC contacts and results in aberrant neuronal morphology, including decreased soma size and hyper-innervation of targets in mice. DNER mutant neurons have elevated activity, and mice lacking neuronal DNER exhibit increased heart rate and thermogenesis, indicative of enhanced sympathetic tone. These results suggest a mechanism whereby innervated targets control assembly of functional neuron-glia units in the sympathetic nervous system.

INTRODUCTION

The sympathetic nervous system is a major conduit for communication between the brain and the periphery. Sympathetic neuron cell bodies, resident in ganglia within the peripheral nervous system (PNS), send long axonal projections throughout the body to innervate diverse peripheral organs and tissues to control a broad spectrum of physiological processes, including heart rate, blood pressure, body temperature, blood glucose levels, and immune function.1 However, sympathetic neurons are not alone in the ganglia; the majority of ganglionic cells are satellite glial cells (SGCs), which fully envelop individual neuron cell bodies and dendrites, as well as covering synapses between pre-ganglionic and post-ganglionic neurons.2,3 Given this intimate association, it has been proposed that each neuron and associated glia form discrete structural and functional units in the transmission of neural signals from the brain to the periphery to influence organ physiology.3,4 Emerging studies highlight diverse roles for SGCs in modulating neuronal excitability,5–7 functional output to peripheral organs,5,8 clearing dying neurons during development,9 and axon regeneration after injury.10 Even amid this increasing attention, SGCs largely remain a vastly under-studied cell type in the nervous system with limited understanding of neuron-SGC communication and how they contribute to peripheral circuits.

SGCs have been defined primarily by their unique morphology, forming characteristic circular patterns around neuronal soma, with only 20 nm of space, the width of a synaptic cleft, between neuron and glial membranes.3,11–13 Multiple SGCs surround a single neuron and are connected with each other, and with the neuron, via adhesive contacts and gap junctions.3,12,14 The intimate physical contacts between SGCs and neuronal soma ideally position these cells to monitor and regulate the neuronal microenvironment and to be critical regulators of neuronal connectivity, synaptic transmission, and homeostasis. However, how these neuron-SGC units are established during development and how disruptions in this process impact neuronal connectivity and function remain largely undefined.

Like sympathetic neurons, SGCs, which are specific to the PNS, are derived embryonically from multi-potent migratory neural crest precursors. Compared to sympathetic neurons, relatively little is known about SGC development. Based on limited studies in rats, neural crest precursors are committed to glial or neuronal fates as they migrate into sympathetic ganglia at embryonic days E11–E12.15 The bulk of SGCs are generated during a rapid phase of proliferation between E16 and E18.16 By birth, SGCs appear to have established contacts with neuronal soma and, 2 weeks later, acquire their mature glial architecture of ring-like sheaths around cell bodies.5,16 The timing of SGC proliferation and association with sympathetic neurons are coincident with axon innervation of target tissues,1,16 raising the intriguing possibility that a peripheral signal dictates satellite glia development. The importance of innervated targets in instructing neuron development has been appreciated since the pioneering work of Rita Levi-Montalcini, Viktor Hamburger, and colleagues.17 For sympathetic neurons, the neurotrophin nerve growth factor (NGF) is a target-derived signal that is essential for many aspects of neuron development, including survival, growth of axons and dendrites, synapse formation, and acquisition of neurotransmitter phenotypes.1 Since NGF is produced by sympathetic targets, NGF signaling is communicated long distance in neurons via retrograde trafficking of internalized NGF:TrkA receptor complexes.18,19 NGF binds to its TrkA receptor tyrosine kinase expressed on sympathetic axons, prompting endocytosis of ligand-receptor complexes into vesicles.18,19 TrkA vesicles signal in axons to acutely regulate local growth events and are also transported back to cell bodies to exert transcriptional control of developmental programs.

Here, we uncover a contact-mediated mechanism triggered by NGF signaling, which underlies neuron-SGC interactions in sympathetic ganglia during development. We found that loss of the NGF receptor, TrkA, from sympathetic neurons elicits defects in neuron-SGC contacts. We show that TrkA controls the expression of a transmembrane protein, Delta/Notch-like EGF-related Receptor (DNER), in sympathetic neurons, and this expression is confined to somatodendritic compartments where SGCs attach. Neuron-specific deletion of DNER disrupts neuron-SGC interactions, similar to the absence of neuronal TrkA. Further, loss of neuronal DNER elicits pronounced effects on neuronal morphology, metabolic signaling, and neuronal activity, resulting in decreased neuron soma size, hyper-innervation of targets, increased neuronal activity, and enhanced release of the sympathetic neurotransmitter, norepinephrine (NE). Consistently, DNER conditional knockout (cKO) mice exhibit elevated sympathetic responses, including heart rate and non-shivering thermogenesis, indicative of enhanced sympathetic tone. Together, our results provide evidence that neuron-SGC contacts established during development are necessary for regulating neuronal morphology, metabolic support, neuronal activity, and output to peripheral organs/tissues. Further, our study suggests a mechanism whereby retrograde signaling from innervated peripheral target organs/tissues controls sympathetic neuron-SGC interactions in the ganglia to coordinate the assembly of functional circuits.

RESULTS

TrkA receptors expressed in sympathetic neurons are required for SGC organization

SGC morphogenesis and enveloping of neuronal cell bodies coincides with the period when sympathetic axons innervate end-organs and gain access to target-derived NGF.7,16,20 Thus, we asked whether retrograde NGF:TrkA signaling in neurons is involved in neuron-SGC interactions. We examined SGC organization in mice with conditional deletion of TrkA from sympathetic neurons (Th-Cre;TrkAfl/fl mice). To prevent neuronal death in the absence of TrkA, we concomitantly deleted Bax, an essential gene for neuronal apoptosis,21 to generate Th-Cre;TrkAfl/fl; Bax−/− mice (henceforth referred to as TrkA:Bax dKO mice). Immunostaining using an anti-TrkA antibody indicated a pronounced loss of TrkA immunoreactivity in the superior cervical ganglia, the rostral-most ganglia in the sympathetic chain, from TrkA:Bax dKO mice, relative to control (Th-Cre;Bax−/−) mice at postnatal day 14 (P14) (Figures S1A and S1B), indicating effective deletion of TrkA in mutant ganglia. To assess SGC morphology and ensheathment of sympathetic neurons in TrkA;Bax dKO mice, we used immunohistochemistry for tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) to mark sympathetic neurons and Fatty Acid Binding Protein 7 (FABP7, also known as Brain Lipid Binding Protein) to label SGCs from the superior cervical ganglia in P14 mice. We chose P14 as the time point for analyses because SGCs have fully enveloped sympathetic neuron cell bodies and have acquired their mature architecture by this time.5 In control mice (TrkAfl/fl;Bax−/− or Th-Cre;Bax−/− mice), SGCs formed their characteristic ring-like structures around neuronal cell bodies (Figures 1A–1C), while this pattern was disrupted in the TrkA:Bax dKO ganglia (Figures 1D–1F). Since SGCs do not express the TrkA receptor,2 these results suggest that TrkA expression in sympathetic neurons is required for SGC organization around sympathetic neuronal cell bodies.

To further address neuron-SGC association, we performed immunostaining for Sox2, a transcription factor expressed in SGCs,22 but not other ganglionic cell types,2 together with TH to mark sympathetic neurons. Sox2 also allows for more accurate assessment of SGC numbers compared to cytoplasmic FABP7, which marks multiple satellite glial cells connected in a syncytium. We observed fewer Sox2-positive glial cells juxtaposed to cell bodies of individual sympathetic neurons in mutant ganglia (Figures 1G–1J). Quantification revealed that each mutant neuron was associated with fewer glial cells, compared to controls (Figure 1K). However, quantification of total Sox2-immunoreactive cells revealed no significant differences in glial cell numbers between control and mutant ganglia (Figure S1C). These results suggest that loss of neuronal TrkA receptors leads to disrupted contacts between neuronal soma and neighboring SGCs, without affecting glial cell proliferation or survival.

DNER is expressed in sympathetic neurons in a TrkA-dependent manner

Given the direct contacts between sympathetic neurons and SGCs, we sought to uncover the molecular basis for this transcellular interaction. In searching the literature, DNER stood out as an attractive candidate.23 DNER is a single-pass transmembrane protein with 10 epidermal growth factor (EGF) extracellular repeats and a short cytoplasmic tail,24 is reported to be a Notch ligand, and is highly enriched in neurons.23 Global deletion of DNER in mice disrupts glial morphogenesis and Purkinje neuron-Bergmann glia interactions in the developing cerebellum.23 Notably, in sympathetic ganglia, Dner was identified as an NGF-dependent gene based on transcriptomic profiling of NGF knockout mice.25 Together, these studies prompted us to investigate DNER expression and its regulation in sympathetic ganglia during development.

Using in situ hybridization, we observed Dner expression in sympathetic ganglia (superior cervical ganglia) at E18.5 (Figures S2A and S2B), a developmental time point when sympathetic axons are innervating targets and gaining access to target-derived NGF.20 Dner is specifically expressed in sympathetic neurons and is not detected in non-neuronal ganglionic cell types, including SGCs, based on RT-PCR analyses of cultures of isolated sympathetic neurons and non-neuronal ganglionic cells, respectively (Figure S2C). Of note, SGCs are the most abundant cell type in the superior cervical ganglia.2 Brain lysates were used as a positive control for Dner expression.23 Using whole-mount immunostaining with an anti-DNER antibody, we observed prominent DNER immunoreactivity in neuronal cell bodies and dendrites in sympathetic ganglia (Figures 2A and 2B), while it was excluded from sympathetic axons innervating distal target fields (Figures 2C–2E) in mice at P14. Our findings of DNER sub-cellular localization, specifically, in somatodendritic compartments in sympathetic neurons are consistent with previous reports of DNER distribution in central nervous system (CNS) neurons.24

Previously, Dner was identified as one of several hundred NGF-regulated genes in a microarray-based profiling of sympathetic ganglia isolated from neonatal NGF knockout mice.25 To determine whether NGF:TrkA signaling indeed regulates DNER expression in sympathetic neurons, we assessed DNER protein levels in TrkA;Bax dKO mice using immunohistochemistry. We observed a pronounced reduction of DNER expression in sympathetic ganglia from TrkA;Bax dKO compared to control mice at P14 (Figures 2F–2I), which was further validated by immunoblotting (Figures 2J and 2K). To directly address whether NGF regulates DNER expression, cultured sympathetic neurons were stimulated with NGF (100 ng/mL) for 8 h. We found that NGF treatment resulted in a significant 2.1-fold increase in DNER protein expression in sympathetic neuron cultures as assessed by immunoblotting (Figures S2D and S2E).

Together, these results support that DNER is a neuron-specific protein that is expressed in sympathetic neurons in an NGF:TrkA-dependent manner.

Sympathetic neuron-SGC contacts are disrupted in DNER cKO mice

Given the unique sub-cellular localization of DNER in somatodendritic compartments, which are usually enveloped by SGCs, and that DNER expression in sympathetic neurons is NGF dependent, we asked whether DNER might influence sympathetic neuron-SGC interactions. Thus, we generated conditional knockout mice, where DNER is deleted from noradrenergic neurons, including sympathetic neurons, by crossing Th-Cre mice with Dnerfl/fl mice. Th-Cre;Dnerfl/fl mice (henceforth referred to as DNER cKO mice) were born with no obvious abnormalities, at expected Mendelian frequencies, and survived to adulthood. Immunostaining using an anti-DNER antibody and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis revealed a drastic downregulation of DNER in sympathetic ganglia from postnatal DNER cKO mice relative to control (Dnerfl/fl mice) at P14 (Figures S3A–S3E). Loss of neuronal DNER did not affect FABP7 protein or transcript levels, as shown by immunoblotting (Figures S3F and S3G) and qPCR analysis (Figure S3H). Similarly, transcript levels of other core SGC markers, including Sox10, Connexin-43 (Gja1), and the inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir4.1 (Kcnj10),2 were unchanged in mutant compared to control ganglia (Figure S3H).

We next assessed SGC ensheathment of sympathetic neurons in tissue sections of the superior cervical ganglia using TH and FABP7 immunohistochemistry in 2-week-old mice. We observed a pronounced disruption in the characteristic ringlike organization of SGCs around neuronal soma, with the glial sheaths appearing to be thinner, discontinuous, or even missing around many neurons in DNER cKO ganglia, similar to our observations in TrkA;Bax dKO ganglia (Figures 3A–3F). As an additional measure to visualize SGCs, we used immunostaining using an anti-Kir4.1 antibody, where Kir4.1 is an inwardly rectifying K+ channel that is enriched in SGCs.2,26 Similar to our findings using FABP7 immunostaining, the Kir4.1-positive glial envelopes surrounding neurons appeared to be thinner and more “patchy” in DNER mutant ganglia compared to controls (Figures S3I–S3N). Levels of Kcnj10 transcript, which encodes for Kir4.1, were similar between control and mutant ganglia as noted above (Figure S3H), suggesting that aberrant Kir4.1 distribution in DNER cKO ganglia reflect disruptions in SGC morphology and ensheathment rather than altered Kir4.1 expression. The disruption in SGC organization is not due to decreased glial numbers since the number of Sox2-positive SGCs was similar between DNER cKO and control ganglia (Figure S3O). Further, EdU labeling revealed similar levels of cell proliferation between DNER cKO and control ganglia at P14 (Figures S3P–S3R), a developmental stage when SGCs are typically undergoing substantial proliferation.27 Despite similar numbers of glial cells, we observed fewer Sox2-positive cells that were associated with individual neuron cell bodies in DNER mutant ganglia (Figures 3G–3K), consistent with the notion that loss of neuronal DNER results in reduced contacts between sympathetic neurons and SGCs.

For further examination of neuron-glia contacts in sympathetic ganglia, we turned to transmission electron microscopy (TEM). At the ultrastructural level, SGCs can be distinguished from other non-neuronal ganglionic cells based on (1) the presence of a thin SGC sheath enveloping the neuronal soma, (2) relatively large SGC nuclei with clumped heterochromatin, and (3) noticeable thickening of the SGC cytoplasm around the nuclear region.3,13 Applying these criteria, we observed a compacted arrangement of neuronal cell bodies (pseudo-colored in teal) with SGCs (pseudo-colored in magenta) that were in close juxtaposition to neurons and enveloping neuronal soma in control sympathetic ganglia at P14 (Figures 3L and 3M). In contrast, SGC-like cells (fulfilling criteria 2 and 3 but not 1) were often found detached from neuronal cell bodies in the extracellular space in DNER mutant ganglia (Figures 3N and 3O). Further, neuronal soma appeared to be atrophied with more spacing between individual neurons in the mutant ganglia (Figures 3N and 3O).

Together, these results suggest that loss of neuronal DNER results in defective neuron-SGC contacts.

Loss of DNER elicits downregulation of noradrenergic enzyme expression and soma atrophy in sympathetic neurons

When performing TH immunostaining to visualize sympathetic neuron morphology, we noted a striking downregulation of TH immunoreactivity in sympathetic neuronal cell bodies from DNER cKO ganglia, relative to control (Figures 4A and 4B; also see Figures 3A and 3D). TH is the rate-limiting enzyme in the biosynthesis of NE, the classical sympathetic neurotransmitter. qPCR analyses revealed a marked downregulation in Th and Dopamine Beta-Hydroxylase (Dbh) transcript levels (46% and 35% decrease, respectively) (Figure 4C). DBH converts dopamine to NE in the NE biosynthetic pathway. Immunoblotting further confirmed reduced TH expression in mutant neuronal cell bodies, showing a 33% decrease in TH protein levels in DNER cKO ganglia compared to controls (Figures S4A and S4B). However, expression of other canonical regulators of sympathetic neuron specification and maturation, including transcription factors, Isl1 and Phox2b, as well as the TrkA receptor (Ntrk1),28 were unaffected by loss of neuronal DNER (Figure 4C). Thus, loss of DNER in sympathetic neurons has a specific effect in downregulating noradrenergic biosynthetic machinery, without affecting sympathetic neuron identity or differentiation, during development.

We also observed pronounced atrophy of neuronal cell bodies in DNER cKO sympathetic ganglia (Figures 4D and 4E). Given the downregulation in TH expression, we used TrkA instead of TH immunohistochemistry to visualize neuronal morphology. Quantification of neuronal soma sizes revealed significantly reduced soma areas in DNER cKO ganglia, with a greater distribution of smaller-sized cell bodies in mutant ganglia relative to controls (Figure 4F). Average soma areas from tissue sections were 202.8 ± 12.57 μm2 for control neurons versus 158 ± 8.9 μm2 for mutant neurons. Soma size has been postulated to correlate with increased translational and metabolic machinery in neurons,29–31 suggesting that DNER-mediated neuron-glia contacts may be required for these biochemical functions in sympathetic neurons. In particular, the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway is a known regulator of neuronal soma size.30,32,33 Thus, we assessed expression of phosphorylated ribosomal protein 6 (p-S6), a key downstream effector of the mTOR pathway.34 We observed a marked reduction in p-S6 expression in DNER cKO ganglia compared to control using immunostaining and immunoblotting (Figures S4C–S4F). Despite soma atrophy, sympathetic neuron numbers were not significantly different from control littermates when assessed at several developmental stages (P1, P7, P14, and P28) (Figures 4G–4I).

The phenotypes of downregulated noradrenergic biosynthetic machinery, impaired mTOR signaling, and reduced soma size in DNER cKO sympathetic neurons are remarkably similar to our previous observations in mice with genetic ablation of SGCs.5 Based on the similar phenotypes in DNER cKO and SGC-ablated mice (where DNER is present in sympathetic neurons), we reason that DNER acts in a cell-non-autonomous manner to influence sympathetic neuron morphology, metabolic signaling, and noradrenergic enzyme expression, likely via regulating neuron-glia interactions.

DNER deletion from sympathetic neurons results in hyper-innervation of target tissues

We next visualized sympathetic axon innervation of targets using TH immunostaining in iDISCO-cleared whole mounts of peripheral organs/tissues and/or in tissue sections from 2-week-old DNER cKO and control mice. Given the atrophy in neuronal cell bodies in mutant ganglia, we anticipated that there would be reduced sympathetic innervation of target tissues indicative of an overall reduction in neuronal growth. Surprisingly, we observed sympathetic hyper-innervation in several target organs/tissues, including the heart (Figures 5A and 5B), brown adipose tissue (Figures 5D and 5E), and salivary glands (Figures 5G and 5H) in DNER cKO mice relative to controls. Quantification of axon density showed increased sympathetic innervation in all the target tissues that were examined (Figures 5C–5F and 5I). To assess whether the increased innervation was a developmental effect, we also assessed TH-positive sympathetic nerves in the heart in older mice at 1 month of age. We observed that 1-month-old DNER cKO animals also exhibited hyper-innervation of target tissues relative to control animals (Figures S5A–S5C).

Intriguingly, despite the marked reduction in Th mRNA and protein in neuronal cell bodies in mutant ganglia, we did not observe a noticeable difference in TH levels in sympathetic axons in DNER cKO mice, based on TH immunostaining of axon innervation in target fields (Figures 5A, 5B, 5D, 5E, 5G, 5H, S5A, and S5B). To further assess TH levels in sympathetic axons, we performed immunoblotting on lysates from salivary glands, a target tissue densely innervated by TH-positive sympathetic fibers. TH protein levels were similar between control and DNER cKO samples (Figures S5D and S5E), indicating that axonal TH expression is maintained despite the loss of neuronal DNER, in contrast to the reduction observed in neuronal cell bodies. We also assessed sympathetic innervation of targets using a pan-neuronal marker, Tuj1. Similar to observations using TH immunostaining, we found a marked increase in Tuj1-positive axons innervating the salivary glands in DNER cKO mice at P14 (Figures S5F–S5H). Since the salivary glands are richly innervated by sympathetic and parasympathetic axons,35 these results suggest that loss of DNER in sympathetic neurons contributes to the enhanced innervation of peripheral organs/tissues.

We next sought to determine whether the sympathetic hyper-innervation phenotype in DNER cKO mice was a cell-autonomous effect. Thus, we isolated sympathetic neurons from DNER cKO and control mice and grew them as dissociated cultures in the presence of NGF, an essential survival and growth factor for sympathetic neurons, for 16 h. Visualization of sympathetic neuron morphology using TH and β-III-tubulin immunostaining, as well as quantification of neurite lengths and branching, revealed no deficits in these parameters in isolated neurons from DNER cKO mice (Figures 5J–5M). Thus, loss of DNER from sympathetic neurons does not result in neuronintrinsic effects on excessive neurite growth or branching, suggesting that the hyper-innervation of targets in vivo likely reflects a cell-non-autonomous effect of losing neuron-SGC contacts.

Together, these results reveal a previously uncharacterized role for SGCs in acting as a “brake” to curtail excessive axon innervation of target tissues during development.

DNER cKO mice have increased sympathetic neuron activity, elevated heart rate, and enhanced cold tolerance

Using genetic ablation in mice, we recently reported that SGCs play a key role in modulating the activity of sympathetic neurons.5 Specifically, genetic ablation of SGCs resulted in enhanced sympathetic neuron activity in mice.5 Further, SGC-ablated mice showed elevated heart rate (tachycardia) and increased pupil size,5 both of which are consistent with an increase in sympathetic tone. To address how perturbations in neuron-SGC contacts through the loss of DNER in sympathetic neurons might affect neuron activity and function, we performed immunostaining for c-Fos, an immediate-early transcription factor that serves as a reporter of neuronal activity.36 We observed a 2.3-fold increase in the number of c-Fos-positive sympathetic neurons in DNER cKO ganglia compared to control (13.8 ± 1.9 in control versus 32.0 ± 4.5 in mutant) (Figures 6A–6C). Further, we found a significant increase (1.7-fold) in circulating levels of NE in DNER cKO mice compared to control animals (Figure 6D). Sympathetic nerves have been reported to contribute significantly to plasma NE levels.37 These results suggest that, despite the downregulation of NE biosynthetic machinery in neuronal cell bodies (see Figures 4A–4C), neurotransmitter secretion is augmented in sympathetic axons, consistent with an increase in neuronal activity as indicated by enhanced c-Fos expression in DNER cKO mice.

To determine whether increased neuronal activity in DNER cKO ganglia is associated with a reactive glial state, we assessed the expression of Connexin 43 (Cx43), a gap junction protein enriched in SGCs,2,38 which is upregulated under conditions of injury or pathology.6,39,40 Cx43 immunostaining revealed a decrease in the immunofluorescence signal in DNER cKO ganglia (Figures S6A and S6B), although qPCR (Figure S3H) and immunoblotting analyses (Figures S6C and S6D) showed no change in Gja1 transcript (encoding for Cx43) or total Cx43 protein levels. These findings suggest gap junction connectivity is reduced between SGCs and/or between SGCs and neurons due to the loss of neuron-glia contacts rather than a change in overall Cx43 expression. Together with our observation that cell proliferation was unaltered in DNER cKO ganglia (Figures S3P–S3R), where increased proliferation is another marker of glial hyperactivity,39 these results suggest that SGC activation is unaffected in mutant ganglia.

To address the functional consequences of loss of neuronal DNER, we measured heart rate using electrocardiogram (ECG) recordings in adult DNER cKO and control mice (6–8 weeks of age).41 Strikingly, DNER cKO mice exhibited elevated heart rates compared to controls (Figures 6E and 6F), recapitulating the phenotype previously observed in SGC-ablated animals.5 As a second and independent assessment of autonomic function in DNER cKO mice, we also assessed non-shivering thermogenesis, which is primarily mediated by sympathetic innervation to the brown adipose tissue.42 DNER cKO and control mice (6–8 weeks old) were housed at a thermoneutral temperature (26°C) for 3 weeks and then exposed to 4°C for 1 h every day for a total of 10 days. This cold acclimatization paradigm is sufficient to elicit an adaptive increase in non-shivering thermogenesis in mice.43 Mice were then subjected to an acute cold exposure at 4°C for 4 h, where body temperatures were measured every 1 h. Both DNER cKO and control animals showed an initial drop in their internal temperatures within the first 2 h at 4°C. However, the mutant animals were better able to maintain their body temperature with prolonged cold exposure compared to the controls (Figure 6G), indicative of increased BAT-mediated thermogenesis. Consistent with the notion that DNER cKO animals have enhanced BAT-mediated thermogenesis, we also observed that mutant animals had lower body weights despite increased food intake relative to control mice (Figures S6E and S6F).

Together, these results indicate that DNER deletion in sympathetic neurons results in elevated sympathetic neuron activity, increased NE release, and autonomic behavioral defects that are consistent with elevated sympathetic tone in mice. Given the striking similarities in these phenotypes between DNER cKO and SGC-ablated mice, we reason that loss of neuron-glia interactions underlie the phenotypes in DNER cKO mice.

DISCUSSION

Sympathetic neurons have long served as an excellent model to understand how peripheral organs/tissues instruct key events in neuronal development, including survival, axon/dendrite morphogenesis, and synapse formation, through the release of target-derived neurotrophins (NGF). Here, we show that retrograde neurotrophin signaling controls not only neuron development but also the establishment of contacts between sympathetic neurons and neighboring SGCs in ganglia. We identify DNER as a transmembrane protein expressed in neurons, specifically in somatodendritic compartments, that is required for interactions of sympathetic neurons with SGCs. Deletion of DNER from sympathetic neurons during development disrupted neuron-SGC contacts and elicited pronounced effects on neuronal morphology, neurotransmitter homeostasis, activity, and autonomic functions in mice, with the majority of phenotypes bearing striking resemblance to those previously observed with SGC ablation in mice. These results suggest that DNER acts in a cell-non-autonomous manner, likely via regulating neuronSGC interactions, to influence sympathetic neuron structure, activity, and function (Figure 7). Together, our results define a mechanism whereby innervated peripheral target organs/tissues control sympathetic neuron-SGC interactions in ganglia to coordinate the assembly of functional circuits.

A hallmark feature of SGCs is the manner in which they wrap around neuronal cell bodies in the PNS, and in sympathetic ganglia, also enveloping the dendrites and synapses.3,11–13 The tight neuron-glia units allow for rapid and dynamic bidirectional responses that modulate efferent sympathetic outflow to peripheral organs. Our study defines DNER, expressed on neuronal surfaces, under the control of retrograde NGF signaling, as a key regulator of the formation of sympathetic neuron-SGC units during development. Loss of DNER in sympathetic neurons resulted in fewer SGCs that were attached to neurons, although total glial numbers were unaffected. Whether loss of neuronal DNER affects neuron-SGC recognition or adhesion remain to be determined.

A key question also arises about the DNER-binding partner in SGCs. In the developing cerebellum, DNER localized on Purkinje neuron surfaces binds Notch1 receptors in Bergmann glia to induce glia morphogenesis.23 DNER has also been shown to bind Receptor Type Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase ζ (Ptprz1),44 a chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan, which is involved in cell-cell adhesion and migration.45–47 Both Notch1 and Ptprz1 have known roles in regulating neuron-glia interactions.45,47–49 Notch1 and Ptprz1 are specifically enriched in SGCs based on our recent single-cell RNA-sequencing data,2 making them promising candidates for future studies. Further, in a recent study, physical contacts between co-cultured SGCs and human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived sensory neurons were found to promote acquisition of pseudo-unipolar neuron morphology and expression of terminal differentiation markers in sensory neurons.50 The contact-dependent effects were mediated by PlexinA4 and Sema3a/6a expression in neurons and SGCs, respectively, as well as glial gap junction signaling mediated by Connexin 43.50 Thus, DNER, likely, works in concert with additional surface proteins to promote and/or maintain neuron-glia contacts. In particular, retrograde NGF-TrkA signaling is known to regulate the expression of several neuronal surface proteins,25 several of which could also contribute to neuron-SGC recognition, adhesion, and glial morphology.

Previously, we reported that depletion of SGCs in 1-month-old mice amplified neuronal activity leading to increased circulating NE levels and elevated sympathetic tone, revealed by enhanced heart rate and pupil size in mice.5 In the current study, loss of neuronal DNER, starting at embryonic stages, which resulted in aberrant neuron-glia contacts, elicited strikingly similar neuronal phenotypes of increased number of c-Fos-positive sympathetic neurons, impaired mTOR signaling, enhanced plasma levels of NE, as well as elevated heart rate. Further, loss of neuronal DNER, which is confined to somatodendritic compartments, resulted in soma-specific effects in sympathetic neurons (i.e., TH downregulation and soma size decrease). Downregulated NE biosynthesis and mTOR signaling in neuronal cell bodies likely reflect compensatory responses to heightened sympathetic activity caused by the loss of neuron-glia contacts and/or reduced availability of glia-derived factors that maintain noradrenergic enzyme expression and neuronal metabolism. It is also notable that axonal TH levels were not perturbed in DNER cKO neurons, similar to our previous observations in SGC-ablated animals.5 Th mRNA is trafficked to sympathetic axons, where it is locally translated.51 Thus, enhanced Th mRNA trafficking and local synthesis might be a mechanism to maintain axonal TH expression and to locally regulate NE release in DNER cKO sympathetic neurons. Together, our results suggest that key phenotypes in sympathetic neuron morphology, metabolic signaling, neurotransmitter homeostasis, activity, and autonomic functions in DNER cKO mice can be primarily attributed to loss of neuron-glia interactions rather than intrinsic effects of DNER signaling in neurons. Our findings also underscore that a key role for SGCs is in restraining neuronal activity in sympathetic neurons, as revealed by two independent manipulations in mice: specifically, the developmental loss of neuron-SGC contacts in DNER cKO mice and ablation of SGCs in older animals. SGCs have been proposed to influence neuronal excitability by regulating ion (K+), neurotransmitter (acetylcholine, GABA), and/or neuromodulator (ATP) homeostasis in the neuronal micro-environment.3,5,7,26,52 Thus, perturbed neuron-glia contacts in DNER cKO mice could result in dysregulation of the homeostatic micro-environment around sympathetic neurons.

Despite soma atrophy in DNER cKO mice, we observed an unexpected increase in sympathetic axon innervation of several target organs, including the heart and brown fat in 2-week-old mutant mice. Since this phenotype was not seen with SGC ablation in 1-month-old mice,5 these results could reflect a developmental role for sympathetic neuron-glia interactions in curtailing excessive axon innervation of target tissues or a cell-autonomous effect of DNER in neurons. We consider the latter scenario unlikely since DNER mutant neurons grown in isolated cultures without SGCs had similar neurite growth and branching as control neurons. How SGCs surrounding neuronal cell bodies influence distal events in axonal processes is an intriguing question. Sympathetic axons are known to undergo activity-dependent pruning of excessive axon collaterals in end organs, independent of neuron loss, to refine connectivity with targets.53 If SGCs have a developmental role in limiting neuronal activity, then aberrantly elevated activity may underlie defects in axon innervation in DNER cKO mice. It seems paradoxical that one of the consequences of retrograde NGF signaling, best known to promote robust developmental axon growth, should be to suppress excessive innervation of target tissues through regulating DNER-mediated neuron-SGC interactions. However, NGF is a versatile signaling factor that is known to induce both pro-growth and regressive axonal events to sculpt the final patterns of axon innervation in target tissues.54 For example, retrograde NGF signaling promotes the synthesis of Brain Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) in sympathetic neurons.25 BDNF is essential for competitive activity-dependent elimination of sympathetic axon collaterals during pruning.53 We propose that retrograde regulation of neuron-SGC interactions is another such mechanism by which target-derived NGF controls the refinement of axon innervation patterns in final target fields.

The importance of innervated peripheral targets in instructing neuron development has been appreciated since the pioneering work of Rita Levi-Montalcini, Viktor Hamburger, and colleagues.17 For sympathetic neurons, the target-derived neurotrophin, NGF, is a master regulator that is essential for many aspects of sympathetic neuron development. Recent studies highlight that sympathetic neurons are remarkably heterogeneous and that neuron specialization, needed to meet the functional demands of peripheral organs, is established during the first 3 weeks of postnatal life in a manner dependent on target innervation.55 We recently identified four SGC sub-types in the mouse sympathetic ganglia,2 suggesting that SGCs are not interchangeable but that a division of labor exists to accommodate the specialized needs of their neuronal neighbors. Here, we show that target-derived NGF signaling from the periphery instructs not only sympathetic neuron development but also the assembly of functional neuron-SGC units. Our findings in this study provide a platform for further analyses to address an exciting possibility that end-organs exert ultimate control of neuron-glia interactions in the ganglia to coordinate the assembly of pathway-specific sympathetic circuits that underlie distinct organ functions.

Limitations of the study

The timing of SGC morphogenesis and association with post-ganglionic sympathetic neurons also coincides with cholinergic input to these neurons from pre-ganglionic neurons.56,57 Thus, neuron-SGC interactions may be shaped by both target-dependent NGF:TrkA signaling and activity-dependent mechanisms. Further studies are warranted to characterize the role of neuronal activity in SGC development and neuronal contacts by blocking ganglionic cholinergic neurotransmission using mice lacking functional n-acetylcholine receptors (α3 nAChR knockout mice)58 or the administration of pharmacologic inhibitors. Further, while we used Th-Cre mice in this study, the use of viral transduction approaches or tamoxifen-inducible CreERT2 mice for temporal deletion of sympathetic-derived DNER will be important to resolve developmental versus adult functions.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Animals

All animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Johns Hopkins University Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUC) and NIH guidelines. Animals were group housed in a standard 12:12 light-dark cycle with excess water and food ad libitum. All efforts were made to minimize the pain and number of animals used. The ages of mice are indicated in the figure legends and/or methods. Mice were maintained on a C57BL/6 background or mixed C57BL/6J and 129P, and both male and female mice were used for analyses. The following lines were used in this study: Th-Cre60 and TrkAF592A (TrkAfl/fl)61 mice were gifts from Dr. C. Gerfen (NIH), and Dr. D. Ginty (Harvard Medical School) respectively. Bax ± mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (B6.129X1-Baxtm1Sjk/J, JAX #002994). Th-Cre, TrkAfl/fl, and Bax−/− mice were crossed to generate Th-Cre;TrkAfl/fl;Bax−/− (TrkA;Bax dKO) mice. Th-Cre;Bax−/− or TrkAfl/fl;Bax−/− mice were used as controls in experiments. Dnertm3a KO first allele mice were purchased from EMMA Repository (C57BL/6NTac-Dnertm3a(EUCOMM)Hmgu/Ieg, EM #08389). Dnertm3a mice were crossed to a ROSA26-FLPe mouse (129S4/SvJaeSor-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm1(FLP1)Dym/J, JAX #003946) to knock out the LacZ and neo cassette to generate the conditional Dnertm3c allele (Dnerfl/fl) mice, which were then mated to Th-Cre mice in order to generate Th-Cre;DNERfl/fl (DNER cKO) mice. Littermate DNERfl/fl mice were used as controls in all experiments. Sprague-Dawley rats were purchased from Charles River (Strain code: 400).

Cultures of sympathetic neurons and non-neuronal ganglionic cells

Superior cervical ganglia (SCGs) were harvested from Sprague-Dawley rat pups (P0.5) or mice of indicated genotypes and ages. Neurons were enzymatically dissociated and cultured as described previously.63 Briefly, neurons were cultured in high-glucose DMEM media supplemented with 10% FBS, 1U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, 100 ng/mL NGF,59 and 10 μM AraC to prevent proliferative cell growth. To assess DNER expression in response to NGF stimulation, sympathetic neurons were cultured in the above culture media containing NGF (100 ng/mL) for 5 days. Subsequently, neurons were deprived of NGF by culturing neurons in high glucose DMEM containing 1% FBS, and BAF (50 μM), a broad-spectrum caspase inhibitor, for 24 h. Neurons were then stimulated with NGF (100 ng/mL) for 8 h, neuronal lysates prepared and subjected to immunoblotting as described below. For cultures of non-neuronal ganglionic cells, superior cervical ganglia harvested from 5 to 6 rats were plated on collagen-coated 60 mm dishes and cultured for 4–5 days in vitro in the above culture media conditions but devoid of NGF or AraC to eliminate sympathetic neurons but retain proliferating non-neuronal ganglionic cell types, including SGCs. FABP7 immunostaining was used on occasion to determine that non-neuronal ganglionic cells in cultures largely consisted of SGCs.

METHOD DETAILS

In vitro neurite extension

Neurons were cultured for 16 h prior to fixation with 4% PFA/PBS for 20 min, washed with PBS, and blocked with 1% BSA/PBS. Primary antibodies rabbit anti-TH (1:400), and mouse anti-β-III-tubulin (1:500) were diluted in a blocking solution and incubated at room temperature for 2–3 h. Following washes with PBS, cells were additionally incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 and −546 conjugated anti-rabbit and anti-mouse secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, washed, and mounted with Fluoromount containing DAPI. Images were acquired with a Zeiss LSM 700 confocal microscope. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ. Number of branches and neurite lengths were quantified where the length of the longest neurite was determined. At least 15 neurons each from four individual experiments were analyzed.

Isolation of RNA and quantitative RT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from dissected superior cervical ganglia using the TRIzol-chloroform extraction method. cDNA was prepared using Superscript IV First-Strand Synthesis System. Real-time qPCR analysis was performed using a Maxima SYBR green/Rox Q-PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and gene-specific primers, in a 7300 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystem). All samples were analyzed in triplicate reactions. Fold change was calculated using the 2 (ΔΔCt) normalized to 18S transcript. Primer sequences are listed in the Key Resource table.

In situ hybridization

in situ hybridization was performed with 904-bp digoxigenin-labeled probes spanning exons 6–12 of mouse DNER. Superior cervical ganglia from E18.5 embryos were isolated and fresh-frozen in 30% sucrose for 3 days at 4°C, embedded in OCT, and serially sectioned at 12 μm. Sections were post-fixed in 4% PFA for 20 min followed by washes with PBS, and permeabilization with 0.1% TX-100 for 10 min. Sections were washed with PBS and acetylated with 0.25% acetic anhydride in 0.1M triethanolamine with 0.9% NaCl for 10 min. Following acetylation, sections were equilibrated in hybridization solution (50% formamide, 5X Denhardt’s, 250μg/mL MRE 600 tRNA, 5X sodium chloride/sodium citrate (SSC) for 2 h at room temperature, then hybridized overnight at 68°C in a hybridization oven with 2μg/mL of probe diluted in hybridization solution. Following hybridization, sections were washed with 0.2X SSC 3 times for 20 min at 60°C, blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 10% goat serum in buffer (0.1M Tris pH 7.5, 0.15M NaCl), and treated with anti-DIG (1:5000) antibody overnight at 4°C. The alkaline phosphatase reaction was carried out with NBT/BCIP tablets for 4–6 h, rinsed in PBS, fixed in 4% PFA, and mounted in AquaMount (EMD Chemicals). Solutions were treated with diethylpyrocarbonate (DEPC) and autoclaved prior to use to inactivate RNases.

Immunoblotting

Superior cervical ganglia from postnatal day 14 mice were homogenized in Laemmli buffer (50mM Tris pH 6.8, 0.5% SDS, 10% glycerol, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.0125% bromophenol blue). Protein lysates were subjected to immunoblotting using primary antibodies, including rabbit anti-FABP7 (1:1000), goat anti-DNER (1:200), rabbit anti-TH (1:4000 for cell bodies; 1:1000 for axons) rabbit anti-p-S6 (1:1000), rabbit anti-Cnx43 (1:500), rabbit anti-α-tubulin (1:1000), mouse anti-β-III-tubulin (1:3000 for cell bodies; 1:1000 for axons), and anti-p85 (1:1000). All primary antibodies were diluted in Hikari signal enhancer buffer (Nacalai) and incubated overnight at 4°C, followed by three washes with TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20) and incubations with anti-rabbit, anti-goat or anti-mouse HorseRadish Peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1–2h. Blots were then washed with TBST for 3 times. All immunoblots were visualized using Pierce ECL Plus substrate (Thermo Scientific) and scanned using Typhoon 5 Variable Mode Imager (GE Healthcare).

Neuronal counts

Neuronal counts were performed as previously described.5 In brief, torsos from mice at postnatal days 1, 7, 14, and 28 ages were fixed in 4% PFA/PBS for 4 h to overnight and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose/PBS for 2–3 days. Torsos were mounted in OCT and serially sectioned with 12 μm thickness using a cryostat. Every fifth section was stained with a solution containing 0.5% cresyl violet (Nissl). Cells in sympathetic ganglia with characteristic neuronal morphology and visible nucleoli were counted using ImageJ.

EdU labeling

Mice (P12) were injected intraperitoneally with 50μL EdU (100μg/mL in 3:1 PBS/DMSO) for two consecutive days before harvesting at P14. Mouse torsos were collected and cryo-sectioned (14μm thickness). Tissue sections were processed for 30 min at room temperature in EdU reaction cocktail (Thermo Scientific EdU kit C10337; Click-iT buffer, Buffer additive, CuSO4 solution, and Alexa Fluor 488). Sections were then washed in PBS +0.1% Triton X-100 and mounted with Fluoromount containing DAPI. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal scanning microscope equipped with a spectral detector. Images were captured in z-stacks at intervals of 1 μm using a 20X objective (0.40 NA) with 0.5X digital zoom. EdU+ cells were counted using the ‘cell counter’ plug-in (ImageJ). 5–11 sections per animal were analyzed.

Immunohistochemical analyses

For immunostaining, torsos from P14 mice were fixed in 4% PFA/PBS overnight and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose/PBS for 3–4 days. Torsos were embedded in OCT (Sakura Finetek) and stored at −80°C. Superior cervical ganglia were cryo-sectioned at 12 μm. For paraffin embedding, superior cervical ganglia were fixed in Bouin’s solution for 1 h at room temperature, washed 2 times with 70% ethanol, and left in 70% ethanol at room temperature overnight, followed by dehydration with 95% ethanol/5% methanol, 100% ethanol and xylene. Tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 6 μm using a microtome, deparaffinized using xylene, and rehydrated using 100%, 95%, 85%, 75%, and 50% ethanol. Paraffin sections were incubated in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6) at 95°C for 10 min, followed by incubation in 0.2 M glycine for 10 min. Cryosections were permeabilized in 1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Blocking was done in 10% goat or donkey serum/3% bovine serum albumin in 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1hr at room temperature. Primary antibodies used were rabbit/sheep/mouse anti-TH (1:300), rabbit anti-FABP7 (1:200), goat anti-DNER (1:50), rabbit anti-TrkA (1:500), rabbit anti-Kir 4.1 (1:200), rabbit anti-p-S6 (1:200), rabbit anti-Cnx43 (1:200) with incubations overnight at 4°C. Slides were washed with 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488, −546, or −647 conjugated anti-rabbit, anti-sheep, anti-mouse or anti-goat secondary antibodies (1:500) in blocking solution for 1hr at room temperature. Tissues were then washed and mounted in an Aqueous Mounting Medium containing 100 μg/mL DAPI and imaged at 0.5–1 μm optical slices using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal scanning microscope. Maximum-intensity images were generated using ImageJ.

For Sox2 immunostaining, torsos from P14 mice were fixed in 4% PFA/PBS overnight and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose/PBS for 3–4 days. Torsos were embedded in OCT (Sakura Finetek) and stored at −80°C. Superior cervical ganglia were cryo-sectioned at 30 μm. Sections were washed with PBS and incubated in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6) at 95°C for 10 min, followed by incubation in 0.2 M glycine for 10 min. Blocking was done in 10% goat serum/3% bovine serum albumin in 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 2hr at room temperature. Sections were incubated with rabbit anti-Sox2 antibody (1:200) and mouse anti-TH (1:400). Sections were washed 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS 3 times for 10 min and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488, −546 conjugated anti-rabbit, and anti-mouse secondary antibodies (1:500) overnight at 4°C. After washing, sections were mounted in Aqueous Mounting Medium containing 100 μg/mL DAPI. High-magnification images were acquired using a 63X oil objective on an LSM 800 confocal scanning microscope. Under this resolution, Sox2-positive SGC nuclei that appeared to be in contact with neuronal cell bodies were classified as ‘juxtaposed,’ whereas SGC nuclei not touching neuronal cell bodies and located in the extracellular space were classified as ‘non-juxtaposed’. For this analysis, 8–11 sections were examined per animal, and approximately 300 neurons and adjacent glial cells, including ‘juxtaposed’ and ‘non-juxtaposed’ glia, were counted manually per genotype.

cFos immunostaining was performed as described previously.62 Briefly, superior cervical ganglia were dissected from P14 mice. Superior cervical ganglia were freshly cryoprotected in 30% sucrose/PBS for 1hr at 4°C, embedded in OCT, and stored at −80°C until sectioning. 16 μm thick sections were prepared using a cryostat, fixed with 2% PFA/PBS for 5 min, and permeabilized with freshly prepared 1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Blocking was done in 5% goat serum/1% bovine serum albumin in 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS for 1.5 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with a monoclonal anti-rabbit cFos (1:1000) antibody diluted in blocking solution overnight at 4°C. After several washes with 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS, sections were incubated in Alexa Fluor 546 conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody overnight at 4°C. After washing sections were mounted Aqueous Mounting Medium containing 100 μg/mL DAPI and images were acquired using LSM 800 confocal scanning microscope.

iDISCO and wholemount immunostaining

iDISCO-based tissue clearing for whole mount immunostaining of target organs (heart, brown adipose tissue, and salivary glands) from P14 or 1-month mice was performed as previously described.64 Briefly, target organs were fixed in 4%PFA/PBS, dehydrated by methanol/water series (20–80%), and incubated overnight in 66% dichloromethane (DCM)/33% methanol. Tissues were bleached with 5% H2O2 in methanol at 4°C overnight, re-hydrated with methanol/PBS/0.2% Triton X-100 series (80–20%), and permeabilized first with 0.2%Triton X-100/PBS for 2 h followed by permeabilizations for 3 days with 0.2% Triton X-100/20%DMSO/0.3M glycine in PBS. Tissues were incubated in blocking solution (0.2% Triton X-100/10% DMSO/6% Normal Goat Serum in PBS) for 2 days and then incubated with rabbit anti-TH (1:300) in 0.2% Tween 20/0.001% heparin/5% DMSO/3% Normal Goat Serum in PBS at 37°C for 4 days. Samples were then washed with 0.2% Tween 20/0.001% heparin in PBS and incubated with anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 546 secondary antibody (1:400) in 0.2% Tween 20/0.001% heparin/3% Normal Goat Serum in PBS for 4 overnight. Tissues were extensively washed with 0.2% Tween 20/0.001% heparin in PBS and dehydrated in methanol. Tissues were cleared by successive washes in 66% DCM/33% methanol, 100% DCM and 100% Dibenzyl Ether. Tissues were imaged using LSM 800 microscope. For quantification of target innervation, images of whole-mounts were acquired in 2D using an LSM 800 confocal microscope with optical sectioning. Integrated TH fluorescence density per unit area was calculated from multiple randomly selected images using ImageJ. A similar number of images (7–14 sections per animal) were analyzed for control and mutant animals, with images selected from the same target region in both groups.

For wholemount immunostaining of ganglia and iris muscle; superior cervical ganglia and eyes were dissected from P14 mice and fixed with 4%PFA/PBS at 4°C for 4 h and 1 h, respectively. Superior cervical ganglia were permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X-100/20%DMSO/0.3M glycine/PBS for overnight at room temperature and blocked with 0.2% Triton X-100/10% DMSO/6% Donkey Serum at room temperature for 6–8 h. SCGs were then incubated with primary antibody anti-goat DNER (1:50) and anti-chicken MAP2 (1:500) in 0.2% Tween 20/0.001% heparin/5% DMSO/3% Donkey Serum at room temperature for 2 days. SCGs were washed with 0.2% Tween 20/0.001% heparin/PBS extensively and incubated with anti-goat Alexa Fluor 546 and anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody (1:400) in 0.2% Tween 20/0.001% heparin/3% Donkey Serum/PBS at room temperature for 2 days. The iris muscle was washed and incubated with 10% Donkey serum/3% BSA/0.3% Triton X-100 overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with primary antibodies anti-Rabbit TH (1:300), and anti-goat DNER (1:50) in blocking solution overnight at 4°C. Tissues were washed three times for 15 min each with PBS, and incubated with donkey-anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 and donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 546 secondary antibodies in a blocking solution overnight at 4°C. Tissues were mounted using Aqueous Mounting Medium, and images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal scanning microscope equipped with a spectral detector. Images of ganglia were captured in z-stacks at intervals of 1 μm using a 63X oil objective (1.40 NA) with 0.5–1.5X digital zoom. Images of iris were captured in z-stacks at intervals of 1 μm using a 40X oil objective (1.30 NA) with 1X digital zoom. Images were processed using ImageJ, and a single plane of the z-stacks (for ganglia) was used as a representative image.

Transmission electron microscopy

Superior cervical ganglia from P14 mice were dissected and fixed in 1% formaldehyde, 2.5% glutaraldehyde, 3% sucrose, 1mM MgSO4 in 0.1M phosphate or cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4). SCGs were then postfixed in 1% buffered osmium tetroxide with 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide. SCGs were embedded in Eponate resin, sectioned at a thickness of 70 nm, and collected on single-slot grids with a carbon-coated Formvar film. Sections were poststained with uranyl acetate followed by lead citrate. Large-scale transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on a JEOL 1400 TEM at 5,000x yielding a 2nm/pixel lateral resolution image. Images were then mosaiced using the nornir-buildscripts (https://nornir.github.io/), creating large-scale TEM composite images as described previously.65 To distinguish SGCs from other non-neuronal ganglionic cells, we applied the following criteria: (1) the presence of a thin SGC sheath enveloping the neuronal soma, (2) relatively large SGC nuclei with clumped heterochromatin, and (3) noticeable thickening of the SGC cytoplasm around the nuclear region.3,13 For SGCs not in contact with neuronal cell bodies in mutant ganglia, we used criteria (2) and (3) to identify them as SGCs.

Food intake

Mice (6–8 weeks) were weighed and then individually housed for 7 days. 100 gm of food ad libitum was given to each mouse on the first day. The uneaten food was measured each day for a total of 7 days. Total food intake was calculated and normalized to the body weight per mouse.

Norepinephrine (NE) ELISA

Blood samples (100 μL) were drawn retro-orbitally from anesthetized P14 mice. Plasma was prepared by centrifuging blood at 3000 rpm for 15 min, 4°C, and 10 μL plasma was used for NE measurements using ELISA (NE High Sensitive ELISA Kit, Rocky Mountain Diagnostics) according to manufacturer’s protocol.

Electrocardiograms

ECG recordings were performed on adult mice as previously described.5 Briefly, mice (6–8 weeks) were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane, intubated, and placed on ventilator support (settings 1.2 mL/g/min at 80 breaths/min). The animal’s abdomen was shaved, scrubbed with betadine and alcohol, and draped with a sterile barrier with the surgery site exposed. A small 0.5 cm midline incision was performed, and a telemetry unit (DSI Harvard Bioscience Inc.) was implanted intraperitoneally. ECG leads were implanted subcutaneously and sutured over the right upper and left lower chest. Body temperature was maintained at 37°C. Immediately following implantation, the wound was closed with a 3–0 silk suture. Anesthesia was turned off and the animal was monitored for spontaneous breathing and was given a subcutaneous buprenorphine injection (0.01–0.05 mg/kg buprenorphine, IM) to alleviate pain. ECGs were subsequently recorded continuously in conscious animals for approximately 7 days using the Ponemah Software (DSI Harvard Bioscience Inc.). Mice were kept at a stable temperature with a regular 12 h light/dark cycle. To exclude the effects of pain and anesthesia, continuous ECG recordings between days 5–7 post-lead implantations were only included in the analysis of mean heart rates.

Cold tolerance

Mice (6–8 weeks) were housed at a thermoneutral temperature (26°C) for at least 3 weeks. Mice were then cold-acclimatized by exposing them to 4°C for one hour each day for a total of 10 days. On the day of the experiment, mice were transferred to a 4°C cold cabinet for 4 h, where they were individually housed. Rectal temperatures were recorded each hour using a rectal probe.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSES

For practical reasons, analysis of soma-size measurements, and neuron numbers analysis were performed in a semi-blinded manner. The experimenter was aware of the genotypes prior to the experiment but performed each immunostaining and analysis without knowing the genotypes of individual samples. All physiological analyses were performed in a blinded manner with the experimenter only keeping track of the ear tag numbers. All graphs and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Statistical significance was determined using an unpaired, two-tailed student t test, one sample t test, or a two-way ANOVA test for more than one variable. All error bars are represented as the standard error of the mean (SEM).
