The alternative splicing events and the function of TLN1 in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Xiaolei Xin, Zhengying Zhang, Xin Lan, Junting Li, Peiming Bai, Lin Fang, Wei Yan, Guangcheng Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most common and aggressive subtype of kidney cancer, exhibits strong metastatic potential. Alternative splicing (AS) represents a critical regulatory layer of gene expression, yet its dysregulation in ccRCC remains insufficiently characterized. ccRCC datasets from GEO were analyzed using DESeq2 for differential gene expression and AS profiling. Gene Ontology enrichment was performed for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially AS event genes (DASEGs). Findings were compared with TCGA-KIRC to identify shared and Chinese-specific DASEs. An expression regulatory network linking differentially expressed RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to DASEGs was constructed to screen key AS-regulated genes. Functional roles of the candidate gene TLN1 were validated by molecular and cellular experiments. Analysis of GSE126964 identified 2,295 DEGs and 1,331 DASEs, both enriched in platelet activation and degranulation pathways. Intersecting DEGs and DASEGs yielded 153 key genes. Comparison with TCGA-KIRC revealed 281 Chinese-specific DASEGs, including ALDOA, TLN1, LAMP2, and CD63. The RBP–DASEG network highlighted ALDOA, TLN1, and LAMP2 as potential core regulators associated with platelet pathways. TLN1 was markedly overexpressed in ccRCC tissues and cell lines, and its knockdown significantly reduced proliferation, migration, and colony formation in vitro, supporting a pro-tumorigenic function. This study uncovers platelet-related AS dysregulation in ccRCC and identifies TLN1 as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target, offering new insights into ccRCC pathogenesis and treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05366-6.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is among the most common urological malignancies worldwide [1]. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most aggressive histological subtype, accounts for roughly 75% to 85% of all RCC cases [2]. Despite advances in surgical techniques and systemic treatments, a substantial number of patients with ccRCC eventually experience tumor metastasis, which remains the primary cause of cancer-related death in this population [3].

Platelets play a critical role in facilitating the complex cascade of tumor metastasis. They help circulating tumor cells adhere to the vascular endothelium, shield them from immune detection, and drive angiogenesis and tumor proliferation through the release of bioactive mediators like VEGFA [4]. Clinical evidence shows that elevated platelet counts correlate closely with a heavier metastatic burden. At the same time, AS has emerged as a key post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism in tumor biology. Aberrant splicing events often produce oncogenic isoforms that significantly disrupt the cell cycle, metabolism, and immune evasion of tumor cells. These alterations are deeply linked to the metastatic potential of ccRCC and patient prognosis [5]. The crosstalk between platelet-associated pathways and AS abnormalities during ccRCC progression is still not fully understood.

Importantly, TLN1 is the core gene investigated in this study. TLN1 not only has significant functions in platelet biology but also occupies a broader role in gene networks related to cell death and stress responses. A recent study revealed that cytoskeleton-associated genes heavily influence stress-dependent tumor susceptibility, identifying TLN1 as a crucial member of this disulfide bond cleavage-associated gene set [6]. Building on this context, our study integrates bioinformatics analysis with experimental validation to systematically explore specific AS events and their molecular characteristics in a Chinese ccRCC population. By focusing on the TLN1-driven regulatory axis, we hope to offer new insights into ccRCC pathogenesis and highlight potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

Acquisition and preprocessing of public dataset

The GSE126964 sequencing dataset was obtained from the public Sequence Read Archive (SRA). Raw SRA files were converted into FASTQ format using the fastq-dump function implemented in the NCBI SRA Toolkit. Quality control of the raw reads was then performed with the FASTX-Toolkit (v.0.0.13; http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/), during which low-quality bases and reads were removed. The integrity and overall quality of the resulting clean reads were subsequently evaluated using FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc).

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) profiles and matched clinicopathological data for ccRCC were retrieved from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Ensembl IDs were mapped to official gene symbols utilizing standard annotation files, after which the expression data were normalized and log2(x + 1)-transformed.

Read alignment and identification of differentially expressed genes

High-quality reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using TopHat2 [7], allowing a maximum of four mismatches per read. Only reads that mapped uniquely to the genome were retained for downstream analyses. Gene expression levels were quantified based on read counts and normalized as Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads (RPKM). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package (1.38.3) [8]. Genes were defined as differentially expressed when they satisfied the following criteria: a fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5, a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05, and an expression level of FPKM ≥ 1 in at least 80% of the analyzed samples.

Alternative splicing analysis

Alternative splicing events were identified and quantified using the ABLas pipeline, following previously established methodologies [9, 10]. This approach detects splicing variations based on splice junction reads and categorizes events into ten distinct types, including exon skipping (ES), alternative 5’ splice sites (A5SS), alternative 3’ splice sites (A3SS), intron retention (IR), mutually exclusive exons (MXE), mutually exclusive 5’ and 3’ untranslated regions (5pMXE and 3pMXE), cassette exons, as well as composite splicing patterns (A3SS&ES and A5SS&ES).

For pairwise sample comparisons, Fisher’s exact test was applied using model junction reads and alternative junction reads to assess statistical significance. The DASE ratio was calculated to represent changes in the proportion of alternatively spliced reads relative to constitutively spliced reads between conditions. Events with a DASE ratio ≥ 0.2 and a P value ≤ 0.05 were considered significant. In analyses involving biological replicates, Student’s t-test was used to evaluate differences in splicing ratios. Under these conditions, events were classified as DASEs if they met all of the following thresholds: P value ≤ 0.01, splicing ratio difference ≥ 0.15, and junction read support of at least 10 in no less than 80% of the samples.

Functional enrichment analysis

To sort out functional categories of DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways were identified using KOBAS 2.0 server [11]. Hypergeometric test and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR controlling procedure were used to define the enrichment of each term. Reactome (http://reactome.org) pathway profiling was also used for functional enrichment analysis of the sets of selected genes.

Correlations analysis

To explore the potential regulatory associations between RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and AS outcomes, we examined the relationship between RBP expression levels and splicing inclusion levels, measured as Percent Spliced In (PSI). Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) was calculated for each RBP–splicing event pair. According to the magnitude and direction of the PCC values, the associations were classified as positively correlated, negatively correlated, or showing no apparent correlation.

To investigate the associations between TLN1, DASEGs, and selenoprotein-related genes, we retrieved the expression profiles of the respective genes from the TCGA-KIRC cohort. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was utilized to compute the expression correlation coefficients (R values) among the genes. Statistical significance was established at P value < 0.05. Visualization of the results was conducted using the R package ggplot2.

Assessment of the mRNA stemness index (mRNAsi)

Utilizing the algorithm described in [12], we computed the mRNAsi for all individual samples within the TCGA-KIRC cohort. The mRNAsi serves as a quantitative metric to evaluate the extent of cancer cell dedifferentiation.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

To identify gene modules highly associated with ccRCC cancer stemness (mRNAsi), a co-expression network was constructed using the WGCNA R package. The expression data initially underwent quality control to exclude outlier samples. Subsequently, a similarity matrix was generated by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients across all gene pairs. By applying an appropriate soft-thresholding power, this similarity matrix was converted into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to satisfy the criteria for a scale-free network topology. Based on the TOM dissimilarity measure (1-TOM), average linkage hierarchical clustering was employed to assign genes with similar expression patterns into distinct modules. Finally, we evaluated the phenotype-module associations by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficients between the module eigengenes (MEs) and the mRNAsi data. The module exhibiting the highest statistically significant correlation with mRNAsi was selected as the key module for subsequent downstream analyses.

Statistical analysis and visualization

All bioinformatics analyses in this study were performed using R software(4.3.3). PCA was performed to assess overall sample distribution and clustering based on the first two principal components, using the factoextra R package (https://cloud.r-project.org/package=factoextra). Gene expression levels were normalized as Transcripts Per Million (TPM) prior to downstream analyses. Visualization of next-generation sequencing data together with genomic annotations was carried out using the proprietary software sogen. Hierarchical clustering analysis was conducted based on Euclidean distance and implemented with the pheatmap R package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html). Statistical differences between two groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test. In addition, the relationship between normalized RBP expression patterns and specific sample phenotypes was assessed using the globaltest framework [13, 14].

Assessment of gene expression

GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) was used as a control gene for assessing the relative expression of specific genes. cDNA synthesis was done by standard procedures and RT-qPCR was performed on the QuantStudio5 with HieffÔ qPCR SYBR® Green Master Mix (Low Rox Plus; YEASEN, China, cat. No. 11202ES03). The information of primers is presented in Table S1. The concentration of each transcript was then normalized to GAPDH mRNA level using 2^(-ΔΔCt) method [15].

To quantitatively analyze the two different splicing isoforms of a specific ASE using a qPCR approach, the primers for detecting pre-mRNA splicing are listed in Table S1. Primers complementary to the splice junction of the constitutive exon and alternative exon was designed to specifically amplify each of these two isoforms. cDNA synthesis was done by standard procedures and RT-qPCR was performed on the QuantStudio5 with HieffÔ qPCR SYBR® Green Master Mix (Low Rox Plus, YEASEN, China, cat. No. 11202ES03). PCR amplifications were quantified using 2^(-ΔΔCt)method.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human kidney-2 cell line (HK-2) and the renal clear cell carcinoma cell lines A498, Caki-1, and 786-O were used in this study. All cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Caki-1 and 786-O cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (1640, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. No. C11875500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. No.A5256701). HK-2 and A498 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. No. C11995500BT) containing 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ and were routinely authenticated and tested to ensure the absence of mycoplasma contamination.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, cat. No. R0010) supplemented with both protease (Solarbio, cat. No. P0100) and phosphatase inhibitors (MedChemExpress, cat. No. HY-K0021). Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA protein assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. No. 71285-M). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, cat. No. 03010040001). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight (antibody details are provided in Table S2), followed by incubation with the appropriate secondary antibodies. Signal detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (ThermoFisher, cat. No. CPSOC), and images were acquired with an Azure C300 imaging system (Azure Biosystems).

siRNA transfection

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting human TLN1 (siTLN1-1, siTLN1-2, and siTLN1-3) and a non-targeting control siRNA (siNC) were synthesized by a commercial provider (HonorGene, China). Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and transfected at 50–60% confluence. siRNA transfection was performed using a lipid-based nucleic acid transfection reagent (YEASEN, China, cat. No. 40802ES01) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, siRNAs were diluted in Opti-MEM (Opti-MEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. No. 31985062) and mixed with the Yeasen transfection reagent to form transfection complexes for 10–15 min at room temperature. The complexes were then added to the culture medium at a final siRNA concentration of 50 nM. Cells were collected 48 h after transfection for subsequent assays. Knockdown efficiency was verified by quantitative real-time PCR and Western blotting.

Wound healing assay

A wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the migratory capacity of A498 cells following TLN1 knockdown. Cells transfected with siNC or siTLN1#3 were seeded into 6-well plates and grown to approximately 90% confluence. A straight scratch was generated across the cell monolayer using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed by washing twice with PBS. Serum-free medium was added to minimize proliferation-related effects on wound closure. Images of the wound area were acquired at 0, 24, and 48 h using an inverted phase-contrast microscope. The wound closure rate represents how much the wound width has decreased relative to the starting width. Quantification was performed using ImageJ software. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

Colony formation assay

A colony formation assay was conducted to assess the long-term proliferative ability of A498 cells after TLN1 silencing. Following transfection with siNC or siTLN1#3, cells were trypsinized and seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well. Cells were cultured for 10 days until visible colonies appeared, with the culture medium replaced every three days. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. After washing and air-drying, colonies containing at least 50 cells were counted. Colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ. All experiments were performed in biological triplicate.

CCK-8 assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (APExBIO, cat. No. K1018). A498 cells transfected with siNC or siTLN1#3 were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10³–3 × 10³ cells per well. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) was measured using a microplate reader. The relative OD value reflects the number of viable cells. Each time point was measured in five replicate wells, and all experiments were independently repeated three times.

Patient clinical samples and data

A total of three RCC tissue samples were obtained at initial diagnosis from patients treated at Zhongshan Hospital, Xiamen University, between 2023 and 2025. Retrospective study of patients’ data were collected and are summarized in Table S3. All tissue specimens were immediately fixed or stored according to standard biobanking procedures before subsequent analysis. Ethical approval for the use of human samples was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Xiamen University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

To further explore the correlation between platelets and ccRCC, we retrospectively gathered platelet counts from 160 ccRCC patients and 160 healthy individuals (Table 1), and univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. The results all showed a significant correlation between platelet count and ccRCC (P value < 0.05), indicating that a higher platelet count is a risk factor for ccRCC (odds ratio > 1) (Table 2).

Differential expression and GO enrichment analysis

To explore the impact of platelets on ccRCC, we conducted differential expression analysis and AS analysis using the RNA sequencing dataset GSE126964 from Chinese ccRCC patients, as shown in Fig. 1A. First, we performed differential expression analysis of genes using the DESeq2 software. The results showed that 2295 genes were differentially expressed, with 1268 genes upregulated and 1027 genes downregulated (Fig. 1B). We also visualized the 15 most significantly upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 1D). PCA indicated a significant difference in clustering between tumor and normal samples in GSE126964 (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, we performed GO enrichment analysis for the upregulated and downregulated genes. The analysis showed that upregulated genes were mainly enriched in platelet activation and angiogenesis pathways, while downregulated genes were mostly involved in cellular energy metabolism processes (Figs. 1E-F).

Alternative splicing events and GO enrichment analysis in Chinese ccRCC patients

To systematically reveal the characteristics of AS occurrences in ccRCC patients, we detected a total of 1,331 ASEs (Fig. 1A). Among them, A5SS (alternative 5’ splice site) and cassette exon (alternative exon) were the most common event types (Fig. 2A). PCA showed that the clustering differences between tumor samples and normal samples were also significant (Fig. 2B). We then generated a heatmap of the DASEGs (Fig. 2C).

Next, we conducted GO enrichment analysis on the DASEGs. The results indicated that these genes were mainly involved in pathways related to cellular lipid metabolism, GTPase regulation, axon guidance, platelet activation, and platelet degranulation (Fig. 2D). This result is generally consistent with the analysis of DEGs.

To validate these findings further, we selected four DASEGs (VEGFB, PTK2, SYK, FYN) from the platelet activation and degranulation pathway. Box plots revealed that the splicing ratios of these four genes were significantly reduced in tumor samples (Fig. 2E). The consistent reduction in multiple genes suggests that the AS mechanism is significantly disrupted in ccRCC.

Finally, we analyzed the intersection between DASEGs and DEGs, which resulted in 153 key genes that showed significant changes in both expression and splicing (Fig. 2F). Further GO analysis of these 153 genes showed that they were mainly enriched in core cancer processes. These include lipid metabolism, signal transduction, cytoskeletal remodeling, transmembrane transport, and angiogenesis (Fig. 2G). We then selected FGFR2 (MXE event) and ARHGAP24 (A5SS event), which are enriched in the angiogenesis pathway, to show their AS ratios and expression levels (measured in FPKM)(Fig. 2H). The results indicated that the trends in AS and expression changes for these two genes were consistent, suggesting that they may play important roles in the mechanisms behind ccRCC.

Comparison with TCGA data revealed specific cancer-related splicing events in ccRCC in the Chinese population

To screen for specific and potentially functionally meaningful DASEs in Chinese ccRCC patients, we did an AS analysis of the TCGA-KIRC dataset (Fig. 3A) and did GO and KEGG enrichment analysis on the DASEGs (Supplementary Figs. 1 A-B). We then crossed the NIR from the GSE126964 and TCGA-KIRC datasets (Fig. 3B). The analysis of the overlapping significantly expressed NIR DASEs in both sample groups revealed that 349 DASEs changed significantly in both cohorts (Fig. 3B). These common DASEGs were mostly found in pathways related to angiogenesis, such as SYK, PTK2, and FGFR2 (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Figs. 1 C-D).

Additionally, 281 specific DASEs were found in the Chinese cohort that were unique to the Chinese population. The top 10 significantly enriched pathways included things like cellular lipid metabolism, platelet degranulation, and platelet activation (Fig. 3D-E). Among these, ALDOA, TLN1, LAMP2, and CD63 were closely related to the aforementioned platelet-related pathways. We visualized representative splicing profiles using IGV sashimi plots and compared splicing ratios between tumor and paired adjacent normal tissues. TLN1 and ALDOA were presented in the main figures (Fig. 3F–G), while LAMP2 and CD63 were shown in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Figs. 1E–F). The results indicated that ALDOA and LAMP2 exhibited increased splicing ratios in tumor samples, whereas TLN1 and CD63 showed decreased splicing ratios.

Subsequently, we selected the top 10 genes (APEH, AUH, ALDOA, TLN1, LAMP2, FGFR2, CD63, VEGFB, PTK2, SYK) for AS detection, which occurred in tumors and paired samples, and the splicing results of these 10 genes matched up with our analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2A).

Analysis of RNA-binding proteins co-regulated with alternative splicing levels in the population of Chinese ccRCC

AS abnormalities are often related to changes in RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). Therefore, we analyzed the RBPs that changed together with DASEs associated with platelet activation and degranulation pathways. To further investigate their regulatory roles, we found 143 RBPs that were differentially expressed (Fig. 4A). These differentially expressed RBPs showed significant differences in expression between tumor and adjacent normal tissues, and the heatmap clustering made it easy to tell the two sample types apart (Fig. 4B).

Next, we did a GO enrichment analysis on the 143 RBP-targeted DASEGs. The top 10 pathways included things like regulation of cell morphology, platelet activation, platelet degranulation, and blood coagulation (Fig. 4C). To reveal the role of RBPs in AS regulation, we constructed a network showing how these genes work together based on the top 10 key RBPs (Fig. 4D). Several key DASEGs (such as ALDOA, TLN1, CD63, PTK2, etc.) were located at the core of the network, with ALDOA, TLN1, and LAMP2 closely linked to the platelet regulation pathway. These genes could be potential cancer biomarkers or treatment targets for kidney cancer patients in China.

TLN1 is highly expressed in ccRCC and promotes tumor cell proliferation and migration

Among the three platelet-related genes, ALDOA and TLN1 showed differential expression in ccRCC (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Since the function of ALDOA in ccRCC has been reported before, we further investigated TLN1. Our AS analysis revealed that the level of NIR of TLN1 in ccRCC was significantly lower (Fig. 3F), and its cassette exon level was significantly positively correlated with the total expression level of TLN1 (Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). RT-qPCR showed that the AS level of TLN1 was significantly lower in ccRCC cell lines and ccRCC tissue samples (Supplementary Fig. 3C-D). Based on TCGA-KIRC, we conducted a correlation analysis between TLN1 and RBP, and found that TLN1 was significantly positively correlated with APEH (Supplementary Fig. 3E). Additionally, we explored the clinical characteristics of TLN1 in TCGA-KIRC, and found that the protein level expression of TLN1 was higher in tumor tissues, but its RNA level expression was downregulated and was associated with poor prognosis of patients (Fig. 5A-B, Supplementary Fig. 3F-K), suggesting that it may have complex transcriptional regulation and post-translational modifications.

In addition, based on the TCGA-KIRC data, we used the mRNA dryness index (mRNAsi) algorithm to calculate the cancer dryness index of ccRCC samples and constructed a gene co-expression network. We found that the genes in the red module had the most significant correlation with mRNAsi, and the expression levels of TLN1 were significantly positively correlated with DASEGs (Supplementary Fig. 4A-B). The GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the red module genes was significant, and they were enriched in cancer cell dryness-related pathways (Supplementary Fig. 4C-D). We also analyzed the expression correlation between TLN1 and human selenium protein-related genes, and found that it was most significantly correlated with 3 selenium protein-related genes (TXNRD3, SELENOF, and TXNRD13) (Supplementary Fig. 4E-F). The GO and KEGG enrichment analysis showed that it was significantly enriched in the selenium compound metabolism-related pathways (Supplementary Fig. 4G-H).

The preliminary analysis indicated that there was a reduction in selective splicing and an increase in transcriptional level of TLN1 in patients with renal cancer. Subsequently, we confirmed its abnormal expression and explored its function: Western blot analysis showed that compared with normal renal cells HK-2, the expression of TLN1 was increased in various renal cancer cell lines (A498, 786-O, Caki-1); in clinical tumor tissues, its expression level was also significantly higher than that in adjacent tissues (Fig. 5A-B).

We used three siRNAs to target the TLN1 gene. Among them, siTLN1#3 showed the strongest gene silencing effect (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. 5A). Functional experiments demonstrated that the downregulation of TLN1 significantly inhibited the proliferation ability of A498 and 786-O cells. The cell proliferation rate was observed to decrease significantly in the CCK-8 experiment. Moreover, the downregulation of TLN1 also reduced the cell migration ability. The scratch healing rates at 24 h and 48 h both showed significant decreases. The colony formation experiment further revealed that the downregulation of TLN1 significantly reduced the number of cell colonies, suggesting a decrease in the long-term growth potential of the cells (Fig. 5C-E, Supplementary Fig. 5B-F). Additionally, we preliminarily explored the effects of TLN1 on the core genes of the platelet activation/degranulation pathway (SYK, PTK2, and VEGFB). It was found that after siTLN1 treatment, the levels of SYK, PTK2, and VEGFB were all downregulated, but PTK2 did not show a statistically significant difference (P value > 0.05), indicating that TLN1 may have a certain regulatory effect on this pathway.

In summary, TLN1 is abnormally overexpressed in renal cancer and promotes tumor cell growth and migration. This phenomenon may be related to the reduction of AS, which is consistent with previous research results and supports its role in promoting tumor progression in RCC.

Discussion

The high metastatic rate and prognostic heterogeneity of ccRCC present significant challenges for its clinical management. By integrating transcriptomic data with in vitro functional validation, this study uncovers the potential roles of platelet-related pathways and aberrant AS in ccRCC progression. Through a comparative analysis between the TCGA dataset and a Chinese cohort, we identified Chinese population-specific DASEGs, notably TLN1 and ALDOA. These population-specific splicing events not only point to underlying oncogenic mechanisms but also hold clear translational potential. They highlight the necessity of incorporating ethnic and genetic backgrounds into future precision medicine strategies, as these population-specific molecular features could serve as precise targets for individualized prognostic assessment and therapeutic intervention.

While previous research has predominantly focused on the classical functions of TLN1 in focal adhesion formation and physical tissue remodeling, this study provides the first systematic evidence linking its aberrant AS events to the malignant phenotypes of ccRCC. Mechanistically, we hypothesize that the NIR occurring within the TLN1 gene alters its mRNA stability or translational efficiency, thereby contributing to the abnormal upregulation of its overall expression in ccRCC. To further map the regulatory cascade, we identified and constructed an upstream network driven by differentially expressed RBPs. Specifically, the APEH-TLN1 splicing regulatory axis appears to play a central role in the dysregulated platelet signaling within ccRCC: dysregulated upstream APEH expression remodels TLN1 transcript splicing, resulting in upregulated TLN1 expression, which subsequently mediates abnormal platelet activation and degranulation in the tumor microenvironment. This TLN1-driven disruption of platelet pathways enhances the proliferative and migratory capacities of tumor cells, ultimately driving the malignant progression of ccRCC.

Furthermore, given that various molecules are implicated in cancer stemness [16, 17], our analysis utilizing the mRNAsi revealed that TLN1 is closely associated with CSC transcriptional networks and related modules [12]. It is reasonable to infer that TLN1 may facilitate ccRCC progression through an additional mechanism: modulating the self-renewal capacity and homeostatic properties of CSCs. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests a therapeutic role for selenium in ccRCC management [18]. By evaluating the expression correlation between TLN1 and key selenoprotein-encoding genes, we found that TLN1 expression levels hold promise as a translational indicator. It could serve as a predictive biomarker for the efficacy of adjuvant selenium therapy in ccRCC patients, offering a novel approach for evaluating nutritional and metabolic interventions in oncology.

Although this study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying ccRCC, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as [19] emphasized, oncogenic driver networks typically arise from complex interactions across multiple layers, including transcription, splicing, and signal transduction. The AS events identified here are primarily derived from computational inferences of bulk transcriptomic data; thus, they currently represent candidate regulatory events. Direct confirmation via targeted RNA sequencing or isoform-specific RT-PCR in independent, large-scale cohorts is still lacking. Second, the findings regarding “Chinese population specificity” are largely observational and specific to the current datasets. Given the inherent limitations of bulk transcriptomics—where differential expression and pathway enrichment are highly susceptible to technical variations such as sample composition and sequencing batches—rigorous orthogonal validation in independent clinical cohorts remains essential [20, 21]. Third, the RBP-TLN1 network constructed in this study is fundamentally a predictive model; establishing a definitive causal regulatory relationship requires direct genetic perturbation assays. Finally, the functional assessments were conducted exclusively in vitro. Without in vivo animal models and further confirmation of phenotypic specificity through targeted rescue assays, the broad applicability of these biological conclusions should be interpreted with caution.

In summary, this study identifies TLN1 as a critical node linking aberrant post-transcriptional splicing, platelet activation pathways, and tumor progression in ccRCC. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for understanding the mechanisms of ccRCC advancement and for discovering novel biomarkers. Future comprehensive validation within a multi-omics framework will be vital for translating these discoveries into clinical practice.
