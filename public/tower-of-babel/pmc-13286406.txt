Clinical efficacy of Si-Jun-Zi decoction and artificial tear eye drops for perioperative dry eye management in cataract surgery: A retrospective observational study
Authors: Zi-yang Chen, Xiao-yue Zhou, Zhao-da Ye, Jun Hu, Fa-jie Ke, Yan-hong Hu, Sheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to assess the efficacy of Si-Jun-Zi decoction (SJZD) combined with artificial tear eye drops on tear film, Meibomian gland secretion, and visual quality in patients with dry eye during the perioperative period of cataract surgery (PPCS-DE). A retrospective observational study including 72 patients with PPCS-DE was conducted between January and October 2023. The observation group included 36 patients who had received artificial tear eye drops and SJZD decoction in addition to conventional perioperative treatment. The control group included 36 patients who had received only artificial tear eye drops. The primary outcome was efficacy. Secondary outcomes included the noninvasive breakup time (NIBUT), tear secretion, Meibomian gland secretion character score, discharge ability score, subjective ocular symptom score, visual quality, and safety. Compared with the control group, the observation group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in NIBUT, tear secretion, scores for Meibomian gland secretion character, and discharge ability (all P < .05). The subjective ocular symptom score in the observation group was lower than in the control group (all P < .001). The improvement in the foreign body sensation score from baseline in the observation group was greater than in the control group (P = .003). Compared with those in the control group, the total higher-order aberrations, coma, and spherical aberration of the total cornea and total higher-order aberrations corneal anterior surface in the observation group were significantly improved (all P < .05). The overall efficacy in patients in the observation group was higher than in the control group (P < .05). SJZD combined with artificial tear eye drops appears to be an effective and safe therapy for patients with PPCS-DE, and improves visual quality.

1. Introduction

Age-related cataract (ARC) is the most prevalent form of cataract and has emerged as the primary cause of blindness worldwide. Cataract phacoemulsification, known for its minimal surgical trauma, short duration, and relatively quick postoperative recovery, has become the preferred surgical procedure for cataract treatment. Studies have shown that more than 50% of patients with ARC already have dry eye before undergoing cataract surgery.[1,2] Cataract phacoemulsification can induce or worsen dry eye symptoms, making it the major cause of postoperative discomfort in patients. The application of eye drops during the perioperative period of cataract surgery inevitably causes damage to the ocular surface, potentially worsening dry eye symptoms.[3]

Alongside improvements in quality of life, the demand for visual function and eye comfort after cataract surgery has increased. Therefore, clinicians need to pay close attention to perioperative dry eye management. Currently, Western medicine predominantly uses artificial tear eye drops to moisten the ocular surface during the perioperative period of cataract surgery in order to increase tear volume and ameliorate symptoms. Despite this measure, patient complaints of foreign body sensation and discomfort in the eyes persist. Dry eye during the perioperative period of cataract surgery (PPCS-DE) involves multiple factors and has a complex underlying mechanism. Sole reliance on Western medicine has yielded unsatisfactory results. Therefore, a combination of Chinese and Western medicine may help alleviate dry eye symptoms and improve therapeutic efficacy in this context.

According to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), elderly patients experience physical decline, weakened splenic function, deficiency of qi and blood, and the inability of essence and blood to nourish their eyes, resulting in dry eye and lens opacity.[4] While cataract surgery can improve patients’ visual acuity, invasive operations can damage healthy qi as well as influence the structure of the ocular surface and the stability of the tear film, leading to the development or exacerbation of dry eye. TCM treatment focuses on invigorating the spleen and replenishing qi. Si-Jun-Zi decoction (SJZD), documented in the Prescriptions of Peaceful Benevolent Dispensary (Tai Ping Hui Min He Ji Ju Fang) during the Song Dynasty, is used to treat spleen deficiency syndrome. Research has shown that it has good efficacy and safety in treating ophthalmic diseases caused by spleen deficiency syndrome, such as dry eyes and cataracts.[5,6]

Based on the concepts of a holistic approach, syndrome differentiation, and treating the root cause of the disease, this study aims to evaluate the clinical efficacy of SJZD combined with artificial tear eye drops in treating PPCS-DE and its effect on visual quality in order to provide new ideas and methods for the clinical management of this condition.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This retrospective observational study assessed the efficacy of SJZD combined with artificial tear eye drops in patients with PPCS-DE at the Second People’s Hospital Affiliated with Fujian University of TCM (Fujian, China). The primary outcome was efficacy. Secondary outcomes included changes in noninvasive breakup time (NIBUT), tear secretion, Meibomian gland secretion score, discharge ability score, subjective ocular symptom score, visual quality, and safety. The study was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Second People’s Hospital Affiliated with Fujian University of TCM (SPHFJP-Y20230308-01).

2.2. Participants

Cases were included if they met the following inclusion criteria: met the criteria for PPCS-DE, surgical criteria for cataract phacoemulsification combined with intraocular lens implantation, and diagnostic criteria for TCM syndrome; were 50 to 80 years of age; and presented to Second People’s Hospital Affiliated with Fujian University of TCM from January 2023 to October 2023. Cases were excluded if they lacked complete clinical records; had severe dry eyes and poor ocular condition and had not undergone cataract surgery; had other eye diseases; had a recent history of taking oral medications that caused dry eyes; had severe liver, kidney, brain, or other organ damage, as well as mental illness; had taken other TCM or therapies before or during treatment that negatively impacted the evaluation of treatment effectiveness; and/or had poorly controlled underlying diseases such as diabetes and hypertension (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of the study. PPCS-DE, dry eye during the perioperative period of cataract surgery. PPCS-DE = dry eye during the perioperative period of cataract surgery, TCM = traditional Chinese medicine.

The included cases were divided into an observation group and a control group according to the interventions they had received. Two groups of patients underwent phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation surgery (procedures were performed by the same chief physician). Tobramycin eye drops (s.a. ALCON-COUVREUR n.v.; Registration No. H20020174) combined with fluorometholone eye drops (Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.; Registration No. H20080025) were administered every 2 hours for 1 week, and then 4 times a day for 3 weeks, after surgery. Artificial tear eye drops (Hypromellose 2910, Dextran 70 and Glycerol Eye drops, Alcon Laboratories, Inc.; Registration No. H20140264) were administered 4 times a day for 4 weeks. In addition, the observation group was given SJZD orally for 4 weeks after surgery (one dose orally every day). The decoction contained 9 g of Radix Codonopsis, 9 g of Rhizoma Atractylodis macrocephala, 9 g of Poria, and 6 g of Radix et Rhizoma Glycyrrhizae.

Diagnostic criteria for PPCS-DE were based on “Chinese expert consensus on dry eye: Examination and Diagnosis (2020)”[7] and “Chinese expert consensus on prevention and treatment of dry eye during the perioperative period of cataract surgery (2021),”[8] and included subjective symptoms: sensation of dryness, visual fatigue, foreign body sensation, burning sensation, tears, increased secretions, and vision fluctuations; significant decrease in tear film stability: NIBUT < 10 s; and tear secretion (no-anesthesia Schirmer|test) < 10 mm/5 minutes.

The surgical criteria for cataract phacoemulsification combined with intraocular lens implantation were derived from the “Chinese guideline for cataract surgery in adults (2023),”[9] and included the following: when the patient’s visual function cannot meet the daily visual needs owing to cataract, the patient is willing to improve vision, and cataract extraction surgery is expected to improve existing visual function; significant refractive error, anisometropia, monocular diplopia, glare, and other factors; lens opacity affects the diagnosis and treatment of posterior eye disease; is accompanied by lens-derived inflammatory reactions or secondary glaucoma; the lens causes primary angle closure or primary angle closure glaucoma; secondary angle closure or increased intraocular pressure caused by the abnormal position of the crystalline body; and although the non-photosensitive eye or the operation is not expected to improve visual function, removal of the cloudy lens is necessary for cosmetic reasons.

Diagnostic criteria for traditional Chinese medicine syndrome, derived from “Ophthalmology of traditional Chinese medicine,”[10] included the following – main symptoms: dry and gritty eyes; cloudy crystalline lens; blurred vision; secondary symptoms: fatigue and weakness; shortness of breath and reluctance to speak; etiolate complexion; and tongue and pulse manifestations: pale tongue with a thin white coating, and a weak and slow pulse. To diagnose the condition of spleen qi deficiency, the main symptoms must be present, with 2 or more secondary symptoms, along with the combination of tongue and pulse manifestations.

2.3. Clinical outcomes and instruments

The following indices were assessed before and 1 month after surgery.

2.3.1. Noninvasive breakup time

A 3-dimensional topography analysis system (SIRIUS) was used to measure the NIBUT value. This involved the examinee resting their lower jaw on the chin rest, pressing their forehead against the headband to keep the head stable, and lightly closing and then naturally opening both eyes. The Placido disk was then projected onto the examinee’s corneal surface. After successful focusing, examinees were instructed to blink twice continuously and then keep their eyes open and fixed on the central red dot. The machine automatically captured the tear film image; the measurement was repeated 3 times, and the average of the measurements was taken.

2.3.2. Tear secretion (Schirmer I test)

A strip of tear detection filter paper was prepared, and the end was folded with the mark to form a 5 mm tab, which was placed at the outer 1/3 junction of the examinee’s lower conjunctival sac, with the examinee in an unanesthetized state. The filter paper reading was read after 5 minutes.

2.3.3. Subjective ocular symptom score[11,12]

To assess subjective ocular symptoms, patients were asked if they had any subjective symptoms (redness, photophobia, itching, eye fatigue, dryness, foreign body sensation, or burning sensation). Each item was scored as follows: 0 points: none; 1 point: occasional; 2 points: frequent; and 3 points: persistent.

2.3.4. Meibomian gland secretion characteristic score (MGSCS)[13]

Under a slit-lamp microscope, the characteristics of the gland secretions were observed. 0 points: clear and transparent fluid; 1 point: cloudy fluid; 2 points: cloudy and granular secretion; and 3 points: opaque, toothpaste-like secretion.

2.3.5. Meibomian gland secretion discharge ability score (MGSDAS)[13]

Under a slit-lamp microscope, the patients’ eyelids were pressed with a sterile cotton swab. The openings of the central 5 MGs were observed, and the number of openings that could discharge secretions was recorded as follows: 0 points: 5 glands have secretion discharge capability; 1 point: 3 or 4 glands have secretion discharge capability; 2 points: 1 or 2 glands have secretion discharge capability; and 3 points: no eyelid glands have secretion discharge capability.

2.3.6. Visual quality assessment

Higher-order aberrations (HOAs; including total HOAs, coma, and spherical aberration [SA]) at 10 seconds after blinking in the central 5 mm diameter area of the total cornea and corneal anterior surface were detected via a 3-dimensional topography analysis system (SIRIUS). During the measurement process, the subject’s eyes remained open, and the cornea was fully exposed. The instrument automatically measures the relevant data, with the quality of each acquisition displayed as qualified. Each eye was measured 3 times, and the average value was taken.

2.3.7. Overall efficacy evaluation

In accordance with the Chinese Medicine Clinical Research of New Drugs Guiding Principles,[14] the improvement rate of the clinical symptom score was calculated as follows: (total score of symptom score before treatment − total score of symptom score after treatment)/total score before treatment × 100%. Efficacy was graded as follows: slightly effective: descending range of symptom score ≥ 60%, BUT ≥ 10 s and SIt ≥ 10 mm/5 minutes; effective: descending range of symptom score ≥ 30% and <60%, 5 s ≤ BUT < 10 s, 5 mm/5 minutes ≤ SIt < 10 mm/5 minutes; ineffective: failure to meet the above criteria or further deterioration of the condition. Overall efficacy was calculated as the sum of slightly effective and effective cases as a proportion of total cases.

2.3.8. Safety evaluation

Visual acuity and intraocular pressure were measured in all patients before and after treatment. Patients were observed for drug allergies and adverse reactions during treatment, as well as postoperative complications such as infection, macular edema, and intraocular inflammation.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Sample size: Given that this study is the first to explore the effects of combined use of SJZD and artificial tear eye drops in patients with PPCS-DE, effect size and variance data from similar studies are lacking, and relevant parameters cannot be obtained through pilot tests. Therefore, this study is positioned as an exploratory study. Blinding: In this retrospective study, the data analysts responsible for processing were blinded to the group assignments, and the data were provided to them in a pre-processed, anonymized format.

The data were analyzed with IBM SPSS 26.0 software. The clinical features, including dichotomous or categorical variables, are presented as absolute numbers or percentages. Continuous variables were subjected to tests for normality and homogeneity. Data conforming to a normal distribution are displayed as the mean ± standard deviation or as medians with interquartile ranges. Differences in measurement data between 2 groups were analyzed via the independent sample t-test or the Mann–Whitney U rank sum test. Count data were compared via the chi-square test. For within-group comparisons, the paired sample t-test was used based on differences conforming to a normal distribution. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used if the distribution was not normal. All hypothesis tests were conducted via 2-sided tests. Ordinal Logistic Regression was used to analyze the confounding factors influencing therapeutic efficacy. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3.2. Outcomes for noninvasive breakup time (NIBUT) and tear secretion (Schirmer I test, unanesthetized)

After treatment, the NIBUT was longer in both groups than at baseline (observation group: t = −12.433, P < .001, 95% CI: −8.308–-5.976; control group: t = −14.547, P < .001, 95% CI: −6.429–-4.854). Significantly higher improvements in NIBUT were observed in the observation group than in the control group (13.00 ± 2.74 seconds vs 11.38 ± 2.81 seconds, t = 2.470, P = .016, 95% CI: 0.310–2.917; Fig. 2A). Tear secretion volumes in both groups were greater after treatment than at baseline (observation group: t = −14.430, P < .001, 95% CI: −6.907–-5.204; control group: t = −18.193, P < .001, 95% CI: −5.990–-4.788). Compared with the control group, the observation group presented significantly greater improvements in tear secretion (12.0 [10.0, 14.0] mm vs 11.0 [9.0, 12.8] mm, Z = −1.970, P = .049, Hodges–Lehmann 95% CI: 0.000–2.000; Fig. 2B).

3.7. Safety evaluation

At 1 month, the visual acuity (logMAR) of patients in the 2 groups improved to preoperatively estimated levels (observation group: 0.10 [0.00, 0.10], control group: 0.10 [0.00, 0.20]), and there was no significant difference between the groups (P = .190). Patients in the 2 groups had no abnormal fluctuations in intraocular pressure during treatment and no adverse drug reactions or postoperative complications.

4. Discussion

The occurrence of PPCS-DE may be related to the use of surface anesthetics and disinfectants before surgery, the surgical incision made during surgery, the ultrasonic emulsification energy, and the use of anti-inflammatory and anti-infective eye drops throughout the perioperative period. All of these factors can decrease tear film stability, lower tear production, and exacerbate dry eye. Artificial tear eye drops are the most widely used topical treatment for dry eye in clinical practice. Among these, hydroxypropyl glycerin eye drops are mainly composed of dextran and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, which together act on the mucin layer and can absorb water molecules, effectively increasing the viscosity of tears and increasing the stability of the aqueous layer.[16] In this study, cases in the control group received artificial tear treatment, which was found to significantly prolong the NIBUT, increase tear secretion, improve the subjective symptoms of patients, reduce various HOAs in the anterior cornea, and improve visual quality.

In TCM, the development of cataracts is closely related to the health of the liver, kidneys, and spleen. In TCM clinical practice, cataract development is considered a manifestation of liver and kidney deficiencies or spleen qi weakness. Treatment methods mainly focus on nourishing the liver and kidneys or invigorating the qi in the spleen. Consistent with this concept, PPCS-DE is closely related to spleen qi deficiency. If the spleen qi is deficient and unable to transport fluids upward, it can result in a decrease in tear production, leading to dry and uncomfortable eyes. Dysfunction of the spleen and stomach can cause the 9 orifices to lose their nourishment ability and fail to perform their normal physiological functions. As stated in “Exegesis on ‘Shen Nong’s Canon of Materia Medica’ (Shennong ben cao jing shu),”[17] “spleen deficiency causes dry eyes but can be alleviated through supplementation.”

SJZD is composed of 4 medicinal ingredients: Radix et rhizoma ginseng, Rhizoma atractylodis macrocephalae, Poria, and Radix et rhizoma glycyrrhizae. Among them, Radix et rhizoma ginseng supplements qi and tonifies the spleen as the monarch; Rhizoma atractylodis macrocephalae strengthens the spleen and eliminates dampness as the minister; Poria strengthens the spleen and aids in urination as the assistant; and Radix et rhizoma glycyrrhizae supplements qi, tonifies the middle, and harmonizes the various medicines as the envoy. The combination of the 4 ingredients works together to strengthen the spleen and supplement qi. Modern pharmacological studies have shown that SJZD has antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects, promotes wound healing, and exerts antifatigue effects.[18-20] Clinical studies have shown that SJZD can reduce the progression of spleen deficiency-induced cataracts[5] and potentially prevent the apoptosis of human lens epithelial cells.[21] Shi[5] clinically used modified SJZD to treat Sjogren’s disease, finding that the breakup time of the tear film and tear secretion volume significantly increased compared with those before treatment, with no recurrence observed during the 1-year follow-up. “Ben Cao Zheng Yi”[22] reported that “Radix Codonopsis is not very different from Radix et rhizoma ginseng,” both of which have the effect of invigorating the spleen and supplementing Qi. Owing to the high cost of Radix et rhizoma ginseng, Radix Codonopsis was used in clinical practice. This study applied SJZD in the treatment of PPCS-DE. The results also showed that patients who took SJZD combined with artificial tear eye drops orally experienced greater amelioration of subjective eye symptoms, longer NIBUT, and increased tear secretion than the control group did, indicating that the treatment preserved tear film stability.

The “five wheel theory” proposes that the eyelid is a flesh wheel and corresponds to the spleen, indicating a strong connection between eyelid diseases and the spleen and stomach. In anatomy, the flesh wheel refers to the upper and lower eyelids, and it is also the location of the Meibomian glands. Patients with ARC often have a weakened spleen and qi deficiency as well as impaired ability to achieve ascending clarity and descending turbidity, which can lead to obstruction of the secretion and excretion functions of the Meibomian glands, ultimately reducing the quality of the tear film. This, in turn, affects the stability of the tear film, causing ocular surface damage and leading to dry eye.[23]

This study further investigated the influence of SJZD on the characteristics of Meibomian gland secretions and their excretion capacity. The results showed that artificial tear eye drops did not have a notable effect on the shape or excretion ability of the Meibomian gland secretions, while the addition of SJZD significantly improved the shape and excretion capacity of the Meibomian gland secretions. This manifested as a change from a turbid to a transparent appearance of the Meibomian gland secretions and an unobstructed discharge process. This further improved the lipid layer of the tear film, increased tear film stability, and prolonged the breakup time. Recent research shows that the obstruction of Meibomian gland ducts and abnormal Meibomian gland secretion in patients with dry eye are closely related to the ocular surface microenvironment.[24] SJZD contains various medicinal components with antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects,[25] such as polysaccharides from Codonopsis pilosula, polysaccharides from Atractylodes macrocephala, and flavonoids from Atractylodes macrocephala. The SJZD-induced improvement in Meibomian gland secretion capacity and secretion characteristics may be related to its anti-inflammatory effects. In addition, the study revealed that patients who took SJZD orally had notably lower subjective symptom scores than did the control group, indicating significant amelioration of foreign body sensation. This may be related to the improved quality of Meibomian gland secretions and the greater stability of the tear film.

Typically, tears are evenly distributed on the corneal surface after blinking, maintaining a good optical surface of the cornea. However, patients with dry eye experience reduced tear film stability, resulting in a shortened breakup time, corneal surface roughness, increased wavefront aberrations, and increased light scattering, which affects the quality of their vision. The generation of HOAs is due mainly to subtle irregularities or defects in the ocular optical system. Compared with healthy individuals, patients with dry eye are more prone to corneal irregular astigmatism and HOAs, which, in turn, affect visual quality.[26] Furthermore, the improvement in the quality of vision of patients with dry eye can be attributed to the reduction in anterior corneal aberrations due to improved tear film stability.[27] Therefore, HOAs are an important indicator for evaluating visual quality in patients with dry eye.

Patients with PPCS-DE had unstable postoperative tear film and increased HOAs. Lee et al.[28] found that cyclosporine A was effective in alleviating optical aberrations in PPCS-DE. However, cyclosporine A has not been recommended by guidelines for anti-inflammatory treatment for cataract in the perioperative period. Montés-Micó et al.[29] found that the use of artificial tear eye drops can reduce optical aberrations and improve optical quality in patients with dry eye. Our study findings align with these findings. Usually, coma is caused by factors such as eyelid pressure or gravity acting on the tear film, resulting in its asymmetric distribution in the vertical and horizontal directions.[30] Compared with the control group, the total HOAs, coma, and SA of the total cornea in individuals who took SJZD orally were greatly improved. Together with the results of the Meibomian gland secretion characteristics and discharge ability score, SJZD has a synergistic effect on improving total HOAs, and the coma of patients with PPCS-DE may be related to the improvement in Meibomian gland secretions. The SA of the corneal anterior surface is related to the more rapid breakup of the tear film in the central area than in the peripheral area, leading to symptoms such as blurred vision at night, which affects nighttime vision.[30] The present results revealed that the 2 groups did not show significant differences in improvement in SA and coma of the corneal anterior surface.

These contributions notwithstanding, this study has several limitations. All participants in this study had preoperative dry eyes, and owing to the inconvenience of conducting SIt and Meibomian gland secretion tests after surgery, the impact of surgery on the dry eye condition was not further analyzed. However, during the study, the degree of lens nucleus hardness was recorded in detail in both groups, ensuring that comparable ultrasonic energy was used during the surgery and minimizing the impact of surgical factors on the results. This study identifies the synergistic effect of SJZD and artificial tear eye drops, and future research could further evaluate the efficacy of SJZD when used alone. In addition, dry eye sequelae may persist for 3 to 6 months; given this, the present retrospective study lacked long-term follow-up data, and the sample size was small. The sample size should be expanded for prospective RCTs or multicenter observation and long-term outcome assessments and safety evaluation, and future studies should aim to explore potential mechanisms of action of SJZD in treating dry eye in order to provide scientific evidence for its clinical application.

5. Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the combination of SJZD with artificial tear eye drops has the potential to improve NIBUT and tear secretion; decrease the subjective ocular symptom score; improve Meibomian gland secretion characteristics and discharge ability score; and reduce HOAs (including total HOAs, coma, and SA) in patients with PPCS-DE. SJZD may therefore serve as a promising complementary treatment option for PPCS-DE and improve visual quality, warranting further exploration in prospective RCTs, mechanistic studies, and long-term outcome assessments in the future.
