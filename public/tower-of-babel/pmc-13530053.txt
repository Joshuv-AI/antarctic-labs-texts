The prevalence of measles vaccination in measles-infected healthcare workers: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sujata Saha, Sumanta Saha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsDocumentable evidence of previous receipt of 2-dose of measles-containing vaccine (MCV) is often sufficient as proof of measles immunity in healthcare workers (HCWs). The usefulness of this policy was assessed by estimating the global prevalences of different MCV-receipt statuses in measles infected HCWs.MethodsEligible publications since 1974 were searched in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus databases. Pooled prevalences were estimated by prevalence meta-analysis and meta-regressed using durations since MCV inclusion in the national immunization schedule (NIS) of respective study countries to measles outbreak years as a moderator.ResultsOf the 7571 citations retrieved, 27 studies reporting on 1319 measles-infected HCWs aged 18-60 years (outbreak years: 1985–2019) were reviewed. Prevalences of measles-infected HCWs were– a. by MCV-receipt statuses: ≥1-dose: 41.0% (95% CI: 28.0, 55.0); ≥2-dose: 28.0% (95% CI: 11.0, 46.0); unvaccinated: 31.0% (95% CI: 17.0, 46.0); unknown: 21.0%; b. by gender: females: 65.0% (95% CI: 57.0, 72.0); males: 35.0% (95% CI: 28.0, 43.0); c. by birth year: born after MCV inclusion in NIS: 77.0% (95% CI: 66.0, 87.0) and in born before: 6.0% (95% CI: 0.0, 17.0); d. by health profession: nurses: 31.0% (95% CI: 24.0, 39.0); doctors: 27.0% (95% CI: 19.0, 36.0); students 5.0% (95% CI: 1.0, 12.0). The measles risk in ≥2-dose of MCV recipient HCWs increased with duration since MCV inclusion in NIS to outbreak occurrences (p < .05).ConclusionIn measles infected HCWs, as the rates of being ≥2-dose MCV recipient or unvaccinated were substantial and comparable, the role of documented 2-dose of MCV receipt alone as a proof of measles immunity remains uncertain.

Introduction

Measles is a highly contagious viral infection caused by Paramyxovirus. It claimed more than two million lives annually before the availability of measles vaccine (Orenstein et al., 2024). In 1963, live attenuated measles virus containing vaccine (MCV) was licensed for the first time in the US (Berche, 2022). Subsequently, it was introduced in other high-income countries like the UK, South Korea, and Australia (UK Health Security Agency, 2026, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 2007, National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance, 2025). In the US, about 15 million children were vaccinated within the first 3 years of licensing of MCV – leading to 50% drop in measles incidence (Review of Selected Federal Vaccine and Immunization Policies: Based on Case Studies of Pneumococcal Vaccine, 1979). This quick success inspired a national campaign in the US in 1966 to eliminate measles from the US (Review of Selected Federal Vaccine and Immunization Policies: Based on Case Studies of Pneumococcal Vaccine, 1979). By early 1970s, the potential of MCV in measles prevention was well understood and the World Health Organization (WHO) included measles vaccination in its Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) to expand MCV coverage to resource poor nations (MMWR Recomm Rep, 1997b). As the global MCV coverage increased, the measles-related deaths decreased drastically. By 1990s, measles-related deaths dropped by about 88%, and then, within the first two decades of the 2000s, measles vaccination programs helped averting nearly 32 million deaths globally (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 1997; Dixon et al., 2021). However, even after five decades since the EPI initiative, measles outbreaks continue to happen globally, primarily due to disproportionate 1-dose and 2-dose of MCV coverage. Post COVID-19, in 2023, the global MCV1 coverage dropped to 80%, lowest since 2008, and estimated measles cases globally crossed 10 million (Minta et al., 2024). Even high-income measles eliminated countries with long standing high coverage of two doses of MCV are experiencing whopping surge in measles cases. Recently, Canada and the UK lost their measles elimination status in 2025 and 2026, respectively, due to protracted measles outbreaks (Taylor, 2026).

These surge in measles cases influence health service utilization – leading to an increased measles exposure risk in healthcare workers (HCWs). HCWs are usually the first point of contact to measles infected people, and their risk of acquiring measles is estimated to be 19-times greater than the general population (Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices. Centers for Disease Control Prevention, 2011; Steingart et al., 1999). Measles has been reported to occur in about 5-44% of HCWs (Augusto et al., 2019; Botelho-Nevers et al., 2011; Filia et al., 2017; Gohil et al., 2016; Komitova et al., 2011; Torner et al., 2015). Measles antibody screening data have documented measles seronegativity (i.e. measles antibody levels below the protective threshold) in about 6-19% of the HCWs (Andrew et al., 2016; Aypak et al., 2012; Bianchi et al., 2022; Kanamori et al., 2014; Koivisto et al., 2017). Susceptible HCWs may acquire measles and cause onward transmission of measles to susceptible contacts in healthcare settings like immunocompromised patients and unimmunized infants (Von Linstow et al., 2021). At this backdrop, its essential to review the existing policies for protection of HCWs from measles infection.

Presently, while there is no consensus on such policies, documentable evidence of previous receipt of 2-dose of MCV is popularly considered as an evidence of presumptive measles immunity in HCWs in countries like the US, Australia, New Zealand, and Canada (Australian Technical Advisory Group on Immunisation (ATAGI), 2022, HEALTH NEW ZEALAND, 2025, Public Health Agency of Canada, 2025, U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 2024). Ideally, 1-dose of MCV would be protective too; however, as about 5% of the population doesn’t seroconvert following the initial dose, the 2nd dose of MCV is administered (Berche, 2022). This 2-dose regimen is known to be effective in generating immunity in about 97-98% of vaccinated individuals and considered to be protective for life (U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 2025, Health New Zealand, 2025; Orenstein et al., 2024). However, preliminary data since the early 2000’s from countries with long existing high coverage of 2-dose of MCV have reported measles cases in individuals previously vaccinated with 2-dose of MCV. Age-irrespectively, the measles rates reported in the US and Canada during the 2025 measles outbreaks in 2-dose of MCV recipients were 4-5% (U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 2026, PUBLIC HEALTH AGENCY OF CANADA, 2026). A substantial proportion of these cases have been reported in older children and young adults – Australia (2014-2024, 20-49 year olds): 9% (57/626) (Gorrell et al., 2026); California, the US (2000-2015, young adults and children): 11% (26/232) (Cherry and Zahn, 2018); NZ (2019-2020, in 20-29 year olds): 9% (67/712);(The New Zealand Institute for Public Health and Forensic Science (PHF Science), 2020) South Korea (2019, in 10-29 year olds): 11.7% (11/94) (Choi et al., 2020; Han et al., 2021); Quebec, Canada (2011, in 12 to ≥18 year olds): 5% (53/1116) (De Serres et al., 2012). Therefore, it’s critical to review the extent to which 2-dose of MCV should be continued to be considered as a criterion of presumptive measles immunity in HCWs. Best known to us, presently there is no compiled summative evidence from measles outbreak data on HCWs – which provides a more direct and definitive way of studying measles susceptibility by bypassing the interpretational intricacies of measles antibody measuring commercial assays (Latner et al., 2020; Saha et al., 2025b) and lack of a correlate of protection for measles (Bolotin et al., 2020; Chen et al., 1990; Orenstein et al., 2024) – to understand how the prevalences of measles-infected HCWs previously vaccinated with ≥1-dose of MCV or ≥2-dose MCV and those unvaccinated differ. Therefore, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis using measles outbreak data on HCWs to estimate the following –(1) The global pooled prevalence of measles-infected HCWs of different previous measles vaccination statuses – ≥1 dose recipients, 1 dose recipients, ≥2 dose recipients and unvaccinated.(2) The prevalence of measles-infected HCWs by gender, HCWs’ profession type, and birth year (before vs after implementation of MCV at national level in respective nations (in national immunization schedule (NIS) usually) irrespective of their previous measles vaccination status.(3) Determining the effect of the duration since the year of inclusion of MCV in NIS to the year of outbreak on prevalences of measles-infected HCWs of different previous measles vaccination statuses – ≥1 dose recipients, 1 dose recipients, ≥2 dose recipients and unvaccinated.(4) The estimated prevalence rates of measles-infected HCWs of different previous measles vaccination statuses – ≥1 dose recipients, 1 dose recipients, ≥2 dose recipients and unvaccinated – in various WHO regions.

Methods

This review report follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (PRISMA checklist: Supplementary File: File S1: Table S1) (Page et al., 2021). The protocol for this review is available in the PROSPERO (CRD42024519337) (Saha and Saha, 2024).

Eligibility criteria

Observational studies and descriptive reports published since 1974 to the present date, reporting the number of measles-infected HCWs during measles outbreaks with the MCV receipt statuses of these HCWs, were included in this review. Studies from any country were eligible. Following study types were excluded – editorials, letters, commentaries, and conference abstracts.

Studies published from 1974 onwards were considered eligible as measles vaccination was introduced in the EPI for a widespread global coverage from this year. Before 1974, only in a few high-income industrialized nations like the US had licensed MCV. The definitions of measles infection and HCW were accepted as per the authors of the reviewed studies.

Search strategy and data collection

Eligible studies were searched (last search date: 5 March, 2026) in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus databases using the following search terms – ‘rubeola’ OR ‘measles’ AND (‘mmr vaccine’ OR ‘mmr vaccination’ OR ‘measles mumps rubella vaccine’) AND (‘healthcare workers’ OR ‘healthcare professionals’ OR ‘doctors’ OR ‘nurses’ OR ‘physiotherapists’ OR ‘physicians’ OR ‘medical students’). The following MeSH Terms (PubMed) were used in this search ‘measles’, ‘measles mumps rubella vaccine’, ‘measles vaccine’. Bibliographies of the publications reviewed in full-text were manually scrutinized for any additional eligible studies.

Study selection, data abstraction, and risk of bias (RoB) assessment

Citations retrieved by the database searches were uploaded in Rayyan systematic reviews software (Ouzzani et al., 2016). After excluding the duplicates, two reviewers independently skimmed the titles and abstracts of the remaining papers to assess their eligibility for inclusion in this review. A full-text reading ensued when a citation seemed to match fully or partially against the pre-stated eligibility criteria.

Data items

From the studies included in this review, the review authors independently abstracted the salient features like author name, year of publication, study design, study population, measles diagnostic method, measles outbreak year, number of measles infected HCWs and their MCV receipt statuses, the profession of the HCWs, and if born before or after the inclusion of MCV in NIS of the respective nations.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of measles-infected HCWs with following MCV receipt statuses (based on the number of MCV doses received): ≥1-dose, ≥2-dose, 1-dose, and unvaccinated (no MCV doses received). HCWs with confirmed previous MCV receipt status but no information on the number of MCV doses received were included in the ≥1-dose recipient category. For HCWs with no MCV receipt information were categorized as unknown or not reported. The number of MCV doses received by infected HCWs was accepted as per the study authors, regardless of whether they were verified with vaccination records or not as measles vaccination records are usually unavailable in published literature for about 40-50% of HCWs (Kanamori et al., 2014; Urbiztondo et al., 2013).

When more than one study reported of an outbreak that occurred at the same time of the year in a country or in a specific state/region of a country – we included one that reported measles infection data for the longest period.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes were estimated measles prevalence of the following HCW categories: – male and female HCWs, HCWs of different profession types, and HCWs born before and after MCV inclusion in NIS.

Profession wise, the HCWs were categorized into four categories – doctors (including resident physicians), nurses (including midwives and nurse assistants), students (including students of different health-related courses like medicine, nursing, pharmacy, etc.), and other HCWs (anyone other than those mentioned previously like paramedical professionals, social workers, ambulance workers, porters, and other hospital support staff).

To determine if the year of birth was before or after inclusion of MCV in NIS, HCWs were presumed to be born in the nation where the outbreak occurred – according to the studies included in this paper – unless the country of birth was mentioned explicitly. When the year of birth was unavailable but age-range or information indicative of the time of birth was available, we subtracted the minimum and maximum age of the age-range data (when reported) from the year of outbreak to calculate the year of birth. For instance, if an outbreak occurred in 2019 and the oldest person was 50 years old, the year of birth of that person would be 1969. In such situations, where the number of HCWs with the minimum or maximum ages were not mentioned, we included one HCW of that age in the numerator as it confirms the presence of at least one person of that age. The year of MCV inclusion in the NIS was retrieved from citable sources and these have been enlisted below in the results section for countries included in this review.

Risk of bias (RoB)

Using the study design-specific quality assessment tool of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI), the RoB of the included studies were determined by SS1 and SS2 independently for each study (Munn Z, 2020).

Meta-analysis

Using random effect (DerSimonian and Laird) meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of measles infected HCWs of following MCV receipt statuses were estimated: ≥1-dose, 1-dose, ≥2-dose, and unvaccinated. It was also estimated for those with MCV status unknown or unreported in the parent study. Additionally, measles prevalence estimates were calculated by gender, health profession types, born before and after MCV inclusion in NIS. The 95% confidence interval (CI) and stabilization of variances were determined by the exact binomial procedure and Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation, respectively (Nyaga et al., 2014). Heterogeneity was estimated by I-squared (I 2 ) (categorized as low, moderate, and high based on its values of 25%, 50%, and 75%, respectively) and Chi 2 test statistics (statistically significant at p < .1). The predictive intervals were estimated for respective meta-analysis models.

Cumulative meta-analysis models were used to examine the changes in prevalence trends in HCWs of different previous MCV receipt statuses from oldest reported outbreak to the latest reported outbreak (sorted by outbreak year) for WHO regions where ≥10 studies were available to fit in the meta-analysis model.

Subgroup analysis

For the primary outcomes reported in ≥ 10 studies, subgroup-based analysis was done by categorizing countries by WHO regions to which they belong.

Metaregression

For any high heterogeneity in the primary meta-analysis models, a meta-regression analysis was performed by including the effect sizes as the outcome and duration (in years) since inclusion of MCV in NIS to outbreak year in respective nations as moderator. This duration was calculated by subtracting the former from the latter. It was used as an estimate of the period a country had an existing routine measles vaccination program as a part of its national immunization program. For studies reporting measles outbreak periods extending beyond 12 months (e.g. 2015–2019) the outbreak period was fitted in the meta-regression models in the following manner: model 1: considering the outbreak start year of extended outbreaks as the year of outbreak, model 2: considering the outbreak end year of extended outbreaks as the outbreak year, and model 3: dropping studies reporting extended outbreaks from the model. Bubble plots were used for a visual assessment of these models.

Publication bias and heterogeneity assessment

Publication bias was assessed visually utilizing funnel plots presenting the prevalence against its standard error and statistically using the Egger’s test.

Sensitivity analysis

Two types of sensitivity analyses were done – 1. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis: dropping a study each time, the meta-analysis models for the primary outcomes were iterated to evaluate the changes in overall effect sizes; 2. to determine the robustness of the meta-regression findings, the meta-regression models were iterated assuming moderate levels of heterogeneity (I2 value of 50%).

We used Stata statistical software (version 19.5; StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) metaprop package for analyses.

Salient Characteristics of Reviewed Studies.

Majority of these studies were based on outbreaks reported in WHO regions of Europe (63.0%; 17/27), followed by Western Pacific (22.2%; 6/27), Americas (11.1%; 3/27), and Eastern Mediterranean (3.7%; 1/27) region. Measles was diagnosed by one or more of these methods – lab-diagnosed (reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) testing, clinically-diagnosed, or epidemiologically traced linked cases (Table 1). Country of birth was not specified explicitly in any of the studies. The years of MCV inclusion in NIS of the countries included in this study ranged between 1964 and 1987 (File S2: Table S1). The age of HCWs in these studies ranged between 18 and 60 years. Risk of bias evaluation of the reviewed studies are included in File S2: Table S2. After assessing risk of bias, we decided to include all the studies in this review. The full dataset is available in File S3.

Measles prevalences

Measles vaccination status wise, the pooled prevalences of measles-infected HCWs were - ≥1-dose of MCV recipients: 41.0% (95% CI: 28.0, 55.0; I2 : 92.8%), ≥2-dose of MCV recipients: 28.0% (95% CI: 11.0, 46.0; I2 : 93.0 %), and unvaccinated: 31.0% (95% CI: 17.0, 46.0; I2 : 92.7%) (Figure 2). The estimated prevalence of one does of MCV recipients were 10.0% (95% CI: 6.0, 15.0; I2 : 51.2%) (File S2: Fig S1). Measles prevalence by gender and professional category of HCWs

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

For all outcomes, the visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s test findings did not suggest any small study effect (File S2: Table S4).

Upon sensitivity analysis, the overall pooled prevalences of the respective outcomes obtained in each iteration closely resembled the preliminary estimates (File S2: Table S5-S11).

Besides, sensitivity analysis assuming moderate heterogeneity (50%) in the meta-regression models replicated the findings of the primary models (File S2, Table S12).

Discussion

Ensuring measles protection of HCWs is crucial as the role of measles transmission in healthcare settings has been increasingly recognized in sustained measles outbreaks in World Health Organization’s Measles and Rubella Strategic Framework 2021–2030 (World Health Organization, 2020). Most countries use the documented receipt of 2-dose of MCV as a key criterion to presumptive measles immunity definition. In this study, we estimated pooled prevalence of previous MCV receipt statuses in measles infected HCWs with ≥2-dose of MCV status, and for other MCV receipt statuses – unvaccinated and ≥1-dose of MCV recipient. The prevalence of all these MCV receipt statuses were significant among measles-infected HCWs, and the overlapping 95% confidence intervals between being ≥1-dose of MCV recipient versus unvaccinated and ≥2-dose of MCV recipient versus unvaccinated suggested an absence of statistically significant differences between these groups. These findings call for attention to the trade-offs between the assumption of lifetime immunity in 2-dose of MCV recipients versus the need for occupational serological screening mandates in HCWs irrespective of previous documentable MCV receipt status. Such mandates give a quantifiable estimate of humoural immunity against measles to decide the need for pre-employment MCV administration. Ideally, the gold-standard plaque-reduction neutralization test (PRNT) should be considered for such testing; however, given its practical limitations – being expensive, labour intensive, time consuming, and technologically demanding – tests that are well-correlated with PRNT like the bead-based micro immunoassay with flexibility of multiplexing may be considered instead of traditional commercial EIAs (Dorigo-Zetsma et al., 2015; Latner et al., 2020; Saha et al., 2025b). Simultaneously, research towards the establishment of population specific correlates for measles protection is important to ensure better utilization of these screening tests.

Next, policies on HCWs’ year-of-birth-related presumptive measles immunity definitions tend to be inconsistent among the different countries. For instance, MCV is recommended to HCWs born before 1957 in the US, born during or since 1966 in Australia, and born 1969 onwards in New Zealand (Australian Technical Advisory Group on Immunisation (ATAGI), 2022, U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 2024, Health New Zealand, 2025). Whereas in Canada, the year of birth is not considered as a criterion of presumptive measles immunity definition for HCWs (Public Health Agency of Canada, 2025). Individuals born in the 1950s or 1960s are often considered immune to measles as measles virus circulation was widespread then and almost every child was thought to acquire measles by their adolescence (Hendriks and Blume, 2013). However, the US reported nearly 30% of all measles cases in HCWs between 1985 and 1992 occurred in those born before 1957 (unpublished data) (MMWR Recomm Rep, 1997a). About 5-7% of HCWs born before 1957 were reported to be measles seronegative in the US (Braunstein et al., 1990; Houck et al., 1991; MMWR Recomm Rep, 1997b; Smith et al., 1990). We estimated about 6% of measles cases occurred in HCWs born before inclusion of MCV in NIS. Though distinguishing them by their measles vaccination status was impossible due to paucity of reporting of the same in the parent studies, measles occurrences in these HCWs emphasize the need for their routine pre-employment screening of measles immunity status by measuring the measles protective antibody levels. Compared to the prevalence of measles-infected HCWs born before MCV inclusion in the NIS, the estimated prevalence of those born after MCV inclusion in NIS was nearly 13-times greater (77%), and the 95% confidence intervals suggested a statistically significant difference form the former. This corresponds to the contemporary measles antibody-based serological screening data. A pre-employment measles antibody screening of HCWs in NZ reported lower seropositivity rates against measles (87%) in HCWs born after introduction of MCV in NZ than those born in pre-vaccination era (98% in individuals born before 1969; presumably immune due to the wide circulation of the wild measles virus) (Williamson et al., 2010). A meta-analytic estimate of measles antibody levels in HCWs from European countries reported that HCWs born in the post-vaccination era (defined as 1980 onwards) had 3-fold (95% CI: 2.2–3.5) greater measles susceptibility (due to seronegativity) than those born in pre-vaccination era (before 1980) (Bianchi et al., 2022).

Then, our findings on the progressive increase in measles acquisition risk in ≥2-dose of MCV recipient HCWs with lengthening of the gap between the large-scale implementation of MCV (usually by inclusion in NIS) - (File S2, Fig S6) and measles outbreak in respective nations supports the waning of measles immunity dogma. In countries with long existing high population-level MCV coverage, such as UK, the US, Australia, and NZ, boosting of the vaccine acquired immunity against measles during childhood is unlikely to happen due to a lack of widespread wild measles virus circulation unlike in the pre-measles-vaccination era before the 1960s. Measles antibody titres tend to be lower in measles vaccinated HCWs than measles infected HCWs (Jang et al., 2022) and in individuals born to vaccinated mother than in those born to mothers who acquired measles immunity naturally (Leuridan et al., 2010). Nearly 9-17% of the children vaccinated with 2-dose of MMR turns measles seronegative by their adolescence and early adulthood (Davidkin et al., 2008; Haralambieva et al., 2011; Mollema et al., 2014). Studies from Australia and South Korea reported measles seronegativity or equivocal measles antibody levels in about 20-25% of young people aged between 20 and 29 years (Gidding et al., 2018; Kang et al., 2017). Altogether, these suggests that in post-vaccination era in countries with long existing high coverage of 2-dose of MCV there is a progressive decline in population-level measles immunity (waning of immunity) indicating the need for periodic serosurveys, especially in frontline high risk occupational groups (like HCWs) to ensure effective immunological surveillance and public health policies. Furthermore, this highlight the importance of investigating the potential of MCV delivery via alternative vaccine delivery routes, such as aerosol or intradermal (e.g. microarray patches), in generating more durable and strong measles antibody response compared to the traditional intramuscular or subcutaneous MCV delivery (Baker et al., 2023; Low et al., 2015; Saha et al., 2025a).

Gender wise pooled measles susceptibility from measles outbreak data in HCWs are limited. Our estimated prevalence of measles-infected female HCWs was almost twice higher compared to males. Outbreak data from general population from seven high-income nations reported similar findings in those in their 40s and 60s, but no difference was reported in adolescents and young adults (Green et al., 2022). Measles antibody measurement-based estimates in HCWs are inconsistent. A meta-analysis didn’t find any difference in measles susceptibility between male and female HCWs based on measles antibody measurements estimated using a range of Enzyme Immunoassays (Bianchi et al., 2022). Whereas, in a study conducted in Russia, seronegativity was more common in female HCWs in the 4th decade of life; however, seronegativity wise there was no difference in those aged <39 years and ≥50 years (Kostinov et al., 2021).

Strengthens and limitations

This review draws its main strength by being the first of its kind to estimate the global prevalence of measles-infected HCWs by their MCV receipt statuses to understand how measles burden in HCWs vary by their measles vaccination statuses. Besides, including studies published since 1974 when measles was included in the EPI schedule, makes this paper a comprehensive review of relevant articles published over the last five decades. Furthermore, best known to us, durations since MCV inclusion in NIS to outbreak year has not been used previously to study the changes in measles infection rates in HCWs by their previous MCV receipt statuses. We used MCV inclusion in NIS as the starting time point to reduce between study heterogeneity instead of year of MCV introduction or licensing in a country as this could produce biased estimates because mere introduction of the vaccine doesn’t necessarily equate to its widespread coverage – as happened in Australia where though MCV was introduced in 1968, it was not until 1975 when MCV got incorporated in the NIS (National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance, 2025).

Despite these strengths, our study has certain limitations. Since the studies included in this review are primarily representative of three WHO regions – European region, Americas, and Western Pacific – the figures may not be generalizable to the rest of the WHO regions. Also, we couldn’t ascertain if all MCV doses were from documented vaccination records due to paucity of reporting of such data in the parent studies. Finally, due to the lack of reporting of gender-, occupation-, and age-specific MCV receipt statuses of HCWs in the parent reports, we reported the overall measles prevalence for these categories and were unable to categorize these further by their previous MCV receipt statuses.

Conclusion

The burdens of measles-infected HCWs of different MCV-receipt statuses were substantial and comparable; therefore, it’s critical to review the adequacy of considering previous documented 2-dose of MCV receipt as presumptive proof of measles immunity in HCWs. Measles antibody screening of HCWs may be used as an alternative to it or as an adjunct. While serological evidence of immunity doesn’t warrant measles protection in all HCWs (Bolotin et al., 2020) given that the true correlates of protection for measles remain unknown, using the conventional seroprotection cut-offs to assess measles immunity status remains the only quantifiable way of measuring measles immunity in HCWs. Longer the time passed since the countries have included MCV in their NIS, the burden of measles-infected HCWs previously vaccinated with ≥2-dose of MCV tend to increase. Besides, prevalence of female HCWs and those born after measles vaccination was included in NIS were more common among measles-infected HCWs than their counterparts, respectively. The burden of measles-infected doctors, nurses, and other HCWs were high and comparable and greater than students enrolled in healthcare professions.
