The prevalence, pathogens, and biochemical determinants of common nosocomial infections among colorectal cancer patients
Authors: Syed Sameer Aga, Muhammad Anwar Khan, Hamad Mutlaq Radhi, Abduljawad Ramli Saleh, Mohammed Abdulaziz Alghamdi, Muath Bader Almutairi, Saniya Nissar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Colorectal cancer (CRC) patients are immunocompromised, making them more susceptible to infection. The aim of this study was to estimate the prevalence of nosocomial infection among CRC patients at Prince Noora Oncology Centre in National Guard Health Affairs and to analyze the relationship between various pathogens and clinico-pathological characteristics of CRC.Methodology:A cross-sectional study was carried out at PNOC from the period 2016 until 2024 and included CRC patients whose age was >18 and excluded others. Cancer patients’ demographics, personal and family history, cancer characteristics, infection characteristics, and pathogens were collected from medical records.Results:A total of 377 patients with CRC were included, and 222 (58.9%) of them were males. The mean age was estimated to be 62.1 (SD13.4) years. About 235 (62.3%) of these patients were infected. The total number of detected pathogens was 690, of which 222 (32.2%) were Escherichia coli, followed by Klebsiella pneumonia, 86 (12.5%), and candida species, 61 (8.8%). A significant relationship was found to be between the presence of infection and several variables like blood group (O +ve), cancer stage, and metastasis (P < 0.05).Conclusion:Two-thirds of CRC patients developed nosocomial infections. Gram-negative bacteria like Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumonia are the most involved. In addition, blood group, metastasis, and late-stage cancer were identified as significant risk factors for such infections.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks the third most diagnosed cancer in the world and the second in cancer-related mortality.[1] In Saudi Arabia, CRC is the most common type of cancer among males and the third among females.[23] CRC has many hereditary and environmental risk factors. Family history is responsible for 10–20% of CRC patients, while age and male gender have a strong an association with disease incidence.[4] Several other environmental risk factors like inflammatory bowel diseases, diabetes mellitus, obesity, cholecystectomy, cigarette smoking, red-meat rich diet, alcohol consumption, and disruption of the gut microbiota have also been found to be associated with CRC.[567]

CRC is a multifactorial disease. The epithelial cells of colorectal mucosa can undergo hyperplasia, atypical hyperplasia (mild, moderate, severe), and adenomas, which can eventually develop into carcinoma.[7] This process is usually initiated by carcinogenic factors, causing structural changes in DNA, leading to the malignant transformation of cells into cancer. Generally, cancer results from inactivating tumor suppressor genes and activating oncogenes which are caused by genetic and epigenetic alterations. There are three key pathways for the development of CRC: chromosomal instability (CIN), CpG island methylation phenotype (CIMP), and microsatellite instability (MSI).[7]

CRC and nosocomial infections are inextricably linked through the surgical and hospital-based care pathways, with one influencing the other’s course and outcome. CRC patients are especially vulnerable to hospital-acquired infections because they usually require major colon surgery, take immunosuppressive systemic medications, and have comorbidities like diabetes or malnutrition that impair host defenses.[89]

Immunocompromised patients are more susceptible to infections that can lead to serious clinical complications.[10] These can result in delaying the initiation of chemotherapy treatment, decreasing the routine dosage, prolonging hospitalization, increasing morbidity, decreasing long-term survival rate, and increasing the financial burden on the patients and their families.[11] CRC has been shown to be associated with various infections, which can be due to parasites, viruses, bacteria, or fungi.[6121314] Among CRC patients, Cryptosporidium species, Cystoisospora belli, Cyclospora cayetanensis, Toxoplasma gondii, and Blastocystis hominis are parasites that can cause opportunistic infections.[6101516] CRC patients were more susceptible to a severe SARS-COV2 infection and mortality in comparison to non-CRC.[12] Infections induced by CRC chemotherapy and/or surgery can be caused by several pathogens such as Clostridium difficle, Candida albicans, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.[5131417] It has been demonstrated that reactivation of hepatitis C virus can occur with chemotherapy in CRC patients.[18] In contrast, hepatitis B virus was found to reduce the incidence of liver metastasis in CRC patients.[19] Recently, a study conducted in Qatar found that Epstein Barr Virus (EBV) and Human Papillomavirus (HPV) are also associated with CRC patients.[20]

Nonetheless, the current literature positions nosocomial infection as both a consequence of CRC management and a modulator of CRC prognosis, emphasizing infection prevention and early aggressive control as fundamental components of high-quality CRC care. A focused research in this field benefits both practitioners and patients directly by identifying modifiable risk factors and effective preventive interventions, resulting in safer surgery, implementation of preventive measures, and development of improved treatment strategies for CRC patients for the better oncologic outcomes. Hence, the aim of this study was to investigate the prevalence, pathogens, and risk factors associated with common nosocomial infections among CRC patients at National Guard Hospital, Jeddah, KSA.

Methods

Study settings and participants

This study was carried out at Princess Noora Oncology Centre (PNOC) at King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC) in Jeddah between the periods of 2016 and 2024. All patients ≥18 years of age and diagnosed primarily with CRC were included using International Statistical Classification of Diseases and related health problems version 10 (ICD-10) code C18.9 and C20, whereas other patients diagnosed with other types of cancer were excluded from the study.

Consent and ethical approval

The study was carried out in line with the Helsinki protocol, and ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of King Abdullah International Medical Research Centre (KAIMRC), KSAU-HS, Jeddah was duly acquired prior to conducting this study (Reference No: IRB/0505/23; SP23J/014/02; Dated: 27/02/2023). No participant names or identification were gathered, and the data were kept on the PI’s work PC in 64-bit encrypted software that was difficult for unauthorized individuals to access.

Study design and sample size

The study was an analytical cross-sectional study. Sample size was calculated using Raosoft® software. The required sample size was estimated at the 95% confidence level with a response distribution of 50% and a margin of error of ±5%. The required sample size was determined to be 377. The non-probability consecutive sampling technique was used for this study.

Data collection

The data collection sheet was composed of the following: age, gender, weight, height, BMI, blood group, educational status, marital status, any inherited syndromes, chronic disease, past history of cancers, family history, family history of cancers, grade, stage, tumor location, laterality, date of admission, date of diagnosis, type of surgical intervention, lymph node yield, metastasis, recurrence rate and mortality, date of detection of infection, method of detecting infection, type of infection, name of the bacteria, name of the virus, name of the fungi, name of the parasite, whether the infection was before or post cancer treatment, local or systemic infection, treatment of infection, treatment outcome, date of resolution of the infection, and side effects of infection treatment.

Data management and statistical analysis

The data collected were stored on a work computer accessed only by the author, and they were collected only by authorized members. The data were analyzed using JMP 17. Quantitative data were presented by the mean and standard deviation, while qualitative data were presented by frequency and percentage. For comparing quantitative data, t-test was used. On the other hand, the Chi-squared test was used to compare the qualitative data. P value ≤0.05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 377 CRC patients were studied. Among them, males were predominant, 222, and the mean age was found to be 62.1 (SD13.4) years. The body mass index (BMI) of the respective patients was normal, 131 (34.7%), followed by overweight, 112 (29.7%) [Table 1]. The most common blood group was O+ 135 (35.8%) [Figure 1]. The comorbidities associated with these patients were diabetes mellitus (DM), 153 (40.6%), hypertension, 151 (40.1%), and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), 51 (13.5%) [Tables 2 and 3].

Clinico-pathological characteristics of CRC patients

There was a significant association (P = 0.0007) between blood group distribution and infection presence. The most common blood groups among infected people were O+ (35.3%) and A+ (27.2%), followed by B+ (14.5%). The noninfected group had a similar percentage of O+ (36.6%) and A+ (23.9%) but a lower proportion of B+ (7.7%). Infected individuals had a higher prevalence of blood categories AB + and O− (3.4% and 4.7%, respectively) compared to noninfected persons (0.7% and 0.7%).

Out of all patients, 20 (5.3%) had a prior history of cancer. Additionally, 36 people (9.6%) have a positive family history of cancer, with colon cancer, 18 (4.8%), being the most prevalent form, followed by other cancers, 14 (3.7%), breast cancer, 3 (0.8%), and lung cancer, 1 (0.3%). Also, grade 2 stage (staging as per AJCC) was found to be the most common cancer grade, 273 (72.4%) [Table 2].

A significant variation in cancer stage distribution was found between patients with and without infection (P = 0.0454). Individuals with infection had a larger proportion of severe disease, with 63.8% classed as Stage 4 compared to 47.2% in the noninfected group, while earlier stages were less represented. Tumor site did not differ substantially between groups (P = 0.9336), and the colon, rectal, and colorectal distributions were all comparable. In contrast, metastasis was much more likely among infected individuals (65.5%) than among those without infection (50.7%), indicating a statistically significant association (P = 0.0022). 64.9% of the patients had undergone surgical intervention. The most common surgical intervention was low anterior resection, 74 (19.6%), and only 8% had experienced recurrence. The mortality rate among our patients in the study was 38.6% [Table 4]. The most common pathogen to infect CRC patients was found to be Escherichia coli, N = 222 (32.2%) [Table 5].

Discussion

Limited data were found on the prevalence of nosocomial infection among CRC patients, which makes comparison difficult as most studies are concerned with surgical site infections in CRC patients. Our study shows 235 (62.3%) CRC patients developed nosocomial infections, which is relatively high compared to other studies associated with SSI in CRC. Examples of these studies are Japan (32.1%), Saudi Arabia (27.2%), Greece (21.8%), Spain (12.3%), and China (3.7%).[21] In contrast to our case, they found a significant increase in risk of infection related to diabetes and hypertension as comorbidities. One reason might be that their study was only concerned with wound infections which could have delayed healing caused by diabetes and hypertension. In addition, the most three common pathogens were found to be Escherichia coli (32.2%), Klebsiella pneumonia (12.5%), and Candida species (8.8%). This was consistent with the literature with E. coli followed by K. Pneumonia as the reasons; however, candida species were less common.[212223]

Our findings did not show any significant association of BMI with higher infection rates, which is quite contradictory to other studies. This could be explained by the development of proinflammatory cytokines, impairment in immune function, decreased perfusion to adipose rich tissue, delayed wound healing, and extended operation time related to obesity.[24] Also, there was no correlation of other comorbidities like DM and HTN and cardiovascular disease was with more infections, which is in accordance with other research.[25] Although CVD is not a potent independent risk factor of infection, it may reduce tissue repair and immune response by promoting chronic inflammation, reducing microvascular perfusion, and causing endothelial dysfunction.[25]

Furthermore, the higher infection risk was seen in patients with advanced CRC and metastasis. This can be attributed to various pathophysiological and procedural factors. A recent multicenter study by Aktaş et al. found that patients with advanced stage were over twice as likely to develop surgical site infections. Greater surgical complexity, extensive tissue dissection, prolonged operative time, and higher intraoperative blood loss associated with advanced stage may explain this correlation.[2627] Moreover, patients with advanced-stage CRC are usually more prone to systemic inflammation, malnutrition, and reduced immunity, collectively compromising wound healing and host defense.

Zhang et al. (2024)[28] also reported that patients with advanced-stage CRC often show signs of vulnerability and poor nutrition before the surgery, making them more vulnerable to postoperative infections. These findings increase the reliability of our results, showing that advanced tumor stage raises infection susceptibility through both surgical and systemic mechanisms. In contrast, Páramo-Zunzunegui J et al.[25] did not find such an association which could be attributed to the difference in the sample size. CRC metastasis showed a significant association with the development of infections. This could be explained by the weakened immune system and intestinal barrier, allowing pathogens and their toxins to leak into bloodstream causing more infection.[29] Also, more aggressive chemotherapy or surgical interventions given to metastatic CRC patients increases their risk of infection.

In addition, our results showed a significant relationship between blood groups and the presence of infections in CRC patients (P = 0.0007). Such association might be explained by biological mechanisms linking ABO antigens to immune modulation and microbial adhesion. ABO antigens are proteins found not only on red blood cells but also on surfaces of tissues like epithelial and endothelial surfaces, where they act as binding sites for various pathogens, influencing susceptibility to infection. Additionally, changes in these antigens can affect gut microbiota and immune response, which could impact tumor-related or postoperative infection rates in CRC patients.[3031] Unfortunately, studies of similar variables and objectives did not highlight if there is any relationship; however, this could open a field for other researchers to investigate.

Study limitations

This study may have some drawbacks. Because this retrospective analysis was conducted at a single oncology center in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, the data may not be representative of the larger community. Due to its retroactive methodology, the data contained considerable irregularities in several parameters, particularly missing data on critical variables, which could cause interpretation bias.

Conclusion

Two-thirds of CRC patients developed nosocomial infections. Gram-negative bacteria like Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumonia were the most involved. In addition, blood group, metastasis, and late-stage cancer were identified as significant risk factors for such infections.
