Anti-adhesive agents in tendon repair: mechanisms, preclinical evidence, clinical challenges, and future perspectives—a narrative review
Authors: Mona Kadkhodaei, Arian Karimi Rouzbahani, Bahar Amiri, Tahereh Fereydoonnezhad, Arya Behzadi, Abdolrazagh Marzban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTendon injuries constitute a significant challenge to musculoskeletal health, comprising approximately 50% of all related injuries in the United States and resulting in considerable treatment expenditures. Conventional conservative and surgical interventions frequently do not succeed in mitigating peritendinous adhesions, resulting in compromised functionality, pain, and an increased risk of re-rupture. This analysis investigates the underlying mechanisms of adhesion formation in the context of tendon healing, highlighting the disparity between intrinsic and extrinsic factors throughout the phases of inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. The restoration of tendon gliding and mobility underscores the significance of adhesion prevention. This review concentrates on anti-adhesive compounds, encompassing physical barriers such as collagen and chitosan, as well as pharmacological agents including 5-fluorouracil and platelet-rich plasma. The analysis covers their mechanisms of action and preclinical efficacy demonstrated in vitro and in animal models, along with their diverse bioactivities, which include anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects. The principal findings indicate significant reductions in adhesions; however, they also highlight challenges such as inconsistent degradation rates, potential toxicity, and a paucity of clinical evidence. Future directions encompass the implementation of randomized controlled trials, the establishment of standardized protocols, and the development of multifunctional biomaterials aimed at addressing research gaps and improving patient outcomes in the field of orthopedic surgery.Keywords: Tendon repair, Adhesion prevention, Biomaterials, Physical barriers, Pharmacological agents, Hyaluronic acid, Animal models, Clinical challenges

Background

Tendons, which join muscles to bones, are essential components of the musculoskeletal system and enable muscle forces to be transferred to bone [1]. Despite being extremely resistant to mechanical loads and made of densely packed collagen, tendons are prone to injury [2]. About half of musculoskeletal injuries reported in the United States annually involve soft tissue injuries, such as ligament and tendon damage [3]. As a result, the healthcare systems must pay a high treatment cost due to this prevalence. Annually, the United States government pays up to $7 billion for treatments related to tendon injuries causing shoulder pain [4]. Tendon injuries can result from trauma, wear and tear from repeated use, or aging naturally [5]. The risk of tendon injuries increases with repetitive motions at work, in sports, or in daily activities [6]. Tendon injuries can manifest in a variety of ways, depending on the location and severity of the damage. Acute tendon injuries are characterized by a sudden tear, which can be partial or complete [7, 8]. This type of injury often occurs by direct trauma or an excessive amount of force applied to the tendon [9, 10]. In contrast, chronic tendon injuries, like tendinopathy, occur gradually as a result of overuse or repetitive stress on the tendon [11]. Both acute and chronic tendinopathy cause significant morbidity and necessitate expensive and time-consuming treatment [12].

The clinical manifestations and risks of adhesions differ according to tendon type. Adhesions inside the synovial sheath make it very challenging for the flexor tendons in the hand to glide, which limits the range of motion (ROM) of the fingers and makes them less useful [13].

Healing entails angiogenesis and the migration of epitenon fibroblasts, while intrasynovial sheath cells penetrate the repair site, facilitating adhesion formation and hindering tendon excursion, particularly during immobilization. In Achilles tendon ruptures, adhesions lead to stiffness and gait abnormalities, although the risk of rerupture is elevated due to mechanical stresses [14].

Nonoperative treatment and arthroplasty alternatives exhibit significant complication rates, including failure (34%) and reoperation (11%), while biological failures, such as avascular necrosis, intensify stiffness. When the rotator cuff is repaired in the shoulder, it can cause contracture and adhesions, which can lead to long-term pain and less movement in the shoulder. Substance P levels are linked to stiffness, where plasma SP is significantly higher (81.06 vs. 23.49 pg/ml) in stiff shoulders, indicating that neuronal upregulation is responsible for symptoms and the development of adhesive capsulitis. These differences highlight the necessity for tendon-specific strategies in adhesion prevention, as the tendon unit, a morpho-functional entity affected by mechanical, hormonal, and pathological stimuli, undergoes healing through collagen synthesis, underscoring the restoration of homeostatic balance as a therapeutic objective [15, 16].

Currently, there are two main approaches for managing tendon injuries: conservative therapy and surgery. Both face several challenges [17, 18]. Usually, non-invasive, conservative therapies are used at the start of treatment to promote healing and minimize symptoms. Conservative therapy includes rest, bracing, corticosteroid injection, cryotherapy, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), eccentric exercises, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections, extracorporeal shock wave therapy, and therapeutic ultrasound [19, 20]. Although conservative therapies can occasionally be beneficial for specific tendon injuries, they may present certain drawbacks. A single dose of corticosteroid is effective in reducing pain, but continuous use can cause tendon rupture, atrophy, and skin depigmentation [21]. NSAIDs can be used for acute pain relief, but long-term use can lead to gastrointestinal bleeding and is not recommended [22, 23]. Eccentric exercises showed promising results, but due to the time and motivation needed, they don’t work in all patients [24]. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) made from the patient’s blood contains various growth factors that may promote healing. Although animal research and basic science have demonstrated the effectiveness of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) in treating orthopedic conditions, definitive high-level clinical evidence is currently lacking [20, 25]. Furthermore, PRP treatment is costly and not covered by insurance [25]. Extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) is a safe, non-invasive treatment option [26]. Few complications have been reported for ESWT, but they can include tendon injury risk and pain [27]. In general, a conservative approach to treating tendinopathy may not be effective for 10% to 45% of patients, depending on the anatomic location [28]. Surgical intervention is needed when conservative therapy fails or in complete tendon rupture [25]. There are side effects associated with surgery as well. Peritendinous adhesions, scar formation, and surgical site inflammation are main drawbacks [18]. Furthermore, complete tendon function is rarely restored by current surgical procedures [10].

Many techniques to lessen and minimize adhesion formation have been developed over the past few decades [29]. One promising approach to improve tendon healing and repair is the use of anti-adhesive compounds [30]. These compounds are intended to prevent the formation of adhesions, which are abnormal connections between tendons and surrounding tissues that can impair normal tendon function and healing. Anti-adhesive compounds work by minimizing adhesions, which can improve tendon gliding, relieve pain, and promote more efficient rehabilitation. These approaches can be divided into two groups: physical barriers and biological treatment [29]. Application of anti-adhesive compounds in tendon injuries is a relatively new field of research, with ongoing studies exploring their effectiveness and safety. Anti-adhesive compounds have the ability to change the treatment of tendon injuries and enhance patient outcomes by preventing the formation of excessive scar tissue and creating a favorable healing environment.

This review will discuss the adhesion formation in the tendon healing process and the importance of preventing adhesions after surgery. In particular, we will focus on recent outcomes of studies on anti-adhesive compounds with potential multiple bioactivities, such as antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and proliferating activities, by analyzing their mechanism of action, cons and pros, and their efficacy.

Methods

This narrative review was conducted to synthesize existing literature on anti-adhesive agents in tendon repair. A comprehensive search was performed across five major databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and Google Scholar) from inception to September 2025, using keywords such as “tendon repair,” “adhesion prevention,” “biomaterials,” “physical barriers,” “pharmacological agents,” and “anti-adhesive compounds,” combined with Boolean operators. Initially, 1200 articles were identified after removing duplicates. Titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers, resulting in 450 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. Inclusion criteria focused on studies addressing mechanisms, preclinical evidence, or clinical applications in tendon adhesion prevention; exclusions included non-English articles, case reports, and unrelated topics. Ultimately, 178 studies were included, with data extraction emphasizing mechanisms, efficacy, limitations, and future directions. Narrative synthesis was employed to integrate findings, prioritizing critical analysis and comparative insights. No formal meta-analysis was performed due to the heterogeneous nature of the studies. Ethical approval was not required for this review.

Adhesion formation in tendon repair

Tendon healing is a long, complex process that involves three overlapping phases: inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [8]. During the inflammation phase, which lasts about a week, inflammatory cells move to the healing site and release various growth factors and cytokines that encourage the recruitment and proliferation of macrophages and fibroblasts. The synthesis of type-III collagen also begins in the first phase [13]. The proliferation phase encompasses several key elements, such as fibroblast proliferation, excessive extracellular matrix [31] synthesis, activation of tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs), and tendon differentiation [32]. In this stage, Type-I collagen begins to predominate [33]. A few weeks later, the remodeling phase, which can last from 6 weeks to 12 months, begins. Reduced collagen and glycosaminoglycan synthesis and decreased cellularity are the hallmarks of this phase [34]. During the remodeling phase, recently generated collagen fibers align longitudinally along the tendon’s axis [35].

Long-term exposure to inflammatory mediators like prostaglandin E1 can cause fibrosis and adhesions. This was demonstrated in rat Achilles models, where injections of PGE1 around the tendons led to acute inflammation characterized by increased water retention and weight. By day 35, the tendons began to break down, resulting in a model for Achilles tendinopathy that exhibited adhesions in half of the tendons by day 21 [36].

After tendon repair, stiffness happens because of both peritendinous adhesions and intrinsic scar formation within the tendon. This makes the tissue less flexible. Intrinsic scarring entails modifications in extracellular matrix composition, characterized by elevated type III collagen and a disorganized microstructure, resulting in diminished mechanical strength and heightened rigidity [37].

Tendon healing culminates in a fibrotic scar without complete regeneration, where extrinsic cells exacerbate scarring and adhesions, while intrinsic cells from tendon parenchyma mitigate fibrosis; their equilibrium is crucial [38].

This underscores that accumulated damage in the tendon core precedes vascular recruitment, with disorganized ECM compromising integrity and increasing the risk of re-injury. This internal factor makes external adhesions worse, which makes functional impairment worse [39].

For example, peritendinous adhesions after digital flexor tendon repair cause stiffness and functional impairment, which shows how important it is to identify ways to reduce these adhesions.

Tendon healing involves both extrinsic and intrinsic factors [40]. Intrinsic tendon healing includes activating bioactive factors in synovial fluid and encouraging the proliferation and migration of tenocytes [41]. Extrinsic tendon healing occurs when exogenous fibroblasts proliferate in the granulation tissue that grows into the damaged tendon, leading to scarring and fibrous adhesion [32]. Imbalance between these two mechanisms leads to peritendinous adhesions [42]. The extrinsic mechanism tends to be active earlier in the healing process, while the intrinsic mechanism often takes longer to be activated. Furthermore, the tissue surrounding the tendon, called the synovial sheath, reacts more strongly in terms of growth and inflammation compared to the inner parts of the tendon [43]. When the extrinsic healing mechanism dominates, it can result in more collagen at the injury site and reduced collagen organization and strength in the repaired tendon tissue. As a result, this dominance can lead to the formation of scars and adhesions between the tendon and nearby structures [35].

Researchers have indicated that following tendon repair, 7–15% of patients experience problems like scarring, an increase in tendon adhesions, and restricted joint movement during the early healing phase [30]. The mechanical properties of the healed tendon do not typically return to those of the uninjured tissue. The repaired tendon has less incorporation of collagen fibers, featuring a higher proportion of collagen type III (which has a smaller diameter) compared to collagen type I, and exhibits a shorter crimp pattern than the normal tendon, resulting in a tissue that is mechanically weaker. Consequently, the tendon thickens and becomes more rigid to compensate for the decreased mechanical strength, and this reduces the quality and functionality of the tendon [44, 45]. Furthermore, these changes in the healed tendon can cause decreased joint stability, degenerative changes, impaired functionality, and a higher risk of re-rupture [46].

Comparative Analysis: Comparative examination of adhesion formation studies reveals nuanced differences in phase-specific contributions. For example, inflammation-phase models like PGE1 injections in rats [36] emphasize extrinsic mediator-driven fibrosis, contrasting with intrinsic scarring in remodeling phases [37–39], where type III collagen dominance weakens mechanics [44, 45]. Intrinsic mechanisms [41] promote organized healing but are outpaced by extrinsic ones [32, 42, 43], leading to adhesions in 7–15% of cases [30]. Overall, studies like Thomopoulos et al. [13] and Voleti et al. [8] highlight that while extrinsic dominance exacerbates re-rupture risk [46], balancing these via targeted interventions could mitigate functional impairments more effectively than phase-isolated approaches. Building on these mechanisms, the next section addresses a core question: Why is preventing adhesions critical for restoring tendon gliding and functionality?

Importance of adhesion prevention during tendon healing

Tendon injuries may develop from both severe and minimal hand trauma [40]. Clinicians continue to confront hurdles in managing tendon injuries. Given that our knowledge of tendon biology is still lagging behind that of the rest of the musculoskeletal system parts, there are currently no effective therapies for tendon injuries [47]. Current knowledge indicates that there are two parallel mechanisms via which tendon repair takes place. Tenocyte proliferation and extracellular matrix formation are two processes involved in intrinsic healing [48]. The adhesion may develop throughout the tendon healing process. Postsurgical adhesions between the injured tendon and the adjoining tissues have been reported after up to 30–40% of tendon injuries. These adhesions emerge when the tendon is punctured, lacerated, or compressed, which disrupts the gliding process [48]. In 1963, Potenza theorized that adhesion development was a necessity for blood vessel ingrowth into the tendon. This theory provided credence to the theory of extrinsic tendon repair [49]. Cells within the surrounding sheath and synovium encircle the tendon structure and invade the tendon during extrinsic repair, aiding in adhesion development. Tendons lack self-renewal ability thanks to their limited cell density and low growth factor activity. When there is damage to the tendon or tendon sheath, extrinsic repair takes precedence [48].

Adhesions between the injured tendon and the adjacent synovial sheath during healing might exacerbate the clinical condition of tendon injuries. Adhesions are a known source of reduced tendon mobility, although they are difficult to anticipate after surgical correction [50]. All tendon injuries, irrespective of their etiology, recover less than ideally as a result of the creation of disorganized scar tissue. Such constrictive adhesions inside the synovial sheaths of the tendons cause decreased function and a higher chance of re-rupture, which severely impairs the results of tendon healing [51].

The primary intraoperative variables influencing adhesion development are the source of damage, the extent of manipulation, and the existence of a tendon gap. The kind of rehabilitation program and the duration of immobility are other variables [48]. Despite advancements in surgical procedures and postoperative mobilization, tendon adhesion remains the primary complication following tendon damage repair, resulting in gliding dysfunction, discomfort, and potentially the need for reoperation [52]. This results in a reduced digital range of motion (ROM) for the patient [48].

It is important to bear in mind that, after tendon repairs, any disruption of tendinous motion or ligamentous control might change skeletal alignment and cause issues that restrict function. Regaining the range of motion and strength necessary for normal hand use is the aim of flexor tendon surgery and postoperative therapy. To appropriately adjust conventional methods and avoid irreversible modifications, specific attention should be given to the varied early presentations of adhesions in tendons based on mechanical and physiological parameters. Despite the multitude of rehabilitation treatments and guidelines designed to enhance the gliding amplitude of healed tendons, secondary problems, such as reduced range of motion and stiffness associated with tendon adhesions, often appear. If these early issues are not addressed, they may lead to further complications [53].

Researchers have investigated a wide range of cutting-edge therapeutic approaches, both surgical and non-surgical, to suppress exogenous healing, encourage endogenous healing, lessen inflammation, and ultimately fully restore tendon sliding function. These approaches are based on research regarding the mechanism of tendon adhesion [54]. Adhesion prevention measures can involve biological or mechanical factors. Biologically oriented techniques encompass the use of pharmacological and molecular manipulation of scar formation, whilst targeted mechanical aspects include early postoperative mobilization and atraumatic management of tendons [48]. The earliest measures to prevent tendon adhesion formation can be traced to the 1980s and consisted of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as ibuprofen. When injected into the site of injury, these drugs mitigate the expression of pro-inflammatory factors, notably arachidonic acid, which is believed to lessen post-traumatic endogenous local injury. On the other hand, overuse of NSAIDs may have negative consequences on the genitourinary or cardiovascular systems. Various biologicals, including growth factors and genes, are being investigated to affect cell destiny and functions at the delivery site by modifying signal transduction to solve these problems [55].

Comparative Analysis: Prevention strategies vary in efficacy across studies; for instance, early mobilization and atraumatic techniques [48, 53] reduce adhesions by 30–40% in extrinsic-dominant cases [48, 49], outperforming NSAIDs like ibuprofen [55], which mitigate inflammation but risk systemic side effects. Biological approaches [54] synergize with mechanical ones to suppress exogenous healing, as in Potenza’s extrinsic model [49], yet limitations like unpredictability [50] and re-rupture risk [51, 52] persist compared to advanced molecular manipulations, highlighting the need for integrated paradigms to optimize ROM restoration. Transitioning from prevention’s importance, the subsequent section delves into how anti-adhesive materials modulate these pathways mechanistically, addressing the question: What are the key signaling cascades and their synergies in reducing adhesions?

Mechanistic studies of the anti-adhesive function of materials

Anti-adhesive agents work by changing how inflammation, fibrosis, and tissue remodeling happen. Inflammation is a key factor in starting and controlling fibrosis. Pro-inflammatory cytokines cause fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition. Complications from joint and tendon injections can include infections like septic arthritis and tendon ruptures. This shows how important it is to use aseptic techniques to stop inflammation-driven fibrosis [56].

Pathway of TGF-β and SMAD

The TGF-β/SMAD pathway is crucial for adhesion formation; TGF-β activates SMAD2/3, which increases collagen production and fibrosis [57]. SMAD7 agonists stop SMAD3 phosphorylation, which lowers fibrotic genes like col1a1 and α-SMA and improves adhesion in rat models. Dysregulation results in excessive scar tissue; however, inhibitors can alleviate this condition [58]. TGF-β inhibits immune cells but may worsen inflammation in certain diseases, with activation by ROS, TSP-1, and proteases promoting fibrosis.

M1/M2 macrophage polarization

Immune cells, especially macrophages, play a crucial role in the formation of adhesions through their polarization. The M1 phenotype, which is pro-inflammatory, tends to worsen fibrosis, whereas the M2 phenotype, known for its anti-inflammatory properties, aids in the resolution of this process [59]. The M2 macrophages contribute to an increase in scar tissue but also help maintain a balance in the extracellular matrix (ECM). Furthermore, the use of scaffolds for modulation can lead to a reduction in adhesions. Transitioning to M2 decreases adhesions in experimental models [60], since M2 encourages tenogenic differentiation. However, extended activity can result in extrinsic healing and adhesions, highlighting the importance of the M1/M2 ratio in the repair process.

Reactive oxygen species and oxidative stress

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress play a significant role in the formation of adhesions by enhancing inflammation and promoting fibroblast proliferation [61]. The elevated levels of ROS in tendinopathy lead to damage of lipids, proteins, and DNA, which in turn hinders the regeneration of tendon-derived stem cells (TDSCs). Antioxidants have the potential to mitigate this effect [61], as CUR/CEL-loaded EPM facilitates drug release in reaction to ROS, thereby synergistically decreasing adhesion and inflammation.

Comparative Analysis: Mechanistic studies demonstrate synergies, such as TGF-β/SMAD inhibitors [57, 58] reducing fibrosis more effectively than macrophage modulation alone [59, 60], though M1/M2 shifts offer broader anti-inflammatory benefits in models [60] compared to ROS-targeted antioxidants [61], which excel in oxidative stress but lack pathway specificity. Limitations include paradigm over-reliance on rat models, potentially overlooking human variability; integrating these (e.g., SMAD with ROS inhibition) could enhance efficacy beyond isolated approaches.

Physical barriers

Fibrin glue is a surgical sealant consisting of a ready-to-be-activated fibrinogen complex and thrombin [62]. After being applied to the surgical site, it follows the last step of the natural coagulating process, and fibrinogen particles turn into fibrin polymers mediated by thrombin [63]. This process takes place in the presence of calcium and leaves a stable, adhesive, and hemostatic sealant [64]. The final scaffold of fibrin strands is not only biocompatible and degradable but also is an ideal mediator to deliver intended drugs, stem cells [65], exosomes, and growth factors to the surrounding area, which makes it a favorable substance to utilize in tendon repair [62]. Besides, fibrin glue is found to be quick to use, and with its soft texture, it has the least invasion of the delicate injured area [66].

Still, there are shortcomings to this material, such as degrading in about two weeks, which is relatively short for the tendon repair process [65]. One study also demonstrated that if used alone without sutures, fibrin glue does not add more resilience to the repaired flexor tendon, unlike some other sealants [67]. Another concern is the risk of viral transmission, which some studies aimed to overcome with heterologous fibrin sealant from other natural resources [64, 68]. Recently Reichsollner et al. suggested that the chemical properties of fibrin glue are a less investigated area, which can affect its hemostatic and healing effects [69]. For example, a hyperosmolar fibrin glue can trigger cell death signals and thus lead to less desirable healing results. These lesser-known properties may explain some controversial findings indicating more gliding resistance in tendons after fibrin glue application [70].

One of the most studied natural materials for tendon repair augmentation is collagen. Collagen is a polymer protein that makes up around 30% of human dry body mass, and with 29 different subtypes, it is present in all forms of connective tissues, tendons, and ligaments [71]. With a gigantic four-level molecular structure, collagen supports tissues mechanically and also affects different intercellular processes such as cell adhesion and migration [72]. Being a common biomaterial between humans and animals, collagen has been extracted from numerous resources [73]. In this regard, marine creatures are probably superior to other animals since they carry less risk of zoonosis infection transmission [74]. Like many other natural sealants, not only its biocompatibility and less antigenicity but also the porous structure, which lets the passage of growth factors and cytokines, are winning properties [75]. Still, the first clinical trial of using a collagen membrane around ruptured flexor tendons did not reveal any significant difference between the two groups [76]. However, the small population and the inclusion of only clear-cut tendon injuries are the probable explanations for the equivocal results.

Previous in vivo studies yielded better results, especially by combining collagen with glycosaminoglycan [75, 77]. Integra, which is a combination of bovine collagen and glycosaminoglycan extracted from shark cartilage, was found to cause less inflammatory response (which is favorable for attenuation of adhesion) compared with collagen alone [77], but the downside was its large size, which made it difficult to suture [75]. Also, animal-originated glycosaminoglycan has very diverse structures; thus, with more uncertainty in the human environment, it is harder to obtain to clinical trials [78].

Synthetic collagen was a focus of many studies since it has more safety regarding disease transmission. Scaffolds similar to collagen type I are more anti-adhesive since they result in lesser fibroblast activation, while scaffolds similar to collagen type III have the opposite effect with more probability of inducing adhesion [79]. Nevertheless, the shortcomings of synthetic collagen are its higher cost than the natural form and its rapid degradation in biological spaces in the span of a few days [73]. Ahmad et al. tried to overcome this problem by creating more cross-linking in collagen fibers with ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide and N-hydroxysuccinimide, but these cross-linking agents harmed tendon healing themselves [80]. Also, too much cross-linking could delay the degradation time more than necessary and lead to tendon stiffness.

Another option for preventing tendon injury adhesion is hydrogel. Hydrogel is a general term for natural or synthetic polymers that are constituted of hydrophilic structures [81]. These structures absorb considerable amounts of water and swell to play their therapeutic role. Natural hydrogels are superior to synthetic counterparts in terms of biocompatibility and their ability to mimic cell-matrix interaction signals, which facilitate differentiation and cell migration [82]. Being an important part of the extracellular matrix [31], hyaluronic acid (HA) hydrogel can be a promising anti-adhesive material [83]. Since the HA hydrogel is challenging to inject and is not useful in small or irregular injury sites, Xu et al. studied a granular type of HA hydrogel that was injectable and easy to use [84]. Although investigated in many studies, recently a systemic review showed that HA hydrogel alone probably has no significant effect on developing adhesion after tendon injuries except for when it is injected repeatedly during the repair period [85]. Similar to collagen, the short half-life of HA was conquered with an auto-crosslinked form of it called Hyaloglide [86]. This hyaluronan derivative is more viscous-elastic and remains in the injury site for longer. (About 10 days)

Many studies that showed favorable results for using HA hydrogel usually use it in combination with other materials. For example, combined with platelet-rich plasma (PRP), HA can enhance the PRP healing results by decreasing fibroblast attachment and lubricating the gliding movement of the injured tendon [87, 88]. Another study used HA as the outer layer of a three-layered electrospun tube for flexor tendon injuries, which demonstrated satisfactory results [89]. Both PRP and the electrospun tube, which contained naproxen, seem to yield significant outcomes by adding an anti-inflammatory effect to using HA hydrogel.

Elastin is another ECM component, mostly accumulated in soft tissues with elasticity properties. These tissues include ligaments, tendons, and vessel walls [90]. As the amount of elastin in a tendon increases, its ability to recoil and its elastic properties also improve [91]. Also, the higher concentration of elastin found in a healing tendon probably provides some elasticity, which protects it from retear in the early stages of the repair process [92].

Alginate is a natural polysaccharide polymer extracted from algae and composed of repeated blocks of mannuronate and guluronate residues. This anionic structure turns into an applicable gel material by adding divalent cations and is compatible with human biology, like other natural anti-adhesives [93]. Aside from benefits similar to other natural products, such as easy processing and low antigenicity, alginate possesses a close similarity to the ECM of human soft tissues while taking much longer to hydrolyze in the human body due to lack of proper enzymes [94]. Still, batch-to-batch variation, not enough resilience, and an uncontrolled degradation rate are remarkable disadvantages [95].

Similar to many other natural products, researchers tried to use alginate in combination with other adhesives to achieve better results. A semi-synthetic adhesive made of synthetic polymer and alginate used polyvinyl alcohol as the strengthening part to make up for alginate weakness [96]. Therefore, they could use the progressive degradation of alginate to have a constant and slow infiltration of tenocytes into vacant spaces. Manoukian et al. also discovered that in a combination of alginate with synthetic nanofibers, a low molecular weight form has a more uniform coating than nanofibers, so with less adhesion and unwanted propagation, the final tendon has more tensile strength [97]. Another group studied the efficacy of a tendon reconstruction made of both synthetic and natural polymers (polyester and collagen) braided into each other and coated with a layer of alginate, which successfully prevented adhesion during the healing period [98]. The lack of human studies and clinical trials is evident in the current literature. Only one study used sheets of alginate combined with HA in a patient with a flexor pollicis longus tendon injury, which resulted in no adhesion formation upon the second inspection of the area [99].

Chitosan is another natural polysaccharide that originates from the deacetylation of a component of crustaceans’ exoskeleton called chitin [100]. Unlike alginate, chitosan with repeated sequences of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine and D-glucosamine is a cationic polymer, which makes it more similar to natural ECM with glycosaminoglycan. Again, this bioadhesive has satisfactory biological compatibility with the human body, but the structure has relatively weak adhesion. Partial degradation also happens in 10 days, which is not long enough [101]. Adhesion strength and durability can be augmented with more crosslinking via crosslinking agents but at the cost of more inflammatory response to these agents [102]. Fang et al. overcame this obstacle by filtering the oxidative agent responsible for chitosan cross-linking before its usage. This resulted in more durable and more adhesive material.

Chitosan is unique among natural adhesives in possessing not only angiogenic [103] but also antibacterial and fungistatic properties [104]. These characteristics, combined with easy processing and maintenance, make chitosan highly favored for tendon injuries.

Synthetic adhesives were introduced to address the shortcomings of natural products, such as batch-to-batch variation and short degradation time. An electrospun synthetic polymer tube called DegraPol could efficiently maintain a physical barrier around the flexor tendon for up to 12 weeks, although some inflammatory response was detected with the start of degradation due to resulting particles [105]. Not being perfectly biocompatible and having inflammatory responses to synthetic products were addressed in a study with a combination of a polyester membrane with anti-inflammatory agents (curcumin/celecoxib) as a reservoir, which yielded promising results [106]. Synthetic anti-adhesives are also easier to process into a porous scaffold [101] and have mechanical advantages such as better water absorption and higher gel strength [81]. Their chemical and biological properties can also be tailored in a way that helps in cell differentiation and cell immigration cues [107]. Drugs and growth factors carried by these mediums are delivered in a more sustained and longer duration due to their predictable degradation and longer half-lives [108]. Adding bioactive agents such as basic fibroblast growth factor and platelet-derived growth factor to synthetic tubes has been shown to reduce inflammatory response and balance collagen synthesis and ultimately lead to less adhesion formation [108, 109].

Comparative Analysis: Among physical barriers, natural ones like collagen and HA [71–86] offer superior biocompatibility but suffer rapid degradation [73, 85], contrasting with synthetics like DegraPol [105] that provide longer barriers (12 weeks) yet induce inflammation [106]. Synergies shine in combinations, e.g., HA-PRP [87, 88] reducing adhesions more than HA alone via anti-inflammatory effects, while alginate-synthetics [96–98] enhance tensile strength over standalone alginate [95]. Limitations in paradigms include batch variability [95] and limited clinical trials [99], suggesting hybrid approaches could outperform single-material strategies for sustained efficacy. Having examined physical barriers, the following section explores pharmacological agents, probing synergies between barriers and agents for optimized anti-adhesion.

Pharmacological agents

Corticosteroids, encompassing injected and oral forms, as well as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), play a pivotal role in the modulation of inflammatory processes to mitigate the formation of adhesions. Injected steroids have been shown to diminish adhesions through the inhibition of fibrosis; however, they also pose a risk of tendon weakening and potential rupture [110]. Oral corticosteroids, such as prednisone, are known to provide systemic relief; however, they also elevate the risk of atrophy. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are utilized for short-term pain relief, yet they may impede the healing process and are associated with gastrointestinal complications [56]. Infections and ruptures are common complications, and although a direct comparison between corticosteroids and NSAIDs is not available, the importance of aseptic techniques is notably emphasized.

Losartan, classified as an angiotensin II receptor blocker, demonstrates anti-fibrotic properties through the inhibition of TGF-β signaling. This action leads to a reduction in adhesions observed in tendon models, effectively countering TGF-β in the context of postoperative fibrosis associated with muscle and cartilage repair. Despite its antifibrotic characteristics, there is no significant reduction in MUA rates following total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [111, 112].

Additional anti-fibrotic agents encompass metformin, which inhibits TGF-β through AMPK activation, thereby reducing adhesions, blocking SMAD2/3 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation, and promoting apoptosis [113]. Quercetin is noted for its ability to suppress oxidative stress and fibrosis, as it increases SOD/GPx levels, decreases MDA, and reduces adhesion both macroscopically and histologically [114]. Collagenase-induced Achilles tendinopathy results in tendon injury that activates oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. This process leads to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which adversely affect cellular structures, contribute to the degradation of the collagen matrix, and facilitate fibrosis and adhesions through the upregulation of type III collagen and the induction of apoptosis. Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), when administered in a hyaluronic acid hydrogel, functions by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) through its polyphenolic composition. This mechanism enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), thereby neutralizing free radicals and mitigating oxidative damage. This facilitates tenogenic repair by enhancing the expression of COLL-1 and SCX, which are crucial for the synthesis and remodeling of a mature extracellular matrix, while concurrently reducing COLL-3 levels to mitigate scarring. Simultaneously, EGCG demonstrates an inhibitory effect on apoptosis through the suppression of caspase-3 and caspase-7, thereby maintaining the viability of tenocytes. The aforementioned actions collectively contribute to the alleviation of inflammation, restoration of collagen alignment, prevention of adhesions, and promotion of tendon regeneration, as evidenced by findings in rat models [115].

Colchicine, an anti-inflammatory alkaloid, functions by binding to tubulin and disrupting microtubule polymerization. This mechanism inhibits neutrophil chemotaxis, inflammasome activation, and the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α. Additionally, it suppresses fibroblast proliferation and excessive collagen synthesis, which serves to minimize fibrotic scarring. This modulation maintains the inherent healing capabilities of tenocytes within tendons, minimizes the formation of adhesions without diminishing the strength of the repair, and improves tendon excursion. This is supported by the enhanced range of motion and lower adhesion scores observed in animal models, indicating its potential to prevent postoperative adhesions in clinical tendon repair scenarios [116].

The phenomenon of peritendinous fibrosis subsequent to tendon injury is characterized by an overabundance of extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, activation of myofibroblasts, and increased cellular proliferation. These processes are attributed to dysregulated autophagy and heightened TGF-β signaling, ultimately resulting in adhesions that hinder tendon gliding functionality. Rapamycin, an inhibitor of mTOR, counteracts this mechanism by activating autophagy through the suppression of mTORC1. This process promotes the formation of autophagosomes, as evidenced by increased levels of LC3B and decreased levels of p62, facilitating the degradation of unnecessary cellular components. Consequently, this leads to a reduction in TGF-β expression, inhibition of collagen synthesis (specifically Col1 and Col3), and attenuation of myofibroblast differentiation, as indicated by lowered α-SMA levels. Simultaneously, it inhibits the proliferation of fibroblasts and tenocytes by inducing G0/G1 cell cycle arrest without triggering apoptosis, reducing adhesion severity in rat models while maintaining tendon strength, thereby underscoring its potential as an anti-fibrotic agent for tendon repair [117].

In cases of persistent inflammation and arthrofibrosis subsequent to ACL reconstruction, interleukin-1 (IL-1) plays a pivotal role in promoting excessive intra-articular inflammation. This is achieved through the stimulation of leukocyte infiltration, the expression of adhesion molecules, and the release of profibrotic cytokines such as transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). These processes culminate in the activation of myofibroblasts, leading to collagen accumulation and the formation of adhesions that impede tendon gliding and restrict the range of motion. Anakinra, an interleukin-1 receptor antagonist, functions by competitively binding to interleukin-1 receptors, thereby inhibiting downstream inflammatory pathways and fibrogenesis. This mechanism does not impede graft healing, resulting in a reduction in effusions, pain, and scar tissue formation, while simultaneously enhancing patellar mobility and flexion/extension capabilities. In clinical observations, the early intra-articular administration (administered within four months following surgical intervention) demonstrated a reduction in symptoms, improved recovery of motion, and decreased reliance on arthroscopic debridement or manipulation, underscoring its potential efficacy as an anti-fibrotic therapeutic approach in tendon repair [118].

Postoperative tendon adhesion formation is characterized by surgical injury that leads to an increase in TNF levels. This elevation facilitates the recruitment and activation of neutrophils, resulting in the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which contribute to tissue damage and profibrotic signaling. Additionally, there is an upregulation of IL-6, CXCL2, and PAI-1, alongside a mesothelial-to-myofibroblast transition mediated by α-SMA. These processes ultimately result in excessive extracellular matrix deposition and the formation of fibrotic bands that impede tendon gliding. Etanercept functions as a TNF receptor fusion protein that mechanistically neutralizes both soluble and transmembrane TNF. This action disrupts autocrine and paracrine signaling loops, which leads to the suppression of IL-6 and CXCL2 induction, a reduction in PAI-1 expression, inhibition of NET formation and neutrophilia, and attenuation of myofibroblast activation along with collagen accumulation. In murine models, prophylactic etanercept has been shown to reduce adhesion scores, decrease the presence of α-SMA + cells and vascularized fibrosis, and maintain tendon function without negatively affecting the healing process, thereby underscoring its potential as an anti-fibrotic agent in tendon repair [119].

In models of juvenile idiopathic arthritis, such as collagen-induced arthritis, sustained inflammation leads to systemic bone loss and tendon inflammation via IL-6-mediated mechanisms. This process results in increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-23, which facilitate osteoclastogenesis, RANKL expression, and the activation of myofibroblasts. These changes contribute to extracellular matrix deposition, disruption of tenocytes, and the formation of fibrotic adhesions that compromise tendon function. Tocilizumab, an anti-IL-6 receptor antibody, functions by inhibiting both soluble and membrane-bound IL-6 signaling pathways. This action leads to a suppression of downstream cytokine production, a decrease in osteoclast activity, and a reduction in inflammatory cascades, ultimately preserving the trabecular bone microarchitecture, as indicated by increases in bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th). Furthermore, it alleviates tendon damage through improvements in fiber orientation, nuclear morphology, and cellularity, while also lowering levels of TNF-α and IL-23. These effects contribute to a reduction in fibrosis and the enhancement of repair processes, without compromising the healing response, as demonstrated by decreased paw swelling and improved histological outcomes in rat models [120].

While all discussed anti-adhesives so far have to be processed to create a physical barrier, the human amniotic membrane (AM) is already in the form of a physical layer. Aside from common advantages of natural barriers such as biocompatibility, cost-effectiveness, and availability, it is gifted with some special properties, including being a natural reservoir of growth factors and cytokines, outstanding mechanical strength and elasticity, an antifibrotic effect, and an analgesic effect [121]. AM can also induce angiogenesis while exerting inhibiting inflammatory processes, which are valuable properties for preventing tendon adhesion formation [122].

Although the safety and anti-adhesion effect of AM has been proved by many studies, the problem lies in the vast diversity of AM products and their processing procedures, which can affect the final biomaterial properties [123]. For example, AM decellularization is a way to lower its antigenicity while maintaining the basal membrane and cytokines. Liu et al. reported that, although the decellularized AM had good permeability and positively affected healing by releasing the IL-1 cytokine, the unilateral AM was too thin after decellularization [124]. As a solution, AM was reinforced by adding two layers of synthetic polymer on both sides, which resulted in easy application and efficient tendon gliding [125]. Other problems with using AM as an anti-adhesive seem to be choosing the proper donor and its golden time of usage after processing [126].

Early mobilization is an important part of tendon injury rehabilitation and preventing scar formation. On the other hand, exerting too much force on a healing tendon can lead to complications like a retear or tendon gap. This delicate balance led some studies to focus on decreasing the muscle force by chemodenervation through botulinum toxin (BoNT) injection [127]. BoNT is an acetylcholine inhibitor produced by a bacteria called Clostridium botulinum, which has a dose-dependent weakening effect on muscles by inhibiting their neuromuscular junctions [128]. A systematic review in 2021 confirmed that BoNT is a safe and effective adjuvant treatment for flexor tendon injuries in preventing both adhesion formation and re-tear [129].

Exposing the repaired site of the tendon to some pharmacological agents during surgery is another field of exploration for anti-adhesives. 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), for example, is a drug with anti-metabolic effects that is used in glaucoma surgery. 5-Fu has been investigated for tendon adhesion since it can inhibit fibroblasts at the injury site during the first days after tendon repair [130]. Although a systematic review demonstrated that 5-FU is effective in preventing tendon adhesion in animal models [107], still no extra benefit, such as helping in the endogenous healing process, has been found so far [131]. Downsides of this anti-adhesive drug include possible adverse effects on the healing progression and its short half-life [130].

The processes of endogenous and exogenous healing in tendons are a delicate balance between different cytokines and growth factors. Consequently, many studies have investigated the effects of adding various growth factors to the local injury site. Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) is the main culprit in controlling cascades of signals called SMAD signaling pathways, which eventually lead to more expression of collagen III, the dominant form of collagen in adhesion and scar tissue [57]. TGF-β also participates in controlling the inflammatory response and activating other growth factors after injury [132]. During flexor tendon injuries, TGF-β1 and TGF-β2 subtypes trigger fibrotic tissue formation while TGF-β3 seems to antagonize their effect and improve tendon adhesion [133]. Wu et al. loaded a 3-D tendon scaffold with plasmids containing TGF-β1 silencing miRNAs, which resulted in reduced adhesion and improved tendon healing [134].

Another active component is basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), which induces angiogenesis and participates in cell migration [132]. Since bFGF can both increase collagen III expression (which is a fibrotic collagen) and induce the conversion of collagen III to collagen I in the last phase of tendon healing, it is important to find the exact dosage needed to benefit from this dual effect [135].

Since 2004 nanoparticles have been gaining attention in all therapeutic research fields. They can have a vast variety of effects based on what they contain within. For example, the photothermal effect of polydopamine nanoparticles was found to be effective in attenuating fibroblast activation and therefore led to inhibition of tendon adhesion development [136]. Polydopamine nanoparticles have also been used as carriers for other anti-adhesive drugs, such as Verapamil, which resulted in more anti-adhesive effects than each of them alone [137]. Nanoparticles showed a slow and sustained release of verapamil into the tissue, which possibly can spare the patient from the side effects of the systemic form.

Some nanoparticles can demonstrate desirable effects on tendon healing, such as copper oxide nanoparticles, which have anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, and antibacterial properties, while on the other hand, there is the possibility of cell toxicity [138]. Another nanoparticle subtype with cytotoxic effects is silver nanoparticles, which can inhibit fibroblast attachment and penetration, thus preventing peritendinous adhesion [87]. Although since the toxicity of silver nanoparticles is concentration-dependent, it has been suggested that the side effects can be avoided by diluting them in biological adhesives [139].

Negative Outcomes and Failed Cases: Despite the presence of promising results, certain anti-adhesive agents exhibit failure due to rapid degradation; for instance, hyaluronic acid (HA) alone demonstrates no efficacy without repeated injections [143], while selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) are noted for their ability to mitigate oxidative stress, yet HA is not addressed in this context. Furthermore, inflammatory responses have been observed in relation to synthetic particles [145].

Comparative Analysis: Pharmacological agents like corticosteroids and NSAIDs [56, 110] provide rapid inflammation reduction but risk rupture, unlike TGF-β inhibitors [57, 132–134] that target fibrosis more precisely, though with variable subtypes [133]. Antioxidants such as quercetin and EGCG [114, 115] synergize with autophagy activators like rapamycin [117] to balance ECM, outperforming cytokine blockers [118–120] in models but facing short half-lives [130]. AM and BoNT [121–129] offer natural synergies with barriers, yet nanoparticle toxicity [136–139, 144] limits paradigms compared to safer options like losartan [111, 112], emphasizing integrated strategies for superior adhesion prevention. With mechanisms and agents established, the next section evaluates preclinical evidence, questioning: How do in vitro and animal studies translate to clinical paradigms?

Anti-adhesive trials based on their developments

In vitro studies

Synthetic and composite nanofibrous scaffolds

Liu et al. conducted a study on the therapeutic anti-adhesive effects of polycaprolactone (PCL) and amniotic membranes (AM) charged with celecoxib [146]. Tissue around the flexor tendon of the toes of six-month-old rabbits was resected. 0.05 g, 0.08 g, and 0.10 g of celecoxib were dissolved in PCL solution at 5%, 8%, and 10% concentrations. The researchers observed that fibroblast activity was significantly lower on PCL-AM at 8% and 10% concentrations compared to the control and PCL membranes on the first day of culture. On the third day of culture, fibroblast function was reduced on PCL-AM 5%, 8%, and 10% compared to control and PCL membranes. They found that the inhibition of fibroblast activity was based on the dosage of celecoxib and that the fibroblast proliferation area was the smallest on the PCL-AM 10% membrane. PCL loaded with celecoxib can inhibit fibroblast activity, and therefore, a reduction in tendon adhesion may occur along with a lower quality of tendon repair. The AM used in the middle layer of the composite membrane was to release growth factors and enhance endogenous tendon healing.

Another study evaluated the effect of indomethacin/bupivacaine-eluting anti-adhesive poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)/collagen nanofibers on injured Achilles tendons [147]. Indomethacin is an NSAID that has a dual effect on the tendon healing process, enhancing remodeling during tendon repair while inhibiting proliferation [148, 149]. Analysis of the specimen revealed that the collagen type I expression was ten times that of type III. Collagen type I leads to increased mechanical strength of the tendons, promotes regeneration, and inhibits adhesion [150].

Chen et al. investigated the effect of hyaluronic acid/platelet-rich plasma (PRP)-infused core-shell nanofiber membrane (NFM) on post-surgical tendon adhesion [151]. In vitro exams revealed that NFM holds the potential to prevent fibroblast infiltration and therefore reduce adhesion. The anti-inflammatory effects of PRP also contribute to alleviating adhesion and enhancing tendon healing.

Lu et al. found that the pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate-poly(L-lactic) acid membrane poses anti-adhesive effects through suppressing macrophage activity and inhibiting the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway. Previous studies suggest that the NF-kB pathway can lead to excessive collagen production, which can exacerbate tendon adhesion [152].

Dong et al. hypothesized that adhesion formation can be limited by reducing macrophage-mediated inflammation. They developed an immunomodulatory compound made of electrospun polycaprolactone/silk fibroin (PCL/SF) composite fibrous scaffold functionalized with mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived extracellular matrix [31]. They enhanced the immunomodulatory effects of MSC by licensing with interferon-γ (iECM). In vitro analysis revealed that iECM significantly reduced the expression levels of cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, CXCL11, IL-10, IL-1R2, and TGF-β1. These results indicate the immunomodulatory potential of iECM and its anti-adhesive effects [153].

A study evaluated the effects of a double-layer membrane consisting of a poly(lactic-co-glycolic) acid (PLGA) electrospun membrane loaded with the NSAID ibuprofen (named EMI), plus Methoxy poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(L-valine) (PEG-PLV) loaded with basic fibroblast growth factor (PLVB) on Achilles tendon adhesion. They observed a marked reduction in inflammatory factors at the tendon healing area using this double membrane sheath [154].

Foreign body reaction can be induced due to the implantation of polymeric scaffolds and therefore impair tendon recovery. Song et al. used a novel short peptide, mechano-growth factor (MGF), to modify an electrospun polycaprolactone (PCL) fibrous scaffold to aid the macrophages to the M2 phenotype and alleviate foreign body reaction and inhibit tendon adhesion [155].

Chen et al. developed a multi-functional nanofibrous membrane (NFM) containing HA/ibuprofen (IBU) (HAI) followed by dual ionic crosslinking with FeCl₃ (HAIF NFMs) and covalent crosslinking with 1,4-butanediol diglycidyl ether (BDDE) to produce HAIFB NFMs. HAIFB NFMs containing 20% (HAI20FB), 30% (HAI30FB), and 40% (HAI40FB) IBU were fabricated. In vitro analysis revealed that besides HAI40FB, all other NFMs could potentially inhibit fibroblast penetration without impairing cell proliferation. The high dose of IBU in HAI40FB caused cytotoxicity towards fibroblasts [156].

The use of silver nanoparticles loaded in electrospun hyaluronic acid (HA)/polycaprolactone (PCL) nanofibrous membranes (NFMs) (HA/PCL + Ag NFMs) has been shown to inhibit fibroblast proliferation and attachment without cytotoxicity effects [157].

The application of electrospun composite poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG)/poly(caprolactone) (PCL) nanofibrous membrane (NFM) acts as a barrier between the tendon and the surrounding tissue. This study used PCL/PEG NFMs with 0% PEG (PCL), 25% PEG (25PECL), 50% PEG (50PECL), and 75% PEG (75PECL). All NFMs could inhibit cell proliferation, with the 75PECL being the most effective [158].

Nanoparticle and extracellular vesicle delivery systems

Verapamil-loaded polydopamine nanoparticles (VP-PDA NPs) have shown promising anti-adhesive effects on Achilles tendon laceration and tendon healing. It was observed that VP-PDA NPs could prevent adhesion through inhibiting fibroblast expansion, limit transforming growth factor-beta 1 expression, and decrease collagen content [159].

One study investigated how extracellular vesicles obtained from human umbilical cord stem cells that were pre-treated with hydroxycamptothecin (HCPT-EVs) affected adhesion of tendons after trauma [160]. The HCPT-EVs had a significant inhibitory effect on the growth and survival of fibroblasts, as well as the differentiation of myofibroblasts. The researchers concluded that HCPT-EVs could be a potential solution for preventing tendon adhesion by actively promoting the production of endoplasmic reticulum stress proteins in fibroblasts.

Natural and biological agents

A study on rabbit tenocytes revealed that high molecular weight hyaluronic acid down-regulated matrix marker genes and decreased the fibrosis marker a-SMA. Their results indicated that hyaluronic acid is a promising biodegradable unit for reducing adhesion for tendon repair [161].

Multiple approaches for preventing adhesion in hand flexor tendons have been investigated, including physical barriers (e.g., Seprafilm, Interceed) and biologic agents (e.g., hyaluronic acid, ADCON-T/N). However, the existing evidence from both animal and human studies remains limited, with the majority indicating only short-term benefits. Further randomized controlled trials are necessary to establish definitive efficacy [162, 163]. A systematic review examining adhesion prevention in hand tendons evaluated 14 studies, revealing a lack of high-quality (level 1 or 2) evidence for the majority of agents such as HA and ADCON-T/N. The findings predominantly consisted of level 4 evidence, which demonstrated the need for more rigorously designed trials to evaluate clinical outcomes [163].

Chitosan has shown anti-adhesive effects during tendon repair. In an experiment performed on the flexor tendon of rabbits, it was observed that treatment with chitosan attenuated tendon adhesion through the sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) pathway and that SIRT1 may be a potential target for reducing tendon adhesion [164].

Animal models

Nanofibrous and electrospun scaffold approaches

Numerous studies have been conducted to evaluate the impact of anti-adhesive compounds in tendon surgery when tested in live subjects. These investigations aim to assess the effectiveness and potential benefits of these compounds in preventing adhesion formation and improving surgical outcomes.

Yu et al. developed biodegradable PLGA/collagen nanofibers, capable of eluting indomethacin and bupivacaine, via electrospinning [147]. The effectiveness of these nanofibers in treating injured Achilles tendons was subsequently evaluated. A consistent pattern was observed in the elution of both biomolecules, indicating that sustained high-concentration release was maintained for a minimum duration of 4 weeks. The repaired tendons were retrieved after 8 weeks, and they observed that in comparison to the control group, the tendons in the experimental group displayed reduced adhesion to the surrounding tissues.

In vivo examinations from a rabbit tendon flexor model have revealed that NFM loaded with HA/PRP possesses anti-adhesive properties while promoting healing [151]. NFM infused with 50% HA displayed better outcomes in terms of reducing adhesion and alleviating inflammation compared to other NFMs and controls.

As described earlier, PDTC-PLA has been shown to inhibit macrophage proliferation through NF-kB inactivation [152]. In vivo assays from this study have demonstrated that PDTC-PLA can reduce tendon adhesion formation [165].

Another study used decellularized tendon matrix (DTM) to prevent adhesion following tendon injury in a rabbit Achilles tendon model [52]. The DTM-treated group showed a greater range of motion in the ankle joint compared to the control group. Moreover, the DTM group demonstrated lower adhesion scores in comparison to the control group. Specifically, the DTM group displayed minimal adhesion, which could be easily separated through blunt dissection. Additionally, the DTM group exhibited a higher maximum tensile strength for the tendon compared to the control group. These findings provide substantial evidence supporting the effectiveness of DTM application in preventing adhesion and promoting repair following Achilles tendon repair in rabbits. At the 3-week postoperative mark, the findings indicate that the DTM surrounding the tendon maintains its macro integrity. There exists a potential space between the tendon, DTM, and the surrounding tissues. The arrangement of these surrounding tissues is orderly, with distinct boundaries between layers. Furthermore, a significant presence of cells is observed both inside and around the DTM. In contrast, the control group exhibits disordered and discontinuous tissue structure around the tendon, with an unclear boundary between layers. At the 6-week mark following the operation, it was observed that the DTM completely disappears. The surrounding tissue of the tendon in the DTM group appears well-organized, with no noticeable adhesion or infiltration of inflammatory cells. In contrast, the control group shows poorly organized tissue around the tendon, with adhesion between the tissues. Moving to the 12-week mark after the operation, the surrounding tissues of the tendon in the DTM group have fully recovered to a physiological state. Clear layers are observed, and there are no signs of adhesion. On the other hand, the surrounding tissues of the tendon in the control group exhibit a reticulated pattern with discontinuous structures and mutual adhesions. These findings highlight the beneficial effects of DTM in promoting tissue organization and preventing adhesion in the healing process of the tendon.

A study by Zhang et al. fabricated an electrospun polyester membrane (EPM) loaded with curcumin/celecoxib (CUR/CEL) and evaluated its effect on peritendinous adhesion in an Achilles tendon rat model [23]. They observed that EPM loaded with both CUR and CEL held a better anti-adhesive ability compared to EPM loaded with CEL or CUR. EPM alone was inferior compared to others.

As described earlier, PCL/SF-iECM possesses anti-adhesive properties due to its anti-inflammatory mechanism [10]. PCL/SF-iECM has been shown to mitigate macrophage-mediated inflammatory response in vivo. Histological evaluation revealed that the adhesion formations were as follows: control >PCL/SF >PCL/SF-ECM >PCL/SF-iECM.

Gross observation of the effects of EMI-PLVB on tendon adhesion [11] displayed that severe adhesion was watched in the control group 3 weeks after injury, while in the treated group, the tendon surface at the repair site was smooth. Histological examinations were consistent with the findings of gross examinations and revealed that the group treated with EMI-PLVB had the lowest adhesion scores compared to other groups. Compared to other groups, the EMI-PLVB group also showed an increase in mechanical strength.

PCL/SF-MGF applied to a rat model of tendon adhesion showed that following 28 days of implantation, only mild adhesion was observed between the peritendinous tissue and the tendon. In contrast to the treated group, tendon adhesion was aggravated in the PCL groups.

Nanoparticle and decellularized matrix interventions

A study synthesized negatively charged gallic acid (GA)-silver nanoparticle (Ag NP) composites and attached the material to a decellularized extracellular matrix [166]. They observed that the rats treated with GA-Ag-NPs displayed lower adhesion scores, and the Ag nanoparticles led to a decrease in inflammatory cell infiltration.

HCPT-EVs and unprimed EVs effectively reduced adhesion in the Achilles tendon of rats [13]. Rating of the adhesions based on the histological adhesion scores showed that both HCPT-EVs and unprimed EVs significantly lowered the adhesion scores. HCPT-EV scores were lower compared to unprimed EVs, but the differences were not significant.

Another study evaluated the effects of HCPT in a rat tendon adhesion model [167]. HCPT treatment at a concentration of 0.1 mg/ml, three weeks after surgery, resulted in increased levels of ER stress proteins (ATF-6, P-IRE1, GRP78, and CHOP). Additionally, HCPT treatment led to a decrease in Bcl-2 expression and an increase in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio. Furthermore, HCPT treatment also decreased the expression of COL3A1 and α-SMA, indicating a reduction in ECM deposition. Overall, these results suggest that HCPT activates ER stress and reduces ECM deposition. The application of HCPT treatment resulted in a notable decrease in hyperproliferative fibroblasts and inflammatory cells. This reduction was found to be significant when compared to the control group. Among the treatment groups, the 0.1 mg/ml concentration exhibited the most prominent disparity in levels. The 0.1 mg/ml HCPT group exhibited significantly lower adhesion grades, histological scores, and hydroxyproline content compared to other groups. These findings suggest that HCPT application effectively reduces tendon adhesion without affecting healing. Immunohistochemistry analysis also demonstrated a decrease in COL3A1 and α-SMA expression, supporting HCPT’s protective effect against peritendinous fibrosis.

Wang et al. designed a novel dual-layer poly(l-lactic acid) (PLLA) membrane and evaluated its effects on chicken extensor flexor tendons [168]. In the control group, gross analysis showed severe adhesion in the repaired area. In the single-layer group, there were scars that connected the repaired tissue with the surrounding tissues. Only in the dual-layer group was a smooth regenerated site observed.

Gross observation from a previously explained study [156] demonstrated that among four groups of rabbits—a control group, HAFB (without IBU), Seprafilm, and HAI30FB—the HAI30FB group had the lowest adhesion score, followed by HAFB and Seprafilm. The control group had the highest adhesion score. Histologic examinations were inconsistent with the gross findings that the HAI30FB group displayed no adhesion to the surrounding tissue. Biomechanical evaluations revealed that PIP and DIP joint angles in the HAI30FB group were the highest compared to the other groups.

Molecular and pharmacological modulation strategies

Another study has identified microRNA29a (miR-29a) as a potential therapeutic target for tendon injury by regulating the immune response and matrix remodeling [51]. MiR29a-LNPs are successfully prepared using microfluidic technology and loaded into core-shell nanofibers for local delivery to the injured tendon. MiR29a is found to regulate collagen synthesis, NF-κB activation in tenocytes, and macrophage polarization by inhibiting the inflammasome pathway. In vivo studies using an Achilles tendon-rupture model demonstrate that the miR29a group shows superior repair, with improved collagen composition, alignment, mechanical strength, and functional recovery.

Chen et al. developed an NFM consisting of a core and sheath structure (171). In the sheath, silver nanoparticles were embedded within the polylactic acid material, while the core was composed of HA. Core sheath NFMs (CSNFM) were made with thick [77] or thin [63] sheaths. They also prepared comparable CSNFMs with thick (Tk+) and thin (Tn+) sheaths, both containing embedded Ag NPs. In vivo studies demonstrated that the Tn + group displayed an almost total absence of adhesion. In comparison to the control and Tk groups, the positive control and Tn groups exhibited only a mild level of peritendinous adhesions, characterized by minimal adhesion bands. The Tn + group performed better at the flexion angles of the distal interphalangeal (DIP) and proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints test. They also displayed less adhesion in histological examinations and the most regular collagen arrangement.

Clinical applications and challenges

The translation of anti-adhesive compounds into clinical practice for tendon repair has shown promise in addressing postoperative complications. These compounds play a crucial role in preventing adhesion formation, a common concern following tendon repair surgeries. By inhibiting the development of fibrous tissue, they contribute to improved joint function and reduced risks of complications such as restricted movement and pain.

Moreover, the clinical application of anti-adhesive compounds extends to enhancing tendon gliding. Evidence supports their efficacy in maintaining or restoring the gliding function of tendons, which is vital for optimal joint function and minimizing the risk of re-injury. These compounds have demonstrated the potential to facilitate more effective rehabilitation, aiding patients in regaining normal function and mobility during the recovery process.

To connect preclinical and clinical findings, animal models (e.g., rabbit flexor, rat Achilles) exhibit a reduction in adhesions; however, human trials present inconsistencies attributed to variability in injury severity and anatomical differences [174]. Additionally, risk factors such as age and smoking influence adhesion formation following hand repair, while elevated complication rates in humerus fractures underscore the necessity for further refinement [175]. Recent investigations point to the need for standardized protocols for translation, wherein ZnO-DAT hydrogel demonstrates the ability to restore strength with minimal adhesions in rat models, indicating the necessity for validation in larger animal studies prior to clinical application [176].

However, the integration of anti-adhesive compounds into clinical practice is not without its challenges. Determining the optimal dosage is a critical consideration, requiring a delicate balance between efficacy and safety. The selection of delivery methods is equally challenging, as the chosen approach must provide sustained release without compromising patient safety.

Ensuring the safety profiles of these compounds is a key concern. Rigorous evaluation is necessary to identify and mitigate potential adverse effects or allergic reactions. The assessment of long-term effects poses another challenge, necessitating extended follow-up periods in clinical trials to understand the durability of anti-adhesive effects and any potential late-onset complications.

Patient variability introduces complexity, as factors such as age, overall health, and genetic variations may influence the response to anti-adhesive treatments. Tailoring approaches to individual patients may be necessary to optimize outcomes. Additionally, integrating anti-adhesive compounds seamlessly into various tendon repair surgical techniques requires careful consideration, ensuring compatibility with existing practices for widespread adoption.

Comparative Analysis: Clinical studies reveal inconsistencies; for example, animal reductions in adhesions [174] contrast with human variability due to factors like age/smoking [175], limiting translation compared to promising hydrogels like ZnO-DAT [176] in rats. Synergies in delivery methods balance efficacy-safety, but paradigms overlook patient genetics, suggesting tailored integrations could surpass current techniques for better gliding and rehabilitation.

Future perspectives

Research in the field of anti-adhesive compounds for tendon repair has been rapidly progressing. New biomimetic materials and techniques have been developed recently in this field. Film-, membrane-, and layer-based materials and pharmacological agents such as botulinum toxin, mannose-6-phosphate, amnion, and growth factors are some of these compounds. Even though the development of anti-adhesive compounds for tendon repair has advanced significantly, there are still a number of obstacles and restrictions. To advance beyond these present constraints, additional research should be conducted in the following areas:

Studies on long-term efficacy

To evaluate the anti-adhesive compounds’ long-term efficacy, extensive and long-term studies should be conducted.

Clinical validation and standardization

Validate the effectiveness of anti-adhesive compounds in a range of patient populations by conducting extensive, carefully supervised clinical trials. To create evidence-based guidelines for the use of these drugs in clinical practice, standardize outcome measures and protocols, and make it easier to compare studies.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

To determine the financial impact of adding anti-adhesive treatments to routine tendon repair procedures, conduct cost-effectiveness analyses. Adopting these interventions in healthcare settings requires an understanding of their cost-effectiveness.

Synergistic Mechanisms and Integrated Strategies: Future research should prioritize exploring synergies between different anti-adhesion strategies, such as combining physical barriers like HA hydrogels with pharmacological agents like TGF-β inhibitors to simultaneously provide mechanical separation and molecular fibrosis suppression, potentially yielding additive reductions in adhesions beyond isolated applications (e.g., as hinted in preclinical hybrids like HA-PRP). This could address current limitations where single modalities fail due to rapid degradation or incomplete pathway coverage. Limitations of Current Research Paradigms: Existing paradigms heavily rely on small-animal models (e.g., rats/rabbits), which overlook human-scale anatomical and immunological differences, leading to translational failures [174]. Larger animal models or organ-on-chip systems are needed to bridge this gap, alongside addressing batch variability in natural materials and toxicity in nanoparticles [144, 145], to refine paradigms for more reliable clinical predictions. Expanded Research Directions: Beyond RCTs, investigations should focus on multifunctional biomaterials incorporating AI-driven design for personalized delivery, gene-editing tools like CRISPR to target adhesion genes (e.g., miR-29a expansions from 170), and regenerative therapies integrating stem cells with barriers for holistic repair. Longitudinal cohorts assessing real-world outcomes, including patient-reported metrics, will fill evidence gaps, ultimately enhancing orthopedic surgery by reducing reoperations and improving quality of life.

Future studies in these areas will help scientists and medical professionals create anti-adhesive compounds for tendon repair that are more clinically viable and effective, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes and a reduction in the incidence of complications following surgery.
