Optimization of gridding algorithms for FFT by vector optimization
Federico Achini, Paola Causin, Sara Vanini, Ke Chen, Simone Scacchi

Abstract
The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is widely used in applications such as MRI, CT, and interferometry; however, because of its dependence on uniformly sampled data, it requires the use of gridding techniques for practical implementation. The performance of these algorithms strongly depends on the choice of the gridding kernel, with the first prolate spheroidal wave function (PSWF) regarded as optimal. This work redefines kernel optimality through the lens of vector optimization (VO), introducing a rigorous framework that characterizes optimal kernels as Pareto-efficient solutions of an error shape operator. We establish the continuity of such operator, study the existence of solutions, and propose a novel methodology to construct kernels tailored to a desired target error function. The approach is implemented numerically via interior-point optimization. Comparative experiments demonstrate that the proposed kernels outperform both the PSWF and the state-of-the-art methods (MIRT-NUFFT) in specific regions of interest, achieving orders-of-magnitude improvements in mean absolute errors. These results confirm the potential of VO-based kernel design to provide customized accuracy profiles aligned with application-specific requirements. Future research will extend this framework to multidimensional cases and relative error minimization, with potential integration of machine learning for adaptive target error selection. Keywords : gridding algorithm, FFT, vector optimization, Pareto front, uncertainty principle

Introduction

1.1
Background and related works

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is a fundamental algorithm that, since its introduction by Cooley and Tukey in 1965
[
8
,
30
]
, has proven to be a cornerstone in numerous applications such as MRI
[
1
,
47
,
10
]
, interferometry
[
48
,
37
,
3
]
, and CT
[
32
,
24
]
, to name just a few. The FFT enables the computation of the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) of a discrete signal of length
N
in only
O(N\log N)
time, in contrast to the
O(N^{2})
complexity required by straightforward matrix-vector multiplication
[
8
]
.

An important assumption for the application of the FFT is that the signal has been
sampled uniformly
. However, in real-world scenarios this is rarely the case, due to device imperfections or specific features of the underlying physical system (see, for example,
[
26
,
16
,
15
]
). Therefore, directly applying the FFT on nonuniformly sampled signals yields inaccurate or noisy results
[
9
,
10
,
47
]
. To address this limitation, signals are first approximated on a uniform grid using a gridding technique
[
10
,
32
,
17
]
; that is, the data are interpolated onto a regular grid via convolution with a predefined kernel (the so-called
gridding kernel
). Such gridding kernel is assumed to have a small support, to ensure that the convolution can be fast computed (see
Section

2.1
). Finally, the spectrum thus obtained is corrected by multiplication with a compensation function (sometimes called
apodization correction
or
deapodization

[
6
,
42
]
) to improve the reconstruction accuracy. This compensation function is introduced to correct errors that come from the interpolation step, and its choice depends on the gridding kernel.

The selection of the optimal gridding kernel and deapodization function has been the subject of extensive research
[
37
,
48
,
36
,
10
]
. The first prolate spheroidal wave function (PSWF) of zeroth order
[
39
,
22
,
40
]
is frequently cited as optimal due to its unique property of being the bandlimited function with maximum energy concentration in the time domain
[
37
,
3
]
. This property helps suppress image aliasing and artifacts in the reconstruction. Direct computation of the first PSWF is challenging; for this reason, it is usually approximated by the Kaiser–Bessel function, which is more practical and efficient.

A lot of work has been done to systematically find gridding kernels and compensation functions with good performance. In particular, Fessler
[
10
]
analysed in detail the error produced by gridding algorithms. A numerical procedure to generate optimal kernels and deapodization functions was described, parametrized by the size of the support of the gridding kernel and the oversampling factor chosen for the interpolation. The key idea was based on min-max optimization of the error, resulting in the gridding kernel and deapodization function minimizing the worst-case scenario. In fact, the proposed optimization involves the minimization of the error sup-norm; thus, it aims to find the gridding kernel that is
globally optimal
. The same technique was also used in the same paper to find the optimal parameters for the Kaiser-Bessel kernel. Moreover, in subsequent papers by other authors, it was adapted to the
L^{2}
norm
[
18
,
47
]
.

The above approach has been questioned in scenarios where the goal is to have a very high accuracy only on a specific portion of the spectrum
[
5
]
. In
[
48
]
, the authors propose an optimization procedure to determine the optimal gridding kernel for a restricted region of the field of view, demonstrating improved performance compared to the PSWF. The key idea is to minimize the approximation error introduced by the gridding+FFT algorithm specifically within the region of interest (ROI), rather than over the entire domain.

The aim of this paper is to further generalize this approach by introducing a novel notion of optimality grounded in vector optimization theory (VO). To the authors’ knowledge, the existing literature on gridding algorithms lacks a clear and consistent definition of what
optimality
means for a gridding kernel in this more general case. We address this issue by defining an optimal gridding kernel as a kernel lying in the Pareto front of an operator
\Lambda
, which will be called
error shape operator
. More specifically, we define an optimal kernel as a function that satisfies a minimality condition with respect to a partial ordering defined on a suitable function space. Building on this framework, we demonstrate how to construct optimal gridding kernels that yield an error profile across the spectrum that resembles a desired shape, which we refer to as the
target error function
.

Gridding algorithms

In this section we describe the theoretical aspects of the gridding algorithms (
Section

2.1
), and find an upper bound for their error (

2.2
). The results presented here are a generalization of those given in

2.1
Gridding algorithms for nonuniform FFT

Suppose we are given a discrete signal
\bm{u}=(u_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, with
N\gg 1
an integer, and we wish to compute the following nonuniform DFT for nonuniform time samples
\bm{t}=(t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
:

y(x)=\sum_{n<N}u_{n}e^{-2\pi ixt_{n}}

(1)

for any frequency
x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. Computing
eq.

1
involves
N
complex products and
N-1
additions: thus, it can be done in
O(N)
operations. Usually, we uniformly discretize the spectrum interval
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
into
M
points
x_{m}=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{M}
, with
m=0,\dots,M-1
, and compute
y(x_{m})
for each of them. Thus, we get a total of
O(MN)
operations. The (continuous) function
y(x)
will be considered the ground truth whose values in the points
x_{m}
we would like to reproduce.

If
M
and
N
are large, as is usually the case, the computational cost becomes overwhelming. Using gridding algorithms, we aim to compute an approximation of
y(x)
exploiting the speed of the FFT algorithm. We recall that one of the requirements of the FFT algorithm is the signal to be uniformly sampled in the time domain. Thus, since
\bm{u}
is nonuniformly sampled, we first have to approximate it on a uniform grid, which is exactly why gridding algorithms were developed. This approximation is performed by convolving the signal with a kernel
C
(
eq.

3
); then, we apply the FFT to the resampled signal and correct the resulting spectrum by a deapodization function
h
(
eq.

2
). The approximation
y^{*}(x)
of
y(x)
is then given by

y^{*}(x)=h(x)\sum_{k<\gamma N}u^{*}_{k}e^{-2\pi ixk/\gamma},

(2)

where

u^{*}_{k}=\sum_{n<N}u_{n}C(k-\gamma t_{n}).

(3)

The vector
\bm{u}^{*}=(u_{k}^{*})
is the resampled signal. Notice that
\bm{u}^{*}
belongs to
\mathbb{C}^{\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor}
, where
\gamma\geq 1
is a parameter called
oversampling factor
and
\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor
denotes the largest integer
\leq\gamma N
. Thus, we do not exclude the possibility that the signal is interpolated on a finer grid.

The two functions
C
and
h
are defined as follows:

-

C
is a complex function in
\mathcal{C}(I_{W})
, where
I_{W}=[-W,W]
for some chosen parameter
W\geq 1
, called
gridding kernel
. It is convolved with
\bm{u}
to produce the new signal
\bm{u}^{*}
with uniform sampling. We will see that the choice of
W
affects the speed of the gridding algorithms, as well as their accuracy. In general, higher values of
W
result in an increased computational cost and accuracy.

-

h
is a complex function in
\mathcal{C}([-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}))
. It is called
gridding correcting function
,
gridding compensation function
, or
deapodization function
. Its purpose is further correcting some imperfections that could occur in the approximated signal
\bm{u}^{*}
.

The scalar
\gamma
and the functions
C
and
h
should be considered as parameters which can be used to calibrate the gridding algorithm, depending on our specific requirements.

Remark 2.1.1

(Computational cost)
.

Assume now for simplicity that
M=N
, so that the number of operations we need to calculate
eq.

1
is
O(N^{2})
. If we assume that
W\ll N
,
eq.

3
can be calculated with only
O(W)
operations, since most of the
C(k-\gamma t_{n})
terms are null (remember that
C
has support in
I_{W}
, so
C(k-\gamma t_{n})=0
whenever
|k-\gamma t_{n}|>W
). Repeating the computation for all
k<\gamma N
, we get a total number of operations of
O(\gamma WN)
, which is linear in
N
, provided that
\gamma
is not too high. If we then exploit the speed of the FFT to compute
eq.

2
, we see that we can compute the vector
(y^{*}(x_{m}))\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
with only
O(\gamma N\log(\gamma N))
) operations. Finally, the multiplication with
h
requires
O(N)
operations. Therefore, summing all the contributions, the total number of operations required by the gridding procedure (
eq.

3
) and the FFT
eq.

2
) is
O(\gamma WN+\gamma N\log(\gamma N))
: a number much smaller than
O(N^{2})
. In other words, the scheme given by gridding+FFT is able to quickly compute an approximation of
eq.

1
. Usually, one will choose
W
and
\gamma
depending on the desired speed. In general, higher values of these two parameters result in a higher accuracy but a slower computation.

The problem of choosing
C
and
h
is tackled in the following sections.

2.2
Gridding error

Let
x
be a fixed frequency in
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. The (squared) error between
y(x)
and
y^{*}(x)
depends on the signal
\bm{u}
, the frequency
x
, and the functions
C
and
h
; we stress this fact by writing
y(\bm{u};x,h,C)
and
y^{*}(\bm{u};x,h,C)
. It can be expressed as

\begin{split}E(\bm{u};x,h,C)&=|y(\bm{u};x,h,C)-y^{*}(\bm{u};x,h,C)|^{2}\\
&=\Big|\sum_{n<N}u_{n}e^{-2\pi ixt_{n}}-h(x)\sum_{k<\gamma N}\sum_{n<N}u_{n}C(k-\gamma t_{n})e^{-2\pi ixk/\gamma}\Big|^{2}\\
&=\Big|\sum_{n<N}u_{n}e^{-2\pi ixt_{n}}\cdot\Big(1-h(x)\sum_{k<\gamma N}C(k-\gamma t_{n})e^{-2\pi ix(k/\gamma-t_{n})}\Big)\Big|^{2}\\
&\leq\sum_{n<N}|u_{n}|^{2}\cdot\sum_{n<N}\Big|1-h(x)\sum_{k<\gamma N}C(k-\gamma t_{n})e^{-2\pi ix(k/\gamma-t_{n})}\Big|^{2},\end{split}

(4)

where the last row contains a term that depends only on the signal
\bm{u}
, and a term that depends only on
x
,
C
, and
h
. This latter term is the most interesting, since it suggests that we can control the error in
x
by
C
and
h
, and it has a very natural interpretation, since it is the operator norm of the linear functional

E(-;x,h,C):\mathbb{C}^{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C},

(5)

with
\mathbb{C}^{N}
endowed with the
2
-norm. In fact, from
eq.

4
we see that, for every
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,

|E(\bm{u};x,h,C)|\leq\|\bm{u}\|_{2}\cdot\theta(x,h,C),

(6)

where

\theta(x,h,C)=\sqrt{\sum_{n<N}\Big|1-h(x)\sum_{k<\gamma N}C(k-\gamma t_{n})e^{-2\pi ix(k/\gamma-t_{n})}\Big|^{2}}\geq 0.

Notice that

\theta(x,h,C)=\inf\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\text{ $|$ }|E(\bm{u};x,h,C)|\leq\|\bm{u}\|_{2}\cdot\alpha,\forall\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\},

that is,

\theta(x,h,C)=\|E(-;x,h,C)\|,

where
\|E(-;x,h,C)\|
is the operator norm of
E(-;x,h,C)
.

In general, one wants to keep the error as small as possible. This means that, for each
x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
, we would like to find
C
and
h
such that
\|E(-;x,h,C)\|
is minimum. Writing
\gamma t_{n}=\lfloor\gamma t_{n}\rfloor+\{\gamma t_{n}\}
, where
\lfloor t\rfloor
denotes the largest integer smaller than
t
and
\{t\}\equiv t-\lfloor t\rfloor
, we can define
k^{\prime}=k-\lfloor\gamma t_{n}\rfloor
, so that
k^{\prime}\in\{t_{n}\}+I_{W}\subseteq[-W,W+1)=I_{W}^{\prime}
. Thus, we can conveniently rewrite the sum in the second factor of the last expression of
eq.

4
as

\sum_{k^{\prime}\in I^{\prime}_{W}}C(k^{\prime}-\{\gamma t_{n}\})e^{-2\pi ix(k^{\prime}-\{\gamma t_{n}\})/\gamma},

which has the advantage that
\{t_{n}\}\in[0,1)
for all
n<N
. For the sake of clarity, in the following we drop the symbol
′
from
k
with a little abuse of notation.

If
N\gg 1
, the sequence
\{\gamma t_{n}\}
can be considered to be drawn randomly from a uniform distribution on
[0,1)

[
43
, Theorem 3.3.5]
. Therefore,
\|E(-;x,h,C)\|^{2}
can be seen as the Monte Carlo approximation of a finite integral on
[0,1)
:

\begin{split}\|E(-;x,h,C)\|^{2}&=\sum_{n<N}\Big|1-h(x)\sum_{k\in I^{\prime}_{W}}C(k-\{\gamma t_{n}\})e^{-2\pi ix(k-\{\gamma t_{n}\})/\gamma}\Big|^{2}\\
&\approx N\cdot\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\Big|1-h(x)\sum_{k\in I_{W}^{\prime}}C(k-\nu)e^{-2\pi ix(k-\nu)/\gamma}\Big|^{2}d\nu}_{\ell(x;h,C)}.\end{split}

(7)

From now on, we focus on the function
\ell:[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which approximates
\|E(-;x,h,C)\|/N
and does not depend on the specific sampling
t_{n}
,
n=0,\dots,N-1
.

Since
|z|^{2}=z\overline{z}
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we can expand the integrand in
\ell(x)
:

\begin{split}\ell(x;h,C)&=1-h(x)\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k\in I^{\prime}_{W}}C(k-\nu)e^{-2\pi ix(k-\nu)/\gamma}d\nu}_{A}-\overline{h}(x)\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k\in I^{\prime}_{W}}\overline{C}(k-\nu)e^{2\pi ix(k-\nu)/\gamma}d\nu}_{\overline{A}}\\
&\quad+|h(x)|^{2}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k_{1}\in I^{\prime}_{W}}\sum_{k_{2}\in I^{\prime}_{W}}C(k_{1}-\nu)\overline{C}(k_{2}-\nu)e^{-2\pi ix(k_{1}-k_{2})/\gamma}d\nu}_{B}\\
&=1-h(x)A-\overline{h}(x)\overline{A}+|h(x)|^{2}B\\
&=1-\Re(h)(A+\overline{A})-i\cdot\Im(h)(A-\overline{A})+\Re(h)^{2}B+\Im(h)^{2}B\\
&=1-2\Re(h)\Re(A)+2\Im(h)\Im(A)+\Re(h)^{2}B+\Im(h)^{2}B\end{split}

(8)

The last row of
eq.

8
shows that
\ell
is a
real
polynomial of degree 2 in the
real
variables
\Re(h)
and
\Im(h)
, which we denote by
F(\Re(h),\Im(h))
(notice that
B
is real and positive by definition). To keep the gridding error defined in
eq.

4
as small as possible, we need
\ell(x;h,C)
to be as small as possible. For this reason, we find the function
h^{*}
such that
(\Re(h^{*}),\Im(h^{*}))
is the minimum point for
F
. This function
h^{*}
must exist and be unique, since
F
describes an upward elliptic paraboloid, and it depends on
C
, because the coefficients
A
and
B
of
F
depend on
C
. Hence, we will write
h^{*}=h^{*}(x;C)
and
\ell^{*}=\ell^{*}(x;C)=\ell(x;h^{*},C)
.

A simple analysis of the partial derivatives reveals
(\Re(h^{*}),\Im(h^{*}))=\big(\frac{\Re(A)}{B},-\frac{\Im(A)}{B}\big)
. Therefore, we get

h^{*}(x;C)=\frac{\overline{A}}{B},

(9)

and

\ell^{*}(x;C)=1-\frac{|A|^{2}}{B}.

(10)

We can obtain more meaningful expressions for
A
and
B
. First, notice that

\begin{split}A&=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k\in I^{\prime}_{W}}C(k-\nu)e^{-2\pi ix(k-\nu)/\gamma}d\nu\\
&=\sum_{k\in I^{\prime}_{W}}\int_{0}^{1}C(k-\nu)e^{-2\pi ix(k-\nu)\gamma}d\nu\\
&=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{k-1}^{k}C(\xi)e^{-2\pi ix\xi/\gamma}d\xi\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}C(\xi)e^{-2\pi ix\xi/\gamma}d\xi,\end{split}

(11)

so
A=\widehat{C}(x/\gamma)
, the Fourier transform of
C
in
x/\gamma\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. Notice that we substituted
I^{\prime}_{W}
with
\mathbb{Z}
in the third equality, since
C
has a bounded support.

Next, consider
B
. As a first step, define
k_{1}-k_{2}=\beta
and
k_{1}=\alpha
, so that the two internal sums become

\sum_{\alpha\in I^{\prime}_{W}}\sum_{\beta\in\alpha-I^{\prime}_{W}}C(\alpha-\nu)\overline{C}(\alpha-\beta-\nu)e^{-2\pi ix\beta/\gamma}.

Since the sums are finite, we can also switch them with the integral sign

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{\alpha\in I^{\prime}_{W}}\sum_{\beta\in\alpha-I^{\prime}_{W}}&C(\alpha-\nu)\overline{C}(\alpha-\beta-\nu)e^{-2\pi ix\beta/\gamma}d\nu\\
&=\sum_{\alpha\in I^{\prime}_{W}}\sum_{\beta\in\alpha-I^{\prime}_{W}}\int_{0}^{1}C(\alpha-\nu)\overline{C}(\alpha-\beta-\nu)e^{-2\pi ix\beta/\gamma}d\nu.\end{split}

\mathbb{Z}

3.1
Background on vector optimization

Let
Y
be a real topological linear space. A subset
D\subseteq Y
is called a
cone
if it is closed under multiplication by nonnegative scalars. It is a
proper
cone if
\{0\}\not=D
, and
pointed
if
D\cap-D=\{0\}
. Finally,
D
is
convex
if
D+D\subseteq D
. In this paper, we will always assume that
D
is a proper pointed convex cone.

Example 3.1.1
.

Let
Y=\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
N\geq 1
. Then

D=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\text{ $|$ }x_{1}\geq 0,\dots,x_{N}\geq 0\}

is a proper pointed convex cone.

Example 3.1.2

(Example 1.51,
[
19
]
)
.

Let
Y=L^{p}(\Omega)
, with
\Omega
a measure space and
p\in[1,\infty]
. Then

D=\{f\in L^{p}(\Omega)\text{ $|$ }f(\omega)\geq 0\text{ for almost all }\omega\in\Omega\}

is a proper pointed convex cone.

Any proper pointed convex cone
D
induces a partial ordering on
Y
defined by

y_{1}\preceq y_{2}\Longleftrightarrow y_{2}-y_{1}\in D,\quad\forall y_{1},y_{2}\in Y.

The inequality is strict if
y_{1}\neq y_{2}
, and we write
y_{1}\prec y_{2}
.

Let
Z\subseteq Y
,
Z\not=\varnothing
. Consider the following problem:

\begin{split}&\text{minimize }z\in Z\subseteq Y\\
&\text{with respect to the ordering }\preceq\text{ defined by $D\subseteq Y$.}\end{split}

(15)

Problems of this type are called
vector optimization problems
. Frequently,
Z
is the image of a function
f:S\rightarrow Y
, where
S\subseteq X
and
X
is a real topological linear space. As
\preceq
is not a total order in general, we cannot expect to find a unique solution, but rather a set of minimal points.

Definition 3.1.3
.

A point
z^{*}\in Z
is
Pareto efficient
(or
Pareto optimal
) if it is minimal, that is, there does not exist another
z\in Z
,
z\not=z^{*}
, such that
z\prec z^{*}
. The set of all Pareto optimal points is called the
Pareto front
of
Z
.

The study of Pareto optimal solutions is usually pursued by
scalarization
; that is, by converting the original problem into one or more optimization problems with only one cost function. This concept has proven to be useful both from a theoretical and a practical point of view.

Scalarization is achieved by minimizing monotonic (possibly nonlinear) functionals of
Y
.

Definition 3.1.4
.

A functional
\phi:Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is

(i)

D
-increasing
if
y_{1}\preceq y_{2}\Rightarrow\phi(y_{1})\leq\phi(y_{2})
,

(ii)

strongly
D
-increasing
if
y_{1}\prec y_{2}\Rightarrow\phi(y_{1})<\phi(y_{2})
,

for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
.

It is easy to see that if
\phi
is strongly
D
-increasing, then it is also
D
-increasing.

Example 3.1.5
.

Let
Y=L^{p}(\Omega)
and
D
as in
Example

3.1.2
. If
p<\infty
, then the
L^{p}
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is a strongly
D
-increasing functional.

Proposition 3.1.6

(Lemma 5.14,
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\phi:Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a functional and suppose there exists
z^{*}\in Z
such that

f(z^{*})\leq f(z),\quad\forall z\in Z.

(16)

Then:

(i)

if
\phi
is
D
-increasing and
z^{*}
is uniquely determined by
eq.

16
, then
z^{*}
is a Pareto optimal element of
Z
;

(ii)

if
\phi
is strongly
D
-increasing, then
z^{*}
is a Pareto optimal element of
Z
.

We refer,
e.g.
, to
[
19
,
27
,
20
]
for an extended background on these topics.

The
L^{p}(\Omega)

In this paper, we are particularly interested in applying VO to the case of

spaces, as we will see that the functions describing the error the gridding algorithms have finite

Suppose we are given a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
, where
\Omega
is a topological space,
\Sigma
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
and
\mu
is a complete measure
\Sigma\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
. Let
H
be a (real) topological vector space
H
and
S
a nonempty subset of
H
. Suppose also that we are given a cost function

L:\Omega\times S\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

(17)

such that, for a fixed
p\in[1,\infty)
, the function
L_{h}\equiv L(-,h):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
belongs to
L^{p}(\Omega)
for all fixed

. If we define the operator

\Lambda:S\longrightarrow L^{p}(\Omega)\quad\text{such that}\quad\Lambda(h)=L_{h}=L(-,h),

(18)

the connection with vector optimization theory becomes evident. In fact, we saw in
Example

3.1.2
that
L^{p}(\Omega)
comes with a natural choice of a proper pointed convex cone

We can now see that we are in the same setting described in problem (

), with

(the

). Moreover, in the

case we can interpret
\Omega
as a set of indexes for multiple cost functions. Notice that, if
\Omega=\{1,\dots,N\}
for some
N\geq 1
, then

, and so we are dealing with finite-dimensional linear spaces, as for example in
[

]
.

In virtue of
Proposition

\eta\in Y

\eta\preceq z
for any

minimal

-distance from

-norm.

Fix

(19)

where
\rho>1
is a penalty coefficient and
(f-\eta)_{+}=\max\{0,f-\eta\}

Let

Proof.

See

.
∎

3.2
The optimization problem for
\ell^{*}(x;C)

The error bound function
\ell^{*}(x;C)
from
Theorem

2.2.2
depends only on the gridding kernel
C
. We apply vector optimization theory to find the
minimal
bounds, that is, the Pareto optimal solutions. By definition, a minimal solution will be a solution such that
if we try to decrease the error at some subset of frequencies by modifying the solution, this will necessarily cause an increase of the error at the remaining set of frequencies
. So, a Pareto optimal solution represents a minimal error in a very intuitive sense.

Formally, let
\Omega
denote the spectrum domain
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
endowed with the Lebesgue measure, and let
H=L^{2}(I_{W})
and
S=\{C\in H\text{ $|$ }\|C\|_{2}=1\}\subseteq H
. The choice of
S
is justified by the fact that the function
\ell^{*}(x;C)
is invariant to multiplication by a not null complex scalar of the gridding kernel
C
; that is,
\ell^{*}(x;C)=\ell^{*}(x;\alpha C)
, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.

Fix any
p\in[1,\infty]
and define the nonlinear operator

\begin{split}\Lambda:S\longrightarrow L^{p}(\Omega)\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad\Lambda C=\Lambda C(x)=\ell^{*}(x;C)&=\frac{\sum_{m\not=0}|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma+m)|^{2}}{\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma+m)|^{2}}\\
&=1-\frac{|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma)|^{2}}{\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma+m)|^{2}},\end{split}

(20)

where
L^{p}(\Omega)
is in this case the space of
real
functions with finite
p
-norm. The operator
\Lambda
will be called the
error shape operator
. Notice that we are now working in a more general setting than that of
Section

2
, where
C
was an element of
\mathcal{C}(I_{W})
. However, since
\mathcal{C}(I_{W})
is dense in
L^{2}(I_{W})
, this fact does not constitute an issue. In fact, we will prove that
\Lambda
is continuous; therefore, if we find a kernel
C\in L^{2}(I_{W})
that minimizes
\Lambda
, we will always be able to produce
a posteriori
a continuous function
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(I_{W})
such that
\Lambda C^{\prime}
is close to
\Lambda C
; which means that
C^{\prime}
is close to being Pareto optimal.

Lemma 3.2.1
.

The gridding error shape operator
\Lambda
defined in
eq.

20
is well defined for any
p\in[1,\infty]
.

Proof.

We have to prove that
\Lambda C
is measurable and that its
p
-norm is finite, for any
p\in[1,\infty]
.

By the Paley-Wiener theorem (see
[
34
]
, section 19.1), we know that
\widehat{C}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
can be extended to an entire function on the complex plane; in particular, it is measurable. Therefore, for a fixed
C
, the function
\ell^{*}(x;C)
is measurable, being the ratio of two pointwise limits of measurable functions, provided that the denominator is null at most on a set of measure zero. This is indeed the case, as the zero set of a holomorphic function is at most countable (Theorem 10.18,
[
34
]
).

Finally, let
x
be an element of
\Omega
in which
\ell^{*}(x;C)
is well defined; that is, an element such that the denominator of
\ell^{*}(x;C)
does not vanish. We have
0\leq\ell^{*}(x;C)\leq 1
by definition. But then
\ell^{*}(-;C)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and thus
\ell^{*}(-;C)\in L^{p}(\Omega)
for all
p\in[1,\infty]
, since
\Omega
has finite measure.
∎

Since
L^{p}(\Omega)
is a partially ordered topological real space (see
Example

3.1.2
), we can apply the vector optimization machinery. We thus define the following vector optimization problem:

\begin{split}&\text{minimize }\Lambda C\in\Lambda(S)\subseteq H\\
&\text{with respect to the partial ordering $\preceq$ induced by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{ex:Cone for Lp(Omega)}}\\
&\text{subject to }C\in S.\end{split}

(21)

3.3
Continuity of
\Lambda

The following two results are necessary for the proof of the continuity of
\Lambda
.

Lemma 3.3.1
.

Fix a compact
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and define the space
P_{K}\subseteq L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
of continuous and integrable functions whose Fourier transforms have support inside
K
. Then the linear
periodization operator

\Pi:P_{K}\longrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)\quad\text{ such that }\quad f\mapsto\Pi f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f(x+n)

(22)

is well defined and continuous.
Analogously, the linear operator

\Pi_{\not=0}:P_{K}\longrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)\quad\text{ such that }\quad f\mapsto\Pi_{\not=0}f(x)=\sum_{n\not=0}f(x+n)

(23)

is well defined and continuous.

Finally, if
f(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\Pi_{\not=0}f\leq\Pi f
.

Proof.

For any
x\in\Omega
, we have

\begin{split}|\Pi f(x)|=\Big|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f(x+n)\Big|&=\Big|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{f}(k)e^{-2\pi ixk}\Big|\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{f}(k)|,\end{split}

where the second equality is an application of the Poisson summation formula. Since the support of
\widehat{f}
is contained in
K
, the last sum is composed of a finite number of terms not larger than
c_{K}=\#(K\cap\mathbb{Z})
. Thus,
\Pi f(x)
is a well defined function in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Moreover, it is known that
\|\widehat{f}\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{1}
(Theorem 1.13,
[
14
]
). Therefore, we get

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{f}(k)|\leq c_{K}\|\widehat{f}\|_{\infty}\leq c_{K}\|f\|_{1},

and, since this is true for any
x\in\Omega
,

\|\Pi f\|_{\infty}\leq c_{K}\|f\|_{1}.

Hence,
\Pi
is bounded and continuous. The result for
\Pi_{\not=0}
follows from the fact that
\Pi_{\not=0}f(x)=\Pi f(x)-f(x)
, for any
x\in\Omega
.

Finally, condition
\Pi_{\not=0}f(x)\leq\Pi f(x)
is trivial if
f(x)\geq 0
,
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3.2
.

Let
f_{n}\geq 0
be a sequence of functions in
P_{K}
converging to
f\not=0\in P_{K}
in the
L^{1}
-norm. Then the sequence of ratios
\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}/\Pi f_{n}
converges to
\Pi_{\not=0}f/\Pi f
pointwise almost everywhere.

Proof.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be the intersection of all the domains of the functions
\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}/\Pi f_{n}
and
\Pi_{\not=0}f/\Pi f
and let
x\in X
. We know that
\mathbb{R}\setminus X
has measure zero (since all the
f_{n}
and the
f
functions must be entire functions by the Paley-Wiener theorem), so it is sufficient to prove the result on
X
. We have

\begin{split}\Big|\frac{\Pi_{\not=0}f(x)}{\Pi f(x)}-\frac{\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}(x)}{\Pi f_{n}(x)}\Big|&=\Big|\frac{\Pi f_{n}(x)\cdot(\Pi_{\not=0}f(x)-\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}(x))+\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}(x)\cdot(\Pi f_{n}(x)-\Pi f(x))}{\Pi f_{n}(x)\cdot\Pi f(x)}\Big|\\
&\leq\Big|\frac{\Pi_{\not=0}f(x)-\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}(x)}{\Pi f(x)}\Big|+\Big|\frac{\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}(x)}{\Pi f_{n}(x)}\cdot\frac{\Pi f_{n}(x)-\Pi f(x)}{\Pi f(x)}\Big|\\
&\leq\Big|\frac{\Pi_{\not=0}f(x)-\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}(x)}{\Pi f(x)}\Big|+\Big|\frac{\Pi f_{n}(x)-\Pi f(x)}{\Pi f(x)}\Big|.\end{split}

(24)

Notice that
\Big|\frac{\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}(x)}{\Pi f_{n}(x)}\Big|\leq 1
, since
f_{n}\geq 0
. Now fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that for any
n\geq N
we have

|\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}(x)-\Pi_{\not=0}f(x)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\Pi f(x)|\quad\text{and}\quad|\Pi f_{n}(x)-\Pi f(x)|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\Pi f(x)|.

Such
N
exists by
Lemma

3.3.1
. Using these two inequalities in the last expression of
Equation

24
, we get

\Big|\frac{\Pi_{\not=0}f(x)}{\Pi f(x)}-\frac{\Pi_{\not=0}f_{n}(x)}{\Pi f_{n}(x)}\Big|\leq\varepsilon,

which is the thesis.
∎

We are now in the position to prove the result.

Theorem 3.3.3
.

The function
\Lambda:S\rightarrow L^{p}(\Omega)
is continuous for any fixed
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Proof.

Fix
p\in[1,\infty)
. Let
K
be a bounded interval containing the supports of all the autocorrelations of elements in
L^{2}(I_{W})
; for example, we could take
K=[-2W,2W]
. The function
\phi:S\rightarrow P_{K}
such that
\phi(C)=|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma)|^{2}
is well defined, since we showed that
\phi(C)\in P_{K}
in
Lemma

2.2.1
. Moreover, it is continuous, being the composition of the Fourier transform
S\subseteq L^{2}(I_{W})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and the squared absolute value
|-|^{2}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Finally,
\phi(C)\geq 0
for any
C\in S
.

By definition, we have

\Lambda=\frac{\Pi_{\not=0}}{\Pi}\circ\phi.

Therefore, we are left to prove that the operator
\frac{\Pi_{\not=0}}{\Pi}
is continuous.

Let
f_{n}\in S
be a sequence converging to
f\in S
in the
L^{2}
-norm. Then
\phi(f_{n})
converges to
\phi(f)
in
P_{K}
(i.e., in the
L^{1}
-norm) and we can apply
Lemma

3.3.2
to deduce that
\Lambda(f_{n})
converges to
\Lambda(f)
pointwise almost everywhere.

Finally, we know that
|\Lambda(f_{n})|\leq\bm{1}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
. Thus, the result follows by an application of Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem in
L^{p}(\Omega)
.
∎

3.4
Existence of approximate solutions for the minimization problem

In this section we discuss the existence of Pareto optimal solutions of problem (
21
). Unfortunately, determining whether this problem has minimal solutions is very difficult. Even if we managed to prove the continuity of the error shape operator in
Section

3.3
, the noncompactness (actually, the nonclosedness) of
S
in
H
prevents us from deducing the existence of optimal solutions by classical arguments. To circumvent this issue, we limit ourselves to the search for
approximate solutions
, as explained below.

3.4.1
Existence of approximate Pareto optimal solutions

Let us simplify the problem by approximating the space of admissible gridding kernels with a suitable finite-dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(I_{W})
. In this way, we can exploit the continuity of
\Lambda
and prove the existence of
approximate
solutions. More precisely, we will restrict it to the space spanned by the first
L
elements of the Slepian basis defined in
[
39
]
. This basis is composed of the PSWFs, and is known to possess compressing properties in the representation of bandlimited functions. A summary of the most important facts about it is given in
Appendix

B
.

The following result was taken and adapted from
[
23
]
.

Theorem 3.4.1

(Theorems 1 and 3,
[
23
]
, adapted)
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, let
E(\varepsilon)
be the set of functions
f
in
L^{2}(I_{W})
such that
\|f\|_{2}=1
and

\int_{|x|>1/2}|\widehat{f}(x)|^{2}dx=\varepsilon^{2}.

Let
X\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be the space of functions that are Fourier transforms of functions in
L^{2}(I_{W})
. Then, for any fixed
L\geq 0,L\in\mathbb{Z}
, the minimum of

\min_{\{\phi_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L}\subseteq X}\max_{f\in E(\varepsilon)}\min_{\{a_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L}}\Big\|\widehat{f}-\sum_{l=0}^{L}a_{l}\widehat{\phi}_{l}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

is achieved by the first
L+1
PSWFs,
\psi_{0},\dots,\psi_{L}\in X
.

Moreover, if
L\geq\lfloor W\rfloor
, then

\Bigg\|\widehat{f}-\sum_{l=0}^{L}a_{l}\psi_{l}\Bigg\|^{2}_{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{2},

(25)

where the
a_{l}
are the Fourier coefficients of
\widehat{f}
in its expansion in the Slepian sequence and
C
is a constant independent of
f
,
\varepsilon
, and
W
, that may be taken as 12.

This theorem implies that the PSWFs are the best bandlimited basis to represent bandlimited functions. A study on how to improve the value of the constant
C
is carried out in
[
23
]
, to which we refer the reader.

Let
H_{L}\subseteq L^{2}(I_{W})
be the vector space spanned by the first
L+1
elements of the Slepian sequence, and let
S_{L}=\{C\in H_{L}\text{ $|$ }\|C\|_{2}=1\}
be the unit sphere in
H_{L}
. The vector minimization problem becomes

\begin{split}&\text{minimize }\Lambda C\in\Lambda(S_{L})\subseteq H_{L}\\
&\text{with respect to the partial ordering $\preceq$ inherited from $H$}\\
&\text{subject to }C\in S_{L}.\end{split}

(26)

To show the existence of Pareto optimal solution for this problem, we use the following result proved in
[
19
]
.

Theorem 3.4.2

(
[
19
, Theorem 6.9]
)
.

Every weakly compact subset of a partially ordered separable normed space with a closed pointed ordering cone has at least one properly minimal element.

Since
H_{L}
has finite dimension, its unit sphere
S_{L}
is compact. From the continuity of
\Lambda
, the subset
\Lambda(S_{L})
is compact, and,
a fortiori
, weakly compact. Since
L^{p}(\Omega)
is a separable space for
p<\infty

[
2
, Theorem 4.13]
, we can then apply
Theorem

3.4.2
and prove the existence of minimal solutions.

3.4.2
Existence of Pareto optimal solutions with prescribed shapes

In some scenarios, we are more interested in a restricted portion of the spectrum; for example, we may expect it to carry more useful information. In this case, we would like to use a gridding kernel whose error shape is minimal at the meaningful frequencies, while we can allow ourselves to be less accurate on the remaining part. We can use vector optimization to address this task.

Suppose that we are given a function
\eta\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
supported in
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
, which we call
target error shape
. The informal intuition behind
\eta
is that, for each
x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
, the smaller
\eta(x)
is, the more accurate we want to be at
x
; that is, the smaller
\eta(x)
is, the smaller
\|E(-;x,h,C)\|
should be (
E
was defined in
eq.

5
). In other words, the function
1/\eta
could be interpreted as a
utility function
(with the convention that
1/0=+\infty
), in the sense described, for example, in
[
44
, Chapter 4]
: it assigns to each
x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
a
rank
, so that areas with higher ranks should be considered preferred or more important.

In an ideal case, an optimal gridding kernel
C
should satisfy
\Lambda C(x)\leq\eta(x)
for all
x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. However, for a general
\eta
, this inequality cannot be satisfied everywhere, because the error shape operator
\Lambda
is not onto; more precisely, the
L^{1}
norm of
\Lambda C
is bounded from below by a positive constant
\mu
. Thus, if the
L^{1}
norm of
\eta
is less than
\mu
, there is no hope that we could find
C\in S
such that
C(x)\leq\eta(x)
for all
x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. This deep property of
\Lambda
is proved in
Section

3.5.1
, where its connection with the uncertainty principle is also discussed.

This issue represents the core of vector optimization: even if we cannot find
C
such that
\Lambda C\preceq\eta
, we can still find a gridding kernel
C\in S
such that
\Lambda C
is the Pareto front of
\Lambda(S)
and, at the same time, has minimal
p
-distance from
\eta(x)
. This task can be achieved by using the Wierzbicki’s penalty scalarizing functionals defined in
eq.

19
.

More precisely, fix
\rho>1
and define the function

\lambda_{\rho,\eta}:S\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad\lambda_{\rho,\eta}(C)=s_{\rho}(\Lambda C-\eta)=-\|\Lambda C-\eta\|_{p}^{p}+\rho\|(\Lambda C-\eta)_{+}\|_{p}^{p}.

(27)

Then we solve the (classical) minimization problem

\begin{split}&\text{minimize }\lambda_{\rho,\eta}(C)\\
&\text{subject to }C\in S_{L}.\end{split}

(28)

Notice that, as in
Section

3.4
, we restricted our attention to the finite-dimensional Slepian space
H_{L}
defined above (for some
L\geq 0)
. Since
S_{L}
is compact, we see that also
\Lambda(S_{L})
is compact, and thus we have at least one solution by Weierstrass’ theorem (the continuity of
\lambda_{\rho,\eta}
following).

Remark 3.4.3
.

The two terms in the definition of
\lambda_{\rho,\eta}
can be interpreted in the following way (as suggested in
[
46
]
): if
\Lambda C\succeq\eta
for all
C\in S_{L}
(with respect to the order
\succeq
defined by the cone of
L^{p}(\Omega)
), then we should try to decrease the distance between
\Lambda C
and
\eta
to achieve a better solution (thus, the positive term of
\lambda_{\rho,\eta}
wins). If instead
\eta\in\Lambda(S_{L})+D
(where
D
is the cone of
L^{p}(\Omega)
), then the target error has been reached: in this case, we should maximize the distance
\Lambda C
and
\eta
to achieve an optimal solution, that is, a solution lying in the Pareto front of
\Lambda(S_{L})
.

3.5
More properties of
\Lambda

3.5.1
The relationship between
\Lambda
and the uncertainty principle

The behaviour of the error shape operator
\Lambda
is strictly dependent on the uncertainty principle. An easy example is given by the result below, which follows from the Paley-Wiener theorem. Actually, the Paley-Wiener theorem can be interpreted as a (qualitative) uncertainty principle (see, for example,
[
13
]
for a detailed discussion on this topic).

Proposition 3.5.1
.

The error shape operator is always positive; that is,
\Lambda C\succ 0
for any
C\in S
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.2.1
, we know that
\Lambda C\succeq 0
. Suppose that
\Lambda C(x)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
. Then, for all
x
in which
\Lambda C(x)
is defined, we have

\sum_{m\not=0}|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma+m)|^{2}=0,

which
a fortiori
implies
\widehat{C}(x)=0
for almost every
x\in[m-\frac{1}{2\gamma},m+\frac{1}{2\gamma})
for every
m\neq 0
. Since
\widehat{C}
is a holomorphic function, this can happen only if
\widehat{C}(x)=0
for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore,
C
should be the zero function, which is impossible, since
\|C\|_{2}=1
.
∎

A more refined result is based on the work by Slepian, Landau and Pollack on the PSWFs
[
22
,
23
,
39
,
41
,
40
]
. The proof of the previous proposition relies on the extremal properties of the PSWFs, which are a consequence of the uncertainty principle.

Proposition 3.5.2
.

If
\gamma=1
, there exists a positive constant
\Theta
such that
\|\Lambda C\|_{1}>\Theta
, for any
C\in S
.

Proof.

Let
C\in S
. By definition we have

\|\Lambda C\|_{1}=\int_{\Omega}\Bigg|\frac{\sum_{m\not=0}|\widehat{C}(x+m)|^{2}}{\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x+m)|^{2}}\Bigg|dx.

(29)

By
Lemma

2.2.1
, the denominator of the integrand is

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x+m)|^{2}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{C}(\beta)e^{-2\pi ix\beta}.

The autocorrelation
a_{C}
is zero outside
[-2W,2W)
. Thus we have

\begin{split}\Big|\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{C}(\beta)e^{-2\pi ix\beta}\Big|=\Big|\sum_{-2W\leq\beta\leq 2W}a_{C}(\beta)e^{-2\pi ix\beta}\Big|&\leq\sum_{-2W\leq\beta\leq 2W}|a_{C}(\beta)|.\end{split}

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get the estimate

|a_{C}(\beta)|=\Bigg|\int_{-W}^{W}C(\nu)\overline{C}(\nu-\beta)d\nu\Bigg|\leq\|C\|_{2}^{2}=1

Therefore

\sum_{-2W\leq\beta\leq 2W}|a_{C}(\beta)|\leq 4W+1.

Substituting in
eq.

29
, we then get

\|\Lambda C\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{4W+1}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{m\not=0}|\widehat{C}(x+m)|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{4W+1}\int_{|x|>1/2}|\widehat{C}(x)|^{2}dx.

(30)

By
[
39
]
, we know that the function in
S
which minimizes the integral in
eq.

30
is
\psi_{0}
, the first PSWF, and the value of the minimum is
1-\mu_{0}>0
, where
\mu_{0}<1
is the eigenvalue relative to
\psi_{0}
. Therefore we get

\|\Lambda C\|_{1}\geq\frac{1-\mu_{0}}{4W+1}>0.

∎

3.5.2
Shifting property of
\Lambda

The following result is basically a
shifting property
of the gridding error shape operator
\Lambda
. Suppose that we are given a gridding kernel
C
such that
\Lambda C
takes small values in a certain subdomain
X
of the spectrum
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. Applying
Proposition

3.5.3
below, we can translate the accuracy we have in
X
into another subdomain of
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. This has proven particularly useful in the numerical tests we did in
Section

5
.

Proposition 3.5.3
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and let
u(\nu)=e^{2\pi ix_{0}\nu}\in L^{2}(I_{W})
. Then

\Lambda(C\cdot u)(x)=\Lambda C(x-x_{0})

for any
x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
such that
x/\gamma-x_{0}\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
.

Proof.

The result follows from the shifting properties of the Fourier transform. Indeed

\begin{split}\Lambda C(x)=\frac{\sum_{m\not=0}|\widehat{C\cdot u}(x/\gamma+m)|^{2}}{\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C\cdot u}(x/\gamma+m)|^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{m\not=0}|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma-x_{0}+m)|^{2}}{\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma-x_{0}+m)|^{2}},\end{split}

and the last term is
\Lambda C(x-x_{0})
provided that
x-x_{0}\in\big[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\big)
.
∎

4
Numerical implementation

As discussed above, the gridding algorithm consists of two main steps:
i)
a
calibration
step, in which the optimal gridding kernel
C
is determined;

ii)
an
evaluation
step, in which the computed kernel is used to evaluate the nonuniform discrete Fourier transform (NUDFT) of a given signal.

We discuss more in detail the two steps in the following sections.

4.1
Calibration step

In the calibration step, we seek the function
C\in\mathcal{C}(I_{W})
that minimizes the functional
\Lambda
with respect to some reference error profile
\eta
. We accomplish this task by minimizing the scalarizing functional
\lambda_{\rho,\eta}
defined in
eq.

27
with respect to
C
. Specifically, one has to evaluate the gridding kernel on a uniform grid of points in the interval
I_{W}
. These values are then stored and subsequently used as a lookup table to perform interpolation. Similarly, the correcting function
h
must also be computed and stored.
We first detail the procedure for numerically computing
\Lambda
and
h
given a gridding kernel
C
.

4.1.1
Numerical computation of
\Lambda
and
h

Let
\bm{C}=(C_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
n=0,\dots,N-1
,
N\gg 1
, be a vector representing the discretized gridding kernel
C
; that is,
C_{n}=C(\nu_{n})
, where
\nu_{n}=-W+n\cdot\Delta\nu
,
n=0,\dots,N-1
, is a uniform discretization of the interval
I_{W}=[-W,W)
, with
\Delta\nu=2W/N
.

Let
\bm{x}=(-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{M})
, for
m=0,\dots,M-1
, be the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
representing the uniformly sampled frequencies in
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
, for
m=0,\dots,M-1
. We want to compute the value of
\Lambda C
at each
x_{m}
. By
Theorem

2.2.2
, we can write

\Lambda C(x_{m})=1-\frac{|\widehat{C}(x_{m}/\gamma)|^{2}}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x_{m}/\gamma+k)|^{2}}.

(31)

Therefore, we need to calculate
|\widehat{C}(x_{m}/\gamma)|^{2}
and
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x_{m}/\gamma+k)|^{2}
for each
m=0,\dots,M-1
.
Since

|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma)|^{2}=\Bigg|\int_{-W}^{W}C(\nu)e^{-2\pi ix\nu/\gamma}d\nu\Bigg|^{2},\qquad x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}),

(32)

we can discretize it as

|(\widehat{C}(x_{m}))_{m}|^{\odot 2}=|\bm{H}\bm{C}|^{\odot 2}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},\qquad\text{with $\bm{H}=\Delta\nu\cdot(e^{-2\pi ix_{m}\nu_{n}/\gamma})_{m,n}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}$},

(33)

The factor
\Delta\nu
is introduced to take into account the integral sign in
eq.

32
. A more refined quadrature formula could be used in future work. Notice that
|\bm{H}\bm{C}|^{\odot 2}
means the elementwise squared absolute value of the matrix-vector product
\bm{H}\bm{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
. This product can be fast computed through a chirp Z-transform (see
[
33
]
), whose speed is of the same order as the FFT one.

Next, consider the term
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x_{m}/\gamma+k)|^{2}
for each
m=0,\dots,M-1
. By
Lemma

2.2.1
, it can be written as

\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{C}(\beta)e^{-2\pi ix_{m}\beta/\gamma},

(34)

where
a_{C}(\beta)
is the autocorrelation function of
C
. Being
C\in\mathcal{C}(I_{W})
, it is easy to show that
a_{C}\in\mathcal{C}(I_{2W})
. Since
\#([-2W,2W]\cap\mathbb{Z})=4W+1
, we can then define
\bm{a}=(a_{C}(\beta))\in\mathbb{C}^{4W+1}
, so that

\Big(\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{C}(\beta)e^{-2\pi ix_{m}\beta/\gamma}\Big)_{m}=\Big(\sum_{-2W\leq\beta\leq 2W}a_{\beta}e^{-2\pi ix_{m}\beta/\gamma}\Big)_{m}=\bm{K}\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

(35)

where

\mathbf{K}=(K_{m,\beta})\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times(4W+1)}\quad\text{s.t}\quad K_{m,\beta}=(e^{-2\pi ix_{m}\beta/\gamma}).

Notice that
\bm{K}\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
since the Fourier transform of a Hermitian function is real, and the autocorrelation of a function of a real variable is always Hermitian.

Thus, we are left to explain how to compute the vector
\bm{a}
. The autocorrelation of
C
is given by

a_{C}(\beta)=\int_{-W}^{W}C(\nu)\overline{C}(\nu-\beta)d\nu.

(36)

Define
\bm{\tilde{\nu}}=(-2W+k\cdot\Delta\nu)_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
, for
k=0,\dots,2N-1
, representing a uniform discretization of the interval
I_{2W}
, and let
\kappa(\beta)
be the index such that
\tilde{\nu}_{\kappa(\beta)}=\beta
(
N
should be taken so that such index exists, i.e.
N/2W\in\mathbb{Z}
). Then
eq.

36
can be discretized as

a_{C}(\beta)=a_{C,\beta}=\Delta\nu\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{2N-1}C_{k}\overline{C}_{k-\kappa(\beta)},

(37)

with the convention that
C_{k-\kappa(\beta)}=0
if
k-\kappa(\beta)\geq 2N
.

To conclude, the final discretized expression for
\Lambda C
evaluated at the points
x_{m}
is

\bm{\Lambda C}=1-(|\bm{H}\bm{C}|^{\odot 2}\oslash\bm{K}\bm{a})\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

(38)

where
\oslash
means the elementwise division. A word of caution should be spent here: in general,
\bm{K}\bm{a}
can have some zero element, even though the probability is zero by
Lemma

3.2.1
. One needs to handle this case, for example by (quite arbitrarily) setting the value of
\Lambda C(x_{m})
to zero. This will not be an issue, as long as
M
is large enough.

In the same way, we can compute
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
, the vector representing the gridding correcting function at the points
x_{m}
. By
eq.

14
, we know that

h(x)=\frac{\overline{\widehat{C}}(x/\gamma)}{\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma+m)|^{2}}.

(39)

Therefore, we can discretize in the following way

\bm{h}=(h_{m})_{m}=\overline{\bm{H}\bm{C}}\oslash\bm{K}\bm{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{M},\quad m=0,\dots,M-1,

(40)

where
\overline{\bm{H}\bm{C}}
is the elementwise complex conjugate of
\bm{H}\bm{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
.

Pseudocode for computing
\Lambda C
and
h
is provided in
Algorithm

1
,
Appendix

C
.

4.1.2
Optimization of
\lambda_{\rho,\eta}(C)

Once we have a discrete expression for
\Lambda C
, we can choose a reference profile
\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
(depending on our task, see
Section

6.2
), a norm parameter
p\in[1,\infty)
, a parameter
\rho>1
, and minimize the functional given by the composition of
\Lambda
with a scalarizing function
s_{\rho}

\lambda_{\rho,\eta}(C)=s_{\rho}(\Lambda(C)-\eta)=-\|\Lambda C-\eta\|_{p}^{p}+\rho\|(\Lambda C-\eta)_{+}\|_{p}^{p},

(41)

where
(-)_{+}
indicates the positive part.

The choice of
\rho>1
, as seemingly for
\eta
, depends on the specific application. Based on the heuristic proposed at the end of
Section

3.4.2
, one could argue that, at least for a
\eta
below the feasible region
\Lambda(S)
(with respect to the relation order
\preceq
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
), a large value of
\rho
corresponds to a gridding error shape more close to
\eta
in the
p
-norm, while a small value corresponds to a gridding error shape less similar to
\eta
, but smaller at other frequencies of the spectrum. In other words,
\rho
acts as a parameter that allows one to choose how much
\Lambda C
should be similar to
\eta
at the cost of worsening the error at less important frequencies.

Following the discussion in
Section

3.4.2
, we restrict the domain space of the optimization search to the space
S_{L}
spanned by the first
L+1
Slepian functions, where
L
is a tunable parameter. The coefficients of the gridding kernel vector
\bm{C}
in the Slepian basis are defined by two vectors
\bm{c}_{\text{real}},\bm{c}_{\text{imag}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the first representing their real part, and the second the imaginary part. Therefore, if
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(L+1)}
is the matrix whose
l
th column is the discretized version of the
l
th PSWF,
\psi_{l}
, with
l=0,\dots,L+1
, then

\bm{C}=\bm{B}\bm{c}_{\text{real}}+i\cdot\bm{B}\bm{c}_{\text{imag}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}.

(42)

The optimization variables actually used are hence the two vectors
\bm{c}_{\text{real}}
and
\bm{c}_{\text{imag}}
.

The choice of the starting matrix
[\bm{c}_{\text{real},0},\bm{c}_{\text{imag},0}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(L+1)\times 2}
has proven to be tricky. Usually, a random matrix leads to poor results, especially if
W
is not small, likely because of the presence of numerous local minima in the objective function. We found that a good starting point is usually given by the coefficients in the Slepian basis of a kernel which is known to perform well all over the spectrum, such as the first PSWF for the case of
\gamma=1
, or the optimized Kaiser-Bessel function prescribed by Fessler
[
10
]
for higher values of
\gamma
. Such kernels sometimes need to be further corrected to match the shape of
\eta
(especially for the
\gamma=1
case) by exploiting the shifting property of
\Lambda
described in
Proposition

3.5.3
.

Finally, notice that the choice of
W
depends on the application at hand: a larger value of
W
increases the accuracy but it decreases the speed of the gridding algorithm.

4.2
Evaluation step

The evaluation step is described by
eq.

2
and
eq.

3
.
Equation

2
can be quickly computed using the FFT. Calculating the
u^{*}_{k}
terms requires some comments. The gridding function
C(t)
is nonzero only if
|t|\leq W/2
. Therefore,
C(k-\gamma t_{n})
can be non null only if
|k-\gamma t_{n}|\leq W/2
, which can happen for not more than
W+1
terms. This means that the sum in
eq.

3
reduces to a sum of not more than
W+1
, and since
N\gg W
, it can be calculated very quickly.

Notice that
a priori
we do not know the values of
C
at points
(k-\gamma t_{n})
. These have to be computed by interpolating the values we found in the calibration step. An interpolation by splines of 3rd degree seems to suffice.

The gridding algorithm is detailed in
Algorithm

,

.

Technical information

All tests are performed in the following manner: for a given set of parameters

,

makes the loss function

It is important to note that, for most tests, we cannot guarantee that the optimization converged to a global minimum. The algorithm usually terminates for the following two reasons:

-

the norm of the steps is smaller than the chosen tolerance, which is very small (
\text{\tt tol}=10^{-16}
);

-

the number of function evaluations exceeds the chosen maximum, which is very large (
\text{\tt MaxFuncEval}=10^{6}
).

Nevertheless, the resulting kernels perform satisfactorily.

The choice of the initial guess for the coefficients of

in the Slepian basis (see

) is made in the following way: for the case
\gamma=1
, we use a shifted version of the PSWF, while for the case
\gamma=2
we use the Kaiser-Bessel function with optimal parameters as found by Fessler. For other choices of the oversampling parameter

\eta
).

The tests consist of calculating the mean absolute errors (MAEs), i.e. the average absolute difference between the DFT (as in
eq.

1
) and the gridding+FFT at each frequency for certain sets of signals described below. This means that the error is actually a vector of errors, each component representing the error at a certain frequency of the spectrum.
For MIRT-NUFFT, we use the implementation given in
[
11
]
; for our optimized kernels and the PSWF, we use our implementation of the gridding+FFT algorithm.

The formula for the calculation of the MAE vector is

\text{MAE}_{\text{alg}}(x)=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}|y(x;\bm{u}_{s})-y_{\text{alg}}(x,\bm{u}_{s})|,

where

is the number of signals used for the test,

is the

is the nonuniform DFT as defined in

is the Fourier transform computed by the algorithm
\text{alg}\in\{\text{MIRT, PSWF, Ours}\}
.

The sets of signals consist of

signals generated randomly by the following steps:

1.

A number

2.

For each

\eta

3.

For each

is randomly chosen using the uniform distribution in the interval
[0.1,1]
.

4.

The final discrete signal

5.2.2
About the oversampling factor

Finally, we also consider other oversampling factors, namely
\gamma\in\{1.25,1.5,1.75\}
. In general, the same observations above can be noticed for these cases. For small
\gamma
, choosing the first PSWF as initial guess leads to better solutions, while for values of
\gamma
closer to
2
, Fessler’s Keiser-Bessel kernel is preferable. Optimization becomes more difficult as the value of
W
increases.

From the definition of
\Lambda
in
eq.

20
, we see that
\gamma
acts as a zooming factor. Let us stress the dependence of the error shape operator from
\gamma
by denoting it
\Lambda_{\gamma}
, so that

\Lambda_{\gamma}C(x)=\frac{\sum_{m\neq 0}|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma+m)|^{2}}{\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma+m)|^{2}}.

(45)

Then we see that
\Lambda_{\gamma}C(x)=\Lambda_{1}C(x/\gamma)
and, for any
p\in[1,\infty)
,

\|\Lambda_{\gamma}C\|_{p,[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})}=\gamma\cdot\|\Lambda_{1}C\|_{p,[-\frac{1}{2\gamma},\frac{1}{2\gamma})},

(46)

and, for
p=\infty
,

\|\Lambda_{\gamma}C\|_{\infty,[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})}=\|\Lambda_{1}C\|_{\infty,[-\frac{1}{2\gamma},\frac{1}{2\gamma})}.

(47)

This means that, introducing oversampling, the error is basically the same given by the restriction of
C
to
[-\frac{1}{2\gamma},\frac{1}{2\gamma})
. Moreover, we have

\min_{x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})}\Lambda_{\gamma}C(x)=\min_{x\in[-\frac{1}{2\gamma},\frac{1}{2\gamma})}\Lambda_{1}C(x)\geq\min_{x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})}\Lambda_{1}C(x).

(48)

This final inequality shows that for each
x
, the error
\Lambda_{\gamma}C(x)
cannot be smaller than
\Lambda_{1}C(x)
. In particular, if
\Lambda_{1}C(x_{0})
is minimum for some

x_{0}

6
Discussion and conclusions

6.1
Achievements of this work

In this paper, we clarified the meaning of
optimal kernel
in the context of gridding algorithms: we proposed an interpretation of this concept through the lens of vector optimization theory and developed a new technique to find optimal kernels based on it.

The results we were able to obtain were superior to those given by the PSWF, which is usually considered the best gridding kernel for the case without oversampling. In particular, our method was capable of obtaining gridding kernels that, on average, produce errors smaller than those produced by the PSWF, at least in some prescribed areas of the spectrum. For the case with oversampling, and in particular the case
\gamma=2
for which the MIRT-NUFFT is optimized, our method usually lead to comparable or even better performance than Fessler’s optimized Keiser-Bessel kernel.

We believe that the existence of the gridding error shape operator
\Lambda
is important in itself. Indeed, if
C
belongs to the Pareto front of
\Lambda
, then
C
is minimal in a very specific sense that seems to be related to the uncertainty principle. From this point of view, we could argue that the Pareto front of
\Lambda
is composed of those functions that are somewhat
extremal
for the uncertainty principle. Thus, studying the properties of
\Lambda
could lead to a better understanding of the relation between timelimiting and bandlimiting operations, as asked in
[
22
]
.

Yet, we know very little about
\Lambda
and, at the time of this work, we are not even able to determine if
\Lambda
possesses a Pareto front or not, although the results of this paper strongly suggest that the answer should be positive.

6.2
About
\eta

The definition of the target error shape function
\eta
may seem somewhat obscure and requires some clarification. One could consider it as a sort of weight function that describes the maximum error that one would like to obtain at each frequency
x\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. For example, if
\eta(0.25)=10^{-7}
, then one would like to get from the gridding algorithm a spectrum that, at frequency
x=0.25
, differs from the ground truth for no more than
10^{-3.5}
. Therefore,
\eta
defines for each frequency
x
the maximum discrepancy from the ground truth that one would like the reconstructed spectrum to satisfy.

Of course, it can happen that the conditions defined by
\eta
are too restrictive (for example, because violating the lower bound proved in
Proposition

3.5.2
). This is the reason why vector optimization is useful: it lets us find the gridding kernel
C
such that
\Lambda C
is Pareto optimal and as close as possible to
\eta
in some
p
-distance.

It remains to understand how one actually defines
\eta
. It follows from what was said above that the definition depends on the application. For example, suppose that we expect the most meaningful frequencies of a signal to lie in a very small portion of the spectrum. This could be the case of MRI, where only a portion of an image needs to be analysed for a diagnosis, like in
[
4
,
5
,
21
]
. The degree of accuracy that is needed in a certain area of the spectrum has to be decided
a priori
by an external expert (a radiologist in the case of MRI), usually called a
decision maker
, and then translated into a target error by some procedure. This approach is known in literature as that of
interactive methods
, that is, methods which involve the subjective opinion of an expert in the field. There are many, like trial and error, interactive reoptimization, trade-off based methods like Guided Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithm (G-MOEA) and more
[
28
]
. The typical procedure is called the
human-in-the-loop
(HITL) approach and is described in
Figure

8
.

Figure 8:
Human-In-The-Loop approach

Another approach, which does not involve any human decision maker, relies on machine learning. In this case, the idea is to learn
\eta
from the data. Suppose for example that we are given a dataset composed of couples
(X,y)
, where
X
is an image and
y
a label living in some space. In a medical context,
X
could be a MRI scan, and
y
a diagnosis. Then we could try to train a machine learning model to recognize which areas of
X
were the most important in determining the label
y
and label each pixel with an importance score value. In this way, we produce a heat-map, usually called
saliency
or
attention map

[
35
]
, which could be used to extract a function
\eta
: indeed, we would like to have a smaller error in those areas where the importance score is higher. The reader can learn about an application of saliency maps in the detection of glaucoma in
[
25
]
.

The authors intend to investigate this approach in future work.

a fortiori

By the Wiener-Kinchin theorem (Chapter 1.9,

a_{C}(\beta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{C}(x)|^{2}e^{2\pi i\beta x}dx.

(49)

In particular,
|\widehat{C}(x)|^{2}
is the Fourier transform of an integrable function, and thus it is continuous (Theorem 1.13,
[
14
]
).

Now we can apply the Poisson summation formula (Theorem 3.3,
[
14
]
) and the scaling property of the Fourier transform to get

\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{C}(\beta)e^{-2\pi ix\beta/\gamma}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{C}(x/\gamma+m)|^{2}.

(50)

This concludes the proof. Notice that the Poisson summation formula can be applied here precisely because
a_{C}
has finite support and
|\widehat{C}(x)|^{2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Appendix B
The Slepian basis

The following is a free readaptation of some concepts discussed in
[
39
]
, and is meant to give the reader a brief introduction to the Slepian basis.

Let
\mathcal{B}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be the set of squared integrable functions
f(t)
whose Fourier transform
\widehat{f}(\omega)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
vanishes for
|\omega|>\Omega
, for a fixed
\Omega>0
. Thus,
\mathcal{B}
is the set of bandlimited functions, where the band limit is
\Omega
. By definition, if
f\in\mathcal{B}
, then

f(t)=\int_{-\Omega}^{\Omega}\widehat{f}(\omega)e^{2\pi it\omega}d\omega.

(51)

We define the
bandlimiting operator

B:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Bf(t)=\int_{-\Omega}^{\Omega}\widehat{f}(\omega)e^{2\pi it\omega}d\omega,

(52)

and by definition we have that
B
is the identity when restricted to
\mathcal{B}
.

In an analogous way, we can define the set of
timelimited
functions
\mathcal{D}
as the set of functions
f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
f(t)=0
if
|t|>T/2
, where
T>0
is fixed. There is no need to explain why these two operators are important: signals coming from real-world applications are always timelimited, and are usually approximated by bandlimited versions to make them tractable from a computational standpoint. Moreover, bandlimited functions are a very well-studied concept, thanks to ground-breaking results like the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem and the Whittaker-Shannon interpolation formula.

Consider now the composition
BD
. We know that
BDf\neq f
, for all
f\neq 0
; otherwise, we would have a function which is both bandlimited and timelimited, in contrast with the uncertainty principle. In general, the composition
BD
transforms a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
in a member of
\mathcal{B}
with a smaller norm. For the theory of gridding algorithms, it makes sense to ask for those functions which preserve most of their energy after having been transformed by
BD
. Indeed, the error resulting from a certain gridding kernel depends on the behaviour of the kernel outside the interval
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
(see
Theorem

2.2.2
). In particular, if the amount of energy outside
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
is low, the error should be small
[
37
]
. If a function
f
minimizes the amount of energy outside
[-\Omega,\Omega]
, then maximizes the following Rayleigh quotient:

\mu=\frac{\|BDf\|_{2}^{2}}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big|\int_{-\Omega}^{\Omega}\widehat{Df}(\omega)e^{2\pi it\omega}d\omega\Big|^{2}dt}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(t)|^{2}dt}.

(53)

We have

BDf(t)=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\rho_{\Omega}(t-s)f(s)ds,\quad\text{where }\rho_{\Omega}(\tau)=\frac{\sin(\Omega\tau)}{\pi\tau}.

(54)

Notice that
\rho_{\Omega}
is real and symmetric; thus the eigenvalues of its integral operator are real and positive. Therefore, by standard arguments, we can reduce the problem of maximizing
eq.

53
to that of finding the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
BD
:

\lambda f(t)=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\rho_{\Omega}(t-s)f(s)ds,\quad|t|\leq T/2.

(55)

Of course,
\mu=\lambda_{0}
, where
\lambda_{0}
is the maximum eigenvalue.

The eigenfunction corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
is precisely the first prolate spheroidal wave function. The other PSWFs can be defined recursively by maximizing
eq.

53
over the set of squared integrable functions that do not belong to the span of the previous PSWFs; that is,

\psi_{n+1}=\arg\max_{f\in L^{2}\setminus\langle\psi_{0},\dots,\psi_{n}\rangle}\frac{\|BDf\|_{2}^{2}}{\|f\|_{2}^{2}},

(56)

where
\psi_{0},\dots,\psi_{n}
are the first
n+1
PSWFs and
\langle\psi_{0},\dots,\psi_{n}\rangle
denotes their span. By definition, these functions are real, bandlimited and their energy in the time domain is almost completely concentrated in the interval
(-T/2,T/2)
; which is why they are so interesting as gridding kernels. Moreover, it turns out that they have many useful properties. For all
n\geq 0
, denote by
\lambda_{n}
the eigenvalue related to
\psi_{n}
. Then:

-

we have
1>\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{1}>\dots>\lambda_{n}>\dots
;

-

the functions
\psi_{n}
are orthonormal on the real line and complete in
\mathcal{B}
;

-

they also are orthogonal in the interval
(-T/2,T/2)
and complete in
L^{2}((-T/2,T/2))
, and

\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\psi_{m}(t)\psi_{n}(t)dt=\begin{cases}\lambda_{n},&\text{if }m=n\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\quad\text{for all }m,n=0,1,2,...;

-

they are a scaled solution of the following differential equation:

(1-t^{2})f^{\prime\prime}-2tf^{\prime}+(\chi_{n}-c^{2}t^{2})f=0,

(57)

where
c=\Omega T/2
and the eigenvalues
\chi_{n}
are defined as the (discrete) set of real values such that the equation has solution in the closed interval
[-1,1]
.

The set
\{\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{n},\dots\}
is called the Slepian basis. The PSWFs possess many more properties; we refer the reader to the monograph
[
31
]
for a detailed and complete discussion.
