Flow IV: Counterfactual Inference In Nonseparable Outcome Models Using Instrumental Variables
Marc Braun, Jose M. Peña, Adel Daoud

Abstract
To reach human level intelligence, learning algorithms need to incorporate causal reasoning. But identifying causality, and particularly counterfactual reasoning, remains an elusive task. In this paper, we make progress on this task by utilizing instrumental variables (IVs). IVs are a classic tool for mitigating bias from unobserved confounders when estimating causal effects. While IV methods have been extended to non-separable structural models at the population level, existing approaches to counterfactual prediction typically assume additive noise in the outcome. In this paper, we show that under standard IV assumptions, along with the assumptions that latent noises in treatment and outcome are strictly monotonic and jointly Gaussian, the treatment–outcome relationship becomes uniquely identifiable from observed data. This enables counterfactual inference even in non-separable models. We implement our approach by training a normalizing flow to maximize the likelihood of the observed data, demonstrating accurate recovery of the underlying outcome function. We call our method Flow IV .



1
Introduction

Estimating causal effects is a fundamental goal in many scientific fields, including the social and medical sciences. However, traditional machine learning approaches rely on purely associational relationships to perform inference. Therefore, authors such as
Pearl 2018

(
Pearl 2018
)
or
Schölkopf et al. 2021

(
Schölkopf et al. 2021
)
argue that, to achieve human level intelligence, AI models need to move from an associational to a causal perspective. A typical causal question is how a treatment variable
A
causally affects an outcome variable
Y
—e.g., how physical exercise influences cholesterol levels. The gold standard to answer such questions is randomized controlled trials (RCTs), where treatments are assigned randomly to individuals in the population. In reality, however, RCTs are often infeasible due to ethical, logistical, or financial constraints, leaving researchers to rely on observational data.

Causal inference from observational data requires assumptions about the data-generating process, such as the absence of hidden confounders—unobserved variables that influence both treatment and outcome
(
Hernan and Robins 2025
;
Lin, Pena, and Daoud 2023
)
. In the example above, age may affect both how much a person exercises and their cholesterol level but might not be observed. This can be modeled by allowing the noise terms
\varepsilon_{A}
and
\varepsilon_{Y}
affecting
A
and
Y
to be dependent
(
Pearl 1995
)
.

Beyond estimating average causal effects, we may wish to ask
counterfactual
questions, i.e. questions asking about the outcome under an alternative treatment, while the actual treatment and outcome was observed. In the example introduced above, a counterfactual questions would be e.g. ”What would an individual’s cholesterol level have been had they exercised eight hours per week when we observe that the actual cholesterol level is 180 mg/dL and they exercised three hours per week?” Such questions go beyond interventions and require modeling the individual-level mechanisms that generate outcomes under alternative treatments. This level of reasoning—
the counterfactual level
—is at the top of Pearl’s causal hierarchy
(
Pearl 2009
)
and is the focus of this work. A formal definition of counterfactual inference can be found in Section Problem Setup.

Instrumental Variables (IVs) provide a powerful framework for identifying causal effects in the presence of unobserved confounders. A variable
Z
is an IV if it influences the treatment but affects the outcome only through that treatment. In linear settings, IVs have long been used to estimate causal effects. More recent work has extended IV methods to nonlinear and nonparametric models, even allowing for counterfactual inference. However, existing approaches typically assume additive, also referred to as separable noise in the outcome equation.

In practice, the outcome function may not be separable. For instance, the effect of aging on the cholesterol-level may depend on how much someone exercises. In this paper, we remove the additive noise assumption and show that counterfactual inference is still possible under alternative structural assumptions. Specifically, we prove that if (i) a valid instrument is available, (ii) the latent noise terms are jointly Gaussian, and (iii) the treatment and outcome are strictly monotonic (bijective) functions of their respective noises, then the structural function from treatment to outcome is identifiable. This allows counterfactual reasoning even in nonseparable models. This reasoning in turn will provide with any causal learner to produce consistent counterfactual estimates for any causal system semi-parametricaly.

We propose
Flow IV
, a method that uses normalizing flows to approximate the structural functions by maximizing the likelihood of the observed data under our assumptions. Our approach offers a flexible way to model complex outcome functions beyond standard additive noise models.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we review related work and contrast the assumptions required by existing methods. Section
3
formalizes our problem setup and IV assumptions. In Section
4
, we present our identifiability result and describe the Flow IV method. Simulation and real-world application results are shown in Section
5
. This application focuses on dispersement of local aid and infrastructural projects in Africa
(
Dreher et al. 2021
)
, an area that attracts considerable attention in global development
(
Jerzak, Johansson, and Daoud 2023
;
Daoud and Johansson 2024
;
Daoud and Dubhashi 2023
)
. We close with a discussion and future directions in Section
6
.

2
Related Work

Utilizing the existence of IVs to estimate causal effects has a long tradition
(
Wald 1940
;
Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin 1996
)
. We formally introduce the definition IV in the next section. The classical IV estimator assumes a linear relationship between
Z
,
A
, and
Y
. This means it models the outcome
Y
as
Y=\beta A+\varepsilon_{Y}
. Assuming that this model reflects the true underlying data generating process (DGP) is what we refer to as
linearity
assumption. In the simplest setup with a single instrument,
\beta
can then be estimated using the so called Wald estimator
\hat{\beta}=\mathrm{Cov}(Y,Z)/\mathrm{Cov}(A,Z)

(
Wald 1940
)
. We refer to this approach as
Usual IV
.

When the linearity assumption is violated, the Usual IV estimator can be used to estimate the complier average treatment effect
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
. In the setting where the instrument corresponds to the treatment assignment, the complier average treatment effect is the causal effect for the subpopulation that takes the treatment when they are assigned the treatment and vice versa.
There is a vast body of literature that utilizes IVs to identify such local average treatment effects (LATE), i.e. causal effects for specific subpopulations
(
Imbens and Angrist 1994
;
Robins 1994
;
Imbens 2010
)
. However, this is not the scope of this paper as we focus on counterfactual prediction tasks.

More recent research relying on the existence of IVs has used deep neural networks to model more complex, nonlinear relationships between the variables. Those approaches can be categorized based on Pearl’s causal hierarchy into approaches that allow counterfactual inference and approaches that only enable the estimation of population level, also referred to as interventional, causal effects. While the former category does not rely on the linearity assumption, they do require an additive, also called separable, noise component in the outcome function
(
Hartford et al. 2017
;
Singh, Sahani, and Gretton 2019
)
. Formally, the
separability
assumption implies that the underlying DGP of
Y
can be written as
Y=g_{Y}(A)+\varepsilon_{Y}
where
g_{Y}
is some nonlinear function. Under this setup,
(
Hartford et al. 2017
)
propose the
Deep IV
framework, where the problem is divided into two prediction tasks. In each stage, deep neural networks can be used. The first network aims at predicting the treatment from the outcome whereas the second network models
g_{Y}(A)+\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon_{Y}]
. To counteract the spurious correlations between
A
and
Y
, their loss function when training the second network involves integrating over the treatment distribution conditioned on the instrument.

If counterfactual inference is not desired, other methods have been developed that can estimate causal effects from observations of data generating processes of arbitrary nonseparable structure
Y=g_{Y}(A,\varepsilon_{Y})

(
Chesher 2003
;
Imbens and Newey 2009
;
Guo and Small 2016
;
Hahn and Ridder 2017
;
Puli and Ranganath 2020
)
. These methods typically use control function approaches, where the treatment
A
is first regressed on an instrument, and then the outcome
Y
is regressed on
A
and the residual (or control function) from the first stage. This residual accounts for endogeneity by controlling for the unobserved component that jointly affects treatment and outcome. Control function approaches trade the ability to perform counterfactual inference for dropping the separability assumption. Additionally, they require the mapping between the treatment noise and the treatment to be a bijection. Because the scope of our paper is on counterfactual inference, we do not compare Flow IV to any control function approach in Section
5
.

Problem Setup

We now introduce the notation and define the general structure of the type of DGPs this paper aims to perform counterfactual inference for.

Let
Z
,
A
, and
Y
be one-dimensional continuous random variables where we call
Z
the instrument,
A
the treatment, and
Y
the outcome.
Z
is an IV if it satisfies the following three conditions.

1.

Relevance condition

Z\mathrel{\not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp}A

2.

exclusion restriction

Y(z,a)=Y(z^{\prime},a)\ \text{ for all }z,\ z^{\prime}

3.

marginal exchangeability

Y(a,z)\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\perp}Z\ \text{ for all }z,\ a

under intervention

.

,

, and

\varepsilon_{Z}
is independent of
\varepsilon_{A}
and
\varepsilon_{Y}
. The joint distribution of
\varepsilon_{A}
and

Note that, under this data generating process,
Z
is a valid IV and
g_{Y}
can be any separable or nonseparable function.

The goal of this paper is to perform counterfactual inference of the form ”What would the value of
Y_{i}
for individual
i
be, had the value of
A
been at the unobserved level
a^{\prime}
and when we actually observe
Y_{i}=y,\ A_{i}=a,\ Z_{i}=z
. We express this counterfactual outcome formally as
Y_{i}(a^{\prime})\mid Y_{i}=y,\ A_{i}=a,\ Z_{i}=z
. Answering these types of counterfactual queries requires three steps
(
Pearl 2009
)
.

1.

Abduction
, where we infer the latent value
\varepsilon_{Yi}
from the observed
(A_{i},Y_{i})
,

2.

Action
, where we replace the value of
A_{i}=a
with
a^{\prime}
, and

3.

Prediction
, where we estimate the counterfactual outcome as
Y_{i}=g_{Y}(a^{\prime},\varepsilon_{Yi})
.

The abduction step requires invertibility of
g_{Y}
w.r.t.
\varepsilon_{Y}
, which explains why all methods presented in Section

that are able to perform counterfactual inference require some form of strict monotonicity. While
(

)
and
(

)
don’t mention it explicitly, note that additive noise models are of course also strictly monotonic w.r.t. the noise. Another necessity to perform the abduction and the prediction steps is knowledge of the function
g_{Y}
. Next, we show how we can find an approximation of
g_{Y}
using the proposed Flow IV method.

4
Approach

We start this section by introducing the main identifiability result that Flow IV builds upon and then show how to implement Flow IV to approximate SCM
1
.

Identifiability of the Structural Causal Model

First, we formally state the two assumptions required for the identifiability result.

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
(\varepsilon_{A},\varepsilon_{Y})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
with correlation
\rho
, where additionally we assume without loss of generality that all marginals of
\varepsilon
are standard normal.

We use the notation
\mathcal{N}_{2}(\rho)
to denote the bivariate normal distribution with standard normal marginals and correlation coefficient
\rho
. The assumption that the marginals are standard normal can be made without loss of generality, because the noise terms can always be transformed as
\Phi^{-1}(F_{\varepsilon_{x}}(\varepsilon_{x}))\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and the inverse transformation can then be performed as part of the function
g_{x}
for all
x\in\{Z,A,Y\}
.
\Phi
denotes the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution and
F_{\varepsilon_{x}}
the CDF of
\varepsilon_{x}
. This assumption does not change the observational, interventional, or counterfactual distribution.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that
g_{A}
and
g_{Y}
are strictly monotonic w.r.t.
\varepsilon_{A}
and
\varepsilon_{Y}
respectively. Without loss of generality we assume that they are strictly increasing, formally expressed as
\frac{\partial g_{A}(Z,\varepsilon_{A})}{\partial\varepsilon_{A}}>0
and
\frac{\partial g_{Y}(A,\varepsilon_{Y})}{\partial\varepsilon_{Y}}>0
.

Theorem 1
.

Under the causal structure specified by the DAG in Figure
1
and under assumptions
1
and
2
, there exists no function
\tilde{g}_{Y}
for any given value
\tilde{\rho}\neq\rho
such that

1.

\tilde{g}_{Y}
satisfies Assumption
2
and

2.

the distribution of
(\tilde{Z},\tilde{A},\tilde{Y})
generated by
\tilde{Z}=g_{Z}(\varepsilon_{Z}),\tilde{A}=\ g_{A}(\tilde{Z},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{A}),\ \tilde{Y}=\tilde{g}_{Y}(\tilde{A},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{Y})
with
(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{A},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{Y})\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}(\tilde{\rho})

is equal to the distribution of
\left(Z,A,Y\right)
generated by SCM
1
.

Note that Theorem
1
does not require any assumptions about the separability of
g_{Y}
but is valid both for separable and nonseparable outcome function.

The theorem immediately implies that
\rho
is identifiable from the distribution of
(Z,A,Y)
once
g_{Z}
and
g_{A}
are known. The intuition behind the proof is that if the function
\tilde{g}_{Y}
existed, it would always have to be a function of
Z
to be able to generate the correct distribution. However, that
\tilde{g}_{Y}
is a function of
Z
contradicts our model setup and specifically the exclusion restriction of the IV
Z
. The proof of Theorem
1
can be found in the appendix.

We can furthermore state the following results. Because
g_{A}
and
g_{Y}
are strictly increasing, there is no other strictly increasing function
\tilde{g}_{Y}
that produces the observed distribution of
(Z,A,Y)
under the actual noise distribution of
\varepsilon
other than
\tilde{g}_{Y}(A,\varepsilon_{Y})=g_{Y}(A,\varepsilon_{Y})
. Because the noise distribution is entirely determined by our identifiability result in Theorem
1
, it follows that
g_{Y}
is identifiable too.

Implementation of Flow IV

We now proceed to show how we can approximate the functions
g_{Z}
,
g_{A}
, and
g_{Y}
. We use normalizing flows to model the bijective mapping between the latent Gaussian space
\varepsilon
and the observed space
(Z,A,Y)
. Hence, we refer to our proposed method as
Flow IV
. So called causal graphical normalizing flows (cGNFs) have been commonly used in the causal inference literature before
(
Balgi, Daoud, and Peña 2022
;
Javaloy, Sanchez-Martin, and Valera 2023
;
Balgi et al. 2024
)
. They learn a conditional normalizing flow
h_{i}
from standard normal noise
\varepsilon_{i}
to each observed variable
V_{i}
conditioned on the parents of
V_{i}
in the corresponding causal DAG. Thereby, they resample autoregressive-flows
(
Kingma et al. 2016
)
but utilize the causal ordering and independencies specified by the DAG. Using this structure, the conditional flow functions then correspond to
g_{Z}
,
g_{A}
, and
g_{Y}
as specified in SCM
1
.

\displaystyle\hat{Z}

\displaystyle=h_{Z}(\varepsilon_{Z};\theta_{Z})

\displaystyle\hat{A}

\displaystyle=h_{A}(\hat{Z},\varepsilon_{A};\theta_{A})

\displaystyle\hat{Y}

\displaystyle=h_{Y}(\hat{A},\varepsilon_{Y};\theta_{Y})

We use Neural Spline Flows
(
Durkan et al. 2019
)
for the flow functions. Each
h_{i}
is then a one-dimensional monotonic rational spline, where the parameters of the spline are given by the output of a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). The MLP takes the conditioning variables (i.e. the parents of
V_{i}
) as input, and the spline then calculates
V_{i}
as a function of
\varepsilon_{i}
.

The parameters
\theta=(\theta_{Z},\theta_{A},\theta_{Y})
of the conditional flow functions in the cGNF can be optimized via maximum likelihood estimation given observations
\mathcal{D}=\{(z_{1},a_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(z_{n},a_{n},y_{n})\}
.

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D};\theta,\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f_{\text{obs}}(z_{i},a_{i},y_{i};\theta,\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\log f_{\varepsilon}\left(h^{-1}(z_{i},a_{i},y_{i};\theta,\rho)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left.\log|\det\mathbb{J}_{h^{-1}(z_{i},a_{i},y_{i})}|\right)

Here,
f_{\text{obs}}(z_{i},a_{i},y_{i})
and
f_{\varepsilon}
are the density functions of the observed variables
\hat{Z},\hat{A},\hat{Y}
and the unobserved
\varepsilon
respectively,
h^{-1}(z,a,y)=(h_{Z}^{-1}(z),h_{A}^{-1}(Z,A),h_{Y}^{-1}(A,Y))
, and
\mathbb{J}_{h^{-1}(z_{i},a_{i},y_{i})}
denotes the Jacobian matrix of
h^{-1}
evaluated at
z_{i},a_{i},y_{i}
. The second equality can be derived via the change of variables formula.

In contrast to traditional applications of cGNFs, where it is assumed that there are no hidden confounders affecting the observed variables, we assume that
\varepsilon_{A}
and
\varepsilon_{Y}
are dependent random variables and that their dependence structure can be expressed via the parameter
\rho
of the density function
f_{\varepsilon}
. Since for
n\rightarrow\infty
we know according to Theorem
1
that
\rho
is identifiable, we use the log-likelihood function to find the values of
\theta
and of
\rho
that, according to the likelihood, best fit our observations.

\displaystyle(\hat{\theta},\hat{\rho})=\arg\max_{\theta,\rho}\log\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D};\theta,\rho)

In our experiments presented in the next section, we found that decomposing the log-likelihood into three components helps to guide the optimization process.

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D};\theta)

\displaystyle=\log\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{D};\theta_{Z})+\log\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{D};\theta_{A})

\displaystyle\qquad+\log\mathcal{L}_{3}(\mathcal{D};\theta_{Y},\rho)

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{D};\theta_{Z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f_{Z}(z_{i};\theta_{Z})

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{D};\theta_{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f_{A\mid Z=z_{i}}(a_{i};\theta_{A})

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{L}_{3}(\mathcal{D};\theta_{Y},\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f_{Y\mid Z=z_{i},A=a_{i}}(y_{i};\theta_{Y},\rho)

For each of the terms we can use the change of variables formula to express the (marginal / conditional) density of any of the observed variables as a function of (marginal / conditional) densities of
\varepsilon
. For large enough
n
we know that the optimum of each of the components is approximately equal to the optimum of their sum. This means that we can first optimize the parameters of
h_{Z}
, then the parameters of
h_{A}
, and lastly the parameters of
h_{A}
jointly with
\rho
. This procedure corresponds to our intuition behind Theorem
1
which states that, knowing
g_{Z}
,
g_{A}
and the joint distribution of
(Z,A,Y)
, we can identify
g_{Y}
and
\rho
under the given assumptions.

5
Experiments

In this section, we evaluate our Flow IV method in different experimental settings by comparing it to the Deep IV approach and as a baseline to the Usual IV approach. Furthermore, we use Flow IV and Deep IV to reanalyse the impact of Chinese foreign aid on a region’s economic development as performed by
Dreher et al. 2021
. We do not use any control function approaches as a comparison, because they are not able to perform counterfactual inference (see Table
1
).

Performance Metric and Synthetic Data Generation

As an evaluation metric, we use the expected squared prediction error for counterfactual outcomes or counterfactual MSE for short. We define it as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(Y(a^{\prime})-\hat{Y}(a^{\prime}))^{2}\mid A=a,Y=y\right]

Here, the expectation is over
(z,a,y)\sim F_{\text{obs}}
and
a^{\prime}\sim F_{A}
where
F_{\text{obs}}
is the observed distribution and
F_{A}
is the marginal observed distribution of
A
. Whenever the DGP simulates
(\varepsilon_{A},\varepsilon_{Y})\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}(\rho)
and we do not specify that we fix
\rho
, the expectation is also over
\rho\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1)
. We draw 20,000 samples from these distribution and use Monte Carlo estimation to estimate the expected value. Note that this is only possible for synthetic data, because only then we can calculate the true counterfactual outcome
Y(a^{\prime})\mid A=a,Y=y
.

We consider three different synthetic DGPs with two cases where the Flow IV approach exceeds existing approaches and a case where its assumptions break. In the first DGP, the outcome is a nonlinear function w.r.t. the treatment but has a separable (and therefore strictly monotonic) Gaussian noise term. We call it DGP 1 and it satisfies the assumptions of Deep IV and Flow IV.
In the second DGP,
Y
is generated from a nonseparable function that is strictly monotonic w.r.t. the Gaussian noise term. Therefore, only the assumptions of Flow IV are satisfied. In a third setup, we consider a linear outcome model, but do not generate the noise terms
(\varepsilon_{A},\varepsilon_{Y})
from a bivariate normal distribution. Therefore, Assumption
1
required for Flow IV is violated while the linearity and therefore also the separability assumptions of Usual IV and Flow IV respectively are fulfilled.

\displaystyle Y=\left(\sin{(A+1.5)}+1\right)\cdot\varepsilon_{Y}

Our experiments illustrate that one approach is not strictly better than another at performing counterfactual inference, but that it depends on which assumptions are fulfilled and which are violated. According to this assessment, the best approach for a specific problem at hand has to be chosen.

We furthermore assess the convergence rate of the three different approaches by training the models on an increasing amount of data and calculating the counterfactual MSE. We used DGP 3 but with Gaussian noise
(\varepsilon_{A},\varepsilon_{Y})\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}(\rho)

It can be seen that the Usual IV seems to converge the fastest while Flow IV and Deep IV need more training data to achieve comparable performance. This can be explained with the fact that the assumptions of the Usual IV are much more strict than the assumptions of the other two methods. Therefore, Usual IV includes a much stronger inductive bias which decreases variance, and because the assumptions are fulfilled, the bias is zero. Figure

Discussion

We proposed the method Flow IV to perform counterfactual inference in nonseparable outcome models. In addition to the usual IV assumptions, Flow IV relies on two assumptions to correctly identify the outcome function. The first one is strict monotonicity of the treatment and outcome noise on the treatment and outcome respectively, and the second one is that the dependence structure between the two noise terms has to be Gaussian. We empirically illustrate that, if the Flow IV assumptions are fulfilled but the outcome function is nonseparable, our approach outperforms existing methods in counterfactual inference tasks. Assessing if when the Flow IV assumptions are satisfied in a real-world setting remains the task of domain experts. In future research, we aim at translating the Flow IV assumptions about the noise terms to assumptions about hidden confounders and independent noise. A first step in this direction has already been done by showing how bivariate Gaussian noise can be equivalently expressed as hidden confounder
(
tba 2025
)
. This result would simplify the task of assessing whether the Flow IV approach is suitable for a specific problem. Furthermore, we plan to generalize the identifiability result and Flow IV approach of this paper to arbitrary DAGs. So far, we have only considered the case of continuous random variables. In practice, variables are often discrete and adapting our approach to handle such variables remains an open task.

Computational Setup

Training the models in this study was performed on a RTX 3060 GPU with 12 GB of VRAM running on Linux. The code is implemented in PyTorch where the exact version of all packages used to run the experiments can be found in the published code repository. We did not perform any hyperparameter tuning but instead used a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with three hidden layers and 20 hidden units per layer as the network for the Deep IV model for all experiments. We used the same structure for the conditioning network in the Flow IV approach and a quadratic monotonic rational spline with 8 bins in a single flow step. The Flow IV model used in all experiments then consisted of three such flow layers. Both models were optimized for 3000 optimization steps with Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001.
