Global Recommendations for the Use of Diagnostic Genomic Sequencing in the Prenatal Setting on Behalf of the ESHG and ISPD
Authors: Zandra C Deans, Lina Basel Salmon, Kwong Wai Choy, Guido de Wert, Zirui Dong, Lilian Downie, Francesca Romana Grati, Sabine Hentze, Paula Jorge, Brynn Levy, Luca Lovrecic, Dora Mayen, Álvaro Mendes, Michael Morris, Ashley Pritchard, Raquel Gouveia Silva, Tina‐Marié Wessels, Francesca Forzano, Natalie J Chandler
Published: 2026

Introduction

Diagnostic genomic sequencing is increasingly being implemented in the prenatal setting for fetuses with structural anomalies identified by imaging. Such testing provides information for multiple purposes that is, guiding treatment decisions, preparation for the birth of an affected child or termination of a pregnancy. Some national committee's statements have been published [1]. However, to date there is only one international position statement on the use of genome‐wide sequencing for prenatal diagnosis issued by the ISPD in 2022 whereby caution was recommended as to testing eligibility criteria and which findings should be reported [2]. Since then, the use of prenatal genomic sequencing has rapidly developed with more countries implementing genome sequencing rather than capture‐based methods [3, 4], due to significant improvements in detecting copy‐number and structural variants and the evolution of new sequencing platforms utilising long‐read technology. Moreover, improvements in fetal imaging and earlier scans have allowed more accurate fetal phenotyping, and in combination with increased availability of genomic sequencing is evolving our understanding of the prenatal features of genetic diseases. This has created a need to update recommendations to respond to this rapidly advancing field.

The International Society for Prenatal Diagnosis (ISPD), and the European Society of Human Genetics (ESHG), identified the need for standardisation and guidance on the delivery of diagnostic exome‐ and genome sequencing, hereafter referred to as genomic sequencing in the prenatal setting. These professional bodies have collaborated to produce this position statement with high‐level recommendations for appropriate clinical practice during the evaluation of pregnant couples at increased risk for genetic anomaly in the fetus. The aim is to provide a framework for the delivery of diagnostic prenatal genomic sequencing to reduce the risk of harms and maximise the benefits of genomic analysis by providing a diagnosis. The framework is designed to provide guidance for those implementing a prenatal sequencing clinical service and to support a gap analysis of current services to improve practice and patient care. It also outlines how to provide a high quality and accurate prenatal genomic sequencing‐based diagnostic service whilst taking into account medical, ethical and legal considerations. It covers diagnostic testing on invasively obtained samples and not non‐invasive exome sequencing as a screening assay or for research interests. It is focused on genome sequencing or exome sequencing using short‐read next‐generation sequencing (NGS) technology with targeted analysis of specific regions of the genome, although future directions are discussed.

Details on Review and Approval

A writing group from the ESHG's Policy and Ethics Committee (PEC), ESHG's EuroGentest Committee (EuC) and the ISPD Board prepared the draft, which was reviewed by PEC and EuC. It was made available for ISPD membership consultation from 23rd September 2025 until 13th October 2025, and ESHG membership from 3rd March 2026 until 31st March 2026. The authors have integrated suggestions where appropriate. The Board of ESHG and ISPD approved the final version in June 2026.

Indications

Clear eligibility criteria for testing must be available and adhered to. These are:

A current pregnancy with a fetus with a major single anomaly or multiple organ system anomalies that are suggestive of an underlying genetic aetiology (beyond indications typically addressed by chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) recommendations).

A current pregnancy with a fetus with a major single anomaly or multiple organ system anomalies that are suggestive of an underlying genetic aetiology (beyond indications typically addressed by chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) recommendations).

A parental history of a prior undiagnosed fetus (or child) affected with a major single or multiple anomalies suggestive of a genetic etiology, and a recurrence of similar (major or minor) anomalies in the current pregnancy, where a genetic diagnosis could not be reached after karyotype or CMA for the current or prior undiagnosed pregnancy.

A parental history of a prior undiagnosed fetus (or child) affected with a major single or multiple anomalies suggestive of a genetic etiology, and a recurrence of similar (major or minor) anomalies in the current pregnancy, where a genetic diagnosis could not be reached after karyotype or CMA for the current or prior undiagnosed pregnancy.

In select scenarios, a current pregnancy in which there is a fetus with a multiple (minor) anomaly ‘pattern’ that strongly suggests a single gene disorder.

In select scenarios, a current pregnancy in which there is a fetus with a multiple (minor) anomaly ‘pattern’ that strongly suggests a single gene disorder.

In families with a history of recurrent stillbirths of unknown etiology after karyotype and/or CMA, where the fetus in the present pregnancy has a recurrent pattern of anomalies.

In families with a history of recurrent stillbirths of unknown etiology after karyotype and/or CMA, where the fetus in the present pregnancy has a recurrent pattern of anomalies.

If there is no major anomaly detected by scanning and only isolated findings, for example, isolated increased nuchal translucency, isolated poly/oligohydramnios, fetal growth restriction etc. then there is not enough evidence to recommend genomic sequencing at present [5]. There is currently very limited evidence that supports routine genomic sequencing on fetal tissue obtained from an invasive prenatal procedure (amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling (CVS), cordocentesis, other) for indications other than fetal anomalies; this would amount to screening. With a diagnostic yield of ∼1% in asymptomatic fetuses and no large prospective studies, the harms related to invasive procedures combined with the risk of variant misinterpretation currently outweigh the clinical benefit [6, 7]. When prenatal diagnosis is performed for couples with high risk of a child with a specific monogenic disorder then a single gene test should be performed rather than a wider genomic analysis.

Phenotyping

In the postnatal setting the classification of variant pathogenicity can be aided by accurate phenotyping. The American Colleges of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines include the PP4 criterion, which refers to a direct match to the phenotype [8, 9]. Similarly, the ClinGen Sequence variant group recommendation for de novo evidence PS2/PM6 also depends on the strength of phenotypic fit [10]. However this is a challenge for interpreting prenatal sequencing findings as prenatal phenotyping is typically less comprehensive than postnatal phenotyping due to the inherent limitations in ultrasound assessment. While prenatal features for many disorders are catalogued in the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database, the information is often incomplete or not fully representative of the disorder's entire phenotypic spectrum. Furthermore, with improvements in ultrasound quality and access, the knowledge of prenatal presentations of genetic disorders is rapidly improving and evolving. An international initiative is currently underway to expand the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) with terms specific to prenatal phenotypic descriptions [11, 12, 13]. Some phenotypes are lethal, leading to their identification primarily or exclusively through prenatal testing.

In cases of nonspecific fetal ultrasound findings, such as a ventricular septal defect (which may be either monogenic or multifactorial), determining a direct causative relationship between pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant(s) and the observed fetal anomaly can be challenging. In such instances, the identified variants may be reported as either (possibly) diagnostic or incidental. On the other hand, due to the limited knowledge of prenatal phenotypes and the possibility of previously unreported phenotypic expansions, an observed variant may still be considered diagnostic, even in the absence of a fetal phenotype traditionally associated with the disorder. Generally, the rarer the observed feature or combination of features, the more specific the phenotype is. Categories of disorders by specificity include recognizable dysmorphic features (which are typically not relevant in the prenatal context except in rare cases), specific laboratory or imaging findings (some of which are relevant prenatally), and the presence of rare anomalies or unusual combinations of anomalies, among others [8, 14].

Due to incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, presentation of the same condition might vary, with manifestations occurring either prenatally or postnatally, even within the same family. Phenotypes may also evolve throughout the pregnancy, particularly for neurological conditions. Manifestation can occur early in pregnancy (e.g., Fraser syndrome) or as late as the third trimester (e.g., overgrowth‐related syndromes). It is recommended to monitor the pregnancy with repeated ultrasound scans, as the evolution of the fetal phenotype may allow for reclassification of the pathogenicity of the variant. Lethal phenotypes skew the datasets as they may have different inheritance patterns then previously associated with a gene or with variants in genes not linked to disease [15].

The focus is usually primarily on the fetus and may result in overlooking atypical or subtle clinical signs in parents or siblings. Furthermore, the clinical data on parental phenotypes available to variant interpretation teams is often limited. In a postnatal study, a discrepancy between the reported clinical findings in proband's family members and the presumed disease‐causing variant segregation was observed in 7.6% of diagnostic exomes. In 75% of these cases, the designation of “affected” versus “unaffected” status for one of the family members was revised by the referring clinician after the variant interpretation team requested additional phenotyping [16].

Often the father is not seen or examined clinically and may self‐report as unaffected bringing issues when segregation analysis is required.

Multidisciplinary Teams

The use of multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) in genomic medicine for decision‐making is well established and has been shown to improve diagnostic yield [17, 18]. The role of the MDT is especially crucial in cases where parents opt for continuation of pregnancy with a suspected or confirmed rare disease in the fetus, sometimes requiring fetal interventions [19]. The MDT can be held in various formats, for example, face to face, virtually, electronically, and there must be a record of the outcomes and a clear definition of who makes the final decision. The MDT should ideally be comprised of, at a minimum of, fetal medicine specialist, laboratory geneticist, and clinical geneticist or genetic counsellors, or specialist with expertise in genetics but may also include, neonatologists, radiologists, pathologists and any other relevant specialists. An ethicist may be included in order to facilitate systematic reflection on inherent moral complexities (see below). The MDT should be able to provide an immediate and long‐term prognosis and this has already been well established in specific fetal anomalies [20]. In presentations with high uncertainty, the MDT serves to gather expert opinion and present unified information to families.

There are different timepoints at which MDT can be utilised, for example pre‐test MDT for case selection, pre‐reporting for results interpretation and reporting decisions, and post‐test for ongoing clinical management and counselling requirements. For selection of cases, experts in fetal phenotyping will be essential for example ultrasound specialists, maternal fetal medicine clinicians and clinical geneticists. As the use of prenatal sequencing increases, there is an emerging understanding of fetal phenotypes [21]. MDT serves as a platform in which ongoing learning and upskilling can occur. This is important as large cohort studies have shown that the diagnostic yield of prenatal exome sequencing is highly dependent on the fetal phenotype [5, 22, 23].Determining which pregnancies are appropriate for prenatal sequencing is essential for good practice and will optimise the utility of the test. As families and patients will have different needs and views of the utility of the test, the involvement of genetic counsellors or other healthcare workers delivering the counselling with an understanding of the family's values, beliefs and needs is essential. Genomic scientists are an essential part of post‐test MDT to aid in variant interpretation and reporting and ideally would also be part of pre‐test MDT to advise on suitability of test strategy for the fetal indication and pre‐reporting to aid result interpretation.

Genetic Counselling

It is essential for people being offered prenatal genomic sequencing to receive pre‐ and post‐test counselling as the results can be complex. Patient‐centred counselling is an essential component of prenatal care and needs to remain paramount with the introduction of complex information such as possible genomic sequencing [24, 25]. Prenatal diagnosis is a time of emotional vulnerability for families, during which decision‐making and consent processes can be particularly challenging with evidence suggesting that wider family units can influence decision making of parents in direct and indirect ways, making it clear what decisions are acceptable [26]. There is an onus on the healthcare professional to identify and respond to the patient's needs to ensure that the salient points for that specific family are addressed and understood, considering cultural backgrounds, languages, values and ability to understand the complex information being delivered. Referrals may also be required to psychological care (Table 1). With the increasing utilisation and mainstreaming of genomic technologies, provision of balanced written and/or electronic information materials combined with decision support tools is recommended to support and augment face to face genetic counselling [27]. This helps to tailor information to individual families, increase understanding and retention of information, and aid families in making decisions congruent with their beliefs and values. It is suggested by parents as a way to ease some of the challenges encountered in discussing decisions and outcomes with family members [26, 28, 29]. Families should understand the possible outcomes of the testing including a diagnosis for the pregnancy, a diagnosis or carrier information about a parent, if and in which cases variants of uncertain clinical significance (VUS) or incidental findings (IFs) will be reported, and the implications of a non‐diagnostic result or uninformative test. Consideration of these outcomes, in the context of what is already known in the pregnancy from imaging or other testing and the setting, for example access to termination of pregnancy, should be taken into account in the pre‐test counselling (Table 1). It should also be stated during genetic counselling that classification of a variant can change as genetic knowledge changes, which may have psychological/emotional repercussions.

Ideally, a Clinical Geneticist or Genetic Counsellor would facilitate the discussion, but it is recognised that this is not always possible due to limited availability or Genetic Counsellor roles not being established. It is imperative that the healthcare professional providing counselling in this context should be appropriately trained to support the families in their decision making. Counselling of sub‐optimal quality, may adversely impact the quality (and validity) of the consent given and subsequently patient outcomes [22]. Central to the consent process is the voluntary nature of the test, along with careful consideration of what the results reveal in the present and their potential to lead to future discrimination and legal consequences which will vary by jurisdiction [30].

The choice of what to report from prenatal sequencing requires a balance between the parental ‘right to know or not know’ and the future child's medical and informational interests and rights, including its ‘right to not know’. Parents may require sequencing results for prenatal decision‐making and perinatal care, as well as for delivery management, and, when applicable, newborn healthcare management. The provision of such information may be fully in line with professional responsibilities. Conversely, the future child's/adolescent's ‘right for an open future’, more specifically, its right to informational self‐determination implies caution in prognostic “labelling” the future individual, as this may adversely impact their right to not to know both genetic predispositions and susceptibilities for late(r) onset disorders and recessive disorder carrier status. Importantly, we are only just beginning to start understanding the impact on children who have been identified as carriers in early childhood [31].

Prenatal Genomic Sequencing

Sequencing technologies are used to detect disease‐causing variants in the genome. Disease‐causing variants can largely fall into different categories: (1) single nucleotide variants (SNVs); (2) small sequence variants that include deletion and duplication (indels; < 50 base pairs); (3) SVs that either change copy number or are copy‐balanced such as insertions, inversions or translocations (copy‐balanced SVs) and (4) repeat expansions. Initially fetal sequencing studies have been focused on detecting SNVs and indels however genomic sequencing strategies are more commonly being utilised for all variant types [22].

There are two different parts to a laboratory workflow—the region of the genome that is sequenced (referred to below as the testing strategy) and what is analysed by the bioinformatics pipeline (referred to as analysis strategy). It is important to understand that if a genome is sequenced this does not equate necessarily to all regions being included in the analytical strategy. A recent systematic review shows the differences in testing and analytical strategies applied prenatally and highlights that there are deficiencies in current analytical reporting practices throughout literature studies preventing full interpretation of the results and understanding of limitations [32].

There are a number of different testing strategies, and the technologies fall into two main categories: capture based where the region of interest is enriched (by hybridisation with amplification removing the genome background), and amplification‐free genome sequencing where most of the genome is sequenced (Table 2). Capture‐based testing strategies commonly used in the prenatal setting range from phenotype‐based small panels of genes such as non‐immune hydrops fetalis which contain the coding regions of 1–100s of genes, clinical exome sequencing (coding regions of ∼5000 genes listed as causing disease in the OMIM database) [33] to exome sequencing (almost all coding sequence of protein‐coding genes—∼20,000 sequenced). The most common method in use according to a recent prenatal sequencing survey [34] is exome sequencing but there is increasing movement to genome sequencing [3, 4].

There are advantages and disadvantages to all approaches, and these are summarised in Table 2. As always the proportionality criteria requires careful balancing. The smaller panels are associated with lower costs, and the more specific captures mean there are fewer challenging variants to interpret, thereby reducing workload, and less risk of incidental findings which need to be handled sensitively. However, panel content is inconsistent and challenging to update, meaning that the diagnostic yield can be lower than using a wider approach. As the majority of disease‐causing variants occur in the coding regions, exome sequencing is clinically appropriate and has been demonstrated to have high diagnostic yield for SNVs and indels with some laboratories additionally reporting similar sensitivity (but not similar coverage) to CMA for CNVs [35]. A disadvantage to exome sequencing is that disease‐causing non‐coding genes or deep intronic/promoter regions are usually not included in the capture and therefore can be missed. In addition, breakpoints for SVs cannot accurately be mapped using capture‐based approaches making these challenging to detect or accurately characterise. Genome sequencing, although more costly than capture‐based methods, has advantages primarily due to the method being amplification or amplification‐free. This means that there is more uniform and complete coverage of the genome and fewer artefacts in the sequencing data (by eliminating capture and PCR artefacts), the laboratory library preparation time is also considerably faster (approximately 0.5 vs. 2 days) which is important when results are required rapidly. Due to the availability of genome‐wide sequencing data, there is better detection of SVs meaning that parallel CMA may be unnecessary. Some centres are using low‐pass genome sequencing as a replacement of CMA as it enables a higher resolution of identifying chromosomal structural abnormalities [36]. Mitochondrial DNA is also sequenced but not commonly analysed as its clinical utility in the prenatal setting still needs to be demonstrated.

There are a range of analytic strategies used which fall into three main categories (Table 3), (1) panel‐based analysis approaches where a selection of genes is applied bioinformatically to exome/genome sequencing data. These are either phenotype‐based panels or panels of genes associated with a prenatal phenotype. The advantages of gene panel analysis include easier and faster result interpretation due to fewer VUSs, lower data interpretation costs, and a reduced risk of encountering professional and ethical dilemmas, and linked with the principles of proportionality, respect for autonomy and justice. Gene panel analysis is most effective for well‐defined phenotypes; however, limiting the analysis to a specific gene list may result in underdiagnosis in prenatal cases with atypical or previously unreported features. Studies indicate that only about 68% of fetuses diagnosed with a monogenic disorder through exome sequencing exhibit typical features [37]. (2) panel‐agnostic inheritance‐based filtering where only variants that fit the expected inheritance pattern associated with the gene are filtered with the requirement to sequence trios and (3) panel‐agnostic phenotype‐based filtering through the use of HPO terms and variant‐prioritising tools [38], although it should be noted that many of these tools also consider inheritance in ranking variants. In addition, strategies used are often complex with combinations of the above that is, inheritance‐based filtering within a panel or panel agnostic along with phenotype‐based panels in case of incomplete penetrance, taking into account fetal sex in X‐linked disorders and regions with absence of heterozygosity related to chromosomal regions of known imprinting disorders/suspected uniparental inheritance. In reality, it is a diagnosis which is being sought, therefore if one is not found by gene panel analysis, then the analysis could be extended if and as appropriate for solving the clinical question. The parents have to be adequately counselled, taking account of their needs and values, as precondition to give informed consent. All types of possible findings, including IF, should be discussed with the couple with further detail provided below.

All methodologies have limitations, and these must be taken into account within the prenatal context (Table 2). For example, not all methods can detect all genetic causes of disease, for example, imprinting disorders, short tandem repeat disorders can be challenging, and the presence of pseudogenes and mosaicism can render analysis difficult.

Family Structures

De novo genomic variants are the most common cause of severe developmental disorders, accounting for 76% of severe likely monogenic developmental disorders without prior diagnosis from CMA and/or single‐gene testing [39]. A study conducted in the prenatal setting showed that 47% of fetuses with ultrasound anomalies and suspected monogenic causes undergoing exome sequencing have disease‐causing de novo variants [40]. Analysing sequencing data of the parents and the proband as a trio, compared to the proband alone, was reported to increase the diagnostic yield by ∼10% in a postnatal setting [41]. Trio testing enables identification of de novo variants and clarification of whether two variants in the same gene with autosomal recessive inheritance are inherited from each parent (in trans). In addition, trio testing shortens the turnaround time by eliminating the need for separate parental variant segregation studies [39]. Nevertheless, although trio sequencing is the preferred model, it is important to keep in mind potential pitfalls in variant interpretation when testing both the parents and the fetus, including identifying unexpected parentage. Variants in genes associated with disorders characterised by imprinting, variable expression and reduced penetrance, if inherited from a parent with minimal or unreported symptoms, may be mistakenly interpreted as non‐disease‐causing [42]. Duo or proband‐only sequencing may be considered when biological contributors are unavailable. Where eligibility criteria exist in an ongoing pregnancy, if a fetal sample cannot be obtained, it is considered appropriate to offer sequencing for both biological parents to look for shared carrier status for autosomal recessive pathogenic variants that might explain the fetal phenotype. However, where possible, obtaining tissue from a previous abnormal fetus or child for sequencing is preferable.

Samples

Dependent on the gestational age, either CVS or amniocentesis can be performed. Using amniocytes means the fetus is being directly tested but as CVS is from the placenta this needs to be considered in the analysis of the test results in case of confined placental mosaicism (CPM). To minimise the risk of CPM then recommended protocols should be used for the dissection on the CVS to ensure that there are cells from the mesenchymal core or culturing the CVS tissue prior to DNA extraction if time permits [43, 44]. Limitations of CVS must be recognised with regard to epigenetic disease as methylation may not be completed. DNA extraction methods in the laboratory should be optimised and validated for these sample types and sizes particularly as amniocytes can yield low amounts of DNA. Parental samples are usually represented by blood samples, but other samples such as saliva can also be utilised if the laboratory has validated the procedure with their testing strategy.

Bioinformatics

A clinical bioinformatics pipeline consists of primary, secondary, and tertiary analytic steps. Primary analysis involves the conversion of raw signal data generated by the sequencer to nucleotide bases and associated quality data. Secondary analysis aligns the resultant sequence reads to a reference genome and performs variant calling at discordant sites. Finally, tertiary analysis provides biological annotation and filters for variants most likely to be disease‐causing [45]. Checks at each step should be embedded to ensure that the data/results are of sufficient quality and have adequate depth and breadth of coverage to enable reliable analysis. Recommendations have been detailed in Table 4.

Parallel Testing

Prenatal sequencing should progress without the need to wait for cell cultures as genome/capture‐based methods all have low input sample options which can be used following validation for prenatal use. However, cell culture is still recommended to enable confirmation or further studies if required. Turnaround times (TATs) are critical if results are impacting on pregnancy management but must be balanced by efficiency and cost. A recent survey shows the most commonly reported turnaround time for prenatal sequencing is 3 weeks [34]. It is important to take into account turnaround times based on the gestational age at which the test is carried out. Diagnosis can be made through rapid targeted testing, such as rapid aneuploidy testing which are delivered by a range of techniques that is, quantitative PCR assay or FISH. These are quick, low‐cost tests and therefore should be performed prior to sequencing. Laboratories must determine at what stage maternal cell contamination is tested for (stand‐alone and/or with assistance from the bioinformatics pipeline), and when sequencing, what level of maternal cell contamination is deemed acceptable before sensitivity is impacted due to bioinformatic pipeline variant allelic frequency (VAF) thresholds.

If testing for CNVs is not incorporated in the sequencing analysis, then this should be performed as a parallel test using CMA. There is a high degree of variation on whether laboratories analyse CNVs from sequencing data [34] and referrers should check with the testing laboratory whether parallel testing is required.

Confirmatory Testing

Confirmatory testing can either be required to confirm the presence of the variant (i.e., a true call) or to confirm the variant is present in the fetus or confined to the placenta (i.e., a true call but not related to the fetus). The analytical validation of NGS detection methods includes acceptable read depth, coverage, quality score and identity confirmations, which have been addressed in the Bioinformatics section and also elsewhere [46, 57, 58]. However, even with strict metrics there are still false positive variants that are filtered in by pipelines such as sequencing or PCR artefacts. Variants at a lower VAF than expected can be challenging to determine if the variant is mosaic or a false positive. If the sample tested is from CVS, then there is also the possibility of confined placental mosaicism and maternal cell contamination levels also need to be considered in interpreting variants with lower VAFs. Due to the nature of short read sequencing larger heterozygous indels can often occur at lower variant allele frequencies and it can therefore be harder to determine if these are truly heterozygous or mosaic. Even for variants in the expected VAF range, indels are often called incorrectly by the bioinformatics pipeline for example an indel of two bases can be called as two different changes and this results in the wrong amino acid change being called. Homologous regions such as genes with pseudogenes also need to be considered when deciding if confirmation testing is required. It is recommended that laboratories should have performed audits for all variant types that have had confirmatory testing including known true positive and false positive variants to determine the quality thresholds that must be met for a variant to be determined to be a true positive without the need for confirmation. If these thresholds are met, then it is acceptable to report a variant without a confirmatory test.

For variants that fall below these thresholds then variant classification should be performed including reviewing the phenotypic fit. If the variant is deemed reportable then confirmatory testing by an orthogonal validated method is required. The methodology employed for confirmatory testing is dependent upon the variant type and size. SNVs/small indels may be confirmed by Sanger sequencing but this is not a suitable test if mosaicism is suspected as it has limited sensitivity to VAFs of around 10% [62] and therefore digital PCR or amplicon‐based PCR‐NGS can be used. For CNVs there are multiple methods depending on the size (quantitative PCR, digital PCR, chromosomal microarray, multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (MLPA) are all options). Copy balanced structural variants are more challenging to confirm, and if too small for karyotyping, would require bespoke assays developing such as inversion/breakpoint PCRs. If confined placental mosaicism is a consideration then testing an alternative sample type is recommended.

Parental and Placental Mosaicism

If a variant appears to be de novo then it is important that NGS alignment files for the parents are checked as low‐level somatic mosaicism in the parents may be filtered out (due to VAF filters) which has implications for recurrence risk. Two studies demonstrate that low level parental somatic mosaicism is present in ∼5% (4.4% & 5.7%) of all de novo cases [62, 63]. As the depth of sequencing is not consistent for every base or across samples, laboratories should consider adding the depth of sequencing in parental samples at the position of the variant in question in the report when reporting de novo variants to aid the clinical team in their counselling. When low level somatic mosaicism is detected then recurrence risk could be as high as 50%.

If low‐level somatic mosaicism is not detected it is important to consider potential gonadal mosaicism when assessing recurrence risk for apparent de novo variants.

While CPM for chromosomal aberrations is well‐documented, recent evidence from deep genome sequencing demonstrates that genetic heterogeneity at the level of small sequence variants is ubiquitous in early placental development [64]. However, it should be noted that despite extensive trophoblast mosaicism for SNVs and small indels, the statistical likelihood of it involving exactly the specific nucleotide in a candidate gene to cause a pathogenic change is extremely low, as the vast majority of these postzygotic variants are located in non‐coding regions. Nonetheless, as CVS samples can harbour cell clones with postzygotic variants not present in the fetus, the identification of a mosaic pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in placental tissue, regardless of whether it matches the current fetal phenotype or if it relates to a condition that may manifest later in development or postnatal life, should be followed by confirmatory testing on amniocytes, which represent the gold standard for verifying the true fetal genotype. If such a P/LP variant is identified on CVS and does not correlate with the fetal phenotype, it may fall into the category of an IF.

Variant Classification and Reporting

Variant classification should be performed according to variant classification guidelines relevant to the centre and this should be evident on the report [8, 9, 65]. While sequencing technologies provide resolution at single nucleotide level, it is fundamental to recognize that when these tools are used to detect and interpret copy number changing SVs, the interpretive expertise must remain rooted in cytogenetic principles, as is the case for any other technology identifying such variants (e.g., CMA, karyotype, FISH, Optical Genome Mapping (OGM)). Briefly, these guidelines use codes at varying strength for different types of evidence to determine the pathogenicity of a variant such as presence in population databases or affected probands in the literature, in silico tools and inheritance pattern. Note that for the ACMG guidelines there have been multiple follow up publications on the use of specific criteria (e.g., for loss of function variants (PVS1) [66] or de novo criteria (PS2/PM6(10)) and different rules for specific genes or phenotypes (such as rasopathies) [67]. It should therefore be clearly stated on the report which guidelines have been applied to classify the variant. Initial variant classification should be performed by the laboratory team members with evidenced competency in the process involved and if complex then reviewed by another competent individual prior to reporting. There are no specific variant classification guidelines for prenatal sequencing at present, however as a rule, only P/LP variants that are related to the phenotype and compatible with the mode of inheritance should be reported. However, in agreement with the referring clinician or discussion at MDT, laboratories might report a VUS where there is a high level of supporting evidence and it is found in a gene that strongly correlates with the fetal phenotype, also commonly known as a ‘hot VUS’. In such cases, it is crucial to evaluate both phenotypic compatibility and specificity and familial phenotype data [14].

Most disease‐related variant databases, including ClinVar [53] and the Human Gene Mutation Database [68], do not provide specific annotations distinguishing between prenatal and postnatal testing settings. It is also a challenge where the phenotype can be more severe prenatally then postnatally or variable for example variants in ARSL [69], RASA1 [70], EPHB4 [71] and many other genes. Such variants may be present in population databases such as gnomAD making these variants challenging to classify. In addition, inherited variants for ‘unaffected’ parents do not fit with expected inheritance pattern and will not filter in if using solely inheritance filtering in the bioinformatics pipeline.

X‐linked novel hemizygous missense variants inherited from carrier mother are very challenging in the prenatal setting, as there is no evidence applicable for the fact that the inheritance pattern fits with disease mechanism. This means that, to determine a variant to be pathogenic, it is required to either have a strong level of phenotypic evidence which, unless biochemical testing is available, is very difficult to achieve in prenatal setting due to partial phenotype and limitations of investigations, or segregation studies requiring several male relatives to be tested in a short time frame, which is not always feasible. One study demonstrated the challenges for one particular gene (ARSL) where all variants were classified as VUSs using ACMG criteria [69]. As a result, variants of this group may often classify as low scoring VUSs and therefore not be reported/discussed with the MDT [17].

VUS reported during pregnancy may later be reclassified either as likely pathogenic or as likely benign, based on new clinical data i.e., an evolving phenotype or a non‐genetic confirmatory test may be available during pregnancy (e.g., demonstration of enzyme deficiency in amniocytes for biallelic DHCR7 variants). For some disorders, early postnatal treatment can significantly improve prognosis if initiated before symptoms appear or early in the disease course. Reporting VUS may be reasonable if further classification is possible or if the condition is treatable and in such cases the report should recommend additional early phenotyping after birth [72, 73, 74, 75].

Previously unreported diagnostic pathogenic variants identified in affected individuals might be classified as VUS if detected in fetuses without visible abnormalities. In a study of 156 patients with neurodevelopmental disorders diagnosed through postnatal exome sequencing, 24% (16/66) of causative nonprotein‐truncating and noncanonical splicing variants would have been classified as VUS if sequencing had been conducted during pregnancy due to the lack of a specific phenotype [76].

Reporting of susceptibility loci for neurodevelopmental disorders will depend on local policy and may differ depending on if the finding may explain all or part of the observed fetal phenotype or is an IF.

Reporting

The reporting of the result(s) must be in a clear, concise format which minimises any misinterpretation. There are multiple recommendations [9, 77, 78, 79] on how to report germline genomic test results which must be followed whilst complying with International Standards. The family can receive a copy of the report, and this should be taken into consideration when using technical or medical terminology. The scope and limitations of the testing performed must be outlined for the reader of the report to interpret the findings in the context of the clinical scenario. Appropriate identifiers must be used to identify which pregnancy the testing relates to. Where results are directly related to the parents, then these must not detract from the fetal result and therefore should be reported separately. An example report is supplied as supplementary information.

Reanalysis

The interpretation of the sequencing results is based on various fetal imaging modalities, but this is limited compared to examination after birth. In some cases, with no prenatal diagnosis, postnatal reanalysis of the sequencing data might achieve a genetic diagnosis. The laboratory's reporting policies should be considered, in particular whether VUS are reported in a prenatal setting and if a panel was used for analysis. If not, then a variant relevant to the postnatal phenotype may be reported in a postnatal reanalysis. To optimise the benefit of reanalysis, thorough postnatal phenotyping should inform the analysis. This should be the subject of a new referral to test (reanalysis of existing data).

Result interpretation will be based on current knowledge and potential changes over time are likely to occur therefore reanalysis of the data may reveal the causative variant. Families should understand if re‐analysis is performed routinely by the laboratory or if it would need to be initiated by a clinical interaction. Due to the evolving nature of genomic interpretation, all families should be offered a postnatal review of the prenatal genetic results prior to additional use of that information, for example for a subsequent prenatal or preimplantation test.

Non‐Diagnostic Findings

In the context of broad genomic sequencing, the main types of variants detected in individuals tested include primary, or diagnostic findings (PF), IFs, and secondary findings (SF). PFs are variants related to the indications that prompted the testing. IFs are unintentionally discovered, clinically relevant findings not related to the primary referral reason that were not actively sought [80, 81]. SFs are variants not related to the referral reason but actively looked for from a predefined gene list defined on the ground of potentially medically actionable implications during lifetime. In parents of a proband, reproduction‐related findings (RRF) may also be identified (e.g., carrier status for the same disorder in both parents), which are relevant for future pregnancies or for the medical management of an already born child. Non‐PF variants must be interpreted with caution as there is the absence of phenotypic data compared to indication‐based testing results [82]. A careful phenotypic evaluation and a long‐term management strategy should be applied when IFs or SFs are identified. Additionally, the detection of such findings, whether in prenatal or postnatal setting, may raise ethical dilemmas about what information should be disclosed [83]. These considerations include severity of the disorder, age of onset, its penetrance, and availability of effective treatments [84, 85]. In a prenatal setting, the timing of disclosure can be particularly sensitive, since disclosing certain findings may lead to pregnancy termination. Certain disorders may not be relevant until later in life, raising questions about when disclosure is most appropriate and local policy and legislation must be adhered to.

Moreover, in some instances, it is impossible to definitively establish a causative relationship between a detected variant and an observed fetal abnormality, making it unclear whether the variant is diagnostic or incidental. The functional impact of a previously unreported, potentially disease‐causing variant in a fetus can be challenging to predict [86]. For fetuses whose phenotype becomes evident as the pregnancy progresses or only becomes noticeable after birth, what is initially classified as an IF may later be reclassified as a PF [87].

While IF cannot be completely avoided during sequencing, there are many views on whether to analyse and report them, therefore the laboratory must have a policy dependent upon the local and national position for both the fetus and parents.

ISPD guidelines [2] state that fetal IFs should only include genes associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, intellectual disabilities, or metabolic conditions that are highly penetrant and known to cause moderate to severe childhood disorders. For parental IFs, each parent should provide separate consent for inclusion or exclusion of the reporting of IFs and it must be recognised that the sequencing of parental DNA is performed to aid the analysis and interpretation of the fetal exome. Full analysis of the parental exome/genome is not part of the test being performed, although it may be interrogated at a different time for different purposes.

Genomic sequencing provides a chance to detect IFs that could be significant for fetal and newborn health due to availability of effective prenatal or postnatal treatments or interventions. Criteria have been proposed for consideration of reporting actionable fetal findings and lists of treatable disorders affecting fetuses and newborns have been compiled [88].

The ACMG guidelines advise reporting incidental P/LP variants from exome sequencing [87]. Fu et al. [89]. reported IFs in 6.1% of cases in which prenatal exome sequencing was performed to evaluate fetal malformations. Regardless of variant classification, it is currently recommended by ACMG [87] that: (1) IFs without a known fetal or childhood phenotype should not be reported; (2) fetal carrier status for autosomal recessive and X‐linked disorders (females only) unrelated to the test indication are not to be reported.

Concerning SFs, there is no existing clear recommendation in prenatal settings [90], while in postnatal setting there is considerable variation in approaches.

The ESHG recommendations on opportunistic genomic screening [91] argue that actively searching for a list of SFs would amount to a form of screening, which cannot be recommended as standard of care. When considered in postnatal settings, it should be embedded in a research or pilot framework requiring proper informed consent and adequate collection of data on the overall process, to accrue data on its potential effectiveness. Moreover, ESHG suggests that searching for later‐onset conditions in genomic sequencing data in children is premature. While the Recommendations focus on the postnatal settings, the argumentations and conclusions are even more relevant in a prenatal setting. It is unclear how the search for SFs in such a critical and distressing situation, would be helpful in empowering prospective parents and support them in making difficult decisions over the pregnancy. As European example, under French law the search for SFs is prohibited, but IFs are permitted to be included in laboratory reports provided to patients [1]. In the USA, the ACMG permits that SFs according to a defined gene list, could be reported from prenatal genomic sequencing, unless future parents choose to opt out [2, 87, 92].

Screening Fetuses With No Identifiable Phenotype

It is not recommended to employ prenatal genomic sequencing as a screening test in pregnancies with no ultrasound anomalies. Variant classification criteria were developed for diagnosis with phenotypic information. A limited prenatal phenotype or testing a pregnancy without scan anomalies makes variant interpretation more challenging. Although not currently recommended by professional societies, predictive prenatal sequencing is increasingly being performed in some countries on fetuses with no detectable sonographic anomalies as a screening test [93, 94, 95, 96] as more than half of children diagnosed with monogenic neurocognitive disorders did not have a (detectable) prenatal phenotype [95]. Additionally, among those who reported prenatal findings, only 23.8% had documented fetal structural abnormalities, and another 23.8% had other ultrasound anomalies, such as fetal growth restriction, polyhydramnios, or elevated nuchal translucency, which may not have represented an indication for prenatal sequencing. A systematic review including four studies incorporating 1916 fetuses showed that 1.6% of phenotypically normal fetuses with a normal G‐banded karyotype or CMA may have a P/LP variant identified on prenatal exome sequencing, most of which occur de novo. It was determined that the likelihood of a variant being associated with severe disease in such fetuses is 0.5% [7]. A potentially supportive argument is that the ACMG recommends reporting IFs in neurodevelopmental genes, even if unrelated to the fetal phenotype, with parental opt out [87].

Such testing raises significant professional, medico‐legal, and ethical concerns, see Genetic Counselling section [6]. Any predictive prenatal sequencing should be treated like other genomic ‘screening’ tests (reproductive carrier screening and genomic newborn screening) where the thresholds of reporting are more stringent. Only P/LP variants with compatible inheritance pattern should be reported. This is important to reduce false‐positives and the associated anxiety and possibility of a termination of an unaffected fetus. Other challenges include unknown penetrance data for many conditions, detection of disease‐causing variants in a mosaic state, the inability to validate results with another method such a biochemical test or clinical examination and the potential for the variant to be reclassified over time which may alter the diagnosis. The use of genomics in newborn screening is now being piloted in multiple large international studies [97] and may help to inform the future use of genomics in healthy children. It may help to provide penetrance data, add guidance on curation of variants in a screening setting and refine the conditions that are appropriate to look for in the screening setting. These data will provide a useful body of knowledge which could inform future developments in prenatal testing.

Data Sharing and Storage

Data sharing supports the interpretation of variants through resources such as ClinVar [53] however there are limitations when it comes to interpreting in the prenatal setting. It is not currently possible to search for prenatal specific variants therefore the International Fetal Genomics Consortium are developing a dedicated database of variants identified in fetuses and associated phenotypes to aid the data interpretation. It is also important to update HPO terms associated with conditions as we learn about prenatal phenotype. Data storage is very important in the setting of prenatal diagnosis, particularly where the fetus/baby dies. However, data might have an expiration date shorter than fetal DNA/sample, as it might not be readable using future software. Furthermore, there may be a cost associated with data storage, and it may be cheaper to re‐sequence at a later date. The sharing and storage of data must comply with international and national regulations. The European Union has the General Data Protection Regulation in place [98], and many countries have similar legislation which must be adhered to.

Quality Assurance

The International Organisation for Standardisation (ISO) [99] has globally recognised standards tailored to ensure safe healthcare systems including laboratory testing and reporting. It is important that laboratories providing diagnostic fetal sequencing operate according to appropriate nationally or internationally recognised quality standard, for example ISO 15189 [100]. This provides service users with confidence that the laboratory has processes in place to ensure high quality testing performed by a trained and competent workforce. To comply with the ISO standard, laboratories must participate in regular external quality assessments (EQAs) or proficiency testing (PT) for fetal sequencing and clinical reporting of the results. This is an external mechanism to demonstrate accuracy of the testing and relevant knowledge to report the findings in a fetal setting as well as early detection of a suboptimal service so corrective and preventative actions can be taken. EQA/PT also brings an educational element through use of educational samples, exercises, sharing of evolving methods and testing strategies. In European Union countries, the IVDR (Regulation (EU) 2017/746) provides the legislative context for in vitro devices which includes genetic tests [101]. Clinical centres should be supported to deliver high quality patient services either through EQA/PT, peer networks, ongoing training and continual medical education of which aid the continued development of the fetal sequencing service globally.

Future Perspectives

Genomic advancements have transformed the diagnosis of individuals with suspected genetic disorders. However, in more than half of the cases, the diagnosis remains inconclusive therefore new technologies give opportunities to increase diagnostic yield. Technology moves fast and options for additional testing methods RNA sequencing (RNA‐Seq), long‐read sequencing, OGM or epigenetic signature analysis. Emerging future strategies are discussed in the Supplementary material.
