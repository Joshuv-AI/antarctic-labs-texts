Belief Propagation guided decimation on random k k -XORSAT ∗
Arnab Chatterjee, Amin Coja-Oghlan, Mihyun Kang, Lena Krieg, Maurice Rolvien, Gregory B. Sorkin

Abstract
. We analyse the performance of Belief Propagation Guided Decimation , a physics-inspired message passing algorithm, on the random k k -XORSAT problem. Specifically, we derive an explicit threshold up to which the algorithm succeeds with a strictly positive probability Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \Omega(1) that we compute explicitly, but beyond which the algorithm with high probability fails to find a satisfying assignment. In addition, we analyse a thought experiment called the decimation process for which we identify a (non-)reconstruction and a condensation phase transition. The main results of the present work confirm physics predictions from [Ricci-Tersenghi and Semerjian: J. Stat. Mech. 2009] that link the phase transitions of the decimation process with the performance of the algorithm, and improve over partial results from a recent article [Yung: Proc. ICALP 2024]. Introduction and results

1.1.
Background and motivation

The random
k
-XORSAT problem shares many characteristics of other intensely studied random constraint satisfaction problems (‘CSPs’) such as random
k
-SAT.
For instance, as the clause/variable density increases, random
k
-XORSAT possesses a sharp satisfiability threshold preceded by a reconstruction or ‘shattering’ phase transition that affects the geometry of the set of solutions
[
2
,
12
,
17
,
24
]
.
As in random
k
-SAT, these transitions appear to significantly impact the performance of certain classes of algorithms
[
6
,
16
]
.
At the same time, random
k
-XORSAT is more amenable to mathematical analysis than, say, random
k
-SAT.
This is because the XOR operation is equivalent to addition modulo two, which is why a
k
-XORSAT instance translates into a linear system over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
In effect,
k
-XORSAT can be solved in polynomial time by means of Gaussian elimination.
In addition, the algebraic nature of the problem induces strong symmetry properties that simplify its study
[
3
]
.

Because of its similarities with other random CSPs combined with said relative amenability, random
k
-XORSAT provides an instructive benchmark.
This was noticed not only in combinatorics, but also in the statistical physics community, which has been contributing intriguing ‘predictions’ on random CSPs since the early 2000s
[
19
,
22
]
.
Among other things, physicists have proposed a message passing algorithm called
Belief Propagation Guided Decimation
(‘
\mathtt{BPGD}
’) that, according to computer experiments, performs impressively on various random CSPs
[
21
]
.
Furthermore, Ricci-Tersenghi and Semerjian
[
25
]
put forward a heuristic analysis of
\mathtt{BPGD}
on random
k
-SAT and
k
-XORSAT.
Their heuristic analysis proceeds by way of a thought experiment based on an idealised version of the algorithm.
We call this thought experiment the
decimation process
.
Based on physics methods Ricci-Tersenghi and Semerjian surmise that the decimation process undergoes two phase transitions, specifically a reconstruction and a condensation transition.
A key prediction of Ricci-Tersenghi and Semerjian is that these phase transitions are directly linked to the performance of the
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm.
Due to the linear algebra-induced symmetry properties, in the case of random
k
-XORSAT all of these conjectures come as elegant analytical expressions.

The aim of this paper is to verify the predictions from
[
25
]
on random
k
-XORSAT mathematically.
Specifically, our aim is to rigorously analyse the
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm on random
k
-XORSAT, and to establish the link between its performance and the phase transitions of the decimation process.
A first step towards a rigorous analysis of
\mathtt{BPGD}
on random
k
-XORSAT was undertaken in a recent contribution by Yung
[
27
]
.
However, Yung’s analysis turns out to be not tight.
Specifically, apart from requiring spurious lower bounds on the clause length
k
, Yung’s results do not quite establish the precise connection between the decimation process and the performance of
\mathtt{BPGD}
.
One reason for this is that
[
27
]
relies on ‘annealed’ techniques, i.e., essentially moment computations.
Here we instead harness ‘quenched’ arguments that were partly developed in prior work on the rank of random matrices over finite fields
[
3
,
8
]
.

1.2.
Belief Propagation Guided Decimation

The first result vindicates the predictions from
[
25
]
concerning the success probability of
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm.

\mathtt{BPGD}
sets its ambitions higher than merely finding a solution to the
k
-XORSAT instance
\boldsymbol{F}
: the algorithm attempts to sample a solution uniformly at random.
To this end
\mathtt{BPGD}
assigns values to the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
of
\boldsymbol{F}
one after the other.
In order to assign the next variable the algorithm attempts to compute the marginal probability that the variable is set to ‘true’ under a random solution to the
k
-XORSAT instance, given all previous assignments.
More precisely, suppose
\mathtt{BPGD}
has assigned values to the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}
already.
Write
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{BP}}(x_{1}),\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{BP}}(x_{t})\in\{0,1\}
for their values, with
1
representing ‘true’ and
0
‘false’.
Further, let
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}
be the simplified formula obtained by substituting
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{BP}}(x_{1}),\ldots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{BP}}(x_{t})
for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}
.
We drop any clauses from
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}
that contain variables from
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}\}
only, deeming any such clauses satisfied.
Thus,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}
is a XORSAT formula with variables
x_{t+1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
Its clauses contain at least one and at most
k

x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}
).

Let
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}
be a uniformly random solution of the XORSAT formula
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}
, assuming that
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}
remains satisfiable.
Then
\mathtt{BPGD}
aims to compute the marginal probability
{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}(x_{t+1})=1\mid\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}\right]

\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}

to true.
This is where Belief Propagation (‘BP’) comes in.
An efficient message passing heuristic for computing precisely such marginals, BP returns an ‘approximation’

of
{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}(x_{t+1})=1\mid\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}\right]
.
We will recap the mechanics of BP in Section
2.2
(the value
\mu_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}
is defined precisely in (

)).
Having computed the BP ‘approximation’,
\mathtt{BPGD}
proceeds to assign
x_{t+1}
the value ‘true’ with probability
\mu_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}
, otherwise sets
x_{t+1}
to ‘false’, then moves on to the next variable.

Let us pause for a few remarks.
First, if the BP approximations are exact, i.e., if
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}
is satisfiable and

\boldsymbol{F}
.
However, there is no universal guarantee that BP returns the correct marginals.
Accordingly, the crux of analysing
\mathtt{BPGD}
is precisely to figure out whether this is the case.
Indeed, the heuristic work of
[
25
]
ties the accuracy of BP to a phase transition of the decimation process thought experiment, to be reviewed momentarily.

Second, the strategy behind the
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm, particularly the message passing heuristic for ‘approximating’ the marginals, generalises well beyond
k
-XORSAT.
For instance, the approach applies to
k
-SAT verbatim.
That said, due to the algebraic nature of the XOR operation,
\mathtt{BPGD}
is
far
easier to analyse on
k
-XORSAT.
In fact, in XORSAT the marginal probabilities are guaranteed to be half-integral as seen in Fact

(1.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}(x_{t+1})=1\mid\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}\right]\in\{0,1/2,1\}.

As a consequence, on XORSAT the
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm effectively reduces to a purely combinatorial algorithm called Unit Clause Propagation
[

25

1.4.
The decimation process

In addition to the
\mathtt{BPGD}

Just like

1.5.
Phase transitions of the decimation process

Ricci-Tersenghi and Semerjian heuristically identify several phase transitions in terms of
d
and
t
that the decimation process undergoes.
We will confirm these predictions mathematically and investigate how they relate to the performance of
\mathtt{BPGD}
.

The first set of relevant phase transitions concerns the so-called non-reconstruction property.
Roughly speaking, non-reconstruction means that the marginal
\pi_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}={\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}(x_{t+1})=1\mid\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}\right]
is determined by short-range rather than long-range effects.
Since Belief Propagation is essentially a local algorithm, one might expect that the (non-)reconstruction phase transition coincides with the threshold up to which
\mathtt{BPGD}
succeeds; cf. the discussions in
[
5
,
17
]
.

To define (non-)reconstruction precisely, we associate a bipartite graph
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
with the formula
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}
.
The vertices of this graph are the variables and clauses of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}
.
Each variable is adjacent to the clauses in which it appears.
For a (variable or clause) vertex
v
of
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
let
\partial v
be the set of neighbours of
v
in
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
.
More generally, for an integer
\ell\geq 1
let
\partial^{\ell}v
be the set of vertices of
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
at shortest path distance precisely
\ell
from
v
.
Following
[
17
]

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|{{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}(x_{t+1})=1\,\Big|\,\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}},\,\left\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}(y)}\right\}_{y\in\partial^{2\ell}x_{t+1}}}\right]-{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}(x_{t+1})=1\mid\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}\right]}\right|\,\big|\,\boldsymbol{F}\mbox{ satisfiable}}\right]

\displaystyle=0.

2.2.
Belief Propagation

Having done our analytic homework, we proceed to
recall how Belief Propagation computes the ‘approximations’
\mu_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}
that the
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm relies upon.
We will see that due to the inherent symmetries of XORSAT the Belief Propagation computations simplify and boil down to a simpler message passing process called Warning Propagation.
Subsequently we will explain the connection between Warning Propagation and the fixed points
\alpha_{*},\alpha^{*}
of
\phi_{d,k,\lambda}
.

It is probably easiest to explain BP on a general XORSAT instance
F
with a set
V(F)
of variables and a set
C(F)
of clauses of lengths between one and
k
.
As in Section
1.5
we consider the graph
G(F)
induced by
F
, with vertex set
V(F)\cup C(F)
and an edge
xa
between
x\in V(F)
and
a\in C(F)
iff
a
contains
x
.
Let
\partial v=\partial_{F}v
be the set of neighbours of
v\in V(F)\cup C(F)
.
Additionally, given an assignment
\tau\in\{0,1\}^{\partial a}
of the variables that appear in
a
, we write
\tau\models a
iff
\tau
satisfies
a
.

With each variable/clause pair
x,a
such that
x\in\partial a
Belief Propagation associates two sequences of ‘messages’
(\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
,
(\mu_{F,a\to x,\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
directed from
x
to
a
and from
a
to
x
, respectively.
These messages are probability distributions on
\{0,1\}
, i.e.,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell}=(\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell}(0),\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell}(1)),\,\mu_{F,a\to x,\ell}=(\mu_{F,a\to x,\ell}(0),\mu_{F,a\to x,\ell}(1)),

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell}(0)+\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell}(1)=\mu_{F,a\to x,\ell}(0)+\mu_{F,a\to x,\ell}(1)

\displaystyle=1.

The initial messages are uniform, i.e.,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mu_{F,x\to a,0}(s)=\mu_{F,a\to x,0}(s)

\displaystyle=1/2

\displaystyle(s\in\{0,1\}).

Further, the messages at step
\ell+1
are obtained from the messages at step
\ell
via the
Belief Propagation equations

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mu_{F,a\to x,\ell+1}(s)

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1\}^{\partial a}}\mathbb{1}\{\tau_{x}=s,\,\tau\models a\}\prod_{y\in\partial a\setminus\{x\}}\mu_{F,y\to a,\ell}(\tau_{y}),

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell+1}(s)

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{b\in\partial x\setminus\{a\}}\mu_{F,b\to x,\ell}(s).

In (
2.7
)–(
2.8
) the
\propto
-symbol represents the normalisation required to ensure that the updated messages satisfy (
2.5
).
In the case of (
2.8
) such a normalisation may be impossible because the expressions on the r.h.s. could vanish for both
s=0
and
s=1
.
In this event we agree that

\displaystyle\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell+1}(s)=\begin{cases}\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell}(s)&\mbox{ if }\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell}(s)\neq 1/2\\
\mathbb{1}\{s=0\}&\mbox{ otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle(s\in\{0,1\});

in other words, we retain the messages from the previous iteration unless its value was
1/2
, in which case we set
\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell+1}(0)=1
.
The same convention applies to
\mu_{F,a\to x,\ell+1}(s)
.
Further, at any time
t
the BP messages render a heuristic ‘approximation’ of the marginal probability that a random solution to the formula
F
sets a variable
x
to
s\in\{0,1\}
:

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mu_{F,x,\ell}(s)

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{b\in\partial x}\mu_{F,b\to x,\ell}(s).

We set
\mu_{F,x,\ell}(0)=1-\mu_{F,x,\ell}(1)=1
if the normalisation in (
2.9
) fails, i.e., if
\sum_{s\in\{0,1\}}\prod_{b\in\partial x}\mu_{F,b\to x,\ell}(s)=0
.

Fact 2.3
.

The BP messages and marginals are half-integral for all
t
, i.e., for all
t\geq 0
and
s\in\{0,1\}
we have

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell}(s),\mu_{F,a\to x,\ell}(s),\mu_{F,x,\ell}(s)\in\{0,1/2,1\}.

Furthermore, for all
\ell>2\sum_{a\in C(F)}|\partial a|
we have
\mu_{F,x,\ell}(s)=\mu_{F,x,\ell+1}(s)
.

Proof.

The half-integrality (
2.10
) follows from a straightforward induction on
\ell
.
Furthermore, another induction on
\ell
and inspection of (
2.7
)–(
2.8
) shows that for any
x,a,\ell
such that
\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell}(1)\neq 1/2
we have
\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell+1}(s)=\mu_{F,x\to a,\ell}(s)
(
s\in\{0,1\}
).
A similar statement holds for
\mu_{F,a\to x,\ell+1}(s)
.
In particular, the number of messages that take the value
1/2
is monotonically decreasing in
\ell
.
Since the total number of messages is bounded by
2\sum_{a\in C(F)}|\partial a|
, we conclude that the messages will have converged pointwise after this number of iterations.
∎

Finally, in light of Fact
2.3
it makes sense to define the approximations for
\mathtt{BPGD}
by letting

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mu_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mu_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}},x_{t+1},\ell}(1),

\displaystyle\mu_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mu_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}},x_{t+1},\ell}(1).

2.3.
Warning Propagation

Thanks to the half-integrality (
2.10
) of the messages, Belief Propagation is equivalent to a purely combinatorial message passing procedure called
Warning Propagation
(‘WP’)
[
19
]
.
Similar as BP, WP also associates two message sequences
(\omega_{F,x\to a,\ell},\omega_{F,a\to x,\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
with every adjacent variable/clause pair.
The messages take one of three possible discrete values
\{\mathtt{f},\mathtt{u},\mathtt{n}\}
(‘frozen’, ‘uniform’, ‘null’).
Essentially,
\mathtt{n}
indicates that the value of a variable is determined by unit clause propagation.
Moreover,
\mathtt{f}
indicates that a variable is forced to take the value
0
once all variables in the
2
-core of the hypergraph representation of the formula are set to
0
.
The remaining label
\mathtt{u}
indicates that neither of the above applies.
To trace the BP messages from Section
2.2
actually only the two values
\{\mathtt{n},\mathtt{u}\}
would be necessary.
However, the third value
\mathtt{f}
will prove useful in order to compare the BP approximations with the actual marginals.
Perhaps unexpectedly given the all-uniform initialisation (
2.6
), we launch WP from all-frozen start values:

(2.12)

\displaystyle\omega_{F,x\to a,0}=\omega_{F,a\to x,0}=\mathtt{f}

\displaystyle\mbox{for all }a,x.

Subsequently the messages get updated according to the rules

(2.13)

\displaystyle\omega_{F,a\to x,\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathtt{n}&\mbox{ if }\omega_{F,y\to a,\ell}=\mathtt{n}\mbox{ for all }y\in\partial a\setminus\{x\},\\
\mathtt{f}&\mbox{ if }\omega_{F,y\to a,\ell}\neq\mathtt{u}\mbox{ for all }y\in\partial a\setminus\{x\}\mbox{ and }\omega_{F,y\to a,\ell}\neq\mathtt{n}\mbox{ for at least one }y\in\partial a\setminus\{x\},\\
\mathtt{u}&\mbox{ otherwise},\end{cases}

(2.14)

\displaystyle\omega_{F,x\to a,\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathtt{n}&\mbox{ if }\omega_{F,b\to x,\ell}=\mathtt{n}\mbox{ for at least one }b\in\partial x\setminus\{a\},\\
\mathtt{f}&\mbox{ if }\omega_{F,b\to x,\ell}\neq\mathtt{n}\mbox{ for all }b\in\partial x\setminus\{a\}\mbox{ and }\omega_{F,b\to x,\ell}=\mathtt{f}\mbox{ for at least one }b\in\partial x\setminus\{a\},\\
\mathtt{u}&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

In addition to the messages we also define the
mark
of variable node

by letting

(2.15)

\displaystyle\omega_{F,x,\ell}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathtt{n}&\mbox{ if }\omega_{F,b\to x,\ell}=\mathtt{n}\mbox{ for at least one }b\in\partial x,\\
\mathtt{f}&\mbox{ if }\omega_{F,b\to x,\ell}\neq\mathtt{n}\mbox{ for all }b\in\partial x\mbox{ and }\omega_{F,b\to x,\ell}=\mathtt{f}\mbox{ for at least one }b\in\partial x,\\
\mathtt{u}&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

For all

2.4.
The check matrix

Since the XOR operation is equivalent to addition modulo two, a XORSAT formula
F
with variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and clauses
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
translates into a linear system over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, as follows.
Let
A_{F}
be the
m\times n
-matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
whose
(i,j)
-entry equals one iff variable
x_{j}
appears in clause
a_{i}
.
Adopting coding parlance, we refer to
A_{F}
as the
check matrix
of
F
.
Furthermore, let
y_{F}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
be the vector whose
i
th entry is one plus the sum of any constant term and the number of negation signs of clause
a_{i}
mod two.
Then the solutions
\sigma\in\mathbb{F}_{n}^{n}
of the linear system
A_{F}\sigma=y_{F}
are precisely the satisfying assignments of
F
.

The algebraic properties of
A_{F}
therefore have a direct impact on the satisfiability of
F
.
For example, if
A_{F}
has rank
m
, we may conclude immediately that
F
is satisfiable.
Furthermore, the set of solutions of
F
is an affine subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
(if non-empty).
In effect, if
F
is satisfiable, then the number of satisfying assignments equals the size of the kernel of
A_{F}
.
Hence the nullity
\nul A_{F}=\dim\ker A_{F}
of the check matrix is a key quantity.

Indeed, the single most significant ingredient towards turning the heuristic arguments from
[
25
]
into rigorous proofs is a formula for the nullity of the check matrix of the XORSAT instance
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}
from the decimation process.
To unclutter the notation set
\boldsymbol{A}_{t}=A_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}
.
We derive the following proposition from a recent general result about the nullity of random matrices over finite fields
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
.
The proposition clarifies the semantics of the function
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}
and its maximiser
\alpha_{\max}
.
In physics jargon
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}
is known as the Bethe free entropy.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
d>0
and
\lambda=-\log(1-\theta)
.
Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{t}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha_{\max})

\displaystyle\mbox{in probability}.

2.5.
Null variables

Proposition
2.6
enables us to derive crucial information about the set of satisfying assignments of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}
.
Specifically, for any XORSAT instance
F
with variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
let
V_{0}(F)
be the set of variables
x_{i}
such that
\sigma_{i}=0
for all
\sigma\in\ker A_{F}
.
We call the variables
x_{i}\in V_{0}(F)

null variables
.
Since the set of solutions of
F
, if non-empty, is a translation of
\ker A_{F}
, any two solutions
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
of

set the variables in
V_{0}(F)
to exactly the same values.
The following proposition shows that WP identifies certain variables as null.

Proposition 2.7
.

W.h.p. the following two statements are true for any fixed integer
\ell>0
.

(i)

We have
V_{\mathtt{n},\ell}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})\subseteq V_{0}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
.

(ii)

We have
|V_{\mathtt{u},\ell}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})\cap V_{0}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})|=o(n)
.

Propositions
2.6
and
2.7
enable us to calculate the number of null variables of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}
, so long as we remain clear of the point
\theta_{\mathrm{cond}}
where
\alpha_{\max}
is discontinuous.

Proposition 2.8
.

If
\theta\neq\theta_{\mathrm{cond}}
then
|V_{0}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})|=\alpha_{\max}n+o(n)
w.h.p.

Let us briefly summarise what we have learned thus far.
First, because all Belief Propagation messages are half-integral, BP reduces to WP.
Second, Proposition
2.5
shows that the fixed points
\alpha_{*},\alpha^{*}
of
\phi_{d,k,\lambda}
determine the number of variables marked
\mathtt{n}
or
\mathtt{f}
by WP.
Third, the function
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}
and its maximiser
\alpha_{\max}
govern the nullity of the check matrix and thereby the number of null variables of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}
.
Clearly, the null variables

are precisely the ones whose actual marginals

are
not
uniform.
As a next step, we investigate whether BP/WP identify these variables correctly.

In light of Proposition
2.5
, in order to investigate the accuracy of BP it suffices to compare the
numbers
of variables marked
\mathtt{n}
by WP with the true marginals.
The following corollary summarises the result.

Corollary 2.9
.

For any
d
,
\theta
the following statements are true.

(i)

If
d<d_{\mathrm{min}}
,
or

d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\theta<\theta_{\mathrm{cond}}
,
or

or

or

By contrast, if

In addition, to settle the (non-)reconstruction thresholds set out in Theorem

as in (

).

Indeed, for a XORSAT instance
F

.

Assume that

.

(i)

If

(ii)

2.6.
Proving
\mathtt{BPGD}
successful

We are left to prove Theorem
1.1
.
First, we need to compute the (strictly positive) success probability of
\mathtt{BPGD}
for
d<d_{\mathrm{min}}
.
At this point, the fact that
\mathtt{BPGD}
has a fair chance of succeeding for
d<d_{\mathrm{min}}
should not come as a surprise.
Indeed, Corollary
2.9
implies that the BP approximations of the marginals are mostly correct for
d<d_{\mathrm{min}}
, at least on the formula
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}
created by the decimation process.
Furthermore, so long as the marginals are correct, the decimation process
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}
and the execution of the
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{BP},{t}}
move in lockstep.
The sole difficulty in analysing
\mathtt{BPGD}
lies in proving that the estimates of the algorithm are not just mostly correct, but correct up to only a
bounded
expected number of discrepancies over the entire execution of the algorithm.
To prove this fact we combine the method of differential equations with a subtle analysis of the sources of the remaining bounded number of discrepancies.
These discrepancies result from the presence of short (i.e., bounded-length) cycles in the graph
G(\boldsymbol{F})
.
Finally, the proof of the second (negative) part of Theorem
1.1
follows by coupling the execution of
\mathtt{BPGD}
with the decimation process, and invoking Theorem
1.3
.
The details of both arguments can be found in Section
6
.

2.7.
Discussion

The thrust of the present work is to verify the predictions from
[
25
]
on the
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm and the decimation process rigorously.
Concerning the decimation process, the main gap in the deliberations of Ricci-Tersenghi and Semerjian
[
25
]
that we needed to plug is the proof of Proposition
2.8
on the actual number of null variables in the decimation process.
The proof of Proposition
2.8
, in turn, hinges on the formula for the nullity from Proposition
2.6
, whereas Ricci-Tersenghi and Semerjian state the (as it turns out, correct) formulas for the nullity and the number of null variables based on purely heuristic arguments.

Regarding the analysis of the
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm, Ricci-Tersenghi and Semerjian state that they rely on the heuristic techniques from the insightful article
[
11
]
to predict the formula (
1.6
), but do not provide any further details; the article
[
11
]
principally employs heuristic arguments involving generating functions.
By contrast, the method that we use to prove (
1.6
) is a bit more similar to that of Frieze and Suen
[
13
]
for the analysis of a variant of the unit clause algorithm on random
k
-SAT instances, for which they also obtain the asymptotic success probability.
Yet by comparison to the argument of Frieze and Suen, we pursue a more combinatorially explicit approach that demonstrates that certain small sub-formulas that we call ‘toxic cycles’ are responsible for the failure of
\mathtt{BPGD}
.
Specifically, the proof of (
1.6
) combines the method of differential equations with Poissonisation.
Finally, the proof of Theorem
1.1
(ii) is an easy afterthought of the analysis of the decimation process.

Yung’s work
[
27
]
on random
k
-XORSAT is motivated by the ‘overlap gap paradigm’
[
14
]
, the basic idea behind which is to show that a peculiar clustered geometry of the set of solutions is an obstacle to certain types of algorithms.
Specifically, Yung only considers the Unit Clause Propagation algorithm and (a truncated version of)
\mathtt{BPGD}
.
Following the path beaten in
[
20
]
, Yung performs moment computations to establish the overlap gap property.
However, moment computations (also called ‘annealed computations’ in physics jargon) only provide one-sided bounds.
Yung’s results require spurious lower bounds on the clause length
k
(
k\geq 9
for Unit Clause and
k\geq 13
for
\mathtt{BPGD}
).
By contrast, the present proof strategy pivots on the number of null variables rather than overlaps, and Proposition
2.8
provides the precise ‘quenched’ count of null variables.
A further improvement over
[
27
]
is that the present analysis pinpoints the
precise
threshold up to which
\mathtt{BPGD}
(as well as Unit Clause) succeeds for any
k\geq 3
.
Specifically, Yung proves that these algorithms fail for
d>d_{\mathrm{core}}
, while Theorem
1.1
shows that failure occurs already for
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
with
d_{\mathrm{min}}<d_{\mathrm{core}}
.
Conversely, Theorem
1.1
shows that the algorithms succeed with a non-vanishing probability for
d<d_{\mathrm{min}}
.
Thus, Theorem
1.1
identifies the correct threshold for the success of
\mathtt{BPGD}
, as well as the correct combinatorial phenomenon that determines this threshold, namely the onset of reconstruction in the decimation process (Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
).

The
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm as detailed in Section
2.2
applies to a wide variety of problems beyond random
k
-XORSAT.
Of course, the single most prominent example is random
k
-SAT.
Lacking the symmetries of XORSAT, random
k
-SAT does not allow for the simplification to discrete messages; in particular, the BP messages are not generally half-integral.
In effect, BP and WP are no longer equivalent.
In addition to random
k
-XORSAT, the article
[
25
]
also provides a heuristic study of
\mathtt{BPGD}
on random
k
-SAT.
But once again due to the lack of half-integrality, the formulas for the phase transitions no longer come as elegant finite-dimensional expressions.
Instead, they now come as infinite-dimensional variational problems.
Furthermore, the absence of half-integrality also entails that the present proof strategy does not extend to
k
-SAT.

The lack of inherent symmetry in random
k
-SAT can partly be compensated by assuming that the clause length
k
is sufficiently large (viz. larger than some usually unspecified constant
k_{0}
).
Under this assumption the random
k
-SAT version of both the decimation process and the
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm have been analysed rigorously
[
7
,
10
]
.
The results are in qualitative agreement with the predictions from
[
25
]
.
In particular, the
\mathtt{BPGD}
algorithm provably fails to find satisfying assignments on random
k
-SAT instances even below the threshold where the set of satisfying assignments shatters into well-separated clusters
[
1
,
17
]
.
Furthermore, on random
k
-SAT a more sophisticated message passing algorithm called Survey Propagation Guided Decimation has been suggested
[
21
,
25
]
.
While on random XORSAT Survey Propagation and Belief Propagation are equivalent, the two algorithms are substantially different on random
k
-SAT.
One might therefore hope that Survey Propagation Guided Decimation outperforms
\mathtt{BPGD}
on random
k
-SAT and finds satisfying assignments up to the aforementioned shattering transition.
A negative result to the effect that Survey Propagation Guided Decimation fails asymptotically beyond the shattering transition point for large enough
k
exists
[
15
]
.
Yet a complete analysis of Belief/Survey Propagation Guided Decimation on random
k
-SAT for any
k\geq 3
in analogy to the results obtained here for random
k
-XORSAT remains an outstanding challenge.

Finally, returning to random
k
-XORSAT, a question for future work may be to investigate the performance of various types of algorithms such as greedy, message passing or local search that aim to find an assignment that violates the least possible number of clauses.
Of course, this question is relevant even for
d>d_{\mathrm{sat}}(k)
.
A first step based on the heuristic ‘dynamical cavity method’ was recently undertaken by Maier, Behrens and Zdeborová
[
18
]
.

3.
Proof of Proposition
2.2

Even though a few steps are mildly intricate, the proof of Proposition
2.2
mostly consists of ‘routine calculus’.
As a convenient shorthand we introduce

\zeta_{\lambda}(z)=\zeta_{d,k,\lambda}(z)=\phi_{d,k,\lambda}(z)-z=1-\exp\left({-\lambda-dz^{k-1}}\right)-z.

Its derivatives read

(3.1)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=d(k-1)\;z^{k-2}\;\exp(-\lambda-dz^{k-1})\;-1\;\quad\text{and}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=d(k-1)\;z^{k-3}\;\exp(-\lambda-dz^{k-1})\;\left[(k-2)-d(k-1)z^{k-1}\right].

Also let

(3.3)

\displaystyle z_{0}

\displaystyle=z_{0}(d,k)=\left(\frac{k-2}{d(k-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{{k-1}}}.

We begin by investigating the zeros of
\zeta_{\lambda}
, obviously identical with fixed points of
\phi_{d,k,\lambda}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
\lambda>0
.

(i)

The function
\zeta_{\lambda}
has either one or three zeros in
z\in[0,1]
, possibly including multiple zeros.
If
\zeta_{\lambda}
has three zeros, then at least one lies in the interval
[0,z_{0}]
and at least one lies in the interval
[z_{0},1]
.

(ii)

Also,
\zeta_{\lambda}
has at most two stationary points, a minimum and a maximum, and if it has both, the minimum occurs left of the maximum.

(iii)

If
\zeta_{\lambda}
has a unique zero, then
\alpha_{*}
is a stable fixed point of
\phi_{d,k,\lambda}
and
\sup_{z\in[0,1]}\phi_{d,k,\lambda}^{\prime}(z)<1
.

(iv)

If
\zeta_{\lambda}
has three zeros but no double zero, then
\alpha_{*},\alpha^{*}
are stable fixed points of
\phi_{d,k,\lambda}
. Additionally,
\phi_{d,k,\lambda}
possesses an unstable fixed point
\alpha_{\mathrm{u}}\in(\alpha_{*},\alpha^{*})
.
Furthermore, there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(d,\lambda)>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in[0,\alpha_{*}+\varepsilon]}\phi_{d,k,\lambda}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle<1,

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in[\alpha^{*}-\varepsilon,1]}\phi_{d,k,\lambda}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle<1.

Proof.

Since
\zeta_{\lambda}(0)>0
and
\zeta_{\lambda}(1)<0
, the number of zeros must be odd, so towards (i) it suffices to show that there cannot be more than three zeros.
Indeed, by Rolle’s theorem, between any two zeros of
\zeta_{\lambda}
there is a zero of
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime}
.
So, if
\zeta_{\lambda}
had four or more zeros then
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime}
would have at least three zeros in
(0,1]
, and in turn
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}
would have at least two.
From (
3.2
) it is clear that
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}
has only two zeros, at
z=0
(outside the relevant range) and at the inflection point where
k-2=d(k-1)z^{k-1}
, namely for
z=z_{0}
.
So,
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}
has at most two zeros, thus
\zeta_{\lambda}
has at most three zeros, therefore either one or three.

The second assertion follows from
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(z_{0})=0
and that by inspection of (
3.2
),
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(z)
is decreasing in
z
, so a local minimum of
\zeta_{\lambda}
at
z_{1}
implies
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(z_{1})>0
thus
z_{1}<z_{0}
, and symmetrically a local maximum at
z_{2}
implies that
z_{2}>z_{0}
.

Moving on to (iii), we observe that
\zeta_{\lambda}(\alpha_{*})=0
.
Furthermore, since
\zeta_{\lambda}(0)>0
while
\zeta_{\lambda}(1)<0
, we conclude that
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\alpha_{*})<0
, which implies that
0<\phi_{d,k,\lambda}^{\prime}(\alpha_{*})<1
.
Hence,
\alpha_{*}
is a stable fixed point.

With respect to (iv), if
\zeta_{\lambda}
has three zeros, then
\alpha_{*}<\alpha^{*}
are the smallest and the largest zero, respectively.
Since we assume that
\zeta_{\lambda}
does not have a double zero, the same reasoning as under (iii) shows that
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\alpha_{*})<0
and thus
0<\phi_{d,k,\lambda}^{\prime}(\alpha_{*})<1
.
Further, if
\zeta_{\lambda}
has three zeros, then by Rolle’s theorem and (ii) the function has a local minimum followed by a local maximum, which is followed by the zero
\alpha^{*}
.
Hence,
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\alpha^{*})<0
, and thus
0<\phi_{d,k,\lambda}^{\prime}(\alpha^{*})<1
.
∎

The following statement implies that
\phi_{d,k,\lambda}
has only a single fixed point if
d<d_{\mathrm{min}}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\lambda>0
.
If
d<d_{\mathrm{min}}
, then
\zeta_{\lambda}
has a unique zero and is strictly decreasing.

Proof.

Suppose that
z
is a zero of
\zeta_{\lambda}
.
Then
\exp(-\lambda-dz^{k-1})=1-z
and thus

(3.4)

\displaystyle\phi_{d,k,\lambda}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=d(k-1)z^{k-2}\exp(-\lambda-dz^{k-1})=d(k-1)(z^{k-2}-z^{k-1}).

The expression on the r.h.s. is positive for
z\in(0,1)
and zero at
z\in\{0,1\}
.
Moreover, its derivative works out to be

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}d(k-1)(z^{k-2}-z^{k-1})

\displaystyle=d(k-1)z^{k-3}(k-2-(k-1)z).

Thus, the expression on the r.h.s. of (
3.4
) takes its maximum value of
d((k-2)/(k-1))^{k-2}
at
z^{\dagger}=(k-2)/(k-1)
.
Hence, (
3.4
) implies that
\phi_{d,k,\lambda}^{\prime}(z)<1
and thus
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)<0
.
Consequently, the function
\phi_{d,k,\lambda}
only has stable fixed points and thus has only a single fixed point by Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Proceeding to average degrees
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
, we verify that the values
\lambda_{*},\lambda^{*}
from Section
1.5
are well defined and satisfy the inequality (
1.9
).

Lemma 3.3
.

If
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
, then the polynomial
d(k-1)z^{k-2}(1-z)-1
has precisely two roots
0<z_{*}<z^{*}<1
and the values
\lambda_{*},\lambda^{*}
defined in (
1.9
) satisfy
\lambda_{*}>\lambda^{*}
.
Furthermore,
d_{\mathrm{core}}>d_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\lambda^{*}=0
iff
d\geq d_{\mathrm{core}}
.

Proof.

Let
z^{\dagger}=(k-2)/(k-1)
.
The polynomial
z^{k-2}(1-z)
is non-negative on
[0,1]
, strictly increasing on
[0,z^{\dagger}]
and strictly decreasing on
[z^{\dagger},1]
.
Hence, at
z^{\dagger}
the polynomial attains its maximum value of

(3.5)

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq z\leq 1}z^{k-2}(1-z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-2)^{k-2}}{(k-1)^{k-1}}.

If
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
, the equation

(3.6)

\displaystyle z^{k-2}(1-z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d(k-1)}

therefore has two distinct solutions
0<z_{*}<z^{\dagger}<z^{*}<1
.
Letting

\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}(z)=-\log(1-z)-\frac{z}{(1-z)(k-1)},

we obtain
\lambda_{*}=\mathfrak{l}(z_{*})
and
\lambda^{*}=\max\{\mathfrak{l}(z^{*}),0\}
.

The function
\mathfrak{l}(z)
is positive and monotonically increasing on
(0,z^{\dagger})
, and monotonically decreasing on
(z^{\dagger},1)
.
Indeed, the derivative works out to be

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{l}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{k-2-(k-1)z}{(k-1)(1-z)^{2}},

which is positive for small
z>0
and has its unique zero at
z^{\dagger}
.
Since
z_{*}<z^{\dagger}
, we conclude that
\lambda_{*}>0
.

Further,
[
8
, Theorem 1.2]
shows that at
d=d_{\mathrm{core}}
we have
\mathfrak{l}(z^{*})=0
.
Since
z^{*}
is an increasing function of
d
while
\mathfrak{l}(z)
is strictly decreasing in
z>z^{\dagger}
, we conclude that
\mathfrak{l}(z^{*})<0
for
d>d_{\mathrm{core}}
,
\mathfrak{l}(z^{*})=0
for
d=d_{\mathrm{core}}
and
\mathfrak{l}(z^{*})=\lambda^{*}>0
for
d_{\mathrm{min}}<d<d_{\mathrm{core}}
.

Thus, we are left to verify that
\lambda_{*}>\lambda^{*}
, which amounts to showing that
\mathfrak{l}(z^{*})<\mathfrak{l}(z_{*})
.
Rearranging (
3.6
) into
d=1/((k-1)(1-z_{*})z_{*}^{k-2})
and
d=1/((k-1)(1-z^{*})z^{*\,k-2})
and applying the inverse function theorem, we obtain

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial z_{*}}{\partial d}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(k-1)(1-z_{*})^{2}z_{*}^{k-1}}{k-2-(k-1)z_{*}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial z^{*}}{\partial d}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(k-1)(1-z^{*})^{2}z^{*\,k-1}}{k-2-(k-1)z^{*}}.

Combining (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) with the chain rule, we arrive at

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial d}\mathfrak{l}(z_{*})

\displaystyle=-z_{*}^{k-1},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial d}\mathfrak{l}(z^{*})

\displaystyle=-z^{*\,k-1}.

Since
z^{*}>z_{*}
for all
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
, integrating (
3.9
) on
d
shows that
\lambda_{*}>\lambda^{*}
, thereby completing the proof.
∎

We are ready to identify the zeros of
\zeta_{\lambda}
for
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
, depending on the regime of
\lambda
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\lambda>0
and assume that
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
.

(i)

If
\lambda<\lambda^{*}
, then
\zeta_{\lambda}
has a unique zero.

(ii)

If
\lambda^{*}<\lambda<\lambda_{*}
, then
\zeta_{\lambda}
has three distinct zeros.

(iii)

If
\lambda>\lambda_{*}
, then
\zeta_{\lambda}
has a unique zero.

Proof.

Assume that
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
.
For fixed
k
and
d
, the function
\zeta_{\lambda}
varies continuously with
\lambda
, so there are contiguous regimes of
\lambda
where it has one zero, regimes where it has three zeros, and these regimes are divided by critical values of
\lambda
where
\zeta_{\lambda}
has three zeros two of which consist of a double zero.
In this case, the slope at the double zero is also 0.
(By Rolle’s theorem, the slope is 0 somewhere between the two zeros, and this is the limiting case.)

Thus, the separation between the regimes with one and three zeros occurs at values of
\lambda
such that
\zeta_{\lambda}(z)=\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime}(z)=0
.
Recalling the definition of
\zeta_{\lambda}
and the derivative
\zeta_{\lambda}^{\prime}
from (
3.1
), we obtain

(3.10)

\displaystyle 1-z=

\displaystyle\exp(-\lambda-dz^{k-1})

and

\displaystyle d(k-1)z^{k-2}=\frac{1}{\exp(-\lambda-dz^{k-1})}.

Substituting the left equation for the exponential in the right equation, we conclude that (
3.10
) holds only if
z
is a solution to (
3.6
).
Further, substituting the two solutions
0<z_{*}<z^{\dagger}=(k-2)/(k-1)<z^{*}
into either one of the equations from (
3.10
) and solving for
\lambda
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{*}

\displaystyle=-\log(1-z_{*})-\frac{z_{*}}{(1-z_{*})(k-1)},

\displaystyle\lambda^{\star}

\displaystyle=-\log(1-z^{*})-\frac{z^{*}}{(1-z^{*})(k-1)}.

Observe that
\lambda^{*}=\max\{\lambda^{\star},0\}
.

Suppose
0<\lambda<\lambda_{*}
.
Since
\zeta_{\lambda_{*}}(z_{*})=0
, the function
\lambda\mapsto\zeta_{\lambda}(z_{*})
is strictly increasing and
\zeta_{\lambda}(0)>0
, we conclude that
\zeta_{\lambda}
has a zero in the interval
(0,z_{*})
.
Similarly, if
\lambda>\lambda^{*}
, then the function
\zeta_{\lambda}
has a zero in the interval
(z^{*},1)
.
Hence, (ii) is an immediate consequence of Lemma
3.1
.

Now assume that
0<\lambda<\lambda^{*}
.
Since
\lambda_{*}>\lambda^{*}
by Lemma
3.3
, Lemma
3.1
implies that
\zeta_{\lambda^{*}}
has precisely three zeros.
The largest one is
\alpha^{*}=z^{*}
, satisfies
\alpha^{*}>z^{\dagger}>z_{0}
, is a double zero and simultaneously a local maximum of
\zeta_{\lambda^{*}}
.
Since
\alpha^{*}
is a double zero and a local maximum, the smallest zero
\alpha_{*}
satisfies
\alpha_{*}<z_{0}
by Rolle’s theorem.
Hence,
\zeta_{\lambda^{*}}^{\prime}(z)<0
for all
0<z<\alpha_{*}
.
Since the function
\lambda\mapsto\zeta_{\lambda}(z)
is strictly increasing for all
z\in(0,1)
, Lemma
3.1
(i) implies that for
\lambda<\lambda^{*}
only a single zero remains, which is smaller than
z_{0}
.

Finally, suppose that
\lambda>\lambda_{*}>\lambda^{*}
.
Lemma
3.1
(i) implies that
\zeta_{\lambda_{*}}
has precisely three zeros, with a double zero occurring at
z_{*}
and another zero at
\alpha^{*}(\lambda_{*})>z^{\dagger}>z_{0}
.
By Lemma
3.1
and the choice of
z_{*},\lambda_{*}
, the double zero at
z_{*}
is a local minimum.
Therefore,
\zeta_{\lambda_{*}}^{\prime}(z)<0
for all
z>\alpha^{*}
.
Since the function
\lambda\mapsto\zeta_{\lambda}(z)
is strictly increasing for all
z\in(0,1)
, we conclude that
\zeta_{\lambda}(z)>0
for all
\lambda>\lambda_{*}
and
z\in[0,z_{0}]
.
Hence, by Lemma
3.1
(i) for
\lambda>\lambda_{*}
only a single zero remains, which lies in the interval
[z_{0},1]
.
∎

Combining Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
, we can now verify the analytic properties of the function
\lambda\mapsto\alpha_{*}
and
\lambda\mapsto\alpha^{*}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
0<d<d_{\mathrm{sat}}
and
\lambda>0
.

(i)

If
d<d_{\mathrm{min}}
, then the function
\lambda\in(0,\infty)\mapsto\alpha_{*}=\alpha^{*}
is analytic with derivative

(3.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\alpha_{*}}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha_{*}}{1-d(k-1)\alpha_{*}^{k-2}(1-\alpha_{*})}<1.

(ii)

If
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
, then
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{*})\mapsto\alpha_{*}
is analytic with derivative (
3.11
).

(iii)

If
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
, then
\lambda\in(\lambda^{*},\infty)\mapsto\alpha^{*}
is analytic differentiable with derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\alpha^{*}}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha^{*}}{1-d(k-1)\alpha^{*\,k-2}(1-\alpha^{*})}.

Proof.

Assume that
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{*})
.
We know from the proof of Lemma
3.4
that
z_{*}
is a double root and local minimum of
\zeta_{\lambda_{*}}
.
Furthermore,
z_{*}<z_{0}
and the function
\lambda\mapsto\zeta_{\lambda}(z)
is strictly increasing in
\lambda
. Hence, Lemma
3.1
implies that for any
0<\lambda<\lambda_{*}
the function
\zeta_{\lambda}
has a unique zero in
(0,z_{*})
.
Similarly, if
d<d_{\mathrm{min}}
then Lemma
3.2
shows that
\zeta_{\lambda}
has a unique zero at
\alpha_{*}
.
Therefore, the implicit function theorem implies that in cases (i) and (ii) the function
\lambda\mapsto\alpha_{*}
is continuously differentiable.

Thus, we are left to work out
\partial\alpha_{*}(d,k,\lambda)/\partial\lambda
.
Consider the function
\mathcal{A}:\binom{z}{\lambda}\mapsto\binom{\zeta_{\lambda}(z)}{\lambda},
which is one-to-one in an open interval around
\alpha_{*}
.
The Jacobi matrix reads

\displaystyle D\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\partial(\phi_{d,k,\lambda})/\partial\alpha-1&\partial\phi_{d,k,\lambda}/\partial\lambda\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi_{d,k,\lambda}}{\partial\alpha}\big|_{\alpha=\alpha_{*}}

\displaystyle=d(k-1)\alpha^{k-2}\exp(-\lambda-d\alpha^{k-1})\big|_{\alpha=\alpha_{*}}=d(k-1)\alpha_{*}^{k-2}(1-\alpha_{*}),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi_{d,k,\lambda}}{\partial t}\big|_{\alpha=\alpha_{*}}

\displaystyle=\exp(-\lambda-d\alpha^{k-1})\big|_{\alpha=\alpha_{*}}=1-\alpha_{*}.

Hence, by the inverse function theorem the derivative of
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
reads

\displaystyle(D\mathcal{A})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\left[{\partial\phi_{d,k,\lambda}/\partial\alpha-1}\right]^{-1}&-\left[{\partial\phi_{d,k,\lambda}/\partial\lambda}\right]/\left[{\partial\phi_{d,k,\lambda}/\partial\alpha-1}\right]\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

and thus

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\alpha_{*}}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\phi_{d,k,\lambda}/\partial\lambda}{\partial\phi_{d,k,\lambda}/\partial\alpha-1}=\frac{1-\alpha_{*}}{1-d(k-1)\alpha_{*}^{k-2}(1-\alpha_{*})}.

Thus, we obtain (i) and (ii).
A similar argument applies to
\lambda\in(\lambda^{*},\infty)\mapsto\alpha^{*}
in the case
d>d_{\mathrm{min}}
and yields (iii).
∎

As a final preparation towards the proof of Proposition
2.2
we investigate the solution
\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}
to the differential equation (
1.11
); notice that Lemma
3.5
shows that this ODE does indeed possess a unique solution on
(d_{\mathrm{min}},d_{\mathrm{sat}}]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
0<d<d_{\mathrm{sat}}
we have
0<\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}<\lambda_{*}
.
Furthermore, for all
0<\lambda<\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}
we have
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha_{*})>\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha^{*})
, while
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha_{*})<\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha^{*})
for
\lambda_{*}>\lambda>\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}
.

Proof.

For
d<d_{\mathrm{sat}}
define

(3.12)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}^{*}=\inf\{\lambda\geq 0:\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha^{*})>\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha_{*})\}.

For any
d<d_{\mathrm{sat}}
we have
\Phi_{d,k,0}(0)>\Phi_{d,k,0}(z)
for all
0<z\leq 1
; this follows from the characterisation of the
k
-XORSAT threshold from
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
.
Hence,
\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}^{*}>0
for all
d<d_{\mathrm{sat}}
.

Further, the function
\zeta_{\lambda_{*}}
has a double zero and a local minimum at
\alpha_{*}=z_{*}
.
Since the sign of
\zeta_{\lambda_{*}}(z)
matches the sign of
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{*}}^{\prime}(z)
, this means that
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{*}}(\alpha^{*})>\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{*}}(\alpha_{*})
.
Hence, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for
0<\lambda_{*}-\varepsilon<\lambda<\lambda_{*}
we have
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha^{*})>\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha_{*})
.
Therefore,

(3.13)

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}^{*}<\lambda_{*}.

As a next step we show that

(3.14)

\displaystyle\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha_{*})<\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha^{*})

\displaystyle\mbox{ for $\lambda_{*}>\lambda>\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}^{*}$}.

To this end, we compute the derivatives of
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha_{*})
,
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha^{*})
with respect to
0<\lambda<\lambda_{*}
.
Since
\alpha_{*},\alpha^{*}
are stationary points of
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}
, the chain rule yields

(3.15)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha_{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}}{\partial\lambda}\big|_{\alpha_{*}}+\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}}{\partial\alpha}\big|_{\alpha_{*}}\frac{\partial\alpha_{*}}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}}{\partial\lambda}\big|_{\alpha_{*}}=-\exp(-\lambda-d\alpha_{*}^{k-1})=\alpha_{*}-1,

(3.16)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}(\alpha^{*})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{*}-1.

Since
\alpha_{*}<\alpha^{*}
for all
\lambda^{*}<\lambda<\lambda_{*}
, (
3.14
) follows from (
3.15
)–(
3.16
).

Finally, we verify that
\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}^{*}
equals the solution
\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}
to the differential equation (
1.11
).
Recalling the definition (
3.12
), we see that it suffices to check that
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}(\alpha_{*})=\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}(\alpha^{*})
for all
d_{\mathrm{min}}<d<d_{\mathrm{sat}}
.
To this end, we notice that by definition of
d_{\mathrm{sat}}
we have
\Phi_{d_{\mathrm{sat}},k,0}(0)=\Phi_{d_{\mathrm{sat}},k,0}(\alpha^{*})
, in line with the initial condition
\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}(d_{\mathrm{sat}})=0
.
Additionally, we claim that
\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}(d_{\mathrm{sat}})
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}}{\partial d}\big|_{\alpha^{*}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}}{\partial d}\big|_{\alpha_{*}}.

Indeed, using the chain rule and the fact that
\alpha_{*},\alpha^{*}
are stationary points, with
\lambda=\lambda(d)
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}(\alpha_{*})}{\partial d}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}}{\partial d}\big|_{\alpha_{*},\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}+\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}}{\partial\alpha}\big|_{\alpha_{*},\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}\frac{\partial\alpha_{*}}{\partial d}+\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}}{\partial\lambda}\big|_{\alpha_{*},\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}}{\partial d}\big|_{\alpha_{*},\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}+\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda}}{\partial\lambda}\big|_{\alpha_{*},\lambda}=\alpha_{*}^{k-1}+(\alpha_{*}-1)\frac{\partial\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}{\partial d}.

Analogously,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}(\alpha^{*})}{\partial d}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{*\,k-1}+(\alpha^{*}-1)\frac{\partial\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}{\partial d}.

Hence, the solution
\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}
to (
1.11
) satisfies
\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}(\alpha_{*})=\Phi_{d,k,\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}}(\alpha^{*})
, and thus
\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{cond}}^{*}
.
Therefore, the assertion follows from (
3.15
) and (
3.14
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

The first assertion is an immediate consequence of Lemmas
3.2
and
3.5
.
Moreover, the second assertion follows from Lemmas
3.3
,
3.4
and
3.5
.
Finally, the last assertion follows from Lemma

.
∎

4.
Warning Propagation and local weak convergence

In this section we prove Propositions
2.5
and
2.7
.
The proofs rely on the concept of local weak convergence.
Specifically, we are going to set up a Galton-Watson process that mimics the local topology of the graph
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
up to any fixed depth
\ell
.
Subsequently we will analyse WP on the Galton-Watson tree and argue that the result extends to
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
.

4.1.
Local weak convergence

The construction of the Galton-Watson process
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{T}(d,k,t)
is pretty straightforward.
The process has two types called
variable nodes
and
check nodes
.
The process starts with a single variable node
v_{0}
.
Furthermore, each variable node begets a
{\rm Po}(d)
number of check nodes as offspring, while the offspring of a check node is a
{\rm Bin}(k-1,1-t/n)
number of variable nodes.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be the Galton-Watson tree rooted at
v_{0}
that this process generates;
\mathbb{T}
may be infinite.
Hence, for an integer
\ell
obtain
\mathbb{T}^{(\ell)}
from
\mathbb{T}
by deleting all variable/check nodes at distance greater than
2\ell
from
v_{0}
.
Thus,
\mathbb{T}^{(\ell)}
is a finite random tree rooted at
v_{0}
.
For any graphs
T,T^{\prime}
rooted at
v,v^{\prime}
, respectively, we write
T\cong T^{\prime}
if there is a graph isomorphism
\iota:T\to T^{\prime}
such that
\iota(v)=v^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, for a vertex
v
of
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
and an integer
\ell
we let
\partial^{\leq\ell}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}v
be the subgraph obtained from
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
by deleting all vertices at distance greater than
2\ell
from
v
, rooted at
v
.
Finally, for a rooted graph
g
and an integer
\ell
we let
\boldsymbol{N}_{t}^{(\ell)}(g)
be the number of vertices
v
of
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
such that
\partial^{\leq\ell}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}}v\cong g
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any rooted tree
g
we have

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|{\boldsymbol{N}_{t}^{(\ell)}(g)-(n-t){\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathbb{T}^{(\ell)}\cong g}\right]}\right|=o(n).

Proof.

The proof is based on a routine second moment argument; that is, we claim that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{N}_{t}^{(\ell)}(g)}\right]

\displaystyle=(n-t){\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathbb{T}^{(\ell)}\cong g}\right]+o(n),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\boldsymbol{N}_{t}^{(\ell)}(g)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=(n-t)^{2}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathbb{T}^{(\ell)}\cong g}\right]^{2}+o(n^{2}).

Combining (
4.2
) with the Markov and Chebyshev inequalities then yields the assertion.

We prove (
4.2
) and thereby (
4.1
) by induction on
\ell
.
Recall that
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}}
is a XORSAT instance with variables
x_{t+1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
Let us begin with the estimate of the first moment.
Due to the linearity of expectation, it suffices to show that

(4.3)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\partial^{\leq\ell}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}},x_{t+1})\cong g}\right]={\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathbb{T}^{(\ell)}\cong g}\right]+o(1).

For
\ell=0
there is nothing to show.
Hence, suppose that (
4.1
) is true with
\ell
replaced by
\ell-1
.
Furthermore, let
\Delta
be the degree of the root
r
of
g
and let
1\leq\kappa_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\kappa_{\Delta}\leq k
be the degrees of the children of the root; thus, we order the children of
r
so that their degrees are increasing.
For an integer
1\leq i\leq k
let
K_{i}
be the number
j\in[\Delta]
such that
\kappa_{j}=i
.
Further, let
(g_{i,j})_{1\leq i\leq\Delta,1\leq j\leq\kappa_{i}}
be the trees pending on the grandchildren of the root.
In addition, let
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
be the degree of
x_{t+1}
in
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
and let
1\leq\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\leq k
be the degrees of the neighbours of
x_{t+1}
.
Then
\partial^{\leq\ell}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}},x_{t+1})\cong g
is possible only if
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\Delta
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}=\kappa_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq\Delta
.
Since the clauses of the random formula
\boldsymbol{F}
are drawn uniformly and independently and
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
is obtained from
G(\boldsymbol{F})
by deleting the variable nodes
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}
along with any ensuing isolated check nodes, we conclude that the event
\mathcal{D}=\{\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\Delta,\,\bigwedge_{1\leq i\leq\Delta}\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}=\kappa_{i}\}
has probability

(4.4)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{D}}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left[{{\rm Po}(d)=\Delta}\right]\binom{\Delta}{\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{{\rm Bin}(k-1,1-t/n)=i}\right]^{\kappa_{i}}.

Further, let
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{i,j}:1\leq i\leq\Delta,\,1\leq j\leq\kappa_{i}\}
and let
\mathcal{E}
be the event that
\boldsymbol{N}_{t}^{(\ell-1)}(\gamma)=(n-t){\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathbb{T}^{(\ell-1)}\cong\gamma}\right]+o(n)
for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
.
Then by induction we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{E}\mid\mathcal{D}}\right]

\displaystyle=1-o(1).

Now, obtain
G^{-}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
from
G(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{DC},{t}})
by deleting
x_{t+1}
along with its adjacent check nodes.
Let
\boldsymbol{N}_{t}^{(\ell),-}(g_{i,j})
be the number of vertices
v
of

Each of these rows contains precisely a single non-zero entry.
The positions of the non-zero entries are chosen uniformly without replacement.
Thus, the extra
t
rows have the effect of fixing
t
uniformly random coordinates to zero.
Since the distribution of the random matrix
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
is invariant under column permutations, we conclude that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{t}

\displaystyle{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize dist}}}{{=}}}\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{t}^{\prime}.

Further, let
\boldsymbol{A}[\lambda]
be the matrix obtained from
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
by adding a random number of
\boldsymbol{l}={\rm Po}(\lambda n)
of rows.
Each of these rows contains a single non-zero entry, which is placed in a uniformly random position.
The extra rows are chosen mutually independently (thus, ‘with replacement’) and independently of
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}
.
By Poisson thinning, for any column index
j\in[n]
the probability that one of the new
\boldsymbol{l}
rows has a non-zero entry in the
j
th column equals
1-\exp(-\lambda)=\theta
.
Since
t\sim\theta n
, the total number of such indices

has distribution
{\rm Bin}(n,\theta)
.
Since
{\mathbb{P}}\left[{|{\rm Bin}(n,\theta)-t|\leq\sqrt{n}\log n}\right]\geq 1-1/n
by the Chernoff bound, we can couple
\boldsymbol{A}_{t}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{A}\left[{\lambda}\right]
such that

(5.4)

\displaystyle\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{t}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\nul\boldsymbol{A}\left[{\lambda}\right]+o(n)\mbox{ w.h.p.}

Finally, let
\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}[\lambda]
be the matrix obtained as follows.
Let
\mathfrak{d},\mathfrak{k}
have probability generating functions

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(z)

\displaystyle=\exp((\lambda+d)(z-1)),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{dz^{k}+k\lambda z}{d+k\lambda}.

In other words,
\mathfrak{d}
has distribution
{\rm Po}(d+\lambda)
while
\mathfrak{k}
equals one with probability

and equals
k
with probability

.
The definition (
5.5

\displaystyle\bar{\mathfrak{d}}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(1)=\lambda+d,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathfrak{k}}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}(1)=\frac{k(d+\lambda)}{d+k\lambda}.

Hence, the number
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}_{n}{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize dist}}}{{=}}}{\rm Po}(n\bar{\mathfrak{d}}/\bar{\mathfrak{k}})
of rows of
\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{A}_{n}
can be written as a sum of independent random variables
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}+\mathfrak{m}^{\prime\prime}
with distributions

(5.7)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}

\displaystyle={\rm Po}(dn/k),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle={\rm Po}(\lambda n).

The first summand
\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
prescribes the number of rows of
\mathfrak{A}
with
k
non-zero entries, while
\mathfrak{m}^{\prime\prime}
details the number of rows with a single non-zero entry.
Consequently, (
5.7
) shows that the numbers of rows with
k
or with just a single non-zero entry have the same distributions in both
\mathfrak{A}
and
\boldsymbol{A}[\lambda]
.

We are left to argue that in
\mathfrak{A}
the positions of the non-zero entries in the different rows are nearly independent and uniform.
To see this, let
(\boldsymbol{h}_{i,j})_{1\leq i\geq\mathfrak{m},1\leq j\leq k}
be a family of mutually independent and uniform random variables with values in
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Moreover, let
\boldsymbol{X}
be the number of indices
1\leq i\leq\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
such that there exist

such that
\boldsymbol{h}_{i,j_{1}}=\boldsymbol{h}_{i,j_{2}}
; in other words,
\boldsymbol{h}_{i,1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{h}_{i,k}
fail to be pairwise distinct.
A routine calculation shows that

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{X}]

\displaystyle=O(1).

Now, let us think of
(\boldsymbol{h}_{i,j})_{1\leq i\leq\mathfrak{m}^{\prime},1\leq j\leq k}
and
(\boldsymbol{h}_{i,1})_{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}<i\leq\mathfrak{m}}
as the ‘bins’ where

randomly tossed ‘balls’ land.
Then the standard Poissonisation of the balls-into-bins experiment shows that given the event (
5.1

.
Therefore, (
5.8
) shows that
\boldsymbol{A}[\lambda],\mathfrak{A}
can be coupled such that

(5.9)

\displaystyle\nul\boldsymbol{A}[\lambda]

\displaystyle=\nul\mathfrak{A}+o(n)\mbox{ w.h.p.}

Combining (

\displaystyle\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nul\mathfrak{A}+o(n)

Hence, Theorem

(5.11)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{t}

\displaystyle=\max_{z\in[0,1]}\mathfrak{F}(z)

Further, recalling the definitions (

Lemma 5.2
.

We have

(5.13)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{|\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\nul\boldsymbol{A}_{t}]|>\sqrt{n}\log n}\right]

We apply this observation to the matrix

Furthermore, Bennett’s concentration inequality for Poisson variables shows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[{|\boldsymbol{m}-dn/k|>\sqrt{n}\log^{2/3}n}\right]

We are going to estimate

Lemma 5.3

Let

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}|V_{0}(\boldsymbol{A}[\lambda])|}{n}-1.

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime\prime}=\ell}\right]\mathbb{E}[\nul\boldsymbol{A}[\lambda]\mid\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime\prime}=\ell]=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\nul\boldsymbol{A}[\lambda]\mid\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime\prime}=\ell]\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\frac{(\lambda n)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\exp(-\lambda n)

6.1.
Unit Clause Propagation redux

The simple-minded Unit Clause Propagation algorithm attempts to assign random values to as yet unassigned variables one after the other.
After each such random assignment the algorithm pursues the ‘obvious’ implications of its decisions.
Specifically, the algorithm substitutes its chosen truth values for all occurrences of the already assigned variables.
If this leaves a clause with only a single unassigned variable, a so-called ‘unit clause’, the algorithm assigns that variable so as to satisfy the unit clause.
If a conflict occurs because two unit clauses impose opposing values on a variable, the algorithm declares that a conflict has occurred, sets the variable to false and continues; of course, in the event of a conflict the algorithm will ultimately fail to produce a satisfying assignment.

(ii).

We couple the execution of the decimation process and of the
