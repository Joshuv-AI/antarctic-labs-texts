Association of haematological cancer drugs recommendation levels in Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology guideline with different approval approaches: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lirong Zhang, Hongbin Yi, Liping Kuai, Sheng Han, Hong Sun, Jiaqin Cai, Xiaoxia Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo evaluate the associations between approval approaches and the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines for haematological cancer drug indications.MethodsHaematological cancer drug indications approved via different approaches between 2015 and 2023 were retrieved from the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) website, and their CSCO guideline recommendation levels were analysed. Basic characteristics were analysed descriptively. Fisher’s test and t-tests were used to respectively explore the associations of the approval approaches and the recommendation level, and the time difference between the indications obtaining the recommendation level and being approved by the NMPA. Changes in recommendation levels were also analysed.ResultsThe NMPA approved 52 haematological cancer drug indications (33 anticancer drugs) from 2015 to 2023. 5 indications were approved via routine approval (RA) and 47 were approved via accelerated approval (AA). 39 indications obtained CSCO guideline recommendation level I. Indications approved via the AA and RA were similarly likely to obtain level I. The average time for the indications approved via the RA to obtain the recommendation level is 7.6 months before the indications were approved by the NMPA. And the average time for the indications approved via AA to obtain the recommended level is −5.51 months. For indications approved via priority review and RA, there was a difference in the average time from obtaining recommendation level to obtaining NMPA approval. Compared with CSCO’s initial recommendation levels, 15 indications obtained higher recommendation level in the updated CSCO guideline.ConclusionsMost haematological cancer drug indications obtained CSCO guideline recommendation level I, with no significant difference between AA and RA in the recommendation level. Indications approved via RA obtained recommendation level more quickly. Some indications obtained a higher recommendation level in the updated guideline. Strengthening communication between the NMPA and CSCO and ensuring timely integration of emerging evidence may improve the alignment between approval decisions and recommendation level.Keywords: Cross-Sectional Studies, Public Health, Medical Countermeasures

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Introduction

The proportion of the Chinese population aged 65 years and over is projected to reach 14.2% in 2021 and will increase to 14.9% in 2022. This has resulted in China becoming a rapidly ageing society. It is anticipated that the proportion will exceed 20% approximately 2032, marking the onset of a super-ageing society.1 The ageing of the Chinese population has been a significant contributing factor to the observed increase in the incidence of malignant tumours in recent years.2 3 Haematological cancer represents a significant public health concern in China, with the latest data indicating that it represents one of the ten most common cancers in the country in terms of morbidity and mortality.4 The advent of tube-based chemotherapy, targeted therapies, immunotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors and chimeric antigen receptor T-cells has led to significant improvements in the care of patients with haematological cancer. However, drug resistance remains a major feature of these diseases and a major cause of relapse and treatment failure.5–7 The combination of two or more drugs has emerged as a novel strategy to overcome drug resistance in patients with haematolog.8

To facilitate and expedite the development and review of new drugs to meet unmet medical needs for the treatment of serious or life-threatening conditions, the many regulatory agencies have established expedited approval approaches.9–13 For example, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)’s accelerated approval (AA) permits approval based on surrogate or intermediate clinical endpoints that are reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit, with post-marketing confirmatory trials required to verify efficacy.13 In contrast, the European Medicines Agency’s (EMA) conditional marketing authorisation grants temporary authorisation when comprehensive clinical data are not yet available but the anticipated benefits outweigh the risks, with renewal contingent on the submission of additional evidence.14 In response to the increasing prevalence of health concerns and the slow pace of regulatory approvals, China also initiated a regulatory reform process with the aim of accelerating the review and approval of medicines.15 The National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) subsequently implemented a series of reforms to enhance the Provisions for Drug Registration and gradually introduced four AA, including special approval (SA), breakthrough therapies (BT), conditional approval (CA) and priority review (PR).16 BT provides enhanced regulatory interaction for indications showing substantial improvement during early clinical development. CA allows earlier market entry based on preliminary evidence reasonably predicting clinical benefit. PR shortens review timelines for drugs addressing urgent clinical needs, and SA is reserved for emergency public health situations. RA is the standard approval pathway, requiring mature evidence from well-controlled clinical trials—typically randomised studies demonstrating clear clinical benefit—before authorisation.16 A comparative summary of these approval approaches, their criteria and implications for drug evaluation is provided in online supplemental eTable S1. The AA has significantly enhanced China’s drug regulatory reforms and research and development capabilities.17 18 Furthermore, these reforms have greatly improved the outcomes for Chinese cancer patients.19–21 Nevertheless, as regulatory reform progresses, studies are beginning to indicate that the clinical benefits of drugs with AA might not be as evident as previously thought. A 2013–2023 cohort study in the USA revealed that most indications approved via AA demonstrated no clinical benefit after 5 years of follow-up.22 The proportion of new drugs with clinical benefits approved in China since 2018 is less than 50% in China between 2005 and 2021.18

Clinical practice guidelines represent crucial instruments in the delivery of healthcare in China. They serve to standardise clinical decision-making and facilitate the development of clinical research.23 24 Clinical practice guidelines, including those of the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO), are a fundamental prerequisite for drugs to be included in the National Healthcare Security Administration.25 The CSCO guidelines are compiled by subspecialists in various oncology fields with the aim of developing clinical practice guidelines that are in line with the principles of evidence-based medicine, the accessibility of healthcare resources and social values.26 27 The CSCO clinical practice guidelines undergo periodic updates to align with both domestic and international developments. These guidelines have emerged as the most authoritative source of oncology recommendations in China, reflecting the integration of evidence-based medical evidence, expert consensus and accessibility and potency considerations to inform the recommendation level.26 Given the pivotal role of the CSCO guideline recommendation level in clinical practice, patient care and healthcare decisions, a retrospective study was conducted to investigate the associations between the different approval approaches of the NMPA and the CSCO guideline.

Materials and methods

This study did not involve the collection of individual patient data. Publicly available drug data were used. Therefore, ethics committee approval was not required. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines were followed.

Data sources

The indications for drugs approved for the treatment of haematological cancer between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2023 were extracted from the official NMPA public website.28 29 The recommendation level for indications was also extracted from the official CSCO website.26

Data extraction and screening

On the basis of the approval reports published on the NMPA website, information related to haematological cancer drugs via AA and routine approval (RA) from 2015 to 2023 was extracted, including the drug name, indications, approval time, approval approaches and drug manufacturer information. Three time points for each haematological cancer drug indication were extracted: the time of NMPA approval, the time of the indication initial obtaining CSCO guideline recommendation level and the time of indication latest obtaining CSCO guideline recommendation level. The initial and updated CSCO guideline recommendation levels for each indication were also extracted. The criteria used to determine the CSCO guideline recommendation levels are described in online supplemental eTable S2. This study exclusively encompasses indications that were initially approved by the NMPA. Consequently, both generic and biosimilars were excluded. Furthermore, indications that did not obtain the CSCO guideline recommendation level were also excluded. Figure 1 shows the data selection process for this study.

Statistics

We employed descriptive statistical analysis to quantify the number of indications for haematological cancer drugs obtained via various approval approaches. To further compare the differences in recommendation levels among these indications approved via different approaches, we adopted Fisher’s exact test. To verify the normality and homogeneity of variances within the dataset, we conducted the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. On the basis of the results of these tests regarding normality and variance homogeneity, we selected the appropriate type of t-test. We subsequently used the t-test to conduct an in-depth analysis of the time span between the indication obtaining CSCO guideline recommendation level and being approved by the NMPA. For the statistical analysis, we set the significance threshold for the two-tailed test at p<0.05 and relied on V.4.3.1 of R software to comprehensively execute the statistical analysis tasks. Descriptive analysis was conducted to analyse the changes in the CSCO guideline recommendation levels.

Results

A total of 52 indications for 33 haematological cancer drugs were included in this study, including 16 indications for haematologic malignancies and 36 indications for lymphomas. Among all the indications, 5 indications were approved via RA and 47 indications were approved via AA. 22 indications were from domestic manufacturers, which were all approved via AA, and 30 indications were from imported manufacturers. Association between approval pathways and CSCO guideline recommendation levels

For the CSCO guideline recommendation level, 39 indications obtained recommendation level I, 11 indications obtained recommendation level II and 2 indications obtained recommendation level III. Among the indications approved via AA, those approved via BT+PR and CA all obtained recommendation level I. Indications approved via AA and RA were similarly likely to obtain level I (p>0.05) (Figure 2 and online supplemental eTable S3). No significant differences in the CSCO guideline recommendation level for haematological cancer drug indications were observed between different manufacturers (online supplemental eTable S4).

Time difference between NMPA approval and CSCO guideline recommendation

Among all the indications, 33 haematological cancer indications obtained the CSCO guideline recommendation level after approval by the NMPA, and 19 indications obtained recommendation level before being approved by the NMPA (including 14 indications approved via AA) (online supplemental figure S1). The average time for indications approved via RA to obtain the CSCO recommendation level is 7.6 months before the indications were approved by the NMPA. The average time for indication approved via AA to obtain the recommended level is 5.51 months after the indications were approved by the NMPA. This finding indicated that indications approved via RA would obtain the CSCO guideline recommendation level earlier, whereas indications approved via AA would obtain the CSCO guideline recommendation level later. For indications approved via PR and RA, there was a difference in the average time from obtaining CSCO guideline recommendation level to obtaining NMPA approval (p=0.03) (table 2 and online supplemental eTable S5).

Changes in CSCO guideline recommendation levels

For the indications obtaining the CSCO guideline recommendation level, 18 haematological cancer drug indications’ recommendation levels were changed, and 34 indications’ recommendation levels were unchanged. One example is sintilimab for treating classical Hodgkin’s lymphoma, which obtained the CSCO guideline recommendation level II in 2019 and changed to recommendation level I in 2023. Among the 18 indications for which the recommendation level was changed, most haematological cancer drug indications’ recommendation level (83%, 15/18) was updated. These indications were mainly approved via PR, CA+PR or CA. Five indications’ recommendation levels were decreased, four of which are approved via the AA (figure 3 and online supplemental eTable S6).

Discussion

This study explored the associations between approval approaches and the CSCO guideline for haematological cancer drug indications. Our findings demonstrated that haematological cancer drug indications were more likely to obtain CSCO guideline recommendation level I, and no significant difference was observed between the AA and RA for the CSCO guideline recommendation level. The timing required to obtain CSCO guideline recommendation level varied based on approval approaches, and indications approved via RA obtained CSCO guideline recommendations level more quickly. Furthermore, some haematological cancer drug indication’s recommendation levels were updated in the updated CSCO guidelines compared with the initial CSCO guideline recommendation level.

Anticancer drugs approved via AA typically rely on surrogate endpoints and have been approved more rapidly, which means the supporting evidence is often less mature at the time of regulatory decision-making.30–32 This immaturity has led agencies such as the FDA and EMA to adopt more cautious or less favourable evaluations for indications approved via AA in their subsequent assessments.13 33–35 This perspective is consistent with findings reported by Edward et al, which showed that anticancer drug indications approved via AA were assigned lower evidence and treatment preference ratings in the national comprehensive cancer network (NCCN) guidelines compared with those via RA.36 However, this pattern was not observed in the CSCO guideline recommendation level. The underlying reason lies in CSCO’s broader and multidimensional evaluative framework. Beyond evidence maturity, the CSCO may also consider clinical need, drug accessibility, affordability and the clinical expert experience.37 38 These contextual factors may buffer the direct impact of approval approaches on recommendation level, resulting in those indications approved via AA not necessarily obtaining lower recommendation levels as observed in the NCCN guidelines. This holistic approach also helps explain why CSCO guideline recommendation levels tend to be generally optimistic: 39 indications obtained recommendation level I in our study. Strengthening evidence communication between the NMPA and CSCO is particularly important, given the differences in their decision-making logic and evidentiary requirements.

However, although AA’s intention to accelerate access to promising therapies, our study found that indications approved via AA generally obtained CSCO guideline recommendation levels later than those approved via RA. This delay appears to be causally linked to differences in evidence maturity. Indications approved via AA are frequently supported by surrogate endpoints with weaker links to overall survival, and confirmatory or post-marketing evidence often lags.16 22 39–46 This uncertainty directly influences clinical expert judgement, prompting the CSCO guideline committee to adopt a more cautious stance when evaluating indications approved via AA. Consistent with this interpretation, indications approved via AA commonly required significantly longer times to obtain guideline recommendation levels than indications approved via RA. These observations highlight how differences in evidence certainty translate into differing speeds of guideline adoption. The CSCO may further enhance timely integration of emerging domestic and international evidence to adjust recommendation level more quickly.

In this study, the CSCO guideline also adjusted some indications’ recommendation levels. This may reflect the CSCO’s dynamic updating mechanism. These changes were mainly driven by three interrelated dimensions.37 38 First, real-world evidence (RWE) has become an increasingly important component of the CSCO evidence framework. Well-designed real-world studies (such as multi-centre retrospective cohorts and case-control study) are classified as 2A/2B evidence level and recognised as valuable supplements to clinical trial data.38 As high-quality RWE accumulates, it strengthens expert consensus and can support adjusting certain indications’recommendation level in updated guideline revisions.38 For example, real-world studies in China showed that axicabtagene achieved better therapeutic outcomes than those reported in its pivotal trial, which increased expert confidence and contributed to its elevation to a recommendation level I in the 2023 CSCO lymphoma guideline.38 47 Second, safety, toxicity and affordability are crucial considerations for haematological cancer, which often require prolonged treatment courses.48–50 High-cost therapies with substantial toxicity profiles may initially receive more conservative recommendation levels. As more data from routine clinical practice accumulate—demonstrating manageable toxicity in real-world settings—and once a drug is included in the National Basic Medical Insurance formulary, thereby improving its economic accessibility, the recommendation level may be upgraded accordingly.51 Acalabrutinib is a representative example: despite its favourable efficacy in chronic lymphocytic leukaemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma, its initial CSCO recommendation was constrained by accessibility and cost considerations (level II).52 53 After NMPA approval, expanding clinical evidence, improved affordability following insurance inclusion and better characterisation of its safety and tolerability, its recommendation level was upgraded from level II to level I.38 54 55 Third, the evolving therapeutic landscape influences CSCO recommendation levels. Growing evidence indicates that bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy, offer superior efficacy and safety compared with conventional chemo-immunotherapy for relapsed lymphoma.56 57 Therefore, CSCO has correspondingly increased recommendation level for BTK inhibitors and downgraded some traditional regimens.38

In some cases, indications obtained CSCO recommendation level before being approved by the NMPA via AA. This often reflected urgent clinical need or the absence of effective alternatives in China. A representative example is ivosidenib for relapsed or refractory isocitrate dehydrogenase 1-mutated (IDH1) acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). AML primarily affects older adults, progresses rapidly and carries an extremely poor prognosis without timely treatment, and no targeted therapy addressing IDH1 mutations was available in China at that time.58 Multiple trials demonstrating its meaningful clinical benefit and a favourable safety.59–62 The NMPA accepted its application in 2021.63 Based on the overall evidence, the CSCO assigned the recommendation level II to this indication.64 In specific contexts of clinical urgency, the CSCO’s decision-making logic may diverge from that of the NMPA.

This study is the first to assess the association between NMPA approval approaches for haematological cancer drug indications and CSCO guideline recommendation levels, the time to obtain recommendation level, and the adjustments of recommendation level. However, there are several limitations to this study that should not be overlooked. First, prior to 2017, the CSCO guidelines had not yet subdivided drugs into specific recommendation levels but only distinguished between essential and optional drugs, and this historical context limited our in-depth analyses. Second, not all guidelines issued by the CSCO explicitly provide a division into evidence categories. Thus, it was not possible to conduct an in-depth analysis on the basis of the evidence level. Furthermore, the relatively limited number of indications approved via the BT+PR and BT+PR+CA approaches may have affected the generalisability and representativeness of the results to a certain extent, thus introducing a potential bias in the data analysis. Finally, because the systematic nature of the NMPA in terms of drug data disclosure has yet to be strengthened, we may not have been able to cover all the relevant information during the data extraction process, and there is a certain risk of data omission.

Conclusion

Most indications ultimately obtained high recommendation levels, indications approved via AA and RA obtained similar recommendation levels but obtaining the CSCO guideline recommendation levels at different speeds. Improved communication of evidence between the NMPA and CSCO may help ensure the timely integration of newly emerging domestic and international evidence into guideline updates, supporting more effective and consistent guideline recommendations. Additionally, whether adjustments in guideline recommendation levels ultimately translate into tangible patient benefit remains uncertain, and future studies should focus on better aligning guideline recommendation level with real-world patient outcomes.
