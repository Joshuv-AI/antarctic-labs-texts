Bigraph independence: a mixture of the five natural independences
Nicolas Gilliers, David Jekel

Abstract
. We introduce a notion of non-commutative joint independence for multiple algebras in a non-commutative probability space. The pairwise relationships between these algebras are encoded by a graph with two edge sets—a combinatorial structure we call a bigraph — and naturally encompass the five fundamental types of independence: tensor, free, (anti)monotone, and Boolean. It subsumes the BMT independence of Arizmendi–Mendoza–Vazquez-Becerra (when all pairwise relationships are Boolean, (anti)monotone, or tensor) and the ϵ \epsilon or Λ \Lambda -independence of Młotkowski (when the pairwise relationships are tensor and free). We present explicit combinatorial moment formulas, a Hilbert space construction, and natural associativity relations within this setting. Furthermore, we demonstrate that bigraph independence emerges in the asymptotic behavior of tensor product random matrix models with respect to a vector state, encompassing the Charlesworth–Collins model for ε \varepsilon -independence as a special case and offering a random matrix perspective on BMT independence.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

Free probability originated largely from the work of Voiculescu in the 80’s and has since grown into a surprisingly effective framework for the study of high-dimensional phenomena in random matrix theory, among many other achievements of Voiculescu’s theory. While freeness, as a non-commutative analogue of classical independence, was the main focus of Voiculescu’s work, it was soon realised that the notion of non-commutative independence, formalised in the early 2000s, contains more than freeness and classical stochastic independence. In particular, Speicher
[
34
]
, Schürmann, Ben Ghorbal
[
15
]
and Muraki
[
29
]
independently classified the five “natural” notions of non-commutative independence (that arise from an associative
binary product
on non-commutative probability spaces): tensor (or
classical
),
free
,
Boolean
,
monotone
, and
anti-monotone
independence. The parallels between these five independences go strikingly far: the joint moments for several independent random variables can be described using the combinatorial machinery of cumulants, expressing joint moments as a sum of cumulants
[
31
,
32
,
2
,
24
]
indexed by partitions (for tensor independence, all partitions are used; for free, non-crossing partitions; for Boolean, interval partitions; and for monotone, labelled partitions satisfying a certain ordering condition). Further, there are parallel central limit theorems (and limit theorems for iterated convolution in general) with both additive and multiplicative versions
[
31
,
32
,
28
,
35
]
.

Beyond these five natural independences, numerous constructions have emerged that “independently” join non-commutative probability spaces, broadening the concept beyond the historical categorical formulation. Generally, by a non-commutative independence relation, we mean a rule prescribing how to compute
joint moments
of elements from several subalgebras
A_{1}
, …,
A_{N}
of a non-commutative probability space
(A,\varphi)
, in terms of the individual moments
\varphi|_{A_{j}}
. For the five natural independences, this rule for any number
N
of algebras follows from the prescription at
N=2
. However, this need not always be the case—and in the present work, it is not.

Our focus is on
mixed
non-commutative independences, where the
pairwise relationship
between
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
can be any one of Muraki’s five types. Thus, different pairs of algebras may exhibit distinct independences: for instance,
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
might be freely independent,
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
tensor independent, and
A_{1}
and
A_{3}
Boolean independent. Importantly, these pairwise relations alone do not determine the joint moments of
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
, and
A_{3}
(see Remark
1.6
; a mixed independence relation must explicitly prescribe these moments.

There are already several examples of such mixed independences in the literature, appearing in various contexts including graph products of algebras, random matrix models, and operator algebras. In particular, we mention the following:

•

A mixture of tensor and free independence is studied in
[
30
]
(
\Lambda
or
\varepsilon
independence). This arises from graph products of von Neumann algebras.

•

A mixture of free, Boolean, and monotone (anti-monotone) independence is studied in
[
18
,
19
]
. This generalises the mixtures of monotone and Boolean independence in
[
37
]
and the mixtures of free and Boolean independence in
[
21
]
.

•

A mixture of Boolean, monotone (anti-monotone), and tensor products is given in
[
3
]
.

Thus, two distinct models yield the pairwise relations of Boolean, monotone, and anti-monotone independence as described in
[
3
]
and
[
19
]
. Beyond these, multiple approaches exist for combining different notions of independence in frameworks involving two or more states, such as the
c-free independence
of Bo
\dot{\rm z}
ejko, Leinert and Speicher
[
8
]
. In this work, however, we restrict our attention to non-commutative probability spaces with a single state. We also mention traffic independence, traffic spaces and the recently introduced notion of freeness for tensors
[
7
]
, which extend the original categorical framework to the category of operadic algebras over a certain operad of graphs. We emphasise that our work is only concerned with plain standard algebras, although associativity for our mixed independence will naturally be formulated by using the language of operads in §
2.5
.

It is important to note that, in all previously cited works, the mixed independences studied involve only
some
of the five natural types. To date, no mixed independence relation has accommodated all of Muraki’s five. In this paper, we define for the first time a mixed independence that permits
all five pairwise relations
—tensor, free, Boolean, monotone, and anti-monotone independence—to coexist with a
single state
. We term this new notion
bigraph independence
, as it is characterised by a pair of graphs on the same vertex set encoding the pairwise relations.

Our mixed independence generalises both the tensor-free graph independence of
[
30
]
and the BMT independence of
[
3
]
, and thus differs from
[
18
,
19
]
for free-Boolean-(anti-)monotone mixtures in general. Our bigraph independence arises naturally in random matrix models on a tensor product over several sites (or “strings”) as in
[
27
,
11
]
, incorporating rank
1
projections in some tensorands to mimic the tensor product model of
[
3
]
. We also present a Hilbert space model realizing our mixed independence (see
3
, thereby enabling the construction of independent copies of arbitrary non-commutative probability spaces.

1.2.
Bigraph independence

We assume familiarity with the basic theory of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras. By a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-probability space
we mean a pair
(A,\varphi)
where
A
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and
\varphi:A\to\mathbb{C}
is a state. We will begin by introducing the combinatorial data and stating the formula for joint moments in our model of mixed independence.

Definition 1.1
.

A
bigraph
is a triple
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})
where

•

\mathcal{V}
is a (finite or countable) set of vertices.

•

\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
are edge sets of two types.

•

\mathcal{E}_{1}
is reflexive:
\Delta:=\{(v,v):v\in\mathcal{V}\}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{1}
.

•

\mathcal{E}_{2}
is irreflexive:
\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\Delta=\varnothing
.

•

\mathcal{E}_{2}
is symmetric:
(v,w)\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\iff(w,v)\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
.

We also write
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}:=\{(v,w)\in\mathcal{V}^{2}:(w,v)\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\}
.

We note that
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{1})
is a directed graph with reflexive loops, while
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{2})
is an undirected, loopless graph. In Proposition
1.5
, we show how Boolean, monotone, free, and tensor pairwise relations are encoded by these two edge sets.
Let
(A,\varphi)
be a non-commutative probability space, and let
(A_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
be a family of subalgebras of
A
.
We aim to express
\varphi(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})
for
a_{j}\in A_{c(j)}
given a labeling
c:[k]\to\mathcal{V}
.
As usual, joint moment formulas are indexed by set partitions of
[k]
. A partition
\pi
is a family of disjoint nonempty blocks whose union is
[k]
; the set of all partitions is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(k)
. For
i,j\in[k]
, we write
i\sim_{\pi}j
if
i
and
j
lie in the same block of
\pi
.

Definition 1.2
.

Given a bigraph
\mathcal{G}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and a labeling
c:[k]\to\mathcal{V}
, define
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})\subseteq\mathcal{P}(k)
to be the set of partitions
\pi
satisfying:

(1)

If
i\sim_{\pi}j
, then
c(i)=c(j)
.

(2)

If
i_{1}<j<i_{2}
and
i_{1}\sim_{\pi}i_{2}
, then
(c(i_{1}),c(j))\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
.

(3)

If
i_{1}<j_{1}<i_{2}<j_{2}
and
i_{1}\sim_{\pi}i_{2}
and
j_{1}\sim_{\pi}j_{2}
and
i_{1}\not\sim_{\pi}j_{1}
, then
(c(i_{2}),c(j_{1}))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
.

Figure 1.
On the left, example of a bigraph
\mathcal{G}
: edges of type
1
are drawn as solid black edges and edges of type
2
are drawn as dashed edges. On the right : partitions in the leftmost column belong to
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, those in the rightmost column do
not
belong to
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
.

Remark 1.3
.

The set
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
depends only on the induced sub-bigraph on
{\rm Im}(c)
; thus if
\mathcal{G}_{|c}
denotes the restriction of
\mathcal{G}
to vertices appearing in
c
, then
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G}_{|c})
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k)
a partition, it will be convenient to work
sometimes with colorings
c:[k]\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
and sometimes with colorings defined on
the blocks of
\pi
, that is, functions
\pi\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
.
The space
\mathcal{V}^{\pi}
can be canonically identified with the subspace of
\mathcal{V}^{k}
consisting of functions which are constant on each block. We use this identification throughout the rest of the paper.

Definition 1.4

(Bigraph independence)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})
be a bigraph. In a non-commutative probability space
(A,\varphi)
, a family of subalgebras
(A_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
is
\mathcal{G}
-independent if for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, labeling
c:[k]\to\mathcal{V}
, and elements
a_{j}\in A_{c(j)}
,

(1.1)

\varphi(a_{1}\cdots a_{k})=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})}K^{\free}_{\pi}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}),

where
K^{\free}_{\pi}
denotes the
partitioned free cumulant
(see
[
31
]
).

This definition is not vacuous: in Section
3
we construct
\mathcal{G}
-independent copies of arbitrary
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-probability spaces.
Moreover, the pairwise relations of Boolean, (anti)monotone, free, and tensor independence are exactly encoded by the sets
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
as follows.

Proposition 1.5
.

Consider the situation of Definition
1.4
. Let
v,w\in\mathcal{V}
with
v\neq w
. Then
A_{v}
and
A_{w}
are:

(1)

Boolean independent if
(v,w)\in\mathcal{E}_{\Bool}:=\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}\setminus(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1})
;

(2)

monotone independent if
(v,w)\in\mathcal{E}_{\mono}:=\mathcal{E}_{1}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
;

(3)

anti-monotone independent if
(v,w)\in\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{\mono}=\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{1}
;

(4)

freely independent if
(v,w)\in\mathcal{E}_{\free}:=(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1})\setminus\mathcal{E}_{2}
;

(5)

tensor independent if
(v,w)\in\mathcal{E}_{\ten}:=\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
;

(6)

if
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
, then
(A_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
are graph-independent (
\varepsilon
-independent) in the sense of
[
30
]
with respect to
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{2})
;

(7)

if
\mathcal{E}_{2}\supseteq(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1})\setminus\Delta
, then
(A_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
are BMT-independent in the sense of
[
3
]
with respect to the digraph
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{1}\setminus\Delta)
.

For proofs, see Section
2.2
.

Remark 1.6
.

In the case where
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\varnothing
, the pairwise relations between the algebras will be free, (anti)monotone, or Boolean independence. Thus it is naturally to compare these cases of bigraph independence with the mixtures coming from digraph independence in
[
19
]
. We can already see from
[
19
, Remark 3.24]
that these independences provide different joint moments when the pairwise relations are only (anti)monotone and Boolean, since bigraph independence agrees with BMT independence
[
3
]
in this case. In the case of a general free, (anti)monotone, and Boolean mixture, both bigraph independence and digraph independence are special cases of the tree independence of
[
18
]
; can therefore evaluate the joint moments of both in terms of the moment formula from that paper, and show that they disagree.

Remark 1.7
.

The reader may notice that the edges in
\mathcal{E}_{2}\setminus(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1})
do not play any role in the definitions. Indeed, in Definition
1.2
(3), if
i_{1}<j_{1}<i_{2}<j_{2}
and
i_{1}\sim_{\pi}i_{2}
and
j_{1}\sim_{\pi}j_{2}
, we have by (2) that
(c(i_{1}),c(j_{1}))\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and also
(c(j_{1}),c(i_{2}))\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
, and therefore
(c(i_{1}),c(j_{1}))
is automatically in
\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
. Moreover, in Proposition

, if

, the pairwise relation of

and

is unaffected by whether
(v,w)\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
. Hence, in general,
we can assume without loss of generality
that
\mathcal{E}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
by discarding the other elements from
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, and the definition of
\mathcal{G}
-independence will be unaffected. However, allowing more flexibility in the choice of
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is convenient for stating our random matrix construction in Theorem
1.10
.

Remark 1.8
.

Any pairwise combination of Boolean, (anti)monotone, free, and tensor independence can be realized by some bigraph, which can additionally be taken with
\mathcal{E}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
. Indeed, suppose we are given a vertex set
\mathcal{V}
and sets

\mathcal{E}_{\Bool},\mathcal{E}_{\mono},\mathcal{E}_{\free},\mathcal{E}_{\ten}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}

satisfying

•

\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{E}_{\Bool}\sqcup\mathcal{E}_{\mono}\sqcup\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{\mono}\sqcup\mathcal{E}_{\free}\sqcup\mathcal{E}_{\ten}
,

•

\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{\Bool}=\mathcal{E}_{\Bool}
and
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{\free}=\mathcal{E}_{\free}
.

•

\Delta\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{\free}
.

We can then set

\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathcal{E}_{\mono}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\free}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\ten},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{2}=\mathcal{E}_{\ten},

and this choice will satisfy

\mathcal{E}_{\Bool}=(\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V})\setminus(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}),\quad\mathcal{E}_{\mono}=\mathcal{E}_{1}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1},\quad\mathcal{E}_{\free}=\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{2},\quad\mathcal{E}_{\ten}=\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}.

In
3
we provide a Hilbert space model for bigraph independence (Theorem

) that allows us to realize
\mathcal{G}
-independent copies of any

-probability spaces.

.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bigraph, and let
(A_{v},\varphi_{v})
be
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-probability spaces. Then there exists a

-probability space

-independent.

We remark that, as in the monotone and Boolean cases for instance, the maps

. The Hilbert space is a direct sum of subspaces indexed by words on the alphabet

; we

The proof of Theorem

1.3.
Matrix models

We next describe how our bigraph independence arises naturally from random matrix models in a tensor-product setting, which generalize the construction of
[
11
]
.

Theorem 1.10
.

•

Consider two sets
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{V}
.

•

For each
v\in\mathcal{V}
, let
S_{v}^{(1)}
,
S_{v}^{(2)}
,
S_{v}^{(3)}
be a partition of
\mathcal{S}
such that
S_{v}^{(1)}
is non-empty.

•

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})
, where

\mathcal{E}_{1}=\{(v,w):S_{v}^{(1)}\cap S_{w}^{(2)}=\varnothing\},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{2}=\{(v,w):S_{v}^{(1)}\cap S_{w}^{(1)}=\varnothing\}.

•

Let
{\mathbb{M}_{N}}
denote the set of
N\times N
complex matrices; fix a unit vector
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
; and consider
{\mathbb{M}_{N}}^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}
as a non-commutative probability space with the state given by the vector
\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}
.

•

Define the (not necessarily unital)
*
-homomorphism

\lambda_{v}^{(N)}:{\mathbb{M}_{N}}^{\otimes S_{v}^{(1)}}\to{\mathbb{M}_{N}}^{\otimes\mathcal{S}},\qquad\lambda_{v}^{(N)}(A)=A\otimes(\xi\xi^{*})^{\otimes S_{v}^{(2)}}\otimes I_{N}^{\otimes S_{v}^{(3)}},

where
\xi\xi^{*}
denotes the rank-one projection onto the span of
\xi
.

•

For each
v\in V
, let
I_{v}
be a finite index set, and let
\mathbf{A}_{i}^{(N)}=(A_{v,i}^{(N)})_{i\in I_{v}}
be random matrices satisfying, almost surely,

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\|A_{v,i}^{(N)}\|<\infty.

•

For each
v\in\mathcal{V}
, let
U_{v}^{(N)}
be a Haar random unitary matrix in
{\mathbb{M}_{N}}^{\otimes S_{v}^{(1)}}
, such that the
U_{v}^{(N)}
’s for
v\in\mathcal{V}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
are jointly independent of the
(A_{v,i}^{(N)})
’s for
v\in\mathcal{V}
,
i\in I_{v}
, and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and let

\mathbf{B}_{v}^{(N)}=(\lambda_{v}^{(N)}(U_{v}^{(N)}A_{v,i}^{(N)}(U_{v}^{(N)})^{*}))_{i\in I_{v}}.

Then almost surely the families
\mathbf{B}_{v}^{(N)}
for
v\in\mathcal{V}
are asymptotically
\mathcal{G}
-independent in the following sense: For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
c:[k]\to\mathcal{V}
and non-commutative polynomials
p_{j}
in variables indexed by
I_{c(j)}
with no constant term, we have

(1.2)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\biggl|\left\langle\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}\,,\,p_{1}(\mathbf{B}_{c(1)}^{(N)})\dots p_{k}(\mathbf{B}_{c(k)})\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}\right\rangle-\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})}K_{\free,\pi}(p_{1}(\mathbf{B}_{c(1)}^{(N)}),\dots,p_{k}(\mathbf{B}_{c(k)}))\biggr|=0

almost surely, where the free cumulants are computed with respect to the state given by
\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}
. We also remark that for each
v
and polynomial

with no constant term,

(1.3)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Bigl|\left\langle\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}\,,\,p(\mathbf{B}_{v}^{(N)})\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}\right\rangle-\tr_{N^{S_{v}^{(1)}}}[p(\mathbf{A}_{v})]\Bigr|=0\text{ almost surely.}

[
11
]
. Indeed, the random matrix models from

). When

Meanwhile, the matrix model also includes the BMT product construction of

\{(v,w):S_{v}^{(1)}\cap S_{w}^{(2)}=\varnothing\}=\mathcal{E}_{1}.

Meanwhile, note that for

, we have

S_{v}^{(1)}\cap S_{w}^{(1)}=\begin{cases}\{(v,w),(w,v)\}&\text{if }(v,w)\not\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\\
\varnothing&\text{if }(v,w)\in\mathcal{E}_{2},\end{cases}

and hence

\mathcal{E}_{2}=\{(v,w):S_{v}^{(1)}\cap S_{w}^{(1)}=\varnothing\}.

1.4.
Further questions

We propose the following questions as a natural continuation of our work:

(1)

Prove central limit theorem, Poisson limit theorem, and more general limit theorems for sums of independent random variables with respect to a sequence of bigraphs
\mathcal{G}_{n}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|\operatorname{Hom}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime},\mathcal{G}_{n})|}{|\mathcal{V}_{n}|^{|\mathcal{V}^{\prime}|}}

exists for every bigraph
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Note that in many situations the limit is described by the homomorphisms into a
bigraphon
, providing the analogue of graphon convergence. We expect that limit theorems analogous to
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
will hold. The main challenge will be the generalization to measures with unbounded support since we have not yet developed the appropriate analytic theory of convolution.

(2)

Prove analogous theorems for products of independent variables. We remark that a challenge in this case is that the independent subalgebras
A_{v}
may not be
unitally
included into
A
, but a unitary in
A_{v}
can be extended to a unitary in
A
by adding
1_{A}-1_{A_{v}}
as in
[
18
, §10.3]
.

(3)

Given a bigraphon, construct a Fock space and appropriate operators to model the central limit distribution, and more generally, the analogues infinitely divisible distributions. Such a construction should generalize
[
10
, §2]
and proceed analogously to
[
19
, §6]

(4)

The matrix models in Theorem
1.10
could be generalized in various ways. For instance, it would be natural to consider a Haar random unitary chosen from the subgroup that fixes
\mathbb{C}\xi^{\otimes A}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes B}
for some disjoint subsets
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{S}
. For instance, we could force the unitary acting
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes S_{j}^{(1)}}
to fix a subspace of this form. In principle, the same techniques with Weingarten calculus could be used to analyze this model as well, leading potentially to new moment conditions.

(5)

In Theorem
1.10
, one could also consider uniformly random permutation matrices in place of Haar random unitaries, and attempt to analyze these models using similar techniques as
[
4
]
and
[
12
]
.

1.5.
Organization

The remainder of the paper is divided into three sections.

In Section
2
, we prove a formula for the joint moments of
\mathcal{G}
-independent variables in terms of Boolean cumulants. We use this formula to compare with other existing independences. We also prove at the end of this section that our

-independence is associative, in a certain sense.

In Section

, we construct a Hilbert space and inclusions allowing the construction of

-independence copies of variables.

Finally, in Section

-independence.

Bigraph independence and relations to other independences

2.1.
Combinatorial setup

We begin with some combinatorial definitions and lemmas that will be useful in the sequel.

Definition 2.1

(Partitions)
.

We use the notation
[k]=\{1,\dots,k\}
. A
partition
of
[k]
is a collection
\pi
of non-empty subsets of
[k]
, called
blocks
, such that
[k]=\bigsqcup_{B\in\pi}B
. The set of partitions of
[k]
is denoted
\mathcal{P}(k)
.

Definition 2.2

(Crossings, non-crossing partitions)
.

For a partition
\pi
of
[k]
, a
crossing
is a sequence of indices
i<i^{\prime}<j<j^{\prime}
such that
i
and
j
are in some block
B
and
i^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime}
are in some block
B^{\prime}\neq B
. In this case, we also say that
B
and
B^{\prime}
are
crossing
.

We say that a partition
\pi
is
non-crossing
if it has no crossings. We denote by
\mathcal{NC}(k)
the set of non-crossing partitions of
[k]
.

Definition 2.3

(Nested blocks)
.

If
\pi
is a partition of
[k]
and
B^{\prime},B\in\pi
, we say that
B^{\prime}
is
nested inside

B
if there exist
i,j\in B
such that
i<j
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq\{i+1,\dots,j-1\}\subseteq[k]\setminus B
; in other words, there are no intervening indices of
B
between
i
and
j
, and
B^{\prime}
lies entirely between
i
and
j
. In this case, we write
B\prec B^{\prime}
.

Definition 2.4

(Separated blocks)
.

If
\pi
is a partition of
[k]
and
B,B^{\prime}\in\pi
, we say that
B
and
B^{\prime}
are
separated
if there exists
j\in[k]
such that either
B\subseteq\{1,\dots,j\}
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq\{j+1,\dots,k\}
or
B^{\prime}\subseteq\{1,\dots,j\}
and
B\subseteq\{j+1,\dots,k\}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k)
and let
B
and
B^{\prime}
be two distinct blocks in
\pi
. Then exactly one of the following occurs:

•

B
and
B^{\prime}
are crossing.

•

B
and
B^{\prime}
are separated.

•

B
is nested inside
B^{\prime}
.

•

B^{\prime}
is nested inside
B
.

This fact is well-known. For proof, see
[
19
, Lemma 2.6]
; note that there the entire partition
\pi
is assumed to be non-crossing, but the proof shows that if the two blocks
B
and
B^{\prime}
are non-crossing, then
B
and
B^{\prime}
are either separated or one is nested inside the other.

We next describe the natural choice of morphisms for bigraphs, which will then allow us to reformulate Definition
1.2
in terms of morphisms of bigraphs.

Definition 2.6

(Bigraph morphisms)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{1}=(\mathcal{V}^{(1)},\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(1)},\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(1)})
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}=(\mathcal{V}^{(2)},\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(2)},\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(2)})
be two bigraphs (see Definition
1.1
). A
bigraph morphism

\mathcal{G}_{1}\to\mathcal{G}_{2}
is a set-map
f\colon\mathcal{V}^{(1)}\to\mathcal{V}^{(2)}
sending an edge of type
1
(resp. of type
2
) to an edge of type
1
(resp. of type 2), i.e.,

(v,w)\in\mathcal{E}_{i}^{(1)}\implies(f(v),f(w))\in\mathcal{E}_{i}^{(2)}

for
i=1,2
and
v,w\in\mathcal{V}
.

Each partition of
[k]
has a naturally associated bigraph defined as follows.

Definition 2.7

(Bigraph associated to a partition)
.

Let
\pi
be a partition of
[k]
. We define a bigraph
\mathcal{G}(\pi)=(\pi,\mathcal{E}_{1}(\pi),\mathcal{E}_{2}(\pi))
where the vertices are the blocks of
\pi
and the two edge sets are given by

\mathcal{E}^{\pi}_{1}=\{(B,B^{\prime})\in\pi\times\pi\colon\exists i_{1},i_{2}\in B,j\in B^{\prime},i_{1}<j<i_{2}\}\cup\Delta,

where
\Delta=\{(B,B):B\in\pi\}
, and

\mathcal{E}^{\pi}_{2}=\mathcal{E}^{\pi}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\pi}}\backslash\Delta,

Remark 2.8
.

In light of Lemma
2.5
, for a partition
\pi
and
B,B^{\prime}\in\pi
:

•

(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}(\pi)
if and only if
B^{\prime}
and
B
are crossing or
B^{\prime}
is nested inside
B
.

•

(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{2}(\pi)
if and only if
B
and
B^{\prime}
are crossing.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k)
, such that
c
is constant on the blocks of
\pi
then
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})}
if and only the labelling
c
defines a bigraph morphism
\mathcal{G}(\pi)\to\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k)
such that
c
is constant on the blocks of
\pi
. The labelling
c
defines a bigraph morphism
\mathcal{G}(\pi)\to\mathcal{G}
if and only if for any two blocks
B,B^{\prime}\in\pi
,

(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}_{1}\Rightarrow(c(B),c(B^{\prime}))\in\mathcal{E}_{1},

(B,B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}^{\pi}_{2}\Rightarrow(c(B),c(B^{\prime}))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}.

By definition of
\mathcal{E}^{\pi}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\pi}_{2}
, this is equivalent to conditions (2) and (3) of Definition
1.2
. Condition (1) is satisfied by hypothesis.
∎

.

Let

\varepsilon=\mathcal{E}_{2}

Proof.

The claim will follow from comparing our moment formula with the one for

[

be a

be
*

for

(2.1)

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bigraph with
\mathcal{E}_{2}\supseteq\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\setminus\Delta

in the sense of
[

Lemma 2.12
.

Fix a bigraph
\mathcal{G}
and
c:[k]\to\mathcal{V}
. Assume that
\mathcal{E}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\setminus\Delta
. Then
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})

\pi\leq\ker_{\mathcal{G}}(c)

Assuming that
\mathcal{E}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\setminus\Delta

(3’)

For each

\pi

and

\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})

Lemma 2.19

Fix

and
\mathcal{G}
as above and let

(1)

(2)

Proof.

(1) First, note that since

is antisymmetric and irreflexive. So it remains to show that

is transitive. Suppose that

. Then

and
B_{0}\prec B_{1}\prec B_{2}
, which verifies the first two conditions of Definition
2.15
. For the third condition, suppose that
B_{0}\prec B^{\prime}\prec B_{2}
. We apply Lemma
2.5
to

and
B^{\prime}
, and note that
B^{\prime}
and
B_{1}
cannot be separated since
B^{\prime}\prec B_{2}
and
B_{1}\prec B_{2}
.

•

If
B^{\prime}
crosses

, then since
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, we must have
(c(B^{\prime}),c(B_{1}))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{2}\cup\Delta
as desired.

•

If

, then
B_{1}\prec B^{\prime}\prec B_{2}
and hence
(c(B^{\prime}),c(B_{1}))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\cup\Delta
by definition of
B_{1}\prec_{un}B_{2}
.

•

If
B^{\prime}\prec B_{1}
, then
B_{0}\prec B^{\prime}\prec B_{2}
, and hence

.

Hence,

.

(2) Suppose that

and

. Since

and

, the blocks
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
cannot cross. They also cannot be separated since

and

. Hence, by Lemma

, we have

or

. If

and

(3) This is immediate from (2).

Lemma 2.20
.

Fix
c
and
\mathcal{G}
as above. Then for each
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, there exists a unique
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}
such that
\sigma\geq\pi
and for every
B\in\sigma
,
\min B\sim_{\pi}\max B
.

Proof.

For each block
B\in\pi
, let
m(B)
be the minimal block such that
m(B)\prec_{un}B
using Lemma
2.19
. Define
B\sim_{un}B^{\prime}
if
m(B)=m(B^{\prime})
. Let
\sigma
be the partition whose blocks are the unions of the equivalence classes of
\sim_{un}
. This means in particular that if
B\prec_{un}B^{\prime}
in
\pi
, then
B
and
B^{\prime}
are in the same block of
\sigma
.

We first show that for
B\in\sigma
, we have
\min B\sim_{\sigma}\max B
. Each
B\in\sigma
is the union of some equivalence class of
\sim_{un}
. By construction of
\sim_{un}
, the equivalence class has a unique minimal element
B^{\prime}
. Since
B^{\prime}\prec B^{\prime\prime}
for all the other
B^{\prime\prime}
in the equivalence class, we have that
\min B=\min B^{\prime}
and
\max B=\max B^{\prime}
. Hence,
\min B
and
\max B
are in the same block
B^{\prime}
of
\pi
.

Next, we show that
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, checking each condition of Definition
1.2
:

(1)

Clearly, the blocks of
\sigma
are monochromatic since each equivalence class of
\sim_{un}
is monochromatic.

(2)

Suppose that
i_{1}<j<i_{2}
and
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
are in the same block
B
of
\sigma
. By making
i_{1}
smaller and
i_{2}
larger if necessary, we can assume without loss of generality that
i_{1}=\min B
and
i_{2}=\max B
. Hence, by the preceding paragraph,
i_{1}\sim_{\pi}i_{2}
. Since
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, we have
(c(i_{1}),c(j))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
.

(3)

Suppose that two distinct blocks
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
of
\sigma
cross. If there exist blocks
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
of
\pi
with
B_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq B_{1}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq B_{2}
such that
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
cross, then we must have
(c(B_{1}^{\prime}),c(B_{2}^{\prime}))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
because
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, so in this case we are done.

Hence, suppose that no blocks of
\pi
within
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
cross each other. Recall that
\min B_{1}
and
\max B_{1}
are in the same block
B_{1}^{\prime}
of
\pi
, and
\min B_{2}
and
\max B_{2}
are in the same block
B_{2}^{\prime}
of
\pi
. Note that
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
cannot be separated since then
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
could not cross. We assumed that
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
do not cross, and therefore
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec B_{2}^{\prime}
or
B_{2}\prec B_{1}^{\prime}
. By symmetry, assume without loss of generality that
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec B_{2}^{\prime}
. Now because
B_{1}
crosses
B_{2}
, there must be some element
j
of
B_{1}
between
\min B_{2}^{\prime}
and
\max B_{2}^{\prime}
. The block
B_{1}^{\prime\prime}
of
\pi
that contains

must be nested inside in
B_{2}^{\prime}
(since by assumption it cannot cross it). Therefore,
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec B_{2}^{\prime}\prec B_{1}^{\prime\prime}
, and
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec_{un}B_{1}^{\prime\prime}
since

. By definition of
\prec_{un}
, we have
(c(B_{1}^{\prime}),c(B_{2}^{\prime}))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\cup\Delta
.

Finally, note that

cannot equal
c(B_{2})
; indeed, if this happened, then

would imply that
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec_{un}B_{2}^{\prime}
by the same reasoning as in Lemma
2.19
(2), which would force

to equal
B_{2}
. Hence,
(c(B_{1}),c(B_{2}))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
as desired.

Next, we show that
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}
. Suppose for contradiction that
B_{1}\prec_{un}B_{2}
in
\sigma
. For
j=1,2
, let
B_{j}^{\prime}
be the block of
\pi
that contains
\min B_{j}
and
\max B_{j}
. We claim that
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec_{un}B_{2}^{\prime}
. It is immediate that
c(B_{1}^{\prime})=c(B_{2}^{\prime})
and
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec B_{2}^{\prime}
. Suppose that
B^{\prime}
is a block of
\pi
with
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec B^{\prime}\prec B_{2}^{\prime}
. Let
B
be the block of
\sigma
that contains
B^{\prime}
, and we will show that
(c(B_{1}),c(B))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\cup\Delta
. If
B
crosses
B_{1}
or
B_{2}
, then this holds because
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, and we are done. Suppose that
B
does not cross
B_{1}
or
B_{2}
. Then, because
B
contains
B^{\prime}\prec B_{2}^{\prime}
, we must have that
B\prec B_{2}^{\prime}
and hence
B\prec B_{2}
. Similarly, since
B_{1}
contains
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec B^{\prime}
, we have
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec B
, and hence also
B_{1}\prec B
since they do not cross. Therefore,
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec_{un}B_{2}^{\prime}
. It follows that
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
must be in the same block of
\sigma
, which contradicts that
B_{1}\prec B_{2}
.

Finally, we show that
\sigma
is unique. Let
\sigma^{\prime}
be another partition satisfying the conditions in the lemma. First, we show that
\sigma\leq\sigma^{\prime}
, or equivalently, if two blocks
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
of
\pi
are in the same equivalence class, they must be in the same block of
\sigma^{\prime}
. It suffices to show that
B_{1}^{\prime}
must be in the same block as

in
\sigma^{\prime}
. Hence, it suffices to show that if
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec_{un}B_{2}^{\prime}
in
\pi
, then
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
are in the same block of
\sigma^{\prime}
.

Suppose for contradiction that
B_{1}^{\prime}
is in the block
B_{1}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
is in the block
B_{2}
in
\sigma
with
B_{1}\neq B_{2}
. Then we claim that
B_{1}\prec_{un}B_{2}
in
\sigma^{\prime}
. Clearly,
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are the same color. Since
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are the same color (hence cannot cross) and
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec B_{2}^{\prime}
, we must have
B_{1}\prec B_{2}
. Lastly, suppose that
B_{1}\prec B\prec B_{2}
for some block
B
in
\sigma^{\prime}
. Let
B^{\prime}
be the block of
\pi
containing
\max B
and
\min B
, and let
B_{1}^{\prime\prime}
be the block of
\pi
containing
\max B_{1}
and
\min B_{1}
. Then
B_{1}^{\prime\prime}\prec B^{\prime}\prec B_{2}^{\prime}
. Then the desired conclusion that
(c(B_{1}^{\prime\prime}),c(B^{\prime}))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\cup\Delta
will follow if we can show that

. This is immediate if
B_{1}^{\prime\prime}=B_{1}^{\prime}
. If
B_{1}^{\prime\prime}\neq B_{1}^{\prime}
, then we show that
B_{1}^{\prime\prime}\prec_{un}B_{1}^{\prime}
. Clearly, they are the same color and
B_{1}^{\prime\prime}\prec B_{1}^{\prime}
. Moreover, if
B_{1}^{\prime\prime}\prec C\prec B_{1}^{\prime}
in
\pi
, then
C
is either contained in
B_{1}
or it crosses
B_{1}
and hence
(c(B_{1}),c(C))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\cup\Delta
. So

as claimed.

This completes the proof that
\sigma^{\prime}\geq\sigma
. To conclude that
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma
, it remains to show that if
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are different blocks in
\sigma
, then they cannot be contained in the same block of
\sigma^{\prime}
. Of course, this is immediate if
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are different colors. Suppose
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are the same color, and hence do not cross.

•

Suppose that
B_{1}\prec B_{2}
. Since
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}
, we cannot have
B_{1}\prec_{un}B_{2}
, and so there exists another block
B
of
\sigma
with
B_{1}\prec B\prec B_{2}
and
(c(B_{1}),c(B))\not\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\cup\Delta
. If
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
were in the same block of
\sigma^{\prime}
, then this block would cross the block containing

, which contradicts
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
.

•

Of course, the same applies when
B_{2}\prec B_{1}
.

•

Suppose that
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are separated, and they are contained in the same block
A
of
\sigma^{\prime}
. Since nested blocks of
\sigma
cannot be in the same block of
\sigma^{\prime}
, the block

must the union of separated blocks
A_{1}
, …,
A_{m}
of
\sigma
(listed in order from left to right). By our hypotheses on
\sigma^{\prime}
,
\min A
and

are in the same block of

. But

and

are in different blocks of

The

Lemma 2.23

Fix
\mathcal{G}
and

. Then there is a bijection

\Phi:\bigsqcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}}\prod_{B\in\sigma}\mathcal{NC}_{\operatorname{irr}}(B)\to\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G}),\quad\Phi((\pi_{B})_{B\in\sigma})=\bigcup_{B\in\sigma}\pi_{B}.

The inverse
\Phi^{-1}
is obtained as follows: Given
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, let
\sigma=\operatorname{Env}^{0}(\pi)
, and for
B\in\sigma
, let
\pi_{B}
be the restriction of
\sigma
to
B
, and then
\Phi^{-1}(\pi)=(\pi_{B})_{B\in\sigma}
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi
be the map that sends
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
to
(\pi|_{B})_{B\in\sigma}
where
\sigma=\operatorname{Env}^{0}(\pi)
. To prove the lemma, we proceed by verifying that
\Phi
and
\Psi
map into the codomains that we have asserted, and that they are inverses of each other.

First, we show that
\Phi
maps into
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
. Fix
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}
and
\pi_{B}\in\mathcal{NC}_{\operatorname{irr}}(B)
for
B\in\sigma
. Let
\pi=\Phi((\pi_{B})_{B\in\sigma})
, and we will show that
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
. We verify the conditions of Definition
1.2
for
\pi
in turn:

(1)

The blocks of
\pi
are monochromatic since
\pi\leq\sigma
.

(2)

Suppose that
i_{1}<j<j_{2}
with
i_{1}\sim_{\pi}i_{2}
. Then
i_{1}\sim_{\sigma}i_{2}
since
\pi\leq\sigma
. Hence, because
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, we have
(c(i_{1}),c(j))\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
.

(3)

Suppose that two blocks
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
in
\pi
cross. Since each
\pi_{B}
is non-crossing,
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
must be in different blocks of
\sigma
. Call these blocks
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
. Then
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are crossing. Hence,
(c(B_{1}),c(B_{2}))\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
as desired.

Next, we show that
\Psi
maps into
\bigsqcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}}\prod_{B\in\sigma}\mathcal{NC}_{\operatorname{irr}}(B)
. Fix
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, and let
\sigma=\operatorname{Env}^{0}(\pi)
. Let
\pi_{B}=\pi|_{B}
for
B\in\sigma
. By construction and Lemma
2.20
,
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}
. Since
B
is monochromatic and since
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
, each
\pi_{B}
is non-crossing. Moreover, by construction or by Lemma
2.20
, for each
B\in\sigma
, we have
\min B\sim_{\pi}\max B
, which means that the minimal and maximal elements of
\pi_{B}
are in the same block of
\pi_{B}
, which means that
\pi_{B}
is irreducible.

Next, we show that
\Psi\circ\Phi=\id
. Fix
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}
and
\pi_{B}\in\mathcal{NC}_{\operatorname{irr}}(B)
for
B\in\sigma
. Let
\pi=\Phi((\pi_{B})_{B\in\sigma})
, and we will show that
\Psi(\pi)=(\pi_{B})_{B\in\sigma}
. Since the
\pi_{B}
’s are uniquely determined from

, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{Env}^{0}(\pi)=\sigma
. By the preceding paragraph
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}
and by construction

. Moreover, for each

, since

is irreducible and non-crossing,

and

are in the same block of
\pi
. Therefore, by the uniqueness claim in Lemma

,

must equal
\operatorname{Env}^{0}(\pi)
.

Finally, we show that
\Phi\circ\Psi=\id
. Fix
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
and let
(\pi_{B})_{B\in\sigma}=\Psi(\pi)
. Since
\pi_{B}
are groupings of the blocks of

, we have

.

Lemma 2.29

Remark 2.31
.

Computing the free or classical cumulants of
\mathcal{G}
independent variables is rather difficult (let alone the monotone cumulants). For instance, one
cannot
use the same argument to show that
K_{k}^{\free}[a_{1},\dots,a_{k}]
is the sum over connected partitions in
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
. Indeed, in the preceding proof, we used the fact that a partition is in
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
if and only if each of its irreducible components is in
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
. The same reasoning could
not
be applied for the connected components of
\pi
. This is because
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
has requirements about which blocks can be nested inside each other, which cannot be tested on each of the connected components in isolation. More precisely, if
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})
and if
B_{1}\prec B_{2}
are blocks in its non-crossing envelope, then
B_{1}
will contain some block
B_{1}^{\prime}
of
\pi
with
B_{1}^{\prime}\prec B_{2}
, and then all the blocks of
\pi
in
B_{2}
are nested inside
B_{1}^{\prime}
and so Definition
1.2
(2) applies to them. Thus, not all partitions in
\mathcal{P}(c|_{B_{2}},\mathcal{G})
are allowable.

2.4.
Free–(anti)monotone–Boolean mixtures in the tree framework

As mentioned in Remark
1.6
, there are now two different constructions of a mixture of Boolean, monotone, and free independence. The first is given by digraph independence from
[
18
,
19
]
, while the second is the one in this paper, since in a graph with
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\varnothing
there will be no tensor edges. The difference between these two constructions was noted in
[
19
, Remark 3.24]
in the case of Boolean–monotone mixtures; this remark showed that for a given set of pairwise Boolean and (anti)monotone relationships, the mixture in the digraph framework does not agree with the mixture in the BMT framework of
[
3
]
in general (though they agree when the edge set is a partial order), and as shown above our bigraph framework extends the BMT framework.

We will now consider Boolean-(anti)monotone-free pairwise relations in general and comment on the relationship between the digraph framework of
[
19
]
and our bigraph framework. Consider a bigraph
\mathcal{G}
with
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\varnothing
. Let
G
be the digraph
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{1}\setminus\Delta)
. Consider algebras
A_{v}
for
v\in\mathcal{V}
in a non-commutative probability space
(A,\varphi)
. Let
c:[k]\to\mathcal{V}
and
a_{i}\in A_{c(i)}
.

•

If the algebras
A_{v}
are digraph independent with respect to
G
, then by
[
19
, Definition 3.18]
,

\varphi(a_{1}\dots a_{k})=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(c,G)}K^{\Bool}_{\pi}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}),

where
\mathcal{NC}(c,G)
is given by
[
19
, Definition 3.12]
(explained further below).

•

If the algebras
A_{v}
are bigraph independent with respect to
\mathcal{G}
, then

\varphi(a_{1}\dots a_{k})=\sum_{\pi\in{\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})}^{0}}K^{\Bool}_{\pi}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}).

The difference between these two formulas lies completely in the two sets of partitions. In the both cases, the partitions are non-crossing; indeed, in the bigraph setting crossings are excluded when
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\varnothing
. In both cases, the partitions are required to be consistently colored by
c
. However, the two frameworks have different requirements about what happens when one block is nested inside another:

•

For
\pi
to be in
\mathcal{NC}(c,G)
means that whenever block
V_{2}
is
immediately
nested inside block
V_{1}
, then
(c(V_{1}),c(V_{2}))\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\setminus\Delta
.

•

For
\pi
to be in
{\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})}^{0}
means that whenever block
V_{2}
is nested inside block
V_{1}
, then
(c(V_{1}),c(V_{2}))\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
. Moreover, if
V_{2}
is immediately nested inside block
V_{1}
, then
c(V_{1})\neq c(V_{2})
.

It is thus clear that
{\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})}^{0}\subseteq\mathcal{NC}(c,G)
, but the inclusion is strict in general since
\mathcal{NC}(c,G)
only considers immediate children in the nesting forest of
\pi
, while
{\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})}^{0}
consider all descendants.

Both the digraph and bigraph independences (when
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\varnothing
) fit into the tree independence framework of
[
18
]
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{V},\free}
be the rooted tree whose the vertices are the alternating words on the alphabet
\mathcal{V}
, with
w
being adjacent to
jw
whenever
jw
is an alternating word, and with the root being the empty word. For every rooted subtree
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{V},\free}
one can define
\mathcal{T}
-independence which satisfies the formula
[
18
, Theorem 4.21]

\varphi(a_{1}\dots a_{k})=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(c,\mathcal{T})}K^{\Bool}_{\pi}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}),

where
\mathcal{NC}(c,\mathcal{T})
is the set of non-crossing partitions that are consistently colored by
c
, satisfying the following condition: Given a chain
V_{1}
,
V_{2}
, …,
V_{k}
where
V_{1}
is an outer block of
\pi
and
V_{j+1}
is immediately nested inside
V_{j}
, then
c(V_{k})\dots c(V_{1})\in\mathcal{T}

[
18
, Definition 4.18]
. Digraph independence corresponds to the case where
\mathcal{T}=\operatorname{Walk}(G)
is the set of walks in the digraph
G
, written from right to left; see
[
18
, Definition 3.18]
and
[
19
]
. Meanwhile, bigraph independence (when
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\varnothing
) corresponds to the tree

(2.11)

\mathcal{T}=\{v_{1}\dots v_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{V},\free}:i<j\implies(v_{j},v_{i})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\}.

While the tree
\mathcal{T}
in (
2.11
) is different from
\operatorname{Walk}(G)
in general, they will agree when
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is transitive. Indeed, if
v_{k}\dots v_{1}
is in
\operatorname{Walk}(G)
, then
(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
, …,
(v_{k-1},v_{k})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
, and hence by transitivity,
(v_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
for
i<j
. Therefore, we have the following observation.

Proposition 2.32
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bigraph with
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\varnothing
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
transitive. Then bigraph independence with respect to
\mathcal{G}
is equivalent to digraph independence with respect to
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{1})
.

We note that in the special case where
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\varnothing
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is transitive and symmetric, one obtains a mixture of Boolean and free independence, which by the above proposition agrees with the BF independence of Kula and Wysoczanski
[
21
]
. In general, however, the mixtures of Boolean and free independence from the bigraph framework and the digraph framework are distinct.

For general Boolean, (anti)monotone, free mixtures, there are several other ways to form a mixture which interpolate between the bigraph and digraph versions. For instance, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\mathcal{T}^{(m)}=\{v_{1}\dots v_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{V},\free}:i<j\leq i+m\implies(v_{j},v_{i})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\}.

Then
\mathcal{T}^{(1)}=\operatorname{Walk}(G)
and
\bigcap_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{T}^{(m)}=\mathcal{T}
. Another way to interpolate is to set

\mathcal{S}^{(m)}=\{v_{1}\dots v_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{V},\free}:i<j\leq\max(i+1,k-m)\implies(v_{j},v_{i})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\}.

Thus,
\mathcal{S}^{(m)}
checks the adjacency condition
(v_{j},v_{i})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
when either
j=i+1
or when
j\leq k-m
. Then
\mathcal{S}^{(0)}=\operatorname{Walk}(G)
and
\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{S}^{(m)}=\mathcal{T}
.

2.5.
Operad associativity

In this section, we present a natural associativity property under “composing” independences with respect to different bigraphs. Similar to the setting of tree independence and digraph independence studied in
[
18
]
, we formulate a symmetric operad of bigraphs. We refer to
[
25
]
for general background on operads, and we recall a self-contained definition here.

Definition 2.33
.

A
(plain) operad
consists of a sequence
(P(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of sets, a distinguished element
\id\in P(1)
, and composition maps

\circ_{m,n_{1},\dots,n_{m}}:P(m)\times P(n_{1})\times\dots\times P(n_{m})\to P(n_{1}+\dots+n_{m})

denoted

(f,f_{1},\dots,f_{m})\mapsto f(f_{1},\dots,f_{m}),

such that the following axioms hold:

•

Identity:
For
f\in P(m)
, we have
f(\id,\dots,\id)=f
and
\id(f)=f
.

•

Associativity:
Given
f\in P(m)
and
f_{j}\in P(n_{j})
for
j=1
, …,
m
and
f_{j,i}\in P(m_{j,i})
for
i=1
, …,
I_{j}
and
j=1
, …,
n
, we have

f(f_{1}(f_{1,1},\dots,f_{1,I_{1}}),\dots,f_{m}(f_{m,1},\dots,f_{m,I_{m}}))\\
=[f(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})](f_{1,1},\dots,f_{1,I_{1}},\dots\dots,f_{1,m},\dots,f_{m,I_{m}}).

The elements of
P(m)
are said to have
arity
m
.

Definition 2.34
.

A
symmetric operad
consists of an operad
(P(n))
together with a right action of the symmetric group
S_{m}
on
P(m)
, denoted
(f,\sigma)\mapsto f_{\sigma}
, satisfying the following axioms:

•

Let
f\in P(m)
and
f_{j}\in P(n_{j})
for
j=1,\dots,m
. Let
\sigma\in S_{m}
, and let
\tilde{\sigma}\in S_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{m}}
denote the element that rearranges the order of the blocks
\{1,\dots,n_{1}\}
,
\{n_{1}+1,\dots,n_{1}+n_{2}\}
,
\{n_{1}+n_{2}+1,\dots,n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}\}
according to
\sigma
. Then

f_{\sigma}(f_{\sigma(1)},\dots,f_{\sigma(m)})=[f(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})]_{\tilde{\sigma}}.

•

Let
f
and
f_{j}
be as above. Let
\sigma_{j}\in S_{n_{j}}
, and let
\sigma\in S_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{m}}
be the element which permute the elements within each block
\{n_{1}+\dots+n_{j-1}+1,\dots,n_{1}+\dots+n_{j}\}
by the permutation
s_{j}
, without changing the order of the blocks. Then

f((f_{1})_{\sigma_{1}},\dots,(f_{m})_{\sigma_{m}})=f(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})_{\sigma}.

Definition 2.35

(Operad of bigraphs)
.

We define the operad
\operatorname{BG}
as follows. For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\operatorname{BG}(m)
be the set of bigraphs on vertex set
[m]
.

Let
\id
be the bigraph
(\{1\},\{(1,1)\},\varnothing)
,

Define the composition operation as follows. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}=([m],\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{1},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{2})\in\operatorname{BG}(m)
and for
j=1
, …,
m
let
\mathcal{G}^{j}=([n_{j}],\mathcal{E}_{1}^{j},\mathcal{E}_{2}^{j})\in\operatorname{BG}(n_{j})
. Let
N_{j}=n_{1}+\dots+n_{j}
and
N=N_{m}
, and define
\iota_{j}:[n_{j}]\to[N]
by
\iota_{j}(i)=N_{j-1}+i
, so that
[N]=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{m}\iota_{j}([n_{j}])
.

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{G}^{1},\dots,\mathcal{G}^{m})
be the bigraph
\mathcal{G}=([N],\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})
where
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are defined as follows:

•

If
v,w\in[n_{j}]
and
i\in\{1,2\}
, then
(\iota_{j}(v),\iota_{j}(w))\in\mathcal{E}_{i}
if and only if
(v,w)\in\mathcal{E}_{i}^{j}

•

If
v\in[n_{j}]
and

(\iota_{j}(v),\iota_{j^{\prime}}(w))\in\mathcal{E}_{i}

.

The permutation action is defined as follows: Given
\mathcal{G}=([m],\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})\in\operatorname{BG}(m)
, define
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}=([m],(\sigma^{-1}\times\sigma^{-1})(\mathcal{E}_{1}),(\sigma^{-1}\times\sigma^{-1})(\mathcal{E}_{2}))
.

Observation 2.36
.

\operatorname{BG}
with the composition and permutations operations described above is a symmetric operad.

We leave the routine verification of this fact as an exercise. We now turn to the statement and proof of associativity for bigraph independence.

Theorem 2.37
.

\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\in\operatorname{BG}(m)

\mathcal{G}^{j}\in\operatorname{BG}(n_{j})

, …,

Lemma 2.38

Let

\mathcal{G}^{i}\in\operatorname{BG}(n_{i})
for

, …,
m

and

(3)

Suppose that

(1)

The blocks of

.

(2)

Suppose that

(3)

Suppose that

3.
Hilbert space model

3.1.
Construction of the product Hilbert space

By a
pointed Hilbert space
, we mean a pair
(H,\xi)
where
H
is a Hilbert space and
\xi
is a unit vector. Our goal is to describe a product Hilbert space construction that allows us to realize bigraph independence. As in the case of free-tensor graph independence (
\varepsilon
-independence)
[
30
]
, the Fock-like free product Hilbert space needs to be modified to arrange commutation for the elements that will be tensor independent. Thus, we will similarly need to several of the same definitions pertaining to words over the vertex set of our graph (such as
reduced
and
equivalent
words with respect to
\mathcal{G}
), as well as the new definition of
permissible
words.
We collect them in the following.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})
be a bigraph, and let
(H_{v},\xi_{v})
be pointed Hilbert spaces. Let
\mathcal{W}
be the set of words
v_{1}\dots v_{k}
on the alphabet
\mathcal{V}
.

•

A word
w=v_{1}\dots v_{k}
is
reduced
if whenever
i_{1}<i_{2}
and
v_{i_{1}}=v_{i_{2}}
, there exists
j
between
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
such that
v_{j}\neq v_{i_{1}}
and
(v_{i_{1}},v_{j})\not\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
(
v_{i_{1}}
and
v_{j}
are not in tensor relation).

•

For
w=v_{1}\dots v_{k}
and
w^{\prime}=v_{1}^{\prime}\dots v_{k}^{\prime}
, we say that
w\sim_{0}w^{\prime}
if
w^{\prime}
is obtained by switching two consecutive letters
v_{i}
and
v_{i+1}
such that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
.

•

We say
w
and
w^{\prime}
are
equivalent
, or
w\sim w^{\prime}
if there exists a sequence of words
w\sim_{0}w_{1}\sim_{0}\dots\sim_{0}w_{k-1}\sim w^{\prime}
. In other words,
\sim
is the transitive closure of
\sim_{0}
.

•

A word
v_{1}\dots v_{k}
is
permissible
if whenever
i_{1}<i_{2}
, we have
(v_{i_{2}},v_{i_{1}})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
.

Note that the notions of reduced words and equivalence are exactly the same as in
[
30
,
9
]
for the graph
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix a bigraph
\mathcal{G}
, and let
w
and
w^{\prime}
be words.

(1)

If
w\sim w^{\prime}
, then
w
is reduced if and only if
w^{\prime}
is reduced.

(2)

If
w\sim w^{\prime}
, then
w
is permissible if and only if
w^{\prime}
is permissible.

(3)

Let
v\in\mathcal{G}
. Then
vw\sim vw^{\prime}
if and only if
w\sim w^{\prime}
.

Proof.

(1) is already known from
[
30
,
9
]
.

(2) Suppose that
w^{\prime}
is obtained from
w
by swapping
v_{j}
and
v_{j+1}
, where
(v_{j},v_{j+1})\in\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
. Assume
w
is permissible, so for every
i_{1}<i_{2}
, we have
(v_{i_{2}},v_{i_{1}})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
. In
w^{\prime}
, all pairs of indices are in the same order as before except for
j
and
j+1
, so the condition of permissibility is preserved for all the other pairs. But it is also preserved for the pair
j<j+1
because both
(v_{j},v_{j+1})
and
(v_{j+1},v_{j})
are in
\mathcal{E}_{1}
.

(3) It is clear that if
w\sim w^{\prime}
, then
vw\sim vw^{\prime}
. Conversely, if
vw\sim vw^{\prime}
, then consider the sequence of transformations
\tilde{w}_{1}
, …,
\tilde{w}_{k}
from
vw
to
vw^{\prime}
. Let
w_{j}
be given by deleting the first occurrence of
v
from
\tilde{w}_{j}
. Then
w_{1}
, …,
w_{k}
witnesses equivalence of
w
and
w^{\prime}
.
∎

Fix
\mathcal{G}
and let
W_{\mathcal{G}}
be the set of permissible reduced words (including the empty word). We also fix a set
W_{0}
of representatives for the equivalence classes of permissible words. (For instance, we could choose the element that is minimal in the lexicographical order from each equivalence class.)
Let
r:W_{\mathcal{G}}\to W_{0}
be the function sending each word to its equivalent representative. Fix pointed Hilbert spaces
(H_{v},\xi_{v})
for
v\in\mathcal{V}
, and let
H_{v}^{\circ}=\{h\in H_{v}:\left\langle h,\xi_{v}\right\rangle=0\}
. For
w=v_{1}\dots v_{k}\in W_{\mathcal{G}}
with
k\geq 1
, let

H_{w}^{\circ}:=H_{v_{1}}^{\circ}\otimes\dots\otimes H_{v_{k}}^{\circ},

and for the empty word
\emptyset
, set

H_{\emptyset}^{\circ}:=\mathbb{C}.

For
w\in W_{\mathcal{G}}
, let

U_{w}:H_{w}^{\circ}\to H_{r(w)}^{\circ}

be the canonical isomorphism obtained by permuting the tensorands according to the rearrangement of
w
into
r(w)
. (Note that
U_{w}
is independent of the sequence of swaps, since it is uniquely determined by sending the
k
th occurrence of each letter

in
w
to the
k
th occurrence of
v
in
r(w)
.)

Definition 3.3

(Bigraph product Hilbert space)
.

H:=\bigoplus_{w\in W_{0}}H_{w}^{\circ},\qquad\xi:=1\in\mathbb{C}=H_{\emptyset}^{\circ}\subset H.

We call
(H,\xi)
a
\mathcal{G}
-independent product of the pointed spaces
(H_{v},\xi_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
. Note that
(H,\xi)
depends on the choice of representatives
W_{0}
.

For each
v\in\mathcal{V}
, define a (not necessarily unital)
*
-homomorphism
\lambda_{v}:B(H_{v})\to B(H)
.
Given
w\in W_{\mathcal{G}}
, write

for the word obtained by left-appending

. Set

\displaystyle W_{v}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\{w\in W_{0}:vw\in W_{\mathcal{G}}\},\quad W_{v}^{(2)}:=\{r(vw):w\in W_{v}^{(1)}\},

and

\displaystyle W_{v}^{(3)}

\displaystyle:=W_{0}\setminus\big(W_{v}^{(1)}\cup W_{v}^{(2)}\big).

By Lemma
3.2
(3), the map

is a bijection
W_{v}^{(1)}\to W_{v}^{(2)}
.

For

, using

, define

T_{v,w}:H_{w}^{\circ}\oplus H_{r(vw)}^{\circ}\xrightarrow{\ \id\oplus U_{vw}\ }H_{w}^{\circ}\oplus H_{vw}^{\circ}\cong(\mathbb{C}\otimes H_{w}^{\circ})\oplus(H_{v}^{\circ}\otimes H_{w}^{\circ})\cong H_{v}\otimes H_{w}^{\circ},

where the middle identifications are canonical and

maps to

. Set

\mathcal{H}^{(v)}_{-}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{w\in W_{v}^{(1)}}H_{w}^{\circ},\qquad\mathcal{H}^{(v)}_{+}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{w\in W_{v}^{(2)}}H_{w}^{\circ},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{0}^{(v)}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{w\in W_{v}^{(3)}}H_{w}^{\circ}.

Define the isometric inclusion

S_{v}:=\bigoplus_{w\in W_{v}^{(1)}}T_{v,w}^{*}:H_{v}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{(v)}_{-}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{(v)}_{-}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{(v)}_{+}\subseteq H.

Definition 3.4

(Bigraph product inclusions)
.

For
A\in B(H_{v})
, define

\lambda_{v}(A):=S_{v}\bigl(A\otimes\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{H}^{(v)}_{-}}\bigr)S_{v}^{*}\in B(H).

Equivalently, for each
w\in W_{v}^{(1)}
,
\lambda_{v}(A)
acts on

H_{w}^{\circ}\oplus H_{r(vw)}^{\circ}
via

\lambda_{v}(A)\upharpoonright_{H_{w}^{\circ}\oplus H_{r(vw)}^{\circ}}=T_{v,w}^{*}\bigl(A\otimes I_{H_{w}^{\circ}}\bigr)T_{v,w},

and
\lambda_{v}(A)
vanishes on
\mathcal{H}^{(v)}_{0}
.

Our main result for this section is that this construction realizes
\mathcal{G}
-independence; we postpone the proof to §
3.2
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Fix a bigraph
\mathcal{G}
and pointed Hilbert spaces
(H_{v},\xi_{v})
for

.
Let

Remark 3.7
.

We now relate our construction in the case that
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\setminus\Delta=\mathcal{E}_{2}
with the construction for BMT independence in
[
3
]
. We will see that although the Hilbert spaces need not coincide in general, the Hilbert space from our construction will embed isometrically into the one from
[
3
]
.

To describe this construction, we need a fixed ordering of the vertices of
\mathcal{G}
. In fact, we will assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\dots,p\}
. Consider the tensor product Hilbert space

\tilde{H}={H}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{H}_{p},

and injections
\pi_{i}:B(H_{v})\to B(\tilde{H})
given by

\pi_{i}(A)=P_{i,1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{i,i-1}\otimes A\otimes P_{i,i+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{i,p}

where
P_{i,j}=I_{{H}_{j}}
if
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
P_{i,j}=P_{\xi_{j}}
otherwise. We note that by expressing
H_{v}\cong\mathbb{C}\oplus H_{v}^{\circ}
, we can write

(3.1)

\tilde{H}\cong\bigoplus_{k\in\{0,\dots,p\}}\bigoplus_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}}H_{i_{1}}^{\circ}\otimes\dots\otimes H_{i_{k}}^{\circ}.

The terms in the sum can be regarded as indexed by the power-set
\mathcal{P}([p])
. We can then compare the terms appearing in this direct sum with those that appear in bigraph product Hilbert space
H
from Theorem
3.5
.

To this end, we note several facts about the permissible, reduced words associated to
\mathcal{G}
in the case that
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\setminus\Delta
.

•

In a permissible reduced word
w=v_{1}\dots v_{m}
, each letter appears at most once.
Indeed, suppose that there are two occurrences of the same letter, say
v_{i}=v_{j}
with
i<j
. Assume without loss of generality that
v_{k}\neq v_{i}
for
k\in\{i+1,\dots,j-1\}
. Let
k\in\{i+1,\dots,j-1\}
. Since the word is permissible, we have
(v_{k},v_{i})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
(v_{i},v_{k})=(v_{j},v_{k})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
. Hence,
(v_{i},v_{k})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
, and so
(v_{i},v_{k})\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
. Therefore, all the letters between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
are adjacent by an edge in
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, which contradicts
w
being reduced.

•

Two permissible reduced words
w=v_{1}\dots v_{m}
and
w^{\prime}=v_{1}^{\prime}\dots v_{m}^{\prime}
are equivalent if and only if
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}=\{v_{1}^{\prime},\dots,v_{m}^{\prime}\}
.
The “only if” implication is immediate. For the converse, we proceed by induction on
m
. Suppose that
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}=\{v_{1}^{\prime},\dots,v_{m}^{\prime}\}
. Then there is a permutation
\sigma
such that
v_{\sigma(j)}^{\prime}=v_{j}
for all
j
. If
\sigma(j)<\sigma(1)
, then we have
(v_{j},v_{1})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
by permissibility of
w
and at the same time
(v_{1},v_{j})=(v_{\sigma(1)}^{\prime},v_{\sigma(j)}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
by permissibility of
w^{\prime}
; therefore,
(v_{1},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
. Hence,
v_{1}^{\prime}\dots v_{\sigma(1)-1}^{\prime}
are
\mathcal{E}_{2}
-adjacent to
v_{\sigma(1)}^{\prime}=v_{1}
, and thus can be swapped with it to produce an equivalent word. Therefore,

w^{\prime\prime}:=v_{\sigma(1)}^{\prime}v_{1}^{\prime}\dots v_{\sigma(1)-1}^{\prime}v_{\sigma(1)+1}^{\prime}\dots v_{m}^{\prime}\sim w^{\prime}.

By induction hypothesis, the words
v_{1}^{\prime}\dots v_{\sigma(1)-1}^{\prime}v_{\sigma(1)+1}^{\prime}\dots v_{m}^{\prime}
and
v_{2}\dots v_{m}
are equivalent since they have the same set of letters. Therefore,
w^{\prime\prime}
is equivalent to
w
, and hence
w^{\prime}
is equivalent to
w
.

In light of these two observations, there is an injection from the set of permissible reduced words to the power-set of
\mathcal{V}
given by mapping
v_{1}\dots v_{m}
to
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}
. The terms in the product Hilbert space
H
thus match up with a subset of the terms in the expansion (
3.1
) for
\tilde{H}
. We thus naturally obtain an isometry
U:H\to\tilde{H}
given by matching the corresponding terms in the direct sum and permuting the tensorands in each term
H_{v_{1}}^{\circ}\otimes\cdots\otimes H_{v_{m}}^{\circ}
to match the order they appear in
\tilde{H}
.

We claim that for
A\in B(H_{v})
,

U\lambda_{i}(A)=\pi_{i}(A)U.

It suffices to check the equality on vectors that span the Hilbert space. For this purpose, consider a word
w=v_{1}\dots v_{m}\in\mathcal{W}
such that
iw
is permissible, and we will show equality on vectors in
H_{w}^{\circ}\oplus H_{r(iw)}^{\circ}
. Let
j_{1}\dots j_{\ell}ij_{\ell+1}\dots j_{m}
be the increasing rearrangement of
v_{1}\dots v_{m}
. Consider the commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{i}\otimes H_{w}^{\circ}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{w}^{\circ}\oplus H_{r(iw)}^{\circ}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{i}\otimes H_{j_{1}}^{\circ}\otimes\dots\otimes H_{j_{m}}^{\circ}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{j_{1}}^{\circ}\otimes\dots\otimes H_{j_{\ell}}^{\circ}\otimes H_{i}\otimes H_{j_{\ell+1}}^{\circ}\otimes\dots\otimes H_{j_{m}}^{\circ}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes H_{p},}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{i,w}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\id\otimes U}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cong}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\subseteq}

where the bottom horizontal map is the permutation of the tensorands, and the bottom vertical map is inclusion. The maps in the commutative square are isomorphisms given by distributing tensor products over direct sums and permuting the tensorands in the natural way, so commutativity is clear. The action of
\lambda_{i}(A)
on the top right space
H_{w}^{\circ}\oplus H_{r(iw)}^{\circ}
corresponds to
A\otimes\id
on the top left space by Definition

. The action of
\pi_{i}(A)
on the

is given by
P_{i,1}\otimes\dots\otimes P_{i,i-1}\otimes A\otimes P_{i,i+1}\otimes P_{i,p}
. Note that since

is permissible, all the letters

satisfies

and so
P_{i,v_{j}}=1
. We thus see that the restriction of
\pi_{i}(A)
to the subspace
H_{j_{1}}^{\circ}\otimes\dots\otimes H_{j_{\ell}}^{\circ}\otimes H_{i}\otimes H_{j_{\ell+1}}^{\circ}\otimes\dots\otimes H_{j_{m}}^{\circ}
in the bottom right part of the square is simply
\id\otimes\dots\otimes\id\otimes A\otimes\id\otimes\dots\otimes\id
. Hence, the action of
\pi_{i}(A)
translates into
A\otimes\id
on the space in the bottom left of the square. Comparing this with
A\otimes\id
on the top left part of the square, we see that the action of
\lambda_{i}(A)
on the top right corresponds to the action of
\pi_{i}(A)
on the bottom right, as desired.

Having shown the equality on the span of
H_{w}^{\circ}\oplus H_{r(iw)}^{\circ}
where
iw
is permissible, we now consider the orthogonal complement of the Hilbert space, which is the span of

where

such that

and

into

which vanishes on

and

Thus, although the Hilbert space constructed here is smaller than

and

maps the state vector to the state vector. In particular, the cyclic subspaces generated by the state vector in the two constructions under the action of some family of operators on the

3.2.
Inductive computation of the vectors

In order prove Theorem
3.5
, we want to show that

(3.2)

\left\langle\xi,\lambda_{c(k)}(a_{k})\dots\lambda_{c(1)}(a_{1})\xi\right\rangle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}}\prod_{B\in\pi}K^{\Bool}_{|B|}[a_{j}:j\in B].

Here we have written the indices in reverse order for notational convenience in our inductive arguments. Hence, in the Boolean cumulants the indices
a_{j}:j\in B
should be understood to be in
decreasing
order for each block as well. We will in fact give a combinatorial formula in general for the element

\lambda_{c(k)}(a_{k})\dots\lambda_{c(1)}(a_{1})\xi,

since this is more suitable to compute by induction.

We introduce first some more preparatory notation. Let
P_{v}\in B(\mathcal{H}_{v})
be the rank-one projection onto
\mathbb{C}\xi_{v}
, and let
Q_{v}=1-P_{v}
. Let

\displaystyle a_{j}^{(0,0)}

\displaystyle=P_{c(j)}a_{j}P_{c(j)},\quad a_{j}^{(0,1)}=P_{c(j)}a_{j}Q_{c(j)}

\displaystyle a_{j}^{(1,1)}

\displaystyle=Q_{c(j)}a_{j}Q_{c(j)},\quad a_{j}^{(1,0)}=Q_{c(j)}a_{j}P_{c(j)}.

Then

. We may thus write

(3.3)

\left\langle\xi,\lambda_{c(k)}(a_{k})\dots\lambda_{c(1)}(a_{1})\xi\right\rangle=\sum_{\delta_{1},\epsilon_{1},\dots,\delta_{k},\epsilon_{k}\in\{0,1\}}\left\langle\xi,\lambda_{c(k)}(a_{k}^{(\delta_{k},\epsilon_{k})})\dots\lambda_{c(1)}(a_{1}^{(\delta_{1},\epsilon_{1})})\xi\right\rangle.

Our goal is to show that certain of the terms in the sum vanish, while the others correspond to partitions in
{\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})}
and evaluate to the product of Boolean cumulants in the asserted formula. Note that
a_{j}^{(\delta,\epsilon)}
annihilates
\mathcal{H}_{c(j)}^{\circ}
when
\epsilon=0
and annihilates
\mathbb{C}\xi_{c(j)}
when
\epsilon=1
, and its image is contained in
\mathbb{C}\xi_{c(j)}
when
\delta=0
and
\mathcal{H}_{c(j)}^{\circ}
when
\delta=1
.

Examining the definition of the maps
\lambda_{v}
in Definition
3.4
, we conclude the following. Let
w=v_{1}\dots v_{k}
.

Fact 3.8
.

•

\lambda_{c(j)}(a^{(0,0)})
maps
H_{w}^{\circ}
into itself if
w\in W_{c(j)}^{(1)}
and vanishes on
H_{w}^{\circ}
otherwise.

•

\lambda_{c(j)}(a^{(1,0)})
maps
H_{w}^{\circ}
into
H_{r(c(j)w)}^{\circ}
if
w\in W_{c(j)}^{(1)}
, and vanishes on
H_{w}^{\circ}
otherwise.

•

\lambda_{c(j)}(a^{(0,1)})
maps
H_{w}^{\circ}
into
H_{w^{\prime}}^{\circ}
if
w=r(c(j)w^{\prime})\in W_{c(j)}^{(2)}
(where
w^{\prime}\in W_{c(j)}^{(1)}
), and vanishes on
H_{w}^{\circ}
otherwise.

•

\lambda_{c(j)}(a^{(1,1)})
maps
H_{w}^{\circ}
into itself if
w\in W_{c(j)}^{(2)}
, and vanishes on
H_{w}^{\circ}
otherwise.

With this information in mind, we can then consider the effect of applying several operators
\lambda_{c(j)}(a^{(\delta_{j},\epsilon_{j})})
consecutively to the state vector
\xi
, and thus determine which direct summand of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
contains the vector

\lambda_{c(j)}(a_{j}^{(\delta_{1},\epsilon_{1})})\dots\lambda_{c(1)}(a_{1}^{(\delta_{1},\epsilon_{1})})\xi

for each
j\leq k
. First, to keep track of the number of tensorands, we introduce
for each vertex

v\in\mathcal{G}
a
height function

h_{v}
associated to the sequence of indices
(\delta_{j},\epsilon_{j})_{1\leq j\leq k}
and a coloring
c=c_{1}\cdots c_{k}
. Let, for any
m\in\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
:

h_{v}(m)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}_{c(j)=v}(\delta_{i}-\epsilon_{i}).

Note that
h_{v}(0)=0
, and
h_{v}(j+1)-h_{v}(j)\in\{-1,0,1\}
and if
|h_{v}(j+1)-h_{v}(j)|=1
, then
c(j)=v
.
By inductive application of the observations above, one can show that

\lambda_{c(j)}(a_{j})\dots\lambda_{c(1)}(a_{1})\xi\in H_{w(j)}^{\circ}

for some word
w(j)\in W_{0}
with
h_{v}(j)

occurrences of the letter

v
(allowing the possibility that the vector is zero):

h_{v}(j)=\{i\in\llbracket 1,|w(j)|\rrbracket\colon w_{i}(j)=v\}.

If
h_{v}(j)
is ever
-1
, then the first time that
h_{v}(j)=-1
, we are applying an ‘annihilation operator’
\lambda_{c(j)}(a_{j}^{(0,1)})
(with
c(j)=v
) to a vector which does not have any tensorands from
H_{v}^{\circ}
, which results in :

\lambda_{c(j)}(a_{j}^{(\delta_{j},\epsilon_{j})})\dots\lambda_{c(1)}(a_{1}^{(\delta_{1},\epsilon_{1})})\xi=0,~\textrm{if }h_{v}(j)=-1.

Hence also, if
h(i)<0
for any
i\leq j
, then
\lambda_{c(j)}(a_{j}^{(\delta_{j},\epsilon_{j})})\dots\lambda_{c(1)}(a_{1}^{(\delta_{1},\epsilon_{1})})\xi=0
. Furthermore, at the last step, for the inner product to be nonzero,
\lambda_{c(k)}(a_{k}^{(\delta_{k},\epsilon_{k})})\dots\lambda_{c(1)}(a_{1}^{(\delta_{1},\epsilon_{1})})\xi
must be in
\mathbb{C}\xi
, and hence

h_{v}(k)=0.

Therefore, in the expansion
3.3
, only the summands which have nonnegative height functions
h_{v}
, for each
v\in\mathcal{G}
, with
h_{v}(k)=0
will remain. Also, whenever
h_{v}
hits

at some point

and
h(j+1)=0
too, one must have
\delta_{j+1}=\varepsilon_{j+1}=0
for the vector not to be
0
.

We want to express these in terms of non-crossing partitions of the sets
\{c_{i}=v\}
, for each

and see these partitions are compatible in the sense that they yield a single partition of
[k]
in
\mathcal{P}(c,\mathcal{G})^{0}
.

Thus, we recall the following fact, which is a generalization of the well-known bijection between non-crossing
pair
partitions and Dyck paths. A similar statement is given in
[
18
, Lemma 4.24]
. In our setting, we visualize the indices
1
, …,
k
as running from
right
to
left
. The idea is that the
(0,0)
indices correspond to singletons, the
(1,0)
indices correspond to the right endpoints of blocks, the
(0,1)
indices correspond to the left endpoints, and the
(1,1)
indices correspond to middle elements of a block. However, since in the larger argument we consider building partition inductively by adding on new indices to the left side, then at the intermediate stages we have to allow that some blocks are “unfinished” or are missing their left endpoints.

Definition 3.9
.

An
unfinished partition

\pi
is a partition
\pi
of
[k]
together with a labeling of each of block as “finished” or “unfinished.” Similarly, an
unfinished non-crossing partition
is an unfinished partition such that
\pi
is non-crossing.

An unfinished partition
\pi\in[k]
has a restriction to
[k-1]
, which is also an unfinished partition. Let
\pi
be an unfinished partition of
[k]
and let
B
be the block containing
k
. We define the restriction
\tilde{\pi}
of
\pi
to
[k-1]
, by declaring that all blocks of
\pi
different from
B
are blocks of
\tilde{\pi}
and if
B\neq\{k\}
,
B\backslash\{k\}
is a unfinished block of
\tilde{\pi}
. This construction will be used in the proof of our key result, Lemma
3.14
.

Lemma 3.10
.

There is a bijection between the following sets:

(1)

The set of unfinished non-crossing partitions of
[k]
.

(2)

The set of sequences
(\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1})
, …,
(\delta_{k},\varepsilon_{k})
such that the height function

h(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\delta_{i}-\epsilon_{i}),~j\in\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket,

is always nonnegative and
h(j)=h(j+1)=0
implies
\varepsilon_{j+1}=\delta_{j+1}=0

The bijection is described as follows:

(1)

j
is a finished singleton block of
\pi
if and only if
(\delta_{j},\varepsilon_{j})=(0,0)
.

(2)

j
is the right endpoint of a block that is not a finished singleton block if and only if
(\delta_{j},\varepsilon_{j})=(1,0)
.

(3)

j
is the left endpoint of a finished non-singleton block if and only if
(\delta_{j},\varepsilon_{j})=(0,1)
.

(4)

j
is not the right endpoint of a singleton block and not the left endpoints of a finished block if and only if
(\delta_{j},\varepsilon_{j})=(1,1)
.

Proof.

Let us proceed by induction on
k
. The case
k=1
is trivial, if
h(1)=0
, then
(\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1})=(0,0)
and the only unfinished non-crossing partition of
[1]
is
\{\{1\}\}
which is a finished singleton block. If
h(1)=1
, then
(\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1})=(1,0)
and the only unfinished non-crossing partition of
[1]
is
\{\{1\}\}
which is an unfinished block.
Suppose the result holds for
k-1
and let us prove it for
k
. Let
(\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}),\ldots,(\delta_{k},\varepsilon_{k})
be a sequence such that the height function
h
is nonnegative and
h(j)=h(j+1)=0
implies
\varepsilon_{j+1}=\delta_{j+1}=0
. Let
\tilde{h}
be the restriction of
h
to
\llbracket 0,k-1\rrbracket
and let
\tilde{\pi}
be the unfinished non-crossing partition of
[k-1]
associated to
(\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}),\ldots,(\delta_{k-1},\varepsilon_{k-1})
by the induction hypothesis. We define the unfinished non-crossing partition
\pi
of
[k]
associated to
(\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}),\ldots,(\delta_{k},\varepsilon_{k})
as follows:

•

If
(\delta_{k},\varepsilon_{k})=(0,0)
, then
\pi
is obtained from
\tilde{\pi}
by adding the finished singleton block
\{k\}
.

•

If
(\delta_{k},\varepsilon_{k})=(1,0)
, then
\pi
is obtained from
\tilde{\pi}
by adding the unfinished block
\{k\}
.

•

If
(\delta_{k},\varepsilon_{k})=(1,1)
, then
h(k-1)>0
and
\pi
is obtained from
\tilde{\pi}
by adding
k
to the unfinished block of
\tilde{\pi}
containing the largest element.

•

If
(\delta_{k},\varepsilon_{k})=(0,1)
, then
h(k-1)>0
and
\pi
is obtained from
\tilde{\pi}
by adding
k
to the unfinished block of
\tilde{\pi}
containing the largest integer, making this block finished.

The converse map is obtained by reversing the above construction. This concludes the proof.
∎

4.4.
Almost sure convergence

To complete the proof of Theorem
4.1
(and hence of Theorem
1.10
), we only need to upgrade the convergence of expectation from Proposition
4.2
to almost sure convergence. This follows by standard techniques from concentration of measure, which has become a standard tool for random matrix theory since
[
5
,
16
]
.

We recall that the unitary group
\mathbb{U}_{N}
, equipped with the Haar measure and the Riemannian metric associated to the inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\Tr_{N}}
, satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality with constant
6/N

[
26
, Theorem 5.16]
. Using e.g.
[
22
, Corollary 5.7]
,
[
26
, Theorem 5.9]
, one can easily deduce that the product space
\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{U}_{N^{\#S_{v}^{(1)}}}
, where the Riemann metric is the sum of the original Riemann metrics over the different components, with the product measure, satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}6/N^{\#S_{v}^{(1)}}\leq 6/N
. This in turn implies that it satisfies the Herbst concentration estimate with constant
6/N
; see e.g.
[
1
, Lemma 2.3.3]
,
[
26
, Theorem 5.5]
. (See
[
17
, §5.3]
for a similar application of these results with more explanation.)

Lemma 4.3

(Herbst concentration)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bigraph, and let
(U_{v}^{(N)})_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
be Haar unitaries as in Theorem
4.1
. Let
f:\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{U}_{N^{\#S_{v}^{(1)}}}\to\mathbb{C}
be
L
-Lipschitz with respect to the geodesic distance associated to the direct sum of the Riemannian metrics
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\Tr_{N^{\#S_{v}^{(1)}}}}
,
v\in\mathcal{V}
. Then

\mathbb{P}(|f(U_{v}^{(N)}:v\in\mathcal{V})-\mathbb{E}[f(U_{v}^{(N)}:v\in\mathcal{V})]|\geq\delta)\leq 4e^{-N\delta^{2}/12L^{2}}

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We start by relating the geodesic distance in Lemma
4.3
to other distances better suited to random matrix theory.
Since the inner product on
\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{U}_{N^{\#S_{v}^{(1)}}}
is the restriction of the sum of the Hilbert-Schmidt scalar products
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\Tr_{N^{\#S_{v}^{(1)}}}}
on
\mathbb{M}_{N^{\#S^{(1)}_{v}}}
, the geodesic distance, which we will denote by
d_{\operatorname{geo}}
, is bounded above by the Hilbert–Schmidt distance
d_{HS}
given by

d_{HS}((U_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}},(U_{v}^{\prime})_{v\in\mathcal{V}})^{2}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\Tr_{N^{\#S_{v}^{(N)}}}((U_{v}-U_{v}^{\prime})^{*}(U_{v}-U_{v})).

Next, let
d_{\infty}
be the operator-norm distance given by

d_{\infty}((U_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}},(U_{v}^{\prime})_{v\in\mathcal{V}})=\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\|U_{v}-U_{v}^{\prime}\|.

Since the Hilbert–Schmidt norm dominates the operator norm, we have
d_{\infty}\leq d_{HS}
. The upshot of this discussion is that
d_{\infty}\leq d_{\operatorname{geo}}
, and therefore, any function which is
L
-Lipschitz with respect to the operator norm must be
L
-Lipschitz with respect to
d_{\operatorname{geo}}
.

Now consider the function
f:\prod_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{U}_{N^{\#S_{v}^{(1)}}}\to\mathbb{C}
given by

f^{(N)}(U_{v}:v\in\mathcal{V})=\left\langle\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}\,,\,\lambda_{c(1)}^{(N)}(U_{c(1)}A_{1}^{(N)}U_{c(1)}^{*})\dots\lambda_{c(k)}^{(N)}(U_{c(k)}A_{k}^{(N)}U_{c(k)}^{*})\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}\right\rangle.

Since we assume that
\|A_{j}^{(N)}\|
is bounded by some constant (independent of
N
) and the unitaries are of course bounded in operator norm by
1
, it is straightforward to check that

\lambda_{c(1)}^{(N)}(U_{c(1)}A_{1}^{(N)}U_{c(1)}^{*})\dots\lambda_{c(k)}^{(N)}(U_{c(k)}A_{k}^{(N)}U_{c(k)}^{*})

is a Lipschitz function of
(U_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
with respect to
d_{\infty}
in the domain and the operator norm in the target space. Since
\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}
is a unit vector, we then see that
f^{(N)}
is Lipschitz with respect to
d_{\infty}
, hence with respect to
d_{\operatorname{geo}}
, with a Lipschitz constant
L
independent of
N
. Therefore, by Lemma
4.3
,

\mathbb{P}(|f^{(N)}(U_{v}^{(N)}:v\in\mathcal{V})-\mathbb{E}[f^{(N)}(U_{1}^{(n)},\dots,U_{m}^{(n)})]|\geq N^{-1/3})\leq 4e^{-N\cdot N^{-2/3}/12L^{2}}.

Because
\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}e^{-N^{1/3}/12L^{2}}<\infty
, the Borel–Cantelli lemma implies that almost surely, for sufficiently large
N
, we have

|f^{(N)}(U_{v}^{(N)}:v\in\mathcal{V})-\mathbb{E}[f^{(N)}(U_{v}^{(N)}:v\in\mathcal{V})]|<N^{-1/3},

and thus, almost surely,

(4.25)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\biggl|\left\langle\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}},\lambda_{c(1)}^{(N)}(U_{c(1)}^{(N)}A_{1}^{(N)}(U_{c(1)}^{(N)})^{*})\dots\lambda_{c(k)}^{(N)}(U_{c(k)}^{(N)}A_{k}^{(N)}(U_{c(k)}^{(N)})^{*})\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}\right\rangle\\
-\mathbb{E}\left\langle\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}},\lambda_{c(1)}^{(N)}(U_{c(1)}^{(N)}A_{1}^{(N)}(U_{c(1)}^{(N)})^{*})\dots\lambda_{c(k)}^{(N)}(U_{c(k)}^{(N)}A_{k}^{(N)}(U_{c(k)}^{(N)})^{*})\xi^{\otimes\mathcal{S}}\right\rangle\biggr|=0.

Now combining (
4.25
) with (
4.8
) from Proposition

and the triangle inequality, we obtain the claim (
4.1
) asserted in Theorem
4.1
.
