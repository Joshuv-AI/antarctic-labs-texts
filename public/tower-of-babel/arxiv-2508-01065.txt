Inequalities for Optimization of Classification Algorithms: A Perspective Motivated by Diagnostic Testing
Paul N. Patrone and Anthony J. Kearsley

Abstract
Motivated by canonical problems in medical diagnostics, we propose and study properties of an objective function that uniformly bounds uncertainties in quantities of interest extracted from classifiers and related data analysis tools. We begin by adopting a set-theoretic perspective to show how two main tasks in diagnostics – classification and prevalence estimation – can be recast in terms of a variation on the confusion (or error) matrix 𝐏 {\boldsymbol{\rm P}} typically considered in supervised learning. We then combine arguments from conditional probability with the Gershgorin circle theorem to demonstrate that the largest Gershgorin radius ρ max \rho_{\max} of the matrix 𝐈 − 𝐏 {\boldsymbol{\rm I}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm P}} (where 𝐈 {\boldsymbol{\rm I}} is the identity) yields uniform error bounds for both classification and prevalence estimation. In a two-class setting, ρ max \rho_{\max} is minimized via a measure-theoretic “water-leveling” argument that optimizes an appropriately defined partition U U generating the matrix 𝐏 {\boldsymbol{\rm P}} . We also consider an example that illustrates the difficulty of generalizing the binary solution to a multi-class setting and deduce relevant properties of the confusion matrix.

An overarching technical theme of our analysis is the fact that multiple layers of probabilistic conditioning separate the information that diagnosticians want – the numbers and identities of samples in each class – from the measurements that can be made. In particular, the prevalence

{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
(i.e. confusion matrix) through the selection of

{\boldsymbol{{q}}}
to
{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}

A key limitation of our work is that we assume exact knowledge of the

. This is for several reasons. First, an analysis of this situation is sufficiently rich that it warrants a study of its own. Second, we anticipate that a full analysis of the uncertainty induced by not knowing the

is difficult enough so as to be beyond the scope of this manuscript. Here our goal is to determine guaranteed best bounds on uncertainty in prevalence estimates, which correspond to an infinite-data limit. While this is clearly not achievable in practice, many machine-learning settings are sufficiently rich with data that we anticipate the results presented herein are accurate approximations.

Finally, we note that while the work herein applies more generally to classification theory, the original impetus arose from medical diagnostics. Thus, we frequently use tasks and concepts from this field to motivate and explain our results.

2.1
Mathematical Context: Classification Theory as Motivated by Diagnostics

Diagnostic and classification problems assume the existence of a discrete population
\Omega
whose members or “individuals”
\omega
belong to one of
c
distinct classes
C_{k}
(
k\in\{1,2,...,c\}
) according to some probability distribution. This setting is rigorously described in terms of an underlying probability space associated with the multinomial distribution
[
29

30
]
. For our purposes, it suffices to consider the prototypical case of a single sample point
\omega
. The corresponding probability space is constructed by considering: (i) the set of classes (i.e. outcomes)
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{k}\}
for an individual
\omega

2^{\mathcal{C}}
of possible events; and (iii) the discrete probability
{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}=(q_{1},q_{2},...,q_{c})
of belonging to a class, which induces the measure
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}
associated with each event in
2^{\mathcal{C}}
. Then the triple
(\mathcal{C},2^{\mathcal{C}},\mu_{\boldsymbol{\rm q}})
is the probability space associated with
\omega
. In this context,
{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}
is often an unknown quantity to be estimated, and it plays an elevated role contexts such as epidemiology.

Definition 1

Assume that a population has
c

be a discrete probability density, where
q_{k}

C_{k}

as the

In practice, we are not given the true class

{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}

To proceed further, it is necessary to establish certain facts and assumptions about
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
. The following definition informs Question (IIIa).

Definition 3

Let
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
be a confusion matrix. We refer to

\displaystyle\rho_{\max}=\max_{j}\{1-P_{j,j}\}

(6)

(P1)

]

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}({\boldsymbol{\rm I}})={\rm E}[({\boldsymbol{\hat{\rm q}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}})^{{\rm T}}({\boldsymbol{\hat{\rm q}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}})]={\rm E}[({\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}})^{{\rm T}}({\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}})],

(12)

in terms of
\rho_{\max}
.

Lemma 1
(Variance Bound)

Assume a partition
U
such that the
c\times c
matrix
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
satisfies property (P3) for
c\geq 2
. Let
\mathcal{S}
denote a collection of points as defined previously, and let
s
be the number of elements in
\mathcal{S}
, with repeated elements counting as distinct. Then
\sigma^{2}
satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}({\boldsymbol{\rm I}})\leq\frac{2c\rho_{\max}-\frac{c^{2}}{c-1}\rho_{\max}^{2}}{s(1-2\rho_{\max})^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{c}\frac{q_{j}(1-q_{j})}{s}.

(13)

Remark 5

We drop the dependence of
\sigma^{2}
on
{\boldsymbol{\rm I}}
when clear from context.

Proof 1

Decompose the difference
{\boldsymbol{\hat{\rm q}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}
via conditional expectation as follows. Let
{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}
denote the random-vector with elements
\tilde{q}_{j}=m_{j}/s
, where
m_{j}
is a random number of elements drawn from class
C_{j}
according to a multinomial distribution with density
{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}
and
s
total events. Clearly
{\rm E}[{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}]={\boldsymbol{\rm q}}
. Next express the difference

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}={\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}})+({\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}).

(14)

Observe that
{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}
is the random vector associated with the fraction of samples in each class, whereas
{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}
is the random vector associated with the fraction of samples in each domain of
U
.
Taking the conditional expectation of Eq. (
14
) yields

\displaystyle{\rm E}[{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}|{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}]={\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}.

(15)

By the law of total variance (or conditional variance)
[
48
]
, we therefore recognize that

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}={\rm E}\left[{\rm E}[({\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}})^{\rm T}({\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}})|{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}]\right]+{\rm E}[({\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}})^{\rm T}({\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}})].

(16)

Known properties of the multinomial distribution yield
[
29
]

\displaystyle{\rm E}[({\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}})^{\rm T}({\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm q}})]=\sum_{j}\frac{q_{j}(1-q_{j})}{s},

(17)

which is the second term on the right-hand side (RHS) of Eq. (
13
).

Next consider that the vector 2-norm induces the corresponding matrix norm, yielding

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}})^{\rm T}({\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}})=||{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}})||_{2}^{2}\leq||{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}||_{2}^{2}\,\,||{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}||_{2}^{2},

(18)

which implies that

\displaystyle{\rm E}\left(||{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}})||_{2}^{2}\big|{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}\right)\leq||{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}||_{2}^{2}\,\,{\rm E}\left(||{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}||_{2}^{2}\big|{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}\right)

(19)

Writing the last term explicitly, one finds

\displaystyle{\rm E}\left(||{\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}||_{2}^{2}\big|{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}\right)=\sum_{i,j}\frac{P_{i,j}(1-P_{i,j})\tilde{q}_{j}}{s}.

(20)

Let
\rho_{j}=1-P_{j,j}
. One finds

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\frac{P_{i,j}(1-P_{i,j})\tilde{q}_{j}}{s}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{j}\left\{\sum_{i}P_{i,j}(1-P_{i,j})\right\}\sum_{k}\frac{\tilde{q}_{k}}{s}=\frac{1}{s}\max_{j}\left\{\sum_{i}P_{i,j}(1-P_{i,j})\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\max_{j}\left\{\rho_{j}(1-\rho_{j})+\sum_{i;i\neq j}P_{i,j}-P_{i,j}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\max_{j}\left\{\rho_{j}(2-\rho_{j})-\sum_{i;i\neq j}P_{i,j}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{s}\max_{j}\left\{\rho_{j}(2-\rho_{j})-\frac{1}{c-1}\left[\sum_{i:i\neq j}P_{i,j}\right]^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\max_{j}\left\{\rho_{j}(2-\rho_{j})-\frac{\rho_{j}^{2}}{c-1}\right\}=\frac{2\rho_{\max}-\frac{c}{c-1}\rho_{\max}^{2}}{s}.

(21)

The second and third lines come from property P2. The fourth line is an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
[
49
]
, and the fifth line leverages the pair of observations that
\rho_{j}\leq\rho_{\max}<0.5
(properties P3 and P5; see also Fig.
1
) and
2\rho-\rho^{2}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{c-1}
is a monotone increasing function of
\rho
for
0<\rho<0.5
and
c\geq 2
.
Further recognize that,

\displaystyle||{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}||_{2}^{2}=\left[\min_{{\boldsymbol{\rm x}},||{\boldsymbol{\rm x}}||_{2}=1}||{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}{\boldsymbol{\rm x}}||_{2}^{2}\right]^{-1}\leq c\left[\min_{{\boldsymbol{\rm x}},||{\boldsymbol{\rm x}}||_{1}=1}||{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}{\boldsymbol{\rm x}}||_{1}^{2}\right]^{-1}\leq\frac{c}{(1-2\rho_{\max})^{2}},

(22)

where the last inequality arises by analyzing
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
element-wise.

Combining these equations one finds that

\displaystyle{\rm E}\left(||{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\hat{{\boldsymbol{{q}}}}}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}})||_{2}\big|{\boldsymbol{\tilde{\rm q}}}\right)\leq\frac{2c\rho_{\max}-\frac{c^{2}}{c-1}\rho_{\max}^{2}}{s(1-2\rho_{\max})^{2}}.

(23)

Since the conditional expectation is bounded by a deterministic quantity, we therefore arrive at inequality (
13
) via Eq. (
16
). ∎

Corollary 1

The weighted variance
\sigma^{2}(A)
is bounded by

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}({\boldsymbol{\rm A}})\leq||{\boldsymbol{\rm A}}||_{2}^{2}\sigma^{2}({\boldsymbol{\rm I}}),

(24)

where
||{\boldsymbol{\rm A}}||_{2}
is the induced matrix 2-norm.

Remark 6

Recall that
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
is both a push-forward operator
[
41
]
and a stochastic matrix [in the sense of (P1)]. Its deviation from the identity characterizes the extent to which
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
“redistributes” probability mass when using
{\boldsymbol{{q}}}
in lieu of
{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}
. The
\rho_{\max}
quantifies the maximum extent to which this mixing occurs. This observation also explains why inequality (
13
) contains two terms, only one of which depends on
\rho_{\max}
. The second term on the RHS quantifies the inherent variation due to the finite number of samples per class, which we can never eliminate except in the limit
s\to\infty
. The first term on the RHS of (
13
) quantifies the excess uncertainty due to mixing of the probability density
{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}
. This also clarifies the role of conditioning in Eq. (
18
), i.e. to separate these sources of uncertainty.

In practice, we can often make additional assumptions on the structure of
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
so as to sharpen inequality (
22
). The following corollary considers such a situation.

Corollary 2

Assume that
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
is symmetric. Then we obtain the tighter bound

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\leq\frac{2\rho_{\max}-\frac{c}{c-1}\rho_{\max}^{2}}{s(1-2\rho_{\max})^{2}}+\sum_{j}\frac{q_{j}(1-q_{j})}{s}.

(25)

Remark 7

Inequality (
25
) differs from (
13
) in that the former removes a factor of dimension
c
from the bound on variance. See Sec.
4
for applications of this result.

Proof 2

Proceed exactly as in Lemma
1
up through inequalities (
21
). In place of inequality (
22
), recognize that

\displaystyle||{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}^{-1}||_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{{s}_{\min}^{2}}=\frac{1}{|\lambda_{\min}|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{(1-2\rho_{\max})^{2}},

(26)

where
{s}_{\min}
is the smallest singular value of
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
, and
\lambda_{\min}
is the eigenvalue with the smallest magnitude (see P5). ∎

Remark 8

In light of Lemma
1
, it is useful to define the following auxiliary quantities

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\rho_{\max}}=\frac{2c\rho_{\max}-\frac{c^{2}}{c-1}\rho_{\max}^{2}}{s(1-2\rho_{\max})^{2}},

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rho_{\max}}=\frac{\varepsilon_{\rho_{\max}}}{c},

(27a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\sigma}=\varepsilon_{\rho_{\max}}+\sum_{j=1}^{c}\frac{q_{j}(1-q_{j})}{s},

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\sigma}=\epsilon_{\rho_{\max}}+\sum_{j=1}^{c}\frac{q_{j}(1-q_{j})}{s},

(27b)

where terms denoted by
\varepsilon
and
\epsilon
correspond to inequalities (
13
) and (
25
), respectively. Note that
\varepsilon_{\rho_{\max}}
and
\epsilon_{\rho_{\max}}
are the excess uncertainty in prevalence due to overlap of the conditional probability densities.

4
Minimizing
\rho_{\max}

When
{\boldsymbol{\rm q}}
has more than two dimensions, it is not clear that there exist analytical formulas for the optimal partition
U^{\star}
that minimizes
\rho_{\max}
. However, the two-dimensional case is both extremely common in diagnostics (and thus useful) and analytically tractable; see, e.g.
[
2
]
. Therefore, consider the following problem:

\displaystyle U^{\star}={\rm\arg}\!\min_{U}[\rho_{\max}(U)].

(28)

The solution to Eq. (
28
) can be constructed via a water-leveling algorithm that bears resemblance to the both the bathtub principle in measure theory
[
50
]
and methods for optimal classification
[
13
]
. The following definition establishes the latter connection.

Definition 4
(Optimal Binary Classification Domains)

Let
p_{1}(r)
and
p_{2}(r)
be the conditional PDFs associated with classes
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. Let
0\leq t<\infty
. Then the sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>tp_{2}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\}\cup\mathcal{B}_{1},

(29a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})<tp_{2}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\}\cup\mathcal{B}_{2},

(29b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{b}(t)

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=tp_{2}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup\mathcal{B}_{2},

(29c)

are the
optimal binary classification domains
associated with prevalence
{q=q_{1}=(1+t)^{-1}}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are arbitrary partitions of the boundary set
\mathcal{D}_{b}
.

Remark 9

It is straightforward to show that Eqs. (
29a
) and (
29b
) minimize the error (
2
) in the binary classification setting. See, e.g. Refs.
[
12
,
40
]
.

Construction of
U^{\star}
also makes use of the corresponding measures

\displaystyle\mu_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{j}(t)}p_{j}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\rm r},

\displaystyle\mu_{b,j}(t)=\int_{\mathcal{B}_{j}(t)}p_{j}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\rm r},

(30)

for
j=1,2
. The optimal partition
U^{\star}=\{D_{1}^{\star},D_{2}^{\star}\}
is then given by the point at which the difference
\Delta(t)=\mu_{1}(t)-\mu_{2}(t)
crosses zero. More precisely:

Lemma 2
(Water-Leveling Construction)

Assume that the function
\Delta(t)
attains the value
\Delta(t^{\star})=0
for some
t^{\star}
on
0<t<\infty
and
\mathcal{D}_{b}(t^{\star})
has zero measure with respect to both
p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
and
p_{2}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
. Then the partition

\displaystyle D_{1}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{1}(t^{\star})\cup\mathcal{B}_{1}(t^{\star})

(31a)

\displaystyle D_{2}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{2}(t^{\star})\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}(t^{\star})

(31b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{b}(t^{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{1}(t^{\star})\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}(t^{\star})

(31c)

minimizes
\rho_{\max}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{1}(t^{\star})
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}(t^{\star})
are an arbitrary partition of the set
\mathcal{D}_{b}(t^{\star})
. Alternatively, if (i)
\mathcal{D}_{b}(t^{\star})
has positive measure with respect to any distribution or (ii)
\Delta(t)
is discontinuous at
t^{\star}
and
\pm\Delta(t^{\star}\pm\epsilon)<0
for any positive
\epsilon
, Eqs. (
31a
)–(
31c
) minimize
\rho_{\max}
subject to the additional constraint on
\mathcal{B}_{1}(t^{\star})
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}(t^{\star})
that

\mu_{1}(t^{\star})+\mu_{b,1}(t^{\star})-\mu_{2}(t^{\star})-\mu_{b,2}(t^{\star})=0.

(32)

Proof 3

Observe that
\Delta(t)
is a monotone decreasing function of
t
.

Consider the case in which
\Delta(t^{\star})=0
, and temporarily assume that the set
\mathcal{D}_{b}(t^{\star})
has zero measure. Note that
{\boldsymbol{\rm I}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
is symmetric (since
\Delta(t^{\star})=0
) and has the form

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\rm I}}-{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}=\begin{bmatrix}1-\mu_{1}(t^{\star})&\mu_{2}(t^{\star})-1\\
\mu_{1}(t^{\star})-1&1-\mu_{2}(t^{\star})\end{bmatrix}.

(33)

Choose an arbitrary subset
\delta_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{1}(t^{\star})
with positive measure with respect to
p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
. Clearly we cannot remove such a
\delta_{1}
from
D_{1}(t^{\star})
and add it to
D_{2}(t^{\star})
, since this will increase the sum of absolute values in the first column, and thus
\rho_{\max}
. Neither can we exchange points between domains. To see this, define a second set
\delta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{2}(t^{\star})
having positive measure with respect to
p_{2}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
, and consider the sets
\bar{D}_{1}=(D_{1}(t^{\star})/\delta_{1})\cup\delta_{2}
and
\bar{D}_{2}=(D_{2}(t^{\star})/\delta_{2})\cup\delta_{1}
with corresponding measures
\bar{\mu}_{1}
and
\bar{\mu}_{2}
. Observe that the differences in measures is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{1}-\mu_{1}(t^{\star})=-\int_{\delta_{1}}p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\rm r}+\int_{\delta_{2}}p_{1}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\rm r}

(34a)

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{2}-\mu_{2}(t^{\star})=-\int_{\delta_{2}}p_{2}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\rm r}+\int_{\delta_{1}}p_{2}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\rm r}.

(34b)

Using the definitions of
\mathcal{D}_{1}(t^{\star})
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}(t^{\star})
, (
34a
) and (
34b
) imply

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{1}-\mu_{1}(t^{\star})<-t^{\star}[\bar{\mu}_{2}-\mu_{2}(t^{\star})].

(35)

Recognize that
t^{\star}>0
. Thus, there are three possibilities: the RHS of Eq. (
35
) is positive, negative, or zero. In all three cases,
\bar{\mu}_{j}<\mu_{j}(t^{\star})
for either
j=1
or
j=2
. We thereby conclude that the minimum value of
\rho_{\max}
is given by the zero of
\Delta(t)
, which is unique by monotonicity and the assumption that
\mathcal{D}_{b}(t^{\star})
has zero measure. Note that in this case, the sets given by Eqs. (
29a
) and (
29b
) automatically partition
\Gamma
up to a set of measure zero, whose points we may assign to either
D_{1}
or
D_{2}
.

When
\Delta(t^{\star})=0
but the set
\mathcal{D}_{b}(t^{\star})
does not have measure zero, select
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
such that
\mu_{b,1}(t^{\star})-\mu_{b,2}(t^{\star})=0
. We then apply the steps leading to inequality (
35
). This same argument applies to the case that
\Delta(t)
is discontinuous at
t^{\star}
, with sole modification that the difference
\mu_{b,1}(t^{\star})-\mu_{b,2}(t^{\star})
be equal to the jump discontinuity in
\Delta t
at
t^{\star}
. ∎

5
Discussion

5.1
Challenges of Generalizing Lemma
2

An interesting observation of Lemma
2
is that the diagonal elements of
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
are equal when
\rho_{\max}
is minimized. It seems reasonable that this property should carry over to the multiclass setting and thereby generalize Eqs. (
31a
)–(
31c
). However, the situation is not straightforward. A simple example illustrates the key challenges.

Example 1
(Partially Disjoint Uniform Distributions)

Define

\displaystyle u(x;y)=\begin{cases}1&y-1/2\leq x\leq y+1/2\\
0&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}

(39)

which is the uniform distribution in
x
on a unit interval centered at
y
. Let

\displaystyle p_{1}(r)

\displaystyle=u(r;0.5)

(40a)

\displaystyle p_{2}(r)

\displaystyle=u(r;1.4)

(40b)

\displaystyle p_{3}(r)

\displaystyle=u(r;2).

(40c)

See also Fig.
4
.

Figure 4
:
Three uniform distributions given by Eqs. (
40a
)–(
40c
). Note that there is significantly less overlap between
p_{1}(r)
and
p_{2}(r)
than between
p_{2}(r)
and
p_{3}(r)
.

Note that for these distributions, a partition
U
can be defined in terms of two points
x_{1,2}
and
x_{2,3}
that act as boundaries between classes
C_{j}
and
C_{j+1}
,
j=1,2
. It is straightforward to show that an optimal partition
U^{\star}
is given by
x_{1,2}=0.9
and
x_{2,3}=1.7
, which corresponds to the matrix

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}(U^{\star})=\begin{bmatrix}0.9&0&0\\
0.1&0.8&0.2\\
0&0.2&0.8\end{bmatrix},

(41)

having
\rho_{\max}=0.2
. We could also choose
x_{1,2}=0.8
, which would make all three diagonal entries identical without changing
\rho_{\max}
. However, this would be a poor decision from a diagnostic standpoint, since only
p_{1}(r)
has probability mass on the domain
r\in[0.8,0.9]
.

This example illustrates several important properties that differentiate the multiclass setting from its binary counterpart. As an analogy, first observe that we may interpret each class as the
j
th vertex or node in a graph, with an edge connecting vertices
j
and
j^{\prime}
if
p_{j}(r)
and
p_{j^{\prime}}(r)
have overlapping support. Thus, connected nodes indicate pairs of PDFs between which we can redistribute probability mass (by changing
U
) to minimize
\rho_{\max}
. In the binary problem, there is only one such edge, whereas in the multiclass problem, mass can be redistributed along several distinct paths. Compounding this, the mass that can be transferred from class
C_{j}
to
C_{j^{\prime}}
may not equal the mass that can be transferred from
C_{j^{\prime}}
to
C_{j^{\prime\prime}}
. This implies that two optimal partitions
U^{\star}
and
V^{\star}
can differ by more than boundary sets defined by analogy to Eq. (
29c
).

Despite this complication, we can still deduce certain properties of the collection
\mathcal{P}
of matrices
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
that minimize
\rho_{\max}
, and these properties may be useful for constructing useful optimization algorithms. The following lemma is motivated by the structure of Eq. (
33
).

Lemma 3
(Constant Diagonal Matrix)

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of
c\times c
matrices
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}
that minimize
\rho_{\max}
for a given set of conditional PDFs
p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
for
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in\Gamma
,
k=1,2,...,c
. Then
\mathcal{P}
contains a matrix whose diagonal entries are equal.

Proof 4
(By Construction)

Assume
{\boldsymbol{\rm P}}\in\mathcal{P}
. Let
K=\{k_{1},k_{2},...,k_{m}\}
denote the columns for which
P_{k,k}
attains its minimum. If
m=c
, then the lemma is trivially true.

Assume therefore that
m<c
. Recall that by definition,
P_{j,k}
is the probability mass from the
k
th distribution contained in domain
D_{j}
. Observe that there must exist at least one
k^{\prime}\in K
such that

\displaystyle{\rm supp}[p_{k^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})]\bigcap\left[\bigcup_{j:j\notin K}D_{j}\right]=\emptyset.

(42)

\displaystyle\tilde{D}_{k}

\displaystyle=D_{k}\bigcup D_{k,j_{k}}

\displaystyle k\in K

\displaystyle\tilde{D}_{j}

\displaystyle=D_{j}/\left[\bigcup_{k:k\in K}D_{k,j_{k}}\right],

\displaystyle j\notin K

(44)

(45)

This implies that the

To prove the main result, let

for which Eq. (

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}=P_{j,j}-\min_{n}P_{n,n}.

\displaystyle D_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=D_{k}\bigcup\left[\bigcup_{j:j\neq K}\hat{D}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle D_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=D_{j}/\hat{D}_{j},

\displaystyle j\notin K

\displaystyle D_{n}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=D_{n}

\displaystyle n\in K,n\neq k

Remark 10

Lemma 4

Assume that there exists a discrete probability density

Note first that for any overlap matrix

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{c}q_{k}P_{k,k}\geq\min_{k}\{P_{k,k}\}\sum_{k=1}^{c}q_{k}=\min_{k}\{P_{k,k}\}=1-\rho_{\max},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{c}q_{k}P_{k,k}^{\star}=P_{j,j}^{\star}=1-\rho_{\max}^{\star},

To show that

\Gamma

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{c}q_{k}P_{k,k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{c}\int_{D_{k}^{\prime}}q_{k}p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\rm r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{c}\left[\int_{D_{k}^{\prime}\cap D_{k}^{\star}}q_{k}p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\rm r}+\sum_{j:j\neq k}\int_{D_{k}^{\prime}\cap D_{j}^{\star}}q_{k}p_{k}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\rm r}\right]

Lemma

[

. Such questions are left for future work.

Equation (

Remark 13

Equations (

This example illustrates the intuitive idea that too much noise renders a classifier meaningless. Note that Eq. (

5.3
Gershgorin Radius as an Objective Function for Assay Optimization

Equations (
9
) and (
13
): (I) are uniform bounds on the classification error and prevalence uncertainty; and (II) only depend on the assay through the Gershgorin radius
\rho_{\max}
. These features imply that
\rho_{\max}
is a valid objective function whose minimum quantifies uncertainties of interest. Given a choice among assays (or more generally, input spaces
\Gamma
), we can identify the “best” one as that whose minimum value of
\rho_{\max}
is smallest.

The utility of inequality (
13
) is rooted in our ability to numerically solve this optimization problem. Section
4
accomplishes this for binary classification under the assumption that the
p_{j}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
are known. In practice, we are only given empirical data drawn from these distributions. In such cases, statistical modeling of the conditional PDFs may be prohibitive if the points
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
are high-dimensional and/or there is insufficient data to account for correlations between different dimensions.

Several empirical methods exist to address this problem. Clustering algorithms can be used to approximate classification domains, for which we can define the empirical estimates

\displaystyle P_{j,k}\approx\tilde{P}_{j,k}=\frac{1}{m_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{k}}\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{i,k}\in D_{j})

(73)

where
\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{i,k}
is the
i
th point from the
k
th class,
m_{k}
is the number of training samples in class
k
, and
D_{j}
is the domain associated with the
j
th cluster. More recently, in a binary setting, we showed that efficient homotopy methods can be used to minimize the misclassification rate of empirical training data without needing to assume the full conditional PDFs
[
18
]
. However, we acknowledge that efficient optimization of
\rho_{\max}
remains an interesting problem. See Refs.
[
12
,
14
,
40
,
56
]
for further discussion of such issues.

5.4
Additional Open Directions

Uniform bounds on assay performance are useful in settings where it is difficult to predict how prevalence will change. However, slowly spreading diseases may settle into an endemic state characterized by a time-invariant prevalence. In such cases, uniform bounds may overestimate the uncertainty associated with a diagnostic assay, leading to the need to determine whether tighter, prevalence-dependent bounds on uncertainty can be deduced, especially in a multi-class setting.
