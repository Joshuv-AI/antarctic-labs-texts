Comparative evaluation of classical and modified Tamra Bhasma: Acute oral toxicity, antimicrobial, and antioxidant profiles
Authors: Guruprasad C. Nille, Ritu Raj Patel, Himanshu Ranjan, Amaresh Kumar Singh, Meenakshi Singh, Nitesh Singh, Vijay M. Shinde, Anand Kumar Chaudhary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTamra Bhasma, a traditional Ayurvedic copper-based formulation, has been used for its antimicrobial and therapeutic benefits. However, its complex preparation and the risk of toxicity when not made according to classical methods have limited its broader clinical use. Recent efforts aim to simplify its preparation and scientifically validate its safety and efficacy.ObjectiveThe present study aimed to evaluate and compare the acute oral toxicity of two Tamra Bhasma preparations viz. Classical Tamra Bhasma I (CTB I) and a chemically processed form (CTB II) in comparison with synthetic copper oxides (A-CuO and S-CuO). The antimicrobial and antioxidant profiles are also compared between CTB I and CTB II.Materials and methodsCTB I and CTB II were prepared using standard Ayurvedic protocols with different raw materials. Acute oral toxicity was tested in female rats using OECD guideline 425 at a limit dose of 2000 mg/kg, followed by a two-week observation period. Hematological, biochemical, and histopathological analyses were performed. Antimicrobial activity was assessed against Enterococcus faecalis, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae using the disk diffusion method. Antioxidant activity was measured by DPPH radical scavenging assay.ResultsNo mortality, behavioral abnormalities, or significant weight changes were observed at the test dose. Hematological and biochemical parameters remained within normal ranges. Histopathological findings showed mild liver congestion in the CTB I group and increased sinusoidal space in the S-CuO group, with normal architecture in other organs. CTB I exhibited stronger antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria, whereas CTB II was more effective against Gram-negative strains. CTB I also showed higher antioxidant activity than CTB II, though less potent than ascorbic acid.ConclusionCTB I and CTB II have LD50 more than 2000 mg/kg and demonstrate differing antimicrobial and antioxidant effects depending on preparation methods. These results support further investigation into their therapeutic mechanisms and potential applications.

Introduction

In Ayurveda, Tamra Bhasma has been used for centuries to cure various disease conditions such as Panduroga (Anaemia), Udararoga (Ascitis), Shwasaroga (Asthama), Amlapitta (Hyperacidity), etc. due to its multifaceted mechanism [1]. The therapeutic excellence of Tamra Bhasma has been limited due to its potential untoward effects if not prepared well. "Ashtamahadoshas" refers to the eight major ill effects including Daha (burning sensation), Sweda (excessive perspiration), Aaruchi (loss of appetite), Murcha (fainting or syncope), Kleda (Excessive sebaceous/fluid secretion), Rechana (purgation), Vamana (emesis), and Bhrama (vertigo), that can occur if Tamra Bhasma is not prepared according to traditional methods [1]. Previous studies have shown the safety of Tamra Bhasma in animal models [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. However, there is little information about the risks of taking large amount of Tamra Bhasma, even by accident and for a longer time. Even though studies suggest that Ayurvedic herbo-mineral formulations with heavy metals are safe, concerns about their toxicity remain [6,7].

The method of preparation and the chemical composition of Tamra Bhasma play a crucial role in order to consider it safe and useful as effective therapeutics. Therefore, Tamra Bhasma should be tested on standard safety parameters provided by the OECD Test Guidelines. Furthermore, Tamra Bhasma is chemically identified as cupric oxide (CuO) with a crystalline nature and an average crystallite size of 32.2 nm. It is noted that the size of Tamra Bhasma particles was larger than that of engineered cupric oxide nanoparticles, which have an average size of 23.6 nm [8,9]. On the other hand, few studies have reported that Tamra Bhasma contains copper sulfide as a major phase, and it is also crystalline in nature [[10], [11], [12]].

The process of Tamra Bhasma (CTB I - Classical Tamra Bhasma I) preparation involves several steps, particularly the initial 35 steps of Samanya Shodhan (general pharmaceutical processes for making the raw material fit for further uses) [13]. In this study, lab-prepared cupric oxide powder (CuO) was used as the precursor material (S-CuO – Standard Cupric Oxide) to prepare the Tamra Bhasma (CTB II – Classical Tamra Bhasma II) [13]. Additionally, we also exposed the lab-prepared cupric oxide powder (S-CuO) to the Amritikarana process only (A-CuO – Amritikarana processed Cupric Oxide), the last step of pharmaceutical process during the preparation of Tamra Bhasma [14]. Although, they are similar structures to certain extends when characterized with high end techniques like X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), X-Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), etc. (unpublished data), there is a need to critically assess and compare the safety associated with all aforementioned preparations. While conducting these procedures using lab-prepared cupric oxide powder, we also compared the safety parameters with the inherent properties of the lab-grade cupric oxide powder. After analysing and confirming the formation of Tamra Bhasma, both the final forms of Tamra Bhasma (CTB I and CTB II) were examined for their antimicrobial and antioxidant potential by following antimicrobial disk susceptibility tests and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity, respectively.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of CTB I, CTB II, A-CuO, and S-CuO

CTB I was prepared by using copper sheet pieces (Tamra Patra) and following the standard reference method of preparation of Tamra Bhasma in Ayurveda [13]. The 35 steps of Samanya Shodhan process (general purification method) performed followed by single step of Vishesh Shodhan process. Then the sample was subjected to Puta (calcination cycles) and finally Amritikarana process (last process to make the sample safe and useful) was performed as mentioned in reference method [13,14].

In case of CTB II sample, the fine powder of lab-prepared cupric oxide (CuO) was subjected to Vishesh Shodhan process only by omitting 35 steps of Samanya Shodhan process. Then, CTB II sample was subjected to Puta and Amritikarana process following same method of CTB I.

In case of A-CuO sample, the fine powder of lab-prepared cupric oxide (CuO) was subjected to Amritikarana process only as performed in case of CTB I and CTB II samples. Finally, S-CuO sample was not subjected to any of the aforementioned processes and used as a pure fine powder of lab-prepared cupric oxide (CuO). Acute oral toxicity

An acute oral toxicity was assessed following OECD guideline 425 (reviewed, adopted on 16 October 2008; Corrected on 30 June 2022) with 2000 mg/kg as limit test on substances CTB I, CTB II, A-CuO, and S-CuO [15]. Before commencing the study, we thoroughly reviewed all available information on test substances CTB I, CTB II, A-CuO, and S-CuO, including toxicity test results on the substances, structurally related compounds, or similar mixtures. Based on the existing data [3,5], a limit test was conducted for CTB I, CTB II, A-CuO, and S-CuO, administering a single oral dose of 2000 mg/kg to each female rat. Each substance was administered as a single oral dose of 2000 mg/kg to all 5 female rats. The substances were suspended in distilled water as a vehicle (10 ml/kg) and the suspension was vortexed to ensure uniform dosing. Rats, aged 8–12 weeks and weighing 150 ± 20 g, were selected arbitrarily and acclimated to laboratory conditions for at least 5 days. Prior to dosing, food was withheld for 3–4 h, and the animals were monitored individually for any signs of toxicity at specified intervals as per OECD guidelines. The weights of the animals were regularly monitored and recorded throughout the study. Subsequently, to evaluate hematological and biochemical parameters, blood samples were obtained from the cardiac puncture under anesthesia induced by high dose isoflurane. After euthanasia, the liver, spleen, and kidneys were isolated, weighed, and preserved in 10% buffered formalin for 24 h at 4°C for further histopathological examinations. Further, some tissues were also kept at −80°C for biochemical analysis for further examination.

Hematological analysis

There were no mortality and all rats survived at the end of 14 days after a single oral administration of CTB I, CTB II, A-CuO, and S-CuO, at 2000 mg/kg. The blood samples collected under anesthesia from rats (n = 3) were stored in EDTA-tubes containing K3-EDTA at a concentration of 1.5 mg/ml for hematological assessments. A complete blood count (CBC) was performed within 2 h of blood samples collection. Samples were kept at room temperature (22–25°C) during the experimentation. Parameters include total red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin (Hb) concentration, packed cell volume (PCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), white blood cell (WBC) count, and differential leukocyte counts including monocytes (M), lymphocytes (L), neutrophils (N), and eosinophils (E). Additionally, platelet count was also determined using a hematology analyzer (Mindray BC-230 (Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., China).

Biochemical analysis

Along with hematological assessments, the blood samples were also analyzed by using fully automated biochemical analyzer (Mindray BS-230 (Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., China), for various biochemical parameters including urea, creatinine (Creatinine Kit by Modified Jaffe's Kinetic Method), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) by Aspartate aminotransferase Kit (IFCC Method; Agappe Diagnostics Ltd., India), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) by Alanine Aminotransferase Kit (IFCC Method; Agappe Diagnostics Ltd., India), alkaline phosphatase, very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) (Detergent Method, Beacon Dignostics Pvt. Ltd.), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) (PEGME Method, Beacon Dignostics Pvt. Ltd.), cholesterol (CHOD/PAP Method, Pathozyme Dignostics Pvt. Ltd.), and triglycerides (GPO-PAP Method, Pathozyme Dignostics Pvt. Ltd.) levels.

Histopathological assay

In accordance with CCSEA guidelines, the anesthetized animals were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation on day 15 for histopathological assay. The rats were confirmed dead by the absence of respiration and heartbeat, along with a lack of corneal reflex. The liver, spleen, and kidney tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h at 4°C. The tissues were processed for histological analysis. In brief the tissues were dehydrated in graded ethyl alcohol (50%, 70%, 90% for 1 h each and absolute alcohol for overnight) and then treated with xylene and then embedded in paraffin wax, section were cut using Leica semi-automatic microtome to a thickness of 5 μm, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin. The stained tissue sections were examined under a bright field light microscope (Nikon Ecilipse E200, Japan). Imaging of kidney and liver sections were performed at 400X and spleen at 100X magnification.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test

Culture and test sample preparation

The bacterial cultures of all four species used in this study i.e. Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923), Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC 29212), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 27853) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (ATCC 13883) were obtained from the Institute of Medical Sciences, Banaras Hindu University, Varanasi and maintained on Muller Hinton Agar plates. The test samples were prepared by dissolving Tamra Bhasma nanoparticles i.e. CTB I and CTB II in both distilled water and acetone having concentration of 100 mg/ml, sonicated for 10 min. Distilled water was selected as a universal, biocompatible solvent, whereas acetone was used because certain metallic and mineral nanoparticle formulations exhibit poor aqueous solubility and require a semi-polar solvent to ensure complete dispersion. Using both solvents allowed us to compare the effect of solvent-mediated solubilization on antibacterial activity. A single screening concentration (100 mg/mL) was employed in this study to allow direct comparison between the two CTB preparations and solvents. Since this work was designed as a preliminary evaluation of antimicrobial potential, a fixed dose was chosen for consistency rather than a full dose-response, which will be addressed in future studies.

Agar disk diffusion method

Antimicrobial susceptibility test was performed in compliance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute's (CLSI) guidelines [16] to determine the antibacterial activity of the two different batches of Tamra Bhasma nanoparticles i.e. CTB I and CTB II. Modified agar disk diffusion method was used evaluate the antibacterial activity of CTB I, CTB II with respect to standard drugs against both Gram positive (S. aureus and E. faecalis) as well as Gram negative (P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae) bacteria [17]. Overnight cultures of all four species were used to make bacterial suspension of 0.5 MacFarland (≈1-2 × 108 colony-forming units per ml) in autoclaved normal saline (0.85%). The prepared inoculums were spread over the Muller Hinton Agar (Hi-media) plates with a sterile cotton swab to achieve homogeneous microbial growth. The plates were then left at room temperature for ∼15 min to allow excess surface moisture to be absorbed before placing the disks. After that, the sterile paper disks (6 mm in diameter) prepared from Whatman no. 1 filter paper were placed over the bacterial lawn. The paper disks were impregnated with 20 μl of each test sample along with the respective standard antibiotic disk (Hi-media) and the agar plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The antibacterial activity of the test samples was determined by measuring the diameter of the zone of inhibition formed around the disks. Negative-control disks containing 20 μL of distilled water and acetone were also tested under identical conditions. Each test was performed in triplicate, and results were recorded as mean ± standard deviation.

Antioxidant activity (DPPH (2,2-Diphenyl-1-Picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging activity)

Stock solutions of CTB I and CTB II, were prepared at various concentrations (31.25-2000 μg/ml) by reconstituting them in their respective solvents. 60 μl of each working concentration was then added to 180 μl of a 0.1 mM DPPH solution in methanol. Plates were mixed, covered and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 20 min and then the absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a microplate reader (Agilent BioTek Epoch Spectrophotometer), against a blank containing only methanol. A separate control well containing only the 0.1 mM DPPH solution in methanol served as a reference for background absorbance. Ascorbic acid was used as standard antioxidants for comparison. The experiment was performed three times (triplicate) for each concentration. The percentage of DPPH radical scavenging activity was calculated using the following formula:% DPPH Scavenging = [(A0 - A1)/A0] x 100

Here, A0 represents the absorbance of the control well (containing only DPPH solution and solvent), and A1 represents the absorbance of the well containing the test/standard mixture, DPPH solution, and solvent [18].

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the experiment results was statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 5.0. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc Tukey test was employed for comparisons between groups. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean.

Acute oral toxicity

Estimated LD50

The median lethal dose (LD50) for all the four test substances was noted at the end of acute oral toxicity study. In the limit test conducted for CTB I, CTB II, A-CuO, and S-CuO at a dose of 2000 mg/kg body weight, with distilled water used as the vehicle (10 mL/kg body weight), no mortality was observed. Therefore, the LD50 of all the test substances is estimated to be greater than 2000 mg/kg body weight based on OECD guideline 425 limit test. (Supplementary Table 1).

Antimicrobial activity

The antibacterial activity of Tamra Bhasma nanoparticles, CTB I and CTB II dissolved in distilled water and acetone having concentration of 100 mg/ml, was determined by measuring the diameter of zone of inhibition formed around the disks loaded with test samples as shown in Fig. 3. Negative-control disks (distilled water and acetone) produced no measurable inhibition (0 mm) against all test strains (Supplementary Fig. 1), confirming that the observed inhibition was attributable to CTB formulations. The obtained zone of inhibition (mm, mean ± SD, n = 3), along with statistical analysis using two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni posttests, is presented in Supplementary Table 3 and Fig. 4. Among Gram-positive strains, E. faecalis showed the highest susceptibility, with 23 ± 2 mm and 21 ± 1 mm for CTB I (A) and CTB I (D.W), respectively, which were greater than the standard drug Nitrofurantoin (14 ± 1 mm). In S. aureus, inhibition zones of 17 ± 1, 14 ± 2, 15 ± 2, and 12 ± 1 mm were observed for CTB I (D.W), CTB I (A), CTB II (D.W), and CTB II (A), respectively, compared with Linezolid (23 ± 2 mm). In Gram-negative bacteria, moderate inhibition was observed. The highest zone of 13 ± 1 mm was recorded with CTB II (D.W) in both P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae. CTB I (A) and CTB I (D.W) yielded 11 ± 2 mm and 10 ± 2 mm, while CTB II (A) produced 11 ± 1 mm in P. aeruginosa and 9 ± 2 mm in K. pneumoniae. These were considerably smaller than the inhibition zones of their respective antibiotic controls, Levofloxacin (22 ± 1 mm) and Ciprofloxacin (14 ± 1 mm). The inhibition zones observed for Tamra Bhasma nanoparticles (9-23 mm) indicate moderate antibacterial activity, with relatively stronger effects against Gram-positive bacteria and comparatively weak activity against Gram-negative pathogens such as P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae. Although, the tested Tamra Bhasma nanoparticles exhibited moderate activity yet fall within ranges reported for other nanoparticle systems. Such as, Tamra Bhasma nanoparticles tested by earlier researchers produced zones as large as 30 mm against P. aeruginosa, 22 mm for K. pneumoniae, and 15 mm for S. aureus [19]. Copper-doped ZnO NPs demonstrated 24 mm zones against S. aureus, 18-19 mm against Gram-negative strains including E. coli and K. pneumoniae [20]. Green-synthesized CuO nanoparticles have also shown activity against MDR bacteria, with K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa among affected pathogens [21]. Discussion

Bhasmas are recognized as ancient Indian nanomedicines [23]. Their nanoparticulate nature and longstanding therapeutic uses are documented in ethnopharmacological literature [24]. The Bhasmas derived from metals are complex chemical substances that showcase ancient nanotechnology and resemble engineered metal nanoparticles of today. However, they exhibit distinct physicochemical and toxicological properties. Certain steps in the traditional methods make them superior in terms of safety and effectiveness. Nevertheless, the material science approach offers opportunities to blend traditional and complementary knowledge for the optimization and synthesis of desired and superior materials. The invention of pharmaceutical procedures like Shodhan, Maran, and Amritikarana process were significant milestones in the evolution of Rasa Shastra for converting metals into their safest and most effective dosage form, known as Bhasma. The use of Tamra (copper) can be traced back to the Vedic and Samhita periods. It has been used as a medicine since medieval times, and the detailed methods of preparing Tamra Bhasma are described in ancient literature of Rasa Shastra.

Copper oxide nanoparticles are utilized in semiconductors, antimicrobial agents, heat transfer fluids, and intrauterine contraceptive devices [25]. Experimental studies have shown that copper nanomaterials can have toxic effects on the liver and kidneys [26]. Nano-copper has resulted in severe impairment in the liver, kidney, and spleen in experimental animals. After oral administration and interacting with gastric juice, highly reactive ionic copper is formed, which is then accumulated in the kidneys of exposed animals [27,28]. In an in-vitro study, copper oxide nanoparticles (50 nm) were reported to be genotoxic and cytotoxic, disturbing cell membrane integrity and inducing oxidative stress [29]. The previous studies have reported Tamra Bhasma as cupric oxide (CuO) with a crystalline nature and an average crystallite size in the range of nanometres [8,9] and also as copper sulfide as a major phase with a crystallite size in the range of nanometres [[10], [11], [12]].

After analyzing CTB I and CTB II using X-ray diffraction (XRD), we confirmed that CTB I is a pure mixture of CuS and Cu2S, with only a few remaining peaks corresponding to sulfur (S). Similarly, CTB II was also identified as a pure mixture of CuS and Cu2S, with a small number of peaks related to sulfur. When comparing the XRD findings of CTB I and CTB II, we observed that all the peaks from both samples overlapped. Additionally, the results from X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further supported the presence of Cu2S and CuS in both CTB I and CTB II, aligning with our XRD findings. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate and compare the toxicity profiles of Tamra Bhasma prepared by traditional method (CTB I) and other two products (CTB II & A-CuO) using lab-prepared cupric oxide powder (S-CuO).

OECD TG-425 was followed using a single-sex (preferably females) to reduce variability and minimize the number of animals used. Literature surveys of conventional LD50 tests indicate that there is typically little difference in sensitivity between the sexes. However, when differences do occur, females are generally reported to be slightly more sensitive [30]. Hence, acute toxicity was evaluated in female rats. The test drugs CTB I, CTB II, A-CUO, and S-CUO, at a dose level of 2000 mg/kg orally, did not produce any mortality in the treated rats, suggesting that the LD50 value for each batch is higher than 2000 mg/kg by the oral route. In addition, Sisco Research Laboratory Pvt. Ltd. has published safety data showing that the LD50 for CuO is greater than 2500 mg/kg, which also supports the findings of this study [31].

Behavioral parameters were also found normal during the study period of 14 days (Supplementary Table 2). Body weight change reflects the cumulative effects across different body systems, and a decrease in body weight of 10% or more is typically considered indicative of toxicity induced by the substance [32]. However, in this study, no significant decrease in body weight occurred in the CTB I, CTB II, A-CUO, and S-CUO treated groups, suggesting that the administered doses did not cause substantial toxicity based on this parameter alone.

Furthermore, in the blood parameters, including both hematological and biochemical assays, normal values were observed in comparison to the vehicle-treated group. Additionally, in the histopathological study, apart from mild congestion in the liver of CTB I group with reduced sinusoidal space, and increase in sinusoidal space in S-CUO group, otherwise all groups showed normal histology.

Distilled water was chosen as the vehicle for all batches due to its common use in administering Tamra Bhasma containing herbo-mineral formulations in Ayurveda. Water serves as a typical Anupana (drug vehicle) for many medicines that contain Bhasma. Therefore, to assess its compatibility with Tamra Bhasma, we used distilled water as the vehicle in this study.

Further, the advancement of nanoscience has led to the development of copper nanoparticles, known for their superior antimicrobial properties due to their high surface area to volume ratio [33]. This increased surface area enhances their reactivity and ability to disrupt microbial cells [34]. The precise antimicrobial mechanism of copper is not fully understood. However, it is known that the physical interaction of copper nanoparticles with microbial cell membranes results in their destruction, making the cells susceptible to damage from copper ions [35,36]. Nanoparticles in the size range of 1–10 nm are particularly effective at this mechanism due to their ability to infiltrate the cells [37].

From the result, it can be concluded that the traditionally synthesized Tamra Bhasma nanoparticle CTB I dissolved in water showed moderate antibacterial activity (ZOI up to 23 mm) against Gram positive bacteria (E. faecalis and S. aureus) in comparison with Gram negative bacteria (P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae) as shown in Fig. 4 through bar diagram representation. Whereas, CTB II nanoparticles dissolved in distilled water were more effective against Gram negative bacteria than CTB I nanoparticles, with noticeable antibacterial property against S. aureus as well. The antibacterial potency of copper oxide nanoparticles has also been reported in many studies carried out in the past [38,39]. Based on the results, we can hypothesize that the inhibition of bacterial cell growth in Gram positive bacteria upon treatment with Tamra Bhasma nanoparticles CTB I could be because of its binding to peptidoglycan associated receptors present on the cell membrane whereas in Gram negative bacteria, CTB II nanoparticles could bind to the outer lipopolysaccharides resulting in break-down of cell membrane [40]. However, further investigation is needed to determine its exact mechanism of action at this stage.

The DPPH assay evaluates antioxidant activity based on the DPPH radical, which absorbs between 515 and 518 nm and appears deep purple. Antioxidants reduce the DPPH radical, causing decolorization and a decrease in absorbance. In this experiment, ascorbic acid served as the standard antioxidant. The association of organic moieties with Tamra Bhasma was investigated using FTIR analysis. The FTIR study revealed the presence of various functional groups in Tamra Bhasma after the Amritikarana process [41]. These organic moieties may stabilize Bhasma particles by acting as capping agents. These capping agents may play a crucial role in the antioxidant activity of Bhasma [42]. By increasing the amount of CTB I, CTB II, and ascorbic acid, samples of Tamra Bhasma represented the slight increase in activity as compared to ascorbic acid. Both CTB I and CTB II exhibited comparable antioxidant activity due to traditional method of preparation which may also lead to incorporation of diverse organic moieties and a slight difference in structural differences between CTB I and CTB II samples of Tamra Bhasma.

Conclusion

The test substances CTB I, CTB II, A-CuO, and S-CuO showed an LD50 greater than 2000 mg/kg body weight in the limit test. However, only CTB I and CTB II were selected for further studies due to the significance of the Bhasmikarana process in Rasa Shastra, which enhances the safety and utility of these products. Furthermore, the antimicrobial activity of CTB I and CTB II, was tested on Gram-positive and Gram-negative microbes, suggesting their potential as an antimicrobial agent. Future research should explore the mechanistic pathways behind the diverse activities of these two samples of Tamra Bhasma. Although the free radical scavenging activity of Tamra Bhasma is relatively low, this may be attributed to the traditional preparation methods and the presence of different organic moieties in the samples. Future research could focus on optimizing the pharmaceutical processing to enhance the antioxidant activity of Tamra Bhasma.
