Multifocal fatty sparing areas in a steatotic liver mimicking hepatic lesions: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Hala Qasrawi, Tasneem Dweikat, Inam Hindi, Omar Issam Shakhshir, Abdallah S Alem, Ali Shakhshir, Isra’ A Salahat, Jihad Hamaida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFatty liver disease is a major global health problem that can be challenging to diagnose, particularly when focal fatty-sparing mimics hepatic neoplasms. These imaging findings can result in undue concern and may prompt invasive procedures that could otherwise be avoided. Accurate differentiation between benign focal fat-related changes and malignant lesions requires careful correlation of imaging features with clinical and laboratory findings.Case presentationA 34-year-old Arab female with a history of hyperlipidemia and hypothyroidism was hospitalized for acute appendicitis. During her admission, multiple hepatic nodules were incidentally detected. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a diffusely fatty liver with multiple hypoechoic nodules suspicious for malignancy. However, contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan (CT scan) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) characterized these lesions as multifocal fatty-sparing areas rather than true neoplasms. Laboratory evaluation supported a diagnosis of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with dyslipidemia. Follow-up imaging demonstrated stability of the lesions, confirming their benign etiology.ConclusionThis case highlights the diagnostic challenge of distinguishing focal fatty sparing from malignant hepatic lesions in patients with NAFLD. Advanced imaging techniques, combined with clinical and laboratory correlation, are essential for accurate diagnosis and to avoid unnecessary invasive interventions.Keywords: NAFLD, Focal fatty-sparing, Hepatic neoplasms, Hepatic nodules, Ultrasound

Introduction

Liver steatosis or fatty liver is a well-defined health care problem, characterized by fat accumulation in the liver, particularly infiltrating more than 5% of hepatocytes [1, 2]. This condition is considered a major health issue worldwide with a prevalence of 25–30% [1]. Causes may be idiopathic or secondary with alcoholic liver disease and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) being the most common [3]. NAFLD is a diffuse parenchymal process that can be associated with a wide spectrum of diseases, for example, diabetes, obesity, hyperlipidemia, severe hepatitis, chronic alcohol consumption, and steroid therapy [4, 5].

Although NAFLD is common, it can exhibit atypical patterns, resulting in diagnostic challenges; therefore, knowledge of characteristic patterns of fatty changes in the liver is essential, as it can affect the detection of focal liver lesions. Fatty changes in the liver can be diffuse, focal, multifocal, perivascular, and subscapular [6, 7].

Fatty infiltration with focal fatty-sparing areas is one of the uncommonly reported patterns of fatty liver [3]. This deposition pattern can be focally localized mimicking the appearance of a benign tumor like adenoma or hepatocellular carcinoma, or multifocal showing a multinodular appearance like malignant lesions and metastasis [1, 3, 8]. Focal fatty-sparing areas can present in different shapes, such as geographic, wedge-shaped, or nodular, and can occur in typical locations (e.g., pericholecystic segments, IV and V, and posterior part of segment) or atypical sites [9].

The gold standard for fatty liver diagnosis is biopsy, however, being an invasive procedure, it has been replaced by other diagnostic criteria based on imaging techniques like ultrasound. The typical homogeneous pattern of fatty liver is easily recognized, appearing hyperechoic on ultrasonography and hypodense on noncontrast CT scan. [1]. As these lesions have a wide range of differential diagnoses, relying on the ultrasound alone for diagnosis can be challenging. Therefore, the knowledge of advanced imaging techniques like Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) findings is essential, as it’s the most precise noninvasive method for measuring hepatic fat and detecting focal liver lesions [6, 7].

In conclusion, it is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis in atypical patterns of deposition in fatty liver, especially with fatty-sparing areas, to accurately recognize it as a benign entity and avoid unnecessary invasive medical interventions.

In this case report, we present a case of fatty liver with focal fatty-sparing lesions in a 34-year-old woman with hyperlipidemia, which mimicked hepatic adenomatous lesions.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old Arab female presented with acute-onset sharp abdominal pain that began 2 days ago. The pain was initially peri-umbilical, then localized 1 day prior to admission to the right lower quadrant. On the admission day, the pain became more severe, constant & diffuse. The patient also reported nausea and three episodes of gastric-content vomiting prior to admission. The patient has a medical history of hypothyroidism and hyperlipidemia with a body mass index (BMI) of 28.7. The patient has unremarkable surgical history, drug allergies, or relevant family history, and reported no alcohol or tobacco use.

On physical examination, the patient was alert and oriented with stable vital signs except for mild tachycardia; abdominal examination revealed diffuse tenderness with mild guarding, and there were no signs of chronic liver disease.

Abdominal ultrasound revealed findings suggestive of perforated appendicitis, the patient was diagnosed with acute appendicitis, for which laparotomy was performed.

During her hospitalization, the abdominal ultrasound (Fig. 1), as part of the workup for abdominal pain, incidentally found multiple hepatic nodules. The liver was noted to be hyperechoic in echotexture (fatty liver) with multiple well-defined relatively hypoechoic nodules, and the largest measuring 2 cm. These findings prompted further imaging for evaluation.

Ultrasound demonstrates diffuse increased echogenicity of the liver suggesting diffuse fatty infiltration (A). Multiple small focal relatively hypoechoic lesions are noted (white arrows). This most likely represents a focal fatty sparing areas in the background of diffuse hepatic steatosis

Laboratory tests revealed dyslipidemia and disturbed liver function tests. Chronic dyslipidemia was manifested through LDL cholesterol—172 mg/dL, reference below 130 mg/dL; triglycerides—388 mg/dL, reference below 150 mg/dL; and total cholesterol—281 mg/dL, reference below 200 mg/dL. There was also a slight elevation of the total bilirubin levels: 1.218 mg/dL, upper reference level below 1.2 mg/dL, and gamma-glutamyl transferase, GGT 47 U/L, reference value 5–36 U/L. The white blood cell count was also high at 12.1 × 103/µL with a reference interval of 4.6–11 × 103/µL.

CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis showed the liver to be 27 cm in craniocaudal dimension, diffusely infiltrated by fat, with multiple hepatic nodules measuring up to 2.4 cm that were relatively hyperdense on unenhanced images and showed enhancement with IV contrast (Fig. 2). The lesions were suspicious of metastasis. A dedicated liver MRI was suggested for better characterization of the lesions. MRI with IV contrast showed multiple well-circumscribed variable sized hepatic lesions; the largest one was 2 cm in segment 4b. The lesions were hyperintense on T1 and T2 weighted images exhibiting a comparable level of enhancement to the liver on post-contrast images with no evidence of diffusion restriction (Figs. 3 and 4). MRI in-phase and out-of-phase images (Fig. 5) showed increased signal intensity of the liver on T1 in phase sequence, on T1 out of phase sequence there is drop of the signal intensity of the liver with the lesions becoming more conspicuous on background of fatty liver representing multifocal fatty-sparing areas of the liver.

T1-weighted images A: without contrast, B: arterial phase, C: venous phase, D: delayed phase showing multiple well-defined, variable-sized hyperintense hepatic lesions (white arrow) exhibiting a comparable level of enhancement to the liver on post-contrast images

Heavily weighted T2 image A showing multiple well-defined, variable-sized hyperintense hepatic lesions (white arrow) with no evidence of diffusion restriction B: Diffusion Weighted Image, C: Apparent Diffusion Coefficient image

MRI in-phase and out-of-phase axial cuts A and B respectively showing increased signal intensity of the liver on T1 in phase sequence (diffuse fatty liver infiltration) and multiple well-defined, variable-sized hepatic lesions (white arrows). However, on T1 out of phase sequence there is drop of the signal intensity of the liver with the lesions becoming more conspicuous on background of fatty liver representing a focal fatty-sparing areas of the liver

The patient underwent upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopies to exclude underlying malignancy, as the lesions were suspicious of metastasis on CT scan. The upper endoscopy did show a hiatal hernia, Grade 3, with gastritis of the gastroesophageal junction extending into the fundus. Colonoscopy was unremarkable.

Interventions in this case, were aimed at dealing with the metabolic dysfunction and the diagnostic uncertainty due to the presence of the hepatic nodules. Lifestyle adjustment, metabolic risk optimization, diagnostic MRI characterization, and periodic surveillance were all part of the management plan.

The patient adopted a healthy diet and lifestyle. In which, it could reduce hepatic fat by decreasing de novo lipogenesis, increasing β-oxidation, and improving insulin sensitivity. MRI was used as a diagnostic intervention to distinguish focal fatty sparing from true lesions. Follow-up liver protocol MRI after 6 months (Fig. 6) revealed decreased diffuse fatty liver infiltration (showing mild drop of signal on out-of-phase images in comparison previous studies) and the multiple well-circumscribed hepatic nodules remained unchanged.

Follow up MRI in-phase and out-of-phase axial cuts A and B, respectively showing decreased diffuse fatty liver infiltration (showing mild drop of signal on out-of-phase images in comparison to Fig. 5) and stable multiple well-defined, variable-sized hepatic lesions (white arrows)

Together, these therapies addressed the underlying metabolic state while providing an accurate diagnosis and safe follow-up.

The patient was found to have fatty-sparing areas in a background of fatty liver disease. She remains in regular follow-up for monitoring of her fatty liver and also her metabolic profile.

Discussion

Noninvasive imaging is crucial to the diagnosis and monitoring of fatty liver disease, allowing clinicians to detect steatosis and assess fibrosis while reducing reliance on liver biopsy. The choice of modality depends on availability, required diagnostic precision, and the individual clinical concern [10].

Ultrasonography is the most accessible first-line tool [10]. It detects moderate to severe steatosis based on increased echogenicity[11] but has limited sensitivity for mild disease and reduced accuracy in obesity. It cannot differentiate simple steatosis from steatohepatitis or accurately measure fibrosis [12]. CT scan can detect moderate to severe steatosis on noncontrast imaging [13]; however, its limited sensitivity for mild disease and exposure to ionizing radiation limits its utility for routine evaluation or longitudinal follow-up [14]. MRI provides the most reliable noninvasive assessment of liver steatosis by chemical shift imaging [13]. Its utility is limited by cost and availability [14].

CT and MRI also play a central role in differentiating benign from malignant hepatic lesions, particularly in patients with NAFLD where focal fatty sparing or deposition can mimic neoplastic processes [1]. On noncontrast CT, steatosis typically appears as diffuse hypoattenuation of the liver, while areas of focal fatty sparing appear relatively hyperdense [15]. These regions preserve normal vascular architecture, lack mass effect, and typically follow predictable anatomical patterns—features that strongly favor a benign etiology[16]. In contrast, malignant lesions often appear as distinct masses with irregular margins, disruption of adjacent vessels, or characteristic enhancement patterns such as arterial-phase hyperenhancement and delayed washout [17].

MRI provides even greater diagnostic precision. Areas of focal fatty sparing retain signal intensity on opposed-phase imaging, reflecting the absence of intracellular fat—a distinguishing feature from true neoplastic lesions[18], which do not demonstrate signal loss on chemical-shift sequences [19]. Furthermore, malignant lesions often exhibit restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging and display aggressive dynamic contrast enhancement behaviors [20], whereas benign fatty-sparing areas remain iso- or mildly hyperintense without suspicious enhancement or diffusion characteristics. These MRI features were critical in confirming the benign nature of the nodules identified in our patient [17].

This case illustrates the difficulties in diagnosis caused by focal fatty sparing in a liver affected by diffuse steatosis. Focal fatty sparing, which can mimic neoplastic lesions, complicates the radiological and clinical assessments, particularly when the patient has nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, as our patient with hyperlipidemia does [1, 2, 21].

Focal fatty sparing typically appears as geographic areas within a background of diffuse steatosis, and its imaging features may resemble those of both benign and malignant liver lesions. In our patient's case, the preliminary ultrasound showed several hypoechoic nodules in an otherwise hyperechoic liver, aligning with pseudolesions noted in existing literature [5]. This led to additional evaluation with contrast-enhanced CT and MRI. The CT findings of hyperdense nodules on unenhanced images and contrast enhancement, coupled with the MRI features (lesions hyperintense on T1-weighted sequences with retained signal on out-of-phase images) —a key feature that distinguishes fatty sparing from neoplasms [22]) provided critical clues for a benign etiology. When these imaging features are considered alongside the clinical context and stable follow-up findings, it aids in preventing misinterpretations that could result in unnecessary invasive interventions [23, 24].

Although multifocal fatty-sparing areas can be confidently characterized on ultrasound and MRI [25], assessment of the underlying diffuse liver disease often requires additional noninvasive tools [26]. FibroScan (transient elastography) plays a complementary role in this context by quantifying global hepatic steatosis through the Controlled Attenuation Parameter (CAP) and by measuring liver stiffness to evaluate fibrosis severity [27]. However, because transient elastography lacks high spatial resolution and provides an averaged measurement over a broad region of the liver, it cannot identify, localize, or characterize focal or multifocal fatty-sparing regions [28]. Thus, its value lies not in diagnosing the focal sparing itself, but in assessing the background steatosis and fibrosis that typically accompany these imaging findings [27].

Numerous case reports [1, 2, 6] and reviews [21] have highlighted instances of focal fatty sparing that resemble tumors, emphasizing the significance of advanced imaging techniques in evaluating these abnormalities. Although noninvasive imaging techniques like CT scans and MRIs provide reliable diagnostics as long as they are interpreted within the correct clinical context, biopsy remains conclusive in situations that are unclear [23, 24]. This diagnostic puzzle is compounded by the wide range of possible differential diagnoses that include both benign (such as hepatic adenomas) and malignant lesions (such as metastases and hepatocellular carcinoma). The diagnosis of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) was more favored in this patient compared to a primary neoplastic development on the basis of his clinical history as well as lab reports (dyslipidemia and mild liver function abnormalities). Regular imaging follow-up found the lesions to be benign and confirmed their stability. This approach is consistent with the emerging scientific consensus that improved imaging, in conjunction with clinical and laboratory results, is critical for avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures [29].

Conclusion

This case highlights the need for a thorough diagnostic approach in patients with fatty liver disease who present with atypical focal lesions. Understanding the imaging spectrum of focal fatty sparing—particularly its variable appearance across ultrasound, CT, and MRI—and correlating these findings with clinical and laboratory data are paramount. With such a method, correct diagnosis can be made, appropriate measures can be taken, and unnecessary invasive procedures can be avoided.
