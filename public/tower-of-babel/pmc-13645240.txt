Vertigo-unsolved Mystery (Meniere’s Disease) Care Redefined: Insights from a Tertiary Care Center
Authors: Arti Rajpoot, Rajat Jain, Sunil Kumar, Abhishek Bahadur Singh, Veerendra Verma, Manish Chandra, Anupam Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Meniere’s disease remains a poorly understood inner ear disorder since its first description by Prosper Meniere in the early 1800s. Characterized by episodic vertigo, fluctuating hearing loss, and tinnitus, it continues to challenge clinicians due to its unpredictable course and variable treatment response. Lifestyle modification alone often fails to adequately control vertigo episodes.Aim and Objectives:To assess the effectiveness of oral pharmacotherapy in controlling vertigo and stabilizing hearing in patients with Meniere’s disease, and to develop a practical, non-invasive treatment protocol suitable for routine clinical use.Materials and Methods:Patients diagnosed with Meniere’s disease as per 1995 AAO-HNS criteria were managed with standard lifestyle advice followed by oral pharmacotherapy, including an antihistaminic agent (betahistine) and a combination of diuretics (furosemide and spironolactone). Treatment response was evaluated based on vertigo control, reduction in episode frequency, and stabilization of audiometric thresholds. Surgical options were considered only for patients with persistent, disabling vertigo despite maximal medical therapy.Results:Lifestyle measures alone did not provide adequate vertigo control. In contrast, combined oral pharmacotherapy resulted in marked improvement, with significant reduction in vertigo frequency and satisfactory stabilization of hearing levels. Only a small proportion of patients required evaluation for surgical intervention.Conclusion:A regimen combining betahistine with diuretics offers a reliable, non-invasive, and effective approach to managing Meniere’s disease. This protocol provides substantial vertigo control and hearing stabilization, and may be adopted as a first-line strategy before considering surgical options.

INTRODUCTION

Meniere’s disease eludes understanding to this date since its first description as a constellation of symptoms-episodic vertigo, fluctuating hearing loss, and tinnitus by Prosper Meniere in the early 1800s.[1] Several attempts have been made to clearly understand and elicit the pathophysiology of this unique disease. This is because the disease is highly variable in presentation, characterized by unpredictable acute exacerbations and spontaneous remissions. The management of Meniere’s disease is thus a challenge to the treating clinician. The following is an attempt to put forward our experience in the management of patients suffering from Meniere’s disease.

Being a tertiary center of care, we come across a large population of patients who complain of dizziness, instability, swaying, spinning, aural fullness, along with several nonspecific symptoms which they perceive as “vertigo”. These patients also complain of a wide array of associated symptoms, such as nausea, vomiting, hearing disturbances, blackouts, spontaneous falls, and visual disturbances. These symptoms are often brought on by a change in head position or triggers such as loud noises and bright light, excessive intake of caffeine or head trauma. It is essential to determine if the feeling experienced by the patient is true vertigo. Thus, a detailed history is of utmost importance to characterize the symptoms, duration, type, and associated symptoms to identify the underlying problem.

In our setup, all patients presenting with complaints of dizziness or swaying are first allowed to describe their episodes of “vertigo” to the best of their abilities. A note is made of the nature of dizziness or instability experienced by the patient – whether it is swaying, lightheadedness, turning, or spinning. Any accompanying symptoms, if present, such as nausea or vomiting, are also taken into account. A brief inquiry is also made into the events leading to vertigo or any trigger factors that the patient might have noticed that bring on an attack of vertigo. Some patients may experience vertigo following exposure to bright light or loud sounds. Certain strong fragrances are also known to cause nausea, headache, photophobia, and even dizziness. In such a setting, a diagnosis of migraine is more likely. The duration of a single attack of dizziness also gives great insight into the probable underlying problem. A fleeting vertigo of 2–3 s triggered by a specific head position points towards benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV); posterior semicircular canal involvement being the most common type. Episodic vertigo lasting for about 20 min–24 h which is spontaneous in onset and also accompanied by fluctuating hearing loss and tinnitus, is more characteristic of Meniere’s disease. Prolonged dizziness over days to weeks typically points toward vestibular neuronitis. Severe prostrating vertigo with autonomic symptoms is characteristic of labyrinthitis. Taste disturbances and symptoms pointing to other cranial nerve dysfunction help to delineate between a central cause of vertigo, rather than the labyrinth. A history of recent road traffic accident or fall from height resulting in head injury usually contributes to the diagnosis of BPPV as a cause of vertigo, which can be easily remedied by positioning maneuvers. Any neurological deficit or lack of coordination may also point towards a cerebellar lesion, which might be producing a sense of instability for the patient. Cardiovascular affections such as myocardial infarction, stenting, or atherosclerosis leading to vertebrobasilar insufficiency may also lead to vertigo in an individual. An impaired glucose-insulin metabolism has also been observed to play a major role in the etiopathogenesis of Meniere’s disease[2] and thus a history of diabetes mellitus and review of glycemic control also forms an essential part of the evaluation of a patient with vertigo.

After a careful history of the vertiginous episodes was taken, neurotological examination was done to rule out other peripheral causes of vertigo, such as BPPV, labyrinthitis, drug induced vertigo, etc. In patients with Meniere’s disease all of these tests came out to be negative, as it is primarily a disease of the inner ear arising from disturbances in the endolymphatic drainage. The diagnostic criteria proposed by AAO-HNS (1995) were used to establish a working diagnosis of Meniere’s disease. A battery of vestibular tests was performed to rule out other peripheral vestibular disorders. These patients were subsequently treated using a step-up protocol and monitored 12 months.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective study spanning 12 months was ethically approved by the Institutional Review Board (No 1058/Ethics/2022 dated August 26, 2022). Verbal consent was obtained from every patient, and all procedures involving human participants were in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

As per the 1995 AAO-HNS criteria for the diagnosis of Meniere’s disease, patients falling in the category of “Probable Meniere’s Disease” and “Definite Meniere’s Disease” were enrolled in the study. A total of 52 patients were enrolled. The nature of the disease was duly explained to the patients before enrolling them in the study. All enrolled patients had presented to us with complaints of episodic vertigo lasting for 20 min–24 h accompanied by hearing loss. These patients belonged to the age group of 20–80 years. Vestibular assessment

A battery of vestibular tests was performed elucidate the diagnosis of Meniere’s disease supplemented by the suggestive history. A brief description of the tests performed is as follows:

Romberg’s test

During Romberg’s test, the patient was made to stand with feet as close together as possible with their eyes closed. A positive test was indicated by a fall to either side in case of central vestibular disorders such as dorsal column disease or multiple sclerosis. The side to which the patient falls indicated the side of the lesion.

Unterberger’s test

The patient was asked to march on the spot with their eyes closed and hands stretched forwards for 1 min. A deviation of more than 30° was considered positive and was suggestive of central vestibular pathology.

Spontaneous nystagmus

Nystagmus was observed in primary gaze i.e. with the patient looking straight ahead. A jerky nystagmus with fast and slow phase is very characteristic if peripheral vestibular disorder.

Head impulse test

It was used to test the vestibulo-ocular reflex. The head of the patient was held firmly with both hands and turned to one side in a swift but gentle motion while the patient was asked to fix the gaze onto the examiner’s forehead or root of the nose. A corrective saccade to fixate on the examiner’s forehead/root of nose was suggestive of a hypo-functioning ipsilateral peripheral vestibular system.

Dix-Hallpike test

Used to test for vertigo due to posterior canal BPPV. The patient was seated on the examination table. The head of the patient was turned 45° to one side and the patient was made to lie down swiftly, such that the head was 30° below the edge of the table. The patient was held in this position till nystagmus appeared or at least for 1 min to account for the latency of peripheral vertigo. Nystagmus in this position indicated the semicircular canal involved. The results were interpreted as per the following description[3] detailed in Table 2.

Head hanging test

This test was used to for anterior canal BPPV. The patient was swiftly taken from a sitting to a supine position, ensuring that the occiput touched the edge of the examination table. Downbeating nystagmus (toward the patient’s chin) was suggestive of anterior canal BPPV.

McClure Pagnini test

It was used to test for lateral canal BPPV. The patient was made to lie down supine with head elevated 30° using a pillow. The head was turned to one side, and the position was maintained until the nystagmus stopped or at least for 1 min in case there was no nystagmus. The head was turned back and the position maintained for 1 min before turning the head to the other side. The presence of nystagmus or vertigo in the direction of head turn indicated the side of the lateral canal involved.

Audiological assessment

A baseline pure tone audiogram was obtained to aid the diagnosis of Meniere’s disease as per the AAO-HNS criteria. Pure tone audiometry indicated sensorineural or mixed hearing loss. In addition, Short Increment Sensitivity Index (SISI) testing was performed to reinforce the diagnosis. A SISI score exceeding 70% was considered positive, indicating potential cochlear pathology and enhancing the sensitivity of our diagnostic approach. PTA was also done every 3 months to monitor the progression of the disease.

A neurology consultation was sought to exclude central causes of vertigo in patients with a history of head trauma and headache. A contrast-enhanced MRI of the head was also conducted for documentation. Vestibular Evoked Myogenic Potential (VEMP) studies were not feasible at our facility due to resource limitations. Following a thorough neurotological examination and exclusion of alternative causes of vertigo in our patients, according to our protocol, we provided medical treatment. In cases of intractable vertigo that did not respond to medical management, surgical intervention was considered as the next step.

Oral pharmacotherapy

Patients with severe symptoms and frequent unpleasant episodes of vertigo were offered pharmacological support in addition to lifestyle modifications. This group consisted of 41 patients. Acute episodes characterized by dizziness and troublesome vomiting were managed by tablet cinnarizine 25 mg TDS or tablet Prochlorperazine 5 mg TDS for 5 days in the form of mouth dissolving tablets along with tapering doses of prednisolone. In addition, these patients were also advised bed rest. Any form of vestibular testing was strictly avoided during acute episodes. Although an audiological assessment (PTA) to document any change in the hearing thresholds was done, keeping in mind the comfort of the patient during the vertiginous episode. For maintenance therapy, these patients were started on a combination of vasodilator and diuretics to relieve the endolymphatic hydrops (ELH) and prevent further end-organ damage. These patients were prescribed a daily dose of tablet Furosemide 50 mg + spironolactone 20 mg along with tablet betahistine 48 mg/day in 2 divided doses.

Surgical management

In our study population, four patients had intractable vertigo. Despite maximum medical management, these patients suffered frequent attacks of vertigo and experienced other unpleasant symptoms such as nausea, tinnitus, aural fullness, and progressive worsening of hearing. These patients were offered surgical intervention to relieve the ELH. These patients, after proper counseling, were taken up for cortical mastoidectomy with endolymphatic sac decompression.

RESULTS

In our experience with 52 patients suffering from Meniere’s disease, the first step in management was dietary modifications, supplemented by pharmacological control of vertiginous episodes. Patients were advised to avoid smoking and caffeinated drinks such as tea and coffee. Salt intake was also restricted to up to 50 mg/day. Acute episodes of vertigo were managed by diuretics and antihistaminic drugs (betahistine). In addition to lifestyle modifications in the form of dietary salt restriction, caffeine restriction, cessation of smoking, and stress management, oral pharmacotherapy was offered to enrolled patients. Antihistaminic drugs (betahistine) in combination with diuretics (furosemide + spironolactone) were started for a period of 6 months. Antihistaminic medication (betahistine) combined with diuretics (furosemide + spironolactone) was initiated for a duration of 6 months. Among 52 patients, 46 individuals receiving this combination (betahistine 24 mg twice daily along with furosemide + spironolactone) achieved significant management of vertigo and reported a decrease in its frequency. At the initial assessment, all patients showed varying degrees of hearing loss, as indicated by pure tone audiometry results. DISCUSSION

As previously stated, Meniere’s disease is difficult to diagnose at the preliminary evaluation because of its vast spectrum of clinical presentation. Some patients may experience a classical triad of symptoms and may be readily diagnosed after careful vestibular examination and audiological tests. While some patients may only suffer from a nonspecific feeling of aural fullness and episodic vertigo with little or no perception of hearing loss. Unfortunately, only 27% patients present with a classical triad of symptoms.[4] Thus, it seems far-fetched to suspect a diagnosis of Meniere’s disease in such patients, and they remain undiagnosed and untreated for a long time. The disease can manifest unilaterally or bilaterally, and hearing loss may not affect both ears simultaneously. Owing to its complex etiology and variable presentation, there is no universally accepted treatment protocol to date for this age-old disorder. Therefore, drawing from our successful experience with the disease, we propose a diagnostic and treatment protocol that has proven effective, is noninvasive, cost-effective, and well-received by patients without the need for complex diagnostic equipment and procedures.

As far as our experience is concerned, all 52 patients enrolled in our study presented with acute vertigo but did not have any of the “red flag” signs and symptoms,[5] ruling out a central etiology. We followed a carefully laid down diagnostic protocol for patients presenting with complaints of vertigo at our center. A battery of 7 aforementioned vestibular tests, allowed us to gain insights into the cause of vertigo, facilitating diagnosis rather than merely providing symptomatic relief to the patient. The testing procedure was straightforward and did not require the use of sophisticated instruments. In today’s era of technological advancements, various modalities are available to confirm the diagnosis of Meniere’s disease, such as Electrocochleography, which involves using a transtympanic probe to record Summating Potential (SP) and Action Potentials (AP). The utility of Electrocochleography has been well documented; for instance, Gibson et al. found that the SP/AP ratio in Meniere’s disease was 51%, compared to 25% in normal ears, indicating abnormal summation potential due to electrolyte disturbances in the inner ear fluids.[6] Similarly, since ELH is the underlying pathophysiology in Meniere’s disease, assessing saccular function and the inferior vestibular nerve through VEMP studies can aid in objectively diagnosing the condition.[7]

However, as per our experience with patients of Meniere’s disease, a good clinical history and neurotological examination is sufficient to establish a working diagnosis. Electrophysiological studies such as electrocochleography, electronystagmography, cervical VEMP studies undoubtedly objectively help the clinical diagnosis but are not essential. In addition, studies have shown that only 27% patients with Meniere’s disease show abnormal results on electrocochleography, and electronystagmography can detect unilateral hypofunctioning in only 54% of patients.[91011] Thus, objective results of these studies are not necessarily required to diagnose Meniere’s disease. They can be considered more of an adjunctive modality than an absolute requirement in the diagnosis of Meniere’s disease.

Our primary goal of treatment in Meniere’s disease was to control symptoms, prevent disease progression, and to improve quality of life.

The first line of management offered in our treatment protocol is dietary and lifestyle modifications, such as cutting down salt and intake of caffeinated drinks. Tyrrell et al.[12] also advocate cessation of smoking as well as limiting alcohol consumption. They also believe that indulgence in tobacco as well as chocolates exacerbates vertigo.[12] A salt-restricted diet (50 mg/day) is recommended in patients with severe symptoms. The beneficial effects of salt restriction have been highlighted in a number of previously conducted studies.[1314] However, all of these studies differ in the degree of restriction depending upon the functional impairment of the patient caused by episodes of vertigo. The low sodium intake is believed to lower systemic blood pressure and thus lower the endolymphatic system pressure.[15]

In our experience, compliance was better with the NAS (no added salt) diet than with the salt-restricted diet. Lifestyle modification should be supplemented with oral pharmacotherapy. The cornerstone of our recommended treatment protocol involves combining betahistine with diuretics in oral tablet form supplemented by antioxidants. Betahistine has been widely used in the treatment of Meniere’s disease as well as in treating vertigo of other origins. Its efficacy has been proven beyond doubt and is attributed to an increase in cochlear blood flow as well as a decrease in the sensory input from the vestibular end organs.[16] A major advantage with the use of betahistine is that it does not cause unwanted effects such as headaches, flushing, blurred vision, vomiting, or sedation.[16] Acharya et al. reported a significant reduction in the number of vertigo attacks in patients treated with betahistine alone. These patients were administered a daily dose of 24 mg betahistine at bedtime for 6 weeks and achieved good control in the severity of vertigo as well.[17] In our experience, the most effective results for patients were achieved with a combination of betahistine and lasilactone, alongside antioxidant supplementation. This approach led to a notable decrease in the frequency of vertigo episodes and helped stabilize hearing loss. The rationale for including antioxidants is supported by their known benefits in treating idiopathic sudden sensorineural hearing loss and their ability to mitigate glutamate excitotoxicity and oxidative stress, as demonstrated in animal models of ELH by Low et al.[18] These factors contribute to apoptotic processes in type 1 spiral ganglion cells, which are linked to the permanent hearing loss seen in this condition. Another study by Takumida et al.[3] also identifies antioxidants as a promising new adjunct to the medical treatment of Meniere’s disease. Their work with free radical scavengers such as rebamipide, Vitamin C, and glutathione demonstrated improvements in patients with poorly controlled Ménière’s disease.

In case oral pharmacotherapy is ineffective, we recommend surgical options to address the problem. During our study period, surgical decompression of the endolymphatic sac was required only in four patients with intractable vertigo. These patients did not benefit adequately, even with maximal oral pharmacotherapy and lifestyle modifications. The patients were posted for cortical mastoidectomy under general anesthesia, and endolymphatic sac was identified posterior to posterior semicircular canal below the Donaldson line. Sac decompression was achieved by removing the overlying bone. The procedure was found to be curative in our experience. The utility of ESS remains a topic of debate within the medical community. Following Thomsen’s endolymphatic sac sham study in 1981, the popularity of ESS has waned.[19] One faction of clinicians argues that any benefits observed from ESS are merely placebo effects. Conversely, another group contends that decompressing or shunting the endolymphatic sac enhances endolymphatic flow, thereby potentially resolving symptoms associated with Meniere’s disease.

Our clinical experience with ESS is limited, as only four of our patients underwent endolymphatic sac decompression. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that all four patients achieved satisfactory control of vertigo following the surgical intervention. This suggests a potential efficacy of ESS in managing Meniere’s disease symptoms, although based on a small sample size.

Intratympanic steroid injections also find their use in the treatment of Meniere’s disease. Dexamethasone increases the principal epithelial sodium transporter of semicircular canals by threefold[20] by its potent mineralocorticoid action. Another trial substantiates the effectiveness of intratympanic steroids in Meniere’s disease – 91% of patients with Menière’s disease had adequate symptom control and did not progress to require more ablative therapies.[21] Intratympanic injections of gentamicin have also been found to be useful in Meniere’s disease by virtue of their selective vestibulotoxic action on type I vestibular hair cells. However, various studies have reported varying degrees of hearing loss following gentamicin therapy.[2223]

Having achieved excellent response by oral pharmacotherapy and lifestyle modifications itself, we opted not to utilize invasive treatments such as intratympanic steroids injections, vestibulotoxic agent injections, vestibular neurectomy, or labyrinthectomy. CONCLUSION

As per our experience, satisfactory results in terms of vertigo control and stabilization of hearing loss were achieved using oral pharmacotherapy. The need for surgical intervention in the management of Meniere’s disease is a far alternative to be explored in cases of intractable vertigo and end-organ damage. Thus, our proposed protocol is not only noninvasive and easily applicable, but also effective.
