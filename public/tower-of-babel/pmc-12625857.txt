Clinical utility and cost-effectiveness of BeginNGS newborn screening by genome sequencing and standard newborn screening for severe childhood genetic diseases: an adaptive, international and comparative clinical trial
Authors: Rebecca Reimers, Miranda Bailey, Chester Brown, Kee Chan, Tom Defay, Terri Finkel, Scott Kahn, Liana Protopsaltis, Lauren Stoddard, Ajay J Talati, Kristen Wigby, Ammira Sarah Al-Shabeeb Akil, Meredith Wright, Stephen F Kingsmore, Stephen F Kingsmore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn the last 60 years, newborn bloodspot screening (NBS) has expanded as a public health intervention from a single severe childhood genetic disease (SCGD) to up to as many as 80 SCGD and testing of ~40 million newborns/year worldwide. However, the gap between current NBS and its potential to increase the efficiency, effectiveness and global equity of healthcare delivery for SCGD is large and rapidly growing. There are now effective therapeutic interventions—drugs, diets, devices and surgeries—for up to 2000 SCGD. Since almost all SCGD can be identified by bloodspot genome sequencing, it has been a longstanding goal to supplement current NBS with genome sequencing-based NBS (gNBS) for all eligible SCGD. We recently described a novel gNBS platform (named Begin Newborn Genome Sequencing (BeginNGS)) with the potential to overcome several major challenges to gNBS (cost, scalability, false positives and an unprepared healthcare workforce). A pilot clinical trial of BeginNGS for 412 SCGD in a level IV neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) had a true positive rate of 4.2%, sensitivity of 83%, positive predictive value of 100% and clinical utility rate of 4.2%, indicating readiness of the platform for use in a powered, multicentre study.Methods and analysisThe BeginNGS study is a single group, international, multicentre, adaptive clinical trial to compare utility, acceptability, feasibility and cost-effectiveness of BeginNGS gNBS (experimental intervention) with standard NBS (control). A minimum of 10 000 neonates (aged <28 days, maximum of 100 000) will be enrolled across 25 racial, ethnic and ancestry populations and five enrolment site types (high-risk obstetrician offices, labour induction office visits, newborn nurseries, NICUs and well-baby visits). BeginNGS is gNBS for circa 2000 SCGD (currently 508 SCGD). The primary objective of the trial is to generate equitable evidence to support broad implementation of gNBS. Enrolled newborns receive both interventions (BeginNGS and standard of care NBS). Newborns who screen positive receive confirmatory testing and medical follow-up for at least 1 year to obtain outcomes data. The primary outcome measure is clinical utility, defined as the proportion of diagnoses identified by BeginNGS and state NBS during infancy that are likely to benefit (likely to have an improved outcome) from treatment. We hypothesise that BeginNGS has a greater rate of clinical utility than standard NBS. An adaptive design was chosen rather than a traditional, fixed design to allow accumulating results to make the trial more efficient, informative, equitable and ethical by addition or removal of SCGD and genetic variants, population enrichment (for under-represented racial, ethnic and ancestral groups) and sample size re-estimation. Adaptive design will also facilitate meta-analysis with other clinical trials of gNBS, providing greater power to test utility in ultra-rare SCGD. Parents will be approached (in person, via phone or via electronic communication) to provide informed consent to enrol their newborns prenatally, postnatally in newborn nurseries or NICUs or at well baby outpatient visits. This study is part of phase III of the BeginNGS programme. Patient and public voices have been engaged in the design and execution of each BeginNGS phase through individuals and groups joining the BeginNGS consortium and participating in the family and community engagement work group. gNBS has the potential to transform the way we diagnose and treat childhood genetic diseases. Preliminary data suggest that national adoption of BeginNGS for all births has the potential to improve outcomes of >50 000 US children per year.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the WCG Clinical institutional review board on 14 February 2024, and the most recent amendment approved on 7 October 2025 (approval number 20235517). Study findings will be shared through research consortium workshops, national and international conferences, community presentations and peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberNCT06306521.

Introduction

Newborn bloodspot screening (NBS) is the risk evaluation of the birth population for single severe childhood genetic disease (SCGD) that meets the classic Wilson and Jungner principles.14 In brief, this is the subset of SCGD that has early onset, suitable methods for identification and available effective therapies for which early intervention can reduce morbidity and mortality.14 The rationale for NBS is that risk identification at birth enables early diagnosis and timely treatment, minimising morbidity and mortality, with consequent benefits for the individual child, their family and society.24 In the last 60 years, NBS has expanded as a public health intervention from one SCGD to up to 80, testing of ~40 million newborns/year worldwide and identification of ~12 500 affected US infants per year (https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CFH/DGDS/Pages/nbs/default.aspx#:~:text=Newborn%20screening%20began%20in%20California,congenital%20(present%20at%20birth.511 Despite this expansion, the gap between the current scope of public health NBS and its potential to increase the efficiency and effectiveness of healthcare delivery for SCGD is large and rapidly growing. There are now effective therapeutic interventions—drugs, diets, devices and surgeries—for up to 2000 SCGD.1220 Furthermore, almost all the circa 10 000 known SCGD can be identified by a single test—bloodspot genome sequencing.21 22 Thus, it has been a longstanding goal to supplement current NBS with genome sequencing-based NBS (gNBS) for all SCGD that meet the classic principles.12325 Rapid genome sequencing is already a proven first-line test for diagnosis of SCGD in critically ill newborns, and policies exist for its use in neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) populations in 15 US State Medicaid programmes and the National Health Service in England and Wales.21 22 While early research studies demonstrated the feasibility and safety of genome-based or exome-based NBS, they were limited by cost, lack of scalability and technological immaturity.2631 Recently, the cost, analytic performance and scalability of genome sequencing have advanced towards meeting the requirements of population screening.21 In addition, technology innovations in the gNBS platform Begin Newborn Genome Sequencing (BeginNGS) have shown the potential to overcome several other major challenges to future implementation (interpretation cost, false positives and an unprepared healthcare workforce).32 33

Clinical trials are now needed to evaluate the clinical utility and cost-effectiveness of gNBS. Recently, numerous groups worldwide have started, or are planning, independent clinical trials to do so.1213 3445 Initial results are encouraging. In a pilot clinical trial of BeginNGS gNBS with 412 carefully chosen SCGD in 120 newborns in a NICU who were not under consideration for genetic testing, the true positive rate (TPR) was 4.2%, sensitivity was 83%, positive predictive value (PPV) was 100% and clinical utility rate was 4.2%.34 With 2000 genes representing the sum of almost all those under consideration for gNBS, the TPR in the same newborns was 10.8%, with sensitivity of 93% and clinical utility rate of 10%.34 Unsurprisingly, each gNBS study design and protocol reported to date has evidence gaps.46 47 First, each study evaluates only a subset of the circa 2000 SCGD that appear to meet criteria for population gNBS.13 33 34 Second, individual studies are underpowered to test end points for individual, ultra-rare SCGD (childhood prevalence less than one in 50 000).48 Traditionally, the minimum requirement for inclusion of a new disorder in standard NBS was that at least one true positive (TP) be identified in a prospective trial (in addition to evidence that all other criteria for inclusion were met).8 49 When SCGD are defined as gene-disorder dyads (rather than disorders), many of the circa 2000 SCGD proposed for gNBS have a childhood prevalence between 1 per 100 000 and 1 per million.33 Further stratification is needed for gene-disorder dyads that are associated with more than one mechanism of disease, and thus more than one therapeutic intervention set (and clinical utility measurement). Third, almost all protocols are limited to single geographic locations and thus many minority genetic ancestries are under-represented. If uncorrected, this gap will lead to inequitable evidence generation in minorities. Under-representation of many populations must be addressed to help reduce existing inequities, facilitate equitable access to the potential benefits of genomics and advance global equity in genomics.50 51 Historically, under-representation of non-northern European individuals has been a significant limitation in genome research.52 For gNBS, this is particularly problematic since the birth prevalence and causal variants of many individual SCGD vary widely between genetic ancestries.33 Furthermore, for many SCGD, we do not yet know the impact of ancestral heterogeneity or genetic background on natural history or response to treatment. Fourth, the gNBS methodologies employed have been borrowed from diagnostic genome sequencing and lack solutions for the major challenges to future implementation listed above. In particular, interpretation of diagnostic genome sequencing is phenotype delimited and performed in individuals with high pretest probability of SCGD.21 22 In contrast, gNBS interpretation is performed without phenotypes and with low pretest probability of SCGD.34 Thus, gNBS using ‘diagnostic’ interpretation methods tends to suffer from very low PPV due to false positive diplotypes that contain pathogenic (P) variants that are not SCGD causal.32 33 These are variants that, while P or likely pathogenic (LP), have low penetrance and/or expressivity in children.32 33 While this is not a concern when performing diagnostic genome sequencing of affected children, low PPV is unacceptable in population gNBS.25 53 54 The BeginNGS study has several phases. The first phase was to develop a prototypic gNBS platform for 388 SCGD and examine analytic performance retrospectively in 459 083 cases and controls.32 The second phase was to develop a first version clinical gNBS platform for 412 SCGD and examine analytic performance retrospectively (in 618 290 cases and controls) and prospectively (in 120 newborns in the Rady Children’s Hospital (RCH) NICU who were not suspected of having SCGD).33 34 The current third phase entails developing a fully automated gNBS platform for >2000 SCGD, examining analytic performance retrospectively in >1 million cases and controls, and starting a large, adaptive, multicentre clinical trial (described here). Informed by phase III, a future phase will further develop the gNBS platform, complete the adaptive trial and undertake implementation pilot studies.

Informed by the phase II pilot study34 and gap analysis of other planned trials, we have designed a complementary protocol with five distinctive elements: first, it employs an innovative gNBS platform with prequalification of diseases, genes, variants, modes of inheritance and therapeutic interventions to provide high PPV and high likelihood that interventions improve SCGD outcomes.32 33 Second, it features innovative software to address implementation challenges related to informed consent, return of results, counselling, confirmatory testing and therapeutic guidance.16 17 32 33 55 Third, an adaptive design was chosen rather than a traditional, fixed design to allow accumulating results to make the trial more efficient, informative and ethical. Performance will be re-evaluated once per year and the gNBS platform and study adapted by addition or removal of BeginNGS disorders and genetic variants, population enrichment (for under-represented racial, ethnic and ancestral groups), place of consent (perinatology visits, newborn nurseries, NICUs and well-baby visits) and sample size re-estimation.56 Fourth, enrolment is multistate and multinational to achieve powered sampling of diverse genetic ancestries. Fifth, it features all 2000 SCGD anticipated to merit inclusion. Sixth, there are multiple secondary endpoints to facilitate harmonisation and meta-analyses with other studies.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This is an international, multicentre, adaptive clinical trial to compare utility, acceptability, feasibility and cost-effectiveness of BeginNGS gNBS (experimental intervention) with standard NBS (control) (figure 1). Enrolled newborns receive two interventions: BeginNGS gNBS for ~2000 SCGD (minimum 412) and standard NBS for each jurisdiction. There is no allocation to different intervention groups and, therefore, no blinding to interventions. Both interventions are performed on bloodspots obtained from neonates by a heel prick between birth and age 28 days. Newborns who screen positive for standard NBS will receive standard-of-care confirmatory testing and medical follow-up. Newborns who screen positive for BeginNGS will receive specific confirmatory testing for that condition and medical follow-up to evaluate study outcomes. The study hypothesis is that BeginNGS gNBS has greater clinical utility than standard NBS. The null hypothesis is that BeginNGS does not have greater clinical utility than standard NBS. The primary outcome measure is clinical utility, defined as the proportion of diagnoses identified by BeginNGS and standard NBS during infancy that are likely to benefit (likely to have an improved outcome) from treatment. An adaptive design was chosen rather than a traditional, fixed design to allow accumulating results to make the trial more efficient, informative, equitable and ethical by addition or removal of SCGD and genetic variants, population enrichment (for under-represented racial, ethnic and ancestral groups) and sample size re-estimation. Adaptive design will also facilitate meta-analysis with other clinical trials of gNBS, providing greater power to test utility in ultra-rare SCGD.

Flow diagram of the adaptive clinical trial of BeginNGS newborn screening for hundreds of severe childhood genetic diseases by genome sequencing. The analytic framework for comparison of the clinical utility, acceptability, feasibility and cost-effectiveness of BeginNGS and standard state public health NBS is shown. This analytic framework is based on that used by the US Preventive Services Task Force for evidence reviews of healthcare interventions.71 BeginNGS, Begin Newborn Genome Sequencing; gNBS, genome sequencing-based newborn bloodspot screening; GTRx, genome-to-treatment; TP, true positive.

The trial sponsor is Rady Children’s Institute for Genomic Medicine (RCIGM), San Diego, California, USA. The trial is overseen by a seven-member steering committee whose chair is the principal investigator (PI). The steering committee receives recommendations from a larger advisory council with specific working groups. The steering committee and advisory council meet four times per annum. Consent is provided by parents or guardians of newborns. Consent includes sharing of results of state NBS with study personnel, an additional heel prick for BeginNGS, BeginNGS testing with return of results via a physician of record, parental questionnaires and sharing of electronic medical record (EMR) information with study personnel to evaluate outcome measures, additional future testing of proband DNA and additional future research using the proband genome sequence.

This study is part of phase III of a larger, multiphased BeginNGS programme. Patient and public voices have been engaged in the design and execution of each BeginNGS phase. This occurs through individuals and groups joining the BeginNGS consortium and participating in the family and community engagement work group. Suggestions and feedback are solicited and incorporated.

Study aims

This study has three aims. Aim 1 is to compare the utility of BeginNGS gNBS with that of standard NBS. The primary objective of the trial is to generate evidence to support or refute broad implementation of BeginNGS gNBS. Therefore, the primary outcome measure is clinical utility, defined identically to Genome England’s Generation Study (which will enrol 100 000 newborns in the UK).57 Clinical utility is defined as the proportion of enrollees who have a confirmed diagnosis in infancy (by 1 year of age) and are likely to benefit (likely to have an improved outcome) from an indicated therapeutic intervention. Indicated therapeutic interventions and outcomes will be assessed using a Delphi method by an expert panel assisted by a virtual management guidance system (genome-to-treatment (GTRx)).16 17 32 33 GTRx contains structured information for each BeginNGS disorder including confirmatory testing, indicated specialist consultants, indicated therapeutic interventions, efficacy of those interventions and evidence supporting their efficacy. For each disorder (or group of similar disorders with overlapping treatments), the panel will determine a categorical likelihood of treatment (both in infancy and in childhood) and improved outcome (all, most, some, none). We hypothesise that gNBS will demonstrate significantly greater clinical utility than standard NBS. Greater clinical utility may result from a greater number of disorders screened, greater sensitivity for disorder identification or greater likelihood of changes in management. Secondary outcome measures of the utility of BeginNGS and standard NBS include: (a) test positive rate (PR); (b) confirmed TPR (and PPV) and (c) diagnosis rate during infancy (adjudged to be affected by the disorder by 1 year of age). Determination of outcomes will be made through a combination of study generated values, medical record review and participant surveys. Since analytic performance metrics have previously been measured for standard NBS and gNBS and are known, they were not chosen as the primary outcome measure. Other secondary outcome measures of the utility of BeginNGS and standard NBS are: (a) proportion in whom an indicated therapeutic intervention is commenced and (b) proportion in whom a disease outcome is changed. These were not chosen as the primary outcome because they will not be measurable for many BeginNGS disorders for several years. Subgroup analysis of outcome measures by race, ethnicity, genetic ancestry and groups of disorders will evaluate the generalisability of utility. Adaptive sample size re-estimation will ameliorate variation between jurisdictions in NBS disorders tested, between prevalence in disorders (total and by genetic ancestry) and between/within disorders in disease severity (and treatment intensity).

Aim 2 is to compare the acceptability and feasibility of BeginNGS and standard NBS. Acceptability will be measured by (1) the proportion of parents who agree to enrol their newborn in the study and (2) results of physician and parental questionnaires. We will also perform subgroup analysis of acceptability by race and ethnicity (as proxies for environmental effects), genetic ancestry and enrolment setting (perinatology visits, newborn nurseries, NICUs and well-baby visits). Feasibility will be measured by (1) time to return of results, (2) time to confirmation of results as TP (by specific confirmatory testing) and (3) time to diagnosis (time to be ascertained to be affected based on symptoms or signs of disease). Aim 3 is to determine the cost-effectiveness and budget impact of BeginNGS to the US healthcare system. Previous studies determining the cost-effectiveness of NBS have used decision analytic Markov models.5864 A range of economic approaches is being explored for gNBS, to estimate the life-time health effects and costs of gNBS versus NBS and no screening for relevant sentinel diseases and/or disorder groups. To improve the transparency, comparability and policy relevance of gNBS in different sites across the world, we aim to harmonise where possible our approach to data collection for inputs and outputs to compare costs and benefits using similar model structures.57 We anticipate that the final model structure and/or inputs may still differ from those developed by other gNBS groups, largely influenced by differences in data availability and healthcare system nuances. The model will aim to use the outcomes of the primary end point of the study to inform the timing and resultant change in clinical practice. Data on long-term outcomes and for control patients (those identified through NBS or via a clinical diagnosis) are planned to be obtained from literature, local data and expert opinion where necessary. Cost-effectiveness outcome measures are expected to include cost per quality adjusted life year, the comparative cost per TP result, cost per diagnosis and other measures previously used to evaluate standard NBS58 (eg, average cost-effectiveness ratio to prevent one infant death) as appropriate for the time horizon of the clinical trial. Extrapolations to longer time horizons (eg, lifetime) will also be performed. The current cost per positive result is US$3150 and cost per diagnosis by standard NBS in the US State of California is US$118 125 (at an NBS fee of US$210 per newborn). Summary data for each sentinel disease and/or disorder group will enable estimation of cost-effectiveness based on the expected incidence rate of the gene mutation and/or the type (eg, acute, chronic, definitive) and cost of care provided. This method should avoid the need for individual cost-effectiveness assessments for each new addition to the gene panel.

Disorder, gene, mode of inheritance, variant and therapy selection

SCGD, genes, modes of inheritance, variants and therapies are selected in a phased manner with clinical re-evaluation of the panel every 12 months that is informed by knowledge at that time. Phase I was completed on 1 September 2022 and comprised 29 865 P/LP variants associated with 388 SCGD.32 Phase II was completed on 5 December 2024 and comprised 53 855 P/LP variants, 342 genes, 412 SCGD and 1603 therapies.33 Phase III prospective gNBS enrolment described in this protocol started 1 July 2024, including 412 SCGD in the first year. Analysis is anticipated to eventually contain circa 2000 genes and >500 000 variants. Thus, BeginNGS is an adaptive test with analytic and clinical performance that improves with time. Lists of selections and methods of selection have been described in detail and are very briefly summarised here.32 33 SCGD were chosen for review that met the following criteria: acute presentations that could lead to neonatal, paediatric or cardiovascular intensive care unit admission; having somewhat effective treatments (defined as having some efficacy in management of primary or secondary manifestations of the condition); high likelihood of rapid progression without treatment and diagnosable by genome sequencing. Publications relating to interventions associated with these SCGD were extracted, curated and adjudicated by a national panel of clinical and biochemical geneticists using a modified Delphi technique and electronic data capture. Interventions were retained for which consensus existed regarding their indication for treatment of each specific SCGD, indication for use in early childhood, contraindicated subgroups, efficacy category (curative, effective or ameliorative) and existence of evidence supporting efficacy. For SCGD with at least one specific therapeutic intervention with at least ameliorative efficacy, candidacy for inclusion in BeginNGS was evaluated by traditional Wilson and Jungner criteria (Is the natural history of this genetic disease well-understood? Is this genetic disease a significant risk for morbidity and mortality in infants or young children? Is there at least one therapeutic intervention available that is specific, at least ameliorative and accepted? Does early treatment improve outcome? Do the benefits of early intervention clearly outweigh the risks? For genes with more than one associated disorder, do their treatments differ, and can they be distinguished by diagnostic genome sequencing or additional testing?). Disorders passing this evaluation were evaluated further (Did the gene have several phenotype associations that represent a single phenotype spectrum? Was the gene-disorder association of uncertain significance? Is the SCGD phenotype restricted to a very small subset of observed variants? Can a majority of affected individuals be identified by short-read genome sequencing? Is childhood penetrance sufficient for population screening?). Disorders passing this evaluation were further evaluated regarding mode of inheritance (For disorders with variable inheritance, would the burden of follow-up of asymptomatic positives overwhelm primary care paediatricians if the more permissive mode of inheritance was chosen? For X linked disorders, does lyonisation lead to sufficiently severe disease in female carriers to warrant population screening?). For retained disorders, responses to each of these questions were used to populate a web resource for BeginNGS, called GTRx (https://gtrx.rbsapp.net).16 17 32 33

Selected variants were germline that mapped to the selected SCGD, had P or LP assertions according to ClinVar or Genomenon in May 2023, and with evidence curation and variant interpretation according to standard American College of Medical Genetics clinical guidelines. Variants unlikely to cause SCGD because of excessive frequency in large population datasets or absence of supportive evidence of disease causality in functional databases were excluded, as described.32 33

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Parents will be approached at enrolment sites (in person, via phone or via electronic communication) to provide informed consent to enrol their newborns in three contexts: (1) prenatally (at obstetrician visits including induction of labour); (2) postnatal inpatients (in newborn nurseries or NICUs) or (3) at well baby visits in the outpatient setting. Parents approached in person and by phone will be a convenience sample based on enrolment staff availability. Inclusion criteria are: (1) neonates (aged <28 days) at enrolment sites; (2) parents have identified a primary care provider (or group). Exclusion criteria are: (1) neonates whose mother is <18 years of age; (2) who are wards of the state; (3) whose parent/legal guardian is unable to provide consent; (4) parents with a home address outside the jurisdiction of the enrolment sites. For example, for San Diego enrolment, parents resident in Mexico; (4) neonates or fetuses who are ill and in whom enrolment or sampling is anticipated to interfere with healthcare provision at delivery; (5) neonates who are under active consideration for a rapid diagnostic genome sequence or other diagnostic genetic testing.

Informed consent

A partial waiver of Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) authorisation was granted to review medical records to prescreen potentially eligible study participants including pregnant persons for recruitment purposes. The recruitment procedures are considered minimal risk to the potential participants with potential for benefit. Recruitment could not practically be carried out without a waiver. A partial waiver of HIPAA authorisation was not anticipated to adversely affect the rights and welfare of the potential participants as records will be accessed only by study staff and any recorded information will be stored securely with minimal risk to loss of confidentiality. All information for those not ultimately enrolled in the study will be destroyed at the conclusion of this study. Treating physicians will be informed whether potential participants qualify for the study.

Study staff will communicate with one or both parents and review study procedures and consent documents either in person, via electronic communication or by phone. Informed consent will be sought for newborns only. Enrolment for vulnerable populations, such as neonates, will follow 45 CFR 46 as outlined in the code and section 12. For most participants, this research falls under section 404 of 45 CFR Part 46, Subpart D (the research is not greater than minimal risk). If the family declines enrolment, research staff will ask the parent if they are willing to provide their reason for not participating. Free-text documenting the reason for non-participation will be entered into the study database. Following informed consent, copies of the fully executed consent documents will be provided to the parents as well as placed into the study site EMR. E-consent technology that is HIPAA and 21 CFR Part 11 compliant may be used. Parents or legal guardians may withdraw consent at any time. On withdrawal of consent, no further follow-up information will be obtained, and all identification codes will be removed from health information, genome sequences and variant call files. De-identified health information, genome sequences and variant call files will be retained for de-identified research analyses and laboratory quality assurance.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome measure is comparison of the clinical utility of BeginNGS gNBS and state NBS, defined by the proportion of enrollees likely to benefit (likely to have an improved outcome) from an indicated therapeutic intervention (figure 1④). Clinical utility is determined at 1 year of age by the number of TP (confirmed) enrollees adjudged to be affected (diagnosed) who are likely to be treated (based on the natural history of the disease) and likely to benefit from treatment (based on treatment efficacy). GTRx provides an objective, structured framework for measuring the primary trial outcome.16 17 32 33 GTRx provides the natural history for each disorder with and without treatment, the likely age when each therapeutic intervention would be started and how efficacious each treatment is. The likelihood of initiating treatment and likelihood of benefit from each treatment will be reviewed by an expert panel using a Delphi method (assisted by GTRx) for each disorder (or group of disorders with similar presentations, treatments and outcomes). We hypothesise that the clinical utility of BeginNGS is significantly greater than that of state NBS. The null hypothesis is that the clinical utility of BeginNGS is not significantly greater than state NBS.

Rationale for choice of primary outcome

According to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2022 extension, the primary outcome should provide “the most clinically relevant and convincing evidence directly related to the primary objective of the trial”.65 The magnitude of the difference in clinical utility between BeginNGS and standard NBS will be very important in justifying broad implementation of gNBS. Notably, the overall clinical utility has not previously been published for standard NBS. Prior comparisons have shown that gNBS is less sensitive for recommended uniform screening panel (RUSP) conditions than state NBS. For these reasons, it is not obvious a priori what the magnitude of the increase in clinical utility of BeginNGS will be. Importantly, the primary outcome measure is common to that of the Generation Study, facilitating comparison of results and meta-analysis.57 The Generation Study is enrolling 100 000 British newborns with different racial and ethnic characteristics to many of the enrollees in our study. We did not choose the proportion of enrollees in whom an indicated therapeutic intervention was commenced or the proportion in whom a disease outcome was changed due to treatment as the primary outcome direct measurement, since for many BeginNGS disorders these events may not occur during infancy. The severity of disorders may be insufficient to warrant treatment for years, outcomes may take years to occur and treatments may not change outcomes measurably for years. However, for disorders where it is feasible to directly measure clinical utility during infancy, we will do so as a secondary outcome, and the results will be compared with the primary outcome measure. While the confirmed PR and diagnosis rate are much easier to measure than clinical utility, we rejected these as the primary outcome because they have previously been compared in multiple studies for gNBS and standard NBS for RUSP conditions.2631 While standard NBS has been criticised as a ‘weak’ comparator to gNBS, it has been the standard of care for over 60 years.311 It is currently performed on 98% of US newborns and is considered clinically and cost-effective.311 We rejected alternative designs such as an observational study (with no comparator arm) or randomisation (with a ‘placebo’ no-test arm or other experimental comparator arm, such as an NBS panel test). There is significant merit in the evaluation of the overall clinical utility of RUSP NBS since such an assessment has not been published hitherto.

Sample size determination

While the sample size is limited by the budget available for enrolment, testing and follow-up, it must be sufficient to allow statistical significance testing of the primary outcome measure. To evaluate sample size requirements, we undertook a pilot comparative clinical trial of 120 newborns in a regional (level IV) NICU who were not under consideration for genetic testing. Enrollees received BeginNGS and standard NBS. The clinical utility rate (primary outcome measure of the current protocol) of BeginNGS gNBS with 412 SCGD was 4.2%. For BeginNGS gNBS with 2000 genes, it was 10%. For standard California NBS, it was 2.7%.34 The design of the pilot study was similar to the current study and suggested a minimal sample size for level IV NICU enrolment with the BeginNGS 412 SCGD and 2000 gene tests. The minimum number of individuals needed to detect a difference in clinical utility between 4.2% and 2.7% with α=0.05 and β=0.05 is 1795, while between 10% and 2.7% is 468 (table 1). Based on the pilot study, enrolment of 468–1795 newborns in level IV NICUs is necessary to test the primary outcome measure, dependent on how many SCGDs are evaluated.

In addition to level IV NICU enrolment, we propose consent and/or sampling in level II and level III NICUs, newborn nurseries, high-risk obstetrician visits and well-baby outpatient visits, where the pretest probability of an SCGD is much less. For example, standard CA NBS of all ~400 000 newborns per year leads to diagnosis of ~800 SCGD (TPR 0.2%), which is 14-fold less than in the pilot study. Like BeginNGS, the Genomic Uniform-screening Against Rare Disease in All Newborns (GUARDIAN) study is evaluating gNBS, but with 156 SCGD with established interventions and 99 neurodevelopmental disorders in the general New York City newborn population.44 In the first 3982 GUARDIAN enrollees, the TPR for standard NBS was similar to that of CA NBS (0.15%, n=6).44 In contrast, the TPR for gNBS in GUARDIAN was 3.0% (n=120, although 77% of TPR were glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency).44 With the conservative assumptions that all standard NBS TPs and one-quarter of GUARDIAN gNBS TPs will meet the current study definition of clinical utility, and that BeginNGS will have a similar TPR to GUARDIAN gNBS in the general newborn population, the minimum number of individuals needed to detect a difference in clinical utility between gNBS and standard NBS with α=0.05 and β=0.05 is 3231 (table 1).

In addition to evaluating the primary outcome measure with consent and/or enrolment in diverse clinical settings, we wish to test it in all US Census Bureau populations. The average race and ethnicity of live births in the USA from 2021 to 2023 was 25.0% Hispanic, 50.9% white, 14.0% black, 0.7% American Indian/Alaska Native and 6.3% Asian/Pacific Islander. For efficient achievement of statistical significance in all US Census Bureau populations, we are enrolling in San Diego County, California (where 39.4% of births are Hispanic and 13.6% are Asian/Pacific Islander), and in Shelby County, Tennessee (where 56.8% of births are black). The sample size to test the primary outcome in Hispanic babies in San Diego County is 1188–8201, depending on the number of disorders tested and clinical setting of sampling. For black babies in Shelby County, it is 824–5688. Assuming most efficient enrolment in San Diego and Shelby Counties, and future sites in Hawaii and Alaska, the sample size to test the primary outcome in all US Census Bureau populations is 2750–30 400 (table 1).

For the primary outcome to be significant from an equity, rather than a statistical, standpoint, it requires the sample set to include adequate representation of the genetic diversity of all regions in which implementation of BeginNGS is sought. The paired fixation index (FST) measures genetic distance between populations and might be used to determine how many diversity groups should be sampled to encompass human genetic diversity of the regions in which implementation of BeginNGS is sought. In the 1K genome project, for example, 15 populations across the geographies of Europe, East Asia and South Asia were sampled that had >0.01 FST. Thus, a paired FST of >0.01 might be used to determine how many populations should be sampled for adequate representation of genetic diversity. With this cut-off, the genetic diversity of East Asia, for example, can be represented by 16 populations.67 68 While the 1K genome project did not attempt to represent the full genetic diversity of African populations, their sample had 25 populations from five continental regions.69 70 There are no clear precedents for sample size estimation for each population from an equity standpoint. The 1K genome project sampled 100 genomes from each population, which was considered sufficient to evaluate common genetic variant diversity.69 70 A potential scope for the current study from an equity standpoint could be a minimum of 468 newborns from each additional 1K genome population not represented by the US Census Bureau populations, or 9828 newborns.

These considerations inform a total anticipated sample size requirement of 12 578–40 229. For this reason, the study goal is a minimum of 10 000 neonates (maximum of 100 000) newborns to be enrolled across all racial, ethnic and ancestry groups and all clinical site types. The study design is adaptive regarding sample size and population enrichment to allow accumulating results to make the trial more efficient, informative, equitable and ethical. Adaptation will be informed by prespecified interim primary end point testing on enrolment of (1) 500 level IV NICU neonates; (2) 500 high-risk obstetric office enrollees; (3) 1000 total enrollees; (4) 1000 San Diego enrollees; (5) 1000 Shelby County enrollees; (6) 1000 enrollees in each additional geography where genetic distance is thought to be paired FST of >0.01 and (7) 3000 total enrollees.

In addition to global clinical utility comparisons, it is desirable to evaluate the clinical utility of gNBS for each SCGD, and, ideally, for each SCGD in each population. McNemar’s test with Edward’s correction indicates that clinical utility in six or more BeginNGS participants is sufficient to demonstrate a difference from state NBS (p<0.05). Thus, it should be possible to evaluate the primary end point for glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, sickle cell disease, Klinefelter syndrome, Turner syndrome, beta thalassaemia, cystic fibrosis and possibly haemophilia A and Duchenne muscular dystrophy. It should also be possible to evaluate the primary end point for groups of disorders with unifying phenotypes and therapeutic interventions, such as immune deficiencies, anaemias, infant-onset genetic seizure disorders and infant-onset genetic conditions causing respiratory failure, heart failure, failure to thrive, severe jaundice, severe hyperglycaemia or hypoglycaemia, metabolic acidosis and endocrine abnormalities.

BeginNGS gNBS testing

The BeginNGS test format is adaptive, allowing improvements with time. Genomic DNA will be extracted from DBS punches and sequencing libraries prepared without PCR as previously described.33 34 Following 2×101 or 2×150 nt sequencing on Illumina HiSeq 6000 and NovaSeq X Plus instruments, genome sequences will be aligned to human genome assembly GRCh37 or GRCh38 and variants identified with DRAGEN V.4.3 (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA), dependent on the clinical protocol in use at that time. Interpretation and reporting are performed using both a current clinical method and a research method. The clinical method is manual and uses the clinical protocol for BeginNGS at that time (currently V.3 with 508 SCGD, online supplemental table 1). Variant call files (VCF) are uploaded to Enterprise (Fabric Genomics, Oakland, California, USA). Filtering is performed within Enterprise to retain only genotypes within genes included in BeginNGS and consistent with the BeginNGS mode of inheritance for each SCGD. These are manually interpreted by a variant scientist, molecular geneticist and genetic counsellor according to standard guidelines.33 34 In the future, Fabric GEM and Transformer will automatically annotate and rank genotypes in genes associated with SCGD. Variants classified as P or LP that occur within BeginNGS genes and that occur in diplotypes that fit the mode of inheritance of that disorder are reported. Variant genotypes may be confirmed by orthogonal testing at laboratory director discretion. For positive and negative results, the physician of record will be notified, and a BeginNGS test report will be placed in the EMR within 2 weeks of DBS collection. RCIGM genetic counsellors are available to explain BeginNGS test results to physicians and parents. If a newborn is discharged from hospital prior to return of results, the primary care paediatrician will be notified of the BeginNGS test results. For positive BeginNGS test results, a second clinical guidance (GTRx) report is issued by a study medical geneticist, which explains the reported findings in plain English, disorder inheritance, disorder prevalence, available confirmatory tests (to evaluate whether the result is a true or false positive), clinical features of the disorder (to evaluate whether the newborn is affected), specialist consultants to whom the newborn can be referred if a TP and effective therapeutic interventions indicated for that SCGD.16 17 32 33 Thus, positive BeginNGS results are reported in a similar manner to the workflow for positive state newborn screens, whereby the primary care paediatrician will be notified and a plan for follow-up and communication will be made. The primary care paediatrician and/or the study staff geneticist or proxy will communicate with the family regarding BeginNGS test results and next necessary actions. Genetic counselling support will be available to participants.

The second method of interpretation is automated and uses the research version of BeginNGS at that time (V.3 at present with 2000 genes). VCFs and variant metadata from Fabric Enterprise, Ensembl, Gnomad, ClinVar and variant effect prediction will be imported to a sparse multidimensional array database (TileDB Cloud, Boston, Massachusetts, USA), and queried in a batch for the presence of ~250 000 P and LP variants associated with ~2000 BeginNGS V.3 SCGD that are present in diplotypes that fit the modes of inheritance for BeginNGS disorders. No research BeginNGS results will be returned to physicians, parents or recorded in the EMR. The research BeginNGS results are compared with those of the clinically validated gNBS test to evaluate precision and recall. P and LP variants that are not causal for SCGD are identified based on genotype prevalence in large datasets and review of evidence for pathogenicity. Non-SCGD-causal variants are added to a block list. Approximately once a year, the research version of BeginNGS will be validated for clinical use and become the clinical method in use at that time. A new evolved research version is then developed at that time. In this way, the BeginNGS test and GTRx clinical guidance are adaptive. New variants, pathogenicity annotations, disorders and therapies are added or removed as the literature and evidence evolves. When a new clinical version of BeginNGS is introduced, results for prior enrollees are regenerated. Consent includes the ability to recontact the family and physician of record for new positive findings, which are reported as noted above.

Future screening across the lifespan

The incremental cost of testing an existing genome sequence for additional disorders is minimal. There are many genetic disorders that meet the criteria described above for screening, but which have onset later than early childhood. For example, the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) recommends that all diagnostic genome sequencing results include findings for 81 genes to ‘identify and manage risks for selected highly penetrant genetic disorders through established interventions aimed at preventing or significantly reducing morbidity and mortality’. Through collaboration with Dr Jonathan Berg, University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, we propose to develop three panels of genetic disorders that meet the criteria described above for screening, but which have onset later than age 4 years, 11 years and 16 years. We propose an opt-in consent mechanism, whereby parents can indicate whether they would like to be recontacted in the future for genome re-analysis for these panels. During the next 4 years, we will develop and validate the first of those panels. At those times, we will seek informed consent to perform re-analysis under a separate research protocol. At age 11, we will also seek assent from the child. At age 16, in most individuals, it will be the child who provides informed consent.

Statistical analyses

We will use McNemar’s agreement test with Edward’s correction for 2×2 contingency tables with dichotomous traits, with matched pairs of tests. We will use the McNemar-Bowker test for r×r contingency tables. Significance will be measured with a p value of 0.05. CIs will be measured by Wilson score. We will control for type I error in the primary analysis step (clinical utility) and control for testing of multiple secondary end points with a false discovery rate correction.

Interim comparative data analysis

The study design is adaptive regarding sample size and population enrichment to allow accumulating results to make the trial more efficient, informative, equitable and ethical. Adaptation will be informed by prespecified interim primary end point testing on enrolment of (1) 500 level IV NICU neonates; (2) 500 high-risk obstetric office enrollees; (3) 1000 total enrollees; (4) 1000 San Diego enrollees; (5) 1000 Shelby County enrollees; (6) 1000 enrollees in each additional geography where genetic distance is thought to be paired FST of >0.01; (7) 3000 total enrollees. Following interim primary end point testing, adaptations may include addition/removal of SCGD and genetic variants, population enrichment (for traditionally under-represented racial, ethnic and ancestral groups), place of consent (high-risk obstetrician visits, newborn nurseries, NICUs and well-baby visits) and sample size re-estimation. Adaptation decisions will be made by the steering committee, with guidance from the advisory council and working groups.

Data access and management

Data sharing and privacy

Hospital staff have access to confidential information in the form of patient medical records, research plans for public and private institutions and research results that, if made public, could adversely impact the patient. Medical information is protected under federal and local privacy laws and under the terms of the consent process.

Participant confidentiality

All research records, PHI and genomic sequencing data will be electronically stored in a password-protected database accessible only to research personnel. HIPAA identifiers of all study participants will be kept in a password-protected database and will be linked only with a study identification number for this research. All computer entry and networking programmes will be done using study identification numbers only. All data will be entered into a computer and will be password protected. All sequencing data and biospecimens will be stored securely by RCIGM. Clinical information will not be released without written permission of the participant, except as necessary for monitoring by IRB, the Food and Drug Administration, the Office for Human Research Protections, the Sponsor or the Sponsor’s designee. Data will be stored in a locked office of the investigators and maintained for a minimum of 3 years after the completion of the study.

Records to be kept

Clinical testing information may be recorded in clinical laboratory information systems. These will be stored according to that specific laboratory’s Standard Operating Procedures in compliance with HIPAA and Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act (HITECH). A master linking list will be maintained that links the participant’s medical record number with their study number. De-identified genomic sequencing data will be stored in a password-protected relational database.

Secure storage of data

The research record generated will consist of electronic files and/or a secured Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system (or similar secure database). Security measures include storage of the password-protected electronic files on a password-protected computer in a restricted access departmental folder limited to only listed study personnel and/or in a secured, password-protected REDCap database with access limited to research study members. All study-related material will be stored securely. Access to research records, prescreening logs and the master list will be limited to study personnel and maintained securely with password protection on a secure internal server. Study information, personal identifiers, such as name, medical records number and contact information will be stored in REDCap.

Publication of results

De-identified data and results of analyses will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at medical conferences. As far as possible, results will be aggregated to anonymise individual results. Certain de-identified results, such as genetic variants or case studies, will be published or presented without aggregation.

Data statement

Types and amount of scientific data expected to be generated in the project

This project will produce medical, genome sequence, confirmatory testing and perception/opinion data generated or obtained from EMRs, genome sequencing, analysis and interpretation, surveys and data repositories. Data will be collected from up to 100 000 (minimum 10 000) research participants (newborns) and their parents, generating 100 000 datasets totaling approximately eight petabytes (minimum 800 terabytes) in size. Enrolment and consent, participant demographics and clinical data such as case report forms will be captured by each site using secure, password-protected electronic data capture systems (such as REDCap). Laboratory data related to the performance of sequencing will be recorded via the Rady Children’s Institute databases (RCIGM Genomic Portal, Case Analysis Tracker). Metadata, other relevant data and associated documentation

To facilitate interpretation of the data, metadata, documentation, protocols, informed consent forms and data collection instruments will be shared and associated with the relevant datasets. The Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) Data and Specimen Hub (DASH) is a centralised resource that allows researchers to share and access de-identified data from studies funded by NICHD. For data submitted to DASH, variable-level metadata will be provided using the DASH Codebook, which is a templated data dictionary, and will include details of Common Data Elements, definitions and standards used for data collection and sharing.

Related tools, software and/or code

Test orders are captured via RCIGM-developed Genomic Portal application. Clarity LIMS (Illumina Software) is used to execute lab procedures and workflows and collect laboratory data. Illumina DRAGEN is used for generation of genomic data, which are then uploaded to Fabric Genomics software for interpretation. Interpretation will be augmented by use of Fabric Genomics GEM, Transformer, Variant Annotation, Analysis and Search Tool (VAAST) and Phenotype Driven Variant Ontological Re-ranking (PHEVOR interpretation algorithms. Demographic data, outcome measure data, molecular findings data, variant data, confirmatory testing data, clinical feature data, survey data and metadata will be made available in csv format and will not require the use of specialised tools to be accessed or manipulated. Text data will be made available in pdf format. Genomic data will be made available in BAM, CRAM or VCF format, which requires the use of specialised tools, such as Fabric Enterprise to be accessed and manipulated, and is available for a fee through Fabric Genomics. Custom tools to provide assessments of genes, disorders, variants, patterns of inheritance, clinical features, subspecialist consultation, nomenclature, incidence, natural history, mechanisms and treatments for NBS-by-GS, and custom tools to provide management guidance for parents and practitioners receiving results of NBS-by-GS have been developed by the research team and by Illumina (including DRAGEN) and Fabric Genomics (including Enterprise). Requests for these tools should be directed to the PI or Illumina and Fabric Genomics, respectively. Clinical and laboratory data will be collected in the electronic data capture system (such as REDCap) and analysed using open-source statistical software packages. Any study-specific analysis software, such as R code, will be available through GitHub.

Data standards

Patient level (enrolment and consent, participant demographics and clinical data) will use NIH Common Data Elements Repository. Genomic data files will be generated for each participant, including whole-genome sequences (BAM/CRAM; https://samtools.github.io/hts-specs/SAMv1.pdf) and VCF (https://samtools.github.io/hts-specs/VCFv4.2.pdf). Clinical features data will be standardised using HPO whenever possible. Genetic and genomic data will be standardised using Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) nomenclature. Disease names will be standardised using Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM whenever possible. Variant pathogenicity will be standardised according to ACMG guidelines. CONSORT 2010 guidelines, including the Adaptive Designs CONSORT Extension and CONSORT-Outcomes 2022 Extension, will be followed for complete and transparent reporting of the clinical trial. Reporting of patient-reported outcomes will follow the CONSORT PRO extension.

Repository where scientific data and metadata will be archived

NICHD DASH (clinical and laboratory data); database of Genomes and Phenotypes (dbGaP)/Sequence Read Archive (SRA, Genomic data); ClinVar (variant pathogenicity assertions).

How scientific data will be findable and identifiable

Clinical and laboratory data will be findable and identifiable using a Digital Object Identifier (DOI) created by DASH. Genomic data will be findable and identifiable using a dbGaP study accession number and SRA sequence record accession numbers generated by National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI. Variant pathogenicity assertions will be findable and identifiable by accession numbers generated by ClinVar.

When and how long will the scientific data be made available?

The study team will submit an initial batch of processed and cleaned clinical and laboratory data to DASH after completion of the Interim Comparative Analysis of 1000, 5000 and 10 000 enrollees. The repository will make the data accessible within 3 years of the last patient’s last study visit or at the end of the performance period, or no later than the date of publication, whichever is sooner. The study will be registered in dbGaP at the time of data cleaning and quality control measure initiation. Genomic data will be shared according to Gene Expression Omnibus Dataset (GDS policy, which requires that genomic data must be submitted within 3 months following completion of data generation and released within 6 months of data submission to the repository or at acceptance of the publication, whichever is first. The first submission to dbGaP/SRA will be timed to the completion of the Interim Comparative Analysis of data generated for the first 1000 samples, and the second submission will be timed to the completion of the Interim Comparative Analysis of data generated for 5000 samples. Data will be preserved for as long as possible within the repositories’ data retention policies.

Factors affecting subsequent access, distribution or reuse of scientific data

Clinical data will be shared with controlled access in DASH for general research use, as allowed by the participant’s informed consents and the institutional certification. Genomic data will be shared with controlled access in dbGaP/SRA for general research use, as allowed by the participant’s informed consents and the institutional certification.

Whether access to scientific data will be controlled

Data will be under controlled access with the General Research Use Data Use Limitation, as allowed by the informed consent and the institutional certification.

Protections for privacy, rights and confidentiality of human research participants

Informed consent documents used for the proposed clinical trial will include explicit language informing the participant or legally authorised representative that residual biological specimens, including DNA, may be stored in a biorepository for other scientific investigations. The informed consents will contain language permitting secondary use with broad data sharing under controlled access with general research use restrictions in DASH (clinical data) and dbGaP/SRA (genomic data). Patients will not be contacted or re-consented for future sharing or accessing data through repositories. Privacy and confidentiality protections consistent with applicable federal, Tribal, state and local laws, regulations and policies will be followed. Data will be de-identified by removing all 18 HIPAA identifiers prior to sharing, and the study will have a Certificate of Confidentiality from NIH.

Oversight of data management and sharing

Data will be submitted by a project data manager from the PI’s project team. The data manager will oversee data collection, analysis, storage and sharing. Compliance with the plan will be monitored by the PI routinely. The PI will conduct monthly meetings with key study personnel to ensure the timeliness of data entry and will review data to ensure quality of data entry. The PI will ensure data are submitted and shared according to this data management and sharing plan (DMSP.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public engagement has been included at each phase of the study design and execution. The BeginNGS study has a patient and family and community engagement work group, which meets every other month virtually and twice per year in person to review current study materials, recruitment status and discuss future plans. Suggestions and feedback are incorporated into the adaptive trial.
