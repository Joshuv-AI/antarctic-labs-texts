AI as a differentiated cognitive partner in medical education: a comparative study of popular science versus research-based curriculum extensions in histology and embryology
Authors: Dejian Chen, Lin Ma, Shengbing Liu, Yaling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study compared the efficacy of Popular Science (PS) and Research-Based (RS) curriculum extensions in a Histology and Embryology course, and explored how artificial intelligence (AI) tools can be designed as differentiated cognitive partners to support distinct pedagogical goals.MethodsTwo parallel first-year clinical medicine classes (N = 118) were non-randomly assigned to PS (n = 60) and RS (n = 58) arms. The PS group completed narrative, clinically connected tasks supported by the Intelligent Quiz Platform (IQP). The RS group undertook inquiry-driven literature analysis supported by the Literature Analysis Assistant (LAA). Outcomes included examination scores, validated questionnaire ratings, AI usage logs, and semi-structured interview themes.ResultsThe RS group was associated with higher comprehensive application (16.8 ± 2.6 vs. 14.2 ± 2.9, p < 0.01) and innovative thinking scores (9.0 ± 1.6 vs. 7.6 ± 1.8, p < 0.01). The PS group reported significantly higher learning interest and satisfaction (p < 0.01). The PS group used IQP more frequently (8.5 ± 2.1 vs. 5.2 ± 1.8, p < 0.001), while the RS group engaged more deeply with LAA (4.8 ± 1.5 vs. 1.2 ± 0.9, p < 0.001). IQP use correlated with basic knowledge (r = 0.42, p < 0.01); LAA use correlated with comprehensive application (r = 0.51, p < 0.001) and innovative thinking (r = 0.46, p < 0.01). Interviews confirmed AI acted as a “tutor” for PS and a “research assistant” for RS.ConclusionPS and RS extensions are associated with complementary competencies. A tiered, AI-differentiated model may support personalized learning to cultivate clinically engaged communicators and critically thinking physician-scientists.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09393-3.

Introduction

Histology and Embryology, a cornerstone of medical education, is often perceived as challenging due to its abstract microscopic content, which can lead to student disengagement and difficulty in clinical integration [1]. The “New Medical Education” paradigm calls for a shift from passive knowledge transmission toward active learning, critical thinking, and early scientific literacy [2]. Curriculum extensions are widely recognized as vital pedagogical strategies to achieve these ends [3].

Two prominent yet pedagogically distinct extension models are the Popular Science‑based (PS) model and the Research‑based (RS) model. The PS model prioritizes narrative accessibility, clinical contextualization, and affective engagement, lowering learning thresholds and linking basic morphology to real‑world clinical scenarios. The RS model centers on scientific inquiry, critical literature analysis, and hypothesis generation, immersing students in authentic research processes to build higher‑order cognitive abilities [4]. Although both models are advocated, comparative empirical evidence on their specific impacts in foundational medical courses remains scarce. Concurrently, the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in education offers unprecedented potential for personalized and adaptive learning [5].

Most existing AI education applications adopt a one‑size‑fits‑all design, with limited attention to pedagogical alignment. A crucial, underexplored question persists: How can AI be strategically designed to differentially scaffold the unique cognitive and affective demands of PS versus RS learning modalities? Compared with traditional teaching tools, AI enables scalable adaptive practice and intelligent research scaffolding that manual methods cannot efficiently provide.

This study, conducted at an application‑oriented university, aimed to: (1) empirically compare the effects of PS and RS extensions on knowledge acquisition, skill development, and student perceptions in a Histology and Embryology course; and (2) investigate the differentiated empowering role of AI tools specifically aligned with each model’s objectives. The findings are intended to inform the design of an intelligent, tiered pedagogical framework that leverages AI not as a uniform tool, but as a versatile cognitive partner for personalized competency development.

Materials and methods

Participants and Ethics

Two intact classes of first-year clinical medicine students (Class A/PS: n = 60; Class B/RS: n = 58) participated. Non‑randomized group assignment by intact class was employed to comply with institutional teaching scheduling arrangements and avoid cross‑group contamination during the intervention. Baseline academic metrics showed no significant differences (p > 0.05), confirming baseline equivalence between groups. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jiaxing University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Instructional design and AI Integration

Both groups received identical core curriculum instruction. The 8-week extension phase differed fundamentally. PS Group: Focused on “Life Mysteries & Clinical Connections”. Tasks involved creating popular science posters/videos explaining complex concepts (e.g., “Epithelial Guardians of the Body”); RS Group: Focused on “Frontiers & Scientific Inquiry.” Tasks involved writing a mini-review on a research frontier (e.g., “Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition in Cancer Metastasis”).

AI tools were not uniformly applied but were deliberately differentiated. For PS Support, Intelligent Quiz Platform (IQP): An adaptive system that provided spaced repetition quizzes, instant feedback, and AI-generated clinical vignettes linked to weekly topics, reinforcing mastery and contextualization. For RS Support, Literature Analysis Assistant (LAA): A tool leveraging NLP to help students summarize papers, visualize conceptual connections within a knowledge graph, and generate potential research questions based on identified gaps, scaffolding the inquiry process.

Both AI tools were researcher‑configured prototypes, not commercial products. The IQP used a spaced‑repetition algorithm (SM‑2 variant) with question banks created by the course team; clinical vignettes were generated via GPT‑4 prompts reviewed by faculty. The LAA employed a BERT‑based summarisation model and a Neo4j knowledge graph. Students accessed both tools through the university’s online learning portal using individual accounts. A typical IQP session consisted of 5–10 multiple‑choice questions, followed by immediate feedback and optional repeats. A typical LAA session involved uploading a PDF, reviewing an auto‑generated summary, exploring the knowledge graph, and optionally using the “Q‑Generation” button to obtain research question suggestions. Usage was not rigidly standardised to allow natural variation, but instructor mediation was limited: a teaching assistant provided only technical support, not content guidance. Theoretical alignment of AI tools

The term “differentiated cognitive partner” indicates that AI tools are purposefully designed to match pedagogical goals and learning theories. The IQP was grounded in behaviorist‑cognitivist principles, emphasizing reinforcement and mastery to consolidate foundational knowledge. The LAA was designed within a constructivist and cognitive‑load framework, supporting inquiry and synthesis to reduce cognitive burden during complex literature processing.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 and NVivo 12. Quantitative data expressed as mean ± SD; t-tests and ANOVA were applied. Qualitative data were coded and triangulated with quantitative results. Significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Academic performance

The RS group (Class B) scored significantly higher on comprehensive application (16.8 ± 2.6 vs. 14.2 ± 2.9, p < 0.01) and innovative thinking (9.0 ± 1.6 vs. 7.6 ± 1.8, p < 0.01) components of the final exam. No significant difference was found in basic knowledge scores (p > 0.05).

Student perceptions and engagement

Questionnaire results (Table 2) revealed a clear dichotomy: the PS group rated items related to stimulated interest and course satisfaction significantly higher (p < 0.01). Conversely, the RS group rated items related to deepened conceptual understanding, self-directed learning, and enhanced scientific thinking significantly higher (p < 0.01). Both groups positively evaluated their respective primary AI tool (IQP for PS, LAA for RS).

Differentiated AI tool engagement

Analysis of backend logs provided compelling evidence of AI as a differentiated partner (Table 3; Fig. 1). The PS group engaged significantly more with the IQP, completing more quizzes and exhibiting a higher rate of repeated practice for mastery. The RS group engaged significantly more deeply with the LAA, analyzing more articles and utilizing its advanced research scaffolding features far more frequently.

Correlation analysis between AI-tool usage frequency and learning outcomes

Pearson correlation analyses (Fig. 2) indicated that in the PS group, IQP‑quiz frequency correlated moderately with basic‑knowledge scores (r = 0.42, p < 0.01), (95% CI [0.19, 0.61]; Cohen’s d = 0.93) but not with innovative‑thinking scores (r = 0.18, p = 0.17). Mechanistically, spaced repetition and adaptive feedback in IQP strengthen memory consolidation, directly supporting foundational knowledge retention. In the RS group, LAA article‑analysis frequency correlated strongly with comprehensive‑application scores (r = 0.51, p < 0.001), (95% CI [0.29, 0.68]; Cohen’s d = 1.19) and moderately with innovative‑thinking scores (r = 0.46, p < 0.01). (95% CI [0.22, 0.65]; Cohen’s d = 1.04). Literature synthesis and hypothesis generation via LAA promote deep cognitive processing, driving improvements in higher‑order skills. Further behavioral log analysis within the RS group revealed that the correlation with innovative thinking was driven predominantly by students who engaged deeply with the LAA’s advanced “Q‑Generation” feature, rather than by surface‑level use, underscoring the importance of usage depth alongside usage frequency.

Response differences by academic proficiency level

Students were stratified into low, medium, and high proficiency levels based on pre‑test quartiles: low (≤ 25th percentile), medium (25th–75th), high (≥ 75th). In the PS group, low n = 14, medium n = 32, high n = 14; in the RS group, low n = 13, medium n = 32, high n = 13. A one‑way ANOVA within the RS group showed a significant effect of proficiency level on innovative thinking scores (F (2,55) = 6.32, p < 0.01, η² = 0.19). Post‑hoc comparisons (Tukey HSD) revealed that high‑proficiency students scored significantly higher than both medium (mean difference = 1.85, p < 0.01) and low (mean difference = 2.42, p < 0.01) students.

Within the PS group, proficiency level significantly affected interest gain scores (F (2,57) = 4.87, p < 0.05, η² = 0.15). Post‑hoc tests showed that low‑proficiency students reported higher interest gains than high‑proficiency students (mean difference = 0.88, p < 0.05). These findings support the proposed tiered model: low‑proficiency students benefit most from the engaging, low‑threshold PS approach, while high‑proficiency students thrive in the inquiry‑driven RS environment. Qualitative insights: the student experience

Thematic analysis of interviews reinforced and explained the quantitative findings. PS students frequently described learning as “fun,” “like storytelling,” and valued the AI-generated clinical cases that “made tissues come alive.”—consistent with quantitative data showing higher interest and satisfaction in the PS group. RS students emphasized the “challenge” and the “sense of being a real researcher,” highlighting how the LAA helped them “navigate dense papers” and “see connections to formulate [their] own questions.”—consistent with superior comprehensive application and innovative thinking scores in the RS group. The perceived role of AI differed: for PS, it was a “tutor” for practice; for RS, it was a “research assistant” for analysis, directly validating the “differentiated cognitive partner” framework.

Discussion

This study provides evidence that PS and RS curriculum extensions serve complementary yet distinct functions in medical education. More importantly, it suggests that the educational value of AI is maximized when its design and functionality are strategically differentiated to align with specific pedagogical paradigms, thereby acting not as a generic aid but as a tailored cognitive partner. This fills a key gap in current AI‑education literature, which mostly focuses on uniform tool application rather than pedagogy‑aligned differentiation [5].

Our findings are consistent with the view that PS extensions excel at affective and foundational engagement. By transforming abstract concepts into narratives, they lower affective filters and enhance perceived relevance, aligning with principles of situated learning [6]. RS extensions, conversely, are powerful tools for developing higher‑order cognitive skills. Their superiority in fostering analytical thinking and knowledge synthesis supports the literature on the benefits of early research exposure for building scientific literacy [7].

The divergent AI‑usage patterns constitute a central contribution. Unlike traditional paper quizzes, mastery‑oriented use of the IQP delivers adaptive practice and instant clinical contextualization, aligning with a behaviorist‑cognitivist model of learning to consolidate foundational knowledge [8]. In stark contrast, unlike manual literature review, the RS group’s analytical, inquiry‑driven use of the LAA reduces cognitive load via automated summarization and knowledge graphing, aligning with a constructivist model to support literature synthesis and hypothesis generation [9]. This bifurcation illustrates that “AI in education” is not monolithic; its impact is contingent on a purposeful pedagogy‑technology alignment.

The significant correlations between AI‑tool usage and specific learning outcomes further underscore this alignment. The mechanistic interpretation that deeper engagement with the LAA’s advanced features drives higher innovative thinking suggests that AI can not only support but also amplify the development of higher‑order competencies when appropriately designed.

We considered several competing interpretations. First, RS tasks are inherently more complex than PS tasks, which alone might explain higher innovation scores. However, the correlation between LAA usage depth and innovation (r = 0.46) suggests AI scaffolding contributed beyond task difficulty. Second, both groups received novel AI tools, so novelty effects, if present, should affect both groups similarly – but the outcomes differed in opposite directions (PS higher in interest, RS higher in innovation), making novelty an unlikely primary driver. Third, total active time on tasks, extracted from logs, was similar between groups (PS: mean 6.2 h/week; RS: 5.9 h/week; p = 0.34), so time‑on‑task is not a confound. Fourth, intact class assignment could introduce unmeasured class‑level differences (e.g., peer culture, group dynamics). Although baseline metrics were equivalent and instructor support was consistent, we acknowledge that this limitation remains. The quasi‑experimental design does not permit definitive causal attribution.

Regarding generalizability, these findings are highly applicable to application‑oriented medical universities with similar foundational medical curricula and student cohorts. Generalization to research‑intensive medical universities, non‑clinical majors, or other basic medical courses (e.g., Anatomy, Physiology) requires further multi‑center validation.

Based on these insights, we propose a “Popular Science Initiation, Research Enhancement, AI‑Differentiated Support” model for foundational medical courses. The core curriculum can integrate PS‑style elements and adaptive AI practice (like the IQP) to ensure universal engagement and competency. Subsequently, optional or advanced RS modules, supported by analytical AI tools (like the LAA), can cater to students with strong inquiry motivation, nurturing future physician‑scientists. This tiered, AI‑informed approach personalizes the learning pathway at scale.

This study has several critical limitations that warrant attention. First, the quasi‑experimental design with intact class assignment precludes definitive causal inference, as unmeasured classroom‑level confounding factors cannot be fully excluded, unlike randomized controlled trials. Second, the single‑institution sample constrains external validity, and results may not directly translate to medical schools with different positioning or student populations. Third, self‑reported questionnaire and interview data may be subject to social desirability bias. Fourth, the 8‑week intervention duration is relatively short, limiting assessment of long‑term impacts on clinical reasoning, research performance, or knowledge retention. Future research should employ randomized controlled designs across multiple institutions, conduct longitudinal tracking, and explore more immersive AI applications such as virtual reality for 3D morphological exploration in PS modules or AI‑powered peer‑review simulators for RS training.

Conclusion

This comparative study demonstrates that Popular Science and Research‑Based curriculum extensions target different but essential axes of medical student development. Crucially, it reveals that AI tools, when differentially designed, can effectively partner with each pedagogical approach—scaffolding mastery for engagement and scaffolding inquiry for innovation. Moving forward, a deliberate, tiered integration of these extensions, powered by pedagogically‑aligned AI, offers a viable blueprint for cultivating a new generation of clinicians who are both knowledgeable communicators and critical scientific thinkers.
