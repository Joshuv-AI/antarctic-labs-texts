1.
Introduction

This paper is a compendium of results about distinguishing a Siegel modular form by its arithmetically interesting Fourier coefficients, especially in the level aspect. Let
A(F,T)
denote the Fourier coefficients of a Siegel modular form on some congruence subgroup (see Section
2
). We say that a Fourier coefficient
A(F,T)
of
F
has ‘property
P
’ if the indexing matrix
T
has the property
P
. For instance, we say that
A(F,T)
is ‘fundamental’ (resp. ‘prime’) if
T
is such that
d(T):=|\mathrm{discriminant}(T)|
is a fundamental discriminant (resp. a prime). We make this convention throughout this paper.

When the property
P
is arithmetic, such results are known to be of significance in the analytic theory of
L
-functions, and also in their arithmetic properties. This connection is the main motivation behind this article. For instance, in one of the first non-trivial results in this direction, Saha
[
Sah13
]
showed the existence of non-zero ‘fundamental’ Fourier coefficients of any non-zero Siegel cusp form of level one – this implied the existence of good Bessel models for
\operatorname{GSp}^{+}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, which has lots of applications to special values of
L
-functions originating in the works of Furusawa.
Similarly, in degree
3
and level one, ‘prime’ Fourier coefficients imply, upon using the integral representation for the spinor
L
-function
Z_{F}(s)
of an eigenform
F
obtained by Pollack
[
Pol17
]
, an unconditional proof of its functional equation. This was proved in
[
BD22
]
, and it was remarked that the argument therein might work for higher levels, “
but we expect more complicated answers (compare
[
Ana24
]
for
n=1
). One may have to take into account the Fourier expansions at all cusps simultaneously, and one may expect new difficulties concerning primes dividing the level
.” Our work (and
[
Was25
]
) shows the cusp at
\infty
is enough.

Apart from its own intrinsic interest (cf. the above remark), one of the main reasons for our interest in this topic is the recent result by Eischen et al
[
ERS24
]
. In that paper, the algebraicity of the special values of the spinor
L
-function
Z_{F}(s)
of a newform
F
on the group
\Gamma^{(3),0}(N)
, of scalar weight
2k\geq 12
(upto periods, in the sense of Deligne’s conjectures), were obtained at the subset
2\leq r\leq k-2
of the set of critical points
\{3-k,\ldots,k-2\}
. This result was conditional (see
[
ERS24
, Condition 1.1.1]
) on the existence of a non-zero Fourier coefficient of
F
corresponding to a maximal order in a quaternion algebra over
\mathbb{Q}
. Prior to this paper, very recently, Washburn
[
Was25
]
made notable progress on this topic, but his results are not enough to settle the above issue. We will see below that the results of this paper make
[
ERS24
]
unconditional when specialized to scalar weights and newforms.

1.1.
Main results

Let us now describe the main results of the paper.

Let
\rho
be a polynomial, not necessarily irreducible, representation of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Denote by
M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
the vector space of holomorphic vector-valued Siegel modular forms on
\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)
with automorphy factor
\rho
(see Section
2
for more details) and ‘determinantal’ weight
k(\rho)
with
k(\rho)-\frac{n-1}{2}\geq 2
(see Section
2
for the definition). We need one more piece of notation. Let
\Lambda_{n}
denote the set of all
n\times n
symmetric, positive semi-definite, half-integral matrices.
For
M\in\Lambda_{n}
, denote by
d(M)
its ‘absolute discriminant’ (i.e., ignoring the usual sign), defined by

(1.1)

d(M):=|\mathrm{disc}(2M)|=\begin{cases}\det(2M)\text{ if }n\text{ is even;}\\
\frac{1}{2}\det(2M)\text{ if }n\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Further, put

(1.2)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{S}}_{F}(X):=\{d\leq X,\,d\,\mbox{\rm{odd, square-free}},\,(d,N)=1\,\mid d(T)=d\,\mbox{\rm{ for some }}T\mbox{\rm{ and }}A(F,T)\neq 0\}.

1.1
.

Let
N>1
be odd integer. Let
F\in M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
be non-zero and
k(\rho)-\frac{n-1}{2}\geq 2
. Suppose that there exists a
T_{0}\in\Lambda_{n}
with
(T_{0}(n,n),N)=1
such that
A(F,T_{0})\neq 0
. Then, the following quantitative result holds: for any given
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\#{\mathfrak{S}}_{F}(X)\gg\begin{cases}X^{1-\epsilon}&\text{ if }n\text{ is odd };\\
X^{5/8-\epsilon}&\text{ if }n\text{ is even }.\end{cases}

where the implied constant depends only on
F
and
\epsilon
.

Qualitatively, the theorem says that for any
F\in M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
with the same condition on the weights as above, there are infinitely many
T\in\Lambda_{n}
with
(d(T),N)=1
such that
A(F,T)\neq 0
as
d(T)\to\infty
along the set of fundamental discriminants.
The assumption
N>1
is not a restriction as the result is known for
N=1
from
[
BD22
]
.

Throughout this paper we take
N>1
.

We remark here that the
T_{0}
in the hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
need not be positive-definite (see Remark
1.3
). But the
T
in the conclusion of the Theorem will be positive-definite by virtue of the condition
(d(T),N)=1
and
N>1
.

We make a few remarks about the non-cuspforms treated in Theorem
1.1
. In
[
Sah13
, Remark 2.6]
, it was asked whether its main result is true for non-cuspforms, and it was indicated that the similarity of the growth properties for Fourier coefficients of Siegel-and Klingen–Eisenstein series makes the situation rather delicate.
Incidentally, such a result in level one was first obtained in
[
BD18
, Proposition 7.7]
, which was generalized to higher degrees in level one in
[
BD22
]
. Whereas in
[
BD18
]
, this was obtained as an application of an asymptotic formula for the Fourier coefficients of non-cusp forms, with our approach, we can settle this issue without working with an explicit description for the Fourier coefficients of Eisenstein series.

For the bigger group
\Gamma_{1,\det}^{(n)}(N)
(see Section
2.1
for definition), we have the following result.

1.2
.

Let
N>1
be an odd integer. The following are equivalent conditions on a non-zero

.

(1)

F
has a non-zero primitive Fourier coefficient.

(2)

F
has a non-zero Fourier coefficient whose discriminant is odd, square-free, and away from

.

(3)

F
has a non-zero Fourier coefficient whose discriminant is odd, square-free.

In (2) and (3) above, we can replace ‘odd, square-free’ with ‘fundamental’.

As other corollaries of Theorem

(see Corollary

for the statements), we get improvements over several previous results in this direction (e.g.,
[

]
,
[

,

, etc). Further, based on Theorem

, we also make the following conjecture for the congruence subgroup

.

hold for

1.2.
Some difficulties in higher degrees and levels

The proof of Theorem
1.1
requires a very careful selection of induction hypotheses and the associated congruence subgroup! Since this point is the crux of the matter in our approach, with degrees
n\geq 3
being the main points of interest, we wish to discuss it in some detail (see also the introduction in
[
Was25
]
).

1.2.1.
Vector-valued setting

First, working in the setup of vector-valued modular forms is indispensable in our approach – this was of course the case in
[
BD22
]
as well, which we follow in many aspects. Even if one wishes
only
a result for scalar-valued modular forms, our induction-based approach makes it necessary to pass through vector-valued objects – hence, starting from vector-valued objects is a necessity. Of course, treatment of these objects is much desirable in their own right, and has applications (see, e.g.,
[
Mar18
]
).

1.2.2.
Usage of the Fourier-Jacobi(FJ) model

A large part of the analytic theory of Siegel modular forms in higher degrees has seen development via the so-called Fourier-Jacobi models. In the present context, it was used in
[
Yam09
]
,
[
Sah12
]
,
[
Sah13
]
and also in
[
BD22
]
. The main idea behind the success of the approach in
[
BD22
]
with respect to the FJ-model – which we follow in this paper as well – is that we work with the so-called (non-zero) ‘primitive’ theta components of suitable FJ-coefficients as opposed to the ‘Eichler-Zagier map’ (say, as used in
[
SS13
]
), which is commonly employed to pass to the half-integral weight setting. However, in general, this map may be identically zero, even in degree
2
and non-square-free levels – at least it is difficult to show that it is not zero in higher degrees and matrix indices. More recently in
[
Was25
]
, the problem was considered for the first time for higher levels in degree at least
3
, and the same issue about the ‘Eichler-Zagier map’ arose. This issue also appeared in
[
AD19
]
, and it requires quite some amount of work to overcome this – by considering character-twisted Eichler-Zagier maps and controlling the levels, or working away from the level.

1.2.3.
Correct induction setup

This is perhaps the most serious point in our approach.
Even while working with the vector-valued setup, in the case of higher levels, especially when the degree
n\geq 2
, there is the issue of using some notion of ‘new-ness’ of the modular form either in the sense of
[
IK12
]
(which we denote by the property of being
c
-new or
c
-old), or assuming that
F
is an eigenfunction of the Hecke operators
U(p)
,
p|N
(cf.
[
SS13
]
), or something else along these lines. Otherwise, old forms provide obvious counterexamples to the desired result about fundamental discriminants. The point being: these properties do not usually pass along to the smaller degrees, which is required to invoke the induction hypothesis.
This issue is inevitable – it was discussed in some detail in
[
Was25
]
, and it was partially overcome by restricting the setup to the case of primitive nebentypus (in which case there is no
c
-oldspace) using the relevant results in
[
IK12
]
. This setup then behaves well with the induction argument.

For instance, if one wants to start with the ‘minimal’ assumption: say that any
F
on
\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N)
with a non-zero ‘primitive’ Fourier coefficient has a fundamental Fourier coefficient, the corresponding induction argument doesn’t seem to work in any straightforward manner! (cf. Remark
7.1
)

In the context of applications, even if one could prove that the Langlands-newforms, say as considered in
[
ERS24
]
, are newforms in the sense of
[
IK12
]
, we see no immediate way to attack the problem just from this information. Usually, the only input one uses about the newforms is that they have a primitive Fourier coefficient, which, as explained in the previous paragraph, is unlikely to give workable inroads.

One faces the same issue if the initial hypothesis was the existence of at least one non-zero Fourier coefficient whose content or the discriminant is away from
N
. In some special cases, one can indeed prove something similar to the above-mentioned statements, see Corollary
7.1
.

In our approach, we need to invoke induction a few times, with varying induction hypotheses. Each induction step facilitates the next. For instance, we start with a mild condition:
F
has a non-zero Fourier coefficient
A(F,T_{0})
whose right lower entry is co-prime to
N
. This, via induction (with the correct congruence subgroups, see Section
1.2.4
), implies the same property for all the diagonals of another index
T_{1}
. We next assume this information as an induction hypothesis and proceed. This is illustrated in Section
1.3
and the flow-chart therein. It seems very difficult for us to argue in any different manner.

1.2.4.
Working with the correct congruence subgroup

Tied to the previous point is the choice of which congruence subgroup of level
N
to start with. The reason is the following.
At various points in the proof, we employ congruences
\operatorname{mod}N
to pass from a condition on a specific Fourier coefficient to the same condition on the full support of the Fourier expansion. This is crucial for us – say while passing onto degrees
n-1
via the Taylor-series based construction
F\mapsto F_{(n-1)}
(Section
2.3
).
Now, if one attempts to proceed by induction from the setup of
M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N))
by our approach, then it doesn’t work as these types of subgroups are not preserved by the congruence procedure. It turns out that the groups
\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)
work well. See section
2.1
for the definition. It should not be confused with another related, bigger congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{1,\det}^{(n)}(N)
, which is not suitable for us to start with.

1.2.5.
Choosing the correct setting of levels

Quite surprisingly, it seems to us that there is not much hope via our method if one starts with, say, odd and square-free levels, as was the case in e.g.,
[
SS13
]
or
[
Was25
]
. In degree
2
(
[
SS13
]
), this was successful; however, the final fundamental discriminants that one obtained may not have been co-prime to the level. In higher degrees (
[
Was25
]
), this was also successful, but the primitive nebentypus of
F
was a somewhat strict condition. For instance, this condition doesn’t render the result in
[
ERS24
]
as unconditional.
For the result in
[
ERS24
]
to become unconditional, one requires this result for the trivial nebentypus (albeit for the groups
\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)
).

There are two reasons why working with a specific type of level or nebentypus is not good for us:

(i) When we take congruences, the level inflates, and the congruence subgroup might change.

(ii) Unless the discriminants involved are co-prime to the level, the central technical machinery of producing ‘primitive’ non-zero theta components of the FJ coefficients breaks down (see Proposition
3.2
).

Therefore, we need to simultaneously start with
arbitrary
odd levels and work with suitable conditions away from the level. We have to pay a price for this, though. The Jacobi forms that arise in this way are on a proper sub-lattice of the form
N\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
of
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
, say when
n=1
. Many of the existing results in the literature have to be adapted to this setting for our method to work.

1.4.
Some applications

We give two applications of our main results. First, when the degree
n
is odd, we can strengthen Theorem
1.1
(as in
[
BD22
]
) to odd and prime discriminants (see Theorem
7.1
). This is achieved by the ideas from
[
BD22
]
.
When this is applied for
n=3
in Corollary
7.2
, we can prove that the special value result in
[
ERS24
]
is unconditional. To do this, we use the close relationship between newforms on
\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)
and
\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N)
, see Lemma
7.2
.

The second application is to the representation numbers of one quadratic form by another. This is possible since we have included non-cusp forms in our setup. We show that any ‘primitive’ lattice of odd level represents infinitely many ‘fundamental’ lattices of smaller orders. See Corollary
7.2
. This result may have uses in the theory of quadratic forms. Certainly this seems not easy to obtain without the use of modular forms.

It would be definitely very interesting to investigate the analogous question for other kind of congruence subgroups of
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
like the paramodular groups, or for the unitary (Hermitian modular) groups or the orthogonal groups
O(2,n)
etc. We expect that the ideas in this paper to carry over, but one may expect some specific obstacles to be overcome.

2.1.

\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)

We use the following congruence subgroups of

Let
\rho
be a polynomial, and not necessarily irreducible representation

where

is finite-dimensional with

. The largest nonnegative integer

is

. We tacitly use the fact that this weight does not decrease if we tensor

with another polynomial representation or restrict it to some

sitting inside
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
as an algebraic subgroup. This follows easily by looking at the entries of

in any matrix realization of

.

Let
\mathbb{H}_{n}
denote the Siegel upper half space of degree

. The symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
acts on

by

; for a polynomial representation
\rho

with values in

we define the stroke operator action on

on

by

(F\mid_{\rho}g)(Z):=\rho(CZ+D)^{-1}F(g\langle Z\rangle).

A Siegel modular form of degree

and automorphy factor
\rho
for the group
\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)

is then a

-valued holomorphic function
F
on
\mathbb{H}_{n}
satisfying

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)

with the standard additional condition of
boundedness at cusps
in degree

.
Any such
F\in M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
has a Fourier expansion given by

(2.10)

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{Z})=\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}A(F,T)e(\tr(T\mathcal{Z})),

where
\Lambda_{n}
(resp.
\Lambda_{n}^{+}
) denotes the set of all

symmetric, positive semi–definite (resp. positive–definite), half–integral matrices. If

is cuspidal, then this summation is supported on
\Lambda_{n}^{+}
.

We denote by
M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
the vector space of all such functions and by

S_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
the subspace of cusp forms; if
\rho=\det^{k}
(that is, scalar valued), we write
as usual
M_{k}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
and
S_{k}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
.

Let

be such that
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}(U^{t})^{-1}&0\\
0&U\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)
. Then we have

. Comparing the Fourier coefficients, we get

(2.11)

\displaystyle A(F,T[U])=\rho(U)A(F,T).

For any

, define the content
\mathbf{c}(T)
as below:

(2.12)

\mathbf{c}(T)=\max\{d\in\mathbb{N}\;|\;d^{-1}T\in\Lambda_{n}\}.

We say that

is primitive if

.

2.2.1.

2.2.2.
The scalar-valued Jacobi forms

The case
\rho=\det^{k}
, i.e., the scalar-valued case, we encounter Jacobi forms of weight
k
, index
S\in\Lambda_{g}
and lattice
\mathcal{L}_{N}:=N\cdot M_{g,n}(\mathbb{Z})\times M_{g,n}(\mathbb{Z})
for the group
\Gamma^{n,g,J}_{1}(N):=\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)\ltimes\mathcal{L}_{N}

A holomorphic function
\phi
on
\mathbb{H}_{n}\times M_{g,n}(\mathbb{C})
is called a Jacobi form of weight
k
, index
S
and lattice
\mathcal{L}_{N}:=N\cdot M_{g,n}(\mathbb{Z})\times M_{g,n}(\mathbb{Z})
for the group
\Gamma^{n,g,J}_{1}(N):=\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)\ltimes\mathcal{L}_{N}
if

(1)

\phi|_{k,S}\gamma=\phi\quad\forall\gamma\in\Gamma^{n,g,J}_{1}(N)
.

(2)

\phi
is bounded at the cusps of
\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)\backslash\mathbb{H}_{n}
(automatic for
n\geq 2
, due to the Köcher principle).

Any such
\phi
as a Fourier expansion given by

(2.14)

\phi(\tau,z)=\underset{4T-S^{-1}[R]\geq 0}{\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}\sum\nolimits_{R\in M_{g,n}(\mathbb{Z})}}C_{\phi}(T,R)e(\tr(T\tau+R^{t}z)).

If the Fourier coefficients survive only for
4T-S^{-1}[R]>0
, then
\phi
is called a Jacobi cusp form.
J_{k,S}(\Gamma_{1}^{n,g,J}(N),\mathcal{L}_{N})
(resp.
J^{cusp}_{k,S}(\Gamma_{1}^{n,g,J}(N),\mathcal{L}_{N})
) denotes the space of Jacobi forms (resp. Jacobi cusp forms) of weight
k
and index
S
on
\mathbb{H}_{n}\times M_{g,n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Let
\phi_{S}\in J_{k,S}(\Gamma_{1}^{n,g,J}(N),\mathcal{L}_{N})
, then for any
\lambda\in M_{g,n}(\mathbb{Z})
we have

(2.15)

\displaystyle\phi_{S}(\tau,z+N\lambda\tau)=e(-\tr(N^{2}S[\lambda]\tau+2Nz^{t}S\lambda))\phi_{S}(\tau,z).

Comparing the Fourier coefficients, we see that

(2.16)

\displaystyle C_{\phi_{S}}(T_{1},r_{1})=C_{\phi_{S}}(T_{2},r_{2})\text{ if }r_{1}\equiv r_{2}\bmod 2NS\text{ and }T_{1}-S^{-1}[r_{1}/2]=T_{2}-S^{-1}[r_{2}/2].

As a consequence, by rearranging the Fourier expansion in (
2.14
), we see that the scalar-valued Jacobi forms
\phi_{S}\in J_{k,S}(\Gamma_{1}^{n,g,J}(N),\mathcal{L}_{N})

\displaystyle\phi_{S}(\tau,z)=\sum\nolimits_{\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{NS}^{g,n}}h_{\mu}(\tau)\cdot\Theta_{NS,\mu}(N\tau,z),

where

and

Here we use
\tilde{\mu}:=(2S)^{-1}\cdot\mu
. The Fourier expansion of

is of shape

(2.18)

and its Fourier coefficients are given by

(2.19)

\mu^{t}/2&S\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right).

provided that
\phi_{S}
is the Fourier-Jacobi coefficient of
some scalar-valued Siegel modular form

will be discussed later.

We have the following transformation properties of

(see
[
Zie89
]

\displaystyle\Theta_{S,\mu}(\tau+1,z)

\displaystyle=e(\tr(S^{-1}[\mu/2]))\Theta_{S,\mu}(\tau,z)

\displaystyle=\det(S)^{-n/2}\det(\tau/i)^{g/2}e(S[z/2]\tau^{-1}))\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{S}^{g,n}}e_{2}(-\tr(\eta^{t}S^{-1}\mu))\Theta_{S,\eta}(\tau,z).

Let
M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. Since

\end{smallmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle\Theta_{S,\mu}(M\tau,z(C\tau+D)^{-1})=\det(C\tau+D)^{g/2}e(\tr(S[z](C\tau+D)^{-1}C))\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{N}_{S}^{g,n}}\varepsilon_{S}(\mu,\nu;M)\Theta_{S,\nu}(\tau,z),

Let

as in (

\end{smallmatrix}\right).

3.1.
General results about Primitive Fourier coefficients

In this section, we state a series of lemmas about the primitive Fourier coefficients of modular forms that will be used later in the proofs. The first of them is a generalisation of
[
Yam09
, Theorem 2]
for vector-valued Siegel modular forms of degree
n
for the group
\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)
.

{lemma}

Let
F\in M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
. If
A(F,T)=0
for all
T\in\Lambda_{n}
with
\mathbf{c}(T)|N
, then
F=0
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one in
[
Yam09
]
. Let
F(\mathcal{Z})=\sum_{S\in\Lambda_{n-1}}\varphi_{S}(\tau,z)e(\tr(SZ))
. Suppose
F
is non-zero, let
S\in\Lambda_{n-1}
be such that the Fourier-Jacobi coefficient
\varphi_{S}\neq 0
. Then from Proposition
3
, we get a non-zero component
\phi_{S}
of
\varphi_{S}
, a scalar-valued Jacobi form of weight
k\geq k(\rho)
for the group
\Gamma^{1,n-1,J}_{1}(N)
. Using the Taylor expansion for
\phi_{S}
around
z=0
, we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\phi_{S}(\tau,z)=\sum\nolimits_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\frac{1}{\lambda!}\left.\frac{\partial^{\lambda}}{\partial z^{\lambda}}\phi_{S}(\tau,z)\right|_{z=0}z^{\lambda}.

\nu_{0}=\min\{\nu(\lambda):\left.\frac{\partial^{\lambda}}{\partial z^{\lambda}}\phi_{S}(\tau,z)\right|_{z=0}\neq 0\}
. For some
\lambda
with
\nu(\lambda)=\nu_{0}
, let
f(\tau):=\left.\frac{\partial^{\lambda}}{\partial z^{\lambda}}\phi_{S}(\tau,z)\right|_{z=0}
. Then
f\neq 0
and since
\phi_{S}\in J_{k,S}(\Gamma^{1,n-1,J}_{1}(N),\mathcal{L}_{N})
, we see that
f\in M_{k+\nu_{0}}(\Gamma_{1}(N))
. The Fourier coefficient of
f
is given by

(3.7)

\displaystyle a_{f}(n)=\frac{1}{\lambda!}\sum\nolimits_{r}A\left(F,\left(\begin{smallmatrix}n&r/2\\
r/2&S\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)(2\pi ir)^{\lambda}.

Now, suppose that
A(F,T)=0
for all
T
with
\mathbf{c}(T)|N
. In particular,
A(F,T)=0
when
\mathbf{c}(T)=1
. Thus from (
3.7
), we see that
a_{f}(n)=0
whenever
(n,(r,\mathbf{c}(S)))=1
. If
((r,\mathbf{c}(S)),N)=1
, we get the required contradiction, since
f=0
in that case (see
[
BD22
, Lemma 4.2]
). Otherwise, we can write (see
[
Lan12
, Theorem 3.1]
)

(3.8)

\displaystyle f(\tau)=\sum_{p|(r,\mathbf{c}(S),N)}g_{p}(p\tau);\quad g_{p}\in M_{k+\nu_{0}}(\Gamma_{1}(Np^{2})).

Thus, we can conclude that the Fourier coefficients of
f
are supported on the divisors of
d:=(r,\mathbf{c}(S),N)
. That is, the Fourier coefficients of
f
are of the form
a_{f}(d^{\prime}n)
for some
d^{\prime}|d
.

Next, we note that
\mathbf{c}\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}d^{\prime}n&r/2\\
r/2&S\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)=(d^{\prime}n,r,\mathbf{c}(S))
and
(d^{\prime}n,r,\mathbf{c}(S))|N
. Since
A(F,T)=0
for all
T
with
\mathbf{c}(T)|N
,
a_{f}(d^{\prime}n)=0
for all
n
and
d^{\prime}|d
. As a consequence, we get that
f=0
, which is a contradiction.
∎

{lemma}

The following are equivalent for any
F\in M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
.

(1)

F
has a non-zero primitive Fourier coefficient.

(2)

There exists a
T\in\Lambda_{n}
with
(\mathbf{c}(T),N)=1
such that
A(F,T)\neq 0
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove
(2)\implies(1)
since
(1)\implies(2)
follows trivially.
Towards this, consider the modular form
G:=\sum_{T\colon(\mathbf{c}(T),N)=1}A(F,T)e(\tr(TZ))
. Then
G\in M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N^{2}))
since
G=\sum_{d|N}\mu(d)F|U_{d}B_{d}
. Further, on assuming
(2)
,
G
is non-zero. Now from Lemma
3.1
, we see that there exists a
T
with
\mathbf{c}(T)|N^{2}
such that
A(G,T)\neq 0
. However, since
G
is supported on
(\mathbf{c}(T),N)=1
and
A(G,T)=A(F,T)
for such
T
, we get the required conclusion.
∎

We will now prove a general version of the Lemma
3.1
. First, let
\mathbf{c}_{r}(T)
denote the content of the lower
r\times r
principal block of
T
. For
r>1
, we say that
T
is
r
-primitive if
\mathbf{c}_{r}(T)=1
. When
r=1
, we say
T
is
r
-primitive if there exists a prime
p
with
(p,N)=1
such that
\mathbf{c}_{1}(T)=p
. Then we have the following.

{proposition}

Let
1\leq r\leq n
. Then the following are equivalent for any
F\in M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
.

(1)

There exists an
r
-primitive
T
such that
A(F,T)\neq 0
.

(2)

There exists a
T\in\Lambda_{n}
with
(\mathbf{c}_{r}(T),N)=1
such that
A(F,T)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Clearly
(1)\implies(2)
. Also note that
r=n
case follows from Lemma
3.1
. Thus, it is enough to prove
(2)\implies(1)
for
r<n
. Let
T
be such that
(\mathbf{c}_{r}(T),N)=1
and
A(F,T)\neq 0
. Then consider
F_{T}
. Then
F_{T}
is non-zero and supported on
(\mathbf{c}_{r}(T),N)=1
.

First, consider the case
r>1
. Consider the degree
r
form
(F_{T})_{(r)}
(see Section
2.3
). From Proposition
2.3
,
(F_{T})_{(r)}\neq 0
and
(F_{T})_{(r)}\in M_{\rho^{\prime}}(\Gamma^{(r)}(N)
,
for some representation
\rho^{\prime}
of
\operatorname{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
. Thus, there exists
S\in\Lambda_{r}
such that
A((F_{T})_{(r)},S)\neq 0
. Further note that
(\mathbf{c}(S),N)=(\mathbf{c}_{r}(T),N)=1
. Thus from Lemma
3.1
, we get a primitive
S_{0}\in\Lambda_{r}
with such that
A((F_{T})_{(r)},S_{0})\neq 0
. But we have that

(3.9)

\displaystyle 0\neq A((F_{T})_{(r)},S_{0})=\sum_{\lambda:\nu(\lambda)=\nu_{0}}\left.\frac{\partial^{\lambda}}{\partial z^{\lambda}}\varphi_{S_{0}}(\tau,z)\right|_{z=0}.

Now we can "lift"
S_{0}
to get a
T_{0}\in\Lambda_{n}
such that
A(F_{T},T_{0})\neq 0
. This completes the proof for
r>1
case since
\mathbf{c}_{r}(T_{0})=\mathbf{c}(S_{0})=1
.

When
r=1
, we have to take a different approach since Lemma
3.1
doesn’t give a prime Fourier coefficient. However, note that
(F_{T})_{(1)}
is a degree
1
modular form with a non-zero Fourier coefficient away from the level. Thus, we can use
[
BD22
, Lemma 5.2]
to arrive at our conclusion. Note that the Lemma (loc. cit.) is essentially based on the multiplicity-one on
\mathrm{GL}_{2}
.
∎

{lemma}

Let
F\in M_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
and
T_{0}\in\Lambda_{n}
be as in the hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
. Then
F_{T_{0}}\neq 0
and there exists a
T_{1}\in\Lambda_{n}^{+}
with
(T_{1}(n,n),N)=1
such that
A(F_{T_{0}},T_{1})\neq 0
.

Proof.

The condition on weight,
k(\rho)-\frac{n-1}{2}\geq 2
, excludes the existence of ‘singular’ forms (see
[
Wei83
]
). Since the weight does not decrease upon the construction of
F_{T_{0}}
(see Lemma
3
),
F_{T_{0}}
is non-singular and hence there exists a
T_{1}\in\Lambda^{+}_{n}
such that
A(F_{T_{0}},T_{1})\neq 0
. Further, since
T_{1}\equiv T_{0}\bmod N
, we get that
(T_{1}(n,n),N)=(T_{0}(n,n),N)=1
from Lemma
3
.
∎

{remark}

Lemma
3.1
ensures that the modular forms defined using the congruence condition w.r.t
T_{0}
(see Lemma
3
) are not supported on
\Lambda_{n}\setminus\Lambda^{+}_{n}
. This is crucial since
T
in the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
are in
\Lambda^{+}_{n}
. Of course, there is no issue with cusp forms.

3.2.
Theta components of Jacobi forms

For any
T\in\Lambda_{n}
, we have the following non-vanishing criteria for the Fourier coefficients of
H_{\mu}(\tau):=h_{\mu}(d_{T}\tau)
, where
d_{T}
is as in (
2.23
).

{lemma}

Let
\phi\in J_{k,T}(\Gamma_{1}^{1,n,J}(N),\mathcal{L}_{N})
and
h_{\mu}
be a
T
-primitive theta component. Then
H_{\mu}
has all non-zero Fourier coefficients away from
d_{T}
.

Proof.

As
\mu
is
T
-primitive, we have the property that
\displaystyle(2T)^{-1}[\mu]=\frac{\alpha}{d_{T}}
with
(\alpha,d_{T})=1
. Now from the Fourier expansion of
h_{\mu}
in (
2.18
), we see that if
\mu
is
T
-primitive, then its Fourier expansion can be written as

(3.10)

\displaystyle H_{\mu}(\tau)=\sum\nolimits_{n^{\prime}\geq 1,}c_{\phi}(\frac{n^{\prime}+\alpha}{d_{T}},\mu)e(n^{\prime}\tau).

Since
c_{\phi}(n/d_{T},\mu)=0
when
d_{T}\nmid n
and
(\alpha,d_{T})=1
, we see that the Fourier expansion of
H_{\mu}
is supported away from
d_{T}
. Note that we have arrived at (
3.10
) from (
2.18
) by rewriting
n-(2T)^{-1}[\mu]=\frac{n^{\prime}}{d_{T}}
. This completes the proof.
∎

For
M\in\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
,
S\in\Lambda_{g}
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{N}_{S}^{g,n}
, let
\varepsilon_{S}(\mu,\nu;M)\in\mathbb{C}
be as in (
2.22
). We have the following relation for
\varepsilon_{S}(\mu,\nu;M)
(see also
[
Mar25
, (18)]
,
[
ST23
, (13)]
).

{lemma}

Let
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, then there exists a constant
\omega(M_{1},M_{2})\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\omega(M_{1},M_{2})|=1
such that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{S}(\mu,\nu;M_{1}M_{2})=\omega(M_{1},M_{2})\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{S}^{g,n}}\varepsilon_{S}(\mu,\eta;M_{1})\varepsilon_{S}(\eta,\nu;M_{2}).

Proof.

For
M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\tau\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
, let
j(M;\tau)=\det(C\tau+D)
. Then using (
2.22
) for
M_{1}M_{2}
,
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
in sequence at
z=0
, we get

(3.12)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{S}(\mu,\nu;M_{1}M_{2})=\frac{j(M_{1};M_{2}\tau)^{g/2}j(M_{2};\tau)^{g/2}}{j(M_{1}M_{2};\tau)^{g/2}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{S}^{g,n}}\varepsilon_{S}(\mu,\eta;M_{1})\varepsilon_{S}(\eta,\nu;M_{2}).

Using the co-cycle condition, we see that the ratio at the front is independent of
\tau
and is of absolute value
1
(see
[
Fre06
, Section 3]
). This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

{definition}

Let
N\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a=(a_{1},...,a_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. We say that
N|a
iff
N|a_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq r
.

{lemma}

Let
n,k,N
be positive integers and
N
be odd. For an odd prime
p
with
(p,N)=1
, let
\phi\in J_{k,p}(\Gamma_{1}^{n,1,J}(N),\mathcal{L}_{N})
be non-zero. Then the theta components
h_{N\mu}
are non-zero for any
\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{p}^{1,n}
, where
\mathcal{N}_{p}^{1,n}
is as in (
2.17
).

Proof.

The proof uses similar arguments as in
[
ST23
, Proposition 2]
with certain modifications, as the set-up for us is somewhat different. We write it here for clarity and because this result is crucial for our purpose. Using the transformation properties of
\phi
w.r.t
\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)
and the transformation properties of theta series from the theta decomposition of
\phi
as in (
2.17
), for any
M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)
we can write

(3.13)

\displaystyle\det(CZ+D)^{-k+1/2}\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{N}_{Np}^{1,n}}\varepsilon_{p}(\mu,\nu;M)h_{\nu}(MZ)=h_{\mu}(Z),

where
\det(CZ+D)^{1/2}
is defined using the principal branch of
\log
and
\varepsilon_{p}(\mu,\nu;M)\in\mathbb{C}
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1_{n}\\
-1_{n}&0\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\mathcal{T}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&1_{n}\\
0&1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. We consider
M=\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}^{\ell}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}^{m}\mathcal{S}
with
\ell,m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
M\in\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)
. That is,
\ell\equiv m\equiv 1\bmod N
. For such an
M
, we claim that
\varepsilon_{p}(N\mu,\nu;M)=0
if
N\nmid\nu
.

To see this, using the transformation properties of theta series from (
2.20
) and (
3.11
) (we can assume
w(M_{1},M_{2})
in (
3.11
) to be
1
by choosing
\tau
appropriately as in
[
ST23
]
), we can write

(3.14)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}(N\mu,\nu;M)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(4N^{2}p^{2})^{n}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{N}_{Np}^{1,n}}e\left(-\frac{\alpha\mu^{t}}{2p}+\frac{\ell\alpha\alpha^{t}}{4Np}-\frac{\beta\alpha^{t}}{2Np}+\frac{m\beta\beta^{t}}{4Np}-\frac{\nu\beta^{t}}{2Np}\right)

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(4N^{2}p^{2})^{n}}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{N}_{Np}^{1,n}}e_{4Np}\left(\ell\alpha\alpha^{t}-2N\mu\alpha^{t}\right)\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{N}_{Np}^{1,n}}e_{4Np}\left(m\beta\beta^{t}-2(\alpha+\nu)\beta^{t}\right).

Let
\mu=(\mu_{i})
,
\nu=(\nu_{i})
,
\alpha=(\alpha_{i})
and
\beta=(\beta_{i})
. Then

(3.16)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}(N\mu,\nu;M)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(4N^{2}p^{2})^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha_{i}\bmod 2Np}e_{4Np}(\ell\alpha_{i}^{2}-2N\alpha_{i}\mu_{i})\sum_{\beta_{i}\bmod 2Np}e_{4Np}(m\beta_{i}^{2}-2(\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i})\beta_{i})

(3.17)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(4Np)^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha_{i}\bmod 2Np}e_{4Np}(\ell\alpha_{i}^{2}-2N\alpha_{i}\mu_{i})G(m,-2(\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i}),4Np),

where
G(a,b,c)
denotes the generalized quadratic Gauss sum. We choose
m=4m_{1}
and
(Np,m_{1})=1
. Then noting that
G(a,b,c)=0
if
(a,c)\nmid b
, the Gauss sum
G(4m_{1},-2(\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i}),4Np)
survives only when
2|(\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i})
and in that case we have
G(4m_{1},-2(\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i}),4Np)=4G(m_{1},-(\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i})/2,Np)=4\epsilon_{4p}\sqrt{Np}(\frac{m_{1}}{Np})e_{4Np}(-\overline{4m_{1}}(\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i})^{2})
. Here
4m_{1}(\overline{4m_{1}})\equiv 1\bmod Np
and
\epsilon_{d}=1
or
i
depending on
d\equiv 1
or
3

\operatorname{mod}4
, respectively. Thus we get

(3.18)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}(N\mu,\nu;M)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(Np)^{n/2}}\left(\epsilon_{4p}\Big(\frac{m_{1}}{Np}\Big)\right)^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\underset{\alpha_{i}\equiv-\nu_{i}\bmod 2}{\sum_{\alpha_{i}\bmod 2Np}}e_{4Np}(\ell\alpha_{i}^{2}-2N\alpha_{i}\mu_{i}-\overline{4m_{1}}(\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i})^{2})

(3.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(Np)^{n/2}}\left(\epsilon_{4p}\Big(\frac{m_{1}}{Np}\Big)\right)^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha_{i}\bmod Np}e_{4Np}(\ell(2\alpha_{i}-\nu_{i})^{2}-2N(2\alpha_{i}-\nu_{i})\mu_{i}-\overline{4m_{1}}4\alpha_{i}^{2})

(3.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(Np)^{n/2}}\left(\epsilon_{4p}\Big(\frac{m_{1}}{Np}\Big)\right)^{n}e_{2p}(\mu\nu^{t})e_{4Np}(\ell\nu\nu^{t})\prod_{i=1}^{n}G(\ell-\overline{m},(\ell\nu_{i}+N\mu_{i}),Np).

Recall that
N\nmid\nu
. Therefore,
N\nmid\nu_{i}
for some
i
. Since
(N,\ell)=1
,
N\nmid(\ell\nu_{i}+N\mu_{i})
. But by our choice
\ell\equiv m\equiv 1\bmod N
,
N|\ell-\overline{m}
. Thus
G(\ell-\overline{m},(\ell\nu_{i}+N\mu_{i}),Np)=0
and we have our claim.

Coming back to the proof of the lemma, suppose
h_{N\mu}=0
for some
\mu\in\mathcal{N}_{p}^{1,n}
, then from the previous calculations, we get

(3.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{N}^{1,n}_{p}}\varepsilon_{p}(N\mu,N\nu;M)h_{N\nu}(Z)=0.

That is,

(3.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}\left(\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{N}^{1,n}_{p}}\varepsilon_{p}(N\mu,N\nu;M)c_{\phi}\left(\frac{T+N^{2}\nu^{t}\nu}{4p},N\nu\right)\right)e(T\tau/4p)=0.

We also have that
c_{\phi}(T_{1},r_{1})=c_{\phi}(T_{2},r_{2})
if
r_{1}\equiv r_{2}\bmod 2Np
and
4pT_{1}-r_{1}^{t}r_{1}=4pT_{2}-r_{2}^{t}r_{2}
. Therefore, as in
[
ST23
]
it is enough to find
\ell,m
such that
M\in\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)
and

(3.23)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}(N\mu,N\nu;M)

\displaystyle\neq 0\text{ if all entries of }\nu\text{ are }p\text{ or }2p;

(3.24)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}(N\mu,N\nu;M)+\varepsilon_{p}(N\mu,-N\nu;M)

\displaystyle\neq 0\text{ otherwise}.

Note that
\varepsilon_{p}(N\mu,N\nu;M)
is the same equation as in
[
ST23
, eq. (15)]
. Now the proof follows from the arguments in
[
ST23
, Proposition 2]
by choosing
\ell,m
such that
p\nmid(\ell-1)m
,

,

. Note that these choices of

are consistent with the earlier choices of the same. This completes the proof, since

The next proposition assures the existence of non-zero ‘primitive’ theta components of a
\phi\in J_{k,T}(\Gamma_{1}^{1,g,J}(N),\mathcal{L}_{N})
. The notion of ‘primitive’ vectors for a matrix index
M\in\Lambda_{g}^{+}
is recalled in Definition
2.2.2
. This result is comparable to the results in
[
BD22
, Proposition 3.5]
and
[
Ana24
, Theorem 1]
, although here we are dealing with Jacobi forms on a different subgroup and a smaller lattice. This result forms the link between the discriminants of matrices indexing the Fourier coefficients in degree
n-1
and
n
, respectively, under the induction procedure. Here we remind the reader that the conditions
(N,d(T))=1
and
d(T)
odd and square-free are necessary to arrive at the forthcoming conclusions.

Towards this, we first define a generalized quadratic Gauss sum of degree
g
. Let
M\in\Lambda^{+}_{g}
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then define the generalized Gauss-sum associated to
a,M
by

(3.25)

\displaystyle G(a,M):=\frac{1}{\det(2M)}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{N}_{M}^{g,1}}e\left(aM^{-1}[\eta/2]\right).

We have the following lemma evaluating
G(a,M)
from which we only need the non-vanishing property.
This lemma can be deduced from the results in
[
Sta82
,
Sty84
]
by putting together various assertions, but for completeness and the reader’s convenience, we include a short proof.

{lemma}

Let
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
M\in\Lambda^{+}_{g}
be such that
(d(M),a)=1
. Then
G(a,M)\neq 0
. Moreover,

(3.26)

\displaystyle G(a,M)=(1+i^{a})^{g}d(M)^{-1/2}\left(\frac{d(M)}{a}\right),

where
\left(\tfrac{\cdot}{a}\right)
denotes the Kronecker’s symbol.

Proof.

First note that if
2M=\mathrm{diag}(m_{1},....,m_{g})
, then we have

(3.27)

\displaystyle G(a,M)=\frac{1}{d(M)}\prod_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{\eta\in\mathbb{Z}/2m_{j}\mathbb{Z}}e\left(a\eta^{2}/4m_{j}\right)=\frac{1}{d(M)}\prod_{j=1}^{g}\left(\frac{m_{j}}{a}\right)(1+i^{a})m_{j}^{1/2}.

The last equality follows from
[
BEW98
, Theorem 1.5.4]
and is clearly non-zero. Thus, we get
G(a,M)\neq 0
in this case.

For the general case, we consider the Smith normal form of
2M
: thus, we get
U,V\in\mathrm{GL}_{g}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
2M=UAV
with
A
being diagonal. Now
\displaystyle\eta\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}/2M\mathbb{Z}^{g}=\mathbb{Z}^{g}/UAV\mathbb{Z}^{g}=\mathbb{Z}^{g}/UA\mathbb{Z}^{g}
.

In (
3.25
), we make a change of variable defined by
\eta=U\cdot\beta
. Then
\eta_{1}\equiv\eta_{2}\operatorname{mod}{UA\mathbb{Z}^{g}}\iff\beta_{1}\equiv\beta_{2}\operatorname{mod}{A\mathbb{Z}^{g}}
.
Thus, as
\eta
varies over
\mathbb{Z}^{g}/UA\mathbb{Z}^{g}
, the variable
\beta
varies over
\mathbb{Z}^{g}/A\mathbb{Z}^{g}
. Thus, we get
G(a,M)=G(a,A)\neq 0
, which gives the lemma.
∎

Before we proceed, we obtain a suitable set of coset representatives for
\mathcal{N}_{NT}^{g,1}
when

5.
Proof of Proposition

.

We will give two proofs of Proposition

, each having its own advantages and interest. The first proof is essentially reliant on multiplicity-one on

and the second – on representation of primes by unimodular matrices with congruence conditions. We begin with some preparatory results related to the second method.

5.1.
Representation of primes by quadratic forms

We need a lemma about the representation of primes by elements of subgroups of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
defined by congruence conditions. This is a very difficult problem when approached through algebraic number theory (see e.g.,
[
Cho10
]
). Successful answers seem rare, but they do provide precise answers when they work. However, analytic methods, like the circle method, sieves, etc., are well-equipped to give quantitative answers, as the lemma given below shows. It can be deduced either from
[
Ple66
]
, which uses the circle method or from
[
Iwa74
]
, which uses sieves.
As far as we are aware, the lemma given below is not available in the literature and may be of independent interest.

First, we recall a result concerning quadratic polynomials from
[
Ple66
]
.

{lemma}
[
Ple66
]

Let
\phi(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},...,x_{n}]
be a quadratic polynomial which takes integer values for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. If
\phi
is such that GCD of the coefficients of
\phi
in their lowest terms is
1
and
\phi(\mathbf{x})\not\equiv 0\bmod 2
for some
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, there exists
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that
(\phi(\mathbf{y}),m)=1
.

{lemma}

Let
N
be an integer and
T\in\Lambda_{n}
be such that the lower
2\times 2
block is primitive with
(T(n,n),N)=1
. Then there exists a
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with
U\equiv 1_{n}\bmod N
such that
T[U]=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}*&*\\
*&p\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
for infinitely many primes
p
with
(p,N)=1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(0,..,0,y)
and
X=(N\mathbf{y},Nx+1)^{t}
denote the
n
-th column of a
U\equiv 1_{n}\bmod N
. We write
T=(t_{ij})
and
t_{ii}=t_{i}
instead of using
T(i,j)
for ease of notation. Then the
(n,n)
entry of
T[U]
is given by the polynomial
P(\mathbf{y},x):=X^{t}TX
. A simple calculation shows that

(5.1)

\displaystyle P(y,x)=

\displaystyle=N^{2}y^{2}t_{n-1}+(Nx+1)Nyt_{n(n-1)}+(Nx+1)^{2}t_{n}

(5.2)

\displaystyle=N^{2}y^{2}t_{n-1}+N^{2}xyt_{n(n-1)}+N^{2}x^{2}t_{n}+Nyt_{n(n-1)}+2Nxt_{n}+t_{n}.

We aim to use
[
Iwa74
]
to show that

represents infinitely many primes. To that end, we need to show the following.

(1)

P(y,x)
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[y,x]
.

(2)

P(y,x)
depends essentially on two variables.

(3)

Given any integer

assumes infinitely many integers co-prime to
m
.

Proof of (1):
If

(5.3)

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{Nc_{1}}{2t_{n}}\left(t_{n(n-1)}\pm\sqrt{t_{n(n-1)}^{2}-4t_{n}t_{n-1}}\right).

We have

\displaystyle A(Nyt_{n(n-1)}+2Nxt_{n}+2t_{n})+B(2Nyt_{n-1}+Nxt_{n(n-1)}+t_{n(n-1)})=0

(5.5)

\displaystyle At_{n(n-1)}+2Bt_{n-1}=0\text{ and }2At_{n}+Bt_{n(n-1)}=0\implies B(t_{n(n-1)}^{2}-4t_{n}t_{n-1})=0.

in the support of the Fourier expansion of
F_{(n-1)}
may be imprimitive. At least there seems to be no immediate way to rule this out.

7.2.
Prime discriminants and application to special values of
L
-functions

Let
\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)
be the group defined by

(7.3)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(n),0}(N):=\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}):B\equiv 0\bmod N\}.

For any integer
k
, the space of modular forms of weight
k
for the group
\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)
is defined analogously as in Section
2.1
and we denote it by
M_{k}(\Gamma^{(n),0}(N))
. For
p|N
, let
U^{0}(p)
be the operator on
M_{k}(\Gamma^{(n),0}(N))
given by

(7.4)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{T}A(F,T)e(\frac{\tr(TZ)}{N})\mapsto\sum\nolimits_{T}A(F,pT)e(\frac{\tr(TZ)}{N}).

Then we have that

(7.5)

\displaystyle F|U^{0}(p)=p^{-n(n+1)/2}\sum_{S=S^{t},S\bmod p}F|\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&NS\\
0_{n}&p\cdot 1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right).

The above definition comes from the Hecke algebra. To see this for the group in question, note that
\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)\cap\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&p_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)^{-1}\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&p_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\Gamma^{(n),0}(Np)
and
\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)=\cup_{S=S^{t}\bmod p}\Gamma^{(n),0}(Np)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&NS\\
0_{n}&1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Thus
\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&p\cdot 1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)=\cup_{S=S^{t}\bmod p}\Gamma^{(n),0}(Np)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&NS\\
0_{n}&p\cdot 1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
As a consequence, we get

(7.6)

\displaystyle p^{-n(n+1)/2}F|\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&p\cdot 1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)=p^{-n(n+1)/2}\sum_{S=S^{t},S\bmod p}F|\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&NS\\
0_{n}&p\cdot 1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)=F|U^{0}(p).

Let
\mathcal{M}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}N\cdot 1_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and consider
G(Z)=F(NZ)=F|\mathcal{M}
. Since
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}N\cdot 1_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)^{-1}\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}N\cdot 1_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N)
, it is easy to see that
G\in M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N))
.

{lemma}

Let
p|N
and
F\in M_{k}(\Gamma^{(n),0}(N))
be an eigenfunction of
U^{0}(p)
. Then
G=F|\mathcal{M}
is an eigenfunction of
U(p)
.

Proof.

The proof is by computation. Suppose the eigenvalue of
F
under
U^{0}(p)
is
\lambda_{p}
. We have

(7.7)

\displaystyle G|U(p)

\displaystyle=p^{-n(n+1)/2}\sum\nolimits_{S}F|\left(\begin{smallmatrix}N\cdot 1_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)|\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&S\\
0_{n}&p\cdot 1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)

(7.8)

\displaystyle=p^{-n(n+1)/2}\sum\nolimits_{S}F|\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&NS\\
0_{n}&p\cdot 1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)|\left(\begin{smallmatrix}N\cdot 1_{n}&0_{n}\\
0_{n}&1_{n}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)

(7.9)

\displaystyle=F|U^{0}(p)|\mathcal{M}

(7.10)

\displaystyle=\lambda(p)G.\qed

7.1
.

Let
n
be odd. Let
F\in S_{\rho}(\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N))
be non-zero and
k(\rho)-\frac{n-1}{2}\geq 2
. Suppose that there exists a
T_{0}\in\Lambda_{n}
with
(T_{0}(n,n),N)=1
such that
A(F,T_{0})\neq 0
. Then there exist
T\in\Lambda_{n}^{+}
with
d(T)
assuming infinitely many odd prime values, such that
A(F,T)\neq 0
. Moreover, the following stronger quantitative result holds: for any given
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\#\left({\mathfrak{S}}_{F}(X)\cap\mathbf{P}\right)\gg X/\log X,

where the implied constant depends only on
F
and
\epsilon
.

Proof.

For
F
as in the statement, from Theorem
1.1
, we get a
T_{1}
with
d(T_{1})
odd, square-free and
(d(T_{1}),N)=1
such that
A(F,T_{1})\neq 0
. Now consider
F_{T_{1}}
from Lemma
3
. Again using Theorem
1.1
, we get a non-zero scalar-valued Jacobi form
\varphi_{S_{0}}
of weight
k^{\prime}\geq k(\rho)
from the Fourier-Jacobi expansion of
F_{T_{1}}
with
d(S_{0})
odd, square-free and
(d(S_{0}),N)=1
. Now, from Proposition
3.2
, we get a non-zero theta component
h_{\mu}
of
\varphi_{S_{0}}
, where
\mu
is either
N^{2}S_{0}
or
S_{0}
-primitive. As in the proof of Theorem
1.3
, define
H_{\mu}(\tau)=h_{\mu}((d_{S_{0}})\tau)
. Then
H_{\mu}\in S_{k^{\prime}-\frac{n-1}{2}}(\Gamma_{1}^{(1)}(d_{S_{0}}^{2}N^{2}))
. Further, note that
H_{\mu}
has all non-zero Fourier coefficients away from
d_{S_{0}}N
(see Section
6.1
for details).

The rest of the proof now follows as in
[
BD22
, Theorem 5.1]
and will not be repeated here.
∎

{proposition}

Let
n
be odd and
F\in M_{k}(\Gamma^{(n),0}(N))
be an eigenfunction of
U^{0}(p)
for all
p|N
. Then there exist infinitely many
T
with odd and prime discriminant such that
A(F,T)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let
G=F|\mathcal{M}
be as above. Then, the Fourier expansion of
G
is given by

(7.11)

\displaystyle G(Z)=\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{n}^{+}}A(F,T)e(\tr(TZ)).

Next, since
F
is an eigenfunction
U^{0}(p)
, from Lemma
7.2
we get that
G
is also an eigenfunction of
U(p)
for all
p|N
. Now Corollary
7.1
shows that
G
has a non-zero primitive Fourier coefficient, and thus using the
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivalence of Fourier coefficients of
G
, there exists a
T_{0}
with
(T_{0}(n,n),N)=1
such that
A(G,T_{0})\neq 0
.

Now invoking Theorem
7.1
, we see that there exist
T\in\Lambda_{n}^{+}
with
d(T)
assuming infinitely many odd prime values, such that
A(G,T)\neq 0
. Since
A(G,T)=A(F,T)
, we see that the same conclusion holds for
F
. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

{corollary}

The statement of the special-value result stated in
[
ERS24
, Theorem 1.1.2]
is unconditional, i.e.,
[
ERS24
, Condition 1.1.1]
is true.

Proof.

In
[
ERS24
]
, the authors consider a newform for the group
\Gamma^{(3),0}(N)
. Such an
F
has a Fourier expansion of the form

(7.12)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{3}^{+}}A(F,T)e(\frac{TZ}{N}).

It follows from the discussion in
[
BD22
, Section 5.1]
and
[
ERS24
, Condition 1.1.1]
that the special-value result in
[
ERS24
]
holds whenever
F
has a non-zero Fourier coefficient
T
with odd, prime discriminant. Indeed, in this case, via the Clifford correspondence (see e.g.,
[
Pol17
, Proposition 3.3]
),
T
corresponds to a maximal order in a quaternion algebra over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Since such an
F
is an eigenfunction of
U^{0}(p)
for all
p|N
, the existence of such a
T
follows from Proposition
7.2
, and this gives us the corollary.
∎

{remark}

It is quite plausible that
F
and
G
in the above proof have the same Satake parameters and hence the same spinor
L
-function. Then the algebraicity result should also hold verbatim for
G
.

7.3.
Representation numbers, theta series

One frequently used application of a result like Lemma
5.1
to modular forms is to show the existence of a prime
p
such that
A(F,T)
has the property that
T(n,n)=p
.
For the group
\Gamma^{(n)}_{0}(N)
, the Lemma
5.1
can be proved much more elementarily, see
[
Mar25
]
or can be deduced at once from
[
Ple66
]
or
[
Iwa74
]
, as the entire unimodular group can be embedded in it. In
[
Mar25
]
, it was shown more generally that a primitive half-integral matrix
T\in\Lambda_{n}^{+}
represents another primitive matrix
S
of lower dimension. We can show the following generalization, which may have some implication in the theory of quadratic forms.

Suppose
T_{0}\in\Lambda_{n}^{+}
and
1\leq r\leq n
. Define the theta series
\theta_{T_{0}}
associated to
T_{0}
by

(7.13)

\displaystyle\theta_{T_{0}}(Z)=\sum_{X\in M_{n,r}(\mathbb{Z})}e(\tr T_{0}[X]\cdot Z)=\sum_{S\in\Lambda_{r}}r(T_{0};S)e(\tr SZ)\quad(Z\in\mathbb{H}_{r}),

where
\displaystyle r(T_{0};S)=\{X\in M_{n,r}(\mathbb{Z})\mid T_{0}[X]=S\}

denotes the number of integral representations of
S
by
T_{0}
. Further put

(7.14)

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{level}(T_{0}):=\min\{M\geq 1\mid MT_{0}^{-1}\in\Lambda_{n}^{+}\}\quad\text{ and }

(7.15)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{T_{0}}(X)

\displaystyle:=\{d\leq X,\,d\,\mbox{\rm{odd, square-free}},\,(d,N)=1\,\mid d(S)=d\,\mbox{\rm{ for some }}S\in\Lambda_{r}^{+}\mbox{\rm{ and }}r(T_{0},S)\neq 0\}.

7.2
.

With the above notation, for any primitive
T_{0}
such that
\mathrm{level}(T_{0})
is odd, and
n
even,
n\geq r+3
; one has for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{T_{0}}(X)\gg_{T_{0},\epsilon}\begin{cases}X^{1-\epsilon}&\text{ if }r\text{ is odd };\\
X^{5/8-\epsilon}&\text{ if }r\text{ is even }.\end{cases}

Proof.

The proof follows by combining Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. First, it is well-known that
\theta_{T_{0}}\in M_{n/2}(\Gamma_{0}^{(r)}(N),\chi_{Q})
, where
Q
is the quadratic form defined by
T_{0}
, and
\chi_{Q}
being the quadratic character attached to
Q
. Clearly,
\theta_{T_{0}}\in M_{n/2}(\Gamma_{1,\det}^{(r)}(N))
.

Second, from
[
Mar25
]
, we get that
A(T_{0};S_{0})\neq 0
for some primitive
S_{0}\in\Lambda_{n}^{+}
. Then from the proof of
(1)\implies(2)
of Theorem
1.2
, we see that the hypothesis
(S_{0}(r,r),N)=1
of Theorem
1.1
is satisfied. The weight condition is easily checked. Since
{\mathcal{S}}_{T_{0}}(X)={\mathfrak{S}}_{\theta_{T_{0}}}(X)
, the theorem is proved.
∎

Note that for any
S
represented by
T_{0}
as in the Theorem, the vector
X
representing it is necessarily ‘primitive’ in the sense that it can be completed to an unimodular matrix. See also
[
Mar05
]
on this, for example. We remark that Theorem
7.2
may not be easy to obtain without the use of modular forms.
