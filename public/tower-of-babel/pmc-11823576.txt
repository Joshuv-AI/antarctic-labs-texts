Comparative evaluation of antibacterial efficacy of N-acetylcysteine, Aegle marmelos, and chitosan as intracanal medicaments against Enterococcus faecalis biofilm – An in vitro study
Authors: Kannan Subiksha, Amit Jena, Priyanka Sarangi, Saumyakanta Mohanty, Sajan Sahoo, Rashmi Rekha Mallick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Context:The main objective of root canal treatment is the removal of bacteria. Established medicaments and their combinations have been compromised in efficacy against Enterococcus faecalis, causing the need to explore novel intracanal medicaments.Aim:The aim of the study was to evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of chitosan, N-acetylcysteine (NAC), and Aegle marmelos as intracanal medicaments against E. faecalis biofilm.Materials and Methodology:Minimum inhibitory concentration and susceptibility of medicaments determined. Two hundred and forty dentin disc specimens were prepared and inoculated with E. faecalis for 21 days. Samples were divided into four groups (A – N-acetylcysteine; B – Aegle marmelos; C – Chitosan; D –Control) (n = 60), and two subgroups (n = 30) based on the duration of medicament placed (subgroup 1: 24 h, subgroup 2: 7 days). Thereafter, dentinal shavings were retrieved, incubated in agar plate, visible colonies counted, and statistically analyzed.Results:At 24 h Group C1 exhibited the lowest CFUs, followed by Group A1, Group B, and Group D1. On the 7th day, Group B2 showed the lowest CFUs, followed by Group A2, Group C2, and Group D2.Conclusions:Against E. faecalis, NAC has the highest antimicrobial properties closely followed by Aegle marmelos and both provide promising novel possibilities for use as intracanal medicaments.

INTRODUCTION

The main objective of root canal treatment is the removal of bacteria from the pulp space. Clinical practices often utilize mechanical debridement and disinfection protocols, like irrigation, to decrease the microbial burden.[1] Nonetheless, challenges such as anatomical complexities, large periapical lesions, and bacteria retention in the dentinal tubules can lead to persistent infection and ongoing inflammation of periradicular tissues. These considerations emphasize the significance of intracanal medicaments as an essential armamentarium of root canal therapy.[2]

While endodontic infections typically involve multiple microorganisms, Enterococcus faecalis is a predominant cause of secondary and persistent infections.[3] Although different formulations of calcium hydroxide as an intracanal medicament have shown enhanced antimicrobial effectiveness,[4] unfortunately, evidence indicates that established medicaments such as calcium hydroxide, chlorhexidine, and their combinations have limited efficacy against E. faecalis.[5] Consequently, there is a growing need to explore novel intracanal medicaments.

N-acetylcysteine (NAC), is a thiol-containing drug possessing good antibacterial and antibiofilm effectiveness against different endodontic pathogens, such as E. faecalis, Actinomyces naeslundii, Lactobacillus salivarius, Streptococcus mutans, and Staphylococcus epidermidis.[6] It is also a potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent reducing levels of pro-inflammatory tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukins (IL-6 and IL-1 β).[7]

Tracing its origin to the crustaceans, chitosan a natural polymer obtained by deacetylation of chitin is known for its biocompatibility, anti-inflammatory, wound healing ability, promising antimicrobial action against a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms, and also fungi.[8]

Aegle marmelos, commonly known as “Bael” is renowned for its therapeutic properties. It has been utilized for various conditions such as asthma, anemia, and wound healing and has demonstrated high antimicrobial effectiveness against E. faecalis.[9] Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties make bael an intriguing option for investigation as an intracanal medicament. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore its potential as an intracanal medicament in endodontics.

Hence, the aim of the study was to evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of NAC, Aegle marmelos, and chitosan as intracanal medicaments against E. faecalis biofilm. The null hypothesis was E. faecalis is not susceptible to the medicaments with no quantitative reduction in microbial count and no difference among the intracanal medicaments.

MATERIALS AND METHODOLOGY

The manuscript for this laboratory study adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory Studies in Endodontology guidelines. Ethical approval was granted by the institutional ethical committee (IEC/SCBDCH/233/2023). The sample size was determined based on a significance level of 5% (α) and a power of 80%, resulting in a planned sample size of 30 per group.

Microorganism

E. faecalis strain ATCC35550 (MTCC, Chandigarh) was cultured on a blood agar-sealed petri dish at 37°C for 24 h. The resulting colonies were then suspended in 5 mL of brain heart infusion broth and adjusted to a concentration of 1 McFarland using a spectrophotometer (Thermofisher Scientific Inc., USA).

Preparation of medicaments

For the Aegle marmelos group, leaves harvested from the tree were washed thoroughly with water and sun-dried for 4 days. The dried leaves were pulverized into a powder using a mortar and pestle. Then 50 g of the powder was mixed with 100 mL of methanol in a shaker and left at room temperature for 24 h with periodic agitation. The resulting mixture was filtered using a Soxhlet apparatus and lyophilized for maximal extraction. The pure methanolic extract of bael leaves was then diluted to obtain its minimum inhibitory concentration (1 mg/mL). Pure NAC powder (1 g/mL) (AM Nutra Tech Pvt Limited, Delhi) was weighed and mixed with distilled water to a paste consistency. Two percent chitosan gel (Vedayukt Pvt Limited, Jharkhand) was used in the study.

Disc diffusion test

Thirty discs were divided into three equal parts and inoculated with a standardized 10 µL of E. faecalis using a glass spreader. Three 6 mm filter discs, saturated with medicaments were positioned on the agar media discs (Sisco Research Laboratory Pvt. Ltd.) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The zones of inhibition were measured in both anteroposterior and mesiodistal directions.

Specimen preparation and biofilm growth

Two hundred and forty noncarious single-rooted teeth, extracted for orthodontic or periodontal causes were collected and the presence of single canal was confirmed using radiographs taken in both buccolingual and mesiodistal directions. Teeth with calcified canals, resorbed roots, length of root <15 mm, and lumen diameter exceeding 0.9 mm (as identified during dentin block preparation) were excluded from the study.

The root length of each tooth was measured from the cementoenamel junction to the root apex using a divider. The root was divided into three equal thirds and the middle third was then sectioned to a standardized external dimension of 5 mm × 5 mm using a rotary diamond disc on a slow-speed micromotor handpiece. The dentin disc was then smoothed on all surfaces using fine (180) grit sandpaper. The internal or lumen diameter was standardized to 0.9 mm using a size no. 2 peeso reamer (Mani. Inc, Japan). All the blocks were cleaned by immersing them in an ultrasonically activated distilled water bath, followed by sequential immersion in 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 5% sodium hypochlorite, and then again in an ultrasonically activated distilled water bath. The immersion time in each solution was standardized to 5 min. Subsequently, samples were subjected to two cycles of autoclaving (121°C, 15psi, 15 min).

Inoculation – Biofilm growth

The dentin blocks were placed in individual microcentrifuge tubes containing 5 mL of TS broth, to which 50 µL of bacterial inoculum was added. All inoculated blocks were then incubated at 37°C for 21 days to allow for biofilm formation, with the culture media being changed every 3 days. This process was conducted within a biosafety cabinet. After 21 days, the blocks were removed and washed with 0.9% saline solution to eliminate the media and planktonic cells from the surface.

Intervention and sample collection

Using online software (www.randomizer.org) randomization and allocation concealment were performed by a volunteer not involved in the study. Samples were divided into four groups (n = 60), and two subgroups (n = 30) based on the duration of medicament placed (24 h: subgroup 1 and 7 days: Subgroup 2). Groups were labeled as follows: Group A (A1 and A2): NAC, Group B (B1 and B2): Aegle marmelos (Bael), Group C (C1 and C2): Chitosan, and Group D (D1 and D2): Control. A standardized quantity of each medicament was placed in the dentin blocks. Twenty milligrams of NAC was applied using a plastic instrument, while 20 µL Aegle marmelos and chitosan were applied using a micropipette (Tarsons, India). Dentin blocks were sealed with paraffin wax on both sides and incubated at 37°C for 24 h (Subgroup 1) and 7 days (Subgroup 2).

Sample collection and CFUs count

After incubation, the paraffin wax was removed and the blocks were washed with distilled water. With a no. 5 peeso reamer (Mani, INC, Japan), used in downward strokes (3 times), dentinal shavings were retrieved from the block’s lumen in a centrifuge tube containing 1 mL of sterile broth. The shavings were serially diluted, plated on a TS agar plate, and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Visible colonies that formed were counted under x10 and tabulated.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained was entered into Microsoft Excel Worksheet and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, IBM Corp Released 2020. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 Armonk, NY, USA: IBM Corp.). The normal distribution of data was checked with Shapiro–Wilk’s test and appropriate nonparametric tests were employed. Mean, median, and standard deviation (SD) were computed for all continuous variables. Comparison of mean colony-forming units among four study groups at 24 h and 7 days was done using the Kruskal–Wallis Test. Mean disc proliferation values were compared among three study groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Comparison within a group between 24 h and Day 7 was done using the Mann–Whitney U-test. P < 0.05 was considered significant for all statistical inferences.

DISCUSSION

This was a single-blinded study, only the investigator was blinded, and the principal operator could not be blinded due to the different colors and consistencies of the medicaments. Among the three levels of infection described in the laboratory model,[10] our study evaluated medicament action against level two (biofilm) and level three (infected tubules). The penetration of medicaments into dentinal tubules up to a depth of 600 µm was determined. The sensitivity of E. faecalis to the medicaments was tested by disc diffusion and bacterial count reduction was evaluated using the Agar plate count method. The agar plate count method is a widely used outcome measure in research studies for its accessibility, reliability, and cost-effectiveness.[11]

In our study, E. faecalis showed susceptibility to all three medicaments in the disc diffusion test and reduction in bacterial count in the Agar plate count method; hence the null hypothesis was rejected. Unfortunately, no medicament was able to completely eradicate the bacteria in the biofilm. The characteristics that contribute to the resistance of E. faecalis include its ability to penetrate deeply into dentinal tubules, adaptive mechanisms, resilience to acidic environments, and survival in extreme temperatures and pH levels.[12] The most commonly used strains of E. faecalis in Endodontic in vitro studies are ATCC 29212, ATCC 4083, and ATCC 35550.[13]

Previous research on chitosan’s effectiveness against E. faecalis biofilm has demonstrated that 2% Chitosan exhibits superior antibacterial action compared to calcium hydroxide in permanent teeth[8] and equivalent efficacy in primary teeth.[14] In a study of canals with persistent infection, the combination of 2% Chitosan and 2% chlorhexidine demonstrated enhanced efficacy against E. faecalis.[15]

Chitosan activity being higher on the 1st day and significantly reducing on the 7th day can be attributed to factors such as molecular weight,[14] acetylation level,[15] pH, and concentration-dependent killing. The cationic chitosan binding electrostatically to the negative lipopolysaccharide layers suggests gram-negative bacteria are more vulnerable than Gram-positive bacteria. The latter’s cell wall thickness might also impede chitosan binding.[16]

On the 1st and 7th day, NAC demonstrated strong bactericidal action. The disulfide bond disruption mechanism damages cell walls and inhibits the biofilm’s ability to adhere to the surface of the tooth.[5] Our findings accord with research conducted against E. faecalis biofilm by Quah et al.,[17] Palaniswamy et al.,[18] and Abu Hasna et al.[19] and contradict the findings of Ulusoy et al.[20] and Adl et al.[21]

The leaves of Aegle marmelos contain bioactive compounds responsible for antibacterial action such as marmelosin, cuminaldehyde, tannins, marmin, and terpenoids.[2223] Methanolic extract has shown greater activity against E. faecalis than aqueous,[24] hence used in our study. Our study findings have revealed Aegle marmelos to be a slow-acting medicament with peak effect on the 7th day signifying consistent antimicrobial efficacy. Since the inherent antibacterial property of thickening agents might influence the action of medicaments,[2526] Aegle marmelos preferred to be used in its purest liquid form. This also indicates that the effectiveness of Aegle Marmelos is independent of its consistency.

Limitations of the study include employing a single-species biofilm, particularly given the polymicrobial nature of root canal infections. Furthermore, any laboratory study assessing the antimicrobial effectiveness of a substance does not fully replicate the in vivo conditions of an infected root canal.[27]

Future directions should include studies to confirm the antibacterial properties of NAC, Aegle marmelos, and chitosan intracanal medicaments, both in the laboratory and in vivo. Further studies should investigate medicaments depth of penetration, interactions with thickening agents, radiopaque materials, and their anti-inflammatory effects.

CONCLUSIONS

Within the study’s limitations, NAC showed the highest antimicrobial property against E. faecalis biofilm on both 1st day and 7th day. The antibacterial activity of Aegle marmelos increased with time (7th day > 1st day) while chitosan’s activity reduced on the 7th day as compared to the 1st day. NAC and Aegle marmelos provide promising novel possibilities for use as intracanal medicaments.
