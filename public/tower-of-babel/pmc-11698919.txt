Brain oscillatory activity evoked by chirp modulated tones in healthy children
Authors: Nadia Inés Irazabal, Elena Urrestarazu, Miguel Valencia, Oscar Manzanilla, Nerea Crespo-Eguilaz, María Belén Isaguirre, Rocío Sánchez-Carpintero
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimTo describe the features of cortical oscillatory activity and neuronal synchronization by monitoring responses evoked by chirp-modulated tone (CMT) and look for relationships with cognition in healthy children.MethodsWe recruited 23 healthy children, 1–18 years old, assessed their cognitive abilities and recorded the cortical oscillatory activity evoked by CMTs. We obtained descriptive statistics and looked for correlations with cognitive abilities.ResultsIn the low gamma band, the neuronal synchronization evoked by CMTs increased with age, reaching adult features by 12 years of age (rho = 0.5; p = 0.042). In the high gamma band, neuronal recruitment was greater at younger ages (rho = −0.55; p = 0.029). In four of the six under-6-year-old participants, there was no CMT-evoked response. We found that the greater a child's receptive vocabulary skills, the lower the frequency at which maximal neuronal recruitment occurred (rho = −0.65; p = 0.003).InterpretationCMT-evoked cortical oscillatory activity is affected by degree of brain maturation and could be a potential biomarker of language-related disability. Our description of the cortical responses evoked by CMTs in healthy children is a step towards recognition of abnormal patterns and the possible use of this approach as a biomarker of brain disorders in children.

Information processing in the brain requires an ability to recruit and synchronize diverse neuronal populations operating at different frequencies. Cortical oscillatory activity is a fundamental mechanism that supports neuronal synchronization. Such activity consists of periodic variations in the amplitude and frequency of brain electrical activity generated mainly by the action of the postsynaptic potentials of neuronal populations [1]. Several studies on adults have found a relationship between cognition and cortical oscillatory activity, mainly within the gamma range [2,3]. Similar studies on children are scarce [4,5].

When the brain is exposed to a periodic sensory stimulus, the neural networks involved in processing that stimulus can synchronize their activity to match its frequency. This synchronization can lead to the generation of a steady-state potential, which is a stable oscillatory activity with the same frequency as that of the original stimulus. The steady-state potential can be detected and recorded with electroencephalography (EEG) or magnetoencephalography (MEG). Auditory steady-state potentials or responses (ASSR) are typically investigated within what is referred to as the gamma band, which ranges from about 30 to 100 Hz. In adults, the highest amplitude of response in the gamma band is at about 40 Hz [6].

Cortical oscillatory responses at different frequencies within the gamma band are challenging to obtain, as the subject needs to be tested at each frequency, making the study lengthy and requiring a high degree of subject collaboration. Consequently, most studies have focussed only on the responses at 40 Hz. Artieda et al. developed a non-invasive, 20-min-long technique using a chirp-modulated tone (CMT) to obtain cortical oscillatory activity in response to auditory stimuli [7,8] over a range of frequencies. A CMT is a constant carrier tone modulated by a sine wave whose frequency linearly ramps up along a short time interval. Being relatively quick, this approach is good for use with children [9].

Some authors have described differences in cortical oscillatory activity evoked by CMTs in subjects with brain diseases. Arrondo et al. compared the CMT-evoked cortical oscillation in patients with multiple sclerosis with those in healthy controls, concluding that in patients with cognitive impairment the frequency at which oscillation amplitude peaked was lower [3]. Griskova-Bulanova et al. analysed evoked-envelope following responses to chirp stimuli and found differences between subjects with schizophrenia and healthy controls. Relative to controls, patients with schizophrenia had low gamma-band peak amplitude at a lower frequency, and a lower inter-trial coherence (ITC) – a measure of the degree of synchronization - in the high gamma band [10].

Little has been published about the features of gamma-frequency oscillatory brain responses in children. The few studies that have been reported use different methodologies to obtain or analyse the responses, making it difficult to draw any general conclusions. Poulson et al. studied how ASSRs change as children get older and described changes in the frequency of peak amplitude, concluding that, by the age of 11 years, a child's 40-Hz ASSR is similar to that observed in adults [11]. De Stefano et al. found this age to be between 10 and 11 years [12]. Arutiunian et al., using MEG to study the 40 Hz-ASSR, described an increase in ITC and a decrease in amplitude in event-related fields with age [13]. For auditory stimuli with a frequency other than 40 Hz, however, less is known about how cortical oscillatory activity changes with age.

To our knowledge, in the literature there is no description of the CMT-evoked cortical oscillatory activity of healthy children. Such a description, together with information about how oscillatory activity evolves during normal brain development and its relationship with cognition, is necessary in order to be able to detect departures from the norm and even, perhaps, to detect and study childhood-onset brain pathology. The aim of this study is to describe cortical oscillatory activity evoked by CMTs in children between 1 and 18 years old and to look for possible relationships between this activity and different aspects of cognition.

Methods

Standard protocol approval and participants consent

All procedures were approved by the research ethics committee of the Navarra government (CEIm; Comunidad Foral de Navarra). All subjects over 16 years old gave informed consent. All parents or legal tutors of children under 16 years old gave written informed consent. All children over 12 years old agreed to participate.

Participants

For this cross-sectional study we recruited children and adolescents aged 1–18 years from families of hospital employees and from people attending the general paediatrics clinics at the Clínica Universidad de Navarra. We included only healthy children without cognitive impairment: children with a personal or family background of epilepsy or any other behavioural or developmental disorder were excluded. Children receiving chronic or acute pharmacological treatment were also excluded.

Cognitive evaluations

We evaluated language skills (receptive and expressive vocabulary, grammar and syntax, global adaptive language, pragmatic language), behaviour (social skills, range of interests), non-verbal abilities (visual-motor integration, perceptive reasoning, motor skills), and selective attention and global adaptive development (Table 1). Methodology of recordings and signal analysis.

A subject's concentration on stimulus CMT has little impact on the obtained evoked responses [7] and so, in order to decrease artefacts caused by fidgeting, children were allowed to watch a silenced video during recording [9].

We examined the traces off line and manually excluded trials with visible artefacts or signs of subject drowsiness. Between 218 and 496 artefact-free trials were selected per subject.

Signal recordings were analysed in the manner described by Artieda et al. [8].The objective was to obtain two values: the evoked power (EP), which indicates the degree of recruitment of neurons; and the inter-trial coherence (ITC), which reflects the degree of neuronal synchronization. To this end, the recorded signal was processed in two ways in the Matlab platform (Mathworks, USA) (Table 2). However, to control the effect of differences in the number of artefact-free trials in the estimation of the EP and ITC values, we applied a trial correction or adjustment [9] (Table 2). This formula causes ITC values to exceed 1 because we divide by a number less than 1. The interpretation is that higher values indicate greater coherence.

EP and ITC values for each electrode can be plotted as colours in graphs of frequency (y axis) over time (x axis), showing the capacity of subjects to generate responses to frequencies from 1 to 120 Hz. If there is an oscillatory response, this appears in the graph as a diagonal band of colour from bottom left to top right, as the response follows the increasing frequency over the duration of the CMT.

In a previous CMT study in adults, Artieda et al. [8] identified two peaks: one in the low gamma range (30–79 Hz) and the other in the high gamma range (80–120 Hz). In view of this, we calculated EP and ITC values for both bands.

Earlier studies have highlighted that the midline and parasagittal electrodes, specifically F3, F4, Fz, C3, C4, Cz, P3, P4, and Pz, are optimal for analysing auditory-evoked cortical oscillatory activity, with frontal electrodes exhibiting the highest amplitudes [3,[7], [8], [9],21,22]. In our sample, frontal electrodes also exhibited the highest amplitude values, making them good candidates for the subsequent analysis. However, to facilitate data interpretation, we considered it necessary to select a single electrode. For this reason, through mixed models and Friedman's test, we confirmed that no significant differences relevant to the current study existed between trials recorded by F3, F4, and Fz. Consequently, for our analysis, we selected trials from frontal electrode that showed least artefact and the clearest response.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed in R/S-plus. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

The analysis of the electrodes involves comparing the maximum power values and their corresponding frequencies for both EP and ITC obtained from 207 electrodes of 23 participants. Data were collected from 9 electrodes per subject, resulting in multiple measurements per individual. Due to the inherent correlation in repeated measurements, it was necessary to use a method that accounted for this correlation to perform multiple comparisons. Consequently, mixed models were chosen, as they allow for the handling of dependent data by including both fixed and random effects, providing greater flexibility and precision in analyzing complex data. In our study, the subjects were treated as a random effect and the electrodes as a fixed effect, with the aim of determining whether there were significant differences between electrodes. To apply this model, the assumptions of Normality, Linearity, and Homoscedasticity of residuals, as well as the Normality of the random effect, needed to be met. If these assumptions were not met, the non-parametric Friedman test was used.

Participants were split into two groups on the basis of whether or not they showed an oscillatory response (that is, the presence or absence of a diagonal band on the color-coded frequency-response plot). These two groups: the Response Group (RG) and the Non-response Group (NRG) were analysed descriptively due to the small sample size of one of them. For the RG, we used Spearman's rank correlation to explore potential relationships between features of oscillatory activity (EP and ITC) and demographic and cognitive variables. Since we conducted multiple correlations between EP and ITC with cognitive skills, we applied the Bonferroni correction.

Discussion

To our knowledge, our study is the first on CMT-evoked oscillatory responses in healthy children and the first to include children of all ages (from 12 months old to 18 years old) to enable a description of age-related changes in these evoked responses. The study shows that age is a key factor determining the characteristics of brain oscillatory activity. We observed different responses at different ages in both low and high gamma bands (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). In four of the six under-6-year-old participants, it was not possible to detect a CMT-evoked response. In the low gamma band, neuronal synchronization (ITC) increased with age, becoming similar to that reported for adults by 12 years of age. In the high gamma band, neuronal recruitment (EP) decreased with age. We did not find any relationship between age and the frequencies of power peaks of EP or ITC - corresponding to maximum neuronal recruitment and synchronization during an evoked response - in either low or high gamma bands.

By doing two tests separated by a period of 18 months, Poulsen et al. studied the development of ASSR at 40 Hz and of auditory EFRs in healthy 10-year-old children. While amplitudes were not observed to change, the peak EFR frequency was found to increase from approximately 35 Hz–40 Hz by 11 years of age [11]. In our cross sectional study, we found neuronal synchronization increased with age becoming similar to adults by 12 years of age. Ono et al. reported a similar increase in the control group of their study about children with autism spectrum disorder aged between 5 and 7 years old [27].

Nineteen out of 23 children (82.6 %) in our study had cortical oscillatory activity evoked by CMTs. The response with highest power occurred at around 40 Hz, with a second peak at around 95 Hz. Artieda et al. described similar activity in adults, who had a low gamma peak at 45.9 Hz and a high gamma one at 90 Hz [8]. Only half of the adults in the Artieda et al. study had a definite high gamma band peak [8], whereas 82 % of children in our sample did. Levi et al. studied amplitude-modulated following responses in infants and concluded that the most effective frequency at evoking responses in infants was 80 Hz [28]. These findings suggest that the developing brain generates better responses to stimuli at high gamma frequencies than at low gamma ones. An explanation for this observation is that the source of responses changes during brain development (perhaps in relation to myelination). Herdman et al. found that, while higher frequency responses are generated from the brainstem (which is myelinated at birth), responses at 40 Hz originate predominantly from the cortex (in which myelination progresses gradually) [29].

In a previous study by our research group, Sanchez-Carpintero et al. found no relationship between global IQ and CMT-evoked cortical oscillatory in children with Dravet syndrome [9]. Therefore, in this study we looked for relationships between CMT responses and specific cognitive skills. We found a correlation with the frequency of the power peak: the better the receptive vocabulary skills, the lower the frequency at which maximal neuronal recruitment occurred. The same trend was observed with expressive vocabulary skills, but statistical significance was not reached after Bonferroni adjustment. In contrast to our results, a study using CMTs in adults with multiple sclerosis who had cognitive impairment found that the worse the cognitive impairment, the lower the low-gamma frequency of the oscillatory response [3]. In another study, of children with language difficulties and ASD, children with poor language ability tended to have lower ITC [5]. Different methodologies and sample populations make it impossible to draw conclusions, but results suggest a possible relationship between auditory-evoked neuronal activity in the gamma band and the language neural network. In this respect it is interesting to note that Poulsen et al. described hemispheric asymmetries in cortical responses that could be related to the speed of discrimination between language and noise [11].

Several investigations in children have studied the relationship between features of autism and cortical oscillatory activity. In individuals with Fragile X Syndrome (FXS), Ethridge et al. described correlations between neuronal recruitment and sensory hypersensitivities and between neuronal synchronization and autistic behaviours [30,31]. Lower neuronal synchronization in patients with ASD has also been reported by De Stefano et al. [12]. We assessed sociability and restricted interests, both of which are impaired in ASD, but, in healthy children, we did not find any correlation with oscillatory activity.

The main limitation of this study is the small number of subjects in each age group, which may have resulted in overlooking some correlations with cognitive abilities.

In conclusion, use of CMTs proves to be a quick, non-invasive way to evaluate brain oscillatory activity in children. Age emerges as the primary determinant of the response regarding synchronization ability (ITC). While healthy children aged 12 and above exhibit two peaks in the low and high gamma bands, resembling what is described in adults, younger children and infants display distinctive characteristics, likely associated with ongoing brain maturation. The ability to generate cortical oscillatory activity evoked by CMT may not be developed in children under 6 years old. Additionally, in this age range, the peak may be observed in the high gamma range, rather than in the low gamma range as seen in those over 12 years old. In contrast, children older than 6 but younger than 12 years show intermediate developmental changes in cortical oscillatory activity evoked by CMT. Our findings also relate auditory cortical oscillatory activity to receptive and expressive vocabulary skill. While other researchers have found a relationship between features of CMT-evoked oscillatory activity and behavioural features related to ASD in children with ASD, we found no such relationships in healthy children. This finding raises the possibility that CMT-evoked oscillatory activity might be used as a biomarker of neurocognitive disabilities in children. In this respect, our description of the cortical oscillatory activity evoked by a CMT in healthy children helps establish a basis on which to recognize abnormal patterns.
