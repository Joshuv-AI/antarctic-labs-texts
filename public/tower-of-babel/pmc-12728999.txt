DNA-guided 3D pathway reconfiguration of composite nanopores for ion transport regulation
Authors: Jiarong Guo, Tao Gao, Ying Ma, Jinwen Zhu, Jiayue Shi, Baoguo Wang, Peng Miao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIon current rectification (ICR) is essential for understanding analyte-driven nanofluid transport within nanopores. However, the rapid flow rates and limited reaction times in this process can impede electrochemical reactions at electrode interfaces, which consequently lead to electrical noise challenges. Here, we propose a simple strategy to enhance the ICR effect and reduce noise interference through the construction of bio/solid composite pores. The composite pores comprise θ-shaped glass pores that have been sequentially modified with 3-glycidyloxypropyltrimethoxysilane (GLYMO), succinic anhydride (SA) and single-stranded DNA. Three-dimensional (3D) biochannels formed from acrylamide-DNA hydrogels are integrated within glass pores. High-density 3D channels and a highly viscous environment can decelerate analyte traversal, thereby establishing a stable reaction environment. Concurrently, acrylamide-DNA hydrogels modulate pore size through cascade reactions triggered by analytes, thereby altering the transport pathways of ion and affecting ICR. Furthermore, we have developed a sensor featuring adjustable ion transport pathways based on this technology. The detection range can be readily expanded to include nucleic acids, proteins, glycans and a multitude of biomolecules through modifying different aptamers. Introduction

Nanopore-based sensors have emerged as a prominent tool for the analysis of single molecules in solutions. The primary molecular readout method involves measuring the temporal fluctuations of ionic currents that pass through the nanopores [1–3]. As an emerging electroanalytical technique, nanopore sensor offers several advantages, including speed, reliability, accuracy, portability, and cost-effectiveness [4, 5]. Currently, nanopore detection technology primarily employs two strategies including resistive-pulse sensing (RPS) and ion current rectification (ICR). RPS achieves single-molecule detection by measuring the transient blockage of ionic current as molecules traverse the nanopore, offering excellent temporal resolution and high sensitivity to individual molecules. However, this method imposes stringent requirements on pore conductance and electrical noise levels to ensure accurate signal detection [6–8]. ICR relies on the unique phenomenon of overlapping electric double layers (EDL) within asymmetric nanopores [9, 10]. It achieves the detection through the modulation of ionic transport behavior under specific voltage conditions. Unlike RPS, ICR does not depend on high pore conductance or temporal resolution; instead, its asymmetric current-voltage (I-V) curves are modulated by analyte-induced changes to the nanopore surface [11, 12]. However, ICR sensor relies on physical or chemical reactions between the analyte and the inner wall of the nanopore. Due to the rapid flow rate and limited interaction time, the nanopore does not provide a stable reaction environment.

Filling nanopores with porous biological media (such as hydrogels) can reduce the transport rate of analytes through the pores, finely tune the transport of specific molecules in a predictable manner and enable further functionalization of nanopores [13, 14]. Among numerous materials, DNA hydrogel stands out as a promising candidate for advanced sensing interface design due to the programmable molecular recognition capabilities, the environment-responsive characteristics, and the dynamic tunability of the three-dimensional (3D) network structure [15–17]. By rationally designing the sequences, DNA hydrogel can specifically recognize target molecules and initiate multiple stimulus responses, thereby converting chemical binding events into measurable physical signals [18–20]. Additionally, the spontaneous hybridization of single-stranded DNA at room temperature facilitates the assembly with limited side reactions. Meanwhile, the functional integrity of the DNA strands can be preserved within the hydrogel.

Inspired by this concept, we used acrylamide-DNA hydrogel to fill solid-state pores, thereby constructing bio/solid composite pores. Prior to filling, the solid-state pores underwent a stepwise modification and were finally coupled with single-stranded DNA. This single-stranded DNA could form complementary pairs with the linker strands of the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel. They could effectively fill the gaps between the hydrogel and the pore, preventing interface bubbles from interfering with EDL [21, 22]. The high pore density of acrylamide-DNA hydrogel significantly amplifies the current response of the composite pore [23, 24]. Additionally, we expanded the solid-state pore from nanoscale pores to larger micropores to enhance ICR effect and reduce noise interference [25, 26]. Interestingly, the composite pore demonstrated anomalous ICR due to the influence of 3D ion transport channels and high viscosity environment [27–29]. As an application, we developed a sensor with adjustable ion transport pathways and achieved excellent detection performance for organophosphorus (OP) pesticides in complex environments through specific adaptor sequences designed within composite pores. Furthermore, confining DNA hydrogels within nanopores significantly reduces DNA consumption, lowering material costs. Unlike conventional DNA hydrogel-based sensors requiring large amounts of DNA for assembly, nanopore-based systems need as little as one microliter of hydrogel solution. The spatial confinement of nanopores enables highly sensitive detection of minute structural changes in DNA hydrogels via amplified electrical signals, offering cost-effective and efficient high-resolution molecular recognition.

Materials and methods

Reagents and instruments

The dual-barrel borosilicate glass capillaries were purchased from the World Precision Instruments Company (USA). 3-glycidyloxypropyltrimethoxysilane (GLYMO) was purchased from Yuanye Biological Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Succinic anhydride, naled, malathion and acrylamide were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). N, N,N′,N′-Tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) and ammonium persulfate (APS) were purchased from Aladdin Biological Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Chloropyriphos, prothiofos and triazophos were purchased from Bailingwei Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All oligonucleotides were synthesized and purified by Sangong Biotech (Shanghai, China). The sequences were listed in Table S1. Electrochemical measurements were carried out on a CHI 660E electrochemical workstation (CH Instruments, China). Gel electrophoresis images were taken by a GelDoc XR + Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Atomic force microscope (AFM) images were taken by Dimension Icon (Bruker Instruments, Germany). Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images were taken with a FEI Quanta 400FEG scanning electron microscope (FEI Co., Eindhoven, Netherlands).

Preparation of dual micron pore electrode (MPE)

The θ-shaped borosilicate capillaries were immersed in Piranha solution at 80 °C for 30 min (highly dangerous), followed by a series of washing procedures involving immersion and sonication in anhydrous ethanol and deionized water for 5 min, respectively. The Sutter P97 microelectrode puller was employed to pull the capillaries, fabricating nanopipettes with required pore size. The parameters were set as follows: HEAT, 555; PULL, 25; VEL, 30; and TIME, 200.

Synthesis of DNA hydrogel

Tris-HCl buffer (10 mM, 200 mM NaCl, pH = 8.0) was first prepared, which was deoxygenated by passing nitrogen gas. Next, 0.4 mM DNA probe L1 was added into 1 mL of Tris-HCl buffer (referred as Solution A), along with the addition of APS and TEMED to achieve the final concentrations of 1.4%. Similarly, 0.4 mM DNA probe L2 was added to the same buffer (referred as Solution B), along with APS and TEMED addition. The two solutions were then polymerized separately in a vacuum desiccator at 37 °C for 20 min. The resulting polymers (referred as PS-A and PS-B) were purified through filtration, which were subsequently mixed and incubated at 60 °C for 20 min. After cooling to 37 °C, the polymers were cross-linked to form the DNA hydrogel (L1/L2).

Modifications of the MPE

The micron pore electrode was prepared through sequential modifications. Initially, the prepared GLYMO ethanol solution (0.4%) was injected into one pore, while nitrogen was introduced into the other. The entire micron pore was then incubated in GLYMO ethanol solution for 4 h under light protection. Next, the micron pore was cleaned with deionized water for three times. Afterward, it was dried in a vacuum at 60 °C for 30 min and then cured at 85 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of the succinic anhydride solution was injected into the GLYMO-functionalized pore. The incubation lasted for 6 h, ensuring proper sealing to avoid solution evaporation. The succinic anhydride solution in the micron pore tip was aspirated and rinsed with deionized water for three times. Then, DNA was injected into the micron pore and was incubated overnight. Finally, PS-A and PS-B solutions were mixed and rapidly injected into the micron pore, where they were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The completed MPE was washed with deionized water for three times and stored at 4 °C before future use.

Electrochemical measurements

All electrochemical experiments were carried out using a traditional two-electrode system. An appropriate volume of 0.1 M KCl solution was injected into the double pores of the electrodes. Subsequently, two Ag/AgCl wires, each with a diameter of 0.2 mm, were inserted into these pores. The modified pore was utilized as the working electrode and the unmodified pore was applied as the reference electrode. The LSV measurement was conducted with the voltage range between − 1.5 and 1.5 V, the scanning rate of 0.1 V/s and the sample interval of 0.01 V. For asymmetric LSV curves, the ICR ratio was defined as the absolute value of the ratio of current at −1.5 V to the current at 1.5 V, expressed as │i− 1.5 V/i1.5 V│. The ICR ratio represented the average of three independent LSV measurements. Furthermore, the electrochemical noise measurement was carried out with a sample interval of 0.1 s for a duration of 300 s. A voltage of 400 mV was employed for the translocation signal test. Prior to the translocation signal test, pores were washed for five times with deionized water, followed by a 10-min i-t test at 400 mV to remove any unreacted DNA.

Results and discussion

Characterizations of MPE and acrylamide-DNA hydrogel modification

Acrylamide-DNA hydrogel-filled MPE is synthesized through a stepwise modification of the micron pore combined with DNA self-assembly. The process is illustrated in Fig. 1a. First, after treatment with piranha solution, a number of weak hydroxyl groups appear on the surface of the θ-shaped MPE [30]. The methoxyl group of GLYMO reacts with the hydroxyl group, forming silica-oxygen bond. GLYMO could thus be effectively anchored. Following the introduction of succinic anhydride (SA), a ring-opening reaction occurs between SA and the epoxy group of GLYMO. Carboxyl groups are thus incorporated onto the inner wall of the MPE, which further interact with acrydite-DNA L1 and acrydite-DNA L2. These two strands contain complementary sequences (Figure S1). Meanwhile, additional L1 and L2 branch from the linear polyacrylamide polymer to form polymer strands 1 and 2 (PS-1 and PS-2), respectively. After being introduced into the MPE, the complementary sequences between PS-1 and PS-2 facilitate their cross-linking and promote complementary pairing of L1 and L2. As the hybridization progresses, the polymer is ultimately transformed into the hydrogel within the θ-shaped MPE. The MPE produced under a specific heating and stretching procedure has identical tip morphology and comparable current response (Figure S2). The MPE is then characterized via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As illustrated in Fig. 1b, the tip contains two internal holes with equal diameter. One is designated for the filling of acrylamide-DNA hydrogel, while the other accommodates the reference electrode to mitigate additional current blockage. The pore diameter is determined to be 1 μm, and the inter-pore spacing is around 100 nm.

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) is conducted to validate the modification process of the MPE (Fig. 1c and d). The curve for the bare electrode is linear, exhibiting an ohmic characteristic due to the high symmetry of the charge distribution and geometry [31]. Following hydroxylation treatment, the electrode displays a non-linear current-voltage (I-V) response with an ICR ratio of 1.15. This behavior can be attributed to two factors: (i) the misaligned geometry of the tip, which results in a differential finite transfer rate of ions into and out of the pore; (ii) the presence of negatively charged weak hydroxyl groups on the inner wall, which enhances the selectivity for chloride ions. The functionalization with GLYMO reverses the ion rectification effect, yielding an ICR ratio of 0.13. The introduction of SA brings a large number of carboxyl groups to the micron pore, creating a highly negatively charged environment within the inner wall. Therefore, a further reversal of the ion rectification with an increased ICR ratio of 4.03 is generated. The successful modification of DNA within the pores can be confirmed by energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis. As illustrated in Fig. 1e, the surface of the SA-modified glass contains three elements: carbon (C), oxygen (O), and silicon (Si). Following the introduction of DNA, specific phosphorus (P) and nitrogen (N) elements are observed on the glass surface, leading to significant differences in the elemental compositions. Once the DNA is stably bonded to the inner walls of the MPE, acrylamide-DNA hydrogel can be formed by cross-linking within the pore through the introduction of additional L1 and L2. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) confirms the successful assembly of the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel in the MPE (Fig. 1f). The 3D network structure of the hydrogel divides the large pore (~ 1 μm) at the tip of the glass tube into multiple smaller pores, resulting in slower ion transport and increased electrochemical impedance. Concurrently, the I-V curve exhibits abnormal ICR behavior (Fig. 1g). During the negative potential phase, the ion current remains low, indicating blocked ion transport. In the positive potential phase, the ionic current initially increases before displaying saturation behavior at a relatively high voltage. The result suggests that acrylamide-DNA hydrogel can efficiently rectify the ion current while enhancing ion flux. The 3D network structure within the hydrogel facilitates ion transport, rather than relying on the pores in the glass tip, substantially extending the effective transport path [32]. Furthermore, the polyacrylamide presented in the hydrogel creates a high-viscosity environment, enabling reverse electroosmotic flow to draw the aqueous solution into the pores. The viscosity is thus reduced [27]. Figure 1h shows the ion distribution around the DNA strand within the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel. Adsorbed K+ ions dissociate from the hydrogel along with the polarisation of the DNA. The ion depletion zones are formed under positive high pressure and the channel is saturated with ionic current. The saturation of ionic currents in the positive high-voltage region ceases following the introduction of deoxyribonuclease 1 (DNase I) into acrylamide-DNA hydrogel-filled micron pore. DNase I effectively degrades acrylamide-DNA hydrogel, thereby disrupting the accumulation of cations on the DNA strands, resulting in a reduced contribution of cations to ionic currents under positive potentials (Figure S3).

Structural switch in acrylamide-DNA hydrogel

Given the correlation between ionic currents and 3D network structures in acrylamide-DNA hydrogel, we hypothesize that changes in the pore size of acrylamide-DNA hydrogel might influence ionic currents in the positive potential region. As a proof-of-concept, we have designed cascade strand displacement reactions to modify the pore size of acrylamide-DNA hydrogels at the submicron scale. As shown in Fig. 2a, the hybridized product L1/L2 in the hydrogel exposes sticky ends, which are capable of recognizing S1 with hairpin folding capability. It leads to the generation of a higher molecular weight product L1/L2/S1 by strand displacement reaction (Figure S4). S2 and S3 strands serve as the fuel strands and cascade reaction occurs, significantly extending the length of the linker DNA within the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (Figure S5). An observable increase in hydrogel pore size can be ultimately achieved. To mitigate additional side reactions, the ends of these three extended strands are capped with complementary Lock1 and Lock2.

Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) is first applied to validate the cascade strand displacement reaction. As shown in Fig. 2b, bands in lanes 1, 2 and 3 are ascribed to L1, L2 and the hybridized L1/L2, respectively. The band in lane 4 depicts the initiator strand H1, which comprises S1 featuring a stem-loop structure and a segment of the closed sequence, Lock1. The bands in lanes 5 and 6 represent the fuel strands of H2 and H3, which comprise the S2 and S3 strands along with their respective terminal locks. Upon the addition of H1 to L1/L2, a higher molecular weight product appears, indicating a successful reaction between H1 and L1/L2 (lane 7). Moreover, the presence of additional bands in lane 8 suggests that the introduction of H2 and H3 formed a longer cross-linked strand. On the other hand, AFM characterization of the DNA products before and after the strand displacement reaction can be used to assess changes in the hydrogel network structure. As shown in Fig. 2c, the DNA products assembled from L1 and L2 exhibit anticipated network structure. The height is measured to be 2 nm, which is consistent with the diameter of the double helix. The introduction of three strands (S1, S2, and S3) results in the formation of a larger and looser DNA network structure as expected (Fig. 2d). SEM is also applied to visualize the network structure of the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel. Figure 2e and f present the images of acrylamide-DNA hydrogels before and after the strand displacement reactions. The acrylamide-DNA hydrogel exhibits a uniform, porous, and interconnected internal network structure, which facilitates ionic current flow and allows for the accumulation of counterions on the porous skeleton. The SEM results align closely with AFM conclusions, indicating that the hydrogel prior to the strand displacement reaction (acrylamide-DNA L1/L2 hydrogel) is more tightly cross-linked with smaller pore sizes, whereas the hydrogel after the strand displacement reaction (acrylamide-DNA L1/L2/S1/S2/S3 hydrogel) becomes looser with larger pore sizes. Notably, the actual pore size of the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel is likely much larger than the pore size observed in the AFM images. This discrepancy is because the process of DNA polymerization to form hydrogels does not adhere to a predetermined cross-linking point; instead, it first generates very long DNA cross-linking strands, which ultimately entangle to form acrylamide-DNA hydrogel.

The nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms of acrylamide-DNA hydrogels are shown in Fig. 2g and h. All acrylamide-DNA hydrogels exhibit typical type IV curves. The presence of desorption hysteresis curves indicates multilayer adsorption within the hydrogels, which is attributed to capillary condensation occurring at elevated relative pressures (P/P0). The average pore size, as determined by the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method, in acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2) is determined to be 15.67 nm. Notably, this average pore size is increased to 175.24 nm in acrylamide-DNA hydrogel following the incorporation of S1/S2/S3 strands. The introduction of the S1 during the strand displacement reaction leads to the unwinding of the L1/L2, resulting in the gradual extension of the linker DNA strands. Consequently, this process induces the formation of additional pores with larger dimensions, which is consistent with the SEM results.

Electrochemical responses of hydrogels of different pore sizes

Compared to acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2), acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2/S1/S2/S3) incorporates a higher number of DNA strands along with an increased pore size, potentially influencing the differences in electrochemical noise. The power density spectra of these two hydrogel-filled micron pores are illustrated in Fig. 3a and b. The average current of the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2/S1/S2/S3) is reduced. This phenomenon is attributed to the introduction of additional DNA strands, which repel the electrochemical species more significantly. Concurrently, the larger pore size allows easier passage of ionic currents, resulting in smaller discrete currents than those of acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2) and better current stability. The current signals from micron pore are at the microampere level, and the background noise currents from both micro pores do not interfere with the translocation and ion rectification signals. Additional verification of the resistance changes between the two hydrogels is provided by EIS tests, which demonstrate an increase in the impedance value of acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2/S1/S2/S3) from 39.5 to 60.4 Ω (Fig. 3c), corroborating the power density noise results.

The power density spectra of (a) acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2) and (b) acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2/S1/S2/S3). (c) Nyquist diagrams of the two hydrogels. (d) PAGE analysis of DNA tetrahedron assembly. (e) I-t curves and (f) LSV curves of the hydrogels

Changes in the pore size of hydrogel can affect the transport rate of substances surrounding the pore. To investigate the differences in translocation signals among hydrogels with varying pore sizes, we select DNA tetrahedron for transportation. DNA tetrahedron shares the same components as the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel, thereby avoiding additional chemical reactions during the translocation process. Each DNA tetrahedron consists of four strands (T1, T2, T3, and T4), with each edge measuring 17 base pairs in length (Figure S6). The PAGE image confirms the successful assembly of the DNA tetrahedron nanostructure, with T1/T2/T3/T4 exhibiting the largest molecular weight and migration position in the gel (Fig. 3d). AFM image reveals that the DNA tetrahedron nanostructures are uniformly distributed onto the mica surface, with a length of approximately 10 nm and a height of about 3 nm (Figure S7). This size is significantly smaller than the pore size of the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel, enabling successful transport through the hydrogel pores to the exterior of the micron pores. Conductance blocking serves as a direct measure of the volume occupied by DNA tetrahedron at the narrowest part of the pore. Counting the number of peak blocking currents over a consistent time interval provides insights into the transport efficiency of DNA tetrahedron. At a bias voltage of 500 mV, translocation events involving DNA tetrahedron are observed in both large-pore and small-pore hydrogels (Fig. 3e). The statistical results of peak height indicate that the frequency of translocation events increases from 0.9 Hz to 3.43 Hz with larger pore size, indicating that more DNA tetrahedron nanostructures reach the micron pore tip within the same time frame (Figure S8).

Changes in the pore size of hydrogel can be assessed by recording the frequency of transport events. These changes can be coupled with the target to enable RPS. However, the introduction of transport substances may interfere with the target’s reaction rate, thereby affecting detection accuracy. Moreover, due to the spatial confinement effect of nanopores, transport substances may accumulate at narrower points within the hydrogel channel, which could lead to potential blockages. Therefore, RPS is more suitable for trace-level target detection and single-molecule detection. In contrast, ICR is modulated by analytically induced changes on the nanopore surface. This method does not require the addition of transport substances, making it a promising candidate for development as a universal detection technique. To explore ionic current differences across hydrogels with varying pore sizes, we have conducted LSV testing. Comparative analysis of the ionic currents reveals that the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2/S1/S2/S3) exhibits a reduced slope in the I-V curve and higher resistance (Fig. 3f). As the pore size of the hydrogel increases, the number of cross-linking points within the unit cross-section of micron pores decreases, resulting in a reduced concentration of adsorbed cations and a declining trend in the ionic saturation current. Overall, compared with acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2), acrylamide-DNA hydrogel (L1/L2/S1/S2/S3) contains a greater number of DNA strands, is larger in size, and exhibits higher resistance. However, the reduced number of cross-linking points per unit cross-section is advantageous for particle translocation and ion transport.

Electrochemical sensing principle of OP

In the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel-filled micron pore, minor changes in the network structure can be amplified by the ICR characteristic of the asymmetric micron pore. As a result, electrochemical sensors can be facilely developed through the target-activated strand displacement reactions. Moreover, various DNA sequences can be designed to incorporate distinct aptamers tailored for the detection of different substances. Building on this principle, we have constructed an electrochemical micron pore sensor for the detection of OP compounds by combining an aptamer sequence with the hairpin S1, which carries the sequence of Lock1. As shown in Fig. 4a, due to the high affinity between the aptamer and OP compounds, the aptamer sequence can be displaced from S1. Consequently, through self-hybridization, hairpin structure of S1 is formed, which initiates the downstream strand displacement reaction. Ultimately, a change in the pore size of hydrogel is generated. This physical structural change is amplified into an observable electrochemical signal through the micron pore, allowing quantification of OP concentration.

Electrochemical response of acrylamide-DNA hydrogel-filled micron pore to OP

Herein, we have developed an electrochemical sensor for the detection of profenofos as an example. Profenofos is a broad-spectrum insecticide. Its molecular structure includes a phosphate ester group (-O-P = O) and a thioether bond (-S-), enabling it to exert toxic effects by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase (AChE) in insects. Residues of profenofos may lead to contamination of water resources, agricultural soils, and agricultural products, thereby posing potential health risks to humans and aquatic organisms. The profenofos aptamer comprises a DNA strand of 35 base pairs in length. The binding affinity between the aptamer and target is confirmed through molecular docking (Fig. 4b), with AutoDock simulations indicating that the aptamer interacts with profenofos via hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Specifically, thymine at position 5 and cytosine at position 29 contribute to the binding stability of profenofos through hydrophobic interactions. Furthermore, the cytosine at position 7 forms a halogen bond with the chlorine atom presented in profenofos. The cumulative contribution of electrostatic and van der Waals forces is approximately − 48.9 Kcal/mol.

To evaluate the analytical performance, LSV measurements are conducted across various concentrations of profenofos. As shown in Fig. 4c, the ion saturation current decreases with larger profenofos level, while the corresponding rectification ratio increases. The rectification ratio exhibits a positive correlation with the concentration of profenofos. The detailed relationship is depicted in Fig. 4d. The regression equation is established as follows: y = 0.011 log c(OP) + 0.424 (R2 = 0.99). A linear relationship is observed between rectification ratio and OP concentration within the range of 10 pM to 50 nM. The limit of detection (LOD) as low as 7.9 pM is achieved. Additionally, other OP pesticides are utilized to evaluate the selectivity of the sensor. As illustrated in Fig. 4e, the rectification ratio exhibits a substantial change upon the addition of only profenofos. In contrast, no significant differences in the rectification ratio are observed when testing solutions containing naled, chloropyriphos, malathion, triazophos, prothiofos, or mixtures of these structural analogues of profenofos (at concentrations 50-fold higher than profenofos). The results indicate that the interference from these compounds is negligible. Consequently, the micron pore sensor demonstrates excellent selectivity.

Real sample analysis

To further investigate the practicality of this micron pore sensor in real aquatic settings, water samples collected from various environments are first spiked with profenofos. Given the significant variations in the sizes of rivers, natural lakes, and artificial lakes, as well as the composition of plant communities, the disturbances (such as humic substances and salt ions) present in the water samples differed accordingly. Therefore, water samples from the Yangtze River, Taihu Lake and Jinji Lake are chosen, which are representative sites in Suzhou, China (Figs. 5a-c). Water samples are filtered to remove impurity particles and prevent the channel from clogging before testing. Different concentrations of profenofos (100 pM, 500 pM, and 50 nM) are then added to these water samples for analysis. As illustrated in Fig. 5d, there are no significant differences among the water samples. The relative standard deviations (RSDs) are within 0.4% for 100 pM, 500 pM, and 50 nM profenofos. This result indicates that the micron pore sensor is reliable and demonstrates satisfactory accuracy for detecting profenofos in environmental waters.

(d) Detection of profenofos in different spiked real water samples. (e) Detection of profenofos residue in peeled and unpeeled apples. (f) The corresponding heatmap

The micron pore sensor is then used to analyze the residues of profenofos in fruits. Both peeled and unpeeled apples are selected for analysis, as the majority of pesticide residues in fruits are concentrated in the peel. A 100 nM solution of profenofos is prepared and sprayed evenly onto the surface of apples using a spray bottle to mimic typical pesticide application conditions. Apples treated with profenofos are left at room temperature for 12 h and subsequently divided into two groups: one group with the peel removed and the other untreated group. After juicing the apples, 5 µL of juice is taken for electrochemical testing. Using LSV, I-V curves are obtained, and the corresponding rectification ratios are calculated. The results presented in Fig. 5e indicate higher rectification ratios in the unpeeled apples. Following this, the concentration of profenofos in these samples is calculated based on the equation established in the previous section. When these values are summarized in a heat map, significantly higher concentrations of profenofos are observed in the unpeeled apples, with the difference being statistically significant (Fig. 5f). Furthermore, we have conducted a time-dependent study on profenofos residues on apples, revealing a half-life of approximately 8–9 days, with detectable residues persisting for up to two weeks (Figure S9). In summary, although profenofos on apples degrades slowly, effective residue reduction can be achieved through peeling. These findings demonstrate the satisfactory applicability of acrylamide-DNA hydrogel-filled micro pores for detecting OP residues in real sample matrices.

The universality of composite pores for electrochemical sensing

DNA possesses molecular recognition ability, stability, programmability, and ease of chemical modification, which renders it a versatile tool for constructing biosensors. Through the design of DNA sequences that recognize specific biomolecules, the detection range of the composite pore can be extended to encompass nucleic acids, proteins, glycans and a multitude of biomolecules. To evaluate its universality, we have combined Lock3 strand with hairpin S1 to construct an electrochemical sensor for target DNA detection (Figure S10a). Lock3 strand hybridizes with the complementary region of the target DNA, thereby inducing S1 to adopt a stable hairpin structure and initiating downstream signal amplification. The sensor demonstrates a LOD of 0.12 pM and excellent linearity over a concentration range from 0.5 pM to 10 nM (Figure S10b). These results demonstrate that composite-pore-based sensor, initially designed for OP detection, can be tailored for DNA detection through modifications of the target recognition sequence conjugated onto DNA hairpin S1. This approach further establishes a framework for developing sensors targeting proteins and other biomolecules.

Conclusion

In summary, we propose a composite biopore/solid-state pore that provides a stable reaction environment for analytes transportation within the pore. The acrylamide-DNA hydrogel, which functions as the biopore, can adjust the pore size through analyte-induced cascade reactions, thus changing the ion transport pathway and affecting ICR. This composite pore structure enables electrochemical detection in sample volumes less than 5 µL. Moreover, we have systematically investigated the structural changes of the hydrogel network induced by the strand displacement reaction and elucidated the ionic current rectification mechanism. The linker DNA of the hydrogel features an exposed viscous end, which facilitates the recognition of the target and the assembly of the fuel strand. The subsequent extension of the linker DNA results in altered ionic conduction properties of the acrylamide-DNA hydrogel. Unlike conventional detection strategies based on translocation signals, the ion rectification signals are less susceptible to noise interference from complex matrices. As an illustrative application, it is successfully employed to detect profenofos, achieving a LOD as low as 7.9 pM. Furthermore, the sensor proves effectiveness in real water samples and fruits.

The composite pore is anticipated to serve as an effective tool for investigating ICR and fluid transport in micro/nano devices. The detection range based on this composite pore can be readily expanded to include nucleic acids, proteins, glycans and a multitude of biomolecules through modifying different aptamers. This capability will facilitate the molecular assessment of trace chemicals in fields such as food safety analysis, environmental monitoring, and biomedical detection. With further enhancements, this method is poised to yield accurate in situ quantitative data in submicron structures (e.g., single cells, subcellular organelles), thus presenting a viable alternative to traditional solid-state nanopore electrodes.
