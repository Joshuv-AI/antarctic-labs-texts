Use of artificial intelligence in magnetic resonance imaging across the epileptic patient's journey: A meta‐analysis of four clinical applications
Authors: Judy Chen, Ella Sahlas, Yigu Zhou, Natalie Chen, Jim Xie, Farhan Wadia, Lorenzo Caciagli, Aristides Hadjinicolaou, Alexander G. Weil, Roy W. Dudley, Dewi V. Schrader, Andrea Bernasconi, Neda Bernasconi, Boris C. Bernhardt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe application of artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) promises to enhance and support clinical decision‐making in epilepsy. However, there currently lacks an appropriate assessment of clinical utility and study rigor of current AI/ML‐driven models that are targeted toward supporting decision‐making within the clinical workup in epilepsy.MethodsWe systematically reviewed and examined the ability of current AI/ML‐driven models in MRI across four main applications within the clinical workup(s) for epilepsy: (1) diagnosis, (2) temporal lobe epilepsy lateralization, (3) lesion (focal cortical dysplasia) localization, and (4) postsurgical outcome prediction. We additionally assessed the risk of bias for each study model. Studies that employed AI/ML classification models trained on any MRI modality or sequence type were selected for qualitative assessment; those reporting accuracy rates were subsequently included in the meta‐analysis.ResultsOf 3227 searched articles, we identified 159 studies (n = 26 732 participants) for qualitative evaluation and 127 studies (n = 20 456) for inclusion in the meta‐analysis. Our results reveal that AI/ML on MRI could accurately distinguish epilepsy patients from healthy controls (overall accuracy = .87, 95% confidence interval [CI] = .85–.89), lateralize temporal lobe epilepsy (.90, 95% CI = .87–.93), localize epileptogenic lesions (.82, 95% CI = .74–.87), and predict postsurgical seizure freedom (.83, 95% CI = .78–.87). However, systematic assessment indicated a very high risk of bias in the literature, suggestive of overly optimistic performance estimates.SignificanceAlthough our results support overall high accuracy of AI/ML models in epilepsy diagnostics and prognostics, the literature remains susceptible to bias in participant recruitment and validation methods. Furthermore, most models were limited by study architecture that demands strict adherence to nonstandard, highly specific data acquisition and processing protocols that cannot be easily deployed for clinical implementation. We encourage closer interdisciplinary collaboration between clinical and scientific groups to improve validation studies, and outline suggested recommendations for future study design, analysis, and reporting.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a common chronic neurological disorder, affecting >60 million people worldwide. 1 In one third of patients, seizure control cannot be achieved through pharmacotherapy. Considering the adverse effects of ongoing seizures and antiseizure medication on health, cognition, and psychosocial functioning, current clinical care guidelines recommend referral of these patients to specialized epilepsy units for comprehensive presurgical investigation. Randomized trials and retrospective studies have shown that surgical treatment consistently leads to higher rates of seizure freedom than continued medical therapy. 2 , 3 , 4 However, at present, only a subgroup of surgical candidates receives surgery each year, with an average wait of 20 years between the first seizure and epilepsy surgery. 5 , 6 , 7 Moreover, despite epilepsy surgery being a highly effective treatment to achieve seizure control and restore quality of life for pharmacoresistant patients, it is not failproof. Indeed, 30%–40% of patients who undergo surgery will experience seizures postoperatively. 8 , 9 These challenges motivate the need to improve diagnostic and prognostic methods.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has revolutionized both the evaluation and management of pharmacoresistant epilepsy, playing a pivotal role in the noninvasive detection and characterization of epileptogenic lesions. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 A lesion on MRI is a reliable indicator of the surgical target, and its surgical removal is a robust predictor for postoperative seizure freedom. Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) related to mesiotemporal alterations and epilepsy related to focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) remain prevalent diagnoses among pharmacoresistant patients, and they in particular benefit from advanced MRI imaging. Accurately lateralizing TLE and precisely localizing FCDs and other focal epilepsies are essential to successful postsurgical outcomes, yet both can be clinically challenging. In addition to use of suboptimal imaging protocols, these lesions may be subtle and can be easily overlooked 10 , 14 Artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) have been increasingly suggested to have potential to support key applications throughout the clinical workup: epilepsy diagnosis, TLE lateralization, lesion localization (including FCD localization), and prediction of postsurgical seizure freedom.

The application of AI/ML models has mainly been limited to specialized centers, and tools have not been translated broadly. Moreover, models have been somewhat heterogeneous in the choice of AI/ML algorithms, as well as with respect to MRI sequences, scanners, and patient cohorts. 15 , 16 , 17 As such, there is currently no clear understanding of how the wide variation in modeling strategies and imaging protocols affects model performance. So far, no study has systematically evaluated nor critically appraised the extant literature that employs AI/ML‐driven approaches in MRI of epilepsy.

The objectives for this systematic review and meta‐analysis were (1) to assess the performance and model architecture of current AI/ML models for four applications (diagnose epilepsy, lateralize TLE, localize the epileptic lesion with a specific focus on FCD detection, predict postsurgical outcomes) and (2) to examine study rigor by evaluating bias in the literature.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This review adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines and was registered on PROSPERO (Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews; CRD42023474405) prior to analysis. 18

Search strategy

We searched Ovid MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar from inception to January 1, 2025. Details of search terms, keywords, and medical search headings are provided in Table S1.

Study selection

All titles/abstracts and full‐text articles were independently screened by two reviewers (J.C. and Y.Z.). Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (J.X.). Publications using AI/ML classification methods on MRI‐based images/features reporting on at least one of the following applications were included: (1) diagnosis of epilepsy or differentiation between people with epilepsy and other populations, (2) TLE lateralization, (3) lesion localization, and (4) predicting postsurgical outcomes. Studies that did not report any one of the evaluation metrics (i.e., sensitivity/specificity, positive predictive value/negative predictive value, F1 score, AUROC, or raw accuracy rate), with <10 included patients, without full‐text publications, or without original data were excluded. A list of all included studies and their characteristics can be found in Table S2.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

Four reviewers (J.C., F.W., J.X., N.C.) independently extracted data in duplicate. Our primary summary measure for the meta‐analysis was classification percentage accuracy rate. We also extracted the following: (1) study characteristics (name of first and corresponding author, publication year, sample size), (2) demographic and baseline variables (patient and control cohorts' age, sex, duration and/or age of onset of epilepsy, pharmacoresistance status, postsurgical seizure freedom, MRI‐negative status), (3) data acquisition variables (MRI field strength, modality, data input), (4) AI/ML characteristics (feature selection process if applicable, type of algorithm), and (5) any evaluation metrics (sensitivity/specificity, positive predictive value/negative predictive value, F1 score, AUROC, or raw accuracy rate). Two authors were assigned to each article; any discrepancy was resolved through discussion. For studies that reported multiple applications with differing cohort groups (e.g., TLE lateralization of both MRI‐negative and MRI‐positive patients), all corresponding accuracy rates were included, and for studies that reported multiple accuracy rates across different algorithmic designs, the classification accuracy proportion of the best performing model was included; this resulted in a higher number of reported accuracy rates than the number of studies. Risk of bias (ROB) of studies was evaluated using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST), categorized across four domains (participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis). Domains with at least one signaling question judged as high risk were determined to be high risk, as opposed to low or unclear risk. 19 , 20 Each quality assessment was conducted by at least two independent researchers, and discrepancies were resolved via judgment by the third independent researcher (J.C., E.S., J.X.).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using metafor (version 4.4.0) in R (version 4.3.1, R Project for Statistical Computing). 21 We used metaprop, metareg, forest, funnel, metabias, and trimfill commands to conduct the meta‐analysis of proportions and meta‐regression analyses and to assess for publication bias. Due to multiple contributions of data points from certain publications, a multilevel meta‐analysis was also performed for comparison. Proportions were defined as the number of correct classification events of the total sample size. Included studies were further subgrouped by the four main classification aims. A generalized linear mixed‐effects model was used to pool logit‐transformed proportions data, and Cochrane Q and I 2 tests of heterogeneity were conducted to assess for heterogeneity between studies and subgroups. 22 , 23 , 24 Presence of publication bias was evaluated using Peter test and the trim and fill method. 25 , 26 Meta‐regression analyses were conducted to assess the moderating effects of MRI modality, classification method, publication date, and cohort size on the pooled estimate using linear mixed‐effect models and the Wald test. To further explore potential heterogeneity of results, we also conducted subgroup meta‐analyses stratified based on age (adult vs. pediatric), drug‐resistance, MRI findings (negative vs. positive), and magnet strength (3 T vs. 1.5 T).

Lateralizing TLE

Thirty‐three of 37 studies reported a total of 38 accuracy rates that were used in the meta‐analysis. The mean accuracy rate was .90 (95% CI = .87–.93, n = 1071 [patients]/2396; Figure 2). SVM and discriminant analysis (DA) were the most common algorithm types used (n = 20, 13), and the most common MRI sequence used was T1w (n = 16).

Moderator and subgroup analysis

Meta‐regression

In meta‐regression analyses, year of study publication significantly moderated overall pooled accuracy rate (Table 1) but only explained .8% of the between‐study variance (Q m = 5.97, df = 1, p = .015), with further effect reduction when assessed across separate predictor aims (Table S6.1–4). Cohort size moderated pooled estimates of classification accuracy for diagnostic and postsurgical outcome predictions and were negatively correlated in the mixed‐effects linear model (Figure 3A; r = −.45, −.51; p < .01). MRI modality explained 16.5% of the variance in pooled classification accuracy proportions and trended toward moderating pooled estimates (Q m = 9.073, df = 4, p = .059). However, there were few overall differences between uni‐ and multimodality or between any single modality choice (Figure 3C; p < .001). Although classification algorithms moderated pooled accuracies of diagnostic and postsurgical outcomes only (p < .05), they explained 69% of variance within the overall meta‐regression. SVM and LR were additionally associated with the highest accuracy rates (Figure 3B), but differences between algorithm types were not significant in the overall or any of the four predictor aims.

Performance of AI/ML‐driven MRI models in subgroup populations

Subgroup analyses were conducted on studies stratified by age (i.e., adult and pediatric populations), clinical characteristics such as pharmacoresistance and MRI‐negative status, and magnet strength during data acquisition (Figure S3.1–6). There were no pediatric cohorts among lateralization studies. Pooled accuracy rates showed little difference between subgroup and overall cohort results.

Publication bias

Funnel plots were asymmetric (Figure S1), and the Peter test was significant for diagnostic studies (slope = 27.81, p = .008). All four predictor outcomes underwent the trim and fill procedure, adding 40 total studies that slightly reduced pooled estimates of classification accuracy for diagnosis (.85, 95% CI = .83–.88), lateralization (.87, 95% CI = .84–.90), localization (.78, 95% CI = .69–.85), and postsurgical outcome prediction (.82, 95% CI = .79–.85; Figure S2.1–4). Peter tests were not significant after trim and fill (Table S5).

ROB assessment

All papers had an overall high ROB when evaluated across PROBAST's four domains: participants, predictors, analysis, and outcome (Figure 4). Participant bias was reported at 87% (n = 67/76) for diagnostic studies, 64% (n = 24/37) for lateralization studies, 88% (n = 32/36) for localization studies, and 69% (n = 18/26) for postsurgical outcome studies. High participant bias was attributed to recruitment strategies in the work. Patients should have been recruited prospectively as opposed to retrospectively after definitive clinical diagnosis; for example, in distinguishing epilepsy patients from controls, MRI data should be acquired during the diagnostic investigation. All studies involved in localization or lateralization had high predictor bias, while 88% (23/26) of studies predicting postsurgical outcome and 89% (67/76) of studies evaluating diagnostic value had high predictor bias. Similarly, all (76/76) diagnostic studies had high analysis bias, which was also present in 97% (36/37) of lateralization studies, 83% (30/36) of localization studies, and 84% (22/26) of surgical outcome studies. The high predictor and analysis risks of bias can be attributed to retrospective assessment of disease diagnoses, limited cohort sizes (heuristically defined as <100 total participants with a minimum of 50 in each classification cohort), and lack of an independent test cohort to validate performance metrics. Finally, the ROB for evaluating outcomes was low in postsurgical prediction studies (0% [0/26]), as seizure freedom is a clearly defined outcome, but remained high for diagnosis (100% [73/73]), localization (86% [31/36]), and lateralization (97% [36/37]) applications. PROBAST elements that were not applicable to the study in question were noted and excluded from the overall judgment.

DISCUSSION

The advent of AI/ML methods has catalyzed progressive interest toward its application on multimodal MRI to support and enhance epilepsy diagnosis and management. Our systematic review and meta‐analysis synthesizes this rapidly growing literature (159 total publications with n = 9928 [patients]/26 732 total participants) and highlights key avenues to clinical translation. Our review shows that pooled accuracy of AI/ML models remained relatively high overall, was moderately associated with MRI modality, cohort size, and to a lesser extent, classification algorithm, but showed significant improvements over time across the four main clinical aims. Despite promising results, subsequent systematic ROB assessment based on the PROBAST framework also highlighted noteworthy limitations in previous study design and analysis across the literature, which may lead to inflated performance estimates relative to prospective “real world” settings.

Among study design choices, sample size was a key factor for diagnostic and postsurgical outcome prediction aims. Smaller sample sizes had greater variability in reported accuracy, particularly due to the larger percentage increments per correctly classified patients and comparisons to features derived from other imaging modalities, demonstrating inferiority to their combinations (e.g., fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography combined with T1w MRI). Larger cohorts, nonetheless, were highly correlated with reduced model accuracy across the four predictor aims. This may be reflective of overfitting, particularly when more lenient classifier evaluation schemes are used. Although small sample settings thus relate to ROB, these findings also indicate a prevailing issue regarding current barriers to patient recruitment. Patients often wait years if not decades prior to referral to tertiary care centers for surgical evaluation and intervention, whereby they may also participate in research studies with generally more optimized, high‐quality MRI sequences. 15 This inadvertently reduced patient selection may fail to account for effects from key biological factors such as age and sex. Furthermore, it may bias results due to effects from disease duration, medication, and preexisting epilepsy‐related impairment, consequently limiting model generalizability and lowering confidence in performance. In addition to standardizing MRI investigations and bringing these to earlier disease stages, we propose that the clinical pipelines should be streamlined to improve the referral process from primary to tertiary care centers, improving the generalizability of research studies to the general population of people with epilepsy. 10

Choice of MRI modality appeared to moderate model performance more than choice of classifier model consistently across predictor aims, suggesting that importance of data quality supersedes refining classification techniques. Similar sentiments have been echoed previously, where increasingly sophisticated techniques failed to outperform simpler models in diagnostic and laterality classifications when limited by image granularity. 15 Nonetheless, MRI multimodality did not seem to markedly influence accuracy in our systematic assessment despite notable work and the clear clinical consensus that suggest otherwise. 27 , 28 , 29 The apparent lack of significant effects in the current work may be due to cross‐study heterogeneity that cannot be fully accounted for by meta‐analysis techniques. Future work to systematically compare classifier or MRI sequence(s) combinations on accuracy rates, particularly within studies, will be needed to more sensitively reveal speculated advantages.

The considerable heterogeneity observed across studies reflects substantial variability in study design beyond sample size or MRI modality; algorithm class accounted for the largest proportion of the variance within the meta‐regression framework, yet no single algorithm dominated in performance. These results suggest that high heterogeneity may be primarily driven by differences in modeling strategies, and to a lesser extent, dataset and acquisition methods, but may not have exerted as much influence on pooled accuracy results. Overall, our results motivate rigorous within‐study comparisons of MRI modalities, preprocessing techniques, and algorithm types to improve cross‐study comparability and ensure sufficiently large and representative cohort sizes to account for generalizability and clinical feasibility.

ROB evaluation using PROBAST has identified several sources to address. First, the timing of patient recruitment and data acquisition would ideally be prospective. Most retrospective datasets include the same MRI data used for diagnosis or lesion identification. However, using the same data to define the ground truth (e.g., left vs. right TLE, epileptogenic lesion) as a predictor for models could lead to inflated accuracy rates, particularly given that MRI findings are, and should be, integral to epilepsy diagnosis 10 , 30 ; this was an especially common cause for high predictor bias among most studies, and can be mitigated by using several independent reference standards independent of MRI. These involve including only patients with long‐term Engel IA outcomes after surgery and/or histological verification. On the other hand, some studies have limited the analysis to MRI‐negative‐only cohorts. However, application of each of these criteria and their combinations may reduce sample size, thus challenging the feasibility of conducting large‐scale assessments. These standards also present their own set of challenges, as rates of seizure freedom are not static over time, and histopathological assessments may similarly vary depending on the resected slide. 9 , 31 As no perfect “ground truth” exists, a multipronged approach to define a good reference standard is recommended to strengthen the reliability of reported results. Second, some patients recruited at tertiary care centers may have had decades‐long disease durations, given intrinsic challenges in diagnosing pharmacoresistant epilepsy early in the disease and shortcomings in referral patterns. As cortical and subcortical changes have been shown to progress over the disease duration in many patients, 32 , 33 , 34 long durations may bias models to identify features most affected long‐term as opposed to features that may be most discriminatory upon earlier evaluation. 23 , 24 , 25 Limited sample sizes available at single centers can result in repeated use from patient data across studies, even when these are pooled via multicenter retrospective consortium initiatives afterward. Third, many studies were at risk for overestimation of model performance, because they did not account for key biological and sociodemographic covariates such as age and sex, use external datasets for independent validation, nor comprehensively report model performance metrics. These sources of bias can be addressed concomitantly with expanding cohort size and streamlining the clinical referral pipeline as mentioned previously. 29 MRI data from patients referred earlier onto their diagnostic journey would be ideal for diagnostic classification (i.e., FCD identification, TLE lateralization) and surgical outcome prediction. Increased cohort sizes will allow for greater patient heterogeneity and normative modeling of biological covariates. 35 , 36 , 37 By addressing these aforementioned recommendations, more studies can leverage this modality for predicting pharmacoresistance in new onset patients given its ubiquitous use during clinical diagnosis and strong associations between imaging abnormalities and development of pharmacoresistance. 38 Finally, studies are encouraged to comprehensively report performance metrics (i.e., sensitivity/specificity, precision/recall, overall accuracy, and AUROC) for better between‐study comparisons.

Some limitations of the current study include possible performance overestimation, particularly for models designed to localize the epileptogenic lesion. Only including the highest performing model architecture and results in the meta‐analysis could lead to overoptimism of AI/ML techniques compared to traditional methods. Furthermore, lesion localization performance was defined on a subject level instead of voxel‐ or surface‐based overlap with gold standard radiologist mapping and did not count false positive findings, which may further inflate the true efficacy of such models. Moreover, false positives or little overlap with the true lesion could even reduce efficiency of clinical providers having to rule out false positive findings and cause confusion when having to delineate obscure, MRI‐negative lesions. We recommend such work to report precise spatial localization metrics, such as the Dice similarity coefficient or the Jaccard index, as well as model specificity to prove superior performance. For all clinical outcomes, important MRI acquisition parameters, such as voxel size, repetition time/echo time, field strengths, and manufacturers, were not included as a source of heterogeneity in our study, which could have either masked possible technical contributors to performance variability or somewhat confounded meta‐regression results; it is possible that significant improvements in model performances across time may have simply reflected advances in MRI hardware. Nevertheless, despite possible performance inflation, our results still allow for reasonable comparisons between subgroup findings, data features, and AI/ML techniques to guide future methodological development and standardized evaluation frameworks.

Promising models designed for both lesion detection in focal epilepsy and TLE lateralization have attempted to build toward clinical feasibility and interpretability for physician end users. 15 , 16 , 26 These studies included large multisite cohorts, rigorously tested for model performance, and have considered leveraging data derived from standard MRI protocols, yet remain underutilized. Highly specific requirements for MRI sequences, complex data processing pipelines, and imperfect harmonization of data across centers may limit ease of applicability for widespread adoption. Thus, future efforts should focus not only on continuing prospective model validation but also on collaborating closely with clinical end users and industry partners to identify optimal strategies for integration. For example, few AI/ML‐driven models have been open access, reproduced, and validated by other groups beyond academic centers. Health care providers could be included to assess clinical usability and workflow integration and to ensure translation to meaningful improvements in patient outcomes. Importantly, potentially esoteric, highly technical AI/ML code designed for clinical application should be deployed as intuitive, user‐friendly tools that integrate with existing medical platforms. Our results also encourage physicians to adhere to the International League Against Epilepsy‐recommended and standardized MRI protocols and research groups to leverage these clinically acquired scans for external, prospective validation. 10 The ubiquitous use and accessibility of routine clinical scans, together with increasingly available low‐field MRI acquisitions, 39 provides a critical opportunity for real‐world testing and should be strategically leveraged to advance broad clinical implementation.

Clinical applications of AI/ML in epilepsy have already demonstrated impressive advancements, and future work to mitigate the remaining sources of bias will help clinically integrate models to directly support patients. Given that current clinical guidelines do not solely use MRI for decision‐making but interpret findings holistically with other diagnostic tools (e.g., semiology, electroencephalography, positron emission tomography), the integration of AI/ML models applied to MRI with these other modalities may also serve as a future research direction for this field. Nevertheless, future studies must prioritize robust study design and move toward clinical integration by emphasizing ease of clinical integration, interpretability, and external site validation. We encourage closer collaborations within the health care system and between care centers and research groups for wider patient recruitment, and we emphasize the need for inclusive, large, multicenter datasets that reflect real‐world clinical diversity.
