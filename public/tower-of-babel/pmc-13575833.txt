Fourteen-Day Amoxicillin- or Tetracycline-Containing Bismuth Quadruple Therapy versus 14-Day Metronidazole-Based Triple Therapy as the Treatment for Clarithromycin-Resistant Helicobacter pylori Infection: A Multicenter Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Myeongseok Koh, Cheol Woong Choi, Dae Gon Ryu, Seung Jung Yu, Sung Eun Kim, Kyoungwon Jung, Bong Eun Lee, Moon Won Lee, Dong Chan Joo, Jin Lee, Ra Ri Cha, Sam Ryong Jee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsCombination therapy comprising a proton pump inhibitor (PPI), amoxicillin, and metronidazole (PAM) is used to treat clarithromycin-resistant Helicobacter pylori in the Republic of Korea, but eradication rates are decreasing due to an increasing incidence of clarithromycin resistance. We compared PAM, bismuth compounds plus PAM (PAM-B), and the combination of PPI, bismuth compounds, metronidazole, and tetracycline (PBMT) to determine whether PAM-B can achieve an eradication rate higher than that of PAM and comparable to that of PBMT.MethodsThis prospective multicenter study enrolled patients with clarithromycin-resistant H. pylori infections in the Busan and Gyeongsangnam-do of the Republic of Korea between December 2022 and February 2024.ResultsIn the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, the eradication rate was significantly lower in the PAM group (68.2%) than in the PAM-B group (84.8%, p=0.024), whereas the difference from the PBMT group (81.8%) was not significant (p=0.070). The rate remained lowest in the PAM group (75.4%) in the per-protocol (PP) analysis (PAM-B, 96.5%, p=0.001; PBMT, 94.6%, p=0.004). Eradication rates were comparable between the PAM-B and PBMT groups in both the ITT (p=0.640) and PP analyses (p=0.633). Nausea and vomiting were significantly less frequent in the PAM-B group than in the PBMT group (6.8% vs 25.0%; p=0.007). Severe adverse events were rare, and all symptoms resolved after treatment discontinuation.ConclusionsPAM-B yielded eradication rates higher than those of PAM and comparable to those of PBMT, with no significant increase in the incidence of adverse events, suggesting the potential of PAM-B as a first-line treatment for clarithromycin-resistant H. pylori infection. Further large-scale studies are needed to validate these results. Registered retrospectively with the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS; KCT0012265).

INTRODUCTION

Helicobacter pylori infection is a common infectious disease worldwide.1 The prevalence of this infection is particularly high in East Asia, which includes the Republic of Korea.2 The seropositivity of H. pylori in the Republic of Korea is approximately 51%.3 H. pylori, classified as a group 1 carcinogen, infects the gastric mucosa and is associated with conditions such as chronic gastritis, peptic ulcers, and, in the long term, gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma, gastric dysplasia, and gastric adenocarcinoma.4 To treat and prevent these various conditions, clinical guidelines recommend eradication therapy for H. pylori infections.5,6

Eradication therapy usually combines strong acid suppressants—such as proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) or potassium-competitive acid blockers—with antibiotics like amoxicillin, clarithromycin, and metronidazole. In the Republic of Korea, current guidelines recommend empirical treatment using the standard triple therapy of a PPI, amoxicillin, and clarithromycin.5

The eradication rates for H. pylori infections are decreasing as clarithromycin resistance rates continue to rise.7 The eradication rate in the Republic of Korea has been steadily declining to approximately 70% over the past few decades.8,9 Attempts to confirm clarithromycin resistance using dual-priming oligonucleotide polymerase chain reaction (DPO-PCR) techniques aim to implement tailored treatments based on the resistance status and enhance eradication rates.5 According to the 2020 Korean guidelines for H. pylori treatment, representative therapies for H. pylori eradication in the presence of clarithromycin resistance include PPI, amoxicillin, and metronidazole (PAM) for 7 days and PPI, bismuth compounds, metronidazole, and tetracycline (PBMT) for 10–14 days; however, each regimen has its own disadvantages.5 The PAM regimen has a low success rate in eradicating the infection,10 and the PBMT regimen poses a risk of developing new resistance to the antibiotic used in the therapy (tetracycline), with a relatively high rate of side effects;5 therefore, alternative treatment regimens are necessary. Among various alternative treatments being considered, bismuth, which is used for gastrointestinal disorders, has shown promise. Previous reports have shown that bismuth used in combination with the eradication therapy overcomes resistance.11-13

This study aimed to introduce a regimen incorporating bismuth into the PAM regimen (PAM-B) for empirical first-line treatment of clarithromycin-resistant H. pylori and compare its effects with those of the PAM and PBMT regimens (conventional quadruple therapy using tetracycline).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study population and design

This prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled trial evaluated and compared the eradication rates of the PAM-B, PAM, and PBMT regimens as first-line therapies for H. pylori infection. From December 2022 to February 2024, patients diagnosed with H. pylori infection via endoscopy were recruited from seven tertiary care hospitals (Dong-A University Hospital, Pusan National University Yangsan Hospital, Busan Paik Hospital, Kosin University College of Medicine, Pusan National University Hospital, Haeundae Paik Hospital, and Gyeongsang National University Changwon Hospital) located in Busan and Gyeongsangnam-do, Republic of Korea. Patients between the ages of 18 and 80 who were confirmed to have H. pylori infection through methods such as histology, rapid urease testing, serology, or the urea breath test and underwent DPO-PCR to assess clarithromycin resistance were considered eligible for inclusion in the study. The following patients were excluded from the study: (1) patients with previous H. pylori eradication treatment; (2) patients with a history of gastric surgery; (3) patients with severe liver or kidney impairment; (4) patients with a history of antibiotic allergy; (5) patients who had taken antibiotics within 4 weeks of study commencement; and (6) pregnant or lactating patients.

Eligible patients were randomly assigned to the PAM-B, PAM, or PBMT groups using a computer-generated randomization table for eradication treatment following a 1:1:1 allocation ratio. Data were collected from medical records, including treatment outcomes.

2. Randomization and allocation

Eligible patients were randomly assigned to the PAM, PAM-B, or PBMT groups in a 1:1:1 ratio using a computer-generated randomization sequence. Treatment assignments were allocated to consecutive numbers from 1 to 198 before study initiation. At each participating center, consecutively enrolled patients were assigned the next available randomization number in the order of enrollment. No block randomization or stratification factors were applied. Owing to the nature of the eradication regimens, neither participants nor investigators were blinded to treatment allocation.

3. Diagnosis of H. pylori clarithromycin resistance

To diagnose H. pylori infection and determine the presence of clarithromycin resistance, DPO-PCR was performed using DNA extracted from gastric tissue samples. During endoscopic examination, gastric tissue samples were collected from the antrum and body of the stomach. PCR was performed to amplify a segment of the 23S rRNA gene to identify A2142G and/or A2143G mutations associated with clarithromycin resistance.

4. Outcomes and confirmation of H. pylori eradication

The primary outcome of this study was the difference in eradication success rates among the PAM, PAM-B, and PBMT treatment groups, while the secondary outcomes included differences in adverse events related to eradication therapy and patient compliance during the respective treatment periods. H. pylori eradication success was evaluated using the 13C-urea breath test (UBiT-IR300; Otsuka Electronics Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), performed at least 4 weeks following the completion of the treatment. After fasting for 4 hours, participants ingested a 100 mg tablet of 13C-urea (UBiTkit; Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) accompanied by 100 mL of water. Breath samples were collected before and 20 minutes after tablet ingestion. An exhaled 13CO2 value below 2.5‰ represented successful H. pylori eradication.14

5. Eradication regimens

Following the random allocation of all patients into the PAM, PAM-B, and PBMT treatment groups, specific medications were administered based on the assigned regimen. The PAM regimen consisted of 20 mg rabeprazole and 1 g amoxicillin, each administered twice daily, and 500 mg metronidazole taken thrice daily. The PAM-B regimen consisted of the PAM regimen with the addition of 300 mg of tripotassium dicitrato bismuthate (120 mg as Bi2O3) four times daily. The PBMT regimen included administering 20 mg rabeprazole twice daily, 500 mg metronidazole three times daily, and 500 mg tetracycline combined with 300 mg tripotassium dicitrato bismuthate (120 mg as Bi2O3) four times daily. All patients were treated for 14 days with their respective regimens.

6. Assessment of drug compliance and adverse events associated with treatment

The patients underwent a urea breath test 4 weeks after completing the eradication therapy and reported drug compliance and any drug-related adverse events. All participants received instructions on documenting any adverse events and tracking their medication adherence. Drug compliance was assessed based on the consumption of the prescribed medication, with noncompliance defined as the consumption of <80% of the total medication, determined by the number of remaining pills. Adverse events were classified as mild, moderate, or severe based on treatment adherence and interference with daily activities, as commonly defined in H. pylori eradication trials.15,16 Mild adverse events were defined as drug adherence above 80% with no impact on daily functioning; moderate adverse events as adherence below 80% with interference in daily activities; and severe adverse events as compliance below 50% or adverse effects limiting daily activities. Common adverse events included bitter taste, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, urticaria, and changes in stool or urine color.

7. Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

In previous studies, the eradication rates of PAM and PBMT were reported as 76% and 95%, respectively.17 To ensure adequate statistical power (α=0.05, β=0.20) to confirm the superiority of the PAM-B group, the minimum number of participants required was 54 for comparison with the PAM group and 59 for comparison with the PBMT group. Considering a dropout rate of 10%, each group required a minimum of 66 participants; thus, the calculated target sample size was 198 participants. Despite the dropout rate being slightly higher than expected, a sufficient sample size was ultimately retained to adequately evaluate the primary study hypothesis. The comparison between PAM-B and PBMT was performed to assess comparative efficacy. Treatment efficacy regarding eradication rates was examined for all groups using both intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses. All randomly assigned patients were included in the ITT analysis (n=198), in which patients lost to follow-up were counted as treatment failures. Because follow-up data were unavailable for these patients, baseline characteristics and adverse events were analyzed in the 178 patients who returned for follow-up assessment (PAM-B, n=59; PAM, n=59; PBMT, n=60). The PP analysis further excluded patients whose medication adherence was <80%, leaving 170 patients (PAM-B, n=57; PAM, n=57; PBMT, n=56). The Wilson score method was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test without continuity correction. Continuous variables were assessed using the Student t-test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

8. Ethics

All participants were fully informed about the nature of the study and given the option to decline participation. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the institutional review boards of all participating hospitals (Dong-A University Hospital: DAUHIRB-22-257, Pusan National University Yangsan Hospital: 04-2022-048, Busan Paik Hospital: 2022-10-026-005, Kosin University College of Medicine: 2022-10-019, Pusan National University Hospital: 2302-022-123, Haeundae Paik Hospital: HPIRB 2022-11-009-002, and Gyeongsang National University Changwon Hospital: GNUCH 2023-01-008). This study was retrospectively registered in the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS), a WHO-recognized primary registry, under the registration number KCT0012265. In addition, although this was a prospective multicenter randomized trial, it was not registered in a public clinical trial registry prior to patient enrollment, limiting transparency regarding prespecified outcomes and analyses. All study procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines established by the relevant institutional and national review boards, as well as the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent revisions.

RESULTS

1. Participant characteristics

Between December 2022 and February 2024, 198 patients were enrolled in this study and were randomly allocated to the PAM (n=66), PAM-B (n=66), and PBMT (n=66) groups. There were no significant differences in the clinical characteristics of patients (Table 1).

Assessment of eradication success was not performed for a number of patients owing to loss to follow-up after treatment: seven patients in the PAM-B group, seven patients in the PAM group, and six patients in the PBMT group. Failure to consume >80% of the prescribed medication was observed in two patients in the PAM-B group, two patients in the PAM group, and four patients in the PBMT group. In the ITT analysis, 66 patients from each group were included, for a total of 198 patients. In the PP analysis, 57 patients from the PAM-B group, 57 from the PAM group, and 56 from the PBMT group were included, for a total of 170 patients. Fig. 1 illustrates the patient enrollment and follow-up processes throughout the study.

2. Compliance and loss to follow-up

Loss to follow-up occurred primarily because of patients not returning for follow-up urea breath testing or being unavailable owing to personal circumstances unrelated to treatment. Noncompliance was mainly attributed to gastrointestinal discomfort, forgetfulness, or difficulty adhering to the prescribed dosing schedule. The distribution of noncompliance reasons and loss to follow-up was similar across all three groups. Baseline demographic or clinical characteristics did not differ significantly between patients who completed the study and those who were lost to follow-up or excluded from the PP analysis.

3. Overall eradication rates according to PAM, PAM-B, and PBMT regimens

In the ITT analysis, the eradication success rate was lowest in the PAM group (45/66, 68.2%; 95% CI, 56.2% to 78.2%). Compared with PAM, the eradication rate was significantly higher in the PAM-B group (56/66, 84.8%; 95% CI, 74.1% to 91.3%; p=0.024), whereas the difference from the PBMT group (54/66, 81.8%; 95% CI, 70.7% to 89.2%) did not reach statistical significance (p=0.070). No statistically significant difference was observed in the eradication rates between the PAM-B and PBMT groups (84.8% vs 81.8%; absolute difference, 2.9 percentage points; 95% CI, −11.8 to 17.6 percentage points; p=0.640) (Fig. 2). In the PP analysis, the eradication success rate was also significantly lower in the PAM group (43/57, 75.4%; 95% CI, 62.8% to 85.0%) than in the PAM-B (55/57, 96.5%; 95% CI, 87.9% to 99.1%; p=0.001) and PBMT groups (53/56, 94.6%; 95% CI, 85.4% to 98.3%; p=0.004). The eradication rates were also comparable in this analysis between the PAM-B and PBMT groups (96.5% vs 94.6%; absolute difference, 1.9 percentage points; 95% CI, −7.4 to 11.2; p=0.633) (Fig. 3).

In both analyses, the eradication rates of PAM-B were comparable to those of PBMT and were significantly higher than those of PAM.

4. Adverse events

Adverse events were reported in 27.1% (16/59) of patients in the PAM-B group, compared with 44.1% (26/59) in the PAM group (p=0.055) and 40.0% (24/60) in the PBMT group (p=0.137) (Table 2). Among the total of 66 patients who experienced adverse events, two in the PAM-B group (diarrhea, nausea, and/or vomiting), one in the PAM group (gastric soreness), and three in the PBMT group (diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting) showed severe adverse reactions. These six patients were classified as non-compliant because their medication consumption was <80%. Their adverse event symptoms disappeared after discontinuation of the eradication medications and administration of symptom-control treatment. None of the six patients required hospitalization. Nausea and vomiting were significantly less frequent in the PAM-B group than in the PBMT group (4/59, 6.8% vs 15/60, 25.0%; absolute difference, −18.2 percentage points; 95% CI, −32.4 to −4.0 percentage points; p=0.007). With respect to mild adverse events, the incidence was significantly lower in the PAM-B group than in the PAM group (14/59, 23.7% vs 24/59, 40.7%; absolute difference, −17.0 percentage points; 95% CI, −33.8 to −0.2 percentage points; p=0.049). DISCUSSION

This study was motivated by the potential of bismuth to overcome antibiotic-resistant strains and aimed to demonstrate the efficacy of the PAM-B regimen as an initial treatment method for patients with H. pylori infections resistant to clarithromycin. The primary objective was to prove that the PAM-B regimen was as effective as the PBMT regimen and offered a higher eradication rate than the PAM regimen. Furthermore, this study maximized the eradication rates by extending the treatment duration to 14 days instead of the standard 7 days.

In the Republic of Korea, the 7-day PAM regimen is recommended for the treatment of clarithromycin-resistant H. pylori;5 however, the resistance rate to metronidazole has been an issue with the PAM regimen, and the reported eradication rates are not sufficiently high, necessitating an alternative treatment regimen.10 A study in the Republic of Korea reported insufficient eradication rates with 7-day PAM therapy in patients with clarithromycin resistance (55.4% [51/92] in the ITT analysis and 66.2% [51/77] in the PP analysis), suggesting the need to increase the dosage of metronidazole and extend the treatment duration.18 The reason metronidazole is not sufficiently effective in treating H. pylori strains resistant to clarithromycin is the high rate (25.2%) of concurrent resistance to metronidazole in cases of clarithromycin resistance.19

Because of the low eradication success rate of the 7-day PAM regimen, the PBMT regimen is also used as an initial treatment for patients with clarithromycin-resistant H. pylori infection.5,20 This regimen is reported to have a high success rate of approximately 90%, attributed to the low resistance rate to tetracycline.19 Combining various antibiotics can increase the eradication success rate; however, this approach is often associated with common side effects.19 In addition, there are concerns about the development of resistance to tetracycline, especially in cases where treatment fails, making it difficult to choose salvage therapy options in the future. Tetracycline is known to be associated with poor medication adherence owing to its toxicity;5 therefore, it is necessary to find non-antibiotic alternatives that can improve the eradication success rate of the standard therapy instead of administering new antibiotics. Another treatment option is extending the duration of the regimen. Numerous studies have reported differences in the eradication success rates based on treatment duration, which is a trend that is also mentioned in the 2020 Korean guidelines for H. pylori.5

Bismuth compounds have long been used to treat gastrointestinal disorders21,22 and are commonly incorporated into H. pylori eradication regimens. Despite its antibacterial activity, bismuth is not classified as an antibiotic and has limited efficacy as monotherapy;23 rather, it likely enhances eradication outcomes by potentiating the effects of antibiotics and helping to overcome antibiotic resistance.11-13 The proposed mechanisms include disruption of bacterial cell wall integrity, inhibition of essential enzymatic and metabolic processes, and suppression of virulence factors, although the exact mechanisms remain incompletely understood.11,21,24,25

Our results revealed that the PAM-B regimen outperformed the PAM regimen, with eradication rates of 84.8% and 68.2% in the ITT analysis, and 96.5% and 75.4% in the PP analysis, respectively, for clarithromycin-resistant H. pylori infection. The generally accepted thresholds for successful eradication are at least 80% and 90% in ITT and PP analyses, respectively. PAM consistently exhibited lower eradication rates in both analyses, suggesting that it may be suboptimal as a first-line option for clarithromycin-resistant infection. In contrast, PAM-B achieved eradication rates exceeding these thresholds, justifying treatment initiation with PAM-B and its potential clinical utility. The main difference between the PAM-B and PBMT regimens is the use of amoxicillin instead of tetracycline, which is considered a last-resort antibiotic for treating H. pylori infections. Given its low resistance rate, tetracycline remains an option for salvage therapy when other treatment methods fail, ultimately aiding in achieving final eradication success.

Bismuth compounds are insoluble in water and have a very low absorption rate, resulting in a low incidence of side effects.11 The common side effects reported with bismuth use include a metallic taste, nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, dizziness, and changes in stool color.26 Bismuth is known to be safe and well-tolerated in H. pylori eradication.26 Our results indicated that the frequency of adverse effects was comparable between the bismuth- and non-bismuth-based regimens.

Although the combination of amoxicillin and metronidazole alone may not be sufficient to overcome antibiotic resistance in H. pylori infections that are resistant to clarithromycin, incorporating bismuth can significantly enhance the success rate of eradication by overcoming antibiotic resistance. Despite the additional use of bismuth, there was no apparent increase in the incidence of significant adverse effects, which suggests that a high success rate can be achieved without the need for tetracycline; thus, the use of bismuth has the potential to improve eradication treatment without unnecessarily increasing antibiotic resistance. Notably, despite the addition of bismuth, the overall adverse event incidence was not higher with PAM-B than with PAM. This may be explained by the fact that bismuth is not an antibiotic and is generally well tolerated, with a relatively low propensity to induce gastrointestinal adverse effects. In contrast, most adverse events observed in the PAM group were mild gastrointestinal symptoms, which may be influenced by individual susceptibility rather than a specific drug effect. In addition, the total number of adverse events was small, and most between-group differences were not statistically significant. Therefore, the observed difference in adverse event rates should be interpreted with caution.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, we did not perform H. pylori culture or antimicrobial susceptibility tests and identified resistance to clarithromycin only through DPO-PCR testing; therefore, we were unable to determine resistance to other antibiotics. However, culture tests for H. pylori are not widely utilized in clinical practice owing to several limitations, including strain variability across regions, technically demanding culture conditions, and a lack of standardized methods.27 To ensure accurate isolation of strains, PPIs must be discontinued for at least 2 weeks and antibiotics for at least 4 weeks prior to testing. Additionally, biopsy specimens should be processed as quickly as possible. Although the sensitivity of culture testing is relatively low (generally ≤73%), it offers the advantage of comprehensive clarithromycin resistance evaluation.28 Accordingly, in the present study, clarithromycin resistance was assessed using DPO-PCR, reflecting real-world clinical practice in settings where culture-based antimicrobial susceptibility testing is not routinely available. This approach may not fully capture phenotypic resistance patterns but enhances the practical relevance and everyday clinical applicability of our findings. Second, we did not investigate H. pylori virulence factors (vacA and cagA). Infections caused by strains positive for vacA s1 and cagA are more amenable to eradication therapy than infections caused by vacA s2 and cagA-negative strains. Third, the seven participating hospitals are located only in Busan and Gyeongsangnam-do, Republic of Korea; thus, to overcome this limitation, a nationwide multicenter study is required. Because this study focused solely on Korean patients, the results may not reflect similar outcomes across different regions and ethnicities. Fourth, the small number of participants included in the study necessitates further research with larger patient populations. Although this study was not registered in a public clinical trial registry prior to patient enrollment, it has since been retrospectively registered in CRIS (KCT0012265). Future prospective randomized studies should be registered in a public database prior to enrollment to ensure full transparency. Fifth, although treatment effects may vary according to center or patient characteristics such as age, sex, smoking status, or indication, this study was not powered to perform formal center-based or subgroup analyses. The relatively small number of participants per center and subgroup may have resulted in unstable estimates. Further studies addressing all of these limitations are necessary to validate our findings.

The strengths of this study include its prospective, multicenter randomized design and the enrollment of patients with molecularly confirmed clarithromycin resistance, reflecting real-world clinical practice. Nevertheless, the relatively limited sample size and regional recruitment warrant cautious data interpretation. Further large-scale studies are needed to validate the effects of PAM-B as a first-line regimen.

In conclusion, in patients with clarithromycin-resistant H. pylori infection, the 14-day PAM-B regimen demonstrated significantly higher eradication efficacy than the PAM regimen using the same antibiotics and efficacy comparable to that of the PBMT regimen containing tetracycline. Thus, PAM-B may effectively overcome antibiotic resistance while potentially reducing the risk of further antibiotic resistance and may be considered a viable first-line treatment option. However, confirmation through larger, nationwide randomized studies is required before firm recommendations can be established.
