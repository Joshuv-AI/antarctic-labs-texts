Surface-Initiated Thiol–Ene Click Polymerization for Fabricating 3D Cross-Linked Chiral Composite Membranes with Enhanced Enantioselectivity and Permeability
Authors: Yu Zang, Jingwen Zhang, Shuang Xia, Jianjun Wang, Liang Xu, Jiao Liu, Xiangming Fu, Ruyan Xie, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional chiral separation membranes, such as those composed of linear chiral polymers, often struggle to simultaneously achieve high enantioselectivity and high permeability due to issues like low density of recognition sites and poor structural stability, facing the significant “selectivity-permeability” trade-off. In this study, a three-dimensional cross-linked chiral composite membrane (P-SiO 2 ) was constructed on a porous silica (SiO2) support via surface-initiated polymerization, employing a thiol–ene click chemistry strategy. This 3D cross-linked network not only enhanced the spatial regularity and interfacial stability of the chiral recognition sites but also endowed the membrane with a network flexibility intermediate between that of linear polymers and rigid porous materials, effectively improving the membrane’s separation performance and mechanical strength. The effects of key parameters, including the type of cross-linker, the pore size of the SiO2 support, reaction concentration, and the content of the chiral monomer, on the membrane structure and separation performance were systematically investigated. The results demonstrated that the P-SiO 2 composite membrane with cross-linked network exhibited excellent chiral resolution performance for the d,l-phenylalanine racemate, with a permeability coefficient of 1.97 × 10–10 m2/h and an enantiomeric excess (ee%) as high as 98.3%. Mechanistic studies revealed that the chiral recognition process follows a delayed transport mechanism, in which the composite membrane exhibited stronger adsorption for l-phenylalanine, thereby delaying its transport and allowing the d-enantiomer to permeate preferentially. This study provides an innovative design strategy and a feasible preparation pathway for developing chiral separation membrane materials that combine excellent film-forming ability, high enantioselectivity, superior stability, and good mechanical properties, showing broad application prospects in the efficient separation and purification of chiral pharmaceuticals.

Introduction

Chirality, as a fundamental property of nature, holds paramount significance in fields of the pharmaceutical industry, life sciences, and materials science. According to statistics, over 50% of clinical drugs are chiral compounds, and their enantiomers often exhibit markedly different pharmacological activities, metabolic pathways, and toxic side effects in biological systems. − The infamous “Thalidomide Tragedy” profoundly revealed the potentially fatal differences in physiological activity between enantiomers of chiral drugs, making the acquisition of enantiomerically pure single-enantiomer drugs a core challenge in ensuring drug safety and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. , To address this challenge, various chiral separation technologies have been developed industrially, such as crystallization resolution and chromatographic separation. , Among these, membrane separation has garnered considerable attention due to its green advantages, including low energy consumption, continuous operation, and ease of scale-up, which is regarded as a promising method for large-scale chiral separation. − However, despite its promising prospects, the practical application of chiral separation membranes still faces a long-standing core challenge the “trade-off effect” where it is often challenging to simultaneously enhance both membrane selectivity and permeability. − Traditional chiral polymer membranes with linear structures, such as those based on helical polyacetylenes, polyamides, or polypeptides, have been extensively explored, − exhibit certain chiral recognition capabilities and good film-forming abilities. Nevertheless, their linear chains are prone to swelling or structural relaxation during operation, leading to a decline in separation performance over time, and their selectivity and long-term stability are often difficult to guarantee. Essentially, the high flexibility of linear polymer chains, after film formation, becomes a detrimental factor limiting mass transfer efficiency and structural integrity. Consequently, research advances along two main paths. In material design, chiral polymer micro/nano-objects with stable, controllable morphologies provide a novel platform to overcome the stability-selectivity trade-off of linear membranes. In fundamental studies, dynamically tunable chiral supramolecular materials (e.g., based on azobenzene) enable intelligent regulation of chirality. However, such micro/nano-objects or supramolecular assemblies often cannot directly form large-area, defect-free membranes with robust mechanical properties, and their scalable fabrication and long-term stability remain challenging.

To overcome the aforementioned “trade-off effect” and enhance the overall performance of membranes, researchers have turned their attention to novel composite membrane materials. By combining inorganic materials, such as silica (SiO2) and graphene oxide (GO), as supporting substrates with chiral selectors, organic-inorganic hybrid membranes can be constructed, effectively improving membrane stability. For example, Wang et al. achieved a significant enhancement in separation performance by modifying a SiO2 substrate with a chiral hypercross-linked polymer, highlighting the advantage of the substrate support effect. Concurrently, in the pursuit of higher enantioselectivity and stability, researchers are exploring porous crystalline materials with the well-defined nanochannels, such as metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) and covalent organic frameworks (COFs), as well as rigid porous polymers like conjugated microporous polymers (CMPs). These materials, with their tunable pore structures, high specific surface areas, and functionalizable frameworks, provide a novel platform for chiral separation. − By introducing selectors such as cyclodextrins and chiral ionic liquids, these materials exhibit great potential in enhancing chiral separation performance. − For instance, Fan et al. constructed a bilayer chiral membrane via a MOF-on-MOF heterojunction strategy, achieving a separation factor of 4.56 for p-hydroxyphenylglycine, demonstrating excellent chiral selectivity. Zhang et al. fabricated a chiral cyclodextrin-MXene composite membrane by condensing carboxylated cyclodextrin with MXene, which achieved 96.8% enantiomeric excess separation for racemic ibuprofen within 1 h, with a permeation flux of 9.2 mmol·m–2·h–1, showcasing outstanding enantioselective separation performance. Mao et al. systematically reviewed the synthesis strategies of chiral covalent organic frameworks and their applications in enantiomer separation, highlighting that precise construction of COF materials with well-defined morphology and abundant chiral recognition sites can be achieved by post-synthetic modification or direct synthesis methods. Our group constructed CCMP-M1–4/SiO2 and CCMP-SiO2 composite membranes via cascade reactions. The former achieved an enantiomer separation efficiency of 96.8%, while the latter, through a dual-mechanism synergy of chiral recognition sites, exhibited outstanding enantiomeric excess (ee%) up to 99.7% and excellent permeability coefficient of 3.3 × 10–8 m2/h, providing a novel approach for high-throughput chiral separation. However, these porous materials (e.g., MOFs, COFs, and CMPs) still face several common challenges when utilized for fabricating high-performance separation membranes. A major bottleneck lies in the scalable preparation of defect-free, large-area continuous membranes. The inherent nano or microscale crystals or particles of these materials tend to form grain boundaries or interfacial defects during membrane fabrication, thereby compromising their mechanical integrity and separation selectivity. Meanwhile, the interfacial adhesion between most porous materials and polymer substrates or supports is often weak, potentially leading to interlayer delamination under prolonged operation or pressure-driven conditions, resulting in performance degradation. Additionally, the relatively harsh synthesis conditions and/or high raw material costs for some of these materials also pose difficulties for their practical application and scalable fabrication.

To address this dilemma, the key lies in developing a novel material that can achieve a balance between chain flexibility and framework rigidity. The ideal membrane material should possess moderate network flexibility to form open transport channels while achieving excellent membrane-forming ability and interfacial stability through a robust cross-linked structure. How to achieve the high-density, uniform, and stable construction of chiral recognition sites at the interface, and precisely regulate the membrane’s microstructure to balance selectivity and permeability, remains a significant challenge in the field. Click chemistry, particularly thiol–ene click reactions, with its green synthetic advantages including high efficiency, good regioselectivity, and mild reaction conditions (often metal-free catalysis) provides a powerful tool for precisely constructing functionalized microenvironments within chiral membranes. − This also offers a novel approach to solving issues such as uneven modification and weak binding strength that may arise in traditional chiral membrane fabrication processes.

In this study, a chiral cross-linked polymer composite membrane (P-SiO 2 ) was innovatively fabricated on the surface of a porous silica (SiO2) substrate via an in situ surface-initiated thiol–ene click polymerization strategy. The three-dimensional cross-linked network constructed via click chemistry demonstrates the core advantages of achieving precise regulation of membrane microstructure through designable chemical structures, imparting appropriate flexibility and excellent stability to the membrane by the cross-linked network, and enabling simple, controllable synthesis of composite membranes through the green and efficient click chemistry reaction. This strategy provides an innovative design concept and a reliable preparation pathway for realizing chiral separation membranes with high enantioselectivity. The design of thiol–ene formulations is fundamentally grounded in the characteristic advantages of click chemistry, namely its high efficiency, exceptional selectivity, and the absence of metal catalysts. These features facilitate the rapid and environmentally benign formation of stable carbon–sulfur (C–S) bonds, which subsequently yields a three-dimensional cross-linked network. Regarding monomer selection, monomer A1 was chosen to incorporate the pivotal chiral center responsible for enantioselectivity, while monomer A3 was employed to ensure sufficient cross-linking density, thereby guaranteeing the mechanical robustness and chemical stability of the resulting polymeric architecture. The strategic combination of these monomers aims to fabricate a polymer backbone that concurrently exhibits chiral recognition capability and structural integrity. Furthermore, the thiol-based cross-linker serves as a critical tunable element. By precisely designing its molecular structure, the microstructure of the composite membrane can be finely modulated to optimize its ultimate chiral separation performance.

By systematically regulating key parameters such as the type of cross-linker, substrate pore size, and reaction concentration, precise control over the membrane’s microstructure and chiral recognition microenvironment was achieved. This composite membrane not only overcomes the selectivity-permeability trade-off challenge inherent to traditional chiral membranes but also enhances mechanical stability through its three-dimensional cross-linked network. The flexibility of this cross-linked network lies between the high flexibility of linear polymer chains and the brittleness of rigid porous materials, enabling the formation of sufficiently open transport channels while achieving robust film formation via interfacial chemical bonding. Experimental results demonstrate that the optimized P-SiO 2 membrane exhibits an enantiomeric excess (ee%) as high as 98.3% and a permeation coefficient of 1.97 × 10–10 m2/h for racemic phenylalanine, providing an innovative solution for efficient chiral drug separation. This work also focuses on discussing the structure-property relationship between the membrane architecture and separation performance, and elucidates its chiral recognition mechanism in depth. The findings are expected to provide new design principles and experimental foundations for developing next-generation high-performance chiral separation membranes, thereby advancing chiral drug separation technologies toward higher efficiency and better sustainability.

Experimental Section

Materials

(3-Mercaptopropyl)­trimethoxysilane (MPS, 99.0%), 1,4-benzenedithiol (BDT, ≥98.0%), 4,4′-thiobisbenzenethiol (≥98.0%), 2,2-dimethyl-2-phenylacetophenone (DMPA, ≥98.0%), cinchonine (≥98.0%), and 2,4,6-trivinyl-2,4,6-trimethylcyclotrisiloxane (≥98.0%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Bis­(2-mercaptoethyl) ether (≥98.0%) was supplied by Shanghai Darui Fine Chemicals Co., Ltd. d,l-Phenylalanine (d,l-Phe, ≥98.0%, HPLC grade) was purchased from TCI (Shanghai) Chemical Trade Co., Ltd. Porous silica substrates (G2–G5) were provided by Henan Sigele Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All the other chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of the Polymers PR1 and PR2

The cross-linker 4,4′-thiobisbenzenethiol (B2R1, 161 mg, 643 μmol), the photoinitiator 2,2-dimethyl-2-phenylacetophenone (DMPA, 16.4 mg, 64.1 μmol), the trifunctional alkene monomer 2,4,6-trivinyl-2,4,6-trimethylcyclotrisiloxane (A3, 92.0 μL, 341 μmol), and the chiral monomer (+)-cinchonine (A1, 75.5 mg, 0.256 mmol) were dissolved in a mixed solvent of ethanol and tetrahydrofuran (v/v = 5:3, 6.20 mL). The mixture was magnetically stirred at room temperature for 5 min to ensure thorough mixing, then irradiated under a UV lamp (λmax = 365 nm, 100 W) for 10 min to initiate the thiol–ene click polymerization. After that, the reaction mixture was allowed to stand for 1 h to ensure complete reaction. The resulting solid product was washed successively with ethanol ten times to fully remove unreacted monomers and then dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C for 24 h, yielding a yellow powder product, PR 1 (301 mg), with a yield of 95.3%.

The synthesis procedures for PR 2 are similar to those of PR 1 , except that bis­(2-mercaptoethyl) ether (B2R2, 88.7 mg, 643 μmol) was used as the cross-linker instead of B2R1. A white solid product, PR 2 (299 mg), was obtained with a yield of 92.0%.

Synthesis of Composite Membranes

Synthesis of the Functionalized Porous SiO2 Substrate (SiO2–SH)

A porous SiO2 substrate with a diameter of 25.0 mm and a thickness of 1.50 mm was immersed in a conical flask containing 1.00 mol/L hydrochloric acid (100 mL) and subjected to ultrasonication for 1 h. The substrate was then sequentially rinsed five times with deionized water and tetrahydrofuran, followed by vacuum drying for 4 h, yielding an activated porous SiO2 substrate (denoted as SiO2–OH) (Scheme ). To a two-necked flask, (3-mercaptopropyl)­trimethoxysilane (MPS, 176 mg, 800 μmol), 4.00 mL of a 90% ethanol solution adjusted to pH 3.5 with hydrochloric acid, and the activated SiO2–OH substrate were added. The reaction was carried out at 60 °C for 6 h. The resulting substrate was washed thoroughly with anhydrous methanol and dried under vacuum for 12 h, affording a functionalized porous SiO2 substrate modified with surface thiol (−SH) groups, designated as SiO2–SH.

Synthesis of P-SiO 2 Composite Membranes and Chiral Separation

Synthesis of the P-SiO2 Composite Membrane

A mixture of cross-linker B2R1 or B2R2 (214 μmol), photoinitiator DMPA (5.47 mg, 23.4 μmol), A3 (30.6 μL, 114 μmol), and A1 (25.2 mg, 85.3 μmol) in a mixed solvent of ethanol and tetrahydrofuran (5:3 (v/v), 2.00 mL) was stirred for 5 min. The reaction solution was then dripped onto the SiO2–SH substrate. The system was irradiated under a UV lamp at room temperature for 10 min, followed by standing for 1 h. The resulting composite membrane was washed ten times with ethanol and dried in a vacuum oven for 24 h, yielding the P-SiO 2 composite membrane (Scheme ).

Enantiomer Resolution Experiments

In this work, the resolution of racemic phenylalanine was performed using a static dialysis method, as described below. A racemic phenylalanine aqueous solution (concentration: 12.0 mmol/L, volume: 50.0 mL) and ultrapure water (volume: 4.00 mL) were prepared. The membrane to be tested was fixed between the two compartments, providing an effective permeation area of 3.14 cm2. The experiment was conducted at ambient temperature and pressure with continuous stirring at 230 rpm for 24 h. Every hour, a sample was taken from the permeate side, and the enantiomer content was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) under the following conditions: chiral column CR­(+), mobile phase consisting of aqueous perchloric acid and methanol, and a flow rate of 0.200 mL/min. After each sampling and analysis, the permeate side was replenished with fresh ultrapure water, and the permeation experiment was continued.

Static Adsorption Tests

Polymer powder (200 mg) was added to a brown glass vial containing 2 mL of an aqueous solution of racemic phenylalanine (concentration: 3.00 mmol/L). The mixture was stirred with a magnetic stirrer at 200 rpm. After 4 days, the solid polymer was removed from the solution by filtration through a membrane filter. The enantiomer content in the resulting solution was then analyzed by HPLC with chiral column.

Gel Content Determination

To evaluate the network cross-linking density of the synthesized chiral cross-linked polymers, gel content measurements were conducted. Polymer powder samples (1.95 g for the 8:20 ratio and 1.45 g for the 10:20 ratio) were accurately weighed and wrapped in a pre-weighed filter paper bag. The sample bags were then subjected to continuous Soxhlet extraction and subjected to continuous reflux extraction using tetrahydrofuran as the solvent for 24 h. After extraction, the sample bags were removed and dried to constant weight in a vacuum oven at 40 °C. The gel content was calculated based on the mass of the sample before and after extraction.

Characterizations

The chemical structures, morphologies, and properties of the synthesized polymers and composite membranes were characterized using the following techniques. Solid-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrum was recorded on a Bruker Avance 400 MHz spectrometer. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained using a Shimadzu IRTracer-100 spectrometer with the KBr pellet method. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were measured on a JASCO J-1500 spectropolarimeter. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on a TA Instruments Q5000IR thermogravimetric analyzer under a nitrogen atmosphere with a heating rate of 10 °C/min. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer using a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source. The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the membranes were observed using a KYKY-2800B scanning electron microscope (SEM), coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) for elemental mapping. Static water contact angles were measured at room temperature using a CA-SL200B contact angle goniometer to evaluate the surface wettability. The enantiomeric composition of the feed, permeate, and adsorption solutions was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on a Shimadzu LC-16 system equipped with a chiral column (CR­(+), 250 × 4.6 mm).

Computational Details

Molecular dynamics simulations of the complex were conducted using the Xtb software. The specific parameters were as follows: simulation time 100 ps, temperature 400 K, time step 1 fs, and the mass of hydrogen atoms was set to four times their actual mass to slow down hydrogen motion, allowing the use of a larger time step. During the simulation, trajectories were recorded every 50 fs. After the simulation, all recorded trajectory frames were subjected to batch optimization at the GFN0-xTB level, followed by deduplication of the optimized structures and energies. The structural deduplication threshold was set to 0.5 Å, and the energy deduplication threshold was 0.5 kcal/mol. For the resulting non-redundant structures, further structural optimization was performed at the HFN2-xTB level. After optimization, structure and energy deduplication were carried out again using the same thresholds (0.5 Å for structure and 0.5 kcal/mol for energy), ultimately yielding the three structures with the lowest energies. Subsequently, the Gaussian 16 (A.01) software package was used to perform structural optimization and frequency calculations for these three structures at the B3LYP­(D3BJ)/6-31+G* level, resulting in the identification of the complex structure with the lowest energy and highest stability. Finally, based on this stable structure, calculations were performed at the B3LYP­(D3BJ)/def2-TZVP level: first, the energies of the complex (SPC) and each monomer (SPA and SPB) were calculated separately, and then the binding energy (E b) was obtained using the formula E b = SPC – (SPA + SPB).

Results and Discussion

Structures and Characterization of Polymers PR1 and PR2

Through an efficient thiol–ene click chemistry reaction, chiral cross-linked polymers PR 1 and PR 2 with three-dimensional network structures were successfully synthesized by reacting the chiral alkene monomer cinchonine (A1) and the trifunctional alkene monomer (A3) with the dithiol monomer (B2) under UV light irradiation, giving products with yields of 95.3% and 92.0%, respectively. The synthesized chiral cross-linked polymers (PR 1 and PR 2 ) were systematically characterized by FT-IR, solid-state 13C NMR, XPS, CD, and TGA. FT-IR analysis (Figure a and Figure S1) shows that the characteristic absorption peak of the thiol (−SH) group at 2555 cm–1 completely disappears both in PR 1 and PR 2 , while the alkene (CC) stretching vibration peak around 1600 cm–1 is significantly attenuated, indicating that the thiol and alkene monomers have reacted thoroughly. This confirms the successful implementation of the thiol–ene click reaction and the formation of polymers PR 1 and PR 2 . In the 13C NMR spectrum (Figure b), signals in the range of 114–130 ppm are assigned to aromatic ring carbon atoms. Resonances observed between 130 and 155 ppm correspond to the overlapping signals of aromatic carbons bonded to sulfur (C–S) or nitrogen (C–N), as well as aromatic carbons influenced by hydroxyl groups. The chemical shift at 81 ppm is attributed to the hydroxymethyl carbon (CH–OH), while signals in the region of 63–80 ppm arise from methylene (−CH2−) and methine (−CH−) carbon atoms adjacent to nitrogen atoms. Resonances in the 12–37 ppm region arise from long-chain methylene carbons (−CH2−), and the peak at 10 ppm corresponds to the terminal methyl carbon (−CH3). These spectroscopic features align with the expected structure, confirming the successful formation of PR 1 via thiol–ene click chemistry. The XPS survey spectra (Figures S2a and S3a) clearly show the characteristic peaks of C, O, and S elements. The high-resolution C 1s spectra (Figures S2b and S3b) reveal binding energy peaks for PR 1 at 284.5 eV (C–C/CC), 285.1 eV (C–N), 286.0 eV (C–S), and 284.0 eV (C–Si), while for PR 2 , a prominent C–O–C characteristic peak appears at 286.8 eV, further confirming the effective progress of the click chemistry reaction. Circular dichroism (CD) analysis (Figure c) shows that both PR 1 and PR 2 exhibit a positive Cotton effect at 229 nm, consistent with that of the chiral monomer A1 (see Figure S4 for the CD spectrum of A1), indicating that the chiral information on the monomer is preserved and successfully transferred to the polymer backbone during polymerization. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Figure d) reveals that the initial decomposition temperature (425 °C) and the temperature at 10% mass loss (514 °C) of PR 1 are significantly higher than those of PR 2 (285 °C and 348 °C). This difference is attributed to the introduction of the aromatic ring structure in the B2R1 cross-linker, which imparts considerably higher rigidity compared to the aliphatic chain of B2R2. The three-dimensional cross-linked network PR 1 , with lower chain entropy relative to PR 2 , demonstrates excellent thermal stability with approximately 60% char residue at 600 °C.

Structure and Characterization of the Composite Membrane P-SiO2

Using a surface-initiated click polymerization approach, a chiral composite membrane (P-SiO 2 ) with a three-dimensional cross-linked structure was successfully fabricated on a porous silica (SiO2) support substrate. The PR 1 -SiO 2 and PR 2 -SiO 2 composite membranes were analyzed by FT-IR, XPS, and EDS. FT-IR analysis (Figure S5) demonstrates that a characteristic −CH3 peak appears at 2975 cm–1 for the functionalized SiO2–SH substrate, confirming the successful grafting of the thiol-functional silane. The spectra of both the PR 1 -SiO 2 and PR 2 -SiO 2 composite membranes display characteristic peaks at 2975 cm–1 (−CH3), 1001 cm–1 (Si–O–Si), and 1634 cm–1 (CN), confirming the participation of monomers A3 and A1 in the reaction. Meanwhile, the characteristic peaks at 2555 cm–1 (−SH) and 1600 cm–1 (CC) disappear, verifying that the thiol groups of cross-linker B2R1 have reacted with the alkene groups of the monomers via click chemistry, successfully constructing the composite membranes. XPS characterization (Figure S6) reveals that the survey spectra contain characteristic peaks for C 1s, O 1s, Si 2p, and S 2p. The high-resolution C 1s spectrum of the PR 1 -SiO 2 composite membrane (Figure a) exhibits peaks at 286.0 eV (C–S), 285.1 eV (C–N), 284.5 eV (C–C/phenyl), and 284.0 eV (C–Si), confirming the successful incorporation of aromatic structures from cross-linker B2R1 into the polymer layer. The O 1s spectrum (Figure b) shows a doublet at 533.1 eV (C–O–H) and 531.9 eV (O–Si), and the Si 2p spectrum (Figure c) displays distinguishable doublets at 101.8 and 103.6 eV corresponding to Si–O bonds from the cross-linker and the SiO2 substrate, respectively. These results demonstrate the formation of a stable organic-inorganic structure through chemical bonding between the polymer layer and the substrate (see Figure S7 for the XPS spectrum of SiO2–SH). The S 2p spectrum (Figure d) exhibits characteristic features of thioether bonds of C–S–C at 163.4 eV (2p3/2) and 164.6 eV (2p1/2), further verifying the formation of thioether linkages via the thiol–ene click reaction. In contrast, due to the presence of ether bonds (C–O–C) in cross-linker B2R2, the C 1s spectrum of the PR 2 -SiO 2 composite membrane (Figure e) shows a distinct C–O peak at 286.8 eV, and its O 1s spectrum (Figure f) displays a C–O–C feature at 532.6 eV. These spectral characteristics differ markedly from those of PR 1 -SiO 2 , confirming the effective modulation of the composite membrane’s surface chemical environment by different cross-linkers. EDS analysis of the PR 1 -SiO 2 composite membrane (Figure S8) indicates a uniform distribution of S elements within the polymer layer, providing spatial elemental evidence for the successful construction of the composite membrane.

High-resolution XPS spectra of PR1-SiO2 : (a) C 1s, (b) O 1s, (c) Si 2p, (d) S 2p; for PR2-SiO2 : (e) C 1s, (f) O 1s, (g) Si 2p, (h) S 2p.

The surface microstructure of the composite membranes was systematically characterized by contact angle measurements and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Contact angle test results (Figure a–c) reveal that the pristine SiO2 substrate exhibits high hydrophilicity (contact angle approximately 0°). In contrast, the composite membranes PR 1 -SiO 2 and PR 2 -SiO 2 , synthesized via click reaction and possessing a three-dimensional cross-linked polymer layer, display increased contact angles of 123° and 104°, respectively. SEM images reveal that PR 1 and PR 2 uniformly cover the SiO2 surface, forming dense polymer layers (Figure d–f). Cross-sectional images of the composite membranes confirm the formation of continuous polymer layers on the SiO2 substrate, with thicknesses of 41 and 28 μm, respectively (Figure h and i), providing the structural foundation for efficient enantiomer separation.

Chiral Separation Performance of the P-SiO2 Composite Membrane

Effect of SiO2 Substrate Pore Size and Cross-Linker on Chiral Separation Performance

The pore size of the SiO2 substrate plays a crucial regulatory role in the interfacial structure of the polymer functional layer and the resulting separation performance. Meanwhile, the cross-linker, serving as the backbone for constructing the chiral cross-linked polymer network, directly determines the key membrane properties through its chemical structure (e.g., rigidity, flexibility). The substrate pore size and cross-linker do not independently influence membrane performance, rather they jointly determine the final separation efficiency through the synergistic interplay between the polymer network structure and the substrate support effect. Therefore, this study systematically investigated the synergistic influence of SiO2 substrate pore size (G2–G5) and cross-linker type (B2R1 and B2R2) on the separation performance for racemic phenylalanine (Table ). For the large-pore G2 substrate (10–15 μm), the polymer solution penetrated into the internal pores of the substrate via capillary action which was confirmed from the SEM images of composite membranes (Figure a and e, Figure S9a and e, Figure S10). The permeability coefficient for racemic phenylalanine was very high (13.8 × 10–9 m2/h), the enantioselectivity was almost lost (only 3.38%) (Table , No. 1). For substrates with progressively smaller pore sizes (G3–G5), the polymer layer was mainly distributed on the substrate surface, forming a continuous skin layer with a thickness of approximately 18–22 μm (Figure f–h and Figure S9f–h). This surface-polymerization mode on the SiO2 substrate is more conducive to constructing a well-ordered chiral recognition interface. Compared to G4 and G5, which have very small pore sizes, the composite membrane prepared using the G3 substrate (pore size 4.3–9.0 μm) exhibited a relatively high permeation rate (1.72 × 10–9 m2/h) with enantioselectivity of 23.7% (Table , Nos. 2–4). This phenomenon is attributed to a moderate pore-size coupling effect between the G3 substrate and the polymer layer, which ensures unobstructed mass-transfer channels while enhancing the spatial matching of chiral recognition sites. In contrast, the composite membrane prepared with cross-linker B2R2 showed a permeability coefficient nearly two orders of magnitude lower (0.06 × 10–9 m2/h) compared to the B2R1 system. Due to the “trade-off effect”, its enantioselectivity increased by 2.5-fold (61.4%) (Table , Nos. 2 and 5). This significant performance difference can be attributed to the distinct microstructures of the polymer networks formed by the different cross-linkers. The flexible chains of B2R2 likely promote the formation of a tighter cross-linked network, enhancing the probability of contact between chiral recognition sites and isomers, thereby improving enantioselectivity. However, this simultaneously increases mass-transfer resistance, leading to a sharp decline in permeability. These results clearly demonstrate that tuning the rigidity of the cross-linker can modulate the selectivity permeability trade-off of the membrane to a certain extent. In summary, the combination of the rigid cross-linker B2R1 and the G3 substrate is a key strategy for achieving the best balance between high permeability and high selectivity.

Effect of Reaction Concentration on Chiral Separation Performance

To investigate the regulatory mechanism of reaction concentration on the structure construction and chiral separation performance of the composite membrane, this study examined the influence of two key parameters: monomer concentration (c) and the total amount of substance in the reaction system (n) (n is the sum of the amounts of chiral monomer A1, alkene monomer A3, and cross-linker B2R1). As the monomer concentration increased from 0.10 mol/L to 0.25 mol/L, the thickness of the polymer layer in the composite membrane increased significantly from 18 μm to 41 μm (Table , Nos. 1–4), showing a clear positive correlation (Figure a). This phenomenon indicates that a higher initial monomer concentration favors the formation of a thicker functional layer on the substrate surface via the thiol–ene click reaction, providing a foundation for precise modulation of the chiral recognition microenvironment. Correspondingly, when the polymer layer thickness increased from 18 μm to 29 μm, the permeability coefficient (P) decreased from 1.72 × 10–9 m2/h to 1.19 × 10–10 m2/h. This demonstrates that a thicker polymer layer corresponds to a longer mass-transfer pathway, thereby increasing the resistance to molecular permeation. Concurrently, the enantiomeric excess improved from 23.7% to 92.0% (Figure b). When the monomer concentration was maintained at 0.20 mol/L, the polymer layer thickness increased linearly from 11 ± 2.0 μm to 45 ± 5.0 μm as the total amount of substance (n) rose from 0.24 mmol to 0.44 mmol, further confirming that adjusting the total amount of substance allows effective control over the skin-layer thickness of the composite membrane (Figure c). Simultaneously, the permeability coefficient (P) exhibited a decreasing trend, dropping from 6.19 × 10–9 m2/h to 0.197 × 10–9 m2/h, while the enantiomeric excess (ee%) increased from 8.2% to 98.3% and then stabilized (Figure d). This further demonstrates that increasing the skin-layer thickness of the composite membrane can effectively reduce membrane defects and enhance the density of chiral recognition sites, thereby improving enantioselectivity. However, an overly dense skin layer greatly restricts molecular permeability, and an excessively thick polymer layer can lead to the blockage of mass-transfer channels. In summary, the optimal synthesis conditions for the composite membrane are a reaction concentration of 0.20 mol/L and a total amount of substance of 0.40 mmol (Table , No. 8), under which the composite membrane exhibits high enantioselectivity.

Effect of Chiral Monomer Content on Separation Performance

Since chiral monomer A1 contains only a single alkene functional group, it acts as chiral source and a chain-terminating agent, directly affecting the amount of chiral sites and cross-linking density of the polymer network. To investigate the influence of chiral selector content on the separation performance of the composite membrane, the enantiomeric resolution performance of the prepared membranes was evaluated by adjusting the molar ratio of the chiral monomer A1 to the cross-linker B2R1 (6:20 to 10:20) under the conditions of a total concentration of 0.20 mol/L and a 1:1 ratio of thiol to alkene functional groups (Table S1 and Figure a). As the A1:B2R1 ratio increased from 6:20 to 8:20, the enantiomeric excess (ee%) improved from 33.7% to 92.0%. The enantioselectivity remained relatively stable when the ratio was between 7:20 and 9:20; however, when the ratio reached to 10:20, the enantioselectivity decreased to 81.7%. This phenomenon is attributed to the excess chiral monomer acting as a chain terminator, which lowers the system’s average functionality and thus its cross-linking density. To verify this, we measured the gel content of polymer PR 1 . As the A1:B2R1 ratio increased from 8:20 to 10:20, the gel content dropped markedly from 43.1% to 24.8%, confirming the formation of a looser, less-cross-linked network. This is consistent with a chain-termination mechanism, where excess monofunctional monomer A1 increases chain ends, causing internal plasticization and enhanced chain mobility. Together, these results directly explain the observed reduction in enantioselectivity.

Regulation of Substrate Concentration on Separation Performance

To investigate the stability of membrane separation performance under practical application conditions, we further examined the effect of racemic phenylalanine feed concentration on the separation efficiency of the composite membrane. As shown in Figure b, as the feed concentration increased from 9.0 mmol/L to 18 mmol/L, the enantiomeric excess (ee%) first increased and then decreased (see Table S2). In the low concentration range (9.0–12 mmol/L), the ee% improved with increasing concentration. When the feed concentration reached 12 mmol/L, the composite membrane exhibited a high enantioselectivity of 98.2%. This is mainly attributed to the fact that, in the low concentration region, increasing the substrate concentration enhances the collision frequency and the probability of specific binding between chiral recognition sites and the target enantiomer, thereby improving membrane enantioselectivity. When the concentration was raised to 18 mmol/L, the ee% dropped to 37.0%. This decline may be due to saturation of the chiral sites resulting from excessive adsorption. Figure c illustrates the permeation permeability (Q) of d,l-phenylalanine separated by the PR 1 -SiO 2 composite membrane as a function of time. As shown in Figure , the peak-area ratio of d-Phe to l-Phe shifted from 1:1 to 2:1, indicating that the PR 1 -SiO 2 composite membrane exhibits preferential permeation toward d-Phe. Moreover, as separation time increased, the permeated quantity (Q) of both d-Phe and l-Phe on the permeate side increased. The permeation flux exhibited a linear relationship with separation time, indicating that the composite membrane possesses favorable separation stability during enantiomer separation.

Mechanism of Delayed Transport in the Enantioselective Separation by the P-SiO2 Composite Membrane

To investigate the transport process and interaction mode of enantiomers within the membrane, we examined the interaction between the membrane and the target molecules, as well as the mass-transfer process, by combining static adsorption experiments with enantiomer permeation behavior analysis. HPLC analysis of the membrane before and after separation (Figure a) demonstrates that after a 24 h permeation experiment, the chromatographic peak intensity of d-Phe in the permeate is significantly higher than that of l-Phe. Calculation reveals that the membrane exhibits a permeability coefficient (P) for phenylalanine as high as 1.97×10–10 m2/h, with an enantiomeric excess (ee%) value of 98.3%. This confirms the pronounced selective permeation capability of the PR 1 -SiO 2 composite membrane toward d-Phe. Figure b illustrates the results of the enantiomer adsorption experiment using polymer powder. After 24 h of static adsorption, the peak-area ratio of d-Phe to l-Phe in the solution changed from the initial 1:1 to 7:3, indicating that PR 1 exhibits stronger adsorption for l-Phe. Combined with the observation that the PR 1 -SiO 2 composite membrane preferentially permeates d-Phe, the enantiomer separation mechanism of this composite membrane aligns with the retarded transport mechanism (Figure ).

To elucidate the origin of the enantioselectivity at the molecular level, computational modeling of the interaction between the chiral selector and l-Phe was performed. The optimized structure of the complex (Figure S11) shows a distinct hydrogen bond between the amino group (−NH2) of l-Phe and a nitrogen atom in the fused aromatic moiety of the selector, forming an N–H···N interaction with a length of 3.09 Å and an angle of 143°. The binding energy for this complex was calculated to be −20.48 kcal/mol, confirming a specific and stable association. The polymer PR 1 exhibits stronger adsorption affinity for l-Phe. This adsorption, on the one hand, “delays” the l-enantiomer within or on the surface of the membrane, leading to selective association between d- and l-enantiomers. On the other hand, due to the weak interaction between the l-Phe association layer and the d-Phe adsorption layer, as well as the concentration gradient, a relatively faster mass-transfer pathway is provided for the d-enantiomer, allowing it to permeate preferentially. Consequently, a product enriched with the d-enantiomer is obtained on the permeate side.

Conclusions

This study developed a surface-initiated polymerization strategy based on thiol–ene click chemistry for the precise construction of chiral cross-linked polymer composite membranes (P-SiO 2 ) on porous SiO2 substrates. First, it was demonstrated that the thiol–ene click reaction enables efficient and reliable growth of a three-dimensional cross-linked polymer network on the SiO2 surface, successfully fabricating PR 1 -SiO 2 and PR 2 -SiO 2 composite membranes. Subsequently, through systematic optimization of preparation conditions, composite membranes with excellent separation performance were obtained. Under optimal conditions, the PR 1 -SiO 2 composite membrane exhibited high enantioselectivity (ee% up to 98.3%) and outstanding permeation coefficient (1.97 × 10–10 m2/h) for an aqueous solution of d,l-phenylalanine. Compared with traditional chiral separation membranes, the prepared PR 1 -SiO 2 composite membrane significantly enhanced permeability while maintaining high enantioselectivity, effectively alleviating the “selectivity-permeability” trade-off. This superior permeability can be attributed to the stable mass-transfer channels provided by the suitably flexible three-dimensional cross-linked network. Mechanistic studies revealed that the PR 1 -SiO 2 composite membrane preferentially adsorbs l-enantiomer, while the d-enantiomer is selectively diffused and permeated, a process consistent with the retarded transport model. These results not only demonstrate the effectiveness of the membrane fabrication strategy based on thiol–ene click chemistry, but also provide important structural design principles for developing novel high-performance chiral separation membranes.
