Positive selection on brain cis-regulatory elements in the human lineage drives changes in gene expression and susceptibility to neuropsychiatric disorders
Authors: Youness Touissi, Eric J Vallender
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHuman brain evolution is hypothesized to be driven primarily by regulatory rather than protein-coding changes. Identifying evolutionary changes in regulatory elements, however, has been constrained by numbers and relationships of taxa and by methodology targeting conserved sequences. These approaches are independent of any experimental assessment of function and overlook potential neofunctionalization in less constrained regions. Here, we leveraged 243 primate genomes to apply a human branch-specific positive selection test to over 350,000 experimentally confirmed human brain-active cis-regulatory elements, identifying 8,522 elements under positive selection on the human lineage. Genes regulated by human positively selected elements showed differential expression between humans and macaques during fetal and adult stages compared to non-selected elements. Elements under positive selection that remained active across fetal and adult stages showed the strongest enrichment for genetic variants associated with neuropsychiatric conditions, including schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.Keywords: brain evolution, comparative genomics, cis-regulatory elements, HARs, positive selection, psychiatric disorders

Introduction

Human brain evolution has involved structural and functional changes resulting in unique cognitive abilities compared to other primate species.1 Interestingly, this specialization is largely not correlated with major differences in protein-coding genes, particularly compared to our closest ape relatives.2 Instead, changes in how genes are regulated appear to drive the key differences in human brain development.2 This evolution of gene regulation is driven by changes in the non-coding parts of the genome3 containing elements, such as enhancers and promoters, that orchestrate spatial and temporal expression during development.4,5 The modular nature of cis-regulatory elements (CREs) drives evolutionary innovation by allowing gene expression changes in tissues, cell types, or developmental time points without producing deleterious pleiotropic effects elsewhere.6 This regulatory evolution is common across various contexts but is most evident in the brain, which shows significant enrichment for human-specific CRE activity compared to other tissues.7,8,9

The evolution of the human brain has been associated with the unique susceptibility of humans to a range of neuropsychiatric disorders, particularly those with neurodevelopmental origins.7,10 Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) consistently show that more than 90% of genetic variation linked to disorders such as schizophrenia and autism are located in the non-coding, regulatory parts of the genome.11,12 Human-evolved regulatory elements, such as human gained enhancers (regulatory regions that gained activity in humans) and human accelerated regions (conserved regions that accumulated sequence changes in the human branch), are significantly enriched near genes associated with autism or schizophrenia risk.7 Genetic variation in regulatory elements active during brain development account for a substantial portion of inherited differences in cognitive and psychiatric traits.13 Together, these convergent lines of evidence suggest that studying the evolution of brain-relevant CREs is essential for a better understanding of the genetic basis of both human brain evolution and psychiatric disorders.14

Previous efforts to find functionally significant non-coding regions have focused on finding highly conserved sequences across species, inferring functional importance,15,16,17 and identifying regions where the human lineage is more rapidly diverging as evidence of positive selection pressure.8,18,19 Large-scale comparative genomics, which involve numerous primate species, have proven highly reliable at identifying constrained functional elements.17 Identifying accelerated regions, however, is more complex, requiring statistical approaches that detect positive selection while distinguishing it from rapid sequence evolution simply due to loss of functional constraint.

Current approaches to regulatory divergence, including those focused on human accelerated regions (HARs), have sought sequences that are highly conserved across mammals or primates but carry an excess of substitutions on specific branches, such as the human branch since divergence from chimpanzees.20 Another approach, human ancestor quickly evolved regions (HAQERs), addresses sequence divergence from an inferred human-chimpanzee ancestral sequence, specifically in previously unconstrained regions of the genome.21

While HARs have identified many relevant loci, including fetal brain enhancers,22 this approach assumes that human specific adaptations mainly arise in regions that were under strong evolutionary constraint during all of primate evolution. This leads to overlooking neofunctionalization, adaptations emerging from less conserved or previously neutral sequences.23 Additionally, the statistical power to detect these regions is strongly influenced by the number of genomes and taxonomic relationships among species used for detection. Fewer numbers and less diverse species increase the risk of both false positives and false negatives. Previous analyses using few primate genomes identified far fewer HARs than more recent studies using a wider range of primate genomes.24 Moreover, when larger numbers of genomes were considered, hundreds of previously identified HARs no longer showed significant acceleration in some cases because the “human-specific” changes originally identified were no longer unique, also occurring in other primates once more data were included.24

Current methods for identifying positive selection may also depend on biased neutral proxies to set a baseline substitution rate. These neutral proxies include ancient repeats or 4-fold degenerate sites, which may also be subject to cryptic selective pressures or mutational biases.23 Ancient repeats can be co-opted as regulatory elements and remain functionally constrained, while 4-fold degenerate sites may experience selective pressures through different mechanisms including exonic splice regulation, codon usage, mRNA stability, or others.25,26,27,28 Using these biased sequences as neutral proxies can result in false signals of acceleration or mask true adaptive changes.23

Additionally, many prior analyses have identified HARs through genome-wide approaches rather than by targeting regions with known activity in specific tissues and have relied on predictions of regulatory activity inferred from sequence conservation22,29 While this strategy is comprehensive, it does not take into account the biological context in which regulatory elements are active. Sequence conservation is used to identify candidate HARs as well as to argue for their functional importance. Conservation suggests, but may not always necessitate, functional importance; conserved regulatory regions can be active in some species but inactive in others.30,31 Similarly to HARs, HAQERs are initially identified from sequence conservation and divergence with a posteriori experimental validation. Regardless, experimental validation of predicted regions of interest has relied on massively parallel reporter assays, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), or other in vitro systems, which limits our understanding of function during actual human brain development.32,33,34,35

More importantly for the purposes of understanding evolutionary processes, especially in humans, nearly half of the regulatory active regions in the human genome are not conserved across species.36 In the current study, we used an atlas of experimentally validated brain-active CREs defined by chromatin accessibility (DNase-seq and ATAC-seq), chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), and histone modifications assays across cortical and subcortical brain regions and from the PsychENCODE Consortium.37 We also leveraged an expanded set of 243 primate genomes currently available to study the evolution of these CREs across the diversity of primates and specifically in the human lineage.17 We applied a branch-specific positive selection test tailored to non-coding elements to detect regulatory regions under directional selection specifically on the human lineage using validated neutral models.23 Using this integrated approach enabled a rigorous and systematic identification of brain-active CREs that have been subject to human-specific selective pressures, potentially underlying uniquely human cognitive and neuropsychiatric traits.

Material and methods

Brain-active CRE dataset

The brain-active CREs (b-CREs) were obtained from the PsychScreen web platform (https://psychscreen.wenglab.org). It contains 361,844 b-CREs identified through ATAC-seq and DNase-seq chromatin accessibility assays integrated with ChIP-seq data for histone modifications (H3K4me3 and H3K27ac) and CTCF binding. It includes data from 110 brain samples (96 adult, 14 fetal) across multiple brain regions, capturing the majority of human genomic cis-regulatory information.37 The b-CREs are further classified into 130,908 adult-specific brain regulatory elements, 108,206 fetal-specific brain regulatory elements, and 122,730 elements active in both developmental stages (shared).

Primate genome alignment

The multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) for each b-CRE region were retrieved using three UCSC (University of California Santa Cruz) Genome Browser tracks.38 MSAs were initially extracted from the Zoonomia+Primates 447-way track (cons447way), which contains alignments of 447 mammalian genomes, including a subset of 243 primate species from a recent primate genome sequencing project.17,39,40 However, as alignment coverage varies across each genome, the full set of species is not always available for every region. When the cons447way track alignments contained fewer than 61 primate sequences for a given region, an alternative MSA was extracted from the 470 Mammals track (cons470way),41 which contains alignments of 470 mammalian genomes, including 61 primate species. Both alignments for the same region were then compared, and the one that provided a higher primate sequence representation was retained. In cases where the selected alignment yielded fewer than 27 primate sequences, alignments were further extracted from the UCSC 30-way track (cons30way), which contains alignments of 30 species, including 27 primates.42 These three UCSC comparative genomics tracks were constructed using either multiz progressive alignment from pairwise BLASTZ alignments (cons30way and cons470way) or progressive Cactus reference free alignment (cons447way),43,44 For each b-CRE, the final MSA retained was that which contained the greatest number of primate sequences. The entire process was automated by a custom Python script.

Multiple sequence alignment processing

The extracted MSAs were further processed using ClipKIT with the kpic-gappy mode,44 which retains both parsimony-informative and constant sites while removing highly gapped regions (sites with >90% gaps). This approach allows the retention of parsimony-informative sites and less aggressive removal of gaps and has been shown to yield superior phylogenetic inference.45,46,47 Additionally, to further ensure that trimming parameters remained within optimal ranges,45,46 a custom Python script was created to analyze each MSA before and after trimming, measuring the percentage of sites removed during trimming, the retention rate of parsimony-informative sites, and changes in gap distribution. This ensured that trimming removed no more than 30% of the original sites and maintained the average percentage of gapped sites per sequence below 20%. When these thresholds were exceeded, alignments were manually inspected and ClipKit parameters adjusted accordingly.

MSAs with fewer than 15 species were excluded to ensure robust phylogenetic inferences, as previous studies have conclusively shown that phylogenetic errors decrease with increased taxon sampling, with marked accuracy improvements around 15 or more taxa.48,49,50,51 A minimum alignment length of 140 bp was used (around ∼150 bp recommended by adaptiPhy),23 maintaining adequate power to detect evolutionary signals while preserving a substantial MSA set for analysis.

Phylogenetic analysis

adaptiPhy (v.2.0.0) was used to identify branch-specific positive selection in the b-CREs (human branch since the split from chimpanzee common ancestor).23 This method provides improved sensitivity and specificity for distinguishing positive selection from neutral evolution and relaxation of constraint by comparing CRE substitution rates to a curated set of neutrally evolving genomic elements.

For neutral reference sequences, we utilized non-functional regions (NFRs) that were generated by a previous study.23 These NFRs were initially derived by excluding known functional elements such as exons, RNAs, enhancers, regulatory elements, and repeat sequences, from the hg19 human genome. Substitution rates were calculated for each remaining region, and those with significantly deviating rates were removed to exclude potential outliers that may represent functional elements that were not annotated and are not evolving neutrally. LiftOver from the UCSC Genome Browser was used then to convert the coordinates of these regions from hg19 to hg38.37 Since the neutrality of these regions was originally assessed on only 5 primates’ species, we further verified their neutrality across an extended set of primate species using a custom Python script. This script retrieved phyloP basewise conservation scores from the UCSC Genome Browser’s 447 primate phyloP track and calculated the average phyloP score for each NFR.38 We retained only the regions with average PhyloP scores between −1.3 and 1.3 (equivalent to a p value of 0.05), thus excluding NFRs with statistically significant conservation or acceleration. For the retained regions, we further removed individual sites with phyloP scores outside the −1.3 to 1.3 range to ensure strict neutral references.

The adaptiPhy method uses a likelihood ratio test (LRT) in HyPhy to compare a null model of neutral expectation against an alternative model of positive selection.52 To avoid being trapped in local maxima, each region was fitted 20 times, and the best maximum likelihood estimate was extracted. We then used a Bonferroni correction to adjust the p values for multiple comparisons, identifying regions under significant positive selection at a corrected p value threshold of p < 0.05.

Genomic coordinates of previously identified HARs and HAQERs were compiled from prior literature.21,24,53,54 These datasets were first intersected to determine whether they identified the same regions across studies or whether each study yielded a different set. bedtools intersect was then used to overlap the combined HARs/HAQERs with the whole b-CRE dataset to isolate the subset of HARs and HAQERs that are active in brain tissue.55 These brain-active HARs/HAQERs were subsequently intersected with the HPS (human positively selected) b-CREs identified in the current study to distinguish previously characterized regions among positively selected b-CREs.

Motif enrichment analysis

Motif enrichment using the MEME Suite package56 was performed to identify transcription factor motifs that were overrepresented in HPS b-CREs compared to b-CREs that are not under selective pressures. HPS b-CREs for each developmental stage (adult, fetal, and shared) were designated as the foreground sequences, with the corresponding non-HPS b-CREs serving as the background sequences. The AME tool was then used to identify known motifs enriched in the foreground sequences against the background using the HOCOMOCO v.12 database of human transcription factor binding motifs.57 De novo motifs were discovered using STREME and subsequently cross-referenced against the HOCOMOCO v.12 database using TOMTOM to identify matches with known motifs58,59 For each significant transcription factor motif identified, the average expression of the corresponding transcription factor was calculated from the corresponding developmental periods using a brain gene expression dataset from previous work. For motifs enriched in shared b-CREs, the average expression of each enriched transcription factor was calculated in fetal and adult brain separately.

b-CRE-gene association

Target genes for each b-CRE were identified using the ENCODE-rE2G,60 ABC (Activity-by-Contact),61 EPIraction,62 and GraphReg63 predictive models accessed through the SCREEN database (https://screen.wenglab.org/). These models integrate chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, 3D chromatin interactions, linear proximity, and CRISPR perturbation data to identify genes regulated by CREs rather than simply using a nearest-neighbor approach. For each b-CRE, predicted target genes were retrieved and filtered to retain only interactions specific to brain tissue.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the g:gost tool from g:Profiler to determine enriched biological functions and pathways in genes linked to HPS b-CREs.64 Multiple testing was controlled for using the g:SCS p value correction method with a significance level of 0.05. For each developmental stage (adult, fetal, and shared), genes lined to HPS b-CREs were set as the foreground against a background set of all genes associated with non-HPS b-CREs. To ensure that gene enrichment results were not driven by gene list size or background genes composition, we ran a permutation test with 1,000 iterations where, at each developmental stage, a random gene set of size equal to that of the HPS foreground set was chosen randomly from the non-HPS background set and then queried with g:Profiler using the exact same parameters as used for the HPS foreground set.

Enrichment of psychiatric disorder variants

A GWAS enrichment analysis assessed the enrichment of genetic variants associated with major psychiatric disorders within positively selected b-CREs using GWAS meta-analyses obtained from the Psychscreen platform (https://psychscreen.wenglab.org). The list of variants associated with each psychiatric disorder was expanded to include all variants in linkage disequilibrium with GWAS significant hits using reference data from the 1000 Genomes Project phase 3 European population.65 PLINK was then used to include all variants in linkage disequilibrium with significant GWAS variants based on an r2 threshold of 0.7 within 400 kb windows to create comprehensive variant sets for each trait.66 Fisher’s exact test was then used to determine whether positively selected b-CREs were enriched for each trait-associated variant compared to non-positively selected regions; non-brain-active CREs and non-brain traits served as negative controls. Significantly enriched traits were then retested again using a permutation test. This permutation generated 10,000 sets of non-positively selected regions (the null sets) that were matched to the positively selected b-CREs for GC content, sequence length, b-CRE type (e.g., promoter, distal enhancer), and developmental stage to control for sequence composition biases.

b-CRE target gene expression across primates

Comparative gene expression analysis across human, chimpanzee,67 and macaque,68 spanning developmental and adult time points, was performed to determine whether positively selected b-CREs were associated with human-specific gene expression changes, as previously described.7 The processed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data were retrieved from the PsychENCODE Human Brain Evolution platform (http://evolution.psychencode.org/). The analysis started by calculating a normalized Z score for every gene in each species. This Z score measured each gene expression at a specific time point against its average expression over all time points. Next, the Δ expression Z score was calculated by taking the difference between the human Z score and the macaque Z score at matching time points as described below. The Δ Z score value indicated whether a gene’s expression was relatively higher or lower in the human brain at a given time point compared to macaque. For the adult stage, which was represented by a single time point, log2 fold change (log2FC) was directly calculated in humans and macaques (or chimpanzees), and Δ log2FC was calculated for each gene. Finally, the distribution of these Δ expression Z scores (or log2FC for adult data) for genes linked to the HPS b-CREs was compared against those linked to non-HPS b-CREs using a Mann-Whitney U test, and a false discovery rate of 5% was used to correct for multiple-test comparison.

Developmental timing differences were assessed through expression breakpoint analysis of previously published transcriptomic data, which identifies the specific developmental stage where genes undergo abrupt expression changes.69 Δ breakpoint scores were calculated as the difference between human and macaque breakpoint timing based on developmental event scores, and distributions were compared between genes regulated by HPS b-CREs versus non-HPS b-CREs using Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) tested enrichment of genes regulated by HPS b-CREs across published co-expression modules,67 using logistic linear regression with false discovery rate (FDR) 5% correction for multiple testing.

Results

Quality control for orthology identification and sequence coverage filtered out 11,167 elements (3.1%) from the initial dataset of 361,844 b-CREs, leaving 350,677 for phylogenetic analysis. This analysis identified 8,522 b-CREs with significant signatures of positive selection on the human lineage (Figure 1A). The identified human HPS b-CREs consisted of 3,331 adult-specific elements (Table S1), 1,963 fetal-specific elements (Table S2), and 3,228 shared elements (Table S3). Genomic coordinates of previously identified HARs and HAQERs were compiled into a consolidated dataset of 5,497 non-overlapping accelerated regions, although the independent source studies21,24,53,54 exhibited minimal overlap with one another (Figure 1B). The comparison of the entire b-CRE dataset against the compiled HARs/HAQERs revealed that 844 (15.4%) overlapped with brain-active regions (Figure 1C). Only 42 previously characterized HARs/HAQERs overlapped with the HPS b-CREs, while 8,480 HPS b-CREs (99.5%) were uniquely identified in this study.

Motif enrichment analysis

Shared HPS b-CREs, those that were expressed in both fetal and adult stages, contained 133 significantly enriched motifs, whereas adult-specific and fetal-specific HPS b-CREs contained 114 and 8, respectively (Tables S4, S5, and S6; including de novo STREME/TOMTOM results). Adult b-CREs displayed strong enrichment for zinc-finger protein binding, with ZN770 (Adj p = 1.09 × 10−56) and ZN135 (Adj p = 6.54 × 10−51) being the most significant. CTCF (Adj p = 1.70 × 10−22) and its paralog CTCFL (Adj p = 1.26 × 10−31) were also overrepresented in adult sequences. Fetal b-CREs were enriched for zinc-finger proteins as well, including ZN560 and ZN441, which represented 6 of the 8 identified motifs. The shared b-CREs were enriched for ETS-family transcription factors, particularly ELK4 (Adj p = 7.60 × 10−33), ELK1, ELK3, ETV5, and the ELF subfamily. SP/KLF-family binding motifs were also enriched, including NRF1 (Adj p = 2.32 × 10−29), SP1, SP3, SP4, and multiple KLF members. Finally, five zinc-finger factors (ZN135, ZN560, ZN121, ZN441, and ZN284) were consistently enriched across all developmental stages (Figure 2).

Gene overrepresentation analysis

Functional enrichment analysis of genes linked to HPS b-CREs identified chemosensory signaling, including olfactory receptor activity, G protein-coupled receptor activity, transmembrane signaling receptor activity, chemical stimulus detection, and olfactory transduction, as the prominent enriched pathway across all developmental stages. Genes regulated by shared HPS b-CREs were uniquely enriched for chromatin functions, specifically structural chromatin constituents, histone deacetylation, and DNA methylation, along with general transcription and RNA polymerase II transcription pathways. Antigen binding was uniquely enriched in genes linked to fetal and shared HPS b-CREs, while genes linked to fetal stages additionally showed enrichment for antigen processing pathways. Genes linked to shared stages also showed enrichment for immune system pathways. Promoter motif analysis identified enrichment for developmental transcriptional regulators in adult and shared stages, particularly Pitx2, Ikaros, and HNRPUL1 (Figure 3; Table S7). None of the enriched terms were recovered through permutation testing, with non-HPS regions selected randomly (Table S8).

Enrichment of psychiatric disorder-associated variants

HPS b-CREs exhibited significant enrichment for variants associated with multiple psychiatric disorders and behavioral traits. Schizophrenia (SCZ)-associated variants showed the strongest enrichment within shared b-CREs (odds ratio [OR] = 6.26, Adj p = 1.35 × 10−38), while adult b-CREs also showed significant association (OR = 3.97, Adj p = 1.81 × 10−14). Major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder followed a similar pattern, where shared b-CREs showed the highest enrichment (MDD: OR = 10.49, Adj p = 5.61 × 10−23; bipolar disorder: OR = 10.51, Adj p = 5.24 × 10−22). Alzheimer’s disease variants were enriched across both HPS adult and shared b-CREs. Additional traits showing significant enrichment in shared b-CREs included autism spectrum disorder (OR = 7.77, Adj p = 2.57 × 10−6), sleep duration, Parkinson’s disease, and intelligence. Cigarettes per day showed enrichment exclusively in adult b-CREs, whereas fetal b-CREs lacked significant enrichment for any tested trait. Finally, non-brain-active CREs showed no enrichment for psychiatric traits, and non-brain traits were not enriched in HPS b-CREs (Figure 4; Table S9).

Target gene differential expression across primates

Genes regulated by HPS fetal b-CREs showed higher expression in humans compared to macaques at early fetal development (13 post-conception weeks [PCWs], Adj p = 2.28 × 10−9) but lower expression at late fetal development (37 PCWs, Adj p = 2.85 × 10−4) (Figure 5A). Adult-specific b-CREs were associated with higher gene expression in humans relative to macaques (Adj p = 1.24 × 10−13) (Figure 5B). Shared b-CREs showed higher human expression relative to macaques during late fetal development (37 PCWs, Adj p = 1.69 × 10−2) (Figure 5C) and adulthood (Adj p = 1.52 × 10−12) (Figure 5D) but lower expression at mid-fetal development (16 PCWs, Adj p = 1.20 × 10−2) (Figure 5C). Notably, genes regulated by adult and shared b-CREs showed no significant expression differences when compared to chimpanzees (Figures 5B and 5D). Analysis of expression breakpoints showed that the timing of expression shifts was significantly delayed in humans compared to macaques for genes regulated by fetal b-CREs (p = 6.78 × 10−3), while shared b-CREs showed no difference (Adj p = 5.57 × 10−1) (Figure 5E). Finally, gene co-expression analysis found enrichment in modules 200 (OR = 6.52, Adj p = 2.4 × 10−4) and 159 (OR = 2.65, Adj p = 3.1 × 10−4) for shared b-CREs, while fetal b-CREs showed depletion in module 19 (OR = 0.27, Adj p = 9.99 × 10−5) (Table S10).

Discussion

This study identified 8,522 experimentally confirmed brain-active CREs under positive selection on the human lineage since the last common ancestor with chimpanzees. Only 42 of these regions overlapped with previously characterized HARs or HAQERs, indicating that 99.5% represent uniquely identified regions. This limited overlap reflects the sensitivity of acceleration detection methods to both the number and phylogenetic diversity of species analyzed. Statistical and bioinformatics approaches used by different studies have resulted in datasets with substantially different properties. The number of species used for alignments from previous studies ranged from as few as 6 primates in early studies to 49 primate species in more recent analyses, with some studies incorporating broader mammalian alignments of up to 241 species (including 43 primates) to increase statistical power.21,23,53,54,70

The intersection of a compiled set of 5,497 previously published HARs and HAQERs with the PsychENCODE atlas of b-CREs revealed that only 844 regions (15.4%) show experimentally confirmed activity in the brain. This contrasts with previous studies suggesting that 49%–70% of HARs exhibit regulatory activity in the human brain that relied on computational predictions and in vitro assays.34,71 The current study was based on stringent ex vivo validation, restricting analysis to b-CREs identified through chromatin accessibility (DNase-seq and ATAC-seq) and histone modification data derived from fetal and adult human brain tissue across cortical and subcortical regions and experimentally validated through in vitro and ex vivo studies.37 This approach ensured that the identified HPS b-CREs are functionally relevant to human brain biology.

Recent work has shown that CREs between distantly related species, such as chickens, mice, and humans, lack sequence conservation but maintain functional activity through conserved chromatin signatures and transcription factor binding sites despite substantial rearrangement of these sites between orthologs.72 Since CREs can maintain a conserved regulatory function despite sequence divergence across species,73 identifying truly adaptive changes requires distinguishing changes under positive selection for their regulatory impact from neutral changes or those accumulating under relaxed constraint. The current set of HPS b-CREs was identified using adaptiPhy, a method that detects regions evolving faster than neutral expectations while distinguishing positive selection from relaxation of constraint.23 These candidate regions may harbor adaptive regulatory changes, though their functional impact remains to be validated experimentally.

Fetal HPS b-CREs had fewer enriched transcription factor binding motifs than shared or adult HPS b-CREs, which likely stems from two factors. First, the small number of fetal HPS b-CREs might have reduced statistical power to detect enriched transcription factor binding motifs. Second, evolutionary changes in fetal b-CREs may operate through mechanisms independent of motif turnover, such as altered binding affinity through flanking sequence modifications74 or changes in spacing and orientation between motifs.75 Such changes would not be detected by motif enrichment analysis, consistent with fetal brain regulatory regions showing deep conservation with outlier accelerated positions.76 Shared and adult HPS b-CREs, however, involved the gain and loss of transcription factor binding sites, perhaps reflecting a different evolutionary architecture.

Zinc-finger transcription factors constitute the predominant enriched family across all developmental stages. The C2H2-type zinc-finger family plays essential roles in neural stem cell regulation and brain development.77 Disruption of these proteins has been implicated in various neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders, including intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorders, schizophrenia, and mood disorders.77

The rapid expansion and diversification of KRAB-ZFPs within the primate lineage78,79 aligns with ZN560, ZN135, and other zinc-finger motifs being the most enriched motifs across the different classes of HPS b-CREs. Although the specific brain functions of these factors remain uncharacterized, their high enrichment in HPS b-CREs is consistent with the divergent KRAB-ZFP expression patterns between humans and chimpanzees, defining an evolving neural network.80 This regulatory evolution extends into chromatin spatial organization, where CTCF and CTCFL enrichment in adult HPS b-CREs suggests 3D genome reorganization during human brain evolution, where chromatin loops mediated by CTCF establish human-specific architecture to control genes essential for neuronal development and synaptic plasticity.81,82,83 The enrichment of ETS (ELK1/3/4) and SP/KLF (SP1/3/4) motifs within shared HPS b-CREs further suggests that evolutionary selection acted on neuronal differentiation, maturation, and plasticity processes in the human brain.84,85 Furthermore, regulatory modules coupling mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism to neuronal activity are further implicated by NRF1 enrichment in both shared and adult HPS b-CREs,86 which may be supporting prolonged brain development and the high energy demands of the human brain.87,88

Most prior studies examining regulatory elements assumed that the gene closest to a CRE is the one it regulates. However, genome-wide analyses have demonstrated that fewer than one-third of regulatory elements control their nearest gene.7,89 Linear proximity poorly predicts functional relationships, and perfunctorily assigning regulatory elements to the nearest gene without other evidence may lead to false conclusions. In the present study, several predictive models were used to infer genes regulated by b-CREs in each adult and fetal brain.60,61,62,63 This approach is more grounded in the biological realities of gene regulation and allows for a more appropriate understanding of the cellular and molecular phenotypes under evolutionary pressure.

Genes linked to HPS b-CREs across all developmental stages showed marked enrichment for chemosensory signaling pathways across developmental stages, including olfactory receptor activity, G protein-coupled receptor signaling, and chemical stimulus detection. This enrichment is consistent with broader analyses of lineage-specific accelerated regions (LinARs) across primates, which have shown that human and great ape LinARs are significantly associated with genes involved in sensory perception and detection of chemical stimuli.24 The evolution of sensory signaling genes represent a shared feature of primate and mammalian brain evolution, with the human lineage showing extensive pseudogenization.90 While previous studies have suggested novel functionality of olfactory receptors independent of odor detection based on expression patterns in cortical and subcortical regions,91 this remains an open field.

Genes regulated by shared HPS b-CREs were enriched for chromatin regulatory functions, including histone deacetylation, DNA methylation, and RNA polymerase II-dependent transcription. This suggests that regulatory elements controlling epigenetic modifiers were subject to evolutionary changes, potentially enabling coordinated regulation of large gene networks during human brain development. Human-specific changes in histone methylation at regulatory sequences are associated with accelerated nucleotide substitution and emergent transcription factor binding motifs,92 while altered histone acetylation levels can drive expansion of cortical progenitor populations.93 Fetal HPS b-CREs regulate genes enriched for immune pathways like antigen binding pathways, which are involved during brain development in neural progenitor dynamics, neuronal migration, and synaptic pruning.94,95,96,97 Given that gene families linked to mammalian encephalization show peak expression during neocortical development, evolutionary modifications to genes and networks implicated in immune signaling likely drove human-specific cortical expansion.98,99

Comparative transcriptomic analyses showed that HPS b-CREs are associated with shifts in gene expression at different time points in brain development. Genes regulated by fetal HPS b-CREs show increased expression in the human brain during early fetal development, followed by decreased expression at late fetal stages compared to macaques. This aligns with previous comparative transcriptomic studies, showing that human brain development is characterized by delayed and prolonged gene expression trajectories relative to other primates.100,101 This is further supported by breakpoint analyses, which show delayed expression breakpoints in humans for genes regulated by fetal HPS b-CREs. Delays in developmental gene expression timing have been repeatedly documented in the human cortex and are thought to contribute to extended periods of neurogenesis, synaptic development, and neuronal plasticity.100,101

Genes regulated by adult and shared HPS b-CREs show increased expression in adult human brains compared to macaques but not compared to chimpanzees. Previous findings have demonstrated that gene expression differences between closely related species, such as human and chimpanzee, are considerably smaller than those observed between more distantly related primates.102,103 This suggests that a subset of regulatory changes may have arisen after divergence from Old World monkeys but prior to the human-chimpanzee split,104 and human-specific fine-tuning of regulatory activity in cell type- or region-specific expression patterns not detectable in bulk tissue analyses.105,106

Shared b-CREs exhibit the most significant enrichment for brain traits, carrying the strongest heritability compared to fetal or adult b-CREs.37 Within these shared b-CREs, variants associated with schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, and bipolar disorder are strongly enriched in the HPS subset of shared b-CREs. Although fetal b-CREs generally contribute to significant heritability to brain traits, including neuropsychiatric disorders,37 the lack of detectable enrichment in fetal HPS b-CREs suggests that their role as fundamental neurodevelopmental CREs is sufficient to explain this susceptibility. Conversely, adult HPS b-CRE enrichment for Alzheimer’s disease and smoking behavior suggests that selection also acted on regulatory programs regulating later-life processes and behavioral traits. This broad impact across different developmental stages distinguishes HPS b-CREs from HARs and HAQERs, which are primarily involved in early neurodevelopmental gene networks and show stronger associations with neurodevelopmental disorders like schizophrenia.7,33,107 Ultimately, the robust enrichment of variants in shared HPS b-CREs suggests that selection favored regulatory plasticity from fetal development through adulthood to enable adaptive human behaviors while simultaneously introducing a vulnerability to dysregulation through genetic variations.

The findings of this study must be interpreted in light of certain limitations. The PsychScreen is the most comprehensive atlas of b-CREs; however, it may not encompass all active elements in the human brain, particularly those in underrepresented cell types, regions, or specific developmental stages. Also, the phylogenetic analyses were limited to b-CREs with sufficient MSA coverage and quality, which may exclude true positively selected elements that are unique to a specific lineage or located in poorly aligned genomic regions. The reliability of downstream gene analyses also depends on the gene-CRE predictive model performance; however, this remains a more accurate approach than traditional and simplistic proximity-based methods for CRE-gene assignment. Furthermore, b-CREs and their target genes were aggregated across all brain regions and cell types, which may obscure important region or cell type-specific functional patterns in the downstream results; a more specific focus on subpopulations may yield important insights if statistical barriers can be overcome or circumvented. Finally, while GWAS enrichment analyses identified associations between HPS b-CREs and psychiatric disorders, the specific functional consequences of the enriched variants remain to be experimentally validated.

This study demonstrates the role of positive selection on thousands of previously uncharacterized regulatory elements on human brain evolution. These elements control both neurodevelopmental programs and adult brain expression networks. The convergence between these evolutionary changes and psychiatric risk variants suggests that the innovations distinguishing the human brain are tied to the genetic architecture of these disorders. Furthermore, the identification of specific transcription factors and networks under selection represents a framework for studying the molecular mechanisms linking evolutionary adaptation to psychiatric disorder susceptibility and how regulatory evolution shaped the human brain. Specifically, b-CREs under positive selection identified here represent critical targets for future experimental validation to elucidate their functional roles in human brain evolution and disease etiology.
