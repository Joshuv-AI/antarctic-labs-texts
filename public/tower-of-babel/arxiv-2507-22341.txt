Reducing Circuit Depth in Lindblad Simulation via Step-Size Extrapolation
Pegah Mohammadipour, Xiantao Li

Abstract
We study algorithmic error mitigation via Richardson–style extrapolation for quantum simulations of open quantum systems modelled by the Lindblad equation. Focusing on two specific first-order quantum algorithms, we perform a backward-error analysis to obtain a step-size expansion of the density operator with explicit coefficient bounds. These bounds supply the necessary smoothness for analyzing Richardson extrapolation, allowing us to bound both the deterministic bias and the shot-noise variance that arise in post-processing. For a Lindblad dynamics with generator bounded by l l , our main theorem shows that an n = Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ) n=\Omega\bigl(\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr) -point extrapolator reduces the maximum circuit depth needed for accuracy ε \varepsilon from polynomial 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( l ​ T ) 2 / ε ) \mathcal{O}((lT)^{2}/\varepsilon) to polylogarithmic 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( l ​ T ) 2 ​ log ⁡ l ​ log 2 ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ) \mathcal{O}\bigl((lT)^{2}\log l\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)\bigr) scaling, an exponential improvement in 1 / ε 1/\varepsilon , while keeping sampling complexity to the standard 1 / ε 2 1/\varepsilon^{2} level, thus extending such results for Hamiltonian simulations to Lindblad simulations. Several numerical experiments illustrate the practical viability of the method.

Introduction

Simulating quantum systems is widely regarded as one of the central applications of quantum computing
[
12
]
. However, in practice, quantum systems are never perfectly isolated; they inevitably interact with their surrounding environment, leading to phenomena such as decoherence and dissipation. These effects necessitate the study and simulation of
open quantum systems
, whose dynamics are no longer governed solely by the Schrödinger equation but instead require more general frameworks that account for environmental interactions. The evolution of open quantum systems is typically described by
quantum master equations
for the system’s density operator
\rho(t)
, which in the Markovian regime, is governed by the
Lindblad master equation

[
22
,
14
]
,

\frac{d}{dt}\rho=-{i}[H,\rho]+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\kappa_{j}\left(L_{j}\rho L_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},\rho\}\right).

(1)

The Hermitian operator
H
is the system Hamiltonian, which induces the unitary (coherent) part of the evolution. The operators
L_{j}
, called
jump operators
, model different types of interactions between the system and its environment, such as energy loss, decoherence, or particle exchange. Each
\kappa_{j}\geq 0
is a rate parameter that quantifies the strength of the corresponding interaction. Throughout the paper, we will absorb them into
L_{j}
and thus set
\kappa_{j}=1
.

As interest in modeling open quantum systems grows, so too does the need for efficient quantum simulation methods. Numerous quantum algorithms have recently been proposed to simulate open-system dynamics with potential exponential speedups in system dimension
[
20
,
5
,
7
,
21
,
30
,
3
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
9
,
16
,
18
,
4
,
37
]
.
Despite recent progress, circuit depth remains the dominant practical bottleneck for simulating open quantum systems on current noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices.

To approximate the system’s evolution over a total time
T
, most algorithms divide the dynamics into smaller intervals, i.e., time steps, and apply a quantum circuit for each one. As the number of time steps increases, so does the total number of quantum gates. This deepens the circuit and accumulates hardware imperfections such as gate errors and stochastic noise. As a result, the simulated quantum noise may no longer reflect the intended physical model but rather the limitations of the device itself.

This issue parallels that of Hamiltonian (closed-system) simulation, where the quantum circuit approximates the continuous evolution
e^{-iHt}
using discrete gate sequences such as Trotter–Suzuki decompositions. For example, in a first-order Trotter scheme, the circuit depth required to simulate time
T
with error no more than
\varepsilon
, besides a commutator factor, scales as
\mathcal{O}(T^{2}/\varepsilon)
. The parameter
\varepsilon
here quantifies the desired
simulation precision
, i.e., how closely the implemented circuit approximates the ideal evolution.

A promising near-term remedy is
algorithmic error mitigation
, which has been proposed and empirically validated for Hamiltonian simulations
[
11
,
28
,
36
,
24
]
using the Trotter algorithm as the base method. The strategy is elegantly simple:

1.

Run a low-order algorithm (e.g., first-order Trotter) at several different coarse time step sizes
\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots<\tau_{n}
, keeping each quantum circuit shallow.

2.

Combine the resulting expectation values using classical post-processing, typically via Richardson extrapolation or polynomial fitting, to cancel leading-order discretization errors.

The classical extrapolation step requires only
{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3})
floating-point operations. Yet, it effectively eliminates the dominant step-size error term without increasing the quantum depth, replacing the typical inverse-polynomial depth scaling
\mathcal{O}(T^{1+1/p}/\varepsilon^{1/p})
with a polylogarithmic dependence on the target precision
\varepsilon

[
36
]
. This trade-off is especially attractive for NISQ devices, where coherence time is limited and quantum depth is at a premium.

Formally, for any observable
O
we define

f_{\tau}(T)={\Tr}\bigl(\rho_{\tau}(T)\,O\bigr),

(2)

where
\rho_{\tau}(T)
is the density operator produced by a step-size-
\tau
integrator after time
T
.

The dependence on the stepsize is often interpreted from an expansion,

f_{\tau}(T)=f(0)+\alpha_{1}\tau^{p}+\alpha_{2}\tau^{p+1}+\cdots,

(3)

where
f(0)
is the true observable value in the zero-step-size limit, and the
\alpha_{1}\tau^{p}
represents a leading error term of order
p.

The postprocessing step constructs coefficients
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
such that

f_{\text{extrap}}(0)=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\gamma_{i}f_{\tau_{i}}(T),

(4)

Each
f_{\tau_{i}}
is an empirical mean of circuit-level measurements, while the
\gamma_{i}
are determined, for instance, by an interpolation using a polynomial via solving a Vandermonde system or a least-squares fit (regression) to a polynomial of lower degree. In either case, the leading term in the error in
Equation

3
will be improved to
p+m-1
. No additional quantum resources are required, yet the dominant step-size error can be suppressed by
m-1
orders, thus dramatically reducing the maximum circuit depth needed to reach precision
\varepsilon
.

Despite the intuitive appeal of Richardson–style extrapolation, its practical efficacy hinges on two subtle issues.
First, the error expansion (
3
) is valid only when the observable map
f(\tau)
is sufficiently smooth in
\tau
; the derivative bounds depend on the chosen algorithms and must be established rigorously for each algorithm.
Second, each empirical estimate
f(\tau_{i})
in the linear combination (
4
) is subject to statistical fluctuations due to finite sampling (shot noise). As the number of grid points increases, the variance of the extrapolated result may also grow, potentially offsetting the benefits of bias reduction.

In this work, we perform a full bias–variance analysis for two Lindblad-simulation primitives and prove that extrapolation still delivers a net benefit even after accounting for statistical fluctuations.
Our results extend the extrapolation method for Hamiltonian simulations to the inherently irreversible dynamics of open quantum systems and provide the complexity guarantees for error-mitigated Lindblad evolution.

Contributions.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

We analyze two specific quantum algorithms for simulating Lindblad dynamics

.

\|\mathcal{L}\|\leq l,\quad l:=2\norm{H}+2\sum_{j=1}^{J}\norm{L_j}^{2}.

Background

The main purpose of this paper is to approximate the solution to the Lindblad master equation (
1
). We consider the evolution of the density operator
\rho(t)
until the time
T
according to the Lindblad equation (
1
).
To approximate the solution of the Lindblad equation numerically, we rewrite (
1
) as the following initial-value problem:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(t)=\mathcal{L}\rho(t),\quad\rho(0)=\rho_{0},

(8)

with the exact solution:

\rho(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}}\rho_{0}.

In practice, computing the full exponential of
\mathcal{L}
is often infeasible, so we discretize the continuous-time evolution by employing a one-step numerical scheme to approximate the evolution.

Let
\tau>0
be the time-step size, and define the discrete approximation to
e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}
by a linear map
\mathcal{K}(\tau)
, which serves as the
discrete solution operator
. That is,

e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}\approx\mathcal{K}(\tau).

Given this approximation, the state at time
t_{n}=n\tau
is computed iteratively as:

\rho_{n}=\mathcal{K}(\tau)^{n}\rho_{0},

(9)

where
\rho_{n}\approx\rho(t_{n})
is the numerical estimate of the density operator at time
t_{n}
, and
\mathcal{K}(\tau)^{n}
denotes
n
successive applications of
\mathcal{K}(\tau)
. The choice of
\mathcal{K}(\tau)
depends on the specific simulation method used—for instance, it may arise from a Kraus operator expansion, a dilation-based approximation of the Lindblad dynamics, or a Trotter product formula.

We can expand the discrete solution operator
\mathcal{K}

\mathcal{K}(\tau)=I+\tau\mathcal{M}_{1}+\tau^{2}\mathcal{M}_{2}+\tau^{3}\mathcal{M}_{3}+\cdots,

(10)

where
\mathcal{M}_{1}=\mathcal{L}
. This is due to the fact that as
\tau\to 0
, the approximate solution has the same generator as the exact dynamics. This is often referred to as consistency in ODE solvers
[
8
]
.

We observe that the numerical solution exhibits an expansion with powers of the step size.
To improve the accuracy of observable estimates in time-discretized Lindblad simulations, we apply
Richardson extrapolation

[
31
]

to the observables in
Equation

2
.
Specifically, we can evaluate the observable at multiple step sizes
\tau_{j}=T/n_{j}
. A polynomial interpolation or fitting is given by,

p_{n}(\tau)=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\gamma_{j}(\tau)f_{\tau_{j}}.

(11)

For example, when polynomial interpolation is employed, the coefficients
\gamma_{j}
are determined by

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\gamma_{j}\tau_{j}^{m}=0\text{ for }m=p,\dots,p+n-1,\text{ and }\sum_{j=0}^{n}\gamma_{j}=1.

These algebraic conditions help to eliminate the
n
leading error terms, and improve the error to
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{p+n})
.
Aside from polynomial interpolation, regression methods can be used as well, for which the analysis is similar
[
33
]
.

The performance of extrapolation heavily depends on the smoothness properties of
f
in
Equation

2
, which in turn depends on the expansion of
\mathcal{K}(\tau)
in
Equation

10
. In the context of Suzuki-Trotter algorithms for Hamiltonian simulations, such expansion was obtained through an expansion of the shadow Hamiltonian
[
28
]
. In this paper, we use backward error analysis from numerical analysis
[
8
]
and identify the equations governing the coefficients in the error expansion. We summarize the results as follows.

Lemma 1
.

Assume that the approximation method has an expansion in
Equation

10
.
Let
\rho_{\tau}(t)
denote the corresponding approximate solution at time
t
with stepsize
\tau
. There exists a sequence of smooth functions
\Gamma_{k}(t)
such that

\rho_{\tau}(t)=\rho(t)+\tau\Gamma_{1}(t)+\tau^{2}\Gamma_{2}(t)+\dots,

(12)

where
\rho(t)
is the solution to the exact evolution (
1
). For a discrete solution (
10
), these coefficient matrices satisfy the initial value problems
\Gamma_{k}(0)=0
for all
k\geq 1
, and evolution equations,

\Gamma^{\prime}_{k-1}(t)=\mathcal{L}\Gamma_{k-1}(t)-\frac{\mathcal{L}^{k}\rho(t)}{k!}+\sum_{i=2}^{k}\left(\mathcal{M}_{i}\Gamma_{k-i}(t)-\frac{\Gamma^{(i)}_{k-i}(t)}{i!}\right).

(13)

The expansion of the numerical solution with respect to the stepsize follows from standard backward error analysis
[
8
]
. Our analysis considers the Lindblad dynamics and provides an explicit form of the governing equations for the coefficients in the error expansion (
12
). For a detailed proof, refer to
Appendix

A
.

As concrete examples, we consider two approximation methods: Kraus form approximation for the channel induced by the Lindblad equation (
1
) and a dilation-based approximation expressed in a Stinespring form. With these two specific approximations, we will derive the expansion of the corresponding
\mathcal{K}(\tau)
and therefore the error expansion in
Equation

12
by analyzing
Equation

13
. Importantly, we will use these bounds to analyze the improvement brought forth by the extrapolation methods.

3
First-order Approximation by a Kraus Form

One specific construction of the discrete solution operator
\mathcal{K}
is to regard
e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}
as a quantum channel and build the approximation in Kraus form
[
7
]
. We define Kraus operators as follows,

F_{0}=I+\left(-iH-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}\right)\tau,\quad F_{j}=L_{j}\sqrt{\tau},1\leq j\leq J,

(14)

Then, the evolution from
\rho_{n}
to
\rho_{n+1}
can be expressed in the
Kraus form
:

\rho_{n+1}=\mathcal{K}[\rho_{n}]=F_{0}\rho_{n}F_{0}^{\dagger}+F_{1}\rho_{n}F_{1}^{\dagger}+\cdots+F_{J}\rho_{n}F_{J}^{\dagger}.

(15)

One can verify that the scheme in (
15
) constitutes a
first-order
numerical method for simulating Lindblad dynamics
[
7
]
. That is, for sufficiently small step size
\tau
, the channel
\mathcal{K}
approximates the exact evolution
e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}
with an error that scales
l^{2}\tau^{2}
. More precisely, for a fixed initial state
\rho
, the local error satisfies

\left\|\mathcal{K}[\rho]-e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}[\rho]\right\|=\mathcal{O}(l^{2}\tau^{2}),

(16)

which implies that the global error after
N=T/\tau
steps accumulates as
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
. This convergence rate is characteristic of first-order integrators, such as the forward Euler method in classical numerical analysis. The construction in (
14
) can thus be interpreted as a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) analogue of the Euler method tailored for open quantum systems.

The algorithms in
[
7
]
incorporate a compression scheme to achieve higher-order accuracy. However, this approach relies on sophisticated logical gate constructions, which we do not employ here, as our focus is on near-term implementability. Moreover, due to the explicit form of the Kraus operators, each being at most linear in
\tau
, the expansion of
\mathcal{K}
in (
10
) and (
15
) satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{i}=0
for all
i\geq 3
, and

\norm{\mathcal{M}_2}\leq\norm{F_0 - I}^{2}.

(17)

We observe that this is effectively the norm of the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian. Furthermore, using
Equation

14
, we can refine the bound:

\norm{\mathcal{M}_2 }\leq\left(\norm{H}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\norm{L_j}^{2}\right)^{2}=\Theta(l^{2}).

(18)

Using the Kraus form above, we derive bounds on the coefficients in the error expansion (
12
). To obtain explicit estimates, we introduce a generating sequence that characterizes the magnitude of the terms
\Gamma_{k}
and their derivatives appearing in (
12
).

Definition 3.1

(Generating sequence for Kraus Form)
.

We define
c_{i,j,k}
as non-negative real numbers such that
c_{0,0,k}=0\text{ for }k\geq 1
,
c_{i,j,0}=\delta_{j,0}l^{i}
for
i,j\geq 0
, and the rest of the entries are generated from the recursion relations below:

\displaystyle c_{0,j,k}=

\displaystyle\frac{B}{j}\cdot c_{0,j-1,k-1}+\delta_{j,1}\cdot\frac{l^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}+\sum_{p=1}^{k-j}\frac{c_{p+1,j-1,k-p}}{j(p+1)!},\,j=1,2,\cdots,k

(19)

\displaystyle c_{i,j,k}=

\displaystyle l\cdot c_{i-1,j,k}+B\cdot c_{i-1,j,k-1}+\delta_{j,0}\cdot\frac{l^{i+k}}{(k+1)!}+\sum_{p=1}^{k-j}\frac{c_{i+p,j,k-p}}{(p+1)!},\,j=0,1,\cdots,k-1

\displaystyle c_{i,k,k}=

\displaystyle l\cdot c_{i-1,k,k}.

where

is the bound on the Lindblad generator
Equation

7

The elements of the sequence can be generated recursively using the definition above. Moreover, with an inductive argument, we can establish the following bounds on the growth of the sequence.

Let

. This result is important because it provides explicit bounds on the terms

Remark 3.4
.

Let
f
be as in
Theorem
3.3
, and let
p_{n}(\tau)
denote the degree-

polynomial interpolant of

at the equidistant nodes
\{\tau_{j}=jh\}_{j=1}^{n+1}
, where

. Then, for any desired extrapolation accuracy

, if

(28)

we obtain the approximation guarantee

\bigl|f(0)-p_{n}(0)\bigr|<\varepsilon,

which is calculated using the classical exponential convergence of analytic functions under interpolation at equispaced points
[
33
]
. This shows that extrapolation using equidistant nodes can, in principle, achieve

However, as observed in prior work
[
36
,
2
]
, each term in the extrapolated estimator

p_{n}(0)=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\gamma_{j}f(\tau_{j})

(29)

must be independently sampled and is subject to statistical error. The magnitude of this noise is controlled by the quantity
\|\gamma\|_{1}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}|\gamma_{j}|
, which coincides with the
Lebesgue constant
of the interpolation process
[
33
]
. For equidistant nodes, the Lebesgue constant grows exponentially with

as

\|\gamma\|_{1}=2^{n+1}-1,

(30)

as shown in classical interpolation theory
[
1
]
. Combining this with the lower bound (
28

, the resulting

in the estimator, assuming fixed shot noise per circuit, scales as
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{4})
, which renders the method impractical for high-precision settings. Thus, despite the favorable bias error from extrapolation, the large variance due to equispaced interpolation negates any advantage over direct estimation.

Successful extrapolation methods are typically based on Chebyshev nodes due to their superior stability and approximation properties. In our setting, we adapt this strategy by mapping Chebyshev nodes to lie within the interval
[0,\,\tau_{\max}/(T^{2}\log T)]

[

Lemma 4
.

Let
f
be as in Theorem

, and let

denote the degree-

[0,\,\tau_{\max}/(T^{2}\log T)]
:

\tau_{k}=\frac{\tau_{\max}}{2T^{2}\log T}\left(1-\cos\theta_{k}\right),k=1,\dots,n+1,

(31)

where
\theta_{k}=\frac{2k-1}{2(n+1)}\pi
. Then, for
n\geq\frac{1}{3\log 2}\log\left(\sigma/\varepsilon\right)-\frac{2}{3}
, we have

|f(0)-p_{n}(0)|<\varepsilon.

The result follows by applying the definition of Chebyshev nodes, mapping them to the desired interval, and using the interpolation error formula, all of which are available in
[
33
]
.

Lemma 5
.

[
33
, Theorem 15.2]

Let
\{\tau_{j}=\frac{\tau_{\max}}{2T^{2}}\left(1-\cos\theta_{j}\right)\}_{j=1}^{n},
where
\theta_{j}=\frac{2j-1}{2n}\pi
, be the Chebyshev nodes of the first kind mapped to
[0,\,\tau_{\max}/(T^{2}\log T)]
, then the sum of the absolute values of the Lagrange basis functions evaluated at zero,
\gamma_{j}=L_{j}(0)
, is bounded logarithmically:

\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\gamma_{k}|=\mathcal{O}(\log n).

(32)

The importance of the logarithmic bound of
\norm{\gamma}_{1}
can be appreciated from the Hoeffding bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|S_{N_{S}}-\mathbb{E}(S_{N_{S}})|\geq\varepsilon)\leq 2\exp(- \frac{\varepsilon^2 N_S}{2 \alpha^2 (\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^n \abs{\gamma_j} )^2}).

(33)

Equivalently, to ensure accuracy
\varepsilon
with failure probability at most
\delta
, we require
N_{S}
samples

N_{S}\geq\frac{2\alpha^{2}\|\gamma\|_{1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}.

(34)

While the exponential dependence in
Equation

30
will lead to higher sampling complexity, a polynomial or a logarithmic scaling with
n
will retain a standard
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
complexity.

In any digital simulations of the Lindblad dynamics, the step size
\{\tau_{j}\}

must be chosen such that the number of steps is an integer. This practical constraint means we cannot use the ideal Chebyshev nodes directly and must instead use nearby ’quantized’ time steps, the effect of which must be taken into account.
Intuitively, to ensure that the perturbed nodes remain distinct and well-conditioned, the perturbation magnitude
|\tau_{j}-\xi_{j}|
must be smaller than the minimal spacing between Chebyshev nodes. From
Theorem
3.3
, this spacing is of order
\mathcal{O}(\tau_{\max}/n^{2}T^{2}\log T)
, which provides a bound on the allowable perturbation size. The next lemma investigates the robustness of the Chebyshev node configuration by analyzing how perturbations affect the associated Lebesgue constant. Although the robustness of Chebyshev interpolation is well studied in numerical analysis
[
35
]
, we provide a self-contained proof in the Appendix tailored to this discrete, integer-aligned perturbation. Unlike the analysis in
[
23
]
, our argument does not rely on a Taylor expansion that neglects higher-order terms.

Lemma 6

(Effect of Perturbed Chebyshev Nodes on Variance Bound)
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\tau>0
, and define Chebyshev nodes on
[0,\tau]
by

\xi_{j}=\frac{\tau}{2}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{2j-1}{2n+2}\pi\right)\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,n+1.

(35)

If the time parameter
\hat{T}>\pi^{2}\tau n^{2}
, define the perturbed nodes by

k_{j}:=\bigl\lceil\hat{T}/\xi_{j}\bigr\rceil,\qquad\tau_{j}:=\frac{\hat{T}}{k_{j}},\qquad j=1,\dots,n+1.

Then the following statements hold:

(i)

The perturbed nodes are strictly ordered:
0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\dots<\tau_{n+1}<\tau
.

(ii)

k_{1}>k_{2}>\dots>k_{n+1}
are pairwise distinct positive integers.

(iii)

There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
(independent of
n
and
\hat{T}
)
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\absolutevalue{\gamma_j}\leq Cn^{\frac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}}\log n.

(36)

Furthermore, if
\hat{T}>2\tau\,n^{2}\log n
, then
\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\absolutevalue{\gamma_j}=\mathcal{O}(\log n).

We provide the proof in
Appendix

E
. In view of
Equation

24
, we set
\hat{T}=1
and

\tau:=\frac{\tau_{\max}}{T^{2}\log T}.

Under the lemma’s threshold
\hat{T}>\pi^{2}\tau n^{2}
, this is equivalent to

T^{2}\log T>\pi^{2}\,\tau_{\max}\,n^{2}=\Omega\bigl(\tau_{\max}n^{2}\bigr).

If we instead enforce the stronger condition
\hat{T}>2\,\tau\,n^{2}\log n
, then

T^{2}\log T>2\,\tau_{\max}\,n^{2}\log n=\Omega\bigl(\tau_{\max}n^{2}\log n\bigr).

(37)

In what follows, we adopt this latter scaling. Combining it with the target precision
\varepsilon
(and the decay prescribed by Theorem
3.3
), it is sufficient to choose

T\geq\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)\,\sqrt{\log\log(1/\varepsilon)}}{\,l\,\sqrt{\log l}\,}.

(38)

Combining the Gevrey-class smoothness from
Theorem

3.3
, with the variance control for perturbed Chebyshev nodes from
Lemma

6
, and the Hoeffding bound
Equation

33
, we arrive at the following end-to-end complexity guarantee:

Theorem 3.5
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, assume that the simulation time satisfies
Eq.

38
.
The Richardson extrapolation
p_{n}(\tau)
of
f(\tau)
using
n+1
Chebyshev nodes, with each expectation value
f(\tau_{j})
, generated from the first-order approximation (
15
) and sampled with
N_{S}
shots, is guaranteed to produce an estimator

\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{p_n(0) - f(0) }<\varepsilon\right)>1-\delta,

(39)

provided that
n=\Omega(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
,
T=\Omega\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\right)
, and
N_{S}=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

In addition, to prepare
\rho(T)
(i.e.,
{\hat{\rho}}(1)
from (
24
)), the maximum circuit depth of is
d_{\max}=\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}=\frac{T^{2}}{\tau_{\max}}n^{2}=(lT)^{2}\log l\log^{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}.

In contrast, the circuit depth without extrapolation, in order for the error
l^{2}T\tau<\varepsilon
, has to be
T/\tau=\mathcal{O}((lT)^{2}/\varepsilon)
. Therefore, our bound is exponentially better in

and remains the same in

The results above can be readily extended to polynomial regression using Chebyshev nodes. In this case, the coefficients

Further, the number of nodes scales logarithmically with

4
First-order Approximation by Hamiltonian Dilation

Another type of algorithm is based on a dilation with
a dilated Hamiltonian
[
7
,
9
]
. The main idea is to simulate the non-unitary Lindblad dynamics by embedding them into a unitary evolution on a larger Hilbert space. To this end, one introduces an ancilla register with Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{A}=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{1},\ket{2},\dots,\ket{J}\},

(41)

which acts as a control register. A unitary channel is then constructed on the composite space
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{S}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{S}
is the system Hilbert space. This unitary approximates the dissipative evolution of the open system when followed by a partial trace over the ancilla register. This operation is repeated at every time step. Specifically, let
\epsilon=\sqrt{\tau}
, and define the block-structured Hamiltonian as follows:

H:=\epsilon^{2}H_{0}+\epsilon H_{1},

(42)

with

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes H_{S},

(43)

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\outerproduct{j}{0}\otimes L_{j}+\outerproduct{0}{j}\otimes L_{j}^{\dagger}\right).

Then, one step of the Lindblad evolution can be approximated by

e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}\rho\approx\mathcal{K}(\tau)\rho:=\tr_{A}\left(e^{-iH}\left(\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes\rho\right)e^{iH}\right).

(44)

This operation is repeated at each time step.

Unlike the Kraus-form approximation in
Eq.

14
, this Stinespring-type dilation does not require amplitude amplification. On the other hand, the associated error expansion
Equation

10
, which is needed for the backward error analysis, is more involved. In
Appendix

F
, we carry out this analysis, and our main result is summarized below.

Theorem 4.1

(Local Error Expansion)
.

The dilated Hamiltonian approach (
44
) produces an approximation as a reduced density operator
\rho_{\text{R}}
. It can be expanded as,

\rho_{\text{R}}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\ \tau^{k}\rho^{(2k)}_{\text{R}}.

(45)

In particular,
\rho^{(0)}_{\text{R}}=\rho
and
\rho^{(2)}_{\text{R}}=\mathcal{L}\rho
.
Furthermore for every integer
k\geq 2

\norm{\rho^{(2k)}_\text{R}}\leq(J+1)\frac{l^{k}}{k!}.

(46)

This shows that the corresponding terms in expansion (
10
) have the following bounds,

\mathcal{M}_{k}\leq(J+1)\frac{l^{k}}{k!},\quad k\geq 2.

(47)

Building on the derivative bounds above, we obtain a concentration bound on the sampling complexity, analogous to the previous case. The result is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
. Assume that the simulation time satisfies
Eq.

38
.
The Richardson extrapolation
p_{n}(\tau)
of
f(\tau)
using
n+1
perturbed Chebyshev nodes, with each expectation value
f(\tau_{j})
, generated from the dilated Hamiltonian approximation (
44
) and sampled with
N_{S}
shots, is guaranteed to produce an estimator

\mathbb{P}\left(\absolutevalue{p_n(0) - f(0) }<\varepsilon\right)>1-\delta,

(48)

provided that
n=\Omega(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
, and
N_{S}=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

In addition, to prepare
\rho(T)
(i.e.,
{\rho}(1)
from (
24
)), the maximum circuit depth of is
d_{\max}=\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}=\frac{T^{2}}{\tau_{\max}}n^{2}=(lT)^{2}\log l\log^{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}.

6
Summary and further discussions

We have carried out a comprehensive bias–variance analysis of Richardson-style

algorithmic error mitigation
for first-order quantum algorithms that simulate
Lindblad dynamics. Without invoking any
a-priori
smoothness assumptions, we proved that an

n=\Omega\bigl(\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr)
-point extrapolator
reduces the maximum circuit depth required to reach precision
\varepsilon

from
\mathcal{O}((lT)^{2}/\varepsilon)
to

\mathcal{O}\bigl((lT)^{2}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)\bigr)
, while preserving the
standard
1/\varepsilon^{2}
sampling complexity.
Extensive numerical experiments confirmed that the predicted depth savings
translate into measurable fidelity gains on noisy hardware. This framework opens the door to provably robust quantum simulations of dissipative dynamics on NISQ devices, an essential ingredient for many problems in quantum chemistry and physics.

Extensions.

•

Higher-order integrators.
The backward-error machinery developed
here applies verbatim to second-order product formulas

[
6
]
and quantum channel or dilation schemes

[
21
,
9
]
. Updating the algebraic expansion
yields Richardson weights of the same closed form; the asymptotic depth
further improves by a constant factor.

•

Integration with Trotter Extrapolation.
The dilated Hamiltonian approach is implemented by Hamiltonian simulation, for which a Trotter algorithm provides a simple implementation. More importantly, we can integrate the current approach with the extrapolation methods for
Trotter algorithms
[
23
,
36
]
. Together, they can provide a powerful near-term approach for simulating open quantum systems.

•

Joint mitigation of algorithmic and physical errors.

Recent work
[
11
,
24
]
shows that step-size
extrapolation can be combined with zero-noise extrapolation.
Integrating our bias bounds with a hardware-noise model is a promising
route to end-to-end error budgets on NISQ devices.

Outlook.

The analysis confirms that algorithmic extrapolation is not merely a heuristic
but a provably effective depth-reduction strategy for open-system simulation.
Because the technique requires no additional quantum resources beyond repeated
execution of shallow circuits, it can be implemented on current hardware
without modification. Future work includes extending the framework to
time-dependent Lindblad generators, Lindblad control problems,
as well as exploring adaptive grid selection to
optimize the bias–variance trade-off in real time.

Detailed derivation of the error expansion

Lemma
1
.

Let
\rho_{\tau}(t)
denote the approximate solution at time
t
with stepsize
\tau

\Gamma_{k}(t)
such that

\rho_{\tau}(t)=\rho(t)+\tau\Gamma_{1}(t)+\tau^{2}\Gamma_{2}(t)+\dots,

(50)

where
\rho(t)
is the solution to the exact evolution (
1

10
), these coefficient matrices satisfy the initial value problems
\Gamma_{k}(0)=0
for all
k\geq 1
, and evolution equations,

\Gamma^{\prime}_{k-1}(t)=\mathcal{L}\Gamma_{k-1}(t)-\frac{\mathcal{L}^{k}\rho(t)}{k!}+\sum_{i=2}^{k}\left(\mathcal{M}_{i}\Gamma_{k-i}(t)-\frac{\Gamma^{(i)}_{k-i}(t)}{i!}\right).

(51)

Proof.

The first part of the lemma follows Theorem 4.37 from
[
8
]
. The part that remains to prove is (
51
). Note that we are using a numerical scheme that updates the approximate solution in each step according to:

\rho_{n+1}=\mathcal{K}(\tau)\rho_{n}.

(52)

On the left-hand side of (
52
), we can expand
\rho_{\tau}(t+\tau)
. Since
\rho(t+\tau)=e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}\rho(t)
,
we have

\rho_{\tau}(t+\tau)=e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}\rho(t)+\tau\Gamma_{1}(t+\tau)+\tau^{2}\Gamma_{2}(t+\tau)+\cdots.

By expanding

\displaystyle e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{k}}{k!}\mathcal{L}^{k}

\rho_{\tau}(t+\tau)=\rho(t)+\tau\left(\mathcal{L}\rho(t)+\Gamma_{1}(t+\tau)\right)+\tau^{2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}^{2}\rho(t)}{2!}+\Gamma_{2}(t+\tau)\right)+\tau^{3}\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}^{3}\rho(t)}{3!}+\Gamma_{3}(t+\tau)\right)+\cdots

(54)

which can be compactly written as

(55)

Using the Taylor series, we can also expand

\Gamma_{k}(t+\tau)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{m}}{m!}\,\Gamma^{(m)}_{k}(t).

(56)

Substituting this expansion into (

\rho_{\tau}(t+\tau)=\rho(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tau^{k}\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}^{k}\rho(t)}{k!}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\Gamma^{(k-j)}_{j}(t)}{(k-j)!}\right)=\rho(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\tau^{j}\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}^{j}\rho(t)}{j!}+\sum_{i=0}^{j}\frac{\Gamma^{(i)}_{j-i}(t)}{i!}\right).

(57)

On the right-hand side of (

The equality of (

\frac{\mathcal{L}^{j}\rho(t)}{j!}+\sum_{i=0}^{j}\frac{\Gamma_{\,j-i}^{(i)}(t)}{i!}=\sum_{p=0}^{j}\mathcal{M}_{\,j-p}\,\Gamma_{p}(t).

Rewriting the left sum by isolating the

\Gamma_{j}(t)=\sum_{p=0}^{j}\mathcal{M}_{\,j-p}\,\Gamma_{p}(t)-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{\Gamma_{\,j-i}^{(i)}(t)}{i!}-\frac{\mathcal{L}^{j}\rho(t)}{j!}

Rearranging the terms gives,

\Gamma^{\prime}_{k-1}(t)=\mathcal{L}\Gamma_{k-1}(t)-\frac{\mathcal{L}^{k}\rho(t)}{k!}+\sum_{i=2}^{k}\left(\mathcal{M}_{i}\Gamma_{k-i}(t)-\frac{\Gamma^{(i)}_{k-i}(t)}{i!}\right).

(62)

Appendix D
Error bounds

Theorem
3.3
.
Let

24

For a fixed final time

Appendix E
The Lebesgue constant for the perturbed Chebyshev nodes

Lemma

6
(Effect of Perturbed Chebyshev Nodes on Variance Bound)
Let
n\geq 2
and
\tau>0
, and define Chebyshev nodes on
[0,\tau]
by

\xi_{j}=\frac{\tau}{2}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{2j-1}{2n+2}\pi\right)\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,n+1.

(100)

If the time parameter
\hat{T}>\pi^{2}\tau n^{2}
, define the perturbed nodes by

k_{j}:=\bigl\lceil\hat{T}/\xi_{j}\bigr\rceil,\qquad\tau_{j}:=\frac{\hat{T}}{k_{j}},\qquad j=1,\dots,n+1.

Then the following statements hold:

(i)

The perturbed nodes are strictly ordered:
0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\dots<\tau_{n+1}<\tau
.

(ii)

k_{1}>k_{2}>\dots>k_{n+1}
are pairwise distinct positive integers.

(iii)

There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
(independent of
n
and
\hat{T}
)
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\absolutevalue{\gamma_j}\leq Cn^{\frac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}}\log n.

(101)

Furthermore, if
\hat{T}>2\tau\,n^{2}\log n
, then
\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\absolutevalue{\gamma_j}=\mathcal{O}(\log n).

Proof.

(i)–(ii)
Since
j\mapsto\xi_{j}
is strictly increasing,
j\mapsto\hat{T}/\xi_{j}
is strictly decreasing, hence
k_{1}\geq\cdots\geq k_{n+1}
. To see distinctness, note

\frac{\hat{T}}{\xi_{j}}-\frac{\hat{T}}{\xi_{j+1}}=\hat{T}\,\frac{\xi_{j+1}-\xi_{j}}{\xi_{j}\xi_{j+1}}\geq\frac{\hat{T}}{2\tau n^{2}},

using
\xi_{j+1}-\xi_{j}\geq\tau/(2n^{2})
and
\xi_{j}\xi_{j+1}\leq\tau^{2}
.
Since
\hat{T}>\pi^{2}\tau n^{2}
, the difference exceeds
\pi^{2}/2>1
, so

\lceil\hat{T}/\xi_{j}\rceil\neq\lceil\hat{T}/\xi_{j+1}\rceil
. Because
\tau_{j}=\hat{T}/k_{j}
, the
\tau_{j}
are strictly increasing and satisfy
\tau_{j}\leq\xi_{j}<\tau
.

(iii)

Write
r_{j}:=\hat{T}/\xi_{j}
, so
k_{j}=\lceil r_{j}\rceil
and

0\leq\frac{\xi_{j}-\tau_{j}}{\xi_{j}}=1-\frac{r_{j}}{k_{j}}=\frac{k_{j}-r_{j}}{k_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{r_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}n^{2}}=:\delta_{\max}.

(102)

Hence
|\log(\tau_j/\xi_j)|\leq 2\delta_{\max}
because
\delta_{\max}\leq 1/2
.
Let
P:=\prod_{m=1}^{n+1}\xi_{m}
and
\widetilde{P}:=\prod_{m=1}^{n+1}\tau_{m}
. Then

\Bigl|\frac{\widetilde{P}}{P}-1\Bigr|=\Bigl|\exp\Bigl(\sum_{m=1}^{n+1}\log(\tau_m/\xi_m)\Bigr)-1\Bigr|\leq\frac{A_{P}}{n},\qquad A_{P}:=\frac{2e^{2/\pi^{2}}}{\pi^{2}}<\tfrac{1}{4}.

(103)

For the pairwise gaps, set
g_{jk}:=\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}
. Summing the consecutive-gap bound

\xi_{m+1}-\xi_{m}\geq\tau/(2n^{2})
shows

|g_{jk}|\geq\frac{\tau}{2n^{2}}\,|j-k|.

(104)

Writing
\tau_{j}-\tau_{k}=g_{jk}\,(1+\varepsilon_{jk})
with

\varepsilon_{jk}=\frac{\xi_{j}(\tau_{j}/\xi_{j}-1)-\xi_{k}(\tau_{k}/\xi_{k}-1)}{g_{jk}},

(105)

and using
\xi_{j},\xi_{k}\leq\tau
and
|\tau_{\ell}/\xi_{\ell}-1|\leq\delta_{\max}
, we obtain

|\varepsilon_{jk}|\leq\frac{2\tau\,\delta_{\max}}{|g_{jk}|}\leq\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\,\frac{1}{|j-k|},\qquad j\neq k.

(106)

Define
D_{j}:=\prod_{k\neq j}(1+\varepsilon_{jk})
. Since

|\varepsilon_{jk}|\leq 4/\pi^{2}<\tfrac{1}{2}
, the inequality

|\log(1+x)|\leq|x|/(1-|x|)
gives

|\log D_{j}|\leq\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{|\varepsilon_{jk}|}{1-|\varepsilon_{jk}|}\leq\frac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{1}{m}\leq\frac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}\log(en).

(107)

Hence both
|D_{j}|\leq A\,n^{\frac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}}
and
|1/D_{j}|\leq A\,n^{\frac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}}
with
A:=e^{\frac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}}
.

Weights.

Let
\gamma_{j}
(resp.
\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}
) be the barycentric/extrapolation weights for
\{\xi_{j}\}
(resp.
\{\tau_{j}\}
). From the product formulas,

\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}=\left(\frac{\widetilde{P}}{P}\right)\left(\frac{\xi_{j}}{\tau_{j}}\right)\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}}{\tau_{j}-\tau_{k}}=\frac{\widetilde{P}/P}{\tau_{j}/\xi_{j}}\cdot\frac{1}{D_{j}},

(108)

so using the bounds above,

\biggl|\frac{\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}\biggr|\leq\Bigl(1+\frac{A_{P}}{n}\Bigr)\,(1+\delta_{\max})\,A\,n^{\frac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}}\leq C\,n^{\frac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}}\quad(n\geq 2).

(109)

Since
\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}|\gamma_{j}|=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
for Chebyshev nodes, we conclude

\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}|\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}|\leq C\,n^{\frac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}}\log n
.

Improved threshold.

If
\hat{T}>2\tau n^{2}\log n
, then

\delta_{\max}\leq 1/(2n^{2}\log n)
, which sharpens

|\varepsilon_{jk}|
by a factor
1/\log n
. Consequently

|\log D_{j}|\leq\tfrac{4}{\pi^{2}-4}\,\frac{\log(en)}{\log n}
,
so
D_{j}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\sum_{j}|\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}|=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
.
∎

(118)

Lemma 9
.

For the expansion (
111
)
satisfies the bound,

\norm{\rho^{(2n)}}\leq\frac{(\Lambda)^{n}}{n!}.

(119)

Proof.

This bound comes from the direct expansion in
Equation

117
. We notice that,

\|\operatorname{ad}_{H_{0}}\rho\|=\|[H_{0},\rho]\|\leq\Lambda_{0}\|\rho\|,\quad\|\operatorname{ad}_{H_{1}}\rho\|\leq{\Lambda_{1}}\|\rho\|.

Thus each term with a label in the bitstring in
Equation

117
can be bounded as,

\Lambda_{0}^{n-m}\Lambda_{1}^{2m}.

Recall that each bit string contains
2m
symbols 1’s and
n-m
0’s, and in total, contains
\frac{(n+m)!}{(2m)!\,(n-m)!}
terms, leading to the total bound,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{(n+m)!}{[(2m)!]^{2}[(n-m)!]^{2}}(\Lambda_{0})^{\,n-m}\Lambda_{1}^{2m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{2^{n+m}}{(2m)!(n-m)!}(\Lambda_{0})^{\,n-m}\Lambda_{1}^{2m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{2^{n}}{m!(n-m)!}(\Lambda_{0})^{\,n-m}\Lambda_{1}^{2m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(\Lambda)^{n}}{n!}.

Here we have used
(2m)!\geq 2^{m}m!
and the binomial bound
\binom{n+m}{n-m}\leq 2^{n+m}
, together with the norm definition
Equation

118
.
∎

We are now ready to state the main theorem regarding the local error expansion. By noticing that,
the partial trace over the ancilla,

\rho^{(2n)}_{\text{R}}=\tr_{A}\left(\rho^{(2n)}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\left(I_{A}\otimes P_{j}\right)\rho^{(2n)}\left(I_{A}\otimes P_{j}\right),

(120)

we reach the following theorem.

Theorem F.1

(Even–order bound)
.

Let
\Lambda
be as defined in
Eq.

118
.
Then, the dilated Hamiltonian approach (
44
) produces an approximation
\rho_{\text{R}}
that can be expanded as,

\rho_{\text{R}}(\epsilon)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\epsilon^{2n}\rho^{(2n)}_{\text{R}}.

(121)

Furthermore,
for every integer
n\geq 0

\norm{\rho^{(2n)}_\text{R} (\epsilon)}\leq(J+1)\frac{\Lambda^{n}}{n!}.

(122)

Proof.

Lemma
7
shows that

\rho^{(k)}
is block–diagonal (ancilla–diagonal) when
k
is even, and purely off–diagonal when
k
is odd.
Because the partial trace over the ancilla keeps only the diagonal
blocks, we have

\tr_{A}\bigl(\rho^{(k)}\bigr)=0\quad\text{whenever }k\text{ is odd}.

(123)

Hence

\rho_{R}(\epsilon):=\tr_{A}\!\bigl(U(\epsilon)\rho_{0}U(\epsilon)^{\dagger}\bigr)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\epsilon^{2n}\rho_{R}^{(2n)},\qquad\rho_{R}^{(2n)}:=\tr_{A}\bigl(\rho^{(2n)}\bigr),

(124)

establishing the expansion (
121
).
For the last part, by
Eq.

120
, and taking the partial trace over
\mathcal{H}_{A}
yields

\rho_{R}^{(2n)}=\tr_{A}\!\bigl(\rho^{(2n)}\bigr)=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\tr_{A}\!\left(I_{A}\otimes P_{j}\right)\rho^{(2n)}\left(I_{A}\otimes P_{j}\right).

(125)

For every
j
, the map

\mathcal{P}_{j}(X):=(I_{A}\otimes P_{j})X(I_{A}\otimes P_{j})
is a contraction
in operator norm,
hence

\|\rho_{j}^{(2n)}\|\;=\;\bigl\|\tr_{A}\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{j}(\rho^{(2n)})\bigr)\bigr\|\;\leq\;\bigl\|\mathcal{P}_{j}(\rho^{(2n)})\bigr\|\;\leq\;\bigl\|\rho^{(2n)}\bigr\|.

(126)

By the lemma proved earlier,

\|\rho^{(2n)}\|\leq\Lambda^{\,n}/n!
.

Using the triangle inequality,

\bigl\|\rho_{R}^{(2n)}\bigr\|\;=\;\Bigl\|\sum_{j=0}^{J}\rho_{j}^{(2n)}\Bigr\|\;\leq\;\sum_{j=0}^{J}\bigl\|\rho_{j}^{(2n)}\bigr\|\;\leq\;(J+1)\,\frac{\Lambda^{\,n}}{n!}.

(127)
