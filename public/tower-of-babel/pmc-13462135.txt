A vision-language foundation model improves preoperative diagnosis of follicular thyroid neoplasms using ultrasound images
Authors: Shufang Pei, Xin Chen, Hui Shen, Yingjia Li, Kai Yang, Juanjuan Liu, Bin Huang, Xuewei Wu, Ting Liang, Dan Yang, Yunjun Yang, Fei Wang, Jingjing You, Zhe Jin, Wenle He, Jie Sun, Lijuan Liu, Fei Guo, Zhihong Lan, Guifeng Tu, Lizhu Ouyang, Shuyi Liu, Xiaoxiao Feng, Yue Huang, Shuixing Zhang, Zhong Liu, Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preoperative discrimination between follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) and follicular thyroid adenoma (FTA) remains challenging, as imaging and cytological approaches often show limited efficacy. Even fine-needle aspiration (FNA) biopsy and intraoperative frozen sections frequently fail to provide conclusive results. Thus, follicular thyroid neoplasms (FNs) typically necessitate complete surgical excision for definitive diagnosis, leading to unnecessary thyroidectomies for benign conditions or delayed treatment for malignancies. To address this gap, we developed FTC-Net, a vision-language foundation model, to preoperatively classify FNs using ultrasound images. In a multicenter retrospective study of 2421 patients (6477 images) from 14 institutions, FTC-Net was trained on 1462 patients and validated in two independent cohorts (n = 578 and n = 381). FTC-Net achieved AUCs of 0.836 and 0.841 in external validation, outperforming benchmark deep learning models and established TI-RADS systems. It also substantially reduced both total FNA rates and unnecessary FNA rates compared to ACR TI-RADS and C-TI-RADS. FTC-Net has the potential to serve as a non-invasive and advanced tool for the preoperative diagnosis of FNs, thereby improving clinical decision-making and reducing unnecessary procedures.

Introduction

Follicular thyroid neoplasms (FNs) are epithelial-origin thyroid tumors that primarily consist of two subtypes: follicular thyroid adenoma (FTA) and follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC)1. FTA represents the most prevalent benign follicular-patterned thyroid tumor, while FTC constitutes 10–18% of all thyroid malignancies, ranking as the second most common differentiated thyroid cancer after papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC)2. In contrast to PTC, FTC predominantly metastasizes hematogenously to distant sites such as lungs and bones, resulting in worse prognosis, with some patients presenting with bone or pulmonary metastases at initial diagnosis3.

Ultrasound and fine-needle aspiration (FNA) cytology have been established as first-line modalities for evaluating thyroid nodules. However, unlike PTC which demonstrates distinctive imaging features, FTC, and FTA share considerable imaging overlap, significantly limiting the diagnostic accuracy of radiological assessment alone4. When evaluated using the American College of Radiology Thyroid Imaging, Reporting and Data System (ACR TI-RADS), FNs are typically categorized as TI-RADS 3 or 4 lesions, indicating low-to-intermediate suspicion nodules for which FNA biopsy is recommended per current guidelines5. However, while FNA biopsy can identify follicular-patterned lesions, it cannot reliably distinguish between FTA and FTC6. This fundamental diagnostic gap stems from FNA’s inability to evaluate the histological hallmarks of malignancy, specifically capsular or vascular invasion7. Although molecular testing reveals associations between PAX8-PPARG fusions, RAS mutations, TERT promoter alterations, and malignant potential, their diagnostic utility remains constrained by the significant overlap in oncogenic pathways between FTC and FTA8. Additionally, the low sensitivity (43%) of intraoperative frozen section (IFS) argues against its routine application for intraoperative decision-making regarding the extent of surgery in FNs9,10. Thus, conventional preoperative and intraoperative tools, including ultrasound, risk stratification systems (RSSs), FNA biopsy, molecular analysis, and IFS, lack sufficient reliability to distinguish FTA from FTC. A conclusive diagnosis ultimately depends on the histologic identification of capsular invasion, vascular invasion, or extrathyroidal extension in surgically resected specimens11. This diagnostic paradigm poses substantial challenges for radiologists, thyroid surgeons, and cytopathologists, emphasizing the urgent need for improved preoperative risk stratification tools that are crucial for appropriate therapeutic interventions of FNs.

The rapid development of advanced imaging analysis techniques, particularly those leveraging radiomics and deep learning (DL) technologies, has opened new avenues for enhancing the preoperative diagnosis of FNs. While several radiomic models12–16 have been built to differentiate FTC from FTA, they have faced challenges such as suboptimal performance and risk of overfitting. Leveraging its powerful pattern recognition capabilities, DL has demonstrated strong performance in image and visual recognition tasks, significantly transforming medical diagnostics through robust tools for analyzing and interpreting imaging data. While earlier studies17–19 have largely applied DL to differentiate benign from malignant thyroid nodules, few studies have specifically used ultrasound images to distinguish between FTC and FTA.

Currently, the pre-trained vision-language foundation models have achieved remarkable success in the various downstream tasks20–22. The models typically demonstrate superior generalization performance compared to single-modality models (e.g., image or text only). We aimed to refine a pre-trained vision-language foundation model called Sigmoid Loss for Language-Image Pre-Training (SigLIP23) to develop an FTC recognition network (hereafter, FTC-Net) that differentiates FTC from FTA using ultrasound images from multi-institutional cohorts. We evaluated the diagnostic performance of FTC-Net against other DL models and general-purpose (ACR TI-RADS24 and Chinese TI-RADS25, referred to as C-TI-RADS) and specialized (follicular TI-RADS26, referred to as F-TI-RADS) RSSs. We assume that the proposed FTC-Net has the potential to refine clinical decision-making for FNs by providing a noninvasive tool to reduce unnecessary biopsies and surgeries while optimizing risk stratification.

During training and internal validation, the FTC-Net did not demonstrate any statistically significant advantage in diagnostic accuracy over DL architectures. However, when evaluated on the external validation cohorts, the FTC-Net demonstrated superior discriminative performance, achieving AUC values of 0.836 (95% CI: 0.795–0.877) and 0.841 (95% CI: 0.787–0.895), respectively. These results significantly outperformed those of VGG16 (AUC = 0.711, 95% CI: 0.664–0.758 and 0.761, 95% CI: 0.704–0.818), ResNet50 (AUC = 0.729, 95% CI: 0.683–0.774 and 0.771, 95% CI: 0.715–0.827), Inception-ResNet-V2 (AUC = 0.532, 95% CI: 0.478–0.586 and 0.632, 95% CI: 0.565–0.699), MiTB0 (AUC = 0.771, 95% CI: 0.730–0.813 and 0.760, 95% CI: 0.701–0.819), EfficientNet-B0 (AUC = 0.726, 95% CI: 0.688–0.763 and 0.702, 95% CI: 0.655–0.749), and ConvNeXt-B (AUC = 0.768, 95% CI: 0.732–0.802 and 0.769, 95% CI: 0.723–0.810). DeLong’s test confirmed the superiority of FTC-Net with statistically significant differences (all P values < 0.05). In addition, the FTC-Net yielded the most favorable balance between the sensitivity (80.9% vs. 36.9–87.3% and 76.4% vs. 57.3–73.0%) and specificity (79.3% vs. 54.4–72.4% and 82.2% vs. 48.6–82.5%) when compared to DL models. The FTC-Net demonstrated superior negative predictive value (NPV) compared to DL approaches, achieving 91.8% (95% CI: 88.2–94.7%) vs. 75.5–92.0% in the external validation cohort 1 and 92.0% (95% CI: 88.8–94.5%) vs. 85.5–88.3% in the external validation cohort 2.

Stratification analysis of performance in the FTC-Net

To evaluate the robustness of the FTC-Net across clinically relevant subgroups, we conducted stratified analyses by age (≤50 vs. >50 years), sex, nodule size (≤3 vs. >3 cm), ultrasound device manufacturer, and the number of input images (≤3 vs. >3) (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). In the two external validation cohorts, the FTC-Net exhibited relatively consistent accuracy across age (AUC: 0.832 vs. 0.842; 0.828 vs. 0.858), sex (AUC: 0.828 vs. 0.839; 0.847 vs. 0.834), and nodule size categories (AUC: 0.877 vs. 0.815; 0.825 vs. 0.925). However, ultrasound manufacturer variations were found in the external validation cohort 1 (AUC range: 0.614–0.922), likely due to sample size limitations for some devices. The variations were less pronounced in external validation cohort 2 (AUC range: 0.786–0.929). In addition, the model showed an increase in diagnostic accuracy when processing more than three input images compared to three or fewer, across both external validation cohorts (cohort 1: 0.863 vs. 0.804; cohort 2: 0.925 vs. 0.825).

FNA rate and unnecessary FNA rate: FTC-Net versus ACR TI-RADS and C-TI-RADS

We carried out a simulation analysis to evaluate various strategies’ impact on FNA biopsy utilization (Supplementary Table 3). In the external validation cohorts 1 and 2, the FTC-Net demonstrated significantly lower total FNA rates compared to ACR TI-RADS and C-TI-RADS (37.0% [214/578] vs. 83.7% [484/578] vs. 53.8% [311/578], respectively; 31.5% [120/381] vs. 34.4% [131/381] vs. 67.7% [258/381], respectively). Similarly, FTC-Net achieved the lowest rates of unnecessary FNAs compared to ACR TI-RADS and C-TI-RADS (15.0% [87/578] vs. 59.3% [343/578] vs. 30.4% [176/578], respectively; 13.6% [52/381] vs. 47.2% [180/381] vs. 20.2% [77/381], respectively).

Discussion

This multicenter retrospective study addressed the global clinical challenge of distinguishing FNs by developing a noninvasive DL diagnostic system. The FTC-Net achieved high accuracy and sensitivity in the external validation cohorts, which significantly outperformed DL models and existing RSSs. A small-scale prospective validation yielded favorable results. To our knowledge, this is the largest multicenter study to date focused on differentiating FTC from FTA. The system shows considerable potential as a practical tool to reduce unnecessary biopsies and surgical interventions, thereby improving clinical decision-making and management of FNs.

While ultrasound provides limited diagnostic value for FNs, they contain underlying tumor profiles that can be effectively extracted via DL architectures, thereby enhancing their clinical utility. Several previous studies employed convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to analyze very small sample sizes (≤100) of histopathological images of FTC and FTA27,28. While the preliminary findings appear promising, these models relied exclusively on postoperative pathological images, which limits their applicability before surgery. To date, only preliminary attempts have been made to differentiate FTC from FTA using ultrasound-based DL approaches with small sample sizes29–31. For instance, Yang et al.31.+ built an ultrasound-based model for differentiating FTC from FTA using Inception-ResNet-V2. Nevertheless, the limited sample size (n = 503) in this study may have reduced model robustness, reflected by performance metrics all below 70%. Additionally, the FTC proportion in their cohort was 50%, higher than previously reported rates and our own cohort (20–30%), which could lead to overestimated sensitivity and higher false-positive rates in clinical practice. These findings suggest that conventional CNN models may lack sufficient predictive accuracy for this task. To this end, we developed preoperative diagnostic models for FNs by integrating the modified SigLIP with ultrasound images in a large-scale multicenter cohort, showing superior performance in differentiating FTC from FTA. In the two independent external validation cohorts, the FTC-Net significantly outperformed both conventional CNNs (i.e., VGG16 and ResNet50; AUC range: 0.711-0.771), CNN-based hybrid or advanced architectures (i.e., Inception-ResNet-V2 and EfficientNet-B0; AUC range: 0.532–0.726), and Transformer-based architectures (i.e., MiT-B0 and ConvNeXt-B; AUC range: 0.760–0.771), with consistently higher discriminative accuracy (AUC: 0.836 and 0.841, respectively). Although the FTC-Net showed comparable performance to other DL models in the training and internal validation sets, it presented superior generalization capability in external validation cohorts, highlighting the enhanced robustness of the foundation model.

Thyroid RSSs significantly improve clinical decision-making by standardizing malignancy risk assessment, optimizing biopsy selection, and reducing unnecessary procedures while maintaining high diagnostic accuracy for clinically significant cancers32. Preoperative assessment of FNs is challenging using the current RSSs that are applicable to PTCs. The ACR TI-RADS is among the most widely adopted thyroid nodule RSSs worldwide; however, its predictive value for FNs is quite limited due to the significant differences in sonographic features between FNs and PTCs. Our study revealed that ACR TI-RADS had a limited sensitivity of approximately 50% for detecting FTC due to the underestimation of malignancy risk in FNs. In 2023, Liang et al.26 developed an F-TI-RADS using retrospective ultrasound data to differentiate between FTC and FTA. While F-TI-RADS showed superior performance compared to general-purpose RSSs, its reliance on radiologist-dependent feature interpretation inevitably introduces subjectivity. The sensitivity of F-TI-RADS in the training set (56.4%) was significantly lower than its specificity (79.4%). In contrast, in the validation cohort, the sensitivity (81.4%) was notably higher than the specificity (60.7%). However, our results showed that the sensitivity of F-TI-RADS was close to 50%, while the specificity exceeded 70%, which was similar to the results from the training set by Liang et al.26. A potential explanation may be the limited sample size in Liang et al.'s validation cohort, which could have introduced selection bias. Among all RSSs, C-TI-RADS demonstrated the highest sensitivity but comparatively lower specificity, which may be attributed to the more granular risk stratification in C-TI-RADS. Thus, there is currently no RSS that is very suitable for FNs, and further research is needed. In comparison, our foundational model significantly outperforms existing RSSs in terms of sensitivity and specificity metrics.

Although the FTC-Net was developed using data from postoperatively confirmed patients, it maintains clinical utility for preoperative decision-making. The FTC-Net demonstrates at least two key applications in clinical scenarios: First, for patients with either Bethesda IV nodules confirmed by FNA biopsy or FNs diagnosed through core-needle biopsy, it provides reliable FTC or FTA discrimination. Second, recognizing the limited availability of diagnostic biopsies, particularly in resource-limited settings, our model remains applicable for non-biopsied cases by leveraging specific sonographic patterns of FNs. Sonographic characteristics associated with FNs typically present as solitary, solid, or predominantly solid nodules with an anteroposterior-to-transverse diameter ratio of ≤1, isoechoic or mildly hypoechoic echogenicity, and a peripheral hypoechoic halo33–35. Therefore, for suspicious FNs identified through cytological results or ultrasound, our foundational model not only helps to avoid unnecessary surgeries for patients with available cytological results but also has the potential to reduce unnecessary invasive procedures for those who have not undergone cytological testing.

Our study also had some limitations. First, this study was retrospective, which means that selection bias cannot be entirely eliminated. In the future, we plan to conduct a prospective, multicenter validation study including diverse geographical regions and ethnic populations to evaluate the real-world diagnostic impact of the model. Second, data heterogeneity arising from multi-center image acquisition and the exclusive inclusion of Chinese patients may limit generalizability. Fourth, retrospective datasets often emphasize “diagnosable moments”, frames prescreened as most representative by sonographers, which may overestimate model performance and reduce generalizability to real-time dynamic ultrasound scanning. Thus, further investigation is warranted to evaluate FTC‑Net’s applicability in live clinical imaging.

In summary, we developed FTC‑Net, a noninvasive and robust diagnostic tool based on an adapted vision language foundation model, for differentiating FTC from FTA. This DL system offers an adjunctive means for the preoperative evaluation of FNs using ultrasound images. FTC‑Net has the potential to optimize therapeutic planning and reduce unnecessary invasive procedures, especially in resource‑limited settings where molecular testing is unavailable or costly. Further refinement and large-scale prospective validation, yet, are necessary prior to its integration into routine clinical workflows.

Methods

Patients and datasets

This multicenter retrospective analysis was conducted following institutional review board approval from Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (approval number: S2025-1022-01) and the ethics committees of all participating institutions, including the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University (approval number: JNUECKY-20251205-017), Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University, the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine, the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University, the First People’s Hospital of Foshan, the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University, the People’s Hospital of Wenshan Prefecture, Zhuhai Clinical Medical College of Jinan University, the Second People’s Hospital of Foshan, Shunde Hospital of Southern Medical University, Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center, the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province, and the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University. All procedures were carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. In accordance with its retrospective design, the study was granted a waiver of informed consent. We screened consecutive records of 2678 patients who underwent thyroidectomy (total or lobectomy) for pathologically confirmed follicular neoplasms across 14 centers. Eligibility required patients to be over 18 years of age, have a postoperative histopathological diagnosis of FTA or FTC according to the WHO Classification of Tumors of Endocrine Organs (5th edition), and have undergone preoperative ultrasound examination. Exclusion criteria comprised unavailable, poor-quality, or non-diagnostic ultrasound images, as well as a history of prior thyroid surgery. Ultimately, 2421 patients (6477 images) with FTA or FTC were included, of which, 1884 (77.8%) and 537 (22.2%) were confirmed as FTA and FTC, respectively. A total of 1462 patients (3553 images) from four institutions were included from April 2010 to November 2024. These were randomly divided into training and internal validation (holdout) datasets in a 6:4 ratio. An external validation cohort 1 comprised 578 patients (1883 images) collected from six institutions between June 2014 and March 2025, while external validation cohort 2 consisted of 381 patients (1041 images) from four institutions over the period February 2012 to February 2025. Both external cohorts were reserved solely for evaluating model generalizability. Image acquisition, nodule annotation, and image preprocessing

Ultrasound images were acquired in JPEG format using devices from 11 different manufacturers (Supplementary Table 4): HITACHI (n = 822), GE Healthcare (n = 377), TOSHIBA (n = 332), Philips (n = 235), Mindray (n = 238), Canon (n = 154), Siemens (n = 115), Supersonic (n = 73), Esaote (n = 71), SONOSCAPE (n = 2), and KANGDA (n = 2). Ultrasound examinations were performed using 5–14 MHz linear array transducers. All participating sonographers underwent rigorous training to ensure consistent imaging parameter adjustment and scanning procedures in accordance with the American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine thyroid ultrasound practice guidelines. Both longitudinal and transverse image sets were required to include the target nodule. When nodules extended beyond the field of view, partial visualization was achieved by repositioning the scanning plane. All ultrasound images were de-identified by removing protected health information. Sonographically identified nodules were matched with histologically confirmed lesions based on anatomical location and size. Imaging data from each center were independently reviewed by two senior sonographers. Low-quality images, such as those with poor resolution or significant artifacts, were excluded. A standardized imaging protocol was implemented across all centers. Preoperative risk stratification of follicular neoplasms was performed according to the ACR TI-RADS24, C-TI-RADS25, and F-TI-RADS26, respectively.

Ultrasound image annotation was performed using the open-source visual annotation tool Python labeIImg (v1.8.6). Two board-certified sonographers (H.S. and S.F.P., with 5 and 10 years of thyroid ultrasound experience, respectively) independently reviewed all images in a blinded fashion, with no prior discussion or access to each other’s evaluations. Cases of disagreement were resolved by a senior radiologist (L.J.L., 15 years of experience) to establish a final annotation consensus. All images were resized to a uniform input dimension of 224 × 224 pixels, with original aspect ratios preserved through zero-padding as needed.

Development of deep learning networks

We proposed a modified SigLIP model, namely FTC-Net, for differentiating FTC from FTA using ultrasound images. The model consists of a Text-Image Matching Module followed by a softmax layer. TIMM calculates similarity scores between text descriptions (e.g., “an image of FTA” or “an image of FTC”) and ultrasound images. The softmax layer then converts these scores into diagnostic probabilities, with the higher probability determining the final prediction.

The TIMM module of FTC-Net was backboned by the fundamental model of Sigmoid Loss for Language-Image Pre-Training (SigLIP23), consisting of parallel text and image encoders, each constructed with 12-layer Transformers36, and a similarity computation unit. The text encoder processes input descriptions through token embedding, positional encoding, and multi-head self-attention to generate contextual text features. Similarly, the image encoder extracts hierarchical visual features by processing image patches through Vision Transformer blocks, thereby capturing both local textures and global semantics. To adapt the pre-trained SigLIP model for our specific task while preserving its original capabilities, we integrated Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) modules37 into both encoders, enabling efficient fine-tuning without modifying the pre-trained parameters.

FTC-Net was trained using the data from the training cohort. To mitigate the risk of overfitting, ultrasound images were augmented through random rotation (−10° to 10°), left-right flip (with a probability of 30%), optional Gaussian blur (kernel size 3 × 3, σ: 0.1–1.0, with a probability of 30%), and slight adjustments to brightness and contrast (±0.1). Since the pre-trained SigLIP backbone within FTC-Net expects RGB inputs, each single-channel ultrasound image was converted to three channels by replicating the original channel across the red, green, and blue dimensions. All images were normalized within the pre-trained SigLIP backbone as follows: pixel values were first scaled to a [0, 1] range, and then standardized using the parameters mean = (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) and std = (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) across RGB channels. The final pixel values were transformed to a [−1, 1] range. All parameters of TIMM within FTC-Net were initialized by those of pre-trained SigLIP and frozen during training except for the layers of LoRA, enabling task-specific adaptation without altering the base model. We employed focal loss to address the class imbalance and optimized the model using Adam (initial learning rate = 1e-4). Conventional DL models followed the same training protocol but were initialized with ImageNet-pre-trained weights38. The models we trained and initially tested the models on the internal cohort, and further evaluated it on the external cohorts. For cases with multiple ultrasound images, all relevant images were evaluated, and the highest predicted probability was used to determine the final diagnosis. The FTC-Net was preliminarily evaluated in a small prospective cohort consisting of 10 cases (5 FTC and 5 FTA).

Furthermore, we carried out stratification analyses to evaluate the influence of patient age (≤50 vs. >50 years), sex (male vs. female), nodule size (≤3 vs. >3 cm in maximum diameter), ultrasound device manufacturer (external validation cohort 1: HITACHI vs. GE vs. Mindray vs. Canno vs. Philips vs. others; external validation cohort 2: TOSHIBA vs. SIEMENS vs. HITACHI vs. Philips vs. Mindray vs. others), the number of the input images (≤3 vs. >3) on the FTC-Net’s performance across internal and external validation cohorts.

For methodological benchmarking, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of FTC-Net against six DL models (i.e., VGG1639, ResNet5040, MiT-B041, Inception-ResNet-V242, EfficientNet-B043, and ConvNeXt-B44), each modified with a final two-neuron fully connected layer for binary classification. Detailed parameter settings are provided in Supplementary Tables 5–10. To assess clinical utility, we compared the FTC-Net with general-purpose (ACR TI-RADS and C-TI-RADS) and specialized (F-TI-RADS) RSSs in terms of discrimination power. Since F-TI-RADS does not provide FNA recommendations, we compared FTC-Net with ACR TI-RADS and C-TI-RADS in terms of total FNA rate and unnecessary FNA biopsy rate. The total FNA rate was calculated as the proportion of nodules that received biopsy recommendations from ACR TI-RADS, C-TI-RADS, or FTC-Net.

Unnecessary biopsies were defined as procedures conducted on pathologically confirmed benign nodules that met the FNA criteria according to the RSSs or FTC-Net17,45,46. For FTC-Net, nodules were not recommended for FNA if the model’s output was negative (i.e., below the cutoff value established in the training cohort)17,45,46.

Statistical analysis

Baseline patient and nodule characteristics were compared by independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. To evaluate the performance of the DL models and the several RSSs, multiple metrics were computed, including AUC, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV. The differences between two AUC values derived from the same set of individuals were evaluated using DeLong’s test47. The McNemar’s test was applied to assess differences in sensitivity and specificity between the models48. The 95% CI was estimated through bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. The optimal diagnostic thresholds for FTC were determined by maximizing the Youden index, thereby optimizing both sensitivity and specificity. The confusion matrix of the FTC-Net was depicted. The calibration curve was plotted to assess the agreement between model-predicted and actual malignancy probabilities49. Decision curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the clinical usefulness of our models in guiding by quantifying the net benefits50. Interobserver and intraobserver agreement for each TI-RADS was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistics. For both intra- and inter-TI-RADS evaluations by the same sonographer, at least a one-month washout period was required between assessments to minimize recall bias. The strength of agreement for κ values was interpreted as follows: κ < 0.20, poor; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, good; and 0.81–1.00, excellent51. Model construction was performed using MATLAB (Version R2023a). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.
