On the surface Bloch waves in truncated periodic media
Bojan B. Guzina, Shixu Meng, Prasanna Salasiya, Long Nguyen

Abstract
Much like their counterparts in elastic solids, waves in periodic media can be broadly classified into Floquet-Bloch body waves , and evanescent surface waves . Our goal is to elucidate the latter boundary layers, termed surface Bloch (SB) waves, affiliated with rational surface cuts and homogeneous Neumann data. To this end we adopt the scalar wave equation with periodic coefficients as a test bed and develop a unit cell-of-periodicity-based, reduced order model of the SB waves that is capable of describing both their dispersion, waveforms, and “skin depth”. The centerpiece of our analysis is a quadratic eigenvalue problem (QEP) for the effective unit cell of periodicity – deriving from a geometric interplay between the mother Bravais lattice, orientation of the surface cut, and the direction of SB wave – that seeks the complex wavenumber normal to the cut plane given (i) the excitation frequency and (ii) wavenumber in the direction of the SB wave. In this way the sought boundary layer is derived via superposition of the evanescent QEP eigenstates, whose strengths are obtained by imposing the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition. With the QEP eigenspectrum at hand, evaluation of an SB wave – in terms of both dispersion characteristics and evanescent waveforms – entails only a low-dimensional eigenvalue problem. This feature caters for rapid exploration of the effect of (periodic) surface undulations, and so enables manipulation of the SB waves via optimal design of the surface cut. For completeness, our analysis includes an account for the power flow and “skin depth” of a surface Bloch wave, both of which are critical for the energetic relevance of boundary layers.



1
Introduction

Since the late nineteenth century
bose1898rotation
, locally-periodic structures have been devised
(
kock1948metallic
;
cummer2016controlling
;
kadic20193d
, e.g.)
to control “macroscopic” waves whose wavelength exceeds the characteristic lengthscale of medium periodicity. Commonly, such structures – often referred to as metamaterials – are designed through the prism of their dispersive characteristics, which concern the wave motion in an infinite periodic medium. Yet, the metamaterials’ capacity as wave regulators is inevitably brought to bare via interaction of their
bounded subsets
with the environment
assouar2018acoustic
;
zangeneh2020topological
;
zhu2013acoustic
.

Historically, the analyses of wave motion in unbounded periodic media have been pursued via the Floquet-Bloch expansion
(
bensoussan2011asymptotic
;
brillouin1946wave
;
kuchment2012floquet
;
wilcox1978theory
, e.g.)
. This approach results in a dispersion map relating the frequency and wavenumber of a Floquet-Bloch wave, commonly obtained by solving a linear eigenvalue problem
bensoussan2011asymptotic
over the unit cell of medium periodicity. In recent years, Floquet-Bloch waves have been used to develop reduced-order models for a variety of wave scattering problems
(
hussein2009reduced
;
krattiger2018generalized
;
tsukamoto2014real
, e.g.)
and help establish the convergence results of relevant numerical algorithms
fliss2016solutions
;
lechleiter2017floquet
;
kirsch2023periodic
. Novel applications of metamaterials, however, necessitate the computation of an extended dispersion relationship that accounts for complex wave vectors, e.g. those inside a band gap. This can be achieved by prescribing the frequency of a Floquet-Bloch wave and seeking the admissible wave vector component(s) – which results in a quadratic eigenvalue problem (QEP)
tisseur2001quadratic
over the unit cell of periodicity. Recently, such an approach to computing the complex dispersion relationship has attracted much attention in mathematics
engstrom2009spectrum
;
Lack2019
;
palermo2020reduced
, physics
brule2016calculation
;
raman2010photonic
;
rybin2016inverse
, and engineering
hussein2014dynamics
;
hussein2009theory
;
toader2004photonic
. When considering the wave motion in bounded subsets of periodic media, on the other hand, one must account for the emergence of
boundary layers
whose analysis is considerably more difficult
(
cakoni2019scattering
;
joly2006exact
;
Kulpe2015
;
doi:10.1137/17M1118920
, e.g.)
. Typically, the evanescent (surface or interface) waves arising in this class of problems are evaluated implicitly via suitable computational scheme
fliss2010computation
and deemed as “waves to avoid”
doi:10.1137/17M1118920
. Even in the works where the boundary layers are addressed explicitly, their analysis is either (i) not simpler than that of the full boundary value problem
cakoni2016homog
;
cakoni2019scattering
, or (ii) based on numerical, supercell-driven evaluation
(
charara2023cell
;
fang2024coupled
;
wang2015topological
, e.g.)
which sheds little light on the problem. By allowing for complex wave vectors, however, the “quadratic” Bloch eigenstates solving the QEP have recently emerged as viable building blocks supporting the reduced-order models of boundary layers in periodic media
Kulpe2015
;
willis2016negative
;
salasiya2024plug
.

To date, there are no comprehensive analytical and computational frameworks elucidating the boundary layers – and in particular surface waves – in periodic media. Even within the realm of topological mechanics
huber2016topological
;
xin2020topological
(governed by differential equations), the analysis of interfacial states revolves around the topological invariants introduced by analogy with topological physics
bernevig2013topological
(governed by Hamiltonians). For instance, topological invariants may reveal which (evanescent) interfacial states may be topologically protected from scattering-induced losses, but they furnish no information about the “skin depth” of such boundary layers – that is critical for meaningful energy tunneling. Similarly, tools are available to manipulate the surface waves in homogeneous media by grating or periodically decorating (with resonators) their free surfaces
(
colombi2016seismic
;
colquitt2017seismic
, e.g.)
; yet, little is known about the possibility of manipulating boundary layers propagating along the free surface of a periodic medium.

In this vein our goal is to understand, simulate, and control the boundary layers propagating along the free surface of a periodic medium, characterized by the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition. For the sought surface wave to retain its (periodic) Bloch character, we require both the surface cut and the direction of propagation to make a rational slope relative to the Bravais lattice of a periodic medium.
As a test bed for the analysis, we consider the scalar wave equation with periodic coefficients. In the context of multi-dimensional problems, the very ability of the QEP to produce complex wave vectors allows one to identify and compute the evanescent Bloch eigenstates that propagate (resp. decay) in the direction parallel (resp. orthogonal) to the boundary. This motivates us to pursue the sought boundary layer, termed surface Bloch wave, as a linear combination of suitably-defined QEP eigenstates satisfying the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition. With the “mother” QEP eigenspectrum at hand, evaluation of the surface Bloch wave – in terms of both dispersion characteristics and evanescent waveforms – entails only a low-dimensional eigenvalue problem. This in turn caters for a rapid exploration of the design space in terms of (periodic) undulations of the free surface, and so opens the door toward controlling boundary layers in periodic media via optimal design of the surface cut. For completeness, our analysis includes an account for the power flow and “skin depth” of a surface Bloch wave, both of which are critical for the energetic relevance of boundary layers.

For completeness, we remark that a similar term, “Bloch surface waves”, has appeared earlier
(
liscidini2007enhancement
, e.g.)
in the context of photonics involving one-dimensional (1D) periodic arrangements of homogeneous strata. Another akin expression, “Rayleigh-Bloch waves”, refers to the boundary layers due to diffraction gratings
(
wilcox2012scattering
, e.g.)
generated by either (i) grating the boundary of, or (ii) inserting a strip of a 1D-periodic lattice in, an otherwise homogeneous medium
(
antonakakis2014asymp
;
colquitt2015rayleigh
;
chaplain2025acoustic
, e.g.)
.

(1)

The reciprocals of

where

is the wave vector,

2.1
Bloch waves in an unbounded periodic medium

Our focus is on the time-harmonic scalar wave equation with periodic coefficients, namely

\nabla\!\cdot\!(G(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla u)+\omega^{2}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\hskip 0.7ptu\>=\>0,\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(3)

where
G
and
\rho
are
Y
-periodic;
\omega
is the frequency of oscillations, and the wavefield
u(\boldsymbol{x})
carries implicit time dependence
e^{-\text{i}\omega t}
. Hereon, we assume that
G
and
\rho
are real-valued
L^{\infty}(Y)
functions bounded from below away from zero in that
G_{\inf}\leqslant G\leqslant G_{\sup}
and
\rho_{\inf}\leqslant\rho\leqslant\rho_{\sup}
for some positive constants
G_{\inf},G_{\sup},\rho_{\inf}
and
\rho_{\sup}
. In the case of horizontally-polarized (SH) elastic wave motion,

and

signify respectively the shear modulus, mass density, and transverse displacement. We shall conveniently make use of this nomenclature in the sequel.

Recalling the Floquet-Bloch theorem
(
Floq1883
;

)
, we factor the wavefield

as

u(\boldsymbol{x})\>=\>\phi(\boldsymbol{x})\,e^{\text{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\>=\>\phi(\boldsymbol{x})\,e^{\text{i}k_{j}x^{j}},\qquad\phi:\>\text{$Y$-periodic}

(4)

where
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
is the germane wave vector, and
\phi
depends implicitly on
\omega
and
{\boldsymbol{k}}
. From (
3
) and (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\text{(a) ~~}\qquad\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{k}}}\!\cdot\!(G(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{k}}}\phi)+\omega^{2}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\phi=0,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in Y

(5)

\displaystyle\text{(b) ~~}\phi|_{x^{i}=-\frac{1}{2}}=\phi|_{x^{i}=\frac{1}{2}},\quad~\boldsymbol{\nu}\!\cdot\!G\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{k}}}\phi|_{x^{i}=-\frac{1}{2}}=-\boldsymbol{\nu}\!\cdot\!G\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{k}}}\phi|_{x^{i}=\frac{1}{2}},\quad~i\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt\overline{1,d}

where
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\nu^{i}\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
is the unit outward normal on
\partial{Y}
and

\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{k}}}\hskip 0.7pt:=\,\nabla\!+\text{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\,=\,\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}+\text{i}k_{j}\Big)\boldsymbol{b}^{j}.

(6)

2.2
Quadratic eigenvalue problem (QEP)

To help formulate the problem, we introduce the Hilbert spaces

\displaystyle L_{p}^{2}(Y)

\displaystyle\!\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt\!

\displaystyle\{g\in L^{2}(Y):\,(g,g)<\infty,\>g|_{x^{i}=-\frac{1}{2}}=g|_{x^{i}=\frac{1}{2}}\},

\displaystyle H^{1}_{p}(Y)

\displaystyle\!\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt\!

\displaystyle\{g\in L^{2}(Y):\,\nabla g\in(L^{2}(Y))^{2},\>g|_{x^{i}=-\frac{1}{2}}=g|_{x^{i}=\frac{1}{2}}\}

where
(g,h)=\int_{Y}g(\boldsymbol{\xi})\hskip 0.7pt\overline{h}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\overline{h}
is the complex conjugate of

. By virtue of a test function
\psi\!\in\!H^{1}_{p}(Y)
, a weak statement of (
5
a) can be computed as

\int_{Y}G\,\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{k}}}\phi\hskip 0.7pt\cdot\hskip-0.2pt\nabla_{\!\hskip-0.2pt-{\boldsymbol{k}}}\overline{\psi}\,\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}\>-\>\omega^{2}\!\!\int_{Y}\rho\,\phi\,\overline{\psi}\,\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}\>=\>0\qquad\forall\psi\in H_{p}^{1}(Y),

(7)

subject to the boundary conditions (
5
b).

On introducing an integer
\alpha\hskip-0.2pt\in\overline{1,d}
and letting
\beta\!=\!\overline{1,d}\,\backslash\hskip 0.7pt\{\alpha\}
, we next pose an eigenvalue problem that consists in seeking
\kappa\hskip-0.2pt:=\hskip-0.2ptk_{\alpha}
and
\phi
that satisfy (
7
) for given
\omega
and

. In this setting, we obtain the skew-lattice QEP

\kappa_{n}^{2}\hskip 0.7pt\Gamma^{\alpha\alpha}\!\!\int_{Y}G\hskip 0.7pt\phi_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\overline{\psi}\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}\,+\,\kappa_{n}\bigg\{2\sum_{\beta}k_{\beta}\hskip 0.7pt\Gamma^{\alpha\beta}\!\!\int_{Y}G\hskip 0.7pt\phi_{n}\,\overline{\psi}\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}\hskip 0.7pt-\hskip 0.7pt\text{i}\,\Gamma^{\alpha j}\!\!\int_{Y}G\left(\frac{\partial\phi_{n}}{\partial x^{j}}\overline{\psi}-\phi_{n}\frac{\partial\overline{\psi}}{\partial x^{j}}\right)\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}\bigg\}\\
\,+\,\bigg\{\sum_{\beta}\sum_{\gamma}\Gamma^{\beta\gamma}k_{\beta}\hskip 0.7ptk_{\gamma}\!\int_{Y}G\hskip 0.7pt\phi_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\overline{\psi}\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}\,-\,\text{i}\sum_{\beta}k_{\beta}\Gamma^{\beta j}\!\!\int_{Y}G\left(\frac{\partial\phi_{n}}{\partial x^{j}}\overline{\psi}-\phi_{n}\frac{\partial\overline{\psi}}{\partial x^{j}}\right)\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}\\
+\int_{Y}\Big(G\hskip 0.7pt\nabla\phi_{n}\hskip-0.2pt\cdot\hskip-0.2pt\nabla\overline{\psi}-\omega^{2}\!\rho\hskip 0.7pt\phi_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\overline{\psi}\Big)\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}\bigg\}\>=\>0\qquad\forall\psi\in H_{p}^{1}(Y),

(8)

where
\beta,\gamma\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt\overline{1,d}\,\backslash\hskip 0.7pt\{\alpha\}
;

For an orthogonal lattice in

with

2.3
Analysis of the QEP

Letting
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the inner product affiliated with
H^{1}_{p}(Y)
, we introduce operators

\mathcal{A}:H^{1}_{p}(Y)\mapsto H^{1}_{p}(Y)
,
\,\mathcal{B}:H^{1}_{p}(Y)\mapsto H^{1}_{p}(Y)
and
\,\mathcal{C}:H^{1}_{p}(Y)\mapsto H^{1}_{p}(Y)
respectively by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}\phi,\psi\rangle\!\!\!

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\sum_{\beta}\sum_{\gamma}\Gamma^{\beta\gamma}k_{\beta}\hskip 0.7ptk_{\gamma}\!\int_{Y}G\hskip 0.7pt\phi\hskip 0.7pt\overline{\psi}\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}-\text{i}\sum_{\beta}k_{\beta}\Gamma^{\beta j}\!\!\int_{Y}G\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^{j}}\overline{\psi}-\phi\frac{\partial\overline{\psi}}{\partial x^{j}}\right)\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle+\int_{Y}\big(G\hskip 0.7pt\nabla\phi\hskip-0.2pt\cdot\hskip-0.2pt\nabla\overline{\psi}-\omega^{2}\!\rho\hskip 0.7pt\phi\hskip 0.7pt\overline{\psi}\Big)\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}},\quad\forall\phi,\psi\in H^{1}_{p}(Y),

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{B}\phi,\psi\rangle\!\!\!

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\!\!\!2\sum_{\beta}k_{\beta}\hskip 0.7pt\Gamma^{\alpha\beta}\!\!\int_{Y}G\hskip 0.7pt\phi\,\overline{\psi}\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}}\hskip 0.7pt-\hskip 0.7pt\text{i}\,\Gamma^{\alpha j}\!\!\int_{Y}G\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^{j}}\overline{\psi}-\phi\frac{\partial\overline{\psi}}{\partial x^{j}}\right)\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}},\quad\forall\phi,\psi\in H^{1}_{p}(Y),

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C}\phi,\psi\rangle\!\!\!

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\Gamma^{\alpha\alpha}\!\!\int_{Y}G\hskip 0.7pt\phi\hskip 0.7pt\overline{\psi}\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}Y_{\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{x}},\quad\forall\phi,\psi\in H^{1}_{p}(Y).

With such definitions, QEP (
8
) becomes

\mathcal{A}\phi_{n}+\kappa_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\mathcal{B}\phi_{n}+\kappa_{n}^{2}\hskip 0.7pt\mathcal{C}\phi_{n}\,=\,0.

(10)

To aid the ensuing developments, we establish several key properties of (
10
) via the following claims whose proofs are provided in Appendix A, electronic supplementary material (ESM).

Lemma 1
.

Operators
\mathcal{A}:H^{1}_{p}(Y)\mapsto H^{1}_{p}(Y)
,
\,\mathcal{B}:H^{1}_{p}(Y)\mapsto H^{1}_{p}(Y)
and
\,\mathcal{C}:H^{1}_{p}(Y)\mapsto H^{1}_{p}(Y)
are self-adjoint.

Lemma 2
.

Operator
\mathcal{B}:H^{1}_{p}(Y)\mapsto H^{1}_{p}(Y)
is compact, and operator

\mathcal{T}(\lambda)=\mathcal{A}+\lambda\mathcal{B}+\lambda^{2}\mathcal{C}

is Fredholm of index zero.

Propositon 1
.

If
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)\hskip 0.7pt\phi=0\,
for some non-zero
\phi\in H^{1}_{p}(Y)
, there must exist a nontrivial
\psi\in H^{1}_{p}(Y)
such that
\mathcal{T}(\overline{\lambda})\hskip 0.7pt\psi=0
. Hence all eigenvalues of QEP (
8
) come in complex-conjugate pairs.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that there exists
\tau\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathcal{T}(\tau)
is injective. Then the eigenvalues
\lambda
affiliated with the quadratic pencil
\mathcal{T}(\lambda)
form a discrete set, with infinity being the only possible accumulation point.

3
Surface Bloch (SB) waves

We consider the propagation of surface waves in a periodic medium that is truncated along a cut plane
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
endowed with unit outward normal
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\hskip-0.7pt\mathcal{S}}
. Across the cut plane we assume the traction-free, i.e. homogeneous Neumann, boundary condition

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\hskip-0.7pt\mathcal{S}}\cdot\big(G(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla u\big)|_{\mathcal{S}}\,=\,0.

(11)

In this setting, we seek to expose the Bloch wave-like boundary layer(s) in a semi-infinite domain
\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}\!\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
occupying the
-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\hskip-0.7pt\mathcal{S}}
side of
\mathcal{S}
. For specificity, the surface wave is taken to propagate in a direction given by the unit vector
\boldsymbol{e}\hskip-0.2pt\perp\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\hskip-0.7pt\mathcal{S}}
. For such boundary layer to have a Bloch-like character, however, both
\boldsymbol{e}
and
\mathcal{S}
must satisfy certain conditions that are examined next.

Definition 1
.

The direction of propagation,
\boldsymbol{e}
, of a surface wave has a “rational slope” relative to lattice
\boldsymbol{R}
if there exist
q^{i}\!\in\mathbb{Z}
(
i\!=\!\overline{1,d}
) such that
\boldsymbol{e}\hskip-0.2pt\parallel\hskip-0.2ptq^{i}\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
. In the sequel, we refer to
\{q^{i}\}
as the
d
-tuplet containing the smallest such values of
q^{i}
, see Fig.
1
for an example in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
q^{1}\!=3
and
q^{2}\!=1
.

We note that for
d=2
, the direction
\boldsymbol{e}
of a surface wave uniquely specifies the orientation of the cut plane. For
d=3
, on the other hand, we need an additional restriction on
\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 2
.

The cut plane
\mathcal{S}
has a “rational slope” relative to lattice
\boldsymbol{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
if
\,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\hskip-0.7pt\mathcal{S}}=\pm\hskip 0.7pt\boldsymbol{e}\times\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}
, where the unit vectors
\boldsymbol{e}
and
\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}
each have a rational slope relative to
\boldsymbol{R}
. This condition ensures that the trace of
\boldsymbol{R}
on
\mathcal{S}
is periodic. In the sequel, we denote the unit cell of periodicity of such projection by
\,\Xi_{\mathcal{S}}\!\subset\mathcal{S}
, the latter being dependent exclusively on the choice of
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\hskip-0.7pt\mathcal{S}}
.

Assumption 1
.

Hereon, our focus is on the cut planes
\mathcal{S}
and surface wave directions
\boldsymbol{e}
with rational slopes relative to lattice
\boldsymbol{R}
. An extension of the analysis to irrational slopes, that is beyond the scope of this work, is possible via the lifting (i.e. cut-and-projection) approach
kozlov1979ave
;
gerard2012homog
.

3.1
QEP basis of a “rational” surface wave

With the foregoing results in place, we assume that

\displaystyle d=2,3:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}\parallel q_{1}^{i}\hskip 0.7pt\boldsymbol{a}_{i}~~\text{for some}~\{q_{1}^{i}\}\!\subset\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle d=3:\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\hskip-0.7pt\mathcal{S}}=\pm\hskip 0.7pt\boldsymbol{e}\times\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}~~\text{where}~~\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}\parallel q_{2}^{i}\hskip 0.7pt\boldsymbol{a}_{i}~~\text{for some}~\{q_{2}^{i}\}\!\subset\mathbb{Z}.

, we proceed by adopting a new lattice basis

, one has
\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\alpha}\!\!\perp\hskip-0.2pt\mathcal{S}
. Since
{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}=\tilde{k}_{j}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{x}^{j}
and
\tilde{x}^{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\alpha}
, on the other hand, it is clear that
k_{\alpha}\!\in\mathbb{C}
– the sought eigenvalue of the QEP – inherently quantifies the decay of evanescent Bloch modes in the direction orthogonal to
\mathcal{S}
.

With reference to Fig.
1
, the foregoing setup allows us to consider the unit “multi-cell” and companion Brillouin zone

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}=\,\big\{\boldsymbol{x}=\tilde{x}^{i}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}:\>-\tfrac{1}{2}<\tilde{x}^{i}\!<\tfrac{1}{2}\big\},\qquad\tilde{B}=\,\big\{{\boldsymbol{k}}=\tilde{k}_{j}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{j}:\>-\pi<\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{k}_{j}\hskip-0.2pt<\pi\big\},

(14)

and to pursue the skew-lattice QEP (
8
) written over
\tilde{Y}
which seeks
\tilde{\kappa}_{n}\hskip-0.2pt:=\tilde{k}_{\alpha}\!\in\mathbb{C}
and
\tilde{\phi}_{n}\!\in H_{p}^{1}(\tilde{Y})
for given
\omega
and
\{\tilde{k}_{\beta}\}
. Specifically, we consider

\tilde{\kappa}_{n}^{2}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\Gamma}^{\alpha\alpha}\!\!\int_{\tilde{Y}}G\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\phi}_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\overline{\psi}\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}\tilde{Y}_{\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\,+\,\tilde{\kappa}_{n}\bigg\{2\sum_{\beta}\tilde{k}_{\beta}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\Gamma}^{\alpha\beta}\!\!\int_{\tilde{Y}}G\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\phi}_{n}\,\overline{\psi}\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}\tilde{Y}_{\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\hskip 0.7pt-\hskip 0.7pt\text{i}\,\tilde{\Gamma}^{\alpha j}\!\!\int_{\tilde{Y}}G\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}_{n}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{j}}\overline{\psi}-\tilde{\phi}_{n}\frac{\partial\overline{\psi}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{j}}\right)\,\text{d}\tilde{Y}_{\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\bigg\}\\
\,+\,\bigg\{\sum_{\beta}\sum_{\gamma}\tilde{\Gamma}^{\beta\gamma}\tilde{k}_{\beta}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{k}_{\gamma}\!\int_{\tilde{Y}}G\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\phi}_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\overline{\psi}\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}\tilde{Y}_{\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\,-\,\text{i}\sum_{\beta}\tilde{k}_{\beta}\tilde{\Gamma}^{\beta j}\!\!\int_{\tilde{Y}}G\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}_{n}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{j}}\overline{\psi}-\tilde{\phi}_{n}\frac{\partial\overline{\psi}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{j}}\right)\,\text{d}\tilde{Y}_{\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\\
+\int_{\tilde{Y}}\big(G\hskip 0.7pt\nabla\tilde{\phi}_{n}\hskip-0.2pt\cdot\hskip-0.2pt\nabla\overline{\psi}-\omega^{2}\!\rho\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\phi}_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\overline{\psi}\big)\hskip 0.7pt\,\text{d}\tilde{Y}_{\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\bigg\}\>=\>0\qquad\forall\psi\in H_{p}^{1}(\tilde{Y}),

(15)

where
\{\tilde{k}_{\beta}\}\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt\{k_{e},0\}
(resp.
\tilde{k}_{\beta}\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2ptk_{e}
) for
d\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt3
(resp.
d\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt2
);
k_{e}\hskip-0.2pt\in\mathbb{R}
is the prescribed component of the wave vector
{\boldsymbol{k}}=\tilde{\kappa}_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\alpha}\!+\sum_{\beta}\tilde{k}_{\beta}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\beta}
in direction
\boldsymbol{e}\in\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\beta}/\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\beta}\|\}
;
\tilde{\Gamma}^{ij}\!=\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{i}\!\cdot\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{j}
are the components of the contravariant metric tensor, and implicit summation is assumed over the repeated index
j=\overline{1,d}
. In the sequel, we endow
\tilde{\phi}_{n}
with a unit
L_{p}^{2}(\tilde{Y})
-norm.

Remark 2
.

Assuming without loss of generality that the outward normal to the half-space is oriented s.th.
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\hskip-0.7pt\mathcal{S}}\hskip-0.2pt\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\alpha}\!<0
, we shall hereon slightly abuse the notation and refer to
\{\tilde{\kappa}_{n},\tilde{\phi}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
as the subset of the full eigenspectrum containing the eigenvalues with strictly positive imaginary parts, i.e.

\Im(\tilde{\kappa}_{n})\hskip 0.7pt>\hskip 0.7pt0,

(16)

which by (
4
) ensures the exponential decay,
e^{-\Im(\tilde{\kappa}_{n})\tilde{x}^{\alpha}}
, of an evanescent wave with distance from
\mathcal{S}
into the half-space. Hereon, we arrange
\tilde{\kappa}_{n}
in the order of ascending imaginary part in that
\Im(\tilde{\kappa}_{n+1})\geqslant\Im(\tilde{\kappa}_{n})
.

In this setting, our focus is on the surface Bloch wavefield propagating along
\mathcal{S}
in direction
\boldsymbol{e}
with prescribed wavenumber
k_{e}
, namely

u(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\eta_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})e^{\text{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n}\cdot\hskip 0.7pt\boldsymbol{x}}\,=\;\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\eta_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\,e^{\text{i}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\kappa}_{n}\tilde{x}^{\alpha}}\Big)\,e^{\text{i}\sum_{\beta}\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{k}_{\beta}\tilde{x}^{\beta}},\quad~\{\tilde{k}_{\beta}\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}k_{e},&d=2\\
\{k_{e},0\},&d=3\end{array}\right.

(17)

where
\eta_{n}\!\in\hskip-0.2pt\mathbb{C}
are the modal amplitudes and the modulation term
e^{\text{i}\hskip 0.7pt{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}
is parsed to separate the direction of evanescence,
\tilde{x}^{\alpha}
, from the surface cut direction(s)
\tilde{x}^{\beta}
.

3.2
Undulated surface cut

On denoting the
in-plane
position vector
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\hskip-0.2pt\in\hskip-0.2pt\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
across
\mathcal{S}\hskip 0.7pt
by

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\>=\>\sum_{\beta}\tilde{x}^{\beta}\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\beta},\quad~\tilde{x}^{\beta}\!\hskip-0.2pt\in\mathbb{R},~~~\beta=\overline{1,d}\,\backslash\hskip 0.7pt\{\alpha\},

(18)

we consider a cut plane located at “depth”
\mathfrak{d}\in[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})
within
\tilde{Y}
, namely

\mathcal{S}\;=\;\big\{\boldsymbol{x}\hskip-0.2pt\hskip 0.7pt:\>\boldsymbol{x}={\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\mathfrak{d}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha}\big\}.

(19)

With such definitions, the reference unit cell
\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}\!\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
of the cut plane, which specifies the “in-plane” periodicity of a surface Bloch wave, can be written as

\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}\;:=~\mathcal{S}\cap\tilde{Y}\;=\;\big\{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}=\tilde{x}^{\beta}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\beta}\hskip-0.2pt:\,-\tfrac{1}{2}<\tilde{x}^{\beta}\!<\tfrac{1}{2}\big\}.

(20)

Recalling Definition
2
, for generality we endow the cut surface (
19
) with an
\Xi_{\mathcal{S}}
-periodic Lipshitz undulation,
\Xi_{\mathcal{S}}
being the unit cell of
in-plane medium periodicity
. For
d\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt3
,
\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}
consists of an integer number of unit cells
\Xi_{\mathcal{S}}
(see Fig.
4
(a) for an illustration), the latter being dependent on the orientation
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\hskip-0.7pt\mathcal{S}}
of the cut plane – but not on the direction of propagation
\boldsymbol{e}
. For
d\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt2
, on the other hand, we have
\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}=\Xi_{\mathcal{S}}
for the orientation of the cut plane specifies (up to a sign) the direction
\boldsymbol{e}
of a surface wave.

As a result, for a rational surface cut and rational incident direction
\boldsymbol{e}
,
every
\Xi_{\mathcal{S}}
-periodic undulation will also be
\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}
-periodic
. Assuming the undulation
\delta\mathcal{S}
to be given by a periodic bijective map
f\!:{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, this allows us to consider the surface waves propagating in direction
\boldsymbol{e}
along
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}\hskip 0.7pt:=\hskip 0.7pt\mathcal{S}+\delta\mathcal{S}\hskip 0.7pt
subject to the boundary condition

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\!\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}\cdot\big(G(\boldsymbol{x})\nabla u\big)|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}\,=\,0,

(21)

where

\mathcal{S}_{\delta}\;=\;\big\{\boldsymbol{x}\hskip-0.2pt:\>\boldsymbol{x}={\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+(\mathfrak{d}\hskip-0.2pt+\hskip-0.2ptf({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}))\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha},\>f\!:\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}\textrm{-periodic}\big\};

(22)

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
is given by (
18
), and
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\!\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}
is the unit outward normal to
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
.

To cater for situations where
\delta\mathcal{S}
may feature folding, one may conveniently generalize (
22
) by describing
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
as a set of
\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}
-periodic bijective mappings,
f_{p}\hskip-0.2pt:\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}\hskip 0.7pt\supset\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
as

\mathcal{S}_{\delta}\;=\;\big\{\boldsymbol{x}\hskip-0.2pt:\>\boldsymbol{x}={\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+(\mathfrak{d}+f_{p}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}))\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha},~f_{p}\hskip-0.2pt:\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}\textrm{-periodic},~p=\overline{1,P}\big\},

(23)

see Fig.
2
for an illustration with
d\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt2
and
P\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt3
.
By the finiteness of
P
, this definition ensures that the set of points on
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
where
\sum_{\beta}|\partial\tilde{x}^{a}/\partial\tilde{x}^{\beta}|^{2}
is unbounded is of zero measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. This very set, namely points for
d\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt2
and lines for
d\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt3
, together with the subset of
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
where
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\!\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}
is not uniquely defined (namely “kinks” or “creases” in the surface) delineates the respective supports of
\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}
within
\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}
as shown in the figure.

Schematics of an undulated cut (
d=2
,

with relevant features and notations. In the example, the surface cut

is made at “1:2” rational slope (

) relative to an orthogonal lattice
\boldsymbol{R}

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\mapsto\zeta\hskip-0.2pt:=\tilde{x}^{\beta}
and

) and (

), the surface traction on the ligaments extending “above” the cut plane

3.3
Surface-wave eigenproblem

By virtue of (
17
), the traction-free condition (
21
) becomes

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\eta_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\big(G(\boldsymbol{x})\,\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\hskip-0.2pt\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n}}\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{\phi}_{n}\>e^{\text{i}\hskip 0.7pt{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n}\cdot\hskip 0.7pt\boldsymbol{x}}\big)\Big|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}=\>0,\qquad{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n}\hskip 0.7pt=\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\kappa}_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\alpha}+\sum_{\beta}\tilde{k}_{\beta}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\beta}

(24)

where
\boldsymbol{x}=\tilde{x}^{j}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}
,
\,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\!\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}\!=\tilde{\nu}_{\!\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}^{j}\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}
, and
\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{k}}}=(\partial/\partial\tilde{x}^{j}\!+\text{i}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{k}_{j})\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{j}
. Recalling Remark
2
, we conveniently consider an approximation of the
rescaled
boundary condition
e^{-\text{i}\sum_{\beta}\tilde{k}_{\beta}\tilde{x}^{\beta}}\!\hskip-0.2pt\times\eqref{evan2}
restricted to
N
“deepest-penetrating” surface wave modes, namely

\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\hskip-0.2ptn}\big|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}=\>\boldsymbol{0},\qquad a_{n}=e^{\text{i}\tilde{\kappa}_{n}\mathfrak{d}}\,\eta_{n},

(25)

where

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\hskip-0.2ptn}(\boldsymbol{x})\,=\,G(\boldsymbol{x})\,\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n}}\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\>e^{\text{i}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\kappa}_{n}(\tilde{x}^{\alpha}-\mathfrak{d})}

(26)

denotes the normalized surface traction due to
n
th Bloch mode, and the normalization introduced via
a_{n}
prevents ill-conditioning of the ensuing eigenproblem due to possible disparity in the magnitudes of
e^{\text{i}\tilde{\kappa}_{n}\mathfrak{d}}
(
n\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt1,2,\ldots
) for
\tilde{\kappa}_{n}
with large imaginary parts. In this setting, boundary condition (
25
) over an undulated surface cut can be parsed with the aid of (
23
) as

\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\hskip-0.2ptn}(\boldsymbol{x})=\>\boldsymbol{0}\qquad\text{for}\quad{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}},\quad\boldsymbol{x}={\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\big(\mathfrak{d}+f_{p}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\big)\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha},\quad p=\overline{1,P}.

(27)

Remark 3
.

We note that both
\boldsymbol{x}-{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\hskip-0.2ptn}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\hskip-0.2ptn}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+(\boldsymbol{x}-{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}))
in (
27
) are
\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}
-periodic functions of
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
, which motivates introduction of the rescaled boundary condition
\eqref{evan3}\hskip 0.7pt:=\hskip 0.7pte^{-\text{i}\sum_{\beta}\tilde{k}_{\beta}\tilde{x}^{\beta}}\!\hskip-0.2pt\times\eqref{evan2}
.

With
a_{n}
(
n\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt\overline{1,N}
) solving (
25
) at hand, the reduced-order model (ROM) of a surface Bloch wave obtained by restricting (
17
) to
N
deepest-penetrating modes can be written as

{\sf u}(\boldsymbol{x})\>:=\>\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\,e^{\text{i}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\kappa}_{n}(\tilde{x}^{\alpha}-\mathfrak{d})}\Big)\hskip 0.7pte^{\text{i}\sum_{\beta}\hskip-0.2pt\tilde{k}_{\beta}\tilde{x}^{\beta}}.

(28)

On introducing an auxiliary set of support functions

H^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}\\
0,&{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\notin\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}\end{array}\right.,\qquad p=\overline{1,P}

(29)

boundary conditions (
27
) can be rewritten over the unit cell of in-plane SB wave periodicity as

H^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\hskip-0.2ptn}(\boldsymbol{x})=\>\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{for}\quad{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\!\mathcal{S}},\quad\boldsymbol{x}={\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\big(\mathfrak{d}+f_{p}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\big)\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha},\quad p=\overline{1,P}.

(30)

We are now in position to expand (
30
) in Fourier series with respect to
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
for each
p
. For completeness, we shall treat separately the situations where
d=2
and
d=3
.

3.3.1
Two-dimensional problems (
d=2
)

Since
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}=\tilde{x}^{\beta}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\beta}
with no summation in this case, the cut plane
\mathcal{S}
degenerates to a cut line which allows us to rewrite (
30
) in terms of a scalar variable
\zeta:=\tilde{x}^{\beta}
as

H^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}(\zeta)\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\,\hat{\tau}_{n}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}(\zeta)\>=\>0\quad\text{for}\quad\zeta\in\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{S}},\quad p=\overline{1,P}

(31)

which deploys an auxiliary notation

\hat{g}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}(\zeta)=g(\boldsymbol{x}(\zeta))\quad\text{for}\quad\boldsymbol{x}(\zeta)=\zeta\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\beta}+\big(\mathfrak{d}+f_{p}(\zeta)\big)\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha}.

Since
\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{S}}=\{\zeta:-\tfrac{1}{2}<\hskip-0.2pt\zeta\hskip-0.2pt<\tfrac{1}{2}\}
, the left-hand side of (
31
) can be expanded in Fourier series as

\displaystyle H^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}(\zeta)\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\,\hat{\tau}_{n}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg\{\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\,\hat{\psi}_{mn}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}\bigg\}e^{{i}2\pi m\zeta},\qquad\hat{\psi}_{mn}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}=\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}H^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}(\zeta)\,\hat{\tau}_{n}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}(\zeta)\,e^{-{i}2\pi m\hskip 0.7pt\zeta}\,\textrm{d}\zeta.

(32)

This allows us to enforce the boundary condition (
31
) over the leading
2M\!+\hskip-0.2pt1
Fourier terms as

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\hat{\psi}_{mn}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}\,a_{n}=0\quad\text{for}\quad m=\overline{-M,M}~~~\text{and}~~~p=\overline{1,P}.

(33)

Recalling (
17
), algebraic system (
33
) can be rewritten as a (nonlinear) matrix eigenvalue problem

\boldsymbol{\Uppsi}\hskip-0.2pt(k_{e})\hskip 0.7pt\boldsymbol{a}\>=\>\boldsymbol{0}

(34)

where
\boldsymbol{\Uppsi}
is a complex-valued
P\hskip 0.7pt(2M\!+\hskip-0.2pt1)\times N
matrix collecting
\hat{\psi}_{mn}^{\mbox{\tiny($p$)}}
; eigenvalue
\,k_{e}
is a prescribed wavenumber in direction
\boldsymbol{e}
featured by QEP (
15
), and
\boldsymbol{a}\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt\boldsymbol{a}(k_{e})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is the vector of SB wave amplitudes
a_{n}
. In the sequel, we refer to (
34
) as the
surface-wave eigenproblem
and assume

P\hskip 0.7pt(2M\!+\hskip-0.2pt1)\geqslant N,

(35)

which caters for (but does not guarantee) having an overdetermined system.

To expose the wavenumber
k_{e}
(i.e. the surface-wave eigenvalue) and amplitudes
a_{n}\!\in\mathbb{C}
of the respective QEP eigenmodes (i.e. the surface-wave eigenvector) that describe the SB wave propagating along
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
, we next consider the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\boldsymbol{\Uppsi}
, namely

\boldsymbol{\Uppsi}(k_{e})\>=\>\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\hskip 0.7pt\overline{\boldsymbol{V}}

(36)

where
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{V}
are the unitary matrices of the left and right singular vectors, and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\text{diag}\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots\sigma_{N}\}
compiles the (real, non-negative) singular values arranged in a descending order. In this setting, (
34
) permits a non-trivial solution for
k_{e}=k_{e}^{\star}
where the condition number of
\boldsymbol{\Uppsi}
becomes unbounded, i.e.

C_{\boldsymbol{\Uppsi}}(k_{e}):=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{N}}\to\infty\quad\text{as}\quad k_{e}\to k_{e}^{\star}.

(37)

By virtue of (
37
) the surface Bloch wave(s), propagating at frequency
\omega
in direction
\boldsymbol{e}
along
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
, can be computed from the quadratic eigenspectrum of (
15
) – written over
\tilde{Y}
– via a line search seeking the peak location(s)
k_{e}^{\star}
of
C_{\boldsymbol{\Uppsi}}(k_{e})
. On recalling (
28
), for each
k_{e}^{\star}
one obtains a ROM of the surface Bloch wave as

{\sf u}^{\hskip-0.4pt\star}\hskip-0.4pt(\boldsymbol{x})\>=\,\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}^{\hskip-0.2pt\star}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\,e^{\text{i}\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{\kappa}_{n}(\tilde{x}^{\alpha}-\mathfrak{d})}\Big)\hskip 0.7pte^{\text{i}\sum_{\beta}\tilde{k}_{\beta}\tilde{x}^{\beta}},

(38)

where
a_{n}^{\star}
are the components of
\boldsymbol{a}(k_{e}^{\star})
, and
\{\tilde{\kappa}_{n},\tilde{\phi}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
solve QEP (
15
) with
\{\tilde{k}_{\beta}\}\!=\!\{k_{e}^{\star},0\}
(resp.
\tilde{k}_{\beta}\!=\!k_{e}^{\star}
) for
d\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt3
(resp.
d\hskip-0.2pt=\hskip-0.2pt2
). On denoting by
\boldsymbol{v}_{\!N}^{\star}
the
N
th right eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{\Uppsi}(k_{e}^{\star})
i.e. the
N
th column of
\boldsymbol{V}\hskip-0.2pt(k_{e}^{\star})
, from (
25
), (
34
) and (
36
) we obtain the eigenvector of modal amplitudes as

\boldsymbol{a}(k_{e}^{\star})\>=\>\boldsymbol{v}_{\!N}^{\star}

(39)

and adopt the normalization
\|\boldsymbol{a}\|=1
.

Three-dimensional problems (

In this case, the set

with

being given by (

). In this way, for a prescribed cut plane
\mathcal{S}
and direction of propagation

, the analysis of surface Bloch waves can be again described by (

)–(

), with the dimension of
\boldsymbol{\Uppsi}=\boldsymbol{\Uppsi}(k_{e})
being
P\hskip 0.7pt(2L\hskip-0.2pt+\hskip-0.2pt1)\hskip 0.7pt(2M\hskip-0.2pt+\hskip-0.2pt1)\times N
and condition (
35
) superseded by

P\hskip 0.7pt(2L\!+\hskip-0.2pt1)(2M\!+\hskip-0.2pt1)\hskip 0.7pt\geqslant\hskip 0.7ptN.

(43)

Reduced orthogonal basis for non-bijective undulations.

For non-bijective undulations (
P\hskip-0.2pt>\hskip-0.2pt1
) featuring “small” supports

L
such that

\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}e^{-\Im(\tilde{\kappa}_{n}+\tilde{\kappa}_{m})\hskip 0.7pt\ell}\>|a_{n}^{\star}\hskip 0.7pta_{m}^{\star}|\;>\;(1-\vartheta)\,\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{1}{1-e^{-\Im(\tilde{\kappa}_{n}+\tilde{\kappa}_{m})}}\,|a_{n}^{\star}\hskip 0.7pta_{m}^{\star}|,\qquad\vartheta=o(1),

(61)

which yields the skin depth

\mathfrak{s}\,=\,\frac{L_{\mathfrak{s}}\hskip-0.2pt+\hskip-0.2pt1}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\alpha}\|}\,=\,(L_{\mathfrak{s}}\hskip-0.2pt+\hskip-0.2pt1)\hskip 0.7pt\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha}\|.

(62)

As another avenue toward estimating the skin depth, one may recall (
19
) and specify
\mathfrak{s}
as the normal distance from the cut surface,

\mathfrak{s}\,=\,(\tilde{x}^{\alpha}\!-\mathfrak{d})\hskip 0.7pt\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha}\|\,:=\,\tilde{s}\hskip 0.7pt\hskip 0.7pt\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\alpha}\|

(63)

where the
nominal amplitude
of a surface Bloch wave becomes negligible relative to its surface value. By virtue of (
55
), this parameter can be estimated via the condition

\sum_{n=1}^{N}|a_{n}^{\star}|\,e^{-\Im(\tilde{\kappa}_{n})\hskip 0.7pt\tilde{s}}~=:~\vartheta\sum_{n=1}^{N}|a_{n}^{\star}|,\qquad\vartheta=o(1).

(64)

Clearly, both estimates due to (
61
) and (
64
) take into account the relative strengths of individual QEP eigenmodes, the key difference being that they rely on the decay with depth of power flow density and evanescent wave amplitude, respectively.

6
Summary

In this study, we establish a general framework for the analysis of boundary layers, termed surface Bloch (SB) waves, in periodic media truncated along a plane making rational slopes relative to the primitive vectors of the Bravais lattice. To provide a focus for the analysis, we consider the propagation of scalar waves and assume homogeneous Neumann data on the cut surface. Our analysis revolves around a quadratic eigenvalue problem (QEP) for the effective unit cell of periodicity – deriving from a geometric interplay between the mother Bravais lattice, orientation of the surface cut, and rational direction of the SB wave – that seeks the complex wavenumber normal to the cut plane given (i) the excitation frequency and (ii) wavenumber in the direction of the SB wave. In this way, the boundary layer is obtained as a linear combination of the evanescent QEP eigenstates, whose amplitudes are computed by imposing the boundary condition on the cut surface. With the QEP eigenspectrum at hand, evaluation of an SB wave – in terms of both dispersion characteristics and evanescent waveforms – entails only a low-dimensional eigenvalue problem. This feature caters for rapid exploration of the effect of (periodic) surface undulations, and so enables manipulation of the SB waves via optimal design of the surface cut. The proposed framework is readily extendable to other types of boundary conditions, governing equations (e.g. Navier, Maxwell), and interfacial waves arising between bonded periodic media.
