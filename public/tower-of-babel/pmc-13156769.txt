Taste-masked tilmicosin-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers at pilot scale for improved bioavailability and enhanced efficacy against Salmonella infection in swine
Authors: Jiahao Gong, Zedong Zhang, Xingxing Cheng, Dafeng Wang, Huixin Wang, Deshan Miao, Yuxi Yin, Yuzhen Silang, Zeng Luo, Xia Chen, Qian Gao, Yawei Qiu, Xiuge Gao, Junren Zhang, Dawei Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalmonellosis remains one of the most prevalent bacterial enteric diseases in swine production worldwide. Tilmicosin (TMS) is widely used in swine bacterial infections, but its oral application is limited by gastric degradation and intense bitterness, reducing bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. This study aimed to develop and evaluate pilot-scale TMS-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers (TMS@NLCs) to overcome these obstacles. Herein, TMS@NLCs were fabricated at 100-L scale via high-pressure homogenization. Physicochemical properties of TMS@NLCs were characterized, along with stability in simulated gastrointestinal fluids and in vitro antibacterial activity. Palatability was assessed by piglet drinking behavior and electronic tongue. Pharmacokinetics and therapeutic efficacy against Salmonella infection were evaluated in piglets. TMS@NLCs exhibited uniform spherical morphology with mean diameter 231.9 ± 33.3 nm, high encapsulation efficiency (95.50 ± 3.31%) and drug loading (4.703 ± 0.179%). Notably, they also showed excellent gastric stability, and superior taste-masking evidenced by reduced bitterness and 34% higher water intake versus commercial TMS solution. TMS@NLCs enhanced intracellular Salmonella clearance and increased oral bioavailability. In Salmonella-infected piglets, TMS@NLCs significantly reduced bacterial loads, attenuated inflammation and restored intestinal barrier, with no detectable adverse effects. Together, pilot-scale TMS@NLCs successfully overcome the inherent limitations of pronounced bitterness and gastric instability associated with the raw drug. This formulation demonstrates exceptional performance in enhancing oral bioavailability and antibacterial efficacy, highlighting its substantial potential for clinical veterinary translation.Keywords: Tilmicosin, Nanostructured lipid carriers, Pilot scale, Bitter-masking, Oral delivery, Bioavailability, Salmonella

Introduction

Salmonellosis remains one of the most prevalent bacterial enteric diseases in swine production worldwide, causing significant economic losses due to mortality, reduced growth performance, and increased medication costs. Tilmicosin (TMS) is a semisynthetic macrolide antibiotic extensively used to treat bacterial infections in livestock and poultry (Wei et al., 2013; Norcia et al., 1999). It exhibits potent activity against many Gram-positive bacteria, certain Gram-negative bacteria, and Mycoplasma (Stephens et al., 1993; Salmon et al., 1995; Yan et al., 2023), and is commonly applied for the management of pneumonia (Clark et al., 2008), mastitis (Stephens et al., 1993), and mycoplasmosis (Modric et al., 1999), and respiratory infections in pigs (Blackall et al., 1995). TMS is available as injectable formulations and oral preparations, such as water-soluble powders and premixes (Hoflack et al., 2001). However, injectable suspensions have been associated with cardiotoxicity at doses of 5 mg/kg (Committee, C.V.P., 2015), which often makes oral administration the preferred route.

Current oral formulations of TMS face two principal limitations. First, the drug's pronounced bitterness reduces palatability and animal compliance (Xiong et al., 2019). Second, although TMS shows chemical stability over pH 2.0–7.4 (Al-Ani, 2007), it remains vulnerable to degradation in the gastric environment, which lowers oral bioavailability. Poor palatability and gastric instability, therefore, hinder therapeutic efficacy and underscore the need for novel oral formulations that both mask bitterness and protect TMS from gastric degradation.

Several formulation approaches, including microcapsules (Huang et al., 2024), microparticles (Dung et al., 2023), and solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) (Wolska and Brach, 2022), have been explored to improve the palatability of veterinary drugs. Many of these techniques, however, involve complex processes that complicate clinical or industrial translation. SLNs are attractive because of their biocompatibility and low production cost (Arabestani et al., 2024), but they can suffer from low drug loading and drug expulsion from the lipid matrix, limiting long-term stability and taste-masking performance (Seyyedi-Mansour et al., 2025).

Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), which incorporate solid and liquid lipids, overcome several SLN shortcomings by offering higher drug-loading capacity and improved colloidal stability (Almeida et al., 2022). NLCs can be produced by various established methods, including high-shear homogenization (Chaudhari et al., 2021), solvent emulsification-evaporation (Kim et al., 2023), microemulsion techniques (Graván et al., 2023), and high-pressure homogenization (HPH) (Luisa Ludtke et al., 2022). Among these methods, HPH is robust and scalable, widely used in the pharmaceutical and food industries (da Silva et al., 2022; Unnisa et al., 2023) and well-suited for scale-up from laboratory to pilot or industrial production (Gong et al., 2025).

In this study, we facilely prepared pilot-scale TMS-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers (TMS@NLCs) by HPH and assessed their ability to improve gastric instability and bitterness of TMS (Scheme 1). TMS@NLCs exhibited good physicochemical characterization, favorable stability in simulated gastric fluids. Compared with a commercial TMS solution (C-TMS), TMS@NLCs effectively suppressed intracellular Salmonella infection and delivered superior taste-masked effect. Pharmacokinetic evaluation and pharmacodynamic analysis in piglets demonstrated increased oral bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy against Salmonella infection for TMS@NLCs, with no observable side effects, supporting their promise as a clinically translatable oral veterinary formulation.

Preparation of TMS@NLCs

The pilot-scale TMS@NLCs were fabricated by the HPH method based on our previously reported laboratory-scale development (Wen et al., 2021). The oil phase (9 kg OA, 1 kg SA, and 2.57 kg T80) containing 6 kg TMS was heated to 80 °C with magnetic stirring at 70 rpm and then added to the heated (80 °C) aqueous phase (100 L of 1% PVA 1788 solution containing 0.43 kg P188). The mixture was stirred at 80 °C and 70 rpm for 15 min to form a pre-emulsion, which was then passed five cycles through a high-pressure homogenizer (GJB 0.5–80, Junzhi Machinery, Changzhou, China) at 65 °C, 35 MPa. The resulting dispersion was filtered (0.8 μm) to obtain the TMS@NLCs.

Characterization of TMS@NLCs

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Tecnai 12, Philips, Netherlands), diluted TMS@NLCs were deposited onto 200-mesh copper grids, air-dried, and imaged. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM; SU8600, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), samples were drop-cast onto silicon wafers, air-dried, sputter-coated with platinum, and analyzed. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a MiniFlex 600 (Rigaku, Japan). Lyophilized powder was placed on a glass slide and scanned from 20° to 85° at 2°/min under 40 kV/40 mA. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded by IR instrument (Shimadzu, Japan). Lyophilized TMS@NLCs powder was mixed with KBr (1:150 w/w), pressed into pellets, and scanned from 4000 to 500 cm−1. Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) were quantified by HPLC as described previously (Wen et al., 2021).

HPLC analysis of TMS

TMS concentrations were quantified using an Agilent 1200 HPLC system equipped with a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 reverse-phase column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile, tetrahydrofuran, water, and n-dibutylamine phosphate buffer (12:5.5:80:2.5, v/v) delivered at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and UV detection was performed at 291 nm. Prior to automatic injection (20 μL), all samples were filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane. Quantification of TMS was achieved via a previously established HPLC linear regression model (y = 31.314x-2.0603, R2 = 0.9999), confirming high linearity within the concentration gradient of 1 to 20 μg/mL (Yu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019).

Stability in simulated gastrointestinal fluids

Firstly, simulated gastric fluids (SGF, pH 1.2) and simulated intestinal fluids (SIF, pH 6.8) were prepared. Specifically, SGF was prepared by dissolving 3.2 g of pepsin, 2.0 g of NaCl, and 7.0 mL of HCl in ultrapure water, and the final volume was adjusted to 1000 mL. And SIF was prepared by dissolving 6.8 g of KH2PO4 in 500 mL of ultrapure water and adjusting the pH to 6.8 using 0.1 M NaOH. Subsequently, 10.0 g of trypsin was added to the solution, mixed thoroughly, and diluted to a final volume of 1000 mL with ultrapure water. TMS@NLCs were diluted tenfold with SGF or SIF and incubated at 37 °C. At 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, and 12 h, samples were collected to measure HD, PDI, and ZP.

The release of TMS in simulated gastrointestinal fluids

A dialysis bag (MWCO 100 kD) containing TMS@NLCs (equivalent to 40 mg TMS) or free TMS was immersed in simulated gastrointestinal fluids and stirred at 100 rpm. At predetermined time points, 1 mL aliquots of the release medium were withdrawn and replaced with 1 mL free medium. TMS concentrations were determined by HPLC.

In vitro antibacterial activity of TMS@NLCs

Activity against extracellular bacteria

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of TMS@NLCs and C-TMS against E. coli ATCC25922, S. typhimurium SL1344, S. aureus QWJAB25, and S. agalactiae A27 were determined by the microbroth dilution method (Jie et al., 2025). The growth curves of E. coli ATCC25922 and S. typhimurium SL1344 in the presence of the formulations (TMS@NLCs or C-TMS) was measured hourly via a microplate reader (Infinite M200 Prо, Tecan, Switzerland).

Activity against intracellular Salmonella

Intracellular antibacterial activity was evaluated using a gentamicin protection assay (Božić et al., 2020). IPEC J2 cells were seeded at 4.0 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Cells were washed and infected with S. typhimurium SL1344 (multiplicity of infection (MOI) = 10) for 1 h. After infection, cells were washed and incubated in fresh medium containing 100 μg/mL gentamycin for 2 h. Cells were then treated with 1 mL DMEM/F12 containing TMS@NLCs or C-TMS (30 mg/L TMS) for 10 h. After treatment, cells were washed and lysed, and lysates were diluted and plated onto Luria-Bertani (LB) agar. Viable bacteria were determined as CFU.

Taste-masked effect of TMS@NLCs

The release of TMS in artificial saliva

Diluted TMS@NLCs (equivalent to 8 mg TMS) were placed in a dialysis bag and immersed in 40 mL artificial saliva with stirred at 100 rpm. Dialysate (0.5 mL) was collected at 5 s, 10 s, 30 s, 2 min, and 5 min, and replaced with fresh artificial saliva. TMS in the dialysate was quantified by HPLC.

Palatability evaluation in piglets

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University and conducted according to local guidelines (No. NJAU20211227206) and the ARRIVE guidelines. Healthy [Duroc×(Landrace×Yorkshire)] piglets (60–70 days old, 20 ± 2.5 kg, equal numbers of males and females) were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 4/group: Control, C-TMS and TMS@NLCs groups) using a computer-generated list of random numbers and housed in separate pens. Formulations for the treatment groups were diluted to a final TMS concentration of 75 mg/L in the drinking water. On day 1, the three groups were provided with water containing TMS-NLCs, water containing C-TMS, and plain water, respectively. The drinking media was alternated on subsequent days to minimize individual preference bias. The piglets were granted access to these media twice daily (1 h per session), with water intake recorded daily over a 3-day monitoring period. The experimental analysis was performed by investigators blinded to the group assignments.

Electronic tongue analysis

1 mg/mL of TMS@NLCs were transferred into the sample cup of the electronic tongue (SA-402B, INSENT, Japan) and positioned on its automatic injector. The electronic sensor was cleaned in a cleaning solution and reference solutions. Afterward, the sensor was calibrated to the equilibrium position before being transferred to the sample cup. Each sample was tested four times, with the initial dataset discarded. The average of the remaining three datasets was calculated to analyze the taste components.

Pharmacokinetics study of TMS@NLCs in piglets

Healthy [Duroc×(Landrace×Yorkshire)] piglets (60–70 days old, 20 ± 2.5 kg, equal numbers of males and females) were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 4/group: C-TMS and TMS@NLCs groups) using a computer-generated list of random numbers. Piglets in both groups were orally administrated their respective formulations at a dose equivalent to 20 mg/kg b.w. of TMS. Blood samples (4 mL) were collected from the anterior vena cava before dosing (0 min) and at 10 min, 20 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h and 48 h post-dose. Plasma was separated and pretreated as described previously (Zhang et al., 2020). TMS concentrations were quantified by HPLC, and pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using a non-compartmental model via WinNonLin 5.2 software. The experimental analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the group assignments.

Treatment efficacy of TMS@NLCs in Salmonella-infected piglets

Healthy [Duroc×(Landrace×Yorkshire)] piglets (60–70 days old, 20 ± 2.5 kg, males) were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5/group: PBS + PBS (healthy control), Sal. + PBS (infection control), Sal. + C-TMS and Sal. + TMS@NLCs groups) using a computer-generated list of random numbers. To establish the infection model, piglets in the three Sal. groups were orally inoculated with S. typhimurium SL1344 (5 × 108 CFU). Piglets exhibiting diarrhea and with detectable S. typhimurium in their feces were considered successfully modeled. Treatment groups (Sal. + C-TMS and Sal. + TMS@NLCs) were orally administrated their respective formulations at a dose equivalent to 20 mg/kg b.w. of TMS for three consecutive days starting upon confirmation of infection; healthy and infection control groups received equal volumes of PBS. Piglets were euthanized on day 6 post-infection. Tissue homogenates (0.5 g) from liver, spleen, and colon were plated on LB agar to enumerate CFU. The content of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and IL-1β) in the colon tissues was assessed by ELISA. Furthermore, the expression of tight junction markers (Claudin-1 and Occludin) in the colon was examined by RT-qPCR (primer sequences listed in Table 1) and immunohistochemistry. Liver and colon samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological examination. The above experimental analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the group assignments.

Safety assessment of TMS@NLCs

Healthy [Duroc×(Landrace×Yorkshire)] piglets (60–70 days old, 20 ± 2.5 kg, males) were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 5/group: Control, C-TMS, and TMS@NLCs groups) using a computer-generated list of random numbers. The C-TMS and TMS@NLCs groups were orally administered their respective formulations at a dose equivalent to 20 mg/kg b.w. of TMS daily for one consecutive week; the control group received an equal volume of PBS. At the end of the experiment, the piglets were euthanized, blood samples were collected for blood routine analysis. In addition, main organs such as liver, spleen and kidney were also collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with H&E for histopathological evaluation. The above experimental analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the group assignments.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses and graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and OriginPro 10 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using a noncompartmental model with WinNonLin 5.2 (Pharsight, Mountain View, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups and among multiple groups were performed using an unpaired Student's t-test and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test, respectively. ⁎P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; ⁎⁎P < 0.01 was considered highly significant.

Results

Preparation and characterization of pilot-scale TMS@NLCs

In this work, pilot-scale TMS@NLCs were prepared by high-pressure homogenization, and the preparation process is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Hydrodynamic diameter (HD) and polydispersity index (PDI) of TMS@NLCs decreased progressively with increasing homogenization cycles (Table 2). After five homogenization cycles, HD and PDI reached 235.4 ± 7.0 nm and 0.212 ± 0.030, respectively; additional cycles produced only marginal reductions in HD, indicating that five cycles represent the optimal homogenization condition for preparing TMS@NLCs. Subsequently, the formulation was scaled to 100 L, and three production batches were manufactured for physicochemical characterization.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed spherical nanoparticles with uniform dispersion (Fig. 1B); the mean particle diameter was <200 nm. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) showed well-dispersed particles with smooth surfaces (Fig. 1C). Single, narrow peaks in the ZP and HD distributions (Fig. 1E-F) further indicate a uniform dispersion. The formulation exhibited high EE (95.50 ± 3.31%) and DL (4.703 ± 0.179%), as shown in Fig. 1G, indicating effective encapsulation of TMS and suggesting potential for bitterness masking. XRD spectrum of TMS has no diffraction peak (Fig. 1H), indicating that TMS is an amorphous structure. Different from the small diffraction peak of NLCs, TMS@NLCs show no diffraction peaks, only a wide coherent scattering spectrum that unlike TMS. Favorable stability of TMS@NLCs

The stability of TMS@NLCs were systemically evaluated and the schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 2A. Because oral formulations are commonly diluted during administration, we first evaluated dilution stability. After 100-, 500-, 1000-, and 2000-fold dilutions, HD showed only minor increases, PDI remained below 0.3, and ZP maintained an absolute value >20 mV (Fig. 2B), indicating preservation of colloidal stability even at high dilutions. Variations in centrifugation temperature and time had minimal effect on HD, ZP, and PDI; the centrifugal stability parameter Ke remained well below 0.3 (Fig. 2C-D), consistent with good resistance to phase separation.

Temperature sensitivity was examined next (Table 3). At 25 °C, physicochemical parameters were essentially unchanged after 10 days; at 60 °C, HD increased by ∼80 nm with no major changes in other parameters. Storage at 4 °C caused >2-fold increases in HD and PDI and a ∼ 50% reduction in EE. These observations indicate optimal stability at 25 °C. Long-term storage stability was assessed at 25 °C for 90 days with sampling at 30-day intervals. The mean HD increased from ∼228 nm to ∼265 nm (Fig. 2E). PDI modestly increased but remained <0.3. ZP, DL, and EE fluctuated within acceptable ranges (Fig. 2E-F). Therefore, TMS@NLCs demonstrated satisfactory storage stability at 25 °C for 90 days.

In SGF, HD remained stable over 12 h and PDI stayed <0.3 for the first 6 h (Fig. 2G), demonstrating good resistance against acidic conditions, a key requirement for oral delivery. In SIF, HD, and PDI gradually increased over time, which contributes to sustained release of TMS (Fig. 2H). ZP reversed from negative to positive in SGF owing to gastric acidity and assumed an absolute magnitude of ∼30 mV in SIF (Fig. 2I).

Potent antibacterial activity of TMS@NLCs

We evaluated the antibacterial activity of TMS@NLCs against clinically relevant Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus and S. agalactiae) and Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli and S. typhimurium) strains. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of TMS@NLCs were identical for E. coli, S. aureus, and S. lactis (Fig. 3A). For S. typhimurium, the MIC of TMS@NLCs (32 μg/mL) was lower than that of C-TMS (64 μg/mL). Considering MICs from multiple clinical isolates of E. coli and Salmonella, however, the overall antimicrobial efficacies of TMS@NLCs and C-TMS were comparable (Table 4).

Subsequently, we selected S. typhimurium SL1344 and E. coli ATCC25922 to compare growth curves under treatment. Both TMS@NLCs and C-TMS reduced OD600 during the stationary phase (Fig. 3B-C). Bacterial viability, calculated from OD600 at 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h, showed that for S. typhimurium, TMS@NLCs matched C-TMS at 2 μg/mL and were superior at 4 μg/mL (Fig. 3D). For E. coli, TMS@NLCs were less active than C-TMS at both 2 and 4 μg/mL (Fig. 3E). Overall, TMS@NLCs matched C-TMS at lower concentration and outperformed it at the higher concentration against S. typhimurium. Because S. typhimurium is an intracellular pathogen capable of persistent infections, we examined intracellular antibacterial efficacy (Fig. 3F). Both TMS and TMS@NLCs significantly reduced intracellular bacterial load compared with control, whereas C-TMS did not effectively inhibit intracellular S. typhimurium.

Superior taste-masking of TMS@NLCs

Using a dialysis-bag method to simulate release in the oral cavity (artificial saliva), TMS@NLCs exhibited a controlled release profile (Fig. 4A), which may reduce the immediate bitter taste of TMS. To investigate the palatability of TMS@NLCs, we then assessed effects on piglets' drinking behavior. Piglets [Duroc×(Landrace×Yorkshire)] were randomly assigned to three groups (Control, C-TMS and TMS@NLCs groups) and offered three types of drinking media: plain water, water containing C-TMS, and water containing TMS@NLCs (equivalent to 75 mg/L of TMS). To minimize individual preference bias, the drinking media was alternated on subsequent days. The piglets were granted access to these media twice daily (1 h per session), with water intake recorded daily over a 3-day monitoring period. The results showed that average water intake was 6900 mL for Control, 3930 mL for C-TMS, and 5930 mL for TMS@NLCs. Statistical analysis revealed significant differences among the three groups (Fig. 4B), indicating that TMS@NLCs markedly improved palatability compared with C-TMS. To analyze the flavor of TMS@NLCs, an electronic tongue was used. The output values of each flavor are shown in Fig. 4C, the bitterness value of TMS@NLCs is significantly smaller than that of C-TMS (Fig. 4D), indicating that TMS@NLCs emulsions had more potent bitterness-masked effect.

Increased oral bioavailability of TMS@NLCs

All animal experiments comply with the ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (No. NJAU20211227206). To compare the pharmacokinetic characteristics of TMS@NLCs and C-TMS, the [Duroc×(Landrace×Yorkshire)] piglets were orally administered their respective formulations at a dose equivalent to 20 mg/kg body weight of TMS. Blood samples were collected from the anterior vena cava at 0, 10, 20, and 30 min, and 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h post-dose, and plasma was subsequently separated and pretreated. TMS concentrations were quantified by HPLC. The plasma concentration-time profiles of TMS following oral administration of C-TMS and TMS@NLCs are shown in Fig. 5. Non-compartmental pharmacokinetic parameters (Table 5) were derived from the individual concentration-time data and calculated with WinNonLin 5.2 software. The Cmax of C-TMS was slightly higher than that of TMS@NLCs, whereas the latter displayed a broader and more sustained plasma concentration profile. In addition, TMS@NLCs produced a substantially greater systemic exposure than C-TMS, as reflected by an increased area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC). The 21% increase in AUC with TMS@NLCs suggests that a lower dose or less frequent administration could achieve equivalent therapeutic effect, potentially reducing drug costs and labor in farm settings. And mean residence time (MRT) were significantly greater for TMS@NLCs than for C-TMS, indicating improved bioavailability. These results provide a practical basis for further efficacy testing and potential clinical applications.

Enhanced treatment efficacy of TMS@NLCs in Salmonella-infected piglets

A Salmonella-infected piglets' model was used to evaluate therapeutic efficacy of TMS@NLCs. Piglets orally inoculated with S. typhimurium that exhibited diarrhea and had detectable bacteria in their feces were considered successfully modeled. This was followed by the administration of therapeutic materials (C-TMS and TMS@NLCs) for three consecutive days starting upon confirmation of infection. The infection and treatment timeline are shown in Fig. 6A. Piglets were euthanized on day 6 post-infection. The liver, spleen and colon were collected and homogenized to compare the in vivo antibacterial activity of TMS@NLCs and C-TMS. The results showed both treatment groups showed significantly reduced tissue bacterial loads; notably, tissue counts in the Sal. + TMS@NLC group were lower than in the Sal. + C-TMS group (Fig. 6B-D), indicating enhanced prevention of tissue invasion, accompanied by marked improvement in clinical signs, including reduced diarrhea severity and faster recovery of appetite (data not shown). The severity of intestinal infection correlates with elevated levels of proinflammatory factors. As shown in Fig. 6E-F, the proinflammatory cytokine levels of IL-1β and IL-6 was significantly increased in the Sal. + PBS group compared to Control group, and were significantly downregulated in the Sal. + TMS@NLC group compared with Sal. + C-TMS group. Because pathogens impair intestinal barrier integrity by downregulating tight-junction proteins, we assessed tight-junction expression in the colon. qPCR analysis showed TMS@NLC significantly increased mRNA levels of Claudin-1 and Occludin in intestine compared with the Sal. + PBS and Sal. + C-TMS group (Fig. 6G-H). And immunohistochemistry further confirmed that, Sal. infection greatly reduced Occludin localization at cellular borders, whereas TMS@NLC restored their localization and intensity, which surpasses C-TMS (Fig. 6I). To evaluate the histological recovery after treatment, liver and colon samples from the piglets were examined by H&E staining. Histopathological analysis corroborated these results. Piglets in the Sal. + PBS group exhibited marked pathological changes, including increased lymphocyte infiltration and erythrocyte accumulation in the liver, and mucosal bleeding and shedding in the colon (Fig. 6J). These lesions were substantially attenuated by C-TMS treatment and were absent after TMS@NLC treatment.

Good biocompatibility of TMS@NLCs

Last, in vivo biosafety of TMS@NLCs was assessed in piglets that were orally administered formulations at a dose equivalent to 20 mg/kg TMS daily for one week. Subsequently, piglets were euthanized, and blood samples were collected for hematological analysis. The results showed hematological parameters, RBC, WBC, platelets (PLT), and neutrophil percentage (Neu), in the TMS@NLC group were within normal ranges and did not differ significantly from Control and C-TMS (Fig. 7A). To investigate whether TMS@NLCs cause tissue toxicity, main organs, including the liver and spleen, were collected and subjected to H&E staining. H&E staining results of heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney revealed no significant pathological alterations (Fig. 7B). Hence, TMS@NLCs are well tolerated at 20 mg/kg body weight in piglets.

Discussion

HD, PDI, ZP, DL and EE are critical quality attributes for nanocarriers (Wang et al., 2017), which play an important role in the stability and in vitro release effect of nanoparticle. In this work, three batches of nano-emulsions produced in the pilot scale showed minimal inter-batch variations and were consistent with laboratory-scale preparations (Wen et al., 2021) and supporting the reproducibility of the formulation and scale-up process. It is noteworthy that TMS@NLCs exhibited a high absolute ZP value (≥25 mV), which is sufficient to generate repulsive electrostatic forces that counter attractive van der Waals interactions, thereby promoting colloidal stability (Gasparelo et al., 2014). Furthermore, the ultrahigh EE suggests that TMS were almost completely encapsulated within NLCs, which is of great significance for taste-masking effect of TMS and controlling drug dissolution. Actually, in vitro release assay of TMS has indicated that TMS@NLCs had a satisfactory slow-release effect and preliminarily had good stability in gastrointestinal fluids. Collectively, characterization results indicate that pilot-scale NLCs are a viable delivery carrier for TMS.

Stability is essential to ensure the safety and efficacy of nanomedicine (Singh et al., 2017). In this work, TMS@NLCs had excellent dilution, centrifugal, storage, and gastrointestinal stability, indicating their significant potential for market transformation. Interestingly, TMS@NLCs had poor stability at 4 °C, which likely results from solidification of the liquid lipid (oleic acid) at low temperature, which can disrupt the lipid matrix and promote drug expulsion. Hence, the future transformation strategy might be to achieve low-temperature preservation through lyophilization. In addition, pH-dependent ZP switching, positive in gastric conditions (SGF) and negative in intestinal conditions (SIF), demonstrates responsiveness to the physiological environment and may enhance intestinal uptake and oral bioavailability of TMS (Xiao et al., 2024).

Interestingly, in vitro antibacterial activity assay, TMS@NLCs showed higher MICs than C-TMS for several isolates (e.g., E. coli E-3, E-6, E-9, Salmonella SD3, SD4, SD8, etc.). This variation may reflect differences in strain susceptibility or the effects of particle sizes on antimicrobial activity. Chen et al. similarly reported that TMS-based nanosuspensions with larger particle sizes exhibited higher MICs than native TMS (Chen et al., 2014). The anti-intracellular S. typhimurium results showed that TMS has good cellular permeability to kill intracellular bacteria (Zhou et al., 2020). Notably, our previous work showed that lab-scale TMS@NLCs enhance cellular uptake and raise intracellular TMS concentration (Wen et al., 2021). Collectively, TMS@NLCs have promising efficacy against bacteria especially intracellular bacteria.

Palatability is a critical consideration for oral veterinary formulations (Chachlioutaki et al., 2025; Nayak and Nayak, 2025). Taste is the result of the dissolution of molecules that interact with different targets located in the taste buds of the tongue. A physical barrier is established between the tongue and the incorporated TMS, thereby impeding drug release and achieving a taste-masked effect. The results of palatability indicated that TMS@NLCs exhibited minimal impact on the water intake of piglets during clinical administration. These findings suggest that TMS@NLCs could enhance acceptability and compliance in piglets, supporting more reliable oral dosing. Gustatory perception initiates through the binding of chemical stimuli to orosensory taste receptors, which subsequently transmit signals via afferent neural pathways to the central nervous system (CNS). The CNS integrates these chemosensory inputs and orchestrates aversive behavioral responses (e.g., refusal to swallow, drug spitting), thereby directly compromising therapeutic efficacy in clinical veterinary practice (Li et al., 2007; Keast et al., 2001). Electronic tongue, an intelligent analytical system for taste assessment, has found widespread application in pharmaceutical fields (Zniber et al., 2023). In our work, the output bitterness value of TMS@NLCs is smaller than that of C-TMS, further demonstrating superior taste-masked property of TMS@NLCs, which may be attributed to the fact that TMS@NLCs prepared by high-pressure homogenization has an ultrahigh encapsulation rate (95.50 ± 3.31%) and controlled release in saliva. In summary, the improved palatability of TMS@NLCs translates directly into practical benefits for swine producers, as it ensures more uniform drug intake during water-mediated mass medication, reducing the risk of underdosing and treatment failure.

In pharmacokinetic analysis, the comparable Tmax values for C-TMS and TMS@NLCs suggest that NLCs did not markedly delay gastrointestinal absorption. Furthermore, the elimination half-life of TMS remained largely unchanged (P>0.05), indicating that the enhanced exposure of TMS@NLCs was primarily attributable to improved oral bioavailability rather than alterations in systemic clearance. And the increased bioavailability of TMS@NLCs can be attributed to several factors. First, encapsulation within a lipid matrix protected TMS from acidic degradation in the stomach, as evidenced by its improved stability in simulated gastrointestinal fluids. Second, the NLCs system likely enhanced intestinal absorption through improved solubilization and prolonged residence time at the absorption site (Feng et al., 2025). Third, improved palatability and taste masking reduced aversive responses in piglets, potentially minimizing variability in oral intake and contributing to more consistent systemic exposure.

Given the ubiquity and severity of S. typhimurium in pig husbandry (Horvathova et al., 2023; Wales and Davies, 2017), a piglets-infecting Salmonella model was established to evaluate in vivo efficacy of TMS@NLCs. The data indicate that TMS@NLCs provide superior in vivo antibacterial efficacy compared with C-TMS, likely attributable to improved stability in gastrointestinal fluids, enhanced intracellular antimicrobial activity, better palatability, and increased oral bioavailability. Finally, because biocompatibility is a critical requirement for nano-formulations (Singh et al., 2019), the biocompatibility of TMS@NLCs in piglets was investigated. The results showed that TMS@NLCs did not cause obvious toxicity. Nevertheless, further biocompatibility studies, particularly long-term safety assessment, are necessary to fully confirm safety of TMS@NLCs for veterinary use.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrate the feasibility of producing pilot-scale TMS@NLCs by high-pressure homogenization. The resulting nanoparticles favorable stability and resistance to gastric conditions. Compared with the commercial TMS solution, TMS@NLCs showed enhanced antibacterial activity against intracellular Salmonella, improved piglet's palatability due to superior bitterness-masked efficacy, and increased bioavailability. Pharmacodynamic evaluation in a Salmonella-infected piglets model confirmed its enhanced therapeutic efficacy with negligible side effects. Taken together, these findings support the translational potential of TMS@NLCs as a practical oral delivery strategy to reduce the bitterness of veterinary antimicrobials for swine health management. Future work should further evaluate long-term biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy against other bacterial infections in swine.
