Application of PDCA cycle management in the rational use of perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis in orthopedic clean surgery
Authors: Ya-ni Chai, Xiao-bin An, Jing Xue, Bo-yang Zhou, Ya-juan Shi, Nan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal antimicrobial prophylaxis has been demonstrated to reduce the incidence of surgical site infections (SSIs). The PDCA cycle management approach has been extensively adopted in the healthcare industry. The objective of this study is to examine the impact of PDCA cycle management on the rational use of perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis in orthopedic clean surgeries.MethodsFollowing a thorough evaluation of the antibiotic prescription practices for patients undergoing orthopedic clean surgeries at the hospital during the initial three-month period of 2023, a series of pertinent measures were devised and initiated as a dynamic intervention from April 2023 to February 2024. The rationality of perioperative prophylactic antimicrobial use and clinical outcomes were compared before and after the intervention.ResultsThe irrational use of perioperative prophylactic antibiotics was significantly reduced. It is mainly reflected in the utilization of surgical antibiotic prophylaxis [94.7% vs. 84.8%, p < 0.001, 95% CI (1.753,6.851)], inappropriate prophylaxis [68.7% vs. 47.3%, p < 0.001,95% CI (0.276,0.595)], unindicated prophylaxis [18.0% vs. 2.9%, p < 0.001,95% CI (0.045,0.273)], inappropriate drug selection [13.3% vs. 2.9%, p < 0.001, 95% CI (0.057,0.413)], and inappropriate intraoperative redosing [1.7% vs. 0%, p = 0.024,95% CI (0.964,0.996)]. In addition, the rate of inappropriate dosage [1.3% vs. 0%, p = 0.120, 95% CI (0.971,0.996)], inappropriate timing [3.9% vs. 1.7%, p = 0.146,95% CI (0.092,1.463)], and inappropriate postoperative antibiotic switching [0.4% vs. 0%, p = 0.494,95% CI (0.984,0.996)] were also decreased. However, the rate of inappropriate prophylaxis duration [42.1% vs. 42.3%, p = 0.965, 95% CI (0.680, 1.462)] and combination treatment [1.3% vs. 7.1%, p = 0.002, 95% CI (2.052, 23.292)] increased. With regard to clinical outcomes, the length of stay[15(10) to 13(7), p = 0.012, 95% CI(0.000,3.000)], hospitalization expenses[50244.96(53,376.00) to 41,539.92(31,809.94), p = 0.01, 95% CI (2157.160, 15,935.620)], medication costs[4326.215(3672.48) to 3272.17(2885.15), p < 0.001, 95% CI (498.170, 1745.440)], antibiotic costs [274.435(351.89) to 219.12(239.04), p = 0.004, 95% CI (19.920,119.520)], and other perioperative infections rates [46.3% to 26.4%, p < 0.001, 95% CI (1.427,4.090)] all decreased significantly after the intervention.ConclusionThe PDCA cycle management strategy was found to be an effective method of optimizing the rational use of perioperative prophylactic antibiotics and clinical outcomes in orthopedic clean surgeries. Background

Perioperative infections, including surgical site infections (SSIs), systemic infections, and localized infections occurring outside the surgical site, such as lung and urinary tract infections [1], are defined as infections that occur at or near the surgical incision within 30 days post-surgery, or within 90 days if a prosthetic device is implanted during the surgery [1–3]. SSIs represent a prevalent category of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) [2, 4, 5], accounting for approximately one-fifth of all HAIs [3, 6]. Statistically, around 0.5% to 3% of surgical patients will develop an infection [2]. There are significant differences in the incidence of SSIs between developed and developing countries [1]. The incidence of SSIs in Europe is presently shown to range from 5 to 18% [6], though it is significantly elevated in low-income nations [1, 3, 6]. SSIs can substantially impact patient outcomes. Patients who experience SSIs postoperatively have a 2- to 11-fold increased risk of mortality compared to those without SSIs [2, 7, 8]. Moreover, these patients endure extended hospitalizations [2, 3, 8], elevated complication rates [6], and augmented readmission rates [3, 6, 8]. Surgical antibiotic prophylaxis (SAP) entails the preoperative prescription of antibiotics to prevent SSIs during clean and clean-contaminated surgeries [9–11]. Effective preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis significantly decreases the incidence of SSIs [9, 11–13]. Specific guidelines have been established by diverse surgical societies to guarantee objectivity and clarity in the surgical domain [1, 9, 14–16]. Correct application of the preventive strategies outlined in clinical guidelines and protocols can avert around 60% of SSIs [5, 17]. Despite attempts to enhance adherence to established agreements, compliance continues to be inadequate, and occurrences of non-compliance frequently remain unreported [18]. Statistics show that up to 66.3% of patients often do not fully adhere to these preventative regimens [5].

In orthopedic clean operations, including joint replacement, internal fixation of fractures with screws, pins, plates, and nails, as well as spinal surgeries, SSIs significantly affect outcomes due to the presence of implanted biomaterials. Therefore, a thorough range of procedures must be implemented before, during, and after surgery to alleviate the severe consequences of infection. Notwithstanding preventive measures, the indiscriminate prophylactic use of antibiotics persists extensively [19]. Antibiotics are often misused in terms of timing, duration, and dosage, and unnecessary combinations of antibiotics are prescribed.

Excessive or improper use of antimicrobials in the aforementioned conditions may elevate the risk of multidrug-resistant microbial illnesses and impose an expensive burden on patients. Consequently, it is essential to execute initiatives, including antimicrobial stewardship, to enhance adherence to perioperative antibiotic prophylactic protocols [5, 9]. The Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle, introduced by American quality management expert Dr. W. Edwards Deming, is a continuous improvement paradigm consisting of four iterative stages [20]. This methodology serves as an essential management tool for optimizing institutional processes. The PDCA cycle overcomes the limitations of conventional quality improvement methods, which lack targeted and planned improvements [21]. The PDCA cycle is a scientific and effective management approach extensively employed in the medical sector, including nutrition management for oncology patients [21], hyperglycemia management in critically ill patients [22], gestational diabetes mellitus [23], postoperative care for hip arthroplasty patients [24], and nanoemulsion quality control [25], yielding favorable outcomes.

The present study implements the PDCA cycle to manage the perioperative prophylactic administration of antimicrobial drugs in orthopedic patients within a hospital setting. A range of strategies is employed, including education, training, and assessment, to investigate the impact of PDCA cyclic management on antimicrobial prophylaxis for patients undergoing orthopedic clean surgeries.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

A retrospective analysis was conducted to evaluate the reasonableness of perioperative prophylactic antimicrobial medication usage in 233 patients undergoing orthopedic clean surgeries at the hospital from January to March 2023. The pertinent data were employed in the initial phase of the preintervention group (pre-1). The PDCA management cycle was implemented in March 2023. The 218 patients from April to June 2023 formed the initial cohort of the postintervention group (post-1). In July 2023, the data displayed irregular fluctuations, prompting the initiation of the second round of intervention. The preintervention group of the second round (pre-2) consisted of 85 patients from July 2023, whereas the postintervention group of the second round (post-2) encompassed 253 patients from August to October 2023. In November 2023, the data displayed irregular variations, prompting the commencement of the third round of intervention. The preintervention cohort of the third round (pre-3) consisted of 82 patients from this month, while the postintervention cohort of the third round (post-3) encompassed 239 patients from December 2023 to February 2024. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study’s inclusion criteria mandated that patients satisfy three conditions: (1) undergo aseptic orthopedic surgery; (2) receive perioperative antimicrobial drugs; and (3) possess comprehensive clinical data. Patients having preoperative infections were excluded according to the study's exclusion criteria. The hospital ethics committee sanctioned the study.

Interventions

The present study employed the PDCA cycle management approach. Clinical pharmacists implemented targeted interventions for perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis in clean orthopedic surgeries, which specifically encompassed: (1) Compliance with guidelines must be guaranteed by the implementation of training and subsequent evaluations; (2) A monthly evaluation of antibiotic prophylactic utilization in clean surgical procedures will be undertaken; and (3) Regular review and feedback meetings with prescribing physicians will be conducted. Interventions were perpetually refined according to results.

Antibiotic practices before and after the PDCA were evaluated against the Guiding Principles for Clinical Application of Antimicrobial Drugs (2015 Edition) (hereinafter referred to as “Guiding Principles”) and specialty consensus guidelines as standards. Study index

A comparative analysis of perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis practices was conducted before and after three rounds of interventions. The evaluation indicator system was established based on national clinical guidelines, pharmacological specifications, and institutional-specific considerations. A comprehensive set of seven key quality indicators was developed, encompassing: indication, drug selection, dosage, timing, intraoperative redosing, prophylaxis duration, combination, and postoperative switching (detailed in Table 2).

Additionally, the study defined a series of outcome variables for assessment, encompassing perioperative infections, length of stay (LOS), hospitalization costs, medication costs, and antibiotic costs.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent-samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and analyzed with Mann–Whitney U tests or Kruskal–Wallis H tests. Categorical data are reported as percentages (%) and compared by χ2 tests. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p < 0.05. Data visualization was conducted with GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Time-series forecasting employed autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) modeling via the SPSSAU online platform (https://spssau.net/index.html) and DMSAS 1.4.2 (Guangzhou Bowei Data Technology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China).

Results

Baseline characteristics

The six groups demonstrated similar baseline characteristics, with no statistically significant variations among them in any category (Table 1; Kruskal–Wallis H tests for continuous data, chi-square test for categorical data; all p > 0.05).

Adherence to perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis guidelines

The prophylactic use of antimicrobial drugs in general

To evaluate the impact of PDCA cycle management on perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis in orthopedic clean surgery, we analyzed prophylaxis utilization and inappropriate usage rates across six groups. As detailed in Fig. 2, the intervention significantly reduced both the overall prophylaxis rate [Fig. 2A, from 94.7% to 84.8%, χ2 = 13.757, p < 0.001, 95% CI (1.753, 6.851)] and inappropriate prophylaxis rate [Fig. 2B, from 68.7% to 47.3%, χ2 = 22.134, p < 0.001, 95% CI (0.276,0.595)]. Notably, statistically significant reductions in prophylaxis rate were only observed after the first two intervention cycles [Fig. 2A,Cycle 1: χ2 = 11.861, p < 0.001, 95% CI (1.642,6.349); Cycle 2: χ2 = 6.645, p = 0.01, 95% CI (1.499,14.675); Cycle 3: χ2 = 0.348, p = 0.555, 95% CI (0.648,2.822)]. In contrast, inappropriate prophylaxis rates decreased significantly after every cycle [Fig. 2B, Cycle 1: χ2 = 32.001, p < 0.001, 95% CI (0.223,0.483); Cycle 2: χ2 = 3.835, p = 0.05, 95% CI (0.352,0.986); Cycle 3: χ2 = 8.438, p = 0.004, 95% CI (0.257,0.791)].

Adherence to perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis guidelines

Indication

Prophylactic antibiotics are typically not advised for orthopedic clean procedures. The utilization is warranted following foreign body implantation due to the diagnostic challenges, therapy difficulties, and elevated chance of disability related to implant infections [26–28]. Further indications encompass prolonged operations and advanced age, as detailed in Table 2. Before intervention, 18.0% of orthopedic clean surgeries at the institution received unnecessary antimicrobial prophylaxis. Following three PDCA cycles, this proportion decreased significantly to 2.9% [Fig. 2C, χ2 = 28.902, p < 0.001, 95% CI (0.045, 0.273)].

Drug selection

Prophylactic antibiotics should be directed toward probable intraoperative contaminants. Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, and Streptococcus spp are the primary pathogens identified in orthopedic surgeries. The Guiding Principles indicate that cefazolin or cefuroxime serves as the first-line drug. In cases of suspected Methicillin-resistant staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) colonization or within MRSA-endemic institutions, the use of vancomycin/norvancomycin is warranted. Topical vancomycin has been shown to decrease the occurrence of SSIs after spinal surgery and arthroplasty procedures [1]. Patients with cephalosporin allergies may be administered vancomycin/norvancomycin or clindamycin to ensure Gram-positive coverage (Table 2). Following three PDCA cycles, (1) inappropriate drug selection rates decreased from 13.3% to 2.9% [Fig. 2D, χ2 = 17.158, p < 0.001, 95% CI (0.057,0.413)]; (2) postintervention inappropriate selections were primarily attributable to patients with positive skin tests [Supplementary material 1, from 9.7% to 57.1%; χ2' = 5.694, p = 0.017,95% CI(0.010,0.517)]; and (3) the utilization of cefoxitin, the most common inappropriate drug, declined from 11.6% to 0%[ Supplementary material 1, χ2 = 29.376, p < 0.001, 95% CI (0.843,0.923)].

Dosage

Preintervention data revealed consistently low rates of inappropriate antimicrobial dosing (1.3%) for clean orthopedic surgeries at the institution. Following the first PDCA cycle, this rate decreased to 0.5% [Fig. 2E, Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.624 vs. baseline,95% CI(0.156,2.359)]. Notably, no further dosing inappropriateness was observed during the subsequent two intervention cycles, with rates remaining at 0% throughout the sustainability phase [Fig. 2E, Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.120, 95% CI (0.971,0.996) vs. baseline].

Timing

Timely administration of antimicrobials is crucial for achieving therapeutic tissue concentrations during surgical site exposure, thereby enhancing efficacy against potential contaminants (Table 2). The baseline rates of inappropriate timing for prophylaxis in orthopedic clean surgery were consistently low (3.9%). Despite a progressive decline in inappropriate prophylaxis timing across intervention cycles, culminating in a low of 1.6%, no cycle exhibited a statistically significant reduction [Fig. 2F, Cycle 1: χ2 = 0.922, p = 0.337, 95% CI (0.137,1.794); Cycle 2: χ2' = 0.424, p = 0.515, 95% CI (0.089,1.969); Cycle 3: χ2' = 0.389, p = 0.533,95% CI(0.085,2.037)]. Following three PDCA cycles, the inappropriate timing of prophylaxis shifted to exclusively postoperative administration (1.7%), resulting in the complete eradication of preoperative timing inappropriateness [Supplementary material 1, Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.029,95% CI(0.957,0.996) vs. baseline].

Intraoperative antimicrobial redosing

The effective coverage period of antimicrobial drugs should encompass the entire surgical procedure, with additional doses administered intraoperatively as necessary [9, 14, 29](Table 2). Following each intervention round, the incidence of intraoperative additions during orthopedic clean surgeries decreased. After three intervention rounds, the intraoperative addition rate demonstrated a notable reduction from 11.2% to 5.4% [Supplementary material 1, χ2 = 5.092, p = 0.024,95% CI (1.105,4.748)]. Simultaneously, a further reduction in inappropriate intraoperative redosing was observed, decreasing from 1.7% to 0% [Fig. 2G, 1.7% reduction; Fisher's exact test, p = 0.024, 95% CI(0.964,0.996) vs. baseline].

Duration of prophylaxis

Prolonging the duration of antimicrobial prophylaxis postoperatively is not advisable. Extending the prophylactic period excessively does not improve preventive efficacy and instead raises the risk of infections from drug-resistant bacteria (Table 2) [1]. Before the intervention was implemented, a significantly high percentage (42.1%) of orthopedic clean surgeries at the hospital involved the administration of inappropriate durations of antibiotic prophylaxis. Following each intervention, a decrease in the percentage of inappropriate prophylaxis duration was noted, with significant reductions occurring only in the first and third cycles [Fig. 2J, Cycle 1: χ2 = 9.127, p = 0.003, 95% CI (0.358,0.815); Cycle 2: χ2 = 2.652, p = 0.103,95% CI(0.384,1.081); Cycle 3: χ2 = 6.504, p = 0.011, 95% CI (0.314,0.874)]. Nonetheless, no statistically significant difference was noted when compared to the baseline [χ2 = 0.002, p = 0.965, 95% CI (0.680,1.462)]. A comparative analysis of prophylactic administration duration was conducted, revealing a similar trend [Fig. 2K, pre-1:48(60), post-1:36(48), pre-2:72(114), post-2:48(108), pre-3:72(96), post-3:48(60); Cycle 1: U = 21,616.00, p = 0.005, 95% CI (0.000,12.000); Cycle 2: U = 9496.50, p = 0.104, 95% CI(0.000,24.000); Cycle 3: U = 7458.00, p = 0.001, 95% CI (0.000, 36.000)].

Combination or switching

The guidelines recommend the prioritization of monotherapy in prophylactic antimicrobial use, avoiding unnecessary combination therapy and unwarranted postoperative alterations in antimicrobial drugs (Table 2). The results showed the following: (1) Following each intervention round, the frequency of inappropriate combination therapy declined, though none of these declines achieved statistical significance [Fig. 2I, Cycle 1: Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.624, 95% CI (0.156,2.264); Cycle 2: χ2 = 0.139, p = 0.709, 95% CI (0.307,3.012); Cycle 3: χ2 = 0.179, p = 0.672, 95% CI (0.326,2.544)]; (2) In comparison to the baseline, the frequency of combination therapy increased after three rounds of intervention [Fig. 2I, Baseline: 1.3%, Post-3: 7.1%, χ2 = 9.867, p = 0.002, 95% CI (2.052,23.292)]; (3) A significant reduction in the proportion of unjustified postoperative antimicrobial changes was noted only after the third round of intervention [Fig. 2H, Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.016,95% CI (0.919,0.989)]; (4) Compared to the baseline, the proportion of unjustified postoperative antimicrobial changes decreased after three rounds of intervention, yet this decrease was not statistically significant [Fig. 2H, Baseline: 0.4%, Post-3: 0%, Fisher's exact test, p = 0.494, 95% CI (0.984, 0.996)].

Clinical outcomes of antimicrobial stewardship in orthopedic clean surgeries

Improving clinical outcomes is a primary goal of perioperative management. Effective antimicrobial prophylaxis has been shown to decrease rates of SSIs, reduce LOS, and lessen economic impacts [2, 3, 8, 9, 11, 13]. This study reported no occurrences of surgical site infections during hospitalization, nor were there any readmissions attributed to SSIs (Fig. 3E). A notable reduction in non-SSI perioperative infections was recorded after Cycle 3 [Fig. 3C, 46.3% to 26.4%, χ2 = 11.304, p < 0.001, 95% CI (1.427,4.090)]. A statistically significant reduction in LOS was noted following the Cycle 3 [Fig. 3A, pre-3:15[10], post-3:13[7], U = 7870.5, p = 0.012, 95% CI (0.000,3.000)]; however, the overall reduction did not achieve statistical significance when compared to baseline values [Fig. 3A, pre-1:13[10], U = 27,666.5, p = 0.905, 95% CI (− 1.000,1.000)]. Postintervention, hospitalization costs exhibited a decrease, with significant reductions becoming apparent only after the Cycle 3 [Fig. 3B, pre-3:50,244.96(53,376.00), post-3:41,539.92(31,809.94), U = 7943.0, p = 0.01, 95% CI (2157.160, 15,935.620)], demonstrating notable declines relative to baseline levels [Fig. 3B, pre-1:49,192.16(54,127.27), U = 23,224.0, p = 0.002, 95% CI (2960.420, 13,696.920)]. After the Cycle 3 intervention, a notable decrease in drug costs was recorded [Fig. 3C, pre-3:4326.215(3672.48), post-3:3272.17(2885.15), U = 7227.0, p < 0.001, 95% CI (498.170, 1745.440)]; however, this reduction did not achieve statistical significance relative to the baseline [Fig. 3C, pre-1:2863.34(3105.86), U = 27,675.0, p = 0.909, 95% CI (− 384.800, 327.940)]. The financial burden of antibiotic treatment demonstrated significant cyclical fluctuations following the implementation of all three interventions [Fig. 3D, pre-1:179.28(239.04), post-1:139.44(199.20), pre-2:358.56(438.24), post-2:199.20(358.58), pre-3:274.435(351.89), post-3:219.12(239.04); Cycle 1: U = 21,644.0, p = 0.006, 95% CI (0.000, 40.600); Cycle 2: U = 9070.5, p = 0.030, 95% CI (0.000, 119.520); Cycle 3: U = 7723.0, p = 0.004, 95% CI (19.920, 119.520)]. Nonetheless, the overall reduction relative to the baseline was statistically non-significant [Fig. 3D, U = 27,418.5, p = 0.773, 95% CI (− 16.640, 23.200)]. Discussion

Antimicrobial resistance represents a significant global challenge, requiring proper application of antibiotics and improved infection control measures in hospitals [1]. SSIs, the most common orthopedic HAI, can be significantly reduced through the use of antimicrobial prophylaxis [7]. Nevertheless, limited research has assessed the efficacy of pharmacist-led interventions in enhancing antimicrobial prophylaxis for orthopedic clean surgeries. This study employed the PDCA cycle, a validated management approach in healthcare [21, 23, 24], to assess its effect on perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis in orthopedic clean surgeries.

Analysis of the effects of antibiotic intervention in clean orthopedic surgery

The use of the PDCA cycle management approach has shown significant improvement in the effective management of perioperative prophylactic antibiotics in orthopedic surgery. This method has demonstrated the ability to improve compliance with established guidelines and to lead to positive changes in clinical outcomes. The subsequent key findings are outlined: (1) The data indicate a reduction in the use of surgical antibiotic prophylaxis, mainly due to a decrease in unindicated prophylactic applications; however, this rate still surpasses the national benchmark of 30%. This may be associated with the hospital's elevated percentage of orthopedic clean surgeries that include implant placement; (2) Furthermore, the rates of inappropriate prophylaxis following the intervention showed a significant decrease. Nonetheless, ongoing issues arose following three PDCA cycles, such as unindicated use, suboptimal drug selection, incorrect timing, unreasonable combination regimens, and extended durations, with the latter becoming the primary concern; (3) Clinical outcomes demonstrated significant enhancement. A decrease in perioperative infection rates was noted; however, confounding factors, including seasonal peaks of respiratory infections in specific regions, cannot be excluded. Furthermore, a decrease in hospital stays and a reduction in medical costs at discharge were observed.

After three rounds of intervention, the inappropriate selection of drugs predominantly included levofloxacin and ceftriaxone (refer to Supplementary Material 1), primarily due to positive skin tests for cefuroxime. According to established guidelines, vancomycin is the only alternative for patients who have tested positive for skin tests at the hospital. Clinicians’ concerns regarding the use of “special restricted antimicrobial drugs” led to their replacement with ceftriaxone or levofloxacin, which was the main factor contributing to suboptimal selection. A considerable number of patients with positive skin tests do not demonstrate IgE-mediated reactions or a history of severe allergies and can tolerate cephalosporins. Preoperative assessment of cephalosporin allergy risk is advisable; however, its implementation is often hindered by strained physician–patient relationships.

The timing of antibiotic administration is essential for the effectiveness of surgical prophylaxis. The optimal timing for this procedure continues to be a subject of controversy. Research shows that administering medication more than 120 minutes before or after surgery elevates the risk of surgical site infection compared to administration within 120 min before surgery [8]. Additionally, there is no significant difference in efficacy when administered within the 120-minute preoperative window [8, 30–34]. For drugs characterized by a short half-life, such as cefazolin and cefuroxime, guidelines suggest administration within 60 min before surgery to ensure adequate tissue concentrations [29, 35]. Cefuroxime exhibits optimal efficacy when given 10–25 min before surgery [36]. Current guidelines suggest administering antibiotics 30–60 min before surgery or at the onset of anesthesia [9, 14, 15, 29, 35, 37], taking into consideration the antibiotic half-life [9, 35, 38]. Vancomycin and fluoroquinolones require administration 120 min before surgery [15, 29]. A non-statistically significant reduction in the proportion of inappropriate timing from 3.9% to 1.7% was noted, with errors now predominantly occurring in the postoperative phase (see Supplementary material 1). This suggests that clinicians acknowledge the significance of timing, yet do not possess a comprehensive understanding. This intervention requires continuous promotion and reinforcement.

Post-wound closure antibiotic redosing is typically unnecessary [39] and may elevate the risks of antimicrobial resistance, C. difficile infection, and acute kidney injury [39–41]. Single-dose regimens show non-inferiority compared to multi-dose regimens administered within 24 h for the prevention of SSIs [42]. Guidelines suggest a maximum of 24-hour prophylaxis for clean orthopedic surgeries [29], with research indicating that shorter durations are non-inferior in preventing HAI. Extended prophylaxis, lasting up to 48 h, may be warranted for spinal fixation and joint replacements [39]. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis should preferably utilize single-drug regimens, thereby avoiding unnecessary combinations. Following three PDCA cycles, the 48-hour discontinuation rate showed no statistically significant change from baseline, whereas the use of irrational combination therapy increased unexpectedly. Despite physicians' familiarity with prophylactic guidelines, the emphasis on preventing postoperative infections led to unnecessary use of single or combined antibiotics. Unpredictable intraoperative factors additionally influenced decisions regarding postoperative prophylaxis. These elements collectively represent fundamental obstacles to strict adherence to guidelines.

The role of the PDCA cycle in the intervention of antibiotic use in clean orthopedic surgery

The PDCA cycle is a commonly utilized management tool in the field of medicine [21–24]. In the past, the management of perioperative antimicrobials within the orthopedics department has been characterized by a lack of standardization and organization. The implementation of a multi-departmental PDCA model systematically intervened in this process. The findings indicate that the PDCA cycle serves as an effective management tool for enhancing the appropriateness of prophylactic antimicrobial use in orthopedic clean surgery. The decrease in inappropriate use proved to be unsustainable, a finding further corroborated by the ARIMA model (Supplementary materials 2–5). The findings suggest that the PDCA management model is time-dependent, aligning with previous research [20], likely attributable to its inadequacy in swiftly addressing emerging issues.

This study presented three significant potential limitations: (1) This study, being single-center and single-department with a limited sample size and no multi-department comparisons, may exhibit issues with generalizability and accuracy; (2) The suboptimal timing of PDCA interventions has led to a rebound in efficacy; and (3) Most importantly, insufficient exploration of the root causes behind clinicians’ implementation barriers has led to non-targeted measures, which ultimately failed to maintain improvements in inappropriate medication practices.

A three-pronged approach is proposed to address these limitations. Improving promptness: an antimicrobial stewardship decision support system, currently under development, will incorporate prescription alerts for inappropriate drug selection, dosage, or duration, thereby enhancing hospital-wide perioperative management. Strengthening accountability through institution-level performance evaluation, which has been implemented, has shown improved clinician compliance. Additionally, root-cause intervention is being addressed. The Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) questionnaires are being developed to identify barriers faced by frontline providers. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) will systematically analyze implementation determinants across five domains, utilizing Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) consensus to guide strategy selection.

Conclusions

In summary, the PDCA cycle standardizes perioperative antimicrobial use in orthopedic clean surgeries; however, its effectiveness is constrained by time limitations. Future research should focus on enhancing interdepartmental collaboration, improving information technology integration, and optimizing management strategies to sustainably advance antimicrobial stewardship.
