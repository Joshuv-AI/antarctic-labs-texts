Association of “a body shape index” with the risk of developing colorectal cancer in U.S. patients with metabolic syndrome: evidence from the NHANES 1999–2018
Authors: Airepati Kurexi, Jingqi Peng, Juyi Yao, Lin Wang, Qisan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide and presents a significant challenge to public health. Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a condition that is predominantly characterized by abdominal obesity and metabolic abnormalities such as hypertension, hyperglycemia, and hyperlipidemia, and it is one of the critical risk factors for CRC. Traditional anthropometric measures have limitations in accurately assessing the risk associated with abdominal obesity. This study aimed to investigate the association between “A Body Shape Index” (ABSI) and the risk of developing CRC among individuals with MetS utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).MethodsThis cross-sectional study conducted a statistical analysis of all adult participants who met the diagnostic criteria for MetS in the NHANES data from 1999 to 2018. The ABSI was calculated to quantify abdominal obesity. ABSI is derived from a formula that incorporates waist circumference (WC), body mass index (BMI), and height, and is calculated as ABSI = WC / (BMI^(2/3) × Height^(1/2)). Multivariate logistic regression modeling was used to examine the independent association between ABSI and CRC incidence. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were employed to analyze the ability of ABSI compared to traditional metrics in identifying CRC risk.ResultsThis study involved 16,018 MetS patients with a mean age of 51.8 years, of whom 50.3% were male and 49.7% were female. Logistic regression adjusted for confounders revealed a significant association between an elevated ABSI and an increased risk of developing CRC (odds ratio (OR): 1.433, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.116 to 1.841; P = 0.005). ROC analyses confirmed that the predictive accuracy of the ABSI for the risk of developing CRC area under the curve (AUC): (0.668, 95% CI: 0.624 to 0.713) surpassed that of traditional measurement methods.ConclusionAmong individuals with MetS, the ABSI is linked to an elevated risk of developing CRC. Compared with traditional anthropometric indices, the ABSI is a superior predictive marker for the risk of developing CRC.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major public health challenge worldwide, and it is the third most common cancer in the world [1]. According to data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), among the many diseases worldwide, CRC ranks highly in both incidence and mortality rates [2]. Lifestyle factors, such as dietary habits, weight management, smoking behavior, and physical activity levels, have been proven to be closely linked to the risk of developing CRC [3, 4]. In particular, dietary patterns high in fat and low in fiber, along with obesity and a lack of physical activity, are identified as primary modifiable risk factors associated with an increased risk of developing CRC [5, 6]. Fortunately, the survival rate of patients with CRC has significantly improved over the past few decades due to the widespread implementation of screening programs and advancements in treatment methods [7]. Thus, the importance of early diagnosis and timely treatment in improving survival rates cannot be overstated.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) status, characterized by a cluster of risk factors such as hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and abdominal obesity, is associated with an increased risk of developing CRC [8, 9]. The presence of MetS reflects an unhealthy metabolic state that could increase the risk of developing CRC through various mechanisms, including inflammation, insulin resistance, and hormonal imbalances [10]. A meta-analysis further confirmed the associations of obesity, elevated blood sugar, and increased blood pressure with a greater risk of incident CRC and CRC-related mortality among MetS patients [11–13].

Traditionally, indicators such as body mass index (BMI), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), waist-to-weight ratio, and body roundness index (BRI) have been widely utilized to assess an individual’s obesity level and health risks [14–16]. However, these indicators have limitations regarding their accuracy in the assessment of abdominal obesity and its associated health risks [17]. The introduction of “A Body Shape Index” (ABSI) provides a new solution to this issue. The ABSI was developed by Krakauer et al. using linear least squares regression using three commonly used body measurements, weight, height, and waist circumference (WC), and was initially applied to predict mortality risk in populations [18]. Further studies have shown that the ABSI stands out as the sole abdominal obesity index unaffected by the obesity paradox, and is statistically independent of BMI, providing a more precise reflection of the impact of abdominal obesity on health risks [19]. Compared to other indices, the ABSI demonstrates greater sensitivity and specificity for health issues related to abdominal obesity among patients with MetS [20]. Studies have shown an association between ABSI and CRC prevalence in the general population [21, 22]. To our knowledge, no study has explored the association between abdominal obesity levels assessed by the ABSI and the risk of developing CRC among patients with MetS. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database is a national survey of the health of adults and children in the United States. Information on demographics, dietary habits, and medical history is collected through a standardized process. NHANES has a distinctive methodology that combines both interviews and physical examinations, making it a comprehensive resource for assessing the overall health status of the population [23]. Therefore, in this study, data from the NHANES were utilized to investigate the association between ABSI-assessed abdominal obesity levels and the risk of developing CRC among individuals with MetS.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a cross-sectional study. The data for the study were derived from the NHANES for the years 1999 to 2018. According to the data gathered across nine cycles of the NHANES from 1999 to 2018, a cohort of 19,833 participants was initially identified as meeting the established diagnostic criteria for MetS. To ensure the specificity and relevance of the study population to the research objectives, a rigorous exclusion process was implemented. First, participants who were diagnosed with non-CRC cancers, including but not limited to skin, respiratory, hematologic, head and neck, breast, musculoskeletal, and genitourinary cancers, were excluded from the study, which included a total of 2,066 individuals. Subsequently, the analysis further excluded 570 participants due to the absence of complete ABSI data. Additionally, to maintain a focus on adult populations, 1,179 minor participants were also removed from the potential study cohort. Following these exclusions, the study ultimately included a total of 16,018 participants who were diagnosed with MetS, all of whom met the stringent criteria established for this research (Fig. 1). The study data were approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Diagnostic criteria for CRC

The diagnosis of CRC in this study was based on self-reported data collected during the NHANES survey. Participants were asked two key questions: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had cancer or a malignancy of any kind?”; “What kind of cancer was it and when was it diagnosed?”. Participants who reported a diagnosis of colorectal cancer were classified as having CRC. This method follows the NHANES protocol for cancer identification, which relies on participant-reported medical history. To ensure the validity of the CRC diagnosis, individuals reporting cancers other than CRC (e.g., skin, respiratory, hematologic, head and neck, breast, musculoskeletal, and genitourinary cancers) were excluded from the analysis.

Diagnostic criteria for MetS

In this study, MetS was diagnosed using the diagnostic criteria established by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [24]. A participant was considered to have MetS if they met three to five of the following criteria: (1) central obesity, defined as a WC of ≥ 88 cm for women or ≥ 102 cm for men; (2) DM, indicated by a history of DM, fasting glucose levels ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels > 6.5%, or the use of medication for DM treatment; (3) hypertension, defined as blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg, a history of hypertension, or treatment with antihypertensive medication; and (4) low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), with levels < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women, or undergoing treatment for lipid abnormalities. (5) High triglyceride (TG) levels ≥ 150 mg/dL or receiving pharmacological treatment for elevated TG.

ABSI calculation

The ABSI is an innovative metric designed to accurately evaluate abdominal fat distribution independent of BMI, focusing on WC relative to height and weight. The formula for the ABSI, as established by Krakauer, is\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{WC}{{BMI}^{2/3}\times\:{height}^{1/2}}$$\end{document}, where WC represents waist circumference in meters, BMI represents body mass index (kg/m2), and height is the height in meters [18].

Collection of covariates

The covariates in this study were evaluated based on previous literature on CRC-related variables and clinical experience. First, general demographic information was collected, including gender, age, marital status, and race. Physical examination data included weight, height, and waist circumference. Laboratory test indicators included lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, serum creatinine concentration, and lipid marker levels. Information regarding specific medical conditions, such as smoking and drinking history, diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and certain types of cancer, was obtained through structured questionnaires. Dietary data were collected using the 24-hour dietary recall method, and the overall diet quality of participants was assessed using the Healthy Eating Index (HEI) [25]. Marital status was categorized into two groups: cohabitation (including marriage and living with a partner) and solitude (including widowed, divorced, separated, and unmarried individuals). Smoking status was assessed based on the number of cigarettes smoked in the past year and was categorized as current smokers, former smokers, or non-smokers. Alcohol consumption was evaluated based on the total grams of alcohol consumed in the past year and was categorized as current drinkers, former drinkers, or non-drinkers [26]. Physical activity was assessed using metabolic equivalents of task (MET), which quantify the intensity and duration of physical activity [27]. Participants reported their activity levels, which were converted into MET scores to reflect energy expenditure. Based on MET scores, participants were categorized as ideal (≥ 8000 MET-minutes/week), intermediate (600–7999 MET-minutes/week), or poor (< 600 MET-minutes/week). BMI was calculated as BMI = weight (kg) / height² (m²). BRI was calculated using the formula 364.2–365.5 × (1 - [WC(m)/2π]² / [0.5 × height(m)]²)½. WHtR was calculated as WHtR = WC(cm) / height(cm) [15]. All of the above information can be downloaded from the NHANES website under “Survey Data and Documentation.”

Statistical analysis

The NHANES database employs a complex sampling design. In this study, all analyses were meticulously weighted using the “Survey” package in R software (version 4.3.1) to account for the stratified and clustered sampling design inherent to the NHANES [23, 28]. To facilitate a thorough comparison of baseline characteristics among the study population, participants were stratified into quartiles based on their ABSI. Continuous variables were presented as weighted means ± standard deviations, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (weighted percentages). The Rao‒Scott chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare continuous variables. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were employed to examine the independent relationship between ABSI and the incidence of CRC. Multivariate models were adjusted for potential confounders, including demographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables. Subgroup analyses were used to investigate the relationship between the ABSI and CRC incidence across demographic characteristics (including age group and sex) and MetS components. The discriminatory power of the ABSI versus other body measurements in individually predicting CRC incidence in patients with MetS was assessed by receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance in all of the above analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of MetS patients grouped by ABSI quartiles

In this investigation, a cohort of 16,018 MetS participants was analyzed, representing an estimated 6,124,239 adults with MetS within the United States, according to weighted samples. The cohort exhibited a mean age of 51.8 years, with a nearly balanced sex distribution: 7,837 (50.3%) were male, and 8,181 (49.7%) were female. According to the quartiles of the ABSI, participants were categorized into four groups: Quartile 1 (Q1), with an ABSI < 0.080; Quartile 2 (Q2), ranging from 0.080 to 0.083; Quartile 3 (Q3), from 0.083 to 0.086; and Quartile 4 (Q4), with an ABSI > 0.086. The analysis revealed distinct characteristics across the ABSI quartiles (Table 1). In Quartile 1, characterized by the lowest ABSI, there was a notably lower incidence of CRC, particularly among females, solitary individuals, and those with higher education levels, with only 19 patients (0.4%). Quartile 2 participants displayed elevated BMI, albumin, and LDL-C levels, as well as reduced HDL-C and TG levels. Conversely, Quartile 4, denoting the highest ABSI, comprised older individuals, a greater proportion of smokers, and increased incidences of CVD and chronic kidney disease (CKD), as well as elevated WC, neutrophil count, fasting glucose, uric acid, and HDL-C levels. This group also presented lower BMI, weight, and albumin levels. Within the context of MetS components, Quartile 4 manifested higher rates of DM, obesity, and hypertension but lower rates of reduced HDL-C and elevated TGs. Remarkably, this quartile exhibited the highest prevalence of CRC, with 82 cases (1.6%).

Logistic regression analysis of the association between the ABSI and the risk of developing CRC among the MetS population

Logistic regression analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between an increased ABSI and the incidence of CRC among the MetS population (Table 2). Initial findings from the unadjusted model revealed a significant association, indicating that for each unit increase in ABSI, the risk of developing CRC increased by approximately 73.7% (OR: 1.737, 95% CI: 1.352 to 2.233, P < 0.001). Subsequent adjustments were made to account for a range of baseline conditions. Model 1 included adjustments for sex, ethnicity, marital status, educational attainment, smoking and drinking habits, HEI, physical activity, and the presence of CVD and CKD. Even with these adjustments, the ABSI continued to exhibit a significant association with the risk of developing CRC (OR: 1.469, 95% CI: 1.146 to 1.883, P = 0.003). Model 2 included further adjustments for pertinent laboratory indicators, maintaining the relationship between the ABSI and increased risk of developing CRC (OR: 1.433, 95% CI: 1.116 to 1.841; P = 0.005). An in-depth analysis concerning ABSI quartile groupings and their relation to CRC incidence was also undertaken. According to the refined Model 2, participants in the ABSI Q4 group demonstrated a significantly greater risk of developing CRC than those in the Q1 group (OR: 2.426, 95% CI: 1.010 to 5.823, P = 0.047). The trend across quartiles remained significant, with a P-value for the trend of 0.035.

Subsequent subgroup analyses were conducted to further elucidate the relationship between the ABSI and the risk of developing CRC within specific demographic segments and among varying components of MetS (Fig. 2). These analyses revealed a notably stronger association between a greater ABSI and increased CRC incidence among participants aged less than 60 years, with an OR of 2.612 (95% CI: 1.032 to 6.609, P = 0.043), and a significant interaction was observed (P = 0.024). In addition, the ABSI was associated with the development of CRC across sex and MetS components. Notably, males with an elevated ABSI had a greater risk of developing CRC (OR: 2.251, 95% CI: 1.726 to 2.935, P < 0.001), and there was an interaction effect (P = 0.005). Moreover, no association between the ABSI and the risk of developing CRC was detected in the hypertriglyceridemia population (OR: 1.281, 95% CI: 0.914 to 1.793, P = 0.149).

Diagnostic ability of the ABSI to predict CRC in a MetS population

To assess the predictive accuracy of various anthropometric measures for the risk of developing CRC in the MetS population, ROC curves were used to analyze the ability of each measure to predict CRC individually (Table 3). The area under the curve (AUC) values for each anthropometric measure from the ROC analysis were as follows: Height (AUC: 0.525, 95% CI: 0.480–0.570), weight (AUC: 0.507, 95% CI: 0.460–0.554), and BMI (AUC: 0.531, 95% CI: 0.485–0.577) demonstrated very low predictive value, barely better than chance discrimination. Waist circumference (AUC: 0.554, 95% CI: 0.508-0.600), BRI (AUC: 0.542, 95% CI: 0.499–0.585), and WHtR (AUC: 0.597, 95% CI: 0.550–0.643) showed moderate predictive value for CRC risk. Notably, the ABSI exhibited the highest predictive accuracy for the risk of developing CRC in the MetS population, with an AUC of 0.668 (95% CI: 0.624–0.713) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrated a substantial correlation between an elevated ABSI and an increased risk of developing CRC in the MetS population, highlighting the potential of the ABSI as a superior predictive marker compared to conventional indices such as BMI and WC.

The severity of metabolic abnormalities in MetS significantly influences an individual’s risk of developing cardiovascular diseases, gastrointestinal disorders, and other health issues [24]. Studies have shown that the visceral fat area is an important factor influencing CRC risk in MetS patients. The amount of visceral adipose tissue is associated with increased expression of inflammatory markers and decreased secretion of adiponectin (APN) [29]. A study of populations showed that those at risk as assessed by ABSI exhibited elevated levels of leukocytes, C-reactive protein, and neutrophils [30]. These inflammatory factors play a crucial role in the carcinogenic process, as they can induce DNA damage, catalyze cell proliferation, and inhibit apoptosis, thus creating a favorable environment for cancer development [31, 32]. Additionally, a study on the Korean population indicated that insulin resistance (IR) is associated with early-onset CRC in individuals younger than 50 years old [33]. IR is a hallmark of metabolic dysfunction, characterized by impaired response of muscle, fat, and liver cells to insulin, which leads to difficulties in glucose absorption from the bloodstream. Further studies have revealed that individuals in the high ABSI risk group had elevated fasting glucose levels and a higher prevalence of diabetes and obesity-related metabolic disorders [34]. This impaired insulin sensitivity results in elevated insulin levels, which activate the insulin-like growth factor (IGF) system. IGF plays a crucial role in cell growth and differentiation and can also inhibit apoptosis, thereby creating a favorable condition for the survival and proliferation of cancer cells [35, 36].

Unlike traditional measures of obesity, which do not differentiate between types of fat distribution, the ABSI specifically focuses on abdominal obesity, which is more closely associated with MetS and its related metabolic derangements [20, 37]. Visceral fat, which the ABSI indirectly quantifies through WC adjusted for BMI and height, is metabolically active and secretes numerous substances that contribute to the mechanisms described above, linking MetS to CRC. Several studies have similarly supported our inference. A comprehensive meta-analysis of nineteen prospective cohort studies underscored that abdominal obesity—quantified through WC and the waist-to-hip ratio (WHR)—bears a significant correlation with the development of CRC [38]. In American adults, a correlation was found between levels of visceral adiposity, as indexed by BRI, and the risk of developing CRC [5]. In addition, in a prospective cohort study of a United Kingdom population, ABSI was found to be significantly associated with five cancers, including CRC [22]. ABSI was initially used as a predictor of mortality in populations, and as the study progressed, it was found that ABSI did indeed represent abdominal adiposity well [39]. Currently there are no studies evaluating the association of ABSI with CRC in obese or MetS populations. Previous studies in Chinese and Korean populations have shown that abdominal obesity in patients with MetS is associated with an increased risk of developing CRC [40, 41]. Despite the prevalent use of traditional metrics such as WC and BMI to evaluate abdominal obesity in previous studies, our findings demonstrate the superior ability of the ABSI to predict the risk of developing CRC in the MetS population. The advancement of the ABSI over previous metrics illustrates its unique role in assessing the critical dimension of abdominal obesity, thereby improving the accuracy of CRC risk assessment in the MetS population.

This study also demonstrated that the association between ABSI and CRC risk was more pronounced in MetS patients under the age of 60. Several observational studies have indicated that obesity and MetS are associated with an increased risk of early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC) in populations from China [42], the United States [43], South Korea [33], and Germany [44]. However, the exact pathogenesis of EOCRC remains unclear. Many scholars currently believe that its development is related to changes in dietary habits (e.g., high-fat, high-oil diets), gut microbiota dysbiosis, elevated inflammation levels due to obesity, and dysregulation of obesity-related hormones [45]. Our study also revealed an interaction between ABSI and sex, with a higher risk observed in males. Previous studies have confirmed that males indeed have a higher risk of obesity-related cancers (ORC) compared to females. Among MetS patients, males tend to exhibit more severe metabolic disorders and insulin resistance than females [46]. Notably, this study found that abdominal obesity was also associated with CRC in females. A meta-analysis showed that MetS may increase CRC-related mortality in males but not in females [47]. Additionally, a study on American women indicated that BMI and the presence of MetS were not associated with CRC risk, while waist circumference showed a strong positive correlation [48]. This finding further supports our hypothesis that height and weight alone are insufficient to accurately differentiate visceral fat. A study from the UK Biobank further corroborated our results, showing that central obesity, as defined by waist circumference, increases the risk of endometrial cancer and postmenopausal breast cancer in metabolically healthy individuals [49].

In this study, data from the NHANES, a comprehensive, nationally representative sample, were utilized, increasing the generalizability of the results to the more extensive American adult MetS population. The ability of the ABSI to specifically quantify abdominal obesity, which is considered an important component of MetS and a known risk factor for CRC, sets it apart from traditional anthropometric methods. No research has investigated the relationship between the ABSI and the risk of developing CRC among the MetS population, which is a strength of this study. However, the study has several limitations. Firstly, as CRC is often clinically asymptomatic in its early stages, there is a possibility of misclassification, where individuals may be incorrectly diagnosed as having or not having CRC. This potential for misdiagnosis could introduce bias into the results. Additionally, the cross-sectional design, while effective in identifying associations, does not allow for causal inferences. Another limitation is the reliance on self-reported data for certain variables, such as smoking and drinking habits, which may introduce reporting bias. Furthermore, NHANES lacks information on several important risk factors for CRC, including family history of CRC, the presence of polyps, and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which could limit the comprehensiveness of the analysis. Lastly, while the American population selected for this study provides valuable insights, further research is needed to determine whether these findings can be generalized to populations in other regions.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates a positive and significant association between the ABSI and the risk of developing CRC in the MetS population, emphasizing the superior predictive value of the ABSI compared to traditional anthropometric measures. This novel insight suggests that the ABSI has potential as a critical tool for early CRC risk identification among individuals with MetS, suggesting that it could be integrated into preventive strategies to effectively mitigate the risk of developing CRC.
