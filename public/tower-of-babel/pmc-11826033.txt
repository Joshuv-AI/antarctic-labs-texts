Multi‐Society Guideline on Cytoreductive Surgery and HIPEC: Methodology and Overview of the Results
Authors: Michela Cinquini, Shigeki Kusamura, Antonino Carmelo Tralongo, Veronica Andrea Fittipaldo, M Deraco, Marta Monteforte
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo provide evidence‐based guidelines about Peritoneal Surface Malignancy (PSM) treatment for the Peritoneal Surfaces Oncology Group International. The GRADE ADOLOPMENT method was used to adapt recommendations from the Italian Medical Oncology Association Guideline on PSM. Seven scientific society were involved to form five guideline panels composed of 28 medical professionals from 14 countries. Panelists prioritized 7 relevant questions. A knowledge synthesis team updated evidence and summarized information. The panel agreed on seven recommendations.Keywords: GRADE adolopment, guideline, peritoneal malignancy

Background

Over the past three decades, randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the benefits of CRS and HIPEC in specific peritoneal surface malignancy (PSM) settings: as secondary cytoreduction following neoadjuvant systemic chemotherapy in advanced epithelial ovarian cancer [1], after cytoreduction in platinum‐sensitive epithelial ovarian cancer [2], and as adjuvant therapy following the resection of T4 primary colorectal cancer to prevent peritoneal metastasis [3]. Additionally, observational studies have consistently reported favorable survival outcomes with CRS and HIPEC in rare peritoneal malignancies [4, 5], leading the PSOGI to recommend these procedures as the treatment of choice in its guidelines for malignant peritoneal mesothelioma and pseudomyxoma peritonei [6, 7].

Although CRS and HIPEC are recognized as treatment options in various international guidelines [8, 9, 10] for different PSM subsets, its uniform acceptance within the medical community still needs to catch up. Therefore, the Peritonel Surface Oncology Group International launched the project of a multisocietal consensus to extend the list of panelists aiming to achieve a broader agreement by the multitude of professionals taking part in the management of PSMs. Several international societies took part in the consensus process to increase the likelihood of uniform implementation across the oncologic centers across the globe.

Therefore, the purpose of the project was to develop a guideline with evidence‐based recommendations to help surgeons, oncologists, and other clinicians in the decision making to manage PSMs.

Methods

Organization and Oversight

The project has been organized and coordinated by PSOGI and was a collaboration with other Scientific Societies: Europen Society of Surgical Oncology, European Reference Network for Rare Adult Solid Cancers, Society of Surgical Oncology, International Mesothelioma Interest Group, International Society for the Study of Pleura and Peritoneum, and International Gastric Cancer Association.

PSOGI appointed and convened the guideline panel, applied conflict‐of‐interest policies, and developed agreements with the external reviewers for approval, and dissemination of the guideline.

Under a paid agreement with PSOGI, an evidence review team (ERT) based in Milan at the Mario Negri Institute for Pharmacological Research IRCCS (IRFMN‐IRCCS) was involved in the project. ERT was led by two senior and one junior methodologists.

Panel Selection

Each partner society nominated one individual to serve on the guideline panel. This nomination was based on content expertize and conflict‐of‐interest status. PSOGI staff supported panel appointments and coordinated meetings but had no role in choosing the guideline questions for adaptation or determining the recommendations. Staff and members of the partner societies also did not have any such role. PSOGI vetted all nominated individuals, including for conflicts of interest, and formed the panel to include two methodologists, oncologists, and surgeons. To keep the same level of participation for all of the societies, there was no panel chair. The work of the panel was facilitated by one methodologist, who led all sessions and discussions but did not participate in decisions regarding the direction or strength of the recommendations.

Guideline Funding and Management of Conflicts of Interest

This adapted guideline was fully funded by PSOGI, a nonprofit medical specialty society that represents oncologists and surgeons. PSOGI did not receive any industry funding to underwrite its support of panel and guideline development.

Members of the guideline panel received travel reimbursement for attendance at in‐person meetings in Venice in October 2023 but received no other payments.

Conflicts of interest of all participants were managed according to recommendations of the Guidelines International Network [11]. Participants disclosed all the financial and nonfinancial interests relevant to the guideline topic.

Preliminar Guidelines Search and Grade‐Adolpment

An initial literature search was conducted to identify existing Guidelines on the topic. The most recent Guideline was the Guideline on peritoneal malignancies developed by the Italian Association of Medical Oncology (AIOM) updated to 2022.

The model we used in this guideline, GRADE ADOLOPMENT approach [12] allowed us to generate recommendations that can be tailored for an international setting.

Methodologists, who are free from conflict of interest, conducted systematic reviews of existing evidence and facilitated the guideline panel in using the GRADE ADOLOPMENT approach.

Selecting Questions for Adaptation

PSOGI, together with the panel, selected five clinical questions reported in the Guideline on peritoneal malignancies developed by the AIOM.

Two other clinical question were developed ex‐novo following the GRADE approach [13].

Using Existing Evidence Reviews and Inclusion of Local Data

The strengths of the GRADE ADOLOPMENT approach reside in the use of existing Evidence to Decision (EtD) Frameworks to develop locally relevant recommendations. The detailed methods are described elsewhere [14]. In short, the core of the procedure is to evaluate the complete EtD framework, rather than only the recommendation. If the panel considers that the EtD framework is appropriate to the context in which the recommendation was applied, it may decide to adopt the recommendation without change. Another option is that panelists believe that most of the framework can be reused, but important additional data must be added. In this case, the framework is updated, and an adapted recommendation is developed. Finally, the last option is that panelists believe that the original EtD framework needs major modifications. In this case, the process is conceptually equivalent to developing a new recommendation using GRADE methods. It is important to note that the ADOLOPMENT method is applied at the recommendation level, not to the entire guideline. Thus, it is possible that adoption, adaptation, and development coexist in a particular guideline.

In this case, the methods team and the guideline panel judged that the systematic reviews about health effects from the original guidelines could be reused, but important data about resource use, accessibility, feasibility, and impact on health equity needed to be added to reflect the context in which the present guideline was implemented. Therefore, the methods team and the guideline panel conducted an adaptation of the original guidelines.

For each of the selected questions, the methods team updated the electronic search of randomized trials and observational studies of the original guidelines (using the same search strategies) and conduct a comprehensive search of the evidence about patient values and preferences, resource use, accessibility, feasibility, and impact on health equity. The results of the electronic and hand searches were combined and reviewed independently by the two methodologists. The evidence selection and data extraction were done independently and in duplicate. Disagreements were recorded and resolved by consensus. Finally, guideline panelists were asked to provide any information that they consider relevant.

The data from the original guidelines were summarized by the methods team on the EtD framework using the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool (www.gradepro.org, McMaster University and Evidence Prime Inc., Kraków, Poland).

The updated EtD frameworks circulated among guideline panelists before the meeting to develop recommendations.

The same materials were provided for the clinical questions developed ex‐novo.

Development of Recommendations

During several web‐meeting, panel members discussed evidence summarized in the EtD frameworks and formulated the recommendations based on the evidence. The panelists made judgments on every relevant domain included in the EtDs and then define the direction and strength of every recommendation.

The direction of the recommendation was decided by a simple majority, whereas an 80% majority was required to issue a strong recommendation.

Document Review

Draft recommendations were reviewed by all panel members, revised, and sent for external review by stakeholders.

Document Reporting

The AGREE‐reporting checklist was followed to guide the reporting of recommendations [15].

Results

The 7 involved scientific society formed 5 guideline panels composed of 28 medical professionals from 14 countries.

The panel confirmed the indication of CRS and HIPEC as a conditional recommendation for metachronous peritoneal metastasis from colorectal and gastric cancer and as a strong recommendation for intervention in pseudomyxoma peritonei, peritoneal mesothelioma, and advanced epithelial ovarian cancer after neoadjuvant chemotherapy. However, the panel voted for either the intervention or comparison for the prevention of peritoneal metastasis from high‐risk colorectal cancer and platinum sensitive epithelial ovarian cancer recurrence (Table 1).

Discussion

The recommendations were developed using the GRADE methodology, which offers several advantages for creating clinical guidelines compared to other approaches. GRADE provides a transparent and systematic framework for evaluating the quality of evidence, facilitating the distinction between strong and weak recommendations. Moreover, GRADE emphasizes not only the balance between benefits and harms of the interventions but also patient values and preferences, ensuring that the guidelines are patient‐centered. This methodology also allows for the integration of various types of evidence, including randomized trials and observational studies, thereby enhancing the applicability of the recommendations [13].

In contrast to previous PSOGI agreements [6, 7, 16], the current decision regarding the direction and strength of recommendations extended beyond simply weighing positive and negative outcomes. This iteration systematically considered other critical dimensions, often overlooked in other guideline development approaches, such as the importance of the question, values and preferences, acceptance, equity, and feasibility [12].

This consensus‐building process was significantly streamlined by leveraging the prior work conducted by the AIOM, which developed a guideline for the management of PSMs. This guideline was adopted as a reference document by the Italian Ministry of Health [17]. By utilizing the GRADE ADOLOPMENT approach, which facilitates the adaptation of existing guidelines, many of the PICO questions were re‐proposed rather than developed from scratch. This method allows for the efficient adaptation of previously validated recommendations to new contexts, ensuring consistency and reducing the need for extensive re‐evaluation while maintaining the rigor and relevance of the guidelines.

The current agreement on CRS and HIPEC in managing peritoneal surface malignancies (PSMs) provides a more standardized treatment framework. This consensus integrates evidence from both trials and observational studies while adapting existing guidelines. The strong recommendations for specific interventions, particularly in advanced ovarian cancer, pseudomyxoma peritonei, and malignant peritoneal mesothelioma, offer clear clinical guidance, which is likely to lead to more uniform practices and improved patient outcomes.

While this consensus represents a significant advancement in the management of PSMs, several objectives remain to be pursued in the future to enhance its impact. Key areas for improvement include fostering participation from major oncology societies, such as the European Society for Medical Oncology and the American Society of Clinical Oncology, which, despite formal invitations, did not participate in the current process. Additionally, the recommendations were often based on very low overall certainty of evidence for most clinical questions, highlighting the need for more clinical trials to establish a more consistent body of evidence in Peritoneal Surface Oncology. Although the expert panel considered patient values and preferences, the inclusion of patient representatives would have further strengthened this dimension by providing a more direct and trustworthy perspective on patient experiences and needs. Moreover, incorporating cost‐effectiveness analyses will be crucial for better integrating these recommendations into diverse healthcare systems, particularly in resource‐limited settings. As the field evolves, ongoing research, updates, and broader engagement will be necessary to refine and strengthen these guidelines.

Conclusion

In summary, this effort marks a pivotal step forward, offering robust, evidence‐based recommendations. Addressing the identified challenges, particularly in stakeholder inclusion and cost‐effectiveness considerations, will further enhance the guidelines' impact and improve outcomes for patients with PSMs.
