CXCL8-dependent recruitment of neutrophils by the tumor microenvironment drives poor prognosis in glioblastoma patients
Authors: Jiajun Wang, Chao Wang, Zhiming Sun, Antian Zhang, Zihui Niu, Kang Zhang, Shuai Wang, Chao Zhang, Xingjie Shi, Dong Yu, Shiqiang Hou, Ning Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma remains a therapeutic challenge due to its immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and resistance to immunotherapy. This study investigates the role of CXCL8 signaling in recruiting immunosuppressive neutrophils and driving poor prognosis in glioblastoma. Analysis of TCGA data revealed significant upregulation of CXCL8 in glioblastoma tissues, correlating with reduced survival and neutrophil chemotaxis pathway activation. We identified a distinct low-density neutrophil subpopulation (L-NEUs) in GBM patient blood, characterized by immature markers (CD16low, CD13low, CD10low) and CXCL8-dependent migration. Integrative bioinformatics identified RNASE2 and THBD as L-NEU-specific markers, both upregulated in GBM and associated with poor prognosis. Functional studies demonstrated that CXCL8 overexpression in macrophages enhanced L-NEU recruitment. RNASE2 (secretory) and THBD (transmembrane) emerged as dual biomarkers and therapeutic targets, with pan-cancer survival analysis linking their overexpression to adverse outcomes in glioblastoma, renal carcinoma, and others. These findings define CXCL8-driven neutrophil recruitment as a key immunosuppressive mechanism in glioblastoma and propose RNASE2/THBD-targeted strategies to improve immunotherapy efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14698-5.Keywords: Glioblastoma, CXCL8, Neutrophils, Tumor microenvironment, RNASE2, THBD

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), the most aggressive primary brain tumor, is characterized by a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) dominated by myeloid cells, exhausted T cells, and aberrant angiogenesis [1]. Despite advances in immunotherapy, clinical outcomes remain dismal, with median survival rarely exceeding 15 months [1, 2]. Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, particularly neutrophils, in driving immunosuppression and poor prognosis [3]. Elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios (NLRs) and intratumoral neutrophil accumulation are strongly correlated with resistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), accelerated tumor progression, and reduced survival in GBM patients [4–6]. These neutrophils, often polarized toward a pro-tumor (N2) phenotype, facilitate immune evasion by suppressing cytotoxic T cell activity, promoting angiogenesis, and remodeling the extracellular matrix [7, 8]. However, the molecular mechanisms governing neutrophil recruitment and their functional reprogramming within the GBM TME remain poorly defined.

Recent work by Liu et al. provides critical insights into the role of interleukin-8 (IL-8/CXCL8) in orchestrating myeloid-driven immunosuppression in glioma [9]. Their study demonstrates that CXCL8, produced by tumor cells, myeloid cells, and a unique subset of innate-like CD4+ T cells (termed “Th8”), acts as a central regulator of the immunosuppressive TME [10]. Using an CXCL8-humanized mouse model, the authors show that CXCL8 signaling through CXCR1/2 receptors drives myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) infiltration, angiogenesis, and T cell exhaustion, thereby blunting the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy [9]. Notably, CXCL8 blockade or CXCR1/2 inhibition disrupts MDSC recruitment, reprograms tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward an anti-angiogenic phenotype, and synergizes with ICB to restore antitumor immunity. These findings position the CXCL8-CXCR1/2 axis as a pivotal therapeutic target for overcoming immunosuppression in glioma.

Building on this foundational work, our study aims to dissect the role of CXCL8 signaling in neutrophil-driven immunosuppression and therapeutic resistance in GBM. Our analysis based on the GEPIA platform (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html) revealed significantly elevated CXCL8 expression in GBM tumor tissues, with high CXCL8 levels strongly correlating with poor patient prognosis. CXCL8-associated gene sets were found to regulate neutrophil chemotaxis pathways. In peripheral blood from GBM patients, we identified a distinct population of low-density, large-volume, and low-granularity neutrophils exhibiting immature characteristics, which were recruited in response to CXCL8 signaling. Intersection analysis between the gene signatures of these low-density neutrophils and CXCL8-related gene sets identified two conserved markers, RNASE2 (Ribonuclease A2) and THBD (Thrombomodulin). RNASE2+ and THBD+ neutrophils are significantly increased in GBM tumor tissues. These findings suggest that RNASE2 and THBD may serve as biomarkers for monitoring GBM progression and represent potential therapeutic targets. This work not only extends the findings of Liu et al. but also provides a mechanistic framework for targeting neutrophil plasticity in GBM immunotherapy.

Material and method

Human samples

The clinical specimens utilized in this research were sourced from Anhui Medical University Affiliated Chuzhou Hospital. The use of clinical samples was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chuzhou Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, and informed consent was secured from all participants involved in the study. The collection and utilization of clinical samples were conducted in rigorous compliance with the stipulated guidelines.

Flow cytometry

Peripheral blood samples from glioblastoma patients were processed using Ficoll-Paque PLUS density gradient centrifugation (Cytiva, 17144003) at 400g for 30 min at room temperature to isolate peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs). Cells were stained with the following anti-human monoclonal antibodies: BV605-conjugated CD45 (HI30, BioLegend, 304036), PE-conjugated CD19 (HIB19, BD Biosciences, 555413), PerCP-Cy5.5-conjugated CD3 (UCHT1, BioLegend, 300430), APC-conjugated CD66b (G10F5, BioLegend, 305112), BUV395-conjugated CD16 (3G8, BD Biosciences, 563785), FITC-conjugated CD13 (WM15, BD Biosciences, 555394), PE-Cy7-conjugated CD10 (HI10a, BioLegend, 312214), and PECF594-conjugated CD11b (ICRF44, BD Biosciences, 562682). After 30 min incubation at 4 °C in the dark, cells were washed twice with PBS containing 2% FBS and analyzed on an Agilent NovoCyte Advanteon flow cytometer. Compensation was performed using single-stained UltraComp eBeads (Thermo Fisher, 01-2222-42). Data were processed using FlowJo v10.9 software (BD Biosciences) with sequential gating strategies to identify neutrophil subsets based on CD45+CD3−CD19−CD66b+ expression and exclusion of lymphoid (CD3+CD19+) populations.

Transwell assay

Low-density neutrophils (L-NEUs) were isolated from glioblastoma patient peripheral blood via Ficoll density gradient centrifugation (400g, 30 min) and seeded into the upper chamber of a 5-µm pore Transwell system (Corning, 3421) at 1 × 106 cells/chamber. The lower chamber contained THP-1 macrophages transfected with CXCL8-overexpressing plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher). After 8-hour incubation in RPMI-1640 medium (10% FBS, 37 °C, 5% CO2), migrated L-NEUs in the lower chamber were collected, and quantified by flow cytometry (Agilent NovoCyte).

Differentially expressed gene acquisition

Differentially expressed genes were defined using |log2(fold change)| >0.585 (corresponding to linear fold change > 1.5) and unadjusted P < 0.05 calculated through DESeq2’s Wald test, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction disabled during initial screening as specified in our analytical workflow.

Gene ontology (GO) pathway analysis

GO enrichment analysis was performed using the OE Cloud Platform (https://cloud.oebiotech.com). Differentially expressed genes were analyzed under biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC) categories with Homo sapiens GO database. Significant terms were filtered by hypergeometric test (p < 0.05, FDR < 0.1).

STRING interaction network analysis

Protein-protein interaction networks were constructed using STRING Cloud Platform (https://string-db.org) with differentially expressed genes. Homo sapiens database (v12.0) was selected, retaining interactions with confidence scores > 0.7. Functional enrichment was analyzed via KEGG and GO terms (FDR < 0.05).

TCGA database analysis

We conducted comprehensive analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data using the GEPIA (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis) suite, including GEPIA (2017, http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html), GEPIA2 (2019, http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index), and GEPIA2021 (http://gepia2021.cancer-pku.cn/index.html) modules [11]. The vertical axis (nTPM) represents normalized transcripts per million, a standardized metric for gene expression levels enabling cross-sample comparisons. In the figures: “T” denotes GBM tumor tissues; “N” represents normal control data (generated by matching TCGA normal and GTEx data). For differential expression analysis, tumor tissues (n = 163 GBM samples) were compared against normal controls (GTEx database, n = 207 brain tissue samples) using log2 fold change (|FC|>2) and statistical significance thresholds (p < 0.01, FDR < 0.05). The “Expression DIY” module generated pan-cancer heatmaps to visualize gene expression patterns across 33 malignancies.

Survival analysis was performed using the “Survival Analysis” module with Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests. Patients were stratified into high/low expression groups by median cutoff, with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals calculated. The “Correlation Analysis” module evaluated gene–gene interactions via Pearson correlation (R > 0.3, p < 0.001).

GEPIA2021 introduced single-cell sequencing integration, allowing cell-type-specific expression validation in TME subsets. All analyses utilized UCSC Xena-processed RNA-seq data (FPKM normalized). Visualization outputs included volcano plots for differential genes and interactive survival maps for multi-omics correlation. Data reproducibility was confirmed through three independent analytical pipelines.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad) and the OE Cloud Platform (https://cloud.oebiotech.com). Bar graphs (mean ± SD) were generated in Prism for group comparisons, with significance determined by Student’s t-test. Heatmaps and Venn diagrams were constructed via the OE Cloud “Advanced Visualization” module, using log2-transformed fold-change values and FDR-adjusted p-values. For Venn analysis, gene lists were filtered by |log2FC| >1 and p < 0.05. Survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank test with hazard ratios calculated through Cox proportional hazards models. Statistical significance thresholds were defined as follows: p < 0.05 (*), < 0.01 (**), < 0.001 (***), and < 0.0001 (****).

Results

CXCL8 signaling as a significant predictor of poor prognosis in glioblastoma patients

CXCL8 (Interleukin-8) is a pro-inflammatory chemokine belonging to the CXC family, primarily known for its role in recruiting and activating neutrophils during inflammatory responses [12, 13]. It binds to receptors CXCR1 and CXCR2, driving angiogenesis, immune cell infiltration, and tumor progression in cancers like glioblastoma [14]. The work of Liu et al. further revealed elevated CXCL8 levels in the GBM TME, which recruit MDSCs to exacerbate immunosuppression and suppress antitumor immunity [9]. MDSCs are a heterogeneous population classified into monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) and polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) [15, 16]. PMN-MDSCs constitute the predominant MDSC subpopulation in humans, outnumbering M-MDSCs [17–19]. Despite sharing morphological and phenotypic similarities with normal neutrophils, PMN-MDSCs exhibit distinct immunosuppressive functions [20]. The CXCL8-CXCR1/2 axis is a canonical pathway for neutrophil recruitment, and previous studies have identified PMN-MDSC markers in various cancers, such as CD13, CD36, and GPR84 [21–23]. However, the interplay between CXCL8 signaling and neutrophils in the GBM TME remains poorly understood.

We first analyzed TCGA database via the GEPIA platform to assess CXCL8 expression and its prognostic significance in GBM. Results demonstrated significantly higher CXCL8 levels in GBM tissues compared to normal brain tissues (Fig. 1A), with elevated CXCL8 expression strongly correlating with poor patient survival (Fig. 1B). We also compared other chemokines capable of mobilizing neutrophil migration [24] (Figure S1A, B). The results demonstrated that CXCL2/3/8 are highly expressed in GBM tumors (Figure S1C), while overexpression of CXCL1/3/5/8 correlates with poor prognosis in GBM patients (Figure S1D). Furthermore, CXCL8 exhibits the highest baseline expression level among these chemokines in GBM tissues (Figure S1E). Therefore, we focus on the CXCL8 signaling in GBM patients to investigate the mobilization mechanisms of immunosuppressive neutrophil subsets. The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database revealed that CXCL8 is predominantly secreted in the cerebral cortex of healthy donors (Fig. 1C). We further screened top 50 CXCL8-associated genes in GBM patients (Table S1), and enrichment analysis highlighted their roles in regulating inflammatory responses and neutrophil chemotaxis (Fig. 1D), consistent with prior functional studies of CXCL8. STRING interaction network analysis positioned CXCL8 as a central hub in these regulatory pathways (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, these functionally associated genes exhibited robust positive correlations with CXCL8 expression (Figure S2), indicating their coordinated involvement in CXCL8-driven processes such as neutrophil chemotaxis, angiogenesis, and inflammatory signaling. Given the link between CXCL8-related genes and neutrophil recruitment, we analyzed neutrophil infiltration in GBM using the GEPIA 2021 platform. Data showed a significant increase in tumor-infiltrating neutrophils in GBM patients (Fig. 1F), with high neutrophil infiltration correlating with adverse prognosis (Fig. 1G). Thus, TCGA-based findings indicate that upregulated CXCL8 in GBM drives neutrophil migration into the TME, worsening patient outcomes.

Low-density, large-volume, and low-granularity neutrophil subpopulations emerge in the peripheral blood of glioblastoma patients

Neutrophils, the largest immune cell subpopulation in humans, primarily circulate in the bloodstream [25, 26]. We subsequently analyzed peripheral blood from GBM patients to investigate the emergence of novel neutrophil subpopulations. Since polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) exhibit higher density compared to other immune cells, Ficoll-based density gradient centrifugation of whole blood conventionally separates these cells into the high-density fraction, while peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) remain in the low-density layer (Fig. 2A). Flow cytometric analysis of GBM patient blood revealed an aberrant neutrophil distribution (Figure S3A): the low-density PBMC layer in healthy donors contained negligible neutrophils (Fig. 2B), whereas GBM patients displayed a substantial neutrophil population within this compartment (Fig. 2B). Strikingly, high-density neutrophils from GBM patients phenotypically aligned with those from healthy donors, whereas low-density neutrophils in GBM formed a distinct cluster, diverging from both healthy donor neutrophils and high-density GBM neutrophils (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, our analysis of neutrophils before and after Ficoll density gradient centrifugation revealed a minor subpopulation of L-NEUs characterized by CD11bhiCD66blo expression, with over 30% being LOX1 positive (Figure S3B, C). This closely aligns with the 35.3% LOX1+ L-NEUs reported by Condamine et al. [27]. Critically, these subsets significantly outnumbered blood eosinophils (Figure S3D, E), confirming that L-NEUs in GBM patients represent a distinct cell population rather than eosinophil contaminants. Therefore, the low-density neutrophils isolated from GBM patients in our study likewise represent an immunosuppressive specialized subpopulation.

Forward Scatter (FSC) and Side Scatter (SSC) are key parameters in flow cytometry [28, 29]. FSC measures cell size via forward-angle light scattering, while SSC reflects granularity/internal complexity through side-angle scattering. This dual-parameter analysis underpins cell population identification, functional assessment, and sorting, serving as a cornerstone for experimental and clinical workflows. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that low-density neutrophils (L-NEU) in the peripheral blood of GBM patients exhibited significantly higher FSC and lower SSC values compared to their high-density neutrophil (H-NEU) counterparts (Fig. 2C, D). Notably, H-NEU from GBM patients showed no significant differences in FSC/SSC profiles relative to healthy donor neutrophils (Fig. 2C, D). These findings indicate that GBM-associated L-NEU represent a morphologically distinct subpopulation characterized by enlarged cell volume and reduced granularity, likely reflecting their pathological reprogramming within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

Immature phenotype and CXCL8-mediated recruitment of low-density neutrophils in glioblastoma patients

To further characterize L-NEU in GBM patients, flow cytometric analysis was performed on peripheral blood samples. Results demonstrated that L-NEU exhibited significantly reduced expression of neutrophil maturation markers [30, 31] including CD16, CD13, and CD10, compared to their H-NEU counterparts (Fig. 3A, B). This attenuated expression profile aligns with an immature phenotypic state, reminiscent of PMN-MDSCs, which are known to accumulate in immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments. Notably, the cellular origin of MDSCs remains a subject of ongoing academic debate. Recent studies suggested that MDSCs represent developing immune cells derived from bone marrow precursors that migrate to peripheral circulation in cancer patients [32]. Conversely, another perspective suggests these cells constitute a unique reprogrammed myeloid subpopulation generated within the bone marrow, spleen, or tumor microenvironment of affected individuals [33]. Our experimental findings provide evidence that regardless of their developmental origin, the pathologically expanded neutrophil population (PMN-MDSCs) observed in GBM patients exhibits a distinct immature phenotype.

Given that CXCR1/2 expression progressively upregulates with maturation in both human and mouse neutrophils (Figure S4A, B), L-NEUs would theoretically lack responsiveness to CXCL8-mediated recruitment. This hypothesis directly prompted our Transwell experiments to definitively test whether L-NEUs retain CXCL8 chemotactic responsiveness. We further evaluated the chemotactic response of L-NEUs from GBM patients to CXCL8 signaling using a Transwell migration assay. In this experimental setup, THP-1 macrophages transfected with CXCL8-overexpressing plasmids were seeded in the lower chamber, while L-NEUs isolated from peripheral blood of GBM patients (1 × 106 cells/chamber) were placed in the upper chamber (Fig. 3C). After 8 h of co-culture, the CXCL8-transfected group showed significantly higher numbers of migrated L-NEUs in the lower chamber compared to the control group (Fig. 3D), demonstrating that L-NEUs from GBM patients retain responsiveness to CXCL8-mediated chemotaxis. Furthermore, we directly examined the recruitment of L-NEUs by glioblastoma. We collected supernatant from U87 MG cell cultures and placed it in the lower chamber of the Transwell system as a chemoattractant (Figure S4C). Compared to the PBS control, the tumor-derived supernatant (TDS) significantly promoted the migration of L-NEUs (Figure S4D). Given that glioblastoma exhibits virtually no expression of CXCL1/2/3/5/6/7 (Figure S1E), this indicates that the CXCL8-mediated recruitment of L-NEUs also occurs within the TME of GBM patients.

RNASE2 and THBD serve as specific markers for CXCL8 signaling-associated low-density neutrophils in cancer patients

To systematically identify characteristic markers of L-NEUs in cancer patients, we conducted integrative bioinformatics analysis on multiple datasets from the GEO database (GSE79404), including bulk RNA sequencing data of peripheral blood neutrophils from healthy donors, as well as H-NEUs and L-NEUs from cancer patients [27]. The heatmap demonstrates 196 characteristic genes specifically associated with L-NEUs (Fig. 4A). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these signature genes were significantly enriched in cytoplasmic transport-related pathways (e.g., cytosolic ribosome and cytoplasmic translation) (Fig. 4B).

Building on the demonstrated responsiveness of L-NEUs to CXCL8-mediated chemotaxis, we performed intersection analysis between CXCL8 pathway-related gene sets and LDN signature gene sets. Venn diagram analysis identified MAP3K8, RNASE2, and THBD as core overlapping genes (Fig. 4C). TCGA data analysis demonstrated significant positive correlations between these genes and CXCL8 expression (Fig. 4D), with concomitant overexpression in GBM tissues (Fig. 4E). Notably, survival analysis of the TCGA cohort revealed that GBM patients with high MAP3K8 expression showed no significant difference in overall survival (OS) compared to controls (Fig. 4F), whereas those expressing elevated RNASE2 or THBD exhibited markedly reduced OS. These findings establish RNASE2 and THBD not only as specific markers for CXCL8-associated L-NEUs, but also as independent prognostic indicators for GBM patients. Moreover, the HPA database confirms that RNASE2 and THBD are not expressed in neutrophils under physiological conditions (Figure S5A, B). Through qPCR validation, we further demonstrated that L-NEUs significantly upregulate RNASE2 and THBD compared to H-NEUs (Figure S5C). Therefore, the expression of RNASE2 and THBD in peripheral blood neutrophils of GBM patients likely indicates their reprogramming within the tumor microenvironment.

RNASE2+/THBD+ neutrophils as potential therapeutic targets for glioblastoma

We additionally analyzed GBM tumor tissues, with flow cytometry revealing that about 20% of cells were infiltrating neutrophils (Figure S6A), and about 6% of these neutrophils were LOX1+ (Figure S6B). These findings imply that L-NEUs likely infiltrate GBM tissues and may exert pro-tumor functions. Critically, RNASE2 and THBD show promise as novel biomarkers for identifying tumor-infiltrating L-NEUs in GBM, potentially reflecting disease progression and prognostic relevance. Analysis via the GEPIA 2021 platform revealed a significant increase in the proportion of RNASE2+ and THBD+ neutrophils in GBM patients (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). A strong positive correlation was observed between RNASE2 and THBD expression levels in GBM tissues (R = 0.73, P-value < 0.0001) (Fig. 5B). Pan-cancer analysis demonstrated upregulated expression trends of both genes across most tumor types, including GBM (Fig. 5C, S7). Survival heatmaps indicated that high expression of RNASE2 and THBD correlated with poor prognosis in GBM and low-grade glioma (LGG) patients (Fig. 5D). Specifically, elevated THBD expression was associated with adverse outcomes in mesothelioma (MESO) and thyroid carcinoma (THCA) (Fig. 5D), while high RNASE2 expression predicted poor prognosis in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (KIRC), hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) (Fig. 5D). Functionally, RNASE2 acts as a secretory factor, whereas THBD (CD141) is a cell surface marker. Both biomarkers hold potential for pathological monitoring in GBM, with THBD representing a therapeutic target for eliminating tumor microenvironment-derived L-NEUs due to its transmembrane properties.

Discussion

This study elucidates the pivotal role of CXCL8 (IL-8) in orchestrating immunosuppressive neutrophil recruitment within the GBM microenvironment, thereby driving tumor progression and resistance to immunotherapy. By integrating multi-omics analyses, functional assays, and clinical validation, we identified a distinct low-density neutrophil subpopulation (L-NEUs) characterized by immature markers (CD16low, CD13low, CD10low) and CXCL8-dependent chemotaxis. Furthermore, RNASE2 and THBD emerged as dual biomarkers of these pathogenic neutrophils, with their overexpression correlating with poor prognosis in GBM and other malignancies. These findings expand our understanding of myeloid-driven immunosuppression in GBM and provide actionable targets for therapeutic intervention.

CXCL8, also termed IL-8, is a member of the CXC chemokine family and is unique in its evolutionary trajectory. Unlike many cytokines conserved across species, CXCL8 is absent in rodents but plays a critical role in human innate immunity, particularly in neutrophil recruitment [34]. This human-specificity underscores its importance in translational research and explains why murine models often fail to fully recapitulate CXCL8-driven immune dynamics [9, 35]. The dual nomenclature, CXCL8 and IL-8, stems from its discovery as both a chemokine (CXCL8) and a pro-inflammatory cytokine (IL-8) [34, 36]. Our TCGA analysis confirmed its aberrant overexpression in GBM tissues, aligning with prior studies linking CXCL8 to angiogenesis and myeloid cell infiltration. Notably, CXCL8’s human-centric biology highlights the need for humanized models or primate studies to better mimic its role in GBM pathophysiology. Although CXCL8 dominates chemokine expression in GBM, CXCL1/3/5 overexpression also correlates with poor prognosis. These ligands signal via CXCR2, a key neutrophil chemotaxis receptor, and may synergize with or compensate for CXCL8. Notably, CXCL1/3/5 are minimally expressed in glioblastoma cells, suggesting non-tumor sources (e.g., stromal cells) may contribute to neutrophil recruitment. While CXCL8 blockade attenuated tumor growth, combinatorial inhibition of CXCR1/2 ligands could enhance efficacy. Our chemokine screening framework provides a roadmap to dissect context-specific roles of these factors in shaping the GBM immune landscape.

The identification of L-NEUs in GBM patients raises critical questions about their origin and functional specialization. These cells exhibit an immature phenotype, reduced granularity, and enhanced migratory capacity, reminiscent of PMN-MDSCs. While PMN-MDSCs are classically thought to arise from perturbed myelopoiesis in the bone marrow [19] our findings suggest that L-NEUs may also undergo phenotypic reprogramming within the GBM microenvironment. For instance, the reduced expression of maturation markers (CD16, CD13, CD10) could reflect either arrested differentiation or active suppression by tumor-derived factors such as CXCL8. Functionally, L-NEU accumulation correlates with immunosuppressive signatures by secreting reactive oxygen species (ROS), arginase-1, or other soluble mediators that inhibit T cell activation [37–39] a hypothesis warranting further validation. Their CXCL8-dependent recruitment further positions them as key mediators of the “cold” tumor immune phenotype [9, 13, 14, 40] which resists checkpoint blockade therapies.

Our L-NEUs exhibit phenotypic (CD16loCD10lo) and functional (CXCL8-responsive) similarities to PMN-MDSCs and N2 neutrophils, yet conceptual distinctions persist. PMN-MDSCs are defined by functional immunosuppression (e.g., T-cell inhibition) [15, 41] while N2 polarization denotes a pro-tumor transcriptional state [7, 42]. L-NEUs represent a density-segregated subpopulation enriched in LOX1+ cells but lacking canonical functional validation [38]. Critically, these subsets likely represent overlapping contexts of the same plastic cell type: immature neutrophils co-opted by the TME [39, 43]. We propose “L-NEU” as an operational term for CXCL8-recruited, low-density neutrophils in GBM, acknowledging shared markers (e.g., ARG1, S100A8/9) while cautioning against premature equivalence to functionally defined PMN-MDSCs.

The discovery of RNASE2 and THBD as L-NEU-specific markers represents a significant advance in GBM diagnostics and therapy. RNASE2, a secretory ribonuclease, is traditionally associated with eosinophil function but now emerges as a novel player in neutrophil-mediated immunosuppression [44, 45]. Its upregulation in GBM may facilitate extracellular RNA degradation, impairing dendritic cell antigen presentation or promoting tumor cell survival. Conversely, THBD, a transmembrane glycoprotein, is best known for its anticoagulant properties but may also modulate inflammation via interactions with thrombin or complement pathways [46, 47]. The strong correlation between RNASE2/THBD expression and poor prognosis suggests their dual utility as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. For instance, neutralizing antibodies against THBD could deplete L-NEUs, while RNASE2 inhibitors might disrupt their immunosuppressive secretory profile. Importantly, their pan-cancer overexpression in malignancies like renal carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma underscores their broad relevance beyond GBM.

While this study provides mechanistic insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the reliance on peripheral blood samples precludes direct assessment of intratumoral L-NEU dynamics. Future work should incorporate spatial transcriptomics or single-cell RNA sequencing of GBM tissue to resolve their spatial distribution and interactions with other immune subsets. Second, the functional characterization of RNASE2 and THBD remains incomplete. For example, whether RNASE2 directly suppresses antitumor immunity or merely serves as a bystander marker requires experimental validation using knockout models. Similarly, the role of THBD in neutrophil adhesion or endothelial interactions remains unexplored. Third, while CXCL8 blockade shows therapeutic promise [14, 48, 49] its human-specific nature complicates preclinical testing, necessitating the development of humanized mouse models or alternative strategies targeting downstream effectors like RNASE2/THBD.

While this work delineates CXCL8-driven recruitment of immunosuppressive L-NEUs in GBM, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, functional validation of L-NEU immunosuppressive activity (e.g., T-cell suppression, ROS/arginase production) was precluded by neutrophil fragility ex vivo [50] restricting mechanistic depth. Second, reliance on peripheral blood samples limits direct insight into intratumoral neutrophil dynamics. Though we observed LOX1+ neutrophil infiltration in GBM tissues, spatial resolution of RNASE2/THBD+ cells remains uncharacterized. Third, while bioinformatics identified RNASE2/THBD as L-NEU markers, the small gene intersection between CXCL8-associated and L-NEU signature sets underscores the need for orthogonal validation. Future studies employing spatial transcriptomics, genetic perturbation models, and humanized systems will bridge these gaps.

While the sample size (n = 5 some groups) for key experiments reflects clinical challenges in procuring GBM specimens, statistically robust differences were observed in migration and phenotypic. Findings were further validated in large-scale TCGA cohorts. We recommend expanded validation in future multi-center studies.

In summary, this study establishes CXCL8 as a central regulator of neutrophil-driven immunosuppression in GBM, mediated through the recruitment and reprogramming of L-NEUs. The identification of RNASE2 and THBD as biomarkers of this pathogenic neutrophil subset opens new avenues for prognosis and therapy. While challenges remain in translating these findings to the clinic, targeting the CXCL8-RNASE2/THBD axis represents a promising strategy to reverse immunosuppression and enhance the efficacy of existing immunotherapies. By bridging molecular insights with clinical outcomes, this work underscores the importance of neutrophil plasticity in shaping the GBM microenvironment and offers a roadmap for future investigations into myeloid-targeted treatments.
