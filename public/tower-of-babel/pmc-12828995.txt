Litigation meets evidence: a novel approach to understanding preventable complications in breast reconstruction
Authors: Daihun Kang, Seung Eun Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast reconstruction surgery can result in various complications, some of which lead to medical litigation or formal mediation. However, systematic comparison of these medicolegal cases with evidence-based preventive strategies from randomized controlled trials remains unexplored. This study employed a novel methodology comparing complications resulting in litigation/mediation with contemporary randomized controlled trial (RCT) evidence. A mixed-methods approach was utilized, combining systematic review of RCTs (PubMed, 2020–2024) following modified PRISMA guidelines with comprehensive analysis of medical litigation and mediation databases. Narrative synthesis compared complication patterns between medicolegal cases and preventive interventions evaluated in RCTs. Nineteen RCTs and seven medicolegal cases (5 litigations, 2 mediations) were analyzed. All cases involved complications for which preventive strategies have been evaluated in recent RCTs. In implant-based reconstruction, tissue necrosis leading to implant loss (3/5 cases, 60%) occurred despite RCT evidence showing 50% reduction with indocyanine green assessment and 37% reduction with prostaglandin E1 (34.3% to 21.6%, p < 0.001). Both autologous reconstruction cases developed abdominal hernias, contrasting with 1–3% rates using synthetic mesh versus 38% with biological materials (p < 0.001). None of these cases documented use of such strategies. Informed consent violations were identified in 86% of cases. Total awards (compensation plus consolation money) ranged from $0—34,509, granted in 86% (6/7) of cases. These findings suggest a gap between available evidence and documented clinical practice in complications severe enough to prompt litigation or formal mediation. The preventive strategies identified in this analysis—including indocyanine green perfusion assessment, prostaglandin E1 administration, and selective synthetic mesh reinforcement—represent potential opportunities for reducing catastrophic complications and medicolegal risk, though implementation must be individualized based on patient-specific factors, risks, costs, and resource availability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03425-3.Keywords: Breast reconstruction, Medical litigation, Evidence-practice gap, Surgical complications, Preventive interventions, Implementation science

Introduction

“To know and not to do is not yet to know” — a proverb often attributed to Confucius and later popularized by Covey — aptly encapsulates a persistent dilemma in modern surgical practice [1]. Despite robust evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCT) supporting effective preventive strategies, major complications in breast reconstruction continue to occur with troubling frequency [2, 3]. This discordance between knowledge and implementation represents a critical barrier to quality improvement in contemporary surgical care.

Breast reconstruction has evolved into an essential component of comprehensive breast cancer care, with profound implications for patient quality of life and psychosocial recovery [4]. Technical advances over the past decade have yielded remarkable innovations: indocyanine green perfusion assessment can reduce tissue necrosis by 50% [5], prostaglandin E1 dramatically decreases skin complications [6], and the use of synthetic mesh has been associated with a substantial reduction in postoperative abdominal hernia rates [7]. These interventions have been demonstrated in clinical trials and represent important advances in reconstructive surgery [5–7].

Yet the persistence of preventable catastrophic complications tells a different story. When patients experience complete reconstruction failure, permanent disfigurement, or severe functional impairment despite the availability of preventive strategies, the consequences extend far beyond clinical outcomes. These failures often culminate in medical litigation, representing not just individual tragedies but systematic breakdowns in translating knowledge into practice [8].

This study introduces a novel methodology to illuminate this evidence-practice gap: the systematic comparison of breast reconstruction complications resulting in litigation with contemporary randomized controlled trial evidence for their prevention. By analyzing the intersection of what went wrong (litigation cases) with what could have prevented it (RCT evidence), we move beyond traditional approaches that examine clinical outcomes and medicolegal cases in isolation.

The objectives of this study were to systematically compare breast reconstruction complications resulting in litigation with contemporary RCT evidence, identify gaps in preventive strategy implementation, and develop an evidence-based protocol to reduce catastrophic complications. This analysis provides practical guidance for clinicians seeking to implement interventions evaluated in RCTs and improve patient safety.

Methods

Study design

This mixed-methods study employed a modified PRISMA framework for systematic review of RCTs alongside qualitative analysis of medical litigation cases. The modification involved narrative synthesis rather than meta-analysis due to the heterogeneous nature of comparing clinical trial outcomes with legal proceedings [9].

For this analysis, “preventive strategies evaluated in RCTs” refers to interventions that demonstrated benefit in randomized controlled trials published between 2020–2024. The strength and quality of supporting evidence varies across interventions—some supported by multiple trials with consistent findings, others by single studies requiring further validation. These strategies represent potential preventive options rather than universal mandates. Clinical decision-making necessarily weighs potential benefits against associated risks, costs, logistical constraints, patient-specific factors (anatomy, comorbidities, preferences), and local resource availability.

Systematic review of RCTs

A systematic search of PubMed was conducted following modified PRISMA guidelines [10]. The search strategy included: (breast reconstruction) AND (complication OR adverse event), limited to RCTs in English (2020–2024). Two reviewers (D.K. and S.E.H.) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review of potentially eligible articles. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Data extraction was performed independently by both reviewers using a standardized form, including study characteristics, patient demographics, intervention details, and complication rates. Selection criteria and detailed search strategy are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The PRISMA flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1.

Legal case identification

Medical litigation cases were identified through systematic searching of Korean legal databases including BigCase®, CaseNote®, Supreme Court database, and Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency records. Search terms included “breast reconstruction,” “breast surgery,” and related medical terms combined with “medical litigation” or “medical dispute” in Korean. To capture all relevant precedents, the databases were systematically searched on August 5, 2025, with no date restrictions applied to the search itself.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic literature search identified 10,999 articles through PubMed. After applying filters for randomized controlled trials with full-text availability in English, 76 articles remained for screening. Following title and abstract review, 57 articles were excluded for lack of associated data availability, resulting in 19 RCTs meeting our inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). These trials, published between January 2020 and December 2024, enrolled patients undergoing breast reconstruction (Supplemental Table 1).

The comprehensive search of Korean legal databases without date restrictions identified 7 breast reconstruction-related cases, comprising 5 court litigations [3 first-instance (district court), 2 appellate (high court), none in the Supreme Court] and 2 medical dispute mediations, spanning from 2018 to 2023.

Patient demographics and case distribution

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the seven legal cases. All patients were female. Five cases (71.4%) involved implant-based reconstruction, including four direct-to-implant (DTI) procedures and one staged tissue expander reconstruction. Two cases (28.6%) involved autologous reconstruction using either deep inferior epigastric perforator flap (DIEP) or bilateral transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap (TRAM). General surgeons performed two procedures (28.6%), while plastic surgeons performed five (71.4%). The primary indications for reconstruction were breast cancer (n = 5, 71.4%), congenital breast hypoplasia (n = 1, 14.3%), and one case (14.3%) requiring reconstruction after removal of injected large foreign body material (cosmetic filler).

Notably, none of the litigated cases (Cases 1–5) documented the use of contemporary preventive measures such as ICG angiography or PGE1. In Case 5, the use of an acellular dermal matrix (Megaderm®) for abdominal closure was documented, which preceded a hernia—a finding consistent with RCT evidence on the inferior performance of such biological materials for this purpose. The timeline analysis reveals prolonged periods from the initial surgery to definitive complication management, such as the 13 months of multiple failed reconstructions in Case 1 and the 21-month delay to definitive hernia repair in Case 5.

Comparison with contemporary RCT evidence

Table 2 summarizes complication rates from the 19 included RCTs, revealing substantial improvements with evidence-based interventions. For implant-based reconstruction, baseline skin necrosis rates of 34.3% were reduced to 21.6% with PGE1 administration (p < 0.001), with even more dramatic reductions in nipple necrosis from 29.4% to 15.5% in nipple-sparing mastectomies [6]. Seroma formation showed the most striking difference: 38% with biological mesh versus 3.8% with synthetic mesh (p = 0.011) [11]. The choice of acellular dermal matrix (ADM) also impacted outcomes, with one product (DermACELL®) demonstrating superior incorporation (99.8% vs 93.4%) and lower seroma rates (14.55% vs 30.91%) compared to alternatives [12].

For autologous reconstruction, contemporary techniques showed marked improvements. The pedicle measurement technique reduced venous congestion from 8.3% to 0.5% (p < 0.001) [14], while closed-incision negative pressure therapy provided a 21.6% absolute risk reduction in donor site wound dehiscence [15]. Notably, both autologous reconstruction cases that resulted in litigation involved abdominal hernias following closure with ADM alone, whereas contemporary evidence demonstrates synthetic mesh maintains hernia rates at 1–3% [7, 18, 19]. This suggests that the choice of closure material may be a critical determinant of litigation risk.

Risk factor analysis

Table 3 presents risk factors identified across the RCTs. Obesity emerged as a universal risk factor, with each BMI unit increase associated with a 14% increased risk of matrix-related complications (OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.05–1.24, p = 0.001) [13]. Prepectoral implant placement with ADM support carried a 4.53-fold increased risk compared to subpectoral placement (95% CI 1.82–11.3, p = 0.001) [13]. Previous breast conservation therapy was associated with a 60% rate of fat necrosis in autologous fat grafting procedures [16].

Legal outcomes and compensation

Healthcare providers were found partially liable for medical negligence in two of five litigation cases (40%), with courts assigning liability of 30% and 40% respectively. The remaining three litigation cases resulted in no liability determination despite significant complications. Separate from negligence findings, informed consent violations resulted in consolation money awards in most cases. ‘Consolation money’ (위자료, wija-ryo) is a distinctive feature of Korean tort law, representing compensation for emotional distress and violation of patient autonomy. This is awarded independently of proven medical negligence, reflecting Korea’s emphasis on informed consent as a separate legal duty beyond the standard of care [22, 23].

Total compensation (including consolation money) varied substantially. Among litigation cases, awards ranged from ₩0 to ₩48,067,779 ($0 ~ $34,509, based on an exchange rate of ₩1,393 = $1 USD on August 3, 2025). The two cases with established negligence liability received the highest total compensation: ₩24,094,209 ($17,299) for the implant case with 40% liability and ₩48,067,779 ($34,509) for the DIEP flap case with 30% liability. These amounts included both negligence-based damages and additional consolation money. Among cases without negligence findings, four of five still received consolation money ranging from ₩5,000,000 ~ ₩15,000,000 ($3,590 ~ $10,769) for informed consent violations, demonstrating judicial recognition of patient autonomy violations independent of surgical fault. The two mediation cases were resolved with consolation payments of ₩7,000,000 ($5,025) and ₩10,000,000 ($7,179) respectively.

Implementation gap analysis

Critical analysis revealed that none of the litigation cases documented use of preventive strategies that have shown benefit in contemporary RCTs. Specifically, no cases utilized ICG perfusion assessment (which reduces necrosis by 40–86%) [24], PGE1 prophylaxis (reduces skin complications from 34.3% to 21.6%) [6], or synthetic mesh for abdominal closure (maintains hernia rates at 1–3%) [7, 11]. The absence of documented use of these strategies in cases resulting in litigation represents a central observation of this study and suggests opportunities for improved perioperative decision-making and documentation.

Discussion

This study compared breast reconstruction complications resulting in litigation with preventive strategies evaluated in contemporary RCTs. The novel hybrid methodology, illustrated in Fig. 2 bridges the gap between worst-case clinical failures (litigation outcomes) and best-practice evidence (contemporary RCTs) to systematically identify implementation gaps. The analysis revealed important gaps between available evidence and clinical practice that merit careful consideration.

Conceptual framework: Novel Hybrid Methodology. This study employs a novel approach comparing catastrophic complications resulting in medical litigation (n = 7 cases) with preventive strategies evaluated in contemporary randomized controlled trials (n = 19 studies, 2020–2024). Principal findings

Among seven litigation cases analyzed, tissue necrosis dominated implant-based reconstruction complications (3/5 cases), while both autologous reconstruction cases developed abdominal hernias. These complication patterns align precisely with areas where contemporary RCTs demonstrate the greatest preventive potential—with interventions showing 50% or greater reduction in these specific complications. The observation that none of the cases documented use of these preventive measures suggests potential opportunities for improving both clinical practice and documentation, though multiple factors influence individual clinical decisions.

Understanding the gap between available evidence and clinical practice

The observation that none of the seven medicolegal cases documented use of preventive strategies that have shown benefit in recent RCTs raises an important question: what factors influence the adoption of such interventions in routine practice? Implementation science literature identifies multiple barriers to translating research findings into clinical care, including organizational factors, individual clinician behaviors, and system-level constraints [25]. Analysis of these cases, alongside broader contextual understanding, suggests several interrelated factors—economic, institutional, cultural, and paradoxically, medicolegal—that may help explain patterns of adoption and documentation.

Economic barriers

Economic constraints represent a substantial barrier to implementing preventive technologies. Indocyanine green angiography, while associated with 40–86% reduction in tissue necrosis [24, 26], requires substantial capital investment in specialized imaging equipment that many institutions find difficult to justify [27], particularly in healthcare systems with limited resources or restrictive reimbursement policies [28]. Similarly, PGE1 administration, despite its association with reduced skin complications (from 34.3% to 21.6%) [6], incurs additional pharmaceutical costs that are not universally covered by insurance, creating financial disincentives for routine use in some settings [29].

Institutional and cultural barriers

Beyond economic factors, institutional patterns and surgeon preference create barriers to practice change. For instance, the preference for biological materials over synthetic mesh in abdominal closure, evident in both autologous reconstruction litigation cases, exemplifies specialty-specific practice patterns. Despite synthetic mesh being more cost-effective and supported by RCT evidence, plastic surgeons may default to familiar ADM products routinely used for implant coverage [30, 31]. This specialty-based variation—where surgeons adhere to techniques from their training rather than adopting cross-specialty best practices—represents an important factor in practice patterns.

Breast reconstruction represents a field where new evidence and techniques constantly emerge, yet not all practitioners remain current with evolving literature. This pattern may be particularly pronounced when traditional methods appear to work adequately—as most reconstructions proceed without catastrophic failure, the perceived urgency for continuous learning may diminish. The cognitive and temporal demands of integrating new evidence, combined with comfort with established routines, can create inertia that persists even when preventive interventions could potentially reduce complication rates [32, 33]. This phenomenon may be especially relevant when adopting evidence-based strategies requires not just new equipment or training, but fundamental shifts in surgical approach—from accepting baseline complication rates to actively pursuing prevention through continuous practice evolution.

Medicolegal considerations

The medicolegal environment itself may create unique influences on practice patterns. In the Korean system, where maximum compensation for catastrophic complications averaged only $34,509 in this series—substantially lower than comparable cases in some Western jurisdictions—the economic incentive for institutions to invest in expensive preventive technologies may be limited. This creates a potential calculus where the cost of implementing comprehensive preventive protocols (ICG equipment, routine PGE1 use, selective mesh reinforcement) may exceed the financial risk of occasional litigation. While ethically complex, this observation suggests that lower liability exposure might contribute to the evidence-practice gap by providing less economic pressure for systematic safety improvements. This finding highlights how medicolegal frameworks may shape not just individual surgical decisions but also institutional investment in patient safety infrastructure, potentially helping explain why certain interventions remain underutilized even in cases severe enough to prompt litigation.

Material selection also impacts outcomes, with synthetic mesh demonstrating advantages over biological alternatives for both implant coverage and abdominal closure in selected studies [7, 11].

In autologous reconstruction, technical refinements such as precise pedicle measurement (reducing venous congestion from 8.3% to 0.5%) [14] and selective use of synthetic mesh for fascial closure (maintaining hernia rates at 1–3%) [7, 11, 34] represent important advances supported by trial evidence. The use of closed-incision negative pressure therapy for donor sites provides additional benefit with minimal added complexity [15].

Analysis of the litigation cases reveals a pattern: both autologous reconstruction lawsuits involved abdominal hernias following TRAM/DIEP procedures where only biological materials were documented for closure. The decision for mesh reinforcement should be individualized based on specific technical factors: width of fascial incision (> 3 cm typically associated with higher risk), number of perforators harvested (multiple perforators increase risk), and lateral row perforator selection which requires more extensive dissection [35–38].

While mesh-related complications including infection (2–5%), extrusion, and chronic foreign body reaction must be considered [39], analysis of these litigation cases suggests that the risk of hernia without reinforcement in high-risk scenarios may outweigh these potential complications. The key lies in appropriate patient selection: identifying those with multiple risk factors (wide fascial defects, multiple perforators, lateral row harvest) who would most benefit from mesh reinforcement, while avoiding unnecessary mesh use in low-risk cases.

When mesh reinforcement is deemed unnecessary based on these criteria, surgeons should thoroughly document the rationale for this decision, as our medicolegal analysis demonstrates that appropriate documentation can provide important protection even when complications occur.

Medicolegal implications

The legal analysis revealed striking patterns that extend beyond individual case outcomes, offering insights into how different medicolegal systems shape clinical practice and risk management strategies.

Comparative analysis of medical liability systems

Analysis uncovered fundamental differences between Korean and Western medicolegal environments that profoundly impact breast reconstruction practice (Table 5). In the Korean system, healthcare providers were found liable for medical negligence in only 28.6% of cases despite all involving significant complications. However, 86% of cases resulted in monetary compensation through Korea’s distinctive dual-track liability system—a pattern that contrasts sharply with Western medicolegal frameworks [22, 23, 40].

Impact on risk management strategies

These jurisdictional differences create fundamentally different risk environments that shape surgical decision-making and documentation practices. Korean surgeons operate under strong incentives for meticulous consent documentation while enjoying relative protection for reasonable clinical judgments [23, 41]. American practitioners face the opposite dynamic: minimal exposure for consent issues but potentially substantial damages for negligence findings [42].

For the global medical community, these findings highlight how identical complications can yield vastly different legal outcomes. As medical tourism increases and practitioners work across borders, understanding these disparate approaches to liability, burden of proof, and compensation becomes essential. The high rate of consent violations (86%), despite relatively low negligence findings (28.6%) in this series, suggests that documentation practices may merit attention alongside clinical competence, emphasizing the need for structured consent processes that transcend jurisdictional boundaries. These findings suggest that attention to contemporary RCT evidence in clinical practice may help improve patient outcomes while also reducing medicolegal risk.

Study strengths and limitations

The primary strength of this study lies in its novel hybrid methodology, which, to our knowledge, is the first to systematically bridge the gap between two extremes of clinical evidence: up-to-date, best-practice standards derived from randomized controlled trials and the worst-case clinical failures severe enough to result in litigation. By directly comparing the pinnacle of evidence-based prevention with the reality of catastrophic, litigated outcomes, this approach offers unique and powerful insights into the systemic failures of translating knowledge into practice. This method provides an unfiltered view of the most critical, yet preventable, complications, creating an actionable framework for quality improvement initiatives.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the small number of cases and the inherent selection bias toward severe outcomes in litigation data limit the generalizability. The seven cases analyzed likely represent the ‘tail of the distribution’—the unfortunate minority who experienced severe complications, as most patients achieve satisfactory outcomes without these preventive interventions. Second, formal risk-of-bias assessment (e.g., RoB 2) of individual RCTs was not performed. This study’s primary objective was to identify potential gaps in implementation by comparing litigated complications with available contemporary evidence, rather than to conduct systematic appraisal or meta-analysis of intervention efficacy. The RCTs served as reference points for contemporary preventive options rather than as primary data requiring quality weighting for pooled estimates. Consequently, the strength and quality of evidence for individual interventions varies considerably—some supported by multiple trials with consistent findings, others by single studies requiring further validation. Readers should interpret these comparisons as hypothesis-generating and descriptive rather than establishing definitive causal relationships. The study maps where litigation intersects with known risk domains and identifies potential opportunities for evidence-informed practice, rather than asserting that non-implementation caused the observed outcomes.

Third, limitations arise from the data sources. Public court rulings, while detailed in legal reasoning, often lack exhaustive clinical data (e.g.,comprehensive comorbidity lists, BMI) found in medical records. Cases resolved through formal mediation are subject to confidentiality and not publicly accessible, further restricting available data. Fourth, temporal factors merit consideration, as several evidence-based interventions (e.g., specific ICG protocols, PGE1 administration) were published after some litigation cases occurred, highlighting the evolving nature of surgical standards and the challenge of retrospectively applying current best practices to historical cases. Finally, this analysis is situated within the specific Korean medicolegal environment, and its findings may not be directly applicable to all healthcare systems.

Conclusion

This analysis reveals a notable gap between preventive strategies evaluated in contemporary RCTs and their documented use in breast reconstruction complications severe enough to result in litigation or mediation. While these findings are descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than establishing causation, they suggest concrete opportunities for quality improvement. The framework presented in Table 4 synthesizes interventions that have demonstrated benefit in recent trials—including indocyanine green perfusion assessment, prostaglandin E1 administration, selective synthetic mesh reinforcement, closed-incision negative pressure therapy, and structured informed consent processes. However, implementation must be individualized based on patient anatomy, comorbidities, institutional resources, clinical judgment, and careful consideration of each intervention’s risks, costs, and practical constraints.

To translate these findings into practice, three actions are recommended: First, surgeons and institutions should systematically evaluate their current practices against evolving evidence, implementing validated strategies where appropriate for their patient populations. Second, clinical decision-making rationale—including factors influencing selection or non-use of specific interventions—should be thoroughly documented to improve both care quality and medicolegal protection. Third, professional societies should develop context-sensitive implementation frameworks that promote evidence-informed practice while acknowledging resource heterogeneity across healthcare systems. By aligning clinical practice and documentation with contemporary evidence—while maintaining individualized, patient-centered decision-making—the reconstructive surgery community can actively work to reduce preventable catastrophic complications and their associated medicolegal consequences.
