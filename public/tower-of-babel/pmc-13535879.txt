Macroscopic, Histological and Ultrastructural Features of the Tongue of the Anatolian Wild Boar ( Sus scrofa libycus )
Authors: Fatma Işbilir, Banu Kandil, Muhammed Zahid Atli, İhsan Işbilir, Barış Can Güzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study provides a detailed characterization of the macroscopic anatomy and microscopic features (light and scanning electron microscopy) of the lingual papillae in the adult Anatolian wild boar ( Sus scrofa libycus ). Tissue samples were taken from two adult wild boars found dead in their natural habitat within an average of 1.5 h. Following macroscopic observation, samples underwent routine histological processing, histochemical staining, and high‐resolution surface morphology analysis via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Morphological analysis revealed five distinct types of papillae: filiform, fungiform, conical, foliate, and vallate. Filiform papillae, exhibiting either sharp or blunt apices, showed structural adaptations specialized for mechanical function. Fungiform papillae were identified in circular and oval forms; notably, taste buds were restricted to the circular type. Foliate papillae, comprising 3–5 distinct folds, lacked taste buds. A pair of vallate papillae, each demarcated by a prominent trench and housing lateral taste buds, was observed on the caudal tongue. Histochemical evaluation using Periodic Acid‐Schiff (PAS) and Alcian Blue (AB) confirmed the presence of both neutral and acidic mucins within the lingual glands. These findings suggest that the lingual morphology of the Anatolian wild boar is closely adapted to its specific dietary habits and ecological niche. This data serves as a critical baseline for comparative anatomy, forensic veterinary medicine, and future morphological research.Keywords: Anatolian wild boar, lingual papillae, macroscopy, microscopy

Introduction

As the biological ancestor of domestic pigs, the wild boar belongs to the species Sus scrofa , though various subspecies have been proposed based on geographical distribution (Genova 1999). While naturally spanning Europe and Asia, the introduction of domestic hybrids and farmed individuals has established feral populations globally (Cushman et al. 2004; Wilson 2005). Morphologically, the species is distinguished by its prominent dorsal ridges, formidable tusks, and robust cranial‐heavy proportions (Wilson 2005). This physical build supports an opportunistic omnivorous diet ranging from subterranean roots and seeds to invertebrates and small vertebrates (Lee and Lee 2019). Such dietary plasticity is fundamentally reflected in the animal's oral anatomy and feeding mechanisms (Shao et al. 2010). In mammals, the tongue is the primary organ for food procurement and processing. It facilitates critical behaviors—such as licking, lapping, and grazing‐ and orchestrates the intraoral transport of food from the incisors to the molars for bolus formation and deglutition (Abumandour et al. 2023; Dursun 2006). Beyond its role in ingestion, the tongue is involved in grooming (Gonçalves et al. 2020) and vocalization (Kılınç et al. 2010). The lingual mucosa is characterized by a specialized papillary system: mechanical papillae (filiform, conical, and lenticular) facilitate mastication, while gustatory papillae (vallate, fungiform, and foliate) mediate taste perception (Toprak et al. 2020). The evolutionary trajectory and morphological diversity of these papillae are closely linked to environmental adaptations and functional requirements (Gonçalves et al. 2020; Iwasaki 2002). Consequently, variations in epithelial morphology serve as indicators of a species' dietary specialization and habitat adaptation (Menezes et al. 2013; Massoud and Abumandour 2020).

The microscopic architecture of the tongue has been extensively documented in domestic mammals, including the cat (Iwasaki 1992), wolf (Haligur et al. 2019), buffalo (Can and Atalgın 2015), sheep (İşbilir et al. 2025), and donkey (Abd‐Elnaeim et al. 2002). While comparative studies exist for fetal (Ateş et al. 2013) and adult domestic pigs (Reginato et al. 2020), comprehensive data on the Anatolian wild boar remain scarce. Given that the pig is an established model for human taste perception due to remarkable structural similarities (Montavon and Lindstrand 1991), understanding its wild progenitor's anatomy becomes even more significant.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to integrate macroscopic examination with advanced microscopic techniques—including diverse histochemical staining and scanning electron microscopy (SEM)‐ to analyze the wild boar tongue. By detailing these features, we aim to provide a baseline for comparative anatomy and forensic veterinary science. Furthermore, these findings contribute to a broader understanding of the relationship between the species' ecological and behavioral characteristics, as well as its biological resilience and ecological niche.

Materials and Methods

Specimen Collection

The dead pigs were determined to have died as a result of a traffic accident, and the individuals were reached within approximately 1.5 h after being reported by local residents. Due to the winter season and snow cover, no macroscopic decomposition was observed, and samples were transported to the laboratory under cold chain conditions. Both individuals were identified as male and adult; this was confirmed by the presence of fully developed permanent teeth, tooth wear, body and coat characteristics, and the closure of the epiphyseal plates in the femur. Research protocols were authorized by the General Directorate of Nature Conservation and National Parks of the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry of the Republic of Türkiye. Ethical clearance was provided by the Siirt University Experimental Animals Application and Research Center (Ethics Committee Approval No: 2025/12/71).

Macroscopic Analysis

The specimens were first screened to ensure the absence of clinical lesions or pathological signs. Morphological features, including the spatial distribution and density of lingual papillae, were documented. Detailed surface examination and imaging were performed using a stereomicroscope (Olympus SZ61, Japan).

Microscopic and Histochemical Procedures

Tissue samples (n = 5) were systematically collected from the apex, corpus, and radix regions, targeting specific papillary localizations. Following fixation (immersion fixation) in 10% buffered formaldehyde for 24 h, the samples underwent routine histological processing and paraffin embedding. Sections (5 μm thick) were then prepared from the blocks. Crossmon's trichrome stain (Crossmon 1937) was utilized for general histological evaluation. To characterize the secretory profile of the lingual glands, Periodic Acid‐Schiff (PAS) and Alcian Blue (AB, pH 2.5) stains were applied to differentiate between neutral and acidic mucosubstances, respectively (Bancroft et al. 1994).

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

For ultrastructural analysis, tissue fragments were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (pH 7.2) for 24 h. Post‐fixation, samples were rinsed three times in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 10 min each. Secondary fixation was conducted in 1% osmium tetroxide prepared in phosphate buffer for 2 h on a rotator at room temperature, followed by a second triple‐rinse in phosphate buffer. The specimens were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (25%, 50%, 75%, 90%; 15 min each), followed by final incubation in 96% and 100% ethanol for 30 min. After dehydration in graded ethanol, the samples were dried in an oven at 60°C for 48 h before being coated with gold for SEM analysis. Subsequently, the samples were coated with gold and examined using SEM to evaluate the surface micro‐morphology (İşbilir et al. 2024).

Results

General Morphology

Macroscopic observation confirmed the tongue's anatomical position within the cavum oris proprium. The organ was securely anchored to the floor of the oral cavity by a well‐developed frenulum linguae.

Filiform Papillae

Filiform papillae were distributed across the entire dorsal lingual surface, with the highest concentration observed at the apex (Figure 1A). Combined light and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) identified two morphologically distinct variants: those with sharp apices and those with blunt apices (Figure 2A–F). The sharp tip papilla formed a spine‐like projection and had a rough surface (Figure 2A,B). The tip of the blunt‐ended papilla exhibited a cap‐like structure and had a smooth surface (Figure 2C,D). Both papilla types were surrounded by stratified squamous keratinized epithelium (Figure 2A–D). Notably, the posterior surface of the sharp papillae exhibited distinct spine‐like keratinized processes within the epithelial layer (Figure 2B). The connective tissue contained skeletal muscle fibers and blood vessels (Figure 2A,C).

Conical Papillae

Macro‐morphological analysis identified numerous conical papillae localized on the dorsal surface of the lingual root, all distinctly oriented in a caudal direction (Figure 1B). Further characterization via light microscopy and SEM categorized these papillae into two morphological variants: sharp tip and blunt (Figure 3A–C,I). The sharp tip papillae were cone‐shaped, having a broad base and a tapering, stalk‐like apex (Figure 3A,B,D,I). The blunt‐ended papillae were dome‐shaped and had a broad base, but their tips were blunt (Figure 3A,C,D,I). Histologically, both variants were surfaced by a stratified squamous keratinized epithelium (Figure 3A–F), characterized by a relatively thin stratum corneum (Figure 3E,F). Aggregated lymphoid follicles, blood vessels, fat cells, skeletal muscle fibers, seromucous glands, and excretory ducts were observed in the connective tissue beneath the epithelium (Figure 3A–H).

Fungiform Papillae

Mushroom‐shaped fungiform papillae were densely distributed across both the dorsal and ventral surfaces of the lingual apex, with their frequency progressively diminishing toward the root. While these papillae were predominantly oval (Figure 1C), circular variants were localized specifically along the lateral regions of the corpus (Figure 1D). Notably, a high‐density cluster of fungiform papillae was identified cranial to and interspersed between the vallate papillae.

Comparative light microscopy and SEM categorized the fungiform papillae into two distinct types based on their geometry: circular and oval (Figure 4). Both types were demarcated from the adjacent filiform papillae by a well‐defined circumferential groove (Figure 4B,D). Both papilla types had a smooth surface and were surrounded by stratified squamous keratinized epithelium (Figure 4B,D–F). The stratum corneum was prominent on both the dorsal and lateral surfaces of the oval‐shaped fungiform papillae (Figure 4B). The stratum corneum was more prominent on the lateral surface of the circular‐shaped fungiform papilla than on its dorsal surface (Figure 4E,F). Taste buds were not observed in the epithelial layer of the oval‐shaped fungiform papilla (Figure 4B,G). However, multiple taste buds were detected in the epithelial layer on the dorsal surface of the circular‐shaped fungiform papilla (Figure 4E,F,H). In oval‐shaped papillae, the taste buds opened onto the dorsal surface (Figure 4E,F). In the connective tissue underlying both papilla types, skeletal muscle fibers, blood vessels, and fat cells were identified, whereas lingual glands were absent (Figure 4A–D).

Foliate Papillae

Macro‐ and microscopic evaluations identified the foliate papillae as a series of 3–5 distinct folds separated by intervening grooves, situated along the caudolateral margins of the tongue (Figures 1E and 5). These papillae were surfaced by a stratified squamous non‐keratinized epithelium, which exhibited a characteristic rough texture on the dorsal aspect. Notably, histological screening confirmed the total absence of taste buds within the epithelial layer. The underlying lamina propria was well‐vascularized (Figure 5C,D). Similar to fungiform papillae, there were no lingual glands in the connective tissue (Figure 5A,B).

Vallate Papillae

Macroscopic examination identified two vallate papillae positioned bilaterally on the caudal corpus, proximal to the lingual root, each demarcated by a profound circular groove (Figure 1F). Histologically, these papillae exhibited a mushroom‐like configuration encircled by a prominent groove (Figure 6A). The papillae displayed an irregular morphology with a notably rough dorsal surface (Figure 6A,C). The entire structure was enveloped in a stratified squamous keratinized epithelium (Figure 6A–E), with the stratum corneum being significantly more prominent on the dorsal aspect compared to the lateral surfaces (Figure 6C,D). Oval‐shaped taste buds were identified as being embedded within the epithelial layer. They were located on the lateral surfaces of the papillae and opened into the papillary groove (Figure 6A,B,D,E). The dorsal surface of the papillae was devoid of taste buds (Figure 6A–C). A small number of seromucous glands were identified in the connective tissue immediately beneath the epithelial layer of the vallate papilla (Figure 6B,F,I). However, there were numerous serous (Von Ebner's glands) glands in the connective tissue below the papillae (Figure 6A,G,J). In addition, skeletal muscles, excretory ducts, nerve plexuses, blood vessels, and fat cells were detected in the connective tissue (Figure 6A,B,F–J). Like taste buds, the excretory ducts also opened into the groove region (Figure 6B,E).

Consistent with the orientation of the taste buds, the excretory ducts of the underlying glands also discharged into the base of the trench (Figure 6B,E). The papilla was surrounded by a thick ring‐shaped pad (annular pad) or vallum structure, which was separated from the papilla by the trench. Filiform papillae were irregularly distributed on this pad (Figure 6K,L).

The mucous and serous epithelial cells of the seromucous glands exhibited a strong positive reaction to PAS staining (Figure 7A). Likewise, Von Ebner's glands exhibited strong PAS positivity in their secretory cells (Figure 7B). However, the ductal epithelial cells showed a negative PAS reaction (Figure 7A,B). With Alcian Blue (pH 2.5) staining, strong positivity was observed in the ductal epithelial cells and mucous epithelial cells of the seromucous glands, whereas weaker staining was detected in the serous epithelial cells of both the seromucous glands and Von Ebner's glands (Figure 7C,D).

Discussion

This investigation provides a comprehensive characterization of the lingual morphology in the Anatolian wild boar, integrating macroscopic, histological, and ultrastructural analyses. Our findings identified five distinct papillary types: filiform, fungiform, conical, foliate, and vallate, each exhibiting structural adaptations specialized for mechanical or gustatory roles.

Filiform papillae, the most abundant type across the mammalian tongue, serve essential mechanical functions during mastication, such as intraoral food transport and grinding, facilitated by their characteristic surface roughness (Haggag et al. 2020). Consistent with general mammalian patterns, these papillae were densely distributed along the dorsal surface, with their frequency progressively diminishing toward the caudal region (Kılınç et al. 2010). However, notable inter‐population variations exist within the species. For instance, in wild boars from Timor Island, filiform papillae exhibit a predominantly cylindrical and elongated morphology with blunt apices (Harum et al. 2025). Harum et al. (2025) also reported larger papillae with thicker keratinization in the lingual corpus compared to the apex, whereas our findings reveal a more heterogeneous pattern including both sharp and blunt types, particularly with distinct adaptations in the apical region. Kumar and Bate (2004) identified blunt‐tipped papillae on the middle dorsal surface of the pig tongue; however, this observation was not confirmed by Chamorro et al. (1994). Our results help reconcile this discrepancy by demonstrating the coexistence of both pointed and blunt filiform papillae in the Anatolian wild boar.

While the sharp variants in our study exhibited a characteristically rough surface, the blunt‐type papillae displayed a smoother apical morphology. Notably, the identification of blunt‐tipped papillae in the apex region represents a localization not previously emphasized in domestic pig models (Kumar and Bate 2004). Despite these morphological differences, both types were consistently covered by stratified squamous keratinized epithelium, reinforcing their primary mechanical role and suggesting adaptation to environmental and dietary pressures.

Conical papillae were localized in the lingual radix and displayed two morphological variants (sharp and blunt), consistent with previous reports in carnivores and pigs (Dellmann et al. 2006; Ojima et al. 1997). While rudimentary forms of these papillae have been described in the apex during fetal development (Ateş et al. 2013), their clear localization in the radix in adult specimens indicates postnatal structural differentiation. This distribution diverges from the observations of Tichý (1991) but aligns with those reported by Kumar and Bate (2004). Additionally, the presence of seromucous glands in the subepithelial connective tissue supports earlier anatomical descriptions (Tichý 1991). A noteworthy finding of this study is the presence of clustered lymphoid nodules, which were not reported in fetal specimens (Ateş et al. 2013), suggesting a postnatal adaptive response of the lingual mucosa to environmental exposure and potential pathogens. Consistent with the morphological patterns reported in carnivores such as cats and dogs (Dellmann et al. 2006; Ojima et al. 1997), we identified two distinct types of conical papillae within the lingual radix. While rudimentary forms of these structures have been documented at the lingual apex during fetal development (Ateş et al. 2013), our findings in adult specimens show a distinct caudal extension toward the radix. This distribution diverges from the observations of Tichý (1991) but aligns with the spatial arrangement described by Kumar and Bate (2004). Morphologically, the conical papillae in the Anatolian wild boar presented with either sharp or blunt apices, providing a more refined classification than the exclusively blunt‐tipped, cone‐shaped structures reported in the Timor Island population (Harum et al. 2025). Furthermore, the presence of seromucous glands within the subepithelial connective tissue corroborates earlier anatomical descriptions by Tichý (1991). A noteworthy finding in our study was the identification of clustered lymphoid nodules; these immune‐related structures, absent during the fetal period (Ateş et al. 2013), suggest a postnatal functional adaptation of the lingual mucosa to environmental pathogens and the species' specific dietary habits.

Fungiform papillae exhibited two distinct morphological types—circular and oval—with differences in both structure and function. Their distribution pattern is consistent with previous studies in pigs, goats, and dogs (Kumar and Bate 2004; Tadjalli and Pazhoomand 2004; Fonseca et al. 2011; El Sharaby et al. 2014). While Ateş et al. (2013) reported that only certain papillae near the vallate region were surrounded by grooves, our findings demonstrate that all fungiform papillae were encircled by a circumferential groove, including a dense cluster located between and cranial to the vallate papillae. Functionally, taste buds were detected only in the circular papillae, whereas oval forms lacked gustatory structures, suggesting functional specialization (Toprak et al. 2020).

Furthermore, our study clarifies discrepancies regarding epithelial keratinization. It has been reported that in Timor Island wild boars and Canadian hybrid pigs, the epithelium is non‐keratinized and taste pores are absent (Kumar and Bate 2004; Harum et al. 2025). In contrast, our findings demonstrate a smooth, stratified squamous keratinized epithelium. This keratinization pattern varied between the two types: prominent across all surfaces in oval papillae, yet restricted primarily to the lateral surfaces in circular papillae. The identification of the stratum corneum is supported by more recent studies on pig models (Reginato et al. 2020; Kumar and Kumar 2012), and it is thought that these features may be an adaptation to the natural omnivorous diet of the Anatolian wild boar, which includes roots, tubers, and hard plant materials.

Foliate papillae, located along the caudolateral margins of the tongue, consisted of 3–5 folds, consistent with previous reports (Chamorro et al. 1994). However, in contrast to earlier studies that describe the presence of taste buds within these structures (Kumar and Bate 2004; Chamorro et al. 1994; Reginato et al. 2020), no taste buds were detected in the present study using either SEM or histological analysis. Additionally, the non‐keratinized epithelial covering aligns with findings in Canadian hybrid pigs and fetal wild boars (Kumar and Kumar 2012; Ateş et al. 2013), rather than with keratinized patterns reported in other adult populations (Harum et al. 2025; Reginato et al. 2020). This finding may indicate species‐specific variation, functional modification, or ecological adaptation; however, the limited sample size should also be considered. Furthermore, the absence of serous glands contrasts with previous reports (Harum et al. 2025; Reginato et al. 2020), suggesting additional structural variability.

The presence of two vallate papillae is consistent with previous findings in pigs (Mack et al. 1997; Harum et al. 2025), although this number is lower than that reported in many domestic mammals (İşbilir et al. 2025; El Sharaby et al. 2014). Taste buds were located on the lateral surfaces of the papillae and opened into the surrounding trench, in agreement with earlier studies (Kumar and Bate 2004; El Sharaby et al. 2014). The presence of filiform papillae on the annular pad surrounding the vallate papillae is also consistent with previous morphological descriptions (Chamorro et al. 1994; Reginato et al. 2020). Histologically, the vallate papillae were covered by stratified squamous epithelium with a thin keratin layer, confirming previous findings (Harum et al. 2025).

Histological examination confirmed that the vallate papillae are clad in stratified squamous epithelium featuring a thin stratum corneum, corroborating previous reports (Harum et al. 2025). Situated in the connective tissue, the Von Ebner's glands were observed to discharge serous secretions into the trench, a mechanism essential for clearing tastants to allow for continuous sensory reception (Iwasaki 2002). The direct opening of the excretory ducts from these glands into the trench region provides further evidence for the structural arrangement previously described by Kumar and Bate (2004).

The presence of neutral glycoproteins in the Iranian squirrel is suggested by PAS‐positive mucus‐secreting cells, which exhibit a distinct red coloration (Sadeghinezhad et al. 2018). Such a predominance of neutral glycoconjugates is mirrored in the lingual glands' mucous acini, where strong PAS positivity has also been detected (Goździewska‐Harłajczuk et al. 2023). Nevertheless, our observation of intense positive staining with Alcian blue at pH 2.5 indicates that these secretions are not exclusively neutral; they also incorporate sulfated and carboxylated glycoproteins alongside acidic mucopolysaccharides. This dual secretory profile aligns with findings in the adult aardvark (Goździewska‐Harłajczuk et al. 2018) and matches the staining patterns described by Erdoğan and Sağsöz (2018) for both glandular and ductal epithelial cells.

Collectively, these observations suggest that while lingual gland compositions are species‐specific, they generally maintain a balance of neutral and acidic mucins. This biochemical versatility likely serves critical physiological roles, ranging from facilitating food transport and oral cavity protection to the fine‐tuning of taste perception.

Collectively, these findings indicate that while lingual papillae organization is generally conserved among pig species, significant variations exist in epithelial keratinization, taste bud distribution, and glandular composition. These differences are likely associated with dietary habits, environmental conditions, and species‐specific adaptations (Davydova et al. 2017; Kuru et al. 2017; Čížek et al. 2020).

Finally, the absence of marginal and lenticular papillae, which have been reported in other species or developmental stages (Schneider et al. 1996; Shindo et al. 2008; Schlechta et al. 1996; Ateş et al. 2013), further emphasizes the species‐ and stage‐specific nature of lingual morphology.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of the macroscopic, histological, and ultrastructural features of the tongue in the Anatolian wild boar ( Sus scrofa libycus ), integrating light and scanning electron microscopy to examine filiform, fungiform, conical, foliate, and vallate papillae. The observed spatial distribution and morphological specializations of these papillae reflect clear functional adaptations to the species' omnivorous diet, with filiform and conical papillae contributing to mechanical processing, and fungiform and vallate papillae supporting gustatory function.

The histochemical profile of the lingual glands, demonstrating the presence of both neutral and acidic mucins, further highlights the functional complexity of the tongue in facilitating food processing, oral protection, and taste modulation. These findings provide novel baseline data for the Anatolian wild boar and contribute to the broader understanding of structure–function relationships in mammalian lingual morphology.

However, the limited sample size and the descriptive nature of the study should be considered when interpreting the results. Future research incorporating larger sample populations, quantitative morphometric analyses, and advanced molecular or functional approaches will be essential to further elucidate the adaptive and biological significance of lingual structures in this species.
