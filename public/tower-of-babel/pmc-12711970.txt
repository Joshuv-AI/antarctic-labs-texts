Morphological attributes, biochemical parameters, phytochemical constituents and antioxidant activities of endangered Citrus macroptera Mont (Hatkora.) from North-East India
Authors: Esther Lalruatsangi, Tridip Kumar Hazarika, Rody Ngurthankhumi, Lalnunzawma, Vandenhawii Chawlkha, M Vabeiryureilai, R Lalnungrenga, Vanlalhmuaka Ngente
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCitrus macroptera Mont commonly known as Melanesian papeda or hatkora is grown in wild or semi-wild state in north eastern part of India The main objective of this present study was to investigate the morphological characters, biochemical parameters, phytochemical constituents and antioxidant activities within 30 hatkora accessions from Mizoram, north-east India. The results indicated wide range of variability in terms of morphological characters, biochemical parameters, phytochemical constituents and antioxidant activities of hatkora. Significant differences exhibited among the studied accessions in polymorphic qualitative features such as leaf lamina shape, leaf lamina margin, leaf apex shape, petiole wing shape, fruit shape, fruit apex shape, fruit base shape, seed shape, and colour. Fruit quantitative characters, such as fruit weight, fruit volume, fruit length, fruit diameter, rind thickness, juice volume, juice percentage, and number of seeds per fruit and biochemical parameters as well phytochemical constituents and antioxidant activities of the fruits, such as TSS, juice pH, ascorbic acid, titratable acidity, total, reducing and non-reducing sugars, total phenolics, total flavonoids, DPPH, and ABTS activities were also varied significantly among the studied accessions. The wide range of variation in all these parameters of hatkora fruits indicated the great scope for selection of the elite types for future genetic improvement programme based on these characters.Keywords: Citrus macroptera, Hatkora, Morphological variation, Biochemical variation, Seeding pattern, MizoramSubject terms: Biochemistry, Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The north-eastern part of India is considered to be the natural habitat of numerous Citrus species blessed with ideal agro-climatic conditions for their cultivation. Citrus plants growing in deep forests undisturbed by abiotic factors have also been reported from the region, thus bestowing this area with a special status of ‘‘treasure house’’ of Citrus germplasm1. Citrus macroptera Mont., commonly known as ‘Melanesian papeda’, ‘wild orange’, ‘Cabuyao’, ‘Satkara’ or ‘Hatkora’ and ‘wild orange’ is a semi wild species of Citrus2. As per IUCN norms, C. macroptera Mont. falls under the category of endangered species as indicated by threat perception analysis. A rich genetic diversity of Hatkora exists in north eastern region of India including bordering areas of Bangladesh and Myanmar. It is found to be grown in semi wild form in Shella and Dawki area near Cherrapunjee in Meghalaya, Chandel district of Manipur, Jampui Hills of Tripura and Mizoram. It needs special and immediate attention for conservation due to their endemism and high degree of threat perception3.

As a neglected crop, the hatkora is cultivated in mixed crops with other forest trees without appropriate management practices. Since they are exclusively propagated by seeds, their population exhibits significant genetic heterogeneity in terms of plant appearance and fruit physico-chemical traits.

Although the north-eastern area of India has a rich genetic diversity of hatkora4, very recently the population of hatkora are decreasing in an alarming rate the biodiversity hotspots of the world. Unaware of its health benefits, many farmers has shifted to other fruits that give more assured income with better government support. In addition, lack of value addition also leads to declining in cultivation of this particular fruits. Similarly, due to gradual decrease of farmer’s interest in commercial cultivation and clearing of forest cover caused by shifting cultivation also the species becoming endangered. In spite of all these facts, a number of high yielding accessions are still available in wild and semi wild state in the region, but, till now no any systematic research have been carried out for studies on genetic diversity of hatkora of the region. In light of the current situation of the rapid extinction of such valuable unexploited fruit, it is crucial to exploit existing variability, characterise morphologically and biochemically, identify superior genotypes, and to conserve them. However, compared to morphological data, molecular tools provide abundant information and are highly efficient and insensitive to environmental factors. The use of molecular markers is hereby suggested for further research on hatkora as it is an ideal means for identifying genotypes, estimation of relation between different accessions and following inheritance of economically important characters.

Identification of elite genotypes is an essential component of every breeding programme. In order to enrich the information and acquaint the citrus breeder to interpret phenotypic values in terms of potential genetic gain, the present study was undertaken to elucidate the genetic variability of hatkora accessions with respect to morphological characters, biochemical parameters, phytochemical constituents and antioxidant activities of the fruits from natural population for scientific validation of hatkora of Mizoram, north-east India. Characterization of different metabolites including polyphenols and antioxidants will assist in the retrieval of the indigenous resources for breeding elite cultivars and promotion of hatkora fruits as a functional food.

Materials and methods

Study area

Based on the availability of hatkora in Mizoram, five districts namely Aizawl, Kolasib, Mamit, Lunglei, and Serchhip were chosen for the study. As shown in Figs. 1 and 2 and a map across different districts of Mizoram has been generated illustrating the places where the samples were collected by using ArcGIS software (version 10.8). A comprehensive study was carried out in five districts of Mizoram namely, Aizawl, Lunglei, Kolasib, Serchhip and Mamit covering 30 locations in total of different geographic coordinates. These districts represent a range of geographical populations, elevation and climatic conditions prevalent in Mizoram. Each tree was given an accession number consisted of letter ‘MH’ for Mizoram hatkora, 01 to 05 for districts and T1 to T6 for plant number. The collection of plant material was complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation. In Mizoram, north-east India, it is a very common plant and is well known to everyone from the plant canopy and architecture of the plant as well very peculiar fruit shape and size. Their formal identification was done by the researcher only with the help of orchard owners after taking the verbal permission from village head.

Climatic observations

The meteorological data (temperature, relative humidity and precipitation) during the study period were obtained from State Meteorological Station, Directorate of Science and Technology, Government of Mizoram as shown in Fig. 3.

Climatic conditions of selected districts.

Morphological attributes

Data on morphological parameters of trees, leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds of the selected hatkora trees were recorded based on IPGRI descriptor on Citrus5. To identify the relationships among the accessions of hatkora within the studied regions, the gathered data was further examined using statistical and clustering approaches. For study on morphological variations, a comprehensive set of 60 variables were taken into consideration in the selected five districts of Mizoram. For the investigation of seed morphological characters of hatkora, the fruits were collected at ripened stage, gently washed and cut open for extraction of seeds. The observation encompasses a wide range of traits pertaining to trees, leaves, flowers, fruits, and seeds of hatkora.

The data collected were further analyzed using statistical techniques and clustering methods to identify relationships among the, morphological characters of fruits, leaves, flower and seeds of hatkora within the studied region.

Fruit samples

During flowering time (March-April), two to three flowering branches were tagged. The fruits were picked between 120 and 140 days after flowering, when they were at their peak maturity. The fruit maturity indices employed were calendar date by counting the number of days after fruit set and change in colour.

Biochemical analysis

Standard quality parameters were determined using 10 fruits for each accession. The total soluble solids were measured by using Atago digital refractometer and fruit juice pH was determined by digital pH meter cyberscan 510. AOAC6 standard protocols were followed for measuring the titratable acidity, reducing, non-reducing and total sugars of the fruit juices. The ascorbic acid content of the fruits was estimated using 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol dye method7, while Folin Ciocalteau standard protocols were followed for the estimation of phenols in the fruits. The DPPH and ABTS free radical reduction activity were performed according to Leong & Shui8 and Re et al.9respectively with slight modification.

Statistical analysis

The study was conducted by using three replications. Fisher’s method of analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for statistical analysis of the quantitative data using the software OPSTAT. Significance of variance among data was calculated out by calculating the value of ‘F’ at 5% probability level. The ArcGIS software (version 10.8) was utilized to plot geographical coordinates and construct the study area map. The principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed using PAST 4.11 programme and dendrogram was constructed using the Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) based on the numerical data generated using morphological characters. The polymorphic qualitative characters of tree, leaves, flower, fruit and seed were scored and subjected to cluster analysis using UPGMA for grouping of accessions by following Citrus descriptor.

Results

Analysis of variance (ANOVA)

The investigation of variation of hatkora accessions from different districts of Mizoram based on morphological (tree, leaf, flower, fruit, seed) and biochemical characters revealed that there is a significant differences among the selected districts (p < 0.05) indicating variation in the morphological characters including seeding pattern and biochemical traits.

Morphological attributes

The study on morphological characteristics comprehends a total of 60 characters on trees, leaf, flower, fruit and seed parameters. The analysis revealed significant variations across the accessions from the different districts. Among the studied characters, the tree, leaves and flower characters exhibited significant variations among the studied accessions. Tree shapes recorded were obloid, spheroid and ellipsoid type with erect and spreading growth habit. The leaf lamina attachment varied between brevipetiolate and longipetiolate. The leaf lamina shape recorded was elliptic and ovate type with an entire leaf margin. The different leaf apex shapes were recorded as emarginated and acute. Wide variability petiole wing shape i.e. obovate, obcordate and obdeltate was recorded with a broad and medium petiole wing width (Fig. 4). The observations on flower characters (as shown in Fig. 5) revealed that flowers were whitish in colour with yellowish anther colour of short and medium length of anthers relative to stigma. The sex forms were hermaphrodite with both solitary and inflorescence arrangement of flowers. The flower positions were both axillary and terminal. Four different durations of flowering were recorded which varied between 50 to 90 days.

The study on fruit qualitative characters (Fig. 6) revealed that considerable variations exhibited among the hatkora accessions in terms of fruit shape (oblique, spheroid and ellipsoid), shape of fruit base (truncate, concave and convex), shape of fruit apex (depressed, rounded and truncate), fruit skin colour (light yellow, green yellow and yellow), fruit skin surface texture (papillate, smooth and rough), nature of oil glands (strongly conspicuous, very weakly conspicuous and conspicuous). The observations on leaf quantitative characters revealed that the leaf length ranged between 13.30 cm and 17.60 cm; leaf width 4.27 cm to 5.80 cm; leaf length/width ratio ranging between 2.29 and 4.09. Significant variations were observed in the seed characteristics among the selected accessions of different districts. The seed count of Hatkora fruits (Fig. 7) revealed that Mamit district recorded maximum number of seed count. Five different seed shapes were recorded i.e. semideltoid, clavate, ovate, spheroid and fusiform with creamish and whitish colour. Significant observations on seed quantitative characters revealed that number of seeds per fruit ranged between 15.67 and 23.33 of mean value 19.91 and seed weight ranging 1.02 g to 2.46 g with an average seed weight of 2.20 g.

Biochemical analysis

The analysis of biochemical parameters in thirty Hatkora accessions revealed substantial variation across the studied districts as presented in Fig. 10. The different observations revealed that there was significant variation in TSS, juice pH, titratable acidity, ascorbic acid and sugars content among the accessions. The highest TSS was recorded in accession MH02T2 (9.80°Brix) while the lowest was in accession MH02T5 (7.10°Brix) with an average TSS of 8.93°Brix. The highest pH was recorded in accession MH03T2 (3.19) while lowest pH was in accession MH02T4 (1.76). The average value of pH for the accessions was recorded to be 1.98. The highest titratable acidity was recorded in accession MH04T4 (11.56%) while lowest titratable acidity was in accession MH01T5 (8.20%). The highest titratable acidity for district wise was recorded in district D3 (9.43%) while lowest of 9.06% was recorded in D4 (Fig. 10). The highest ascorbic acid was recorded in accession MH03T2 (67.42 mg/100 ml) while lowest in accession MH01T3 (38.70 mg/100 ml).

The highest percentage of reducing sugars was recorded in accession MH02T6 (1.27%) while lowest was recorded in accession MH03T5 (0.71%) of mean 1.02%. The highest percentage of non-reducing sugars was recorded in MH04T1 (1.63%) while lowest was recorded in accession MH03T3 (0.14%) with an average of 0.87%. Among the accessions, MH04T1 (2.83%) recorded highest total sugars while lowest was recorded in MH01T6 (1.32%). The mean percentage of total sugars for the accessions was recorded to be 1.92%.

Discussion

The analysis of morphological characters enables researchers by featuring an important insight into variations within a plant species. The close examination of the morphological characteristics gave a valuable insights into the diverse traits and variations within the different plant species, cultivars, and varieties but also within the same cultivar and variety across different regions10.

The present investigation revealed that there was significant variation among the morphological characters including tree height, trunk girth, canopy diameter, spine length, leaf width, leaf length/width ratio, petal length, petal width, fruit weight, fruit volume, fruit length, fruit diameter, rind thickness, segments per fruit, juice percentage, juice vesicle length, number of seeds per fruit and seed weight among the 30 accessions from the selected 5 districts for the study, thus, indicating variations in important tree, leaf, flower, fruit and seed characters of hatkora fruits. The variation in tree height among different accessions might be due to difference in age of the tree, plant physiological status, different climatic conditions and nutrient status of the soil. Similar observations in tree quantitative characters were also observed in Assam lemon11 and pummelo12.

From the leaf quantitative characters, there were variations observed in leaf width and leaf length/width ratio and similar observations in leaf quantitative characters as well. Leaf lamina attachment was recorded as longipetiolate and brevipetiolate; leaf lamina shape of ovate and elliptic; leaf apex shape of emarginate and acute; obovate, obcordate and obdeltate petiole wing shape. The research investigation by Chetry et al.13. in C. indica reported that there are morphological variations among the Citrus species in leaf qualitative and quantitative characters.

The variations in flower and fruit morphological characters were observed qualitatively and quantitatively. Duration of flowering varies from 51 to 90 days among the accessions. The variation in duration of flowering might be due to the genetic makeup, physiological status of the plant and the environmental factors.

The fruit quantitative characters varied significantly as fruit juice percentage varies from 24.16% to 81.53%; fruit weight of 143.89 g to 349.31 g; fruit volume of 165.93 cc to 338.02 cc; segments per fruit from 13.67 to 15.33 and juice vesicle length of 0.68 to 1.17 cm. Among the qualitative characters of fruit, variations were observed in fruit shape (oblique, spheroid and ellipsoid), shape of fruit base (truncate, concave & convex), shape of fruit apex (depressed, rounded & truncate), fruit skin colour (light yellow, green yellow & yellow), fruit skin surface texture (papillate, smooth & rough) and nature of oil glands (strongly conspicuous, conspicuous & very weakly conspicuous). The results are in conformity with Sangma and Perinba14 in C. macroptera from Meghalaya. Factors such as climate, soil composition, and agricultural practices create regional variations in farming15.

The finding on seeding pattern of hatkora accessions highlights the variations in terms of qualitative and quantitative characteristics. The number of seeds per fruit and seed weight (g) varied from 15.67 to 23.33 and 1.02 to 2.46 g Among the seed quantitative characters, five different seed shapes were recorded i.e. clavate, semideltoid, ovoid, spheroid and fusiform of cream and white colour. The variations in seed counts among the different accessions observed in different districts highlight the complexity of many factors influencing the seed growth and development. The combination of environmental conditions, genetic mutations, and soil nutrient variations may contribute to the observed diversity in seed counts. It was previously reported that the environmental factors and genetic mutations act as a potential influencer on the seeding pattern of fruits16.

The substantial morphological divergence among the hatkora accessions is demonstrated by PCA (Principal Coordinates Analysis) employing morphological characteristics. According to the results, there are four distinct groups that vary from each other in terms of their morphological traits. In order to enhance the understanding of the complex dynamics within the accession, the four distinct group highlights the importance of hatkora accessions considering the morphological diversity, which acts as implication for genetic differences and possible breeding strategies. Similar observations were also documented for UPGMA clustering analysis, where a total of 30 accessions formed two distinct and four sub-clusters. Fourteen polymorphic characters of trees, leaf, flower, fruit and seed are used for the clustering analysis of different accessions collected from different geographical locations of Mizoram.

Apart from the above-mentioned observations, variations in different biochemical parameters were observed from 30 accessions of hatkora across the selected districts. The observations on total soluble solids of fruit juice varied significantly among the accession ranging between 7.1° Brix to 9.8° Brix. The variation in TSS content among the genotypes and different locations might be attributed to sugar and acid content of the accessions which might be influenced by the genetic makeup, environmental factors and sunlight. Fruits growing in arid region with limited water tended to accumulate more of dry matter and lower moisture which may result in higher TSS in fruits17. The juice pH varies between 1.76 and 3.19; ascorbic acid content of 38.70 mg 100 ml− 1 to 67.42 mg 100 ml− 1; titratable acidity of 8.20% to 1.56% and total sugars of 1.32% to 2.83%. Our findings are in agreement with study reported by Li et al.18 in Assam Lemon of Kamrup districts. The observed variations in juice content across different regions can be attributed to a range of regional factors, such as climate, soil composition, agricultural practices, and even genetic variations19. Agusti et al.20 reported that there was inverse relationship between temperature and acid content in grapefruit, pummelos and mandarin orange with a conclusion that the highest the day and night temperature, the lower the percentage of titratable acidity in satsuma mandarin. Hence, the observed variations in sugar concentration may be attributed to the influence of these organic acids, which subsequently affect both the sugar concentration and the pH levels of the juice21. By catering to demands for high yield, substantial juice content, and the absence of seeds, the outcomes of this study can play a pivotal role in guiding cultivation practices and meeting the evolving expectations of consumers22.

DPPH is a stable free radical that accepts an electron or hydrogen radical to become a stable diamagnetic molecule. It is based on the ability of DPPH, a stable free radical to decolorize in the presence of antioxidant. The antioxidant activity of various hatkora juice extracts revealed that the activity against DPPH increases steadily up to certain concentration and declined thereafter for all genotypes. The results reveals that the antioxidant content of hatkora is higher as compared to other Citrus species as reported by Hazarika et al.23.

ABTS assay is an excellent tool for determining the antioxidant activity of hydrogen-donating antioxidants like scavenging aqueous phase radicals and of chain breaking antioxidants such as scavenging lipid peroxyl radicals. The highly significant differences in antioxidant activity of different germplasm could be due to presence of polyphenols24. Antioxidants scavenge the harmful free radical in the body and combat the pathogenesis of major diseases including arthritis, diabetes and cancer. In Citrus, ascorbic acid, hesperidin, naringin are important contributors to antioxidant activity25. In addition, a synergistic action between different category of antioxidants such as carotenoids and flavonoids may also be responsible for antioxidants effects. Thus, the variation in antioxidant activity of different genotypes presented in this study could be due to the individual composition of the extract along with synergistic or antagonistic relation between different phenolic compounds26.

Flavonoids are widely distributed group of polyphenolic compounds, called ‘nutraceutical substances’ having anticancer, antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties. The study of total flavonoid content conducted by Ezeabara et al.27 on lime, lemon, pummelo, mandarin, grapefruit and sweet orange showed that hatkora has exhibited high total flavonoid content as compared to other Citrus species as a good oxidative agent.

All the accessions showed the presence of varying phenolic compounds in different concentrations. Total phenolic content investigated in this study is similar to that of Lalruatsangi et al.28 in C. macroptera. Hatkora has good phenolic content in comparison with other Citrus fruits as fruit juice having higher phenolic contents are found to exhibit higher antioxidant potential.

The soil composition and nutrient availability play a pivotal role in shaping the morphological characters of plants, including various fruit traits such as length, diameter, peel thickness, seed number, and more29. Thus, the findings from this study will benefit the growers and plant breeders for identification of elite or superior genotypes for release of commercial varieties and for further crop improvement programme on hatkora.

Conclusion

The present study indicated that there is wide range of variability in terms of morphological and biochemical parameters of hatkora in Mizoram. The observations on polymorphic qualitative characters revealed that there is considerable variations in terms of leaf lamina shape, leaf lamina margin, leaf apex shape, petiole wing shape, fruit shape, fruit apex shape, fruit base shape, seed shape and colour. Significant differences were observed among fruit quantitative characters i.e fruit weight, fruit volume, fruit length, fruit diameter, rind thickness, juice volume, juice percentage and number of seeds per fruit. The effect of different geographical locations of the districts was also found to be significant for fruit length, fruit weight and juice percentage. It is evident that there is influence of climatic factors on trait variations as locations expressing higher temperature and humidity recorded superior traits notably on the biochemical parameters. However, some of the morphological parameters showed no significant differences among the districts which indicated that the morphological parameters were not affected by the environmental factors but largely contributed by the genetic makeup of the tree.

Even though hatkora is an underutilized fruits facing extinction in the region, the present study showed that it is a rich source of ascorbic acid and sugars having high potential in pharmaceutical industries. There is no standard and commercialized variety of hatkora available in India as a whole. Hence, wide variability in the crop gives an opportunity to the plant breeder to select superior traits for development of improved varieties. In addition, further research on molecular level variation by use of genetic markers could be used to assess the relationships for detection of variation at the genomic level.

The present data revealed that hatkora is a good source of phenolic compounds with excellent radical scavenging activity as compared to other Citrus fruits. This endangered citrus species should be encouraged for processing into juices or other by products due to its good functional properties. Moreover, by processing these fruits, their utilization may be enhanced by reducing waste, ensuring availability throughout the year, and creating better selling prospects via the production of value-added commodities. These fruits may be used to effectively manage plant biodiversity via targeted conservation through domestication . The need for conservation of hatkora should be encouraged among the indigenous populations of north-east India considering its high antioxdiant potential as compared to other Citrus species.
