Bicriteria Polygon Aggregation with Arbitrary Shapes
Lotte Blank, David Eppstein, Jan-Henrik Haunert, Herman Haverkort, Benedikt Kolbe, Philip Mayer, Petra Mutzel, Alexander Naumann, Jonas Sauer

Abstract
We study the problem of aggregating polygons by covering them with disjoint representative regions, thereby inducing a clustering of the polygons. Our objective is to minimize a weighted sum of the total area and the total perimeter of the regions. This problem has applications in cartographic map generalization and urban analytics. Here, the polygons represent building footprints and the clusters may represent urban areas. Previous approaches forced the boundaries of the regions to come from a fixed subdivision of the plane, which allows the optimal solution (restricted in this way) to be found from a minimum cut in a dual graph. It is natural to ask whether the problem can still be solved efficiently if this restriction is removed, allowing output regions to be bounded by arbitrary curves. We provide a positive answer in the form of a polynomial-time algorithm. Additionally, we fully characterize the optimal solutions by showing that their boundaries are composed of input polygon edges and circular arcs of constant radius. Since some applications favor straight edges, we also study two problem variants in which the output regions must be polygons, but are not restricted to have boundaries from a fixed subdivision. In the first variant, region vertices must lie on the boundaries of the input polygons. The second variant requires them to be vertices of the input polygons. We show that both variants can be approximated up to a constant factor in polynomial time by altering an optimal solution for the unrestricted problem. Our experimental evaluation on real-world building footprints demonstrates that these approximate solutions are visually similar to the optimal unrestricted ones and achieve near-optimal objective values.

Introduction

We study the task of aggregating a set of polygons by covering them with a set of disjoint representative regions. This arises in various applications of geographical information systems (GIS), most prominently in map generalization and urban analytics. In map generalization, a given map must be transformed into a less detailed map to be displayed at a smaller scale. To keep the map legible to the human eye at increasingly smaller scales, details must be omitted. Especially at the transition from large-scale to small-scale topographic maps, buildings must be grouped into areas representing entire settlements
[
12
,
25
,
24
]
.
The delineation of settlement areas is also an important task in urban analytics, e.g., to study urban growth and morphology. Recent data-driven approaches propose to do this by aggregating building footprints, which provide evidence of urban land use
[
2
,
9
,
14
]
. While commercial GIS software provides tools for polygon aggregation
[
18
]
, the solutions are not optimal with respect to mathematically defined objectives.

When aggregating polygons, the resulting regions should typically have low shape complexity, but they should not include too much area not present in the input polygons.
These two criteria can conflict, so it is natural to consider different trade-offs depending on the scale at which the map is viewed: larger scales prioritize faithfulness to the original input, whereas smaller scales prioritize simplicity.

.

.
A property that is useful for achieving consistency between the solutions for different scales is
nestedness
: a coarser solution for a smaller scale should contain the larger-scale solutions
[
12
,
25
,
24
]
.
Ideally, the output should also be robust regarding changes in the input.
For example, adding a small polygon should not change the solution too much.

Most existing approaches for polygon aggregation do not optimize a global objective function.
For example, popular methods apply morphological operators
[
8
]
or use
\alpha
-shapes
[
12
]
.
An alternative approach is the adopt merge amalgamation
[
19
]
framework.
Here, a subdivision of the plane is fixed (e.g., a constrained Delaunay triangulation) and a solution consists of all input polygons plus a selection of cells from the subdivision.
Within this framework, Rottmann et al.
[
25
]
formulate polygon aggregation as a combinatorial optimization problem.
A solution
\mathcal{S}
is optimal if it minimizes the linear combination
g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S})=A(\mathcal{S})+\alpha P(\mathcal{S})
of the overall area
A(\mathcal{S})
and the overall perimeter
P(\mathcal{S})
.
The scaling parameter
\alpha
controls the trade-off between faithfulness to the original input, measured by the area, and the legibility of the output map, measured by the perimeter.
Minimizing the perimeter corresponds to Tufte’s minimum-ink principle
[
29
]
.
Moreover, the length of linework has been proposed as a measure of map complexity
[
20
,
15
]
.

The objective function
g_{\alpha}
is conceptually simple and has powerful implications.
For example, when optimizing
g_{\alpha}
, the perimeter decreases monotonically with increasing
\alpha
, which does not hold for
\alpha
-shapes (see
\Cref
fig:alphashapes in Appendix
A
for an example). Furthermore, optimal solutions are nested with respect to
\alpha

[
25
]
and, when scaling the input, an optimal solution for some value of
\alpha
remains optimal (although for a different choice of
\alpha
).
As shown in
[
25
]
, the problem for fixed
\alpha
can be reduced to finding a minimum cut in the dual graph of the subdivision.
This yields an optimal solution in
\mathcal{O}{\left(n^{2}/\log n\right)}
time, where
n
is the number of vertices in the input.

Although the formulation in
[
25
]
is quite general, it still requires a fixed subdivision to be chosen in advance.
This makes the problem easier to solve, but at the cost of arbitrarily constraining the solution space.
In this paper, we investigate whether the problem with the objective function
g_{\alpha}
remains tractable if this restriction is weakened or removed entirely. We study a hierarchy of problems with progressively stricter constraints, which turn out to be intimately related, both structurally and computationally.

In the unrestricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{F}
, the output regions are not limited to polygons.
Rather, they may be bounded by arbitrary curves (see Figure
2(a)
).
For analytical applications, this offers maximal accuracy with respect to the objective function. However, for visualization purposes, the complexity of arbitrary curves may be too high. Therefore, we also consider
the straight-line problem
P^{\alpha}_{L}
which adds the constraints that the output regions are polygons with vertices on the boundary of input polygons (see Figure
2(b)
).
If the vertices of the output polygons are required to be vertices of the input polygons, we obtain the vertex-restricted straight-line problem
P^{\alpha}_{V}
(see Figure
2(c)
).
Finally, the most restricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}}
is the one solved in
[
25
]
, in which a fixed subdivision
\mathcal{D}
is given and the output polygons must consist of cells in
\mathcal{D}
(see Figure
2(d)
).

Our Contributions.

•

We fully characterize solutions for the unrestricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{F}
and show that their boundary curves consist of input polygon boundaries and circular arcs of constant radius.
Note that unlike in previous approaches, the shapes of the boundary curves are not specified or constrained in advance.
Rather, they emerge naturally as a result of minimizing the (simple) objective function.

•

Using this characterization, we construct a subdivision
\mathcal{D}_{C}
of size
\mathcal{O}{\left(n^{4}\right)}
such that an optimal solution for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
can be obtained as an optimal solution for the subdivision-restricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}_{C}}
.
This leads to an
\mathcal{O}{\left(|\mathcal{D}_{C}|^{2}/\log n\right)}\subseteq\mathcal{O}{\left(n^{8}/\log n\right)}
time algorithm for the unrestricted problem by using the minimum-cut approach in
[
25
]
.

•

We show that the optimal solutions for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
are nested with respect to
\alpha
, and that introducing new input polygons will only grow the optimal solution.

•

Since the complexity of
P^{\alpha}_{L}
and
P^{\alpha}_{V}
is still open, we show that the exact algorithm for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
can be used to derive approximations with small constant factor for both problems in polynomial time.

•

We experimentally evaluate prototypical implementations of the presented algorithms on real-world building footprints, showcasing their computational feasibility for instances with up to
1\,623
polygons. In particular, we observe that the approximated solutions to
P^{\alpha}_{L}
and
P^{\alpha}_{V}
are very close to the optimal ones for the unrestricted problem.

Further Related Work.

Besides polygon aggregation, map generalization involves a range of well-studied operators, including elimination, displacement, exaggeration, detail elimination, squaring, and typification (see
[
21
,
8
]
for further reading). As this work focuses solely on aggregation, we limit our discussion to related studies on this operator.

Some approaches focus primarily on the simplification of polygons in large-scale maps, where individual buildings are still distinguishable
[
16
,
6
]
. Buchin, Meulemans, and Speckmann
[
6
]
propose a method based on iterative local simplification, which also includes an aggregation step that merges pairs of nearby buildings. However, maps of scales smaller than
1:50\,000
usually do not show individual buildings but rather polygons representing entire settlements
[
28
]
.

Polygon aggregation can be viewed as a combination of two subproblems: partitioning the polygons into clusters and computing a representative shape for each cluster.
Several popular approaches specify the type of representative shape in advance, which reduces it to a clustering problem.
If the only objective is to minimize the perimeter (which corresponds to
g_{\infty}
), the optimal representative shapes are convex hulls.
In this case, finding an optimal clustering is a special case of the fencing problem, which can be solved in
\mathcal{O}{\left(n\cdot\text{polylog}(n)\right)}
time
[
1
]
.
However, minimizing only the perimeter causes large empty areas to be included in the solution.
The same problem occurs when using the
\chi
-hull, a generalization of the convex hull for aggregation purposes
[
11
]
.
Other generalizations of the convex hull, such as the concave hull introduced by Moreira and Santo
[
22
]
, or
\alpha
-shapes, mitigate this problem.
However, both of these approaches can lead to narrow bridges in the output.
Recently, Funke and Storandt
[
12
]
proposed an approach that first clusters the polygons based on a distance threshold
\beta
and then computes an
\alpha
-shape for each cluster. Clusters with fewer points than some threshold value
\mu
are discarded altogether.
Their approach runs in
\mathcal{O}{\left(n\log n\right)}
time. Although this allows for the aggregation of country-sized instances within minutes, a major drawback is that the three parameters (
\alpha,\beta
and
\mu
) must be specified independently by the user, and the narrow bridge problem is only alleviated if they are chosen appropriately for the given instance.

For the subdivision-restricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}}
, Rottmann et al.
[
25
]
also consider the problem of finding a set
\mathfrak{S}
that contains an optimal solution for every possible value of
\alpha
.
They show that
\mathfrak{S}
has size
\mathcal{O}{\left(n\right)}
due to the nesting property and can be computed using standard techniques for bicriteria optimization.
The problem of computing
\mathfrak{S}
can be further reduced to the source-sink-monotone parametric minimum cut problem
[
3
]
.
This allows it to be solved in the same asymptotic runtime as computing a single solution for fixed
\alpha
.
Using this approach, instances representing large cities can be solved optimally within seconds.

2
Problem Definition

We introduce some basic notation. A curve is a continuous map
f\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We will also identify a curve with its image. The length of a (continuous) rectifiable curve
f
is denoted by
\ell(f)
. The area of a (Lebesgue-measurable) closed region
S
is denoted by
A(S)
.
The perimeter
P(S)
is the length of its boundary
\partial S
.
If
S
is a polygon, its vertices are denoted by
V(S)
, its edges by
E(S)
, and the convex hull of
V(S)
by
\text{conv}(S)
.
We extend the above definitions to sets
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
of (disjoint) closed regions or polygons.

Definition 1

(The unrestricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{F}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a set of interior-disjoint polygons in the plane and
\alpha\geq 0
. A (feasible) solution to
P^{\alpha}_{F}
is a set
\mathcal{S}
of disjoint closed regions that cover all polygons, i.e., for every polygon
B\in\mathcal{B}
there exists a region
S\in\mathcal{S}
with
B\subseteq S
. A solution
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is optimal if it minimizes the objective function

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S})=A(\mathcal{S})+\alpha P(\mathcal{S})

over all feasible solutions.
For
\alpha=\infty
, the objective function becomes
g_{\infty}(\mathcal{S})=P(\mathcal{S})
.

This objective function is equivalent to the objective function
f_{\lambda}(\mathcal{S})=\lambda A(\mathcal{S})+(1-\lambda)P(\mathcal{S})
proposed in
[
25
]
under the reparameterization
\alpha=\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}
.

Definition 2
.

A curve
f
from
u
to
v
is a
boundary piece
of a solution
\mathcal{S}
if
f\subseteq\partial\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}
includes the area to the right of
f
.
A boundary piece
f
is
constrained
if
f\subseteq\partial\mathcal{B}
and
free
if
(f\setminus\{u,v\})\cap\partial\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
.
The set of inclusion-maximal free pieces in
\mathcal{S}
is denoted by
F_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S})
.

We make a few observations:

•

Every boundary curve can be described as an alternating sequence of constrained and maximal free boundary pieces. The constrained pieces may consist of a single point.

•

A free piece
f
may be a closed curve. In this case, the start- and endpoint coincide, i.e.,
u=v
, and this is the only point in
f
that may belong to
\partial\mathcal{B}
.

•

Each maximal free piece
f\in F_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S})
is either a closed curve that touches
\partial\mathcal{B}
at most once or a non-closed curve whose endpoints are on
\partial\mathcal{B}
.

Imposing restrictions on
F_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S})
yields the following problem variants.

Definition 3
.

(Specialized problems
P^{\alpha}_{L}
,
P^{\alpha}_{V}
,
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}}
)

•

Straight-line problem
\boldsymbol{P^{\alpha}_{L}}
:
Every free piece
f\in F_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S})
must be a straight-line segment.

•

Vertex-restricted straight-line problem
\boldsymbol{P^{\alpha}_{V}}
:
Every free piece
f\in F_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S})
must be a straight-line segment with endpoints in
V(\mathcal{B})
.

•

Subdivision-restricted problem
\boldsymbol{P^{\alpha}_{D}}
:

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a planar subdivision of
\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})\setminus\mathcal{B}
. Every free piece
f\in F_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S})
must be a path in
E(\mathcal{D})
.

Figure
2
depicts solutions for each problem on a small instance.
A natural choice for the subdivision
\mathcal{D}
is a triangulation
\mathcal{D}_{T}
of
V(\mathcal{B})
whose edge set contains
E(\mathcal{B})
.
With this subdivision, the restrictions on the free pieces are progressively stricter with each problem variant.
For a problem
P
, we denote by
\Pi(P)
the set of allowed solutions.
Then we have

\displaystyle\Pi(P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}_{T}})\subseteq\Pi(P^{\alpha}_{V})\subseteq\Pi(P^{\alpha}_{L})\subseteq\Pi(P^{\alpha}_{F}).

Note that the first inclusion may no longer hold for more complex subdivisions.

Remark 4
.

For simplicity of exposition, we assume that all input polygons are simple.
If they contain holes, the holes can be solved as separate problem instances.
Similarly, a self-intersecting polygon can be treated as multiple interior-disjoint polygons.
Note that it is possible for the regions of a solution to contain holes, even if the input does not.

3
The Unrestricted Polygon Aggregation Problem

In this section, we investigate the unrestricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{F}
. We begin by characterizing the shape of the free boundary pieces and show that only a polynomial number of curves need to be inspected to find an optimal solution. We then use these curves to build a subdivision
\mathcal{D}_{C}
of
\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})\setminus\mathcal{B}
that has polynomial size, thereby reducing
P^{\alpha}_{F}
to the subdivision-restricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}_{C}}
.
This allows us to apply the minimum cut-based algorithm proposed in
[
25
]
to solve the unrestricted problem in polynomial time.
Finally, we note that the optimal solutions to
P^{\alpha}_{F}
are nested with respect to
\alpha
and with respect to introducing new input polygons.
For the sake of exposition, we sketch some proofs and omit others; a full version can be found in
\Cref
sec:unrestricted:full.

We start by characterizing the optimal solutions of the (simpler) corner cases
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=\infty
.
In the former, the input polygons
\mathcal{B}
form an optimal solution because they minimize the area.
In the latter, the perimeter must be minimized, so every region
S
in an optimal solution must be the convex hull
\text{conv}(\mathcal{B}(S))
of its contained polygons
\mathcal{B}(S)
.
For the general case
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, we note that it can be reduced to the case
\alpha=1
by scaling the input appropriately.

Observation 5
.

For a set
\mathcal{R}
of regions and
c>0
, let
\tau_{c}(\mathcal{R})
denote the same set of regions scaled uniformly by the factor
c
.
A solution
\mathcal{S}
for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
with input polygons
\mathcal{B}
and
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
is optimal iff
\tau_{1/\alpha}(\mathcal{S})
is optimal for
P^{1}_{F}
with input polygons
\tau_{1/\alpha}(\mathcal{B})
.

Proof.

Scaling both the input and the solution yields a valid problem instance and a valid solution for the scaled instance. The lengths of all curves increase by a factor of
\frac{1}{\alpha}
, and all areas increase by a factor of
\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}
. Moreover, the objective value increases by
\alpha^{2}
due to

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S})=A(\mathcal{S})+\alpha P(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}A(\tau_{1/\alpha}(\mathcal{S}))+\alpha^{2}P(\tau_{1/\alpha}(\mathcal{S}))=\alpha^{2}g_{1}(\tau_{1/\alpha}(\mathcal{S})).\qed

Consequently, we only consider the cost function
g_{1}
for the proofs in this section.

\Cref
obs:scale describes a crucial property of the problem formulation: the parameter
\alpha
corresponds directly to the scale at which the input map is viewed, in the sense that multiplying
\alpha
by some factor
c
is equivalent to scaling the map by a factor of
1/c
.
It follows that the set of solutions that are optimal for at least one value of
\alpha
does not change when scaling the input.

We continue by investigating which curves locally optimize the area-perimeter trade-off.

Definition 6
.

Let
f
be a curve from
u
to
v
that does not intersect the line through
u
and
v
properly.
Let
R^{{uv}}_{f}
denote the region enclosed by
\overline{uv}
and
f
.
The
efficiency
of
f
is defined as

e_{uv}(f)=\begin{cases}-\ell(f)+\ell(\overline{uv})+A(R^{uv}_{f})&\text{if $f$ is to the right of~$\overrightarrow{uv}$,}\\
-\ell(f)+\ell(\overline{uv})-A(R^{uv}_{f})&\text{if $f$ is to the left of~$\overrightarrow{uv}$.}\end{cases}

If we consider a solution that uses
\overrightarrow{uv}
as a free boundary piece such that the enclosed area is to the right of it, then
e_{uv}(f)
is the improvement in the objective value if we replace
\overrightarrow{uv}
with
f
(assuming that this yields a feasible solution).
We now characterize the curves that have maximum efficiency and show that any solution that uses a curve of non-maximum efficiency can be improved locally.

\ell(f)
, the enclosed area is maximized if

Lemma 7
.

Let
u,v
be two points. For fixed length
l
, the (unique) most efficient curve
f
from
u
to
v
with
\ell(f)=l
that does not intersect the line through
u
and
v
properly is a circular arc that lies to the right of
\overrightarrow{uv}
.

Proof.

Let
C_{r}
be the circular arc with radius
r
from
u
to
v
in the half-plane to the left of
\overrightarrow{uv}
such that
\ell(C_{r})+\ell(f)=2\pi r
(see
\cref
fig:ArcIsBest).
The curve
f
lies to the right of
\overrightarrow{uv}
since the efficiency is non-positive otherwise. Hence,
C_{r}
together with
f
is a closed curve with perimeter
P=2\pi r
. The isoperimetric inequality
[
26
,
17
]
states that for every closed curve
C
with enclosed region
R
, it holds that
4\pi A(R)\leq\ell(C)^{2}
. Furthermore, equality holds if and only if
C
is a circle.
It follows that
C_{r}
together with
f
has to be a circle to maximize the enclosed area and in turn maximize the efficiency. Consequently,
f
is a circular arc.
∎

\Cref

lemma:arcs_enclose_much restricts the curve
f
such that it does not intersect the line through
u
and
v
properly. We will see later (from
\Cref
lemma:local_structure) that the claim holds without this restriction.

In the remainder of this paper, we denote by
C_{r}^{uv}
the circular arc of radius
r
and central angle
\theta\leq\pi
that connects the points
u
and
v
and lies to the right of
\overrightarrow{uv}
.
An analytical argument shows that the most efficient circular arcs have radius
1
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
u,v
be two points with
\ell(\overline{uv})<2
. Among all circular arcs from
u
to
v
with central angle
\theta<\pi
that lie to the right of
\overrightarrow{uv}
, the arc
C_{1}^{uv}
has the largest efficiency.

We can combine Lemmas
7
and
8
to show that free pieces that are not circular arcs with radius
1
can be improved locally.

Lemma 9

(local improvements)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a solution for
P_{F}^{1}
and
u,v
two points on the boundary of
\mathcal{S}
such that the enclosed area lies to the right of
\overrightarrow{uv}
.
If
u
and
v
are connected by a free piece other than
C^{uv}_{1}
, then there exists a solution
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with a smaller objective value.

Using this insight, we can characterize the free boundary pieces that can appear in an optimal solution.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be an instance of
P^{\alpha}_{F}
for
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
. There exists a solution
\mathcal{S}
that minimizes
g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S})
, such that the boundary
\partial\mathcal{S}
is a collection of rectifiable curves and every free boundary piece
f
with endpoints
u
and
v
has the following properties:

P1:

The distance
d=\ell(\overline{uv})
of the endpoints is smaller than
2\alpha
.

P2:

The piece
f
is circular with radius
\alpha
.

P3:

The central angle
\theta
of
f
is smaller than
\pi
.

P4:

If
u
or
v
is in the interior of a polygon edge
e\in E(\mathcal{B})
, then
f
is tangential to
e
.

P5:

The piece
f
bends inwards (towards the interior of
\mathcal{S}
).

Proof Sketch.

The existence of an optimal solution with rectifiable boundary curves is a consequence of the Arzela-Ascoli theorem (see
\Cref
prop:there_exists_a_solution:full in
\Cref
sec:unrestricted:full).
P1, P2 and P5 follow directly from
\Cref
lemma:local_structure, except for the case
d=2\alpha
.
If
f
has a central angle
\theta>\pi
, then we can replace it with the corresponding arc
f^{\prime}
with central angle
2\pi-\theta
that bends in the same direction; see Figure
5
.
Analysis shows that the area gain is always outweighed by the perimeter loss.
By
\Cref
lemma:distance2 in Appendix
B
, arcs with
d=2\alpha
or
\theta=\pi
can be removed by transforming the solution without increasing the objective value.

To show P4, we assume
\alpha=1
by
\Cref
obs:scale.
Consider an arc
f=C_{1}^{uv}
such that
v
lies in the interior of a polygon edge
e
and
f
is not tangential on
e
.
If no other maximal free piece has
v
as an endpoint, then we can take a point

on

that is sufficiently close to

and apply Lemmas
7
and
8
to show that the concatenation of

and
\overline{vz}
can be replaced with

; see Figure
5
.
The case where another circular arc

with radius

has

as an endpoint can be handled with similar arguments.
∎

If we relax P1 and P3 to allow
d=2\alpha
and
\theta=\pi
, then P1–P5 are fulfilled by every optimal solution.
However, we disallow these corner cases because they lead to ambiguity: for two parallel edges with distance
2\alpha
, there are infinitely many ways to connect them with tangential circular arcs of radius
\alpha
and central angle
\pi
.
Without such arcs, we can use Proposition
10
to limit the number of inclusion-maximal free pieces that need to be inspected to find an optimal solution.

Definition 11
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be an instance of
P^{\alpha}_{F}
. For
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, let
F_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B})
denote the set of circular arcs
f=C_{\alpha}^{uv}
such that (1) the endpoints
u
and
v
lie on the input boundary, (2)
f
fulfills properties P1–P4 of
\Cref
prop:arc_properties, and (3)
f
does not intersect
\mathcal{B}
properly.
We define
F_{0}(\mathcal{B})=\emptyset
and
F_{\infty}(\mathcal{B})
as the set of straight-line segments
\overline{uv}
with
u,v\in V(\mathcal{B})
that do not intersect
\mathcal{B}
properly.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{C}
denote the subdivision of
\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})\setminus\mathcal{B}
induced by

and

, and let

denote the total number of vertices, edges and cells in

.

We show that

has polynomial size and is sufficient to construct an optimal solution.

Lemma 12
.

For an instance
\mathcal{B}
of problem
P^{\alpha}_{F}
with
|V(\mathcal{B})|=n
, the subdivision
\mathcal{D}_{C}
has size
|\mathcal{D}_{C}|\in\mathcal{O}{\left(n^{4}\right)}
and every optimal solution for
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}_{C}}
is an optimal solution for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
.

Proof Sketch.

It follows from
\Cref
prop:arc_properties that there is an optimal solution
\mathcal{S}
for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
whose inclusion-maximal free pieces are contained in
F_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B})
.
Since
\alpha
is fixed, this set contains at most two arcs per vertex-vertex pair, two per vertex-edge pair, and one per edge-edge pair.
With
|E(\mathcal{B})|=n
, we have
|F_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B})|\in\mathcal{O}{\left(n^{2}\right)}
.
Each pair of arcs in
F_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B})\cup E(\mathcal{B})
intersects properly at most a constant number of times, so the number of vertices, edges and cells in
\mathcal{D}_{C}
is in
\mathcal{O}{\left(n^{4}\right)}
.
By construction, every boundary piece in
\mathcal{S}
is a path in
\mathcal{D}_{C}
.
Hence,
\mathcal{S}
is a solution for
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}_{C}}
.
Every optimal solution for
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}_{C}}
is also a solution for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
.
To be optimal for
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}_{C}}
, it must have the same objective value as
\mathcal{S}
, so it is also optimal for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
.
∎

Using
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}_{C}}
, the existence of a polynomial-time algorithm for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
follows from
[
25
]
.

Theorem 13
.

For an instance
\mathcal{B}
of problem
P^{\alpha}_{F}
with
|V(\mathcal{B})|=n
, an optimal solution can be computed in
\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{C}|^{2}}{\log n}\right)}\subseteq\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{n^{8}}{\log n}\right)}
time.

Proof.

Because every polygon vertex appears in
\mathcal{D}_{C}
, we have
|\mathcal{D}_{C}|\in\Omega(n)
.
Hence, the subdivision can be constructed in
\mathcal{O}{\left(|\mathcal{D}_{C}|\log n\right)}\subseteq\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{C}|^{2}}{\log n}\right)}
time by computing all the intersection points of
F_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B})
and
E(\mathcal{B})
.
Then an optimal solution
\mathcal{S}
for
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}_{C}}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{C}|^{2}}{\log n}\right)}
time with the minimum cut-based approach proposed in
[
25
]
.
By
\Cref

P^{\alpha}_{F}
.
∎

Our experiments in Section
5
show that
|\mathcal{D}_{C}|
remains manageable for moderately small values of
\alpha
.
The nestedness of the solutions with respect to
\alpha
follows from the proof of Lemma 2 in
[
25
]
, which is applicable as long as intersections and unions of solutions also are solutions.

Corollary 14

(
\alpha
-nestedness)
.

For an instance
\mathcal{B}
of
P^{\alpha}_{F}
and two values
0\leq\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}
, let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
be the respective optimal solutions.
Then
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

Moreover, our problem exhibits an additional nesting property (proven in Appendix
B
):

Corollary 15

(subset-nestedness)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be two sets of input polygons, such that
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. For a given parameter
\alpha
, let
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}
be a solution for
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
minimizing
g_{\alpha}
. Then there exists a solution
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}}
minimizing
g_{\alpha}
for
\mathcal{B}
, such that
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Subset-nestedness also holds in the case where an input polygon grows, because this is equivalent to adding new polygons that touch the old one.
Algorithmically, subset-nestedness suggests that divide-and-conquer and incremental approaches may be promising.
This property is also useful when polygons are added sequentially (e.g., in historical data where buildings are added over time).
In this case, subset-nestedness implies nestedness over time.

4
The Straight-Line and Vertex-Restricted Problems

By
\Cref
theorem:algorithm the unrestricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{F}
can be solved in polynomial time.
For the subdivision-restricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}}
, this was already known
[
25
]
.
The computational complexity of the straight-line problem
P^{\alpha}_{L}
and the vertex-restricted straight-line problem
P^{\alpha}_{V}
remain open.
In
\Cref
section:ilp, we develop an integer linear program (ILP) for
P^{\alpha}_{V}
. While solving it yields optimal solutions, it may require exponential time. The ILP formulation leverages insights into the correspondence between optimal solutions for
P^{\alpha}_{V}
and triangulations, as also observed in
[
4
]
.
For the remainder of this section, we focus on approximating
P^{\alpha}_{L}
and
P^{\alpha}_{V}
up to a constant factor in polynomial time. Detailed proofs are given in Appendix
C
.

The approximation algorithm for
P^{\alpha}_{V}
starts by invoking the algorithm proposed in
\Cref
theorem:algorithm to obtain an optimal solution
\mathcal{S}_{0}
for the unrestricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{F}
.
The algorithm then operates in phases, each of which ensures that all free pieces satisfy a particular property.
After Phase 1, they must be straight-line segments.
Phase 2 ensures that at least one endpoint of each free piece is in
V(\mathcal{B})
, and Phase 3 ensures it for the second endpoint as well.
The approximation algorithm for
P^{\alpha}_{L}
is the same but stops after the first phase.

For each phase, we bound the increase in the objective value by a constant factor.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{i}
denote the solution after Phase
i
, and let
\mathcal{V}_{i}\subseteq F_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S}_{i-1})
denote the subset of maximal free pieces that violate the property we want to ensure in Phase
i
.
Each free piece
f\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
is replaced with new boundary pieces that are constrained or satisfy the property.
Let
\Delta(f)
be the change in the objective value that results from replacing
f
.
We show that
\Delta(f)
can be bounded by some constant
\kappa
multiplied with the lengths of some boundary pieces and areas of sub-regions in
\mathcal{S}_{i-1}
.
Furthermore, we ensure that none of these boundary pieces and sub-regions appear in the bound for any other violating free piece
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{i})=g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{i-1})+\sum_{f\in\mathcal{V}_{i}}\Delta(f)\leq(\kappa+1)\cdot g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{i-1}).

By multiplying the bounds for the different phases, we have that
g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{i})
is within a constant factor of
g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{0})
.
Because
g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{0})
is optimal for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
and the problems
P^{\alpha}_{L}
and
P^{\alpha}_{V}
are more restrictive, it is a lower bound for the optimal solution values.
We use the following notation throughout the remainder of the section:
For two curves
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
with coinciding start- and endpoints that do not intersect elsewhere, we denote their enclosed region by
G(s_{1},s_{2})
.

are circular, we have

be a circular arc connecting two points

Let

Lemma 16
.

Phase 1 produces a solution
\mathcal{S}_{1}

Proof Sketch.

We use
\ell(c)\leq\ell(f)
and
A(G({c},f))\leq A(G(\overline{uv},f))
.
Because

is the circle segment induced by

, its area is the product of

and a term

We obtain

∎

If we stop after Phase 1, we obtain an approximation algorithm for

Theorem 17
.

There exists a
c
-approximation algorithm (with

) for the straight-line aggregation problem
P^{\alpha}_{L}
with runtime
\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{n^{8}}{\log n}\right)}
.

Phase 2.

This phase enforces the property that every free piece has at least one endpoint in
V(\mathcal{B})
. Consequently, the violating free pieces
\mathcal{V}_{2}
are those that have both endpoints in the interior of polygon edges. Let
f=\overrightarrow{uv}
be a free piece that connects interior points
u
and
v
of two input edges
e_{u}
and
e_{v}
such that the enclosed area lies to the right of
f
.
We construct a new boundary piece
f^{\prime}
by starting out with

and then moving it in parallel and to the right of
f
.
We stop as soon as

touches an input vertex
y\in V(\mathcal{B})
; see Figure

.
The point

can either be one of the endpoints of
e_{u}
and
e_{v}
or some other input vertex.
Our transformation removes the quadrilateral
Q_{f}
bounded by the edges

,
f^{\prime}
,
t_{v}=\overline{vv^{\prime}}
, and
t_{u}=\overline{uu^{\prime}}
.
This changes the perimeter by replacing

with
f^{\prime}
,

and

.

Lemma 18
.

Phase 2 produces a solution
\mathcal{S}_{2}
for

in which every free piece

has length
\ell(f)<2\alpha
and one endpoint in

. Additionally,
g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{2})\leq 3g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{1})
holds.

Proof Sketch.

Let
f=\overrightarrow{uv}
be any free piece with an endpoint

in the interior of an input edge.
Because the interior angle
\beta
spanned by

and
f
at

is less than

(see Figure

), we have
\ell(f^{\prime})<\ell(f)<2\alpha
.
It also follows that when moving

in parallel, it must touch an input polygon before intersecting another free piece properly (see
f
and

in Figure

For the approximation guarantee, observe that
e_{u}
and

cannot be parallel, which follows from the proof for

. Hence, their induced lines

and

intersect at a point

to the right of
\overrightarrow{uv}

and

(see Figure

), the newly introduced perimeter pieces

,

\Delta(f)\leq\begin{cases}\begin{cases}2\alpha\ell(f)&\text{if }\gamma\geq\frac{\pi}{3}\\
\frac{5}{3}A(Q_{f})&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\end{cases}\leq 2(\alpha\ell(f)+A(Q_{f}))

and

Phase 3.

After Phase 2, all remaining free pieces have at least one endpoint in
V(\mathcal{B})
.
The violating free pieces
\mathcal{V}_{3}
for Phase 3 are those whose other endpoint lies in the interior of an input edge.
Unlike the previous phases, we do not handle each violating free piece individually.
Instead, we consider the set
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{2})
of constrained boundary pieces that are part of a single input polygon edge and inclusion-maximal among those.
Let
q=\overrightarrow{v_{1}u_{2}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{2})
be such a constrained piece.
If both endpoints are in
V(\mathcal{B})
, nothing needs to be done.
If exactly one endpoint
x
is in
V(\mathcal{B})
, we introduce the dummy violating free piece
f_{x}=\overrightarrow{xx}
of length
0
.
With this, we can assume that both endpoints of
q
are also endpoints of violating free pieces

and

, where
u_{1}
and
v_{2}
are input vertices (see

We define
p
as the curve given by the concatenation of
f_{1},q
and
f_{2}
. For
p
, we proceed similarly to Phase 1.
Let
G:=G(\overline{u_{1}v_{2}},p)
denote the region enclosed by
\overline{u_{1}v_{2}}
and
p
.
Moreover, let
C
denote the convex hull of the points in
V(\mathcal{B})
that lie in
G
(note that these include
u_{1}
and
v_{2}
).
If
C=\text{conv}(\{u_{1},v_{2}\})

with
c=\overline{u_{1}v_{2}}
.
Otherwise, we replace
p
with

.

Lemma 19
.

After Phase 3, we obtain a solution
\mathcal{S}_{3}
for

with
g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{3})\leq 3g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{2})
.

Proof Sketch.

Again, we have
\ell(c)\leq\ell(p)
. Thus, the increase

in the objective value is bounded by

. We enclose

in a rectangular box with base length

and height

.

.
The free pieces
f_{1}
and

only appear in the bound for

and

are input vertices.

.

5
Experimental Study

We experimentally evaluate prototypical implementations of our algorithms on real-world data, focusing on the runtime and the approximation quality.
For the geometric computations, we use the CGAL library
[
27
]
.
For the minimum cut computation, we use the max-flow algorithm by Boykov and Kolmogorov
[
5
]
, which is available in the Boost graph library
[
7
]
.
Although this algorithm does not have a strongly polynomial worst-case runtime, it is known to perform well on planar or near-planar instances, such as ours
[
5
]
.

Setup.

All experiments were run on a single thread of a machine with an AMD EPYC 7543P CPU, 32 cores and 256 GB RAM. The code was implemented in C++ and compiled with GCC 11.4.0.
For each instance, we sample
38
different
\alpha
-values: we use increments of
1
for
\alpha\in[0,20)
, and increments of
10
for
\alpha\in[20,200]
.
For each run with a fixed
\alpha
value, we set a timeout of
30
minutes.
If an algorithm times out for some
\alpha
, we do not run it for values
\alpha^{\prime}\geq\alpha
on the same instance.
This is based on the assumption that the size of the subdivision increases with
\alpha
, which our results support.

Instances and Examples.

Our instances are building footprints from villages in Western Germany, which were extracted from OpenStreetMap
[
23
]
and processed as described in
[
25
]
. We use ten instances given by villages with
333
–
1623
polygons with
1875
–
9457
vertices. Because our algorithms cannot solve these instances for all
\alpha
values in the allotted time, we consider a second dataset consisting of
10
diverse sub-regions extracted from the town of Euskirchen. These selections have
22
–
42
polygons with
186
–
636
vertices.
A full overview of the datasets is given in
\Cref
sec:data.
An example is given in Figure

, which shows the village of Bokeloh along with three superimposed optimal solutions for different values of
\alpha
.

Our theoretical analysis in Section

To assess its impact in practice, we plot the subdivision complexity as a function of

The maximum possible distance between the endpoints of the circular arcs is

Once

is the diameter of the vertex set of the input polygons, all possible circular arcs are considered, which explains the plateauing effect.
Beyond this point, the complexity of the subdivision only depends on the intersections of the circular arcs with each other and with the input polygons.
We note that our current implementation of the algorithm naively constructs the full subdivision and runs the minimum cut algorithm on it.
By leveraging the nesting properties and further structural investigation, we believe that the size of the subdivision can be reduced, making the approach scalable for larger instances.

6
Conclusion

We conclude the paper by outlining potential areas for future work.
For applications in time-variant polygon aggregation or urban analytics, our straightforward implementation of the exact algorithm for
P^{\alpha}_{F}
is not (yet) fast enough.
A next step would be to improve the runtime using algorithm engineering methods, for example by leveraging the nesting properties.
On the theoretical side, several questions remain open:

•

Can
P^{\alpha}_{V}
and
P^{\alpha}_{L}
be solved optimally in polynomial time?

•

Do the optimal solutions for
P^{\alpha}_{V}
and
P^{\alpha}_{L}
exhibit
\alpha
-nestedness? An interesting consequence of the
\alpha
-nestedness of
P^{\alpha}_{V}
would be that for any instance

Appendix B
The Unrestricted Polygon Aggregation Problem (Including Proofs)

In this section, we investigate the unrestricted problem
P^{\alpha}_{F}
. We begin by characterizing the shape of the free boundary pieces and show that only a polynomial number of curves need to be inspected to find an optimal solution. We then use these curves to build a subdivision

of

that has polynomial size, thereby reducing

to the subdivision-restricted problem

.
This allows us to apply the minimum cut-based algorithm proposed in
[
25
]
to solve the unrestricted problem in polynomial time.
Finally, we note that the optimal solutions to

are nested with respect to
\alpha
and with respect to introducing new input polygons.

We start by characterizing the optimal solutions for the (simpler) corner cases

and
\alpha=\infty

\alpha=0
, the solution that consists only of the input polygons is optimal since it minimizes the area. For
\alpha=\infty
, where the objective is to minimize the perimeter, we show that all maximal free pieces are straight-line segments connecting polygon vertices.

.

For an instance

of

Proof.

We show that in an optimal solution

, every region

is the convex hull of the polygons

contained in

.
Then, the claim follows.
Assume for the sake of contradiction that
S\neq\text{conv}(\mathcal{B}(S))
.
Then the perimeter satisfies

Let

be the set of regions that intersect

Next, we discuss the general case

We next show that our problem always has an optimal solution in which all boundary curves are rectifiable.

Proposition 24
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be an instance of the aggregation problem
P_{F}^{1}
. There exists a solution
\mathcal{S}
that minimizes
g_{1}(\mathcal{S})
, such that the boundary
\partial\mathcal{S}
is a collection of rectifiable curves.

Proof.

We show that the existence of a solution to the problem of minimizing
g_{1}(\mathcal{S})
is essentially a consequence of the Arzela-Ascoli theorem. To sketch the argument, consider a sequence of solutions
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, such that
g_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{k})
converges to the infimum of
g_{1}
. To simplify the situation, we consider only a single free piece of the boundary of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, described by a single curve
f_{k}
in the plane, possibly by passing over to a subsequence. For this, note that we can first of all assume that every set in
\mathcal{S}_{k}
has non-empty intersection with the convex hull
\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})
of the input polygons. Indeed, it suffices to note that replacing
\mathcal{S}_{k}
with the solution obtained from intersecting the sets in
\mathcal{S}_{k}
with
\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})
decreases both the area and also the perimeter (since the convex hull of a set of points has the minimum perimeter of all regions containing these points).
We note also that a closed free piece touching a polygon at at most one point cannot be optimal by
\Cref
lemma:local_structure and P3 of
\cref
prop:arc_properties:full. By approximating each
f_{k}
by a differentiable function, we can assume that each
f_{k}
is
C^{1}
and again by passing to a subsequence, we can assume that the arc-lengths of
f_{k}
are bounded. To apply the Arzela-Ascoli theorem, we need to show that the sequence
(f_{k})_{k}
of free pieces approaching a part of the infimum of
g_{1}
is both uniformly bounded and has uniformly bounded derivatives.

Since
f_{k}
is confined to the convex hull of the input polygons, the sequence
(f_{k})_{k}
is uniformly bounded. This also follows simply from the fact that the objective function contains the perimeter of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
and the perimeter values induced by
f_{k}
converge. This implies pointwise convergence of a subsequence (also denoted by
(f_{k})_{k}
) of
(f_{k})_{k}
to a limit function. In particular, again by passing to a subsequence, we can assume that the terms
f_{k}
join the same edges of
\mathcal{B}
. Assume that the sequence
(f_{k})_{k}
does not have uniformly bounded derivatives. By the previous Lemmas
7
,
8
, and
9
, we can locally improve each
f_{k}
by replacing (small) pieces by (short) circular arcs. The derivatives of these circular arcs are bounded (after the coordinate system is adjusted appropriately). By smoothing the resulting curve by an arbitrary small amount in the neighborhood of the points where the inserted circular arcs join with the original curve, we obtain a
C^{1}
curve that is closer to the infimum than the original curve. Furthermore, by inserting such pieces locally in the curves where necessary, we have eliminated regions where the curves
f_{k}
have diverging derivatives. As a result, we obtain a sequence with uniformly bounded derivatives and this implies that there a (sub)sequence that is uniformly bounded and uniformly equicontinuous. By the Arzela-Ascoli theorem, there is a subsequence that converges uniformly, implying continuity of the limit. By continuity of the limit for all free pieces, the enclosed area is well-defined, which also implies a well-defined perimeter, since the objective value is finite, so the free pieces are rectifiable curves.
∎

The approximation algorithm for

For each phase, we bound the increase in the objective value by a constant factor.
Let

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{i})=g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{i-1})+\sum_{f\in\mathcal{V}_{i}}\Delta(f)\leq(\kappa+1)\cdot g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{i-1}).

that also ends in the interior of
e_{v}
cannot intersect
f^{\prime}
because it would also intersect
c
.
∎

Lemma 32
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{1}
be the solution after Phase 1 and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
after Phase 2.
Then
g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{2})\leq 3g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{1})
.

Proof.

Let
f=\overrightarrow{uv}
be a violating free piece connecting the interiors of
e_{u}
and
e_{v}
. Let
f^{\prime}
be the result of the transformation, and let
t_{u}
and
t_{v}
the line segments on
e_{u}
and
e_{v}
between
f
and
f^{\prime}
. The edges
e_{u}
and
e_{v}
induce lines
l_{u}
and
l_{v}
that, as mentioned earlier, cannot be parallel.
The lines intersect to the right of
f
at some point
x
, forming an angle
\gamma
at
x
; see Figure
21
.
The angle
\gamma
and the central angle
\theta
of the circular arc are related by
\theta=\pi-\gamma
.
Let
s_{u}=\overline{ux}
and
s_{v}=\overline{vx}
.
Note that
\ell(t_{u})=\ell(t_{v})\leq\ell(s_{v})=\ell(s_{u})
.
We show that

\ell(t_{u})\leq\begin{cases}\ell(f)&\text{if }\gamma\geq\frac{\pi}{3},\\
\frac{4}{3\alpha}A(Q_{f})&\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

If
\gamma\geq\frac{\pi}{3}
, then it is the largest angle in the isosceles triangle
\triangle_{xuv}
.
Because
f
is the side opposing
\gamma
, it holds that
\ell(t_{u})\leq\ell(s_{u})\leq\ell(f)
.
For the case
\gamma<\frac{\pi}{3}
, we observe that
Q_{f}
is a trapezoid with parallel sides
f
and
f^{\prime}
and height
\cos(\gamma/2)\cdot\ell(t_{u})
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle A(Q_{f})=\frac{\cos(\gamma/2)\cdot\ell(t_{u})\cdot(\ell(f)+\ell(f^{\prime}))}{2}\geq\frac{\cos(\gamma/2)\cdot\ell(t_{u})\cdot\ell(f)}{2}.

For
\gamma\in[0,\frac{\pi}{3}]
, we have
\cos{(\frac{\gamma}{2})}\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
and
\ell(f)=2\alpha\sin\left(\frac{\pi-\gamma}{2}\right)\geq\sqrt{3}\alpha
, so the bound for
\ell(t_{u})
follows.

Moving the free piece
f
increases the objective value by

\Delta(f)=\alpha(\ell(t_{v})+\ell(t_{u})+\ell(f^{\prime})-\ell(f))-A(Q_{f})=\alpha(2\ell(t_{u})+\ell(f^{\prime})-\ell(f))-A(Q_{f}).

With
\ell(f^{\prime})\leq\ell(f)
and the above, we have

\Delta(f)\leq\begin{cases}\begin{cases}2\alpha\ell(f)-A(Q_{f})&\text{if }\gamma\geq\frac{\pi}{3}\\
\frac{5}{3}A(Q_{f})&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\end{cases}\leq 2(\alpha\ell(f)+A(Q_{f})).

Note that the proof of
\Cref
lemma:vertex_is_feasible_app implies that for two violating edges
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
the quadrilaterals
Q_{f_{1}}

Q_{f_{2}}
are interior-disjoint. Additionally, each violating free piece

appears only in the bound of its own local objective value change
\Delta(f)
. Hence, the overall increase in the objective value is

\displaystyle\Delta=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{V}_{2}}\Delta(f)\leq 2g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S}_{1})

and the approximation guarantee follows.

After Phase 2, all remaining free pieces have at least one endpoint in

. Additionally, the substitution of each curve

Let

Appendix D
An Integer Linear Program for the Vertex-Restricted Straight-Line Problem

In this section, we introduce an alternative characterization of the problem
P^{\alpha}_{V}
based on triangulations. Using this characterization, we present an integer linear program (ILP) that can be used to solve the problem optimally.

In a solution
\mathcal{S}
to
P^{\alpha}_{V}
, all inclusion-maximal boundary pieces
F_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S})
must be straight-line segments
f=\overline{uv}
connecting
u,v\in V(\mathcal{B})
.
Hence, for fixed
\mathcal{S}
, there exists a triangulation
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}
of
\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})\setminus\mathcal{B}
such that
F_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq E(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}})
. This was previously observed in the context of polygon simplification
[
4
]
. Consequently, if we knew which triangulation to select for a given
\alpha
, we could reduce our problem to the subdivision problem
P^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}}
. However, this triangulation is not known in advance. Nevertheless, using this correspondence, we can reformulate the problem as follows:

Definition 35

(An alternative definition of
P^{\alpha}_{V}
)
.

Given a set of polygons
\mathcal{B}
and a value
\alpha
, find a triangulation
\mathcal{D}^{*}
of
\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})\setminus\mathcal{B}
and a selection
\mathcal{X}^{*}
of triangles of
\mathcal{D}^{*}
such that
\mathcal{S}^{*}=\mathcal{X}^{*}\cup\mathcal{B}
minimizes
g_{\alpha}(\mathcal{S})
for all triangulations
\mathcal{D}
and selections
\mathcal{X}
of triangles.

This definition is the motivation for the ILP presented in the following. The ILP is able to choose a triangulation and a selection of triangles at the same time. The main challenge is the derivation of the edges that contribute to the objective function.

Before introducing the ILP, we need some notation. Let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of all possible edges
\overline{uv}
, where
u,v\in V(\mathcal{B})
, that are either in
E(\mathcal{B})
or have their interior fully contained in
\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})\setminus\mathcal{B}
.
Similarly,
\mathcal{T}
is the set of all possible triangles
t^{uvw}
with vertices
u,v,w\in V(\mathcal{B})
and interiors completely contained in
\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})\setminus\mathcal{B}
.
For an edge
e=\overrightarrow{uv}
, we define
H^{e}_{\text{left}}
(
H^{e}_{\text{right}}
) as the set of triangles
t^{uvx}\in\mathcal{T}
where the third vertex
x
is to the left (right) of the directed line from
u
to
v
. Note that we can assign indices to the vertices of
V(\mathcal{B})
, for example, using the input order. Then, unless otherwise stated, the edge
\overline{v_{i},v_{j}}
, with
i<j
, is directed from
i
to
j
.
An edge
e
may have adjacent triangles on only one side. For simplicity, we assume such edges are directed so that the triangles are always in
H^{e}_{\text{left}}
. We can partition the set
\mathcal{E}
into four disjoint subsets
\mathcal{E}=F\sqcup H\sqcup P\sqcup U
(see Figure
24
for examples of the different edge types):

•

the set
U
of all edges
e
such that
e\in E(\mathcal{B})
and
e\in\partial\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})
,

•

the set
P
of all edges
e
such that
e\in E(\mathcal{B})
and
e\not\in\partial\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})
,

•

the set
H
of all edges
e
such that
e\not\in E(\mathcal{B})
and
e\in\partial\text{conv}(\mathcal{B})
,

•

the set
F
of free edges, i.e,
F=\mathcal{E}\setminus H\cup P\cup U
.

We now introduce our ILP. For each triangle
t\in\mathcal{T}
, we define two variables:
t_{\text{active}}
and
t_{\text{inactive}}
. For each edge
e\in H\cup P\cup F
, we introduce a variable
e_{\text{active}}
and two auxiliary variables,
h^{e}_{\text{right}}
and
h^{e}_{\text{left}}
.
The objective function and constraints are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\text{maximize}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}t_{\text{active}}\cdot A(t_{\text{active}})

\displaystyle+\alpha\cdot\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}e_{\text{active}}\cdot\ell(e)\;\;\;{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}(+\;c_{\text{poly}})}

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in H_{\text{left}}^{e}}t_{\text{active}}+t_{\text{inactive}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in H_{\text{right}}^{e}}t_{\text{active}}+t_{\text{inactive}}

\displaystyle\forall e\in F

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in H_{\text{left}}^{e}}t_{\text{active}}+t_{\text{inactive}}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\forall e\in P\cup H

(4)

\displaystyle h^{e}_{\text{left}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in H_{\text{left}}^{e}}t_{\text{active}}

\displaystyle\forall e\in F\cup P\cup H

(5)

\displaystyle h^{e}_{\text{right}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in H_{\text{right}}^{e}}t_{\text{active}}

\displaystyle\forall e\in F

(6)

\displaystyle h^{e}_{\text{right}}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\forall e\in P

(7)

\displaystyle h^{e}_{\text{right}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall e\in H

(8)

\displaystyle e_{\text{active}}

\displaystyle=h^{e}_{\text{right}}\oplus h^{e}_{\text{left}}

\displaystyle\forall e\in H\cup P\cup F

(9)

\displaystyle t_{\text{active}}

\displaystyle,t_{\text{inactive}}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall t\in\mathcal{T}

(10)

The objective function is given as the
\alpha
-trade-off of the sum of areas of the active triangles and the lengths of the active edges. The constraints ensure that the active triangles form a valid triangulation and that the active edges bound the solution.

Constraints
3
and
4
are derived from the triangulation polytope constraints by de Loera, Hosten, Santos and Sturmfels
[
10
]
. They ensure that the set of triangles with
t_{\text{active}}=1
or
t_{\text{inactive}}=1
forms a valid triangulation. Constraint
4
ensures that each boundary edge (which is part of any triangulation) has exactly one adjacent triangle, while Constraint
3
ensures that each interior edge in the triangulation has exactly one adjacent triangle on both its left and right sides. The triangles with
t_{\text{active}}=1
are those selected for the optimal solution.
The remaining constraints determine the active perimeter. Constraints
5
and
6
specify if there is active area (given by triangles) on either side of an edge. Constraints
7
and
8
handle the special cases of polygon edges and convex hull edges. An edge
e\in H
always has inactive area to its right, as it lies on the convex hull, and an edge
e\in P
always has active area to its right, as it lies on the boundary of an input polygon. Note that we introduce Constraints
5
–
8
only to enhance the readability of the ILP.
An edge is part of the active perimeter if it is adjacent to inactive area on one side and active area on the other side; see Figure
24
. This is modeled with Constraint
9
, which is not linear.
However, an XOR constraint
z=x\oplus y
can be linearized as follows:

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\leq x+y

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\geq x-y

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\geq y-x

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\leq 2-y-x

Constraints
10
enforce the binary conditions for the triangle variables. Note that all other variables are implicitly binary as well.
Lastly, our solution does not account for the area of the input polygons
\mathcal{B}
or the perimeter defined by edges in
U
. The solutions remain correct, as the additional area and perimeter are included in every possible solution. If the exact objective value is required, a constant
c_{\text{poly}}=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}A(B)+\sum_{e\in U}\ell(e)
can be computed in a pre-processing step and added to the objective function.

Although the ILP may not run in polynomial time, it is a good starting point since the complexity of
P^{\alpha}_{V}
for
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
remains an open question. Note that the two boundary cases, i.e., minimizing only area or only perimeter, can be solved in polynomial time. Area minimization is achieved by not selecting any free boundary pieces, and perimeter minimization has been addressed in a fencing context
[
1
]
.
