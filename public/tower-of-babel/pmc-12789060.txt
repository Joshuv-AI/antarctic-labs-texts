Beyond colonoscopy, faecal DNA mutation screening provides a potential and viable path to early colorectal cancer detection
Authors: Alonzo Alfaro-Núñez, Stina Christensen, Jette Ellehauge, Jens Ole Eriksen, Gitte Andersen, Esther A Jensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, with early detection critical for improving clinical outcomes. In Denmark’s CRC screening program, nearly 60% of patients undergoing colonoscopy do not have polyps requiring treatment, highlighting the need for more sensitive and less invasive screening methods to improve early detection and reduce unnecessary procedures. In this study, we assessed the potential of two faecal DNA-based methods for detecting CRC-related mutations. Extracted DNA was analysed using next-generation sequencing (NGS) and digital PCR (dPCR). NGS targeted a gene panel covering the most frequently mutated sites in CRC, while dPCR focused on six specific CRC-associated mutations. A common limitation across both methods was the low abundance of human DNA in faecal samples, which reduced the reliability of variant detection. Nevertheless, both NGS and dPCR showed promise, particularly when DNA input was sufficient. Significant associations were observed between mutation counts and clinical diagnosis, with key CRC mutations reliably detected. The findings suggest that, with further implementation, these methods could serve as a less invasive and more cost-effective alternative to colonoscopy, especially for high-risk individuals. However, the sensitivity of both methods was limited in samples with low DNA yield, likely due to inhibitory effects during sample preservation or extraction. While faecal samples may be better suited for gut microbiome profiling, our findings underscore the potential of human DNA-based faecal screening in CRC and highlight the need for optimized extraction protocols to improve diagnostic accuracy, minimize unnecessary procedures, and provide personalized care for high-risk populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30802-y.Keywords: Colorectal cancer (CRC), Molecular markers, Gut microbiome, Gastrointestinal bleeding, Early detection, Non-invasive screening, Colonoscopy alternativeSubject terms: Cancer, Genetics, Molecular biology

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most diagnosed cancer worldwide1, with a similar prevalence observed in Denmark, where it ranks as the third most frequent malignancy2,3. Sporadic CRC is driven by a stepwise accumulation of somatic mutations and epigenetic alterations that ultimately lead to the malignant transformation of colonic epithelial cells4–8. In recent decades, the incidence of early-onset colorectal cancer—defined as CRC diagnosed in individuals under 50 years of age—has been rising9–12. Since CRC is often asymptomatic in the early stages, late-stage diagnosis is associated with high mortality rates13. Therefore, it is crucial to develop new strategies to identify risk factors and early tumour formation, enabling timely prevention, treatment, and improved patient outcomes14.

Screening programs have proven effective in reducing CRC-related mortality, prompting the introduction of a national screening initiative in Denmark in 2014. All citizens aged 50 to 74 are invited to participate by submitting a stool sample for analysis15,16. The program employs a faecal immunochemical test (FIT), which detects blood in stool using antibodies. A positive test result, defined by a cutoff value of ≥ 100 ng/L, leads to a follow-up colonoscopy within 14 days3. While colonoscopy remains the gold standard, it is invasive and often unnecessary; data from Region Zealand show that nearly 60% of individuals undergoing colonoscopy after a positive FIT do not have lesions requiring treatment17. In light of the challenges posed by traditional screening methods, the development of molecular diagnostics, such as faecal DNA-based screening, offers a promising alternative that could significantly improve early detection while reducing the number of unnecessary colonoscopies, particularly in high-risk populations such as those with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), previous CRC history, or genetic predispositions.

The integration of alternative screening methods that do not rely on the detection of occult blood in stool would be particularly advantageous for individuals with underlying conditions that predispose them to gastrointestinal bleeding, such as major forms of inflammatory bowel diseases (e.g., Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis), haemorrhoids, and peptic ulcers. Additionally, patients receiving anticoagulant therapy, chemotherapy, or radiation treatment are more likely to present with blood in their stool, increasing the risk of false-positive results in faecal blood-based screening protocols18. Thus, molecular diagnostics could serve as a complementary tool, potentially reducing unnecessary colonoscopies and providing more accurate and less invasive screening for individuals in these higher-risk groups.

Epithelial cells are continuously shed from the colonic mucosa into the intestinal lumen19, and their DNA can be extracted from faecal samples collected through routine FIT screening20–24. Studies have demonstrated that faecal-derived DNA can be analysed for somatic mutations, enabling the detection of both precancerous polyps and colorectal tumours21–24. In this study, we evaluated two alternative approaches for detecting colorectal cancer-related mutations from faecal-derived human DNA: short-read next-generation sequencing (NGS) and digital PCR (dPCR). The objective was to assess whether these methods could support a non-invasive screening strategy that does not rely on the detection of occult blood. Specifically, we tested whether somatic mutations could be reliably detected in faecal DNA and potentially serve as markers for the presence of moderate- to high-risk polyps or malignant tumours, thereby guiding referral for colonoscopy25,26. The detection of mutations in stool DNA could facilitate personalized treatment strategies, enabling tailored prevention plans for individuals at high risk of developing CRC based on genetic and molecular markers.

Materials and methods

Faecal samples were collected from patients in Region Zealand who tested positive in the Danish national colorectal cancer (CRC) screening programme between January 2022 and January 2023 (FIT ≥ 100 ng/mL)3. All participants provided informed consent. Two additional CRC cases and seven tumour biopsy samples were included for comparison and validation purposes (Table 1). All participants were instructed to collect the faecal sample prior to initiating bowel cleansing, and this was confirmed during enrolment. The average time interval between sample collection and colonoscopy was 3 to 5 days, depending on appointment scheduling.

Faecal DNA was extracted using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen) with RNase treatment27. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumour samples were processed with the GeneRead DNA FFPE Kit (Qiagen). Sequencing read depth and mapping efficiency statistics are summarised in Supplementary S1.

For next-generation sequencing (NGS), libraries were prepared with the QIAseq Targeted DNA Pro kit (Qiagen), targeting regions commonly mutated in CRC (e.g., KRAS, APC, TP53, BRAF, PIK3CA)28. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiniSeq platform. Library quality and sequencing performance were evaluated by assessing average read depth, on-target mapping percentage, and total reads per sample. Thresholds for downstream variant calling required a minimum of 100 reads mapped to the target region and ≥ 1% on-target mapping rate, in accordance with established faecal DNA sequencing protocols26. Variant calling was done using CLC Genomics Workbench software (Qiagen), with variants filtered based on minimum quality and coverage thresholds.

Digital PCR (dPCR) was performed using the QIAcuity Probe PCR Kit and a panel targeting six validated single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) in CRC driver genes (including KRAS G12V, G12D, G13D; BRAF V600E)29,30. Detailed information about the targeted sequencing panel and dPCR assays, including mutational targets and coordinates, is provided in Supplementary S2. Reactions were carried out on a QIAcuity Digital PCR System. Each sample was analysed in duplicate, and a mutation call required a minimum of two positive partitions per replicate. Based on previous studies showing improved accuracy with higher DNA input31, we implemented a conservative quality threshold requiring > 200 wild-type (WT) partitions per reaction to ensure detection confidence. For group comparisons of mutation burden, participants were categorised into two groups: those with lesions requiring treatment (i.e., cancer, high-risk and moderate-risk polyps) and those without (low-risk polyps or clean colon). As data were not normally distributed, the Mann–Whitney U test was used32. Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to compare WT partition counts across diagnostic groups (cancer, high-risk, moderate-risk, low-risk, clean colon), followed by Dunn’s post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction33.

Diagnostic accuracy measures — prevalence, sensitivity, and positive predictive value (PPV) — were calculated using colonoscopy-confirmed findings as the gold standard, following standard diagnostic test evaluation frameworks34–36.

All statistical analyses, statistical plots and data visualisations were conducted and generated in a combination of R (v 4.4.2) and Python 3.11 using Matplotlib (v3.7), Seaborn (v0.12), Pandas (v1.5), and NumPy (v1.24). Volcano plots were generated to illustrate differential mutation frequencies. The log₂ fold change (log₂FC) was calculated by comparing the observed mutation frequency against a fixed background frequency of 5% or against published reference values. –log₁₀(p-values) were derived using Fisher’s exact test or binomial probability tests, depending on data availability and comparison type. Three volcano panels were constructed to support different analytical aims: A) to visualise mutations detected in this study alone; (B) to enhance statistical power by integrating prevalence values from indexed peer-reviewed studies; and (C) to provide a theoretical reference by including simulated mutation data. The simulated values were generated computationally and not derived from faecal samples. Specifically, synthetic log₂FC values were sampled from a uniform distribution between − 3 and + 3, and corresponding p-values were drawn from a log-uniform distribution ranging from 10−4 to 1. Simulated points were filtered to avoid overlap with actual mutation coordinates and served purely illustrative purposes — to recreate a canonical V-shaped volcano distribution and enable visual interpretation of effect size and statistical signal. These values were used only for the comparative plotting panel and excluded from any diagnostic or statistical performance analyses.

A simple linear regression analysis was applied to assess the relationship between patient age (ranging from ≥ 50 to 74 years) and the total number of somatic mutations detected per patient. The model provided estimates of slope, intercept, R-squared (coefficient of determination), and p-value.

Although the Danish CRC screening programme officially covers individuals aged 50–74, a small number of patients outside this range were included due to tumour-based reference samples and opportunistic stool collection during follow-up colonoscopy. These samples were retained for exploratory analysis and do not affect the main screening conclusions.

Results

Of the 71 sequencing DNA libraries generated from faecal samples, only three yielded more than 100 coverage, highlighting the challenge of recovering host DNA from stool material due to high gut microbiome content and inhibitory substances34. Sequencing of matched tumour biopsy samples and FFPE-derived controls confirmed expected high coverage and detection of oncogenic variants (e.g., KRAS G12D, BRAF V600E), validating the NGS panel design and analytical pipeline. Across the 33 patients successfully analysed, a total of 163 faecal DNA variants were detected by either NGS or dPCR. Of these, 21 were classified as oncogenic or likely oncogenic in colorectal cancer, and five were pathogenic variants not directly linked to CRC37. Classification was based on known CRC hotspot mutations (e.g., KRAS G12D, BRAF V600E) and variant annotations provided by the CLC Genomics Workbench output. Mutation burden differed significantly across diagnostic groups: patients with high-risk polyps exhibited the highest number of variants per sample (mean: 5.9), while patients with low-risk polyps had the lowest (mean: 0.1)38. When grouped by treatment need, patients with treatable lesions had an average of 3.61 mutations, significantly higher than the 0.65 mutations observed in the no-treatment group (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.0125)32 (Table 3).

Within-sample replicability was low for NGS-detected mutations, with variant detection differing between technical duplicates in several cases. This inconsistency suggests stochastic dropout, likely attributable to low template concentration or sample-specific inhibitors39.

Digital PCR results were stratified by input DNA quality. In the complete set (n = 692 reactions), 276 reactions yielded fewer than 100 WT partitions and were excluded. Applying a ≥ 200 WT partition quality threshold increased both sensitivity and confidence in mutation detection31. The prevalence of common CRC driver mutations in our faecal samples was consistent with prior tumour-based reports. For instance, KRAS G12D and G12V were each detected in 15.1% of patient samples, while BRAF V600E was not detected in faecal samples but appeared in tumour tissue controls40. These frequencies aligned with reported ranges in The Cancer Genome Atlas and other large-scale CRC sequencing efforts41. These frequencies are further detailed in Supplementary S3, including patient-level detection and classification data. Significant variation in WT partition counts was observed across diagnostic categories (Kruskal–Wallis χ² = 52.236, df = 4, p < 0.001). Dunn’s post hoc tests showed that cancer patients had significantly more WT partitions than patients in other groups (p < 0.002 for all comparisons)33. However, partition counts did not correlate linearly with increasing disease severity across groups, suggesting that while cancer patients had significantly higher DNA input, this trend did not hold for intermediate lesion categories, potentially reflecting biological variability in epithelial shedding or DNA preservation.

Diagnostic performance was evaluated by comparing detection outcomes with colonoscopy-confirmed findings. Illumina NGS had a sensitivity of 57.1% and PPV of 100%. dPCR applied to all samples yielded 38.6% sensitivity and 94.4% PPV, while dPCR applied only to samples with > 200 WT partitions increased sensitivity to 80.0% with a similar PPV of 94.1%35. These findings (Table 2) highlight the impact of input DNA quality on performance.

Discussion

Colorectal cancer (CRC) screening programs are implemented globally, but the approaches vary widely in terms of testing strategies and clinical workflows44. Most European countries rely on faecal occult blood tests (FOBT), either guaiac-based (gFOBT) or faecal immunochemical tests (FIT), while some nations have adopted colonoscopy as the primary screening modality45. FIT has been widely adopted, but their reliance on the presence of occult blood may limit sensitivity, particularly for detecting adenomas or early-stage malignancies44–46. Our study aimed to assess the feasibility of two faecal DNA-based methods—Illumina next-generation sequencing (NGS) and digital PCR (dPCR)—to detect CRC-associated mutations. The incorporation of molecular screening tools into national or regional CRC screening programs has the potential to significantly increase participation rates, especially for individuals who are hesitant to undergo colonoscopy. We demonstrate that both next-generation sequencing (NGS) and digital PCR (dPCR) can reliably identify oncogenic mutations in faecal samples, offering a promising complementary or stand-alone strategy for non-invasive CRC screening.

Importantly, our study cohort included not only patients with confirmed colorectal cancer but also individuals with moderate- and high-risk adenomas, which are recognised as precancerous lesions within the adenoma–carcinoma sequence4,5,8. These groups are a critical focus for effective screening programmes, as early identification and removal of such lesions can prevent progression to invasive cancer. We observed that the mutation burden was significantly higher in patients with high-risk and moderate-risk polyps compared to those with low-risk findings or clean colons (Table 3). This suggests that faecal DNA mutation detection can capture relevant molecular signals prior to the development of overt cancer, aligning with the goals of early detection. Although low DNA yield remains a challenge in most of the samples, several patients with moderate-risk lesions yielded sufficient quality DNA for successful mutation detection, indicating that high tumour burden is not a prerequisite for assay sensitivity. These findings support the feasibility of faecal DNA screening not only for advanced cancers but also for clinically actionable early-stage lesions.

Consistent with previous studies47,48, the mutational spectrum observed in our faecal DNA samples includes hallmark mutations in key CRC driver genes such as KRAS, BRAF, TP53, and PIK3CA. The inter-individual variation in gene-level mutation patterns is visualised in Fig. 2, which reveals the heterogeneity of faecal mutation profiles, based on actual detected variants across both NGS and dPCR assays. This observation aligns with the established genetic diversity of CRC and supports the rationale for designing assays that cover multiple gene targets20,24.

Furthermore, our comparison of faecal and tumour biopsy mutation profiles showed high concordance for frequently mutated genes, suggesting that faecal DNA could serve as a proxy for tumour genotyping in most cases. Figure 3 demonstrates this overlap, supporting the notion that non-invasive faecal sampling may serve as a molecular surrogate for biopsy in selected clinical scenarios. This finding corroborates recent reports validating faecal DNA as a minimally invasive source for tumour-derived biomarkers21.

The diagnostic performance of NGS and dPCR was evaluated in terms of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) using colonoscopy as the gold standard. Our results show that NGS achieved a sensitivity of 57.1% and a specificity of 100%, while dPCR using samples with ≥ 200 wild-type (WT) partitions improved sensitivity to 80%. However, specificity decreased to approximately 75%, and the positive predictive value (PPV) correspondingly declined to 91%. These metrics are summarised in Fig. 5, highlighting the diagnostic strengths of each method. These values are comparable to, or in some cases exceed, those reported for FIT-based methods49,50, highlighting the clinical potential of faecal mutation profiling as a complementary diagnostic strategy.

While somatic mutation burden is generally thought to increase with age, particularly in cancer51, our dataset showed a weak inverse association (R² = 0.05), with fewer faecal DNA mutations detected in older individuals. One possible explanation is that younger patients may have presented with more advanced molecular lesions, leading to greater epithelial shedding or inflammatory turnover and thus higher mutation release into stool. Alternatively, reduced DNA quality or yield in older participants may have hindered detection. This inverse trend may also be stochastic, as the low R² indicates that age explains only a small fraction of the observed variance. Although not statistically significant, this observation contrasts with the established concept of age-associated somatic mutation accumulation and may carry implications for age-adjusted screening thresholds52. Nonetheless, it raises the possibility that younger individuals are being diagnosed earlier, potentially due to proactive colonoscopy use or stool-based mutation detection, which could support future age-adjusted screening approaches.

To explore mutation enrichment and visualise statistical signal, we constructed a volcano plot analysis (Fig. 4). In Panel A, based solely on our study data, a limited number of mutations surpassed the significance threshold. Panel B integrates mutation frequency data from peer-reviewed literature, resulting in stronger statistical signals for key variants such as KRAS G12V, BRAF V600E, and APC R1450. Panel C extends this by simulating additional values to create an idealised V-shaped distribution—a visual reference for interpreting enrichment and dispersion in the dataset. Real mutations remain highlighted, while simulated data are clearly annotated as such. This integrative approach underscores the value of combining empirical data with curated mutation prevalence reports53,54.

While promising, our findings are subject to several limitations. A key challenge was the consistently low yield of human DNA from faecal samples, which is likely due to both biological factors and practical aspects of sample handling. In Denmark’s CRC screening programme, patients must undergo colonoscopy within two weeks of a positive FIT result15, and some may provide stool samples post–bowel preparation, further reducing human DNA content. Despite using a targeted gene panel for NGS, most reads failed to map to the human genome due to overwhelming microbial background, consistent with previous findings that host DNA often constitutes less than 5% of total faecal DNA50–52,55–57. This low host DNA input likely reduced the efficiency of hybridisation-based capture, increasing the likelihood of off-target binding and non-specific amplification of bacterial DNA. Similar artefacts have been observed in low-input faecal sequencing protocols, where microbial DNA fragments are inadvertently enriched due to GC content bias or partial homology with probe sequences58. As a result, only a small subset of samples achieved adequate coverage (> 100×) for reliable variant detection, thereby reducing the effective sample size for NGS-based analyses. This represents a key limitation of our study. Future work should focus on optimising DNA preservation, extraction, and host-enrichment protocols to improve sequencing efficiency and mutation calling. For dPCR, however, we observed a more robust performance. Applying a quality threshold of > 200 wild-type (WT) partitions significantly improved sensitivity (80%) and yielded a high positive predictive value (PPV) of 94.1%. This suggests that quality control metrics can effectively triage samples and enhance test reliability. Nonetheless, even with this threshold, 4 of 20 patients with confirmed CRC were missed, reinforcing the need for further optimisation.

CRC is genetically heterogeneous, both within and between patients20,21. Even well-established mutations like KRAS or BRAF may not be present in all tumours (Figs. 2 and 4), and limiting detection to a small number of loci may reduce diagnostic sensitivity (Fig. 5). This limitation is inherent in targeted assays like dPCR, which cannot survey the full mutational landscape. Broader panels or whole-genome approaches may offer improved sensitivity but would require solutions to address faecal DNA degradation and background noise.

When comparing mutations detected in faecal DNA with those identified in matched tumour biopsies (Fig. 3), we observed partial concordance: 4 of 7 tumour mutations were verified in faecal DNA, while additional mutations were found only in the stool samples. These discrepancies could reflect tumour heterogeneity or contributions from other tumour sites but also raise questions about specificity. Nevertheless, the detection of mutations not found in biopsies suggests that faecal DNA analysis might capture a broader spectrum of tumour-associated mutations, potentially offering a more comprehensive diagnostic tool for CRC screening.

As previously mentioned, colonoscopy (with histopathology) is universally considered the gold standard for CRC detection and classification. Accordingly, non-invasive tests are evaluated by comparing their results to colonoscopy findings. In the literature, stool DNA tests’ performance is typically benchmarked against colonoscopy diagnosis. For example, Imperiale et al. reported that using colonoscopy-confirmed neoplasms as the reference, a multitarget stool DNA test achieved 92% sensitivity for CRC, significantly higher than the 74% sensitivity of the faecal immunochemical test (FIT)23. This exemplifies how sensitivity (and specificity) is computed with colonoscopy as the truth standard. Positive predictive value (PPV)—the probability that a positive test truly indicates disease—is also assessed relative to colonoscopy outcomes. Studies in screening populations show stool DNA tests have substantial PPV. In one large clinical series, 16% of screened individuals tested positive by a stool DNA assay, and among those, roughly two-thirds had neoplasia confirmed on follow-up colonoscopy (overall PPV ≈ 67% for any colorectal neoplasia)21,48. Even when focusing strictly on advanced lesions, colonoscopy-based evaluations find meaningful PPVs (e.g. ~27% for advanced adenomas and cancer in one analysis)23. It is important to note that PPV is influenced by disease prevalence in the cohort. Colonoscopy findings establish this prevalence: for instance, in a 9,989-patient trial, colonoscopy identified CRC in 0.7% and advanced precancerous lesions in 7.6% of individuals23. These figures define the context in which any non-invasive test operates. Using colonoscopy as the gold standard thus allows accurate calculation of sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and negative predictive value, grounded in the actual prevalence of CRC/adenomas. Studies consistently follow this framework, validating that our evaluation of diagnostic performance—using colonoscopy-based classification as the gold standard—is the correct and standard approach. In summary, measuring test sensitivity and PPV against colonoscopy-determined diagnoses is not only appropriate but expected in CRC screening research, ensuring that performance metrics are aligned with the true disease status.

Digital PCR (dPCR) assays, including droplet digital PCR, rely on partitioning the sample into many reactions (micro-droplets). The number of total (or wild-type) partitions is directly tied to assay sensitivity and statistical confidence—too few partitions can lead to unreliable results52–54. For this reason, researchers impose minimum partition count thresholds as a quality control measure. This practice is well-documented in the literature. For example, an Oxford clinical chemistry study excluded any dPCR sample wells that produced fewer than 10,000 total droplets, reasoning that low droplet counts (due to generation errors or inhibition) cannot yield trustworthy quantification52. Similarly, when detecting rare targets against a wild-type background, having enough wild-type (negative) partitions is critical to confidently call a true negative. A droplet PCR assay for SARS-CoV-2 set explicit criteria: a result was deemed invalid if a supposedly negative sample had fewer than 10,000 negative (wild-type) droplets, or if a positive had < 6,000 total droplets53. This ensures that a negative result isn’t simply due to insufficient sample or partitions. In the context of CRC stool DNA dPCR, thresholding quality based on wild-type partitions (e.g. requiring > 200 WT partitions) follows the same principle, albeit with a more lenient cutoff. It provides a safeguard that the assay had enough total DNA templates (as reflected in wild-type counts) to reliably detect mutations if present. While many published studies use thresholds in the thousands (owing to typically high partition yields in blood/plasma assays)52,53, the underlying concept is identical. Indeed, digital PCR experts note that more partitions yield higher resolution and sensitivity54. Thus, setting a minimum of > 200 wild-type partitions is a justified QC step to ensure data quality in a faecal DNA dPCR test. Studies precedents confirm that implementing a partition-count threshold is a valid approach to maintain result integrity in mutation detection assays53. In conclusion, the study’s criterion (> 200 WT partitions) is supported by the broader dPCR literature to guarantee sufficient analytical sensitivity and reliable negative results.

Ultimately, our results suggest that while NGS and dPCR show promise for non-invasive CRC screening, they are not yet suitable as standalone replacements for FIT or colonoscopy. The main limitation is not the molecular methods themselves, but rather the quality and consistency of faecal DNA input. We speculate that components in the preservation buffer solution may have contributed to reduced detection efficiency. Future studies should focus on optimizing sample collection protocols, buffer formulations, and DNA extraction methods to improve yield and reduce inhibition. Enhanced protocols will be essential for achieving the sensitivity required for accurate CRC screening.

Each of the methods used in the CRC faecal DNA study—from nonparametric statistical tests (Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s post hoc) to colonoscopy-referenced diagnostic metrics and dPCR quality thresholds—is well-supported by prior research. Literature demonstrates clear precedent and appropriateness for these choices, confirming that the study’s methodology aligns with accepted practices in colorectal cancer biomarker diagnostics52–54.

Despite these caveats, our study provides compelling evidence that mutation-based faecal DNA screening is feasible and may augment current CRC detection strategies. Future large-scale studies are needed to validate these findings and define standardised workflows, cut-off thresholds, and cost-effectiveness in real-world settings. While molecular screening methods such as NGS and dPCR are relatively affordable (estimated at ~€270 per test in Denmark), their cost-effectiveness as a triage tool depends on accurate identification of high-risk individuals who would otherwise undergo unnecessary colonoscopy. If implemented selectively, such methods could optimise resource use by reducing overtreatment and improving early cancer detection. However, a formal health-economic analysis is needed to quantify these benefits, particularly when factoring in downstream treatment costs and patient compliance. While current sensitivity remains suboptimal, the high PPV of dPCR under stringent quality control is encouraging. Our findings contribute to a growing body of literature supporting the transition toward precision diagnostics in cancer screening. When implemented within a personalised framework, faecal DNA mutation analysis may reduce unnecessary colonoscopies and enable early detection of high-risk lesions. Addressing barriers to sample quality will be key to realizing the full potential of faecal DNA testing.
