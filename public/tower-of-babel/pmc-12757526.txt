Anaphylaxis and pregnancy: Epidemiology, elicitors, and recurrences☆
Authors: Luciana Kase Tanno, Pham Thao Van Luong, Megane Dieval, Caroline Dunoyer, Emma Di Meglio, Florent Fuchs, Nicolas Molinari, Isabella Annesi-Maesano, Pascal Demoly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnaphylaxis during pregnancy implies a risk to both mothers and newborns. However, limited data are available. Therefore, our aim is to explore patterns of hospital admissions diagnosed with anaphylaxis in pregnant women in France from 2012 to 2021, using a national hospital billing database and to evaluate potential determinants, trends and recurrences.MethodsDescriptive study using data from the French Program for the Medicalization of Information Systems, from 2012 to 2021. We included all patients hospitalized for anaphylaxis using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes listed as a primary diagnosis. Two groups of patients were included: pregnant (PW) and non-pregnant women (NPW), adjusted by age. Severe anaphylaxis (SA) was defined based on the need of admissions in intensive care units. Sankey graphics were employed to visualize data related to recurrences.ResultsDuring a nine-year study period, 37,962 female patients had 42,399 admissions due to anaphylaxis for all causes. We evaluated data from 734 admissions for anaphylaxis experienced by 653 PW. In both groups 65% of cases were unspecified anaphylaxis, with high number of recurrences. PW experienced higher proportion of SA (8.7%, 95%CI [6.8%, 11.1%]) and recurrences (1.51, 95%CI [1.3, 1,7]), compared with NPW. Most of SA in PW was triggered by drugs (23%, 95%CI [22.3%, 23.6%]). Different from NPW, no seasonal trend was observed in the PW group. From all 670 PW who experienced non-SA, 0.33% (95% CI 0.09–1.2) evolved with SA, and 0.92% (95% CI 0.42–1.99) were admitted for different etiologies in forthcoming admissions.ConclusionBeyond providing a snapshot of characteristics of anaphylaxis during pregnancy in France, we were able to explore trends and recurrence of anaphylaxis in this population. Although further research is needed in the field, we strongly believe that the data presented contributed as a starting point of public health advancements to ensure quality of care of patients suffering from anaphylaxis.Keywords: Anaphylaxis, Epidemiology, Hospitalization, International classification of diseases, Pregnancy, Risk factors, Severe anaphylaxis

Background

Anaphylaxis is a non-communicable life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction, which requires prompt recognition and immediate treatment.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Anaphylaxis can occur at virtually any time of the life cycle. It requires special attention when it occurs in vulnerable populations, such as in pregnant women since it implies a potential risk of fatality to both mother and fetus/newborn and can lead to sequelae in newborns related to brain damage.7 Understanding characteristics, trends, and recurrences of anaphylaxis during pregnancy can support improvements in care, tailor public health actions to reduce the morbidity and mortality, and prevent avoidable deaths.

During pregnancy, anaphylaxis is rare, with an estimated incidence of 1.6–2.7 per 100,000 deliveries, but it is associated with significant morbidity for mother and the fetus.8, 9, 10, 11 The specific anaphylaxis-related maternal mortality rate was estimated to be 0.09 per 100,000 live births (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.01–0.30) over the period 2012 to 2014 in the United Kingdom.8 A recent European study reported that anaphylaxis had very poor outcomes for 1 in 7 mothers, and 1 in 7 babies; the maternal fatality rate was 3.2% (95%CI 0.4–11.0) and the neonatal encephalopathy rate was 14.3% (95%CI 4.8–30.3).12

Despite its clinical and public health relevance, there is only a paucity of articles addressing data on anaphylaxis during pregnancy.9 There is also a lack of information regarding seasonality or recurrences based on official statistics.

The most robust data sets currently available are based largely on hospital admissions, and emergency room (ER) datasets can help to ensure data consistency. In France, all hospitalization data are systematically collected by the national Program for the Medicalization of Information Systems (PMSI) and codified with the International Classification of Diseases (ICD),13, 14, 15 currently the ICD-10, in both public and private sectors. But, up to now, there is no study in France to explore hospital admissions related to anaphylaxis during pregnancy in the view of understanding its determinants, trends, and recurrences. Therefore, our aim was to explore patterns of hospital admissions diagnosed with anaphylaxis in pregnant women in France from 2012 to 2021, using the PMSI database and to evaluate potential determinants, trends and, recurrences.

Methods

Type of study, data source, and collection

Our study utilized administrative health data from the French Programme de Médicalisation des Systèmes d’Information (PMSI) over the period 2012–2021. The PMSI is nationwide electronic health information system that comprehensively captures hospital admissions across all healthcare institutions in metropolitan France, thereby achieving full population coverage.14 The dataset is fully anonymized and contains no personal identifiable information. All data follow the diagnostic coding process, currently using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), to identify the primary diagnosis indicating the principal reason for admission, and the secondary data, such as comorbidities.

From overall 4,021,488 total number of hospitalization records extracted for the time of the study, cases of anaphylaxis during pregnancy were identified based on predefined anaphylaxis-related ICD-10 codes identified as the primary diagnosis (T78, T78.0, T78.2, T78.3, T80.5, T88.2, T88.6).10 Secondary variables were derived to assess coexisting medical conditions (Supplementary Table S1).

The analytic cohort was stratified into 2 subgroups: (i) pregnant women hospitalized for anaphylaxis, and (ii) non-pregnant women aged 13–49 years hospitalized for anaphylaxis, enabling age-standardized comparisons. Subsequent analyses were conducted based on the etiology and clinical severity of the anaphylactic episodes (Fig. 1). Etiological categories included drug/iatrogenic, food, insect venom, and unspecified triggers. Severity categorization was operationalized as follows: (i) non-severe anaphylaxis (NSA), encompassing mild to moderate clinical presentations; (ii) severe anaphylaxis (SA), defined by admissions to intensive or critical care units; and (iii) fatal anaphylaxis, limited to cases where in-hospital mortality occurred. Deaths that transpired outside of healthcare settings and were later certified in emergency departments were excluded from this classification. Recurrence of anaphylaxis was defined as having more than 1 recorded hospital admission with anaphylaxis listed as the primary diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0).

Descriptive analyses comprised annual rates of hospitalization and mortality due to anaphylaxis per 100,000 population, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated for each year using the Wilson score method. Population denominators were based on yearly estimates from the French National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies (INSEE).13 Categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-square tests, while the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was employed for continuous variables and categorical variables with more than 2 categories.

To assess the temporal influence of year and month of exposure on hospital admissions for anaphylaxis—both among pregnant and non-pregnant individuals—as well as on admissions classified as severe across different etiological categories, separate modeling approaches were employed to account for long-term trends and seasonal variation (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Annual trends were evaluated using Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) with a Poisson distribution, adjusted for age group. Incidence Rate Ratios (IRRs) and corresponding 95% CIs were calculated to estimate the association between year of admission and the incidence of anaphylaxis overall and by trigger. Model fit was assessed using the Pseudo R-squared statistic, with values greater than 0.75 indicating strong model performance.

Seasonal distribution of anaphylaxis by trigger in pregnant and non-pregnant patients. Data from the French Program for the Medicalization of Information Systems (PMSI), 2012–2021.

Seasonal (monthly) trends were analyzed using Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) with a Poisson distribution, also adjusted for age groups. Model performance was evaluated using the adjusted R-squared.

Poisson regression models were applied to examine potential risk factors associated with different anaphylaxis triggers (Table 2) and severity levels. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05. Additional modeling was performed to evaluate the effects of year- and month-level exposures on the incidence of each etiological subtype and across severity strata (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) with a Poisson distribution were used to analyze annual trends, while Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) with a Poisson distribution were applied to capture monthly patterns. Where appropriate, models were adjusted for age and sex. Model adequacy was assessed using adjusted R-squared values. Furthermore, an unsupervised learning algorithm was applied post hoc to detect latent structures or clustering patterns within the dataset.

Recurrence analysis was conducted by evaluating the number of admissions per patient, stratified by trigger and severity, across both pregnant and non-pregnant cohorts. Visualization of recurrence pathways was performed using Sankey diagrams generated in Power BI.

Economic evaluation of hospitalizations was based on the mean cost per admission for anaphylaxis, calculated on a per capita basis.

Results

Anaphylaxis admissions rates and trends

Over the 9-year study period, a total of 37,962 female patients accounted for 42,399 hospital admissions due to anaphylaxis from all causes, corresponding to a mean of 1.11 admissions per patient. Among these, 734 admissions were recorded for 653 pregnant women, and 17,585 admissions for 15,752 non-pregnant women (Fig. 1). The mean number of anaphylaxis admissions per patient was comparable between pregnant women (1.12 [95% CI: 1.00–1.23]) and non-pregnant women (1.11 [95% CI: 1.10–1.13]). The average annual hospitalization rate for anaphylaxis over the study period was 0.11 per 100,000 female population, while admissions for severe anaphylaxis (SA) occurred at a rate of 0.06 per 100,000 per year. Among non-pregnant women, the admission rate for anaphylaxis was 6.57 per 100,000 female population per year. There were 21 anaphylaxis-related deaths (0.1%) among non-pregnant women, whereas no fatalities were reported among pregnant women (Fig. 1).

GLM with Poisson regression to analyze trends in hospitalization related to anaphylaxis over the years of the study demonstrated that pregnant patients presented with a significant decreasing trend of unspecified anaphylaxis hospitalizations, with a mean trend of −5.2% per year (95%CI: 8.1, 2.2). Conversely, for non-pregnant patients, a significant trend was observed in the overall number of hospitalizations overtime, with a mean increasing trend of +0.7% per year (95%CI: 0.2, 1.2). Hospitalizations associated with specific triggers for non-pregnant women also showed significant trend for hospitalizations to drug-related anaphylaxis, with a mean increasing trend of +4% per year (95%CI: 2.9, 5.1); insect-related anaphylaxis admissions had a mean increasing trend of +6.7% per year (95%CI: 3.3, 10.2), food-related anaphylaxis admissions had a mean increasing trend of +17% per year (95%CI: 15, 19), and unspecified anaphylaxis related admissions had a mean decreasing trend of −2.6% per year (95%CI: 3.3, −2). Hospitalizations due to SA showed a significant upward trend for non-pregnant patients of +6.6% per year (95%CI: 4.3, 9). It was observed a significant trend of admissions for SA among non-pregnant women triggered by food (+14.4%), unspecified causes (+4.8%) and drugs (+4.4%). Pseudo R squared values exceeding 75% indicated a strong association most models, except for the models predicting the number of hospitalizations due to SA related to food, unspecified, in which Pseudo R–squared values ranged from 25% to 75%, signifying a moderate fit (Fig. 2).

Fig. 3 demonstrates GAM model with a Poisson distribution adjusted for age groups to assess the monthly trends, in which we observed a non-significant seasonality in the pregnant group for each etiology. On the other hand, we observed possible seasonal trend of cases of anaphylaxis by trigger over the months per each year of the study in non-pregnant women. In this population, there was a significant increase of insect sting-induced anaphylaxis in non-pregnant patients during the months of June, July, and August (P<0.001), and an increase was noted for unspecified anaphylaxis during the months of June and July (P<0.005). Adjusted R-squared value for these 2 models were 92% and 96%, respectively.

Most of admissions, in both pregnant and non-pregnant women, were due to unspecified anaphylaxis (94%, 95%CI [94%, 94.7%]). However, pregnant women experienced higher proportion of SA (8.7%, 95%CI [6.8%, 11.1%]), compared with non-pregnant patients (5.4%, 95%CI [5.0%, 5.7%]). In both pregnant and non-pregnant women, most admissions for anaphylaxis were classified as unspecified (65%, 95CI [64%, 65.4%]), followed by triggered by drugs (23%, 95%CI [22.3%, 23.6%]), food (9.5%, 95%CI [9.1%, 9.9%]) and insect sting (2.7% 95%CI [2.4%, 2.9%]) (Table 1, Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Mean duration of hospitalization varied from 1.05 to 1.18 days, with no significant difference between groups, but the mean cost of hospitalization was higher in pregnant women (979.54 euros, 95%CI [872, 1087]) when compared with non-pregnant women (801.09 euros, 95%CI [775, 827]).

Presence of angioedema (P<0.001) and diseases of the circulatory system (P=0.02) were significantly associated with significantly associated with anaphylaxis in both pregnant and non-pregnant women (Tables 2S and 3S).

Recurrences of anaphylaxis admissions

Recurrences of admissions due to anaphylaxis occurred significantly more frequently in pregnant (1.51, 95%CI [1.3, 1,7]) than in non-pregnant patients (1.44, 95%CI [1.3, 1,6]). From overall 653 pregnant women, 50 (7.6%) were admitted twice for anaphylaxis and 31 (4.7%) required more than 2 hospital admissions. For the 15,752 non-pregnant patients, 1135 (7.2%) were hospitalized twice and 698 (4.4%) admitted more than 2 times (Table 2).

Table 2 shows that unspecified anaphylaxis and food were the main causes of recurrent admissions in both groups (P=0.01). For the pregnant group, unspecified cases of anaphylaxis (mean number of admissions: 1.17) required higher number of hospitalization than food (mean number of admissions: 1.05). However, the frequency of cause of anaphylaxis recurrent admissions reverses in non-pregnant women.

Fig. 4 shows that pregnant patients presented with a higher rate of unspecified anaphylaxis admission (1.17), followed by food- (1.05), drug- (1.04), and insect venom-induced anaphylaxis 1 (P=0.019). Severe anaphylaxis was notably prevalent in unspecified cases (48, 95% CI 35.9–61.2), followed by drug (44%, 95% CI 31.6–56.7), food (6.3%, 95% CI 2.02–16) and insect (1.6%, 95% CI 0.08–9.54) (p < 0.001). From all admissions for anaphylaxis in pregnant women, we observed that 0.92% (95% CI 0.42–1.99) were admitted for different etiologies in the second and third admissions. From 64 cases firstly admitted for SA, 5.7% (95% CI 1.9–15.4) decreased the severity of anaphylaxis in the second and third admissions. On the other hand, from all 670 pregnant patients who experienced NSA, 0.33% (95% CI 0.09–1.2) evolved with more severe episodes.

In non-pregnant women, a high rate of admissions due to unspecified anaphylaxis was observed across each admission. Higher number of recurrences were observed with food-induced anaphylaxis (1.14), followed by unspecified (1.13), drug- (1.08), and insect venom-induced anaphylaxis (1.05) (p < 0.001). SA was notably prevalent in unspecified (41%, 95% CI 37.6–44) and drug-induced (41%, 95% CI 37.5–43.9) cases, followed by food (15%, 95% CI 12.6–17.2) and insect (3.8%, 95% CI 2.71–5.28) (p < 0.001). Deaths were mostly associated with drug-induced anaphylaxis (38%, 95% CI 19–61.3) (p < 0.001). Frequency of SA was high in the first admission (87%, 95% CI 84.9–89.2), and severity changed in other admissions in 8.9% (95% CI 7.2–11.1) cases. Death was exclusively observed in the first admission (21/21) (P = 0.04). Change of possible cause of anaphylaxis admissions changed in 1.82% (95% CI 1.6–2) of cases.

Discussion

We here present the first report on anaphylaxis with hospitalization in pregnancy based on French official hospital statistics. We were able to identify key characteristics and patterns of this condition. The outcomes of our manuscript support the identification of potential triggers and trends of SA and NSA, and recurrence patterns, which should be the target for preventive measures.

In 2021, France had a population of approximately 66,548,530 inhabitants in 2024, with fertility rate estimated by 1.64 per women.13 The national PMSI is a core component of the French health system, established to collect hospitalization data with the aim of monitoring and reducing disparities in resource allocation across healthcare institutions.14 Initially introduced in the mid-1980s as an epidemiological tool, the PMSI later evolved into a mechanism for hospital budget allocation. Its use became mandatory for public hospitals in 1991 and for private hospitals in 1996.14

Our data suggest that the incidence of anaphylaxis during pregnancy in France is 0.11 per 100,000 female population per year, lower than the estimated in European countries, in which it is suggested an overall incidence of 1.5 per 100,000 women per year.12 Although valuable, the French data used in this last study was not based on official statistics. We are aware that NSA can be underdiagnosed in population-based studies. The number of admissions to the hospital can be reduced due to the possible spontaneous remission of anaphylaxis and patients not reaching hospitals. More than half of all anaphylaxis cases resolve spontaneously without treatment. Indeed, only a minority of anaphylaxis cases require hospitalization. The reported data on hospitalizations for anaphylaxis varies with the observation time stipulated by the hospital emergency department and whether the visit was coded as a hospital admission or an emergent consultation.16 This variability in hospital procedures may limit the extrapolation of our findings to other centers or locations. Stalking was the difference of incidence of anaphylaxis between pregnant and non-pregnant women. We hypothesize that the reduced numbers in pregnant women can be related to better health care of these patients, with less exposure to potential triggers, combined with more strict medical care of this population.

Our findings suggested a paradoxical situation with higher percentage of severe anaphylaxis cases among pregnant women and more deaths among non-pregnant women. Anaphylaxis in pregnant women seems to be less severe than non-pregnant women, with decreasing trend overtime. Although not definitively established, this likely association may be influenced by the woman's underlying better quality of health status which enables pregnancy, as well as by the closer medical monitoring typically provided during gestation.17 In general, pregnant women are more concerned with their health and may look for early health support that non-pregnant women to avoid self-medication. Therefore, the early manifestations of anaphylaxis can be diagnosed before evolving to severity and relapse. It can probably explain the lack of deaths in this group, compared with non-pregnant women. In general, due to the fear of complications by their health care workers, pregnant women with anaphylaxis would be admitted more readily than non-pregnant women and have increased care and surveillance, which may have implications in the reduction of deaths in this population.

Frequency of triggers generally varies according to the period of pregnancy (before or during/after the delivery). 7 During the first trimester of pregnancy, the most frequent causes of anaphylaxis are the same as in non-pregnant women (food, venom, drugs). However, during the second and third trimesters, drugs and latex are the most commonly reported cause of anaphylaxis.7,9,18, 19, 20 Although populational-studies are not able to prove etiology nor identify the trimester of pregnancy, we observed that drugs were the main cause of SA in both pregnant and non-pregnant women.

Latex is second cause of anaphylaxis during pregnancy,7,9 but we were not able to capture cases of latex-induced anaphylaxis due to the lack of specific ICD-10 code to represent this condition. This condition may gain more representativeness in the future with the implementation of the ICD-11.6,15,21,22

Published data suggest that drugs are the main cause of perioperative anaphylaxis during cesarean sections.23,24 Different from other Latin American and Caribbean countries,25 where cesarean section is estimated by 44.3% of births, in France, the global cesarean delivery rate is stable around 20% of all births since 2010.26

Food-induced anaphylaxis was also cause of admissions in both groups, but most of cases were non severe. Though unspecified cases represent almost 65% of all cases, the majority are non-severe, but relapse more. This group possibly consists of patients with no or incomplete allergy work-up, which highlights the need of optimized healthcare pathways through strengthening the collaboration between specialists involved in the care of these patients, both during the acute phase and in the follow-up.9

We were equally able to confirm seasonal patterns of anaphylaxis in non-pregnant women, following the French population admitted for anaphylaxis.27 Seasonal trend was not observed in pregnant women, probably due to stricter exposure control to potential triggers during pregnancy. It is expected that countries from South hemisphere would present the opposite pattern. Although we still do not have objective data, it is possible that our finding may be impacted by climate changes.28 Therefore, prospective studies may be required to follow the trends.

It is rather surprising to observe the recurrence of admissions for anaphylaxis in both populations, but particularly in pregnant women. Very few published data explore recurrence of anaphylaxis in this population. Most of recurrent admissions were related to unspecified cases and drug-related anaphylaxis. It was observed that the severity of anaphylaxis and etiology changed over the admissions in a low proportion of cases, which reinforce the fact that the severity of the index reaction cannot predict the severity of forthcoming episodes.29 For those patients who had admissions for anaphylaxis of different causes, we hypothesize that can be a multiple allergic population and/or have an underlying condition, which increases the risk of the reactions, such as mast cell disorders or hereditary alpha-tryptasemia.30,31

This study presents some limitations. As with all analyses based on administrative health data, certain caveats must be acknowledged, including potential inaccuracies in diagnostic coding, clinical identification of anaphylaxis, and the underrepresentation of cases managed exclusively in the community or treated in emergency departments without subsequent hospital admission. Nonetheless, these limitations are likely consistent over the 9-year study period. As it is typical of population-based studies, detailed clinical information, such as biphasic or refractory presentations, could not be assessed. Different from the analysis of registries, the data from national administrative databases do not allow the assessment of some details such as the use of adrenaline or vasopressor agents for the management of reactions or the outcomes of allergy work-up. However, the data here presented are based on official statistics, which are the source of evidence able to reflect the reality of the population. Exclusive ER data have not been added in the current study (only ER data included were those cases first seen in the ER and hospitalized) but may be the basis of future research due to the relevance of ER data to capture severe cases of anaphylaxis. The generalizability of findings derived from French data to other countries may be limited, although groups of anaphylaxis triggers tend to be similar globally. Mild reactions are often underrepresented, primarily due to the limitations of ICD-10 coding, which does not adequately capture less severe forms of anaphylaxis.32,33 On the other hand, severe reactions where those cases admitted in intensive or critical care units. The severity categorization applied in our approach differs from conventional systems due to the fact that it is not possible to apply known severity anaphylaxis scores based on the current available ICD-10 codes; it was therefore based on objective indicators such as the level of care required during hospitalization. In general, the need of hospitalization is highly dependent on hospital and ER policies at individual centers and in different communities or countries. In some centers, patients are often admitted for observation even for moderate anaphylaxis. In addition, it is likely that pregnant women with anaphylaxis would be admitted more readily than non-pregnant women. The admissions in intensive care units can be arguably due to the need monitoring the pregnancy (2 living beings) and may have influenced the number of cases considered severe. However, the better care of this population may be one of the reasons why we observed no deaths due to anaphylaxis in the pregnant women. Though ICU admissions and severe reactions are not synonyms, but French published data covering maternal ICU admissions suggests that there is increased trend in the clinical severity of women admitted in ICU and underlines that maternal ICU admission is an indicator of severe maternal morbidity.34,35

Although several versions of ICD-10 were used over the study period, the codes related to anaphylaxis have remained stable—albeit imprecise—across versions.33,36, 37, 38 The transition to the ICD-11 offers a promising development, as it enables more accurate classification and coding of anaphylaxis and severity of the reactions.21,36 External validation of our findings is warranted in other countries and through prospective national studies. Finally, our model could not assess outcomes of anaphylaxis in neonates.

Conclusion

Our findings contribute to the better understanding of characteristics of hospitalizations for anaphylaxis in pregnant women. Although no deaths have been recorded in pregnant women, this population experienced higher proportion of hospitalization in intensive care units due to anaphylaxis and recurrences if compared to non-pregnant women. Seasonal trend was observed in non-pregnant women, but not in pregnant patients.

More than providing a snapshot of characteristics of anaphylaxis during pregnancy in France, we were able to explore trends and recurrences of anaphylaxis in this population, to be validated prospectively. Although further research is needed in the field, we strongly believe that the data presented contributed as a starting point of public health advancements to ensure quality of care of patients suffering from anaphylaxis.
