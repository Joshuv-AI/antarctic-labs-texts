The International Agency for Research on Cancer: from global evidence to national action
Authors: Anna Schmütz, Véronique Chajàs, Clément Chauvet, Dorothy Keefe, Elisabete Weiderpass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAs we mark the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) 60th anniversary, this is an opportunity to reflect on our contribution to cancer evidence and action. Global cancer incidence continues to rise, with the largest proportional increases projected in countries that are the least equipped to respond. However, the central challenge is no longer only to produce evidence, but to understand why evidence is translated into policy and practice in some settings more effectively than in others. Drawing on the International Agency for Research on Cancer Impact in Practice series—30 country reports co-produced with national partners across IARC participating states—we examine how shared international cancer science influences policy and practice in national settings. Four recurrent pathways help explain that translation: (1) independent assessment that makes evidence usable under contestation; (2) surveillance that makes burden actionable; (3) sustained technical support that builds durable institutional capacity; and (4) co-governance through which countries can help to shape the science that they later apply. Across countries, IARC evidence is widely available and frequently cited, but citation alone does not ensure use. What determines whether evidence becomes embedded in policy and implementation is an enabling layer of registries, training platforms, expert participation, and governance relationships. This enabling layer is cumulative, slow to build, and most fragile in settings where the dependence on shared infrastructure is greatest. For IARC's next phase, we identify three priorities: (1) securing predictable, multiyear support for the shared scientific functions that participating states collectively rely upon; (2) evaluating the IARC medium-term strategy 2026–30 not only by output, but also by the extent to which evidence is translated into and embedded within national policy and practice; and (3) broadening meaningful participation in IARC's scientific and governance processes, so that a wide range of countries can help to shape the science they rely upon. Without renewed investment in that enabling layer, the risk is not abrupt failure but gradual erosion of the conditions that allow evidence to become action.

Introduction

Latest Global Cancer Observtory (GLOBOCAN) data show a familiar yet increasingly accelerated trajectory: global cancer incidence continue to rise, with the largest proportional increases projected in low-income and middle-income countries.1 Cancer burden estimates, however, are only part of the story. Cancer burden estimates show where the challenge lies, but not why some countries are better able than others to respond or what allows evidence to be translated into policy and implementation.

Uneven global cancer control is not primarily a problem of insufficient knowledge. Up to four in ten cancers can be prevented by addressing established and modifiable risk factors.2 Even so, evidence on primary and secondary prevention is still unevenly taken up in policy, programme design, and practice. In public health, such translation is rarely immediate; delays between evidence generation and uptake are a persistent feature of health systems across diseases and settings.3 That gap is often framed as a problem of national capacity, but is also a problem of shared scientific capacity: whether countries can draw on institutions, standards, and platforms that make evidence comparable, trusted, and usable across borders.

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) was created to provide those functions. Established in 1965 as WHO's specialised cancer agency, IARC was founded on the premise that understanding cancer causation and preventing its consequences required forms of coordinated international science beyond the reach of any single country. 60 years later, many of the core scientific functions of cancer control—comparable burden estimation, large-scale aetiological research, independent hazard evaluation, and support for surveillance and prevention—still depend on that same premise.

What has changed is the environment in which those functions should now be sustained. IARC enters its seventh decade amid continued pressure on multilateral institutions: constrained core funding, growing reliance on earmarked short-cycle financing, geopolitical fragmentation and contestation, and demands for rapid, attributable results.4, 5 These pressures sit uneasily with cancer prevention, which depends on cumulative, independent work over decade-long time horizons. These pressures are also compounded by the fact that, despite its strong economic returns, cancer prevention remains chronically underfunded relative to treatment.6 IARC's 60th anniversary therefore invites a broader question: as cancer burden rises, what forms of shared scientific infrastructure enable countries to translate evidence into public health action, and what is put at risk when those infrastructures are allowed to erode?

How IARC evidence becomes national action

To examine how IARC's scientific functions translate into national policy and practice, we draw on the IARC Impact in Practice series: 30 country reports co-produced with national partners across IARC's participating states.

Analysing these country case studies reports (appendix pp 1–2), four recurring pathways help explain how shared international cancer science becomes usable nationally: independent assessment that makes evidence usable under contestation; surveillance that makes burden actionable; sustained technical support that builds durable institutional capacity; and co-governance, through which countries can help to shape the science that they later apply.

Independent assessment under contestation

The policy value of IARC's evaluations lies not only in the evidence that they review, but in the independence and transparency of the process through which judgements are reached. As no single government or commercial interest controls these assessments, IARC's evaluations give national authorities a defensible basis for action when science is contested and, in some settings, help translate evidence into durable policy action. The IARC monographs often play this role by providing an authoritative reference in areas where regulatory debate remains unsettled.7 Asbestos is a recurring example across the reports: its classification as carcinogenic by the IARC monographs8 helped to establish and reinforce a common scientific basis for regulation, worker protection, and compensation. In the USA,9 for example, that evaluation has supported workplace standards, abatement requirements, disease recognition, and compensation systems. In Japan,10 IARC's contribution to the evidence on human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination effectiveness helped to support the resumption of proactive vaccination recommendations in 2022 after nearly a decade of suspension, providing an independent source of reassurance amid public distrust.

Governability through surveillance

Burden estimates influence policy only when they can be incorporated into routine planning and priority-setting by health systems. Their value, therefore, depends not only on the production of data, but also on the quality, completeness, and credibility of the underlying surveillance systems and on the institutional arrangements that turn these data into actionable intelligence. IARC's contribution extends beyond publishing global figures to helping build those conditions. In some settings, IARC estimates provide an interim basis for planning while national systems are still being strengthened, as in Qatar,11 where global estimates continue to inform policy while registry capacity develops with IARC support. In France,12 co-produced surveillance analyses have entered the national cancer strategy with a degree of domestic ownership that externally generated estimates alone would be unlikely to achieve.

Capacity through sustained technical support

A third pathway operates through long-term IARC support for the systems that translate evidence into routine care and public health (eg, cancer registry hubs, training pipelines, screening platforms, and technical standards). What distinguishes this pathway from conventional technical assistance is that it gradually builds systems that are capable of generating and using knowledge with increasing independence over time. In Brazil,13 IARC helped to turn the evidence for HPV-based cervical screening into national reform by supporting platform design, policy modelling, quality assurance, and delivery across a large public health system. In Morocco,14 IARC-supported training helped create a cadre that now trains others, initiating the multiplier effect and positioning the country as a regional reference point.

Reciprocity through co-governance

Countries derive the greatest value from shared scientific institutions when they help to shape the research questions, measurement methods, classification systems, and prevention standards that later guide national policy. In institutional settings, evidence is more likely to be owned and embedded nationally. Shared platforms also serve as a forum for scientific and health diplomacy through which countries can influence global agendas by contributing to the evidence base themselves. The development of the WHO cervical cancer elimination strategy illustrates this dynamic: over several decades, IARC-coordinated research on HPV helped establish causality, strengthen the evidence base for vaccination and screening, and develop the tools and standards that later underpinned the global elimination strategy.15, 16 Countries whose scientists contributed to that chain of evidence, such as Australia, helped to shape the normative framework. Co-governance, in that sense, increases not only national ownership of evidence, but also countries’ influence over the global standards that they later apply.

Structural gaps in translating evidence into action

The IARC Impact in Practice series shows that these pathways have enabled tangible progress: independent evaluations that have informed the regulation of asbestos, tobacco, and alcohol; surveillance systems that are now used in national cancer planning across multiple regions; cervical screening programmes that have been redesigned around HPV-based approaches; and a global elimination strategy built on decades of shared research. However, the reports show in concrete national terms that producing global public goods is not enough to make these advances consequential. In practice, international cancer science operates on two levels. The first is an access layer of burden estimates, hazard assessments, and methodological standards that countries can draw on directly. The second is the enabling layer of training platforms, cancer registry support, participation in expert processes, and governance arrangements that determine whether accessible evidence becomes embedded in routine practice. Across the reports, GLOBOCAN estimates and IARC monographs recur with striking regularity, suggesting that availability is not the main constraint. What differs is whether countries have sufficient institutional capacity to use these products repeatedly and authoritatively. The implementation gap is, in that sense, also an embedding gap.

Settings where mortality-to-incidence ratios are highest (ie, where cancer is most likely to be diagnosed late and treated ineffectively) are often those where the enabling layer is thinnest. Countries with strong research and public health infrastructures use IARC platforms as force multipliers for already robust research systems. Countries with poorer surveillance systems, analytical capacity, and institutional resources often depend on the same platforms as constitutive infrastructure, with no domestic substitute. This asymmetry is not only an equity concern, but also affects how well global cancer evidence reflects the settings where burden is highest. When countries with the greatest needs have fewer opportunities to participate in shaping research priorities, methods, and standards, the resulting evidence base is less likely to reflect their institutional realities and implementation constraints.

The enabling layer is cumulative and slow to build. Membership often acts as an accelerator, but rarely with immediate effect. The benefits that matter most (eg, training pipelines, expert participation, standard-setting roles, and durable institutional relationships) accumulate over time, especially in settings where research and surveillance capacity are still being consolidated. Newly joined participating states show how formal membership can quickly intensify collaboration. In Portugal,17 for example, accession in 2025 created new channels for engagement that, within a short period, generated a substantial portfolio of projects that are now being implemented nationally. Longer-standing partnerships point to a slower progression from recipient to co-producer. In Morocco, Brazil, and India, sustained participation has supported not only a national uptake of evidence, but also growing roles in standard-setting, implementation research, and regional capacity-building. Such progression, however, depends on the enabling layer being maintained. Countries unable to sustain participation—or excluded altogether—risk remaining users of global outputs while having little influence over the platforms through which those outputs are shaped.

These vulnerabilities are sharpened by the current funding environment. Pressures to streamline and provide rapid attributable results systematically favour visible outputs over the conditions that make outputs usable. However, cancer surveillance platforms, training relationships, methodological continuity, and institutional credibility are cumulative assets that take time to build and are poorly suited to episodic financing. Their fragility is compounded by a global research agenda that continues to privilege discovery science and biopharmaceutical innovation, leaving these less visible but essential public health functions undersupported. What follows is less likely to be sudden failure than progressive attrition: slower methodological renewal, thinning cancer registry ecosystems, weakening training platforms, and gradual loss of the reputational capital that allows shared scientific assessments to carry weight across jurisdictions. Once eroded, these functions will not be easily rebuilt, if at all. What is at stake is more than a set of programmes. It is the institutional basis that makes shared international cancer science usable in practice.

Implications for IARC's next phase

Read comparatively, recurrent patterns across the 30 country reports support three governance shifts, with relevance for other institutions that provide shared scientific functions.

Shared scientific functions need to be financed as infrastructure

Surveillance networks, training systems, methodological continuity, and collaborative relationships accumulate value over time and deteriorate when support becomes intermittent. Financing models therefore need to distinguish between functions that require long-term continuity and functions that can be supported through time-limited projects. Although IARC has taken steps to put sustainable financing squarely on the governance agenda, these efforts have yet to deliver the predictable support that the enabling layer requires. Participating states should therefore finance IARC's medium-term strategy 2026–30 through multiyear core support that is aligned with the strategy cycle, ensuring the continuity of surveillance platforms, training pipelines, and methodological stewardship. Such core functions are best protected through predictable assessed contributions, rather than short-cycle, earmarked funding.

Evaluation should capture embedding and production

Publication counts and citation metrics remain dominant, partly because they are legible to non-specialist funders and governing boards, even when these metrics do not meet the conditions that determine whether evidence becomes usable in practice. IARC is already moving in this direction through the Impact in Practice series,1 which documents country-level uptake and outcome-oriented influence beyond output counts alone. The next step is to extend that logic to the evaluation of the medium-term strategy 2026–30 by assessing not only what is produced, but also whether the platforms, training pipelines, expert networks, and governance relationships that enable uptake are sustained, and whether outputs are integrated into routine decision-making and strengthen the capacity to act.

Governance arrangements should widen participation in producing shared cancer science

Broader participation would make the evidence base both fairer and more relevant, and reduce the extent to which a small group of participating states are expected to carry the infrastructure alone. IARC has made deliberate efforts in this direction. Recent accessions (including Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and Portugal) reflect a growing recognition of the value of shared cancer science across regions. Membership expansion, however, is insufficient unless accompanied by mechanisms that make participation substantive rather than nominal. This shift requires income-scaled contributions, graduated participation models, and sponsored access to technical and governance forums. Meaningful participation also depends on sustained investment in registry networks, analytical capacity, and regional platforms (eg, IARC cancer registration hubs and regional Learning Centres) through which scientific engagement is built over time. Without that foundation, formal inclusion alone will not correct the asymmetries that it is meant to address.

Conclusion

The 60th anniversary of IARC is a reminder that global cancer control depends on shared scientific functions that are easily undervalued because they operate upstream of visible results. The Impact in Practice country series clearly highlights what allows countries to respond: surveillance that detects change early enough to act, standards that hold under contestation, capacities that endure long enough to carry evidence into practice, and forms of co-governance through which countries help to shape the science that they later apply. If the first 60 years of IARC has helped to build the foundations of shared cancer science, the next 60 years will depend on whether countries are willing to sustain the infrastructures that turn that science into action.

Contributors

Search strategy and selection criteria

This Viewpoint draws primarily on the International Agency for Research on Cancer Impact in Practice series, comprising 30 country reports co-produced with national partners across all participating states (see appendix for details). We also did a targeted search of PubMed, Google Scholar, and reference lists of relevant publications for papers in English published between Jan 1, 2010, and Jan 31, 2026. Search terms included combinations of “cancer prevention”, “implementation”, “translation into policy”, “global health financing”, “global public goods”, “multilateral health institutions”, and “cancer research funding”. Selection was based on direct relevance to the conceptual framing of the Viewpoint.

For IARC Impact in Practice series see https://www.iarc.who.int/iarc-impact-in-practice-from-global-cancer-science-to-national-action
