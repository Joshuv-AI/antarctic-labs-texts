Simple generators of rational function fields
Alexander Demin, Gleb Pogudin

Abstract
Consider a subfield of the field of rational functions in several indeterminates. We present an algorithm that, given a set of generators of such a subfield, finds a simple generating set. We provide an implementation of the algorithm and show that it improves upon the state of the art both in efficiency and the quality of the results. Furthermore, we demonstrate the utility of simplified generators through several case studies from different application domains, such as structural parameter identifiability. The main algorithmic novelties include performing only partial Gröbner basis computation via sparse interpolation and efficient search for polynomials of a fixed degree in a subfield of the rational function field.



1
Introduction

Let
k
be a field, and consider the field of rational functions
k(\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
are indeterminates.
Intermediate subfields
k\subset\mathcal{E}\subset k(\mathbf{x})
are ubiquitous in algebra and algebraic geometry.
Their theoretical properties have been extensively studied during the 20-th century, in particular, due to the central role they play in the Lüroth problem
[
9
]
and Hilbert 14-th problem
[
96
,
79
]
.

A starting point for the computational study of the subfields of the rational function field is the fact that every such field can be generated over
k
by finitely many elements
[
80
, Ch. VIII, Ex. 4]
;
this contrasts sharply with the situation for intermediate subalgebras of the polynomial algebra
k\subset\mathcal{A}\subset k[\mathbf{x}]

[
106
]
.
Such a finite generating set is then a natural data structure for representing subfields of
k(\mathbf{x})
.
Within this framework, a number of algorithmic results have
been obtained in the 90-s
[
98
,
115
,
73
,
94
,
95
]
, in particular, a solution to the membership problem.
One of the key technical tools introduced first by Ollivier
[
98
, Ch. III, §2]
and then by Müller-Quade and Steinwandt
[
94
,
95
]

has been
certain polynomial ideals in
k(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{y}]
with
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
which we call
OMS ideals
(
Section

2.7
); they play a prominent role in this paper as well.
Another line of research
has been
devoted to subfields of transcendence degree one which have a particularly nice structure due to the Lüroth theorem
[
14
,
53
]
.

More recent advances in computing with rational function fields are often motivated by particular applications such as computer vision
[
13
]
or structural parameter identifiability of dynamical models
[
99
,
37
]
.
In the latter case, the field
\mathcal{E}
of interest consists of quantities that
may be inferred from the input-output data of the model.
The existing algorithms for computing the generators of
\mathcal{E}
typically produce extremely complicated expressions, which cannot be further analyzed and interpreted by a modeler.
Therefore, one of the key questions in this context is the question of
generator simplification
, which can be stated as follows:

Given:

\mathbf{g}\coloneqq(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\in k(\mathbf{x})
, such that
\mathcal{E}=k(\mathbf{g})
;

Find:

\mathbf{h}\coloneqq(h_{1},\ldots,h_{\ell})\in k(\mathbf{x})
, such that
\mathcal{E}=k(\mathbf{h})
and
\mathbf{h}
are
simpler
than
\mathbf{g}
.

We will deliberately not give a formal definition of what being simple means, as we do not expect that there is a single mathematical definition capable of capturing the intuitive notions of simplicity used in applications.
In general, it may be preferable to have
a generating set of small cardinality, where each element is a sparse rational function of low degree with small coefficients.
To give some intuition on how the simplification may look like and what benefits it may bring, let us give an illustrative example.

Example 1.1
.

Consider a triangle with side lengths
a,b,c
.
Using Heron’s formula, we can write the length of the altitude from
a
as

h_{a}^{2}=\frac{4s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}{a^{2}},\quad\text{where }s=\frac{a+b+c}{2},

and analogous formulas hold for
h_{b}
and
h_{c}
.
By computing a simplified generating set of
\mathcal{E}:=\mathbb{Q}(h_{a}^{2},h_{b}^{2},h_{c}^{2})
, we find that
\mathcal{E}=\mathbb{Q}(a^{2},b^{2},c^{2})
.
Since the side lengths are always positive, we conclude that they can be determined from the altitude lengths confirming a well-known fact from elementary geometry.

Already the Lüroth problem can be viewed as a version of the simplification question focusing solely on the cardinality of the generating set.
Specifically, any generating set of
k\subset\mathcal{E}\subset k(\mathbf{x})
over
k
must contain at least
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}\mathcal{E}
elements.
On the other hand, if
\operatorname{char}k=0
, the primitive element theorem implies that
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}\mathcal{E}+1
generators are always sufficient.
Whether or not using only
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}\mathcal{E}
generators is always possible is the Lüroth problem, and the answer is negative starting from
n=3
.
Furthermore, deciding whether
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}\mathcal{E}+1
generators are necessary is equivalent to determining if a unirational variety is rational, which is an old open problem in algebraic geometry
[
114
]
.
For a related Noether problem, an approach based on searching for generators of small degree was used to give a computational solution in several interesting cases
[
73
]
.

The question of simplification in its broader, more intuitive, form was already considered in
[
94
]
where it was proposed to use the coefficients of a reduced Gröbner basis of the OMS ideal of
\mathcal{E}
as a “canonical generating set”.
In the context of structural identifiability, this approach was further refined
[
99
]
by taking this canonical generating set and filtering out redundant generators.
The resulting algorithm has been implemented in Maple
[
99
]
, and the implementation was later incorporated into
a web-application
[
64
]
.
A construction reminiscent of OMS ideals was used in
[
88
,
89
,
87
]
to search for a simple transcendence basis of the relative algebraic closure of
\mathcal{E}
inside
k(\mathbf{x})
also in connection to the structural identifiability problem.

The main contribution of the present paper is a new algorithm
that
takes as input a set of generators of a subfield
\mathcal{E}\subset k(\mathbf{x})
and returns a simplified
set of generators, assuming
\operatorname{char}k=0
.
We provide an open-source implementation of the algorithm for the case
k=\mathbb{Q}
in Julia.
Our approach also relies on OMS ideals but improves upon the state of the art
both in terms of
efficiency
and
quality of simplification
.
Let us describe our contributions along these two axes separately.

From the
efficiency
perspective, the key question is how to perform Gröbner basis computations with OMS ideals.
These ideals belong to the ring
k(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{y}]
over the rational function field which makes computations with them expensive due to the intermediate expression swell.
Our algorithm follows the evaluation-interpolation approach:
it computes
multiple Gröbner bases by specializing
\mathbf{x}
and then reconstructs the coefficients by using sparse rational function interpolation
[
28
,
118
]
.
There are two rationale behind this choice of methodology.

First, interpolation
is one of the standard ways to alleviate intermediate expression swell.
It has been
successfully
employed for similar problems over the rational function field such as GCD computation
[
57
,
118
]
and linear system solving
[
66
]

and explored for
Gröbner basis computation
[
17
, Ch. 5]
and, recently, for
generic parametrized polynomial system solving
[
26
]
.
Our approach includes further optimizations such as computing over finite fields and using tracing to speed up repeated Gröbner basis computations (see
Section

5.5
).
The resulting implementation is competitive for computing Gröbner bases in
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{y}]
, significantly improving upon the prior interpolation-based
algorithms, but may be slower than other, not interpolation-based methods (see
Section

5.6
).

And this is where the second key advantage of our method comes into play: it does not require computing the full Gröbner basis. Using evaluation-interpolation allows us to stop
early,
once only low-degree coefficients of the Gröbner basis have been interpolated, and these coefficients are
the ones
of interest in the simplification problem.
So, we gradually increase the degree of interpolation stopping once the computed coefficients generate the whole field.
Thus, compared to the earlier approach from
[
99
]
, instead of computing all the coefficients and then filtering out the large redundant ones, we now avoid computing them at the first place.
This strategy allows to reduce the number of
evaluations
and, consequently, the runtime dramatically and solve the problems which are out of reach otherwise (see
Section

5.4
).

We improve the
quality of simplification
by creating a pool of potential generators (which contains the coefficients of the OMS ideal) and employing a refined heuristic for selecting the final result from this pool.
The main tool for adding extra candidates to the pool
is our algorithm for finding polynomial elements of the subfield up to a fixed degree, which is of independent interest.
While a theoretical algorithm for this task has appeared already in
[
94
]
, it required computing the full Gröbner basis of the OMS ideal.
In the spirit of the evaluation-based approach we follow, our algorithm performs only Gröbner basis computation
on the specialized ideal
which makes it efficient for practical computation.

Since we do not fix a formal notion of simplicity, assessing the quality of simplification is a delicate task.
First, we run our algorithm and the one from
[
99
]
on a large suite of benchmarks (53 problems) coming from applications to structural identifiability and perform a manual inspection of results.
We show that our algorithm is more efficient and argue that it produces simpler results
(see
Section

6.2
).
Furthermore, we perform a more detailed analysis of the output of our algorithm on several case studies coming from structural identifiability of ODE models, observability of discrete dynamics, and fields of invariants.
In addition to syntactic simplicity, we discuss
the usefulness of the simplification performed by our algorithm.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
We review and adapt the main tools (e.g., sparse interpolation, OMS ideals) in
Section

2
.
The main building blocks of our algorithm are presented in
Section

3
, and we use them to describe and justify our main algorithm in
Section

4
.

Section

5
is devoted to implementation details including discussion of the impact of the main design choices on the performance.
We analyze the quality of simplification by our algorithm on a large benchmark suite in
Section

6
(the details of the suite are summarized in
Appendix

A
).
We finish with a more in-depth discussion of a set of case studies coming from three different application domains in
Section

7
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

Let
k
denote an effective field, meaning that there exist algorithms for the arithmetic in
k
.
For a field
k
, let
\overline{k}

denote its algebraic closure.
We will use shorthands for tuples, such as
\mathbf{x}\coloneqq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
For a multivariate polynomial
f\in k[\mathbf{x}]
, we will write
d_{f}\coloneqq\deg f
for the total degree of
f
and
s_{f}
for the number of nonzero terms in
f
.
We define the degree of zero polynomial to be
-\infty
.
We have
\mathbb{N}_{0}\coloneqq 0,1,2,\ldots
.

Products, sums, and powering of tuples are performed component-wise, more precisely, for every
\bm{\gamma},\bm{\omega},\bm{\sigma}\in k^{n}
and every
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
, we write
\bm{\gamma}\bm{\omega}^{\bm{\alpha}}+\bm{\sigma}\coloneqq(\gamma_{1}\omega_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}+\sigma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}\omega_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}+\sigma_{n})
. We extend this notation to scalars by treating a scalar as a tuple of
n
identical elements. In place of
\bm{\gamma},\bm{\omega},\bm{\sigma}
we may use indeterminates or tuples thereof.
Given a tuple
\mathbf{a}
(say,
\mathbf{a}\in k^{n}
), we write
\Pi(\mathbf{a})\coloneqq\prod_{i=1,\ldots,n}a_{i}
.

The degrevlex monomial ordering is a total ordering on the set of monomials, which we may denote by
<_{\operatorname{drl}}
. For
\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}},\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\beta}}
with
{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
, we have
\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}<_{\operatorname{drl}}\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\beta}}
whenever the sum of elements of
\bm{\alpha}
is smaller than that of
\bm{\beta}
, or, them being equal, the right-most nonzero entry of
{\bm{\alpha}}-{\bm{\beta}}
is positive.

For polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{\ell}
from
k[\mathbf{x}]
, let
\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{\ell}\rangle
denote the ideal in
k[\mathbf{x}]
generated by
f_{1},\ldots,f_{\ell}
.
For an ideal
I
of
k[\mathbf{x}]
and an element
a\in k[\mathbf{x}]
, denote
I\colon a^{\infty}\coloneqq\{r\in k[\mathbf{x}]\mid\exists\ell:ra^{\ell}\in I\}
. This set is also an ideal.

2.2
DeMillo-Lipton-Schwartz-Zippel lemma

Let
k
be an effective field and
\mathbf{x}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be an
n
-tuple of variables.
Let the coordinates of
\bm{\gamma}\coloneqq(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n})
be chosen independently and uniformly from a finite subset
S\subseteq k
.
Many of our probabilistic statements will rely on the following lemma:

Lemma 2.1

(DeMillo-Lipton-Schwarz-Zippel
[
32
,
111
,
127
]
)
.

Let
f\in k[\mathbf{x}]
be a nonzero polynomial of degree
d
.
The probability that
f(\bm{\gamma})=0
is at most
d/|S|
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
f\in k[\mathbf{x}]
be a nonzero polynomial of degree
d
.
Let
\bm{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\sigma}_{T}\in k^{n}
be arbitrary but fixed. Let
\bm{\omega}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\omega}_{T}\in(k\setminus\{0\})^{n}
be arbitrary but fixed.
The probability that there exists

i=1,\ldots,T

such that
f(\bm{\omega}_{i}\bm{\gamma}+\bm{\sigma}_{i})=0
is at most
dT/|S|
.

Proof.

Let
p\coloneqq f(\bm{\omega}_{1}\mathbf{x}+\bm{\sigma}_{1})\cdots f(\bm{\omega}_{T}\mathbf{x}+\bm{\sigma}_{T})
.
Note
p\neq 0
, since for every
i=1,\ldots,T
none of the components of
\bm{\omega}_{i}
are zero. We apply
Lemma

2.1
to
p
.
∎

2.3
Univariate rational function interpolation

Let
F=A/B
be a rational function in
k(x)
with
A,B\in k[x]
,
\operatorname{gcd}(A,B)=1
, and
d_{A}\coloneqq\deg A
and
d_{B}\coloneqq\deg B
.
Let
D_{A}\geqslant d_{a}
and
D_{B}\geqslant d_{b}
be the bounds on the total degrees of
A
and
B
, respectively.
Let
D
be such that
D\geqslant D_{A}+D_{B}+2
, and let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{D}\in k
be pairwise different
such that for every
i=1,\ldots,D
we have
B(u_{i})\neq 0
(assuming
k
is sufficiently large).
Univariate rational function interpolation is the problem of recovering
F
from the evaluations
F(u_{1}),\ldots,F(u_{D})
and the bounds
D_{A},D_{B}
.

The solution is unique up to a scaling of
A
and
B
, and can be computed using univariate polynomial interpolation and the Extended Euclidean Algorithm
[
120
, Corollary 5.18]
. This method is also referred to as Cauchy interpolation.

Remark 2.3
.

When the bounds on the degrees
D_{A}
and
D_{B}
are not known, it is still possible to recover a solution using
[
75
, Algorithm MQRFR]
instead of the EEA. Later in this paper, these two methods may be used interchangeably.

2.4
Sparse polynomial interpolation

Let
f
be a polynomial in
k[\mathbf{x}]
with
\mathbf{x}\coloneqq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. Let
d_{f}
be an integer, the total degree of
f
.
Sparse interpolation is the problem of recovering the sparse representation of
f
in the form

\begin{array}[]{l}f=\sum\limits_{i=1,\ldots,s_{f}}a_{i}\Pi(\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}_{i}})\end{array}

(1)

from a sufficient number of evaluations of
f
, where
s_{f}
is the number of terms in
f
and, for every
i=1,\ldots,s_{f}
, we have
a_{i}\in k\setminus\{0\}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}

are pairwise different.

In the case an upper bound on the number of terms
s_{f}
is known, the algorithm by Ben-Or and Tiwari
[
10
]
(which generalizes the Prony’s method
[
102
]
) recovers a sparse representation of
f
in the form (
1
) deterministically from the evaluations
f(\bm{\omega}^{0}),f(\bm{\omega}^{1}),\ldots,f(\bm{\omega}^{2T-1})
with
T\geqslant s_{f}
for some carefully chosen
\bm{\omega}\in(k\setminus\{0\})^{n}
. We shall call such
\bm{\omega}
an
admissible ratio
, following
[
34
]
.
A variant of the algorithm by Ben-Or and Tiwari is summarized in
Algorithm

1
.

There exist several strategies for choosing an admissible ratio
\bm{\omega}
. A common requirement is that for every
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
such that the degree of
\Pi(\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}})
does not exceed
d_{f}
it is possible to determine the exponent

\bm{\alpha}

from the product
\Pi(\bm{\omega}^{\bm{\alpha}})
.
In practice, when
k=\mathbb{Q}
or when
k
is a finite field of large characteristic, one may take
\bm{\omega}\coloneqq(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})=(2,3,5,\ldots,p_{n})
, where
p_{n}
denotes the
n
-th prime number. In this case, the exponent can be recovered via integer factorization (more precisely, via trial division).
Other approaches include reduction to univariate interpolation by Kronecker substitution
[
120
, Section 8.4]
and picking the evaluation point of a large order in a suitable extension of
k

[
119
]
.

Algorithm 1

Deterministic sparse polynomial interpolation
[
10
, Figure 1]
,
[

, Algorithm 4]

Input:

\bm{\omega}\in(k\setminus\{0\})^{n}
; the evaluations
f(\bm{\omega}^{0}),f(\bm{\omega}^{1}),\ldots,f(\bm{\omega}^{2T-1})
.

Output:

if
T\geqslant s_{f}
, the sparse representation of
f
as in (
1
); if
T<s_{f}
, an incorrect result.

(Step 1)

Compute (via Páde approximation
[
120
, Section 5.9]
) the minimal
t\leqslant T
, a monic
\Lambda\in k[z]

, and

of degree less than

, such that the following identity holds modulo

:

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}f(\bm{\omega}^{i})z^{i}=\frac{N(z)}{\Lambda(z)}.

(Step 2)

Find the roots
\Pi(\bm{\omega}^{-\bm{\alpha}_{1}}),\ldots,\Pi(\bm{\omega}^{-\bm{\alpha}_{t}})

of
\Lambda(z)
using polynomial root-finding.

(Step 3)

Compute the exponents

(Step 4)

(Diversification
[

]
)
.

In the following sections, we will be interested in estimating the probability that for a fixed nonzero
\tilde{f}(\mathbf{x})\coloneq f(\mathbf{x}+\bm{\sigma})
any of the evaluations

2.5
Sparse rational function interpolation

Let
F
be a rational function in
k(\mathbf{x})
with
\mathbf{x}\coloneqq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
Sparse interpolation is the problem of recovering the sparse representation of
F
in the form

\begin{array}[]{lll}F=\dfrac{A}{B},&A=\sum\limits_{i=1,\ldots,s_{A}}a_{i}\Pi(\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\alpha}_{i}}),&B=\sum\limits_{i=1,\ldots,s_{B}}b_{i}\Pi(\mathbf{x}^{\bm{\beta}_{i}}),\\
\end{array}

(2)

from a sufficient number of evaluations of
F
.
Here for every
i=1,\ldots,s_{A}
we have
a_{i}\in k\setminus\{0\}
and
{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
are pairwise different and for every
j=1,\ldots,s_{B}
we have
b_{j}\in k\setminus\{0\}
and
{\bm{\beta}}_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
are pairwise different.
We also
assume that

\operatorname{gcd}(A,B)=1
.
Let
d_{A}
and
d_{B}
be the total degrees of
A
and
B
, respectively.

Assume we have access to a blackbox that evaluates
F
at a point in
k^{n}
.
The algorithm by van der Hoeven and Lecerf
[
118
, Section 3.3.]
is a probabilistic
algorithm for recovering the sparse representation of a rational function as in (
2
) from a sequence of evaluations of the blackbox.
We will show that the probability of failure in this algorithm can be made arbitrarily close to zero assuming we can sample elements independently and uniformly from an arbitrarily large subset
S\subseteq k\setminus\{0\}
.
For completeness, we describe the algorithm here.

Introduce a new indeterminate
x_{0}
. Consider the rational function

\hat{F}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\coloneqq x_{0}^{d_{A}-d_{B}}\frac{A(x_{1}/x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}/x_{0})}{B(x_{1}/x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}/x_{0})},

where we also introduce for convenience

\begin{array}[]{l}\hat{A}\coloneqq x_{0}^{d_{A}}A\left(x_{1}/x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}/x_{0}\right),\\
\hat{B}\coloneqq x_{0}^{d_{B}}B\left(x_{1}/x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}/x_{0}\right).\end{array}

Note that
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
are homogeneous and coprime.

Introduce a new indeterminate
u
. Let
\bm{\sigma}\coloneqq(\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{n})\in k^{n+1}
, and let

\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{F}_{\bm{\sigma}}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n},u)\coloneqq\hat{F}(x_{0}u+\sigma_{0},\ldots,x_{n}u+\sigma_{n}),\\
\tilde{A}_{\bm{\sigma}}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n},u)\coloneqq\hat{A}(x_{0}u+\sigma_{0},\ldots,x_{n}u+\sigma_{n})\coloneqq\tilde{A}_{0}+\tilde{A}_{1}u+\ldots+\tilde{A}_{d_{A}}u^{d_{A}},\\
\tilde{B}_{\bm{\sigma}}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n},u)\coloneqq\hat{B}(x_{0}u+\sigma_{0},\ldots,x_{n}u+\sigma_{n})\coloneqq\tilde{B}_{0}+\tilde{B}_{1}u+\ldots+\tilde{B}_{d_{B}}u^{d_{B}},\end{array}

where for
i=0,\ldots,d_{A}
we have
\tilde{A}_{i}\in k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
and for
i=0,\ldots,d_{B}
we have
\tilde{B}_{i}\in k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
If
\tilde{B}_{0}\neq 0
, then
\tilde{A}_{\bm{\sigma}}
,
\tilde{B}_{\bm{\sigma}}
are coprime by
[
118
, Lemma 1]
.
In the following, assume
\tilde{B}_{0}\neq 0
.

Let
\bm{\omega}\coloneqq(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{n})\in(k\setminus\{0\})^{n+1}
be an admissible ratio (as in
Section

2.4
).
For

2.7
OMS Ideals

Let
k(\mathbf{x})
be the field of rational functions in the indeterminates
\mathbf{x}\coloneq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
We will be interested in subfields
k\subset\mathcal{E}\subseteq k(\mathbf{x})
.
By
[
80
, Ch. VIII, Ex. 4]
every such subfield is finitely generated over
k
, that is,
\mathcal{E}=k(\mathbf{g})
for some
\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
with
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in k(\mathbf{x})
.

One of the key tools in this paper are certain ideals associated to subfields
k\subset\mathcal{E}\subset k(\mathbf{x})
.
These ideals were introduced by Ollivier in
[
98
, Ch. III, §2]
and Müller-Quade and Steinwandt in
[
94
]
.
Thus, we will call them
OMS ideals
.

Definition 2.10

(OMS ideal)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\supset k
be a subfield of
k(\mathbf{x})
represented as
\mathcal{E}=k(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
, where
g_{i}=\frac{p_{i}(\mathbf{x})}{q_{i}(\mathbf{x})}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
.
We denote by
Q
the
\operatorname{lcm}
of
q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}
.
Introduce new indeterminates
\mathbf{y}\coloneqq(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
.
The following ideal in
k(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{y}]
will be called
the OMS ideal
of
\mathcal{E}
:

\operatorname{OMS}_{\mathcal{E}}:=\langle p_{i}(\mathbf{y})q_{i}(\mathbf{x})-q_{i}(\mathbf{y})p_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mid 1\leqslant i\leqslant m\rangle:(Q(\mathbf{y}))^{\infty}.

(3)

Remark 2.11

(Dependence on the choice of generators)
.

Formally, the definition (
3
) depends not only on
\mathcal{E}
but also on the chosen set of generators.
In fact,
[
94
, Lemma 1.2]
shows that
\operatorname{OMS}_{\mathcal{E}}
is generated by

\langle y_{1}-x_{1},\ldots,y_{n}-x_{n}\rangle\cap\mathcal{E}[\mathbf{y}]
and, thus, does not depend on the choice of generators.

This also implies that, if
\operatorname{char}k=0
, then
\operatorname{OMS}_{\mathcal{E}}
is radical.
It may be not the case in positive characteristic:
\operatorname{OMS}_{\mathbb{F}_{p}(x^{p})}
is not radical.

OMS ideals are useful for characterizing the elements of rational function fields algorithmically by associating rational functions with polynomials of special form:

Lemma 2.12

(
[
94
, Lemmas 1.2 and 1.6.]
,
[
98
, p. 51, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\supset k
be a subfield of
k(\mathbf{x})
.
Then:

1.

for every polynomials
p,q\in k[\mathbf{x}]
,
\frac{p}{q}\in\mathcal{E}
if and only if
p(\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{x})-q(\mathbf{y})p(\mathbf{x})\in\operatorname{OMS}_{\mathcal{E}}
.

2.

Let
f_{1}\ldots,f_{s}
be the
reduced Gröbner basis of
\operatorname{OMS}_{\mathcal{E}}
with respect to any ordering.
Then the coefficients of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
generate
\mathcal{E}
over
k
.

The second property from the lemma provides, in particular, a way to compute a new (often, simpler) set of generators for
\mathcal{E}
starting from some generators.

Example 2.13
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\mathbb{Q}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2},x_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{3},x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{4}\right)\subset\mathbb{Q}(x_{1},x_{2})
. We have
Q(y_{1},y_{2})=1
and

\operatorname{OMS}_{\mathcal{E}}=\left\langle\begin{array}[]{ll}y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}-(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}),\\
y_{1}^{3}+y_{2}^{3}-(x_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{3}),\\
y_{1}^{4}+y_{2}^{4}-(x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{4})\end{array}\right\rangle\subset\mathbb{Q}(x_{1},x_{2})[y_{1},y_{2}].

The reduced Gröbner basis of
\operatorname{OMS}_{\mathcal{E}}
with respect to the lexicographic ordering on
y_{1},y_{2}
with
y_{2}<y_{1}
is

y_{1}+y_{2}-\dashuline{(x_{1}+x_{2})},\;y_{2}^{2}-\dashuline{(x_{1}+x_{2})}+\dashuline{\penalty\ x_{1}x_{2}\penalty\ }.

By
Lemma

2.12
, the underlined coefficients generate
\mathcal{E}
, so
\mathcal{E}=\mathbb{Q}(x_{1}+x_{2},x_{1}x_{2})
.
Thus, the computation of the Gröbner basis of the OMS ideal allowed us to recognize that
\mathcal{E}
is the field of symmetric functions and provided us with the most natural set of generators.

Since the definition (
3
) involves saturation, practical computation with
\operatorname{OMS}_{\mathcal{E}}
requires passing through an elimination ordering (if the saturation is done via the Rabinowitsch
trick) or using dedicated algorithms for computing Gröbner bases of saturations.
It would naturally simplify the computations if we could employ the Rabinowitsch
trick with respect to a new variable
t

without performing
elimination.

Notation 2.14

(
\operatorname{eOMS}
)
.

In the notation of
Definition

2.10
, denote

\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}:=\langle p_{1}(\mathbf{y})q_{1}(\mathbf{x})-q_{1}(\mathbf{y})p_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,p_{m}(\mathbf{y})q_{m}(\mathbf{x})-q_{m}(\mathbf{y})p_{m}(\mathbf{x}),tQ(\mathbf{y})-1\rangle\subset k(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{y},t].

(4)

Obviously, the first statement from

2.12
holds if we replace
\operatorname{OMS}_{\mathcal{E}}
with
\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}
.
As we prove in the following lemma, the same is true for the second statement as well.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\supset k
be a subfield of
k(\mathbf{x})
represented by
\mathcal{E}=k(\mathbf{g})
.
Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
be the
reduced Gröbner basis of
\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}
with respect to any ordering (see (
4
)).
Then the coefficients of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
generate
\mathcal{E}
over
k
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the field generated over
k
by the coefficients of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
.
Since the original ideal generators from (
4
) have coefficients in
\mathcal{E}
and Gröbner basis computation does not extend the ground field, we conclude that
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{E}
.
Assume that
\mathcal{F}\neq\mathcal{E}
.
Without loss of generality,
g_{1}\not\in\mathcal{F}
.
We choose a basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\Lambda}
of
\mathcal{E}
over
\mathcal{F}
, where
\Lambda
is either
\{1,2,\ldots,\dim_{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{E}\}
if
\dim_{\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{E}<\infty
or
\mathbb{N}
otherwise.
The basis can be chosen so that
e_{1}=1
.
We write
g_{1}
in this basis as
\sum\limits_{i\in\Lambda}c_{i}e_{i}
, where
c_{i}\in\mathcal{F}

for every
i\in\Lambda
.
Since
g_{1}\not\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists
i_{0}\in\Lambda\setminus\{1\}
such that
c_{i_{0}}\neq 0
.
We write
g_{1}=\frac{p_{1}(\mathbf{x})}{q_{1}(\mathbf{x})}
for
p_{1},q_{1}\in k[\mathbf{x}]
.
Let
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
be the normal forms of
p_{1}(\mathbf{y})-c_{1}(\mathbf{x})q_{1}(\mathbf{y})
and
q_{1}(\mathbf{y})
with respect to
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
.
Since reductions do not extend the ground field,
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{y},t]
.
Using the expansion for
g_{1}
with respect to the basis,

can be written as

(p_{1}(\mathbf{y})-c_{1}(\mathbf{x})q_{1}(\mathbf{y}))e_{1}-\sum\limits_{i\in\Lambda\setminus\{1\}}c_{i}(\mathbf{x})q_{1}(\mathbf{y})e_{i}.

Then the normal form of
p_{1}(\mathbf{y})-g_{1}(\mathbf{x})q_{1}(\mathbf{y})
with respect to

is equal to

r_{1}e_{1}-\sum\limits_{i\in\Lambda\setminus\{1\}}c_{i}r_{2}e_{i}.

This expression is equal to zero, so we have
c_{i}r_{2}e_{i}=0
for every

.
In particular,
c_{i_{0}}r_{2}e_{i_{0}}=0
, so

.
Then
q_{1}(\mathbf{y})\in\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}\implies Q(\mathbf{y})\in\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}\implies 1\in\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}

This contradicts the fact that
\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}
has a solution
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}
.
The contradiction implies that
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{E}
as desired.
∎

Example 2.16

(OMS may bear excess)
.

While coefficients of the OMS ideal turn out to be very useful in finding simpler generating sets for subfields of
k(\mathbf{g})
, not all the coefficients are equally useful.
Fix a positive integer
\ell
and consider a subfield in
k(x_{1},x_{2})
generated by
g_{1}=x_{1}+x_{2}
and
g_{2}=\frac{x_{1}^{\ell}}{x_{2}^{\ell}}
.
Then one can show that the degrevlex Gröbner basis of
\operatorname{OMS}_{k(g_{1},g_{2})}
will consist of
y_{1}+y_{2}-(x_{1}+x_{2})
(which gives one generator,
x_{1}+x_{2}
) and the following polynomial:

y_{2}^{\ell}-\underbrace{\frac{\ell x_{2}^{\ell}(x_{1}+x_{2})}{x_{2}^{\ell}+(-1)^{\ell+1}x_{1}^{\ell}}}_{\text{second generator}}y_{2}^{\ell-1}+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\ell-2}(-1)^{\ell-k}\binom{\ell}{k}\underbrace{\frac{(x_{1}+x_{2})^{\ell-k-1}b^{\ell}}{x_{2}^{\ell}+(-1)^{\ell+1}x_{1}^{\ell}}}_{\text{useless coefficients}}y_{2}^{k}.

Not only the majority of the coefficients are
not needed to generate the field but also the degree of the numerator of the extra coefficients may reach
2\ell-1
, which is almost twice larger that of
the original generators.
One observes similar phenomenon when replacing
\operatorname{OMS}_{k(g_{1},g_{2})}
with
\operatorname{eOMS}_{g_{1},g_{2}}
.

Example 2.17

(Dura lex, sed lex)
.

The generating set coming from the coefficients of the OMS ideal may depend significanlty on the ordering used for the Gröbner basis computation.
Consider a tuple
\mathbf{g}:=(x_{1}(x_{2}-x_{3}),x_{2}+x_{3},x_{2}x_{3})
of elements of
k(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
.
Computing the Gröbner basis of
\operatorname{OMS}_{k(\mathbf{g})}
with respect to either the lexicographic or the degrevlex ordering with
y_{1}>y_{2}>y_{3}
yields

y_{3}^{2}-\dashuline{(x_{2}+x_{3})}y_{3}+\dashuline{x_{2}x_{3}},\;\;y_{2}+y_{3}-\dashuline{(x_{2}+x_{3})},\;\;y_{1}+2\dashuline{\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{3}}}y_{3}-\dashuline{\frac{x_{1}(x_{2}+x_{3})}{x_{2}-x_{3}}}.

This basis gives back the original generators
x_{2}+x_{3}
and
x_{2}x_{3}
and provides additional
\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{3}}
and
\frac{x_{1}(x_{2}+x_{3})}{x_{2}-x_{3}}
, which do not look simpler than the original
x_{1}(x_{2}-x_{3})
.
On the other hand, the Gröbner basis of
\operatorname{OMS}_{k(\mathbf{g})}
with respect to either the lexicographic or the degrevlex ordering with
y_{3}>y_{2}>y_{1}
is equal to

y_{1}^{2}-\dashuline{\penalty\ x_{1}^{2}},\;\;y_{2}+\dashuline{\frac{x_{3}-x_{2}}{2x_{1}}}y_{1}-\dashuline{\frac{x_{2}+x_{3}}{2}},\;\;y_{3}+\dashuline{\frac{x_{2}-x_{3}}{2x_{1}}}y_{1}-\dashuline{\frac{x_{2}+x_{3}}{2}}.

Here, a simple generator
x_{2}x_{3}
has disappeared but another simple generator, namely
x_{1}^{2}
, is discovered.

2.8
Randomized subfield membership testing

Lemma

2.12
provides a constructive way to check if a given function
f\in k(\mathbf{x})
belongs to a subfield
k(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
.
However, this approach would involve computing Gröbner basis with coefficients in the field of rational functions
k(\mathbf{x})
.
This may be quite heavy and one of the leitmotifs of the present paper is to avoid such computations by working with specializations of the corresponding ideals.

Notation 2.18

(Specialization of
\operatorname{eOMS}
)
.

In the notation of
Definition

2.10
, consider
\mathbf{a}\in k^{n}
.
We define
\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{a})
by:

\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{a}):=\langle p_{1}(\mathbf{y})q_{1}(\mathbf{a})-q_{1}(\mathbf{y})p_{1}(\mathbf{a}),\ldots,p_{m}(\mathbf{y})q_{m}(\mathbf{a})-q_{m}(\mathbf{y})p_{m}(\mathbf{a}),tQ(\mathbf{y})-1\rangle\subseteq k[\mathbf{y},t].

For membership testing, we will rely on the following fact.

Theorem 2.19

(
[
37
, Theorem 3.3]
)
.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero.
Let
p,p_{1},\ldots,p_{m},q\in k[\mathbf{x}]
, where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
Let
0<\varepsilon<1
be a real number.
We define

d:=\max(\deg q+1,\deg p,\deg p_{1},\ldots,\deg p_{m})\quad\text{ and }\quad M:=\frac{6d^{n+3}}{\varepsilon}.

Let
\mathbf{a}\coloneqq(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
be integers
sampled
uniformly and independently from
[0,M]
.
Consider the field
\mathcal{E}:=k\left(\frac{p_{1}(\mathbf{x})}{q(\mathbf{x})},\ldots,\frac{p_{m}(\mathbf{x})}{q(\mathbf{x})}\right)
.
Then,

1.

if
p(\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{a})-p(\mathbf{a})q(\mathbf{y})\in\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{a})
, then
\frac{p(\mathbf{x})}{q(\mathbf{x})}\in\mathcal{E}
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
;

2.

if
p(\mathbf{y})q(\mathbf{a})-p(\mathbf{a})q(\mathbf{y})\not\in\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{a})
, then
\frac{p(\mathbf{x})}{q(\mathbf{x})}\not\in\mathcal{E}
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.

This theorem yields a randomized Monte-Carlo algorithm for checking field membership requiring Gröbner basis computation only over
k
.
We can go a bit further and use the approach from
[
63
]
to ensure that the ideal
for which the Gröbner basis computation is performed
is of dimension zero.
This is based on the following lemma implicitly contained in
[
63
, Proof of Theorem 1]
(also reminiscent to the use of cross-section in rational invariant computation
[
60
, Section 3]
).

Lemma 2.20
.

Consider a subfield
\mathcal{E}=k(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\subseteq k(\mathbf{x})
, and let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{s}
be a transcendence basis of
k(\mathbf{x})
over
\mathcal{E}
.
Assume that
g\in k(\mathbf{x})
is algebraic over
\mathcal{E}
.
Then
g\in\mathcal{E}\iff g\in\mathcal{E}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{s})
.

Proof.

The implication
g\in\mathcal{E}\implies g\in\mathcal{E}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{s})
is trivial, let us prove the converse.
Assume that
g\in\mathcal{E}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{s})
and
g
is algebraic over
\mathcal{E}
.
Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell}
be all the roots of the minimal polynomial for
g
over
\mathcal{E}
in
\overline{k(\mathbf{x})}
such that
g_{1}=g
.
We write
g=G(z_{1},\ldots,z_{s})
, where
G
is a rational function from
\mathcal{E}(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{s})
with
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{s}
being new indeterminates.
Assume that
G
is nonconstant and, without loss of generality, that it depends nontrivially on
Z_{1}
.
Then
G(z_{1}+X,z_{2},\ldots,z_{s})
is a nonconstant rational function in
X
.
Since
\overline{k}
is infinite, there exists
c\in\overline{k}
such that
G(z_{1}+c,z_{2},\ldots,z_{s})\neq g_{i}
for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant\ell
.
Since
z_{1},\ldots,z_{s}
are transcendental over
\mathcal{E}
, there exists
[
90
, Proposition 2.4(a)]
an automorphism
\alpha\colon\overline{k(\mathbf{x})}\to\overline{k(\mathbf{x})}
such that
\alpha|_{\mathcal{E}}
is the identity,
\alpha(z_{1})=z_{1}+c
(where
c
is viewed as an element of
\overline{k}\subset\overline{k(\mathbf{x})}
), and
\alpha(z_{i})=z_{i}
for every
i=2,\ldots,s
.
Then
\alpha(g)
satisfies the same equation over
\mathcal{E}
as
g
, so it must belong to the set
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell}\}
.
This contradicts the choice of
c
.
Therefore,
G\in\mathcal{E}
, so
g\in\mathcal{E}
.
∎

Combining
Theorem

2.19
and
Lemma

2.20
, we arrive at the following algorithm.

Algorithm 4

Randomized subfield membership check

Input:

polynomials
p,p_{1},\ldots,p_{m},q\in k[\mathbf{x}]
, where
\operatorname{char}k=0
, and a real number
0<\varepsilon<1
;

Output:

True
if
\frac{p}{q}\in k\left(\frac{p_{1}}{q},\ldots,\frac{p_{m}}{q}\right)
, and
False
otherwise.

(Step 1)

Set
d\coloneqq\max(\deg q+1,\deg p,\deg p_{1},\ldots,\deg p_{m})
.

(Step 2)

Compute the Jacobian
J(\mathbf{x})
of
\frac{p_{1}}{q},\ldots,\frac{p_{m}}{q}
(we assume that the gradients are the rows of
J(\mathbf{x})
) and the gradient
G(\mathbf{x})
of
\frac{p}{q}
.

(Step 3)

Sample each coordinate of
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
uniformly at random from
\left[0,\;\frac{2(2\min(m,n)+1)(d-1)}{\varepsilon}\right]\cap\mathbb{Z}
.

(Step 4)

If
G(\mathbf{a})
does not belong to the rowspan of
J(\mathbf{a})
,
return

False
.

(Step 5)

Take any set of columns of
J(\mathbf{a})
forming a basis of the column space of the matrix.
Let
S\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
be the indices these columns.
Set
\overline{S}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus S
.

(Step 6)

Sample
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with each coordinate drawn uniformly at random from
[0,12d^{n+3}/\varepsilon]\cap\mathbb{Z}
.

(Step 7)

Using Gröbner basis, check if
p(\mathbf{x})q(\mathbf{b})-p(\mathbf{b})q(\mathbf{x})
belongs to

\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{p}/q}({\mathbf{b}})+\langle x_{j}-b_{j}\mid j\in\overline{S}\rangle.

(Step 8)

If belongs,
return

True
, otherwise
return

False
.

Proposition 2.21
.

The output of
Algorithm

4
is correct with the probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Denote
g:=\frac{p}{q}
,
g_{1}:=\frac{p_{1}}{q},\ldots,g_{m}:=\frac{p_{m}}{q}
, and
\mathcal{E}:=k(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
.
Let
\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{x})
be the matrix obtained by adding an extra row
G(\mathbf{x})
to
J(\mathbf{x})
.

First, we consider the case when
g
is transcendental over
\mathcal{E}
.
By
[
38
, Proposition 2.4]
, this implies
\operatorname{rank}J(\mathbf{x})<\operatorname{rank}\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{x})
and, in particular, any maximal nonsingular minor of
\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{x})
contains the row correspodning to
G
.
Let
M(\mathbf{x})
be any maximal nonsingular minor of

\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{x})
.
Assume that
q(\mathbf{a})\neq 0
(so that
\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{a})
is well-defined) and
\det M(\mathbf{a})\neq 0
.
Then

\operatorname{rank}J(\mathbf{a})\leqslant\operatorname{rank}J(\mathbf{x})<\operatorname{rank}\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{rank}\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{a}),

so the algorithm will return
False
at
(Step 4)
which will be the correct answer.
The degree of the numerator of
\det M(\mathbf{x})
does not exceed
\min(m,n)(2d-2)
.
Therefore, by the Schwartz-Zippel lemma (
Lemma

2.1
),
\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{x})
will be well-defined under
\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{a}
and the correct result will be returned with the probability at least

1-\frac{d-1+\min(m,n)(2d-2)}{2(2\min(m,n)+1)(d-1)/\varepsilon}=1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}>1-\varepsilon.

Next, we consider the case when
g
is algebraic over
\mathcal{E}
.
In this case,
G(\mathbf{x})
belongs to the rowspan of
J(\mathbf{x})
by
[
38
, Proposition 2.4]
.
Let
M(\mathbf{x})
be a maximal nonsingular minor of
J(\mathbf{x})
.
Assume that
q(\mathbf{a})\neq 0
and
\det M(\mathbf{a})\neq 0
.
Then
\operatorname{rank}J(\mathbf{a})=\operatorname{rank}J(\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{rank}\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{x})\geqslant\operatorname{rank}\widetilde{J}(\mathbf{a})
.
Therefore, the algorithm will not return on
(Step 4)
.

Furthermore, since
\operatorname{rank}J(\mathbf{a})=\operatorname{rank}J(\mathbf{x})
, every set of columns
which is
a basis of the columns space of

is a specialization of a column basis for

.
Thus,
[
38

implies that
\{x_{j}\mid j\in\overline{S}\}
is a transcendence basis of
k(\mathbf{x})
over
\mathcal{E}
.
Then, by

2.20
,

g\in\mathcal{E}
if and only if

.
The latter containment will be assessed by
(Step 7)
with the error probability not exceeding
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
by

2.19
(where the saturation ideal is replaced by the one given by the Rabinowitsch trick, this does not affect the membership).
Using the same calculation as in the first part of the proof, the probability of

vanishing at

also does not exceed

.

.
∎

3
Building blocks of the algorithm

3.1
Basic minimization

We start with a method that follows straightforwardly from the field membership algorithm. Let
\mathbf{g}\subset k(\mathbf{x})
. The following method computes a subset
\mathbf{h}\subseteq\mathbf{g}
that generates the same field as
\mathbf{g}
over
k
and is minimal with respect to inclusion.

Algorithm 5

Computing a minimal subset

Input:

\mathbf{g}\subset k(\mathbf{x})
, where
\operatorname{char}k=0
,
and a real number
0<\varepsilon<1
;

Output:

\mathbf{h}\subseteq\mathbf{g}
such that
k(\mathbf{h})=k(\mathbf{g})
and
\mathbf{h}
is minimal with respect to inclusion.

(Step 1)

Set
\mathbf{h}\coloneqq(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m})\coloneqq\mathbf{g}
.

(Step 2)

For
i=1,\ldots,m
do

(a)

If
h_{i}\in k(\mathbf{h}\setminus\{h_{i}\})
with probability at least
1-\frac{\varepsilon}{m}
using
Algorithm

4
, then set
\mathbf{h}\coloneqq\mathbf{h}\setminus\{h_{i}\}
.

(Step 3)

Return

\mathbf{h}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Algorithm

5
is correct with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.

Proof.

To prove correctness, it suffices to show that
k(\mathbf{h})=k(\mathbf{g})
and that there does not exist
h\in\mathbf{h}
such that
k(\mathbf{h}\setminus\{h\})=k(\mathbf{h})
.
Initially, we have
k(\mathbf{h})=k(\mathbf{g})
, and the field generated by
\mathbf{h}
does not change during the iterations.
Thus, it suffices to show that such
h
does not exist.
Assume that it does. Then,
h\in k(\mathbf{h}\setminus\{h\})
. However, at some iteration
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we had
h=h_{i}
, so
h
would have been removed from
\mathbf{h}
, which is a contradiction.

There are
m
independent applications of
Algorithm

4
, so we combine
Proposition

2.21
with the Bernoulli inequality to obtain that the overall probability of correctness is at least

\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{m}\right)^{m}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon.

∎

Remark 3.2

(On simplicity)
.

We note that result depends on the order of elements in the input
\mathbf{g}
. We may order
\mathbf{g}
so that ,
simple elements come last,
so that they are used to remove other elements from the list.
In practice, we use the following total order. Let
a/b,p/q\in k(\mathbf{x})
. Recall that for

,
d_{f}
is the degree of

and

is the number of terms in

We will ensure

We say that

, or, in the case of equality,

•

if

3.2
Low-degree coefficients of Gröbner bases over rational function fields

As we have seen, simple generators can be obtained from the low-degree coefficients of Gröbner bases of OMS ideals.
In this section, we show how to compute those coefficients efficiently using sparse interpolation.
We note that our algorithm is not restricted to OMS ideals and can be used with arbitrary ideals over the field of rational functions.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in k(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{y}]
, where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t})
.
In this section, all computations are done with respect to the same fixed monomial ordering.
Let
G
be the reduced Gröbner basis of
\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle
. We have
G=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{\eta})\subset k(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{y}]
, where for every
i=1,\ldots,\eta
,

g_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{s_{g_{i}}}c_{i,j}\Pi(\mathbf{y}^{\bm{\alpha}_{i,j}}),

where for every
j=1,\ldots,s_{g_{i}}
we have
c_{i,j}=p_{i,j}/q_{i,j}\in k(\mathbf{x})\setminus\{0\}
with coprime
p_{i,j},q_{i,j}\in k[\mathbf{x}]\setminus\{0\}
, and
\bm{\alpha}_{i,j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}
are pairwise different.

Let
d
be an integer and

\displaystyle A_{\leqslant d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(i,j)\mid\deg p_{i,j}+\deg q_{i,j}\leqslant d,\penalty\ i=1,\ldots,\eta,\penalty\ j=1,\ldots,s_{g_{i}}\},

(5)

\displaystyle C_{\leqslant d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{c_{i,j}\mid(i,j)\in A_{\leqslant d}\}\subset k(\mathbf{x}).

Note
C_{\leqslant d}

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in k(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{y}]
and any monomial ordering, there exists a nonzero polynomial
g(\mathbf{x})\in k[\mathbf{x}]
with the following property.
For every
\mathbf{a}\in k^{n}
such that
g(\mathbf{a})\neq 0
the sets of polynomials in
k[\mathbf{y}]
are well-defined and equal:

•

the reduced Gröbner basis of
\langle f_{1}(\mathbf{a})(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,f_{m}(\mathbf{a})(\mathbf{y})\rangle
;

•

the image of the reduced Gröbner basis of
\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle\subset k(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{y}]
under the substitution
\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{a}
.

Proof.

Consider the run of the Buchberger’s algorithm for
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
over
k(\mathbf{x})
.
We set
g
to be the least common multiple of all the denominators occurring in the input and during the computation as well as the numerators of the leading coefficients of all input and intermediate polynomials.
Consider
\mathbf{a}\in k^{n}
such that
g(\mathbf{a})\neq 0
.
The Buchberger’s algorithm, in addition to polynomial operations, queries the coefficients of polynomials in the following cases:

1.

it queries the leading terms to form critical pairs and compute S-polynomials;

2.

it queries all the nonzero coefficients of a polynomial when reducing it.

We consider the runs of the algorithm for
f_{1}(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,f_{m}(\mathbf{y})
and for its specialization
f_{1}(\mathbf{a})(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,f_{m}(\mathbf{a})(\mathbf{y})
.
Due to the assumption
g(\mathbf{a})\neq 0
, the queries of the first type will yield the same leading monomials and the returned coefficients will commute with the specialization.
For the queries of the second type, the only possible difference is if a non-leading coefficient has vanished under specialization.
In this case, we will assume that its coefficient is still returned but is equal to zero, so there will be an extra reduction performed with zero coefficient which will not affect the result.
Therefore, the polynomial operations performed will be the same regardless if the specialization was performed before the Gröbner basis computation or after.
∎

Lemma 3.6

Let

Then

In the opposite direction, assume that there is

Notation 3.7

Let

Remark 4.4

Remark 4.5

(Computing with zero-dimensional ideals)
.

Field membership (
Algorithm

4
) specializes some indeterminates in the OMS ideal until the ideal becomes
zero-dimensional.
In theory, a similar specialization could be used for computing the coefficients of Gröbner bases in
Algorithm

6
.
In this case, in some polynomials in the resulting ideal, the monomials may glue together, and their coefficients (which are multivariate rational functions) combined may become large. In our experiments, we observed that this makes interpolation harder, and overall slows down the algorithm despite using zero-dimensional ideals.

Remark 4.6
.

After we have computed the coefficients of the Gröbner basis that generate the field, we can use them instead of the original generators in the subsequent steps of the algorithm (as in
(Step 4)
). This typically speeds up the computation when these coefficients are simpler than the original generators.

Remark 4.7

The generating set computed by

(Step 4)

5.3
Sparse interpolation over finite fields

For interpolating sparse multivariate rational functions over finite fields, we implemented the algorithm by van der Hoeven and Lecerf
[
118
]
discussed in
Section

. We also implemented the algorithm by Cuyt and Lee
[
28
]
because, in theory, it may require fewer blackbox evaluations; see
[
118
, Remark 3.]
. In most of our examples, the algorithms used the same number of evaluations, so we prefer van der Hoeven and Lecerf’s algorithm because it was easier to implement efficiently.

For interpolating sparse multivariate polynomials over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we use the Ben-Or and Tiwari algorithm
[
10
]
with the admissible ratio
\bm{\omega}=(2,3,5,\ldots,p_{n})
and recover exponents via integer factoring (see
Section

2.4
). Therefore, to interpolate polynomials of degree
d
, we must have
p_{n}^{d}<q
.
To facilitate efficient arithmetic in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we choose

to be a prime that fits in
64
bits.
For example, with
d=8
, the largest number of variables such that
p_{n}^{d}<2^{64}
is
n=54
, which has been sufficient in our use cases.
An alternative would have been Kronecker substitution (
[
120

,
[
4
]
), which requires, roughly,
d^{n}<q
.
Our choice between the two is motivated by the fact that we usually have

We double the number of evaluations until interpolation succeeds.
We found this approach easier to implement in an efficient way than the early termination strategies (as, for example, in
[
69
,
70
]
), since we could rely on the existing Páde approximation and root-finding functionality from Nemo.jl.
Thus, our implementation overestimates the required number of blackbox evaluations by at most a factor of two.

\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\in k^{n}
.
For
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, we wish to compute
the reduced Gröbner basis of
\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{a}_{i})
.
With the use of tracing, the computation is organized in two steps:

(Learn)

Run a modified Buchberger’s algorithm to compute the Gröbner basis of
\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{a}}_{1})
. The modified algorithm additionally records the information about which S-polynomials reduced to zero; this information could be stored as a collection of indices of the corresponding iterations of the algorithm.

(Apply)

For
i=2,\ldots,\ell
, run a (differently) modified Buchberger’s algorithm to compute the Gröbner basis of
\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{a}}_{i})
.
This version runs as usual but does not reduce the S-polynomials that correspond to the ones that reduced to zero during the
(Learn)
stage,
since those are expected to reduce to zero.

Correctness of this method
can be analyzed using
Lemma

3.3
.
Indeed, there exists a nonzero polynomial
g\in k(\mathbf{x})
such that for every
\mathbf{b}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{2}\in k^{n}

if

g(\mathbf{b}_{1})\neq 0
and
g(\mathbf{b}_{2})\neq 0
then
the Buchberger’s algorithm applied to the generators of
\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{b}}_{1})
and
\operatorname{eOMS}_{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{b}}_{2})
produces the same S-polynomials and the same reductions of S-polynomials (that is, with the same support but possibly different values of coefficients). Thus, when for every
i=1,\ldots,\ell
we have
g(\mathbf{a}_{i})\neq 0
, the method correctly computes all Gröbner bases.

Gröbner tracing fits naturally in
Algorithm

6
. Of course, we might not be able to ensure that for every
i=1,\ldots,\ell
we have
g(\mathbf{a}_{i})\neq 0
, but the probabilistic statement in
Proposition

3.4
holds.
In our implementation,
k
is the field of integers modulo a prime, where the prime fits in
64
bits.
We use the F4 algorithm
[
39
]
instead of the Buchberger’s algorithm for more
efficiency.
Tracing in the F4 algorithm can be arranged similarly, with a difference that instead of S-polynomials we have rows of a Macaulay matrix.
We use the package Groebner.jl
[
33
]
, which implements the F4 algorithm and tracing as described above.

]
.
Then we will showcase simplifications achieved by our algorithm using several systems from modeling literature and demonstrate that the simplified generating set is better suited to derive insights about the structure of the model.
However, since mathematical modeling is not a central topic in this paper, we will not get deep into the domain-specific details.

We consider a parametric system of differential equations

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{g}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{x}),

(8)

where
\mathbf{x}
is a vector of unknown functions of time,
\bm{\mu}
is a vector of scalar parameters, and
\mathbf{g}
is a vector of rational functions from
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu})
defining the dynamics.
The system may also involve external inputs, we will omit them to simplify the presentation.
In a typical experimental setup, it is not feasible to measure the values of all the coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
.
Thus, data is available only for some functions of
\mathbf{x}
and
\bm{\mu}
typically referred to as
outputs
of the model.
For the sake of simplicity, we will consider the case of a single output
y=f(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{x})
.

A parameter
\mu_{i}
or, more generally, a function
h(\bm{\mu})
of parameters is
(structurally) identifiable
if its value can be uniquely reconstructed from the output data assuming continuous and noise-free measurements (see
[
56
, Section 2]
for more details).
Since identifiability is a prerequisite for meaningful parameter estimation, it is an important and desirable property.
The set of identifiable functions can be shown to be closed under addition, multiplication, and division, so they form
the field of identifiable functions
.
The elements of this field (also called identifiable combinations) are exactly the quantities whose values can be in principle inferred from data, so computing a convenient representation of this field is a natural question.

Example 7.1
.

Consider a model

x_{1}^{\prime}=\mu_{1}x_{2},\quad x_{2}^{\prime}=-\mu_{2}x_{1},

and assume that the observation is
y=x_{1}
, the first coordinate.
First, we observe that the scaling
x_{2}\to\lambda x_{2},\mu_{2}\to\lambda\mu_{2},\mu_{1}\to\frac{1}{\lambda}\mu_{1}
preserves the output function for any nonzero constant
\lambda
.
Thus, neither of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
can be identified even from perfect knowledge of
y
.
On the other hand,
y^{\prime\prime}=-\mu_{1}\mu_{2}y
, so the product
\mu_{1}\mu_{2}
can be found as long as
y
is not identically zero.

The classical approach for finding a generating set of the field of identifiable functions going back to
[
98
]
is the following.
First, we compute the minimal polynomial differential equation (first with respect to the order, then degree) with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}(\bm{\mu})
satisfied by
y
(called
the input-output
equation).
By normalizing one of the coefficients to one, we can write it as

m_{0}(y,\ldots,y^{(h)})+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}(\bm{\mu})m_{i}(y,\ldots,y^{(h)})=0,

(9)

where
m_{0},\ldots,m_{M}
are distinct monomials in
y,y^{\prime},\ldots,y^{(h)}
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{M}\in\mathbb{C}(\bm{\mu})
.
Then, under certain mild assumptions, the functions
c_{1},\ldots,c_{M}
generate the whole field of identifiable functions
[
100
, Corollary 1]
.

Example 7.2

(
Example

7.1
continued)
.

The minimal differential equation satisfied by
y
in this example is
y^{\prime\prime}+\mu_{1}\mu_{2}y=0
.
Therefore,
[
100
, Corollary 1]
implies that identifiable functions for this model are precisely rational functions in
\mu_{1}\mu_{2}
.

Unlike the toy example above, in more realistic models, the functions
c_{1},\ldots,c_{M}
are often large and complicated (see
Appendix

A
); this makes such generating set practically useless.
Thus, the challenge is, given a generating set
c_{1},\ldots,c_{M}
, to produce a
simpler
one which can later be understood and interpreted by a modeler.
Below we will demonstrate our simplification algorithm on several fields coming from the structural identifiability problem.

Example 7.4

(
SIRT

.

We consider an epidemiological model describing a spread of infection which takes into account that the individuals receiving treatment are less infectious
[
117

:

\begin{cases}S^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{\beta S(t)I(t)}{N}-\frac{\delta\beta S(t)T(t)}{N},\\

\bm{\mu}&=&[\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\nu,N]\end{matrix}

(12)

In this model,
S
and
I
denote the number of susceptible and infectious individuals, respectively, while
T
stands for the number of individuals receiving treatment.
The observation is taken to be
y(t)=T(t)
.

The generating set of the field of identifiable functions extracted directly from the input-output equation (
9
) consists of twelve rational elements: polynomials up to degree six and rational functions with linear denominator and numerator up to degree five.

Algorithm

8
produces the following generating set for the same field:

\alpha+\gamma+\nu,\quad\nu(\alpha+\gamma),\quad\nu+\delta\gamma,\quad\frac{N\gamma}{\beta}.

(13)

The first two generators are the elementary symmetric functions in
\alpha+\gamma
and
\nu
hinting at a symmetry between the outflow rates of the

and

[

and
[

, Example 4.1]
).

To facilitate the interpretation of the third generator in (

), we observe that the nonlinear terms in the first two equations (

) can be factored as

suggesting

(“total infectiousness”) as a natural quantity to consider.
Rewriting the equation for

\widetilde{I}(t)

T^{\prime}(t)=\gamma\widetilde{I}(t)-(\dashuline{\nu+\gamma\delta})T(t),

In this model,
S
and
I
denote the number of susceptible and infectious individuals, respectively.

and

stand for the number of individuals in the latent and remission stages, respectively.
Furthermore,

is an external

(the proportion of vaccinated individuals) and the observation is taken to be the prevalence (the proportion of infectious individuals), that is,

.

.

7.2
Identifiability and observability for discrete-time systems

In this section we will apply our algorithm for the problems of similar flavor as in the previous subsection but for models in discrete time.
The general setup is very similar although the computation will be quite different.
Specifically, we consider a parametric system of difference equations (cf. (
8
)):

\mathbf{x}(t+1)=\mathbf{g}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{x}(t)),

(15)

where
\mathbf{x}(t)
is a vector of time-series (for
t=0,1,2,\ldots
),
\bm{\mu}
is a vector of unknown parameters, and
\mathbf{g}
is a vector of rational functions from
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu})
.
We assume that we observe an output time series
y(t)=f(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{x}(t))
, and are interested in identifiable functions in this context.
In other words, we are given a sequence
y(0),y(1),y(2),\ldots
or a finite part of it, and we want to find out which functions of parameters
\bm{\mu}
and initial conditions
\mathbf{x}(0)
we can reconstruct.
For generic values of
\bm{\mu}
and
\mathbf{x}(0)
(so that none of the relevant denominators vanish), these functions form a field which we will call the field of identifiable functions (in the literature, for initial conditions
\mathbf{x}(0)
, term
observable functions
is also often employed).

Despite of the similarity of the general question to the one in Section
7.1
, the approach via input-output equations does not work in the difference case as well as it does in the differential (see
[
84
]
for a discussion).
Therefore, we will use the following more direct method (cf.
[
72
]
).
We define rational functions
\mathbf{g}^{\circ j}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{x}(0))
by

\mathbf{g}^{\circ 0}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{x}(0)):=\mathbf{x}(0)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{g}^{\circ(j+1)}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{x}(0)):=\mathbf{g}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{g}^{\circ j}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{x}(0)))\text{ for }j\geqslant 0.

Comparing with (
15
), we observe that
\mathbf{x}(j)=\mathbf{g}^{(j)}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{x}(0))
.
Thus,
y(j)
can be written as a rational function
f(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{g}^{(j)}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{x}(0)))
, and the field of functions identifiable from
y(0),\ldots,y(\ell)
is generated by these rational functions for
j=0,\ldots,\ell
.
However, as we will see below, these rational functions become complicated very quickly, so simplification is a key step in finding an interpretable set of generators.

Example 7.6

(General fractional-linear model)
.

We will consider a one-dimensional model with dynamics defined by a general fractional-linear function:

x(t+1)=\frac{\mu_{1}x(t)+\mu_{2}}{\mu_{3}x(t)+\mu_{4}},\quad y(t)=x(t).

We start with computing simple generators for the field generated by the values of the output at
t=0,1,2,3
.
These values can be written as rational functions in
\bm{\mu},x(0)
as follows:

\displaystyle y(0)

\displaystyle=x(0),\;\;y(1)=\frac{\mu_{1}x(0)+\mu_{2}}{\mu_{3}x(0)+\mu_{4}},\;\;y(2)=\frac{(\mu_{1}^{2}+\mu_{2}\mu_{3})x(0)+\mu_{1}\mu_{2}+\mu_{2}\mu_{4}}{(\mu_{1}\mu_{3}+\mu_{3}\mu_{4})x(0)+\mu_{2}\mu_{3}+\mu_{4}^{2}}

\displaystyle y(3)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mu_{1}^{3}+2\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}+\mu_{2}\mu_{3}\mu_{4})x(0)+\mu_{1}^{2}\mu_{2}+\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{4}+\mu_{2}^{2}\mu_{3}+\mu_{2}\mu_{4}^{2}}{(\mu_{1}^{2}\mu_{3}+\mu_{1}\mu_{3}\mu_{4}+\mu_{2}\mu_{3}^{2}+\mu_{3}\mu_{4}^{2})x(0)+\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}+2\mu_{2}\mu_{3}\mu_{4}+\mu_{4}^{3}}

Our simplification algorithm finds the following much simpler generators for the same field:

x(0),\;\;\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{4}},\;\;\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{4}},\;\;\frac{\mu_{3}}{\mu_{4}}.

One can show that taking further time points will not add any new information since the fraction in the right-hand side is invariant under multiplying all the
\mu
’s by the same number.
On the other hand,
the output of our algorithm suggests
that the fields obtained by taking fewer time points are strictly smaller.
Therefore, the field of identifiable functions is generated by
x(0),\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{4}},\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{4}},\frac{\mu_{3}}{\mu_{4}}
and four time points are enough to find these values.

Example 7.7

(Logistic growth)
.

The following logistic growth model is used, for example, in population modeling
[
3
, p. 12]
:

x(t+1)=x(t)\left(1+\mu_{1}\left(1-\frac{x(t)}{\mu_{2}}\right)\right).

We will further assume that what is observed is an unknown but fixed fraction of the population, that is,
y(t)=\mu_{3}x(t)
.
In order to find identifiable functions, we will consider the field generated by
y(0),y(1)
, and
y(2)
, where the latter is of the form
\frac{P(x(0),\bm{\mu})}{\mu_{2}^{3}}
with
\deg P=8
.
In contrast to this, our algorithm finds the following generators for the same field

\mu_{3}x(0),\;\;\mu_{1},\;\;\mu_{2}\mu_{3}.

By repeating this computation with
y(4)
, one can show that adding new time points does not change the generators.
Since the field is smaller if only one or two time points are taken, we conclude that the three functions above generate the field of identifiable functions and knowledge of only three data points is generically enough to reconstruct them.

Example 7.8

(SIS/SIR epidemiological model)
.

The following model combines the dynamics of discrete-time SIS and SIR models
[
2
, Sections 3 and 4]
:

\begin{cases}S(t+1)=S(t)(1-\beta I(t))+\alpha I(t),\\
I(t+1)=I(t)(1-\alpha-\gamma+\beta S(t)),\\
R(t+1)=R(t)+\gamma I(t),\\
y(t)=\eta I(t),\end{cases}\quad\text{where}\quad\begin{matrix}\mathbf{x}&=&[S,\;I,\;R]\\
\bm{\mu}&=&[\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\eta]\end{matrix}

(16)

As in the previous examples, we compute the field generated by the values
y(0),y(1),\ldots
and observe that after
y(0),\ldots,y(3)
the field stabilizes.
Simplifying
y(0),\ldots,y(3)
, where the latter is a polynomial of degree
16
with
102
terms, we obtain a much simpler generating set of identifiable functions:

\gamma,\;\;\eta I(0),\;\;\beta I(0),\;\;\beta S(0)-\alpha.

These simpler expressions also admit biological interpretations:
\eta I(0)
is the observed number of infections at
t=0
,
\beta I(0)
is essentially the total infection rate at
t=0
, and
\alpha-\beta S(0)
is the total rate of the flow
from the
I
-compartment to the
S
-compartment.

7.3
Fields of invariants

Let
G
be a group and consider its action on an
n
-dimensional
k
-vector space
V
(in other words, a homomorphism
G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
).
This action naturally defines the action of
G
on the ring of polynomials
k[V]
and the field of rational fractions
k(V)
.

Invariant theory
studies the invariants of this action: the ring of polynomial invariants
k[V]^{G}
and the field of rational invariants
k(V)^{G}
.
Historically, the invariant theory was focused on the polynomial invariants (see, e.g.
[
122
]
), but in the recent years rational invariants have attracted attention
[
7
,
59
,
77
,
15
]
.
From the applications point of view, invariants are useful to characterize data (signals or images) which is defined up to a group action
[
7
]
.
The degrees of the generators are related to the sampling complexity of the reconstruction problem for such data
[
7
]
, and the generators themselves can be used to distinguish different orbits with respect to the action
[
44
]
.

A general algorithm for computing a generating set of
k(V)^{G}
for an algebraic group
G
was proposed by Hubert and Kogan in
[
60
]
(see also
[
74
]
).
The method is reminiscent of the computation of a generating set via the OMS ideals (as in
Lemma

2.12
): the resulting generating set is composed of the coefficients of the reduced Gröbner basis of a certain ideal over the rational function field
k(V)
.
In the following examples, we will demonstrate how the output of this algorithm can be further simplified using the methods and software described in this paper.
For the computations below, we used an implementation of the Hubert-Kogan algorithm from the AIDA
[
61
]
Maple package.

Example 7.9
.

Consider the following subgroup
G\subset\operatorname{GL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
:

G:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\
0&a^{-1}&0\\
0&0&a^{2}\end{pmatrix}\mid a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},\;b,c\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

The group acts on the space of
3\times 3
matrices by left multiplication.
We will parametrize the
3\times 3
matrices with coordinates
z_{1},\ldots,z_{9}
as follows:

Z:=\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}&z_{2}&z_{3}\\
z_{4}&z_{5}&z_{6}\\
z_{7}&z_{8}&z_{9}\end{pmatrix}.

Output of the AIDA package consists of 16 rational functions including
\frac{z_{5}}{z_{6}},\frac{z_{4}}{z_{6}},\frac{z_{7}}{z_{9}},\frac{z_{8}}{z_{9}}
and twelve more complicated fractions with degrees of numerators and denominators ranging from two to six.
Our simplification algorithm find the following more concise generating set:

\frac{z_{4}}{z_{6}},\;\frac{z_{5}}{z_{6}},\;\frac{z_{7}}{z_{9}},\;\frac{z_{8}}{z_{9}},\;z_{6}^{2}z_{9},\;\frac{z_{6}\operatorname{det}(Z)}{z_{5}z_{9}-z_{6}z_{8}}.

The fact that the numerator of the last fraction is of the form
z_{6}\operatorname{det}(Z)
is not detected by the algorithm automatically, we wrote it this way to be more concise and show that the algorithm may find “interpretable” expressions.

Example 7.10

(
\operatorname{SL}_{2}
-invariants from
[
35
]
)
.

Following
[
60
, Example 4.1]
, we consider the following seven-dimensional representation of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
from
[
35
, Example 6.4]
.
A matrix
\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{2}\\
\lambda_{3}&\lambda_{4}\end{pmatrix}
sends a vector
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{7})
to a vector
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{7})
, where

\displaystyle g_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}z_{1}+\lambda_{2}z_{2},\quad g_{2}=\lambda_{3}z_{1}+\lambda_{4}z_{2},\quad g_{3}=\lambda_{1}z_{3}+\lambda_{2}z_{4},\quad g_{4}=\lambda_{3}z_{3}+\lambda_{4}z_{4},

\displaystyle g_{5}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}^{2}z_{5}+2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}z_{6}+\lambda_{2}^{2}z_{7},\quad g_{6}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}z_{5}+(1+2\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3})z_{6}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{4}z_{7},

\displaystyle g_{7}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{3}^{2}z_{5}+2\lambda_{4}\lambda_{3}z_{6}+\lambda_{4}^{2}z_{7}.

A direct application of the Hubert-Kogan algorithm gives a list consisting of two quadratic polynomials and eight rational functions with degrees of numerators and denominators ranging from two to four.
However, as discussed in
[
60
, Example 4.1]
, if one knows a simple cross-section for the orbits, it can be used to get the following simpler generating set:

z_{1}z_{4}-z_{2}z_{3},\;\;z_{1}^{2}z_{7}-2z_{1}z_{2}z_{6}+z_{2}^{2}z_{5},\;\;\frac{z_{3}^{2}z_{7}-2z_{3}z_{4}z_{6}+z_{4}^{2}z_{5}}{(z_{1}z_{4}-z_{2}z_{3})^{2}},\;\;\frac{z_{1}z_{3}z_{7}-z_{1}z_{4}z_{6}-z_{2}z_{3}z_{6}+z_{2}z_{4}z_{5}}{z_{1}z_{4}-z_{2}z_{3}}.

On the other hand, we can apply our algorithm (with the result minimized using
Algorithm

5
) to the ten original generators obtained without any additional insights on the group action and find the following slightly simpler generators:

z_{1}z_{4}-z_{2}z_{3},\;\;z_{5}z_{7}-z_{6}^{2},\;\;z_{3}^{2}z_{7}-2z_{3}z_{4}z_{6}+z_{4}^{2}z_{5},\;\;z_{1}z_{3}z_{7}-z_{1}z_{4}z_{6}-z_{2}z_{3}z_{6}+z_{2}z_{4}z_{5}.

Note that this way we recover a subset of the generators of the ring of invariants from
[
35
, Example 6.4]
.

Example 7.11

(Moment invariants for 2D images)
.

In this example, we will focus on
moment invariants
which is a popular tool in computer vision and patter recognition
[
58
,
44
,
42
,
54
]
.
For our purposes, for any fixed integer
d
, the space of moments
M_{d}
will simply be the space of nonconstant monomials of degree at most
d
in variables
x
and
y
.
For example,
M_{2}
is spanned by
x,y,x^{2},xy,y^{2}
.
The monomial
x^{i}y^{j}
will be denoted by
m_{i,j}
.
We will consider the action of the planar rotation group
\operatorname{SO}(2)
induced by its action on the
(x,y)
-plane.
More precisely:

\text{for }R=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\operatorname{SO}(2)\rightsquigarrow R\cdot m(x,y):=m(ax+by,cx+dy).

For example,
m_{2,0}+m_{0,2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
is an invariant of this action since rotations preserve the distance to the origin.
The same is true for
m_{1,0}^{2}+m_{0,1}^{2}
which represents the same polynomial in
x
and
y
but is different as a function of the moments and thus is considered as a different invariant.

The problem of computing invariants of this action goes back to the work of Hu
[
58
]
who proposed a set of seven invariants in the
d=3
case.
This set turned out to be redundant
[
44
]
and was not complete (which is a desirable property, see
[
43
]
).
A new irredundant set of invariants was proposed by Flusser
[
44
]
, this set was not generating the whole field
k(M_{3})^{\operatorname{SO}(2)}
but formed its transcendence basis.
Another set was proposed in
[
54
]
which was still not a generating set of
k(M_{3})^{\operatorname{SO}(2)}
.
In the same paper, the Hubert-Kogan algorithm was applied to the problem
[
54
, p. 231]
but the resulting invariants were deemed too complicated.
Indeed, the output of the algorithm for
k(M_{3})^{\operatorname{SO}(2)}
takes about two screens to display.
However, after applying our simplification algorithm (with the result minimized using
Algorithm

5
) to the output of Hubert-Kogan algorithm, we arrive at the following set of nine generators:

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=m_{2,0}+m_{0,2},\quad f_{2}=m_{2,0}m_{0,2}-m_{1,1}^{2},

\displaystyle f_{3}

\displaystyle=m_{0,1}^{2}+m_{1,0}^{2},

\displaystyle f_{4}

\displaystyle=m_{3,0}m_{1,2}-m_{2,1}^{2}+m_{2,1}m_{0,3}-m_{1,2}^{2},

\displaystyle f_{5}

\displaystyle=m_{3,0}^{2}+3m_{3,0}m_{1,2}+3m_{2,1}m_{0,3}+m_{0,3}^{2},

\displaystyle f_{6}

\displaystyle=m_{0,1}m_{3,0}+m_{0,1}m_{1,2}-m_{1,0}m_{2,1}-m_{1,0}m_{0,3},

\displaystyle f_{7}

\displaystyle=m_{0,1}^{2}m_{0,2}+2m_{0,1}m_{1,0}m_{1,1}+m_{1,0}^{2}m_{2,0},

\displaystyle f_{8}

\displaystyle=m_{2,0}m_{2,1}m_{0,3}-m_{2,0}m_{1,2}^{2}-m_{1,1}m_{3,0}m_{0,3}+m_{1,1}m_{2,1}m_{1,2}+m_{0,2}m_{3,0}m_{1,2}-m_{0,2}m_{2,1}^{2},

\displaystyle f_{9}

\displaystyle=m_{0,1}m_{2,0}m_{0,3}-2m_{0,1}m_{1,1}m_{1,2}+m_{0,1}m_{0,2}m_{2,1}+m_{1,0}m_{2,0}m_{1,2}-2m_{1,0}m_{1,1}m_{2,1}+m_{1,0}m_{0,2}m_{3,0}.

Unlike the lists exhibited in
[
44
,
54
]
, this set of invariants is complete in the strongest sense, as it generates
k(M_{3})^{\operatorname{SO}(2)}
.
It has lower degrees than the list from
[
44
]
(3 instead of 4) and smaller maximal monomial count than
[
54
, p. 231]
(6 instead of 15).
On the other hand, our list recovers several classical invariants, so it can be viewed as a natural extension of the earlier lists:

•

f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are equivalent to
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
in the Hu’s list
[
58
,
44
]
; the same holds for
f_{4}
and
f_{5}
above and
\varphi_{4}
and
\varphi_{5}
.

•

f_{3}
and
f_{7}
are the first and the third elements, respectively, of the list given in
[
54
, p. 231]
.

Therefore, we expect that out simplification algorithm can help to get the best of the two worlds: the strong completeness guarantees of the Hubert-Kogan algorithm and compactness and interpretability of the invariants derived in the pattern recognition community.
