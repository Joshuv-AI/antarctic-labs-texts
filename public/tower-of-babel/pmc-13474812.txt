“Creation involved a lot of inspiration which drove me to create, and the creation was a record of myself at different moments”: a nine-month photovoice study of creative practices and recovery from bipolar disorder
Authors: Samson Tse, Winnie W Y Yuen, Iris W K Lo, Sheri L Johnson, Erin E Michalak, Luke Clark, Theresa S K Luk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany individuals with bipolar disorders (BD) value creativity, and some consider it a strength associated with BD. Existing literature supports a link between creativity and BD. This photovoice study represents the first of its kind, offering novel insights into the complex relationships among mood, creativity, and the lived experiences of individuals with BD in a non-Western cultural setting.MethodsOur research team recruited 10 Chinese individuals in recovery from BD. They participated in either painting or expressive writing. Participants used a photovoice methodology over a nine-month period to document their creative processes and recovery, culminating in a public art exhibition and “human library” event, sharing their experiences and reducing stigma.ResultsThe study revealed that mood had a significant influence on participants’ creativity over time. During hypomanic episodes, some participants described heightened creativity, characterized by bursts of inspiration and increased output. Some participants reported an enhanced sense of perception during depressive episodes. Participants also reported that their ambitions and ability to execute their plans for achieving their goals seemed to play a significant role in explaining the relationship between mood episodes and creativity levels. Through activities such as drawing and writing, the participants gained a deeper understanding of themselves, exploring and making sense of their thoughts, emotions, and experiences. The sharing of creative work on social media during the study period emerged as a catalyst for fostering community, motivation, and self-confidence. Limitations include all‑female participants with high school or higher education levels, and variability in attendance and artistic output.ConclusionsThis investigation offers insights into strengths-based approaches to supporting those with BD through the transformative power of the creative arts. The participants in this study emphasized the importance of finding meaning in their creative goals and cultivating determination by connecting their aspirations to personal values and intrinsic strengths. The present findings add to studies exploring the talents and skills of Chinese individuals with BD and to reducing stigma toward this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08232-5.Keywords: Creativity, Strengths-based, Chinese, Hypomanic, Depression

Background

Traditional research on bipolar disorder (BD) has focused on the symptoms and maladaptive functioning associated with this severe form of mental illness [1–3]. In recent decades, a focus on personal recovery has emerged as an alternative discourse that describes the individual trajectories of people with mental illnesses [4, 5]. Research on the factors that contribute to personal recovery among people with BD has identified respect, hope, empowerment [6], and social support [7] as essential components in their recovery. Taking a strengths-based approach in which individuals with BD are encouraged to use their personal talents could instill hope and empowerment, which could in turn promote recovery [8, 9].

Bipolar disorder and creativity

Many individuals with BD value creativity, and some consider it a strength associated with BD. Several findings support the link between creativity and BD. In early research, symptoms of BD were found among prominent authors [10, 11], poets [12], and artists [13]. For example, in a review of over 1,000 people engaged in eight creative professions, whose biographies were reviewed in the New York Times Book Review, Ludwig reported that 8.2% appeared to have had experiences of mania [14]. Although studies of famous individuals involved in the creative works may be biased, similar profiles have emerged in examinations of national representative datasets, in which those diagnosed with BD and their family members were found to be more likely to practice creative professions than those without BD [15, 16].

Several studies suggest that those at high risk of BD and those with mild forms of BD show particularly heightened creativity. Richards and colleagues proposed an inverted U-shaped relationship between illness severity and creativity; among a small sample, they found that those who experienced hypomania and mild forms of BD showed higher levels of creativity than people without BD, but also those who experienced full manic episodes [17]. Similarly, using a large-scale, representative sample, another study found that individuals with a family history of BD but who had not personally experienced manic episodes showed elevations in creativity [16].

The findings regarding an association between BD and creativity have led many to consider processes that might explain this relationship [18, 19]. Greenwood suggested that people with BD share similar trait dispositions and cognitive characteristics with individuals involved in the creative process [20]. For example, individuals with BD and creative talents have been found to score higher in openness compared with the general population [21], with a tendency to seek new experiences, which could promote their ability to connect ideas in a novel manner [22, 23]. Individuals who are prone to mania have been found to exhibit elevated lifetime ambitions for success [24]. They are also more likely to commit to creative goals and succeed in creative pursuits [25].

Although some of these cognitive and personality characteristics may be present during periods of euthymia, people with BD can experience frequent fluctuations in their mood, which are important to consider in evaluating creativity. When manic symptoms are present, individuals report high levels of positive affect, energy, impulsivity, and confidence. In one study, the presence of hypomanic was tightly correlated with self-rated creativity [26]. Further associations have been shown between BD and divergent and magical thinking [2, 27]. The unusual perceptions of individuals in a manic state may enable people with BD to make idiosyncratic associations that nurture creativity [28, 29].

Considerable research suggests that depressive symptoms can deter the production of creative products [30]. Depressive symptoms may also diminish divergent thinking [31]. Johnson and colleagues wrote that “it is possible that creativity is not a trait-like feature of BD, but rather ebbs and flows in a state-like manner. Eminence and lifetime accomplishment could be driven by intermittent bouts of creative thoughts, perhaps related to mood variability among those with BD” [18]. Despite the potential importance of symptom fluctuations and other dynamic processes, very few studies have taken a repeated-measures lens, or gathered the requisite data across extended time periods to understand these potential dynamic fluctuations of creativity in BD. This study was designed to address this issue.

Beyond the gaps in understanding dynamic fluctuations in creativity, it is unclear whether previously reported findings on BD and creativity can be generalized outside of Western countries. Within the traditional Chinese perspective [32], views of BD remain focused on pathology; very few research studies have focused on the positive aspects of creativity [33, 34]. Hence, the present study examines creativity, personal recovery, and BD in a Chinese context.

Personal recovery and creativity in bipolar disorder

Multiple authors have described the benefits of creativity for people with BD, including helping them to reframe negative experiences, see themselves beyond their illness, and improve the rate and quality of their recovery [6, 35, 36]. In one qualitative study, participants with BD described how creative activities elevated their perceptual sensitivity and imagination [37]. People with BD may use positive terms, such as “fireworks,” and “super-connectedness,” to describe their experiences of creativity [35, 38]. Moreover, creativity has been shown to help individuals with BD to accept and foster a more positive self-identity, combating stigma [38]. Nonetheless, understanding the psychological mechanisms through which creativity facilitates personal recovery is still an emerging field of study (for a recent comprehensive review examining creativity in the context of mental health, see Zhao and colleagues [39]). A qualitative approach represents a useful methodological approach which to deepen our understanding of how experiences of creativity relate to personal resources [40], life satisfaction [41], and dynamic recovery processes [42].

Photovoice

To capture the dynamic and complex process of creativity across time, we conducted a qualitative study using a photovoice methodology. Photovoice is a strategy using photography to record participants’ concerns, narratives, and processes [43]. It has been used with marginalized groups [44] and individuals with mental illness [45] to capture voices and experiences that may not be easily accessed through traditional research methods [46]. By sharing images with symbolic representations in discussions, photovoice can stimulate self-reflection for individuals with mental illness and help them develop new meanings and a sense of empowerment about personal experiences [47]. Previous studies have used the photovoice method to understand experiences of mental illness and recovery [48, 49]; the current study aimed to capture, through participants’ photos, the dynamic process of creativity among individuals with BD, along with changes in their experience of recovery, all in a nonthreatening manner [50, 51].

Recent literature increasingly frames photovoice not only as a participatory research tool, but also as a creative practice that fosters self‑expression, identity formation, and empowerment [52–54]. In this context, creativity is understood as the process of generating new meanings and perspectives, while art serves as the medium—through photography and other visual artefacts—by which this process unfolds. The concept of “frozen phenomenological moments” further illustrates this dynamic, referring to how photographs capture lived experiences at a particular point in time that can later be revisited and re‑interpreted [54–56]. Such moments highlight the reflective and temporal dimensions of creativity in photovoice, as participants construct narratives and insights. This methodology also allows participants to identify, represent, and enhance their sense of community as people pursuing creative activities during the focus groups as part of the photovoice study [43, 45]. Clarifying the relationship between art and creativity strengthens the theoretical grounding of such work. Art provides the tangible form—photography, paintings, or writing—through which creativity is expressed. Creativity, in turn, refers to the cognitive and affective processes of meaning‑making and reflection that underlie these artistic forms. Recognizing their interconnections, while analytically distinguishing the process from the product, reinforces how photovoice functions simultaneously as a research method and a creative practice.

Research aims

Building upon the literature on creativity and BD, the current study is novel in four ways. First, we extend the literature from its focus on Western populations to add to the small literature on creativity among Chinese individuals with BD. Second, we use photovoice as a qualitative methodology over an extended period of time to explore the role of creativity in the life experiences and personal recovery processes of individuals with BD. Third, our use of photovoice allows for a repeated-measures lens, as compared to typical cross-sectional studies. Fourth, instead of studying creativity in general, as is common in the existing literature, we focus specifically on individuals engaging in painting and writing; this more targeted approach provides a more specific context and improves our understanding of the issues under study. Overall, the present study offers novel insights into the complex relationship between mood, creativity, and the lived experiences of individuals with BD in a non-Western cultural setting.

Methods

Setting and participants

This qualitative study is part of a larger investigation on BD and creativity. Participants with and without BD were invited to complete questionnaires and tasks, and to participate in a follow-up qualitative study. Recruitment, using convenience sampling, was promoted through email, posters at the authors’ university (masked authors initials), nongovernmental organizations providing mental health support, and painting classes for the general public in the community. Fourteen participants with BD expressed interest in the qualitative study. The selection criteria were as follows: aged 18 to 65 years; ethnic Chinese; Cantonese speaker; diagnosed with BD I or II; currently participating in creative activities related to writing or painting for at least eight hours per month; and able to give informed written consent. The exclusion criteria included: an inability to concentrate for more than 15 min; being a danger to oneself or others; and substance use disorder within the past year. All participants were approached by the researcher and 13 participants provided full written informed consent to participate. Three participants were removed from the study because of their limited availability and/or hospitalization. All of the participants were women. Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University Human Research Ethics Committee for Non-Clinical Faculties. The study was conducted in accordance with the Belmont Report principles of respect for persons, beneficence, and justice. All participants completed written informed consent procedures and were provided with a detailed explanation of the study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of their participation, their right to withdraw from the study, and the risks and benefits of the study. Researchers took steps to mitigate the risks of identifying participants and inappropriate data disclosures, such as anonymizing and de-identifying personally identifiable information, blurring faces, and using pseudonyms. All data were securely stored in password-protected systems accessible only to authorized personnel.

Procedures

The participants were introduced to the methodology in a group meeting held in the first author’s university, which involved a discussion of the photovoice methodology and the procedural guidelines (e.g., measures for confidentiality, the approximate number of photos to be taken, and ongoing contact with researchers). The nine-month study included a six-month photo logging period with two concurrent focus groups. The final three months featured a third focus group and a concluding public art event. We chose the time period to allow for a sufficient duration in regard to capturing how the participants’ creativity ebbed and flowed in the real world, but also to avoid a long period of time that risked being psychologically taxing for them. As described below, some participants also took part in individual interviews. Data collection, coding, and focus groups were conducted in Cantonese. For the purposes of publication, we translated quoted transcriptions to English.

Photo logging

During the study period, the researchers kept a log of photos of the participants’ creativity journeys every two weeks, as well as a work progress (or product) log, allowing the research team to address the central research question about the degree to which mood state is related to creative output for those with BD.

Focus groups

The focus groups were facilitated by the three researchers (ST, WY, and IL), who had completed postgraduate training in mental health, psychology, and social work. Collectively, they had extensive experience conducting qualitative research with people in recovery and had published work in this area, ensuring both methodological rigor and sensitivity to the study population. No prior relationship was established between the researchers and participants before the commencement of the study. Participants were informed of the general purpose of the focus group study. The researchers used the SHOWeD approach [43] to facilitate participant recall and descriptions of their photos:

What do you See here?

What is really Happening?

How does this relate to Our lives (e.g., creative pursuits, challenges)?

Why does this problem or strength exist?

How could this image Educate the general public?

What can we Do about it?

Participants shared their understanding of creativity and the pursuit of creative goals, with different views expressed and discussed. The third focus group session was used to verify emerging themes, to plan the public art event, and to gather feedback on the photovoice and focus group processes. All focus groups were conducted online due to COVID-19, with participants requested to join from a quiet room at home, using stable Wi‑Fi, to ensure privacy and smooth participation. Each session lasted approximately 75 min and was audio‑recorded for transcription purposes.

Individual interviews

Two individual interviews were used to gather personal and in-depth insights, including discussions of some topics that were not suitable for group discussions. Participants were selected for interviews based on their exceptional experiences and valuable insights concerning creativity and personal recovery from BD. The interviews, conducted via Zoom due to COVID-19 restrictions, lasted approximately one hour each and were conducted in Cantonese. Participants joined from a private room with stable Wi‑Fi, and all interviews were audio‑recorded for analysis.

The photovoice protocol (including a focus group guide) and interview guide were developed specifically for this study and were pilot-tested with a small group of undergraduate students to ensure clarity and relevance. The English-language versions of these materials are included as a supplementary file and are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Exhibition and last round-up session

At the culmination of the project, a six-day art exhibition was held on the university campus, featuring the creative works of the 10 participants (Fig. 1). Decisions on which items to include were made through discussions during the focus groups. Participants provided accompanying text for their displayed artworks. The exhibition aimed to showcase the artists’ recovery stories and promote the reduction of stigma associated with severe mental illness. The exhibition was professionally curated and organized, with artists given the opportunity to engage with the public.

Public exhibition of the artworks created by the research participants. who volunteered to take part in the event

An engaging event (a human library) where the artists shared their recovery stories and their creative production processes with the public took place on the last day of the art exhibition. Five of the artists participated, sharing their journeys of creation and personal recovery from BD. Both the art exhibition and the human library were warmly received by attendees. The exhibition attracted significant attention, including full-page coverage in a prominent local Chinese newspaper.

Data analysis and research rigor

Our analytic approach was grounded in a phenomenological tradition with narrative elements, focusing on participants’ lived experiences of creativity and recovery [57, 58]. The emphasis was on meaning‑making, rather than visual analysis of the artwork itself. Photovoice functioned chiefly as a reflective method, enabling participants to articulate their voices in relation to the photographs they took, and the associated creative processes. Paintings and photographs served as prompts for reflection, but the data analyzed were the narratives generated in focus groups and interviews. Each account was treated as a unit of analysis, and themes were identified inductively by comparing and contrasting stories to highlight commonalities and differences. For example, one participant described how photographing her drawings helped her reinterpret feelings of isolation, while another reflected on painting as a way to externalize mood fluctuations. These accounts were coded and organized into themes, illustrating pathways among creativity, identity, and recovery.

In practice, the transcripts were subject to four layers of analysis. First, the material was organized using thematic analysis [59, 60], requiring multiple readings and coding of relevant units closely aligned with participants’ accounts. This initial coding framework was developed iteratively through independent coding, joint reviews, and revisions among the four researchers (ST, WY, IL, and TL). Second, the content was conceptualized into broader themes according to its relevance to the research aims, such as the achieving of creative goals and the factors involved. The first step yielded themes such as depressive mood or creative activities; in the second step, these were grouped into broader categories such as bipolarity and aspirations. Third, the team identified pathways and mechanisms related to the topics under study and finally explored convergence and divergence across narratives. Credibility was strengthened through the triangulation of data sources and member checking, whereby participants were invited to review and comment on the preliminary findings and interpretations during a third focus group. Data saturation was deemed to have been achieved when no new themes emerged from either the images or the discussions, and participants affirmed that their perspectives had been fully expressed and represented.

Furthermore, the researchers maintained detailed notes from interviews and focus groups, and engaged in reflexivity, reflecting on how their perspectives, roles, and limited interactions with participants might shape their interpretations [61]. Acknowledging their positionality as scholars with differentiated power, they compared participants’ artwork and texts to remain critically aware of influence. Participants articulated their views clearly and confidently, with no evidence of researchers’ input shaping their accounts.

Results

Each participant discussed issues surrounding creativity, mood, their ambitions in terms of their creative journey, and their recovery. In line with the research aim of exploring the interplay between mood, creativity, and lived experiences of individuals with BD in a non‑Western context, four main themes were identified.

Theme 1: Bipolarity and creativity

Creativity is a safe outlet

Participants expressed the belief that creativity serves as a safe outlet for emotions, enabling them to channel their emotional experiences into creative production. Notably, participants described the way in which there were no stringent skill requirements or rigid guidelines, allowing them to freely follow their instincts when creating their work. One participant pointed out that, “[u]sually, it is difficult for individuals with bipolar disorder to express their emotions through speech accurately. For me, artwork allows me to organize and illustrate my own feelings” (P6). Another participant added: “So, actually, when I’m depressed, I don’t really have much motivation, but […] I still want to express my feelings through art” (P12).

Creativity across depressive and elevated mood states

Participants generally indicated a connection between their mood and creativity over the six-month photo-logging period. Some participants reported that both depressive and elevated mood states were linked to an increase in “thinking outside the box” ideas and heightened perceptual sensitivity. This, in turn, allowed them to have more profound creative experiences. For instance, participants in an elevated mood state reported having a greater number of ideas and thoughts, enabling them to engage with a more vivid imagination, make associations more rapidly, and surpass conventional boundaries. P6 commented: “I would do something out of my ordinary routine”; specifically, she would draw on her own and deliberately incorporate a wider range of colors and contrasts in her drawings (Fig. 2).

Strong contrasting colors (created by P6)

Contrary to the general assumption that depression suppresses creativity and energy levels, participants in depressive episodes reported heightened sensitivity to their experiences. Some participants experienced an intensified sense of perception during depressive episodes. They described heightened clarity of thought and a sharpened focus, which provided a distinctive perspective that influenced their creative processes. This enhanced state of perception, which allowed for more intense mental engagement and contributed to their creative output, was less likely to occur during periods of calmness and tranquility. As one participant (P3) mentioned: “During my depressive episodes, my sensory perceptions became heightened. Every sight evoked intense emotions, allowing me to envision vivid scenes and articulate them in words.”

Lower creativity linked to decreased mood variability

Participants highlighted that their creativity (in terms of sensitivity to their experiences, imaginative thoughts, and creative outputs) diminished when they experienced fewer fluctuations in mood. One participant felt these changes in creativity, stating:

I experienced intense emotions and a sense of sentimentality when riding a bus on rainy days. During those moments, I would capture numerous photos and create various artworks inspired by the atmosphere. However, once I achieved a stable state or experienced emotional flatness as a result of medication, I noticed a significant decline in my ability to visualize images in my mind, and my once vibrant imagination seemed to fade away. (P2)

Another participant agreed with this sentiment during the focus group: “Previously, during episodes of elevated mood, I would actively engage in activities such as drawing, writing poems, and blogging. However, since experiencing emotional flatness, my creative output has noticeably declined” (P8).

Theme 2: Goals, processes, and sustainability

Mood and ambition

Participants pursued a range of creative processes and activities that showcased their creativity and ambitions, such as designing a picture book for children, creating an escape room game at work, and establishing an online mental wellness group for their peers during the pandemic. These endeavors held great personal significance for them, and they were completed within a short timeframe.

Most of the participants interpreted ambition as encompassing aspirations, determination, and achieving set goals. Participants described themselves as having more goals and ambitions, more ideas, higher energy levels, self-confidence, and clarity of thought during periods of elevated mood. As one participant described:

My brain [had] a lot of ideas, which encouraged me to write more. For example, I once completed writing 40 Christmas cards for friends in one night. At that time, I was energetic, determined, and urged myself to do so. (P7; Fig. 3, eight Christmas cards)

Great productivity and high mood (created by P7)

One participant acknowledged that her depressive emotional states reduced her confidence, but her goals and aspirations remained unchanged. Self-doubt tended to impede participants’ sense of ambition during depressive episodes, as one participant described:

I was ambitious at the beginning. I wanted to do it [create art] and I did it well. […] When I was very depressed, I still wanted to accomplish it [creating art] but I was not that confident. This reflected [how] my mood affected my confidence. I still wanted to do it, but I started to question myself whether I had exaggerated my ability. (P3)

The roles of meaningfulness and determination in achieving goals

Participants described factors that seem to influence their creative processes. While some participants had high ambitions that were seen as unrealistic or difficult to achieve, one participant found solace in her goals. She commented: “These ambitions are meaningful to me. I believe they are within my capabilities, and pursuing them allows me to shift my focus away from myself and my illness” (P2).

One participant (P9) highlighted mixed episodes of BD, where individuals navigate a delicate balance in their elevated and depressive emotional states, which was seen as a crucial factor in achieving the participants’ goals. This underscores the influence of finding equilibrium between the intensity of low moods and the strengths of determination and decisiveness.

Theme 3: Creativity and its influence on mental health

Creativity as lived experiences of healing

Due to my tendency to suppress traumatic experiences and associated emotions, they eventually seek an outlet. Self-harm thoughts emerge, overwhelming me. However, once I become aware of them, I make efforts to accept them. I symbolize these thoughts using stars, as they serve as reminders of self-care. I embrace whatever thoughts arise in my mind and hold love for them as well. (P2)

Participants placed emphasis on how artistic media (e.g., painting, poetry, photography, dance, music, and digital media) can support people experiencing mental health conditions and how art activities have been used to promote their lived experiences of recovery. Participants described the positive influence of creating artwork as a means of emotional expression, particularly when verbal communication falls short in conveying their emotions. For instance, one participant used darker colors to convey her sense of hopelessness and her feelings of being trapped and experiencing limited motivation:

The past, like chains and fetters, restrains the future. Trauma and betrayal gave rise to an armored coat, a thick wall blocking others while isolating myself. Chained by the past, a siege mentality emerged, trapping me within this armor. The suffocating and painful feeling of being trapped unveiled invisible wounds day by day. (P11)

Sometimes, the creative work may mutually “speak back” to the participants, facilitating their emotional awareness and self-reflection. For example, one participant stated: “When I looked back at an angry figure drawn on my phone, I could feel how irritated I was at that moment. It seemed that the image could represent myself as a mirror” (P2). Another participant commented: “There were so many paintings coming from our unconscious with meanings behind them. If you turned around and looked at it, you could discover many things about yourself” (P13).

Furthermore, several participants used artwork and photos to record moments and moods during the study period. These creative expressions captured their lived experiences and served as reminders of positive moments, sources of support, and the importance of self‑care. For example, one participant commented: “Creation involved a lot of inspiration, which drove me to create, and the creation was a record of myself at different moments. When I recorded the moment, I needed to bring back some memories. Sometimes, memories help me move forward” (P2; see Fig. 4, a life-sized drawing of a map of emotions).

Life-sized drawing of a map of emotions (created by P2)

Participants shared how the influence of their artistic creations depended on their psychological readiness to process the contents of these creations. One participant stated: “Sometimes, you experience some life events that might be too big to bear. If you create some art, it would reveal your hurt. So, it would not be a good time to express yourself in this way” (P2). Another participant found painting too direct, and said writing was more helpful in calming herself down:

When you already have a scene in your mind, if you drew it out, you would be frightened by the picture because the picture would present the event directly. Then, there is a chance it will scare you, and there would be more fluctuations. (P13)

Enhancing self-understanding through creative expression

Despite initially considering themselves as individuals not engaged in creative pursuits, some participants who were unaccustomed to drawing and writing began engaging in artistic endeavors after the onset of their BD. They discovered that these creative activities facilitated self-exploration and self-exposure. Two participants specifically mentioned how art helped them in the process of exploring and developing their identities. One of these participants recollected:

During my primary and secondary school years, I believed I was bad at drawing. However, after the onset of my BD, I became more open to self-exploration. I started using a phone to draw, which required fewer drawing skills, enabling me to express myself freely. (P2)

Furthermore, another participant commented: “I rediscovered my creativity after experiencing this disorder. Society had conditioned me to be rational and critical, but deep down, I had always possessed an artistic side. This illness allowed me to liberate myself and find a sense of equilibrium” (P13).

Participants experienced joy and self-confidence through self-expression. One participant shared an example of drawing a picture in response to a happy photo she had taken earlier, which allowed her to relive and appreciate that positive feeling again (P2; see Fig. 5).

Happy moment and recapturing joy (created by P2)

Some participants found hope in the connections they established with others through their artwork. One participant explained that engaging in drawing and other creative work enabled her to self-reflect and establish connections with others, pointing out that creativity had been instrumental in her recovery journey:

Over the past few years, I have grown accustomed to relying solely on myself, but there are limitations to what one person can accomplish alone. At times, I feel weary […]. However, whenever I found myself at a dead-end, there were always individuals who extended a helping hand, and I would like to express my sincere gratitude to everyone who has supported me on this journey. (P7; Fig. 6)

Companionship (created by P7)

Another participant also mentioned that she started to draw because she wanted to make other people happy even though she was not capable of making herself happy:

Garfield was my favorite cartoon when I was a kid. It [is] one of my best childhood memories. Garfield gives me a sense of relief. I believe it signifies a lasting connection between me and my father. I wish [that] the balloons [would] continue to be our bridge and bring you joy and happiness too. (P4; Fig. 7)

Theme 4: Creativity and cyber-connection

During the period of the photovoice study, many participants had online accounts through which they shared their artwork and formed connections, even with strangers. Sharing their creative work with others not only fueled participants’ motivation to create more art and writing, but also fostered a sense of connection, confidence, self-acceptance, and the ability to influence people’s lives. One participant shared a photo online expressing her helplessness, and many people liked it, creating a sense of connection as they related to her feelings. She reflected:

I do take photos, like that flower‑dropping one from earlier. Even though it’s very simple, when I look back at what I wrote or the photos I took, many people resonate with it. I posted that photo on social media, and a lot of people connected with it—they even gave it a like. (P2)

Another participant said:

Quite a few netizens have messaged me directly, saying that the way I write from this perspective touches them […] deeply. They feel it is authentic, and that authenticity creates stronger reactions and a sense of resonance. I realized that one does not have to be optimistic to be considered good or to influence others. At times, what I share may not be entirely positive nor entirely negative, but that genuine feeling is what prompts more people to respond. (P7)

Discussion

The current study explored the relationship between BD and creativity among 10 Chinese women, employing a photovoice methodology, focus groups, and individual interviews over a nine‑month period that culminated in an exhibition of the participants’ creative work. During this time, participants experienced both elevated and depressive moods, alongside varying levels of creative accomplishment. The findings contribute to the limited body of research on BD and creativity in Asian populations; to date, only a few studies have considered links between BD and creativity in Asia. For example, among 1,558 Japanese undergraduates, higher polygenic risk scores for BD were associated with stronger performance in regard to a measure of divergent thinking [62]. In a Taiwanese study, creativity was linked to neurocognitive correlates of BD, in that lower divergent thinking scores were associated with reduced cognitive flexibility in BD [63], along with decreased gray matter volume in the medial prefrontal cortex [64]. In a qualitative case study, the personal narrative of a Chinese migrant woman with BD illustrated how her individuality was overlooked, leading to more medication and the loss of passion for concerts, stage plays, writing, and piano, as well as diminished creativity, independence, and critical thinking [65]. We are unaware of other studies that have considered the dynamics of bipolar mood fluctuations and creativity across time, using careful qualitative methods, and conducted among Asian samples.

The current findings extend previous work on the inverted-U model, which posits that moderate vulnerability or hypomanic symptoms may enhance creative output, whereas severe manic or depressive episodes will impair functioning and diminish sustained creativity [18, 66–68]. In the present sample, during hypomanic states, participants reported bursts of inspiration and efficient output, aligning with prior findings that energy, positive affect, and a reduced need for sleep may be advantageous for creativity [18].Some participants also noted enhancements during depressive episodes, describing intensified focus that offered unique perspectives on their creative process. While most research emphasizes the immediate negative impact of depression on creativity [66], our findings suggest the need to consider longer‑term influences, including how shifts in mood‑state perception may carry into creative work after recovery. Nonetheless, these results must be contextualized: Large‑scale epidemiological studies indicate that a history of depression is not broadly associated with creative occupations in fields such as art, music, or design, but effects more specific to the creative occupation of authorship are reported [15, 16]. Any benefits of a history of depression may manifest more as creative insights than sustained output. One possible way to consider the contrasts between the general sense that depression is not an advantage to creativity and our findings here is through recent findings that the effects of chronic versus acute depressive symptoms may diverge [68]; that is, acute episodes may confer future advantages that chronic symptoms do not, and some previous work may not have fully considered this element. Taken together, more work is needed on acute and chronic mood effects, as well as which facets of perceptions may carry over to artistic processes after recovery.

Participants also reported that their ambitions and ability to execute their plans in relation to achieving their goals played a significant role in explaining the relationship between mood and creativity levels. Previous research findings lend support to our results; individuals with higher mania propensity were more likely to sustain effort during difficult tasks, including creative activities [38, 69], even in the face of little objective reward [19]. This finding is highly relevant for China and wider Asian cultures, where high levels of stigma and discrimination against people with mental illnesses have been documented [70, 71].

In the present study, we found that engaging in creative activities had positive influences on the participants’ mental health. Creative expression provided a sense of release, allowing participants to process emotions and alleviate distress. Through activities such as drawing and writing, they gained a deeper understanding of themselves, exploring and making sense of their thoughts, emotions, and experiences. Approaches to mental health recovery emphasize the development of a new identity [72], the search for meaning [6, 73], and existential growth [74] as central processes of personal recovery. In relation to BD, one review highlighted difficulties, losses, and tensions experienced throughout the recovery process. To capture these complexities, the POETIC framework (Purpose and meaning, Optimism and hope, Empowerment, Tensions, Identity, and Connectedness) was proposed as a useful lens through which to understand recovery in this context. This approach underscores the importance of professionals presenting personal recovery in a realistic and balanced manner, responding to criticisms from service user organizations that view it as overly optimistic [75]. Furthermore, research has shown that artistic products, when undertaken at times of emotional tolerance—when individuals feel stable enough to engage—enabled the expression of traumatic experiences and facilitated reflection and recovery [75]. Ambitious creative goals, even when difficult to achieve, were imbued with meaning—a recognized component of recovery across cultures [65, 73, 75, 76]. Creative expression also fostered connection, which is a critical element of recovery in BD and across Asian contexts [77, 78]. Overall, the creative process supported exploration, identity formation, joy, and social connectedness, highlighting its central role in recovery.

Finally, the process of self-understanding in this study was not solitary, as participants described how online social media facilitated connections and collaborations with other artists and individuals. This aspect of connectedness has not been reported in the existing literature on mental health conditions and artistic or creative work. Using the photovoice methodology, we were able to track the dynamic relationships between creativity and recovery, as well as the interaction between the artists and their online audiences over the one-month period. Our findings highlight the importance of connection in personal recovery among individuals with BD [73], and the way in which their creativity can act as a means to achieve it. The digital space creates a platform for showcasing artwork, receiving feedback, and expanding creative networks. In recent years, an increasing number of individuals with mental illnesses have been turning to social media to share their experiences, and to seek advice and support [79, 80]. This appears to offer them benefits in regard to social connectedness and feelings of group belonging, helping them develop coping strategies in relation to the challenges they face. Similarly, for some individuals in the study, cyber-connections played a significant role in enriching their creative journeys and fostering their recovery from BD.

In summary, this project makes several unique contributions to research into the creative processes and recovery experiences of individuals with BD, revealing a dynamic journey over time. Our study is distinctive in focusing specifically on drawing and writing, rather than adopting a broad notion of creativity. This emphasis enabled an in-depth exploration of how these forms of expression contribute to recovery and identity, offering a more nuanced perspective than studies that treat creativity in general terms. The use of the photovoice methodology introduced a creative, participatory dimension to the investigation. Key learnings include how Chinese participants, who tended to be modest and said little about their creative work, were able to express themselves fully through the images produced in photovoice, which offered rich insights and prompted them to speak up. Notably, this study also extends the exploration of creativity and BD into the realm of social media, where participants shared their creative artwork, thereby broadening the scope of engagement and visibility. Finally, this project included active initiatives to reduce stigma, including the organization of an exhibition and a human library, providing a platform for individuals to openly share their experiences and creative expressions. These initiatives aimed to foster understanding and acceptance, ultimately challenging societal stereotypes surrounding BD.

Our study has two major limitations. First, despite significant recruitment efforts, the study lacked male participants, limiting the generalizability of findings to women with BD. Beyond gender, the sample was homogenous in other ways—all participants were under the age of 35, had an education level of high school or higher, and approximately 90% were either studying or employed—which further restricts the applicability of our findings. Second, there was considerable attrition (three out of 13 people or 23% left the study) and fluctuating attendance in our three gatherings (focus groups) and the exhibition. This attrition may have hindered the robustness of our findings. However, the ups and downs intrinsic to BD may lead to fluctuations in engagement across studies.

We recommend caution in drawing implications from this study, due to these limitations. Mental health practitioners should recognize the complex interplay between symptoms of bipolar illness, psychosocial factors, and creativity, while remembering that each individual’s experience is unique. Even so, creative activities such as painting, photography, and writing can provide meaningful, culturally-sensitive avenues for self‑expression. Mental health services may consider integrating such practices into recovery programs, while policy frameworks could support and fund their inclusion to ensure accessibility and sustainability.

Directions for future research highlight several priorities. Tracking sleep, confidence, cognitive functioning, and organization may provide a more refined understanding of symptoms that support or hinder creativity [81]. Comparative cross‑cultural studies could clarify how cultural contexts shape the relationship between mood episodes, creative expression, and recovery. Long‑term investigations of sustained creative engagement may determine its role in fostering stability, resilience, and improved functioning. Integrating technology such as mobile applications or virtual platforms into creative interventions may further enhance accessibility, engagement, and opportunities for self‑expression among individuals with BD. Embracing these research directions will advance our understanding and pave the way for innovative approaches to supporting the well-being and creative potential of individuals with BD.

Conclusions

This nine‑month photovoice study, culminating in a public art exhibition, illuminates the complex and dynamic relationship between BD and creativity among Chinese women engaged in expressive writing and painting. The findings show how mood fluctuations shape creative expression over time, with both hypomanic and depressive states enabling distinct and meaningful forms of creativity, thereby extending the inverted‑U model beyond a linear understanding of mood and creative work. Sustained creative engagement emerged as a core element of mental health recovery, supporting self‑understanding, identity reconstruction, and social connectedness, particularly through digital and community‑based platforms. Together, these findings demonstrate the value of culturally grounded creative practices and support their integration into recovery‑oriented mental health programs to promote well‑being while sustaining the creative capacities of individuals living with BD.
