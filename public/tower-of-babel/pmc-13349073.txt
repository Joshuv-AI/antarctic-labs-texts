Acute effects of the FIFA 11 + kids warm-up program compared with a judo-specific warm-up on explosive and functional performance in youth judo athletes: a randomized crossover study
Authors: Hakkı Mor, Halit Şar, Tayfun Arslan, Soner Akgün, Fatih Karakaş, Ahmet Mor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe FIFA 11 + Kids (FIFA 11+) warm-up program is effective for injury prevention and to enhance sports performance, including vertical jump (VJ), sprint, balance, and agility. However, no previous study has examined the acute effects of FIFA 11 + on performance outcomes in youth judo athletes. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the acute effects of the FIFA 11 + warm-up program on selected physical performance parameters of youth judo athletes compared to the judo-specific warm-up (JSWU). This study employed a randomized, counterbalanced crossover design. MethodsSixteen male competitive youth judo athletes (age: 11.81 ± 1.52 years; height: 151.44 ± 11.79 cm; weight: 46.25 ± 14.42 kg; body mass index (BMI): 19.95 ± 5.50 kg/m2; training age: 3.56 ± 1.31 years) completed two warm-up conditions (FIFA 11 + vs. JSWU). The following performance tests were conducted during the test sessions: vertical jump, 5–10 m sprint, bilateral leg strength (LS), back strength (BS), handgrip strength (HS), lower-limb functional strength (single hop for distance (SLHD), triple hop for distance (THD), crossover hop for distance (CHD)), change of direction (CoD), broad jump and overhead medicine ball (OMB) throw.ResultsThere were significant differences between JSWU and FIFA 11 + conditions for vertical jump (39.44 ± 7.21 vs. 41.88 ± 6.67, p = 0.008, η² = 0.40), anaerobic power (641.45 ± 211.13 vs. 665.69 ± 228.44, p = 0.010, η² = 0.38), and overhead medicine ball throw (375.19 ± 110.72 vs. 410.44 ± 114.95, p = 0.002, η² = 0.50). Significant differences were also observed in SLHD (right and left) and CHD (left), although the effect sizes were moderate to large.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that the FIFA 11 + program produced small acute improvements in selected explosive and unilateral functional performance outcomes (i.e., vertical jump, OMB throw, SLHD, and CHDLeft), while most other variables showed no significant differences compared with the judo-specific warm-up. These findings should be interpreted cautiously despite the large effect sizes, as the magnitude of improvement was practically small. The FIFA 11 + program may be a potential alternative warm-up option, though its practical application should be interpreted with caution.Trial registrationThe randomized controlled trial was retrospectively registered on 03/23/2026 at ClinicalTrials.gov, under the registration number NCT07487558 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07487558).

Introduction

Judo is a complex Olympic combat sport characterized by high-intensity, intermittent actions that demand rapid directional changes, explosive throwing techniques, grip-based upper-body engagement, and repeated transitions between standing and groundwork phases [1]. Judo performance is closely linked to several physical factors. Muscular strength is crucial for controlling and immobilizing the opponent, while strength endurance is essential for maintaining a grip in grip battles. Muscle power is a fundamental component of effective throwing techniques, and anaerobic endurance supports repeated high-intensity attacks during matches [2]. Additionally, successful competitive performance depends on integrating multiple physical qualities, including maximal and explosive strength [3], upper-body strength [4], agility [5], anaerobic power, handgrip strength [1], and technical–tactical efficiency [6]. Notably, in youth athletes, these demands are especially relevant, as the ages of 10 to 14 represent a sensitive developmental period during which neuromuscular coordination, motor skills, and foundational physical capacities undergo rapid maturation [7, 8]. In addition, biological maturation has been shown to significantly influence anthropometric characteristics, body composition, and physical performance in youth athletes, potentially affecting training responses and competitive outcomes [9]. Importantly, improving these physical qualities not only enhances performance but also reduces injury risk, as stronger muscles and better coordination help absorb impacts and stabilize joints. Despite the acknowledged importance of these factors, the literature lacks guidance on integrating them into training programs for youth judo athletes, underscoring the need for further research. Furthermore, judo’s inherently high-contact nature, involving grip fighting, being thrown, rotational loading, and unilateral stance phases, increases the risk of lower- and upper-limb injuries, especially at the knee, shoulder, and fingers, with being thrown being the most common cause of injuries. Additionally, the most frequently reported injury types among youth judo athletes are contusions/abrasions, fractures, and sprains/strains [10]. For these reasons, optimizing warm-up strategies in youth judo athletes is crucial not only for improving acute performance but also for reducing injury risk.

Warm-up routines act as a bridge between rest and high-intensity activity, producing beneficial physiological changes, including increased muscle temperature, faster nerve conduction, improved joint mobility, and improved oxygen uptake kinetics [11]. Research consistently shows that dynamic warm-ups are better than static stretching for preparing athletes for explosive and fast movements, as static stretching can temporarily reduce force production [12–14]. Additionally, neuromuscular warm-up techniques that involve balance, proprioception, coordination, plyometrics, and light strength exercises are recommended for youth athletes because they can improve motor control and reduce injury risk [15, 16]. Since judo places high technical demands and requires rapid force production under unstable conditions, targeted neuromuscular preparation may directly enhance sport-specific performance.

Among modern warm-up interventions, the FIFA 11 + program has become one of the most widely used evidence-based neuromuscular injury prevention protocols worldwide. Originally developed for youth and adult football players, FIFA 11 + combines running drills, unilateral balance tasks, trunk stabilization exercises, plyometric sequences, and strength exercises to enhance neuromuscular control, proprioception, and movement quality [17–21]. Meta-analyses show that FIFA 11 + can reduce overall injury risk by 30–70% among youth football players [20] and reduce head and neck injuries by approximately 40% [18]. Recent evidence indicates that FIFA 11 + is most effective when used for fewer than 9 weeks and with at least 3 training sessions per week. Its preventive effects seem to be strongest in adult players (18 years and older), males, and those competing at higher football levels (Tier 3 or above) [22]. Moreover, it significantly improves sprint speed [23], vertical jump height [24, 25], change-of-direction (CoD) speed [26, 27], and lower-limb strength [28, 29] in young football players. Studies on acute or short-term adaptations also report enhancements in dynamic balance and explosive performance after several weeks of FIFA 11 + training [29–32]. These findings suggest that the neuromuscular components of the FIFA 11 + protocol may provide substantial benefits beyond their initial use in football.

Although FIFA 11 + has proven effective in team sports, its application in combat sports such as judo remains largely unexplored. More importantly, to date, no study has investigated the acute effects of the FIFA 11 + warm-up program on physical performance indicators in youth judo athletes. The neuromuscular demands of judo, characterized by one-legged stance phases, multidirectional hopping, body rotation, breakfalls, and high-intensity gripping, differ significantly from those of running-based field sports [1, 3, 10]. Therefore, understanding how FIFA 11 + influences judo-specific performance measures, such as unilateral hop distance, jump height, strength, and speed-agility, requires systematic research. The absence of studies on neuromuscular warm-up programs in judo leaves a notable gap in the literature, especially considering the sport’s higher incidence of lower-limb ligament injuries, which align with the original focus of FIFA 11+ [33–35].

Interval training is commonly used to enhance judo performance and yields positive adaptations; however, elite judo athletes show limited responses to these adaptations due to high adaptation to sport-specific loads. As a result, developing anaerobic conditioning in elite athletes is hampered, and integrating anaerobic training into comprehensive judo programs remains a significant challenge [36]. Given these limitations in improving anaerobic conditioning, identifying and monitoring key physical performance indicators becomes especially important for assessing training effectiveness in judo. To justify selecting these indicators, vertical jump height is a well-established measure of lower-limb explosive power [37], and jumping ability has also been shown to be strongly associated with sprint performance and overall athletic capability [38], while hop tests reveal interlimb asymmetry (or bilateral difference, bilateral asymmetry), which may relate to sprinting, jumping [39], strength [40], and change of direction [41]. In judo, handgrip strength is a key predictor of success because gripping is central to both offensive and defensive actions [42, 43]. Although agility and sprinting are not primary determinants of sport-specific performance, they reflect an athlete’s ability to quickly transition, reposition, and start offensive sequences [3]. Therefore, understanding each metric as a sensitive indicator of acute readiness can clarify the logical flow of interventions to improve these key parameters. Considering these connections, any warm-up intervention that effectively enhances neuromuscular readiness may acutely impact the performance indicators mentioned above. Although the FIFA 11 + program was originally developed for football players, many of its neuromuscular components are not sport-specific and may therefore benefit athletes in other sports requiring dynamic balance, unilateral stability, and rapid force production. Judo performance frequently involves single-leg support phases, multidirectional movement patterns, explosive hip extension during throwing techniques, and rapid stabilization following interaction with the opponent. The FIFA 11 + program includes exercises targeting trunk stabilization, unilateral balance, plyometric actions, and lower-limb strength, which are biomechanical and neuromuscular qualities also relevant to judo performance. Therefore, investigating the potential transferability of this warm-up protocol to judo represents a logical extension of previous research.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has investigated the acute performance effects of the FIFA 11 + in youth judo athletes using a randomized crossover design. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the acute effects of the FIFA 11 + warm-up program compared to a JSWU on selected performance measures in youth judo athletes, including vertical jump, sprint performance, agility, unilateral hop tests, and handgrip strength. Vertical jump and unilateral hop tests (SLHD, THD, and CHD) were selected as the primary outcomes due to their relevance to explosive power and unilateral function in judo. The remaining variables were treated as secondary outcomes reflecting general athletic performance. Based on the neuromuscular characteristics of the FIFA 11 + program and previous evidence demonstrating improvements in lower-limb strength, balance, and explosive capacity, we hypothesized that the FIFA 11 + warm-up would produce greater acute improvements in the primary performance outcomes compared to JSWU.

Methods

Participants

The study sample included 16 male competitive judo athletes (age: 11.81 ± 1.52 years; height: 151.44 ± 11.79 cm; weight: 46.25 ± 14.42 kg; body mass index (BMI): 19.95 ± 5.50 kg/m2; training age: 3.56 ± 1.31 years) from Trabzon Metropolitan Municipality Sports Club, competing in the pupil age categories (Table 1). The required sample size was determined using G*Power software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany; version 3.1.9.2). Based on an effect size of 0.50, a confidence interval of 1 − β = 0.95, a significance level of α = 0.05, and an actual power of 0.96, it was calculated that 16 participants would be sufficient for the study. The study results of Chen et al. [44], who investigated countermovement jump, 30-m linear sprint, and medicine ball overhead forward throw, along with their physical parameters, under three different warm-up protocols, were used to determine the sample size. All participants trained five days a week for about 90 min each session and regularly competed at the national level. Therefore, they are classified as Tier 3 [45]. Inclusion criteria were: (I) being aged 10–14 and actively participating in judo, (II) having at least 1 year of regular judo training, (III) training at least 4–5 days per week, and (IV) having competition experience at the national or club level. Exclusion criteria included: (I) a serious musculoskeletal injury affecting the limbs within the past 6 months, (II) any orthopedic, neurological, or chronic health condition that could impact test results or performance. Before participation, athletes and their parents received detailed information about the research procedures and provided written informed consent. This study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at Sinop University (Reference number: E-57428665-050.04-395624, date: 05 November 2025). The study was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07487558). This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. The participants’ CONSORT flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1.

Study design

This study employed a randomized, counterbalanced crossover design with repeated measures to examine the acute effects of the FIFA 11+ (FIFA 11+) warm-up protocol and a judo-specific warm-up (JSWU) on selected athletic performance parameters in youth judo athletes. Participants completed the two intervention protocols in a counterbalanced crossover order, ensuring that each protocol occurred equally often in each period and sequence position, thereby reducing the potential influence of order and carryover effects. This design was selected to minimize inter-individual variability and enhance the sensitivity for detecting differences between the two warm-up protocols [46]. To control potential order effects, participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups. Participants were randomly assigned to either group using specialized software (www.randomizer.org) by the independent researcher. The allocation sequence was generated by an independent researcher and was not disclosed to outcome assessors before participant assignment. One group performed the JSWU protocol first, followed by the FIFA 11 + protocol after a 1-week washout period, whereas the second group followed the reverse order. Certified trainers supervised both warm-up protocols. Both warm-up conditions were matched for duration (20 min) and administered under standardized procedures; however, internal intensity was not directly quantified (e.g., by heart rate or perceived exertion). Warm-up Protocols

For each warm-up condition, performance assessments were administered on two separate testing days, with a 72-hour recovery period between them. This method was used to reduce cumulative fatigue and measurement bias arising from conducting multiple tests in a single session, which has been shown to compromise test validity and reliability [47, 48].

Before each testing session, athletes completed either the JSWU [49] or the FIFA 11 + program, which includes seven exercises designed to improve balance, coordination, strength, and joint stability, as described by Rössler et al. [19]. Performance testing began 3 min after completion of each warm-up protocol. This interval was selected to allow for potential acute neuromuscular activation effects following warm-up. The test battery was systematically organized around physiological demands and administered over two testing days. Participants completed familiarization before test sessions to minimize learning effects. Day 1 focused on neuromuscular performance and speed-agility characteristics, including vertical jump, handgrip strength, OMB throw, standing broad jump, 5–10 m sprint, and the Agility T-test. Day 2 evaluated lower-limb functional performance and maximal isometric strength, including single-leg hop tests (SLHD, THD, and CHD), back strength, and leg strength. Throughout each session, tests were conducted in an order moving from lower to higher metabolic and neuromuscular demands to reduce fatigue-related performance decrements [50].

All testing sessions took place between 4:00 p.m. and 6:00 p.m., aligning with the athletes’ habitual training time. To reduce the influence of potential confounding variables, participants were instructed to maintain their usual sleep patterns and dietary habits throughout the study period. Athletes were also asked to avoid strenuous physical activity for at least 24 h before each testing session. Training loads during the test period were monitored through communication with the coaching staff to ensure that no unusually intense sessions occurred before test days. Although not strictly experimentally controlled, these variables were standardized through participant instructions and monitored via coaching staff communication.

All assessments were conducted under the same environmental conditions, with standardized instructions, test order, and recovery intervals for both warm-up protocols. To ensure procedure consistency, all testing sessions were conducted by the same researchers. Participants received familiarization trials before data collection, especially for the functional hop tests, to improve measurement reliability and reduce learning effects [39, 47]. This experimental design allowed for a valid and reliable comparison of the acute effects of the two warm-up protocols while effectively controlling for fatigue accumulation, learning effects, and order-related bias. Procedures

Anthropometric Assessments

Participants’ heights were measured in centimeters with a portable stadiometer, and their body weights were determined in kilograms using a body composition analyzer. Following the acquisition of height and weight measurements, body mass index (BMI) was calculated as body weight divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²).

Vertical jump and anaerobic power test

The participants’ vertical jump performance was measured using the Takei 5406 Jump-MD Vertical Jumpmeter (Tokyo, Japan). Athletes were asked to jump from a 90-degree squat position while standing barefoot on a rubber mat. The highest value was recorded with an accuracy of ± 1 cm [51]. The athletes’ anaerobic power was calculated using the Lewis formula based on body weight and vertical jump height: Anaerobic Power (W) = {√4.9 × [Body Weight (kg)] × √Vertical Jump (m)} [52].

5–10 m Sprint Test

The 5–10 m sprint test results for the participants were collected in the judo hall using a photocell system (Seven, SE-165 Photocell Chronometer, Istanbul, Türkiye). Participants started from a designated starting line in a standing position. Timing began when the athlete activated the photocell and stopped when they crossed the finish line. An intermediate gate was placed at the 5-meter mark, and participants were instructed to sprint at maximum speed. The test was performed twice with a 3-minute rest between attempts. Sprint times were recorded in seconds and milliseconds, and the best time was used for analysis [53].

Agility t-test

The athletes’ agility and quickness were assessed using the T Test protocol. For the setup of the T Test course, four cones were positioned on the testing surface according to the standardized layout. At the start signal, participants began the trial from cone “A” and sprinted forward to cone “B,” where they were required to touch the cone with their right hand. Next, they performed a lateral movement (side step) to the left toward cone “C” and touched it with their left hand. From cone “C,” athletes sprinted to the right toward cone “D,” making contact with the cone using their right hand. They then returned to cone “B” via a lateral side step, touched it with their left hand, and completed the test by backpedaling to cone “A,” at which point the timing was stopped. The distance between cones A and B was set at 9.14 m, while the lateral distances from cone B to cones C and D were both 4.57 m. Three test trials were performed with a 3-minute rest interval between each, and the fastest trial was recorded for statistical analysis [54].

Handgrip strength test

Grip strength was measured using a hand grip dynamometer (Takei TKK 5401, Tokyo, Japan). The device was adjusted so that each participant could comfortably grasp the handle with proper contact between the palmar eminences and the intermediate phalanges. Participants stood upright with the testing arm extended and not touching the torso, and measurements were taken while the arm was positioned at a 45° angle relative to the body. The procedure was performed twice for both the right and left hands, and the highest value was recorded [55].

Overhead Medicine Ball (OMB)throw

The OMB test was performed using a modified version of the method previously employed by Beckham et al. [56] and Stockbrugger & Haennel [57]. Athletes stood behind a straight line with their feet positioned at shoulder width while holding a 3-kg medicine ball. They were instructed to bring the ball overhead until it touched the upper back and then throw it forward with maximum effort. During the throw, participants had to avoid stepping over the line. A 30-second rest period was given between trials. Each athlete completed the test three times with 30-second rest intervals, and the highest score was recorded.

Functional performance tests

Lower limb functional performance tests were performed on a surface marked with a 0.3-cm-wide starting line and a 6-m-long, 15-cm-wide testing strip. Participants first completed three familiarization trials before moving on to the main testing phase, which involved three official attempts for each test. The best hop for distance achieved by each participant was recorded. A successful trial required a controlled, stable landing on a single leg, with the participant maintaining balance for at least 3 s; any attempt that did not meet this criterion was repeated. Arm swings were allowed throughout the tests [58].

Participants performed three hop tests for distance using standardized procedures. For each test, they stood on the designated testing leg, with their toes behind the starting line, and maintained a landing on the same leg throughout. In the Crossover Hop for Distance (CHD), participants completed three maximal forward hops, crossing a 15-cm-wide area on each hop, starting on the same side of the measuring tape as the test leg. In the Single-Leg Hop for Distance (SLHD), participants placed their hands on their hips and completed one maximal forward hop. In the Triple Hop for Distance (THD), they performed three consecutive maximal forward hops, minimizing ground contact after the first two. For all tests, participants were required to “stick” the final landing and hold for 2 s; failure to do so resulted in a void trial followed by a 60-second rest before retesting. Distances were measured from the starting line to the heel position at final landing and recorded to the nearest centimeter [39].

Leg strength test

The subjects’ leg strength was measured using a back-leg dynamometer (Takei TKK-5402, Tokyo, Japan) with a capacity of up to 300 kg. The device can be adjusted to fit participants’ heights or the center of applied force via its variable-length chain mechanism. During the test, participants placed their feet on the platform, kept their arms relaxed at their sides, and adopted a slightly forward-flexed trunk position. They were instructed to pull the dynamometer bar with maximum effort and speed using only their legs, avoiding back involvement, until reaching full knee extension-a movement lasting no more than 3 s. The test was performed twice with a three-minute rest between trials, and the highest value was recorded for analysis [51].

Back strength test

The strength of the back muscles was assessed by measuring the maximum isometric strength of the trunk muscles in a standing position with 30° lumbar flexion. A digital back muscle strength dynamometer (Takei TKK-5402, Tokyo, Japan) was used. The athletes were instructed to pull the dynamometer bar with maximum effort and speed, using only their back and avoiding leg involvement, until they achieved full knee extension, a movement lasting no more than 3 s. The test was performed twice with a three-minute rest between trials, and the highest value was recorded for analysis [59].

Broad jump test

The test was performed on a hard surface, and participants were instructed to perform a horizontal jump as far as possible following standard procedures. Participants started by standing with both feet on the starting line, swinging their arms, then jumping. Both the jump and landing phases had to be done with both feet simultaneously. The distance was measured in centimeters from the jump point to the heel of the nearest foot during landing. Each participant completed two trials with a 30-second rest between trials, and the best score was recorded for analysis [60].

Statistical analyses

Prior to selecting statistical tests for the study data, the Shapiro–Wilk normality test was conducted to assess whether the error terms were normally distributed. Differences between variables (within-subject variability) were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA (Mixed Design ANOVA). Mauchly’s test of sphericity was applied, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was used when the assumption of sphericity was violated. Between-group differences were evaluated using the Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes, mean differences (MD), and confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated to assess the magnitude of time-related differences. The effect size index was computed using eta squared (η²) [61], and η² values were interpreted as small (0.01), moderate (0.06), and large (0.14) effect sizes [62]. The findings of the study were presented as mean ± standard deviation (X ± SD), and statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0.

Results

The primary outcomes of this study were vertical jump (VJ) and unilateral hop tests (SLHD, THD, and CHD), which were selected for their relevance to explosive power and unilateral function in judo. All other performance variables were considered secondary outcomes and are interpreted accordingly. The participants had an average age of 11.81 ± 1.52 years, an average body mass of 46.25 ± 14.42 kg, and an average height of 151.44 ± 11.79 cm. Their mean body mass index (BMI) was 19.95 ± 5.50 kg/m². The group had an average training experience of 3.56 ± 1.31 years (Table 1).

No differences were observed in the 5 m sprint (MD=-0.03, 95% CI [-0.08 to 0.02], f = 1.608, p = 0.226, η²=0.10, Table 2) and 10 m sprint (MD=-0.05, 95% CI [-0.10 to 0.01], f = 4.516, p = 0.052, η²=0.24, Table 2) tests between JSWU and FIFA 11 + conditions. No significant differences (p > 0.05) were found between JSWU and FIFA 11 + for the T-agility test (MD = 0.05, 95% CI [-0.16 to 0.27], f = 0.295, p = 0.596, η²=0.02, Table 2) and broad jump (MD=-2.06, 95% CI [-5.17 to 1.04], f = 2.030, p = 0.176, η²=0.13, Table 2). However, significant differences emerged between JSWU and FIFA 11 + conditions for vertical jump (MD=-2.44, 95% CI [-4.14 to -0.74], f = 9.447, p = 0.008, η²=0.40, Table 2) and anaerobic power (MD=-24.24, 95% CI [-41.84 to -6.64], f = 8.724, p = 0.010, η²=0.38, Table 2), with the FIFA 11 + condition showing higher scores. Similarly, the FIFA 11 + condition demonstrated a significant difference in OMB throw (MD=-35.25, 95% CI [-55.54 to -14.96], f = 13.883, p = 0.002, η²=0.50, Table 2). These significant differences were associated with large effect sizes, although the absolute magnitude of change in performance variables was relatively small.

Discussion

The primary aim of the present study was to examine the effects of the FIFA 11 + warm-up protocol on selected performance parameters in youth judo athletes. Although the FIFA 11 + program is widely used in football, its application in judo, especially alongside sport-specific performance tests, represents a unique contribution to existing research. This uniqueness stems from the inclusion of both general athletic tests and functional hop assessments that closely match the neuromuscular and biomechanical demands of judo.

Analysis of the findings showed that, after the warm-up protocols, the FIFA 11 + program produced statistically significant improvements in VJ, anaerobic power, OMB throw ability, SLHD (right and left), and left-leg CHD performance. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the present study examined only acute responses rather than long-term training adaptations. Although the increases in VJ height and OMB throw are statistically significant and associated with large effect sizes, their practical importance should also be considered. Therefore, the observed effects should be interpreted as statistically meaningful but practically modest in magnitude. For instance, an increase of approximately 2–3 cm in VJ performance may seem modest; however, even small increases in explosive power may reflect modest improvements in acute neuromuscular readiness. In young athletes, such neuromuscular developments may reflect increased readiness for high-intensity movements rather than long-term adaptations. Therefore, the observed changes may indicate improved acute neuromuscular readiness, although this was not directly measured in a sport-specific context. In contrast, there were no significant differences in 5-m and 10-m sprint times, agility, broad jump, BS, LS, HS, THD, or right-leg CHD performances. Overall, the findings partially support our hypothesis and suggest that the FIFA 11 + may indicate small improvements in selected explosive and unilateral functional performance outcomes.

The current study observed significant improvements in vertical jump height, anaerobic power, and OMB throw performance following the FIFA 11 + protocol. While Yurtseven et al. [63] reported no changes in VJ or anaerobic power after an eight-week FIFA 11 + intervention in 10–14-year-old football players, other athletic populations have shown different outcomes. For example, Kahraman et al. [64] found that FIFA 11 + produced significant gains in VJ performance compared with a dynamic warm-up in futsal players. Likewise, Pomares-Noguera et al. [65] reported that a four-week FIFA 11 + program resulted in significant improvements in vertical and horizontal jump performance compared to a general warm-up. The improvement observed in OMB throw performance aligns with the literature supporting the test’s validity and reliability as a measure of upper-body explosive power [57]. This enhancement may be attributed to more efficient kinetic-chain coordination, particularly improved trunk stabilization and force transfer to the upper limbs, which are crucial mechanical qualities in judo gripping, pulling, and throwing. However, these interpretations remain speculative, as no direct measures of judo-specific performance, such as throwing techniques or competitive outcomes, were assessed in the present study.

On the other hand, no significant differences were found in sprint, agility, or broad jump performance. Several studies suggest that programs involving Nordic hamstring exercises (NHE) can lower the risk of hamstring injury [66] and lead to modest improvements in short-distance sprinting [67]. Since the FIFA 11 + includes eccentric posterior-chain loading, the increases in lower-limb power measures observed in our study may be partly attributable to this component. Moreover, research on football indicates that multimodal neuromuscular warm-ups, especially modified FIFA 11 + programs, can improve balance, strength, and agility [68]. Systematic reviews also show reductions in injury risk alongside selective gains in performance outcomes [32]. Our results support this body of research, particularly for tests that depend on neuromuscular control and lower-limb stability. The lack of significant improvements in sprint and agility performance in our study might be due to the short-term nature of the intervention, as both sprinting and change-of-direction skills usually need sustained, targeted, and sport-specific stimuli to produce measurable changes [23, 69]. Although some studies, such as those by Kahraman et al. [64] and Rössler et al. [19], reported improvements in sprint or agility after longer interventions, others have reported inconsistent results. For example, Nawed et al. [70] observed significant gains in sprint speed after 12 weeks of FIFA 11+, while Hwang and Kim [71] found improvements only at 30 m, but not at 10 m. Differences in findings probably reflect variations in intervention length, training context, and age group. Moreover, previous research has highlighted the complex and sometimes inconsistent relationships between speed, agility, and jump performance variables in youth athletes, emphasizing the need for sport-specific evaluation approaches [72].

Regarding functional strength tests, our findings showed significant improvements in both limbs on the SLHD (right/left) and CHD (left) tests following the FIFA 11 + protocol. In contrast, THD performance and CHDRight performance did not show significant changes. This pattern suggests that while the program enhances neuromuscular control and unilateral power, more complex tasks requiring multi-planar coordination and greater plyometric demand, such as the THD, may require longer training interventions. Previous research supports this interpretation. Hop tests are strongly associated with lower-limb strength, dynamic stability, and interlimb asymmetry [73–75]. Zarei et al. [76] also reported that a ten-week FIFA 11 + program improved hop performance in youth football players. However, biomechanical studies have cautioned that hop distance alone may mask movement-quality deficits, since jump length does not always reflect proper kinematic symmetry [77]. Furthermore, some studies have reported no improvement in functional strength after shorter FIFA 11 + protocols [78], whereas others have demonstrated significant gains in strength-related outcomes, including THD and jumping performance [33]. The more complex nature of THD and CH, which require rotational control, multiplanar force production, and quick stabilization, could explain why improvements in these tests were less pronounced in the current study.

Handgrip strength, a key factor in judo performance and a means of distinguishing between competitive levels [1], did not differ significantly across warm-up protocols. This is expected, as maximal strength usually requires extensive training and is less affected by short-term warm-up effects. Previous studies also show that acute neuromuscular warm-ups primarily affect balance, coordination, and explosive power rather than maximal strength [23]. Broad jump performance, a common measure of overall coordination and lower-limb explosive power [79], also showed no significant change, likely due to the brief intervention period. However, judo-specific strength and plyometric training have been shown to improve VJ and linear speed in youth judo athletes [80], suggesting that longer interventions may yield greater gains.

Overall, the findings from this study suggest that the FIFA 11 + may contribute to statistically meaningful but practically small improvements in explosive lower-limb performance, such as vertical jumps, single-leg hops, and crossover hops, as well as in OMB throw performance. These changes may reflect modest acute neuromuscular responses. This apparent discrepancy between effect size and practical magnitude may be explained by low within-subject variability in the sample. However, to achieve greater gains in sprinting, agility, and maximum strength, longer training periods or judo-specific plyometric and strength exercises may be necessary. Additionally, athletic performance is influenced by multiple interacting factors, including physiological, neuromuscular, and contextual variables, which may contribute to variability in performance responses across different tests and conditions [81].

This study should be considered with its limitations. Additionally, the statistical approach should be interpreted with caution, as the crossover design was analyzed without explicitly modeling potential period, sequence, or carryover effects. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 16), which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Although the crossover design reduces interindividual variability, larger sample sizes would improve the robustness of the findings. Second, the study population consisted of competitive youth judo athletes from a single sports club. Therefore, the results may not apply to athletes at different competitive levels, with different training backgrounds, in different age groups, or to female judo athletes. Third, pubertal maturation was not assessed, which may have influenced strength, sprint, and jumping performance outcomes in this age group. Fourth, this research only examined the acute effects of the FIFA 11 + warm-up protocol. As a result, the findings do not reflect potential long-term neuromuscular adaptations that may develop after extended program use. Longitudinal studies are necessary to determine whether repeated exposure to FIFA 11 + results in lasting improvements in judo-related performance parameters. Fifth, anaerobic power was indirectly estimated using the Lewis formula rather than measured directly (e.g., via force platform or Wingate testing), which may limit its validity, particularly in pediatric athletes. Sixth, trial registration was retrospective, which may raise concerns about the prespecification of outcomes and should be considered a limitation of the study. Lastly, although FIFA 11 + is widely recognized for its potential to reduce injury risk, this study did not monitor injury rates or movement quality during the intervention. Therefore, the current findings cannot directly inform the program’s preventive effects on judo athletes. Future research should include larger, more diverse groups; incorporate female athletes and athletes from different age ranges; examine the long-term effects of FIFA 11 + on judo training; and track injury and movement data.

Conclusions

The FIFA 11 + Kids warm-up produced small acute improvements in certain explosive and unilateral functional performance measures, specifically vertical jump, OMB throw, SLHD, and CHD outcomes, while most other variables-such as sprint, agility, maximal leg and back strength, and handgrip strength-showed no significant differences compared to a judo-specific warm-up. These results should be interpreted with caution due to the modest magnitude of performance improvements despite large effect sizes. Therefore, FIFA 11 + Kids may be considered a possible alternative warm-up for youth judo athletes; however, stronger practical recommendations require further confirmation through larger, long-term studies.
