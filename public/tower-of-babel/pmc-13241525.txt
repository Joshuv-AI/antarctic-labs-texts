Marine microplastic concentration and associations with stroke and chronic disease prevalence
Authors: Sai Rahul Ponnana, El-Hussain Shamsa, Zhuo Chen, Tejas Rajagopalan, Tong Zhang, Santosh Sirasapalli, Skanda Moorthy, Jean-Eudes Dazard, Sadeer Al-Kindi, Salil V Deo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroplastics (MPs), synthetic polymer particles <5 mm, are emerging environmental contaminants with potential cardiometabolic relevance. We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 709 coastal census tracts ( < 200 meters from shoreline) by linking NOAA marine microplastic measurements with chronic disease prevalence estimates from the CDC PLACES database. A total of 154 demographic, socioeconomic, and environmental variables including age, sex, race, median household income, Social Vulnerability Index, insurance coverage, and PM2.5 were incorporated to adjust for confounding. Adjusted Poisson regression models showed that tracts with the highest microplastic exposure had higher prevalence of stroke (PR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.13–1.29), diabetes (PR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.10–1.24), and hypertension (PR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.06–1.14) compared with low-exposure areas. Among all covariates, XGBoost models with SHAP interpretation identified microplastic concentration as an important environmental predictor of stroke prevalence. These ecological findings suggest microplastics were associated with chronic disease patterns and warrant further investigation.Subject terms: Cardiovascular diseases, Cardiology

Introduction

Plastic particles are a highly persistent form of pollution and have long been implicated in disruption of ecological systems and more recently in human health1–4. Micro and nano plastics (MNPs) are plastic particles with a size below 5 mm, with microplastics (MPs) defined as <5 mm and nano plastics (NPs) < 1 micron, which are ubiquitous in the environment5. MNPs can be primary, e.g., manufactured MNPs deliberately added to cosmetic products, or secondary byproducts of the chemical and/or mechanical fragmentation of plastics-related waste6–8. MNPs are widespread throughout the Earth’s biosphere and are detectable in the air, water, food, and drinking water2,9–13. Through these same routes, recent estimates suggest that humans might inhale or ingest millions of MP and nanoparticle fragments during their life implicating their role in chronic diseases14. Among the range of possible effects on human health, MNPs are emerging as a possible risk factor for the development of cardiovascular diseases (CVD). Findings from in vitro studies advance the hypothesis that MNPs trigger a range of pathophysiological pathways in cells relevant for the development of CVD. These involve pathways through endothelial and immune cells and involve pathophysiologic alterations that include endothelial dysfunction and immune activation, which are corroborated by animal models that suggest such alterations following exposure to MNPs15. Preliminary evidence from humans substantiates the accumulation of and a possible pathological role of MNPs in the cardiovascular system4,15–19. Indeed, several reports have noted the presence of at least one type of MNPs in ex vivo samples derived from the cardiovascular system. In a recent paper, patients in whom MNPs were detected within the atheroma in the carotid artery, obtained during carotid endarterectomy, had a higher risk for a primary end-point event than those in whom MNPs were not detected. This is the first evidence of a prospective association between MNPs and a hard health outcome, and in particular CVD20. We hypothesized that MNP levels in the environment are associated with, and may help predict, cardiovascular events and stroke21.

In this study, we use water and sediment samples as estimates of relative plastic exposure in coastal cities with the aim of showing that MNP exposure is a key risk factor for chronic disease in coastal populations. Our goal in this study is to highlight microplastic pollution as a chief public health concern to promote further research and initiatives to decrease human exposure to plastic particles.

Results

The 709 census tracts used in the analysis across the United States are shown in Fig. S1 of supplementary material stratified by microplastics concentration. Descriptive analysis revealed that census tracts with higher microplastic concentrations tended to have higher prevalence rates of high blood pressure, diabetes, and stroke (Table 1). The ANOVA results further reinforced this, with significant differences in disease prevalence observed across microplastic concentration categories (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). Stroke prevalence was highest in areas classified as having very high microplastic concentrations (mean prevalence: 8.2%), followed by coronary heart disease (CHD) (7.1%), High BP (42.5%), and diabetes (13.8%). These values were significantly higher compared to areas with very low microplastic concentrations, where stroke prevalence was 4.5%, CHD was 5.3%, High BP was 35.2%, and diabetes was 9.1%. Importantly, total population, median age, percentage of Black residents, and social vulnerability index tended to increase with higher MP concentrations. Similarly, environmental factors such as light pollution, PM2.5 levels, sulfate concentration, and traffic proximity were also elevated in census tracts with high MP exposure. Median household income and Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) show a decreasing trend with increasing microplastic concentration. Additionally, we compared the environmental characteristics and health outcomes of census tracts included in our analysis (N = 709) to those not considered (N = 62 360) (Table 2). These comparisons between census tracts included in the analysis and those excluded revealed only minor differences in health-related factors, while several statistical differences were observed due to the large sample size, absolute differences in key disease prevalence measures were modest, suggesting limited practical selection bias. While included census tracts had a slightly higher median age (44 vs. 40 years) and lower NDVI (0.43 vs. 0.57), the prevalence of key health conditions, including stroke (3.10 vs. 3.14), diabetes (10.9 vs. 10.9), and high cholesterol (35.3 vs. 34.4), remained similar across both groups. Thus, the selection of census tracts did not significantly impact the prevalence of the observed conditions.

Correlation analysis in Fig. S2 in the supplementary material confirmed significant positive trends between disease prevalence and microplastics, with microplastic concentration exhibiting moderate correlations with High BP (r = 0.24, p < 0.001), diabetes (r = 0.30, p < 0.001), and stroke (r = 0.26, p < 0.001). Fully adjusted Poisson regression models, accounting for tract-level age, sex, race/ethnicity, median household income, Social Vulnerability Index, percentage uninsured, and PM2.5, further confirmed these associations—the prevalence ratios for chronic conditions across different microplastic exposure categories were derived, demonstrating that high microplastics exposure census-tracts were at significantly higher prevalence of these conditions (Fig. 1). The prevalence ratio for stroke increased from 1.02 (95% CI: 1.01–1.04) in medium microplastic exposure areas to 1.21 (95% CI: 1.13–1.29) in very high exposure areas. Similarly, for diabetes, the prevalence ratio increased from 1.04 (95% CI: 1.02–1.06) in medium microplastic exposure areas to 1.17 (95% CI: 1.10–1.24) in very high exposure areas. High BP showed a consistent increase in prevalence ratio, rising from 1.03 (95% CI: 1.02–1.05) in medium microplastic exposure areas to 1.10 (95% CI: 1.06–1.14) in very high exposure areas.

The XGBoost model to predict stroke prevalence achieved an RMSE of 0.44 and an R² of 0.73 on the test set, indicating reasonable predictive performance. SHAP analysis demonstrated that microplastic exposure contributed meaningfully to predicted prevalence but ranked below socioeconomic and environmental factors such as PM2.5, traffic exposure, and median income (Fig. 2), suggesting a potential dose-response relationship between MP exposure and stroke. SHAP analysis provided a ranked list of important predictors influencing the model output. Among these predictors, microplastic concentration had a considerable impact on stroke prevalence, with a SHAP value of 0.027, making it one of the top contributors alongside PM2.5 (SHAP value: 0.014), traffic proximity (SHAP value: 0.014), and socioeconomic variables like median household income (Fig. 3). Further analysis of microplastic exposure in relation to socioeconomic and environmental variables revealed additional insights (Table 1). High microplastic concentration areas tended to overlap with regions characterized by lower median household income (mean: $48,500 vs. $62,300 in low exposure areas), higher rates of uninsured population (15.4% vs. 9.8%), and increased levels of air pollution (PM2.5 mean concentration: 12.3 µg/m³ vs. 9.5 µg/m³ in low exposure areas). This suggests that environmental justice issues may exacerbate the health risks associated with microplastic pollution. Moreover, the presence of microplastics was strongly associated with other known risk factors for chronic disease, such as smoking prevalence (mean: 23.1% vs. 16.8%), lower access to healthcare, and increased social vulnerability index (SVI score: 0.62 vs. 0.44). Additional analyses of other disease outcomes are detailed in Figs. S3–S8 in the supplementary material.

Discussion

There are several important findings in this study. We provide evidence that microplastic pollution may be linked to increased prevalence of high blood pressure, diabetes, and stroke. The prevalence ratios increased with increasing environmental presence of MP. Stroke prevalence was highest in areas classified as having very high microplastic concentrations (mean prevalence: 8.2), followed by CHD, high BP and diabetes (Table 1). High MP concentration areas tended to overlap with regions characterized by lower socioeconomic status, education and higher rates of pollutants. Given this overlap, we used a machine learning approach, XGBoost, which leverages decision trees to select features sequentially, thus reducing the impact of collinear variables by naturally handling redundant features and effectively models non-linear interactions. XGBoost identified MP concentration as a key predictor of stroke prevalence, with SHAP analysis suggesting it to be one of the top contributors alongside measures of urbanization, socioeconomic variables, and median household income (Figs. 2, 3). Importantly, these machine learning analyses showed that microplastic concentration was one of the most important predictors of stroke prevalence amongst environmental factors used in the study, including pollutants such as PM2.5 and ozone and environmental indices such as NDVI.

The observed associations align with mechanistic hypotheses suggesting that microplastics contribute to systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and hormonal and metabolic dysregulation, all of which are established risk factors for cardiovascular and metabolic disorders18,22. Recently, several papers have noted the presence of MPs and nano plastics in human atheroma. In carotid plaques excised from 257 patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy were subject to Pyrolysis–gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS) to provide an estimate of the concentration of different plastic types and followed-up for 3 years to monitor the incidence of myocardial infarction, stroke, and all-cause mortality19. Roughly 150 patients had evidence of PE within the plaque, with 32 also having measurable amounts of PVC. Analysis with electron microscopy revealed “jagged-edged particles” among plaque macrophages. Patients with MNPs had increased plaque expression of inflammatory markers, i.e., IL-18, IL-1β, TNFα, IL-6, CD68, and CD3, and a lower abundance of collagen within plaque. Importantly, patients with detectable MNPs in atheroma were at higher risk for a primary end-point event than those with no evidence23.

The negative correlation between microplastic exposure and cancer prevalence is an intriguing finding that warrants further exploration, but, in some ways, this negative association strengthens the association with cardiovascular conditions. One possible explanation is that high microplastic exposure areas may also have distinct demographic or environmental characteristics that reduce cancer risk, such as improved healthcare access or genetic predispositions. Another hypothesis is that regions with high MP pollution might lack industrial carcinogenic exposures that typically contribute to elevated cancer rates in other populations. However, more longitudinal studies are needed to disentangle these associations and determine whether microplastic exposure directly influences cancer risk or if it interacts with other environmental factors.

Our study has multiple limitations, beginning with the fact that we could only report on predominantly coastal communities, which represent a small fraction of overall census tracts, but document information on environmental presence of MP. Since this is an ecological analysis using tract-level aggregated data, individual-level causal inferences cannot be drawn, and ecological fallacy is possible. Census tracts may exhibit spatial clustering; therefore, residual spatial autocorrelation may remain despite adjustment for regional socioeconomic and environmental covariates. Microplastic measurements were collected over multiple years prior to 2019, whereas disease prevalence and covariates were derived from 2019 datasets. Therefore, temporal mismatch may introduce exposure misclassification. However, marine microplastic burden tends to persist geographically over time, suggesting relative spatial rankings may remain informative. Further, a substantial overlap with other contaminants and socioeconomic factors renders a direct causal association difficult. However, the co-prevalence of many other variables also associated with chronic health conditions is not only seen with MPs but with several other plastic associated chemicals and indeed with other environmental pollutants.

However, despite limitations, our study does highlight the need for carefully conducted epidemiological studies when more data on the concentration of MPs and nano plastics are available. Prospective cohort studies and controlled experimental models are also essential for elucidating the precise biological pathways through which microplastics contribute to disease risk. By advancing our understanding of these mechanisms, we can develop targeted interventions to mitigate the potential health hazards posed by microplastic pollution.

This study provides epidemiological evidence linking microplastic exposure to chronic disease prevalence in U.S. coastal regions. The observed associations with high blood pressure, diabetes, and stroke suggest that higher microplastics concentration at tract-level may represent an emerging environmental risk factor, warranting further investigation. Given the increasing global burden of NCDs, understanding the role of environmental pollutants, including microplastics, is essential for developing effective public health interventions and regulatory policies. The results underscore the need for future research to explore causative mechanisms and inform targeted interventions aimed at reducing microplastic exposure and mitigating its health effects.

Methods

This study utilized a cross-sectional design to examine the association between microplastic concentration and the prevalence of chronic diseases across U.S. coastal census tracts. Data on environmental microplastic concentrations were obtained from the National Oceanographic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) database, covering microplastic pollution levels in marine and coastal regions24. The NOAA NCEI global Marine Microplastics product provides access to aggregated, comprehensive, standardized, quality controlled, global data on microplastics in marine settings, from 1972 to 2023. For this analysis, we restricted NOAA microplastic measurements to the period [1972–2019], which aligns with the period of densest sampling in U.S. coastal waters. Sampling locations in the NOAA NCEI marine microplastics product are derived from multiple oceanographic and coastal monitoring campaigns rather than from a uniform, probability-based sampling frame. As a result, measurements are denser in some estuaries and coastal regions than others, reflecting both scientific priorities and logistical feasibility rather than underlying microplastic gradients per se. The database contains information on microplastics concentrations (reported in units based on marine setting—pieces/m3 for water samples, pieces/10 min for handpicked measurements, or pieces/kg dry weight (d.w.) for sediment samples), date of collection, latitude and longitude where data was collected, sampling instruments used for collection, and links to publications on the data25. The prevalence rates of high blood pressure, diabetes, stroke, and cancer were sourced from the CDC PLACES database, which provides small-area health estimates for geographic regions, allowing for spatial correlation analyses. CDC PLACES 2019 prevalence estimates reflect underlying BRFSS 2019 data. Thus, our linkage represents a cross-sectional alignment of microplastic measurements from (1972–2019) with chronic disease prevalence estimates around 2019.

Socioeconomic and environmental variables were integrated to control potential confounders. These variables included median household income, employment rates, Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), air pollution levels (PM2.5), traffic proximity, and population demographics. All the variables included in the analysis were listed in Table S1 in the supplementary material. The selection of these variables was guided by prior research indicating their relevance in disease epidemiology and environmental health studies25,26. The dataset consisted of 709 coastal census tracts, classified into five microplastic concentration categories: very low (0–0.0005 pieces/m3, 0–2 pieces/kg d.w., or 0–1 pieces/10 min), low (0.0005–0.005 pieces/m3, 2–20 pieces/kg d.w., 1–2 pieces/10 min), medium (0.005–1 pieces/m3, 20–150 pieces/kg d.w., 2–40 pieces/10 min), high (1–10 pieces/m3, 150–200 pieces/kg d.w., 40–200 pieces/10 min), and very high ( > 10 pieces/m3, >200 pieces/kg d.w., >200 pieces/10 min). For analysis, census tracts categorized as having “Very Low” and “Low” microplastic concentrations were merged into a single reference group to improve statistical power and ensure stable model estimates, given their similar exposure levels and comparable disease prevalence patterns.

Data preprocessing and analysis

The NCEI microplastic dataset includes geospatial information in the form of latitude and longitude coordinates. To integrate this data with health and socioeconomic datasets, we linked microplastic measurements according to their coordinates to the corresponding U.S. census tracts. Specifically, for each microplastic measurement point, we calculated the nearest census tract within a 200-m radius using spatial operations in R. This was accomplished by utilizing the “sf” package to read and transform geographic data.

We first obtained U.S. census tract boundaries for 2019 using the Tigris package and transformed them to the WGS 84 coordinate reference system. Microplastic measurement points were also projected to the same coordinate system. A 200-meter buffer was applied to each census tract to capture nearby microplastic measurements. A spatial join operation was then performed to associate microplastic concentration levels with the closest census tract. Census tracts were eligible for inclusion if at least one NOAA microplastic sampling point fell within a 200-meter buffer of the tract’s coastal boundary. Tracts without any overlapping microplastic measurements were excluded because no tract-level microplastic exposure estimate could be derived from the NOAA data. We did not have direct microplastic measurements for excluded tracts, but we compared their demographic, socioeconomic, environmental, and health characteristics to included tracts to evaluate the potential for selection bias.

To ensure robustness, we filtered out census tracts without any microplastic data and categorized microplastic exposure levels using descriptive statistics. The dataset was then aggregated at the census tract level, computing the mean, median, and mode of microplastic concentration values. This allowed us to analyze the distribution of microplastic pollution across different census tracts and examine its association with chronic disease prevalence.

The collected dataset underwent extensive preprocessing to ensure data quality and consistency. Missingness across variables was generally low to moderate, with a maximum of 17.63% for any individual variable. Missing data were handled using Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). We generated 5 imputed datasets over 10 iterations using predictive mean matching for continuous variables and then used the imputed data for downstream analyses. Disease prevalence outcomes were standardized where appropriate, and regression models were adjusted for age, sex, and race-related tract demographics.

Statistical and machine learning analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in R. We employed correlation tests to evaluate the strength of association between microplastic levels and disease prevalence. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences in disease rates across microplastic concentration groups. We fitted Poisson regression models with robust standard errors to evaluate associations between tract-level microplastic exposure categories and chronic disease prevalence. Microplastic exposure was modeled as an ordinal factor. Based on known determinants of cardiovascular and metabolic disease and environmental inequality, we selected a set of tract-level confounders a priori: Median age, sex (percentage female), racial/ethnic composition, median household income, Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), percentage of residents without health insurance, and PM2.5 concentration. These covariates capture demographic, socioeconomic, and environmental conditions known to influence both microplastic pollution patterns and chronic disease burden. All primary Poisson models included this same adjustment set.

Lastly, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) regression model was trained on all 154 socioeconomic and environmental features to predict tract-level prevalence of each chronic disease outcome. XGBoost models were trained using repeated 10-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning and internal validation, followed by final model fitting on the training data and evaluation on the held-out test set. Data were randomly split into 80% training and 20% test sets. Hyperparameters included a learning rate (eta) of 0.1, maximum depth of 20, and up to 100 boosting iterations with early stopping based on validation error (10-round patience). Performance was evaluated using root mean squared error (RMSE) and coefficient of determination (R²) on the test set. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were used to quantify feature contributions. Models included microplastic exposure together with the full set of demographics, socioeconomic, and environmental variables used in the merged dataset.
