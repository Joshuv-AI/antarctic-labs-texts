Pre-Lecture Lesson Plans and Learning: insights from Undergraduate Medical Students (PLP-UMS)
Authors: Pooja Agrawal, Aditya Singhal, Nasra Banu, Anju Saxena, Sujata Singh, Shalini Chandra, Iram Shefali, Abhinav Srivastava
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLesson plans are structured outlines that enhance clarity, focus and learning engagement in medical education. Sharing pre lecture lesson plans (PLPs) may improve preparedness, attention and satisfaction among students. This study aimed to assess undergraduate medical students’ perceptions of PLPs and validate a new scale to measure these perceptions.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 251 s year MBBS students using a 11-item questionnaire on a 5-point Likert scale. Item analysis, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was applied to assess validity of questionnaire of pre-lecture plan. Reliability was measured using Cronbach’s α. Criterion validity was examined using Spearman’s correlation between individual domains and total PLP-UMS scores.ResultsEFA yielded an 11-item, three factor structure (participation, attitude, satisfaction) explaining 71.9% of total variance. Reliability analysis demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.773,0.765,0.714) for all domains. Inter-factor correlations indicated strong associations between participation and attitude(r = 0.789) and moderate associate with satisfaction(r = 0.516 − 0.491). the majority of students agreed that PLP was beneficial and were satisfied that pre-lecture lesson plan enhanced their participation and learning during lectures.ConclusionsPLP-UMS is valid and reliable tool to measure students’ perception of lesson plans. Pre-lecture lesson plans significantly improve students’ preparedness, confidence, engagement and satisfaction, supporting their better adoption in undergraduate medical curriculum.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08144-0.Keywords: Pre-lecture lesson plan. medical education, Students’ engagement, Factor analysis, Teaching methods

Background

Effective teaching in medical education requires more than just context expertise; it demands structured, goal -oriented planning to facilitate meaningful learning experiences. One such tool is the lesson plan. A lesson plan is a structured document that outlines the objectives, content, instructional methods, resources and assessment strategies for a particular teaching session. It serves as a roadmap for educators, ensuring that learning goals are systematically addressed, classroom activities are organized and student learning is effectively monitored [1].

The relevance of structured lesson planning has been highlighted in numerous educational theories and practices, particularly within the framework of outcome-based education. In medical education, where content is vast, complex and clinically oriented, the value of coherent and logically sequenced instruction cannot be overstated [2].

The importance of lesson planning extends beyond organizational benefits; it directly influences quality of learning. Studies in science education demonstrate that detailed lesson plans assist in teaching in sequencing content logically, framing effective questions, and anticipating learners’ misconceptions [3, 4]. This structured approach promotes cognitive scaffolding, allowing students to build upon prior knowledge in a meaningful way. Furthermore, lesson planning enhances classroom management by minimizing disruptions and creating a conducive environment for active participation [5]. Medical educator who adopts systematic planning have been reported to experience higher level of confidence and instructional efficiency, thereby fostering improved academic achievement among learners [6, 7].

Another dimension of lesson planning lies in its role in reflective practice and teachers’ professional development. structured plans allow medical educators to critically analyze the effectiveness of their instructional strategies, adapt them to diverse learners need, and in cooperate feedback for continuous improvement [8]. In students-centered learning environments, lesson planning has been associated with deeper engagement, as it facilitates differentiation instruction tailored to learners’ diversity. Additionally, incorporating evidence -based strategies and assessment techniques into lesson plans ensues alignment between intended learning outcomes and students’ performance, reinforcing the value of planning as pedagogical tool [9].

Lesson planning facilitates the integration of the cognitive, psychomotor and affective learning domains thereby enhancing both knowledge acquisition and clinical application. A well-structured lesson plan promotes clarity, consistency and efficiency in the delivery of educational content. They assist instructors in maintaining focus, adhering to time constraints and cooperating with active learning strategies such as case- based learning, discussion and formative assessments [10]. Studies have also shown that when students are made aware of lesson objectives and expected outcomes, their motivation, satisfaction and academic performance tend to improve [11].

Despite the acknowledged benefits of lesson planning from the teacher’s perspective, there remains a paucity of empirical data examining its perceived value from the student’s side particularly in undergraduate medical education. Student perception is crucial, as it directly influences teaching methods, levels of participation and overall learning efficacy. Understanding how students view the role of lesson plans in enhancing their educational experience may offer valuable insights for curriculum development, faculty training and structural design [12].

Despite these advantages, lesson planning is sometimes underutilized, particularly in resource-constrained settings where teachers may rely on improvisation. Research also highlights gaps in understanding how lesson planning directly translates into measurable improvements in students’ performance across different disciplines and educational levels. This study aims to assess the perceptions of undergraduate medical students regarding the role of lesson plans in enhancing their learning experience. This study seeks to evaluate the impact of pre-lecture lesson plans on students’ engagement, confidence and active participation during lectures and investigate how pre-lecture lesson plans shape their study habits and self-directed learning strategies.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study conducted in the Department of Pharmacology RMCH, Bareilly among students studying in the second year of their MBBS professional course.

Sample size

Study place and participants

All second-year MBBS students present at that time in the classroom and willing to fill out the Google form were included in the study. Those with incomplete forms and those who did not consent to participate were excluded from our study. The responses of the students were recorded Google forms. The information collected from the participants in the study was kept confidential.

Students were divided into small groups of 25 students each. Each group was supervised by a designated faculty member and postgraduate students who were previously trained to facilitate the process. The students communicated to bring their mobile phones to fill out the Google forms one day prior. All the students filled out the form at the same time. This was done to eliminate peer bias at the time of completion of the form.

A pre formed questionnaire (Annexure I) was circulated among the students through Google forms. The form was validated through a pilot study of 10 students from a third-year MBBS professional course. The form included a consent form, name initials, questions about lesson plans, questions related to perceptions of lesson plans, questions related to satisfaction, and alternatives to lesson plans. Questions related to perception were evaluated on 5-point Likert scale [13] ranging from strongly disagree = 0, disagree = 1, neutral = 2, agree = 3, strongly agree = 3. Later, an analysis of the response was performed.

Ethical clearance

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the university to which the authors are affiliated (approval number RMCH/10/2025/APR).

Data analysis

For the statistical analysis, first, an item analysis was performed. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and criterion-related validity were performed to assess the form validity. Internal consistency and reproducibility were examined to assess reliability. Jamovi version 2.6 was used for analysis.

Item analysis

The standard deviations and means of all the items estimated to detect the range, which were assigned in the 1–5 range. The item- total (I-T) correlations were examined [14].

Validity analysis

Content validity and psychometric testing

The qualitative and quantitative assessment of content validity was carried out I two phases. Both the content validity index (CVI) and the content validity ratio were calculated. The preliminary version consisted of 22 items and was revived by a panel of 10 experts. These experts were faculty members from various medical teaching and research institutions in India, with 5–25 years of teaching experience and a demonstrated interest in medical education. The evaluation process was conducted via email and google forms [15].

Content validity was evaluated using the content validity index (CVI) and content validity ratio (CVR). For CVI, experts rated each item on a four -point Likert scale (1 = not relevant to 4 = = highly relevant). CVI was calculated using the formula:

A CVI value of 0.78 or higher was considered acceptable for content validity. To determine the CVR, experts were also asked to rate the necessity of each item on a three- point Likert scale:1-not necessary,2-useful but not essential and 3-essential. The CVR for each item was then computed using Lawshe’s formula. The minimum acceptable CVR values were determined using Lawshe’s Table [16].

Exploratory factor analysis

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted to identify the underlying factor structure related to the lesson plan0based student learning. The Kaiser-Meyer- Olkin (KMO) test assessed sampling adequacy, and Bartlett’s specificity test evaluated the suitability of data for factor analysis. Scree plots were examined to determine the optimal number of factors, considering eigenvalues ≥ 1 and the proportion of variance explained. A maximum likelihood (ML) method with ProMax rotation was applied. Items with factor loadings < 0.5 were excluded. The chi-squared test, goodness-of-fit index (GFI), comparative fix index (CFI) and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) were used to assess data fit. The goodness of fit of the model was considered very good when GFI >0.95, CFI >0.90 and RMSEA < 0.05. The ratio of chi-square values with respect to the degrees of freedom(x2/df < 2.00) was also evaluated. For criterion validity, Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the correlation between individual items the total PLP-UMC scale [17, 18].

Reliability analysis

The entire form and each sub-form were analyzed via Cronbach’s alpha coefficient to confirm the reliability of the form [19].

Response analysis

The responses were analyzed via descriptive statistics and are expressed as percentages and means ± standard deviations.

Results

Approximately 251 participants were included in the study, among whom 141 were males and 110 were females. The mean PLP-UMS scores for males and females were (max score 110; minimum score 22) 82.6 and 82.5, respectively, with no significant differences. An overwhelming majority of the students (99.25%) reported that sharing lesson plans before scheduled lectures was beneficial. Similarly, 98.87% of the students believed that sharing lesson plans should continue in the future. The sharing of lesson plans 24 h prior to the lecture was mostly (57.86%) preferred by the students, whereas some (30.83%) students preferred sharing plans 48 h before the lecture. In response to the sharing of lesson plans with the students, they were expected to prepare an action plan in which they decided on how to prepare for the lecture where 52.08% of the students would “go through the topic”, whereas 35.85% wanted to prepare for the lecture by going through the pre-lecture assignment as well.

Evaluation of reliability and validity of the PLP-UMS

Data from 251 undergraduate medical students were used for analysis. The majority of the items had a frequency of 4-point responses. Items 14 and 17 had a frequency of two-point responses; however, all the questions had a frequency of response above 90%. The maximum mean and standard deviation of each item were 4.17 ± 0.68, and the minimum value was 2.64 ± 0.63, with no ceiling or floor effects. I-T correlations were significantly correlated with the overall scores of all the items. Thus, no items were removed from the item analysis.

Validity testing

An item analysis (as shown in Table 1) was performed on the 21 items of the proposed scale; no item was deleted. Therefore, an EFA was performed on all 21 items via ML methods and Promax rotation. The KMO index of the sampling adequacy was 0.934. On the basis of on eigenvalues >1.0 an scree plots, the number of factors was three. Items with loading values >0.5 were retained. Eleven items [3, 8, 9, 13–20] were removed because their factor loadings were < 0.5. Finally, an 11-item scale with a three-factor structure was employed. Each factor was interpreted and named by comparing the theoretical characteristics derived from the three-factor structure employed in the EFA.

Reliability testing

Internal consistency: An analysis of the internal consistency of the final three-factor 11-item scale revealed high reliability for all the factors. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the overall scale was 0.937. The coefficients for the subscale’s personal participation, attitude perception and satisfaction perception were 0.773,0.765 and 0.714, respectively.

Response from students

A significant percentage of the students (75.56%) agreed or strongly agreed that they were better prepared for competency, and 78.95% reported improvement in their proficiency level. The majority of the students (64.66%) agreed or strongly agreed that they were less bored in class, and 81.58% felt that it helped increase their attention span. Approximately 89.10% of the students were either satisfied (59.02%) or very satisfied (30.08%) with the sharing of the pre-lecture lesson plan. A total of 85.59% felt more involved in the class, and 81.20% felt confident in answering questions related to pre-lecture assignments. A total of 71.80% reported better performance in assessments and exams, and 78.20% were able to retain knowledge in an organized manner. A total of 71.05% were motivated to read the topic before the lecture and 81.20% were able to integrate competency with other subjects. A total of 86.09% found objectives clearly understandable, and 78.41% felt that SLO helped them focus on important areas. Similarly, 89.10% of the students were satisfied (57.89%) or very satisfied with the overall concept of the lesson plan.

The students were asked about feedback related to improvements in the lesson plan. A total of 74.24% suggested that no improvement is needed, 4.17% suggested improving the content and format, 4.55% suggested that the lesson plan should be shared before 48 h or even a week before the lecture, and 16.66% of the students expressed interactive elements during the lecture and post lecture assignment questions. Discussion

This study explains that undergraduate medical students’ perceptions regarding sharing of pre lecture lesson plans and validated a new tool, the PLP-UMS to measure these perceptions. These findings clearly demonstrate that PLPs were perceived as beneficial in enhancing preparation, classroom engagement, confidence and satisfaction. Moreover, the PLP-UMS exhibited strong psychometric properties, confirming its utility as valid and reliable tool for assessing the impact of lesson plan sharing in medical education.

Comparison of perception of lesson plan sharing

The majority of students (98%) endorsed the sharing of lesson plans as helpful with most preferring to receive them 24-48gours in advance. This observation highlights the importance of providing students with sufficient time to engage in preparatory learning activities without overwhelming them. Previous studies have reported that pre-class exposure to learning objectives or preparatory materials encourages self-regulation, improves comprehension and promotes meaningful learning [20–22]. Our findings are consistent with Diggele et.al [23] and Evans et al. [24]who observed that advance distribution of learning objectives significantly enhanced readiness and fostered a sense of ownership among students. Importantly more than half of students in this study reported using PLPs to review the topic prior to class and over on third engage with pre-lecture assignments, reinforcing the principle of self-directed learning-a cornerstone of competency-based education [25].

Psychometric evaluation of PLP-UMS

The development of an 11-item, three-factor scale represents a novel contribution to educational assessment.th factors-participation, attitude and satisfaction-capture different but complementary aspects of students experience with PLPs. Participation perception reflects the degree of active engagement and preparedness, attitude perception encompasses confidence, curiosity and ability to apply learning; and satisfaction perception represents overall acceptance and endorsement of the intervention. The three-factor solution explained nearly half explained nearly half of variance, which is within the acceptable range for educational and psychological scales [26, 27]. Internal consistency for the overall scales was excellent(α = 0.937), with all subscales achieving reliability above the recommended threshold of 0.70 [28]. furthermore, criterion validity was strongly supported by the significant positive corelations between subscales and overall PLP-UMS, confirming that the instrument adequately captures students’ perception across different domains.

Impact of lesson plans on students learning

The results suggest that PLPs positively influence multiple facts of the learning process. The students reported greater preparedness for competencies, improved proficiency and increased attention span during lectures. These findings resonate with cognitive learning theories, particularly Ausubel’s theory of advance organizers, which posits that prior exposure to structured material facilitates meaningful assimilations of new information [29]. This observation that students linked theoretical concepts to clinical applications also aligns with Biggs constructive alignment framework, where pre-class preparation enables learners to achieve intended learning outcomes more effectively [30].

Moreover, lesson plans appear to enhance students’ confidence in classroom interactions. Over 80% of respondents expressed confidence in answering questions or raising queries during lectures, reflecting not only increased preparedness but also reduced anxiety in engaging with faculty. This aligns with prior work by Ghani et.al [31] which emphasized that structured guidance prior to learning sessions increase learners’ confidence and participation. Additionally, the reported ability to integrate competencies across subjects and retain knowledge in an organized manner underscores the potential role of PLPs in bridging preclinical and clinical learning, a critical expectation in medical curriculum [32, 33].

Students’ satisfaction and feedback

This high satisfaction rate (nearly 90%) demonstrates strong acceptance of lessons plans as an educational tool. While the majority felt that no modifications were required, some students suggested earlier distribution of lesson plans and incorporation of interactive element such as post lecture assignment or discussion prompts. This suggests that while PLPs provide a foundation for learning, students increasingly except bended strategies that combine students’ preparation with active, participatory methods during lectures. Studies done by Benamer et.al [34] and Berglund et.al [35] have shown active learning techniques significantly improve students’ performance compared to traditional didactic lectures. Integrating lesson plans with such strategies may yield synergistic effects, enhancing both engagement and knowledge retention.

Conclusion

Prelecture lesson plans represent an evidence-based method for enhancing students’ learning in medical education. Thus, support students’ autonomy, improve engagement and foster academic success, provided they are intentionally designed, adequately supported and meaningfully aligned with course goals.
