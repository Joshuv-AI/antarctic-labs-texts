Hybrid approximate multiplier architectures for low power DWT image compression
Authors: R Anitha, Sriadibhatla Sridevi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractApproximate computing is a means for energy efficient computing by allowing some degree of arithmetic imprecision for error tolerant processing applications such as image compression. This work presents a high-efficiency image compression architecture which exploits the use of hybrid approximate multipliers in the discrete wavelet transform (DWT)-pipeline. The work presents four approximate variants which are realized using hybrid Wallace, Dadda and Baugh-Wooley multipliers which results in significant reduction in delay, power consumption and hardware resource usage. Approximation is only used in the least significant areas of the calculation, and exact calculations are used for the most significant calculation areas to maintain visual accuracy. Experimental results show great improvements with the fourth variant of the approximate DWT. The proposed design shows a PSNR of 35.9 dB, a SSIM of 0.94 and compression ratio of 1.9 when integrated to the wavelet-based compression scheme. There is little deviation between original and reconstructed images based on the error analysis. Hardware performance analysis in terms of area, power, and delay is performed by FPG synthesis targeting Artix-7 platform. Overall, the obtained results show that strategic use of approximation in selected computational stages allows energy-efficient wavelet-based image compression without noticeable loss in the perceptual quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54818-0.Keywords: Approximate multipliers, Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT), Hybrid arithmetic circuits, Image compressionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The rapid development of multimedia systems, digital imaging and edge-based computing systems has resulted in the growing need for efficient image compression techniques with higher compression ratios and hardware efficiency while maintaining the quality of the images. Transform-based approaches, i.e., Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) and Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) have been widely used because of their good energy compaction property and the ability of exploiting the perceptual redundancy in images1. However, the realization of these transforms in hardware require computationally intensive arithmetic operations (especially multiplications) which has a significant impact on the utilization of area, power and processing delay2.

Approximate computing has come to be an effective framework to meet these issues by deliberately accepting small computational errors in return for better efficiency of the hardware. In error-tolerant applications such as image compression, where the human visual system is not very sensitive to small distortions, approximate arithmetic circuits offer considerable power-delay-area trade-offs with minimum loss of perceived quality3. Several approximate arithmetic designs have been proposed which have demonstrated promising improvements in the computational throughput and energy efficiency, such as the Vedic multipliers, the compressor-based structures and the tree-based multipliers4–6.

Among the transform-based compression techniques, the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) has gained a lot of prominence because of its multiresolution representation ability, its spatial-frequency localization, and its ability to strongly compact the energy7. DWT is used to divide image into several sub-bands with different perceptual significance, which allows approximation of the less important components of the image and preserving the overall image fidelity8. This property makes DWT particularly suitable to combine it with approximate arithmetic units so that conventional multipliers in the filtering stages can be replaced with approximate ones in order to minimize power consumption and area overhead.

Recent developments in approximate computing have further increased the need for the evaluation of arithmetic approximations not only at the circuit level, but also in complete system implementations and under diverse imaging conditions. Consequently, there is a growing demand for compression architectures having a consistent performance on many different data sets and that are competitive against the emerging types of nanoscale and CMOS based approximate designs reported in the literature. To this end, there is an increased emphasis in modern studies on the use of external benchmarking and cross-domain evaluation to measure generalization capability on other than standard test images. Inspired by this direction, the present work extends its evaluation framework with comparative benchmarking by using state-of-the-art approximate multipliers and comprehensive evaluation of the image quality by the Kodak Lossless Image Suite. It enables strict analysis of hardware-quality trade-offs of approximate DWT designs and guarantees the high-performance compression of a wide variety of visual data.

Despite these developments important gaps remained in the existing literature. Most of the previous work does not address arithmetic-level design decisions, multiplier-level implementation, and end-to-end DWT reconstruction behavior all in a common evaluation framework, but instead either single blocks of approximate arithmetic compaction or transform-level image compression. In particular, little attention has been paid to the coordinated use of approximate versions of the full adder, the hybrid multiplier structures, and approximate versions of the final stage prefix addition in a complete FDWT-IDWT image compression pipeline. Therefore, there needs to be a more integrated hardware-aware and application-aware evaluation to determine the realistic trade-offs between hardware efficiency and the quality of reconstructed image.

Although previous studies have demonstrated the benefits of approximate adders and multipliers, many of those studies only focus on the arithmetic unit by itself, or only focus the evaluation on the forward DWT stage. In contrast, this study takes hybrid approximate multipliers and integrates it into full-forward and full-inverse DWT (FDWT - IDWT) image compression scheme and tests several multiplier architectures under a uniform experimental environment. Approximation is localized to least significant stages of computation, thus maintaining perception dominant information and significant reductions in power consumption and processing delay. To meet these drawbacks, this work proposes a hierarchical approximate-arithmetic based DWT based image compression scheme, where approximate full adder variants are used for hybrid multiplier architectures and Approximate Hybrid Kogge-Stone Adder (AHKSA) is used for the final accumulation stage.

Literature review

Approximate computing has become another efficient way of achieving area and power efficiency in image and signal processing hardware where we do not need exact precision. A number of researchers has considered a variety of architectures, which use approximate arithmetic units in combination with efficient image compression and transformation methods.

For image processing projects, an approximate 8:2 compressor was suggested, just by using XNORs instead of XOR gates9. The non-hierarchical architecture managed to give 5% improvement in delay and 17% area reduction of the traditional hierarchical design, however, the design was not validated on advanced compression algorithms. Similarly, an approximate Vedic multiplier optimized for digital signal processing applications named finite impulse response (FIR) filters was proposed in the study in10. The architecture was able to achieve 52% area, 42% power and 59% delay reduction but was limited in its performance by the use of a simple ripple carry adder. Further comparative analysis of the Vedic multipliers with various architectures for adders has been presented in11. It was observed that Kogge-Stone Adders (KSAs) gave the lowest delay as compared to Carry Lookahead and Carry Save adders. However the hierarchical design of the design was a limitation to scalability in low power implementation. To make the multiplication more efficient12, combined Wallace Tree Algorithm with Baugh-Wooley multipliers for multiply-accumulate (MAC) units. This approach provided some number of improvements in area, delay, and power consumption but were found to be suitable for mostly large size bit-width computations.

High-speed approximate arithmetic structures have also been explored for power constrained applications such as in image. The design in13 used XOR-MUX based tree compressors and carry-maskable adders and got 12.6% delay reduction. Nevertheless, the approximation had not considered the bits that were not processed, which led to a very slight loss of the accuracy. Likewise, work in14 proposed a 16-bit high speed approximate multiplier based on multiple adder compressors and OR based adder combined with adaptive voltage scaling. The implementation showed speed increased by 55.44% which makes it applicable for error tolerant tasks like image processing and data mining with loss of precision for higher values of the product. In15 an approximate addition has been applied to Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) stage of the image compression. By replacing exact adders by approximate adders in a Carry Save Adder (CSA) tree, the system was able to compute faster while having low hardware complexity and image quality was acceptable (PSNR approx. 30 dB). However, some visual deterioration was found in the high-frequency area and the approach can’t be used for lossless compression. Complementing this the authors of16 suggested the use of an Approximate Full Adder (AFA) based on Type-3 MUX design for 4:2 compressors for Sobel edge detection. The implemented solutions achieved up to 66% downturn in power and 58% downturn in delay with a high PSNR values without significant drops in SSIM in case of sharp pixel transitions.

Approximate arithmetic has also been added to transform-based image compression architectures. The work in17 explored the application of energy efficient discrete haar wavelet transform (DHWT) Building designs using imprecise adders. Implemented on FPGA, the design has been able to achieve more than 40dB PSNR with a delay of 0.33ns only, the only drawback of such approximation to uniform distribution over the image was that some texture information could be lost. Expanding on the concept of transform-domain compression18, proposed an efficient DWT-based image compression based on wavelet decomposition and sub-bands. The method got PSNR of 45.15 dB and noticeable size reduction but hardware architectural aspects were not handled. The work in19 proposed a Sub-band Replacement DWT (SR-DWT) method, in which chrominance components were replaced sub-bands, for the sake of better compression efficiency. This approach resulted in PSNR values of 45–56dB with low RMSE for high resolution images but the band substitution was fixed, lowering the adaptability. Biorthogonal wavelets-based approaches have also been investigated in20, where the authors were able to construct symmetric filters for perfect reconstruction with a better visual quality if compared to orthogonal wavelets. But despite their benefits, dual filter banks did create an increase in the computational complexity and memory requirements.

Further optimization was done in21 in which biorthogonal wavelet based compression algorithm with multiplier-less 2D filter mask operations is presented. Using shift-and-add logic and Hilbert scanning for encoding, the system was able to improve compression ratios by as much as 60% as compared to that of the standard image compression in the form of a JPG without reducing the PSNR in the image. However, the limited flexibility of the fixed dyadic filters, with complicated treatment of the boundaries, limits its hardware flexibility. Finally, the hardware-oriented point of view was also covered in22, where a lifting-based 9/7 biorthogonal DWT architecture for Visual Sensor Networks (VSNs) was proposed. Implemented in Verilog with pipelined shift-add operations, the design had high throughput with a low level of hardware complexity, concentrating mostly in the design of 1D DWT, and more resources for 2D scalability. Overall, from the existing literature, there is a steady trend of the approximate arithmetic technique as well as transform-based compression technique to ensure a balance between computations and visual quality. However, in the absence of adequate error analysis and validation of hardware scalability, many designs are taking the tradeoff between precision and power and area efficiency. Therefore, there is still room for the use of architectures with adaptive approximation and transform-based compression together with controlled error metrics for high-quality real-time image processing application.

From the reviewed literature, it can be noticed that previous works have primarily focused on approximate compressors, approximate multipliers or transform-based compression as individual optimization problems. In contrast, the current work focuses on a combined architecture-level and application-level view by integrating AFA-based hybrid multiplier and AHKSA assisted final addition stage in a complete DWT compression - reconstruction pipeline. This allows for a cohesive evaluation of power, delay, area and reconstructed image quality, which is not so often handled together in existing approximate DWT-oriented studies.

Methodology

Discrete wavelet transform

The discrete wavelet transform is one of the basic methods of image processing and has been widely applied to compression, feature extraction and smoothing of the image. DWT represents an image using discretely sampled wavelets that contain information both in the domain of space and frequency. Wavelets are derived from a function of finite energy and zero mean, called a mother wavelet, by a combination of scaling and translation operations, and so on to construct a series of orthogonal wavelet basis functions. This facilitates the acquisition of multiresolution representation of input image23. The discrete wavelet transform is implemented in filter bank implementation in real-world image processing applications. For processing two-dimensional images, one-dimensional filters (low and high-pass) are first applied on the rows of the image and then on the columns. As seen in Fig. 123, this 2D decomposition gives rise to 4 major sub bands: Low-Low LL (Approximation), Low-High LH (Horizontal details), High-Low HL (Vertical details), and High-High HH (Diagonal details).

LL, LH, HL, and HH sub-bands are displayed in the 2D Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) decomposition.

The LL (low-low) sub-band consists of the low-frequency representations and gives the crude approximation of the input image.

Horizontal edge features are detected using the LH (Low-High) sub-band which applies a high pass to columns and a low pass to rows.

Vertical edge features are extracted in the HL (high-low) sub-band, where high pass filtering is applied horizontally and low pass filtering is applied vertically.

The diagonal edge information is obtained by HH (High-High) sub-band (high pass applied in both rows and columns).

In case of image processing, the DWT is implemented in 2 dimension by separable filtering - Implementation of the 1D DWT along the row direction, followed by column direction. Considering that bulk of the image information is concentrated with in the low frequency components, the LL band is usually encoded with more precision, whereas the other bands can be less precisely quantified. This technique causes a lot of compression in an image and very little perceptual damage19,23.

This property allows the lossless or near-lossless recovery in the inverse transformation process and is conducive to the suitability of the DWT for image compression applications.

The big novelty of the proposed approach is that conventional multipliers in the DWT filtering operations are replaced by approximate multipliers. Exact arithmetic modules, on which the traditional DWT systems are based, cause a significant increase in processing latency, power consumption and hardware complexity. However, the layout suggested uses approximating multiplier circuits to allow for arithmetic inaccuracy to be controlled in exchange for a significant minimization in power, delay and area. The error resilience characteristic of image data, especially in the higher frequencies of any sub-band makes possible the utilization of overly approximate multipliers with only modest impact on perceptual image quality and PSNR.

Once the image-compression framework based on DWT has been established, the methodology presents the hierarchy of the proposed approximate arithmetic units whereby the first image-compression block, the approximate-adder, is presented, followed by the multiplier architecture based on the approximate-adder.

An approximate adder is an arithmetic logic unit component that does binary addition but tolerates controlled imprecision in an effort to achieve important benefits in such hardware efficacy factors as power, area and delay. In contrast to traditional adders that guarantee accurate outcomes to guarantee efficiency, approximate adders compromise accuracy to guarantee both area and speed efficiency that render them highly effective in error-tolerant programs e.g. video processing, image compression and machine learning. Approximate adders tend to be categorized as either dynamic or static adders. A fixed architecture approximate adder is one with a fixed structure and thus produces the same output given that the inputs all combine in a particular combination. The reduction in the complexity of logic used in these adders results in reduced dynamic activity reduced delay and reduced area used. To get these efficiency gains however comes at some cost to the accuracy. Conversely, there are dynamic approximate adders that have adaptive approximation that allows the circuit to give accurate or approximate sums to be determined based on the requirements of the system. In order to further enable this flexibility dynamic approximate adders add error-detection and compensation logic to minimize the difference between precise and approximate answers. Although this provides greater flexibility, the additional circuitry and compute cycles may cause the efficiency improvements to be less than the efficiency improvements of static adders and in that scenario dynamic adders may be less efficient than static adders. Experiments on real life applications like video-processing24 revealed that the reduction in power consumption realized using dynamic approximate designs is similar to that achieved using the static designs. Thus, this paper will concentrate on the static approximate adders due to the fact that it needs less hardware and more appropriate to integrate into the ASIC and FPGA.

Approximation strategy

The approximation in the proposed multiplier architectures is introduced selectively in order to reduce the cost of the hardware while limiting the degradation to the arithmetic. First, partial product generation is retained with the help of standard AND based logic so that large errors at input stage are avoided. Second, approximation is used in the method of partial product reduction, where the proposed approximate full adders (AFA1-AFA4) are used in the place of accurate full adders in the lower-weight columns. Third, in the last several bits, where the proposed AHKSA will have approximate carry handling in the least significant bits, and will have exact computation in the more significant stages. This strategy of selective approximation limits the dominant approximation effects in the computation of low significance computation and is thus appropriate for DWT-based image compression.

Kogge–Stone Adder (KSA) and approximate AHKSA

The Kogge- Stone Adder (KSA) is a high speed parallel prefix adder and widely used for wide-bit arithmetic operations due to its low fan-out and logarithmic logic depth25. Unlike ripple-carry and conventional carry-lookahead adders, the KSA generates the carry signals in parallel using a prefix-tree structure and reduces the propagation delay26. Approximate Hybrid Kogge–Stone Adder

The proposed Approximate Hybrid Kogge-Stone Adder (AHKSA) replaces the exact full adders in the least significant bit (LSB) stages with the proposed approximate versions of the full adders, and keeps exact computation in the more significant stages as shown in Fig. 3. This hybrid structure decreases the delay, power consumption and area utilization without a big impact on the overall arithmetic accuracy. Since approximation is limited to low-significance positions, the impact of approximation on the quality of DWT reconstruction is limited. In contrast to a conventional KSA that uses exact logic in the entire prefix network, the proposed AHKSA uses the speed benefit of parallel-prefix carry processing on the one hand, and localized approximation in the LSB-region on the other, making it appropriate for hardware-efficient final-stage accumulation in the proposed multiplier architectures.

Multiplier architectures

Baugh Wooley

One of the hardware optimized multipliers for the multiplication of signed digital numbers in 2’s compliment form is the Baugh Wooley multiplier. Conventional multiplication of signed numbers typically needs additional components for the processing of negative partial products and sign bits which makes the propagation delay and the complexity increase. The Baugh Wooley algorithm has the signed bit built directly in the product array. This technique facilitates multipliers to compute the resulting product in an optimal and efficient way without further adjustment steps making it suitable for fast and energy efficient digital systems such as DSP units, embedded processors, signal processing hardware and microcontrollers29,30. The design of the Baugh-Wooley multiplier is based on a systematic design of arrays which is used to align partial product generated by AND gates one after another. In the currently known techniques, these partial products are added, using carry save adders and full adders, which take care of the effects of signed bits. These types of architecture reduces the complexity of the hardware and also ensure the correct processing of signed numbers at the same time supports the scalability to the higher order of multiplications. Implementation on FPGAs such integration of soft CPU has shown optimized processing efficiency and execution in comparison to other signed multiplication methods like the Booth multiplier30,31. More recent research has been done for speeding up and making the Baugh Wooley multiplier more efficient. Strategies such as fast multiplier trees and optimized full adder structures have been introduced in order to reduce the leading to decrease the computational latency and power consumption, while maintaining the throughput at the same time. Comparative studies show the superior results of the Baugh Wooley Multiplier compared to the conventional multipliers in terms of hardware utilization, speed and energy efficient which is a favorable choice for arithmetic intensive applications like matrix multiplication, FIR filters and other DSP operations32,33. Its high speed, low power and scalable architecture ensures that it is an important architecture in modern digital systems.

Dadda multiplier

One of the most optimized fast parallel multipliers that are used in digital signal processing and VLSI technology design is the Dadda multiplier34. The aim of the Wallace and Dadda multipliers is to minimize the delay caused by the combination of partial products. However, the main difference is that the Dadda multiplier compresses only enough to meet computed threshold in order to minimize the total number of adders required, whereas the Wallace tree technique simply compresses as heavily at every level35. This gives an architecture with a near equal performance of Wallace with minimized power and area use.

The bulletin step in the multiplication process is to take partial products of the multiplicand and multiplier by using the bitwise AND operation. These intermediate products are an array of which each column can consist of several bits. The Dadda multiplier uses ordered minimization technique using (2,2) counters (half adders) and (3,2) counters (full adders) rather than adding them one after another which may be slow36. This architecture can be put into three levels (i) generation of partial products by simple AND gates (ii) Hierarchical minimization by Full adders and half adders controlled by Dadda’s reduction rules. (iii) Final propagate addition step to come up with the product37. Recent study has demonstrated the original architecture by using approximate computation techniques to satisfy the reliability, as well as energy requirements of modern applications. For example, gate level approximation method by using 4:2 compressors which has proved an improvement up to 35% in delay and 60% in power consumption for an 8-bit Dadda multiplier which is efficient in image processing application where small inaccuracies are negligible38.

Wallace Tree multiplier

One of the fundamental algorithms to use in high speed multipliers to minimize the delay caused by a partial product sum is the Wallace tree. Findings show that “The Wallace Tree Multiplier is better suited for high speed applications as a consequence of its parallel levels of reduction, which implies reduction of the delay”39. The outlined three conventional steps of binary multiplication - Partial Product Generation, Partial Product Reduction, Final Addition - are the outline for the Wallace Tree Method. The Wallace Tree Algorithm is focused on the PPR, the second step which adds a significant portion of the circuit’s latency. The basis of algorithm is to minimize generated partial products at each stage as much as possible as compared to Dadda algorithm which have limited reduction at each stage. The Wallace Tree algorithm implements a systematic reduction of the generated partial products which shorter the computational time required leveraging the speed of overall multiplier. The image processing applications and in particular the image compression make use of the unsigned numbers. This enables the Wallace Tree reduction based multiplier to be a very efficient multiplier of unsigned multiplies40. Various modified techniques of the Wallace Tree Multiplier have been designed over the years based on the original Wallace Tree Algorithm40,41. Presents implementation of an optimised WTM for image processing applications42. The study was carried out to understand basic logic behind the algorithm and few of adaptations which have been published to design efficient low power approximate multiplier based upon the Wallace Tree Algorithm.

Comparative analysis

This sub-section shows the extended evaluation structure that will be utilized in testing the proposed hybrid approximate multiplier architectures outside of the main hardware-synthesis and DWT-reconstruction tests. In addition to synthesis-based hardware analysis, the framework comprises external benchmarking, dataset-based image quality evaluation and hardware-versus-quality-analysis in order to measure generalization capability and relate the efficiency of the circuit level to the perceptual reconstruction performance.

Dataset-based image-quality evaluation using Kodak image suite

To assess the behaviour of the proposed architectures for various image characteristics, 6 images of the Kodak Lossless Image Suite were used: kodim01, kodim05, kodim12, kodim14, kodim17 and kodim23. These images correspond to smooth areas, textured areas, complicated edges, and different illumination conditions. All the images were set to grayscale and run by the DWT compression pipeline in MATLAB on the AFA1–4 architecture and using the GSA baseline RCA. Reconstruction quality has been evaluated with PSNR, SSIM, RMSE, and MAE that can collectively give an all-round measurement of the system-level effect of arithmetic approximation. Wavelet sub-band quantization applied to the wavelet coefficients in compression with reduced processing precision of the higher frequency band and inverse quantization and image reconstruction.

Hardware–quality trade-off and correlation analysis

To understand the link between circuit-level efficiency and end-to-end reconstruction performance, the average results for the image-quality reconstruction of the Kodak database were correlated with corresponding hardware synthesis metrics of each of the architectures. Power consumption, critical path delay, power-delay product (PDP) were analysed along with the PSNR, SSIM, RMSE and MAE. The architectures that are most beneficial in terms of hardware efficiency and the quality of reconstructed images have been determined using trade-off plots and a correlation analysis.

Integration within the overall evaluation workflow

The long analysis node is composed of the hardware synthesis and application level image-quality estimation in a workflow. At the hardware level, the architectures of the multipliers are proposed, which are analyzed in terms of power, area and delay. At the application level, the same architectures are incorporated into the image-compression chain based on DWT in Matlab where the quality of reconstruction is tested by applying objective quality metrics. In addition, external benchmarking, dataset-based analysis, and hardware-quality trade-off evaluation are used to relate the efficiency of implementation with the perceptual image performance.

Results and discussion

The results demonstrate that the combination of hybrid approximate multipliers with the DWT based image compression pipeline achieves an attractive hardware quality trade-off, with the AFA4 approximation proving to be the best configuration overall. Synthesis of FPGA on the Artix-7 platform demonstrates that the AFA4-based platforms lower critical-path delay and power consumption compared to the identical RCA baselines without compromising LUT or flip-flop usage. Among the Baugh-Wooley, Dadda and Wallace families, the hybrid designs are generally better in each of the multiplier classes than conventional ones. At the application level, the AFA4 architectures exhibit the best reconstruction fidelity compared to the other approximate architectures in terms of higher PSNR and SSIM values and lower MAE and RMSE values while achieving competitive compression ratios. Visual analysis also shows that AFA4-based reconstructions are much closer to the original images than are the more artifact-prone AFA1 and AFA2 cases. External benchmarking places the proposed approach between ultra low power nanoscale designs and architectures with better accuracy but more complex and costly integration (CMOS-based) and the Kodak-image trade off analysis confirms that there is a monotonic increase from AFA1 to AFA4 and that the most attractive practical trade-off point is AFA4.

Hardware utilization

Hardware metrics like area (LUTs, FFs and slices), power and delay are used to assess the efficiency of the proposed multiplier architectures. The performance of each proposed multiplier is summarised in Table 3.

The preliminary results show that the architectures with the fourth approximation (AFA4) are better in overall performance compare to the other proposed variants. Based on the analysis of the results obtained it can be found that every one of the different multiplier architectures has its own advantage over others in specific parameters. For example, while the other architectures demonstrate advantages such as area or latency, HBWM is a better solution when it comes to power consumption. In terms of area occupancy, the HBWM (BW+AFA1) has a 10.4% less area occupancy than traditional designs. Other important improvement over current designs are 2.03% and 2.55%, respectively, from the HDM and HWM. In terms of power consumed by the multiplier the proposed design (HBWM) has 5% improvement over traditional architecture. HWM and HDM architecture produces little power consumption of 47.2 and 46.6 mW respectively. Other designs results in significant reduction in the amount of power consumed over the existing design. To determine the speed of the multiplier, the critical route delay, that is the longest route between the inputs and the outputs of the multiplier, is calculated. The hybrid architecture proposed to be better than the traditional designs in each of the various forms implemented and tested. Results shows that the HBWM architectures (AFA4, AFA3) have 10.93%, 7.81% respectively over the traditional methods. The HDM architecture (AFA1, AFA2) has good improved 12% delay when compared the proposed HBWM design. Other proposed designs like HDM (AFA4, AFA3) with 11.66%, 10% and HWM (AFA4, AFA3) with 11.2% and 9.67% better performance than standard multiplier architecture of their respective variants. The fourth approximation gives the best overall performance as it contains both aggressive least significant bit approximation and balanced reduction behaviour leading to a reduced delay and power consumption without significant error propagation to higher significance levels.

Interpretation

The CNTFET-based compressors and multipliers in44 have an ultra-low power consumption and picosecond-scale delay, which is the lower range of the nanoscale implementation spectrum. On the other hand, the reconstruction quality of PAWM1 and PAWM243 is extremely high with PSNR > 51 dB, but with a higher power budget. The lifting based DWT architecture45 gives meaningful algorithmic comparison and no hardware metrics. These reference designs characterize a practical range of operation of approximate multipliers and contextualise the trade-offs of efficiency and image quality achieved by the proposed hybrid architecture. Concurrently to these hardware based methods, Kumar47 describes an area efficient approximate multiplier with error correction logic and hence makes the case of error conscious approximation in CMOS designs more convincing. At the algorithmic level, Al-Khafaji and Ramaha48 report high quality image compression using the help of hybrid deep learning and underline the competitiveness of hardware-efficient deep-DWT-based solutions with data-intensive models.

Conclusion

This work presents an approximate DWT-based image compression framework which integrates well-designed approximate arithmetic units providing effective hardware-software trade-off between implementation cost and computational accuracy. Through the insertion of approximate multipliers in the wavelet-filtering stages, the proposed architecture makes use of the inherent error tolerance of high frequency DWT sub-bands without sacrificing the perceptual image quality. In the total of considered designs, the AFA4 approximation variant of Hybrid Dadda Multiplier (HDM) integrated with AHKSA has the most preferred relationship between energy efficiency and reconstruction performance. The proposed architecture attains substantial improvements in power consumption, area and delay as compared with accurate and ripple carry based implementations while maintaining high perceptual quality with regard to PSNR and SSIM. Notably, the AFA4 HDM has a low power consumption of 46.6 mW and a delay of 5.3 ns which is one of the best hardware results among the evaluated designs. The quantitative and visual analyses reveal that AFA4-based hybrid structures result in a quality of the reconstruction very close to those of the exact RCA-based implementation. In addition, the AFA4 HDM has a maximum PSNR of 35.6 dB, a SSIM of 0.94 and a compression ratio of 1.9. Error analysis shows further that AFA4 HDM results in a low MAE of 3.8 and an error rate of 1.25%. Extended benchmarking and evaluation based on Kodak images prove the robustness of the proposed designs, and the hardware quality vs. trade-off analysis shows that the AFA4 variant is most appropriate for practical DWT based image compression systems. These results show that the proposed framework is suitable for real-time and resource constrained imaging applications such as edge devices and embedded vision systems.
